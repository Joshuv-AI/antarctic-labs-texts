Conservative Management of Invasive Cervical Resorption: A Case Report

Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT“Invasive cervical resorption” (ICR) is a disorder that impacts the region of root surface that is present below the level of attachment of epithelium. Clinically, it manifests as a pink stain on the cervical part of the tooth. It may be due to visualization of the densely vascularized granular tissue across the resorbed structure of the tooth. The invasive defect should be made visible, the granulation tissue should be removed, and the defect should be sealed using a variety of restorative materials. In this report, a patient who presented with trauma related Class II ICR in the cervical region of the left second premolar of the maxilla was treated conservatively. It was shown that patients who are reluctant to undergo surgery or in whom surgery is not recommended could be treated with this conservative approach to stop the advancement of ICR.

INTRODUCTION

“Invasive cervical resorption” (ICR) is a disorder that impacts the region of root surface that is present below the level of attachment of epithelium. ICR is broadly defined as “a localized resorptive process that commences on the surface of the root below the epithelial attachment and the coronal aspect of the supporting alveolar process, namely the zone of the connective tissue attachment.”[1]

Patients who initially appear with this illness typically do not exhibit any symptoms, until there is additional periodontal infection or pulpal infection resulting from the lesion invading the pulp.[2] Clinically, it manifests as a pink stain on the cervical part of the tooth. It may be due to visualization of the densely vascularized granular tissue across the resorbed structure of the tooth. Usually, during an everyday dental assessment, it is discovered as a co-incidental radiography finding.[3]

While a number of etiological factors, such as periodontal therapy and dentoalveolar surgery, have been linked to this form of resorption, orthodontic therapy is thought to be the most prevalent predisposing factor. It may be followed by intra-coronal whitening and trauma.[4] To make the right diagnosis and present the patient with a workable treatment plan, it is imperative to distinguish ICR with internal resorption.[56]

When making a diagnosis, radiographic reports and clinical characteristics are crucial. Using the parallax approach, peri-apical imaging can be used to track the pulp chamber’s continuous contour. While the lesion in ICR appears to migrate with the change in the X-ray tube’s alignment, the lesion in internal resorption stays localized on the root canal.[345]

“Cone beam computerized tomography” (CBCT) is an alternative that may be utilized to help precisely evaluate the dimension, depth, and extent of the lesion. Relative to conventional radiography, CBCT enhances the accuracy of the diagnosis.[234]

The invasive defect should be made visible, the granulation tissue should be removed, and the defect should be sealed using a variety of restorative materials.[123] In this report, a patient who presented with trauma-related Class II ICR in the cervical region of the left second premolar of the maxilla was treated conservatively.

CASE REPORT

The primary complaint of a male patient, aged 25 years, who came to the Division of Endodontics was about pain and discoloration in his left second premolar of the maxilla. The patient had no relevant medical history. He stated that during the previous 3 to 4 months, the color shift he had noticed [Figure 1] had gotten worse. The patient’s dental history disclosed an untreated injury from trauma that occurred 3 years prior. On intraoral assessment, every region of the tooth (Buccal and palatal) had normal probing periodontal depths, with the exception of the distopalatal surface, which had necrotic material and a sinus tract. Preoperative radiographs of tooth #25’s distal aspect showed an uneven and mottled pattern in the cervical region of the exterior root surface [Figure 1a and b]. To pinpoint the precise location and size of the lesion, an IOPAR was taken. The supraosseous nature of the lesion was confirmed by the PA image [Figure 1c]. Tooth #25 was diagnosed as necrotic pulp with exterior cervical resorption of roots (“Heithersay’s Class III”), and a root canal treatment plan was created [Figure 1].

Clinical presentation of case before treatment: (a) buccal view, (b) palatal view, and (c) periapical radiograph

Local anesthetic was given after getting informed consent, and the tooth was isolated using a rubber dam. The tooth’s working length was ascertained, and a coronal access opening was created. The “F5 ProTaper universal rotary system” was utilized for the mechanical-chemical preparation process. The root canal received irrigation with a combination of normal saline and five milliliters of 3% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl).

Next, the lateral and heated vertical condensation approach was used to obturate the root canal. For periodontal reconstructive surgery, the patient was directed to the Periodontics Section [Figure 2]. A ”mucoperiosteal flap” was elevated under local anesthetic to reveal the area of operation and a sizable granulation tissue-filled resorptive region [Figure 2a]. Applying a hand excavator, granulation tissue was meticulously removed; no pulpal breach was seen. Following removal of tissue, “Biodentine” was used to fill the resorptive deformity and shape it to resemble the exterior root structure. The “mucoperiosteal flap” was sutured utilizing absorbable suture material and returned to its original position after the filling material had been appropriately positioned and adjusted [Figure 2b]. The same has been confirmed radiographically by taking PA View [Figure 2c]. After a review 2 months afterward, the patient underwent a gingivectomy to guarantee the best possible gingival health and contour.

DISCUSSION

The patient’s early childhood trauma incident was probably the source of the ICR in this managed case in point. It may take some time to realize the association between the two because ICR frequently becomes apparent a lot later than the initial trauma.

In a research by Heithersay, it was found that 15% of the teeth that had been traumatized later experienced ICR.[34] Because of their position in the arch, “central incisors” of the maxilla are more often the teeth that experience trauma and are consequently more likely to acquire ICR.[56]

Both the surgical therapy and the root canal were finished in just one visit to reduce the chance of a subsequent infection. To encourage the best possible recovery following root canal treatment, a full-thickness flap being elevated. Ninety percent “trichloroacetic acid” (TCA) and saline were used to debride the resorptive region in order to improve the restorative material’s adaptability.[234]

“Glass ionomer cement” (GIC) and composite resin materials usually do not anticipate periodontal reattachment. Despite providing superior periodontal attachment, “mineral trioxide aggregate” (MTA) was not chosen in this instance because of its lengthy setting duration and handling challenges.[345] The novel calcium silicate cement “Biodentine” was selected instead because of its bioactivity and easy handling characteristics. With a compressive strength of 225 MPa, a rapid setting period of less than 12 minutes, and superior sealing abilities, biodentine is an effective dentin alternative. Because of its capacity for dispensing calcium ions, the surroundings become more alkaline, which encourages osteoblastic activity.[67] Furthermore, BMP-2, alkaline phosphatase, and pyrophosphatase are stimulated by the discharge of calcium and hydroxide ions, which helps the procedure of mineralization even more.[456]

Another bioceramic cement, called “Bio-C Sealer,” is intended for use in root canal restorations and has shown encouraging results.[34] Another bioceramic cement, “Bio-C Repair,” is recommended for internal root resorption therapy among other uses. In comparison with “Biodentine or ProRoot MTA,” it has demonstrated exceptional cytocompatibility.[567]

Follow-up and outcome

The literature reports post-treatment follow-up periods that vary from 1 to 9 years in length. Clinical and radiographic assessments throughout these investigations revealed no growth of ICR lesions, no fresh resorption regions on radiographs, and no symptoms.[1234] A 12-month follow-up in this patient revealed similar outcomes, such as increased osseous development in the middle region and adequate adaption of the prosthetic element in the cervical region.[567]

CONCLUSION

The successful management of this particular case involved the utilization of both surgical and root canal therapy. For an acceptable result and long-term preservation of the damaged tooth, early detection, complete excision of underlying resorptive defect, and proper restorative therapy are essential. Because of its bioactive qualities, biodentine is a substance that works well for treating ICR and is also easy to work with and compatible with periodontal tissues
