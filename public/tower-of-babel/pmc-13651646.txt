Measles resurgence in Bangladesh: a preventable crisis in a transitioning immunisation system
Authors: Md Zakiul Hassan, Md Ariful Islam, Abir Shaqran Mahmood, Homayra Rahman Shoshi, Md Tajul Islam A. Bari, Mahmudur Rahman, Mohammod Jobayer Chisti
Published: 2026

Bangladesh is experiencing one of the largest measles resurgences, with 49,159 suspected, 6819 laboratory-confirmed measles, and 336 deaths reported as of May 2026.1 This indicates sustained community transmission and a reversal of the country's long-standing immunisation gains.

Bangladesh’s routine immunisation schedule includes two doses of measles-rubella vaccine at 9 and 15 months of age. The current resurgence affecting a large proportion of children under 6 months of age likely reflects a widening immunity gap due to declining measles-rubella vaccination coverage, with some reports suggesting coverage as low as 57%.2 Disruptions to routine immunisation services and persistent coverage gaps among underserved populations, along with missed doses and delayed vaccination, may have contributed to the gradual accumulation of susceptible individuals over time.

Bangladesh has not conducted a nationwide measles campaign since 2020. Catch-up activities were disrupted during the COVID-19 pandemic, a planned 2024 campaign was not implemented due to political unrest, and procurement delays in 2025 further delayed the campaign.3 Rapid urbanisation and internal migration have further compounded vaccine accessibility to under-reached populations. Earlier success in immunisation coverage was achieved through Bangladesh’s Expanded Program on Immunisation, supported by external partners such as GAVI, UNICEF, WHO, and other partners. The current measles resurgence is occurring during Bangladesh’s transition towards greater domestic responsibility for immunisation financing and procurement, alongside vaccine supply and procurement challenges that affected routine immunisation activities.4 The current outbreak affecting substantial proportion of infants below the age of routine vaccination highlights the importance of maintaining high population-level immunity to protect those not yet eligible for vaccination.

The government response included measles-rubella vaccination campaigns targeting children between 6 and 59 months of age in high-risk districts, alongside intensified surveillance, vaccine deployment, and the planned national expansion of vaccination activities.5 Hospitals, overburdened by severe measles complications, are supported through initiatives such as nationwide implementation of improvised bubble CPAP (an innovative, locally made, low-cost, non-invasive respiratory support that maintains continuous positive airway pressure using an underwater seal), expanded ventilator support, and dedicated measles treatment strategies.

Containing the current outbreak will require rapid closure of immunity gaps through targeted catch-up vaccination, strengthened microplanning, and improved identification of under-vaccinated children. Bangladesh should also rapidly assess measles vaccine coverage and timeliness, evaluate vaccine effectiveness where feasible, and conduct genotyping of circulating strains to guide public health action. Beyond the emergency response, sustaining measles control will require resilient domestic financing, timely procurement, dependable local supply chains, and protection of routine immunisation services from further disruption.
