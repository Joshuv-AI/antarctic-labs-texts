Three dimensional morphometric analysis of cervical uncinate process from C3 to C7 with surgical implications
Authors: Şeref Barbaros Arik, Ahmet Metehan Öcal, Nurcan Ercikti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe uncinate process is a key anatomical structure in the cervical spine, essential for uncovertebral joint formation and spinal biomechanics. Although its linear dimensions have been previously described, the relationship between its vertical height and angular orientation remains underexplored. This study aimed to evaluate the three-dimensional morphometry of the uncinate process from C3 to C7, focusing on correlations between height and angular parameters. Fifty-one dry human cervical vertebrae (C3–C7), obtained from multiple adult donors (donor count unknown; sex unknown), were analyzed using high-resolution CT (0.75-mm slices). Although specimens were unevenly distributed across vertebral levels, measurements included uncinate height, uncinate angle, and endplate angle on coronal reconstructions. Statistical analysis involved Wilcoxon signed-rank, Kruskal–Wallis, and Spearman’s correlation tests. No significant side-to-side differences were found in uncinate height or angle, though the endplate angle was significantly higher on the left. Inter-level comparisons revealed significant differences in left uncinate height and bilateral endplate angles. Importantly, uncinate height showed a strong negative correlation with uncinate angle (Right: ρ = − 0.557; Left: ρ = − 0.677; both p < 0.001) and a strong positive correlation with endplate angle (Right: ρ = 0.777; Left: ρ = 0.809; both p < 0.001). These findings underscore the anatomical variability and interdependence of vertical and angular features of the uncinate process, with implications for uncovertebral joint mechanics and surgical approach planning. Introduction

The cervical segment of the vertebral column consists of seven vertebrae, designated C1 to C7. The uncinate process (processus uncinatus) is a bony projection located on the superior aspect of the vertebral bodies from C3 to C71. These structures articulate with the inferior surface of the vertebra above to form the uncovertebral joints. Biomechanically, these joints serve to limit lateral flexion of the head2,3. Each uncinate process has anterior and posterior slopes, a distinct apex, and medially oriented articular surfaces4. In the coronal plane, they exhibit a concave morphology along the superior endplate of the vertebral body5. Notably, the uncinate processes are positioned more anteriorly in the upper cervical spine and shift posteriorly in the lower cervical segments6.

The joint capsule of the uncovertebral articulation is thin, rendering it susceptible to degeneration in cases of repetitive stress, overuse, or poor posture. Nevertheless, such degenerative processes tend to progress slowly7–9. Although the classification of uncovertebral joints as synovial remains debated10. Huang et al.11 demonstrated the presence of cartilage within these joints, suggesting a synovial nature. Similar to other synovial joints, uncovertebral joints are prone to degenerative changes, including osteophyte formation. In the cervical spine, such pathological changes may affect neighboring anatomical structures—most notably, the vertebral artery and cervical spinal nerves—leading to clinical syndromes such as radiculopathy, myelopathy, or vertebrobasilar insufficiency7,8,12.

From a neurosurgical standpoint, detailed morphometric evaluation of the uncinate process is essential for procedures such as uncinotomy and uncovertebral foraminotomy. Although several studies have examined the morphometry of the uncinate process, most have focused primarily on linear dimensions or isolated level-based differences13,14. However, the relationship between vertical height and spatial orientation (angle) of the uncinate process remains underexplored, particularly in three-dimensional reconstructed imaging datasets. This represents a relevant gap, as both vertical and angular variations may influence surgical access during anterior cervical procedures and alter joint biomechanics15,16. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have addressed the potential correlation between uncinate height and angular parameters such as the endplate angle or uncinate inclination, especially using high-resolution CT imaging in a Turkish population. By investigating this relationship, the present study aims to provide additional insight into level-specific and potentially interdependent anatomical variations of the uncinate process. These data may support improved anatomical understanding and inform future research or surgical planning related to the cervical spine.

Methods

This study included 51 dry human cervical vertebrae (C3–C7) from adult Turkish donors (estimated age 65–75 years). Specimens were sourced from an anonymized osteological collection; the exact number of donors was not recorded, and donor sex was unavailable. Only intact specimens exhibiting typical cervical vertebral features were included. Vertebrae with fractures or structural damage were excluded from the study. Measurements were obtained from vertebrae ranging from C3 to C7. Among the specimens, 14 were C3, 17 were C4, 9 were C5, 4 were C6, and 7 were C7 vertebrae. We volumetrically scanned 51 vertebrae at a slice thickness of 0.75 mm via a “Siemens Somatom Scope 16-slice CT” scanner. We then reconstructed the images on a workstation to create MPR (multiplanar reconstruction) and 3D reformatted views, and we performed all the measurements using these reconstructed images. To minimize measurement error, the same researcher conducted all measurements.

All procedures in the study were performed in accordance with national research committee standards and ethical guidelines for the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and later amendments. Ethical approval was obtained from the local non-invasive ethics committee (date: 2025/02, number: 2025-97).

Statistical analysis

To summarize the study’s findings, descriptive statistics for numerical variables were presented in tables based on their distribution, including both mean and median values. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Distribution was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots, supporting the use of non-parametric procedures. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for all paired comparisons and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for all non-parametric group comparisons. Spearman’s correlation test was applied to examine the relationships between uncinate process height and angular parameters.

All statistical analyses were performed in jamovi (Version 2.6; The jamovi project, Sydney, Australia; 2025; https://www.jamovi.org). A p value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results:

Descriptive and comparative analyses of the uncinate process measurements between the right and left sides are presented in Table 1. The mean uncinate height was 5.2 ± 1.6 mm on the right and 5.3 ± 1.5 mm on the left, with no statistically significant difference between sides (p = 0.204). The mean endplate angle was significantly greater on the left (28.47 ± 6.63°) compared to the right side (26.34 ± 5.97°), and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.002). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in the uncinate angle between the right (121.87 ± 14.34°) and left (119.64 ± 14.17°) sides (p = 0.283).

Further comparisons across cervical levels (C3–C7) revealed statistically significant differences in specific uncinate process parameters (Table 2). The left uncinate process height varied significantly among vertebral levels (p = 0.031), as did both right and left endplate angles (p = 0.048 and p = 0.010, respectively). In contrast, no significant differences were found in right uncinate height (p = 0.301), right uncinate angle (p = 0.169), or left uncinate angle (p = 0.056). These results indicate that the geometry of the uncinate process and its angulation relative to the vertebral endplate may differ depending on cervical level (Table 2).

Correlation analysis between uncinate process height and angular parameters is presented in Table 3. A statistically significant negative correlation was observed between uncinate height and uncinate angle on both the right and left sides (Right: ρ = − 0.557; Left: ρ = − 0.677; both p < 0.001), indicating that taller uncinate processes tend to exhibit a more vertical orientation. Conversely, a strong positive correlation was found between uncinate height and endplate angle on both sides (right: ρ = 0.777; left: ρ = 0.809; both p < 0.001), suggesting that as the uncinate process height increases, the angle between its tip and the vertebral endplate becomes steeper. These results highlight a complex morphological relationship between vertical and angular parameters of the uncinate process.

Discussion

Our principal finding is that uncinate process height is strongly and directionally coupled with angular morphology: taller processes are associated with steeper endplate angles and narrower medial inclination, whereas shorter processes show wider uncinate angles. These correlations, demonstrated bilaterally, suggest that vertical and angular dimensions of the uncinate process co-vary in a manner that likely influences uncovertebral joint mechanics and surgical corridors during anterior cervical procedures.

In 1834, Rathke described the uncinate process as a projection located at the posterocraniolateral margin of the vertebral body17. In 1858, von Luschka defined the articulation formed between the uncinate process and the vertebral body of the superior segment as the uncovertebral joint18. The uncovertebral joint forms the medial boundary of the foramen transversarium and lies in close proximity to the vertebral artery. It is believed to contribute to the stability and mobility of the cervical spine by limiting lateral flexion between adjacent vertebrae19. Additionally, it has been proposed that the uncovertebral joint facilitates axial rotation of the cervical vertebrae20.

In our study, we did not evaluate the morphometry of the T1 vertebra. However, Tubbs et al.1 reported in their study that they did not observe any specimens with an uncinate projection on the T1 vertebra. Tubbs et al.1 reported that the height of the uncinate process ranges from 2 to 6.1 mm, with a mean of 4.8 mm. They also noted that taller uncinate processes were generally located below the C3 vertebral level. In a morphometric study, Civelek et al.21 reported that the uncinate process height at the C5 vertebra was shorter than those at the adjacent C4 and C6 levels. Additionally, the uncinate height at C7 was reported to be shorter than at C6. Raveendranath et al.14 observed that uncinate height gradually increased from C3 to C6, followed by a decrease at C7. Similarly, Yilmazlar et al.22 also reported a progressive increase in uncinate process height from C3 to C7. According to findings from previous literature (Table 4), the lowest recorded uncinate height was 2 mm at C7, and the highest was 10.5 mm at C61,23. These morphometric discrepancies may be attributed to variations in sample types, including the use of both dry bones and fresh cadaveric specimens. In our study, the mean uncinate process height was found to be 5.2 ± 1.6 mm on the right side and 5.3 ± 1.5 mm on the left side. We found that the uncinate processes at the C7 vertebra exhibited the lowest height measurements in our sample.

Tubbs et al.1 reported that the angle of the uncinate process from the midline ranged between 10° and 15°, with an average of 13°. Panjabi et al.24 measured the inclination of the uncinate processes relative to the sagittal plane and found a relatively constant angle, peaking at the C7 level, with a mean value of 40.3°. Necdet et al.13 reported this angle as 46.13 ± 15.6° on the right side and 47.25 ± 15.64° on the left side in their study.

Saringer et al.25 measured the angle between the long axis of the uncinate processes and the sagittal plane and reported that this angle increases toward the lower cervical levels. The mean value of this angle was reported as 5.06°. Ugur et al.26 assessed the angle between the medial surface of the uncinate process and the superior surface of the vertebral body, reporting a highly variable range between 90° and 162°.

Because our angular measurements are based on anatomical landmarks that differ from those in prior studies, direct comparisons are necessarily limited. Variations in angle definitions, reference planes, and tangent selection may alter absolute values, even when overall trends are similar. The measurement method applied here may offer a more consistent and practical reference framework for evaluating uncinate morphology in three-dimensional reconstructions. These findings may broaden the understanding of geometric relationships within uncinate anatomy—an aspect that appears underrepresented in current anatomical and surgical literature. In our cohort, the mean endplate angle measured 26.34° ± 5.97° on the right and 28.47° ± 6.63° on the left, whereas the uncinate angle measured 121.87° ± 14.34° on the right and 119.64° ± 14.17° on the left.

Additionally, we conducted a comparison not previously addressed in the literature: the correlation between uncinate process height and angular parameters (Table 3). A statistically significant negative correlation was found between uncinate height and the uncinate angle, indicating that shorter uncinate processes tend to exhibit wider medial inclination. Conversely, a statistically significant positive correlation was observed between uncinate height and the endplate angle, suggesting that taller processes are associated with steeper endplate trajectories.

These findings suggest that the morphology of the uncinate process is not an isolated feature but part of a complex anatomical configuration that may influence joint biomechanics, osteophyte formation, and the safety margins for anterior cervical surgical approaches. Recognizing these correlated variations may improve both preoperative radiological assessments and intraoperative navigation accuracy. From a radiological and surgical perspective, these results highlight that shorter uncinate processes with wider inclination may alter the uncovertebral joint space, potentially affecting patterns of degeneration and modifying access angles during anterior cervical procedures. Overall, these correlations underscore the importance of accounting for individual morphometric variability when planning surgical strategies and interpreting cervical spine imaging.

Tubbs et al.1 noted that the general morphological characteristics of the uncinate processes—such as thickness and height—were approximately similar in both young and elderly skeletal specimens. Although the exact age of the dry bones used in our study was not known, the estimated age range of our specimens was between 65 and 75 years.

Tubbs et al.1 also reported that there were no statistically significant differences in uncinate process measurements based on sex or laterality. Since the sex of the vertebrae in our study was unknown, a sex-based comparison could not be performed. Consistent with their findings, we observed no significant differences in uncinate height or angle between sides. However, a statistically significant difference was found in the endplate angle (p < 0.01), with higher values on the left side (Table 1). Although this difference reached statistical significance, the absolute magnitude was relatively small. Therefore, its clinical relevance may be limited. Nonetheless, the finding underscores the importance of recognizing subtle anatomical variations between sides during preoperative planning—particularly in procedures involving anterior cervical approaches or uncovertebral joint decompression.

Previous studies examining differences across cervical levels have shown that uncovertebral joints tend to be located more posterolaterally in the lower cervical spine, and that the basal width of the uncinate process gradually increases at these levels. Uncovertebral joints situated closer to the superior vertebral body are exposed to greater mechanical stress compared to other regions of the vertebral body, which may contribute to delayed degenerative changes2,3.

In our morphometric analysis of measurements among cervical vertebral levels (C3–C7), statistically significant differences were observed in the left uncinate process length (p = 0.031) and both right and left endplate angles (p = 0.048 and p = 0.010, respectively) (Table 2). These findings suggest that the geometry of the uncinate process and its spatial relationship to the vertebral endplate may vary depending on the vertebral level.

From a clinical standpoint, these anatomical differences are particularly important in procedures involving the uncovertebral joint or the anterior approach to the cervical spine (e.g., anterior cervical discectomy and fusion—ACDF). Pre-operative CT can be used to extract the same height and angle metrics we report. Higher uncinate height with steeper endplate angle implies a narrower, more cranially inclined corridor over the uncovertebral joint. In such cases, surgeons may select a slightly more medial starting point for osteophyte removal to avoid skiving laterally toward the vertebral artery, orient the burr trajectory more cranio-medially to respect the endplate slope, and anticipate reduced working room between the apex and superior endplate—favoring thinner instruments and incremental bony resection. Conversely, shorter uncinate height with wider medial inclination suggests a broader lateral working angle, but may increase the tendency to over-resect laterally; frequent fluoroscopic checks and attention to the medial border of the foramen are advisable. These morphology-guided adjustments can refine safety margins and help tailor decompression to level-specific anatomy2,19,23,25,26. Therefore, understanding vertebral level–specific morphologic characteristics can enhance surgical precision, reduce complications, and potentially guide the design of level-specific implants or surgical navigation protocols2,3,25.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of vertebrae analyzed was limited, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, due to the retrospective design and the use of dry vertebrae, information regarding the sex and exact age of the specimens was unavailable, and the exact number of donors was not recorded; together, these constraints prevented subgroup analyses based on sex, age, or donor-related factors and may have introduced sampling imbalance. Third, dry bone specimens may exhibit anatomical alterations due to postmortem changes or long-term storage conditions, which could influence the accuracy of certain measurements. Additionally, artificial intelligence (AI) use. No generative AI was used for writing, interpretation, or decision-making. Earlier drafts leveraged AI tools only to orchestrate standard statistical routines; however, all analyses were re-run and cross-checked in jamovi (Version 2.6). This is acknowledged as a limitation. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the morphometric characteristics of the uncinate process and their potential clinical relevance.

Conclusion

This 3D morphometric analysis of the uncinate process between C3 and C7 offers new quantitative insights into its vertical and angular anatomy. The findings indicate that uncinate process height and its spatial orientation not only vary across cervical levels but also show significant correlations with angular parameters. Specifically, taller uncinate processes tended to be associated with steeper endplate angles and narrower medial inclinations, while shorter processes exhibited wider uncinate angles.

Although side-to-side comparisons revealed a statistically significant difference in endplate angles, the clinical significance of this asymmetry may be limited. Nevertheless, these subtle anatomical variations highlight the importance of accounting for individual morphometric differences, particularly in the context of preoperative imaging evaluation and surgical planning.

From a radiological and anatomical perspective, a more detailed understanding of uncinate morphology may improve the interpretation of cervical spine imaging, assist in identifying uncovertebral joint degeneration, and support safer, more customized anterior surgical interventions.

In summary, this study provides correlation-based morphometric data that may help refine preoperative planning and inform future anatomical or surgical research. The observed interdependence between vertical and angular dimensions adds to the anatomical literature by offering a potentially useful framework for understanding uncinate process variation.
