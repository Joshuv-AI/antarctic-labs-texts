Differential Effects of Antidepressants on Hypothalamic Serotonin Levels in Rats: An In Vivo Microdialysis Study
Authors: Jae Heon Kim, Hyung Gun Kim, Miho Song, Chan Young Lee, Yun Seob Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAntidepressants can induce urinary retention, difficulty in voiding, and delayed micturition, effects thought to result from detrusor relaxation and increased urethral sphincter tone mediated by enhanced central serotonergic activity. However, direct measurements of brain serotonin following antidepressant treatment remain limited. The relative risk of voiding difficulty across different antidepressant classes may help guide individualized antidepressant selection. This study aimed to assess changes in hypothalamic serotonin levels after administration of various antidepressants using an animal microdialysis model.MethodsMale Sprague-Dawley rats (280–330 g) were randomly assigned to 5 groups (n=8 per group) and received intraperitoneal injections of saline, clomipramine, fluoxetine, sertraline, or paroxetine (10 mg/kg). Dialysate samples were collected every 20 minutes in freely moving animals and analyzed for serotonin levels via high-performance liquid chromatography with electrochemical detection.ResultsAll tested antidepressants significantly elevated extracellular serotonin in the hypothalamus compared to baseline. Considering that clomipramine and paroxetine combine high serotonergic elevation with notable anticholinergic activity, these drugs may pose a higher risk of voiding difficulties than fluoxetine or sertraline. • HIGHLIGHTS

- Awake-animal microdialysis demonstrated distinct hypothalamic serotonergic responses to commonly used antidepressants.

- Clomipramine and sertraline produced rapid and pronounced serotonin elevations, whereas paroxetine and fluoxetine showed slower, more moderate increases.

- These findings provide neurochemical insight into antidepressant effects on central pathways involved in lower urinary tract regulation and may help inform antidepressant selection in patients at risk of voiding difficulty.

INTRODUCTION

The use of antidepressants has risen substantially in recent decades and voiding difficulties have been recognized as a potential adverse effect of these drugs [1-5]. In a large pharmacovigilance study of 53,042 psychiatric patients between 1993 and 2000, urinary retention (UR) requiring catheterization was reported in about 0.025% of patients receiving selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), either alone or in combination, with higher incidence when multiple antidepressants were used concurrently [6]. Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), particularly clomipramine, were associated with UR approximately twice as often as SSRIs [6].

Antidepressants such as clomipramine (TCA) and SSRIs (e.g., sertraline, paroxetine, fluoxetine) increase synaptic serotonin by blocking its reuptake at nerve terminals. In vitro studies suggest that sertraline is a more potent serotonin reuptake inhibitor than clomipramine or fluoxetine, whereas paroxetine is even more potent than sertraline.

Serotonergic neurotransmission plays a central inhibitory role in the control of lower urinary tract function. Activation of central serotonergic pathways suppresses parasympathetic excitatory input to the urinary bladder and modulates urethral sphincter activity through spinal and supraspinal mechanisms. Supraspinal circuits involving the periaqueductal gray, pontine micturition center, hypothalamus, and parahippocampal cortex integrate emotional, cognitive, and visceral inputs to regulate continence. Previous studies demonstrated that N-methyl-Daspartate receptor activation enhances hippocampal 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) release via interneuron-dependent mechanisms (Fig. 1) [7-12].

Excess central serotonin can inhibit detrusor muscle contraction and increase urethral sphincter tone, leading to urinary hesitancy or retention—a mechanism proposed to underlie antidepressant-induced voiding difficulties. Clinically, the reported risk of voiding difficulty varies among serotonergic antidepressants, with clomipramine showing the highest incidence, followed by paroxetine, fluoxetine, and sertraline. However, it remains uncertain whether this hierarchy directly reflects the magnitude of serotonin elevation in the brain [13-17]. Notably, few studies have prospectively measured central serotonin changes following different antidepressants.

If it were possible to predict and rank the likelihood of voiding difficulty according to different classes of antidepressants, this could substantially aid clinicians in managing and preventing antidepressant-induced voiding dysfunction as an adverse effect of treatment.

The hypothalamus is a key regulator of micturition and integrates stress responses via the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. We hypothesized that antidepressant-induced changes in hypothalamic serotonin might correlate with their propensity to cause voiding difficulties. Using an awake animal microdialysis model, we measured extracellular serotonin in the rat hypothalamus following administration of clomipramine and SSRIs. Importantly, we did not directly measure UR or voiding function in these animals; instead, our study focused on central serotonergic changes as a mechanistic surrogate for antidepressant-induced voiding difficulties. This study aimed to determine whether the magnitude and temporal profile of serotonin elevation differ among these drugs and whether such differences could help explain their known clinical risk of voiding difficulties.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and Drug Administration

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (280–330 g) were randomly assigned to 5 groups (n=8 per group) and received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of either saline (control) or 1 of 4 antidepressants: clomipramine, fluoxetine, sertraline, or paroxetine, each at a dose of 10 mg/kg. This dose was selected to represent a therapeutically relevant acute exposure in rats.

Awake Animal Microdialysis Procedure

The experimental timeline and setup are summarized in Fig. 2. Under anesthesia, rats were secured in a stereotaxic frame. Using coordinates from the Paxinos and Watson rat brain atlas [18], a 3-mm cranial hole was drilled 0.9 mm posterior to bregma and 0.6 mm lateral to the right. A guide cannula was implanted targeting the right medial preoptic area (MPOA) of the hypothalamus at a depth of 8.0 mm from the dura. The cannula was fixed to the skull with a screw and dental cement. Following surgery, a dummy probe was inserted, and animals were allowed to recover in their home cages for 24 hours. On the day of the experiment, the dummy probe was replaced with a microdialysis probe (CMA/12, 2-mm membrane length, 20,000 Da cutoff; CMA Microdialysis AB, Sweden), positioned in the MPOA. Rats were placed in a CMA/120 awake microdialysis system, which allowed free movement. The microdialysis apparatus included a counter-balanced swivel and tether system that minimized any torque or tension on the probe as the rat moved, thereby preserving probe placement stability throughout the experiment. We monitored each animal closely and observed no signs of probe displacement or distress; all probes remained securely in the target region during the entire sampling period. The probe was perfused continuously with oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (125mM NaCl, 2.5mM KCl, 1.18mM MgCl2, 1.26mM CaCl2) at 1.5 μL/min. After a ~2-hour equilibration period, baseline dialysates were collected. Antidepressant or saline injections were administered at time 0, followed by collection of dialysate samples every 20 minutes for up to 5 hours. Each fraction (~30 μL) was collected into 5 μL of 0.1M perchloric acid to prevent monoamine oxidation and either analyzed immediately or stored at -70°C. Serotonin Analysis by HPLC-ECD

Extracellular serotonin concentrations were quantified using HPLC with electrochemical detection (HPLC-ECD). The system included a Pharmacia LKB 2248 pump and a reverse-phase octadecylsilane (ODS) analytical column (Beckman Ultrasphere ODS, 5 μm, 10 ×250 mm). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1M potassium dihydrogen phosphate buffer with 0.1mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid and 1mM sodium octanesulfonate, supplemented with 18% methanol (pH, 3.1). The mobile phase was filtered (0.22 μm) and degassed. Flow rate was 1.2 mL/min. Serotonin was detected with an LC-4C electrochemical detector (Bioanalytical Systems, USA) at +0.7 V versus Ag/AgCl. Peak heights were compared to serotonin standards, and all samples from a single experiment were analyzed in the same run to minimize interrun variability.

Verification of Probe Placement

After microdialysis, probe placement was confirmed histologically. Rats were deeply anesthetized with chloral hydrate (360 mg/kg i.p.) and transcardially perfused with 10% neutral-buffered formalin. Brains were removed, fixed for at least 24 hours, embedded in paraffin, and coronal sections (5 μm) through the probe track were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Only data from animals with probe tips verified in the MPOA were included in the analysis (Fig. 4).

Data and Statistical Analysis

Serotonin concentrations were expressed as mean±standard error of the mean for each time point and also normalized to each animal’s baseline. Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess effects of time and drug×time interactions. One-way ANOVA or t-tests were applied for comparisons at specific time points, with post hoc Newman-Keuls tests to identify significant differences. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. Prior to analysis, data distribution was checked for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) to validate the use of parametric tests. Given the modest sample size, a non-parametric repeated-measures test (Friedman test) was also considered as an alternative approach to confirm the time-course effects if normality assumptions were violated.

RESULTS

Baseline and General Observations

All groups had similar hypothalamic baseline serotonin levels (~0.8–0.9 pmol/mL), with no significant differences prior to drug administration. Antidepressants were generally well tolerated; no acute toxicity or severe behavioral depression was observed, though clomipramine caused transient mild sedation.

Serotonin Elevation after Antidepressants

All antidepressants significantly increased extracellular serotonin in the hypothalamus compared with baseline (paired ttest, P<0.05). In contrast, saline-treated controls showed no appreciable changes over time. The magnitude and time course of serotonin elevation varied among drugs (Fig. 5).

Effect of clomipramine and SSRIs on hypothalamic serotonin levels over time. Serotonin concentrations are expressed as percentage of each group’s basal (predrug) level (mean±standard error of the mean, n=8 per group). Clomipramine and sertraline produced a rapid, pronounced increase in serotonin, peaking at 120 minutes, whereas paroxetine and fluoxetine showed a slower, more modest rise, peaking at 240 minutes. Clomipramine produced the largest increase, peaking at ~472% of baseline at 120 minutes. Sertraline reached a similar peak (~440% of baseline at 120 minutes). Paroxetine and fluoxetine showed more moderate, delayed increases, peaking at ~400% and ~350% of baseline at 240 minutes, respectively. Overall, mean serotonin levels ranked: clomipramine > sertraline > paroxetine > fluoxetine.

Time-Course Patterns

Clomipramine and sertraline caused rapid rises, reaching a plateau by 60–120 minutes, then slightly declining. Paroxetine and fluoxetine exhibited slower, prolonged increases, peaking at 240 minutes. Significant elevations in clomipramine and sertraline groups were evident as early as 40 minutes.

Group Comparisons

Clomipramine and sertraline did not differ significantly at any time point, nor did paroxetine and fluoxetine. However, clomipramine and sertraline produced significantly higher serotonin levels than paroxetine and fluoxetine at 40, 60, and 120 minutes (Newman-Keuls post hoc, P<0.05). For example, at 60 minutes, serotonin levels were 363% and 305% of baseline for clomipramine and sertraline, versus 181% and 115% for paroxetine and fluoxetine (P<0.05). Paroxetine and fluoxetine curves largely overlapped, with minor differences at 60 and 120 minutes.

DISCUSSION

Consistent with their pharmacological profiles, clomipramine and the SSRIs significantly increased extracellular serotonin in the rat hypothalamus. Prior microdialysis studies have similarly shown elevated brain serotonin following administration of these drugs [13, 14, 19-23]. In our study, the magnitude of serotonin elevation followed the order: clomipramine > sertraline > paroxetine > fluoxetine, partially reflecting known drug potencies and some clinical observations.

Sertraline and paroxetine are potent SSRIs, with sertraline reportedly ~14 times more effective than fluoxetine in stimulating serotonergic activity in anesthetized rats [13, 19], and paroxetine 2–6 times more potent than sertraline in inhibiting reuptake [8]. Clomipramine, as a TCA, produced the largest serotonin surge, consistent with its dual inhibition of serotonin and other monoamine reuptake [19-23].

Differences in temporal serotonin profiles likely reflect pharmacokinetic variations. Sertraline shows incomplete absorption and extensive first-pass metabolism, fluoxetine has a long halflife with an active metabolite, and paroxetine is rapidly absorbed with a short half-life and higher anticholinergic activity [18, 19, 24]. In our study, paroxetine and fluoxetine showed similar serotonin trajectories after 1 hour, suggesting that factors like distribution and elimination rates modulated their effects over time. Therefore, even with an identical 10-mg/kg dose, each drug likely resulted in a different actual brain exposure due to these pharmacokinetic disparities. It is noteworthy that we used the same 10-mg/kg dose for all drugs, even though their typical clinical doses differ (e.g. clomipramine ~50 mg, sertraline 100 mg, fluoxetine 40 mg, paroxetine 40 mg daily in humans). The uniform dosing in rats, while controlling for quantity, does not reflect equipotent clinical dosing; this might exaggerate clomipramine’s effect relative to SSRIs. For example, a 10-mg/kg clomipramine dose in rats likely yields a higher relative drug exposure than the same dose of an SSRI, since clomipramine’s typical effective human dose (~50 mg/day) is lower than that of sertraline (~100 mg/day) or fluoxetine (~40 mg/day). Future studies could consider dose-adjusted comparisons.

Clinically, clomipramine and paroxetine are more frequently associated with voiding difficulty than fluoxetine or sertraline [15, 16]. While clomipramine and sertraline produced higher hypothalamic serotonin in our study, this did not perfectly match voiding difficulty incidence. For instance, sertraline induces high serotonin but rarely causes voiding difficulty, suggesting that central serotonin elevation alone is insufficient to explain clinical voiding difficulty risk. Anticholinergic activity is a major contributor: clomipramine has strong anticholinergic effects that impair detrusor contraction, and paroxetine, among SSRIs, has relatively higher anticholinergic activity, aligning with its higher voiding difficulty incidence. Fluoxetine and sertraline have minimal anticholinergic activity, correlating with low voiding difficulty risk despite elevated serotonin [25-29]. It should be noted that our experiment did not measure anticholinergic activity directly; our discussion of anticholinergic contributions to voiding difficulty risk is based on known drug profiles from the literature, not on direct evidence from this study.

Additional neurotransmitter interactions may also play a role. Clomipramine enhances norepinephrine and dopamine signaling, which can further stimulate serotonin release via heteroreceptor mechanisms, possibly explaining its maximal serotonin effect. Elevated norepinephrine additionally increases urethral sphincter tone via α1 receptors, potentially compounding voiding difficulty risk [25-29].

Homeostatic mechanisms, such as autoreceptor-mediated feedback inhibition of serotonergic neurons, may limit excessive serotonin release, indicating that further serotonin elevation does not necessarily produce proportional increases in voiding difficulty. The regulation of micturition involves complex integration of central and peripheral signals, so beyond a certain threshold, additional serotonin may have minimal effect on urinary function [30].

Overall, our results confirm that antidepressants elevate hypothalamic serotonin, a region implicated in voiding reflex modulation. The combination of serotonin elevation and strong anticholinergic activity in drugs like clomipramine and paroxetine likely underlies their higher voiding difficulty risk. In contrast, SSRIs such as sertraline or fluoxetine, with minimal anticholinergic effects, are less likely to induce voiding difficulty despite increased serotonin. Clinically, caution is advised when prescribing highly anticholinergic antidepressants to elderly patients, those with benign prostatic hyperplasia, or individuals receiving other anticholinergic medications. However, these neurochemical findings alone do not establish a causal relationship with voiding difficulty. We did not evaluate bladder function directly in this study, so we cannot confirm that the observed increases in hypothalamic serotonin actually led to voiding difficulty in the rats. Nevertheless, prior research has shown that central serotonergic modulation can influence the lower urinary tract—for example, administering 5-HT receptor agonists or antagonists alters voiding reflexes [31]—supporting the idea that increased brain serotonin could impair micturition.

This rank-order of central serotonin level changes after different antidepressants represents an understanding of how antidepressants may influence voiding difficulties through serotonergic pathways. If it were possible to predict and rank the likelihood of voiding difficulty according to different classes of antidepressants, this could substantially aid clinicians in managing and preventing antidepressant-induced voiding dysfunction as an adverse effect of treatment.

Limitations include acute dosing in healthy rats without direct urodynamic measurements, and examination limited to the hypothalamus. Chronic treatment could induce adaptive changes, and other regions involved in micturition (e.g., pontine micturition center, spinal cord) may show different neurotransmitter dynamics. Moreover, our analysis was confined to central serotonin; we did not investigate other physiological factors that could lead to UR. We did not measure changes in other neurotransmitter systems (e.g., noradrenaline or dopamine) or direct peripheral effects on the bladder, nor did we evaluate any structural or behavioral factors. This narrow focus means that while we have characterized the serotonergic response, other mechanisms potentially contributing to antidepressant-induced UR remain unaddressed in our study. Additionally, we did not directly measure urinary function in these animals. Future research correlating neurotransmitter changes with urodynamic measurements would strengthen the causal link between central serotonin and UR. Furthermore, the sample size in each group (n=8) was relatively small, which may have limited the statistical power of our analyses. We verified that the data distribution was approximately normal and accordingly used repeated-measures ANOVA, but given the sample size, a nonparametric Friedman test could also be applied to validate the time-course differences. We acknowledge the importance of reporting effect sizes and confidence intervals to better quantify the observed differences; future studies with larger sample sizes should include these metrics to complement P-values. Finally, note that our microdialysis measurements provide an indirect index of neural activity. An elevated extracellular serotonin level in the hypothalamus reflects increased neurotransmitter availability (due to reuptake inhibition and/or release), but it does not directly demonstrate heightened firing of serotonergic neurons or overall hyperactivity of that brain region.

Importantly, the present study was not designed to evaluate UR or voiding dysfunction directly. Rather, it focused on characterizing central serotonergic responses to antidepressant administration as a neurochemical endpoint. Accordingly, any reference to UR in this manuscript should be interpreted in the context of existing literature on serotonergic modulation of micturition, not as an outcome demonstrated by the current data. Future studies correlating neurotransmitter changes with urinary function would clarify the causal relationship between central serotonin and antidepressant-induced voiding difficulty.

In conclusion, the relative risk of voiding difficulty across different antidepressant classes may help guide individualized antidepressant selection. The present study demonstrates that clomipramine and commonly used SSRIs differentially elevate extracellular serotonin levels in the rat hypothalamus, with distinct temporal and quantitative profiles. These findings provide neurochemical insight into how antidepressants may influence central pathways involved in lower urinary tract regulation. However, because bladder function was not directly assessed, the present results do not establish a causal relationship between hypothalamic serotonin elevation and UR. Future studies integrating neurochemical measurements with urodynamic and electrophysiological assessments will be necessary to clarify the functional significance of these serotonergic changes.
