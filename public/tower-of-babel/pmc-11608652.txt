Brain regions associated with balance function assessed by the Berg Balance Scale in post-stroke patients
Authors: Aki Hirata, Yuki Uchiyama, Midori Mochizuki, Tetsuo Koyama, Kazuhisa Domen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract [Purpose] To assess the neural fiber damage causing balance deficits in post-stroke patients. [Participants and Methods] Diffusion tensor imaging was conducted during the second week after stroke onset, and the Berg Balance Scale scores were recorded at discharge from our affiliated rehabilitation facility. The total score of the motor component of the Stroke Impairment Assessment Set and the Functional Independence Measure motor score were also documented. Brain images were analyzed using tract-based spatial statistics to extract the mean fractional anisotropy values from representative neural tracts. Stepwise multivariate analyses were conducted using fractional anisotropy values in the lesioned hemispheres as explanatory variables and clinical outcomes as target values. [Results] This study included 65 patients. The analyses identified the following significant associations: the corticospinal tract and the motor component of the Stroke Impairment Assessment Set score; the corticospinal tract, cingulum bundle, and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus and the Berg Balance Scale score; and the superior longitudinal fasciculus and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus and the Functional Independence Measure motor score. [Conclusion] The brain areas associated with balance function include the corticospinal tract and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, implying that the balance function links extremity functions to activities of daily living. This finding highlights the importance of rehabilitative training for balance function in post-stroke patients.Keywords: Magnetic resonance imaging, Prognosis, Recovery

INTRODUCTION

In stroke rehabilitation, balance evaluation is essential because strokes often impair balance and stability, posing challenges in fundamental activities such as standing and walking1, 2). Assessment of balance impairment enables rehabilitation specialists to implement exercises and interventions designed to enhance postural control and reduce the risk of falls3). Furthermore, balance evaluation plays a pivotal role in improving overall functional independence in activities of daily living (ADLs)4). Thus, the assessment of balance is essential for rehabilitation professionals to optimize the recovery trajectory and enhance the overall quality of life of post-stroke patients5).

Among the various tools for assessing balance impairment, the Berg Balance Scale (BBS) is among the most widely used for evaluating balance and functional mobility6). Comprising a series of 14 tasks, the BBS assesses a patient’s ability to maintain balance during various functional activities, including sitting, standing, standing unsupported, reaching, turning, and stepping. Each task is scored on a 5-point scale, with 0 indicating the lowest level of function and 4 indicating the highest. The maximum total score is 56, reflecting the optimal balance performance. The BBS tasks address both static and dynamic aspects of balance and provide a comprehensive overview of an individual’s balance abilities.

Neuroimaging techniques such as computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are used to plan appropriate rehabilitative interventions for patients after stroke7,8,9). Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is an MRI sequence that enables the in vivo assessment of neural fiber degeneration related to clinical symptoms7). Previous studies have investigated the relationship between neural degeneration and outcomes such as hemiparesis and cognitive decline10,11,12). However, very few studies have focused on the relationship between balance impairment and neural damage assessed by DTI13, 14). Accordingly, this study investigated the relationship of neural fiber damage with balance impairment as well as hemiparesis and disability in ADLs.

PARTICIPANTS AND METHODS

This study extends our previous work that retrospectively investigated post-stroke outcome prediction using fractional anisotropy (FA), in which data were collected at Nishinomiya Kyoritsu Neurosurgical Hospital (Nishinomiya, Japan) and data analyses were performed at Hyogo Medical University (Nishinomiya, Japan)15, 16). The present study is a retrospective survey of patients diagnosed with stroke and admitted to Nishinomiya Kyoritsu Neurosurgical Hospital between April 2021 and September 2023. Typically, patients were promptly referred to our hospital after the onset of stroke and underwent conservative management, which included the administration of antihypertensive medications. Surgical treatment was performed when deemed necessary. Additionally, participants received rehabilitative therapy involving physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy for a maximum of 180 min daily, following the 2021 revisions of the Japanese Guidelines for Stroke Management17).

To address potential confounding factors stemming from variations in pre-stroke health status and lesion location, the study cohort was restricted to individuals experiencing their first-ever stroke with supratentorial lesions who demonstrated independent ambulation and functional autonomy in ADLs before the stroke. Individuals with a history of neurological disorders such as Parkinson’s or Alzheimer’s disease were excluded10, 11). Patients exhibiting subsequent deterioration of consciousness or severe comorbid conditions were also excluded. To standardize the rehabilitative interventions, the study used data only from patients who were transferred to our affiliated long-term rehabilitation facility, Nishinomiya Kyoritsu Rehabilitation Hospital10, 11). The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hyogo Medical University (approval no. 4546). Informed consent was obtained through the opt-out method via the hospital website.

The method for DTI acquisition and subsequent image processing has been reported previously15). In summary, DTI scans were performed in the second week following admission to our acute care ward. A 3.0-T scanner (MAGNETOM Trio; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with a 32-channel head coil was used for these scans. Using a single-shot echo-planar imaging sequence in the anterior-to-posterior direction, the acquisition involved 30 images with non-collinear diffusion gradients (b=1,500 s/mm2) and one non-diffusion-weighted image (b=0 s/mm2). A total of 80 contiguous axial slices were acquired from each patient, with a field of view measuring 256 × 256 mm, an acquisition matrix of 128 × 128, and a slice thickness of 2 mm. The imaging parameters included an echo time of 96 ms, a repetition time of 10,900 ms, and a flip angle of 90°. To address distortions caused by eddy currents and echo-planar imaging, two additional non-diffusion-weighted images were acquired in both the anterior-to-posterior and posterior-to-anterior directions.

The image processing workflow for the DTI scans was implemented using the MRtrix3 and FSL software packages18, 19). First, the Gibbs ringing artifact was eliminated, and distortions induced by eddy currents and echo-planar imaging were corrected. Next, bias field corrections were applied, and brain masks were generated from the bias field-corrected images. Then, FA brain images were generated. For further image processing, we applied tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS)20).

TBSS facilitates the assessment of the integrity of neural fibers based on their inherent anisotropic properties. It projects the FA of local tract structures onto a virtual skeleton, providing an alignment-invariant representation of the central aspects of the tracts. We aligned each participant’s FA map to a standard brain template (FMRIB58_FA) within the FSL suite, using a non-linear spatial transformation tool. Subsequently, a composite FA skeleton representing the central parts of all tracts common to the group was created by setting a voxel threshold for FA values. The aligned FA data for each patient were then projected onto the template skeletonized FA image by identifying the highest local FA value perpendicular to each tract and assigning this value to the skeleton. In this study, to enhance the statistical power, the FA images obtained from the left hemisphere lesion groups were flipped for alignment with those of the right hemisphere lesion groups.

To quantitatively assess intracerebral neural fiber degeneration (an FA decrease), skeletonized FA images of each patient were sampled using the region-of-interest (ROI) method. For this study, ROIs were defined within the corticospinal tract (CST), superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), anterior thalamic radiation (ATR), cingulum bundle (CNG), and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), based on the standard white matter brain map implemented in FSL (Fig. 1). The inferior longitudinal fasciculus and uncinate fasciculus defined in the standard brain template were excluded from the ROIs because they largely overlap with the IFOF. The hippocampus was also excluded due to a markedly smaller voxel size compared with the other ROIs. FA values within ROIs were extracted from lesioned and non-lesioned hemispheres.

During the patients’ stay in our long-term rehabilitation facility, the BBS was scored every 2 weeks. Additionally, motor function impairment in the upper and lower extremities on the hemiparetic side was evaluated using the motor component of the Stroke Impairment Assessment Set (SIAS-motor)21). This assessment comprises five components scored on a scale from 0 to 5 and covers arm, finger, hip, knee, and ankle functions. In this study, the total sum of the SIAS-motor scores was calculated for each patient to quantify gross motor function on the paralyzed side, following the method outlined in our previous studies15, 16). Furthermore, the motor component of the Functional Independence Measure (FIM-motor; null to full, 13 to 91), a commonly used instrument for assessing independence in ADLs, was scored22). These scores were also assessed every 2 weeks and data for this study were collected at the time of discharge from our long-term rehabilitation facility. The total length of hospital stay, including acute medical care, was also recorded.

The impact of neural fiber degeneration (an FA decrease) within different ROIs was evaluated in relation to dysfunctions due to stroke. First, simple regression analyses were performed between FA values in the lesioned hemispheres and outcome measurements (SIAS-motor total, BBS, and FIM-motor) for all possible pairs of these values. Then, multivariate regression analyses were performed, with FA values in the CST, SLF, ATR, CNG, and IFOF of the lesioned hemispheres used as explanatory variables, and SIAS-motor total, BBS, and FIM-motor scores set as target values. Parameters for the final regression models were set according to forward stepwise selection based on the Akaike Information Criterion. Furthermore, to outline multiple collinearity relationships among the five explanatory variables, Pearson’s correlation testing was performed for all possible pairs.

To evaluate the relationships between the BBS and other clinical parameters (shown in Table 1), Spearman’s rank correlation test was conducted. Dummy variables were created for the analysis: sex (0 for male, 1 for female), lesioned hemisphere (0 for left, 1 for right), and type of stroke (0 for ischemic, 1 for hemorrhagic). All statistical analyses were performed using the JMP software package (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The characteristics of patients entered into our analytical database are detailed in Table 1. The study involved a cohort of 65 patients, consisting of 44 men and 21 women, or 30 patients with left hemisphere lesions and 35 patients with right hemisphere lesions. The median age was 65 years, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 56.5 to 75 years. The median BBS score was 52, with an IQR of 37.5 to 56. For the SIAS-motor total data, the median was 20, with an IQR of 12.5 to 25. For the FIM-motor data, the median was 85, with an IQR of 69 to 90.

The FA values derived from the ROI analysis are presented in Table 2. Compared with the FA values in the non-lesioned hemispheres, the FA values in the CST and SLF were significantly decreased, whereas the decreases were smaller in the ATR, CNG, and IFOF. This observation indicates that the impact of stroke varied among neural tracts. The results obtained from correlation analyses between FA values in the lesioned hemisphere and outcome measurements are presented in Table 3. Most of the correlation coefficients obtained ranged between 0.3 and 0.6, indicating that an FA decrease in the lesioned hemispheres globally affected clinical outcomes.

DISCUSSION

This study involved 65 samples from 30 patients with left hemisphere lesions and 35 patients with right hemisphere lesions. The median patient age was 65 years. FA values were significantly decreased in the CST and SLF in the lesioned hemispheres, with less evident decreases in the ATR, CNG, and IFOF. Correlation coefficients between FA values and clinical outcomes ranged from 0.3 to 0.6. Multiple regression analysis identified the CST as significant for the SIAS-motor; the CST, CNG, and IFOF as significant for the BBS; and the SLF and IFOF as significant for the FIM-motor. BBS score was strongly correlated with lower extremity functions and the FIM-motor.

From the correlation analyses among clinical manifestations, the BBS showed strong relationships with both the SIAS-motor total and the FIM-motor (Table 6). However, the brain areas associated with these outcome measurements differed. As shown in Table 4, extremity function assessed by the SIAS-motor solely involved the CST, whereas independence in ADLs, as scored by the FIM-motor, was related to the SLF and IFOF. Brain areas associated with the BBS included the CST and IFOF, implying that the balance function assessed by the BBS stands between extremity functions and ADLs. The findings of the present study support the significance of balance function as a link between extremity functions and ADLs. The importance of rehabilitative training for balance function is now supported by clinical findings linked to brain imaging5).

Our samples exhibited significant FA decreases in the CST and SLF but small FA decreases in the ATR, CNG, and IFOF. We sampled stroke patients with supratentorial lesions, including intracerebral hemorrhage in the thalamus or putamen and cerebral infarction in the middle cerebral artery territories. The differences in FA decrease by brain area might be attributable to the nature of the stroke lesions10, 11). However, statistical analysis revealed that lower FA values in the IFOF were associated with lower balance function and reduced levels of independence in ADLs (Tables 3 and 4). In our previous study23), we investigated the impact of age on extremity functions and ADLs in relation to FA decreases in the cerebral peduncles. The results indicated that an FA decrease in the cerebral peduncles is a primary determinant of the outcome of extremity functions, whereas age contributed equally to FA decreases in determining levels of independence in ADLs23). These results suggest that FA decreases in the IFOF may be attributed to aging because FA values generally decrease with age24).

As shown in Table 4, extremity functions were associated with the CST, whereas ADLs were related to the SLF and IFOF12). Both the SLF and IFOF are considered to be involved in cognitive functions such as spatial recognition and executive functions25). For the assessment of ADLs in this study, we used the FIM-motor, which comprises the following 13 items: eating, grooming, bathing, dressing the upper body, dressing the lower body, toileting, bladder management, bowel management, transfer to a bed/chair/wheelchair, transfer to a toilet, transfer to a tub/shower, walking/propelling a wheelchair, and using stairs22). These items do not simply reflect the motor function of extremities alone and also include cognitive functions such as self-body proprioception and executive function (e.g., dressing and transfers). The present findings regarding the relationships between brain regions and the modalities of clinical outcomes are compatible with the functional topography within the brain25).

It is well-established that the left hemisphere is primarily associated with language functions while the right hemisphere is more involved in spatial attention26). Previous studies have suggested a correlation between right hemisphere lesions and balance instability, often linked to hemi-spatial neglect27, 28). However, the results of the present study differed from those of previous reports, with our statistical analysis revealing no significant correlation between BBS severity and lesion laterality (Table 6). To further investigate this matter, we performed direct comparisons of images between groups with left and right hemisphere lesions, incorporating BBS score, age, and type of stroke as covariates12). Despite this thorough analysis, we found no statistically significant differences in voxel-based comparisons. This discrepancy between our findings and earlier reports might stem from differences in the study populations. As shown in Table 1, the median FIM-motor score of our sample was 85, indicating that most participants had achieved functional independence in their ADLs. This suggests that our participants may have had a better overall recovery and better compensatory abilities compared with those in other studies.

This study has several limitations. First, due to technical challenges in analyzing brain images within the brainstem and cerebellar regions20), the study included only patients with supratentorial lesions. However, in clinical practice, patients with balance disorders often have subtentorial lesions. Further studies incorporating patients with lesions in various brain regions are necessary to assess the effectiveness of the method applied in this study. Second, the study population was limited to first-ever stroke patients who were functionally independent before the onset of their stroke10, 11). Consequently, the applicability of the present findings to geriatric patients requiring assistance in ADLs before a stroke remains uncertain. Third, the sample size for the present study was 65 participants. Although this sample is not small in the context of neuroimaging studies related to stroke7, 16), adjustments were made for a more robust analysis of brain images, using covariates derived from various clinical parameters. Future studies with larger sample sizes are desirable to address this issue and enhance the quality of the findings. Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates that brain regions such as the CST, SLF, and IFOF contribute to balance function in patients after a stroke, providing rehabilitation specialists with insights for implementing interventions aimed at optimizing recovery.
