Radon transforms and the SYK model
Michael Stone

Abstract
Motivated by recent work on the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) model, we consider the effect of Radon or X-ray transformations, on the Laplace eigenfunctions in hyperbolic Bolyai-Lobachevsky space. We show that the Radon map from this space to Lorentzian-signature de Sitter space is easier to interpret if we use the Poincare disc model and eigenfunctions rather than the upper-half-plane model. In particular, this version of the transform reveals the geometric origin of the boundary conditions imposed on the eigenfunctions that are involved in calculating the SYK four-point function.



1
Introduction

The Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) model
kitaev-syk
;
sy
;
maldacena
is a one-dimensional quantum-mechanics model with interesting physical properties. It is
solvable at strong coupling, maximally chaotic, and has an emergent conformal symmetry. There are strong hints that it has a AdS/CFT gravity dual. For reviews see
rosenhaus
;
sarosi
.

The present paper is not so much about the physics of this model, but rather is about some mathematics that appears in its analysis. In solving the bi-local integral equation that arises when computing the 4-point correlation function, the authors of
joeP
need to find the eigenfunctions of a Schrödinger operator whose potential is unbounded below. Although this seems an ill-defined problem, they were able to find a complete orthonormal set of suitable eigenfunctions by imposing some boundary condition infinitely deep in the potential abyss. The resulting spectrum has both a continuous set of scattering states and a discrete set of normalizable bound states — both sets of eigenfunctions involving an unusual linear combination Bessel functions.

It turns out that the unbounded-below Schrödinger operator has been previously discussed in the literature
dunster90
;
gitman
;
andrianov
, and it is known that it possesses a one-(or even two-
kobayashi
)-parameter family of boundary conditions each of which lead to a self-adjoint operator and hence a complete set of eigenfunctions. The particular boundary condition selected from this infinite set by the authors of
joeP
was required to ensure compatibility with the
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
symmetry of the integral equation. The analytic necessity is clear, but given that the SYK model is expected to describe some sort of quantum geometry it would be useful to find a geometric principle behind the selection.

A key hint comes from the observation
jevicki
that the peculiar combination of Bessel functions that compose the selected eigenfunctions also arises as the result of the application of a Radon, or X-ray, transformation to the modified Bessel-function eigenfunctions of the Laplacian on the Poincaré upper-half-plane
H_{+}^{2}
.

There is an extensive literature on Radon transforms on hyperbolic spaces
helgason
;
gelfand
;
hyperbolic_radon
but we seldom find in it explicit transforms of individual
L^{2}[H_{+}^{2}]
eigenfunctions.
The authors of
jevicki
evaluate the Radon transform integral by using a Bessel identity from Watson’s Treatise
watson
(§12.11, Eq.4). There are very many Bessel-function identities, but they tend to fall into families that either reflect general properties of hypergeometric functions or have some geometric origin. This one seems rather obscure and not associated with such a family. The purpose of the present paper is explore how this and some other “special function” identities relate to the geometry of the Radon transformation. In particular we will see that evaluating the Radon transformation of the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian in the Poincaré disc model (as opposed to those of the upper-half-plane model) reveals why the particular choice of self-adjoint extension is special.

In order to have the necessary geometry in our mind, in the next section we review the geometry of hyperbolic space and its two standard models: the Poincaré disc and the upper half plane. We then do the same for the Lorentzian-signature analogue AdS/dS. We show how actions of the isometry groups
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
and
{\rm SU}(1,1)
on the space of geodesics leads to their parametrization as cosets, and in section
4
we obtain the normalized eigenfunctions that provide the spectral decompositions on these cosets. In section
5
we use the asymptotic properties of the eigenfunctions to evaluate their Radon transform integrals. In the case of upper-half-plane we simply recover the results of
jevicki
, but for the Poincaré disc the transform leads to some rarely-met special functions and at the same time reveals the origin and necessity of the boundary conditions selected in
joeP
. Two appendices review how the coset metrics arise from
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\simeq{\rm SU}(1,1)
, and derive the orthogonality properties of the rarely-met special functions.

3
Isometries and Geodesics

Just as the positive-curvature two-sphere has as symmetry the rotation group
{\rm SO}(3)
which acts via its double cover
{\rm SU}(2)
, the negative-curvature Bolyai-Lobachevski space has symmetry group
{\rm SO}^{+}(2,1)
which acts via its double cover
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
. The natural way of expressing the action depends on the choice of coordinate system.

3.1
Isometries of
H_{+}^{2}

The Möbius map

z\mapsto z^{\prime}={az+b\over cz+d},\quad g=\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\cr c&d\end{matrix}\right)\in{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}}),

(34)

maps the upper half-plane 1-1 onto itself and preserves the metric

ds^{2}={dzd\bar{z}\over y^{2}}={dx^{2}+dy^{2}\over y^{2}}.

(35)

The Möbius map is insensitive to the overall sign of the
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
matrix, so the group that is acting is really

{\rm PSL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})/\{1,-1\}.

(36)

This group provides a continuous family of orientation preserving isometries of
H^{2}_{+}
. As the isometry group of the Minkowski-embedded hyperboloid is the time-orientation preserving Lorentz group
{\rm SO}^{+}(2,1)
we deduce that there is 2-to-1 mapping between
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
and
{\rm SO}^{+}(2,1)
.

Elements of
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
are classified as
elliptic
if they fix a point in the interior of
H^{2}_{+}
,
parabolic
if they possess a single fixed point on the boundary, and
hyperbolic
if they fix
two
points on the boundary. These conditions are determined by the trace: elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic elements have
{\rm tr}(g)
respectively less than, equal to, or greater than two.

The infinitesimal Möbius maps

\delta z={(1+\delta a)z+\delta b\over\delta c\,z+(1+\delta d)}-z=(\delta b)+(\delta a-\delta d)z-(\delta c)z^{2},\quad\delta a+\delta d=0,

(37)

generate the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
.
This algebra has a matrix representation
kitaev

L_{-1}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&1\cr 0&0\end{matrix}\right],\quad L_{+1}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&0\cr-1&0\end{matrix}\right],\quad L_{0}={1\over 2}\left[\begin{matrix}1&0\cr 0&-1\end{matrix}\right],

(38)

with

[L_{n},L_{m}]=(n-m)L_{n+m}.

(39)

It is convenient to select the sign for the quadratic Casimir so that

C_{2}\equiv-L_{0}^{2}+(L_{-1}L_{1}+L_{-1}L_{1})/2.

(40)

In the matrix representation

C_{2}\to-{3\over 4}{\mathbb{I}}.

(41)

Using the
L_{n}
matrices we can write

\left(\begin{matrix}\delta a&\delta b\cr\delta c&\delta d\end{matrix}\right)=\delta bL_{-1}+(\delta a-\delta d)L_{0}-\delta cL_{+1}.

(42)

The infinitesimal displacements
\delta z=\delta x+i\delta y
define
vector fields
\delta x\,\partial_{x}+\delta y\,\partial_{y}

which are differential operators

\displaystyle L_{-1}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle{\partial\over\partial x}

\displaystyle L_{+1}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle(x^{2}-y^{2}){\partial\over\partial x}+2xy{\partial\over\partial y}

\displaystyle L_{0}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle x{\partial\over\partial x}+y{\partial\over\partial y}\

(43)

that act on functions on
H_{+}^{2}
.
The Casimir operator in the differential operator representation becomes

C_{2}\equiv-L_{0}^{2}+{\textstyle{1\over 2}}(L_{+}L_{-}+L_{-}L_{+})\mapsto-y^{2}\left({\partial^{2}\over\partial x^{2}}+{\partial^{2}\over\partial y^{2}}\right)

(44)

and coincides with (minus) the Laplacian on
H^{2}_{+}
.

With
x=\xi
,
y=1+\eta
with small
(\xi,\eta)
we have

\displaystyle{L_{-1}+L_{+1}\over 2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\eta{\partial\over\partial x}+\xi{\partial\over\partial y}+O(\xi^{2},\eta^{2},\xi\eta)\,

(45)

which describes a rotation about
z=i
. Thus the generator
(L_{-1}+L_{+1})/{2}
exponentiates to a compact
{\rm SO}(2)
elliptic subgroup

of
{\rm SL}(2{\mathbb{R}})
corresponding to the rotation in the two-to-one

morphism.

3.2
Upper-half-plane geodesics

The isometries act transitively on
the upper half-plane geodesics, which are semicircles with centres on the real axis.
Every geodesic lies on the isometry orbit of a reference geodesic which has its centre at
x=0
and with ends on the real axis at
x=\pm 1
.

To see how this works consider:

\bullet

the hyperbolic subgroup
A=\{\exp\sigma(L_{-1}-L_{1})/2\}
leaves the points
x=\pm 1
on the
x
-axis fixed and so preserves the reference geodesic as a set, although increasing
\sigma
flows points along the geodesic from
x=-1
towards
x=+1
.

\bullet

the hyperbolic subgroup
H=\{\exp\xi L_{0}\}
leaves fixed the points
x=0
and

and acts on the reference geodesic to dilate its (Euclidean) radius from unity to
e^{\xi}
.

\bullet

the parabolic subgroupgroup
\tilde{N}=\{\exp tL_{-1}\}
leaves only the point at infinity fixed and rigidly translates the geodesic semicircle along the
x
axis through a distance
t
.

The last two operations generate can generate any upper-half-plane geodesic. Thus the space of geodesics may be identified with the coset
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})/A
parameterized by
t,\xi
.

The orbit of the point
z=i
under the action of

g=\exp\{tL_{-1}\}\exp\{\xi L_{0}\}\exp\{\sigma(L_{-1}-L_{1})/2\}

(46)

is

\displaystyle x(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\xi}\,{\rm tanh\,}\sigma+t

\displaystyle y(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\xi}\,{\rm sech\,}\sigma

(47)

and so
\sigma
parameterizes points on the particular geodesic
\gamma(t,\xi)
, which is the circle with equation

(x(\sigma)-t)^{2}+y(\sigma)^{2}=e^{2\xi}.

(48)

From

(49)

we see that
\sigma
is the hyperbolic arc length.

As a coset, the space of geodesics inherits a metric from the group. In the

parameterization the metric is

ds^{2}=d\xi^{2}-e^{-2\xi}dt^{2}.

(50)

With

this becomes

ds^{2}={-dt^{2}+dy^{2}\over y^{2}}

(51)

which is the AdS

, or dS

, metric in the Poincaré patch.

The Poincaré patch covers only one-half of AdS

. What happened to the other half? Although we were able obtain all the geodesics

by our group acting on the reference geodesic, the flows parameterized by
\sigma
all went from left to right. If we want all elements in the space of
oriented
geodesics we must include flows from right to left. These we get by sending
\sigma\to-\sigma
. This gives us the other half, and shows that AdS

is actually the space of
oriented
geodesics

3.3
Isometries of the Poincaré disc

The Möbius map acting on

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}e^{i(\phi-\theta)/2}\cosh\xi/2&e^{i(\phi+\theta)/2}\sinh\xi/2\cr e^{-i(\phi+\theta)/2}\sinh\xi/2&e^{-i(\phi-\theta)/2}\cosh\xi/2\end{matrix}\right),

the Poincaré disc is the orbit of this group action on its central point
Z=X+iY=0
.
The factor
e^{-\theta\Lambda_{0}}
leaves this point fixed, while

. The further action by
e^{\phi\Lambda_{0}}

g(\phi,\xi,\theta)(Z=0)=Z=e^{i\phi}{\rm tanh\,}(\xi/2).

(63)

Poincaré disc geodesics

The geodesics on the Poincaré disc are circles that intersect the boundary at right angles. An example is the vertical straight line from
Z=-i
to
Z=+i
. We can parameterize this curve by
(X(\sigma),Y(\sigma))=(0,{\rm tanh\,}\sigma/2)
where
\sigma
is the hyperbolic arc-length. It is mapped into itself by the isometries generated by
{\rm exp}\{\tau\Lambda_{2}\}
, but is carried into other geodesics by
{\rm exp}\{\xi\Lambda_{1}\}
. This operation provides a family of arc-length parametrized geodesic curves

X(\sigma)={\sinh\xi\cosh\sigma\over 1+\cosh\xi\cosh\sigma},\quad Y(\sigma)={\sinh\sigma\over 1+\cosh\xi\cosh\sigma},

(64)

These curves are circles with equation

(X(\sigma)-{\rm coth\,}\xi)^{2}+Y(\sigma)^{2}={\rm csch\,}^{2}\xi.

(65)

For positive
\xi
the circles intersect the boundary at points with polar angles

\tan\alpha=\left.{Y(\sigma)\over X(\sigma)}\right|_{\sigma\to\infty}=\pm{1\over\sinh\xi}.

(66)

We also find that

X(\sigma)^{2}+Y(\sigma)^{2}=1-{2\over 1+\cosh\xi\cosh\sigma}.

(67)

As the Euclidean distance is related to the hyperbolic polar radius by

X^{2}+Y^{2}\equiv t^{2}={\rm tanh}^{2}(\rho(\sigma)/2)

(68)

we have

\cosh\rho(\sigma)={1+t^{2}\over 1-t^{2}}=\cosh{\xi}\cosh\sigma.

(69)

The polar-parametric form of the geodesic is therefore

\displaystyle\rho(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm arccosh}(\cosh{\xi}\cosh\sigma),

\displaystyle\phi(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm arctan}({\rm csch\,}{\xi}\,{\rm tanh\,}\sigma).

(70)

We can rotate this family by an angle
\theta
, so the general set of geodesics in the Poincaré disc constitute the coset

{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})/{\rm exp}\{\tau\Lambda_{2}\}.

If we parameterize

(71)

we find the group metric to be

ds^{2}=2{\rm tr}\{(g^{-1}dg)^{2}\}=d\tau^{2}+d\xi^{2}-d\theta^{2}-2\sinh\xi d\tau d\theta

(72)

The metric on the coset which forgets
\tau

at fixed

(73)

which is a global AdS

twice

4
Spectral decompositions

4.1
Hyperbolic Laplace eigenfunctions

The curved-space scalar Laplacian is

\nabla^{2}={1\over\sqrt{g}}{\partial\over\partial x^{\mu}}\sqrt{g}g^{\mu\nu}{\partial\over\partial x^{\nu}}.

(75)

In Poincaré-disc coordinates this becomes

\nabla^{2}={1\over 4}(1-X^{2}-Y^{2})^{2}\left({\partial^{2}\over\partial X^{2}}+{\partial^{2}\over\partial Y^{2}}\right).

(76)

In the upper-half-plane coordinates
it is

\nabla^{2}=y^{2}\left({\partial^{2}\over\partial x^{2}}+{\partial^{2}\over\partial y^{2}}\right),

(77)

and in hyperbolic polar coordinates we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over\sinh\rho}{\partial\over\partial\rho}\sinh\rho{\partial\over\partial\rho}+{1\over\sinh^{2}\!\rho}{\partial^{2}\over\partial\phi^{2}}

(78)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial\over\partial\cosh\rho}\sinh^{2}\!\rho{\partial\over\partial\cosh\rho}+{1\over\sinh^{2}\!\rho}{\partial^{2}\over\partial\phi^{2}}.

Formally
-\nabla^{2}
is a self-adjoint positive operator. As Lobachevsky space is non-compact, it will possess a complete set of eigenfunctions with a continuous spectrum.

By analogy with the spherical harmonics we anticipate that the group
{\rm PSL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\simeq{\rm SO}^{+}(2,1)
with
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\simeq{\rm SU}(1,1)\simeq{\rm Sp}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
will act on the set of functions with a given value of the Laplace eigenvalue. This is so, and each infinitely-degenerate energy level furnishes an irreducible group representation that is one of the integer spin cases of Bargmann’s
Principal continuous series
of representation of the non-compact Lie group
bargmann
.

4.1.1
The upper half-plane

To find the Laplacian eigenvalues and eigenfunctions we begin with the upper-half-plane coordinate system.
Here
-\nabla^{2}
is formally self-adjoint with respect to the
L^{2}[H_{+}^{2}]
inner-product

{\langle\phi,\chi\rangle}=\int_{H_{+}^{2}}\phi^{*}(x,y)\chi(x,y)\sqrt{g}dxdy=\int_{y>0}\phi^{*}(x,y)\chi(x,y){dxdy\over y^{2}}

(79)

The Laplace operator has
x
-translation invariance so it is natural to seek eigenfunctions of the form

\chi(x,y)=e^{ikx}f(y)
.

Consider first the case
k=0
.
The eigenvalue equation
-\nabla^{2}\varphi=\lambda\varphi
becomes

-y^{2}{d^{2}\over dy^{2}}\chi=\lambda\chi

(80)

and has a regular singular point at
y=0
. We immediately see that
y^{s}
is an eigenfunction with
\lambda=-s(s-1)
, but what values of
s
are suitable?

If we take
s={1\over 2}+i\kappa
,
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
, a complete set of
k=0
eigenfunctions is

\chi_{\kappa}(y)=y^{1/2+i\kappa}

(81)

with

\lambda=(\kappa^{2}+{\textstyle{1\over 4}}).

The orthogonality condition is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\chi^{*}_{\kappa}(y)\chi_{\kappa^{\prime}}(y){dy\over y^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{1/2-i\kappa}y^{1/2+i\kappa^{\prime}}{dy\over y^{2}}

(82)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{i(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime})}{dy\over y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime})\xi}d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\delta(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime}),

and the
corresponding completeness relation is

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{d\kappa\over 2\pi}\chi_{\kappa}(y)\chi^{*}_{\kappa}(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{yy^{\prime}}\delta(\ln y-\ln y^{\prime}),

(83)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle yy^{\prime}\delta(y-y^{\prime}).

A substitution
y=e^{\xi}
,
y^{\prime}=e^{\xi^{\prime}}
shows that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{yy^{\prime}}\delta(\ln y-\ln y^{\prime})f(y^{\prime}){dy^{\prime}\over y^{\prime 2}}=f(y),

(84)

so the RHS of (
83
) is indeed the appropriate hyperbolic Dirac delta-function.

The case with
k\neq 0
is more complicated.
The eigenvalue equation becomes

-{d^{2}\chi\over dy^{2}}+\left(k^{2}-{\lambda\over y^{2}}\right)\chi=0,

(85)

which, with
\lambda=-(\nu^{2}-{\textstyle{1\over 4}})
, would have solutions
\sqrt{y}J_{\pm\nu}(ky)
. However
-\nabla^{2}
is a positive operator so the eigenvalue
\lambda
should be positive. With
\lambda>0
the solutions will oscillate for small
y
but either decay or grow rapidly once
yk>\sqrt{\lambda}
. The normalizable eigenfunctions will be those solutions that decay.

There is a powerful strategy that exploits the
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
symmetry to find an expression for the general-
k
eigenfunctions as an integral. We illustrate it in detail here because we will need to refer to it later in several places.

We
use
y^{s}=({\rm Im}(z))^{s}
being an eigenfunction of
-\nabla^{2}
with eigenvalue
-s(s-1)
together with the fact that the Laplacian commutes with the map
z\to-1/z
to see that

\left({\rm Im}\left(-{1\over z}\right)\right)^{s}={y^{s}\over(x^{2}+y^{2})^{s}}

(86)

remains an eigenfunction of
-\nabla^{2}
with eigenvalue
-s(1-s)
. This eigenvalue equals
\kappa^{2}+{\textstyle{1\over 4}}
when
s={\textstyle{1\over 2}}+i\kappa
. Then, because
-\nabla^{2}
commutes with
z\to z+u
,
we can form linear combinations and construct eigenfunctions

\displaystyle\chi_{k,\kappa}(x,y)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left({\rm Im}\left({1\over u-z}\right)^{\!\!s}\right)e^{iku}du,\quad s=i\kappa+{\textstyle{1\over 2}},

(87)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{y^{s}\over((x-u)^{2}+y^{2})^{s}}e^{iku}du,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{ikx}y^{s}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{1\over(u^{2}+y^{2})^{s}}e^{iku}du,\quad y>0,

with the property
\chi_{k,\kappa}(x+a,y)=e^{ika}\chi_{k,\kappa}(x,y)
.
With
s=\nu+\textstyle{{1\over 2}}
this expression is proportional to
e^{ikx}\sqrt{y}K_{\nu}(|k|y)
where
K_{\nu}(y)
is Basset’s expression

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(\nu+{\textstyle{1\over 2}}){(2y)^{\nu}\over 2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{e^{iu}\over(y^{2}+u^{2})^{\nu+1/2}}du,\quad{\rm Re}(\nu)>-1/2,\quad|{\rm Arg}(y)|<\pi/2

(88)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(\nu+{\textstyle{1\over 2}}){(2y^{-1})^{\nu}\over 2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{e^{iyu}\over(1+u^{2})^{\nu+1/2}}du

for the MacDonald function —
i.e.
the modified Bessel function.

The more familiar definition

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-{y\over 2}\left(u+{1\over u}\right)\right\}u^{\pm\nu-1}du,

(89)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}(y/2)^{-\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-s-{y^{2}\over 4s}\right)s^{\nu-1}{ds}

can be recovered from Basset’s by writing

\Gamma(\nu+{\textstyle{1\over 2}}){1\over(1+u^{2})^{\nu+\textstyle{{1\over 2}}}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{\nu+\textstyle{{1\over 2}}}e^{-s(1+u^{2})}{ds\over s}

(90)

and evaluating the resulting Gaussian integral over
u

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}{ds\over s}s^{\nu+\textstyle{{1\over 2}}}e^{iyu}e^{-s(1+u^{2})}\right)du

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sqrt{\pi}\over 2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{ds}s^{\nu-1}\exp\left\{-s-{y^{2}\over 4s}\right\}.

With purely imaginary index
\nu=i\kappa
,

\left(-{d^{2}\chi\over dy^{2}}+k^{2}-{\kappa^{2}+1/4\over y^{2}}\right)\sqrt{y}K_{i\kappa}(|k|y)=0,

(92)

so the eigenvalue is again
\lambda=({\textstyle{1\over 4}+\kappa^{2}})
. The functions

\displaystyle\chi_{k,\kappa}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{ikx}\sqrt{y}K_{i\kappa}(|k|y),\quad-\infty<k<\infty,\quad\nu>0

(93)

constitute a complete set of eigenfuctions for
-\nabla^{2}
.

As

{\rm K}_{i\kappa}(y)={1\over 2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-{y\over 2}\left(t+{1\over t}\right)\right\}t^{i\kappa-1}dt={1\over 2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-y\cosh\xi\right\}e^{i\kappa\xi}d\xi.

(94)

these functions are real-valued when
0<y<\infty
, and obey
{\rm K}_{i\kappa}(y)={\rm K}_{-i\kappa}(y)
. They decay exponentially for
y>\kappa
and oscillate infinitely many times between
y=\kappa
, and
y=0
.

A plot of

.

For large positive
x
the leading behaviour is

K_{\nu}(x)\sim\sqrt{{\pi\over 2x}}e^{-x}

(95)

independently of
\nu
.
For small positive
x

\displaystyle{\rm K}_{i\kappa}(x)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle{i\pi\over 2\sinh\pi\kappa}\left\{{(x/2)^{i\kappa}\over\Gamma(1+i\kappa)}-{(x/2)^{-i\kappa}\over\Gamma(1-i\kappa)}\right\}

(96)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\sqrt{\pi\over\kappa\sinh\pi\kappa}\left\{e^{i\alpha(\nu)}(x/2)^{i\kappa}-e^{-i\alpha(\kappa)}(x/2)^{-i\kappa}\right\}

for some real
\alpha(\kappa)
. We have used, at the last step,

|\Gamma(1+i\kappa)|^{2}=\Gamma(1+i\kappa)\Gamma(1-i\kappa)={\pi\kappa\over\sinh\pi\kappa}.

(97)

The small-
x
formula (
96
) together with

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|^{i\kappa}|x^{\prime}|^{-i\kappa}d\kappa=2\pi\delta(\ln|x|-\ln|x^{\prime}|)=2\pi x\delta(x-x^{\prime}),\quad x,x^{\prime}>0,

(98)

is sufficient to show that these functions satisfy a completeness condition

{1\over\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}2\kappa\sinh\kappa\pi\,{\rm K}_{i\kappa}(x){\rm K}_{i\kappa}(x^{\prime})\,d\kappa=x\delta(x-x^{\prime}).

(99)

The small-
x
formula may only appear give a completeness relation valid for small
x
, but it reveals that the
x
-independent normalization factor is
(2\kappa/\pi^{2})\sinh\kappa\pi
. As there are no bound states to be added to the completeness relation, the condition must hold for all
x
.

The completeness relation implies
the orthogonality property

{1\over\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{dx\over x}{\rm K}_{i\mu}(|k|x){\rm K}_{i\nu}(|k|x)={\delta(\mu-\nu)\over 2\nu\sinh\nu\pi}.

(100)

We therefore find that the normalized eigenfunctions

\chi_{k,\kappa}(x,y)={1\over\pi}\sqrt{2\kappa\sinh\pi\kappa}\,e^{ikx}\sqrt{y}K_{i\kappa}(|k|y)

(101)

satisfy both

-\nabla^{2}\chi_{k,\kappa}(x,y)=\left(\kappa^{2}+\textstyle{{1\over 4}}\right)\chi_{k\kappa}(x,y),

(102)

and the the orthogonality condition

\displaystyle{\langle\chi_{k,\kappa},\chi_{k^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\int_{0}^{\infty}{dy\over y^{2}}\chi^{*}_{k,\kappa}(x,y)\chi_{k^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}}(x,y)

(103)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\delta(k-k^{\prime})\delta(\kappa-\kappa^{\prime}).\

The orthogonality properties of
K_{i\nu}
give us the
Kontorovich-Lebedev
transform pair

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\nu)\equiv K[f](\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm K}_{i\nu}(x)f(x)\,dx,

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over\pi^{2}x}\int_{0}^{\infty}2\nu\sinh\nu\pi\,{\rm K}_{i\nu}(x)\tilde{f}(\nu)\,d\nu.

(104)

Hyperbolic polar coordinates

Here the Laplacian is

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over\sinh\rho}{\partial\over\partial\rho}\sinh\rho{\partial\varphi\over\partial\rho}+{1\over\sinh^{2}\!\rho}{\partial^{2}\varphi\over\partial\phi^{2}},

(105)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial\over\partial\cosh\rho}\sinh^{2}\!\rho{\partial\varphi\over\partial\cosh\rho}+{1\over\sinh^{2}\!\rho}{\partial^{2}\varphi\over\partial\phi^{2}},

and
-\nabla^{2}
is self-adjoint with respect to the inner product

{\langle\phi,\chi\rangle}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{*}(\rho,\phi)\chi(\rho,\phi)\sinh\rho\,d\rho d\phi

(106)

Separating variables as

\varphi(\rho,\phi)=\chi(\cosh\rho)e^{im\phi},\quad m\in{\mathbb{Z}},

(107)

and setting
x=\cosh\rho
makes the eigenvalue equation
-\nabla^{2}\varphi=(\kappa^{2}+\textstyle{{1\over 4}})\varphi
into a version

-{d\chi\over dx}(x^{2}-1){d\chi\over dx}+{m^{2}\over x^{2}-1}\chi=(\kappa^{2}+\textstyle{{1\over 4}})\chi

(108)

of the associated Legendre equation, but for the less usual range
x\geq 1
.
The
m=0
solutions that are finite at
x=\cosh\rho=1
are the
Conical functions

\varphi_{\kappa}(x)\equiv P_{i\kappa-\textstyle{{1\over 2}}}(x)
. They are the solutions to the differential equation

(109)

in the interval
[1,\infty]
that obey the boundary condition
\varphi_{\kappa}(1)=1
. The

are real-valued when

is real and positive,

and the obey the orthogonality condition

\int_{1}^{\infty}\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\varphi_{\mu}(x)\,dx={1\over\lambda{\rm tanh\,}(\pi\lambda)}\delta(\lambda-\mu),\quad\lambda,\mu>0,

(110)

The set
\{\varphi_{\kappa}:\,\,0\leq\kappa<\infty\}
is complete in
L^{2}[1,\infty]
, and the invertible
Mehler-Fock transform

\tilde{f}(\lambda)
of a function

is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\lambda\,{\rm tanh\,}\pi\lambda\int_{1}^{\infty}\varphi_{\lambda}(x)f(x)\,dx,

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{\lambda}(x)\tilde{f}(\lambda)d\lambda.

(111)

4.2
Liouville Potential

As we have seen in section
4.1.1
, the Liouville-potential Schrödinger equation

\left(-{d^{2}\over d\xi^{2}}+m^{2}e^{2\xi}\right)\psi=E\psi(\xi)

(120)

possesses a purely continuous spectrum with
\delta
-normalized eigenfunctions

\psi_{\nu}(\xi)=\left({2\nu\sinh\nu\pi\over\pi^{2}}\right)^{1/2}K_{i\nu}(|m|e^{\xi}),\quad E=\nu^{2},

(121)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{\mu}(\xi)\psi_{\nu}(\xi)\,d\xi=\delta(\nu-\mu).

(122)

Changing the sign
m^{2}\to-k^{2}
leads to the Schrödinger operator

H=-{d^{2}\over d\xi^{2}}-k^{2}e^{2\xi},

(123)

which appears in
joeP
, and
in which the potential is unbounded below.
Comparing with Bessel’s equation

\left(x^{2}{d^{2}\over dx^{2}}+x{d\over dx}+x^{2}-\nu^{2}\right)\psi=0

(124)

and writing
x=e^{\xi}
,
E=-\nu^{2}
shows us that
the differential equation
H\psi=E\psi
has solutions

\psi_{\nu}(\xi)=J_{\pm\nu}(|k|e^{\xi}),\quad E=-\nu^{2}.

(125)

The problem is to select from these solutions a complete orthonormal set.

Lagrange’s identity for the Liouville problem

\int_{a}^{b}(\psi^{*}H\chi-(H\psi)^{*}\chi)d\xi=[\psi^{*}\partial_{\xi}\chi-(\partial_{\xi}\psi)^{*}\chi]_{a}^{b},\quad a,b\in{\mathbb{R}},

(126)

translates to the Bessel integral

(\beta^{2}-\alpha^{2})\int_{a}^{b}{dx\over x}J_{\alpha}(|\mu|x)J_{\beta}(|k|x)=\left[x\left(J_{\alpha}(|k|x)\partial_{x}J_{\beta}(|k|x)-(\partial_{x}J_{\alpha}(|\mu|x))J_{\beta}(|\mu|x)\right)\right]_{a}^{b},\quad a,b>0.

(127)

Near zero, and for any value of
\alpha
except negative integers,

J_{\alpha}(x)\sim{1\over\Gamma(\alpha+1)}x^{\alpha}={1\over\Gamma(\alpha+1)}e^{\alpha\xi}.

(128)

For large
|x|
,
|{\rm Arg}(x)|<\pi
we have

J_{\alpha}(x)\sim\sqrt{{2\over\pi x}}\cos\left(x-\alpha\pi/2-\pi/4\right).

(129)

We see that for

5
Radon Transforms

A Radon or X-ray transformation is map from functions
f(z)
on some Riemann manifold
M
to the space
\Gamma(m)
of geodesic curves on
M
. The map is performed by integrating the function over the curve

f(z)\mapsto[{\mathcal{R}}f](\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}f(z(\sigma))d\sigma,

(150)

where
\gamma(\sigma)
is a geodesic parametrized by its arc length.

\Gamma(M)
is an example
integral-geometry
of a
“kinematic space,” which has a number of applications to AdS/CFT duality
hyperbolic_radon_AdS
;
Ma1
;
Ma2

As we have seen in section
2
the space of geodesics on Bolyai-Lobachevski space can be regarded as a coset of
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
by a hyperbolic subgroup. As such, it inherits a metric from the group, and from the group-Casimir a Laplacian. This Laplacian is actually a wave-operator
\nabla^{2}_{dS}
on dS
2
. The key fact is that
{\mathcal{R}}
intertwines the Laplacian
\nabla^{2}_{H}
on the Euclidean signature hyperbolic space with that on the Lorentz signature (de-Sitter) kinematic space. In other words

-\nabla^{2}_{dS}({\mathcal{R}}f)={\mathcal{R}}(-\nabla^{2}_{H}f).

(151)

An eigenfunction of

is therefore transformed into an eigenfunction of

with the same eigenvalue. The intertwining property comes about because both the wave operator

and the Laplacian

are the same differential operator — the
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
group Casimir — but acting on functions on the group that are constant along the fibres of the different equivalence classes that constitute the cosets. The Radon transform takes sums of eigenfunctions that are constant on

equivalence classes. This is a special case of the strategy that converted

The large-

This single expression (

Taking into account the normalization of the Legendre functions from appendix

5.3
Bound states

The Schrödinger equations that arise for both the upper-half-plane and the hyperbolic polar coordinate transform have bound-state solutions as well as scattering-state solutions.

For the polar coordinates we can remove the first order derivative in the Legendre equation

\left({\partial\over\partial\sinh\xi}(1+\sinh^{2}\xi){\partial\over\partial\sinh\xi}+{k^{2}\over 1+\sinh^{2}\xi}\right)\psi(\xi)=-(\nu^{2}+1/4)\psi(\xi)

(183)

by writing
\psi(\xi)=(\cosh\xi)^{-1/2}W(\xi)
so
W
obeys

\left(-{d^{2}W\over d\xi^{2}}+{1/4-k^{2}\over\cosh^{2}\xi}\right)W(\xi)=\nu^{2}W(\xi).

(184)

This Pöschl–Teller equation has scattering solutions
W(\xi)=(\cosh\xi)^{1/2}P^{k}_{i\nu-{1\over 2}}(\pm i\sinh\xi)
, or equivalently
(\cosh\xi)^{1/2}Q^{k}_{i\nu-1/2}(\pm i\sinh\xi)
.

When
k\geq 1
the potential becomes attractive and we have bound as well as scattering states. The standard Pöschl–Teller operator is

H=-{d^{2}\over d\xi^{2}}-{\lambda(\lambda+1)\over\cosh^{2}\xi}

(185)

so
\lambda=k-{1\over 2}
. From the theory of Pöschl–Teller equations we know (for example
stone
eq. 19.62) that
the bound-state eigenvalues are
-\nu_{n}^{2}
,where
\nu_{n}
is given by the
\nu>0
zeros of

{\Gamma(\nu+1)\Gamma(\nu)\over\Gamma(\nu+\lambda+1)\Gamma(\nu-\lambda)}.

(186)

For
\lambda=k-{1\over 2}
this leads to the finite set

\nu_{n}=1/2,3/2,5/2,\ldots,(2k-1)/2
.

This is an apparently different spectrum than that found for the upper-half plane, which, with the Radon-transform selected self-adjoint extension, has only half as many bound states:
\nu_{n}=3/2,7/2,\ldots
. However the Poincaré-patch coordinate
system in the kinematic space of the upper-half plane covers only
half
of AdS
2
, and the self-adjoint extension in the corresponding eigenvalue problem imposes boundary condition at infinity — which corresponds to
\xi=0
in the present coordinates.
The Pöschl–Teller equation on the other hand, sees the whole of AdS
2
, and hence the denser spectrum. But — just as in the half-plane case — we should select a subset of the spectrum
that has even or odd-parity depending on whether
k
is even or odd. The lowest energy Pöschl–Teller bound state
\nu_{k}=(2k-1)^{2}/4
has even parity, the next odd, and so on. As a consequence, the parity selection eliminates half of the Pöschl–Teller bound states and leaves exactly the same bound-state spectrum,
\nu_{n}=3/2,7/2,\ldots
, as that found in upper-half plane.

In either realization of hyperbolic space, the Radon transform takes the complete set of Euclidean-signature Laplace eigenfunctions to only the scattering-state eigenfunctions of the dS
2
wave operator. While the bound states can be obtained as the images non-normalizable modes such as
e^{ikx}I_{\nu_{n}}(|k|y)
(
jevicki
Eqn. 3.18), these are not part of the hyperbolic space mode expansion. The map

{\mathcal{R}}:L^{2}[H^{2}_{+}]\to L^{2}[dS_{2}]

(187)

is therefore not
surjective.

It is worth noting that the analytic continuation of the continuous-spectrum singular value expression

\Lambda(\nu)={1\over\sqrt{2\pi}}\Gamma\left({\textstyle{1\over 4}+{i\nu\over 2}}\right)\Gamma\left({\textstyle{1\over 4}-{i\nu\over 2}}\right)\sqrt{\cosh\pi\nu}

(188)

has zeros at the the bound-state eigenvalues

i\nu\to\nu=3/2,7/2
etc. Taken literally, when applied to a bound-state function, the inverse transform would lead to an infinite multiple of the normalized inverse-image mode. This is reasonable as the
e^{ikx}I_{\nu_{n}}(|k|y)
are not even
\delta
-normalizable.

6
Conclusions

The manner in which in the Poincaré-disc Radon transform alternates between two different families,
E^{k}_{\nu}(\xi)
and
O^{k}_{\nu}(\xi)
, of special functions depending on whether
k
is odd or even may seem strange, but in fact it explains exactly what is special about the
\theta=3\pi/4
parameter choice in
joeP
. The Radon transform does not care about the orientation of the geodesics when we perform the integrals. The resultant function must therefore be indifferent to which normal to the plane we use in figure
6
. In other words, the transformed function must take the same values at antipodal points in dS
2
. When this transformed function is of the form
e^{ik\theta}f(\xi)
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, its values at the pair of antipodal points
(\theta,\xi)
and
(\theta+\pi,-\xi)
will be different unless either
k
is even and
f(\xi)=f(-\xi)
or
k
is odd and
f(\xi)=-f(-\xi)
. The target space of the Radon transform is therefore
{\rm dS}_{2}/{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
where
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
is the antipodal map. This is not obvious in the spectral decomposition in the upper-half-plane coordinate system, nor in the
t_{1}
-
t_{2}
dS
2
coordinate system in the integral equation in
joeP
. The antipode-identified space remains an
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
homogeneous space, which is why it allows the authors of
joeP
the use of the
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
group action to obtain the general eigenfunction from a simple one — the same strategy we have used in (
87
) and (
118
).

The antipodal identification arises also in the non-local mapping of general-dimensional Euclidean de Sitter space to Lorentzian-signature de Sitter, as is discussed extensively in
antipode
. It would be interesting to relate those ideas the space-time duality aspects of SYK.
