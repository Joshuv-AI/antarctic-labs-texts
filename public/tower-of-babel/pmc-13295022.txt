Engineering Three-Chamber Core–Sheath Cellulose Acetate Nanofibers via Multifluid Electrospinning for Enhanced Wettability and Tunable Initial Drug Release Behavior
Authors: Yubo Liu, Xiaohong Chen, Jiale Pan, Boyu Si, Xubao He, Guangjian Lu, Ping Liu, Xiangde Lin, Hui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional materials are often constrained by their structural and functional limitations. To address this challenge, multifluid electrospinning has emerged as an advanced nanofabrication technique capable of producing complex structures with enhanced performance. Inspired by rocket stage separation, we utilized cellulose acetate, a biomacromolecule derivative, as the polymer matrix to fabricate complex three-chamber core–sheath nanofibers with TEM-verified structures and enhanced performance. The surface wettability of the fibers was effectively tuned by adjusting the polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) concentration in the outermost layer, where a higher PVP content enabled a rapid transition from hydrophobic to hydrophilic states. Furthermore, the strategic colocalization of PVP and drug in the sheath layer yielded a biphasic release profile, characterized by an initial burst followed by a sustained release phase, which successfully eliminated the tailing effect in the late stage. Increasing the drug loading in the outer layer from 1% to 3% significantly enhanced the initial release amount from 65.15% ± 11.19% to 79.26% ± 0.42%. This work establishes a robust material design strategy based on structural and componential control, offering new insights into the development of high-performance nanofibrous systems for tailored drug delivery and functional applications.

Introduction

The staged separation of rocket components inspires the emergence of innovative concepts in the field of material structure design. − Traditional materials typically feature a singular structure and a solitary function, which struggle to satisfy the increasing and complex demands of various applications. Fortunately, the multilayer structure and functional synergy during the rocket staging separation process offer us a hierarchical and modular material design concept. − Structural complexity often directly correlates with functional diversity. − By precisely regulating and ingeniously designing the structures, it becomes feasible to optimize and enhance the properties of materials. An in-depth comprehension and effective utilization of the complexity inherent in material structures play a crucial guiding role in the development of high-performance materials.

Electrospinning and electrospraying, as popular electrohydrodynamic atomization methods, are continuously providing new tools for converting a wide variety of polymers and active ingredients for various functional applications. They have evolved from the single-fluid blending process, to coaxial, to side-by-side, and to triaxial, trifluid side-by-side, other complex processes and large-scale production. Although these processes and structures are able to support abundant process-structure-performance relationships for developing novel functional materials, the strategies based on components and compositions manipulation on these multichamber configurations are still very limited except the bichamber core–sheath nanofibers. ,

With the rapid development of industry, modern technological innovations have made significant progress, making it possible to conduct ingenious design of micronano structures based on structure–property relationships. , Multifluid electrospinning technique is an advanced method for fabricating complex structures. Owing to its low material costs and high fabrication efficiency, this technique offers significant advantages in the production of functional multistructured nanofibrous materials. , Multifluid electrospinning, distinct from conventional single-fluid electrospinning, is designed to integrate multiple working fluids. This feature theoretically allows for the creation of a broader range of novel structures, as shown in Figure A. However, the increased working fluids introduces heightened complexity into the dynamical electrospinning process. Specifically, there exists significant ambiguity regarding whether solutions of varying physicochemical properties will exhibit adverse interactions during their convergence at the spinneret, or whether the resultant composite Taylor cones can sustain stable jet formation under uniform electric field conditions. As depicted in Figure B, the statistical results from the Web of Science reveal that electrospinning-related publications have been exhibiting an upward trajectory annually. However, there is a significant disparity in the publication output among coaxial, triaxial, and multifluid electrospinning techniques, particularly those capable of fabricating nanofibers with hierarchical structural features. Among these, pressurized spinning has emerged as an effective process for constructing core–sheath nanofibers, offering a sustainable and efficient solution for the controlled and sustained release of active ingredients in applications such as wound care. − These advances lay an important foundation for the precise fabrication and release regulation of multichamber core–sheath fibers. Nonetheless, research outputs remain notably scarce for multifluid electrospinning, especially studies involving three or more working fluids. The primary contributing factor to this discrepancy lies in the inherently more intricate nature of coaxial and higher-order electrospinning technologies, which increase the complexity of nanofiber fabrication. Specifically, the electrospinning process exhibits reduced stability compared to traditional single-fluid systems due to the multifluid dynamics interactions, often resulting in defects such as bead formation and nonuniform fiber diameters. Additionally, the productivity of these advanced configurations remains relatively low, thereby constraining their large-scale adoption and further research exploration.

Driven by advances in fundamental disciplines including nanoscience and electrohydrodynamic, electrospinning technology has undergone a transformative evolution in its processing methodology, progressively evolving toward precision nanofabrication. This capability has enabled the development of functional nanofibrous systems for diverse high-impact applications. , Low-concentration nonspinnable sheath solutions can significantly enhance the production efficiency of nanofibers during electrospinning, primarily owing to their critical buffering function in the electrospinning process. Building on this insight, an advanced multifluid electrospinning technology has been developed, enabling precise control over the structural characteristics of the resulting nanofibers. By extending the solvent evaporation window of composite solutions, these fluids improve the stability of the liquid-to-solid fiber transition. This discovery not only enhances the structural tunability of nanofibers but also explores novel pathways for the design of complex hierarchical nanostructure. Notably, this technology enables nanoscale precision control over sheath thickness in core–shell structure. These advancements lay a solid technical foundation for the diversified development of nanofiber materials in emerging fields such as biomedical engineering and energy storage systems.

The rapid advancement of nanoscience has given rise to a variety of nanostructures. Precise manipulation of structures at the nanoscale can enhance the functionality of materials. Recently, researchers were increasingly to devote efforts on the design of nanostructures, such as Janus structure, core–shell structure, and three-chamber core–sheath structure. The Janus nanostructure represents a typical asymmetric configuration, whose core advantage lies in its spatially precise asymmetric design. , This structure creates two sides with distinctly different physical or chemical properties along the axial scale of a single fiber, thereby moving beyond simple material mixing. In contrast, the core–sheath structure, as a classic binary nanostructure, achieves not only functional integration of material components through precise design but also enhances interfacial adhesion between components, effectively improving overall mechanical performance. Further extending this design concept, the three-chamber core–sheath structure expands the layered architecture into three independent and orderly arranged functional compartments. Its structural advantages are mainly reflected in three aspects. First, different functional components can be triggered in a preset sequence or spatial location. Second, the intermediate layer acts as both a buffer and an isolation zone, which not only prevents mutual interference or deactivation between core components but also provides a structural basis for integrating more diverse functionalities. Third, the precise spatial arrangement of multiple components can generate synergistic effects far exceeding those of simple mixtures or bilayer structures, thereby significantly enhancing the overall comprehensive performance of the system.

Strategic material selection and their synergistic combinations can endow materials with advanced functionalities. − Moreover, the hierarchical integration of multichamber micro/nanostructures enables the fabrication of precision-engineered materials with complex structures, thereby enabling functional diversification. Cellulose is a natural polysaccharide biomacromolecule and a primary component of plant cell walls. Its chemically modified form, cellulose acetate, is widely employed as a versatile excipient in drug delivery owing to its exceptional film-forming capacity. Cellulose acetate (CA), a biodegradable biopolymer characterized by inherent hydrophilicity, biocompatibility, and environmental sustainability, exhibits broad solubility in organic solvents, endowing it with exceptional fiber-forming capability. , In this work, CA was selected as the primary, biodegradable fiber-forming matrix to provide structural integrity and to enable a sustained release profile, leveraging its film-forming ability and moderate hydrophobicity as a release-retarding skeleton. These attributes not only render CA highly suitable for micro/nanostructure design but also facilitate its applications in advanced fields such as dye wastewater treatment, bioactive compound encapsulation-release systems, and oil–water separation membranes. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), a hydrophilic polymer, establishes hydrogen bonding interactions with diverse pharmaceutical compounds, thereby facilitating their homogeneous dispersion within fiber matrices and mitigating crystalline formation. − Therefore, PVP was incorporated as a key hydrophilic excipient to achieve molecular-level dispersion of the hydrophobic drug curcumin via hydrogen bonding, which is critical for inhibiting drug crystallization and for modulating the dissolution and release kinetics within the CA matrix. This synergistic effect enhances drug bioavailability by reducing particle aggregation and improving dissolution kinetics. Consequently, PVP has been extensively utilized in pharmaceutics to promote the controlled release of hydrophobic drugs. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) was utilized as a hydrophilic modifier and pore-forming agent. Its incorporation aims to fine-tune the hydrophilicity and the initial release profile of the composite nanofibers, potentially adjusting the initial burst release and optimizing the release kinetics.

In this work, biomimetic hierarchical core–sheath nanofibers with three-chamber inspired by rocket staging mechanisms have been successfully fabricated. Specifically, PVP functions as the matrix polymer for the outer layer, while CA is incorporated into the middle and inner layers. Curcumin, a natural hydrophobic polyphenol with potent bioactivity, is strategically loaded into both the outer and inner compartments. The fabricated nanofibers were systematically characterized using systematic analytical techniques. Furthermore, this study comprehensively analyzed the influence of drug spatial distribution and material composition on release kinetics, revealing multistage release mechanisms through in vitro dissolution studies.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The white CA (acetyl group 39.8 wt %, hydroxyl group 3.5 wt %.) and PEG (M w = 2000) powders were supplied from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). PVP (M w = 10000) were supported by Sigma-Aldrich Trading Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ethanol, Cur, N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), acetone, and Tween 80 were supported from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All chemical reagents were used directly in the state as received, without further purification or other treatment.

Preparation of Three-Chamber Core–Sheath Nanofibers by Multifluid Electrospinning Technology

In the structural design of these structural nanofibers, a low concentration and nonspinnable PVP solution is designed as the main component of the outer layer. On the one hand, the nonspinnable solution can facilitate the successful fabrication of three-chamber core–sheath nanofibers by acting as a “lubricant”. On the other hand, it can also induce an initial rapid drug release by leveraging the water solubility of PVP. Microscopic Morphology and Internal Structure

Prior to observation, nanofibers should be cut to a square of 10 mm × 10 mm. To enhance the conductivity, the samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of platinum. Scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Florida, USA) was conducted to observe the microscopic morphology with the working voltage of 20 kV and working distance of 11.3 mm. In addition, a minimum of 100 nanofibers were collected from the SEM image for diameter analysis, which was performed with ImageJ software (version 1.52a, Maryland, USA).

For internal structure observation, nanofibers were first collected on a grid-patterned cardboard. Following transfer to a copper grid using doubly distilled water and subsequent drying, the sample was imaged by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi, Japan).

XRD and FTIR Analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to determine the crystal structure of the nanofibers. The measurements were carried out on a Bruker diffractometer (Bruker-AXS, Karlsruhe, Germany) with a 2θ angle ranging from 10° to 70° at a scanning rate of 5° min–1.

The chemical structures of the fiber components were analyzed using Attenuated Total Reflection Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker, Ettlingen, Germany). The spectral data were acquired in the range of 500–4000 cm–1 with 8 accumulated scans per spectrum.

Water Contact Angle and Water Absorption

Following preparation by cutting the fibrous membrane into 60 mm × 20 mm rectangles, samples were affixed smoothly onto glass slides employing double-sided tape. The wettability was subsequently quantified using a JC2000 C1 instrument (Shanghai, China). A water droplet was formed by the steady advancement of the syringe, with a minimum of six droplets assessed per sample. The corresponding contact angles, computed via the integrated software, are presented as mean ± SD.

Precisely weighed samples (approximately 0.01 g) were immersed in 200 mL of preheated PBS buffer (pH 6.7, 37 °C). The container was then transferred to a SHA-AB shaker operating at 50 rpm. At designated time points, the samples were retrieved, weighed, and the water uptake efficiency was derived from the ratio of mass gain to the initial dry mass.

In-Vitro Drug Release Performance

The in vitro drug release study was conducted following the paddle method prescribed by the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2015). Briefly, 0.01 g of the fibrous sample was immersed in 45 mL of release medium (PBS, pH 6.7, 37 °C, with 0.5% Tween 80) under continuous shaking at 50 rpm using a SHA-AB shaker. At each sampling point, 4 mL of the medium was collected and simultaneously replenished with an equal volume of fresh, preheated medium. The drug concentration in the withdrawn aliquots was quantified by UV–vis spectroscopy at a wavelength of 425 nm. All assays were conducted with a minimum of three independent replicates (n ≥ 3), and data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and Discussion

Macroscopic Fabrication of Three-Chamber Core–Sheath Fibers

The complexity of nanostructure is positively correlated with the difficulty in fiber preparation. Multifluid electrospinning technology puts forward higher requirements for the complexity of parameter regulation and the compatibility among components of working fluids. In this study, the same solvent system employed significantly enhances the stability of the Taylor cone during the spinning process. Moreover, the electrospinning fiber formation process occurs rapidly. A continuous fluid stream passes through the Taylor cone, undergoes stretching and solvent evaporation within merely seconds, and ultimately solidifies into nanofibers. This short window prevents different working fluids from intermixing. Consequently, under such a strong electrostatic field, the composite droplet formed at the three-chamber core–sheath spinneret develops a stratified structure, with each layer remaining independent without interfering with the others. To clearly observe this process, the fluids were stained using methylene blue and basic fuchsin solutions, then recorded the dynamics with a high-speed camera. As shown in Figure A, the inner layer and the outer layer are controlled by the same pump, while the middle layer is controlled by the separate pump. Once the voltage and flow rate reach a dynamic equilibrium state, nanofibers featuring a three-chamber core–sheath structure can be successfully fabricated.

Approximately 1 mg of methylene blue and basic fuchsin was weighed as dyes to observe the Taylor cone during the spinning process.

The complexity of nanostructure is positively correlated with the difficulty of fiber fabrication. The electrospinning of complex fibers entails increased complexity in parameter adjustment and elevated demands for compatibility among the material components. Employing electrospinning to construct nanofibers with complex structures not only complicates the optimization of processing parameters, but also imposes more stringent requirements on the compatibility among the material components.

In the electrospinning process, the spinneret is a critical component where the working fluid transitions from solution into solid nanofibers. Therefore, the macroscopic structure of the spinneret ultimately determines the microscopic structure of the resulting nanofibers. As shown in Figure B, aided by dyes, the composite Taylor cone generated by three-fluid electrospinning exhibits a distinct triple-layer structure. From outer to inner, these layers are transparent, red, and blue, with the three solutions remaining mutually noninterfering. Consequently, the jet ejected from this composite Taylor cone inherently possesses a triple structure. As it passes through a bending and whipping area, the solvent evaporates quickly. This multisolute jet, with its triple-layer structure, can be rapidly stretched and ultimately solidifies into nanofibers with a three-chamber core–sheath configuration, which are collected on a flat aluminum foil.

Morphology and Internal Structure of the Fibers

Figure A–E presents the typical micromorphology and corresponding diameter distribution of the three-chamber core–sheath nanofibers (denoted as D1 to D5) with different compositions. Most D1 fibers exhibited a cylindrical, linear, and weblike network morphology, with an average diameter of 590 ± 260 nm. The average diameters of D2-D5 fibers were also around 500 nm. Occasionally, irregular spindle-like beads were observed within the D1 fibers, as shown in Figure A. A similar phenomenon was also identified in the D2-D5 fibers in Figure B–E. The presence of these spindle structures resulted in a broad diameter distribution. A one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test was performed on the prepared D1–D5 fibers, as showed in Figure G, to analyze significant differences in fiber diameter. The results revealed a statistically significant difference between the diameters of the D1 and D3 fibers.

Morphology of three-chamber core–sheath nanofibers D1-D5 prepared by multifluid electrospinning: (A-E) SEM images, (F) TEM image of D1 fibers, (G) ANOVA one-way Tukey’s test was used to evaluate the significant difference between fibers, p < 0.05 means that there is a statistically significant difference between samples.

As shown in Figure A–D, the D1 fibers exhibited the lowest number of spindles, approximately 16. The D2, D3, D4, and D5 fibers contained 36, 42, 47, and 39 spindles, respectively. In the electrospinning process, the fiber diameter is primarily governed by parameters including the spinneret diameter, solution flow rate, applied voltage, and collection distance. Moreover, polymer concentration and the flow rate of the working fluids are also critical. Additionally, the formation of spindle-like beads in the D1–D5 fibers is also related to the amounts of PEG in the middle layer and PVP in the outermost layer of the spinning solutions.

The grayscale contrast in the TEM micrographs corresponds to the varying material densities within the fiber structure. Due to the top-down imaging principle, the core region exhibits a higher grayscale intensity as it represents the superposition of signals from all layers. A representative TEM image of the three-chamber fiber D1 (Figure F) clearly reveals three distinct grayscale levels from the interior to the exterior, unequivocally demonstrating its three-chamber core–sheath structure. Furthermore, the interlayer thickness is governed by the polymer solution concentration. This is evidenced in fiber D1, where the use of a low-concentration solution for the outermost layer directly led to the formation of a thin sheath.

XRD and FTIR Analysis

As shown in Figure A, the XRD patterns of PEG and Cur exhibit numerous sharp diffraction peaks, indicative of their crystalline nature. The pattern of CA powder shows a few sharp peaks, suggesting the presence of some crystalline regions, while the broad halo observed for PVP is characteristic of a typical amorphous polymer. For the D1 nanofibers, no distinct sharp peaks are observed in the XRD pattern, indicating the amorphous state of the fiber. In the electrospinning process, the spinning solution is typically a homogeneous, clear mixture. The rapid solvent evaporation during the brief spinning time scale rapidly solidifies the fibers, effectively immobilizing the drug molecules within or on the solid fiber matrix before they can recrystallize or aggregate. Therefore, electrospinning serves as an effective physical method for converting a crystalline drug into its amorphous form. This conclusion is further supported by the XRD patterns of D2-D5 nanofibers provided in Figure S1A in the Supporting Information, which similarly lack sharp crystalline peaks and confirm the amorphous state of all multichamber core–sheath fibers.

Figure B presents the FTIR spectra of the raw materials and the D1 fibers, used to investigate the chemical compatibility among the components. The characteristic peaks of PVP are observed at 2952 cm–1, 1660 cm–1, and 1285 cm–1. In the IR spectrum of PEG, the peak at 2884 cm–1 is assigned to the asymmetric bending vibration of the methylene group (−CH2), while the peaks at 841 cm–1 and 946 cm–1 are associated with the asymmetric stretching vibration of the ether group (−C–O−). The characteristic peaks of Cur at 1626 cm–1, 1602 cm–1, and 1506 cm–1 are related to its carbonyl (CO) and alkene (−CC−) groups. For CA, the strong peak at 1739 cm–1 corresponds to the carbonyl (−CO) stretching vibration, and the peaks at 1368 cm–1, 1224 cm–1, and 1036 cm–1 are attributed to the stretching vibrations of the methyl (CH3) and ether (C–O–C) groups, respectively. Since the D1 fiber is primarily composed of CA and PVP, its spectrum contains all characteristic peaks of CA, along with the carbonyl (−CO−) stretching vibration of PVP at 1662 cm–1. Furthermore, some characteristic peaks of Cur appear in the IR spectrum of the D1 fiber, such as the alkene (−CC−) peak at 1515 cm–1 and the carbonyl (CO) peak at 1587 cm–1. The slight shifts in these peaks compared to those of pure Cur are likely due to hydrogen bonding interactions between Cur and either PVP or CA. As illustrated in Figure C, the Cur molecule contains two hydroxyl (−OH) groups and two carbonyl (CO) groups. The carbonyl group of PVP can act as a hydrogen bond acceptor, forming hydrogen bonds with the hydroxyl groups of Cur. CA also possesses multiple carbonyl and hydroxyl groups, enabling various potential hydrogen bonding configurations with Cur. The formation of these hydrogen bonds plays a positive role in enhancing the compatibility among the components within the fiber. The ATR-FTIR spectra of the prepared D2-D5 nanofibers in Figure S1B in the Supporting Information are consistent with the analysis above, displaying characteristic peaks of CA and PVP along with slight shifts in Cur-related bands indicative of hydrogen bonding interactions similar to those observed for D1.

Hydrophilic Properties and the Underlying Mechanism of Three-Chamber Core–Sheath Fibers

Figure A illustrates the interfacial wettability of the D1 fiber. As shown, the D1 fiber exhibited excellent hydrophilicity, with a WCA of 55.55 ± 3.94° at 1 s, which rapidly decreased to 17.92 ± 2.03° by 2 s. The water droplet was almost completely absorbed by the fiber within this brief 2 s period. This superior hydrophilicity is attributed to the material selection and structural design of the hydrophilic PVP in the outer layer of the three-chamber core–sheath fiber. A digital photograph taken after the WCA test reveals a distinct layered structure in the wet D1 fiber: a large, light-colored outer region corresponding to the PVP sheath, which dissolved and diffused upon water contact; a dark inner core representing the drug reservoir; and an intermediate transition layer identified as the blank middle chamber.

Hydrophilic properties of the three-chamber core–sheath fibers. (A) Water contact angle and water absorption capacity of fiber D1, (B) dynamic water contact angles of fibers D2-D5, (C) schematic illustration of the proposed hydrophilic mechanism.

The water absorption kinetics, plotted against time, further demonstrated the exceptionally high initial water uptake efficiency of the D1 fiber. It reached a remarkable absorption capacity of 3770.72% ± 314.90% within just 5 min and stabilized after 15 min. It is important to clarify that this value represents water uptake relative to the dry fiber mass, not mass loss from dissolution of the hydrophilic components (PVP and PEG), as the latter constitute only a minor fraction and their complete dissolution results in negligible mass loss. This behavior is directly related to the excellent hydrophilicity of the fiber, which facilitates rapid water intrusion. As shown in Figure S2 in the Supporting Information, D2–D5 fibers similarly demonstrate highly efficient initial water absorption, with all formulations reaching over 3000% swelling ratio within the first 10 min and then gradually stabilizing, confirming the rapid water intrusion behavior facilitated after PVP/PEG dissolution. The superior final water retention capacity observed in fiber D2, compared to the other trichamber core–sheath fibers, is likely attributed to the absence of PEG in its intermediate layer. This absence prevents the formation of microchannels by PEG, which consequently enhances the water retention capability of the fiber. This is consistent with our previous work, the incorporation of PEG into the intermediate layer of multichamber core–sheath fibers induces a dramatic transition in fiber hydrophilicity.

Figure B presents the dynamic WCAs of the D2-D5 fibers. The WCAs of D2, D4, and D5 fibers showed a sharp decrease within the first 2 s, stabilized after 4 s, and reached a super hydrophilic state. The rapid initial decline in WCA is primarily due to the instantaneous dissolution of the water-soluble PVP in the outermost layer. Furthermore, reducing the concentration of this outer PVP layer, as evidenced by the D3 fiber, effectively slowed the initial decrease rate of the WCA. This confirms a positive correlation between the content of hydrophilic polymer in the fiber and its overall hydrophilic performance.

To quantitatively correlate wettability with structural parameters and validate the proposed water intrusion mechanism, we further analyzed the composition and water contact angle. The results demonstrate that the content of the hydrophilic PVP in the outermost layer is the primary factor controlling initial wettability. Reducing the sheath PVP content from 8% to 4% caused the water contact angle at 2 s to rise sharply from 17.82° to 110.85°, followed by a rapid decline to 56.9° at 3 s. This confirms the dominant role of PVP dissolution in initiating water contact and penetration. In contrast, the PEG-containing intermediate layer exerts a secondary influence. Comparing fibers with the same sheath PVP content of 8%, the absence of PEG in D2 fibers led to a slightly higher 2 s water contact angle of 22.96°, compared to 17.82° in D1 fibers with PEG present. While initial wetting is governed by the rapid dissolution of PVP, the subsequent dissolution of PEG guides deeper water intrusion. As shown in Figure C and supported by WCA trends, water intrusion proceeds in two distinct phases. The first involves rapid dissolution of the outer PVP layer upon contact, captured quantitatively by the dramatic water contact angle shift with PVP content. The second phase, facilitated by PEG dissolution, promotes the formation of microchannels or micropores that direct further water penetration toward the drug-loaded core. Thus, the sequential dissolution of PVP and PEG jointly enables fast water migration. Conversely, the microchannels formed after PEG dissolution reduce the water retention capacity of fibers. Therefore, wettability and water absorption can be quantitatively explained by the sequential dissolution kinetics of PVP and PEG.

Drug Release Performance

Release Profile of Three-Chamber Core–Sheath Nanofibers

Understanding the distribution and release behavior of a drug within a carrier is essential, as it not only directly impacts the sustained-release profile and targeted delivery but also provides critical guidance for the rational design and development of new formulations. , Raw materials and dosage forms significantly influence the drug release performance. , The prepared three-chamber core–sheath fiber D1 exhibited a drug loading capacity of 81.37% ± 6.06%. Figure A illustrates its drug release profile over 48 h. The fiber D1 achieved a maximum drug release of 95.32% ± 1.66% within 24 h. It is noteworthy that the actual cumulative drug release from D1 fiber reached 35.8% within the first hour, which is close to the theoretical drug content (30%) in the sheath layer, with the deviation being within an acceptable range. This initial burst release is primarily attributed to the rapid dissolution of the water-soluble PVP matrix in the sheath layer, which accelerates the entry of the drug into the release medium. Consequently, the drug release from the D1 fiber exhibited a typical biphasic pattern. While the slow release at later stages, referred to as the “tailing effect”, is typically therapeutically ineffective and often inevitable, the release profile of D1 fiber in Figure A shows that the drug was almost completely released by 12 h, thereby mitigating the tailing effect to some extent. This phenomenon is likely due to the combined action of the hydrophilic PVP sheath and the drug distributed within it.

Drug release behavior of the three-chamber core–sheath nanofibers. (A) In vitro drug release profile over 48 h. Fitting of the release data to the (B) first-order kinetics, (C) Higuchi, and (D) Korsmeyer-Peppas mathematical models.

The drug release data from the D1 fiber were further analyzed using mathematical models. The fitting results for the first-order kinetic, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer-Peppas models are presented in Figure B–D, respectively. The high R 2 value in Figure B indicates that the release process conforms to the first-order kinetic model. However, the R 2 value for the Higuchi model was below 0.9 (Figure C), suggesting that drug release from the D1 fiber does not follow a diffusion process based solely on polymer porosity. In the Korsmeyer-Peppas model, the release exponent (n) is a critical parameter for evaluating the drug diffusion mechanism. The n value for the D1 fiber was 0.26, as depicted in Figure D. Typically, an n value less than 0.5 is often associated with Fickian diffusion in homogeneous systems. This observation aligns with studies on similar core–sheath systems, where the Peppas model has also been applied to describe the release of curcumin from core–sheath nanofibers and has successfully elucidated its diffusion mechanism. , However, it is worth noting that the D1 fiber is a complex three-chamber core–sheath structure where the drug is distributed in both the outermost and innermost layers, with the outermost layer being a water-soluble PVP matrix. Theoretically, besides Fickian diffusion, the drug release mechanism in D1 should also involve diffusion mediated by polymer matrix erosion. Generally, the mathematical models employed here are suitable for homogeneous, single-structure materials with uniform drug distribution. Studies have suggested that although the core–sheath structure of drug carriers can modulate the release rate, the long-term release behavior remains influenced by multiple factors, and traditional models may not fully capture their dynamic variations. Therefore, due to the nonuniform drug distribution and structurally complex architecture of the D1 fiber, the fitted n value (0.26) should be interpreted with caution and does not independently and conclusively prove a purely Fickian diffusion mechanism. Instead, it may serve as a preliminary indication that diffusion plays a significant role, while erosion and the heterogeneous structure also contribute to the overall release behavior.

Effect of Material Composition on Drug Release

To investigate the influence of polymer composition on the drug release performance of three-chamber core–sheath fibers, the release behaviors of D2 and D3 fibers were studied and compared with that of the control fiber D1, as shown in Figure . The drug loading efficiencies of the D2 and D3 fibers were 85.53% ± 3.79% and 77.61% ± 3.70%, respectively. According to the cumulative release profiles in Figure A, fibers D1 and D2 exhibited similar drug release curves. This indicates that the presence or absence of a small amount of water-soluble PEG in the intermediate layer had a negligible impact on the overall drug release performance when the outermost layer contained a sufficient amount of water-soluble PVP. Notably, both D1 and D2 fibers achieved nearly complete release by 12 h, effectively mitigating the common “tailing effect” observed in sustained-release systems. Fiber D3 exhibited a more pronounced delayed release during the middle and late stages compared to D1 and D2, reaching a plateau only after 24 h. This suggests that no significant sustained slow release was observed in the later stage for these three-chamber fibers with nonuniform drug distribution.

Based on the hydrophilicity tests shown in Figure A and B, fibers D2-D5 all demonstrated good hydrophilicity, with D3 showing slightly weaker hydrophilicity due to its lower PVP content in the sheath layer. Since enhanced hydrophilicity generally accelerates drug release, it is hypothesized that excessively strong hydrophilicity might weaken or even eliminate the sustained release effect imparted by the core–sheath structure in the later stages. The D3 fiber, with its relatively lower PVP concentration, achieved a moderate improvement in hydrophilicity, thereby partially retaining the sustained release effect conferred by the core–sheath structure in the later release phase.

To gain further insight into the initial drug release behavior, Figure B presents the release profiles of the fibers during the first 4 h. PVP, a polymer with excellent biocompatibility, can form solid dispersions with active pharmaceutical ingredients to promote drug dissolution and prevent recrystallization or precipitation. As the primary carrier matrix in the outermost layer, PVP was intended to modulate the initial release rate of Cur. The theoretical initial release for D1-D3 fibers was approximately 42.86%. However, the actual cumulative releases at 0.5 h were 21.58% ± 2.99%, 19.29% ± 7.44%, and 19.75% ± 4.09% for D1, D2, and D3, respectively. It is postulated that during the modified triaxial electrospinning process, drug diffusion from the low-concentration PVP/drug outermost layer into the intermediate high-concentration CA/PEG blank layer might have occurred within the composite Taylor cone. Consequently, not all the drug in the PVP outermost layer was released immediately in the initial phase; some may have remained on the surface of the CA/PEG intermediate layer. Furthermore, compared to D1 and D2, fiber D3 showed a significantly reduced release amount between 0.5 and 4 h. Therefore, the physicochemical properties of the CA polymer matrix, combined with the multichamber core–sheath structure, collaboratively retarded the drug release between 0.5 and 4 h.

The drug release data for fibers D2 and D3 were fitted using the Zero-order, First-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer-Peppas models, as shown in Figure C–F. The low correlation coefficients (R 2) for the Zero-order model fittings indicated that the release processes of D2 and D3 fibers did not follow Zero-order kinetics. In contrast, the relatively high R 2 values for the First-order model suggested that the first-order kinetic model provided a good description of their release behavior. For the Higuchi model, the R 2 values for both fibers were below 0.9 in Figure E, indicating that the release mechanism was not solely a diffusion process based on polymer porosity. In the Korsmeyer-Peppas model, the release exponent (n) is a key parameter for evaluating the drug diffusion mechanism. In Figure F, the n values for D2 and D3 fibers were 0.26 and 0.29, respectively, both being less than 0.45. Typically, in homogeneous systems, such n values are associated with Fickian diffusion; however, given the limitations of the model for nonuniform structures, these n values should be interpreted with caution. It is noteworthy that the D3 fiber exhibited the highest R 2 value in the Korsmeyer-Peppas model among these fibers, indicating the best fitting result for this model among the tested models, though the fit is still not ideal due to structural complexity.

It is important to note that the drug was distributed nonuniformly within the D2 and D3 fibers, located in both the outermost and innermost layers, with the outermost layer being a water-soluble PVP matrix. The rapid hydrolysis of PVP leads to the instantaneous release of some drug due to polymer matrix erosion. Therefore, the drug release mechanism in these three-chamber core–sheath nanofibers likely involves both Fickian diffusion and matrix erosion. Consequently, the n values from the Korsmeyer-Peppas model cannot independently confirm a purely Fickian diffusion mechanism, they only provide a preliminary indication that diffusion plays a significant role. The Korsmeyer-Peppas model is typically suitable for homogeneous and single-structured materials with uniform drug distribution. The structural complexity and compositional diversity of the three-chamber core–sheath fibers are the primary reasons for the observed deviations between the model fittings and theoretical expectations.

Figure B presents the drug release profiles during the initial 4 h. A stepwise increase in both the initial release rate and cumulative release amount was evident with increasing Cur content in the outermost layer. Specifically, fiber D4, with 1% Cur in the outermost layer, showed a cumulative release of 65.15% ± 11.19% within 4 h, while fiber D5, with 3% Cur in the outermost layer, reached a significantly higher release of 79.26% ± 0.42% in the same period.

To further analyze the release kinetics, the release data were fitted using Zero-order, First-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer-Peppas models, as shown in Figure C–F. In Figure C, the low correlation coefficients R 2 for the Zero-order model indicate that the release processes of D4 and D5 fibers do not follow Zero-order kinetics. Conversely, the relatively high R 2 values for the First-order model in Figure D suggest that their release profiles are well described by First-order kinetics. The R 2 values for the Higuchi model were below 0.9 for all fibers in Figure E, implying that the drug release mechanism is not purely diffusion-based through polymer pores. In the Korsmeyer-Peppas model, the release exponent n serves as a key parameter for evaluating the drug transport mechanism. The n values for both D4 and D5 fibers were less than 0.5 in Figure F. Typically, in homogeneous systems, n < 0.5 is interpreted as Fickian diffusion; however, given the complex and nonuniform structure of the present fibers, these n values should be viewed as a preliminary indication that diffusion plays a significant role, rather than as definitive proof of a purely Fickian mechanism.

It should be noted that the water-soluble PVP in the outermost layer serves as a carrier for a portion of the drug in these three-chamber core–sheath fibers. Theoretically, a drug release mechanism involving rapid matrix erosion of PVP should also be considered. Consistent with previous discussions, the Korsmeyer-Peppas model is primarily applicable to homogeneous, single-structure materials with uniform drug distribution. The structural complexity and component diversity of the three-chamber core–sheath fibers are the main factors contributing to the deviations in the Korsmeyer-Peppas model fittings. Although these traditional models exhibit limitations when analyzing complex drug delivery systems, they can still offer a preliminary, semiquantitative comparison of release mechanisms through release exponents and kinetic constants, help facilitate quantitative analysis of different formulations, and provide key parameters for constructing subsequent, more complex structures. In this study, the application of the Peppas model provides a basic, though imperfect, reference for understanding the regulatory effect of a three-chamber core–sheath structure on drug release, while keeping in mind the model’s limitations for heterogeneous systems.

Mechanism of Controlled Drug Release in Three-Chamber Core–Sheath Fibers

The drug release data indicate that both the polymer composition and the spatial distribution of the drug significantly influence the release behavior from the three-chamber core–sheath nanofibers. The outermost PVP component effectively enhances the hydrophilicity of the fibers and substantially increases the initial drug release amount. Furthermore, increasing the drug concentration in the outermost layer further augments the early stage release. When the PVP concentration is reduced, the improvement in fiber hydrophilicity occurs at a slower rate, consequently delaying the drug release in the later stages. While the hydrophilic components in the sheath layer enhance hydrophilicity and accelerate drug release, they also diminish the protective function inherent in the two-chamber core–sheath structure to some extent. Water contact angle measurements confirm that the hydrophilic PVP in the outermost layer has already significantly enhanced the overall hydrophilicity of the fibers. Theoretically, the small amount of PEG in the intermediate layer of fiber D3 could also contribute to hydrophilicity. However, once a certain level of hydrophilicity is achieved, the addition of minor hydrophilic components has a limited impact on both the hydrophilicity and the subsequent drug release behavior.

As illustrated in Figure , the drug release from the three-chamber core–sheath fibers can be described as a process where water molecules infiltrate the fiber and carry the drug out via diffusion. Therefore, the intrinsic hydrophilicity of the fiber, which is governed by its microstructure and polymer composition, greatly affects the drug release performance. Upon contact with water, the outermost PVP layer dissolves immediately, leading to the rapid release of its loaded drug through a matrix erosion mechanism. This rapid dissolution of PVP facilitates further intrusion of water molecules into the fiber interior, with the PVP content directly influencing the rate of water penetration. As water molecules proceed inward, they traverse the intermediate blank layer and reach the innermost drug reservoir. The drug then diffuses out of the fiber matrix into the release medium, driven by the water molecules. The external blank layer surrounding the drug reservoir acts as a “barrier”, increasing the diffusion path length for the drug and prolonging its release time, thereby resulting in a sustained release profile.

Schematic illustration of the drug release mechanism from trichamber core–sheath fibers.

Moreover, the content of hydrophilic components in the sheath layer of core–sheath fiber not only affects its hydrophilicity but also compromises the protective role of the sheath. This is because these dissolved hydrophilic components can form microchannels that guide the rapid intrusion of water molecules. The content of hydrophilic components in the outermost layer is directly correlated with the enhancement of fiber hydrophilicity. When the fiber already possesses good hydrophilicity, the intermediate layer contributes little to its further improvement. If the content of hydrophilic components in the outermost layer is low, the overall hydrophilicity increase is relatively modest, resulting in a slower initial water intrusion rate. Under these conditions, the blank intermediate layer in the three-chamber structure still offers some protective effect for the core drug reservoir. However, when the content of hydrophilic PVP in the outermost layer is high, the overall hydrophilicity of the fiber is significantly enhanced, rendering the protective function of the intermediate layer less critical. This leads to rapid initial drug release and eliminates the tailing release phenomenon in the later stage.

Under the premise of constant polymer composition and three-chamber core–sheath structure, increasing the drug loading in the outermost layer alters the overall drug distribution within the fiber. Based on the water-soluble nature of PVP, a higher drug load in this layer increases the amount of drug released during the initial phase, while relatively reducing the proportion of drug released in the later stage.

In summary, besides the structural design of the three-chamber core–sheath, the content of hydrophilic polymer in the sheath layer and its drug loading are key factors influencing the drug release behavior. The established relationship between material composition regulation, based on the multichamber core–sheath structure, and drug release performance provides valuable insights for developing novel controlled-release drug delivery systems. This understanding of structure–property relationships can guide the rational design of advanced fibrous platforms for tailored drug release applications.

Conclusion

This study successfully fabricated nanofibers with a well-defined three-chamber core–sheath structure using a modified triaxial electrospinning technique. By strategically positioning hydrophilic PVP and part of the drug in the outermost layer, rapid initial drug release was achieved, demonstrating a distinct advantage over conventional two-chamber structured fibers. Systematic investigation revealed that the PVP concentration in the outermost layer effectively regulates the dynamic wetting behavior of the fibers: at 8% PVP, the water contact angle transitioned from hydrophobic to hydrophilic within 2 s, whereas reducing the concentration to 4% extended this transition period to 3–5 s. Notably, variations in the outer layer composition had limited impact on the final water absorption capacity. Regarding drug release properties, the hydrophilic drug-loaded outer layer not only facilitated rapid initial drug release but also accelerated the later release phase by enhancing the overall hydrophilicity of the fibers, while effectively eliminating the tailing effect commonly observed in the final stages of drug release. Furthermore, the presence or absence of 1% PEG in the intermediate layer had negligible influence on the release behavior, whereas reducing the PVP concentration in the outermost layer maintained moderate hydrophilicity while preserving the barrier function of the intermediate blank layer, thereby delaying drug release between 0.5 and 4 h. Additionally, increasing the drug concentration in the outermost layer from 1% to 3% significantly enhanced the initial release amount from 65.15% ± 11.19% to 79.26% ± 0.42%. Mechanistic studies revealed that the PVP layer not only rapidly guides water molecules into the fiber interior but also, due to its strong hydrophilicity, compromises the physical barrier effect of the core–sheath structure. The complex structure, multicomponent nature, and nonuniform drug distribution within the fibers were identified as primary reasons for deviations observed when fitting data to conventional release models. These findings provide an important theoretical foundation and practical guidance for the design of intelligent drug delivery systems based on multichamber core–sheath structures.

The key innovation of this work lies in the rational design of a triaxial structured fiber that decouples rapid initial release from sustained later-phase delivery through a hydrophilic outer layer and a functional intermediate layer. Compared with conventional two-chamber or monolithic fiber systems, the proposed triaxial architecture demonstrates superior control over the release profile, particularly in eliminating the tailing effect and enabling tunable burst release without compromising the barrier function of the core–sheath structure. Future work should systematically evaluate the biocompatibility and in vivo performance of these triaxial fibers, especially in wound dressing applications. In addition, exploring other polymer–drug combinations and scaling up the triaxial electrospinning process are necessary steps toward translational development. Overall, the conclusions regarding material design and release regulation established in this study serve as a clear reference for future research on multichamber core–sheath structures for intelligent drug delivery.
