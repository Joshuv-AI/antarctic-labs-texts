A General Framework of Linear Elasticity Enhanced Multiscale Coupling Methods for Crystalline Defects
Yanbo Zhan, Yangshuai Wang, Hao Wang

Abstract
. The atomistic-to-continuum (a/c) coupling methods, also known as the quasicontinuum (QC) methods, are a important class of concurrent multisacle methods for modeling and simulating materials with defects. The a/c methods aim to balance the accuracy and efficiency by coupling a molecular mechanics model (also termed as the atomistic model) in the vicinity of localized defects with the Cauchy-Born approximation of the atomistic model in the elastic far field. However, since both the molecular mechanics model and its Cauchy-Born approximation are usually a nonlinear, it potentially leads to a high computational cost for large-scale simulations. In this work, we propose an advancement of the classic quasinonlocal (QNL) a/c coupling method by incorporating a linearized Cauchy-Born model to reduce the computational cost. We present a rigorous a priori error analysis for this QNL method with linear elasticity enhancement (QNLL method), and show both analytically and numerically that it achieves the same convergence behavior as the classic (nonlinear) QNL method by proper determination of certain parameters relating to domain decomposition and finite element discretization. More importantly, our numerical experiments demonstrate that the QNLL method perform an substantial improvement of the computational efficiency in term of CPU times.

Crystalline defects, such as vacancies, dislocations, and cracks, play a crucial role in determining mechanical behavior of the materials

In order to achieve a balance between accuracy and computational efficiency, these methods decompose the computational domain into a small core region , which is modeled with detailed atomistic scale approaches such as molecular mechanics, and a large far-field region, which is often described by macroscopic continuum elasticity that can be further discretized by the finite element method to reduce the total degrees of freedoms (DoFs).

Concurrent multiscale methods may date back to 1980’s and it was in
[
32
]
that the Cauchy-Born approximation was introduced to the methods to initiate the atomisti-to-continuum (a/c) coupling methods, which are also well known as the quasicontinuum (QC) methods among the engineering community

. Cauchy-Born approximation is essentially the continuum elasticity model derived the from a molecular mechanics model by certain homogenization process

,

,

,
19
,

. It is an important step in concurrent multiscale modeling to guarantee that the models of different scales produce the same result under elastic deformation, which is also known as the first level consistency of the method
[

It was later discovered that the energy-based a/c coupling methods suffer from the so-called “ghost force” at the interface between the regions where models of different scales transit

[

]

Considerable effort has been made in the recent two decades for the development and the mathematical analysis of the consistent a/c coupling methods so that the interface error is controllable and will not ruin the overall accuracy of the solution. Shimokawa et al. first proposed the Quasi-Nonlocal (QNL) method
[
42
]
, which eliminates the ”ghost force” by reconstructing the energy of atoms near the interface. E, Lu and Yang proposed the Geometric Reconstruction (GRC) method
[

[

,

. Ortner, Ortner and Wang performed a priori and a posteriori error analysis on the QNL and QNL with coarse-graining methods based on negative norms

[

,

, which extends the QNL method to two-dimensional cases. We refer to
[
26
]
for a thorough review of the QNL method, and we will follow its theoretical framework to complete some of the proofs in this paper.

The a/c methods improves the computational efficiency compared with the full molecular mechanics simulations in terms of the substantial reduced DoFs as a result of the finite element discretization of the continuum elasticity model. Therefore existing works concentrate mainly on the convergence rate for different a/c methods, which illustrate that for a given error how many DoFs are required or vice versa

However, the molecular mechanics model we employ in the core region is usually highly nonlinear and thus is the corresponding Cauchy-Born approximation in the elastic far field as well as the a/c coupling model in whole. The high nonlinearity of the a/c model may still results in an excessive computational cost, especially in large scale simulations, which motivates this study.

The current work focuses on developing and analyzing an energy-based a/c coupling methods that integrates the classic (nonlinear) quasinonlocal (QNL) method
[
42
,
34
,
23
]
with the linearized Cauchy-Born approximation, which we name as the QNLL method. The purpose of such development is to enhance computational efficiency of the a/c method through the proper application of linearized model in the elastic far-field region while retain the same level of accuracy as the fully nonlinear coupling scheme. We then perform a rigorous
a prior
analysis of the QNLL method. We identify that the optimal rate of convergence of the method can be achieved by choosing certain parameters according to the regularity of the defect equilibrium. In particular, by balancing the lengths of the regions where different models apply and the size of the finite element discretization, we obtain a sharp error estimate of the QNLL method which retain the same order of convergence as that of the fully nonlinear QNL method. We validate our theoretical results through a series of numerical experiments. In addition, our numerical experiments demonstrate that QNLL achieve a substantial improvement of computational efficiency compared with the fully nonlinear QNL method in terms of CPU times.

To set out our ideas in a clear way, we consider a one dimensional atomistic system with nearest and next-nearest neighbor multibody interactions. However, we note that the error analysis and balancing techniques proposed are general and are expected to be extended to higher dimensions problems and other fully nonlinear a/c coupling methods with finite interaction ranges
[
52
,
39
]
. This study serves as a proof of concept, laying the foundation for possible further extension, which are discussed in the conclusion.

2.
Atomistic to Nonlinear-Linear Elasticity Coupling Method

In this section, we present the atomistic-to-continuum (a/c) coupling methods in one dimension. While the extension to higher dimensions is relatively straightforward, it involves significantly more complex notations. To ensure clarity and brevity, we focus on the one-dimensional case. Section
2.1
introduces the atomistic model, which serves as the reference framework. Section
2.2
provides an overview of the classical nonlinear quasinonlocal (QNL) method. Finally, Section
2.3
details the linearization of the Cauchy-Born model and the QNL method with the Linearized Cauchy-Born (QNLL) formulation, which is the main focus of this work.

2.1.
Atomistic model

We consider an infinite atomistic chain or one dimensional crystal lattice indexed by
\mathbb{Z}
. The reference configuration is given by
F\mathbb{Z}
, where
F>0
is a macroscopic strain.
We define the space of the displacements and the
admissible
set of deformations by

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle:=\{u:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}~|~\nabla u\in L^{2}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle:=\{y(x)=Fx+u(x)~|~u\in\mathcal{U}\}.

Let
r_{{\rm cut}}>0
, we fix an interaction range
\mathcal{R}:=\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm r_{{\rm cut}}\}
. For each
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define the finite difference stencil

Dy(\xi):=\big(D_{\rho}y(\xi)\big)_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}},\quad\text{where}~~D_{\rho}y(\xi):=y(\xi+\rho)-y(\xi).

For simplicity, we fix
r_{{\rm cut}}=2
throughout this work. However, the analysis can be readily extended to a general interaction range.

Let
V\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{R}})
be the interatomic many-body site potential. For a deformation
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, we define the energy of the infinite atomistic model by

(2.1)

\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{{\rm a}}(y):=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi}(y):=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[V\big(Dy(\xi)\big)-V(F\mathcal{R})\right],

where
\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi}(y)
is the site energy (per atom energy contribution
[
26
]
) for the site
\xi
.

For analytical purpose, we assume the regularity of site potential
V
, i.e.,

\displaystyle m(\bm{\rho})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{i=1}^{j}|\rho_{i}|\sup_{\bm{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{R}}}\|V_{\rho}(\bm{g})\|\quad\text{for}\ \bm{\rho}\in\mathcal{R}^{j},\ \text{and}

\displaystyle M^{(j,s)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\bm{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{j}}m(\bm{\rho})|\bm{\rho}|^{s}_{\infty},

where
V_{\rho}
is the derivative of the energy functional
V
with respect to
\rho
and
|\bm{\rho}|_{\infty}:=\max_{i=1,\dots,j}|\rho_{i}|
.

By the definition of
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{E}^{{\rm a}}
is well-defined on
\mathcal{Y}
, which means that the sum on the infinite lattice is actually finite
[
26
, Proposition 3.7]
. Given a dead load
f\in\mathcal{Y}
, the atomistic model is defined by the following minimization problem

(2.2)

\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}\in\arg\min\{\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{{\rm a}}(y)-\langle f,y\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}\ |\ y\in\mathcal{Y}\},

where
\langle f,y\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}}f(\xi)y(\xi)
and “
\arg\min
” is understood as the set of local minimizers. Furthermore, we denote the local minimizer of the displacement as
\bar{u}^{\rm a}:=\bar{y}^{\rm a}-Fx
.

If
\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}
solves (
2.2
), then it satisfies the first order optimality condition

(2.3)

\langle\delta\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{{\rm a}}(\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}),v\rangle=\langle f,v\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}},\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{U}.

In addition to (
2.3
), if
\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}\in\mathcal{Y}
satisfies the second-order optimality condition which is given by

(2.4)

\langle\delta^{2}\bar{\mathcal{E}}^{{\rm a}}(\bar{y}^{{\rm a}})v,v\rangle\geq c_{0}\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}},\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{U},

for some
c_{0}>0
, we say that the solution
\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}
is a strongly stable local minimizer.

To model practical defects and establish a foundation for consistency and stability analysis
[
14
]
, we introduce the decay hypothesis, which describes the decay and regularity of local minimizers (equilibrium). This assumption ensures smooth asymptotic behavior, which is crucial for the subsequent analysis.

Decay Hypothesis
(DH)
: There exists a strong local minimizer
\bar{u}^{\rm a}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\alpha>1/2
, for
x
sufficiently large such that

(2.5)

|\nabla^{j}\bar{u}^{\rm a}(x)|\leq C_{\bm{DH}}x^{-\alpha+1-j},\quad j=0,1,2,3,

where
C_{\bm{DH}}>0
is a constant that depends on lattice and interatomic potentials.

2.2.
Classic quasinonlocal (QNL) methods

The atomistic problem defined by (
2.2
) is computationally intractable due to its formulation on an infinite lattice, its reliance on a nonlocal interaction potential across the entire domain, and the fact that each atom is treated as a separate degree of freedom. To address these challenges, atomistic/continuum (a/c) coupling methods employ domain decomposition strategies to truncate the infinite computational domain, introduce a reduced model in certain regions, and further decrease the number of degrees of freedom through coarse graining. In this section, we illustrate the construction of a/c methods using the classical quasinonlocal (QNL) method
[
34
]
, which serves as the foundation for the developments presented in subsequent sections.

The a/c coupling methods often make the following three steps of approximations. The first step is to truncate the infinite lattice to a finite domain on which the computation is carried out. The second step is to derive a
local
and continuum approximation for the
nonlocal
and discrete atomistic model. The third step is to decompose the domain so that the atomistic and the continuum models are properly utilized in different regions and to make a special treatment at the interfaces where the two different models meet so that nonphysical phenomenon is avoided. We now elaborate the construction of the QNL method according to the aforementioned three steps.

2.2.1.
Truncation

We first truncate the infinite domain simply by fixing an
N\in\mathbb{N}
and define the truncated computational domain to be
\Omega:=[-N,N]
. The set of lattice inside the computational domain is given by
\Lambda:=\Omega\cap\mathbb{Z}=\{-N,\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots,N\}
.

Though the first step is very easy, we pause here to introduce an auxiliary problem that simplifies our error analysis. Specifically, we apply a Dirichlet boundary condition on
\Omega
and define the finite dimensional space of displacements and the corresponding admissible set of deformation as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{N}

\displaystyle:=\{u\in\mathcal{U}\ |\ u(\xi)=0\text{ for }\xi\leq-N\text{ or }\xi\geq N\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{N}

\displaystyle:=\{y(x)=Fx+u(x)\ |\ u\in\mathcal{U}_{N}\}.

We can then define the truncated atomistic method (often denoted by ATM
[
14
,
26
]
) as

(2.6)

y^{{\rm a}}\in\arg\min\{\mathcal{E}^{{\rm a}}(y)-\langle f,y\rangle_{N}\ |\ y\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}\},

where
\mathcal{E}^{{\rm a}}(y):=\sum_{\xi=-N}^{N}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi}(y)
and
\langle f,y\rangle_{N}=\sum_{\xi=-N}^{N}f(\xi)y(\xi)
, which can be considered as a Galerkin approximation of the original atomistic model (
2.2
).

Since our atomistic/continuum coupling methods are all defined on the finite domain
\Omega
, we can bound the error between the solution of any of the a/c methods
y^{\rm ac}
and that of original atomistic model
\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}
by

(2.7)

\|y^{\rm ac}-\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}\|\leq\|y^{\rm ac}-y^{{\rm a}}\|+\|y^{{\rm a}}-\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}\|.

We will only concentrate on the first part of the right hand side of (
2.7
) in the error analysis and the estimate of the second part, which is essentially the truncation error, is given by the following lemma
[
26
, Theorem 3.14]
:

Lemma 2.1.

Let
y^{{\rm a}}
be a strong local minimizer of (
2.3
) satisfying
(DH)
and (
2.4
). Then there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
N\geq N_{0}
, there exists a strong local minimizer
y^{{\rm a}}
of (
2.6
) satisfying

(2.8)

\|\nabla\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}-\nabla y^{{\rm a}}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{16M^{(2,0)}C_{\bm{DH}}}{\sqrt{2\alpha-1}}N^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}.

2.2.2.
Continuous approximation

Next, we consider the second approximation, which involves using a local continuum model to approximate the original nonlocal atomistic model. To further reduce the number of degrees of freedom, we apply the continuum approximation to the atomistic potential (
2.1
) based on the Cauchy-Born rule
[
36
]
, where we define the strain energy density from the interaction potential
V
as

(2.9)

W(F):=V(F\mathcal{R}).

2.2.3.
Domain decomposition

Finally, we will achieve the third approximation through the atomistic-to-continuum coupling method. The Atomistic-to-Continuum coupling method achieves an quasi-optimal trade-off between computational cost and accuracy by coupling the atomistic model with its continuum approximation. In this paper, the coupling scheme we use is the reflection method, which is show to be universally stable
[
35
]
, and we will discuss in stability analysis. Because we fix
r_{{\rm cut}}=2
, the reflection method is equivalent to the Quasi-nonlocal method (QNL method) in this case
[
26
]
. The QNL method eliminates the “ghost force” term by providing a precise definition of the energy on the interface
[
34
]
. Therefore, for the sake of simplicity in naming, the remaining sections of this paper will use the QNL method to uniformly refer to the reflection method.

We first of all decompose the computational domain
\Omega
into three different regions. The first one is the atomistic region
\Omega_{{\rm a}}
in which we assume the defect core is contained. The second one is the continuum region
\Omega_{{\rm c}}
where we assume the deformation is smooth enough. The third one is th interface region
\Omega_{{\rm i}}
which consists of a small number of layers of atoms between
\Omega_{{\rm a}}
and
\Omega_{{\rm c}}
. The lattice points in the three regions are defined by
\Lambda_{\rm a}:=\Omega_{{\rm a}}\cap\Lambda
,
\Lambda_{\rm c}:=\Omega_{{\rm c}}\cap\Lambda
and
\Lambda_{\rm i}:=\Omega_{{\rm i}}\cap\Lambda
. To be more specific, we denote them by (for simplicity, we denote
\bar{K}=K+r_{{\rm cut}}
)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm a}

\displaystyle:=\{-K,-K+1,\dots,K\},\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad~~\text{(Atomistic region)},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm i}

\displaystyle:=\{-\bar{K},-\bar{K}+1\}\cup\{\bar{K}-1,\bar{K}\},\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad~~\text{(Interface region)},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm c}

\displaystyle:=\{-N,-N+1,\dots,-\bar{K}-1\}\cup\{\bar{K}+1,\bar{K}+2,\dots,N\},\qquad~\text{(Continuum region)}.

Since we will calculate the integral on continuum region, we define the following three continuous intervals:

\displaystyle\Omega_{{\rm a}}

\displaystyle=[-K-1,K+1],

\displaystyle\Omega_{{\rm i}}

\displaystyle=[-\bar{K},-\bar{K}+1]\cup[\bar{K}-1,\bar{K}],

\displaystyle\Omega_{{\rm c}}

\displaystyle=[-N,-\bar{K}]\cup[\bar{K},N].

Moreover, we define, for any
x\in(\xi-1,\xi),\nabla y(x)=y(\xi)-y(\xi-1)
, which is a piecewise constant interpolation of
y
with respect to lattice sites. The specific construction of the QNL model is given as follows:

(2.10)

\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnl}}(y)=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi}(y)+\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm i}}\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{\xi}(y)+\int_{\Omega_{{\rm c}}}W(\nabla y)\,\text{d}x,

where the interface energy reads

\displaystyle\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{-L}(y)

\displaystyle=V(-D_{2}y,-D_{1}y,D_{1}y,D_{2}y)+\int_{-L-1}^{-L-\frac{1}{2}}W(\nabla y)\,\text{d}x,

\displaystyle\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{-L+1}(y)

\displaystyle=V(2D_{-1}y,D_{-1}y,D_{1}y,D_{2}y),

\displaystyle\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{L-1}(y)

\displaystyle=V(D_{-2}y,D_{-1}y,D_{1}y,2D_{1}y),

\displaystyle\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{L}(y)

\displaystyle=V(D_{-2}y,D_{-1}y,-D_{-1}y,-D_{-2}y)+\int_{L+\frac{1}{2}}^{L+1}W(\nabla y)\,\text{d}x.

Given a dead load
f\in\mathcal{Y}
, we seek solution

(2.11)

y^{{\rm qnl}}\in\arg\min\{\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnl}}(y)-\langle f,y\rangle_{N}\ |\ y\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}\}.

Similarly, if
y^{{\rm qnl}}
solves (
2.11
), then it satisfies the first order optimal condition

(2.12)

\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnl}}(y^{{\rm qnl}}),v\rangle=\langle f,v\rangle_{N},\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{U}_{N}.

2.3.
QNL with linearized Cauchy-Born (QNLL) method

In this section, we will introduce the QNL Methods with Linearized Cauchy-Born (QNLL). The idea behind QNLL method is to replace a nonlinear elasticity model with a linear elasticity model to further simplify the computation. To that end, we make further approximations: based on the QNL method, we use a linear elasticity model to approximate the nonlinear elasticity model. Compared to the nonlinear model, the linear model can effectively reduce computational costs. However, this also introduces new errors, which will be analyzed in the next section.

First, we construct the linear elasticity model. Here, we obtain the linear elasticity model by performing a Taylor expansion of the Cauchy-Born energy (
2.9
) around the uniform deformation. After introducing linearization, we will introduce the nonlinear-linear elasticity coupling (QNLL) model, which essentially replaces a portion of the nonlinear continuum energy functional
W
in a continuum region
\Omega_{{\rm c}}
with a linear energy functional
W_{{\rm L}}
. Here, we first provide the form of
W_{{\rm L}}
. For simplicity, we denote
W^{(k)}_{F}=W^{(k)}(F),\ k=0,1,2
. We use Taylor’s expansion in order to linearize Cauchy-Born strain energy density:

W(\nabla y)=W_{F}+W^{\prime}_{F}\nabla u+\frac{1}{2}W^{\prime\prime}_{F}(\nabla u)^{2}+\dots.

We then define the linearized Cauchy-Born strain energy density:

W_{{\rm L}}(\nabla y)=W_{F}+W^{\prime}_{F}\nabla u+\frac{1}{2}W^{\prime\prime}_{F}(\nabla u)^{2}.

Next, based on the region partitioning of the QNL method, we will further subdivide
\Lambda_{\rm c}
into nonlinear and linear regions. We denote them by

\displaystyle\Lambda_{{\rm nl}}

\displaystyle=\{-L,L+1,\dots,-\bar{K}-1\}\cup\{\bar{K}+1,\bar{K}+2,\dots,L\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{{\rm l}}

\displaystyle=\{-N,-N+1,\dots,L-1\}\cup\{L+1,L+2,\dots,N\}.

Similarly, we will also divide
\Omega_{{\rm c}}
into nonlinear and linear parts:

\displaystyle\Omega_{{\rm nl}}

\displaystyle=[-L,-\bar{K}]\cup[\bar{K},L],

\displaystyle\Omega_{{\rm l}}

\displaystyle=[-N,L]\cup[L,N].

In the linearized continuouum region, by approximating the nonlinear elasticity model with the linear elasticity model, we obtain the QNLL model:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi=-N}^{N}\Phi^{{\rm qnll}}_{\xi}(y)

(2.13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi}(y)+\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm i}}\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{\xi}(y)+\int_{\Omega_{{\rm nl}}}W(\nabla y)\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega_{{\rm l}}}W_{{\rm L}}(\nabla y)\,\text{d}x.

Given a dead load
f\in\mathcal{Y}
, we seek

(2.14)

y^{{\rm qnll}}\in\arg\min\{\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y)-\langle f,y\rangle_{N}\ |\ y\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}\}.

If
y^{{\rm qnll}}
solves (
2.14
), then it satisfies the first order optimal condition

(2.15)

\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y),v\rangle=\langle f,v\rangle_{N},\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{U}_{N}.

A Priori Analysis for the QNLL Method

In this section, we will provide the
a priori
error estimate for the atomistic to nonlinear-linear elasticity coupling model
\|\nabla\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}-\nabla y^{{\rm qnll}}\|_{L^{2}}
, following analytical framework shown in
[
26
,
34
,
37
]
. We will first present the consistency error estimate and stability analysis results of the QNLL model, and then give the
a priori
error estimate based on the inverse function theorem (Lemma
Appendix A
) provided in the Appendix. The detailed consistency error analysis is provided in Section
3.1
, stability analysis in Section
3.2
, and the final
a priori
error estimate in Section
3.3
. Finally, in Section
3.4
, based on the
a priori

(DH)

3.1.
Consistency error

In the consistency error estimate, we decompose the total error using the triangle inequality into two components: (i) the coupling error, which quantifies the difference between the atomistic model and the QNL model, and (ii) the linearization error, which arises from the transition from the QNL model to the QNLL model. By estimating these two errors separately, we derive the overall consistency error between the atomistic model and the QNLL model. Specifically, we have

\displaystyle T(y^{{\rm a}})

\displaystyle=\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm a}}(y^{{\rm a}}),v\rangle-\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y^{{\rm a}}),v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm a}}(y^{{\rm a}}),v\rangle-\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnl}}(y^{{\rm a}}),v\rangle~~\qquad\text{(the coupling error)}

\displaystyle\ \ +\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnl}}(y^{{\rm a}}),v\rangle-\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y^{{\rm a}}),v\rangle.\quad\text{(the linearization error)}

In the following, we establish an error bound for
T(y^{\rm a})
.

3.1.1.
QNL coupling error

We define the weighted characteristic function of a bond
(\xi,\xi+\rho)
by

(3.1)

\chi_{\xi,\rho}(x):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &|\rho|^{-1},&x\in\text{int}({\rm conv}\{\xi,\xi+\rho\}),\\
&\frac{1}{2}|\rho|^{-1},&x\in\{\xi,\xi+\rho\},\\
&0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

We then obtain for
D_{\rho}v(\xi)=v(\xi+\rho)-v(\xi)
that

(3.2)

D_{\rho}v(\xi)=\int_{\xi}^{\xi+\rho}\frac{\rho}{|\rho|}\nabla v\,\text{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho\chi_{\xi,\rho}(x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x.

The first variation of the atomistic energy functional (
2.1
) at
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}
is given by

\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm a}}(y),v\rangle=\sum_{\xi=-N}^{N}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho}(y)D_{\rho}v(\xi).

We apply (
3.2
), it follows that

(3.3)

\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm a}}(y),v\rangle=\int_{-N}^{N}S^{{\rm a}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x.

where

(3.4)

S^{{\rm a}}(y;x)=\sum_{\xi=-N}^{N}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\rho\chi_{\xi,\rho}(x)\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho}(y).

The first variation of the energy functional of QNL approximation defined by (
2.10
), for any
v\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}
, we have

(3.5)

\begin{split}\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnl}}(y),v\rangle&=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho}(y)D_{\rho}v(\xi)+\int_{\Omega_{{\rm c}}}\partial_{F}W(\nabla y)\nabla v\,\text{d}x\\
&=:\int_{-N}^{N}S^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x.\end{split}

where

(3.6)

S^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\rho\chi_{\xi,\rho}(x)\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho}(y)+\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm i}}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\rho\chi_{\xi,\rho}(x)\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{\xi,\rho}(y),&x\in\Omega_{{\rm a}}\cup\Omega_{{\rm i}},\\
&\partial_{F}W(\nabla y),&x\in\Omega_{{\rm c}}.\end{aligned}\right.

We now define the error in QNL model stress as

T^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x):=S^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x)-S^{{\rm a}}(y;x).

Next, we will provide a point estimate of the stress tensor for the QNL model. For the sake of presentation simplicity, we leave the detailed proof, please refer to Appendix
Appendix A.1
.

Proposition 3.1.

Let
y\in\mathcal{Y},x\in[-N,N]
, then

|T^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x)|\lesssim\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,&x\in\Omega_{{\rm a}}\backslash\overline{\Omega_{{\rm a}}},\\
&M^{(2,1)}\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(v_{x})},&x\in\Omega_{{\rm i}}\cup\overline{\Omega_{{\rm a}}},\\
&M^{(2,2)}\|\nabla^{3}u\|_{L^{\infty}(v_{x})}+M^{(3,2)}\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(v_{x})}^{2},&x\in\Omega_{{\rm c}}\end{aligned}\right.

where
v_{x}:=\big[\lfloor x\rfloor+1-2r_{{\rm cut}},\lfloor x\rfloor+2r_{{\rm cut}}\big]
is the neighborhood of some
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the meaning of
\lfloor x\rfloor
here is to take the floor of
x
, which is the greatest integer less than or equal to
x
, and
\overline{\Omega_{{\rm a}}}=[-K,-K+r_{{\rm cut}}]\cup[K-r_{{\rm cut}},K]
.

Based on the point estimate of
T^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x)
provided above, we will give the consistency error estimate for the QNL model. Please refer to Appendix
Appendix A.2
for the proof.

Proposition 3.2.

We split coupling error into two parts:

\int_{-N}^{N}T^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x=\int_{\Omega_{{\rm i}}\cup\overline{\Omega_{{\rm a}}}}T^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega_{{\rm c}}}T^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x.

For any
v\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}
, we have

(3.7)

\int_{\Omega_{{\rm i}}\cup\overline{\Omega_{{\rm a}}}}T^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x\lesssim M^{(2,1)}\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm i}}})}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm i}}\cup\overline{\Omega_{{\rm a}}})},

and

(3.8)

\int_{\Omega_{{\rm c}}}T^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x\lesssim(M^{(2,2)}\|\nabla^{3}u\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}})}+M^{(3,2)}\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{4}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}})}^{2})\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm c}})},

where
\overline{\Omega_{{\rm i}}}=[-\bar{K}+1-2r_{{\rm cut}},-\bar{K}+4+2r_{{\rm cut}}]\cup[\bar{K}-3-2r_{{\rm cut}},\bar{K}+2r_{{\rm cut}}],\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}}=[-N-2r_{{\rm cut}},-\bar{K}-1+2r_{{\rm cut}}]\cup[\bar{K}+1-2r_{{\rm cut}},N+2r_{{\rm cut}}]
.

3.1.2.
Linearization error

Firstly, we calculate the first variation of the energy functional of Nonlinear-linear elasticity coupling energy defined by (
2.13
), for any
v\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}
, is then given by

\langle\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y),v\rangle=:\int_{-N}^{N}S^{{\rm qnll}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x,

where

(3.9)

S^{{\rm qnll}}(y;x):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\rho\chi_{\xi,\rho}(x)\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho}(y)+\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm i}}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\rho\chi_{\xi,\rho}(x)\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{\xi,\rho}(y)\quad x\in\Omega_{{\rm a}}\cup\Omega_{{\rm i}},\\
&\partial_{F}W(\nabla y)\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\quad~~x\in\Omega_{{\rm nl}},\\
&\partial_{F}W_{\text{L}}(\nabla y)\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad~x\in\Omega_{{\rm l}}.\end{aligned}\right.

We now define the error in linearization as

T^{{\rm qnll}}(y;x)=S^{{\rm qnll}}(y;x)-S^{{\rm qnl}}(y;x).

We will provide a point-wise estimate of the stress tensor for the QNLL model using the definition of
W_{{\rm L}}
.

Theorem 3.3.

Let
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{N},x\in[-N,N]
, we have

(3.10)

|T^{{\rm qnll}}(y;x)|\leq\frac{1}{2}M^{(3,0)}\|\nabla u(x)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})},\ x\in\Omega_{{\rm l}}.

Proof.

We notice that
T^{{\rm qnll}}(y;x)\neq 0
, only for
x\in\Omega_{{\rm l}}
. We could directly know

\partial_{F}W_{{\rm L}}(\nabla y)=W^{\prime}_{F}+W^{\prime\prime}_{F}\nabla u.

After using the Taylor’s expansion, we obtain

|\partial_{F}W_{\text{L}}(\nabla y)-\partial_{F}W(\nabla y)|\leq\frac{1}{2}M^{(3,0)}|\nabla u(x)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}M^{(3,0)}\|\nabla u(x)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})}.

∎

Based on the point estimate of
T^{{\rm qnll}}(y;x)
provided above, we will now present the consistency error estimate for the QNLL model.

Theorem 3.4.

For any
v\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}
, we have

(3.11)

\int_{-N}^{N}T^{{\rm qnll}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}M^{(3,0)}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})}.

Proof.

After using (
3.10
), we have

\int_{\Omega_{{\rm l}}}T^{{\rm qnll}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega_{{\rm l}}}\frac{1}{2}M^{(3,0)}\|\nabla u(x)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})}|\nabla v|\,\text{d}x.

We consider (
A.8
) and obtain

\int_{-N}^{N}T^{{\rm qnll}}(y;x)\nabla v\,\text{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}M^{(3,0)}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})}.

∎

Finally, by combining Proposition
3.1.1
and Theorem
3.1.2
, and applying the triangle inequality, we provide the consistency error estimate between the QNLL model and the atomistic model.

Theorem 3.5.

For any
y,v\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}
, the consistency error estimate between the QNLL model and the atomistic model is

(3.12)

\|T\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{N}^{*}}\leq M^{(2,1)}\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm i}}})}+M^{(2,2)}\|\nabla^{3}u\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}})}+M^{(3,2)}\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}})}M^{(3,0)}+\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})}.

3.2.
Stability

In this section, we establish two key results regarding the stability of the QNLL model: (i) For uniform deformations, the QNLL model exhibits universal stability, similar to the QNL model; (ii) For non-uniform deformations, the QNLL model progressively stabilizes as the atomistic region expands.

Firstly, we calculate the second variation of the energy functional of Nonlinear-linear elasticity coupling energy defined by (
2.13
), for any
v\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}
, is then given by

(3.13)

\begin{split}\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y)v,v\rangle=&\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)\\
&\ +\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm i}}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)\\
&\ +\int_{\Omega_{{\rm nl}}}\partial_{F}^{2}W(\nabla y)(\nabla v)^{2}\,\text{d}x\\
&\ +\int_{\Omega_{{\rm l}}}\partial_{F}^{2}W_{{\rm L}}(\nabla y)(\nabla v)^{2}\,\text{d}x.\end{split}

If we focus on the second variation evaluated at the homogeneous deformation
y^{F}
, and use the fact
\partial_{F}^{2}W(\nabla y^{F})=\partial_{F}^{2}W_{{\rm L}}(\nabla y^{F})=W^{\prime\prime}_{F}
. Hence, we can obtain

\int_{\Omega_{{\rm nl}}}\partial_{F}^{2}W(\nabla y^{F})(\nabla v)^{2}\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega_{{\rm l}}}\partial_{F}^{2}W_{{\rm L}}(\nabla y^{F})(\nabla v)^{2}\,\text{d}x=\int_{\Omega_{{\rm c}}}\partial_{F}^{2}W(\nabla y^{F})(\nabla v)^{2}\,\text{d}x.

After a direct calculation, we have

(3.14)

\begin{split}\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y^{F})v,v\rangle=&\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y^{F})D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)\\
&\ +\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm i}}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y^{F})D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)\\
&\ +\int_{\Omega_{{\rm c}}}\partial_{F}^{2}W(\nabla y^{F})(\nabla v)^{2}\,\text{d}x\\
=&~\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnl}}(y^{F})v,v\rangle.\end{split}

We then define the stability constants (for homogeneous deformations)
\gamma^{{\rm a}}_{F},\gamma^{{\rm qnl}}_{F},\gamma^{{\rm qnll}}_{F}
as

(3.15)

\displaystyle\gamma^{{\rm a}}_{F}

\displaystyle=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm a}}(y^{F})v,v\rangle}{\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}},

(3.16)

\displaystyle\gamma^{{\rm qnl}}_{F}

\displaystyle=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnl}}(y^{F})v,v\rangle}{\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}},

(3.17)

\displaystyle\gamma^{{\rm qnll}}_{F}

\displaystyle=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y^{F})v,v\rangle}{\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}.

The QNL method was propose as a “universally stable method” (cf.
[
35
, Theoorem 4.3]
). Combining this result with (
3.14
) we obtain

(3.18)

\gamma^{{\rm a}}_{F}=\gamma^{{\rm qnl}}_{F}=\gamma^{{\rm qnll}}_{F}.

To make (
3.13
) precise, we will

split
the test function
v
into an atomistic and continuum component, using the following lemma [
[
26
]
, Lemma 7.3]. We leave the proof to Appendix
Appendix A.3
.

Lemma 3.6.

Let
\beta\in C^{1,1}(-\infty,\infty)
, with
0\leq\beta\leq 1
. For each
v\in\mathcal{Y}
, there exists
v^{\text{a}},v^{\text{c}}\in\mathcal{Y}
such that

(3.19)

\displaystyle|\sqrt{1-\beta(\xi)}D_{\rho}v(\xi)-D_{\rho}v^{\text{a}}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq|\rho|^{\frac{3}{2}}\|\nabla\sqrt{1-\beta}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}({\rm conv}(\xi,\xi+\rho))},

(3.20)

\displaystyle|\sqrt{\beta(\xi)}D_{\rho}v(\xi)-D_{\rho}v^{\text{c}}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq|\rho|^{\frac{3}{2}}\|\nabla\sqrt{\beta}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}({\rm conv}(\xi,\xi+\rho))},

(3.21)

\displaystyle|\nabla v^{\text{a}}|^{2}+|\nabla v^{\text{c}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\nabla v|^{2}.

According to Lemma
3.2
, we can obtain the conclusion about the stability of the QNLL model in the case of non-uniform deformations.

Theorem 3.7.

Let
y\in\mathcal{Y}
satisfy the strong stability condition (
2.4
) and suppose that there exists
\gamma^{{\rm qnll}}_{F}>0
such that

(3.22)

\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y^{F})v,v\rangle\geq\gamma^{{\rm qnll}}_{F}\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(-N,N)}\text{ for all }v\in\mathcal{U}.

Then

(3.23)

\begin{split}\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y)v,v\rangle&\geq\min(c_{0},\gamma^{{\rm qnll}}_{F})\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(-N,N)}^{2}\\
&\ -2M^{(2,\frac{1}{2})}K^{-1}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(-N,N)}^{2}-C_{\bm{DH}}M^{(3,0)}K^{-\alpha}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(-L,L)}^{2},\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{U}.\end{split}

Proof.

According to Lemma
3.2
, let
K^{{}^{\prime}}:=\lfloor K/2\rfloor<K
, and let

\beta(x):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,&-K^{{}^{\prime}}\leq x\leq K^{{}^{\prime}},\\
&\hat{\beta}(\frac{x+K^{{}^{\prime}}}{K^{{}^{\prime}}-K}),&-K\leq x\leq-K^{{}^{\prime}},\\
&\hat{\beta}(\frac{x-K^{{}^{\prime}}}{K-K^{{}^{\prime}}}),&K^{{}^{\prime}}\leq x\leq K,\\
&1,&-N\leq x\leq-K\text{ or }K\leq x\leq N.\end{aligned}\right.

where
\hat{\beta}(s)=3s^{3}-2s^{2}
. We know from
[
26
, Section 8.3]
that

(3.24)

\|\nabla\sqrt{\beta}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla\sqrt{1-\beta}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{\beta}K^{-1}.

We can now write

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y)v,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi=-N}^{N}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)\big(1-\beta(\xi)\big)D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\xi=-N}^{N}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm qnll}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)\big(\beta(\xi)\big)D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)

(3.25a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi=-N}^{N}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)\big(1-\beta(\xi)\big)D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)

(3.25b)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)\big(\beta(\xi)\big)D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)

(3.25c)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm i}}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm i}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)\big(\beta(\xi)\big)D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)

(3.25d)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega_{{\rm nl}}}\partial_{F}^{2}W(\nabla y)\big(\beta(x)\big)(\nabla v)^{2}\,\text{d}x

(3.25e)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega_{{\rm l}}}\partial_{F}^{2}W_{{\rm L}}(\nabla y)\big(\beta(x)\big)(\nabla v)^{2}\,\text{d}x.

where we also use the fact that according to our definition of
\beta
, the first sum ranges only over those sites where
\Phi^{{\rm qnll}}_{\xi}=\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi}
.

We apply estimate (
3.19
) to (
3.25a
), and we obtain

(3.26)

\begin{split}\sum_{\xi=-N}^{N}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)\big(1-\beta(\xi)\big)D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)&\geq\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm a}}(y)v^{\text{a}},v^{\text{a}}\rangle-2M^{(2,\frac{1}{2})}K^{-1}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(-N,N)}^{2}\\

We apply the estimate (
3.20
) to (
3.25b
) to get

(3.27)

\begin{split}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)\big(\beta(\xi)\big)D_{\rho}v(\xi)D_{\zeta}v(\xi)\geq&\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)D_{\rho}v^{\text{c}}(\xi)D_{\zeta}v^{\text{c}}(\xi)\\
&\ -2M^{(2,\frac{1}{2})}K^{-1}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm a}})}^{2}.\end{split}

By the definition of
v^{\text{c}}
, we notice that for

,
\nabla v^{\text{c}}=0
. After using Taylor’s expansion at
y^{F}
and
(DH)
assumption, we obtain

(3.28)

\begin{split}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y)D_{\rho}v^{\text{c}}(\xi)D_{\zeta}v^{\text{c}}(\xi)\geq&\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda_{\rm a}}\sum_{(\rho,\zeta)\in\mathcal{R}^{2}}\Phi^{{\rm a}}_{\xi,\rho\zeta}(y^{F})D_{\rho}v^{\text{c}}(\xi)D_{\zeta}v^{\text{c}}(\xi)\\

∎

3.3.
A priori existence and error estimate

In this section, based on the consistency error estimate (
3.12
) and stability analysis (

) of the QNLL model, we will provide a priori error analysis for the QNLL model using the inverse function theorem.

Theorem 3.8.

Let
\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}\in\mathcal{Y}
be a strongly stable atomistic solution satisfying (
2.4
) and
(DH)
. Consider the QNLL problem (
2.14
), supposing, moreover, that
\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}
is stable in the reference state Theorem
3.2
. Then there exists
K_{0}
such that, for all
K\geq K_{0}
, (
2.14
) has a locally unique, strongly stable solution
y^{{\rm qnll}}
which satisfies

(3.33)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\bar{y}^{{\rm a}}-\nabla y^{{\rm qnll}}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim 8

\displaystyle M^{(3,0)}(\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm i}}})}+\|\nabla^{3}u\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}})}+\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}})}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})}+N^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha})/\big(\min(c_{0},\gamma^{{\rm qnll}}_{F})\big)^{2}.

Proof.

We will first provide the a priori error estimate for
\|\nabla y^{{\rm qnll}}-\nabla y^{{\rm a}}\|_{L^{2}}
using the quantitative inverse function theorem, with

\mathcal{G}(y^{{\rm a}}):=\delta\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y^{{\rm a}})-\langle f,\cdot\rangle_{N}.

We first apply that the scaling condition implies a Lipschitz bound for
\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}
,

(3.34)

\|\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y)-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(v)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{*})}\leq M\|\nabla y-\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}},\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{U},

where
M\lesssim M^{(3,0)}
. Since
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
, we can also replace the
L^{\infty}
- norm on the right-hand side with the
L^{2}
-norm.
The residual estimate (
3.12
) gives

\|\mathcal{G}(y^{{\rm a}})\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{N}}\lesssim M^{(2,1)}\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm i}}})}+M^{(2,2)}\|\nabla^{3}u\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}})}+M^{(3,2)}\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}})}+M^{(3,0)}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})}

From Theorem
3.2
we obtain that

\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y^{{\rm a}})v,v\rangle\geq(\min(c_{0},\gamma^{{\rm qnll}}_{F})-CK^{-\min(1,\alpha)})\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Let
\gamma:=\frac{1}{2}\min(c_{0},\gamma^{{\rm qnll}}_{F})
. Applying the Lipschitz bound (
3.34
), and we obtain

\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y^{{\rm a}})v,v\rangle\geq(2\gamma-CK^{-\min(1,\alpha)}-CK^{-1/2-\alpha})\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Hence, for
K
sufficiently large, we obtain that

\langle\delta\mathcal{G}(y^{{\rm a}})v,v\rangle\geq\gamma\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{U}_{N}.

Thus, we deduce the existence of
y^{{\rm qnll}}
satisfying
\mathcal{G}(y^{{\rm qnll}})=0
. The error estimate implies

\displaystyle\|\nabla y^{{\rm qnll}}-\nabla y^{{\rm a}}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{2M\eta}{\gamma^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2M^{(3,0)}(\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm i}}})}+\|\nabla^{3}u\|_{L^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}})}+\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\overline{\Omega_{{\rm c}}})}+\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})})/\gamma^{2}.

Finally, by using the triangle inequality and truncation error (
2.8
), we yield the stated result.

∎

Discussion of the (quasi-)optimal choice of the length of regions

The quasi-optimal choice of

3.4.2.
The quasi-optimal choice of
N

After balancing the lengths of nonlinear continuum region and linear continuum region, we will now balance the computational domain length, which will follow the principles:

(1)

We should ensure that the truncation error term
N^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}
do not dominate among the various types of errors after balancing the length of the computational domain;

(2)

We choose the length of the computational domain as small as possible for computational simplicity.

According to the first principle mentioned above, we understand that the truncation error
N^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}
must be balanced against one of the terms of coupling error or linearization error (or higher-order terms). According to the second principle, to select the computational domain length

as small as possible, we must balance it against the lowest-order term of coupling error or linearization error (balancing against higher-order terms would need a longer computational domain length).

After balancing the lengths of nonlinear continuum region and linear continuum region, the lowest-order term is

L^{\frac{1}{2}-2\alpha}\approx N^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha},\quad\text{that is},\ N\approx L^{\frac{2\alpha-1/2}{\alpha-1/2}}.

4.
QNLL Method with Coarse-Graining

The QNLL method we analyze in Section
3
is not a computable scheme since it considers every atom in the computational domain as a degree of freedom and the computational cost gets high fast as the computational domain or
N
goes large. Therefore, as a common practice of the a/c method, we need to coarse grain the continuum region to reduce the number of degrees of freedom and consequently the computational cost.

In this section, we follow the same analysis framework as Section
3
. However, the difference is that in the consistency error part, we incorporate the error introduced by coarse graining. First, we give the formulation and analysis of the coarse-grained QNLL method. Then, we focus on the balancing of the atomistic, nonlinear, and linear regions so that the (quasi-)optimal convergence of the QNLL method, comparable to that of the QNL method, is achieved. Finally, we present several numerical experiments to demonstrate that the QNLL method, with proper balance of the different regions, retains the same level of accuracy as the QNL method while substantially lowering the computational cost measured by CPU time.

4.1.
Coarse-graining and analysis of the QNLL method

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\{T_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}:=\big\{[v_{j-1},v_{j}]\ |\ j=1,\dots,J\big\}
be a regular partition of the computational domain
[-N,N]
into closed intervals or elements
T_{j}
. We assume that the vertices of the partition are all at atoms or lattice sites and are denote by
\mathcal{N}_{h}:=\{v_{0},\dots,v_{J}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}
(which are often termed as rep-atoms in the language of the quasicontinuum method). We define the coarse-grained space of displacements by

(4.1)

\mathcal{U}_{h}:=\{u_{h}\in\mathcal{U}_{N}\ |\ u_{h}(-N)=u_{h}(N)=0\text{ and }u_{h}\text{ is piecewise affine with respect to }\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

and subsequently the admissible set of deformations by

(4.2)

\mathcal{Y}_{h}:=\{y\ |\ y=Fx+u_{h},\ u_{h}\in\mathcal{U}_{h}\}.

We define interpolation operator
I_{h}:\mathcal{Y}_{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}_{h}
by
I_{h}v(\zeta)=v(\zeta),\forall\zeta\in\mathcal{N}_{h}
and
I_{h}v\in\text{P1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
, which is the piecewise affine nodal interpolation with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. We firstly introduce the following proposition obtained from Poincare’s inequality for future usage.

Proposition 4.1.

Let
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h},\ T\subset[-N,-\bar{K}]\cup[\bar{K},N]
and
u\in\mathscr{U}
. Then

\|\nabla u-\nabla I_{h}u\|_{L^{2}(T)}\lesssim h_{T}\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(T)}.

For each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we let
h_{T}:=\text{diam}(T)
. Thus, for
f,g\in\mathcal{U}_{N}
, we define

\langle f,g\rangle_{h}:=\int_{-N}^{N}I_{h}(f\cdot g)\,\text{d}x=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{2}h_{T}\big\{f(v_{j-1})\cdot g(v_{j-1})+f(v_{j})\cdot g(v_{j})\big\}.

The coarse-grained QNLL model we aim to solve is the following:

(4.3)

y^{{\rm qnll}}_{h}\in{\rm argmin}\{\mathcal{E}^{{\rm qnll}}(y_{h})-\langle f,y_{h}\rangle_{h}\ |\ y_{h}\in\mathcal{Y}_{h}\}.

4.1.1.
Coarsening error of the internal forces

The first variation of the continuum energy contribution
\int_{\Omega_{{\rm c}}}W(\nabla y)\text{d}x
is given by

\int_{\Omega_{{\rm nl}}}\partial_{F}W(\nabla y)\nabla v\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega_{{\rm l}}}\partial_{F}W_{\text{L}}(\nabla y)\nabla v\,\textrm{d}x.

Elements of
\mathcal{U}_{h}
are defined pointwise, but give rise to lattice functions through point evaluation. Since finite element nodes lie on lattice sites, this is compatible with our interpolation of lattice functions.

The following lemma estimates the error contribution from this operator induced by finite element coarsening and reduction to a finite domain.

Theorem 4.2.

Let
u\in\mathscr{U}
satisfy
(\mathbf{DH})
, and
0<\bar{K}<L\leq N/2
. Then

\displaystyle\Bigg|\int_{\Omega_{{\rm nl}}}\big(\partial_{F}W(\nabla I_{h}y)

\displaystyle-\partial_{F}W(\nabla y)\big)\nabla v_{h}\,\text{d}x+\int_{\Omega_{{\rm l}}}\big(\partial_{F}W_{{\rm L}}(\nabla I_{h}y)-\partial_{F}W_{{\rm L}}(\nabla y)\big)\nabla v_{h}\,\text{d}x\Bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim M^{(2,0)}\|h\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega_{{\rm nl}})}\|\nabla v_{h}\|_{L^{2}},\quad\text{for all}\ v_{h}\in\text{P1}(\mathcal{T}_{h}).

Proof.

Firstly, we calculate the first integral on nonlinear region. After using the fact that
\int_{T}\nabla I_{h}u\,\text{d}x=\int_{T}\nabla u\,\text{d}x
, for any
T\in\Omega_{{\rm nl}}
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigg|\int_{T}\partial_{F}W(\nabla I_{h}y)-\partial_{F}W(\nabla y)\,\text{d}x\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg|\int_{T}\partial^{2}_{F}W(\nabla I_{h}y)(\nabla I_{h}u-\nabla u)\,\text{d}x\Bigg|

\displaystyle+M^{(3,0)}\int_{T}|\nabla I_{h}u-\nabla u|^{2}\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla I_{h}u-\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(T)}\lesssim\|h\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(T)}.

Summing over
T\subset\mathcal{T}_{h}
and again applying H
\ddot{\text{o}}
lder‘s inequality yields

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|h\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(T)}\|\nabla v_{h}\|_{L^{2}(T)}\leq\|h\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega_{{\rm nl}})}\|\nabla v_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm nl}})}.

Next we focus on the linear region, after using the definition of
W_{{\rm L}}(F)
, and we have

\partial_{F}W_{\text{L}}(\nabla I_{h}u)-\partial_{F}W_{\text{L}}(\nabla u)=W^{\prime\prime}_{F}(\nabla I_{h}u-\nabla u).

Moreover, using the fact that
\int_{T}\nabla I_{h}u\text{d}x=\int_{T}\nabla u\text{d}x
, since
\nabla v_{h}
is constant in
T
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{T}W^{\prime\prime}_{F}(\nabla I_{h}u-\nabla u)\nabla v_{h}\,\text{d}x=W^{\prime\prime}_{F}\ \nabla v_{h}|_{T}\cdot\int_{T}(\nabla I_{h}u-\nabla u)\,\text{d}x=0.

∎

4.3.
Discussion of the (quasi-)optimal choice of the length of the nonlinear and linear region

In this subsection, we will discuss how to achieve the quasi-optimal choice of the lengths for finite element mesh
h
, nonlinear continuum region, linear continuum region, and computational region to obtain quasi-optimal convergence order for the QNLL model. We observe that due to this error balance, we only need a very short nonlinear region, this is the key motivation that we introduce this coupling methods to gain the same accuracy but a much more efficient method.

4.3.1.
Optimizing the finite element grid

The finite mesh size
h
is the first approximation parameter that we will optimize. In this section, we use a classical technique to optimize the mesh grading.

For each
x\in[-N,N],\ x\in\text{int}T
, let
h(x):=h_{T}
. For
x<-N
or
x>N
, let
h(x):=1
. The coarse-grained error occurring in the coarsening analysis that depends on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
are the interpolation error term
\|h\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm c}})}
. Suppose that
u\in\mathcal{U}_{h}
satisfies
(DH)
and
L>r_{0}
. Then

\|h\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm c}})}\lesssim\|hx^{-\alpha-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm c}})}.

We wish to choose
h
to minimize this quantity, subject to fixing the number of degrees of freedom
\mathcal{N}_{h}
, which is given by

\mathcal{N}_{h}=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{h}}1=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{h}}h_{j}\frac{1}{h_{j}}=\int_{-N}^{N}\frac{1}{h}\,\text{d}x.

We ignore the discreteness of the mesh size function and solve

\min\|hx^{-\alpha-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm c}})}\quad\text{subject to }\int_{-N}^{N}\frac{1}{h}\,\text{d}x=\text{const}.

The solution to this variational problem satisfies

h(x)=\lambda|x|^{\frac{2}{3}(\alpha+1)}\ \text{for }x\in\Omega_{{\rm c}}.

for some constant
\lambda>0
. This gives us an optimal scaling of the mesh size function.

We now impose the condition
h(L)\approx 1
, which yields

(4.5)

h^{\text{NL}}(x)\approx(\frac{|x|}{L})^{\frac{2}{3}(\alpha+1)}=:\tilde{h}(x)\ \text{for }x\in\Omega_{{\rm c}}.

If
\alpha^{\prime}:=\frac{2}{3}(\alpha+1)
, then
\alpha^{\prime}>1
, and hence this choice of
h
yields(for simplify, we only calculate the domain
[\bar{K},N]
)

(4.6)

\int_{\bar{K}}^{N}\frac{1}{h}\,\text{d}x\approx\frac{\bar{K}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(N^{1-\alpha^{\prime}}-\bar{K}^{1-\alpha^{\prime}})}{1-\alpha^{\prime}}\approx\frac{\bar{K}}{\alpha^{\prime}-1}.

Thus, the choice (
4.5
) gives a comparable number of degrees of freedom in the linear region to that in the atomistic, interface and continuum regions. The resulting interpolation error bound can be estimated by

(4.7)

\|hx^{-\alpha-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm c}})}\approx\frac{\bar{K}^{\frac{1}{2}-(\alpha+1)}}{(\alpha^{\prime}-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\approx\bar{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}.

4.3.2.
The quasi-optimal choice of
N

Before introducing specific finite element mesh generation algorithm, we need to discuss how to determine the length of our computational domain
N
. We follow two principles:

(1)

We should ensure that the truncation error term
N^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}
do not dominate among the various types of errors after balancing the length of the computational domain;

(2)

We choose the length of the computational domain as small as possible for computational simplicity.

According to the first principle mentioned above, we understand that the truncation error
N^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}
must be balanced against one of the terms of modelling error or coarsening error (or higher-order terms). According to the second principle, to select the computational domain length as small as possible, we must balance it against the lowest-order term of modelling error or coarsening error (balancing against higher-order terms would need a longer computational domain length).

For
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
, the lowest-order term is the linearization error
\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega_{{\rm l}})}\lesssim L^{\frac{1}{2}-2\alpha}
, we should choose
N
such that

L^{\frac{1}{2}-2\alpha}\approx N^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha},\quad\text{that is},\ N\approx L^{\frac{2\alpha-1/2}{\alpha-1/2}}.

For
\alpha\geq 1
, the lowest-order term is the coarse-grained error
\|h\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{{\rm c}})}\lesssim\bar{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}
, we choose
N
such that

\bar{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}\approx N^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha},\quad\text{that is},\ N\approx\bar{K}^{\frac{\alpha+1/2}{\alpha-1/2}}.

We now turn this formal motivation into an explicit construction of the finite element mesh.

(

);
N:=\lceil\bar{K}^{\frac{2\alpha-1/2}{\alpha-1/2}}\rceil
(
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
).

Step 2

: Set
\mathcal{N}_{h}:=\{0,1,\dots,\bar{K}\}
.

Step 3

: While
n:=\max(\mathcal{N}_{h})<L
:

Step 3.1

: Set
\mathcal{N}_{h}:=\mathcal{N}_{h}\cup\{\min(L,n+\lfloor\tilde{h}(n)\rfloor)\}
.

Step 4

: While
n:=\max(\mathcal{N}_{h})<N
:

5.
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we incorporate the linear elasticity model to enhance the computational efficiency of the QNL method. Specifically, we introduce a coupling energy formulation that integrates the nonlinear QNL method with the linearized Cauchy-Born model, referred to as the QNLL method. Through a rigorous
a priori
error analysis, we demonstrate that the convergence of the QNLL method depends on the balance between the lengths of the computational, atomistic, nonlinear continuum, and linear continuum regions, as well as the finite element coarse-graining. Our analysis ensures that the QNLL method achieves the same convergence order as the original QNL method while significantly reducing computational cost. Numerical experiments validate these theoretical findings, highlighting the efficiency and practical advantages of the QNLL method.

However, several problems remain open which deserve further investigation.

•

a Posteriori Error Control:
A natural extension of our approach is the development of
a posteriori
error control methods, which have a rich body of literature
[
46
,
38
,
53
,
51
]
. The main challenge in this context lies in adaptively selecting the lengths of the regions and constructing the corresponding error estimators or indicators for these lengths. Several strategies, such as balancing error estimates with mesh refinement techniques, could be explored to improve computational efficiency while maintaining accuracy in the error bounds. This extension is crucial for future work, where the focus will be on optimizing these error indicators and integrating them effectively into multiscale models.

•

Extension to Higher Dimensions:
Extending our framework to higher dimensions requires a shift from QNL to GRAC methods
[
40
,
33
]
. The introduction of linearized Cauchy-Born in this context raises significant concerns regarding the linearization errors, especially when extending to higher dimensions. Specifically, challenges arise in quantifying how these linearization errors propagate through the coupled system, and ensuring the stability of the method in higher dimensions is a direct issue. Further research is needed to assess the extent of these linearization errors in higher-dimensional settings and to determine whether additional modifications or error control techniques are required to maintain robustness and computational efficiency in the model.

•

Generalization to Other a/c Coupling Methods:
Expanding our approach to incorporate other a/c coupling techniques, such as blending methods, is an important next step
[
1
,
21
,
22
,
48
]
. The introduction of linearized Cauchy-Born in these methods requires further exploration, particularly with regard to how nonlinear to linear transitions are handled. A critical question is whether blending is necessary to manage this transition smoothly. Additionally, the force-based method formulation in the context of linearized Cauchy-Born is worth investigating
[
9
,
47
]
, as this approach may offer significant computational speedup by reducing the problem to linear equations in the continuum region.

•

Theoretical Estimates for Computational Efficiency Improvements:
While we provide the priori anlysis of our method, further work is needed to quantify the theoretical estimate in computational efficiency. Specifically, optimization techniques can be applied to estimate how the proposed multiscale framework could lead to cheaper computational cost. Future research could focus on developing a theoretical framework for computational cost reduction based on the error estimates and meshing strategies, offering a more complete understanding of how to improve computational efficiency alongside accuracy.

•

Possible Connections with Sequential Multiscale Methods:
Although our current focus is on concurrent multiscale methods, future work should consider potential connections with sequential methods, particularly force-based methods like the Flexible Boundary Condition (FBC) Method
[
55
,
18
,
44
]
. The FBC method, while widely used for simulating defects in materials, still faces challenges such as slow convergence speed. Our approach may offer useful insights for the convergence analysis of FBC methods, particularly regarding error propagation and the balance between the lengths of regions. A key area of future investigation is the adaptation of existing sequential methods by improving convergence rates, such as extending the relaxation region and applying finite element coarsening techniques. Furthermore, exploring hybrid methods that integrate both sequential and concurrent techniques could lead to new strategies that harness the strengths of both approaches, improving both accuracy and computational efficiency.

Both the theoretical and practical aspects discussed above will be explored in future work.
