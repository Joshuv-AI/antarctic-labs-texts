Pattern of couples’ childbearing behaviors: a focused ethnographic study in Iran
Authors: Faranak Safdari -Dehcheshmeh, Mahnaz Noroozi, Soraya Memar, Fariba Taleghani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOver the last three decades, the total fertility rate, especially in developing countries like Iran, has dramatically decreased. To make policies and adopt any practical program to increase fertility, it is important to identify the beliefs and values governing couples’ decisions for childbearing. Therefore, the present study aimed to explain the pattern of couples’ childbearing behaviors.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted in 2020–2022 using an ethnographic method. Forty three participants were selected using a purposeful sampling method and a maximum variation strategy in Isfahan, Iran. In-depth semi-structured individual interviews, focused group discussions and daily notes were used to collect the data. Braun and Clarke’s approach of thematic analysis method was used to analyze the data.ResultsThe cultural pattern obtained from data analysis was “reconsidering childbearing based on current lifestyle”. Accordingly, participants believed that they should delay childbearing and have fewer children.ConclusionsBased on research findings, to increase the total fertility rate, policymakers should take into consideration the most significant challenges and cultural factors contributing to couples’ reluctance to childbearing or strengthen factors that can play a substantial role in fostering fertility and childbearing desires.

Background

While having children is still one of the most basic and important events in many people’s lives, the global birth rate is declining [1]. Over the last three decades, the total fertility rate (TFR), especially in developing countries, has decreased dramatically [2]. Parallel to these changes, Iran has also faced a low fertility rate due to a considerable decrease in fertility [3]. Over the past half-century, Iran has experienced an unprecedented demographic transition. With a population exceeding 85 million, Iran’s population growth has recently declined to below 1% per year, compared with nearly 4% in the 1980 s [4]. It is predicted that Iran will face the phenomenon of population aging, and the elderly population will reach 20% in 2050 if TFR continues to decrease [5]. The continuous decline in TFR not only changes the composition and the structure of the population but also brings about economic and social consequences [6].

Regarding economy and production, the active labor force will be low, and the index of overhead and economic dependence will increase; consequently, the governments will face problems such as a reduction in tax revenues, the debt of pension organizations, the increase in social program costs for the elderly, and the cost burden of caring for them [7, 8]. Moreover, the negative consequences of population decline in Iran include a lack of economic growth, aging, generation discontinuity, disruption of social communication among children from low-population families, and psychological problems resulting from the lack of familial ties [9]. Considering the statistical situation of Iran’s population and the concerns of this area, the Iranian government encouraged couples to have more children since the early 2010 s and approved policies to increase childbearing since 2013. These incentive policies include a monthly salary for unemployed women with young children, increasing the duration of maternity leave, free food baskets for pregnant women, full insurance coverage for the treatment of primary and secondary infertility, etc. However, the statistics still indicate a low fertility rate in the country [10].

Recently, several studies have investigated various methodologies of changes in childbearing behaviors [5, 6, 11–14]. Although these studies provide valuable information about different aspects of childbearing behaviors, most of them are focused on women’s perspectives and desires about childbearing and have ignored men’s perspectives in this regard. Furthermore, although numerous studies have investigated this issue, they have failed to comprehensively explain how couples’ beliefs, values, and attitudes can lead to certain childbearing behaviors due to the complexity of such behaviors. The city of Isfahan is the capital of Isfahan province, the third-largest city in Iran, and a historically rich, culturally diverse city. According to the studies, TFR in Isfahan in 2019 was 1.4, and that of the country was 1.5. Currently, Isfahan, at rank seven, has the lowest TFR. It also ranks 6th in having an aging population [15, 16]. Population growth has a major impact on various aspects of life, such as markets, the environment, education, employment, and ultimately economic growth [16]. Although the current fertility trend among Isfahani couples is partly influenced by existing social and economic conditions, beliefs, norms, and social customs also play an essential role in the formation of couples’ childbearing behaviors [11, 17]. To make policies and adopt any practical program to increase fertility, it is important to gain a clear view of the attitudes, beliefs, and values governing couples’ decisions about childbearing. Since focused ethnography, as an applied research approach, studies a phenomenon or subject within a specific context for a limited period [18], the present study aimed to explain the pattern of couples’ childbearing behaviors.

Methods

The present qualitative study was conducted between 2020 and 2022 using the focused ethnographic method.

Setting, samples, and recruitment

The participants included 27 women and 16 men in the city of Isfahan, Iran. The sampling was initiated using the purposive sampling method and continued with the maximum variation strategy, focusing on age, education, occupation, economic situation, number of children, and duration of marriage. Due to the coincidence of the study with the COVID-19 epidemic and the limitation in access to the participants, snowball sampling was also used. The participants were accessed through comprehensive health centers, ultrasound centers, laboratories, gynecologists’ and midwives ' offices, premarital counseling centers, universities, shopping centers, cinemas, parks, cultural centers, restaurants, and entertainment centers. The participants were invited through face-to-face meetings or phone calls. No one refused to participate or dropped out of this study once they were recruited to participate. The first author (F.S) had no prior communication or relationship with the participants or centers. Due to the dynamics of behavioral patterns and changes in the childbearing behaviors of couples in recent years in Iran [2], as well as the possibility of more access to couples, married women and men in the past five years were included in the study. Other inclusion criteria were: being legally and religiously married, Iranian Shia couples who lived in Isfahan, age range of 20–35 years old, first-time married couples, willingness to participate in the study, signing the informed consent form, ability to communicate and speak in Persian, no history of infertility or known psychological disorders (based on the person’s self-report). Since living in Isfahan city for study or temporary work and having a child (children) with special problems could somehow affect the decision of couples about childbearing, these cases were considered as exclusion criteria. Data collection

In-depth semi-structured interviews, focused group discussions (FGDs) and daily notes were used to collect the data. Using multiple sources of data provided comprehensive view and a deep understanding of participants’ beliefs, perspectives, and experiences about childbearing. The first author (F.S) conducted individual face-to-face interviews. As a mother of three children, she had 28 years of experience in midwifery and was a Ph.D. candidate in reproductive health at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. Three other authors had previous experience in qualitative paper/report writing, as well as interviewing. To eliminate the influence of personal opinions and biases, provide a clear understanding of the data collected, and obtain results that were as accurate and neutral as possible, the first author (F.S) strived to minimize personal biases and subjective perspectives. Also, the self-awareness and critical reflection of the first author (F.S) on her own attitudes, values, beliefs, experiences, and assumptions about childbearing from the selection of research questions to the interpretation of results, led to a more accurate and valid interpretation of the data. In the present study, the reasons for the study were explained before each interview. Individual interviews were conducted at a place and time convenient to the participants. No one else was present at the interview besides the participant and the researcher. All interviews were digitally recorded with an MP4 device. Questions and guides were provided, and this was piloted in two pilot interviews. The interviews started by asking the following general questions: “What conditions do you think are required for a couple to decide about having a child? What is your plan or decision for having a child? What factors influenced your decision? What do you think about the current childbearing programs in Iran? Why did people have more children in the past? In your opinion, what are the differences between previous and current generations in childbearing?” Then, the interviews continued based on the participants’ open answers and interpretations. Given the diverse educational levels of the participants, if clarification was needed on general questions, this was provided by the first author (F.S). Individual interviews (lasting 50 to 120 min) were conducted with 43 participants, but to complete the obtained data, complementary interviews were conducted with five women and three men. Since qualitative data analysis involves generating meanings through interpretation, the decision about the number of samples and stopping data collection was situational and subjective [19]. In the present study, the interviews continued until data saturation was reached, having interviewed 40 persons, with no new codes being formed, and all previously obtained codes being duplicated. However, to avoid false data saturation, the researchers conducted three additional interviews after repletion of codes in interview No. 40, to ensure more confidence in achieving accurate data saturation, with no new data collected in the next three interviews. At this point, the researchers concluded that they would stop data collection and analysis, as data saturation had been achieved. In this study, a FGD was held by the first author (F.S) for five women for 100 min, and a separate FGD was held for six men for 95 min. The first author (F.S) also asked the participants to record any daily issues they experienced concerning fertility and childbearing, if they wished, and hand them over to her (daily notes).

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted manually using a thematic analysis method, following Braun and Clarke’s approach [20], and no software was employed. Simultaneously with each interview, the researcher transcribed the data regularly. Then, the interviews were repeatedly studied to gain a comprehensive understanding. Data analysis was performed in six stages: (1) getting familiar with the data, (2) generating the initial codes through open codification by the first author (F.S), (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing the themes, (5) defining and naming the themes, and (6) producing the report. In the present study, the forward and back translation processes were used to produce accurate and culturally relevant translations.In this regard, first, the quotes of the participants were translated into English by a translator, and then the quotes were translated into Farsi by a different translator. By doing this process, the translations were deemed acceptable if the back translation matches the source text.

Rigor and trustworthiness

In this study, to increase trustworthiness, the coded interviews were discussed with three participants, and their final opinions were summarized in subsequent sessions, allowing the participants to review the interviews. Also, triangulation and a combination of several data collection methods were used. In addition, there was a long-term involvement in the research field (21 months), and the participants were selected using a maximum variation method. To increase dependability and confirmability, through a detailed description of the research context, the path of decisions, analysis steps, and data extraction, it was possible to evaluate the study results by experts. In this regard, the opinions of four experts (who were not part of the research team) were used to audit and ensure the consistency of the coded data with what was stated by the participants (external check). In addition, to increase transferability, the findings were presented to six men and women with the same characteristics as the participants, who did not participate in the study, to compare the similarity of the study results with their own experiences.

Ethical considerations

The research approval is under the code IR.MUI.RESEARCH.REC.1399.610 was obtained from the ethics committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. The informed consent forms were obtained, and the anonymity, confidentiality of information, and the right to withdraw at any time were observed.

Results

A total number of 2257 inferential codes was obtained from data analysis. The data are then formed into 47 sub-themes, 17 secondary themes and seven main themes. Relationships developed between main themes and finally, “reconsidering childbearing based on current lifestyle” was identified as a pattern of couples’ childbearing behaviors (Table 2). Accordingly, participants believed that they should delay childbearing and have fewer children.

Results of data analysis

Reconsidering childbearing based on current lifestyle

According to findings, when couples decide on childbearing, they consider security in economic, social, political, cultural, and environmental dimensions. The ease of access to information in today’s life leads to a constant revision of values, which results in a focus on personal growth and development. Also, due to the increasing importance of individualistic values, people do not spend their limited resources only on raising children, but they care more about providing welfare for the family and prefer having fewer worries in this regard. Following modern values ​​such as gender equality highlights the achievement of status through women’s social roles. However, this creates work-family conflict in the absence of support systems. On the other hand, concerns about parenthood and childrearing highlight the need for couples to prepare for having children. Making revisions in values for childbearing leads to prioritizing child quality, directing couples to make maximum investments in child growth and development. With increasing knowledge about reproductive rights, couples consider any decision in this regard to be private, which, along with the ineffectiveness of population incentive policies, has led couples to reconsider the matter, postpone childbearing, and have fewer children. This final theme was driven by seven main themes. The main themes are as follows:

The need for couples to prepare for childbearing

The participants considered parenthood a significant change and influence on their lives, which requires planning, preparation, and learning. This main theme was driven by two secondary themes.

Ability to accept parenthood

Although the participants were afraid of accepting the role of a parent, female participants were more afraid than men of accepting such a role. Findings showed that couples need to develop self-efficacy and should be equipped with life and parenting skills to effectively accept parenthood.

“It is important how to bring up the child since he/she gets accustomed to that way of bringing up.” (Interview with a 34-year-old woman)

“It is important how to bring up the child since he/she gets accustomed to that way of bringing up.” (Interview with a 34-year-old woman)

They also need to be psychologically and spiritually prepared to become parents.

“Having a baby is stressful. Even someone who is psychologically healthy can deal with stress.” (A 34-year-old man in FGD).

“Having a baby is stressful. Even someone who is psychologically healthy can deal with stress.” (A 34-year-old man in FGD).

Appropriate attitude towards childbearing and parenthood

From the female participants’ point of view, parenthood helps people meet their psychological and emotional needs.

“Becoming a parent and having children helps us to develop more fully psychologically.” (Interview with a 30-year-old woman).

“Becoming a parent and having children helps us to develop more fully psychologically.” (Interview with a 30-year-old woman).

However, most men saw the need for parenthood as a result of interest in the continuation of the generation.

“People want their generation to continue, they want to preserve their existence in this world.” (Interview with a 33-year-old man).

“People want their generation to continue, they want to preserve their existence in this world.” (Interview with a 33-year-old man).

Confrontation of childbearing with women’s roles and employment

The female participants considered community attendance as part of their social identity. However, the confrontation of women’s employment with other roles has caused many problems for them, leading to conflict between multiple roles and a sense of lack of support, which finally results in decreased desire for childbearing. This main theme was driven by two secondary themes.

Conflict of women’s multiple role expectations with childbearing

According to the participating working women, the interference of employment with motherhood was a factor of time limitation for taking care of children and meeting their emotional needs.

“I can’t devote the time I need to raising a child … I lack motivation to have another child.” (Interview with a 34-year-old woman).

“I can’t devote the time I need to raising a child … I lack motivation to have another child.” (Interview with a 34-year-old woman).

Also, they believed that the culture governing the administrative systems has been designed based on masculine characteristics, and the job requirements of a pregnant or lactating woman are similar to those of a man.

“I had a fever. I called and said, my child is sick, and (the employer) told me, you have to come. I really didn’t know what to do?” (A 24-year-old woman in FGD).

“I had a fever. I called and said, my child is sick, and (the employer) told me, you have to come. I really didn’t know what to do?” (A 24-year-old woman in FGD).

Lack of attention to the women’s professional role

According to the results, working women had to be involved with issues such as taking care of children, and psychological pressures caused by being away from children in unsupportive systems.

“If there were a nursery school at or near my workplace, I could go and see my child and have no worries about how he/she feels there.” (Interview with a 31-year-old woman).

“If there were a nursery school at or near my workplace, I could go and see my child and have no worries about how he/she feels there.” (Interview with a 31-year-old woman).

Also, statements from some participants indicated that there was a defect in the implementation of mother-child friendly laws, or that the directives and regulations for the protection of women with young children were arbitrarily implemented, all of which led to a decreased desire for childbearing.

“I believe that working women do not have the right to have children because if they do, no one will support them.” (A 29-year-old woman in FGD).

“I believe that working women do not have the right to have children because if they do, no one will support them.” (A 29-year-old woman in FGD).

Following modern values and changes in lifestyle

According to the results, today’s couples do not care about old values and traditions. This is evident in all aspects of life, including marriage and childbearing. This main theme was driven by four secondary themes.

Condemning sexism in the contemporary world

According to the results, discriminatory gender norms that were perceived or experienced by women about family and laws played a role in their decision to childbearing. That is why the participants considered parenting as a factor that increasingly weakens women’s position in society.

“When even becoming a manager in an office brings about many problems for a woman, women are right not to accept responsibilities of having children.” (Interview with a 34-year-old man).

“When even becoming a manager in an office brings about many problems for a woman, women are right not to accept responsibilities of having children.” (Interview with a 34-year-old man).

Change in norms of marriage and childbearing

Most participants believed that getting married traditionally is not good, and considered marriage as a personal choice that does not necessarily lead to having children.

“I married the woman I love. Now I want to enjoy being with her. Why do I have to have children?” (A 33-year-old man in FGD).

“I married the woman I love. Now I want to enjoy being with her. Why do I have to have children?” (A 33-year-old man in FGD).

They associated fewer children with values such as awareness, open-mindedness, accountability, and foresight.

“Instead of having more children, I prefer having one and helping him/her to achieve its goals.” (Interview with a 28-year-old woman).

“Instead of having more children, I prefer having one and helping him/her to achieve its goals.” (Interview with a 28-year-old woman).

Individualistic preferences

Female participants preferred personal goals (such as maintaining beauty and fitness, attractiveness in sexual relationships, and achieving social success) to having children. Therefore, they did not decide to have children until they achieved their goals.

“I will not have children until I find a suitable job.” (Interview with a 30-year-old woman).

“I will not have children until I find a suitable job.” (Interview with a 30-year-old woman).

Also, female participants narrated that for men, sexual relations is one of the priorities in married life.

“For my husband, sexual pleasure is more important than having children.” (Daily note of a 31-year-old woman).

“For my husband, sexual pleasure is more important than having children.” (Daily note of a 31-year-old woman).

Desire to have more welfare

Participants considered more welfare as a “need” of today’s life and wanted to get the necessary welfare facilities before childbearing.

“I prefer not having children to failure to provide the welfare they need.” (A 35-year-old man in FGD).

“I prefer not having children to failure to provide the welfare they need.” (A 35-year-old man in FGD).

Confusion in planning for childbearing

Participants narrated that the performance of the health system and population policies are not in line with their needs and do not provide couples with reproductive rights. This main theme was driven by two secondary themes.

Defects in providing reproductive health services

The results indicated a lack of comprehensive planning of the health system to prepare couples to face parenting challenges. Most participants reported that they need to acquire knowledge and skills for pregnancy, parenthood, and adapting to multiple roles. In addition, they did not consider the performance of service providers in public hospitals to be client-oriented, and for this reason, most of them preferred to receive services from the private sector.

“I was hospitalized in a public hospital for my first birth, and I had seen appropriate behavior of its staff. So, I went to a private hospital for my second birth.” (Interview with a 34-year-old woman).

“I was hospitalized in a public hospital for my first birth, and I had seen appropriate behavior of its staff. So, I went to a private hospital for my second birth.” (Interview with a 34-year-old woman).

Inefficient incentive policies for childbearing

According to the participants, the prescriptive nature of the policies and distrust in the implementation of material incentives for childbearing were some of the factors that made couples resist these policies.

“The incentive policies make me feel that I am forced to have children … and the goal is only to increase the births.” (A 26-year-old woman in FGD).

“The incentive policies make me feel that I am forced to have children … and the goal is only to increase the births.” (A 26-year-old woman in FGD).

Parental and childbearing concerns

Participants believed that, in addition to perceived happiness for having children, there are concerns as well. The perceived limitations for childbearing play an important role in couples’ decisions to have children. This main theme was driven by two secondary themes.

Concerns of parenting consequences

Participating women were worried about the consequences and limitations of childbearing. The most important of which were spending too much time taking care of the child, losing freedom, and disrupting their normal routine.

“With a child, I really don’t have time to relax, watch TV, or go to parties.” (Interview with a 24-year-old woman).

“With a child, I really don’t have time to relax, watch TV, or go to parties.” (Interview with a 24-year-old woman).

Participating men expressed concern about being neglected by their wives when a child entered the family.

“Some women neglect their husband after having a baby… sometimes the husband thinks that his presence or absence does not matter to his wife.” (A 34-year-old man in FGD).

“Some women neglect their husband after having a baby… sometimes the husband thinks that his presence or absence does not matter to his wife.” (A 34-year-old man in FGD).

Considering all parental concerns, the couple preferred to postpone having children or have no children.

Concerns about proper education and protecting children from social harm

Based on the results, some of the participants’ concerns for children’s education were non-standard educational spaces and low-quality education in schools and universities. Additionally, they were concerned about protecting their children from moral threats they might encounter in virtual spaces and society. Therefore, they did not want to have children.

“Today it is very difficult to control children. Bad friends, getting involved in sexual relations before marriage, and addiction are worrying. This makes a person not want to have children at all!” (A 32-year-old woman in FGD).

“Today it is very difficult to control children. Bad friends, getting involved in sexual relations before marriage, and addiction are worrying. This makes a person not want to have children at all!” (A 32-year-old woman in FGD).

Problems in providing economic well-being

According to the results, the participants’ evaluation of economic conditions was the key factor in their decision to have children. Economic instability and the high costs of raising children led the participants to be desperate for economic well-being, which in turn prompted them to postpone having children. This main theme was driven by two secondary themes.

Economic burden of childbearing

Most participants believed that, in the conditions of economic insecurity and increasing living costs, the economic pressure imposed on families by children’s costs is an obstacle to childbearing.

“From the time a woman becomes pregnant until the birth, a lot of money has to be spent. After that, the parents have to spend a lot of money to raise the child.” (A 33-year-old man in FGD).

“From the time a woman becomes pregnant until the birth, a lot of money has to be spent. After that, the parents have to spend a lot of money to raise the child.” (A 33-year-old man in FGD).

“To help children progress, we have to pay much money for university entrance exam preparation classes and counseling. How can I afford it?” (Interview with a 28-year-old woman).

“To help children progress, we have to pay much money for university entrance exam preparation classes and counseling. How can I afford it?” (Interview with a 28-year-old woman).

Economic hardship to Meet the family’s needs

Based on the results, the tension caused by economic instability, despair over improving conditions, and increasing inflation were the most significant obstacles for the participants in deciding whether to have few children. Inadequate income had directed most of the male participants to multiple jobs. They believed that the tiredness and exhaustion caused by multiple jobs prevented them from meeting their children’s needs, which in turn led to them avoiding having more children.

“I work in my car until 11 p.m. and I go to the office in the mornings. My kid is 3 and has no good relationship with me; he is more intimate with his uncle because I don’t have enough time to play with him.” (Interview with a 34-year-old man).

“I work in my car until 11 p.m. and I go to the office in the mornings. My kid is 3 and has no good relationship with me; he is more intimate with his uncle because I don’t have enough time to play with him.” (Interview with a 34-year-old man).

Lack of a sense of security and social trust

According to the results, couples’ desire for childbearing is based on their evaluation of the present and future, aiming to provide security and well-being for their children. Therefore, if they evaluated the social and health conditions as unfavorable or were uncertain of the prospects of society for themselves and their children, they would hesitate to have children or more children. This theme was driven by three secondary themes.

Dealing with the COVID-19 crisis

Based on the results, the unknown nature of the COVID-19 pandemic and the concerns about its risks for pregnant women and their fetuses, limitations in receiving care during pregnancy and delivery, as well as the impact of social distancing and quarantine on psychological health and economic life of the couples, had led them to postpone having children.

“Even doctors don’t have much information about COVID-19; they have no idea what’s happening. So, I’m really afraid to be pregnant in such conditions.” (Interview with a 26-year-old woman).

“Even doctors don’t have much information about COVID-19; they have no idea what’s happening. So, I’m really afraid to be pregnant in such conditions.” (Interview with a 26-year-old woman).

Environmental concerns about childbearing

Some participants stated that the insufficient resources for the future, as well as the pollution of water, air, and food, had led them to decide not to have children or have fewer children.

“How will my child survive in an environment with so many pollutants? I’m not saying these are the main concerns, but they are important for the decision to have children.”(Interview with a 31-year-old man).

“How will my child survive in an environment with so many pollutants? I’m not saying these are the main concerns, but they are important for the decision to have children.”(Interview with a 31-year-old man).

Social disorder

Participants evaluated the social conditions, including social security, equality in welfare and education, as well as the predictability of society, are necessary for the growth and development of their children. Therefore, if their evaluation was negative, they were more cautious about childbearing. A part of this negative feeling was related to social insecurity (theft, etc.), and some part of it was due to the sense of political insecurity (internal and external political crises and wars).

“We really live in a society where people steal traffic signs, etc. Now, if my child is going to grow up in this environment, do I have to constantly think about whether something bad will happen to her/him?” (A 33-year-old man in FGD).

“We really live in a society where people steal traffic signs, etc. Now, if my child is going to grow up in this environment, do I have to constantly think about whether something bad will happen to her/him?” (A 33-year-old man in FGD).

Some participants postponed having children to facilitate migration and start a family in the designated country.

“If we could go to a developed country, we would have fewer problems. … and I would like to have children in a foreign country.” (Interview with a 32-year-old woman).

“If we could go to a developed country, we would have fewer problems. … and I would like to have children in a foreign country.” (Interview with a 32-year-old woman).

Discussion

The present study aimed to explain the pattern of couples’ childbearing behaviors. The final theme extracted was “reconsidering childbearing based on current lifestyle”. Couples’ reconsidering childbearing in line with today’s lifestyle indicates their focus on human needs at higher levels; that is, they care about the development of themselves and their children in addition to meeting their basic needs. Giddens argued that reconsidering is a characteristic of the modern world, through which social procedures are examined and corrected in the light of received information [21]. An important part of this reconsideration emerges from the interaction between individual and aggregate-level factors that determine fertility decisions. In a micro-macro model of fertility, fertility rates depend on the conditions prevailing in a society (societal level). These ‘macro’ conditions do not directly affect fertility but rather impinge on the fertility decision-making processes of individuals (individual level). Hence, a society’s fertility rates are the aggregate-level result of the myriad of fertility decisions taken by individuals and couples [22]. The results of the present study showed that most couples were not prepared for parenthood. This result was in line with several studies [23–25]. Preparation for parenthood varies across different societies and encompasses specific physical, psychological, social, cultural, and spiritual aspects [26]. The fact that the female participants emphasized preparation for parenthood more than the male participants was due to Iranian culture, in which families still follow gender roles and mothers are mainly responsible for taking care of children. Bandura emphasized that parental experiences and physiological situations are strong predictors of parental self-efficacy [27]. In this regard, previous experiences play a role in attitudes toward parenting. The age range of the participants in the present study was between 20 and 35 years old, who, due to birth control policies, often grew up in small families. In this sense, they did not have experience in caring for children and thus felt they lacked the required skills for childbearing. Therefore, the readiness of the couple in terms of physical, emotional, psychological, and social aspects is necessary to accept the roles and responsibilities of parents. The results showed that parenting consequences were challenging in several areas, including leisure time, couples’ relationships, and safety during pregnancy and childbirth. In general, the marital structure and lifestyle change after childbirth, which may cause challenges and stresses for both parents [23, 28, 29]. Many women face difficulties in conforming the role of motherhood to their identity as a sexual partner. Given that women spend most of their time taking care of babies, they may have less time and energy for a romantic relationship with their husband [30]. It appears that implementing policies and interventions, such as providing education, offering parenting training courses during pregnancy, and preparing couples for the transition to parenthood, will contribute to the proper planning of childbearing. In the present study, couples were also concerned about the status of their children’s education. Therefore, education policymakers must take necessary measures to create a desirable educational model in educational centers. This measure can reduce couples’ concerns and be effective in increasing their desire for childbearing.

The results of the present study indicated the negative effects of conflicts between work and family on childbearing in women of Iranian society. This result was in line with other studies [31, 32]. According to Tajfel’s social identity theory, membership in social groups brings a position and sense of belonging to people [33]. Therefore, it is expected that job identity brings higher self-esteem to a woman; however, if the woman is a mother as well, the expectations of these two identities do not clearly match [34], and family and work areas can demand contradictory roles from women. Choosing a role makes it difficult to fulfill commitments and tasks of another role, which is naturally a two-way process [35]. In combination with difficult roles, it is expected that a role is delayed, which does not necessarily imply neglecting or avoiding that specific role. Thus, the final behavior depends on the attitude towards alternative behaviors. According to gender equity theory, women’s new opportunities from education and the labor market raise perceptions of unfairness when women are not supported if they become mothers, and thus may reduce childbearing [36]. In the present study, most women had positive attitudes towards employment and motherhood; however, their strategies for combining different roles involved delaying having children and limiting the number of children. Therefore, to increase the fertility of working women, gender roles must be changed in such a way that men also participate in household labor. Non-recognition of women’s role and especially “motherhood-related discrimination” in the workplace in Iranian society were other reasons for the conflict between employment and childbearing. It seems that in Iran, the masculine culture in the workplace and the presence of men in executive positions are the basis of discriminatory behaviors (less employment, lower wages, and lack of job promotion as equal to men) towards women and judgments that hinder performing tasks in workplaces. As a result, most working women experience decreased quality in their personal and professional lives [37] and change their childbearing patterns. Although women try to be empowered in the conflicts between the two distinct worlds of work and staying at home, “family-friendly policies” should be revised to reconcile reproduction with women’s employment to motivate women for childbearing.

The results of the present study showed that most couples make decisions based on modern values, resulting in fewer children, postponing childbearing, and, in some cases, having no children at all. This finding is in line with Van Bavel and Kok [38]. In modern society, people act based on their internal wishes rather than following social or normative expectations. In addition, people refuse to be socially controlled by traditional institutions. Therefore, the authority and control of such social institutions and parents are restricted in the processes of selecting spouses or childbearing, and such processes are considered to be in the personal independence area [39]. “Consumerism” is another important outcome of modern life and a major reason for the declined in reproduction. Due to social changes and changes in consumption patterns, people in modern life tend to use expensive goods and services and the latest technological equipment and facilities [40]. Additionally, parenting has undergone significant changes, and due to shifts in conditions, the needs for childbearing have evolved. This requires spending costs, which is consistent with childless or few-children lifestyles. Another issue in the current society of Iran is the pressure imposed on families by the media that represent an ideal body image of women and puts them on the fence about “motherhood” and “beauty”. These contradictory identities become a source of conflict and dissonance for women who think they have to sacrifice one identity to attain the other. Therefore, policy-making and enforcement of interventions, such as increasing couples’ reproductive knowledge and modifying their attitudeS, can be helpful in better decision-making regarding childbearing. Planning for reproduction in each society is largely influenced by health system policies and facilities. Although it is difficult to evaluate childbearing incentives correctly, the results of the present study showed that the health system in Iran does not fully cover women’s expectations and needs for childbearing in a participants did not have a positive attitude towards these policies and doubted their effectiveness. This finding was in line with McDonald’s study [41]. Family policies can be effective only when they are designed according to society’s expectations and characteristics [42–44]. The World Health Organization (WHO) considered six characteristics of health care: client-oriented, fair, accessible, effective, efficient, and safe. According to the client-oriented principle, health care should not only be appropriate in terms of cultural factors, but it also should include personal needs regarding childbearing [45]. Ranjbar et al. also emphasized the crucial role of factors such as access to government-provided housing or land, comprehensive coverage of pregnancy-related medical costs, availability of livelihood packages, and extended maternity leave in influencing childbearing preferences [28]. Therefore, health policymakers should take steps to increase couples’ childbearing preferences, taking into account couples’ expectations and the country’s existing socio-economic challenges. Also, given the relative ineffectiveness of some of the current government’s incentive policies it seems necessary to review and amend these policies.

The results of the present study showed that couples’ perception and evaluation of economic conditions in Iranian society are key and influential factors in deciding to have children, which is in line with many studies [3, 40, 46–49]. Although children are not commodities and the decision to have children cannot be reduced to an economic act, at a time when many families struggle to make ends meet, they must evaluate and predict their children’s financial expenses to plan their family finances [50]. According to Becker’s model of child quantity-quality, the behaviors of family members are intended to improve the family. Becker considers the cost of education as a part of the cost of children’s quality and believes that education leads to the improvement of children’s quality. Preference for “quality or quantity” influences the size of a family [51]. Therefore, the budget a family considers for children’s supplementary educational services is spent to “buy hope”, and for parents, investment in their children is an approach to achieving academic success and improving the family’s social status [52]. Based on the results, the hardship of meeting family needs is associated with economic instability, job insecurity, and limited access to desired housing. In this regard, economic insecurity leads not only to a delay in getting married but also to postponing or avoiding reproduction [53]. Thus, the economic environment of Iranian society is one of the factors that play an important role in delaying or avoiding childbearing. The high cost of living (especially rent and the ever-increasing price of housing) and the lack of proportionality of income with inflation, especially for couples who have a more uncertain employment situation, make it difficult to achieve the goals of childbearing programs in the country. In this sense, to restore the fertility rate in the society, politicians should ensure the security of the family to cover the childbearing costs by applying correct economic policies and curbing inflation. The need for a sense of security is one of the necessities of human life. In Maslow’s needs pyramid of human needs, the basic needs are followed by the need for safety and security. Any objective or subjective distressing situation that causes insecurity stimulates changes in childbearing behaviors [54]. Based on the results of the present study, the limitation in receiving care during pregnancy and childbirth was one of the reasons for postponing the childbearing plan of couples during the COVID-19 epidemic in Iran. This finding is consistent with the results of a study by Sienicka et al. [55]. Additionally, in the present study, the pollution from various sources (water, air, and food) has led some couples to hesitate about having more children. This finding is consistent with Wesolowski’s study in Ukraine [56]. Furthermore, the results of the present study indicated a decline in social capital and a collapse in social trust in the new generation of couples. Social trust is associated with positive outcomes, such as the feeling of security, which is of great importance for couples to consider when deciding on childbearing [57]. In the present study, the participants perceived insecurity in different economic, social, and political dimensions. It seems that due to the spread of poverty and livelihood difficulties, as well as increased crimes in society, trust in social institutions has decreased, and couples decide to have fewer children to protect themselves against harm. Also, couples were concerned about threats to children’s security and well-being in the virtual worlds. This finding is in line with Aassve et al. [58] and Douglass et al. [59]. In this sense, to achieve optimal fertility in Iranian society, various sources of insecurity must be eliminated. It appears that increasing social trust will reduce the perceived insecurity among couples and contribute to better decision-making regarding childbearing.

Study limitations

Considering that in qualitative studies, especially in ethnographic studies, the researcher is viewed as a tool for collecting data, all interviews with participants were conducted by the first author (F.S). This case provided an opportunity for the researcher to enter the unknown world of the participants regarding the phenomenon under investigation, and at the same time, she faced a challenge in achieving this. The lack of a same-sex interviewer with male participants was one of the study’s limitations; despite the researcher’s efforts to make the male participants feel comfortable, they may not have felt at ease with the female interviewer. This limitation was present in the FGDs with men in such a way the researcher’s gender might have influenced male participants’ answers. Also, the coincidence of this study with the COVID-19 epidemic was another limitation. Due to this epidemic, healthcare providers couldn’t conduct face-to-face counseling with clients in comprehensive health centers, and consultations were conducted over telephone calls. In this sense, the observation of interactions between clients and healthcare providers by the researcher was limited. In addition, given that this study was conducted on Shia couples in Isfahan, the generalizability of its results should be used with caution.

Conclusion

This study, conducted for the first time in Iran, employed a focused ethnographic method to investigate the childbearing behaviors of couples. The cultural pattern resulting from the data analysis was “reconsidering childbearing based on current lifestyle”. Accordingly, participants believed that they should postpone childbearing and have fewer children. Therefore, policymakers can consider the pattern of childbearing behaviors identified in this study when formulating strategies and measures to increase fertility rates. Additionally, it is anticipated that the results of the present study will serve as a basis for future research in this field and lead to the identification of new research domains. The findings of this study can be used to inform research and design interventional programs aimed at eliminating existing obstacles in the field of childbearing and increasing the desire of couples to have children.
