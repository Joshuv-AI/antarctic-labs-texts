Teleconnection-driven winter sea surface temperature regime shift and ecosystem reorganization in the western part of the East/Japan Sea
Authors: Hae Kun Jung, Chung Il Lee, Hyo Keun Jang, In-Seong Han, Huitae Joo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Western part of the East/Japan Sea (WES) has warmed rapidly in recent decades, yet the mechanisms of its recent reorganization remain unclear. Using 1990–2024 observations, we identify a likely winter sea surface temperature (SST) regime shift around 2015, supported by complementary non-parametric change-point and persistence diagnostics and by the persistence of positive anomalies thereafter. Before 2015, SST variability reflected a balance between atmospheric forcing and East Korea Warm Current (EKWC) transport; afterward, EKWC-related variability and a northward Kuroshio displacement became more prominent, concurrent with a meridional reorganization of the Aleutian Low. Despite stable chlorophyll-a, phytoplankton size structure shifted toward pico-dominance at the expense of nano-classes, consistent with enhanced stratification and potentially altered nutrient supply pathways. These results suggest that teleconnection-driven boundary-current variability may increasingly shape both physical and biological variability in this marginal sea, highlighting its role as a sentinel of basin-scale Pacific decadal variability and its relevance for inter-basin climate linkages and decadal prediction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55134-3.Subject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Ocean sciences

Introduction

The WES is a semi-enclosed marginal sea bordered by Korea, Japan, and Russia, situated at the interface between the northwestern Pacific and the Asian continent. Its hydroclimate is shaped by the combined influence of North Pacific atmospheric and oceanic circulation1,2. The Tsushima Warm Current (TWC) and East Korea Warm Current (EKWC), both branches of Q2the Kuroshio Current, supply heat and salt and strongly influence sea surface temperature (SST) variability, with more than 80% of the EKWC inflow in winter originating from Kuroshio waters2–4. On the atmospheric side, variability in the Siberian High (SH) and Aleutian Low (AL) influences stratification5,6, mixed layer depth1,7, and SST5– 7, and these regional forcings are further modulated by large-scale modes such as the Arctic Oscillation (AO) and Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO)1,2,8,9.

The WES lies within one of the most productive fisheries regions of the Northwest Pacific, where commercially important populations have historically responded to climate variability10. In this study, the WES refers to the western coastal sector of the East/Japan Sea influenced by the EKWC, with the winter SST index calculated from the region bounded by 36°–38°N and 127°–131°E. This region lies along the main poleward conduit of the EKWC, where Kuroshio-origin warm water directly influences Korean coastal waters before dispersing into the broader East/Japan Sea2,10. Thus, the study region is not intended to represent the entire East/Japan Sea, but rather an EKWC-influenced coastal sector where local boundary-current variability and remote teleconnection forcing can be jointly expressed. In recent decades, the WES has undergone rapid environmental change. Since 2015, the ocean heat content (OHC) accumulation rate has been nearly 9.3 times higher than the 67-year mean, coinciding with winter SST anomalies of up to + 1.5 °C 7,11. These changes are tightly coupled with variability in the AO, SH, AL, PDO, and the East Asian Winter Monsoon (EAWM): under positive AO phases, polar-vortex intensification suppresses cold-air outbreaks and weakens the SH, reducing surface wind speed and heat loss and thereby promoting winter warming8,12,13. On the oceanic side, PDO-related basin-scale adjustments shift the Kuroshio axis eastward in the East China Sea, modulating EKWC inflow into the WES and altering stratification and mixed layer depth2,9,14,15. Historically, North Pacific climate regime shifts (CRS) in 1976–1977, 1988–1989, and 1998–1999 each produced profound physical and ecological reorganizations1,16. A more recent CRS in the mid-2010s—characterized by a horseshoe-shaped warming pattern and extreme marine heatwaves of + 3–6 °C17–19—has been documented across the broader North Pacific, but its specific imprint on the WES remains underexplored.

These physical reorganizations have direct ecological implications. Enhanced stratification in the WES suppresses nutrient supply, contributing to long-term declines in primary production20–23. Recent satellite and in situ observations have further documented an expansion of pico-phytoplankton dominance across Korean seas24, consistent with warming-driven shoaling of the mixed layer that favors smaller taxa adapted to oligotrophic conditions25,26. Such changes in winter stratification and circulation may therefore propagate into lower-trophic-level dynamics and alter ecosystem structure on seasonal to decadal scales.

Despite these advances, the post-2015 winter SST regime in the WES remains insufficiently understood in three specific respects. First, prior studies have largely relied on bivariate correlations between individual climate indices and SST1,2,17, and quantitative attributions that disentangle the simultaneous contributions of atmospheric and oceanic drivers in the post-2015 period are scarce8. Second, although spatial shifts of the AL center and the Kuroshio axis are recognized as key modulators of WES SST2,14,27, their relative importance has not been systematically compared between the pre- and post-2015 regimes. Third, whether the recent ecosystem-level changes—particularly the pico-phytoplankton expansion—are coupled more strongly to atmospheric or to oceanic forcing in the post-2015 regime has yet to be quantitatively assessed. Filling these gaps is essential for understanding whether the post-2015 warming represents a continuation of earlier modes of variability or a structurally distinct regime.

Accordingly, this study investigates winter SST variability in the WES from 1990 to 2024 using an integrated atmosphere–ocean–ecosystem framework. We assess whether a regime shift occurred around 2015 and evaluate the statistical support for its timing using complementary change-point and persistence diagnostics. We further explore whether comparable SST increases in different periods were governed by distinct physical mechanisms and evaluate whether these reorganizations propagated into phytoplankton community structure. By linking atmosphere–ocean variability with ecosystem responses, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the causes and ecological consequences of recent environmental change in the WES.

Results

Detection of a winter SST regime shift and associated changes in atmospheric and oceanic drivers in the WES

To statistically evaluate whether the WES experienced a significant winter SST regime shift, we applied a suite of complementary non-parametric change-point tests to annual mean SST anomalies from 1990 to 2024, explicitly distinguishing onset-based detectors (Pettitt’s test, CUSUM, and binomial persistence tests), which identify the year that marks the beginning of a sustained anomaly sign reversal, from distribution-based detectors (Mann–Whitney U tests), which identify the split year that maximizes the contrast between pre- and post-shift sample distributions. Pettitt’s test identified 2015 as the most probable shift year, with an associated p-value of 0.119; we acknowledge that this value does not reach the conventional 0.05 threshold and interpret it as a limitation inherent to applying Pettitt’s test to a relatively short record (n = 35) with strong interannual variability. The CUSUM of SST anomalies, calculated relative to the 1990–2024 full-period mean, attained its minimum at 2015, indicating the onset of a sustained sign reversal. The persistence of post-onset positive anomalies was evaluated relative to the 1990–2014 pre-onset climatology. Sequential Mann–Whitney U tests yielded lower p-values at 2018 (p = 0.0019), 2016 (p = 0.0046), and 2015 (p = 0.0070); however, for onset-type transitions these tests may favor slightly later split years because already-transitioned warm years can be included in the pre-shift sample, increasing the apparent pre–post contrast. A one-sided binomial persistence test further supports 2015, which captures 8 of 9 post-breakpoint years with positive anomalies (p = 0.020), whereas later choices either include fewer warm years (BP = 2016: 7/8, p = 0.035) or leave an insufficient post-shift sample size for stable downstream regression (BP = 2018: n = 6). Accordingly, we adopt 2015 as the onset of the regime shift for all subsequent analyses, with full numerical results of the breakpoint comparison provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. This choice is consistent with the broader mid-2010s North Pacific regime shift (Fig. 1).

Following the 2015 transition, the SST anomaly record showed remarkable persistence. Except for 2018, when anomalies briefly approached climatological values, all years from 2016 to 2024 exhibited positive winter SST anomalies relative to the 1990–2014 mean. The magnitude of anomalies ranged between + 0.3 and + 1.5 °C, with 2019 and 2020 standing out as extreme warm winters. This indicates not only a one-time change but also a sustained reorganization of the thermal baseline of the WES, consistent with the definition of a climatic regime shift (Fig. 1). This upward shift in the winter SST baseline is quantitatively summarized by the SST boxplot in Fig. 5, which compares the distribution of warm-winter SST anomalies between Group A (pre-2015 warm winters) and Group B (post-2015 warm winters).

To identify the mechanisms underlying this transition, we conducted multiple linear regression analyses using air temperature (AT), wind speed (WS), latent heat flux (LHF), and EKWC strength as explanatory variables, with SST as the response variable. Separate regressions were performed for the pre-shift and post-shift periods. Because buoy-derived LHF was available only from 2002 onward, the regressions including LHF as a predictor were restricted to 2002–2015 (n = 14) for the pre-shift period and 2016–2024 (n = 9) for the post-shift period. Before 2015, the model explained 74.2% of SST variability (R2 = 0.74, p < 0.01). Both AT (β = 0.509, p = 0.02) and EKWC (β = 1.653, p = 0.038) had statistically significant positive effects. The AT–SST association likely reflects coupled air–sea interactions rather than strictly one-way forcing, as SST anomalies can also feed back on near-surface atmospheric conditions through turbulent heat exchange; the EKWC–SST association, by contrast, represents oceanic heat advection and is not subject to such two-way coupling. WS and LHF, despite showing expected signs, did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.5) (Table 1).

After 2015, the overall explanatory power of the model increased to 84.1% (R2 = 0.84). However, the post-shift coefficients must be interpreted with caution: the short post-2015 record (n = 9) combined with strong inter-predictor correlations produced elevated variance inflation factors for AT, WS, and LHF (VIF > 10; Supplementary Table S1), indicating that the individual coefficients of these atmospheric predictors are not stably estimated. To isolate each predictor’s unique association with SST after accounting for collinearity, we computed partial correlations. EKWC (partial r = 0.67, p = 0.048) and LHF (partial r = 0.71, p = 0.031) retained statistically significant unique relationships with SST, while AT (partial r = − 0.38, p = 0.31) and WS (partial r = − 0.52, p = 0.15) did not. Together with the pre-shift results, these findings suggest that EKWC and LHF showed stronger unique associations with SST in the post-shift period after accounting for collinearity, although the short post-shift record limits the precision and robustness of individual coefficient estimates (Table 1; Table S1).

Relative contribution of atmospheric (ΔT) and oceanic (EKWC) factors

To quantify the relative roles of atmospheric and oceanic forcing, we restructured the regression model to include ΔT (SST minus AT, representing air–sea thermal contrast) and EKWC strength as predictors. During the pre-shift period (1990–2015), regression coefficients for ΔT and EKWC were 0.29 and 2.02, respectively, with an overall R2 of 0.26. This indicates that only about a quarter of SST variability was explained, and both atmospheric and oceanic drivers contributed moderately (Fig. S2).

In contrast, during the post-shift period (2016–2024), the coefficient for ΔT slightly declined to 0.23, while the EKWC coefficient was larger (3.58). Two complementary approaches were used to quantify the relative contribution of each predictor. Standardized regression weights indicated that EKWC accounted for 61.9% of the combined standardized contribution in 1990–2015, increasing to 70.0% during 2016–2024. Dominance analysis28, which partitions R2 while accounting for predictor covariance, indicated a consistent direction with an even larger apparent change (68.5% → 90.5%). However, we explicitly note two important limitations: (i) the total explanatory power of the model remained modest (R2 = 0.26 and 0.23 for the two periods), meaning that roughly three-quarters of SST variability is not captured by these two predictors, and (ii) the apparent change in the EKWC coefficient was not statistically significant (Z-test: Z = − 0.54, p = 0.59), reflecting the short post-2016 record (n = 9). We therefore interpret this pattern as a qualitative indication of relatively stronger EKWC-related association after 2015, rather than as evidence for a statistically confirmed structural shift in forcing contributions (Fig. S2).

Expanding correlation analyses provided complementary evidence. The SST–EKWC correlation remained statistically significant (p < 0.05) across the record, while correlations with SST–AT and SST–ΔT showed different temporal patterns (Fig. S3). Correlations between SST and AT remained relatively stable, while SST–ΔT correlations weakened substantially after 2015. Fisher’s Z-tests confirmed that none of these changes were individually significant at the 0.05 level, consistent with the limited post-2015 sample size. Bootstrap validation of the contribution change was also not statistically significant (p = 0.802). Nevertheless, the qualitative direction is consistent across analyses, which we interpret as a tentative indication of stronger EKWC-related association that warrants verification with longer post-shift records.

Intensification and atmospheric linkages of the East Korea warm current

The EKWC itself underwent substantial changes after the mid-2010s. A two-sample t-test confirmed a significant increase in mean EKWC strength after 2015 (p = 0.0018). This intensification was not only a local phenomenon but also coincided with persistent positive SST anomalies. The correlation between EKWC and SST across the full record was r = 0.564 (p = 0.0006), and this relationship remained statistically significant after linear detrending (r = 0.502, p = 0.003), supporting that the association is not solely attributable to shared long-term trends. Because the EKWC metric used here represents western-channel transport rather than the meridional position, pathway, or areal footprint of the EKWC, the stronger post-shift EKWC–SST association should be interpreted as an EKWC-related heat-advection signal that may include both transport variability and changes in the spatial proximity of EKWC-influenced waters to the study region.

Spatial correlation maps between EKWC intensity and mean sea level pressure (SLP) over the Northwest Pacific showed that statistically significant positive correlations were spatially concentrated over the Kuroshio and Kuroshio Extension region around 30°N, while surrounding areas exhibited weak or non-significant relationships (Fig. 2). This spatially focused pattern is physically consistent with the EKWC originating as a branch of the Kuroshio system: interannual variability of EKWC transport is primarily modulated by atmospheric pressure anomalies over the Kuroshio source and extension region, which may influence the partitioning of Kuroshio water into its northward branches. In particular, during winters when pressure anomalies over the Kuroshio Extension favored stronger northward transport, more Kuroshio-origin water was supplied to the WES through the EKWC (Fig. 2).

Meridional shifts of the Kuroshio current as a key driver

Analysis of long-term Kuroshio Current indices revealed a significant northward migration of the mean axis after 2015 (p = 0.0016), even though current intensity did not undergo a regime shift (p = 0.138) (Fig. 2). Correlation analysis indicated that stronger flows tended to coincide with a more northerly axis (r = 0.563, p = 0.0008) (Fig. S4). Meridional shifts of the Kuroshio (MKC) showed a weak positive correlation with WES SST (r = 0.355, p = 0.046), whereas Kuroshio Current Intensity (KCI) showed no statistically significant association. These correlations suggest that the geographical pathway of warm water transport may be more closely associated with WES conditions than the magnitude of transport, although the correlational nature of this result precludes a direct causal interpretation.

SST variability and dominant climate drivers

Across the full observational period, meridional shifts of the Aleutian Low (MIAL) showed the strongest correlation with SST (r = 0.4821) (Fig. S4). Regression models involving MIAL consistently explained the most variance. For instance, Arctic oscillation index (AOI)–MIAL achieved R2 = 0.3261 (p = 0.00181), while Pacific Decadal Oscillation index (PDOI)–MIAL and North Pacific index (NPI)–MIAL also performed comparably. These results identify MIAL as the atmospheric variable most consistently correlated with WES SST variability across analyses, although such associations do not by themselves establish direct causal influence (Table S2).

Period-specific analyses highlighted clear contrasts. From 1990 to 2015, AOI, PDOI, and MIAL were the most prominent atmospheric predictors, with the AOI–PDOI–MIAL model achieving R2 = 0.4594 (p = 0.00317). This indicates that hemispheric-scale atmospheric circulation and decadal ocean–atmosphere variability were jointly associated with SST variability during the earlier regime. In the post-shift period, no single atmospheric index provided a statistically robust explanation of SST variability (all single-index p > 0.10). The AOI–EAWMI–MIAL combination showed moderate apparent explanatory power (R2 = 0.41), but the model was not statistically significant (p = 0.42), likely reflecting the short post-shift sample size. These results therefore suggest a weakening of PDO-related associations after 2015, but they do not provide statistically robust evidence for strengthened atmospheric-index control during the short post-shift period (Table S2). Across the full record, MIAL remained one of the most consistent atmospheric correlates of SST variability, highlighting the potential relevance of Aleutian Low meridional positioning.

Atmospheric pattern transitions across the SST regime shift

To examine large-scale atmospheric linkages of WES winter SST, we first computed spatial correlations between SST anomalies and Northern Hemisphere atmospheric fields for the full 1990–2024 period. SST correlated positively with SLP near Korea and the KE, but negatively east of the Aleutians, forming a zonal dipole. Correlations with wind speed revealed a latitudinal contrast, with negative values around 30° N—where weaker winds were linked to warming—and positive values north of 45° N (Fig. 1).

To further clarify structural changes across the mid-2010s regime shift, we performed composite anomaly analyses for winters with above-normal WES SST before and after 2015. Prior to 2015 (1990, 1992, 1995, 1998, 1999, 2000, 2002, 2004, 2007, 2008, 2009, hereafter Group A), warm events were accompanied by widespread positive AT anomalies across Eurasia, including Siberia, reflecting a predominantly continental-scale warming pattern. WS anomalies displayed moderate weakening near 30–35° N and strengthening at higher latitudes, while SLP anomalies exhibited a clear east–west dipole across the Aleutians, consistent with longitudinal modulation of the AL (Fig. 3).

After 2015 (2016, 2017, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, hereafter Group B), the AT anomaly pattern shifted toward an oceanic focus, with weaker anomalies over Siberia and stronger warming over lower-latitude marine regions, notably the East China Sea and the western subtropical Pacific. WS anomalies displayed a strengthened latitudinal dipole, with pronounced weakening in the 30–35° N band adjacent to the Korean Peninsula and enhanced strengthening near the Aleutians. SLP anomalies transitioned to a meridional structure, with reduced Arctic anomalies, lower pressures around the Aleutians, and higher pressures across the central North Pacific and the Korean Peninsula. This evolution from a zonal to a meridional anomaly configuration indicates a fundamental reorganization of atmospheric wave activity and air–sea coupling processes in the post-2015 regime (Fig. 3). These SLP, AT, and WS composite transitions are schematically synthesized in Fig. 5 together with the AO, Siberian High, and oceanic (EKWC, Kuroshio) components within a broader PDO-to-Victoria Mode framework.

Correlation patterns among key ocean–atmosphere variables

Pearson correlation matrices showed statistically significant associations among several variables. EKWC (r = 0.56, p < 0.01) and MIAL (r = 0.47, p < 0.01) were strongly correlated with SST. MKC also correlated with SST (r = 0.35, p < 0.05) and covaried with EKWC (r = 0.45) and MIAL (r = 0.40), consistent with coherent covariation among these indices. These correlations indicate association rather than causality; the physical attribution is developed through the dynamical arguments in the Discussion (Fig. S4).

Expanding cumulative correlations highlighted temporal evolution in the associations between winter SST and candidate drivers. During 2000–2015, SST was strongly correlated with atmospheric indices, particularly AOI (r = 0.68, p = 0.004). After 2015, the AOI–SST relationship weakened substantially (r = + 0.04), whereas the EKWC–SST association became more apparent in recent expanding-window analyses, reaching approximately r = 0.62 for 2018–2024. MKC also showed a stronger recent association with SST. These results suggest a post-2015 weakening of atmospheric-index associations and a relatively stronger recent linkage between SST and oceanic variables, although the short post-shift record limits statistical robustness (Fig. S3).

Long-term variation of chlorophyll-a and phytoplankton size composition

Annual mean surface chlorophyll-a (chl-a) concentrations between 2003 and 2024 ranged from 0.57 to 1.00 mg m−3, with no significant trend. The mean values before and after 2015 (0.77 ± 0.11 vs. 0.77 ± 0.07 mg m−3) were statistically indistinguishable (p > 0.05). This suggests that total phytoplankton biomass has remained relatively stable despite substantial physical changes (Fig. 4). However, phytoplankton size class (PSC) revealed striking shifts. Pico-phytoplankton (pico) dominated areas increased steadily (slope = + 1.4% per year, r = 0.66, p < 0.01), while nano-phytoplankton (nano) areas declined symmetrically (slope = − 1.4% per year, r = 0.66, p < 0.01). Micro-phytoplankton (micro) remained nearly constant. Comparing the pre-shift and post-shift periods, pico-dominated areas increased from 59.0% during 2003–2015 to 73.8% during 2016–2024 (+ 14.8%), whereas nano-dominated areas decreased from 38.5% to 22.1% (− 16.4%). These shifts in community structure, despite stable total chl-a, imply a profound reorganization of lower-trophic-level dynamics, likely linked to enhanced stratification and potentially altered nutrient supply associated with intensified EKWC inflow and suppressed vertical mixing (Fig. 4). Stratification–PSC relationships were marginal but consistent with the proposed mechanism, particularly in the southern sub-domain after 2015, where pico-phytoplankton showed a positive association with stratification (r = + 0.70, p = 0.053) and nano-phytoplankton showed a negative association (r = − 0.68, p = 0.062; Table S3).

Regime-dependent phytoplankton–climate relationships

Before 2015, chl-a and micro exhibited significant positive correlations with the PDOI (chl-a: r = 0.65, p = 0.017; micro: r = 0.59, p = 0.033), while nano and pico showed significant associations with the NPI, but with opposite directions (nano: r = − 0.56, p = 0.045; pico: r = 0.64, p = 0.017), suggesting that basin-scale climate variability was more strongly associated with phytoplankton variability before 2015. In contrast, after 2015, these large-scale linkages weakened or disappeared; chl-a showed no significant association with any index, micro was significantly negatively correlated with LHF (r = − 0.70, p = 0.035), and nano was significantly negatively correlated with the EKWC (r = − 0.71, p = 0.032), while pico exhibited no significant correlations, indicating a transition toward regional oceanic and air–sea flux controls (Table S5). This regime-dependent shift in phytoplankton–climate coupling was confirmed by multivariate tests: PERMANOVA detected a significant difference between pre- and post-shift structures (F = 3.05, R2 = 0.132, p = 0.007), and a Procrustes test comparing PCA loading structures also indicated a significant reorganization (M2 = 0.845, p = 0.011; Table S6). Consistently, PCA biplots showed that phytoplankton variables clustered with PDOI and NPI in the pre-shift period but aligned more closely with SST, EKWC, and LHF in the post-shift period. Standardized multiple regression further quantified these shifts: in the pre-shift period, chl-a and micro were mainly explained by PDOI, while nano and pico were best accounted for by NPI, with moderate explanatory power across models (R2 = 0.39–0.61); however, in the post-shift period, PDOI and NPI were no longer retained, and instead chl-a and micro were linked to LHF, AOI, and KCI, whereas nano and pico were explained by EKWC, SST, and MKC, with comparable model fits (R2 = 0.57–0.61), suggesting that phytoplankton variability became more closely associated with regional processes such as ocean currents and air–sea fluxes after 2015 (Table S4).

Discussion

Atmospheric drivers of WES winter SST variability

The long-term variability of winter SST in the WES is strongly associated with large-scale atmospheric and oceanic variability. Among these, the AO and the meridional position of the AL consistently emerged as prominent correlates of interannual SST anomalies, underscoring their close association with regional SST variability. Regression analyses repeatedly identified the AOI and MIAL as key explanatory variables, highlighting their fundamental contribution to regional SST variability8,17,29. This finding is consistent with earlier studies that link the AO to interannual–decadal SST changes and the occurrence of marine heatwaves (MHWs) in the Northwest Pacific8,17. During positive AO phases, anomalous anticyclonic wind stress curl develops over the WES, driving surface convergence and Ekman downwelling, which suppresses the upward supply of cold subsurface waters17. This reduced entrainment allows anomalously warm waters to persist, thereby increasing the likelihood of MHWs8. In addition, AO-related modulation of the SH exerts a critical influence on East Asian winter monsoon (EAWM) strength29,30. Positive AO phases strengthen the polar vortex and confine cold air to high latitudes, weakening SH development, whereas negative AO phases weaken the vortex and promote southward cold-air intrusions, strengthening the SH and reinforcing cold advection into the WES8. These AO–polar vortex transitions thus constitute a key dynamical pathway linking the SH, EAWM variability, and WES SST. The AL plays a complementary role by regulating monsoon strength through its central position. When the AL shifts southward, SLP over the KE region decreases, enhancing the SLP gradient between the SH and KE and driving stronger northwesterly advection of cold, dry air from Siberia into the WES30,10. Conversely, northward AL displacement weakens the pressure gradient, reduces cold-air advection, and favors warm SST anomalies1. Together, AO state transitions, SH intensity changes, and AL displacements strongly modulate winter monsoon dynamics and SST variability in the WES.

The 2015 regime shift and transition from PDO- to Victoria Mode-related variability

A regime shift in WES SST was identified around 2015, after which winter SSTs remained predominantly above the climatological mean in nearly all years. This “WES regime shift” coincided with basin-scale atmospheric–oceanic reorganizations, including strengthening of the East Korea Warm Current (EKWC), northward migration of the Kuroshio axis, and displacement of the AL. The statistical associations between SST and candidate climate indices also changed across the shift. Before 2015, AO-, PDO-, and MIAL-related models showed stronger explanatory power, whereas after 2015 no single atmospheric index provided a statistically robust explanation of SST variability, likely because of the short post-shift record31,32. These results are consistent with the broader framework of North Pacific climate regime shifts (CRS), which have historically occurred in 1976–77, 1988–89, and 1998–9916,33. Earlier CRS were largely PDO-centered, accompanied by AL intensity shifts and Pacific–North American (PNA) teleconnection patterns34–38. PDO variability, as the leading EOF of North Pacific SST anomalies, explains ~ 25% of variance and strongly couples with AL intensity via ENSO–PNA linkages34,35. Intensification of the AL during positive PDO phases enhances central North Pacific wind stress curl, alters thermocline depth, and generates westward-propagating Rossby waves, ultimately modulating Kuroshio Current (KC) strength and KE position39–41.

By contrast, the post-2015 CRS was distinct, reflecting a reduced association with PDO-related variability and a stronger apparent role of the Victoria Mode (VM)18,35,42,43. The VM, as the second EOF of North Pacific SST anomalies, accounts for ~ 12–15% of variance and exhibits a subtropical–midlatitude dipole structure35. Its variability is closely linked to the West Pacific (WP) teleconnection, characterized by an anomalous high over the western subtropics and a low over the high-latitude North Pacific38. Sustained VM activity drives north–south AL displacements, reorganizes wind stress curl, and produces thermocline changes that propagate to the KE via westward Rossby wave transmission37,39–41. Unlike the PDO, which is often associated with KC intensity through AL strength, the VM has been linked to meridional variability in the KC position32,42. Our findings are consistent with the interpretation that, after 2015, meridional AL displacement may have contributed to wind-stress-curl reorganization and northward displacement of the KC–KE system, even without strong AL intensification40,41. These results are consistent with a possible transition in WES variability from stronger PDO-related associations toward stronger VM-related associations (Fig. 5).

Role of oceanic processes and boundary-current variability

The strengthened role of oceanic processes is further supported by satellite-derived MHW analyses, which revealed that cumulative MHW intensity in the WES increased by 29.6 °C days per decade from 1982 to 2020, more than twice the global mean rate44. These trends are consistent with enhanced contributions from EKWC and Kuroshio variability, although surface fluxes may also contribute. The regression and partial-correlation results suggest that EKWC-related variability became more apparent after 2015, although the short post-shift record and collinearity among atmospheric variables preclude definitive attribution. This interpretation is consistent with long-term heat budget analyses demonstrating that in boundary current regions, horizontal and vertical heat advection dominates SST variability, while air–sea fluxes often damp anomalies rather than generate them45–47. For the EKWC, enhanced advection redistributes heat along its pathway, producing regional contrasts in SST trends: acceleration in some regions, suppression in others48. These findings suggest an increasing association between current-driven variability and winter SST changes in the WES. In the WES, the EKWC flows poleward along the Korean coast as a narrow boundary current, and its spatial displacement is constrained by coastal topography. Previous work2 has shown that variations in EKWC transport intensity are directly linked to changes in water-column structure, including salinity and stratification in the inshore area, indicating that transport strength is a major modulator of regional heat delivery. Nevertheless, the EKWC metric used here represents western-channel volume transport and does not explicitly resolve the meridional position, pathway, or areal footprint of the current. We therefore acknowledge that the stronger post-shift EKWC–SST association may partly reflect changes in the spatial proximity of EKWC-influenced waters to the study region, in addition to transport variability. Separating these contributions will require spatially resolved current observations or ocean reanalysis in future work.

The role of atmospheric modes after 2015 should not be overlooked. AO spatial patterns weakened during warm years, which may be linked to the quasi-biennial oscillation (QBO). Stratospheric conditions associated with QBO phases modulate polar vortex strength and planetary wave propagation, thereby influencing the AO–AL connection49. During westerly QBO phases, stronger polar vortices enhance AO–AL teleconnections, while easterly phases weaken them. Thus, the reduced AO influence on SST variability after 2015 may partly reflect stratospheric modulation. Nevertheless, ocean circulation changes, particularly EKWC-related variability, emerged as a prominent correlate of post-2015 SST anomalies.

Ecological implications: PSC reorganization under enhanced stratification

The ecological implications of this structural shift are significant. In the WES, where terrestrial inputs are minimal, winter hydrography and stratification strongly precondition nutrient availability and spring bloom dynamics50,51. Enhanced stratification under warming shoals the mixed layer, suppresses nutrient entrainment, and shifts phytoplankton composition toward smaller size classes adapted to oligotrophic conditions25,26,52. Our analysis revealed that while long-term chl-a biomass exhibited no significant trend during 2003–2024, phytoplankton size composition reorganized: pico-dominant areas expanded while nano dominance declined, especially after 2015. These patterns align with global reports of smaller phytoplankton dominance under warming53–55 and with PSC-based analyses in Korean waters showing expansion of pico-dominant areas in the WES23,24,56. The potential linkage between SST-driven stratification and nutrient supply has been further suggested by recent studies. As demonstrated in ref.11, post-2015 winter stratification intensification in the Northwest Pacific was primarily driven by rising SST, which steepened vertical density gradients and enhanced nitrate accumulation below the pycnocline. This suggests that surface warming may reorganize subsurface nutrient reservoirs and constrain upward nutrient supply, thereby potentially altering pre-bloom conditions. Our own analysis provides suggestive support for this coupled pathway. The correlation between winter stratification and pico-dominance became stronger after 2015, particularly in the southern sub-domain where EKWC-derived warm waters are expected to exert greater hydrographic influence (Table S3). The weaker signal in the northern sub-domain may reflect the more limited influence of EKWC inflow and the presence of colder, less-stratified waters, suggesting that the SST–stratification–PSC linkage is more apparent in the zone influenced by Kuroshio-origin transport, whereas total chl-a appears less directly related to this pathway. Our findings are consistent with these insights and suggest that enhanced EKWC transport coincided with PSC reorganization, although the role of winter hydrographic shifts as precursors of lower-trophic-level dynamics should be further tested with longer records. While multiple lines of evidence support a post-2015 transition, short post-shift samples limit formal detection power. We mitigated this with robust tests and persistence diagnostics, but future work should extend the record, incorporate ocean reanalyses for full heat-budget closure, and explore hierarchical or Bayesian change-point frameworks to jointly estimate timing and driver weights.

Synthesis, limitations, and future directions

In summary, the 2015 regime shift in the WES represents a transition toward stronger oceanic association of winter SST variability (Fig. 5). Before 2015, SST anomalies were primarily linked to AO- and PDO-related atmospheric circulation, whereas after 2015, VM-related meridional shifts of the AL and EKWC-related variability became more prominent associations. These physical changes were associated with enhanced winter stratification, potentially altered nutrient supply pathways, and reshaped phytoplankton size structure. The WES thus exemplifies how coupled atmosphere–ocean reorganizations in the North Pacific propagate into boundary current systems and ultimately into ecosystem dynamics (Fig. 5). Sustained, long-term monitoring of stratification and phytoplankton size composition will be essential to assess ecosystem resilience under continued climate change. More broadly, the identified regime shift in the WES reflects basin-scale Pacific decadal reorganization that is consistent with known teleconnections involving the PDO and AL, with potential implications for cross-basin climate variability. These results suggest the potential relevance of marginal sea processes in the northwest Pacific to Pacific–Atlantic inter-basin linkages, underscoring the need to incorporate such regional dynamics into decadal climate prediction frameworks.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the post-regime-shift period (2016–2024) is relatively short, which constrains the statistical power to detect structural changes; however, the observed trends remain consistent with our hypotheses and conceptual framework. Second, the ecological responses were inferred from phytoplankton size-class composition derived from satellite observations, which may not fully capture in situ community dynamics. Previous studies, however, have demonstrated significant correspondence between satellite-derived size classes and field measurements, lending confidence to this approach. Third, ecosystem changes in the WES were assessed mainly in relation to surface SST, whereas ecological restructuring cannot be fully explained by upper-ocean thermal variability alone. Fourth, EKWC variability was represented by western-channel transport intensity, whereas the meridional position, latitudinal shift, pathway, and areal footprint of the EKWC within the WES were not explicitly quantified. Therefore, the stronger post-shift EKWC–SST association may reflect not only increased transport but also changes in the spatial proximity of EKWC-influenced waters to the study region. Future work using spatially resolved current observations or ocean reanalysis should separate transport-intensity effects from EKWC pathway or footprint changes. Fifth, our framework targets interannual variability in the mean winter state and does not explicitly resolve sub-seasonal SST persistence or the coupled air–sea feedbacks through which SST anomalies may in turn modify atmospheric variables such as AT and LHF; the reported relationships should therefore be interpreted as interannual, winter-mean associations rather than strictly one-way forcing. Future studies integrating water-column structure, nutrient dynamics, and primary production from field observations will be required to better evaluate the mechanisms of ecosystem change.

Conclusion

Our results provide evidence for a winter SST regime shift in the WES around 2015, after which anomalies remained persistently positive relative to the pre-onset baseline. Before 2015, SST variability reflected a balance between atmospheric forcing and oceanic advection, whereas after 2015, oceanic processes—particularly EKWC-related variability and a northward Kuroshio displacement—became more prominent, suggesting stronger oceanic associations in the post-shift period. This transition is consistent with a shift from PDO-related to Victoria Mode-related teleconnection patterns, accompanied by meridional reorganization of the AL and altered regional air–sea coupling. Despite stable chl-a biomass, phytoplankton communities underwent structural reorganization toward pico-dominance, consistent with enhanced stratification and potentially altered nutrient supply. Collectively, these findings suggest that teleconnection-driven ocean circulation may increasingly shape both physical and ecological variability in this marginal sea, underscoring its sentinel role for Pacific decadal variability and the importance of sustained monitoring for prediction and ecosystem resilience.

Data and methods

Sea surface temperature (SST) data

SST data were obtained from the Operational Sea Surface Temperature and Sea Ice Analysis (OSTIA) product developed by the UK Met Office. OSTIA provides daily global-scale SST and sea ice fields on a 0.05° latitude–longitude grid, and the dataset is reanalyzed for public use based on field observations from the International Comprehensive Ocean–Atmosphere Data Set (ICOADS). These high-resolution foundation SST fields combine satellite observations and in situ measurements through optimal interpolation techniques.

In this study, daily SST values were averaged into monthly means, and then aggregated to generate winter seasonal averages, defined as the mean of January, February, and March (JFM) for each year from 1990 to 2024. Spatially, SST data were extracted from the EKWC-influenced coastal sector of the WES, bounded by 36°–38°N latitude and 127°–131°E longitude (Fig. 6). This region was used to construct the winter SST index and is distinguished from the broader East/Japan Sea regional context discussed in the Introduction.

Atmospheric variables in the Northern Hemisphere

To characterize large-scale atmospheric conditions influencing wintertime SST variability in the WES, we used three key surface variables from the ERA5 reanalysis dataset: 2 m air temperature (T2M), 10 m wind speed (U10, V10), and SLP. All variables were obtained from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) ERA5 monthly averaged data on a 0.25° × 0.25° horizontal resolution grid57.

For each variable, we extracted data over the Northern Hemisphere (20°–90°N) and computed seasonal winter means by averaging monthly values from December, January, and February (DJF) for each year during the 1990–2024 period. The resulting 35-year winter climatology was used in spatial correlation analyses and compositing to assess large-scale atmospheric patterns associated with interannual SST anomalies in the WES.

Latent heat flux (LHF) data

To estimate wintertime LHF, we used daily ocean buoy observations from a KMA open-ocean buoy located ~ 88 km east of Donghae (37.53°N, 130.00°E; station 22105), obtained through the KMA Open Data Portal (https://data.kma.go.kr/) for the WES. The dataset included wind speed, sea-level pressure, relative humidity, air temperature, and SST from January to March for the period 2002–2024 (Fig. 6). Because buoy LHF observations are available only from 2002 onward, regression analyses involving LHF were restricted to the 2002–2024 period, while all other analyses used the full 1990–2024 record. To assess the spatial representativeness of this single-point buoy measurement, we cross-validated the buoy-derived JFM LHF against ERA5 monthly averaged reanalysis on single levels57 using the surface latent heat flux variable averaged over ocean grid cells within the study domain (36°–38°N, 127°–131°E). The two records exhibited a statistically significant positive correlation in interannual variability (r = 0.50, p = 0.015) over the common period, supporting the representativeness of the buoy record for the regional domain.

Climate index

To investigate the climatic drivers of SST variability in the WES, we selected five large-scale climate indices commonly used in studies of North Pacific climate variability: the AOI, PDOI, NPI, EAWMI, and SHI. These indices represent major modes of atmospheric circulation and pressure systems that influence regional oceanographic conditions such as SST, SLP, and winter monsoon strength10.

The AOI represents the leading mode of sea level pressure variability in the Northern Hemisphere (20°–90°N, 180°E–180°W) and is indicative of the strength of the polar vortex and the westerly jet58. The PDOI is a long-term mode of Pacific SST variability, calculated as the leading principal component of monthly SST anomalies in the North Pacific Ocean poleward of 20°N, after removing the global mean SST anomaly9. The NPI is defined as the area-weighted mean SLP over the region 30°–65°N and 160°E–140°W and is used as an index for the strength of the AL34. The EAWMI is calculated from zonal wind speeds at 300 hPa over East Asia (27.5°–37.5°N, 110°–170°E) and serves as a proxy for the intensity of the East Asian winter monsoon29. Finally, the SHI quantifies the strength of the SH based on winter mean SLP averaged over continental Asia (40°–60°N, 70°–120°E), reflecting continental cooling and its associated anticyclonic system59.

Detection of Kuroshio current intensity and latitudinal shift

To assess both the intensity and meridional displacement of the Kuroshio Current over the period 1993–2024, we utilized delayed-time satellite altimetry products provided by AVISO (Archiving, Validation, and Interpretation of Satellite Oceanographic data), which include gridded fields of absolute dynamic topography (ADT) and geostrophic surface velocities (ugos and vgos). The data were retrieved at a spatial resolution of 1/4° and daily temporal resolution, and then averaged over the winter season (January to March) to construct annual mean fields.

Detection of the meridional and zonal position of the Aleutian low

To estimate the meridional position of the AL in boreal winter, we used monthly SLP fields from the ERA5 reanalysis dataset provided by ECMWF, covering the period from 1990 to 2024. Winter averages were constructed using data from December of the previous year and January–February of the current year, resulting in seasonal DJF means.

From the full SLP field, we selected a North Pacific domain spanning 30°N–60°N and 150°E–210°E, which encompasses the typical range of the AL. Within this region, we extracted all grid points where MSLP was less than or equal to 1005 hPa, a threshold commonly used to represent the AL core38,60. Among these grid points, we selected the lowest 5% of MSLP values, which best capture the core low-pressure center of the AL while minimizing the influence of surrounding synoptic systems.

For each year, the mean latitude and longitude of these lowest-pressure grid cells were calculated to define the annual center position of the AL. The mean latitude was used as the MIAL, and the mean longitude as the zonal position index of AL (ZIAL). This approach is consistent with prior methodologies and provides a robust, objective representation of the interannual shift in the AL’s geographic location.

Identifying climatic drivers of SST variability before and after 2015

To investigate whether the dominant climatic drivers of SST variability in the WES changed across the regime shift year, a period-specific analytical framework was employed. Based on the identification of 2015 as the onset year of a sustained SST anomaly transition, the full observation period (1990–2024) was divided into two subperiods: pre-shift (1990–2015; Group A) and post-shift (2016–2024; Group B). A total of seven large-scale climate indices were selected as candidate predictors representing distinct atmospheric systems and modes of variability: AOI, EAWMI, NPI, PDOI, SHI, MIAL, ZIAL. These indices were chosen to capture polar teleconnections, mid-latitude pressure variability, and decadal-scale ocean–atmosphere interactions across the North Pacific. For each subperiod and the full period, Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the linear relationships between annual SST anomalies and each climate index. Only statistically significant correlations (p < 0.05) were retained for interpretation. To quantify the joint influence of multiple climate indices on SST variability, multiple linear regression models were constructed with SST as the dependent variable. All possible combinations of two to six predictors were tested. Model performance was evaluated using adjusted coefficients of determination (adjusted R2) and significance levels (p-values). This step enabled identification of optimal predictor sets and assessment of explanatory power across different levels of model complexity. To examine temporal shifts in the role of individual indices, interperiod comparisons of regression coefficients were conducted using Z-tests for independent estimates. In addition, expanding window correlation analysis was employed to visualize the evolving strength of relationships between SST and its potential drivers throughout the study period. Fisher’s Z-transformation was applied to test for statistically significant differences in correlation coefficients before and after 2015 (Fig. S3). Collinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), where VIF < 5 indicates acceptable collinearity, 5–10 moderate concern, and > 10 high collinearity. To isolate the unique association of each predictor with SST after accounting for the remaining predictors, partial correlations were additionally computed by regressing each pair of variables on the other predictors and correlating the residuals. Regression coefficients in Table 1 are reported in their raw (unstandardized) form with variables expressed in their native physical units; standardized contributions are reported separately in Supplementary Fig. S2b.

Satellite data and phytoplankton size classes (PSCs) algorithm

The PSC algorithm used in the WES was based on a deep neural network (DNN) model61 further applied to analyze the long-term patterns of PSCs in the littoral seas of Korea24. The model architecture consisted of eight fully connected layers. Input variables included SST, total suspended solids (TSS), and total chl-a concentration, while output variables were the fractional contributions of micro (> 20 μm), nano (2–20 μm), and pico (< 2 μm) for each pixel. The reliability of this DNN-based classification was rigorously validated against an independent test dataset (n = 291), yielding an overall accuracy of 70.5%. This performance represents a substantial improvement over existing regional models, such as the spectral-based Aph algorithm (54.3%) and the three-component model (13.4%), when applied to the complex optical environments of the Korean littoral seas61.

To obtain the input data for the PSC algorithm, satellite-based ocean color data were acquired from MODIS-Aqua Level-3 products (4 km × 4 km resolution) provided by the NASA Goddard Space Flight Center (https://oceandata.sci.gsfc.nasa.gov/). We used monthly mean SST, chl-a, and remote sensing reflectance at 555 nm (Rrs555). TSS was not directly available from NOAA, so it was estimated using an empirical algorithm specifically designed and validated for Korean waters62. Rrs555 was used due to its strong sensitivity to water quality parameters and its widespread application in regional optical water quality assessments62.

The analysis focused on the region between 36°–38°N and 127°–131°E. For PSCs, the annual variability was derived from monthly data (2003–2024) by determining the most frequent (mode) size class for each pixel and then calculating the proportion of each class within the defined spatial domain. For chl-a, annual means were computed from the corresponding monthly data over the same period.

Winter stratification intensity in the WES

Winter stratification intensity was obtained from the hydrographic dataset described in ref.11, in which stratification was quantified using the Brunt–Väisälä frequency (N2) derived from in situ temperature and salinity profiles archived by the Korea Oceanographic Data Center (KODC; https://www.nifs.go.kr/kodc/eng/index.kodc). The hydrographic dataset covered February observations from 1990 to 2023. For the stratification–PSC analysis, only the overlapping period between the stratification and satellite PSC datasets was used (2003–2023). To assess spatial heterogeneity within the WES, the domain was divided into northern (approximately 37°–38°N) and southern (approximately 36°–37°N) sub-domains, and annual winter stratification indices were compared with PSC proportions and total chl-a over this common period (Supplementary Table S3). Because these stratification data were derived from a previously published hydrographic analysis11, the results were interpreted as supportive evidence for a hydrographic mechanism rather than as direct evidence of nutrient limitation.
