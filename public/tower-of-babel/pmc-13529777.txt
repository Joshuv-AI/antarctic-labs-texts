Multicenter fine annotated surgical video dataset for minimally invasive glaucoma surgery
Authors: Deming Wang, Yinhang Zhang, Yirui Li, Kai Zhou, Zefeng Yang, Zeng You, Dilimulati Xiaokaiti, Fengqi Zhou, Yu Chen, Xiaoyan Li, Xiaowei Yan, Guihua Xu, Zhixuan Wang, Jiaxuan Jiang, Lin Xie, Xiaomin Zhu, Ling Jin, Zhenyu Wang, Ying Han, Guangxian Tang, Sujie Fan, Fei Li, Mingkui Tan, Xiulan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMinimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) presents substantial challenges due to its intricate procedures and high technical demands, necessitating robust surgical navigation systems. The development of these systems is highly dependent on the availability of precisely annotated datasets. Addressing this need, we have created the first multicenter, large-scale, fine-annotated surgical video dataset for MIGS. This dataset includes millions of frames, extensively annotated with both surgical instruments and anatomical structures. The annotations are organized into two primary tasks: Task I, which involves phase recognition to identify different stages of the surgery, and Task II, which focuses on semantic segmentation of surgical instruments and anatomical details. By providing a foundational resource for training computer vision models, our dataset aims to facilitate the development of advanced computer-assisted interventions that can improve the precision and safety of glaucoma surgeries.

Introduction

Glaucoma is a complex and multifactorial disease characterized by the apoptosis of retinal ganglion cells and their axons, leading to irreversible blindness1–3. As the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, glaucoma affects approximately 60 million people globally, with a projected increase to 110 million by 20404–6. Surgery is one of the primary treatment options for glaucoma, and due to the disease’s inherent complexity and refractoriness, there are numerous surgical procedures available, each targeting different mechanisms7–14. In recent years, with the continuous evolution of glaucoma surgery, Goniotomy (GT) has emerged as a type of minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) that has been proven to be an effective treatment for glaucoma by removing the trabecular meshwork (TM) and the inner wall of the Schlemm’s canal10,12,15–23. However, despite GT’s advantages over traditional trabeculectomy, including fewer complications, the procedure remains technically challenging and requires a significant learning curve24,25. The main difficulty lies in identifying the TM, which is a crucial yet challenging step in GT, due to the intricate anatomy of the anterior chamber angle and the high individual morphological variability26–28. This requires GT surgeons to have a thorough understanding of the anterior chamber and trabecular meshwork anatomy, as incorrect identification of the TM can lead to complications such as anterior chamber hemorrhage or failure to achieve the desired reduction in intraocular pressure28–32.

The rapid advancement of computer vision technology has led to its increasing application in medical surgery navigation33,34. By enabling real-time analysis and decision-making support, computer-assisted interventions (CAI) have shown great promise in augmenting the surgeon’s situational awareness with real-time anatomical overlays, accelerating intraoperative tissue identification, and providing early warning systems for adverse events, thereby improving surgical outcomes35–37. However, ophthalmic surgery poses unique challenges due to the delicate and intricate nature of the eye38. The small size of the eye and the proximity of its structures require extremely high precision, making surgical learning curves steep and increasing the risk of complications38–40. To develop effective CAI systems for ophthalmic surgery, large-scale annotated datasets are essential41,42. Unfortunately, such datasets are scarce, and to the best of our knowledge, there is no publicly available annotated dataset for glaucoma surgery. This lack of data hinders the development of deep learning-based approaches and frameworks that can enhance glaucoma surgery outcomes.

In this study, we present the first multicenter, large-scale, fine-annotated surgical video dataset for minimally invasive glaucoma surgery43. Our dataset includes a comprehensive annotation of multiple surgical steps, instruments, and anatomical structures, providing a robust foundation for the development of deep learning-based surgical navigation systems. We performed exhaustive annotations of each surgical step in the videos (Task I: phase recognition) and key surgical instruments and anatomical structures (Task II: semantic segmentation). This dataset is designed to support the development of computer vision models for surgical workflow understanding, instrument and anatomical structure recognition, and intraoperative guidance. To demonstrate its utility, we further developed a prototype goniotomy navigation system (GNS) based on the dataset.

Methods

Data collection

This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (ZOC) (ID: 2025KYPJ039). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. This study was conducted in strict adherence to the medical ethics principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the guidelines of the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS). All personally identifiable information in the videos (e.g., patient ID, sex, age) was removed prior to analysis. Owing to the retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All data were fully anonymized prior to public release and contain no personally identifiable information. The study was registered at Researchregistry.com (Identifier: researchregistry11279).

A study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Between November 2020 and September 2022, we collected surgical videos of 120° GT from three centers: ZOC (n = 163), Handan City Eye Hospital (HCEH) (n = 32), and Shijiazhuang People’s Hospital (SPH) (n = 11). Data diversity across centers arose from differences in instruments, surgeon preferences and experience, ocular characteristics (iris color, pupil size, TM color, iris root insertion), and image-acquisition parameters (illumination, magnification, contrast, hue, saturation).

Dataset construction

The ZOC dataset comprises surgical videos of 163 cases of 120° GT surgeries. These videos were recorded using an AG OPMI Lumera 700 surgical microscope (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) integrated with the AIAVS-ST12 surgical video recording platform (AIIST Co. Ltd., Suzhou, Jiangsu, China), delivering a resolution of 1920 × 1080 at 25 frames per second (fps). Validation data were collected from two other tertiary-level medical facilities. The HCEH provided 32 videos of 120° GT surgeries recorded using AG OPMI Lumera T surgical microscope (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) or M844/M820 Microsystems AG (Leica, Schweiz) integrated with HDVR surgical video recording platform (JKQ Technology Co. Ltd., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China), while the SPH obtained 11 cases recorded using an AG OPMI Lumera T surgical microscope (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) integrated with HV-UHD301 surgical video recording platform (SYHC Technology Co. Ltd, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China). This multicenter acquisition enabled evaluation across diverse microscopes, recording systems, and surgical environments.

Quality control used a peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR)44 threshold of < 20 decibels (dB), excluding 15, 4, and 1 videos from ZOC, HCEH, and SPH, respectively; the final dataset comprised 186 videos (ZOC: 148; HCEH: 28; SPH: 10).

Only one eye of each patient was enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients were (1) with a clinical diagnosis of primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG); (2) treated with 120° GT surgery, which was recorded with a clear operation video and accompanied by a complete medical history; (3) with an open angle on gonioscopy; and (4) at least 18 years of age. The exclusion criteria encompassed: patients with (1) eyes that underwent prior laser treatment or ocular surgery, particularly other types of intraocular pressure (IOP)-lowering surgery; (2) coexisting ophthalmic conditions potentially affecting clarity during video capture and surgery, including corneal endothelial decompensation, corneal edema, corneal opacity, or iridocorneal endothelial syndrome; (3) eyes diagnosed with other types of glaucoma (e.g., angle-closure glaucoma, traumatic glaucoma, congenital glaucoma); (4) severe systemic disease. POAG was defined as previously described45,46 and independently reviewed/confirmed by a glaucoma specialist.

To minimize inter-surgeon variability, all procedures within each center were performed by a single glaucoma specialist, each of whom was qualified to independently perform GT and had over 10 years of experience in GT surgery, and the recorded expert maneuvers served as the reference for annotation and model training. Annotators referred to a practice recommendation prepared by five clinical experts (X.Z., G.T., S.F., F.L., and L.X.), which enumerated candidate surgical instruments and ocular structures by phase. To ensure high-quality labeling, we implemented a structured training and quality control protocol. Before formal annotation, each of three annotators independently graded approximately 5% of videos (9 cases; 261,420 frames for Task I and 1,542 frames for Task II) as a trial set. A senior glaucoma specialist then performed in-depth quality control, followed by cross-verification to assess inter-rater agreement. Agreement for each annotator was quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC); formal annotation proceeded only when PCC > 0.9. Disagreements or ambiguities during either the trial or formal phase were adjudicated in consensus meetings involving the three ophthalmologists and the senior specialist; cases without consensus were excluded.

For Task I (phase recognition), we annotated 186 untrimmed 120° GT videos (4,986,546 frames) to train the phase recognizer. From ZOC, 148 cases were randomly split into a training set of 111 videos (2,961,748 frames) and a validation set of 37 videos (1,081,067 frames). The external test set comprised 38 videos (943,731 frames) from HCEH (n = 28) and SPH (n = 10). For real-time processing, each full-length GT video was treated as a continuous stream, where the current input frame, along with a cache of recent frames, formed a stream buffer for phase inference. Figure 2A summarizes the eight surgical phases and provides examples. For each video, annotators completed a comma-delimited file with drop-down lists of predefined actions. To improve efficiency at scale, phase labeling focused on start and end frames; given temporal continuity and precise phase definitions, each intermediate frame was uniquely assigned to one of eight phases: Corneal Incision, Carbachol Injection, OVD Injection, Gonioscopy Observation, Incision of Schlemm’s Canal, OVD Irrigation/Aspiration, Wound Closure, and Other/Invalid (e.g., out-of-field or blank frames). Because visualization of the TM requires instrumental assistance, Incision of Schlemm’s Canal is temporally nested within gonioscopy observation.

For Task II (instrument/structure segmentation), as only a subset of frames contains clinically relevant instruments or structures, we applied stratified random sampling of unedited, intact videos with enrichment for phases in which targets are most prevalent (e.g., Gonioscopy Observation and Incision of Schlemm’s Canal). For sampled frames, annotators recorded the presence/absence of each of the ten target instruments/structures and provided spatial localization (e.g., bounding region) when visible. Frames with uncertainty were flagged for adjudication; frames without targets were labeled None. This procedure balanced efficiency with adequate coverage of rare classes and challenging visual conditions (blur, glare, occlusion). We downsampled the selected intact videos by uniformly extracting frames at a 50:1 ratio for further fine-grained annotation. Because only a subset of surgical frames contains clinically relevant instruments or structures, many frames lacked substantive content. Accordingly, frames were first screened as valid or invalid; only valid frames proceeded to detailed annotation of key instruments and anatomical landmarks. Frames were deemed invalid if they exhibited defocus blur, hemorrhage, irrigation obscuring the field, or idle periods during microscope adjustment or instrument exchange.

A standardized annotation platform and workflow were established for Task II using the Rosetta system (Stardust Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA). Downsampled frame sequences from randomly selected videos were uploaded and annotated frame-by-frame for valid/invalid status. For each valid frame, annotators applied polygon masks to anatomical structures relevant to prognosis—corneal limbus, iris (under gonioscopy), trabecular meshwork (under gonioscopy)—and delineated commonly used instruments for trabecular incision, including the Kahook Dual Blade (KDB; New World Medical, Rancho Cucamonga, CA), Tanito microhook (TMH; Inami & Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and a self-made 25-G needle knife. Masks with poor fit or misalignment were returned for correction until they met the annotation consensus during iterative proofreading.

For Task II, after uniformly downsampling randomly selected unprocessed videos at 50:1, we screened 13,075 frames as valid or invalid; Invalid frames (n = 5,280)—e.g., idle periods, irrigation obscuration, hemorrhage, or defocus blur—were excluded (illustrated in Fig. 2B). Valid frames (n = 7,795) underwent fine-grained polygon instrument and structure annotation and served as inputs to train the segmentation model. From ZOC, 34 cases were split into a training set of 24 videos (5,378 valid frames) and a validation set of 10 videos (1,176 valid frames). The external test set comprised 8 multicenter videos (1,241 valid frames). During real-time validation, each current frame served as input, yielding pixel-level predictions of instruments and anatomical structures.

Demonstration model development of the GNS

To demonstrate the applicability of the dataset for real-time surgical guidance tasks, we developed a prototype GNS using the annotated data. For Task I, we adopted the Transformer-based End-to-End Long-form Online Action Detection (E2E-LOAD) network51 for its strong online performance and open-source availability. In line with contemporary protocols, videos were sampled at 24 frames per second and segmented into 6-frame chunks, yielding one decision every 0.25 s at the chunk level.

For Task II, we used the real-time semantic-segmentation model known as the Proportional-Integral-Derivative Controller Network (PIDNet)52. During training, inputs were resized to 1536 × 1024; evaluation was performed at the native 1920 × 1080 resolution.

Development of the intraoperative guidance tool of the GNS

To convey surgical warnings effectively while preserving established surgical workflows, we adopted phase-specific navigation tools that combine audio alerts with enhanced visualization on a third-party monitor. Under this scheme, guidance is triggered only when and where it is clinically relevant.

Critical operating area (COA) and alert logic. For each phase, we defined a COA—the region adjacent to key anatomy most likely to influence prognosis—based on expert consensus. The COA determines the time window for audio alerts: when the predicted instrument region overlaps the phase-specific COA, GNS emits an audio alert. Overlap is computed between the binary masks of the instrument region and the COA on the current frame. For Corneal Incision, Carbachol Injection, Ophthalmic Viscoelastic Devices (OVD) Injection, and OVD Irrigation/Aspiration, the corneal rim serves as the COA. For Gonioscopy Observation and Incision of Schlemm’s Canal, the minimum bounding rectangle of the predicted TM is designated as the COA.

Figure 4C illustrates the user interface, which provides real-time intraoperative reference and displays phase durations and percentages.

Pre-clinical evaluation

Three experienced GT experts not involved in prior annotations independently assessed 5% of videos (9 cases; 261,420 frames) using the same protocol and marked intervals they judged most deserving of real-time reminders. Explicit COA marking was not required; experts were free to convey their clinical judgment.

We harmonized expert labels using a majority-vote rule: intervals endorsed by >50% of experts were retained as the expert-panel standard. Agreement between panel-approved alert intervals and GNS-predicted intervals was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

To enable multicenter evaluation of real-time usability, we deployed an online server. Videos were processed in the cloud (GPU service) through an integrated algorithmic interface, and results were returned via the web UI. We enrolled 104 surgeons from 61 medical centers: 43 glaucoma specialists (>20 years’ experience; ≥100 independent GT operations), 36 associate chief/attending surgeons (independent surgical experience), and 25 novices (physicians or medical students without independent surgical experience). A post hoc questionnaire, adapted from a prior study56, assessed acceptability and perceived usefulness of GNS intraoperative guidance across experience levels.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Kruskal-Wallis tests and Pearson’s χ² tests were used to compare baseline characteristics of continuous and categorical variables, respectively. For Task I, the model’s performance was quantified using calibrated average precision (cAP), which has been specifically proposed for long-form online action detection to account for temporal continuity and class imbalance in untrimmed videos57,58. To facilitate comparison with commonly used metrics in surgical workflow recognition, we additionally report mean average precision (mAP). We employed summary measurements, including the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC), accuracy, recall, F1 score and Dice score, to inspect the performance. For Task II, the model’s performance on internal and external datasets was assessed using the mean of Intersection over Union (mIoU), recall, precision and Dice score. Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) evaluated inter-rater agreement among the three annotators. ICC59 quantified agreement between the durations of GNS-predicted alert intervals and expert-panel intervals. We interpreted ICC values of 0.75–0.90 as good and >0.90 as excellent reliability59,60. All analyses were performed in SPSS v26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) with codes developed by the authors. Tests were two-sided with α = 0.05.

Formal statistical sample-size estimation methods for artificial intelligence (AI)-based surgical navigation are currently lacking. We therefore benchmarked adequacy against the literature: Garcia Nespolo et al.56 developed and validated an intraoperative guidance tool for cataract surgery using 111 videos. By comparison, our dataset comprises 186 MIGS videos from three centers. The relatively large case count and dense frame-level annotation in the present dataset strengthen the robustness of the analysis and may serve as an empirical reference for future sample-size estimation in this field.

Data Records

All datasets and annotations generated in this study are publicly available on Zenodo for research use43 (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.18231909). The dataset comprises a large-scale, multicenter collection of minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) videos, together with fine-grained annotations supporting surgical phase recognition and instrument/anatomical structure segmentation.

For Task I (surgical phase recognition), untrimmed surgical videos are released in MP4 format. Frame-level phase annotations are provided in structured JSON files, with each video annotated by the start and end frames corresponding to predefined phases of 120° GT. These annotations enable precise temporal localization of surgical steps and can be readily used for training and evaluation of phase-recognition models.

For Task II (instrument and anatomical structure segmentation), the released data include the annotated original images (JPG format) and their corresponding pixel-wise annotation masks stored as grayscale images (PNG format). In addition, several sample videos showing model outputs are provided for qualitative reference and comparison. The surgical videos involved in Task II are also stored in MP4 format and released; notably, these videos are a subset of those included in Task I.

All images, videos, and annotation files are fully anonymized prior to release, with all personally identifiable information removed. The dataset reflects real-world multicenter variability in surgical techniques, instruments, imaging conditions, and ocular characteristics, thereby enhancing the robustness and generalizability of models trained on this resource.

Overall, these data records provide a comprehensive and standardized benchmark for developing and evaluating deep learning models for surgical phase recognition, instrument and anatomy segmentation, and intraoperative navigation in MIGS.

Technical Validation

To validate the quality and utility of the dataset, we evaluated model performance on two representative tasks: surgical phase recognition and instrument/anatomical structure segmentation.

Baseline characteristics of the data

For Task I, ZOC contributed 148 untrimmed 120° GT videos, split into training (111 videos; 2,961,748 frames) and validation (37 videos; 1,081,067 frames). The external test set comprised 38 videos (943,731 frames) from HCEH and SPH.

For Task II, 34 videos from ZOC yielded training (24 videos; 5,378 frames) and validation (10 videos; 1,176 frames) sets. Valid frames from eight multicenter videos formed the external test set (1,241 frames).

As summarized in Table 1, the dataset shows a balanced distribution across training, validation, and external test sets. For Task I, 186 videos comprising nearly 5 million frames were annotated, with substantial variation across surgical phases, where Gonioscopy Observation and Incision of Schlemm’s Canal accounted for the largest proportions. For Task II, 42 videos and 7,795 valid frames were included, covering key anatomical structures and surgical instruments, with emphasis on clinically relevant targets such as the trabecular meshwork and gonioscope.

As shown in Table 3, there were no significant differences among the three datasets in sex, spherical equivalent, or pattern standard deviation (visual field). In contrast, age, preoperative IOP, axial length, mean deviation (visual field), and whether the procedure was a first-time versus repeat anti-glaucoma surgery differed significantly across datasets.

Video durations varied widely, reflecting case complexity and surgeon technique. We include three illustrative examples of final annotations with distinct durations. Data diversity is also evident in surgeon-dependent behaviors, such as the duration of pauses/hesitation and the frequency of instrument exchanges. Figure 2C shows three annotated examples with distinct durations. The violin plot in Fig. 2D summarizes the distribution of time allocated to each step, demonstrating that the number of annotated frames is uneven across steps; Gonioscopy Observation and Incision of Schlemm’s Canal occupy the most significant portions of operative time, consistent with their critical role in GT and the expertise required.

Additional heterogeneity arises from instrument selection, individual expert preferences and experience, ocular characteristics (iris color, pupil size, trabecular meshwork color, iris root insertion), and image-acquisition parameters (illumination, magnification, contrast, hue, saturation). Together, these factors contribute to substantial data diversity across sources.

Performance of GNS

Task I

Table 4 summarizes phase-recognition performance. The average cAP was 99.0% on the validation set and 97.3% on the external test set, supporting the high quality and consistency of the annotations. The corresponding average mAP values were 97.9% and 95.1%, respectively. Mean AUC-ROC reached 98.7% in validation and 97.2% in external testing. The average F1 scores were 87.8% and 86.8% in the validation and external test sets, respectively. The average Dice scores were 80.7% and 76.0% in the validation and external test sets, respectively. These results indicate robust real-time phase prediction.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although this study included an external test set and the model showed favorable performance on that independent dataset, the current external validation remains limited. The external test set was relatively small compared with the development dataset, and although it was collected from two additional centers, these centers were geographically related. As a result, the variability in surgical style, clinical workflow, and operative environment may still have been constrained. Future studies should further evaluate the dataset and derived models in broader surgical environments, ideally using larger and more geographically diverse cohorts, as well as centers with different operative preferences, imaging systems, and surgeon training backgrounds.

Second, although 104 ophthalmologists completed the usability questionnaire, this assessment reflects user-reported acceptability and perceived usefulness rather than objective surgical performance. Participants evaluated the system through an online pre-clinical platform rather than during live surgery. Instead, these results provide preliminary evidence that the system is considered acceptable and potentially helpful by users with different levels of experience. Future studies should further evaluate the system in real surgical settings using objective endpoints, such as surgical efficiency, decision accuracy, complication-related events, and learning-curve improvement.

In addition, the procedure-specific limitation of this study should also be acknowledged. While 120° GT represents a typical and highly representative MIGS procedure, the present dataset does not encompass the full spectrum of MIGS surgeries and therefore is not generalizable to all MIGS procedures. Different MIGS techniques may involve distinct surgical workflows, instruments, anatomical landmarks, and operative objectives, which are not fully represented in the current dataset. Therefore, the dataset and the derived models should be interpreted as procedure-specific resources for GT rather than universal tools for all MIGS surgeries. Future studies should extend this framework to other MIGS procedures and broader glaucoma surgical settings.

Usage Notes

This dataset provides a comprehensive resource for developing and evaluating computer vision models in minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS). It supports multiple downstream tasks, including surgical phase recognition, instrument and anatomical structure segmentation, and intraoperative guidance system development.

For phase recognition (Task I), users can directly utilize the frame-level annotations in JSON format to train temporal models for surgical workflow analysis or real-time phase prediction. For segmentation (Task II), the pixel-wise annotated images can be used to train and benchmark deep learning models for fine-grained instrument and tissue segmentation.

Due to the multicenter nature of the dataset, it contains substantial variability in surgical techniques, imaging conditions, and anatomical features. This variability is beneficial for improving model generalizability but may also require careful preprocessing or domain adaptation strategies when developing models.

We recommend that users consider the imbalance across surgical phases and the presence of invalid frames when designing training pipelines.

All data are fully anonymized and released for research purposes. Users can access the dataset and associated resources through the provided repository.
