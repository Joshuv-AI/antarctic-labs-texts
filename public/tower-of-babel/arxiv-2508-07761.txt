On Hodge Laplacians on General Simplicial Complexes
Philipp Bartmann, Matthias Keller

Abstract
. We study Laplacians on general countable weighted simplicial complexes from a conceptual point of view. These operators will first be introduced formally before showing that those formal operators coincide with self-adjoint realizations of operators arising from quadratic forms. A major conceptual perspective is the correspondence to signed Schrödinger operators unveiling the Forman curvature. The main results are criteria for essential self-adjointness via lower bounded Forman curvature and a Gaffney type result via completeness. Finally, we study spectral relations between these Laplacians.



1.
Introduction

The study of simplicial complexes has a long-standing tradition in mathematics. As a tool in topology, they serve as triangulations of manifolds, discrete objects that encode topological information. Moreover, Laplacians on simplicial complexes are discrete analogues of Laplacians on differential forms on manifolds and can be considered higher dimensional generalizations of graph Laplacians.

Traditionally, they were studied on finite complexes, going back to the work of Eckmann
[
Eck45
]
, who established a theory which stands in analogy to the Hodge theory on compact manifolds (see the appendix by Dodziuk in
[
Cha84
]
). Since then, they have received substantial attention. For instance, in
[
Dod76
]
it was shown that discrete Laplacians on triangulations can be used to approximate the Laplacian on a given Riemannian manifold, thus combining the discrete and continuous setting.

Similar to graphs, there is often no canonical choice of weights, which provide a notions of volume. For example, one may
count the number of vertices in a set which arises from the constant weight one on the vertices, or one can count the number of edges in a set which arises from choosing the vertex degree as weight. Whenever one is faced with a non-canonical choice, it is often advisable to study all possible weights at once. This has proven to be successful for graphs, which have seen a vibrant interest with numerous results; see, e.g., the recent monographs
[
Bar17
,
JP23
,
KLW21
,
KN23
]
and references therein.

For finite weighted simplicial complexes, there is the seminal work
[
HJ13
]
that studies various spectral properties of the corresponding operators. However, there has also been a substantial push to study infinite simplicial complexes. For example, in relation to topology
[
For03
]
, group theory
[
BŚ97
,
Opp15a
,
Opp15b
]
, random walks
[
LLP20
,
PR17
,
Ros24
]
, operator theory
[
Che18
,
BH21
,
GP19
,
Mas09
]
or heat kernel asymptotics
[
HL18
]
. Although substantial progress has been made in various special cases, a conceptual study of infinite simplicial complexes seems to be missing. This is the purpose of the present paper.

We study Laplacians on general countable weighted simplicial complexes. This includes, in essence, all the settings above and allows for an infinite vertex set, infinite dimension and non-locally finite complexes. Our starting point is axiomatic with an operator
\delta
for which we ask

\delta\delta=0

and a basic compatibility assumption with the combinatorics of the simplicial complex, see Definition
2.2
. We choose a weight function
m
on the simplicial complex to introduce
\partial
as a formal adjoint of
\delta
. Although in most relevant cases we also have
\partial\partial=0
, this is no longer guaranteed in full generality (as was already noticed in
[
PR17
]
), which shows that care has to be taken. An important aspect is that we define
\delta
and
\partial
on functions rather than forms. Although this seems unnatural at first glance, we show that our approach is indeed equivalent in Section
2.2
. Yet, our approach has some advantages; especially it allows for a more convenient notation. Similar ideas have already been pursued elsewhere,
[
BSSS12
,
HK24
,
JM21
]
.

For the operators
\delta
and
\partial
, we establish a Stokes’ theorem and Green’s formulas on appropriate function spaces, which form the backbone of our analysis in Section
2.3
. In order to study self-adjoint realizations of these operators, we take the approach via quadratic forms. To this end, we make a mild assumption on the weight function
m
called
local summability
which means that for all simplices
\tau\neq\varnothing
in the complex

\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)<\infty,

where the sum runs over all
\sigma
which have
\tau
as a face, see also e.g.
[
Str21
]
. This guarantees that finitely supported functions are included in the form domain. We also introduce a stronger condition called
strong local summability
in Section
3.1
, which guarantees
\partial\partial=0
.

When restricting the quadratic forms to
\ell^{2}
, we observe that there is, in general, a whole family of closed forms, Section
3.2
. This comes as no surprise, as this appears also in the continuum where the Sobolev spaces
W^{1,2}_{0}
and
W^{1,2}
in general do not coincide. This is also a well studied phenomenon in the graph case, see e.g.
[
JP23
,
KL12
,
KLW21
,
HKMW13
,
Woj08
]
. These closed forms then give rise to a family of Laplacians
\Delta^{+}
,
\Delta^{-}
and
\Delta^{H}
that we show to be restrictions of
\partial\delta
,
\delta\partial
and
(\delta+\partial)^{2}
respectively, where the latter is equal to
\partial\delta+\delta\partial
in most (yet not all) relevant cases.
We then view our approach through the intriguing lens of Hilbert complexes in Section
3.3
, which gives rise to two particular instances of
\Delta^{H},
see e.g.
[
BL92
]
. These are of particular interest, as their kernels are tightly connected to the reduced
\ell^{2}
-cohomology groups of the simplicial complex.

A major conceptual perspective is then developed in Section
3.4
where we interpret our Laplacians as specific
signed Schrödinger operators
, Theorem
3.12
. Here, we take full advantage of the fact that our operators are defined on functions rather than forms.
We highlight two conceptual benefits which arise from this perspective: First of all, this representation unveils a quantity which is known as Forman curvature
[
For03
,
JM21
]
. This is one of a multitude of notions of discrete Ricci curvature that has been studied abundantly for graphs in recent years
[
BHL+15
,
EM12
,
HLLY19
,
Mü18
,
MW19
,
Oll10
]
.
Secondly, from this point on, we can draw from the rich and in many aspects well-developed theory of signed Schrödinger operators on graphs. In that section, Section
3.4
, we already characterize and give criteria when compactly supported functions are in the operator domain before we deal with the uniqueness of the operators itself.

We then come to the part that can be considered the main result of the paper, which is concerned with uniqueness of the operators in Section
4
. As a first step, we characterize boundedness of the operators for finite dimensional complexes in terms of boundedness of an explicit function resembling the vertex degree for graphs, Section
4.1
.
Then we address the unbounded case, where we study form uniqueness, i.e., existence of only one closed form, and essential self-adjointness, i.e., existence of only one self-adjoint extension of the Laplacians restricted to compactly supported functions.
We give two explicit criteria for this to hold:

(1)

Lower bounded Forman curvature and uniformly positive weight, Theorem
4.9
.

(2)

Compactness of distance balls for certain distances on the 1-skeleton, Theorem

.

The second result can be seen as an analogue of a famous theorem often attributed to Gaffney

]
in the case of manifolds, see also
[

,

]
. Furthermore, these considerations extend the known results of
[

,

,

We then turn to the study of spectral relations between the Laplacians in Section
5
. It is well known for finite simplicial complexes or compact manifolds that the spectra of the operators
\Delta^{+}
,
\Delta^{-}
and

coincide apart from zero,
[

]
. However, in case of non-uniqueness of the operators, this is not true anymore as it is well known that an operator with Dirichlet- or Neumann boundary condition can have different spectra already in the graph case, see e.g.
[

]
.
Thus, we establish the relations which still hold in the general setting, Theorem

We end the paper by looking at two natural choices of weights. The first is the combinatorial weight, which assigns constant weight equal to one to each simplex. Here, many of the quantities of the paper simplify significantly and become much clearer and combinatorial in nature. Moreover, we want to emphasize that the metric in the Gaffney-type result of Theorem

can be chosen as an intrinsic metric on the underlying 1-skeleton, which was already used for graphs in
[
HKMW13

, see Theorem

[

2.1.
Basic Definitions

We define simplicial complexes as well as coboundary
\delta

\partial

and

Definition 2.1

(Simplicial Complex)

Let

\tau\in\hat{\Sigma}.
Further, we denote by

-

Whenever

of
\sigma
or that
\sigma
is a

of

Simplices that have no cofaces are called

. We will define the

and the

We call a simplicial complex

if for all
\tau\in\hat{\Sigma}

\tau\neq\varnothing

\#\{\sigma\in\hat{\Sigma}\mid\sigma\succ\tau\}<\infty.

Whenever convenient, we will identify
\hat{\Sigma}_{0}
with

We refer to
\bigcup_{l\leq k}\hat{\Sigma}_{l}
as the
k
-

of
\hat{\Sigma}.

Note that
\varnothing
is the only element of
\hat{\Sigma}_{-1}
and that
\varnothing\prec v
for every

This (rather pathological) fact creates difficulties when it comes to defining the proper function spaces on infinite simplicial complexes that are consistent with the established theory on finite complexes as in
[

HJ13
]
.
We deal with this by introducing a weight function

m:\hat{\Sigma}\rightarrow(0,\infty)

and defining

\Sigma=\hat{\Sigma}\quad\mbox{ if }\sum_{v\in\Sigma_{0}}m(v)<\infty\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\Sigma=\hat{\Sigma}\backslash\{\varnothing\}\;\mbox{ otherwise}.

For the rest of the paper we will identify
\Sigma
with
\hat{\Sigma}
, even though, strictly speaking, it might not be a simplicial complex in the sense of Definition

. This is justified since we do not lose any information about

by considering
\Sigma
instead. Furthermore, we may extend

to subsets

via

m(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in A}m(\sigma)

and in this sense we can think of

as a measure of full support.

We define

F=F(\Sigma)=\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}

and define
{F}(\Sigma_{k})
analogously.
Naturally,
{F}(\Sigma)
can be identified with
\bigoplus_{k}{F}(\Sigma_{k}).

Next, we introduce the coboundary operator.

Definition 2.2

(Coboundary Operator)
.

A linear map
\delta:D(\delta)={F}\rightarrow{F}
is called a

if for all
\tau,\sigma\in\Sigma
, we have
\delta 1_{\tau}(\sigma)\in\{-1,0,1\},

\delta 1_{\tau}(\sigma)\neq 0
iff
\tau\prec\sigma
and

\delta\delta=0.

Given
\tau,\sigma\in\Sigma
we will write

\theta(\tau,\sigma)=\delta 1_{\tau}(\sigma),

where
1_{\tau}
is the characteristic function of
\tau
. For
\omega\in{F}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma

\delta\omega(\sigma)=\sum_{\tau\prec\sigma}\omega(\tau)\theta(\tau,\sigma)

since
\sigma
only has finitely many faces

. Note that
\delta
does not depend on the choice of the weight

Remark
.

We may identify
\delta
with
\bigoplus_{k}\delta_{k},
where
\delta_{k}:{F}(\Sigma_{k})\rightarrow{F}(\Sigma_{k+1})
is the restriction of
\delta

Most results in this paper concerning

Remark
.

We define the coboundary operator in a nonstandard, more axiomatic way compared with what one usually finds in the literature (see

Next, we define the spaces

\displaystyle{F}^{1}={F}^{1}(\Sigma,m)

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in{F}\mid\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)|\omega(\sigma)|<\infty~\text{for all}~\tau\in\Sigma\},

Lemma 2.3

We have

Lemma 2.4

We have

\partial\partial=0\qquad\mbox{on ${F}^{2}$}.

For the second equality, we changed the order of summation with respect to

2.2.
Relation to Coboundary Operators on Alternating Forms

Traditionally, the coboundary operator associated to a simplicial complex is defined as a linear operator acting on alternating forms on oriented simplices. More precisely, given
\tau=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}\}\in\Sigma,
one defines oriented simplices arising from
\tau
as equivalence classes
[(v_{\pi(1)},\ldots,v_{\pi(k+1)})],
where
\pi
is a permutation. Two such classes are the same iff the respective permutations have the same parity. In particular, if
k\geq 1
then one always has exactly two oriented simplices associated to
\tau.

With slight abuse of notation, we will denote the two equivalence classes by
[\tau]
and
\overline{[\tau]}.
For
v\in\Sigma_{0}
, there is only one equivalence class
[v]
. We denote by
\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}}
the set of oriented simplices.

An alternating form
\omega
then is a function
\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\omega([\tau])=-\omega(\overline{[\tau]})
for all oriented simplices
[\tau].
The coboundary operator
\tilde{\delta}
is then defined as

(
\ast
)

\tilde{\delta}\omega([v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}])=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{j+1}\omega([v_{1},\ldots,\hat{v}_{j},\ldots,v_{k+1}]),

where
\hat{v}_{j}
means that the vertex
v_{j}
is omitted. It is easy to show that
\tilde{\delta}\tilde{\delta}=0.
See
[
HJ13
,
HL18
,
PR17
]
for more details.

In contrast to Definition
2.2
we consider oriented simplices and alternating forms instead of simplices in
\Sigma
and functions acting on them. We show that both settings are essentially equivalent and differences are merely of a cosmetic nature.

Let
\Omega(\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}})
be the space of alternating forms. For each
\tau\in\Sigma
, pick one of the (at most) two associated oriented simplices
[\tau]
and
\overline{[\tau]}
and call it
\tau^{\mathrm{or}}.
This way we can identify alternating forms with functions on
\Sigma.
More precisely, the map

T:\Omega(\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}})\rightarrow{F}(\Sigma)
with

T\omega(\tau)=\omega(\tau^{\mathrm{or}})

is a bijection with inverse

T^{-1}f(\tau^{\mathrm{or}})=f(\tau)=-T^{-1}f(\overline{\tau^{\mathrm{or}}}).

then

h([\sigma])=(-1)^{k-i+1}h([\tau])\theta(\tau,\sigma).

Proof.

In order to show that
h
is alternating, we recall that whenever
\rho\prec\tau^{\prime},\tau^{\prime\prime}\prec\sigma
and
\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau^{\prime\prime},
then

0=\delta\delta 1_{\rho}(\sigma)=\theta(\rho,\tau^{\prime})\theta(\tau^{\prime},\sigma)+\theta(\rho,\tau^{\prime\prime})\theta(\tau^{\prime\prime},\sigma).

Choosing
\rho=\tau_{i-1},\tau^{\prime}=\tau_{i},\tau^{\prime\prime}=\tau_{i+1}\backslash\{v_{i}\},\sigma=\tau_{i+1}
, we observe that

h([v_{1},\ldots,v_{i},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{k+1}])=\theta(\rho,\tau^{\prime})\theta(\tau^{\prime},\sigma)\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i-1,i\end{subarray}}^{k}\theta(\tau_{j},\tau_{j+1})\\
=-\theta(\rho,\tau^{\prime\prime})\theta(\tau^{\prime\prime},\sigma)\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i-1,i\end{subarray}}^{k}\theta(\tau_{j},\tau_{j+1})=-h([v_{1},\ldots,v_{i+1},v_{i},\ldots,v_{k+1}]).

This shows that
h
is alternating. In particular, this shows that
h
is well-defined, i.e., independent of the choice of the representative of
[\tau]
.
With this at hand, the formula follows immediately

h([\sigma])=(-1)^{k+1-i}h([v_{1},\ldots,\hat{v}_{i},\ldots,v_{k+1},v_{i}])=(-1)^{k-i+1}h([\tau])\theta(\tau,\sigma).\hfill\qed

Given the form
h
, we define a map
U:\Omega(\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}})\rightarrow{F}(\Sigma)
as

U\omega(\tau)=h([\tau])\omega([\tau]),

which is well defined and bijective. The inverse is given by

U^{-1}f([\tau])=h([\tau])f(\tau).

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\delta
be a coboundary operator as in Definition
2.2
. Then

\tilde{\delta}\omega([v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}])=(-1)^{k}U^{-1}\delta U\omega([v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}])

satisfies (

∗

‣
2.2
).

Proof.

The proof is a direct application of Lemma
2.7
.
∎

Our final observation is that
U
is unitary when restricted to the Hilbert space

\Omega^{2}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}})=\Omega^{2}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}},m^{\mathrm{or}})=\{\omega\in\Omega(\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}})\mid\sum_{[\tau]\in\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}}}m^{\mathrm{or}}([\tau])|\omega([\tau])|^{2}<\infty\},

where the weight is given by
m^{\mathrm{or}}([\tau])=m(\tau)/\penalty 2
for
\tau\in\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{0}
and
m^{\mathrm{or}}([v])=m(v)
if
v\in\Sigma_{0}.
Indeed,
U
maps
\Omega^{2}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}})

onto
\ell^{2}(\Sigma,m)=\{\varphi\in{F}\mid\sum_{\Sigma}m\varphi^{2}<\infty\}.
The fact that the restriction of
U
to
\Omega^{2}(\Sigma^{\mathrm{or}})
is unitary is now obvious, as it is in essence multiplication with a sign.
In consequence it can be shown that suitable restrictions of
\tilde{\delta}
and
\delta
are unitarily equivalent by the theorem above.

2.3.
Stokes’s Theorem and Green’s Formulas

It is well known that for finite simplicial complexes the boundary and coboundary operators are adjoints of each other. In this section, we provide a first treatment of this phenomenon in the infinite setting, which can be understood as a discrete version of Stokes’ theorem.

Theorem 2.9

(Stokes’ theorem)
.

For
\omega\in{F}^{c}
and
\eta\in{F}
or
\omega\in{F}^{1}
and
\eta\in{F}^{c},

\sum_{\Sigma}m(\partial\omega)\eta=\sum_{\Sigma}m\omega(\delta\eta).

Proof.

The statement follows from Fubini’s theorem as in either case the sum

\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau\prec\sigma}m(\tau)|\omega(\tau)||\eta(\sigma)|

converges. ∎

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
\omega\in{F}^{c}
and
\eta\in{F}
or
\omega\in{F}^{2}
and
\eta\in{F}^{c}.
Then

\sum_{\Sigma}m(\partial\omega)(\delta\eta)=0.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
,
\partial{F}^{c}\subseteq{F}^{c}
as well as
\partial{F}^{2}\subseteq{F}^{1}.

The statement now follows from Theorem
2.9
and Lemma
2.4
as either
\delta\delta\eta=0
or
\partial\partial\omega=0.

∎

The Green’s formulas below will be of major importance in the following.

Theorem 2.11

(Green’s formulas)
.

(a)

If
\omega\in{F}^{1}
and
\eta\in{F}^{c},
then

\sum_{\Sigma}m(\delta\partial\omega)\eta=\sum_{\Sigma}m(\partial\omega)(\partial\eta)=\sum_{\Sigma}m\omega(\delta\partial\eta).

(b)

If
\omega\in D(\partial\delta)=\{\varphi\in{F}\mid\delta\omega\in{F}^{1}\}
and
\eta\in{F}^{c},
then

\sum_{\Sigma}m(\partial\delta\omega)\eta=\sum_{\Sigma}m(\delta\omega)(\delta\eta).

(c)

If
\omega\in F^{+}=\{\varphi\in{F}\mid\sum\limits_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)\sum\limits_{\tau^{\prime}\prec\sigma}|\varphi(\tau^{\prime})|<\infty,~\tau\in\Sigma\}
and
\eta\in{F}^{c},
then

\sum_{\Sigma}m(\partial\delta\omega)\eta=\sum_{\Sigma}m\omega(\partial\delta\eta).

Proof.

Statement
(a)
and
(b)
follow from Theorem
2.9
using
\partial\eta\in{F}^{c}
whenever
\eta\in{F}^{c}
by Lemma
2.3
. Statement
(c)
follows from Fubini’s theorem, where
\omega\in D(\partial\delta)
is implied by the triangle inequality.
∎

3.
Quadratic Forms and Laplacians

In this section, we introduce Laplacians on simplicial complexes. We pursue this goal via closed and positive quadratic forms arising from
\delta
,
\partial
and
\delta+\partial
and obtain Laplacians as their corresponding self-adjoint operators. We then study their action and find out that they are restrictions of
\partial\delta
,
\delta\partial
and
(\delta+\partial)^{2}.
The latter coincides with
\partial\delta+\delta\partial
on a certain subspace. In general there is no unique self-adjoint realization of these operators and we discuss in particular the so-called Dirichlet and Neumann Laplacian as the two most significant cases. Furthermore, we introduce a Laplacian which naturally arises in the context of Hilbert complexes.

3.1.
General quadratic forms and local summability

For
\omega\in{F}
, we consider

\|\omega\|=\left(\sum_{\Sigma}m\omega^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

This defines a norm on

\ell^{2}=\ell^{2}(\Sigma,m)=\{\omega\in{F}\mid\|\omega\|<\infty\},

which is a Hilbert space with scalar product
\langle\omega,\eta\rangle=\sum_{\Sigma}m\omega\eta
,
\omega,\eta\in\ell^{2}
.

The Laplacians will be introduced by virtue of their quadratic forms, which we first define on their maximal domains of definition beyond
\ell^{2}
.
Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{+}:{F}\rightarrow[0,\infty],

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{+}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\|\delta\omega\|^{2}=\sum_{\Sigma}m|\delta\omega|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{-}:{F}^{1}\rightarrow[0,\infty],

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{-}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\|\partial\omega\|^{2}=\sum_{\Sigma}m|\partial\omega|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{H}:{F}^{1}\rightarrow[0,\infty],

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{H}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\|\delta\omega+\partial\omega\|^{2}=\sum_{\Sigma}m|\delta\omega+\partial\omega|^{2}.

Throughout the rest of this paper,
{\circ}
will stand for either
+,-
or
H
, i.e.,

{\circ}\in\{+,-,H\}.

We define the spaces of functions of finite energy as

\mathcal{D}^{\circ}=\mathcal{D}^{\circ}(\Sigma,m)=\{\omega\in{F}\mid\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}(\omega)<\infty\},

which is a vector space due to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

From this point on, we will always assume that the weight
m
is
locally summable
, i.e., for all
\tau\in\Sigma
, we have

\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)<\infty.

This has several important consequences that we summarize as follows.

Lemma 3.1

(Locally summable)
.

The weight
m
is locally summable iff
{F}^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{+}.

Moreover, if
m
is locally summable, then the following inclusions hold:

(a)

{F}^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{\circ}
.

(b)

\ell^{2}\subseteq{F}^{1}
.

(c)

{F}^{c}\subseteq D(\partial\delta)
.

(d)

\delta{F}^{c}\subseteq{F}^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\tau\in\Sigma
be fixed. Then

\mathcal{Q}^{+}(1_{\tau})=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}m(\sigma)|\delta 1_{\tau}(\sigma)|^{2}=\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma),

which is finite for all choices of
\tau
iff
m
is locally summable. This shows the desired equivalence as well as
(a)
for
{\circ}=+.
The rest of
(a)
follows from Lemma
2.3
as
\partial{F}^{c}\subseteq{F}^{c}\subseteq\ell^{2}
so
{F}^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{-}
and, therefore,
{F}^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{+}\cap\mathcal{D}^{-}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{H}
which holds by the triangle inequality.

To see
(b)
, let
\omega\in\ell^{2}.
Then, for
\tau\in\Sigma
, we see with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)|\omega(\sigma)|\leq\|\omega\|\left(\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)\right)^{1/\penalty 2},

which is finite by the local summability assumption. Hence,
\omega\in{F}^{1}
.

Statement
(c)
follows from
(a)
and
(b)
because
(a)
implies that
\delta{F}^{c}\subseteq\ell^{2}.

To see
(d)
we fix
\rho,\tau_{0}\in\Sigma.
Then

\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau\succ\rho}m(\sigma)|\delta 1_{\tau_{0}}(\sigma)|=\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau\succ\rho}m(\sigma)1_{\tau_{0}\prec\sigma}=\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau_{0}}m(\sigma)\sum_{\tau}1_{\sigma\succ\tau\succ\rho}\\
\leq 2\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau_{0}}m(\sigma)<\infty.

Here, that for fixed
\rho,\sigma\in\Sigma
there are at most two
\tau\in\Sigma
such that
\sigma\succ\tau\succ\rho.

∎

We introduce an even stronger condition. We say
m
is
strongly locally summable
if it is locally summable and for all
\rho\in\Sigma

\sum_{\tau\succ\rho}\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)=\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau\succ\rho}m(\sigma)<\infty.

Lemma 3.2

(Strongly locally summable)
.

If
m
is strongly locally summable, then

\ell^{2}\subseteq{F}^{2}.

In particular,
\partial\partial=0
on
\ell^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in\Sigma
and
\omega\in\ell^{2}.
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that for each
\sigma
there are at most two
\tau
such that
\rho\prec\tau\prec\sigma
, we obtain
\omega\in F^{2}
since

\left(\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau\succ\rho}m(\sigma)|\omega(\sigma)|\right)^{2}=\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}m(\sigma)|\omega(\sigma)|\sum_{\tau\prec\sigma}1_{\rho\prec\tau}\right)^{2}\leq 2\|\omega\|^{2}\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau\succ\rho}m(\sigma),

which is finite due to strong local sumability. The “in particular” statement follows from Lemma
2.4
. ∎

3.2.
Dirichlet and Neumann Laplacians

In this section, we introduce self-adjoint operators associated to two natural
restrictions of
\mathcal{Q}^{{\circ}}
,
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
.

Let us first define

Q^{\circ}_{N}=\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}\mid_{D(Q^{\circ}_{N})},\qquad D(Q^{\circ}_{N})=\mathcal{D}^{\circ}\cap\ell^{2}.

By Fatou’s lemma
\mathcal{Q}^{{\circ}}
restricted to
\mathcal{D}^{\circ}\cap\ell^{2}
is lower semi continuous and, therefore, closed, see e.g.
[
KLW21
, Theorem B.9]
. By general theory, see e.g.
[
Wei80
, Theorem 5.37]
, the positive closed quadratic form
Q^{{\circ}}_{N}
gives rise to a
unique positive self-adjoint operator
\Delta^{\circ}_{N}
such that for all
\omega\in D(Q^{\circ}_{N})
and
\eta\in D(\Delta^{\circ}_{N})
one has

Q^{\circ}_{N}(\omega,\eta)=\langle\omega,\Delta^{\circ}_{N}\eta\rangle.

We respectively call the operators
\Delta^{+}_{N},\Delta^{-}_{N},\Delta^{H}_{N}
the
discrete up-, down-
and
Hodge-Laplacian
with
Neumann boundary conditions.
The name arises as
D(Q^{{\circ}}_{N})
corresponds to the Sobolev spaces
W^{1,2}
in the continuum.
Furthermore, note that by the triangle inequality

D(Q^{+}_{N})\cap D(Q^{-}_{N})\subseteq D(Q^{H}_{N}).

Since
Q^{{\circ}}_{N}
is closed, its restriction to
F^{c}
is closable in
\ell^{2}
and we define

Q^{{\circ}}_{D}=Q^{\circ}_{N}\mid_{D(Q^{\circ}_{D})},\quad D(Q^{{\circ}}_{D})=\overline{{F}^{c}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Q}^{{\circ}}}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}}^{2}=\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}(\cdot)+\|\cdot\|^{2}_{2}.
These are positive, closed restrictions of
\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}
and give rise to unique positive self-adjoint operators
\Delta^{{\circ}}_{D}
such that for all
\omega\in D(Q^{\circ}_{D})
and
\eta\in D(\Delta^{\circ}_{D})
one has

Q^{\circ}_{D}(\omega,\eta)=\langle\omega,\Delta^{\circ}_{D}\eta\rangle.

Note that by Corollary
2.10
we have

Q^{H}_{D}=(Q^{+}_{D}+Q^{-}_{D})\mid_{D(Q^{H}_{D})}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad D(Q^{H}_{D})\subseteq D(Q^{+}_{D})\cap D(Q^{-}_{D}).

We respectively call the operators
\Delta^{+}_{D},\Delta^{-}_{D},\Delta^{H}_{D}
the
discrete up-, down-
and
Hodge-Laplacian
with
Dirichlet boundary conditions.
The name arises as one may consider the form domains
D(Q^{{\circ}}_{D})
as analogues of the Sobolev spaces
W^{1,2}_{0}
in the continuum.

Definition 3.3

(Laplacian)
.

A
Laplacian

\Delta^{{\circ}}
,
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
, associated to a simplicial complex
(\Sigma,m)
is the unique positive self-adjoint operator associated to a closed quadratic form
Q^{{\circ}}_{D}\subseteq Q^{{\circ}}\subseteq Q^{{\circ}}_{N}
.

Next, we determine the action of these Laplacians.

Theorem 3.4

(Action of Laplacians)
.

Let a Laplacian
\Delta^{{\circ}}
be given,
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
. Then,
D(\Delta^{+})\subseteq D(\partial\delta)
,
D(\Delta^{-})\subseteq D(\delta\partial)
, and
D(\Delta^{H})\subseteq D((\delta+\partial)^{2})
, and

\displaystyle\Delta^{+}

\displaystyle=\partial\delta

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\mbox{ on }D(\Delta^{+}),

\displaystyle\Delta^{-}

\displaystyle=\delta\partial

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\mbox{ on }D(\Delta^{-}),

\displaystyle\Delta^{H}

\displaystyle=(\delta+\partial)^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\mbox{ on }D(\Delta^{H}).

If
\Delta^{H}=\Delta^{H}_{D}
or
m
is strongly locally summable, then
D(\Delta^{H})\subseteq D(\partial\delta+\delta\partial)

and

\displaystyle\Delta^{H}

\displaystyle=\partial\delta+\delta\partial

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\mbox{ on }D(\Delta^{H}).

We start the proof with a lemma which is interesting in its own right.
Observe that in general, we only have
\Delta^{H}=(\delta+\partial)^{2}
but not necessarily equality with
\partial\delta+\delta\partial
. Indeed, in Example
2.5
, we discussed that
\partial\partial\omega_{0}\neq 0
for a certain
\omega_{0}\in D(\partial\partial)
while
\partial\partial\omega=0
for
\omega\in F^{2}
by Lemma
2.4
. We extend this observation to obtain the formula
\Delta^{H}_{D}=\partial\delta+\delta\partial.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\omega\in D(Q^{-}_{D})
and
\eta\in\mathcal{D}^{+}.
Then

\partial\partial\omega=0\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\langle\partial\omega,\delta\eta\rangle=0.

Proof.

Observe that
\partial\omega\in\ell^{2}\subseteq F^{1}=D(\partial)
and that there is a sequence
(\omega_{n})_{n}
in
F^{c}
such that
\partial\omega_{n}\rightarrow\partial\omega
in
\ell^{2}.
Moreover, for
\rho\in\Sigma
and according to Stokes’ theorem, Theorem
2.9
, we have

m(\rho)\partial\partial\omega(\rho)=\langle\partial\omega,\delta 1_{\rho}\rangle.

Since
\partial\omega
is the limit of
(\partial\omega_{n})
in
\ell^{2}
, Corollary
2.10
gives both statements.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

First, let
\omega\in D(\Delta^{+})\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{+}
. Hence,
\delta\omega\in\ell^{2}\subseteq F^{1}=D(\partial)
by Lemma
3.1

(b)
, so we get
\omega\in D(\partial\delta).
Now, the statement follows from Green’s formula, Theorem
2.11

(b)
, with
\varphi=1_{\tau}/m

\Delta^{+}\omega(\tau)=\langle\varphi,\Delta^{+}\omega\rangle=Q^{+}(\varphi,\omega)=\sum_{\Sigma}m\varphi\partial\delta\omega=\partial\delta\omega(\tau).

To see
\Delta^{-}\omega=\delta\partial\omega
, recall that
D(\Delta^{-})\subseteq\ell^{2}\subseteq{F}^{1}=D(\delta\partial).
Then the statement follows from Green’s formula, Theorem
2.11

(a)
, similar to
\Delta^{+}
.

For
\Delta^{H}
, let
\omega\in D(\Delta^{H})
. Observe first that
(\delta+\partial)\omega\in\ell^{2}
because
\omega\in\mathcal{D}^{H}.
Secondly, by local summability, Lemma
3.1

(b)
, we have
\ell^{2}\subseteq F^{1}=D(\partial)
. As
D(\delta)=F
, we therefore have
(\delta+\partial)\omega\in D(\delta+\partial)
, i.e.,
\omega\in D((\delta+\partial)^{2})
. Now, let additionally
\varphi=1_{\tau}/m,
for
\tau\in\Sigma
. Then, similar as for
\Delta^{+},

\Delta^{H}\omega(\tau)=Q^{H}(\varphi,\omega)=\sum_{\Sigma}m(\partial+\delta)\varphi(\partial+\delta)\omega=\sum_{\Sigma}m\varphi(\partial+\delta)^{2}\omega=(\partial+\delta)^{2}\omega(\tau),

where the second to last equality follows by applying Stokes’ theorem, Theorem
2.9
, twice (recall that
(\partial+\delta)\omega\in F^{1}).

Finally, if
\Delta^{H}=\Delta^{H}_{D}
or if
\Sigma
is strongly locally summable, then by Corollary
2.10

\sum_{\Sigma}m\partial\omega\delta\varphi=0=\sum_{\Sigma}m\delta\omega\partial\varphi.

Thus, employing Green’s formula, Theorem
2.11

(a)
and
(b)
, gives

\Delta^{H}\omega(\tau)=\sum_{\Sigma}m\delta\omega\delta\varphi+\sum_{\Sigma}m\partial\omega\partial\varphi=\sum_{\Sigma}m\varphi(\delta\partial+\partial\delta)\omega=(\delta\partial+\partial\delta)\omega(\tau).\hfill\qed

Below we discuss that Example
2.5
demonstrates that
\Delta^{H}_{D}\neq\Delta^{H}_{N}
in general. Later in Section
4
, we give criteria when the operators indeed coincide.

Example 3.6

(Example
2.5
continued)
.

Reconsidering Example
2.5
we find that
\omega_{0}\in\ell^{2}\cap\mathcal{D}^{+}\cap\mathcal{D}^{-}\subseteq D(Q^{H}_{N}).
On the other hand,

|\langle\delta\omega_{0},\partial\omega_{0}\rangle|=m(\{0,1\})=1\neq 0.

However, this implies that
\omega_{0}\not\in D(Q^{H}_{D})\subseteq D(Q^{-}_{D})
as this would contradict Lemma
3.5
. As a consequence,
Q^{H}_{D}\neq Q^{H}_{N}
and in particular
\Delta^{H}_{D}\neq\Delta^{H}_{N}.

Let us define

\delta_{D}=\delta\mid_{D(Q^{+})},\quad\partial_{D}=\partial\mid_{D(Q^{-})}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad(\partial+\delta)_{D}=(\delta+\partial)\mid_{D(Q^{H})}

\delta_{N}=\delta\mid_{D(Q^{+}_{N})},\quad\partial_{N}=\partial\mid_{D(Q^{-}_{N})}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad(\partial+\delta)_{N}=(\delta+\partial)\mid_{D(Q^{H}_{N})}.

By
[
Wei80
, Theorem 5.1]
these are closed operators and by
[
Wei80
, Theorem 5.39]
the compositions with their respective adjoints are self-adjoint operators.

The next lemma shows in which precise sense
\delta
and
\partial
are adjoints of each other.

Lemma 3.7

(Adjoints of
\delta
and
\partial
)
.

We have

\delta^{*}_{D}=\partial_{N},\qquad\partial^{*}_{D}=\delta_{N}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad(\delta+\partial)^{*}_{D}=(\delta+\partial)_{N},

\delta_{D}=\partial^{*}_{N},\qquad\partial_{D}=\delta^{*}_{N}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad(\delta+\partial)_{D}=(\delta+\partial)^{*}_{N}.

Proof.

First, let
\omega\in D(\delta^{*}_{D})
, i.e., for every
\eta\in D(\delta_{D})
, we have

\langle\omega,\delta\eta\rangle=\langle\delta^{*}_{D}\omega,\eta\rangle.

Choosing
\eta=1_{\rho}/\penalty m
and using Stokes’ theorem, Theorem
2.9
, shows
\delta^{*}_{D}\omega=\partial\omega.
In particular,
\partial\omega\in\ell^{2}
and consequently
\omega\in\mathcal{D}^{-}\cap\ell^{2}.
Hence,
\delta^{*}_{D}\subseteq\partial_{N}.

Now, let
\omega\in\mathcal{D}^{-}\cap\ell^{2}=D(Q_{N}^{-})=D(\partial_{N})
and
\eta\in D(Q^{+}_{D}).
We can choose a sequence
(\eta_{n})
in
{F}^{c}
such that
\eta_{n}\rightarrow\eta
and
\delta\eta_{n}\rightarrow\delta\eta
in
\ell^{2}.
As
\partial\omega,\omega\in\ell^{2}
we deduce from Stokes’ theorem, Theorem
2.9
, once again

\langle\omega,\delta\eta\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\omega,\delta\eta_{n}\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\partial\omega,\eta_{n}\rangle=\langle\partial\omega,\eta\rangle.

In particular,
\omega\in D(\delta^{*}_{D})
and, therefore,
\partial_{N}\subseteq\delta^{*}_{D}
which gives
\delta^{*}_{D}=\partial_{N}.
The equality
\delta_{D}=\delta_{D}^{**}=\partial_{N}^{*}
follows by
[
Wei80
, Theorem 5.3 (b)]
since
\delta_{D}
is closed.
The other two cases follow along the same line of arguments.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

We have

\Delta^{+}_{D}=\partial_{N}\delta_{D},\quad\Delta^{-}_{D}=\delta_{N}\partial_{D}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta^{H}_{D}=(\delta+\partial)_{N}(\delta+\partial)_{D},

\Delta^{+}_{N}=\partial_{D}\delta_{N},\quad\Delta^{-}_{N}=\delta_{D}\partial_{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta^{H}_{N}=(\delta+\partial)_{D}(\delta+\partial)_{N}.

Proof.

A straight forward calculation that uses the definition of adjoint operators and Green’s formulas, Theorem
2.11
, shows that

\Delta^{+}_{D}=\delta_{D}^{*}\delta_{D},\quad\Delta^{-}_{D}=\partial_{D}^{*}\partial_{D}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta^{H}_{D}=(\delta+\partial)^{*}_{D}(\delta+\partial)_{D}

as well as

\Delta^{+}_{N}=\delta^{*}_{N}\delta_{N},\quad\Delta^{-}_{N}=\partial_{N}^{*}\partial_{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta^{H}_{N}=(\delta+\partial)^{*}_{N}(\delta+\partial)_{N}.

Thus, the statements are a direct consequence of Lemma
3.7
. ∎

3.3.
The Hilbert Complex Perspective

In this section we will see how the operators introduced in Section
3
can be described in the language of Hilbert complexes. For a more detailed introduction to Hilbert complexes, we refer to
[
BL92
]
(see also
[
HS12
]
) whose setting we will closely follow here. We denote the range of an operator
A
by
R(A).

Definition 3.9

(Hilbert complex)
.

Given a sequence of mutually orthogonal Hilbert spaces
H_{k}
and closed, densely defined operators
d_{k}
on
H_{k}
to
H_{k+1}
such that
R(d_{k})\subseteq D(d_{k+1})
and

d_{k+1}d_{k}=0

for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
we obtain a
Hilbert complex
in form of a chain complex (in the sense of homological algebra)

\ldots\xrightarrow{d_{k-2}}D(d_{k-1})\xrightarrow{d_{k-1}}D(d_{k})\xrightarrow{d_{k}}D(d_{k+1})\xrightarrow{d_{k+1}}\ldots.

It follows easily from the definition of a Hilbert complex that the adjoints of
d_{k}
form a Hilbert complex as well, called the
dual complex
, i.e.,
R(d_{k}^{*})\subseteq D(d_{k+1}^{*})
and
d_{k}^{*}d_{k+1}^{*}=0
,

\ldots\xleftarrow{d^{*}_{k-1}}D(d^{*}_{k-1})\xleftarrow{d^{*}_{k}}D(d^{*}_{k})\xleftarrow{d^{*}_{k+1}}D(d^{*}_{k+1})\xleftarrow{d^{*}_{k+2}}\ldots.

Given a general Hilbert complex associated to operators
d_{k}
and
d=\bigoplus_{k}d_{k}
on
H=\bigoplus_{k}H_{k}
, one defines a Laplacian
\Delta
as

\Delta=d^{*}d+dd^{*},

which by von Neumann’s theorem
[
Wei80
, Theorem 5.39]
is always self-adjoint as
\Delta=(d+d^{*})^{2}
and
d+d^{*}
is self-adjoint. The fact that
d+d^{*}
is densely defined is a consequence of
d_{k+1}d_{k}=0.
The operator
d+d^{*}
is also known as Gauss-Bonnet-Operator (
[
ATH15
,
Par17
]
). The quadratic form associated to
\Delta
on
H
is given by
D(d+d^{*})\to\mathbb{R}
,
f\mapsto\|(d+d^{*})f\|^{2}
. An important tool within this theory is the weak Hodge-decomposition, which is shown in

[
BL92
, Lemma 2.1. and Lemma 2.2.]

H=\ker\Delta\oplus\overline{R(d)}\oplus\overline{R(d^{*})}.

For the convenience of the reader we will provide a proof of this result in context of simplicial complexes below.

First, we show how our operators fit in this framework. To associate a Hilbert complex to a simplicial complex, we set
H_{-1}=\{0\}
or
H_{-1}=\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{-1},m)\cong\mathbb{R},
depending on whether
\Sigma=\hat{\Sigma}\setminus\{\varnothing\}
or
\hat{\Sigma}=\Sigma
,
H_{k}=\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{k},m)
,
k>-1
and
H_{k}=\{0\}
,
k<-1,
as well as
d_{k}=\delta_{N,k}
, where
\delta_{N,k}
is the restriction of
\delta_{N}
to
\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{k},m)\cap D(Q^{+}_{N}).
Those are closed and densely defined by the same arguments as
\delta_{N}
is closed and densely defined.
The defining property of
\delta
, i.e.,
\delta\delta=0
on
F
gives

R(\delta_{N})\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{+}\cap\ell^{2}=D(\delta_{N})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\delta_{N}\delta_{N}=0,

which then transfers to
\delta_{N,k}
. Hence, we obtain a Hilbert complex

0\rightarrow D(\delta_{N,-1})\xrightarrow{\delta_{N,-1}}D(\delta_{N,0})\xrightarrow{\delta_{N,0}}D(\delta_{N,1})\xrightarrow{\delta_{N,1}}\ldots.

Furthermore, in view of Lemma
3.7

we have
\partial_{D}=\delta_{N}^{*}
which gives rise to the dual complex by defining
\partial_{D,k}
as the restriction of
\partial_{D}
to
D(\partial_{D,k})=\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{k},m)\cap D(Q^{-}_{D})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}

0\leftarrow H_{-1}\xleftarrow{\partial_{D,0}}D(\partial_{D,0})\xleftarrow{\partial_{D,1}}D(\partial_{D,1})\xleftarrow{\partial_{D,2}}\ldots.

Note, that
H_{-1}=D(\delta_{N,-1})=R(\partial_{D,0}).

The self-adjoint Laplacian
\Delta=\Delta_{\Sigma}
associated to the Hilbert complex is then given by

\Delta_{\Sigma}=\delta_{N}\partial_{D}+\partial_{D}\delta_{N}=(\delta_{N}+\partial_{D})^{2}=\Delta^{+}_{N}+\Delta^{-}_{D}.

The quadratic form
Q_{\Sigma}
associated to
\Delta_{\Sigma}
is given
as

\displaystyle Q_{\Sigma}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\|(\delta_{N}+\partial_{D})\omega\|^{2}=\|\delta_{N}\omega\|^{2}+\|\partial_{D}\omega\|^{2},

\displaystyle D(Q_{\Sigma})=

\displaystyle D(\delta_{N}+\partial_{D})=D(Q_{N}^{+})\cap D(Q_{D}^{-}).

Hence,

Q_{D}^{H}\subseteq Q_{\Sigma}\subseteq Q_{N}^{H}.

The following result then relates harmonic functions with respect to
\Delta
to the reduced
\ell^{2}
-cohomology groups
H^{k}_{(2)}(\Sigma),
which are defined as

H^{k}_{(2)}(\Sigma)=\ker\delta_{N,k}/\penalty\overline{R(\delta_{N,k-1})}.

Theorem 3.10

(Weak Hodge-Decomposition)
.

One has the orthogonal decomposition

\ell^{2}=\ker\Delta_{\Sigma}\oplus\overline{R(\delta_{N})}\oplus\overline{R(\partial_{D})}.

In particular, for the restriction
\Delta_{\Sigma,k}
of
\Delta_{\Sigma}
to
\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{k},m)
,
k\geq 0
,

H^{k}_{(2)}(\Sigma)\cong\ker\Delta_{\Sigma,k}.

Proof.

First, we argue that

\ker\Delta_{\Sigma}=\ker(\delta_{N}+\partial_{D})=\ker\delta_{N}\cap\ker\partial_{D}.

The first equality follows since
\Delta_{\Sigma}=(\delta_{N}+\partial_{D})^{2}=(\delta_{N}+\delta_{N}^{*})^{2}
by Lemma
3.7
and
since
\ker T^{*}T=\ker T
for a self-adjoint operator
T
, see e.g.
[
Wei80
, Theorem 5.39]
. The second equality uses
\delta_{N}\delta_{N}=0,
which readily gives
\langle\delta_{N}\omega,\partial_{D}\eta\rangle=0
again by Lemma
3.7
. Thus,
R(\delta_{N})\perp R(\partial_{D}),
which implies the inclusion
\subseteq
in the second equality, while the other inclusion is trivial.

As
\delta_{N}
is closed and
\delta_{N}^{*}=\partial_{D}
, we have
\ker(\delta_{N})^{\perp}=\overline{R(\delta_{N}^{*})}=\overline{R(\partial_{D})}
by
[
Wei80
, Theorem 4.13]
and, therefore,

\ell^{2}=\ker\delta_{N}\oplus(\ker\delta_{N})^{\perp}=\ker\delta_{N}\oplus\overline{R(\partial_{D})}.

Moreover, as
\delta_{N}\delta_{N}=0
, we have
R(\delta_{N})\subseteq\ker\delta_{N}
and since
\ker\delta_{N}
is closed. Thus,

\ker\delta_{N}=\overline{R(\delta_{N})}\oplus(\ker\delta_{N}\cap\overline{R(\delta_{N})}^{\perp})=\overline{R(\delta_{N})}\oplus(\ker\delta_{N}\cap\ker\partial_{D}),

where we used again
\overline{R(\delta_{N})}^{\perp}=\ker(\delta_{N}^{*})=\ker{(\partial_{D})}
by
[
Wei80
, Theorem 4.13]
and Lemma
3.7
.
All together, this yields the desired decomposition.

Restricting to
\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{k},m)
and taking the quotient over
\overline{R(\delta_{N})}
yields the result about the
\ell^{2}
-cohomology.
∎

In Section
4
, we provide criteria for
Q^{H}_{D}=Q^{H}_{N},
which is equivalent to
\Delta_{D}^{H}=\Delta_{\Sigma}=\Delta_{N}^{H}
since
Q_{D}^{H}\subseteq Q_{\Sigma}\subseteq Q_{N}^{H}
. In this case, Theorem
3.10
applies to
\Delta_{D}^{H}
,
\Delta_{N}^{H}
as well.

It is natural to ask whether we obtain Hilbert complexes by considering
\delta_{D}
or
\partial_{N}
instead.
Again, Lemma
3.7
gives
\delta_{D}^{*}=\partial_{N}
. So by the discussion above, if one of the two gives rise to a Hilbert complex, then so does the other.
However, a counterexample for
\partial_{N}\partial_{N}=0

is given in Example
2.5
and its continuation in Example
3.6
, which shows that for
\partial_{N}
this is not the case in general. On the other hand, we always have
\delta_{D}\delta_{D}=0,
which, however, may not be densely defined. Thus,
\delta_{D}
and
\partial_{N}
yield Hilbert complexes if and only if

R(\delta_{D})\subseteq D(\delta_{D}).

In this case, we obtain a self-adjoint Laplacian

\Delta^{\prime}_{\Sigma}=\delta_{D}\partial_{N}+\partial_{N}\delta_{D}=(\delta_{D}+\partial_{N})^{2}=\Delta^{+}_{D}+\Delta^{-}_{N}

whose quadratic form
Q^{\prime}_{\Sigma}
also satisfies

Q_{D}^{H}\subseteq Q^{\prime}_{\Sigma}\subseteq Q^{H}_{N}.

Obviously, this is trivially satisfied if
\Delta_{D}^{+}=\Delta_{N}^{+}
which simply means
Q^{+}_{D}=Q^{+}_{N}
and
\delta_{D}=\delta_{N}.

Another criterion is given by strong local summability of the weight
m
.

Theorem 3.11
.

If the weight
m
is strongly locally summable, then
\delta_{D}
and
\partial_{N}
give rise to Hilbert complexes and

\ell^{2}=\ker\Delta^{\prime}_{\Sigma}\oplus\overline{R(\delta_{D})}\oplus\overline{R(\partial_{N})}

and

\ker\delta_{D,k}/\penalty\overline{R(\delta_{D,k-1})}\cong\ker\Delta_{\Sigma,k}^{\prime}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
strong local summability implies
\partial\partial=0
on
\ell^{2}
.
Consequently,
R(\partial_{N})\subseteq D(\partial_{N})
and
\partial_{N}\partial_{N}=0
. By Lemma
3.7
,
\delta_{D}^{*}=\partial_{N}
. Thus,
\partial_{N}
and
\delta_{D}
give rise to Hilbert complexes. The rest of the proof carries over verbatim from the proof of Theorem
3.10
.
∎

3.4.
The Signed Schrödinger Operator Perspective

Our goal in this section is to relate the Laplacians introduced above to discrete signed Schrödinger operators. This will enable us to prove statements about Laplacians by exploiting the theory that has already been developed there.

Given a locally summable weight
m
on a simplicial complex
\Sigma
and
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
, we define
b^{{\circ}}:\Sigma\times\Sigma\to[0,\infty)
for
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma
by

\displaystyle b^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=m(\tau\cup\tau^{\prime})1_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\prec\tau\cup\tau^{\prime}},

\displaystyle b^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{m(\tau)m(\tau^{\prime})}{m(\tau\cap\tau^{\prime})}1_{\tau\cap\tau^{\prime}\prec\tau,\tau^{\prime}},

\displaystyle b^{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=|b^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})-b^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})|.

Then
b^{{\circ}}
is symmetric, zero on the diagonal and satisfies for all
\tau\in\Sigma

\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{{\circ}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})<\infty

by local summability. Observe that if
b^{{\circ}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})>0
, then
\dim(\tau)=\dim(\tau^{\prime})
.
Furthermore, let
o^{{\circ}}:\Sigma\times\Sigma\to\{-1,0,1\}
be given for
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma
by

\displaystyle o^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\theta(\tau,\tau\cup\tau^{\prime})\theta(\tau^{\prime},\tau\cup\tau^{\prime})1_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\prec\tau\cup\tau^{\prime}}

\displaystyle o^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\theta(\tau\cap\tau^{\prime},\tau)\theta(\tau\cap\tau^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})1_{\tau\cap\tau^{\prime}\prec\tau,\tau^{\prime}},

\displaystyle o^{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=o^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\mathrm{sgn}(b^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})-b^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})).

Clearly,
o^{{\circ}}
is symmetric and is zero on the diagonal as well.
Finally, let
c^{{\circ}}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
be given for
\tau\in\Sigma
by

\displaystyle c^{+}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{dim}(\tau)\sum_{\tau\prec\sigma}m(\sigma),

\displaystyle c^{-}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\frac{m(\tau)}{m(\rho)}\Big(m(\tau)-\sum_{\rho\prec\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau}m(\tau^{\prime})\Big),

\displaystyle c^{H}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\frac{m^{2}(\tau)}{m(\rho)}+\sum_{\tau\prec\sigma}m(\sigma)-\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\sum_{\rho\prec\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau}\Big|\frac{m(\tau)m(\tau^{\prime})}{m(\rho)}-\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\prec\sigma}m(\sigma)\Big|.

These quantities give rise to signed Schrödinger operators, cf.
[
GKS16
]
. We define the
formal signed Schrödinger operator

\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
acting as

\mathcal{H}^{\circ}\omega(\tau)=\frac{1}{m(\tau)}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})(\omega(\tau)-o^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\omega(\tau^{\prime}))+\frac{c^{\circ}(\tau)}{m(\tau)}\omega(\tau)

on

{F}^{\circ}={F}^{\circ}(\Sigma)=\{\eta\in F\mid\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})|\omega(\tau^{\prime})|<\infty~\text{for all}~\tau\in\Sigma\}.

Because
b^{H}\leq b^{+}+b^{-}
we see that

F^{c}\subseteq F^{{\circ}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad F^{-}\cap F^{+}\subseteq F^{H}.

It is easy to show that
F^{+}
coincides with the respective set in Theorem
2.11
(c), hence the same name.
The term
c^{H}/\penalty m
is known in the literature as
Forman curvature
or
Forman-Ricci curvature
(
[
For03
,
JM21
]
). In a sense, the Schrödinger operator representation for
\Delta^{H}
resulting from Theorem
3.13
below can be interpreted as Weitzenböck identity (
[
Cha84
, Proposition A3.]
) in which
c^{H}
plays the role of the Ricci curvature, see for example
[
For03
]
and
[
JM21
]
. Furthermore,
o^{\circ}
can be viewed as a magnetic potential.

The aim now is to relate
\mathcal{H}^{+},\mathcal{H}^{-}
and
\mathcal{H}^{H}
to
\partial\delta
,
\delta\partial
and
\partial\delta+\delta\partial
.

Theorem 3.12

(Action of
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
)
.

One has
F^{+}\subseteq D(\partial\delta)
,
F^{-}=D(\delta\partial)
,
{F}^{+}\cap F^{-}\subseteq D(\partial\delta+\delta\partial)
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{+}

\displaystyle=\partial\delta

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt\mbox{ on }{F}^{+},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{-}

\displaystyle=\delta\partial

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt\mbox{ on }F^{-}=D(\delta\partial),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{H}

\displaystyle=\partial\delta+\delta\partial

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt\mbox{ on }{F}^{+}\cap{F}^{-}.

If the simplicial complex is locally finite, then equality holds on
{F}
. Furthermore, for
\eta\in{F}^{c}

\displaystyle\sum_{\Sigma}m(\mathcal{H}^{+}\omega)\eta

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Sigma}m(\delta\omega)(\delta\eta),

\displaystyle\omega\in{F}^{+},

\displaystyle\sum_{\Sigma}m(\mathcal{H}^{-}\omega)\eta

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Sigma}m(\partial\omega)(\partial\eta),

\displaystyle\omega\in F^{-}=D(\delta\partial),

\displaystyle\sum_{\Sigma}m(\mathcal{H}^{H}\omega)\eta

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Sigma}m\delta\omega\delta\eta+\sum_{\Sigma}m\partial\omega\partial\eta,

\displaystyle\omega\in{F}^{+}\cap{F}^{-}.

The first step in the proof is achieved by the following representation of the quadratic form
Q^{{\circ}}_{D}
.

Lemma 3.13
.

For
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
and
\omega\in D(Q^{\circ}_{D})
,

Q^{\circ}_{D}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})(\omega(\tau)-o^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\omega(\tau^{\prime}))^{2}+\sum_{\tau\in\Sigma}c^{\circ}(\tau)\omega^{2}(\tau).

Proof.

We denote
Q^{{\circ}}=Q^{{\circ}}_{D}
for the proof.
First, notice that for any fixed
\tau\in\Sigma
, one has with
C_{\tau}=(\mathrm{dim}(\tau)+1)

\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau}|Q^{+}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}})|\leq\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\prec\sigma}m(\sigma)=\sum_{\tau\prec\sigma}m(\sigma)\hskip-5.69046pt\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\prec\sigma,\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau}\hskip-5.69046pt1=C_{\tau}\sum_{\tau\prec\sigma}m(\sigma)<\infty

by local summability. A similar calculation shows that
\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau}|Q^{\circ}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}})|<\infty
for all
\tau\in\Sigma
and
{\circ}\in\{-,H\}.

Thus, by writing
\omega\in{F}^{c}
as
\omega=\sum_{\tau}\omega(\tau)1_{\tau}
and rearranging terms, we may write
Q^{\circ}(\omega)
as

Q^{\circ}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}}|Q^{\circ}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}})|\left(\omega(\tau)+\mathrm{sgn}(Q^{\circ}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}}))\omega(\tau^{\prime})\right)^{2}\\
+\sum_{\tau\in\Sigma}\Big(Q^{\circ}(1_{\tau})-\sum_{\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}}|Q^{\circ}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}})|\Big)\omega^{2}(\tau).

Therefore, we obtain the asserted representation with

\displaystyle b^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=|Q^{\circ}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}})|,

\displaystyle o^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{sgn}(Q^{\circ}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}})),

\displaystyle c^{\circ}(\tau)

\displaystyle=Q^{\circ}(1_{\tau})-\sum_{\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}}|Q^{\circ}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}})|

for
\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}
and
b^{{\circ}}=o^{{\circ}}=0
on the diagonal.
To finish the proof, we compute
b^{{\circ}}
,
o^{{\circ}}
and
c^{{\circ}}
explicitly. The result for
\omega\in D(Q^{{\circ}})
follows then by density of
{F}^{c}
in
D(Q^{{\circ}})
.

We see that for
\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}
,

Q^{+}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}})=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}m(\sigma)\delta 1_{\tau}(\sigma)\delta 1_{\tau^{\prime}}(\sigma)=\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau,\tau^{\prime}}m(\sigma)\theta(\tau,\sigma)\theta(\tau^{\prime},\sigma).

Note that there is at most one
\sigma\succ\tau,\tau^{\prime}
which is then
\tau\cup\tau^{\prime}
. This immediately yields the representations of
b^{+}
and
o^{+}.
The summability of
b^{+}
follows directly from the local summability of
m
.
Likewise, we get
Q^{+}(1_{\tau})=\sum_{\sigma}m(\sigma)1_{\tau\prec\sigma}.
Consequently, as there are exactly
\dim(\tau)+2
faces in every
\sigma\succ\tau

c^{+}(\tau)=\sum_{\tau\prec\sigma}m(\sigma)-\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\prec\sigma}m(\sigma)=-\mathrm{dim}(\tau)\sum_{\tau\prec\sigma}m(\sigma).

Analogously we see that for
\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}

Q^{-}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}})=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma}m(\rho)\partial 1_{\tau}(\rho)\partial 1_{\tau^{\prime}}(\rho)=\sum_{\rho\prec\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\frac{1}{m(\rho)}m(\tau)\theta(\rho,\tau)m(\tau^{\prime})\theta(\rho,\tau^{\prime}).

There is at most one
\rho\prec\tau,\tau^{\prime}
which is then
\tau\cap{\tau}^{\prime}
.
This yields the representations for
b^{-}
and
o^{-}.
The summability of
b^{-}
follows directly from the local summability of
m
.
Likewise,
Q^{-}(1_{\tau})=\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}{m(\tau)^{2}}/{m(\rho)}
and the representation of
c^{-}
follows immediately.

Finally, as
Q^{H}=Q^{+}+Q^{-}
on
F^{c},
we find that

Q^{H}(1_{\tau},1_{\tau^{\prime}})=o^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})b^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})+o^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})b^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}).

The representations of
b^{H}
and
o^{H}
follow by noting that
o^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-o^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
whenever
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
share a common coface
\sigma,
which is a reformulation of
\delta\delta 1_{\rho}(\sigma)=0
(cf. the proof of Lemma
2.7
). The representation of
c^{H}
also follows immediately.
∎

To relate the representation for the quadratic form to the operator
\mathcal{H}^{{\circ}}
we need a Green’s formula.

Proposition 3.14

(Green’s formula for
\mathcal{H}^{{\circ}}
)
.

(a)

Let
\omega\in F^{\circ},\eta\in{F}^{c}.
Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{\Sigma}m(\mathcal{H}^{\circ}\omega)\eta=\sum_{\Sigma}m\omega(\mathcal{H^{\circ}\eta}).

(b)

Let
\omega,\eta\in{F}^{c}.
Then,

\sum_{\Sigma}m(\mathcal{H}^{\circ}\omega)\eta=Q^{{\circ}}(\omega,\eta).

Proof.

The statement
(a)
follows directly from
[
GKS16
, Lemma 2.1.]
and for
(b)
we combine this with Lemma
3.13
above.
∎

After we took care of how the action of (the quadratic forms associated to)
\mathcal{H}^{{\circ}}
relates to the one of our Laplacians, we next come to the issue of their domains.

Lemma 3.15
.

We have the following inclusions:

(a)

F^{-}=F^{1}=D(\delta\partial)
.

(b)

F^{+}\subseteq D(\partial\delta)
.

(c)

F^{-}\cap F^{+}\subseteq D(\partial\delta+\delta\partial)
.

If the simplicial complex is locally finite, then also

{F}^{+}={F}^{H}=D(\partial\delta)=D(\partial\delta+\delta\partial)={F}.

Proof.

(a)
By the definition of
b^{-}
we obtain

\displaystyle F^{-}

\displaystyle=\{\eta\in F\mid\sum_{\tau^{\prime},\tau\cap\tau^{\prime}\prec\tau}\frac{m(\tau)m(\tau^{\prime})}{m(\tau\cap\tau^{\prime})}|\omega(\tau^{\prime})|<\infty~\text{for all}~\tau\in\Sigma\}.

We observe for
\omega\in F

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau^{\prime},\tau\cap\tau^{\prime}\prec\tau}\frac{m(\tau)m(\tau^{\prime})}{m(\tau\cap\tau^{\prime})}|\omega(\tau^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=m(\tau)\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\frac{1}{m(\rho)}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\succ\rho,\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau}m(\tau^{\prime})|\omega(\tau^{\prime})|.

There are at most finitely many
\rho\prec\tau
for any
\tau\in\Sigma
, so we have
\inf_{\rho\prec\tau}m(\rho)>0
and, therefore, the right hand side is finite if and only if
\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\succ\rho}m(\tau^{\prime})|\omega(\tau^{\prime})|<\infty
for all
\rho\in\Sigma
. However, by definition this means
\omega\in F^{1}
. Furthermore, since
\partial
maps
F^{1}=D(\partial)
to
F=D(\delta)
, we infer
F^{1}=D(\partial)=D(\delta\partial)
.

(b)
By the triangle inequality, we have for
\omega\in F^{+}

\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)|\delta\omega(\sigma)|\leq\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\prec\sigma}|\omega(\tau^{\prime})|=\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})|\omega(\tau^{\prime})|+|\omega(\tau)|\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma).

The right hand side is finite as
\omega\in F^{+}
and due to local summability. Hence,
\delta\omega\in F^{1}
and, therefore,
\omega\in D(\partial\delta)
.

(c)
This follows now directly from
(a)
and
(b).

In the locally finite case, we have
{F}^{+}={F}^{H}={F},
which yields the result.
∎

With these preparations, we come to the proof relating Schrödinger operators to Laplacians.

Proof of Theorem
3.12
.

Let
\omega\in{F}^{+}
. By the lemma above we have
\omega\in D(\partial\delta)
. We write
\omega=\sum_{\tau}\omega(\tau)1_{\tau}
and use Green’s formulas, Proposition
3.14

(a)
,
(b)
, and Theorem
2.11
(c),

m(\sigma)\mathcal{H}^{+}\omega(\sigma)=\sum_{\Sigma}m(\mathcal{H}^{+}\omega)1_{\sigma}=\sum_{\Sigma}m\omega(\mathcal{H}^{+}1_{\sigma})=\sum_{\tau\in\Sigma}\omega(\tau)Q^{+}(1_{\tau},1_{\sigma})\\
=\sum_{\tau\in\Sigma}\omega(\tau)\sum_{\Sigma}m(\partial\delta 1_{\tau})1_{\sigma}=m(\sigma)\partial\delta\omega(\sigma).

The statements for
\mathcal{H}^{-}
and
\mathcal{H}^{H}
follow analogously. In the locally finite case, the lemma above gives
{F}^{\circ}={F}
.
The formulas follow now directly from Green’s formulas, Theorem
2.11
, (a) and (b).
∎

The theorem, together with Theorem
3.4
, readily gives for any Laplacian

\Delta^{-}=\mathcal{H}^{-}\qquad\mbox{on }D(\Delta^{-}).

Next, we give an example which shows that the inclusion
F^{+}\subseteq D(\partial\delta)
is strict in general and, therefore, shows that the situation for
\Delta^{+}
and
\Delta^{H}

is more complicated.

Example 3.16

(
{F}^{+}\neq D(\partial\delta)
)
.

Let
V=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\}
. We consider the finite dimensional simplicial complex whose maximal faces are the triangles
\tau_{n}=\{0,n,\infty\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a locally summable weight
m
.
Let

\omega=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\theta(\{0,n\},\tau_{n})1_{\{0,n\}}-\theta(\{n,\infty\},\tau_{n})1_{\{n,\infty\}}\right)\frac{1}{m(\tau_{n})}.

Then,
\delta\omega=0
and, hence,
\omega\in D(\partial\delta)
. However,

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}b^{+}(\{0,\infty\},\{n,\infty\})|\omega(\{n,\infty\})|=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}m(\tau_{n})\frac{1}{m(\tau_{n})}=\infty,

which gives
\omega\not\in{F}^{+}
. Indeed, a similar construction of
\omega
can be carried out in any simplicial complex, where one has non-locally finiteness on the level of triangles.

4.
Form Uniqueness and Essential self-adjointness

From the previous section it can be seen that, in general, one cannot speak of
the
Laplacian on an infinite simplicial complex. There is rather a family of Laplacians
\Delta^{{\circ}}
whose quadratic forms
Q^{{\circ}}
satisfy
Q_{D}^{{\circ}}\subseteq Q^{{\circ}}\subseteq Q_{N}^{{\circ}}
for each
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
. The goal of this section is to study criteria for uniqueness, i.e., when one can speak of
the
Laplacian.

From an operator theoretic point of view, we answer this question in two ways. The first is to give criteria which yield
\Delta_{D}^{{\circ}}=\Delta_{N}^{{\circ}}
, so there is a unique Laplacian in the sense of Definition
3.3
.
The second is to show essential self-adjointness of the restrictions
\Delta^{\circ}_{c}
of
\Delta^{\circ}
to
{F}^{c}
. However, this requires
\Delta^{\circ}_{c}
to be a symmetric operator on
\ell^{2},
for which we gave criteria in Theorem
3.17
and Proposition
3.18
. This also excludes the existence of more self-adjoint operators which are restrictions of
\delta\partial
,
\partial\delta
and
\delta\partial+\partial\delta
but may not have quadratic forms that are restrictions of
Q^{{\circ}}_{N}
. To study these questions, we heavily draw from the theory of magnetic Schrödinger operators, to which we bridged the gap in Section
3.4
.

We start by characterizing boundedness in the next section, which trivially gives the desired uniqueness. In the section that follows, we provide various abstract criteria which are the foundation of the concrete results. Such are criteria on the simplicial complex as a measure space involving the curvature and as metric space.

4.1.
Boundedness

In this section, we characterize boundedness of the operators. For graphs, it is well known that boundedness of the graph Laplacian is equivalent to boundedness of the vertex degree. Here, we identify quantities which play a similar role. For
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
, we let
\gamma^{{\circ}}(\tau)=\mathcal{Q}^{{\circ}}(1_{\tau})
which is given for
{\circ}=+
by

\displaystyle\gamma^{+}(\tau)=\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma).

We obtain a characterization for finite dimensional complexes and a sufficient criterion in general for boundedness of the operators.

Theorem 4.1

(Boundedness)
.

Let
\Delta^{{\circ}}
be a Laplacian.

(a)

If
\sup_{\Sigma}(\dim+1)\gamma^{+}/\penalty m<\infty
, then
\Delta^{\circ}
is bounded for all
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
.

(b)

If
\Sigma
is finite dimensional, then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\Delta^{{\circ}}
is bounded for some (all)
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
.

(ii)

\gamma^{+}/\penalty m
is bounded.

It is remarkable that one can characterize the boundedness of several Laplacians
\Delta^{{\circ}}
arising from forms
Q^{{\circ}}_{D}\subseteq Q^{{\circ}}\subseteq Q^{{\circ}}_{N}
simultaneously.

Theorem
4.1
will be deduced from the following proposition, which provides a characterization even if
\Sigma
is infinite dimensional. On the downside, this characterization includes an assumption on the lower semiboundedness of the quadratic form
q^{{\circ}}
on
{F}^{c}
given by

q^{\circ}(\omega)=2\sum\gamma^{\circ}\omega^{2}-\mathcal{Q}^{\circ}(\omega),

which is far less explicit than boundedness of the function
\gamma^{+}/m
. The next proposition is essentially a direct application of
[
Sch20

for magnetic Schrödinger operators.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let

our form

4.2.

Proposition 4.3

(Gauss-Bonnet Operator)

The following are equivalent:

(i)

(ii)

(\delta+\partial)_{D}=(\delta+\partial)_{N}

(ii)

The Gauss-Bonnet operator

(i)\Leftrightarrow(ii)

(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)

(Form uniqueness)
.

The following are equivalent:

\alpha>0

\eta=(\Delta^{\circ}_{D}+\alpha)^{-1}((\Delta^{\circ}_{c})^{*}+\lambda)\omega=(\Delta^{\circ}_{D}+\alpha)^{-1}(\mathcal{H}^{\circ}+\lambda)\omega\in D(\Delta^{\circ}_{D}).

If
\Delta^{\circ}_{D}\neq(\Delta^{\circ}_{c})^{*}
, then there is
\omega\neq\eta
. On the other hand, since
\Delta_{D}^{{\circ}}
is a restriction of
\mathcal{H}^{{\circ}}
by Theorem
3.19
, we have

(\mathcal{H}^{\circ}+\lambda)(\omega-\eta)=0
which contradicts
(iii)
.∎

We finish the section by showing that essential self-adjointness of
\Delta^{\pm}_{c}
can be deduced from the essential self-adjointness of
\Delta^{H}_{c}.

Proposition 4.8
.

If
F^{c}\subseteq D(\Delta_{D}^{H})
and
\Delta^{H}_{c}
is essentially self-adjoint, then
\Delta^{\pm}_{c}
is essentially self-adjoint.

Proof.

We have that
F^{c}\subseteq D(\Delta_{D}^{H})
implies
F^{c}\subseteq D(\Delta_{D}^{+})
(cf. Proposition
3.18
or the proof of Theorem
3.17
) and we always have
F^{c}\subseteq D(\Delta_{D}^{-})
by Theorem
3.17
. Thus,
\ell^{2}\subseteq F^{+}\cap F^{-}
and
\mathcal{H}^{H}=\delta\partial+\partial\delta
on
\ell^{2}
by Proposition
3.18
and Theorem
3.12
. Now, observe for
\omega\in F^{c}

\|(\delta\partial+\partial\delta)\omega\|^{2}-\|\delta\partial\omega\|^{2}-\|\partial\delta\omega\|^{2}=2\langle\delta\partial\omega,\partial\delta\omega\rangle=2\langle\partial\omega,\partial\partial\delta\omega\rangle=0,

where the second equality holds by Stokes’ theorem, Theorem
2.9
as
\partial\omega\in F^{c}
and the last equality holds by Lemma
2.4
as
\delta\omega\in F^{2}
by Lemma
3.1
(d).
Thus, we have

\|\Delta_{c}^{\pm}\omega\|^{2}\leq\|\delta\partial\omega\|^{2}+\|\partial\delta\omega\|^{2}=\|(\delta\partial+\partial\delta)\omega\|^{2}=\|\Delta_{c}^{H}\omega\|^{2},

which means that the operators
\Delta_{c}^{\pm}
both are
\Delta_{c}^{H}
-bounded with bound
1
and symmetric by Green’s formula, Proposition
2.11
. Thus, the statement follows from
[
Kat95
, Theorem 4.6]
and the essential self-adjointness of
\Delta_{c}^{H}
.

∎

4.3.
A Weight and Curvature Criterion

In this section we give a criterion arising from the weight and curvature. Recall to this end that the quantity
c^{H}/m
introduced in Section
3.4
is referred to as Forman curvature.

Theorem 4.9
.

If
m
is uniformly positive and the Forman curvature

is bounded from below, then
\Delta^{\circ}_{c}
is essentially self-adjoint for all
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
.

To prove this, we apply the theory of Schrödinger operators on graphs. By Section
3.4
we have the graphs
b^{{\circ}}
associated to
Q^{{\circ}}
. We call a sequence of elements of
\Sigma
a path if it is a path with respect to
b^{{\circ}}
.
Recall the quantities
\gamma^{{\circ}}(\tau)=Q^{{\circ}}(1_{\tau})
,
\tau\in\Sigma
, introduced to characterize boundedness in Section
4.1
. The following criterion is based on
[
Gol14
,
GKS16
]
. To this end observe that for
\tau\in\Sigma

\gamma^{\circ}(\tau)-c^{\circ}(\tau)=Q^{{\circ}}(1_{\tau})-c^{{\circ}}(\tau)=\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{{\circ}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\geq 0.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
.
If there is
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that for every infinite path
(\tau_{n})
with respect to
b^{{\circ}}
one has

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}m(\tau_{n})\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(1+\frac{c^{\circ}(\tau_{j})-\alpha m(\tau_{j})}{\gamma^{\circ}(\tau_{j})-c^{\circ}(\tau_{j})}\right)^{2}=\infty,

then

whenever
{\circ}=\pm
. If, in addition,
{F}^{c}\subseteq D(\Delta^{\circ}_{D})
, then
\Delta^{\circ}_{c}
is essentially self-adjoint and, in particular,
Q^{{\circ}}_{D}=Q^{{\circ}}_{N}.

Proof.

The proof of
[
GKS16
, Theorem 2.14]
shows that under the present assumption there are no non-trivial solutions
\omega\in F^{{\circ}}\cap\ell^{2}
such that
(\mathcal{H}^{{\circ}}-\lambda)\omega=0
for
\lambda
smaller than a certain
\lambda_{0}
. Since
\mathcal{H}^{-}=\mathcal{L}^{-}
, we conclude
Q^{-}_{D}=Q^{-}_{N}
from Proposition
4.5
and
Q^{+}_{D}=Q^{+}_{N}
from Proposition
4.4
. Moreover, we have
\mathcal{H}^{{\circ}}=\mathcal{L}^{{\circ}}
on
F^{c}
if
{F}^{c}\subseteq D(\Delta^{\circ}_{D})
by Proposition
3.18
and Theorem
3.4
. Thus, the essential self-adjointness follows from Proposition
4.7
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.9
.

According to Theorem
3.17
we have
{F}^{c}\subseteq D(\Delta^{\circ}_{D}).
We assume
c^{\circ}\geq-Km
for some
K>0
. Choosing
\alpha=-K
, we find that

\frac{c^{\circ}+Km}{(\gamma^{\circ}-c^{\circ})}\geq 0

since
\gamma^{\circ}-c^{\circ}\geq 0
.
Thus, the statement for

as uniform positivity of

∎

4.4.
A Metric Space Criterion

In this section we show a criterion for form uniqueness and essential self-adjointness in terms of a metric on the 1-skeleton of the complex. This stands in analogy to a theorem of Gaffney
[
Gaf51
,
Roe60
,
Che73
,
Str83
]
that on a complete Riemannian manifold all Laplacians on forms are essentially self-adjoint. To this end, we define a metric on the 1-skeleton which itself is a graph. Here we have the usual adjacency relation
\sim
between vertices if they are connected by an edge, i.e., contained in a common
1
-simplex. We will define a path metric on the 1-skeleton of
\Sigma
as follows.
Let
v,v^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{0}.
We say that
v
and
v^{\prime}
are connected if
b^{+}(v,v^{\prime})>0.
In that case we will write
v\sim v^{\prime}.
We then define

d^{{\circ}}(v,v^{\prime})=\inf_{v=v_{0}\sim\ldots\sim v_{n}=v^{\prime}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}w^{{\circ}}(v_{k},v_{k+1}),

where
w^{{\circ}}(x,x)=0
and for
x\sim x^{\prime}

\displaystyle w^{+}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\tau\cap\{x,x^{\prime}\}\neq\varnothing}\Big(\frac{\dim(\tau)+1}{m(\tau)}\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)\Big)^{-1/2},

\displaystyle w^{-}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\tau\cap\{x,x^{\prime}\}\neq\varnothing}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\succ\tau^{\prime}\cap\tau}\frac{m(\tau^{\prime})}{m(\tau\cap{\tau^{\prime}})}\Big)^{-1/2},

\displaystyle w^{H}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\min\{w^{+}(x,x^{\prime}),w^{-}(x,x^{\prime})\}.

This yields path pseudo metrics on
\Sigma_{0}.

Remark
.

The motivation to study this metric stems from the concept of intrinsic metrics, see e.g.
[
KLW21
, Chapter 11]
and references therein and has shown to be vital for a Gaffney theorem for graphs, see
[
HKMW13
]
or
[
KLW21
, Chapter 12.3]
.

For Riemannian manifolds metric or geodesic completeness is equivalent to compactness of closed metric balls by the Hopf-Rinow theorem. For graphs this remains true in the locally finite case
[
KLW21
, Theorem 11.16]
.

Theorem 4.11

(Gaffney-type theorem)
.

If for
d^{{\circ}}
,
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
, all metric balls are finite and
\Sigma
is locally finite or balanced, then
\Delta^{\circ}_{c}
is essentially self-adjoint.

Remark
.

In
[
ATH15
]
the authors use a different notion of completeness, that is the existence of certain cut-off functions. However, this is equivalent to the existence of an intrinsic metric with finite metric balls,
[
KLW21
, Theorem 11.31]
.

For the proof, we draw again from the theory of operators on graphs. The following proposition is a variation of
[
GKS16
, Theorem 2.16]
or
[
HKMW13
, Theorem 2]
.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
and let
d
be an intrinsic metric, i.e. a pseudo metric such that
\sum_{\tau^{\prime}}b^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})d^{2}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\leq m(\tau)
for all
\tau\in\Sigma.
Assume that all metric balls are finite. If
{F}^{c}\subseteq D(\Delta^{\circ}_{D}),
then
\Delta^{\circ}_{c}
is essentially self-adjoint.

Proof.

By restricting our attention to connected components, we assume that the graph
b^{\circ}
is connected. By Proposition
3.18
we know that
\ell^{2}\subseteq F^{\circ}.

Let
u\in\ell^{2}
and
\lambda>0
such that
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}u+\lambda u=0.
For
g\in F^{c}
we have

Q^{\circ}_{N}(ug)+\lambda\|ug\|^{2}=Q^{\circ}_{D}(ug)+\lambda\|ug\|^{2}=Q^{(u)}(g)

with

Q^{(u)}(g)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}b^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})o^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})u(\tau)u(\tau^{\prime})|g(\tau)-g(\tau^{\prime})|^{2}.

This follows from a direct computation (see also
[
GKS16
, Proposition 2.18]
). Now let
R>0
and fix
\tau_{0}\in\Sigma.
Define

\eta_{R}(\tau)=1\wedge\frac{(2R-d(\tau,\tau_{0}))_{+}}{R}.

By assumption all
\eta_{R}
have finite support and
1_{R}\leq\eta_{R}\leq 1
where
1_{R}
is the characteristic function on the metric ball with radius
R.
We get

Q^{\circ}(u\eta_{R})+\lambda\|u\eta_{R}\|^{2}=Q^{(u)}(\eta_{R})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})(u(\tau)^{2}+u(\tau^{\prime})^{2})|\eta_{R}(\tau)-\eta_{R}(\tau^{\prime})|^{2}\\
\leq\frac{1}{R^{2}}\sum_{\tau\in\Sigma}u^{2}(\tau)\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})d^{2}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{R^{2}}\|u\|^{2}.

Letting
R
tend to infinity, we see that
\|u\eta_{R}\|
has to vanish in the limit since the left hand side is greater than zero. The statement now follows from Theorem
4.7
.
∎

We come to the proof of Theorem
4.11
.

Proof of Theorem
4.11
.

We want to apply the proposition above. We have to show that the metric
d^{\circ}
gives rise to an intrinsic metric on the entire simplicial complex.

Inspired by the Hausdorff distance between subsets of metric spaces, we define the pseudo metrics
d_{h}^{\circ}:\Sigma\rightarrow[0,\infty)
as

d_{h}^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\max\{\max_{v\in\tau}\min_{v^{\prime}\in\tau^{\prime}}d^{\circ}(v,v^{\prime}),\max_{v^{\prime}\in\tau^{\prime}}\min_{v\in\tau}d^{\circ}(v,v^{\prime})\}.

If all distance balls on the
1
-skeleton are finite, then, as consequence of the fact that every simplex has only finitely many vertices, it follows easily that all distance balls on
\Sigma
with respect to
d_{h}^{\circ}
are finite as well. Moreover, it turns out that
d_{h}^{\circ}
is intrinsic: First of all, if
b^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})>0
for
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma
, then

\displaystyle d_{h}^{\circ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\max\{\min_{v^{\prime}\in\tau^{\prime}}d^{\circ}(\tau\setminus\tau^{\prime},v^{\prime}),\min_{v\in\tau}d^{\circ}(v,\tau^{\prime}\setminus\tau)\}\leq d^{\circ}(\tau\backslash\tau^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\backslash\tau)

Secondly, we can write for
v,v^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{0}

w^{\pm}(v,v^{\prime})=\inf_{\tau\cap\{v,v^{\prime}\}\neq\varnothing}\lambda_{\pm}\Big(\frac{1}{m(\tau)}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{\pm}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\Big)^{-1/2},

where
\lambda_{+}=1,\lambda_{-}=1/\penalty 2.
Now observe that whenever
b^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})>0,
then
\tau\backslash\tau^{\prime}
and
\tau^{\prime}\backslash\tau
are adjacent vertices in the 1-skeleton of
\Sigma.
Similarly, if
b^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})>0,
then
\tau\backslash\tau^{\prime}
and
\tau^{\prime}\backslash\tau
are vertices in
\Sigma_{0}
which are connected in the 1-skeleton of
\Sigma
through any vertex in
\tau\cap\tau^{\prime}.

With these observations it follows that

\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})d^{-}_{h}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})^{2}\leq\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})d^{-}(\tau\setminus\tau^{\prime},\tau\setminus\tau^{\prime})^{2}\\
\leq\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{-}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\left(\inf_{v\in\tau\cap{\tau^{\prime}}}\left(w^{-}(\tau\setminus\tau^{\prime},v)+w^{-}(v,\tau\setminus\tau^{\prime})\right)\right)^{2}\leq m(\tau)

and similarly

\sum_{\tau^{\prime}}b^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})d^{+}_{h}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})^{2}\leq m(\tau)
, so
d^{\pm}_{h}
are intrinsic.
Finally, since
b^{H}=|b^{+}-b^{-}|\leq b^{+}+b^{-}
and
\sqrt{2}d^{H}\leq d^{\pm}
we see that
d^{H}_{h}
is intrinsic as well.
Thus, the statement follows from Theorem
4.12
.
∎

5.
Spectral Relations between Laplacians

In this section, we study several spectral relations between different Laplacians that are well known for finite complexes.
While the overall picture remains consistent, new phenomena occur due to the non-uniqueness of the Laplacian.

The first result is due to an abstract functional analytic fact that operators of the type
T^{*}T
and
TT^{*}
share the same spectrum apart from
0
.

Theorem 5.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\sigma(\Delta^{+}_{D})\backslash\{0\}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\Delta^{-}_{N})\backslash\{0\},

\displaystyle\sigma(\Delta^{-}_{D})\backslash\{0\}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\Delta^{+}_{N})\backslash\{0\},

\displaystyle\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{D})\backslash\{0\}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{N})\backslash\{0\}.

The same is true for the essential, pure point, absolutely continuous and singular continuous spectrum.

Proof.

This follows directly
from
[
Tha92
, Corollary 5.6.]
or
[
FLW23
, Proposition 1.23]
and Corollary
3.8
.
∎

Remark
.

If we restrict our attention to
\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{k},m)
and denote the appropriate restriction of
\Delta^{\circ}_{D}
by
\Delta^{\circ}_{D,k}
then the first two equalities in Theorem
5.1
become

\sigma(\Delta^{+}_{D,k})\backslash\{0\}=\sigma(\Delta^{-}_{N,k+1})\backslash\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma(\Delta^{+}_{N,k})\backslash\{0\}=\sigma(\Delta^{-}_{D,k+1})\backslash\{0\},

which is a well known relation between up- and down-Laplacians on finite simplicial complexes (see
[
HJ13
]
). The statement about the Hodge-Laplacian is remarkable, as to our knowledge there is no such relation for Hodge-Laplacians defined on
\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{k},m).
This seems to be a special feature of the operator defined across multiple dimensions. Also note that a Hodge-Laplacian with Neumann boundary condition on
\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{k},m)
would not be well defined as
\delta+\partial
maps
{F}(\Sigma_{k})
into
{F}(\Sigma_{k+1})\oplus{F}(\Sigma_{k-1}).

The theorem above excludes statements about
0,
which is only natural. However, if
0
is in
\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{D})
, then it is in all the other spectra, as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
0\in\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{D}),
then
0\in\sigma(\Delta^{\pm}_{D})\cap\sigma(\Delta^{\pm}_{N})\cap\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{N}).
The same is true if
0
is in the essential spectrum of
\Delta^{H}_{D}.

Proof.

Assume
0\in\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{D})
. Since
Q^{H}_{D}\subseteq Q^{H}_{N}
, we clearly have
0\in\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{N})
.
Furthermore, by Weyl’s criterion we find a normalized sequence
\omega_{n}
in
D(\Delta^{H}_{D})
such that
\lim_{n}\|\Delta^{H}_{D}\omega_{n}\|=0.
Therefore,
Q^{H}_{D}(\omega_{n})=\langle\Delta^{H}_{D}\omega_{n},\omega_{n}\rangle
tends to zero as well. However, because
Q^{H}_{D}
is a restriction of
Q^{+}_{D}+Q^{-}_{D}
and all forms are positive, we conclude that
Q^{+}_{D}(\omega_{n})
and
Q^{-}_{D}(\omega_{n})
tend to zero individually.
Thus,
0\in\sigma(\Delta^{\pm}_{D})
and
0\in\sigma(\Delta^{\pm}_{N})
since
Q^{\pm}_{D}\subseteq Q^{\pm}_{N}
.
The statement about the essential spectrum follows the same way by choosing
\omega_{n}
weakly converging to zero (
[
KLW21
, Theorem E.4]
).
∎

The final result shows that, in the case
Q^{H}_{D}=Q^{H}_{N}
, all nonzero spectra agree. Recall that Proposition
4.6
shows that
Q^{H}_{D}=Q^{H}_{N}
implies
Q^{\pm}_{D}=Q^{\pm}_{N}
.

Theorem 5.3
.

If
Q^{H}_{D}=Q^{H}_{N},
then for
\Delta^{{\circ}}=\Delta^{{\circ}}_{D}=\Delta_{N}^{{\circ}}
,
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
,

\sigma(\Delta^{+})\backslash\{0\}=\sigma(\Delta^{H})\backslash\{0\}=\sigma(\Delta^{-})\backslash\{0\}.

The following lemma is vital to the proof.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\lambda\neq 0.
Then

\Delta^{+}_{N}+\Delta^{-}_{D}-\lambda=-\lambda^{-1}(\Delta^{+}_{N}-\lambda)(\Delta^{-}_{D}-\lambda).

Proof.

Let
\omega\in D(\Delta^{+}_{N}+\Delta^{-}_{D})=D(\Delta^{+}_{N})\cap D(\Delta^{-}_{D}).

Then
\delta\Delta^{-}_{D}\omega=\delta\delta\partial\omega=0.
This shows
\Delta^{-}_{D}\omega\in D(\Delta^{+}_{N})
and
\Delta^{+}_{N}\Delta^{-}_{D}\omega=0.
Therefore,

-\lambda^{-1}(\Delta^{+}_{N}-\lambda)(\Delta^{-}_{D}-\lambda)\omega=\Delta^{+}_{N}\omega+\Delta^{-}_{D}\omega-\lambda\omega

which shows that
\Delta^{+}_{N}+\Delta^{-}_{D}-\lambda\subseteq-\lambda^{-1}(\Delta^{+}_{N}-\lambda)(\Delta^{-}_{D}-\lambda).

Assume
\omega\in D((\Delta^{+}_{N}-\lambda)(\Delta^{-}_{D}-\lambda)).
This means in particular that
\omega\in D(\Delta^{-}_{D}).
Moreover,
\Delta^{-}_{D}\omega-\lambda\omega\in D(\Delta^{+}_{N}).
However, this tells us that
-\lambda\delta\omega=\delta(\delta\partial-\lambda)\omega\in\ell^{2}
so
\omega\in D(Q^{+}_{N}).
Furthermore, for
\nu\in D(Q^{+}_{N})
we get

\langle-\lambda\delta\omega,\delta\nu\rangle=\langle\delta(\Delta^{-}_{D}-\lambda)\omega,\delta\nu\rangle=\langle\Delta^{+}_{N}(\Delta^{-}_{D}-\lambda)\omega,\nu\rangle.

Thus, we conclude that
\omega\in D(\Delta^{+}_{N}).
With this at hand we again have the identity
\Delta^{+}_{N}\omega+\Delta^{-}_{D}\omega-\lambda\omega=-\lambda^{-1}(\Delta^{+}_{N}-\lambda)(\Delta^{-}_{D}-\lambda)\omega
and conclude the statement.∎

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Let
Q^{H}_{D}=Q^{H}_{N}
and
\lambda\neq 0.
This implies
\Delta^{\pm}=\Delta^{\pm}_{D}=\Delta_{N}^{\pm}
by Proposition
4.6
, as well as
\Delta^{H}=\Delta^{+}+\Delta^{-}
by the discussions in Section
3.3
. Furthermore, according to Lemma
5.4
we have
\Delta^{H}-\lambda=-\lambda^{-1}(\Delta^{+}-\lambda)(\Delta^{-}-\lambda).

If
\lambda\in\sigma(\Delta^{H})
, then we find by Weyl’s criterion a normalized sequence
(\omega_{n})
in
D(\Delta^{H})
such that
\|(\Delta^{H}-\lambda)\omega_{n}\|\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
However, this means that either
\|(\Delta^{-}-\lambda)\omega_{n}\|\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
or we find a subsequence (also denoted as
(\omega_{n})
) such that
\eta_{n}=(\Delta^{-}-\lambda)\omega_{n}
is uniformly in
n
bounded away from
0
and
\|(\Delta^{+}-\lambda)\eta_{n}\|\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
We conclude with Weyl’s criterion that
\lambda\in\sigma(\Delta^{+})\cup\sigma(\Delta^{-}).
By Theorem
5.1
we get
\lambda\in\sigma(\Delta^{\pm}).
Thus,
\sigma(\Delta^{H})\backslash\{0\}\subseteq\sigma(\Delta^{\pm})\backslash\{0\}.

Now, let
\lambda\in\sigma(\Delta^{+}).

We find a normalized sequence
\omega_{n}\in D(\Delta^{+})
such that

\lim_{n}\|(\Delta^{+}-\lambda)\omega_{n}\|=0.
Let us define the sequence

\mu_{n}=\delta\omega_{n}.

Because
\omega_{n}\in\mathcal{D}^{+}
, we find that
\mu_{n}\in\ell^{2}
and
\|\mu_{n}\|^{2}=\|\delta\omega_{n}\|^{2}=Q^{+}(\omega_{n}).
This implies that
\|\mu_{n}\|
is bounded away from zero for large enough
n
since
\lambda>0
and

Q^{+}(\omega_{n})=\langle(\Delta^{+}-\lambda)\omega_{n},\omega_{n}\rangle+\lambda.

Next, we observe that
\delta\mu_{n}=0
and
\partial\mu_{n}=\partial\delta\omega_{n}=\Delta^{+}\omega_{n}.
Consequently,

\sum_{\Sigma}m|(\delta+\partial)\mu_{n}|^{2}=\|\Delta^{+}\omega_{n}\|^{2}<\infty.

This tells us that
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{D}^{H}
and all together
\mu_{n}\in D(Q^{H}_{N}).

However, by assumption we have that
Q^{H}=Q^{H}_{D}=Q^{H}_{N}.

We now get for
\nu\in D(Q^{H}_{D})

|Q^{H}(\mu_{n},\nu)-\lambda\langle\mu_{n},\nu\rangle|=|\langle\delta\mu_{n},\delta\nu\rangle+\langle\partial\mu_{n},\partial\nu\rangle-\lambda\langle\mu_{n},\nu\rangle|\\
=|\langle\Delta^{+}\omega_{n},\partial\nu\rangle-\lambda\langle\delta\omega_{n},\nu\rangle|=|\langle(\Delta^{+}-\lambda)\omega_{n},\partial\nu\rangle|\leq\|(\Delta^{+}-\lambda)\omega_{n}\|Q^{H}_{D}(\nu)^{1/2}\to 0.

From
[
Sto01
, Lemma 1.4.4]
(see also
[
KLW21
, Theorem E.3]
) we conclude that
\lambda\in\sigma(\Delta^{H}).
Thus
\sigma(\Delta^{\pm})\backslash\{0\}\subseteq\sigma(\Delta^{H})\backslash\{0\}
by Theorem
5.1
.
∎

Remark
.

For the restrictions of
\Delta^{\circ}
to
\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{k},m)
Theorem
5.3
reads as

\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{k})\backslash\{0\}=(\sigma(\Delta^{+}_{k})\cup\sigma(\Delta^{-}_{k}))\backslash\{0\},

which is another well known relation on finite simplicial complexes.

6.
Examples of Specific Weights

6.1.
Combinatorial Weight

The weight
m
given by
m\equiv 1
is called the
combinatorial weight
. It is by far the most natural choice, but also quite restrictive. By the local summability assumption, it already requires the complex
\Sigma
to be locally finite, that is, every simplex is contained in only finitely many cofaces. On the other hand, many of the formulas above simplify and have interesting combinatorial interpretations. Looking at the Schrödinger operator representation from above, we see that the Forman curvature
c^{H}
simplifies in the following way.
For
\tau\in\Sigma
with
k=\dim(\tau)
, we have

\displaystyle c^{H}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\#\{\rho\prec\tau\}+\#\{\sigma\succ\tau\}-\sum_{\tau^{\prime}}b^{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=2(k+1)+(k+2)\#\{\sigma\succ\tau\}-\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\#\{\tau^{\prime}\succ\rho\}.

Since
c^{H}
is a lower bound for
Q_{D}^{H}
every lower bound on
c^{H}
yields a lower bound for the spectrum
\sigma(\Delta^{H}_{D}).
Of course, this is only useful if
c^{H}
is positive to begin with.

Another consequence is that
b^{{\circ}}
takes values in
\{0,1\}

and
b^{+}
,
b^{H}
become subgraphs of
b^{-}
where
b^{H}
is the complement of
b^{+}
in
b^{-}.

We summarize the results of Section
4
for the combinatorial weight in the following theorem. To this end, we denote the vertex degree
\deg:\Sigma_{0}\to\mathbb{N}

\deg(v)=\#\{e\in\Sigma_{1}\mid e\succ v\}.

Furthermore, let the degree path metric on
\Sigma_{0}
be given by

\rho(v,w)=\inf_{v=v_{0}\sim\ldots\sim v_{n}=w}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\max\{\deg(v_{k}),\deg(v_{k-1})\}^{-1/2}.

Here
v\sim w
means the usual adjacency relation on graphs, i.e.,
v
and
w
are connected by an edge in the 1-skeleton of
\Sigma.

Theorem 6.1

(Combinatorial weight)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a connected finite dimensional simplicial complex with combinatorial weight
m\equiv 1
and
\Delta^{{\circ}}
a Laplacian,
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
.

(a)

\Delta^{{\circ}}
is bounded if and only if
\deg
is bounded.

(b)

F^{c}\subseteq D(\Delta^{{\circ}})
.

(c)

If the Forman curvature
c^{H}
is bounded from below, then
\Delta^{{\circ}}_{c}
is essentially self-adjoint.

(d)

If
(\Sigma_{0},\rho)
is a complete metric space, then
\Delta^{{\circ}}_{c}
is essentially self-adjoint.

Proof.

(a)
We have for
\tau\in\Sigma,v\in\tau

\gamma^{+}(\tau)=\#\{\sigma\succ\tau\}\leq\deg(v)

so boundedness follows from Theorem
4.1
.

(b)
This is clear due to local finiteness and Theorem
3.17
.

(c)
This follows directly from Theorem
4.9
.

(d)
We first observe that finite metric balls are equivalent to completeness for locally finite path metrics, see
[
KLW21
, Theorem 11.16]
or
[
KM19
, Theorem 2.1.]
.
We define a path pseudo metric on
\Sigma_{k},

0\leq k\leq\dim(\Sigma)
, as follows. Let

w(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=((\dim(\Sigma)+1)\min_{v\in\sigma\cap\sigma^{\prime}}\deg(v))^{-1/2}

whenever
b^{H}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})>0
(we write
\sigma\sim\sigma^{\prime}
) and
w(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=0
otherwise. Then

d(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\inf_{\sigma=\sigma_{0}\sim\ldots\sim\sigma_{n}=\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}w(\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k-1})

defines a path pseudo metric. Moreover,
d
is intrinsic for
b^{H}
as for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}

\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}b^{H}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})d^{2}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\leq\sum_{\rho\prec\sigma}\sum_{\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}\succ\rho}((\dim(\Sigma)+1)\min_{v\in\rho}\deg(v))^{-1}\\
\leq\sum_{\rho\prec\sigma}\frac{\gamma^{+}(\rho)}{(\dim(\Sigma)+1)\min_{v\in\rho}\deg(v)}\leq 1.

We continue the proof with two claims

Claim 1.
For all
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{k}
, we find
v\in\sigma,v^{\prime}\in\sigma^{\prime}
such that

\rho(v,v^{\prime})\leq(\dim(\Sigma)+1)^{1/2}d(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

Proof of the claim.

Take an arbitrary path between
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime},
i.e.,
\sigma=\sigma_{0}\sim\ldots\sim\sigma_{n}=\sigma^{\prime}.
Now let
v_{k}
be any vertex in
\sigma_{k}\cap\sigma_{k+1},
which is non empty as
b^{H}(\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k+1})>0.
For any
k=0,\ldots,n-1
, we either have
v_{k}=v_{k+1}
or
v_{k}
and
v_{k+1}
are connected by an edge in the 1-skeleton of
\Sigma.
This is because both are contained in
\sigma_{k+1}.
That way we obtain a path in the 1-skeleton of
\Sigma
that connects
v_{0}\in\sigma
and
v_{n}\in\sigma^{\prime}.
The claim follows as

(\dim(\Sigma)+1)^{1/2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}w(\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k-1})\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\deg(v_{k-1})^{-1/2}\geq\rho(v,v^{\prime}).

Claim 2.
If
\rho
has finite distance balls then
d
has finite distance balls as well.

Proof of the claim.
Assume there is an infinite distance ball of radius
r
centered at some
\sigma_{0}\in\Sigma
for
d
. By local finiteness there are infinitely many disjoint
\sigma_{n}\in\Sigma
such that for
C=(\dim(\Sigma)+1)^{1/2}

Cr\geq Cd(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})\geq\rho(v_{0},v_{n})

with vertices
v_{0}\in\sigma_{0}
and
v_{n}\in\sigma_{n}
by the claim above. As
\sigma_{0}
contains only finitely many vertices, there is an infinite distance ball for
\rho,
which settles the claim.

By Claim 2 and since
d
is intrinsic, we get essential self-adjointness of
\Delta^{H}_{c}
from Proposition
4.12
. The statement for
\Delta^{\pm}_{c}
then follows from Theorem
4.8
.∎

6.2.
Normalizing Weight

In the case of weighted graphs, one typically defines the normalizing weight
m
on vertices as
m(x)=\sum_{e_{x}}m(e_{x}),
where the sum runs over all edges
e_{x}
that contain the vertex
x
. This has two very useful effects. First, it makes the graph Laplacian
\Delta
bounded such that the spectrum is contained in the interval
[0,2].

The second effect is that
\Delta
takes the form
\Delta=I-P
where
P
is the transition matrix, which defines the standard random walk on the graph. This means that the kernel
p(x,y)
of
P
can be seen as the probability of jumping from a vertex
x
to a vertex
y.

In
[
HJ13
, Theorem 3.2.]
the weight

m^{+}(\tau)=\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m^{+}(\sigma)

for
\tau\in\Sigma
, with
m^{+}
arbitrary on the maximal faces, was already considered and it was shown that the spectrum on
\Sigma_{k}
is included in
[0,k+2]
. Here, we discuss that a similar result holds for infinite complexes.

Theorem 6.2
.

For
m^{+}
, all operators
\Delta^{{\circ}}
,
{\circ}\in\{\pm,H\}
, are bounded if
\Sigma
is finite dimensional. Furthermore, the restrictions to
\Sigma_{k}
satisfy
\sigma(\Delta^{\circ}_{k})\subseteq[0,k+2].
Moreover, if every
\tau\in\Sigma_{k}
has a coface, then
\Delta^{+}_{k}
acts on
\ell^{2}(\Sigma_{k},m)
as

\Delta^{+}_{k}\omega(\tau)=\omega(\tau)-\frac{1}{m^{+}(\tau)}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\theta^{+}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\omega(\tau^{\prime}).

Proof.

Boundedness follows as
\gamma^{+}/m^{+}=1
by Theorem
4.1
. Indeed, as
\Sigma^{N}=\bigcup_{k\leq N}\Sigma_{k}
is a subcomplex of
\Sigma,
all restrictions are bounded. Let
m^{+}_{k},\omega_{k}
be the restrictions of
m^{+}
and
\omega
to
\Sigma_{k}
for some
\omega\in F
. The statement about the spectrum follows by

\|\delta_{k}\omega_{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{\Sigma_{k+1}}m^{+}_{k+1}|\delta_{k}\omega|^{2}\leq(k+2)\sum_{\Sigma_{k}}m^{+}_{k}|\omega_{k}|^{2},

where we used the definition of
m^{+}
and the fact that every
k+1
simplex has
k+2
faces. This yields
\sigma(\Delta^{+}_{k})\subseteq[0,k+2].
The respective statement for
\Delta^{-}_{k}
and
\Delta^{H}_{k}
follow from Theorem
5.3
and Theorem
5.1
and the remarks thereafter.
The proof about the action of
\Delta^{+}_{k}
is just a simple and straight forward calculation.
∎

Observe that while we achieve to write
\Delta^{+}=I-\tilde{P}
, the operator
\tilde{P}
is not a stochastic matrix and not even positive anymore, which makes a probabilistic interpretation a challenging task, see e.g.
[
PR17
]
.

Note that the particular choice of weight in the theorem above makes the diagonal of
\Delta^{+}_{k}
constant, provided every element
\tau\in\Sigma_{k}
has a coface. For
\Delta^{-}_{k}
, the weight

m(\tau)=\Big(\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\frac{1}{m(\rho)}\Big)^{-1}

achieves the same, although in general one does not obtain a bounded operator in this way, cf. Theorem
4.1
. Note that the particular choice of
m
for
\Delta^{+}_{k}
depends on the weights on
\Sigma_{k+1}
where for
\Delta^{-}_{k}
it depends on the weights on
\Sigma_{k-1}.

Therefore it is not surprising that in order to have constant diagonal of
\Delta^{H}_{k}
one needs to control the weights on both
\Sigma_{k+1}
and
\Sigma_{k-1}.
To see this, note that we want

m(\tau)=\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma}b^{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})+c^{H}(\tau)=\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\frac{m^{2}(\tau)}{m(\rho)}+\sum_{\tau\prec\sigma}m(\sigma)

or equivalently

0=m^{2}(\tau)\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\frac{1}{m(\rho)}-m(\tau)+\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma).

This polynomial in
m(\tau)
has positive real solutions, if the coefficients
\sum_{\rho\prec\tau}\frac{1}{m(\rho)}
and
\sum_{\sigma\succ\tau}m(\sigma)

are small enough. In this case, one obtains an operator
\Delta^{H}_{k}
that has constant diagonal.
