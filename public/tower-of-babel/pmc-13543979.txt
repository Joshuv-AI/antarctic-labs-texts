Chemical profiling of vegetable polyphenols reveals matrix-dependent inhibition of heterocyclic amine formation in charcoal-grilled pork
Authors: Shang-Ming Huang, Cheng-Hong Hsieh, Kai-Ching Teng, Wei-Lun Hung, Kuo-Chiang Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Twelve commonly consumed vegetables were screened for heterocyclic amine (HCA) inhibition in charcoal-grilled pork. Ethanolic extracts were evaluated for antioxidant capacity, and polyphenol composition was profiled by UPLC–MS/MS. Principal component analysis identified sweet potato leaves, water spinach, and coriander as the top candidates. HCA inhibition was assessed under three systems: direct marination (1%), commercial barbecue sauce, and homemade barbecue sauce. Inhibition was matrix-dependent (42.85–92.74%), with coriander achieving 92.74% under direct marination and sweet potato leaves 76.78% in homemade sauce. Molecular docking predicted ellagic acid and chlorogenic acid binding near the myosin actin-binding cleft. These findings suggest that HCA mitigation is associated with polyphenol composition and sauce matrix chemistry rather than antioxidant capacity alone, while the docking results provide a testable hypothesis regarding possible polyphenol–myosin interactions.

Introduction

Heterocyclic amines (HCAs) are a class of mutagenic and carcinogenic compounds generated during the thermal processing of protein-rich foods, particularly through the Maillard reaction between amino acids, creatine, and reducing sugars at temperatures exceeding 150 °C (Dong et al., 2020; Jägerstad et al., 1991; Skog et al., 1998). More than 25 HCA species have been identified, classified into two major groups: thermic HCAs (aminoimidazo-azaarenes such as IQ, MeIQ, 8-MeIQx, and PhIP), which form at moderate cooking temperatures (150–300 °C), and pyrolytic HCAs (amino-carbolines such as Harman, Norharman, Trp-P-1, Trp-P-2, AαC, and MeAαC), which form at temperatures above 300 °C (Sugimura et al., 2004). Epidemiological studies have linked dietary HCA intake to increased risks of colorectal, breast, and prostate cancers (Barzegar et al., 2019; Cross et al., 2005; Sinha et al., 2009). Consequently, developing effective strategies to reduce HCA formation during cooking has become an important food safety objective.

Among various mitigation approaches, the application of natural antioxidants has received considerable attention. Plant-derived polyphenols, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, and tannins, have been shown to suppress HCA formation through multiple mechanisms: direct scavenging of free radical intermediates in the Maillard reaction pathway, chelation of catalytic metal ions, and physical interaction with meat proteins to limit the release of HCA precursors (Gibis, 2016; Meurillon & Engel, 2016; Nadeem et al., 2021; Oguri et al., 1998; Oz & Kaya, 2011). Structure–activity studies have further shown that the hydroxyl substitution pattern of polyphenols significantly influences their inhibitory potency against HCA formation (Yang et al., 2023), and recent evidence confirms that polyphenols mitigate Maillard reaction products through multiple convergent pathways including radical scavenging, carbonyl trapping, and protein interaction (Ye et al., 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated HCA inhibition by extracts from rosemary (Tsen et al., 2006), green tea (Quelhas et al., 2010), hibiscus (Gibis & Weiss, 2010), local spices (Jinap et al., 2015), grape seed (Rounds et al., 2013), hawthorn (Tengilimoglu-Metin et al., 2017), artichoke (Tengilimoglu-Metin & Kizil, 2017), berry fruits (Khan et al., 2021), and various fruits (Li et al., 2022). More recently, we demonstrated that freeze-dried Taiwanese fruit powders (guava, lemon, papaya, and mango) inhibited HCA formation by up to 99.8% in charcoal-grilled pork, with spraying and mixing methods significantly outperforming marination (Li et al., 2026); the same fruit powders also suppressed polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon formation in charcoal-grilled pork (Huang et al., 2024). However, the majority of these investigations focused on individual plant materials tested under a single cooking condition, making it difficult to compare the relative efficacy of different botanical sources or assess their performance across varying culinary applications.

Vegetables represent an abundant and economically accessible source of dietary polyphenols, yet systematic comparisons of multiple vegetable species for HCA inhibition under identical experimental conditions remain scarce. Furthermore, most published studies evaluated extract efficacy only through direct marination of raw meat, without considering the more realistic scenario of incorporating extracts into commercially relevant barbecue sauce formulations. The gap between laboratory marination protocols and actual consumer practices limits the translational value of existing research.

Beyond their well-established antioxidant functions, vegetable polyphenols are increasingly recognized as multifunctional bioactive compounds capable of modulating food quality through non-covalent interactions with proteins. In meat systems, polyphenols may bind to myofibrillar proteins, particularly myosin, through hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic contacts, and van der Waals forces, potentially altering protein conformation, thermal stability, and the accessibility of amino acid residues that serve as HCA precursors (Nawaz et al., 2024). These protein–polyphenol interactions are especially relevant during thermal processing, where protein denaturation exposes previously buried residues, creating new binding opportunities and simultaneously releasing free amino acids, creatine, and creatinine into the reaction pool. Molecular docking has emerged as a useful computational tool for predicting such interactions at the atomic level and generating testable hypotheses that complement empirical HCA inhibition data (Yang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). Understanding how specific polyphenol structures interact with meat proteins is therefore critical not only for explaining observed HCA reductions but also for rational selection and formulation of plant-based food ingredients targeting process-induced contaminant mitigation.

We hypothesized that vegetable extracts rich in polyphenols could inhibit HCA formation not only through radical scavenging activity but also via direct interactions with meat proteins, and that incorporation into barbecue sauce formulations could provide a practical delivery system without compromising sensory acceptability. To test this hypothesis, twelve commonly consumed vegetables were first screened under identical extraction and analytical conditions to establish an antioxidant capacity ranking. The three most promising candidates were then evaluated for HCA inhibition under three progressively more application-relevant marination systems: direct extract marination, commercial barbecue sauce supplementation, and homemade barbecue sauce formulation. Molecular docking was employed to investigate the plausible binding interactions between identified polyphenols and porcine myosin. Finally, a preliminary sensory acceptability assessment was included to determine whether extract incorporation caused obvious negative effects on basic eating quality attributes.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

HCA standards included IQ (2-amino-3-methylimidazo[4,5-f]quinoline), MeIQ (2-amino-3,4-dimethylimidazo[4,5-f]quinoline), 8-MeIQx (2-amino-3,8-dimethylimidazo[4,5-f]quinoxaline), PhIP (2-amino-1-methyl-6-phenylimidazo[4,5-b]pyridine), Trp-P-1 (3-amino-1,4-dimethyl-5H-pyrido[4,3-b]indole), Trp-P-2 (3-amino-1-methyl-5H-pyrido[4,3-b]indole), AαC (2-amino-9H-pyrido[2,3-b]indole), MeAαC (2-amino-3-methyl-9H-pyrido[2,3-b]indole), harman (1-methyl-9H-pyrido[3,4-b]indole), norharman (9H-pyrido[3,4-b]indole), and Glu-P-1 (2-amino-6-methyldipyrido[1,2-a,3′,2′-d]imidazole). The internal standard 4,7,8-TriMeIQx (2-amino-3,4,7,8-tetramethylimidazo[4,5-f]quinoxaline) was used for HCA quantification. DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl), Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, gallic acid, quercetin, potassium ferricyanide, trichloroacetic acid, ferric chloride, sodium carbonate, and aluminum chloride were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Polyphenol standards (gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, vanillic acid, syringic acid, EGCG, p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid, ellagic acid, and quercetin) and HCA standards (IQ, MeIQ, 8-MeIQx, PhIP, Harman, Norharman, Trp-P-1, Trp-P-2, AαC, MeAαC, and Glu-P-1) were obtained from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, ON, Canada). 4,7,8-TriMeIQx was used as the internal standard. HPLC-grade acetonitrile and formic acid were purchased from J.T. Baker (Phillipsburg, NJ, USA) and Sigma-Aldrich, respectively. QuEChERS extraction kits (Bond Elut) were obtained from Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Vegetable extract preparation

Twelve vegetables commonly consumed in Taiwan were selected based on their reported polyphenol content, culinary prevalence, and commercial availability. Fresh vegetables were purchased from a local market in Taichung, Taiwan, during autumn 2023: sweet potato leaves (Ipomoea batatas), water spinach (Ipomoea aquatica), coriander (Coriandrum sativum), garland chrysanthemum (Glebionis coronaria), Chinese celery (Apium graveolens var. secalinum), pak choi (Brassica rapa subsp. chinensis), bok choy (Brassica rapa var. chinensis), spinach (Spinacia oleracea), chili pepper (Capsicum annuum), green bell pepper (Capsicum annuum Grossum), sweet bell pepper (Capsicum annuum Grossum), and cabbage (Brassica oleracea var. capitata). Fresh leaf portions were freeze-dried, ground to fine powder, and extracted with 70% ethanol (1,20 w/v) at room temperature for 24 h with continuous shaking. The extracts were filtered, concentrated by rotary evaporation at 40 °C, and freeze-dried to obtain dry extract powder. The extracts were evaluated as experimental food ingredients in the present study. Residual-solvent specifications and applicable regulatory requirements should be assessed before any future commercial food application.

Antioxidant capacity determination

DPPH radical scavenging activity was measured according to the method of Brand-Williams et al. (1995) with modifications. Extract solutions at various concentrations were mixed with 0.1 mM DPPH methanolic solution, and absorbance was measured at 517 nm after 30 min incubation in the dark. The EC50 value (concentration required for 50% radical scavenging) was determined by linear interpolation from the dose-response curve. Reducing power was assessed following the method of Oyaizu (1986). Extract solutions were mixed with potassium ferricyanide and incubated at 50 °C for 20 min, followed by trichloroacetic acid precipitation and ferric chloride color development. Absorbance was measured at 700 nm, and EC50 (concentration at absorbance = 0.5) was determined by interpolation. Total phenolic content (TPC) was determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu method (Singleton & Rossi, 1965) and expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per gram dry weight (mg GAE/g DW). Total flavonoid content (TFC) was measured by the aluminum chloride colorimetric method (Chang et al., 2002) and expressed as mg quercetin equivalents per gram dry weight (mg QE/g DW).

Polyphenol profiling by UPLC-MS/MS

Ten polyphenol compounds were quantified by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) using a Thermo Accela UHPLC system coupled with an AB Sciex 6500+ QTRAP mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) operated in positive electrospray ionization mode. Chromatographic separation was performed on an Atlantis T3 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 3 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) with a gradient elution of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions were optimized for each compound (Table S6). Calibration curves, limits of detection (LOD, S/N ≥ 3), and limits of quantification (LOQ, S/N ≥ 10) were established for all analytes (Table S1). Representative UPLC-MS/MS chromatograms of polyphenol standards are provided in Fig. S1.

Meat preparation and marination

Fresh pork loin was purchased from a local market in Taichung, Taiwan, trimmed of visible fat, and cut into uniform pieces (0.5 cm thick; 10 cm × 10 cm; approximately 50 g each). Three marination systems were evaluated:

(a) Direct marination: Pork loin was marinated with 1% (w/w) vegetable extract dissolved in water for 30 min at 4 °C, a duration consistent with previous HCA inhibition studies (Quelhas et al., 2010; Tengilimoglu-Metin & Kizil, 2017). A preliminary dose-response experiment using 0.1%, 0.5%, and 1% water spinach extract confirmed that 1% concentration provided optimal inhibition (Table S3).

(b) Commercial barbecue sauce (CBS): A commercially available barbecue sauce (Wan Ja Shan BBQ Sauce, Wan Ja Shan Brewery Co., Ltd., Taoyuan, Taiwan) was used. The declared ingredients included water, invert sugar syrup, salt, brewed soy sauce (non-GMO soybeans), grain-brewed vinegar, chili, paprika oleoresin, yeast extract, and potassium sorbate (preservative); the complete ingredient declaration is provided in Table S8. The sauce was supplemented with 1% (w/w) vegetable extract, and pork loin was marinated in the fortified sauce for 30 min at 4 °C.

(c) Homemade barbecue sauce (HBS): A barbecue sauce was formulated according to a traditional Taiwanese recipe (Table S9). The major components included soy sauce (34.1%, w/w), water (36.0%), rock sugar (15.2%), rice wine (11.4%), garlic (1.5%), lemon juice (1.1%), and cornstarch (0.6%), with minor additions of chili powder, white pepper powder, and Chinese five-spice powder (<0.1% each). The sauce was heated to dissolve the sugar, thickened with cornstarch, and cooled before use. Each sauce batch was supplemented with 1% (w/w) vegetable extract (pH 5.00 after addition). Both the extract-fortified and unfortified sauces were autoclaved at 121 °C for 15 min to ensure microbiological stability, and cooled to room temperature before marination. This heat treatment was applied consistently to both sample types to ensure comparability among treatments. Pork loin was marinated in the sauce for 30 min at 4 °C.

Commercial hardwood charcoal briquettes (Natural Wind BBQ Charcoal, Taiwan), known for consistent combustion characteristics, were used as the heat source. All marinated samples were placed on a stainless-steel grill positioned 6 cm above the charcoal bed and charcoal-grilled at approximately 200–250 °C (measured at the meat surface using an infrared thermometer) for 5 min per side until fully cooked (internal temperature > 75 °C measured with a probe thermometer). For each treatment, three independent grilling batches were conducted on separate occasions under the same standardized conditions. One pork piece was prepared and analyzed per treatment in each batch, yielding three independent biological replicates (n = 3).

HCA extraction and quantification

HCAs were extracted from grilled meat using a modified QuEChERS method adapted from Jian et al. (2019). Briefly, 2 g of homogenized grilled meat was mixed with acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid, vortexed, and subjected to salting-out extraction with MgSO4 and NaCl. The supernatant was cleaned up with a Bond Elut QuEChERS dispersive SPE kit (PSA + C18 + MgSO4). The purified extract was dried under nitrogen, reconstituted in 150 μL of 50% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid and 1 ppb 4,7,8-TriMeIQx as internal standard (sample-to-solution conversion factor: 0.075 mL/g, used to convert solution-based concentrations to ng/g meat), and analyzed by UPLC-MS/MS. Eleven HCA species, including IQ, MeIQ, 8-MeIQx, PhIP, Trp-P-1, Trp-P-2, AαC, MeAαC, harman, norharman, and Glu-P-1, were monitored in MRM mode (Table S2, Table S7). Chromatographic conditions were identical to those used for polyphenol analysis. Quantification was performed using external standard calibration with solvent-based standard curves. The internal standard 4,7,8-TriMeIQx (1 ppb) was included in all samples to monitor and correct for variations in extraction efficiency and instrument response across analytical batches. Linearity (R2 > 0.99 for all analytes), LOD (S/N ≥ 3), and LOQ (S/N ≥ 10) were established from standard solutions and are summarized in Table S2. The HCA analytical method, including the QuEChERS extraction procedure, UPLC-MS/MS conditions, internal standard, and MRM transitions, was validated as part of a parallel study conducted using the same analytical platform and charcoal-grilled pork matrix. The validation results, including linearity, recovery, precision, LOD, LOQ, and matrix-effect evaluation, have been reported by Li et al. (2026) and are summarized in Table S11. In the present study, the same validated analytical workflow was applied to all samples, and HCA inhibition rates were calculated as percentage reductions relative to the corresponding within-system controls processed within the same analytical batches. Representative UPLC-MS/MS chromatograms of a HCA standard mixture, a control grilled pork sample, and an extract-treated sample are provided in Fig. S2. Total HCA content (ng/g meat) and inhibition rate (%) were calculated.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed to investigate the binding interactions between the six major polyphenols identified in the top three vegetable extracts (ellagic acid, chlorogenic acid, quercetin, gallic acid, caffeic acid, and ferulic acid) and porcine myosin heavy chain. The three-dimensional structure of porcine myosin was obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (UniProt ID: Q9TV61), and the S1 subfragment (motor domain, residues 1–843) was extracted for docking. Ligand structures were retrieved from PubChem (CIDs: 5281855, 689043, 1794427, 5280343, 370, and 445,858). The receptor was prepared by adding polar hydrogens and converting to PDBQT format using Open Babel. Ligands were energy-minimized and converted to PDBQT format. Blind docking was performed using AutoDock Vina 1.2.5 (Eberhardt et al., 2021) with exhaustiveness = 64, covering the entire S1 domain to identify preferred binding sites without bias. Binding energies, hydrogen bonds (donor-acceptor distance <3.5 Å), and hydrophobic contacts (within 4 Å) were analyzed for the best-scoring pose of each ligand.

Sensory evaluation

Preliminary sensory acceptability assessment was conducted with 32 untrained panelists (aged 20–30 years) recruited from the university campus. Four samples were evaluated: CBS alone, CBS with 1% sweet potato leaves extract, HBS alone, and HBS with 1% sweet potato leaves extract. Sweet potato leaves were selected for sensory testing because they showed strong and consistent HCA inhibition across the three marination systems and exhibited the highest inhibition in the HBS system, which was the primary formulation used for practical sauce evaluation. Panelists evaluated each sample on a 9-point hedonic scale (1 = dislike extremely, 9 = like extremely) for five attributes: overall acceptability, appearance, flavor, texture, and aftertaste. Samples were presented in random order under white lighting at room temperature. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the sensory evaluation. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of China Medical University and Hospital (Approval No. CMUH115-REC3–079) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. No personally identifiable information was collected or disclosed.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the standardized data matrix (12 vegetables × 13 variables: DPPH EC50−1, RP EC50−1, TPC, TFC, and 9 individual polyphenols; EGCG was excluded as it was not detected in any sample) using a correlation-based approach. EC50−1 was used so that higher values indicate stronger activity. The first two principal components (F1 and F2) were used to construct a biplot for visualization of vegetable clustering and variable associations.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise stated. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test was used to determine significant differences among treatments (p < 0.05). The principal significance groupings were additionally verified using Tukey's HSD test and remained unchanged. For individual HCA measurements, data points were excluded only when a documented analytical failure occurred, such as extraction loss or instrument malfunction. All available replicate measurements were retained, except in cases of documented analytical failure; no statistical outlier removal was applied. Total HCA content was calculated as the sum of individual HCA mean concentrations to ensure consistency between individual and total values. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). PCA was performed using Python 3.11 with scikit-learn.

Results and discussion

Antioxidant capacity screening of twelve vegetable extracts

All four antioxidant indicators were strongly intercorrelated (Pearson r = 0.87–0.96, p < 0.01; Table S5), indicating that vegetable extracts with high polyphenol content consistently exhibited strong radical scavenging and reducing capacity. This observation is consistent with Velioglu et al. (1998), who reported high correlations between TPC and DPPH scavenging activity across 28 plant materials.

Polyphenol profiling and principal component analysis

UPLC-MS/MS analysis identified and quantified up to ten polyphenol compounds in the twelve vegetable extracts (Table S4). The top three vegetables exhibited distinct polyphenol profiles (Table 2). Sweet potato leaves were characterized by exceptionally high chlorogenic acid (2320 ± 311 ng/mg) and caffeic acid (191 ± 25 ng/mg), consistent with previous reports that Ipomoea batatas leaves are a rich source of caffeoylquinic acid derivatives (Islam et al., 2002). Water spinach contained the highest chlorogenic acid among the top three (1083 ± 196 ng/mg) and moderate ellagic acid (1126 ± 208 ng/mg). Coriander was distinguished by the highest ellagic acid content (1863 ± 140 ng/mg) and was the only vegetable among the top three containing detectable quercetin (3.29 ± 0.05 ng/mg). The distinct polyphenol profiles of these three vegetables suggest complementary pathways that may contribute to HCA inhibition. Sweet potato leaves, dominated by chlorogenic acid and caffeic acid (both hydroxycinnamic acids), are expected to contribute primarily through carbonyl trapping and radical scavenging in the aqueous phase of the meat matrix (Yang et al., 2023). Coriander, with its exceptionally high ellagic acid content, may provide additional inhibition through direct protein binding, given that ellagic acid exhibited the strongest predicted affinity for myosin in our docking analysis (Section 3.4). Water spinach presents an intermediate profile combining both hydroxycinnamic acids and ellagic acid, which may explain its consistent but not outstanding inhibition across all three systems. These observations underscore the importance of polyphenol composition, rather than total phenolic content alone, in predicting HCA-inhibitory efficacy (Ye et al., 2024). PCA of the combined antioxidant and polyphenol dataset (13 variables) revealed that the first two principal components (eigenvalues: F1 = 5.28, F2 = 2.02) explained 56.17% of the total variance (F1 = 40.64%, F2 = 15.53%; Fig. 1). The score plot (Fig. 1A) clearly separated the three high-antioxidant vegetables (sweet potato leaves, water spinach, and coriander) on the positive F1 axis from the remaining nine vegetables. The loading plot (Fig. 1B) showed that DPPH, RP, TPC, TFC, caffeic acid, ellagic acid, and chlorogenic acid all contributed strongly to F1, confirming that these variables collectively drive the differentiation between high- and low-antioxidant vegetables. These three vegetables were therefore selected for subsequent HCA inhibition experiments. The convergence of results across four complementary assays (DPPH, reducing power, TPC, and TFC) strengthens this selection, as it indicates that the chosen vegetables are not merely rich in total phenolics but contain redox-active compounds capable of both electron donation and radical quenching. This multi-assay approach is particularly relevant for HCA mitigation, because HCA formation during grilling involves multiple redox pathways and Maillard-derived reactive intermediates rather than a single radical species (Dong et al., 2020; Gibis, 2016). When incorporated into commercial barbecue sauce (CBS), the inhibition pattern shifted: water spinach achieved the highest inhibition (60.29%), followed by sweet potato leaves (50.25%) and coriander (42.85%). Notably, the HCA profile in CBS-marinated meat differed from direct marination; only pyrolytic HCAs (Harman, Norharman, Trp-P-1, Trp-P-2) were detected, while thermic HCAs (IQ, MeIQ, 8-MeIQx, PhIP) were absent (Table 5B). This shift from thermic to pyrolytic HCA dominance may be attributed to several factors related to the sauce matrix: (i) the reducing sugars present in the commercial sauce could competitively consume creatinine through alternative Maillard pathways, diverting it from thermic HCA formation (Skog et al., 1998); (ii) the soy sauce and spice components in CBS may have contributed antioxidant activity and altered the relative formation of thermic and pyrolytic HCAs; and (iii) the sauce matrix may have altered heat transfer, surface dehydration, and precursor distribution during charcoal grilling, thereby changing the relative formation of individual HCA species. These observations are consistent with Meurillon and Engel (2016), who noted that formulation ingredients can simultaneously promote and inhibit different HCA species depending on the precursor balance. However, these explanations remain hypothetical because sauce composition, surface temperature, water activity, and precursor concentrations were not independently measured in the present study.

In the homemade barbecue sauce (HBS) system, sweet potato leaves exhibited the strongest inhibition (76.78%), followed by coriander (67.11%) and water spinach (65.60%). The HBS control produced substantially higher total HCA (184.61 ng/g) compared to direct marination (15.43 ng/g) and CBS (94.47 ng/g), likely due to the higher sugar and soy sauce content in the homemade formulation providing abundant Maillard reaction substrates. Despite this elevated baseline, all three vegetable extracts achieved inhibition rates exceeding 65%, demonstrating robust efficacy even under challenging cooking conditions.

The marked differences in total HCA baseline concentrations across the three systems (direct: 15.43, CBS: 94.47, HBS: 184.61 ng/g) merit further discussion, as they reflect the critical influence of sauce matrix composition on HCA formation pathways. The substantially higher HCA formation in HBS can be attributed to the abundance of soy sauce (34.1%, w/w) and rock sugar (15.2%), which together provide elevated concentrations of free amino acids, reducing sugars, and creatinine—the three essential precursor classes for thermic and pyrolytic HCA formation (Skog et al., 1998). In contrast, CBS, despite containing sugars, also included grain-brewed vinegar and potassium sorbate that may have modulated pH and microbial-derived precursor accumulation. The shift from mixed thermic/pyrolytic HCA profiles in direct marination to predominantly pyrolytic HCAs in sauce systems is particularly noteworthy. In CBS, the complete absence of thermic HCAs (IQ, MeIQ, 8-MeIQx, PhIP) suggests that the sauce matrix either diverted creatinine into alternative reaction pathways or altered the surface temperature kinetics to favor β-carboline formation over aminoimidazoarene routes (Gibis, 2016; Jägerstad et al., 1991). From a food safety perspective, this profile shift has practical significance: thermic HCAs such as PhIP and MeIQx are classified as Group 2B carcinogens by IARC (1993), whereas the toxicological profiles of β-carbolines (harman, norharman) are less well established (Dong et al., 2020). Therefore, the preferential suppression of thermic HCAs observed in sauce-marinated samples may represent a disproportionately greater reduction in genotoxic risk than the total HCA inhibition percentages alone would suggest.

Across all three systems, the inhibition efficacy was not strictly proportional to the antioxidant capacity ranking of the extracts. Coriander, which ranked third in DPPH and RP, outperformed the other two vegetables under direct marination (92.74%), whereas sweet potato leaves, which ranked first in DPPH, showed the best performance in the HBS system (76.78%). This observation suggests that HCA inhibition may involve factors beyond simple radical scavenging. The molecular docking analysis below was therefore used to explore a testable hypothesis involving possible protein-polyphenol interactions and precursor availability. This finding is consistent with Kang et al. (2022), who reported that natural ingredient marinades significantly reduced HCA formation in grilled pork belly through mechanisms that extended beyond antioxidant activity alone. Importantly, the reversal of ranking across systems—coriander being the most effective under direct marination but the least effective under CBS—indicates that HCA inhibition is not an intrinsic property of the extract alone but arises from a three-way interaction among polyphenol composition, sauce matrix chemistry, and heating conditions. This matrix-dependent behavior has practical implications: a single laboratory marination test cannot reliably predict real-world efficacy, and future studies should evaluate candidate extracts under the specific formulation conditions intended for application. The finding also suggests that optimizing extract–matrix compatibility (e.g., matching polyphenol solubility and stability to sauce pH, sugar content, and water activity) may be as important as maximizing polyphenol concentration. It should also be noted that HCA quantification in the present study used solvent-based external calibration; however, the QuEChERS-UPLC-MS/MS workflow had been validated previously in a comparable charcoal-grilled pork matrix using the same analytical platform and sample preparation protocol (Li et al., 2026). Therefore, absolute HCA concentrations should be interpreted in the context of the reported method performance, whereas the within-system percentage comparisons provide a robust basis for evaluating relative inhibition efficacy.

The rationale for this target is based on the hypothesis that polyphenols may interact with myosin and reduce the thermal release or accessibility of HCA precursors (amino acids, creatine, creatinine) during cooking (Gibis & Weiss, 2012; Nawaz et al., 2024). Recent studies have demonstrated that polyphenols such as resveratrol can form stable non-covalent complexes with myofibrillar proteins, reducing the availability of bound HCA precursors (Yang et al., 2024). Blind docking identified two distinct binding pockets on the myosin S1 domain. Pocket A, located in the upper 50 kDa domain near the actin-binding cleft (residues 115–135, 182–188, and 241–245), accommodated four of the six polyphenols: ellagic acid (EA, −8.6 kcal/mol), chlorogenic acid (CGA, −8.5 kcal/mol), gallic acid (GA, −7.4 kcal/mol), and ferulic acid (FA, −6.5 kcal/mol). EA and CGA exhibited the strongest binding affinities and were highly convergent in their binding poses (EA mode 1–2 RMSD = 0.1 Å), indicating a well-defined binding site. Pocket B, located in the N-terminal SH3-like domain (residues 18–28, 83–89, and 149), was occupied by quercetin (QUR, −8.2 kcal/mol) and caffeic acid (CA, −6.5 kcal/mol). The spatial separation between the two pockets (~24 Å) suggests that polyphenols binding at different sites would not compete with each other, potentially enabling additive or synergistic inhibition when multiple polyphenol species are present simultaneously in a vegetable extract.

The predicted localization of Pocket A near the actin-binding cleft is potentially relevant to the interaction hypothesis. This region is involved in the conformational changes of myosin during muscle contraction and is expected to undergo structural perturbation during thermal denaturation. Polyphenol binding at this site could potentially contribute to reduced precursor accessibility during thermal denaturation, although this hypothesis requires experimental validation through protein-polyphenol binding assays and precursor release measurements. GA formed the most hydrogen bonds (17) despite its relatively modest binding energy (−7.4 kcal/mol), attributable to its small molecular size and high hydroxyl group density, which enables extensive hydrogen bonding with multiple residues across a broader interaction surface. These computational findings are consistent with recent density functional theory analyses demonstrating that phenolic compounds can interact with HCA-associated intermediates to suppress heterocyclic ring formation (Zhou et al., 2024), and with molecular dynamics studies showing that spice-derived polyphenols form stable complexes with meat proteins under thermal processing conditions (Zheng et al., 2025).

The localization of four out of six polyphenols in Pocket A, situated near the actin-binding cleft in the upper 50 kDa domain, has potential implications for HCA precursor accessibility during cooking. The actin-binding region undergoes significant conformational changes during thermal denaturation of myosin (Rayment et al., 1993), and polyphenol binding at this site could plausibly modulate the rate at which amino acid residues are released into the surrounding medium. If confirmed experimentally, this interaction pathway could complement the experimentally supported radical scavenging pathway and the literature-proposed carbonyl trapping pathway by adding a possible physical shielding component, as proposed by Nawaz et al. (2024). The observation that EA and CGA, which dominated the polyphenol profiles of coriander and sweet potato leaves respectively, also exhibited the strongest docking affinities provides a structure–activity link between the compositional data (Section 3.2) and the observed HCA inhibition patterns (Section 3.3). However, it should be emphasized that in real food systems, multiple polyphenols coexist and may compete for the same binding sites, and the effective concentration reaching the protein surface will be modulated by solubility, matrix binding, pH, and thermal degradation during cooking. These factors cannot be captured by single-ligand docking and represent important directions for future molecular dynamics simulations using multi-component polyphenol systems.

It should be noted that molecular docking provides a static approximation of protein–ligand interactions and does not fully account for protein flexibility, solvent effects, thermal denaturation, or the complex food matrix. Because docking was performed using individual compounds and a single protein conformation, the results should be interpreted as computational support for a plausible polyphenol–myosin interaction hypothesis rather than direct evidence of inhibition during grilling. Experimental approaches such as fluorescence quenching, isothermal titration calorimetry, or measurement of free amino acid and creatine release during cooking would be needed to confirm these computational predictions. Sensory evaluation

Preliminary sensory acceptability assessment (Fig. S3; Table S10) revealed that HBS without extract addition received the highest numerical scores across all five sensory attributes (overall acceptability 6.91, appearance 6.81, flavor 6.91, texture 6.81, aftertaste 6.78). One-way ANOVA indicated significant differences among the four treatments for overall acceptability (p < 0.05) and appearance (p < 0.001), whereas flavor (p = 0.109), texture (p = 0.131), and aftertaste (p = 0.094) did not differ significantly. For overall acceptability, HBS alone scored significantly higher than the other three treatments (Duncan's test, p < 0.05). For appearance, three distinct groups were identified: CBS and HBS shared the highest scores (6.81), CBS + SP was intermediate (5.84), and HBS + SP received the lowest score (4.78), attributable to the dark green coloration imparted by the extract. Importantly, despite the reduced appearance scores, flavor (6.06), texture (6.09), and aftertaste (6.00) of HBS + SP all remained above 6.0 on the 9-point hedonic scale, indicating that the extract did not negatively affect taste perception.

For the CBS system, extract addition had minimal impact on sensory scores; overall acceptability was identical between CBS alone and CBS with extract (both 6.19), suggesting that the dark color and strong flavor of commercial barbecue sauce effectively masked the vegetable extract. This observation parallels the findings of Gibis and Weiss (2010), who demonstrated that marinades containing hibiscus extract could reduce HCA formation in fried beef patties by up to 50% without negatively affecting sensory quality. Similarly, our recent study on fruit powder barbecue sauce sprays showed that guava and papaya formulations maintained overall liking scores above 6.0 on a 9-point hedonic scale while achieving 63.5–63.8% HCA inhibition (Li et al., 2026). This finding has practical implications: commercial sauce formulations may be better vehicles for vegetable extract incorporation because they minimize the visual impact that reduces consumer acceptance in lighter-colored homemade sauces.

It should be noted that the sensory panel was limited to 32 panelists aged 20–30 years, and results may not be generalizable to broader consumer demographics. Future studies with larger and more diverse panels would strengthen these findings.

Conclusions

This study established a systematic framework for identifying vegetable-derived HCA inhibitors and evaluating their potential application in barbecue sauce formulations. Among twelve vegetables screened under identical conditions, sweet potato leaves, water spinach, and coriander were identified as the most potent sources of antioxidant polyphenols. These three extracts effectively inhibited HCA formation in a matrix-dependent manner, with reductions ranging from 42.85% to 92.74% across direct marination, commercial barbecue sauce, and homemade barbecue sauce systems. Molecular docking predicted that major polyphenols, particularly ellagic acid and chlorogenic acid, may preferentially interact with the upper 50 kDa domain of porcine myosin S1 near the actin-binding cleft; this provides a testable hypothesis that requires experimental validation. Preliminary sensory assessment suggested that 1% sweet potato leaves extract could be incorporated into barbecue sauce without markedly compromising flavor, texture, or aftertaste; however, larger and more diverse consumer panels are required before market-oriented conclusions can be drawn. Additionally, sensory evaluation of coriander and water spinach extracts in barbecue sauce was not conducted; given the distinctive aroma profile of coriander, its sensory impact on barbecue sauce formulations warrants future investigation. Among the tested systems, sweet potato leaf extract in homemade barbecue sauce showed a favorable combination of HCA reduction (76.78%) and preliminary sensory performance, although color modification remains a formulation issue for future application-oriented studies. Overall, the results indicate that HCA mitigation by vegetable extracts is associated with polyphenol composition and sauce matrix chemistry rather than antioxidant capacity alone, while possible protein-binding interactions remain a computationally derived hypothesis (Fig. 4). This study provides a bioactive-ingredient framework for selecting and applying vegetable polyphenols to reduce process-induced toxicants in grilled meat systems.
