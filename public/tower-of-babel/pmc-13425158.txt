Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) collaborative care involving acupuncture for axial spondyloarthritis: 6-week exploratory results of a pragmatic randomised controlled trial
Authors: Yu Heng Kwan, Ting Hui Woon, Charmaine Wai Yan Sum, Xiang Ling Ang, Bee Choo Tai, Youyi Huang, Marcel Bilger, Jie Kie Phang, Hui Chin Tan, Su-An Quek, Ader Lim, Chiah Yuen Wong, Shin Yoong Chua, Clara Eng, Jia Ven Lee, Choy Tip Tan, Bao Qiang Dong, Hwee Ling Koh, Ying Ying Leung, Siaw Ing Yeo, Swee Cheng Ng, Charmaine Tze May Wang, Kok Yong Fong, Julian Thumboo, Truls Østbye, Warren Fong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis pragmatic randomised controlled trial aimed to assess the clinical effectiveness and safety of a Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) collaborative model of care (TCMCMC) in patients with active axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA). Here, we present the week 6 analysis.MethodsPatients with active axSpA (Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index [BASDAI] ≥4), spinal pain ≥4 and inadequate response to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs were randomised to receive either usual rheumatologic care (control) or TCMCMC (intervention, ie, usual rheumatologic care with TCM consultations and 10 acupuncture sessions). Spinal pain at week 6 was the primary outcome. Evaluation of other clinical and quality of life outcomes at week 6 was exploratory.ResultsWe recruited 139 participants, with 69 and 70 in the control and intervention groups, respectively. Median (IQR) age was 42.0 (33.0-56.0) years and 98 (70.5%) participants were male. Spinal pain and BASDAI scores were 0.75 (95% CI: −1.46, −0.03) and 0.71 (95% CI: −1.30, −0.13) points lower in the intervention than the control group (below the minimal clinically important differences of 1.5–1.6 and 1.3–1.4, respectively). Adjustments for baseline scores did not significantly alter the results. Ankylosing Spondylitis Quality of Life scores were 1.21 (95% CI: −2.30, −0.11) points lower in the intervention after adjusting for baseline scores. 36-item Short Form Health Survey Mental Component Summary was 4.30 (95% CI: −8.03, −0.56) lower in the intervention when unadjusted, but insignificant after adjusting for baseline. Other outcomes were not statistically significant. One adverse event was observed in each group.ConclusionsThe intervention group had improved spinal pain, disease activity, and disease-specific quality of life, without significant safety risks.

INTRODUCTION

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic inflammatory disease characterised by low back pain, stiffness, and fatigue, which greatly affect patients’ physical function and overall health. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are the first line of treatment, but response rates based on the Assessment of Spondyloarthritis International Society (ASAS) 40% (ASAS40) criteria average only 35% [1]. Use of NSAIDs is also limited by adverse effects such as gastrointestinal ulcers, renal impairment, hypertension, and other severe cardiovascular events [2,3]. Although biological disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) such as tumour necrosis factor inhibitors and IL-17 inhibitors are effective alternatives, response rates based on the 50% improvement of the initial Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) (BASDAI50) and ASAS40 criteria ranged between 50 and 72% [4]. Furthermore, these drugs are costly [5]. JAK inhibitors have recently been shown to be effective for radiographic axSpA, but their use is restricted by unclear long-term safety data in axSpA, especially regarding risks of cardiovascular, thromboembolic events and malignancies [[6], [7], [8]]. Hence, as many patients either still do not achieve desired treatment outcomes or experience intolerable side effects of current conventional treatment, patients with axSpA often seek complementary and alternative treatment [9].

Acupuncture is one of the treatment methods in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and involves stimulating specific acupoints by inserting and manipulating acupuncture needles, with the aim of regulating vital energy (‘qi’) in the body, as an imbalance of ‘qi’ is associated with susceptibility to disease [10]. Acupuncture is effective in relieving pain for conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, chronic low back pain and cancer pain [[11], [12], [13]], as well as possibly reducing inflammation [14]. A meta-analysis on the effect of acupuncture in ankylosing spondylitis showed that the supplementary use of acupuncture helped reduce pain, stiffness, and disease activity. However, as pointed out as well, these results obtained might have limited generalisability to the broader axSpA population owing to regional differences in practices in China [15], which might differ from international guidelines for the management of axSpA. Hence, this pragmatic randomised controlled trial (RCT) was designed to assess the clinical effectiveness and safety of a TCM collaborative model of care (TCMCMC) in axSpA, to add to the evidence available. TCMCMC involved both TCM physicians and rheumatologists, who participated in the care of patients, with patients receiving consultations, acupuncture, and nonpharmacological advice from TCM physicians, combined with usual rheumatologic care.

METHODS

Patients and study design

This pragmatic RCT was conducted in accordance with the Pragmatic Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary Framework-2 (PRECIS-2), extended Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines for pragmatic trials and Standards for Reporting Interventions in Clinical Trials of Acupuncture statement for acupuncture [[16], [17], [18]]. The full protocol containing the study design, intervention, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and variables collected has been reported previously [19]. Pragmatic elements of the trial are shown in Figure 1.

PRECIS-2 design elements. PRECIS-2, Pragmatic Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary Framework-2.

Briefly, patients were eligible if they were aged ≥21 years, diagnosed with axSpA by a rheumatologist, fulfilled the ASAS 2009 classification for axSpA [20,21], had active disease, and spinal pain ≥4 (out of 10) in BASDAI item 2, with inadequate response to 2 sequential NSAIDs (including cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitor) at maximal tolerated doses for ≥4 weeks. Active disease was defined as having a BASDAI ≥4. Patients who were on treatment with concomitant methotrexate or sulfasalazine had to be on the drug for ≥12 weeks and at a stable dose for ≥4 weeks before randomisation. Patients on systemic corticosteroids had to be on a stable dose of ≤10 mg/d of prednisolone or its equivalent for at least 2 weeks before randomisation. Patients were ineligible if they received bDMARDs within the past 3 months, were pregnant or breastfeeding, on antiplatelets or anticoagulants, or had bleeding disorders or blood-borne communicable diseases. The attending rheumatologists assessed the patients for eligibility. Participants were recruited from dedicated speciality clinics in Singapore General Hospital, a tertiary hospital in Singapore. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before study recruitment. The study was approved by the SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (CIRB) (Ref: 2017/2088) and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03420404).

Study procedures

Participants were assigned to the control or intervention group in a 1:1 ratio using permuted block randomisation, of randomly varying block sizes of 4 and 6, stratified by good (BASDAI 50% response) vs poor response to NSAIDs, using a list generated using the ralloc command of STATA version 14 (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA). The list was provided by the study biostatistician and kept by the research coordinator to ensure allocation concealment. Random assignment was done by the research coordinator, and the attending rheumatologists and statistician were blinded to the group assignment. Participants were not blinded to the group allocation and were requested not to disclose the allocation to their rheumatologists.

The control group received usual rheumatologic care for axSpA, consisting of physiotherapy, medication, monitoring of disease activity, and monitoring for complications of axSpA [7]. Participants were followed up at prespecified intervals (ranging from 6 weeks to 6 months) after the baseline visit, and the attending rheumatologist was allowed to prescribe the full range of medications as per routine care and according to local treatment guidelines after the primary outcome at week 6. No changes to the medication were allowed in the 6-week period. However, we did not collect data on the use of NSAIDs or analgesic consumption. The intervention group received TCMCMC (ie, usual rheumatologic care with TCM consultations and acupuncture). Participants received 10 acupuncture sessions (ie, 2 courses of treatment) over 5 weeks. Each course of acupuncture treatment consisted of 5 sessions held over 2 weeks, with each acupuncture session lasting 30 minutes, and with a break of 3 days to a week between each course. Acupoints were identified a priori. Main acupoints were used in all participants. Secondary acupoints varied across participants and were selected based on the TCM syndrome (as assessed and determined by TCM physicians) and pain location (Fig 2). All TCM physicians with at least 3 years of experience underwent training for the acupuncture techniques and protocol prior to the study to ensure treatment standardisation, and documented components of the treatment in standardised logbooks. Both the control and intervention groups were followed up at regular intervals (week 6, week 12, week 24, and week 52). Here, we present the primary 6-week results of the Acupuncture in Spondyloarthritis (AcuSpA) study (NCT03420404).

Acupoints used. Main acupoints were used in all patients randomised to the intervention group. Patients were then classified into 1 of the 5 TCM syndromes based on their clinical presentation, and the respective secondary acupoints were used based on the individual’s syndrome. Additional secondary acupoints were selected depending on the location of pain that the patient experienced. TCM, Traditional Chinese Medicine.

Outcomes

Outcome measures collected were selected based on the ASAS-Outcomes Measures in Rheumatology core domain set for axSpA [22] (Table 1). BASDAI was collected for disease activity, Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI) for functional impairment, Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ) for disability, Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Patient Global Score (BAS-G) for global well-being, 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) for generic quality of life, and Ankylosing Spondylitis Quality of Life (ASQoL) for disease-specific quality of life [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27]].

Primary outcome was the spinal pain (measured on an 11-point numerical rating scale) at week 6 in the intervention as compared with the control group, as pain was regarded as one of the most important quality of life domains affecting patients with axSpA [28], and this was the time point immediately after 10 sessions of acupuncture. Evaluation of the other clinical and quality of life outcomes (BASDAI, BASFI, BAS-G, HAQ, SF-36, and ASQoL) at week 6 were not prespecified in the original protocol and has been done as exploratory analyses.

For safety, adverse events were recorded throughout the study. The study would be stopped if a serious adverse event deemed to be related to the intervention occurred.

Statistical analysis

As this was the first study to explore the use of a collaborative model of care in axSpA, the sample size calculation was based on a previous study on acupuncture for chronic low back pain in older adults [29]. With an estimate of a 0.6-point difference on an 11-point scale in pain score between both the intervention and control groups, and assuming a standard deviation of 1.2 for both groups, approximately 64 participants were needed in each group (total of 128 participants) to obtain a statistical power of 80% (2-sided Type I error rate of 0.05) based on a 1:1 treatment allocation. To account for a dropout rate of approximately 20%, 160 participants, with 80 participants per group, were needed.

Analysis was done based on modified intention to treat. Outcomes were compared using Student’s t-test, and further adjustments were made with baseline scores using analysis of covariance. Safety was evaluated for all participants using a preidentified checklist of adverse events. All tests were performed assuming a 2-sided type I error rate set at 0.05. Data analyses were done using STATA version 15.

RESULTS

Baseline demographics, clinical characteristics, and patient-reported outcome measures of study participants

Participant recruitment was conducted from 5 March 2018 to 11 November 2024. Of the 529 patients assessed for eligibility, 385 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 5 declined participation. We recruited 139 participants, with 70 randomised to the intervention and 69 to the control group (Fig 3). Of those in the intervention group, 3 did not complete the stipulated 10 sessions of acupuncture. The median (IQR) age was 42.0 (33.0-56.0) years and 70.5% were male. As for the subtype of axSpA, 78.4% had radiographic axSpA. The median (IQR) disease duration was 5.0 (1.0-12.2) years and 79.3% were positive for human leukocyte antigen B27.

Trial profile.

The mean (SD) spinal pain at baseline was 6.3 (1.2). For other clinical outcome measures, mean (SD) BASDAI, BAS-G, BASFI, and HAQ were 5.3 (1.0), 52.2 (15.7), 2.8 (2.3), and 0.4 (0.5), respectively. For the quality of life measures, mean (SD) SF-36 Physical Component Summary (PCS), SF-36 Mental Component Summary (MCS), and ASQoL were 39.5 (11.5), 41.2 (12.2), and 6.5 (4.4), respectively (Table 2).

Outcomes at week 6

The outcomes at week 6 are presented in Table 3. For the primary outcome of spinal pain at week 6, the mean score in the intervention was 0.75 (95% CI: −1.46, −0.03) points lower than the control, where mean (SD) scores were 4.03 (2.04) and 4.78 (2.18), respectively. Adjustments for baseline scores did not alter the results of the primary outcome, where the mean score in the intervention was 0.76 (95% CI: −1.46, −0.06) points lower than the control.

For exploratory outcomes, mean BASDAI was 0.71 (95% CI: −1.30, −0.13) points lower in the intervention than the control and similarly this outcome was not affected after adjusting for baseline scores, where the mean score in the intervention was 0.68 (95% CI: −1.22, −0.13) points lower than the control. No statistical significance was observed for other clinical outcomes BASFI, BAS-G, and HAQ. For ASQoL, the unadjusted score in the intervention was 0.03 (95% CI: −1.57, 1.51) lower than the control, though no statistically significant difference was observed, but the adjusted mean ASQoL score was 1.21 (95% CI: −2.30, −0.11) lower in the intervention than the control. The mean SF-36 MCS in the intervention was 4.30 (95% CI: −8.03, −0.56) points lower than the control when unadjusted, but differences were not statistically significant after adjusting for baseline scores, with the intervention being 1.50 (95% CI: −4.12, 1.12) points lower than the control. No statistical significance was observed for SF-36 PCS when adjusted and unadjusted.

Safety

Adverse events were reported in 2 participants. One participant in the intervention group had bruising, which was deemed to be related to the intervention. One participant in the control group had giddiness, which was not related to the treatment of axSpA. Both events were resolved within 1 week. No serious adverse events were observed.

DISCUSSION

This is the first pragmatic RCT to assess the clinical effectiveness and safety of a TCMCMC in the treatment of patients with axSpA. At week 6, we found that the intervention group receiving TCMCMC had lower spinal pain and BASDAI scores than controls, and also lower ASQoL scores, with no significant adverse events.

The lower spinal pain and BASDAI scores in the intervention group are consistent with prior studies which showed that acupuncture was effective in reducing pain and possibly inflammation, reflected as improvements in disease activity [11,30]. However, the effect size for spinal pain at week 6 observed in this study was 0.75 (95% CI: 0.03, 1.46), which was lower than that for celecoxib, where the effect was 1.64 [31]. This was also lower than the minimal clinically important difference for spinal pain of 1.5-1.6 for axSpA [32], though it should also be noted that this study was powered to detect a 0.6-point difference. Effect size for BASDAI was also lower than the minimal clinically important difference of 1.3-1.4 [32]. A longer duration of acupuncture treatment might be beneficial, as a higher effect size of 0.9 was observed in patients who received 3 months of acupuncture for chronic low back pain [33].

At week 6, unadjusted ASQoL scores were not statistically different between the intervention and control groups, though the intervention group had lower ASQoL scores after adjusting for baseline ASQoL scores. This could possibly be because ASQoL is a disease-specific quality of life measure and has been shown to be correlated with disease activity [34]. Unadjusted SF-36 MCS was lower in the intervention group, possibly owing to baseline differences (43.3 in the control and 39.1 in the intervention). Adjusted SF-36 MCS and SF-36 PCS scores were not statistically different in both intervention and control groups, signifying that the intervention did not exert much change when a generic quality of life measure was used. Factors such as sleep quality, perception of illness, social factors, and psychological stressors are independently associated with quality of life [[35], [36], [37]], and might persist even with improvements in inflammation and pain if not managed specifically, hence possibly causing the lack of improvement in the generic quality of life measures. This might contribute to the responsiveness of ASQoL in measuring the effect of clinical interventions [38], as compared with SF-36.

Although the intervention group had lower spinal pain and disease activity than the control group, TCMCMC had a limited impact on functional impairment and disability in the short term. The levels of functional impairment and disability are also influenced by structural damage [39], which might be irreversible, unlike inflammation, and hence limit the extent of improvement, since 78.4% of the participants in our study had radiographic axSpA.

Strengths of this study include the use of a pragmatic trial design to reflect real-world practice, which increases the external validity of the trial results and facilitates policy decision-making and implementation of the intervention. This study enrolled patients with active disease despite NSAIDs use, which would be the population likely to seek complementary and alternative treatment in clinical practice. The method of participant recruitment, setting of the trial, and the expertise and resources required in this trial were also similar to and feasible in routine care settings. Adjustments to acupoints based on the participants’ TCM syndromes and pain location were allowed in this trial to reflect the nature of TCM treatment, where treatment plans are tailored to the patient (eg, based on TCM syndromes).

Limitations of this study include the recruitment of participants from a tertiary hospital, where patients might have more severe disease, which may limit the generalisability of these results to all patients. However, in the local context, patients with axSpA are seldom managed in primary care. Only 139 of the estimated 160 participants were recruited. However, the calculated number of 160 accounted for a 20% withdrawal rate, which was greater than that observed in this study, which had 3 participants withdrawing, and 98% of the participants completing the primary outcome at week 6. Any increase in the use of analgesics between baseline and the follow-up at week 6 was not allowed, but we did not collect data on the use of analgesics to further ascertain any possible dose changes. Comparator wise, the lack of blinding of study participants may introduce a placebo effect and confound the interpretation of results, especially since outcomes were patient reported [40]. Sham acupuncture was not done in this pragmatic trial to simulate real-world clinical practice, but may be relevant in future studies involving acupuncture for blinding effects. Although processes were in place to ensure allocation concealment, there were no specific validation procedures thereafter to confirm this. Outcomes assessed did not include established outcomes such as the ASAS response criteria, which might be a more robust indicator of clinical improvement. However, patient-reported outcome measures used in this study capture results such as pain, functional impairment, and quality of life, which might be of greater relevance to patients, in line with the design elements of PRECIS-2. Although the results show better spinal pain, BASDAI, and ASQoL in the intervention group, this study was only powered to detect changes in spinal pain. Analyses of BASDAI and ASQoL were exploratory in nature and might increase the risk of type I error.

CONCLUSION

In summary, we found that participants who received TCMCMC had lower spinal pain, disease activity (based on BASDAI scores), and better disease-specific quality of life (based on ASQoL scores) at week 6, with modest effects observed, compared with those who only received usual rheumatologic care. However, further follow-up would be needed to determine the long-term trajectory of the effectiveness of TCMCMC on spinal pain, disease activity, and disease-specific quality of life, given that axSpA is a chronic inflammatory condition with fluctuations in disease activity throughout the disease course.
