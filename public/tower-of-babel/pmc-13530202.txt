Alternating-chiral charge density waves and associated spin polarization in monolayered NbTe2
Authors: Yusong Bai, Guohua Cao, Hui Zhang, Jinghao Deng, Chuqi Zhang, Haomin Fei, Dongyu Li, Xiaoyu Lin, Leiqiang Li, Chao Zhu, Zemin Pan, Tao Jian, Da Huo, Zhengbo Cheng, Li Huang, Hong-Jun Gao, Ping Cui, Chendong Zhang, Zhenyu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntertwining of different quantum degrees of freedom manifests exotic quantum phenomena in many-body systems, especially in reduced dimensionality. Here we show that monolayered NbTe2 serves as a promising platform where lattice, charge, and spin degrees of freedom manifest cooperatively, leading to a threading order of chirality. Through meticulous real-space atomic structure analysis, we reveal that the √19 × √19 phase of NbTe2 encodes alternating-chiral atomic displacements alongside charge density order, characterized by two chiral units of opposite handedness within the reconstructed cell. Using spin-polarized scanning tunneling microscopy, complemented by correlative techniques, we present evidence for emergent spin polarizations spreading over the primitive cell, where the spin texture appears to be correlated with the alternating handedness of chiral order. Our first-principles studies identify the origin of intertwined orders as being correlation-driven, with the threading order of chirality emerging when the on-site Coulomb repulsion exceeds a critical value. The observed spin arrangement can be understood in terms of a hybrid character, combining contributions from itinerant electrons and localized d-orbitals. Collectively, these findings expand the realm of chiral order in correlated electron systems and facilitate an appealing platform for chiral spintronic and related applications.Subject terms: Two-dimensional materials; Structural properties; Surfaces, interfaces and thin films

Introduction

Instabilities near the Fermi surface are inherently driven by many-body interactions in solids, and are accompanied by the emergence of exotic collective orders1–5. The intertwining/competition of different macroscopic quantum orders is an important paradigm in condensed matter physics to understand and manipulate emergent collective electron behaviors. For instance, in Cu- or Fe-based materials, particular spin orders (e.g., Mott antiferromagnetism) are believed to be parent states of matter for harboring unconventional superconductivity6–8. With dimensionality reduction, such collective orders become even more prominent, especially in layered materials with atomic thickness. One compelling platform is served by the T-phase group-VB transition metal dichalcogenides, e.g., TaS2, TaSe2, and NbSe2, where the synergistic effects of electron-electron and electron-phonon interactions collectively lead to a √13 × √13 charge density wave (CDW) order characterized by notable in-plane lattice distortions. Strikingly, within each unit cell, thirteen metal atoms collectively reposition themselves to create the well-known Star of David (SoD) cluster9–12. Furthermore, the significant on-site Coulomb repulsion associated with the suppression of inter-unit cell hopping helps to convert these nonmagnetic compounds into correlated magnets13,14, with the magnetic moments fairly localized at the SoD centers15–17, while the magnetic ground states are still under active debate18–20.

In addition to the commonly encountered orderings of lattice, charge, and spin, chirality can serve as yet another distinct and intriguing degree of freedom in the formation of long-range order. So far, a homogeneous chiral order on the macro/meso scale length has been frequently observed in materials with a chiral nature. In contrast, alternating chiral order, which refers to the simultaneous existence of both types of chirality with alternation in space, has been less realized in naturally forming materials. The concept of anti- or alternating-chiral order was initially introduced to describe the packing of left- and right-handed helices in polymers or soft condensed matters21,22 and later actively developed in the applications of artificially designed meta-materials by taking advantage of the chirality-dependent mechanical or acoustical properties23–25. In the realm of many-body physics, structural chirality was known to be closely connected to the movements and interactions of spin and charge26, stimulating a number of theoretical designs of exotic spin textures based on the anti-chiral order27–29. However, alternating-chiral order-mediated correlation phenomena have been rather rare in practical experiments. One pioneering example was reported in URu2Si230, where alternating left- and right-handed f orbitals at the uranium sites were attributed to a nonmagnetic phase transition at low temperature. The desire to realize structures with different chiral properties is naturally inherited in the emerging field of two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) materials, as exemplified by the very recent observations of heterochirality via vdW stacking, reflecting the alternation of chirality between two neighboring layers31,32.

In this work, combining scanning tunneling microscopy/spectroscopy (STM/S) and density functional theory (DFT) calculations, we reveal that in the recently discovered √19 × √19 CDW structure of monolayered (ML) NbTe2, both mirror and inversion symmetries are broken, thus giving rise to a chiral nature to the CDW order33,34. Notably, owing to the presence of non-radial displacements, a pair of structurally chiral sites with opposite handedness, i.e., counterclockwise and clockwise, appears within a reconstructed primitive cell. As a result, an in-plane alternating chiral order with triangular checkerboard patterns emerges, dubbed alterchirality. Moreover, in contrast to the local magnetic moments in the SoD CDW phase15–17, our spin-polarized (SP) STM/S measurements ascertain the presence of an extended spin density spreading over the √19 × √19 superstructure, with the chirality order correlating with the spin texture. Our DFT calculations further help to identify the physical origin of the above experimental observations as being vitally correlation-driven, with the threading order of chirality emerging only when the on-site Coulomb repulsion exceeds a critical value. The calculated spin distribution reproduced the experimental observation and is attributed to the synergistic effects of itinerant electrons and localized d-orbitals. Collectively, these findings demonstrate an expanded regime of chiral order in correlated electron systems, presenting viable platforms for chiral spintronic26,35 and related applications.

Results

Among the family of group-VB dichalcogenides, the collective electronic behaviors of NbTe2 have been less explored experimentally in the monolayer limit, while its bulk form is known to be a 3 × 1 CDW superconductor with TC = 0.7 K36,37. Recent studies have shown that the √19 × √19 CDW ordered phase is the most stable structure of ML NbTe2 at sufficiently high temperatures38,39. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, here we further find that the √19 × √19 phase of NbTe2 can only exist in the monolayer limit, while an earlier study has shown that it can be stabilized in few-layer TaTe240. In principle, the √19 × √19 super-periodicity can be considered as an expanded SoD, as collectively described by the formula that the total number of metal atoms per unit cell is 6n + 141, where n is the number of shells surrounding the central atom (i.e., n = 2 for the √13 × √13 CDW, and n = 3 for the √19 × √19 CDW; see Fig. 1a).

Figure 1b shows a large-scale STM image of a 1T-NbTe2 monolayer grown on a bilayer graphene/SiC(0001) substrate (see growth details in Methods), demonstrating controlled growth of macroscopic samples with a uniform √19 × √19 phase. Within a wide range of negative sample bias, this superstructure is visualized as being composed of three representative spot sites distinguished by their relative topographic heights (see, for example, Fig. 1c at −1.0 V). The bias-dependent morphological evolution for these three spots is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2. A rhombohedral unit cell of the √19 × √19 superstructure is labeled in Fig. 1c with the corners located on the brightest spots. The primitive vector is measured to be ~15.8 Å, while the atomic lattice constant in a pristine NbTe2 cell is measured to be 3.64 Å38. In Fig. 1d, the fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern acquired from a high-resolution image (inset in Fig. 1d) explicitly manifests that the rotation angle between the basis vectors of the CDW pattern and atomic lattice is 23.4°, consistent with the schematic model in Fig. 1a. In our previous work, we have ruled out the possibility that this superstructure originates from the moiré pattern38. To further verify its CDW nature, we analyzed bias-dependent dI/dV maps via Fourier transformation and normalized the CDW reciprocal lattice intensity (ICDW) to the 1 × 1 Bragg peak intensity (see Supplementary Fig. 3 for details). As shown in Fig. 1e, the normalized ICDW vs. energy exhibits a line shape similar to the dI/dV spectrum, with its magnitude suppressed by two orders of magnitude near the Fermi level (EF). This behavior confirms that the spectroscopic dip corresponds to the CDW gap with a magnitude of 2ΔCDW ≈ 100 meV42,43. Following the mean field formula1, 2Δ = αkBT, the transition temperature of the √19 × √19 CDW is estimated to be ~331 K in the weak coupling limit (α = 3.5). This theoretical speculation aligns with our STM observations, which demonstrate that the CDW pattern remains stable at elevated temperatures and even persists at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 4). Figure 1f shows the calculated phonon dispersion of the pristine 1T-NbTe2 monolayer, from which we could find several soft modes, with the lowest lying one appearing near the wave vector 0.25b (b = 2π/a*(1,0)) along the Γ-M direction. The existence of such a soft mode implies the system’s tendency towards developing local order driven by collective atomic displacements. In addition, the 2D mapping of phonon dispersion (Supplementary Fig. 5a) shows that although the reciprocal vectors of the √19 × √19 superlattice appear to be slightly misaligned with that of the minimum frequency mode, these vectors are still deeply located within the unstable region and are therefore expected to have a significant impact on the lattice dynamics. Moreover, the Fermi surface of the pristine monolayered 1T-NbTe2 (Supplementary Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 6) shows that the √19 × √19 R23.4° Fermi-surface nesting vectors well connect the parallel segments of slightly warped triangular pockets around the K points, suggesting that the Fermi-surface nesting may play a role in the formation of CDW orders. However, the electronic susceptibility of this system shows no sharp peak or strong divergence at the nesting vectors39, suggesting that the nesting alone may not be strong enough to spontaneously stimulate the CDW transition. Based on these observations, the formation of CDW phases should actually be structural phase transitions driven by the concerted action of electrons and ions, i.e., the Fermi-surface nesting and electron-phonon coupling39.

Figure 2a displays a magnified image with atomic resolution taken at a close tip-to-sample separation (Vb = +10 mV, It = 5 nA; and a similar image in large scale is seen in Supplementary Fig. 2a). The relaxed √19 × √19 atomic structure obtained from DFT calculations is displayed side by side and partially superimposed with the experimental one. A cluster containing 19 Nb atoms is outlined by the blue dashed lines, exhibiting an apparent deviation from an ideal hexagon with six-fold symmetry. Based on the close agreement between the experimental and theoretical results of bias-dependent topographic contrasts (Supplementary Fig. 2b–g), the supercell defined in Fig. 1c can be drawn on the atomic model as labeled. The corner of the rhombohedral cell (brightest spot in Fig. 1c) corresponds to the center of the 19-Nb cluster, referred to as the “center” site. The two hollow sites can be identified as one bright hollow (“BH”, centered on a bottom-layer Te atom) and one dark hollow (“DH”, centered on a top-layer Te atom). It is intriguing that the DH site manifests as a six-bladed propeller with counterclockwise rotation (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2a).

To quantify the experimentally observed atomic distortions, the 19 top-layer Te atoms within a CDW supercell are indexed as Te #1-#19. The atoms from Te #1 to #12 are located inside a 19-Nb-atom cluster, forming a truncated hexagon with three long and three short edges. Our measurements reveal that the distances between the three inner Te atoms (e.g., Te #1, #2, and #3) are uniformly compressed to 3.0 Å, notably shorter than ~3.64 Å in pristine 1T-NbTe2, suggesting a radial contraction towards the central Nb atom. The situation for the outer-shell Te atoms (e.g., from Te #4 to #12) is different. As seen from the line profiles in Fig. 2b, the distances between the Te atoms along the truncated hexagon edges exhibit substantial variations. The short edges (marked by the cyan lines) are significantly stretched with an average Te-Te distance of 4.20 Å, while the long edges (marked by the pink lines) are compressed with an average Te-Te distance of 3.25 Å. The statistical distributions of Te-Te distances over a large-scale image (containing more than 50 supercells) are shown in Fig. 2c and d. These experimental values are in reasonably good agreement with the calculated ones, where the average Te-Te distances along the stretched and compressed edges are 3.91 and 3.51 Å, respectively. The calculated interatomic distance among Te #1-#3 is 3.48 Å, also shortened from the pristine value, as qualitatively observed experimentally. Our quantitative analyses for other Te atoms near the BH site (e.g., from Te #13 to #18) further demonstrate the overall consistency between the experimental and theoretical results (Supplementary Fig. 7). To verify these peculiar atomic displacements, we also performed non-contact atomic force microscopy (nc-AFM) measurements with a qPlus sensor44. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, the AFM-derived atomic displacements are largely consistent with those obtained from STM.

In the √19 × √19 CDW phase of the NbTe2 monolayer, the divergent stretched vs. contracted atomic displacements along the originally equivalent zig-zag directions suggest non-radial distortions (Fig. 2a), contrary to the universal radial contraction in the √13 × √13 CDW phase9–11. As shown in Fig. 2e, the arrows represent the in-plane displacement vectors extracted by comparing the √19 × √19 superstructure with the pristine 1T-NbTe2 structure. Distinctive behaviors can be visibly revealed for the center, BH, and DH sites. Within each region, the displacements of Te and Nb atoms are concerted and exhibit qualitatively similar tendencies, and for clarity in our subsequent presentations, only the Nb atoms are depicted. In the center region, seven Nb atoms are enclosed in a green shadow, and the radial contraction towards the fixed central Nb atom is the dominant movement. More strikingly, we find that the rotations of the vectors at the DH (masked with the cyan shadow) and BH regions (masked with the pink shadow) form a pair of alternating chiral vortices, corresponding to counterclockwise and clockwise vortices, respectively. Therefore, the observed CDW phase possesses a 2D alternating chiral order with the local chirality centered on the two triangular half supercells associated with alternating handedness. In analogy to the chiral CDW phase of the √13 × √13 SoD33,34, here the newly observed chiral √19 × √19 superstructure can be classified as an alterchiral CDW.

The microscopic driving force for the alterchiral CDW was investigated by systematically examining the dependence of the structure on various physical factors, including strain, interfacial coupling, and electron correlations using DFT calculations. We find that, under comparable physical conditions, the on-site Coulomb repulsion U plays a vital role in inducing the CDW superstructure. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9a, when the on-site Coulomb repulsion U = 0, the Te-Te distances along the stretched (3.68 Å) and compressed (3.55 Å) edges are very close, and the superstructure is barely chiral. In contrast, the distinct alterchiral superstructure emerges from the √19 × √19 CDW phase only when U is large enough (U ≥ 1 eV, Supplementary Fig. 9b). Consequently, the underlying physical origin of the alterchirality is rooted in electron correlations. Note that the emergence of magnetic order in this CDW phase also depends strongly on the U value, as will be elaborated later.

We then examined whether the √19 × √19 CDW phase is accompanied by magnetism. For this purpose, we first fabricated Pb/NbTe2 heterostructures. Clear Kondo resonances were observed at the interface (Supplementary Fig. 15)45,46, similar to previous findings in √13-SoD (e.g., 1T-NbSe2) monolayer/metal heterostructures15,17,47,48, where conduction electrons in the metal layer (Pb here) scatter off magnetic moments in the CDW monolayer. These observations indicate that the √19 × √19 CDW phase drives otherwise nonmagnetic NbTe2 into a magnetic state, motivating further investigation of its spin structure.

To resolve the spin textures within the supercell, we performed SP-STM measurements using a 40-nm-Cr-coated W tip (tip #1; calibration in Supplementary Fig. 11). The antiferromagnetic Cr tip has a small stray field, minimizing perturbation to the sample magnetization, and remains stable under the applied magnetic fields49. Figures 3a and b show the tunneling spectra measured on the BH and DH sites, respectively, at B = 0 T and at B = ± 2 T. Data for the center site are provided in Supplementary Fig. 12a. At both BH and DH sites, the dI/dV spectra at 0 T differs distinctly from those at finite magnetic field, indicating that the probed electronic states are spin polarized. To quantify this contrast, we define the conductance ratio Rw/wo as the dI/dV spectra at B = ± 2 T divided by that at 0 T (Fig. 3c, d). The Rw/wo profiles first show that BH and DH sites exhibit opposite responses to the external magnetic field, while the center site mimics the BH sites (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b), suggesting that the spin polarizations are synchronized with the alternating structural chirality.

We then performed differential conductance mapping to visualize the spatial distribution of the spin contrast (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d). Because the spin-averaged CDW modulation is much stronger than the spin-dependent contribution, we isolated the latter by constructing a difference map between B = 0 T and +2 T. As discussed in Supplementary Fig. 12, such subtraction is necessary to extract the weaker spin-dependent signal from the dominant spin-averaged spatial modulation, and similar differential analysis is standard in SP-STM studies of antiferromagnetic and ferrimagnetic systems8,50,51. To visualize the real-space distribution of the spin polarization ratio P (defined as 2 dI/dV∣0T−dI/dV∣2TdI/dV∣0T+dI/dV∣2T)52, we divided the difference map by the sum of the same two maps, as shown in Fig. 3e. The opposite spin polarization P at the DH and BH sites can be unambiguously observed. Figure 3f shows the profile along the supercell diagonal (dashed black line in Fig. 3e), revealing a sharp spin-up (i.e., positive P) at the DH site (P ~ 3 %), contrasting with spin-down dominance at the BH (P ~ −4 %) and center (P ~ −1.5 %) sites.

The observed spin-polarized features were verified by repeated measurements on different NbTe2 samples using multiple Cr-coated W tips. As shown in the Supplementary Fig. 13a–f, three independently prepared tips (#2–#4; calibration in Supplementary Fig. 17), fabricated following the same procedure as tip #1, all reproduce the spectroscopic contrast between the BH and DH sites. Figure 3g summarizes the extracted P values from all four tips. The consistency across multiple tips confirms that the observed contrast originates from genuine spin polarization rather than a tip-dependent artifact. In particular, P has opposite signs at the DH and BH sites, establishing a robust local spin signature. Although the absolute sign depends on the tip magnetization direction – tips #3 and #4 show the same field dependence as tip #1, whereas tip #2 exhibits an overall sign reversal – the underlying DH/BH contrast remains unchanged. In addition, we provided another spatial mapping of P acquired with tip #4 (Supplementary Fig. 14a–c), which closely mirrors the pattern shown in Fig. 3e. As a control, neither the spectroscopic contrast nor the P map is observed when using a nonmagnetic W tip (Supplementary Fig. 13g, h and Supplementary Fig. 14f). Further details are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

Beyond the opposite field responses at the BH and DH sites, we note that the spectral change does not reverse upon reversing the field polarity. This unusual behavior may point to a preferred spin orientation in ML-NbTe2. One possible scenario is that both the thick Cr-coated tip49 and the sample are predominantly in-plane polarized, such that the spin-polarized tunneling contribution is strongest near zero field and becomes suppressed at finite fields of either polarity. Further discussion on this tentative scenario, along with related control measurements, is provided in Supplementary Note 2.

To gain insight into the origin of the unexpected magnetism, we carried out DFT calculations by further considering the effect of the on-site Coulomb repulsion U. Even though the alterchiral superstructure emerges when U ≥ 1 eV, as discussed earlier, the system exhibits magnetism only for U > 2.5 eV. Remarkably, as U further increases to 3 eV or above, oppositely oriented spin-charge densities develop at the BH and DH sites (see Supplementary Fig. 19), indicating that the strong on-site Coulomb repulsion and resultant electron correlation effects must have played an important role in the emergence of the local magnetism. With U = 4 eV, we obtain the most stable structure of the √19 × √19 CDW phase with a net magnetization of approximately 2.55 μB per supercell. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, the atomic-scale distributions of the magnetic moments at the center site resemble well with those of the SoD cluster in the √13 × √13 CDW phase, where the magnetization orientations of the surrounding Nb atoms are anti-parallel to that of the central one. In contrast, in the BH or DH region, the spin directions of the central three Nb atoms remain mutually parallel, consistent with the expectation of the P3 space group of the √19 × √19 superstructure. In addition, the spin configurations between the BH and DH sites are anti-parallel to each other, while the spin direction at the center site is the same as that at the BH site, as displayed in Fig. 4a, b. All these features agree well with the dI/dV spectra and spin-polarized STM images shown in Fig. 3. Furthermore, the energetic preference for this distinct spin configuration was verified by examining eight initial candidate spin configurations (Supplementary Fig. 20). Upon full energy relaxation, the calculations converged to two degenerate ground states (Supplementary Fig. 20i, j), corresponding to the spin configuration in Fig. 4a and its time-reversed counterpart. This comparison demonstrates that the observed anti-parallel spin arrangement is not solely determined by differences in the local atomic configuration (specifically, alternating chirality), but also arises from magnetic exchange interactions among the center, BH, and DH sites.

The projected band structure and density of states (DOS) of the CDW phase without and with spin polarization are shown in Fig. 4c and d, respectively. Strong hybridization between the Nb-4d and Te-5p orbitals and pronounced spin splitting of the electronic states near the Fermi level are observed. In real space, this spin splitting also leads to a reduction in charge density of the itinerant electrons, corresponding to the states in the energy window of [EF −0.08 eV, EF] (Supplementary Fig. 21). In addition, compared with the nonmagnetic case, the energy gain resulting from magnetization and time-reversal symmetry breaking amounts to 180.12 meV per supercell.

Besides the on-site Coulomb repulsion and resultant electron correlation effects, we also examine non-local effects that may contribute to the unexpected magnetic properties in the √19 × √19 CDW phase. In doing so, we consider the exchange parameters of the Heisenberg Hamiltonian and the Stoner criterion of N(EF)I, with N(EF) and I being the DOS at the Fermi level and the strength of the exchange interaction, respectively. First, when the Nb-Nb interatomic distances are larger than 6 Å, the exchange parameters are very small (~0.02 meV) (Supplementary Fig. 22), indicating that the long-range interactions between Nb ions mediated by itinerant electrons, such as the Ruderman–Kittel–Kasuya–Yosida (RKKY) interaction, are weak. Secondly, the core of the Stoner criterion is the spin-splitting instability induced by a high DOS at the Fermi level, which is a universal driving force for magnetic ordering, especially ferromagnetism in metals. In particular, the physical principle of the universal Stoner instability can also be extended to understanding the stabilization of ferrimagnetic order, especially in materials with complex lattices53. The value of N(EF)I is calculated to be ~0.903, close to 1, indicating that 1T-NbTe2 is close to the border of itinerant magnetism. Based on the above analyses, we attribute the spin polarizations to the synergistic effects of localized d-orbitals (governed by strong on-site Coulomb repulsion and electron correlation) and itinerant electrons (i.e., Stoner instability).

Discussion

In conclusion, we present systematic evidence for spin polarization induced by the √19 × √19 CDW transition in monolayered NbTe2. Most strikingly, an alterchiral order, which is so far absent in crystalline monolayers, has not only been realized in the lattice and charge degrees of freedom, but is also synchronized with the spin texture. The alternating spin polarization has been further shown to stem from hybrid contributions of the itinerant electrons and localized d-orbitals of the Nb atoms. These findings manifest a rare opportunity to explore the rich physics of chirality and alterchirality in correlated electron systems that exhibit mutually coupled multi-degrees of freedom, with important application potentials in sight.

Methods

Sample growth

A NbTe2 monolayer was grown on an epitaxial bilayer graphene (BLG)-terminated 6H-SiC(0001) substrate in an ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) molecular beam epitaxy chamber with a base pressure of ~2 × 10−10 Torr. High-purity Nb (99.5%) and Te (99.999%) were co-evaporated on the BLG substrate54 from an electron-beam evaporator and a standard Knudsen cell, respectively. The flux ratio was Nb:Te = 1:20, and the substrate was held at 350 °C during the film deposition. After growth, the films were annealed at 400 °C for 2 hours to improve the sample quality. To protect the sample from oxidation in air, 10-nm Te capping layers were deposited on ML-NbTe2 at room temperature before moving it from the ultrahigh vacuum environment to perform SQUID measurements.

STM/S and qPlus nc-AFM measurements

All STM/S measurements were performed on a commercial Unisoku 1300 LT-STM system at 4.8 K under UHV conditions. For spin-averaged STM/S measurements, an electrochemically etched tungsten tip was used and calibrated on a clean Cu(111) surface before measurements. STM topographic images are acquired in constant-current mode. For spin-polarized STM/S measurements, 5-nm and 40-nm Cr layers were deposited on clean W tips, followed by annealing at 500 K for 10 min. Prior to the Cr deposition, the W tips were flashed to ~2000 K to remove the oxides on the tips. The spin-polarized properties of the Cr-coated tips were examined on standard magnetic samples of Co islands on Cu(111) and cleaved Fe1+yTe surface (see Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Fig. 17). The dI/dV spectra and conductance maps at a constant tip-sample distance were taken by using a standard lock-in technique with a bias modulation frequency of 973 Hz. The nc-AFM measurements were performed in constant-height mode by monitoring the frequency shift (Δf) of a qPlus sensor oscillating with an amplitude of 100 pm. The resonance frequency and quality factor of the sensor were f0 = 28.46 kHz and Q = 50206, respectively.

DFT calculations

All first-principles calculations were performed within DFT implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)55 and Quantum Espresso56, using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof (PBE)57 as the exchange-correlation functional. For VASP calculations, a 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-mesh58 was used to sample the first Brillouin zone. The core electrons were treated fully relativistically by the projector augmented-wave method59, while the valence electrons were processed within the scalar relativistic approximation, with a plane-wave cutoff of 500 eV. All the atoms were allowed to fully relax during structural optimization until all the forces on each atom were less than 0.01 eV/Å. All calculations were performed using the DFT + U formalism60 within VASP, unless otherwise specified. To capture the lattice-driven reconstruction, a Gaussian smearing of 0.02 eV was adopted. The phonon spectrum of 1T-NbTe2 was calculated using density functional perturbation theory61, as implemented in the Quantum Espresso package, with a Gaussian smearing of 0.002 Ry and a 12 × 12 × 1 q-mesh.
