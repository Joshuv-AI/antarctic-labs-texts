FABP5 drives silica-induced myofibroblast senescence and pulmonary fibrosis through a FASN/UCP2 metabolic-mitochondrial axis
Authors: Wenqing Sun, Ting Wang, Qingyan Yang, Wenxiu Lian, Xinying Jia, Wei Wang, Yi Liu, Chunhui Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Silicosis is a devastating occupational lung disease driven by chronic oxidative stress and the persistent accumulation of senescent cells. However, the metabolic mechanisms underlying silica-induced senescence and subsequent fibrotic remodeling remain elusive. Here, we identify fatty acid binding protein 5 (FABP5) as a central metabolic driver of silica-induced myofibroblast senescence. Intracellularly, FABP5 interacts with and shields fatty acid synthase (FASN) from ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation, disrupting lipid homeostasis and causing uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2)-dependent mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular senescence. Extracellularly, senescent fibroblasts secrete FABP5 as an unconventional senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factor. This secreted FABP5 activates β-catenin signaling, creating a paracrine feed-forward loop that drives the activation of neighboring quiescent fibroblasts. Therapeutically, systemic silencing of FABP5 using lipid nanoparticles (siFabp5-LNPs) robustly attenuates cellular senescence, metabolic disruption, and fibrotic remodeling in young and aged mouse models of pulmonary fibrosis induced by silica dust. Collectively, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized FABP5/FASN/UCP2 metabolic-redox axis that drives stress-induced senescence. By linking intracellular mitochondrial dysfunction with extracellular pro-fibrotic signaling, FABP5 emerges as a highly promising therapeutic target and potential biomarker for silicosis and other oxidative stress-driven fibrotic diseases.

Introduction

Pneumoconiosis, predominantly represented by silicosis, is a devastating occupational lung disease characterized by irreversible pulmonary fibrosis. Despite improved occupational protections, the emergence of industries like artificial stone fabrication has fueled new outbreaks of rapidly accelerated silicosis [[1], [2], [3]]. Currently, therapeutic options are largely palliative, with lung transplantation remaining the only definitive treatment [4]. This lack of disease-modifying therapies highlights a critical gap in our understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving fibrotic progression. Therefore, elucidating the fundamental cellular drivers of silicosis is imperative for identifying novel targets capable of halting or reversing the disease.

Cellular senescence underpins the development of a wide range of chronic diseases [5,6]. Although senescent cells undergo stable cell cycle arrest, they remain metabolically active and acquire a pro-fibrotic senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [7,8]. Consequently, clearing senescent cells has proven effective in alleviating pulmonary fibrosis in experimental models [9,10]. Unlike idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, which is inherently age-related, silicosis is driven by specific occupational toxicants. The chronic stress and sterile inflammation induced by internalized silica particles act as potent triggers for premature cellular senescence. However, the fundamental metabolic and molecular mechanisms underlying stress-induced senescence in silicosis remain poorly understood.

Metabolic reprogramming is a defining feature of cellular senescence, supporting the energetic and biosynthetic demands of the SASP [11,12]. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses consistently identify lipid metabolic alterations—particularly dysregulated fatty acid oxidation and lipid droplet accumulation—as hallmarks of the senescent state [[13], [14], [15]]. Fatty acid metabolism regulates redox balance, mitochondrial function, and epigenetic remodeling, thereby closely linking it to senescence [16]. Crucially, the intracellular trafficking and utilization of these fatty acids are coordinated by fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs), a family of lipid chaperones essential for metabolic homeostasis. Increasing evidence links aberrant FABP expression to aging and metabolic dysfunction. Notably, the combined deletion of Fabp4 and Fabp5 extends healthspan and protects against age-associated pathologies in mice [17], and FABP5 has recently been identified as a pro-senescent factor in specific tissues such as the aging retina [18]. Despite these observations, whether FABP5-mediated lipid metabolic remodeling acts as a driver of stress-induced cellular senescence and fibrotic progression in silicosis remains unclear.

In this study, we identify FABP5 as a critical driver of stress-induced cellular senescence and a novel therapeutic target for silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis. Utilizing an established murine model of silicosis, we observed a significant accumulation of senescent myofibroblasts characterized by markedly upregulated FABP5 expression. Mechanistically, we reveal that FABP5 promotes senescence by competitively inhibiting the ubiquitination of FASN. This protein-protein interaction stabilizes FASN, thereby disrupting intracellular lipid homeostasis and provoking UCP2-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction—a potent trigger for cellular senescence. Furthermore, we demonstrate that senescent myofibroblasts actively secrete FABP5 into the pulmonary microenvironment. Acting as a novel SASP component, extracellular FABP5 exacerbates the activation of adjacent fibroblasts, creating a paracrine feed-forward loop that perpetuates the fibrotic cascade. Crucially, in vivo knockdown of FABP5 profoundly attenuated silica-induced cellular senescence, lipid metabolic disruption, and subsequent pulmonary fibrosis. Collectively, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized FABP5-FASN-UCP2 metabolic axis in stress-induced senescence and highlight the dual intracellular and extracellular roles of FABP5 in fibrogenesis, providing a compelling mechanistic rationale for its targeted inhibition in silicosis.

To determine whether silica-induced senescence is an intrinsic fibroblast phenotype, we analyzed primary lung fibroblasts isolated from in vivo exposed mice. Ex vivo profiling confirmed a sustained retention of the senescent state, evidenced by intense SA-β-gal positivity (Supplementary Fig. S1a) and persistent upregulation of p21 and FABP5 (Supplementary Fig. S1b). Furthermore, this senescent programming could be triggered via epithelial-mesenchymal crosstalk. NIH/3T3 cells co-cultured with silica-pretreated MLE-12 epithelial cells underwent pronounced paracrine senescence, accompanied by a marked concomitant upregulation of p21 and FABP5 (Supplementary Fig. S1c-d). Thus, both direct silica injury and the injured epithelial microenvironment drive stable fibroblast senescence and FABP5 induction.

To definitively confirm whether FABP5 acts as a master pathological driver, we performed gain-of-function experiments via plasmid-mediated FABP5 overexpression in myofibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S4a). Ectopic FABP5 expression increased p16 and p21 levels, enhanced SA-β-gal positivity and promoted γH2AX foci formation (Supplementary Fig. S4b-d). To define the mechanism by which FABP5 promotes myofibroblast senescence, we first examined whether FABP5 regulates key enzymes involved in lipid metabolism. Given the lipid accumulation observed in senescent fibroblasts, we focused on fatty acid synthase (FASN). Silencing FABP5 reduced FASN protein abundance without affecting its mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. S5a, b), suggesting post-transcriptional regulation. Co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed an endogenous interaction between FABP5 and FASN (Supplementary Fig. S5c). Cycloheximide chase assays showed that FASN degradation was accelerated upon FABP5 depletion, whereas FASN stability was increased in senescent fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S5d). Inhibition of the proteasome with bortezomib resulted in FASN accumulation, whereas lysosomal inhibition had no detectable effect (Supplementary Fig. S5e), indicating that FASN is primarily degraded through the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway. Consistently, ubiquitination assays demonstrated a reduction in ubiquitinated FASN following FABP5 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S5f). These data establish that FABP5 stabilizes FASN by limiting its ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation.

We next examined whether stabilized FASN mediates the pro-senescent effects of FABP5. Knockdown of FASN in etoposide-treated fibroblasts reduced p16 and p21 expression, decreased SA-β-gal activity and γH2AX foci formation (Fig. 5a–d), and suppressed transcription of multiple SASP components (Supplementary Fig. S6a). Moreover, depletion of FASN attenuated the pro-senescent and pro-fibrotic effects induced by FABP5 overexpression, as evidenced by reduced activation of naive fibroblasts exposed to conditioned medium from these cells (Fig. 5b). Because fatty acid metabolism is closely linked to mitochondrial homeostasis, we next assessed mitochondrial function. FASN depletion reduced intracellular ROS levels and restored ATP production and mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1) (Fig. 5c–e; Supplementary Fig. S6b), suggesting improved mitochondrial function. TEM further revealed that FABP5 overexpression induced mitochondrial swelling and cristae disruption (Fig. 6a). These structural abnormalities were alleviated by pharmacological inhibition of FASN or by silencing UCP2, a mitochondrial uncoupling protein responsive to fatty acid availability. Notably, previous studies have established UCP2 as a critical driver of myofibroblast senescence [19]. Consistently, inhibition of FASN or depletion of UCP2 restored mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 6d, left panels) and reduced FABP5-induced p16 and p21 expression and SA-β-gal activity (Fig. 6b and c, left panels). Discussion

In this study, we identified a profound upregulation of FABP5 in senescent myofibroblasts during silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis. We show that FABP5 is markedly enriched in senescent myofibroblasts in both murine silicosis models and human fibrotic lung tissue. Mechanistically, FABP5 stabilizes FASN and engages a downstream UCP2-dependent mitochondrial pathway, thereby promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and senescence. In parallel, we uncover a paracrine role for extracellular FABP5 in activating neighboring fibroblasts through β-catenin signaling. Together, these findings position FABP5 at the intersection of metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial homeostasis, and fibroblast fate determination during silicosis progression.

Silicosis remains a major occupational lung disease worldwide. Chronic inhalation of crystalline silica results in persistent inflammation, progressive alveolar destruction, and irreversible fibrotic remodeling. Despite improvements in workplace regulation, outbreaks linked to engineered stone processing highlight the continuing global relevance of this disease [[20], [21], [22]]. The absence of effective anti-fibrotic therapies reflects, in part, an incomplete understanding of the molecular drivers underlying fibrotic progression. Our study addresses this gap by linking lipid metabolic dysregulation to fibroblast senescence in silicosis.

Cellular senescence has emerged as a central feature of age-related tissue dysfunction and chronic disease. Accumulating evidence indicates that the persistent accumulation of senescent myofibroblasts results in pathological ECM deposition and the secretion of a pro-fibrotic SASP [[23], [24], [25]]. Consistent with previous reports in IPF and bleomycin-induced injury, we observed excessive accumulation of senescent myofibroblasts in silica-induced fibrotic lungs, particularly in aged animals. We propose that initial silica-induced oxidative stress within the alveolar microenvironment triggers a cascade of metabolic reprogramming in fibroblasts. Unlike transient chemical injuries, the persistent physical presence of indigestible silica crystals causes continuous cycles of macrophage death, frustrated phagocytosis, and excessive generation of reactive oxygen species. We postulate that this relentless inflammatory and oxidative microenvironment imposes extraordinary metabolic demands on resident fibroblasts, forcing FABP5 upregulation as an initial stress response that ultimately culminates in irreversible senescence.

Growing evidence demonstrates that metabolic dysregulation is a critical risk factor for a wide spectrum of human diseases [[26], [27], [28]]. Within this context, dysregulation of lipid metabolism is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of pulmonary fibrosis. Previous studies have demonstrated that disrupted fatty acid homeostasis contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction, redox imbalance, and epigenetic remodeling, all of which are central features of the senescent phenotype [13,[29], [30], [31]]. In fibrotic lung disease, transcriptomic and functional analyses have similarly identified coordinated changes in lipid metabolic pathways, implicating metabolic reprogramming as a driver rather than a consequence of fibrosis [32,33]. Consistent with these observations, our DIA-based proteomic profiling revealed substantial remodeling of fatty acid metabolic networks in silica-induced fibrotic lungs, with FABP5 emerging as one of the most prominently upregulated lipid chaperones. Published datasets from aged and senescent fibroblasts similarly show increased FABP5 expression, supporting a broader association with cellular aging [34,35]. Functionally, FABP5 overexpression was sufficient to induce senescence-associated phenotypes, whereas its depletion attenuated DNA damage responses, SASP expression, and mitochondrial dysfunction. These observations suggest that FABP5 is not merely a marker but an active contributor to fibroblast senescence.

Mechanistically, we identify FASN as a key downstream effector of FABP5. Emerging evidence has increasingly positioned FASN not merely as a metabolic enzyme, but as an active driver of cellular senescence. For instance, heightened FASN activity has been shown to be indispensable for the initial stages of senescence induction [36], and specific post-translational modifications of FASN directly govern lipid-mediated senescent phenotypes in other models, such as chondrocytes [37]. Consistent with this pro-senescent role, inhibition of FASN robustly attenuated fibroblast senescence in our silicosis model. FABP5 interacted with FASN and increased its protein stability by limiting ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation. Traditionally, FABPs are recognized primarily as intracellular lipid shuttles; however, our findings highlight a non-canonical, protein-protein interaction paradigm. We hypothesize that the physical binding of FABP5 to FASN may induce a conformational change or provide steric hindrance that obscures specific lysine residues on FASN, thereby shielding it from E3 ligase recognition. This chaperone-mediated stabilization reveals a novel layer of post-translational metabolic regulation, effectively locking myofibroblasts into a senescent and pro-fibrotic trajectory.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of senescent cells and a driver of ROS amplification [38]. We demonstrate that the FABP5/FASN axis converges on UCP2, a mitochondrial inner membrane protein, to orchestrate mitochondrial depolarization and senescence-associated phenotypes during silicosis. This mitochondrial metabolic cascade strongly aligns with and significantly extends the landmark findings of Rangarajan et al. [19], who identified UCP2 as a critical driver of myofibroblast senescence in IPF and bleomycin-induced injury. In their study, chronic UCP2 elevation induced pro-oxidant senescent reprogramming, dissipated mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨ), impaired fatty acid oxidation, and promoted fibrosis progression. Importantly, in vivo silencing of UCP2 in aging-associated fibrosis induced regression of established fibrosis, establishing UCP2 as a pro-fibrotic redox regulator in chronic lung disease. While these findings position UCP2 as a functional mediator of senescence, the upstream metabolic signals sustaining its activation under chronic fibrotic stress remain incompletely defined. Unlike age-associated IPF or transient chemical-induced bleomycin (BLM) injury, silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis represents a chronic, particle-driven inflammatory condition characterized by persistent oxidative burden and prolonged metabolic remodeling. Within this context, our data place the FABP5/FASN-mediated lipogenic pathway upstream of UCP2 and suggest that dysregulated lipid synthesis may condition UCP2 activation during chronic silica exposure.

Mechanistically, UCP2 is not constitutively active but is facilitated by long-chain fatty acids, functioning in part through fatty acid–dependent transport mechanisms [39,40]. Enhanced de novo lipogenesis downstream of FABP5-mediated FASN stabilization may therefore increase the intracellular availability of lipid species capable of modulating UCP2 activity under sustained silica-induced stress. Although we did not directly quantify the precise mitochondrial pools of FASN-derived free fatty acids (FFAs), which remains a technical challenge to be addressed in future lipid-tracer studies, this proposed metabolic link is highly consistent with the established biophysical properties of UCP2. Although the precise bioenergetic role of UCP2 remains context-dependent and may limit excessive ROS under certain physiological conditions [41], sustained upregulation in a lipid-overloaded and oxidatively stressed environment could compromise mitochondrial coupling efficiency, reduce ATP production, and destabilize membrane potential. Consequently, this persistent activation leads to the altered mitochondrial membrane potential, redox imbalance, and fibroblast senescence observed in fibrotic lung disease [19,42]. Thus, rather than demonstrating direct biophysical coupling, our findings strongly support a model where the FABP5/FASN-driven lipid accumulation serves as a critical metabolic switch that triggers UCP2-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, locking fibroblasts into an irreversible pro-fibrotic senescent phenotype.

Beyond delineating a linear signaling cascade, these findings suggest that the FABP5/FASN/UCP2 axis represents a lipid-sensitive metabolic–mitochondrial regulatory interface in fibroblasts. Excessive endogenous fatty acid synthesis may increase substrate burden and perturb mitochondrial redox homeostasis. In this context, UCP2 upregulation may initially serve as a compensatory adaptation to lipid stress; however, persistent activation could reinforce mitochondrial dysfunction and promote stable senescence commitment. Thus, rather than acting as an isolated effector, the FABP5/FASN/UCP2 pathway appears to integrate lipid flux with mitochondrial bioenergetics and influence fibroblast fate under chronic silica-induced stress.

Notably, our study uncovers a novel, functionally distinct extracellular role for FABP5, challenging the traditional paradigm of this protein acting strictly as a cytosolic lipid chaperone. Emerging evidence highlights the capacity of FABP5 to be actively secreted, functioning as a potent systemic and microenvironmental signaling mediator; for instance, circulating FABP5 facilitates endocrine metabolic crosstalk in type 2 diabetes [43], while extracellular FABP5 actively reprograms the transcriptome and metabolome of recipient stem cells [44]. In line with this unconventional secretory and signaling capacity, we detected elevated circulating FABP5 in silica-inhaled mice and patients, as well as increased secretion from senescent fibroblasts. While the canonical SASP is largely characterized by inflammatory cytokines and proteases [45], our data expand this repertoire, highlighting secreted FABP5 as an unconventional, lipid chaperone-associated SASP component. Consistent with this secretory framework, we demonstrate that extracellular FABP5 acts as a potent paracrine signal that directly triggers β-catenin-mediated activation of neighboring resident fibroblasts, a pathway that both aligns with and substantially extends recent findings in pulmonary hypertension models [46]. While Lei et al. focused on intracellular FABP5-mediated endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition during vascular remodeling, we unveil a compartment-specific bifunctional model of FABP5 in parenchymal lung fibrosis. In this dual-action paradigm, intracellular FABP5 drives myofibroblast senescence through the FASN/UCP2 mitochondrial axis, whereas extracellularly secreted FABP5 acts as a paracrine mediator to propagate and amplify fibrogenic signals throughout the tissue stroma. This raises an intriguing mechanistic question for future studies: whether secreted FABP5 directly stimulates cell-surface receptors as a protein ligand, or functions as a specialized lipid-delivery shuttle transporting specific pro-fibrotic lipophilic species. Ultimately, this self-amplifying loop, in which intracellular FABP5 drives senescence commitment while secreted FABP5 activates surrounding fibroblasts, explains how fibrotic remodeling is sustained within the alveolar niche even after the initiating silica stimulus has subsided.

Therapeutically, the systemic delivery of siFabp5-LNPs robustly attenuated senescence markers and fibrotic remodeling in both young and aged mice, supporting the translational relevance of targeting this pathway. Importantly, metabolic reprogramming typically precedes irreversible structural remodeling. Therefore, early intervention targeting FABP5-mediated lipid flux may offer superior clinical benefits compared to strategies aimed solely at suppressing inflammation or extracellular matrix deposition. Moreover, by silencing FABP5, this nanotherapeutic approach effectively dismantles both the intracellular metabolic crash and the extracellular SASP-mediated fibrotic cascade. In addition to its therapeutic promise, the elevated circulating FABP5 observed in our experimental and clinical samples indicates its potential as a non-invasive biomarker. Monitoring serum FABP5 levels could provide valuable insights into the ongoing fibrotic activity or senescence burden in silicosis patients, pending future validation in larger clinical cohorts.

This study has limitations. First, although we place UCP2 downstream of the FABP5/FASN axis, the specific FASN-derived lipid species and real-time flux facilitating UCP2 activation remain to be directly quantified, and FABP5 likely interfaces with additional metabolic pathways beyond FASN/UCP2. Second, while we focused on fibroblasts, secreted FABP5 may engage surface receptors on other lung-resident or immune cell populations, potentially contributing to broader remodeling processes like macrophage polarization. Third, the exact biophysical mechanism by which secreted FABP5 activates downstream β-catenin signaling remains a fascinating biochemical question for future investigations, particularly regarding whether it functions as a pure protein ligand or a lipid-delivering vehicle. Finally, longitudinal clinical studies will be required to determine whether circulating FABP5 levels correlate with disease severity or progression in pneumoconiosis.

In summary, our findings define FABP5 as a dual-function metabolic regulator linking lipid reprogramming to mitochondrial dysfunction and myofibroblast senescence in silicosis. By integrating intracellular FASN/UCP2 metabolic control with extracellular β-catenin-mediated paracrine signaling, FABP5 emerges as a multifaceted mediator of fibrotic progression and a highly compelling therapeutic target in occupational lung disease.

Materials and methods

1Animal models

1Animal models

Animal models

Young (8-10 weeks old) and aged (15-16 months old) male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing, China). All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility with standard light/dark cycles and free access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-2402034) of Nanjing Medical University.

To induce pulmonary fibrosis, young and aged mice were intratracheally injected with saline or a silica (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) suspension at a dose of 125 mg/kg body weight. After 28 days, the mice were sacrificed, and blood and lung tissues were collected for further analysis.

For the in vivo Fabp5 knockdown model, Fabp5 siRNA-loaded lipid nanoparticles (siFabp5-LNPs) or scramble siRNA-loaded lipid nanoparticles (siNC-LNPs) were administered via tail-vein injection on days 1, 7, 14, and 21 following the silica instillation. To evaluate LNP biodistribution, mice were anesthetized and imaged using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer, USA) at 24 h post-injection of DIR-labeled LNPs. 2Preparation and characterization of siFabp5-LNPs

Preparation and characterization of siFabp5-LNPs

Lipid nanoparticles were formulated as delivery vehicles to encapsulate Fabp5 siRNA. Briefly, a lipid mixture consisting of SPC, DOTAP, cholesterol, DSPE-PEG-2K, and DIR was dissolved in 2 mL of anhydrous ethanol and transferred to a round-bottom flask. The Fabp5 siRNA or scramble siRNA was dissolved in citrate buffer containing 25% ethanol, slowly added to the lipid mixture, and incubated for 20 min. After sonication and extrusion through a liposome extruder (100 nm polycarbonate filter membrane, Mini-extruder, Avanti, US), unentrapped siRNA was removed by dialysis using a nano-dialysis device (polycarbonate membrane, 50 nm pore size). Finally, the siRNA-LNPs were diluted with deionized water. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the siRNA-LNPs were measured using a NanoBrook 90Plus PALS particle size analyzer (dynamic light scattering, DLS) (Brookhaven Instruments, US). The morphology of the nanoparticles was observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2-20). The physicochemical characteristics of the siRNA-LNPs are detailed in the Supplementary Figures.3.Proteomics analysis

3.Proteomics analysis

Proteomics analysis

Total proteins were extracted from lung tissues of control and silica-treated mice. Briefly, lung tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to remove debris. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. For proteomic analysis, equal amounts of protein from each sample were reduced with dithiothreitol (DTT), alkylated with iodoacetamide (IAA), and digested with sequencing-grade trypsin overnight at 37°C. The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 solid-phase extraction columns and dried under vacuum. Peptides were analyzed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) using a high-resolution mass spectrometer operated in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. The DIA-based proteomic profiling, including raw data acquisition, spectral library construction, peptide identification, and protein quantification, was performed by BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). Protein identification was conducted against the UniProt mouse reference proteome database, and the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at <1% at both peptide and protein levels.

The processed protein quantification matrix generated by BGI was used for subsequent bioinformatic analyses. Downstream statistical analyses were performed using R software. Data were log2-transformed and normalized prior to analysis. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between control and silica-treated groups were identified based on fold change and statistical significance (P < 0.05).4.Histopathological and tissue immunofluorescence analysis

4.Histopathological and tissue immunofluorescence analysis

Histopathological and tissue immunofluorescence analysis

The right lower lung lobes of the mice were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, the lung tissues were paraffin-embedded and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. For histopathological assessment, lung sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), using standard procedures to evaluate the degree of structural disruption and fibrosis.

Lung tissue sections from control subjects and silicosis patients were obtained after informed consent at the Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (Nanjing, China) in accordance with institutional review board protocols.

For tissue multiplex immunofluorescence staining, sections were subjected to deparaffinization, rehydration, and heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking with 5% BSA, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against α-SMA, p21, and FABP5. The sections were then washed and incubated with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a confocal scanning microscope.5.Cell isolation, culture, and treatments

5.Cell isolation, culture, and treatments

Cell isolation, culture, and treatments

Primary pulmonary fibroblasts were isolated from adult C57BL/6 mice. Briefly, fresh mouse lung tissues were washed in PBS containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin (New Cell & Molecular Biotech), minced, and digested in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, VivaCell Biosciences) supplemented with 0.1% collagenase I (Gibco) for 30 min at 37°C. Digestion was terminated by adding an equal volume of DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, VivaCell Biosciences). Following the addition of 0.01% DNase I for 10 min at 37°C, the cell suspension was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in complete DMEM and cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Human embryonic lung fibroblasts (MRC-5), mouse fibroblasts (NIH/3T3), and alveolar epithelial cells (MLE-12) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in MEM or DMEM/F12 appropriately supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

To induce senescence in vitro, fibroblasts were treated with etoposide for 6h, and then the medium was removed and replaced with fresh medium, followed by further incubation for 72h. In paracrine senescence models, NIH/3T3 cells were co-cultured with silica-pretreated MLE-12 cells. For conditioned medium (CM) experiments, CM was collected from relevant fibroblast cultures, filtered, and applied to recipient cells. Where indicated, cells were also treated with recombinant human FABP5 protein (MCE, HY-P75214).6.RNA interference and plasmid transfection

6.RNA interference and plasmid transfection

Specific siRNAs targeting FABP5 (including siFABP5-S2), FASN, UCP2, and β-catenin, along with corresponding scramble controls, were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transient transfections of siRNAs or plasmids were performed using riboFECT™ CP Reagent (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer's protocols. 7.RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from lung tissues and cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Tiangen, Beijing, China). 8.Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis

Total protein from lung tissues was extracted using T-PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Scientific), and cellular proteins were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using an Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein (60 μg) were separated on 10%–15% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-FABP5 (Abclonal, A27255), anti-FASN (Abclonal, A22462), anti-Fibronectin (Abclonal, A12977), anti-Collagen I (Abclonal, A1352), anti-α-SMA (Abclonal, A17910), anti-p16 (HUABIO, ET1602-9), anti-p21 (HuaBio, HA500005), anti-AMPK (Abclonal, A12718), and anti-p-AMPK (Abclonal, AP1441). 9.Cellular immunofluorescence staining

Cellular immunofluorescence staining

Cells cultured in confocal dishes were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. Nile Red staining

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

Intracellular ATP and ROS assays

14.Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

To examine endogenous protein interactions, Co-IP assays were performed. Cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Cell lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatant was pre-cleared with Protein A/G agarose beads. The lysates were then incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies or control IgG. Next, Protein A/G agarose beads were added and incubated for an additional 2–4 h at 4°C to capture the immune complexes. The beads were washed extensively with lysis buffer, and the bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer. 15.Protein stability, degradation, and ubiquitination assays

Protein stability, degradation, and ubiquitination assays

To evaluate the half-life and stability of the FASN protein, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays were performed. Briefly, fibroblasts under relevant treatments were treated with the protein synthesis inhibitor CHX (10 μg/mL, HY-12320, MedChemExpress, China) for the indicated time intervals. 17Statistical Analysis

Statistical Analysis

All experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software.
