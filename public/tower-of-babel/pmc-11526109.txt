Chromosome-level genome assembly and annotation of the skinnycheek lanternfish Benthosema ptertum
Authors: Qiaohong Liu, Xiaoying Cao, Lisheng Wu, Huan Wang, Hai Li, Longshan Lin, Shufang Liu, Shaoxiong Ding
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLanternfish not only boast the most abundant biomass among marine fish species but also play a vital role in marine ecosystems. As one of the lanternfish species with the highest global catch, the skinnycheek lanternfish (Benthosema pterotum) is widely distributed in the Indo-Pacific region, playing a pivotal role in the marine biological pump. This study constructed the first chromosome-level genome of B. pterotum using a combination of short-read sequencing, PacBio, and Hi-C sequencing technologies. The genome size of B. pterotum is 1,272.53 Mb, with a contig N50 of 810 Kb and a scaffold N50 of 54.49 M. More than 99.65% of contigs were successfully anchored onto 24 pseudochromosomes, and 95.7% of BUSCO genes were identified within the genome, demonstrating the high level of completeness in genome assembly. A total of 24,934 protein-coding genes were predicted, of which 99.02% were functionally annotated. The successful assembly of a high-quality genome for B. pterotum provides valuable genetic resources for better understanding its biological characteristics and potentially those of all lanternfish species.Subject terms: Genomics, Ichthyology

Background & Summary

Lanternfishes are the most abundant taxa within marine fish biomass, with 258 documented species spanning 37 genera and 2 families1,2. Most species of lanternfish have photophores distributed along their sides, which enable them to bioluminesce. According to current data, they produce and emit bioluminescence using a luciferin–luciferase reaction3–6. The number and arrangement of photophores are key characteristics used to identify different species of lanternfish7,8. They are widely distributed across all major oceans, predominantly inhabiting the mesopelagic zone, which extends from depths 200 to 4,000 meters9. Most species display diel vertical migration, a behavior that plays a pivotal role in material cycling and energy flux within their marine habitats. Therefore, they represent a crucial component of the biological pump10,11. Lanternfishes prey upon small marine organisms such as krill and copepods, rendering themselves significant food sources for wrasse, hairtail fish, and other higher trophic level organisms, thereby playing an indispensable role in the marine ecological food web12–14. Despite their critical role within the global marine ecosystem, the whole genome of lanternfish species has not been reported to date, severely limiting biological and ecological research, as well as resource conservation efforts for these taxa.

The lanternfish species Benthosema pterotum, belonging to the family Myctophidae within the order Myctophiformes, is one of the largest catches globally among lanternfish species1,15. It is also one of the few lanternfish species independently recorded in the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations database, with its yield continuing to increase annually15. Widely distributed in the Indo-Pacific region, B. pterotum stands out as one of the dominant species along the Chinese coast10,11. In the Red Sea, research using ROV deployments, a hull-mounted 38 kHz echo sounder, and net sampling revealed that the B. pterotum performs a normal diel vertical migration from depths of 500 to 750 meters during the day to the epipelagic zone (upper 200 meters) at night11,16. This finding indicates that the species exhibits unique diel vertical movement behaviors. Additionally, as a cosmopolitan species, the classification of B. pterotum remains contentious15,16. For example, the lanternfish Benthosema panamense was initially considered a subspecies of B. pterotum, restricted to the western coast of Central America. However, differences in the position of photophores behind the eyes and the number of anal fin rays have led to its current recognition as a distinct and valid species17. Furthermore, Zahuranec et al. reported the potential existence of cryptic species within B. pterotum18. They collected six specimens from the Arabian Sea in the Indian Ocean and two from the Sea of Japan. Phylogenetic trees constructed using three mitochondrial genes (16S rRNA, 12S rRNA, and Cyt b) indicated that these samples clustered into two distinct clades. Unfortunately, due to limited sample size and insufficient genetic information, further confirmation of cryptic species in B. pterotum remains unresolved. Currently, research on lanternfish genetics mainly focuses on mitochondrial genes9,18, a few nuclear genes19, and ultraconserved elements20. Although there is existing genomic data for Benthosema glaciale (GCA_900323375.1), the genome was assembled using only second-generation sequencing with a coverage of only 19.0X21, which is insufficient to support more in-depth genomic research.

Therefore, a high-quality genome of B. pterotum provides valuable genomic information for resolving taxonomic disputes and unraveling the genetic mechanisms underlying its unique biological characteristics. Additionally, it can serve as a foundation for subsequent research on resource conservation and rational exploitation. In this study, we report the first high-quality chromosome-level genome of B. pterotum using short-read sequencing, PacBio and Hi-C sequencing technologies. The genome size of B. pterotum is 1,272.53 Mb with scaffold N50 of 54.49 Mb. The successful assembly of a high-quality genome provides valuable genetic resources for further elucidating the biological characteristics of B. pterotum and even all lanternfish species.

Methods

Sample collection

The sequencing samples were obtained through trawling during the fishery resources survey in the East China Sea in May 2019. The samples were collected at coordinates 121.23°E and 27.08°N, with a water depth of 58 meters. Muscle tissues were collected for short-read sequencing, PacBio, and Hi-C sequencing, while eye, muscle, liver, gonad, and skin tissues were collected for RNA sequencing. These tissues were promptly snap-frozen with liquid nitrogen and then stored in a refrigerator at −80 °C for preservation. This work was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Xiamen University. All experiments were performed according to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in China.

Genome sequencing

DNA from muscle tissues was extracted through the TIANamp Genomic DNAKit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). For short-read sequencing, the paired-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing strategy was used on BGISEQ-500 platform (Shenzhen, China), yielding 134.40 Gb of data, with a Q20 rate of 98.39% and a Q30 rate of 94.41% (Table 1). For long-read sequencing, a PacBio SMRTbell library with an average insert size of 60 kb was constructed from genomic DNA using the Pacific Biosciences SMRTbell template prep kit 2.0. The library was sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA), yielding 119.42 Gb (~107X) of data with an average read length of 15,557 bp and an N50 length of 25,412 bp for the subread sequences (Table 1). For Hi-C sequencing, muscle tissues were used for cell cross-linking with Formaldehyde. Subsequently, Hi-C library was constructed according to the standard protocol and sequenced on the BGISEQ-500 platform, yielding 125.37 Gb (Table 1). Additionally, RNA from eye, muscle, liver, gonad, and skin tissues were extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Five RNA-seq libraries were constructed and sequenced on BGISEQ-500 platform. Each RNA libraries yielded an average 6 Gb reads for auxiliary annotation purposes (Table 1).

Genome size estimation and assembly

The raw data underwent filtration using SOAPnuke (v1.5.2)22 with the following parameters: -M 2 -l 10 -q 0.1 -n 0.05 -Q 2 -G 2. This process targeted low-quality reads, adapter sequences, and reads containing excessive Ns, and 123.81 Gb (~111.54X) clean data were retained. The possibility of sample contamination was ruled out by comparing the filtered data to the NT database using BLAST (v2.13.0)23. Subsequently, a genome survey was performed through K-mer analysis using the software Jellyfish (v1.1.10)24 with parameter of ‘-m 17’ and GenomeScope (v2.0)25 with parameter of ‘k = 17’. The results showed that the genome of B. pterotum was 1.11 Gb in size and 2.06% heterozygosity, exhibiting distinct heterozygous peaks (Fig. 1), which suggests that the genome of B. pterotum is highly heterozygous.

K-mer distribution of the B. pterotum with a k-mer size of 17.

The clean reads from PacBio SMRTbell library were assembled using Canu (v1.7) pipeline26 with the parameter: minReadLength = 1,000 minOverlapLength = 500. An initial assembly was generated with total size of 2,350.46 Mb, almost two folds of the estimated genome size. So HaploMerger2 software27 was used to trim the redundancy in the assembly caused by heterozygosity, with the following parameters: identity = 75, redundantFilter = 85, minLen = 2,500 and minCoverage = 80. Subsequently, polishing was performed using Pilon software28 based on short reads. Lastly, the quality control of data from Hi-C library was performed using HiC-Pro (v.2.8.0)29. Approximately 99.65% of the contigs and scaffolds could be anchored to 24 pseudochromosomes (chr) according to Juicer (v1.5)30 and 3D de novo assembly (3d-dna, v.170123)31 pipeline (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 1). In summary, we obtained a chromosome-level highly heterozygous genome with a genome size of 1,272.53 Mb, a contig N50 of 810 Kb, and a scaffold N50 of 54.49 Mb (Table 2).

Repeat annotation

We identified repetitive sequences within the B. pterotum genome through a combination of de novo and homology annotation methods. For the de novo approach, we scanned the genome using RepeatModeler (v1.0.8) and LTR_FINDER (v1.0.6) piepline to identify repetitive sequences, which were subsequently classified using RepeatMasker (v4.0.6)32,33. For the homology search, we utilized RepeatMasker and RepeatProteinMask to retrieve repetitive sequences and classify them based on the Repbase library (v21.01)32,34. A total of 763.85 Mb of repetitive sequences were identified, constituting 60.03% of the genome (Table 3). Among these, DNA transposons were predominant, comprising 506.37 Mb and representing 39.79% of the genome (Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 3). LTR elements followed, totaling 147.21 Mb and accounting for 11.57% of the total genome length (Supplementary Table 2). In summary, the B. pterotum genome exhibits a highly heterozygous and repetitive complex structure, with its genome size likely correlated with the abundant content of transposable elements (TEs).

Additionally, we annotated a total of 7,966 tRNAs, 6,027 rRNAs, 957 snRNAs, and 340 miRNAs in the B. pterotum genome through comparison with known non-coding RNA (ncRNA) libraries (Supplementary Table 3).

Gene prediction and function annotation

We predicted the structure of protein-coding genes in B. pterotum using de novo, homology search and transcriptome-based methods. AUGUSTUS (v3.1) work flow was employed for de novo prediction35. For homology annotation, amino acid sequences from Ensembl and NCBI database were retrieved for Clupea harengus (GCA_900700415.2), Danio rerio (GCA_000002035.4), Oncorhynchus mykiss (GCA_013265735.3), Oryzias latipes (GCA_002234675.1), Salmo salar (GCA_905237065.2), Salmo trutta (GCA_901001165.1) and Takifugu rubripes (GCA_901000725.2). These protein sequences were then aligned to the genome of B. pterotum using GeneWise to identify homologs36. For transcriptome-based prediction, we utilized HISAT2 (v2.1) to align the transcriptome data from five tissues to the genome, and gene information was subsequently predicted using StringTie37. The integration of three types of evidences was facilitated using Glean software with default settings38. In total, 24,934 protein-coding genes were predicted in the B. pterotum genome with an average length of 20,175 bp and an average coding region (CDS) length of 1,675 bp (Table 4).

Data Records

The B. pterotum genome project has been deposited in the NCBI database under BioProject accession PRJNA1097602. The genome assembly data have been deposited at GenBank under the WGS accession GCA_039105355.139. The DNA and RNA sequencing data were submitted to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under the SRA IDs: SRP50049240. The genomic assembly and annotation results of B. pterotum, including gene structure and functional prediction, have been deposited at the figshare database41.

Technical Validation

The accuracy and completeness of the assembly quality of the B. pterotum genome were assessed through a range of methods. First, using BWA (v0.7.12)42 for alignment, the clean short reads compared to the B. pterotum genome showed a mapping rate of 97.27% and a coverage rate of 95.26% (Supplementary Table 4). These findings suggest a high level of completeness in the genome assembly. Furthermore, the average sequencing depth and GC content of the bases were computed using a 10 kb sliding window without repetition. The results revealed that the GC_Depth distribution was concentrated, indicating no significant GC bias in sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 1). The consensus quality value (QV), representing per-base consensus accuracy, was estimated using Merqury (v1.3)43, resulting in a QV of 30.23 and completeness of 75.99%. Lastly, the integrity of the B. pterotum genome was evaluated using the actinopterygii_odb10 dataset with the Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO v5.4.3) method44. The results revealed that 95.70% of BUSCO genes were successfully detected in the genome assembly (Table 6).

To ensure the integrity of the annotated gene set, we compared the predicted gene set with multiple protein databases, revealing that 99.02% of genes were annotated in at least one database (Table 4). Furthermore, we conducted a comparative analysis of length distribution, including genes CDS, exons, introns, and exon numbers between B. pterotum and four fish species (D. rerio, O. latipes, C. harengus, O. mykiss) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Our findings demonstrate a high level of consistency in gene distribution features among these species. Additionally, we subjected the gene sets to BUSCO evaluation using the actinopterygii_odb10 database. The results demonstrated that 89.2% of BUSCO (v5.4.3)44 genes could be completely annotated (Table 6), indicating a high integrity of the gene sets.
