Stochastic Two-temperature Nonequilibrium Ising model
Debraj Dutta * , Ritwick Sarkar, Urna Basu

Abstract
We investigate the nonequilibrium stationary state (NESS) of the two-dimensional Ising model under a stochastic dichotomous modulation of temperature, which alternates between T c ± δ T_{c}\pm\delta around the critical temperature T c T_{c} at a rate γ \gamma . Both magnetization and energy exhibit non-monotonic dependence on γ \gamma , explained by a renewal approach in the slow-switching limit, while for small δ \delta dynamical response theory quantitatively captures the γ \gamma -dependence of the observables. In the fast-switching regime, the NESS appears Boltzmann-like with a γ \gamma -dependent effective temperature. However, a finite energy current flowing through the system from hot to cold reservoir confirms the intrinsic nonequilibrium nature of the dynamics.



I
Introduction

The Ising model has served as a cornerstone of statistical physics for nearly a century
Brush 1967
;
Budrikis 2024
. Originally introduced to understand critical phenomena
Ising 1925
, it has been extensively studied using various dynamical rules
Glauber 1963
;
Kawasaki 1966a
;
Kawasaki 1966b
that preserve detailed balance with respect to the equilibrium Boltzmann distribution. Despite its apparent simplicity, the Ising model exhibits a wide range of nontrivial phenomena, including phase transitions, domain coarsening, phase separation, and giant fluctuations. Owing to this rich behavior, its influence extends far beyond statistical physics, with applications in diverse fields such as biology, sociology, and economics
Hopfield 1982
;
Castellano et al. 2009
;
Bornholdt 2001
.

The Ising model also provides a natural framework for exploring out-of-equilibrium behaviour. Over the years, several nonequilibrium versions of the model have been explored in different contexts. A commonly studied setup involves coupling different regions of the system to thermal baths at different temperatures. This naturally drives currents and leads to nontrivial steady-state profiles of magnetization and energy. Such arrangements have been investigated in both one
Zia and Schmittmann 2007
;
Rácz and Zia 1994
;
Mazilu and Williams 2009
;
Lecomte et al. 2005
;
Borchers et al. 2014
;
Lavrentovich and Zia 2010
;
Lavrentovich 2012
and two dimensions
Blote et al. 1990
. Generalisations involving spatially varying couplings or temperature fields have also been investigated
Garrido and Marro 1987
;
Blöte et al. 1991
. Another direction focuses on models that break detailed balance at the microscopic level—typically by introducing spin-flip rates that depend on two or more temperatures. These systems have been examined using superstatistics
Cheraghalizadeh et al. 2021
, and comparisons across different update schemes (Metropolis, Glauber, Swendsen-Wang) suggesting that certain signatures of equilibrium criticality may persist even far from equilibrium
Tamayo et al. 1994
. In related studies, the interplay between finite and infinite temperature baths has been shown to induce interesting steady-state features, such as nonmonotonic variation of surface tension
Hurtado et al. 2004
. More recently, time-dependent temperature protocols have attracted attention, where the Ising system is alternately coupled to thermal baths at different temperatures. These studies range from exploring calorimetric response in mean-field models
Beyen et al. 2024
to exploring applications such as protein sequence inference
Dietler et al. 2025
. However the nonequilibrium stationary behaviour of the Ising model under such stochastic temporal driving remains mostly unexplored.

In this paper we investigate a scenario where the two-dimensional Ising model is subject to a stochastic dichotomous temperature. In particular, we consider the case where the temperature intermittently switches between two values
T_{c}\pm\delta
, with a constant rate
\gamma
. We characterize the resulting NESS by measuring the mean and fluctuations of magnetization and energy. Surprisingly, for large
\delta
, the average magnetization and energy both show a non-monotonic behaviour as
\gamma
is increased. We show that this non-monotonicity originates from the difference in the typical relaxation times of the equilibrium Ising model below and above the critical temperature
T_{c}.
On the other hand for small
\delta
, we characterise the NESS using dynamical response formalism and show that the second-order response coefficient changes its sign as
\gamma
is increased. Numerical evidence further suggests that, in the large
\gamma
regime, the stationary weight of the spin configurations admits a Boltzmann-like form with a
\gamma
and
\delta
dependent effective temperature
T_{\text{eff}}<T_{c}
. However the dynamics remains nonequilibrium, which is manifest from a persistent flow of energy current through the system.

The paper is organised as follows. In the next section we define the model and present a brief summary of our main results. Section
III
focuses on the behaviour of magnetization and energy in the stationary state. The effective temperature picture in the large
\gamma
regime is discussed in Sec.
IV
. We conclude with some general remarks in Sec.
V

II
Model and Results

We consider a periodic square lattice of size
N=L\times L
where each site
i
is associated with a classical spin
s_{i}=\pm 1
. The spins interact via the nearest neighbour Ising Hamiltonian, where the energy of a configuration
C=\{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots s_{N}\}
is given by,

\displaystyle H(C)=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_{i}s_{j}.

(1)

The system is alternately coupled to two thermal reservoirs at temperatures
T_{c}-\delta
and
T_{c}+\delta
, and the switching between them is governed by a dichotomous stochastic process with constant rate
\gamma
[see Fig.
1
]. The configuration of the system evolves via a Glauber-like local detail balanced single spin-flip dynamics where a randomly chosen spin flips with a rate,

\displaystyle w_{\sigma}(\Delta E)=\frac{\psi_{0}}{2}\Big[(1+\sigma)e^{-\beta_{+}\Delta E/2}+(1-\sigma)e^{-\beta_{-}\Delta E/2}\Big],

(2)

where
\Delta E
is the change in the energy due to the proposed spin flip and
\beta_{\sigma}\equiv 1/T_{\sigma}=1/(T_{c}+\sigma\delta)
denotes the inverse temperature with
\sigma=\pm 1
. The intermittent change in the temperature is incorporated via the dichotomous process
\sigma\to-\sigma
with rate
\gamma
.
Note that,
\psi_{0}
is taken to be a constant, independent of temperature, and characterises the inverse of the typical time-scale of spin flips in the system.

For
\delta=0
the system is equivalent to the ordinary Ising model at a constant temperature
T_{c}
. In that case, for any finite size
L
, the system reaches an equilibrium state with average magnetization
M_{c}
and average energy
E_{c}
, that are independent of
\gamma
. In the thermodynamic limit,
L\to\infty
, the aforementioned equilibrium state becomes critical. For
\delta>0
, the system is expected to reach a nonequilibrium stationary state (NESS) the properties of which depends explicitly on
\gamma
and
\delta
. Clearly, this NESS remains invariant under the transformation
\delta\to-\delta
. Our goal in this work is to characterize this NESS. In particular, we focus on the behaviour of magnetization
M
per site and energy
E
per bond, defined as,

\displaystyle M=\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\right|,\quad E=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_{i}s_{j}.

(3)

Typical time evolutions of
M(t)
and
E(t)
are shown in Fig.
1
. Using numerical simulations, we measure the mean and variance of these observables in the NESS, and find that these quantities exhibit certain unusual features, including non-monotonic behaviour as a function of
\gamma
for a finite sized system. We analytically characterise the behaviour in the limiting scenarios of small and large temperature switching rate
\gamma
. Before going into the details, we provide a brief summary of our main results.

•

For large
\delta\lesssim T_{c}
, both average magnetization
\langle M\rangle
and average energy
\langle E\rangle
show non-monotonic behaviour with
\gamma
— while
\langle M\rangle_{\gamma}
first decreases and then increases,
\langle E(\gamma)\rangle
first shows an increase, eventually decreasing as
\gamma
is increased [see Figs.
2
(a) and
4
(a)]. For small
\delta
, the non-monotonicity disappears, where
\langle M\rangle_{\gamma}

\big(\langle E\rangle_{\gamma}\big)
monotonically increases (decreases) as a function of
\gamma
. Moreover, the variance of magnetization
\sigma_{M}^{2}
shows a highly non-trivial behaviour with
\gamma
, whereas the variance of energy
\sigma_{E}^{2}
decreases monotonically with
\gamma
[see Figs.
2
(b) and
4
(b)].

•

In the small
\gamma
regime the typical duration between two successive temperature switching events is much larger compared to the typical relaxation time of the Ising model at a given temperature. Using this time-scale separation, we obtain the behaviour of
\langle M\rangle_{\gamma}
and
\langle E\rangle_{\gamma}
in the small
\gamma
regime. This also explains the emergence of the non-monotonicity for large
\delta\lesssim T_{c}
[see Fig.
6
].

•

Investigating the behaviour of
\langle M\rangle
and
\langle E\rangle
as a functions of
\delta
, we find that for small
\delta
,

\displaystyle\langle M\rangle_{\delta}-M_{c}=\chi_{2}^{M}(\gamma)~\delta^{2}+O(\delta^{4}),\quad\text{and}\quad\langle E\rangle_{\delta}-E_{c}=\chi_{2}^{E}(\gamma)~\delta^{2}+O(\delta^{4}).

(4)

where
M_{c}
and
E_{c}
denote the equilibrium values of average magnetization and energy, respectively, at
T=T_{c}
. Using dynamical response theory, we investigate the
\gamma
dependence of the second order susceptibilities
\chi_{2}^{M}
and
\chi_{2}^{E}
[see Fig.
7
]. Interestingly, we find that both
\chi_{2}^{M}
and
\chi_{2}^{E}
change sign as
\gamma
is increased beyond certain threshold values.

•

We show that, in the
\gamma\to\infty
limit, the spin-flip rate
w(\Delta E)
can be expressed as that of an equilibrium Ising model with an effective temperature

\displaystyle T_{\text{eff}}=T_{c}\Big(1-\frac{\delta^{2}}{T_{c}^{2}}\Big).

(5)

In fact, we find that for large
\gamma\gg\psi_{0}
, an effective equilibrium picture emerges, with a
\gamma
and
\delta
dependent effective temperature [see Fig.
8
]. However, the inherent nonequilibrium nature of the process is apparent from a non zero energy current flowing through the system [see Fig.
10
].

In the next section, we study the behavior of the NESS in detail.

III
Characterization of the NESS

To characterise the stationary state of this stochastic two-temperature nonequilibrium Ising model, we first focus on the behaviour of mean and variance of magnetisation and energy. For such a nonequilibrium setting it is generally hard to compute these quantities analytically. Hence we take recourse to Monte-Carlo simulations to explore the behaviour of these quantities for a finite system in the
\delta-\gamma
plane. For the sake of completeness, we briefly describe the details of the simulation.

We initialize the system in a random configuration, where each spin
s_{i}
is chosen to be
\pm 1
with equal probability
1/2
. Additionally, we initialize
\sigma=\pm 1
with equal probability
1/2
, which corresponds to a temperature
T_{\sigma}=T_{c}+\sigma\delta
. The temperature remains constant for a time
\tau
which is drawn from an exponential distribution
P(\tau)=\gamma e^{-\gamma\tau}
, during which the spins evolve via Glauber dynamics—a randomly chosen spin flips with the rate
w(\Delta E)
defined in Eq. (
2
). After time
\tau
is elapsed,
\sigma\to-\sigma
resulting in a temperature switch. The temperature now remains constant at
T_{-\sigma}
for a time drawn from
P(\tau)
. This dynamics eventually leads to an NESS where we measure the relevant observables.

Let us first investigate the behaviour of magnetization and energy as functions of
\gamma
. Figure
2
(a) shows a plot of
\langle M\rangle
versus
\gamma
for different values of
\delta
. Evidently, for
\delta=0
, the magnetization remains constant at
\langle M\rangle=M_{c}
, where
M_{c}
is the average value of magnetization for the equilibrium
L\times L
Ising model at temperature
T_{c}
. For
\delta>0
, average magnetization shows a non-trivial dependence on
\gamma
—for small values of
\gamma
,
\langle M\rangle<M_{c}
, while for large
\gamma
,
\langle M\rangle>M_{c}
. In fact, it appears that for some intermediate
\gamma=\gamma^{\star}_{M}
,
\langle M\rangle=M_{c}
irrespective of
\delta
, which corresponds to the crossing point of the different curves in Fig.
2
(a). Remarkably, for large values of
\delta
, i.e., as the system gets further away from equilibrium, the dependence of
\langle M\rangle
on
\gamma
becomes non-monotonic in the
\gamma<\gamma^{\star}_{M}
regime. However, for
\gamma>\gamma^{\star}_{M}
, the magnetization monotonically increases for all values of
\delta
indicating an overall increase in the spin alignment. Unusual signatures are also exhibited by the variance of the magnetization,
\sigma_{M}^{2}=\langle M^{2}\rangle-\langle M\rangle^{2}
, which shows a peak near
\gamma=\gamma^{\star}_{M}
that becomes sharper with increasing
\delta
[see Fig.
2
(b)].

We also explore the behaviour of mean and variance of the energy per bond as functions of
\gamma
. Figure

—similar to magnetization, the curves corresponding to different values of

\langle E\rangle

III.1
Small
\gamma
Regime

To understand the behaviour of the NESS in the samll
\gamma
regime, it is useful to consider a renewal approach. Since the time evolution of the system between two consecutive temperature switches follows ordinary Ising dynamics at a constant temperature, we can write a last renewal equation,

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}(C,t|C_{0})

\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma t}\sum_{\sigma_{0}}\rho(\sigma_{0})\mathbb{P}_{T_{\sigma_{0}}}(C,t|C_{0})+\gamma\sum_{\sigma,C^{\prime}}\rho(\sigma)\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}~e^{-\gamma t^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{T_{\sigma}}(C,t^{\prime}|C^{\prime})P_{\gamma}(C^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}|C_{0}),

(6)

where,
P_{\gamma}(C,t|C_{0})
denotes the probability that the system is in spin configuration
C
at time
t
starting from
C_{0}
. This equation can be understood by considering contributions from trajectories with no temperature switch, indicated by the first term and atleast one temperature switch, indicated by the second term. In the second term,
t^{\prime}
denotes the time elapsed since the last temperature switch to the value
T_{\sigma}
,
\mathbb{P}_{T}(C,t^{\prime}|C^{\prime})
denotes the probability that ordinary Ising model at constant temperature
T
is in configuration
C
at time
t^{\prime}
starting from
C^{\prime}
. Moreover,
P_{\gamma}(C^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}|C_{0})
denotes the probability that the system has configuration
C^{\prime}
at the time of last temperature switch,
t-t^{\prime}
, starting from
C_{0}
.
Here we have assumed that at
t=0
, the system can be at temperature
T_{\pm}
with equal probability
1/2
, i.e.,
\sigma_{0}=\pm 1
with
\rho(\sigma_{0})=1/2
.

Plots of (a) average magnetization
\langle M\rangle
and (b) average energy
\langle E\rangle
as functions of

. The horizontal dashed lines mark the corresponding equilibrium values

and
E_{c}

In general, it is hard to solve the integral Eq. (
6
) to obtain
P_{\gamma}(C,t)
explicitly. However, in the small
\gamma
limit, this equation can be used to obtain the stationary value of the average of any observable
\langle O\rangle
as discussed below. For ordinary 2D Ising model with Glauber dynamics, the relaxation time
\tau_{r}\sim\psi_{0}^{-1}L^{2}
. For small values of
\gamma\ll\tau_{r}^{-1}
, the typical duration between consecutive temperature flips
\gamma^{-1}
is much larger compared to the typical relaxation time of ordinary Ising model. Consequently, the system approaches close to equilibrium at temperatures
T_{+}
and
T_{-}
between successive temperature switches. In this regime,
\mathbb{P}_{T_{\sigma}}(C,t^{\prime}|C^{\prime})
becomes independent of the initial spin configuration
C^{\prime}
and Eq. (
6
) reduces to,

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}(C,t)\simeq\frac{1}{2}e^{-\gamma t}\Big[\mathbb{P}_{T_{+}}(C,t)+\mathbb{P}_{T_{+}}(C,t)\Big]+\frac{\gamma}{2}\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}~e^{-\gamma t^{\prime}}\Big[\mathbb{P}_{T_{+}}(C,t^{\prime})+\mathbb{P}_{T_{-}}(C,t^{\prime})\Big],

(7)

The expectation values of any single-time observable
O(C)
, like magnetization and energy, in the stationary state, can thus be expressed as,

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{C}O(C)P_{\gamma}(C,t)=\frac{\gamma}{2}\intop_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}~e^{-\gamma t^{\prime}}\Big[\langle O(t^{\prime})\rangle_{\rm eq}^{+}+\langle O(t^{\prime})\rangle_{\rm eq}^{-}\Big].

(8)

The above expression allows us to compute the stationary average
\langle O\rangle_{\gamma}
of any observable using its equilibrium relaxation behaviour
\langle O(t)\rangle_{\rm eq}^{\pm}
for ordinary Ising model at temperature
T_{\pm}
. For any finite-sized system at temperature
T_{\sigma}
, the observables typically show an exponential relaxation to a equilibrium value
\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}^{\sigma}
. Thus we can assume,

\displaystyle\langle O(t)\rangle_{\rm eq}^{\sigma}=\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}^{\sigma}+b_{\sigma}e^{-t/\tau_{\sigma}},

(9)

where
\tau_{\sigma}
denotes the relaxation time-scale at temperature
T_{\sigma}
, and
b_{\sigma}
is an empirical constant. Substituting Eq. (
9
) in Eq. (
8
) and performing the integration over
t^{\prime}
, we get,

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}^{+}+\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}^{-}\Big]+\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big[\frac{b_{+}\tau_{+}}{1+\gamma\tau_{+}}+\frac{b_{-}\tau_{-}}{1+\gamma\tau_{-}}\Big].

(10)

Clearly, in the limit
\gamma\to 0
, we have
\langle O\rangle_{\gamma}=\Big[\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}^{+}+\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}^{-}\Big]/2
, as expected. Equation (
10
) allows us to determine the small
\gamma
behaviour of
\langle M\rangle
and
\langle E\rangle
in the NESS, using the corresponding
\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}^{\sigma}
,
b_{\sigma}
and
\tau_{\sigma}
values extracted from the equilibrium relaxation dynamics at
T_{\sigma}
. This can be done by looking at,

\displaystyle g_{O}^{\sigma}(t)=\langle O(t)\rangle_{\rm eq}^{\sigma}-\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}^{\sigma}

(11)

for ordinary Ising model at
T_{\sigma}
and extracting the parameters
\tau_{\sigma}
and
b_{\sigma}
, which depend on the observable, by fitting it to the form given in Eq. (
9
). We estimate these parameters for a given
\delta
, for both energy and magnetization, from their equilibrium relaxation dynamics and compute their
\gamma
-dependence. Figure
6
compares these predicted behaviours with
\langle M\rangle_{\gamma}
and
\langle E\rangle_{\gamma}
obtained from numerical simulations, which show an excellent match. This validates our prediction Eq. (
10
) and the assumptions leading to it.

This analysis also explains the non-monotonic behaviour of
\langle M\rangle_{\gamma}
and
\langle E\rangle_{\gamma}
. In the small
\gamma
regime, at each temperature switching event
\sigma\to-\sigma
, the expectation value
\langle O(t)\rangle_{\gamma}
starts from
\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}^{\sigma}
, eventually reaching
\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}^{-\sigma}
before the next switch [see Fig.
1
]. Thus,
b_{+}
and
b_{-}
must have opposite signs. For example, for magnetisation
b_{+}>0
and
b_{-}<0
, while for energy
b_{+}<0
and
b_{-}>0
. It is then easy to show that, for
\tau_{+}\neq\tau_{-}
, Eq. (
10
) has an extremum at some intermediate value of
\gamma
. Since, the relaxation in the ferromagnetic phase is much slower compared to that in the paramagntic phase,
\tau_{-}>\tau_{+}
, with their difference becoming larger as
\delta
is increased. This difference in the relaxation time-scales leads to the non-monotonic variation of
\langle M\rangle_{\gamma}
and
\langle E\rangle_{\gamma}
in the small
\gamma
regime, as shown in Fig.
6
.

III.2
Small
\delta
regime

In this section, we study the behaviour of average energy and average magnetization for small values of
\delta
, i.e., when
\delta/T_{c}\ll 1
. The
\delta=0
limit corresponds to the case of the equilibrium Ising model at temperature
T_{c}
. Therefore, the small-
\delta
behaviour of the system can be explored by applying the dynamical response theory
Maes and van Wieren 2006
;
Basu et al. 2015
;
Maes 2020a
;
Maes 2020b
around the equilibrium limit, treating
\delta
as the perturbation parameter. Since for
\delta=0
, the temperature flip dynamics is decoupled from the spin dynamics, the unperturbed system corresponds to two independent equilibrium processes, namely, the Ising dynamics at temperature
T_{c}
and a dichotomous process,
\sigma\to-\sigma
with rate
\gamma
. For non-zero values of
\delta
the coupled dynamics reaches an NESS which remains invariant under the transformation
\delta\to-\delta
and the expectation value of any observable
\langle O\rangle_{\delta}
must be an even function of
\delta
[see Fig.
5
]. Therefore, for small
\delta
, we can write,

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle_{\delta}=\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}+\delta^{2}\chi_{2}+\mathscr{O}(\delta^{4}),

(12)

where
\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}
denotes the equilibrium value of the observable and
\chi_{2}
represents the second order response coefficient. In the following, we compute this coefficient using the non-linear dynamical response theory, where frenetic contributions play a significant role
Basu et al. 2015
;
Maes 2020a
.
For a generic observable
O(\omega)
, which depends on the trajectory
\omega
of the system over a time-interval
[0,t]
, the second order response coefficient is given by,

\displaystyle\chi_{2}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle O(\omega)\left[-D^{\prime\prime}(\omega)+\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime\prime}+(D^{\prime})^{2}(\omega)+\frac{1}{4}(S^{\prime})^{2}(\omega)-D^{\prime}(\omega)\,S^{\prime}(\omega)\right]\right\rangle_{\rm eq},

(13)

where
S^{\prime}(\omega)
and
D^{\prime}(\omega)
denote the first-order excesses in entropy and frenesy, generated due to the perturbation. The corresponding second-order contributions are indicated by
S^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
and
D^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
. For Markov jump-processes,
S(\omega)
and
D(\omega)
along the trajectory
\omega
, have the general form
Maes 2020b
,

\displaystyle S(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\ln{\frac{k(x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell}},x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell+1}})}{k(x_{t^{\prime}_{{\ell+1}}},x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell}})}}

(14)

\displaystyle D(\omega)

\displaystyle=\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}~\big[\lambda(x_{t^{\prime}})-\lambda_{0}(x_{t^{\prime}})\big]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\ln{\Big[k(x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell}},x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell+1}})k(x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell+1}},x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell}})\Big]}

(15)

where
k(x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell}},x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell+1}})
is the transition rate for the
\ell
-th jump from the configuration
x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell}}
to
x_{t^{\prime}_{\ell+1}}
,
\lambda(x)=\sum_{y\neq x}k(x,y)
denotes the escape rate from the state
x
and
\sum_{\ell}
denotes a sum over all transitions
\ell
along the trajectory
\omega
.

Since we are interested in the response coefficient of single-time observable like
M(t)
and
E(t)
, it is useful to consider a time-antisymmetric observable of the form
O(\omega,t)=O(t)-O(0)
in Eq. (
13
). In this case, the the correlations
\langle(D^{\prime})^{2}O\rangle_{\rm eq}
,
\langle(S^{\prime})^{2}O\rangle_{\rm eq}
, and
\langle D^{\prime\prime}O\rangle_{\rm eq}

vanish due to the time-reversal invariant nature of equilibrium dynamics. Consequently, to second order in
\delta
, we have,

\displaystyle\langle O(t)\rangle_{\delta}-\langle O\rangle_{\rm eq}=\delta^{2}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\langle S^{\prime\prime}O(t)\rangle_{\rm eq}-\langle S^{\prime}D^{\prime}O(t)\rangle_{\rm eq}\Big].

(16)

Clearly, the second order response coefficient is a sum of two terms—the first one,
\langle S^{\prime\prime}O(t)\rangle_{\rm eq}
is purely entropic while the second one,
\langle S^{\prime}D^{\prime}O(t)\rangle_{\rm eq}
involves the excess dynamical activity.

To proceed further, we note that for our two-temperature Ising model, the trajectory is characterised by
\omega=\{C(t^{\prime}),\sigma(t^{\prime});0<s<t\}
, where
C(t^{\prime})
denotes the spin configuration and
\sigma(t^{\prime})
indicates the instantaneous temperature
T(t^{\prime})=T_{c}+\sigma(t^{\prime})\delta
at time
t^{\prime}
. The transition rates are given by,

\displaystyle k\big(\{C,\sigma\},\{C^{\prime},\sigma\}\big)

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}~\exp\Big(-\frac{\beta_{\sigma}}{2}\big[H(C^{\prime})-H(C)\big]\Big),~~

for spin-flips,

(17)

\displaystyle k\big(\{C,\sigma\},\{C,\sigma^{\prime}\}\big)

\displaystyle=\gamma~~

for temperature switches,

(18)

where
H
is the Hamiltonian of the Ising system given in Eq. (
1
) and
\beta_{\sigma}=1/(T_{c}+\sigma\delta)
. Since we need to compute excesses of
S(\omega)
and
D(\omega)
with respect to
\delta
, it suffices to consider only the
\delta
dependent terms,

\displaystyle S(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\sigma}\beta_{\sigma}\Delta{E}^{\sigma}(t)+{\rm const.},

(19)

\displaystyle D(\omega)

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}~\sum_{k=1}^{L^{2}}e^{-\beta_{\sigma(t^{\prime})}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})/2}+{\rm const}.

(20)

Here,
\Delta E^{\sigma}(t)
in Eq. (
19
) is the total energy gained by the system from the reservoir at temperature
T_{\sigma}
during the interval
[0,t]
. Moroever,
\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})
in Eq. (
20
) denotes the energy cost associated with flip of the
k
-th spin in the configuration
\{C(t^{\prime}),\sigma(t^{\prime})\}
.

The excess entropy can be computed by taking derivative of Eq. (
19
) with respect to
\delta
and then taking
\delta=0
.

\displaystyle S^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathscr{J}_{E}(t)}{T_{c}^{2}}=\frac{1}{T^{2}_{c}}\Big[\Delta E^{+}(t)-\Delta E^{-}(t)\Big],

(21)

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{U(t)}{T_{c}^{3}}=-\frac{2}{T_{c}^{3}}\Big[\Delta E^{+}(t)+\Delta E^{-}(t)\Big],

(22)

where,
\mathscr{J}_{E}(t)
denotes the time-integrated energy current flowing from the hot reservoir at temperature
T_{+}
to the cold reservoir at temperature
T_{-}
. Moreover,
U(t)
denotes the net energy gain of the system from both the reservoirs during the time interval
[0,t]
.

The excess frenesy can be similarly obtained from Eq. (
20
) and is given by,

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi_{0}}{2T_{c}^{2}}\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}~\sigma(t^{\prime})\sum_{k=1}^{L^{2}}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})\,e^{-\beta_{c}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})/2}.

(23)

Substituting the above equations in Eq. (
16
), we explicitly obtain the second-order response coefficient, which can be conveniently expressed as,

\displaystyle\chi_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\chi^{(1)}_{2}(t)+\chi^{(2)}_{2}(t),

(24)

where,

\displaystyle\chi_{2}^{(1)}(t)=-\frac{1}{T_{c}^{3}}\big\langle U(t)O(t)\big\rangle_{\rm eq},

(25)

denotes the purely entropic contribution, and

\displaystyle\chi_{2}^{(2)}(t)=-\frac{\psi_{0}}{2T_{c}^{4}}\Big\langle O(t)\mathscr{J}_{E}(t)\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}~\sigma(t^{\prime})\sum_{k=1}^{L^{2}}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})\,e^{-\beta_{c}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})/2}\Big\rangle_{\rm eq}

(26)

involves the contribution from frenesy, explicitly depending on the dynamics. In the limit
t\to\infty
,
\chi_{2}(t)
reaches the stationary response coefficient.

It is noteworthy that the net energy gained

can be interpreted as the perturbation to the original Hamilonian (

Evidently the entropic term
\chi_{2}^{(1)}

IV
Large
\gamma
regime and effective temperature description

In this section, we focus on the behavior of the NESS in the large
\gamma
regime, i.e., when
\gamma\gg\psi_{0}
. In this regime, the typical interval between two successive temperature switching events is much smaller than the typical time-scale of spin-flips. Hence, in the limit of infinite temperature switching rate, we can replace
\sigma
by
\langle\sigma\rangle=0
in Eq. (
2
) to write the effective spin-flip rate as,

\displaystyle w_{\rm eff}(\Delta E)

\displaystyle\simeq\psi_{0}\cosh\Big[\frac{\delta\beta_{\infty}\Delta E}{2T_{c}}\Big]\exp\Big[-\frac{\beta_{\infty}}{2}\Delta E\Big],~~~\text{with},~~~\beta_{\infty}^{-1}\equiv T_{\infty}=T_{c}\Big(1-\frac{\delta^{2}}{T_{c}^{2}}\Big).

(32)

Clearly,
w_{\rm eff}(\Delta E)/w_{\rm eff}(-\Delta E)=\exp(-\beta_{\infty}\Delta E)
and Eq. (
32
) corresponds to a detailed balanced Glauber dynamics at an effective temperature
T_{\infty}
. Thus, in the limiting scenario of
\gamma\to\infty
, we expect the spin configuration weight to have the Boltzmann form
P(C)\sim e^{-\beta_{\infty}H(C)}
. Physically, this corresponds to a scenario where the temprature switches happens so fast that the Ising spins are not able to distinguish between the two reservoirs.

Nonequilibrium stationary states can sometimes be partially characterised by an effective temperature picture
Cugliandolo 2011
. For example, it has been shown that an Ising model in contact with two or more heat reservoirs can sometimes be described using an equilibrium-like picture with an effective temperature
Garrido et al. 1987
;
Tamayo et al. 1994
. An effective temperature picture also describes a generalised FDT for nonequilibrium critical dynamics of Ising model
Mayer et al. 2003
.

Such an effective temperature description is not expected to hold when the time-scales of temperature switch and spin-flip become comparable. This is also apparent from Fig.

The existence of a unique effective temperature suggests that the weight of the spin configuration

in the NESS admits a Boltzmann-like form
P(C)\sim\exp\big[-H(C)/T_{\text{eff}}\big]
in the large
\gamma

Katz et al. 1983
;

For fixed
\gamma

can be estimated from the ratio of number of successful spin-flips to attempted spin-flips with energy change

\displaystyle T_{\rm eff}

From numerical simulations, we extract

for
the allowed values of

and
\pm 8

obtained from the stationary values of the observables using Eq. (
33

\gamma\gg\psi_{0}
, all the curves converge, indicating that the dynamics effectively does satisfy detailed balance. Thus it appears that the spin degrees, when considered independently of the bath degrees of freedom, exhibit an effective equilibrium-like behaviour in this large switching rate regime.

However, the full system consisting of the spin degrees of freedom and the two reservoirs is inherently nonequilibrium in nature. To illustrate this, we measure dynamical observables like the energy current flowing through the system, which should vanish in equilibrium. The average instantaneous current flowing to the system from the reservoir at temperature
T_{\sigma}
in the stationary state is given by,

\displaystyle j_{E}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\langle\Delta E^{\sigma}(t)\rangle,

(35)

where

, introduced in Eqs. (

)-(

[0,t]
. Then the average instantaneous energy current flowing through the system from the hot reservoir to the cold reservoir is given by,

\displaystyle j_{E}

\displaystyle=j_{E}^{+}-j_{E}^{-}

(36)

Figure

\gamma

\delta

increases with increasing
\gamma
and eventually saturates. The average current also increases with increasing
\delta
as shown in Fig.

\gamma
regime. This is a signature of the inherent nonequilibrium nature of the stochcastic two-temperature Ising model dynamics.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have studied the two-dimensional Ising model subjected to a stochastic dichotomous temperature. The temperature alternates between
T_{c}\pm\delta
with a rate
\gamma
which leads the system to a nonequilibrium stationary state characterised by unusual behaviour of observables like magnetization and energy. For example, the average magnetization and average energy change non-monotonically as functions of flip-rate
\gamma
. We have characterised this NESS through two complementary approaches. In the small
\gamma
regime, we use a renewal approach to explain the origin of the non-monotonic variation of the obseravbles. On the other hand, for small
\delta
, we employ the dynamical response theory to understand the
\gamma
dependence of the observables.

We find that the NESS admits an effective temperature description for fast temperature switches, i.e., for large values of
\gamma
. We explicitly compute this effective temperature in the
\gamma\to\infty
limit. Using extensive numerical simulation, we show that the effective temperature description remains valid for a finite range of
\gamma
and breaks only in the small
\gamma
regime.
We further show that such an effective temperature description does not imply that the system reaches an equilibrium state—the inherent nonequilibrium nature of the dynamics leads to a non-zero energy current flowing through the system from the hot reservoir to the cold reservoir.

It would be interesting to investigate how the behaviour of the NESS changes as the system size is varied. In particular, how the threshold values of the flip rate and the effective temperatures depend on the system size. Another natural open question is whether similar effects are observed in other systems that belong to the Ising universality class. In a more general sense, our study can be thought of as a special case of a driving protocol where a control parameter of a system is randomly switched between two distinct values according to a dichotomous process. In particular it would be interesting to study the behaviour of systems with underlying nonequilibrium dynamics like the Katz-Lebowitz-Spohn model
Katz et al. 1983
or the driven lattice gas
Schmittmann and Zia 1995
.

Appendix A
Response coefficient in the limiting case of infinite switching rate

In this appendix, we derive the expressions for second-order response coefficient
\chi_{2}(t)
in the limiting scenario of infinite switching rate, i.e., for
\gamma\to\infty
. We start by considering the
\gamma
dependent part of the response coefficient quoted in Eq. (
26
),

\displaystyle\chi_{2}^{(2)}(t)=-\frac{\psi_{0}}{2T_{c}^{4}}\Big\langle O(t)\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\sigma(t^{\prime\prime})j_{E}(t^{\prime\prime})\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}~\sigma(t^{\prime})\sum_{k=1}^{L^{2}}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})\,e^{-\beta_{c}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})/2}\Big\rangle_{\rm eq}.

(A.1)

In writing the above expression we have used,

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}_{E}(t)=\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\sigma(t^{\prime\prime})\mathscr{I}(t^{\prime\prime}),

(A.2)

in Eq. (
26
), where
\mathscr{I}(t)
denotes the instantaneous energy current flowing into the system. In the unperturbed dynamics, i.e., for
\delta=0
, the dichotomous process
\sigma\to-\sigma
and the Ising spin-flip dynamics at temperature
T_{c}
become statistically independent, allowing the averages over
\sigma
and over the spin configurations to be factorised. Consequently, Eq. (
A.1
) now simplifies to

\displaystyle\chi_{2}^{(2)}(t)=-\frac{\psi_{0}}{2T_{c}^{4}}\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}~\big\langle\sigma(t^{\prime})\sigma(t^{\prime\prime})\big\rangle_{\rm eq}\Big\langle O(t)j_{E}(t^{\prime\prime})\sum_{k=1}^{L^{2}}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})\,e^{-\beta_{c}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})/2}\Big\rangle_{\rm eq}.

(A.3)

For large
\gamma
,
\sigma
effectively emulates a white noise with autocorrelation,

\displaystyle\langle\sigma(t^{\prime})\sigma(t^{\prime\prime})\rangle\to\frac{1}{\gamma}\delta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime}).

(A.4)

Substituting Eq. (
A.4
) in Eq. (
A.3
) and subsequently performing the integral over
t^{\prime\prime}
, yields

\displaystyle\chi_{2}^{(2)}(t)=-\frac{\psi_{0}}{2\gamma T_{c}^{4}}\intop_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\Big\langle O(t)j_{E}(t^{\prime})\sum_{k=1}^{L^{2}}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})\,e^{-\beta_{c}\Delta\varepsilon_{k}(t^{\prime})/2}\Big\rangle_{\rm eq}.

(A.5)

It is thus evident from the above expresion that in the limit
\gamma\to\infty
,
\chi_{2}^{(2)}
vanishes like
1/\gamma
.
