HybridProver: Augmenting Theorem Proving with LLM-Driven Proof Synthesis and Refinement
Jilin Hu, Jianyu Zhang, Yongwang Zhao, Talia Ringer

Abstract
Formal methods is pivotal for verifying the reliability of critical systems through rigorous mathematical proofs. However, its adoption is hindered by labor-intensive manual proofs and the expertise required to use theorem provers. Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) offer new opportunities for automated theorem proving. Two promising approaches are generating tactics step by step and generating a whole proof directly with an LLM. However, existing work makes no attempt to combine the two approaches. In this work, we introduce HybridProver , a dual-model proof synthesis framework that combines tactic-based generation and whole-proof synthesis to harness the benefits of both approaches. HybridProver generates whole proof candidates for evaluation directly, then extracts proof sketches from those candidates. It then uses a tactic-based generation model that integrates automated tools to complete the sketches via stepwise refinement. We implement HybridProver for the Isabelle theorem prover and fine-tune LLMs on our optimized Isabelle datasets. Evaluation on the miniF2F dataset illustrates HybridProver ’s effectiveness. We achieve a 59.4% success rate on miniF2F, where the previous SOTA is 56.1%. Our ablation studies show that this SOTA result is attributable to combining whole-proof and tactic-based generation. Additionally, we show how the dataset quality, training parameters, and sampling diversity affect the final result during automated theorem proving with LLMs. All of our code, datasets, and LLMs are open source.

Introduction

The field of formal methods plays a pivotal role in ensuring the reliability of critical systems across mathematics, computer science, and engineering
Bjørner and Havelund 2014
. It rigorously verifies system correctness through formal specification and math-based proof. However, the labor-intensive nature of manual proof construction and the expertise required for the effective use of interactive theorem provers (ITPs) present significant barriers to widespread adoption. To address this challenge, automated theorem proving has emerged as a paradigm to streamline formal verification
Barrett et al. 2011
;
De Moura and Bjørner 2008
;
Groom 2018
;
Schulz 2002
;
Weidenbach et al. 1996
, but its large proof-search space and limited adaptability to diverse proof scenarios have constrained its practicality.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have opened new frontiers for automated theorem proving. Generating proofs is similar to code generation, where LLMs have shown outstanding capabilities
Chen et al. 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2024
. Moreover, formal proofs are written in formal languages that can be verified by ITPs, e.g., Isabelle/HOL
Nipkow et al. 2002
, Rocq
Sozeau et al. 2025
, Lean
Moura and Ullrich 2021
, and HOL Light
Harrison 1996
. Thus, the dangers of LLM hallucinations can be significantly reduced in this process.

In this work, we introduce
HybridProver
, an LLM-driven automated theorem-proving framework.
Before
HybridProver
, two predominant approaches had emerged
for LLM-driven automated theorem-proving:
(1) whole-proof synthesis that produces complete formal proof attempts
Xin et al. 2024
;
First et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024b
, and (2) tactic-based generation that guides LLMs stepwise
Yang et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2024b
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Polu and Sutskever 2020
.
Whole-proof synthesis excels at high-level planning, but is error-prone at the level of details; tactic-based generation excels at the details, but may struggle with higher-level planning.

HybridProver
is the first system that we are aware of to combine these two approaches. In doing so,
HybridProver
gets the best of both worlds—with state-of-the-art (SOTA) results to show for it.

HybridProver
achieves this by employing a dual-model architecture: First,
HybridProver
uses a whole-proof synthesis model to generate candidate proofs based on the user-provided theorem statement. If any of these candidates are correct whole proofs, then
HybridProver
is done. Otherwise,
HybridProver
mechanically abstracts details from those candidate proofs to build proof sketches—abstract proof plans with subgoals in which the detailed tactics are omitted.

HybridProver
uses the ITP to check the proof sketch to ensure its correctness. Finally,
HybridProver
uses a tactic-based generation model to refine the proof sketch with tactic details and produce a correct whole proof, checked by the ITP.

In this way,
HybridProver
uses the notion of a proof sketch to bridge the gap between the two approaches.
As a consequence, the core ideas of
HybridProver
can be used to compose
any
whole-proof synthesis model with
any
tactic-based generation model for the same target ITP.
This is in contrast with prior work generating
proof sketches and filling in the details
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Mikuła et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
,
which does not offer a way to repurpose existing whole-proof synthesis models to generate proof sketches.
Nonetheless,
HybridProver
can also take advantage of the benefits of these prior works by integrating hammers
Blanchette et al. 2016
, existing tools that automate the proving of simple theorems using symbolic techniques.

While the ideas for
HybridProver
can be implemented for many ITPs, we focus on Isabelle/HOL
Nipkow et al. 2002
,
using Sledgehammer
Paulson 2012
as the hammer. We choose Isabelle both because of the unique challenges that it presents for LLM-based proof synthesis,
as well as the unique advantages that it provides for
curating an optimized dataset (see Section
2
).
We evaluate
HybridProver
on miniF2F
Zheng et al. 2022
, a popular mathematical theorem proving dataset. We improve the SOTA
on miniF2F from 56.1% to 59.4%. We show that the combination of whole-proof synthesis and tactic-based generation improves the proof performance. With the integration of hammers,
HybridProver
performs even better.
Additional ablation studies show how the dataset quality, training parameters, and sampling diversity affect the final proof result. We open-source our optimized Isabelle datasets and fine-tuned LLMs on Huggingface. We also provide the source code for
HybridProver
.

Contributions:

(1)

We propose
HybridProver
, an LLM-driven automated theorem proving framework that combines whole-proof synthesis and tactic-based generation, using proof sketches as a bridge between the two approaches to get the best of both worlds.

(2)

fine-tuning LLMs on our custom-optimized Isabelle datasets.

HybridProver

(3)

HybridProver
and how the data quality, training parameters, and sampling diversity affect LLM’s ability in theorem proving.

Background: why Isabelle?

We implement
HybridProver
to automate formal proof in the Isabelle ITP. ITPs like Isabelle
Nipkow et al. 2002
, Rocq
Sozeau et al. 2025
, and Lean
Moura and Ullrich 2021
are specialized systems that make it possible to formally prove software systems and mathematical theorems.
They rigorously check these formal proofs using
small, trustworthy logic checkers
Nipkow et al. 2002
;
Pierce et al. 2010
.
Unfortunately, using these ITPs requires substantial work
Klein et al. 2009
;
Gu et al. 2016
;
Leroy et al. 2016
. Complementary tools called automated theorem provers (ATPs) take a different approach of full automation (CVC4
Barrett et al. 2011
, Z3
De Moura and Bjørner 2008
, Vampire
Groom 2018
, E
Schulz 2002
, SPASS
Weidenbach et al. 1996
). However, the applicability of ATPs is limited, as they typically use just first-order logic, and they face search-space explosion. Hence, ITPs are useful for expressiveness and scalability. Automated theorem proving with LLMs promises to make ITPs more accessible without compromising on this expressiveness and scalability.

Isabelle is a popular ITP that
widely applied to the formalization of mathematical proofs and formal verification
Hales et al. 2015
;
Paulson 2021
;
Klein et al. 2009
;
Zhao et al. 2017
, including proving the correctness of computer hardware or software and proving properties of programming languages and protocols. The most popular instance of Isabelle is Isabelle/HOL, which provides an expressive higher-order logic for complex systems. We use Isabelle/HOL and write it as Isabelle in the rest of this paper for simplicity.
Our choice of Isabelle as a target ITP addresses its unique challenges while also taking advantage of its unique benefits.

Challenges of targeting Isabelle: broad use cases

Targeting Isabelle presents unique challenges compared to other ITPs like Lean.
More work uses LLMs for Lean and gets better results on miniF2F than for Isabelle, because Lean is mainly used for mathematics, with the largest math library (mathlib
Moura and Ullrich 2021
), and miniF2F is a mathematical benchmark suite. In contrast, Isabelle users tend to focus more on system verification. In system verification, proofs of correctness vary significantly for different implementations, and for different notions of correctness specific to those systems.

Isabelle has the largest formal proof dataset of high quality with 200K more theorems, compared to Lean’s 140K and Rocq’s 71K
Drori et al. 2025
;
Reichel et al. 2023
. This dataset is both an asset and a burden. To its credit, this dataset covers many fields, including mathematical proof, software verification, and programming languages.
However, proofs can vary significantly by field, and it can be challenging for LLMs to learn a common proof pattern to solve proofs across these different fields.

Advantages of targeting Isabelle: two proof styles

There are two styles of proof in Isabelle.
This may appear to complicate things even further, but it ends up being our greatest asset and a major benefit to targeting Isabelle. The two styles are (1) apply-style proofs
Wenzel 2025
and (2) Isar proofs
Wenzel and Team 2025
. Both styles employ deductive reasoning. Apply-style proofs emphasize incremental tactic application, where a tactic is a primitive proof step that refines the current proof state to an updated state. Apply-style proofs use tactics step-by-step and reach the conclusion from the initial assumptions. In contrast, Isar proofs are written in Isar, a high-level proof language that provides a structured, human-readable framework for writing formal proofs akin to mathematical proofs. Isar has been designed to make proofs easy to read and to better align with people’s ways of thinking and proving
Wenzel and Team 2025
.

Note that these two styles of proofs can be combined, with Isar sketching the final proof while apply-style tactics discharge the remaining subgoals to fill in the sketch.
For example, for the proof in Listing

sorry

We use this insight to improve our automation, splitting our dataset across these two styles to train our two models, using sketches as bridges between styles.
This combination of styles is a fundamental idea that our work uses to establish a new SOTA.

Step 1:
The input of

whole-proof synthesis model

Step 2:
The whole-proof synthesis model will generate
n

proof filter
. Specifically, we write proof patterns in the proof filter to make sure that the Isabelle proof is syntactically correct. If the response is not in a JSON format that we require or does not begin with keywords in Isabelle, it must be wrong. Then the syntax-correct proofs will be evaluated by the
checker
, i.e., the Isabelle theorem prover.
The initial candidate proof in
Fig.

1
is syntax-correct but fails to pass the checker because the tactic
by presburger
for the first subgoal is wrong.
The synthesis and evaluation of
n
proofs are both fast due to the concurrency support in
HybridProver
. We set
n
to be 128 in our experiments. If one of the proofs passes the checker, we provide the final whole proof with the theorem to the user.

Step 3:
If none of the
n
proofs are correct, we use a
sketch builder
to reconstruct those syntax-correct proofs that pass the proof filter. The detailed proof steps will be hidden while only a proof sketch is left. Unlike previous work, we directly generate proof sketches mechanically from candidate proofs.
Specifically, we substitute all proof tactics for each subgoal with the keyword
sorry
in Isabelle. The checker will assume the subgoal is correct when it meets
sorry
. An example of a proof sketch is shown in
Fig.

1
. Then we evaluate these proof sketches in Isabelle
checker
and filter out incorrect sketches, e.g., using a nonexistent proof method in the beginning. Most of the sketches are correct without detailed proof steps. Again, this process is very fast due to concurrency support.

Step 4:
Subsequently, we use a
tactic-based generation model
to find proof steps for each subgoal in one proof sketch. It is composed of a fine-tuned LLM for proof steps and Sledgehammer with heuristics in Isabelle. It also generates
n
possible responses for each subgoal. This model focuses on generating proofs for small subgoals, while the whole-proof synthesis model focuses on generating whole proofs with premises.
Premises are auxiliary lemmas for proving the final theorem. Existing work selects premises in two ways.
The first one is to build a premise library and retrieve related premises based on the theorem input
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Yang et al. 2023
. Another way is to train the model to memorize premises and learn to use them when similar theorems appear
First et al. 2023
. We choose the second method and require our whole-proof synthesis model to do premise selection.

Step 5:
Then we use a
proof refinement builder
to refine
sorry
with generated proof steps and send the whole proof to Isabelle. The output of the refinement builder is the same as the final whole proof. As shown in
Fig.

1
, all instances of the keyword
sorry
in the proof sketch are replaced with specific proof steps, i.e.,
by simp
. If the candidate proof does not pass the
checker
, the refinement builder will refine the proof sketch with other proof steps from the tactic-based generation model.
Finally, we provide the proof result to the user. The algorithm of
HybridProver

3.2
Dataset optimization

We extract Isabelle datasets using the PISA environment
Jiang et al. 2021
.
Then, we purify the data because the original data will largely mislead the LLM. Specifically, we purify extra blanks and tabs. Then we delete all the comments and rewrite some symbols, e.g., the open and close brackets. Finally, we transform all proofs into a structured output format {
Isabelle_proof: proof code
} for models to learn.

After data purification, we split the dataset into two datasets depending on the proof style: an Isar proof
Wenzel and Team 2025
dataset to fine-tune the whole-proof synthesis model and an apply-style proof
Wenzel 2025
dataset to fine-tune the LLM in the tactic-based generation model. We further analyze the number of proof steps for each theorem and filter out some theorems. Specifically, we find that an apply-style proof generally has 1-5 proof steps and an Isar proof has 11-30 proof steps. We filter out theorems that exceed 5 steps in apply-style proofs to keep the tactic-based generation model focused on finding concise proofs of details. We also filter out theorems that are more than 50 or fewer than 5 proof steps, because they are either too simple or too complex for the model to learn proof sketches.

Experiments

We first introduce the setup for
HybridProver
(
Section

4.1
), including the dataset, the interactive environment with Isabelle, and the experimental setup. Then, we show the main results to demonstrate the effectiveness of
HybridProver
in establishing a new SOTA (
Section

4.2
). Finally, we describe additional ablation studies (
Section

4.3
) to answer four questions, with the following takeaways:

1)

The more similar an LLM-generated proof pattern is to the training data, the higher the quality of the proof.

2)

The quality of the data, the learning rate, and the number of epochs do affect the results, while the number of parameters in LoRA has a trivial impact.

3)

Sampling diversity impacts the result. The sampling temperature should be moderate, and a higher sampling rate brings a higher success rate.

4)

Providing premises, few-shot examples, and labels has a trivial impact on improving the miniF2F success rate.

4.1
Setup

Dataset

We extract and optimize Isabelle libraries (
Section

3.2
) as the training datasets, with the help of PISA
Jiang et al. 2021
. These datasets are used to train the whole-proof synthesis model and the tactic-based generation model separately. PISA includes the Isabelle/HOL library and the Archive of Formal Proofs (AFP) with version 2021-10-22. The former is more foundational, with around 70K theorems. The latter is a proof library contributed by most Isabelle researchers, with around 170K theorems. It diverges from mathematical problems to formal verification of software and hardware. We follow the same configurations of prior works
Jiang et al. 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Jiang et al. 2023
and split AFP theorems into 95%/1%/4% for the training/validation/test sets. The Isabelle/HOL library is classified in the training dataset because it might be used by the AFP theorems.

We choose miniF2F
Zheng et al. 2022
as our evaluation dataset because it is used in many research studies
Yang et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2024b
. It is an open-source collection containing 488 high school math competition problems. These problems are divided into two groups: 244 for validation and 244 for final testing. All problems have been translated into 4 proof assistant languages: Lean, HOL light, Metamath, and Isabelle. Since we do not use the validation set during training, we use only the test set for our experiments.

Interactive environment

We interact with Isabelle using Isabelle-client
Koepke et al. 2022
. It is a Python client library that strictly follows the Isabelle system manual
Wenzel 2021
. We use one server, one client, and multiple sessions for concurrency.
watchdog_timeout
is set to 30s for evaluation. Evaluation in Isabelle is time-consuming even for a small sampling rate and a small dataset. It costs 300 CPU hours to evaluate 244 theorems in miniF2F with a 32 sampling rate for one LLM. Thus, we do ablation studies with a relatively low sampling rate and small timeout. To set up Sledgehammer, we follow the default Isabelle2021 configuration
Jiang et al. 2021
, where the Sledgehammer timeout limit is 30s, with the on-machine five default ATPs (E, SPASS, Vampire, Z3, and CVC4).
We also complement Sledgehammer with 11 heuristics for proofs:
[’by auto’, ’by simp’, ’by blast’, ’by fastforce’, ’by force’, ’by eval’, ’by presburger’, ’by sos’, ’by arith’, ’by linarith’, ’by (auto simp: field_simps)’]
.

Experimental setup

In initial small-model experiments, we generated proofs using more than 15 different open-source models. In the end, we chose Qwen-Coder-14B as our base model for further training because it had the highest proof success rate. For LLM fine-tuning, we use 8 Nvidia A40 GPUs. Fine-tuning our model with the whole optimized Isabelle dataset and 3 epochs takes 200 GPU hours. The cost of ablation studies for other LLMs and configurations is not included. We use LoRA
Hu et al. 2022
to train around 1% of all parameters with a
lora_rank
32 and all target layers open for adjustment.
In the first 10% of steps, the learning rate warms up linearly from 0 to the maximum value
e^{-5}
. Then it decays to 0 following a cosine schedule. The context length for the training data is 2048 tokens, and the total batch size is 64.
For evaluation, we use a 128-core Intel CPU with 256GB RAM while setting the concurrent processes to 64. The whole evaluation process cost nearly 10,000 CPU hours.

4.3
Additional ablation studies

Is the proof pattern similarity equal to the proof quality?

During the fine-tuning stage, we try to feed the model enough theorem-proof pairs. The LLM learns to generate proof patterns similar to the training data. But is the proof pattern similarity equal to the quality? Thus, we not only test the success rate of the miniF2F dataset, but also record all responses from the sampling during the whole evaluation. We make a comparison between them and find that generally, the higher the total correct response is, the higher the success rate for all theorems is. It demonstrates that proof pattern similarity is strongly relevant to the proof quality, reflecting the improvement from training.

How do data quality and training parameters affect fine-tuning effects?

We use the original Qwen-Coder-7B to build our baseline for computing source restrictions. The original model has difficulty following our instructions because it is only a generically pretrained model. So we prompt the model and additionally enforce a structured output format for the original model’s responses. We try fine-tuning on variants of our optimized Isabelle dataset with different hyperparameter combinations, i.e., learning rate, lora_rank, and number of epochs. We generate responses with the same prompt, as shown in Listing
.
We find that the quality of data, learning rate, and number of epochs do affect the results.
These heuristics can be takeaways to researchers using LLMs for similar tasks. If we fine-tune the model with original data, the model’s output is deeply polluted by useless tokens and tabs. The LLM will also output a lot of meaningless tokens in the responses. After data purification described in
Section

3.2
, the model’s pass rate on miniF2F improves at least 10%. For epochs, we find that 3 epochs is the best compared to 1, 6, and 10 epochs. More training will only lead to overfitting. Besides, the type of theorems in the training data varies from math to software and systems, while miniF2F is only about math. For learning rate, we tried
e^{-4},5e^{-5},e^{-5},5e^{-6},e^{-6}
, and
5e^{-7}
with a warm-up and cosine schedule. We find that the best is
e^{-5}
. Moreover, we tried different lora ranks, including 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256. Here, a rank 32 modifies around 0.92% of the full parameters in an LLM. However, we find that the lora_rank is almost irrelevant to the results.

Does sampling diversity impact the results?

After we have a fine-tuned model, we can choose different sampling parameters for response generation, e.g., sampling temperature
T
and sampling rate. For proof generation tasks, the model is asked to be stable enough to generate formatted and syntactically correct responses. Thus,
T
is typically set to 0.2-0.5. However, existing works sample multiple responses for one theorem, and a higher
T
can improve the possibility of success. For example, LISA uses a
T
= 1.0 and Thor uses
T
= 1.2. Sampling rate @32 means that if any response in the 32 generated ones for one theorem passes the evaluation, the theorem is true. In our work, we performed an ablation study on a fine-tuned Qwen-Coder-7B model, where
T
ranges from 0.3 to 1.2 and sampling rate from @32 to @128. The results are shown in

T

T

Does providing premises, few-shot examples, and labels help with proving?

Most theorems in the Isabelle library require pre-defined premises to prove. Thus, we provide theorems with premises in the training data as input, where the output is the proof. We ask the model to learn how to find relevant premises from the library and how to build proofs based on these premises. Then we record all the error messages and notice that
undefined fact

The reason is that most miniF2F theorems require no premises. 6
Conclusion and future work

We introduced
HybridProver
, an LLM-driven proof synthesis framework for automated theorem proving.
HybridProver
’s power comes from a novel combination of whole-proof synthesis and tactic-based generation with the help of hammers, using stepwise refinement of proof sketches as the bridge between the two approaches.
Our implementation of
HybridProver
for the Isabelle theorem prover achieves a SOTA result of 59.4% accuracy on the widely-used miniF2F dataset, surpassing the previous SOTA of 56.4% for Isabelle.

One limitation of
HybridProver
is that it uses a simple proof search method during the proof sketch refinement. When the number of subgoals inside the proof sketches grows, the refinement and evaluation process will be very slow.
In the future, we plan to integrate proof search into our tactic-based generation model to make it more efficient. We also plan to use extended pretraining and reinforcement learning to further improve the quality of LLM-generated proofs. Finally, we plan to develop an integrated tool that is easy to use.

Technical Appendices and Supplementary Material

A.1
Hammers

We integrate hammers in our framework to augment theorem proving.
Blanchette et al.
Blanchette et al. 2016
define hammers as methods that “automate reasoning over large libraries developed with formal proof assistants.” Most ITPs have their own hammers, e.g., Coqhammer in Rocq and Sledgehammer in Isabelle. Sledgehammer in Isabelle is the most powerful one
Paulson 2012
. Providing the input theorem, Sledgehammer first finds relevant premises using machine learning and a traditional filtering algorithm
Paulson 2012
. Then it translates the input theorem together with a heuristic selection of hundreds of premises into goals that external ATPs can recognize.
Then, ATPs solve these problems and return the results to Sledgehammer. Sledgehammer thus gets the useful premises and finally reconstructs proofs for the Isabelle trusted kernel to check.
Every ITP has a trusted kernel, a minimal and rigorously verified core that checks manually constructed proofs following mathematical logic rules
Nipkow et al. 2002
;
Pierce et al. 2010
.
Since the kernel checks these proofs, it is unnecessary for the user to worry about the correctness of the generated proof.

Sledgehammer makes writing proofs convenient. Isabelle users only need to write the assumptions and the final goal that they want to prove. Then users can call Sledgehammer directly to finish the proof. Sledgehammer is good at generating short proofs
Paulson 2012
. Thus, if users also provide a proof sketch with some subgoals introducing how the proof might be done, Sledgehammer will find proofs for each subgoal and construct the whole proof more easily. It is worth mentioning that Sledgehammer does not do induction very well. LLMs might help with theorems that require induction proofs.
