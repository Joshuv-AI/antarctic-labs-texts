Impact on families and caregivers when a loved one is in the surgical intensive care unit (SICU)
Authors: Andrew Maneval, Galinos Barmparas
Published: 2025

Introduction

The surgical intensive care unit (SICU) is a special part of the hospital where very sick or badly hurt patients go to get strong medical care. Doctors and nurses watch these patients closely all the time and use special machines and treatments to help them survive and heal. When someone you love is in the SICU, it can be a very hard and stressful time. It’s not just tough for the patient, it can also be very hard on families and caregivers. That’s why it’s important to understand how this experience can affect them too.

Physical and financial strain

Think about how someone’s life can change when a loved one is in the SICU after a serious accident. Their normal day-to-day routine gets turned upside down, and they start planning everything around hospital visiting hours. Emotional and psychological impact

Having a loved one in the SICU is not just hard on the body, it can be very hard on the mind, too. The SICU can be a scary place. There are lots of machines, beeping sounds, and medical words that are hard to understand. It’s very common for family members and caregivers to feel nervous and worried about their loved one’s health. These feelings often don’t go away, even after the hospital stay is over. Some people may feel sad, anxious, or even have trouble sleeping because of what they went through. Support groups are available to help patients and families talk to others who went through the same thing, so they don’t feel alone.

Navigating communication and decision-making

When a loved one, especially a parent or grandparent, is admitted to the SICU, some family members may be asked to help the medical team make decisions that decide the care for their loved one based on what their loved one would have wanted. This can be difficult for anyone to face, even when the whole family is involved and supportive. Some families choose one person as the point of contact for the medical team. Although this may help to avoid confusion, it can cause stress and anxiety for the person who was chosen to help make decisions. Resources exist both in the hospital and online to help families cope with these pressures and understand the reasoning behind different medical treatments.

After the SICU: recovery and the long haul

When a patient is doing well enough to leave the SICU, it’s a good sign they are starting to get better. But it’s important to remember that their healing journey is not over. After leaving the SICU, the patient and their family often have to do more on their own. This might include giving medicine, helping with bathing, eating, or caring for wounds. Sometimes the patient needs to go to a rehab center for more help before going home, which can add more stress and cost more money. If the patient has a lasting injury or disability, the family may need to make big changes at home, and this can affect their health and happiness too.

A realistic example

Imagine a middle-aged woman whose husband is in the SICU after a car crash. At first, she feels shocked and scared, surrounded by machines and medical words she doesn’t understand. Her whole day is focused on visiting him, checking how he’s doing, and waiting for news. After a week, she feels very tired and stressed, but she doesn’t want to leave in case something happens and she’s gone. She finds comfort in talking to a hospital chaplain and the SICU nurse, who explain what’s going on and help her feel less alone. When her husband gets better and leaves the SICU, she feels very happy, but also worried about how they’ll manage at home. A social worker helps her make a plan and introduces her to a support group, which later becomes a big help to her.

What support should you expect from the hospital

Some hospitals offer amenities and programs specifically designed to support families during a SICU stay. These may include:

Quiet family waiting areas with reclining chairs or sleeping spaces.

Having a loved one in the SICU is very hard, but you don’t have to go through it alone. There are people and resources that can help you. It’s important to ask for support and to take care of yourself, too. When you care for your own health and feelings, it helps you be stronger for your loved one.
