Iridocorneal endothelial syndrome manifesting 4.5 years after toric implantable collamer lens implantation: a case report
Authors: Kunning Wang, Zhihao Zhang, Jiahao Wang, Haorun Zhang, Mengjun Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIridocorneal endothelial syndrome (ICE) is a rare unilateral disorder with subtle early manifestations and is therefore easily overlooked in clinical practice. Reports of ICE occurring after implantable collamer lens (ICL) implantation remain exceedingly rare. In the present case, localized peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) were identified preoperatively, whereas corneal endothelial morphology and endothelial cell density (ECD) were essentially normal. The eye remained clinically stable throughout long-term follow-up until classic features of ICE became apparent 4.5 years later, underscoring the novelty and clinical significance of this case.Case presentationA 19-year-old woman with high myopia underwent bilateral toric implantable collamer lens (TICL) implantation in 2021. Preoperative examination revealed localized PAS and mild superior pupillary displacement in the right eye, while the cornea was clear and ECD was within the normal range. Early and intermediate postoperative follow-up was largely unremarkable. At 4.5 years after surgery, the patient presented with blurred vision in the right eye. Ophthalmic examination demonstrated corneal edema, segmental iris atrophy, progression of PAS, corectopia, and secondary ocular hypertension. In vivo confocal microscopy using the Heidelberg Retina Tomograph III with the Rostock Cornea Module (HRT-RCM) showed disorganized endothelial cell architecture, cellular enlargement, and a marked focal reduction in endothelial cell density. Based on these clinical findings, ICE syndrome of the right eye was diagnosed. The patient subsequently underwent viscocanalostomy, trabeculectomy, and peripheral iridectomy, after which intraocular pressure remained well controlled.Given the patient’s history of extreme myopia, ICL explantation was deferred to avoid marked anisometropia and substantial impairment of visual function, and careful follow-up was continued.ConclusionIn patients with preoperative localized PAS or subtle iris abnormalities, underlying ICE cannot be definitively excluded, even when the cornea is clear and ECD is normal. This case suggests that ICE syndrome may exist in a subclinical stage before ICL implantation, with its characteristic features emerging only later in the disease course. Long-term follow-up after ICL implantation is therefore essential, with particular attention to serial assessment of the corneal endothelium, anterior chamber angle, and iris configuration, to enable early recognition of occult progressive disease.

Background

Implantable collamer lens (ICL) surgery has become an important surgical approach for myopia correction in recent years because of its good visual quality and minimal impact on corneal biomechanics and its safety has been well-proven [1, 2]. The common complications of ICL include subcapsular cataract, increased intraocular pressure, pigment diffusion, pupillary block, and decreased corneal endothelial cell count. Most of these complications occur in the early postoperative period and are often related to factors such as abnormal arch height, mechanical contact between the ICL and intraocular tissues, and inflammatory reactions [3].

Iridocorneal endothelial syndrome (ICE) is a rare unilateral disease characterized by the abnormal proliferation and migration of corneal endothelial cells, which leads to progressive peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS), structural changes in the iris, corneal edema, and secondary glaucoma [4]. The pathogenesis of ICE is not fully understood, but it is currently believed to be related to factors such as endothelial cell phenotypic transformation and viral infection [5]. The clinical progression of ICE is usually chronic and gradual, with no obvious symptoms for a long period until the development of corneal decompensation or glaucoma [6].

No case of ICE has been reported after ICL implantation, and the relationship between the two remains unclear. This study reports a case in which ICE occurred 4.5 years after ICL implantation.

Case presentation

The patient was a female and born in March 2002. She presented to our hospital at the age of 19 years (August 2021) with high myopia. The preoperative subjective refraction results were as follows: right eye uncorrected visual acuity (UDVA) of 0.05, best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 0.6 (− 15.00 DS/−4.00 DC × 180), and left eye UDVA of 0.1, BCVA of 0.6 (− 14.25 DS/−3.25 DC × 175). Intraocular pressure (IOP) was 21 mmHg in the right eye and 22 mmHg in the left eye. Slit-lamp examination revealed slight upward pupillary displacement in the right eye, mild anterior synechiae of the iris in the 9 to 1 o’clock positions, and a small amount of pigmentation on the anterior surface of the lens (Fig. 1A). No obvious abnormalities were observed in the left eye. Ophthalmological examination using a three-dimensional anterior segment analysis system (Pentacam, Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) revealed central corneal thickness (CCT) of 579 μm in the right eye and 576 μm in the left eye; the anterior chamber depth (ACD) was 3.05 mm in the right eye and 3.15 mm in the left eye (Fig. 1B). Ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) revealed adhesion between the iris root and the inner corneal surface at the 9 to 1 o’clock positions in the right eye, with no abnormalities observed in the other angle structures (Fig. 1C). Corneal endothelial microscopy revealed regular morphology of the corneal endothelial cells in both eyes, with endothelial cell counts of 3139.1 cells/mm² in the right eye and 3011.0 cells/mm²in the left eye (Fig. 1D). Following comprehensive evaluation, bilateral TICL (V4c) implantation was performed on August 9, 2021. The implanted lenses were 12.1 mm − 18.00/+2.5/090 in the right eye and 12.1 mm − 17.50/+2.5/085 in the left eye. The surgery was successful without any complications and the patient was scheduled to undergo regular follow-up appointments postoperatively (Table 1).

Four and a half years after TICL implantation (January 2026), the patient returned to the clinic because of blurred vision in the right eye. Ophthalmological examination revealed a right UDVA of 0.4, a left UDVA of 0.6, and a palpable intraocular pressure (IOP) of T + 2 in the right eye and 22 mmHg in the left eye. Slit-lamp examination revealed corneal edema, segmental iris atrophy, and significantly enlarged peripheral anterior iris adhesions compared with preoperative levels, with further upward displacement of the pupil (Fig. 2A). Corneal confocal microscopy (HRT-RCM; Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) revealed significantly irregular morphology and an increased number of corneal endothelial cells in the right eye, with a localized endothelial cell count of approximately 436 cells/mm² in the central nasal region. The evaluation also revealed patchy hyporeflective and irregular hyperreflective material that adhered to the endothelial surface (Fig. 2D). On the basis of the progressive enlargement of the anterior iris adhesions, abnormal structure of the iris, corneal edema, and secondary hypertensive manifestations, ICE (internal icterus) was suspected in the right eye. After the completion of the relevant auxiliary examinations, the patient underwent scleral sinus dilation, trabeculectomy, and iridectomy in the right eye. Intraoperatively, 100 µL of aqueous humor was extracted for viral nucleic acid testing and the results were negative for CMV, EBV, HSV-1, HSV-2, and VZV. Postoperatively, the patient’s intraocular pressure remained stable.

Discussion

A review of the patient’s medical history revealed that before TICL implantation, the patient had peripheral anterior synechiae at the 9 to 1 o’clock positions. However, the cornea was transparent, and the morphology and density of the endothelial cells were normal, with no evidence of functional impairment. These findings did not indicate an ICE diagnosis preoperatively. Early and midterm follow-up postoperatively showed stable findings. Four and a half years post-surgery, the patient developed decreased vision in the right eye, corneal edema, an expanded area of anterior synechiae, and a local corneal endothelial cell count that decreased to approximately 436 cells/mm². At this time point, the clinical presentation was consistent with a diagnosis of ICE. Considering the late onset and slow progression, the clinical presentation was more consistent with the natural progression of a chronic disease than with a postoperative subacute or chronic complication. At the one-year follow-up, the endothelial cell count in the right eye decreased from 3139.1 cells/mm² preoperatively to approximately 2400 cells/mm²postoperatively, while no significant decrease was observed in the left eye. The postoperative endothelial reduction after ICL surgery is usually mild and relatively symmetrical in both eyes [7]. The unilateral and significant endothelial cell count reduction noted in the present patient was indicative of ICE.

From the pathogenesis perspective, the core mechanism of ICE is the abnormal phenotypic transformation of endothelial cells [4]. Abnormal endothelial cells can exhibit epithelial-like characteristics; that is, they may possess migration and covering capabilities, forming anterior synechiae at the anterior chamber angle [5]. This process often involves a long latency period. In this case, confocal microscopy revealed irregular morphology, increased volume, and abnormal reflective material attachment to endothelial cells. These findings indicated the formation of an abnormal corneal endothelium. The decrease in the corneal endothelial cell count one year post-surgery may represent the early cellular changes in the disease. Currently, no evidence supports that ICL directly induces the development of ICE. However, given the presence of local structural abnormalities in the present patient, intraocular surgery may lead to changes in the anterior chamber microenvironment. In this case, the decision of whether to explant the ICL requires careful balancing of potential risks and benefits. Given the patient’s history of extreme myopia, immediate ICL removal could induce marked anisometropia and substantially impair quality of life. Therefore, the current management strategy focuses on intraocular pressure control, stabilization of the anterior segment, and close follow-up, while reserving ICL explantation for cases in which it becomes clinically necessary. However, whether ICL removal can effectively delay disease progression remains uncertain.

For differential diagnosis in this case, first, Fuchs’ endothelial dystrophy should be ruled out, which typically progresses symmetrically in both eyes and is accompanied by droplet-like changes in the posterior corneal wall [8]. Second, viral keratoendotheliitis should be ruled out. In this case, there was no history of recurrent inflammatory episodes, and the aqueous humor viral PCR test was negative. Thus, no infectious factor was present.

A major limitation of this case is that, although localized peripheral anterior synechiae and mild pupillary abnormalities were noted preoperatively, slit-lamp examination and preoperative specular microscopy revealed no significant abnormalities. As a result, gonioscopy and confocal microscopy were not performed at the initial consultation, potentially leading to the failure to detect subtle lesions that may already have been present before surgery.

This case report highlights the significant heterogeneity of ICE as a rare clinical disease. Because typical corneal endothelial abnormalities are frequently absent in the early stages, ICE is easily missed or misdiagnosed in clinical practice. In the early stages, some patients present only with localized peripheral anterior iris adhesions or mild changes in the iris [4]. Corneal edema and secondary glaucoma usually appear gradually with disease progression. Therefore, even with a clear cornea and normal endothelial cell density, the presence of localized peripheral anterior iris adhesions or structural abnormalities in the iris do not completely rule out the possibility of underlying ICE.

In conclusion, this case suggests that ICE syndrome may be present in a subclinical stage before ICL implantation, with its characteristic clinical manifestations emerging only during long-term postoperative follow-up. It also highlights the importance of thorough preoperative evaluation and close long-term follow-up in eyes with isolated peripheral anterior synechiae to enable earlier detection of disease progression.
