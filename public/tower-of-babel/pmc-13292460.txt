Patient-centered communication in home-based dementia caregivers: A concept analysis
Authors: Gebrezabher Niguse Hailu, Maria Isabel Da Conceicao Dias Fernandes, Allyne Fortes Vitor, Dandara Nayara Azevedo Dantas, Jonas Sami Albuquerque De Oliveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to clarify and analyze the concept of Patient-Centered Communication (PCC) among home-based dementia caregivers. Although patient centered communication is recognized as a cornerstone of quality dementia care, its defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents remain poorly delineated in the context of home-based caregiving.DesignA concept analysis using the Walker and Avant (2019) eight-step framework was employed to systematically examine the structure and meaning of PCC within home-based dementia care.MethodRelevant peer-reviewed studies published between 2009 and 2025 were identified through an integrative literature review of major databases. Fifteen studies meeting inclusion criteria were analyzed to extract definitions, defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents of patient centered communication.ResultsSeven defining attributes of patient centered communication were identified: cognitive-appropriate communication, active listening, empathy, non-verbal sensitivity, compassion, individualization, and respect for autonomy. Antecedents included progressive cognitive decline, communication difficulties, caregiver knowledge and skills, and the need for emotional expression. Consequences of patient centered communication were enhanced emotional well-being, improved communication effectiveness, reduced agitation and anxiety, increased participation in care, and improved quality of life.ConclusionPatient-centered communication is a multidimensional, relational approach that helps preserve personhood, dignity, and emotional safety for individuals with dementia in home care. Clarifying its key attributes offers a basis for designing communication training, improving caregiver education, and shaping dementia care policies that support compassionate, person-centered practice.

Introduction

As the global prevalence of dementia continues to rise, the demand for high-quality, compassionate, and coordinated home-based care has become increasingly critical (World Health Organization 2021; World Health Organization 2021). Dementia care requires not only clinical competence but also effective communication strategies that acknowledge the individuality, emotions, and lived experiences of persons with dementia (Prince et al., 2015; Williams et al., 2009). In this context, patient-centered communication (PCC) has emerged as a fundamental component of quality care, emphasizing respect for patients’ values, preferences, and needs while promoting shared understanding and trust between care providers, patients, and families (Kwame and Petrucka, 2021; Schmid Mast et al., 2005).

Home-based dementia care is mostly delivered by informal caregivers—most commonly spouses, adult children, or close relatives—assume a pivotal and highly engaged role in daily support (Kim et al., 2024). They often provide round-the-clock assistance despite having little or no formal training.

Despite the centrality of communication in home-based care, the practical and conceptual application of patient centered communication within this setting remains limited and inconsistently interpreted across disciplines (Hoel et al., 2021). As a result, differences in understanding and practice among care providers often contribute to fragmented communication, unmet patient needs, and increased caregiver burden (Piculell et al., 2021).

A comprehensive conceptual understanding of patient centered communication in home-based dementia care is therefore essential to guide effective communication practices, enhance interdisciplinary collaboration, and improve patient and caregiver outcomes. Concept analysis offers a systematic approach to clarifying the defining attributes, antecedents, and consequences of patient centered communication within this specific context (Carpiac-Claver and Levy-Storms, 2007). Accordingly, this paper aims to analyze and refine the concept of patient-centered communication in home-based dementia caregivers, thereby contributing to theory development and improved care delivery for people with dementia.

The literature on communication in dementia care uses both the terms patient-centered communication and person-centered communication. Although these terms share common principles, they have been used in different contexts and are not always considered synonymous. Patient-centered communication remains an established communication framework within healthcare research and practice. (Williams et al., 2009) Given the continued use of patient-centered communication in the literature, the present concept analysis focuses specifically on this concept while acknowledging its conceptual relationship with person-centered communication and person-centered communication may align soundly with in home care setting. Background

According to the Cambridge Dictionary, “communication” is defined as the act of sharing information to increase understanding between people (Walker and Avant, 2019). In healthcare, patient-centered communication (PCC) is a specialized form of communication that extends beyond information exchange by emphasizing patients’ preferences, values, and individual needs (Cambridge University Press 2025). Compared with task-oriented communication, which focuses primarily on the completion of clinical duties (Naughton, 2018 ), patient centered communication highlights empathy, partnership, and shared decision-making (Kim et al., 2024; Kwame and Petrucka, 2021; Schmid Mast et al., 2005). Similarly, process-centered communication prioritizes data collection and clinical efficiency but often limits patient participation in care decisions (Schmid Mast et al., 2005). In contrast, patient centered communication actively engages patients and caregivers through mutual dialogue and collaboration, promoting dignity, trust, and respect for their preferences (Cambridge University Press 2025).

Patient-centered care represents the broader philosophy that places patients and families at the center of all healthcare decisions (Schmid Mast et al., 2005; Tsige et al., 2024), while patient centered communication functions as the specific communicative approach that operationalizes this philosophy through meaningful interaction and shared decision-making. Therapeutic communication, meanwhile, is a core component within PCC, focusing on empathy and supportive dialogue that helps patients cope with stress, build trust, and improve health outcomes (Edgman-Levitan and Schoenbaum, 2021; Schmid Mast et al., 2005).

In dementia care, communication presents unique challenges due to the progressive decline in memory, cognition, and language abilities (Kudubes et al., 2023; Savundranayagam et al., 2020). Standard communication techniques are often inadequate for meeting the emotional and psychological needs of individuals with dementia (Savundranayagam and Lee, 2017; Shaw et al., 2022; Wilson and Litterini, 2021). Although the principles of patient centered communication are widely acknowledged, their application in home-based dementia care remains poorly conceptualized. Therefore, this study seeks to clarify the meaning and defining characteristics of patient centered communication among home-based dementia care task forces. By identifying its attributes, antecedents, and empirical referents, this concept analysis will provide a conceptual foundation for guiding education, research, and practice in home-based dementia care.

Method

Concept analysis

The concept was analyzed using Walker and Avant’s concept analysis framework (2019). This systematic approach consists of eight sequential steps designed to capture the essential meaning of a concept: (1) selecting the concept of interest, (2) identifying the aims or purposes of the analysis, (3) exploring all possible uses of the concept, (4) determining its defining attributes, (5) constructing a model case, (6) identifying borderline, related, contrary, invented, and illegitimate cases, (7) outlining the antecedents and consequences, and (8) defining the empirical referents.

Data sources

A systematic literature review on patient-centered communication was conducted. The following electronic databases were searched for studies published between 2009 and 2025: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and Wiley Online Library. Articles were screened and included if they were published in English, available in full text, and focused on patient-centered communication in dementia care settings. The PRISMA 2020 checklist was applied to ensure completeness, transparency, and rigor throughout the identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion processes.

Data analysis

EndNote 21 was used to manage references and remove duplicates. The titles and abstracts of all retrieved studies were independently screened by two researchers against the predefined inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third researcher. Full-text articles were then reviewed using the same independent screening process. For each study included, data were extracted and analyzed by two researchers to explore the meaning of patient-centered communication and to identify its defining attributes, antecedents, and empirical referents within home-based dementia care task forces.

Results

The initial search yielded 2,340 articles. After removing duplicates, 1,825 articles remained. No additional records were identified from other sources such as books, conference proceedings, or magazines. Use of the concept of patient-centered communication

Patient-centered communication (PCC) has been widely recognized as a core element of high-quality healthcare, particularly in caring for older adults and those with dementia. The concept emphasizes tailoring communication to individual needs, preferences, and values while fostering trust, empathy, and collaborative decision-making between patients, families, and care providers (Kudubes et al., 2023; Kwame and Petrucka, 2021; Lin et al., 2025; Prince et al., 2015; Savundranayagam et al., 2020; Schmid Mast et al., 2005; Williams et al., 2018). In practice, patient centered communication is used to enhance patient engagement, reduce behavioral and psychological symptoms, improve adherence to care plans, and promote emotional well-being (Alsawy et al., 2017; Bender et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2025; MY and Johnson, 2020; Schmid Mast et al., 2005; Williams et al., 2018). Within home-based dementia care, patient centered communication guides caregivers and multidisciplinary task forces in adapting language, tone, pace, and non-verbal cues to align with the cognitive and emotional capacities of individuals with dementia (Bender et al., 2022; Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015; Williams et al., 2018). The concept also informs training programs for formal caregivers, encouraging the use of active listening, empathy, and attentiveness to strengthen the therapeutic relationship and ensure that care is individualized and person-centered (Lin et al., 2025; MY and Johnson, 2020; Savundranayagam et al., 2020).

Identify a model case

In the following case, all defining attributes of patient-centered communication are demonstrated. This scenario is a fictional example developed solely to illustrate how these attributes manifest in the context of home-based dementia care task forces.

Mr. Daniel, a 74-year-old man with moderate dementia, lives at home under the care of a community-based dementia task force composed of a nurse, a home caregiver, and a social worker. One morning, Nurse Helen visits him for his routine assessment. When she arrives, Mr. Daniel appears confused and anxious, repeatedly asking where his late wife is. Instead of correcting him abruptly, Helen gently acknowledges his feelings and says, “You must miss her very much, Mr. Daniel,” demonstrating her genuine concern and understanding of his emotional state (Empathy). While speaking, she maintains steady eye contact, uses a calm tone, and sits at his eye level to convey reassurance and attentiveness (Non-verbal sensitivity).

Understanding his cognitive limitations, she uses simple, clear language and visual prompts to explain what she is about to do (Cognitive-appropriate communication). As he begins to express his frustration, Helen leans forward, nods, and paraphrases his concerns to show she is listening carefully (Active listening). She recalls from his care plan that he enjoys gardening, so she engages him in conversation about his favorite flowers while checking his vital signs (Individualization). When it is time for medication, Helen gives Mr. Daniel two options—taking it before or after breakfast—and praises his cooperation (Respect for autonomy). Observing that he still looks a bit tense, she places her hand gently on his shoulder and reassures him, “You’re doing great today. You’re safe, and I’m here with you” (Compassion). By the end of the visit, Mr. Daniel appears calmer, smiles, and thanks her for taking the time to talk.

Identify borderline, related, contrary cases

Borderline case

Borderline cases contain most of the defining attributes of the concept being examined. In the following example, several key features of patient-centered communication, including empathy, active listening, non-verbal sensitivity, and compassion, are demonstrated. This case is a fictional example created for illustrative purposes only.

Mrs. Ana, an 82-year-old woman with moderate dementia, is cared for at home by a visiting nurse and a home caregiver. One afternoon, Nurse Helena visits to assist with medication management and daily care. When she arrives, Mrs. Ana appears withdrawn and confused. Helena greets her calmly and smiles, sitting beside her to maintain a friendly tone (non-verbal sensitivity). She explains the purpose of her visit using simple, slow speech and listens as Mrs. Ana expresses fear about taking her medications (active listening).

Helena tries to reassure her by saying, “You’re doing well, and I’m here to help you” (compassion), and she responds gently to her emotional expressions (empathy). However, she fails to ask about Mrs. Ana’s preferences regarding the order of care tasks or involve her in decision-making (lack of respect for autonomy). She also neglects to incorporate Mrs. Ana’s personal interests or past experiences to make the conversation more meaningful (missing individualization).

Related case

Related cases are similar to and share certain characteristics with the concept being studied but do not fully represent the concept itself. In the following case, aspects of task-focused communication are described as being related to patient-centered communication, with the main attribute being the ability to provide care and instructions in a polite and clear manner. This case is a fictional example created for illustrative purposes only.

Mr. Carlos, a 78-year-old man with mild dementia, is visited at home by a nurse, Mariana, for routine care. Mariana explains the purpose of her visit and provides instructions about medication administration using simple language. She also answers Mr. Carlos’s questions politely and provides reassurance when he expresses minor concerns.

However, while Mariana is polite and informative, she does not actively explore Mr. Carlos’s emotions or concerns beyond the immediate task, and she does not adjust her approach based on his personal preferences or past experiences. Her communication is primarily task-focused, and she does not engage in behaviors that reflect deep empathy, active listening, non-verbal sensitivity, or compassion.

Contrary case

In the following case, the defining attributes of patient-centered communication among home-based dementia care task forces are completely absent. This case is a fictional example created for illustrative purposes only.

Mrs. Beatriz, an 80-year-old woman with moderate dementia, is visited at home by a nurse, Carla, for her routine care. Carla speaks quickly and gives instructions without checking whether Mrs. Beatriz understands. She ignores Mrs. Beatriz’s attempts to express her feelings, does not acknowledge her emotions, and does not adjust her tone, gestures, or body language (no empathy, active listening, non-verbal sensitivity, or compassion). Carla also disregards Mrs. Beatriz’s preferences and personal history, performing tasks in a rigid, task-focused manner without engaging or involving her in decisions (no individualization, cognitive-appropriate communication, or respect for autonomy).

As a result, Mrs. Beatriz becomes anxious and withdrawn, and the interaction fails to promote trust, comfort, or meaningful engagement. This scenario illustrates the opposite of patient-centered communication, highlighting the absence of all its defining attributes.

Identify antecedents and consequences

Antecedents

Consequences

Walker and Avant (2019) explained that consequences are the events or outcomes that result from the occurrence of a concept. According to the literature, the consequences of patient-centered communication include Improved sense of identity (Lin et al., 2025), Improved quality of Health (Brooker and Latham, 2016; Lin et al., 2025; Savundranayagam and Orange, 2014; Savundranayagam et al., 2016; Zwakhalen and Bormans, 2021), Reduced agitation and anxiety (Liu et al., 2023; Savundranayagam et al., 2016; Williams et al., 2018), Improved Caregiver Communication Skills (Bender et al., 2022; Eggenberger et al., 2013; Stanyon et al., 2016), Enhanced emotional wellbeing (Alsawy et al., 2017; Brooker and Latham, 2016; MY and Johnson, 2020; Savundranayagam and Orange, 2014; Savundranayagam et al., 2016; Zwakhalen and Bormans, 2021), Improved expression of needs (MY and Johnson, 2020; Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015; Schmid Mast et al., 2005), Improved trust (Alsawy et al., 2017), Increased participation in care (Eggenberger et al., 2013; Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015), reduced caregiver frustration (Sprangers et al., 2015; Stanyon et al., 2016), Improved communication effectiveness (Sprangers et al., 2015) [Fig. 2]

Define empirical referents

According to Walker and Avant (2019), empirical referents are observable and measurable phenomena that demonstrate the existence of a concept. In the present analysis, the empirical referents of Patient-Centered Communication (PCC) derived from the included studies, were grouped into four main categories: behavioral, emotional, interactional, and functional indicators.

Behavioral Indicators: Behavioral indicators are observable patient and caregiver behaviors that demonstrate the presence of patient centered communication during interactions. Several studies reported that patients demonstrated calmness or reduced agitation during interactions, reflecting that communication adapted to cognitive ability helps minimize distress (Brooker and Latham, 2016; Lin et al., 2025; Prince et al., 2015). Caregivers were observed to use non-verbal strategies such as gestures or touch to maintain engagement (Williams et al., 2018), and to use simple, short sentences tailored to the patient’s level to facilitate understanding (Eggenberger et al., 2013; Savundranayagam and Orange, 2014; Young et al., 2011). In addition, caregivers who validated and acknowledged patient emotions demonstrated behavioral manifestations of empathy and respect for personhood (Alsawy et al., 2017; Stanyon et al., 2016).

Emotional Indicators: Emotional indicators reflect the affective outcomes of compassionate, individualized, and respectful communication. Patients who showed trust and engagement with caregivers displayed emotional responses that arise from feeling valued and understood (Brooker and Latham, 2016; Eggenberger et al., 2013; Prince et al., 2015; Savundranayagam and Orange, 2014; Schmid Mast et al., 2005; Zwakhalen and Bormans, 2021). Reports of patients feeling happy and demonstrating enhanced emotional well-being with reduced anxiety and agitation further indicate the emotional dimension of patient centered communication (MY and Johnson, 2020; Williams et al., 2018). These signs illustrate how emotionally attuned communication nurtures a sense of safety and belonging, supporting the therapeutic relationship between caregivers and individuals with dementia. When caregivers validate emotions and respond empathetically, the interaction evokes trust and contentment, key emotional referents of patient centered communication

Interactional Indicators: Interactional indicators capture the mutual and dynamic nature of communication between caregivers and patients. In several studies, patients expressed needs or emotions more clearly, suggesting that patient centered communication facilitates mutual understanding and communication effectiveness (Brooker and Latham, 2016; MY and Johnson, 2020; Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015; Zwakhalen and Bormans, 2021). Moreover, patients appeared less socially isolated and more involved, reflecting that patient centered communication fosters inclusion and social participation (Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015; Zwakhalen and Bormans, 2021). Caregivers’ efforts to maintain eye contact and adapt communication to the patient’s cognitive level were noted as important interactional behaviors that sustain meaningful exchanges (Eggenberger et al., 2013; Savundranayagam and Orange, 2014). Other studies described observed communication outcomes such as resident responsiveness, reduced distress, and increased engagement as indicators of interactive success (Sprangers et al., 2015). Collectively, these referents confirm that patient centered communication functions as a two-way relational process in dementia care.

Functional Indicators: Functional indicators represent the overall outcomes and benefits resulting from effective patient centered communication in care relationships. Several studies described improved communication effectiveness and quality of life indicators as major functional outcomes of patient centered communication (MY and Johnson, 2020; Savundranayagam and Orange, 2014; Sprangers et al., 2015). Other reports included improved caregiving skills and care participation, which highlight how person-centered approaches enhance both caregiver competence and patient involvement (Eggenberger et al., 2013; Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015). Functional referents also included reduced frustration and confusion, as communication becomes clearer and more supportive (Sprangers et al., 2015), as well as improved quality of health as a broader result of positive communication experiences (Prince et al., 2015; Williams et al., 2018; Young et al., 2011). Discussion

This concept analysis examined Patient-Centered Communication (PCC) among home-based dementia care task forces to clarify its defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents. The findings highlight that patient centered communication represents a multidimensional and relational process that enables meaningful interaction between caregivers and persons with dementia.

The analysis revealed that cognitive-appropriate communication is one of the most essential defining attributes of patient centered communication. Caregivers’ ability to adapt language, tone, and non-verbal cues to the cognitive capacities of persons with dementia facilitates comprehension and engagement while reducing agitation and confusion (Lin et al., 2025; Morris et al., 2020; Pepper and Dening, 2023; Savundranayagam and Orange, 2014; Savundranayagam et al., 2016). For instance, Williams et al. (2018) emphasized that communication tailored to cognitive level promotes calmer interactions and greater resident engagement (Lin et al., 2025). Similarly, Zwakhalen et al. (2021) found that modifying speech patterns, pacing, and non-verbal behavior enhances care participation and communication effectiveness (Eggenberger et al., 2013).

Empathy and active listening were also identified as core dimensions of patient centered communication. Empathy involves recognizing and responding to the emotional experiences of persons with dementia, allowing caregivers to establish trust and psychological safety (Alzheimer Society of British Columbia; Bąk-Sosnowska et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2024). Active listening, through sustained attention, paraphrasing, and reflective responses, demonstrates respect and genuine concern, thereby strengthening the therapeutic relationship (Baker et al., 2015; Clarke et al., 2010; Schmid Mast et al., 2005). Research consistently links empathic and attentive listening behaviors to improved emotional well-being and reduced behavioral distress among older adults with dementia (Alsawy et al., 2017; Brooker and Latham, 2016; Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015; Williams et al., 2018).

Non-verbal sensitivity and compassion were observed as critical behavioral expressions of patient centered communication. Given that verbal communication often declines as dementia progresses, caregivers’ awareness and interpretation of non-verbal signals—such as facial expression, gaze, and tone—become vital in maintaining meaningful connection (Alzheimer’s Society 2022; Magai et al., 1996; Williams et al., 2018). Compassion, on the other hand, transforms empathy into concrete actions, such as reassurance, gentle touch, and individualized attention, that affirm the person’s dignity and emotional safety (Dewar and Nolan, 2013; Vitali et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024). Compassionate care fosters a sense of being valued, which has been shown to alleviate anxiety and promote cooperation in dementia care (Dewar and Nolan, 2013; Pérez-Belmonte et al., 2021).

Individualization and respect for autonomy emerged as higher-order relational attributes that reflect the ethical and personhood-based dimensions of patient centered communication. Individualization involves tailoring communication strategies to align with each person’s life history, preferences, and emotional needs, reinforcing their sense of identity and continuity (Edvardsson et al., 2008; Kitwood, 2007; Sinclair et al., 2017). Respecting autonomy entails recognizing the person’s right to participate in care decisions, even within the limits imposed by cognitive decline (Beauchamp and Childress, 2019; Brownie and Nancarrow, 2013). These principles ensure that care is not only effective but also aligned with the moral imperative of upholding personhood and dignity in dementia care (Kitwood, 1997; Sinclair et al., 2017).

The antecedents identified—such as cognitive decline, caregiver knowledge and skill, and communication vulnerability—underscore the contextual prerequisites for patient centered communication. Progressive deterioration in language and memory often challenges interaction, demanding caregivers to possess specialized communication skills and awareness (Eggenberger et al., 2013; Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015; Savundranayagam et al., 2016; Williams et al., 2018). Meanwhile, consequences such as improved emotional well-being, increased participation in care, and enhanced trust illustrate that PCC contributes directly to better psychosocial and functional outcomes (Brooker and Latham, 2016; Lin et al., 2025; MY and Johnson, 2020; Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015; Savundranayagam and Orange, 2014; Schmid Mast et al., 2005). These outcomes support the idea that PCC is not a static technique but a dynamic relational process that transforms the care environment into one of mutual respect and understanding.

Empirical referents from reviewed studies demonstrate that patient centered communication can be observed and measured across four dimensions—behavioral, emotional, interactional, and functional. Behavioral referents include caregivers’ use of simple language, validation, and non-verbal gestures, while emotional indicators include patient calmness, happiness, and trust (Alsawy et al., 2017; Brooker and Latham, 2016; Prince et al., 2015; Savundranayagam and Orange, 2014; Williams et al., 2018). Interactional indicators—such as clearer expression of needs and reduced social isolation—capture the two-way nature of patient centered communication (Eggenberger et al., 2013; Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015; Sprangers et al., 2015). Functional indicators, including improved quality of life, enhanced caregiver competence, and reduced frustration, provide evidence of patient centered communication’s practical value in home-based dementia care (MY and Johnson, 2020; Savundranayagam and Moore-Nielsen, 2015; Savundranayagam and Orange, 2014; Sprangers et al., 2015).

Implications for clinical practice

The clarified concept of patient-centered communication can guide caregivers in home-based dementia care to adopt structured, empathetic, and individualized communication approaches. It supports the development of training programs, care guidelines, and communication tools that enhance caregiver–patient interactions, improve care quality, and promote patient dignity and engagement in the home setting.

Implications for future research

Future studies should empirically test the proposed attributes, antecedents, and consequences of patient-centered communication in home-based dementia care. There is a need to develop and validate measurement tools based on the identified empirical referents, and to examine the effectiveness of communication-focused interventions across diverse cultural and care contexts.

Limitation

This study is limited by its design as a concept analysis, which is inherently theoretical and dependent on existing literature. It does not involve primary data collection or empirical validation, and the findings are based on interpretation of available studies rather than direct observation or measurement.

Conclusion

This concept analysis clarified the meaning, attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents of Patient-Centered Communication (PCC) within home-based dementia care. The findings demonstrate that patient centered communication is a multidimensional, relational process that integrates cognitive adaptation, empathy, compassion, active listening, non-verbal sensitivity, individualization, and respect for autonomy. These attributes enable caregivers to engage with persons with dementia in ways that maintain dignity, foster trust, and promote emotional well-being.
