Synthesis, spectral, thermal, and biological characterization of Se(IV) nanocomplexes derived from vitamin E and amino acid mixed ligands as a metal-drug model
Authors: Mohamed Y. El-Sayed, Ibrahim M. A. Alatawy, Hazim M. Ali, Tamer H. A. Hasanin, I. M. Ahmed, Moamen S. Refat, Kareem A. Asla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Researchers have been interested in inorganic pharmaceutical chemistry in recent years. They have prepared antioxidants and anticancer compounds to lower the cost of manufacturing platinum and palladium medications and as alternatives to some known side-effect drugs. The current work focused on selenium complexes with mixed ligands, which included histidine, tryptophan, and tyrosine as the second ligand and one of the interesting vitamins, like vitamin E, as the first ligand. The synthesis was done in a molar ratio of (1:2:1) (selenium: vitamin E: amino acid). Different characterization techniques were used to check and discuss the chemical structures and morphological properties of the synthesized complexes, including elemental analysis, conductance, FTIR, XRD, UV-Vis, TG/DTG, 1HNMR, SEM-EDX, and TEM investigations. Vitamin E was chelated as a monodentate ligand through the oxygen atom of the OH group. On the other hand, all amino acids were coordinated as bidentate secondary ligands through the nitrogen of the amino group and the oxygen of the carboxylic group. The complexes have strong scavenging properties and antioxidant activity. Their nanoscale synthesis significantly boosts human cells’ uptake of selenium, resulting in the most effective medicinal product. The anticancer potential of each complex examined in this study was assessed against the HepG2 and A2780 cell lines. Based on these facts, the complex Se/Vit E/Tyr can be employed as an anticancer agent.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30281-1.

Introduction

Recent research explores the biosynthesis of SeNPs, which offers a promising approach for producing biocompatible and effective therapeutic agents. Furthermore, coating SeNPs with bioactive compounds such as fucoidan has been shown to cause significant DNA damage in cancer cells, highlighting their capacity to induce genotoxic stress selectively1. It plays an essential role in the function of glutathione peroxidase enzyme, which responds to H2O2 and other harmful lipids and phospholipid hydroxides to prevent dangerous free radicals, inhibit DNA breakdown, and increase the efficiency of metabolically active carcinogens2. Selenium is incorporated into the enzyme selenocysteine, where the sulfur is placed. The biochemical property of selenium emphasizes its significance in the body’s antioxidant defense system by making it easier to minimize the amount of sulfur3. The selenium antioxidant effect prevents the oxidation of Cholesterol LDL4, decreases inflammation, reinforces the immune system, increases body resistance towards oxidative stress, and therapy indirectly lowers HIV virulence5. Some toxic elements, such as mercury toxicity, were reduced by selenium through inhibiting their absorption by forming insoluble compounds6. In the human body, selenium can boost immunity and stave off a wide range of diseases, including cancer, arthritis, cataract, dental caries, and the Kaschin-Beck disease7,8. According to the results of typical human clinical trials, supplementing modest selenium can cut the risk of developing cancer by 35%, with the mortality falling to 50%. The Se content of animal products reflects the Se levels in their consumed diet9. Vitamin E, recognized for its antioxidant properties, has been shown to modulate the effects of metal complexes. Notably, vitamin E alleviates the cytostatic effects of platinum-group metal complexes, implying that its presence in mixed ligand systems could influence the biological activity and stability of Se(IV) complexes10. The combination of amino acids and vitamin E with Se(IV) may therefore enhance therapeutic efficacy, possibly through synergistic effects or stabilization of the complex10. L-tyrosine-derived Schiff bases and their metal complexes are crucial components of biological tests like the ML2 [H2O]2 assay where (M = manganese (II), cobalt (II), Nickel (II) and Copper (II) were prepared and evaluated for their antimicrobial activity (fungi infection) against Phytophthora capsica, the casual organism of foot rot of black pepper11. Tryptophan is one of the 10 essential amino acids that the body uses to synthesize proteins it needs12. It is well known for its part in the production of messengers in the neurological system, which are connected to relaxation, stillness, and sleep. There are two main uses for Tryptophan. First, the liver converts a little portion quantity of the tryptophan we take in our diet (approximately 3%) of the we consume into Niacin (vitamin B3). Because of this inversion, when dietary intake of this vitamin is very low, the symptoms of niacin deficiency do not manifest. Second, tryptophan serves as a building block for serotonin, a neurotransmitter vital for the body’s regulation of mood, sleep cycles, and appetite. Tryptophan has been used therapeutically to treat a range of disorders, most notably somnia, depression, and anxiety due to its capacity to raise serotonin levels. The metabolism of tryptophan requires vitamin B6, vitamin C, folic acid, and Mg. Additionally, tryptophan competes with tyrosine and phenylalanine for absorption13. It is crucial to look at tryptophan’s complex structure and chelation with various metal ions because of the crucial role it plays in biological systems. For creating and maintaining a favorable nitrogen balance in human nutrition, it is the most crucial component of proteins14. It is well known that proteins and oligopeptides use Histidine as an essential metal ion binding site. Histidine is an alpha-amino acid with one carboxylic group and dibasic characteristics. Histidine is produced if the imidazole group is substituted for the alpha-amino acid’s “R” group. The presence of the imidazole group causes the basicity of histidine. Four distinct groups can act as bonding sites when they form a complex with a metal ion. The carboxyl and NH2 groups found in an alpha amino acid are examples of these groups with bonding sites. The imidazole complex, however, is responsible for the other two, such as the secondary and tertiary nitrogen atoms15. The coordination chemistry of amino acids is characterized by protonated and polynuclear complexes because the structure of amino acids prohibits all their potential binding sites from being attached to the same metal ion at the same time. L-Histidine is a model system of great interest and is particularly useful in the field of amino acid coordination chemistry16. Histidine has been proven by numerous authors to play an anchoring role for metal ions like copper (II) or nickel (II), for example, and is typically one of the amino acid side chain donor groups that can coordinate metal ions in large molecules17. However, numerous proteins have ATCUN motifs, which stand for amino-terminal copper (II) and nickel (II) binding, for instance, human serum albumin, which has an Asp-Ala-His sequence for copper (II) transport18. Strong metal binding sites are also present in Aβ, the proteolytic oligopeptide fragment of the amyloid precursor protein, at its N-terminal region19. In addition to the imidazole moieties of internal histidine, it is hypothesized that the free N-terminal amino group is important in metal ion binding20.

Vitamin E compound consists of three main distinct moieties, described as (i) the functional domain, responsible for the antioxidant property and therefore, properties of vitamin E, represented by hydroxyl group in α-tocopherol, (ii) the signaling domain, comprised of aromatic rings (phenol- and chromanol ring21, and (iii) the hydrophobic domain, responsible for docking the agents in circulating lipoproteins and biological membranes22. Vitamin E deficiency in humans has been shown only in premature infants and in people suffering from malabsorption. In addition to causing a decrease in blood tocopherol levels, the most common symptom that appeared in these cases is an increase in hemolysis of the red blood cells, which also shows up as vitamin E-induced anemia. Symptoms of vitamin E deficiency in animals include abnormalities of the neurological, reproductive, muscular, cardiovascular, and hematological systems. In general, metal ions are required for numerous significant biological processes in humans23. An essential component of medical bioinorganic chemistry is the capability to identify the molecular level of diseases and develop treatments for them when metal-ion function is inadequate24. The development of modern chemotherapy using metal complexes has an important role in raising the efficiency of drugs, for instance, copper complexation with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medications to mitigate some of these medications’ gastrointestinal adverse effects25. However, many organic medicines with well-developed pharmacological and toxicological characteristics delivered as metal complexes26 can act as donors, and the ensuing complexes of metal-drug are crucial to coordination chemistry and biochemistry27. Current synthetic bioinorganic chemistry and chelation chemistry face significant difficulty because the chelation study of metal-drug is still in its early phases.

Experimental

Materials and instruments

All chemicals in this study were used without any further treatment. Distilled water used in all the experiments was obtained by a Milli-Q Direct 8 purification system (Millipore, France). Amino acids Tyr, Trp, His, α-tocopherol (Vit E), and SeCl4 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. NH4OH, methanol, diethyl ether, dimethyl sulfoxide (Sigma, Aldrich). The molar conductance was measured by a HANNA Edge conductivity meter. The Microanalytical analyses (% carbon, % hydrogen, and % nitrogen) contents were measured using a Perkin Elmer CHN 2400 (USA). The metal content was determined gravimetrically by converting the compounds into their corresponding carbides or oxides. Infrared spectra of Se (IV) complexes were measured in the range of 400–4000 cm− 1 and recorded as KBr discs on an FT-IR Nicolet 6700, Thermo Scientific, USA. The 1H-NMR spectra of Se complexes were recorded on a JEOL resonance NMR spectrometer, using DMSO–d6 as solvent and TMS as an internal reference. Thermogravimetric analysis was carried out in the temperature range of 25–600 °C in a nitrogen atmosphere by a thermogravimetric analyzer, Shimadzu, TGA-51, with a heating rate of 10 °C/min, with a platinum crucible. The X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 Adv., Germany) with Ni-filtered Cu-Kα line as the radiation source (λ = 1.54056 Å) was used to create XRD patterns at room temperature in the range of 20 to 80°. The UV-Vis spectra were measured in DMSO solvent with concentration (10− 3M) for the free amino acids, vitamins, and their complexes using Agilent Cary 60 spectrophotometers (Agilent Technologies, USA) with a 1 cm quartz cell, in the range 200–900 nm. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL JSM-6610LV, Tokyo, Japan) was used to analyze the surface morphology. Energy-dispersive X-ray detection was taken in JEOL JSM-6390LA equipment with an accelerating voltage of 20KV. The transmission electron microscopy images were performed using JEOL JEM-1200 EX II, Japan, at 60–70 KV.

Synthesis of se amino acid- α-tocopherol (Vit E) mixed complexes

The complexes were synthesized with a ratio of (Metal: mixed Ligands), Se: L1: L2 (1:2:1), where L1 = Vitamin, and L2 = His, Trp, Tyr. To 25 ml (1mmol) of SeCl4 in methanol solution, a Mixture of 25 ml (1mmol) of methanol solution of amino acid and 25 ml (2mmol) of methanol solution of α-tocopherol was added slowly. The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 7–8 using ammonium hydroxide, and the solution was refluxed for 3 h at 55 °C. The resulting faint pink to reddish- brown solution was reduced to ½ of its volume, then the solid precipitate was collected and washed with petroleum ether and dried over anhydrous CaCl2.

Antioxidant activities assessment

The scavenging activity of all produced compounds was assessed against DPPH (diphenyl picryl hydrazyl)28. The stable free radical DPPH can convert into a stable, diamagnetic molecule by accepting an electron or a hydrogen radical. At 517 nm, DPPH has a significant absorption band. To reach a critical concentration (0.1 mmol/L), the test samples were dissolved in 1.5 ml of aqueous methanol (0.2 mmol/L) and then added to an equivalent volume of (0.2 mmol/L) DPPH solution. Several test solution concentrations (2.5, 5, and 10 µg/ml) were added to the DPPH solution. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm against a blank after 30 min of incubation at 20 °C with vigorous shaking. The experiment was run three times, and the outcomes were averaged. Equation was used to calculate the % of inhibition (I%) of free radical generation from DPPH.

Cytotoxicity and anticancer activity

HepG2 and A2780 cell lines were purchased from ATCC (https://www.atcc.org/). Sorafenib (SML2653) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, solubilized in DMSO, and kept at − 20 °C as a stock solution. The tested compounds were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (10 mM stock) (DMSO Cat. No. 20385.02, Serva, Heidelberg, Germany) and stored at − 20 °C.

The cytotoxicity of medications is evaluated using the colorimetric MTT test. The determine cell viability, the MTT test involves the reduction of MTT, a tetrazolium dye, to insoluble formazan (purple color) in living cells29. By adding an appropriate solvent, the insoluble purple formazan is converted into a colorful solution. This colored solution’s absorbance can be measured at a specific wavelength of 570 nm. By comparing the amount of purple formazan produced by untreated control cells with dosage response curves, it is possible to estimate a drug’s capacity to kill cells. (100 µL per well) A 96-well plate was used to seed the cancer cells. The cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere with either 50 µM of each test compound or 0.5% (v/v) DMSO. After 48 h, MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) dissolved in PBS at 5 mg/mL was introduced, followed by an additional 4-hour incubation. The resulting formazan crystals were then solubilized using an acidic SDS solution (10% SDS, 0.01 N HCl in PBS). Following a 14-hour incubation period, absorbance readings were taken at 570–630 nm using a Biotek Gen5TM microplate reader30,31. Based on the results of the preliminary screening, the cells were exposed to serial dilutions of compounds 1–6 for 48 h, after which the vitality of the cells was assessed using the MTT reagent.

DFT calculation

DFT Computations were carried out to verify the hypothesized geometrical structures of Se (IV) mixed ligand complexes. We used the B3LYP/ 6-311G basis set to optimize the geometry32. The computations were done using the Gaussian 09 software package33. The structure of selenium (IV) complexes of mixed ligands was calculated.

Results and discussion

Microanalytical and molar conductance measurements

The prepared selenium (IV) mixed ligand complexes were isolated in solid state forms, which are stable in room temperature and have low solubility in polar and nonpolar solvents and high solubility in DMSO as an organic solvent. The molar conductance values of [Se(Vit E)2(His)Cl] (1), [Se(Vit E)2(Trp)Cl] (2) and [Se(Vit E)2(Tyr)Cl] (3) are 68, 55 and 50 Ω–1cm2 mol–1, respectively confirmed that nonelectrolyte nature for the three complexes34. These results are regarded to be low due to the existence of one chlorine atom inside the coordination sphere. These complexes have a melting point within the 230–250 °C range, with a yield of 83–89%. The presence of Cl− ions was checked using AgNO3 reagent. Elemental analysis data (Table 1), % carbon, % hydrogen, and % nitrogen as well as % Se metal, was in good agreement with the proposed structure formula.

Electronic spectra

UV-Vis spectra of Se(IV)/Vit E/amino acid mixed ligand complexes in DMSO solvent are scanned, demonstrated characteristic absorption peaks within the region of 200–600 nm. The free vit E and amino acids have distinct absorption bands at 290 nm and 280 nm, respectively. The formation of Vit E/amino acids SeNPs was confirmed using UV-Vis electronic spectral scanning, due to change the color from colorless of Se (IV) chloride to brown, yellow brown and pink colored (SeNPs), with absorption maximum (λmax) at (270–390 nm). Suggesting the ligands are binding to selenium metal through lone pair of electrons on -NH2 and oxygen atom of -OH groups The absorption band at 500 nm was assigned to the ligand to metal charge transfer L→M35.

Infrared spectral studies

Infrared spectra of the selenium complexes 1–3 are presented in Fig. 1. These FT-IR spectra are very similar, with easily distinguished spectral bands. This led to the conclusion that the coordination statements in all selenium (IV) complexes 1–3 are the same. The proposed spectral assignments for the studied complexes are summarized in Table 2, allowing for a broad interpretation of their vibrational characteristics. These assignments were determined using established literature36,37 along with insights from a relevant supporting reference38.

In the solid state, free amino acids are present in zwitterion formulations. The absence of the characteristic v(NH3 +) and δ(NH3 +) bands and the shift in the wave numbers of the stretching vibrations corresponding to carboxylate in the free ligand prove the existence of a non-zwitterionic formula. From Table 2, the stretching frequencies of the NH2 group existed at 3100, 3200, and 3250 cm− 1 in the case of free amino acids His–histidine, Trp-tryptophan, and Tyr–tyrosine, respectively. These referenced bands were shifted to lower wavenumbers due to complexation with selenium metal, and their presence at 3075, 3180, and 3200 cm− 1 is due to the nitrogen atom of the − NH2 group coordinating with the selenium (IV) complexes 1–3, respectively. The antisymmetric and symmetric COO− frequencies of Se-His, Se-Trp, and Se-Tyr complexes at (1620 and 1410) cm− 1, (1630 and 1409) cm− 1, and (1610 and 1407) cm− 1, respectively. From IR data, the energy difference between both vas (COO) and vs(COO) vibrations (∆= 203–221 cm− 1) indicates the participation of the carboxylate group in monodentate binding. The NH2 bending frequencies in the spectra of these complexes are absent, as expected from symmetry considerations. The complexes exhibited two new frequencies at 530–590 and 420–440 cm−¹, respectively, due to Se-O and Se-N stretching. In the case of the free Trp ligand, the − NH frequency of the indole ring appears at 3402 cm− 1, and so, in selenium (IV)-Trp complexes, the stretching vibrational motion at 3401 cm− 1 is assigned to the − NH frequency of the indole ring. Its presence at the same place without shifting in the spectrum of the complex is evidence for the -NH group not being involved in complexation with selenium metal. To confirm the participation of the alcoholic ν(OH) group of vit-OH in coordination, the stretching vibration bands of ν(C-O) in the selenium vit-OH complex were analyzed. The results revealed a shift in ν(C-O) to lower wavenumbers from 1144 to 1110 cm− 1 in the free ligand to 1130 and 1090 cm− 1, 1120 and 1088 cm− 1, and 1100 and 1050 cm− 1 in the His, Trp, and Tyr complexes, respectively. This shift suggests that the alcoholic group is involved in coordination, supporting the role of vit-OH as a monodentate ligand. Additionally, the vit-OH complex exhibited two weak bands.

The absence of the OH of the carboxylic group in the proton spectra of the three selenium metal complexes associated with mixed two ligands (vitamin E and amino acids) substantiates the involvement of the carboxylate oxygen atom in the coordination mode.

Thermogravimetric analysis of the complexes (1–3)

TGA curve (Fig. 2a) of the three selenium (IV) complexes (1–3). Thermal decomposition of complex (1) took place in three decomposition steps between 100 °C- 600 °C. The second step of decomposition occurred in the range 130 °C – 380 °C at maximum differential thermogravimetric peak DTGmax = 290 °C with mass loss 88.4% attributed to loss of organic moieties, including two vit E molecules & histidine amino acid. The final degradation phase occurs at 580 °C (DTGmax), exhibiting a 22% mass loss that results from tyrosine molecule breakdown and selenium sublimation.

Theoretical studies of Se(IV) complex (1–3)

The computationally optimized molecular structures and their frontier molecular orbitals are represented in Figs. 8, 9 and 10 and Figures (4–9)S in the supplementary section. We calculated multiple quantum chemical parameters through molecular modeling simulations to characterize the electronic properties. These included structural features (bond lengths and angles), frontier orbital energies (EHOMO and ELUMO), and the derived energy gap that governs intramolecular charge transfer processes. Additionally, we determined key reactivity descriptors: electronegativity (χ), chemical potential (µ), global hardness (η), global softness (S), and the electrophilicity index (ω). The computational results are systematically organized in Table 5 and Tables (1–6)S in the supplementary section. The bond lengths and angles confirm a distorted octahedral geometry for complexes (1–3). The negative HOMO and LUMO values for the compounds (1–3), indicate their stability. The Se(IV) complexes with a lower energy gap (ΔE) are more stable and highly reactive. Furthermore, electron density-based molecular properties such as electronegativity, electrophilicity, and chemical hardness indicate that compound platinum (IV) metal complex is the most active since it has a higher value of electronegativity and electrophilicity than other compounds. The significantly higher electronegativity and electrophilicity values mean that the compound interacts more effectively in the biological system41. The negative values of the chemical potential of all compounds indicated that all coordination processes are spontaneous42. Figure 10 and (5–6)S show the estimated molecule electrostatic potential surfaces (MEP) for gaseous phases. regions colored in red indicate high electron density, making them potential sites for electrophilic attacks. Conversely, blue regions represent electron-deficient areas, which are more susceptible to nucleophilic attacks. The intensity of the colors (red, green, and blue) corresponds to the potential, with blue indicating the strongest attraction and red representing the strongest repulsion43.

Conclusion

Three novel selenium(IV) complexes were synthesized using mixed-ligand systems, where vitamin E served as the primary ligand and the amino acids histidine (His), tryptophan (Trp), and tyrosine (Tyr) acted as secondary ligands. The complexes were formulated as [Se(Vit E)₂(His)Cl(H₂O)] (1), [Se(Vit E)₂(Trp)Cl(H₂O)] (2), and [Se(Vit E)₂(Tyr)Cl(H₂O)] (3), based on spectroscopic and physicochemical analyses. The synthesis was carried out in a 1:2:1 molar ratio (Se: vitamin E: amino acid). The vibrational spectra of compounds (1–3) reveal that vitamin E coordinates in a monodentate manner via the oxygen atom of its phenolic -OH group, while the amino acids act as bidentate ligands, binding through both the amino nitrogen and carboxylate oxygen. The molar conductance measurements reveal the nonelectrolytic nature of the synthesized compounds. The UV-visible spectra predict the distorted Oh environment around the Se(IV). Thermal analysis indicated that the complexes (1–3) exhibit high thermal stability. Nanostructural characterization revealed that the complexes formed spherical selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) with an average crystallite size of 5–50 nm, as confirmed by TEM imaging. The synthesized complexes demonstrated strong free radical scavenging activity and antioxidant properties. Additionally, their anticancer potential was evaluated against HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma) and A2780 (ovarian cancer) cell lines. Among the three complexes, [Se(Vit E)₂(Tyr)Cl(H₂O)] (3) exhibited the most promising anticancer activity, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent. DFT calculations are in good agreement with the results obtained from the experimental data.
