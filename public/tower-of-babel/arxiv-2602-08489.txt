Beyond Correctness: Learning Robust Reasoning via Transfer
Hyunseok Lee, Soheil Abbasloo, Jihoon Tack, Jinwoo Shin

Abstract
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has recently strengthened LLM reasoning, but its focus on final‑answer correctness leaves a critical gap: it does not ensure the robustness of the reasoning process itself. We adopt a simple philosophical view—robust reasoning should remain useful beyond the mind that produced it—and treat reasoning as a form of meaning transfer that must survive truncation, reinterpretation, and continuation. Building on this principle, we introduce Reinforcement Learning with Transferable Reward (RLTR), which operationalizes robustness via transfer reward that tests whether a partial reasoning prefix from one model can guide a separate model to the correct answer. This encourages LLMs to produce reasoning that is stable, interpretable, and genuinely generalizable. Our approach improves sampling consistency while improving final‑answer accuracy, and it reaches comparable performance in substantially fewer training steps. For example, on MATH-500, RLTR achieves a +3.6%p gain in Maj@64 compared to RLVR and matches RLVR’s average accuracy with roughly 2.5 × 2.5\times fewer training steps, providing both more reliable reasoning and significantly more sample-efficient.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) combined with reinforcement learning (RL) have recently demonstrated remarkable performance across various domains, such as solving complex mathematical problems
(
19
)
,
serving as coding assistants
(
8
)
, and contributing to scientific discoveries
(
16
)
.
Compared with simple supervised fine-tuning, RL enables LLMs to learn more generalizable and robust behaviors
(
14
)
.
However, the success of RL in LLMs is often bottlenecked by its heavy reliance on reward models.
While reward models reduce the need for costly human annotations, they introduce critical challenges such as reward hacking
(
22
)
and increased training complexity
(
1
)
.

To tackle this issue, reinforcement learning with verifiable reward (RLVR) has recently emerged
(
19
;
5
)
.
RLVR replaces learned reward models with rule-based verification of the correctness of final answers, which significantly simplifies the training pipeline and alleviates reward hacking and the need for extensive human annotations.
As a result, RLVR achieves remarkable success on complex reasoning tasks, especially in competitive math domains.
Yet despite these gains, RLVR optimizes reasoning solely through final‑answer correctness, leaving a critical dimension of reasoning quality underexplored: the robustness of the reasoning process itself.
In real‑world and practical settings, models that consistently produce correct answers across different sampled solutions, decoding variations, or reasoning paths are far more valuable than models that succeed only sporadically.
However, RLVR’s objective evaluates only the generator’s final output, providing no incentive for intermediate reasoning to be robust, reusable, or stable under such perturbations.
Prior work has observed that the model loses consistency and diversity as more samples are generated, suggesting that final-outcome correctness alone may be an incomplete signal for promoting reliable reasoning behavior
(
27
)
.

To move beyond outcome‑only evaluation, we draw inspiration from human learning: strong reasoning is not merely correct—it is also
explainable
in a way that others can reliably follow.
This motivates our central concept,
reasoning transferability
, defined as the extent to which a reasoning prefix produced by one model can be completed by another model to reach a verifiably correct answer.
We operationalize this idea by truncating a generator’s reasoning trace and asking a separate receiver model to continue it; transfer succeeds when the combined reasoning yields a correct final answer.
Building on this principle, we propose Reinforcement Learning with Transferable Reward (RLTR), a new reinforcement learning framework that explicitly optimizes cross‑model reasoning transferability.
The core mechanism is a
transfer reward
that evaluates a generator’s partial reasoning via a receiver‑model continuation, integrated into RLVR through a transfer roll‑out procedure and a weighted combination of answer, transfer, and format rewards.
To our knowledge, RLTR is the first RLVR framework that directly incentivizes LLMs to produce reasoning traces that are transferable across models.
An overview of RLTR is shown in Figure
1
.

We demonstrated the effectiveness of RLTR through extensive evaluations on multiple reasoning datasets covering a wide range of difficulties across mathematical and scientific domains.
Overall, RLTR consistently improves Majority Voting at K (Maj@K), a proxy for multi-sample consistency, while also improving average accuracy.
For instance, on AMC23
(
12
)
, RLTR improves Maj@64 from 61.7 to 67.5 compared to RLVR.
Across training, RLTR achieves the same average accuracy level with about
2.5\times
fewer training steps than RLVR on MATH-500, indicating faster learning and improved sample efficiency.
Finally, we show transferability correlates with multi-sample consistency by tracking transferability and Maj@
K
throughout training.

We highlight the main contributions of the paper below:

•

We introduce RLTR, a simple extension of RLVR that adds a transfer reward to optimize reasoning transferability across models.

•

We demonstrate that RLTR increases consistency, as shown by higher majority voting accuracy, which reflects more robust reasoning compared to RLVR.

•

Our training framework improves training sample efficiency e.g., RLTR matches RLVR’s performance with
2.5\times
fewer training steps and improved average accuracy on MATH-500.

Related Work

Reinforcement Learning in LLM.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a cornerstone for aligning LLMs with human intent, primarily through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF;
14
).
Standard approaches typically employ Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO;
18
) to optimize a policy against a learned reward model (RM) that mimics human preferences.
However, this dependence on neural reward models introduces significant challenges.
As the policy optimizer exploits the reward model, it often leads to reward hacking, where the model generates high-reward but low-quality or nonsensical outputs due to the imperfections of the proxy reward
(
22
;
3
;
17
)
.
Furthermore, training a robust reward model requires extensive human annotation and often creates a bottleneck in scaling reasoning capabilities.

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward.

To mitigate the limitations of neural reward models, recent research has shifted towards RL with Verifiable Reward (RLVR), where the reward is derived from ground-truth correctness (e.g., mathematical answers or code execution) rather than a learned proxy.
Pioneering works such as DeepSeek-Math
(
19
)
and DeepSeek-R1
(
5
)
introduced Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to stabilize RL training without a critic model, with verifiable reward that can induce emergent reasoning behaviors, significantly boosting performance on complex benchmarks.
The following works were mainly focusing on stabilize the learning algorithm and enhance the learning objectives
(
26
;
11
)
.
While these methods effectively address reward hacking by relying on objective outcomes, they primarily evaluate the result rather than the process.
Unlike prior approaches that rely solely on self-consistency or final-answer correctness, RLTR introduces a novel reward signal based on
reasoning transferability
.

Process Reward Models.

Process Reward Models (PRMs) offer an alternative by providing dense step-level supervision using
learned verifiers trained on large annotated reasoning traces.
Process supervision has been shown to reduce reasoning errors compared to
outcome-only training
(
23
)
.
Verifier-based step scoring has been explored for GSM8K
(
2
)
,
and large-scale datasets (e.g., PRM800K) enable powerful verifier models
that outperform outcome-only training on MATH
(
10
)
.
Recent variants include Q-value PRMs
(
9
)
,
reasoning-driven PRMs
(
20
)
, and generative verifier models
for long chain-of-thought
(
7
)
.
However, PRMs require high-capacity learned step-level verifiers, substantial
step-level annotations, and reintroduce proxy reward mismatch.
Unlike PRMs, RLTR operates without any additional annotation requirements beyond those of the answer-verification dataset, and does not require training any reward models for intermediate reasoning supervision.

RLTR: Reinforcement Learning with Transferable Reward

In this section, we introduce Reinforcement Learning with Transferable Reward (RLTR), a reinforcement learning framework that encourages robust and reusable reasoning traces by augmenting RLVR with a transfer reward.
Building on RLVR-style final answer supervision, RLTR introduces a transfer reward that evaluates whether another model (namely, the receiver model) can recover the correct answer from a truncated reasoning prefix,
and maximizes this signal as an auxiliary reward objective.
We first formalize the learning framework, RLVR, in Section
3.1
and at the top of Figure
1
.
We then present RLTR, the core training algorithm that integrates the transfer reward into the RL objective (Section
3.2
and bottom in Figure
1
).

3.1
Preliminary: RLVR

RLVR optimizes the model to produce a correct final answer under a fixed output format.
Given an input
x
, the generator model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{gen}}
samples a full response (including reasoning and the final answer), i.e.,
y_{\text{gen}}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\text{gen}}(\cdot|x)
.
The reward is primarily defined by an answer reward that checks whether the generator’s final answer matches the ground truth, i.e.,

\text{R}_{\text{ans}}(y_{\text{gen}})=\mathbbm{1}[\mathrm{answer}(y_{\text{gen}})=y_{\text{gt}}]
.
In addition, a format reward
\text{R}_{\text{fmt}}(y_{\text{gen}})
is used to encourage well-formed outputs (e.g., valid reasoning structure and answer formatting), and to penalize invalid generations.

The overall reward is defined as

\text{R}_{\text{RLVR}}(y_{\text{gen}})=a\,\text{R}_{\text{ans}}(y_{\text{gen}})+\text{R}_{\text{fmt}}(y_{\text{gen}}).

(1)

For a given dataset
\mathcal{D}:=\{(x,y)\}
, the generator is optimized via reinforcement learning with the objective as:

\max_{\mathcal{M}_{\text{gen}}}\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{\text{gt}})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y_{\text{gen}}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\text{gen}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\text{R}_{\text{RLVR}}(y_{\text{gen}})\right]\right].

(2)

In this paper, we mainly optimize the RL policy with group relative policy optimization (GRPO;
19
;
5
), which is known to be simple yet strong when use with RLVR.

Overall, conventional RLVR optimizes the correctness of the generator’s own final output.
However, it does not explicitly account for step-level noise and brittleness in the reasoning trajectory, nor does it directly encourage reusable or interpretable reasoning that can be reliably continued by other models or decoding processes.

3.2
Reinforcement Learning with Transferable Reward

To address this limitation, we propose RLTR, a training method that augments RLVR with a transfer reward to encourage reasoning
transferable
, whether partial reasoning that can be reliably reused by another model to reach the correct answer.
Intuitively, a generator has higher transferability when its truncated reasoning prefixes contain stable and informative intermediate structure, enabling a receiver to reliably complete the solution, rather than relying on the generator’s own continuation.

Transfer Reward.

We define transfer reward which identify the reasoning transferability.
Concretely, given an input
x
, the generator model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{gen}}
produces a full reasoning sequence
y_{\text{gen}}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\text{gen}}(\cdot|x)
. Then, we truncate the prefix of reasoning sequence and passed to a receiver model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{rcv}}
for continuation,
y_{\text{rcv}}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\text{rcv}}(\cdot|x,y_{\text{gen}}[:\ell])
, where truncation ratio
\tau\in(0,1]
and truncation length
\ell=\lfloor\tau\,|y_{\text{gen}}|\rfloor
.
We define a transfer reward as:

\text{R}_{\text{trans}}(y_{\text{gen}},\mathcal{M}_{\text{rcv}})=\mathbbm{1}[\mathrm{answer}(y_{\text{rcv}})=y_{\text{gt}}]

(3)

which indicates whether the receiver can reach the correct answer from the truncated reasoning.
In practice, we compute
\text{R}_{\text{trans}}
using a single continuation, and sample
\tau
uniformly between 0.3 and 0.9 to ensure the model learns reasoning pattern robust arbitrary interruptions, rather than overfitting to fixed truncation boundaries.

The overall reward is defined as

\text{R}_{\text{RLTR}}(y_{\text{gen}},\mathcal{M}_{\text{rcv}}):=\text{R}_{\text{RLVR}}(y_{\text{gen}})+t\,\text{R}_{\text{trans}}(y_{\text{gen}},\mathcal{M}_{\text{rcv}})

(4)

Training Objective.
The generator is optimized via reinforcement learning with the objective

\max_{\mathcal{M}_{\text{gen}}}\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{\text{gt}})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y_{\text{gen}}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\text{gen}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\text{R}_{\text{RLTR}}(y_{\text{gen}},\mathcal{M}_{\text{rcv}})\right]\right].

(5)

Evaluation setup.
We mainly report majority voting at K (Maj@K) and average accuracy (Acc.) as a sampling-based metric.
Maj@K selects the final answer by taking the most frequent answer among the K samples, and counts it as correct if this majority answer matches the ground truth.
Specifically, Maj@K captures reasoning consistency and robustness by evaluating whether the correct answer dominates across K stochastic generations.
Average accuracy is expectation of the single-sample’s accuracy, which acquired by simply averaging the accuracy of K sampled solutions.
Additionally, in analysis we measure transferability as the average accuracy under a cross-model reasoning transfer continuation.
For each model and benchmark, we repeat sampling-based evaluation three times with different random seeds and report the average.

Performance of RLTR

We present the main result by comparing the performance on math problem-solving.
Here, we mainly evaluate RLTR against RLVR and the base model, reporting average accuracy (Acc.) and Majority at K (Maj@K) to assess performance and consistency.
The main results were evaluated in (i) two moderate-difficulty tasks: MATH-500 and GSM8K and (ii) two challenging tasks: AIME2024 and AMC23.

RLTR performance gain on moderate-difficulty tasks.

We first evaluate RLTR on two moderate-difficulty tasks that one is in-distribution benchmark (i.e., MATH-500
(

) and the other is out-of-distribution benchmark (i.e., GSM8K
(
2

As shown in Table
1(a)

It is worth noting that while RLVR achieves strong single-sample average accuracy performance (71.0

This suggests that RLVR can produce reasoning trajectories that are brittle. As more samples are drawn, variations in the reasoning paths leads to conflicting outcomes rather than converging toward a consistent correct answer, which in turn manifests as reduced robustness under majority‑vote evaluation.
In contrast, RLTR yields substantially better high-K multi-sample aggregation behavior, mitigating RLVR’s degradation in Maj@K at large K and consistently outperforming both RLVR and the base model.
For example, on MATH-500, RLTR improves Maj@16 from 81.2 to 83.8 and Maj@64 from 82.6 to 84.2, and also improves average accuracy from 71.0 to 77.0.
The stronger gains in Maj@K suggesting that the added transfer reward provides a direct learning signal for robust intermediate reasoning rather than only final-answer correctness.

We observe the same, and even clearer, trend on the out-of-distribution math benchmark, GSM8K.

In contrast, RLTR improves both accuracy and consistency across all metrics (e.g., Acc.: 89.1

This consistent improvements on an out-of-distribution benchmark suggests that the transfer reward provides a general process-level supervision signal, improving robustness beyond the training distribution rather than overfitting to training dataset specific patterns.

4.2
Training Dynamics of RLTR

To better understand the effect of the proposed transfer reward, we analyze the training dynamics of RLTR using the following three metrics: (i) average accuracy, (ii) Maj@64 accuracy, and (iii) reasoning transferability.
Here, we evaluate intermediate checkpoints on MATH-500 and compare RLTR with RLVR.

RLTR improves sample efficiency.

As shown in Figures
2(a)
, RLTR improves substantially faster than RLVR in average accuracy, and also attains a higher final accuracy, consistent with the overall results in Table
1(a)
. For example, RLTR require about
2.5\times
fewer training steps to reach a comparable accuracy with RLVR.
This result suggest that the transfer reward provides a richer process-level signal that incentivizes robust reasoning trajectories, helping the model converge faster than RLVR.
Moreover, as shown in Figure
2(b)
, the Maj@64 curve also follows this improvement, indicating that the gains are not driven by sporadic correct samples but by a progressively more reproducible and consistent reasoning mode.
We discussed additional analysis about compute-performance trade-off in Section
4.4
and Table

Relationship between transferability and Maj@K.

We next study how reasoning transferability evolves over training by truncating a generator’s reasoning trace into a prefix and continually generating a separate, frozen receiver model to complete it.
Specifically, we retain the first
t
fraction of tokens with a fixed truncation ratio
t=0.7
, and measure transferability as the receiver’s verifiable final-answer accuracy under this cross-model continuation protocol.
Across training steps, RLTR exhibits consistently stronger transferability growth than RLVR, with the gap generally widening as training progresses.
Notably, RLVR shows a clear drop in transferability at later training stages even when average accuracy remains stable, and this drop coincides with a degradation in Maj@64.
This coupling suggests that RLVR can become less consistent over training: it may preserve average correctness while increasingly relying on fragile or idiosyncratic traces that do not transfer well and thus fail to support self-consistency.
In contrast, RLTR improves transferability together with Maj@64, implying that the transfer-based reward promotes reasoning traces that are both reusable under cross-model continuation and more reliably reproducible.
Overall, these dynamics provide direct evidence that the proposed transfer reward is effectively optimized during training, shaping the generator toward robust, “transferable” reasoning prefixes that remain useful beyond the original model’s continuation.

\tau\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}

Notably, RLTR still outperforms RLVR even with the smallest receiver (Qwen-1.5B).
These results indicate that the benefits of the transfer reward are not dependent on a large receiver, although stronger receivers further improve high-K consistency.

5
Conclusion

We introduced Reinforcement Learning with Transferable Reward (RLTR), a transfer-augmented RLVR framework that optimizes
reasoning transferability
, the ability of a truncated reasoning prefix to be completed by another model to a verifiably correct answer.
By leveraging a transfer reward, RLTR encourages reasoning traces that remain reusable under truncation and cross-model continuation.
Across multiple reasoning benchmarks, RLTR improves average accuracy and sampling-based consistency (e.g., majority voting) indicating enhanced robustness of learned reasoning processes.
Moreover, RLTR significantly improves sample efficiency which reduce training steps to achieve comparable performance with baseline.
We further shows robust generalization across base models and out-of-distribution settings.
Overall, our results suggest that optimizing transferability leads to more robust LLM reasoning.

Impact Statement

This work aims to advance robust LLM reasoning by introducing transferability as a training signal.
We show that RLTR can improve not only accuracy but also reasoning consistency, while significantly enhancing sample efficiency and enabling faster learning.

Today, training and sample efficiency is an important goal to both research and industry, because of the increasing computation cost and concerns about sustainable AI development.
By improving the consistency and sample efficiency, our approach has a potential to benefit to the communities.

Furthermore, in the application side, domains that require consistency and heavily annotated training samples (e.g., Vision Language Action (VLA) model or Computer Use Agent (CUA) ) can easily adopt our method by augmenting the existing RLVR frameworks.
Consequently, by enabling reliable reaosning in such complex applications, our method may contribute to the societal/technological innovation.
