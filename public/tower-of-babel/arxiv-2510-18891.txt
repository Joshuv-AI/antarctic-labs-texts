Primes in LCM recurrences: a density theorem via companion sieves
Benoît Cloitre

Abstract
Let ( a n ) (a_{n}) be defined by a 1 = 1 a_{1}=1 and a n = a n − 1 + lcm ⁡ ( n , a n − 1 ) a_{n}=a_{n-1}+\lcm(n,a_{n-1}) . The multiplicative increments b n = n / gcd ⁡ ( n , a n − 1 ) b_{n}=n/\operatorname{gcd}(n,a_{n-1}) are conjectured to be either 1 1 or prime. Unlike Rowland’s GCD-based sequence where elementary techniques suffice, pointwise proofs for the LCM variant encounter the Linnik barrier at L = 2 L=2 . We introduce the Companion–Sieve framework. It bypasses pointwise arguments by analyzing a guaranteed factor reservoir on average. Using the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem and elementary sieve bounds, we prove unconditionally that the conjecture holds for a set of integers of asymptotic density 1 1 . A variant of the LCM recurrence reveals an inhibition mechanism that dynamically encodes the Twin Prime Conjecture, illustrating how LCM-driven recurrences connect to classical problems in multiplicative number theory.



1
Introduction: from Rowland’s simplicity to LCM’s depth

1.1
The landscape of prime-generating recurrences

The search for simple recursive formulas that generate prime numbers has captivated mathematicians for generations. In 2008, Eric Rowland
[
8
]
studied a remarkably simple recurrence:

a_{1}=7,\qquad a_{n}=a_{n-1}+\operatorname{gcd}(n,a_{n-1})\quad(n\geq 2),

(1)

with the striking property that the first differences
b_{n}:=a_{n}-a_{n-1}
are always either
1
or prime. Rowland’s result admits a complete elementary proof.

The essential mechanism in Rowland’s sequence is local stabilization: the GCD operation
\operatorname{gcd}(n,a_{n-1})
captures only the common factors between
n
and the accumulated history, which can be fully controlled by tracking residues modulo a finite set of primes. This locality makes the sequence tractable.

1.2
The LCM variant

In the same year, we proposed a superficially similar but profoundly different recurrence (
OEIS A135504
):

a_{1}=1,\qquad a_{n}=a_{n-1}+\lcm(n,a_{n-1})\quad(n\geq 2).

(2)

The multiplicative increments are defined as:

b_{n}=\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-1}}-1=\frac{\lcm(n,a_{n-1})}{a_{n-1}}=\frac{n}{\operatorname{gcd}(n,a_{n-1})}.

(3)

The first few terms of
\{b_{n}\}
are:

\boxed{2,1,2,5,1,7,1,1,5,11,1,13,1,5,1,17,1,19,1,1,11,23,\ldots}

Numerical evidence overwhelmingly suggests:

Conjecture 1.1

(Main LCM conjecture)
.

For every
n\geq 2
, we have
b_{n}\in\{1\}\cup\mathbb{P}
.

At first glance, this appears to be a simple variant of Rowland’s sequence. However, this expectation is dramatically false.

1.3
Why LCM is fundamentally harder

The replacement of GCD by LCM transforms the problem in three profound ways.

First, while Rowland’s GCD extracts common factors locally, the LCM operation causes global accumulation: every prime that appears in any
(1+b_{k})
for
k<n
becomes permanently embedded in
a_{n}
. The sequence exhibits

a_{n}=\prod_{k=2}^{n}(1+b_{k}),

making
a_{n}
a massive product that encodes the entire history.

Second, Ruiz-Cabello
[
9
]
proved that a pointwise approach to conjecture
1.1
requires showing that for every prime
p
and integer
k\geq 1
, there exist at least
k
primes
q\equiv-1\pmod{p}
with
q<p^{k+1}
. This is equivalent to the strongest form of Linnik’s theorem with constant
L=2
. The current best bound is
L\leq 5

[
10
]
, and
L=2
remains a major open problem.

Third, as we show in section
9
, the
K=2
variant of this recurrence dynamically encodes the Twin Prime Conjecture through an inhibition mechanism. This reveals that LCM-driven recurrences touch fundamental questions about prime gaps and correlations that are completely absent in Rowland’s sequence.

1.4
Main results and the companion-sieve philosophy

Faced with the Linnik barrier for pointwise proofs, we adopt a different strategy: prove the conjecture on average rather than for every individual
n
.

The key observation is that at every prime step
q\neq 3
, the sequence is forced to multiply by
(q+1)
, creating a deterministic guaranteed stock of factors:

C_{n}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq n,q\in\mathbb{P}\\
q\neq 3\end{subarray}}(q+1).

This stock grows exponentially and divides
a_{n}
. By constructing companions
B_{n}
with
b_{n}\mid B_{n}
that depend only on this guaranteed stock (not on the full recursion), we can analyze
B_{n}
using average prime distribution results like the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem.

Our main contributions are:

Theorem 1.2

(Unconditional density 1)
.

The set of integers
n
for which
b_{n}\in\{1\}\cup\mathbb{P}
has asymptotic density
1
.

Theorem 1.3

(Effective finite reduction)
.

There exists an effectively computable bound
N^{*}(P_{0})
such that for all
n\geq N^{*}(P_{0})
, any potential counterexample to the conjecture must involve only prime factors larger than
P_{0}
. For
P_{0}=10^{4}
, we compute
N^{*}(10^{4})=99460729
.

Theorem 1.4

(Twin Prime encoding)
.

The
K=2
variant exhibits an inhibition lemma: if
(p,p+2)
are twin primes and the increment at
p
equals
p
, then the increment at
p+2
must equal
1
. Under the conjecture for this variant, having infinitely many such forced-
1
increments at primes is equivalent (under an additional plausible assumption) to the Twin Prime Conjecture.

Remark 1.5

(Companion sieve vs auxiliary sieve)
.

Our “Companion–Sieve” framework is a conceptual tool for proving density results by relating a complex sequence to a simpler, analyzable one. It should not be confused with “auxiliary sieves” found in the algorithmic number theory literature, which are computational components of algorithms for calculating the prime counting function
\pi(x)
(such as the Meissel–Lehmer algorithm). The former is analytical, the latter algorithmic.

Methodological stance.

Rather than sieving directly on the raw recursion
a_{n-1}=\prod_{k<n}(1+b_{k})
—which intertwines the values
b_{k}
at composite indices with the entire past history—we pass to a deterministic, monotone majorant of its
p
-adic content. This
auxiliary sieve
viewpoint underpins the Companion–Sieve framework and is what makes Bombieri–Vinogradov usable “off the shelf” in our setting; see Remark
4.10
.

Preliminaries and standard inputs

We collect here the standard results from analytic number theory that will be used throughout the paper.

2.1
Notation

Remark 2.1

(Standard notation)
.

We use
\vp(n)
for the
p
-adic valuation of
n
. The logarithmic integral is
\Li(x)=\int_{2}^{x}\frac{dt}{\log t}
. The prime counting function in arithmetic progressions is:

\pi(x;q,a)=\#\{p\leq x:\ p\in\mathbb{P},\ p\equiv a\pmod{q}\},

with error term:

E(x;q,a)=\pi(x;q,a)-\frac{\Li(x)}{\varphi(q)}.

We write
f\ll g
(or
f=O(g)
) if
|f|\leq Cg
for some absolute constant
C
, and
f\asymp g
if both
f\ll g
and
g\ll f
hold.

2.2
Basic properties of the sequence

Lemma 2.2

(Basic bounds and parity)
.

For all
n\geq 2
, we have
1\leq b_{n}\leq n
. Moreover,
a_{n}
is even for all
n\geq 3
.

Proof.

The bound
b_{n}\leq n
follows from
b_{n}=n/\operatorname{gcd}(n,a_{n-1})\leq n
. For parity:
a_{2}=1+\lcm(2,1)=3
,
a_{3}=3+\lcm(3,3)=6
. Since
a_{3}
is even, and
a_{n}=a_{n-1}+\lcm(n,a_{n-1})
where the LCM is even for
n\geq 3
(as
a_{n-1}
is even), we get
a_{n}
even for all
n\geq 3
by induction.
∎

2.3
Prime distribution in arithmetic progressions

The Siegel–Walfisz theorem provides excellent control over primes in progressions with small moduli:

Theorem 2.3

(Siegel–Walfisz; e.g.
[
3
]
)
.

For any fixed
A>0
, there exists
C(A)>0
such that uniformly for
q\leq(\log x)^{A}
and
\operatorname{gcd}(a,q)=1
,

|E(x;q,a)|\ \ll_{A}\ x\,\exp\bigl(-C(A)\sqrt{\log x}\bigr).

2.4
Elementary sieve bounds

We need two standard sieve estimates to bound exceptional sets.

Lemma 2.4

(Mertens’ estimate; e.g.
[
6
]
Th. 2.7)
.

For
P<Y
, we have:

\sum_{P<p\leq Y}\frac{1}{p}=\log\!\left(\frac{\log Y}{\log P}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{\log P}\right).

Lemma 2.5

(Large prime squares are rare)
.

Let
Q\geq 2
. Then:

\#\{n\leq x:\ \exists\,p>Q,\ p^{2}\mid n\}\ \leq\frac{x}{Q}.

Proof.

Count integers divisible by
p^{2}
for each
p>Q
:

\sum_{p>Q}\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p^{2}}\right\rfloor\leq x\sum_{p>Q}\frac{1}{p^{2}}<x\sum_{m>Q}\frac{1}{m^{2}}\leq\frac{x}{Q}.

∎

Lemma 2.6

(Two distinct large primes are rare)
.

Let
Q=x^{1/2}/(\log x)^{B}
for fixed
B>0
. Then:

\#\{n\leq x:\ \exists\,p_{1}\neq p_{2}>Q,\ p_{1}p_{2}\mid n\}\ \ll_{B}\ x\left(\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

We only count pairs
Q<p_{1}<p_{2}\leq\sqrt{x}
. The number of such
n
is bounded by

\frac{x}{2}\left(\sum_{Q<p\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{2}.

By lemma
2.4
, this sum is
\log(\frac{\log\sqrt{x}}{\log Q})+O(1/\log Q)
. Since
\log Q=\frac{1}{2}\log x-B\log\log x
, the main term is

\log\left(\frac{1}{1-2B\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}}\right).

Using the approximation
\log(1/(1-u))\sim u
for small
u
as
x\to\infty
, this sum is
\asymp_{B}\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}
, and squaring gives the stated bound.
∎

2.5
The Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem

This is the key tool that allows us to handle medium-sized moduli on average.

Theorem 2.7

(Bombieri–Vinogradov; e.g.
[
1
,
3
]
)
.

For any
A>0
, there exists
B=B(A)>0
such that with
Q=x^{1/2}/(\log x)^{B}
,

\sum_{q\leq Q}\max_{\operatorname{gcd}(a,q)=1}\max_{y\leq x}\ |E(y;q,a)|\ \ll_{A}\ \frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}}.

3
Comparison with Ruiz-Cabello’s approach

Before presenting our framework, it is instructive to understand why previous approaches encounter the Linnik barrier and how our method differs fundamentally.

3.1
Ruiz-Cabello’s pointwise strategy

In his 2017 paper
[
9
]
, Ruiz-Cabello gave a comprehensive analysis of the sequence
(a_{n})
and established several important results:

1.

He showed that
b_{n}=n/\operatorname{gcd}(n,a_{n-1})
is always a divisor of
n
, and that
a_{n}=\prod_{k=2}^{n}(1+b_{k})
.

2.

For every prime
p\geq 5
, we have
b_{p}=p
(we reprove this as lemma
4.1
).

3.

The value
b_{n}=3
never appears in the sequence (we also reprove this as proposition
4.4
combined with parity).

3.2
The Linnik conjecture connection

Ruiz-Cabello’s key insight was to formulate a sufficient condition for the conjecture:

Proposition 3.1

(Ruiz-Cabello’s Proposition 4)
.

If for every prime
p
and every integer
k\geq 1
, there exist at least
k
primes
q<p^{k+1}
with
q\equiv-1\pmod{p}
, then conjecture
1.1
holds.

This condition requires that the first prime in the progression
p-1,2p-1,3p-1,\ldots
appears at most at the
p^{2}
-th term, i.e., before
p^{3}
. After normalization, this is precisely Linnik’s theorem with constant
L=2
and coefficient
c=1
. The current best result is
L\leq 5
due to Xylouris
[
10
]
, leaving a substantial gap. Heath-Brown
[
4
]
conjectured that
L=2
should be achievable, but this remains wide open.

3.3
Why pointwise proof is hard

To prove the conjecture pointwise for a given
n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
, one must show that each prime power
p_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}
has been "seen" at least
\alpha_{j}
times in the accumulated product
a_{n-1}
. Specifically, for each
j
, one needs at least
\alpha_{j}
primes
q<n
with
q\equiv-1\pmod{p_{j}}
and
b_{q}=q
.

For small primes
p_{j}
, the Prime Number Theorem provides enough such
q
. But for
p_{j}
close to
\sqrt{n}
with
\alpha_{j}\geq 2
, one needs multiple primes
q\equiv-1\pmod{p_{j}}
in the range
[p_{j},n-1]
. When
n\approx p_{j}^{2}
, this brings us directly to the Linnik
L=2
problem.

3.4
Our departure: average sufficiency

Our approach makes a conceptual shift: instead of proving the conjecture for every
n
, we prove it holds for a set of
n
of density
1
. The essential steps are:

1.

Build companions
B_{n}
with
b_{n}\mid B_{n}
that depend only on the guaranteed stock
C_{n-1}
, not on the full sequence.

2.

Show
B_{n}\in\{1,\text{prime}\}
on average using Bombieri–Vinogradov to control the average distribution of primes in progressions, rather than requiring pointwise control.

3.

Accept exceptions for a sparse set of
n
(size
o(x)
) where medium or large primes create unusual configurations.

This avoids the Linnik barrier because we never need to prove that every progression
p-1,2p-1,\ldots
contains a prime quickly. We only need the average behavior to be good, which Bombieri–Vinogradov provides. Table

4
The Companion–Sieve framework

An auxiliary sieve viewpoint.

Heuristically, we sieve not on the raw recursion but on a
deterministic majorant
of its
p
-adic content. Concretely, we pass to the guaranteed stock

C_{n-1}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq n-1\\
q\in\mathbb{P},\ q\neq 3\end{subarray}}(q+1),

and then to the one-hit companion

B^{(1)}_{n}=\prod_{p}p^{\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)},\qquad\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)=\max\{0,\,\vp(n)-\pi(n-1;p,-1)\}.

This turns the demand–supply balance into a
monotone
object, precisely the form in which Bombieri–Vinogradov (in its
\max_{y\leq x}
version) applies directly, yielding the density-1 result and, for small moduli, the effective checkpoint bound
N^{*}(P_{0})
.

4.1
Prime steps and the guaranteed stock

The foundation of our method is a simple but crucial observation: at prime indices (except
3
), the sequence is forced to accumulate specific factors.

Lemma 4.1

(Prime steps)
.

We have
b_{2}=2
,
b_{3}=1
. For every prime
q\geq 5
, we have
\operatorname{gcd}(q,a_{q-1})=1
, hence
b_{q}=q
.

Proof.

Assume
q\geq 5
is prime and
q\mid a_{q-1}=\prod_{k=2}^{q-1}(1+b_{k})
. Then
q\mid(1+b_{k})
for some
k<q
. Since
1\leq b_{k}\leq k<q
and
q
is prime, we need
b_{k}=q-1
, which forces
k=q-1
.

For this to happen, we would need
b_{q-1}=q-1
, which means
(q-1)\mid(q-1)
and
\operatorname{gcd}(q-1,a_{q-2})=1
. But
q-1
is even (since
q\geq 5
is odd), and
a_{q-2}
is even for
q-2\geq 3
(i.e.,
q\geq 5
) by lemma
2.2
. This contradicts
\operatorname{gcd}(q-1,a_{q-2})=1
.

Therefore,
\operatorname{gcd}(q,a_{q-1})=1
, giving
b_{q}=q/\operatorname{gcd}(q,a_{q-1})=q
.
∎

This lemma tells us that at every prime step
q\neq 3
, the multiplier
(1+b_{q})=(q+1)
is automatically inserted into
a_{q}
. This creates a deterministic reservoir of factors that grows independently of the values
\{b_{k}:2\leq k<q,k\text{ composite}\}
.

Definition 4.2

(Guaranteed stock)
.

We define the guaranteed stock as:

C_{n}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq n\\
q\in\mathbb{P},\ q\neq 3\end{subarray}}(q+1),\qquad C_{1}:=1.

Example 4.3
.

Let’s compute the first few values:

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=(2+1)=3,

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=3\quad\text{(exclude $q=3$)},

\displaystyle C_{4}

\displaystyle=3,

\displaystyle C_{5}

\displaystyle=3\cdot(5+1)=18,

\displaystyle C_{7}

\displaystyle=18\cdot(7+1)=144=2^{4}\cdot 3^{2},

\displaystyle C_{11}

\displaystyle=144\cdot(11+1)=1728=2^{6}\cdot 3^{3}.

Notice how
C_{n}
accumulates factors of
2
and
3
heavily, since every
(q+1)
for odd
q
contributes a factor of
2
.

Proposition 4.4

(Stock divides sequence)
.

For every
n\geq 2
, we have
C_{n-1}\mid a_{n-1}
.

Proof.

By lemma
4.1
, for each prime
q\leq n-1
with
q\neq 3
, the factor
(1+b_{q})=(q+1)
appears in the product
a_{n-1}=\prod_{k=2}^{n-1}(1+b_{k})
. Taking the product over all such
q
yields
C_{n-1}\mid a_{n-1}
.
∎

4.2
Companions: replacing the full recursion with the stock

The idea now is to construct a quantity
B_{n}
that satisfies
b_{n}\mid B_{n}
(so if
B_{n}
is
1
or prime, then so is
b_{n}
) and depends only on
n
and the guaranteed stock
C_{n-1}
, not on the full sequence
(a_{k})_{k<n}
.

Definition 4.5

(Product companion)
.

The product companion is defined as:

B^{(P)}_{n}:=\frac{n}{\operatorname{gcd}(n,C_{n-1})}.

Since
C_{n-1}\mid a_{n-1}
, we have
\operatorname{gcd}(n,C_{n-1})\mid\operatorname{gcd}(n,a_{n-1})
. Therefore,
b_{n}=n/\operatorname{gcd}(n,a_{n-1})
divides
B^{(P)}_{n}=n/\operatorname{gcd}(n,C_{n-1})
. The companion
B^{(P)}_{n}
is a relaxation of
b_{n}
that uses only the guaranteed part of
a_{n-1}
.

However,
B^{(P)}_{n}
is still difficult to analyze directly because the prime factorization of
C_{n-1}
is complex. We introduce a further relaxation based on counting primes in progressions rather than tracking the exact product.

Definition 4.6

(1-hit stock and deficit)
.

For a prime
p
and
m\geq 1
, define the 1-hit stock:

S^{(1)}_{p}(m):=\#\{\,q\leq m:\ q\in\mathbb{P},\ q\neq 3,\ q\equiv-1\!\!\pmod{p}\,\}.

This counts primes
q
(other than
3
) up to
m
that contribute at least one factor of
p
to
C_{m}
via
(q+1)
.

The 1-hit deficit at
n
is:

\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n):=\max\{0,\ \vp(n)-S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)\},

measuring the shortfall of
p
-factors in the stock relative to the demand
\vp(n)
.

The 1-hit companion is:

B^{(1)}_{n}:=\prod_{p}p^{\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)}.

Remark 4.7

(Exclusion of
q=3
)
.

We exclude
q=3
from
S^{(1)}_{p}(m)
since
b_{3}=1
(by lemma
4.1
), so the prime
3
does not contribute to the guaranteed stock. For
p=2
, this exclusion is harmless as the remaining odd primes (
q\geq 5
) provide sufficient supply.

If the stock
C_{n-1}
contains at least
k
factors of
p
(because at least
k
primes
q\equiv-1\pmod{p}
have occurred), then those factors are available to cancel the factors of
p
in
n
when computing the GCD. The deficit
\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)
measures how many factors of
p
in
n
are not covered by the stock.

Lemma 4.8

(Divisibility chain)
.

For all
n\geq 2
, we have:

b_{n}\mid B^{(P)}_{n}\mid B^{(1)}_{n}.

Proof.

From proposition
4.4
,
C_{n-1}\mid a_{n-1}
. Therefore
\operatorname{gcd}(n,C_{n-1})\mid\operatorname{gcd}(n,a_{n-1})
, and taking reciprocals and multiplying by
n
gives
b_{n}\mid B^{(P)}_{n}
.

For the second divisibility, observe that for any prime
p
, each prime
q\leq n-1
with
q\equiv-1\pmod{p}
contributes at least one factor of
p
to
(q+1)
, and hence to
C_{n-1}
. Thus
\vp(C_{n-1})\geq S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)
. The
p
-adic valuation of
B^{(P)}_{n}
is:

\displaystyle\vp(B^{(P)}_{n})

\displaystyle=\vp(n)-\vp(\operatorname{gcd}(n,C_{n-1}))

\displaystyle\leq\vp(n)-\min(\vp(n),S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1))

\displaystyle=\max(0,\vp(n)-S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1))=\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)=\vp(B^{(1)}_{n}).

Since this holds for all primes
p
, we have
B^{(P)}_{n}\mid B^{(1)}_{n}
.
∎

4.3
The deficit criterion

The 1-hit companion
B^{(1)}_{n}
is a product of prime powers, one for each prime
p
, with exponent
\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)
. The key observation is:

Theorem 4.9

(Deficit criterion)
.

If
\sum_{p}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)\leq 1
, then
B^{(1)}_{n}\in\{1\}\cup\mathbb{P}
. Consequently,
b_{n}\in\{1\}\cup\mathbb{P}
by the divisibility chain.

Proof.

If the total deficit is
0
, then
B^{(1)}_{n}=1
. If the total deficit is
1
, then exactly one prime
p
has
\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)=1
and all others have deficit
0
, so
B^{(1)}_{n}=p
. In both cases,
B^{(1)}_{n}\in\{1,p\}
, and since
b_{n}\mid B^{(1)}_{n}
, we also have
b_{n}\in\{1\}\cup\mathbb{P}
.
∎

To prove the conjecture for most
n
, we will show that
\sum_{p}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)\leq 1
for all but
o(x)
integers
n\leq x
.

Remark 4.10

(Why a Companion–Sieve framework?)
.

A direct
p
-adic analysis of
a_{n-1}=\prod_{k<n}(1+b_{k})
is ill-suited to uniform estimates: for composite
k
, the values
b_{k}
depend on the full past, and higher powers
p^{\nu}\mid(q+1)
inject
sporadic

p
-adic spikes into
\vp(a_{n-1})
. By contrast, our guaranteed stock is deterministic and yields the monotone inequality

\vp(C_{n-1})\ \geq\ \#\{\,q\leq n-1:\ q\in\mathbb{P},\ q\equiv-1\!\!\!\pmod{p}\,\}\ =\ \pi(n-1;p,-1),

so that passing to
B^{(1)}_{n}
is a
relaxation
which preserves
b_{n}\mid B^{(1)}_{n}
while turning the analysis into a BV-ready counting problem. This is the structural reason why the Companion–Sieve both delivers density 1 and supports the effective checkpoint bound
N^{*}(P_{0})
, while canonically isolating the residual square-type obstruction.
A “direct” sieve would require controlling
\vp\!\left(\prod_{k<n}(1+b_{k})\right)
uniformly in
n
, including the rare but influential events
p^{\nu}\mid(q+1)
for
\nu\geq 2
, and doing so
without
the monotonicity that
\pi(\,\cdot\,;p,-1)
provides. One can still use large-sieve technology, but the bookkeeping becomes substantially heavier and uniformity in
y\leq x
is delicate. The Companion–Sieve precisely avoids these pitfalls by trading the exact
p
-adic content for a deterministic, monotone majorant on which Bombieri–Vinogradov applies cleanly.

5
Intuition for the three-regime analysis

Before diving into the technical proof of the density-1 result, we explain the conceptual structure of the argument. We will partition the primes into three regimes based on their size relative to
x
, and analyze how each regime contributes to the total deficit.

5.1
Setup: three scales of primes

Fix a large parameter
x
and consider integers
n\in[\sqrt{x},x]
(ignoring the negligible range
n<\sqrt{x}
). We define:

\displaystyle P_{\rm small}

\displaystyle:=(\log x)^{2B+10},

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sqrt{x}}{(\log x)^{B}},

where
B=B(10)
is the constant from the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem (theorem
2.7
) with
A=10
.

This creates three regimes: small primes
p\leq P_{\rm small}
(polynomially small in
\log x
), medium primes
P_{\rm small}<p\leq Q
(from powers of
\log x
up to nearly
\sqrt{x}
), and large primes
p>Q
(exceeding the Bombieri–Vinogradov threshold).

5.2
Regime 1: small primes

For small primes
p\leq P_{\rm small}
, the Siegel–Walfisz theorem (theorem
2.3
) gives individual control:

S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)\gg\frac{n}{p\log n}.

The supply grows like
\frac{\sqrt{x}}{(\log x)^{2B+11}}
, which is much larger than the demand
\vp(n)\ll\log x
. Thus supply dominates demand for all small primes, so
\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)=0
.

5.3
Regime 2: medium primes

For medium primes
P_{\rm small}<p\leq Q
, we use the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem for average control. Let
\varepsilon(x)=1/(\log x)^{5}
.

We identify two types of exceptional integers
n
:

(a)

Integers divisible by bad moduli:
n
divisible by some
p
where
|E(y;p,-1)|>\varepsilon(x)\frac{\Li(x)}{p-1}
. By Bombieri–Vinogradov, the sum of errors is bounded by
x/(\log x)^{10}
, implying
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm bad}}\frac{1}{p}\ll(\log x)^{-4}
. The set
\mathcal{E}_{\rm bad}(x)
of such
n
has size
o(x)
.

(b)

Integers with deficit from good moduli:
n
divisible only by good moduli, but still having a deficit. This means
S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)<\vp(n)
. Using the lower bound for good moduli:

\frac{\Li(n-1)}{p-1}-\varepsilon(x)\frac{\Li(x)}{p-1}<\vp(n).

The error term
\varepsilon(x)\Li(x)\asymp x/(\log x)^{6}
can be much larger than the main term
\Li(n-1)
if
n
is small. However, the inequality implies
\Li(n-1)\ll x/(\log x)^{6}
, which forces
n\ll x/(\log x)^{5}
. The set
\mathcal{E}_{\rm good}(x)
of such
n
is also
o(x)
.

For all but
o(x)
integers
n
, all medium prime divisors have deficit
0
.

5.4
Regime 3: large primes

For large primes
p>Q
, we use elementary sieve bounds. The set of
n
divisible by
p^{2}
(
p>Q
) has size
O(x/Q)=o(x)
, and the set of
n
divisible by
p_{1}p_{2}
(
p_{1},p_{2}>Q
) has size
o(x)
. For all but
o(x)
integers
n
, we have at most one large prime divisor, appearing to exponent
1
. This contributes at most deficit
1
.

5.5
Putting it together

For all but
o(x)
integers
n\leq x
, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{p}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq P_{\rm small}}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)+\sum_{P_{\rm small}<p\leq Q}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)+\sum_{p>Q}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)

\displaystyle=0+0+(\leq 1)\leq 1.

By the deficit criterion (theorem
4.9
),
b_{n}\in\{1\}\cup\mathbb{P}
for all such
n
.

6
Proof of the density theorem

We now prove our main result, following the intuition developed in the previous section.

Theorem 6.1

(Unconditional density 1)
.

The set of integers
n
for which
b_{n}\in\{1\}\cup\mathbb{P}
has asymptotic density
1
.

Proof.

We fix
A=10
in the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem and let
B=B(10)
denote the corresponding constant. Throughout, we consider only integers
n
in the range
[x^{1/2},x]
, since those below
x^{1/2}
form a negligible set of size
o(x)
. For such
n
, we have
\log n\asymp\log x
.

The proof divides naturally into three regimes based on prime size. We define two thresholds:

P_{\rm small}=(\log x)^{2B+10},\qquad Q=\frac{x^{1/2}}{(\log x)^{B}},

and set
\varepsilon(x)=1/(\log x)^{5}
as our error tolerance.

Remark 6.2
.

The proof of lemma
6.4
below requires
B\geq 3
. This is satisfied: standard proofs of the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem (see for instance chapter 28 of Davenport’s book
[
3
]
) show that one can take
B(A)=A+5/2
when using the strongest known zero-free regions for Dirichlet
L
-functions. Thus
B(10)=12.5
, well above the threshold.

Small primes: abundance from Siegel–Walfisz

Consider first a prime
p\leq P_{\rm small}
. Since this modulus is at most a power of
\log x
, the Siegel–Walfisz theorem provides excellent pointwise control over the distribution of primes in the progression
p-1,2p-1,3p-1,\ldots
More precisely, we have

S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)=\pi(n-1;p,p-1)=\frac{\Li(n-1)}{p-1}+O\!\left(n\,e^{-c\sqrt{\log n}}\right).

The main term behaves like
n/(p\log n)
. Since
n\geq x^{1/2}
and
p\leq P_{\rm small}=(\log x)^{2B+10}
, we obtain

S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)\gg\frac{x^{1/2}}{(\log x)^{2B+11}}.

Meanwhile, the demand is at most
\vp(n)\leq\frac{\log n}{\log p}<2\log x
. For sufficiently large
x
, the exponential growth of the numerator dominates the polynomial growth of the denominator, so the supply vastly exceeds the demand. More concretely:

S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)\geq\frac{x^{1/2}}{4P_{\rm small}\log x}\gg\log x\geq\vp(n).

Therefore, for all primes
p\leq P_{\rm small}
and all
n\in[x^{1/2},x]
, the deficit vanishes:
\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)=0
.

Medium primes: controlling the average

For primes in the range
(P_{\rm small},Q]
, we can no longer control each progression individually. However, the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem guarantees that the average behavior is excellent. We must separate two types of exceptional configurations.

Lemma 6.3

(Sparsity of bad moduli)
.

Define a prime
p\in(P_{\rm small},Q]
to be
bad
if the error in the prime number theorem for the progression
p-1\pmod{p}
is unusually large, meaning

\max_{y\leq x}|E(y;p,-1)|>\varepsilon(x)\,\frac{\Li(x)}{p-1}.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\rm bad}(x)
denote the set of such primes, and let
\mathcal{E}_{\rm bad}(x)
be the set of integers
n\leq x
divisible by at least one bad prime. Then
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm bad}(x)}\frac{1}{p}\ll(\log x)^{-4}
and consequently
|\mathcal{E}_{\rm bad}(x)|=o(x)
.

Proof.

By the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem applied with
A=10
, we know that

\sum_{p\leq Q}\max_{y\leq x}|E(y;p,-1)|\ \ll\ \frac{x}{(\log x)^{10}}.

For each bad prime
p
, the error exceeds the threshold
\varepsilon(x)\frac{\Li(x)}{p-1}
. Summing the thresholds over all bad primes gives an upper bound on the left side above:

\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm bad}(x)}\varepsilon(x)\,\frac{\Li(x)}{p-1}\ \leq\ \sum_{p\leq Q}\max_{y\leq x}|E(y;p,-1)|\ \ll\ \frac{x}{(\log x)^{10}}.

Since
\varepsilon(x)=1/(\log x)^{5}
and
\Li(x)\asymp x/\log x
, the left side simplifies to

\frac{x}{(\log x)^{6}}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm bad}(x)}\frac{1}{p}.

Comparing with the bound
x/(\log x)^{10}
yields

\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm bad}(x)}\frac{1}{p}\ \ll\ (\log x)^{-4}.

Finally, any integer divisible by a bad prime
p
contributes at most
x/p
to
|\mathcal{E}_{\rm bad}(x)|
, so

|\mathcal{E}_{\rm bad}(x)|\leq x\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm bad}(x)}\frac{1}{p}\ll\frac{x}{(\log x)^{4}}=o(x).\qed

What about integers
n
divisible only by good moduli? It turns out that if such an
n
has a deficit from some good modulus
p
, then
n
must be unusually small.

Lemma 6.4

(Rarity of deficits from good moduli)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\rm good}(x)
denote the set of integers
n\in[x^{1/2},x]\setminus\mathcal{E}_{\rm bad}(x)
such that there exists a prime
p\in(P_{\rm small},Q]
with
p\mid n
and
\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)>0
. Then
|\mathcal{E}_{\rm good}(x)|\ll x/(\log x)^{5}=o(x)
.

Proof.

Suppose
n
belongs to
\mathcal{E}_{\rm good}(x)
. Then
n
is divisible by some good modulus
p
for which the supply falls short of the demand. Write
k=\vp(n)
, so that
S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)<k
.

Since
p
is good rather than bad, the error term in the prime number theorem satisfies
|E(n-1;p,-1)|\leq\varepsilon(x)\frac{\Li(x)}{p-1}
. Using the lower bound

S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)\geq\frac{\Li(n-1)}{p-1}-\varepsilon(x)\frac{\Li(x)}{p-1},

together with
S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)<k
, we obtain

\Li(n-1)<k(p-1)+\varepsilon(x)\Li(x).

Now we estimate the right side. The first term is at most

k(p-1)\ll(\log x)\cdot Q=\frac{x^{1/2}}{(\log x)^{B-1}},

since
k\leq\log n/\log 2<2\log x
and
p\leq Q
. The second term is

\varepsilon(x)\Li(x)\asymp\frac{x}{(\log x)^{6}}.

Here is where we use
B\geq 3
. When
B\geq 3
, we have
(\log x)^{7-B}\ll x^{1/2}
for large
x
, so the term
x/(\log x)^{6}
dominates
x^{1/2}/(\log x)^{B-1}
. Thus

\Li(n-1)\ll\frac{x}{(\log x)^{6}}.

Since
\Li(n-1)\asymp n/\log x
for
n\in[x^{1/2},x]
, this forces
n\ll x/(\log x)^{5}
. In other words, all elements of
\mathcal{E}_{\rm good}(x)
lie in the small interval
[x^{1/2},x/(\log x)^{5}]
, which has length
o(x)
.
∎

Combining the two lemmas, we see that the set
\mathcal{E}_{\rm med}(x):=\mathcal{E}_{\rm bad}(x)\cup\mathcal{E}_{\rm good}(x)
has size
o(x)
. For any integer
n\in[x^{1/2},x]\setminus\mathcal{E}_{\rm med}(x)
, all medium prime divisors have zero deficit.

Large primes: elementary bounds

For primes exceeding
Q
, we rely on simple combinatorial sieves. An integer
n\leq x
can have at most one prime factor larger than
\sqrt{x}
, so we need only worry about primes in
(Q,\sqrt{x}]
appearing with multiplicity at least two, or about products of two distinct such primes.

By lemma
2.5
, the set

\mathcal{E}_{\rm large}^{(1)}(x):=\{n\leq x:\ \exists\,p>Q,\ p^{2}\mid n\}

has cardinality at most
x/Q=o(x)
.

Similarly, by lemma
2.6
, the set

\mathcal{E}_{\rm large}^{(2)}(x):=\{n\leq x:\ \exists\,p_{1}\neq p_{2}>Q,\ p_{1}p_{2}\mid n\}

has size
\ll x(\frac{\log\log x}{\log x})^{2}=o(x)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\rm large}(x):=\mathcal{E}_{\rm large}^{(1)}(x)\cup\mathcal{E}_{\rm large}^{(2)}(x)
. For any
n\notin\mathcal{E}_{\rm large}(x)
, the factorization of
n
involves at most one prime larger than
Q
, and that prime appears to the first power only. Since
\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)\leq\vp(n)
, we conclude that

\sum_{p>Q}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)\leq 1.

Assembling the pieces

Define the total exceptional set as

\mathcal{E}(x):=\{n<x^{1/2}\}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\rm med}(x)\cup\mathcal{E}_{\rm large}(x).

We have shown that
|\mathcal{E}(x)|=o(x)
. For any integer
n\in[1,x]\setminus\mathcal{E}(x)
, the total deficit decomposes as

\sum_{p}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)=\sum_{p\leq P_{\rm small}}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)+\sum_{P_{\rm small}<p\leq Q}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)+\sum_{p>Q}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n).

The first sum vanishes by our analysis of small primes. The second vanishes because
n\notin\mathcal{E}_{\rm med}(x)
. The third is at most
1
because
n\notin\mathcal{E}_{\rm large}(x)
. Thus

\sum_{p}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)\leq 0+0+1=1.

By the deficit criterion (theorem
4.9
), we have
b_{n}\in\{1\}\cup\mathbb{P}
for all such
n
. Since the set of exceptions has size
o(x)
, the proportion of integers up to
x
satisfying the conjecture is
1-o(1)
, which tends to
1
as
x\to\infty
.
∎

7
Effective finite reduction: stable power checkpoints

The density-1 result is strong, but it leaves open the behavior for small
n
. In this section, we develop an effective method to reduce the conjecture to a finite computation.

7.1
The checkpoint concept

The idea is to find, for each small prime
p
, a threshold
t_{0}(p)
beyond which the stock
S^{(1)}_{p}(\cdot)
is guaranteed to be abundant for all powers of
p
.

Definition 7.1

(Stable power checkpoint)
.

For a prime
p
, let
t_{0}(p)
be the least integer
t\geq 1
such that:

S^{(1)}_{p}(p^{k}-1)\ \geq\ k\qquad\text{for all}\ k\geq t.

For a threshold
P_{0}\geq 2
, the checkpoint bound is:

N^{*}(P_{0}):=\max_{p\leq P_{0}}p^{t_{0}(p)}.

Once we reach
n\geq p^{t_{0}(p)}
, every power
p^{k}
(for
k\geq t_{0}(p)
) has enough primes
q\equiv-1\pmod{p}
below
p^{k}
to cover all
k
factors of
p
. The value
N^{*}(P_{0})
is the maximum such threshold over all primes up to
P_{0}
.

Lemma 7.2

(Existence and effectiveness)
.

For each fixed prime
p
, the value
t_{0}(p)
exists and is effectively computable.

Proof.

By the Prime Number Theorem in arithmetic progressions, for fixed
p
:

S^{(1)}_{p}(p^{k}-1)=\pi(p^{k}-1;p,p-1)\sim\frac{p^{k}}{(p-1)k\log p}.

The ratio
\frac{p^{k}}{(p-1)k\log p}/k=\frac{p^{k}}{(p-1)k^{2}\log p}
tends to infinity as
k\to\infty
for fixed
p
. Thus,
S^{(1)}_{p}(p^{k}-1)\geq k
for all sufficiently large
k
, proving existence.

For effectiveness, use an explicit form of the Prime Number Theorem for the fixed modulus
p
(see
[
3
, Ch. 20]
), which gives computable error terms allowing verification of the inequality for all
k\geq t_{0}(p)
.
∎

Theorem 7.3

(Small moduli vanish beyond
N^{*}(P_{0})
)
.

Fix
P_{0}
. For every
n\geq N^{*}(P_{0})
and every prime
p\leq P_{0}
, we have
\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)=0
.

Proof.

Let
k=\vp(n)
. We consider two cases.

If
k\leq t_{0}(p)
, then since
n\geq N^{*}(P_{0})\geq p^{t_{0}(p)}
, we have
n-1\geq p^{t_{0}(p)}-1
. The supply is

S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)\geq S^{(1)}_{p}(p^{t_{0}(p)}-1)\geq t_{0}(p)\geq k.

If
k>t_{0}(p)
, then since
p^{k}\mid n
, we have
n\geq p^{k}
, so
n-1\geq p^{k}-1
. By the stability property of
t_{0}(p)
:

S^{(1)}_{p}(n-1)\geq S^{(1)}_{p}(p^{k}-1)\geq k.

In both cases, supply exceeds or equals demand, so
\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)=0
.
∎

7.2
Numerical results

We implemented the computation of
t_{0}(p)
for all primes
p\leq 10^{4}
using PARI/GP (see appendix
A
).

Remark 7.4

(Computational verification)
.

The implementation relies on the correctness of PARI/GP’s prime counting functions. To ensure reliability, we cross-verified the computation of
S^{(1)}_{p}(X)
against known prime tables for small
p
and
X
. We manually verified the values of
t_{0}(p)
for several small primes:
t_{0}(2)=3
,
t_{0}(5)=2
,
t_{0}(7)=2
, which matched the computational output. Additionally, we computed
t_{0}(9973)=3
for a prime close to
10^{4}
, confirming the bound
t_{0}(p)\leq 3
extends throughout the range.

The results show that for all
p\leq 10^{4}
, we have
t_{0}(p)\leq 3
, with the maximum attained at
p=463
with
t_{0}(463)=3
. This gives:

N^{*}(10^{4})=(463)^{3}=99460729.

This bound is large but finite. It means that for
n\geq 99460729
, no prime
p\leq 10^{4}
contributes any deficit. Any potential counterexample beyond this bound must involve only primes larger than
10^{4}
.

8
The residual obstruction is thin

Combining the density-1 result with the checkpoint reduction, we obtain a precise characterization of where counterexamples (if any) can occur.

Theorem 8.1

(Thin residual obstruction)
.

Fix
P_{0}\geq 2
and let
N^{*}=N^{*}(P_{0})
. For large
x
, except for a set of integers of size
o(x)
, any integer
n\in[N^{*},x]
satisfies
\sum_{p}\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)\leq 1
. The exceptional set is contained in the union of:

(a)

integers
n
with a prime factor
p>Q
such that
p^{2}\mid n
,

(b)

integers
n
divisible by two distinct primes
p_{1},p_{2}>Q
,

(c)

integers
n
in the exceptional set
\mathcal{E}_{\rm med}(x)
from regime 2,

where
Q=x^{1/2}/(\log x)^{B}
is the Bombieri–Vinogradov threshold.

Proof.

For
n\geq N^{*}
, theorem
7.3
ensures
\delta^{(1)}_{p}(n)=0
for all
p\leq P_{0}
.

The analysis from section
6
shows that deficits from medium moduli (
p\in(P_{0},Q]
) occur only for
n\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm med}(x)
, which has size
o(x)
. Deficits from large moduli (
p>Q
) sum to more than
1
only for configurations (a) and (b), which have size
o(x)
by lemmas
2.5
and
2.6
.

Therefore, the residual obstruction for
n\in[N^{*},x]
is confined to a set of size
o(x)
.
∎

For
n\geq N^{*}(10^{4})\approx 10^{8}
, the only way to violate the conjecture is to have an unusual configuration of large primes (exceeding
\sqrt{x}/(\log x)^{B}
). Such configurations are provably rare, forming a set of density
0
.

9
A
K=2
variant and the Twin Prime connection

We now explore a variant of the LCM recurrence that reveals a beautiful dynamical link to the Twin Prime Conjecture.

9.1
The
K=2
recurrence

Consider the recurrence defined by
x_{1}=1
and:

x_{n}=2x_{n-1}+\lcm(n,x_{n-1}),\qquad c_{n}=\frac{n}{\operatorname{gcd}(n,x_{n-1})}.

This is the
K=2
analogue of our original sequence (which corresponds to
K=1
). The Companion–Sieve framework adapts naturally: we track primes
q\equiv-2\pmod{p}
instead of
q\equiv-1\pmod{p}
, and the density-1 result carries through with minor modifications.

9.2
The inhibition mechanism

The following simple lemma reveals a surprising constraint:

Lemma 9.1

(Inhibition)
.

Let
(p,q)
be twin primes with
q=p+2
. If
c_{p}=p
, then
c_{q}=1
.

Proof.

The recurrence can be written as
x_{n}=x_{n-1}(2+c_{n})
. If
c_{p}=p
, then:

x_{p}=(2+p)\,x_{p-1}=(p+2)\,x_{p-1}=q\,x_{p-1}.

This shows
q\mid x_{p}
. Since the sequence is multiplicative,
x_{p}\mid x_{q-1}
, hence
q\mid x_{q-1}
. Then:

c_{q}=\frac{q}{\operatorname{gcd}(q,x_{q-1})}=\frac{q}{q}=1.

∎

Whenever the increment at
p
equals
p
for a twin prime
p
, the system is forced to produce increment
1
at
p+2
. This creates a dynamical inhibition where twin primes cannot both have maximal increments.

9.3
Connection to Twin Prime Conjecture

Theorem 9.2

(Twin Prime equivalence under hypotheses C1 and C2)
.

Assume
c_{n}\in\{1\}\cup\mathbb{P}
for all
n
(hypothesis C1). Further assume that
c_{p}=p
occurs for infinitely many primes
p
that are the smaller member of a twin prime pair (hypothesis C2). Then
c_{q}=1
for infinitely many primes
q
if and only if there are infinitely many twin primes.

Proof.

Suppose there are infinitely many twin prime pairs
(p,p+2)
. By hypothesis C2,
c_{p}=p
occurs for infinitely many such
p
. Then lemma
9.1
produces infinitely many primes
q=p+2
with
c_{q}=1
.

Conversely, suppose
c_{q}=1
for infinitely many primes
q\geq 5
. For such
q
, we have
q\mid x_{q-1}
, so:

q\mid x_{q-1}=\prod_{k=2}^{q-1}(2+c_{k}).

Thus,
q\mid(2+c_{k})
for some
k<q
. Since
c_{k}\leq k<q
and
q
is prime, we need
2+c_{k}=q
, i.e.,
c_{k}=q-2
.

By definition,
c_{k}\mid k
, so
(q-2)\mid k
. Since
k<q
, the only possibility is
k=q-2
. Under hypothesis C1,
c_{q-2}\in\{1,\mathbb{P}\}
, so
q-2
is prime (since
q\geq 5
). If this occurs for infinitely many
q
, then there are infinitely many twin prime pairs
(q-2,q)
.
∎

Remark 9.3

(Plausibility of hypothesis C2)
.

Hypothesis C2 is strong, as it combines the existence of infinitely many twin primes with a specific behavior of the sequence
(c_{n})
at those primes. However, it is heuristically plausible. The density-1 result (adapted for
K=2
) suggests that
c_{n}
is usually prime or
1
. For a prime
p
,
c_{p}=p
occurs if
\operatorname{gcd}(p,x_{p-1})=1
. While this is not guaranteed (unlike the
K=1
case where
b_{p}=p
for
p\geq 5
), we expect
x_{p-1}
to be free of the factor
p

Conclusion and open directions

We have established three main results. First, unconditionally using only the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem and elementary sieves, the LCM prime-increment conjecture holds for a set of integers of asymptotic density
1
. Second, for
n\geq N^{*}(P_{0})
, any potential counterexample involves only prime factors exceeding
P_{0}
; for
P_{0}=10^{4}
, we computed
N^{*}(10^{4})=99460729
. Third, the
K=2
variant exhibits an inhibition mechanism that dynamically encodes the Twin Prime Conjecture.

The Companion–Sieve framework transforms a pointwise problem (blocked by Linnik’s theorem) into an average-case analysis controllable by standard tools. This demonstrates the power of density methods in additive-multiplicative number theory.

Several natural questions remain open. Can density-1 be improved to "for all
n
"? This likely requires breakthroughs on Linnik’s constant or new methods entirely. Can the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture (an extension of Bombieri–Vinogradov to larger
Q
) be used to further thin the residual obstruction? Our numerical data suggests
t_{0}(p)\leq 3
for all
p\leq 10^{4}
; is there a universal constant
T
such that
t_{0}(p)\leq T
for all primes
p
? Proving this would dramatically simplify the checkpoint analysis. Do the
K=3,K=4,\ldots
variants encode other classical conjectures (prime triplets, Sophie Germain primes, etc.)? Can the framework extend to sequences
a_{n}=K\cdot a_{n-1}+\lcm(n,a_{n-1})
with varying initial conditions? Can we prove that infinitely many primes appear in
\{b_{n}\}
, analogous to Chamizo–Raboso–Ruiz-Cabello’s result for Rowland’s sequence
[
2
]
?

This work illustrates a recurring theme in analytic number theory: pointwise problems are often intractable, but average versions may be accessible. Examples include the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem itself, twin prime conjecture versus twin prime asymptotic, and Goldbach conjecture versus Vinogradov’s theorem. Our Companion–Sieve framework adds to this tradition by showing that even elementary-looking recurrences like the LCM sequence can benefit from averaging techniques.

The journey from Rowland’s simple GCD recurrence to the LCM variant reveals how small changes in definition can lead to profound differences in mathematical depth. While GCD isolates and stabilizes, LCM accumulates and connects, touching deep structures in prime distribution. The fact that this behavior encodes classical conjectures like the Twin Prime Conjecture suggests that dynamical systems built from arithmetic operations may offer fresh perspectives on longstanding problems.

Methodologically, the Companion–Sieve can be read as an auxiliary sieve: it replaces a history-dependent, non-monotone
p
-adic recursion by a deterministic and monotone demand–supply balance aligned with Bombieri–Vinogradov. This is not merely cosmetic: it is what drives both the density-1 theorem and the effective small-modulus elimination via stable checkpoints.

\\ Count Sˆ{(1)}_p(X) = #{q<=X : q prime, q != 3, q == -1 mod p}
S1p_at(p, X) =
{
if (X < p-1, return(0));
my(cnt = 0);
forstep(q = p-1, X, p,

if (q >= 2 && isprime(q), cnt++);
);

}

\\ Compute stable checkpoint index t0(p)
\\ We check for stability over a window (here, size 3) to be robust
\\ against fluctuations near the threshold, leveraging the fact that
\\ Sˆ{(1)}_p grows much faster than k.
t0_of_p(p, Kmax = 50) =
{
my(t = 1, stable = 0);
while (t <= Kmax,
my(S = S1p_at(p, pˆt - 1));
if (S >= t,
stable++;
if (stable >= 3, return(t-2));
,
stable = 0
);
t++;
);
error("No stability found up to Kmax for p=", p);
}

\\ Compute Nˆ*(P_0)
Nstar(P0) =
{

forprime(p = 2, P0,

if (pˆt > max_val,
max_val = pˆt;
max_p = p;
max_t = t;
);
);
return([max_val, max_p, max_t]);
}
