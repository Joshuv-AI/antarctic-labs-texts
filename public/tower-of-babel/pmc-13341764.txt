Quantitative analysis of genetically modified maize based on terahertz spectroscopy and DeepSpectra models
Authors: Yuying Jiang, Xixi Wen, Hongyi Ge, Hao Chen, Mengdie Jiang, Heng Wang, Shilei Wei, Jiahui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the widespread cultivation of genetically modified (GM) maize, accurate detection of GM components has become critical. This study developed three end-to-end DeepSpectra models (V1–V3) to classify GA21 maize at six GM concentrations (blank, levels 1–5). Spectral data were acquired using a terahertz time-domain spectroscopy system. Outliers were removed using an isolation forest, and the spectral data were preprocessed with Savitzky-Golay smoothing, standard normal variate transformation, baseline correction, first derivative (FD), and second derivative. Three comparison models were constructed, a support vector machine (SPA-GS-SVM) and a random forest (SPA-GS-RF) based on successive projection algorithms (SPA) and grid search (GS), as well as a one-dimensional convolutional neural network. Experimental results showed that the DeepSpectraV2 model with FD preprocessing achieved the best classification accuracy of 96.56%, outperforming the best comparative models by 1.52 to 15.52% across other preprocessing methods. This study presents a novel approach for the rapid non-destructive detection of GM crops.Subject terms: Genetics, Agriculture

Introduction

Maize is the most extensively cultivated grain crop in China. It is widely used in food, animal feed, and industrial raw materials, playing an increasingly important role in daily life, with a continuously growing demand1. However, traditional breeding techniques, due to limitations such as long cycles and low efficiency, can no longer meet the needs of modern agriculture for commercial cultivation and quality improvement of maize. Genetic modification technology2, by precisely introducing exogenous genes such as insect and herbicide resistance, significantly improves both yield and quality, while also reducing the usage of pesticides and herbicides. Nevertheless, during open-field cultivation, the genetic components of genetically modified (GM) crops may spread to surrounding species through pollen or seeds, leading to the drift of resistance genes. This can result in the emergence of so-called “superweeds”, which pose potential risks to ecological environments and biodiversity. Therefore, developing efficient and accurate detection techniques for GM crops is of particular importance.

Traditional methods for detecting GM crops, such as protein-based and DNA-based assays, are often limited by prolonged detection cycles, risks of compromising sample integrity, high reagent consumption, and substantial costs. These drawbacks hinder their application in modern agriculture, which demands efficient and precise variety selection technologies. Terahertz (THz) waves, as a special band of the electromagnetic spectrum, offer a novel approach for rapid and non-destructive detection of GM maize. THz waves refer to electromagnetic waves with frequencies ranging from 0.1 to 10 THz, wavelengths between 3 mm and 30 μm, and wavenumbers from 3.3 to 330 cm−13. Positioned between the microwave and infrared regions, this band serves as a transitional zone from classical macroscopic theory to quantum microscopic behavior. Since many molecular interactions, such as intermolecular and intramolecular rotational and vibrational transitions, backbone vibrations of nucleic acid macromolecules, and low-frequency lattice vibrations in crystals, have absorption frequencies within the THz range, THz technology exhibits significant advantages in the detection of biological macromolecules. With its advantages of non-destructive penetration, low-energy photons, and the ability to capture subtle structural differences between molecules, THz technology can effectively reflect internal differences in GM crops. Consequently, THz waves show promising potential in fields such as non-destructive testing4,5, material identification6,7, and quality analysis of agricultural products8,9.

The rotational and vibrational energy levels of most biomolecules correspond to the THz frequency range, different biomolecules absorb THz waves to varying degrees10. This difference in absorption can be exploited, along with terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS) technology, to efficiently identify GM substances11. Liu et al.12 combined THz spectra with weighted linear discriminant analysis (WLDA) to distinguish GM edible oils, and validated the model’s performance using the successive projections algorithm (SPA) and partial least squares (PLS). The experimental results showed that the SPA-WLDA model outperformed the PLS-WLDA model, achieving an accuracy of 96.53%. Sun et al.13 combined THz spectroscopy with support vector regression (SVR) to build a predictive model correlating frequency and Bisphenol A concentration, facilitating the quantitative analysis of bisphenol mixtures. The experimental results showed that the R² of the model reached 0.98, confirming the accuracy and reliability of THz spectra combined with SVR for quantitative analysis of mixtures. Yi et al.14 preprocessed the THz spectral data of GM cotton seeds using adaptive local iterative filtering and wavelet transformation, enhancing and accurately identifying the data features. They applied generalized discriminant analysis for dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis, achieving a recognition accuracy of 98.33% for four types of GM cotton seeds. Although these methods rely on manual feature extraction and model tuning, which results in good classification accuracy, they still have certain limitations.

In recent years, deep learning, as an important branch of machine learning15, has gradually become a mainstream approach in THz spectroscopy analysis due to its end-to-end automatic feature extraction capability and efficient modeling ability. Pu et al.16 combined THz-TDS technology with a convolutional neural network (CNN) to identify the origin of pericarpium citri reticulatae. The study revealed that the Add-CNN model based on spectral-image multimodal fusion exhibited the best performance, achieving an accuracy of 86.17%. Chen et al.17 used THz-TDS and CNN to distinguish 12 wheat varieties. By selecting spectral features through a competitive adaptive re-weighting algorithm, the CNN model achieved accuracies of 98.7 and 97.8% on the calibration and prediction sets, significantly outperforming the support vector machine (SVM). Gu et al.18 applied THz precision spectroscopy and CNN to identify Panax notoginseng origins, achieving 97.62% accuracy on 252 samples and surpassing high-performance liquid chromatography in classification performance. These studies fully demonstrate the great potential of deep learning in THz spectral recognition, significantly improving recognition accuracy and model stability while simplifying the process.

Although significant progress has been made in previous studies, there is limited research on the detection of GM maize using THz and CNN. Therefore, this study focuses on six GM maize GA21 samples, using THz-TDS technology to obtain their time-domain spectra and then calculating their absorption coefficient spectra. The absorption spectra data were preprocessed using Savitzky-Golay (SG) smoothing, standard normal variable (SNV) transformation, baseline correction (BC), first derivative (FD), and second derivative (SD). Based on the processed data, three end-to-end DeepSpectra models (V1–V3) were designed to differentiate the GM maize GA21. Compared to traditional models, the deep learning model exhibited higher accuracy. All models were assessed in terms of classification accuracy to validate the effectiveness of the proposed DeepSpectra framework for GM maize identification.

Results

Dataset overview and experimental design

In the experiment, THz time-domain spectra of GM maize GA21 at different genetic content levels were collected. Each content level of GM maize contained 180 samples, resulting in a total of 1080 spectra. To improve data quality and ensure model accuracy, the Isolation Forest (IF) algorithm was used for outlier detection, with parameters set as n_estimators = 100 and contamination = 0.005. The IF algorithm identified and removed six outlier samples: one from GA21-level 1, two from GA21-level 3, and three from GA21-level 4, as shown in Fig. 1a.

Outlier detection, dataset splitting, and modeling workflow.

a Anomaly detection results of IF in spectral data. b Training and testing set division results after IF processing. c Process of establishing the quantitative analysis model.

After removing the outliers, the number of valid samples in the dataset decreased to 1074. Subsequently, the remaining samples were split into training and testing sets with a ratio of 7:319, as shown in Fig. 1b. Based on the preprocessed dataset, this study used the DeepSpectraV1, V2, and V3 models to model and analyze GM maize GA21 with varying GM content, and built three comparison models: SVM (SPA-GS-SVM) and random forest (SPA-GS-RF) based on SPA and grid search (GS), as well as a one-dimensional CNN (1D-CNN). The distinction effect of each model was compared based on the results from both the training and testing sets. The model construction process is shown in Fig. 1c.

Spectral characteristics and preprocessing

After processing with the IF algorithm, the average THz time-domain spectra for GM maize GA21 at different levels of genetic content were calculated. The average THz time-domain spectra for different levels of GM maize GA21 are shown in Fig. 2a. It can be observed from the figure that all the signals from GM maize GA21 exhibit varying degrees of attenuation compared to the reference signal, with a noticeable time delay on the time axis. Specifically, as the genetic content increases, the attenuation of the signal decreases gradually, while the time delay increases progressively. This phenomenon indicates that higher levels of GM content have a more significant effect on the propagation of THz light signals, resulting in a stronger signal delay effect.

THz spectral characteristics of GM maize GA21 under different preprocessing methods.

a Time-domain spectrum. b Frequency-domain spectrum. Absorption coefficient spectra: c Raw, d SG, e SNV, f FD, g SD, and h BC.

The average frequency-domain spectra of GM maize GA21 at different levels are shown in Fig. 2b. From the figure, it can be seen that all samples show a certain degree of attenuation after 1.2 THz compared to the reference signal. Furthermore, both the reference signal and the sample signals exhibit noticeable oscillations after 1.2 THz, indicating that the signal is increasingly affected by noise. The absorption coefficient spectra shown in Fig. 2c reveal that, within the range of 0.2–1.2 THz, the absorption coefficient gradually increases as the frequency increases. Among these, GA21-level 5 has the highest absorption coefficient, while GA21-level 4 has the lowest absorption coefficient. The absorption coefficients for the other levels (GA21-blank, GA21-level 1, GA21-level 2, and GA21-level 3) show a high degree of overlap, which could be due to the relatively small differences in GM content among these levels, resulting in a minimal impact on the absorption coefficient. Given that the available data range for the refractive index spectrum is limited, this study chose the absorption coefficient spectrum in the 0.2–1.2 THz range for model analysis. The absorption coefficient was chosen as the primary feature because it comprehensively reflects the sample’s overall dielectric response in the THz band, exhibits high sensitivity to the distribution of biological macromolecules and their intermolecular interactions, and offers better robustness against minor surface inhomogeneities in pellet samples compared to other optical parameters.

To eliminate noise introduced during the spectral acquisition process and improve the model’s accuracy, several preprocessing methods, including SG, SNV, FD, SD, and BC, were applied to the spectral data. SG smoothing, through local polynomial fitting, effectively removes high-frequency noise while preserving the main features and trends of the signal. SNV eliminates spectral shifts and amplitude variations caused by changes in particle size or sample scattering. FD effectively removes baseline drift and enhances signal resolution. SD further emphasizes subtle spectral features and helps to eliminate baseline drift more thoroughly. BC is primarily used to remove baseline drift in the spectrum caused by equipment response or experimental conditions. By fitting and adjusting the spectral baseline for normalization, the true characteristics of the sample are emphasized. The results after spectral preprocessing are shown in Fig. 2d–h.

Spectral feature selection results

A total of 121 frequency points were included in the absorption spectrum within the 0.2–1.2 THz range. Among these, some frequency points were found to exhibit weak correlations with the sample characteristics. These irrelevant data not only increased the dimensionality but may also lead to overfitting, reducing the modeling efficiency and prediction accuracy. To simplify the model, reduce data redundancy, and improve modeling efficiency, the SPA was used to select spectral feature variables. The corresponding number of feature variables was chosen based on the minimum root mean square error (RMSE), and these feature variables were extracted. The Pearson correlation coefficient was employed to analyze the correlation between the selected frequency points, further verifying the relevance of the feature variables. The range for the number of variables selected was set to [5, 20]. The process of extracting raw spectral data is shown in Fig. 3a.

As shown in Fig. 3a, when the RMSE reached its minimum, a total of 20 feature variables were selected, with correlation coefficients among these variables ranging from 0.83 to 1. This result indicates that the selected variables were strongly correlated and effectively represent the characteristics of the raw data. Discussion

This study successfully combined THz-TDS with deep neural network (DNN) to achieve high-accuracy classification of GM maize GA21 across six concentration levels, providing a promising approach for the rapid and non-destructive detection of GM crops. The results demonstrate the feasibility of integrating THz spectral features with end-to-end deep learning models for quantitative analysis, with the DeepSpectraV2 model exhibiting superior performance in multi-level classification tasks. After FD preprocessing, DeepSpectraV2 achieved a maximum prediction accuracy of 96.56%, outperforming SPA-GS-SVM, SPA-GS-RF, 1D-CNN, DeepSpectraV1, and DeepSpectraV3 by margins of 11.56, 15.52, 5.24, 2.76, and 1.52%, respectively. These findings indicate that DNN architectures equipped with multi-scale convolutional kernels can effectively capture hierarchical spectral features from THz data, thereby enhancing learning capacity and improving classification accuracy.

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The investigation was restricted to the GA21 maize line and did not include other widely cultivated GM varieties, which may limit the generalization capability of the proposed models. In addition, the stability and robustness of the models under varying environmental conditions, including fluctuations in temperature and humidity, have not yet been systematically evaluated and warrant further investigation to support real-world applications. Moreover, current THz-TDS systems remain relatively bulky and costly, and their lack of simplified, portable configurations constrains widespread adoption for on-site screening.

Beyond these technical and practical considerations, the physical and biochemical mechanisms underlying the observed THz spectral differences between GM and non-GM maize remain insufficiently understood. The interaction of THz radiation with biological materials is inherently complex, involving low-frequency collective vibrations of biomolecules, hydrogen-bonding networks, and water state dynamics. Genetic modification may influence the dielectric properties of maize kernels by altering microscopic characteristics such as protein composition, starch structure, or water distribution, thereby giving rise to distinguishable patterns in THz absorption spectra. At present, the employed deep learning models function primarily as data-driven approaches that establish robust statistical associations between spectral features and GM content, rather than providing explicit causal explanations. Advancing from correlation-based interpretation toward mechanistic understanding will require the integration of explainable artificial intelligence techniques, such as gradient-weighted class activation mapping, with chemometric analyses to identify the most influential spectral regions and relate them to specific biochemical or structural changes induced by genetic modification.

To overcome these limitations and further strengthen both applicability and interpretability, future research should pursue multiple interconnected paths. The establishment of comprehensive and publicly accessible THz spectral databases covering multiple GM varieties, genetic events, and production regions is a fundamental requirement. Concurrently, the incorporation of advanced learning strategies, such as transfer learning and domain adaptation, will be critical to improve model robustness across different instruments and environmental conditions. The development of user-friendly, integrated software platforms is also necessary to facilitate broader adoption by non-specialist users. In parallel, continued efforts toward the miniaturization, cost reduction, and portability of THz sensing devices are indispensable for enabling rapid, on-site detection. Through sustained interdisciplinary collaboration, these advances are expected to enable the realization of efficient, accurate, and field-deployable GM crop detection systems. Such technologies would not only support regulatory oversight and labeling of GM crops but could also be extended to quality monitoring and authenticity assessment across a wide range of agricultural products, offering substantial application potential and positive societal impact.

Methods

Sample preparation

The GM maize GA21 standard variety, provided by Monsanto, was selected as the experimental material for this study. This variety has been modified by introducing the cp4 epsps gene, which confers glyphosate resistance, allowing the maize to survive in the presence of glyphosate herbicide. The experimental samples comprised six groups with distinct GM content gradients. These included one negative control group (GA21-blank, GM content <0.8 g/kg) and five positive groups: GA21-level 1 (0.1%), GA21-level 2 (0.5%), GA21-level 3 (1%), GA21-level 4 (2%), and GA21-level 5 (5%). All samples were subjected to multiple generations of breeding and standardization to ensure the stability of the GM characteristics and were used for the verification and calibration of the GM detection methods.

During the sample preparation process, 200 mg of GM maize powder was accurately weighed using a precision balance and then pressed into thin plates using a tablet press. Each powder sample was pressed under 10 MPa of pressure to form circular disks with a diameter of ~13 mm and a thickness of ~1.2 mm, ensuring that the surface of the disks was smooth and even. After pressing, all samples were stored in a constant-temperature drying oven to prevent moisture absorption. A total of 180 sample disks were prepared for each GM maize group, yielding a total of 1080 sample disks.

THz spectral acquisition system

The experiment used a THz 3D Tomographic Imaging System (QT-TO1000, Quenda, China, Qingdao), with the working principle shown in Fig. 6a. The system’s femtosecond laser has a central wavelength of 1550 nm, with an output pulse width of less than 100 fs and a repetition rate of 80 MHz. The femtosecond laser emits two beams: one as the pump light source and the other as the probe light source. The pump light is transmitted via optical fiber to the transmitting antenna, where, under the influence of a bias voltage, it generates broadband THz waves. After being focused by the THz lens group, the THz waves are directed onto the detection target. The THz waves transmitted through the target are received by the THz receiving antenna. Meanwhile, the probe light is transmitted through the optical fiber to the THz receiving antenna, where photogenerated carriers are generated under the influence of the THz waves, resulting in an electrical current signal. The acquisition card amplifies and collects the detected current signal. The optical path difference is adjusted using an optical fiber delay line to achieve detection and scanning of the THz spectral signals.

THz spectral acquisition system and procedure.

a Schematic diagram of the THz 3D tomographic imaging system. b Acquisition process of THz spectra.

In the experiment, transmission mode was used for sample spectral acquisition. The process of spectral acquisition is shown in Fig. 6b. THz spectral scanning was performed for each sample, with each sample scanned three times, and the average spectrum calculated as the sample data. The parameters of the spectral scans were set as follows: scan time of 10 s, number of scans of 1000, and scan range of 120 ps.

Outlier detection and removal

IF26 is an unsupervised anomaly detection algorithm. Its core idea is to recursively partition the dataset by randomly selecting features and split values until each sample is isolated to a separate leaf node. Since outliers usually exhibit sparsity and significant differences, they are more easily isolated in the feature space, meaning they reach the leaf nodes of the tree earlier. The anomaly score s(x,n) ranges from 0 to 1. When s(x,n) approaches 1, it indicates that sample x is highly likely to be an outlier. When s(x,n) is less than 0.5, it indicates that sample x is likely a normal data point.

Spectral preprocessing methods

Due to the interference of factors such as sample particle size, experimental environment, and operational processes, the spectral data collected by the THz-TDS system inevitably introduces noise unrelated to the sample, while still containing the true sample information. This noise increases the complexity of modeling and can lead to overfitting or underfitting of the model, thereby reducing its predictive accuracy and generalization ability. Therefore, before constructing an analytical model, it is necessary to preprocess the spectral data to effectively remove noise and irrelevant information. Common spectral preprocessing methods include SG, SNV, BC, FD, and SD27.

To reduce or diminish the errors caused by noise, SG smoothing was applied. SG smoothing is one of the most commonly used smoothing methods. Finally, derivative preprocessing was applied. Derivative preprocessing is effective in correcting baseline offsets and drifts in spectra, while also enhancing the recognizability of spectral features. Specifically, FD is used to eliminate baseline shifts and highlight the characteristic absorption peaks and shoulders, thus enhancing signal contrast. The SD is further applied to eliminate high-frequency noise, allowing for the accurate pinpointing of the center of absorption peaks, reducing interference between components, and improving the resolution of spectral data. However, a too high derivative order can lower the signal-to-noise ratio and introduce additional noise interference. In practical applications, the derivative order must be chosen based on the characteristics of the data, ensuring that the preprocessed data effectively removes interference while preserving useful information to the greatest extent.

Feature selection via SPA

The SPA28, a feature selection method, is used to reduce redundancy and select the most representative features by progressively choosing projections. Its core goal is to iteratively select effective features while minimizing the correlation between the newly selected features and the existing ones, ensuring that each feature provides maximal information gain.

The algorithm proceeds in a sequential manner. Initialization is performed by randomly selecting or determining the initial feature vector based on the maximum variance criterion. Subsequently, orthogonal projection residuals of the remaining features in the current feature space are calculated using linear algebra theory, and the feature exhibiting the largest projection residual is selected as a new variable to ensure minimal collinearity with already selected features. This projection and selection procedure is iteratively repeated until a preset number of features or an information gain threshold is reached, yielding an optimal subset of features with high discrimination and low redundancy.

Effective wavelengths with minimal redundancy and covariance are selected step by step through vector projection analysis in the SPA. By extracting key information from numerous wavelengths or features, this method enables noise and redundant information in high-dimensional data to be effectively reduced, thereby improving the training efficiency and predictive accuracy of models.

DeepSpectra model architecture

CNN models are characterized by sparse connectivity, parameter sharing, pooling, and deep architectures. In CNN models, the number of parameters is significantly reduced through local connections between neurons in adjacent layers. In this study, three models, DeepSpectraV1, DeepSpectraV2, and DeepSpectraV3, were constructed based on the Inception V1 and V2 architectures29, specifically designed for spectral data analysis. These models not only increase the depth of the CNN but also enhance its width. The input to the DeepSpectra models consists of raw one-dimensional spectral data, namely absorption coefficient spectra in the range of 0.2–1.2 THz, and the output corresponds to classification results. Based on the Inception module, four parallel paths were designed to extract spectral features at different scales and perform classification modeling. The detailed structure is illustrated in Fig. 7.

Structures of the three DeepSpectra network models.

The DeepSpectraV1 model was developed based on the V1 structure of the Inception module, incorporating four parallel paths to extract spectral features at multiple scales. In the first path, a 1 × 1 convolution was employed for dimensionality reduction, followed by a ReLU activation function for nonlinear mapping. Given that the input consists of one-dimensional spectral data, the 1 × 1 convolution operated along the feature dimension to project the input into a higher-dimensional feature space. The second path combined a 1 × 1 convolution with a 3 × 1 convolution to extract local features, where the 3 × 1 convolution captured local patterns in the spectral data. Padding was applied to maintain a consistent feature length between the input and output. The third path integrated a 1 × 1 convolution and a 5 × 1 convolution to capture broader spectral features, with padding used to prevent information loss and ensure length consistency. The fourth path utilized 3 × 1 max pooling followed by a 1 × 1 convolution to extract global features and perform dimensionality reduction, again applying padding to preserve the output length. The output features from the four paths were concatenated along the channel dimension, flattened, and subsequently passed through two fully connected layers to complete the spectral classification. Owing to its relatively simple structure, this model is suitable for basic spectral classification tasks, although its capability to handle complex spectral data remains limited.

The DeepSpectraV2 model was developed as an optimization version of DeepSpectraV1, incorporating factorized convolutions (1 × 3 and 3 × 1, 1 × 5, and 5 × 1) to effectively reduce the number of convolutional parameters and improve computational efficiency. Additionally, batch normalization was introduced in V2 to accelerate the training process. In the first path, a 1 × 1 convolution was used for dimensionality reduction and initial feature extraction, followed by batch normalization and a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function to enhance training stability. The second path consisted of a 1 × 1 convolution followed by a 3 × 1 convolution for local feature extraction, with padding applied to maintain consistent output dimensions. In the third path, a 1 × 1 convolution was combined with two successive factorized 3 × 1 convolutions to capture broader spectral features while further reducing the number of parameters involved in the convolutional operations. The fourth path employed a 3 × 1 max pooling operation followed by a 1 × 1 convolution to extract global features, and batch normalization and an activation function were applied after the pooling layer to further optimize model performance. The output features from the four paths were concatenated along the channel dimension, flattened, and passed through two fully connected layers to complete the spectral classification task.

The DeepSpectraV3 model was constructed by stacking two V2 modules, increasing network depth and enhancing feature extraction capability. This structure allows for more flexible kernel sizes and channel numbers in each path, enabling better adaptation to spectral features of varying scales. The first V2 module followed the same four-path architecture as in DeepSpectraV2 to extract multi-scale features. A max pooling layer was inserted between the two V2 modules to reduce the dimensionality of the feature maps while capturing more representative global information. The second stacked V2 module further enhanced the model’s ability to extract features and was configured with adjustable kernel sizes and channel numbers in each path to accommodate different spectral resolutions. Through the integration of two V2 modules and an intermediate max pooling layer, the DeepSpectraV3 model was able to more effectively capture complex spectral patterns. The final classification was performed using fully connected layers. Compared to the V1 and V2 models, the V3 architecture demonstrated notable improvements in both feature extraction performance and computational efficiency.

In the experiments, each convolutional layer in the V1 model was followed by a ReLU activation function. In contrast, both the V2 and V3 models incorporated batch normalization followed by a ReLU activation after each convolutional layer. Additionally, a ReLU activation function was applied after the first fully connected layer in all models to introduce nonlinearity. The Adam optimizer was employed for training, in conjunction with the ReduceLROnPlateau learning rate scheduler, which automatically adjusted the learning rate when no improvement in validation accuracy was observed. The categorical cross-entropy loss function was used to optimize the model training performance.
