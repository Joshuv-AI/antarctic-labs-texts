Finite-time performance of a cyclic 2d quantum Ising heat engine
S. P. Katoorani, C. Kohlfürst, F. Queisser, G. Schaller, R. Schützhold

Abstract
We discuss the limit cycle regime of a finite-time quantum Otto cycle with a frictionless two-dimensional anisotropic Ising model as the working fluid. From Onsagers exact equilibrium solution, we first find optimal parameters for the operational modes of work extraction and cooling for infinitely slow cycles. The equilibrium points in these optimal cycles correspond to different phases of the model, such that the non-equilibrium dynamics during the cycle bypasses the phase transition. Finite-time cycles allow for finite power extraction or cooling currents, but for such cycles we point out that – already within the regime of weak system-reservoir coupling – energetic changes of the system during dissipative strokes may contain a significant portion of coupling and decoupling control work and should thus not be directly identified with heat. For ultrafast cycles, the required control work spoils performance, such that to maximize work extraction or cooling heat per cycle time, there is an optimal cycle duration. We also find that net zero-energy transitions may lead to undesired reservoir heating.

First, in continuously operating engines

Therefore, for interacting spin systems that are challenging on their own one often focuses on the class of finite-stroke engines, for which many interacting quantum working fluids have already been analyzed.

II
Finite-time quantum Otto cycle

II.1
Quantum Otto cycle

In the classical Otto cycle, one has two isentropic strokes (only work) and two isochoric ones (only heat exchange).
A quantum analog cycle that (at least in principle) allows for a similarly simple distinction between work and heat
is known as quantum Otto cycle
[
54
]
.
It is composed of four strokes
(see Fig.
1
for an illustration):

A\to B

During this isentropic stroke, the working fluid only evolves under the time-dependent Hamiltonian
H_{S}(t)
such that
H_{S}(t_{A})=H_{S}^{c}
and
H_{S}(t_{B})=H_{S}^{h}
.
Accordingly, its entropy remains constant, its energetic changes are interpreted as work, and the reservoirs need not be explicitly modeled.

B\to C

During this dissipative stroke (the analog of the isochoric stroke in the classical Otto cycle), the working fluid is subject to the constant system Hamiltonian
H_{S}^{h}
and also to the coupling
H_{I}^{h}
to the hot reservoir
H_{B}^{h}
. Energetic changes of the working fluid are typically interpreted as heat.

C\to D

This isentropic stroke is analogous to the stroke
A\to B
, except that the transform is reversed, i.e.,
H_{S}(t_{C})=H_{S}^{h}
and
H_{S}(t_{D})=H_{S}^{c}
.

D\to A

The cycle is closed by this dissipative stroke, during which the working fluid is subject to the constant system Hamiltonian
H_{S}^{c}
and the coupling
H_{I}^{c}
to the cold reservoir
H_{B}^{c}
.

After completion of one cycle, it is restarted with the stroke
A\to B
.

Figure 1:

Sketch of a quantum Otto cycle in the plane of the working fluid quench parameter
\omega
and the von-Neumann entropy of the working fluid, cyclically operated via
A\to B\to C\to D\to A
.
The Hamiltonian is constant while coupled to the cold (
D\to A
, blue) or to the hot (

During the isentropic strokes (green), the system is subject to a time-dependent system Hamiltonian but decoupled from both reservoirs.
Bold arrows exemplarily sketch the flow of energy into (inward-pointing) or out of (outward-pointing) the working fluid for work extraction:
Positive heat from the hot reservoir increases the working fluid energy (red arrow), whereas the energy balance with the cold reservoir is negative (blue arrow).
Ideally, the difference of the heat contributions (length of red and blue arrows) is available for the net extraction
of work (length of green arrows).
For the refrigerator operational mode the heat and work flows need to be inverted.

At any time, the total Hamiltonian of the model universe is thus comprised of

\displaystyle H(t)=H_{S}(t)+H_{B}^{c}+H_{B}^{h}+H_{I}^{c}(t)+H_{I}^{h}(t)\,,

(1)

where during every stroke of one cycle, different parts of the Hamiltonian are active.

For the heat engine operational mode, it is desirable to maximize the work extracted during one cycle
\Delta W
, which is by our conventions positive when work is performed by the working fluid.
For a closed cycle, energy conservation dictates that
\Delta W=\Delta Q_{h}+\Delta Q_{c}
, where
\Delta Q_{h/c}
denote the heat entering the working fluid (i.e., positive when increasing its energy) during the respective dissipative strokes.
Thus, when we interpret all system energetic changes during the dissipative strokes as the corresponding negative changes in the reservoir (i.e., as heat),
this allows us to estimate the extracted work from the difference of system energies

\displaystyle\Delta W_{\rm sys}=-(E_{B}-E_{A})-(E_{D}-E_{C})\,.

(2)

For the refrigerator operational mode one would like to maximize the heat leaving the cold reservoir, which under the same identification is estimated by the energy
inflow into the system during that stroke

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{c,\rm sys}=E_{A}-E_{D}\,.

(3)

These quantities can e.g. be used to define the efficiency for the heat engine operational mode

\eta=\frac{\Delta W}{\Delta Q_{h}}\Theta(\Delta W)\leq 1-\frac{\beta_{h}}{\beta_{c}}=\eta_{\rm Ca}

and the coefficient of performance for the refrigerator operational mode

\kappa=\frac{\Delta Q_{c}}{-\Delta W}\Theta(\Delta Q_{c})\leq\frac{\beta_{h}}{\beta_{c}-\beta_{h}}=\kappa_{\textrm{C}a}

which are both bound by their respective Carnot limits.
Analogous relations would hold for the heat pump operational mode aiming at heating the hot reservoir, which we will not discuss here.

II.2
Finite-time transition rates

Very often, Fermi-Golden-Rule (FGR) rates are used to model relaxation processes.
In their derivation, one assumes that reservoir and system are coupled for an infinitely long time.
For example, when the system is coupled to reservoir
\nu
that is composed of a continuum of two-level systems held at thermal equilibrium with inverse temperature
\beta_{\nu}
,
the rate for the system undergoing an energy change of
+\Omega
can in the FGR limit (see App.
B
) be expressed as

\displaystyle R^{\nu,\rm FGR}_{\Omega}=\frac{\Gamma_{\nu}(\Omega)}{e^{\beta_{\nu}\Omega}+1}\,,

(4)

where the reservoir spectral function
\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)\geq 0
is analytically continued to the real axis via
\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)=\Gamma_{\nu}(-\omega)
.
For this paper, we will use a Lorentzian representation

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)=\frac{\Gamma_{\nu}\delta_{\nu}^{2}}{\omega^{2}+\delta_{\nu}^{2}}\,,

(5)

where for simplicity we constrain ourselves to symmetric coupling strengths
\Gamma_{\nu}=\Gamma
and widths
\delta_{\nu}=\delta
for both reservoirs.
The FGR rates above obey detailed balance
R^{\nu,\rm FGR}_{-\Omega}=e^{\beta_{\nu}\Omega}R^{\nu,\rm FGR}_{+\Omega}
,
such that relaxation processes favor low-energy states and the stationary state is the thermal equilibrium state of the system at inverse temperature
\beta_{\nu}
.

For very short stroke durations one cannot expect the FGR treatment to apply.
Rather, second order perturbation theory allows to derive effective rates that are coarse-grained over the stroke duration
\tau

[
55
]
.

While by construction, the coarse-grained rates only yield trustworthy results at the end of the stroke, the rate-equation representation allows to apply standard trajectory techniques (see App.
D
) also to non-Markovian settings.

Specifically, the rate for an energy change
\Omega
observed in the system is then (see App.
B
)

\displaystyle R^{\nu,\tau}_{\Omega}=\int\frac{\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)}{e^{\beta_{\nu}\omega}+1}\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[\frac{(\omega-\Omega)\tau}{2}\right]d\omega\,,

(6)

where
{\rm sinc}(x)\equiv\sin(x)/x
.
These coarse-grained rates do in general not obey detailed balance but fall back to the FGR rates (
4
) when
\tau\to\infty
.
Furthermore, in the wideband limit (
\delta_{\nu}\to\infty
, i.e.,
\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)\to\Gamma_{\nu}
), the coarse-grained rates still favor energetically lower states at all
\tau
.
In the limit
\tau\to 0
(and supposing finite
\delta_{\nu}
) all rates will vanish, such that for repeated short couplings to a reservoir the system will not undergo any transitions anymore.
The repeated coupling with an uncorrelated reservoir is analogous to repeated measurements, such that this inhibition of transitions by fast repeated couplings can be seen as a manifestation of the quantum Zeno effect
[
43
,
56
,
57
]
.
That rates vanish for small
\tau
also means that cycles can be expected to become dysfunctional when driven too fast, but note that spectral functions with more structure than (
5
) can be exploited to enhance cycle performance for finite
\tau
via the Anti-Zeno effect
[
58
,
37
]
.

When the system suffers a transition with energy change
\Omega
, the corresponding average energy change in the reservoir
\nu
is (see App.
B
)

\displaystyle\Delta E^{\nu,\tau}_{\Omega}=-\frac{\int\frac{\omega\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)}{e^{\beta_{\nu}\omega}+1}\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[\frac{(\omega-\Omega)\tau}{2}\right]d\omega}{R^{\nu,\tau}_{\Omega}}\,.

(7)

From this it follows that in general
\Delta E^{\nu,\tau}_{\Omega}\neq-\Omega
, i.e., for finite stroke durations there will be a mismatch between system and reservoir energy changes.
This mismatch is invested in the control work used for coupling and decoupling to the reservoir
[
59
]
and should enter the thermodynamic discussion of finite-time cycles, see below.
For infinitely slow cycles we find that
\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\Delta E^{\nu,\tau}_{\Omega}=-\Omega
.
In the particular case of energy-preserving system transitions (
\Omega=0
), one finds from integral transformations that
R^{\nu,\tau}_{0}\Delta E^{\nu,\tau}_{0}\geq 0
, which means that these transitions
will only induce indiscriminate reservoir heating, detrimental for cycle performance.
A similar statement is found for sequential transitions of opposite energetic changes, where one formally finds

R^{\nu,\tau}_{+\Omega}\Delta E^{\nu,\tau}_{+\Omega}+R^{\nu,\tau}_{-\Omega}\Delta E^{\nu,\tau}_{-\Omega}\geq 0
.
This means that these processes will on average also just induce heating of the reservoir without net energy change of the system and should be detrimental to cycle performance.

II.3
Finite-time definitions of heat and work

As exposed above, for finite contact times
\tau
, the change of the cold and hot reservoir energies

\Delta E^{c,\tau}_{D\to A}
and
\Delta E^{h,\tau}_{B\to C}
is not just the negative of the system energy changes – even in the weak-coupling regime.
We therefore define the control work required for coupling and decoupling the reservoirs as the difference

\displaystyle-\Delta W_{\rm ctl}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\Delta E^{c}_{D\to A}+E_{A}-E_{D}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Delta E^{h}_{B\to C}+E_{C}-E_{B}\,,

(8)

such that the net work extracted from the cycle is

\displaystyle-\Delta W_{\rm net}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=-\Delta W_{\rm ctl}^{\tau}-\Delta W_{\rm sys}

\displaystyle=\Delta E^{c,\tau}_{D\to A}+\Delta E^{h,\tau}_{B\to C}\,,

(9)

which requires to calculate the reservoir energetic changes.
Also the net heat extracted from the cold reservoir is given by the corresponding energy change of the reservoir, e.g. for cooling

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{c}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=-\Delta E^{c,\tau}_{D\to A}\,.

(10)

To evaluate these contributions, let us highlight two crucial assumptions:

•

First, we assume that the evolution is frictionless as far as the working fluid is concerned

\displaystyle\left[H_{S}(t),H_{S}(t^{\prime})\right]=0\,,

(11)

which implies that during the isentropic strokes, the time evolution operator for the system can be written without time-ordering

\displaystyle U_{S}^{A/C\to B/D}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-{\rm i}\int\limits_{t_{A/C}}^{t_{B/D}}H_{S}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\right\}\,,

(12)

and that there is a time-independent energy eigenbasis diagonalizing the system Hamiltonian
H_{S}(t)=\sum_{a}E_{a}(t)\left|a\right>\left<a\right|
.

•

Second, we assume the system density matrix to be diagonal (though not necessarily thermal) in the time-independent energy eigenbasis of
H_{S}(t)
at all points
A
,
B
,
C
, and
D
, which beyond an FGR treatment is not a trivial simplification.
The above time evolution operator already implies that diagonal density matrices at points
A
and
C
will also remain diagonal at points
B
and
D
, respectively.
In App.
B.1
we detail that under locality assumptions on the system-reservoir couplings, diagonality of the density matrix in the system energy eigenbasis
is also maintained during the dissipative strokes
D\to A
and
B\to C
.
Therefore, this assumption can be fulfilled by initializing the cycle in a diagonal state, e.g. by using a sufficiently long pre-equilibration with the cold reservoir before
starting the cyclic operations.

Alternatively, one may also imagine that further dephasing reservoirs (whose energy balance is not part of our analysis) active during the dissipative strokes may erase the coherences without changing the systems populations.

Under these assumptions, the system state will not suffer any change during the isentropic strokes.
Thus, it is advisable to perform these strokes infinitely fast,
and the remaining bottleneck to power extraction or cooling power is posed by the dissipative stroke durations.

III
Two-level system

As an example, we study a two-level system
[
22
]

\displaystyle H_{S}(t)=\frac{\omega(t)}{2}\sigma^{z}\,,

(13)

where
\omega(t)=\omega_{c}
during
D\to A
and
\omega(t)=\omega_{h}
during
B\to C
.
The coupling to the reservoirs can be described by
H_{I}^{c}=\sigma^{x}\otimes B^{c}(t)
and
H_{I}^{h}=\sigma^{x}\otimes B^{h}(t)
,
where the reservoir coupling operators
B^{c}(t)
and
B^{h}(t)
are only non-vanishing during the dissipative cold (
D\to A
) and hot (
B\to C
) strokes of the cycle, respectively.
Within a Fermi-Golden-Rule (FGR) treatment, their explicit form will determine the transition rates between ground state
g
and excited state
e
.
For reservoirs
\nu\in\{c,h\}
we consider the Glauber rates (
4
) evaluated at energy difference
\Omega\in\pm\omega_{\nu}
and also symmetrically coupled wideband reservoirs

\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)=\Gamma
, i.e.,

\displaystyle R^{\nu}_{g\to e}=R^{\nu,\rm FGR}_{+\omega_{\nu}}\,,\qquad R^{\nu}_{e\to g}=R^{\nu,\rm FGR}_{-\omega_{\nu}}=e^{+\beta_{\nu}\omega_{\nu}}R^{\nu}_{g\to e}\,.

(14)

For simplicity, we also choose the stroke duration for both dissipative strokes equal and denote it by
\tau
, such that the total cycle duration is
2\tau
.
This allows for a mostly analytic treatment that we expose in App.
A
.

When within the FGR treatment the dissipative strokes last infinitely long, such that thermalization is perfect,
the work extracted from one cycle (
2
) and the heat absorbed from the cold reservoir (
3
), respectively,
can be computed as (see also App.
A.1
)

\displaystyle\Delta W_{\rm FGR}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{h}-\omega_{c}}{2}\left[\tanh\left(\frac{\beta_{c}\omega_{c}}{2}\right)-\tanh\left(\frac{\beta_{h}\omega_{h}}{2}\right)\right]\,,

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{\rm c,FGR}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{c}}{2}\left[\tanh\left(\frac{\beta_{h}\omega_{h}}{2}\right)-\tanh\left(\frac{\beta_{c}\omega_{c}}{2}\right)\right]\,.

(15)

This limit is useful to find conditions under which work extraction (
\Delta W>0
) or cooling functionality (
\Delta Q_{c}>0
) is possible in principle and even optimal.

Still within an FGR treatment, equilibration with the reservoir will not be perfect for finite cycle-times, and a smaller limit cycle will emerge.
One may analytically solve the dynamical equations for the limit cycle and finite stroke durations (see App.
A.2
), and we then find with FGR rates that the extracted work (
2
) and cooling heat (
3
) are simply reduced by a common factor (see App.
A.3
and Ref.
[
36
]
for a generalization beyond frictionless working fluids)

\displaystyle\Delta W_{\rm FGR}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\Delta W_{\rm FGR}^{\infty}\tanh\left(\frac{\Gamma\tau}{2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{\rm c,FGR}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\Delta Q_{\rm c,FGR}^{\infty}\tanh\left(\frac{\Gamma\tau}{2}\right)\,.

(16)

This would formally imply that the extracted power
\Delta W_{\rm FGR}^{\tau}/(2\tau)
and cooling current
\Delta Q_{\rm c,FGR}^{\tau}/(2\tau)
would become maximal for very fast cycles
\tau\to 0
, compare the blue curves in Fig.
2
.
There, we plot the power (top panel) and the cooling current (lower panel) versus the stroke duration for parameters that are optimal for the respective purpose in the infinitely slow limit, i.e., that
maximize (
15
) for the fixed temperature ratio of
\beta_{c}=3\beta_{h}
.

One may be tempted to assess the engine performance using the traditional definitions of work (
2
) and heat (
3
) but with the coarse-grained rates (
6
) instead, compare the black curves in Fig.

and also App.

.
This treatment would correctly reproduce the system dynamics (at least in the weak-coupling regime), but neglects the control work spent for the cycle operation.
For cooling, this leads to the artifact that the apparent cooling current is above the FGR results (Fig.

lower panel) at short strokes.
This increase however is just control work spent to increase the energy of the system and therefore does not contribute to cooling of the cold reservoir.

) for the system and the associated average energetic changes in the reservoir (

) one can address the net extracted work (

) and the net cooling heat (

), compare the red symbols in Fig.

.

Not only do very fast cycles become dysfunctional, but also the performance of very slow cycles is reduced in comparison to the FGR limit as successive energy-preserving transitions (e.g. excitation-de-excitation processes) will lead to reservoir heating.
In the plots, we see that for the net (cooling) power there is an optimal stroke duration of
\Gamma\tau\approx 2
, which leads to a significant reduction of the maximum extractable power and cooling current in comparison to the FGR treatment.

IV
Anisotropic 2d Ising model

The anisotropic 2d classical Ising model

\displaystyle H_{S}(t)=-J_{x}(t)\sum_{ij}\sigma^{z}_{ij}\sigma^{z}_{i+1,j}-J_{y}(t)\sum_{ij}\sigma^{z}_{ij}\sigma^{z}_{i,j+1}

(17)

has an exact equilibrium solution due to Onsager
[
60
]
.
It predicts an order-disorder phase transition at

\displaystyle\sinh(2\beta{\left|J_{x}\right|})\sinh(2\beta{\left|J_{y}\right|})=1\,,

(18)

with the disordered phase at the center
\beta J_{x/y}\approx 0
(corresponding e.g. to random spin orientations at high temperatures) and ordered phases in the quadrants (homogeneous domains, domains with vertical stripes, checkerboard domains, and horizontal stripe domains, respectively, as indicated in the inset of Fig.
3
).
At the phase transition, the correlation lengths diverge with the Ising critical exponent
\nu=1
, but will for the anisotropic model in general depend on direction
[
61
]
.
It is known
[
62
]
that its partition function and hence its equilibrium properties can be mapped to the 1d quantum Ising model in a transverse field
[
63
]
, and the investigations on the latter as a quantum working fluid
[
64
,
65
]
motivate us to consider the properties of this classical model as well.
The model can for example be implemented with silicon, where the spins map to the tilt of silicon dimers on the surface
[
66
]
.
Since energy-wise, the quadrants are equivalent, we concentrate on the first quadrant here and only consider
J_{x},J_{y}>0
as in the main plot of Fig.
3
.

First, for infinite stroke durations – we use the term
Onsager cycle
to denote this limit – we can obtain all energies by computing suitable derivatives of the
partition function as we discuss in App.
C
.
Numerical optimization then yields the parameters
J_{x/y}^{\nu}
that are optimal for work extraction or cooling in one Onsager cycle.
As in the Onsager cycle, points
A
and
C
correspond to exact equilibrium states, we display these points in Fig.
3
, where we see that for optimal performance, the working medium has to explore different phases.
We also mention that the optimum for work extraction is pretty narrow, but the optimal value for cooling (purple
C
-square in Fig.
3
) can be moved vertically without substantial changes to the Onsager cycle performance (its optimal
J_{y}
-value is presumably at infinity).
The optimal Onsager cycles are depicted by the orange dots and black lines in Fig.
4
, where one can see that the orientation of the cycle for cooling is opposite to the orientation for work extraction.

Second, for finite-time cycles, none of the cycle points correspond to exact equilibrium states.
Nevertheless, by computing trajectories (see App.
D
) we can also obtain solutions of rate equations.
In contrast to the two level system with energy differences
\pm\omega_{\nu}
, already single spinflips allow for nine different system energy changes per jump

\Omega\in\{\pm(4J_{x}^{\nu}+4J_{y}^{\nu}),\pm(4J_{x}^{\nu}-4J_{y}^{\nu}),\pm 4J_{x}^{\nu},\pm 4J_{y}^{\nu},0\}
that enter the coarse-grained rates (
6
).
For each system energy change
\Omega
we can also compute the associated average reservoir energy change (
7
).
To model the relaxation technically, we determine the associated waiting times and perform jumps such that on average,
the solution of the rate equation is reproduced, see App
D
.
After an initial rather long equilibration with the cold reservoir, the Otto cycle protocol is repeated for 100 cycles.

V
Conclusions

The main purpose of our study was to investigate the use of a 2d Ising model as a quantum working fluid in a finite-stroke heat engine.
Although the working fluid explores different phases for optimal performance, a frictionless drive does not induce critical slow-down, and the
resulting cyclic operation shows characteristics that are similar to an uncritical two-level system.

Additionally, we pointed out the importance of assessing reservoir energetic changes in finite-time cyclic quantum heat engines.
For finite times, even in the weak-coupling regime and for frictionless working fluids, an analysis of quantum Otto cycles taking the control
work contributions into account reveals the existence of an optimal stroke duration time.
When cycles are driven much faster, the control work spent for coupling and decoupling to reservoirs exceeds the gain.
Also for slower cycles, (net) zero energy transitions may lead to additional reservoir heating.

We observed these effects for finite-time cycles already in the weak-coupling regime.
In the regimes of stronger system-reservoir couplings, the control work can be expected to become even more relevant
[
68
,
69
]
.
Exactly solvable models
[
70
]
may prove useful to calibrate the theoretical methods in this regime,
which is an interesting field of further research.

Appendix A
Two-level system

A.1
Infinitely slow cycle

For infinitely long equilibration times, the states at points
A
and
C
of the cycle will just be thermal ones.
The partition function
Z(\beta)=2\cosh(\beta\omega/2)
yields the energy
E_{\rm th}=-\frac{\omega}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)
in a thermal state at inverse temperature
\beta
.
We can calculate all energies along an idealized cycle

\displaystyle E_{A}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{c}}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta_{c}\omega_{c}}{2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle E_{B}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{h}}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta_{c}\omega_{c}}{2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle E_{C}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{h}}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta_{h}\omega_{h}}{2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle E_{D}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{c}}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta_{h}\omega_{h}}{2}\right)\,,

(19)

where
E_{A}
and
E_{C}
just correspond to the thermal expectation values, and where we have due to the simple structure of the Hamiltonian deduced the other energies from

E_{B}=\frac{\omega_{h}}{\omega_{c}}E_{A}
and
E_{D}=\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega_{h}}E_{C}
.
From this, we in turn obtain the extracted work and the heat entering from the cold reservoir as in Eq. (
15
) in the main text.
The heat entering from the hot reservoir becomes

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{\rm h,FGR}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{h}}{2}\left[\tanh\left(\frac{\beta_{c}\omega_{c}}{2}\right)-\tanh\left(\frac{\beta_{h}\omega_{h}}{2}\right)\right]\,.

(20)

For the extraction of work, it follows that
the efficiency is
\eta=\left(1-\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega_{h}}\right)\Theta(\Delta W)\leq\eta_{\rm Carnot}
, where the last inequality follows by case distinction: When
\omega_{h}>\omega_{c}
, we have to fulfil
\beta_{c}\omega_{c}>\beta_{h}\omega_{h}
, such that the extraction of work is enabled when
\omega_{h}\in[\omega_{c},\omega_{c}\frac{\beta_{c}}{\beta_{h}}]
.
To the contrary, when
\omega_{h}<\omega_{c}
, we would also reqire
\beta_{c}\omega_{c}<\beta_{h}\omega_{h}
, which can for negative
\omega_{\nu}
be achieved as
\omega_{h}\in[\omega_{c}\frac{\beta_{c}}{\beta_{h}},\omega_{c}]
.

To cool the cold reservoir, we have to invest work, leading to a coefficient of performance
\kappa=\Delta Q_{c}/(-\Delta W)\Theta(\Delta Q_{c})=\frac{1}{\frac{\omega_{h}}{\omega_{c}}-1}\Theta(\Delta Q_{c})
.
The additional constraint that heat should be extracted from the cold reservoir leads for
\omega_{c}>0
to cooling in the window
\omega_{h}\in[\beta_{c}\omega_{c}/\beta_{h},\infty)
, or for
\omega_{c}<0
to cooling in the window
\omega_{h}\in(-\infty,\beta_{c}\omega_{c}/\beta_{h}]
.

Thus, we always have
\omega_{h}\omega_{c}>0
for cooling or work extraction, and level crossings (with
\omega_{h}\omega_{c}<0
) would not allow the extraction of work or cooling of the cold reservoir with the cycle operation.

A.2
Finite-time cycles

When we do not quench through a crossing during
A\to B
or
C\to D
(as necessary for useful function), a system starting in an excited state will remain so during the isentropic quenches.
For the finite-time dissipative strokes, the two probabilities
P_{+}
for being in the excited state and
P_{-}=1-P_{+}
for being in the ground state will thus obey
(excitation rate
R_{\uparrow}
and deexcitation rate
R_{\downarrow}
can in principle still depend on the stroke duration)

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{+}

\displaystyle=R_{\uparrow}P_{-}-R_{\downarrow}P_{+}\,,\qquad\dot{P}_{-}=-\dot{P}_{+}\,,

(21)

which can be directly solved

\displaystyle P_{+}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-(R_{\uparrow}+R_{\downarrow})t}P_{+}^{0}+\frac{R_{\uparrow}}{R_{\downarrow}+R_{\uparrow}}\left(1-e^{-(R_{\uparrow}+R_{\downarrow})t}\right)\,.

This allows to construct the full time-dependent solution by concatenating the above evolution along the dissipative strokes with constant populations along the unitary strokes and for alternating thermal reservoirs.
For example, when
P_{+,n}^{A}(t)
denotes the probability to be in the excited state (
+
) during the stroke
D\to A
in the
n
-th repetition of the cycle, it follows from the constant populations during the unitary strokes that the initial occupation of each (dissipative) stroke is given by the final occupation of the previous (dissipative) stroke

\displaystyle P_{+,n}^{C}(0)=P_{+,n}^{A}(\tau_{c})\,,\qquad P_{+,n+1}^{A}(0)=P_{+,n}^{C}(\tau_{h})\,.

(22)

When the system reaches a limit cycle, we may drop the index
n
, and the above two equations constitute a linear system that can be solved for the limit cycle probabilities (overbars)

\bar{P}_{+}^{A}\equiv\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}P_{+,n}^{A}(\tau_{c})
and

\bar{P}_{+}^{C}\equiv\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}P_{+,n}^{C}(\tau_{h})
.
For example, setting the duration of the dissipative strokes equal
\tau_{c}=\tau_{h}=\tau
, the limit cycle probabilities are obtained by solving

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{+}^{A}

\displaystyle=e^{-(R_{\uparrow}^{c}+R_{\downarrow}^{c})\tau}\bar{P}_{+}^{C}+\frac{R_{\uparrow}^{c}}{R_{\downarrow}^{c}+R_{\uparrow}^{c}}\left(1-e^{-(R_{\uparrow}^{c}+R_{\downarrow}^{c})\tau}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{+}^{C}

\displaystyle=e^{-(R_{\uparrow}^{h}+R_{\downarrow}^{h})\tau}\bar{P}_{+}^{A}+\frac{R_{\uparrow}^{h}}{R_{\downarrow}^{h}+R_{\uparrow}^{h}}\left(1-e^{-(R_{\uparrow}^{h}+R_{\downarrow}^{h})\tau}\right)\,,

(23)

where
R^{\nu}_{\uparrow}
and
R^{\nu}_{\downarrow}
are the excitation and decay rates when coupled to reservoir
\nu
, respectively.
From these, we can then compute the system energies at all points of the limit cycle via

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{c}}{2}[2\bar{P}_{+}^{A}-1]\,,\qquad\bar{E}_{B}=\frac{\omega_{h}}{2}[2\bar{P}_{+}^{A}-1]\,,

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{h}}{2}[2\bar{P}_{+}^{C}-1]\,,\qquad\bar{E}_{D}=\frac{\omega_{c}}{2}[2\bar{P}_{+}^{C}-1]\,,

(24)

and from that we find that the apparent work (
2
) per limit cycle and the heat entering the system (
3
) via the cold thermalization stroke, respectively, are limited by the difference of excited state populations via

\displaystyle\Delta\bar{W}=(\bar{P}_{+}^{C}-\bar{P}_{+}^{A})(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c})\,,\qquad\Delta\bar{Q}_{c}=(\bar{P}_{+}^{A}-\bar{P}_{+}^{C})\omega_{c}\,.

(25)

A.3
Fermi golden rule rates

In the specific case where the rates are independent of the cycle time given by
R^{c}_{\uparrow}=\Gamma f_{c}
,
R^{c}_{\downarrow}=\Gamma(1-f_{c})
,
R^{h}_{\uparrow}=\Gamma f_{h}
, and
R^{h}_{\downarrow}=\Gamma(1-f_{h})
with

f_{\nu}=[e^{\beta_{\nu}\omega_{\nu}}+1]^{-1}
, we obtain that work and transferred heats are all reduced by the same factor
as in Eq. (
16
) in the main text

\displaystyle 0\leq\tanh\left(\frac{\Gamma\tau}{2}\right)\leq 1\,,

(26)

such that efficiency and coefficient of performance would just remain the same.
This factor shows that for very large cycle times, the previous results are recovered, but in this limit the power (work by cycle time) and also the cooling current (heat per period by cycle time) will drop to zero.
Additionally, constant rates would suggest that to maximize power, it would be much more favorable to operate in the regime where
\Gamma\tau\ll 1
, where the power roughly approaches
P\approx\frac{\Gamma}{4}\Delta W
, and the cooling current approaches
J_{c}\approx\frac{\Gamma}{4}\Delta Q_{c}
.
For this limit, we can also calculate the second moment of the work

\displaystyle{\langle\Delta W^{2}\rangle}

\displaystyle={\langle(E_{A}-E_{B}+E_{C}-E_{D})^{2}\rangle}

(27)

\displaystyle=\left<\left[\left(1-\frac{\omega_{h}}{\omega_{c}}\right)E_{A}+\left(1-\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega_{h}}\right)E_{C}\right]^{2}\right>

\displaystyle=(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c})^{2}/2

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(1-\frac{\omega_{h}}{\omega_{c}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega_{h}}\right){\langle E_{A}E_{C}+E_{C}E_{A}\rangle}\,.

Eventually, this also yields the second cumulant of the work

\displaystyle\langle\langle\Delta W^{2}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2\left(e^{\Gamma\tau}-1\right)(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c})^{2}}{\left(1+e^{\Gamma\tau}\right)^{2}}[f_{c}+f_{h}-2f_{c}f_{h}]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\left(1-e^{-\Gamma\tau}\right)\left(1+e^{+2\Gamma\tau}\right)(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c})^{2}}{\left(1+e^{\Gamma\tau}\right)^{2}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times[f_{c}(1-f_{c})+f_{h}(1-f_{h})]\,,

(28)

which is always positive and for slow cycles
\Gamma\tau\to\infty
approaches
\langle\langle\Delta W^{2}\rangle\rangle\to(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c})^{2}[f_{c}(1-f_{c})+f_{h}(1-f_{h})]
, which is just the result that one would get by treating the energies at
A
and
C
as statistically independent, such that their cumulants add up.
As a further sanity check, we also mention that when converting this into the error of the power, this also matches the blue error bars obtained from trajectories in Fig.
2
top panel.

A.4
Finite-time rates

We can repeat the above analysis for realistic coarse-grained rates (
6
), from which with exploiting symmetries of the Fermi function
f_{\nu}(\omega)=[e^{\beta_{\nu}\omega}+1]^{-1}
we can write the excitation and deexcitation rates as

\displaystyle R^{\nu,\tau}_{\uparrow}

\displaystyle=\int\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)f_{\nu}(\omega)\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[\frac{(\omega-\omega_{\nu})\tau}{2}\right]d\omega\,,

\displaystyle R^{\nu,\tau}_{\downarrow}

\displaystyle=\int\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[\frac{(\omega-\omega_{\nu})\tau}{2}\right]d\omega-R^{\nu,\tau}_{\uparrow}\,,

(29)

which explicitly depend on the stroke duration
\tau
.
Assuming for simplicity equal stroke durations, the wide-band limit
\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)=\Gamma_{\nu}
, and also equal coupling strengths
\Gamma=R^{\nu,\tau}_{\downarrow}+R^{\nu,\tau}_{\uparrow}
to the reservoirs, we obtain
for the apparent work per cycle and apparent heat uptake from the cold reservoir

\displaystyle{\langle\Delta W\rangle}^{\tau}_{\rm app}

\displaystyle=\frac{(R^{h,\tau}_{\uparrow}-R^{c,\tau}_{\uparrow})(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c})}{\Gamma}\tanh\left(\frac{\Gamma\tau}{2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle{\langle\Delta Q_{c}\rangle}^{\tau}_{\rm app}

\displaystyle=\frac{(R^{c,\tau}_{\uparrow}-R^{h,\tau}_{\uparrow})\omega_{c}}{\Gamma}\tanh\left(\frac{\Gamma\tau}{2}\right)\,,

(30)

which shows that for very short cycle times, both quantities scale quadratically in
\tau
, such that also the power and cooling current will tend to zero for very short stroke durations
\tau
(such a turnover would become visible in Fig.
2
lower panel for even shorter stroke times).

For finite cycle times
\tau
, the system energy changes do not correctly reflect the energetic changes of the reservoir.
Instead, when while coupled to reservoir
\nu
, the system undergoes the energy change
\Omega\in\{-\omega_{\nu},+\omega_{\nu}\}
, the actual associated energy change (
7
) of the reservoir would be

\displaystyle\Delta E^{\nu,\tau}_{\Omega}=-\frac{\int\omega\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)f_{\nu}(\omega)\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[\frac{(\omega-\Omega)\tau}{2}\right]d\omega}{\int\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)f_{\nu}(\omega)\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[\frac{(\omega-\Omega)\tau}{2}\right]d\omega}\,,

(31)

which for
\tau\to\infty
just becomes
-\Omega
.
Thus, even a system undergoing no net transition at all (e.g. after one excitation and one de-excitation) will lead to heating of the reservoir.
To get the average net work extracted or average net cooling heat along the limit cycle, we have to sample over possible trajectories (compare App.
D
) with the coarse-grained rates and track the average reservoir energy changes accordingly, compare the red symbols Fig.
2
.
The net result is that for
\tau\to 0
, fewer transitions per cycle happen and even for a low temperature reservoir excitation processes become just as likely as deexcitation processes:
No work can be extracted, no reservoir can be cooled, and all cycles are dysfunctional.

Appendix B
Coarse-grained transition rates

B.1
General derivation

For finite stroke times
\tau
and weak coupling strengths, time-dependent perturbation theory tells us that the system-bath density matrix can for an interaction
H_{I}
be written as (bold symbols indicate the interaction picture)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t+\tau)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)-{\rm i}\left[\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$H_{I}$}(t_{1}),\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)\right]

(32)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$H_{I}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$H_{I}$}(t_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad-\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}\Theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$H_{I}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$H_{I}$}(t_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}\Theta(t_{2}-t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$H_{I}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$H_{I}$}(t_{2})\,.

Thus, under the usual assumptions (specifically, assuming that at the beginning of the dissipative strokes
\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)=\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)\otimes\bar{\rho}_{B}
with
[\bar{\rho}_{B},H_{B}]=0
, writing furthermore the interaction Hamiltonian with hermitian system and bath coupling operators as
\mbox{\boldmath$H_{I}$}(t)=\sum_{\alpha}\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t)\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\alpha}$}(t)
, the correlation function as
C_{\alpha\beta}(t_{1}-t_{2})={\rm Tr}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\bar{\rho}_{B}\right\}
, and assuming that
{\rm Tr}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\alpha}$}(t)\bar{\rho}_{B}\right\}=0
), the propagated system
density matrix becomes

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t+\tau)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)+\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta}C_{\alpha\beta}(t_{1}-t_{2})\times

\displaystyle\times\Big[\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad-\Theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)

\displaystyle\qquad-\Theta(t_{2}-t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}(t)$}\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\Big]\,.

(33)

In general, the associated coarse-grained dissipator
{\cal L}_{\tau}
defined by
{\cal L}_{\tau}\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)=\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t+\tau)-\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)}{\tau}
need not decouple the evolution of
populations and coherences in the system energy eigenbasis.
Specific system-reservoir interactions may however lead to such a decoupled evolution.
For example, under the plausible assumption that each spin
(ij)
of a 2d spin working fluid (
17
) hosts a single spinflip coupling operator
S_{\alpha}\to S_{ij}=\sigma^{x}_{ij}
to its separate reservoir
C_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)\to C_{k\ell,ij}(\tau)=\delta_{ki}\delta_{\ell j}C_{ij}(\tau)
with correlation function as detailed below in App.
B.2
, it follows that for any two system energy eigenstates
\left|a\right>
and
\left|b\right>
and for an initially diagonal state

\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)=\sum_{c}\rho_{S}^{cc}(t)\left|c\right>\left<c\right|

one has for the time-evolved matrix elements
\rho_{S}^{ab}(t+\tau)\equiv\left<a\right|\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t+\tau)\left|b\right>
the relations

\displaystyle\rho_{S}^{ab}(t+\tau)

\displaystyle=\delta_{ab}\rho_{S}^{aa}(t)+\delta_{ab}\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}\sum_{ij}C_{ij}(t_{1}-t_{2})\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big[e^{-{\rm i}(E_{a}-E_{F_{ij}^{a}})(t_{1}-t_{2})}\rho_{S}^{F_{ij}^{a}F_{ij}^{a}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-e^{+{\rm i}(E_{a}-E_{F_{ij}^{a}})(t_{1}-t_{2})}\rho_{S}^{aa}(t)\Big]\,,

(34)

where
\left|F_{ij}^{a}\right>\equiv\sigma^{x}_{ij}\left|a\right>
.
In other words, in this case initially diagonal density matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)
remain diagonal at time
t+\tau
, which justifies our second main assumption in the main text.

From the above considerations, we can also calculate the change of the system energy

\displaystyle\Delta E_{S}

\displaystyle=E_{S}(t+\tau)-E_{S}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta}C_{\alpha\beta}(t_{1}-t_{2})\times

\displaystyle\quad\times{\rm Tr}\Big\{[\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})H_{S}\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad-\Theta(t_{1}-t_{2})H_{S}\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad-\Theta(t_{2}-t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})H_{S}]\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)\Big\}\,.

(35)

Representing the system coupling operators in the energy eigenbasis
\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t)=\sum_{ab}S_{\alpha}^{ab}\left|a\right>\left<b\right|e^{{\rm i}(E_{a}-E_{b})t}
and assuming that
\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)=\sum_{a}\rho_{S}^{aa}(t)\left|a\right>\left<a\right|
is initially diagonal in that energy eigenbasis, we eventually obtain the expression

\displaystyle\Delta E_{S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ab}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\int\limits_{0}^{\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}C_{\alpha\beta}(t_{1}-t_{2})S_{\alpha}^{ab}S_{\beta}^{ba}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times e^{{\rm i}(E_{a}-E_{b})(t_{1}-t_{2})}(E_{b}-E_{a})\rho_{S}^{aa}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{ab}\tau(E_{b}-E_{a})R_{a\to b}^{\tau}\rho_{S}^{aa}(t)\,,

\displaystyle R_{a\to b}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}S_{\alpha}^{ab}S_{\beta}^{ba}\int d\omega\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[\frac{(E_{a}-E_{b}-\omega)\tau}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\int\tilde{R}_{a\to b}^{\tau}(\omega)d\omega\,,

(36)

where we have inserted the Fourier transform
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\int C_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)e^{+{\rm i}\omega\tau}d\tau
and the effective transition rate
R_{a\to b}^{\tau}
– equivalent to the coarse-graining approach with coarse-graining time
\tau

[
55
]
.
For large
\tau\to\infty
this approaches the Fermi golden rule limit
R_{a\to b}^{\infty}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}S_{\alpha}^{ab}S_{\beta}^{ba}\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(E_{a}-E_{b})
that satisfies detailed balance
R_{b\to a}^{\infty}=e^{-\beta(E_{a}-E_{b})}R_{a\to b}^{\infty}
and for short coarse-graining times the rates scale linearly in
\tau
.

Under analogous assumptions we can also calculate the evolution of the bath energy

\displaystyle\Delta E_{B}

\displaystyle=E_{B}(t+\tau)-E_{B}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}\Big[{\rm Tr_{S}}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\right\}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\rm Tr_{B}}\left\{H_{B}\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\bar{\rho}_{B}\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle\quad-\Theta(t_{1}-t_{2}){\rm Tr_{S}}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)\right\}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\rm Tr_{B}}\left\{H_{B}\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\bar{\rho}_{B}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad-\Theta(t_{2}-t_{1}){\rm Tr_{S}}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\right\}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\rm Tr_{B}}\left\{H_{B}\bar{\rho}_{B}\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\right\}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}{\rm Tr_{S}}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)\right\}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\rm Tr_{B}}\left\{\left[\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\alpha}$}(t_{1}),H_{B}\right]\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\bar{\rho}_{B}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ab}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}S_{\alpha}^{ab}S_{\beta}^{ba}e^{{\rm i}(E_{a}-E_{b})(t_{1}-t_{2})}\rho_{S}^{aa}(t)\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\rm i}\frac{d}{dt_{1}}C_{\alpha\beta}(t_{1}-t_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{ab}\tau\frac{\int\omega\tilde{R}_{a\to b}^{\tau}(\omega)d\omega}{R_{a\to b}^{\tau}}R_{a\to b}^{\tau}\rho_{S}^{aa}(t)\,.

(37)

Here, the rate
\tilde{R}_{a\to b}^{\tau}(\omega)
could be interpreted as a conditional probability density of a reservoir energy change of
+\omega
whilst the system undergoes an energetic change
E_{b}-E_{a}
.
We emphasize that for finite
\tau
one has in general
\Delta E_{B}\neq-\Delta E_{S}
.
Only for infinitely long dissipative strokes one recovers this equivalence as
\int\omega\tilde{R}_{a\to b}^{\infty}(\omega)d\omega=-(E_{b}-E_{a})R_{a\to b}^{\infty}
.
Rather,
\Delta E_{S}+\Delta E_{B}
should be regarded as control work that always has to be invested to keep the cycle running, such that this contribution should be considered when analyzing the efficiencies or coefficient of performance of finite-time cycles.
Furthermore, the energy-resolved rates satisfy the relation
\tilde{R}_{b\to a}^{\tau}(-\omega)=e^{-\beta\omega}\tilde{R}_{a\to b}^{\tau}(+\omega)
.

Alternatively, we can use methods of Full Counting Statistics
[
72
]
to define a moment-generating function for the change of the reservoir energy

\displaystyle M(\chi,\tau)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}_{\chi/2}(\tau)\rho_{S}(t)\otimes\bar{\rho}_{B}\mbox{\boldmath$U$}_{-\chi/2}^{\dagger}(\tau)\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mbox{\boldmath$U$}_{\chi/2}(t)

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}e^{+{\rm i}\chi/2H_{B}}\mbox{\boldmath$H_{I}$}(t)e^{-{\rm i}\chi/2H_{B}}\mbox{\boldmath$U$}_{\chi/2}(t)\,,

(38)

from which moments of the change of the reservoir energy can be obtained by suitable derivatives

{\langle(H_{B}-E_{B}^{0})^{n}\rangle}=(-{\rm i}\partial_{\chi})^{n}M(\chi,\tau)|_{\chi=0}
.
Upon a second order expansion in the interaction we can write it in terms of a shifted correlation function

\displaystyle M(\chi,\tau)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{\alpha\beta}\int\limits_{t}^{t+\tau}dt_{1}dt_{2}{\rm Tr}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\alpha}$}(t_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$S_{\beta}$}(t_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)\right\}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[C_{\alpha\beta}(t_{1}-t_{2}-\chi)-C_{\alpha\beta}(t_{1}-t_{2})\right]\,,

(39)

and the insertion of the Fourier transform allows under the same assumptions on
\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)
as before to write it as

\displaystyle M(\chi,\tau)=1+\tau\sum_{ab}\int d\omega\tilde{R}_{a\to b}^{\tau}(\omega)\left(e^{+{\rm i}\omega\chi}-1\right)\rho_{S}^{aa}(t)\,,

which shows that indeed
\tilde{R}_{a\to b}^{\tau}(\omega)
can be interpreted as a conditional probability density of energy increase
\omega
in the reservoir whilst the system undergoes the transition
a\to b
.

B.2
Specific Example

We can make these considerations more explicit for single-spinflip coupling operators (one for each spin
\alpha
)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha}=\sigma^{x}_{\alpha}\,,\qquad B_{\alpha}=\sum_{k}t_{k\alpha}\sigma^{x}_{k\alpha}

(40)

and a spin-local reservoir of two-level systems
H_{B}=\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{k}\frac{\omega_{k\alpha}}{2}\sigma^{z}_{k\alpha}
,
where for a canonical equilibrium state
\bar{\rho}_{B}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{B}}}{{\rm Tr}\left\{e^{-\beta H_{B}}\right\}}
we find for the correlation function

\displaystyle C_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\int\frac{\Gamma(\omega)[1-f(\omega)]}{2\pi}e^{-{\rm i}\omega\tau}d\omega\,,

\displaystyle f(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}\,.

(41)

Above, we have analytically continued the reservoir spectral function

\Gamma(\omega)=2\pi\sum_{k}t_{k\alpha}^{2}\delta(\omega-\omega_{k\alpha})=\Gamma(-\omega)
as an even function to the complete real axis.
This implies that the Fourier transform of the correlation function is

\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\Gamma(\omega)[1-f(\omega)]
.
As the system energy eigenbasis is the
\sigma^{z}_{\alpha}
eigenbasis, the rates (
36
) become

\displaystyle R_{a\to b}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\delta_{{\left|\mbox{\boldmath$a$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\right|}/2,1}\int d\omega\Gamma(\omega)[1-f(\omega)]\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[\frac{(E_{a}-E_{b}-\omega)\tau}{2}\right]\,,

(42)

which – with
f(-\omega)=1-f(+\omega)
and
\delta_{{\left|\mbox{\boldmath$a$}-\mbox{\boldmath$b$}\right|}/2,1}
enforcing single spin flip energy differences – is equivalent to Eq.(
6
).

B.3
Generic properties of coarse-grained rates

For
\tau\to\infty
we recover from Eq. (
6
) – by using the identity

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[\frac{(\Omega-\omega)\tau}{2}\right]=\delta(\Omega-\omega)

(43)

and further
f(-\omega)=1-f(\omega)
as well as
\Gamma(-\omega)=\Gamma(+\omega)
– the FGR rates (
4

For finite

Nevertheless, in the wideband limit
\Gamma(\omega)\to\Gamma
(e.g. take
\delta_{\nu}\to\infty
in Eq. (
5

it follows that

\displaystyle\Delta R

\displaystyle\equiv R^{\nu,\tau}_{+\Omega}-R^{\nu,\tau}_{-\Omega}

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\tau}{2\pi}\int d\omega f(\omega)\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times[{\rm sinc}^{2}((\omega-\Omega)\tau/2)-{\rm sinc}^{2}((\omega+\Omega)\tau/2)]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\tau}{2\pi}\int d\omega[2f(\omega)-1]{\rm sinc}^{2}((\omega-\Omega)\tau/2)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Gamma\tau}{2\pi}\int d\omega\tanh(\beta\omega/2){\rm sinc}^{2}((\omega-\Omega)\tau/2)\,,

from which from symmetry arguments one can deduce that this is negative when

For highly peaked and specifically tuned spectral functions however, finite-time couplings may favor population inversion, which has been proposed for performance increases of heat engines at fast strokes

The partition function per spin for the 2D anisotropic Ising model (

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\theta_{x}\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\theta_{y}\ln\Big[\cosh(2\beta J_{x})\cosh(2\beta J_{y})

\displaystyle\qquad-\sinh(2\beta J_{x})\cos(\theta_{x})-\sinh(2\beta J_{y})\cos(\theta_{y})\Big]\,.

(45)

Also a single-integral representation is possible, but may suffer from numerical instabilities for

and

\displaystyle{\langle X\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{J_{x}}\ln Z(\beta)\,,\qquad{\langle Y\rangle}=\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{J_{y}}\ln Z(\beta)\,,

\displaystyle{\langle H\rangle}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\beta}\ln Z(\beta)=-J_{x}{\langle X\rangle}-J_{y}{\langle Y\rangle}\,,

\displaystyle={\langle H_{h}\rangle}_{C}=-J_{x}^{h}{\langle X\rangle}_{C}-J_{y}^{h}{\langle Y\rangle}_{C}\,.

(47)

Appendix D
Computation of trajectories

The state
\sigma
of a
100\times 100
spin lattice may assume
2^{10000}
different configurations.
The direct solution of such high-dimensional rate equations for probabilities
P_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}=\sum_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime}}\left[R_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime}\to\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}P_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime}}-R_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\to\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime}}P_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}\right]

(49)

thus easily exceeds computational capabilities even when the (coarse-grained) transition rates
R_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\to\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime}}\geq 0
are restricted to single bitflips.
Therefore, we resort to trajectory solutions composed of jumps between different states, that on average reproduce the probabilities.
The trajectories are constructed as follows:
Being in state
\sigma
at time
t
we compute a waiting time
\tau_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}
for remaining in that state.
To obtain the proper statistics dictated by the transition rates, the waiting time is sampled from a uniformly distributed random number
r\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\tau_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}=\frac{-\ln(1-r)}{\sum\limits_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}R_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\to\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime}}}\,.

(50)

After propagating time
t\to t+\tau_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}
, a jump is performed to a different state
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime\prime}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}
with conditional probability

\displaystyle P_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\to\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime\prime}}^{\rm jump}=\frac{R_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\to\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime\prime}}}{\sum\limits_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}R_{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\to\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{\prime}}}\,.

(51)

Compared to the direct solution of the rate equation, this yields a tremendous reduction in storage, as only the spin configuration
\sigma
needs to be stored.
For large spin systems, already single trajectories may give good indication for the average behaviour.
Additionally, we exploit the translational invariance of the lattice to speed up the computations by applying a procedure known as
N
-fold way
[
73
]
:
We dynamically classify all spins on the lattice according to the configuration
of their nearest neighbours into
3\cdot 3=9
different classes for which (
17
) leads to distinct energy changes upon single spin flips.
The sum over all possible transition rates can then be simplified by realizing that the rates for all members of such a class are identical.
Furthermore, the question of which jump to perform (i.e., which spin to flip eventually) can be broken down to which type of spinflip process (which class) should occur and then
flipping a random representative spin of that class (locally updating its class and that of its neighbours).
