Framing lattices and flow polytopes
Matias von Bell, Cesar Ceballos

Abstract
. Flow polytopes of acyclic oriented graphs arise naturally in combinatorial optimization, and the study of their volumes and triangulations has revealed intriguing connections across combinatorics, geometry, algebra, and representation theory. In this work, we introduce the framing lattice associated with a framed graph, whose Hasse diagram is dual to a framed triangulation of the corresponding flow polytope. Framing lattices are remarkable in that they provide a unifying framework encompassing many classical and well-studied lattice structures, including the Boolean lattice, the Tamari lattice, and the weak order on permutations. They further subsume a broad array of examples such as all type- A A Cambrian lattices, the Grassmann and grid-Tamari lattices, the alt- ν \nu -Tamari and cross-Tamari lattices, the permutree lattices, and the τ \tau -tilting posets of certain gentle algebras. We show, among several foundational structural properties, that the framing lattice is a semidistributive, congruence uniform, and polygonal lattice, with its polygons consisting of squares, pentagons, and hexagons. We study its connections to noncrossing partitions via Reading’s core label orders, simple representations of its join and meet irreducible elements, and several of its lattice congruences and quotients induced by a graph operation called an M-move. Key words and phrases : Framing lattice, flow polytope, DKK triangulation, weak order, Tamari lattice, Cambrian lattice, cross-Tamari lattice.



Introduction

Flow polytopes model the space of flows in directed acyclic graphs.
They form an important family of polytopes in combinatorial optimization, with connections to several other areas, including representation theory

, toric geometry
[

Flow polytopes include many interesting renown examples, such as Tesler polytopes

[

The flow polytopes we consider are limited to those with unit flows, that is, those with an inflow of size one in the unique source, an outflow of size one at the unique sink, and a netflow zero at each other vertex.
An important class of triangulations of flow polytopes with unit flow are its framing triangulations (also called DKK triangulations) due to Danilov–Karzanov–Koshevoy
[
12
]
.
These triangulations arise from assigning a framing
F
to the underlying directed acyclic graph
G
, which is a linear order on incoming and outgoing edges at each vertex.
A directed acyclic graph
G
with a framing
F
is denoted
(G,F)

Hasse diagrams of various combinatorially interesting lattices have appeared as dual graphs of framing triangulations of flow polytopes in the recent literature
[
41
,
38
,
16
]

These include the
\nu
-Tamari lattices of Préville-Ratelle and Viennot
[
29

, principal order ideals in Young’s lattice, the
s
-weak order of Ceballos and Pons
[

, and
\tau
-tilting posets of certain gentle algebras
[
38

In each of these cases, the lattice structure on the dual graph was inherited from a previously known lattice, and no unifying partial order on the facets of the triangulation was given.
It is thus natural to wonder if the lattice structures arise directly from the unifying objects, namely, the underlying framed graphs that determine the framing triangulations.

In this paper, we propose a unified framework for studying the previously discussed lattice structures, as well as many other notable lattices, within the context of framing lattice theory.
Quite remarkably, it turns out that the facets in a framing triangulation of a flow polytope can always be partially ordered to form a lattice, with the lattice structure arising from the underlying framed graph.
We call such lattices
framing lattices
, as each framing of a directed acyclic graph induces such a framing lattice on the corresponding framing triangulation.
We note that independent progress to answer the same question was made by Berggren and Serhiyenko
[
4
, Corollary 4.9]
, who proved the lattice property in the special case that the exceptional paths in the framed graph are incident to a source or a sink, which they call rooted framed graphs.
These rooted framed graphs generalize the amply framed graphs of
[
38
]
, but do not generally include the framed graphs with more than two edges (or paths) between two inner vertices, such as the second framed graph in Figure

.
To simplify our exposition, we assume that a directed acyclic graph
G
has a unique source and sink. While this assumption can be omitted without affecting our results, which are combinatorial and lattice theoretic, we keep it to retain the direct connection to triangulated flow polytopes.

1.3.3.
Semidistributivity

Recall that a lattice
L
is
join-semidistributive
if any elements
x
,
y
, and
z
in
L
with
z\vee x=z\vee y
also satisfy
z\vee(x\wedge y)=z\vee x
.
Similarly,
L
is
meet-semidistributive
if the dual condition holds, i.e.
z\wedge(x\vee y)=z\wedge x
whenever
z\wedge x=z\wedge y
.
A lattice is
semidistributive
if it is both meet- and join-semidistributive.

Lemma 1.3.9
.

(BEZ Lemma for semidistributivity
[
33
, Lemma 9-2.6]
)
Let
L
be a finite lattice and let
x
,
y
,
z
be elements of
L
.
Consider the following criteria:

(i)

If
x
and
y
are covered by a common element and
z\vee x=z\vee y
, then

z\vee(x\wedge y)=z\vee x=z\vee y
.

(ii)

If
x
and
y
cover a common element and
z\wedge x=z\wedge y
, then
z\wedge(x\vee y)=z\wedge x=z\vee y
.

If
L
satisfies (i) then it is join-semidistributive, if
L
satisfies (ii) then it is meet-semidistributive, and if
L
satisfies both (i) and (ii) then it is semidistributive.
∎

To show that framing lattices are semidistributive, we make use of the following lemma.

\widetilde{P}_{x}<_{v}^{\mathrm{cw}}R_{x}
for some

.
Now,

is covered by

and they differ by routes that are also incoherent exactly at

with

contained in the route of

.
Therefore, by

Similarly, we obtain

We can now simply compute:

This finishes our proof.
∎

Proof.

We have shown that framing lattices are finite, polygonal, and semidsitributive, so it remains to find a labeling

If

(1) Let

-extended paths (resp.
\mathrm{cw}
-extended paths), such that
C_{2}=C_{\min}(\widetilde{P}_{2})
(resp.
C_{1}=C_{\max}(\widetilde{P}_{1})
).

Corollary 1.4.10
.

Let
\widetilde{P}_{1}
be an
\mathrm{cw}
-extended path and
\widetilde{P}_{2}
be a
\mathrm{ccw}
-extended path.

(1)

If
\mathfrak{j}=C_{\min}(\widetilde{P}_{2})
is the join irreducible element corresponding to
\widetilde{P}_{2}
and
\mathfrak{j}_{*}
is the unique element covered by
\mathfrak{j}
then
\widetilde{\ell}_{2}(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j})=\widetilde{P}_{2}
.

(2)

If
\mathfrak{m}=C_{\max}(\widetilde{P}_{1})
is the meet irreducible element corresponding to
\widetilde{P}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{m}^{*}
is the unique element covering
\mathfrak{m}
then
\widetilde{\ell}_{1}(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{*})=\widetilde{P}_{1}
.

Proof.

We prove only (1) because (2) follows by symmetry.
Let
\widetilde{P}_{2}^{\prime}:=\widetilde{\ell}_{2}(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j})
.
By
Lemma

1.4.9
, taking
C_{1}=\mathfrak{j}_{*}
and
C_{2}=\mathfrak{j}
we deduce that
\mathfrak{j}=C_{\min}(\widetilde{P}_{2}^{\prime})
. But by the injectivity
Lemma

1.4.6

\mathfrak{j}=C_{\min}(\widetilde{P}_{2})=C_{\min}(\widetilde{P}_{2}^{\prime})
implies that
\widetilde{P}_{2}=\widetilde{P}_{2}^{\prime}
as desired.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.4.4
.

By

1.4.5
and
1.4.6
, the map

is an injective map
from
\mathrm{ccw}
-extended paths to join irreducible elements. This map is surjective by
Lemma

1.4.9
(1).
Its inverse map is given by

\mathfrak{j}\rightarrow\widetilde{\ell}_{2}(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j}).

This proves part (1) of the theorem.

For part (2),

1.4.7
and
1.4.8
imply that

is an injective map from
\mathrm{cw}

1.4.9
(2).
Its inverse map is given by

\mathfrak{m}\rightarrow\widetilde{\ell}_{1}(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{*}).

∎

1.4.2.4.
Bijection between join irreducibles and meet irreducibles

As seen in the proof of
Theorem

1.4.4
, join irreducible elements
\mathfrak{j}
of the framing lattice can be identified with
\mathrm{ccw}
-extended paths, by taking the
\mathrm{ccw}
-extended path labeling
\widetilde{\ell}_{2}(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j})
.
Similarly, meet irreducible elements
\mathfrak{m}
correspond to
\mathrm{cw}
-extended paths, by taking the
\mathrm{cw}
-extended path labeling
\widetilde{\ell}_{1}(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{*})
.
On the other hand,
\mathrm{ccw}
-extended paths and
\mathrm{cw}
-extended paths are in correspondence via the inverse maps
\varphi^{\mathrm{cw}}
and
\varphi^{\mathrm{ccw}}
. So, we can think of these two maps as inverse maps between join irreducible and meet irreducible elements.

The following lemma implies that the join irreducible and meet irreducible elements corresponding to a cover relation are related by these two maps.

Lemma 1.4.11
.

Let
C_{1}\prec C_{2}
be two maximal cliques with
C_{2}=(C_{1}\setminus R_{1})\cup R_{2}
and
R_{1}<_{v}^{\mathrm{cw}}R_{2}
. Let
\widetilde{P}_{1}
and
\widetilde{P}_{2}
be the
\mathrm{cw}
-extended path and
\mathrm{ccw}
-extended path involved in the rotation. Then,

(1)

\widetilde{P}_{1}=\varphi^{\mathrm{cw}}(\widetilde{P}_{2})
.

(2)

\widetilde{P}_{2}=\varphi^{\mathrm{ccw}}(\widetilde{P}_{1})
.

Proof.

We prove only (1) because (2) follows by symmetry.
Let
P=\widetilde{P}_{1}\cap\widetilde{P}_{2}
be the path at which
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are incoherent, and let
w_{1}=\min(P)
and
w_{2}=\max(P)
.
Assume towards a contradiction that
\widetilde{P}_{1}\neq\varphi^{\mathrm{cw}}(\widetilde{P}_{2})
.
Then, there is either (i) an edge
e
entering
w_{1}
between
\widetilde{P}_{1}
and
\widetilde{P}_{2}
, or (ii) an edge
e
exiting
w_{2}
between
\widetilde{P}_{2}
and
\widetilde{P}_{1}
. In case (i), we know that
e
belongs to some route
R\in C_{1}
by
Lemma

1.1.8
. Furthermore,
w_{1}R=w_{1}R_{1}
otherwise
R
would be incoherent with either
R_{1}
or
\Bot(R_{1},R_{2})
in
C_{1}
. But then
R\in C_{1}
would be a route in between
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
which is a contradiction.
Case (ii) is solved similarly.
∎

Theorem 1.4.12
.

The framing lattice satisfies the following properties:

(1)

The map

\mathfrak{j}\rightarrow\mathfrak{m}:=C_{\max}(\widetilde{\ell}_{1}(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j}))

is a bijection from join irreducible to meet irreducible elements.

(2)

The map

\mathfrak{m}\rightarrow\mathfrak{j}:=C_{\min}(\widetilde{\ell}_{2}(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{*}))

is a bijection from meet irreducible to join irreducible elements.

(3)

These two maps are inverse to each other.

Proof.

For (1), consider the composition of maps

\mathfrak{j}\rightarrow\widetilde{P}_{2}:=\widetilde{\ell}_{2}(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j})\rightarrow\widetilde{P}_{1}:=\varphi^{\mathrm{cw}}(\widetilde{P_{2}})\rightarrow\mathfrak{m}:=C_{\max}(\widetilde{P}_{1}).

Each map in this composition is a bijection:

•

the first from join irreducible elements to
\mathrm{ccw}
-extended paths

•

the second from
\mathrm{ccw}
-extended paths to
\mathrm{cw}
-extended paths

•

the third from
\mathrm{cw}
-extended paths to meet irreducible elements

Therefore, this composition of maps is a bijection from join irreducible to meet irreducible elements.
Moreover,
Lemma

1.4.11
implies that
\widetilde{P}_{1}=\widetilde{\ell}_{1}(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j})
.
So, we obtain

\mathfrak{m}=C_{\max}(\widetilde{\ell}_{1}(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j}))

as desired.

For parts (2) and (3), we just need to consider the composition of the inverse maps in reverse direction:

\mathfrak{m}\rightarrow\widetilde{P}_{1}:=\widetilde{\ell}_{1}(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{*})\rightarrow\widetilde{P}_{2}:=\varphi^{\mathrm{ccw}}(\widetilde{P_{1}})\rightarrow\mathfrak{j}:=C_{\min}(\widetilde{P}_{2}).

Again,
Lemma

1.4.11
implies that
\widetilde{P}_{2}=\widetilde{\ell}_{2}(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{*})
.
So, we obtain

\mathfrak{j}=C_{\min}(\widetilde{\ell}_{2}(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{*}))
.
∎

As another consequence of
Lemma

1.4.11
we get the following corollary.

Corollary 1.4.13
.

Let
C_{1}\prec C_{2}
be two maximal cliques with
C_{2}=(C_{1}\setminus R_{1})\cup R_{2}
and
R_{1}<_{v}^{\mathrm{cw}}R_{2}
. Let
\widetilde{P}_{1}
and
\widetilde{P}_{2}
be the
\mathrm{cw}
-extended path and
\mathrm{ccw}
-extended path involved in the rotation. Then,

(1)

If
\mathfrak{j}=C_{\min}(\widetilde{P}_{2})
then
\widetilde{\ell}_{1}(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j})=\widetilde{P}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\ell}_{2}(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j})=\widetilde{P}_{2}
.

(2)

If
\mathfrak{m}=C_{\max}(\widetilde{P}_{1})
then
\widetilde{\ell}_{1}(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{*})=\widetilde{P}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\ell}_{2}(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{*})=\widetilde{P}_{2}
.

In other words, the
\mathrm{cw}
-extended paths and
\mathrm{ccw}
-extended paths involved in the rotations

,
\mathfrak{j}_{*}\prec\mathfrak{j}
and

are the same.

Proof.

We only prove (1) because (2) follows by symmetry.
Applying

1.4.11
to the two covering pairs

and

we get

\widetilde{P}_{1}=\varphi^{\mathrm{cw}}(\widetilde{P}_{2})

1.4.3.
The core label order of a congruence uniforme lattice

In
[
32
]
, Reading introduced the shard intersection order of a finite Coxeter group
W
, a lattice which contains the noncrossing partition lattice
NC(W)
associated to
W
as a sublattice. If
W
is the Coxeter group of type
A_{n-1}
, one recovers the classical noncrossing partition lattice of
[n]
.

One of Reading’s main motivations for considering shards is the study of lattice congruences of the poset of regions of a hyperplane arrangement. Shards are certain closed polyhedral cones obtained by decomposing (or cutting) the hyperplanes of the arrangement. The shard intersection order is the poset of all possible intersections of shards ordered by reverse inclusion.

In his Chapter
[
33
, Section 9-7.4]
, Reading proposed a generalization of the shard intersection order for any congruence uniform lattice
\mathscr{L}=(L,\leq)
. This is an alternate order structure on
L
which was coined the name
core label order
by Mühle in
[
23
]
. If
\mathscr{L}
is the poset of regions of the Coxeter hyperplane arrangement of
W
one recovers the shard intersection order of
W
. If
\mathscr{L}
is the classical Tamari lattice
\Tam(n)
then its core label order is the classical lattice of noncrossing partition of
[n]
.
In this section we recall the definition of the core label order, and propose the core label order of a framing lattice as the natural analog of the poset of noncrossing partitions for framing lattices.

Let
\mathscr{L}=(L,\leq)
be a finite congruence uniform lattice.
For a cover relation
u\prec v
in
L
let
cg(u,v)
be the finest lattice congruence of
\mathscr{L}
in which
u
and
v
are equivalent. These are precisely the
join irreducible lattice congruences
of
\mathscr{L}
. However, different covering pairs
u\prec v
may lead to the same lattice congruence. In fact, if
\mathscr{L}
is congruence uniform then the map
\mathfrak{j}\rightarrow cg(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j})
is a bijection between join irreducible elements and join irreducible lattice congruences of
\mathscr{L}
, see
[
33
, Section 9-7.4]
. In other words, for every covering pair
u\prec v
there is a unique join irreducible element
\mathfrak{j}
such that
cg(u,v)=cg(\mathfrak{j}_{*},\mathfrak{j})
. We denote this unique
\mathfrak{j}
as
\mathfrak{j}_{cg(u,v)}
. We label the edges
u\prec v
of the Hasse diagram of
\mathscr{L}
by their corresponding join irreducible elements
\mathfrak{j}_{cg(u,v)}
.

The following lemma gives a characterization of
\mathfrak{j}_{cg(u,v)}
, cf.
[
15
, Lemma 2.6]
.

Lemma 1.4.14

(Cf.
[
15
, Lemma 2.6]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}=(L,\leq)
be a congruence uniform lattice, and
u\prec v
be a covering pair. Then
\mathfrak{j}_{cg(u,v)}
is the unique join irreducible element
\mathfrak{j}
satisfying:

(1)

\mathfrak{j}\vee u=v

(2)

\mathfrak{j}\wedge u=\mathfrak{j}_{*}

The
core
of an element
x\in L
is the interval
[x_{\downarrow},x]
where

x_{\downarrow}=\bigwedge_{y\in L:y\prec x}y

is the meet of all elements covered by
x
.
The
core label set
of
x
is the set of labels of the edges in the core of
x
:

\psi_{\mathscr{L}}(x):=\left\{\mathfrak{j}_{cg(u,v)}:\ x^{\downarrow}\leq u\prec v\leq x\right\}.

When no confusion may arise, we omit the subscript
\mathscr{L}
and just write
\psi(x)
for simplicity.

The
core label order

CL(\mathscr{L})=(L,\sqsubseteq)
is the poset on
L
with the following relation. For
x,y\in L
we say that
x\sqsubseteq y
if and only if
\psi(x)\subseteq\psi(y)
.

Proposition 1.4.15
.

Let

C_{2}=(C_{1}\setminus R_{1})\cup R_{2}

.

(2) again for the covering pair

Remark 1.4.17
.

We remark that the framing lattice has a richer underlying geometric structure, consisting not only of vertices and edges but also 2-dimensional faces that are squares, pentagons and hexagons, as well as possibly higher dimensional faces. This geometric structure, which we call the framingtope, will be introduced and studied in forthcoming work
[
39
]
. It is a polytopal complex that is dual to the complex of interior faces of the framing triangulation of a flow polytope. As illustrated on the right of Figure
39
, it can be topologically decomposed into a disjoint union of half open cells, one for each element of the lattice. If we identify faces with the same set of labels, then the core label order is just the containment poset of equivalence classes of faces (more explicitly their closures) in this cell decomposition.

) in
G
that is coherent with all routes in
C
.
By Lemma
1.1.8
, the path can be extended to a route
R^{\prime}
that is coherent with all routes in
C
, and hence necessarily also contained in
C
.
Now by construction
e\in R^{\prime}
and
\varphi_{e}(R^{\prime})
contains
R
. So,
R
is in
\Phi_{e}(C)
.
∎

Lemma 1.5.2
.

Given a maximal clique
D
in
(M(G,e),F_{e})
, the fiber
\Phi_{e}^{-1}(D)
is the set of maximal cliques in
(G,F)
that are coherent with the set
S
consisting of the following paths:

(1)

the routes of
D
that are also routes in
G
,

(2)

the paths of the form
(i,j)\cup jR
where
R
is a route in
D
passing through
e_{s}
, and

(3)

the paths of the form
R^{\prime}i\cup(i,j)
where
R^{\prime}
is a route in
D
passing through
e_{t}
.

In particular,

\Phi_{e}^{-1}(D)=[C_{\min}(S),C_{\max}(S)]
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a maximal clique of
(G,F)
. We need to show that
\Phi_{e}(C)=D
if and only if
C
is coherent with
S
.

For the forward direction, if
\Phi_{e}(C)=D
then every path
P
in
S
is necessarily a subpath of a route in
C
. Thus, every route in
C
is coherent with
P
.

For the backward direction, if
C
is coherent with
S
then we need to show that the routes of
D
corresponding to the paths of types (1), (2) and (3) in
S
all belong to
\Phi_{e}(C)
, from which we can deduce that
\Phi_{e}(C)=D
.
For type (1) paths, consider a route
R\in D
that is also a route in
G
. Since
R
is coherent with
C
then
R\in C
by maximality, and
\Phi_{e}(R)=R\in D
.
For type (2) paths, consider a route
R
in
D
passing through
e_{s}
. Since the path
(i,j)\cup jR
is coherent with
C
then it can be extended to a route
R^{\prime\prime}\in C
and
\Phi_{e}(R^{\prime\prime})=R\in D
.
Similarly for type (3) paths, consider a route
R^{\prime}
in
D
passing through
e_{t}
. Since the path
R^{\prime}i\cup(i,j)
is coherent with
C
, it can be extended to a route
R^{\prime\prime}\in C
, and then
\Phi_{e}(R^{\prime\prime})=R^{\prime}\in D
.

This finishes the proof that
C
is coherent with
S
if and only if
\Phi_{e}(C)=D
.
Finally,
S
is a set of paths in
(G,F)
and
\Phi_{e}^{-1}(D)
is the set of maximal cliques that are coherent with
S
. By
Corollary

1.2.21
, we deduce that
\Phi_{e}^{-1}(D)=[C_{\min}(S),C_{\max}(S)]
.
∎

Let
\bm{\alpha}(e)
be the equivalence relation on
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
induced by
x\equiv y
if and only if
x
and
y
belong to the same fiber of
\Phi_{e}
.

Corollary 1.5.3
.

Each equivalence class of
\bm{\alpha}(e)
is an interval in
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
.

Proof.

Each equivalence class is of the form
\Phi_{e}^{-1}(D)
for some maximal clique
D
in
(M(G,e),F_{e})
. As we have just proved
\Phi_{e}^{-1}(D)=[C_{\min}(S),C_{\max}(S)]
, which is an interval.
∎

1.5.2.
Lattice congruences

Or next goal is to show that the equivalence relation
\bm{\alpha}(e)
is a lattice congruence of the framing lattice
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
(
Corollary

1.5.8
).
In order to do this we need to understand the projections mapping each element of
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
to the bottom element and to the top element in its equivalence class. The following lemma will be useful for that purpose.

Lemma 1.5.4
.

Let
C
be a maximal clique in
(G,F)
,
D=\Phi_{e}(C)
and
S
be the corresponding set of paths as in
Lemma

1.5.2
.
A route in
G
is coherent with
S
if and only if it is:

(1)

a route of
C
that does not contain the edge
e=(i,j)
, or

(2)

a route of the form
RijR^{\prime}
where
R
and
R^{\prime}
are routes in
C
containing
e
.

Proof.

We start by proving the forward direction.
Let
R^{\prime\prime}
be a route in
G
that is coherent with
S
. We will show that
R^{\prime\prime}
is of type (1) or (2).

Type (1): Assume that
R^{\prime\prime}
does not contain the edge
e=(i,j)
. If
R^{\prime\prime}\notin C
then there would be a route
R\in C
that is not coherent with
R^{\prime\prime}
. If the edge
e
is not in
R
then
R\in S
, which is a contradiction. If the edge
e
is in
R
then
R^{\prime\prime}
and
R
are incoherent outside
e
, and so
R^{\prime\prime}
is incoherent with either
(i,j)\cup jR
or
Ri\cup(i,j)
; since both paths are in
S
, this is also a contradiction. As a consequence
R^{\prime\prime}\in C
, which means that
R^{\prime\prime}
is of type (1).

Type (2): Now assume that
R^{\prime\prime}
contains the edge
e=(i,j)
. The subpath
R^{\prime\prime}j
must be coherent with
C
, otherwise it would be incoherent with
S
. By
Lemma

1.1.8
, the path
R^{\prime\prime}j
can be extended to a route coherent with
C
, and by maximality this extended route must be equal to a route
R
of
C
. Therefore,
R^{\prime\prime}j=Rj
for some
R\in C
. Similarly, we can show that
iR^{\prime\prime}=iR^{\prime}
for some route
R^{\prime}\in C
. Thus
R^{\prime\prime}=RijR^{\prime}
, which is a route of type (2).

For the backward direction we will show that any route of type (1) or (2) is coherent with
S
.

Type (1): The paths in
S
are either routes in
C
not containing
e
or subpaths of routes of
C
containing
e
. Since every route of
C
is coherent with
C
then it is also coherent with
S
.

Type (2): Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
be routes in
C
containing
e
. Towards a contradiction, assume there is
R^{\prime\prime}\in S
that is incoherent with
RijR^{\prime}
. If
R^{\prime\prime}
does not contain
e
then
R^{\prime\prime}\in C
. Furthermore,
R^{\prime\prime}
must be incoherent with
RijR^{\prime}
outside
e
. This implies that
R^{\prime\prime}
is incoherent with either
R
or
R^{\prime}
, which is a contradiction. If
R^{\prime\prime}
contains
e
then
R^{\prime\prime}
is a path that starts or ends with the edge
e
. In particular, the subpath where
R^{\prime\prime}
and
RijR^{\prime}
are incoherent does not contain the edge
e
, and so
R^{\prime\prime}
is incoherent with either
R
or
R^{\prime}
. Since
R^{\prime\prime}
is a subpath of a route of
C
we also have a contradiction.
∎

We focus our attention on the routes of the form
RijR^{\prime}
where
R
and
R^{\prime}
are routes in
C
containing
e
.
We denote by
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}=RijR^{\prime}
the route obtained when
Rj
is minimal with respect to
\leq_{\mathscr{I}(j)}
and
iR^{\prime}
is maximal with respect to
\leq_{\mathscr{O}(i)}
; this is the clockwise most route through
e
that is coherent with
S
.
Similarly, we denote by
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{ccw}}=RijR^{\prime}
the route obtained when
Rj
is maximal with respect to
\leq_{\mathscr{I}(j)}
and
iR^{\prime}
is minimal with respect to
\leq_{\mathscr{O}(i)}
; this is the counterclockwise most route through
e
that is coherent with
S
.

For the equivalence relation
\bm{\alpha}(e)
on
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
, the
down projection
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}
(resp.
up projection
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\uparrow}
) is defined as the map sending each element to the bottom (resp. top) element in its equivalence class.

Lemma 1.5.5

(Down projection)
.

The projection
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}(C)
of a maximal clique
C
in
(G,F)
can be described equivalently as either:

(1)

the unique maximal clique containing the routes of
C
without
e
and the route
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
, or

(2)

the maximal clique containing the routes of
C
without
e
, and the routes of the form
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}iR
and
RjR_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
, where
R
is a route in
C
containing
e
.

Proof.

Let
D=\Phi_{e}(C)
and
S
be the corresponding set of paths as in
Lemma

1.5.2
.
We know that the fiber containing
C
is the set

\Phi_{e}^{-1}(D)=[C_{\min}(S),C_{\max}(S)]

of maximal cliques that are coherent with
S
, and so
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}(C)=C_{\min}(S)
.

Let
C^{\prime}\in\Phi_{e}^{-1}(D)
be a maximal clique in the fiber containing
C
. We will show first that if
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}\in C^{\prime}
then
C^{\prime}
is the maximal clique containing the routes of
C
without
e
, and the routes of the form
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}iR
and
RjR_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
, where
R
is a route in
C
containing
e
. Our second step in the proof will be to show that this
C^{\prime}
is equal to
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}(C)
.

For the first step, assume that
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}\in C^{\prime}
.
By
Lemma

1.5.4
, the routes of
C^{\prime}
are the routes of
C
without
e=(i,j)
, together with some routes of the form
RijR^{\prime}
where
R
and
R^{\prime}
are routes of
C
containing
e
. But if neither
R
nor
R^{\prime}
is equal to
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
, then
RijR^{\prime}
is incoherent with
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
, which is a contradiction.
Therefore, every route of
C^{\prime}
containing
e
is of the form
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}iR
or
RjR_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
, where
R
is a route in
C
containing
e
.
Since all of these routes are pairwise coherent, it follows that
C^{\prime}
must contain all of them by maximality.

For the second step, consider a maximal clique
C^{\prime\prime}\neq C^{\prime}
in the fiber
\Phi_{e}^{-1}(D)
. There must be a route
RijR^{\prime}
in
C^{\prime\prime}\setminus C^{\prime}
for some
R,R^{\prime}\in C
containing
e
. But then
R\neq R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
and
R^{\prime}\neq R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
, which implies that the route
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}\in C^{\prime}
is clockwise incoherent from the route
RijR^{\prime}\in C^{\prime\prime}
at
e
. So,
C^{\prime\prime}
cannot be smaller than
C^{\prime}
. Since the fiber has a unique minimum element, this minimum must be equal to
C^{\prime}
. Thus,
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}(C)=C^{\prime}
.
∎

By symmetry we get an analogous result for the up projection.

Lemma 1.5.6

(Up projection)
.

The projection
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\uparrow}(C)
of a maximal clique
C
in
(G,F)
can be described equivalently as either:

(1)

the unique maximal clique containing the routes of
C
without
e
and the route
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{ccw}}
, or

(2)

the maximal clique containing the routes of
C
without
e
, and the routes of the form
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{ccw}}iR
and
RjR_{C,e}^{\mathrm{ccw}}
, where
R
is a route in
C
containing
e
.

Corollary 1.5.7
.

The following hold:

(1)

The map
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}
is order preserving.

(2)

The map
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\uparrow}
is order preserving.

Proof.

To prove (1), let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be maximal cliques in
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
satisfying
C\leq C^{\prime}
. We need to show that
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}(C)\leq\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}(C^{\prime})
.
Let
R
be a route in
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}(C)
and
R^{\prime}
be a route in
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}(C^{\prime})
.
By
Lemma

1.5.5
, the route
R
(resp.
R^{\prime}
) behaves purely as a route of
C
(resp.
C^{\prime}
) outside the edge
e=(i,j)
. So, if
R
and
R^{\prime}
are incoherent outside
e
then
R
would be clockwise incoherent from
R^{\prime}
(because
C\leq C^{\prime}
), which is what we want to prove.
So, it is enough to consider the case where
R
and
R^{\prime}
are incoherent at a path containing
e
(in particular,
e\in R
and
e\in R^{\prime}
).
In this case, it suffices to check that
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j\leq_{\mathscr{I}(j)}R_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j
and
iR_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}\leq_{\mathscr{O}(i)}iR_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
.

Suppose toward a contradiction that
R_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j<_{\mathscr{I}(j)}R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j
.
Let
P
be the maximal path in
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j\cap R_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j
containing
j
, and let
k
be the minimal vertex in
P
.
Consider the path
P^{\prime}
formed from
P
by appending the edge of
R_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j
incoming to
k
.
Now since
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j
is minimal with respect to
\leq_{\mathscr{I}(j)}
, the path
P^{\prime}
must be incoherent with some route
R^{*}
in
C
at
k
(otherwise it can be extended to a route in
C
that is smaller in the order
\leq_{\mathscr{I}(j)}
).
However, such a route
R^{*}
is counter clockwise from the routes in
C^{\prime}
containing
R_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j
, which contradicts the fact that
C\leq C^{\prime}
.
This shows that
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j\leq_{\mathscr{I}(j)}R_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j
.
The proof that
iR_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}\leq_{\mathscr{O}(i)}iR_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
uses similar arguments.

The proof of (2), i.e. that
\pi_{\uparrow}^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}
is order preserving, is similar to the proof of (1).
∎

Corollary 1.5.8
.

The equivalence relation
\bm{\alpha}(e)
is a lattice congruence on
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
.

Proof.

The equivalence relation
\bm{\alpha}(e)
is a lattice congruence if and only if each equivalence class in an interval and the maps
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}
and
\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\uparrow}
are order preserving (see e.g.
[
33
, Proposition 9-5.2]
). These properties were proven in
Corollary

1.5.3
and
Corollary

1.5.7
.
∎

1.5.3.
Lattice quotients

Our next goal is to show that the lattice quotient induced by the equivalence relation
\bm{\alpha}(e)
on
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
is isomorphic to the framing lattice
\mathscr{L}_{M(G,e),F_{e}}
(
Corollary

1.5.10
).

Proposition 1.5.9
.

Let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be two minimal elements of the
\bm{\alpha}(e)
equivalence classes on
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
, and let
D=\Phi_{e}(C)
and
D^{\prime}=\Phi_{e}(C^{\prime})
be their images on
\mathscr{L}_{M(G,e),F_{e}}
. Then

C\leq C^{\prime}
if and only
D\leq D^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For the forward direction we do not need to assume that
C
and
C^{\prime}
are minimal elements in their equivalence classes.
If
C
and
C^{\prime}
are maximal cliques of
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
satisfying
C\leq C^{\prime}
then it follows directly that
\Phi_{e}(C)\leq\Phi_{e}(C^{\prime})
.

For the backward direction, assume
D\leq D^{\prime}
in
\mathscr{L}_{M(G,e),F_{e}}
. We will show that
C\leq C^{\prime}
.
Since
C
is the minimal element of its class, we have that
C=\pi^{\bm{\alpha}(e)}_{\downarrow}(C)
. By
Lemma

1.5.5
, the route
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}\in C
and every route of
C
containing
e
is of the form
R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}iR
or
RjR_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}
, where
R
is a route containing
e
.
The similar statement holds for
C^{\prime}
.

Let
R_{D,e_{s}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
(resp.
R_{D,e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
) be the clockwise most route of
D
passing through the edge
e_{s}
(resp.
e_{t}
), and define these routes similarly for
D^{\prime}
. Since
D=\Phi_{e}(C)
and
D^{\prime}=\Phi_{e}(C^{\prime})
then

R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}i=R_{D,e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}i
,

R_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}i=R_{D^{\prime},e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}i

jR_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}=jR_{D,e_{s}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
,

jR_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}=jR_{D^{\prime},e_{s}}^{\mathrm{cw}}

Since the routes of
C
(resp.
C^{\prime}
) behave as routes of
D
(resp.
D^{\prime}
) outside of
e
, if a route of
C
is incoherent with a route of
C^{\prime}
outside
e
then they are incoherent in the right order because
D\leq D^{\prime}
. Therefore, its is enough to consider the case where they are incoherent at a path containing the edge
e
.
In this case, it suffices to check that

R_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j\leq_{\mathscr{I}(j)}R_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}j
and

iR_{C^{\prime},e}^{\mathrm{cw}}\leq_{\mathscr{O}(i)}iR_{C,e}^{\mathrm{cw}}

(as in the proof of
Corollary

1.5.7
).
By the equations above, these two conditions are equivalent to

R_{D,e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}\leq_{\mathscr{I}(t)}R_{D^{\prime},e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
and

R_{D^{\prime},e_{s}}^{\mathrm{cw}}\leq_{\mathscr{O}(s)}R_{D,e_{s}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
.

Following the same lines as in the proof of
Corollary

1.5.7
,
suppose toward a contradiction that
R_{D^{\prime},e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}<_{\mathscr{I}(t)}R_{D,e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
.
Let
P
be the maximal path in
R_{D,e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}\cap R_{D^{\prime},e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
containing
t
, and let
k
be the minimal vertex in
P
.
Consider the path
P^{\prime}
formed from
P
by appending the edge of
R_{D^{\prime},e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
incoming to
k
.
Now since
R_{D,e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
is the minimal route in
D
containing
e_{t}
with respect to
\leq_{\mathscr{I}(t)}
, the path
P^{\prime}
must be incoherent with some route
R^{*}
in
D
at
k
(otherwise it can be extended to a route in
D
that is smaller in the order
\leq_{\mathscr{I}(t)}
).
However, such a route
R^{*}
is counter clockwise from the route
R_{D^{\prime},e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
in
D^{\prime}
at
k
, which contradicts the fact that
D\leq D^{\prime}
.
This shows that
R_{D,e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}\leq_{\mathscr{I}(t)}R_{D^{\prime},e_{t}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
.
The proof that
R_{D^{\prime},e_{s}}^{\mathrm{cw}}\leq_{\mathscr{O}(s)}R_{D,e_{s}}^{\mathrm{cw}}
uses similar arguments.
∎

Corollary 1.5.10
.

For any inner edge
e
of
G
we have
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}/\bm{\alpha}(e)\cong\mathscr{L}_{M(G,e),F_{e}}
.

Proof.

The lattice quotient
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}/\bm{\alpha}(e)
is isomorphic to the restriction of
\mathscr{L}_{G,F}
to the set of minimal elements of the equivalence classes (see e.g.
[
33
, Proposition 9-5.5]
). This restricted poset is isomorphic to
\mathscr{L}_{M(G,e),F_{e}}
by
Proposition

1.5.9
.
∎

Remark 1.5.11
.

It would be interesting to study all lattice quotients of the framing lattice, not just the ones arising from
M
-moves. In particular, we do not know if every quotient is itself a framing lattice of some modified graph. We leave this as an open question for the interested reader.

1.5.4.
A distributive quotient

An
M
-move decreases the number of inner edges in a framed graph
(G,F)
by one, and it can be repeated until the resulting graph has no more inner edges.
We use
M(G)
to denote the graph obtained from
G
by repeating
M
-moves until there are no more inner edges.
Note that the induced framing of the graph
M(G)
, which we denote by
M(F)
, is independent of the order of the performed
M
-moves.
Moreover, since all edges in
M(G)
are incident to the source or sink, it follows from Lemma
1.2.12
(parts (3) and (4)) that the framing lattice
\mathscr{L}_{M(G),M(F)}
is independent of the initial framing
F
. Thus we abbreviate it by simply writing
\mathscr{L}_{M(G)}
.

In this section, we present a simple combinatorial description of the lattice quotient
\mathscr{L}_{M(G)}
as a product of certain distributive lattices described in terms of lattice paths. In particular, this will imply that
\mathscr{L}_{M(G)}
is a distributive lattice quotient of the framing lattice.

of

induced by

. The framing lattice of

is independent of its framing, so we can denote it by
\mathscr{L}_{G_{v}}
.
Furthermore, we have that

(1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{M(G)}=\prod_{v\in G\setminus\{s,t\}}\mathscr{L}_{G_{v}}.

Hence, it is enough to concentrate on the lattices
\mathscr{L}_{G_{v}}

be the in-degree of

in

, and

be its out-degree.

1.6.
Open questions and conjectures

Note that there are many more lattice quotients of a framing lattice as there are lattice quotients of it obtained via
M
-moves. For instance, the graph

has four internal edges and the framing lattice

is the weak order on permutations of

As we have shown, every lattice quotient obtained from

We leave this as an open question, which is interesting even in the simple case of the oruga graph.

Question 1.6.1
.

Is every lattice quotient of a framing lattice isomorphic to a framing lattice?

We also propose the following purely enumerative conjecture, which is based on extensive computational evidence.

.

If

For instance,

Our next question is related to the complexity of framing lattices.

Question 1.6.3

What is the complexity of finding a shortest path between two maximal cliques in the rotation graph of framing lattices? Is it NP-hard? The complexity problem in the case of the classical Tamari lattice is a widely open renown problem.

Theorem 2.4.2
.

For any proper labeling
L
of the rows and columns of
D
,
the framing lattice
\mathscr{L}_{G_{D,L},F_{D,L}}
is isomorphic to the cross-Tamari order
\Tam(D)
.

Proof.

An important feature of
G_{D,L}
is that its routes can be characterized by two edges, namely the edges of the route incident to the source and sink.
We can then express the unique route that uses edges
(s,i)
and
(\overline{j},t)
by
R_{i,\overline{j}}
.
The map
(i,\overline{j})\rightarrow R_{i,\overline{j}}
is a bijection between the lattice points in
D
and the routes in
G_{D,L}
.
One can check by inspection that, under this bijection,
two lattice points are compatible in
D
if and only if their corresponding routes are coherent (see examples of incompatible/compatible pairs of lattice points and their corresponding incoherent/coherent routes in
Figure

51
).
Therefore, maximal cliques of the framing lattice correspond to maximal fillings.
One can also observe that increasing tree rotations correspond to
\mathrm{ccw}
-rotations.
As a consequence, the framing lattice
\mathscr{L}_{G_{D,L},F_{D,L}}
is isomorphic to the cross-Tamari order
\Tam(D)
.
∎

Corollary 2.4.3
.

The cross-Tamari order
\Tam(D)
is an

The following is another immediate but nontrivial consequence that illustrates the power of the framing lattice approach.

Corollary 2.4.4
.

For a cross-shaped grid
D
with
a
columns and
b
rows the following hold:

(1)

All maximal fillings have the same number of elements, equal to
a+b-1
.

(2)

The rotation graph of maximal fillings is connected.

(3)

\Tam(D)
has a unique maximal and a unique minimal element.

Proof.

For simplicity, let
G=G_{D,L}
.
maximal fillings determine the maximal simplices of the framing triangulation of the flow polytope
\mathcal{F}_{G}
. Thus, the number of elements of any maximal filling
T
is equal to

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{F}_{G}+1

\displaystyle=|E(G)|-|V(G)|+2

\displaystyle=(2a+2b-1)-(a+b+2)+2

\displaystyle=a+b-1

This proves item (1). Item (2) follows from the fact that the dual graph of a triangulation of a polytope is connected. Item (3) about
\Tam(D)
having a unique maximal and a unique minimal element follows from the fact of being a lattice.
∎

The following result shows different cross-Tamari lattices obtainable through framing triangulations of the same flow polytope.

Proposition 2.4.5
.

Let
D
and
D^{\prime}
be two cross-shaped grids that are obtainable from each other by a permutation of rows and columns. Then,

(1)

The graph
G_{D,L}
is independent of a proper labeling
L
of the rows and columns of
D
(but the framing depends on
L
). In particular, the flow polytope
\mathcal{F}_{G_{D}}:=\mathcal{F}_{G_{D,L}}
is independent of
L
.

(2)

The two graphs of
D
and
D^{\prime}
are equal:
G_{D,L}=G_{D^{\prime},L^{\prime}}
.

(3)

The Hasse diagrams of the cross-Tamari lattices
\Tam(D)
and
\Tam(D^{\prime})
are the dual graphs of two framing triangulations of the same flow polytope
\mathcal{F}_{G_{D}}=\mathcal{F}_{G_{D^{\prime}}}
.

Proof.

For item (1), it suffices to check that the horizontal line of
G_{D,L}
is fixed independent of the proper labeling
L
.
Or equivalently, that the linear order
\prec
on the vertices
\{s,t\}\sqcup[a]\sqcup[\overline{b}]
is independent of
L
.
This follows from the fact that
(i,\overline{j})\in D
for
L
if and only if
(i,\overline{j})\in D
for
L^{\prime}
, for any two proper labelings
L
and
L^{\prime}
.

For item (2), fix two proper labelings
L
and
L^{\prime}
of
D
and
D^{\prime}
respectively.
Since
D
and
D^{\prime}
are obtainable from each other by a permutation of rows and columns, one can observe that
(i,\overline{j})\in D
if and only if
(i,\overline{j})\in D^{\prime}
. This implies that
G_{D,L}=G_{D^{\prime},L^{\prime}}
.

For item (3), since
G_{D,L}=G_{D^{\prime},L^{\prime}}
then their flow polytopes are equal.
∎

Proposition 2.4.6
.

Any framing of
G_{D,L}
produces a framing lattice that is isomorphic to a cross-Tamari lattice.

Proof.

Consider
G_{D,L}
with framing
F_{D,L}
.
Changing the framing by flipping an edge
(s,i)
with
i\neq 1
drawn above (resp. below) the horizontal line to being drawn below (resp. above) corresponds with moving the column labeled
i
in
D
to the right (resp. left) of the column labeled
1
, while preserving the cross-shaped property.
Similarly, changing the framing by flipping an edge
(\overline{j},t)
with
\overline{j}\neq\overline{1}
drawn above (resp. below) the horizontal line to being drawn below (resp. above) corresponds with moving the column labeled
\overline{j}
in
D
below (resp. above) the row labeled
\overline{1}
.
Any of these operations gives a new cross-shaped grid
D^{\prime}
with proper labeling
L^{\prime}
, with
G_{D,L}=G_{D^{\prime},L^{\prime}}
, while
F_{D,L}
and
F_{D^{\prime},L^{\prime}}
differ by the drawing of a single edge.
Since every framing of
G_{D,L}
can be obtained by iterating these moves, we obtain a corresponding cross-shaped grid and proper labeling for each framing.
∎

Remark 2.4.7
.

The
\varepsilon
-Cambrian lattices (and the
(\varepsilon,I,J)
-Cambrian lattices of Remark

) are special cases of the cross-Tamari lattices.
This follows from the observation that for any Cambrian caracol graph
G_{\varepsilon}
, one can easily construct a
(D,L)
-caracol graph
G_{D,L}
that differs from
G_{\varepsilon}
only by contracting idle edges (thus having isomorphic framing lattices).
More precisely, an internal node
i
of
G_{\varepsilon}
is duplicated to a horizontal edge
i-\overline{i}
, and the pair of edges
(s,i),(i,t)
are transformed to
(s,i),(\overline{i},t)
; Note that in contrast to the cross-Tamari case, the bar labels now appear in increasing order from left to right, so when we translate to the cross-shaped grid the row labels are reversed (labels of rows now
decrease
from longer to shorter, while the labels of columns increase from longer to shorter). A lattice point
(i,\overline{j})
in the grid corresponds to the diagonal
(i,j+1)
in the polygon
P_{\varepsilon}
. See
Figure

52
for an example.

In order to get the
(\varepsilon,I,J)
-Cambrian lattices from
[
27
]
, one just needs to restrict the cross-shaped grid corresponding to
G_{\varepsilon}
to a subset of columns and a subset of rows. The restricted cross-shaped grid gives a cross-Tamari lattice isomorphic to a
(\varepsilon,I,J)
-Cambrian lattice.
