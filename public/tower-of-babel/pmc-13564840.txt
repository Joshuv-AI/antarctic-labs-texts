CaMKIIα holoenzymes self-organize into chain-like mesoscale clusters
Authors: Taisei Suzuki, Takashi Sumikama, Keisuke Matsushima, Kodai Hasegawa, Ayumi Sumino, Kenichi Umeda, Noriyuki Kodera, Tamoghna Das, Carsten Beta, Hideji Murakoshi, Mikihiro Shibata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Calcium- and calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) is highly enriched in dendritic spines at concentrations comparable to those of cytoskeletal proteins and plays a central role in synaptic plasticity. During long-term potentiation, CaMKIIα further accumulates in spines. However, the mechanisms governing its higher-order organization remain poorly understood. Here, we use high-speed atomic force microscopy to visualize interholoenzyme interaction of CaMKIIα at mesoscopic scales (5 to 500 nanometers). Under freely diffusible conditions, CaMKIIα holoenzymes do not form stable clusters. In contrast, when spatially confined, they assemble into chain-like clusters mediated by kinase-domain interactions. These clusters expand upon activation, concomitant with the dissociation of the regulatory segment. Notably, the CaMKIIα Pro212→Leu (P212L) mutant associated with neurodevelopmental disorders forms extensive clusters even in the basal state. Together, our findings demonstrate that CaMKIIα-CaMKIIα interactions drive mesoscale cluster formation and that precise regulation of cluster size and activation-dependent growth might be critical for synaptic signaling.

INTRODUCTION

Calcium- and calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) is a serine/threonine kinase essential for the induction of long-term potentiation (LTP), the cellular basis of learning and memory (1–6). Among the four CaMKII isoforms (α, β, γ, and δ), CaMKIIα and CaMKIIβ are the predominant isoforms expressed in excitatory neurons of the brain (7–10). CaMKIIα knockout mice exhibit impaired spatial memory, illustrating its direct involvement in memory formation (11). At the molecular level, the CaMKIIα subunit consists of a kinase domain, a regulatory segment, a linker, and a hub domain, typically assembling into a dodecameric double-ring holoenzyme (fig. S1, A and B) (12, 13). In the basal state, enzymatic activity is regulated by a pseudosubstrate mechanism, where the regulatory segment binds to the catalytic domain and blocks substrate access (14). Activation is triggered by calcium ion (Ca2+)/calmodulin (CaM) binding to this regulatory segment, which induces its dissociation from the kinase domain, unveiling the catalytic site and promoting an open conformation characteristic of the activated state. This conformational change enables trans-autophosphorylation at Thr286 (T286) within the holoenzyme, which stabilizes the kinase in its open, active configuration. Structural studies have demonstrated a diverse positioning of the kinase domains within the holoenzyme (12), reflecting an inherent flexibility that is also captured in our high-speed atomic force microscopy (HS-AFM) observations (13, 15). Beyond enzymatic activation, biophysical and functional studies have suggested that catalytic domains can engage in dimeric pairings (16, 17). While some crystallographic models did not observe stable interactions between these domains (18), the occurrence and functional significance of such pairings remain subjects of academic discussion. Notably, phosphorylation at T286 (pT286) confers Ca2+-independent activity, allowing CaMKIIα to remain active after intracellular Ca2+ levels decline (fig. S1C).

CaMKIIα is highly expressed in excitatory neurons, accounting for approximately 2% of total protein in the hippocampus (4), a level comparable to that of the cytoskeletal protein actin. At the subcellular level, CaMKIIα is present at high concentrations within dendritic spines, with an estimated density of approximately 960 monomers per 0.1 μm2 (4, 5, 9, 19, 20). This high local concentration suggests that CaMKIIα may serve not only as a kinase but also as a structural component at synapses, potentially functioning as a scaffold in addition to its well-established enzymatic roles (21–24). During LTP induced by high-frequency electrical stimulation, Ca2+ influx through N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptors promotes the accumulation of CaMKIIα at the postsynaptic density (PSD) and its binding to the C-terminal region of the GluN2B subunit (GluN2BCter), leading to the immobilization of CaMKIIα at synaptic sites (24–29). This interaction is considered essential for LTP induction (24, 26, 30, 31). However, because the total number of CaMKIIα molecules far exceeds that of GluN2B subunits, only a fraction of CaMKIIα can directly bind GluN2BCter (5, 10, 19, 32–34). This discrepancy suggests that CaMKIIα self-clustering is likely required to maintain its exceptionally high local concentration at activated synapses (5, 6).

Previous studies have established that CaMKIIα undergoes self-association in vitro and in neurons upon Ca2+/CaM activation, particularly under ischemia-like conditions characterized by low pH and low adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels (35–38). Electron microscopy (EM) analyses under these conditions revealed large aggregates on a micrometer scale (35, 36). In contrast, negative-stain EM has identified smaller clusters or pairs of holoenzymes even in the basal state (39). Whether these processes share a common assembly mechanism at the mesoscale remains to be fully understood. Furthermore, complementary in vitro studies have demonstrated that CaMKIIα undergoes liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) in the presence of Ca2+/CaM and GluN2BCter (40, 41). The high abundance of CaMKII within the PSD, combined with its capacity for high-order assembly and LLPS, suggests that CaMKIIα clustering may be a fundamental mechanism underlying the induction of LTP (40). However, direct visualization of CaMKIIα cluster dynamics at nanometer resolution in a high-density environment has remained elusive.

We previously used HS-AFM to investigate the dynamics of CaMKIIα, CaMKIIβ, and CaMKIIα/β holoenzymes, enabling single-molecule visualization of state-dependent structural rearrangement in the CaMKII holoenzyme (13, 15). HS-AFM is a distinct technique that enables direct visualization of protein behavior in solution with nanometer resolution (15, 42–45).

In this study, we used HS-AFM to observe CaMKIIα holoenzymes at concentrations comparable to those in neurons, allowing visualization of CaMKIIα cluster dynamics over length scales of several hundred nanometers. We found that CaMKIIα holoenzymes assemble into chain-like clusters mediated by kinase domain interactions. Upon Ca2+/CaM binding, these clusters expanded beyond their basal configuration. The de novo mutant CaMKIIα P212L, associated with intellectual disability (ID), formed large clusters even in the basal state. These results suggest that CaMKIIα clustering may play an important role in supporting effective synaptic signaling.

RESULTS

CaMKIIα holoenzymes initiate cluster formation at lower densities than within the PSD

To examine the molecular assembly patterns of CaMKIIα holoenzyme clusters at the single-molecule level, we overexpressed CaMKIIα in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells and subsequently isolated and purified the protein using two independent affinity tags (i.e., His and Strep tags) (see Materials and Methods for details) (15). We first investigated whether CaMKIIα holoenzymes in solution exist as a single holoenzyme or clustered form. To address this, CaMKIIα was added directly to the imaging buffer during HS-AFM scanning, and individual holoenzymes were observed at the moment of adsorption onto the AFM substrate, allowing us to infer their cluster state in solution before surface binding (Condition 1 in Materials and Methods; Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis was performed by counting the number of CaMKIIα holoenzymes that newly appeared on the AFM substrate within each frame. In the basal state, more than 95% of CaMKIIα holoenzymes adsorb as single particles onto the AFM substrate (Fig. 1, B and C, and movie S1). We next examined the activated state by preincubating CaMKIIα with Ca2+/CaM in solution before HS-AFM observations (Condition 1 in Materials and Methods). Under these conditions, CaMKIIα holoenzymes similarly adsorbed predominantly as single holoenzymes, with more than 95% of CaMKIIα holoenzymes adsorbed as single particles onto the AFM substrate (Fig. 1, C and D, and movie S2). Our observations indicate that CaMKIIα holoenzymes are likely present as a single holoenzyme in low-concentration solutions where they can diffuse freely. This is consistent with previous reports (40) showing that, in the absence of GluN2BCter, CaMKIIα holoenzymes remain in a soluble state and do not undergo LLPS in vitro, even at concentrations used for phase separation assay. In our HS-AFM experiments, conducted at lower nanomolar concentrations, the holoenzyme consistently remained in its dispersed state without exhibiting liquid-like condensation.

CaMKIIα exists at a density of 960 monomers (∼80 holoenzymes) per 0.1 μm2 in the PSD of excitatory neurons (4), corresponding to ∼200 holoenzymes per 500 nm by 500 nm. In the basal state, CaMKII binds to F-actin (21, 22, 46–48), whereas upon activation, it binds to GluN2BCter (47, 49–53). This spatial distribution and activity-dependent binding suggest that CaMKII diffusion is constrained within densely packed PSDs. On the basis of this physiological context, we sought to examine the dynamics of CaMKIIα holoenzymes under conditions that mimic both the high molecular density and constrained movement. To this end, CaMKIIα holoenzymes were added to the HS-AFM imaging buffer, and observation conditions were optimized to allow a gradual increase in the number of holoenzymes adsorbed onto the AFM substrate over time. Upon reaching a certain density state, HS-AFM observation was conducted for approximately 5 min, over a 500 nm–by–500 nm imaging area with a temporal resolution of 5 s/frame (Condition 1 in Materials and Methods; Fig. 2A and fig. S2A). At low surface densities, CaMKIIα holoenzymes diffused freely across the AFM substrate. However, as the number of CaMKIIα holoenzymes increased, interholoenzyme contacts became more frequent, leading to the formation of transient holoenzyme pairs and clusters (white boxes in Fig. 2, B and F, and movies S3 and S4). Notably, these interactions were highly dynamic, with holoenzyme pairs and clusters repeatedly forming and dissociating over time.

On the basis of prior single-molecule observations using HS-AFM (15), we characterized CaMKIIα cluster formation by measuring distances between holoenzymes and analyzed their behavior (Fig. 2C; see Materials and Methods). By focusing on the initiation of clustering, we observed that at least one CaMKIIα holoenzyme stops diffusing on the substrate and contacts another holoenzyme, thereby triggering cluster formation (Fig. 2D and movies S3 and S4). To further quantify holoenzyme mobility, we calculated the mean square displacement (MSD) by tracking the trajectories of freely diffusing CaMKIIα holoenzymes and those engaged in clusters. The result indicated that holoenzymes involved in clusters exhibit restricted diffusion (fig. S2, B to F; see Materials and Methods). We next analyzed the number of holoenzymes within individual CaMKIIα clusters. Under basal conditions, the cluster size remained limited to two or three holoenzymes, regardless of the total number of holoenzymes adsorbed on the substrate (Fig. 2E). In contrast, for Ca2+/CaM-bound CaMKIIα, when the holoenzyme number within the 500 nm–by–500 nm area increased to 60 to 90 (corresponding to approximately 30 to 45% of the CaMKIIα density reported in the PSD), the number of holoenzymes per cluster significantly increased compared with the basal state (Fig. 2E). Together, these results indicate that, in the Ca2+/CaM-bound state, high-density environments and restricted movement promote intermolecular interactions among CaMKIIα holoenzymes, leading to the formation of larger clusters.

CaMKIIα holoenzymes form chain-like clusters at approximately half the density in the PSD

Under experimental conditions in which CaMKIIα was continuously supplied in the imaging buffer, individual holoenzymes diffused freely across the AFM substrate, making it difficult to resolve detailed assembly patterns of clusters at high spatial resolution. To promote stable intermolecular interactions of CaMKIIα in the basal state, nonactivated CaMKIIα holoenzymes were deposited onto the AFM substrate at approximately half the reported density within the PSD (30 to 50 holoenzymes per 300 nm–by–300 nm, corresponding to 42 to 69% of the PSD density). After incubation for 5 min at room temperature to allow diffusion to reach a steady-state, HS-AFM imaging was performed with a temporal resolution of 3 s per frame (Condition 2 in Materials and Methods; Fig. 3A). Under these conditions, we observed the formation of chain-like clusters consisting of 2 to 10 CaMKIIα holoenzymes (a representative example is indicated by a white dotted line in Fig. 3B). These clusters remained stable on the AFM substrate and were basically invariant until the end of observations (movie S5). Control experiments performed in the absence of Mg2+ yielded similar results, confirming that Mg2+ is not required for the formation of these basal chain-like clusters. Furthermore, we confirmed that the N-terminal affinity tags did not artifactually promote this assembly; experiments using tag-cleaved CaMKIIα showed clustering behavior indistinguishable from that of the tagged holoenzyme (fig. S3). Further analysis of the contact points between CaMKII holoenzymes indicates that their interactions are mediated through their kinase domains (Condition 3 in Materials and Methods; Fig. 3B).

Next, we examined how structural changes in CaMKIIα induced by Ca2+/CaM binding influence the formation of chain-like clusters. In this experiment, CaMKIIα was preincubated with Ca2+/CaM in a tube and subsequently applied onto the AFM substrate. Similar to the basal state, the Ca2+/CaM-bound holoenzyme density was adjusted to 30 to 50 particles per 300 nm–by–300 nm (Condition 2 in Materials and Methods). As expected, under these conditions, chain-like clusters were observed in Ca2+/CaM-bound CaMKIIα, with intermolecular interactions mediated through the kinase domains (white dotted line in Fig. 3C and movie S6). However, quantitative analysis of cluster composition (see Materials and Methods) revealed that the number of holoenzymes per cluster was significantly increased in the Ca2+/CaM-bound state compared with the basal state (Fig. 3D). To quantify the spatial organization of CaMKIIα holoenzymes on the AFM substrate, we determined the center coordinates of each holoenzyme (hub assembly) and calculated the radial distribution function (RDF) (see Materials and Methods) (54). The RDF represents the probability of finding neighboring holoenzymes as a function of interparticle distance (distance, d, in nanometers). Positions of the peaks in the RDF indicate distances where there is a high probability of neighboring holoenzymes. In the basal state, the RDF exhibited a peak at approximately 19 nm, whereas in the Ca2+/CaM-bound state this peak shifted outward by ∼4 nm to approximately 23 nm (Fig. 3E). Previous single-molecule imaging of CaMKIIα has shown that Ca2+/CaM binding induces an approximately 3-nm extension between the hub and kinase domains (15). Our RDF analysis revealed that the most probable distance between neighboring particles was at 19 nm in the basal state and 23 nm in the Ca2+/CaM-bound state, indicating an extension of approximately 4 nm. This elongation closely matches twice the length of the extended structure observed in a single CaMKIIα holoenzyme, estimated at about 3 nm × 2 = 6 nm. We therefore interpret that the kinase domain adopts an extended structure in the radial direction upon Ca2+/CaM binding within the cluster.

We further evaluated cluster stability by converting the RDF to free energy profiles for each experimental condition (fig. S4 and Materials and Methods). The analysis revealed that the pairwise free energy was nearly identical in both the basal and Ca2+/CaM-bound state (fig. S4). In contrast, when the free energy was calculated on a per-holoenzyme basis within each cluster (Materials and Methods), we found that the basal state exhibited a free energy of −0.31 ± 0.15 kcal/mol, whereas the Ca2+/CaM-bound state exhibited a more favorable free energy of −0.49 ± 0.30 kcal/mol (fig. S4B and table S1). These results indicate that Ca2+/CaM binding enhances cluster stability. Accordingly, in the steady-state where holoenzyme diffusion has stabilized, CaMKIIα holoenzymes assemble into chain-like clusters containing several to 10 holoenzymes linked through their kinase domains. Notably, in the Ca2+/CaM-bound state, a large, stable cluster was observed, consisting of more than 20 CaMKIIα holoenzymes. This extensive clustering is likely driven by Ca2+/CaM-induced structural changes in the kinase domains, which adopt a more extended conformation, thereby promoting multivalent intermolecular interactions.

CaMKIIα holoenzymes form large clusters in the autophosphorylation state

Following Ca2+/CaM binding, CaMKIIα undergoes its initial autophosphorylation at Thr286 (Ca2+/CaM + pT286). Subsequently, upon Ca2+/CaM dissociation, additional autophosphorylation occurs at Thr305 and Thr306, resulting in the pT286/pT305/pT306 state (fig. S1C). To examine how these phosphorylations influence chain-like clustering, each autophosphorylation reaction was induced in solution, followed by HS-AFM observations. The phosphorylation states of CaMKIIα were confirmed by biochemical assays (fig. S5A). In both the Ca2+/CaM + pT286 and pT286/pT305/pT306 states, chain-like clusters linked through the kinase domain were observed (Fig. 3, F and G, and movies S7 and S8). Quantitative analysis revealed that the number of holoenzymes per cluster significantly increased compared with the basal state but was not significantly different from that observed in the Ca2+/CaM-bound state (Fig. 3D). Consistently, the RDF exhibited a peak at approximately 25 nm for both the Ca2+/CaM + pT286 and pT286/pT305/pT306 states, representing an outward shift relative to the basal state and closely resembling the Ca2+/CaM-bound condition (Fig. 3E). Together, these results indicate that autophosphorylation stabilizes an extended kinase-domain conformation and promotes the formation of larger CaMKIIα holoenzyme clusters.

Next, to determine which phosphorylation states of CaMKIIα most strongly influence larger cluster formation, we performed HS-AFM observations using two phosphorylation-dead mutants, Thr286→Ala (T286A) (CaMKIIαT286A) and Thr305→Ala/ Thr306→Val (T305A/T306V) (CaMKIIαT305A/T306V) (Condition 2 in Materials and Methods). Biochemical assays confirmed the phosphorylation states of these mutants (fig. S5, B and C). In CaMKIIαT286A, where autophosphorylation is absent, large clusters were observed solely in the Ca2+/CaM-bound state (Fig. 4, A to C, and G). This suggests that Ca2+/CaM binding alone is sufficient to trigger the formation of larger clusters. Here, the Ca2+/CaM-bound state was inferred from the increased distance between the kinase and hub domains observed in HS-AFM images. In CaMKIIαT305A/T306V, large clusters were observed in both the Ca2+/CaM + pT286 and the pT286 states (following the addition of EGTA to dissociate Ca2+/CaM after Ca2+/CaM/ATP induction) (Fig. 4, D to F, and H). These observations indicate that Ca2+/CaM binding is essential for the large cluster formation. Furthermore, because large clusters were also observed in pT286, activation mechanisms share a common structural consequence, i.e., the displacement of the regulatory segment from the kinase domain, which facilitates the formation of CaMKIIα holoenzyme clusters substantially larger than those in the basal state.

To further investigate the potential involvement of the catalytic T site in cluster formation, we performed HS-AFM observations of CaMKIIαWT in the presence of autocamtide-2–related inhibitory peptide (AIP), a high-affinity peptide that binds to the T site (Condition 2 in Materials and Methods) (55). Even when the T site was occupied by AIP, we observed the formation of clusters similar to those seen in the presence of Ca2+/CaM and ATP (fig. S6). This result suggests that T-site occupancy does not prevent the assembly process, indicating that other surfaces of the kinase domain may also contribute to cluster formation under our experimental conditions.

CaMKIIα mutant associated with neurodevelopmental disorders exhibits cluster sizes distinct from WT

To explore the potential biological implications of chain-like clusters formed among CaMKIIα holoenzymes, we next performed HS-AFM observations on a previously reported de novo CaMKIIα mutant associated with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). Specifically, we focused on the P212L mutant (CaMKIIαP212L) (56–58), identified in patients with ID. P212 lies slightly distal to the regulatory segment of the kinase domain (Fig. 5A), and heterozygous P212L knock-in mice have been reported to exhibit ID-like phenotypes (58). Notably, since this mutation does not affect the catalytic lysine (Lys43, K43) and known phosphorylation sites, it is not expected to directly abolish kinase activity. However, given its strong association with NDDs, this variant has attracted considerable interest as a potential molecular determinant of disease mechanisms (56, 58, 59). Accordingly, we overexpressed CaMKIIαP212L homomeric holoenzymes in HEK293 cells using a similar procedure to that for wild type (WT) (fig. S7), followed by HS-AFM observations to assess their clustering behavior.

Chain-like clusters were observed in CaMKIIαP212L, and both Ca2+/CaM + pT286 and pT286/pT305/pT306 states tended to form large clusters similar to WT (Condition 2 in Materials and Methods; Fig. 5, B to D). Comparison with WT in the basal state revealed that CaMKIIαP212L predominantly formed larger clusters (Fig. 5E). Thus, these results suggest that CaMKIIαP212L intrinsically favors the formation of large clusters even in the basal state. Considering the relevance to NDDs, the ability to form and maintain appropriately sized chain-like clusters among CaMKIIα holoenzymes suggests an important role in effective synaptic signaling and neuronal function.

DISCUSSION

CaMKIIα is highly enriched at excitatory postsynapses and is essential for synaptic plasticity through its high local abundance and interactions with diverse substrates (2, 5, 6, 23). However, the dynamic process by which CaMKIIα holoenzymes assemble into clusters at the mesoscale has not been directly visualized in real-time. Using HS-AFM, we directly visualized CaMKIIα cluster dynamics at mesoscopic scales (5 to 500 nm). We show that CaMKIIα forms chain-like clusters via kinase-domain-mediated intermolecular interactions, that Ca2+/CaM binding promotes an extended holoenzyme conformation and cluster expansion, and that autophosphorylation at T286 stabilizes the enlarged clusters. Moreover, the NDD-associated CaMKIIα variant displays abnormal cluster size even in the basal state (fig. S10A).

Our HS-AFM observations indicate that cluster formation can be initiated by locally confined CaMKIIα holoenzyme and that subsequent cluster growth requires a conformational change in CaMKIIα holoenzymes—specifically, radial extension of the kinase domains upon Ca2+/CaM binding. This suggests that spatial confinement and conformational expansion jointly facilitate CaMKIIα cluster assembly, independent of a specific surface context. In this framework, increasing the effective particle size of CaMKIIα holoenzymes is expected to promote cluster growth. To test this hypothesis, we performed clustering simulations based on holoenzyme trajectories obtained from HS-AFM and examined how restricting holoenzyme motion or increasing particle size affects cluster size (see Materials and Methods, fig. S8, tables S1 and S2, Supplementary Text, and movies S9 to S12). Simulations comparing particles with radii of 13 nm (basal state) and 16 nm (Ca2+/CaM-bound state) revealed that the larger particles formed clusters with a significantly greater average number of holoenzymes, indicative of enhanced cluster growth (fig. S8, H, and I). To replicate the anchoring of CaMKII to the synapse via NMDA receptors at the synapse, 13 fixed holoenzymes were introduced to represent CaMKII-NMDAR complexes, based on previous studies showing 4 to 18 NMDA receptors per synapse (60). This approach led to a significant increase in the average number of holoenzymes per cluster (fig. S8, H, and I). Together, these simulations demonstrate that both an increase in the effective size of CaMKIIα holoenzymes and localized constraints promote the expansion of the CaMKIIα cluster.

Previous studies of CaMKIIαP212L knock-in mice have reported increased autophosphorylation, resulting in abnormally elevated kinase activity. Consistent with this molecular phenotype, these mice exhibited hyperexcitable hippocampal LTP that can be induced by subthreshold low-frequency stimulation (59). In silico analysis using FoldX (61) predicts that P212L substitution destabilizes the hydrophobic core of the kinase domain and weakens interactions between the kinase domain and the regulatory segment (57). Consequently, P212L mutations likely loosen autoinhibited conformation and bias CaMKIIα toward an extended structural state in which the regulatory segment is disengaged. Our HS-AFM observations indicate that the extended structure of the kinase domain promotes the formation of larger CaMKIIα clusters. Accordingly, CaMKIIαP212L adopts a conformation closer to the extended structure in the basal state, thereby facilitating the formation of enlarged clusters (fig. S10A). Such enhanced basal clustering of CaMKIIαP212L may lower the threshold for LTP induction by stimuli that are insufficient to elicit LTP in WT neurons, providing a potential mechanistic link between altered mesoscale organization of CaMKIIα and the hyperexcitability observed in NDDs.

Although the spatial resolution of HS-AFM in our mesoscale observations does not allow for atomic-level identification of the molecular interfaces, the most plausible interaction mode can be narrowed down through structural and thermodynamic considerations. The RDF peak at approximately 19 nm (Fig. 3E) is consistent with a “direct contact” model, where the kinase domains of adjacent holoenzymes interact (fig. S9A). Other configurations, such as a kinase domain reaching a neighbor’s hub or linker, are likely precluded by substantial steric hindrance within the dense assembly of 12 kinase domains surrounding each hub (the “crossover” model; fig. S9B). Furthermore, the high conformational flexibility of these domains around hub (15) makes specific, stable interactions unlikely. This is supported by our AlphaFold3 predictions, which yielded various contact models with similar ranking scores rather than a single unique interface (fig. S9C and table S2). The estimated free energy of association is remarkably low (approximately −0.7 kcal/mol), comparable to thermal energy at room temperature (kBT = 0.6 kcal/mol), suggesting a weak, nonspecific association rather than a high-affinity binding interface. This is further supported by our observation that clusters form even in the presence of AIP, which blocks the specific regulatory T site. Collectively, these findings suggest that the observed clustering is primarily mediated by weak, nonspecific interactions at the outer surfaces of the kinase domains, likely triggered by altered surface charge and hydrophobicity upon the dissociation of the regulatory segment.

CaMKIIα has been reported to undergo self-assembly under ischemia-like conditions (low pH, low ATP/high adenosine diphosphate), forming large aggregates both in vitro and in vivo (35–39). These studies proposed a mechanism in which the intramolecular interaction between the kinase domain and the regulatory segment is co-opted to mediate intermolecular associations between holoenzymes (36, 37). Our HS-AFM observations share several features with these established models: Cluster formation is enhanced by Ca2+/CaM binding, involves contact between kinase domains of different holoenzymes, and is driven by the conformational rearrangement of the regulatory segment upon its dissociation from the kinase domain. Furthermore, the micrometer-scale aggregates previously observed by EM under ischemia-like conditions in purified forebrain CaMKII (35, 36) exhibit structural features along their peripheries that resemble our HS-AFM clusters. It is plausible that the chain-like or “grape-like” clusters identified here serve as the initial nuclei that subsequently fuse and propagate into the macroscopic organizations reported in earlier protein studies (35, 36) and isolated PSDs (62, 63). By providing this bridge between nanoscale molecular interactions and macroscopic assembly, our HS-AFM observations offer a framework for understanding how CaMKIIα clustering propagates across different spatial scales.

At the same time, some of our observations appear to differ from previous findings. While earlier studies reported that the phosphomimetic T286D mutant inhibited self-association (37), we observed expanded clusters under conditions including T286 autophosphorylation (Fig. 3, D and F). To further clarify the specific role of T286 phosphorylation, we used the T305A/T306V mutant and found that T286 phosphorylation alone can promote clustering under our experimental conditions (Fig. 4, F and H). Furthermore, whereas previous models suggested that AIP (a high-affinity peptide targeting the catalytic T site) prevents self-association (36), our data show that clusters can still form even in the presence of AIP (fig. S6). These results suggest that the mesoscale clustering observed here may involve interactions that are not fully accounted for by the previously proposed autoregulatory domain–catalytic interaction model. Nevertheless, several caveats should be considered: (i) The precise stoichiometry of T286 phosphorylation within each holoenzyme cannot be directly verified, (ii) the presence of affinity tags might influence the interaction between the regulatory segment and the kinase domain, and (iii) phosphorylation at sites other than T286/T305/T306 may contribute to cluster stability. Given these considerations, further experimental work will be helpful to fully deconvolve the molecular mechanisms of CaMKIIα cluster formation across different physiological scales.

Biochemical studies using bulk assay have shown that incubating CaMKIIα with Ca2+/CaM in the absence of ATP can lead to enzyme inactivation and a failure to form large aggregates, suggesting a transition into a “clustering-incompetent” state (35, 36, 64). However, our HS-AFM imaging reveals that this state is structurally equivalent to the autophosphorylated (pT286) state in its ability to form mesoscale clusters (Fig. 3, C to F). These results suggest that the primary driver of clustering is the physical displacement of the regulatory segment from the kinase domain, a structural transition that can proceed independently of the enzyme’s catalytic activity or its specific nucleotide-bound state.

The structural basis of these kinase-kinase interactions has been a subject of long-standing discussion. Our HS-AFM observations of mesoscale clusters align with structural and functional models suggesting that activated kinase domains can engage in dimeric pairing (12, 16, 17). While some crystallographic studies did not capture stable catalytic-catalytic contacts (18), our real-time imaging in high-density environments reveals that these potential pairings can propagate into stable multimeric assemblies. This suggests that the inherent flexibility and pairing capacity of the kinase domains, as proposed in earlier biochemical and structural studies, provide the fundamental molecular basis for the formation of the large-scale CaMKIIα clusters observed here.

Previous studies using colloids have demonstrated that isolated, chain-like clusters emerge during the initial stages of gelation, suggesting that short-range intermolecular interactions and hydrodynamic interactions are essential (65). Under the influence of hydrodynamic interactions, the solvent’s incompressibility prevents particles from converging directly toward the center, instead inducing lateral flow. This facilitates the formation of chain-like clusters. We hypothesize that CaMKIIα chain-like clusters exhibit a similar mechanism. As the density of CaMKIIα holoenzymes increases, nonspecific interactions between holoenzymes arise, producing an effect analogous to the initial stages of a colloid gelation. This likely promotes the assembly of chain-like clusters of CaMKIIα holoenzymes.

On the basis of these results, we propose a model for CaMKIIα localization to synapse during LTP (fig. S10B). In the basal state, a CaMKIIα holoenzyme either diffuses in spines or associates with various binding proteins such as F-actin (21, 22, 46, 48), forming pairs or small clusters comprising a few holoenzymes. Because basal clustering observed by HS-AFM reflects CaMKIIα holoenzymes in a two-dimensional and confined environment on the AFM substrate, the observed basal clustering could be amplified compared with that in intact cells. LTP-evoked Ca2+ influx promotes Ca2+/CaM binding, which shifts CaMKIIα toward an extended conformation that permits binding to the GluN2BCter at the PSD (22, 24, 46, 66). This CaMKIIα-GluN2B complex acts as a nucleation site; the cluster enlarges by recruiting adjacent CaMKIIα holoenzymes via kinase-domain contacts. Recent studies have shown that Ca2+/CaM binding to CaMKII, followed by interaction with GluN2BCter, is necessary for the early phase of spine structural plasticity during LTP (24). Therefore, alterations in CaMKIIα-CaMKIIα interactions have the potential to modulate cluster size and the local abundance of CaMKIIα in the spine, thereby influencing the induction and expression of synaptic plasticity. However, the PSD harbors numerous additional proteins, and whether similar CaMKIIα holoenzyme clustering arises in the intact synaptic environment remains to be established.

Our HS-AFM observations are limited to monitoring intermolecular interactions of CaMKIIα in the two-dimensional (2D) space on the AFM substrate. In addition, under current experimental conditions, both Ca2+/CaM and CaMKIIα exhibit non-specific adsorption to the AFM substrate, preventing real-time visualization of the sequential processes involving Ca2+/CaM binding to basal CaMKIIα and the subsequent formation and growth of CaMKIIα clusters. It is also conceivable that Ca2+/CaM molecules adsorbed to the mica surface could recruit CaMKIIα independently of holoenzyme-holoenzyme interactions, thereby influencing the observed clustering behavior. We aim to overcome this limitation by designing a more suitable HS-AFM substrate that minimizes nonspecific adsorption. Improved substrates and imaging conditions should enable direct visualization of the reversible cluster dynamics expected to occur in neurons.

Single-molecule imaging at the mesoscopic scale using HS-AFM directly visualized CaMKIIα clusters formed by CaMKIIα-CaMKIIα intermolecular interactions. Formation of chain-like clusters has the potential to be essential for the normal neuronal morphology and proper LTP induction. These data advance our understanding of the mechanisms governing synaptic targeting and are expected to inform models of learning and memory and to aid in elucidating the causes of NDDs.

Purification of CaM from bacteria

His-tagged CaM was expressed and purified from Escherichia coli (DH5α) following the protocol previously described (15). In brief, the bacterial pellet was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline containing 1% Triton X-100 and 5 mM imidazole, sonicated, and then centrifuged. The resulting supernatant was applied to a Ni2+-nitrilotriacetate (NTA) affinity column (HiTrap, GE Healthcare, USA). The bound protein was washed sequentially with buffers containing 5 and 50 mM imidazole in 20 mM tris (pH7.4) and 150 mM KCl and eluted with a buffer containing 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4) and 500 mM imidazole. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, USA) with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a standard. The resulting protein concentration was typically approximately 500 μM in a volume of 1000 μl. The His-tag attached to CaM was removed by TEV protease. The purified CaM was stored at 4° or −30°C in a buffer containing 20 mM tris-HCl (pH7.4), 150 mM KCl, and 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT).

All CaMKIIα proteins were purified from HEK293 or HEK293T cells following the protocol previously described (15). After 24 hours, the cells were lysed in a cold buffer containing 1% Triton X-100, 5% glycerol, 50 mM tris, 150 mM KCl, and 4 mM EDTA (pH adjusted to 7.6) and then centrifuged at 20,000g for 10 min. To remove the His-Strep tag from CaMKIIαWT, TEV protease and CaMKIIαWT were mixed at a 1:50 (w/w) ratio following the manufacturer’s protocol and incubated for 20 hours at 4°C using a dialysis kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) that exchanged the elution buffer for a buffer containing 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM KCl, and 1 mM DTT. After the reaction, His-tagged TEV protease was removed using an NTA column (Protein Ark, UK). The purity of the sample and the efficiency of tag cleavage were confirmed by silver staining and Western blotting using anti–His-tag and anti-CaMKIIα antibodies. Because this process reduced the sample concentration, HS-AFM observations in the main experiments were performed using His/Strep-tagged CaMKIIα. The purified proteins were stored at −30°C in 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM KCl, 40% glycerol, and 5 mM DTT. The homogeneity of the purified CaMKIIα was verified by direct HS-AFM visualization. The preparation was predominantly dodecameric (97%), with rare 14-mers (3%) and minimal presence of monomeric species, ensuring its suitability for stoichiometric analysis.

Biochemical assay

The phosphorylation state of CaMKIIα was assessed by Western blots to confirm phosphorylation at Thr286, Thr305, and Thr306 (fig. S5). Purified CaMKIIα proteins were used at concentrations of 300 nM CaMKIIαWT, CaMKIIαT286A, CaMKIIαT305A/T306V, 100 nM CaMKIIαP212L, and 300 nM Tag-free CaMKIIαWT. For the Ca2+/CaM-bound state, CaMKIIα proteins were mixed with CaM at a 1:1 molar ratio in a reaction buffer containing 50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM CaCl2. For pT286, CaMKIIα was similarly mixed with CaM at a 1:1 molar ratio in the same reaction buffer, supplemented with 1 mM ATP. For both conditions, reaction mixtures were incubated for 5 min at 30°C. To dissociate Ca2+/CaM from CaMKIIα, 2 mM EGTA was added, followed by a 5-min incubation at 30°C. Subsequently, the samples were incubated for an additional 10 min at 25°C to align with the conditions used for HS-AFM observations. The reactions were terminated by adding SDS sample buffer only for Western blot samples. Western blotting was conducted using the following primary antibodies: anti–phospho-CaMKII (Thr286) (D21E4, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti–CaMKIIα phospho-Thr305 (abx012403, Abbexa), anti–phospho-CaMKII (Thr306) (p1005-306, PhosphoSolutions, USA), anti–His-tag (27E8, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-CaMKIIα (Proteintech, USA), and IRDye 800CW conjugated anti-rabbit/mouse secondary antibodies (LI-COR, USA).

HS-AFM equipment

HS-AFM observations were performed using a custom-built instrument (15, 45). The deflection of the cantilever (AC-7, 8, or 10, MNOIC, Japan) was detected via an optical beam deflection system using an infrared (IR) laser at 780 nm with a power of 0.8 mW. The IR laser was focused on the back of the cantilever through a 20× objective lens (CFI S Plan Fluor LWD 20×, Nikon, Japan). The cantilever’s spring constant was approximately 0.2 N/m, with a resonant frequency around 400 kHz and a quality factor of 2 in liquid. The original HS-AFM tip had a bird beak–like triangular shape; to improve the spatial resolution of HS-AFM, an amorphous carbon tip was fabricated on it via electron beam deposition (EBD) using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Verios 5UC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The additional EBD tip measured about 250 nm in length, with the apex radius reduced to approximately 1 nm after plasma etching with a plasma cleaner (Tergeo, P.I.C. Scientific, USA). The cantilever’s amplitude was set below 1 nm, with the set point maintained at 90% of this amplitude for tip-sample distance control. Image acquisition and tip-scanning were controlled by the custom software UMEX. To minimize sample damage caused by tip scanning, an “only trace imaging” mode (67) was used, in which images were acquired only during the forward scan, while during the retrace, the tip was lifted slightly off the surface and scanned at the same speed without image acquisition.

HS-AFM observations

Condition 1

To observe cluster formation dynamics in the basal state, 600 nM CaMKIIα was added to imaging buffer A [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM KCl, and 10 mM MgCl2] during HS-AFM scanning. For the Ca2+/CaM-bound state, CaMKIIα and Ca2+/CaM were premixed at a 1:1 molar ratio in buffer A with 1 mM CaCl2 and incubated for 5 min at 30°C. Thereafter, 300 nM of the premixed Ca2+/CaM-bound CaMKIIα was added to the imaging buffer A with 1 mM CaCl2 during HS-AFM scanning.

If approximated as a 20-nm sphere, the concentration is approximately 37 μM for holoenzymes (444 μM for monomers). These values are in reasonable agreement with the reported CaMKII concentration of ∼329 μM in the mushroom spine (68). Condition 3

All HS-AFM observations of enlarged contact regions between two CaMKIIα holoenzymes were performed in imaging buffer B (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 15 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2) containing CaCl2 or ATP, depending on the experimental conditions (as above). Under these experimental conditions (characterized by low KCl concentration), the interaction between CaMKIIα and the AFM substrate is strengthened, facilitating accurate observation of the holoenzyme structure in a relatively fixed state. Both CaMKIIα and CaM were premixed at 50 nM each before imaging.

HS-AFM image processing and data analysis

HS-AFM images were processed and analyzed using Fiji (ImageJ) software (NIH, USA) (69). Background subtraction was performed on each image using the “Subtract Background” function with a rolling-ball radius of 150 to 400 pixels. Lateral drift across sequential HS-AFM images was corrected using the “Template Matching and Slice Alignment” plugin (70). To extract the coordinates of CaMKIIα holoenzymes in HS-AFM videos, the “MTrackJ” plugin (71) was used for semiautomated tracking of the intensity-weighted mean location of each CaMKIIα hub assembly. Trajectories of CaMKIIα holoenzymes were analyzed in the basal state (13,107 points across 170 frames from 604 holoenzymes) and in the Ca2+/CaM-bound state (16,925 points across 250 frames from 586 holoenzymes). Quantitative analysis of holoenzyme counts within clusters included the following: for CaMKIIαWT, 419 holoenzymes in 10 images (basal), 459 in 11 images (Ca2+/CaM), 380 in 10 images (Ca2+/CaM + ATP), 376 in 10 images (EGTA post–Ca2+/CaM/ATP), and 419 in 10 images (Ca2+/CaM + ATP + AIP); for CaMKIIαT286A, 465 holoenzymes in 10 images (basal), 446 in 11 images (Ca2+/CaM + ATP), and 339 in 7 images (EGTA post–Ca2+/CaM/ATP); for CaMKIIαT305A/T306V, 536 holoenzymes in 12 images (basal), 507 in 11 images (Ca2+/CaM + ATP), and 430 in 10 images (EGTA post–Ca2+/CaM/ATP); for CaMKIIαP212L, 656 holoenzymes in 14 images (basal), 329 in 8 images (Ca2+/CaM + ATP), and 596 in 11 images (EGTA post–Ca2+/CaM/ATP); and for Tag-free CaMKIIαWT, 211 holoenzymes in 5 images (basal), 344 holoenzymes in 9 images (Ca2+/CaM + ATP), and 373 in 9 images (EGTA post–Ca2+/CaM/ATP).

Definition of cluster formation

CaMKIIα clustering during HS-AFM observations was analyzed using the Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) algorithm (72, 73) applied to the coordinates of individual holoenzymes in each frame. DBSCAN requires two parameters: a distance threshold ε and a minimum number of points (min_samples). We defined a cluster as a group of individual CaMKIIα holoenzymes whose hub assemblies were located within a distance of ε (nanometers) or less. Specifically, the value of ε was determined on the basis of our previous HS-AFM (15), which showed that the distance from the central hub assembly to the kinase domain center measures 10.6 nm in the basal state and 13.6 nm in the Ca2+/CaM-bound state. In addition, the radius of the kinase domain has been reported as 2.3 nm by both HS-AFM (15) and EM (12) studies. Using these values, the distance from the central hub assembly to the outer edge of the kinase domain (i.e., the holoenzyme radius) is 10.6 + 2.3 ≈ 13 nm in the basal state and 13.6 + 2.3 ≈ 16 nm in the Ca2+/CaM-bound state. Because clustering is expected when two holoenzymes touch, we classified holoenzymes as “in cluster” when the distance between their central hub assemblies was less than or equal to twice the holoenzyme radius (basal: 13 nm × 2 = 26 nm; Ca2+/CaM-bound: 16 nm × 2 = 32 nm) (Fig. 2C). These thresholds correspond to the span of the first peak in the RDF, ensuring that the cluster classification remains stable despite minor positional variations. Min-sample was set to 2 to detect clusters comprising two or more adjacent CaMKIIα holoenzymes.

Mean square displacement

To assess CaMKIIα holoenzyme mobility on mica surface in clustered versus nonclustered states, we tracked the hub-assembly center coordinates of Ca2+/CaM-bound CaMKIIαWT and calculated MSDs. DBSCAN was applied per frame to classify each holoenzyme as in cluster or out of cluster; cluster duration thresholds of 15, 50, 75, 100, 125, and 150 s were used. Here, holoenzymes that remained in a cluster for longer than the chosen threshold were designated in cluster, whereas those that remained outside of a cluster for longer than the threshold were designated “out of cluster.” MSD was computed separately for each group. Although MSD typically provides reliable measures of mobility with extended recording and large particle counts, the transient nature of CaMKIIα clustering in our data limited the number of holoenzymes that remained clustered over long intervals. Simulation of CaMKIIα cluster dynamics in a two-dimensional space

To investigate the factors contributing to the growth of CaMKIIα clusters, particle simulations were conducted on a two-dimensional plane in a 500 nm–by–500 nm area. A custom Python-based simulation was developed to reproduce the stochastic dynamics of CaMKII cluster formation as observed by HS-AFM, including both the recruitment of holoenzymes to the substrate and their subsequent lateral interactions. Here, a particle corresponds to a CaMKIIα holoenzyme. The particle radii were estimated on the basis of the distance between the hub and the kinase domains observed by single-molecule HS-AFM of CaMKIIα (15) and the diameter of the kinase domain observed by EM (12). The radius for the basal state was set to rbasal= 13 nm and that for the Ca2+/CaM-bound state to rCa2+/CaM = 16 nm.

First, the adsorption and desorption kinetics of individual holoenzymes to the mica substrate were modeled using the binding rate constant of particles to the substrate (kon), and the dissociation rate constant from the substrate (koff) were estimated on the basis of HS-AFM observations. Three independent HS-AFM datasets were analyzed. The number of particles on the substrate against observation time was fitted by the exponential function, A{1−exp(−kt)}, where A is the number of particles in the steady state, k is the rate constant, and t is time. During HS-AFM measurements, a slight delay can occur between the onset of particle adsorption onto the substrate and the start of image acquisition. To account for this offset and align the time origins among datasets, the fitting was performed using a corrected time variable t + Ci, where C1 = 14 s, C2 = 60 s, and C3 = 0 s for the respective datasets, where Ci was chosen to minimize the difference from the exponential function. Fitting the combined datasets yielded A of 97.8 and k of 0.0064 s−1 (fig. S8). The dissociation rate constant koff was then determined from the experimentally measured dissociation frequency during the steady state, defined as 300 s after data acquisition began. The frequency was the number of particles that detached from the substrate and disappeared from the observation field divided by the observation time. Given a dissociation frequency of 0.1341 s−1 and assuming that the number of particles present on the substrate at steady state is A, the dissociation rate constant was calculated as koff = 0.1341/A, yielding koff = 0.0014 s−1. Because the rate constant k obtained from fitting in a two-state model is the sum of the binding and dissociation rates (k = kon + koff), kon was calculated to be 0.0050 s−1.

Using the obtained values of A, kon, and koff, the total number of particles within the system, Ntotal, was estimated. At equilibrium state, the rate of particle binding to the substrate is balanced by the rate of dissociation. Denoting the number of particles freely diffusing in solution as Noff, this balance is described by Noff × kon = A × koff. Because Ntotal = Noff + A, the total particle number can be expressed as Ntotal = A (kon + koff)/kon. Substituting the experimentally determined parameters into this relation yielded Ntotal ≈ 125.1. This value was subsequently adjusted to 135 so that the simulation results more closely reproduced the HS-AFM observations. To validate the chosen parameters, time-dependent curves obtained from 20 independent simulation trials were also fitted with A{1−exp(−kt)}, resulting in A = 93.8 and k = 0.0065 s−1. The close agreements with the experimental values indicate that the chosen values of kon, koff, and Ntotal are appropriate (fig. S8).

The particles were allowed to randomly walk. The simulation time step (Δt) was set to 1 s. The particle displacement in the simulation step was adjusted so that the diffusion coefficient (D) obtained from the MSD analysis matched the D measured in HS-AFM experiments. The MSD in the simulation was estimated using over 12,000 particles. Linear fitting using MSD = 4Dt was performed for particles observed for more than 200 s, with the fitted time range set at 0 to 100 s. It was found that D in the simulation was close to that in the experiments when the particle displacement followed a normal distribution centered at 1.8 nm per step with an SD of 1 nm (fig. S8). The maximum displacement was set at 10 nm. The direction of movement was randomly assigned on the basis of a uniform distribution. When particles moved outside the simulation area, they were removed from the simulation (open boundary condition). When two particles approached within 32 nm and unnaturally overlapped after particle displacement, both particles were slightly displaced in opposite directions. This adjustment was iteratively performed until all overlaps were eliminated.

Following the establishment of substrate-binding kinetics, intermolecular cluster formation was defined as formed when two particles are within the particle diameter after displacement. To avoid numerical artifacts arising from rounding errors in floating-point calculations, this distance criterion was evaluated with a small offset of 10−12 nm added to the threshold. After cluster formation, particles belonging to the same cluster were assumed to move rigidly such that all particles in a given cluster shared the same displacement magnitude and direction, which were determined by the vector sum of the individual displacement vectors of the particles within the cluster. To estimate the dissociation rate of CaMKII from clusters, the duration during which CaMKII molecules remained in clusters was measured at low density (approximately 60% of the maximum particle number) in HS-AFM observations. The histogram of the duration was fitted with an exponential function, Bexp(−λt), where λ is the dissociation rate and was 0.1416 s−1.

The dissociation probability per one simulation step (P) was derived from λ obtained from HS-AFM measurements under low-density conditions, where clusters predominantly exist as dimers. Let P denote the dissociation probability of an individual particle from a cluster, and let P′ denote the probability that a cluster dissociates. Assuming that dissociation follows a Poisson process, P′ was calculated using the relation P′ = 1 − exp(−λΔt). A dimeric cluster dissociates when either of the two constituent particles dissociates. If dissociation from clusters is independent, the probability that the cluster remains is given by (1 − P)2, which is the probability that neither particle dissociates. Accordingly, the probability that a cluster dissociates within 1 s observed in HS-AFM observations, denoted as P′, is calculated by P′ = 1 − (1 − P)2 = 2P – P2. By substituting the experimentally determined P′ into this relation, the per-particle dissociation probability P was estimated (P = 0.0683), which corresponds to the dissociation rate (0.0708 s−1) that should be used in the simulation. However, an adjustment to this rate was required to match the simulated dissociation rate from clusters with that observed in HS-AFM experiments, yielding a corrected rate of 0.1054 s−1 (P = 0.10).

In simulations including immobile particles, 13 particles were fixed at the coordinates (0, 0), (62.5, 62.5), (−62.5, 62.5), (62.5, −62.5), (−62.5, −62.5), (125, 125), (125, 0), (125, −125), (0, −125), (−125, −125), (−125, 0), (−125, 125), and (0, 125) throughout the simulation. When mobile particles formed a rigid cluster with a fixed particle, the cluster’s motion became rotational since the fixed particle prevents translation. To account for this rotation, the center of mass of the mobile particles within the cluster was first calculated, and the distance between this center of mass and the fixed particle was defined as the rotation radius. A displacement distance chosen from a normal distribution centered at 1.8 nm per step with an SD of 1 nm was converted into an angular increment along the circumference to ensure that the center of mass displacement follows the normal distribution. The direction of rotation, clockwise or counterclockwise, was chosen randomly. The positions of all mobile particles in the cluster were rotated around the fixed particle.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Igor Pro 10 (WaveMetrics, USA) or OriginPro2025 (OriginLab, USA). A significance threshold of α = 0.05 was maintained for all tests, with P values corrected for multiple comparisons when applicable. Normality of data was tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test unless otherwise specified. Homogeneity of variances among groups was checked using Levene’s test. When normality was not confirmed, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for group comparisons. Pairwise comparisons between two groups were performed using the Brunner-Munzel test and Welch’s t test. For comparisons involving multiple conditions, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Kruskal-Wallis test were used to analyze multiple groups with a single independent variable. As a follow-up test to the Kruskal-Wallis test, Dunn’s test was used to compare every mean with every other mean. The figure captions specify the statistical tests used. P > 0.05, not significant (NS); *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 were considered to indicate statistical significance. The data are presented as the means ± SDs.
