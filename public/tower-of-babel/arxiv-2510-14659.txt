Jump Processes with Self-Interactions: Large Deviation Asymptotics
Amarjit Budhiraja, Francesco Coghi

Abstract
. We consider a pure jump process { X t } t ≥ 0 \{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0} with values in a finite state space S = { 1 , … , d } S=\{1,\ldots,d\} for which the jump rates at time instant t t depend on the occupation measure L t ≐ 1 t ​ ∫ 0 t δ X s ​ 𝑑 s L_{t}\doteq\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{X_{s}}\,ds . Such self-interacting chains arise in many contexts within statistical physics and applied probability. Under appropriate conditions, a large deviation principle is established for the pair ( L t , R t ) (L_{t},R_{t}) , as t → ∞ t\to\infty , where R t R_{t} is the empirical flux process associated with the jump process. We show that the rate function takes a simple form that can be viewed as a dynamical generalization of the classical Donsker and Varadhan rate function for the analogous quantities in the setting of Markov processes, in particular, unlike the Markovian case, the rate function is not convex. Since the state process is non-Markovian, different techniques are needed than in the setting of Donsker and Varadhan and our proofs rely on variational representations for functionals of Poisson random measures and stochastic control methods. AMS 2020 subject classifications: 60F10, 60J74, 34H05. Keywords: Level 2.5 2.5 LDP, reinforced random walks, Laplace asymptotics, weak convergence method, empirical current, stochastic approximations.



1.
Introduction

We consider a pure jump process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
with values in a finite state space
S=\{1,\ldots,d\}
for which the jump rates at time instant
t
depend on the occupation measure
L_{t}\doteq\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{X_{s}}\,ds
. This dependence is specified through a collection of
d\times d
rate matrices (infinitesimal generators)
\{Q(\gamma),\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)\}
, where

\mathcal{P}(S)
is the space of probability measures on
S
. Roughly speaking, at time instant
t
, conditionally on
\sigma\{X(s):0\leq s\leq t\}
, and given
X(t)=i
, the jump rate to state
j\neq i
is
Q_{ij}(L_{t})
. A precise definition is given in terms of a certain martingale problem characterization; see Section
2
. Such processes are usually referred to as self-interacting Markov jump processes and arise in many different contexts; see discussion at the end of this Introduction. We note however that the evolution of
\{X_{t}\}
is not Markovian and to get a Markov state descriptor, one needs to consider the pair
\{(X_{t},L_{t})\}
which defines a time inhomogeneous Markov process with state space
S\times\mathcal{P}(S)
. We are interested in the large time behavior of the process under a large deviation scaling. The specific objects we study are the occupation measure process
\{L_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

and the
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d(d-1)}
valued
empirical flux
process
\{R_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
where

R_{t}=(R_{t}^{xy},x,y\in S,x\neq y)
and
R_{t}^{xy}
is the time averaged number of transitions from state
x
to state
y
over the time interval
[0,t]
; see (
2.5
) for a precise definition.

Our results give a large deviation principle (LDP) for
\{(L_{t},R_{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
, in
\mathcal{P}(S)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d(d-1)}
, as
t\to\infty
under suitable conditions on
Q(\cdot)
; see Section
2
and also, comments on these conditions later in the Introduction. In the large deviation literature such a result is referred to as a Level
2.5
LDP, and, in comparison to an LDP for the occupation measure alone, it captures important dynamic details of the process by keeping track of not only how often different states are visited but also how often different types of state transitions occur. Through a contraction principle, such a result also gives an LDP for the
\mathbb{R}^{d(d-1)}
valued
empirical current
process
\{J_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, where
J_{t}=(J_{t}^{xy},x,y\in S,x\neq y)
and
J_{t}^{xy}=R_{t}^{xy}-R_{t}^{yx}
.

In the setting where
Q(\gamma)\equiv Q^{0}
for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)
, for some irreducible rate matrix
Q^{0}
, these large deviation results are classical from the work of Donsker and Varadhan
[
23
,
11
]
.
The rate function,
I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}:\mathcal{P}(S)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{(d(d-1)}\to[0,\infty]
, governing the LDP for
(L_{t},R_{t})
in this Markovian setting is given as

(1.1)

I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}(\gamma,\varsigma)=\begin{cases}\sum_{x\neq y}\gamma_{x}Q^{0}_{xy}\ell\left(\frac{\varsigma^{xy}}{\gamma_{x}Q^{0}_{xy}}\right)&\mbox{ if }\sum_{y:y\neq x}\varsigma^{xy}=\sum_{y:y\neq x}\varsigma^{yx}\mbox{ for all }x\in S\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
(\gamma,\varsigma)\in\mathcal{P}(S)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{(d(d-1)}
and
\ell(x)=x\log x-x+1
for
x\geq 0
.

For the self-interacting case studied in this work, the rate function we obtain can be viewed as a certain dynamical version of the above rate function. A precise definition is in (
6.4
) (see also (
3.6
) for a different formula), however roughly speaking, the rate function we obtain takes the form

(1.2)

I(\gamma,\varsigma)=\inf\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{{Q(M(s))}}(\rho(s),\eta(s))\,ds,\;(\gamma,\varsigma)\in\mathcal{P}(S)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d(d-1)},

where the infimum is taken over all maps
(\rho,\eta):\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathcal{P}(S)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d(d-1)}
with
\gamma=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\rho(s)ds
,

\varsigma=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\eta(s)ds
, and
M(t)=e^{t}\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-s}\rho(s)\,ds
.

An infinite horizon discounted cost in the definition of the rate function arises in a natural fashion from the evolution equation for the empirical measure process (see e.g. (
4.14
)) which says that changes to the empirical measure become vanishingly small as
t
becomes large.
These changes of order
t^{-1}
ensure that contributions to the rate function at large times are suitably discounted, as captured by the exponential factor in the rate function. In the Markovian setting it turns out that the infimum over
(\rho,\eta)
as above is achieved with
\rho_{s}\equiv\gamma
and
\eta_{s}\equiv\varsigma
and thus the above formula for the rate function reduces to the formula given by the Donsker-Varadhan theory (see Section
9
). The form of the rate function above also captures a certain multiscale feature of the system which says that the state process
\{X_{t}\}
rapidly equilibrates locally as the environment described by
\{L_{t}\}
changes slowly. These local equilibria are seen in the form of the local rate function
I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{{Q(M(s))}}(\gamma(s),\eta(s))
that defines the overall rate function
I
.

For discrete time self-interacting finite state Markov chains, similar large deviation behavior has recently been observed in
[
18
]
(see also
[
17
]
). These works do not consider the empirical flux process but if one restricts to the LDP for the empirical measure process
\{L_{t}\}
(obtained by an application of the contraction principle), the rate function we obtain in the current work has analogies with the rate functions in these works (e.g. in terms of an exponential discount factor and multiscale feature of the dynamics). The proof techniques in the current work, which rely on variational formulae for moments of nonnegative functionals of Poisson random measures (PRM), are however quite different.

We now give some background for the self-interacting models studied in this work.
Self-interacting stochastic processes, such as

[

,

and later extensions to general open domains
[

,

,

Other examples of discrete-time self-interacting processes include generalised Pólya urns, which have been used to model genetic drift in biological species
[

as well as novelty dynamics
[

within the emerging field of the science of science. A further motivation for studying self-interacting processes, in particular those depending on the

[
2
]
, and from more general settings in sampling algorithms
[

,

.

In statistical physics, self-interacting Markov jump processes are attracting interest as a natural setting in which to extend the framework of stochastic thermodynamics
[
41
,
35
]
beyond the Markovian limit. Although non-Markovian formulations of stochastic thermodynamics have been approached in several ways
[

, self-interacting processes seem to provide a distinct, physically motivated class where memory effects can be analyzed systematically, as shown in this work. Such memory effects arise naturally when
addressing coarse-graining and measurement limitations

39
]
. When hidden degrees of freedom or limited temporal resolution cause the system’s observed evolution to depend on its own past—through quantities such as empirical occupation measures—the dynamics become effectively self-interacting. The theoretical framework developed here provides a starting point to analyze and quantify emergent non-Markovian behavior, revealing how microscopic information loss gives rise to macroscopic memory and irreversibility.

Most of the research on such self-interacting systems has focused on the study of the law of large numbers and central limit theorems, often via stochastic approximation theory and branching-process techniques, see e.g.
[

,

,

[
2

, certain variants of edge reinforced and vertex reinforced random walks, a type of personalized PageRank algorithm, and certain generalized Pólya urn schemes.
For related literature on large deviations for discrete time models with reinforcement see

We now make some comments on the assumptions that are made in our work. For the upper bound the only condition we require is that the map
\gamma\to Q(\gamma)
is a continuous map.
The lower bound requires additional conditions that are summarized in Assumption
3.2
. The four parts of this assumption are analogous to conditions that are needed in the analysis of the discrete time model in
[
18
]
, the first condition requires a strictly positive solution to the equation
\pi^{*}Q(\pi^{*})=0
; the second
says that
Q
is an affine map; the third imposes a natural communicability structure on the rate matrices
\{Q(\gamma)\}
; and the fourth condition says that the empirical measure
L_{t}
eventually charges all points
S
, a.s.
See Remark
3.3
for a discussion on these conditions.

Next we briefly discuss the proof techniques. As noted earlier in the Introduction, the main tool used in our proofs is the variational representation for functionals of PRM obtained in
[
16
]
(see also
[
15
, Chapter 8]
). This variational representation is well suited to establish the equivalent form of the large deviation principle given in terms of certain Laplace asymptotic formulas (see
[
15
, Corollary 1.10]
). Specifically, the variational formula for PRM gives certain stochastic control representations for the quantities of interest in the Laplace asymptotics (namely the left sides of (
4.1
) and (
4.2
)). With these stochastic control representations the main step in the proof is establishing convergence of the value functions of these stochastic control problems to the optimal value of certain deterministic optimization problems associated with the rate function. One important insight is obtained by considering the scaling
t\mapsto e^{t}
, which, together with a time reversal step leads to dynamics and cost in the stochastic control problems that roughly resemble analogous terms in the limiting deterministic control problem (e.g. compare the evolution of the scaled and time reversed dynamics in (
4.17
) with the identity in
Property
3.1
(b) and the cost on the right side of (
5.33
) with the cost in (
3.4
)). This scaling and time reversal plays an important role in the proof. The proof is completed in two parts.
Section
5
proves the upper bound, namely the inequality in (
4.1
), while the lower bound proof (i.e. the proof of (
4.2
)), which is technically the most demanding part of the work, is completed in Sections
6
and
7
. The upper bound relies on tightness and weak limit point characterizations of certain sequences of random measures constructed from the controlled state processes and control processes. For this inequality it is convenient to consider a different representation of the rate function than the one discussed earlier in the Introduction and which is more directly connected with the weak limits of the
costs expressed as integrals with respect to the above random measures.
The lower bound requires construction of certain feedback controls that are asymptotically near optimal for the variational problem associated with the first representation of the rate function, namely, the one that appears in (
6.4
). This construction requires ensuring that the feedback controls have certain continuity and nondegeneracy properties which are achieved by a series of approximations that are carried out in Sections
6.3
and
6.4
. Section
7
then completes the proof of the lower bound by providing a construction of the feedback controls and proving the convergence of controls, state processes, and costs to the desired limits. Section
8
show that our proposed rate function is indeed a rate function, namely, it has compact sublevel sets. Finally Section
9
contains some discussion and illustrative examples.

2.
Model and Notation

We will consider the following family of self-interacting Markov jump processes.
Let
S=\left\{{1,2,\cdots,d}\right\}
be the finite set of states of the process.
Recall that
\mathcal{P}(S)
is the space of probability measures on
S
which will be identified with the
d-1
dimensional simplex in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, namely

\mathcal{P}(S)=\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\,|\,x\cdot\mathbf{1}=1}\right\},

equipped with the
L^{1}
-distance: for
x,y\in\mathcal{P}(S)
,
\|x-y\|\doteq\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{1}|
.
Throughout, we will denote
\{(i,j)\in S\times S:i\neq j\}=A
.
Let
\mathcal{K}_{0}(S)
be the collection of rate matrices on
S
, namely the space of
d\times d
matrices
\mathbf{q}=(q_{ij})_{i,j\in S}
satisfying

q_{i,j}\geq 0
for
(i,j)\in A
and
q_{ii}=-\sum_{j:j\neq i}q_{ij}
,
i\in S
. We denote by
\mathcal{K}(S)
the subset of
\mathcal{K}_{0}(S)

consisting of rate matrices that are
irreducible , i.e., for any
i,j\in S
, there exist
m\in\mathbb{N}
and distinct
i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\in S
with
q_{i_{k},i_{k+1}}>0
for all
k=1,\ldots,m-1
,
q_{i,i_{1}}>0
and
q_{i_{m},j}>0
.

Let
Q:\mathcal{P}(S)\to\mathcal{K}_{0}(S)
be a continuous map. From the compactness of
\mathcal{P}(S)
and continuity of
Q
it follows that

(2.1)

\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)}\max_{(i,j)\in A}Q_{ij}(\gamma)=c_{Q}<\infty.

We will occasionally suppress
Q
and write
c_{Q}
as
c
.

We are interested in an
S
valued self-interacting Markov jump process,
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, starting with some state
x\in S
, which is uniquely characterized in law by the following properties: For all
f:S\to\mathbb{R}

f(X_{t})-f(x)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{L_{s}}f(X_{s})ds,t\geq 0,

is a
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\{X_{s}:0\leq s\leq t\}
martingale, where

(2.2)

L_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{X_{s}}ds\,,t>0,\;L_{0}=\delta_{x}\,,

and for
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)
,

(2.3)

\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}f(i)=\sum_{j:j\neq i}Q_{ij}(\gamma)(f(j)-f(i))\,,i\in S.

Such a process can be constructed pathwise as follows. Consider a probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
on which are given
a\doteq d(d-1)
mutually independent Poisson random measures (PRM)
\{N_{ij},(i,j)\in A\}
with point space as
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[0,c]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
with intensity given as the Lebesgue measure
\lambda(ds\,du\,dr)=ds\,du\,dr
.

Define an
S\times\mathcal{P}(S)
valued stochastic process
(X_{t},L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by the following system of equations.

(2.4)

X_{t}=x+\sum_{(i,j)\in A}\int_{[0,t]\times[0,c]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}(j-i)1_{[X_{s-}=i]}1_{[0,Q_{ij}(L_{s-})]}(u)1_{[0,1]}(r)N_{ij}(ds\,du\,dr)\,,

with
\{L_{t}\}
defined by (
2.2
).

Together with the above processes, we will be interested in the

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{a}
-valued empirical flux process
R_{t}=(R^{ij}_{t})_{(i,j)\in A}
, where
R^{ij}_{t}
gives the (time averaged) number of transitions from state
i
to state
j
during the time interval
[0,t]
. This process can be described using the above collection of PRM as follows: For
(i,j)\in A
,

(2.5)

R_{t}^{ij}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{[0,t]\times[0,c]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}1_{[X_{s-}=i]}1_{[0,Q_{ij}(L_{s-})]}(u)1_{[0,1]}(r)N_{ij}(ds\,du\,dr).

We note that the process
(X_{t},L_{t},R_{t})
can be constructed recursively from one jump to next by a standard procedure and this process satisfies the martingale property noted above. We also remark that one can give a construction of these processes
using simpler PRM (e.g. those defined on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[0,c]
) or by a collection of time changed standard Poisson processes, however the above construction is useful when studying the large deviation behavior as will be seen below.

3.
Main Result

The goal of the work is to establish an LDP for the collection
\{(L_{t},R_{t})\}
in
\mathcal{P}(S)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{a}
, as
t\to\infty
, with an appropriate rate function
I:\mathcal{P}(S)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{a}\to[0,\infty]
. We recall that such a map is called a rate function if it has compact sublevel sets, i.e. for every
m<\infty
,
\{(\gamma,\varsigma)\in\mathcal{P}(S)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{a}:I(\gamma,\varsigma)\leq m\}
is a compact subset of
\mathcal{P}(S)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{a}
.

We now introduce the rate function
I
that will govern the large deviation behavior of
\{(L_{t},R_{t})\}
as
t\to\infty
.
The fact that
I
has compact sublevel sets will be shown in Section
8
.

Given
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)
and
\varsigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{a}
, let
\theta\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{P}(S\times\mathbb{R}^{a}_{+}\times[0,c]))
be such that
the following properties hold.

Property 3.1
.

(a)

We have

\gamma=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}[\theta(s)]_{1}ds
.

(b)

Let

M(t)\doteq e^{t}\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-s}[\theta(s)]_{1}ds,\;t\geq 0.

Then

\int_{S\times\mathbb{R}^{a}_{+}\times[0,c]}\sum_{y:y\neq x}1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M_{s})]}(u)r_{xy}\theta(s)(dx\,dr\,du)<\infty,\mbox{ for a.e. }s\geq 0

and
for all
f\in\mathbb{B}(S,\mathbb{R})
,

(3.1)

\int_{S\times\mathbb{R}^{a}_{+}\times[0,c]}\sum_{y:y\neq x}(f(y)-f(x))1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M_{s})]}(u)r_{xy}\theta(s)(dx\,dr\,du)=0,\mbox{ for a.e. }s\geq 0.

(c)

For a.e.
s\geq 0
,
[\theta(s)]_{13}(dx\,du)=[\theta(s)]_{1}(dx)c^{-1}du
.

(d)

Writing
\varsigma=(\varsigma^{xy})_{(x,y)\in A}
, for
(x,y)\in A
,

(3.2)

c\int_{\mathbb{R}^{a}_{+}\times[0,c]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}e^{-s}[\theta(s)]_{1}(x)1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M_{s})]}(u)r_{xy}\eta(s)(dr\,du\mid x)ds=\varsigma^{xy},

where
M_{s}
is as in part (b) and
\theta(s)(dx\,dr\,du)=\eta(s)(dr\,du\mid x)[\theta(s)]_{1}(dx)
.

We denote the collection of all such
\theta
as
\mathcal{S}_{\gamma,\varsigma}
, namely,

(3.3)

\mathcal{S}_{\gamma,\varsigma}=\{\theta\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{P}(S\times\mathbb{R}^{a}_{+}\times[0,c])):\mbox{Property \ref{proper:1} holds}\}.

For
\theta\in\mathcal{S}_{\gamma,\varsigma}
, the
M\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+}:\mathcal{P}(S))
defined in part (b) of Condition
3.1
will be denoted as

M^{\theta}
.
Let
\Xi\doteq S\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{a}\times[0,c]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
For
\theta\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{P}(S\times\mathbb{R}^{a}_{+}\times[0,c]))
, let

(3.4)

J(\theta)=c\int_{\Xi}e^{-s}\sum_{y:y\neq x}1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M_{s})]}(u)\ell(r_{xy})\,\theta(s)(dx\,dr\,du)ds\,,

where

(3.5)

\ell(x)=x\log x-x+1,\,\,x\geq 0\,.

Then the rate function is defined as

(3.6)

I(\gamma,\varsigma)=\inf_{\theta\in\mathcal{S}_{\gamma,\varsigma}}J(\theta),\;\;\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S),\varsigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{a}.

See also a different representation for the rate function in (
6.4
).

We can now state the main result of this work. For the upper bound we do not assume any additional conditions beyond the continuity of the map
Q
assumed previously. For the lower bound we will need the following additional assumption. We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{+}(S)
the collection of
m\in\mathcal{P}(S)
such that
m(x)>0

.

Assumption 3.2
.

(a)

There is a
\pi^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(S)

\pi^{*}Q(\pi^{*})=0
.

(b)

For all
\kappa\in[0,1]

Q(\kappa m_{1}+(1-\kappa)m_{2})=\kappa Q(m_{1})+(1-\kappa)Q(m_{2})
.

(c)

There is an
\mathcal{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{K}(S)
such that, with
A_{0}=\{(x,y)\in A:\mathcal{A}^{*}(x,y)>0\}
,
Q_{ij}(m)=0
for all
m\in\mathcal{P}(S)
and
(i,j)\in A\setminus A_{0}
. Furthermore, for some
k_{Q}\in(0,\infty)
, and all
(i,j)\in A_{0}

Remark 3.3

The affine property in part (b) is satisfied in many settings and we refer the reader to Section

The first statement in part (c) in the assumption is a natural communication condition which specifies the permissible transitions in the model, e.g. the jump processes of interest may be nearest neighbor walks so that only transitions to the immediate neighbors are allowed.
If part (b) of the assumption holds, the second part of part (c) will hold as long as there is some

Suppose that we have the stronger property that

In this setting (and assuming that (b) holds), an argument as in

6.

6.3.

The main result of this section given below ensures that

Lemma 6.4

There is a

\inf_{s\geq 0}\inf_{x\in S}\min\{M^{1}_{s}(x),\rho^{1}_{s}(x)\}\geq\varkappa_{1},\;\inf_{s\geq 0}\inf_{(x,y)\in A_{0}}H^{1}_{s}(x,y)\geq\varkappa_{1}.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and let

\rho_{s}^{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)\rho_{s}^{*}+\alpha\pi^{*},\;s\geq 0.

Also, let

\varsigma^{\alpha,xy}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\rho^{\alpha}_{s}(x)H^{\alpha}_{s}(x,y)ds=(1-\alpha)\varsigma^{*,xy}+\alpha\pi^{*}(x)Q_{xy}(\pi^{*}),

and
\gamma^{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)\gamma^{*}+\alpha\pi^{*}
.
Then it is easy to verify that
(\rho^{\alpha},H^{\alpha})\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\gamma^{\alpha},\varsigma^{\alpha}}
and

M^{\rho^{\alpha}}=M^{\alpha}
.
From Assumption
3.2
(c)

(6.9)

\inf_{(x,y)\in A_{0}}Q_{xy}(m)\geq k_{Q}\inf_{x\in S}m_{x},\mbox{ for all }m\in\mathcal{P}(S).

Furthermore, from this assumption, we can find

(6.10)

\inf_{x\in S}\pi^{*}_{x}\geq a_{\pi^{*}}.

Using the linearity Assumption (
3.2
) (b), with

\displaystyle\rho^{\alpha}_{s}(x)Q_{xy}(M^{\alpha}_{s})

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)^{2}\rho^{*}_{s}(x)Q_{xy}(M^{*}_{s})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+r(\alpha)\left(\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{r(\alpha)}\pi^{*}Q_{xy}(M^{*}_{s})+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{r(\alpha)}\rho^{*}_{s}(x)Q_{xy}(\pi^{*})+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{r(\alpha)}\pi^{*}(x)Q_{xy}(\pi^{*})\right).

Similarly write

\displaystyle\rho^{\alpha}_{s}(x)H^{\alpha}_{s}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\rho^{*}_{s}(x)H^{*}_{s}(x,y)+\alpha\pi^{*}(x)Q_{xy}(\pi^{*})

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)^{2}\rho^{*}_{s}(x)H^{*}_{s}(x,y)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+r(\alpha)\left(\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{r(\alpha)}\rho_{s}^{*}(x)H^{*}_{s}(x,y)+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{r(\alpha)}\pi^{*}(x)Q_{xy}(\pi^{*})+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{r(\alpha)}\pi^{*}(x)Q_{xy}(\pi^{*})\right)

Using Lemma
6.3
, for
s\geq 0
and
(x,y)\in A_{0}

(6.11)

Q_{xy}(M^{\alpha}_{s})\rho^{\alpha}_{s}(x)\ell\left(\frac{H^{\alpha}_{s}(x,y)}{Q_{xy}(M^{\alpha}_{s})}\right)=Q_{xy}(M^{\alpha}_{s})\rho^{\alpha}_{s}(x)\ell\left(\frac{\rho^{\alpha}_{s}(x)H^{\alpha}_{s}(x,y)}{\rho^{\alpha}_{s}(x)Q_{xy}(M^{\alpha}_{s})}\right)\\

Note that, for a.e.

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\sup_{(x,y)\in A_{0}}A_{\alpha}(s,x,y)=0,\;\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\sup_{(x,y)\in A_{0}}B_{\alpha}(s,x,y)|\log\alpha|=0.

Also, denoting

(suppressing

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}\ell\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}}{A_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=B_{\alpha}\log B_{\alpha}-B_{\alpha}\log A_{\alpha}-B_{\alpha}+A_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq B_{\alpha}\log B_{\alpha}+B_{\alpha}\log^{-}A_{\alpha}+A_{\alpha}.

From the first line of (

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1/2)}[1+|\varrho_{s}(x,y)\log\varrho_{s}(x,y)|].

In the last line we have used the fact that

The claim now follows on taking

and using the property in (

M
introduced above satisfies the identity

M(t)=e^{t}\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-s}[\theta(s)]_{1}ds,\;t\geq 0.

From the bound in (
8.6
), we see that the finiteness property in part (b) of Property
3.1
holds.
We now verify the second statement in part (b).
Since
\tilde{\theta}^{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{\nu^{n},\varsigma^{n}}
, from part (b)
of Property
3.1
we have that for any continuous and bounded
g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}

(8.7)

\int_{\Xi}g(s)\sum_{y:y\neq x}(f(y)-f(x))1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M^{n}_{s})]}(u)r_{xy}\nu^{n}(dx\,dr\,du\,ds)=0.

Also, by convergence of
M^{n}\to M
, and from arguments similar to those used in (
5.26
), we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\Xi}g(s)|1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M^{n}_{s})]}(u)-1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M_{s})]}(u)|r_{xy}\nu^{n}(dx\,dr\,du\,ds)\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
. Here we have also used the bound in (
8.6
), the superlinearity of
\ell
and the boundedness of
M^{n},M
.
From the above convergence, (
8.7
), and the convergence observed in (
8.3
) we now see that

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Xi}g(s)\sum_{y:y\neq x}(f(y)-f(x))1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M^{n}_{s})]}(u)r_{xy}\nu^{n}(dx\,dr\,du\,ds)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Xi}g(s)\sum_{y:y\neq x}(f(y)-f(x))1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M_{s})]}(u)r_{xy}\nu^{n}(dx\,dr\,du\,ds)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Xi}g(s)\sum_{y:y\neq x}(f(y)-f(x))1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M_{s})]}(u)r_{xy}\nu(dx\,dr\,du\,ds).

Since
g
is arbitrary, recalling the definition of
\theta
, we now see that the last statement in part (b)
of Property
3.1
holds. Part (c) of Property
3.1
is immediate from the fact that
[\nu^{n}]_{134}(dx\,du\,ds)=e^{-s}ds[\theta^{n}(s)]_{1}(dx)c^{-1}du
. Indeed, this identity says that
[\nu^{n}]_{13}(dx\,du)=[\nu^{n}]_{1}(dx)c^{-1}du
and sending
n\to\infty
we have part (c) of Property
3.1
.

Finally a similar calculation as for the verification of part (b) now shows that, as
n\to\infty

c\int_{\Xi}1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M^{n}_{s})]}(u)r_{xy}\nu^{n}(dx\,dr\,du\,ds)\to c\int_{\Xi}1_{[0,Q_{xy}(M_{s})]}(u)r_{xy}\nu(dx\,dr\,du\,ds).

From (
8.4
), and since
\varsigma^{n}\to\varsigma
, we now get that part (d) of Property
3.1
is also satisfied.

Thus we have shown that
\theta\in\mathcal{S}_{\gamma,\varsigma}
and the result follows.
∎

9.
Illustrative Examples

Several examples in the discrete-time setting of self-interacting chains are presented in
[
18
, Section 8]
. These have natural continuous-time counterparts, which we do not detail here. Instead, we turn to a set of examples motivated by applications not covered in
[
18
]
. We begin with the non-interacting case, which falls within the framework of the Donsker–Varadhan theory.

Donsker–Varadhan LDP:

Suppose
Q^{0}\in\mathcal{K}(S)
. The map
Q:\mathcal{P}(S)\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{0}(S)
with
Q(\gamma)=Q^{0}
for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)
is obviously continuous and satisfies Assumptions
3.2
with
\mathcal{A}^{*}=Q^{0}
. Theorem
3.4
in this case gives the large deviation principle for the occupation measure and empirical flux of an irreducible continuous-time Markov jump process on a finite state space (cf.
[
23
,
11
,
4
]
). We now argue that the rate function (
6.4
) in this case
coincides with the Donsker-Varadhan rate function
I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}
given in the Introduction. Fix
(\gamma,\varsigma)\in\mathcal{P}(S)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{a}
. We first show that

\bar{I}(\gamma,\varsigma)\leq I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}(\gamma,\varsigma)
. Assume, without loss of generality that
I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}(\gamma,\varsigma)<\infty
.
Define, for
s\geq 0
,
\rho(s)\doteq\gamma
, and

H_{s}(x,y)=\gamma_{x}^{-1}\varsigma^{xy}1_{\gamma_{x}\neq 0}+Q^{0}_{xy}1_{\gamma_{x}=0},\;(x,y)\in A,

and
H_{s}(x,x)\doteq-\sum_{y:y\neq x}H_{s}(x,y)
, for
x\in S
.

Note that Property
6.1
(a) and (d) hold with the above definition of
(\rho_{s},H_{s})
. Also,
M(s)
defined in part (b) of Property
6.1
in this case equals
\gamma
for all
s\geq 0
. Furthermore, since
I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}(\gamma,\varsigma)<\infty
, we must have that, for
(x,y)\in A
, if
Q^{0}_{xy}=0
,
\varsigma^{xy}=0
and consequently
H_{s}(x,y)=0
as well. This verifies part (b) of Property
6.1
. Again, since
I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}(\gamma,\varsigma)<\infty
, we have that

(9.1)

\sum_{x:x\neq y}\varsigma^{yx}=\sum_{x:x\neq y}\varsigma^{xy}\mbox{ for all }y\in S.

This says that
\sum_{y\in S}\gamma_{x}H_{s}(x,y)=0
for all
y\in S
which verifies (c). Thus we have shown that

(\rho,H)\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\gamma,\varsigma}
. Also, clearly,
\tilde{J}(\rho,H)=I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}(\gamma,\varsigma)
. The inequality

\bar{I}(\gamma,\varsigma)\leq I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}(\gamma,\varsigma)
now follows.

We now argue the reverse inequality:
I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}(\gamma,\varsigma)\leq\bar{I}(\gamma,\varsigma)
.
Fix
\delta>0
and suppose
(\rho,H)\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\gamma,\varsigma}
is such that

(9.2)

\tilde{J}(\rho,H)\leq\bar{I}(\gamma,\varsigma)+\delta.

Using the convexity property in Lemma
6.3

\displaystyle\tilde{J}(\rho,H)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,\infty)}\sum_{(x,y)\in A_{0}}e^{-s}Q^{0}_{xy}\rho_{s}(x)\ell\left(\frac{\rho_{s}(x)H_{s}(x,y)}{\rho_{s}(x)Q^{0}_{xy}}\right)\,ds

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(x,y)\in A_{0}}\left(\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-s}Q^{0}_{xy}\rho_{s}(x)ds\right)\ell\left(\frac{\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-s}\rho_{s}(x)H_{s}(x,y)ds}{\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-s}Q^{0}_{xy}\rho_{s}(x)ds}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,y)\in A_{0}}Q^{0}_{xy}\gamma_{x}\ell\left(\frac{\varsigma^{xy}}{\gamma_{x}Q^{0}_{xy}}\right),

where in the last line we have used Property
6.1
(a) and (d).
Also, from Property
6.1
(c) it is easily checked that (
9.1
) holds. This shows that the last term in the above display equals
I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}(\gamma,\varsigma)
. Thus we have shown that

I^{\mbox{\tiny{DW}}}_{Q^{0}}(\gamma,\varsigma)\leq\bar{I}(\gamma,\varsigma)+\delta
. Since
\delta>0
is arbitrary, the desired reverse inequality now follows.

Affine Autochemotaxis: Self-Attraction:

Autochemotaxis is the ability of organisms to communicate through local secretions that form
chemical trails. We consider
S=\left\{{1,\cdots,d}\right\}
the set of all locations that an autochemotactic organism can visit. Suppose
Q^{0}\in\mathcal{K}(S)
. Define for
K\in(0,\infty)
, interpreted as a relative measure of the chemicals’ lifetime,
Q:\mathcal{P}(S)\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{0}(S)
as

Q_{ij}(\gamma)=Q^{0}(i,j)(\gamma(j)K+1),\hskip 10.00002pt(i,j)\in A,

with
Q_{ii}(\gamma)=-\sum_{j:j\neq i}Q_{ij}(\gamma)
. The rate matrix
Q(\gamma)
is such that the larger the empirical weight at
j
, i.e., the more the autochemotactic organism visit state
j
, the higher the jump rate into it. Note that
Q
is affine, irreducible and satisfies Assumption
3.2
with
\mathcal{A}^{*}=Q^{0}
(see discussion in Remark
3.3
). Thus Theorem
3.4
applies in this setting.

Packet Routing: Source-Destination Congestion:

Consider a network of routers represented by the finite set
S=\{1,\dots,d\}
.
A tracer packet moves through this network, being forwarded from router to router according to baseline routing rates
Q^{0}\in\mathcal{K}(S)
. We measure the congestion of a router by the fraction of time the packet has spent at that router up to the current time. The congestion in the network, in turn, modifies the routing rates of the packet so that if congestion at routers
i
and
j
is high, the rate at which the packet moves from router
i
to
j
is low. This motivates a self-interacting rate matrix

Q_{ij}(\gamma)\coloneqq\bigl(1-\alpha_{i}\gamma(i)-\beta_{j}\gamma(j)\bigr)Q^{0}_{ij},\hskip 10.00002pt(i,j)\in S\times S,\;i\neq j,

with
Q_{ii}(\gamma)=-\sum_{j:j\neq i}Q_{ij}(\gamma)
for
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)
.
Here
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\geq 0
are congestion parameters satisfying
\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}\leq 1-\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
. This model serves as a useful probe for network congestion that does not require the modeling of the whole network traffic explicitly. Since
\gamma\mapsto Q(\gamma)
is affine and for
(i,j)\in A
,
Q_{ij}(\gamma)\;\geq\;\varepsilon Q^{0}_{ij}
, we see that Assumption
3.2
is satisfied (see discussion in Remark
3.3
) and thus Theorem
3.4
applies for this model.

Chemical State Switching with Catalytic Channels:

Let
S=\{1,\dots,d\}
denote a finite set of chemical states available to a single molecule. We track the trajectory of a single molecule through the state space
S
.
At each time
t
, if the molecule is in state
i\in S
, it may switch to state
j\in S
through one of several possible
catalytic channels
labelled by
k\in S
. Physically, this corresponds to a (different) molecule in state
k
acting as a catalyst for the reaction. Formally, we write

i\xrightarrow[\;k\;]{Q^{(k)}_{ij}}j,

to mean that the transition
i\to j
occurs via the
k
-th channel at rate
Q^{(k)}_{ij}
.
The channel index
k
plays the role of a catalyst: it facilitates the transformation
i\to j
but is not itself consumed.

We use the empirical occupation mass
\gamma(k)
as a surrogate measure for abundance of chemical of type
k
available and thus the reaction in channel
k
is enhanced as
\gamma(k)
increases.
The effective rate matrix is then defined by

Q_{ij}(\gamma)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in S}\gamma(k)\,Q^{(k)}_{ij},\hskip 20.00003pt(i,j)\in S\times S,\;i\neq j,

with

Q_{ii}(\gamma)=-\sum_{i\neq j}Q_{ij}(\gamma).

Thus the trajectory of the molecule is a self-interacting jump process on
S
, whose transition structure depends on its own history via
\gamma
. We suppose that for some
\mathcal{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{K}(S)
and the associated
A_{0}
as in Assumption
3.2
,
Q^{(k)}_{ij}>0
for all
k\in S
and
(i,j)\in A_{0}
.
The assumptions of Theorem (
3.4
) are satisfied: Assumption
3.2
(b) holds trivially, Assumption
3.2
(c) holds as well, and in particular
Q(\gamma)
is irreducible for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)
. From the discussion in Remark
3.3
we see that parts (a) and (d) are satisfied as well.
Hence this family of models satisfies all the conditions of Theorem (
3.4
).
