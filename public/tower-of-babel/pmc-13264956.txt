Intimate partner violence victimisation before, during and after the COVID-19 pandemic in Manitoba, Canada: a time series analysis
Authors: Natalia Carolina Pacífico, Monica Serena Perner, Maria Paula Godoy, Okechukwu Ekuma, Douglas Brownridge, Marcelo Luis Urquia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntimate partner violence (IPV) represents a serious public health concern. Reports on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on IPV are inconsistent.AimTo describe trends in justice identified intimate partner violence (JIIPV) victimisation rates in Manitoba, Canada before, during and after the COVID-19 pandemic overall and by key sociodemographic characteristics.MethodsWe used province-wide criminal justice data of Manitoba residents aged 18–64 years. We calculated monthly incidence rates of JIIPV from March 2018 to March 2023, overall and by sex, age, marital status, immigration status, rurality and neighbourhood income. We used interrupted time series (ITS) analysis to compare observed and expected monthly JIIPV incidence rates, accounting for autocorrelation and seasonality. We distinguished the following periods: pre-COVID-19 (March 2018 to February 2020); COVID-19 period, subdivided into early (March 2020, when the lockdown began, to July 2020) and late (August 2020 to January 2022) and post-COVID-19 (February 2022, when the restrictions were lifted, to March 2023).ResultsThere were 43,745 JIIPV incidents in the study period. The incidence of JIIPV was 8.72 per 1000 person-years before COVID-19, 9.45 during the COVID-19 period and 9.62 in the post-COVID-19 period. ITS analysis shows that in the early COVID-19 period the level of JIIPV dropped 18.3% immediately with the lockdown in March 2020, followed by a rebound and peak in July 2020 (11.87 per 1000 person-years), after which rates returned to expected levels. JIIPV rates were higher across all periods among women, young adults, those without a spouse, non-immigrants, living in rural areas and in poor neighbourhoods.ConclusionsJIIPV rates were temporarily disrupted at the onset of the COVID-19 lockdown, rebounded in the subsequent 3 months and returned to pre-existing trends. Higher-risk groups showed the sharpest changes in the early COVID-19 period with a sustained upward trajectory in later periods.Keywords: Violence, COVID-19, Sex Factors, trends

Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is one of the most common forms of domestic violence and violence against women. IPV involves physical, sexual or psychological violence performed by someone with a close personal relationship including marriage, common law or dating.1 While IPV affects all groups in society, women are more likely to be victims of IPV.2 3 Women suffer more repeated incidents, greater abuse and longer impact on psychological and physical well-being than men.4 5

IPV victimisation can result in physical and mental illnesses3 and lead to severe injuries and violent death.6 7 The WHO estimates that 27% of women aged 15–49 years experienced some type of physical and/or sexual violence by an intimate partner.8 9 In Canada, almost 30% of all police-reported violence occurred between intimate partners and the majority of victims were women.10 The province of Manitoba had the second highest rates of police-reported IPV across all Canadian provinces with a rate of 6.07 victims per 1000 individuals in 2024.11

Rates of IPV vary according to marital status. Those who cohabitate or are married are less likely to experience IPV than dating partners.2 12 13 IPV may also vary according to immigration characteristics, such as length of stay, country of origin, age at immigration and home language.7 14 Overall, immigrant women in Canada report lower rates of IPV compared with Canadian-born women and IPV is lower among recent immigrant women compared with nonrecent immigrant women.13 In Canada, rates in rural areas are substantially higher, nearly twice the rate in urban areas.15

Public health responses to the COVID-19 pandemic had the unintended effect of disrupting social and protective networks, increasing time spent at home and decreasing access and availability of social services. This could have led to further isolation for those with an abusive partner at home, which may have contributed to an increased risk of IPV.16,19 Factors such as financial dependence, isolation, lack of access to supportive networks and other stressors may have contributed to an increase in domestic violence.20 21 However, the evidence remains inconclusive, with variability observed at different phases of the pandemic.22,26

Regarding domestic violence calls for police services, a study in the US in early 2020 showed heterogeneous results across cities, with an increase in some cities and a decrease in others.27 Data from Greek police showed an increase of domestic violence reported to authorities in 2021 compared with 2020.28 In England and Wales, the COVID-19 lockdown in 2020 (compared with data from previous years) led to a decline in referrals from general practitioners to domestic violence support services.4 A prospective study conducted in Germany reported unchanged perceived psychological and physical IPV victimisation during the COVID-19 pandemic.29

In Canada, different studies focused on IPV-related visits in healthcare facilities. An Ontario-based study found a slight increase in the frequency and severity of IPV-related visits in emergency departments during the first 15 months following the start of COVID-19.30 Another study, also from Ontario, found a sharp decrease after the COVID-19 lockdown in emergency department encounters for sexual assault.24 In Ottawa, a decrease was found in hospital admissions for sexual assault and domestic violence during COVID-19.31 A qualitative study of Manitoba service providers reported an increase in the frequency of IPV and the severity of IPV during the pandemic.32

Heterogeneity between results could be related to different definitions of IPV, data collection methods and sources.33 34 Different sources capture distinct aspects of IPV and are affected by different forms of reporting and measurement bias. Population-based surveys, for example, often provide higher prevalence estimates when anonymity is ensured, whereas face-to-face surveys may lead to lower disclosure rates, with underreporting associated with lower education and higher social vulnerability.35 However, self-reported IPV may also be affected by recall and reporting biases.36 Health administrative data may underestimate IPV events due to underreporting, coding limitations and a focus on the treatment of injuries.37 Criminal justice data capture serious IPV incidents that involve police intervention and prosecutions38 and may include incidents that did not require medical care. Although police officers are trained to identify and report IPV incidents, many victims may not report or conceal abuse due to fear, reprisal, shame or partner dependence.39 Despite this limitation, criminal justice represents a stable source of high-quality data to analyse time trends.6 39

It remains unclear whether and to what extent the COVID-19 pandemic influenced rates of justice identified intimate partner violence (JIIPV), whether there was a differential impact according to sex, age, marital status, immigration status, rurality or area level income,40 and how trends continued post-pandemic. Our aim was to characterise trends and changes of JIIPV victimisation before, during and after the COVID-19 pandemic and describe time trends by sex, age, marital status, immigration status, area of residence and area level income in Manitoba, Canada.

Methods

Study design and study setting

We conducted an ecological study using interrupted time-series analysis based on retrospective population-based criminal justice and healthcare data from Manitoba, a province located in central Canada, with more than 1.3 million inhabitants.41 Nearly one in five residents (19.7%) are first-generation immigrants, and 18.1% identify as Indigenous (First Nation, Métis or Inuit).42 Data were accessed at the Manitoba Population Research Data Repository, which holds de-identified health, social and justice data from the Government of Manitoba from over 99% of the Manitobans linked through a scrambled personal identifier.43

Study population

The study population consisted of all persons aged 18–64 years living in Manitoba between March 2018 and March 2023. The start of the follow-up included those aged 18–64 years in March 2018 or who turned 18 years between March 2018 and March 2023. The end of the follow-up was marked by turning 65 years, end of universal health coverage (eg, moving out of province), death or the end of the study period.

Justice identified intimate partner violence

The study outcome was monthly incidence rates of JIIPV. Incidents of JIIPV were identified by cross-referencing the Prosecutions Information and Scheduling Management (PRISM) System, which contains information on the date of the incident, the accused and the victim, and the charges laid by the police, and the Criminal Courts Automated Information Network that summarise the outcomes of the court process (dispositions). Both databases contain a flag that identifies domestic violence offences based on the Manitoba Domestic Violence and Stalking Act.44 To remove cases of domestic violence that were not between intimate partners, we excluded incidents in which the accused was a parent, sibling or child of the victim. The date of the JIIPV incident was reported by provincial police services, including rural and remote areas. Rates were calculated as cases per 1000 person-years, with the number of JIIPV incidents in the numerator and person-time in the denominators, based on start/end day of the provincial universal healthcare coverage.

COVID-19 periods

We distinguished the following periods, based on the timing of the public health measures and inspection of the data: pre-COVID-19 (March 2018 to February 2020); COVID-19 period, subdivided into early (March 2020, when the lockdown began, to July 2020) and late (August 2020 to January 2022), and post-COVID-19 (February 2022, when provincial restrictions were lifted, to March 2023). Intervention points were based on the start and end dates of the restrictions and social distancing.45 46 The start of the COVID-19 period was the time of closure of non-critical businesses, meeting restrictions,47 48 with March 2020 as the first intervention point. After assessing the observed data, a second intervention point was set on August 2020, to let the model differentiate between immediate (early) effects of COVID-19 lockdown from March to July 2020, and subsequent or gradual easing of restriction effects from August 2020 onwards.49 50 February 2022, the third intervention point, marked the start of the post COVID-19 period, when all remaining restriction measures were lifted45 until the end of the study period in March 2023 (last month of available data).

Covariates

We included information on sociodemographic characteristics to stratify the analysis. We included sex (categorised as women or men, since the administrative databases do not account for gender) and the marital status of the victim. Marital status was classified as ‘with spouse’ (if coded as married or in common law partnership) and ‘without spouse’ otherwise, obtained through the Manitoba Health Insurance Registry (MHIR). Immigration status was obtained from the Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada Permanent Resident database and the MHIR. To describe the population, we also obtained age information and place of residence (urban or rural) from the MHIR. Remote areas were classified as rural residence. Neighbourhood income was obtained from Canadian censuses aggregated at the dissemination area-level and categorised in quintiles.

Statistical analysis

We first calculated monthly rates of JIIPV, overall and stratified by the key covariates. Reporting weekly or daily counts and rates was not possible due to small cell counts within strata. These data were analysed as an ITS using the three intervention points in segmented regression analyses.51,55

We considered generalised linear, nonparametric and parametric additive models to analyse the monthly incidence rates of JIIPV. Both autocorrelation (the similarity between observations of a random variable at different points in time) and seasonality (the systematic pattern of a time series that repeats regularly over a given period) were checked. We assessed autocorrelation by examining the plots of residuals and the partial autocorrelation function using the software SAS with the procedures AUTOREG and ARIMA. We considered two models (with and without inclusion of seasonality variables). There was evidence of autocorrelation in models that did not include seasonality variables but when seasonality variables were included the autocorrelation disappeared.51 However, to account for residual autocorrelation we used a generalised estimation equation with autoregressive correlational structure as our final model of choice. This model used the negative binomial distribution with the log link function to model the monthly count of JIIPV. The logarithm of person-years was included as an offset to account for partial residence during a given month, due to start or end of health coverage, turning 65 years or death. Months were included as dummy variables to account for seasonality and non-linearity.

To understand the effects of the intervention points (changes between periods), we used the models’ parameters to estimate the increase or decrease in the level of JIIPV (ie, step changes) and the trends within periods (ie, slopes). We also estimated predicted and expected values. Predicted values are those with intervention effects and the expected (counterfactuals) are those without intervention effects (ie, those expected under the assumption that COVID-19 did not occur and based on pre-COVID-19 trends). Using the segmented regression methods for ITS53 and ESTIMATE statements in SAS, we obtained both the predicted and the expected rates. We estimated monthly rate ratios (RR) with 95% CIs between the predicted and the expected counterfactuals, starting on the first intervention point (March 2020) until the end of the study period.

We plotted the observed monthly rates, the predicted rates and the expected rates with 95% CIs. Forest plots displaying the monthly RRs with 95% CI between the predicted and expected incidence rates were restricted to the COVID-19 and post-COVID-19 periods. All analyses were performed overall and stratified by the covariates.

We used STATA V.16.1 and SAS V.9.4 for data analysis and GraphPad Prism V.9 for graphing.

Overall trends

The estimated rate of JIIPV at the start of the study period in March 2018 was 7.9 per 1000 person years (exp(−4.8393)*1000) (online supplemental table 2). In the pre-COVID period, JIIPV exhibited a slight upward trend (0.0029; p-value 0.03). The level of JIIPV dropped 18.3% immediately with the lockdown in March 2020 (exp(−0.2026)−1; p-value<0.003) (RR: 0.89; 95% CI 0.79 to 0.99), followed by a slope increase between the pre-COVID-19 period and the early COVID-19 lockdown (March to July 2020) (0.0826; p<0.001), surpassing the expected rates and peaking in June and July 2020 (figure 1A,B). In the late COVID-19 period predicted rates were not different from their counterfactuals (expected rates). In the post-COVID-19 period (February 2022 to March 2023), the predicted rates did not differ from the expected rates either (RRs in the range 1.00–0.94; figure 1 and online supplemental table 3).

Trends by sex

Women’s rates of JIIPV were markedly higher than those for men at the beginning of the study period in March 2018 (12.8 and 3.3 per 1000 person years for women and men, respectively) and remained higher throughout the three periods (figure 2). For both sexes, rates follow a similar trend to that described for the overall population. The drop in the mean JIIPV rate at the beginning of COVID-19 (March 2020) was more pronounced among men (exp(−0.3075)−1=−26.4%, p-value<0.01) (RR: 0.81; 95% CI 0.66 to 0.98) than for women (exp(−0.1942)−1=−17.6%, p=0.01) (RR: 0.89; 95% CI 0.82 to 0.98), followed by an immediate rebound from March to July 2020, when rates peaked among both men (RR: 1.27; 95% CI 1.15 to 1.41) and women (RR: 1.38; 95% CI 1.31 to 1.46) (online supplemental tables 2 and 3). In August 2020, the predicted rates of JIIPV dropped again for both sexes and remained not different from the expected rates for the remainder of the COVID-19 period and during the post-COVID-19 period.

Trends by marital status

The rates of JIIPV at the beginning of the study period in March 2018 were six times higher for those without a spouse (10.7 vs 1.7 per 1000 person years for those with a spouse, respectively) (figure 3). In general, those without spouse showed a similar trend to that described for the cohort overall (figure 1). The immediate drop in the JIIPV rate at the beginning of the lockdown in March 2020 was stronger among those with spouses (exp(−0.3161)−1=−27.1%) than among those without spouse (exp(−0.2215)−1=−19.9%).

The rebound in March and peaking in July 2020 was only statistically significant for those without a spouse (RR: 1.27; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.36). From August 2020 to February 2022, predicted rates remained within the expected among both groups, although they were lower among those with spouse throughout the whole subperiod and the RRs were of borderline statistical significance due to a smaller sample size. At the beginning of the post-COVID-19 period, the level dropped among those with spouse (exp(−0.2924)−1=−25.3%) and the rates remained lower than the expected from March to June 2022 (RRs ranging from 0.74–0.76; figure 3 and online supplemental table 3) but there were no changes among those without spouse.

Trends by immigration status

In March 2018, rates of JIIPV were five times higher for non-immigrants (exp (−4.6204)=9.8 per 1000 person years) than for immigrants (exp (−6.3048)=1.8 per 1000 person years) (figure 4 and online supplemental table 2). Pre-COVID-19 trends were positive among non-immigrants but not among immigrants. Non-immigrants had a similar trend to that described for the overall population (figures1 4). Both groups experienced a drop in March 2020 (RR: 0.88; 95% CI 0.79 to 0.98 among non-immigrants and 0.86; 95% CI 0.75 to 0.97 among immigrants), followed by increasing trends from March to July 2020, which were statistically significant in both groups (online supplemental table 2). Interestingly, despite a smaller population, immigrants’ JIIPV rates increased from August 2020 to the end of the COVID-19 period and continued their upward trends in the post-COVID-19 period, with RRs ranging between 1.17 and 1.61 (figure 4 and online supplemental table 3).

Trends by age groups

JIIPV rates differed by age groups in March 2018, being highest in the 25–34 age group and lowest in the 55–64 age group (exp(−4.6105)=13.7 and (exp(−6.2993)=1.8 per 1000 person years, respectively) (onlinesupplemental figures 1 2). JIIPV rates were increasing in the pre-COVID-19 period in the youngest age group (18–24 years) (exp(0.0095)−1=1.0%) but not in the other age groups. A statistically significant drop in March 2020 was observed across all age groups, with the largest level change in the 35–44 age group (exp(−0.393)−1=32.5%; RR: 0.76; 95% CI 0.66 to 0.87) (online supplemental table 4). Over the whole study period JIIPV rates remained relatively stable. The upward trend in the 18–24 year age group continued during the COVID-19 and the post-COVID-19 periods, although it did not surpass the trends expected in the absence of COVID-19.

Trends by area of residence

Urban residents showed lower JIIPV rates than those living in rural areas in the pre-COVID-19 period (6.83 vs 11.96 per 1000 person-years, respectively) (table 1). In March 2020 a significant drop in JIIPV rates was observed in the urban stratum (exp(−0.2892)−1=−25.1%) (online supplemental figure 3a and online supplemental table 2) whereas the change in rural areas was not statistically significant. During the early COVID-19 period (March–July 2020), rates increased in both rural and urban areas, and regressed to the pre-COVID-19 trends in the late COVID-19 period and remained within the expected.

Trends by area level income

JIIPV rates showed a clear socioeconomic gradient, with the highest rates in the lowest income quintile and rates that declined steadily in the higher income quintiles throughout the study period; ranging from 20.17 to 21.74 per 1000 person-years to 3.08–3.56 per 1000 person-years (table 1, onlinesupplemental figures 4 5). The peak in the early COVID-19 period was more noticeable in the poorest neighbourhoods. There was no departure from the expected trends in the late and post-COVID-19 periods.

Discussion

Main findings

This study contributes to the growing body of evidence examining the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and its associated restrictions on IPV, providing novel insights into post-pandemic trends. Our findings show that JIIPV rates dropped at the beginning of the COVID-19 period in March 2020, rebounded with a peak in July and regressed to the pre-COVID-19 trends thereafter. Monthly rates remained within the expected rates during the late COVID-19 period and throughout the post-COVID-19 period.

Overall trends

The COVID-19 pandemic was first declared in March 2020, disrupting daily life worldwide.56 In March and April 2020, JIIPV incidence rates (overall and stratified) initially declined in March 2020. It is possible that this decrease may either reflect a reduction in interpersonal contacts or a reduction in help-seeking behaviours out of fear of COVID-19 infection early in the pandemic, a pattern also seen in other service access points, including cancer screening and emergency care.31 57 58 A reduction in help-seeking behaviour is supported by police call data from Winnipeg (Manitoba’s largest city) which indicate a marked decline in calls for assaults and sexual assaults during the early months of the pandemic.59

Different organisations highlighted a possible increase in IPV during the COVID-19 lockdown period.60,62 This aligns with our finding of a sharp increase in the JIIPV rates beginning in May and peaking in July 2020. This surge may be related to the increased isolation and associated growth in the opportunities for manipulation, control, and violence by the perpetrator, and the adaptation of services provision for IPV.3263,65 The peak observed in July 2020 also follows the seasonal pattern in the incidence of JIIPV rates observed throughout the study period. IPV rates tend to increase during warmer months, potentially due to an increase in social interaction, alcohol consumption and heat-related physiological stress.66,68 During the pandemic, these seasonal factors may have converged with additional COVID-19 related stressors, including increased substance use69 70 and financial strain.71

For the remainder of the study period, JIIPV rates gradually returned to pre-COVID-19 trends, showing an increasing trajectory within expected levels across all sociodemographic groups analysed.3472,74 Overall, JIIPV rates increased slightly between pre-pandemic and post-pandemic periods, from 8.72 to 9.62 per 1000 person years. This represents a small absolute increase over time and is consistent with broader national trends indicating rising rates of police-reported family and IPV in Canada from 2014 to 2022.9

Subgroups trends

Overall, JIIPV rates were higher across all periods among women, those without a spouse, non-immigrants, younger age groups (25–34 years old), residents of rural areas and of poorer neighbourhoods. These subgroups not only exhibited higher baseline rates of IPV but also the sharpest increases during the early COVID-19 period. This pattern suggests that pre-existing social and economic inequalities712 75,77 may have amplified the impact of pandemic-related stressors reinforcing the structural nature of IPV.

With our data, it is not possible to tease apart whether the incidence peaks among these subgroups in the early COVID-19 period reflects higher victimisation, increased reporting of victimisation12 75 or both. Broader social changes may have also played a role in reporting trends, such as increased awareness of gender-based violence and social movements that may have reduced stigma and encouraged disclosure.78 Reduced labour market participation among women, particularly in lower-income groups71 may have also contributed to the upward trend.

Increased isolation and related opportunities for perpetrator control and violence have been used as potential explanations for increased violence during the lockdown among cohabiting couples.16 However, our data do not show increases among those with a spouse. Moreover, these arguments may not apply to those dating or not living together as COVID-19 restrictions reduced in-person contact between individuals living in different households. Individuals in dating relationships (without spouse) experienced a significant drop at the beginning of the lockdown, which is consistent with the inability to form new relationships due to the social restrictions and fear of contracting the virus.79 However, it is not clear why they, unlike those with a spouse, experienced a large peak shortly thereafter. Since our measure of marital status does not necessarily reflect cohabitation, as some individuals without a spouse may have been cohabiting with a partner, the true impact of the lockdown on dating violence is likely underestimated in our study.

Immigrants, despite having lower rates than other subgroups across the study period, had a non-statistically significant upward trend in the rates of JIIPV post-pandemic, indicating a need to continue to monitor JIIPV rates going forward. Immigrants experienced higher rates of unemployment, poverty and lack of social networks during COVID-19, social determinants which may increase the risk of future JIIPV.26 80 At the same time, lower observed rates among immigrants may partially reflect underreporting, as structural and cultural barriers may reduce help-seeking and reporting to police.7

Higher JIIPV rates observed in rural areas and a clear socioeconomic gradient across neighbourhood income levels are consistent with prior evidence and reflect structural barriers such as greater social isolation, increased exposure to stressors and limited access to support services.7 15 40 77 81

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, considering the COVID-19 pandemic is a recent phenomenon, this study spans 5 years, including an examination of the post-lockdown period trends. Second, the study design is appropriate to detect departures from the historical trends due to COVID-19. Third, the linkage of several different administrative sources at the provincial level allowed for a comprehensive examination of JIIPV at the population-level. Fourth, the inclusion of sociodemographic information enabled the exploration of heterogeneity in IPV patterns across population subgroups.

This study has some limitations. JIIPV was based on criminal justice records, which may not capture less severe incidents that did not require police intervention. There may be underreporting of IPV incidents among those that did not access the justice system or were not able or willing to call the police for help. Therefore, our measure of JIIPV should not be interpreted as reflecting any or all IPV. Our measure of marital status overestimates single persons because a new health card registration number is assigned to individuals turning 18 years old, many of whom do not update their cards when entering a marital union. Therefore, some married or in common-law relationships may be misclassified as without spouse, potentially diluting differences between these two groups. We could not account for gender diversity, since the administrative data used only captures sex at birth. Finally, we could not account for history of IPV, which may be relevant to distinguish incident IPV and revictimisation. Future studies may delve deeper into these dimensions.

Conclusion

JIIPV rates decreased at the onset of COVID-19 lockdown period for all sociodemographic groups followed by a rebound, after which trends returned to the pre-pandemic upward trajectory. This pattern indicates a transient rather than sustained impact of the pandemic. Ongoing monitoring of rates of JIIPV and other forms of IPV is needed to assess the longer-term trends of the pandemic across sociodemographic groups.
