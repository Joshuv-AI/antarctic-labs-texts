Bare-ancilla fault-tolerant syndrome extraction: General extensions of distance-three codes and structural criteria for graph codes
Harsh Gupta, Mainak Bhattacharyya, Ritik Jain, Ankur Raina

Abstract
The reliability of quantum computation critically depends on the performance of quantum error-correcting codes (QECCs). Performance of QECCs can be severely degraded by hook errors, which effectively reduce the code distance. We develop a systematic framework for bare-ancilla syndrome extraction in [ [ n , k , 3 ] ] [[n,k,3]] stabilizer codes. It provides a criterion for a specific single ancilla fault model and jointly searches for suitable stabilizer generators and data-ancilla interaction orders to correct hook errors. We call such stabilizer generator sets bare-ancilla codes (BACs). We also present two constructions that enlarge the set of valid BACs while preserving minimum distance, either by keeping the number of logical qubits fixed or by increasing it. Under the correlated-data error model, 3177 3177 [ [ 7 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[7,1,3]] graph codes are found to be BACs out of 3379 3379 and all 335415 335415 [ [ 8 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[8,1,3]] graph codes are BACs. We tested 500 500 [ [ 7 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[7,1,3]] and 1000 1000 [ [ 8 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[8,1,3]] graph codes in the presence of anisotropic and depolarizing noise. The number of data-ancilla interactions is an important predictor of the pseudo-threshold of these graph codes. In the analyzed codes, the BAC, which works with the bare ancilla method, usually performs well or better than the flag method, particularly for depolarizing noise. Notably, we report new bare ancilla codes, namely [ [ 6 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[6,1,3]] and [ [ 7 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[7,1,3]] with improved code rate compared to the bare code used in the work of Muyuan Li et al. and Maheshwari et al. respectively.

Introduction

The evolution of hardware architectures for quantum computing has been rapid in recent years to support a reliable fault-tolerant framework for the quantum protocols.
Manipulation of the logical information encoded on the physical qubits should yield high fidelity, indicating highly error-suppressed outcomes.
As a result, the development of a robust Quantum Error Correcting Code (QECC) is fundamental in the advancement of fault-tolerant (FT) quantum computing.
Implementing QECCs in practical scenarios poses significant challenges, particularly due to physical errors that affect the error detection process.
During a computation, fault tolerance can be achieved asymptotically if the physical error rate is below a certain threshold
[

,
36

Crucially, the syndrome extraction process must also be implemented in an FT manner to prevent errors from propagating into the data qubits.

50

This code only detects a Pauli error as its minimum distance is two.
Any limited error-correcting capacity of a QECC imposes significant resource overhead required for FT quantum computation.
Several techniques have been proposed for extracting FT syndrome.

II
Background and Preliminaries

QECCs form the foundation for protecting quantum information against decoherence and operational errors.
In this section, we shall briefly discuss some basic concepts necessary to understand the rest of the paper.

II.1
CSS and non-CSS codes

In 1995, Shor introduced the first scheme capable of correcting arbitrary single-qubit errors using nine physical qubits, achieving a code rate of
1/9

[
44
]
.
Steane subsequently introduced a seven-qubit code with a higher code rate of
1/7

[
46
]
.
Laflamme
et al.
later established the five-qubit “perfect” code, which saturates the quantum Hamming bound and achieves the minimum block size required to protect a single logical qubit from any single-qubit error
[
28
]
.
While these codes differ in code length, they share the same minimum distance and therefore correct arbitrary single-qubit errors.
These examples are described uniformly by the stabilizer formalism
[
21
]
.

An
[[n,k,d]]
stabilizer code is the joint
+1
eigenspace of
n-k
independent mutually commuting Pauli operators, with
-I
excluded from the group generated by those operators.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
be the
n
-qubit Pauli group up to an overall phase, and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}
be the corresponding stabilizer group.
A CSS code has a generating set whose Pauli operators are purely
X
-type or purely

Z
-type
[
8
]
.
If
C_{X}=\ker H_{X}
and
C_{Z}=\ker H_{Z}
, the CSS commutation condition is
C_{Z}^{\perp}\subseteq C_{X}
, equivalently
C_{X}^{\perp}\subseteq C_{Z}
.
A non-CSS stabilizer code, the stabilizer set must have some generator with mixed
X
,
Y
, and
Z
Pauli operators
[
28
,
30
,
45
]
.
For a parity check matrix
H=[H_{X}\mid H_{Z}]
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, mutual commutativity of their corresponding stabilizer generators is equivalent to

\displaystyle H_{Z}H_{X}^{T}+\ H_{X}H_{Z}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\pmod{2}.

However, satisfying the stabilizer commutation relations alone is insufficient for achieving fault tolerance during syndrome extraction.
In practical implementations, faults on ancillary qubits may propagate via two-qubit interactions, generating correlated errors on the data qubits, commonly known as hook errors.

II.2
Hook Errors in QEC

Syndrome measurements in quantum error correction are typically performed by coupling ancilla qubits to several data qubits through two-qubit controlled gates such as
CNOT
or
CZ
gates
[
36
]
.
Pauli errors on the ancilla qubit can propagate through these interactions, generating errors on the data qubits.
They are called hook errors, since a single ancilla fault can effectively hook onto multiple qubits instead of remaining confined to one
[
17
]
.
Consequently, a fault on the ancilla qubit can propagate through later two-qubit gates between data-ancilla qubits and create a correlated error on two or more data qubits.
Such propagation depends on the circuit, the fault location, and the type of Pauli error that occurs on the ancilla.
If untreated, hook errors reduce the effective code distance, thereby significantly limiting the code’s error-correcting capability.
Hence, FT syndrome extraction requires careful circuit design that mitigates hook errors by ensuring that the resulting errors remain correctable.
This requirement motivates the FT syndrome-extraction schemes reviewed next.

II.3
Fault-tolerant syndrome-extraction schemes

Over the past decade, major advances have been made in developing fault-tolerant schemes for syndrome measurement.
Fujiwara showed that suitable redundant stabilizer measurements can protect against faults in the extracted syndrome
[
20
]
.
Building on this idea, Ashikhmin, Lai, and Brun subsequently used classical coding of syndrome bits to reduce repeated-measurement overhead and improve robustness against syndrome measurement errors
[
4
]
.
Crow
et al.
developed a coherent, measurement-free error-correction scheme and analyzed its threshold behaviour
[
15
]
.
Related redundant-syndrome constructions were later developed in Refs.
[
40
,
23
]
.
These methods primarily protect the classical syndrome record; they do not, by themselves, solve the hook-error problem in data qubits studied here.

Yoder and Kim designed syndrome-extraction circuits for triangle codes that remain fault-tolerant under circuit-level noise, at the cost of additional qubits
[
53
]
.
Chao and Reichardt later addressed this issue by introducing a more resource-efficient scheme that requires only two additional qubits, an ancilla qubit, and a flag qubit
[
11
]
.
This approach is applicable to all stabilizer codes
[
10
,
12
]
.
Subsequent work produced several variants of the flag-qubit method, optimizing circuit depth by dynamically and adaptively selecting stabilizer measurements and introducing tools to simulate and optimize the flag approach
[
16
,
6
,
3
,
49
,
39
]
.
Lao and Almudever extended this approach by introducing a geometrical interaction mapping
[
29
]
.
They combine fault-tolerant flagging with quantum circuit mapping to create an efficient flag-bridge implementation of FT quantum error correction on near-term devices.

A further reduction in ancillary-qubit overhead was studied by Muyuan Li
et al.

They presented a
[[7,1,3]]
non-CSS stabilizer code for which a suitable ordering of the data-ancilla interactions can correct hook errors caused by a single fault in the ancilla qubit
[
31
]
.
They evaluated the code under anisotropic and depolarizing noise models.
Under the anisotropic model considered in Ref.
[
31
]
, one bare ancilla per stabilizer measurement was sufficient to satisfy their fault-tolerance condition.
Under the standard two-qubit depolarizing model, however, additional flag qubits were required to detect hook errors.
Related ordering-based bare-ancilla circuits were subsequently developed for Bacon-Shor and compass codes
[
32
,
25
]
.
Maheshwari
et al.
analyzed a
[[8,1,3]]
non-CSS code and reported its pseudo-threshold behavior under depolarizing and anisotropic noise models using bare-ancilla syndrome extraction
[
33
]
.

The works cited above analyze selected codes or code families.
They do not provide a general procedure for searching for suitable stabilizer generators, which would make the code fault-tolerant in the event of a fault in the ancilla qubit.
To address this gap, the next section formulates the conditions for BAC and an exact generator-and-ordering search for a given stabilizer set.

III
Bare-ancilla syndrome extraction conditions and code search

This section states the conditions under which a given
[[n,k,3]]
stabilizer code and its syndrome-extraction circuit form a valid BAC in the presence of a specified fault model.
For a given stabilizer set, the conditions specify how to search for suitable generators for BAC.
For each selected generator, it also searches every allowed data-ancilla interaction order.
Throughout this work, a BAC is specified by the triplet
(\mathcal{C},\Pi,\mathcal{M})
in which
\mathcal{C}
is the underlying stabilizer code, defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n-k}\},

Further,
\Pi
is the set of permutations of the stabilizer operators, defined as:

\displaystyle\Pi

\displaystyle=\{\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n-k}\},

where each permutation
\pi_{a}
is the data-ancilla interaction order used in measuring the stabilizer generator
g_{a}
.
We also use
\mathcal{M}
to denote a predefined single-fault model.
When
\mathcal{M}
is fixed by context, we suppress it from the notation and refer to
(\mathcal{C},\Pi)
as a BAC under
\mathcal{M}
.
Thus, any BAC never refers to the code
\mathcal{C}
alone.

Any permutation of the measured stabilizer does not change the codespace.
However, it does change the hook errors propagated through the two-qubit gates present in the stabilizer measurement circuit, owing to the errors on the ancilla.
We therefore search for valid generators and interaction orders such that every hook error can be attributed to a unique syndrome.
However, if the syndrome is the same across a set of hook errors, the errors are stabilizer-equivalent.

III.1
Exact bare-ancilla fault-tolerance condition

First, we define the condition that a given stabilizer code must satisfy to be FT under the chosen fault model during syndrome measurement.
Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
with binary representations
(\mathbf{p}_{X}\mid\mathbf{p}_{Z})
and
(\mathbf{q}_{X}\mid\mathbf{q}_{Z})

\omega(P,Q)=\mathbf{p}_{X}^{T}\mathbf{q}_{Z}+\mathbf{p}_{Z}^{T}\mathbf{q}_{X}\pmod{2}.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
[[n,k,3]]

\mathcal{S}
.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be generated by

\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n-k}\}

The Normalizer of
\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{S}):=\left\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}:\omega(P,g)=0\text{ for every }g\in\mathcal{S}\right\}.

The syndrome of
E\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
for a given generating set is

s_{\mathcal{S}}(E)=\bigl(\omega(E,g_{1}),\ldots,\omega(E,g_{n-k})\bigr)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n-k}.

For a BAC
(\mathcal{C},\Pi,\mathcal{M})
, we now define the set of all hook errors resulting from any error in the ancilla, during the stabilizer measurement round as
\mathcal{F}(\Pi,\mathcal{M})
.
We can now partition
\mathcal{F}(\Pi,\mathcal{M})
into two disjoint sets
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.
We define
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{F}
, where for any error
E\in\mathcal{A}
there exists
F\in\mathcal{A}
such that
E^{\dagger}F\in\mathcal{S}
.
On the other hand
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{A}
.
We introduce the notation
\operatorname{Valid}_{\mathcal{S}}(.)
to validate the nature of such subsets of
\mathcal{F}
.
For instance the two cases can be summarized into
\operatorname{Valid}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{A})=0
and
\operatorname{Valid}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{B})=1
if every element in
\mathcal{B}
have unique syndrome.
The FT condition of any BAC can be defined as follows:

Definition 1
.

A given BAC
(\mathcal{C},\Pi,\mathcal{M})
is FT if for
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{F}
, there exist unique syndromes for all the hook errors from the subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{F}\implies\operatorname{Valid}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{B})=1

Definition
1

\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{F}:\operatorname{Valid}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{A})=0
unique syndrome attribution is not necessary.
This conditioning of FT BAC follows straightforwardly from the fact that hook errors differ by any stabilizer that affects the codespace in the same way.
Therefore, all such errors can have degenerate syndromes.
However, when any two errors differ by an operator from
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathcal{S}
, their action is not equivalent over the codespace, and therefore they cannot be attributed to the same syndrome.

III.2.1
Un-correlated errors: Affecting only ancilla qubit

The first error class we consider is a very simplified model

We denote this fault model using
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{a}}

We previously discussed that there should be a set

Consider the following lemma:

Lemma 2

For any stabilizer generator

\rho_{g}(2)=P_{a_{2}}\cdots P_{a_{w_{g}}}=gP_{a_{1}}\sim_{\mathcal{S}}P_{a_{1}},

VII.1
Simulation protocol and decoder

The stabilizer circuits are simulated using the CHP (CNOT-Hadamard-Phase) framework
[
1
]
.
Each Monte Carlo trial contains a noisy error-correction block followed by a final ideal error-correction block.
If the initial syndrome measurement returns a nonzero syndrome, all stabilizers are measured again, and a correction is applied using a decoder.
The final correction identifies whether the propagated error belongs to the stabilizer group or to a nontrivial logical coset.
The decoder first assigns the syndromes to the single-qubit errors.
It next assigns the syndromes for hook errors stored for faults in the syndrome-extraction circuit.
Any remaining syndrome is passed to the Look-Up-Table(LUT) decoder.
A trial is counted as a logical failure when the final error commutes with every stabilizer generator but belongs to a nontrivial logical coset.
For total trials
N_{\mathrm{T}}
,

P_{\mathrm{L}}(p)=\frac{N_{\mathrm{L}}(p)}{N_{\mathrm{T}}},

where
N_{\mathrm{L}}(p)
is the number of logical failures.

Two circuit-level noise models are used.
Under depolarizing noise, a single-qubit gate is followed by
X
,
Y
, or
Z
with probability
p_{s}/3
each.
A two-qubit gate is followed by one of the
15
nonidentity two-qubit Pauli operators with probability
p_{t}/15
each.
A measurement outcome is followed by a bit flip error with probability
p_{m}
, while preparation of
\lvert 0\rangle
or
\lvert+\rangle
fails with probability
p_{p}
.

The anisotropic model represents gate-aligned faults associated with under- or over-rotation in trapped-ion gates
[
37
,
48
]
.
A controlled-
P
interaction is followed by
Z\otimes P
with probability
p_{t}
.
The interaction qubits also undergo single-qubit depolarizing noise.
For the simulation, the parameters are defined as
p=p_{t}
, with
p_{s}=p_{m}=p_{p}=4p/15
.
The same rates apply to the bare and flag circuits.

For each implementation,
20
logarithmically spaced values in
10^{-4}\leq p\leq 10^{-2}
are simulated with
N_{\mathrm{T}}=10^{6}
trials per point.
The pseudo-threshold is defined by

P_{\mathrm{L}}(p_{\mathrm{th}})=p_{\mathrm{th}},

(28)

and is obtained by log-log interpolation between the two adjacent points that bracket the crossing.
From the complete bare-code datasets, we selected five hundred
[[7,1,3]]
records and one thousand
[[8,1,3]]

The samples included valid BAC records by the canonical-pivot, depth-one generators

VIII
Conclusion

This work developed a general framework for finding valid BAC from
[[n,k,3]]
stabilizer codes.
Starting with the condition required for hook errors, we gave a search procedure that jointly selects the stabilizer generators and their data-ancilla interaction orders.

We also established two methods for constructing larger BACs from valid BAC seeds.
The pendent extension adds physical qubits while preserving the number of logical qubits and minimum distance
i.e

d=3
in our case.
Blockwise composition combines independent code blocks, thereby increasing the number of logical qubits.
Together, these constructions prove the existence of BACs for larger block lengths under the stated assumptions of the fault model.
Although they do not claim the optimal qubit required to encode that global
k
logical information.
For the correlated ancilla-data fault model,
94.02\%
of the enumerated
[[7,1,3]]
cases and all enumerated eight-qubit cases have a valid BAC.
Many
[[7,1,3]]
codes require noncanonical stabilizer generators, showing that the stabilizer generators and interaction order are essential parts for the BACs.
In the numerical study, the interaction count
Q
is the strongest predictor of the pseudo-threshold.
Circuits with smaller
Q
generally perform better, and the selected bare codes usually match or outperform the corresponding flag method using the bare method, with the clearest advantage in the presence of depolarizing noise.
The code and data are publicly available in our

GitHub repository
.

The observed power-law relation between the pseudo-threshold and
Q
is empirical and depends on the code family, noise model, and decoder.
The count
Q
also does not describe the Pauli types or locations of the interactions.
A location-resolved analysis of the faults and analysis on larger codes would help determine which circuit features control logical performance beyond the total interaction count.
