TL-HDMR: a transfer learning framework for advancing equitable causal inference reveals metabolic signatures of stroke across multiple ancestries
Authors: Lei Hou, Xiao-Hua Zhou, Fuzhong Xue, Hao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe limited genetic diversity in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) poses a significant challenge to the generalizability and equity of biomedical discoveries. Most causal inferences, particularly from high-dimensional phenomes (e.g. metabolomics), are primarily based on European populations, and their applicability to other ancestries remains uncertain. Traditional multivariable Mendelian randomization (MVMR) methods further struggle in high-dimensional and correlated settings due to collinearity and model instability. To bridge this gap, we present a two-step transfer learning framework for high-dimensional MR (TL-HDMR), designed to enhance causal exposure detection in understudied populations. Our approach leverages the Minimax Concave Penalty for asymptotically unbiased estimation amidst exposure correlations. Crucially, we introduce two novel pre-transfer procedures—HDMR.TSD for sourcing beneficial data and HDMR.PRESSO for filtering pleiotropic instruments—to ensure robust knowledge transfer. Extensive simulations demonstrated TL-HDMR’s superior performance in ROC curves and mean absolute error over alternative methods. When applied to identify causal metabolites for stroke across multi-ancestry cohorts (European, East Asian, South Asian, and African), TL-HDMR successfully pinpointed both shared and ethnic-specific causal biomarkers, showcasing its unique capability for equitable causal inference. This work provides a powerful statistical tool that not only addresses critical methodological challenges but also promotes inclusivity and fairness in human health research.

Introduction

Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as a powerful epidemiological tool for assessing putative causal relationships between modifiable exposures and health outcomes. By using genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs), MR can mitigate confounding and reverse causation, key limitations of observational studies [1]. The advent of high-throughput technologies has enabled the large-scale measurement of numerous potential exposures—such as proteins, metabolites, and gene expression levels—ushering in the era of MR with omics exposures [1–3]. This approach holds immense promise for systematically identifying causal mediators in disease pathways and proposing novel therapeutic targets.

However, this promise is tempered by significant methodological challenges. The very nature of omics data introduces a high degree of correlation among exposures, creating a complex network of interrelated variables. This correlation significantly complicates the selection of valid instruments, as genetic variants are frequently associated with multiple correlated exposures—a situation that can result in fewer IVs than the number of high-dimensional exposures. Moreover, horizontal pleiotropy, wherein genetic variants influence the outcome through biological pathways other than the exposure of interest, further challenges the validity of causal inference. Traditional multivariable Mendelian randomization (MVMR) methods—such as multivariable inverse-variance weighted (MVIVW) [4], MVMR-lasso [5], genome-wide MR analysis under pervasive pleiotropy (GRAPPLE) [6], multivariable MR constrained maximum likelihood (MVMRcML) [7], spectral-regularized IVW (SRIVW) [8], and MR using bias-corrected estimating equations (MRBEE) [9]—are designed to handle multiple exposures. However, they often face challenges in high-dimensional, correlated exposure settings, where issues such as collinearity and unstable model fitting arise. To address these limitations, Zuber et al. developed MR-BMA [10], a Bayesian method scalable to high-throughput experimental dimensions that facilitates risk factor selection from a large set of candidates. Similarly, Cho et al. proposed MR-TRYX [11], which employs LASSO regression to identify traits with pleiotropic effects on the outcome, but the LASSO penalty introduces biased causal effect estimates, a well-known limitation of this approach. In this paper, we refer to this class of approaches as high-dimensional MR (HDMR) methods.

A separate yet equally critical challenge in MR is the pervasive Eurocentric bias in genome-wide association studies (GWAS). The majority of large-scale GWAS cohorts are of European ancestry [12], meaning that genetic instruments and exposure-outcome estimates are primarily derived from this population. In contrast, non-European populations—such as Asian and African groups—still have limited sample sizes and lower genome-wide coverage, resulting in reduced true discovery rates (TDR) and increased false discovery rates (FDR) in causal exposure selection and causal inference. This imbalance not only limits the generalizability of findings but also perpetuates health disparities. The generalizability of European findings to other ethnic populations is uncertain due to differences in linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns, allele frequencies, and underlying genetic architectures [13]. Applying instruments and estimates from European populations to non-European populations without adjustment can yield erroneous conclusions, exacerbate health disparities and limit the global applicability of MR findings.

Thus, there is a pressing need to develop novel methodological frameworks capable of simultaneously addressing two major challenges: correlated high-dimensional exposures and cross-population generalization. Transfer learning [14] offers a promising solution by leveraging knowledge from a data-rich source domain (e.g. European populations with well-estimated genetic instruments for correlated exposures) to enhance model performance in a data-scarce target domain (e.g. non-European populations). Hou et al. introduced trans-ethnic Mendelian randomization (TEMR) [15], one of the first methods designed to improve causal estimation in underrepresented ancestries. However, TEMR is a univariable MR (UVMR) approach that relies on the assumption that Wald ratios for genetic variants across ancestries follow a multivariate normal distribution, and it enhances statistical power only when genetic correlations are non-zero. Yang et al. proposed MRBEE-TL [16], one of the first multi-ancestry multivariable MR (MVMR) methods, which integrates transfer learning with bias-corrected estimating equations to boost power in underpowered populations and evaluate cross-ancestry heterogeneity of risk factors. Despite these advances, there remains a lack of transfer learning MR methods specifically designed to handle pleiotropic and correlated high-dimensional exposures.

In this study, we introduce a two-step statistical transfer learning method for high-dimensional MR, termed TL-HDMR, designed to enhance the detection of causal exposures in target populations. To mitigate the influence of correlations among exposures, we incorporate the Minimax Concave Penalty (MCP), which facilitates asymptotically unbiased estimation of causal effects. Additionally, we propose two pre-transfer procedures—HDMR.TSD, an algorithm that identifies source datasets conducive to positive transfer, and HDMR.PRESSO, which filters out pleiotropic instruments—to further optimize the performance of TL-HDMR. We evaluate the performance of the proposed method through extensive simulations and demonstrate its practical utility by applying TL-HDMR to identify causal biomarkers for stroke and its four subtypes among 899 blood metabolites. The results underscore the capability of TL-HDMR to produce equitable and generalizable causal inferences across diverse ancestral backgrounds.

It is based on GWAS summary data and is designed for HDMR settings—specifically, scenarios where the number (P) of exposures is close to or exceeds that of IVs (J), that is P≥ J, and the exposures are also correlated.

Simulation

We conducted a series of simulation studies to evaluate the performance of TL-HDMR. Performance metrics included the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, which reflects variable selection accuracy, and the mean absolute error (MAE), which measures the precision of causal effect estimation. The methods compared consist of traditional HDMR approaches, along with three penalties—LDA.Lasso, LDA.Elas, and LDA.MCP—implemented using only the target dataset, along with versions enhanced by our TL-HDMR method to incorporate information from auxiliary datasets. We also varied multiple parameters across the simulations, including the correlations among IVs, correlations among exposures, genetic correlations between source and target datasets, the number of IVs, and the number of source datasets.

As summarized in Fig. 2a, we compared the performance metrics of traditional HDMR and TL-HDMR after knowledge transfer under three penalty terms, under the condition of zero correlations among exposures and no genetic associations among source and target datasets. The results indicated a marked improvement in the ROC curve following transfer, demonstrating that TL-HDMR achieves more accurate identification of causal variables. Figure 3a presents the corresponding MAE for causal effect estimation. Prior to transfer, the MCP penalty yielded the lowest MAE. After integration of auxiliary data through TL-HDMR, all methods exhibited a pronounced reduction in MAE, reflecting enhanced estimation precision. Figure 2b depicts the scenario with non-zero correlations among exposures. Compared with Fig. 2a, as correlations among exposures increased, the ROC performance of traditional HDMR improved somewhat, while TL-HDMR consistently and significantly outperformed the traditional approach. As shown in Figs S1 and S2, elevated correlations among exposures—particularly at a level of 0.8—lead to more limited improvement in ROC performance with TL-HDMR. Nevertheless, TL-HDMR consistently achieves significantly smaller MAE, underscoring its unbiased and robust properties in causal effect estimation. Figure 2c illustrates the case involving non-zero genetic associations between source and target datasets. Here, stronger genetic correlations resulted in a pronounced decrease in ROC curves for traditional HDMR approaches, including MCP. In contrast, TL-HDMR consistently demonstrated strong performance and achieved a significant reduction in MAE under these conditions (Fig. 3c). As indicated in Figs S3 and S4, TL-HDMR maintains similarly strong performance when genetic associations between source and target datasets are below 0.5. However, at a genetic association level of 0.8, the improvement in ROC becomes more modest, although TL-HDMR continues to achieve a significantly smaller MAE.

Application

We utilized GWAS summary data from four distinct ancestry groups (European (EUR), African (AFR), East Asian (EA) and South Asian (SA)) to investigate the causal relationships between 899 metabolites and stroke as well as its four subtypes (cardioembolic stroke, ischemic stroke, large artery stroke, and small vessel stroke), and separately analyzed ancestry-specific and stroke subtype-specific metabolic biomarkers. Finally, we performed functional enrichment analysis on the identified causal biomarkers. The details of workflow are shown in Fig. S29.

Discussion

In this paper, we introduce TL-HDMR, a two-step transfer learning framework designed to address two major challenges in cross-population causal inference: high-dimensional exposure spaces and limited sample sizes in non-European cohorts. By leveraging genetic information from well-powered source populations, TL-HDMR significantly improves the accuracy and generalizability of causal estimates in underpowered target populations. The method integrates a penalized regression approach using the MCP to handle correlated exposures and introduces two novel pre-transfer procedures—HDMR.TSD and HDMR.PRESSO—to select transferable source datasets and filter out invalid instruments. Through comprehensive simulations and real-data analyses of blood metabolites in relation to stroke, we demonstrate that TL-HDMR outperforms existing high-dimensional MR methods in both ROC performance and robustness, providing a powerful tool for equitable causal discovery across diverse ancestries.

While TL-HDMR builds upon the transfer-learning architecture of standard high-dimensional linear models (e.g. Trans-Lasso), it introduces fundamental adaptations to address the unique complexities of MR. Traditional algorithms require individual-level data and assume a single-stage regression with perfectly measured predictors. To overcome these limitations, TL-HDMR utilizes linkage disequilibrium matrices to transform marginal GWAS summary statistics into conditional statistics, enabling robust summary-level integration. Furthermore, to account for the heteroskedasticity and correlation inherent in genetic estimates, we employ a weighted least squares inverse-weighting approach and decouple the estimation into a two-stage architecture that sequentially models instrument-exposure and instrument-outcome associations. Finally, to mitigate the risks of horizontal pleiotropy and negative knowledge transfer in cross-ancestry MR, we fortified the framework with HDMR.PRESSO to filter invalid instruments and HDMR.TSD to adaptively select compatible source datasets. Collectively, these modifications elevate conventional transfer learning into a tailored causal inference tool.

The proposed TL-HDMR framework offers a highly adaptable and theoretically grounded approach for enhancing causal inference in high-dimensional settings, particularly when generalizing findings across diverse populations. While initially developed and validated in the context of genomic and metabolomic data, the method is by no means limited to omics-based exposures. Its flexible architecture allows for the inclusion of a wide range of exposure types, including clinical variables, imaging-derived features, environmental factors, and behavioral indicators. This significantly broadens its applicability beyond molecular epidemiology to fields such as social science, environmental health, and public policy, where high-dimensional causal inference is increasingly needed but remains challenging due to collinearity and weak instruments.

Notably, the method supports knowledge transfer not only across different ethnic groups but also between subpopulations within the same broader ancestry. Genetic heterogeneity, environmental exposures, and social determinants can drive divergence in causal mechanisms even among groups that share continental ancestry. TL-HDMR enables the use of auxiliary data from any well-characterized source population—whether from a different geographic region, a different cohort within the same ethnicity, or a population with distinct environmental profiles—to strengthen causal estimation in an underpowered target population. This capability is critical for advancing health equity and precision medicine, as it allows more nuanced and representative causal inference across increasingly stratified populations.

A core assumption enabling robust transfer learning is the sparsity of causal effect heterogeneity between source and target populations. More precisely, we assume that the difference in causal effects, denoted as δk, is sparse in the l₁-norm, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\Vert{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^k\Vert}_1<h$\end{document}, where h is bounded above by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $s\sqrt{\mathit{\log}P/{J}_0}$\end{document}. This sparsity condition implies that only a limited subset of exposures exhibits divergent effects across populations. When satisfied, it ensures that the auxiliary data can provide meaningful information gain without introducing substantial bias, thereby facilitating asymptotically unbiased estimation and reducing variance in the target population. This assumption is biologically plausible and supported by empirical evidence across multiple domains. For instance, in pharmaceutical research, drug-target pathways often remain consistent across ancestries, while only specific metabolic enzymes (e.g. CYP450 family) show clinically relevant effect modifications. Similarly, in studies of cardiovascular traits, core biological mechanisms such as lipid metabolism and blood pressure regulation are largely conserved across populations, with heterogeneity confined to a limited number of context-specific genetic or environmental modifiers. In complex trait genetics, most causal variants exhibit consistent effects across ancestries, with heterogeneous effects typically restricted to a small number of loci influenced by local adaptation or gene–environment interactions. Such patterns of limited heterogeneity justify the sparsity assumption in practice and enhance the robustness of transfer learning applications. Even when the strict sparsity assumption is partially violated, the two-step architecture of TL-HDMR provides empirical robustness in the settings examined. The massive variance reduction provided by auxiliary datasets outweighs the minor introduced bias, while the subsequent target-only debiasing step effectively filters out dense structural noises, ensuring consistent etiological network reconstruction.

Despite these robust theoretical properties, our approach remains susceptible to weak instrument bias, particularly in trans-ancestry GWAS where the target populations typically suffer from extremely small sample sizes. Our sensitivity analyses demonstrated that extreme weak IV environments inevitably lead to residual attenuation bias and constrained discovery power. It is crucial to acknowledge the limitations of our current methodological safeguards in mitigating this specific bias. First, while the MCP penalty achieves asymptotic unbiasedness in ideal settings, extreme weak IVs exhibit a fundamentally low signal-to-noise ratio. In such environments, regularization may inadvertently over-shrink true but weak causal signals, failing to fully correct the attenuation bias. Second, while HDMR.PRESSO effectively filters heterogeneous structural noise (i.e. horizontal pleiotropy) by evaluating outlier residuals, weak instruments systematically dilute the global effect size rather than manifesting as distinct outliers, rendering PRESSO ineffective at identifying pure weak IV bias. Future methodological iterations of the TL-HDMR framework could directly tackle this challenge in underpowered target cohorts by integrating Bayesian prior weighting or pleiotropy-robust variance corrections to further stabilize the estimates.

In summary, TL-HDMR provides a scalable, robust, and inclusive framework for causal discovery in complex datasets spanning diverse populations and exposure types. It represents a meaningful step toward more equitable and generalizable scientific insights.

Materials and methods

Model and notation

The TL-HDMR method transfers information from auxiliary source populations to the target population, thereby enhancing both the accuracy of causal variable selection and the precision of causal effect estimation in the target population. It is based on GWAS summary data and is designed for HDMR settings—specifically, scenarios where the number of exposures is close to or exceeds that of IVs, that is, and the exposures are also correlated. The workflow of TL-HDMR is shown in Fig. 1.

HDMR: high-dimensional multivariable MR

The traditional WLS methods are not applicable in the high-dimensional exposures setting. This is firstly due to the existence of many correlated exposures, which WLS cannot handle, and secondly due to the curse of dimensionality, especially when the number of exposures is extremely large (P≈ J or P>J). When γ =1, the Elastic-net penalty reduces to the Lasso penalty. However, both the Lasso and Elastic-net penalty obtain the biased estimation for causal effects of exposures on the outcome. Here, we use the MCP penalty [62].

which achieves asymptotically unbiased estimation of causal effects. Unlike Lasso, which introduces shrinkage bias, non-convex penalties like SCAD and MCP yield asymptotically unbiased estimates. In high-dimensional settings with severely correlated instruments, MCP is theoretically and practically superior to SCAD. Its minimax concavity shrinks unassociated coefficients to zero at a faster rate, preventing the over-penalization of true causal signals that share variance. Consequently, MCP ensures greater feature selection stability and computational efficiency in extremely collinear environments.

We propose TL-HDMR, an algorithm designed to enhance the accuracy of causal estimands in the target population by transferring information from auxiliary source populations. TL-HDMR builds upon the core idea of the Oracle Trans-Lasso algorithm proposed by Li et al. [63] The algorithm comprises two steps. The first step is the transferring step, in which an initial estimator is computed using all the informative auxiliary and target datasets. However, its probabilistic limit is generally biased away from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\boldsymbol{\theta}$\end{document}, since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\boldsymbol{\theta}}^K\ne \boldsymbol{\theta}$\end{document} under typical conditions. This bias is then corrected in the second step using the target data.

The causal effects between the target and auxiliary populations may be either identical or different. When the causal effects are identical, we can directly use the auxiliary populations and leverage the aforementioned TL-HDMR algorithm for transfer to achieve higher accuracy. However, when the causal effects differ, it becomes necessary to filter the auxiliary population to exclude incompatible subsets [63]. HDMR.PRESSO: detection of invalid instruments

The assumption A2 and A3 are easily violated in the applications. We integrate the HDMR into the framework of MR-PRESSO [17] to remove the invalid IVs. Algorithm 3 outlines the HDMR.PRESSO procedure. The HDMR.PRESSO algorithm detects outlier IVs by first refitting the HDMR model after sequentially removing each variant j to obtain causal estimates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{-j}$\end{document}. This observed RSS is then compared against a simulated null distribution—generated under the assumption of no outliers—by repeatedly drawing effect sizes from multivariable Gaussian distributions around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{-j}{\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}}_j$\end{document}. We evaluated the performance of the method using the ROC curve, the MAE, estimation bias, and CP. The ROC curve was constructed via cross-validation over a range of lambda values from e−15 to e5. The ROC metric reflects the accuracy of variable selection across different methods, while MAE measures the precision of causal effect estimation. Furthermore, estimation bias was utilized to assess the accuracy and unbiasedness of the identified causal effects, and CP was utilized to evaluate the reliability of the constructed confidence intervals. The detailed sparsity-aware bias-corrected bootstrap procedure for calculating CP is described in Supplementary Note S1. The entire simulation was repeated 200 times independently to ensure robustness. The compared methods include: (a) Traditional HDMR approaches—specifically, Equation (2)—with three penalty terms named LDA.Lasso, LDA.Elas and LDA.MCP, implemented using only the target dataset; (b) The same three penalized HDMR models as in (a), enhanced with our TL-HDMR method to incorporate both the target and auxiliary datasets; (c) An adaptation for cases where some auxiliary datasets are unavailable, using the HDMR.TSD algorithm to select suitable auxiliary datasets before applying TL-HDMR; (d) A strategy for handling invalid IVs, where HDMR.PRESSO is first used to remove invalid IVs, followed by TL-HDMR estimation.

Application

Metabolites can reveal key pathological pathways and serve as potential biomarkers for early detection or subtype stratification. Identifying causal metabolites—such as lipids or inflammatory mediators—could inform targeted prevention strategies and support the development of personalized interventions. Therefore, we apply TL-HDMR for investigating causal relationships between 899 metabolites and stroke as well as its subtypes. We use GWAS summary statistics from four ancestries: African (AFR), East Asian (EA), South Asian (SA) and European (EUR). GWAS summary statistics for metabolites were extracted from Chen et al. [64], who conducted transethnic and ancestry-specific GWASs from four populations (>8000 European, >100 East/South Asian, >60 African). GWAS summary statistics for stroke and its four subtypes were extracted from Mishra et al. [65] with >1 200 000 European, >200 000 East Asian, >8000 South Asian and >20 000 African. The details are shown in Supplementary Data 1 and 2.

Figure S29 outlines the procedure for IV selection and statistical analysis. For each metabolite, we selected SNPs with a GWAS P-value below 5 × 10−8 as IVs. If no SNP reached this threshold, the most significant SNP was chosen. We then took the union of all candidate IVs and retained only those present across all databases, including 899 metabolite- and five stroke (and its subtypes)-related GWAS summary datasets. This process yielded 552 metabolites (Supplementary Data 3) with IVs available in every database. Further population-specific filtering resulted in 523 IVs in South Asians, 538 in Africans, 536 in East Asians, and 411 in Europeans, noting that some SNPs served as IVs for multiple metabolites (Supplementary Data 4-7). Since TL-HDMR allows for non-zero LD between SNPs, no LD clumping was performed. We subsequently extracted GWAS summary statistics for these SNPs corresponding to each metabolite and stroke outcome. LD reference panels for four ancestries were derived from 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3, and were used to transform the marginal genetic associations into conditional associations. Each ethnic group was alternately treated as the target dataset, with the remaining three serving as candidate auxiliary datasets.

We first applied conventional HDMR using the target dataset alone. We then integrated target and auxiliary datasets by: (i) removing invalid IVs using HDMR.PRESSO, (ii) selecting informative auxiliary datasets with HDMR.TSD, and (iii) performing causal variable selection and causal effect estimation via TL-HDMR. λ is chosen by 10-fold cross validation from the range from e−10 to e−2. The parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${C}_0$\end{document} is set to 1.6. For the pathway enrichment analysis of metabolites, we performed Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) using the MetaboAnalyst tool. The pathways were sourced from the RaMP-DB library, which includes 3694 metabolite and lipid pathways from RaMP-DB (integrating KEGG via HMDB, Reactome, and WikiPathways). All results are shown in Supplementary Data 8 and 9.

To clarify the analytical pipeline for biological interpretation, we emphasize that the TL-HDMR framework operates strictly at the individual metabolite level and does not algorithmically prioritize specific pathways. The pathway enrichment results presented in our study were derived through a structured post-hoc analysis. First, for each stroke outcome, we iteratively applied TL-HDMR—treating one ancestry as the target and the others as auxiliary sources—to robustly identify causal metabolites within that specific population. Second, we cross-referenced the selected causal metabolites across all ancestries to systematically isolate shared causal intersections from ancestry-unique biomarkers. Finally, ORA was conducted on these categorized metabolite sets independently. A parallel comparative pipeline was applied across different stroke subtypes to delineate subtype-specific metabolic pathways. Through this hierarchical workflow, any enriched shared pathways are strictly driven by cross-ancestry consistent metabolites rescued by the transfer learning framework, while population- or subtype-specific pathways are driven entirely by distinct biomarkers preserved by the HDMR.TSD algorithm.

Key Points

A novel statistical framework for genetic causality. TL-HDMR is built on a rigorous two-step transfer learning architecture combined with the Minimax Concave Penalty, providing a statistically robust solution to the challenges of high-dimensionality and correlation in exposure data.
