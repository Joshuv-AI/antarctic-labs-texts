Re‐sequencing 142 rice genomes reveals the fine‐scale population structure and phylogenetic affinities of landraces of Kashmir Valley: Evidence from domestication and adaptive signatures
Authors: Asif Bashir Shikari, V. Shasi Kiran Reddy, Raheel Shafeeq Khan, Noor ul‐Ain, Gazala H. Khan, Najeeb‐ul‐Rehman Sofi, Zahoor A. Dar, Nazir A. Ganai, R. K. Salgotra, Manmohan Sharma, G. A. Parray, A. K. Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of rice (Oryza sativa) as a cultivated crop has represented a landmark in the history of agriculture in Kashmir Valley. Unlike winter cereals, such as wheat and barley, which exhibit clear West‐Asian origins, rice attained a different domestication trajectory linked to East‐Asian Neolithic cultures. However, evidence of such origins and adaptive history of Kashmir's rice landraces have relied on limited archaeo‐botanical data, tool typologies, and information pertaining regional cultural exchange. We present the first comprehensive genomics‐resource for the region produced through whole genome re‐sequencing of 142 rice germplasm lines. By leveraging on the 3000 Rice Genomes Project (3K RGP) database, we analyzed fine‐scale population structures and evolutionary relationships. Our findings reveal that local landraces possess temperate japonica (Tej) genetic signatures with the origins having been traced to North‐East Asia, while the modern bred varieties in the region often align with indica (derivative) lineages from subtropical India. The sequence data were generated with a mean depth of 13.6x, and 3.94 million high‐confidence single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) after stringent filtering. Populations resolved into a bipartite genetic structure (K = 2) comprising two major sub‐populations with an admixed group, with the help of high‐polymorphism information content SNP panels. Genome‐wide diversity analyses revealed the positive Tajima's D values, indicating extensive population structure and balancing selection, with localized signatures of directional selection. The allelic characterization explained the rich diversity at the functionally polymorphic loci linked to domestication, quality, and adaptive traits, particularly those associated with cold tolerance. The gene repository shall provide for development of better quality and climate resilient cultivars.

INTRODUCTION

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a principal staple crop that nourishes more than half of the world's population and serves as a cornerstone of global food security (Wang et al., 2018). The critical objectives of sustaining yield, enhancing grain quality and fortifying stress resilience in the face of climate change, fundamentally rely on the genetic improvement (Singh et al., 2011). Such progress is predicated on broad, accessible genetic diversity, which rice possesses across its domesticated gene pool and wild relatives (Huang et al., 2012; Khush, 1997; Tiozon et al., 2025). The cultivated gene pool comprises more than 120,000 cataloged varieties worldwide including modern cultivars and landraces with nearly 50,000 of them recorded in India (Vasudevan et al., 2014). Particularly, landraces as reservoirs of genetic diversity hold significance as they convey the evolutionary transition from wild to pure domestication (Higgins et al., 2021; Hour et al., 2020). Although, a substantial portion of haplotype diversity present in the landraces has been lost in modern cultivars (Yonemaru et al., 2012), the genomic characterization of landraces in niche‐ecologies promises insights into the adaptive evolution and trait diversity (Li et al., 2021), so that the utilization of genetic resources can be speeded up to achieve new goals in crop breeding.

The temperate valley of Kashmir, located at 34° N and 73° E, represents the northern most extent of rice cultivation in India, with fields spanning altitudes from 1500 to over 2200 m. The unique climatic conditions, altitudinal gradients, alongside traditional farming practices in Kashmir have shaped rice landraces with distinct quality and traits adapted to local stresses, such as cold tolerance (CT) (Najeeb et al., 2018; Parray & Shikari, 2008). Some of these traditional landraces continue to hold significant value in the valley and are deeply embedded in its cultural heritage, exemplified by the aromatic Mushq Budji, Kamad, Kaw Kreer, Loule Anzul, Zag, Nun Beoul, etc. (Khan et al., 2018; Noor et al., 2025). We conducted the first study on genomics of rice germplasm from Kashmir by using uniplex Kompetitive Allele‐specific PCR (KASP) markers (Shikari et al., 2021). This study revealed the population sub‐structure and the information on important genes underlying agronomic traits. Broadly, the rice landraces from Kashmir were grouped as Jap (Shikari et al., 2021), in contrast to the modern bred varieties (MV) of the region falling toward Ind (indica) and admixture groups, resembling to the lineage from subtropical India (Huang et al., 2012; Jing et al., 2023). The study however, could not delineate the information on the individual genes and provided no insights on the phylogenetic relationship with the global rice diversity panels. To gain a comprehensive insight and details on evolutionary relations, allelic polymorphism and origin of Kashmir landraces (KL), we adopted to whole‐genome re‐sequencing approach of core germplasm collections and landraces drawn from the previous study.

The history of rice in Kashmir Valley has so far been limited to earlier archaeobotanical studies, which reported the presence of rice in Megalithic contexts dated between 1800 and 1000 BC (Kajale, 1982; Lone et al., 1993). While these findings provide crucial evidence for the antiquity of rice cultivation in the region, they offer limited insight into the genetic origin, diversification, and dispersal of rice populations that reached to the valley. In the present context, timeline and adaptive history of rice in Himalayan mountains is poorly understood. Hopefully, the genomics‐based approaches have been advocated to better complement the archaeobotanical records by reconstructing the genetic relationships among ancient and modern rice lineages, in order to identify the source regions and routes of introduction (Alam et al., 2025; Van De Loosdrecht et al., 2024).

It is for the first time we are using whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) approach for constructing the genomic mini‐resource of the rice landraces and adapted germplasm, both native and exotic, for the geographical region of Jammu and Kashmir (J&K), India. We leveraged the information present in extensive 3000 Rice Genomes Project (3K RGP) database (Mansueto et al., 2017) to uncover the evolutionary relationship of our landraces to global diversity. Genomics‐based approaches for a given context have been worked out or discussed previously by different workers (Abbai et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2020). Accordingly, we hypothesized that the distribution of alleles associated with domestication and adaptive traits among local rice germplasm would help find such relationships and may guide us to create a genomics resource, meaningful for supporting trait enhancement, gene discovery, and functional genomics research.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials

The present investigation dealt with whole‐genome re‐sequencing of a selected subset of 142 rice germplasm lines maintained at the Crop Gene Bank, Sher‐e‐Kashmir University of Agricultural Sciences and Technology of Kashmir (SKUAST)–Kashmir, J&K, India. Of these, 29 prominent landraces and modern varieties (SR‐1, SR‐2, SR‐3, SR‐4, SR‐5, etc.) were native to Kashmir Valley and five landraces originated from Jammu region. Besides, 21 germplasm accessions (14.8%), 15 bred varieties (10.6%), and four Basmati accessions (2.8%) belonged to subtropical/tropical parts of India. In addition, 21 local collections (14.8%) and 14 breeding lines (9.9%) from the Kashmir Valley, along with 33 exotic collections (23.2%) sourced from our Gene Bank, were included to capture a broad spectrum of indigenous, improved, and introduced rice genetic resources. We named the germplasm panel as, 100 Rice Genomes‐SKUAST‐K (referred hereafter as 100‐RGS) and integrated it with larger reference panels including the 3K RGP to workout the population assignment, besides the elucidation of the key causative variations featuring the genes underlying the traits linked to adaptability, consumer preference, and domestication.

Whole genome re‐sequencing

Genomic DNA isolation

Seedlings were raised under controlled greenhouse conditions and young fresh leaves were harvested for genomic DNA extraction. High‐molecular‐weight genomic DNA was isolated using the Nucleon PhytoPure Kit (GE Healthcare BioSciences) following the manufacturer's protocol. DNA quality and integrity were assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis and concentration was quantified using a Qubit double‐stranded DNA fluorometer (Invitrogen, Life Technologies). To ensure sequencing‐grade purity, DNA was further purified using a silica column clean‐up prior to Illumina library preparation. Only the samples exhibiting high purity and intact DNA were used for downstream library preparation.

Library construction and sequencing

Genomic DNA was used to construct paired‐end sequencing libraries using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) with a mean insert size of approximately 350 base pairs (bp), according to the manufacturer's protocol. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X platform (Illumina) to generate 150 bp paired‐end reads, with an average genome coverage of ∼20×. Raw reads were quality‐checked using FastQC v0.11.9. The raw sequencing reads were processed sequentially using Trimmomatic v0.39 for adapter removal and quality trimming, followed by fastp v0.23.2 for additional quality filtering and quality‐control assessment. After filtering, the effective genome coverage ranged from 88% to 94%, with >94% of bases having Phred quality scores ≥Q30.

Quality filtering, read mapping, and variant calling

The demultiplexing process was carried out using the FASTX Toolkit (version 0.0.13) to isolate samples from the combined bar code data. The Quality Check operation was carried out with the assistance of FastQC (version 0.11.8, http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The distribution of base quality scores, sequence quality scores, the average base content per read, and the GC distribution in the reads were considered. A wrapper script called trim galore (version 0.6.6, https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/) was used to remove Universal Illumina Adapters (AGATCGGAAGAGC). This script was designed to automate quality and adapter trimming, in addition to quality control. The clean reads were aligned to the O. sativa ssp. japonica cv. Nipponbare reference genome (IRGSP‐1.0 assembly) downloaded from Ensembl Plants (Release 60) (https://ftp.ensemblgenomes.ebi.ac.uk/pub/plants/release‐60/fasta/oryza_sativa/dna/). Through the utilization of the MEM algorithm utilized by Burrows‐Wheeler aligner (version 0.7.5), reads were mapped against the reference genome. Afterwards, Sequence Alignment/Map (SAM) files were converted into sorted and indexed Binary Alignment/Map (BAM) files using SAMtools. Joint variant calling was performed using the UnifiedGenotyper module of the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, version 3.8). The resulting variants were filtered to retain high‐quality biallelic single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05 and ≤10% missing data. The filtered SNP dataset was subsequently used for all downstream analyses.

Population structure analysis

Bayesian clustering algorithm was implemented in STRUCTURE v2.3.4 under the admixture model with correlated allele frequencies. Initially, three separate filtered single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sets namely, S‐10, S‐25, S‐50 were created on 142 genotypes and covered 6148, 8210, and 3847 SNPs with low (0.18), medium (0.375), and high polymorphism information content(PIC) values (0.50), respectively (Tables S1 and S2). Because no prior information on population identity was imposed, all the individuals were assigned to a single predefined group (Pop = 0). Each STRUCTURE run comprised a burn‐in of 50,000 iterations, followed by 100,000 Markov chain Monte Carlo repetitions. Simulations were conducted for K‐1 to K‐6, with three independent runs per K‐value to ensure consistency and detect potential convergence issues. The optimal number of clusters was determined using both the log probability of data [LnP(D)] and the ΔK method of Evanno et al. (2005), implemented through STRUCTURE HARVESTER. Clustering patterns and membership coefficients (Q‐values) were summarized using CLUMPP to minimize label switching. Pairwise allele‐frequency divergence between clusters, within‐cluster heterozygosity, and mean admixture coefficients (α) were extracted directly from STRUCTURE output.

fastSTRUCTURE, principal coordinate analysis, and phylogenetic analysis

The more refined population structure (S‐18K), derived from combining the three sub‐marker sets, was inferred with the help of 18,205 SNPs using fastSTRUCTURE v1.0 algorithm, a scalable variational Bayesian framework optimized for large SNP datasets. Finally, a broader set (S‐100K) of 97,955 high‐quality SNPs was selected out of the total SNP data using an allele frequency (AF) threshold of 0.4. SNPs were converted to BED format using PLINK v1.9 (to ensure compatibility with fastSTRUCTURE). Analyses was performed assuming an admixture model with parameters considered for individual data panels. Individual ancestry coefficients (Q‐matrix) were extracted and visualized as stacked bar plots using R. Individuals with membership probability ≥70% were assigned to a specific subpopulation, while those below this threshold were considered admixed. Consistency of inferred population structure was further examined by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). The use of high marker density S‐18K and S‐100K sets of genome‐wide SNPs ensured robust resolution of population stratification with minimized bias.

Phylogenetic analysis was performed using IQ‐TREE 3 under the maximum likelihood framework. The computational workflow was implemented in R for initial data processing, where the readxl package facilitated data import, transposition and formatting into a polymorphic, SNP (∼97K variable sites) multi‐sequence alignment in FASTA format. ModelFinder was used to test various substitution models and identified GTR+F+ASC+R5 as the best‐fit model. This model incorporated Ascertainment Bias Correction (+ASC) to mathematically account for the absence of invariant sites and prevent branch length inflation, while utilizing a FreeRate model (+R5) to manage rate heterogeneity across the genome. Statistical support for the resulting clades was calculated using 1000 Ultrafast Bootstrap replicates. The final maximum‐likelihood tree was exported in Newick format and visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life.

Tajima's D statistic was calculated to assess patterns of nucleotide diversity across the entire 100‐RGS panel and within each STRUCTURE‐defined group (S‐I, S‐II, and Admixed). Genotypes were parsed directly from the SNP matrix as diploid calls and sites with missing genotypes (NN) were excluded on a per‐locus basis. Per‐site nucleotide diversity (πi) was estimated using the unbiased estimator πi = [n/(n − 1)] × 2p(1 − p), where n represents the number of sampled chromosomes and p the alternate AF at locus i. Because missing data caused n to vary among loci, an effective sample size (n eff), calculated as the harmonic mean of per‐site sample sizes, was used to approximate the number of sampled chromosomes for downstream calculations. Watterson's estimator of nucleotide diversity was calculated as θW = S/a1, where S is the number of segregating sites and a1 = Σ(1/i) for i = 1 to n eff − 1. Tajima's D was then estimated as D = (π − θW)/√[e1S + e2S(S − 1)], where e1 and e2 are variance coefficients derived according to Tajima (1989). The analyses was performed in Python 3.12 using the libraries Pandas (v2.2.3) for SNP data processing, NumPy (v2.1.3) for numerical computations, and Math from the Python standard library for variance calculations, with custom scripts implementing the original formulation of Tajima's D (Tajima, 1989).

Allele mining of major genes

A subset of 115 accessions with high genome coverage was retained for downstream analysis to obtain allelic polymorphism for the major genes related to agronomic performance, rice quality, and cold stress tolerance. The resulting data were used to infer the evolutionary relationships and phylogenetic affinities of Kashmir rice in context to global rice diversity. The evolutionary relations were drawn through the analysis of causal variants associated with adaptive‐, quality‐, and domestication‐related genes across the population. The information on causal variants was retrieved from the literature referenced in other sections of this paper. First of all we carried out the in silico analysis of 3K RGP panel using the Rice SNP‐Seek database and RiceVarMap. This allowed us to characterize subpopulation‐specific allele distributions and extract a reference framework of the major rice lineages (e.g., Ind, temperate japonica [Tej], Aus, and aromatic [Aro]). The small discrepancies between two databases were systematically overcome in our comparative analysis. Allelic variation among accessions was examined by visualizing mapped sequencing reads (Binary Alignment/Map files) using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV), v2.18.4.

Variant calling and functional annotation

A custom Python pipeline utilizing the pysam library was developed to process alignment data and characterize genomic variation across the targeted loci. Positional pileup data were extracted from high‐quality reads (mapping quality ≥ 30) aligned to the O. sativa ssp. japonica cv. Nipponbare reference genome assembly (IRGSP‐1.0). Strict evidence‐based genotyping was applied (Q > 40), for filtering out misaligned soft‐clipped read tails and forcing reference calls at loci exhibiting severe strand bias. Genotypes were assigned using dominant AF thresholds (≥0.75 for homozygous, ≥0.25 for heterozygous calls), followed by a cohort‐aware singleton filter (depth < 3x) to remove low‐confidence variants. Utilizing transcript and exon‐level general feature format coordinates from the Rice Annotation Project Database (Sakai et al., 2013), the retained high‐confidence variants were dynamically annotated for their biological effect (e.g., synonymous, missense, frameshift, splice‐site disruption), with Grantham distances calculated to assess the physicochemical severity of any amino acid substitutions.

Haplotype clustering and nearest‐neighbor imputation

To resolve the distinct functional haplotypes across the 100‐RGS panel, the custom linkage disequilibrium (LD)‐aware imputation algorithm pipeline was integrated in Python utilizing pandas and NumPy libraries. Initially, strict “core” haplotypes were established using only accessions with high‐confidence, homozygous calls across all targeted functional loci; any single allelic mismatch among these loci (a functional mismatch distance > 0) resulted in the classification of a distinct haplotype. For accessions containing heterozygous or missing data, unresolved alleles were computationally imputed using a nearest‐neighbor approach. The algorithm calculated the genetic distance (mismatch rate) between the ambiguous accession and all core donors across the entire intragenic background variant profile. Missing or heterozygous functional alleles were then imputed based on the exact profile of the closest genetic twin. In cases of tied mismatch rates, a 5‐kilobase (kb) flanking LD window was utilized to break the tie, ensuring accurate and biologically relevant haplotype assignment based on localized genetic linkage.

RESULTS

Re‐sequencing data quality and genome coverage

A total of 820.28 Gb of high‐quality sequencing data was generated across 142 rice genotypes, with an average of ∼5.94 Gb per genotype. Quality control analysis revealed consistently high base‐calling accuracy, with Q20 and Q30 at 96.22% and 91.06%, respectively. The GC content of re‐sequencing reads averaged 39.53%, closely matching the expected composition of the O. sativa genome and suggestive of minimal GC bias during library preparation and sequencing. The mapping rate averaged at 98.92±0.11% that depicts a sufficient reference genome coverage. The mean sequencing depth across accessions was 13.6x, ranging from 4.3 to 77.7x that is expected to provide for reliable variant discovery (Figure S1).

Genome‐wide SNP discovery and filtering

Genome‐wide variant calling identified 16,755,760 raw SNPs across the rice germplasm panel. Following stringent quality filtering, including retention of biallelic SNPs, a minor allele frequency ≥ 5%, and ≤10% missing data, a total of 3,939,768 high‐confidence SNPs were retained for downstream analysis. The chromosome 1 harbored the highest number of SNPs (427,785), followed by chromosome 2 (370,597) and chromosome 3 (370,408) (Figure S2).

Genome‐wide SNP density distribution pattern

The rice genome was partitioned into 1501 genomic bins. The analysis revealed an average SNP density of 10.49 variants per kb (7.8–12.8) indicating substantial and uniform genome‐wide polymorphism among the rice genotypes included in the study. However, certain genomic regions exhibited markedly higher SNP densities (up to 25.79 variants per kb), indicating potential hotspots of genetic variation, while a few regions showed very low SNP density, which may correspond to conserved genomic regions or areas under strong selective pressure. The mean SNP count of 2624.83 per sliding bin was recorded, with the range from 3 to 6448, reflecting considerable variation in polymorphism levels across genomic regions. SNP density was generally higher towards the distal regions of chromosomal arms, whereas pericentromeric regions exhibited reduced SNP density. This distribution pattern is consistent with recombination landscapes and selective constraints reported in rice genomes. Further, it reflects the differences in historical recombination rates and selection pressures across the genome, supporting the biological validity of the resequencing dataset (Figure S3).

SNP substitution patterns and transition–transversion bias

Among individual substitution classes, AG (1,432,558) and CT (1,433,037) substitutions were the most abundant, followed by AT (300,840), GT (282,935), AC (282,512), and CG (215,076). Overall, transitions (2,865,595) outnumbered transversions (1,081,363), resulting in a transition/transversion ratio of approximately 2.65, consistent with the patterns of high‐quality SNP discovery and reliable variant calling (Table S3).

Tajima's D–based SNP diversity and signatures of selection

The genomic bins mostly exhibited positive Tajima's D values (D > 2.0), indicating an excess of intermediate‐frequency alleles averaging at +1.64 on overall population. Such patterns are characteristic of population structure, balancing selection and historical domestication processes within diverse germplasm collections. In contrast, localized genomic regions with negative Tajima's D values were also detected. These regions may represent signatures of recent positive selection, purifying selection or population expansion, leading to an excess of rare alleles. The coexistence of predominantly positive Tajima's D values with localized negative regions highlights the complex demographic history and selective landscape shaping genetic diversity in rice germplasm (Table S4). The total population was resolved into sub‐populations S‐I, S‐II, and admixed cluster (discussed below). S‐I exhibited a strongly positive Tajima's D value (+2.55), while the admixed group showed a similarly high value (+2.47), indicating an excess of intermediate‐frequency alleles. In contrast, S‐II displayed a slightly negative Tajima's D value (−0.49), suggesting a relative excess of rare alleles. Nucleotide diversity (π) was highest in the admixed group (0.4017), followed by S‐I (0.3319) and S‐II (0.1836). Collectively, the high‐quality re‐sequencing data, robust SNP dataset, transition‐biased substitution patterns, heterogeneous SNP density, moderate nucleotide diversity, and structured Tajima's D landscape demonstrate that the rice germplasm panel harbors substantial genome‐wide genetic variation shaped by both demographic history and selection. These results confirm the suitability of the dataset for downstream analyses such as population structure inference, selection scans, and genome‐wide association studies targeting agronomically important traits (Table 1).

Population structure analysis

Structure analysis based on filtered SNP datasets revealed clear and consistent genetic stratification across the population. The Evanno ΔK method identified “K = 2” as the most likely number of sub‐populations for both S25 and S50 marker panels, whereas the S10 dataset exhibited a peak at “K = 3.” Sub‐population‐I (S‐I) contained maximum number of accessions for all the three marker panels. Sub‐population‐III (S‐III) revealed only under S10, while its contribution disappeared under S25 and S50. The proportion of admixed genotypes increased under S25 and S50 and subpopulation‐II (S‐II) showed a relative increase in S50, indicating finer genetic resolution with the use of markers with higher discriminatory power (PIC). Several germplasm lines, including C‐Mochi, H270‐30‐2‐1, HAMWAM‐15, ONPO‐6, Begum, Mushkandi‐I, and Koshihikari, showed stable cluster assignments across all marker sets. In contrast, accessions such as C17, GS‐284, GS‐471, OSMANCIK‐97, and M10‐1 shifted from pure cluster assignment under S10/S25 to admixed status under S50. Aromatic and Basmati (Pusa Basmati 1121, Sonasal, Taraori Basmati, Jeerakasala, and Type 3 Basmati) were assigned to S‐III under S10 but resolved into S‐I, S‐II, or admixed categories, under S25 and S50. The combined population structure inference was performed using the S18K SNP panel, revealing a strong bipartite structure. At “K = 2,” the estimated log probability of data [LnP(D) = −3,443,925.7] and the mean log‐likelihood (−3,427,800.5) indicated a stable model fit with moderate variance (32,250.3). Mean ancestry proportions were 0.53 for subpopulation 1, 0.29 for subpopulation 2, and 0.18 for admixed genotypes, with a net nucleotide distance of 0.1664. Genotypes with ≥70% membership in a single cluster were classified as pure. The admixture parameter was low (α = 0.1465). Expected heterozygosity was higher in S‐II (He = 0.5045) compared with S‐I (He = 0.4335). Final and high resolution partitioning was obtained using 97,955 (AF > 0.4) high PIC SNPs (S‐100K). The results collaborated with the S25, S50, and S18K marker panels and provided a clear separation of two groups with intermediate admixture types (Figure S4A–C; Tables S5–S9).

Analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) further supported the presence of genetic differentiation among the identified groups. This within‐group diversity represents an important genetic resource for crop improvement, as it provides raw material for selection without necessarily broadening the genetic base through exotic introductions. The observed moderate ΦST value suggested that the two groups are genetically differentiated and are expected to generate sufficient variability within derived progenies (Figure S4D; Table S10). The subpopulation grouping was reported in PCoA space, with intermediate positions between the two main clusters, reflecting partial overlap or admixture. PCoA‐I represented the primary axis of genetic variation and showed a distinct bimodal distribution of genotypes into S‐I, almost exclusively positioned on the negative side (with large negative scores, ∼−10 to −37) and S‐II clustered on the positive side of PCoA‐I (with values ranging from +10 to +65). PCoA‐II captured secondary variation within and across categories and highlights finer‐scale structuring. Several S‐II genotypes (e.g., Pusa Basmati 1121, Jasmine, Gobindobhog, SR‐8, RAHIL1D) showed extreme positive PCoA‐II values, suggesting a distinct aromatic or specialty subcluster. S‐I genotypes generally exhibit moderate to negative PCoA‐II scores, though some accessions (e.g., Kew, Sonasal, OSMANCIK‐97) are displaced upward, indicating intragroup heterogeneity. PCoA‐III further resolves variation associated with specific germplasm subsets. Several S‐I landraces (e.g., Begum, Bata Baber, Nun Beol, Safed Khuch, Tumla Hall) and local selections showed high positive PCoA‐III values, suggesting shared adaptive or evolutionary features. Conversely, strongly negative PCoA‐III values in some S‐I accessions (e.g., SR‐9, GS‐60, C‐Mochi) point toward diversification even within the same major gene pool. S‐II genotypes show relatively compressed variation along PCoA‐III, implying a comparatively narrower adaptive spectrum within this group. The combined evidence from PCoA, STRUCTURE, and AMOVA demonstrates that the germplasm comprises genetically distinct but partially interconnected groups, offering valuable opportunities for parent selection, heterotic grouping, and conservation planning (Figure 1).

Principal coordinate, phylogenetic analysis, and Q‐plot depicting overall population substructure on S‐100K marker panel.

The phylogenetic analysis resolved the two major clusters, I and II, corresponding to Jap and Ind genetic backgrounds, respectively, demonstrating a clear bi‐lineage structure within the studied rice germplasm panel. The Tej cluster constituted a broader group and comprised of three subgroups, encompassing primarily temperate‐adapted lines and the regional traditional landraces. Near to or within the Tej group, a distinct and tightly grouped subcluster of aromatic/Basmati genotypes was observed. WGS results aligned with the genetic divergence based on individual genes, where it can be resolved that KL and high altitude varieties belong to two subgroups. Sub‐group‐II contained Ram Hall, Kew, Zag, Sig Safed, Ratta Ranjha, PRC‐1, and Kaw Kreer that probably belonged to Japx (japonica admixed), whereas the rest of the landraces (Mushq Budji, Kamad, Begum, Bata Baber, Nun Beol, etc.) occupied the subgroup‐I alongside the known Tej, Koshihikari, and Heibao. In contrast, the Ind cluster comprised a diverse set of improved varieties, traditional genotypes, and breeding lines, carrying diverse geographic origins and breeding histories. Landraces which belong to intermediate/subtropical zones of J&K like Bansali, Daggu, and Giza‐14 grouped within cluster‐II, alongside improved varieties, SR‐1, SR‐2, SR‐4, and SR‐7 (Figure 1). Few of the representative germplasm accessions and landraces are presented in Figure 2 and Figure S5.

Gene diversity at quality and adaptability related genes

The section refers to the extent of allelic variation present in genes that control ability of a genetic stock to thrive under specific environments (adaptability) and determine its end‐use (quality) traits, besides the key traits linked to the domestication. Additionally, we analyzed the allele frequencies of these variants within the 3K RGP panel which served as a benchmark to assign population grouping of our germplasm. Kashmir rice landraces (KL) harbored rich allelic diversity at key adaptability genes such as CTB4a, HAN1, Hd1, etc, reflecting selection under cold temperate environments and were characterized for unique haplotypes at quality loci Wx, ALK, and BADH2, underpinning their distinct cooking quality and aroma (Table 2; Table S11).

Waxy (granule‐bound starch synthase)

Among the individuals in a population, Wxin appeared as the most frequent haplotype (29), followed by Wxlv (23), Wxa (20), Wxb (18), and Wxla (2). Among the KLs, such as Mushq Budji and Zag, Wxin was the most prevalent, followed by Wxlv , Wxa , and Wxb . In contrast, the MV carried Wxa , Wxb , and Wxin alleles. Premium quality, Pusa Basmati 1121 and Koshihikari, were found to harbor the Wxin allele. Haplotype analysis of the Wx identified 19 distinct haplotypes. Here, most of the KL belonged to Hap_3; however, specific landraces such as Tral Zaged and Loul Anzul carried Hap_7, while Ram Hall uniquely harbored Hap_17. Additionally, we identified a unique haplotype (GACT) in genotype ABSRD. This variant appears to be a rare allele, as it was absent from the comprehensive 3K RGP panel and has not been previously characterized. As such, six haplotypes were identified in 3K RGP, of which, Wxlv , Wxin , Wxa , and Wxb were found to be the most common haplotypes. The ancestral Wxlv allele was predominant in Aus and Ind2. In contrast, the Wxin allele was the most frequent in Aro and tropical japonica (Trj) sub‐populations, while Wxa was primarily associated with the Ind1A. The Wxb allele was predominantly found in Tej. Notably, the rare Wxla allele was specifically detected within Tej, while Wxop was exclusively present in Indx and Ind3 (Figure 3; Table S12).

Sequence level polymorphism for Wx (granule‐bound starch synthase [GBSS]) locus.

Aroma

The BADH2 locus on chromosome 8 codes for betaine aldehyde dehydrogenase. Mutations at different positions (Exons 2, 7, and 8) within this locus have been reported to cause truncated protein. The materials were scanned for 7 bp deletion in Exon 2, the 8 bp deletion in Exon 7, and the 7 bp insertion in Exon 8. The resultant loss‐of‐function on any one of these loci is expected to result in the accumulation of 2‐acetyl‐1‐pyrroline, the principal compound responsible for fragrance in rice. Of the three variations, none of the accessions carried mutations in Exon 2 or Exon 8; however, the 8 bp deletion in Exon 7 was detected in 15 genotypes, with seven other genotypes carrying this mutation in the heterozygous state. Among the landraces, fragrance allele was identified exclusively in Mushq Budji, Tral Zagad, and Kew. The variant was also detected in several well‐known scented varieties, including Pusa Basmati 1121, Type 3 Basmati, Ranbir Basmati, Taraori Basmati, Jasmine, Katarni Rice, and Shalimar Sugandh 1. Within 3K RGP panel, the deletion was found to be prevalent in the aromatic group (34.21%), followed by Adm (admixture) (9.71%), Trj (7.53%), Ind3 (7.37%), and subtropical japonica (4.46%). The deletion was mostly absent in the Aus and Ind1A groups and had limited frequency other groups (Figure 4).

Sequence level polymorphism for Badh2 locus.

Alkali reaction

At Alk locus, 9 bp insertion/deletion (InDel) located inside the promoter region (−741 bp) and three SNPs (G/A)Ex8‐733, (G/T)Ex8‐864, and (C/T)Ex8‐865 were screened across 100 RG‐SKUAST‐K. Most of the accessions (77) carried the Type‐I promoter matching to the reference, 26 accessions exhibited an alternate 9 bp deletion (Type‐II promoter). Except Kew, all the landraces from Kashmir valley carried Type I promoter. Among the MV, five carried the Type I promoter and five had the Type II promoter. The Type‐I/ Type‐II polymorphism along with exon 8 variation caused five distinct haplotypes, which we designate as: Hap‐1 (Type‐I + GGC), Hap‐2 (Type‐I + GTT), Hap‐3 (Type‐I + AGC), Hap‐4 (Type‐II+ GGC) and Hap‐5 (Type‐II + GTT). The majority of the accessions carried Hap‐1 (43) followed by Hap‐4 (18), Hap‐2 (17), Hap‐3 (3), and Hap‐5 (1). KL mostly carried Hap‐1 for 15 accessions, followed by Hap‐4 and Hap‐3 with two and one accession, respectively. In contrast, Hap‐4 occurred in five MV. Haplotype analysis identified a total of 28 haplotypes out of which KLs carry Hap_1 and Hap_6, with the exception of begum that harbored the unique Hap_13. In the 3K RGP, four haplotypes were identified for ALK loci with Hap‐1 being the most common (42.63%), followed by Hap‐2 (28.24%), Hap‐4 (24.86%), and Hap‐3 (4.27%). Hap‐1 predominated in Trj and Ind2; Hap‐2 in subtropical japonica, Tej, and Ind1B; while Hap‐3 was specific to Tej and japx. Hap‐4 was mainly found in Aus and to a lesser extent, in Ind1A and Aro subgroups (Table S13; Figure S6A).

Red pericarp

The red pericarp in rice is a wild trait that is predominantly controlled by a functional Rc allele. We screened accessions specifically for the presence or absence of the 14‐bp deletion in Rc gene that controls brown pericarp trait. Among the 115 accessions analyzed, 18 carried the wild, functional Rc allele and two were heterozygous. Several well‐known landraces and varieties such as Gobindobhog, Ranbir Basmati, Type 3 Basmati, Black Rice, and Jasmine also harbored this ancestral allele. Among Kashmir‐based cultivars, the landrace Zag and Sig Safed retained the functional (wild) Rc allele, whereas among the MV, only SR‐8 possessed this allele. Among the 3K RGP accessions, the wild Rc allele was present across all sub‐populations except Ind1B, where it was completely absent. Aus uniquely exhibited the wild allele in approximately 63% of its accessions, which occurred at low frequency in admixtures (20%), Ind2 (17%), and subtropical japonica (12%), and rarely in Tej and Aro sub‐populations (Figure S6B).

Grain size

The GS3, a major quantitative trait locus influencing grain length in rice, is characterized by a C to A substitution in the second exon. Here 88 accessions carried the C (shorter grain), against 13 accessions with A allele (longer grain), which included, Pusa Basmati 1121, Type 3 Basmati, and Ranbir Basmati. KL mostly possessed the C allele; however, certain MV of Kashmir Valley such as SR‐3, SR‐7, SR‐8, and Shalimar Sugandh 1 were found to contain the A allele. In the 3K RGP panel, the 61% of accessions contained C allele (confined to Ind1B, Subtropical japonica, and Trj) against A in 39% that predominated in Ind1A, Aus, Ind2, Tej, and Aro sub‐populations (Figure S6C).

GW8 (OsSPL16) manifests itself through a 10‐bp deletion in the promoter region and controls grain width. This loss‐of‐function allele is present in Basmati rice, where it confers a fine‐grained phenotype. Interestingly, 17 out of 20 (Jap) landraces harbored the mutant allele while GS 22 and GS 28 contained the wild allele. Among the MV, Shalimar Sugandh 1, SR‐1, SR‐4, SR‐7, and K‐39 contained the wild type allele. Among the Basmati, Pusa Basmati 1121, Type 3 Basmati, and Ranbir Basmati contained the 10 bp deletion while Taraori Basmati contained wild type alleles for GS3 and GW8. Within the 3K RGP, the mutant allele occurred at very high frequency exceeding 90% in aromatic, Tej and Trj groups. In contrast, the wild‐type allele was retained more than 40% of accessions within Ind1A, Ind2, and Ind3 (Figure S6D).

Shattering

SH4 and qSH1 loci, the two well studied loci were assayed on the germplasm panel. At SH4, two functional SNPs (G/T237 and CT760) produced the haplotypes linked to shattering (GC) and non‐shattering (GT and TC) trait. Eight 5 genotypes were detected with TC haplotype, while the ancestral GC was detected in K‐39 and GS‐181. The locus qSH1, characterized by a strong functional SNP (G/T), had G (shattering) for 88 and T (non‐shattering) with 17 accessions. Taken together, based on both the genes, SH4–qSH1, the accessions were grouped into classes, C1 (SH4[TC]–qSH1[T/T]), C2 (SH4[TC]–qSH1[G/G]), and C3 (SH4[GC]–qSH1[G/G]), distributed among 11, 65, and 1 accession, respectively. The expected nature of resistance to shattering would be expected in an order of C1 > C2 > C3. In 3K RGP, no variation was found at CT760, while in case of qSH1, the T allele was exclusively present in the Tej (28.32%), Japx (17.5%), and Adm (5.88%).

Heading date

The functional variations at heading date loci, Hd1, Hd4, and Hd5 were screened. At Hd1, three previously reported deletions in exon 1 (43 bp) and exon 2 (2 and 4 bp), which are associated with dysfunctional protein, were found in varying proportions across the panel. At exon 1, 101 accessions carried the functional allele, while seven accessions carried the deletion. The 43‐bp InDel, which is distributed as a minor allele in Tej (13.24%) and Japx (4.88%) (3K RGP), was absent in the current set of KL but was observed in the well‐known typical Tej cultivar Koshihikari. At the 4‐bp deletion locus, 96 accessions carried the functional allele, while 12 accessions carried the deletion, besides other six heterozygous lines. The 4‐bp InDel, a predominant allele in the Ind2 subpopulation (3K RGP), was found in our panel in case of ABSRD, Chenab, Daggu, Heera, IRBLz5, Taraori Basmati, and Shalimar Sugandh 1. Similarly, the 2‐bp deletion was identified in five accessions while in another 106 lines, it carried the wild allele, specifically present in Bansali, Milyang‐108, Jasmine, and SR‐4. Meanwhile, the 2‐bp deletion was present as the predominant allele in Trj and Aus (3K RGP). At Hd4 locus, analysis for G/T substitution in exon 1, revealed that 92 accessions (including all KL) carried the functional (G) allele within exon 1. In contrast, five accessions carried the truncated stop codon (TAG) (SR‐5, SR‐9, K‐332, GSR 67, and Shalimar Sugandh 1). While this stop codon is specifically present as a rare allele (1.85%) in Tej subpopulation in 3K RGP and completely absent in rest of the sub‐populations. At Hd5 locus, 81 accessions carried the functional allele, whereas 22 accessions exhibited a 1‐bp deletion causing disrupted open reading frame. Among KL the deletion was observed in Ram Hall and showed heterozygosity in Kaw Kreer and Safed Khuch. Among the MV, the deletion was found in SR‐1, SR‐3, SR‐4, and SR‐7. Among others it was present in Milyang‐108, Chade China, Ginmasarie, Heera, and IRBL9W. While the distribution of this 1 bp deletion was not clear in 3K RGP, the 1 bp deletion was specifically present in Ind1A (6.73%), Ind1B (5.37%), Indx (2.77%), and Japx (1.22%).

At Hd1, several accessions carried unique haplotypes for this locus, specifically Kamad (Hap_44), Kaw Kreer (Hap_45), Loul Anzul (Hap_46), Maghate (Hap_47), Ram Hall (Hap_61), Sig Safed (Hap_71), Tumla Hall (Hap_75), and Zaged (Hap_77). For Hd4, most of the KL accessions clustered into Hap_1, while Kew, Begum, and GS‐28 carried Hap_9, Hap_15, and Hap_17, respectively. Finally, for Hd5, the majority of the accessions were distributed between Hap_1 and Hap_5 (Tables S14–S16).

Awn

The awn trait in rice is predominantly regulated by the An‐1 and LABA1 genes. At the An‐1 locus, a single base‐pair deletion in the second exon leads to a frameshift, generating a truncated protein that confers the awnless phenotype. This 1‐bp deletion was identified in 13 accessions, while in another seven it occurred in a heterozygous state. In 3K RGP, 1‐bp deletion in the An‐1 gene was predominantly associated with Ind2 and Ind3 (∼60%). In contrast, the wild‐type allele was mostly fixed in the Tej, Trj, and Aro groups, suggestive of a trait linked to domestication event.

For LABA1, which is associated with the development of long and barbed awns, two distinct mutations were observed. A 1‐bp insertion in exon 1, leading to a premature stop codon and second, a less frequent Aus‐specific G‐to‐T substitution that conditions a barbless phenotype. The 1‐bp deletion in LABA1 was detected in 34 accessions, whereas 12 accessions were heterozygous. Among the KL, this deletion was observed only in Kew, GS‐28, and Zaged, while within the MV it was more prevalent with SR‐2, SR‐4, SR‐7, SR‐8, and Shalimar Sugandh 1. The Aus‐specific G‐to‐T substitution was comparatively rare and was limited to Bansali, C 12024, Sig Safed, GS‐539, Jasmine, PRC1, and SR‐1.

Tiller angle

In cultivated Asian rice, mutations at key loci like, PROG1 played a central role in shift from a prostrate to an erect growth habit. The PROG1 mutation is fixed across domesticated rice varieties, reflecting its importance in early domestication. Additionally, genes TIG1 and TAC1 have contributed to the development of a more compact plant architecture. KL (alongside Pusa Basmati 1121 and Koshihikari) and MV (SR‐1, SR‐2, SR‐4, and SR‐7) carried G and A alleles, respectively at TAC1 locus, where the mutant G allele drives an erect, compact phenotype via reduced gene expression while the ancestral A allele maintains a wider, spread‐out tiller angle. Overall, 70 accessions carried the G allele and 33 carried A allele. Across the various sub‐populations of the 3K RGP panel, all accessions within the Tej group carried the G allele, while the aromatic subgroup carried this allele in approximately 88% of its accessions. In contrast, the Ind and Aus predominantly carried the A allele. At the TIG1 locus, SNPs in the promoter region define two predominant haplotypes: ACC, considered the strong allele and GTT, regarded as the weak allele associated with the erect tiller phenotype. Among the KL, most of the accessions carried the ACC haplotype, except for GS‐28. Additionally, Safed Khuch exhibited a rare ATC haplotype. In MV, SR‐1, SR‐2, SR‐4, SR‐7, and K‐39 carried the GTT, while the remaining varieties possessed the ACC haplotype. Genotypes such as Black Rice, PRC1, Kew, and GS‐289 possessed the erect phenotype alleles at both the loci. Overall, 23 accessions carried the GTT haplotype, 77 carried ACC, and few accessions showed heterozygosity at one or more sites. Within 3K RGP panel, the Adm, Aro, Aus, Ind3, and Tej groups predominantly carried the ACC haplotype. In contrast, the Ind1A and Ind1B mainly contained the GTT. The Ind2 and Indx groups also exhibited a high frequency of the GTT haplotype, present in approximately 60% of their accessions (Figure S7).

Gn1a

Grain number per panicle is a critical determinant of rice yield. A 16 bp deletion in the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) of this gene plays a key regulatory role in increasing grain count and was identified in 31 accessions, while seven others were found to be heterozygous. KL mostly harbored the wild‐type allele, except for Kew and GS‐28, with the deletion. In contrast, the deletion was prevalent among MV, SR1, SR2, SR4, and Shalimar Sugandh 1. While identifying the distribution of this 16 bp deletion in the 5′ UTR of the Gn1a gene, both IRRI‐SNP‐seek and RiceVarMap suggested that the Aro and japonica (Tej and Trj) lineages predominantly carried the wild‐type allele (96%–99%).

Cold tolerance

Despite its tropical and subtropical origin, rice has adapted to cold environments by consolidating on the genetic variation inherited from its wild progenitors through de novo mutations to shape CT‐specific alleles. We screened the causal loci of five key CT genes known to confer tolerance against cold stress: HAN1 (A/G), CTB4a (C/G), COLD1 (T/C/A), bZIP73 (G/A), and COLD6 (CTC6 or CTC7 repeats). Among these loci, the favorable alleles associated with enhanced CT were identified as, A, G, A, G, and CTC7, respectively. Most of the KL besides Koshihikari carried the CT‐specific alleles for all the five genes. In a few other landraces such as Zag, Tral Zag, Kaw Kreer, and GS‐60, alternate alleles were present at only one or two loci. Among MV, K‐332, SR‐9, and Kohsar possessed four to five favorable alleles, whereas varieties including Shalimar Sugandh 1, SR‐1, SR‐4, and SR‐7 carried alternate alleles.

For the CTB4a locus, the majority of the KL belonged to Hap_2; however, Mehvan and Ram Hall carried Hap_4 and Hap_5, respectively. Similarly, for HAN1, most of the KL accessions clustered into Hap_1, while unique variants were observed in Sig Safed, Ram Hall, Begum, and Mushkandi I, which harbored Hap_11, Hap_14, Hap_16, and Hap_22, respectively. Analysis of bZIP73 showed that while the majority of the panel clustered in Hap_3, Tumla Hall and Zag were found in Hap_1; Ram Hall, Mehvan, and Zaged in Hap_4; and Kaw Kreer in Hap_6. Finally, for the COLD1 locus, the predominant group was Hap_1, whereas Sig Safed, Ram Hall, and Kaw Kreer carried Hap_6 (Tables S17–S20). The allelic distribution of the five loci across the corresponding genes was evaluated within the 3K RGP panel. For the COLD1 gene, a triallelic SNP exhibits a distinct distribution pattern across various sub‐populations. The A allele occurred predominantly (>94%) in Tej. In contrast, the C allele was most common in the Aus group, while the remaining sub‐populations largely carried the T allele. For COLD6, the CTC7 represented the major allele across sub‐populations, though its frequency varied. It was especially enriched in Tej and Aro groups, reaching 100% frequency in Tej. In Ind and Aus populations, the CTC6 occurred at a considerable frequency. In case of bZIP73, the SNP clearly separated the rice subgroups into two distinct classes: the G allele was consistently present across all japonica subgroups, whereas the A allele predominated in Ind, Aus, and Aro populations. For CTB4a, the functional mutation exhibited a sparse distribution across the 3K RGP sub‐populations. It was entirely absent in all Ind groups but occurred specifically within Jap populations, where its frequency ranged from 3.57% to 26.16%, with the highest occurrence in Tej. In the Aro subgroup, the mutation was also detected, though at a relatively low frequency of around 6.58%. Overall, the local KL carried predominant G allele in 89.5% of the accessions. In contrast, this allele was exceedingly rare in the 3K RGP subset of Indian subcontinent, where it was detected in only 16 of 743 accessions (2.2%) against dominant C allele. Among East‐Asian germplasm, the G allele was found with intermediate frequencies in Korea (35.7%; 15/42) and Japan (34.0%; 18/53), although was relatively uncommon in China (8.8%; 41/465).

Similarly, in case of HAN1, mutation was highly enriched in Tej (84.75%) and present at a moderate frequency in the Japx group (28.57%). It appeared only sparsely in Trj, aromatic sub‐populations and was completely absent in Ind, Aus, and subtropical japonica. In Kashmir germplasm, the A allele was the predominant variant, occurring in 16 of 19 accessions (84.2%), indicating a high prevalence of this cold‐adaptive allele in local KL. In contrast, the A allele was rare (1.8%, 10/568) in the Indian subcontinent, against G allele. In temperate East‐Asian germplasm, A allele occurred at high frequencies in Korea (69.0%; 29/42) and Japan (62.3%; 33/53) and at an intermediate frequency in China (20.0%; 90/450). The markedly higher frequency of the CT specific alleles for the genes HAN1 and CTB4a in Kashmir as compared to that of broader Indian rice gene pool, suggests a significant enrichment of alleles associated with adaptation to cold temperate environments (Tables S21 and S22; Figure 5; Figure S8).

Sequence level polymorphism for genes related to cold tolerance.

DISCUSSION

This study reveals a robust and consistent bipartite genetic structure across the “100‐RGS” rice germplasm set maintained at SKUAST‐K, generated using moderate to higher PIC SNP panels (S25, S50, and S18K), while the third cluster (S10) detected using low‐PIC SNPs reflected limited marker resolution rather than true biological subdivision. The increasing proportion of admixed genotypes and refinement of assignments with differentiating SNPs in our study justifies the importance of robust markers for accurately capturing complex ancestry and historical recombination (Goli et al., 2024; Resutik et al., 2026). The stability of cluster membership for several accessions mean genetically coherent lineages, whereas shifts from pure to admixed status under more resolving marker panels reflect mosaic genomes shaped by gene flow and breeding history (Arnold & Kunte, 2017). Aromatic genotypes, which formed a distinct group only under S10, were redistributed into the two main clusters or admixed categories under higher densities, supporting their intermediate and admixed origin rather than an independent lineage (Roy et al., 2020). Since the population structure analysis was performed uniformly on the same set of accessions, it appeared that the low‐PIC markers provided limited power to resolve ancestry and genetic relationships, resulting in less stable clustering and the emergence of an additional cluster. In contrast, intermediate‐ and high‐PIC marker panels consistently recovered the two major genetic groups and identified a proportion of admixed individuals, demonstrating that marker informativeness strongly influences the accuracy and resolution of population structure inference. Low‐PIC markers are associated with rare alleles and eventually classify the whole population for specific haplotypes (with a greater number of classes), therefore, may not account for the overall population sub‐structure. In our case, SNPs with AF ≥ 0.4 provided substantially greater discriminatory power for population structure inference compared to the SNPs with lower AF thresholds because they maximize the expected heterozygosity and PIC. Consequently, the high‐AF SNP panel produced more stable ancestry coefficients, clearer separation of genetic groups, and consistent resolution of the bipartite population structure. Here it may be noted that AF thresholds are usually used to defeat the introduction of wrong calls in the database and for few other purposes, we used it for filtering out the more discriminatory markers sets. The similar studies, though with different objectives and methodology have been performed by Berner (2019) and Linck and Battey (2019). The S18K‐based analysis confirmed two genetically distinct sub‐populations with moderate nucleotide divergence and low admixture parameter, suggesting maintained differentiation despite historical gene flow. The higher expected heterozygosity in S‐II points to a broader genetic base, making it a valuable reservoir of diversity for trait improvement.

AMOVA showed that most variation resides within sub‐populations, with moderate ΦST between groups, a pattern typical of rice and indicative of substantial standing variation that can be efficiently exploited through selection. At the same time, intergroup divergence would be sufficient to generate useful segregation and heterosis in crosses. PCoA independently validated the STRUCTURE results, clearly separating the two groups along the primary axis while revealing finer scale heterogeneity associated with aromatic types and locally adapted landraces along secondary axes. This dispersion reflects the combined effects of directional selection for quality traits and microenvironmental adaptation. The phylogenetic analysis mainly resolved Jap and Ind clusters. The Jap comprised of Japx and Tej and based on our previous studies (Najeeb et al., 2016) occupy mid and higher elevations of J&K region, respectively. In contrast, the Ind cluster comprised a diverse set of improved varieties, traditional genotypes, and breeding lines, carrying diverse geographic origins and breeding histories. Landraces which belong to intermediate/subtropical zones of J&K like Bansali, Daggu, and Giza‐14 grouped separately, alongside improved varieties, SR‐1, SR‐2, SR‐4, and SR‐7 (Figure 1). Few of the representative germplasm accessions and landraces are presented in Figure 2 and Figure S5. Overall, the germplasm comprises two major yet interconnected gene pools, shaped by divergence, admixture, and selection. This genetic architecture provides a strong framework for parent selection, heterotic grouping, and conservation.

Genome‐wide estimates of Tajima's D revealed contrasting patterns among the inferred genetic groups with Tajima's D value (+1.64), indicating an excess of intermediate‐frequency alleles relative to neutral expectations. Such a pattern is consistent with the effects of historical bottlenecks, population subdivision, or balancing selection. The corresponding values for individual sub‐populations S‐I, S‐II, and admixture cluster were +2.55, −0.49, and +2.47, respectively. The negative Tajima's D suggested a modest excess of rare alleles and a demographic history closer to neutrality, potentially reflecting mild population expansion or purifying selection. The high positive Tajima's D value, accompanied by the high nucleotide diversity is likely attributable to (historic) gene flow between genetically differentiated groups, which increases heterozygosity and the frequency of intermediate‐frequency variants. Overall, the results indicate substantial differences in demographic history and evolutionary processes among the identified genetic groups, with admixture and population structure contributing significantly to the observed genome‐wide variation. Among the individual bins, we discuss the windows centered at 27.75 and 28.00 Mb on chromosome 11 (encompassing the Pik resistance‐gene cluster) that exhibited Tajima's D values of 2.74 and 2.10, respectively. This region harbors the Pik locus and its allelic variants, including Pikh, Pikm, and Pikp, which confer resistance to Magnaporthe oryzae. The elevated Tajima's D values indicate a marked excess of intermediate‐frequency alleles and are consistent with the maintenance of multiple resistance haplotypes through balancing or diversifying selection. The coincidence of strong Tajima's D signals with a major blast‐resistance gene cluster suggests that pathogen‐mediated selection may have contributed to the evolutionary dynamics of this genomic region. Also, the strongly negative Tajima's D values of −1.84 and −2.11 were observed in the Chr1: 16.75–17.00 Mb and Chr6: 18.25–18.50 Mb intervals, which suggested an excess of rare alleles consistent with a recent selective sweep or directional selection. Although demographic processes and population structure may contribute to the local patterns of variation, the presence of the domestication‐associated gene OsSPL14 and Hd1 support the possibility that this region has experienced selection during rice improvement. The presence of two distinct sub‐populations (S‐I and S‐II), an admixed group, high positive Tajima's D in the admixed cluster (+2.47), strongly suggests that population structure and admixture are major contributors to the elevated genome‐wide Tajima's D, although, balancing selection may contribute to the maintenance of intermediate‐frequency alleles at the specific loci.

Tracing genetic signatures and a link to archaeobotanical evidence of origin of rice in Kashmir

Rice domestication from wild ancestors such as Oryza rufipogon and Oryza perennis has been explained through monophyletic (Sweeney & McCouch, 2007) and polyphyletic theories (Cheng et al., 2003; Jing et al., 2023). The japonica rice was initially domesticated in China around 9000 years ago (Alam et al., 2025), while South Asia concurrently developed an independent relationship with native wild lineages, moving toward a proto‐indica cultivation phase by 5000 BC (Silva et al., 2018). Subsequently, the evolution has been conditioned by acquiring novel mutations after domestication, the event that favored better adaptability in new agroecosystems. As an example, Tej is reported to have originated from native Japonica (Gutaker et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Mao et al., 2019). The earliest agricultural economy of the Valley has been dominated by West‐Asian cereals such as wheat, barley, and lentil. Contrarily, the introduction of rice into Kashmir has been a part of a broader trans‐Himalayan cultural exchange and support a primary link to Chinese cultural influence, specifically from Longshan cultural zone of China. Palaeobotanical analyses from Gufkral demonstrate the earliest (1700 BC) clear evidence for rice cultivation in Kashmir by Sharma (1982). This Neolithic settlement layers confirm that no wild ancestors of rice are native to Kashmir, therefore, suggest of a foreign introduction. At Burzahom, rice appears later, around 1000 BC, in Megalithic strata (Bandey, 2009). Rice during its initial introduction constituted only around 9.11% of all the cereals, although wheat and barley had been of major importance (Bandey, 2009) as the staggered appearance across sites suggests a progressive and uneven adoption pattern. South Asian archaeobotanical studies point toward japonica‐type rice grains, which were domesticated in East‐Asia and were being present in the Kashmir Valley (Pokharia et al., 2017). They demonstrated that Kashmir was a convergence zone where West Asian crops (wheat, barley) and East Asian crops (millets, rice) coexisted by the mid‐Holocene. Such morphological affinities further reinforce east‐to‐west diffusion pathways. Millet like, Panicum miliaceum and Setaria italica, domesticated in northern China, also appear in Kashmir around 2000 BC, paralleling the rice introduction and strengthening the case for a shared transmission route. Yatoo et al. (2020) provided radiocarbon‐supported evidence of millet cultivation in the Valley that strengthens the argument for East Asian crop influences, since broomcorn millet and foxtail millet were domesticated in North China. As per Fonia (1972), rice became a fully integrated component of Kashmir's agrarian system only by 1000 BC. While later Indo‐Gangetic influences have been reported to have contributed to its expansion during the Megalithic period (2000–1700 BC).

However, the origins and pathways of rice diffusion into Kashmir remain elusive, as no direct accelerator mass spectrometry dating of rice grains and phytoliths has been carried out, leaving the chronology broad and incomplete. The morphometric and biological identity of early Kashmir rice also remains unresolved to determine whether it belongs to japonica (East Asian) or Ind (South Asian) lineages. We highlight that given the estimated age of the CTB4a mutation (∼3200 years ago), it is plausible that rice cultivation in Kashmir happened sometime after 1500 BCE, corresponding to the period of japonica expansion into temperate regions. Wx haplotypes and quality‐related alleles further support a japonica affinity. Taken together, our results provide a molecular framework for understanding the timeline and dispersal routes of rice introduction into the Kashmir Valley that has been discussed about in the literature on existing historical models.

Evidence from CT‐specific genes CTB4a and HAN1

In the present study, we specifically examined the loci that enabled native japonica rice to adapt to cooler environments (of East Asia) during the 4.2 k event, a period marked by a global temperature decline following the Holocene Climatic optimum around 4200 years BP (Gutaker et al., 2020). It was reported that the increased frequency of CT‐favorable SNPs in HAN1 and CTB4a coincide with the inferred evolutionary time‐frame for the origin of Tej (Alam et al., 2025). These two genes CTB4a and HAN1 together have facilitated the expansion of japonica rice into high‐altitude and high‐latitude regions of North‐East Asia (Li et al., 2021; Mao et al., 2019). Therefore, the genes were analyzed for their allelic distribution across the regions spanning the temperate (Kashmir Valley) and sub‐tropical regions of India and Northern‐East Asia. Our previous study (Shikari et al., 2021) demonstrated that the native Kashmir‐based rice landraces (KL) clustered with few of the globally known japonica accessions. Presently, the comparative genomics of the genes related to quality and cold‐stress tolerance (CTB4a and HAN1) helped us to trace a global phylogenetic relationship and a precise ecological origin. The sequence level polymorphism within the genes underlying key adaptability and domestication related traits such as heading, CT, plant architecture, grain shape, shattering and quality, across KL, and the 3K RGP panels provided valuable inference on the high‐resolution population sub‐structure and the lineage of the local landraces of the region. The comparisons drawn across 3K RGP strongly suggest that KL except GS‐28, align with the Tej, with reference to the ancient promoter‐based mutations for the CT genes (Li et al., 2021; Mao et al., 2019). The high altitude varieties, such as K‐332, Kamad, Kohsar, SR‐5, and SR‐9, showed CT‐specific Tej allele, against the commercially bred varieties (MV) that have possibly borrowed the southward lineage from main‐land northern India, where‐from alternate alleles for grain type, starch quality, CT, and heading date have been received. Therefore, allele frequencies of the cold‐adaptive genes HAN1 and CTB4a helped us to predict the origin of KLs. Pertinently, in context of these results, Fuller et al. (2010) have argued that fully domesticated japonica rice reached Northwest India around 1900 BC from China. It travelled along an early precursor of the Silk Road alongside other East Asian crops (like millets). Using spatiotemporal regression modeling and the Fast Marching algorithm, Silva et al. (2018) showed that this “Inner Asia Mountain Corridor” route (the Northwest Indian route) has a 94.9% statistical likelihood of being the primary path that brought japonica rice into South Asia. Further, that the eastern zones of Indian subcontinent are heavily dominated by the indigenous Aus rice group (Fuller, 2011; Stevens et al., 2016; Travis et al., 2015), which is completely distinct from both indica and japonica (Choi et al., 2017; Travis et al., 2015). Critically, Aus rices lack the defining domestication traits inherited from japonica and happens to be different from northern and western Indian populations (Kovach et al., 2007; Sweeney et al., 2007), it is less probable that japonica utilized this north‐eastern trajectory to introduce its domestication genes to proto‐indica. The COLD1 gene harbors a triallelic SNP that exhibits distinct, subpopulation‐specific distribution patterns. The C allele predominates in the Aus sub‐population and appears as a minor variant in Ind2 and admixtures, specifically identified in the Bansali, Jasmine, PRC1, and C104‐PKT genotypes within our panel. Besides, the traces of favorable CT alleles present in sub‐tropical Indian germplasm emphasizes on downward moment of japonica alleles from north towards south (Table S21; Figures 5 and 6).

Loci underlying domestication‐related traits

Haplotype diversity at Wx locus was explained through the key causal variation in exons 2, 4, 5, 6, 10, and the regulatory region of intron 1. The polymorphisms were detected exclusively in Intron 1, Exon 6, and Exon 10. No variation was observed at the causal sites in Exons 4 (linked to opaque endosperm) and 5, since these loci have been originally characterized as artificial mutations (Mikami et al., 1999; Sato et al., 2002). Furthermore, the 23‐bp insertion in Exon 2 known to cause the wx (glutinous) phenotype (Mikami et al., 2008), was absent across all the accessions in 3K RGP and 100‐RGS. Based on our earlier analysis, KL were grouped as Tej (Shikari et al., 2021), presently they predominantly harbored the Wxin allele, a variant found in only ∼12% of global temperate accessions (3K RGP). Thus, it can be presumed that during the introduction of the crop, the Wxin genotype was established either by specific ancestral culinary preference or due to a random chance (founder effect). These KLs were more adapted to mid‐altitude regions in contrast to those that carry standard Tej Wxb allele (Ram Hall and GS 60) alongside MV like K‐332, Kohsar, SR‐5, and SR‐9 and inhabit high altitude mountains. A similar genetic disconnect is evident at the ALK locus. While Tej populations are typically defined by the H2 and H3 haplotypes, KL were dominated by H1 (found in 15 accessions), a haplotype primarily associated with Trj and Ind2 sub‐populations. The standard temperate signature (H3) was rare, appearing in only one landrace, Begum. Kew and GS‐28 stood out by exhibiting H4 haplotype, an allele predominant in Aus populations, rare in Trj, and virtually absent in the japonica. Interestingly, the frequency of this rare allele increased significantly in MV, appearing in five cultivars: SR‐2, SR‐3, SR‐4, SR‐7, and SR‐8. Meanwhile, the H2 was identified in K‐332 and SR‐5. This recurrent dominance of tropical‐like alleles (ALK H1 and Wxin ) divides our KL population in subtypes and correlates well with our previous studies (Naseer et al., 2021; Noor et al., 2025; Shikari et al., 2008).

Interestingly, GS3 and OsSPL16 which shape grain dimension characterize the uniqueness of KR at regional level. All the landraces carried the functional GS3 allele, a well‐established negative regulator of grain size (Fan et al., 2006), which explains their short‐grained classification (Noor et al., 2025). In contrast, the mutant OsSPL16 allele containing a 10‐bp promoter deletion (Wang et al., 2012), is a loss‐of‐function variant typically associated with slender grain type and improved appearance quality in Basmati rice, which does not correspond with the predominantly short, round grain type observed in KL. This suggests that grain width in these landraces is not primarily determined by OsSPL16 but is more explained by GS3, GS6, GW5, and GSE9, which are known to affect grain width in japonica rice (R. Chen et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2015; Weng et al., 2008).

The KL, Ram Hall, and GS‐60, besides, MV K‐332, SR‐5, and SR‐9, exhibited the non‐shattering T allele at shattering gene qSH1. The T at this locus was associated with Wxb allele. The KLs and MV in mid‐altitude regions depicted the presence of alternative alleles. At BADH2 gene, the 8‐bp deletion was identified in landraces Mushq Budji and Tral Zaged, the form found absent in Tej (3K RGP). For LABA1 gene, a 1‐bp deletion confers the short, barbless awn phenotype in Asian rice, however, the wild allele predominates in Tej and Aus sub‐populations and present in a considerable percentage in Ind2 population (3K RGP). Although, the Aus and Ind2 populations have a specific G‐to‐T SNP related to this phenotype (Hua et al., 2015), displaying a pattern of mutual exclusivity where the T allele functionally compensates for the absence of the deletion. This genetic pattern is reflected in the KL where most landraces carried the Tej allele, specific accessions namely, Bansali, C12024, Jasmine, and SR‐1 displayed the Aus‐specific T allele.

The core flowering‐associated genes Hd1, Hd4, and Hd5 are pivotal in conditioning the eco‐geographical adaptation of rice (Zhang et al., 2015) and mechanistically interact to form a functional protein complex that regulates the Ehd1–Hd3a/RFT1 pathway, which governs flowering initiation (Zong et al., 2021). Furthermore, these genes display significant pleiotropy, simultaneously influencing key agronomic traits such as plant height and grain yield (Zhang et al., 2015). Similarly, Hd4 (syn. Ghd7) carries a functional G‐to‐T substitution (Kim et al., 2018). This mutation is present specifically at a low frequency in the Tej population (3K RGP). In case of Hd5, the distribution of the functional 1‐bp deletion was initially unclear in the 3K RGP dataset due to missing data. However, genotype imputation in RiceVarMap revealed that these missing values largely corresponded to the deletion allele in indica populations, a pattern absent in japonica.

Likewise, for the traits such as tiller angle that have characterized the rice domestication, has been largely determined by the fixation of major genes like PROG1 in cultivated populations (Tan et al., 2008). Nevertheless, despite the widespread fixation of PROG1, the variation of tiller angle remains contingent upon other possible loci, modulated by gene regulation that often function distinctively across subspecies (Wang et al., 2022). For instance, in japonica rice, a compact plant architecture is typically associated with reduced TAC1 expression, whereas in Ind, a similar phenotype arises from decreased TIG1 expression (Yu et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2019). These genes underwent selection in specific subspecies, with TIG1 predominantly selected in Ind and TAC1 in japonica, further refining the structural traits favored during rice domestication. In the case of TAC1, a mutation in the 3′ splicing site of its 1.5‐kb intron specifically a change from AGGA to GGGA reduces TAC1 expression. This decrease leads to a more compact plant architecture, characterized by a tiller angle that approaches zero. In case of TIG1, variations in its promoter region contributed to the transition from the inclined tiller growth typical of wild rice to the erect tiller growth characteristic of domesticated Ind cultivars.

As KL are mostly japonica, expectedly they carried japonica‐specific mutant tac1 combined with a functional TIG1 allele. This mutual exclusivity was observed as a predominant pattern in our population. However, with some exceptions, some accessions harbored mutant or functional alleles at both the loci, which is likely to generate a varied spectrum of tiller angles. In 3K RGP, Aus and Ind3 subgroups, carry the strong alleles at both loci, which results in wider tiller angles (Zhang et al., 2019). Overall, PROG1, TIG1, and TAC1 are expected to shape overall plant architecture in an additive manner. Although, PROG1 is already fixed in cultivated rice, variation in tiller angle may be largely governed by the remaining two genes. The presence of weaker alleles of both TIG1 and TAC1 result in a highly compact plant architecture, whereas their stronger alleles lead to a wider tiller angle (Zhang et al., 2019). These insights suggest that modern varieties with compact architecture can be developed by selectively combining the weaker alleles of TIG1 and TAC1, offering a targeted approach for breeding an ideal plant type. In case of Indian landraces such as Gobindobhog, Taraori Basmati, Jeerakasala, Sonasal, and Katarni at TIG1 and TAC1 a distinct japonica signature was observed while the majority of loci in these landraces exhibited a typical Ind profile. Overall, analysis of the 3K RGP data identified distinct Tej‐specific alleles across several key functional loci such as Wx, qSH1, Hd1 (43 bp deletion), COLD1, CTB4a, HAN1, and LABA1 (1 bp deletion). Notably, these Tej specific signatures were found to be significantly well‐represented within the Japx population, compared to the tropical and subtropical japonica subgroups, suggesting a distinct genetic affinity between Japx and the Tej.

The re‐sequencing allowed us to finely resolve on the previously reported population structure based on KASP assay (Shikari et al., 2021). Taken together, the phylogenetic classification delineated the groups, Ind, Indx, Tej, Japx, Trj, Aro. The japonica cluster constituted a broader group comprising of few released varieties and local landraces. Pertinently, the KL from higher mountains (Tej) clustered little away from those from mid‐elevations (Tej/Japx). The nesting of Basmati types near to the broader japonica cluster reassures a closely aligned evolutionary origin with Tej backgrounds, while still maintaining their unique identity. Ind included the contributions mostly from Ind1B and Indx. Few of the loci underlying rice quality, and in particular the CT associated CTB4a and HAN1, suggest a strong affinity with Tej rice lineages that expanded into cold environments following the 4.2‐kiloyear climatic event. This study combines archaeobotanical evidence with population genomics to infer the probable diffusion of rice culture into the Kashmir Valley and draws the phylogenetic relationships of Kashmir rice germplasm with respect to the global rice diversity.
