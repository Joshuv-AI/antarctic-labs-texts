Temporal trends and future projections of fertility and birth rates in Georgia: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Nia Khachidze, Erik Eik Anda, Tinatin Manjavidze, Ingvild Hersoug Nedberg, Ingvild Fossgard Sandøy, Charlotta Rylander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to analyse temporal trends in fertility and birth rates, examine maternal characteristics and forecast future demographic changes in Georgia.DesignThis was a retrospective observational study using population-level data from the National Statistics Office of Georgia (Geostat) and the Georgian Birth Registry (GBR). Temporal trends were analysed using Prais-Winsten regression models and annual percentage changes (APCs) and future projections were generated using autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models.ParticipantsThe study included data on 543 662 live births retrieved from the Geostat for the period 2014–2024. Additionally, maternal characteristics were analysed for 366 684 births recorded in the GBR for the period 2017–2024. Selection criteria included all live births during the study period, with no specific exclusion criteria applied.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcome measures were trends in the total fertility rate (TFR) and crude birth rate (CBR) over the study period (2014–2024) and their projections for 2025–2034. Secondary outcome measures were concurrent changes in maternal characteristics, including maternal age, nationality, place of residence, region of residence and parity.ResultsBetween 2014 and 2024, Georgia’s TFR declined from 2.30 to 1.68 children per woman (APC=3.12%, 95% CI −3.65 to −2.59), and the CBR dropped from 16.3 to 10.7 births per 1000 population per year (APC=4.39%, 95% CI −4.63 to −4.15). Fertility rates decreased most significantly among women aged <20 (APC=−9.93%, 95% CI −10.72 to −9.13) and 20–24 years (APC=−6.41%, 95% CI −7.70 to −5.10). Advanced maternal age increased in both nulliparous (APC=3.65%, 95% CI 1.83 to 5.51) and multiparous women (APC=1.67%, 95% CI 1.12 to 2.22). ARIMA forecast based on current trends showed a continued decline in the TFR to 1.09 (95% CI 0.91 to 1.27) children per woman and the CBR to 7.07 (95% CI 5.97 to 8.16) births per 1000 population by 2034.ConclusionsGeorgia is undergoing a demographic transition characterised by declining fertility rates and delayed childbearing. Assuming the mechanisms underlying 2014–2024 data remain unchanged, projections indicate a continued decline in fertility and birth rates by 2034, which may pose significant social and economic challenges for the country. Further research is needed to explore the underlying factors driving these trends and to develop strategies to address the potential implications of this demographic shift.

Introduction

Fertility and birth rates are key indicators of demographic dynamics, influencing the economic and social development of societies. In recent decades, many major economies have witnessed declines in fertility and increased life expectancy, leading to population ageing.1 2 In most high-income countries, the total fertility rate (TFR) has dropped below the replacement level of 2.1 children per woman and globally, the TFR is projected to fall to this level by 2050.35 By mid-century, 151 countries are expected to have a TFR below the replacement threshold, rising to 183 nations by the end of the century.5 As economies grow, accompanied by increased life expectancy, higher education levels and urbanisation, a global decline in fertility becomes more likely.469 These changes in demography can reshape social norms, delay marriage and influence family roles, cultural values and economic factors like workforce composition, healthcare costs and wealth distribution.1012

Fertility rates across Europe have remained low for decades, ranging between 1.1 and 1.8.13 Italy and Spain are among the countries with the lowest fertility rates, with these rates remaining persistently low for nearly 50 years.13 Fertility decline is known to be driven by the shift to a wealthier, more educated and healthier society, where individuals have greater control over their lives and reproductive choices.2 Also, additional factors such as limited childcare in Italy have made it challenging for mothers to balance work and family, while high housing costs and career pressures in Spain have led to one of Europe’s highest childlessness rates.1416 Despite financial incentives to support families in both countries, these measures have had little success in reversing fertility declines.17 18 The COVID-19 pandemic further worsened the situation, as economic disruptions and health concerns discouraged family formation even more.18 19

Georgia, located in the South Caucasus region, is an upper-middle-income country with a population of approximately 3.7 million. Since gaining independence from the Soviet Union in 1991, the country has faced political instability, armed conflicts and economic collapse, resulting in widespread poverty and mass emigration. However, significant reforms in the 2000s helped stabilise the economy, improve infrastructure and promote democratic development. Along with these advancements, Georgia, like other countries in the South Caucasus and Eastern Europe, has experienced a steady decline in birth and fertility rates over recent decades.13 20

Georgia’s TFR has stayed below replacement level since 1992, with a brief rise above 2.1 during 2014–2016 before declining again.13 While it has been slightly higher than the TFRs of neighbouring Armenia and many European countries during the same period, it is still insufficient to sustain population replacement.13 Widely recognised factors contributing to declining fertility in Europe—such as economic development, increased women’s education, workforce participation and limited access to affordable childcare2124—may also have played a role in reducing fertility rates in Georgia.

Over the past three decades, many working-age Georgians have emigrated to Russia, Turkey and the European Union for better opportunities.25 This trend has persisted since the 1990s and has led to labour shortages, a shrinking pool of potential mothers and a growing reliance on remittances, which accounted for approximately 12% of Georgia’s GDP in 2024.13 26 Emigration has also contributed to the ageing of Georgia’s population, as younger individuals are more likely to leave, leaving behind an older demographic structure.27 Georgia’s demographic challenges are further intensified by the current turbulent political climate in the region.

This study aims to analyse temporal trends in fertility and birth rates in Georgia, examine changes in maternal characteristics such as nationality, place of residence and parity over time, and explore future projections of fertility and birth rates, while also discussing potential factors contributing to the observed fertility trends.

Materials and methods

Study population and data sources

To examine temporal trends in TFR, the age-specific fertility rates (ASFRs), crude birth rate (CBR) and maternal characteristics, we conducted a retrospective observational study. This study adhered to the guidelines of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) cohort reporting and the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) Statement and used the corresponding checklist.28 29 To calculate TFRs and CBR, data on annual live births, total population and population of women from 2014 to 2024 were obtained from the National Statistics Office of Georgia (Geostat).20 The number of live births totalled 543 662 from 1 January 2014 to 31 December 2024. Information about maternal characteristics was extracted from the national Georgian Birth Registry (GBR), which covered all childbirths among women of all age groups from 1 January 2017 to 31 December 2024, in a total of 366 684 live births.

The GBR was established in 2016, and healthcare service providers are legally mandated to digitally record information related to antenatal care (ANC), delivery services and postpartum care. The registry contains over 500 variables, including detailed information on parental characteristics, pregnancy history, ANC visits, intrapartum care and neonatal outcomes. A 2023 audit reported a delivery coverage of 99.7% since 2017. Further details on the implementation and structure of the GBR are available in a previously published paper.30

Mothers are registered in the GBR during their initial interaction with the maternal healthcare system, such as ANC visits, hospital admissions due to obstetric reasons or childbirth. The GBR does not include data on home births, which account for approximately 0.2% of all births, nor does it cover births occurring in the Russian-occupied territories of Abkhazia and Samachablo (located in Shida Kartli). However, some mothers from these regions cross the de facto border to give birth in Georgian-controlled areas, where they are subsequently registered in the GBR.

Variables

The TFR, expressed as children per woman per year, is defined as the average number of children that women would have at the end of their reproductive period (15–49 years) if they were subjected to the ASFRs of a given period throughout their reproductive years and if they were not subjected to mortality.31 The TFR was used to assess fertility levels from 2014 to 2024.

The ASFRs per 1000 women per year were calculated as the annual number of births to women of a specific age group divided by the population of women of the same age group during the same year. Five-year age groups were used for ASFR (15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–35, 35–39 and ≥40 years old). TFR was calculated by summing ASFRs weighted by the number of years (5 years) represented in the corresponding age group.32 A TFR of 1.3 or below is classified as “lowest-low”.33 To calculate the annual CBR, the number of live births each year was used as a numerator, and the total population for the corresponding year studied was the denominator. It was expressed per 1000 population per year.34

Information on maternal characteristics from the GBR for each year of the period 2017–2024 included nationality (Georgian, Azerbaijani, Armenian, other or missing); region of residence (Tbilisi, Abkhazia, Adjara, Guria, Imereti, Kakheti, Kvemo Kartli, Mtskheta-Mtianeti, Racha-Lechkhumi and Kvemo Svaneti, Samegrelo and Zemo Svaneti, Samtskhe-Javakheti, Shida Kartli, non-citizen (those whose region of residence is not recorded) or missing data); place of residence (urban, rural or missing data); parity (nulliparous, multiparous or missing data); average maternal age for nulliparous and all women; advanced maternal age (≥35 years old) in nulliparous and multiparous women were categorised and presented as proportions.

Statistical analysis

Temporal trend analyses of TFR, ASFRs, CBR, and maternal characteristics were carried out using the Prais-Winsten generalised linear regression model35 in the Time Series module of the Stata programme, V.18.0. To estimate the annual percentage change (APC), the outcome variables were log-transformed before analysis. The regression models were then estimated on the logarithmic scale, and the APC and its respective 95% CIs were calculated from the regression coefficients using the formula (exp⁡(β)-1)*100. If the APC was positive, the time series was interpreted as exhibiting an upward trend; if negative, a downward trend and if it was close to zero, it was considered stationary. To evaluate the assumption of constant proportional change, we performed diagnostic visual inspections by plotting the observed log-transformed values against the values predicted by the model for the total number of births, TFR, ASFRs and CBR. This approach allowed us to assess whether the log-linear functional form appropriately captured the underlying trends or if deviations indicated non-constant APCs.

From 2021 onward, maternal nationality became a mandatory field in the GBR. To account for this change in reporting completeness, we analysed nationality separately for the period 2021–2024.

The autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model was used to forecast TFR and CBR for an additional 10 years (2025–2034) based on the yearly rates from 2014 to 2024. The ARIMA model was introduced by Box and Jenkins in 1976,36 and it is a widely used statistical approach for analysing and forecasting non-seasonal stationary time series data. The ARIMA model is defined by three parameters: p, d and q, which represent the order of autoregression (AR), the degree of differencing (I) and the order of the moving average (MA), respectively. The AR component captures the relationship between a variable and its lagged values, while the MA component uses past forecast errors to predict future values. The integration (I) component is applied to transform non-stationary time-series data into a stationary state by eliminating trends through differencing.

The process of constructing an ARIMA model for forecasting TFR and CBR involved several steps. First, the stationarity of the time-series data was assessed using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller unit-root test. If the data were non-stationary, differencing was applied to stabilise the series. Second, the Autocorrelation Function and Partial Autocorrelation Function plots were analysed to determine the appropriate values for p and q. Third, candidate models were evaluated based on statistical criteria such as the Akaike and Bayesian Information Criterion, with the optimal model being the one that minimises these values. Model diagnostics were performed using the Ljung-Box test to ensure the residuals were white noise, indicating a good fit.

Once the optimal ARIMA model was identified, it was used to forecast the target variables. To validate the model’s performance, historical data were used to generate forecasts for a subset of years within the study period (online supplemental figures B1 and B2). These predicted values were then compared with the actual observed values for the same years, ensuring the model’s accuracy and reliability before applying it to forecast future projections.

It is important to note that the ARIMA model is a univariate approach that relies solely on the historical values of the target variable for forecasting. As such, it assumes that past conditions, such as policy, behaviour, economic factors and other dominant drivers of fertility, will remain stable over the forecast period. The forecasts generated by the ARIMA model should therefore be interpreted as conditional projections, contingent on the assumption that no significant changes occur in these external factors.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

During the period from 2014 to 2024, a total of 543 662 live births were registered in Georgia, with the number of births exhibiting an annual declining trend of 4.43% (95% CI −4.67 to −4.20) (table 1). A similar declining trend was observed in the TFR, dropping from 2.30 in 2014 to 1.68 children per woman in 2024, resulting in an annual decline of 3.12% (95% CI −3.65 to −2.59) and 4.39% (95% CI −4.63 to −4.15) for CBR, respectively.

Between 2014 and 2024, the TFR steadily declined with a more pronounced drop in 2023 (figure 1). The ASFR declined most in the age groups <20 years (APC=−9.93%, 95% CI −10.72 to −9.13) and 20–24 years (APC=−6.41%, 95% CI −7.70 to −5.10) (table 1). During 2017–2024, there were no notable changes in the place of residence of mothers giving birth in Georgia; approximately 72% resided in urban and 28% in rural areas (table 2). Missing values for residence also remained stationary.

Regionally, Tbilisi accounted for about one-third of births during 2017–2024, and the temporal trend was stable (APC=−0.44%, 95% CI −1.13 to 0.26). The proportions of births in Adjara in western Georgia increased by 1.56% annually (95% CI 0.96 to 2.17). Several other regions showed declining proportions of births during this 8-year time period: Racha-Lechkhumi and Kvemo Svaneti, Samegrelo–Zemo Svaneti, Samtskhe–Javakheti and Shida Kartli. The share of Abkhazians and non-citizens was inconclusive, while birth proportions in other regions stayed relatively stable over time.

The distribution of nulliparous and multiparous mothers remained stable during 2017–2024. The average maternal age at the time of birth increased from 26.0 years in 2017 to 27.6 years in 2024 (APC=0.99%, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.11) for nulliparous women and from 28.0 years to 29.8 years (APC=0.88%, 95% CI 0.81 to 0.96) for all women.

Advanced maternal age increased by 3.65% (95% CI 1.83 to 5.51) annually among nulliparous women and by 1.67% (95% CI 1.12 to 2.22) annually among multiparous women from 2017 onwards.

Diagnostic inspections of the log-transformed fertility indicators revealed varying degrees of model fit (online supplemental figures A1–A9). Diagnostic plots showed that while total births, CBR and fertility rate among adolescents followed a steady proportional decline, the TFR displayed deviations from the fitted values around the pandemic period in 2021, and ASFRs for women aged 20+ exhibited annual fluctuations around the fitted line. Although the models captured the overall trends, such as declines in the 20–24, 25–29, 30–34 age groups, and increases in those aged 35–39 and 40+, the observed volatility indicates that the rate of change was not constant year to year for these specific age groups.

The ARIMA forecast, based on trends observed during 2014–2024, predicted continued decline in both the TFR and CBR during 2025–2034 (figures2 3). If the conditions driving the TFR decline during the study period remain unchanged, it is predicted that the TFR will decrease from 1.68 (95% CI 1.60 to 1.76) children per woman in 2025 to 1.09 (95% CI 0.91 to 1.27) children per woman in 2034. Similarly, if the factors influencing the CBR decline remain stable, the CBR is forecasted to drop from 10.14 (95% CI 9.67 to 10.60) births per 1000 population in 2025 to 7.07 (95% CI 5.97 to 8.16) births per 1000 population in 2034. Discussion

Our findings reveal a consistent decline in TFR and CBR in Georgia from 2014 to 2024, resulting in a TFR of 1.7 children per woman and a CBR of 10.7 children per 1000 population in 2024. ARIMA models forecast a continued decline in both TFR and CBR in Georgia over the next decade, projecting a drop to 1.09 children per woman and 7.07 births per 1000 population by 2034. This trend mirrors the declining trend in many European nations, where TFRs have remained below the replacement level of 2.1 since the 1970s and 1980s.13

We observed that the TFR decline was heavily influenced by the steep fertility decline in mothers aged <25 years, while there was a slight increase in fertility rates among women aged ≥35 years. Interestingly, the proportion of women with advanced maternal age (≥35 years) increased for both nulliparous and multiparous women, clearly suggesting a fertility shift to older ages. In many countries, the average age for first-time mothers has risen by two to four years over the last few decades and is now exceeding 30 years of age.37 Similarly, our study found that over the past 8 years, the average age of childbirth for Georgian mothers increased by approximately 2 years, reaching 28 years in first-time mothers, and almost 30 years in all mothers. According to the 2023 Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development family database, the average age of women at childbirth exceeds 32 years in several countries, including Italy, Greece and Spain.37 Since the mid-1990s, fertility rates among women in their 20s have steadily declined in Europe. In 1995, the highest fertility was observed among women aged 25–29, but by 2021, it had shifted to women aged 30–35.37

The decline in fertility rates in Georgia can be attributed to various interrelated factors, including the country’s economic development, increased educational attainment among women, their growing participation in the workforce and urbanisation, as shown in other countries.2124 Like many low- and middle-income countries, Georgia has experienced significant economic growth over the past decades. According to the World Bank, Georgia’s GDP grew by 9.4% in 2024.13 Additionally, GDP per capita (current US$) was approximately 4830 US$ in 2014 and 9194 US$ in 2024, a substantial increase from around 750 US$ in 2000.13 It is well-documented that there is a negative correlation between economic development and the TFR.21 38 A study by Cheng et al39 found that consistently low or high GDP per capita over the long term was associated with reduced TFR in most regions of the world. However, the study also identified a positive association between GDP per capita and TFR in Western European countries when the GDP per capita was close to the global average. The reduction in TFR is often explained by the idea that rising income levels lead parents to prioritise providing better education and care for each child.40 41 Hence, economic development could be one of the factors influencing the Georgian trend of declining TFR and CBR.

Additionally, Georgian women have made significant advancements in education, with female enrolment in higher education institutions increasing by 21% in 2024 compared with 2015.20 This aligns with the role of women’s education, particularly at the secondary level, as a key factor driving declining fertility trends.2 42

As of 2025, 62% of Georgia’s population resides in urban areas, which is a 5 percentage point increase compared with 2015, with one-third living in the capital, Tbilisi, making it the city with the largest population in the country.20 Progressive urbanisation significantly influences fertility patterns, as people increasingly move to urban areas in search of better access to education, healthcare and employment opportunities, often leading to delayed marriage and childbearing. The higher cost of living in cities and the shift towards norms valuing smaller family size further contribute to declining fertility rates.24 43

Other factors affecting the TFR include female employment and childcare possibilities. In Georgia, female employment has risen by 11% in 2024 compared with 2014. Although Georgia’s Labour Code mandates that employers provide equal pay for equal work regardless of gender, a significant income gap persists.44 As of 2023, the adjusted gender pay gap is around 21%, highlighting the ongoing disparity.20 Better education and increased urbanisation naturally lead to greater job opportunities, resulting in more women entering the workforce and gaining better access to contraception.45 46 In addition, working mothers’ decisions to have children are greatly affected by their income and the country’s maternity leave policies. A systematic review by Thomas et al47 concluded that fertility rates are positively influenced by increased leave benefits, particularly when the benefits are substantial. Georgia’s Labour Code provides maternity leave of 183 calendar days (200 days in cases of complications or multiple births) and an additional 12 weeks of unpaid parental leave.44 However, while maternity leave reimbursement is mandatory for civil servants and state employees, private organisations—employing around 77% of the workforce20—are not required to provide paid leave, leading to unequal legal protections and disparities in access for female workers. Working women from any sector, however, are eligible to receive a state-funded one-time maternity benefit of 2000 GEL (around 730 US$).48 In addition, maternity leave is not compulsory, allowing women the option—and in some cases, the pressure—to take a shorter leave or forgo it entirely. A Regulatory Impact Assessment conducted by Babych and Mzhavanadze as part of the UN Women project “Women’s Economic Empowerment in the South Caucasus” highlights insufficient income during maternity leave as a key issue affecting mothers in Georgia.49

Despite notable economic growth and development, the Georgian economy remains vulnerable to external shocks due to reliance on foreign trade, remittances and tourism. High external debt, trade deficits and reliance on imports expose the country to global price fluctuations and exchange rate volatility.13 Inflation, unemployment and income inequality persist, with rural areas lagging behind urban centres in opportunities and infrastructure.5052 Political instability and regional tensions, particularly with Russia, further exacerbate economic uncertainty, as they can deter foreign investment and disrupt trade.53 These factors significantly influence the country’s economic stability, which, in turn, can affect fertility trends and reproductive decisions. This notion is well evidenced in papers by Matysiak et al15 and Sobotka et al.54 These studies reveal that economic recessions, marked by unemployment and financial uncertainty, lead to declines in fertility rates as individuals delay or reconsider childbearing. The Great Recession in 2002–2014, for example, caused widespread postponement of births across Europe.15 During the same period, Georgia experienced significant events, including changes in governing parties twice in 2004 and 2012, and a 2008 initiative by the head of the Georgian Orthodox Church to personally baptise every third child born to a family, leading to notable peaks and overall fluctuations in TFR.13 In recent years, Georgia has faced increasing challenges due to regional tensions, which may have contributed to TFR reverting to levels observed in the mid-1990s.13

Our study found that the proportion of Georgian, Azerbaijani and other nationality mothers increased during the study period, from 2017 to 2024. However, when excluding the first four years from the analysis to minimise the impact of missing values on the distribution of nationality, we observed that the proportion of Georgian mothers actually slightly decreased, while the proportions of Azerbaijani mothers and mothers of other nationalities increased during 2021–2024. The decreasing proportion of Georgian mothers can be attributed to the increase in emigration of Georgians over the past decades. Notably, Georgia had one of the highest female emigration rates compared with other countries in the Caucasus and Central Asia in 2015–2016.25 A large proportion of those leaving the country are individuals in the 20–39 age group, which represents the prime reproductive and working-age population. Our study showing an increase in mothers with Azerbaijani and other nationalities can be attributed to the rising number of immigrants in the country, including an influx of Russians and Ukrainians since the start of the Russian war against Ukraine, and an increasing number of foreign students.20

We found a slight increase in the proportion of births in the Adjara region, located in western Georgia, during the study period. This may be attributed to the region’s growing industrialisation and rising population in the city of Batumi, the second most populated city in the country, located in Adjara.20 In contrast, births decreased in the regions of Racha-Lechkhumi and Kvemo Svaneti, Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti, Samtskhe-Javakheti and Shida Kartli, which are more rural and less industrialised compared with Tbilisi or Batumi; this decline may be attributed to a significant reduction in population size in these regions over the past decade.20 However, despite significant population declines in the regions of Kakheti and Imereti over the past few decades, their fertility rates have remained stationary.20 This suggests that other factors, such as economic or cultural factors as well as migration, might also be influencing these shifts in fertility rates.

The ongoing fertility decline in Georgia has been a significant concern in recent years, and future projections indicate that the demographic situation is likely to deteriorate further as the TFR may reach “lowest-low” levels (TFR≤1.3) by 2034. It is known that population dynamics are shaped by a complex interplay of various factors, making predictions about population changes quite challenging.4 However, Georgia’s recent TFR trends suggest it is entering the second demographic transition, marked by fertility rates falling below replacement levels, and shifts in family structures and values are expected. This phase is typically characterised by increased individualism, delayed marriage and childbearing, reflecting societal shifts toward self-fulfilment and gender equality.4 55 A study by Myrskylä et al56 suggested that advanced economies could recover fertility rates through continued economic and social progress. Various strategies in 18 countries, including Norway, Germany and the US, aimed to stabilise population growth, and TFRs in these nations did show a temporary increase between 2000 and 2009. However, this recovery was short-lived, as TFRs have declined again over the past decade. Once fertility rates drop below replacement levels, reversing the trend is challenging, except through immigration, which introduces younger, more fertile populations into a country. For instance, despite low TFRs, the US and the UK maintain population growth due to high immigration.4 As for Georgia, net migration has been negative since the 1990s, with brief positive rates in 2020 and 2022, likely due to the COVID-19 pandemic and the Russian war against Ukraine. In 2023, net migration was approximately −39 200 individuals, or −10.6 per 1000 people,20 further undermining prospects for the sustainment of population growth.

Given the projected trends and global experiences, the continued emigration of young people from Georgia could, without rapid intervention and targeted policies, accelerate population ageing and deepen the country’s demographic and economic challenges. To address these issues, policymakers must act swiftly to create an environment that supports family formation through measures such as providing financial support and accessible childcare for young families. Furthermore, reducing emigration, increasing immigration and creating incentives to encourage the return of young, working-age individuals could help alleviate demographic pressures.

Future research should explore the underlying drivers of Georgia’s declining fertility rates, with a focus on the effects of economic instability, emigration and cultural shifts. Assessing the effectiveness of policy measures, such as better financial incentives, maternity leave and childcare could offer valuable guidance for developing more effective interventions.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the utilisation of comprehensive national datasets, including the data from the GBR and the Geostat, which ensured extensive population coverage and representativeness of Georgian women. The GBR, with a childbirth coverage rate of over 99%, provided detailed and reliable information on numerous maternal characteristics. This high level of coverage reduces the likelihood of selection bias and enhances the reliability of the findings.

Nonetheless, the study had several limitations. One notable limitation is that the GBR lacks detailed and high-quality socioeconomic data, such as household income, employment status or partner characteristics, which are important factors influencing fertility and maternal health outcomes. Although the registry includes some information on education, it is incomplete, with numerous missing values and insufficient detail to evaluate the impact of educational attainment among mothers. Another limitation is the incomplete reporting of maternal nationality prior to 2021, as this field only became mandatory that year. While sensitivity analysis was conducted to address this issue, findings related to maternal nationality trends over just 4 years may not provide strong evidence of definitive patterns and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

It should be noted that this study uses a standard population-based denominator for TFR. Since national registries, including the GBR and the data from Geostat, do not track sexual orientation, relationship status or explicit reproductive intent, this study cannot distinguish between fertility declines driven by biological factors versus those stemming from socio-behavioural shifts. While this does not change the observed downward trend, it is an important consideration for the interpretation of the factors driving Georgia’s demographic transition.

Another limitation of this study is the assumption of a constant proportional change inherent in the log-linear Prais-Winsten model. While this functional form was appropriate for total births, ASFR among adolescents and CBR, our diagnostic plots revealed a notable deviation of TFR from fitted values in 2021 and significant annual volatility in ASFRs for women aged 20 and older. These fluctuations suggest that fertility behaviour in these age groups is highly sensitive to period-specific factors, which may cause the rate of change to accelerate or decelerate annually. Consequently, the reported APCs for these variables should be interpreted as average trends over the study period rather than as a fixed rate of change, as they do not capture the short-term variations observed in the data.

Conclusions

Georgia is experiencing a consistent decline in TFR and CBR, with projections indicating a continued drop, potentially reaching “lowest-low” fertility levels by 2034. Over the past 8 years, the average maternal age, as well as the proportion of mothers aged ≥35 years, increased both for nulliparous and multiparous mothers.
