An introduction to the a posteriori error analysis of parabolic partial differential equations
Iain Smears

Abstract
This article provides a brief introduction to the a posteriori error analysis of parabolic partial differential equations, with an emphasis on challenges distinct from those of steady-state problems. Using the heat equation as a model problem, we examine the crucial influence of the choice of error norm, as well as the choice of notion of reconstruction of the discrete solution, on the analytical properties of the resulting estimators, especially in terms of the efficiency of the estimators.



Key words:
Parabolic partial differential equations,
a posteriori
error analysis

1
Introduction

The numerical approximation of partial differential equations (PDE) is crucial in many scientific and engineering fields.
Ensuring the accuracy of the approximate solutions necessitates robust error control by
a posteriori
error estimators, which provide computable bounds or estimates for the discretization error based on the computed solution and problem data, without requiring knowledge of the exact solution.
The error estimators are also central ingredients in the design of adaptive algorithms, which aim to achieve gains in computational efficiency and prescribed error tolerances.
The purpose of this expository article is to present a brief, accessible, and relatively self-contained, introduction to this topic.
With this purpose in mind, we use as a model problem the heat equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\Delta u

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

\displaystyle u(0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

where
\Omega
is a bounded open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, which we assume to have a Lipschitz boundary, and where
T>0
is the final time. The assumptions on the data will be made precise below.
We do not assume that the reader is familiar with the current research literature.
Instead, we only assume that the reader has some foundations in the analysis of parabolic PDE and Bochner spaces, as can be found in many books, such as
[
24
,
33
,
52
]
.

The analysis of
a posteriori
error estimators for time-dependent PDE poses a number of challenges that are not typically encountered for their steady-state counterparts.
There is not only a very wide range of possible choices of numerical methods, both with regards to temporal and spatial discretization, but even for a chosen method, there can be significant additional complications, such as mesh-adaptation in between time-steps
[
9
,
13
,
15
,
26
,
29
]
.
In addition, there are many approaches and techniques to the derivation and construction of the
a posteriori
error estimators.
To mention only a few, there are techniques based on dual problems
[
15
,
28
]
, elliptic reconstructions
[
31
,
32
]
, energy type bounds
[
5
,
39
]
, or inf-sup stability of the problem
[
19
,
20
,
26
,
50
]
.
However, the purpose here is not to review the breadth of possibilities in the literature.
Instead, the focus is on some fundamental aspects that are relevant to essentially all numerical computations and estimation techniques for parabolic problems.

The first focal point that we consider is the effect of the choice of norm in which to measure the error.
For parabolic problems, there is a plethora of norms in which one can measure the error.
We are particularly interested in those functional settings that are connected to an inf-sup stable formulation of the problem, so that there is an equivalence between the norm of the error and a (usually noncomputable) dual norm of the residual.
In these cases, the question of efficiency of the estimators seems particularly relevant, yet turns out to be a rather challenging one.
The problem, as we detail below, is that the same estimator (more precisely, the temporal jump estimator) appears in the corresponding upper bounds on the error for various norms of the error, yet it is known from counter-examples (c.f. Example
4.7
below) that it is not an efficient estimator for all of these norms.
Furthermore, for some norms where some efficiency results are available, the question of locality of the efficiency bounds is also more complicated than for steady-state problems.
The second issue that we wish to focus on is perhaps more subtle and easy to overlook. For many time-stepping schemes, such as the implicit Euler method, there is an element of choice in how to reconstruct a discrete function that extends the computed values at the time-step points to the whole time interval.
Hence there is some flexibility in the precise notion of numerical solution that is to be compared against the exact solution.
For instance, two popular choices in the case of the implicit Euler method are a piecewise constant-in-time reconstruction, and a continuous piecewise affine-in-time reconstruction.
Yet there is generally no unique answer, for all problem data, as to which provides the smaller error (see Example
4.2
below).
Furthermore, the efficiency of the
same
estimator can depend crucially on the choice of reconstruction of the numerical solution (c.f. Example
4.7
below showing how, depending on the problem data, the estimator might vastly overestimate the error for either one of the reconstructions).

Therefore, we hope that this expository work will help our reader to better navigate the landscape regarding the relationship between the error estimators and the various possible choices of norms and also choices of notion of numerical solution.
We also aim to further clarify the issue of efficiency of the estimators, as this is an aspect of the analysis that has been a challenge for many works in the literature.
In order to highlight how many of the central issues result from time-discretization, we first consider the semi-discrete setting where the problem is discretized only with respect to time. Then, to show how the features of the analysis extend to the fully discrete setting, we also consider the fully discrete setting where space is discretized by a conforming finite element method.
In order to make the presentation as brief, accessible and self-contained as possible, it has been necessary to make some selections on choices of topics.
One direction that we do not discuss is mesh adaptation between time-steps, which requires a more technical analysis.
We refer the reader instead to the research literature mentioned above for further details on this matter.
We also do not cover here several other possible norms for the error, such as
L^{2}(L^{2})
,
L^{\infty}(L^{2})
or
L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})
norms, and instead refer the reader to
[
11
,
14
,
16
,
28
,
32
,
46
,
49
]
.
We also do not go into details on other choices of numerical methods, such as other time-stepping methods, especially higher-order methods
[
2
,
4
,
19
,
20
,
34
,
35
,
42
]
, or nonconformity of the spatial discretization
[
22
,
27
,
36
]
.
Despite its importance, the choice of estimators to handle the spatial discretization is not the central focus here, so in the fully discrete setting, we consider only the equilibrated flux estimators, since it allows for some essential results to be quoted succinctly from the literature, which will be especially useful for the treatment of the
L^{2}(H^{1})
and energy norms.
However, other spatial estimators, such as residual-based estimators, could also be considered.
Finally, we also do not consider extensions to more general parabolic problems, for instance problems with lower-order terms, although we note that the setting of the analysis generalizes via inf-sup stability for some equivalent norms with time-dependent weights (c.f.
[
38
]
).

2
Setting and notation

2.1
Sobolev and Bochner spaces

For a nonempty, bounded, open set
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, and an integer
m\geq 1
, let
L^{p}(\omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
denote the space of Lebesgue measurable vector-fields
v\colon\omega\rightarrow[-\infty,\infty]^{m}
such that
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}(\omega,\mathbb{R}^{m})}\coloneqq\left(\int_{\omega}\lvert v\rvert^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty
. For the case
m=1
, we abbreviate
L^{p}(\omega)\coloneqq L^{p}(\omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, and likewise for its norm.
For the case
p=2
and general integer
m\geq 1
, we shall use the shorthand notation
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\omega}
and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\omega}
to denote respectively the standard norm and inner-product for both scalar- and vector-valued functions on
\omega
, where the arguments of the norms and inner-product will distinguish between the scalar and vectorial cases.
Let the Hilbert space
H^{1}(\omega)\coloneqq W^{1,2}(\omega)
denote the usual Sobolev space of functions in
L^{2}(\omega)
with first-order weak partial derivatives also in
L^{2}(\omega)
.
The space
H^{1}(\omega)
is equipped with the norm

\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}(\omega)}^{2}\coloneqq\lVert v\rVert_{\omega}^{2}+\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{\omega}^{2}\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(\omega),

(2.1)

where
\nabla v
denotes the gradient of
v
.
Let
H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
denote the closure of
C^{\infty}_{0}(\omega)
in the space
H^{1}(\omega)
, where
C^{\infty}_{0}(\omega)
denotes the space of real-valued infinitely differentiable compactly supported functions on
\omega
.
Note that for
\omega
bounded, the Poincaré inequality implies that the mapping
v\mapsto\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{\omega}
defines an equivalent norm on
H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
, see
[
1
, Corollary 6.31, p. 184]
.
Let
H^{-1}(\omega)
denote the dual space of
H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
, with norm

\lVert\Phi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\omega)}\coloneqq\sup_{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\langle\Phi,v\rangle_{H^{-1}(\omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\omega)}}{\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{\omega}}\quad\forall\Phi\in H^{-1}(\omega),

(2.2)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{-1}(\omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\omega)}
is the duality pairing between
H^{-1}(\omega)
and
H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
.
To simplify the notation, for the special case where
\omega=\Omega
the domain in (
1.1
), we shall drop the subscript and simply write
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to denote the duality pairing between
H^{-1}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

For a bounded open set
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the space
L^{2}(\omega)
can be canonically embedded into
H^{-1}(\omega)
through
\langle w,v\rangle_{H^{-1}(\omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\omega)}=(w,v)_{\omega}
for all
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
. The density of
H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
in
L^{2}(\omega)
implies the injectivity of the embedding
L^{2}(\omega)
into
H^{-1}(\omega)
; i.e.
\langle w,v\rangle_{H^{-1}(\omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\omega)}=0
for all
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
if and only if
w=0
.
Thus the spaces
H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
,
L^{2}(\omega)
and
H^{-1}(\omega)
form a
Gelfand triple
, with
H^{1}_{0}(\omega)\subset L^{2}(\omega)\subset H^{-1}(\omega)
, where each embedding is continuous, compact, dense and injective, see also
[
52
, p. 262]
.

In the following, we frequently consider Bochner spaces consisting of functions that map a bounded open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
into various Sobolev spaces over some spatial domain
\omega
.
Although we state here some of the main definitions of these spaces and mention some basic properties, the reader may find a more comprehensive introduction in
[
24
, Ch. 5]
,
[
52
, Ch.4]
, and in
[
53
]
.
Let
V
be a real Banach space. The space
L^{p}(I;V)
is the space of all strongly measurable functions
v:I\mapsto V
such that
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}(0,T;V)}<\infty
, where

\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}(I;V)}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\left(\int_{I}\lVert v(t)\rVert_{V}^{p}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&p\in[1,\infty),\\
\esssup_{t\in I}\lVert v(t)\rVert_{V},&p=\infty.\end{cases}

(2.3)

The main examples of Bochner spaces that we will use below are the spaces
L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\omega))
,
L^{2}(I;L^{2}(\omega))
and
L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\omega))
.
We note that, if
1\leq p<\infty
, the spaces
L^{p}(I\times\omega)
and
L^{p}(I;L^{p}(\omega))
are isometrically isomorphic to each other; however this does not extend to the case
p=\infty
, see
[
41
, p. 24]
.
The notion of weak derivatives extends to Bochner spaces, see
[
24
, p. 285]
: in particular, for
v\in L^{p}(I;V)
, we say that
v
has weak time derivative
w\in L^{1}(I;V)
, and we write
\partial_{t}v\coloneqq w
, if and only if

\int_{I}v(t)\partial_{t}\phi(t)\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{I}w(t)\phi(t)\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(I),

(2.4)

where the integrals in (
2.4
) are
V
-valued Bochner integrals, see
[
24
, p. 650]
and
[
52
, p. 388]
for a definition.
Using the fact that
\langle\Phi,\int_{I}v(t)\mathrm{d}t\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}=\int_{I}\langle\Phi,v(t)\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\mathrm{d}t
for all
v\in L^{1}(I;V)
and
\Phi\in V^{*}
, c.f.
[
24
, p. 650]
, and the Hahn–Banach Theorem, it can be shown that (
2.4
) is equivalent to

\int_{I}\langle\Phi,v(t)\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\partial_{t}\phi(t)\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{I}\langle\Phi,w(t)\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\phi(t)\mathrm{d}t,\quad\forall\Phi\in V^{*},\quad\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(I).

(2.5)

The space
H^{1}(I;V)
is the space of functions in
v\in L^{2}(I;V)
that have weak time derivative
\partial_{t}v\in L^{2}(I;V)
.
A norm on
H^{1}(I;V)
is given by
\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}(I;V)}=\left(\int_{I}\lVert v\rVert_{V}^{2}+\lVert\partial_{t}v\rVert_{V}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

2.2
Function spaces for parabolic PDE

We start by defining several function spaces that will be of special use in the analysis.
First, let

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\coloneqq L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)).

(2.6)

Since
\Omega
is bounded, we shall equip
X
with the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X}
defined by

\lVert v\rVert_{X}^{2}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall v\in X.

(2.7)

We also let
Z\coloneqq X\times L^{2}(\Omega)
denote the product of the spaces
X
and
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
A norm on the space
Z
is defined by

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{v}\rVert_{Z}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lVert v\rVert_{X}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert v_{T}\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\forall\bm{v}=(v,v_{T})\in Z.

(2.8)

Note that the choice of a factor of
\frac{1}{2}
before the term
\lVert v_{T}\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}
in (
2.8
) is motivated by the
energy
norm on the solution of the heat equation, which we will consider in later sections.
Next, let

Y\coloneqq L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega)),

(2.9)

where, to be clear, the space
Y
is the space of functions
\varphi
such that
\varphi\in X=L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
and, after taking the embedding from
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
into
H^{-1}(\Omega)
as described above, then also
\varphi\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
.
A possible choice of norm on
Y
is given by
\varphi\mapsto\sqrt{\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2}_{X}+\lVert\varphi\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}}
, although see (
2.10
) below.
It is known that
Y
is continuously embedded in
C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, see
[
24
, p. 287]
, and thus we can define a more suitable norm on
Y
for the purpose of
a posteriori
error analysis for (
1.1
), given by

\displaystyle\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}\left(\lVert\partial_{t}\varphi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla\varphi\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\lVert\varphi(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in Y.

(2.10)

It will be seen in Section
3.1
below that the choice of norm (
2.10
) leads to optimal constants in the analysis of the inf-sup condition for the bilinear form associated to the heat operator in (
1.1
), which is advantageous for the goal of obtaining quantitative bounds on the error.
Note that the final term in the right-hand side of (
2.10
) is finite as a result of the the continous embedding
Y\subset C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, and thus
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y}
is well-defined and is moreover equivalent to

\sqrt{\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}_{X}+\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}}
.

Moreover, the continuous embedding of
Y
in
C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
also implies that we can define several useful subspaces of
Y
with conditions at the initial and final times.
In particular, we define the spaces

\displaystyle Y_{0}\coloneqq\{\varphi\in Y,\;\varphi(0)=0\},

\displaystyle Y_{T}\coloneqq\{\varphi\in Y,\;\varphi(T)=0\},

(2.11)

which denote respectively the subspaces of function in
Y
that vanish at
t=0
and
t=T
.
We leave
Y_{0}
equipped with norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y}
defined in (
2.10
) above, but we shall equip
Y_{T}
with the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y_{T}}
defined by

\displaystyle\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}\left(\lVert\partial_{t}\varphi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla\varphi\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\lVert\varphi(0)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in Y_{T}.

(2.12)

We will also consider the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}
on the space
Y
defined by

\displaystyle\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}\left(\lVert\partial_{t}\varphi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla\varphi\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\lVert\varphi(0)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\lVert\varphi(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in Y,

(2.13)

The notation
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}
reflects the fact that this norm is invariant with respect to reversal of the time variable, i.e. the map
Y\ni\varphi\mapsto\varphi(T-\cdot)
is an isometry under the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}
.
This invariance property will play an important role later in Section
4.4
for the analysis of bounds for the energy norm of the error.

2.3
The heat equation

Returning to the heat equation (
1.1
), we shall suppose throughout this work that
f\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
and that
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Then, it is well-known that (
1.1
) admits a unique weak solution
u\in Y
that solves

\langle\partial_{t}u(t),v\rangle+(\nabla u(t),\nabla v)_{\Omega}=\langle f(t),v\rangle\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in(0,T),

(2.14)

and
u(0)=u_{0}
, see for instance
[
24
,
33
,
52
]
.
Recall that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the duality pairing between
H^{-1}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

3
Inf-sup stability of the heat equation

3.1
Inf-sup stability:
L^{2}(H^{1})\cap H^{1}(H^{-1})
norm

We first consider the inf-sup stability of the heat equation (
1.1
) when considering the solution in the space
Y=L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
.
We start by defining the bilinear form
B_{Y}\colon Y\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

B_{Y}(\varphi,v)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}\left(\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,v\rangle+(\nabla\varphi,\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right)\mathrm{d}t,

(3.1)

where
\varphi\in Y
and
v\in X
are arbitrary functions.
Then, the problem (
1.1
) admits the following weak formulation: find
u\in Y
such that
u(0)=u_{0}
and such that

\displaystyle B_{Y}(u,v)=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,v\rangle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\forall v\in X.

(3.2)

The well-posedness of (
3.2
) is well-known and can be shown by Galerkin’s method
[
24
,
52
]
.
The following result states an inf–sup stability result for the bilinear form
B_{Y}
for the above spaces equipped with their respective norms.

Theorem 3.1

(Inf–sup identity)
.

For every
\varphi\in Y
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[\sup_{v\in X\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{Y}(\varphi,v)}{\lVert v\rVert_{X}}\right]^{2}+\lVert\varphi(0)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}.

(3.3)

Proof.

For a fixed
\varphi\in Y
, let
w_{*}\in X
be defined by
(\nabla w_{*},\nabla v)_{\Omega}=\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,v\rangle
for all
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, a.e. in
(0,T)
, which implies the identity
\lVert w_{*}\rVert_{X}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\partial_{t}\varphi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathrm{d}t
.
Furthermore, we have

B_{Y}(\varphi,v)=\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla(w_{*}+\varphi),\nabla v)_{\Omega}\,\mathrm{d}t=(w_{*}+\varphi,v)_{X},

(3.4)

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
denotes the inner-product on
X
.
Thus the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality implies that
\sup_{v\in X\setminus\{0\}}B_{Y}(\varphi,v)/\lVert v\rVert_{X}=\lVert w_{*}+\varphi\rVert_{X}
.
The desired identity (
3.3
) is then obtained by expanding the square

\begin{split}\lVert w_{*}+\varphi\rVert_{X}^{2}&=\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla(w_{*}+\varphi)\rVert^{2}_{\Omega}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\lVert\nabla w_{*}\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+2(\nabla w_{*},\nabla\varphi)_{\Omega}+\lVert\nabla\varphi\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\lVert\partial_{t}\varphi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+2\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,\varphi\rangle+\lVert\nabla\varphi\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y}^{2}-\lVert\varphi(0)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2},\end{split}

(3.5)

where we note that we have used the identity
\int_{0}^{T}2\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t=\lVert\varphi(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}-\lVert\varphi(0)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}
.
∎

Theorem
3.1
immediately implies the following identity for the
Y
-norm of the solution of (
1.1
)

\lVert u\rVert_{Y}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\lVert f\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathrm{d}t+\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}.

(3.6)

Indeed, this follows from the fact that
X^{*}
, the dual space of
X=L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
, is isometrically isomorphic to
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
.

3.2
Inf-sup stability:
L^{2}(H^{-1})
norm

The heat equation (
1.1
) can also be given a variational formulation that casts the time derivative onto the test functions. In particular, let the bilinear form
B_{X}\colon X\times Y_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined by

\displaystyle B_{X}(v,\varphi)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}\left[-\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,v\rangle+(\nabla v,\nabla\varphi)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\forall v\in X,\;\varphi\in Y_{T}.

(3.7)

Then, the model problem (
1.1
) admits the following weak formulation: find
u\in X
such that

\displaystyle B_{X}(u,\varphi)=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+(u_{0},\varphi(0))_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in Y_{T}.

(3.8)

Note that in (
3.8
), the initial condition
u(0)=u_{0}
is expressed as a natural condition, appearing in (
3.8
), rather than as an essential condition imposed by the choice of solution space.
Note also that in general, the weak formulation (
3.8
) can be extended to more general source terms
f\in Y_{T}^{*}
the dual of
Y_{T}
, however for the sake of simplicity we shall restrict ourselves here to the case
f\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
, and thus the solution of (
3.8
) is equivalently the solution of (
3.2
).

Theorem 3.2

(Inf–sup identity)
.

For every
v\in X
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varphi\in Y_{T}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{X}(v,\varphi)}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}}.

(3.9)

Proof.

Let
R\colon v\mapsto v(T-\cdot)
denote the time-reversal mapping for functions
v\in X
. It is clear that
R:X\rightarrow X
is an isometry with
\lVert Rv\rVert_{X}=\lVert v\rVert_{X}
for all
v\in X
, while
R:Y_{T}\rightarrow Y_{0}
is an isometry with
\lVert R\varphi\rVert_{Y}=\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}
for all
\varphi\in Y_{T}
.
Observe also that
B_{Y}(R\varphi,v)=B_{X}(Rv,\varphi)
for all
v\in X
and all
\varphi\in Y_{T}
.
Therefore, Theorem
3.1
implies that

\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}=\lVert R\varphi\rVert_{Y}=\sup_{v\in X\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{Y}(R\varphi,v)}{\lVert v\rVert_{X}}=\sup_{v\in X\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{X}(Rv,\varphi)}{\lVert v\rVert_{X}}=\sup_{\widetilde{v}\in X\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{X}(\widetilde{v},\varphi)}{\lVert\widetilde{v}\rVert_{X}}.

(3.10)

Note that in passing to the last identity in (
3.10
), we have simply substituted
\widetilde{v}=Rv
and used the isometry identity
\lVert Rv\rVert_{X}=\lVert v\rVert_{X}
above.
Next, we immediately obtain from (
3.10
) the lower bound

\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert_{X}\geq\sup_{\varphi\in Y_{T}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{X}(v,\varphi)}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}}

\displaystyle\forall v\in X.

(3.11)

To obtain the converse bound, let
\varphi_{*}\in Y_{T}
denote the solution of

B_{X}(w,\varphi_{*})=\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla w,\nabla v)_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall w\in X.

This problem can simply be seen as a backward-in-time heat equation with final time condition
\varphi_{*}(T)=0
. Hence, we have
\lVert v\rVert_{X}^{2}=B_{X}(v,\varphi_{*})
and (
3.10
) implies that
\lVert\varphi_{*}\rVert_{Y_{T}}=\lVert v\rVert_{X}
. This immediately shows the upper bound

\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert_{X}\leq\sup_{\varphi\in Y_{T}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{X}(v,\varphi)}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}}

\displaystyle\forall v\in X,

(3.12)

which completes the proof of (
3.9
).
∎

It follows from Theorem
3.2
that the solution
u
of (
1.1
) satisfies

\lVert u\rVert_{X}=\sup_{\varphi\in Y_{T}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,\varphi\rangle\mathrm{d}t+(u_{0},\varphi(0))_{\Omega}}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}}.

(3.13)

3.3
Inf-sup stability: energy norm

A very commonly used approach to the analysis of parabolic problems such as (
1.1
) is to consider testing the weak formulation of the equation with the solution.
It is well-known that in the case of (
1.1
), the solution
u
satisfies the identity

\lVert u\rVert_{E}^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\lVert u(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,u\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}.

(3.14)

This leads to stability results in what is often called the
energy
norm
\lVert u\rVert_{E}
defined above.
The name
energy norm
here is used in reference to the relation between the concept of energy of the corresponding stationary problem and the principle of testing the equation with the solution.
However, the issue with starting the analysis from (
3.14
) is that, in most cases, it is not clear how to obtain sharp bounds on the term
\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,u\rangle\mathrm{d}t
in the right-hand side of (
3.14
).
To overcome this issue, we present here a different approach that places the energy norm of the solution within the framework of an inf-sup identity that relates the energy norm to a suitable dual norm of a bilinear form.
This leads to sharper
a posteriori
error bounds for numerical approximations as shown in later sections.

To begin, we shall make use of a useful alternative formula for the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}
that was defined in (
2.13
) above.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have the identity

\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}^{2}=2\lVert\varphi(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert(\partial_{t}+\Delta)\varphi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall\varphi\in Y.

(3.15)

Note that in (
3.15
),
\Delta\colon H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\rightarrow H^{-1}(\Omega)
denotes the Laplacian operator.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in Y
be arbitrary and let
R:Y\rightarrow Y
denote the time-reversal map
R\varphi=\varphi(T-\cdot)
.
Theorem
3.1
implies that

\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}^{2}=\lVert R\varphi\rVert_{Y}^{2}=\left[\sup_{v\in X\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{Y}(R\varphi,v)}{\lVert v\rVert_{X}}\right]^{2}+\lVert(R\varphi)(0)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\\
=\int_{0}^{T}\lVert(\partial_{t}+\Delta)\varphi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\varphi(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}.

(3.16)

We then obtain (
3.15
) from (
3.16
) and from the identity
\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}^{2}=\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}^{2}+\lVert\varphi(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}
.
∎

Recall that the space
Z=X\times L^{2}(\Omega)
was defined in Section
2.2
above.
Define the bilinear form
B_{Z}:Z\times Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle B_{Z}(\bm{v},\varphi)\coloneqq(v_{T},\varphi(T))_{\Omega}+\int_{0}^{T}\left(-\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,v\rangle+(\nabla\varphi,\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right)\mathrm{d}t,

(3.17)

for all
\bm{v}=(v,v_{T})\in Z
and all
\varphi\in Y
.
The following theorem states the inf-sup identities for the bilinear form
B_{Z}
.

Theorem 3.4

(Inf-sup identities)
.

We have the identities

\displaystyle\sup_{\varphi\in Y\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{Z}(\bm{v},\varphi)}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\bm{v}\rVert_{Z}\quad\forall\bm{v}\in Z,

(3.18a)

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{v}\in Z\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{Z}(\bm{v},\varphi)}{\lVert\bm{v}\rVert_{Z}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}\quad\forall\varphi\in Y.

(3.18b)

Proof.

We start by showing that

\lvert B_{Z}(\bm{v},\varphi)\rvert\leq\lVert\bm{v}\rVert_{Z}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}\quad\forall\bm{v}\in Z,\;\forall\varphi\in Y.

(3.19)

It follows from the definition of the
H^{-1}(\Omega)
-norm in (
2.2
) that

\begin{split}\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\left(-\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,v\rangle+(\nabla\varphi,\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right\rvert&=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}-\langle\partial_{t}\varphi+\Delta\varphi,v\rangle\mathrm{d}t\right\rvert\\
&\leq\left(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\partial_{t}\varphi+\Delta\varphi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lVert v\rVert_{X},\end{split}

(3.20)

for any
\varphi\in Y
and
v\in X
, where we recall that
\lVert v\rVert_{X}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Hence, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the terms in
B_{Z}(\bm{v},\varphi)
shows that

\begin{split}\lvert B_{Z}(\bm{v},\varphi)\rvert&\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\lVert v_{T}\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\lVert v\rVert_{X}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(2\lVert\varphi(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\partial_{t}\varphi+\Delta\varphi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&=\lVert\bm{v}\rVert_{Z}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}},\end{split}

(3.21)

where we have used Lemma
3.3
in passing to the second line above. This yields (
3.19
).

Next, let
\bm{v}=(v,v_{T})\in Z
be arbitrary, and let
\varphi_{*}\in Y
be the unique solution of the backward parabolic problem: find
\varphi_{*}\in Y
such that
\varphi_{*}(T)=\frac{1}{2}v_{T}
and
\partial_{t}\varphi_{*}+\Delta\varphi_{*}=\Delta v
in
(0,T)
.
Then, it is clear that
B_{Z}(\bm{v},\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\lVert v_{T}\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\lVert v\rVert_{X}^{2}=\lVert\bm{v}\rVert_{Z}^{2}
. Furthermore, using Lemma
3.3
, we have

\lVert\varphi_{*}\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}^{2}=2\lVert\varphi(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\partial_{t}\varphi+\Delta\varphi\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2}\lVert v_{T}\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\lVert v\rVert_{X}^{2}=\lVert\bm{v}\rVert_{Z}^{2}.

(3.22)

Thus we obtain (
3.18a
) from the above identities and the upper bound (
3.19
). To show (
3.18b
), we take
\bm{v}_{*}=(v,v_{T})
with
v=(-\Delta)^{-1}(-\partial_{t}\varphi)+\varphi\in X
, and
v_{T}=2\varphi(T)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and we perform similar computations to find that
\lVert\bm{v}\rVert_{Z}=\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}
and
B_{Z}(\bm{v},\varphi)=\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}^{2}
.
This shows (
3.18b
).
∎

We now show how to apply Theorem
3.4
to the heat equation (
1.1
). After testing (
1.1
) with a test function
\varphi\in Y
and integrating-by-parts in time, we see that the solution
u
solves

(u(T),\varphi(T))_{\Omega}+\int_{0}^{T}\left(-\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,u\rangle+(\nabla u,\nabla\varphi)_{\Omega}\right)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,\varphi\rangle\mathrm{d}t+(u_{0},\varphi(0))_{\Omega},

(3.23)

for all
\varphi\in Y
.
Note that similarly to the weak formulation (
3.8
), the formulation (
3.23
) involves casting the time derivative onto a test function; however in (
3.23
) we allow
\varphi\in Y\setminus Y_{T}
, i.e. we do not require the test functions to vanish at the final time
T
.
Upon defining
\bm{u}\coloneqq(u,u(T))\in Z
, it follows that (
3.23
) can be written equivalently as
B_{Z}(\bm{u},\varphi)=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,\varphi\rangle\mathrm{d}t+(u_{0},\varphi(0))_{\Omega}
for all
\varphi\in Y
.
Hence (
3.18a
) implies that

\begin{split}\lVert u\rVert_{E}^{2}&\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\lVert u(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{Z}^{2}=\left[\sup_{\varphi\in Y\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,\varphi\rangle\mathrm{d}t+(u_{0},\varphi(0))_{\Omega}}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}}\right]^{2}.\end{split}

(3.24)

This gives an alternative characterization of the energy norm in comparison to (
3.14
) above.
As is clear from the proof of Theorem
3.4
, the function
\varphi
that achieves the supremum on the right-hand side of (
3.24
) is generally not the same as
u
, which offers some explanation as to difference between using (
3.24
) over (
3.14
) as the starting point for the analysis of the energy norm.

Remark 3.1

(Bibliographical remarks)
.

The analysis of the heat equation in terms of inf-sup stability has appeared in various forms in
[
17
,
19
,
20
,
43
,
47
,
48
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, the analysis of the energy norm presented above is original.

4
Discretization in time

In this section we first consider the effects of the temporal discretization of (
1.1
), without any spatial discretization. This leads us to considering the
a posteriori
error analysis for a
semi-discrete
method.
Our motivation for considering the temporal discretization first is to highlight some key aspects of the analysis that can be expected to hold regardless of any later choice of spatial discretization.
In this section we shall temporarily assume that
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
in order to avoid some technical matters unrelated to the main ideas; this assumption will be later removed in the following sections once spatial discretization is also introduced.

4.1
Implicit Euler Discretization

The implicit Euler method as a finite difference method.

The implicit Euler method is frequently derived from a finite difference approximation of the time derivative term in (
1.1
).
From this point of view, the discretization is introduced as follows: let
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
, for some integer
N\geq 1
, denote a strictly increasing sequence of time-step points, with
t_{0}=0
and
t_{N}=T
.
Let
\tau_{n}=t_{n}-t_{n-1}>0
denote the time-step length.

For each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, let
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\ni u_{n}\approx u(t_{n})
be defined by

\left(\frac{u_{n}-u_{n-1}}{\tau_{n}},v\right)_{\Omega}+(\nabla u_{n},\nabla v)_{\Omega}=\langle f_{n},v\rangle\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(4.1)

where
u_{0}
is the initial datum from (
1.1
), and where
H^{-1}(\Omega)\ni f_{n}\approx f(t_{n})
is some approximation of the source term which we discuss shortly below.
Note that the existence and uniqueness of the approximations
u_{n}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, follows easily from the Lax–Milgram Theorem applied to a sequence of elliptic variational problems in the space
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Remark 4.1

(Choice of
f_{n}
)
.

In the following analysis, we allow for some flexibility in the choice of
f_{n}
.
A traditional choice for
f_{n}
is simply the time-point value
f(t_{n})
, however this might not be well-defined for general
f\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
due to possible discontinuities in time.
A more general alternative is to let
f_{n}
be the mean-value of
f
over the time-interval
(t_{n-1},t_{n})
, i.e.

f_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}f(t)\mathrm{d}t\in H^{-1}(\Omega).

(4.2)

Observe that
f_{n}
is thus well-defined for any
f\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
.
We do not require that
f_{n}
to be defined by (
4.2
) for all of the following results, although sharper results can sometimes be obtained specifically for the case where (
4.2
) holds.

When viewed in the form (
4.1
), the implicit Euler method defines approximations of the solution at the time-points
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
.
However, for the purposes of
a posteriori
error analysis, the values of the time-point values
\{u_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
of the approximation must be extended to a function on the whole time interval
(0,T)
.
Indeed, the relevant norms for the analysis, which were introduced in the previous sections, require the error to be defined at least at almost every point in the time interval
(0,T)
.
For the case of the implicit Euler method, there are at least two natural, and closely related, approaches to the construction of such functions from the time-point values of the approximations.
We consider each approach in turn.

Piecewise constant in time reconstruction.

Given the time-points
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
, let
I_{n}\coloneqq(t_{n-1},t_{n})
denote the associated
n
-th time-interval.
Note that
\{\overline{I_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{N}
is a partition of
[0,T]
.
Let
\mathbb{V}_{\tau}^{+}
denote the space of functions
v\colon[0,T]\rightarrow H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
that are left-continuous, and also piecewise constant with respect to the time partition. Note that functions
v\in\mathbb{V}_{\tau}^{+}
have a well-defined value
v(t)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and moreover functions that agree a.e. in
(0,T)
are not identified.
The notation
\mathbb{V}_{\tau}^{+}
reflects the fact that we are considering here a forward evolution problem.
Then, given the approximation
\{u_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
defined by (
4.1
),
we define
u_{\tau}\in\mathbb{V}_{\tau}^{+}
as the unique left-continuous piecewise constant function that equals
u_{n}
on the time interval
I_{n}
, and satisfies in addition
u(0)=u_{0}
.
Note that
u_{\tau}\in X
, but in general
u_{\tau}
is discontinuous so
u_{\tau}\not\in Y
.
Also, the left-continuity of
u_{\tau}
ensures that
u_{\tau}(t_{n})=u_{n}
for each all time
n\in\{0,\dots,N\}
, i.e.
u_{\tau}
agrees with the time-point values
\{u_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
at the time-points
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
.

Continuous piecewise affine in time reconstruction.

Alternatively, we can construct a continuous piecewise affine-in-time function from the time-point values.
Let
U_{\tau}
be the unique continuous function that is piecewise affine in time with respect to the time intervals
\{I_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and that equals
u_{n}
at time
t_{n}
, for all
n=0,\dots,N
. In other words,
U_{\tau}
satisfies

U_{\tau}(t)=\frac{t-t_{n-1}}{\tau_{n}}u_{n}+\frac{t_{n}-t}{\tau_{n}}u_{n-1}\quad\forall t\in\overline{I_{n}}.

Note that in particular,
U_{\tau}(0)=u_{0}
.
Owing to the simplifying assumption that
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, it follows that
U_{\tau}(t)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Since
U_{\tau}
is continuous, it follows that
U_{\tau}\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\subset Y
.
Note that the weak time-derivative of
U_{\tau}
is piecewise constant with respect to the time intervals
\{I_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, and thus
\partial_{t}U_{\tau}\in\mathbb{V}_{\tau}
and we have

\partial_{t}U_{\tau}(t)=\frac{u_{n}-u_{n-1}}{\tau_{n}}\quad\forall t\in(t_{n-1},t_{n}),\;\forall n\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

(4.3)

Note that the two reconstructions
u_{\tau}
and
U_{\tau}
can each be recovered from the other: first, we have
u_{\tau}(t_{n})=U_{\tau}(t_{n})
for all
n\in\{0,\dots,N\}
, and
U_{\tau}
can be obtained from
u_{\tau}
and from
u_{0}
by interpolation.
The following identity for the difference will be useful later

u_{\tau}(t)-U_{\tau}(t)=\frac{t_{n}-t}{\tau_{n}}(u_{n}-u_{n-1})\quad\forall t\in(t_{n-1},t_{n}],\quad\forall n\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

(4.4)

The implicit Euler method: variational formulation.

We now show that the implicit Euler method (
4.1
) can be seen as a discretization of (
3.2
), involving both the piecewise constant reconstruction
u_{\tau}
and the continuous piecewise affine reconstruction
U_{\tau}
.
Let
f_{\tau}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
be defined by
f_{\tau}(t)=f_{n}
for all
t\in(t_{n-1},t_{n})
,
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
Thus
f_{\tau}
is piecewise constant in time, similar to functions in
\mathbb{V}_{\tau}
.
With
f_{\tau}
thusly defined, we see that (
4.1
) is equivalent to

(\partial_{t}U_{\tau}(t),v)_{\Omega}+(\nabla u_{\tau}(t),\nabla v)_{\Omega}=\langle f_{\tau}(t),v\rangle\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\forall t\in I_{n},

(4.5)

for each
n=1,\dots,N
.
Since
\partial_{t}U_{\tau}
,
u_{\tau}
and
f_{\tau}
are all also piecewise constant in time, we then see that (
4.5
) is equivalent to the global-in-time form

\int_{0}^{T}\left((\partial U_{\tau},v)_{\Omega}+(\nabla u_{\tau},\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f_{\tau},v\rangle\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{V}_{\tau},

(4.6)

where
\mathbb{V}_{\tau}
denotes the space of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
-valued functions that are piecewise constant with respect to
\{I_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, i.e.

\mathbb{V}_{\tau}\coloneqq\left\{v\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\colon\;v|_{I_{n}}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(I_{n};H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\quad\forall n\in\{1,\dots,N\}\right\},

(4.7)

where
\mathcal{P}_{0}(I_{n};H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
denotes the space of piecewise constant functions in time with values in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
The global-in-time form (
4.6
) should then be compared with the continuous problem (
3.2
).
We can either view (
4.6
) as a conforming
but inconsistent
method for the continuous piecewise affine approximation
U_{\tau}
, or as a
nonconforming
method for the piecewise constant and generally
discontinuous
approximation
u_{\tau}
.
As we aim to make clear in this tutorial, whichever point of view one takes, the inconsistency/nonconformity of the temporal discretization is the source of many of the challenges for the analysis of
a posteriori
error bounds.

Comparison of the reconstructions.

It is natural to ask which of two reconstructions
u_{\tau}
and
U_{\tau}
provides the better approximation of the exact solution
u
.
This turns out to be a subtle question, even for comparing these approximations only in the norm of
X=L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
.
On the one hand, supposing that a problem is fixed where
u\in H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
and assuming the data
f
is sufficiently well-approximated, e.g. if (
4.2
) holds, then it is known from
a priori
error analysis that
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
and
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
both have an overall first-order rate of convergence with respect to the time-step sizes, which is known to be sharp from computational experiments. Thus, in many cases, both yield the same rate of convergence in the small time-step limit.
On the other hand, such results from
a priori
error analysis are by nature asymptotic, and do not answer the question for any given fixed temporal discretization. If instead, we fix the time-step size, and vary the problem to be solved, then it turns out that there can be significant differences between

\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
and
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
.
We illustrate this issue concretely with the following example.

Example 4.2
.

Consider the ordinary differential equation
u^{\prime}+\lambda u=1
on
(0,1)
, with
u(0)=0
, where
\lambda>0
. The exact solution is then
u(t)=\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
This problem can be thought of as arising from a parabolic PDE after a transformation to Fourier modes, and suitable scaling of the parameters.
The solution
u
is approximated by the implicit Euler method using as single time-step of length
1
.
Thus
u_{\tau}(t)=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}
and
U_{\tau}(t)=\frac{t}{1+\lambda}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
Then, direct calculations show that

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}}{\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}}=0,

(4.8)

so
u_{\tau}
is asymptotically a better approximation of
u
than
U_{\tau}
for large
\lambda
. However, for small
\lambda
, the situation is reversed since

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\frac{\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}}{\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}}=0,

(4.9)

so
U_{\tau}
is asymptotically a better approximation of
u
than
u_{\tau}
for small
\lambda
.

The conclusion of Example
4.2
is that there is generally no definitive answer to the question of comparing these two approximations, since either
u_{\tau}
or
U_{\tau}
might be the better approximation, depending on the problem data.
Furthermore, it follows from (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) that it is not possible to bound either
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
or
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
in terms of the other with constants that are robust with respect to parameters of the problem and the discretization.
We will see later in Section
4.3
that this issue has important repercussions concerning the efficiency of the error estimators when comparing the
a posteriori
error analysis in the
X
norm.

4.2
A posteriori error analysis in the
L^{2}(H^{1})\cap H^{1}(H^{-1})
-norm

Since
U_{\tau}
defined in (
4.3
) belongs to the space
Y
, it is natural to consider the error
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{Y}
.
The analysis that follows reveals that the error
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{Y}
is closely related to the
temporal jump estimator

\eta_{J}
defined by

\eta_{J}\coloneqq\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\tau_{n}}{3}\lVert\nabla(u_{n}-u_{n-1})\rVert^{2}_{\Omega}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.10)

Note that the second identity in (
4.10
) above follows from (
4.4
).
The name of jump estimator derives from the fact that
\eta_{J}
measures the difference in the
X
-norm between the generally discontinuous in time function
u_{\tau}
with the continuous in time function
U_{\tau}
, with the difference determined by the temporal jump
u_{n}-u_{n-1}
occurring at the time-step point
t_{n-1}
, for each
n=1\dots,N
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Then

\displaystyle\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{Y}\leq\eta_{J}+\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,Y},

(4.11)

\displaystyle\eta_{J}\leq\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{Y}+\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,Y},

(4.12)

where the data oscillation
\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,Y}
is defined by
\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,Y}\coloneqq\lVert f-f_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))}
.

Proof.

We start by defining the residual
\mathcal{R}_{Y}\colon Y\rightarrow X^{*}
defined by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{R}_{Y}(\varphi),v\rangle_{X^{*}\times X}\coloneqq B_{Y}(u-\varphi,v)

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in Y,\;\forall v\in X,

(4.13)

where
B_{Y}
is defined in (
3.1
) above and where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{X^{*}\times X}
denotes the duality pairing between the dual space
X^{*}
and
X
.
The dual norm of the residuals is naturally defined by
\lVert\mathcal{R}_{Y}(\varphi)\rVert_{X^{*}}\coloneqq\sup_{v\in X\setminus\{0\}}\tfrac{\langle\mathcal{R}_{Y}(\varphi),v\rangle}{\lVert v\rVert_{X}}
.
Theorem
3.1
implies the following equivalence between the error and dual norm of the residual:

\displaystyle\lVert u-\varphi\rVert_{Y}^{2}=\lVert\mathcal{R}_{Y}(\varphi)\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}+\lVert u_{0}-\varphi(0)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in Y.

(4.14)

Recalling that
U_{\tau}(0)=u_{0}=u(0)
, the equivalence between error and residual in (
4.14
) then implies that

\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{Y}^{2}=\lVert\mathcal{R}_{Y}(U_{\tau})\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}+\lVert u_{0}-U_{\tau}(0)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}=\lVert\mathcal{R}_{Y}(U_{\tau})\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}.

(4.15)

We compute the residual
\mathcal{R}_{Y}(U_{\tau})
to find that

\langle\mathcal{R}_{Y}(U_{\tau}),v\rangle_{X^{*}\times X}=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle f,v\rangle-\langle\partial_{t}U_{\tau},v\rangle-(\nabla U_{\tau},\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t\\
=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle f-f_{\tau},v\rangle+(\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}),\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\underbrace{\left[\langle f_{\tau},v\rangle-\langle\partial_{t}U_{\tau},v\rangle-(\nabla u_{\tau},\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right]}_{=0\text{ by }\eqref{eq:IE_1}}\mathrm{d}t\\
=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f-f_{\tau},v\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}),\nabla v)_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall v\in X.

(4.16)

Then, recalling that
X=L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
, we observe that
\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}),\nabla v)_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}t
is the inner-product in
X
between
u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}
and the test function
v\in X
.
Therefore, we apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the last line of (
4.16
) to find that
\lVert R_{Y}(U_{\tau})\rVert_{X^{*}}\leq\eta_{J}+\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,Y}
.
Combined with (
4.15
), this implies the upper bound (
4.11
).
To prove (
4.12
), let
v=u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}
, noting that
v\in X
owing to the hypothesis
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Then, using (
4.16
), we have
\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}^{2}=\langle R_{Y}(U_{\tau}),v\rangle_{X^{*}\times X}-\int_{0}^{T}\langle f-f_{\tau},v\rangle\mathrm{d}t
, which implies that
\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}\leq\lVert R_{Y}(U_{\tau})\rVert_{X^{*}}+\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,Y}
.
This yields (
4.12
) after using (
4.15
).
∎

Remark 4.3

(Case of vanishing data oscillation)
.

Theorem
4.1
immediately implies that in the case of no data oscillation, i.e. if
f=f_{\tau}
, then we have the identity

\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{Y}=\eta_{J}.

(4.17)

In other words, the estimator
\eta_{J}
is then exact for the
Y
-norm error.
As we shall see in the following sections, this connection further remains important also in the fully discrete setting, although additional estimators arising from the spatial discretization will also appear.

Remark 4.4

(Local-in-time lower bounds)
.

In order to keep the presentation simple, we have shown the efficiency bound (
4.11
) as a global-in-time bound, i.e. over the whole time-interval
(0,T)
.
However, it is possible to show that the efficiency is also local in time, in particular

\int_{I_{n}}\lVert\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau})\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{I_{n}}\left[\lVert\partial_{t}(u-U_{\tau})\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+\lVert\nabla(u-U_{\tau})\rVert_{\Omega}+\lVert f-f_{\tau}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

(4.18)

for each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
We leave the proof of (
4.18
) as an exercise to the reader, see also
[
19
]
.

Since Theorem
4.1
allows for general choices of
f_{\tau}
, the data oscillation term
\lVert f-f_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega)}
appears on the right-hand side of both (
4.11
) and (
4.12
).
In particular, this leaves open the possibility that the jump estimator may significantly overestimate the error in the case where the data oscillation is dominant in these bounds.
However, sharper results can be shown in the special case where
f_{\tau}
is given as the temporal mean-value of
f
, c.f. (
4.2
), as shown in Theorem
4.2
below.

Theorem 4.2

(Error-dominated oscillation)
.

Suppose that
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and that
f_{\tau}
is given by (
4.2
). Then

\int_{I_{n}}\lVert\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau})\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\lVert f-f_{\tau}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\leq C\int_{I_{n}}\left(\lVert\partial_{t}(u-U_{\tau})\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla(u-U_{\tau})\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t,

(4.19)

for each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, where
C
is some constant independent of any other quantities.

Proof.

We sketch the proof since it follows some of the ideas in
[
19
, Proof of Theorem 5.1]
.
The main idea is to choose the semi-discrete test function
v\in\mathbb{V}_{\tau}
in (
4.6
) to be given by
v|_{I_{n}}\coloneqq u_{n}-u_{n-1}
and
v
vanishing on
(0,T)\setminus I_{n}
.
Note that
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
implies that
v\in X
in the case
n=1
.
Also, observe that
\int_{I_{n}}\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t=3\int_{I_{n}}\lVert\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau})\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t
as a consequence of (
4.4
).
Then, the definition of
f_{\tau}
in (
4.2
) and the fact that
v\in\mathbb{V}_{\tau}
is constant-in-time implies that
\int_{0}^{T}(f_{\tau},v)_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,v\rangle\mathrm{d}t
.
Thus, we obtain from (
4.6
) that

\displaystyle\int_{I_{n}}\lVert\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau})\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\int_{I_{n}}(\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}),\nabla v)_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}t

(4.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\int_{I_{n}}\left[\langle f,v\rangle-(\partial_{t}U_{\tau},v)_{\Omega}-(\nabla U_{\tau},\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\int_{I_{n}}\left[\langle\partial_{t}(u-U_{\tau}),v\rangle+(\nabla(u-U_{\tau}),\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t.

This shows that
\int_{I_{n}}\lVert\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau})\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t
is bounded by the right-hand side of (
4.19
).
Then, we re-arrange (
4.16
) for general
v\in X
to find also that
\int_{I_{n}}\lVert f-f_{\tau}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathrm{d}t
is also bounded by the right-hand side of (
4.19
).
∎

Remark 4.5

(Bibliographical remarks)
.

A lower bound for the jump estimator similar to those in (
4.12
) and (
4.18
) was first shown by Verfürth in
[
50
]
, in the case of a fully discrete approximation using the
\theta
method in time, which includes the implicit Euler method as a special case.
The approach adopted in
[
50
]
towards proving the bound is substantially different to the one shown here, leading to a constant that possibly depends on the shape-regularity of the spatial meshes.
The analysis for the temporal jump estimator was later improved by Ern, Smears & Vohralík in
[
19
]
, which treats more general discontinuous Galerkin (DG) time-discretizations of arbitrary order, and which includes the implicit Euler method as a special case.
In particular, the lower bound for the jump estimator given in
[
19
, Theorem 5.1]
features an explicit efficiency constant that is independent of the spatial discretization and that is robust with respect to the temporal polynomial degree.
The lower bound of Theorem
4.2
that removes the data oscillation from the right-hand sides appears to be original.
Theorem
4.2
showcases an example where the data oscillation term is dominated by the error, in a similar spirit to the work of Kreuzer & Veeser in
[
30
]
for elliptic problems.

4.3
A posteriori error bound for the
L^{2}(H^{1})
-norm

We now turn to the bounding the
L^{2}(H^{1})
-norms of the error, namely
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
and
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
.
We shall consider both of these quantities, since, depending on the situation, either
u_{\tau}
or
U_{\tau}
might be the better approximation of
u
in the
X
-norm, see Example
4.2
.
Note that the previous section on the
a posteriori
error bounds for the
Y
-norm, where it is recalled that
Y=L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
immediately yield, at least in some sense, upper bounds on the error in the
X
-norm, with
X=L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
.
However, as will be seen below, a more direct approach yields sharper results, especially in terms of the data oscillation.
Recall that the temporal jump estimator
\eta_{J}
is defined in (
4.10
) above.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and that
f_{\tau}
is given by (
4.2
).
Then

\displaystyle\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}\leq\eta_{J}+\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,X},

(4.21a)

\displaystyle\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}\leq\eta_{J}+\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,X},

(4.21b)

where the data oscillation
\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,X}
is defined by

\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,X}\coloneqq\sup_{\varphi\in Y_{T}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\langle f-f_{\tau},\varphi\rangle\mathrm{d}t}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}}.

(4.22)

Proof.

We detail the proof of (
4.21a
), and we leave the proof of (
4.21b
) as an exercise to the reader.
We define the residual functional
\mathcal{R}_{X}\colon X\rightarrow[Y_{T}]^{*}
by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{R}_{X}(v),\varphi\rangle_{[Y_{T}]^{*}\times Y_{T}}\coloneqq B_{X}(u-v,\varphi)

\displaystyle\forall v\in X,\;\forall\varphi\in Y_{T}.

(4.23)

Theorem
3.2
implies that

\displaystyle\lVert u-v\rVert_{X}=\lVert\mathcal{R}_{X}(v)\rVert_{[Y_{T}]^{*}}

\displaystyle\forall v\in X.

(4.24)

Considering the residual
\langle R_{X}(u_{\tau}),\varphi\rangle_{Y_{T}^{*}\times Y_{T}}
for arbitrary
\varphi\in Y_{T}
, we find that

\langle R_{X}(u_{\tau}),\varphi\rangle_{Y_{T}^{*}\times Y_{T}}=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle f,\varphi\rangle+\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,u_{\tau}\rangle-(\nabla u_{\tau},\nabla\varphi)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t+(u_{0},\varphi(0))_{\Omega}\\
=\int_{0}^{T}\underbrace{\left[\langle f_{\tau},\varphi\rangle-\langle\partial_{t}U_{\tau},\varphi\rangle-(\nabla u_{\tau},\nabla\varphi)_{\Omega}\right]}_{=0\text{ by }\eqref{eq:IE_1}}\mathrm{d}t\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rangle+\langle f-f_{\tau},\varphi\rangle\right]\mathrm{d}t,

(4.25)

where we have used the identity based on integration-by-parts in time:

(u_{0},\varphi(0))_{\Omega}=-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,U_{\tau}\rangle\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}U_{\tau},\varphi\rangle\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall\varphi\in Y_{T}.

(4.26)

We now use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to bound

\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rangle\rvert\mathrm{d}t\leq\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}.

(4.27)

where it is recalled that
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y_{T}}
is defined in (
2.12
) above.
Therefore, the equivalence between error and residual (
4.24
) implies that

\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}=\sup_{\varphi\in Y_{T}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\langle R_{X}(u_{\tau}),\varphi\rangle_{Y_{T}^{*}\times Y_{T}}}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y_{T}}}\leq\eta_{J}+\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,X}.

(4.28)

This completes the proof of (
4.21a
).
∎

Remark 4.6

(Comparison of data oscillation terms)
.

To see the difference between the bounds of Theorems
4.1
and
4.3
, consider for example the case where
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and where
f_{\tau}
is the temporal mean-value projection of
f
, i.e.
f_{\tau}
given by (
4.2
).
Then, it is known from
[
20
]
that one obtains the following bound for the data oscillation
\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,X}
:

\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,X}\leq\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\tau_{n}}{2\pi}\lVert f-f_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(I_{n};L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.29)

Thus we see that, in many cases,
\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,X}
can converge to zero faster by an additional half-order in the time-step size in comparison to
\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,Y}
.
Note also that
\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,X}
is often higher-order compared to the error measured in the
X
-norm.
By contrast,
\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,Y}
can be of the same order as the error in the
Y
-norm, although it is bounded by the error in the case of mean-value data approximations (
4.2
), as shown by Theorem
4.2
.

The problem of lower bounds for the
X
-norm errors.

Theorem
4.3
gives upper bounds on the errors
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
and
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
.
However, no lower bound is given.
We address this matter below, where we shall see that the jump estimator is not generally efficient with respect to the smallest between
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
and
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
, but it is efficient with respect to the largest of these quantities.

Example 4.7
.

Consider the setting of Example
4.2
with the same notation. Note that this corresponds to a problem where
f
is constant and thus there is no data oscillation.
Then, it follows from (
4.8
) that

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}}{\eta_{J}}=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}}{\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}}=0.

(4.30)

In this case, the estimator
\eta_{J}
is not efficient relative to the error
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
when
\lambda
becomes large.
It also follows from (
4.9
) that

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\frac{\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}}{\eta_{J}}=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\frac{\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}}{\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}}=0.

(4.31)

In this case, the estimator
\eta_{J}
is not efficient relative to the error
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
when
\lambda
becomes small.

Example
4.7
shows that, in general, either of the effectivity indices
\frac{\eta_{J}}{\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}}
and
\frac{\eta_{J}}{\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}}
can be arbitrarily large. In other words, there are cases where the jump estimator can significantly overestimate the smallest
X
-norm error. It is therefore not possible to show that the estimator
\eta_{J}
is bounded by either
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
or
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
.
At present, it is not known how to obtain a computable estimator that is efficient with respect to the smallest
X
-norm error.

Remark 4.8

(Alternative error measures)
.

The lack of efficiency of the temporal jump estimators in general for the
X
-norm error has motivated various works in the literature to consider combinations of
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
and
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
, along with other quantities, as some stronger measure of the error, see for instance
[
3
,
4
,
35
,
37
,
42
]
.
For example, one can consider the error measure

\mathcal{E}_{X}\coloneqq\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}+\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}.

(4.32)

The motivation for considering these augmented error measures is that the jump estimator is then trivially a lower bound for
\mathcal{E}_{X}
, as a result of the triangle inequality
\eta_{J}\leq\mathcal{E}_{X}
.
However, it is clear from Example
4.7
that there is no general equivalence between
\mathcal{E}_{X}
(or similar augmented error measures) with either of
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
or
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
considered individually.
See Remark
4.10
below on how
\mathcal{E}_{X}
is, in some sense, more naturally related to the
energy norm
error for an alternative reconstruction of the numerical solution.

Remark 4.9

(Rate of convergence of the estimator for smooth solutions)
.

Although
\eta_{J}
is not generally efficient with respect to the smallest of
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
and
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
, this does not contradict the fact that
\eta_{J}
still has optimal rates of convergence with respect to the time-step sizes if the solution has some additional regularity.
As mentioned already in Section
4.1
, for a fixed problem with
u
sufficiently regular, then
a priori
error analysis shows that

\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
,
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}
and thus also
\eta_{J}
all have an overall first-order rate of convergence with respect to the time-step sizes.
We stress that there is no contradiction here with the earlier discussion of the lack of efficiency of the estimators, since the
a priori
analysis results concern a fixed problem and considers the limit of small time-steps, whereas the efficiency of the estimators is a stronger property which must take into account a large range of problems and choices of time-step size.

4.4
A posteriori error bound for the energy norm error and a hypercircle theorem

We now turn to the
a posteriori
error bounds for the energy norm.
Recall that by definition
u_{\tau}
is taken to be left-continuous and satisfies
u_{\tau}(t_{n})=u_{n}
, for all
n=0,\dots,N
, c.f. (
4.1
).
Therefore, it makes sense to consider the energy norm of the error between
u
and its piecewise constant approximation
u_{\tau}
, namely

\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{E}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\lVert u(T)-u_{\tau}(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla(u-u_{\tau})\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

(4.33)

Likewise, we can also consider the energy norm of the error between
u
and the continuous piecewise affine approximation
U_{\tau}
, given by

\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{E}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\lVert u(T)-U_{\tau}(T)\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla(u-U_{\tau})\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

(4.34)

We will see shortly below (c.f. Corollary
4.5
) that, for the special case of vanishing data oscillation, i.e.
f=f_{\tau}
, we have the Pythagoras identity

\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{E}^{2}+\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{E}^{2}=\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{E}^{2}=\eta_{J}^{2},

(4.35)

where we recall that the temporal jump estimator
\eta_{J}
is defined in (
4.10
) above.
Observe that the equality
\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{E}=\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}=\eta_{J}
holds since
u_{\tau}(T)=U_{\tau}(T)=u_{N}
the value of
u_{\tau}
on the final time-interval
I_{N}
.
Similar to the problems encountered with regards to the efficiency of
\eta_{J}
in the
X
-norm setting, we note that Example
4.7
above can be extended to show that, in general, the estimator
\eta_{J}
is not an efficient one for either of
\lVert u-u_{\tau}\rVert_{E}
or
\lVert u-U_{\tau}\rVert_{E}
.

However, the Pythagoras identity expressed by (
4.35
) suggests that some notion of efficiency can be recovered by changing point of view, since it is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lVert u-\overline{u}_{\tau}\rVert_{E}=\frac{1}{2}\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{E}=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{J},

\displaystyle\overline{u}_{\tau}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(u_{\tau}+U_{\tau}).

(4.36)

The identity (
4.36
) shows that the exact solution
u
and its approximations
u_{\tau}
and
U_{\tau}
all lie on a hypercircle of radius
\frac{1}{2}\eta_{J}
and centre
\overline{u}_{\tau}
, when the geometry is determined by the inner-product related to the energy norm.
Thus, the estimator
\eta_{J}
is efficient (in fact, exact) with regards to the energy norm error when we regard the function
\overline{u}_{\tau}
as the notion of numerical solution to be compared against the true solution.
We shall see that it is the second form (
4.36
) that generalizes most readily to the general case where we no longer require the data oscillation to vanish exactly.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\overline{u}_{\tau}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(u_{\tau}+U_{\tau})
.
We have the bounds

\displaystyle\lVert u-\overline{u}_{\tau}\rVert_{E}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\eta_{J}+\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,E},

(4.37a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\eta_{J}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert u-\overline{u}_{\tau}\rVert_{E}+\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,E},

(4.37b)

where the data oscillation term
\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,E}
is defined by

\eta_{\mathrm{osc},\tau,E}\coloneqq\sup_{\varphi\in Y\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\langle f-f_{\tau},\varphi\rangle\mathrm{d}t}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}}.

(4.38)

Proof.

Recall that
Z=X\times L^{2}(\Omega)
.
We start by defining
(\bm{u}_{\tau},\bm{U}_{\tau},\overline{\bm{u}}_{\tau})\in Z^{3}
by

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{\tau}\coloneqq(u_{\tau},u_{\tau}(T)),

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{\tau}\coloneqq(U_{\tau},U_{\tau}(T))

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{u}}_{\tau}\coloneqq(\overline{u}_{\tau},\overline{u}_{\tau}(T)).

(4.39)

We also define
\bm{u}\coloneqq(u,u(T))\in Z
. Then, Theorem
3.4
shows that

\lVert u-\overline{u}_{\tau}\rVert_{E}=\lVert\bm{u}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\tau}\rVert_{Z}=\sup_{\varphi\in Y\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{Z}(\bm{u}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\tau},\varphi)}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}}.

(4.40)

It follows from the weak formulation (
3.23
) that, for any
\varphi\in Y
,

B_{Z}(\bm{u}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\tau},\varphi)=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f,\varphi\rangle\mathrm{d}t+(u_{0},\varphi(0))_{\Omega}\\
-(\overline{u}_{\tau}(T),\varphi(T))_{\Omega}-\int_{0}^{T}\left[-\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,\overline{u}_{\tau}\rangle+(\nabla\overline{u}_{\tau},\nabla\varphi)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(4.41)

Consider now a fixed but arbitrary
\varphi\in Y
.
Integrating-by-parts with respect to time and using the identities
U_{\tau}(T)=u_{\tau}(T)=\overline{u}_{\tau}(T)
,
U_{\tau}(0)=u_{0}
, we obtain from (
4.6
) that

0=(\overline{u}_{\tau}(T),\varphi(T))_{\Omega}-(u_{0},\varphi(0))_{\Omega}+\int_{0}^{T}\left[-\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,U_{\tau}\rangle+(\nabla u_{\tau},\nabla\varphi)_{\Omega}-\langle f_{\tau},\varphi\rangle\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(4.42)

Therefore, adding (
4.42
) to (
4.41
) above and noting some cancellations, we find that

B_{Z}(\bm{u}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\tau},\varphi)=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f-f_{\tau},\varphi\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,\overline{u}_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rangle+(\nabla(u_{\tau}-\overline{u}_{\tau}),\nabla\varphi)_{\Omega}\right)\mathrm{d}t.

(4.43)

Crucially, observe that

\overline{u}_{\tau}-U_{\tau}=u_{\tau}-\overline{u}_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}).

(4.44)

Hence, (
4.43
) simplifies to

B_{Z}(\bm{u}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{\tau},\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rangle+(\nabla\varphi,\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}))_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\langle f-f_{\tau},\varphi\rangle\mathrm{d}t,

(4.45)

which holds for arbitrary
\varphi\in Y
.
Therefore, to prove (
4.37
), it is enough to show that

\sup_{\varphi\in Y\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rangle+(\nabla\varphi,\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}))_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}}=\eta_{J}.

(4.46)

To show (
4.46
), let
R\colon Y\rightarrow Y
denote the time-reversal map
R\varphi=\varphi(T-\cdot)
. Note that
\lVert\mathbb{R}\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}=\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}
for all
\varphi\in Y
, i.e.
R
is an isometry when
Y
is equipped with the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}
.
It is clear that a change of variables gives

\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle\partial_{t}\varphi,u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rangle+(\nabla\varphi,\nabla(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}))_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t=B_{Z}(\bm{v},R\varphi),

(4.47)

where
\bm{v}\coloneqq(R(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}),0)\in Z
. Therefore, we obtain (
4.46
) from the identities

\sup_{\varphi\in Y\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{Z}(\bm{v},R\varphi)}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}}=\sup_{\varphi\in Y\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{Z}(\bm{v},R\varphi)}{\lVert R\varphi\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}}=\sup_{\widetilde{\varphi}\in Y\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B_{Z}(\bm{v},\widetilde{\varphi})}{\lVert\widetilde{\varphi}\rVert_{Y,\mathrm{sym}}}\\
\underset{\eqref{eq:Z_infsup_1}}{=}\lVert\bm{v}\rVert_{Z}=\lVert R(u_{\tau}-U_{\tau})\rVert_{X}=\lVert u_{\tau}-U_{\tau}\rVert_{X}=\eta_{J}.

(4.48)

This completes the proof of (
4.37
).
∎

Theorem
4.4
immediately implies the hypercircle theorem/Pythagoras identity as a corollary when the data oscillation vanishes.

Corollary 4.5

(Hypercircle/Pythagoras identity)
.

Suppose that
f=f_{\tau}
.
Then (
4.35
) and (
4.36
) hold.

Remark 4.10

(Relation to augmented error measures)
.

Recalling Remark
4.8
, several works in the literature have treated augmented error measures, such as the quantity
\mathcal{E}_{X}
defined in (
4.32
) above.
The results in this section give some insight into these alternative error measures, since in the case of vanishing data oscillation, we have the equivalence

2\lVert u-\overline{u}_{\tau}\rVert_{E}\leq\mathcal{E}_{X}\leq 4\lVert u-\overline{u}_{\tau}\rVert_{E},

(4.49)

which follows easily from
2\lVert u-\overline{u}_{\tau}\rVert=\eta_{J}\leq\mathcal{E}_{X}
by the triangle inequality, and from
\mathcal{E}_{X}\leq 2\eta_{J}=4\lVert u-\overline{u}_{\tau}\rVert_{E}
by Theorem
4.3
.
Therefore, the quantity
\mathcal{E}_{X}
is seen to be equivalent to the energy norm error
\lVert u-\overline{u}_{\tau}\rVert_{E}
, at least globally in space and in time.

(Bibliographical remarks)

Prager and Synge
[

]
showed a hypercircle identity for approximations of the system of elasticity.
In fact, Prager and Synge
[
40
, p. 248]
originally suggested the idea of considering the center of the circle as the approximate solution, in this case
\overline{u}_{\tau}
as we have done in Theorem
4.4
above.
Their result has since been extended to a wide range of problems, especially in the context of elliptic problems
[
8
,
12
,
18
,
21
,
45
,

]
.
The hypercircle theorem is thus a fundamental concept in
a posteriori
error analysis, and it is well-understood how it relates to the primal and dual formulations of problems that have an energy-minimization principle.

5
Discretization in time and in space

We now turn to a fully discrete approximation of (
1.1
). For simplicity, we shall restrict our attention to the simplest case of a conforming finite element method on a fixed spatial mesh coupled with the implicit Euler discretization in time.
In order to make use of some results in the literature, we shall restrict our attention to problems in at most three spatial dimensions, i.e. we now assume that
1\leq d\leq 3
.
We further assume that the bounded open set
\Omega
is in additional polytopal and has Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be a conforming simplicial mesh on
\Omega
.
For each element
K\in\mathcal{T}
, we let
h_{K}
denote the diameter of
K
. We define the mesh-size function
h_{\mathcal{T}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
by
h_{\mathcal{T}}|_{K}\coloneqq h_{K}
for each element
K\in\mathcal{T}
.
In the following, the notation for inequalities
a\lesssim b
will allow the hidden constant to depend on the shape-regularity parameter of
\mathcal{T}
, defined as
\theta_{\mathcal{T}}\coloneqq\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{h_{K}}{\rho_{K}}
, where
\rho_{K}
denotes the diameter of the largest inscribed balls in
K
. However, the hidden constants will be otherwise independent of the size of the mesh elements.
Let
p\geq 1
denote a fixed integer which will correspond to the polynomial degree of the finite element space to be defined below.
The
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
-conforming finite element space of degree
p
is denoted by
V_{h}
, and is defined by

V_{h}\coloneqq\{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\;v|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(K)\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}\},

(5.1)

where
\mathcal{P}_{p}(K)
denotes the space of real-valued polynomials of total degree at most
p
on
K
.
Keeping the same notation as in Section
4.1
regarding the partition of
(0,T)
into time-steps, we consider a fully discrete approximation defined as follows: for each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, find
u_{h,\tau,n}\in V_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{u_{h,\tau,n}-u_{h,\tau,n-1}}{\tau_{n}},v_{h}\right)_{\Omega}+(\nabla u_{h,\tau,n},\nabla v_{h})_{\Omega}=(f_{h,\tau,n},v_{h})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall v_{h}\in V_{h},

(5.2)

where
u_{h,\tau,0}\in V_{h}
is some discrete approximation of the initial datum
u_{0}
, and where
f_{h,\tau,n}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is some discrete approximation of
f(t_{n})
. In the analysis below, we allow for rather general choices of
f_{h,\tau,n}
, with the only assumptions required being that
f_{h,\tau,n}|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(K)
for each
K\in\mathcal{T}
, for each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.

Piecewise-constant-in-time reconstruction.

Let
\mathbb{V}_{h,\tau}^{+}
denote the space of all left-continuous functions
v\colon[0,T]\rightarrow V_{h}
that are piecewise constant with respect to the time partition
\{I_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, i.e.
v|_{I_{n}}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(I_{n};V_{h})
for each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, where we recall that
I_{n}=(t_{n-1},t_{n})
is the
n
-th time-interval.
Note that a function
v\in\mathbb{V}_{h,\tau}^{+}
thus has a well-defined value
v(t)\in V_{h}
for each time
t\in[0,T]
. Note that from this point of view, functions in
\mathbb{V}_{h,\tau}^{+}
that agree for almost all
t\in[0,T]
are not identified.
We then define
u_{h,\tau}\in\mathbb{V}_{h,\tau}^{+}
as the unique function that satisfies

\displaystyle u_{h,\tau}(0)=u_{h,\tau,0},

\displaystyle u_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}=u_{h,\tau,n}\quad\forall n\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

(5.3)

Observe also that left-continuity of
u_{h,\tau}
ensures that
u_{h,\tau}(t_{n})=u_{h,\tau,n}
for each
n\in\{0,\dots,N\}
.

Continuous piecewise-affine-in-time reconstruction.

We define also
U_{h,\tau}\colon[0,T]\rightarrow V_{h}
as the unique continuous piecewise-affine function that satisfies

\displaystyle U_{h,\tau}(t)=\frac{t-t_{n-1}}{\tau_{n}}u_{h,\tau,n}+\frac{t_{n}-t}{\tau_{n}}u_{h,\tau,n-1}

\displaystyle\forall t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n}].

(5.4)

Observe that, by definition,
U_{h,\tau}(t_{n})=u_{h,\tau,n}
for each
n\in\{0,\dots,N\}
.

Reformulation of the fully discrete scheme.

We now define the function
f_{h,\tau}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
by
f_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}\coloneqq f_{h,\tau,n}
for
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, where
f_{h,\tau,n}
is the chosen approximation of
f(t_{n})
appearing in (
5.2
) above.
Under the assumptions above on the
f_{h,\tau,n}
, it follows that
f_{h,\tau}
is piecewise constant in time with respect to the time-intervals
\{(t_{n-1},t_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
and piecewise polynomial of degree at most
p
in space with respect to the mesh
\mathcal{T}
.
It is then clear that (
5.2
) is equivalent to:

(\partial_{t}U_{h,\tau}(t),v_{h})_{\Omega}+(\nabla u_{h,\tau}(t),\nabla v_{h})_{\Omega}=(f_{h,\tau}(t),v_{h})_{\Omega}\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h},\quad\text{a.e.\ }t\in I_{n}.

(5.5)

for each
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.

5.1
Construction of the equilibrated flux

We consider now
a posteriori
error bounds for the error based on the construction of an equilibrated flux.
We construct a discrete and locally computable
H(\mathrm{div})
-conforming vector field
\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}
that satisfies the equilibration property

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U_{h,\tau}+\nabla{\cdot}\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}=f_{h,\tau}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(5.6)

where
U_{h,\tau}
is defined above, and
f_{h,\tau}
will be further specified below.
The construction follows the approach from
[
19
,
20
]
, which provides a general construction that was designed to accommodate variable polynomial degrees in
hp
-FEM and also mesh modification between the time-steps.
Since we are restricting ourselves here to the case of a single polynomial degree for all mesh elements and a single mesh for all time-steps, we shall give here a slightly simplified version of the construction from
[
19
,
20
]
.
The construction of the equilibrated flux is based on solving local mixed FEM problems using Raviart–Thomas–Nédélec (RTN) elements on the patches of elements surrounding each vertex of the mesh. The problem for each vertex-patch is independent from the others, so could be solved in parallel.
For an introduction to mixed finite element methods, including RTN spaces, we refer the reader to the books
[
6
,
17
]
.
The final global equilibrated flux
\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}
will then be obtained by summing the contributions from each vertex patch.

Local equilibration on vertex patches.

Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the set of vertices of the mesh
\mathcal{T}
.
Let
\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}=\mathcal{V}\cap\Omega
denote the set of interior vertices and let
\mathcal{V}_{\partial\Omega}=\mathcal{V}\cap\partial\Omega
denote the boundary vertices.
For each vertex
{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}
, let
\psi_{{\bm{a}}}
denote the hat function associated with
{\bm{a}}
.
Note that
\{\psi_{{\bm{a}}}\}_{{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}}
forms a partition of unity of
\overline{\Omega}
, i.e.
\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}}\psi_{{\bm{a}}}(x)=1
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
.
Let
\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}}
denote the set of all elements of
\mathcal{T}
that contain
{\bm{a}}
, and let
{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}\coloneqq\bigcup\{K\colon K\in\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}}\}
denote the vertex patch around
{\bm{a}}
, i.e. the union of all elements of
\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}}
.
With the convention that elements of
\mathcal{T}
are considered to be closed sets, it follows that
{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}
is the support of
\psi_{{\bm{a}}}
.
Let the integer
\widetilde{p}\geq p+1
denote a fixed choice of polynomial degree that will be used for the flux reconstruction, where it is recalled that
p
is the polynomial degree used for
V_{h}
above.
For each
{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}
, let the spaces
\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}})
and
\bm{RTN}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}})
be defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{q_{h}\in L^{2}({\omega_{{\bm{a}}}})\colon q_{h}|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{p}}(K)\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}}\},

(5.7a)

\displaystyle\bm{RTN}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\bm{v}_{h}\in L^{2}({\omega_{{\bm{a}}}};\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon\bm{v}_{h}|_{K}\in\bm{RTN}_{\widetilde{p}}(K)\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}}\},

(5.7b)

where
\bm{RTN}_{\widetilde{p}}(K)\coloneqq\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{p}}(K;\mathbb{R}^{d})+\bm{x}\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{p}}(K)
denotes the RTN space of order
\widetilde{p}
on
K
. In other words,
\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}})
denotes the space of scalar functions that are piecewise polynomials of degree at most
\widetilde{p}
with respect to
\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}}
, and
\bm{RTN}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}})
denotes the space of vector fields that are piecewise RTN of order
\widetilde{p}
with respect to
\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}}
. Note that there are no continuity conditions across mesh elements in the definition of these spaces.
We now define the local mixed finite element spaces
Q_{h}^{{\bm{a}}}
and
\bm{V}_{h}^{\bm{a}}
by

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{{\bm{a}}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\left\{q_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}}),\quad\int_{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}q_{h}\mathrm{d}x=0\right\}&\text{if }{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\Omega},\\
\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}})&\text{if }{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\partial\Omega},\end{cases}

(5.8a)

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{h}^{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\left\{\bm{v}_{h}\in H(\Div,{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}})\cap\bm{RTN}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}})\,\quad\bm{v}_{h}\cdot\bm{n}=0\text{ on }\partial{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}\right\}&\text{if }{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\Omega},\\
\left\{\bm{v}_{h}\in H(\Div,{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}})\cap\bm{RTN}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}})\,\quad\bm{v}_{h}\cdot\bm{n}=0\text{ on }\partial{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}\setminus\partial\Omega\right\}&\text{if }{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\partial\Omega},\end{cases}

(5.8b)

where
\bm{n}
denotes the unit outward normal on
\partial{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}
.
Thus, in the case of an interior vertex
{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}
, the space
\bm{V}_{h}^{\bm{a}}
is the
H(\Div,{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}})
-conforming RTN space of order
\widetilde{p}
with a constraint of vanishing normal component on the boundary
\partial{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}
.
For a boundary vertex
{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\partial\Omega}
, the normal component of functions in
\bm{V}_{h}^{\bm{a}}
are constrained only on the faces of the patch-boundary
\partial{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}
that are not part of the domain boundary
\partial\Omega
. In other words, for interior vertices, these spaces correspond to the usual RTN mixed finite element spaces for an elliptic problem over
{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}
with a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition on
\partial{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}
, whereas for boundary vertices, these correspond to RTN mixed finite element spaces for a problem with mixed Neumann and Dirichlet conditions, with a homogeneous Neumann condition only on
\partial{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}\setminus\partial\Omega
.
For each
{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}
and each
n\in{1,\dots,N}
, define the piecewise polynoimal function
g_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}\colon{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

g_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}\coloneqq\psi_{{\bm{a}}}f_{h,\tau}|_{{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}\times I_{n}}-\psi_{{\bm{a}}}\partial_{t}U_{h,\tau}|_{{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}\times I_{n}}-\nabla\psi_{{\bm{a}}}{\cdot}\nabla u_{h,\tau}|_{{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}\times I_{n}}.

(5.9)

Note in particular that the choice
\widetilde{p}\geq p+1
is used above to ensure that the terms
\psi_{{\bm{a}}}f_{h,\tau,n}
and
\psi_{{\bm{a}}}\partial_{t}U_{h,\tau}
are piecewise polynomials of degree less than or equal to
\widetilde{p}
with respect to the spatial mesh
\mathcal{T}
. Therefore
g_{h,\tau}^{a,n}\in\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{p}}(\mathcal{T}^{{\bm{a}}})
for each
{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}
.
Note also that in the case of an interior vertex
{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\partial\Omega}
, choosing as a test function
v_{h}=\psi_{{\bm{a}}}
in (
5.5
) above implies that

\int_{{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}}g_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}\mathrm{d}x=(f_{h,\tau,n},\psi_{{\bm{a}}})_{\Omega}-(\partial_{t}U_{h,\tau},\psi_{{\bm{a}}})_{\Omega}-(\nabla u_{h,\tau},\nabla\psi_{{\bm{a}}})_{\Omega}=0,

(5.10)

where we recall that
f_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}=f_{h,\tau,n}
by definition.
Therefore,
g_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}
has zero-mean value in the case of an interior vertex
{\bm{a}}
, and thus we conclude that that
g_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}\in Q_{h}^{{\bm{a}}}
for all vertices
{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}
, where it is recalled that
Q_{h}^{{\bm{a}}}
was defined in (
5.8a
) above.
Next, for each time-step
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and each vertex
{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}
, we define the local flux contribution
\bm{\sigma}_{h}^{{\bm{a}},n}\in\bm{V}_{h}^{\bm{a}}
by

\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}\coloneqq\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{v}_{h,\tau}\in\bm{V}_{h}^{\bm{a}}\\
\nabla\cdot\bm{v}_{h,\tau}=g_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}\end{subarray}}\lVert\bm{v}_{h,\tau}+\psi_{{\bm{a}}}\nabla u_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}\rVert_{{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}}.

(5.11)

Crucially, the fact that
\bm{\sigma}_{h}^{{\bm{a}},n}
is well-defined in the case of an interior vertex is a consequence of (
5.10
).
Indeed, this corresponds to the zero mean-value compatibility condition that is required on the source term when considering a mixed finite element discretization of a Poisson equation with pure Neumann boundary conditions. The case of boundary vertices is simpler still, because it is then not necessary to require that
\int_{{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}}g_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}\mathrm{d}x=0
since vector fields in
\bm{V}_{h}^{\bm{a}}
are then only constrained on part of the boundary of
{\omega_{{\bm{a}}}}
.

In order to pass from the local flux contributions to a global equilibrated flux, we now extend the individual local contributions by zero to the whole domain
\Omega
. Note that boundary conditions imposed on vector fields in
\bm{V}_{h}^{\bm{a}}
imply that the extension by zero of
\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}
to the whole domain
\Omega
, also denoted
\bm{\sigma}_{h}^{{\bm{a}},n}
, is in
H(\Div,\Omega)
.
Therefore, we may define the global flux
\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}\in L^{2}(0,T;H(\Div,\Omega))
as the unique piecewise constant-in-time vector field such that

\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}\coloneqq\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}}\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}\quad\forall n\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

(5.12)

It follows that
\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}\in H(\Div,\Omega)
for each
n
, and thus
\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}\in L^{2}(0,T;H(\Div,\Omega))
. We find that the divergence
\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}
satisfies then:

\begin{split}\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}&=\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}}\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}=\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}}g_{h,\tau}^{{\bm{a}},n}\\
&=\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}}\left[\psi_{{\bm{a}}}f_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}-\psi_{{\bm{a}}}\partial_{t}U_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}-\nabla\psi_{{\bm{a}}}\cdot\nabla u_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}\right]\\
&=\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}}\psi_{{\bm{a}}}(f_{h,\tau}-\partial_{t}U_{h,\tau})|_{I_{n}}-\left(\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}}\nabla\psi_{{\bm{a}}}\right)\cdot\nabla u_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}\\
&=f_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}-\partial_{t}U_{h,\tau}|_{I_{n}}\end{split}

(5.13)

where in passing to the last line above we have used the partition of unity property which entails that
\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}}\psi_{{\bm{a}}}\equiv 1
in
\Omega
and
\sum_{{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{V}}\nabla\psi_{{\bm{a}}}\equiv 0
in
\Omega
.
Thus, on re-arranging, we have the equilibration identity (
5.6
) above.

Remark 5.1

(Data approximation)
.

For the sake of simplicity, we have considered here some abstract approximation
f_{h,\tau}
that approximates
f
without entering into too much detail. A more precise treatment can be found in
[
19
]
, which handles variable polynomials degrees between mesh elements, and gives a more specific construction of a suitable approximation of the right-hand side.

5.2
Error bound for the
L^{2}(H^{1})\cap H^{1}(H^{-1})
norm error

Given the equilibrated flux
\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}
defined in (
5.12
), we now consider a posteriori error estimators for the
L^{2}(H^{1})\cap H^{1}(H^{-1})
norm of the error.

Theorem 5.1

(Upper bound)
.

Let
\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}\in L^{2}(0,T;H(\Div,\Omega))
be defined by (
5.12
). Then,

\lVert u-U_{h,\tau}\rVert_{Y}\leq\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(\lVert\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}+\nabla U_{h,\tau}\rVert_{\Omega}+\lVert f-f_{h,\tau}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}t+\lVert u_{0}-u_{h,\tau,0}\rVert_{\Omega}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(5.14)

Proof.

Theorem
3.1
shows that

\lVert u-U_{h,\tau}\rVert_{Y}^{2}=\lVert\mathcal{R}_{Y}(U_{h,\tau})\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}+\lVert u_{0}-u_{h,\tau,0}\rVert_{\Omega}^{2},

(5.15)

where
\mathcal{R}(U_{h,\tau})\in X^{*}
was defined in (
4.13
), and where we note that
u(0)=u_{0}
and
U_{h,\tau}(0)=u_{h,\tau,0}
.
Using the equilibration identity (
5.6
), we find that

\begin{split}\langle\mathcal{R}_{Y}(U_{h,\tau}),v\rangle&=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle f,v\rangle-(\partial_{t}U_{h,\tau},v)_{\Omega}-(\nabla U_{h,\tau},\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle f-f_{h,\tau},v\rangle+(f_{h,\tau}-\partial_{t}U_{h,\tau},v)_{\Omega}-(\nabla U_{h,\tau},\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle f-f_{h,\tau},v\rangle+(\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau},v)_{\Omega}-(\nabla U_{h,\tau},\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\langle f-f_{h,\tau},v\rangle-(\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}+\nabla U_{h,\tau},\nabla v)_{\Omega}\right]\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(5.16)

for all
v\in X
, where in passing to the last equality above we have used the identity
(\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau},v)_{\Omega}=-(\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau},\nabla v)_{\Omega}
for all
v\in X
, a.e. in
(0,T)
.
Therefore, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the definition of
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
imply that

\lvert\langle\mathcal{R}_{Y}(U_{h,\tau}),v\rangle\rvert\leq\int_{0}^{T}\left(\lVert f-f_{h,\tau}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+\lVert\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}+\nabla U_{h,\tau}\rVert_{\Omega}\right)\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}t\quad\forall v\in X,

and thus the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality implies that

\lVert\mathcal{R}_{Y}(U_{h,\tau})\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{T}\left(\lVert f-f_{h,\tau}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+\lVert\bm{\sigma}_{h,\tau}+\nabla U_{h,\tau}\rVert_{\Omega}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

(5.17)

Combining (
5.15
) and (
5.17
) then gives (
5.14
).
∎

Conclusion

In the analysis presented above, we have demonstrated that the analytical properties of
a posteriori
error estimators present a number of challenges and subtleties. In particular, the efficiency of the estimators can depend strongly on the choice of norm in which to measure the error, but also on the choice of reconstruction of the numerical solution that is considered.
Understanding the relationships between the possible choices of norms and of notion of numerical solutions is crucial for interpreting the results of the estimators in practical computations.
