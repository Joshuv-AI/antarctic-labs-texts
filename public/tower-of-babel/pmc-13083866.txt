PsAvh109 suppresses SA-triggered immunity by mimicking TPL function to disrupt mediator complex assembly
Authors: Xinwei Tan, Yujing Sun, Zhaomei Qi, Yonghui Miao, Zhen Liu, Yiman Wan, Xiaoyuan Hou, Hengjing Li, Wenxiu Li, Yuan Chen, Qian Xu, Qunqing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pathogens disrupt transcriptional hubs to subvert host immunity, yet the spatiotemporal mechanisms remain enigmatic. Here, we report a pathogen effector interferes with the core eukaryotic transcriptional machinery by acting as a functional mimic of host repressors, deploying this suppression in synchrony with the plant immune rhythm. We discover the Phytophthora sojae nuclear effector directly targets the host Mediator complex. Crucially, PsAvh109 emulates the host repressor TOPLESS (TPL), competitively occupying the Mediator subunit MED21 and locks the MED21-MED6 interaction interface, repressing salicylic acid (SA)-responsive defense genes. Strikingly, PsAvh109 expression is induced by host-derived SA, the very signal displaces TPL from MED21 to activate immunity. This enables the pathogen to release the essential effector PsAvh109 precisely when the host initiates defense, perpetuating transcriptional repression during a critical vulnerability window. Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized strategy where pathogens exploit host signaling dynamics to release effectors that enforce sustained repression of defense programs.

Introduction

Plants deploy multi-layered innate immunity to thwart pathogen invasion, initiated by the rapid recognition of microbial patterns and effectors and further modulated by phytohormone signaling1,2. This defense hinges on transcriptional reprogramming that redirects resources from growth to immunity. This process entails the rapid activation and repression of numerous genes, a transition that ultimately dictates the survival of plant3,4.

Studies in plants have revealed extensive crosstalk between growth and defense signaling networks, coordinated largely by phytohormones, such as salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), and auxin (IAA)5. These hormones serve as key regulators that fine-tune transcriptional responses through a complex interplay of transcription factors (TFs) and corepressors6. SA is indispensable for resistance against biotrophic pathogens, primarily by driving the activation of pathogenesis-related (PR) genes7. To this end, several TF families, notably WRKY and TGA (TGACG-binding factors), have been identified as pivotal regulators8. Specifically, NPR1 (Nonexpressor of PRs Genes 1) forms a transcriptional activation complex with TGA factors, which bind to the TGACG motif (as-1 element), a conserved cis-regulatory element enriched within the promoters of PR genes9. This NPR1-TGA module bridges SA perception with transcriptional activation, and is essential for mounting of robust immune responses against biotrophic pathogens10.

Plants must balance growth and immunity to optimize fitness5. The defense response is tightly controlled under normal growth, while recognition of invading pathogens triggers a timely, accurate, and effective immune response, including transcriptional response to SA11. This shift is driven by transcriptional reprogramming, which governed by conserved regulators including the Mediator complex12. As a central hub linking TFs to RNA polymerase II in eukaryotes, Mediator orchestrates the assembly of pre-initiation complexes (PICs) to modulate the expression of defense genes13,14. Its core functional module, comprising head (MED6, MED8, MED11, MED17, MED18, MED19, MED20, and MED22) and middle (MED1, MED4, MED7, MED9, MED10, MED21, and MED31) subunits, facilitates signal integration to mediate immune transitions15–19. Central to this process is the physical interaction between MED6 and MED21, a docking event that facilitates PIC recruitment and is indispensable for transcriptional activation20–23.

Crucially, transcriptional corepressor TOPLESS (TPL) targets this interface to enforce state-dependent repression. In Arabidopsis, TPL competitively binds the N-terminal domain of MED21, the identical region required for MED6 interaction, thereby obstructing the assembly of the Mediator complex24,25. TPL integrates signals from various phytohormone pathways (e.g., JA via JAZ/NINJA26,27, IAA via AUX/IAA28, ethylene via ERF29), yet its role in SA signaling remains enigmatic. Despite observations that TPL interacts with SA-associated repressors, such as NPR3 and NIMIN30,31, direct evidence demonstrating that TPL suppresses SA-responsive transcription has yet to be established.

In the dynamic arms race between plants and pathogens, pathogens have evolved preferentially mechanism to undermine host immunity by targeting the Mediator complex. For instance, the effector HaRxL44 from Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis (Hpa) destabilizes the Mediator subunit MED19a through a proteasome-dependent pathway. This destabilization perturbs defense gene reprogramming and augments susceptibility to biotrophic pathogens by attenuating SA-responsive transcription32. HaRxL44, belongs to an extraordinarily large oomycete effector superfamily with a conserved N-terminal RxLR (Arg-any amino acid-Arg-Leu) motif33. Our previous studies identified the transcriptional programming and functional synergy within the P. sojae RxLR effectors. PsAvh109 and other representative “immediate-early” RxLR effectors were highly expressed even prior to infection and were further rapidly induced following infection, while some “early” RxLR effectors were weakly expressed prior to infection but induced 20- to 120-fold during the first 12 h of infection. Misexpression of key “immediate-early” or “early” effectors severely reduced the virulence of P. sojae transformants33. This also implies that such effectors are deliberately expressed in response to a certain infection stage. However, the molecular mechanisms through which pathogens achieve precise temporal coordination of effector-mediated targeting events remain largely elusive. In this study, we demonstrate that the soybean pathogen P. sojae effector PsAvh109 is transcriptionally upregulated in response to host-derived SA signaling. Prior research has demonstrated that PsAvh109 suppresses PAMP-triggered immunity (PTI) induced by INF1 and XEG133,34. Although both the RxLR effectors PsAvh109 and HaRxL44 target the host Mediator complex, their underlying modes of action diverge fundamentally. In this study, we elucidate a molecular mechanism through which the soybean pathogen P. sojae effector PsAvh109 suppresses plant immunity. PsAvh109 functions by mimicking the host repressor TPL, competitively binding to MED21 to disrupt the MED21-MED6 interface, thereby repressing the expression of SA-responsive defense genes. Notably, PsAvh109 expression is transcriptionally induced by SA, the same endogenous signal that triggers TPL dissociation from MED21 to initiate immune activation. This temporal coupling allows PsAvh109 to exploit the SA-mediated immune activation window of host, ensuring maximal suppressive efficacy. Notably, this mechanism is evolutionarily conserved throughout the Phytophthora genus.

Collectively, our findings reveal a previously uncharacterized mechanism by which pathogens harness host signaling dynamics to temporally synchronize effector function with immune activation, highlighting a critical axis of pathogen adaptability within host-pathogen coevolution.

Results

SA induces PsAvh109 expression to synchronize effector deployment

Numerous effector genes of P. sojae are expressed during the early stages of infection, coinciding with the critical window of SA-mediated resistance to biotrophic pathogens. To investigate whether SA functions as an inductive signal for RxLR effector gene expression, P. sojae germ tubes were treated with 100 μM SA. Subsequent RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) revealed that 2608 genes were significantly upregulated and 2993 were downregulated in response to SA treatment compared with ethanol (EtOH) controls (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Among the previously annotated RxLR effectors, 64 exhibited significant upregulated, whereas only six were downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 1b). PsAvh109 was among the most strongly induced effectors and was selected for further investigation due to its demonstrated ability to suppress immune responses triggered by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) such as INF1 and XEG133,34.

To monitor protein accumulation, we generated an in situ complementation strain (C-PsAvh109-6His-mCherry) by replacing the endogenous PsAvh109 gene. Western blot analysis revealed that exogenous SA treatment significantly increased PsAvh109-6His-mCherry protein levels relative to EtOH controls (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Moreover, a comparison of the inductive effects of various exogenous phytohormones, such as SA, JA, and abscisic acid (ABA) demonstrated that SA exerted robust induction of PsAvh109 (Fig. 1a). To evaluate the physiological significance of host-derived SA, we utilized transgenic soybean roots expressing the salicylate hydroxylase NahG, which depletes endogenous SA35, alongside control roots. Quantitative analysis via LC-MS/MS revealed a substantial elevation of endogenous SA in infected control roots, rising from approximately 30 ng/g fresh weight (FW) in mock-inoculated tissues to 130 ng/g FW at 2 h post-inoculation (hpi). This transient peak in host SA accumulation was temporally coupled with the maximal transcriptional induction of PsAvh109 (Fig. 1c, d). In NahG-transgenic roots, both SA accumulation and PsAvh109 induction were significantly attenuated relative to controls, establishing a robust positive correlation between host SA titers and effector expression (Fig. 1c, d). Compared to the control, P. sojae infected NahG-transgenic roots exhibited a 1.4-fold increase in oospore production and a 1.8-fold increase in pathogen biomass (Supplementary Fig. 1d–f), underscoring the indispensable role of SA in plant defense against pathogen. Furthermore, homologous of PsAvh109 in P. capsici (DVH05_001429) and P. parasitica (PPTG_19802) also exhibited SA-inducible expression (Fig. 1e, f), suggesting that SA responsiveness is a conserved trait among these oomycete pathogens.

PsAvh109 requires nuclear localization to suppress immunity and promote the virulence of P. sojae

PsAvh109 is highly conserved across various Phytophthora species, including P. infestans, P. parasitica, P. cactorum and P. capsici. Notably, all identified PsAvh109 homologs harbor a predicted nuclear localization signal (NLS) (Fig. 2a and S2). The mature form of PsAvh109 fused with green fluorescent protein (GFP) accumulated in the nuclei of Nicotiana benthamiana epidermal cells, co-localizing with the nuclear marker histone 2B fused to mCherry (H2B-mCherry) (Fig. 2b). To disrupt nuclear localization, we generated NLS mutants (PsAvh109AAA and PsAvh109NES) by either substituting all conserved lysine (K) and arginine (R) residues with alanine (A) or by appending a nuclear export signal (NES) (Fig. 2a). These modifications abolished nuclear targeting, as evidenced by their exclusion from H2B-mCherry labeled nuclei (Fig. 2b). To directly monitor effector translocation during the infection process, a functional PsAvh109-GFP fusion was expressed in P. sojae. Confocal microscopy revealed prominent GFP signals within the nuclei of soybean cells in immediate proximity to invading hyphae; notably, no such nuclear signal was observed in plants infected with a control strain expressing GFP alone (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Consistently, PsAvh109-GFP was detected in nuclear fractions isolated from infected soybean tissues, providing definitive evidence that PsAvh109 is translocated into the host nucleus during natural infection (Supplementary Fig. 3c).

In soybean hairy roots, the expression of PsAvh109, but not the NLS mutant PsAvh109AAA, significantly enhanced P. sojae virulence, as evidenced by 1.4-fold increase in oospore production and a 1.8-fold increase in pathogen biomass relative to the mCherry control (Fig. 2c–e). Similarly, PsAvh109 promoted P. capsici infection in N. benthamiana, resulting in a 1.3-fold expansion in lesion diameter and a 2.3-fold increase in pathogen biomass (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c). In contrast, the PsAvh109AAA mutant exhibited no such stimulatory effect. Furthermore, PsAvh109 also suppressed INF1-induced programmed cell death (PCD), a suppressive activity that was abolished in the PsAvh109AAA mutant (Supplementary Fig. 4a).

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of PsAvh109 (KO-PsAvh109) in P. sojae did not impair mycelial growth or zoospore production (Supplementary Fig. 4d–f). However, it markedly attenuated virulence, resulting in reduced lesions on soybean hypocotyls and a 50% decrease in pathogen biomass compared with the wild-type strain. Pathogenicity was fully restored in the complementation strain (C-PsAvh109), whereas the nuclear localization-deficient mutant (C-PsAvh109AAA) failed to rescue the virulence defect (Fig. 2f, g). These data indicate that PsAvh109 is essential for full virulence and that its function is strictly dependent on nuclear localization.

PsAvh109 targets the Mediator subunit MED21 to disrupt defense transcription

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening identified Glycine max MED21 (GmMED21), a conserved subunit within the middle module of the transcriptional Mediator complex36,37, as a candidate interactor of PsAvh109 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Further analysis revealed that PsAvh109 also associates with the N. benthamiana homolog, NbMED21, indicating a conserved interaction across different host species (Supplementary Fig. 5). Subsequent directed Y2H assays confirmed the specificity of this interaction, demonstrating that PsAvh109 binds to GmMED21 but not to adjacent Mediator subunits (GmMED6, GmMED7, GmMED10, GmMED19, or GmMED25) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays revealed a robust luminescence signal in N. benthamiana leaves co-expressing GmMED21-nLUC and cLUC-PsAvh109; however, this signal was abolished in NLS mutants, confirming the in vivo interaction between GmMED21 and PsAvh109 (Fig. 3a). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments further corroborated this interaction, as GFP-PsAvh109 effectively co-precipitated GmMED21-mCherry-3HA from N. benthamiana extracts. Consistent with the negative controls, neither GFP-PsAvh109NES nor GFP-PsAvh109AAA was detected in the immunoprecipitated fractions (Fig. 3b). Conversely, in vitro pull-down assays showed that GmMED21-His interacted with GST-PsAvh109, as well as with the GST-PsAvh109NES and GST-PsAvh109AAA mutants (Fig. 3c). These results indicate that PsAvh109 physically interacts with GmMED21 both in vivo and in vitro. To evaluate the roles of MED21 as the effector target of PsAvh109, we performed Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression of INF1 or inoculated P. capsici onto RNAi-NbMED21 N. benthamiana leaves. INF1-induced PCD was significantly attenuated in NbMED21-knockdown tissues (Fig. 3d). Compared with the wild-type (WT) control, silencing of NbMED21 resulted in a 1.2-fold increase in lesion diameter and a two-fold increase in P. capsici biomass in infected leaves (Fig. 3d–f). Notably, the silencing of NbMED21 abolished the virulence-promoting activity of PsAvh109; specifically, as PsAvh109 expression failed to further enhance P. capsici infection in NbMED21-silenced tissues (Fig. 3g). In these silenced tissues, lesion diameter and pathogen biomass were comparable to those observed in the GFP control tissues (Fig. 3g–i). These results suggest that the interaction with MED21 is essential for PsAvh109 to facilitate pathogen infection.

To characterize the transcriptomic changes orchestrated by PsAvh109 during infection, we performed RNA-seq analysis on soybean plants inoculated with either wild-type P. sojae P6497 or an isogenic PsAvh109 knockout mutant (KO-PsAvh109). Comparative transcriptomic profiling revealed a profound shift in the expression landscape of KO-PsAvh109 infected soybean, with 3,518 genes significantly upregulated and 4,562 genes downregulated compared with P6497-infected controls (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) uncovered a significant overrepresentation of defense-related biological processes, notably defense responses, response to SA, and response to chitin (Fig. 3j). Notably, the presence of PsAvh109 led to the potent repression of SA-responsive genes, including seven PR1 homologs (GmPR1-1/2/3/4/5/6/7) that are vital for SA-mediated immunity (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Furthermore, we quantified PR1-1 expression levels in soybean inoculated with either the wild-type strain P6497 or the KO-PsAvh109 mutant. In the early stages of infection, PR1-1 transcription is sharply upregulated in plants inoculated with the KO-PsAvh109 mutant. Conversely, PR1-1 transcript accumulation follows a more gradual trajectory in plants infected with the wild-type strain P6497 (Fig. 3k). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PsAvh109 suppresses SA-dependent defense transcription, specifically PR1 expression, during compatible host interactions, a process likely driven by its interference with GmMED21.

GmMED21 and GmMED6 act as the molecular switch of SA controlled gene transcription and blocked by PsAvh109 via competitive binding

The head and middle modules constitute the functional core of the Mediator complex38,39. Analysis of the promoter regions (1 kb upstream of the start codon) of all GmPR1 homologs revealed the presence of the as-1 (TGACG) element, a conserved binding site for TGA TFs (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by qPCR (ChIP-qPCR) was employed to examine the recruitment of GmMED21 and GmMED6 to the GmPR1 promote in transgenic soybean hairy roots. The P1 (a sequence containing an as-1 element) region of the GmPR1-1 promoter exhibited robust enrichment of both GmMED21 and GmMED6. No significant enrichment of GmMED21 or GmMED6 was detected with the promoter of control gene, GmCYP2 (Supplementary Fig. 7b). These results suggest that GmMED21 and GmMED6 are integral to the transcription of SA-responsive genes, thereby positively regulating plant immunity. Furthermore, silencing of GmMED21 or GmMED6 via RNA interference (RNAi) significantly attenuated the transcriptional levels of GmPR1-1 and GmPR1-6 (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Functional validation demonstrated that the silencing of GmMED21 or GmMED6 in soybean root hairs led to a 1.7-fold increase in P. sojae oospore production and a 2-fold increase in pathogen biomass compared to the control, indicating markedly enhanced susceptibility (Supplementary Fig. 7d–f).

The interaction between MED21 and MED6 is crucial for the full activation of multiple activators40,41. In this study, we generated two GmMED21 truncation variants: a N-terminal deletion mutant (GmMED2116-139) and a discrete N-terminal fragment (GmMED211-15) (Fig. 4a). Y2H and LCI assays mapped the GmMED6 interaction with the N-terminal 15-aa domain of GmMED21. Notably, this same region mediates the interaction with PsAvh109. The GmMED211-15 retained the ability to interact with both PsAvh109 and MED6, whereas GmMED2116-139 failed to interact with either (Fig. 4b–d). Based on the shared binding domain, we hypothesized that PsAvh109 competitively displaces GmMED6 from GmMED21, thereby disrupting the host resistance. Yeast three-hybrid (Y3H) assays confirmed that PsAvh109, but not the PsAvh109AAA mutant, disrupted the GmMED21-GmMED6 interaction (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, in vitro Pull-down assays validated that GST-PsAvh109 reduced MBP-GmMED6 binding to GmMED21-His in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4f). Consistent with this, Co-IP assays revealed that PsAvh109-GFP co-expression attenuated the association between GmMED21-HA and GmMED6-3Flag (Fig. 4g). In LCI assays, co-expression of GFP-PsAvh109 with GmMED21-nLUC and cLUC-GmMED6 in N. benthamiana significantly reduced luminescence intensity compared to the GFP control, whereas GFP-PsAvh109AAA had no effect (Fig. 4h, i). ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed that PsAvh109 was significantly enriched at the P1 region of GmPR1-1 promoter (Fig. 4j). These findings suggest that PsAvh109 interferes with the assembly of the pre-initiation complex by outcompeting MED6 for MED21 binding, thereby modulating the transcription of SA-responsive genes.

The N-terminal domain of MED21 is a hotspot for competitive binding by TPL and PsAvh109

Due to the growth defense trade-off in plants, the transcription of immunity-related genes is generally inhibited during vegetative growth. In yeast, the general corepressor Tup1 hinders RNA polymerase II (Pol II) recruitment by outcompeting MED6 for binding to Srb7p (MED21); this interaction necessitates an intact Srb7p N-terminus24. Notably, the residues of MED21 that interact with Tup1 are identical to those required for the transcriptional repression by the Arabidopsis corepressor TOPLESS (TPL)24,25. Previous reports have shown that the TPD domain of TPL (TPLN188) is the primary determinant for the MED21 interaction22. We hypothesized that repressors TPL suppresses MED21-MED6 complex assembly by competing with MED6 for the MED21 N-terminal domain, and that PsAvh109 functionally mimics this mechanism to disrupt immune signaling.

Given that both Avh109 and TPLN188 compete with MED6 for binding to MED21, we compared the relative interaction affinities between GmMED21 and its respective partners (GmMED6, GmTPLN188, or PsAvh109). LCI assays revealed that the PsAvh109-GmMED21 interaction produced significantly higher luminescence intensity than the interactions of GmMED21 with either GmTPLN188 or GmMED6 (Fig. 5g, h). Furthermore, microscale thermophoresis (MST) measurements demonstrated that PsAvh109 binds GmMED21 with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 0.27 ± 0.04 μM, whereas GmTPLN188 and GmMED6 exhibited higher Kd values of 0.76 ± 0.11 μM and 1.29 ± 0.38 μM, respectively (Fig. 5i). Therefore, PsAvh109 acts as a functional mimic of TPL, displacing TPL from MED21 to disrupt MED21-MED6 complex assembly and suppress immune signaling.

Both N- and C-termini of PsAvh109 are required for its interaction with GmMED21

Phylogenetic analysis revealed the conservation of PsAvh109 across Phytophthora species, including P. parasitica, P. infestans, and P. capsici. All PsAvh109 homologs featured NLS, RxLR-dEER, and TP motifs. Furthermore, the N-terminal 50 residues of PsAvh109 exhibit high sequence conservation among the homologs, whereas the C-terminal 15 residues consist of three invariant and three additional highly conserved residues (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

To further delineate the domains of PsAvh109 required for interaction with GmMED21, we generated a series of truncated mutants by deleting specific motifs or regions: the RxLR-dEER motif (designed as PsAvh109-RxLR), the TP motif (designed as PsAvh109-TP), the N-terminus of PsAvh109 (designed as PsAvh10951-167), the C-terminus of PsAvh109 (designed as PsAvh10921-152), or both termini (designed as PsAvh10951-152) (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Y2H assays revealed that three mutants, PsAvh10951-167, PsAvh10921-152 and PsAvh10951-152 lost their ability to interact with GmMED21, whereas the other truncated variants retained their binding capacity (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Consistently, LCI assays in N. benthamiana showed that the dual-terminal deletion mutant (PsAvh10951-152) produced no detectable luminescence. Conversely, only weak luminescence signals were observed for the single-terminal deletion mutants, PsAvh10951-167 and PsAvh10921-152 (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Additionally, all three mutants failed to inhibit INF1-induced PCD, similar to the PsAvh109AAA, whereas the wild-type PsAvh109 strongly inhibited PCD (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Collectively, these findings indicate that both the N- and C-termini of PsAvh109 are necessary for full interactions with GmMED21.

To identify the residues critical for the PsAvh109-GmMED21 interface, we conducted molecular modeling and protein-protein docking analysis using AlphaFold3. The predicted 3D structure of GmMED21 exhibited high structural homology with Arabidopsis thaliana MED21 (UniProt ID: C0LU16). Structural analysis revealed that specific residues within the N-terminal (V23, R40, Y41, V54, N58, Q66, Y69, F71) and C-terminal (W154, F157, W160, Y161, D167) of PsAvh109 contribute to MED21 binding. Together, they form a “headphone-like” structure that encircles the N-terminal region of MED21 (Supplementary Fig. 9e). This structural configuration likely accounts for the markedly higher binding affinity of PsAvh109 to MED21 compared to the native transcriptional repressor TPL. Notably, key interacting residues in the N-terminus (V23, R40) and C-terminus (F157, W160, D167) of PsAvh109 are highly conserved among its homologs (Supplementary Fig. 2b), supporting the evolutionary conservation of its function in targeting MED21.

No significant difference in TPL accumulation was observed between SA-treated and EtOH control groups (Fig. 6a), thereby ruling out proteasomal degradation. Co-IP assays further demonstrated that exogenous SA significantly attenuated the association between GmMED21-3HA and GFP-GmTPLN188 compared to the EtOH control (Fig. 6b). In contrast, SA treatment had no discernible effect on the interaction between GmMED21-3HA and GFP-PsAvh109 relative to the EtOH control group (Fig. 6c). Additionally, LCI assays revealed that SA treatment reduced luminescence intensity driven by the GmMED21-nLUC and cLUC-GmTPLN188 interaction to approximately 70% of the level observed in the EtOH control (Supplementary Fig. 10). Conversely, SA treatment did not affect the luminescence resulting from the interaction between GmMED21-nLUC and cLUC-PsAvh109 (Supplementary Fig. 10). Discussion

Under biotic stress, plants shift resources from growth to defense, a transition marked by extensive transcriptional reprogramming. In eukaryotes, this reprogramming is coordinated by the Mediator complex, which integrates diverse cellular signals and modulates transcription by regulating the pre-initiation complex (PIC). The functional core of the Mediator comprises its head and middle modules, within which the MED21-MED6 interaction serves as a critical interface for transcriptional activators38–41. Our findings further validate that the disruption of the MED21-MED6 interaction impairs downstream transcriptional activity (Supplementary Fig. 8). This study elucidates an unrecognized mechanism by which P. sojae suppresses plant immunity. We demonstrate that the effector PsAvh109 disrupts the MED21-MED6 interaction, a pivotal interface within the Mediator complex, by binding to the MED21 subunit (Fig. 4e–i). We provide robust evidence that PsAvh109 is translocated into the host nucleus during P. sojae infection (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 3). Furthermore, comparative transcriptomic profiling of plants infected with the wild-type strain versus isogenic PsAvh109 knockout mutants confirms that PsAvh109 specifically suppresses SA-responsive gene expression (Fig. 3j, k).

The N-terminal region of MED21 is essential for its interaction with MED624,42, and in plants, also serves as a docking site for the transcriptional repressors TOPLESS (TPL) and TOPLESS-related (TPR) proteins25. These corepressors orchestrate multiple phytohormone signaling pathways, including those governed by JA, IAA, and ABA26,28,29. However, the specific mechanism by which TPL modulates the SA pathway remains elusive, with only fragmentary reports describing interactions between TPL and components, such as NIMIN and NPR330,31,43. Our findings reveal that TPL binding to the N-terminal region of MED21 impedes the assembly of the MED21-MED6 complex (Fig. 5a–e), a repressive effect that is notably reversed upon SA accumulation (Fig. 6b–d). ChIP-qPCR further confirmed that TPL enrichment at the conserved as-1 element within the promoters of PR1 genes, such as PR1-1 (Fig. 5f). These results indicate that TPL suppresses SA-mediated transcription by disrupting the assembly of the transcriptional machinery.

Recent research on the roles of plant Mediator complex subunits has revealed their pivotal roles in modulating immunity15,44. Subunits, such as MED8, MED14, MED15, MED16, MED18, MED19, MED21, MED25, and CDK8 positively regulate resistance to biotrophic and necrotrophic pathogens by modulating SA, JA, and ethylene (ET) signaling45–47. Conversely, pathogens have evolved diverse strategies to target this core complex. For example, HaRxL44 from Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis(Hpa) disrupts MED19a to attenuate SA-dependent gene expression and increase susceptibility32. MED19a is enriched at the promoter of the oomycete resistance gene PR1, and its upregulation enhances resistance to Hpa48.This study reveals a previously uncharacterized mechanism by which pathogens target the Mediator complex: the P. sojae effector PsAvh109 functions as a functional mimic of TPL to disrupt MED21-MED6 assembly, thereby suppressing the transcription of defense-related genes (Fig. 4e–i). Its affinity for GmMED21 surpasses that of its endogenous partner GmMED6 and the host repressor GmTPL (Fig. 5g–i), accounting for its potent dominant-negative interference. This mechanism is distinct from that of the previously characterized Mediator-targeting effector HaRxL44, highlighting the evolutionary diversity in pathogen tactics to subvert the same host machinery. By the way, this inhibitory mechanism is conserved across Phytophthora species. Taken together, these findings indicate that targeting Mediator complexes has become a widespread pathogenic strategy.

The N-terminal region of MED21 functions as a signaling integration hub, critically bridging upstream signals with the core transcriptional machinery42. Our findings demonstrate that PsAvh109 disrupts transcriptional initiation by interfering with the assembly of the MED21-MED6 complex, potentially affecting multiple layers of growth-defense crosstalk. Notably, PsAvh109 preferentially suppresses the transcription of SA-responsive genes (Fig. 4j). This specificity may reflect the role of SA in restricting biotrophic pathogens during the early stages of infection, a window marked by transcriptional activation of SA-regulated genes49. Crucially, our data establish a spatiotemporal coordination between PsAvh109 expression and host SA accumulation (Fig. 1c, d), consistent with its targeted disruption of SA-dependent responses.

We further propose that PsAvh109 expression is induced by host SA accumulation. Previous studies have identified numerous receptors and receptor-like molecules in Phytophthora species, such as the IRK1/IRK2 co-receptor system involved in sterol sensing and genistein-induced chemotaxis50,51. These findings suggest that P. sojae perceives host immune signals to coordinate effector deployment during infection. Indeed, exogenous SA treatment enhanced PsAvh109 expression (Fig. 1a, b), whereas NahG-overexpression in soybean significantly attenuated its induction during P. sojae infection (Fig. 1c, d). Similarly, PsAvh109 homologs in P. capsici (DVH05_001429) and P. parasitica (PPTG_19802) also exhibit SA-induced upregulation (Fig. 1e, f). While our data strongly link SA to PsAvh109 induction, the underlying perception mechanism remains elusive. Identifying this putative SA-sensing apparatus is essential for a comprehensive understanding of inter-kingdom signaling crosstalk.

In summary, this study elucidates a mechanism wherein the transcriptional corepressor TPL regulates SA-responsive gene expression by modulating the MED21-MED6 interaction. Notably, the P. sojae effector PsAvh109 facilitates infection by acting as a functional mimic of TPL, thereby highlighting an alternative strategy by which pathogens subvert host defense-related transcription. Furthermore, PsAvh109 expression is induced by host SA accumulation, the identical signal that triggers the dissociation of TPL from MED21 to initiate immune activation (Fig. 6e). This temporal synchronization enables PsAvh109 to perturb host transcription precisely within the critical window of immune activation. These findings demonstrate that pathogen effector expression is responsive to host immune signals, indicating that certain effectors function not as static virulence factors, but as dynamic components of counter-defense “sense and respond” modules.

Methods

Plant and microbe cultivation

N. benthamiana plants were grown in a greenhouse maintained at 25 °C under a 14-h light/10-h dark photoperiod. Etiolated soybean seedlings were incubated at 25 °C for 5 days in darkness. P. sojae (P6497) and P. capsici (LT1534) strains were cultured in a 10% vegetable (V8) juice agar medium at 25 °C for 5-7 days.

Bioinformatics analysis

The nucleotide and protein sequences used in this study were obtained from NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). For reference, the amino acid sequences of all PsAvh109 homologous proteins used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 1. Signal peptide (SP) predictions were submitted through the web interface by inputting the signal peptide predictions, which were conducted via the NovoPro SignalP tool (http://novopro.cn/tools/signalp). Multiple sequence alignments of amino acid sequences were conducted using MEGA12 software. All the bar charts presented in this study were generated via GraphPad Prism 9. The quantification of Western blot (WB) bands was carried out using ImageJ software.

N. benthamiana transient expression and virus-induced gene silencing

The plasmid constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 via the heat-shock method34. The A. tumefaciens transformant cell suspensions (OD600 = 0.6) were infiltrated into the leaves of N. benthamiana.

The Tobacco Rattle Virus (TRV)-based virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) system was employed to silence genes in N. benthamiana52. A gene-specific fragment of approximately 300 bp was cloned into the pTRV2 vector and then introduced into Agrobacterium strain GV3101. Subsequently, the Agrobacterium cultures carrying pTRV1 and pTRV2 (or pTRV2-target) were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and used to infiltrate N. benthamiana plants at the four-leaf stage. Fully expanded leaves of the silenced plants were then utilized for inoculation and subsequent quantitative RT-qPCR analysis.

Induction of plant cell death and Phytophthora infection in N. benthamiana

The target genes were cloned into pBIN or p1300 vectors and subsequently transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. The A. tumefaciens transformants cell suspensions (OD600 = 0.6) were infiltrated into 6-week-old N. benthamiana leaves. To assay for suppression of cell death, A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 carrying INF1 were co-infiltrated into the leaves of N. benthamiana. The ensuing cell death response of the leaves was then observed.

For infection assays, the leaves were inoculated with mycelia of P. capsici at 12 h post-inoculation (hpi). P. capsici leaf lesions were photographed under UV light at 36 or 48 hpi. Leaf disks were harvested for RNA analysis at 6 hpi. The accumulation of the protein encoded by the infiltrated constructs was confirmed by immunoblotting, and host transcript levels were assessed by RT-qPCR.

P. sojae culture and transformation

The wild type (WT) P. sojae strain P6497 was cultured in liquid 10% V8 medium for 3-5 days. The mycelium was then subjected to three washes with sterilized water to thoroughly remove any residual medium. To induce sporulation, the washed mycelia were immersed in sterile water and incubated at 25 °C for 9-12 h to release zoospores. To produce infected tissue, seeds of the susceptible soybean variety Williams were germinated for 6 days at 25 °C in total darkness. The resulting etiolated seedlings were inoculated with One hundred zoospores of either the WT or PsAvh109 mutant strains on the hypocotyls. Inoculated seedlings were subsequently incubated at 25 °C in darkness for 2-3 days. A minimum of six etiolated hypocotyls were tested for each biological replicate. Lesion development was photographed at 2-3 days post-inoculation (dpi). Infected tissues were harvested and stored at -80 °C for P. sojae biomass detection.

The CRISPR/Cas9 method was employed to generate mutants of PsAvh109 in P. sojae, along with PsAvh109-6His complementation strains, following established protocol53,54.

RNA extraction and transcript level analysis

To quantify gene transcript levels, total RNA from the P. sojae and plant samples was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions in the Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, RC411). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using HiScript IV reverse transcriptase, following the manufacturer’s instructions provided with the HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, R433).

RT-qPCR was employed to measure gene transcript levels, following the manufacturer’s instructions for the SupRealQ Ultra Hunter SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q713). Specific primers, designed based on gene sequences, were utilized, and the actin gene served as an endogenous control. PsActin, NbEF1a, and GmCYP2 were employed as internal controls. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Data 2.

RNA-seq analysis

For RNA sequencing, total RNA was extracted from soybean. The NEB Next Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, E7760) was employed to generate cDNA libraries. Three independent biological replicates were conducted for both GFP and GFP-PsAvh109 overexpressing soybean hairy roots. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the criteria of a fold-change ≥2 or ≤-2 across samples, together with a p-value < 0.05. Functional annotation and enrichment analysis of significant DEGs were performed using soybean Gene Ontology (GO) terms. The criteria for meaningful enrichment were set at q (false discovery rate) <0.05 and p-value < 0.01. Fold enrichment was used to calculate the -Log10(p-value).

Confocal microscopy

N. benthamiana leaf disks were mounted in sterile in water and analyzed using an LSM 880 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). Excitation wavelengths of 488 nm and 580 nm were utilized to visualize GFP and mCherry fluorescence, respectively, with emission wavelengths ranging from 490 nm to 530 nm for GFP and from 590 to 620 nm for mCherry.

Soybean hairy roots transformation and infection assay

Soybean (Glycine max cv. Williams) seeds were germinated under a 16-h light and 8-h dark photoperiod for 5-6 days. Explants for soybean hairy root transformation were selected from cotyledons that had not fully extended. Surface-sterilized cotyledons were rinsed five times with sterile deionized water. Under sterile conditions, a 3-5 mm small wound was created on the abaxial epidermis of the cotyledon near the hypocotyl with a sterile surgical knife. The wound was then inoculated with Agrobacterium rhizogenes strain K599 carrying the relevant overexpression plasmid. Subsequently, the cotyledons were cultured on MS medium at 25 °C for 3 to 4 weeks, callus formation was induced, followed by the emergence of soybean hairy roots at the wound site.

In vitro GST pull-down

For in vitro pull-down assays, GmMED21 was cloned into pET28a with a C-terminal His-tag, while PsAvh109, PsAvh109AAA, PsAvh109NES, and GmMED6, lacking their respective signal peptides, were cloned into pGEX4T-1 fused with a N-terminal GST-tag. GmMED6 also was cloned into pMAL-c2X with an N-terminal MBP tag. GmMED211-15 was cloned into p1300 with a C-terminal mCherry-3xHA tag. The plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 and incubated at 25 °C. Each strain was induced to express the relevant protein using 0.5 mM IPTG for 10 h at 25 °C. For GST pull-down, bacterial lysates containing GST-tagged proteins were purified with BeyoMag™ GSH Magnetic Agarose Beads (Beyotime, P2258). Then the resin was incubated with bacterial lysates containing His-tagged proteins for 3 h at 4 °C, and then the resin was washed with PBS five times. Alternatively, for His pull-down, bacterial lysates containing His-tagged proteins were purified with BeyoMag™ Anti-His Magnetic Beads (Beyotime, P2235), and then incubated with bacterial lysates containing GST-tagged proteins for 3 h at 4 °C, and then the resin was washed with PBS five times. The resulting pull-down fractions were analyzed by Western blotting using anti-GST (1:10,000, Transgen, HT601) and anti-His (1:10000, Transgen, HT501) antibodies, and, in some experiments, anti-MBP (1:10,000, Transgen, HT701) and anti-HA (1:10,000, Transgen, HT301) antibodies followed by incubation with a 1:10,000 diluted goat anti-mouse (1:10,000, Transgen, HS201) antibody.

Co-Immunoprecipitation and western blotting

For in vivo Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, mature PsAvh109, PsAvh109AAA and PsAvh109NES sequences were cloned into the pBIN vector fused with GFP at the N-terminus. GmMED21 was cloned into the p1300 plasmid fused with 3×HA-mCherry at the C-terminus. Transient co-expression of proteins in N. benthamiana leaves was achieved by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. The total proteins of the N. benthamiana leaves were incubated with anti-HA magnetic beads (AlpalifeBio, KTSM1335) at 4 °C for 12 h. After washing five times with 1×PBS buffer, the magnetic beads with bound proteins were boiled with 1×loading buffer for 10 min. The co-precipitated proteins were detected using anti-GFP (1:10,000, Transgen, HT801), anti-Flag (1:10,000, Transgen, HT201) and anti-HA (1:10,000, Transgen, HT301) antibodies. and, in certain experiments, anti-Histone H3 (1:10,000, Bioss, bsm-33042M) and anti-β-actin (1:10,000, ABclonal, AC004) antibodies followed by incubation with a 1:10,000 diluted goat anti-mouse (1:10,000, Transgen, HS201) antibody.

Luciferase complementation imaging assays

PsAvh109, PsAvh109AAA and PsAvh109NES without signal peptide were cloned into the p1300 plasmid fused with the C-terminus of luciferase (cLUC). GmMED21 was cloned into the p1300 plasmid fused with the N-terminus of luciferase (nLUC). The proteins were transiently co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation for 36 h, then the leaves were sprayed with 1 mM D-luciferin. Luciferase-mediated bioluminescence was observed using a plant live imaging system.

Luciferase activity was quantified by measuring relative bioluminescence intensity. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software, and statistical analysis was based on one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test.

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening and assay

The Y2H screen with pGBKT7-PsAvh109 was performed as follows. The PsAvh109 gene without the signal peptide was cloned into the yeast vector pGBKT7 (Clontech). The soybean cDNA library was constructed in pGADT7 using total RNA extracted from soybean hypocotyl tissues collected 12 and 24 h after inoculation with P. sojae zoospores (Clontech). More than 6 × 106 primary yeast clones (providing three-fold coverage) were screened using pGBKT7-PsAvh109 as the bait. Positive transformants harboring potential PsAvh109-interacting candidates were selected on synthetic defined (SD) medium lacking tryptophan, leucine, histidine, and adenine (SD/-Trp -Leu -His -Ade; SD-TLHA).

Yeast Three-hybrid (Y3H) assay

The pBridge and pGADT7 constructs were co-transformed into Y2H Gold cells and cultured on synthetic defined (SD) medium lacking tryptophan and leucine (SD/-Trp -Leu). After 3 days of incubation at 28 °C, positive colonies were screened and resuspended to OD600 = 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001, respectively, in 0.9% NaCl. The diluted yeast strains were grown on SD/ -Trp -Leu, SD/ -Trp -Leu -His, and SD /-Trp -Leu -His -Met plates at 28 °C for 5 days.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST)

Protein binding affinities between GmMED21 and PsAvh109, GmTPL, or GmMED6 were measured via MST using a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper, Germany). Briefly, recombinant and purified His-GmMED21 was diluted in MST buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 0.05% [v/v] Tween 20). The protein was fluorescently labeled using the Monolith His-tag labeling kit-RED-tris-NTA second-generation dye (NanoTemper, Cat. No. MOL018) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixture was incubated at room temperature (25 °C) for 30 min. A constant concentration of RED-tris-NTA-labeled His-GmMED21 was incubated with a 1:1 serial dilution of GST-tagged ligands (PsAvh109, GmTPL, or GmMED6) for 5 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the samples were loaded into Monolith NT.115 capillaries. Thermophoresis was measured at 25 °C with 60% MST power and 20% excitation power. The curves were fitted and the dissociation constant (Kd) values were calculated using the MO.Affinity Analysis software (v2.3, NanoTemper).

ChIP-qPCR assay

Soybean hairy roots expressing 3Flag fused proteins were utilized for ChIP assays55. ChIP-qPCR was performed on three biological replicates, and the results were normalized to the input DNA. Two independent ChIP-qPCR experiments were conducted, and the results were similar. The primers used for ChIP-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

Statistics and reproducibility

Samples were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Sample sizes were chosen to ensure the generation of robust and reliable results. Throughout the experiments and assays, investigators were blinded to group allocations. All experiments included at least three biological replicates, with the exact number detailed in the respective figure legends. Data were analyzed using bar charts created with Graphpad Prism version 9.0 software.
