Abstract:

In this technical report, rigorous statements and formal proofs are presented for both foundational and advanced folklore theorems on the Radon-Nikodym derivative. The cases of conditional and marginal probability measures are carefully considered, which leads to an identity involving the sum of mutual and lautum information suggesting a new interpretation for such a sum.

Key-words:
Radon Nikodym Derivative.

In mathematics, folklore theorems refer to results that are widely accepted and frequently utilized by experts but are often not formally proven or explicitly documented. In game theory for example, the original Folk theorem earned its name because, although it was widely recognized among game theorists during the 1950s, it remains unpublished and without attribution to particular authors. Folklore theorems populate all areas in mathematics.

At the light of this observation, this technical report presents rigorous statements and alternative proofs for the most common folklore theorems on the RND. These include the change of measure theorem, the proportional-measures theorem, the chain rule, the multiplicative inverse, linearity and continuity theorems, as well as the product-measure theorem and nonnegativity and finiteness theorem. Less common theorems such as the unit measure and two Bayes-like rules of the RND are also formally proved.

Hopefully, these contributions would provide a valuable reference for researchers and students in information theory.

2
Preliminaries

This section introduces relevant notational conventions alongside the Radon-Nikodym theorem.
In particular, some equalities presented in this paper are valid almost surely with respect to a given measure. For clarity, given a measure space
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F},P\right)
, the notation
\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{P}
is introduced and shall be read as “equal for all
x\in\Omega
except on a negligible set with respect to
P
”; or equivalently as “equal almost surely with respect to
P
”. Moreover, given two measures
P
and
Q
on the same measurable space, the notation
{P}\ll{Q}
stands for “the measure
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
”.
Using this notation, the Radon-Nikodym derivative is introduced by the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Radon-Nikodym theorem,
[
5
, Theorem 2.2.1]
)
.

Let
P
be a signed measure and
Q
be a
\sigma
-finite measure both on a given measurable space
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
, with

{P}\ll{Q}
. Then, there exists a Borel measurable
function
g:\penalty\ \Omega\penalty\ \to\penalty\ \mathbb{R}
such that for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
,

P(\mathcal{A})=\int_{\mathcal{A}}g(x)\mathrm{d}Q(x).

(1)

Moreover, if another function
h
satisfies for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
that
P(\mathcal{A})=\int_{\mathcal{A}}h(x)\mathrm{d}Q(x)
, then
g(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{Q}
h(x)
.

The function
g
in (
1
) is often referred to as the Radom-Nikodym derivative of
P
with respect to
Q
; and is also written as
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}
, such that
g(x)\penalty\ =\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x).

The Radon-Nikodym derivative exhibits a number of properties from which many foundational results originate. Some of these properties are certainly folklore theorems and thus, the purpose of the following section is to provide formal proofs. Other properties are formally proved in literature and thus, for the sake of completeness, an alternative proof is presented using the described notation.

3
Basic Folklore Theorems

This section focuses on basic folklore theorems, where “basic” denotes their well-established nature. One of the most common folklore theorems is often referred to as the “change of measure” theorem.

Theorem 2

(Change of Measure)
.

Let
P
be a signed measure and
Q
be a
\sigma
-finite measure both on a given measurable space
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
, with
{P}\ll{Q}
. Let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a Borel
measurable function such that the integral
\int_{\Omega}f(x)\mathrm{d}P(x)
exists. Then, for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}f(x)\mathrm{d}P(x)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}f(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\mathrm{d}Q(x).

(2)

A proof of this theorem is presented in
[
12
, Proposition 3.9]
. For the sake of completeness, an alternative proof is presented hereunder, using the previously described notation.

{IEEEproof}

Assume that
f
is a simple function, i.e. for all
x\in\Omega
,

f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}(x),
for some finite
m\in\mathbb{N}
, disjoint sets
\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{m}
in
\mathscr{F}
, and reals
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
. For all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, let
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{A}_{i}
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}f(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\mathrm{d}Q(x)=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}(x)\mathrm{d}Q(x)

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\mathrm{d}Q(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}P(\mathcal{B}_{i}),

(4)

where the equality in (
4
) follows Theorem
1
and the linearity of the integral
[
5
, Theorem 1.6.3]
. On the other hand, for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}P(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\mathrm{d}P(x)\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}P(\mathcal{B}_{i}),

(5)

where the equality in (
5
) follows from the linearity of the integral
[
5
, Theorem 1.6.3]
. Hence, from Theorem
1
when
f
is simple, the desired equality in (
2
) holds immediately.

The proof proceeds by observing that:
(a)
simple functions are dense in the space of Borel measurable functions
[
5
, Theorem 1.5.5(b)]
, and
(b)
the integral is a continuous map on that space
[
5
, Theorem 1.6.2]
. From
(a)
and
(b)
, it follows that (
2
) also holds for any Borel measurable function
f
. This completes the proof.

Another reputed folklore theorem, which is often referred to as the “proportional measures” theorem, establishes the explicit form of the Radon-Nikodym derivative between two measures, in which one is proportional to the other.

Theorem 3

(Proportional Measures)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be two
\sigma
-
finite measures on the measurable space
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
, such that for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}

Q(\mathcal{A})=cP(\mathcal{A}),

(6)

with
c>0
. Then,

\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{Q}\frac{1}{c},\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{P}c.

(7)

{IEEEproof}

Note that from (
6
),
P
and
Q
are mutually absolutely continuous. Moreover, from Theorem
1
, for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
,

P(\mathcal{A})=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}P(x)\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\,\mathrm{d}Q(x),

(8)

and from (
6
), it follows that

P(\mathcal{A})=\frac{1}{c}\,Q(\mathcal{A})\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{c}\,\mathrm{d}Q(x).

(9)

Hence,
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{Q}\frac{1}{c}
follows directly from Theorem
1
. A similar argument shows that
\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{P}c
.

In the special case
c=1
in (
7
),
P
and
Q
are identical, yielding
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{Q}

\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{P}

1.

The following folklore theorem is often referred to as the “chain rule” and a proof appears in
[

Theorem 4

(Chain Rule)
.

Let
P
be a signed measure and let
Q
and
R
be two
\sigma
-finite measures, all on the same measurable space
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
, with

{P}\ll{Q}
and

{Q}\ll{R}
.
Then,

\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}R}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{R}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}R}(x).

(10)

{IEEEproof}

From the assumptions of the theorem and Theorem
2
, it follows that for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
,

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}P(x)\,=\,\int_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\,\mathrm{d}Q(x)

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}R}(x)\,\mathrm{d}R(x)\,=\,\int_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}R}(x)\,\mathrm{d}R(x),

(12)

where the second equality in (
12
) holds given that
P\ll R
and Theorem
1
which yields (
10
).

The following folklore theorem is a consequence of Theorem
4
and shows the connection between the Radon-Nikodym derivative and its multiplicative inverse.

Theorem 5

(Multiplicative Inverse)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be two mutually absolutely continuous
\sigma
-finite measures on the measurable space
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
; and assume that for all
x\in\Omega
,
\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)>0
.
Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{Q}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\right)^{-1}.

(13)

This result also appears in
[
12
, Corollary 3.10]
.
{IEEEproof}

From Theorem
4
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{Q}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{Q}1,

(14)

where the last equality follows from Theorem
3
, with
c\penalty\ =\penalty\ 1
.

The subsequent folklore theorem establishes the linearity of the Radon-Nikodym derivative. A similar statement is presented in
[
12
, Proposition 3.11]
but left without proof.

Theorem 6

(Linearity)
.

Let
P
be a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
and let also
Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots,Q_{n}
be signed measures on
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
absolutely continuous with respect to
P
. Let
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}
be positive reals; and let
S
be a signed measure on
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
such that for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
,

S(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}c_{t}Q_{t}(\mathcal{A})
.
Then,

\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{P}\sum_{t=1}^{n}c_{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{t}}{\mathrm{d}P}(x).

(15)

{IEEEproof}

From the assumptions of the theorem, it holds that
{S}\ll{P}
, thus for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\mathrm{d}P(x)=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}S(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}c_{t}\int_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}Q_{t}(x)

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{A}}c_{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{t}}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\mathrm{d}P(x)=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}c_{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{t}}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\mathrm{d}P(x),

(17)

where the first equality in (
16
) and the first equality in (
17
) follow from Theorem
2
; and the last equality in
(
17
) follows from the additivity property of the integral
[
5
, Corollary 1.6.4]
.
The proof ends by using Theorem
1
yielding (
15
).

The following folklore theorem establishes the continuity of the Radon-Nikodym derivative.

Theorem 7

(Continuity)
.

Let
P
be a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
, and let
Q_{1},Q_{2},\cdots
be an infinite
sequence of finite measures on
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
, converging to a signed measure
Q
. Suppose that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
{Q_{n}}\ll{P}
.
Then,
{Q}\ll{P}
and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{n}}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{P}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}P}(x).

(18)

{IEEEproof}

From the assumptions of the theorem and Theorem
2
, for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
, it holds that

\displaystyle Q(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{n}(\mathcal{A})\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{A}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{n}}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\,\mathrm{d}P(x),

(19)

where the last equality in (
19
) follows from Theorem
2
and
[
5
, Theorem 1.6.2]
. Now, from the assumption that
Q\ll P
, it follows that for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}
,

Q(\mathcal{A})\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}P}(x)\,\mathrm{d}P(x).

(20)

Therefore, the equality in (
20
) and Theorem
1
yields (
18
).

The ensuing folklore theorem relates the Radon–Nikodym derivative of a product measure to those of its component measures. This result also appears in
[
12
, Exercise 3.12]
.

Theorem 8

(Product of Measures)
.

For all
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
P_{i}
and
Q_{i}
be a signed and a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\left(\Omega_{i},\mathscr{F}_{i}\right)
, respectively; with
{P_{i}}\ll{Q_{i}}
. Let also
P_{1}P_{2}
and

Q_{1}Q_{2}
be the product measures on
\left(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2},\mathscr{F}_{1}\times\mathscr{F}_{2}\right)
formed by
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
; and
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
,
respectively. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{1}P_{2}}{\mathrm{d}Q_{1}Q_{2}}\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathop{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}}_{Q_{1}Q_{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{1}}{\mathrm{d}Q_{1}}\left(x_{1}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{2}}{\mathrm{d}Q_{2}}\left(x_{2}\right).

(21)

{IEEEproof}

By definition of product measures, for all
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
,

\displaystyle P_{1}P_{2}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}P_{1}P_{2}\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)=\iint_{\mathcal{A}_{x_{2}}}\mathrm{d}P_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)\mathrm{d}P_{2}\left(x_{2}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{A}_{x_{2}}}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}P_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)}{\mathrm{d}Q_{1}}\mathrm{d}Q_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)\mathrm{d}P_{2}\left(x_{2}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{A}_{x_{2}}}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}P_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)}{\mathrm{d}Q_{1}}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}P_{2}\left(x_{2}\right)}{\mathrm{d}Q_{2}}\mathrm{d}Q_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)\mathrm{d}Q_{2}\left(x_{2}\right)

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{A}}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}P_{1}}{\mathrm{d}Q_{1}}\left(x_{1}\right)\dfrac{\mathrm{d}P_{2}}{\mathrm{d}Q_{2}}\left(x_{2}\right)\mathrm{d}Q_{1}Q_{2}\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right),

(25)

where
\mathcal{A}_{x_{2}}
is the section of the set
\mathcal{A}
determined by
x_{2}
, namely,
\mathcal{A}_{x_{2}}=\{x_{1}:(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathcal{A}\}
; the equality in (
22
) arises from the definition of
P_{1}P_{2}
as the product of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
; the equalities in (
23
) and (
24
) are direct consequences of Theorem
2
; and finally, the equality in (
25
) is due to the construction of
Q_{1}Q_{2}
as the product measure of
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
.
From the equality in (
25
), it holds that
{P_{1}P_{2}}\ll{Q_{1}Q_{2}}
. Thus, for all
\mathcal{A}\penalty\ \in\penalty\ \mathscr{F}_{1}\penalty\ \times\penalty\ \mathscr{F}_{2}
,

P_{1}P_{2}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}P_{1}P_{2}}{\mathrm{d}Q_{1}Q_{2}}\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)\mathrm{d}Q_{1}Q_{2}\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right).

(26)

From Theorem
1

The following theorem shows a nonnegativity and finiteness property of the RND.

Theorem 9

(Nonnegativity and Finiteness)
.

Consider a probability measure
P
and a
\sigma
-finite measure
Q
on
\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F}\right)
, with
P\ll Q
. Then

P\left(\left\{\omega\in\Omega:0\leqslant\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(\omega)<+\infty\right\}\right)=1.

(27)

{IEEEproof}

Assume that

P\left(\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(\omega)<0\right\}\right)>0.

(28)

Hence,

\displaystyle P\left(\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(\omega)<0\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(\omega)<0\right\}}\mathrm{d}P(x)

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(\omega)<0\right\}}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(x)\mathrm{d}Q(x)

(30)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 0,

(31)

which contradicts that
P
is a probability measure, which is nonnegative.
Alternative, assume that

P\left(\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(\omega)=+\infty\right\}\right)>0.

(32)

Hence,

\displaystyle P\left(\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(\omega)=+\infty\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(\omega)=+\infty\right\}}\mathrm{d}P(x)

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(\omega)=+\infty\right\}}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{P}}{\mathrm{d}{Q}}(x)\mathrm{d}Q(x)

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+\infty,

(35)

which contradicts that
P
is a probability measure, which is a finite measure.

Advanced Folklore Theorems

This section focuses exclusively on probability measures with the aim of establishing closer ties to information measures, which requires some additional notation.
In particular, denote by
\triangle\bigl(\mathcal{X},\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\bigr)
, or simply
\triangle\bigl(\mathcal{X}\bigr)
, the set of all probability measures on the measurable space
\bigl(\mathcal{X},\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\bigr)
, where
\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{X}}
is a
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{X}
.
Using this notation, conditional probability measures can be defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Conditional Probability)
.

A family
P_{Y|X}\,\triangleq\,(P_{Y|X=x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
of elements of
\triangle\bigl(\mathcal{Y},\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}\bigr)
indexed by
\mathcal{X}
is said to be a conditional probability measure if, for all sets
\mathcal{B}\in\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, the map

\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}r@{~}c@{~}l@{}}\mathcal{X}\penalty\ &\longrightarrow\hfil\penalty\ &[0,1]\\

is Borel measurable. The set of such conditional probability measures is denoted by
\triangle(\mathcal{Y}\!\mid\!\mathcal{X})
.

A conditional probability
P_{Y|X}\in\triangle(\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathcal{X})
and a probability measure
P_{X}\in\triangle(\mathcal{X})
jointly determine two unique probability measures in
\triangle(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
and
\triangle(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X})

P_{XY}
and
P_{YX}
, respectively, and for all sets
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle P_{XY}\bigl(\mathcal{A}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int P_{Y|X=x}\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{x}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x),

where
\mathcal{A}_{x}
is the section of the set
\mathcal{A}
determined by
x
, namely,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{x}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\bigl\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}\bigr\}.

Alternatively, for all sets
\mathcal{B}\in\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}\times\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{X}}

(39)

where
\mathcal{B}_{x}
is the section of the set
\mathcal{B}
determined by
x
.

is a measure in

whereas

is a measure in
\triangle\left(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\right)

and

In particular, given a set
\mathcal{A}\in\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}

(40)

Then, from (
37
) and (

P_{XY}\bigl(\mathcal{A}\bigr)=P_{YX}\bigl(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\bigr).

(41)

Definition 2

.
