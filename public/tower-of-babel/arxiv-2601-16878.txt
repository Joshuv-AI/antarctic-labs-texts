A Lyapunov-Tamed Euler Method for Singular SDEs
Tim Johnston, Pierre Monmarché

Abstract
Many applications, such as systems of interacting particles in physics, require the simulation of diffusion processes with singular coefficients. Standard Euler schemes are then not convergent, and theoretical guarantees in this situation are scarce. In this work we introduce a Lyapunov-tamed Euler scheme, for drift coefficients for which the weak derivative is dominated by a function that obeys a certain generic Lyapunov-type condition. This allows for a range of coefficients that explode to infinity on a bounded set. We establish that, in terms of L p L^{p} -strong error, the Lyapunov-tamed scheme is consistent and moreover achieves the same order of convergence as the standard Euler scheme for Lipschitz coefficients. The general result is applied to systems of mean-field particles with singular repulsive interaction in 1D, yielding an error bound with polynomial dependency in the number of particles.

Introduction

Theoretical bounds for numerical approximations of SDEs have long since relied on smoothness and regularity assumptions, which ensure that the behavior of the true solution does not change too radically between grid-points, and additionally that the moments of the approximation and the true solution are suitably bounded (one may consult
[
25
]
for a classic book length study). In particular, both of these assumptions are covered by ensuring that the coefficients of an SDE obey a Lipschitz assumption. In the absence of a Lipschitz assumption, even in the case where the drift coefficient
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
obeys a coercivity-type condition

\langle b(x),x\rangle\leq c(1+\lvert x\rvert^{2}),

(1)

which ensures the polynomial moments of the true solution are bounded, one can obtain bad performance of explicit schemes such as the Euler scheme. In particular, as shown in
[
19
,
18
]
, there is a small probability (for all step-sizes) that the scheme blows up astronomically, therefore leading to a divergence in
L^{p}
for the scheme as the stepsize tends to
0
. Since the moments of the scheme diverge as the stepsize tends to
0
, accurate approximation of the true solution in
L^{p}
is impossible. This insight led to the development of ‘tamed’ Euler schemes in
[
17
]
, in which the drift coefficient
b
is replaced in the definition of the scheme by a new coefficient
b_{n}
which depends on the stepsize. A large number of works have extended and developed this idea, for instance the alternative taming approaches to
[
17
]
considered in
[
40
,
31
]
, schemes that tame also the diffusion coefficient in
[
41
]
, higher order schemes in
[
39
]
, sampling algorithms in
[
3
,
30
]
, SPDEs in
[
14
,
1
]
, particle systems for McKean-Vlasov SDEs in
[
26
]
, SDEs driven by Lévy noise in
[
8
]
, and schemes that preserve exponential stability in
[
20
]
. Furthermore, a very general tamed method for approximating SDEs with non-Lipschitz coefficients was proposed in
[
21
]
, however the generality of this approach meant that no rate of convergence could be guaranteed (and indeed pathological cases as studied in
[
46
,
15
]
show that in such general cases a rate of convergence is unlikely).

In the majority of these works the superlinearly growing drift coefficient obeys a bound of the form

\lvert b(x)-b(y)\rvert\leq L(1+\lvert x\rvert^{l}+\lvert y\rvert^{l})\lvert x-y\rvert.

(2)

for some
l,L>0
. After making such an assumption, given sufficient moment bounds on both the scheme and the true solution it is possible to prove convergence of the Euler scheme using a variation of the arguments one uses in the case of Lipschitz coeffients.

However, less attention has been given to SDEs whose coefficients explode to infinity on a compact
subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is the focus of this article. This class of examples is especially important in modeling particles, in particular in statistical physics and in molecular simulations, where the repulsion forces can typically diverge as two particles get close to one another. One may consult
[
27
]
for example for a book-long study from a mathematical perspective. In particular, in the mean-field regime, at the continuous-time level, these models have been the topic of a number of works over recent years, with important progresses in establishing quantitative propagation of chaos as the number of particles goes to infinity in the presence of singular forces, including the 2D vortex model
[

,

or Coulomb and Riesz gaz
[

In

In considering such coefficients, which can be in a sense ‘discontinuous’ if considered as functions on the whole of

Assumptions

Remark 1
.

The second term on the LHS of (

The assumption that the Lyapunov function

Finally the reason we have assumed the constants

Definition 1
.

Let

where

The assumption on

Our second assumption is a smoothness assumption which pertains to the gradient of

Assumption 2
.

Let

For the constants

given in Assumption

.

For

2.2
Tamed Scheme

As with other tamed schemes in the literature, we shall introduce new coefficients that depend on the stepsize, so as to ‘curb’ the behavior of the scheme in the cases where the drift coefficient would otherwise take very large values. However, in our case we do not hide the fact that our scheme will be nonsensical in these rare cases, and merely rely on the fact that such pathological trajectories will be extremely rare for small stepsize (see (
27
) and Lemma
7
). For this reason, the scheme may not be as practical as others with the same theoretical properties, however we do not bother to address this since the focus of this article is on theoretical convergence.

Our new coefficient
b_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, corresponding to a stepsize
n^{-1}
, will then be given as

b_{n}(x):=b(x)\cdot 1_{x\in D}\cdot 1_{V(x)\leq n^{1/2}}\cdot 1_{\lvert b(x)\rvert\leq n^{1/2}},

(15)

so as to be truncated when
V(x)
or
b(x)
takes too large a value, or when
x\not\in D
. The associated tamed Euler scheme is therefore given as

x^{n}_{m-1}=x^{n}_{m-1}+n^{-1}b_{n}(x_{n})+n^{-1/2}\beta z_{m},\;\;\;x^{n}_{0}=x_{0}\in D,

(16)

where
(z_{m})_{m\geq 1}
is a sequence of
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
random variables. However, for convenience, and in order to study the strong rate of convergence, we shall consider the continuous time lift

dX^{n}_{t}:=b_{n}(X^{n}_{\kappa_{n}(t)})dt+\beta dW_{t},\;\;\;X^{n}_{0}=x_{0}\in D

(17)

where
\kappa_{n}(t):=\frac{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{n}
is the backwards projection onto the grid
\{0,1/n,2/n,...\}
, and
X^{n}_{t}
is driven by the same noise as the continuous time process
X_{t}
given in (
4
).

Properties of Tamed Coefficient

Before proving moment bounds of the scheme and the true solution, it shall be useful to derive some key properties from the assumptions given in the previous section. All the following bounds are given under Assumption

and therefore since

\displaystyle\lvert b(x)\rvert\leq H_{1}(1+V(x))(1+\lvert x\rvert).

Therefore, for every

Now we shall use the monotonicity assumption (

\displaystyle\langle x,b(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle x-x_{0},b(x)-b(x_{0})\rangle+\langle x_{0},b(x)-b(x_{0})\rangle+\langle x,b(x_{0})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\mu\lvert x-x_{0}\rvert^{2}+\lvert x_{0}\rvert(\lvert b(x)\rvert+\lvert b(x_{0})\rvert)+\lvert b(x_{0})\rvert\lvert x\rvert.

Using Young’s inequality, and recalling the generic constant

\displaystyle\langle x,b(x)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq c\lvert x\rvert^{2}+H_{1}\lvert b(x)\rvert+H_{2}

\displaystyle\leq c\lvert x\rvert^{2}+H_{1}(1+\lvert x\rvert)(1+V(x))+H_{2}.

Now let us find a bound for

2.5
Sketch of Proof

As was discussed in the literature some time before the development of tamed schemes, see
[
38
,
32
]
, the main problem with Euler approximations for non-globally Lipschitz coefficients is that the moments of the scheme can explode under certain conditions. Actually, in the case we consider, it shall be possible that the regular Euler scheme always has infinite moments, since the drift coefficient
b
does not have to be locally integrable. Therefore the main challenge shall be proving moment bounds for the tamed Euler scheme we construct. After this is achieved, convergence can be shown by a standard Grönwall-type argument familiar in the numerical analysis literature. To this end our strategy is as follows

•

we introduce the following stopping time

\tau_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0|\;\max\{V(X^{n}_{t}),\lvert b(X^{n}_{t})\rvert\}\geq n^{1/2}\},

(27)

which reflects the first time that
b_{n}(X^{n}_{t})\not=b(X^{n}_{t})
.

•

we prove moment bounds of all orders for the Euler scheme and
V(X^{n}_{t})
up until the stopping time
\tau_{n}

•

we then use this to show that the probability
\tau_{n}\in[0,T]
decays quickly in
n
. Specifically, for all
p>0
, by Markov’s inequality one has

\displaystyle P(\tau_{n}\in[0,T])=P(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\max\{V(X^{n}_{t}),\lvert b(X^{n}_{t})\rvert\}\geq n^{1/2})

\displaystyle=P(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\max\{V(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}})^{p},\lvert b(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}})\rvert^{p}\}\geq n^{p/2})

\displaystyle\leq n^{-p/2}E\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\max\{V(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}})^{p},\lvert b(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}})\rvert^{p}\}.

(28)

•

therefore, so long as the taming we implement means that no trajectory of
X^{n}_{t}
and
V(X^{n}_{t})
diverges too severely with
n\geq 1
, one may show uniform moment bounds of both over the entirety of
[0,T]
. This is the precisely the calculation in (
3.2.1
)

The tool we use to upgrade ‘uniform-in-expectation’ to ‘expectation-of-uniform’ moment bounds

Lemma 1

Let Assumption

Firstly, let us define

Note, since the drift is bounded, that this SDE has a unique strong solution following Theorem 1.1 in

\tilde{\tau}_{R}:=\inf\{t\geq 0|\;V(X^{R}_{t})\geq R\}.

Applying the Itô’s formula in Proposition

\displaystyle V(X^{R}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}})^{p}

\displaystyle=V(x_{0})+\int^{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}}_{0}\mathcal{L}(V^{p})(X^{R}_{s})ds

\displaystyle+\int^{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}}_{0}p\beta V^{p-1}(X^{R}_{s})\langle\nabla V(X^{R}_{s}),dW_{s}\rangle.

(35)

Note that
V(X^{R}_{s\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}})

\displaystyle EV(X^{R}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}})^{p}

\displaystyle\leq V(x_{0})^{p}+E\int^{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}}_{0}(a_{p}+b_{p}V(X^{R}_{s})^{p})ds.

(36)

Then since

\displaystyle EV(X^{R}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}})^{p}\leq V(x_{0})^{p}+a_{p}t+b_{p}\int^{t}_{0}EV(X^{R}_{s\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}})^{p}ds.

(37)

Applying the almost sure boundedness of the integrand again, and additionally Grönwall’s inequality, one obtains

\displaystyle EV(X^{R}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}})^{p}

\displaystyle\leq(V(x_{0})^{p}+a_{p}t)e^{b_{p}t}.

(38)

Observe for
R\geq V(x_{0})
one has

R^{p}P(\tilde{\tau}_{R}\in[0,T])\leq EV(X^{R}_{T\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}})^{p},

(39)

so that
P(\tilde{\tau}_{R}\in[0,T])\to 0
as
R\to\infty
. Furthermore, since
\tilde{\tau}_{R}
is increasing in
R>0
, one has that
\lim_{R\to\infty}\tilde{\tau}_{R}>T
almost surely. Therefore, since
X^{R}_{t}
is a unique strong solution of (
4
) up to
\tilde{\tau}_{R}
, the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
given as
X_{t}:=\lim_{R\to\infty}X^{R}_{t\wedge\tilde{\tau}_{R}}
is well defined for all
t\geq 0
, and yields a unique strong solution of (
4
) on
[0,T]
. We may therefore extend this to a solution on
[0,\infty)
by stitching together solutions on compact intervals. Taking the limit as
R\to\infty
in (
38
) and applying Fatou’s lemma, the stated result follows form Definition
1
.
∎

3.1.2
Regular Moments

Lemma 3
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then for all
T>0
and
p\geq 2

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}E\lvert X_{t}\rvert^{p}\leq H_{2p}.

(41)

Proof.

As before, we introduce a stopping time to assume apriori that all stochastic integrals vanish under expectation. Specifically, let

\bar{\tau}_{R}:=\inf\{t\geq 0|\;\lvert X_{t}\rvert\geq R\}.

(42)

We fix
p\geq 2
as assumed in the Lemma statement. Then one calculates

tr(\nabla^{2}\lvert\cdot\rvert^{p})(x)=p(p+d-2)\lvert x\rvert^{p-2}\leq cd\lvert x\rvert^{p-2},

(43)

where
c>0
is the generic constant introduced in Definition
1
, which in particular depends on
p>0
. Therefore, for
t\in[0,T]
one may apply Itô’s formula to yield

\displaystyle E\lvert X_{t\wedge\bar{\tau}_{R}}\rvert^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\lvert x_{0}\rvert^{p}+cE\int^{t\wedge\bar{\tau}_{R}}_{0}(\lvert X_{s}\rvert^{p-2}\langle X_{s},b(X_{s})\rangle+\beta^{2}d\lvert X_{s}\rvert^{p-2})ds,

(44)

Now we use (
2.4
) to obtain that

\lvert x\rvert^{p-2}\langle b(x),x\rangle\leq c(1+\lvert x\rvert^{p})+H_{p}(1+V(x)^{p})+H_{p},

(45)

so that by Young’s inequality, and since
\beta^{p}d^{p/2}\leq H_{p}
and
\lvert x_{0}\rvert^{p}\leq H_{p}
by Definition
1
, one has

\displaystyle E\lvert X_{t\wedge\bar{\tau}_{R}}\rvert^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\lvert x_{0}\rvert^{p}+cE\int^{t\wedge\bar{\tau}_{R}}_{0}(\lvert X_{s}\rvert^{p}+H_{p}(1+V(X_{s})^{p})+H_{p})ds

\displaystyle\leq c\int^{t}_{0}(E\lvert X_{s\wedge\bar{\tau}_{R}}\rvert^{p}+H_{p}(1+EV(X_{s\wedge\bar{\tau}_{R}})^{p})+H_{p})ds

\displaystyle\leq c\int^{t}_{0}(E\lvert X_{s\wedge\bar{\tau}_{R}}\rvert^{p}+H_{2p})ds,

(46)

where we have used the properties of the generic constant given in (
11
). Therefore, by Grönwall’s lemma and Fatou’s lemma the result follows.
∎

We begin by proving ‘bad’ moment bounds that scale with the step-size parameter

Lemma 4
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then for all

and
p\geq 2
there exists

such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}E\lvert X^{n}_{t}\rvert^{p}\leq H_{p}n^{p/2}.

Proof.

Similarly to in Lemma
3
, let us consider the following stopping time

\tilde{\tau}_{n}^{R}:=\inf\{t\geq 0|\;\lvert X^{n}_{t}\rvert\geq R\}.

(48)

Let
p\geq 2
as in the Lemma statement. By Itô’s formula, the definition of

Lemma 5
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then for every
T>0
and

one has

\sup_{\tau}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}EV(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})^{p}\leq H_{p+q_{p}^{\prime}},

(50)

where the first supremum is over all stopping times
\tau
and
\tau_{n}
is given in (

Proof.

Let
\tau
be an arbitrary stopping time. We begin by proving an increment bound up to
\tau\wedge\tau_{n}
. Particularly, since one has

b_{n}(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}})=b(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}),\;\;\;\lvert b_{n}(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}})\rvert\leq n^{1/2},\;\;\;V(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}})\leq n^{1/2},

by the definition of

and
b_{n}

\displaystyle\lvert b_{n}(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})-b_{n}(X^{n}_{\kappa_{n}(t)\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})\rvert=\lvert b(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})-b(X^{n}_{\kappa_{n}(t)\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})\rvert

\displaystyle\leq(V(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})+V(X^{n}_{\kappa_{n}(t)\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}}))[n^{-1}b(X^{n}_{\kappa_{n}(t)\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})+\beta\lvert W_{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}}-W_{\kappa_{n}(t)\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}}\rvert]

Therefore, applying standard facts about Brownian motion, and since

\displaystyle E\lvert b_{n}(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})-b_{n}(X^{n}_{\kappa_{n}(t)\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})\rvert^{p}\leq H_{p}.

(53)

Now let

. Note that by the definition of
\tau_{n}
and (
8
), the stochastic integral is a true martingale and so vanishes under expectation by the optional stopping theorem. Therefore, applying Itô’s formula and correcting the discretisation via Young’s inequality, one has for

that

\displaystyle EV(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})^{p}=V(x_{0})^{p}+E\int^{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}}_{0}(\mathcal{L}(V^{p}))(X^{n}_{s})ds

\displaystyle+E\int^{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}}_{0}\langle\nabla(V^{p})(X^{n}_{s}),b_{n}(X^{n}_{\kappa_{n}(s)})-b_{n}(X^{n}_{s})\rangle ds

\displaystyle\leq V(x_{0})^{p}+E\int^{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}}_{0}((\mathcal{L}(V^{p}))(X^{n}_{s})+\frac{1}{q_{p}}\lvert\nabla(V^{p})(X^{n}_{s})\rvert^{q_{p}})ds

\displaystyle+E\int^{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}}_{0}\frac{1}{q_{p}^{\prime}}\lvert b_{n}(X^{n}_{\kappa_{n}(s)})-b_{n}(X^{n}_{s})\rvert^{q_{p}^{\prime}}ds.

(54)

Note that
1/q_{p}^{\prime}
is necessarily bounded by a universal constant, so that one obtains via (
6
)

\displaystyle EV(X^{n}_{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})^{p}

\displaystyle\leq V(x_{0})^{p}+E\int^{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}}_{0}a_{p}+b_{p}V(X^{n}_{s})^{p}ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int^{t}_{0}cE\lvert b_{n}(X^{n}_{\kappa_{n}(s)\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})-b_{n}(X^{n}_{s\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}})\rvert^{q_{p}^{\prime}}ds

\displaystyle\leq V(x_{0})^{p}+H_{q_{p}^{\prime}}+E\int^{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{n}}_{0}(a_{p}+b_{p}V(X^{n}_{s})^{p})ds,

(55)

at which point the result follows from Grönwall’s lemma. The dependence on the generic constant follows since
V(x_{0})^{p}\leq H_{p}
and since by (
11

H_{p}+H_{q_{p}^{\prime}}\leq(H_{p}+1)(H_{q_{p}^{\prime}}+1)\leq H_{p}H_{q_{p}^{\prime}}\leq H_{p+q_{p}^{\prime}}.

(56)

∎

Now we may prove uniform moments bounds of

at which point the result follows by the properties given in (

We begin by splitting the

Now we move on to our main application. Following

\displaystyle dX^{i,N}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\biggr(\sum_{j=1,2,...,i-1}(X^{i,N}_{t}-X^{j,N}_{t})^{-\alpha}-\sum_{j=i+1,i+2,...,N}(X^{j,N}_{t}-X^{i,N}_{t})^{-\alpha}\biggr)dt

and since this enforces that

, for

Lemma 9

Appendix A
Itô’s Formula

We begin with a statement of Itô’s formula for a function defined on an open set, that could potentially blow up on the boundary of this set. This result is essentially a restatement of Proposition 1 in
[
23
]
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Wiener martingale defined on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,P,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0})
. Let
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable process satisfying

Y_{t}=Y_{0}+\int^{t}_{0}g_{s}ds+\beta W_{t},

(140)

where
\beta>0
is a constant, and
g_{t}
is a
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable processes satisfying

\int^{t}_{0}g_{s}ds<\infty,

(141)

almost surely. Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a non-empty open set,
\tau
a stopping time such that
X_{t\wedge\tau}\in D
for all
t>0
almost surely, and
f\in C^{2}(D)
. Then one recovers the standard Itô’s formula for
f(Y_{t\wedge\tau})
, that is

f(Y_{t\wedge\tau})=f(y_{0})+\int^{t\wedge\tau}_{0}(\langle\nabla f(Y_{s}),g_{s}\rangle+\frac{\beta}{2}\Delta f(Y_{s}))ds+\int^{t\wedge\tau}_{0}\beta\langle\nabla f(Y_{s}),dW_{s}\rangle.

(142)

Proof.

Let us find a set where
f
and it’s first two derivatives are bounded, and furthermore this property always holds on a small ball around each point. Specifically, let us define

D_{a}:=\{x\in D|\;\lvert f(y)\rvert<a,\;\;\lvert\nabla f(y)\rvert<a,\;\;\lvert\Delta f(y)\rvert<a\;\text{for}\;y\in B_{1/a}(x)\}.

(143)

Since
f\in C^{2}(D)
and
D
is non-empty, this set must be non-empty for some
a>0
. Furthermore, let us define the first exist from this set as the stopping time

\tau_{a}:=\inf\{t\geq 0|\;X_{t}\not\in D_{a}\}.

(144)

Then we may consider a mollification of
f
restricted to
D_{a}
. Particularly, let us fix
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\lvert\lvert\phi\rvert\rvert_{1}=1
, and let us define for every
R>0
the function
\phi_{R}(x):=R^{d}\phi(Rx)
. Then we let

f^{a}_{R}(x):=(f1_{D_{a}})*\phi_{R}.

(145)

It follows from standard properties of mollifiers that the following hold: firstly for every
a,R>0
one has
f^{a}_{R}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, secondly
f^{a}_{R}\to f1_{D_{a}}
,
\nabla f^{a}_{R}\to\nabla f1_{D_{a}}
and
\Delta f^{a}_{R}\to\Delta f1_{D_{a}}
pointwise on
D_{a}
. See for instance the relevant chapter in
[
11
]
. Furthermore by a standard argument involving the limit definition of a derivative, one can pass the derivative to either the mollifier or
f
, and therefore by the compact support of
\phi
and the definition of
D_{a}
, it is straightforward to see for sufficiently large
R>0
one has

\lvert f^{a}_{R}\rvert,\lvert\nabla f^{a}_{R}\rvert,\lvert\Delta f^{a}_{R}\rvert\leq a,

(146)

uniformly on
D_{a}
. As a result, we are able to apply the standard Itô’s formula up to
t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{a}
to obtain

\displaystyle f^{a}_{R}(Y_{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{a}})

\displaystyle=f^{a}_{R}(y_{0})+\int^{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{a}}_{0}(\langle\nabla f^{a}_{R}(Y_{s\wedge\tau_{a}}),g_{s}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\Delta f^{a}_{R}(Y_{s\wedge\tau_{a}}))ds

\displaystyle+\int^{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{a}}_{0}\langle\nabla f^{a}_{R}(Y_{s\wedge\tau_{a}}),dW_{s}\rangle.

(147)

Then by the boundedness of
f^{a}_{R}
and its derivatives, using the dominated convergence theorem and the dominated convergence theorem for stochastic integrals, taking the
R\to\infty
limit yields

\displaystyle f(Y_{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{a}})

\displaystyle=f(y_{0})+\int^{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{a}}_{0}(\langle\nabla f(Y_{s\wedge\tau_{a}}),g_{s}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\Delta f(Y_{s\wedge\tau_{a}}))ds

\displaystyle+\int^{t\wedge\tau\wedge\tau_{a}}_{0}\langle\nabla f(Y_{s\wedge\tau_{a}}),dW_{s}\rangle.

(148)

We note that we have written
f
not
f1_{D_{a}}
in the above expression since
Y_{s\wedge\tau_{a}}\not\in D_{a}
only occurs either at
s=0
, in which the above holds vacuously, or for
s\geq\tau_{a}
, which corresponds to a set of measure
0
and therefore doesn’t contribute to the integrals above. Now we must simply show that
\tau\wedge\tau_{a}\to\tau
almost surely as
a\to\infty
. Suppose not. Then, since
\tau_{a}
is almost surely increasing, there has to be a set of positive measure
A\subset\Omega
such that for every
\omega\in A
one has
\lim_{a\to\infty}\tau_{a}(\omega)=\tau_{\infty}(\tau)<\tau
. Since
Y_{t\wedge\tau}
has almost surely continuous sample paths, for every
\omega\in A
one furthermore has
Y_{\tau_{a_{i}}\wedge\tau}(\omega)\to y(\omega)\in D
. However, by the definition of
D_{a}
, for fixed
\omega\in A
there must exist a sequence
y_{i}
of points such that
\max\{\lvert f(y_{i})\rvert,\lvert\nabla f(y_{i})\rvert,\lvert\Delta f(y_{i})\rvert\}=i
and
\lvert y_{i}-Y_{\tau_{i}}(\omega)\rvert\leq i^{-1}
, so
y_{i}\to y
also. Therefore by the continuity of
f\in C^{2}(D)
on
D
, one cannot have
y\in D
. Therefore we have a contradiction, and the proof is completed.
∎
