Sleep health and sleep disorders in Swiss elite athletes
Authors: Albrecht P. A. Vorster, Michael J. Schmid, Daniel Erlacher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study assessed medically relevant symptoms of sleep disorders in elite athletes across all sport types in a large-scale Swiss sample. In late fall 2023, a total of 2293 out of 5188 members of a national squad at junior or elite level participated in an anonymous online survey (Mage = 22.05 years; SD = 7.5, 46.4% female, 53.6% male), stemming from up to 100 different sports, including alpine skiing, athletics, football, and tennis. A total of 25.8% of athletes exhibited symptoms suggestive of sleep disorders requiring clinical evaluation. The most commonly reported conditions were insomnia (7.5%), sleep apnea (3.4%), restless legs syndrome (6.6%), nightmares (6.3%), and non-REM parasomnias, including sleepwalking and night terrors (8.7%). Furthermore, 18.3% of athletes perceived their sleep as non-restorative, and 20.1% showed poor sleep efficiency. Regular use of sleep medication was reported by 3.1% of athletes. Overall, 17.7% of athletes demonstrated poor sleep health, failing in at least two of the six assessed domains. It appears that one in four elite athletes suffers from medically relevant sleep disorders that may impair performance and increase the risk of mental health problems, particularly depression and anxiety disorders. Routine screening and treatment of the causes of sleep disorders might effectively improve performance among athletes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-026-00446-z.

Introduction

Sleep is of utmost importance for athletes, as it is closely linked to numerous processes that promote adaptation to training and competition. This includes the anabolic metabolism [1], the immune system [2], and cognitive functions [3]. Accordingly, sleep loss is associated with impaired athletic and cognitive performance [4]. Sleep is also a crucial factor for mental health and well-being in individuals. For example, sleep disturbances are likely to be a contributing factor in most mental illnesses [5]. In recent years, this aspect has been emphasised several times in (elite) sports [6, 7]. Not surprisingly, various studies have investigated the sleep of athletes and associated sleep disorders [8]. Elite athletes may be at higher sleep disorder risk due to several sport-specific factors: While high psychological stress, competitive pressure, and performance-related anxiety may contribute to the development or perpetuation of insomnia symptoms, sport-specific anthropometric characteristics, such as increased muscle mass and neck circumference in contact or strength-based sports, may elevate the risk for obstructive sleep apnea. Furthermore, early training schedules, frequent travel and late-evening competitions may disrupt circadian regulation [9].

Although sleep problems in athletes have been increasingly studied, most existing research has focused primarily on insomnia symptoms and sleep duration, often in small or sport-specific samples [8, 10]. Other sleep disorders, such as parasomnias, sleep apnea risk, or restless legs syndrome, have received comparatively little attention in elite athletic populations [11, 12]. Moreover, few studies have applied a multidimensional sleep health framework that integrates multiple domains beyond sleep duration alone [13]. Consequently, comprehensive large-scale data on the spectrum of sleep disorder symptoms and sleep health in elite athletes remain limited. Therefore, a large-scale prevalence study is warranted. Understanding more about sleep in elite athletes will help develop strategies to improve sleep, general health, and performance.

In this study, we aimed to assess (1) the prevalence of symptoms across all sleep disorder categories, and (2) sleep characteristics, including both quantity and quality, across diverse sports and risk domains of sleep health.

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional study, with a single measurement time point, collecting self-report data using an online questionnaire available in three languages (German, French, and Italian). The online survey, programmed in LimeSurvey, was open for 24 days from 17 November 2023 to 10 December 2023. Athletes were reminded up to two times to complete the questionnaire. Each participant received a personal code and a link to the online survey in their language.

Participants and procedure

All Swiss athletes aged 16 years or older who were members of a national squad at junior or elite level were invited to participate in an online survey (N = 5188, Table 1). In total, 2,293 (46.4% female, 53.6% male) completed the survey and were included in the analysis (Mage = 22.1 years; SD = 7.5). Equestrian athletes had the highest mean age (Mage = 34.3 years; SD = 14.1), whereas artistic composition sports had the lowest mean age (Mage = 20.4 years; SD = 4.1) of all sport categories.

Participants were recruited from 100 different sports, the most common being athletics (6.6%), cycling (6.3%), soccer (5.9%), ice hockey (5.9%), floorball (5.9%), alpine skiing (5.7%), volleyball (3.2%), handball (2.9%), rowing (2.6%), and orienteering (2.3%). Of these, participants assigned themselves to a total of 99 different sports from a predefined list by Swiss Olympic, one participant selected “miscellaneous”. For analytical purposes, several closely related disciplines were subsequently grouped (e.g., breaking was assigned to dance; ski, bike, and foot orienteering were combined; sport and ice climbing were merged), which led to 93 different sports in the final analysis. This categorisation was applied to ensure sufficient subgroup sizes and conceptual coherence across sport categories.

On average, athletes had 15.0 (SD = 7.0) weekly training hours and 28.4 (SD = 18.1) competitions per year.

To evaluate the representativeness of the sample, all of the athletes contacted were compared with the individuals who took part in the study (see Table 1).

Participants were also more successful (small to medium effect), potentially because the study was carried out in collaboration with Swiss Olympic, with the result that the athletes in question felt more obliged to take part as they benefited more from support measures [14].

To categorise the athletes across different sports, participants were assigned to eight sport groups based on a modified classification system from Emrich et al. [16] (see Table 3): cgs (e.g., athletics, swimming, triathlon, road cycling); game (e.g., ice hockey, soccer, tennis); artistic composition sports (e.g., gymnastics, dancing, ski freestyle); martial arts (e.g., fencing, judo); equestrian; para/wheelchair sports; motor, air, and underwater (e.g., motorcycling, skydiving, diving).

Measures

The questionnaire contained three main sections: (1) baseline demographics, (2) sleep section (Bern Sleep Health Questionnaire), and (3) dream section. The Bern Sleep Health Questionnaire (BSHQ) was used to assess important aspects of sleep health and identify potential sleep-wake disorders [17]. The Athlete Sleep Screening Questionnaire (ASSQ; [18]) informed the conceptual development of the sleep section; however, as it primarily targets insomnia- and circadian-related complaints and relies on broad response categories, we implemented a structured, frequency-based multi-domain questionnaire to enable precise prevalence estimation across major sleep disorder categories and the six Ru-SATED sleep health domains. This newly developed tool, intended for clinical assessments and research purposes, takes about two to five minutes to complete. The BSHQ evaluates several key dimensions of sleep, including sleep duration, efficiency, timing, regularity, and overall satisfaction with sleep. In addition to general sleep health, the questionnaire screens for various sleep-wake disorders such as insomnia, sleep apnoea, hypersomnolence (excessive daytime sleepiness), restless legs syndrome, parasomnias, and fatigue. The questionnaire uses a graded response format to measure the frequency of symptoms, with options ranging from “Never/Rarely” to “More than 5 times a week.” The dream section included three questions about recent dream recall frequency, the percentage of dreams involving sports, and the occurrence of nightmares over the past few months. A comprehensive analysis of nightmares in this cohort has been published separately [19]. In the present study, only nightmare frequency was included in the analyses, as part of the assessment of parasomnia-related symptoms within the ICSD-3-oriented framework. All questions are framed in the first person and are adopted from previously validated sleep questionnaires (the full codebook can be found in online supplemental material S1).

Sleep problems were evaluated for the six main domains of the International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD-3). The variable insomnia risk was given if participants reported at least one night time symptom (difficulty initiating sleep, insomnia, or early morning awakening; item 33, 34, 35) and daytime impairment (e.g., daytime sleepiness, fatigue, or impact on daytime activities; item 31, 32, 44), each rated more than three times a week (≥ 3 times/wk). Sleep apnea risk was given if snoring was present ≥ 3 times/wk or breathing pauses were reported ≥ 1 time/wk alongside at least one daytime symptom (e.g., daytime sleepiness, napping, impact on daytime activities, low restfulness in the morning; item 31, 38, 44, 45) ≥ 3 times/wk. Hypersomnia risk was given if total sleep duration was ≥ 9 h alongside with at least one daytime symptom (e.g. excessive daytime sleepiness, napping, impact on daytime activities, low restfulness in the morning; item 31, 38, 44, 45) ≥ 3 times/wk. Circadian rhythm sleep disorder risk was given if chronotype was “clearly evening type” (item 26), impact on daytime activities ≥ 3 times/wk (item 44), and no additional sleep disorder (e.g., insomnia) was positive. Non-REM parasomnia risk was given if athletes indicated to walk, talk, eat or act strangely or violently during sleep ≥ 1 time/wk (item 41). REM parasomnia risk was given if nightmares were reported ≥ 1 time/wk (item 61). Restless legs syndrome risk was given if participants responded positively to the single question for restless legs (“When I relax in the evening or try to sleep at night, I have an uncomfortable, restless feeling in my legs that is relieved by moving or walking around”; item 40 [20]) ≥ 1 time/wk.

Sleep quality and sleep health were evaluated as a composite set of six independent domains. We used a set of corresponding items according to the most common conceptual framework proposed by Buysse (RuSATED; [21, 22]): regularity, satisfaction, alertness, timing, efficiency, and duration (see Table 2). For the calculation of a composite sleep health score, following the approach of Fan et al. [23], one point was assigned to each domain meeting criteria for healthy sleep, and zero points were assigned otherwise. Patient and public involvement

Two retired international-level athletes and one retired national-level athlete provided input on the face validation of the questionnaire content and design. A draft from the survey was piloted to athletes for question understanding, clarity, and applicability. Based on their feedback, minor wording adjustments were made to improve comprehensibility. Minor changes were made to the content or structure of the questionnaire (e.g. specifying the time for caffeine intake or optimising the sport-specific questions).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Human Sciences, University of Bern (Approval No. 2023-10-05), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. No identifying parameters were recorded and individuals were not identifiable at any stage of the research. All data were treated confidentially and stored on secure local servers, ensuring the anonymity of athletes at all times. Aggregate demographic metrics of the overall athlete population were obtained from the publicly accessible Swiss Olympic athlete database. These data are anonymised and published with the prior consent of Swiss Olympic members.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies (proportions) for categorical variables and as means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables. Data processing and statistical procedures were performed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., 2021).

Results

We found that 25.8% of athletes (29.9% of females, 22.3% of males) show symptoms of sleep problems that warrant medical attention (Table 3). A significantly higher prevalence of sleep disorder symptoms was found in artistic composition sports (35.7%) and motor, air and underwater sports (36.9%), whereas a lower prevalence was observed in para/wheelchair sports (18.8%). The prevalence of insomnia symptoms was lowest for cgs and game sports (6.7% and 6.6%, respectively) and more than doubled for equestrian sports (15.0%). Equestrian sports also showed the highest prevalence of sleep apnea risk (10.0%). Motor, air, and underwater sports exhibited the highest prevalence of NREM parasomnia symptoms (15.5%) and restless legs syndrome symptoms (14.3%). Athletes in artistic composition sports had the highest prevalence of nightmare disorder symptoms (11.3%).

We used a set of corresponding items according to the most common conceptual framework proposed by Buysse (RuSATED; [21, 22]): regularity, satisfaction, alertness, timing, efficiency, and duration (see Table 2). Problems initiating and maintaining sleep result in poor sleep efficiency. Spending less than 85% of the time in bed asleep is a hallmark of insomnia. Low values were observed in athletes from cgs sports and para/wheelchair sports (around 16%). In contrast, 26.3% of athletes in martial arts sports had a poor sleep efficiency below 85%.

The use of sleep medication may reflect difficulties initiating or maintaining sleep. Sleeping pills were consumed on a regular basis (more than once per week) by 3% of athletes, with doubled values in artistic composition sports and para/wheelchair sports (7.1% and 6.3%, respectively) and low values for martial arts and motor, air, and underwater sports (0.7% and 1.2%, respectively).

Night-by-night irregularity of bedtimes and get-up times contributes strongly to poor sleep quality. We found that 7.3% of all athletes indicated irregular sleep times (deviating by more than 2 h, more than 3 times a week). Irregularity was more frequent in artistic composition sports and martial arts (8.9% and 9.5%, respectively) and less frequent in para/wheelchair sports (6.3%).

Good timing is a prerequisite for good sleep health as it is essential to sleep in phase with one’s circadian sleep window. A late chronotype (evening type) is associated with poorer general health, as people with this chronotype are often forced to sleep too early and are cut short of sleep, as the training schedule is set too early for their normal chronotype. We found that 18.2% of all athletes fall into the late type with outstandingly high values in the para/wheelchair sports (37.5%). Game sports and artistic compositions sports rate themselves least often as morning types (5.8% and 6%, respectively) and show higher values for the evening type (21.3% and 25%, respectively). In contrast, a relatively high number of athletes of motor, air, and underwater sports rate themselves as morning types (17.9%).

Excessive daytime sleepiness or low alertness is the result of either poor sleep quality, most often due to breathing pauses during sleep (sleep apnea) or insufficient sleep duration. In our sample, a total of 3.4% of athletes indicated experiencing unintentional sleep episodes more than three times per week. The prevalence was twice as high in artistic composition sports (6%) compared to equestrian sports, which showed a lower rate of 2.5%.

Overall, 18.3% of all athletes reported being dissatisfied with their sleep on more than three nights per week. The highest level of sleep dissatisfaction was observed in motor, air, and underwater sports (32.1%), while the lowest rate was found in cgs sports (15.9%).

Discussion

This is the first large-scale survey assessing medically relevant sleep disorder symptoms of more than 2000 Swiss elite athletes spanning 100 sports. Our aim was to examine (1) the prevalence of symptoms across all sleep disorder categories, and (2) general sleep characteristics within the sleep health framework.

In terms of medically relevant sleep disorder symptoms we found that one out of four athletes (25.8%) presented with a symptomatic sleep problem that warrants medical attention. Screening and treating these sleep disorders will most likely improve general health, well-being, and athletic performance.

More specifically, we found insomnia symptoms were twice as prevalent in artistic composition sports (dance, 17.4% and Misc artistic composition sports, 15.6%), in line with former studies that found increased values of insomnia in dancers [24]. These sports may attract individuals with perfectionist personality types. Perfectionism is associated with self-reported insomnia severity, likely because it heightens perceived stress, fosters maladaptive coping, impairs emotion regulation, and diminishes mental toughness—all factors associated with poorer sleep [25].

The rate of sleep medication use in our sample may suggest that at least 3% of elite athletes regularly encounter problems initiating or maintaining sleep, however, alternative explanations such as insufficient education, inappropriate guidance, or non-indicated use must also be considered. The rates in our sample are below those for the general population age group between 26–40, that is, 8.6% for women and 5.2% for men taking regularly sleep medication [26].

We found sleep apnea symptoms in 3.5% of all athletes, with the highest values in ice hockey (10%) and equestrian sports (10%). Sleep apnea risk increases with age, BMI, and neck circumference [27]. Accordingly, athletes participating in sports requiring substantial neck muscle strength—such as ice hockey, boxing, and weightlifting—may have a higher risk, consistent with findings from prior research [28]. Furthermore, the high value in equestrian sports may be related to the higher average age in these sports.

Restless legs symptoms were particularly prevalent among athletes in endurance sports, with rates of 15.8% in cross-country skiing, 12.0% in biathlon, 10.2% in rowing, and 9.7% in cycling. The observed prevalence aligns with previous small-scale studies demonstrating increased rates in runners (12.6%) and cyclists (14%) [29, 30]. Endurance sports are particularly prone to iron deficiency, which again is a known trigger for restless legs symptoms, as iron is needed as a cofactor for dopamine synthesis. Maintaining ferritin levels above 75ng/ml in these athletes may improve performance and alleviate sleep problems related to unpleasant sensations and urges to move the legs at night—a key feature of restless legs syndrome [31].

Of all athletes, 6.3% are affected by nightmares at least once a week, comparable to 5.4% of German athletes [32] and slightly higher than in representative population samples [33]. We found that nightmare frequency elevated in artistic composition sports as well as in the following sports: snowboarding, freestyle skiing, sailing, windsurfing, volleyball, fencing, and karate. Generally, nightmares are associated with irregular sleep and alcohol consumption, and tend to be more prevalent in females [25]. Parasomnias, including nightmares, are particularly common during childhood and adolescence, with strongly reduced prevalence in adulthood. We might therefore expect a higher prevalence of parasomnias in sports that favour very young athletes, such as in artistic gymnastics. A detailed analysis of the nightmare prevalence of this dataset was recently published [19].

Overall, sleep problems were most prevalent in rowing (35.6%), cross-country skiing (34.2%), sailing/windsurfing (38.7%), ice hockey (34.6%), freestyle skiing (34.9), misc artistic compositions sports (39.0%), motor, air, and underwater sports (36.9%).

With respect to our second aim, 17.7% of athletes were classified as having a sleep health at risk (failing in at least two of six domains), and 7.2% demonstrated severely poor sleep health, with deficits in at least three domains. In detail, 7.5% of athletes reported insufficient sleep duration (< 6 h per night), 20.1% showed reduced sleep efficiency due to difficulties initiating or maintaining sleep, and 7.3% exhibited very irregular sleep patterns. Late sleep timing was observed in 18.2% of athletes. Overall, 3.4% reported excessive daytime sleepiness, and 18.3% perceived their sleep as non-restorative on more than three days per week.

Clinical implications

Taken together, our findings indicate that a substantial proportion of elite athletes exhibit symptoms suggestive of clinically relevant sleep disorders (25.8%) or suboptimal sleep health (17.7%). Although these findings are based on self-report measures and do not represent clinical diagnoses, the observed prevalence appears considerable when compared to general population estimates or previous research in athletes. These results highlight the importance of systematic sleep screening in elite sports settings and support the integration of sleep health assessment into routine sports medical care. Athletes may benefit from regular screening for sleep disorders, such as insomnia, sleep apnea, or restless legs syndrome. Sleep problems are a potential driving factor in the development of mental health disorders that are high among elite athletes [15]. A two-week sleep protocol, used alongside a standardized screening questionnaire, can provide a comprehensive overview of athletes’ sleep patterns and behaviour. Sleep training for insomnia (cognitive behaviour therapy for insomnia, CBT-I) is feasible and leads to long-lasting effects [34, 35]. Further research is needed to determine to what extent sleep problems may increase injury risk in athletes [36]. Limitations

This is the first large-scale survey of elite athletes to document sleep health and symptoms of all the major sleep disorder categories in this population. However, it is not without limitations. Firstly, we relied on screening tools to identify symptoms rather than conducting clinical evaluations. Consequently, we cannot confirm medical diagnoses for these athletes. Secondly, those experiencing sleep problems may have been more inclined to participate, potentially overestimating prevalence—though our findings are consistent with previous studies [15, 28, 32, 33]. Thirdly, sleep health was operationalised as a composite score with equal weighting of the six Ru-SATED domains. Although previous research has demonstrated good reliability and meaningful associations with health outcomes when equal weighting is applied across domains, the relative contributions of individual domains may differ across populations, and no consensus currently exists on differential weighting strategies. Notwithstanding this limitation, the multidimensional assessment of sleep health has been shown to provide greater explanatory and predictive power than reliance on single parameters, such as sleep duration alone. Lastly, when examining some individual sports, sample sizes were often too small to draw reliable conclusions about specific risks. Overcoming this challenge will likely require larger, multinational surveys that include elite athletes from multiple countries, given the limited number of elite athletes competing at national and international levels.

Conclusion

In summary, one-quarter of all athletes report symptoms of a sleep disorder that requires medical treatment. Sports physicians should be trained in screening and treating these diverse classes of sleep disorders, as they most surely impact the general health, well-being, and performance of the athletes. Untreated sleep disorders pose an often overlooked risk factor in the development of mental illness and injuries in athletes.
