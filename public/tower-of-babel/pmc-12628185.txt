Implementing system-wide digital medical interpretation: a framework for healthcare organizations
Authors: Ulka Kothari, Allison Squires, Jonathan Austrian, Anatoly Feldman, Irfan Syed, Simon Jones
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study evaluates and enhances language access services for Limited English Proficiency (LEP) patients in a large urban health system by integrating interpreter services into the Electronic Health Record (EHR), aiming to reduce care disparities and improve the digital experience for both patients and clinicians.Materials and MethodsUsing a descriptive evaluation approach, the project assessed barriers to interpreter service usage and developed solutions informed by stakeholder engagement. Emphasis was placed on interfacing the EHR with the vendor platform, using existing devices, tracking utilization, ensuring cost-effectiveness, and implementing the solution across multiple hospitals and outpatient settings.Results, Discussion, and ConclusionAfter implementation, audio and video interpreter service use rose significantly, with calls increasing from an average of 9700 calls per month in 2022 to over 68 176 calls per month by the end of 2024. Over 14 000 clinicians used the service for more than 121 077 unique patients. Average waiting times for the top ten languages fell below 30 seconds, and user satisfaction was high, with an average interpreter rating of 4.9. Conclusion: The EHR-integrated interpreter service has improved language service access, reduced waiting times and enhanced user satisfaction, marking noteworthy progress in overcoming language barriers and potentially decreasing healthcare disparities for LEP populations. We plan to assess the impact on patient outcomes.Keywords: interpreter, patients, electronic health record, integrating, outcomes

Introduction

Patients with Limited English Proficiency (LEP) face significant hurdles in the healthcare system, leading to poor health outcomes. There are approximately 67.8 million people in the United States, or 8.5% of the population 5 years and over who speak English less than “very well.”1 Language barriers impede LEP patients’ ability to access healthcare services effectively. LEP patients are less likely to seek medical care, adhere to treatments, comprehend medical instructions, and face disparities in health insurance coverage. They experience higher rates of hospital readmissions, increased medical errors, and overall poorer health outcomes compared to their English-proficient counterparts.2–4

Effective and safe communication between patients and clinicians is critical for providing equitable and high-quality care. Language access services include any combination of telephone, video, and in-person services.5,6 Their purpose is to address language barriers between people seeking health care services and the clinicians serving them to ensure that communication is safe and that their care needs are met. Persons with a language preference other than English—including sign language users—have a right to an interpreter during a healthcare encounter.7,8 Demand for language access services is growing nationally in the US, driven in part by increased rates of immigration, growing numbers of households that speak a language other than English at home, and multiple successful lawsuits filed by the Deaf and Hard of Hearing community against healthcare organizations over failures to provide adequate interpreter services. Healthcare staff who are untrained interpreters or family members as interpreters are more likely to make clinically significant mistakes than qualified medical interpreters.9 All healthcare organizations are required to have a language access services plan to meet the requirements of civil rights laws, the final rule of Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, and Joint Commission accreditation requirements.5,10,11

For healthcare facilities seeking to expand language access services, there is little guidance about how to choose services, evaluate what works best with staff, and ultimately, organize services in a way that can be captured through the electronic health record so quality relationships between language access services delivery and patient outcomes can be understood. The end goal of the efforts is to reduce inequities in patient outcomes, reduce threats to patient safety associated with language barriers, and improve the overall patient experience.

Despite the laws and regulations, however, interpreter services implementation varies widely across all patient groups and quality metrics are not yet standardized for language access services and implementation.12–14 The presence of a service does not mean it is of superior quality. One potential metric of quality is the user uptake of interpreter services. Put simply, if staff are not using the service, there are problems with access, availability, and ease of use of interpreter services as an intervention to bridge a patient-clinician language barrier. Evaluating language access systems is a critical step for ensuring quality. For example, meaningfully measuring interpreter services utilization is challenging because there are no national reporting standards for interpreter services companies. Data capture of patient, provider, and encounter level details are not easily reportable, and if captured by interpreter services companies, it is often of poor quality. Quality is poor because reporting relies on providers giving accurate information to the interpreter services when placing calls and accurately noting interpreter details in their documentation. There are no requirements for the interpreter to document the same information for confirmation. The overall reporting challenges faced by both interpreter services companies and those who use their services as well as inaccurate data management thwarts efforts to improve interpreter service utilization and identify where implementation problems occur.

This study describes how our large, urban health system addressed concerns about language access. We evaluated existing interpreter services across our six hospitals and multiple outpatient care centers, aiming to develop solutions that would enhance interpreter utilization and improve care for our Limited English Proficiency (LEP) patients. We ultimately created a technical partnership/solution with a vendor that would facilitate better access and ultimately increase use of language access services. The goal of the paper is to provide insights into our process, challenges, and successes with implementation of an EHR integrated interpreter that is generalizable to many health systems.

Methods

Design

We used a descriptive evaluation approach to guide the process for improving interpreter service utilization. Evaluation studies both evaluate the result of an intervention as well as capture processes that are leading to a specific issue or outcome.15 They involve identifying known issues from both qualitative and quantitative data as well as stakeholder engagement and are useful for tackling complex issues like language access.15–17

Setting

The health care system for the project is a large urban academic medical center located in the New York City metropolitan area. It serves a linguistically diverse patient population that matches the top ten languages spoken in the US. There are six hospitals in the system, each serving a different patient population group shaped by geography and socioeconomic status. Outpatient facilities cover all specialties.

Primary outcome measure

The outcome metric was utilization of interpreter services by clinicians as measured by number of interpreter calls placed per month.

Organizational assessment process

The process began with internal data that had established that the legacy interpreter services relied on a cumbersome and inconsistent system. Clinicians faced several barriers to accessing interpreters for LEP patients. First, “Device Dependent” Access required clinicians to locate specific, dedicated devices or schedule in-person interpreters. The second issue related to inconsistent Vendor Services. Different vendors were used across the health system, leading to variations in the availability of Over-the-Phone Interpretation (OPI) and Video Remote Interpretation (VRI). Finally, none of the previous options had EHR Integration. This meant clinicians had to navigate a multi-step process outside their regular workflow to connect with and utilize an interpreter service (Figure 1). They would dial a specific number or launch a separate application, provide patient details (MRN, language, etc.) and their own information, and then manually document the interpreter’s details in the patient chart, often in an inconsistent way, thereby making vendor accountability a challenge.

From there, we conducted focus group discussions with multiple stakeholders such as physicians, nurses, trainees, advanced care practitioners, etc. to explore barriers for using the legacy interpreter systems. Confirming our internal data, the most frequently cited barrier was difficulty in locating the right devices required to initiate audio and video calls with an interpreter. Participants also reported significant delays between requesting an interpreter and connecting with one. They identified the process of providing patient and encounter details to the interpreter as time-consuming, as was the need for clinicians to manually record interpreter information in patient charts. All the barriers to patient communication via language services are widely documented in the literature. Therefore, it was important that the intervention—the new language access system—addressed said barriers.14,18–21

With the goal of increasing utilization of language access services across the enterprise, leadership prioritized several key factors identified in the initial assessments. First and foremost was the need to optimize the clinician and patient digital experience. Ease of use and minimal time delays were paramount. Secondly, we sought a solution that leveraged existing enterprise technology and devices already used by clinicians to access the EHR, particularly those with audio and video capabilities. The ability to launch interpreter calls from a variety of devices that seamlessly integrated into patient and provider workflows, particularly in settings requiring maneuverability around patient beds, was critical. Finally, given the anticipated increase in interpreter service utilization, cost-effectiveness was important. With a clear understanding of these core issues and stakeholder preferences, we initiated the vendor selection process.

Vendor selection process

Drawing from our assessment findings, we focused on several key criteria during vendor selection. We prioritized vendors with robust architecture and technology, including the agility and willingness to partner with our health system to achieve deep EHR integration. This integration was crucial for facilitating a seamless clinician digital experience, incorporating both video and audio capabilities with our existing devices, reliability, scalability, workflow support, and strong analytics capability. We specifically sought vendors who could demonstrate a commitment to high-quality interpretation, a broad offering of languages (including languages of limited diffusion such as some Chinese dialects and Indigenous languages from the Americas), and low interpreter wait times for the languages most frequently spoken by our patients. Reliability, scalability, workflow support, and strong analytics capabilities were also essential considerations. A vendor was selected and teams received signoffs from architecture, finance, and clinical teams on proposed designs and workflows.

Core characteristics of the final product

The final product provides both video and audio interpretation, leveraging the existing speakers, microphones, cameras, and screens of devices already used by clinicians during patient encounters (eg, desktops, mobile devices, tablets). While our internal device inventory helped identify the remaining few areas needing additional equipment to ensure comprehensive access, this approach maximized the use of existing resources. To achieve seamless integration, we established a bidirectional connection between our EHR and vendor’s platform using Application Programming Interfaces (APIs). The integration allows for a “single click” connection to the preferred language interpreters (Figure 1). Clinicians can now launch the interpreter application directly from a patient’s chart within the EHR. Upon initiation, relevant patient and encounter data—including encounter ID, preferred language, department, facility location, date and time of the call, and provider ID—are automatically transmitted to the interpreter service via API. After the call concludes, the interpreter service sends back details such as interpreter ID and call duration to the EHR via the APIs. All this information is filed within the EHR under a dedicated “Interpreter Information” note type. In addition to this automated documentation, providers simply note in their progress notes that an interpreter was utilized during the encounter. To ensure ongoing quality monitoring, providers can also rate their satisfaction with interpreter interaction, providing real-time feedback to the vendor.

Training

We provided training to users on using the EHR-integrated interpreter through online modules. To support the initiative, we conducted numerous roadshows during departmental meetings, trained frontline staff, and offered elbow support. The first campus launched the service in November 2022. Over the following year, we rolled out the EHR-integrated interpreter service to five additional campuses and ambulatory sites. The rollout schedule was as follows: February 2023 for the second campus, March 2023 for the third, May 2023 for the fourth, August 2023 for the fifth and sixth campuses, and September-October 2023 for ambulatory sites.

Usage and quality monitoring

To track utilization of the EHR-integrated interpreter, we collaborated with the reporting and analytics teams of both our Medical Center Information Technology and vendor. Some of the key measures include total number of interpreter calls per month, call location, encounter type (inpatient, ED, outpatient, etc.), provider type, language type, average wait time per language, interpreter ratings, duration of calls, audio vs video calls, number of unique LEP patients, and unique providers utilizing interpreter services. These reports have been instrumental in helping department and hospital leadership to create targeted improvement efforts to increase interpreter utilization. For example, the Pediatric and Emergency Medicine departments have created dashboards that measure and improve utilization of interpreter services at various points of patient encounters. This data guides their interventions to improve interpreter utilization. Providers have largely adopted the new system for scheduled patient encounters. The legacy interpreter system is now reserved for specific use cases only, such as unscheduled telephone encounters.

Data analysis

To assess the impact of the new system integration on interpreter service utilization, we conducted a descriptive analysis of clinician interpreter service usage patterns over the past 3 years, encompassing before, during, and after implementation. Beyond overall usage patterns, we delved deeper by analyzing usage based on factors such as care delivery roles, unit types, and other structural elements. This granular approach provided insights into the adoption process of the new service option across different user groups and clinical settings.

Results

Descriptive data demonstrate a steady increase in the number of audio and video interpreter calls from an average of 9700 calls per month in 2022 to over 68 176 calls per month by December 2024 (Figure 2) across the enterprise. From January 2023 to December 2024, 14 336+ unique providers have utilized the EHR integrated interpreter for 12 1077+ unique LEP patients. Overall usage of interpreter services increased 250% since initial implementation. This data does not include LEP patients that refused interpreter services, encounters with a language concordant and certified provider, and when in-person certified interpreter services were utilized. The top 5 languages were Spanish, Chinese/Mandarin, Haitian Creole, American Sign Language (ASL), and Bengali (Figure 3). The average wait time for the top 10 languages has been below the Service Level Agreement (SLA) of 30 seconds (Figure 4). There is increased utilization of EHR integrated interpreters for patients requesting American Sign Language (ASL) (Figure 5).

Discussion

The clear and rapid growth in clinician usage of interpreter services demonstrates that integration of these services with EHR offered a solution that facilitated care delivery for LEP patients by clinicians on the frontlines and addressed many well-documented barriers to interpreter services usage identified in the literature. The implementation and integration of interpreter technology were achieved through a systematic process that involved identifying barriers, engaging frontline staff and leadership to create guiding principles for interpreter services, selecting vendors, developing solutions to integrate interpreter services with the EHR, creating reporting capabilities to monitor utilization, and educating providers. The massive uptake in usage by nurses on the patient care frontlines is a notable finding. We believe that the main usability concerns were mitigated through the EHR integrated interpreter workflow and facilitated uptake by nurses in frontline roles. The integration allowed them to have the frequent contact they have with hospitalized patients during their stay at a level that may not significantly differ from English speaking patients, but that remains a hypothesis to be tested. Furthermore, the improved uptake across all clinician roles is another signal that incorporating interpreter utilization in existing provider workflows and EHR encourages its use because it does not add significant time to their work. Overall, the size of the increased uptake suggests that patient outcomes may be impacted. Future research will examine these outcomes once there is a year’s worth of data, and we can be assured the analyses will be adequately powered. Future studies should also conduct in-depth analyses with clinicians to determine the relative roles of ease of use vs training.

Another critical characteristic of the EHR integrated interpreter solution is the data it generates. Not only can it capture who is using the service, but it documents the use and is able to integrate performance evaluations of interpreters. The interprofessional literature has often noted performance and quality concerns with interpreters as an issue for clinicians—especially those who may have basic discourse competence in another language that would allow them to understand what is being said and evaluate quality of interpretation.9,22–24 The data capture characteristic is an important part of determining service quality. It is also important for patient outcomes research. The level of detail of the data that interpreter services vendors provide will determine whether patient-centered outcomes analyses can be conducted as part of quality improvement efforts. Demonstrating the efficacy of the language access intervention is an important part of justifying the costs of interpreter services and reducing the risk for penalties associated with outcomes like 30-day readmission to the hospital. We strongly recommend a follow-up evaluation as part of the process and at the time of this submission, we are in the process of conducting one that includes stakeholder feedback centered on addressing implementation challenges.

Challenges and limitations

Given that this was a new development, establishing links between our EHR and vendor with all needed attributes took a few months. For some non-EHR integrated use cases, we did not have the needed information to identify location and had to put workarounds to map this information. The health system has multiple Language Service Departments which complicated the billing process as department locations and facilities needed to be custom cross-mapped in the vendor’s system so the invoices could be submitted to the appropriate entity. It also took us some time to create automatic billing based on the department from where the call was launched. We are actively working on a solution that links the interpreter information sent to EHR with provider documentation. The initial set of reporting looked at process measures on utilization, languages, average wait times, provider roles, etc. We are now measuring the potential impact of this project on patient outcomes such as readmissions, length of stay, patient satisfaction, etc. Finally, nurse practitioner roles were grouped with nurses, so we could not disaggregate them for this study. In future analyses, we plan to group nurse practitioner uptake with physician assistant uptake as their roles are more similar. Understanding registered nurse level uptake in greater detail is also planned as they are most likely to have the most frequent contact with LEP patients during a hospitalization or an ambulatory care encounter.

Conclusions

Our hospital system serves a highly diverse patient population. LEP patients are known to have elevated risks of gaps in care including poor access, medical errors, and inferior patient outcomes. It is hard to achieve a provider language concordance and interpreter services help address this gap. The EHR integrated interpreter initiative is a significant step forward in addressing language barriers for patients with LEP. Integration with EHR, utilization of existing devices used for documentation and care delivery, short wait times, and ability to choose audio or video services has led to enhanced provider digital experience as reflected in the increasing utilization of the services. We hope to reduce care disparity for LEP patients and track outcomes and impact from this initiative.
