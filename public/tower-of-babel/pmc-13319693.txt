An automated end-to-end pipeline for the management, de-identification, and distribution of whole-slide images using DICOM: An institutional implementation
Authors: Joshua W Tashman, Chris Gorman, Emilio Madrigal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedical imaging data are an essential resource for research and teaching; however, regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act require careful consideration of protected health information (PHI) contained within images and their metadata. De-identification, the removal of PHI to prevent subject re-identification, is often essential for regulatory compliance. In pathology, whole-slide imaging (WSI) files—high-resolution scans of entire glass slides—are essential for research and machine learning model training. Currently, many scanner vendors use proprietary WSI formats; some vendors offer de-identification tools, which are often manual and tedious to use. Alternatively, physical slides may be re-scanned with obfuscated labels, though this is resource intensive. Here, we describe an institutional-scale automated WSI de-identification pipeline, which utilizes an informatics-based approach to convert clinical WSI from 11 proprietary formats into de-identified WSI in multiple open formats. This approach minimizes manual processes and additional slide-scanning hardware, storage, and personnel. De-identification requests are submitted through a zero-footprint web portal following the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources ServiceRequest data model. De-identification is verified via a human-in-the-loop review, and archives are distributed using cloud-based storage. Since deployment in November 2024, our pipeline has de-identified 819 cases comprising 4322 WSI files generated by 7 scanner models in 4 formats. We demonstrate that the conversion process is predictable, linearly scalable, and reliable across de-identification request sizes (238× variation), image sizes (120× variation), and capture format. This pipeline eliminates the need for manual de-identification or re-scanning while achieving high throughput and reliability at an institutional scale.Keywords: Whole-slide imaging/WSI, De-identification, Digital pathology, DICOM, Machine learning datasets, Computational pathology, Healthcare interoperability

Introduction

Sharing medical imaging data are an established and essential practice for clinical and research activities.1, 2 Imaging modalities and content vary across radiology, clinical medicine, and pathology, but a key shared component is the protected health information (PHI; individually identifiable health information) that can be included in these files.3, 4, 5 To render well-informed second opinions, and for proper billing, PHI must be included with imaging studies, either embedded within the images or linked via associated metadata. For education, research, and commercial use, however, images that are to be released without limitations to their use must be de-identified to comply with patient data sharing regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in Europe.4, 5, 6, 7 Alternatively, in restricted research settings with contractually binding data protections, shared medical data may retain certain PHI elements as permitted by institutional and regulatory oversight.

De-identification requires removing sufficient patient identifying information to make re-identification, that is reconnection of the patient's identity and their imaging, and possibly other studies, as difficult as legally mandated, while retaining as much utility as possible.1, 8 Removing certain PHI is trivial, however, careful consideration of less obvious identifiers is necessary. For a whole-slide imaging (WSI) file, removal of the slide label, which often includes the patient's name and date of birth, hospital of origin and date of creation, is fundamental. Less obvious identifiers reside in the file metadata. These include specimen identifiers extracted from slide barcodes, scanner model and serial numbers, and vendor-specific data elements that may be critical for downstream software interpretation.1, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 A reductionist approach would be to strip all metadata and retain only pixel data; however, this can violate file format requirements, preventing the image from opening in widely adopted viewing software. The balance of de-identification is between the risk of re-identification and the downstream utility for research and education, which often requires more than the bare minimum of additional information.

In fields with mature imaging standards, such as radiology, the process of de-identification has been simplified by the implementation of the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) standard for the management and transmission of medical imaging information.13 The organization of patient information and imaging data within the DICOM standard facilitates rules-based de-identification strategies that align with HIPAA requirements.14 These rules are actively maintained and updated, supporting a robust framework for institutional compliance.

The DICOM standard has supported WSI since 2010 (Supplement 145), but the persistence of proprietary image formats and software has limited its utility in de-identification.15, 16, 17 Some vendors offer de-identification tools within their software suites, but these require local installation and laborious manual operation, limiting scalability for large de-identification requests. Script-based, vendor-specific de-identification has been reported, but without sufficient details to determine the extent of automation and scalability.18 To address these limitations at our institution, we initially attempted manual de-identification using proprietary scanner software with email-based request management, but this approach became untenable as our digital pathology infrastructure matured and WSI files were archived to high-performance storage inaccessible by scanning workstations. Whereas automated, script-based de-identification of WSI in a variety of native formats has been implemented,19 each format requires specifically designed tools, which must be individually maintained; the standardization inherent in DICOM conversion decreases this complexity.

An alternative approach utilized at some institutions is the re-scanning of slides with the slide label obscured or removed. This solution effectively doubles scanning time and storage requirements while diverting personnel from processing new clinical specimens within the routine lab workflow. While functional for small requests (typically ranging from one to a few slides), this approach does not scale to the de-identification of large collections (routinely comprising dozens to hundreds of slides), particularly as most labs lack excess scanner capacity to dedicate toward re-scanning efforts.20, 21 A major impetus for implementing digital pathology is the development of assistive machine learning (ML) tools, which require large datasets to achieve optimal performance. ML model development typically requires thousands to tens of thousands of images, making manual de-identification approaches impractical.22, 23

An ideal solution to de-identification of WSI files would eliminate any need for re-scanning by repurposing existing digital assets while bypassing proprietary software. Such an approach is now feasible: tools for automated, efficient conversion of vendor-specific formats to DICOM exist,24, 25, 26 as do local and web-based de-identifying solutions.27, 28, 29, 30 Furthermore, implementation of DICOM enables normalization of patient information into a standardized format, producing a uniform, predictable schema for archiving and subsequent high-reliability de-identification. It also allows for downstream conversion to file types requested by researchers, preventing issues with file compatibility.

Here, we report on the development and implementation of an institutional-scale pipeline for sharing de-identified WSI files as a bridge for secondary use of clinical data after and separate from our clinical WSI capture operations. Our system is web-based with enterprise-level user authentication. Clinically acquired images from our diverse scanning platforms are converted to DICOM using wsidicomizer and de-identified using a rules-based system on our local cluster. De-identified images are then converted to requested formats and hosted on S3-compatible cloud storage provider. All processes are modular, scalable, and parallelized, enabling de-identification requests to be processed efficiently with the potential for future scaling to even larger datasets for ML.

De-identification pipeline

Our de-identification and distribution pipeline (DeID) integrates with our Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR)-based digital pathology management system using the ServiceRequest resource. At a high level, DeID comprises two main components: (1) a supervisor service and (2) one or more worker services. The supervisor queries incoming requests and maintains a task queue. Workers read tasks from this queue, and either convert slides or packages and upload the results.

DeID is designed to run on a Kubernetes cluster to streamline communication, security, stability, consistency, and scalability. The supervisor service exposes a RESTful HTTP interface accessible to cluster-internal compute resources, avoiding the need for complex authentication mechanisms within the DeID application itself. The supervisor service also exposes a WebSocket interface, which allows worker processes to asynchronously wait for tasks to arrive rather than using time-based polling.

System architecture

DeID is tightly integrated with our digital pathology management system, Insight. Insight provides the front-end interface for, among other things, the WSI de-identification request system. For a WSI file to be de-identified, it must have already been scanned and digitized into Insight via a separate process. Authenticated users can request de-identified versions of any slide they have access to, provided it was scanned in a compatible format. Once a request is submitted, the DeID service queries the Insight API for all active de-identification service requests, performs the conversion for each WSI file, stores the output to a cloud storage provider, and notifies the requester that the conversion is complete (Fig.1). The notification e-mail contains a cryptographically signed link to download the data from the cloud storage provider.

High-level DeID sequence diagram modeling the query for active requests, execution of whole-slide image de-identification, archiving, and delivery to the requester.

The DeID service consists of a supervisor service and one or more worker services. The supervisor is a Python 3.12 application using the FastAPI framework to provide a RESTful HTTP interface. After every N minutes, the supervisor service will query the Insight API for active de-identification service requests (Fig. 2). If there are any new, active service requests the supervisor collects the necessary data for each slide to convert and creates a SlideConversionTask instance for each slide. If all slides in a request have been converted, the supervisor creates a CompleteOrderTask to finalize and distribute the de-identified slides to the requester.

DeID supervisor sequence diagram modeling the supervisor querying for requests, creating WSI de-identification or packaging tasks, and queuing the tasks for workers to execute.

The supervisor service also provides interfaces to the workers to facilitate asynchronous processing (Fig. 3). For example, while the supervisor polls the Insight API on a schedule, the workers connect via long-lived WebSocket connections to the supervisor. When the supervisor adds a new task to the queue, it sends a notification to each of the connected workers over the WebSocket. To avoid messy race conditions or synchronization, the notification only triggers the worker to try to request a new task from the supervisor. The supervisor distributes tasks until the queue is empty, which in turn causes the worker to open a new WebSocket connection and wait for a notification again.

Request submission

Users submit de-identification requests via a dedicated front-end interface (Insight; Fig. 4). Whereas the selection of WSI files is technically a distinct application decoupled from the core de-identification pipeline, it currently allows requesters to search for individual or multiple case numbers. From the search results, users can select specific slides or all available WSI to be aggregated into a single de-identification request. The system's modular architecture is designed to be extensible; while users currently utilize case number searches, the underlying schema can support future integration of additional parameters—such as specimen ID, patient identifiers, diagnoses, or specimen descriptions (e.g., “adrenal gland”)—as institutional needs evolve.

Requester sequence diagram modeling the requester authentication and interaction with Insight, request submission, and submission confirmation.

The requests are recorded as FHIR ServiceRequest resources for each slide and case within the order. To manage the asynchronous workflow, each resource is assigned a status of “active” upon submission. As the supervisor and workers process the queue, these statuses are updated to “received” and then “processing,” providing a granular audit trail of the de-identification lifecycle from submission to completion.

Conversion pipeline

The core conversion process uses the wsidicomizer Python package.24 This tool supports converting several proprietary lossy and lossless WSI file formats into DICOM, either directly or via integrated libraries such as openslide and bioformats (Supplemental Table 1). While the pipeline supports all formats compatible with wsidicomizer, it is noted that certain formats require lossy recompression during normalization, a factor that may influence downstream image analysis.

De-identification of patient data occurs in two passes through direct calls to the wsidicomizer API rather than the command-line interface. This allows the pipeline to programmatically enforce the DICOM Basic Application Level Confidentiality Profile during the initial conversion to DICOM.14 By utilizing the API's internal save function with default parameters, we ensure a reproducible and standardized intermediate state. In the second pass, when the DICOM objects are transcoded to the user-requested format (e.g., svs), the output is written to explicitly include only those technical attributes defined in our institutional WSI Data Provenance Profile (Table 1).

When executing a SlideConversionTask, a worker daemon will convert the specified slide into DICOM with wsidicomizer. In this step, the physical label region is explicitly excluded from the converted slide images and replaced by a generic gray placeholder. For consult slides, which represent a smaller portion of our volume, label removal and obfuscation are handled by a manual review. After DICOM conversion, the worker daemon constructs an Aperio svs-compatible TIFF file for use in Aperio ImageScope or other common viewers. While svs is the current format preferred by our users, the modular pipeline can be readily adapted to output other formats, such as OME-TIFF or native DICOM (Supplemental Table 2). If a thumbnail image is not provided, one will be generated using the lowest-resolution pyramid layer of the input WSI.

Supervisor service

The supervisor service is implemented as a Python FastAPI HTTP server running as a Kubernetes application (Fig. S1). The service is exposed within the cluster as a Service object, thereby allowing access from worker daemons in the same cluster while prohibiting access from outside of the cluster, all without any authentication mechanism configured in the supervisor itself. It should be noted, however, that because the supervisor is just an HTTP server, authentication could be added with an OAuth2-based authentication flow. Adding external authentication in this way would allow for a multi-cluster distributed approach if that was desired.

Worker service and output distribution

The workers are deployed as containerized Python daemon processes running in the same Kubernetes cluster as the supervisor service. When a worker container is created, it connects to the configured URL for the supervisor and attempts to get a new task from the queue. If no task is returned, the worker connects to the notification WebSockets endpoint to wait for a signal. If the worker receives a signal on the open WebSocket, it attempts to retrieve a task from the supervisor again.

If the worker receives a de-identificationTask, it uses the data provided in the task object to locate the input slide and converts it to DICOM using the wsidicomizer package. After the conversion to DICOM, the worker constructs a svs-compatible TIFF image using the image data and metadata from the DICOM instances. Because the output svs file is constructed entirely by the worker, it ensures that no identifiable metadata is included in the de-identified slide file. If a slide fails to convert for any reason, the failure is logged and reported to the user.

If the worker receives a CompleteOrderTask, it means all the slides for a given request have been completed, and the worker is now responsible for naming, packaging, and distributing the WSI files and notifying the requester. During the de-identification process, slide identifiers need to be obfuscated to reduce the risk of reidentification. To that end, each slide is named using a hash function of the slide identifier, request identifier, and a secure secret key shared by the workers and supervisor. While a completely random UUID would work as a filename, our approach allows workers to independently generate output filenames that are consistent across the entire system, which allows us to easily horizontally scale the number of workers without requiring any synchronous communication among them or between worker and supervisor, or any additional database or storage for managing state.

To finalize an order, the worker will read the contents of the order's output directory, then split it into multiple subdirectories based on a given maximum size, e.g., 10 GB. Each output directory is packaged as a tar file and uploaded to an S3-compatible object store. The worker uses the storage provider's API to generate a public, pre-signed sharing URL with an expiry interval of 90 days. The worker uses a template to generate an email with the pre-signed URLs and a lookup table to allow the requester to remap de-identified slides to private identifiers.

After a worker daemon completes either a de-identificationTask or a CompleteOrderTask, it sends a POST request to the supervisor indicating completion and requests a new task.

Results

The described de-identification pipeline was launched in November 2024 after validation studies of de-identification quality were performed. Requesters fill out a form that is received by informatics staff who, in compliance with an institutional zero-tolerance PHI policy, review the requested slides for any slide labels or PHI inadvertently captured in the scanned image, then enter the de-identification order into the pipeline.31 Once the de-identification request is completed, the de-identified archives are delivered to the requester using an automated emailing system. The requester receives links to download the WSI archives, along with a table providing details for re-identification and error tracking. Since the launch of the pipeline, we have received 186 requests for de-identification of 4322 slides from 819 distinct cases. These requests cover slides from four distinct scanning platforms, seven different scanners, and four distinct image formats. The requests are predominantly for research (88%), with the remainder being teaching (12%) sets. The total data volume of the converted slides is 8.33 TB, with individual slides ranging in size from 0.07 to 21.96 GB. Any error reports are forwarded to the requester with the successfully generated de-identified archives. It is at the requester's discretion whether a re-scan of the failed slide is subsequently requested, though we have yet to receive such a request.

To characterize system performance under controlled conditions and evaluate scalability, we submitted 9 different de-identification request orders, using clinical cases, ranging in size from 3 to 713 slides (Table 2). The requests contained WSI from 4 vendors, in 3 proprietary formats, ranging in size from 0.07 to 8.35 GB (120-fold size range). The system processed 1332 WSI files comprising 2.43 TB over 31 h without failure. The mean processing time, which we characterized as the span from initiation of format conversion to completion of de-identification (creation of de-identified svs file), was 1.42 min per slide, with a 118 WSI/h average throughput (Fig. 5). Processing rate averaged 1.24 GB/min consistently across the full-size spectrum, with larger files achieving better throughput rates due to amortized overhead. Comparison of file size and processing time showed a strong linear correlation (R2 = 0.78, p < 0.0001), with more complex models providing only marginally improved fit (Supplemental Table 3). The linear relationship (time = 0.45 × file size +0.53) explains 78% of processing time variance, enabling accurate turnaround time prediction from file metadata alone. This predictable, linear performance enables accurate turnaround time estimates and supports institutional resource planning.

Discussion

The sharing of medical image data is essential for a range of clinical, educational, research, and commercial applications.1, 2 These transfers are governed by regulations such as HIPAA and the GDPR, which mandate the removal of personally identifiable information and PHI to mitigate the risk of re-identification (reconnecting a patient's data to their identity).4, 5, 6, 7 While de-identification is well-established in fields like radiology through robust, rules-based DICOM protocols, pathology has lagged behind.13, 14, 16, 17 Historically, proprietary image formats and manual tools have hindered high-efficiency, large-volume WSI de-identification, despite the availability of format-specific scripts.18, 19 Manual workarounds, such as slide re-scanning, remain untenable due to the doubling of effort, increased storage requirements, and lack of surplus hardware.20, 21 To efficiently build large WSI datasets, which are essential for scaling the development of ML tools, a standardized approach is necessary.22, 23

Here, we have described the development and implementation of an institutional-scale WSI de-identification and sharing system that overcomes legacy difficulties with proprietary image formats and avoids re-scanning of slides. Our pipeline has been in production since November 2024 and has been widely adopted within our department, with a total of 186 requests for conversion of 819 cases comprising 4322 WSI files and 8.33 TB. These WSI files were captured on 7 scanners from 4 vendors and initially output in 4 proprietary formats before conversion to DICOM and subsequently svs (Supplemental Table 4).

The pipeline's performance is predictable, with linear scaling across a wide range of file and request sizes in both real-world and benchmarking settings. This allows for accurate estimation of job duration and hardware requirements for future institutional expansion. At an average throughput of 118 WSI/h, this approach processes in 1 h what previously required approximately one working week using manual vendor-specific software. The throughput is also at least twice as fast as just the re-scanning time for the largest commercially available scanners with a realistic scan-area (2 × 2 cm), and is less likely to require subsequent re-scanning (we had no conversion failures in our benchmark set).32 Whereas alternative bitstream manipulation techniques may offer higher raw processing speeds, our pipeline prioritizes the creation of a decoupled, standards-based derivative to satisfy institutional requirements for clinical data integrity and regulatory compliance. We found this performance to be operationally efficient for institutional-scale requests, particularly as the workflow is designed to occur asynchronously and in conjunction with manual verification steps. As the longest wait time across slides was attributed to queueing, which can be improved with additional hardware and more sophisticated queue management, there is clear potential for further throughput optimization. WSI de-identification using an informatics-based approach has made slide sharing for research, teaching, and clinical applications an easy, efficient process.

Requests for de-identification are captured using a web-based form based on the FHIR standard ServiceRequest resource, which enables high-level integration with the pathology lab information system (LIS) and electronic medical records (EMRs) system. This integration allows for the development of dashboards and analytics tools to monitor de-identification requests and could even enable estimation of re-identification risks. Tracking requests by individual users, combined with knowledge of characteristics such as diagnosis and patient demographics, would allow us to ensure the de-identification service is being utilized within the confines of the data-sharing practices of our institution. For example, researchers requesting unusually large or broad collections of WSI may trigger a review of their Institutional Review Board to ensure proper research governance.

Additionally, integration of the LIS metadata enables tracking of the characteristics of shared WSI files, such as the number of slides from a specific specimen, spacing between different sections from a given block, slides from related specimens, and the number of distinct times collections including each slide have been distributed. Ultimately, these metrics could enable moving beyond simple rules-based de-identification to a more risk-estimation approach to de-identification strategy, possibly allowing for the creation of a risk score for each individual patient. Such a score could be used to judge when additional sharing of de-identified WSI or other pathology images for individual patients should be limited.1

The current implementation covers de-identification requests for WSI files, which have already been captured in clinical settings. Whereas WSI comprises the most frequently requested data type within our department, the pipeline is natively extensible to support the diverse imaging modalities utilized in pathology. These include gross images of en-bloc specimens, single organs, and specific cross sections used for specimen orientation. Other routinely captured media include radiographs for identification of fiducial markers and biopsy clips, electron microscopy, and immunofluorescence imaging. 3D scanning has also seen some adoption in the gross room, to create 3D block diagrams for complex specimens such as total laryngectomies.33 By extending the pipeline's modular architecture to remove PHI burned into these non-WSI formats, these images could be shared as standalone assets or in conjunction with WSI.34, 35

Our current approach replaces the macro-image with an overview image of the full-resolution WSI in de-identified archives, and replaces the label image with a gray placeholder, which is sufficient for slides with well-placed labels. This approach is limited when labels are irregularly affixed or when multiple institutional labels are present, as seen with external consultation slides. In these circumstances, components of the label can be introduced into the WSI tiles, and thus allow PHI to persist in slides de-identified in this manner. We are currently implementing an ML-based approach for automatically identifying these scenarios and redacting information from the full-resolution WSI to ensure proper de-identification.34, 35 This extension will also enable automated removal of hand-written annotations within the pixel data, which could contain PHI. Whereas the development of such ML-based scrubbing tools is a focus of our future work, the rigorous validation required to ensure a zero-tolerance PHI policy for pixel-level redaction is beyond the scope of this implementation. Consequently, our current pipeline maintains a human-in-the-loop review, ensuring the de-identification engine remains a reliable bridge for the clinical-to-academic data lifecycle.

This implementation highlights the fractured, proprietary state of formats and software in the field of digital pathology and the handling of WSI files. Institutions such as ours are procuring multiple scanner models to ensure slide-scanning contingencies. Currently, this means dealing with multiple image formats that are not directly interoperable, hence our pipeline's initial conversion step to DICOM. Before the implementation of this pipeline, we utilized vendor-specific de-identification software (when available), which was manual, laborious, and required dedicated hardware. While our pipeline—via wsidicomizer—provides functional conversion for many formats, several require lossy recompression; this limitation will persist until scanner vendors provide native, lossless conversion solutions. The early state of digital pathology adoption places us in a position to learn from the decades of technology development in radiology and to avoid practices that will make sharing of WSI, including in cases requiring de-identification, difficult or impossible.

Beyond hindering common sharing scenarios, such as second-opinions, teaching sets, and research studies, these proprietary barriers could severely hamper the development of technologies requiring large data sets, such as ML. Large academic institutions generate >1 million new slides a year.18 If only 1% of slides were designated for a ML training set, which subsequently required de-identification, a manual, case-by-case system could take months at 30 WSI/day. Re-scanning could be faster but would occupy even the largest volume scanner for over a week of uninterrupted 24-h cycles and require approximately 20 TB of duplicated storage (using our overall average WSI file size of 1.9 GB). At 118 WSI/h, our pipeline could convert this many WSI in ∼4 days. The only viable, cost-effective solution is to utilize the scans, which are already acquired for clinical use and to leverage an informatics-based approach to de-identify them.

Handling large de-identification requests is a prerequisite for building the massive datasets required for modern ML development. For context, whereas large public WSI repositories currently offer approximately 10,000 slides, state-of-the-art ML tools have been developed using datasets exceeding 300,000 images.36, 37 Scaling to such volumes requires a shift away from manual, case-by-case selection. Whereas our current implementation relies on user-driven searches by case number, the system is architected for modularity. Decoupling the front-end interface from the de-identification engine ensures the pipeline remains agnostic to how files are sourced. Consequently, the request system can be extended to support batch-based requests (e.g., CSV) or sophisticated metadata queries (e.g., tissue type, diagnosis), substantially reducing manual intervention for high-volume research cohorts.

Such a scale also requires robust data transfer and governance. Massive throughput would necessitate a transition toward API-based download orchestration or compute resources directly attached to cloud storage. Furthermore, a zero-tolerance PHI policy at this scale would require replacing human-in-the-loop review with contractual use limitations and ML-based automated scrubbing tools.31 Finally, whereas our pipeline is currently housed on a high-performance cluster with a 400 Gbps storage network, care must be taken in massively scaled projects not to overwhelm clinical networks when requesting patient metadata, though this has not been a limitation in our current implementation.

We have demonstrated a scalable, vendor-neutral pipeline for WSI file de-identification that achieves predictable linear performance and high reliability across diverse formats and file sizes. The FHIR-integrated architecture facilitates institutional governance while supporting large-scale dataset construction essential for modern ML development. This informatics-based approach represents a practical, cost-effective solution for institutions seeking to overcome the limitations of manual de-identification and re-scanning workflows.
