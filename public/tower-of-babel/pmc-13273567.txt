Protocol for synthesis and mechanical characterization of polyacrylamide hydrogels of varying stiffness for cell culture applications
Authors: Charlotte Cresens, Samet Aytekin, Behrad Shaghaghi, Lotte Gerrits, Ana Montero-Calle, Rodrigo Barderas, Paul Kouwer, Susana Rocha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe impact of mechanical cues on cell behavior is increasingly being recognized, rendering hydrogel platforms that mimic the extracellular matrix indispensable in in vitro cell biology research. Here, we present a protocol for the synthesis and rheological characterization of polyacrylamide (PAAm) hydrogels with varying stiffnesses, produced as large, circular, unattached gels customizable in shape and size. We outline techniques for their use in cell culture and downstream applications involving secretome or cell analysis and protein visualization by fluorescence microscopy.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Cresens et al.1

Before you begin

Since their implementation in 1997, polyacrylamide (PAAm, also referred to as PAA) hydrogels have been widely used as a versatile, inexpensive, and convenient platform for a wide variety of cell culture applications.2 They consist of acrylamide (AAm) monomer and N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide (MBAA) crosslinker, chemically crosslinked through a radical polymerization reaction catalyzed by ammonium persulfate (APS) and N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED), where APS provides free radicals.3 The stiffnesses of these synthetic gels lie within the physiological range and can be precisely controlled by varying concentrations of AAm monomer and MBAA crosslinker, making them especially suitable for mechanobiology studies where the influence of extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness on cell behavior can be investigated.3,4 The stiffness of PAAm gels can be tuned across a broad range (∼0.1 to 50 kPa), covering physiological tissue mechanics and pathological stiffening such as fibrosis and tumor desmoplasia.4 Softer substrates are typically used to mimic compliant tissues such as brain or adipose tissue, whereas stiffer substrates recapitulate environments such as muscle or fibrotic tissue. This tunability allows researchers to model both physiological and pathophysiological mechanics and to investigate how matrix rigidity influences key cellular processes spreading, contractility, migration, and differentiation.5

PAAm gels have been applied extensively to evaluate the effect of substrate stiffness on cell morphology, proliferation, adhesion and migration, molecular pathways, cell communication, differentiation, and more.1,2,3,4,5,6 A major advantage of PAAm gels is that they are biochemically inert and must be functionalized with biological adhesion ligands, allowing for full control over the presented adhesion ligands, independently of the mechanical properties of the gel.3,7,8 In addition, PAAm gels are optically transparent at sufficiently low crosslinker concentrations (MBAA ≤ 0.5%), enabling their use in microscopy applications.7

Despite their numerous advantages, there are still several challenges and limitations associated with the use of PAAm hydrogels. Due to the small pore size and lack of cellular adhesion sites, these hydrogels cannot be applied for 3D cell cultures where the cells are embedded inside the hydrogel. Studies where cells are seeded on top of a functionalized 2D PAAm hydrogel, or even where cells are sandwiched between two PAAm hydrogel layers, have been reported.9 Notably, the reaction must be performed in between two surfaces to prevent oxygen from interfering with the radical polymerization. This also renders a flat surface that enables uniform cell attachment and growth.5 While immobilization of the hydrogel by covalent attachment to a surface is a common strategy, this is not a requirement, as demonstrated by Shi & Janmey who produced unattached PAAm gels by polymerization between two polystyrene surfaces.10

Innovation

Despite – or because of – the widespread use of PAAm gels, large variations exist between stiffnesses reported for the same PAAm gel composition. On the one hand, differences might be introduced during the manufacturing when certain critical parameters are disparate, namely gelation temperature, reagent or PAAm gel storage time and temperature, mixing technique and so on.4,5,11 On the other hand, differences might be due to the highly variable measurement techniques used to characterize PAAm stiffness, ranging from nano-to microscale.4 The measured mechanical properties depend strongly on the testing modality, and stiffness values obtained by bulk rheology, compression testing, or micro/nanoindentation methods are therefore not directly interchangeable. We therefore propose here a standardized protocol to detail the controlled conditions for gel production, microscale rheology characterization, and functionalization for 2D cell culture. By integrating these steps into a single workflow, this protocol reduces variability arising from both preparation and measurement, and enables consistent comparison of PAAm gel properties. In addition, we include downstream applications for cells cultured on PAAm gels, including harvesting of cells or medium for downstream biochemical analyses, and visualization of cell morphology or protein organization by fluorescence microscopy.

Institutional permissions

Due to the acute toxicity, flammability, risk to eyes and skin, and long-term health hazards of several reagents used during PAAm gel production, certain solutions and the PAAm gels should be prepared inside a Chemical Level 2 laboratory’s fume hood. Cell lines are handled exclusively in a Biosafety Level 2 laboratory, and their use was approved by the ethical committee of UZ Leuven (reference number: S64861). Experiments are only performed after approval of a risk assessment and notification to the supervisor and HSE Department. Before adopting this protocol, make sure to comply with your institutional and national health, safety, and ethics regulations.

Synthesis of large, unattached PAAm hydrogels

Timing: 1 h per gel + 16 h incubation

Timing: 1 h per gel + 16 h incubation

In this step, a large circular unattached PAAm hydrogel with a flat surface is synthesized (Figure 1 for schematic protocol; Figure 2 for results). To this end, AAm monomer and MBAA crosslinker are mixed in varying concentrations, which determine the stiffness of the resulting PAAm gel. Radical polymerization is initiated by the addition of APS and TEMED, and the gel is cast between the lid and base dish of a circular 92 mm polystyrene Petri dish. These steps are performed on ice with precooled reagents to allow sufficient handling time. The polymerization itself is carried out at 23 °C, as is customary in the field. Expected outcomes

Production of large, unattached PAAm hydrogels

This major step produces customized unattached PAAm hydrogels, swollen for 16 h. We typically create seven 20 mm circular gels for rheological characterization (Figure 2D) or one 75 mm circular gel for large-batch cell culture applications (Figure 2C), both approximately 1.2–1.5 mm thick. Note that gels in the pictures are shown before swelling, and that restamping is necessary after swelling to obtain the final dimensions.

Rheological characterization

Major steps of rheology measurements and rheology data analysis assess the mechanical properties of PAAm hydrogels through rheology. Table 1 provides a summary of the composition and stiffness of different gels. Additional measurement details are listed in Table S1. Outcomes of the obtained key parameters, including hydrogel stiffness, are discussed below.

Since gels are already polymerized prior to the rheology measurement and their mechanical properties should not change over short timescales, time sweep results should appear as horizontal lines (Figure 5A). We find that the storage modulus (G′) is approximately an order of magnitude higher compared to the loss modulus (G″) for the gel compositions tested here.

When evaluating hydrogel stiffness at 1% strain, the PAAm library exhibits a broad stiffness range from 0.90 to 56.13 kPa, recapitulating both physiologically relevant conditions and diseases characterized by altered matrix stiffness.

In agreement with previous reports, we find that increasing the concentration of either AAm monomer or MBAA crosslinker leads to increased stiffness.11,16 For example, 10% AAm and 0.2% MBAA yield a stiffness of 10.07 ± 0.16 kPa (Table 1, composition 8). Higher concentrations, such as 12% AAm or 0.4% MBAA, result in increased stiffness (12.08 ± 1.95 kPa and 14.46 ± 0.06 kPa, respectively, Table 1, compositions 9 and 12). As expected, increasing both AAm and MBAA concentrations to 12% and 0.4% respectively, generates an even stiffer gel of 23.56 ± 3.58 kPa (Table 1, composition 13). However, note that extreme concentrations of either component will result in drastically altered stiffnesses, reflecting a complex relationship that has also been described in the literature.4 In our experiments, for example, when maintaining MBAA concentration at 0.4% but increasing AAm concentrations from 10% to 12% and 18%, stiffness increases drastically from 14.46 ± 0.06 kPa to 23.56 ± 3.58 kPa and 36.70 ± 4.30 kPa respectively (Table 1, compositions 9, 13, and 14). Similarly, lowering MBAA concentrations to 0.06% significantly reduces stiffness to 0.90 ± 0.11 kPa compared to 3.73 ± 0.31 kPa for a gel with comparable AAm and 0.23% MBAA concentration (Table 1, compositions 4 and 5).

Technical replicates obtained by stamping different areas from the same large gel demonstrate that our rheological measurements are robust and repeatable. The average error between technical replicates was 12%, while the maximum error of all compositions was found to be 18.45% (Table S1, for 4.49% AAm and 0.10% MBAA).

Frequency sweeps are expected to appear approximately as horizontal lines, with slightly increasing storage moduli (G′) as the angular frequency increases (Figure 5B). Conversely, loss moduli (G″) decrease slightly with increasing angular frequency. These results are in line with previous reports.10

Amplitude sweeps reveal that storage moduli (G′) are nearly independent of the applied oscillation strain for softer PAAm gels (here ≤ 20 kPa) (Figure 5C). In contrast, stiffer hydrogels (here > 20 kPa) experience a decline in storage moduli when oscillation strains are increased, a trend that is more prominent for increasingly stiff gels. The effect of oscillation strain on loss moduli (G″) also depends on hydrogel stiffness; softer gels exhibit less dependency on oscillation strain, while stiffer gels show an increase in loss modulus with higher oscillation strains. This behavior can be attributed to slipping of the geometry on the surface of stiffer gels when oscillation strains are high, emphasizing the importance of performing stiffness measurements at strains ≤ 1%. Note that these observations are very clear when the data is plotted in standard scale, and that these differences are less prominent when represented on the log scale.

Large-batch cell culture and downstream applications

This section results in adhesion and growth of KM12L4a cells on top of functionalized PAAm hydrogels of different stiffnesses (Figure 6). Note that KM12L4a cells are mechanosensitive, since their morphology and proliferation are influenced by the stiffness of the substrate.

Following growth, cells can be harvested for downstream applications such as Western blots, omics analyses and in vivo experiments (Downstream application 1). When desired, cells can be starved in medium lacking FBS, to simultaneously harvest conditioned medium (secretome) and several million cells. Note that the number of harvested cells depends on the initial seeding concentration and growth time, but possibly also on the stiffness of the gel since it affects cell proliferation.

As an alternative to cell harvesting, proteins of interest can be visualized by immunolabeling and fluorescence microscopy (Downstream application 2). Zoom and Z-stack acquisitions allow detailed reconstruction of subcellular protein expression and localization in 3D (Figure 7).

Limitations

As mentioned earlier, large variations exist in stiffnesses reported for the same PAAm gel composition. The stiffnesses reported here are valid only when the protocol is meticulously followed, considering critical parameters like gelation temperature, the storage time of reagents and PAAm gels, and the gelation and rheology measurement temperature. To eliminate user-induced variations or when using reagents from different sources or storage conditions, we recommend independently characterizing the mechanical properties of the prepared gels.

Before comparing obtained results to those reported in the literature, we recommend carefully evaluating the gel polymerization conditions, as well as the measurement technique. Especially for measurement techniques at different scales, we observed considerable differences between our own results and those reported in the literature.

Despite our best efforts to match conditions between rheology measurements and cell culture, we acknowledge that differences between the stiffness measured by rheology and the actual stiffness sensed by the cells may still arise.

Since DPBS has a slightly higher ionic strength than DMEM, the stiffness of gels in cell culture medium will be slightly higher compared than the rheology values. Note that addition of FBS to DMEM, to create the standard cell culture medium, will not further change the stiffness as no salts are added.11

In addition, cells will deposit their own extracellular matrix that will change not only the local stiffness but also extracellular adhesion ligands that can interact with the cellular adhesion molecules, especially when cells are cultured on a PAAm gel for longer times. However, we argue that for the KM12L4a cell line used in this protocol, we continued to observe changes in cell morphology in cells seeded on top of functionalized PAAm substrates of different stiffnesses during 72 h of culture.

Problem 1: Gel fracture immediately after polymerization

Gel ruptures or breaks into granules upon opening of the polymerization chamber or attempting to retrieve the gel from the polymerization chamber (see step 10).

Potential solution

Prolonged incubation times during polymerization may cause the gel to dehydrate and make it prone to fracture. Especially stiffer gels (>20 kPa) may polymerize swiftly. Monitor closely the completion of the polymerization via the remaining mixture in the conical tube and add dH2O to the edge of the polymerization chamber as soon as polymerization is complete to avoid dehydration.

Problem 5: Extending the protocol to other cell types

This protocol describes steps for culturing metastatic KM12L4a colorectal cancer cells on top of collagen type I functionalized PAAm gels (Major option B). We have also used this protocol for culturing other colorectal cancer cell lines, and anticipate no issues in extending it to other adherent mammalian cell lines.

Potential solution

If a specific cell line does not adhere efficiently, the type and concentration of adhesion-promoting proteins may need to be optimized (e.g., collagen I ∼50–100 μg/mL, fibronectin ∼5–20 μg/mL, laminin ∼10–50 μg/mL), provided they contain primary amine groups compatible with sulfo-SANPAH coupling. Cell seeding density should be adjusted to achieve approximately 60–80% surface coverage after initial attachment. Following seeding, cells should be allowed sufficient time (typically 2–6 h) to establish stable adhesion before any medium change or mechanical disturbance, as premature handling may lead to detachment. Substrate stiffness may need to be tuned depending on the cell type, as softer substrates (<5 kPa) can limit adhesion for some cells, whereas stiffer substrates (>10 kPa) generally promote attachment and cytoskeletal organization.17 In addition, culture medium composition should be adapted according to the requirements of the specific cell line, including appropriate serum content and supplements, as deviations may affect attachment efficiency and proliferation.

Problem 6: Immunolabeled gel floats inside imaging chamber

The immunolabeled gel moves or floats around in the 6-well glass-bottom plate (see steps 75 and 84).

Potential solution

Since the gel is inverted to allow imaging using high NA objectives, and cells are now facing the glass-bottom surface with the gel on top, do not attempt to move or press down the gel with the round-tipped tweezers to prevent disruption of the sample. Instead, add an additional 1 or 2 mL DPBS 1x until it surrounds the gel. Then, again remove a portion to prevent sample floating.

Alternatively, when experimentally feasible, floating can be mitigated by using glass-supported PAAm gels of lower thickness, which remain attached to the surface during imaging. This approach is compatible with established methods for preparing PAAm gels on methacrylated glass coverslips.6
