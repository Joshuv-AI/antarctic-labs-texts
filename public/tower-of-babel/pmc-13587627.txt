Letter to the Editor regarding “Cysteine metabolism dysregulation promotes ferroptosis in osteoarthritis: Insights from multi-species bioinformatics and experimental validation”
Authors: Yiyun Ni, Bin Liu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the original article by Zheng et al. [1], “Cysteine metabolism dysregulation promotes ferroptosis in osteoarthritis: Insights from multi-species bioinformatics and experimental validation”. By integrating cross-species transcriptomics, human cartilage multi-omics, and in vitro and in vivo experiments, the authors propose that dysregulated cysteine/glutathione metabolism drives osteoarthritis (OA) progression through ferroptosis and argue for the therapeutic potential of L-cysteine supplementation. We appreciate the authors’ exploratory contribution at the intersection of metabolism and ferroptosis. However, the single-sex composition of the human cartilage multi-omics validation cohort limits the generalizability of the central claim that this axis is a universal driver of OA.

According to Supplementary Table S2, all three OA patients included in the human cartilage proteomic and metabolomic analyses were female, aged 77, 77, and 80 years, with Kellgren–Lawrence grades III–IV. Thus the most direct human evidence for the “cysteine metabolism – ferroptosis axis” (Fig. 3) comes entirely from elderly postmenopausal women. First, the core regulatory molecules of ferroptosis are under direct estrogen control, and this cohort was in a postmenopausal state characterized by very low estrogen levels. Lv et al. showed that 17β-estradiol suppresses endothelial ferroptosis via NRF2/GPX4 [2]; Cao et al. demonstrated that estrogen receptor α transcriptionally upregulates the System Xc− subunits SLC7A11 and SLC3A2, thereby suppressing ferroptosis [3]; Tian et al. reported that 17β-estradiol deficiency following ovariectomy induces hippocampal ferroptosis and iron accumulation by downregulating DHODH [4]. Thus, both GPX4 and SLC7A11 — precisely the two key nodes of the axis advocated by the authors — are under estrogenic regulation. When the validation cohort consists exclusively of postmenopausal women, the observed downregulation of GPX4/SLC7A11, iron accumulation, and lipid peroxidation could be consistent with OA itself, but could equally be explained by “heightened ferroptotic susceptibility following estrogen withdrawal” — a distinction the study design cannot make.

Second, OA chondrocytes exhibit clear sexual dimorphism. Jain et al. found that female OA chondrocytes have higher PGC1α, fatty-acid oxidation, and oxidative metabolism, whereas male cells show greater glycolysis and COL2A1 synthesis, with opposite SOX9/PGC1α control of COL2A1 [5]. Earlier, Kinney et al. demonstrated that only female human articular chondrocytes exhibit a rapid membrane-associated response to 17β-estradiol [6]. Wang et al. recently confirmed extensive sex-biased gene expression in cartilage, with female-high genes particularly dysregulated in OA [7]. These findings consistently indicate systematic differences in the metabolic phenotypes and hormonal responsiveness of OA chondrocytes between men and women.

Third, OA itself differs by sex in its epidemiology and pathophysiology. Women have higher prevalence and faster progression and are metabolically/inflammatory driven, whereas men are more mechanically driven [7]. An all-female “cysteine–ferroptosis” signature may therefore reflect female-specific, metabolism-dominant OA rather than a universal mechanism.

Although the public datasets used in the cross-species transcriptomics (GSE112641, GSE118559, GSE114007) may include both sexes, transcript-level findings cannot substitute for direct multi-omics validation from human cartilage. In sum, the authors’ central conclusion is currently supported directly only by cartilage data from older female OA patients.

We suggest that the authors: (1) explicitly state in the Limitations section that the human proteomic/metabolomic validation is based on three older female patients and that the conclusions should be framed as exploratory findings of an “older female OA subtype”; (2) re-analyze publicly available OA cartilage transcriptomic data stratified by sex to verify whether alterations in cysteine/ferroptosis-related genes are equally pronounced in male samples; (3) include matched male samples, or use published multi-sex proteomic datasets, in subsequent work to confirm the sex-independence of this axis.

In conclusion, Zheng et al. have provided a compelling and original contribution linking cysteine metabolism to ferroptosis in OA; the considerations raised above are intended as constructive refinements that, if addressed, will clarify the translational scope of this axis across sexes and ultimately strengthen its clinical relevance.
