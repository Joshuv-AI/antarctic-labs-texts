ConformalNL2LTL: Translating Natural Language Instructions into Temporal Logic Formulas with Conformal Correctness Guarantees
Jun Wang, David Smith Sundarsingh, Jyotirmoy V. Deshmukh, Yiannis Kantaros

Abstract
Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) has become a prevalent specification language for robotic tasks. To mitigate the significant manual effort and expertise required to define LTL-encoded tasks, several methods have been proposed for translating Natural Language (NL) instructions into LTL formulas, which, however, lack correctness guarantees. To address this, we introduce a new NL-to-LTL translation method, called ConformalNL2LTL, that can achieve user-defined translation success rates over unseen NL commands. Our method constructs LTL formulas iteratively by addressing a sequence of open-vocabulary Question-Answering (QA) problems with LLMs. To enable uncertainty-aware translation, we leverage conformal prediction (CP), a distribution-free uncertainty quantification tool for black-box models. CP enables our method to assess the uncertainty in LLM-generated answers, allowing it to proceed with translation when sufficiently confident and request help otherwise. We provide both theoretical and empirical results demonstrating that ConformalNL2LTL achieves user-specified translation accuracy while minimizing help rates. 1 1 1 Additional materials, including videos and code, are available on the project webpage at: conformalnl2ltl.github.io

Despite the remarkable performance of LTL-based planners, their key limitation is that mission specification requires significant manual effort and expertise. To address this, natural language (NL) has emerged as an alternative for mission specification. To address this issue, we propose a new translation method, called ConformalNL2LTL, for Conformal translation of NL commands to LTL formulas, that achieves user-defined translation success rates over unseen NL instructions. We consider a robot with a known skill-set (e.g., manipulation, sensing, mobility) tasked with high-level NL missions in an environment containing semantic objects/regions. The NL task requires the robot to apply its skills to specific objects/regions in a temporal/logical order. ConformalNL2LTL converts NL commands to LTL formulas defined over atomic propositions (i.e., Boolean variables) that are grounded in the robot’s capabilities and the environment.

Our approach relies on pre-trained LLMs as the backbone translator, augmented by Conformal Prediction (CP)
[
26
,
27
]
, an uncertainty quantification method, which plays a critical role to achieve user-specified translation success rates. The core of our approach is to construct the LTL formula iteratively by framing the translation problem as a sequence of interdependent question answering (QA) tasks. Each ’question’ incorporates the NL task and the formula constructed so far, prompting the LLM to provide either a temporal/logical operator or an atomic proposition to extend the formula. The answers to these QA tasks collectively determine the final LTL specification. A straightforward way to solve each QA task is to query the LLM directly for an operator or proposition. However, this does not account for the LLM’s confidence in its predictions. To enable confidence-aware construction of the LTL formula, inspired by
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
, we sample multiple responses for each ‘question’ and use their frequency as a coarse-grained measure of uncertainty. Instead of simply selecting the response with the highest confidence score (as e.g., in
[
31
,
29
]
), we employ CP to calibrate the LLM’s uncertainty. This is crucial, as LLMs often generate incorrect outputs with high confidence
[
32
]
, which can lead to translation errors.
CP generates a prediction set that, with high confidence, contains the correct response (either an operator or atomic proposition) to each QA task. This allows the algorithm to detect when the LLM is uncertain about its predictions.
Inspired by
[
32
]

We provide extensive numerical experiments demonstrating that the proposed method can achieve desired translation success rates (e.g.,

Problem Statement

Robot & Environment Modeling:
Consider a robot equipped with a
known
set of skills/actions, denoted as
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{A}\}
(e.g., ‘take a picture’, ‘grab’, or ‘go to’), and operates in an environment
\Omega=\{l_{1},\ldots,l_{M}\}
that contains
M>0
semantic objects and regions (called, hereafter, landmarks)
l_{j}
. Each landmark

is determined by its position and its semantic label (e.g., ‘box’, ‘car’, ‘kitchen’). The robot’s skills can be applied to
l_{j}
(e.g., ‘go to
l_{j}
’ or ‘grab
l_{j}
’).

Natural Language Tasks:
The robot is assigned a task
\xi
, expressed in natural language (NL), which may comprise multiple sub-tasks. The task
\xi
is grounded in the robot skill set
{\mathcal{A}}
and a subset of the environment
\omega\subseteq\Omega
, meaning it can be accomplished by executing a sequence of actions selected from
{\mathcal{A}}
on some of the landmarks within
\Omega
; see Ex.
II.2
. To formalize this, we consider a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
over task scenarios
\sigma_{i}=\{\xi_{i},{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\}
, where
\xi_{i}
is defined over an unknown environment
\Omega_{i}
; see also Remark
II.1
. The subscript

is used to emphasize that the NL-encoded task and the robot skills can vary across scenarios. When it is clear from the context, we drop the dependence on

is unknown but we assume that we can sample i.i.d. scenarios from it.

Linear Temporal Logic:
LTL is a formal language that comprises a set of atomic propositions (AP)
\pi
(i.e., Boolean variables), collected in a set denoted by
\mathcal{AP}
, Boolean operators, (i.e., conjunction
\wedge
, and negation
\neg
), and temporal operators, such as
always

\square
,
eventually

\lozenge
, and until
\mathcal{U}
.
We focus on LTL-encoded robot tasks defined over APs grounded in the robot’s skill set
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
and environment
\Omega_{i}
. Specifically, we consider APs that are true when a skill
a_{i}
(e.g., ‘pick up’) is applied to a landmark
\ell_{j}
(e.g., ‘package’); see also Rem.
II.1
. These LTL formulas are satisfied by robot plans
\tau
, which are sequences of robot states and corresponding actions. A formal presentation of the syntax and semantics of LTL, as well as robot plans satisfying LTL tasks can be found in
[
1
,
15
]
; designing plans
\tau
is out of the scope of this work.

Problem Statement:

Our goal is to design a
probabilistically correct
translation algorithm that can convert NL-encoded task scenarios
\sigma_{i}
into a semantically equivalent LTL-encoded task
\phi_{i}
, defined above, in at least
(1-\alpha)\cdot 100
% of scenarios drawn from
{\mathcal{D}}
, for a user-specified
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Each scenario
\sigma_{i}
contains an NL task
\xi_{i}
and the action space of the robot
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
. An LTL formula
\phi_{i}
is considered semantically equivalent to the NL task
\xi_{i}
if any plan
\tau
satisfying
\phi_{i}
also satisfies
\xi_{i}
, and vice versa. In cases where multiple LTL formulas are semantically equivalent to
\xi_{i}
, our goal is to compute one such formula.
The problem that the paper addresses can be summarized as follows:

Problem 1
.

Design a translation algorithm that, given an NL task scenario
\sigma=\{\xi,{\mathcal{A}}\}
sampled from an unknown distribution
\mathcal{D}
, generates an LTL formula
\phi
such that
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma\sim{\mathcal{D}}}(\phi\equiv\xi)\geq 1-\alpha
, for a user-specified threshold
1-\alpha\in(0,1)
(where
\equiv
stands for semantic equivalence).

Remark II.1

(Distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
)
.

Note that the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
does not generate the environment
\Omega
. Instead, the randomness of
\Omega
is implicitly accounted for when sampling the task
\xi
. This means that the environment may vary across NL tasks
\xi_{i}
sampled from
{\mathcal{D}}
. Similarly,
{\mathcal{D}}
does not generate a predefined set of APs for constructing
\phi
.
Instead, the translation algorithm is tasked with inferring the set

from
\xi

. In practice, this means that users only need to specify the skill set

of the platform and the NL instructions

Example II.2

(NL-to-LTL)
.

Consider an example of a mobile manipulator with an action space
\mathcal{A}=

{
move to, pick up, put down
}
, and an NL task

‘Pick up the red box and place it in the storage area’.
Our proposed algorithm will infer that the corresponding LTL formula should be defined over the following three APs: (i)

is true if the ‘red box’ was ‘picked up’ by the robot;

This will result in the following LTL formula

which requires to eventually satisfy

,

and

III-A

Incremental Translation using LLMs

Consider a translation scenario
\sigma=\{\xi,{\mathcal{A}}\}
drawn from
{\mathcal{D}}
. A key idea of ConformalNL2LTL is to frame the translation process as a sequence of interdependent question-answering (QA) problems, each solved by a pre-trained LLM. The responses to these questions collectively form the LTL formula.
Specifically, at each iteration/step
k
, a QA problem is formulated.
The
question
incorporates information about the NL task, the set
{\mathcal{A}}
of robot skills, and the responses from previous iterations (i.e., the partially constructed LTL formula). This information, denoted by
\ell(k)
, is described textually. We then prompt the LLM using
\ell(k)
to generate an
answer

s(k)
that is either a temporal/logical operator or an AP.
This process continues until the algorithm outputs an ending operator, denoted by ‘
/
’, at step
H
, indicating that the LTL formula
\phi=s(1)s(2)\ldots s(H)
has been fully constructed and is semantically equivalent to
\xi
. The proposed algorithm is summarized in Alg.
1
. In what follows, we first describe the construction of the prompt
\ell(k)
followed by how it is used to generate a response
s(k)
, while accounting for uncertainties/imperfections through CP.

The prompt

Task description
of an NL task
\xi
that needs to be converted into an LTL formula; (d)
Current status
describing the formula
\phi(k-1)
that has been built up to step
k-1
and the current step indicator
k
.

Online Prompt Construction:
Given a scenario
\sigma=\{\xi,\mathcal{A}\}
, the initial prompt
\ell(1)
is manually constructed with the initial formula (denoted as
\phi(0)
) in part (d) being empty [line

Generating Confidence-aware Responses:

Next, we discuss how to obtain a response
s(k)
corresponding to
\ell(k)
. A simple approach would be to query the LLM once and record its response. However, this is uncertainty-agnostic, as it does not account for the model’s confidence in its predictions. To address this, inspired by
[
29
,
28
]
, we adopt a sampling-based approach, querying the model multiple times and using the frequency of each unique response as a coarse-grained measure of uncertainty.

Specifically, we query the model
m
times with the same prompt
\ell(k)
, and construct the set
\hat{\mathcal{S}}(k)=\{\hat{s}^{(i)}(k)\}_{i=1}^{m}
, where
\hat{s}^{(i)}(k)
is the
i
-th response of the LLM. To ensure adherence to the constraints specified in part (a) of the prompt
\ell(k)
(see Fig.
2
), out of all the
m
responses, we filter out any responses that: (i) include an invalid operator that is not part of the predefined set; (ii) APs that violate the rules set up in (a); and (iii) contains multiple operators or APs. This yields a set
{\mathcal{S}}(k)\subseteq\hat{\mathcal{S}}(k)
, such that
{\mathcal{S}}(k)=\{s^{(i)}(k)\}_{i=1}^{m_{k}}
, where
m_{k}
denotes the number of valid responses. We compute the frequency of each distinct response
s\in{\mathcal{S}}(k)
as follows:

\displaystyle F(s|\ell(k),m_{k},\mathcal{S}(k))=\frac{1}{m_{k}}\sum_{s^{(i)}(k)\in{\mathcal{S}}(k)}\mathds{1}(s^{(i)}(k)=s).

(1)

Hereafter, for simplicity, when it is clear from the context, we abbreviate
F(s|\ell(k),m_{k},\mathcal{S}(k))
as
F(s)
. Observe that every response in
{\mathcal{S}}(k)
has a non-negative frequency. For any other response
s
that is not among the
m
generated responses (and thus does not belong to

{\mathcal{S}}(k)
),
we set
F(s)=0
. Note that
F(s)=0
for any response when all
m
responses are invalid, i.e.
{\mathcal{S}}(k)=\emptyset
. We use the frequency
F(s)
as the proxy for the LLM confidence on each response
s\in\mathcal{S}(k)
based on self-consistency theory, which suggests that higher response frequency correlates with greater model confidence
[
44
]
.
This enables our method to work seamlessly with LLM that do not allow users to have logit-access (e.g., GPT 4)
[
29
]
. We note that more fine-grained measures of uncertainty in LLMs can also be employed, as explored in
[
28
]
.

It is possible for two textually different responses,
s^{(i)}(k)
and
s^{(j)}(k)
, to convey the same semantic meaning. This issue arises particularly in responses involving APs, which as discussed in Section
II
, capture the application of a robot action on a landmark, where multiple synonymous terms (e.g., ‘vehicle’ and ‘car’) may be used for the same landmark. To address this, inspired by
[
29
]
, we refine the response frequencies by grouping semantically similar responses and treating them as a single entity; this essentially results in summing their frequencies.

Formally, consider any two responses
s^{(i)}(k),s^{(j)}(k)\in{\mathcal{S}}(k)
that are both APs. To determine their semantic similarity, we first convert them into vector representations
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
, respectively, using pre-trained LLM embeddings. We then compute their cosine similarity as follows:

\displaystyle\cos(s^{(i)}(k),s^{(j)}(k))=\frac{v_{i}\cdot v_{j}}{\lVert v_{i}\rVert\lVert v_{j}\rVert}.

(2)

If the cosine similarity exceeds a predefined threshold, we consider the responses semantically equivalent. In such cases, we retain the more frequent response-i.e., if
F(s^{(i)}(k))\geq F(s^{(j)}(k))
, we replace
s^{(j)}(k)
with
s^{(i)}(k)
in the set
\mathcal{S}(k)
. This process is repeated for all distinct pairs
s^{(i)}(k),s^{(j)}(k)\in{\mathcal{S}}(k)
, that are both APs, to ensure that every remaining response in the set is semantically distinct.
We define
\bar{\mathcal{S}}(k)\subseteq\mathcal{S}(k)
as the set of unique responses in
\mathcal{S}(k)
.
For each response
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bar{s}}\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}(k)
, we compute its score using the frequency
F(\bar{s}|\ell(k),m_{k},{\mathcal{S}}(k))
defined as in (
1
). Hereafter, for brevity, we denote the above response extraction process with frequency scores as
[\bar{\mathcal{S}},F]=\texttt{GetResp}(\ell(k),m)
.

Using these scores, which model the LLM’s confidence, a straightforward approach to selecting the response
s(k)
for a given prompt
\ell(k)
is to choose the response
\bar{s}\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}(k)
with the highest confidence score, i.e.,
s(k)=\arg\max_{\bar{s}\in\bar{\mathcal{S}}(k)}F(\bar{s}|\ell(k),m_{k},\mathcal{S}(k))
. However, these scores do not represent the calibrated confidence
[
32
]
. Thus, a much-preferred approach would be to generate a set of responses (called, hereafter,
prediction set
), denoted by
{\mathcal{C}}(\ell(k))
that contains the ground truth response with a user-specified confidence [line
5
, Alg.
1
]. Here, we assume the prediction sets are provided. We defer their construction to Section
III-B
, but we emphasize that they are critical to achieving the desired
1-\alpha
translation accuracy.

Given
{\mathcal{C}}(\ell(k))
, we select response
s(k)
as follows. If
{\mathcal{C}}(\ell(k))
is a singleton (i.e.,
|{\mathcal{C}}(\ell(k))|=1
), then we select the response i.e., a temporal/logical operator or an AP, included in
{\mathcal{C}}(\ell(k))
as it contributes to the translation process with high confidence [line
9
, Alg.
1
]. Otherwise, our algorithm asks for help from a human operator to select a response
s(k)
from the prediction set
{\mathcal{C}}(\ell(k))
given the prompt
\ell(k)
[lines
6
-
7
, Alg.
1
]; see also Remark
III.3
. If the prediction set
\mathcal{C}(\ell(k))
does not contain any correct responses (e.g., if none of the
m_{k}
generated responses were correct), or if
{\mathcal{C}}(\ell(k))=\emptyset
(none of the
m_{k}
generated responses are valid, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}(k)=\emptyset
), the user can halt the translation process, marking it as a ‘failure’ of our translation algorithm (not shown in Alg.
1
); see Theorem
III.2
.

Once
s(k)
is selected, we update the formula so far as
\phi(k)=\phi(k-1)+s(k)
, where, with slight abuse of notation, summation denotes concatenation of texts [line
10
, Alg.
1
]. The prompt
\ell(k+1)
is constructed by
replacing part (d) of
\ell(k)
with
\phi(k)
and updating the iteration index to
k+1
.

[line
4
, Alg.
1
]; see also Fig.
2
. We repeat this procedure sequentially until, at a step
k=H
, the LLM generates the ending operator ‘
/
’ as a response. In this way, we generate an LTL formula
\phi=s(1)\dots s(k)\dots s(H)

III-B

Constructing Local Prediction Sets using CP

In this section, we discuss how the prediction sets
\mathcal{C}(\ell(k))
, introduced in Section
III-A
, are constructed using conformal prediction (CP), based on a user-defined translation success rate
1-\alpha
(see Problem
1
). The application of CP to LLMs for single-step QA tasks in open vocabulary settings has been explored in
[
28
,
30
]
. We extend this approach to address
multi-step QA tasks in open-vocabulary settings
, which are essential for constructing the LTL formula.

We would like to note that we cannot apply the approach in
[
28
]
to compute prediction sets

{\mathcal{C}}(\ell(k))
at each iteration
k
, as this violates the i.i.d. assumption required to apply CP. The reason is that the prompt
\ell(k)
depends on the previous prompt
\ell(k-1)
, for all
k\in\{2,\ldots,H\}
, by construction. Inspired by
[
32
,
40
,
35
]
, we address this challenge by (i) lifting the data to sequences, and (ii) performing calibration at the sequence level using a carefully designed non-conformity score (NCS) to construct the prediction set. Next, we discuss our approach.

Constructing a Calibration Dataset:
Applying CP requires a calibration dataset constructed as follows. First, we sample
D>0
i.i.d. scenarios
\sigma_{i}
from
\mathcal{D}
and construct the sequence of
H_{i}
prompts for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
as:

\displaystyle\bar{\ell}_{i,\text{calib}}=\ell_{i,\text{calib}}(1),\ldots,\ell_{i,\text{calib}}(H_{i}).

(3)

Each prompt
\ell_{i,\text{calib}}(k)
in the sequence
\bar{\ell}_{i,\text{calib}}
has the structure that has been defined in
III-A
, and contains the ground truth sequence of responses made till step
k-1
. The ground truth LTL formula is denoted as:

\displaystyle\phi_{i,\text{calib}}=s_{i,\text{calib}}(1),\ldots,s_{i,\text{calib}}(H_{i}),

(4)

where
s_{i,\text{calib}}(k)
refers to the correct response to
\ell_{i,\text{calib}}(k)
. This correct formula is constructed as follows. In what follows, we assume that there exists a unique ground truth formula
\phi_{i,\text{calib}}
corresponding to each calibration scenario; relaxing this assumption is discussed in Remark
III.3

First, we obtain

(Translation Success Rates)

Proof.

(a) To show this result, we consider the following cases.

otherwise a distribution shift may occur as calibration sequences are constructed using correct responses.

Remark III.3

(Multiple Semantically Equivalent Formulas)
.

The above results assume that
{\mathcal{D}}
generates task scenarios with a unique corresponding LTL formula. To relax this assumption, the following changes are made during calibration and test time. (i) During calibration, among all LTL formulas that are semantically equivalent to each other and to a given NL task, we select the one constructed by picking the response with the highest frequency at each step
k
. If none of the responses generated is the ground truth, or is not valid, we type out the correct response with the frequency of the ground truth being

\phi_{\text{test}}
in Theorem

refers to the formula that is semantically equivalent to
\xi_{\text{test}}
and has been constructed exactly as in (i). (iii) When user help is required at test time, the user selects the correct response among the ones in the prediction set with the highest frequency. Correctness of (i)-(iii) can be formally shown by following the same steps as in Appendices A3-A4 of

IV-A

Setting Up Translation Scenarios for Robot Tasks

Robot & Environment:
Our experiments focus on robot delivery and transportation tasks. The robot’s action space is defined as
\mathcal{A}=
{move to, pick up, put down, take a picture} across all translation scenarios.
The environments vary in terms of objects (e.g., crates, boxes, cars, bottles, and traffic cones) as well as regions of interest (e.g., kitchens, warehouses, buildings, and parking lots) across our translation scenarios. Since multiple objects of the same semantic label (e.g., ‘box’) may exist in the environment, we assign unique identifiers (e.g., ‘box 1’, ‘box 2’) to distinguish them.

Rules for APs:
As discussed in Section
III-A
, part (a) of each prompt informs the LLM about how APs should be formulated indicating when a robot action is applied to one (if any) of the available landmarks. In our setup, we require the LLM to generate APs in the following format (see Fig.
2
): (i) ‘lmk_X’ is satisfied if the robot is near a landmark with a semantic label ‘lmk’ and unique identifier
X
; (ii) ‘p_lmk_X’ is satisfied if the robot picks up the landmark with label ‘lmk’ and unique identifier
X
; (iii) ‘pd’ is satisfied if the robot puts down a carried object (i.e., opens its gripper); and (iv) ‘photo’ that is satisfied if the robot takes a photo. For instance, the AP ‘p_box_1’ is satisfied if the robot picks up box 1. Observe that (i)-(ii) correspond to specific landmarks, whereas (iii)-(iv) do not. We note that although we provide the LLM with the general structure of APs, we do not provide as input a predefined list of APs; instead our algorithm is responsible for inferring them from the NL task.

Granularity of LLM’s confidence:

As mentioned in Sec.
III-A
, we query the model
m
times, which in turn determines the granularity of the LLM’s confidence on a particular response. For lower values of
m
, a relatively less accurate ranking of LLM responses in terms of its confidence estimated using the frequency score (
1
) is obtained. This leads to higher help rates, and so careful consideration is required for the selection of
m
. We emphasize that the value of
m
does not affect Thm.
III.2
’s correctness as CP depends on the accuracy of ranking of the responses from lowest to highest model confidence, and not on the actual values of the non-conformity score. Even if the scores fail to approximate this ranking, the marginal coverage still holds, albeit with excessively large prediction sets. In all our experiments, we select
m=10
; we also demonstrate how the help rate is affected by setting
m=5
when
1-\alpha=0.95
.

Eliminating Invalid APs:

As soon as the LLM generates a response
s^{(j)}(k)
at a step
k
of Alg.
1
(see Section
III-A
), we first examine if that response is a logical/temporal operator. If not, we examine its candidacy as an AP, i.e., if it satisfies the rules mentioned above. APs that do not satisfy these rules are filtered out as discussed in Sec.
III-A
(e.g., ‘pick_box_1’ would be eliminated). Note that APs that do not have a numerical identifier are not filtered out (e.g., ‘p_box’).

Semantic Similarity:
Let
s^{i}(k),s^{j}(k)
be two valid APs that were generated as responses at step
k
. Before checking their semantic similarity (see (
2
)), we first check if their numerical identifiers match. If not (e.g., ‘box_1’ and ‘package_2’), we set their semantic similarity to zero as these APs refer to two different landmarks. Otherwise, we proceed with the computation of the cosine similarity of their textual parts (e.g., ‘box’ and ‘package’) as in (
2
). The two APs are then merged if the cosine similarity exceeds a specified threshold. A higher threshold requires greater semantic similarity for merging to occur, influencing the granularity of the LLM’s confidence indirectly. For instance, if the threshold is set too high, multiple semantically similar responses may remain unmerged and their frequencies will not be aggregated to reflect the LLM’s overall confidence in those responses. However, as discussed earlier, the granularity of the LLM confidence does not affect correctness of Thm.
III.2
but it may affect the help rates.

V
Conclusions and Future Work

This paper introduced ConformalNL2LTL, a novel NL-to-LTL translation framework. A key contribution of our approach is its ability to achieve user-defined translation success rates. Future work will focus on (i) relaxing the i.i.d. assumption required in Theorem
III.2
; (ii) formally integrating ConformalNL2LTL with existing LTL planners; and (iii) extending this integration to multi-robot systems.
