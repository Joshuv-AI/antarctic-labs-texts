Amirhossein Javaheri, Jiaxi Ying, Daniel P. Palomar, and Farokh Marvasti

Abstract
Graph models provide efficient tools to capture the underlying structure of data defined over networks. Many real-world network topologies are subject to change over time. Learning to model the dynamic interactions between entities in such networks is known as time-varying graph learning. Current methodology for learning such models often lacks robustness to outliers in the data and fails to handle heavy-tailed distributions, a common feature in many real-world datasets (e.g., financial data). This paper addresses the problem of learning time-varying graph models capable of efficiently representing heavy-tailed data. Unlike traditional approaches, we incorporate graph structures with specific spectral properties to enhance data clustering in our model. Our proposed method, which can also deal with noise and missing values in the data, is based on a stochastic approach, where a non-negative vector auto-regressive (VAR) model captures the variations in the graph and a Student-t distribution models the signal originating from this underlying time-varying graph. We propose an iterative method to learn time-varying graph topologies within a semi-online framework where only a mini-batch of data is used to update the graph. Simulations with both synthetic and real datasets demonstrate the efficacy of our model in analyzing heavy-tailed data, particularly those found in financial markets.



I
Introduction

Graph signal processing (GSP) is an interesting research area that combines graph theory and signal processing to model and analyze signals defined on graph structures, which has numerous real-world applications in social networks
[
2
]
, image processing
[
3
]
, data mining, communications
[
4
]
, finance
[
5
]
, and more. The process of inferring the structure of a graph from data is known as graph learning
[
6
]
. There are generally two types of graphs, namely undirected and directed, with each type modeling different characteristics of data. An undirected graph models bilateral relationships or similarities in data, while a directed graph is commonly used to model unilateral causal dependencies
[
7
]
.

There are various approaches in the literature for modeling signals via graphs. Many graph learning methods consider a probabilistic model for the data, in which a graph structure represents the statistics of the data. A foundational technique in this domain is the Gaussian Markov Random Field (GMRF) model
[
8
]
, which assumes that the data follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution. In a general GMRF, the precision matrix encodes the conditional independence relationships between variables, thereby defining the structure of the underlying undirected graph. Recently, there has been growing interest in Laplacian constrained GMRFs
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, where the precision matrix is specifically constrained to be the Laplacian matrix of an undirected graph
[
12
]
.
This constraint is particularly desirable for modeling smooth signals on graphs, where a higher weight between two nodes signifies a stronger similarity between their signal values
[
13
]
.
Under this framework, the graph (the Laplacian matrix) can be inferred via maximum likelihood (ML) or maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation. The Graphical LASSO (GLASSO)
[
14
]
is one of the early works in this regard, which was later improved by introducing structural
[
15
,
11
,
16
,
17
]
and spectral constraints
[
18
]
into the problem of learning the graph from data. Directed graph topologies, used for modeling directional dependencies, can also be learned using different approaches. Some topology identification methods are based on structural equation models
[
19
]
, while many other approaches use vector auto-regressive (VAR) models that can be represented with directed graphs
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. There have also been some recent works that incorporate both directed and undirected graph topologies to model spatial and temporal correlations at the same time
[
23
]
.

The methodologies mentioned above are mostly applicable for learning static graphs. However, in many real-world applications, such as social networks or finance, the network structure is subject to change over time. Therefore, one of the challenges in graph learning is to learn a time-varying graph topology. There are several approaches in the literature for learning such dynamic graphs
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
.
However, the existing approaches lack robustness to data outliers and cannot efficiently model heavy-tailed distributions, such as those observed in financial data. Moreover, these models also fail to capture graph topologies that adhere to specific structural and spectral properties, such as
k
-component graphs. In this paper, we propose a novel framework for learning time-varying graphs that incorporates spectral and structural constraints in the problem of inferring graph topologies. Our approach is based on a stochastic model that can first characterize the statistical properties of heavy-tailed data and second be employed for data clustering. We subsequently investigate the applications of our framework for time-varying data analysis in financial markets.

II-B

Problem Statement

Assume a dynamic signal
{\bf{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
defined over a time-varying undirected graph structure, where each vertex of the graph represents an element of the signal, and the edge weights encode the (bilateral) interactions between these elements. Suppose the weights of the graph vary only at specific time instants (piece-wise constant graph). We may then segment the time indices into frames of length
T_{n}
, where the graph weights are assumed to remain constant within each time frame. Let
F_{n}
denote the time indices of the
n
-th frame of data, where
n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. In the case of no overlap between consecutive frames, as shown in Fig.
2
, we have

T=\sum_{n=1}^{N}T_{n}
.
A time-varying undirected graph structure can subsequently be represented by
{\cal{G}}=\{{\cal V},{\cal{E}}_{n},\mathbf{W}_{n}\}
. Here,
{\cal V}=\{1,\ldots,p\}
denotes the set of vertices (which remains fixed over time),
{\cal{E}}_{n}\subseteq\{\{i,j\}\mid\,i,j\in{\cal V}\}
represents the time-varying set of edges (unordered pairs of nodes connected to each other) at time frame
n
, and
\mathbf{W}_{n}
is the (weighted) adjacency matrix. Each entry
W_{i,j}(n)\geq 0
of
\mathbf{W}_{n}
denotes the weight of the edge between vertex
i
and vertex
j
during time frame
n
.
Alternatively, one can represent an undirected graph by the vector of all possible edge weights
{\bf{w}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p(p-1)/2}
. The edge weights can also be mapped to the adjacency matrix using the adjacency operator
{\cal{A}}

[
18
]
, i.e.,
\mathbf{W}_{n}={\cal{A}}{\bf{w}}_{n}
. Furthermore, the time-varying Laplacian matrix can be derived as
\mathbf{L}_{n}=\mathrm{Diag}(\mathbf{W}_{n}{\bf{1}})-\mathbf{W}_{n}={\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}
, where
{\cal L}
denotes the Laplacian operator
[
18
]
.

Considering
T
snapshots (time measurements) of the signal vertically arranged in the columns of
\mathbf{X}=[{\bf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bf{x}}_{T}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times T}
. Let
\mathbf{X}_{n}=[{\bf{x}}_{t}\mid\,t\in F_{n}]
denote the data matrix at time frame
n
.

A time-varying graph learning problem can be generally formulated as follows:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{\{\mathbf{S}_{n}\mid_{n\in{\cal T}}\}\in\,\Omega_{\mathbf{S}}}{\mathsf{min}}&\hskip-8.0pt\sum_{n\in{\cal T}}f_{1}(\mathbf{S}_{n},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})+f_{2}(\mathbf{S}_{n})+f_{3}(\mathbf{S}_{n},\mathbf{S}_{n-1}),\end{array}

(1)

where
\mathbf{S}_{n}
denotes the graph matrix (Laplacian or the weighted adjacency matrix),
\Omega_{\mathbf{S}}
denotes the set of feasible graph matrices,
{\cal T}\subseteq\{1,\cdots,N\}
denotes the set of frame indices for which the data is available, and
\Sigma_{n}
denotes the data statistics matrix (e.g., sample covariance matrix).
Here
f_{1}(\cdot)
is a fidelity criterion measuring how well the graph matches the statistics of the data,
f_{2}(\cdot)
is a regularization function used to promote properties such as sparsity, and
f_{3}(\cdot)
is a temporal consistency term formulating the smoothness of the graph variations.

In the next part, we will discuss different choices for the objective function proposed by current methods.

II-C

Related Works

The notion of time-dependent graphs has roots dating back to the late 1990s
[
30
]
; however, the
time-varying graph learning
methodology and concept were quite recently established. The time-varying graphical LASSO (TVGLASSO)
[
25
]
is one of the early works on this topic, which extends the well-known GLASSO
[
14
]
inference method to the case of time-varying topologies. Let
\mathbf{L}_{n}
denote the Laplacian of a time-varying graph at frame
n
. Then, the graph learning problem in this paper is formulated as follows:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{\{\mathbf{L}_{n}\mid_{n=1}^{N}\}\succ{\bf{0}}}{\mathsf{min}}&\hskip-8.0pt\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big(-T_{n}\log\det(\mathbf{L}_{n})+T_{n}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{n})\\
&+\lambda\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{n}\right\rVert_{1,\text{off}}\Big)+\beta\sum_{n=2}^{N}h(\mathbf{L}_{n}-\mathbf{L}_{n-1}),\end{array}

(2)

where
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
denotes the data statistics matrix at time frame
n
(usually the covariance matrix) used as a similarity criterion. Moreover,
h(\cdot)
is a regularization function utilized as a measure of the temporal smoothness of the graph variations. The TVGLASSO, however, does not incorporate the structural constraints of the CGL Laplacian matrix
[
11
]
, and the learned matrices
\{\mathbf{L}_{n}\mid_{n=1}^{N}\}
are only positive definite inverse covariance (precision) matrices. Another early work on time-varying graph topology identification is the method in
[
24
]
.
This work incorporates the Laplacian structural constraints by reformulating the problem in terms of the weighted adjacency matrices
\{\mathbf{W}_{n}\mid_{n=1}^{N}\}
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{cr}\underset{\{\mathbf{W}_{n}\mid_{n=1}^{N}\}\geq{\bf{0}}}{\mathsf{min}}&\hskip-8.0pt\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{W}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{n}^{\top})+f_{W}(\mathbf{W}_{n})\right)\\
&+\gamma\sum_{n=2}^{N}h(\mathbf{W}_{n},\mathbf{W}_{n-1}),\end{array}

(3)

where
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
denotes the distance matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry equals the distance between signal elements
i
and
j
at time frame
n
. For Euclidean distance,
[\mathbf{Z}_{n}]_{i,j}=\left\lVert\tilde{{\bf{x}}}_{n_{i}}-\tilde{{\bf{x}}}_{n_{j}}\right\rVert
, where
\tilde{{\bf{x}}}_{n_{i}}
denotes the
i
-th row of the data matrix
\mathbf{X}_{n}
. Here,
f_{W}(\cdot)
is a measure of graph smoothness, and
h(\cdot)
is a regularization function for the temporal variations of the graph weights. Specifically,
f_{W}(\mathbf{W})=-\alpha{\bf{1}}^{\top}\log(\mathbf{W}{\bf{1}})+\beta\left\lVert\mathbf{W}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
and
h(\mathbf{W}_{n},\mathbf{W}_{n-1})=\left\lVert\mathbf{W}_{n}-\mathbf{W}_{n-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
are used in this paper. Another variant of the above formulation with
h(\mathbf{W}_{n},\mathbf{W}_{n-1})=\left\lVert\mathbf{W}_{n}-\mathbf{W}_{n-1}\right\rVert_{1,1}
is also considered in
[
31
]
, which is solved using a primal-dual splitting method.

In
[
26
]
, the static factor graph model in
[
32
]
is generalized to the time-varying graph factor analysis (TGFA) framework, where the graph signal at time-stamp
t
is considered to have a Gaussian distribution as follows:

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}_{t}\sim{\cal{N}}({\bf{0}},\mathbf{L}_{n}^{\dagger}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\mathbf{I}),\quad\forall t\in F_{n}.

(4)

The problem is then formulated as

\begin{array}[]{cr}\underset{\{\mathbf{L}_{n}|_{n=1}^{N}\}\in\,\Omega_{\mathbf{L}}}{\mathsf{min}}&\hskip-8.0pt\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{L}_{n}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{\top})+f_{L}(\mathbf{L}_{n})\right)\\
&+\eta\sum_{n=2}^{N}R(\Delta\mathbf{L}_{n}\odot\mathbf{H}),\end{array}

(5)

where
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{I}-{\bf{1}}{\bf{1}}^{\top}
, and
\Omega_{\mathbf{L}}
denotes the set of feasible CGL Laplacian matrices, with
f_{L}(\mathbf{L}_{n})=-\alpha{\bf{1}}^{\top}\log(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{L}_{n}))

+

\beta\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{n}\right\rVert_{\text{off},F}^{2}
.

Choosing
R(\cdot)=\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1,1}
to promote the sparsity of the temporal variations, a formulation similar to the authors’ prior work
[
31
]
is yielded, which is also solved via a primal-dual splitting method. In
[
27
]
, several approaches for graph learning, including time-varying graphs, are proposed with applications in financial markets. The problem formulation for learning time-varying graphs in this paper is the same as that in the TVGLASSO method (
2
). However, here the structural constraints of the Laplacian matrices
\{\mathbf{L}_{n}|_{n=1}^{N}\}
are incorporated into the formulation (as in (
5
)), and only causal batch data (past data samples) are used for graph learning.
In
[
33
]
, a general framework for time-varying topology learning is introduced that can deal with online streaming data. Three types of models are studied in this paper, namely the time-varying Gaussian graphical model (TV-GGM), the time-varying smoothness-based model (TV-SBM), and the time-varying structural equation model (TV-SEM), where the first two apply to undirected graphical models, and the last one is utilized for directed topology identification. The methodology in this paper is based on a time-varying optimization framework proposed in
[
34
]
. Here, the general formulation for time-varying graph learning is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\mathbf{L}_{t}^{\star}=\underset{\mathbf{L}}{\mathsf{argmin}}&F(\mathbf{L};t)=f(\mathbf{L};t)+\lambda\,g(\mathbf{L};t),\end{array}

(6)

where
\mathbf{L}_{t}^{\star}
denotes the matrix representation for the graph model (e.g., the Laplacian matrix for an undirected graph).
In this formulation,
f(\cdot)
represents a smooth, strongly convex differentiable fidelity (similarity) measure, and
g(\cdot)
denotes a potentially non-smooth convex regularization function. For instance, in the TV-GGM Gaussian graphical model, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}f(\mathbf{L};t)&=-\log\det(\mathbf{L})+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}),\\
g(\mathbf{L};t)&=g(\mathbf{L})={i}_{\Omega_{\mathbf{L}}}(\mathbf{L})=\begin{cases}0,&\mathbf{L}\in\Omega_{\mathbf{L}}\\
\infty,&\mathbf{L}\notin\Omega_{\mathbf{L}}\end{cases},\end{split}

(7)

where
\Omega_{\mathbf{L}}=\{\mathbf{L}\succ{\bf{0}}\}
denotes the set of positive definite matrices.
In this problem, a solution is found via recursive prediction-correction steps. In the prediction step, a quadratic second-order approximation
\hat{F}(\mathbf{L};t+1)
of the unobserved function
F(\mathbf{L};t+1)
is minimized. In the correction step, the exact function
F(\mathbf{L};t+1)
is optimized by exploiting the updated statistics of the data, with the new data sample
{\bf{x}}_{t}
being received.

In addition to time-varying graph learning approaches for undirected structures, there have been efforts to learn time-varying directed topologies arising in structural equation models, including the works in
[
28
]
and
[
35
]
.
Several other online approaches to graph topology identification have also been studied in the literature, e.g.,
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
.

Most of the existing approaches for time-varying graph learning are either tailored for offline (full-batch) data, where all the samples
\{1,\ldots,T\}
are collected for topology identification
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
, or they deal with online streaming data, where the graph topology is updated with every new data sample
{\bf{x}}_{t}
being acquired
[
28
,
33
]
. There is also a causal (batch) approach
[
27
]
that exploits all the past data frames (e.g.,
1,\ldots,n
) for learning the graph at the current time frame (e.g.,
n
). The offline (full-batch) and causal (batch) approaches suffer from delays and the need for large data storage, while the online approach is slow due to the high computational costs incurred by graph learning at every time stamp. This can make it impractical for real-time use unless some form of cache storage is utilized. Additionally, online approaches may struggle to efficiently capture the temporal consistency in the variations of the graph weights.

This challenge can be addressed by proposing a semi-online (mini-batch) approach in which the graph is updated using only the data samples from a single time frame. The length of the data frame
T_{n}
can then be adjusted based on how dynamic the desired graph topology is assumed to be, depending on the application.

The existing time-varying graph learning methods are also designed for Gaussian data, and they cannot efficiently deal with heavy-tailed data or data with outliers, which are very common in real-world applications, e.g., financial markets
[
42
]
. Hence, these methods cannot be applied to learning time-varying topologies of markets in finance. They cannot either learn graphs with specific spectral properties that can be used for clustering, e.g.,
k
-component graphs, which are very applicable in unsupervised machine learning (data mining)
[
43
]
.

Another issue with the current approaches is that they
utilize a simple subtractive model for the graph variations, where the difference
{\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{w}}_{n-1}
is assumed to be smooth. However, in many real cases, this model is not sufficient and a
multiplicative factor
{\bf{a}}
as
{\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{a}}\odot{\bf{w}}_{n-1}
may be required to better model the variations.

Another drawback of the existing methods is that they rely on complete statistics of the data, while in many real-world applications (such as sensor networks), there may be missing entries in the data (due to sensor failure) or the data may be contaminated with noise (due to measurement errors). This issue has been addressed for static graph learning (e.g.,
[
32
,
44
]
) by adopting a joint signal and graph learning approach in which an additional signal denoising/imputation step is performed. However, this has not been well addressed for learning time-varying graphs.

In the following section, we address these issues by proposing a robust predictive approach to learning time-varying graphs with specific properties that apply to heavy-tailed data.

II-D

Contributions

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We propose a probabilistic framework to model the signal and the weights of the graph in time-varying scenarios. Specifically, we utilize a non-negative VAR model to capture the temporal variations in the weights of the graph. Our method is based on MAP estimation of the graph model in a semi-online approach in which the graph is only updated within frames, where the frame length can be adjusted to achieve the optimal balance between complexity and dynamics.

•

We consider a heavy-tailed Student-
t
distribution for the signal characterized by the Laplacian matrix of a time-varying graph. This distribution can efficiently model data with outliers (e.g., financial data) and it can also
handle Gaussian data by choosing the parameter
\nu
large enough.
Our method is also robust to measurement noise and missing data.

•

We incorporate spectral and structural constraints into the problem of learning time-varying graphs. Our method can be used to learn
k
-component graphs applied to data clustering. We achieve this by imposing constraints on the node degrees and the rank of the Laplacian matrix.

•

We propose an iterative method with proof of convergence, to solve the problem using the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), where a majorization of the original function is optimized in each subproblem. Numerical results demonstrate that our proposed method outperforms some state-of-the-art algorithms for learning time-varying graph models, specifically from heavy-tailed data.

III
Proposed Approach

In contrast to the deterministic approaches for time-varying graph learning, we propose a probabilistic framework to address the problem of learning a time-varying graph. To this end, we employ a non-negative vector autoregressive (VAR) equation to characterize the variations in the edge weights of the graph as follows:

\displaystyle{\bf{w}}_{n}=\left({\bf{a}}\odot{\bf{w}}_{n-1}+{\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}}_{n}\right)_{+},\quad n=1,\ldots,N,

(8)

where the positive part function
(\cdot)_{+}
is used to ensure the weights remain non-negative. Here,

{\bf{a}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p(p-1)/2}
models the VAR coefficients vector assumed to have an exponential prior and

{\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}}_{n}
is a zero-mean temporally and spatially white innovation process with the Laplace distribution. The choice of the Laplace distribution is justified for promoting sparsity in the graph weights. Then, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{split}p({\bf{a}})&=\lambda^{p(p-1)/2}\exp\left(-\lambda\,{\bf{a}}^{\top}{\bf{1}}\right),\quad\lambda>0,\\
p({\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}}_{n})&=\frac{1}{(2\sigma_{\epsilon})^{p}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|{\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}}_{n}\|_{1}}{\sigma_{\epsilon}}\right).\end{split}

(9)

We also adopt a stochastic model for the signal, presuming that
{\bf{x}}_{t}
for
t\in F_{n}
follows a Laplacian heavy-tailed multivariate Student-
t
distribution, as follows:

\displaystyle p({\bf{x}}_{t}|{\bf{w}}_{n})\propto\mathrm{det^{*}}({\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n})^{1/2}\left(1+\frac{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}{\bf{x}}_{t}}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+p)/2},\quad

\displaystyle t\in F_{n},\,\quad\nu>2.

(10)

Choosing the Student-
t
distribution for modeling heavy-tailed data, particularly in financial contexts, is extensively supported in the literature
[
42
]
. Now, suppose we have corrupted measurements of the signal, i.e., some samples are missing, and there is also some noise. Therefore, the measurements
{\bf{y}}_{t}
are modeled as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf{y}}_{t}&={\bf{m}}_{t}\odot({\bf{x}}_{t}+{\bf{n}}_{t}),\quad t\in\{1,\ldots,T\},\\
\mathbf{Y}&=\mathbf{M}\odot(\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{N}),\end{split}

(11)

where
{\bf{m}}_{t}
is a given sampling mask vector with binary elements (zeros correspond to missing samples),
\odot
denotes the point-wise Hadamard product, and
{\bf{n}}_{t}
is a zero-mean i.i.d. Gaussian noise vector with distribution
{\bf{n}}_{t}\sim{\cal{N}}({\bf{0}},\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathbf{I})
. We also have
\mathbf{Y}=[{\bf{y}}_{t}\mid_{t=1}^{T}]
,
\mathbf{M}=[{\bf{m}}_{t}\mid_{t=1}^{T}]
, and
\mathbf{N}=[{\bf{n}}_{t}\mid_{t=1}^{T}]
.

To estimate the time-varying graph weights
{\bf{w}}_{n}
, we employ a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation through a semi-online (mini-batch) approach, where only a single data frame is utilized for graph learning.

Let
\mathbf{Y}_{n}=[{\bf{y}}_{t}|\,t\in F_{n}]=\mathbf{M}_{n}\odot(\mathbf{X}_{n}+\mathbf{N}_{n})
denote the matrix of the corrupted signal samples (observations) at the time frame
n
. For inference of
{\bf{w}}_{n}
, the VAR model parameters,
{\bf{a}}
, and the original (clean) signal
{\bf{x}}_{t}
, in a semi-online fashion, data collection is limited to the
n
-th time frame, i.e., we only utilize
\mathbf{Y}_{n}
. In this scenario, we may need to await the availability of
T_{n}
data samples in time frame
n
. Nonetheless, this approach can also be adapted for online inference by setting
T_{n}=1
.
The problem can then be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{{\bf{w}}_{n}\geq{\bf{0}},\,{\bf{a}}\geq{\bf{0}},\,\mathbf{X}_{n}}{\mathsf{min}}&-\log p({\bf{w}}_{n},{\bf{a}},\mathbf{X}_{n}|{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1},\mathbf{Y}_{n},\mathbf{M}_{n})\\
\mathsf{s.t.}&{\bf{w}}_{n}\in\Omega_{\bf{w}}\end{array}

where

-\log p({\bf{w}}_{n},{\bf{a}},\mathbf{X}_{n}|{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1},\mathbf{Y}_{n},\mathbf{M}_{n})=-\log p(\mathbf{X}_{n}|{\bf{w}}_{n})

-\log p(\mathbf{Y}_{n}|\mathbf{X}_{n},\mathbf{M}_{n})-\log p({\bf{w}}_{n}|{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1},{\bf{a}})-\log p({\bf{a}})+\mathrm{const}

and
\Omega_{\bf{w}}
represents the set of equality constraints that define the feasible region of the desired graph weights. In specific, we assume that the underlying graph structure is
k
-connected, and the degrees of the vertices are fixed and equal to a constant vector
{\bf{d}}
, i.e.,

\Omega_{\bf{w}}=\{{\bf{w}}|\,\,\mathrm{rank}({\cal L}{\bf{w}})=p-k,\mathfrak{d}{\bf{w}}={\bf{d}}\},

where
\mathfrak{d}
represents the degree operator, mapping the vector of edge weights to the vertex degrees
[
45
]
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
{\bf{w}}_{n-1}
in (
III
) be replaced by an estimate of the graph weights from the previous time frame, denoted as
\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}
. By expanding the posterior probability for MAP estimation and simplifying, we obtain the following formulation for learning the time-varying graph:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{{\bf{w}}_{n}\geq{\bf{0}},\,{\bf{a}}\geq{\bf{0}},\,\mathbf{X}_{n}}{\mathsf{min}}&\hskip-5.0pt\frac{1}{T_{n}\sigma_{n}^{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{n}-\mathbf{M}_{n}\odot\mathbf{X}_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-\log\det{\!{}^{\ast}}({\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{{n}})\\
&\hskip-5.0pt+\frac{\nu+p}{T_{n}}\sum_{t\in F_{n}}\log\left(1+\frac{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}{\bf{x}}_{t}}{\nu}\right)\\
&\hskip-5.0pt+\alpha\left\lVert{\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{a}}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}\right\rVert_{1}+\beta\left\lVert{\bf{w}}_{n}\right\rVert_{0}+\gamma{\bf{a}}^{\top}{\bf{1}}\\
\mathsf{s.t.}&\hskip-5.0pt{\bf{w}}_{n}\in\Omega_{\bf{w}}\end{array}

(14)

where
\alpha=\frac{2}{T_{n}\sigma_{\epsilon}}
,
\beta=\frac{2\log\sigma_{\epsilon}}{T_{n}}
, and
\gamma=\frac{2\lambda}{T_{n}}
.

Proof.

See appendix
A
.

The non-convex formulation of problem (
14
), coupled with the interleaved equality constraints on
{\bf{w}}_{n}
, renders it challenging to solve. Nevertheless, taking advantage of splitting techniques in convex optimization, particularly ADMM
[
46
]
, a promising approach to address this problem can be devised. We begin by reformulating the problem, introducing the auxiliary variables
\mathbf{L}_{n}={\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}
and
{\bf{u}}_{n}={\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{a}}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}
. We further incorporate an additional penalty function
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left({\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}\mathbf{V}_{n}\mathbf{V}_{n}^{\top}\right)
with
\mathbf{V}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k},\,\mathbf{V}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{n}=\mathbf{I}
to more effectively control the rank of
{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}
. Thus, the problem formulation becomes:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\begin{array}[t]{c}{\mathsf{min}}\\
\scriptstyle{\bf{w}}_{n}\geq{\bf{0}},\,{\bf{a}}\geq{\bf{0}},\\[-2.0pt]
\scriptstyle\mathbf{X}_{n},\,{\bf{u}}_{n},\,\mathbf{L}_{n},\,\mathbf{V}_{n}\end{array}&\hskip-8.0pt\begin{array}[t]{l}f({\bf{w}}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n},{\bf{a}},{\bf{u}}_{n},\mathbf{L}_{n},\mathbf{V}_{n})\triangleq\\
\hskip 10.0pt-\log\det{\!{}^{\ast}}(\mathbf{L}_{n})+\alpha\left\lVert{\bf{u}}_{n}\right\rVert_{1}+\beta\left\lVert{\bf{w}}_{n}\right\rVert_{0}\\
\hskip 10.0pt+\,\frac{\nu+p}{T_{n}}\sum_{t\in F_{n}}\log\left(1+\frac{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}{\bf{x}}_{t}}{\nu}\right)\\[3.0pt]
\hskip 10.0pt+\,\frac{1}{T_{n}\sigma_{n}^{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{n}-\mathbf{M}_{n}\odot\mathbf{X}_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\gamma{\bf{a}}^{\top}{\bf{1}}\\
\hskip 10.0pt+\,\eta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left({\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}\mathbf{V}_{n}\mathbf{V}_{n}^{\top}\right)\end{array}\\
\mathsf{s.t.}&\hskip-8.0pt\begin{array}[t]{l}\mathbf{L}_{n}={\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n},\,\,{\bf{u}}_{n}={\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{a}}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1},\\
\mathfrak{d}{\bf{w}}_{n}={\bf{d}},\,\,\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{L}_{n})=p-k,\,\mathbf{V}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{n}=\mathbf{I}.\end{array}\end{array}

(15)

Hence, the augmented Lagrangian for this problem yields as follows:

\displaystyle L_{\rho}({\bf{w}}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n},{\bf{a}},{\bf{u}}_{n},\mathbf{L}_{n},\mathbf{V}_{n},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n},{\bf{z}}_{n})=

\displaystyle\hskip 5.0ptf({\bf{w}}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n},{\bf{a}},{\bf{u}}_{n},\mathbf{L}_{n},\mathbf{V}_{n})

\displaystyle\hskip 5.0pt+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}-\mathbf{L}_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\langle{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}-\mathbf{L}_{n},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 5.0pt+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert{\bf{u}}_{n}-{\bf{w}}_{n}+{\bf{a}}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}\right\rVert^{2}+\langle{\bf{u}}_{n}-{\bf{w}}_{n}+{\bf{a}}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 5.0pt+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert\mathfrak{d}{\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{d}}\right\rVert^{2}+\langle\mathfrak{d}{\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{d}},{\bf{z}}_{n}\rangle.

(16)

Now, employing ADMM, we derive an iterative solution consisting of six update steps for the primal variables
{\bf{w}}_{n}
,
\mathbf{X}_{n}
,
{\bf{a}}
,
{\bf{u}}_{n}
,
\mathbf{L}_{n}
and
\mathbf{V}_{n}
, along with three update steps for the dual variables
{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
and
{\bf{z}}_{n}
.

\mathbf{L}_{n}
-update step

The subproblem associated with the update step of
\mathbf{L}
possesses a closed-form solution given by:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l+1}&=\hskip-19.0pt\underset{\mathbf{L}_{n}\succeq{\bf{0}},\,\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{L}_{n})=p-k}{\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}}\hskip-19.0pt-\log\mathrm{det^{\ast}}(\mathbf{L}_{n})+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{n}-{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}-\frac{1}{\rho}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{U}^{l}\left({\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}}^{l}+\left({{{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}}^{l}}^{2}+\frac{4}{\rho}\mathbf{I}}\right)^{1/2}\right)\mathbf{U}^{l\top}.\end{split}

(17)

Here,
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}}^{l}
is a diagonal matrix comprising only the largest
p-k
eigenvalues of
{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}+{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l}/\rho
, with their corresponding eigenvectors contained in
\mathbf{U}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(p-k)}
.

{\bf{w}}_{n}
-update step

The subproblem related to the update step of
{\bf{w}}
is expressed as follows:

\begin{split}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1}\!=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{{\bf{w}}_{n}\geq{\bf{0}}}&\hskip 3.0pt\frac{p+\nu}{T_{n}}\hskip-3.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{t\in F_{n}}\log\left(1+\dfrac{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}}}{\nu}\right)+\beta\left\lVert{\bf{w}}_{n}\right\rVert_{0}\\
&+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}-\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l}+\frac{1}{\rho}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\\
&+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l}-{\bf{w}}_{n}+{\bf{a}}^{l}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}+\frac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l}\right\rVert^{2}\\
&+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert\mathfrak{d}{\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{d}}+\frac{1}{\rho}{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l}\right\rVert^{2}\!\!+\eta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left({\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l}\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l\top}\right).\end{split}

(18)

To address this challenging problem, we employ the majorization minimization (MM) technique
[
47
]
to minimize a surrogate majorization function of the original cost. First, using the inequality
\log(x)\leq x-1,\,\,\forall x>0
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log\left(1+\dfrac{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}}}{\nu}\right)&\leq\log\left(1+\dfrac{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}}}{\nu}\right)\\
&\hskip 10.0pt+\frac{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}}+\nu}{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}}+\nu}-1\\
&=\left\langle{\bf{w}}_{n},{\cal L}^{\ast}\left(\frac{{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}}{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l\top}}{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}}+\nu}\right)\right\rangle+\\
&\hskip 132.0pt\mathrm{const}.\end{split}

(19)

Here,
{\bf{w}}^{l}_{n}
represents a fixed point, selected as the solution from the previous iteration.
We can also define a majorization function for the term
\left\lVert{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathfrak{d}{\bf{w}}_{n}\right\rVert^{2}={\bf{w}}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{H}{\bf{w}}_{n}
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{H}{\bf{w}}_{n}&\leq{\bf{w}}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{H}{\bf{w}}_{n}+({\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l})^{\top}(\zeta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})({\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l})\\
&=\zeta\left\lVert{\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}\right\rVert^{2}+2\langle{\bf{w}}_{n},\mathbf{H}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}\rangle+\mathrm{const},\end{split}

(20)

where
\mathbf{H}={\cal L}^{\ast}{\cal L}+\mathfrak{d}^{\ast}\mathfrak{d}
and
\zeta\geq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H})=4p-2

[
18
]
.
Using the proposed majorization functions (upper-bounds) in (
19
) and (
20
) with
\zeta=4p-2
, the update step for
{\bf{w}}_{n}
would be simplified as follows:

\begin{split}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1}&=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{{\bf{w}}_{n}\geq{\bf{0}}}\,\,\,\frac{\rho(4p-1)}{2}\left\lVert{\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{c}}^{l}\right\rVert^{2}+\beta\left\lVert{\bf{w}}_{n}\right\rVert_{0}\\
&=\mathbbm{1}({\bf{c}}^{l}>{\bf{c}}_{th})\odot{\bf{c}}^{l},\end{split}

(21)

where
{\bf{c}}_{th}=\sqrt{\frac{2\beta}{\rho(4p-1)}}{\bf{1}}
and

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\bf{c}}^{l}&\hskip-8.0pt=\left(1-\frac{\rho}{\rho(4p-1)}\right){\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}-\dfrac{1}{\rho(4p-1)}\left({\bf{a}}^{l}+{\bf{b}}^{l}\right),\\
{\bf{a}}^{l}&\hskip-8.0pt={\cal L}^{*}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{l}+{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l}+\rho\left({\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}-\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l+1}\right)+\eta\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l}\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l\top}\right),\\
{\bf{b}}^{l}&\hskip-8.0pt=-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l}-\rho\left({\bf{u}}_{n}^{l}+{\bf{a}}^{l}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}\right)+\mathfrak{d}^{*}\left({\bf{z}}_{n}^{l}-\rho\left({\bf{d}}-\mathfrak{d}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}\right)\right),\\
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{l}&\hskip-8.0pt=\frac{p+\nu}{T_{n}}\sum_{t\in F_{{n}}}\dfrac{{\bf{x}}_{t}{\bf{x}}_{t}^{\top}}{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}{\bf{x}}_{t}+\nu}.\end{array}

(22)

In the equation above,
{\cal L}^{\ast}
and
\mathfrak{d}^{\ast}
represent the
adjoints of the Laplacian and the degree operators, as defined in
[
18
]
.

{\bf{u}}_{n}
-update step

The subproblem associated with to the update step of
{\bf{u}}_{n}
admits a closed-form solution given by:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l+1}&=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{{\bf{u}}}\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert{\bf{u}}_{n}-{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}+{\bf{a}}^{l}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}+\frac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l}\right\rVert^{2}\\
&\hskip 155.0pt+\,\alpha\left\lVert{\bf{u}}_{n}\right\rVert_{1}\\
&={\cal{S}}_{\frac{\alpha}{\rho}}\left({\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}-{\bf{a}}^{l}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}-\frac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l}\right),\end{split}

(23)

where
{\cal{S}}
denotes the soft-thresholding operator
[
48
]
.

\mathbf{X}_{n}
-update step

The update step for
\mathbf{X}_{n}
is obtained by solving the following problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{X}_{n}^{l+1}&=\{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l+1}|_{t\in F_{n}}\}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{\{{\bf{x}}_{t}|_{t\in F_{n}}\}}\,\displaystyle\sum_{t\in F_{n}}f_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}({\bf{x}}_{t})\\
f_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}({\bf{x}}_{t})&=\frac{1}{T_{n}\sigma_{n}^{2}}\left\lVert{\bf{y}}_{t}-{\bf{m}}_{t}\odot{\bf{x}}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\\
&\hskip 50.0pt+\frac{p+\nu}{T_{n}}\log\left(1+\dfrac{{\bf{x}}^{\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}_{t}}}{\nu}\right).\end{split}

(24)

This can be decomposed into smaller problems for each
{\bf{x}}_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l+1}&=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}f_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}({\bf{x}}_{t}),\quad t\in F_{n}.\end{split}

(25)

To find a closed-form solution to this problem, we replace
f_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}({\bf{x}}_{t})
with a majorization function as proposed by the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\tau\geq\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+\frac{p+\nu}{{\bf{x}}^{l\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}+\nu}\lambda_{\max}({\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n})
. Define the function

\displaystyle f_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}^{S}({\bf{x}}_{t},{\bf{x}}_{0})=\frac{\tau}{T_{n}}\left\lVert{\bf{x}}_{t}-{\bf{x}}_{0}+\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{t}{\bf{x}}_{0}-{\bf{c}}_{t}}{\tau}\right\rVert^{2}+C({\bf{x}}_{0}),

(26)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{Q}_{t}&=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\mathrm{Diag}({\bf{m}}_{t})+\frac{p+\nu}{{\bf{x}}^{l\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}+\nu}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n},\\
{\bf{c}}_{t}&=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{\bf{y}}_{t}.\end{split}

(27)

Here,
{\bf{x}}_{0}
and
C({\bf{x}}_{0})
are constants. Then,
f^{S}_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}({\bf{x}}_{t},{\bf{x}}_{0})
serves as a majorization function for
f_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}({\bf{x}}_{t})
, satisfying the inequality

f_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}({\bf{x}}_{t})\leq f^{S}_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}({\bf{x}}_{t},{\bf{x}}_{0}),\,\forall{\bf{x}}_{0}.

Proof.

See appendix
B
.
∎

By applying this proposition with
{\bf{x}}_{0}={\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}
in the context of a majorization-minimization framework, we formulate and solve the following problem for the update step of
{\bf{x}}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l+1}&=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}f_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}^{S}({\bf{x}}_{t},{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l})\\
&={\bf{x}}^{l}_{t}-\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\mathbf{Q}_{t}{\bf{x}}^{l}_{t}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{\bf{y}}_{t}\right).\end{split}

(28)

{\bf{a}}
-update step

The VAR parameters vector
{\bf{a}}
can also be updated using the following closed-form solution:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf{a}}^{l+1}&=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{{\bf{a}}\geq{\bf{0}}}\,\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert{\bf{a}}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}-{\bf{f}}^{l}\right\rVert^{2}+\gamma{\bf{a}}^{\top}{\bf{1}}\\
&={\cal{S}}_{\frac{\gamma}{\rho\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}^{\circ 2}}}\left(({\bf{f}}^{l})_{+}\oslash\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}\right)\odot\mathbbm{1}(\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}>0),\end{split}

(29)

where
{\bf{f}}^{l}={\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}-{\bf{u}}^{l+1}_{n}-\frac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l}
.

\mathbf{V}_{n}
-update step

Next, we have the update formula for
\mathbf{V}_{n}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l+1}

\displaystyle=\hskip-5.0pt\underset{\mathbf{V}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k},\mathbf{V}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{n}=\mathbf{I}}{\mathsf{argmin}}~\hskip-5.0pt\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left({\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1}\mathbf{V}_{n}\mathbf{V}_{n}^{\top}\right)=\mathbf{Q}_{n}^{l+1}[:,1:k].

(30)

Here,

\mathbf{Q}_{n}^{l+1}[:,1:k]
denotes the set of eigenvectors of
{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1}
corresponding to the first
k
eigenvalues, sorted in ascending order.

Update step for the dual variables

Finally, we have the update step for the dual variables as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l+1}&={\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l}+\rho\left({\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1}-\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l+1}\right),\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l+1}&=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l}+\rho\left({\bf{u}}_{n}^{l+1}-{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1}+{\bf{a}}^{l+1}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}\right),\\
{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l+1}&={\bf{z}}_{n}^{l}+\rho\left(\mathfrak{d}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1}-{\bf{d}}\right).\end{split}

(31)

The proposed method is summarized in Algorithm
1
, with Theorem
1
establishing the convergence.

Theorem 1
.

The sequence of the augmented Lagrangian
\big\{L_{\rho}\big(\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l},{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l},{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l},\mathbf{X}_{n}^{l},{\bf{a}}^{l},\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l},{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l}\big)\big\}
generated by Algorithm

converges for any sufficiently large
\rho
. At the limit point, the equality constraints
\mathbf{L}_{n}={\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}
,
{\bf{u}}_{n}={\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{a}}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}

are also satisfied, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lim_{l\to+\infty}\big\|{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}-\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l}\big\|_{F}=0\\

&\lim_{l\to+\infty}\big\|{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l}-{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}+{\bf{a}}^{l}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}\big\|=0.\end{split}

(32)

Proof.

.

Computational Complexity

The update step for
{\bf{w}}_{n}
involves the computation of
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}
, which has a complexity of
{\cal{O}}(T_{n}p^{2})
. Given this, the complexity of the closed-form solution in (
21
) would be
{\cal{O}}(p^{2})
. This also holds for the update of
{\bf{u}}_{n}
via (
23
). However, the update steps in (
17
) and (

) require eigenvalue decomposition of
p\times p
matrices, which is generally
{\cal{O}}(p^{3})
complex. Hence, the overall complexity of the proposed algorithm is
{\cal{O}}(p^{3}+T_{n}p^{2})
. While this may indicate that the proposed method may not be scalable to very large graphs, it is intrinsic to every graph-based clustering method that deals with the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian.

Synthetic data

For synthetic data generation, we consider
p=100
and
T=1000
. We divide the
T
time-stamps into equal frames (windows) of length
T_{n}
(with no overlap), where the graph is assumed to be constant during each time frame. Let
F_{n}
denote the time indices in frame
n
. Random samples of the signal in each frame are generated via

{\bf{x}}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{L}_{n}^{\dagger}\right)^{1/2}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}_{t},\quad{\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}}_{t}\sim\mathrm{St}({\bf{0}},\mathbf{I}),\quad t\in F_{n}
,
where
\mathbf{L}_{n}^{\dagger}
represents the pseudo-inverse of the Laplacian matrix at time frame
n
and
\mathrm{St}
denotes the Student-
t
distribution with zero mean and identity covariance matrix. To model the temporal variations of the graph weights, we use the equation

{\bf{w}}_{n}=\left({\bf{a}}\odot{\bf{w}}_{n-1}+{\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}}_{n}\right)_{+},\quad n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

Here,
{\bf{a}}
is sampled from an exponential distribution and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}}_{n}
is sampled from a normal distribution, where
N
denotes the number of time frames. The initial values for the graph weights
{\bf{w}}_{0}
(and subsequently the Laplacian matrix
\mathbf{L}_{0}
) are sampled from the Stochastic Block Model, where the nodes are partitioned into 4 clusters (blocks) with random intra-cluster and inter-cluster edge weights.

We construct the original data matrix
\mathbf{X}
by concatenating the vectors
{\bf{x}}_{t}
column-wise, covering the range from
t=1
to
t=T
. Following this, we normalize the data matrix such that each row is centered and scaled by its standard deviation. Next, we create a random binary sampling matrix
\mathbf{M}
defined by the sampling rate parameter
\mathrm{SR}
, along with the observation noise matrix
\mathbf{N}
, which consists of i.i.d. Gaussian random entries with zero mean and variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}
. The observation matrix is then formed as
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{M}\odot(\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{N})
.

We then introduce the matrices
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{M}
as inputs to the time-varying graph learning algorithms.

and
\rho=3
. We also set

, and

.

\displaystyle{\bf{u}}_{\text{MSRP},n}^{\star}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{{\bf{u}}_{n}\geq{\bf{0}},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{n}^{\top}{\bf{u}}_{n}=1}{\bf{u}}_{n}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}{\bf{u}}_{n}

\displaystyle\text{GR}\triangleq\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{n}^{\top}{\bf{u}}_{n}}{\sqrt{{\bf{u}}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{n}{\bf{u}}_{n}}}.

To maximize this ratio, we design a portfolio referred to as the Maximum Time-Varying Graph Ratio Portfolio (MTVGRP). The problem for this portfolio design is stated as follows:

\displaystyle{\bf{u}}_{\text{MTVGRP},n}^{\star}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{{\bf{u}}_{n}\geq{\bf{0}},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{n}^{\top}{\bf{u}}_{n}=1}{\bf{u}}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{n}{\bf{u}}_{n}

(34)

V
Conclusion

This paper explores the problem of learning time-varying graphs specifically designed for heavy-tailed data. We propose a novel approach for time-varying graph learning that is tailored to infer graph-structured models capable of effectively capturing heavy-tailed distributions. Our proposed approach employs a probabilistic model to formulate the problem of learning time-varying graphs. We also incorporate spectral constraints into the problem, enabling us to learn multi-component graphs suitable for clustering. We present a solution based on a maximum-a-posteriori estimation framework using a semi-online strategy, wherein a single data frame is utilized to update the graph. To demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of our method in graphical modeling of time-varying heavy-tailed data, particularly within financial markets, we conduct numerical experiments using both synthetic and real datasets.

Proof.

The MAP estimation rule can be expressed as follows:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{{\bf{w}}_{n}\geq{\bf{0}},\,{\bf{a}}\geq{\bf{0}},\,\mathbf{X}_{n}}{\mathsf{min}}&-\log p({\bf{w}}_{n},{\bf{a}},\mathbf{X}_{n}|{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1},\mathbf{Y}_{n},\mathbf{M}_{n})=\\
&-\log p(\mathbf{Y}_{n}|\mathbf{X}_{n},\mathbf{M}_{n})-\log p(\mathbf{X}_{n}|{\bf{w}}_{n})\\
&-\log p({\bf{w}}_{n}|{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1},{\bf{a}})-\log p({\bf{a}})+\mathrm{const}\\
\mathsf{s.t.}&{\bf{w}}_{n}\in\Omega_{\bf{w}}.\end{array}

Now, given the Gaussian distribution of the measurement noise, we may write:

\displaystyle\begin{split}p(\mathbf{Y}_{n}|\mathbf{X}_{n},\mathbf{M}_{n})&=\hskip-3.0pt\prod_{t\in F_{n}}p({\bf{y}}_{t}|{\bf{x}}_{t},{\bf{m}}_{t})\\
&=C_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{Y}_{n}-\mathbf{M}_{n}\odot\mathbf{X}_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right),\end{split}

where
C_{0}
is a constant.
Moreover, assuming the Student-
t
distribution in (
III
) for the data given the underlying graph model, we have:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{X}|{\bf{w}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\prod_{t\in F_{n}}p({\bf{x}}_{t}|{\bf{w}}_{n})

\displaystyle=C_{1}\prod_{t\in F_{n}}\mathrm{det^{*}}({\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n})\left(1+\frac{{\bf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}{\bf{x}}_{t}}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+p)/2}.

with
C_{1}
being another constant.

Now, let
{\bf{v}}_{n}={\bf{a}}\odot{\bf{w}}_{n-1}\geq{\bf{0}}
.
Then, we may write:

\displaystyle p({\bf{w}}_{n}|{\bf{w}}_{n-1},{\bf{a}})

\displaystyle=p({\bf{w}}_{n}|{\bf{v}}_{n})=\prod_{i}p(w_{n}(i)|v_{n}(i)).

Using the non-negative VAR equation
w_{n}(i)=(v_{n}(i)+\epsilon_{n}(i))_{+}
, we have:

\displaystyle p(w_{n}(i)|v_{n}(i))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\mathbbm{P}[\epsilon_{n}(i)=w_{n}(i)-v_{n}(i)]&w_{n}(i)>0\\
\mathbbm{P}[\epsilon_{n}(i)\leq-v_{n}(i)]&w_{n}(i)=0\end{array}\right.

Assuming i.i.d. Laplace distribution for the elements of

as in (

\displaystyle p(w_{n}(i)|v_{n}(i))

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\epsilon}}\exp\left(-\frac{|w_{n}(i)-v_{n}(i)|}{\sigma_{\epsilon}}\right)&w_{n}(i)>0\\
\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{-v_{n}(i)}{\sigma_{\epsilon}}\right)&w_{n}(i)=0\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\epsilon}^{\mathbbm{1}(w_{n}(i)>0)}}\exp\left(-\frac{|w_{n}(i)-v_{n}(i)|}{\sigma_{\epsilon}}\right).

\displaystyle p({\bf{w}}_{n}|{\bf{w}}_{n-1},{\bf{a}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{p}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{\left\lVert{\bf{w}}_{n}\right\rVert_{0}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sigma_{\epsilon}}\left\lVert{\bf{w}}_{n}-{\bf{a}}\odot{\bf{w}}_{n-1}\right\rVert_{1}\right).

Finally, plugging these probabilities in (
III
), also considering
exponential distribution for the VAR parameters
{\bf{a}}
as in (
9
),
we
can obtain (
14
) after simplification.

Proof of Proposition

Proof.

Let
g({\bf{x}}_{t})=\log\left(1+\dfrac{{\bf{x}}^{\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}_{t}}}{\nu}\right)
. Then, using the inequality
\log(x)\leq x-1,\,\,\forall x>0
, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}g({\bf{x}}_{t})&=\log\left(1+\dfrac{{\bf{x}}^{\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}_{t}}}{\nu}\right)\\
&\leq\log\left(1+\dfrac{{\bf{x}}^{l\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}^{l}_{t}}}{\nu}\right)+\frac{{\bf{x}}^{\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}_{t}}+\nu}{{\bf{x}}^{l\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}^{l}_{t}}+\nu}-1\\
&=g({\bf{x}}_{t}^{l})-1+{\bf{x}}_{t}^{\top}\frac{{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}}{{\bf{x}}^{l\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}^{l}_{t}}+\nu}{\bf{x}}_{t}+\frac{\nu}{{\bf{x}}^{l\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}^{l}_{t}}+\nu}\\
&=\frac{1}{{\bf{x}}^{l\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}{\bf{x}}^{l}_{t}+\nu}{\bf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}{\bf{x}}_{t}+h({\bf{x}}_{t}^{l}),\end{array}

where
h({\bf{x}}_{t}^{l})
is a constant.
Then, we may write:

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}({\bf{x}}_{t})&=\frac{1}{T_{n}\sigma_{n}^{2}}\left\lVert\mathrm{Diag}({\bf{m}}_{t}){\bf{x}}_{t}-{\bf{y}}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{p+\nu}{T_{n}}g({\bf{x}}_{t})\\

with

and

.
Now, for

, we may propose another majorization function as follows:

\displaystyle f_{\bf{x}}({\bf{x}}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T_{n}}\Big({\bf{x}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{t}{\bf{x}}_{t}+({\bf{x}}_{t}-{\bf{x}}^{l}_{t})^{\top}(\tau\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}_{t})({\bf{x}}_{t}-{\bf{x}}_{t}^{l})

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt-2{\bf{c}}_{t}^{\top}{\bf{x}}_{t}\Big)+r({\bf{x}}_{t}^{l})

where

To find

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{Q}_{t})&=\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\mathrm{Diag}({\bf{m}}_{t})+\frac{p+\nu}{{\bf{x}}^{{}^{l}\top}_{t}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}\,{{\bf{x}}^{l}_{t}}+\nu}{\cal L}{\bf{w}}^{l+1}_{n}\right)\\

where we applied

[

in the first expression. Hence, it suffices to choose

&\quad\leq L_{\rho}(\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l},{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l},{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l},\mathbf{X}_{n}^{l},{\bf{a}}^{l},\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l},{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l})\\
&\quad\quad+\frac{1}{\rho}\big(\big\|{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l+1}-{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l}\big\|_{F}^{2}+\big\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l+1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l}\big\|^{2}+\big\|{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l+1}\\
&\quad\quad-{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l}\big\|^{2}\big)-\frac{\rho}{2}\big\|{\bf{a}}^{l+1}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}-{\bf{a}}^{l}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}\big\|^{2}.\end{split}

(57)

By calculation, we obtain that if
\rho
is sufficiently large such that

\rho\geq\max_{l}\tfrac{c\big(\big\|{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l+1}-{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l}\big\|_{F}^{2}+\big\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l+1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l}\big\|^{2}+\big\|{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l+1}-{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l}\big\|^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\big\|{\bf{a}}^{l+1}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}-{\bf{a}}^{l}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}\big\|}

(58)

holds with some constant
c>\sqrt{2}
, then we have

\begin{split}L_{\rho}(\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l+1},{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1},{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l+1},\mathbf{X}_{n}^{l+1},{\bf{a}}^{l+1},\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l+1},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l+1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l+1},{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l+1})\\
\leq L_{\rho}(\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l},{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l},{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l},\mathbf{X}_{n}^{l},{\bf{a}}^{l},\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l},{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l}),\end{split}

(59)

for any
l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. Therefore, the sequence
\big\{L_{\rho}(\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l},{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l},{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l},\mathbf{X}_{n}^{l},{\bf{a}}^{l},\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l},{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l})\big\}
is monotonically decreasing and lower bounded, indicating that it is convergent.

By combining (
57
) and (
58
), we further obtain:

\begin{split}&L_{\rho}(\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l},{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l},{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l},\mathbf{X}_{n}^{l},{\bf{a}}^{l},\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l},{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l})\\
&\quad-L_{\rho}(\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l+1},{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1},{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l+1},\mathbf{X}_{n}^{l+1},{\bf{a}}^{l+1},\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l+1},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l+1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l+1},{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l+1})\\
&\geq\frac{(c^{2}-2)\rho}{2}\big(\big\|{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1}-\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l+1}\big\|_{F}^{2}+\big\|\mathfrak{d}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1}-{\bf{d}}\big\|^{2}\\
&\quad+\big\|{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l+1}-{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l+1}+{\bf{a}}^{l+1}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}\big\|^{2}\big).\end{split}

(60)

The convergence of
L_{\rho}(\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l},{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l},{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l},\mathbf{X}_{n}^{l},{\bf{a}}^{l},\mathbf{V}_{n}^{l},{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{n}^{l},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{l},{\bf{z}}_{n}^{l})
yields:

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to+\infty}\big\|{\cal L}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}-\mathbf{L}_{n}^{l}\big\|_{F}=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to+\infty}\big\|\mathfrak{d}{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}-{\bf{d}}\big\|=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to+\infty}\big\|{\bf{u}}_{n}^{l}-{\bf{w}}_{n}^{l}+{\bf{a}}^{l}\odot\hat{{\bf{w}}}_{n-1}\big\|=0,

completing the proof.
∎
