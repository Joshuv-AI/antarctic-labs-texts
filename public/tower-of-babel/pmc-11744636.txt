scHiClassifier: a deep learning framework for cell type prediction by fusing multiple feature sets from single-cell Hi-C data
Authors: Xiangfei Zhou, Hao Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-cell high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) technology enables capturing chromosomal spatial structure information at the cellular level. However, to effectively investigate changes in chromosomal structure across different cell types, there is a requisite for methods that can identify cell types utilizing single-cell Hi-C data. Current frameworks for cell type prediction based on single-cell Hi-C data are limited, often struggling with features interpretability and biological significance, and lacking convincing and robust classification performance validation. In this study, we propose four new feature sets based on the contact matrix with clear interpretability and biological significance. Furthermore, we develop a novel deep learning framework named scHiClassifier based on multi-head self-attention encoder, 1D convolution and feature fusion, which integrates information from these four feature sets to predict cell types accurately. Through comprehensive comparison experiments with benchmark frameworks on six datasets, we demonstrate the superior classification performance and the universality of the scHiClassifier framework. We further assess the robustness of scHiClassifier through data perturbation experiments and data dropout experiments. Moreover, we demonstrate that using all feature sets in the scHiClassifier framework yields optimal performance, supported by comparisons of different feature set combinations. The effectiveness and the superiority of the multiple feature set extraction are proven by comparison with four unsupervised dimensionality reduction methods. Additionally, we analyze the importance of different feature sets and chromosomes using the “SHapley Additive exPlanations” method. Furthermore, the accuracy and reliability of the scHiClassifier framework in cell classification for single-cell Hi-C data are supported through enrichment analysis. The source code of scHiClassifier is freely available at https://github.com/HaoWuLab-Bioinformatics/scHiClassifier.Keywords: single-cell Hi-C, cell type prediction, deep learning framework, multiple feature sets, cell classification

Introduction

Chromosomes’ 3D structure is crucial for biological processes like genomic stability and gene expression, affecting cell fate and phenotype [1–4]. High-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) is a technique that captures genome-wide chromosomal interaction information [5], facilitating the analysis of the correlation between the 3D structure of the genome and biological processes [6, 7]. Single-cell Hi-C extends Hi-C to capture genomic structure at the cellular level [8], but accurately identifying cell types from this data is challenging due to its variability, data sparsity, and coverage heterogeneity [9]. Nonetheless, it offers the potential to elucidate detailed relationships between unique cellular expression patterns and genome conformation [9, 10]. Manual cell type annotation in large datasets is limited by high costs and low accuracy [11]. Thus, this study’s primary aim is to devise an accurate and efficient method for cell type identification from single-cell Hi-C data.

Significant development has been made in cell clustering based on single-cell Hi-C data, with numerous methods such as scHiCluster [9], scHiCExplorer [12], Higashi [10], Fast-Higashi [13], scDEC-Hi-C [14], scHiCSC [15], HiC-SGL [16], Higashi [17], and MRscHiC [18]. However, these unsupervised clustering methods generally exhibit lower accuracy than supervised learning techniques, which can provide more precise classification when labeled data are accessible.

Wu et al. [19] introduced scHiCStackL, a classification framework for single-cell Hi-C data that enhances target chromosome fragment contact information using adjacent fragments. The framework employs KPCA for cell embeddings and constructs a stacked ensemble model with conventional classifiers for cell classification. Although achieving promising classification performance, scHiCStackL exhibits the following limitations: (i) KPCA-based feature extraction lacks interpretability and biological significance, rendering it unsuitable for downstream biological analyses; (ii) its reliance on traditional machine learning models may be less effective for large-scale data than deep learning; (iii) scHiCStackL’s robustness and universality have not been effectively demonstrated, as it has only been tested on two datasets provided by Ramani et al. [20] and Flyamer et al. [21]; (iv) scHiCStackL’s performance has mainly been benchmarked against traditional machine learning classification algorithms and clustering frameworks, lacking comprehensive validation of classification superiority due to limited prior research in the field.

Therefore, we introduce scHiClassifier, a deep learning framework for precise cell-type prediction in single-cell Hi-C data. It incorporates four interpretable and biologically meaningful feature sets extracted from chromosome contact matrices proposed by us, and integrates them via a model consisting of a multi-head self-attention encoder, a 1D convolution module, and a feature fusion module for accurate cell classification. We confirm the superior classification performance and universality of scHiClassifier across six datasets (human and mouse) through comprehensive comparisons with scHiCStackL and three machine learning models. Additionally, we verify the robustness of scHiClassifier through data perturbation and dropout experiments. We also establish the optimality of a four-feature combination in scHiClassifier, as indicated by comparative performance analyses of various feature set combinations. Moreover, we demonstrate the effectiveness and the superiority of our multiple feature set extraction through comparisons with four unsupervised dimensionality reduction methods. Furthermore, we analyze the contribution of different feature sets and chromosomes to cell-type prediction using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Finally, comprehensive gene extraction and enrichment analysis support the accuracy and reliability of the scHiClassifier in single-cell Hi-C data-based cell-type classification.

Methods

0.3 Framework of scHiClassifier

The scHiClassifier framework, depicted in Fig. 1, comprises three key steps: data preparation, extraction of multiple feature sets, and construction of the fusion prediction model. In the first step, raw single-cell Hi-C data are preprocessed into contact matrices for compatibility with subsequent processes. In the second step, four feature sets—smoothed bin contact probability (SBCP), neighbors bin contact probability (NBCP), smoothed small domain contact probability (SSDCP), and plus small domain contact probability (PSDCP)—are extracted from the contact matrix to effectively capture valuable information such as underlying patterns in the data, reduce noise, and enhance the representation learning capability of the deep learning model. They will be utilized as inputs for the deep learning model. The third step constructs a deep learning fusion prediction model that integrates interaction information from the four feature sets for accurate cell classification. The model consists of three core modules: a multi-head self-attentive encoder, a 1D convolution module, and a feature fusion module.

The framework of scHiClassifier. The first step involves converting the original dataset into the format of a contact matrix. Then, multiple feature sets are derived from the contact matrices. Finally, a deep learning fusion prediction model is constructed, integrating interaction information for precise cell categorization.

0.4 Extraction of multiple feature sets

To address the challenge posed by the sparsity of single-cell Hi-C data [9, 15, 19], where the resulting contact matrix often contains a low percentage of non-zero data (see Supplementary Table S2), it becomes crucial to enhance the representation learning capability of deep learning models. To achieve this goal, we propose the extraction of four feature sets, including “SBCP”, “NBCP”, “SSDCP”, and “PSDCP”. These feature sets serve as the fundamental components for constructing the input of our deep learning model, enabling the utilization of the limited yet valuable information available in the data.

0.4.1 Smoothed bin contact probability feature set

The contact probability of bins can be used as a feature for subsequent single-cell Hi-C data analysis [28]. The contact probability within the same bin on the same chromosome varies across different cell stages or cell types [28]. Hence, our framework employs bin contact probabilities as a feature set.

0.4.3 Smoothed small domain contact probability feature set

Most non-zero elements in the single-cell Hi-C contact matrix cluster near the main diagonal [20, 21, 25, 30]. To extract significant contact information from the matrix, we define a rectangular localized domain centered on the elements of the main diagonal, bounded by elements that are linearly adjacent to them. Summing the contact probabilities of all bins within this domain reveals crucial insights into chromosomal interactions. This domain not only captures contact information between bins in close linear proximity but also between different segments within the same bin. By calculating the contact probabilities of these bins, valuable information about the extent of interactions between neighboring bins on the chromosome, as well as interactions within the bins, can be obtained. 0.4.4 Plus small domain contact probability feature set

Smoothing reduces local variations and outliers, and computing SSDCP features on the smoothed matrix minimizes the adverse effects of noise and uncertainty. However, smoothing may blur or discard certain detailed information. To address this limitation, we introduce the PSDCP feature set. 0.5 Construction of the fusion prediction model

A deep learning model for cell classification has been designed to comprehensively integrate interaction information from four feature sets using a multi-head self-attentive encoder module, a 1D convolution module, and a feature fusion module, as depicted in Fig. 2. Optimized integration of these modules boosts the model’s predictive power, adeptly capturing complex feature relationships for robust and reliable cell classification.

The architecture of the fusion prediction model. This model comprises the following three modules: multi-head self-attention encoder module, 1D convolution module, and feature fusion module.

The model’s input consists of four single-channel 3D tensors, each representing a distinct feature set. The batch size of each tensor equals the number of cells in the input to the model and the size of the third dimension of each tensor equals the total number of bins that can be segmented from all chromosomes in a cell. The input layer merges these tensors into a single 3D tensor with four channels, which is then fed into the multi-head self-attention encoder module.

0.5.1 Multi-head self-attention encoder module

The attention mechanism [31] incorporates three inputs: query, key, and value, and aggregates them in attention pooling using the scaled dot-product attention scoring function. It dynamically allocates higher weights to important information and lower weights to irrelevant details, adapting to varying contexts for efficient resource utilization. The self-attention mechanism [31] requires that the input for keys, values, and queries originate from the same tensor to facilitate the learning of correlations among four feature sets present in the input. Supplying the same set of queries, keys, and values, the model is designed to learn various behaviors based on the same attention mechanism, thereby integrating these behaviors to encapsulate more valuable information.

The multi-head self-attention mechanism [31] adopts this approach by combining different representation subspaces of queries, keys, and values, thereby supplementing more valuable information. This module comprises two structurally identical multi-head self-attention encoders, namely Encoder1 and Encoder2. In Encoder1, the queries, keys, and values are first transformed using 20 sets of independently learned fully connected (FC) layers. Each set consists of three distinct FC layers specifically designed for transforming queries, keys, and values individually. Subsequently, the transformed queries, keys, and values from these 20 sets are fed into separate attention pooling operations in parallel, each referred to as a “head”. The outputs of these 20 attention pooling operations are then concatenated together, followed by transformation using another FC layer. The resulting tensor serves as the output of the multi-head self-attention layer.

Following this, an add&norm layer, composed of residual connection and layer normalization components, adds the output of the previous layer to its input and normalizes it, facilitating better information propagation and gradient control. The following layer is a feed-forward network with two FC layers and an activation function, which acts as a 1x1 convolution to individually update each input data point. This layer enhances the model’s performance by transforming the representation space of the attention mechanism’s output vector, enabling it to serve as a better input for the next attention layer. An add&norm layer follows the feed-forward network to further enhance information propagation and gradient control, finalizing Encoder1’s structure.

Encoder2, identical in structure to Encoder1, takes the output of Encoder1 as its input. Its output remains a 3D tensor with a channel size of 4, which is subsequently divided into four tensors with a channel size of 1, and fed into the 1D convolution module.

In summary, this module effectively captures and consolidates correlations among input feature sets, enabling the model to focus on important information, efficiently allocate processing resources, and enhance the representation and learning capabilities of the fusion prediction model.

0.5.2 One-dimensional convolution module

The four 3D tensors from the preceding module, each with a channel size of 1, are fed in parallel into four 1D convolution modules, all possessing identical architectures. Each convolution module comprises two sub-modules: CNN1 and CNN2.

The CNN1’s first layer, a 1D convolution (Conv1d) layer, is tailored for 1D data processing, extracting significant features and learning representations by identifying local spatial relationships and patterns via 1D convolution kernels [32]. Following Conv1d is the BatchNorm layer [32, 33], utilized to prevent gradient explosion and vanishing. Subsequently, the MaxPool layer [32] is employed to reduce feature dimensionality, preserve crucial features, decrease model computations, prevent overfitting, and enhance generalization capabilities. To introduce non-linear components and enhance the model’s expressive capacity, the ReLU activation function connects BatchNorm and MaxPool layers. The dropout layer at the end of CNN1 effectively curbs overfitting by selectively omitting a fraction of neurons during forward propagation [32].

The output of CNN1 is processed by CNN2, which shares an identical structure comprising successively the Conv1d, BatchNorm, ReLU activation, MaxPool, and dropout layers. Each CNN2 module produces a 3D tensor with multiple channels. Consequently, the four 1D convolution modules generate four multi-channel 3D tensors, which are then input into the feature fusion module for integration.

Results

0.6 Comparative analysis of classification performance between scHiClassifier and benchmark frameworks

To demonstrate the superior performance of scHiClassifier in cell classification, we benchmarked scHiClassifier against scHiCStackL and three machine learning models (logistic regression (LR) [35], Random forest (RF) [36], and support vector machine (SVM) [37]) across six datasets. Notably, the input to the machine learning models comprises a vector constructed by concatenating four feature set vectors: SBCP, NBCP, SSDCP, and PSDCP.

To ensure the scientific rigor and accuracy of the experimental results, diminish dependence on specific dataset partitioning, and assess the generalization capabilities of the frameworks, we conducted 50 random splits of the six datasets into training and testing sets, using different random seeds for each split. This resulted in 50 training-testing subset pairs for each dataset separately, with each trained model evaluated on its matched testing subset.

Subsequently, we evaluated the classification performance of the five models using three metrics: accuracy (ACC), balanced accuracy (BACC), and macro-average F1 score (Macro-F1). ACC indicates the overall correct classification rate, although exhibiting potential bias when evaluating imbalanced datasets. BACC is computed as the arithmetic mean of recall across all classes, ensuring equitable evaluation on imbalanced datasets. Moreover, the F1 score, representing the harmonic mean of precision and recall, and the Macro-F1, computed as the arithmetic mean of the F1 scores for all categories, are crucial for evaluating model performance on imbalanced datasets.

The machine learning models identified optimal hyperparameters via grid search across 50 training-testing pairs. scHiCStackL adopted hyperparameters from Wu et al.’s source code [19]. Details on scHiClassifier’s hyperparameters are elaborated in the “Discussion and conclusion”. Additionally, comparison outcomes are presented in Fig. 3.

0.7 Comparative robustness analysis of scHiClassifier and benchmark frameworks under data perturbation and random dropouts

This section presents two experiment sets to evaluate the robustness of scHiClassifier against data alterations: data perturbation and data dropout. The former involves applying +1 or -1 operations to a certain percentage of contact number information randomly, while the latter involves randomly applying -1 operations. Notably, to ensure reliability and scientific validity, all experiments in this subsection also generate 50 pairs of training and testing subsets for each dataset by modifying the random seeds. Hyperparameter selection aligns with the previous subsection.

In the data perturbation tests, two sub-experiments are performed. The first sub-experiment applies perturbations at 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50% to each of six datasets, creating five perturbed versions for each original dataset. These are then split into 50 training and testing subset pairs. Models are trained on each perturbed training subset and tested on the corresponding perturbed testing subset. In the second sub-experiment, we apply 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50% perturbations to testing subsets while keeping training subsets intact, resulting in five groups of datasets for each of the six original datasets. Each group consists of 50 pairs of intact training and perturbed testing subsets. Models are trained on intact subsets and tested on corresponding perturbed subsets. scHiClassifier and four benchmark frameworks are tested through the aforementioned two sub-experiments, with performance assessed by ACC, BACC, and Macro-F1 metrics. 0.8 Validation of the effectiveness and superiority of the multiple feature sets extraction

To evaluate the effectiveness and superiority of the proposed multiple feature sets extraction, we compared it with four unsupervised dimensionality reduction techniques: principal component analysis (PCA) [39], kernel principal component analysis (KPCA) [40], singular value decomposition (SVD) [41], and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [42], using them to extract features from contact matrices.

The dimensions of features extracted by PCA, KPCA, SVD, and t-SNE were aligned with those of the fusion features composed of four feature sets. These features were then fed into LR, RF, and SVM models, with their classification performance assessed by ACC, BACC, and Macro-F1 metrics.

To maintain reliability and scientific validity, experiments in this subsection followed the same protocol as the previous one, involving 50 training and testing subset pairs from six datasets with varied random seeds. In conclusion, the proposed multiple feature sets extraction is highly effective and superior for feature extraction from single-cell Hi-C contact matrices, highlighting its utility and robustness in this context, which may improve the accuracy and interpretability of classifications based on such data.

0.9 Comparative analysis of classification performance using different combinations of feature sets in scHiClassifier

To demonstrate the scHiClassifier’s effectiveness in integrating feature sets (SBCP, NBCP, SSDCP, PSDCP) and its superior classification, we compared it with 14 other frameworks, including those using individual features, dual-feature fusions, and triple-feature fusions. Discussion and conclusion

Accurate identification of cell type based on single-cell Hi-C data not only enhances our understanding of disease models and genomic variations [78] but also holds significant promise for clinical applications, such as pathology diagnosis and treatment based on cell classification [79]. This study presents the scHiClassifier, which offers an accurate and efficient method for identifying cell types using single-cell Hi-C data. This framework encompasses four innovative feature sets based on the contact matrix, characterized by intuitive interpretability and biological relevance, along with a deep learning model designed to integrate these four feature sets. Comprehensive experimental validation on six datasets from mice and humans has demonstrated that the scHiClassifier framework exhibits exceptional, reliable classification performance, robustness, and generalizability in cell classification based on single-cell Hi-C data.

Additionally, during the training of scHiClassifier, we noted that optimal hyperparameter settings correlate with dataset characteristics. These correlations are as follows: (i)Regardless of species, the optimal learning rate is 0.00001 for larger datasets and 0.0001 for smaller ones, though a precise cell count threshold for adjusting the rate remains undetermined due to limited dataset variety. (ii) Without considering the learning rate, the optimal hyperparameter combinations for scHiClassifier can remain consistent across different datasets within the same species, but vary across datasets from different species. The specific hyperparameter combinations for different datasets are detailed in Supplementary Table S3. We hope that future cell classification frameworks using single-cell Hi-C data will standardize hyperparameters across species and cell counts.
