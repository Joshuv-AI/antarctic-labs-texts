Introduction

Colonic actinomycosis is rare chronic infectious disease characterized by infiltrative and granulomatous inflammation associated with multiple abscesses and sinuses formations. Risk factors include previous abdominal/pelvic surgery, abdominal wall trauma, gastrointestinal foreign body, gastrointestinal tract lesions and immunosuppression. Several forms of immunosuppression, such as leukemia, lymphoma, renal insufficiency, renal transplant, and diabetes, have been demonstrated to facilitate this infection [1].

Herein, we present a case of an adult female who was diagnosed with colonic actinomycosis. The patient presented with a painful lump in RIF mimicking neoplasia, initially misdiagnosed as transverse colon carcinoma, with features of complete bowel obstruction conferring diagnostic difficulty due to the rarity of the infection.

This case has been reported in line with the Surgical Case REport (SCARE) 2023 guidelines [2].

Case presentation

A 60-year-old female presented in emergency room with complaints of a painful lump in the right iliac fossa for three weeks. Initially, the pain was mild. However, the pain gradually increased over three days prior presentation. It was associated with abdominal distension, multiple episodes of vomiting and failure to pass stool and flatus. There was no history of fever, anorexia and weight loss. On examination, there was a hard tender lump of size 8 cm × 4 cm approximately, in the RIF. The mass was irregular and immobile. There was no past history of surgical interventions, any comorbid conditions or immunocompromised status.

Blood investigations (Table 1) revealed anemia (Hemoglobin: 10.7 g/dL), and leucocytosis (Total leucocyte count: 13690/cc) with neutrophilia (Differential neutrophil percentage: 88 %). Serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA 19–9) were within normal limits. Ultrasonography was done which showed a complex heterogeneous irregular outlined space-occupying lesion measuring 27.7 mm × 34.4 mm noted at the ileocecal junction. There was minimal probe tenderness and minimal internal vascularity but no calcification. Those findings were suggestive of ileocecal mass. The differential diagnosis was appendicular lump with localized inflammatory changes. CECT abdomen and pelvis [Fig. 1, Fig. 2(a) and (b)] demonstrated an asymmetric circumferential thickening of the bowel loop involving the transverse colon. The involved segment was approximately 9 cm long and the maximal thickness of the involved transverse colon was 17 mm. The resultant narrowing of the colonic lumen was noted in the same region. The thickened wall showed significant contrast enhancement. In addition, there was a lobulated ill-defined soft tissue mass lesion measuring approximately 4.7 cm × 4 cm × 3 cm in mesentery, in the RIF. The soft tissue mass showed heterogeneous contrast enhancement with marked adjacent fat stranding. Lymph nodes were not enlarged significantly. The liver was normal. The findings were suggestive of carcinoma transverse colon with mesenteric metastasis.

With the provisional diagnosis of complete bowel obstruction secondary to transverse colon carcinoma with mesenteric metastasis, the patient was planned for exploratory laparotomy. Nasogastric tube was placed to decompress the distended bowels. With midline incision, abdominal cavity was accessed. Intraoperatively, there was an 8 cm × 5 cm hard, irregular mass (Fig. 3) with dense adhesion incorporating distal ileum, ileocecal junction, ascending colon and transverse colon. Adjacent mesentery and omentum were also incorporated in the mass. The lump was densely adherent to the lateral abdominal wall. Omental and mesenteric lymph nodes were enlarged. Adhesions were released from adjacent abdominal wall. The patient underwent an extended right hemicolectomy with Complete Mesocolonic Excision (CME) with Central Venous Ligation (CVL). No lymph nodes were palpable intraoperatively and D3 lymphadenectomy was performed. The lump including distal ileum with ileocecal junction, cecum, ascending colon and segment of transverse colon supplied by middle colic artery was resected. Ileo-transverse anastomosis was performed in an end-to-end fashion with double layer sutures via hand sewn technique. An abdominal drain was placed.

Operative specimen showing hard, irregular, desmoplastic lesion incorporating distal ileum (black arrow), ileocecal junction, ascending colon and proximal transverse colon (white arrow) with adjacent mesentery following right extended hemicolectomy.

Postoperatively, the patient was managed in the intensive care unit. Blood transfusion was done on first postoperative day. The patient developed fever secondary to a superficial surgical site infection. Swab culture from the surgical wound showed the growth of Methicillin Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) which was sensitive to Piperacillin+Tazobactam, hence, managed accordingly. The abdominal drain and nasogastric tube were removed on the 3rd postoperative day as the post-operative ileus subsided. Then, oral feeding was started and gradually increased.

Histopathological examination [Fig. 4(a)] revealed suppurative foci suggestive acute suppurative colitis, suggestive of infectious colitis likely actinomycosis. All 32 lymph nodes as harvested showed reactive lymphadenopathy. There was no evidence of atypia, malignancy, or features suggestive of tuberculosis. Examination with Gomori Methenamine Silver (GMS) stain [Fig. 4(b)] depicted positive staining with basophilic filamentous aggregates confirming the diagnosis of colonic actinomycosis involving ascending and transverse colon.

(a) and 4(b): Histopathological examination showing suppurative foci suggestive acute suppurative colitis and Gomori Methenamine Silver (GMS) positive staining with basophilic filamentous aggregates suggestive of actinomycosis.

With the diagnosis of ascending and transverse colon actinomycosis, penicillin G was initiated via intravenous route. The patient was discharged on oral amoxicillin after 2 weeks of surgery. The patient received 3 g of oral amoxicillin in three divided doses daily. On follow-up after 4 months, there were no new symptoms.

Discussion

Actinomycosis is a rare infection with an incidence of less than one person in every 300,000 per year worldwide, owing to significant improvement of personal hygiene, increased availability of antibiotics, and aseptic surgical techniques [3]. Most commonly, the causative organism is a gram-positive microaerophilic and anaerobic bacterium, Actinomyces israellii [4]. However, other species associated with the disease includes A. naeslundii, A. viscosus, A. odontolyticus, A. meyeri, A. gerencseriae and others [5]. The organism resides in the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and vagina [6]. Extension to adjacent structures in immunosuppressive patients is common [7]. Although the cause in the majority of cases is unknown (80.7 %), the abscess may develop after any disruption in mucosal integrity [8]. Recent literature has highlighted an increased risk with biliary and pancreatic stent placement for this infection [9].

Infection involving the cervicofacial area is the most common, followed by abdominal, pelvic and thoracic involvement [10]. The involvement of the gastrointestinal tract in actinomycosis is relatively common. In the majority of cases, the lower parts are involved (ileum, caecum, appendix) whereas gastric localization is rare. Common modes of the presentation include weight loss, malaise, change in bowel habits, abdominal pain and per abdominal mass, which might mimic neoplasia [11]. Our case presented with a pseudotumor mimicking malignancy associated with bowel obstruction. The infection mostly remains localized initially; it then spreads contiguously, infiltrating the tissue planes. Lymphadenopathy is often absent. Hematogenous dissemination occurs rarely [12]. The course of the disease is indolent and is similar to that of other diseases such as appendicitis, diverticulitis, colon carcinoma, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, and tubo-ovarian abscess [1].

The preoperative diagnosis may depend mainly on the findings of imaging modalities. However, the imaging often cannot differentiate between actinomycosis and malignant process, Crohn's disease, appendicitis, diverticulitis, or tuberculosis [12]. Preoperative diagnosis is challenging because of non-specific clinical, laboratory and imaging findings. Preoperative radiologic diagnosis is rarely performed due to a lack of pathognomonic features. Ha et al., after analyzing the CT findings of ten patients with abdominal actinomycosis, concluded that the aggressive nature of invasion and infiltration of contiguous tissues and organs, such as the large intestine, greater omentum, or abdominal wall, was remarkable in abdominal actinomycosis [13]. The findings were comparable to that seen in acute necrotizing pancreatitis [13]. While in our case, there was extensive thickening of the bowel wall with infiltrating mass involving omentum and mesentery. This case report highlights the fact that actinomycosis can present with clinical and imaging features mimicking the malignant mass. It has been suggested that the presence of an infiltrative mass on abdominal CT scans should raise suspicions of a diagnosis of actinomycosis especially in patients with mild constitutional symptoms [13]. Low signal intensity on T2-weighted images may support the diagnosis of actinomycosis but Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) has low specificity. The Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scan depicts a high uptake of glucose by activated granulocytes in infected tissue. However, PET is preferable only to monitor the response of treatment due to low specificity in diagnosis [14].

Isolation of the causative bacteria is achieved in only a minority of cases. Demonstration of Gram-positive filamentous organisms and sulphur granules on histological examination indicate actinomycosis. Direct isolation of the organism from a clinical specimen is usually required for a definitive diagnosis [15]. In many cases, the diagnosis is established after surgical exploration or extensive and difficult debulking of lesion extending to nearby structures such as the bowel, ureters or bladder and pathological examination of specimen [16]. Microbiological culture is usually negative and histopathological examination has a crucial role in differentiating actinomycosis from neoplasm [6]. Actinomyces requires specific incubation of fresh material and takes 1 week to grow. The culture is negative in 76 % of cases. Diagnosis is provided by illustrating characteristic sulphur granules containing filaments in 50 % of the cases which are PAS and GMS-positive. However, this is not strictly a pathognomonic sign because sulphur granules can be produced by other species also such as Staphylococci, Nocardia, Aspergillus and Streptomyces [16]. The filaments of Nocardia are thinner in diameter ranging from 0.5 to 1 μm while that of Actinomyces ranges from 1.0 to 1.5 μm. The branching is right angled in Nocardia while more acute angle in Actinomyces. Nocardia triggers intense neutrophilic reaction while actinomycosis is associated with granulomatous reaction with fibrosis [17]. Recently, new molecular genetic methods such as PCR for 16S rRNA sequencing and fluorescence in situ hybridization have been developed to achieve faster and more accurate identification in reference or research laboratories [18].

A study of 87 clinical isolates in UK demonstrated that most organisms were susceptible to β lactams (such as benzylpenicillin, amoxicillin, ceftriaxone, meropenem, and piperacillin-tazobactam), doxycycline, clindamycin, erythromycin, and clarithromycin [19]. Some strains have shown resistance against quinolones and tetracycline. The most preferable first choice remains intravenous 10–20 million units per day of penicillin G over 2 to 6 weeks, followed by oral penicillin V or amoxicillin for 6 to 12 months [15]. Evidence increasingly advocates that medical therapy is the main line of treatment, irrespective of the degree or severity of the infection [12]. Although no surgical intervention guidelines have been established, operative treatment has been indicated in patients with extensive necrotic tissue or large abscesses that cannot be adequately drained [8]. Complete bowel obstruction secondary to pseudotumor or extensive infiltration like in our case might be a rare indication for surgical intervention. The wide spectrum of presentation of colonic actinomycosis indicates the need of multidisciplinary approach of management involving both the surgeons and infectious disease experts.

Follow-up examinations with imaging studies such as ultrasound or CT to aid with duration of therapy are recommended in order to determine the response to treatment or development of any complications [8]. The prognosis is usually excellent in healthy patients, hence, abdominal actinomycosis should be in differential diagnoses of suspected abdominal malignancy [20].

Conclusion

Colonic actinomycosis is a rare infection of the large bowel with a wide spectrum of clinical symptoms, even mimicking infiltrating malignant lesion which might pose diagnostic inaccuracy. Hence, colonic actinomycosis should be considered as differential diagnosis in case of an infiltrative mass lesion even though the infection is rare. Acute presentations like abscess and bowel obstruction might indicate surgical exploration though medical therapy alone suffice in most cases.
