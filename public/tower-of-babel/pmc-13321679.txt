Parallel and comparative transcriptome and proteome provide insight into degeneration-related key candidates in Agaricus bisporus
Authors: Yuanping Lu, Huiqing Zheng, Binrong Ke, Zhongjie Guo, Zhixin Cai, Zhiheng Zeng, Wenzhi Chen, Hui Zeng, Meiyuan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpawn degeneration is a widespread phenomenon during the production process of edible fungi, such as Agaricus bisporus. The degeneration of substrate-decomposing ability brings about the inability of decomposition and utilization of substrate, the weakened growth of mycelium, and decline of production capacity. In order to explore the underlying mechanism and key factors regulating spawn degeneration, we investigated transcriptomic and proteomic alterations using high-throughput RNA sequencing and tandem mass tag-based (TMT) quantitative proteomics technology in A. bisporus degenerated strains 2796-3, 2796-5 and normal strain As2796.ResultsWe identified a total of 257 and 635 DEGs in degenerated strains 2796-3 and 2796-5. Furthermore, we analyzed alteration in translation level and obtained 228 and 290 DEPs. Among these differentially expressed genes/proteins, there were 117 genes/proteins involved with substrate degradation and 16 transcription factors. Integrative analysis of proteome and transcriptome revealed that the top 25 KEGG pathways with the largest sum of DEPs and DEGs were roughly the same, of which 20 pathways were common in the two degenerated strains. We found that the majority of DEGs/DEPs involved in biosynthesis of amino acid, arginine and proline metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, and MAPK signaling pathway showed downregulation in degenerated strains.ConclusionsOur results suggested that transcription factors, biosynthesis of amino acid, arginine and proline metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, as well as MAPK signaling pathway might be associated with strain degeneration in A. bisporus. This study provided valuable databases for identifying potential candidate genes and pathways associated with degeneration. Furthermore, it would lay a foundation for molecular breeding of new A. bisporus strains with improvement of substrate utilization, anti-degenerated trait and yield.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12813-9.Keywords: Mushroom, Degenerated strains, Compost, Degradation of substrate

Background

Mushrooms have been widely consumed and appreciated for humankind owing to their high nutritional value, delicious flavor, and medicinal properties [1–3]. Mushroom cultivation not only converts biomass wastes into protein-rich mushrooms, but also produces economic benefits [2]. In 2013, the value of mushroom industry was estimated to be approximately $63 billion [4].

Agaricus bisporus, one of the most important edible mushrooms, is widely cultivated worldwide with an output of 4.43 billion kg in 2013, mainly produced in China (54%), the USA (9%), Poland (7%), the Netherlands (6%) and India (6%) [4]. Similar to the other edible mushroom, the production of A. bisporus requires elite spawns which are as important as seeds for plants. However, spawns are usually prone to degeneration which is often characterized by weakened growth, delayed fructification, decreased resistance and yield [2, 5]. Sectors, clustering variation and fluffy-shaped mycelium were described in degenerated strains of A. bisporus [6–8]. The characteristics of sectors included altered compost colonization, reduced yield, and inferior quality fruit body [6]. Chromosomal abnormalities including loss of heterozygosity, chromosomal translocations, somatic recombination, etc. were found to be involved in sectors [6]. Clustering variation manifested as clustered and malformed fruit body [7]. Dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase (DLDH) was identified and thought to be related to the clustering variation of A. bisporus [7].

Chen and teammates further reported degeneration occurred in A. bisporus displaying altered compost colonization on compost substrate, and lose of productivity of fruit body [9, 10]. For A. bisporus production, spawns are cultivated on compost substrate providing nutrients. The degenerated strains grew well on PDA, while showed abnormal growth on compost which ultimately led to nonfruiting. Though 9 different fragments and 2 proteins were identified in degenerated strains [9, 10], a global understanding of the differentially expressed genes or proteins and related regulatory pathways is still lacking.

Advances in omics technologies (e.g., transcriptome and proteome) have facilitated comprehensive investigation of gene and protein expression. Recently, RNA-seq has not only been widely adopted to identified candidate genes associated with growth, fruit body formation and development and stress response [11–15], but also used to unravel the potential genes and pathways involved in strain degeneration [16–20]. Differentially expressed genes associated with energy metabolism, toxin biosynthesis, MAPK signaling pathway, substrate degradation, amino acid synthesis and metabolism were linked to strain degeneration [16–18]. Additionally, proteomic techniques have also been successfully employed to explore the mechanism of degeneration in Pleurotus ostreatus [21]. The double-strand breaks (DSBs) would give rise to the strain degeneration in the subcultured mycelium of P. ostreatus [21]. Whereas, one technology was thought to be insufficient to comprehensively investigate any physiological pathway [22]. Combination of transcriptome and proteome has been treated as a powerful strategy and widely applied to identified genes and mechanism involved in development, stress response, postharvest deterioration [23–28]. In this study, we adopted transcriptomics and proteomics to mine genes and proteins associated with degeneration of A. bisporus. The transcriptome and proteome data would enrich the current A. bisporus database and offer clues to investigate underlying mechanisms of this degeneration, which would provide the basis for early prediction and monitor of A. bisporus during genetic breeding, preservation and spawn making.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture

The normal strain As2796 and degenerated strains 2796-3 and 2796-5 growing well on PDA, while abnormally on compost [9] were provided by Fujian Academy of Agricultural Sciences. All of them were cultured as previously described [9], after which mycelium was harvested and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at -80 ℃.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from samples using Trizol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Each strain had three biological replicates, generating a total of 9 samples. The concentration and quality of total RNA were evaluated using Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Agient 2100 (PerkinElmer, USA) according to the RNA integrity value (RIN), respectively. Total RNA of each sample was sequenced at Biomarker Technologies, Beijing, China. For generating clean data, the pair-end raw data were used to filter adapter reads, reads containing unknown sequences and low-quality reads [29]. Subsequently, clean data was aligned to the A. bisporus geonome (https://genome.jgi.doe.g.ov/Agabi_varbisH97_2/Agabi_varbisH97_2.home.html) using software Hisat2.0.4. Cufflinks2.1.1 was adopted to predict novel genes.

Screening differentially expressed genes

The gene expression level of each sample was calculated using HTSeqv0.6.1 package with union model and FPKM (expected number of Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript sequence per Millions base pairs sequenced) [30]. Differential expression analysis between two groups was performed using the DESeq1.10.1 package [31] with the threshold of |log2(FoldChange)| ≥ 1 and padj <0.05 [29]. Then, GO functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were implemented using GOseq and KOBAS (2.0) software.

Total protein extraction, quantification and trypsin digestion

Samples of mycelia were ground individually using liquid nitrogen and transferred to centrifuge tubes containing lysis buffer, and then lysed using ultrasonication on ice for 5 min. Three biological replicates were conducted for each strain. After centrifugation at 4℃ and 12000 g for 15 min, the supernatant was collected and incubated at 56 °C 1 h after adding 10 mmol/L DTT (DL-Dithiothreitol), followed by adding IAM (iodoacetamide) and incubation at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. Subsequently, 4 volumes of precooled acetone were added, and then samples were incubated at -20℃ for at least 2 h and centrifuged at 4℃. The precipitation was collected and were resuspended with 1 mL of pre-cooled acetone followed by centrifugation at 4℃. After air-drying, dissolution buffer was added to dissolve the pelleted proteins. The quantification of protein was carried out using Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. SDS-PAGE was employed to evaluate the quality of extracted protein. For digestion of protein, the dissolution buffer was added to a final volume of 100 µL for each sample. And then, the protein solution was digested with 3 µL of trypsin (1 μg/μL) and 500 μL of 50 mmol/L TEAB buffer at 37 °C overnight.

TMT labeling and LC-MS/MS analysis

After digestion, peptides were desalted using C18 desalting column and labeled using TMT® Mass Tagging Kits and Reagents (Thermo Fisher, USA) with 98% TMT labeling efficiency assessed by the following formula: unique peptides modified by TMT labeling/total identified peptides × 100%. TMT labeling was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol.

After L-3000-HPLC separation using Waters BEH C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) with gradient elution, LC-MS/MS analysis for separated peptides was conducted at Novogene (Beijing, China) using an EASY-nLC™ 1200 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher, USA) coupled with a Q Exactive™ series mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, USA), in which the data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode was employed.

Proteomic data processing and screening differentially expressed proteins

The resulting spectra data was queried against the genome of A. bisporus using Proteome Discoverer 2.2 (PD 2.2, Thermo Fisher, USA) with the searching parameters: precursor ions mass tolerance of 10 ppm; 0.02 Da of mass tolerance for fragment ions; carbamidomethyl as static modification; oxidation of methionine and TMT plex as dynamic modifications; acetylation and TMT 10-plex of tyrosine as N-terminal. And two was set as maximum number of missed cleavage sites.

To improve the quality of analytical result, PD 2.2 was adapted to further filter the search results. The Peptide Spectrum Matches (PSMs) matching with a credibility of more than 99% and proteins containing at least 1 unique peptide were retained and further validated with a false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤ 1.0%. After protein quantitation, T-test was performed and proteins with P value ≤ 0.05 and fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or FC ≤ 0.67 were identified as differentially expressed proteins (DEPs).

For functional annotation, GO (Gene Ontology) and InterPro (IPR) analyses were implemented using Interproscan program against Gene Ontology database (http://www.geneontology.org/) and nonredundant protein databases, respectively. COG annotation and KEGG pathway were conducted using blastp (evalue ≤ 1e− 4) against Clusters of Orthologous Groups database and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Synthesis of cDNA was carried out using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time) (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Actin was used as internal reference gene. Primer Prmier 5.0 was accepted for primer design [32] and primers were listed in Supplementary Table 1. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted on CFX Connect real-time system (Bio-Rad, USA) using TB GreenTM Premix ExTaq TM (TliRNaseHPlus) (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. And we calculated the gene expression level using 2−ΔΔct method [33].

Results

In order to investigate the transcriptional differences, high-throughput sequencing was conducted for RNA samples isolated from mycelium of degenerated and normal strains. After removing low-quality reads, we totally obtained 189,860,728 clean reads and 23.92 Gb sequencing data. Error rate of each sample was no more than 0.02% (Table 1). A Q20% of each sample was over 95.63%. Correlation analysis showed good consistency among three biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. 1). These data suggested that the quality of our sample data was high enough for following investigation.

Identification and functional analysis of DEGs

After screening with the criterion of |log2FC|>=1 and padj < 0.05, a total of 257 DEGs in the group 2796-3 vs. As2796, of which 108 were up-regulated and 149 were down-regulated, respectively (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table 2). In the group 2796-5 vs. As2796, we obtained 635 DEGs. Among these 635 DEGs, 331 and 304 were up-regulated and down-regulated, respectively (Fig. 1A and and Supplementary Table 3). 198 DEGs were overlapped between 2796-3 vs. As2796 and 2796-5 vs. As2796 group (Fig. 1B).

Analysis of differentially expressed genes. A Volcano plot of DEGs. B Venn diagram of DEGs. C GO annotation of DEGs in 2796-3 vs. As2796. D GO annotation of DEGs in 2796-5 vs. As2796. E KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs in 2796-3 vs. As2796. F KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs in 2796-5 vs. As2796

To understand the potential function of DEGs, GO annotation and KEGG analysis were conducted in each comparison group. GO annotation showed that the DEGs in the two comparison groups were mainly enriched in biological process and molecular function. For molecular function, DEGs were significantly involved in heme binding, tetrapyrrole binding, oxidoreductase activity and iron ion binding in 2796-3 vs. As2796 (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Table 4). The DEGs were significantly related to oxidoreductase activity, iron ion binding, heme binding, tetrapyrrole binding, peroxidase activity, cofactor binding, transition metal ion transmembrane, iron ion transmembrane transporter and antioxidant activity in 2796-5 vs. As2796 (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Table 5). For biological process, the significantly enriched GO terms were involved in oxidation-reduction process, single-organism process, transition metal ion transport, iron ion transport and single-organism metabolic process in 2796-5 vs. As2796 (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Table 5), while only oxidation-reduction process was significantly enriched in 2796-3 vs. As2796 (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Table 4).

KEGG analysis displayed that DEGs in 2796-3 vs. As2796 were mainly enriched in tryptophan metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, metabolic pathways, MAPK signaling pathway, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, arginine and proline metabolism (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Table 6). DEGs in 2796-5 vs. As2796 were mainly related to tryptophan metabolism, tyrosine metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, metabolic pathways, glutathione metabolism, etc (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Table 7).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation of selected DEGs

To validate the accuracy of the transcriptome, qRT-PCR was conducted for 4 randomly selected genes. The result of qRT-PCR displayed that the expression of these genes was similar with RNA-seq data (Fig. 2), indicating the reliability of DEGs.

Proteomics overview

Proteomic sequencing provided 304,708 spectra and 59,942 matched spectrums (Fig. 3A). After removing peptides and proteins with FDR > = 1%, we obtained 35,670 peptides and 4,622 proteins (Fig. 3A). The molecular weight of these proteins ranged from 5.6 kDa to 432.2 kDa, and most of them (89.27%) were from 10 kDa to 110 kDa (Fig. 3B). GO annotation of these identified proteins showed that for molecular function, the majority of proteins were found in protein binding, ATP binding, oxidoreductase activity, and nucleic acid binding. In cellular component, numerous proteins were associated with integral component of membrane, nucleus, membrane, and ribosome. For biological process, the identified proteins were mainly enriched in oxidation-reduction process, metabolic process, protein phosphorylation, and transmembrane transport (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table 8). KEGG pathway annotation displayed that the most proteins were involved in metabolism including amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, global and overview maps and lipid metabolism, followed by genetic information processing related with translation, folding, sorting and degradation, and transcription, as well as transport catabolism under the term of cellular processes (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Table 9).

Identification and functional annotation of DEPs

According to the threshold value (fold change ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67, P value ≤ 0.05), a total of 228 and 290 DEPs were identified in the comparison 2796-3 vs. As2796 and 2796-5 vs. As2796, respectively. Among these DEPs, 171 and 182 were up-regulated in 2796-3 and 2796-5, respectively, whereas 57 and 108 were down-regulated (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table 10 and 11). And 155 DEPs were overlapped between 2796-3 vs. As2796 and 2796-5 vs. As2796 group (Fig. 4B).

E KEGG enrichment analysis of DEPs in 2796-3 vs. As2796. F KEGG enrichment analysis of DEPs in 2796-5 vs. As2796

GO annotation revealed that DEPs in 2796-3 vs. As2796 were significantly associated with oxidation-reduction process, single-organism metabolic process, carbohydrate metabolic process, single-organism process under the biological process term. For molecular function, the majority of proteins were related to oxidoreductase activity, heme binding, transition metal ion binding, iron ion binding, and catalytic activity, etc. (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Table 12). In the group of 2796-5 vs. As2796, DEPs were significantly enriched in oxidation-reduction process, single-organism metabolic process, transmembrane transport and carbohydrate metabolic process under the biological process term (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Table 13). For molecular function, oxidoreductase activity, heme binding, peroxidase activity, catalytic activity and antioxidant activity, etc. (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Table 13). However, there was no DEPs significantly found in cellular component.

KEGG analysis displayed that DEPs identified from 2796-3 vs. As2796 were significantly related to steroid biosynthesis, biosynthesis of antibiotics, fructose and mannose metabolism, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, carbon metabolism, metabolic pathways and methane metabolism (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Table 14). For 2796-5 vs. As2796, DEPs were significantly enriched in biosynthesis of antibiotics, steroid biosynthesis, metabolic pathways, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, fatty acid biosynthesis, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Table 15).

Integrative analysis of proteome and transcriptome

To understand the potential degeneration mechanisms, we compared proteome and transcriptome to investigate the coherence KEGG pathway. The result showed that the top 25 KEGG pathways with the largest sum of DEPs and DEGs were roughly the same, of which 20 pathways were common in the group 2796-3 vs. As2796 (Fig. 5A) and 2796-5 vs. As2796 (Fig. 5B). These 20 pathways were mainly categorized into two groups: protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum was involved with genetic information processing; other pathways (steroid biosynthesis, starch and sucrose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, glycerolipid metabolism, etc) were grouped into metabolism. These 20 pathways may be involved in strain degeneration.

Amino acid biosynthesis and metabolism

In amino acid metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism and tryptophan metabolism contained the largest sum of DEPs and DEGs. Totally, 11 differentially expressed factors were generated, of which 9 and 8 identified in arginine and proline metabolism and tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 6A), respectively. The heatmap displayed that compared with normal strain As2796, the expression levels of proline iminopeptidases involved in arginine and proline metabolism and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase involved in tryptophan metabolism were upregulated in the degenerated strains, while 4-hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate aldolase and aromatic amino acid aminotransferase were downregulated in degenerated strains (Fig. 6B). Amidase and aldehyde dehydrogenase were commonly identified in arginine and proline metabolism and tryptophan metabolism. And the expression of amidase was downregulated in degenerated strains. Moreover, 3 of 4 aldehyde dehydrogenase were downregulated in degenerated strains.

As for biosynthesis of amino acid, 9 genes were significantly differentially expressed between degenerated strains and normal strain (Fig. 6A). Among them, cystathionine gamma-lyase (estExt_Genewise1Plus.C_90816) was significantly up-expressed at both the transcriptional and translational levels. Isocitrate dehydrogenase (fgenesh2_kg.10_192_2924_1_CFAF_CFAG_CFAH_EXTA) and S-adenosylmethionine synthase (estExt_fgenesh2_kg.C_20132) showed upregulation at transcriptional level and translational level, respectively (Fig. 6C). The remaining six genes displayed downregulation (Fig. 6A and C). Triosephosphate isomerase (estExt_fgenesh2_kg.C_120172), enolase (estExt_fgenesh2_kg.C_10166), ATP phosphoribosyltransferase (estExt_fgenesh2_kg.C_70161), Fructose-bisphosphate aldolase (estExt_fgenesh2_kg.C_40549) and phosphoglycerate kinase (estExt_fgenesh2_kg.C_20543) showed downregulation of protein levels in degenerated strains. The expression of estExt_fgenesh2_kg.C_40256 encoding aromatic amino acid aminotransferase was only downregulated at transcriptional level (Fig. 6C).

Differentially expressed genes related to MAPK signaling pathway

On the MAPK signaling pathway, 5 DEGs involved in MAPK pathway were obtained (Fig. 7), of which 2 genes (Genemark.2980_g, e_gw1.12.197.1) encoding pheromone B alpha 1 receptor and 1 gene enoding STE3-type pheromone receptor (Novel00105) were down regulated in degenerated strains. Whereas the other two genes encoding STE12 (estExt_fgenesh2_pg.C_90369) and CDK1 (cyclin-dependent kinase 1) (estExt_Genewise1.C_130431) were upregulated in degenerated strains. Similar to Cordyceps militaris, the differential expression of genes involved in MAPK pathway might contribute to the morphological change in degenerated strains.

Enzymes involved in substrate decomposition

After annotation and further investigation of DEGs and DEPs, we identified 117 differentially expressed candidates associated with degradation of substrate. We classified these candidates into two categories, carbohydrate-acting enzymes (CAZymes, including GH, GT, PL, CE, CBM and AA) and lignin-related oxidoreductases except for oxidoreductases belonging to AA. For CAZymes, 18 showed downregulation in degenerated strains, of which 2 CBM-GHs, 10 GHs, 1 GT and 3 AA were downregulated at transcriptional level, whereas 1 GT was significantly down-expressed at both the transcriptional and translational levels, 1 AA showed down-expression at protein level (Fig. 8A). 42 genes including 1 PL, 1 EXPN, 4 CEs, 1 CBM-GH, 2 CBMs, 2 GTs, 17 AA and 14 GHs showed significantly up-regulation in degenerated strains. For lignin-related oxidoreductases, 1 ODC, 2 of 7 GSTs, and 11 of 34 Cytochrome P450s (CYPs) showed downregulation in degenerated strains (Fig. 8B). The remaining 43 of lignin-related oxidoreductases showed upregulation in degenerated strains. These DEGs might play a pivotal role in strain degeneration process.

Discussion

In crops, the quality of seeds is important for cultivation and the yield [34]. Similarly, the cultivation of mushroom needs excellent spawn serving as “seed” [2]. The stability of strains is really important to spawn producers and mushroom growers. However, during the processes of preservation, spawn production and cultivation, degeneration frequently occurs in strains displaying weakened growth and resistance, delayed fruit body formation and reduced yields [5, 35]. For example, strain degeneration during subculture and the change in the morphological characteristics have been described in C. militaris [16, 18] and Volvariella volvacea [17]. Chen and partners reported degeneration-related characterization in Morchella importuna after long-term [36].

The diverse phenomenon of degeneration was recorded in A. bisporus including strong yield depressions, abnormal growth, inferior quality mushrooms and clustering variation [6, 7, 37–39]. Our team previously obtained two degenerated strains derived from A. bisporus strain As2796 [9, 10]. These two degenerated strains grew well on PDA which was similar with the normal strain As2796 on PDA, whereas the degenerated strains displayed degeneration phenomenon including abnormal growth and nonfruiting on compost [9]. Previously, our team cloned 9 different fragments with high homologous to an ATP-binding cassette transporter subfamily A protein, carbohydrate esterase family 4 protein, chitin deacetylase, acetyl xylan esterase II and hypothetical proteins [9]. Actin and a putative NADH dehydrogenase iron-sulfur protein 3 with upregulated-expression pattern and downregulated-expression pattern were identified using two-dimensional electrophoresis (2-DE) in degenerated strains [10]. Although these studies provided genes and proteins, the biological mechanisms involved in this degeneration are not well understood. In this study, the transcriptome and TMT-based proteome analysis were performed to investigate the underlying mechanism associated with substrate degradation ability degeneration im A. bisporus.

The commercial cultivation of A. bisporus was conducted on compost substrate which provided nutrients for the growth and development of A. bisporus. The straw compost contains insoluble polysaccharide materials of plant cell walls such as lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, etc [2]. Prior to absorption and utilization, these materials, however, are required to be broken down by extracellular enzymes into soluble compounds for the growth and development [2, 5] . The CAZymes have been showed to be important for the hydrolysis of plant cell wall polysaccharides and substrate degradation processes [40, 41]. 115 transcripts of GHs, CEs and PLs were showed up-regulation in compost [40]. Lignin-related oxidoreductases functioning on lignin degradation have also been assessed by the team of Morin [40]. PODs, HTPs, β-etherases, laccases, and CYPs, etc., showed highly up-regulated in compost relative to defined medium and were thought to act an important role in lignin of degradation and related compounds [40]. In this study, we identified 117 differentially expressed factors involved in substrate degradation in A. bisporus including 60 CAZymes and 57 lignin-related oxidoreductases. The majority of factors such as HTPs, CYPs, MCOs, GHs, CEs, etc., were found to be upregulated in degenerated strains. Cellulose is the main carbon source required for the growth of edible mushroom [5]. The end product of cellulose decomposition is glucose which enters the glycolytic pathway for metabolism [2, 5]. However, most of DEPs/DEGs involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (Supplemental Fig.S2) were found to be downregulated in degenerated strains which could lead to accumulation of degradation products, potentially cause feedback inhibition and deficiency of ATP and NADH, ultimately weak growth of degenerated strain on compost. The abnormal number of upregulated genes related to substrate degradation might struggle to compensate energy for survival in degenerated strains.

Similar with other mushroom, the growth of A. bisporus, however, requires carbon source while also needs nitrogen source acting as as one of the most important nutrients [2, 5]. Amino acids can be used as one of the organic nitrogen sources and as important factors regulating gene expression, protein phosphorylation cascade [42]. Functional amino acids, such as arginine, proline, cysteine, and tryptophan, regulate crucial metabolic pathways which are required for maintenance, growth, and reproduction [42]. Arginine and its metabolites are essential for the growth, sensing and adaption to the change of environment in plants [43]. And amino acids have already been proved valuable for rejuvenation of degeneration [44]. Our current study displayed that the expression of the majority of factors enriched in biosynthesis of amino acid was downregulated which could contribute to the decreased amino acid synthesis giving rise to the deficiency in nutrition, ultimately forbidding mycelium growth and fruiting body formation on the straw compost. On the other hand, the expression of the arginine and proline metabolism pathway, and tryptophan metabolism was downregulated which could result in reduced degradation of arginine, proline and tryptophan to accumulate these functional amino acids for growth of mycelium in degenerated strains.

The Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPKs) signaling pathway, highly conserved mechanisms of cellular signaling, plays important roles in the regulation of different physiological and developmental processes, including mating, growth, morphogenesis, stress response, sporulation [45]. The MAPK signaling pathway has been recently linked to degeneration [18, 21, 46–48]. PaASK1, one of MAPK cascades, mediated cell degeneration and cell differentiation in Podospora anserina [46]. P. anserina strains mutated in PaASK1 showed defects in mycelium pigmentation, differentiation of aerial hyphae and formation of fruiting bodies [46]. Three Slt2-MAPK cascade mutants of Metarhizium robertsii displayed culture degeneration with sectors producing no conidia [47]. Zhang and Zhuang [48] demonstrated that Hog1-MAPK gene deletion mutants of Trichoderma brevicrassum exhibited conidiophores degeneration and spare conidia. Transcriptome analysis provided dysregulated transcription of MAPK signaling pathway in the degenerated strain of C. militaris, which could participate with phenotypic changes in the degenerated strain, such as reduced radial growth and loss of fruit body formation [18]. Similar to C. militaris, MAPK pathway was significantly enriched among DEGs, which might contribute to the morphological change in degenerated strains in A. bisporus.

Transcription factors functioning as switches play an essential actor in link signaling pathway between target genes expression [18, 45]. A subset of biological functions of TFs has been recently verified to participated in regulating growth, development, metabolism, and degeneration [35, 49, 50, 51]. In Fusarium oxysporum, the development could be mediated by GATA-type transcription factor Pro1 via integrating signals from MAPK pathways [52]. Floss and co-workers found that MYB-like transcription factor MYB1 modulated genes expression, which ultimately regulated arbuscule degeneration [53]. The expression of several genes associated with degeneration was reduced in myb1 but was increased in roots constitutively overexpressing MYB1 [53]. And subsequent development of symbiosis was impaired in roots constitutively overexpressing MYB1 [53]. Gautier et al. have evidenced transcription factor PaPro1 had a role in controlling sexual development and cell degeneration in Podospora anserina [51]. Wang et al. found that the deletion of the TF genes (such as Zn2Cys6-type TF, APSES-type TF) could alter the parental strains with the phenotypes which were largely similar to the degenerate cultures [35]. In present study, we screened out 16 differentially expressed transcription factors, including bHLH, MYB, GATA, NF-YB, C2H2, and bZIP. The current study found that the growth of degenerated strains 2796-3 and 2796-5 was similar to normal strain As2796 on PDA, while was weaker than As2796 on compost medium. Similar to the growth of mycelium, the DEGs and DEPs were very rare on PDA (unpublished), whereas large number of them were identified on compost medium. Given the function of MAPK signaling pathway in cellular responding to environmental stress [54] and the linkage of TFs between MAPK signaling pathway and target genes expression [18, 48] we speculated that TFs could response to the dysregulated transcription of MAPK signaling when strains incubated on compost medium and regulated the expression of down stream components (e.g. biosynthesis of amino acid, arginine and proline metabolism, tryptophan metabolism), bringing out the phenotype change in degenerated strains in A. bisporus. We will confirm the regulatory role of TFs in strain degeneration in further investigation.

Strain degeneration severely jeopardizes industrialized production of A. bisporus which ultimately gives rise to an economic loss on a tremendous scale. The early detection and monitoring of degenerated strains is of great significance for the breeding, conservation and production of A. bisporus. This study shed light on the comprehensive difference between degenerated strains and normal strain at transcriptomic and proteomic level in A. bisporus. 117 genes/proteins involved in substrate degradation and 16 transcription factors were found to be differentially expressed. The majority of DEGs/DEPs associated with biosynthesis of amino acid, arginine and proline metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, and MAPK signaling pathway showed downregulation in degenerated strains, suggesting a potential regulating effect on strain degeneration in A. bisporus. This study offered resources of A. bisporus transcripts and proteins, and provided clues for further study of degeneration in A. bisporus. The DEGs/DEPs and pathways identified in this study could be used as indicators for strain degeneration in industrial production of A. bisporus. The result could provide targets for the directional molecular breeding of A. bisporus strains with improvement of yield and anti-degeneration traits.
