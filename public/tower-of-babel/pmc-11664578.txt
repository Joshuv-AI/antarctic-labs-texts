Beneficial Soil Fungus Kills Predatory Nematodes with Dehydropeptides Translocating into the Animal Gut
Authors: Hannah Büttner, Johannes Rassbach, Constanze Schultz, Jürgen Popp, Markus Gressler, Christian Hertweck
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMortierella alpina is a mold fungus that has gained attention for its positive correlation with soil health, plant growth, and applications as a crop biocontrol agent to suppress the threats of nematode pests. To date, the mechanisms underlying the protective traits of M. alpina against these plant parasites have remained elusive. Here we report that abundantly produced peptidic biosurfactants, malpinin A–D, exhibit robust inhibitory activity against nematodes. Nematode assays with malpinin congeners and chemically synthesized analogues revealed that the dehydro amino acid is critical for activity, whereas the N-terminal amino acid residues modulate the lipophilicity. Complementary imaging by fluorescence microscopy and Raman microspectroscopy, using externally fluorescence-labeled, semisynthetic malpinin or a biosynthetically alkyne-tagged probe generated by precursor-directed biosynthesis, visualized the translocation and enrichment of malpinin in the gut of the model nematode Caenorhabditis elegans. Our findings provide valuable insight into the use of M. alpina as a biocontrol agent, emphasizing the ecologically significant role of malpinins as a protective trait. In addition to solving a long-standing riddle, these findings have translational value for applications in agriculture.

Introduction

Among the most significant plant pathogens responsible for a considerable reduction in crop yields in agricultural settings are plant-parasitic nematodes. These parasites are pervasive in terrestrial ecosystems and have a detrimental impact on plant growth and development.1,2 As the harmful effects of toxic chemicals on the environment and human health become more widely recognized, there is a growing appreciation for environmentally friendly alternatives in agriculture.3−6

In the pursuit for sustainable plant protection, particular phyla of early diverging fungi, including soil fungi of the genus Mortierella, have gained considerable interest due to their positive correlation with soil health and their potential applications in biocontrol strategies.7−9Mortierella species stand out for their capacity to improve soil health through the reduction of xenobiotic metabolites, facilitation of nutrient cycling, and promotion of plant growth.8−10 They are found in ecosystems across the globe,11 and their presence has been correlated with increased plant fitness, enhanced stress tolerance and growth rates of plants,10,12,13 which has been attributed to the production of growth-promoting metabolites14 and phytohormones.15 However, Mortierella species are particularly appreciated for their ability to reduce soil-dwelling parasites.16,17

There are different strategies that these fungi employ to control nematodes. Mortierella globalpina has the unique ability to form nematode-trapping hyphae, which immobilize the animals, and subsequently parasitize the nematodes and their eggs.18Podila verticillata (former Mortierella verticillata), on the other hand, harbors endosymbiotic bacteria that produce potent anthelminthic metabolites (cytotoxic necroximes19,20 and symbiosin21) that protect the symbionts against fungivorous nematodes.19,21 Interestingly, this strategy is shared with other members of the phylum Mucoromycota, specifically Rhizopus microsporus containing toxin-producing endofungal bacteria.22

Another important biocontrol agent is Mortierella alpina, which is prominently used in biotechnology and acknowledged as a safe strain without risks for humans.23M. alpina efficiently controls root-knot nematodes and safeguards tomato plants.17 However, the underlying mechanistic basis for this activity has remained unknown, as neither nematode trapping hyphae nor the presence of toxin-producing endosymbionts have been documented for this species.24,25M. alpina is known to produce a range of specialized nonribosomal peptides, malpicyclins (1–4),25 malpibaldins (5 and 6),26 insecticidal cycloacetamides (7 and 8),27 surfactant malpinins (9–12),26 malpikynines,26 and antimycobacterial calpinactams28 (Figure S1). Yet, the potential protective functions of these metabolites against nematodes have remained elusive. Here we reveal the active nematocidal principle of the biocontrol fungus M. alpina, elucidate structural features relevant for its bioactivity, and visualize its translocation in the nematode. Understanding the molecular principle of M. alpina’s anthelminthic activity sets the basis for its use as a biocontrol agent against plant parasites.

Results and Discussion

To gain insight into the biocontrol capabilities of M. alpina, we prioritized the molecular protection strategy against nematodes. In a propagation assay we examined the number of fungivorous nematodes (Aphelenchus avenae) that survive in coculture with M. alpina (ATCC32222). For comparison, we performed a similar cocultivation experiment with the taxonomically close fungus Mortierella indohii (CBS 460.75),29 which differs from M. alpina in having a distinct, low metabolic profile especially lacking the production of 1–12. We confronted both fungal strains individually with fungivorous nematodes and monitored their fate over 2 weeks. By microscopic evaluation, we observed a drastically reduced propagation of nematodes in the presence of M. alpina. In contrast, M. indohii was overgrown with high numbers of nematodes (Figure 1A and 1B).

To identify potential protective compounds secreted by M. alpina against nematode predators, we subjected all of its identified specialized metabolites individually to in vitro bioassays. Specifically, we tested a mix of the nonribosomal peptides malpicyclins A–D (1–4),25 malpibaldin A and B (5 and 6),26 the cycloacetamides A and B (7 and 8),27 and the malpinins A–D (9–12)26 (Figure 1C and Figure S1), latter being the most abundant detectable metabolites in M. alpina cultures.

For the quantification of the anthelminthic activities of the purified compounds, we used Caenorhabditis elegans, since the bioactivity assays with this model nematode are well-established and standardized, allowing a comparison of potencies. Whereas no activity was detectable for 1–8 at concentrations up to 100 μg mL–1, the malpinins (9–12) clearly affected the nematodes. We determined the highest potency for malpinin A (9) with an IC50 of 5.45 μg mL–1 (IC50 6.35 μM, 6.08–6.66 μM confidence interval (CI); Figure 1D). The potency of malpinin B (10), in which the second leucine is exchanged with valine, is only slightly reduced compared to 9, with an IC50 of 7.22 μg mL–1 (IC50 8.55 μM, 7.34 μM–10.48 μM CI) (Figure 1C and 1D). However, the two congeners 11 and 12, in which the first (and second) leucine is exchanged with valine, have a substantially lower activity (11: IC50 26.81 μg mL–1 ≙ 31.75 μM, 27.45–36.46 μM CI; 12: IC50 24.53 μg mL–1 ≙ 29.54 μM, 25.23–34.28 μM CI; Figure 1C and 1D).

We evaluated the impact of 9 by comparing the proliferation of C. elegans stage L3–4 larvae on solid agar plates, provided with sufficient food, to nematodes on plates containing the quadrupled IC50 concentration of 9. Whereas wells with untreated nematodes were filled with freshly hatched larvae after a few days, nematodes in wells containing malpinin were dead or barely moving, and only very few larvae hatched (Figure S2). Since it is not feasible to genetically modify M. alpina to generate targeted null mutants and thus evaluate the effect of different chemotypes, we tested whether malpinin could confer nematode protection to M. indohii. When supplementing M. indohii cultures with malpinin A, we observed a concentration-dependent protective effect in propagation assays with A. avenae (Figure 1E, Figure S3). These data suggest an important role for malpinins in nematode biocontrol by M. alpina.

The different IC50 values of the individual malpinin congeners suggest that the branched amino acids (Leu) have an influence on the anthelminthic potency. However, a chemically synthesized, truncated variant of 9, N-acetyl-d-leucyl-d-arginyl-d-leucine amide (13, Figure 1F), does not show any activity against C. elegans (Figure S10). Interestingly, the anthelminthic activities of the malpinin congeners correlate inversely with the critical micelle concentration of the malpinins, which increases from 9 to 1226 and correlates with decreasing hydrophobicity. To gain insight into the function of the hydrophobic moieties, we performed a titration experiment in which we exposed the nematodes to a steady concentration of malpinin A (11.6 μM ≙ 2 × IC50) and added increasing concentrations of 13 (0–50 μM; Figure S10). Since the addition of 13 does not rescue the anthelminthic activity against C. elegans, it is unlikely that 13 competes with 9 for a specific binding site. Changes in the N-terminal part of the malpinins therefore rather influence the solubility and bioavailability of the compound.

The dehydrobutyrine Michael acceptor appeared to be the best candidate for the activity-conferring residue of the malpinins. To test this, we synthesized a variant, in which the Z-dehydrobutyrine moiety is replaced by aminobutyrine (Abu; Figure S11). Unexpected stability issues with the resulting Abu-malpinin A (14) gave an inseparable mixture of 14 and its oxidatively degraded product Abu-malpikynin A (15, Figure S12). However, in the liquid C. elegans bioassay the mixture did not show any nematocidal activity up to 50 μM, which is the 10-fold IC50 of 9 (Figure 1D). Thus, one may conclude that the dehydrobutyrine moiety plays a critical role for the anthelminthic activity of malpinins. The hydrophobicity of the N-terminal tripeptide portion plays a less critical role and modulates the bioactivity and/or pharmacokinetic properties such as adsorption or excretion.

Interestingly, microscopic examination revealed that the nematodes stopped feeding on the provided bacterial food source in the presence of malpinin, although there was no discernible effect on their motility. To track malpinin in the nematode, we followed a two-pronged imaging strategy using high-sensitivity fluorescence and high-specificity Raman spectroscopy. Since malpinin A (9) does not contain a chromophore system that would allow fluorescence imaging, we employed a semisynthetic malpinin A-fluorescein adduct (malpinin A-FAM (16); Figure 2A)30 as a mimic. Such fluorescein conjugates are biocompatible and widely established for bioimaging purposes.30−36 However, since the structure of 16 differs from 9 in terms of size and shape, we first verified that it still exhibits anthelminthic activity in the liquid C. elegans assay. The IC50 of 16.92 μM for 16 is within the range of the malpinin congeners (Figure 2B), indicating that the extension on the phenylalanine moiety of malpinins does not significantly alter their bioactivity.

To visualize the translocation of 16 we incubated C. elegans in various developmental stages with either 16 or negative controls (fluorescein or methanol) overnight and analyzed the localization of fluorescence emission in the nematodes (Figure 2C–E). The combination of paralyzing nematodes with NaN3 and mechanical fixation by capillary forces proved sufficient for preventing swimming and large muscular movements for the majority of the imaging time frame. Hence, live worm imaging was conducted, which was particularly important as we observed a fast buildup of an additional autofluorescence in the same spectral window for dying and dead nematodes in all samples, including control samples. (Figures S32, S34 and S35). This observation is well founded as with age, the green autofluorescence of the intestinal region and its surrounding increases, followed by a typically sharp intensification of autofluorescence in the intestine and peripheral regions at the point of death for the blue and green channel.37 By imaging living worms, we minimized interferences from naturally occurring fluorophores in the nematode.

In contrast to the controls, the overview images of nematodes treated with 16 revealed high fluorescence emission originating from the nematodes’ entire digestive tract, including the pharynx and grinder region (Figure 2D, Figures S24 and S25). We observed similar staining for larvae and adult nematodes. Furthermore, we conducted a wavelength-resolved emission scan on a live juvenile nematode treated with malpinin-FAM. The recorded fluorescence exhibited conformity with reported emission properties of the fluorophore FAM (Figure S24). Since changes in pH occur within the nematodes’ digestive tracts, from pH 6 in the pharyngeal lumen to pH 3.5 in the posterior intestine,38 we considered that fluorescein derivatives can exhibit a pH-dependent change in fluorescence intensity (Figure S26)39 or that malpinins can have stability issues. However, we observed pronounced fluorescence staining of the nematodal lumen from anterior to posterior (Figure 2C and 2D) and detected 9 and 16 even after a few days of incubation under acidic conditions (pH 7–3) with only minor degradation of 16.

We obtained further insights into the origin of the nematodes’ fluorescence emission through fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM), which also enables the distinction of fluorophores with identical or overlapping emission spectra based on their lifetime (Figures S27–S35). We examined the lifetime distribution of 16 in adult and larval nematodes following a 1-day and 3-day incubation period (Figure 2D and E, Figures S31–S34). To elucidate the effects of malpinin, we compared samples treated with 16 (i) against a set of controls (ii: FAM and iii: MeOH), which show native emission patterns by autofluorophores inside the nematodes,37 or additionally unspecific binding of FAM (ii) that can also be excited at 488 nm (Figures S31–S34). FLIM enabled the distinction between autofluorescence, and fluorescence induced by 16, even a day after the nematodes had been paralyzed, when the autofluorescence began to increase (Figures S30, S32, S34 and S35).

While fluorescence microscopy allows fast and sensitive measurements, the principal disadvantages of this technique with externally added labels are the considerable dimensions of the tags in comparison to the original molecule’s size and shape. For 16, the change in mass contributes to an increase by 53% compared to the naturally occurring 9. Consequently, the biological behavior of such labeled molecules may diverge from the parent compound, especially concerning mobility behavior, hydrophilicity-related properties, and the potential introduction of additional pharmacophores, necessitating critical review. To exclude any effects caused by the coupling of malpinin to a fluorophore, we additionally designed a vibrationally tagged analog that can be localized in the nematode using Raman microspectroscopy. Spontaneous vibrational Raman spectroscopy has a lower sensitivity compared to fluorescence microscopy and measurements are more time-consuming, allowing only the analysis of smaller areas. However, it offers the advantage of probing vibrations as a ubiquitous and inherent molecular property, with small changes in atomic or bond order patterns partly leading to significant changes in the spectrum. The full potential of Raman spectroscopy is realized when triple bond units or deuterium isotope labels are employed as tags, as they provide spectroscopic and biological orthogonality while still remaining extremely small.40

Alkynes are the most favorable among the triple bond-containing tag options for Raman spectroscopy, exhibiting a 3-fold higher intensity than comparable nitriles.41 The Raman scattering intensity can be further enhanced by increasing the degree of conjugation, either by extending the actual tag fragment to a polyyne or by fusing the tag with another system of delocalized electrons.41 Thus, we favored the para-position of phenylalanine as a position for labeling. To obtain alkyne-labeled malpinin, we followed a precursor-directed biosynthesis approach.30 We added alkyne-Phe (17) to 3 L of a growing culture of M. alpina and succeeded in isolating small amounts (0.2 mg) of vibrationally tagged malpinin A-alkyne (18) in which Phe is replaced by alkyne-Phe (Figure 3). The metabolite was purified by preparative HPLC and structurally validated by analytical methods including HRMS(/MS), comparison of NMR data to known malpinin derivatives, and IR measurements (Table S6, Figures S18–S23).

In a liquid C. elegans assay, 18 showed an activity similar to that of 9 with full inhibition at 31 μM. Given that the anthelminthic properties were retained in the alkyne-tagged malpinin, we proceeded with hyperspectral Raman imaging of nematodes. The sites of tag accumulation were identified within the nematode by integrating around the band center, which is known from the C≡C stretching vibration of 17 (Figure 4A, B). The localization of the vibrationally tagged malpinin within the nematodes’ general morphology by Raman microspectroscopy was supported by the band intensity distributions of ν(CH3) and ν(CH2), which can be assigned to proteins (Figure 4C, green) and lipids (Figure 4C, blue), respectively.42 High protein contents are most abundant in the grinder and the nematodes’ cuticle, which both are made of extracellular matrix, mainly collagen, as a major component.42,43 Lipids are primarily found as droplet-like structures in the epidermis and in close proximity to the digestive tract (Figures S37 and S38).42 We identified the pharyngeal lumen, parts of the grinder, and the intestine as accumulation areas of 18 (Figure 4D), which is fully consistent with our fluorescence-based results with 16.

Considering the accumulation of malpinin on the intestinal membranes of the nematode suggests the possibility of an interaction between malpinin and the mucosal layer, which could result in an inhibition of food digestion. This is supported by the observation that the nematodes do not appear to be paralyzed or immediately killed by malpinin, but cease feeding and proliferation, even when sufficient food is available. A disruption of the intestinal mucosa seems plausible, given the strong biosurfactant properties of the malpinins. However, it is also possible that the malpinins adhere to the mucosa and/or agglutinate the grinder or intestine. Irrespective of the precise mode of action, the bioassays showed that the biosurfactant properties of the malpinins play a significant role in their anthelminthic activity.

Conclusions

M. alpina is a known promotor of plant growth and a biocontrol agent against plant pathogenic nematodes in the soil.17 In this study, we identified fungal specialized metabolites that protect M. alpina from predatory nematodes. Pronounced anthelminthic activities were attributed to malpinins (9–12), dehydro-hexapeptides that are produced in vast quantities by the beneficial fungus. Owing to their exceptionally strong biosurfactant activity, malpinins are excreted in the form of small droplets into the surrounding environment.26 The presence of 9–12 substantially reduces the number of fungivorous nematodes. Experiments with the model nematode C. elegans revealed that malpinins inhibit the feeding on provided food sources. Especially the noncanonical amino acid dehydrobutyrine in malpinin proved to be a pharmacologically important moiety. Its reduction to aminobutyrate was associated with a loss of activity. Accordingly, the presence of the Michael acceptor dehydrobutyrine has a direct influence on the anthelminthic activity, while changes in the hydrophobicity and biosurfactant properties of malpinin modulate the anthelminthic activity. An influence on the anthelminthic activity of natural products has also been observed for other biosurfactants.21,44,45

Compared to other natural product-derived anthelminthics that are in clinical use, such as the highly active semisynthetic emodepside46−48 or ivermectin,47,49 malpinins do not paralyze nematodes. In contrast, imaging and physiological monitoring indicate that malpinins act by a different strategy leading to a prolonged time until death. The slow killing of nematodes is potentially relevant for the treatment of parasitic diseases such as lymphatic filiriasis (elephantitis) indicated with severe immunoresponses. However, the studies conducted here primarily utilized the model organism C. elegans or the fungivorous nematode A. avenae, and a possible transferability of these results to other nematode species still needs to be examined.

Using two complementary imaging techniques, fluorescence microscopy and Raman microspectroscopy, we localized malpinin inside the nematodes’ intestine including the pharynx and the grinding region. While fluorescence microscopy, including FLIM measurements, enabled us to detect minute amounts of FAM conjugate 16 in real time, giving us a distinct picture of the substance distribution by its very characteristic long lifetime component t2, the Raman experiments verified the validity of our data, since the alkyne-tagged 18 has only minor changes to the original 9. We demonstrated the high chemical specificity for detection of small molecules in complex biological matrices using Raman microspectroscopy by exploiting the spectroscopic and biological orthogonality of vibrationally tagged analogues.

Noninvasive Raman measurements have been employed to study nematode morphologies43,50−54 and the dissemination of TiO2 nanoparticles.55 However, the use of imaging methods such as Raman spectroscopy for the localization of anthelminthic metabolites in nematodes is unprecedented. In this context it is noteworthy that the visualization of the alkyne-tagged 18 inside the nematodes is possible even for treatment concentrations of 110 μM, which is low compared to typical Raman experiments on nematodes.43,56 By extending the native compound by only two carbon atoms, we detected a distinct signal owing to the beneficial scattering cross-section of the alkyne conjugated to an aromatic ring.41 This approach, combined with precursor-directed biosynthesis, may be generally applicable for the imaging of peptide natural products. Particularly, the biosynthetic incorporation of a small tag into natural products of interest offers significant advantages over a fully artificial design of complex probes. In addition to eliminating the need for a synthetic route, the successful biosynthetic incorporation of a small alkyne tag minimally alters the native structure, reducing the risk of changes in the molecule’s biological functionality. Taken together, both the highly sensitive live nematode imaging and the superbly specific vibrational spectroscopy yielded comparable information, indicating that the translocation of malpinins in nematodes is independent of the tag.

Our findings extend the known mechanisms of protection against nematodes applied by Mortierella species to fungal produced metabolites. The known mechanisms so far included mechanical nematode trapping with hyphal loops18 and the employment of endosymbiont produced toxic metabolites.19,21 In this context it is interesting to note that the malpinin biosynthesis gene cluster has likely been acquired from bacteria.25 Since the genes coding for the nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) domains are especially similar to Mycoavidus or Paraburkholderia NRPS genes, it is tempting to suspect the protection of M. alpina by malpinin to be an ancient remnant of a previous endosymbiont,25 which protected M. alpina similar to the protection of P. verticillata NRRL 6337 by Candidatus Mycoavidus necroximicus.19,21

This study demonstrates how the interplay between biological assays and imaging methods can be used to reveal active sites of molecules in predators like nematodes and to visualize the translocation of the anthelminthic agent. More importantly, this work provides an explanation for why M. alpina acts as a biocontrol agent against plant pathogenic nematodes. Thus, our findings can help with future developments of environmentally friendly biocontrol- and fertilization strategies and set the basis for the development of urgently needed new anthelminthic agents.
