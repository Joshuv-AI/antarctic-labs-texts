Characterization of four highly efficient ammonifying bacteria from Camellia Oleifera and their effects on soil chemical properties
Authors: Xiaoxiao Zhou, Kai Wang, Ziqiong Luo, Junqin Zhou, Jun Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCamellia oleifera is an ammonium-preferring woody oil crop. However, cultivable ammonifying bacteria from its rhizosphere remain insufficiently characterized, and their potential to improve soil chemical properties and enhance nitrogen availability has not yet been evaluated.ResultsA total of 160 cultivable bacterial isolates were obtained from rhizosphere soil. Among them, 25 isolates showed positive ammonia production in preliminary screening. Quantitative determination revealed significant differences in ammonium production, ranging from 1.016 to 14.753 mg mL− 1. Four high-efficiency strains were selected and identified as Arthrobacter sp., Chryseobacterium sp., Stenotrophomonas sp., and Bacillus sp. based on phenotypic characteristics and 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Culture optimization showed that pH 7–8, 20–30 °C, 200 rpm, and a 2% inoculum size supported optimal bacterial proliferation. No antagonistic interactions were detected among the four strains, indicating compatibility for consortium construction. In a pot experiment, inoculation with individual strains or their consortium significantly increased rhizosphere ammonium nitrogen, nitrate nitrogen, and available nitrogen compared with the control. Soil enzyme activities related to nitrogen transformation were also enhanced, with most reaching peak levels 60 days after inoculation.ConclusionsFour efficient ammonifying bacteria isolated from the C. oleifera rhizosphere exhibited strong ammonium-producing capacity, compatibility for consortium formulation, and improved soil nitrogen supply and chemical properties.

Background

Camellia oleifera is a major woody oil crop widely cultivated in subtropical China. Its seeds yield edible camellia oil rich in unsaturated fatty acids, conferring high nutritional and economic value [1–3]. Despite rapid expansion of planting areas, productivity remains constrained in many orchards, where nitrogen (N) fertilization is frequently intensified to achieve short-term yield gains. Prolonged reliance on chemical N inputs increases production costs and contributes to soil degradation, including nutrient imbalance, acidification, compaction, and broader environmental risks [4]. Therefore, developing strategies that enhance soil nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) while reducing chemical fertilizer inputs is essential for the sustainable development of C. oleifera production systems. Microbial fertilizers and functional inoculants represent promising tools to improve soil nutrient cycling and ecosystem functioning in perennial forest ecosystems [5, 6].

A distinctive physiological feature of C. oleifera is its preference and tolerance for ammonium. When ammonium (NH4+-N) and nitrate (NO3−-N) coexist, roots preferentially absorb NH4+-N, and increasing the NH4+-N proportion can enhance seedling growth [7]. In most soils, plant-available inorganic N is regenerated from the dominant soil N pool—organic nitrogen—through microbially mediated mineralization processes. Soil organic N constitutes the majority of total soil N and can significantly contribute to plant nutrition and NUE, although it has historically received less attention in agricultural management [8]. Ammonium produced during mineralization can be retained on soil colloids, potentially reducing leaching losses; however, it is also rapidly oxidized to nitrate through nitrification, increasing N mobility and loss risk [9]. Thus, strategies that enhance rhizosphere NH4+-N supply via microbial processes may better align soil N dynamics with the ammonium preference of C. oleifera.

Soil microorganisms and their extracellular enzymes are central regulators of organic matter decomposition and nutrient transformation in forest ecosystems [10–12]. Nitrogen cycling processes differ in phylogenetic breadth and ecological stability. Ammonification—the microbial conversion of organic N to NH4+—is considered a phylogenetically broad process carried out by diverse microbial taxa, whereas nitrification is mediated by more specialized functional groups [13]. Differences in functional breadth may influence the stability of N transformation rates across environmental gradients [13]. Despite this, research has predominantly focused on diazotrophs, nitrifiers, and denitrifiers, whereas cultivable ammonifying bacteria in rhizosphere soils remain comparatively underexplored, particularly in woody oil crop systems.

In the context of C. oleifera cultivation, targeted isolation, identification, and optimization of rhizosphere ammonifying bacteria with high NH4+-N producing capacity remain insufficiently developed. In this study, we isolated cultivable ammonifying bacteria from the rhizosphere soil of C. oleifera plantations and screened for high-efficiency NH4+-N producers. Selected strains were characterized using morphological, physiological, biochemical, and 16S rRNA gene analyses, and culture conditions influencing bacterial proliferation and ammonification were optimized. Strain compatibility was evaluated to facilitate consortium construction, and the effects of individual strains and a consortium on rhizosphere N availability, soil enzyme activities associated with N transformation were assessed in a pot experiment. This study provides candidate ammonifying strains and practical parameters to support the development of microbial inoculants aimed at enhancing rhizosphere NH4+ supply and promoting soil nitrogen transformation in C. oleifera production systems.

Materials and methods

Study site and rhizosphere soil sampling

Rhizosphere soil samples were collected from the Wangcheng Camellia oleifera plantation base of Central South University of Forestry and Technology, Changsha, China (28°30′ N, 113°20′ E). The sampled stand was a 10-year-old top-grafted plantation of the cultivated oil tea tree cultivar Camellia oleifera ‘Huajin’, which is a high-yield cultivated variety bred by Central South University of Forestry and Technology, established at a spacing of 2 m × 3 m. Five healthy C. oleifera plants were randomly selected within the plantation, and rhizosphere soil from each plant was collected as an independent field replicate. For each plant, soil adhering closely to the root surface after gentle shaking was collected from the 0–20 cm layer. The five rhizosphere soil subsamples were then pooled and homogenized to form a composite sample. Visible debris and stones were removed manually, and samples were passed through a sterile 2-mm nylon sieve. Homogenized soil samples were stored at − 80 °C until microbial isolation and analysis.

Isolation and screening of ammonifying bacteria

Soil bacteria were isolated by the standard serial dilution plate technique. Distinct colonies were selected based on morphological characteristics and streaked on nutrient agar plate separately in order to obtain pure isolated colonies. All the isolates were screened for the production of ammonia by adapting the method of Cappuccino and Sherman (2005) [14]. Cell pellets and culture supernatants of positive isolates were separated by centrifugation according to the method described by Cappuccino and Sherman (2005) [14]. Culture supernatants were reacted with potassium sodium tartrate solution and Nessler’s reagent, and absorbance was measured at 420 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. Ultrapure water served as the blank control. All treatments were performed in triplicate.

Physiological and biochemical characterization of strains

The physiological and biochemical characteristics of the isolates were determined according to the standard procedures described in the Manual for the Systematic Identification of Common Bacteria [15] and Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology [16]. The assays included Gram staining, catalase activity, Voges–Proskauer (V–P) reaction, indole production, nitrate reduction, ammonia production, motility, litmus milk reaction, starch hydrolysis, and gelatin liquefaction. For each assay, uninoculated medium served as a negative control, and appropriate positive and negative control strains were included where necessary. Cultivation was performed using the media, temperatures, and incubation periods specified in the manuals. Test results were interpreted as positive or negative based on color changes, staining characteristics, or the presence or absence of hydrolysis or liquefaction zones.

16S rDNA gene amplification and phylogenetic analysis

Genomic DNA was used as the PCR template. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). Each 50 µL PCR reaction contained 25 µL 2× HF Master Mix, 2 µL of each primer (10 µM), 1 µL template DNA, and 20 µL sterile ddH2O. PCR products were verified by electrophoresis on 1% agarose gels and visualized under UV illumination. Sequencing was performed by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. Sequences were compared with those in the NCBI database using BLAST (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Closely related reference sequences were aligned, and phylogenetic trees were constructed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA 7.0 with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Determination of bacterial biomass, culture condition optimization, and antagonism assay

Efficient ammonifying strains were inoculated at 2% (v/v) into 50 mL beef extract–peptone broth and incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. Bacterial biomass was monitored by measuring OD600 at designated time points. Culture condition optimization was performed using a single-factor experimental design. After 24 h of incubation under each tested condition, OD600 was measured to quantify biomass. Each treatment included three biological replicates, and uninoculated medium served as the blank control. Strain compatibility was evaluated using a pairwise cross-streak method on nutrient agar plates.

Pot experiment

One-year-old grafted seedlings of the cultivated oil tea tree cultivar Camellia oleifera ‘Huajin’ were used in the pot experiment. Uniform seedlings were transplanted into 15 cm × 15 cm pots, each containing 500 g of sterilized soil. Before bacterial inoculation, the initial physicochemical properties of the pot soil were determined and are shown in Table 1. After a 1-month acclimation period, all pots received 100 mL of a 5% (w/v) urea solution, followed by a soil drench of 100 mL bacterial inoculum. Six treatments were established: four single-strain inoculations treatments, one consortium treatment (MIX) consisting of the four strains mixed at a 1:1:1:1 ratio, and a non-inoculated control (CK) that received an equal volume of sterile water. Sampling was conducted at 0, 20, 40, 60, and 80 days after inoculation, with five biological replicates per treatment. Soil physicochemical properties and enzyme activities were determined using standard methods as listed in Table 2 [17, 18]. All measurements were performed with three biological replicates per treatment.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 25 (IBM, USA). Figures were generated using OriginPro 2021 (OriginLab, USA). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to analysis, data were checked for normality and homogeneity of variance. For ammonium production among bacterial isolates and culture condition optimization experiments, differences among groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. For soil physicochemical properties and enzyme activities in the pot experiment, the effects of inoculation treatment, sampling time, and their interaction were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Multiple comparisons among treatments at the same sampling time and among sampling times within the same treatment were performed using Duncan’s multiple range test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the neighbor-joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA 7.0.

Results

Screening of ammonifying bacteria

A total of 160 bacterial isolates were obtained from the rhizosphere soil of C. oleifera. Preliminary screening using Nessler’s reagent identified 25 isolates exhibiting strong colorimetric reactions, suggesting enhanced ammonification capacity. Quantitative determination of ammonium production revealed significant variation among isolates (Table 3). NH4+-N concentrations ranged from 1.016 ± 0.109 mg mL− 1 (AHA) to 14.753 ± 0.914 mg mL− 1 (AHT), indicating substantial inter-strain variability. Strain AHT exhibited the highest ammonium accumulation, followed by AHB (13.344 ± 0.691 mg mL− 1). Strains AHO (11.862 ± 0.761 mg mL− 1 and AHX (11.571 ± 0.350 mg mL− 1) also showed consistently high ammonium production. Based on ammonification capacity (AHT > AHB > AHO > AHX), these four isolates were selected for subsequent characterization and experimentation, as they represented the highest-performing group among all tested isolates.

Physiological and biochemical characteristics of four selected strains

The four selected strains (AHB, AHO, AHT, and AHX) exhibited largely consistent physiological and biochemical profiles, as summarized in Table 4. All strains were Gram-positive and motile, and tested positive for catalase activity and Voges–Proskauer reaction. All produced ammonia in liquid medium. None of the isolates showed positive reactions for starch hydrolysis, nitrate reduction, or indole production. In the litmus milk test, all strains produced acid, indicating carbohydrate fermentation capacity. Gelatin liquefaction was positive only in AHB, whereas AHO, AHT, and AHX were negative, suggesting strain-specific variation in proteolytic activity.

Phylogenetic analysis of four selected strains

The nearly full-length 16S rRNA gene was successfully amplified from all four isolates, generating fragments of 1385 bp (AHB), 1413 bp (AHO), 1390 bp (AHT), and 1418 bp (AHX), respectively. BLAST analysis indicated high sequence similarity between the isolates and known bacterial taxa in GenBank. Strain AHB shared 99% sequence similarity with Chryseobacterium sp. (accession HM334203.1) and clustered within the Chryseobacterium clade in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1a). Strain AHO exhibited 99.36% similarity to Stenotrophomonas sp. (accession MK139539.1) and grouped within the Stenotrophomonas lineage (Fig. 1b). Strain AHT showed 99% similarity to Arthrobacter sp. (accession MK311309.1) and clustered within the Arthrobacter clade (Fig. 1c). Strain AHX displayed 98% similarity to Bacillus sp. (accession KC465359.1) and was positioned within the Bacillus lineage (Fig. 1d). Phylogenetic relationships inferred using the neighbor-joining method were supported by high bootstrap values (1000 replicates), confirming the taxonomic affiliations of the isolates. Based on sequence similarity and phylogenetic placement, AHB, AHO, AHT, and AHX were preliminarily identified as Chryseobacterium sp., Stenotrophomonas sp., Arthrobacter sp., and Bacillus sp., respectively. Growth characteristics of four selected strains

Growth curves of the four selected strains under identical culture conditions are shown in Fig. 2. All strains exhibited typical sigmoidal growth patterns, including lag, exponential, stationary, and decline phases. During the initial 2–10 h, OD600 values increased gradually in all strains. Rapid biomass accumulation occurred between 10 and 20 h. AHB and AHX reached higher maximum OD600 values (2.07 and 2.08, respectively) compared with AHO (1.61) and AHT (1.63). Peak biomass was reached at 18–24 h in AHB and AHX, whereas AHO and AHT peaked around 20 h. Following the stationary phase, OD600 values gradually declined. Overall, AHB and AHX showed greater biomass accumulation and better persistence during cultivation than AHO and AHT.

Effects of culture factors on strain biomass accumulation

The effects of environmental and nutritional factors on bacterial biomass accumulation are presented in Fig. 3. Under varying pH conditions, all strains showed low biomass accumulation at pH 3–4. Maximum biomass accumulation for AHB, AHT, and AHX was observed at pH 7, whereas AHO maintained relatively high biomass accumulation at pH 7–8. Biomass accumulation declined markedly at pH 9. Temperature also affected biomass accumulation. All strains exhibited the highest biomass accumulation between 20 and 30 °C. Biomass accumulation of AHB, AHO, and AHT decreased sharply above 30 °C, whereas AHX remained relatively stable up to 40 °C. Inoculum size also influenced biomass accumulation. All strains reached the highest OD600 at inoculum sizes of 2–3% (v/v), whereas OD600 declined at 4–5%. Shaking speed likewise affected biomass accumulation. OD600 values increased from 50 to 200 rpm, peaked at 200 rpm, and then declined at 250 rpm. Carbon source preference varied among strains. AHB showed the highest OD600 with glucose (0.946), AHO and AHX on starch (0.863 and 0.978, respectively), and AHT on sucrose (0.929). Growth was consistently low with lactose as the carbon source. Nitrogen source utilization also differed. AHB exhibited optimal growth with urea (0.941), whereas AHO, AHX, and AHT grew best with potassium nitrate (0.638, 0.859, and 0.535, respectively). Ammonium oxalate supported minimal growth across all strains.

Effects of strain inoculation on rhizosphere soil physicochemical properties

Changes in rhizosphere soil properties following inoculation are shown in Fig. 5. Total nitrogen (TN) gradually declined across treatments during the 80-day period, although AHB maintained relatively higher TN levels between 20 and 60 days. Soil pH initially increased in all inoculated treatments compared with CK but decreased below CK levels by day 80. NH4+-N concentrations were consistently higher in inoculated soils than in CK, with peak levels observed at day 60. AHX showed the highest NH4+-N concentration at this time point (26.00 mg kg− 1). At day 80, the consortium treatment (MIX) maintained the highest NH4+-N level (20.00 mg kg− 1). NO3−-N concentrations peaked at day 40 in all treatments. AHB exhibited the highest NO3−-N level (74.13 mg kg− 1), significantly exceeding CK. Available nitrogen (AN) followed a similar trend, peaking at day 40 and remaining higher in inoculated treatments than CK at day 80. Available phosphorus (AP) and available potassium (AK) also increased following inoculation, with peak values generally observed between 40 and 60 days. SOM content exhibited only minor variation, although AHT showed the highest value at day 80.

Effects of bacterial inoculation on soil enzyme activities

Inoculation generally enhanced soil enzyme activities compared with CK (Fig. 6). Most enzymes reached maximum activity at 60 days post-inoculation. Catalase activity was significantly higher in inoculated treatments than in CK at day 60, with AHT showing the highest value. Urease activity also peaked at day 60, particularly in MIX and AHB treatments. Nitrate reductase (NR) activity increased notably in AHB at day 60 and remained higher in AHX and MIX at day 80 compared with CK. Acid protease activity showed a similar trend, with AHT and AHX exhibiting higher values than CK at peak time points. Overall, microbial inoculation enhanced rhizosphere enzyme activities associated with nitrogen transformation, particularly during mid-incubation.

Discussion

Isolation, screening and identification of ammonifying bacteria from the rhizosphere of Camellia oleifera

In this study, 160 cultivable ammonifying bacteria were isolated from the rhizosphere soil of C. oleifera, highlighting the rhizosphere as a hotspot of heterotrophic nitrogen-mineralizing microorganisms. The rhizosphere represents a biologically active interface where plant-derived carbon inputs reshape microbial community structure and metabolic potential [19, 20]. Root exudates, sloughed cells, and decomposing litter continuously supply labile substrates, thereby stimulating microbial proliferation and nitrogen transformation processes [21]. Such carbon enrichment enhances microbial demand for nitrogen and may accelerate organic-N depolymerization and ammonification [22]. From a nitrogen-cycle perspective, ammonification constitutes a phylogenetically broad process within the integrated nitrogen transformation network described by Stein & Klotz (2016) [23]. Unlike nitrification, which is restricted to specialized taxa, ammonification is performed by diverse heterotrophic microorganisms across multiple lineages [13]. Consistent with this framework, our phylogenetic results showed that the four high-efficiency strains belonged to Chryseobacterium, Stenotrophomonas, Arthrobacter, and Bacillus, indicating taxonomic dispersion rather than lineage-specific confinement of ammonification potential. Ecosystem-scale evidence further suggests that phylogenetically broad processes tend to exhibit greater functional redundancy and stability across environmental gradients [13]. However, within such functional groups, a limited number of highly active taxa may disproportionately contribute to process-level fluxes under favorable conditions. This aligns with keystone taxa theory [24], which posits that certain microbial members can exert outsized effects on ecosystem functioning irrespective of abundance. Therefore, although ammonification is widely distributed, the identification of strains with exceptionally high NH4+ production (e.g., AHT and AHB) may be ecologically meaningful, as such taxa could function as high-impact contributors within rhizosphere nitrogen cycling networks.

Culture conditions for efficient ammonifying bacteria from the rhizosphere of Camellia oleifera

All four strains exhibited classical sigmoidal growth dynamics, reaching the stationary phase between 18 and 24 h. This standardized window provides a reproducible harvesting time for inoculant preparation and downstream functional assays. The highest biomass accumulation was observed under near-neutral to slightly alkaline conditions (pH 7–8), at 20–30 °C, with shaking at 200 r/min and a 2% inoculum size. These findings are consistent with general microbial physiological responses under mesophilic and aerobic conditions. Carbon- and nitrogen-source utilization differed among strains, indicating metabolic complementarity. Such complementarity is advantageous for consortium design, as resource partitioning may reduce niche overlap and enhance functional stability. A key constraint in multi-strain formulation is antagonistic interaction, which can destabilize community structure [25]. In the present study, no inhibitory interactions were detected among the four isolates, supporting their compatibility. From a community-assembly perspective, combining strains with distinct physiological traits (e.g., high ammonification capacity, thermal tolerance, substrate breadth) may enhance resilience under fluctuating field conditions. This strategy reflects principles of trait complementarity and functional redundancy within microbial consortia [20, 24].

Effects of strain inoculation on soil chemical properties and enzyme activities in Camellia oleifera

Nitrogen availability is determined not only by external N input but also by the transformation of organic and inorganic N pools in the rhizosphere [11]. In the pot experiment, inoculation with the selected ammonifying bacteria increased rhizosphere NH4+-N, NO3−-N, and available N compared with CK, indicating that bacterial inoculation enhanced soil N transformation rather than simply increasing a single inorganic N form. The increase in NH4+-N, especially during the middle stage of the experiment, is consistent with the ammonifying capacity of the selected strains and suggests enhanced mineralization of organic N into ammonium. The simultaneous increase in NO3−-N further indicates that part of the newly generated NH4+ may have entered downstream nitrification, resulting in a more active inorganic N turnover process in the rhizosphere. These reflect integrated nitrogen-cycle coupling as conceptualized by Stein & Klotz (2016) [23]. Thus, inoculation likely accelerated the turnover rate of soil N pools, increasing flux intensity within the rhizosphere nitrogen cycle. From a broader ecological perspective, global evidence indicates declining nitrogen availability in many terrestrial ecosystems [11]. Under such trends, strategies that enhance internal nitrogen mineralization may help buffer productivity constraints in perennial systems. Our results suggest that rhizosphere-targeted inoculation can locally enhance N cycling rates, potentially counteracting reduced N supply under low-input conditions. The changes in soil enzyme activities provide further evidence for this interpretation. Urease catalyzes the hydrolysis of urea into ammonium and therefore directly contributes to the formation of NH4+-N in soil. The higher urease activity observed in inoculated treatments, particularly during the mid-incubation period, may partly explain the increased NH4+-N level after inoculation. Acid protease participates in the degradation of proteinaceous organic N compounds and releases smaller N-containing substrates that can subsequently be mineralized to ammonium [26]. Therefore, the increase in acid protease activity suggests that bacterial inoculation promoted the depolymerization of organic N pools and provided substrates for ammonification. Nitrate reductase is involved in nitrate transformation, and its increased activity reflects a more active inorganic N conversion process, which is consistent with the observed variation in NO3−-N. Catalase is not directly responsible for N mineralization, but it reflects microbial oxidative metabolism and the capacity to decompose hydrogen peroxide generated during active microbial growth and nutrient transformation [27]. The enhancement of catalase activity therefore suggests increased microbial metabolic activity in the inoculated rhizosphere. Overall, the synchronized increases in N-related enzyme activities and inorganic N contents indicate that the selected ammonifying bacteria modulated rhizosphere N cycling through multiple pathways, including organic N depolymerization, urea hydrolysis, ammonification, and subsequent inorganic N transformation. Most enzyme activities reached relatively high levels around 60 days after inoculation, suggesting that the effect of bacterial inoculation on rhizosphere N transformation was strongest during the middle stage of the pot experiment. However, the decrease in soil pH at the later stage indicates that enhanced NH4+ turnover and nitrification may also contribute to rhizosphere acidification. Therefore, these strains may be useful for improving rhizosphere N availability, but their field application should be combined with appropriate nutrient management to avoid excessive acidification in acidic C. oleifera soils.

Application significance: camellia management and microbial fertilizer strategies

C. oleifera preferentially absorbs NH4+-N [7]. However, NH4+ is rapidly oxidized to NO3− through nitrification [23]. Therefore, enhancing ammonification alone may not ensure sustained NH4+ dominance without complementary nitrification regulation. Under practical orchard management, inoculants may function best as a fertilizer-reduction strategy rather than a complete replacement. Co-application with controlled-release fertilizers or nitrification inhibitors [28, 29] may improve nitrogen retention while minimizing leaching risk. From a systems perspective, Mason et al. (2022) [11] emphasize that nitrogen limitation constrains carbon sequestration and ecosystem productivity globally. Enhancing rhizosphere nitrogen cycling efficiency in perennial woody systems may thus contribute not only to yield improvement but also to broader sustainability objectives. For product development, separate propagation of strains under optimized conditions followed by post-fermentation blending is recommended to ensure production stability. Cost-effective substrates (e.g., starch hydrolysates, molasses, urea) should be prioritized to improve economic feasibility while maintaining strain viability.

Conclusions

This study isolated 160 cultivable ammonifying bacteria from the rhizosphere of Camellia oleifera and identified four strains with high ammonium-producing capacity. Phylogenetic and phenotypic analyses showed that these strains belonged to distinct bacterial lineages. Optimized cultivation conditions were determined, and the strains showed compatibility for potential consortium formulation. Pot experiments further demonstrated that inoculation increased inorganic N contents and enhanced soil enzyme activities related to nitrogen transformation. Overall, efficient ammonifying bacteria from the camellia rhizosphere provide a functional microbial resource for improving nitrogen management, supporting sustainable orchard production, and advancing biofertilizer development.
