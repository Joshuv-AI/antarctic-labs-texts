Are Large Language Models Robust in Understanding Code Against Semantics-Preserving Mutations?
Pedro Orvalho, Marta Kwiatkowska

Abstract
With the widespread adoption of vibe coding , understanding the reasoning and robustness of Large Language Models ( LLMs ) is critical for their reliable use in programming tasks. While recent studies assess LLMs ’ ability to predict program outputs, most focus on accuracy alone, without evaluating the underlying reasoning. Moreover, it has been observed on mathematical reasoning tasks that LLMs can arrive at correct answers through flawed logic, raising concerns about similar issues in code understanding. In this paper we assess whether state-of-the-art LLMs can reason about Python programs or are simply guessing. We apply five semantics-preserving code mutations: renaming variables, mirroring comparison expressions, swapping if-else branches, converting for loops to while , and loop unrolling. These mutations maintain program semantics while altering its syntax. We evaluated nine LLMs , including both open-source and closed-access models, and performed a human expert analysis using LiveCodeBench to assess whether correct predictions are based on sound reasoning. We also evaluated prediction stability across different code mutations on LiveCodeBench and CruxEval . While proprietary models achieve the strongest predictive accuracy and reasoning quality in the expert evaluation, our robustness analysis reveals substantial fragility under semantics-preserving transformations. Our findings show that LLMs trained for code produce correct predictions based on flawed reasoning in between 10% and 50% of cases. Furthermore, LLMs often change predictions in response to our code mutations, with performance drops reaching up to 70%, indicating that they do not yet exhibit stable, semantically grounded reasoning, even when initial accuracy is high.

Introduction

Large Language Models (
LLMs
) have rapidly become integral to a wide range of daily tasks, from writing assistance to code generation. In particular, the software development community has embraced
LLM
-based tools, such as
GitHub Copilot
and
ChatGPT
, to streamline code workflows, assist in debugging, and even automate code completion and review
(
Ding et al. 2024
;
Liang et al. 2024
)
. These tools are widely used, and often blindly, with developers placing significant trust in their capabilities
(
Oh et al. 2024
)
.
However, this growing reliance on
LLMs
for coding tasks raises a fundamental question: To what extent do
LLMs
truly understand code and the underlying semantics of programs?

While recent
LLMs
can produce syntactically correct code and even solve competitive programming problems, there is a risk that their responses may reflect pattern recognition over code syntax rather than genuine semantic understanding
(
Petrov et al. 2025
)
.
Although
LLMs
rely on statistical associations to generate output, their ability to mimic reasoning can obscure the limitations of such models, potentially leading to an overestimation of their reliability in critical development contexts
(
Gu et al. 2024a
)
.
Furthermore, based on the growing dependence of programmers on
LLMs
, several works in the past year have analysed
LLMs
’ reasoning about code through output prediction tasks
(
Ding et al. 2024
;
Gu et al. 2024b
;
Jain et al. 2024
)
. Some
LLMs
, such as
SemCoder

(
Ding et al. 2024
)
, were trained to
understand
program semantics through a monologue reasoning strategy (i.e., Chain-of-Thought),
where the model verbally simulates both the high-level syntax and low-level execution effects of code. However, some recent work has shown that in other domains, such as mathematical competitions
(
Petrov et al. 2025
)
,
LLMs
tend to provide accurate predictions,
but based on flawed reasoning.

In this paper, we evaluate
LLMs
on the task of reasoning about code and predicting program outputs for given inputs, using two well-studied benchmarks:
LiveCodeBench

(
Jain et al. 2024
)
and
CruxEval

(
Gu et al. 2024b
)
. We define
sound semantic reasoning
in
LLMs
in terms of their reasoning traces being semantically accurate, that is, when the models predict the correct output and correctly describe the control and data flow that takes the program from the inputs to the expected output.
First, we conduct a manual expert evaluation on
LiveCodeBench
to determine whether correct predictions are derived from logically sound reasoning, flawed reasoning, or mere guesses. Second, we assess model robustness by applying semantics-preserving code mutations to both benchmarks and analysing output prediction consistency. These mutations, which are small syntactic modifications that preserve runtime behaviour, test whether
LLMs
’ understanding of code is robust to syntactic changes.
We apply five semantics-preserving code mutations explained in Section
4
: (1) renaming variables, (2) mirroring comparison expressions, (3) swapping if-else branches, (4) converting
for
loops to
while
loops, and (5) unrolling the final iterations of loops.
These code transformations allow us to evaluate whether
LLMs
are sensitive to syntax or capable of reasoning about code semantics.

Our experiments consider both open-source and closed-access state-of-the-art
LLMs
.
We evaluate nine different
LLMs
: six models specifically trained for coding tasks,
CodeGemma
,
GraniteCode
,
Mistral
,
Qwen2.5-Coder
,
Qwen3-Coder
, and
SemCoder
, two closed-access models that are widely used as coding agents,
Gemini-3
and
GPT-5.2
, and one general-purpose model,
Llama3.2
, included for comparison.
Our in-depth analysis of
LLMs
’ reasoning about code shows that some
LLMs
produce correct answers based on flawed reasoning in up to 55% of cases for general-purpose
LLMs
, and 50% for
LLMs
trained specifically for coding tasks (
LLMCs
).
Additionally, we observe that interactive querying enhances both prediction accuracy and reasoning quality, increasing the rate of sound reasoning by 14% in
LLMCs
and 19% in
Llama3.2
. Moreover, our experiments show that the evaluated
LLMs
frequently alter their predictions under semantics-preserving code mutations, by up to 70%, indicating that these models do not yet exhibit stable, semantically grounded reasoning.
Variable renaming consistently causes substantial changes in model predictions across both datasets, despite preserving program semantics, indicating that identifier names strongly influence model behaviour, a sensitivity also observed in human comprehension
(
Hofmeister et al. 2019
)
. In contrast, loop unrolling leads to the largest performance drops, highlighting limited structural and semantic generalisation across models.

Our expert evaluation shows that the strongest performance was achieved by
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
, which attained near-perfect prediction accuracy and consistently sound semantic reasoning.
Qwen3-Coder
also demonstrated particularly strong reasoning quality among the open-access models. Nevertheless, when evaluated under semantics-preserving code mutations, even these top-performing models exhibit substantial fragility. In fact,
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
experience some of the largest performance drops across mutations, revealing that high accuracy on the original benchmark does not necessarily imply semantic robustness.
These results must therefore be interpreted in light of
model release timelines
and accessibility constraints. The evaluated versions of
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
correspond to releases from the end of 2025, whereas
LiveCodeBench
was published in 2024. In contrast, most open-access models in our study were released prior to the benchmark’s publication. Consequently,
Qwen3-Coder
,
GPT-5.2
, and
Gemini-3
may have been trained on
LiveCodeBench
or overlapping data, potentially inflating their performance.
Although the exact composition of proprietary training corpora is unknown, the near-perfect results of
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
, and the strong performance of
Qwen3-Coder
, should therefore be interpreted with caution.
Performance differences must also be considered alongside
accessibility
. While the open-access models evaluated in this work are freely available,
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
require paid API access. Thus, although proprietary models achieve substantially higher predictive accuracy and reasoning quality in standard evaluations, this advantage comes with a non-trivial cost differential and does not translate into robustness against semantics-preserving transformations.

Thus, our experiments serve as evidence that semantics-preserving perturbations expose the brittleness of purely data-driven reasoning, and merely scaling models or training on more code will not fix this problem because the flaw is rooted in the
absence of formal semantic constraints
.
Reliable reasoning about code demands
formal guarantees that current
LLMs
cannot provide
. While current state-of-the-art code-focused models excel at pattern recognition and surface-level syntax manipulation, our findings show that they fail to maintain stable predictions under semantics-preserving transformations, revealing a fundamental lack of true semantic understanding.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:

•

We perform a manual expert evaluation to assess whether
LLMs
’ code output predictions are based on logically sound code reasoning, flawed reasoning, or mere guesses.

•

We evaluate
LLMs
’ output prediction stability across five different semantics-preserving code mutations.

•

Experiments show that
LLMs
frequently alter their predictions when subjected to semantics-preserving code mutations, highlighting that they do not yet exhibit stable, semantically grounded reasoning.

•

We discuss fruitful research directions that integrate formal methods with machine learning to create semantics-aware code models, highlighting how such hybrid approaches could improve robustness and security against adversarial code mutations.

Preliminaries

This section provides definitions used throughout the paper.

Definition 1
(Abstract Syntax Tree (AST))

An
abstract syntax tree (AST)
is a syntax tree in which each node represents an operation, and the children of the node represent the arguments of the operation for a given language described by a context-free grammar
(
Hopcroft et al. 2007
)
.
An
AST
represents the grammatical structure of a program
(
Aho et al. 1986
)
.

Definition 2
(Semantics-Preserving Code Mutation.)

Given
(T,G,O,P)
, let
T
be a set of input-output examples (test suite),
G
be a grammar,
O
be the semantics for a particular domain-specific language (DSL), and
P
be a syntactically well-formed program (i.e., a set of statements, instructions, expressions) consistent with
G
and
O
, such that
P
is semantically consistent with the test suite, i.e.,

\forall{(t^{i}_{in},t^{i}_{out})\in T}\ :\ P(t^{i}_{in})=t^{i}_{out}
.

A
semantics-preserving code mutation
is a syntactic program transformation to
P
that generates a new program
P_{m}
by syntactically replacing a subset
S_{1}
of
P
’s statements (
S_{1}\subseteq P
) with another set of statements
S_{2}
consistent with
G
and
O
, such that

P_{m}=((P\setminus S_{1})\cup S_{2})
,
and
P_{m}
is semantically consistent with the original specification:

\forall{(t^{i}_{in},t^{i}_{out})\in T}\ :\ P_{m}(t^{i}_{in})=t^{i}_{out}
.

In Section
4
, we introduce a set of five semantics-preserving program transformations designed to syntactically modify Python programs without altering their semantics, i.e., their runtime behaviour: variable renaming, comparison mirroring, swap if-else statements, for-to-while loop conversion, and partial loop unrolling. These code mutations are essential for tasks such as testing the robustness of code understanding models and augmenting training data in a semantics-preserving manner.

4
Semantics-Preserving Code Mutations

We introduce a set of semantics-preserving program transformations designed to syntactically modify Python programs without altering their semantics, i.e., their runtime behaviour. These code mutations are essential for tasks such as testing the robustness of code understanding models and augmenting training data in a semantics-preserving manner. In this section, we describe five mutations implemented in our work: variable renaming, comparison mirroring, swap if-else statements, for-to-while loop conversion, and partial loop unrolling.
Some of these code mutations have been previously employed
(
Henkel et al. 2022
;
Hu et al. 2019
;
Orvalho et al. 2022
)
to augment benchmarks with semantically equivalent, yet syntactically different, versions of the original programs.
As a running example,
we use the program shown in Algorithm
3
, where the function
f(nums)
returns the sum of all even numbers in the list
nums
.

Variable Renaming.

This program transformation systematically renames local variables, function arguments, or function names using fresh identifiers that do not conflict with existing symbols or Python built-ins. A consistent mapping is maintained within each scope to ensure correctness. This program mutation preserves the program’s semantics while altering its lexical structure.
Algorithm
4
shows the function
f(nums)
from Algorithm
3
after renaming the variable
sum
to
uoWIfiQc
.
While variable renaming preserves syntax and semantics, it affects human comprehension
(
Hofmeister et al. 2019
)
, making it a meaningful probe for model robustness.

Comparison Expression Mirroring.

This transformation mirrors comparison expressions by swapping operands and applying their logically equivalent inverse operators.
This mutation preserves program semantics and applies to all symmetric and reversible binary comparisons. It is particularly useful for assessing models that rely on syntax, such as token order or abstract syntax tree (
AST
) structure, for reasoning about code.
Algorithm
5
shows the function
f(nums)
from Algorithm
3
after applying this mutation to change
n % 2 == 0
into
0 == n % 2
.

Swap If-Else Statements.

Another semantics-preserving mutation we use is the swapping of if-else statements, in which the
if
and
else
blocks are swapped and the
if
condition is logically negated. For example, a condition
if x > 0:
is rewritten as
if not (x > 0):
, with the bodies of the
if
and
else
blocks swapped accordingly. This code mutation maintains the program’s behaviour but alters the logical structure and control flow graph
(
Allen 1970
)
.
Robust models should recognise the semantic equivalence of these logically inverted blocks and produce consistent output predictions, regardless of the branching structure. Algorithm
6
shows the function
f(nums)
from Algorithm
3
after applying this mutation.

For-to-While Loop Conversion.

This code transformation rewrites
for
loops into semantically equivalent
while
loops. It introduces an index variable and manually iterates over the collection using the
len()
function and explicit indexing, while preserving all loop control logic. This transformation is particularly useful for assessing the robustness of
LLMs
in recognising semantically equivalent loop constructs. Algorithm
7
shows the function
f(nums)
from Algorithm
3
after converting the
for
loop into a
while
loop.

Partial Loop Unrolling.

To simulate partial loop unrolling while preserving semantics, we extract the last one or two iterations of a
while
loop body and duplicate them after the loop. The loop condition is also modified to run fewer iterations, e.g., reducing a bound
n
to
n - 1
in a
while i < n
condition. The extracted iterations are then executed sequentially after the loop, preserving the program behaviour.

8
shows the function
f(nums)
from Algorithm
7
, after converting the
for
loop into a
while
loop and then unrolling the last iteration.

LLM
-Based Program Output Prediction

As illustrated in Figure
1
, we address the task of using
LLMs
to predict the output of a Python program for a given input by employing an iterative querying strategy.
We chose code output prediction as our evaluation task because success in this task strongly correlates with “understanding” of code semantics, but it is also easy to check and automate the interaction.
Starting with a program and its input-output specification, we invoke a prompt generator that constructs and submits the query to the
LLM
. We then evaluate whether the predicted output matches the expected one. If the prediction is incorrect, a feedback prompt is generated and sent back to the
LLM
, explicitly requesting a revised answer. This loop continues until the model produces the correct output, the time limit is reached, or the number of iterations exceeds five, typically indicating that the model is stuck and repeatedly returning the same incorrect answer.

6
Experiments

The goal of our experiments was to answer the following research questions (RQs):

RQ1.
Are Large Language Models (
LLMs
) truly reasoning about code semantics, or merely guessing likely answers?

RQ2.
Does the interactive querying process help
LLMs
arrive at correct predictions supported by logically sound reasoning?

RQ3.
Do different code mutations lead
LLMs
to produce different predictions for the same program?

RQ4.
Are
LLMs
robust in understanding code against semantics-preserving mutations?
Our objective is to evaluate
LLMs
on the task of reasoning about code and predicting program outputs for given inputs.
All experiments were conducted with a 5-minute time limit to bound each interaction loop described in Section
5
.

Experimental Setup

All experiments were run using a NVIDIA L40S graphics card with 48GB of memory on an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 5418Y 48-Core CPU Processor and 251GB RAM.

Evaluation Benchmarks:

To evaluate our approach, we use two widely adopted benchmarks of Python programs:
LiveCodeBench

(
Jain et al. 2024
)
and
CruxEval

(
Gu et al. 2024b
)
.

LiveCodeBench
contains 479 programs submitted to programming contests across competition platforms, such as
LeetCode

(
LeetCode 2025
)
and
CodeForces

(
Codeforces 2025
)
.
CruxEval
contains 800 functions generated by
CodeLlama

(
CodeLlama 2023
)
, each accompanied by a set of input-output examples for evaluation.
These benchmarks are commonly used to assess the capabilities of
LLMs
across a range of code reasoning tasks, including test output prediction, program synthesis, and repair.
We apply each of the semantics-preserving code mutations described in Section
4
to the programs in the benchmarks by randomly selecting applicable statements for transformation. We then verify that the semantics of the original program is preserved in the mutated version by checking that the program passes all assertions. For each code mutation, we generate up to two mutated variants per program, ensuring that each variant contains at most one mutation.

Expert Analysis of
LLMs
’ Reasoning About Code

To answer our first research question (
RQ1
), Table
1
presents a detailed expert analysis of the reasoning behaviour of the evaluated
LLMs
on
LiveCodeBench
, focusing on prediction outcomes and code understanding. The lower section of Table
1
reports the proportion of correct predictions that were either guesses based on flawed reasoning or grounded in sound semantic reasoning, distinguishing between first-attempt success and success achieved after iterative refinement.

We define
sound semantic reasoning
as reasoning traces that are semantically accurate, that is, when models not only predict the correct output but also correctly describe the control and data flow leading from the inputs to the expected output. All interactions between the evaluated
LLMs
and programs in
LiveCodeBench
were manually inspected. Labelling was conducted blind to model identity. Each chain-of-thought (CoT) response leading to a correct prediction was independently labelled three times by experts with strong programming backgrounds, and disagreements were subsequently resolved. We did not extend this in-depth analysis to
CruxEval
due to its substantially larger size.

Overall predictive performance.

Among open-access models,
Qwen2.5-Coder
achieves the highest rate of correct predictions (62.6%), followed by
Qwen3-Coder
(50.5%) and
SemCoder
(48.0%). In contrast, the closed-access models substantially outperform all open-access models, with
GPT-5.2
achieving 97.5% and
Gemini-3
100.0% correct predictions.

Reasoning quality and guessing behaviour.

The proprietary models combine very high accuracy with negligible reliance on flawed reasoning. Only 0.2% of correct predictions from both
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
are classified as guesses. Moreover, the overwhelming majority of their correct predictions are grounded in sound reasoning within a single iteration, 91.2% for
GPT-5.2
and 99.8% for
Gemini-3
.
Among open-access models, reasoning robustness varies considerably.
Qwen2.5-Coder
exhibits a relatively low guessing rate (10.3%) and a high share of correct predictions grounded in sound reasoning within a single iteration (81.7%), indicating comparatively stable reasoning when successful.
SemCoder
shows a similarly high proportion of sound reasoning when correct (83.9%), though with lower overall predictive accuracy.

Notably,
Qwen3-Coder
demonstrates a very high proportion of sound first-iteration reasoning (95.4%) and a low guessing rate (3.0%), suggesting that when it predicts correctly, its reasoning is typically coherent and semantically grounded. However, its overall predictive accuracy remains substantially below that of the closed-access models. In contrast,
Llama3.2
,
Mistral
,
CodeGemma
, and
GraniteCode
exhibit markedly higher guessing rates, with 55.1%, 50.0%, 44.7%, and 43.1% of correct predictions, respectively, classified as flawed reasoning, indicating a greater tendency to reach correct outputs without fully sound logical justification.

Impact of interactive querying (RQ2).

To address our second research question (
RQ2
), we examine the percentage of correct predictions achieved through sound reasoning after an initial failed attempt. This metric captures the extent to which models can leverage feedback to refine incorrect reasoning.
Interactive querying yields measurable improvements for several open-access models.
Llama3.2
,
GraniteCode
, and
Qwen2.5-Coder
recover 18.9%, 13.8%, and 8.0% of their correct predictions, respectively, through iterative refinement.
CodeGemma
and
Qwen3-Coder
show only marginal gains (3.8% and 1.6%). These results suggest that iterative querying primarily benefits models with weaker initial reasoning, enabling partial recovery from incorrect first attempts.
For the closed-access models, iterative gains remain limited.
GPT-5.2
attributes 8.6% of its correct predictions to successful refinement after more than one iteration, while
Gemini-3
shows no measurable improvement through iteration, consistent with its near-perfect first attempt accuracy.

Release timeline and potential benchmark contamination.

These results must be interpreted in light of model release timelines. The evaluated versions of
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
correspond to releases from the end of 2025, whereas
LiveCodeBench
was published in 2024. All other evaluated models, except
Qwen3-Coder
, were released prior to the publication of
LiveCodeBench
. Consequently,
Qwen3-Coder
,
GPT-5.2
, and
Gemini-3
may have had access to
LiveCodeBench
or overlapping data during training. Although the exact composition of their training corpora is unknown, it is possible that part of their strong performance is attributable to prior exposure to benchmark instances. Therefore, the near-perfect results of
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
, and the strong reasoning quality of
Qwen3-Coder
, should be interpreted with caution.

Cost and accessibility
considerations.

Finally, performance differences should be considered alongside accessibility. All evaluated open-access models are freely available, whereas
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
are proprietary systems requiring paid API access. Although the closed-access models achieve substantially higher predictive accuracy and reasoning robustness, this advantage comes with a non-trivial cost differential that may limit adoption in academic or resource-constrained settings.

Prediction Stability Analysis

To assess semantic stability more directly, we analyse the proportion of programs for which each model predicts the correct output
only after
applying a mutation, that is, programs for which the model fails on the original version but succeeds on a mutated equivalent. Tables
4
and
5
report these results for
LiveCodeBench
and
CruxEval
, respectively.

On
LiveCodeBench
, several open-access models exhibit substantial gains through syntactic variation.
SemCoder
correctly predicts outputs for an additional 36.5% of programs across all mutations, increasing its effective coverage from 48.0% to over 84%.
Qwen2.5-Coder
gains 29.4%, and
Llama3.2
gains 26.9%.
Qwen3-Coder
shows a smaller but still notable gain of 14.8%. These findings indicate that syntactic variation can unlock correct predictions that were inaccessible under the original formulation.
In contrast, the closed-access models show very limited additional coverage on
LiveCodeBench
.
GPT-5.2
gains only 2.7%, and
Gemini-3
0.0%, reflecting their already near-perfect baseline performance. This suggests that their failures are not systematically corrected through simple syntactic variation.

On
CruxEval
, gains through mutation remain evident across all models.
Llama3.2
exhibits the largest aggregate increase (+21.4%), followed by
SemCoder
(+14.9%) and
Qwen2.5-Coder
(+13.5%).
Qwen3-Coder
gains 6.4%, while
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
increase by 4.6% and 15.5%, respectively. The relatively higher gains for
Gemini-3
on
CruxEval
suggest greater sensitivity to syntactic formulation in this benchmark.

Variable renaming consistently yields the largest recovery across both datasets, reinforcing the observation that identifier names, although semantically irrelevant, strongly influence model predictions. Humans are also known to experience reduced comprehension under renaming
(
Hofmeister et al. 2019
)
, highlighting a parallel sensitivity to naming conventions. Loop unrolling, which requires deeper structural reasoning, produces the most severe instability across models, further underscoring limited semantic generalisation.

Taken together, these findings answer
RQ3
affirmatively: different semantics-preserving mutations frequently lead
LLMs
to produce different predictions for functionally equivalent programs. The observed shifts are often substantial, even for state-of-the-art proprietary models.
Addressing
RQ4
, we conclude that current
LLMs
do not yet demonstrate stable, semantically grounded robustness. A truly robust model would exhibit minimal performance variation across equivalent program rewrites. However, the considerable fluctuations observed across both benchmarks indicate that predictions remain heavily influenced by surface-level syntactic cues rather than invariant program semantics. Robustness to such benign transformations is essential for reliable, generalisable, and explainable code reasoning systems.

Related Work

In recent years, several works have explored the ability of code models to
reason semantically
about programs, primarily through tasks such as code generation and output prediction
(
Barone et al. 2023
;
Ding et al. 2024
;
Gu et al. 2024b
;
Henkel et al. 2022
;
Jain et al. 2024
;
Orvalho et al. 2025
;
Wang and et al 2023
)
.
Prior work proposed improving the robustness of code models by developing a set of semantics-preserving code mutations, such as adding dead code, renaming variables, or inserting print statements, that maintain program semantics while fooling small code models (e.g.,
seq2seq
and
code2seq
)
(
Henkel et al. 2022
)
. These mutations were used to craft adversarial attacks and to enable robust optimization training, helping models better resist such perturbations. However, although comprehensive evaluations were conducted on small-scale code models, these methods were not tested on Large Language Models (
LLMs
).

ReCode

(
Wang and et al 2023
)
evaluates the robustness of code generation models by applying diverse code perturbations to prompts and quantifying the resulting accuracy drops, thereby providing post-hoc metrics for model stability. In contrast, our work moves beyond output robustness and instead scrutinises the underlying semantic reasoning of
LLMs
when confronted with code mutations, highlighting cases where correct predictions are supported by flawed logical processes.
It has also been shown that
LLMs
frequently generate Chain-of-Thought (CoT) explanations that do not faithfully reflect the attributes driving their predictions
(
Turpin et al. 2023
)
. However, that analysis was restricted to question answering datasets rather than code. The present study extends this line of investigation to program understanding and output prediction, revealing similar phenomena within code intelligence.

Recent work further demonstrated that CoT prompting can be brittle, with performance degrading sharply when models are evaluated on tasks, reasoning lengths, or prompt formats that deviate from the training distribution
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
. As in other CoT studies, these evaluations did not focus on code generation or program reasoning tasks, and therefore do not directly address semantic brittleness in program understanding.
In mathematical reasoning, strong performance on benchmark tasks has been shown to coexist with substantial weaknesses in formal proof construction, including flawed logic and unjustified assumptions
(
Petrov et al. 2025
)
. Similarly,
SeqCoBench

(
Maveli et al. 2025
)
introduced a benchmark containing over twenty Python transformations to assess whether
LLMs
can recognise functional equivalence, reporting that models perform only slightly better than syntax-based baselines.

A recent comprehensive survey
(
Song et al. 2026
)
systematically categorises reasoning failures in
LLMs
across informal, formal, and embodied domains, distinguishing between fundamental architectural limitations, application-specific weaknesses, and robustness issues. The survey highlights widespread brittleness under minor input variations and discusses failures in logical reasoning, mathematics, and coding benchmarks. However, it does not focus specifically on program semantics or semantics-preserving code transformations, nor does it provide expert analysis of whether correct code predictions are supported by sound reasoning. In contrast, our work provides an in-depth empirical investigation of semantic reasoning and robustness in program output prediction, demonstrating that even state-of-the-art proprietary models exhibit substantial instability under semantics-preserving mutations.

Several recent works explore
neuro-symbolic methods

(
Kassner et al. 2023
;
Calanzone et al. 2025
;
Jung et al. 2022
;
Mitchell et al. 2022
)
to improve logical consistency and explanation quality in non-coding domains. For instance, a neuro-symbolic loss has been proposed to fine-tune
LLMs
to respect external logical facts and rules, resulting in stronger logical consistency even on unseen but semantically similar knowledge
(
Calanzone et al. 2025
)
. Other approaches formulate explanation trees abductively and cast inference as a satisfiability problem over explanations, improving robustness in true/false question answering and commonsense reasoning
(
Jung et al. 2022
)
. While these approaches demonstrate the effectiveness of combining symbolic reasoning with neural models, they are developed for non-coding tasks such as natural-language inference and commonsense reasoning, and therefore do not address the specific challenges of program understanding or code generation.

Regarding
semantics-preserving code mutations
, there has been growing interest in augmenting program benchmarks through transformations. Syntax-based transformation rules have been proposed for data augmentation in Java programs
(
Yu et al. 2022
)
. Repair patterns have also been mined from Java code samples on Stack Overflow to support automated program repair
(
Liu and Zhong 2018
)
.
For C programs,
MultIPAs

(
Orvalho et al. 2022
)
applies semantics-preserving mutations and controlled bug injection to expand benchmarks. In Python,
BugLab

(
Allamanis et al. 2021
)
trains program repair models via data augmentation by injecting minor defects into correct code.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we assess the code reasoning and semantic robustness of Large Language Models (
LLMs
) for program output prediction, moving beyond accuracy to examine whether correct outputs follow from
sound semantic reasoning
and whether predictions remain stable under semantics-preserving code mutations. Using nine
LLMs
, including both open-source and proprietary models, on
LiveCodeBench
, expert annotation reveals that a substantial share of correct answers arises from flawed or superficial reasoning. Among all evaluated systems,
GPT-5.2
and
Gemini-3
achieve the strongest results in terms of predictive accuracy and reasoning quality, with
Qwen3-Coder
emerging as the most competitive open-access model.

However, high accuracy does not imply robustness. Our mutation-based evaluation on
LiveCodeBench
and
CruxEval
demonstrates marked sensitivity to minor syntactic rewrites despite unchanged semantics. Notably, even the top-performing proprietary models exhibit some of the largest performance drops (up to 70%) under semantics-preserving transformations. Thus, while closed-access models appear highly reliable under standard evaluation, they remain fragile when confronted with structurally equivalent program variants.

These findings demonstrate that accuracy alone is not a reliable proxy for genuine code understanding. For
trustworthy
software engineering applications,
LLMs
should be evaluated not only on correctness but also on reasoning consistency and robustness to semantically equivalent program transformations. Future work should therefore integrate formal semantic constraints, mutation-aware evaluation protocols, and robustness-oriented training objectives to promote stable and semantically grounded code reasoning.
