Beyond renal response: sustained systemic disease control protects against adverse long-term outcomes in lupus nephritis
Authors: Konstantinos Drougkas, Spyridon Katechis, Myrto Kostopoulou, Christina Tsalapaki, Sofia Flouda, Maria Pappa, Marija Bakula, Ivan Padjen, Myrto Nikoloudaki, Pinelopi Konstantopoulou, George Moustakas, Dimitrios Vassilopoulos, George Bertsias, Paraskevi V Voulgari, Dimitrios T Boumpas, Antonis Fanouriakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesLupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) and Definition of Remission in SLE (DORIS) are associated with improved outcomes in SLE; however, their value in lupus nephritis (LN) remains unexplored. We sought to evaluate their prognostic value on organ damage accrual, renal flares and chronic kidney disease in patients with LN.MethodsMulticentre retrospective cohort study of patients with biopsy-proven LN who achieved renal response. Enrolment date was the time of first renal response. Longitudinal data were analysed using Cox proportional hazards models. Modified LLDAS (mLLDAS) and DORIS (mDORIS) attainment and sustained durations were evaluated.Results155 patients with 1703 visits were included. During a median follow-up of 3.5 years (IQR 4.5), 127 patients (81.9%) achieved mLLDAS and 92 (59.4%) achieved mDORIS. Both states demonstrated robust protective effects across all outcomes. For organ damage, mLLDAS attainment was associated with 85% risk reduction (adjusted HR (aHR 0.15, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.32), while mDORIS conferred a 79% reduction (aHR 0.21, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.55). Periods under mLLDAS were also associated with a 73% reduction in renal flare risk (aHR 0.27, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.51), with similar effects for mDORIS. Sustained achievement resulted in progressively greater protection, with the strongest effects seen for ≥24 consecutive months. Early attainment protected against damage accrual (aHR 0.31, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.61) and renal flares (aHR 0.51, 95% CI 0.29 to 1.00).ConclusionsFollowing renal response, achievement of mLLDAS and mDORIS is strongly associated with improved outcomes in LN patients. Earlier attainment and longer duration in these states confer progressively greater protection.

Introduction

Lupus nephritis (LN) represents one of the most severe manifestations of SLE with high rates of morbidity and mortality. Among other factors, renal flares are widely acknowledged as a key contributor to poor long-term outcomes and overall prognosis. Both data from LN clinical trials and real-world evidence indicate a significant burden of LN flares following initial response, with reported rates ranging from approximately 10% to 40%.1 Importantly, each flare of LN usually leads to irreversible nephron loss and shortens kidney lifespan, while subsequent flares are associated with lower rates of treatment response.2 Apart from renal disease activity, extra-renal manifestations frequently persist or emerge during the disease course, especially when treatment tapering is initiated. Notably, persistence of extra-renal disease activity is associated with increased risk of major organ flares, including the kidney and the central nervous system.3 Therefore, in LN, overall disease control, rather than only renal, is an important treatment target to prevent long-term poor outcomes in terms of organ damage accrual, risk of flares and mortality, as highlighted in the overarching principles of the recently updated European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) recommendations for SLE with kidney involvement.4

Two widely adopted treatment targets for overall disease control in SLE are the Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) and the Definition of Remission in SLE (DORIS).5 6 Sustained attainment of either target has been consistently associated with reduced irreversible damage and flares, as well as improved health-related quality of life.7 In the case of LN, a recent study showed that attaining LLDAS or DORIS on top of renal response at 12 months following the onset of nephritis was associated with reduced risk for subsequent renal flares.8 However, the prognostic value of sustained disease control during longitudinal follow-up of LN patients has not been explored yet.

Based on the above, in this retrospective cohort study, we aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of achieving sustained LLDAS and DORIS states following renal response in patients with LN. Specifically, we sought to evaluate (1) the attainability of these targets, (2) the association of each target attainment and its sustained duration with subsequent organ damage accrual, renal flares, chronic kidney disease (CKD) and mortality, as well as (3) the impact of early versus late target achievement on long-term outcomes.

Patients and methods

Study population

This is a retrospective cohort study of adult patients with SLE (classified by 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC)9 and/or 2019 EULAR/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria10), who had a first diagnosis of biopsy-proven LN (class II, III, IV, V, III/IV+V) according to the 2003 International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society LN classification system11 and were followed between January 2010 and December 2025 in seven centres. Patients were included if they achieved a renal response (partial or complete) and had a follow-up time of ≥3 months following that response; patients missing key clinical variables (kidney biopsy results, date and type of renal response, variables required for LLDAS and DORIS ascertainment) from their medical records were excluded from the study.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Attikon University Hospital (protocol number 46/20-01-2025). Because the study relied on routinely collected and deidentified data, informed consent from patients was not required.

Patient and public involvement

Neither patients nor the public were involved in the design or conduct of this study.

Data collection

The inclusion (enrolment) date in the study for each patient was the time of their first renal response. Data from enrolment and all succeeding visits were extracted in a prospective manner from hospital medical records. The minimum required visit frequency was 3 months during the first year since enrolment and then every 6 months.

At baseline, demographics (age, sex, smoking status), clinical history (date of SLE and LN diagnosis, major comorbidities, prior treatments and major SLE manifestations), kidney disease features (pathological and clinical characteristics, type of renal response, kidney function metrics), immunologic profile (ANA, anti-double stranded antibodies (anti-dsDNA), C3 and C4 levels) and LLDAS/DORIS components were documented. During follow-up visits, the following data were captured: kidney function (estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), based on Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula), 24 hours proteinuria and urine sediment; SLE disease activity (SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K))12 and prednisone dose; ongoing treatments; immunologic parameters (anti-dsDNA antibodies, complement levels); renal flares and new organ damage accrual (assessed with the SLICC/ACR Damage Index (SDI)13).

Definitions

Complete renal response (CRR) was defined as a urine protein:creatinine ratio (UPCR)<50 mg/mmol (or proteinuria<0.5 g/24 hours) and normal or near-normal (within 10% of normal eGFR if previously abnormal) eGFR and a prednisone dose ≤10 mg/day. Partial renal response (PRR) was defined as ≥50% reduction in proteinuria to subnephrotic levels and normal or near-normal GFR and a prednisone dose ≤15 mg/day.14 15

Renal flares were defined as follows: (1) proteinuric, as a reproducible doubling of UPCR to >100 mg/mmol (or proteinuria >1 g/24 hours) after CRR or reproducible doubling of UPCR to >200 mg/mmol (or proteinuria >2 g/24 hours) after PRR, (2) nephritic, as a reproducible increase of serum creatinine by ≥30% (or, decrease in eGFR by ≥10%) and active urine sediment with increase in glomerular haematuria by ≥10 red blood cells per high power field, irrespective of changes in proteinuria.14 Development of CKD was defined as eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 in ≥two consecutive tests, spaced ≥3 months apart.16

Attainment or not of low disease activity states was assessed at each visit. The original definitions of LLDAS and DORIS include the Safety of Estrogens in Lupus Erythematosus National Assessment-SLEDAI Physician Global Assessment (PGA) as a component.5 6 However, given the retrospective nature of the study and the fact that PGA was not consistently assessed by the same physician, this component was excluded from determining LLDAS and DORIS status.17 Modified LLDAS (mLLDAS) was defined as: (1) SLEDAI-2K score ≤4, with no activity in major organ systems (renal, neurological, cardiopulmonary, vasculitis and fever), (2) no new features of lupus disease activity compared with the previous assessment, defined as any new SLEDAI-2K component, (3) a current prednisolone (or equivalent) dose of ≤7.5 mg/day and (4) standard maintenance doses of immunosuppressives and approved biologics. Attainment of modified DORIS (mDORIS) remission was defined as: (1) clinical SLEDAI-2K score=0, (2) current prednisolone dose ≤5 mg/day and (3) stable maintenance doses of immunosuppressives and biologics.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used as appropriate. Predictors of mLLDAS attainment were evaluated with univariable logistic regression analysis. Variables with p<0.05 were entered in a multivariable model via forward stepwise selection, with variable retention based on the likelihood ratio test and Akaike information criterion. Longitudinal analyses were performed using visit-level data in a counting-process format, with time measured in months from entry in the cohort. Follow-up intervals were defined between consecutive visits, and all time-varying exposures were evaluated at the start of each interval to ensure temporal ordering.

The associations between mLLDAS or mDORIS attainment and the risk of renal flares, organ damage accrual (SDI increase compared with the previous visit) and the composite outcome of CKD or death were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models. Recurrent events (renal flares and SDI increases) were analysed using the Andersen-Gill extension, whereas the CKD/death composite was modelled as a time-to-first event. All models incorporated robust sandwich variance estimators clustered by patient to account for within-patient correlation and were adjusted for baseline age and sex. Survival curves were derived from the fitted Cox models using predicted baseline hazards, with other covariates fixed at representative values.

To explore the effect of sustained disease control, time-varying variables were constructed for ≥6, ≥12 and ≥24 consecutive months of continuous mLLDAS or mDORIS attainment, evaluated at the start of each interval, with follow-up time before achieving each duration as the reference category. Consecutive duration of continuous mLLDAS or mDORIS attainment was also evaluated as a continuous variable, scaled per 1-month increase. In addition, the proportion of cumulative follow-up time spent in mLLDAS or mDORIS was calculated and analysed both as a binary exposure (≥50% vs <50%) and as a continuous variable scaled per 10% increase. Incidence rates were calculated for each outcome according to exposure status and expressed per 100 person-years, with exact Poisson 95% CIs.

To evaluate the effect of early overall disease control following renal response, while minimising immortal time bias, 12-month landmark analyses were performed. Patients were classified according to whether mLLDAS was achieved at any time during the first 12 months and were included only if event-free and under follow-up at the landmark time point. Subsequent renal flares and SDI increases were modelled using Andersen-Gill Cox models, and Kaplan-Meier curves were used to illustrate time-to-first event.

All analyses were performed with R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, V.4.5.1).

Results

Low disease activity and remission are attainable treatment targets in LN patients

A total of 155 patients with LN who achieved renal response were followed for a median (IQR) period of 3.5 (4.5) years. Mean (SD) age was 39.1 (14.8) years while 22.6% of patients aged >50 years. At baseline (enrolment date), 100 patients (64.5%) achieved CRR while the remaining 55 (35.5%) had PRR with median (IQR) time to first renal response of 5 (5.0) months. Among partial responders, 31 (56.4%) reached CRR during follow-up. Kidney function at enrolment generally reflected a response state with preserved kidney function (mean (SD) eGFR 95.3 (26.2) mL/min/1.73 m²) and low-grade proteinuria (median (IQR) 0.4 (0.6) g/day). Nevertheless, 11.6% of patients had eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73 m² and 16.8% had proteinuria>1 g/day. The disease activity was low-moderate with median (IQR) SLEDAI-2K 4 (6) (0.6). However, 8.4% of the patients had SLEDAI-2K>10 indicating ongoing systemic activity despite renal response. Baseline organ damage (SDI>0) was evident in 29.7% of patients at the enrolment visit. The distribution of damage across SDI domains at baseline and last follow-up is shown in online supplemental figure 1. At baseline, 46.5% were receiving prednisone doses >7.5 mg/day, while serological activity was evident in half of the cohort (anti-dsDNA: 45.2% and low C3/C4: 46.5%).

At baseline, the majority of patients were not in a low disease activity state (103/155, 66.5%). The most common contributing factor for not being in mLLDAS at baseline was prednisone >7.5 mg/day (n=72, 70%), followed by SLEDAI-2K >4 (n=61, 59.2%) and renal activity (n=60, 58.3%), often in combination (online supplemental figure 2A). Among patients with SLEDAI-2K>4, most had combined renal and extra-renal activity (n=43), while 15 had isolated non-renal activity and three had renal activity alone. Non-renal manifestations were mainly anti-dsDNA positivity (n=44) and low complement levels (n=40), followed by rash (n=18) and arthritis (n=15) (online supplemental figure 2B). During follow-up, however, 81.9% of patients achieved mLLDAS at least once, while 59.4% achieved mDORIS remission. The presence of rash (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.98), elevated proteinuria levels (OR: 0.71, 95% CI 0.54 to 0.93, per 1 g/day) and higher glucocorticoid dose (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.90 to 0.99, per 1 mg/day), at the time of renal response, were independently associated with decreased likelihood of subsequently attaining mLLDAS (online supplemental table 1). Overall, mLLDAS and mDORIS were present in 57.9% and 35.9% of all visits, respectively. The median (IQR) time to first attainment of a low disease activity state was 3 (9) months. Among patients achieving these states, the median (IQR) number of visits spent in mLLDAS and mDORIS per patient was 6 (7.0) and 2 (6), respectively. Notably, approximately 63% of patients attained mLLDAS for ≥50% of follow-up, while 36% also achieved remission for the same duration.

Low disease activity and remission are protective against organ damage accrual in LN patients

A total of 47 new organ damage events in 37 (23.9%) patients occurred. Both mLLDAS and mDORIS demonstrated strong protective effects against organ damage accrual (table 2, figure 1A, B). Compared with periods out of target, mLLDAS periods were associated with 85% reduction in subsequent organ damage accrual (adjusted HR (aHR) 0.15, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.32), while mDORIS conferred a 79% reduction (aHR 0.21, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.55). Accordingly, incidence rates for SDI increase were significantly lower during mLLDAS (2.11 per 100 person-years, 95% CI 0.91 to 4.15) and mDORIS (2.04 per 100 person-years, 95% CI 0.66 to 4.77) compared with periods of active disease (14.70 and 10.50 per 100 person-years, respectively). Interestingly, this protective effect was most pronounced for renal damage, with significantly lower incidence rates during mLLDAS periods, while glucocorticoid-related damage was also substantially reduced (online supplemental figure 3).

Both targets demonstrated clear dose-response relationships with duration of sustained disease control. Sustained mLLDAS for ≥6, ≥12 and ≥24 consecutive months showed progressive protection (aHR 0.15, 0.12 and 0.11, respectively; all p≤0.003), while sustained mDORIS for ≥6 months and ≥12 months demonstrated similar effects (aHR 0.24 and 0.20, respectively; both p≤0.012) (figure 2A, C). Notably, no organ damage events occurred among patients maintaining mDORIS for ≥24 consecutive months. Spending ≥50% of follow-up time in either mLLDAS (aHR 0.21, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.42) or mDORIS (aHR 0.16, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.53) was associated with substantial risk reduction. When cumulative exposure was modelled continuously, each 10% increase in time spent in mLLDAS or mDORIS reduced organ damage risk by approximately 20%. Similarly, each additional consecutive month in mLLDAS or mDORIS conferred a 9% risk reduction. Interestingly, achieving mLLDAS during the first year post-response was associated with significantly lower risk of damage accrual (aHR 0.31, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.61) (figure 3A).

Low disease activity and remission are associated with reduced risk of renal flares, chronic kidney disease and death

During follow-up, 53 renal flares occurred in 39 (25.2%) patients (proteinuric: 43, nephritic: 4, mixed: 6). Both low disease activity states demonstrated significant protection against renal flares (table 2, figure 1C, D). Periods of mLLDAS were associated with 73% reduction in renal flare risk (aHR 0.27, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.51), with similar protective effects for mDORIS (aHR 0.28, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.56). Incidence rates of flares during mLLDAS (3.95 per 100 person-years, 95% CI 2.21 to 6.51) and mDORIS periods (3.27 per 100 person-years, 95% CI 1.41 to 6.45) were substantially lower than periods not meeting target criteria (14.30 and 11.20 per 100 person-years, respectively).

The protective effects increased with longer durations of target attainment. Sustained achievement of mLLDAS for ≥6, ≥12 and ≥24 consecutive months showed progressively increasing flare protection (aHR 0.30, 0.25 and 0.15, respectively; all p≤0.001), while mDORIS demonstrated comparable results at these thresholds (figure 2B, D). Spending ≥50% of time in mLLDAS or mDORIS was also associated with a 73–77% reduction in renal flare risk. Both states showed dose-response relationships: each 10% increase in time spent in mLLDAS or mDORIS was associated with an approximately 14% reduction in flare risk, while each additional month of consecutive time in either target resulted in 4% further risk reduction. In addition, achieving these targets within 12 months after initial renal response was associated with reduced flare risk (aHR 0.51, 95% CI 0.29 to 1.00) (figure 3B).

For the composite outcome of CKD or death, a total of 20 events (CKD 16—death 4) occurred during follow-up. Both mLLDAS and mDORIS demonstrated strong protective effects (figure 1E, F). Specifically, mLLDAS periods were associated with 84% risk reduction (aHR 0.16, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.46), with mDORIS similarly conferring 68% reduction (aHR 0.30, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.91). Incidence rates of CKD or death during mLLDAS visits (1.08 per 100 person-years, 95% CI 0.29 to 2.75) and mDORIS (1.24 per 100 person-years, 95% CI 0.26 to 3.64) were significantly lower when compared with periods not meeting these states (6.64 and 4.57 per 100 person-years, respectively). Due to the low number of events, further stratified analysis exploring durations and early achievement was not feasible.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we demonstrate that both mLLDAS and mDORIS remission are attainable treatment targets in LN patients following a first renal response. Sustained attainment of either mLLDAS or mDORIS is strongly associated with reduced organ damage accrual, lower rates of renal flares and decreased risk of CKD or death. Moreover, longer durations in these targets conferred progressively greater protection. Finally, early attainment of low disease activity within the first year after renal response predicted improved long-term outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the effect of sustained systemic disease control in patients with LN.

In our study, we opted to use first renal response as the baseline (enrolment) date, as we sought to assess the value of systemic disease control following initial response of the renal domain, the cardinal manifestation in these patients. This design deliberately focused on the natural history of LN in the ‘post-response’ phase, in which treatment decisions regarding maintenance therapy and disease monitoring are made. In this context, a key reason why systemic targets may be particularly relevant in LN is that renal response at a single time-point does not necessarily reflect subsequent global disease control. The presence of active extrarenal disease is frequent among patients with LN and is associated with increased risk of major organ flares and worse quality of life.18 19 In our cohort, the presence of rash and arthritis was the most frequent active extra-renal manifestations contributing to non-mLLDAS attainment. Importantly, cutaneous involvement was identified as an independent predictor of never reaching a low disease activity state, with this finding being reported in studies of general SLE populations.2023 Further, immunosuppressive treatment in LN requires careful balancing, as both undertreatment can predispose to relapses and overtreatment may increase the risk of infections and toxicity.24 All these elements are appropriately captured in LLDAS and DORIS remission states.

The majority of patients in our cohort achieved a low disease activity state at least once (mLLDAS: ~80% and mDORIS: ~60%), while approximately 60% maintained mLLDAS for ≥50% of follow-up time. This seems to contrast with earlier SLE studies, which have reported that kidney involvement is a negative predictor of LLDAS attainment and/or sustained time spent in this target.5 21 25 Nevertheless, these findings are not necessarily contradictory; rather, they highlight the relative importance of different phases of the disease. By design, our cohort study began when renal activity had improved to a clinically meaningful degree, thereby reducing one major barrier to meeting the ‘no major organ activity (including renal)’ component included in LLDAS. At the same time, the observation that most patients in our cohort were still not in mLLDAS at inclusion indicates that renal response does not automatically translate into systemic disease activity control and adequate glucocorticoid tapering; instead, it appears to create a window of opportunity, in which systemic targets become feasible.

In our cohort, both mLLDAS and mDORIS remission demonstrated robust protective effects across all outcomes examined. Notably, we showed that periods under mLLDAS and mDORIS remission were associated with approximately 80–85% reduction in risk for new irreversible damage, with the most pronounced reductions observed in renal and glucocorticoid-related damage. Similarly, attainment of these states across follow-up visits reduced risk of renal flares by ~72%. These findings are consistent with previous studies in general SLE populations, demonstrating the protective role of low disease activity and remission in long-term outcomes, including damage accrual, disease flares, quality of life and mortality2630; most importantly, they extend this paradigm specifically to patients with LN, in whom control of extrarenal disease has been understudied to date.

Importantly, our findings do not suggest shifting away from a ‘kidney first’ approach or a change in therapeutic hierarchy in patients with LN. Rather, they support a sequential approach: following renal response, treatment decisions during the maintenance phase may reasonably aim to prevent extra-renal activity and flares, address residual renal activity (particularly in partial responders) and minimise glucocorticoid exposure. Since most lupus treatments act on both the renal and extra-renal domains, our results do not advocate for specific therapies, but for treating towards clearly defined targets that reflect overall disease control across organ systems. As much as it dominates the clinical picture, kidney involvement in lupus occurs in the context of a systemic disease, a concept underlined also in the updated EULAR recommendations. In this context, escalation of therapy, including the introduction of biologic agents, may be considered when extra-renal activity persists despite achievement of renal response.

For renal flares in particular, our results corroborate findings from a recent study, which evaluated the effect of attaining LLDAS or DORIS at 12 months following LN onset, demonstrating that LLDAS achievement at this single time point was associated with approximately 70% reduction in subsequent renal flares.8 While this work was the first to describe the importance of attaining low disease activity in LN, the researchers evaluated LLDAS attainment at a single time point (a snapshot), rather than longitudinally. Our study addresses this critical gap by exploring mLLDAS and mDORIS attainment at every follow-up visit, thereby capturing the value of sustained achievement over time. In this context, in our cohort, maintaining mLLDAS and/or mDORIS for several consecutive duration thresholds was associated with progressively stronger protective effects against organ damage and renal flares, with the greatest protection observed in patients sustaining mLLDAS for 24 consecutive months. Our findings parallel those from a recent study by Pitsigavdaki et al, who showed that increasing thresholds of sustained LLDAS or DORIS exposure in SLE patients were associated with progressively lower risks of damage and flares.31 Further, we demonstrated that early attainment of mLLDAS within 12 months after renal response was associated with lower risk of both organ damage accrual and renal flares (aHR 0.31 and 0.51, respectively). This finding supports the existence of a post-response window of opportunity, during which systemic disease control may set patients on a more favourable long-term trajectory.

For the composite outcome of CKD or death, both states were associated with decreased risk (aHR 0.16–0.30), with incidence rates being markedly lower in low disease activity and remission periods. Although the number of events was low, the direction and magnitude were consistent with the overall pattern across outcomes and with the concept that sustained disease control may translate into long-term renal preservation. Similarly, Cheung et al reported fewer events of kidney function deterioration in patients attaining LLDAS 12 months post-nephritis onset, with a respective area under the curve of 0.71.8

Our study has limitations that need to be acknowledged. First, our cohort is comprised exclusively of white participants, which limits the generalisability to other racial and ethnic groups. The limited number of CKD or death events limited generalisability and precision for these outcomes. Also, the study was not designed to directly compare mLLDAS versus mDORIS; because of the overlap between the two states and limited numbers in mutually exclusive categories, our results should not be interpreted as evidence that one target is superior to the other. The exclusion of PGA from the LLDAS and DORIS definitions, due to concerns regarding inter-observer variability in a retrospective cohort, may limit direct comparability with studies using the full criteria. Also, SLEDAI-2K was obtained retrospectively from medical records rather than prospectively assessed, which may have introduced misclassification in mLLDAS and mDORIS ascertainment. Finally, our assumption that patients remain in mLLDAS or mDORIS for the entire duration between visits in which they meet criteria may overestimate time in these states; this would nevertheless probably bias our results toward the null and underestimate the true protective effects.

In conclusion, mLLDAS and mDORIS remission are attainable and clinically meaningful targets in patients with LN following renal response. Sustained attainment of these states is associated with substantially improved long-term outcomes, with additional benefit conferred when targets are achieved earlier. Taken together, these findings support the concept that low disease activity states have the potential to extend the treat-to-target paradigm specifically in LN, bridging the gap between initial renal improvement and sustained long-term disease control.
