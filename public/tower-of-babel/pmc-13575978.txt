Genetic and molecular evidence linking CTSH to Alzheimer's disease pathophysiology
Authors: Cynthia Picard, Gabriel Aumont‐Rodrigue, Isabel Sarty, Julia Loncke, Mélissa Savard, Laurence Maligne‐Bruneau, Judes Poirier, for the PREVENT‐AD research group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONLysosomal dysfunction contributes to Alzheimer's disease (AD) by impairing protein clearance and promoting neuroinflammation. Cathepsin H (CTSH), a lysosomal protease, recently emerged as a protective AD locus. We investigated how CTSH is regulated and how it influences early AD pathophysiology.METHODSWe analyzed genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and brain tissue across three independent clinical and post mortem cohorts to assess CTSH regulation, expression, and disease associations.RESULTSThe coding variant rs2289702 acts as a cis‐regulatory variant, altering CTSH mRNA and protein levels. The T allele associates with better cognition and reduced amyloid plaque burden. CSF CTSH correlates with total tau, phosphorylated tau181, neuronal markers, and multiple glial and complement‐related inflammatory proteins.DISCUSSIONCTSH tracks early neurodegenerative, synaptic, and inflammatory changes, and co‐expression analyses link it to broader immune–metabolic pathways. The findings position CTSH as a genetically regulated contributor to AD pathophysiology.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, biomarkers, cathepsin H, gene regulation, lysosomes, pathophysiology

BACKROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia worldwide and represents a major public health challenge. While advancing age is the most prominent risk factor, extensive evidence demonstrates that genetic variation plays a central role in shaping susceptibility to AD and in defining the biological pathways that drive disease development. Studies of twins and families indicate that a substantial proportion of AD risk is inherited, with heritability estimates commonly exceeding 60%, underscoring the importance of genetic determinants in disease etiology. 1

A subset of AD cases arise from rare, highly penetrant mutations that follow an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance. Pathogenic variants in amyloid precursor protein (APP), presenilin 1 (PSEN1), and presenilin 2 (PSEN2) lead to early‐onset disease, often decades before typical age of presentation. These mutations alter the processing of APP, resulting in abnormal production of amyloid beta (Aβ) peptides. 1

The majority of patients, however, develop late‐onset AD, which does not follow simple Mendelian inheritance but instead reflects the combined effects of many genetic variants with modest individual impact. Among these, allelic variation at the apolipoprotein E (APOE) locus has the largest influence on risk and age at onset, with the ε4 allele conferring substantially increased susceptibility compared to other APOE isoforms. 2 , 3 Beyond APOE, large‐scale genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have uncovered numerous additional risk loci, revealing that genes involved in immune regulation, lipid handling, endocytosis, and intracellular trafficking contribute to disease risk. 4 , 5 , 6

In the largest AD GWAS conducted to date, Bellenguez et al. reported cathepsin H (CTSH) as a newly identified protective locus for the disease. 6 CTSH encodes a lysosomal cysteine protease that contributes to the degradation of proteins within the endo‐lysosomal system. Unlike many cathepsins that function primarily as endopeptidases, CTSH displays both endopeptidase and aminopeptidase activity, enabling it to process a broad range of substrates. CTSH is expressed in multiple tissues, including the central nervous system, and is particularly abundant in immune cells such as microglia, where lysosomal function is critical for debris clearance and immune surveillance. 7

Dysfunction of the lysosomal–autophagic pathway is increasingly recognized as a central feature of AD pathogenesis. 8 , 9 Impaired proteolytic capacity can lead to inefficient degradation of Aβ peptides, accumulation of misfolded proteins, and sustained neuroinflammatory responses. 10 , 11 , 12 Specific cathepsins such as CTSB, CTSD, CTSE, CTSL, and CTSS are implicated in influencing Aβ metabolism and tau pathology, while others like CTSG, CTSH, and CTSX are linked to inflammatory processes that may exacerbate neurodegeneration. 7

Together, these genetic and functional insights support the idea that altered CTSH activity may influence AD pathogenesis, potentially through effects on lysosomal proteolysis, microglial function, neuroinflammation, and the handling of neurotoxic proteins.

In this study, we investigate the regulatory and functional landscape of rs2289702, a coding variant located in CTSH, across independent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and brain cohorts throughout the AD spectrum. We examine its effects on CTSH expression at both mRNA and protein levels, assess its association with cognitive performance and amyloid pathology, and characterize CTSH‐related molecular networks. By integrating genetic regulation, biomarker correlations, and co‐expression analyses, we provide new evidence positioning CTSH as a biologically meaningful contributor to early AD‐related processes.

METHODS

The asymptomatic Pre‐symptomatic Evaluation of Experimental or Novel Treatments for Alzheimer's Disease cohort

The Pre‐symptomatic Evaluation of Experimental or Novel Treatments for Alzheimer's Disease (PREVENT‐AD) cohort comprises asymptomatic individuals who are considered “at risk” of developing sporadic AD due to parental or multi‐sibling history. 13 Most participants were > 60 years old when recruited, although some individuals aged 55 to 59 years were included if they were within 15 years of the onset of their youngest affected relative's symptoms. The Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) were used at the study eligibility visit to confirm normal cognition. Memory, attention, language, and visuospatial abilities were evaluated with the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS). A subset of individuals agreed to undergo a lumbar puncture for CSF collection. PREVENT‐AD participants provided informed consent and took part in a longitudinal follow‐up. All procedures were approved by the McGill University Faculty of Medicine Review Board and complied with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Additional information about the PREVENT‐AD is documented elsewhere. 13

After an overnight fast, lumbar punctures were performed using a Sprotte 24‐gauge atraumatic needle. Within 4 hours, CSF samples were centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 minutes at room temperature, to exclude cells and insoluble materials. CSF samples were aliquoted into 0.5 mL polypropylene cryotubes and stored at –80°C.

AD biomarkers Aβ42, total tau (t‐tau) and phosphorylated tau (p‐tau181) were measured using the validated Innotest enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Fujirebio) following the standardized protocols established by the Biomarkers for Alzheimer's Disease and Parkinson's Disease (BIOMARKAPD) consortium (Aβ42 Cat.# 81583, tTau Cat.# 81579, and pTau181 Cat.# 81581). 14 All other protein levels (including CTSH and synaptic and inflammatory markers) were quantified using the OLINK HT platform (OLINK Proteomics) using Proximity Extension Assay technology. Samples were analyzed in two consecutive runs, with plate and sample controls applied for normalization. Samples that failed quality control were removed. Results are expressed as Normalized Protein eXpression (NPX) values, which are log2‐transformed relative concentrations.

The Religious Orders Study and Memory and Aging Project cohort

The Religious Orders Study and Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP) is a longitudinal clinical–pathologic cohort of aging and AD. Across the United Stated, participants were enrolled from > 40 groups of religious orders (nuns, priests, brothers, etc.). Enrollment required participants to have no recognized symptoms of dementia. Since 1994, medical conditions have been tracked via clinical assessments or self‐reported information. AD status was determined by a computer algorithm based on a cognitive test performance with a series of discrete clinical judgments made by a neuropsychologist and clinician. Individuals were categorized as cognitively unimpaired (CU) if they were diagnosed without dementia or mild cognitive impairment (MCI). The diagnosis of dementia and AD follows recognized clinical guidelines. A clinician reviewed all cases determined by this algorithm. Upon death, a post mortem neuropathologic evaluation was performed to include assessments of AD pathology, cerebral infarcts, Lewy body disease, and other pathologies common in aging and dementia. Subjects’ consent was obtained according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The ROS and MAP studies are described in more detail elsewhere. 17 , 18 The original authors from the ROSMAP study were not involved in the preparation of this manuscript.

Omics data from the ROSMAP cohort

Proteomics data derived from frontal cortical samples were downloaded from the Accelerating Medicines Partnership Alzheimer's Disease (AMP‐AD) Knowledge Portal (https://adknowledgeportal.synapse.org, synapse ID:syn17008935). These measurements were obtained using tandem mass tag (TMT) isobaric labeling and synchronous precursor selection‐based MS3 (SPS‐MS3) mass spectrometry. Gene expression data, obtained by microarray, were downloaded from AMP‐AD Knowledge Portal (synapse ID:syn3800853). Genotyping data, accessed through the same portal (synapse ID:syn3157325) were imputed as previously described for the PREVENT‐AD cohort.

The Douglas–Bell Canada Brain Bank cohort

Autopsy‐confirmed control and AD brains were obtained from the Douglas–Bell Canada Brain Bank (DBCBB, Montreal, Canada). Medical records, neuropsychological examinations, and caregiver reports indicate no evidence of memory loss, or neurological or neuropsychiatric diseases in the older adults control group. Controls only exhibited neuropathology that is associated with healthy aging (plaque and tangles densities < 20/mm3 and < 10mm3 in at least one hippocampal and neocortical section). AD cases were required to meet the histopathological National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke–Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association criteria for a definitive diagnosis of AD. 19 This study conformed to the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association and was approved by the ethics board of the Douglas Mental Health University Institute. This study complied with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Each participant provided written informed consent.

Proteomics data from the DBCBB cohort

Fifty mg of frontal cortical tissue were thawed on ice and homogenized using a Biomasher (Omni International). After a 1‐minute centrifugation at 4°C and at 14,000 g, the pellet was resuspended in 200 µl of Tris‐buffered saline (TBS, 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl) containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid–free protease inhibitors (Pierce). The homogenized samples were then transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 100,000 g for 30 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was removed, aliquoted, and stored at –80°C. For protein quantification, 50 µl (0.5 µg/µl) of supernatant was sent to the McGill Genome Centre and analyzed using the OLINK HT platform (OLINK Proteomics), according to manufacturer's instructions. All samples were run in a single batch.

Genotyping data from the DBCBB cohort

DNA extractions from tissue samples were performed using the Maxwell 16 instrument and the Maxwell 16 Tissue DNA purification kits (Cat#AS1030) from Promega. Genotyping and imputation were performed as previously described for the PREVENT‐AD cohort.

Statistical analyses

Haploview software was used to calculate D' values in linkage disequilibrium (LD) analyses. For quantitative trait locus (QTL) analyses, P values were calculated using PLINK. 15 The significance of rs2289702 T allele distribution in relation to clinical and post mortem evaluations was calculated using the chi‐squared method. Linear models and analyses of variance were constructed with the help of JMP Pro 18 software. For co‐expression study, beta and false discovery rate–corrected P values were obtained using the MatrixEQTL package from R. 20

RESULTS

Rs2289702 (Gly11Arg) is the most significant cis‐polymorphism affecting CTSH protein levels

For cis‐pQTL (protein QTL) analyses, CTSH protein levels were measured in the CSF and brain tissues via OLINK HT technology and the TMT‐based method. Genotyping information was made available using DNA chips and imputation. In the asymptomatic PREVENT‐AD cohort (Figure 1A), in which participants are at risk of developing AD, rs2289702 reached genome‐wide significance (P = 1.38E‐10), despite the relatively small number of baseline samples (n = 81). The AD protective rs12592898 variant reported by Bellenguez et al. 6 approached genome‐wide significance (P = 5.91E‐08) in PREVENT‐AD. In Figure 1B, the post mortem ROSMAP cohort's results yielded to even stronger P values (P = 1.02E‐27 for rs2289702 and P = 3.71E‐23 for rs12592898, n = 274). Rs2289702 reached locus‐wide significance in the DBCBB cohort (Figure 1C, P = 3.89E‐06, n = 79). LD plots (Figure S1 in supporting information) show that rs2289702 and rs12592898 are in very strong LD in the three independent cohorts. These results clearly support the amino acid–altering variant (Gly11Arg) as the functional SNP in this association.

Rs2289702 (Gly11Arg) affects CTSH gene regulation at the mRNA level

To assess if both protein and mRNA levels of CTSH are affected in a similar manner, cis‐expression QTL analysis was performed in the ROSMAP cohort (Figure S2 in supporting information). In 405 frontal cortical samples, mRNA levels were quantified by microarray and contrasted with imputed genotyping data. Rs2289702 exceeded genome‐wide significance (P = 2.32E‐08) while rs12592898 reached the locus‐wide threshold (P = 2.20E‐06). The rs2289702 T allele correlates with diminished levels of CTSH at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure S3 in supporting information). These results suggest that the amino‐acid change in CTSH (T → Arg) influences not only the protein itself but also its regulatory control.

Evidence for a protective role of the rs2289702 T allele

The distribution of the rs2289702 minor allele (T → Arg) was examined regarding both clinical and post mortem evaluations to determine its influence on AD severity. In the asymptomatic PREVENT‐AD cohort (Figure 2A), the global cognitive functioning measured by the total RBANS score did not reveal associations with the genetic variant (P = 0.78). These findings are not surprising, considering that no participants in this cohort show signs of cognitive deficits at baseline (see Table S1 in supporting information). Ante mortem clinical evaluations of the ROSMAP subjects (Figure 2B) indicate that the proportion of the T allele increases progressively with higher Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores (P = 0.01). Neuropathological data from the DBCBB cohort (Figures 2C and D) show that the T allele is most frequent in association with lower cortical plaque burden (P = 0.04) and reduced tangle density (P = 0.15, possible trend). By combining data from ROSMAP (ante mortem, Figure 2B) and DBCBB (post mortem, Figures 2C and D), the T allele clearly appears to confer a protective effect.

CSF CTSH protein levels correlate with t‐tau and p‐tau181 in asymptomatic at‐risk participants

Classical CSF AD biomarker alterations typically emerge years before clinical disease onset. 21 , 22 To determine whether CTSH protein levels change during this period, we turned to the asymptomatic PREVENT‐AD cohort (see Table S1 and Figure S4 in supporting information for biomarker levels). Because the rs2289702 T allele has a substantial impact on CTSH protein levels, all results are stratified accordingly. Figure 3A showed no significant link between CSF CTSH protein levels and Aβ42. On the other hand, t‐tau (Figure 3B) and p‐tau181 (Figure 3C) showed strong correlations with CTSH in the T– group (both with P < 0.0001). In the T+ group, which included fewer participants, the P values indicated only a trend‐level association. These results are consistent with the notion that CTSH is directly associated with early tau dysregulation.

CSF CTSH is associated with synaptic and neuronal injury markers in asymptomatic individuals

Because synaptic dysfunction and loss correlate tightly with early cognitive decline, 23 , 24 , 25 synapse‐derived biomarkers are particularly promising complements to amyloid and especially tau measures. 26 , 27 To evaluate whether early synaptic dysfunction is modulated by the presence of the protective variant in the CTSH gene, we once more relied on the PREVENT‐AD cohort. CSF samples were used to measure the pre‐synaptic markers contactin 5 (CNTN5), growth associated protein 43 (GAP43), synaptosome associated protein 25 (SNAP25), and synaptotagmin 1 (SYT1), the post‐synaptic marker neurogranin (NRGN) as well as the neuronal injury marker neurofilament light chain (NfL). After stratification by the T allele, all six proteins (Figures 4A–F) indicate a strong positive association (P < 0.0001) with CSF CTSH levels, especially in T– subjects. For individuals carrying the T allele, CNTN5, NfL, and SYT1 remained significant (Figures 4A,C,F, P < 0.004). These findings support an early involvement of CTSH, preceding disease onset.

Frontal cortical CTSH protein levels correlate with synaptic markers

The ROSMAP and DBCBB cohorts were studied to ascertain the correlation between CTSH protein levels and synaptic markers throughout the AD spectrum. Although both studies used frontal cortical tissue, they differed in their protein extraction methods, with ROSMAP using total homogenates and DBCBB isolating the soluble fraction. Synaptic proteins are known to accumulate in the CSF as they are released from degenerating synapses, while their abundance decreases in brain tissue because of progressive synaptic loss. The inverse correlations between cortical tissue levels of CTSH and the synaptic markers are thus consistent with this interpretation (Figure S5 in supporting information).

Throughout the AD spectrum, cortical CTSH protein levels inversely correlated with SNAP25 (Figure S5C, P = 0.0003) and SYT1 (Figure S5D, P = 0.0039) in the T– group. In the T+ group, CTSH protein levels showed a trend‐level inverse correlation with GAP43 (Figure S5A, P = 0.0589) and a significant association with SYT1 (Figure S5D, P = 0.0001). Even after stratifying by cognitive status (Table S2 in supporting information), significant negative associations were found between CTSH and SNAP25 in all T– individuals. The strongest association occurred between CTSH and SYT1 in cognitively impaired individuals carrying the T allele (P = 0.0001).

In the soluble fraction extracted from frontal cortices (DBCBB), significant positive associations were found between CTSH protein levels and: NRGN (Figure S6B in supporting information, T– group: P = 0.003), SNAP25 (Figure S6C, T– group: P = 0.0094), and at a trend level with NRGN (Figure S6B, T+ group: P = 0.0718) and SNAP25 (Figure S6C, T+ group: P = 0.0619).

Together with the CSF analyses in presymptomatic AD, these findings indicate that CTSH levels reflect emerging and spreading synaptic degeneration.

CSF CTSH protein significantly correlates with inflammatory markers in the PREVENT‐AD cohort

The inflammatory component in AD has been positioned as a double‐edged sword: while microglial activation may be initially protective against AD pathology, sustained chronic microglial activation may be detrimental in the long run, exacerbating synaptic pathologies, neuronal dysfunction, and neurodegeneration. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 To evaluate the impact of neuroinflammation on CTSH neurobiology, we examined a series of key inflammatory biomarkers detected in the CSF of the PREVENT‐AD cohort. We focused on complement C1q subcomponent subunit A (C1QA) and C3 (innate immune system); chitinase 3‐like protein 1 (CHI3L1) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; astrocytic activation); interleukin (IL)6, IL18, IL18BP, and IL18R1 (inflammatory interleukin signaling pathways); and triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2; microglial activation). Apart from IL6, all other inflammatory markers showed significant positive associations with CTSH (Figure 5). The T– group showed the strongest associations, most reaching P < 0.0001. For CHI3L1 (YKL‐40), the highest R 2 values were observed in T allele carriers (Figure 5C, P < 0.0001, R 2 = 0.45). Overall, CTSH alterations seem to align with chronic glial and complement‐mediated neuroinflammation.

DISCUSSION

To further elucidate the genetic basis of AD, we investigated CTSH, a gene recently linked to protection against AD in a large‐scale GWAS. 6 It is widely recognized that SNPs identified in GWASs often serve as proxies for nearby functional variants. 32 Across three independent cohorts, our pQTL analyses consistently identified rs2289702 as the most robust cis‐acting regulator (Figure 1), aligning with the strong cis‐effects reported for CTSH in proteome‐wide association studies. 33 Unsurprisingly, this SNP is in strong LD with the variant previously reported by Bellenguez et al.’s 6 GWAS (Figure S1). A more recent independent study confirmed that rs2289702 is linked to reduced AD risk in Han Chinese cohorts, strengthening its role as a genuine protective variant. 34

Rs2289702 resides within a transcriptionally active genomic region, and the T allele is associated with diminished transcription factor binding affinity. 34 Our data demonstrate that the T allele is associated with reduced expression at both the transcript and proteins levels in the central nervous system (Figure S3). The Gly11Arg substitution occurs in the CTSH signal peptide, a region where the addition of a positively charged arginine may affect trafficking or proteolytic processing. 35 Disruption of trafficking and degradative processes has been repeatedly linked to core pathological features of AD including impaired Aβ clearance, tau pathology, and neuroinflammation. 7 , 36

In the preclinical phase, CSF CTSH levels were significantly associated with t‐tau and p‐tau181, but showed no correlation with Aβ (Figure 3). The limited Aβ production and deposition among pre‐symptomatic individuals may account, at least in part, for this observation. Throughout the AD spectrum, T allele carriers were overrepresented in the group with reduced cortical plaque burden (Figure 2C). These results suggest that reduced CTSH activity may facilitate Aβ clearance, consistent with observations from CTSH‐deficient microglial cells. 34 Several related cathepsins, specifically B, D, L, and S, have previously been associated with Aβ metabolism. 7 If CTSH does not efficiently degrade Aβ, it could interfere with the ability of other cathepsins to normally process this substrate, acting as a competitive inhibitor or sequestration agent. Cathepsin A, for example, functions as a protective protein, forming a complex with neuraminidase 1 (NEU1) in the lysosome, acting as a chaperone to protect this glycosidase from premature degradation. 37

Along with amyloid plaque accumulation, tau pathology can lead to synapse loss and neuronal damage. 38 , 39 , 40 Our results indicate that CTSH protein levels are associated with synaptic markers including CNTN5, GAP43, NRGN, SNAP25, and SYT1 and with NfL, the premier CSF‐based biomarker for neuroaxonal damage (Figure 4 and Figure S5). The stronger association with SYT1 suggests that presynaptic vesicle dynamics may be particularly sensitive to lysosomal protease balance. CTSH may not directly regulate synaptic proteins but instead serve as a readout of lysosomal or microglial activity that accompanies synaptic degeneration or compensatory pruning.

Microglial activation in AD is tightly linked to elevated neuroinflammatory signaling. 41 C1QA, C3, CHI3L1 (YKL‐40), GFAP, IL18, IL18BP, IL18R1, and TREM2 are all glial‐derived indicators of long‐term immune activation found to be increased in the CSF of AD subjects. 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 They all correlated positively with CTSH, especially in the T– group (Figure 5). IL6, which is not a glial‐specific marker, showed no correlation with CTSH (Figure 5E). IL6 is known to fluctuate rapidly, reflecting time‐specific acute inflammatory states. 47 Our results suggest that CTSH tracks chronic glial activation and complement‐mediated immune responses typically associated with synaptic dysfunction and p‐tau production, 39 processes that evolve slowly and remain relatively stable over time.

Neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction are recognized as key drivers of early cognitive decline. 39 , 48 , 49 In pre‐symptomatic PREVENT‐AD individuals, the frequency of the T allele did not differ significantly across RBANS scores (Figure 2A). While RBANS is more sensitive due to its assessment of a wider array of cognitive domains in individuals without apparent cognitive deficits, the MMSE is better suited for diagnosing MCI and AD subjects. In the ROSMAP cohort, the proportion of protective T allele carriers is increased in subjects demonstrating better MMSE performance (Figure 2B). The T allele might thus offer a slight protective benefit, noticeable only when significant cognitive impairments appear, which are readily identified by general assessment tools like the MMSE.

Rs2289702 has been linked not only to AD but also to cancer, 50 type 1 diabetes, 51 , 52 , 53 and narcolepsy. 35 By modulating CTSH expression in a tissue‐specific manner, this polymorphism may influence lysosomal and immune pathways that are relevant not only to neuroinflammation and Aβ clearance in AD but also to autoimmunity and tumor biology, providing a plausible shared mechanism. It may provide an important biological first clue to the so‐called cancer–AD paradox that refers to the epidemiological finding that cancer survivors have a reduced risk of developing AD and, conversely, individuals with AD have a lower risk of developing cancer. Studies show a 30% to 50% reduced risk of AD in cancer patients, likely rooted in opposing cellular mechanisms (uncontrolled proliferation in cancer vs. cell death in AD). 54

To better characterize the molecular pathways involved, we performed co‐expression analysis. OLINK and TMT‐based proteomics provide complementary proteome coverage and also detect a shared subset of proteins, with their overlapping signals showing moderate quantitative concordance. 55 As a result, we identified a limited number of overlapping proteins co‐expressed with CTSH: ATRAID, CNPY4, CWC15, heparan sulfate 6‐O‐sulfotransferase 2 (HS6ST1), LRP1, SCARB2, SET and mariner transpospase domain methyltransferase metnase (SETMAR), torsin 1A interacting protein 1 (TOR1AIP1), and WWP2 (Table 1). Scavenger receptor SCARB2, which showed the most robust associations, acts as a receptor for certain viruses, a chaperone for lysosomal enzyme trafficking, and a transporter involved in oxydized lipids and lipoprotein handling and internalization. 56 Also localized to lysosomes, ATRAID has been implicated in the regulation of vesicular trafficking. 57 In the brain, LRP1 is highly expressed in both glial and neuronal cells, where it mediates ligand endocytosis, interacts with APP to facilitate Aβ clearance, binds to apoE‐containing lipoproteins such as high‐density lipoprotein, and modulates synaptic transmission. 58 Deeply involved in lipid metabolism, insulin receptor trafficking, and glucose hemostasis, LRP1 has been implicated in both type 1 diabetes 59 and AD. 60 CNPY4, 61 LRP1, 62 SCARB2, 63 SETMAR, 64 and WWP2 65 show varying degrees of involvement in cancer.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that CTSH is under genetic control by rs2289702 and that this regulation is detectable at both transcript and protein levels in CSF and brain tissue. The protective association of the rs2289702 T allele with cognition and amyloid pathology, combined with correlations between CSF CTSH and early AD biomarkers, supports a role for CTSH in AD neuropathology. Moreover, the observation that CTSH is co‐expressed with proteins involved in AD, cancer, and type 1 diabetes suggests that CTSH participates in broader biological networks that intersect with lysosomal function, immune regulation, and cellular stress pathways. Collectively, our findings identify CTSH as a promising molecular node linking genetic variation to early AD‐related processes and highlight its potential as a biomarker or therapeutic target.
