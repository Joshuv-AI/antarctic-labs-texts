Choice, struggle, and resilience: the experience of pre-loss grief among Chinese family caregivers of cancer patients
Authors: Sheng Bao, Yubing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPre-loss grief (PLG) is a critical challenge for cancer caregivers. In China, where family-centered care is profoundly shaped by cultural scripts like filial piety, understanding the digital expression of this experience is crucial for developing effective support. This study aimed to investigate the digitally expressed experience of PLG among Chinese caregivers.MethodsA computationally assisted qualitative analysis was performed on 63,775 user-generated texts from the social media platform Xiaohongshu. We used topic modeling and keyword co-occurrence network analysis to identify initial semantic patterns, which were then developed into final themes through a reflexive thematic analysis guided by a constructivist epistemology.ResultsFour core themes were identified: (1) Deep Involvement in Medical Decision-Making; (2) Caregiving Burden and Psycho-Physical Suffering; (3) Adaptive Shift in Family Communication Strategies; and (4) Narratives of Resilience and Meaning-Making. These themes reveal that the PLG experience is a dynamic process of navigating multiple tensions, including cultural responsibilities, personal suffering, relational adjustments, and seeking meaning.ConclusionsThe PLG experience of Chinese caregivers is a dynamic process defined by profound tensions. These findings underscore grief’s culturally constructed nature, highlighting the need for proactive and culturally sensitive support systems that acknowledge both the inherent burdens and the potential for growth during caregiving.

Background

Cancer has emerged as one of the principal public health challenges globally, with the number of new cases projected to reach 35 million by 2050 [1]. In China, an estimated 4.8 million new cancer cases were diagnosed in 2022, accounting for 24% of the global total and ranking first in the world [2]. Owing to advances in diagnostic and therapeutic technologies, many forms of cancer are gradually transitioning into manageable chronic conditions, thereby extending patient survival [3, 4]. Behind this positive development, however, lies an increasingly prominent social reality: the growing number of cancer patients is inevitably accompanied by the emergence of a far larger cohort of family caregivers. These caregivers, who form the core of the informal support system, not only bear immense physical and psychological burdens but also directly confront the profound sense of loss precipitated by the gradual decline of their loved ones [5–7]. This complex grieving process, which commences long before the patient’s actual death, constitutes a central component of the caregiving experience and exerts a far-reaching influence on the caregiver’s well-being and subsequent post-bereavement adjustment [8]. Therefore, a deep understanding of this pre-loss grief (PLG) phenomenon is particularly urgent and vital for the development of effective caregiver support systems against the backdrop of an ever-increasing cancer burden.

The scholarly exploration of PLG can be traced to the work of Lindemann during World War II [9]. He introduced the concept of “Anticipatory Grief” (AG) after observing that some military spouses would prematurely experience grief and complete their emotional separation to cope with the potential death of their husbands. AG was initially conceptualized as an adaptive “grief work” that helped individuals prepare for an impending loss, thereby mitigating post-loss distress.

However, Fulton and Gottesman systematically argued that simplistic conclusions about AG were untenable due to the high degree of heterogeneity in measurement tools, observation points, and sample contexts across studies [10]. This critical perspective, advanced by Rando [11], prompted a shift in the academic field from purely conceptual debates toward a more clinically valuable viewpoint. Rando not only clarified the terminology but also interpreted PLG within the contexts of the family system and multiple losses, emphasizing the importance of communication, readiness, and role renegotiation [11]. These reflections were systematically summarized in a review by Sweeting and Gilhooly [12], which offered a deep analysis of early research and highlighted its lack of operationalization and measurement heterogeneity. For example, many studies failed to provide a clear operational definition of AG and instead used scales for depression, anxiety or caregiver burden to measure grief, thereby conflating the boundaries between different psychological constructs.

This momentum gradually propelled a paradigm shift away from the binary debate over whether AG is beneficial [13] and toward the more operational framework of PLG. The PLG concept moves away from predicting a “protective function” and instead focuses on describing and understanding the grief reactions that are happening in the present. According to Nielsen et al. [14], its canonical conceptualization is defined as “a grief reaction due to multiple losses during end-of-life caregiving” (p. 2048). The term “multiple losses” here echoes Rando’s [11] early insights, referring not only to the sorrow over the impending death but also to a series of ongoing losses, such as changes in daily life and the forfeiture of a shared future caused by the illness [14, 15]. Therefore, PLG is understood as a multidimensional construct, with prior studies identifying its core experience as comprising both traumatic distress and separation distress [16]. This framework profoundly captures the complex psychological reality that begins long before the loved one’s passing. Given the precision and comprehensiveness of the PLG concept in describing this complex experience, the present study adopts it as the core theoretical framework to explore the digital discursive practices of the Chinese family caregiver community.

In addition, many empirical studies have validated the multiple factors influencing PLG. Studies consistently demonstrate that high levels of PLG are significantly associated with caregivers’ own adverse health outcomes, including greater anxiety, depression, somatic symptoms, and lower life satisfaction [5, 17, 18]. PLG also serves as a potent predictor of post-bereavement adjustment. Caregivers who experience severe PLG are more likely to develop long-term anxiety, depression and prolonged grief disorder following the relative’s death [19, 20]. Furthermore, some demographics have been identified as risk factors, with being female, having poor personal health and lower socioeconomic status all correlating with higher PLG [18, 21]. With a reported prevalence ranging from 14.9% to 33% among caregivers [18, 22, 23], the profound impact of PLG on well-being underscores the critical importance of understanding its expression within specific cultural contexts.

In contrast to Western individualistic cultures, a central entry point for understanding the family caregiving experience within the Chinese culture is “Relationship” [24]. Fei et al. argues that Chinese society is not composed of independent individuals but is constituted by a series of dense social networks [24]. An individual’s identity, obligations and behavioral norms are defined and shaped within their relationships with others, particularly within the family [25, 26]. This relationally-based social structure naturally gives rise to the cultural value of “Collectivism,” which emphasizes that the goals and harmony of the group (e.g., family) take priority over individual needs [27–29].

Confucian thought provides an ethical system for maintaining this relational harmony and collective stability. It guides individual behavior through a series of complex sociocultural scripts. For example, “xiao” (filial piety) is established as one of the most fundamental ethical principles within the family [30]. Filial piety is a multidimensional construct that includes not only the material and physical care of parents by their children but also emotional respect and obedience [31]. This role-based responsibility is reciprocal, also requiring parents to show kindness to their children and spouses to support one another [32, 33]. Consequently, family caregiving is considered an inescapable duty.

In social interactions, “renqing” (the debt of social favors) and “mianzi” (face) also play pivotal roles. Renqing can be understood as an implicit network of social debt based on long-term reciprocity [34]. Accepting help implies incurring a debt that must be remembered and repaid, especially during times of significant hardship. Meanwhile, mianzi is concerned with an individual’s and family’s public image, reputation and dignity within the community [35]. Maintaining mianzi requires presenting an image of a family that is unified, capable and conforming to social expectations [36]. Caregivers may therefore deliberately conceal internal struggles and personal suffering from relatives and friends, striving to appear strong and optimistic while relegating their own emotional needs to a secondary position [37, 38]. Existing studies suggest that such emotional suppression and communication avoidance may be linked to more severe PLG experiences [39].

Regardless of cultural background, caregivers universally experience grief exacerbated by illness uncertainty and care burdens, thereby predicting poor post-bereavement adjustment [14, 40]. However, studies in Western contexts often frame its explanations around psychological variables at the individual level, such as cognitive states like attachment styles or psychological resilience [41]. Grief is primarily viewed as a process of individual psychological adjustment. In contrast, within the Eastern culture of “relationship”, the experience of PLG may be more profoundly embedded in the pressures from the family-system and socio-ethical level. For instance, studies have confirmed that strong family functioning can effectively buffer the impact of caregiver burden on PLG [42], and that the grief experience is deeply intertwined with family roles and cultural duties [43]. These evidences suggest that in Chinese society, ethical responsibilities like filial piety may give rise to unique forms of grief experience. Although these cultural differences are critical for a comprehensive understanding of PLG, studies specifically exploring the subjective experience of PLG among Chinese family caregivers of cancer patients remain limited, leaving this population poorly understood.

While existing studies provide a solid theoretical and empirical foundation for understanding PLG, several gaps remain. First, in an effort to reveal the differing grief experiences engendered by various disease trajectories, a substantial body of studies has explored the PLG experiences of caregivers for specific illnesses such as dementia [44, 45]. However, experiential studies specifically targeting family caregivers of cancer patients remain relatively scarce [46]. Furthermore, within the current research on cancer caregivers, sampling has been heavily reliant on recruitment from offline clinical institutions, such as hospices or palliative care centers [47, 48]. Although this approach ensures the diagnostic clarity of the sample, it potentially overlooks a much larger population of “out-of-hospital” caregivers within Chinese society.

In comparison to Western countries, the professional palliative care and hospice service system in mainland China is still in its nascent stages [49]. Only 0.7% of hospitals in China offer palliative care services, with less than 1% of patients having access to them [50]. A vast number of end-of-life cancer patients, particularly those in non-first-tier cities and rural areas, are not enrolled in professional hospice institutions. Instead, they opt for home-based care, where family members assume the primary, and often sole, responsibility for their care [51]. Even in urban areas, due to a combination of complex factors including cultural norms and financial burdens, patients in the terminal stage of illness show a stronger preference for spending their final days at home [52, 53]. This reality dictates that the majority of caregiving behaviors and grief experiences actually unfold outside the hospitals and hospice facilities. The grief experiences and coping strategies of these out-of-hospital caregivers may differ from those of samples drawn from within professional support systems, yet their voices have, to a large extent, remained unheard.

Social media have thus become platforms where these caregivers spontaneously seek information, express their emotions, and build social connections [54–56]. China’s social media ecosystem is dominated by several mainstream platforms, each with distinct characteristics. For instance, Weibo focuses on fragmented information streams [57], Zhihu is centered around knowledge-based questions and answers [58], and Douyin (the Chinese version of TikTok) primarily utilizes short videos as its main content carrier [59]. After a careful comparison of these platforms, this study selected Xiaohongshu (https://www.xiaohongshu.com/en) as its data source. This platform boasts over 300 million monthly active users as of 2025 and possesses a unique community culture oriented around “life experience sharing.” Its signature notes format naturally encourages users to create long-form, deeply narrative posts that are often accompanied by images. This format allows users to structurally document their caregiving journeys and engage in writing that contains rich details. For exploring the complex experience of PLG, this long-form narrative medium is more capable of conveying an individual’s complex thoughts and emotions than short videos or brief posts, making it an ideal environment for acquiring deep qualitative data [60, 61].

Unlike data collected in specific research contexts, such as through questionnaires or in-depth interviews, user-generated content on social media is characterized by its spontaneity and non-intrusiveness, which ensures a degree of ecological validity [62]. These authentic discourses, produced in the context of everyday life, offer researchers a potential opportunity to capture dimensions of PLG that might be overlooked in traditional research methods due to observer effects or social desirability bias [63].

In light of above considerations, this study aims to analyze posts related to PLG among family caregivers of cancer patients on the Chinese social media platform Xiaohongshu. It seeks to explore the core experiences of PLG among these caregivers, thereby providing a perspective from a digital context for understanding this complex phenomenon. Through an in-depth analysis of these vast and spontaneous digital narratives, we expect to identify the real and pressing needs, dilemmas, and coping wisdom of the caregiver community—aspects that may be currently overlooked by the formal medical support system.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The timeframe for data acquisition was set from January 1, 2020, to June 30, 2025, a period chosen to capture the recent dynamics in discussions surrounding end-of-life cancer care. We constructed a keyword matrix comprising core keywords, synonyms, and related hashtags. This matrix was organized around two primary dimensions: the caregiver identity and the illness, with terms such as “family of advanced cancer patients” and “cancer-fighting diary”; the core experiences of PLG, including terms like “life’s countdown”, “hospice care”, and “the final days”.

Employing Python 3.10 for data retrieval, we systematically searched Xiaohongshu for public user posts and their corresponding comments that were relevant to our keyword matrix. No ranking or filtering based on engagement or popularity was applied during the retrieval process.

Data cleaning

Each post was subjected to a rigorous preprocessing pipeline to convert the unstructured raw text into a format suitable for computational modeling. This procedure was designed to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio by isolating the core semantic units that most accurately reflect the internal thematic variations within the discourse.

To improve the accuracy of text segmentation, we compiled a custom dictionary. This dictionary designated key domain-specific terms as indivisible lexical units for the jieba tokenization tool, such as medical terminology (e.g., “palliative care,” “targeted drug”) and frequently used caregiving phrases.

Subsequently, each document in the corpus was processed through the following sequential steps: (1) removal of all non-Chinese characters, including punctuation, numbers, Latin letters, and emojis; (2) text tokenization using the jieba tool augmented with our custom dictionary; and (3) exclusion of posts containing fewer than five valid words after cleaning. This process yielded a final corpus comprising 63,775 valid texts (including both posts and comments), which formed the basis for the subsequent computational analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol received formal ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the School of Journalism and Communication, Jinan University (Approval No. JNUSJC-2025066). The Ethics Committee waived the need for consent to participate from all participants. This waiver is based on China’s national standard, Information Security Technology—Personal Information Security Specification (GB/T 35273—2020). Specifically, Clause 5.6 of the standard explicitly exempts the requirement for obtaining authorized consent when personal information is used for academic research in the public interest, provided that the data contained in the research results is de-identified [64]. Accordingly, as this study analyzed publicly available content and applied comprehensive anonymization, individual consent was not required. To further ensure the ethical treatment of the data and protect user privacy, we implemented a comprehensive anonymization protocol. These measures included the removal of all personally identifiable information (such as usernames, IDs, and geographic locations) and the necessary paraphrasing or generalization of all quoted texts presented in our reporting to ensure that no individual user could be traced.

Data analysis: a hybrid computational-qualitative approach

Drawing upon the core principles of the Analysis of Topic Model Networks (ANTMN) framework [65, 66], this study employs a computationally assisted qualitative pathway. This choice was made to address the large-scale and unstructured nature of the data. Relying purely on manual open coding for large amounts of social media posts would not only be time-consuming but also introduce a significant risk of selective bias during sampling and coding [67]. Such biases could potentially distort the overall understanding of the corpus.

According to the ANTMN framework, our analysis proceeded in three stages: Topic Modeling, Network Construction and Analysis, and in-depth Qualitative Analysis. Within this analytical pathway, computational methods served an auxiliary function by systematically processing the large-scale text and providing data-driven structural prompts. This approach furnished a more comprehensive and less biased starting point for the subsequent qualitative analysis. However, the ultimate identification of themes and construction of meaning rely on the researchers’ deep qualitative interpretation and theoretical integration. Computational methods enhanced the efficiency of processing large-scale data, while qualitative analysis provided contextual validation, refinement and depth to the computational outputs, thereby achieving methodological complementarity. This framework has demonstrated considerable applicability and analytical potential within the medical and health field, particularly for analyzing large-scale social media discourse [68, 69].

Step 1: topic modeling with latent dirichlet allocation (LDA)

We employed Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), a topic modeling approach, to identify the underlying semantic topics within the corpus. LDA is an unsupervised generative probabilistic model which operates on the assumption that each document is a mixture of various topics, and each topic, in turn, is a mixture of multiple words [70]. This method facilitates a “bottom-up” discovery of data-driven thematic structures from the co-occurrence patterns of words in the text, which may otherwise be overlooked by human researchers.

To empirically determine the optimal number of topics (K value), we computed the Topic Coherence (C_v) and Perplexity scores for models with K values ranging from 2 to 40, at an increment of 3. Topic Coherence is a quantitative metric, widely demonstrated to correlate highly with human judgment, which measures the semantic similarity among the high-frequency words within a single topic; a higher coherence score indicates a better-defined topic [71]. Perplexity, on the other hand, evaluates the model’s predictive performance on unseen data, with lower scores indicating a better-fitting model. However, the selection of the optimal number of topics was not based solely on these quantitative metrics. Two researchers also independently reviewed several candidate models by examining their keyword lists and most representative posts, particularly those with high coherence scores (e.g., K = 3, 4, and 5). Through a rigorous comparative discussion, we assessed each model’s capacity to generate topics that were not only semantically distinct but also theoretically coherent and meaningful within the context of PLG. Ultimately, we selected K = 4 as the optimal number of topics for this study (see Fig. 1), as this model achieved the best equilibrium between quantitative performance and qualitative interpretability.

Step 2: keyword co-occurrence network construction

To further explore the semantic structure within the topics and identify central discursive nodes, we constructed and analyzed a Keyword Co-occurrence Network. We extracted the top 80 core keywords from the final K = 4 model to build a weighted and undirected network. In this network, nodes represent the core keywords, and edges represent the co-occurrence relationships. To emphasize the core structure of the network, we filtered out weak connections with a weight of less than three.

This network was developed primarily for exploratory purposes. It provided a preliminary visualization of semantic structures, allowing us to identify keyword clusters that formed naturally based on co-occurrence frequencies. This data-driven visualization served as an important reference for the subsequent in-depth qualitative thematic analysis of the computational topics in Step 3.

Step 3: qualitative analysis

The final stage of our analysis involved a rigorous qualitative thematic analysis, informed by Braun and Clarke’s framework [72, 73] for reflexive thematic analysis and grounded in a constructivist epistemological stance. From this perspective, we believe that themes are not pre-existing “facts” passively discovered within the data, but are analytical patterns generated by the researchers. These patterns emerge from a sustained dialogue between the material, relevant theories and researchers’ own reflexivity within a specific cultural context. Considering the social media origin of our data, our analytical focus was to understand how users present, construct, and reflect upon their personal experiences and emotions through their public digital texts. To ensure the rigor of this qualitative process and the quality of our findings, we drew upon the criteria for trustworthiness proposed by Lincoln [74]. The process followed the six phases of thematic analysis and integrated the outputs of the computational analysis as a reference of the following qualitative analysis:

(1) Data familiarization: To establish the credibility of our study, researchers engaged in repeated and immersive reading of the original posts. Throughout this process, we maintained reflexive journals to document our initial impressions, emotional responses and theoretical thoughts. This practice allowed us to acknowledge and critically examine how our own stance might influence the interpretive process.

(2) Generating initial codes: Using the four computationally derived topics from the LDA as a guide, we conducted open coding on a representative sample of high-probability posts within each topic. Our coding process was both theory-driven and data-driven. Based on our literature review, we maintained theoretical sensitivity to expressions potentially related to Chinese cultural scripts such as xiao, renqing and mianzi. At the same time, we did not use these concepts as a pre-determined template but instead inductively captured their specific expressions in the caregivers’ own language. Two researchers first independently coded a subset of the same data, followed by a discussion to ensure the consistency of the coding framework.

(3)–(5) Constructing and defining themes: In this core phase, the researchers condensed the data-driven codes into four more interpretive analytical themes. This process was actively informed by the visual patterns observed in the network map from Step 2. For instance, when the network map revealed a strong connection between two conceptual clusters, it prompted us to pay closer attention to their underlying relationship during qualitative reading, which in some cases led to their integration into a broader analytical theme. The researchers engaged in regular peer debriefing to challenge and refine each other’s interpretations, ensuring that the final thematic map was not only grounded in the data but also possessed internal coherence and logical consistency.

(6) Final visualization and reporting: After the four final themes were confirmed, we produced the final visualization of the network graph from Step 2 using Gephi (0.10.1) and colored the nodes corresponding to their final theme (see Fig. 2). In the Results, we present rich and vivid quotations to illustrate the meaning of each theme. The selection of these quotations was guided by two criteria: typicality and richness. Typicality refers to a quotation’s ability to clearly represent a common viewpoint or experience within the theme, while richness refers to its capacity to vividly illustrate the complexity and emotional depth of the theme [72, 75].

Theme 1: deep involvement in medical decision-making

The representative keywords of the first theme, including terms such as “treatment,” “chemotherapy,” “diagnosis,” and “choice,” clearly point to medical-centric caregiving practices. The keyword co-occurrence network further reveals that these medical terms cluster tightly around the central nodes of “cancer” and “choice,” highlighting the pivotal role of decision-making in the caregiving process. Our qualitative analysis of this theme demonstrates that user narratives are not objective medical records. Instead, they are laden with grief experience of making difficult medical choices under huge cultural pressure. User narratives show that caregivers are not merely passive recipients of information but are also active participants in treatment planning and the ultimate bearers of decisional responsibility. This role is often accompanied by significant psychological pressure, particularly when cultural expectations conflict with the patient’s own wishes. One user described this dilemma:The doctor presented two choices: aggressive chemotherapy with a very low quality of life, or returning home to rest… The family let me make the final decision. On one side was the traditional concept that “not giving up” is the only way to fulfill filial piety (xiao); on the other were my father’s true pain and wishes. When I signed the consent form, it felt harder than undergoing surgery myself. (T1-002)

This narrative reflects the profound moral distress of the caregiver. The medical consideration of which treatment is best for the patient becomes deeply entangled with the cultural concept of what it means to be a filial child, making the decision exceptionally difficult. This struggle between fulfilling the cultural expectations of xiao and respecting patient autonomy is a recurrent theme in end-of-life decision-making research in East Asian societies [76]. It suggests that for Chinese caregivers, the PLG experience is deeply interwoven with an anticipatory guilt over potentially failing to meet cultural role expectations.

Notably, a similar decision-making recurred throughout our corpus, where the final medical decisions lay with the adult children rather than the patient. Therefore, caregivers must bear not only their grief but also the immense pressure of acting as surrogate decision-makers.

Beyond these internal family ethics, the decision-making is also constrained by external social relationships, particularly the influence of the cultural script of “mianzi” (face). The discursive practices of caregivers often reflect a form of emotional labor performed to maintain the family’s public image. As one user shared:Every time my mother comes back from chemotherapy, she is completely drained. All our relatives and friends keep asking about her condition, and we have to put on a brave face and say, “She’s doing okay.” We don’t want others to think our family is falling apart. This “face” issue is just so exhausting. (T1-008)

Other caregivers also described the similar situation:My dad is a very strong-willed person who cares a lot about his face. He always felt that palliative care was the same as giving up on treatment. I have to consider his feelings while also fearing judgment from others for not being a sufficiently filial child. (T1-009)

Together, these narratives illustrate how the mianzi directly complicates medical decision-making. Concurrently, xiao constitutes a more internal form of ethical pressure that not only influences illness communication but also drives caregivers to expend extensive resources. Another caregiver’s narrative illustrates this point:In the two years since my dad got sick, our family’s savings have been depleted, and we are also in a lot debt. Many people have told us that with his condition, continuing treatment would just drain our money for an inevitable outcome. But I can’t do it. As long as there’s a glimmer of hope, we have to treat him even if it means selling everything we own. Otherwise, I would be conscience-stricken for the rest of my life, and others would point their fingers at me for being unfilial. (T1-014)

This narrative reveals the operational logic of xiao as a dual-binding force. It functions as an external social norm driven by the fear of public condemnation for being unfilial, which reflects a general concern for social reputation. On the other hand, it also operates through an internalized moral sentiment, captured in the conviction that “I would be conscience-stricken for the rest of my life.”

This anticipatory guilt rooted in the failure to perfectly fulfill one’s role obligations as dictated by xiao profoundly shapes the PLG experience for Chinese caregivers. It can also intertwine with practical hardships such as financial toxicity, further complicating their grief [77].

Theme 2: caregiving burden and psycho-physical suffering

The representative keywords of the second theme, including “mother,” “parents,” “suffering,” “pain,” “caregiving,” and “support,” clearly point to a psycho-physical experience centered on the act of care. The keyword co-occurrence network shows a dense cluster of these terms with the care recipients (“mother/parents”) and negative experiences (“suffering/pain”) as its core nodes, which visually highlights how the caregiver’s personal experience is highly bound to the patient’s state of suffering. The qualitative analysis of this theme further reveals that user narratives generally describe a dual exhaustion including both the physical fatigue from demanding caregiving tasks and the mental exhaustion from seeing a loved one’s suffering while anticipating their loss.

User narratives are filled with descriptions of how their own lives have been severely compressed, with statements such as “it feels like my own life has come to a complete standstill” (T2-025) and “for two years, it’s been only home and the hospital, I have no life of my own” (T2-029). This tangible aspect of caregiver burden is consistent with many studies confirming it as a strong predictor of PLG [78, 79]. This burden is compounded by a profound sense of helplessness from seeing a loved one’s suffering. One caregiver described this in detail:My mother’s pain is getting worse and worse, and she suffers all through the night. I’m with her twenty-four hours in the ward. The hardest part isn’t the exhaustion, but watching her weaken day by day, knowing full well what it means, and being completely powerless. (T2-001)

This narrative reveals one of the core experiences of PLG, where suffering stems not only from the present burden but also from the convergence of current hardship with the foreknowledge of future loss [40]. The sentiment expressed by some caregivers that they are “powerlessly watching a life pass away” is a direct manifestation of this internal rehearsal process.

A deeper cultural layer is evident in the dilemma caregivers face when seeking social support, a struggle under the influence of the renqing cultural script. Many caregivers expressed a similar sense of isolation, with statements like “you can’t talk to outsiders about this kind of suffering” (T2-033) and “I want to talk to someone, but I’m afraid of bringing them negative energy” (T2-056). The experience of one caregiver is highly representative:As the only daughter, all the pressure is on me… My heart feels so heavy and I desperately need verbal support, but I feel embarrassed to keep bothering my friends and colleagues… I feel I am in an unrepayable “debt of renqing”. So, most of the time, I just have to carry it all by myself. (T2-011)

This text clearly demonstrates how renqing becomes a cultural barrier to help-seeking. The caregivers’ discursive practices show that the fear of “owing renqing” leads them to proactively withdraw and self-isolate at the moment they most need support. This “delayed help-seeking” or “self-sacrifice” has been observed in other studies [80] and may deprive caregivers of external buffering resources, thereby exacerbating their grief experience [42].

Theme 3: adaptive shift in family communication strategies

The representative keywords of the third theme, including “family,” “loved one,” “communication,” and “death,” point toward relational dynamics within the family. The network visually suggests the role of communication in navigating end-of-life issues, as the term “communication” serves as a critical bridge node connecting “family” with emotional terms like “death,” “fear,” and “regret.” The qualitative analysis further reveals that family communication strategies evolve from avoidance to limited communication within the digital narratives.

In the initial phase of many narratives, caregivers depict a common communicative dilemma of “conspiracy of silence,” often described as a family strategy to protect the patient’s emotional well-being. As a caregiver wrote:Ever since the diagnosis, no one in our family dares to mention the word “death”. It’s like there’s an unspoken agreement to protect the patient’s final emotional state. We force ourselves to smile and act cheerful in front of my father every day, and we rarely communicate our own genuine fears. (T3-004)

We force ourselves to smile and act cheerful in front of my father every day, and we rarely communicate our own genuine fears. (T3-004)

Other caregivers expressed this experience with statements like “there was just this unspoken rule that no one would be the one to say that” (T3-029). Another shared an attempt to start a conversation that was immediately shut down: “Once, I couldn’t hold it in anymore and tried to talk to my mom about my dad’s final arrangements. She cut me off as soon as I started, telling me not to say such inauspicious things” (T3-043). These texts depict a family atmosphere where talk of death and illness is perceived as taboo. This taboo builds an invisible wall between family members, preventing the authentic sharing and processing of fear and grief. This phenomenon is highly consistent with existing studies, which find that communication avoidance of death and keeping silent in Chinese contexts often inhibit information exchange and emotional expression within families, leading to alienation [81, 82].

However, many narratives proceed to describe a significant shift. This shift from avoidance to limited communication observed in our data is universal and also aligns with the dynamic changes in end-of-life communication identified by Xu et al. in their study of Chinese families [83]. Within these texts, the focus of family communication appears to shift from “avoiding death” to limited communication aimed at “seeking a good death.” Caregivers often attribute this change to a fear of future regret. One caregiver described their family’s decision-making process as such:The thought that I might soon lose my mother fills my heart with countless regrets. Our family discussed it, and our greatest wish is for her to pass away peacefully and without pain. So, in the end, we spoke honestly with my mother, asking her if she had any unfulfilled wishes to strive for a “good death” free of regret. (T3-009)

Similarly, another caregiver wrote, “After much thought, we decided not to hide it from him anymore. We needed to have an honest talk to help him fulfill his final wishes. This is what we should do as his children” (T3-027). These narratives document a perceived adjustment in communication strategies. The rationale for initiating these dialogues centers on the goals of leaving no regrets and achieving a good death. In the Chinese cultural context, a “good death” is a multidimensional aspiration that includes not only a peaceful and dignified passing for the individual but also family harmony and the fulfillment of filial duties by the children [84], which motivates families to initiate end-of-life conversations. The end-of-life conversation is thus reframed from a risk that could disrupt family harmony into a caring and responsible act, reflecting a flexible application of filial piety in the end-of-life context [85]. Faced with impending loss, the communication described by caregivers evolves from a passive avoidance aimed at preserving harmony to a proactive engagement aimed at achieving a good death. This movement from silence to communication can be interpreted as a key coping strategy for the family in navigating PLG. By starting these difficult conversations, families are able to process their fear of regret and work collectively toward a meaningful and peaceful end-of-life process.

Theme 4: narratives of resilience and meaning-making

The final theme illustrates a more positive facet of the grief experience, built around keywords like “meaning,” “memory,” “cherish,” “resilience,” and “continue.” The network also shows a clear topological distance between the cluster of constructive and future-oriented terms (e.g., “resilience,” “meaning”) and those associated with negative affect (e.g., “difficult,” “regret”), which visually suggests a narrative shift from suffering toward positive reflection. The qualitative analysis further reveals two primary meaning-making practices within the caregiver narratives: looking back to maintain connection and looking forward to affirm personal growth.

Many caregivers give transcendent meaning to their loved one’s life by recalling past memories. This discursive practice echoes the meaning reconstruction theory, which posits that individuals strive to integrate the experience of loss into a new life framework to find a sense of continuity amidst suffering [86]. For example, one caregiver shared reflections when looking through the family album, “Looking at our happy family in the old photos brings me so much warmth. These memories are the strength that keeps me going” (T4-012). Another wrote, “Watching him so weak in his bed, my mind is filled with images of him taking me out to play as a child. It feels like those moments make up his entire life in my memory” (T4-014). In addition, one caregiver’s post powerfully presented this process:I’ve recently been secretly making final preparations for my father, and I’ve been looking through many old photos. Those memories seem incredibly precious now. I am starting to understand my father in a new way, reflecting on what his life has brought to us. I have found that the meaning of life is not in its length, but in the moments of being loved and remembered. (T4-003)

I am starting to understand my father in a new way, reflecting on what his life has brought to us. I have found that the meaning of life is not in its length, but in the moments of being loved and remembered. (T4-003)

Some caregivers even construct a future-oriented and resilient self-image. After detailing the prolonged suffering of caregiving, certain texts exhibit a significant narrative change from describing exhaustion to affirming growth. Many caregivers expressed similar sentiments, such as “this experience made me grow up overnight” (T4-023) or “although it hurts, it also taught me the meaning of life” (T4-029). One post exemplifies this complex psychological integration:This period of time accompanying my mom on her final journey has been the darkest yet most precious experience of my life. It was she who taught me how to cherish the present and how to bravely face farewells. Although the process was incredibly difficult, I feel that I have also become more resilient. I know that I will probably still break down and cry on the day she passes, but I believe I will carry my mother’s love and continue on with my life. (T4-008)

This post does not deny the pain of the experience. Instead, it completes a process of positive self-reconstruction within the text by candidly acknowledging the hardship. The narrative transforms a traumatic event into a catalyst for personal growth by reinterpreting the caregiving experience as precious. This discursive practice of narrating an adverse experience as a story of personal growth is highly consistent with the concept of posttraumatic growth [87, 88]. However, these narratives of growth do not signal an end to grief. Caregivers often simultaneously acknowledge future pain, with statements like, “I will probably still break down and cry on the day she passes” (T4-008). This finding aligns with a meta-analytic study, which has shown that posttraumatic growth and posttraumatic distress can coexist [89]. By constructing a self-image of resilience and growth, caregivers empower themselves to face the future while still acknowledging their pain. Therefore, the function of the online platform transcends mere emotional catharsis, becoming a meaning-making space where users actively affirm their inner strength and find direction for their future lives [90, 91].

In summary, the narratives within this theme offer a more complete perspective on the complexity of PLG. These texts reveal that within the discursive practices of PLG, the candid expression of suffering can exist concurrently with the active construction of resilience and life’s meaning.

Discussion

Through an analysis of discourse from Chinese family caregivers of cancer patients on the social media platform Xiaohongshu, this study has identified four core themes that constitute their experience of PLG. These findings not only provide empirical evidence for the specific manifestations of PLG within a contemporary Chinese digital context but also reveal how cultural background, family communication and medical realities collectively shape the grieving process.

A central finding of this study is that the PLG experience of Chinese caregivers is profoundly influenced by cultural scripts. This relationally-centered experience contrasts with PLG research in Western contexts, which tends to focus more on individual psychology. For instance, some studies explain variations in grief through individual psychological traits such as attachment styles and mentalization [41] or through individual-level variables like anxiety, depression and resilience [15]. However, our results demonstrate that caregivers’ grief is not a purely internal emotion but is instead closely shaped and constrained by relational norms like xiao (filial piety), mianzi (face), and renqing (the debt of social favors).

A deep cross-cultural comparison clarifies both the similarities and differences of the Chinese cultural context. On one hand, the family-centered decision-making identified in our study is prevalent in other collectivistic cultures. For example, in Latino cultures, the concept of “familism” similarly emphasizes that the collective needs of the family take priority over individual autonomy, with medical decisions often undertaken by the whole family [92, 93]. This suggests that situating the grief experience within the family system rather than at a purely individual level is crucial for understanding PLG in all collectivistic contexts. On the other hand, our study reveals certain shared features within the Confucian-influenced East Asian cultural sphere. We found that the tendency for adult children to bear the burden of decision-making for their parents is driven by filial piety, which has also been reported in cancer care contexts in South Korea [94]. Furthermore, the practice of concealing the illness or forming a “conspiracy of silence” to maintain harmony is a common mode of end-of-life communication across East Asia [95, 96]. The underlying logic of this behavior shares common ground with the desire for a good death that does not become a burden to the family [97].

However, the finding that renqing and mianzi strongly inhibit help-seeking behavior may point to a unique aspect of Chinese culture. While other cultures also emphasize family responsibility [80], the cultural scripts of viewing seeking help as a “debt” and exposing family struggles as a “loss of face” [98, 99] profoundly shape a unique social support dilemma for Chinese caregivers. This was evident in our analysis of both Theme 1 and Theme 2. As shown in Theme 1, caregivers tend to conceal their true difficulties when questioned by relatives and friends to maintain the family’s mianzi. And the fear of owing renqing often prevents them from seeking help from these same networks [34]. This culturally-shaped reluctance to seek external support likely exacerbates the caregiver’s sense of isolation [100].

Our data further indicate that the PLG experience is a dynamic social process that evolves within the family system. This finding provides qualitative evidence from digital narratives that supports the non-linear nature of grief revealed in recent quantitative studies, which has broadly challenged linear stage models in favor of diverse and dynamic grief trajectories [101, 102]. The adaptive shift from a “conspiracy of silence” to achieve a good death in communication strategies observed in Theme 3 illustrates this dynamic. In the initial post-diagnosis phase, families may opt for silence and concealment to maintain harmony and protect the patient’s emotions [103, 104], suggesting that the grief experience is suppressed at this stage. However, as the illness progresses, we found that communication strategies shift toward a limited openness. The grief expression is thus reframed as a collective family issue that requires active engagement (e.g., fulfilling the patient’s final wishes) to give it meaning [105, 106].

This dynamic is not only present at the family level but also within the individual’s internal experience, where we found that suffering and growth can coexist. This is clearly demonstrated by integrating the findings from Theme 2 and Theme 4. While we observed profound caregiving burden and exhaustion in Theme 2, the narratives of Theme 4 show caregivers actively extracting value from memories and affirming personal growth amidst their pain. The coexistence of suffering and growth has been evidenced in studies of cancer and dementia caregivers across different cultural contexts [107–109]. This further supports the view of grief as a dynamic process of oscillation, where individuals move between a loss-orientation and a restoration-orientation [110].

It is noteworthy that the data for this study were primarily sourced from a social media platform with a predominantly young user base, offering a unique lens on contemporary practices of filial piety. Existing research generally suggests that the concept of xiao is undergoing a significant transformation among younger generations in China, shifting from an “authoritarian filial piety” that emphasizes absolute obedience to a “reciprocal filial piety” that values emotional connection and mutual respect [111, 112]. However, we found that even within the extreme stress of cancer caregiving, these caregivers still consider xiao as a primary behavioral motive and emotional norm to explain and justify their sacrifices. In contrast to traditional private family expressions, many young caregivers choose to document and express their experiences publicly but anonymously on digital platforms [113]. This practice appears to be a strategy for seeking emotional support and navigating real-world interpersonal conflicts [67, 114]. Therefore, the digital platform serves a dual function: it perpetuates the cultural core of xiao while simultaneously providing a relatively safe narrative space where the pain, guilt and conflicts that are difficult to discuss within the family can be presented and discussed [115].

Taken together, both the narrative evolution of family communication and the individual-level coexistence of suffering and growth reveal the PLG experience to be a non-linear and dynamic. As the framework of regulatory flexibility proposed by Bonanno and Burton [116] emphasizes, adapting to grief and crisis is not a linear process with a single strategy. In our study, we found that families and individuals adjust their strategies and find the potential for growth within their suffering. Therefore, PLG is a complex and ambivalent life experience fraught with struggle and loss, yet it also holds the profound potential for adaptation and transformation.

In terms of practical implications, the findings above offer several concrete recommendations for enhancing support services for Chinese cancer caregivers. First, the role of healthcare professionals should extend beyond providing medical information to also include facilitating family dialogue and communication. The objective should be to help caregivers navigate the conflict between the cultural notion that “never giving up fulfills filial piety” and the patients’ actual wills, thereby potentially alleviating the caregivers’ moral burden. Second, social support systems need to be more proactive. Given that cultural factors like “renqing” can create a help-seeking dilemma, services should not passively wait for caregivers to request assistance. Instead, they should implement regular online support groups or standardized emotional screenings to help identify at-risk individuals at an earlier stage [117]. Third, medical and public health organizations should strategically leverage social media platforms such as Xiaohongshu. These platforms can be utilized to disseminate high-quality information on palliative care and grief [118]. More importantly, they can serve as a channel to guide users from informal peer support networks toward professional online or offline health services [119].

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the nature of social media data prevented us from obtaining complete user demographic and clinical information, which hinders a more detailed analysis of subgroup differences. Second, our data were sourced from a single social media platform, Xiaohongshu. Although the platform provides rich, authentic user-generated content, the findings may be influenced by its specific community culture and user interface. Third, this study focuses primarily on the text narratives. This approach largely excluded the multimodal contents (e.g., images and emojis) of the posts as well as the comment interactions, which may have resulted in an incomplete understanding of the user experience. These limitations also point to directions for future research. Future research could employ a mixed-methods design, combining large-scale computational analysis with in-depth interviews to cross-validate the identified themes and provide richer text data. Furthermore, comparative studies across different social media platforms are needed. Finally, building on the unmet needs highlighted by our findings, future research should focus on designing and evaluating culturally sensitive digital interventions aimed at supporting the cancer caregiver community.

Conclusion

By analyzing user-generated content from Chinese cancer caregivers on the social media platform Xiaohongshu, this study investigated the PLG experience. Our analysis reveals the PLG experience to be a complex, dynamic process of navigating multiple tensions, structured around four core themes: Deep Involvement in Medical Decision-Making; Caregiving Burden and Psycho-Physical Suffering; Adaptive Shift in Family Communication Strategies; and Narratives of Resilience and Meaning-Making. The PLG experience of caregivers is profoundly shaped by the interplay between medical realities and local cultural factors such as xiao (filial piety), mianzi (face), and renqing (the debt of social favors), which in turn mediate how they navigate medical decisions, express emotions, and seek support. While this experience is characterized by significant distress, it also holds the potential for self-growth. These findings highlight the culturally constructed nature of grief and underscore the necessity of developing proactive and culturally sensitive support systems.
