Genomic and transcriptomic differences between neuroendocrine neoplasms and carcinomas with human papillomavirus (HPV)
Authors: Morgann Madill, Udhayvir Grewal, Harris Krause, Andrew Elliott, Marya Wahidi, Janet S Rader, Deanna Teoh, Soo J Park, John Wallbillich, David J Benjamin, Arash Rezazadeh Kalebasty, Penelope Duerksen-Hughes, Robert Morris, Radhika Gogoi, Fadi Zaiem, Thomas J Herzog, Premal H Thaker, Dave Bryant, Matthew Oberley, Heloisa Soares, Joanne Xiu, Andrew Hinton, Deepti Jain, Britt K Erickson, Martina Bazzaro, Emil Lou, Rouba Ali Fehmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHuman papillomavirus (HPV) is a well-established oncogenic virus implicated in the development of several epithelial cancers, most notably cervical, anogenital, and oropharyngeal carcinomas. In contrast, neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs)—a heterogeneous group of malignancies arising from neuroendocrine cells across various organ systems—have not traditionally been linked to HPV infection. In this study, we performed extensive genomic and transcriptomic profiling to compare HPV-positive NENs to HPV-positive non-NENs across anatomical sites, aiming to uncover biologically and clinically actionable differences.MethodsHPV16- and HPV18-positive tumors were identified from 101,343 solid tumors profiled at Caris Life Sciences (Phoenix, AZ) with DNA and RNA sequencing. Prevalence of pathogenic mutations and copy number amplifications were calculated. Fisher’s exact/χ2 tests were applied appropriately with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05).ResultsHPV positivity was most frequent in cervical carcinomas (70%, 1200/1716). Importantly, 6% (96/1620) of NENs from all tissues were positive for HPV16 or HPV18. Among HPV-positive NENs, 93% were high-grade compared to 54% observed in HPV-negative NENs (p < 0.001), highlighting a strong association between HPV and tumor aggressiveness in this subset. Analysis of HPV-associated sites (cervix, anorectal region, and head and neck) revealed that HPV-positive NENs possess distinct genomic and transcriptomic landscapes compared to HPV-positive non-NENs. Notably, interferon signaling was significantly suppressed in HPV-positive NENs, suggesting a unique tumor-immune microenvironment.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that HPV-positive NENs form a distinct subset with unique genomic features, including reduced interferon signaling, compared to HPV-positive non-NENs. Thus, future studies focused on evaluating HPV status, along with genomic and transcriptomic characteristics, may uncover biologically and clinically actionable distinctions for this rare yet clinically significant tumor subgroup.Clinical trial numberNot applicableSupplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13027-026-00761-8.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most common sexually transmitted pathogens and a common cause of multiple forms of cancer [1]. Over 85% of sexually active people will acquire the virus at some point in their lifetime [2]. Low-risk genotypes of HPV are most associated with formation of cutaneous and anogenital warts [3]. High-risk genotypes, such as HPV16 and HPV18, are common causes of squamous cell carcinomas of the cervix, head and neck, anus, and rectum, among others. High-risk HPV is associated with 90% of anogenital cancers and 85% of invasive cervical cancers [3]. Despite being potentially transient and eliminated by the immune system, HPV remains a widespread form of infection and a preventable cause of malignancy. With the increased uptake of the HPV vaccine in developed regions, HPV-associated cancers and their precursors are decreasing [2], with the anticipated prevention of 70–80% of all cervical cancers [4].

Given the oncogenic potential of HPV, it is important to comprehend the interplay between HPV-associated malignancies and broader tumor classification systems, including the spectrum of neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs). Regardless of endocrine or non-endocrine origin, NENs are classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO), which considers morphological differentiation, mitotic count, and Ki-67 proliferation index. Well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) have been categorized based on proliferative activity (grades 1–3). NET grade 1 exhibits < 2 mitoses per 2 mm2 and a Ki-67 index < 3%, NET grade 2 shows 2–20 mitoses per 2 mm2 or Ki-67 3–20%, and NET grade 3 demonstrates > 20 mitoses per 2 mm2 or Ki-67 > 20%. However, poorly differentiated NENs have been classified as neuroendocrine carcinomas with mitotic activity of > 20 per 2 mm2 and typically very high Ki-67 indices [5]. The gastrointestinal tract and lungs account for more than 75% of NEN sites of origin [6]. Among less common sites of origin, genitourinary and gynecological NENs comprise 12% of neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) and 4% of NETs, while head and neck NENs account for 2% of NECs and 0.6% of NETs [7, 8]. Notably, HPV has been associated with NENs at specific anatomical locations, and its presence may provide prognostic insight as well as potential therapeutic relevance. In cervical NENs, management strategies and clinical algorithms have been updated to reflect current understanding of the role of HPV status in guiding therapeutic decisions [9].

Despite advances in classifying NENs broadly, site-specific data for cervical, head and neck, and anorectal neoplasms are limited and the molecular features of HPV-positive NENs remain poorly defined. A prior analysis of 49 NENs originating from the cervix reported a high rate of HPV detection among all subtypes (>85%) [10]. Similarly, prior small case series of HPV-NEC originating from the head and neck have noted significant heterogeneity in pathology and a particularly aggressive clinical course [1, 11]. In contrast, HPV-positive head and neck carcinomas tend to have relatively favorable clinical outcomes [12], which alludes to underlying clinically significant differences in tumor biology. Hence, it is important to analyze the relationship between HPV positivity and the molecular characteristics of NENs and their carcinoma counterparts. Gaining a better understanding of molecular features associated with HPV status may uncover biologically and clinically actionable distinctions. In the current study we describe the genomic and transcriptomic landscapes of HPV-positive NENs and investigate key actionable differences between HPV-positive NENs and HPV-positive non-NENs.

Materials and methods

Study design

A total of 101,343 consecutively profiled solid tumors, submitted to Caris Life Sciences (Phoenix, AZ) for comprehensive tumor profiling between 2015 and 2023, were retrospectively investigated. This study was conducted in accordance with guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, Belmont report, and U.S. Common rule. In keeping with 45 CFR 46.104(d)(4), this study was performed utilizing retrospective, deidentified clinical data and is considered IRB exempt and no patient consent was necessary from the subject.

Comprehensive molecular profiling

Prior to testing, tumor-rich regions were identified by board-certified pathologists and micro-dissected to enrich neoplastic cellularity. Genomic DNA and RNA were isolated from FFPE tumor samples. Samples with at least 50 ng were used for next generation sequencing (NGS). Depending on the time of biopsy, DNA sequencing was performed on a targeted gene panel or with whole-exome sequencing (WES). For targeted sequencing, a custom-designed SureSelect XT assay was used to enrich 592 clinically relevant whole-gene targets (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and the resultant library was sequenced on the NextSeq platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA). WES involved target capture of >20,000 genes at lower coverage (average sequencing depth of 230×) and enrichment of 700 clinically relevant genes at higher coverage (average sequencing depth of >1000×). The resultant WES library was sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA).

All variants were detected with >99% confidence based on allele frequency and amplicon coverage, and an analytic sensitivity of 5%. Variants determined as “pathogenic” or “likely pathogenic” according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) standard by molecular geneticists were counted as mutations, while ‘variants of unknown significance’, ‘likely benign’ or ‘benign’ variants were not included for analysis. The copy number alteration (CNA) of each exon was determined by calculating the average depth of the sample along with the sequencing depth of each exon and comparing the calculated result to a pre-calibrated value. HPV status was determined using WES data by aligning sequencing reads to viral genomes enriched with 39 unique baits specific to HPV16 and 50 unique baits for HPV18, enabling complete coverage of the respective genomics. Viral positivity was defined as the presence of ≥ 100 uniquely mapped reads.

RNA sequencing libraries were prepared with an Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V7 bait panel (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), and resultant libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq platform. Raw data was aligned to human reference genome hg19 using RNA-seq read mapper, STAR [13]. For quantification of transcript expression, Salmon software was used to generate transcripts per million (TPM) values [14]. Ki-67 status was evaluated from 3,004 cases with available transcriptomic data. TPM values for expression of MKI67 mRNA were used to infer high grade vs low grade NENs using the 45th percentile as a threshold, which was empirically derived based on a subset of samples with grade annotation available. Additionally, mRNA expression data were subjected to pathway enrichment analysis via gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [15], for which the significance was determined by an FDR q value < 0.2. Tumor immune signaling was evaluated using an 18-gene T cell–inflamed gene expression signature as previously described [16].

Statistical analyses

Comparative analyses were performed using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method; adjusted p < 0.05 was regarded as significant.

Results

Distribution of HPV-positivity in NENs

Among various solid tumor types, rates of HPV positivity were highest in cervical carcinomas (70%, 1200/1716), followed by vulvar squamous cell carcinoma (31%, 126/410) and head and neck carcinomas (28%, 668/2413). NENs from multiple tumor sites also exhibited HPV positivity, particularly with a higher prevalence of HPV16 subtype (Fig. 1). Among 1,620 total NENs, 96 (6%) were positive for HPV (Fig. 2). The highest rates of HPV positivity among NENs were observed in cervical (76%, 55/58), anorectal (AR, 27%, 29/106), female genitourinary tract (FGTM, 23%, 12/52) and head and neck (HN, 19%, 7/36) sites of origin (Fig. 2). Notably, female genitourinary tract malignancies included unspecified gynecologic malignancies, including vulvovaginal and melanomas.

Of the 97 HPV-positive NENs identified, 62 were further categorized as high or low grade based on available Ki-67 RNA (MKI67) expression data. Notably, about 94% of HPV-positive NENs were high-grade compared to 55% of HPV-negative NENs (Fig. 3, p < 0.001). No significant differences in tumor grade between HPV status for each site were noted. Among head and neck, colon, and unknown primary sites, HPV-positive tumors were all high grade, compared to less than 60% high grade HPV-negative tumors at the same sites (Fig. 3).

Leveraging data from DNA sequencing, we noted significant differences in the genomic landscape between non-NEN versus NEN tumors. (Fig. 4). Among HPV-positive tumors from anorectal origin, NEN were enriched with ERBB2 mutations, RAF1 amplifications, and PPARG amplifications compared to non-NENs (p < 0.05). Similarly, among HPV-positive tumors from head and neck origin, NENs exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of CTNNB1 mutations, BARD1 mutations, and FGRF2 amplifications compared to non-NENs (Fig. 4). Similarly, HPV-positive cervical NENs had a significantly higher proportion of AIP mutations and amplifications of SMAD4, SETBP1, and RUNX1T1 when compared to HPV positive cervical non-NENs (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4).

From transcriptomic data, we used GSEA to interrogate expression of gene sets representing molecular pathways between HPV-positive NENs and non-NENs (Fig. 5). Compared to HPV-positive NENs, HPV-positive non-NEN tumors of anorectal, cervical, and head and neck sites were significantly enriched for expression of genes related to interferon alpha response (q < 0.2). Enrichment for expression of genes related to allograft rejection was observed in HPV-positive non-NEN tumors from cervical and head and neck origins (q < 0.2). Enrichment for gene expression related to reactive oxygen species pathways and TGFβ signaling was observed specifically in HPV-positive non-NEN tumors from anorectal origin (q < 0.2). Enrichment for gene expression related to apoptosis, complement, and epithial-to-mesenchymal transition pathways was observed specifically in HPV-positive non-NEN tumors from cervical origin (q < 0.2, all). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the most comprehensive analysis of the genomic and transcriptomic landscapes of HPV-positive NENs to-date. We report the most common sites of origin for HPV positivity using a large cohort of NENs. We also noted clinically relevant characteristics, such as higher prevalence of high-grade tumors among HPV-positive NENs. Through a comprehensive molecular analysis, we reported significant variations of actionable targets among HPV-positive NENs across different sites of origin.

NENs comprise a diverse and heterogeneous group of tumors that have been better categorized with immunohistochemistry over time. Immunohistochemistry is widely used for grading by Ki-67 thresholds in gastroentero-pancreatic NENs and may also be used to determine anatomical site of origin or distinguish well-differentiated and poorly differentiated grade 3 NENs [17]. HPV-positive NENs are a distinct biological subset with unique molecular features. The growing use of genomic and transcriptomic analyses has allowed a more nuanced demonstration of biologic heterogeneity among NENs and facilitated identification of therapeutic targets [18]. We know from other cancers such as HPV-related head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, that HPV positivity adds another layer of heterogeneity to these cancers. For example, patients with HPV-positive head and neck cancers have significantly improved outcomes and are more likely to benefit from anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) therapy [19, 20]. However, since these data are largely missing for HPV-positive NENs, our study addresses a critical gap in existing literature.

Our study identified significantly higher prevalence of several genomic alterations in HPV-positive NENs compared to non-NEN counterparts from the same tumor sites, including actionable therapeutic targets. For example, HPV positive NENs of anus and rectum had a significantly higher prevalence of ERBB2 mutation, which is a developing biomarker for ERBB2 inhibitors [21].

GSEA showed that HPV-positive non-NEN tumors had significantly higher expression of genes related to interferon alpha response than HPV positive NEN, and it can be inferred that HPV-positive non-NEN tumors would have better response to immunotherapy than NEN tumors. These findings reflect the increasing clinical application of immunotherapy for the common HPV-positive non-NENs. For example, treatment of HPV-positive cervical cancers with pembrolizumab have improved survival outcomes [22], while pembrolizumab and nivolumab have improved outcomes in squamous cell cancers of the head and neck [23]. In general, immune checkpoint inhibitors have demonstrated limited clinical activity among extrapulmonary neuroendocrine carcinomas [24]. However, cervical NENs are largely known to lack markers that correlate with clinical benefit of immune checkpoint inhibitors such as PD-L1 positivity, high tumor mutational burden, and microsatellite instability [25, 26].

The study has several inherent limitations. Its retrospective design restricts the ability to establish direct clinical correlations. Additionally, the relatively small sample size of HPV-positive NENs, while reflective of the rarity of these tumors, limits the generalizability of the findings. Another key limitation is that the Caris database only identified HPV16 and HPV18 subtypes at the time of data collection, potentially overlooking the presence of other oncogenic HPV variants that may contribute to NEN pathogenesis.

Despite these limitations, our study offers valuable insights by identifying a distinct subset of HPV-positive NENs. Moreover, the observed differences in the genomic landscape of HPV-positive NENs highlight the potential utility of routine HPV testing at diagnosis to refine molecular classification and guide therapeutic decision-making. Importantly, this study also underscores key genomic and transcriptomic distinctions between HPV-positive NENs and non-NEN malignancies, including potentially actionable alterations that warrant further investigation in prospective clinical trials. Further studies are needed to evaluate outcome differences between HPV-positive and HPV-negative NENs for relevant therapies.
