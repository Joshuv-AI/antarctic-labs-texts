Lower limb necrotising fasciitis descending from malignant colonic perforation: a rare pattern
Authors: Elliot Robbie, Seema Desai, Ashok Ramavath
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryNecrotising fasciitis (NF) is a rare surgical emergency characterised by soft tissue necrosis and systemic compromise. Typically it originates following adjacent skin and soft tissue insult; however, our unusual case required a high index of clinical suspicion to avert potential mortality. A man in his 60s with diabetes mellitus presented with 2 weeks of knee pain, swelling and necrotic skin on the posterior calf. X-ray demonstrated subcutaneous emphysema. Initial debridement confirmed extensive necrotising fasciitis of the whole lower limb with tracking through femoral canal into the abdomen. CT confirmed sigmoid colon perforation. Both life-saving Hartmann’s and hip disarticulation procedures were performed with good outcomes. Histology confirmed locally invasive sigmoid colon adenocarcinoma. Our case highlights lower limb necrotising fasciitis as a rare complication secondary to sigmoid perforation associated with malignancy. In cases where the infection nidus cannot be identified, an abdominal source should be considered.Keywords: Gas/Free Gas, Bone and joint infections, Colon cancer, Orthopaedic and trauma surgery

Background

Necrotising fasciitis (NF) is a rare surgical emergency characterised by extensive soft tissue necrosis and systemic compromise.1 2 It carries significant morbidity and mortality which worsens the longer it is untreated.3 Mortality is 100% without surgical debridement, falling to 16.4% in cases of surgically managed community-acquired NF.4 This condition is usually known to originate following adjacent skin and soft tissue insult. It can be difficult to differentiate at presentation from cellulitis, erysipelas and other soft tissue infections which can lead to a delay in diagnosis. While various scores such as the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotising Fasciitis (LRINEC) score have been created to help differentiate cellulitis from necrotising fasciitis, they have poor specificity in clinical practice.5 Missing diagnosis of NF at admission to hospital is common with only 15–34% of cases having a correct admission diagnosis.2 Ultimately it is a challenging clinical diagnosis; broad-spectrum antibiotics and surgical debridement remain the mainstay of treatment.

Necrotising fasciitis is categorised as polymicrobial or monomicrobial based on available culture results, with polymicrobial being more common.6 Polymicrobial NF (classed as Type 1) usually occurs in patients who are immunocompromised or have diabetes, whereas monomicrobial NF (Type 2) usually occurs in younger, healthy individuals with a history of minor trauma and skin breach or intravenous drug use. The major risk factor for NF is diabetes mellitus but others include chronic kidney disease, peripheral vascular disease, intravenous drug users, older age, immunosuppressive medication and malignancy.7

Once bacteria are inoculated within the subcutaneous tissues, it can spread rapidly along the fascial planes. Fibrous attachments within the hands, feet and scalp often slow the spread of infection; by contrast, the lack of fibrous attachments within the fascia on the trunk and limbs allows a more rapid infectious spread throughout those planes. The extensive soft tissue destruction leads to oedema and erythema. Thrombosis of blood vessels within the subcutaneous tissue from the infection results in subsequent tissue necrosis alongside toxins released by the bacteria.8 Muscle involvement occurs in advanced disease. While necrotising fasciitis is the common term used to describe the disease, the potential involvement of all components of the soft tissues rather than exclusively limited to the fascia has led to ‘Necrotising soft tissue infection’ being used in certain fields.

Clostridium species are anaerobes often present in polymicrobial infections, known to produce gas which can result in crepitus clinically and gas on X-ray; however, gas is not present in all cases of NF and may only be present in around 18–38% of cases.9 10 Alternatively, in monomicrobial infections, virulent bacteria such as Streptococcus pyogenes release toxins with rapid progression to toxic shock syndrome, involving profound systemic compromise and multiorgan failure requiring intensive care support.

The diagnosis of NF relies on a high index of suspicion and debridement should not be delayed; however, in cases of diagnostic uncertainty, further investigations may provide evidence to support the need for surgical interventions. Gross derangement of blood will often be present, including elevated C reactive protein, white cell count, creatine kinase, lactic acidosis and deranged clotting function. Acute kidney injury may also be present.2 Blood cultures may be positive in up to 27% of NF cases if obtained prior to administration of antibiotics, compared with just 2% of cellulitis cases;11 12 however, the time taken to yield blood culture results limits diagnostic value in the acute setting.

X-ray is not a sensitive diagnostic modality and while gas may or may not be visible, absence of it does not rule out NF. MRI is 100% sensitive and 86% specific. It shows gas tracking, fat stranding and fascial thickening. CT is 80% sensitive.13

While investigations can be useful, NF is ultimately a clinical diagnosis and where suspicion of NF is high, investigations should not delay surgical debridement. A delay of 24 hours or more is an independent risk factor for mortality.13 Intraoperative findings may include lack of bleeding, necrotic, non-contractile tissues, ‘dishwater pus’ and lack of resistance in usually adherent tissues.14 Here, we are reporting a rare, unusual case of lower limb necrotising fasciitis which required a high index of clinical suspicion and timely intervention to achieve adequate source control with good outcome.

Case presentation

A man in his 60s presented with a 2 week history of left knee pain, swelling and malaise which had begun after he spent an afternoon working on the deck of a ship. He had a medical history of diet-controlled diabetes and was otherwise independent; WHO performance status 0. His knee pain had progressed rapidly over the last 24 hours and he noticed a small blue patch over the posterior aspect of the calf; due to this, he arrived at the hospital by ambulance at midday (day 1).

Investigations

On arrival, he was septic with a blood pressure of 91/57 mm Hg, heart rate of 86 beats per minute and respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute. He was apyrexial at admission. His white cell count was 23.4×109/L, C reactive protein 331 mg/L, INR 1.7, haemoglobin 110 g/L, potassium of 6.1 mmol/L, eGFR 43 mL/min/1.73m2 and lactate of 8.7 mmol/L. He was noted to be in acute kidney injury stage 2 and his LRINEC score was 8. Examination confirmed a tender, swollen left knee with a restricted range of motion, medial erythema and crepitus. There was a small necrotic skin patch on the posterior part of the calf without clear skin breaks or foreign bodies (figures1 2). His abdomen was not peritonitic. Knee X-ray showed gas within the fascial planes over the medial aspect of the knee (figures3 4). He was initially diagnosed with leg cellulitis but was subsequently referred to orthopaedics late at night as a potential NF.

As per hospital local guidelines he was managed with intravenous fluid resuscitation, catheterisation and intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g three times a day and clindamycin 1.2 g four times a day. An MRI scan confirmed the presence of ‘Widespread diffuse subcutaneous oedema, gas locules noted in the thigh, deep subcutaneous/myofascial plane boundary, extending along the margins of the vastus medialis and sartorius’.

Treatment

Intraoperatively, extensive necrosis was noted throughout the lower limb. The necrosis extended superiorly into the abdomen through the femoral canal; however, no laparotomy was performed after consulting general surgeons (figures58). Amputation was not performed keeping in line with his preoperative wishes. Multiple tissue samples were obtained and sent to microbiology for culture and sensitivities. Postoperatively he was managed with inotropic support in the intensive treatment unit (ITU). The postoperative abdomen-pelvis CT scan showed acute, locally perforated sigmoid diverticulitis with extension into the left iliopsoas and subsequent contamination of the left hip joint (figures911); it could not completely exclude a colonic malignancy.

He stabilised well in ITU and was counselled on his critical condition and consented to hip disarticulation. He underwent a left hip disarticulation 2 days later (day 4). Subsequently on day 6, he underwent Hartmann’s procedure and wound debridement. It was noted that there was a large proximal sigmoid phlegmon perforating the lateral abdominal wall and communicating with the ipsilateral hip joint, which was felt to be malignant in appearance. He underwent further wound revision on day 8.

The patient began to recover after effective surgical source control. Ongoing surgical interventions and antibiotic regimens were regularly discussed in the Bone Infection MDT. Tissue cultures from initial surgery isolated Escherichia coli, Streptococcus constellatus and Fusobacterium necrophorum. Tissue specimens from relook surgery isolated Enterococcus faecium and E. coli in keeping with colonic contamination. Antibiotics at this stage were rationalised to intravenous vancomycin dosed as per trust guidelines and cotrimoxazole 960 mg two times per day. He was extubated and discharged from ITU after 8 days. A final wound debridement and closure of left hip disarticulation was performed on day 11. All the abdominal and hip disarticulation drains were removed on day 13 and day 21, respectively. He had a prolonged 4 week course of cotrimoxazole with oral metronidazole 400 mg three times a day, at which point inflammatory markers settled and he was discharged after MDT discussion.

Outcome and follow-up

Histology from Hartmann’s procedure confirmed a moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma (pT4b) with evidence of perforation and invasion into adjacent structures. None of the 16 lymph nodes were involved (pN0) but there was vascular invasion (V2). The completeness of resection was difficult to ascertain as while certainly an R1 resection it was potentially R2 as the tumour had perforated. A staging CT scan showed an 8 mm left lower lobe lung nodule. His overall staging was pT4b pN0 V2 L0 R1/2. Genotyping showed K RAS wild-type, N RAS mutant, B RAF wild-type and MMR preserved. There was also background diverticulitis with associated abscess formation.

Further oncology review and a positron emission tomography revealed liver metastasis. As a result, 3 month chemotherapy was given with oxaliplatin and capecitabine. The patient was currently undergoing treatment and follow-up at the time of writing of this report.

DISCUSSION

In summary, this man developed left lower limb polymicrobial necrotising fasciitis secondary to a malignant perforation of the sigmoid colon with retroperitoneal spread of gastrointestinal bacteria distally through the femoral canal. His presentation of acute lower limb necrotising fasciitis with no clear skin breach was a distractor to the true abdominal source of his infection, which was only identified after initial surgery and a CT scan. Given his excellent pre-admission functional status with a background only of diet-controlled diabetes mellitus, he has suffered profound morbidity as a result of his illness. His prognosis is uncertain, carefully monitored and will depend on chemotherapy response; however, given his presentation, he was fortunate to avoid the significant fatality often seen during the acute phase of illness.

In clinical practice, we usually encounter an ascending pattern of this life-threatening condition which supports the surgical source control at the origin site of infection focus; but in our case, the origin was in the abdomen and source control could not have been achieved entirely until his original problem was addressed. This case highlights an unusual presentation of NF and as such, a delayed diagnosis at his initial presentation. The lack of a cutaneous breach on the lower limb can be a misleading reassurance that nothing has inoculated the soft tissues beneath and this may contribute to why he was referred to as cellulitis by the medical team at the initial presentation.

Perforation of the sigmoid colon whether by diverticulitis or malignancy is a rarely documented cause of lower limb NF which often results in significant morbidity through involvement of the hip joint; these cases usually result in hip disarticulation as part of radical surgical debridement. These cases often present with NF of the lower limb rather than frank abdominal sepsis due to the retroperitoneal nature of the colonic perforation.

A literature search of PubMed Central for ‘Necrotising Fasciitis Colon Perforation’ yielded three cases of lower limb NF secondary to perforated sigmoid diverticulitis,15,17 and a further fourteen cases secondary to perforation of colonic or rectal malignancy.18,31 All three cases secondary to diverticulitis survived, with two requiring hip disarticulation.15 16 Outcomes for perforations secondary to colon or rectal perforation were suboptimal, with five surviving admission19 20 25 29 30 and nine deaths.1821,24 26 Hip disarticulation was performed in one case31 and surgical debridement in eleven other cases.18,2022 24 This highlights that lower limb necrotising fasciitis remains a rare but well-documented complication of colorectal perforation carrying significant morbidity and mortality.

The initial antibiotic regimen of piperacillin-tazobactam and clindamycin was appropriate pending the culture results. Clindamycin has been shown in animal studies to decrease toxin production in Group A Streptococcal infection and limits toxin-induced tissue damage. Broad-spectrum penicillin and clindamycin are often effective empirical antibiotic cover at initial presentation.32

Lastly, it is important to consider NF as a potential diagnosis when assessing patients at admission to the hospital. As in our case, the necrosis can involve all soft tissue compartments and as such, the term ‘Necrotising soft tissue infection’ is often more reflective of the overall disease that initiates and triggers the severe soft tissue injury. The clinician must cast the net wide and consider whether a necrotising soft tissue infection could be present and investigate if so; this would potentially allow more cases to be diagnosed at an earlier stage of infection and thus morbidity reduced with prompt debridement. However, in our case report, we have used the term ‘necrotising fasciitis’ throughout to allow effective recognition within the current literature.

Learning points

Where no cutaneous breach can be identified while managing lower limb necrotising fasciitis, an intra-abdominal pathology source with tracking through the fascial planes should always be considered as a possibility.

Necrotising fasciitis is increasing in prevalence, which may be due to ageing populations and increasing comorbidities. Given its rapidly progressive nature and high mortality, a prompt diagnosis is crucial. Having a low threshold for investigation and treatment by casting the diagnostic net wide will result in early diagnosis and intervention.
