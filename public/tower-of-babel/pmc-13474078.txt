PVT inhibition mitigates neuropathology and cognitive deficits in chronic cerebral hypoperfusion
Authors: Yuying Guan, Jinghao Ma, Rui Li, Jialu Xu, Taoyue Xu, Gaifen Li, Le Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryChronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH) is a key pathophysiological substrate of vascular cognitive impairment, and the reorganization of neuronal activity across disease progression underlies network dysfunction and intervention strategy. Using immediate-early gene imaging with whole-brain mapping, we characterized spatiotemporal activation patterns in a bilateral common carotid artery stenosis mouse model at early (day 7) and chronic (day 40) stages across 225 brain regions. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis identified the paraventricular thalamus (PVT) as a persistently hyperactive and central region, with early thalamic-hypothalamic hyperactivation giving way to delayed cortical deactivation at chronic stages. Chemogenetic inhibition of PVT during the early post-ischemic phase attenuated neuronal damage in medial prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and white matter, and improved cognitive performance and sleep continuity. These findings implicate PVT hyperactivation as a disease-associated node contributing to network-wide pathology in CCH, and suggest that early suppression of a central hub may help limit distributed injury.Keywords: chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, cognitive function, vascular cognitive impairment, paraventricular thalamus, brain-wide activity

Introduction

Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH) refers to a pathophysiological condition characterized by a persistent reduction in cerebral blood flow. Clinically, it is commonly observed in conditions such as carotid artery stenosis, cerebral small vessel disease, systemic atherosclerosis, chronic heart failure, and age-related impairments in cerebrovascular regulation.1,2,3,4 CCH leads to long-term ischemia and hypoxia in specific brain regions, including the frontal and temporal lobes as well as the limbic system, thereby inducing a continuum of phenotypes ranging from cognitive impairment and depression-like behaviors to vascular dementia (VaD).5,6 Consequently, CCH is widely regarded as a key pathological basis underlying vascular cognitive impairment and dementia.2,7,8 VaD is the second most common type of dementia after Alzheimer disease, accounting for approximately 15%–20% of cases in North America and Europe and up to 30% in Asia, imposing a significant burden on public health.9,10 However, no clearly effective etiological treatments are currently available. Therefore, exploring the mechanisms of CCH remains one of the most promising approaches for identifying translational therapeutic targets.

Previous studies have shown that CCH is accompanied by multiple levels of abnormalities, including metabolic and mitochondrial dysfunction, glial activation, demyelination and axonal injury, and disruption of the blood-brain barrier.2,11,12 Cerebral ischemia first induces the strong release of glutamate, which is excitotoxic and contributes to cell death, a process that is counteracted by the inhibitory neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) through cell hyperpolarization.13,14 Chronic ischemia causes neuronal excitation/inhibition (E/I) imbalance. Notably, disturbance of neuronal activity, particularly the underlying E/I imbalance, is regarded as a central link between structural brain pathology and cognitive dysfunction.15 Furthermore, clinical and animal studies have shown that cerebral ischemia is accompanied by enhanced cortical slow waves and abnormal default mode network-thalamocortical coupling.16 In summary, CCH-induced neuronal E/I imbalance and neural network dysfunction constitute the core mechanisms of cognitive impairment. However, whole-brain, unbiased activity mapping throughout CCH progression remains lacking, which limits the identification of effective intervention targets.

To address this issue, our study employed the immediate-early gene (IEG) marker to label neuronal activity. Among IEGs, c-Fos is the most widely used marker that can dynamically reflect neuronal activation.17,18,19 Using the classical bilateral common carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) mouse model, we mapped whole-brain c-Fos expression on postoperative days 7 and 40 to systematically characterize the activated brain regions and their temporal evolution. The results revealed a pronounced activation of the paraventricular thalamus (PVT) during the early phase of chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH). Notably, the PVT forms dense connections with the medial prefrontal cortex, nucleus accumbens, amygdala, and hippocampus, enabling it to integrate arousal and stress signals, gate reward-seeking and aversive behaviors, and regulate recognition, episodic, and working memory.20,21 These functional domains closely correspond to the cognitive and emotional impairments frequently observed in patients with CCH. There is substantial evidence that cerebral ischemia-induced functional plasticity is partly driven by alterations in neuronal excitability.22 Cerebral ischemia-induced neuronal excitability significantly correlates with lesion severity.23,24,25 Therefore, the early hyperactivation of the PVT, considering its central role in stress processing and limbic regulation, may contribute to post-ischemic network dysfunction. Accordingly, we selectively inhibited PVT activity using a chemogenetic approach and found that suppression of the PVT significantly alleviated CCH-induced cognitive deficits by mitigating pathological damage in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus. Collectively, this study systematically delineated the whole-brain response profile of CCH and identified abnormal activation of the PVT. Chemogenetic inhibition of PVT activity attenuated pathological and behavioral abnormalities, suggesting PVT as a potential therapeutic target for CCH.

Results

Brain-wide imaging and mapping of c-Fos activity in CCH

CCH was modeled using the BCAS paradigm. The initial 3–14 days post-BCAS represent a critical window for pathological evolution, involving glial activation and white matter injury progression.26,27 Within this period, day 7 reflects a remodeling phase characterized by neuroinflammatory upregulation and the initiation of cerebral blood flow recovery, prior to the onset of irreversible white matter lesions.27 In contrast, at the chronic stage (beyond 4 weeks), both structural injury and cognitive deficits typically reach a stable plateau.28

Consistent with this timeline, our longitudinal measurements of cortical cerebral blood flow and behavioral assessments supported the progression of the model. Laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) demonstrated a significant 55%–65% reduction in cortical perfusion at 1 h post-BCAS (Figures S1A and S1B). Subsequent evaluations using arterial spin labeling (ASL-MRI) at 3, 7, 14, and 40 days revealed a persistent state of hypoperfusion (Figures S1C and S1D). Correspondingly, significant memory impairment in the novel object recognition (NOR) test emerged only at day 40, while performance remained intact at earlier time points (Figure S1E). Consequently, day 7 (remodeling phase) and day 40 (decompensated phase) were selected as the optimal time points to identify the early activated hub nodes driving the transition from ischemic stress to chronic cognitive decline.

To unbiasedly and systematically assess whole-brain activation patterns in CCH, we used c-Fos, an IEG, to label neuronal activity and performed three-dimensional imaging and quantification of activated cells throughout the brain. IEGs have been widely employed to visualize neuronal activity in brain regions.17,18,19 To further elucidate the systemic coordination between brain regions, c-Fos immunofluorescence staining was performed, followed by statistical analysis and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), to characterize regional brain activity patterns and identify hub region alterations across disease stages (Figure 1A).

Whole-brain samples processed with iDISCO+ were imaged using high-speed selective plane illumination microscopy. Tissue architecture was well preserved throughout the clearing and imaging procedures. Three-dimensional brain images were reconstructed and registered to the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas for automated brain region segmentation (Figures 1B–1D). A deep learning-based automated cell counting algorithm was applied to identify c-Fos+ cells (Figure 1E). Collectively, these approaches enabled the generation of an activity-dependent whole-brain mapping atlas.

The number of c-Fos+ cells across 225 brain regions was quantified in Sham, BCAS 7 days, and BCAS 40 days groups, and the data were normalized using Z score transformation. Sham 7 days and Sham 40 days groups showed no significant differences in c-Fos expression (Figure S2A). Pooled Sham data were therefore used as a common control reference. To characterize region-specific activation, all brain regions were categorized into six classes according to the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas, including 32 cerebral nuclei subregions, 12 cortical plate regions, 170 cortical subplate regions, 39 thalamic regions, 17 hypothalamic regions, and 5 midbrain regions. Overall, quantitative analysis of c-Fos+ cells revealed pronounced temporal dynamics of neuronal activation during CCH (Figure 1D). At 7 days post-BCAS, c-Fos expression was markedly increased in most brain regions relative to Sham controls, with the thalamus and hypothalamus exhibiting the most prominent activation. Cerebral nuclei and cortical plate regions also showed significant increases. By 40 days post-BCAS, the overall number of activated regions had declined compared with day 7, suggesting adaptive responses to chronic hypoperfusion. Nevertheless, the thalamus and hypothalamus remained highly activated, indicating sustained sensitivity of these diencephalic structures to ischemic stress. These observations are consistent with previous reports, highlighting differential regional sensitivity to ischemia and dynamic whole-brain activation patterns over the course of disease progression.29,30

Generation of CCH-specific whole-brain c-Fos activity maps

To identify activated key brain regions in CCH, we performed systematic differential analysis of whole-brain c-Fos+ cell counts. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation among Sham, BCAS 7 days, and BCAS 40 days samples in the reduced dimensional space (Figure 2A), indicating distinct activation patterns at different stages of ischemia. Volcano plot analysis showed that at 7 days post-BCAS, 33 brain regions were upregulated, and 5 were downregulated (Figure 2B), whereas at 40 days, 22 regions were upregulated and 14 were downregulated (Figure 2C). Venn diagram analysis identified three classes of spatiotemporal neuronal activation dynamics: 23 regions altered exclusively at day 7, 21 regions altered exclusively at day 40, and 15 regions showing sustained changes at both time points (Figure 2D). Compared to Sham, persistently altered regions exhibited two distinct patterns in cortical and diencephalic structures (Figure 2E). Cortical activation showed progressive decline, with posteromedial cortical amygdalar nucleus (COApm) significantly upregulated at day 7 but downregulated at day 40. In contrast, thalamic (lateral habenula [LHb], medial habenula [MH], paracentral nucleus [PCN], PVT) and hypothalamic (arcuate nucleus [ARH], ventral part of paraventricular thalamic nucleus [PVi]) nuclei maintained sustained high activation.

Early stage activation in the BCAS 7 days’ group (Figure 2F) was predominantly confined to thalamic and hypothalamic regions. Key activated nuclei included ventral posteromedial thalamic nucleus (VPM), PCN in the thalamus, and paraventricular hypothalamus (PVH), ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) in the hypothalamus. Chronic-phase neuronal activity analysis (Figure 2G) revealed progressive dissociation between cortical and subcortical responses. c-Fos+ cells in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) showed an upward trend at 7 days post-BCAS but significantly decreased by 40 days post-surgery (Figures S2B–S2D). Deactivation in sensory cortices (auditory cortex [AUD], visual cortex [VIS], retrosplenial agranular cortex [RSPagl]) coexisted with hyperactivation in hippocampal dentate gyrus of hippocampu (DG-sm) and selective thalamic nuclei (dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus [LGd], ventral posterior nucleus [VP]). We did not observe significant changes in other hippocampal regions, possibly due to the delayed neuronal death characteristic of CA1 neurons following global ischemia.29 Notably, we found watershed regions vulnerable to ischemic injury. We compared c-Fos regions with cerebral vascular architecture31 to identify the primary somatosensory cortex (SSp) as a watershed-vulnerable region and the retrosplenial cortex (RSP) as a non-watershed control (Figures S2E and S2F). Quantitative analysis showed that c-Fos density in the SSp significantly increased at 7 days post-BCAS and declined by 40 days, whereas no significant changes were observed in the RSP across these time points (Figures S2G and S2H). These results indicate that neuronal activation patterns in the BCAS model are influenced, at least in part, by the underlying vascular territory.

Collectively, these findings reveal a distinct spatiotemporal evolution of CCH-induced neuronal activation abnormalities: early phase alterations predominantly affected thalamic and hypothalamic nuclei, whereas chronic-phase changes shifted toward cell deactivation, highlighting the heightened vulnerability of diencephalic structures to chronic hypoperfusion. Representative c-Fos expression profiles (Figure 2H) demonstrated robust activation of both the PVH and PVT during the acute phase. These early activation patterns suggest that CCH-induced neural circuit alterations may be intimately associated with stress reactivity and emotional regulation.

Early stage CCH induces widespread reorganization of emotion and cognition networks

To investigate how differential brain regions coordinate during the early stage of ischemia, we performed WGCNA. This analysis identified multiple co-activation modules specifically associated with disease stages (Figure 3A). The “turquoise” module exhibited the strongest correlation with the subacute phase (7 days; r = 0.96, p < 0.001; Figures 3A and S3A). Mapping this module onto the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas revealed that it primarily comprised thalamic regions, including the PVT and ventral posteromedial nucleus (VPM), hypothalamic regions such as the posterior hypothalamus (PH), and the caudal part of the posterior lateral amygdaloid nucleus (COAp) (Figure 3B). Notably, these regions overlapped with the significantly altered areas described previously. Correlation analysis showed positive associations between BCAS 7d and COApm, RSPv, SSp-bfd, OLF, and PVT (module membership [MM] > 0.5, gene significance [GS] > 0.5, Figure 3C).

To determine the functional significance of this module, core nodes were mapped into a connectivity network (Figure 3D). Functional annotation using the Allen Brain Connectivity Atlas revealed enrichment in known emotion and cognition regulatory circuits, including key nodes such as PVT, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex (Figures S3B and S3C). Remarkably, PVT displayed the strongest network centrality among regions shared by both functional networks (Figure 3E), indicating that early stage chronic hypoperfusion induces widespread reorganization of emotion and cognition neural networks, with PVT acting as a pivotal network hub. Similar analysis of BCAS 40d identified negatively correlated cortical regions (VISp, COAp, AUD; MM > 0.5, GS < −0.5, Figures S4A and S4B). The resulting network displayed fragmented architecture dominated by local, intra-regional connections (Figure 3F), suggesting that chronic hypoperfusion damages long-range connectivity. This aligns with previous reports of ischemia-disrupted thalamo-cortical coupling.16,32

To examine the long-term effects of CCH on brain networks, we analyzed the preservation of the early stress module (7-day turquoise module) at 40 days post-ischemia. Results showed extensive network reorganization of regions activated at 7 days. Most regional connections were lost, with only a subset retained in the 40-day red module (Figure S4C), suggesting that disruption of early compensatory circuits may be a primary mechanism of chronic neurological dysfunction. This module consisted primarily of thalamic subregions (Figure 3G). Notably, the PVT showed the highest weighted degree within this cross-temporal preserved network (Figure 3H). In summary, functional network analysis revealed a central role for the PVT in CCH-induced network reorganization. As a key node in the early stress module, the PVT participated in cross-regional emotion and cognition networks. During the chronic phase when networks disintegrated, the PVT showed the highest centrality in the residual network, serving as the main hub for local thalamic connectivity. Thus, PVT dysregulation may represent a key pathological change linking early compensatory responses to chronic dysfunction.

Chemogenetic inhibition of PVT alleviates pathological damage in multiple brain regions

To characterize the temporal profile of PVT activation in CCH, we assessed c-Fos expression in distinct anatomical subregions across multiple time points. The PVT is one of the most prominent midline thalamic structures and comprises anatomically and functionally distinct anterior (aPVT, bregma −0.22 to −0.94 mm) and posterior (pPVT, bregma −1.58 to −2.30 mm) domains (Figure 4A).20,21 These subregions exhibit distinct connectivity patterns and functional specializations: the aPVT primarily projects to the prefrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens, regulating cognitive control and goal-directed behavior.33The pPVT predominantly targets limbic structures, including the amygdala and hypothalamus, mediating stress responses and emotional salience.21 However, the role of the PVT in chronic cerebral ischemia remains unexplored.

In the sham group, only a few neurons within the aPVT and pPVT exhibited c-Fos positivity. In contrast, following BCAS surgery at days 7, 14, and 40, c-Fos+ cell numbers increased markedly in both subregions. Notably, the aPVT displayed a more pronounced activation, reaching its peak at day 7 (Figures 4B and 4C). Alterations in neuronal excitability may underlie pathological processes in cerebral ischemia, consistent with these findings.22,23 We found that overall cell density in PVT decreased 40 days after BCAS, while the number and proportion of c-Fos+ cells were notably increased compared to the sham group (Figures S5A–S5C). Additionally, using TUNEL labeling to mark damaged and apoptotic cells, we observed colocalization of c-Fos and TUNEL signals in PVT 40 days after BCAS (Figure S5D), indicating that some activated cells are undergoing damage-related processes. Therefore, the increase in c-Fos signal likely reflects pathological activation of surviving or stressed cells, rather than an increase in total PVT cell numbers.

Since both the aPVT and pPVT exhibited significant activation following BCAS, we treated the PVT as a complete functional unit for intervention. Chemogenetic inhibition of the PVT was achieved by injecting AAV-hSyn-hM4Di-mCherry (Figure 4D). At least 2 weeks following virus injection, BCAS surgery was performed to induce chronic cerebral ischemia. Clozapine-N-oxide CNO was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) once daily from days 7–21 post-BCAS. Tissue collection and staining were conducted at day 40 post-surgery (Figure 4E). H&E staining showed severe tissue damage in the PVT of BCAS 40 days mice compared to Sham, characterized by increased nuclear pyknosis, cytoplasmic vacuolization, and reduced cell density. The density of damaged cells within the PVT of BCAS+hM4Di mice (25 ± 9 cells/mm2) was significantly lower than that in BCAS 40 days mice (62 ± 11 cells/mm2) and approached Sham levels (21 ± 5 cells/mm2; p < 0.01; Figures 4F and 4G), suggesting that chemogenetic inhibition of PVT activity significantly attenuated pathological damage.

The protective effect of PVT inhibition extended to multiple vulnerable brain regions. In the mPFC, BCAS caused extensive histopathological damage in the cingulate (Cg), prelimbic (PL), and infralimbic (IL) subregions (Figure 5A). PVT inhibition significantly ameliorated these pathological changes (Figure 5B). Similar protective effects were observed in the hippocampal region. Compared to sham controls, PVT inhibition significantly ameliorated BCAS-induced neuronal loss in the hippocampal CA1, CA2, and dentate gyrus (Figures 5C and 5D).

White matter integrity, which is closely associated with cognitive function,34 was also significantly protected. We performed myelin basic protein (MBP) immunofluorescence staining on the corpus callosum, the largest white matter structure in the mouse brain. The results revealed that PVT inhibition significantly attenuated white matter damage in BCAS mice compared to sham (Figures 5E and 5F). Collectively, these findings reveal that the neuroprotective effects of PVT inhibition extend beyond the thalamus to encompass multiple ischemia-vulnerable brain regions, including the cortex, hippocampus, and white matter.

Chemogenetic inhibition of PVT activity mitigates cognitive deficits induced by CCH

To evaluate the specific neuroprotective effects of PVT inhibition in CCH, we conducted a series of cognitive and emotional behavioral assessments at 40 days post-BCAS (Figure 6A). The nest building test is a sensitive indicator of general well-being. Quantitative analysis showed that BCAS significantly impaired nest-building performance by reducing both nest quality scores and the percentage of material utilized. Chemogenetic inhibition of the PVT significantly improved nest quality and increased the nest-covered area in BCAS mice (Figures 6B–6D). The open field test evaluated the emotional state and spontaneous locomotor activity. Total distance traveled did not differ among the groups. Time spent in the center zone showed no significant group differences, effectively excluding motor or affective confounds (Figures 6E–6G). The NOR test revealed significant cognitive impairments in BCAS mice. The recognition index decreased significantly (p < 0.01). PVT inhibition markedly improved this deficit (p < 0.05, Figure 6H). Similarly, in the Y-maze test, BCAS mice showed a significantly decreased exploration index (p < 0.0001). Chemogenetic inhibition of the PVT restored this index to near normal levels (p < 0.01, Figures 6I and 6J). Compared with Sham+mCherry+vehicle group, the Sham+mCherry+ CNO group showed no significant differences in cognitive performance. Both the recognition index in the NOR test and novel arm exploration in the Y-maze test remained unchanged (Figures S6A and S6B). Open-field locomotor activity and anxiety-like indices were also not altered either (Figures S6C and S6D). Thus, PVT inhibition alleviates recognition and spatial working memory deficits induced by chronic cerebral ischemia. Given the critical role of the PVT in arousal regulation, we characterized the behavioral sleep-wake profiles of BCAS mice using 24 h continuous video-based monitoring (Figure 6K). Total sleep time did not differ significantly among the Sham, BCAS, and BCAS + hM4Di groups (Figure 6L). However, BCAS mice showed increased sleep fragmentation, as reflected by a significantly higher number of wakefulness episodes. Chemogenetic inhibition of the PVT reduced the number of wakefulness episodes in BCAS mice (Figure 6M). These findings indicate that PVT inhibition improved memory-related behavioral performance and was accompanied by reduced sleep fragmentation.

Discussion

By combining whole-brain c-Fos mapping with network analysis, our work identifies the PVT as a persistently hyperactive and highly central node in CCH. Chemogenetic inhibition of PVT during the early post-ischemic phase attenuated neuronal loss, white matter damage, and cognitive impairment, suggesting that PVT is not merely associated with disease progression but may represent an upstream-acting node contributing to network-wide pathology. These findings raise two key questions: why the PVT emerges as a central hub following BCAS, and how suppressing its activity confers widespread neuroprotection.

Whole-brain activation patterns and region-specific vulnerability

Our whole-brain c-Fos analysis revealed broad but spatially heterogeneous neuronal activation after BCAS, with clear temporal evolution from the early stage (day 7) to the chronic stage (day 40). Early activation was concentrated in diencephalic and limbic regions, including multiple thalamic nuclei, hypothalamic areas, and selected cortical regions, whereas by the chronic stage some regions returned toward baseline or became hypoactive, while others, most notably the PVT, remained activated. This pattern is consistent with a transition from early network recruitment to chronic maladaptation under persistent hypoperfusion.7,35,36 The mechanisms underlying the decreased c-Fos expression at day 40 remain to be elucidated. On one hand, this phenomenon may be partly attributed to relief in blood flow. With partial recovery of perfusion levels, neurons may no longer be in the early acute ischemic stress state, thereby leading to a decline in c-Fos expression. Because ASL-MRI and c-Fos quantification were performed in independent cohorts, these data were interpreted as a group-level temporal comparison rather than an animal-by-animal correlation. On the other hand, although cerebral blood flow showed some improvement, perfusion levels remained lower than the Sham group. It may reflect the cumulative effects of prolonged chronic low perfusion. Our H&E staining preliminarily suggests the possible presence of localized cellular damage at 40 days, and such pathological structural changes may also contribute to the weakening of c-Fos signals.

The regional distribution of pathology was also selective. Hippocampal CA1 and several cortical areas showed marked vulnerability, in-line with previous studies of chronic ischemia.29,37 Limbic activation, particularly in the amygdala, further suggests that CCH engages circuits involved not only in cognition but also in stress and emotional regulation.33 In contrast, basal ganglia structures were relatively preserved, consistent with the notion that chronic hypoperfusion preferentially affects watershed territories and white matter tracts rather than producing uniform injury across all deep gray matter structures.38,39,40 Together, these findings indicate that BCAS induces a distributed but non-random pattern of dysfunction, within which the PVT stands out because of both the magnitude and persistence of its activation.

PVT as a candidate disease-associated hub in chronic hypoperfusion

The PVT receives convergent inputs from hypothalamic homeostatic centers, brainstem arousal systems, and cortical-limbic circuits,21 while sending broad glutamatergic projections to regions critical for cognition, emotion, and memory, including the mPFC and hippocampus.41,42 This integrative organization may be particularly relevant in BCAS. CCH is not only a vascular insult but also a prolonged challenge to brain-state regulation, involving inflammatory responses, stress-related signaling, arousal regulation, and cognition-related network function.6 Because the PVT is positioned to coordinate these domains, it may be preferentially recruited during disease progression.

Once persistently activated, the PVT is well positioned to influence distributed brain networks. Unlike nuclei that participate mainly in restricted local loops, the PVT links multiple cognitive and affective modules simultaneously. Abnormal activity in this nucleus may therefore exert broad effects on network coordination and functional communication.43 Consistent with this view, our WGCNA analysis identified the PVT as a highly central node at both early and chronic stages after BCAS. Moreover, PVT activation peaked early, whereas prominent structural pathology in cortical and hippocampal regions emerged later. This temporal dissociation is consistent with the possibility that PVT hyperactivation precedes and may contribute to network destabilization, rather than simply reflecting established tissue degeneration. However, causal directionality cannot be established from the present correlational data.

Potential mechanisms underlying the protective effect of PVT inhibition

The finding that focal PVT inhibition reduced pathology in distant regions suggests that PVT hyperactivity propagates injury through network-level mechanisms. We propose several candidate interacting pathways. First, focal suppression may mitigate network excitotoxicity. As a broadly projecting glutamatergic nucleus,20 the hyperactive PVT may impose sustained excitatory stress on downstream targets. Through these long-range projections, the chronic overactivation of the PVT can relay pathological signals to cognitive-related cortical and limbic regions, promoting calcium overload, E/I imbalance, and apoptotic signaling.13,15 Suppressing PVT activity may therefore reduce an upstream source of trans-synaptic excitotoxic stress. Second, reducing hyperactivity alleviates metabolic stress. Sustained neuronal hyperactivity is energetically costly. In the setting of CCH, increased activity-related demand cannot be adequately matched by blood supply.44,45 A persistently active hub such as the PVT may therefore exacerbate the mismatch between metabolic demand and restricted perfusion, accelerating tissue injury. Early inhibition of PVT protects vulnerable networks by lowering this maladaptive activity-dependent load during a critical phase of limited perfusion. Third, PVT inhibition restores sleep homeostasis. Our behavioral profiling revealed that CCH induces significant sleep fragmentation, which was attenuated by early PVT inhibition. Given the critical role of consolidated sleep in glymphatic clearance and synaptic homeostasis,46,47 restoring sleep stability may serve as a complementary mechanism that dampens neuroinflammatory amplification and secondary degeneration. The persistence of these benefits after a 3-week washout period suggests that PVT inhibition may initiate a durable neuroprotective state rather than providing merely transient symptomatic relief, though the underlying mechanisms remain to be defined. Collectively, these findings position the PVT as a disease-associated and intervention-sensitive node, and suggest that early hub-targeted suppression may represent a mechanistically distinct strategy for limiting network-wide injury in CCH.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the identification of PVT as a central hub relies on c-Fos-based activity mapping, which captures a snapshot of neuronal activation rather than direct electrophysiological recording; hub status therefore reflects a network-level inference from indirect functional indices. Second, the precise projection-specific mechanisms underlying the neuroprotective effects of PVT inhibition remain undefined. The present study employed global chemogenetic suppression of PVT neurons, which downstream pathways, such as PVT→mPFC or PVT→hippocampus projections, are most critical for the observed benefits remains to be determined. Third, sleep fragmentation was assessed by video-based behavioral monitoring rather than electroencephalogram (EEG)/electromyogram (EMG) recording; the reported sleep-related findings therefore represent associative observations and do not allow characterization of sleep stage architecture. Electrophysiological validation is required before conclusions about sleep quality can be drawn. Fourth, ASL-MRI and c-Fos quantification were performed in independent cohorts, precluding direct animal-level correlations between perfusion deficits and regional activation. Fifth, all experiments were conducted in young adult male mice. Given that vascular cognitive impairment predominantly affects aged individuals and that cardiovascular risk profiles differ between sexes,48,49 the generalizability of these findings to aged and female populations remains to be established.

Experimental model and study participant details

Adult male C57BL/6J wild-type mice (8 weeks, 22–25 g, Stock No. B205) were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. According to the supplier’s health records, the mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions and had no reported abnormal health or immune status upon arrival. Upon receipt, the mice showed no overt signs of illness based on routine inspection. The animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment, maintained on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle at a constant ambient temperature of 22°C ± 2°C, with free access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Capital Medical University (AEEI-2026-154) and performed according to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

All animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.50 All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and the number of animals used. A total of 71 mice were initially used in this study. After excluding 3 mice due to surgical complications or insufficient blood flow reduction, the remaining 68 mice were assigned to the following experimental groups: Sham (n = 26), BCAS 7 days (n = 6), BCAS 14 days (n = 6), BCAS 40d (n = 14), BCAS + AAV (n = 8), and BCAS + hM4Di (n = 8). Sample sizes were determined based on our previous experimental experience and established literature in the field of chronic cerebral hypoperfusion to ensure sufficient statistical power.28 The Sham group included a larger number of animals as they served as a shared control for multiple experimental cohorts across different ischemia durations and the chemogenetic intervention. Mice were assigned to the experimental groups using simple randomization based on a computer-generated random number sequence. For histology and behavioral assessments, data collection and analysis were performed by investigators who were blinded to the experimental groups to minimize bias.

Method details

Surgical procedure for bilateral common carotid artery stenosis

This study utilized the classic bilateral common carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) surgery to establish a mouse model of chronic cerebral hypoperfusion. Anesthesia was rapidly induced with 5% isoflurane (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) and maintained under 1.5% isoflurane using a ventilator (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd.). The BCAS surgery was performed as described previously.28 Briefly, the bilateral common carotid arteries were exposed and individually ligated using microcoils with an inner diameter of 0.18 mm, a pitch of 0.50 mm, and a total length of 2.5 mm. The two sides of the surgery were performed with a 1-h interval to reduce surgical risk. To confirm stenosis, cerebral blood flow was monitored using laser speckle flowgraphy (RWD Life Sciences Co., Ltd.). In the sham group, the bilateral common carotid arteries were exposed but not ligated with coils. Following surgery, post-operative care, behavioral testing, staining, and data analysis were performed by researchers blinded to the treatment conditions. Cognitive function was evaluated after 40 days of recovery. Although post-operative carotid artery diameter was not directly measured, stenosis severity was standardized by applying microcoils with a defined inner diameter, and successful hypoperfusion was confirmed by LSCI and ASL-MRI measurements. Cerebral blood flow was monitored via laser speckle imaging 1 h post-surgery and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) 3 days post-surgery. Mice were included in the study only if their bilateral regional cerebral blood flow decreased uniformly to approximately 65% of the pre-surgical baseline and they exhibited no severe surgical complications (such as vision loss, motor hemiplegia, seizures, or significant weight loss). In this study, A total of 45 mice underwent the BCAS surgery. Of these, 1 mouse was excluded due to acute surgical complications, and 2 mice were excluded because their cerebral blood flow reduction failed to reach the inclusion criteria The remaining 42 mice in the BCAS cohorts reached the experimental endpoints. Together with 26 mice in the Sham group, a total of 68 mice were included in the final data analysis.

Cerebral blood flow measurement by ASL-MRI

Cerebral blood flow was measured at 3, 7, 14 and 40 days post-BCAS (n = 6 per group) using FAIR-ASL on a 7.0T small animal MRI system (Bruker BioSpec 70/20 USR, Germany). Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane in oxygen and maintained at 1.5% during imaging. Scanning parameters were: TR = 4000 ms, TE = 25 ms, labeling duration = 2.0 s, post-labeling delay = 0.5 s, FOV = 20 × 20 mm, acquisition matrix = 128 × 128, spatial resolution = 0.156 × 0.156 × 1 mm3, slice thickness = 1 mm. Axial continuous scanning covered the entire brain with interleaved acquisition of label and control images. Respiratory rate (90–120 breaths/min) and heart rate (400–600 bpm) were monitored throughout. Images were reconstructed using Fourier transform with baseline correction and spatial registration. Mean cerebral blood flow was calculated from three manually defined ROIs in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) on identical coronal sections and expressed as mL/(100 g min).

Brain tissue section preparation and immunofluorescence staining

The animals were perfused with 0.1 M PBS and fixative (4% paraformaldehyde) for fixation. After brain extraction, post-fixation was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde, and the brain tissue was sectioned into 50 μm thick slices using a vibrating microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Brain sections from the Sham and BCAS groups at 7 days, 14 days, and 40 days (n = 6 per group per time point) were used for immunofluorescence experiments. Briefly, the sections were first placed in sodium citrate buffer for antigen retrieval. The sections were then blocked with 5% donkey serum (Zhong Shan-Golden Bridge Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China) containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, Beijing). Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-c-Fos primary antibody (226008, SYSY, 1:1000), and incubated for 2 h at room temperature in the dark with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated secondary antibody (ab150079, Abcam, 1:1000) and DAPI (ab228549, Abcam, 1:1000) for 20 min. All steps were performed under light-protected conditions to preserve fluorescence, and sections were mounted with antifade medium for microscopy and subsequent analysis.

Brain section registration and cell counting

Whole-brain sections were aligned to the Adult Mouse Brain–Allen Brain Atlas V3p1 using the Aligning Big Brains & Atlases plugin in FIJI, which performs automatic 2D affine and spline-based in-plane registration.51 The BigWarp framework was applied to correct slice orientation and registration when necessary. Brain sections from Sham and BCAS groups at 7 days, 14 days, and 40 days (n = 6 per group per time point) were processed for PVT c-Fos quantification. The aligned sections were then exported to QuPath, where image annotations were loaded and brain regions were labeled according to the atlas. In QuPath, a machine learning–based classifier was used to detect cells expressing the c-Fos protein. Channels and parameters were carefully adjusted to ensure accurate cell detection, and cells with c-Fos expression were annotated as positive. All whole-brain slices were processed in batch using groovy scripts (for QuPath) to perform unbiased cell detection and counting of target cells. Data from the left and right hemispheres were combined, and the c-Fos expression rate for each annotated brain region was calculated as the ratio of c-Fos+ cells to the total number of detected cells. PVT subregions were anatomically defined using the mouse brain atlas and previously published literature. Sections at approximately bregma −0.22 to −0.94 mm were defined as aPVT, whereas sections at approximately bregma −1.58 to −2.30 mm were defined as pPVT.20,52,53 Assignment was further verified by the characteristic midline location of the PVT along the dorsal third ventricle and by surrounding thalamic landmarks. c-Fos+ cells were quantified separately within atlas-defined aPVT and pPVT regions of interest.

IDISCO+ staining and cell counting

Mouse brain tissues were processed using a modified iDISCO+ whole-mount immunofluorescence staining and clearing protocol.54 Mouse whole-brain was fixed in 4% PFA and dehydrated through a methanol gradient. To reduce autofluorescence, brains were bleached with 5% H2O2/methanol solution, followed by rehydration. After sequential processing in permeabilization solution and blocking solution for 2 days each, the samples were incubated with primary antibody and fluorescent secondary antibody for 7 days each. Clearing was then achieved by dehydration through a methanol gradient and treatment with dichloromethane/methanol mixture. Finally, samples were immersed in dibenzyl ether (DBE) for refractive index matching. Cleared whole-brain samples from the Sham and BCAS groups at 7 days and 40 days (n = 6 per group per time point) were imaged in three dimensions using a light-sheet fluorescence microscope (ZEISS Lightsheet Z.1). Images were first processed through the ClearMap pipeline for automated cell detection, in which cells were identified based on signal intensity and voxel morphology, with additional morphological filtering to distinguish true positive signals from background noise. Preprocessed images were registered to the Allen Brain Atlas horizontal template for mouse brain, and brain regions were segmented. A two-step alignment combining rigid and non-linear transformations was performed using the Elastix toolbox to allow automated mapping and counting of positive cells within their corresponding brain region labels.55

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

The c-Fos+ cell density data for each brain region were first Z score normalized to eliminate baseline expression differences across regions, followed by weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA).17 Density data from the Sham and BCAS groups at 7 day and 40 day (n = 6 per group per time point) were incorporated into the network analysis. Pearson correlation matrices were computed for each pair of brain regions to quantify co-activation levels. To enhance the biological relevance of network modules and suppress potential spurious correlations, the correlation matrix was raised to a power to generate an adjacency matrix, with the power exponent determined via soft-thresholding analysis. Brain region topological similarity was then assessed based on the topological overlap matrix (TOM), which reflects the extent of shared connections and the strength of direct connections between regions. TOM matrices were subjected to hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree-cutting to define modules, grouping highly co-activated brain regions together.56 Module eigengenes, calculated via singular value decomposition, represented the overall activity of each module. Correlations between modules and between modules and experimental conditions were statistically assessed using module eigengenes, allowing identification of functional network reorganizations associated with chronic hypoperfusion. Module preservation analysis was employed to evaluate the stability of module connectivity and density across different time points or treatment groups. All analyses were performed in R using the WGCNA package, with network structure significance validated by permutation testing.

Open field test

The open field test is commonly used to assess locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior in mice.57 The experimental apparatus consisted of a white acrylic square arena measuring approximately 40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm. Thirty minutes prior to testing, mice were transferred to the experimental room to acclimate, under conditions of uniform lighting and minimal noise. At the start of the test, individual mice were gently placed in the lower-right corner of the arena to ensure a consistent initial position and allowed to freely explore for 5 min. The arena floor was divided into a central zone (13 cm × 13 cm) and a surrounding peripheral zone. Subsequently, the distance traveled, velocity, and time spent in the center and periphery zones were analyzed.

Novel object recognition test

The novel object recognition (NOR) test was used to assess contextual and spatial memory as well as exploratory behavior in mice.58 The apparatus consisted of a square open-field arena (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm) in a quiet, uniformly lit environment. Recognition objects included two identical plastic cubes and an opaque glass bottle, resistant to displacement and confirmed to elicit no innate preference. Mice were acclimated in the empty arena for 5 min before training. During training, the cubes were placed symmetrically, and mice were gently positioned in the lower-right corner to explore freely for 5 min. Exploration was defined as the nose being within 2 cm of an object with sniffing or forepaw contact; merely passing by was excluded. One hour later, during testing, one cube was replaced with the bottle, and mice explored for 5 min. The recognition index was defined as the ratio of time spent exploring the novel object to the total exploration time (T(novel)/[T(novel) + T(familiar)]).

Y-maze test

The Y-maze test was used to assess spatial working memory and exploratory behavior in mice.59 The experiment was conducted in a quiet, uniformly lit environment using a standard Y-shaped apparatus with three arms (A, B, and C), each measuring approximately 40 cm × 8 cm × 15 cm and positioned at 120° angles to each other. Mice were acclimated in the behavioral room for 30 min prior to testing. Each mouse was placed at the entrance of arm A for all trials. During training, a transparent barrier blocked arm C, and mice were allowed to freely explore the other two arms for 5 min. One hour later, the barrier was removed for the novel arm recognition test, and mice explored all three arms for 5 min. Exploration time and distance in arm C were recorded to calculate the recognition index.

Nest building

Prior to the experiment, mice were individually housed in standard cages (30 cm × 20 cm × 13 cm) for 3 days of habituation to ensure each mouse adapted to the independent environment. On the test day, the mice were transferred to new cages with fresh, unscented bedding, and provided with a clean, pre-weighed cotton fiber nestlet of equal amount. The mice were allowed to behave freely overnight, and the nests were evaluated the following morning. The nest from each mouse was photographed and the nests were graded using scores ranging from 1 (very poor/no nest building) to 5 (optimal nest building) with half-point scores for nests falling between categories according to a previously published scoring scheme by Deacon.60 The percentage of material utilized area was calculated as: [material area (cm2)/total cage floor area (cm2)] × 100%.

Assessment of sleep-wake patterns

To evaluate the sleep-wake patterns, we employed video-based locomotor activity tracking. The mice were subjected to continuous 24-h video recording for the analysis of inactivity/activity. Sleep was behaviorally defined as a state of continuous and complete immobility lasting for ≥40 s. This definition of sleep/wake demonstrates 88–94% agreement with simultaneous EEG/EMG recordings in C57BL/6J mice.61,62 Based on this criterion, total sleep time, sleep duration during the light and dark phases, and the number of wakefulness episodes were quantified to assess the impact of PVT inhibition on sleep-wake patterns in BCAS mice.

All behavioral tests followed standardized procedures. After each trial, the maze was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol to eliminate olfactory cues. Mouse movement trajectories and behavioral parameters were recorded in real time using a video-tracking system (EthoVision XT 17, Noldus Information Technology, The Netherlands). To ensure objectivity, all behavioral tests were conducted by investigators who were blinded to the group assignments. Furthermore, the subsequent data analysis was performed by a researcher who remained blinded to the treatment conditions until the final statistical analysis was completed.

Virus injection and chemogenetic intervention

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3–4% for induction, 1–2% for maintenance) and positioned in a stereotaxic frame (Leica Biosystems). Erythromycin eye ointment was applied to prevent corneal drying. After exposing the skull, stereotaxic coordinates relative to bregma were used to target the paraventricular thalamus (PVT; AP: −1.4 mm, ML: 0.07 mm, DV: −3.2 mm).63 Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups for viral transduction, followed by a minimum two-week recovery period to allow for stable transgene expression. The treatment regimens were administered as follows: (1) Sham (Sham+mcherry+ vehicle) and BCAS (BCAS+mcherry+vehicle)groups received bilateral PVT injections of AAV2/9-hSyn-mCherry (BC-0025, Braincase, China) and were subsequently treated with intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of vehicle (0.9% saline); (2) the Sham+mcherry+CNO group received AAV2/9-hSyn-mCherry and i.p. injections of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; 3.3 mg/kg, dissolved in 0.9% saline) to assess potential off-target effects of the ligand; and (3) the BCAS + hM4Di group received AAV2/9-hSyn-hM4Di-mCherry (BC-0235, Braincase, China) followed by i.p. injections of CNO (3.3 mg/kg). Viral vectors (titer: 2 × 1012 vg/mL, 150 nL per mouse) were injected at 60 nL/min, and the needle remained in place for 10 min before slow retraction to minimize backflow. Following the completion of all experimental procedures, viral expression and targeting were verified in every animal using fluorescence microscopy on reconstructed brain sections. Only mice with mCherry expression restricted within the anatomical boundaries of the PVT were included in the final data analysis.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

After tissue collection, mouse brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and subsequently immersed in sucrose solution until the tissue sank, achieving dehydration and cryoprotection. Samples were then processed for either standard frozen or paraffin embedding, and sectioned at 50 μm thickness. Sections were washed under running PBS to remove residual fixative, followed by hematoxylin staining for 5 min. Excess stain was rinsed off with running water, and differentiation in 1% acid alcohol was performed for a few seconds to enhance nuclear contrast, followed by washing under running water to neutralize. Sections were then stained with 0.5% eosin for 2 min and rinsed to remove background staining. Finally, stained sections were dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests (paired or unpaired), one-way or two-way repeated measures ANOVA (RM ANOVA), with Šídák’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) and R 4.4.2. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance is denoted as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
