Reproductive tract commensal bacterium Enterococcus faecium supernatant induces DNA damage associated stress responses and suppresses the growth of cervical cancer cells
Authors: Jun Yang, Min Zhou, Mengya Gao, Chuan Lin, Li-Na Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) is closely associated with the progression of cervical cancer. Increasing evidence indicates that the vaginal microecology plays an important role in HPV infection outcomes and the development of cervical lesions; however, the direct effects of specific commensal bacteria in the reproductive tract and their underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely unclear. This study aimed to systematically investigate the effects of conditioned commensal bacteria Enterococcus faecium(E. faecium) supernatant on the malignant biological behaviors of cervical cancer cells and to elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms involved.MethodsHeLa, SiHa, and C33A cervical cancer cell lines were used to evaluate the effects of E. faecium supernatant on cell proliferation, migration, cell-cycle distribution, and apoptosis. Immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess nuclear R-loop accumulation and the expression of the DNA damage marker γH2AX, thereby exploring E. faecium supernatant induced cellular damage responses. Furthermore, mRNA transcriptome sequencing was conducted in HeLa cells to identify key differentially expressed genes. Candidate genes were validated by RT-qPCR, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence, and siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments were performed to evaluate the functional roles of key genes in E. faecium supernatant mediated effects.ResultsTreatment with E. faecium supernatant significantly inhibited the proliferation and migration of cervical cancer cells, induced cell-cycle arrest, and promoted apoptosis. In HPV-positive cervical cancer cells, E. faecium supernatant treatment led to abnormal accumulation of nuclear R-loops accompanied by enhanced γH2AX signaling. Transcriptomic analysis revealed substantial alterations in the global gene expression profile following E. faecium supernatant treatment, with differentially expressed genes predominantly enriched in stress response and DNA damage related pathways. Among these, the endoplasmic reticulum stress associated gene HSPA5 was markedly upregulated. Knockdown of HSPA5 further enhanced the inhibitory effects of E. faecium supernatant on cell proliferation and its pro-apoptotic effects in cervical cancer cells.ConclusionThis study provides preliminary evidence that E. faecium supernatant inhibits the proliferation of cervical cancer cells and induces apoptosis, accompanied by nuclear R-loop accumulation and enhanced DNA damage signaling. HSPA5 may be involved in this process and may play a role in limiting the progression of cellular damage toward irreversible cell death in cervical cancer cells. Introduction

Cervical cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors among women worldwide, and its development and progression are closely associated with persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV). It is estimated that in 2022 there were approximately 151,000 new cases of cervical cancer and about 56,000 deaths in China, accounting for nearly 16% of global cervical cancer related deaths [1, 2]. Despite the widespread implementation of HPV vaccination and screening programs, the prevention and control of cervical cancer remain a major public health challenge. In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to the role of vaginal microecology in HPV infection outcomes and the progression of cervical lesions [3, 4]. Emerging evidence suggests that the vaginal microbiota not only serves as a key regulator of mucosal immune defense, but that its metabolic products can also directly interfere with tumor cell proteostasis, endoplasmic reticulum function, and DNA damage responses, thereby influencing tumor cell survival and proliferation [5, 6]. However, the specific molecular mechanisms by which distinct reproductive tract microorganisms and their metabolites participate in HPV-related cervical carcinogenesis remain insufficiently elucidated.

Within the reproductive tract microecological system, Enterococcus faecium (E. faecium), an important member of the lactic acid producing bacterial community, constitutes part of the natural vaginal defense barrier and has attracted considerable interest for its anticancer, antipathogenic, and immunomodulatory activities [7–9]. Previous studies have shown that E. faecium strains isolated from the vaginal microbiota of healthy women can significantly induce apoptosis in tumor cell lines such as AGS and HeLa cells in vitro [10]. Moreover, bacteriocins secreted by E. faecium (e.g., Enterocin 12a) have been demonstrated to selectively inhibit the growth of multiple human cancer cell lines [11], suggesting potential value in cervical cancer prevention. In the female reproductive tract, E. faecium contributes to the maintenance of an acidic vaginal environment through the production of L-lactic acid, forming a core component of the innate mucosal defense system [12]. Accumulating evidence indicates that vaginal dysbiosis is closely associated with persistent HR-HPV infection and the progression of cervical lesions [13–15], and modulation of the vaginal microbiota has emerged as a novel strategy for cervical cancer prevention and treatment [16]. Although E. faecium has already been widely used clinically as a vaginal microecological regulator, however, the precise role of E. faecium within the cervical cancer microenvironment remains largely unexplored. Recent studies have demonstrated that cellular stress responses, inflammation related signaling pathways, and non-coding RNA regulatory networks play important roles in tumorigenesis, tumor progression, and cellular damage processes. The lncRNA AGAP2-AS1 has been reported to promote the progression of inflammation related diseases through the miR-424-5p/AKT3 axis [17]. In addition, classical inflammatory stress pathways, including NF-κB and p38 MAPK signaling, have also been shown to participate in the regulation of cellular inflammatory responses and tissue injury [18]. Nevertheless, whether E. faecium can influence the biological behavior of cervical cancer cells by modulating cellular stress responses, genome stability, and DNA damage related processes remains largely unclear.

In this study, we systematically explored the effects of E. faecium supernatant treatment on the biological behavior of cervical cancer cells and the underlying molecular mechanisms. In vitro experiments demonstrated that E. faecium supernatant significantly inhibited cervical cancer cell proliferation and migration, induced cell-cycle arrest, and promoted apoptosis. At the molecular level, E. faecium supernatant treatment resulted in abnormal accumulation of nuclear R-loop structures and a marked increase in the DNA damage marker γH2AX. Integrated mRNA transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analyses further identified the DNA damage related gene HSPA5 as a key differentially expressed gene that was significantly upregulated following E. faecium supernatant treatment. Notably, siRNA-mediated knockdown of HSPA5 markedly enhanced the antiproliferative effects of E. faecium supernatant on cervical cancer cells.

These findings indicate that E. faecium supernatant promotes the accumulation of DNA damage in cervical cancer cells and exerts antitumor effects. The pronounced upregulation of the endoplasmic reticulum stress related gene HSPA5 suggests that it may function as a stress-buffering node, participating in adaptive cellular responses to DNA damage and transcriptional stress, thereby limiting the transition from cellular injury to irreversible cell death. This study provides new experimental evidence for understanding the role of microecological factors in cervical cancer development and progression, and offers insights into potential microecology based intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Cervical cancer cell culture and preparation of E. faecium supernatant

Human cervical cancer cell lines HeLa (HPV18-positive), SiHa (HPV16-positive), and C33A (HPV-negative) were obtained from the Cell Bank of Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (MeilunBio) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (MeilunBio) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

The E. faecium supernatant (CMCC 32901, provided by Xi’an Zhenghao Biotech Co., Ltd.; transported at 4 °C) had an initial pH of 3.8 at the time of production, which was adjusted to pH 6.0 prior to release. The culture was centrifuged at 6000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile membrane to obtain a sterile conditioned supernatant. The supernatant was aliquoted (1.5 mL per tube) and stored at − 80 °C. Repeated freeze–thaw cycles were avoided, and each aliquot was used only once. Before use, samples were thawed on ice and used immediately. A total of three independent batches of supernatant were used in this study, with three biological replicates performed for each batch. The control group received DMEM/F12 medium without bacterial supplementation (pH 7.4), which was stored at 4 °C and used freshly. During experiments, the conditioned supernatant was mixed with fresh culture medium at a defined ratio before being added to the cells, while the control group received an equal volume of medium. All groups were maintained under identical culture conditions.

Effects of E. faecium supernatant on cervical cancer cell proliferation, cell cycle, and apoptosis

HeLa, SiHa, and C33A cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 400–1000 cells per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After cell attachment, the experimental groups were treated with complete medium containing E. faecium supernatant at different concentrations (1 × 10⁷ or 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL), while control groups received standard complete medium. The medium was refreshed every 3 days, and cells were cultured until typical colonies (single colonies containing > 50 cells) formed in the control group. Cells were then fixed with 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed with PBS, and stained with 1 mL of 0.1% crystal violet for 10 min. After thorough washing with PBS, colonies were photographed, and colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ software.

For cell-cycle analysis, cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and treated with E. faecium supernatant (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) or control medium for 24–48 h. Cells were collected by trypsinization, washed with precooled PBS, and fixed overnight at 4 °C in precooled absolute ethanol. After washing, cells were stained with a solution containing staining buffer, RNase A (MeilunBio), and propidium iodide (PI; MeilunBio) and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. DNA content and light-scattering signals were analyzed by flow cytometry with excitation at 488 nm within 24 h. Cell cycle gating strategy: The main cell population was first selected on the FSC-A versus SSC-A plot to exclude debris and dead cells. Doublets and cell aggregates were then excluded using FSC-A/FSC-H gating, and only singlet cells were included for further analysis. PI fluorescence intensity (PI-A channel) was subsequently analyzed within the singlet population, and the percentages of cells in the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases were determined using FlowJo X 10.0.7 software.

Cell apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (MeilunBio) combined with flow cytometry. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and treated as indicated for 24–48 h. After harvesting with EDTA-free trypsin, cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in 1× binding buffer. A total of 100 µL of cell suspension was incubated with 2.5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 2.5 µL PI for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by the addition of 400 µL binding buffer for flow cytometric analysis. Annexin V⁺/PI⁻ cells were defined as early apoptotic cells, and Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ cells were defined as late apoptotic cells. Data were analyzed using FlowJo X 10.0.7r2 software.

CCK-8 assays and wound-healing assays were used to further evaluate the effects of E. faecium supernatant on cervical cancer cell proliferation and migration. After cell attachment, HeLa, SiHa, and C33A cells were treated with culture medium containing E. faecium supernatant at final concentrations of 1 × 10⁷ or 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL for 24, 48, or 72 h. Cell proliferation was assessed using a CCK-8 kit (Servicebio), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Cell migration was evaluated by wound-healing assays. Briefly, confluent monolayers of logarithmically growing cells in 6-well plates were scratched vertically using a 200 µL pipette tip, and images were captured at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. The wound closure area was quantified using ImageJ software.

Detection of R-loop accumulation and DNA damage responses induced by E. faecium supernatant treatment

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect R-loop accumulation and DNA damage responses in cervical cancer cells treated with E. faecium supernatant (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). HeLa, SiHa, and C33A cells were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 10⁵ cells per well onto sterile coverslips placed in 6-well plates. After attachment, cells were treated with E. faecium supernatant for 24–48 h, while control cells received standard medium. Cells were then fixed, permeabilized, and subjected to immunofluorescence staining. R-loops were detected using the S9.6 antibody (Kerafast), and DNA damage responses were assessed using an anti-γH2AX (Ser139) antibody (Beyotime). Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired under identical settings using a laser-scanning confocal microscope. R-loop and γH2AX levels were quantified by measuring the mean nuclear fluorescence intensity or the area of positive signals using ImageJ software.

Transcriptomic analysis of damage related differentially expressed genes in HeLa cells after E. faecium supernatant treatment

HeLa cells were subjected to mRNA transcriptome sequencing to evaluate transcriptional changes induced by E. faecium supernatant. Cells in the control group and the E. faecium supernatant treatment group (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) were cultured for 24 h, with three biological replicates per group. Total RNA was extracted and quality-controlled prior to cDNA library construction. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina high-throughput sequencing platform. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome using standard bioinformatics pipelines, and gene expression levels were quantified. Pearson correlation analysis and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed to evaluate sample correlation and biological reproducibility. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 package, with thresholds set at |log2FC | >2 and q < 0.05. Identified DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. Based on enrichment results and relevant literature, key damage related genes were selected for subsequent mechanistic studies.

RT-qPCR, western blotting, and immunofluorescence validation of differentially expressed genes

HeLa cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and cultured with or without E. faecium supernatant for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Animal RNA Extraction Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed prior to reverse transcription. Equal amounts of RNA were reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA), with reaction conditions of 37 °C for 15 min followed by 85 °C for 5 s. The resulting cDNA was used for quantitative PCR or stored at − 20 °C.

RT-qPCR was performed using Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (ABclonal) in a total reaction volume of 15 µL. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 30–40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30–34 s. Melting curve analysis was conducted to confirm amplification specificity. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCq method. The analyzed genes included HSPA5, CYP1A1, ZNF467, GCNT3, AKR1C3, CALR, IL6, H1-6, NFATC2, GRM3, HPDL, and ZNF831. Primer sequences were designed based on GenBank data(Supplementary Table S1)。.

Total protein was extracted from treated cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Servicebio), and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (CWBIO). Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred onto membranes, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against GRP78/BiP (Proteintech). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Beyotime) at room temperature for approximately 90 min. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL).

The intracellular localization and expression level of HSPA5 were further examined by immunofluorescence staining. After fixation and permeabilization, cells were incubated with an anti-HSPA5 primary antibody (Proteintech) followed by the appropriate fluorescent secondary antibody (Beyotime). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired under identical settings using fluorescence or confocal microscopy, and perinuclear fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

siRNA mediated knockdown of HSPA5 to assess its role in E. faecium supernatant mediated inhibition of HeLa cells

HeLa cells were transiently transfected with small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting HSPA5 or a corresponding negative control siRNA (GenePharma) using Lipo 6000 transfection reagent (Beyotime). After transfection, cells were cultured for 24–72 h and then treated with E. faecium supernatant (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) or control medium. Subsequently, CCK-8 assays, apoptosis analyses, and cell-cycle analyses were performed as described above. The siRNA sequences were designed and synthesized by a commercial supplier (Supplementary Table S2).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed independently at least three times. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. P values < 0.05 (*p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; **** p < 0.0001;) were considered statistically significant.

Results

E. faecium supernatant inhibits cervical cancer cell proliferation and induces cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis

Colony formation assays demonstrated that E. faecium supernatant inhibited the proliferation of cervical cancer cells in a concentration dependent manner. With increasing treatment concentrations, the number and coverage area of colonies formed by HeLa, SiHa, and C33A cells progressively decreased, with the most pronounced inhibitory effect observed at 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL (p < 0.05, Fig. 1A1-A4). Therefore, a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL E. faecium supernatant was selected for subsequent experiments.

PI staining combined with flow cytometric analysis showed that treatment with E. faecium supernatant (1 × 10^8 CFU/mL) altered the cell cycle distribution of cervical cancer cells (p < 0.05). Specifically, HeLa cells exhibited an increased proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase, which was more pronounced at 48 h than at 24 h (Fig. 1B1/B2). In contrast, SiHa cells mainly exhibited S-phase arrest (Fig. 1C1/C2), whereas C33A cells showed significant accumulation in the G2/M phase (Fig. 1D1/D2).

In addition, Annexin V-FITC/PI double-staining flow cytometry demonstrated that E. faecium supernatant induced apoptosis in all three cervical cancer cell lines in a time dependent manner. In HeLa cells, apoptosis was significantly increased after 24 h of treatment and further elevated to approximately 1.37fold of the control level at 48 h (p < 0.001; Fig. 1E1/E2). In contrast, the pro-apoptotic effects in SiHa and C33A cells were mainly observed at 48 h, with total apoptosis rates increasing to 1.24 fold (11.2 ± 0.81% vs. 9.0 ± 0.86%, p < 0.001) and 1.19 fold (10.86 ± 0.25% vs. 9.06 ± 0.05%, p < 0.001) of the respective control groups (Fig. 1F1/F2, G1/G2). Consistent with these findings, CCK-8 and wound-healing assays further confirmed that E. faecium supernatant inhibited cervical cancer cell proliferation and migration in a time and dose dependent manner (Fig. S1).

E. faecium supernatant induces R-loop accumulation and activates DNA damage responses in cervical cancer cells

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that treatment with E. faecium supernatant induced significant nuclear R-loop accumulation in HPV-positive HeLa and SiHa cells. This effect was detectable as early as 24 h and became more pronounced at 48 h (Fig. 2A–B). Concurrently, the DNA double-strand break marker γH2AX was markedly upregulated in both HeLa and SiHa cells, with stronger punctate nuclear signals observed at 48 h compared with 24 h (Fig. 2D–E). Notably, although no obvious R-loop accumulation was detected in C33A cells (Fig. 2C), E. faecium supernatant treatment still led to increased nuclear γH2AX signals (Fig. 2F), indicating activation of DNA damage responses.

E. faecium supernatant induces transcriptional reprogramming in HeLa cells

mRNA transcriptome sequencing revealed that E. faecium supernatant treatment profoundly altered the transcriptional profile of HeLa cells. A total of 637 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 239 upregulated and 398 downregulated genes (|log2FC| > 1, q < 0.05). Notably upregulated genes included SERPINB3, NOD2, TREM1, GCNT3, and UGT1A4, whereas significantly downregulated genes included PLEKHG4B, CSAG3, ELOA2, GRM3, and XCR1 (Fig. 3B). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses indicated that upregulated genes were predominantly enriched in inflammatory responses, immune regulation, and stress-related pathways, while downregulated genes were mainly associated with metabolic homeostasis, signal transduction, and maintenance of cellular structure. Among these pathways, the TNF signaling pathway showed the most significant enrichment (Fig. 3C–D). Protein–protein interaction network analysis further identified highly connected hub nodes centered on HSPA5, CALR, AKR1C3, and IL6 (Fig. 3E). Based on the PPI analysis results, in combination with GO and KEGG enrichment analyses and relevant literature evidence, DEGs were further screened according to their association with cell injury related pathways, the statistical significance of differential expression, and their representativeness within the network and pathways. Ultimately, six key DEGs closely related to cell injury associated processes were selected for subsequent analysis (Table 1).

Experimental validation of differentially expressed genes

RT-qPCR was performed to validate the expression of the 6 DEGs identified by transcriptome sequencing. The results demonstrated that all selected genes exhibited expression changes consistent with the transcriptomic data in HeLa cells treated with E. faecium supernatant (Fig. 4A). Integrating functional annotation and protein–protein interaction analyses, HSPA5 was identified as the most responsive hub gene. HSPA5 associated stress responses limit E. faecium supernatant induced cell-cycle dysregulation and cell death

To elucidate the functional role of HSPA5 in E. faecium supernatant induced stress responses in cervical cancer cells, HSPA5 expression was silenced in HeLa cells using siRNA. Compared with cells transfected with negative control (NC) siRNA, HSPA5 mRNA levels were reduced to 23.7% of control levels at 48 h after transfection (p < 0.0001; Fig. 5A). CCK-8 assays showed that HSPA5 knockdown alone suppressed HeLa cell proliferation in the absence of E. faecium supernatant treatment. Notably, following treatment with E. faecium supernatant, the antiproliferative effect of E. faecium supernatant was further amplified in HSPA5 silenced cells. Compared with the NC treatment group, cell viability in the siRNA treatment group was significantly reduced at all examined time points (Fig. 5B).

Consistent with these findings, Annexin V-FITC/PI double-staining flow cytometry demonstrated that HSPA5 knockdown markedly enhanced E. faecium supernatant induced apoptosis, resulting in a further increase in the proportion of apoptotic cells (Fig. 5C). Cell-cycle analysis further revealed that, compared with the NC treatment group, G₀/G₁ phase arrest was significantly attenuated in the siRNA treatment group (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that E. faecium supernatant exerts a significant growth inhibitory effect on cervical cancer cells and provides preliminary evidence that this process is associated with R-loop accumulation triggered DNA damage responses. Transcriptomic profiling and functional assays showed that E. faecium supernatant treatment markedly induced HSPA5 expression. Notably, knockdown of HSPA5 further enhanced the growth inhibitory effects of E. faecium supernatant on cervical cancer cells, suggesting that HSPA5 may function as a key stress buffering checkpoint that modulates the antiproliferative effects of E. faecium.

Vaginal microecology plays a pivotal role in maintaining homeostasis of the female reproductive tract. A Lactobacillus dominant microbial community is generally associated with enhanced epithelial barrier integrity and improved resistance to infection [19, 20], and may suppress the initiation and progression of cervical lesions through immunomodulation and inhibition of pro-inflammatory mediators [21]. In contrast, dysbiosis characterized by depletion of Lactobacillus and enrichment of non-Lactobacillus taxa has been consistently linked to persistent high-risk HPV infection, cervical intraepithelial neoplasia and cervical cancer progression [22]. In a 16-week follow-up study of 32 reproductive-age women, Brotman et al. reported that a vaginal microbiota dominated by Lactobacillus gasseri was associated with the fastest HPV clearance, potentially mediated by the anti-inflammatory effects of lactic acid [22, 23]. Another longitudinal cohort study further showed that women with Lactobacillus-dominant vaginal microecology exhibited a significantly higher rate of spontaneous regression of CIN2 lesions within 12 months; conversely, a Lactobacillus-depleted community with colonization by specific anaerobes (e.g., Megasphaera, Prevotella timonensis, and Gardnerella vaginalis) was significantly associated with persistent CIN2 and delayed regression [24]. Moreover, Wang et al. prepared supernatants from Lactobacillus crispatus, L. jensenii, and L. gasseri, and observed that Lactobacillus-derived factors suppressed cervical cancer cell growth by downregulating HPV E6/E7 oncogenes as well as cell-cycle–promoting factors such as CDK2 and Cyclin A, while upregulating the cell-cycle inhibitor p21 [25].

E. faecium is a lactic acid associated bacterium that can colonize the vaginal environment as a conditional commensal in healthy women. Previous studies have shown that E. faecium can produce extracellular superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, thereby inducing DNA damage in epithelial cells and exerting potential antitumor effects [26, 27]. Certain E. faecium strains have been developed and applied clinically for the modulation of gut and vaginal microecology [28]. However, potential antitumor mechanisms of E. faecium within the vaginal ecosystem remain largely unexplored. A key finding of this study is the association between E. faecium supernatant and abnormal R-loop accumulation in cervical cancer cells. R-loops, consisting of an RNA–DNA hybrid and a displaced non-template single-stranded DNA, are widely recognized as endogenous drivers of transcription replication conflicts, replication stress, and genome instability. Aberrant R-loop accumulation can induce DNA double-strand breaks and activate DNA damage response pathways, thereby triggering cell-cycle arrest or apoptosis [29, 30]. Previous studies have also shown that R-loops serve as critical regulatory elements in multiple cellular processes, including transcription termination [31, 32], regulation of gene expression [33] and DNA repair [34–37]. In addition, mounting evidence links R-loop dysregulation to human diseases such as cancer [38, 39], neurodegenerative disorders [40, 41], and autoimmune diseases [42], highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target. We observed that, following E. faecium supernatant treatment, nuclear R-loop signals were markedly increased in HeLa and SiHa cells, accompanied by elevated γH2AX expression. In contrast, no obvious change in R-loop signals was observed in C33A cells, although γH2AX levels were still elevated. These results suggest that different cervical cancer cell lines exhibit distinct nuclear responses to this treatment, the underlying mechanisms of which remain unclear. Transcriptomic analysis of HeLa cells revealed that the differentially expressed genes were mainly enriched in pathways related to stress response, metabolic regulation, and DNA damage, rather than classical inflammatory signaling pathways. Further integration of GO/KEGG enrichment analysis and PPI network analysis identified HSPA5 as a key differentially expressed gene. HSPA5 is a central molecular chaperone involved in endoplasmic reticulum stress and the unfolded protein response, playing critical roles in protein folding, maintenance endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis, and regulation of cell survival [43]. Previous studies have demonstrated that HSPA5 not only participates in adaptive responses to cellular stress, but also influences tumor cell fate by regulating proteostasis, oxidative stress, and apoptotic signaling pathways [44]. Based on our findings, we speculate that HSPA5 may function as an adaptive buffering factor during E. faecium induced cellular stress and DNA damage. Functional experiments further confirmed that siRNA-mediated knockdown of HSPA5 enhanced the inhibitory effect of E. faecium supernatant on cervical cancer cell proliferation, suggesting that HSPA5 may act as a cytoprotective molecule that limits the progression of stress-induced cellular damage toward irreversible cell death under stress conditions.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to demonstrate a direct inhibitory effect of reproductive tract commensal E. faecium supernatant on cervical cancer cells, mediated through perturbation of transcriptional stress and genome stability. Importantly, transcriptomic and functional experiments established the regulatory role of HSPA5 in the inhibitory effects of E. faecium supernatant. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the protective roles of vaginal microecology, but also provide a theoretical rationale for exploring specific microbes or their bioactive products as intervention targets to expand strategies for cervical cancer prevention and treatment.

Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this study was primarily based on in vitro cell experiments, which cannot fully recapitulate the complex in vivo environment involving vaginal microbiota interactions and host immune responses. Therefore, the current findings require further validation in animal models and clinical samples. Second, normal epithelial cells were not included as control models in this study. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the observed effects are specific to tumor cells. In addition, the effects observed in this study were primarily derived from the conditioned supernatant of E. faecium, rather than from the direct action of the bacteria themselves. The active components in the supernatant have not yet been systematically characterized, and its antitumor effects may involve the combined actions of multiple soluble factors, including metabolic products, proteins, and bacteria derived extracellular vesicles. The precise composition and biological functions of these factors remain to be further elucidated through future proteomic and metabolomic analyses. Furthermore, R-loop detection in this study mainly relied on S9.6 immunofluorescence without RNaseH treatment for specificity validation. Accordingly, the observed results should be interpreted more cautiously as changes in RNA–DNA hybrid–associated signals. Meanwhile, this study did not further investigate the involvement of ER stress/UPR related pathways or their downstream effectors, and thus the upstream mechanisms by which E. faecium induces cellular stress and DNA damage remain to be further elucidated. Finally, although altered HSPA5 expression was observed in this study, functional experiments have not yet established a direct causal relationship between HSPA5 and R-loop formation or γH2AX elevation. Based on the transcriptomic analysis, we identified significant alterations in multiple biological pathways associated with endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, all of which are closely related to DNA damage responses. These findings suggest that E. faecium supernatant may contribute to R-loop accumulation and DNA damage by disrupting cellular homeostasis and activating stress related signaling pathways. However, direct functional evidence for the involvement of key candidate genes and related regulatory pathways is still lacking. Future studies integrating gene knockout/overexpression experiments, multi-omics analyses, and investigations of DNA damage repair mechanisms are warranted to further elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying E. faecium supernatant induced R-loop and γH2AX accumulation.

Conclusion

In summary, conditioned supernatant from the reproductive tract commensal E. faecium induces abnormal accumulation of nuclear R-loops and activates DNA damage response pathways in cervical cancer cells, with HSPA5 potentially participating in this process. These findings provide new experimental evidence for understanding the interactions between vaginal microecology and cervical cancer, and lay a foundation for further mechanistic investigations and the development of microecology based intervention strategies.
