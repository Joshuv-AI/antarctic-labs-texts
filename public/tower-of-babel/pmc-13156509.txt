Delayed diagnosis of mucopolysaccharidosis type I in a patient with spinopelvic instability, short stature, and skeletal dysplasia
Authors: Oskar Windels, Mona Lindschau, Felix Boschann, Ralf Oheim, Nicole Muschol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathogenic variants in IDUA, encoding α-L-iduronidase (IDUA), cause the autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPS I). Deficiency of α-L-iduronidase leads to the accumulation of partially degraded glycosaminoglycans in various tissues, including the musculoskeletal system. Clinically, typical skeletal manifestations are dysostosis multiplex, joint contractures, and low BMD. Mucopolysaccharidosis type I represents a spectrum of disease severity. Especially in attenuated disease, establishing an accurate diagnosis and initiating disease-specific therapy, including enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) and osteological treatment, is challenging. A 33-yr-old female patient presented with a history of juvenile idiopathic arthritis, persistent spinopelvic instability, and reduced BMD. Multiple surgical attempts to stabilize the spinopelvic nonunion had failed, thus osteological co-treatment with teriparatide to promote bone healing was recommended. Despite a significant BMD increase, spinopelvic nonunion persisted. Given the combination of short stature, joint contractures, and corneal clouding, a genetic skeletal dysplasia was suspected. Exome sequencing (ES) was inconclusive, whereas short-read genome sequencing (GS) revealed the combination of a 14 kb deletion and a promotor variant in IDUA. Thus, the diagnosis of MPS I in the attenuated form was made, and ERT was initiated. This case highlights the importance of considering MPS I in patients with short stature, joint contractures, skeletal dysplasia, and low BMD. Diagnosis can be overlooked in this attenuated form, underscoring the value of genetic testing, with GS offering advantages over ES. These findings emphasize the need for early diagnosis, systematic osteological evaluation, and individualized treatment strategies in patients with skeletal dysplasia.

Introduction

Inherited metabolic bone disorders are highly variable with respect to age of onset and clinical severity, and consequently can lead to delayed or incorrect diagnosis. Nevertheless, a timely and accurate diagnosis using genetic testing is essential to enable the initiation of individualized precision medicine, including bone-specific treatment and enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), if applicable.

The autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPS I) is caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in IDUA.1 Deficiency of α-L-iduronidase leads to the accumulation of partially degraded glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) in various tissues, including musculoskeletal tissues. Here, impaired lysosomal function in chondrocytes and osteoblasts disrupts endochondral ossification and bone remodeling.2,3 This can lead to characteristic radiographic features, such as thickened diaphyses and hypoplastic epiphyses, summarized as dysostosis multiplex.4 These abnormalities contribute to skeletal dysplasia with bone deformities, thoracolumbar kyphosis, hip dysplasia, and short stature.4 In addition, some patients present with early-onset low BMD.5–7 Mucopolysaccharidosis type I is sub-classified into a severe (Hurler syndrome), an intermediate (Hurler-Scheie syndrome), and an attenuated (Scheie syndrome) phenotype. Based on these phenotypes, different treatment options are available, including ERT with laronidase and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), which is only indicated in patients with Hurler syndrome.1,8 Nonetheless, HSCT and ERT have been reported to have a limited effect on skeletal abnormalities in MPS I.8–10 With respect to BMD and bone remodeling, specific osteological treatments might be beneficial, but to date, data on this is limited. To initiate disease-specific treatments such as ERT, the correct diagnosis needs to be confirmed first, which can be particularly challenging in patients with Scheie syndrome, as symptom onset and severity are variable.1,11 Therefore, genetic diagnostic tools, which are becoming more accessible in routine clinical practice, including exome and genome sequencing (ES/GS), play an increasingly important role.

Case information

A 33-yr-old female patient was referred to our specialized osteological outpatient clinic with persistent spinopelvic instability after 8 surgical spinopelvic reconstructions had failed in different hospitals (Figure 1A-C). Despite different surgical approaches starting at the age of 13, the spinopelvic implants showed repeated loosening and breakage (Figure 1C). She reported severe functional impairment, with constant severe pain while sitting and standing. No underlying diagnosis had been established over the preceding years. Musculoskeletal symptoms, including joint pain and stiffness, as well as short stature, were already present in childhood, leading to a misdiagnosis of juvenile idiopathic arthritis at the age of 3 yr and decades of treatment with disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs and systemic corticosteroids. Additionally, the patient presented cornea dystrophy. Despite these symptoms, no further osteological or other diagnostic work-up was initiated in the past. At initial presentation to our osteological outpatient clinic, skeletal assessment showed a hip aBMD Z-score of −3.8, while LS measurement was not feasible due to implants. HR-pQCT showed a reduction of trabecular bone mass (BV/TV) primarily due to a reduction in trabecular number (Tb.N) (Figure 1D and E). Given persistently low bone formation markers and repeated failure of surgical stabilization of the spinopelvic instability, teriparatide therapy was initiated to promote bone healing together with surgical revision. Under this treatment, bone formation markers stayed within the reference range, whereas bone resorption markers remained increased. Therefore, denosumab was added, and the treatment regimen was switched to the DATA Scheme (denosumab and teriparatide administration).

Due to the combination of symptoms, including corneal dystrophy, severe skeletal dysplasia, and short stature with a height of 142 cm (height Z-score of −2.9), an inherited skeletal dysplasia was suspected, and molecular genetic testing was initiated. Unfortunately, ES did not identify pathogenic variants that could explain the clinical findings. Therefore, GS was performed, revealing biallelic variants in IDUA (NM_000203.3): First, a heterozygous 14 kb deletion comprising exon 1 and 2 (NC_000004.11:g.974949_989321del) and second, a hemizygous non-coding variant in the promotor region (c.-72G>C), thereby confirming that both variants are located in trans (Figure 1F-H). As it was unclear whether the promotor variant impacts IDUA gene expression, and as the ACMG criteria are not suited to classify non-coding variants, the variant was initially reported as a variant of uncertain significance.12 The patient was subsequently referred to the International Center for Lysosomal Disorders (ICLD) for an extensive interdisciplinary evaluation. Medical history and clinical presentation were consistent with the attenuated form of MPS I (Scheie syndrome). The previously described skeletal manifestations were the main clinical findings and included short stature, multiple joint contractures involving the hands, fingers, hips, and knees, craniocervical stenosis, carpal tunnel syndrome, equinus deformity, and pes cavus. Furthermore, lung function revealed restrictive pulmonary disease, most likely caused by thoracic stiffness, and the patient had a history of umbilical hernia. There was no cognitive impairment, cardiac involvement, hepatosplenomegaly, or hearing impairment. Finally, biochemical testing confirmed an attenuated form of MPS I with elevated urinary GAGs and reduced α-IDUA activity (Figure 1I). Based on the clinical and biochemical results, as well as the in silico prediction by the recently released PromotorAI tool (variant-score: −0.9, indicating a strong underexpression effect), the promotor variant was classified as pathogenic.13 The family history was negative, and no other family members were affected. Weekly intravenous ERT with recombinant α-iduronidase (laronidase) was initiated, and an interdisciplinary follow-up was performed (Figure 2A-C). DATA treatment led to an increase of 22% in aBMD over the course of 1 yr, but spinopelvical bone healing was not achieved due to the pronounced bone defect (Figure 2B). After initiation of ERT, the osteological treatment was subsequently terminated, as there was no clear indication for long-term antiresorptive treatment at that time, and the primary clinical problem was nonunion rather than osteoporosis and/or osteoporotic fractures. Enzyme replacement therapy was associated with reduced pain in arms and hands, improved finger mobility, decreased soft tissue firmness, stable organ size without hepatosplenomegaly prior to treatment initiation, and normalization of urinary GAG levels. However, ERT alone was insufficient to fully maintain the previously achieved gains in aBMD (Figure 2B). Therefore, denosumab, romosozumab, and zoledronic acid were subsequently administered. Over nearly 6 yr of follow-up, the patient showed a persistently elevated deoxypyridinoline/creatinine ratio, while bone formation markers remained within the reference range, with bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (bone ALP) consistently near the lower limit (Figure 2C). Baseline bone turnover markers and DXA measurements were compared with those of 3 other Scheie patients with a similar BMI undergoing laronidase treatment at baseline, as well as 3 healthy adults, who were referred to us with suspected but unconfirmed bone disease (Figure 2DandE). In the 3 other Scheie patients from our cohort, a similar dissociation between bone resorption and formation was observed in addition to the reduced BMD (Figure 2D), with elevated bone resorption markers but bone formation markers within the (lower) reference range (Figure 2E).

Discussion

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in the attenuated Scheie phenotype of MPS I, which is characterized by clinical variability in onset and severity of symptoms.1,11 In patients with unexplained skeletal dysplasia and/or short stature accompanied by additional signs, such as corneal clouding, MPS I should be suspected, and further diagnostics should be initiated. While urinary GAG analysis followed by IDUA activity assay could support the initial diagnostic workup when MPS is suspected, this case further demonstrates the diagnostic power of GS. First, smaller deletions affecting only 1 or 2 exons can be missed by structural variant callers, and non-coding but “near exome” located variants in the 5′-UTR or promotor region are most often not captured by ES. Establishing an accurate and definitive diagnosis, achieved in this case through GS, is essential to enable targeted therapy, such as ERT, and individualized osteological treatment. Given that, the clinical effect of ERT is maximized when initiated early, timely, and accurate diagnosis remains crucial to optimize outcomes.14–17

With regard to the skeletal manifestations in MPS I and their (osteological) treatment, only limited data are available, and the impact of ERT is not fully understood.8,9 However, it is recognized that in MPS I, the beneficial effects of ERT on the skeletal manifestations are limited unless treatment is initiated very early.1,8,18 Consistent with previous studies, skeletal alterations in our patient did not improve with ERT when treatment was started later.4,19 Furthermore, in this patient and in 3 other Scheie patients from our cohort, a dissociation of bone turnover markers was observed, characterized by elevated bone resorption markers while bone formation markers were within the (lower) reference range. Similar alterations in bone turnover markers have been reported by others,20 suggesting that GAG accumulation and its pathophysiological consequences have negative effects on bone metabolism and alter bone turnover. Therefore, osteological co-treatment should be considered as it may be effective to treat early-onset low BMD, as demonstrated here by the significant BMD response to DATA treatment. However, as antiresorptive treatment was discontinued due to no long-term indication, a partial BMD loss occurred despite initiation of ERT, showing that ERT alone was not sufficient to fully maintain the gained BMD. If the latter is the treatment goal, prolonged antiresorptive treatment should be considered and ended with intravenous bisphosphonates.

Moreover, this case underlines not only the relevance of genetic diagnostic tools and shows the effect of osteological treatment in MPS I, but also highlights the need to improve the interdisciplinary approach in patient care. While radiographs and orthopedic evaluations are routinely performed in the context of dysostosis multiplex and its skeletal alterations, assessments of bone density and microarchitecture, such as DXA and HR-pQCT, as well as bone turnover markers, are rarely performed. Consequently, knowledge and evidence remain limited, leading to significant gaps in osteological care for patients with MPS I. Implementing systematic osteological assessments into the standard of care for MPS I would not only benefit affected patients but also generate the data needed to systematically analyze and better understand osteological manifestations in MPS I.

In summary, in cases of unexplained skeletal dysplasia and low BMD MPS I should be considered, and further diagnostics, including genetic testing as well as urinary GAGs, should be initiated to establish a diagnosis. Moreover, osteological diagnosis should be included in routine clinical care in MPS I in order to enable osteological treatment where indicated, as there is a considerable unmet need for effective management of bone disease in these patients.
