Large dimensional Spearman’s rank correlation matrices: The central limit theorem and its applications
Hantao Chen, Cheng Wang

Abstract
This paper is concerned with Spearman’s correlation matrices under large dimensional regime, in which the data dimension diverges to infinity proportionally with the sample size. We establish the central limit theorem for the linear spectral statistics of Spearman’s correlation matrices, which extends the results of [ Ann. Statist. 43(2015) 2588–2623]. We also study the improved Spearman’s correlation matrices [ Ann. Math. Statist 19(1948) 293–325] which is a standard U-statistic of order 3. As applications, we propose three new test statistics for large dimensional independent test and numerical studies demonstrate the applicability of our proposed methods.



1
Introduction

In multivariate statistical analyses, the covariance matrix is a fundamental tool used to describe the relationships among features. Its theoretical property is crucial for understanding many statistical methods. For the classical setting where the data dimension
p
is fixed and the sample size
n
tends to infinity, these properties and their applications in various methods are summarized in textbooks, e.g.,
Anderson (2003)
.

In the last few decades, large amounts of work are focused on the large dimensional regime,

\displaystyle n\to\infty,\quad p=p(n)\to\infty,\quad p/n=y_{n}\to y\in(0,\infty).

(1)

Random matrix theory, as a powerful tool, provides insights into the behavior of large dimensional sample covariance matrices, extending the famous Wishart distribution theory. The pioneering work
Marc̆enko and Pastur (1967)
derived the limiting spectral distribution (LSD) which is called Marc̆enko-Pastur (MP) law. With the LSD, we can describe the limits of linear spectral statistics (LSS). Furthermore,
Bai and Silverstein (2004)
firstly derived the central limit theorem (CLT) of LSS. The following works include
Pan and Zhou (2008)
;
Anderson and Zeitouni (2008)
;
Lytova and Pastur (2009)
;
Pan (2014)
;
Zheng et al. (2015)
and so on. As applications of RMT on the sample covariance matrix,
Dobriban and Wager (2018)
and
Wang and Jiang (2018)
studied the prediction errors of ridge regression and regularized linear discriminant analysis;
Hastie et al. (2022)
demonstrated the double descent phenomenon in the simple linear regression;
Bai et al. (2009)
proposed a bias correction to the likelihood ratio test;
Wang et al. (2013)
and
Wang and Yao (2013)
considered the identify test and the sphericity test of covariance matrices, respectively. For more results on large dimensional covariance matrices, it is referred to
Paul and Aue (2014)
and
Yao et al. (2015)
for a comprehensive review.

Normalization is a common procedure in data analysis. By standardizing the sample covariance matrix, we obtain Pearson’s correlation matrix, a scale-invariant measure. Recent research has extensively studied Pearson’s correlation matrices.
Jiang (2004b)
first derived the limiting spectral distribution.
Bao et al. (2012)
and
Pillai and Yin (2012)
established limiting distributions for the extreme eigenvalues.
Mestre and Vallet (2017)
and
Gao et al. (2017)
developed the CLT of LSS of Pearson’s correlation matrices.
Zheng et al. (2019)
extended the CLT to general covariance structures. See also
Jiang (2019)
and
Parolya et al. (2024)
. For a large class of population distributions,
El Karoui (2009)
demonstrated that the spectral properties of Pearson’s correlation matrices resemble those of sample covariance matrices. Typically, these studies assume finite fourth moments for the features. However, for distributions with infinite fourth moments, such as heavy-tailed populations, the applicability of these results may require additional verification or may no longer hold. For instance,
Heiny and Parolya (2024)
justified the CLT of log-determinant statistics of Pearson’s correlation matrices and
Heiny and Yao (2022)
discovered a new LSD result for heavy-tailed distributions.

To address the challenges posed by heavy-tailed data, non-parametric statistics offer robust correlation measures. Among these, Spearman’s rank correlation matrix and Kendall’s rank correlation matrix are particularly popular due to their distribution-free nature, making them suitable for heavy-tailed data. Recent research has explored the properties of these rank-based correlation matrices. For example,
Leung and Drton (2018)
and
Wang et al. (2024)
studied a class of rank-based U-statistics for independence test. In the realm of random matrix theory,
Bai and Zhou (2008)
and
Wu and Wang (2022)
investigated the LSD of Spearman’s correlation matrices.
Bandeira et al. (2017)
and
Li et al. (2023)
studied the LSD of Kendall’s correlation matrices. As far as the CLT,
Bao et al. (2015)
considered asymptotic distributions of polynomial functions of Spearman’s correlation matrices and
Li et al. (2021)
studied the CLT of LSS of Kendall’s correlation matrices.

In this paper, we focus on Spearman’s correlation matrices and aim to establish a central limit theorem for general linear spectral statistics. Due to introducing ranking, the independence among samples are violated and thus, we turn to consider Gram matrices. The rescaled Gram matrix is a sample covariance matrix related to the distribution which are independent and uniformly distributed on the permutations of
\{1,\cdots,n\}
. In
Bao et al. (2015)
, they adopted the celebrated moment method and derived the CLT for polynomial functions. In this work, we follow the classical technique developed by
Bai and Silverstein (2004)
and consider the asymptotic distribution of Stieltjes transforms. Key challenges arise in computing the covariance of quadratic forms and establishing concentration inequalities for these forms. For uniform distribution on
\{1,\cdots,n\}
, it is challenging to derive the explicit covariance of quadratic forms. We derive the three leading terms which all contribute to the final CLT. More details can be found in our Lemma
A.1
and Lemma
A.2
. The obtained results are consistent with
Bao et al. (2015)
for polynomial functions and are also applicable to more general LSS such as log-determinant functions. The resulting CLT of Stieltjes transform can connect to many other covariance or correlation matrices.

In non-parametric statistics,
Hoeffding (1948)
theoretical analyzed the Spearman’s correlation from the perspective of U-statistics and proposed an improved version. Specifically, Spearman’s correlation can be expressed as a U-statistics of order
3
with an additional term. To address this,
Hoeffding (1948)
introduced an improved Spearman’s correlation which is a standard U-statistic of order
3
. Sample covariance matrices and Kendall’s correlation matrices are well-known examples of U-statistics of order
2
, and their CLTs have been extensively studied in
Pan (2014)
and
Li et al. (2021)
, respectively. To the best of our knowledge, there are no CLTs for general LSS of U-statistics of order higher than
2
. While the improved Spearman’s correlation matrix is challenging to analyze directly, we can evaluate the difference between it and the classical Spearman’s correlation matrix. This approach enables us to establish a CLT for standard U-statistics of order 3. This result is of interest for covariance/correlation matrices of U-statistic types and may contribute to the development of CLTs for LSS of general U-statistics of higher order.

As applications of such CLTs, we revisit hypothesis testing for independence. Numerous studies have proposed various test statistics based on different correlation matrices, including
Jiang (2004a)
,
Zhou (2007)
,
Gao et al. (2017)
,
Bao et al. (2015)
,
Leung and Drton (2018)
,
Bao (2019a)
,
Li et al. (2021)
. Our proposed test statistics fall into two categories: those based on Euclidean distance and those based on Stein’s loss. Through extensive numerical experiments, we demonstrate the competitive performance of our proposed methods compared to well-established approaches.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

1.

For Gram matrices, we study a novel population distribution which is uniformly distributed on the permutations of
\{1,\cdots,n\}
. Unlike the independent component model or elliptical distributions, the quadratic forms associated with this distribution exhibit a complex covariance structure. By carefully analyzing three leading terms, we derive a new central limit theorem.

2.

For Spearman’s correlation matrices, we establish a CLT of general linear spectral statistics, extending the work of
Bao et al. (2015)
which focused on polynomial functions. Our approach, based on classical random matrix techniques and the Stieltjes transform, provides a more direct connection to other classical results, shedding light on the underlying structure of Spearman’s correlation.

3.

From a U-statistic perspective, Spearman’s correlation is not a standard U-statistic.
Hoeffding (1948)
proposed an improved version which is a U-statistic of order 3. By carefully evaluating the difference between the classical and improved Spearman’s correlation matrices, we derive the explicit impact on the asymptotic mean and establish a CLT for the improved Spearman’s correlation matrix. As we know, this is the first CLT for standard U-statistic of order 3 in random matrix theory.

4.

Spearman’s correlation matrices, derived from ranking and standardizing the original data matrix, can be viewed as both sample covariance and Pearson-type correlation matrices. From a U-statistic perspective, Spearman’s correlation matrices are of order 3, while Kendall’s correlation matrices are of order 2. Thus, Spearman’s correlation matrices can be connected with many existing random matrix models and the corresponding CLT results can also be connected with well-established CLT results. The obtained results allow us to gain deeper insights into the asymptotic distribution of linear spectral statistics for various sample covariance and correlation matrices.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 introduces the necessary background knowledge and tools from random matrix theory. Section 3 presents our main results, including the CLTs for Gram matrices, Spearman’s correlation matrices, and improved Spearman’s correlation matrices. Section 4 applies our theoretical results to hypothesis testing for independence and conducts numerical experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed methods. In Section 5, we summarize our CLTs with discussions and the Appendix provides detailed proofs of our theoretical results.

2
Preliminary result in RMT

Let
{\bf H}_{n}
be any
n\times n
Hermitian matrix with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
. The empirical spectral distribution (ESD) is defined as

\displaystyle F^{{\bf H}_{n}}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(\lambda_{i}\leq x),

(2)

where
I(\cdot)
is the indicator function. If
F^{{\bf H}_{n}}
converges weakly to some limiting distribution
F
, then we call
F
the limiting spectral distribution of
{\bf H}_{n}
.

With the LSD, we can study the linear spectral statistic which is defined as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\lambda_{i})=\int f(x)dF^{{\bf H}_{n}}(x).

Here
f(\cdot)
is any bounded and continuous function. By the property of weak convergence, we can conclude

\displaystyle\int f(x)dF^{{\bf H}_{n}}(x)\to\int f(x)dF(x).

Some common functions in statistics include

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda^{k}_{i}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}({\bf H}^{k}_{n}),~k=1,2,\cdots,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}-1)^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|{\bf H}_{n}-{\bf I}_{n}\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}-\log(\lambda_{i})-1=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}({\bf H}_{n})-\frac{1}{n}\log\det({\bf H}_{n})-1,

and so on. If
{\bf H}_{n}
is a random matrix, we can further consider the central limit theorem of linear spectral statistics.

In random matrix theory, one of the most powerful tools is Stieltjes transform, which is defined as

\displaystyle m_{F}(z)=\int\frac{1}{x-z}dF(x),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

(3)

with respect to any distribution function
F
. Here
\mathbb{C}^{+}
is the upper half space of the complex plane. Similar to the characteristic function in probability, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the probability distribution and its Stieltjes transform, e.g.,

\displaystyle F_{n}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,F~\iff m_{F_{n}}(z)\to m_{F}(z),~\forall~z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

With the Stieltjes transform, by the residue theorem of complex analysis,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\lambda_{i})=\int f(x)dF^{{\bf H}_{n}}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f(z)m_{F^{{\bf H}_{n}}}(z)dz,

where
\oint_{\mathcal{C}}
is closed and taken in the positive direction, enclosing the support of
F^{{\bf H}_{n}}
. Furthermore, we can study the asymptotic distribution, e.g.,

\displaystyle\int f(x)dF^{{\bf H}_{n}}(x)-\int f(x)dF(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f(z)\left(m_{F}(z)-m_{F^{{\bf H}_{n}}}(z)\right)dz.

In summary, to find the LSD of a random matrix
{\bf H}_{n}
, we can study its Stieltjes transform

\displaystyle m_{F^{{\bf H}_{n}}}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf H}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{n}\right)^{-1}.

To explore the asymptotic distribution of the LSS, we need to find the asymptotic distribution of

\displaystyle m_{F}(z)-m_{F^{{\bf H}_{n}}}(z),

which is usually a Gaussian process. The Gaussian process further yields the asymptotically normal distribution of the LSS. It is referred to
Bai and Silverstein (2010)
for a comprehensive survey on random matrix theory.

3
Main result

For independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples
{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
we denote their rank statistics as
{\bf r}_{k}=(r_{i1},\cdots,r_{ip})^{\top},~i=1,\ldots,n
. For each feature
j\in\{1,\cdots,p\}
,
(r_{1j},\ldots,r_{nj})
are uniformly distributed on the permutations of
\{1,\cdots,n\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}r_{ij}=\frac{n+1}{2},~\mbox{var}(r_{ij})=\frac{n^{2}-1}{12}.

With the rank statistics, the Spearman’s rank correlation matrix is defined by

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}_{n}=\frac{12}{n(n^{2}-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}({\bf r}_{k}-\frac{n+1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{p})({\bf r}_{k}-\frac{n+1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{p})^{\top},

(4)

which is the Pearson’s correlation matrix based on
{\bf r}_{1},\ldots,{\bf r}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Due to ranking,
{\bf r}_{1},\ldots,{\bf r}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are not independent anymore and it is hard to tackle the Spearman’s rank correlation matrix directly. Here we turn to its Gram matrix.

3.1
Gram matrix

Standardizing rank statistics, we denote

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{12}{n^{2}-1}}\begin{pmatrix}r_{11}-\frac{n+1}{2}&\cdots&r_{1p}-\frac{n+1}{2}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
r_{n1}-\frac{n+1}{2}&\cdots&r_{np}-\frac{n+1}{2}\end{pmatrix}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf s}_{1},&\cdots,&{\bf s}_{p}\end{pmatrix}.

If the features are completely independent,
{\bf s}_{1},\ldots,{\bf s}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are i.i.d.
and have been centered, e.g.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\bf s}_{i}=\mathbf{0}_{n},~\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\mbox{cov}({\bf s}_{1})=\frac{n}{n-1}\left({\bf I}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\right).

Then, we can study the sample covariance matrix of
{\bf s}_{1},\ldots,{\bf s}_{p}
,

\displaystyle{\bf g}_{n}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}{\bf s}_{i}{\bf s}_{i}^{\top}.

(5)

This sample covariance matrix can also be regarded as the Gram matrix of the original rank statistics, that is,

\displaystyle{\bf g}_{n}=\frac{12}{p(n^{2}-1)}\left(({\bf r}_{k}-\frac{n+1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{p})^{\top}({\bf r}_{k}-\frac{n+1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{p})\right)_{n\times n}.

Thus,
{\bf g}_{n}
and
\bm{\rho}_{n}/y_{n}
share the same non-zero eigenvalues.

Denoting
m_{n}(z)
as the Stieltjes transforms of
\bm{\rho}_{n}
i.e.,

\displaystyle m_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p})^{-1},

it is proven in
Bai and Zhou (2008)
that
m_{n}(z)\to m(z)
almost surely and

\displaystyle m(z)=\frac{1-y-z+\sqrt{(1+y-z)^{2}-4y}}{2yz}.

(6)

This result shows that the LSD of
\bm{\rho}_{n}
converges weakly to the M-P law
F_{y}
almost surely, whose density function is

\displaystyle p_{y}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{(x-(1-\sqrt{y})^{2})((1+\sqrt{y})^{2}-x)}}{2\pi xy}I\left((1-\sqrt{y})^{2}<x<(1+\sqrt{y})^{2}\right),

for
y\leq 1
and has a point mass
1-1/y
at origin for
y>1
.

We further denote
s_{n}(z)
and
\underline{s}_{n}(z)
as the Stieltjes transform of
{\bf g}_{n}
and
\bm{\rho}_{n}/y_{n}
, respectively

\displaystyle s_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}({\bf g}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{n})^{-1}=y_{n}^{2}m_{n}(y_{n}z)-\frac{1-y_{n}}{z},

\displaystyle\underline{s}_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}(\frac{\bm{\rho}_{n}}{y_{n}}-z{\bf I}_{p})^{-1}=\frac{1}{y_{n}}(s_{n}(z)+\frac{1}{z})-\frac{1}{z},

and almost surely

\displaystyle s_{n}(z)\to s(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1-y_{0}-z+\sqrt{(1+y_{0}-z)^{2}-4y_{0}}}{2y_{0}z},

\displaystyle\underline{s}_{n}(z)\to\underline{s}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{-(1-y_{0}+z)+\sqrt{(1+y_{0}-z)^{2}-4y_{0}}}{2z},

where
y_{0}=1/y
. Then, the LSD of
{\bf g}_{n}
converges weakly to the M-P law
F_{y_{0}}
almost surely.

For the LSS of
{\bf g}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\int f(x)dF^{{\bf g}_{n}}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\left(\lambda_{i}({\bf g}_{n})\right),

where
f
is an analytic function and
\lambda_{1}({\bf g}_{n})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}({\bf g}_{n})
are eigenvalues of
{\bf g}_{n}
, we have almost surely

\displaystyle\int f(x)dF^{{\bf g}_{n}}(x)\to\int f(x)dF_{y_{0}}(x).

Further, we study the asymptotic distribution of the LSS. Let

\displaystyle G_{n}(x)=n\left(F^{{\bf g}_{n}}(x)-F_{n/p}(x)\right),

and we focus on

\displaystyle\int f(x)dG_{n}(x)=n\left(\int f(x)dF^{{\bf g}_{n}}(x)-\int f(x)dF_{n/p}(x)\right).

(7)

Our central limit theorem is presented as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\{X_{ij}:i=1,\ldots,n;j=1,\ldots,p\}
are doubly independent and absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
be functions on
\mathbb{R}
and analytic on an open interval containing

\displaystyle[I(y_{0}<1)(1-\sqrt{y_{0}})^{2},(1+\sqrt{y_{0}})^{2}].

(8)

Then, as
n/p\to y_{0}\in(0,\infty)
, the random vector

\displaystyle\left(\int f_{1}(x)dG_{n}(x),\cdots,\int f_{k}(x)dG_{n}(x)\right)

converges weakly to a Gaussian vector
(G_{f_{1}},\cdots,G_{f_{k}})
with the asymptotic mean

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}G_{f}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f(z)\mu(z)dz,

and the asymptotic covariance function

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}(G_{f},G_{g})=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}f(z_{1})g(z_{2})\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})dz_{1}dz_{2},

where

\displaystyle\mu(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{y_{0}\underline{s}^{3}(z)\left(1+\underline{s}(z)\right)}{\left(\left(1+\underline{s}(z)\right)^{2}-y_{0}\underline{s}^{2}(z)\right)^{2}}-\frac{2y_{0}\underline{s}^{3}(z)}{\left(\left(1+\underline{s}(z)\right)^{2}-y_{0}\underline{s}^{2}(z)\right)\left(1+\underline{s}(z)\right)}

\displaystyle+\frac{\underline{s}^{3}(z)}{\left(1+\underline{s}(z)\right)^{2}-y_{0}\underline{s}^{2}(z)},

\displaystyle\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{2\underline{s}^{\prime}(z_{1})\underline{s}^{\prime}(z_{2})}{\left(\underline{s}(z_{1})-\underline{s}(z_{2})\right)^{2}}-\frac{2}{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}}-\frac{2y_{0}\underline{s}^{\prime}(z_{1})\underline{s}^{\prime}(z_{2})}{\left(1+\underline{s}(z_{1})\right)^{2}\left(1+\underline{s}(z_{2})\right)^{2}}.

The contour
\oint_{\mathcal{C}}
is closed and taken in the positive direction, each enclosing the support (
8
).

For concrete functions such as logarithms and polynomials, we will derive CLTs in next section. In details, the integral involving
\underline{s}(z)
can be calculated explicitly for most cases. Furthermore, we present a remark on the asymptotic mean and covariance.

Remark 3.1
.

Regarding
{\bf s}_{1},\ldots,{\bf s}_{p}
as an i.i.d. sample from the population distribution
{\bf s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\bf s}=\mathbf{0}_{n},~\mbox{cov}({\bf s})=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\frac{n}{n-1}\left({\bf I}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\right),

we have

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}({\bf s}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf s},{\bf s}^{\top}{\bf B}{\bf s})=2\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}{\bf B})-\frac{6}{5}\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})-\frac{4}{5n}\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})+O\left(\|{\bf A}\|\|{\bf B}\|\right),

where
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
are non-random
n\times n
symmetric matrices. Denoting

\displaystyle\mu(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\mu_{1}(z)+\mu_{2}(z)+\mu_{3}(z),~\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\sigma_{1}(z_{1},z_{2})+\sigma_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})+\sigma_{3}(z_{1},z_{2}),

\mu_{1}(z)
and
\sigma_{1}(z_{1},z_{2})+\sigma_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})
firstly appeared in the seminal work of
Bai and Silverstein (2004)
which are due to the term
2\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}{\bf B})
.
The second term
\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})
was firstly studied by
Pan and Zhou (2008)
. The third term
\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})/n
is new which has similar effect as
\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})
under the current setting and these two terms contributes to
\mu_{2}(z)
and
\sigma_{3}(z_{1},z_{2})
together. The remaining
\mu_{3}(z)
comes from the difference between
\Sigma
and
{\bf I}_{n}
. More specifically, the Marc̆enko-Pastur equation of
F_{n/p}
is

\displaystyle s_{n}^{(0)}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{p}zs_{n}^{(0)}(z))-z}.

For
\Sigma
which has
n-1
eigenvalues equal to
n/(n-1)
and one zero eigenvalue, the corresponding Marc̆enko-Pastur equation is

\displaystyle s_{n}^{(1)}(z)=

\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{t(1-\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{p}zs_{n}^{(1)}(z))-z}dF^{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n-1}{n}\left[\frac{n}{n-1}\left(1-\frac{n}{p}-\frac{n}{p}zs\right)-z\right]^{-1}-\frac{1}{nz}.

Through careful calculation, we can conclude

\displaystyle n\left(s_{n}^{(1)}(z)-s_{n}^{(0)}(z)\right)\to\frac{\underline{s}^{3}(z)}{\left(1+\underline{s}(z)\right)^{2}-y_{0}\underline{s}^{2}(z)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\mu_{3}(z).

More details can be found in the proof, e.g., the equation (
41
).

3.2
Spearman’s rank correlation matrix

For Spearman’s rank correlation matrix
\bm{\rho}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
which has the same non-zero eigenvalues as the ones of
y_{n}{\bf g}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we have

\displaystyle\int f(x)dF^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}f(\lambda_{i}(\bm{\rho}_{n}))=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(y_{n}\lambda_{i}({\bf g}_{n}))+\frac{p-n}{p}f(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{y_{n}}\int f(y_{n}x)dF^{{\bf g}_{n}}(x)+\frac{p-n}{p}f(0).

In addition, by the property of M-P law,

\displaystyle\int f(y_{n}x)dF_{n/p}(x)=y_{n}\int f(x)dF_{y_{n}}(x)+(1-y_{n})f(0),

which yields

\displaystyle\int f(x)dF_{y_{n}}(x)=\frac{1}{y_{n}}\int f(y_{n}x)dF_{n/p}(x)+\frac{p-n}{p}f(0).

Therefore, we can study the asymptotic distribution of

\displaystyle T(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}p\left(\int f(x)dF^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}(x)-\int f(x)dF_{y_{n}}(x)\right)=\int f(y_{n}x)dG_{n}(x).

By Theorem
3.1
, we have proven the CLT of
\int f(yx)dG_{n}(x)
and the remaining is to show

\displaystyle\int f(y_{n}x)dG_{n}(x)-\int f(yx)dG_{n}(x)=o_{p}(1),

whose details can be found in the proof. Based on these observations, we state the CLT for
\bm{\rho}_{n}
in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that
\{X_{ij}:i=1,\ldots,n;j=1,\ldots,p\}
are doubly independent and absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Let
f_{1},\cdots,f_{k}
be functions analytic on an open interval containing

\displaystyle\left[I(y<1)(1-\sqrt{y})^{2},(1+\sqrt{y})^{2}\right].

(9)

Then, as
p/n\to y\in(0,\infty)
, the random vector
(T(f_{1}),\cdots,T(f_{k}))
converges weakly to a Gaussian vector
(Z_{f_{1}},\cdots,Z_{f_{k}})
with mean function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z_{f}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}f(yz)\mu(z)dz=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f(z)\frac{\mu(z/y)}{y}dz,

(10)

and covariance function

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}(Z_{f},Z_{g})=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{2}}f(yz_{1})g(yz_{2})\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})dz_{1}dz_{2},

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}f(z_{1})g(z_{2})\frac{\sigma(z_{1}/y,z_{2}/y)}{y^{2}}dz_{1}dz_{2},

(11)

where
\mu(z)
and
\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})
are defined in Theorem
3.1
, and the contour
\oint_{\mathcal{C}}
is closed and taken in the positive direction, each enclosing the support (
9
).

Remark 3.2
.

In
Bao et al. (2015)
, they derived the asymptotic distribution of
\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\rho}_{n}^{k})
for for any positive integer
k\geq 2
. Technically, they utilized Anderson and Zeitouni’s cumulant method
(
Anderson and Zeitouni, 2008
)
and proposed a two-step comparison approach to obtain the
explicit mean and covariance of CLTs. Here we adopt the classical proof technique from the seminal work of
Bai and Silverstein (2004)
. Taking
f(x)=x^{k}
, we will revisit the results of
Bao et al. (2015)
in (
16
).

Remark 3.3
.

Note that
\bm{\rho}_{n}
is a correlation matrix which means
\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\rho}_{n})=p
. Thus,
f(x)=x
is a degenerate case. Taking
f(x)=x
, we can show that the asymptotic mean and the variance are both zero.

Remark 3.4
.

For sample covariance matrices, using the sample mean or the true population mean has impacts on the final CLT and
Pan (2014)
compared the two types of sample covariance matrices. More specifically,
Zheng et al. (2015)
proposed a substitution principle which adjusted the sample size from
n
to
n-1
for the sample covariance matrix based on the sample mean. For Spearman’s rank correlation matrices, although the sample mean of the rank statistics is constant, e.g.,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf r}_{i}=\frac{n+1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{p},

it still contributes to the CLT. In details, the population covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
related the Gram matrix
{\bf g}_{n}
has one zero eigenvalue. Following
Zheng et al. (2015)
, we can also use
n-1
and consider the ratio
p/(n-1)
. Then, the additional term such as
\mu_{3}(z)
can be removed and more details can be found in Remark
3.1
.

3.3
Improved Spearman’s correlation matrix

The Spearman’s rank correlation is a classical method in non-parametric statistics.
We note that the rank statistics can be transformed into the sum of indicators, which implies

\displaystyle r_{ij}-\frac{n+1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\not=i}\mbox{sign}(X_{ij}-X_{kj}).

Here
\mbox{sign}(\cdot)
is the sign function. Denoting the sign vector

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{ij}=\mbox{sign}({\bf X}_{i}-{\bf X}_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{sign}(X_{i1}-X_{j1})\\
\vdots\\
\mbox{sign}(X_{ip}-X_{jp})\end{pmatrix},

we have

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}_{n}=\frac{3}{n(n^{2}-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j,k\not=i}{\bf A}_{ij}{\bf A}_{ik}^{\top}.

In the form of non-parametric U-statistics, it can be decomposed into two U-statistics,

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}_{n}=\frac{3}{n(n^{2}-1)}\sum_{i,j}^{*}{\bf A}_{ij}{\bf A}_{ij}^{\top}+\frac{3}{n(n^{2}-1)}\sum_{i,j,k}^{*}{\bf A}_{ij}{\bf A}_{ik}^{\top}=\frac{3}{n+1}{\bf K}_{n}+\frac{n-2}{n+1}\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n},

where

\displaystyle{\bf K}_{n}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i,j}^{*}{\bf A}_{ij}{\bf A}_{ij}^{\top}

is Kendall’s rank correlation matrix and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}=\frac{3}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{i,j,k}^{*}{\bf A}_{ij}{\bf A}_{ik}^{\top}

is the improved Spearman’s rank correlation matrix proposed by
Hoeffding (1948)
. Here
\sum^{*}
denotes summation over mutually different indices and more details can be found in
Wu and Wang (2022)
.

As can be seen, the Kendall’s correlation matrix
{\bf K}_{n}
is a U-statistic of order 2 and
Li et al. (2021)
studied the asymptotic distribution of its linear spectral statistics. The improved Spearman’s rank correlation matrix
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}
is a U-statistic of order
3
, that is difficult to analyze it directly. Based on the CLT of
\bm{\rho}_{n}
, we can study the difference between
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}
and
\bm{\rho}_{n}
which is
3({\bf K}_{n}-\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n})/(n+1)
. For LSD
(
Wu and Wang, 2022
)
, this difference can be ignored. However, for the CLT of LSS, this deviation does contribute a non-trivial term to the asymptotic distribution. Considering the centered statistic

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}p\left(\int f(x)dF^{\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}}(x)-\int f(x)dF_{y_{n}}(x)\right),

we present the main result in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.2
, as
y_{n}\to y
, the random vector
(\widetilde{T}(f_{1}),\cdots,\widetilde{T}(f_{k}))
converges weakly to a Gaussian vector
(\widetilde{Z}_{f_{1}},\cdots,\widetilde{Z}_{f_{k}})
with mean function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\widetilde{Z}_{f}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f(yz)\left(\mu(z)+\widetilde{\mu}(z)\right)dz,

(12)

and covariance function

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}(\widetilde{Z}_{f},\widetilde{Z}_{g})=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}f(yz_{1})g(yz_{2})\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})dz_{1}dz_{2},

(13)

where
\mu(z)
,
\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})
are defined in Theorem
3.1
, and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}(z)=\frac{\underline{s}^{3}(z)\left(2+\underline{s}(z)\right)}{\left(1+\underline{s}(z)\right)^{2}-\underline{s}^{2}(z)/y}.

Compared with Theorem
3.2
for
\bm{\rho}_{n}
, the asymptotic variance is the same and there is a new additional term to the asymptotic mean which is due to the difference
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-\bm{\rho}_{n}
.

4
Application

For sample correlation matrices, one important application is to test mutual independence among features. More specifically, we consider the hypotheses testing problem

\displaystyle H_{0}:{\bf R}={\bf I},

where
{\bf R}
is a population correlation matrix such as the classical Pearson’s correlation matirx, Spearman’s correlation matrix, Kendall’s correlation matrix and so on.

To evaluate
{\bf R}={\bf I}
, we have the following equivalent definitions

\displaystyle\ell_{2}~\mbox{loss:}\quad

\displaystyle\|{\bf R}-{\bf I}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})-2\mathrm{tr}({\bf R})+p=0;

Stein’s loss:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf R})-\log({\bf R})-p=0;

\displaystyle\ell_{\infty}~\mbox{loss:}\quad

\displaystyle\|{\bf R}-{\bf I}\|_{\infty}=0.

Based on an estimator
\widehat{{\bf R}}
, intuitively one can conduct test statistics

\displaystyle T_{1}(\widehat{{\bf R}})=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{{\bf R}}^{2})-2\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{{\bf R}})+p,

\displaystyle T_{2}(\widehat{{\bf R}})=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{{\bf R}})-\log(\widehat{{\bf R}})-p,

\displaystyle T_{3}(\widehat{{\bf R}})=

\displaystyle\|\widehat{{\bf R}}-{\bf I}\|_{\infty}.

For large dimensional data, it is challenging to derive the asymptotic distribution of these statistics. In the past 20 years, significant progress has been made in this field and many important methods were proposed in literature. In special,
\ell_{2}
loss and Stein’s loss can be expressed by linear spectral statistics and in RMT, they motivate the study on the LSS of these correlation matrices. We summarize the developments of testing correlation matrices in Table
1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Under the conditions of Theorem

, we have

\displaystyle\log|\bm{\rho}_{n}|+(n-p)\log(1-y_{n})+p\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,N(\mu_{\log},\sigma_{\log}^{2}),

(14)

\displaystyle\log|\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}|+(n-p)\log(1-y_{n})+p\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,N(\widetilde{\mu}_{\log},\sigma_{\log}^{2}),

(15)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\rho}_{n}^{k})-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{py_{n}^{j}}{(j+1)}\binom{k}{j}\binom{k-1}{j}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,N(\mu_{x^{k}},\sigma_{x^{k}}^{2}),

(16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}^{k})-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{py_{n}^{j}}{(j+1)}\binom{k}{j}\binom{k-1}{j}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,N(\widetilde{\mu}_{x^{k}},\sigma_{x^{k}}^{2}),

(17)

where the asymptotic means are

\displaystyle\mu_{\log}=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\log(1-y)+2y,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{\log}=

\displaystyle\mu_{\log}-\frac{y^{2}}{1-y},

\displaystyle\mu_{x^{k}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left[(1-\sqrt{y})^{2k}+(1+\sqrt{y})^{2k}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}^{2}y^{k-j}-\frac{2}{y}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(y-1)^{j}\binom{2k-j}{k-2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(y-1)^{j}\binom{2k-j-1}{k-2},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{x^{k}}=

\displaystyle\mu_{x^{k}}-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{k}{j}(y-1)^{j}\binom{2k-j-2}{k-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(y-1)^{j}\binom{2k-j}{k-1},

and the asymptotic variances are

\displaystyle\sigma_{\log}^{2}=

\displaystyle-2\log(1-y)-2y,

\displaystyle\sigma_{x^{k}}^{2}=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{k-1}\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j_{1}}\binom{k}{j_{2}}\left(y-1\right)^{j_{1}+j_{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{k-j_{1}}l\binom{2k-1-(j_{1}+l)}{k-1}\binom{2k-1-j_{2}+l}{k-1}

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{y}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{k}\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j_{1}}\binom{k}{j_{2}}\left(y-1\right)^{j_{1}+j_{2}}\binom{2k-j_{1}}{k-1}\binom{2k-j_{2}}{k-1}.

5
Discussion

For the Stieltjes transform of the considered matrix, we can get study its limit which yields the limit of LSS and the CLT which yields the CLT of LSS. We summarize the results as following for
\Im(z)>c
.

•

For the Gram matrix
{\bf g}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mbox{LSD}:~F^{{\bf g}_{n}}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,F_{1/y},~a.s.;

\displaystyle\mbox{Stieltjes transform:}~s_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}({\bf g}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{n})^{-1}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{a.s.}}}\,s(z)=m(1/y,z);

\displaystyle\mbox{CLT:}~n\left(s_{n}(z)-\mathbb{E}s_{n}(z)\right)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,\mbox{Gaussian Processes}\left(0,\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})\right),

where

\displaystyle n\mathbb{E}s_{n}(z)=n\cdot m(n/p,z)+\mu(z)+o(1).

•

For re-scaled Spearman’s rank correlation matrix
\bm{\rho}_{n}/{\bf y}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,

\displaystyle\mbox{LSD}:~F^{\bm{\rho}_{n}/{\bf y}_{n}}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,\underline{F}_{1/y},~a.s.;

\displaystyle\mbox{Stieltjes transform:}~\underline{s}_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\rho}_{n}/{\bf y}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{n})^{-1}=\frac{n}{p}\left(s_{n}(z)+\frac{1}{z}\right)-\frac{1}{z}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{a.s.}}}\,\underline{s}(z)=\frac{1}{y}\left(s(z)+\frac{1}{z}\right)-\frac{1}{z};

\displaystyle\mbox{CLT:}~p\left(\underline{s}_{n}(z)-\mathbb{E}\underline{s}_{n}(z)\right)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,\mbox{Gaussian Processes}\left(0,\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})\right),

where

\displaystyle p\mathbb{E}\underline{s}_{n}(z)=

\displaystyle n\cdot m(n/p,z)+\mu(z)+\frac{n-p}{z}+o(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\left(m(n/p,z)+(1-\frac{p}{n})\frac{1}{z}\right)+\mu(z)+o(1).

•

For Spearman’s rank correlation matrix
\bm{\rho}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}

\displaystyle\mbox{LSD}:~F^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,F_{y},~a.s.;

\displaystyle\mbox{Stieltjes transform:}~m_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{n})^{-1}=\frac{1}{y_{n}}\underline{s}_{n}(z/y_{n})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{a.s.}}}\,\frac{1}{y}\underline{s}(z/y)=m(z)=m(y,z);

\displaystyle\mbox{CLT:}~p\left(m_{n}(z)-\mathbb{E}m_{n}(z)\right)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,\mbox{Gaussian Processes}\left(0,\frac{\sigma(z_{1}/y,z_{2}/y)}{y^{2}}\right),

where

\displaystyle p\mathbb{E}m_{n}(z)=p\cdot m(p/n,z)+\frac{\mu(z/y)}{y}+o(1).

•

For improved Spearman’s rank correlation matrix
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}

\displaystyle\mbox{LSD}:~F^{\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,F_{y},~a.s.;

\displaystyle\mbox{Stieltjes transform:}~\widetilde{m}_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{n})^{-1}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{a.s.}}}\,m(z)=m(y,z);

\displaystyle\mbox{CLT:}~p\left(\widetilde{m}_{n}(z)-\mathbb{E}\widetilde{m}_{n}(z)\right)\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,\mbox{Gaussian Processes}\left(0,\frac{\sigma(z_{1}/y,z_{2}/y)}{y^{2}}\right),

where

\displaystyle p\mathbb{E}\widetilde{m}_{n}(z)=p\cdot m(p/n,z)+\frac{\mu(z/y)}{y}+\frac{\widetilde{\mu}(z/y)}{y}+o(1).

With these CLTs, we can construct hypothesis tests based on Spearman’s and improved Spearman’s correlation matrices. Our simulation studies demonstrate the practical applicability of these new test statistics.

In this work, we study the improved Pearson’s correlation which is a standard U-statistic of order 3. Studying general U-statistic typed correlation matrices could be a topic of future work. Moreover, we compare the test statistics through simulations. Investigating the asymptotic distribution of test statistics under local alternatives could be another interesting future work.

Proof

6.1
Proof sketch

The rigorous proof of Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
will be presented in this section. As can be seen, the Gram-type of Spearman’s rank correlation matrix
{\bf g}_{n}
is formulated as the sum of independent outer product matrices, which reveals similar structure with sample covariance matrix. Therefore, our methodology is originated from the proof in
Bai and Silverstein (2004)
that establish the CLT of LSS of large dimensional sample covariance matrices.

We denote
s_{n}^{(0)}(z)
and
\underline{s}_{n}^{(0)}(z)
as the Stieltjes transforms of
F_{n/p}
and
\underline{F}_{n/p}
respectively. By Cauchy’s integral formula, we have

\displaystyle\int f(x)dG_{n}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int f(z)\cdot n\left(s_{n}(z)-s_{n}^{(0)}(z)\right)dz,

(18)

where the contour of this integral is closed and enclose the extreme eigenvalues of
{\bf g}_{n}
. It is noted that if the limit superior and limit inferior of extreme eigenvalues of
{\bf g}_{n}
are contained in the support of
F_{y_{0}}
with probability
1
, then for any function
f
analytic on (
8
) and closed contour enclosing (
8
), the formula (
18
) holds for all sufficiently large
n
with probability 1. However the concerntration of extreme eigenvalues are not trivial at all, and a more stronger control is presented in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Under the same assumptions in Theorem
3.1
, for any
\eta_{l}<(1-\sqrt{y})^{2}
,
\eta_{r}>(1+\sqrt{y})^{2}
and any
m>0
,

\displaystyle P\left(\lambda_{1}(\bm{\rho}_{n})>\eta_{r}\right)=o(n^{-m}),\quad P\left(\lambda_{\min\{n,p\}}(\bm{\rho}_{n})\leq\eta_{l}\right)=o(n^{-m}).

(19)

Remark 6.1
.

The boundness of the largest eigenvalue is a direct corollary of Proposition 2.3 in
Bao (2019b)
. Due to the strong local law, the rigidity on the left edge can be derived with the same steps as right edge. Therefore, the boundness of the smallest eigenvalue can also be concluded.

As has been discussed in the above, the focus of the problem is shifted to establishing the asymptotic distribution of

\displaystyle M_{n}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}n\left(s_{n}(z)-s_{n}^{(0)}(z)\right)=p\left(\underline{s}_{n}(z)-\underline{s}_{n}^{(0)}(z)\right).

Since the CLT of LSS is obtained through a process of integration, we define a contour
\mathcal{C}
enclosing interval (
8
) as follows. Let
\eta_{l}
and
\eta_{r}
be any two numbers such that
\eqref{form:support}\subset(\eta_{l},\eta_{r})
, and choose
v_{0}>0
. The contour is described as a rectangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\{x\pm iv_{0}:x\in[\eta_{l},\eta_{r}]\}\cup\{x+iv:x\in\{\eta_{l},\eta_{r}\},v\in[-v_{0},v_{0}]\}.

For further analysis, we consider
\widehat{M}_{n}(z)
instead, a truncated version of
M_{n}(z)
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{n}(z)=\begin{cases}M_{n}(z),&z\in\mathcal{C}_{n},\\
M_{n}(x+in^{-1}\varepsilon_{n}),&x\in\{\eta_{l},\eta_{r}\}\text{ and }v\in[0,n^{-1}\varepsilon_{n}],\\
M_{n}(x-in^{-1}\varepsilon_{n}),&x\in\{\eta_{l},\eta_{r}\}\text{ and }v\in[-n^{-1}\varepsilon_{n},0],\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{x\pm iv_{0}:x\in[\eta_{l},\eta_{r}]\}\cup\{x\pm iv:x\in\{\eta_{l},\eta_{r}\},v\in[n^{-1}\varepsilon_{n},v_{0}]\}
and
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}
is a sequence decreasing to zero satisfying
\varepsilon_{n}\geq n^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. It follows that
\widehat{M}_{n}(z)
pauses at
x+in^{-1}\varepsilon_{n}
when
z
tends to the real line, which makes the imaginary gap a natural bound to control the spectral norm or Euclidean distance of Stieltjes transforms. Besides, this truncation step have no influence on the limiting behavior of (
18
) since for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathcal{C}}f(z)\left(M_{n}(z)-\widehat{M}_{n}(z)\right)dz\right|\leq

\displaystyle C\varepsilon_{n}\left(\left|(1+\sqrt{y_{0}})^{2}\vee\lambda_{1}({\bf g}_{n})-\eta_{r}\right|\right.

(20)

\displaystyle\left.+\left|I(y_{n}>1)(1-\sqrt{y_{0}})^{2}\wedge\lambda_{p}({\bf g}_{n})-\eta_{l}\right|\right),

which converges to zero. So now we have prepared all ingredients and the proof of Theorem
3.1
can be completed by the following lemma establishing the convergence of
\widehat{M}_{n}(z)
on
\mathcal{C}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Under the same assumptions in Theorem
3.1
,
\{\widehat{M}_{n}(\cdot)\}
, as a stochastic process on
\mathcal{C}
, converges weakly to a Gaussian process
M(\cdot)
with mean function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}M(z)=\mu(z),

and covariance function

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}(M(z_{1}),M(z_{2}))=\sigma(z_{1},z_{2}),

where
\mu(z)
and
\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})
are defined in Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem
3.2
is a corollary of Theorem
3.1
with the application of Cauchy’s integral formula. More specifically,
if
f(y\cdot)
is analytic on an open interval containing (
8
),
f(y_{n}\cdot)
converges to
f(y\cdot)
uniformly and by the method of Stieltjes transform,

\displaystyle T(f)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}f(y_{n}z)M_{n}(z)dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}f(y_{n}z)\widehat{M}_{n}(z)dz+o_{P}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}f(yz)\widehat{M}_{n}(z)dz+o_{P}(1),

where the contour
\mathcal{C}
encloses a neighborhoog of (
8
). The second equality holds by the same procedure of (
20
), and the last equality holds by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{\mathcal{C}}\left(f(y_{n}z)-f(yz)\right)\widehat{M}_{n}(z)dz\right|\leq

\displaystyle|\mathcal{C}|\cdot\sup_{z\in\mathcal{C}}\left|f(y_{n}z)-f(yz)\right|\cdot\sup_{z\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}\left|\widehat{M}_{n}(z)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o(1).

Therefore, by the convergence of
\widehat{M}_{n}(z)
stated in Lemma
6.2
, we obtain Theorem
3.2
.

The proof of Theorem
3.3
basically follows the same approach as Lemma
6.2
. With the help of Theorem
3.2
, we only need to figure out the difference of
F^{\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}}
and
F^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}
. By Cauchy’s integral formula,

\displaystyle p\left(\int f(x)dF^{\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}}(x)-\int f(x)dF^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}(x)\right)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int f(z)\cdot p\left(m_{F^{\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}}}(z)-m_{F^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}}(z)\right)dz,

and we are supposed to find the limit of

\displaystyle L_{n}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}p\left(m_{F^{\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}}}(z)-m_{F^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}}(z)\right).

As has been discussed before, we consider
\widehat{L}_{n}(z)
, a truncated version of
L_{n}(z)
, defined on
\mathcal{C}
by the same way of
\widehat{M}_{n}(z)
. By the rigidity of the edge of Kendall’s correlation matrix, we are able to control the extreme eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}
as in Lemma
6.1
, which implies
\int f(z)L_{n}(z)dz-\int f(z)\widehat{L}_{n}(z)dz\to 0
almost surely. The following Lemma states the convergence of
\widehat{L}_{n}(z)
, which conclude Theorem
3.3
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Under the same assumptions in Theorem
3.3
,
\{\widehat{L}_{n}(\cdot)\}
, as a stochastic process on
\mathcal{C}
, converges weakly to a non-random function

\displaystyle L(z)=\widetilde{\mu}(z/y)/y,

where
\widetilde{\mu}(z)
is defined in Theorem
3.3
.

6.2
Proof of Lemma
6.2

We decompose
\widehat{M}_{n}(z)
into two parts as

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{n}(z)=

\displaystyle n\left(s_{n}(z)-\mathbb{E}s_{n}(z)\right)+n\left(\mathbb{E}s_{n}(z)-s_{n}^{(0)}(z)\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle M_{n}^{(1)}(z)+M_{n}^{(2)}(z),

where
M_{n}^{(1)}(z)
is the random part and
M_{n}^{(2)}(z)
is the non-random part. For simplicity, denote

\displaystyle{\bf D}(z)=

\displaystyle{\bf g}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{n},\quad{\bf D}_{j}(z)={\bf g}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{n}-\frac{1}{p}{\bf s}_{j}{\bf s}_{j}^{\top},

\displaystyle\beta_{j}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{p}{\bf s}_{j}^{\top}{\bf D}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf s}_{j}},\quad\overline{\beta}_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}{\bf D}_{j}^{-1}(z)},\quad b_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}{\bf D}_{1}^{-1}(z)},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{j}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}{\bf s}_{j}^{\top}{\bf D}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf s}_{j}-\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}{\bf D}_{j}^{-1}(z),\quad\delta_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{p}{\bf s}_{j}^{\top}{\bf D}_{j}^{-2}(z){\bf s}_{j}-\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}{\bf D}_{j}^{-2}(z).

Note that
\beta_{j}(z)
,
\overline{\beta}_{j}(z)
and
b_{n}(z)
are all bounded by
\frac{|z|}{\Im(z)}
, where
\Im(\cdot)
is the imaginary part. And by some matrix identity, we have

\displaystyle{\bf D}_{j}^{-1}(z)=

\displaystyle{\bf D}^{-1}(z)+\frac{1}{p}\beta_{j}(z)^{\top}{\bf D}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf s}_{j}{\bf s}_{j}^{\top}{\bf D}_{j}^{-1}(z),

(21)

\displaystyle\underline{s}_{n}(z)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{z}\cdot\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}(z).

(22)

Step 1. Finite-dimensional weak convergence of
M_{n}^{(1)}(z)
.

Let
\mathbb{E}_{k}(\cdot)
be the conditional expectation with respect to the
\sigma
-field generated by
{\bf s}_{1},\cdots,{\bf s}_{k}
. By martingale difference decomposition,

\displaystyle M_{n}^{(1)}(z)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\mathbb{E}_{j}\mathrm{tr}{\bf D}^{-1}(z)-\mathbb{E}_{j-1}\mathrm{tr}{\bf D}^{-1}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\mathbb{E}_{j}-\mathbb{E}_{j-1}\right)\beta_{j}(z){\bf s}_{j}^{\top}{\bf D}_{j}^{-2}(z){\bf s}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\mathbb{E}_{j}-\mathbb{E}_{j-1}\right)\left(\overline{\beta}_{j}(z)-\overline{\beta}_{j}^{2}(z)\varepsilon_{j}(z)+\overline{\beta}_{j}^{2}(z)\beta_{j}(z)\varepsilon_{j}^{2}(z)\right){\bf s}_{j}^{\top}{\bf D}_{j}^{-2}(z){\bf s}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}_{j}\left(\overline{\beta}_{j}(z)\delta_{j}(z)+\overline{\beta}_{j}^{2}(z)\varepsilon_{j}(z)\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}{\bf D}_{j}^{-2}(z)\right)+o_{P}(1),

where the second-to-last equality holds by the identity
\beta_{j}(z)=\overline{\beta}_{j}(z)-\overline{\beta}_{j}^{2}(z)\varepsilon_{j}(z)+\overline{\beta}_{j}^{2}(z)\beta_{j}(z)\varepsilon_{j}^{2}(z)
, and the last equality holds by Lemma
A.2
.
The dominant term denoted by

\displaystyle Y_{j}(z)=\mathbb{E}_{j}\left(\overline{\beta}_{j}(z)\delta_{j}(z)+\overline{\beta}_{j}^{2}(z)\varepsilon_{j}(z)\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}{\bf D}_{j}^{-2}(z)\right),\quad j=1,\cdots,p,

is still a martingale difference sequence. For some fixed
r>0
, since

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}M_{n}^{(1)}(z_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}Y_{j}(z_{i})+o_{P}(1),

by martingale CLT in Lemma
A.3
, it suffices to verify

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}Y_{j}(z_{i})\right|^{4}\to 0,

(23)

and find the limit of convergence in probability of

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{j-1}Y_{j}(z_{1})Y_{j}(z_{2}).

(24)

By Lemma
A.2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|Y_{j}(z)\right|^{4}\leq C\left(\frac{|z|^{4}}{\Im^{4}(z)}\mathbb{E}|\delta_{j}(z)|^{4}+\frac{|z|^{8}}{\Im^{16}(z)}\mathbb{E}|\varepsilon_{j}(z)|^{4}\right)=o(n^{-1}),

which implies (

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\right)\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}.

We further expand
\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}-\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}.

By the rigidity of the edge of Kendall’s rank correlation matrix,
\|{\bf K}_{n}\|
is uniformly bounded almost surely, and then subsequently
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}=(n+1)\bm{\rho}_{n}/(n-2)-3{\bf K}_{n}/(n-2)
also has uniformly bounded spectral norm. So we have

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}+o_{P}(1),

and similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}+o_{P}(1),

which implies

\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{n}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}+o_{P}(1).

Denote by
{\bf A}_{i}
the conditional expectation of
{\bf A}_{ij}
given
{\bf X}_{i}
,
{\bf A}_{i}=\mathbb{E}[{\bf A}_{ij}|{\bf X}_{i}]
. By the Hoeffding’s decomposition of
{\bf A}_{ij}
illustrated in
Bandeira et al. (2017)
,
Wu and Wang (2022)
and
Li et al. (2023)
,

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{ij}={\bf A}_{i}+{\bf A}_{j}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{ij},

where
\bm{\varepsilon}_{ij}
is uncorrelated with
{\bf A}_{i}
and
{\bf A}_{j}
.
So we naturally approximate
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}
and
{\bf K}_{n}
by

\displaystyle{\bf U}_{n}=\frac{3}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf A}_{i}{\bf A}_{i}^{\top}

and

\displaystyle{\bf V}_{n}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf A}_{i}{\bf A}_{i}^{\top}+\frac{1}{3}{\bf I}_{p}

respectively.
The error of this approximation can be well controlled as follows.

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose
{\bf X}_{1},\cdots,{\bf X}_{n}
i.i.d. from a poplulation
{\bf X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, whose entries are independent and absolutely continuous respect to the Lebesgue measure. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-{\bf U}_{n}\right\|_{F}^{2}=o(p),

(50)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|{\bf K}_{n}-{\bf V}_{n}\right\|_{F}^{2}=o(p).

(51)

To replace
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}
and
{\bf K}_{n}
with
{\bf U}_{n}
and
{\bf V}_{n}
, we consider

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}-\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\left({\bf U}_{n}-\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\right)\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}.

By Cauchy’s inequality and Lemma
6.4
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\left({\bf U}_{n}-\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\right)\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(p\cdot\|{\bf K}_{n}\|\left\|\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\right\|^{2}\left\|\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\right\|\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle p^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o(p),

which conclude by Markov’s inequality that

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}+o_{P}(1).

Following similar steps above, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf K}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf V}_{n}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}+o_{P}(1).

And similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf U}_{n}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}+o_{P}(1).

Observing that

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf V}_{n}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-2}{\bf V}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf V}_{n},

so it suffices to find the limit of

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf V}_{n}

(52)

and

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}{\bf U}_{n}.

(53)

For simplicity of writing, we denote

\displaystyle{\bf t}_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{n}}{\bf A}_{i},\quad{\bf H}(z)={\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p},\quad{\bf H}_{j}(z)={\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}-{\bf t}_{j}{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}.

As for the first term,

\displaystyle\eqref{form:item_1}=\frac{2}{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}+\frac{1}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}(z).

By the leave-one-out method,

\displaystyle{\bf H}^{-1}(z)={\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z)-\frac{{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z)}{1+{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}},

and then

\displaystyle{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}=\frac{{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}}{1+{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}}.

Since that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}(z)}{1+{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z)\right|+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left|\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z)-\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}(z)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{1}{1+{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}}-\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}(z)}{\left(1+{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}(z)\right)}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z)\right|+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left|\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}_{j}^{-1}(z)-\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}(z)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}),

we have

\displaystyle\eqref{form:item_1}=\frac{2n}{n+1}\cdot\frac{\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}(z)}{1+\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}(z)}+\frac{1}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}(z)+o_{P}(1).

By Theorem 2 of
Wu and Wang (2022)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}{\bf H}^{-1}(z)\to m(z)=\frac{1-y-z+\sqrt{(1+y-z)^{2}-4y}}{2yz},\quad\text{almost surely}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\eqref{form:item_1}\to\frac{2ym(z)}{1+ym(z)}+ym(z),\quad\text{in probability}.

And similarly,

\displaystyle\eqref{form:item_2}=\frac{3}{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bf t}_{j}^{\top}{\bf H}^{-1}(z){\bf t}_{j}\to\frac{3ym(z)}{1+ym(z)},\quad\text{in probability}.

To sum up,

\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{n}(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-2}\left({\bf V}_{n}-{\bf U}_{n}\right)+o_{P}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{n+1}\frac{d}{dz}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bf U}_{n}-z{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\left({\bf V}_{n}-{\bf U}_{n}\right)+o_{P}(1)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle ym^{\prime}(z)-\frac{ym^{\prime}(z)}{\left(1+ym(z)\right)^{2}},\quad\text{in probability}.

Since

\displaystyle\underline{s}(z)=\frac{-1+y-z+\sqrt{(1+y-z)^{2}-4y}}{2z},

we have

\displaystyle y_{0}\underline{s}(y_{0}z)=

\displaystyle\frac{-1+y_{0}-y_{0}z+\sqrt{(1+y_{0}-y_{0}z)^{2}-4y_{0}}}{2y_{0}z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-y+1-z+\sqrt{(y+1-z)^{2}-4y}}{2z}=m(z).

Therefore,

\displaystyle ym^{\prime}(z)-\frac{ym^{\prime}(z)}{\left(1+ym(z)\right)^{2}}=y_{0}\underline{s}^{\prime}(y_{0}z)-\frac{y_{0}\underline{s}^{\prime}(y_{0}z)}{\left(1+\underline{s}(y_{0}z)\right)^{2}}=y_{0}\widetilde{\mu}(y_{0}z).

Step 2. Tightness of
\widehat{L}_{n}(z)
.

Similar with the step 2 of the proof of Lemma
6.2
, it suffices to verify

\displaystyle\sup_{n;z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\frac{\mathbb{E}|\widehat{L}_{n}(z_{1})-\widehat{L}_{n}(z_{2})|^{2}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}}<\infty.

Since

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}|\widehat{L}_{n}(z_{1})-\widehat{L}_{n}(z_{2})|^{2}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z_{1}{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}-z_{2}{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}-\mathrm{tr}\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z_{1}{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\left(\bm{\rho}_{n}-z_{2}{\bf I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{(\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-z_{1})(\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-z_{2})}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{i}-z_{1})(\lambda_{i}-z_{2})}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{(\lambda_{i}+\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-z_{1}-z_{2})(\lambda_{i}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{i})}{(\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-z_{1})(\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-z_{2})(\lambda_{i}-z_{1})(\lambda_{i}-z_{2})}\right|^{2},

where
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}
and
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\widetilde{\lambda}_{p}
are the eigenvalues of
\bm{\rho}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}
respectively. By Weyl’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{i}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\right|\leq\left\|\bm{\rho}_{n}-\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\right\|=\frac{3}{n+1}\left\|{\bf K}_{n}+\widetilde{\bm{\rho}}_{n}\right\|.

By the rigidity of edge of
{\bf K}_{n}
and
\bm{\rho}_{n}
and the truncation of
\widehat{L}_{n}(z)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{(\lambda_{i}+\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-z_{1}-z_{2})(\lambda_{i}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{i})}{(\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-z_{1})(\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-z_{2})(\lambda_{i}-z_{1})(\lambda_{i}-z_{2})}\right|^{2}\leq C_{1}p\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left|\lambda_{i}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\right|^{2}\leq C_{2},

uniformly in
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
.

Appendix A
Auxiliary lemmas

Lemma A.1
.

For non-random
n\times n
symmetric matrices
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\bf s}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf s}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}{\bf A};

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}({\bf s}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf s},{\bf s}^{\top}{\bf B}{\bf s})=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}{\bf B})-\frac{6}{5}\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})-\frac{4}{5n}\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})+O(1)\|{\bf A}\|\|{\bf B}\|.

Proof.

Noting

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\bf s}=\mathbf{0}_{n},~\mbox{cov}({\bf s})=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\frac{n}{n-1}\left({\bf I}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}\right),

we have
\mathbb{E}{\bf s}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf s}=\mathrm{tr}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}{\bf A}
. Furthermore, we calculate the covariance of the interaction term
\mbox{cov}(s_{i}s_{j},s_{k}s_{l})
.

By Faulhaber’s formula,

\displaystyle\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2})=\mathbb{E}(s_{1}^{2}-1)^{2}=\frac{144}{n(n-1)^{2}(n+1)^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(i-\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{4}-1=\frac{4}{5}-\frac{12}{5(n^{2}-1)}.

Noting
s_{1}+\ldots+s_{n}=0
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\mbox{cov}\left(s_{1}^{2},s_{1}(s_{1}+\ldots+s_{n})\right)=\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2})+(n-1)\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{1}s_{2}),

which yields

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{1}s_{2})=-\frac{1}{n-1}\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2}).

Similarly, since
s_{1}^{2}+\ldots+s_{n}^{2}=n
,

\displaystyle 0=\mbox{cov}\left(s_{1}^{2},s^{2}_{1}+\ldots+s^{2}_{n})\right)=\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2})+(n-1)\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s^{2}_{2}),

which yields

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s^{2}_{2})=-\frac{1}{n-1}\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2}).

By exploiting this trick, we can get

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{2}s_{3})=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{(n-1)(n-2)}\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2}),

\displaystyle\mbox{var}(s_{1}s_{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{n(n-2)}{(n-1)^{2}}-\frac{1}{n-1}\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2}),

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{1}s_{3})=

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{(n-1)^{2}}+\frac{2}{(n-1)(n-2)}\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2}),

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{3}s_{4})=

\displaystyle\frac{2n}{(n-1)^{2}(n-3)}-\frac{6}{(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)}\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2}).

Now, we are ready to derive the covariance

\displaystyle\mbox{cov}({\bf s}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf s},{\bf s}^{\top}{\bf B}{\bf s})=\mbox{cov}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}s_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}s_{i}s_{j},\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{kk}s_{k}^{2}+2\sum_{k<l}b_{kl}s_{k}s_{l}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,k}a_{ii}b_{kk}\mbox{cov}(s_{i}^{2},s_{k}^{2})+2\sum_{i,k<l}a_{ii}b_{kl}\mbox{cov}(s_{i}^{2},s_{k}s_{l})

\displaystyle+2\sum_{i<j,k}a_{ij}b_{kk}\mbox{cov}(s_{i}s_{j},s_{k}^{2})+4\sum_{i<j,k<l}a_{ij}b_{kl}\mbox{cov}(s_{i}s_{j},s_{k}s_{l})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}b_{ii}+\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s^{2}_{2})\sum_{i,k}^{*}a_{ii}b_{kk}

\displaystyle+2\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{1}s_{2})\sum_{k,l}^{*}a_{kk}b_{kl}+\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{2}s_{3})\sum_{i,k,l}^{*}a_{ii}b_{kl}

\displaystyle+2\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{1}s_{2})\sum_{i,j}^{*}a_{ij}b_{ii}+\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{2}s_{3})\sum_{i,j,k}^{*}a_{ij}b_{kk}

\displaystyle+2\mbox{var}(s_{1}s_{2})\sum_{i,j}^{*}a_{ij}b_{ij}+4\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{1}s_{3})\sum_{i,j,k}^{*}a_{ij}b_{jk}+\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{3}s_{4})\sum_{i,j,k,l}^{*}a_{ij}b_{kl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mbox{var}(s_{1}^{2})-\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s^{2}_{2})-4\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{2}s_{3})+4\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{2}s_{3})-2\mbox{var}(s_{1}s_{2})+8\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{1}s_{3})\right.

\displaystyle\left.-6\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{3}s_{4})\right)\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})+\left(\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s^{2}_{2})-2(s_{1}^{2},s_{2}s_{3})+\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{3}s_{4})\right)\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})

\displaystyle+\left(2\mbox{var}(s_{1}s_{2})-4\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{1}s_{3})+2\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{3}s_{4})\right)\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}{\bf B})

\displaystyle+\left(2\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{1}s_{2})-2\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{2}s_{3})-4\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{1}s_{3})+4\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{3}s_{4})\right)\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mbox{diag}({\bf A})

\displaystyle+\left(2\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{1}s_{2})-2\mbox{cov}(s_{1}^{2},s_{2}s_{3})-4\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{1}s_{3})+4\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{3}s_{4})\right)\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mbox{diag}({\bf B})

\displaystyle+\left(4\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{1}s_{3})-4\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{3}s_{4})\right)\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}+\mbox{cov}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{3}s_{4})\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}{\bf B})-\frac{6}{5}\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})-\frac{4}{5n}\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})+O(1)\|{\bf A}\|\|{\bf B}\|,

where we use the facts

\displaystyle\sum_{i,k}^{*}a_{ii}b_{kk}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}a_{ii}b_{jj}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}b_{ii}=\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B});

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l}^{*}a_{kk}b_{kl}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}a_{ii}b_{ij}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}b_{ii}=\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mbox{diag}({\bf A})-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B});

\displaystyle\sum_{i,k,l}^{*}a_{ii}b_{kl}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}\left(\sum_{k,l}b_{kl}+2b_{ii}-2\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{il}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{kk}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}+2\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})-2\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mbox{diag}({\bf A})-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B});

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}^{*}a_{ij}b_{ii}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mbox{diag}({\bf B})-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B});

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k}^{*}a_{ij}b_{kk}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mathbf{1}_{n}+2\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})-2\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mbox{diag}({\bf B})-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B});

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}^{*}a_{ij}b_{ij}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}b_{ij}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}b_{ii}=\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}{\bf B})-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B});

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k}^{*}a_{ij}b_{jk}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,k}^{*}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}b_{jk}-a_{ii}b_{ik}-a_{ik}b_{kk}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}{\bf B})-\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mbox{diag}({\bf A})-\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mbox{diag}({\bf B})+2\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k,l}^{*}a_{ij}b_{kl}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k}^{*}a_{ij}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}b_{kl}-b_{ki}-b_{kj}-b_{kk}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}^{*}a_{ij}\left(\sum_{k,l}b_{kl}-\sum_{l=1}^{n}b_{il}-\sum_{l=1}^{n}b_{jl}\right)-\sum_{i,j,k}^{*}a_{ij}\left(b_{ki}+b_{kj}+b_{kk}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}-2\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}+2\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mbox{diag}({\bf A})

\displaystyle-2\left(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}{\bf B})-\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mbox{diag}({\bf A})-\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mbox{diag}({\bf B})+2\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mathbf{1}_{n}+2\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})-2\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mbox{diag}({\bf B})-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}-4\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}+4\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mbox{diag}({\bf B})+4\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mbox{diag}({\bf A})

\displaystyle-\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}-\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mathbf{1}_{n}-6\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})+2\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}{\bf B})+\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})

and the bounds

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}\circ{\bf B})=O(n)\|{\bf A}\|\|{\bf B}\|,\mathrm{tr}({\bf A})\mathrm{tr}({\bf B})=O(n^{2})\|{\bf A}\|\|{\bf B}\|,\mathrm{tr}({\bf A}{\bf B})=O(n)\|{\bf A}\|\|{\bf B}\|,

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mbox{diag}({\bf A})=O(n)\|{\bf A}\|\|{\bf B}\|,\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mbox{diag}({\bf B})=O(n)\|{\bf A}\|\|{\bf B}\|,

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}=O(n^{2})\|{\bf A}\|\|{\bf B}\|,\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}{\bf A}{\bf B}\mathbf{1}_{n}=O(n)\|{\bf A}\|\|{\bf B}\|.

The proof is completed.
