Suture-Button Stabilisation for Thumb Carpometacarpal Joint Instability and Dislocation: A Retrospective Case Series
Authors: David Gao, Jai Sungaran, Luke McCarron, Brahman Sivakumar, David J. Graham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Suture-button suspension is commonly used post trapeziectomy, stabilising the first metacarpal base to its adjacent counterpart. It has more recently been used in the setting of carpometacarpal instability, avoiding the morbidity of tendon harvest and large tunnels required in traditional stabilisation techniques. Evidence for its use in this setting remains limited.Methods:A retrospective review of 13 consecutive patients, including 4 adolescents, with CMC1 instability treated with suture-button stabilisation by 2 fellowship-trained hand surgeons between 2018 and 2024 was performed. Mean follow-up was 11.8 months (3-24). Ten patients had chronic instability; 3 had acute traumatic dislocation with persistent instability. Outcomes included visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores, Kapandji scores, key pinch strength, complications, radiographic reduction, and return to work or sport.Results:Seven chronic instability patients had paired quantitative data. Mean VAS decreased by 4.43 points (P < .001), and mean Kapandji improved by 1.57 points (P = .042). The remaining 3 chronic instability patients reported improved pain, motion, and function; and achieved full opposition and stability at final follow-up. All acute dislocation patients regained stability, maintained reduction, and achieved full opposition with pain resolution by 12 weeks. Complications included 1 periprosthetic fracture, 1 corticosteroid injection for pain, and an elective button removal. All patients returned to work or sport by 12 weeks, with no fixation failures or recurrent subluxations.Conclusions:Suture-button stabilisation provided reliable restoration of stability, pain relief, and early functional recovery. It may be considered an alternative to ligament reconstruction or Kirschner wire stabilisation in select patients.

Introduction

The trapeziometacarpal or first carpometacarpal (CMC1) joint consists of a biconcavo-convex saddle joint, and permits a wide range of motion to the thumb, facilitating fine pinch, forceful grip, and overall hand function.1,2 However, the ample mobility provided by diminished skeletal congruity also confers an increased risk of instability. Accordingly, the primary stabilisers of the CMC1 are the surrounding capsuloligamentous structures, and laxity or disruption of these soft-tissue stabilisers results in pathological hypermobility and altered joint mechanics.3-5 Instability leads to pain, reduced pinch and grip strength, and progressive subluxation of the metacarpal base, commonly in the dorsoradial direction.3-5 The progressive subluxation and altered mechanics increase point loading and contact pressures, eventually contributing to joint degeneration.4,6,7 Acute instability may follow a traumatic dislocation, which commonly occurs when an axial force is imparted through a flexed thumb, resulting in dorsal displacement with injury to the dorsoradial and volar oblique ligaments and predisposing to ongoing laxity, whilst atraumatic instability may be caused by idiopathic generalised ligamentous laxity, inflammatory conditions including rheumatoid arthritis, and heritable connective tissue disorders (such as Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and Marfan syndrome).3,4,8

Management of CMC1 instability aims to restore a stable, pain-free thumb while approximating native joint contact forces. 9 Initial management of acute dislocation following closed reduction is often nonoperative in nature, comprising splinting, strengthening, and corticosteroid injections, which may result in clinically relevant improvements in both pain and hand function, but does not address the underlying ligamentous injury, potentially leading to residual gross instability.3,10-13 In these cases, as well as those with chronic atraumatic instability, surgical intervention may be indicated to restore joint degeneration, and prevent degeneration, pain, and dysfunction.3-5,13

Patients with basal thumb instability are often younger and more physically active than those with arthritis. 14 In this cohort, surgical intervention would ideally involve minimal donor-site morbidity or invasiveness to facilitate quicker rehabilitation and return to function. Stabilisation has traditionally been achieved via the Eaton-Littler ligament reconstruction, wherein a distally based slip of the flexor carpi radialis tendon is passed through an osseous tunnel in the first metacarpal and then onto itself to reconstruct the joint’s soft-tissue stabilisers.7,15 Whilst effective, the associated donor site morbidity and need for immobilisation lend to a lengthy rehabilitation and return to function.7,9 In addition, the use of autologous tissue may be ineffective in individuals with underlying ligamentous laxity or connective tissue disorders. 9

Although several alternative techniques have been described, no technique has demonstrated clear superiority.4,13 In contrast, suture-button suspensionplasty has been widely used in the treatment of basilar thumb arthritis, where the first metacarpal is suspended from the second metacarpal following a trapeziectomy. The success of suture-button stabilisation in this setting has led to consideration of its use without trapeziectomy in instability, where it would offer a less-invasive alternative when compared with traditional methods.

There is limited literature examining the role of trapezial sparing suture-button stabilisation in subluxation secondary to early basilar thumb arthritis, and none describing its use in traumatic CMC1 dislocation.9,14,16 This study aimed to evaluate the outcomes of suture-button stabilisation in the setting of CMC1 instability and traumatic dislocation.

Methods

This study was performed in a retrospective cohort fashion and conducted according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 17 Following institutional human research ethics committee approval, a chart review was conducted across 2 tertiary-care centres and 3 private hospitals in 2 states in Australia. All patients who underwent suture-button stabilisation for CMC1 joint instability or dislocation between 2018 and 2024 under the care of 2 fellowship-trained hand surgeons (DG and JS) were screened for inclusion. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were: greater than 12 years of age; had sustained subluxation or dislocation at the CMC1 joint; did not have any evidence of arthritis in this joint; and had achieved a minimum postoperative follow-up of 3 months. Patients who underwent suture-button suspension in conjunction with a trapeziectomy, or those for whom it was used in the stabilisation of partial articular fractures of the first metacarpal base (Bennett fractures), were excluded.

Patient charts were assessed for baseline demographics, as well as both patient- (PROMs) and clinician-reported outcome measures (CROMs). Patient-reported outcome measures included pain as measured by a visual analog scale (VAS), self-reported return to work and/or sport, and subjective stability. Clinician-reported outcome measures included thumb opposition as per the Kapandji score, lateral pinch strength measured using an E-LINK Jamar-style dynamometer (Biometrics, UK), and complications. Radiographic outcomes (such as alignment, maintenance of reduction, and evidence of fracture or implant-related complications) were assessed on all postoperative imaging. All physical assessments were performed by certified hand therapists at each institution.

Simple statistical analysis was performed where appropriate, with continuous variables reported descriptively as means with standard deviations. 18 Change in VAS pain and Kapandji opposition scores between preoperative and 3-month postoperative timepoints were analysed using paired t tests, and results presented as mean differences with standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals. All tests were 2-sided, with statistical significance set at P < .05.

Surgical Technique

The patient was placed supine with the arm abducted on a radiolucent arm table and an above-elbow tourniquet applied. The procedure was performed using a standardised surgical approach and either a metal-button suture device (Mini TightRope, Arthrex, USA) or an all-suture construct (MicroLink, ConMed, USA), according to implant availability and surgeon preference.

A longitudinal skin incision, approximately 2 cm in length, was fashioned along the dorsoradial aspect of the base of the first metacarpal, typically at the junction of the glabrous and nonglabrous skin just radial to the abductor pollicis longus (APL) tendon. The subcutaneous tissue was carefully dissected, with meticulous protection of branches of the superficial radial nerve. The intrinsic musculature was elevated to expose the radial base of the first metacarpal, and fluoroscopy was used to identify the intended entry site, approximately 3 mm distal to the articular surface.

A second incision of similar length was created over the dorsoulnar border of the second metacarpal base. Dissection was performed through the extensor compartment, with care to protect crossing dorsal radial sensory nerve branches. The insertion of the second dorsal interosseous was elevated to allow visualisation of the ulnar border of the metacarpal.

Once the bases of the first and second metacarpals were exposed, the carpometacarpal joint was concentrically reduced under fluoroscopic guidance. Reduction was confirmed on orthogonal views. A targeting guide may be used per surgeon preference, with the assistant surgeon maintaining reduction and holding the jig at the desired exit point on the ulnar base of the second metacarpal, volar to the midlateral axis to ensure adequate second dorsal interosseous muscular coverage to minimise dorsal irritation. The jig barrel was placed on the radial base of the first metacarpal.

A graduated guidewire was then advanced across the base of the first metacarpal into the base of the second metacarpal, taking care to avoid violating the joint surface. The suture-button construct was passed using the wire loop and deployed such that one button rested flush against the radial base of the first metacarpal and the second button against the ulnar base of the second metacarpal. The suture was then tensioned and tied while longitudinal traction and pressure over the first metacarpal base were applied to maintain reduction. Stability was confirmed with dynamic fluoroscopic assessment under axial load.

Following irrigation and haemostasis, the muscular layer was closed over the buttons where possible to minimise irritation. Appropriate skin closure was performed, the wound was dressed, and the hand was immobilised in a thumb-spica splint.

Patients underwent hand therapy review within the first postoperative week for dressing change, immobilisation in either a cast or thermoplastic splint (per surgeon preference), and initiation of range-of-motion exercises as appropriate. Patients were subsequently seen at approximately 2-weekly intervals and progressed through a structured programme of active range-of-motion exercises for the thumb and digits. At 6 weeks, clinical and radiographic review was performed, and immobilisation was weaned or discontinued according to symptoms and stability. Return to unrestricted activities of daily living was generally thereafter, with heavier lifting and sport progressing gradually as guided by symptoms and clinical assessment.

Results

A total of 13 patients underwent suture-button stabilisation for symptomatic CMC1 instability or dislocation in the period of interest between 2019 and 2024. This cohort comprised 9 male and 4 female patients, with a mean age of 29 [13-52]. Patients were followed up for a mean of 11.8 months [3-24]. Three patients suffered acute traumatic dislocations, and 10 had chronic instability. No patient had a diagnosed connective tissue disorder or ligamentous laxity.

Chronic Instability

Ten patients — 4 female and 6 male, with a mean age of 26 [13-46] – underwent suture-button stabilisation for chronic, symptomatic CMC1 instability. Intraoperative fluoroscopic stress testing confirmed dynamic instability under axial load before fixation in all cases, with restoration of joint stability confirmed following construct placement (Figure 1). All patients were nonsmokers without preoperative radiographic degenerative changes and were refractory to nonoperative measures.

Seven patients had a full set of quantitative outcomes available at baseline and 3 months postoperative (Table 1). Mean VAS pain improved from 6.57 ± 1.51 to 2.14 ± 0.90 — a mean improvement of 4.43 ± 1.62 points (95% CI 2.94-5.92, P < .001). Kapandji opposition score increased by a mean of 1.57 ± 1.62 points (95% CI 0.07-3.07, P = .042), from 6.43 ± 1.62 to 8.00 ± 0.00. Mean key pinch strength at 3 months was 4.33 ± 0.88 kg. A single patient required a corticosteroid injection for recurrent pain at 12 months, and another underwent elective removal of a metallic button (Mini TightRope) at 11 months due to thenar-side prominence, with no loss of stability.

Clinical documentation for the 3 remaining patients, though limited, mirrored the functional improvements of the primary cohort. Patient 8, a 13-year-old female, demonstrated significant improvement, progressing from a preoperative patient-rated wrist evaluation score of 46.5 and Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH) score of 35 to full, pain-free motion by 9 weeks post stabilisation, with a Kapandji score of 10. Patient 9, a 15-year-old female, achieved complete pain resolution by 6 weeks and reached a Kapandji score of 10 by 6 months. However, patient 10, a 16-year-old male, did sustain a second metacarpal periprosthetic fracture at 8 weeks following a premature return to tennis. This was managed nonoperatively with immobilisation and graded rehabilitation. Radiographs demonstrated progressive union, and by 16 weeks post stabilisation, the patient had regained range of motion with a Kapandji score of 10 and no recurrent instability or pain.

At final follow-up, all 10 patients demonstrated a subjectively and clinically stable CMC1 with no recurrent subluxation or dislocation, and had resumed school, work, or recreational activities between 6 and 12 weeks postoperatively, depending on functional demands.

Acute Instability

Three male patients (age range 26-52 years) underwent suture-button stabilisation for persistent instability following acute traumatic CMC1 dislocations (Figure 2). All were initially treated with closed reduction and immobilisation, but developed recurrent subluxation with pain and clicking, prompting surgical stabilisation.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that suture-button stabilisation is a viable option for managing both acute and chronic instability of the CMC1. It shows that the technique consistently restores clinical stability, achieves significant pain reduction, and facilitates an early return to functional activity without the need for more invasive stabilisation requiring tendon harvest.

The present findings align with the limited literature on suture-button CMC1 stabilisation performed without trapeziectomy. Kask 16 first described a similar technique for “prearthritic” CMC1 laxity in 3 patients, reporting marked improvement in DASH scores with no complications. Similarly, Gajewski and Jones reported on 2 patients with hyperlaxity-associated instability who achieved stable alignment, full opposition, and return to full activities following stabilisation. 9 Shoji documented the largest series to date, reporting significant improvements in QuickDASH and VAS pain in 11 patients treated for painful CMC1 synovitis or early arthritis at a mean follow-up of 24 months. 14

Complications across these studies have been infrequent. Shoji noted a second-metacarpal periprosthetic fracture sustained during weightlifting, which healed with nonoperative management. 14 We observed a similar fracture at 8 weeks following premature return to tennis that likewise united without compromising stability or function. This suggests that while second-metacarpal fracture is a recognised risk, outcomes are favourable when identified and managed appropriately. We also had 2 minor late events in the chronic subgroup: an episode of recurrent pain managed with corticosteroid injection, and elective removal of a prominent metal-button, highlighting the importance of detailed preoperative counselling and meticulous soft-tissue coverage over residual hardware. All-suture constructs, such as the MicroLink (ConMed), may be preferred over metal-button devices to reduce the risk of soft-tissue irritation and the need for future hardware removal. Importantly, these events did not compromise the maintenance of reduction or clinical stability.

The suture-button system leverages biomechanical principles aligned with current understandings of CMC1 instability. With the joint inherently unstable due to limited osseous restraint, disruption to the soft-tissue stabilisers permits subluxation, often in the dorsoradial direction due to the pull of the APL in this direction.19,20 By linking the radial base of the first metacarpal to the ulnar base of the second, the device provides a tension-sharing effect that counteracts the radial vector. In addition, it supports the compressive action of the first dorsal interossei, which serves as a key dynamic stabiliser to counteract dorsoradial translation and generate distal elevation of the metacarpal base.19,21

Common therapeutic options for chronic CMC1 instability range from initial conservative management with splinting and anti-inflammatory medication, 4 to operative techniques including the Eaton-Littler ligament reconstruction, 22 dorsoradial capsulodesis, 23 and thumb metacarpal osteotomy. 24 In selected cases, CMC1 arthrodesis may be used at the expense of motion. In the setting of acute traumatic dislocation, closed reduction with percutaneous Kirschner wire fixation can be used – however, the limited evidence reports mixed outcomes, with patients often requiring subsequent ligament reconstruction or arthrodesis.25,26 The results of our study suggest that suture-button stabilisation is a less-invasive and joint-preserving alternative to other options while producing similar outcomes, with comparable early stability and consistent pain relief.

A key advantage of suture-button stabilisation over ligament reconstruction is that stability is provided immediately by the implant, permitting early active mobilisation rather than the typical 4-week immobilisation period required for healing of reconstructed soft tissues. Earlier motion may shorten the overall rehabilitative journey and facilitate earlier return to work and activities of daily living. In addition, the technique requires only small incisions and avoids tendon harvest, eliminating both donor-site morbidity and reliance on autologous tendon quality, which may be relevant in patients with underlying tissue laxity. This approach does not preclude concurrent or future dorsoradial ligament repair should it be deemed necessary.27,28 In our series, however, this was unnecessary, with suture-button fixation alone providing adequate stability.

There were several limitations to this study. The sample size is small, and the retrospective, uncontrolled design limits the interpretation of cause and effect. Follow-up was relatively short, precluding meaningful analysis of long-term outcomes such as progression of arthritis or late construct failure. There was heterogeneity in outcomes reporting, with missing baseline data in some patients potentially introducing measurement bias. However, this is a consecutive series with a consistent surgical technique for clearly defined indications, which reduces selection bias.

Conclusions

This study shows promising early-to-medium-term clinician- and patient-reported outcomes following suture-button stabilisation for both acute and chronic CMC1 joint instability. Future research should include prospective evaluation in larger cohorts with direct comparison to established procedures such as capsuloligamentous reconstruction. Long-term clinical and radiographic follow-up is required to clarify whether suture-button stabilisation alters the natural history of degenerative change or simply provides durable symptom control. Formal health-economic analyses, incorporating implant costs against potential gains from shorter immobilisation and earlier return to work and sport, are also warranted.
