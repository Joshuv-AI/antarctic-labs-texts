Going deep and going wide: Counting logic and homomorphism indistinguishability over graphs of bounded treedepth and treewidth This work extends articles [ 17 , 6 ] which were presented at CSL and MFCS 2024. It is also part of second author’s PhD thesis [ 16 ] .
Isolde Adler, Eva Fluck, Tim Seppelt, Gian Luca Spitzer

Abstract
We study the expressive power of first-order logic with counting quantifiers, especially the k k -variable and quantifier-rank- q q fragment 𝖢 q k \mathsf{C}^{k}_{q} , using homomorphism indistinguishability. Recently, Dawar, Jakl, and Reggio (2021) proved that two graphs satisfy the same 𝖢 q k \mathsf{C}^{k}_{q} -sentences if and only if they are homomorphism indistinguishable over the class 𝒯 q k \mathcal{T}^{k}_{q} of graphs admitting a k k -pebble forest cover of depth q q . After reproving this result using elementary means, we provide a graph-theoretic analysis of the graph class 𝒯 q k \mathcal{T}^{k}_{q} . This allows us to separate 𝒯 q k \mathcal{T}^{k}_{q} from the intersection 𝒯 ​ 𝒲 k − 1 ∩ 𝒯 ​ 𝒟 q \mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q} , provided that q q is sufficiently larger than k k . Here 𝒯 ​ 𝒲 k − 1 \mathcal{TW}_{k-1} is the class of all graphs of treewidth at most k − 1 k-1 and 𝒯 ​ 𝒟 q \mathcal{TD}_{q} is the class of all graphs of treedepth at most q q . We are able to lift this separation to a (semantic) separation of the respective homomorphism indistinguishability relations ≡ 𝒯 q k \equiv_{\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}} and ≡ 𝒯 ​ 𝒲 k − 1 ∩ 𝒯 ​ 𝒟 q \equiv_{\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}} . We do this by showing that the classes 𝒯 ​ 𝒟 q \mathcal{TD}_{q} and 𝒯 q k \mathcal{T}_{q}^{k} are homomorphism distinguishing closed, as conjectured by Roberson (2022). In order to prove Roberson’s conjecture for 𝒯 q k \mathcal{T}_{q}^{k} we characterise 𝒯 q k \mathcal{T}_{q}^{k} in terms of a monotone Cops-and-Robber game. The crux is to prove that if Cop has a winning strategy then Cop also has a winning strategy that is monotone. To that end, we show how to transform Cops’ winning strategy into a pre-tree-decomposition, which is inspired by decompositions of matroids, and then applying an intricate breadth-first ‘cleaning up’ procedure along the pre-tree-decomposition (which may temporarily lose the property of representing a strategy), in order to achieve monotonicity while controlling the number of rounds simultaneously across all branches of the decomposition via a vertex exchange argument.

Homomorphism indistinguishability is an emerging framework for measuring the expressivity of equivalence relations comparing graphs, cf. the monograph

Fragments of counting logic and graph decompositions.

Equivalence with respect to

Recently, Dawar, Jakl, and Reggio

As a first step, we reprove their result using elementary techniques inspired by Dvořák

]

and graphs from which homomorphisms are counted in an inductive fashion. By carefully imposing structural constraints, we are able to extend the original correspondence from

and graphs of treewidth at most

to

Guarded counting logic plays a crucial role in the theory of properties of higher arity expressible by graph neural networks

Towards this goal we introduce a new graph class called

Theorem 1.1
.

Two graphs are equivalent over the
k
-variable and quantifier-depth-

Separating
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
and
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
.

The main contribution of this work, however, concerns the relationship between the graph classes
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
and the class
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
of graphs which have treewidth at most
k-1

and
treedepth at most
q
.
Given the results of
[
15
,

is characterised by homomorphism indistinguishability with respect to

. The central result of this paper asserts that this intuition is wrong.

As a fist step towards this, we prove, building on

\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
and
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
are distinct if

is sufficiently larger than

In other words, the existence of both a tree-decomposition of small width and the existence of a forest cover of small depth does not guarantee the existence of a decomposition which has both small width and depth simultaneously.

Theorem 1.2
.

For

sufficiently larger than
k
, it holds that
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}\subsetneq\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}

However, this, let us say syntactical, separation of the graph classes

That distinct graph classes induce—under certain mild assumptions—distinct homomorphism indistinguishability relations was recently conjectured by Roberson

His conjecture asserts that every graph class which is closed under taking minors and disjoint unions is homomorphism distinguishing closed. Here, a graph class
\mathcal{F}

Since

,
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}
, and

are closed under disjoint unions and minors, the confirmation of Roberson’s conjecture would readily imply the semantic counterpart of

4.22

[
43
]
,
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}

[
36
]
, graphs of degree at most two
[
43
]
, paths
[
43
]
,

-minor-free graphs
[

]
,
and for graph classes which are essentially finite

]
, cf.
[

.
We add to this short list of examples:

Theorem 1.3
.

For
q\geq 1
, the class
\mathcal{TD}_{q}
is homomorphism distinguishing closed.

Hence, as the intersection of homomorphism distinguishing closed graph classes
[
43
]
,
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
is homomorphism distinguishing closed as well.
Finally, we show that
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
is also homomorphism distinguishing closed.

Theorem 1.4
.

For
k,q\geq 1
, the class
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
is homomorphism distinguishing closed.

Thereby, we lift the syntactic separation from
Theorem

1.2
to a separation of the homomorphism indistinguishability relations
\equiv_{\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}}
and
\equiv_{\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}}
for all

.

Corollary 1.5
.

For
q
sufficiently larger than
k
,
\equiv_{\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}}
is strictly coarser than

.

The proofs of
Theorems

1.3
and
1.4
follow a strategy laid out by Neuen
[
36
]
.
Given a graph
G\not\in\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}
, he shows that the Cai–Fürer–Immerman graphs
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
of

are homomorphism indistinguishable over
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}
and admit a different number of homomorphisms from

.
CFI graphs
[
11
,
43
]
are highly similar graphs constructed from

by replacing edges and vertices by suitable gadgets.
The difference between
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
is a
‘twist’
located at the gadget corresponding to some vertex of
G
.
In order to show that
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
are equivalent in some counting logic fragments,
one must argue that the Duplicator player has a winning strategy in a suitable model comparison game played on
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
.
This strategy essentially amounts to Duplicator hiding the twist from its opponent who tries to locate it.
Thus, the model comparison game on
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
reduces to a graph searching game played on the graph
G
.

Monotonicity of Cops-and-Robber games.

Both treewidth and treedepth can be characterised using
graph searching games
as introduced by Parsons and Petrov
in
[
39
,
40
,
41
]
.
In these games a fugitive moving on a graph tries to evade capture by a set of searchers.
Different rules on the movement of both the searchers and the fugitive lead to characterizations of different parameters in directed and undirected graphs
[
8
,
9
,
18
,
32
,
34
,
29
,
37
,
47
]
.
For treewidth this is the
Cops-and-Robber game
as introduced in
[
8
,
47
]
.
We introduce a suitable variant of this game and we use it to characterise the graph class
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
.
The equivalence of the first and last assertion of
Theorem

1.6
is what allows us to prove
Theorem

1.4
.

Theorem 1.6
.

For any graph
G
and any
k,q\in\mathbb{N}
, the following are equivalent:

1.

There exists a
k
-pebble forest cover of depth
\leq q
of
G
, i.e.
G\in\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
.

2.

There exists a tree-decomposition of
G
with width
\leq k-1
and depth
\leq q
.

3.

Cops wins the monotone
k
-cops
q
-round Cops-and-Robber game on
G
.

4.

Cops wins the
k
-cops
q
-round Cops-and-Robber game on
G
.

As it is the case for treewidth,
a tree-decomposition straightforwardly gives rise to a winning strategy for Cops.
The challenge is to prove the converse direction, i.e. that a winning strategy for Cops gives rise to a tree-decomposition.

To that end, it is crucial to show that the Cops-and-Robber game can, without loss of generality, be restricted to its
monotone
version,
that is, the Cops always have a winning strategy in which a previously cleared area never needs to be searched again.
The monotonicity of a graph searching game is a non-trivial property.
There are games where the two variants are equivalent, such as the games corresponding to treewidth
[
47
]
and tree depth
[
20
]
, as well as games where they are not, such as games corresponding to directed treewidth
[
30
]
or hypertreewidth
[
4
,
21
]
.
For treewidth the proof of monotonicity in
[
47
]
builds on the dual concept of brambles.
In
[
34
]
the authors give a proof of this monotonicity that does not rely on any dual object,
[
5
]
gives a similar proof for a more general variant of the Cops-and-Robber game.
Building on these proof ideas we can show that the game variant that characterises the graph class
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
is monotone.

In order to do that, we introduce the notion of
pre-tree-decomposition
, based on tree-decompositions of matroids
[
27
,
28
]
.
To characterise treewidth using the matroid decomposition, one equips a tree with a bijection from its leafs to the edges of the graph.
Then every node of the tree induces a partition of the edges and the width of such a decomposition is the maximum size of the boundary of such a partition minus one.
We show how one can construct such a pre-tree-decomposition from a winning strategy of Cop and give a top-down construction that turns such a pre-tree-decomposition into a tree-decomposition.

Outline.

We start in
Section

3
with three different decompositions and prove that they all yield the same graph class

.
In
Section

4
we introduce a variant of the Cops-and-Robber game and show that it exactly captures

This includes a construction that turns a possibly non-monotone strategy of Cop into a tree-decomposition.
We conclude the section with a proof that the graph classes

\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}

We then turn to the homomorphism indistinguishability relation induced by

We give a new proof that this relation is exactly

-equivalence.
We use the same proof technique to describe

Lastly in
Section

and
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}

We do so by proving that the graph classes
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
and
\mathcal{TD}_{q}
are homomorphism distinguishing closed.

2.2.3
Cops-and-Robber

Both treewidth and treedepth enjoy characterisations in terms of node searching games, the so called
Cops-and-Robber games
.
The general Cops-and-Robber game is played on a graph
G
by two players: Cop, controlling a number of cops; and Robber, controlling a single robber.
Cop and the Robber are positioned on vertices of
G
.
The goal of Cop is to place a cop on Robber’s position, while Robber tries to avoid capture by moving along paths free from cops.
The players play in rounds where first Cop announces the next position(s) of the cops (with possible restriction on how many cops may be moved and where they may be positioned) and then Robber moves the robber along some path avoiding all vertices where before and after his move there is a cop.
Treewidth can be characterised by the minimum number of cops needed to capture the robber where neither the movement of Cop nor Robber is restricted (see e.g.
[
47
]
).
A well-known characterization of treedepth is the minimum number of cops needed in a stationary game where a cop can not be moved after it is positioned on the graph (see e.g.
[
20
]
).
It is equivalent to count the number of vertices that a cop is placed on during a game.
Therefore we use the unified definition of
q
-rounds
k
-Cops-and-Robber game

\CR^{k}_{q}(G)
, where
G
is a non-empty graph and
k,q\geq 1
.
Cop positions are sets
X\in\binom{V(G^{\prime})}{\leq k}
, Robber positions are vertices
v\in G
.
We define a function
\escape\colon\binom{V(G^{\prime})}{\leq k}\times V(G)\rightarrow 2^{V(G)}
as follows

\escape(X,v)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\{v\}&\text{if }v\in X,\\
V(C)&\begin{split}&\text{otherwise, with }C\text{ connected component }\\
&\text{of }G\setminus X\text{ such that }v\in V(C).\end{split}\end{cases}

We say
\escape(X,v)
is the
escape space
of Robber.
We write
(X,v)
or
(X,\escape(X,v))
for the positions of the game.
The game is initialized with Cop on the empty set and Robber on a vertex of his choice, that is the initial position is
(\emptyset,V(C))
, for some connected component
C
of
G
.
In round

to

if

.
Robber can move to a new position

Cops wins if at any point the play reaches a position

with

Robber wins if Cop did not win after

If Cop plays in such a way that

in every move, we call the game monotone and write

.

In some applications we need a slightly different Cops-and-Robber game, a game where Robber can hide in an edge, rather than a vertex.
In this game Robber is caught if he hides in an edge where both endpoints are occupied by a cop and Cops wins immediately if the graph contains no edges.

\escapeE(X,uv)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\{uv\}&\text{if }u,v\in X,\\
\{xy\in E(G)\mid x\in V(C)\}&\begin{split}&\text{otherwise, with }C\text{ connected component }\\
&\text{of }G\setminus X\text{ such that }\{v,u\}\cap V(C)\neq\emptyset.\end{split}\end{cases}

It is easy to see that Cop wins

if and only if Cop wins

for every graph

that contains edges.
In a graph without edges Cop wins one round earlier.
We can circumvent this difference by playing on a graph

.
The game played on the graph

The same holds for the monotone game.

We write

and

We will mainly consider

Lastly, building upon a result from

.

Let

The implication from (1) to (2) is trivial.
To see the implication from (2) to (3) we first need some preliminary definitions and lemmas.
The following sections are dedicated to this proof.
But first we prove the implication from (3) to (1).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

be a graph and

. Cop wins the game
\monCR_{q}^{k}(G)
.

Proof.

W.l.o.g.

.

As

, there is a
k
-pebble forest cover
(F,\boldsymbol{r},p)
of depth at most

.
For any set

and any vertex

and

, and

to denote the maximal element with respect to

.
From this we construct a winning strategy for Cop in the game

such that for every position

1.

X\preceq y
and

.

The strategy is then defined as

\mathcal{S}(X,y)\coloneqq\begin{cases}X\cup\{b\},\text{ if }X\neq X^{\prime}\\

.
Then

either is some root vertex if there are no cops on

and otherwise

is a child of
\max_{\preceq}X
.
To see that
(1)
holds first observe that

.
Assume that there is some

in

.
Since

is a forest cover of

4.1
Pre-tree-decomposition, exactness and submodularity

Here we consider a definition of tree-decompositions that is inspired by tree-decompositions of matroids.
We relax this definition into what we call a pre-tree-decomposition.

7
.

Let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be a graph.
Let
X\subseteq E(G)
.
Let
\pi\in\partitions_{\emptyset}(E(G))
.
We define

\Delta(\pi)\coloneqq\{v\in V(G)\mid\exists X\in\pi,v\in\Delta(X)\}.

A tuple
(T,r,\beta,\gamma)
, where
(T,r)
is a rooted tree,
\beta\colon V(T)\rightarrow 2^{V(G)}
and
\gamma\colon\overrightarrow{E(T)}\rightarrow 2^{E(G)}
, is a
rooted pre-tree-decomposition
if:

(PD.1)

\beta(r)=\emptyset
and for every connected component
C
of
G
, there is a child
c
of the root with
\gamma(r,c)=E(C)
.

(PD.2)

For every leaf
\ell\in L(T)
with parent
t
, it holds that
|\gamma(t,\ell)|\leq 1
.

(PD.3)

For every internal node
t\in V(T)\setminus L(T)
, we define
\pi_{t}\coloneqq(\gamma(t,t_{1}),\ldots,\gamma(t,t_{d}))
, where
N(t)=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\}
an arbitrary enumeration of the neighbours of
t
, and for a leaf
\ell\in L(T)
with parent
p
we define
\pi_{\ell}\coloneqq(\gamma(\ell,p),\overline{\gamma(\ell,p)})
.
For every
t\in V(T)
, the tuple
\pi_{t}
is an ordered partition of
E(G)
and
\Delta(\pi_{t})\subseteq\beta(t)
.

(PD.4)

For every edge
st\in E(T)
, it holds that
\gamma(s,t)\cap\gamma(t,s)=\emptyset
.

We call an edge
st\in E(T)

exact
if
\gamma(s,t)\cup\gamma(t,s)=E(G)
, we call
(T,r,\gamma,\beta)

exact
, if every edge is exact and
\beta(t)=\Delta(\pi_{t})
, for all
t\in V(T)
.
We call
\beta(t)
the
bag
at node
t
and
\gamma(s,t)
the
cone
at edge
st
.

The function
\gamma
describes a partition of the edges of the graph at every inner node, whereas the function
\beta
gives a vertex separator for this partition.
This separator may contain more vertices than necessary at a certain node, which is needed to define the depth of a pre-tree-decomposition, as seen below.
For some edge
st\in V(T)
with
s\prec t
, we can view
\gamma(s,t)
as the set of edges that need to be decomposed in the subtree below and
\gamma(t,s)
as the set of edges that is for sure decomposed somewhere else within the tree.
With this point of view the axioms correspond to the following ideas:

(PD.1)

We start by separating the different connected components of the graph and assign one distinct subtree to decompose each component. This way we also ensure that all of the graph is decomposed in some subtree.

(PD.2)

We want to decompose the graph into single edges. We do allow empty leafs and even empty subtrees, to ease our cleaning up procedure in the following sections. The reader may recall that the root of a rooted tree is never a leaf, by definition.

(PD.3)

At every node of the tree we make sure that all edges of the original graph are accounted for and that
\beta
is indeed a separator for the given partition.

(PD.4)

If a parent node assigns an edge to the set that still has to be decomposed, the child node can not assign this edge to the set that is already decomposed somewhere else. But the other direction is possible, if the parent node assigns an edge to the set that is decomposed somewhere else, the child can still assign it to one of its subtrees. If the latter is also not the case the edge is exact.

Similar to the definition of width and depth for tree-decompositions we define the width and depth of a pre-tree-decomposition.
We slightly adapt the definition of depth as
(TD.2)
does not hold in pre-tree-decompositions.

8
.

The
width
of a partition
\pi
of the edges of a graph is

\wid(\pi)\coloneqq|\Delta(\pi)|.

The
width
of a pre-tree-decomposition is

\wid(T,r,\beta,\gamma)\coloneqq\max_{t\in V(T)}|\beta(t)|-1.

The
depth
of a rooted pre-tree-decomposition is

\dep(T,r,\beta,\gamma)\coloneqq\max_{t\in V(T)}\sum_{r\prec s\preceq t}|\beta(s)\setminus\beta(p_{s})|.

The reader may note that the width of a pre-tree-decomposition only gets smaller if one sets
\beta(t)\coloneqq\Delta(\pi_{t})
, for all nodes

, but the depth can become larger.

Lemma 4.3

(
[

)
.

For every graph
G
,
\wid

Proof.

Let
\pi=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}),\pi^{\prime}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})\in\partitions_{\emptyset}(E(G))
.
We prove that

\wid(\pi)+\wid(\pi^{\prime})\geq\wid(\pi_{X_{1}\rightarrow\overline{Y_{1}}})+\wid(\pi^{\prime}_{Y_{1}\rightarrow\overline{X_{1}}}),

which is enough to prove the lemma by symmetry.

If
X_{1}=E(G)
, then
\pi=\pi_{X_{1}\rightarrow\overline{Y_{1}}}
and
\pi^{\prime}=\pi^{\prime}_{Y_{1}\rightarrow\overline{X_{1}}}
, thus the lemma holds.

If
X_{1}=\emptyset
, then
\pi^{\prime}_{Y_{1}\rightarrow\overline{X_{1}}}=(E(G),\emptyset,\ldots,\emptyset)
and thus
\wid(\pi^{\prime}_{Y_{1}\rightarrow\overline{X_{1}}})=0
.
Furthermore
\Delta(\pi_{X_{1}\rightarrow\overline{Y_{1}}})\subseteq\Delta(\pi)\cup\Delta(Y_{1})\subseteq\Delta(\pi)\cup\Delta(\pi^{\prime})
and thus
\wid(\pi_{X_{1}\rightarrow\overline{Y_{1}}})\leq\wid(\pi)+\wid(\pi^{\prime})
, thus the lemma holds.

If
Y_{1}=E(G)
or
Y_{1}=\emptyset
the lemma holds analogously.

Thus let
\emptyset\neq X_{1},Y_{1}\neq E(G)
.
Trivially we get that
\Delta(\pi_{X_{1}\rightarrow\overline{Y_{1}}})\subseteq\Delta(\pi)\cup\Delta(Y_{1})
and
\Delta(\pi^{\prime}_{Y_{1}\rightarrow\overline{X_{1}}})\subseteq\Delta(\pi^{\prime})\cup\Delta(X_{1})
.
Assume there exists some
v\in\Delta(\pi_{X_{1}\rightarrow\overline{Y_{1}}})\setminus\Delta(\pi)
, then
v\in\Delta(Y_{1})
and thus
v\in\Delta(\pi^{\prime})
.
Furthermore we get that
E(v)\cap X_{1}=\emptyset
and thus it holds that
E(v)\subseteq Y_{1}\cup\overline{X_{1}}
.
But then

.
Analogously we can show that
\left(\Delta(\pi^{\prime}_{Y_{1}\rightarrow\overline{X_{1}}})\setminus\Delta(\pi^{\prime})\right)\cap\Delta(\pi_{X_{1}\rightarrow\overline{Y_{1}}})=\emptyset
.
Thus all in all every vertex that is newly introduced to one of the boundaries

We continue this section with some lemmas, that help us to get comfortable with the definition of a pre-tree-decomposition and are useful to prove that our cleaning up procedure in the following sections is correct.
We start with a lemma about the cones along a path of exact edges.
It is a direct consequence of exactness and the fact that the cones incident to a vertex form a partition of the edges.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(T,r,\beta,\gamma)
be a pre-tree-decomposition of a graph
G
.
Let
P=t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell}
be a path in
T
, such that every edge
t_{i}t_{i+1}
, for
i\in[\ell-1]
, is exact.
Then it holds that

\gamma(t_{1},t_{2})\supseteq\gamma(t_{2},t_{3})\supseteq\ldots\supseteq\gamma(t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell})
.

Proof.

We show that
\gamma(t_{1},t_{2})\supseteq\gamma(t_{2},t_{3})
and then the lemma follows by induction over the path.
As
\pi_{t_{2}}
is a partition of the edges, we get that
\overline{\gamma(t_{2},t_{1})}\supseteq\gamma(t_{2},t_{3})
.
By definition of exactness we have that
\overline{\gamma(t_{2},t_{1})}=\gamma(t_{1},t_{2})
, which concludes the proof.

.

Lemma 4.12
.

For all
i\in[n_{T}]
,
(T,r,\beta_{i},\gamma_{i})
is a pre-tree-decomposition.
Furthermore all edges in
E(T_{i})
are exact.

Proof.

(PD.1)
holds as all edges leaving the root are already exact in
\gamma
, thus we change nothing in step 1 where the root is considered and every
F^{i}
, with
i>1
, only contains edges from a single component of
G
by construction.

We observe that the changes from
\gamma_{i-1}
to
\gamma_{i}
at some node
t\in V(T_{i})\setminus\{s_{i}\}
corresponds to an
F^{i}
- or
F^{*i}_{j}
-extension of
\pi^{i-1}_{t}
at the set that corresponds to the edge, that points towards
s_{i}
.
Furthermore
\pi^{i}_{s}
is a partition of the edges by construction and for all
t\in V(T_{i})
we set
\beta_{i}(t)=\Delta(\pi^{i}_{t})
.
As
\gamma_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
are equal to
\gamma
and
\beta
at all vertices that are not part of
V(T_{i})
, this shows by induction that
(PD.3)
still holds.

Next we observe that at every edge that is not incident to some
t^{i}_{j}
we add to one direction exactly what we remove from the other direction.
Furthermore by construction the edges
s_{i}t^{i}_{j}
are exact after the construction.
Lastly, for all children
c
of
t^{i}_{j}
, we only remove edges from
\gamma_{i}(t^{i}_{j},c)
.
Thus again by induction we get that
(PD.4)
holds and that all edges of
T_{i}
are exact.

It remains to show that
(PD.2)
holds.
Let
i\in[n_{t}]
and
j\in[a_{i}]
such that
t^{i}_{j}\in L(T)
.
From
Lemma

4.11
we know that
\gamma_{i-1}(s_{i},t^{i}_{j})\subseteq\gamma(s_{i},t^{i}_{j})
and thus
|\gamma_{i-1}(s_{i},t^{i}_{j})|\leq 1
.
Furthermore we know that
\gamma_{i-1}(t^{i}_{j},s_{i})=\gamma(t^{i}_{j},s_{i})=E(G^{\circ})\setminus\gamma(s_{i},t^{i}_{j})
.
Therefore we get that
F^{i}_{j}\subseteq\gamma(s_{i},t^{i}_{j})
and thus
\gamma_{i}(s_{i},t^{i}_{j})\subseteq\gamma(s_{i},t^{i}_{j})
.
By construction, in step

, such that

, we do nothing.
And in all other steps
\alpha>i
, we have that
t^{i}_{j}\not\preceq s_{\alpha}
and thus we only remove edges from
\gamma_{\alpha}(s_{i},t^{i}_{j})
.
This shows that for all
\alpha\in[n_{T}]
we have
|\gamma_{\alpha}(s_{i},t^{i}_{j})|\leq 1
.

We show below that the width and depth are as stated in the theorem.

Our construction does not change the width of the decomposition.
To prove this we observe that in step
i
the bound in

is minimal.
We then push the change through the subtree

and find that if a change would increase the width, we could push this change back to the node

Lemma 4.13
.

\wid(T,r,\beta_{i},\gamma_{i})\leq\wid(T,r,\beta,\gamma)
, for all
i\in[n_{T}]
.

Proof.

We prove the statement for all
0\leq i\leq n_{T}
by induction.
The statement clearly holds for
i=0
, as
(T,r,\beta_{0},\gamma_{0})=(T,r,\beta,\gamma)
.
For all
i\in[n_{T}]
, it remains to show that
\wid(T,r,\beta_{i},\gamma_{i})\leq\wid(T,r,\beta_{i-1},\gamma_{i-1})
.
Obviously
|\beta_{i}(t)|=|\beta_{i-1}(t)|
, for all
t\notin T_{i}
.
Furthermore by construction
|\beta_{i}(s_{i})|\leq|\beta_{i-1}(s_{i})|
.
Let
j\in[a_{i}]
, let
X\coloneqq\gamma_{i}(s_{i},t^{i}_{j})
and let
Y\coloneqq\gamma_{i-1}(t^{i}_{j},s_{i})
.
We observe that

\pi^{i}_{t^{i}_{j}}=\pi^{i-1}_{t^{i}_{j},Y\rightarrow\overline{X}}.

Thus it holds that

|\beta_{i}(t^{i}_{j})|=|\wid(\pi^{i-1}_{t^{i}_{j},Y\rightarrow\overline{X}})|\leq|\wid(\pi^{i-1}_{t^{i}_{j}})|\leq|\beta_{i-1}(t^{i}_{j})|

as otherwise by submodularity for the partitions
\pi^{i-1}_{t^{i}_{j}}
and
\pi^{i}_{s_{i}}
, we get that

|\wid(\pi^{i}_{s_{i},X\rightarrow\overline{Y}})|<|\wid(\pi^{i}_{s_{i}})|,

which contradicts the minimality of the bound for
F^{i}_{1},\ldots,F^{i}_{a_{i}}
.

Lastly assume there is a node
t
in
V(T_{i})\setminus\{s_{i},t^{i}_{1},\ldots,t^{i}_{a_{i}}\}
such that
|\beta_{i}(t)|>|\beta_{i-1}(t)|
.
We assume
t
is of minimal distance to
s_{i}
with this property.
Let
x_{0}=t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{b}=s_{i}
be the path from
t
to
s_{i}
.
By minimality of the distance we know that
|\beta_{i}(x_{1})|\leq|\beta_{i-1}(x_{1})|
.
Additionally we know that all edges on the path from
s_{i}
to
x_{1}
are exact in
\gamma_{i}
, as well as the edge
x_{1}t
in
\gamma_{i-1}
.
Now let
Y\coloneqq\gamma_{i-1}(t,x_{1})
and, for all
0\leq\alpha<b
, let
X_{\alpha}\coloneqq\gamma_{i}(x_{\alpha+1},x_{\alpha})
and
Z_{\alpha}\coloneqq\gamma_{i}(x_{\alpha},x_{\alpha+1})
.
The transition from
i-1
to
i
at
t
corresponds to
\pi^{i-1}_{t,Y\rightarrow F}=\pi^{i-1}_{t,Y\rightarrow\overline{X_{0}}}
.
Thus if
\wid(\pi^{i-1}_{t,Y\rightarrow F})=|\beta_{i}(t)|>|\beta_{i-1}(t)|=\wid(\pi^{i-1}_{t})
we get by submodularity that
\wid(\pi^{i}_{x_{1}})>\wid(\pi^{i}_{x_{1},X_{0}\rightarrow\overline{Y}})
.
As the edge
x_{1}t
was exact at step
i-1
, we know that

F^{\prime}\coloneqq\overline{Y}\setminus X_{0}=F^{i}\setminus Y\subseteq F^{i}.

We now push this change back to
s_{i}
along the path
x_{1},\ldots,x_{b}
and again we construct a contradiction to the minimality of the bound of
F^{i}_{1},\ldots,F^{i}_{a_{i}}
.
For this, let us assume we have pushed the change to
x_{\alpha}
, that is we changed
\pi^{i}_{x_{\alpha}}
to
\pi^{*}_{x_{\alpha}}=\pi^{i}_{x_{\alpha},X_{\alpha-1}\rightarrow F^{\prime}}
and we know that
\wid(\pi^{*}_{x_{\alpha}})<\wid(\pi^{i}_{x_{\alpha}})
.
As the edge
x_{\alpha}x_{\alpha+1}
is exact in
\gamma_{i}
, we get that
\pi^{*}_{x_{\alpha},(Z_{\alpha}\setminus F^{\prime})\rightarrow\overline{X_{\alpha}}}=\pi^{i}_{x_{\alpha}}
.
Let

\pi^{*}_{x_{\alpha+1}}\coloneqq\pi^{i}_{x_{\alpha+1},X_{\alpha}\rightarrow\overline{(Z_{\alpha}\setminus F^{\prime})}}=\pi^{i}_{x_{\alpha+1},X_{\alpha}\rightarrow F^{\prime}},

then by submodularity
\wid(\pi^{*}_{x_{\alpha+1}})<\wid(\pi^{i}_{x_{\alpha+1}})
.
When we have pushed the change to
\alpha=b
, we find the desired contradiction.
∎

To prove that our construction does not increase the depth we show that in every step
i
the depth up to the nodes in
T_{i}
is bounded by the depth up to these nodes in the original tree.
We prove this by induction on the number of steps.
In step
i
every change in any bag at some node in

is closely related to the change at the considered node
s_{i}
.
Additionally we find that the vertices in a bag at some child of
s_{i}
that is not present in the bag at

is exactly the vertex the cop player newly placed in the corresponding move of the game.

Lemma 4.14
.

For all
i\in[n_{T}]
and all
t\in V(T_{i})
, it holds that

\sum_{r\prec s\preceq t}|\beta_{i}(s)\setminus\beta_{i}(p_{s})|\leq\sum_{r\prec s\preceq t}|\beta(s)\setminus\beta(p_{s})|.

Proof.

Let
\ell\in[n_{T}]
.
As by construction
\beta_{\ell}(t)=\Delta(\pi^{\ell}_{t})
, for all
t\in V(T_{\ell})
, we get from
Lemma

4.6
and
Lemma

4.12
that
|\bigcup_{s\preceq t}\beta_{\ell}(s)|=\sum_{r\prec s\preceq t}|\beta_{\ell}(s)\setminus\beta_{\ell}(p_{s})|
.
Thus it suffices to show that
|\bigcup_{s\preceq t}\beta_{i}(s)|\leq\sum_{r\prec s\preceq t}|\beta(s)\setminus\beta(p_{s})|.

We prove the statement by induction on the steps
i
.
Let us recall that by construction
(T,r,\beta_{0},\gamma_{0})=(T,r,\beta,\gamma)
, thus the statement holds for
i=0
.
Now assume the statement holds for
i-1
, thus for all
t\in V(T_{i-1})
it hold that
|\bigcup_{s\preceq t}\beta_{i-1}(s)|\leq\sum_{r\prec s\preceq t}|\beta(s)\setminus\beta(p_{s})|.

We recall that
V(T_{i})=V(T_{i-1})\cup\{t^{i}_{1},\ldots,t^{i}_{a_{i}}\}
and that
s_{i}\in L(T_{i-1})

For the nodes

we can directly build upon the induction hypothesis.
But the nodes

We can prove for these nodes that every vertex newly placed at one of these nodes in step

is also newly placed in the original strategy.
Then we can show that the difference between depth at these nodes and their parent in step

is bounded by the difference in the original strategy tree.

Every

Observe that if

Let

Let

Let

.
As

Let

As by

|\beta_{i}(t^{*})|\leq|\beta_{i-1}(t^{*})|

By

By this

\left|\bigcup_{s\preceq t}\beta_{i}(s)\right|\leq\left|\bigcup_{s\preceq t}\beta_{i-1}(s)\right|
.

Otherwise
t=t^{i}_{j}
for some

.
We get
\bigcup_{s\preceq{t}}\beta_{i}(s)=\bigcup_{s\preceq{s_{i}}}\beta_{i}(s)\cup\beta_{i}(t)\setminus\beta_{i}(s_{i})

We have shown that
|\bigcup_{s\preceq{s_{i}}}\beta_{i}(s)|\leq\sum_{r\prec s\preceq{s_{i}}}|\beta(s)\setminus\beta(p_{s})|

Thus we get

Proof of
Lemma

4.10
.

Let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be a graph, let

and assume Cop wins
\CR^{k}_{q}(G)
.
Then Cop wins
\eCR^{k}_{q}(G^{\circ})
and there is some winning strategy
\sigma\colon V(G^{\circ})^{\leq k}\times E(G^{\circ})\rightarrow V(G^{\circ})^{\leq k}
, where
\sigma(X,uv)=\sigma(X,u^{\prime}v^{\prime})
for all

From this winning strategy we construct a strategy tree, thus pre-tree-decomposition of

Combining

we get that there exists an exact pre-tree-decomposition of

of width
\leq k
and depth
\leq q

\eCR^{k}_{q}(G^{\circ})

The theorem then follows from

∎

The reader may note that the way we define monotony is known as robber-monotony in some literature.
There also is an alternative definition called cop-monotony, where Cop is not allowed to move to a vertex that he moved away from in an earlier round.
For standard Cop-and-Robber game these two notions are known to be equivalent.
Our proofs show that they are also equivalent in our game, as the strategy that results from a tree-decomposition is both robber-monotone and cop-monotone.

4.4
Separating
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
from
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}

We aim to show that the graph class
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
is a proper subclass of
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
.
One can only hope to separate the classes if

is larger than

\mathcal{T}_{q}^{q}=\mathcal{TD}_{q}=\mathcal{TW}_{q-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
.
Using the characterizations of
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
via a Cops-and-Robber game, we show that there indeed are graphs for which one can not simultaneously bound the width to treewidth and the depth to treedepth.
The graph we consider is the
(h\times\ell)
-grid

, with

. It is well known that
\tw(G_{h\times\ell})=h
and
\td(G_{h\times\ell})\leq h\lceil\log(\ell+1)\rceil
, cf.
Figure

. We give a lower bound to the number of cop rounds that Robber can win in a, possibly non-monotone, game
\CR^{h}(G)
, which is linear in both
\ell
and

.

Lemma 4.15
.

For
1<h<\ell-2
and
q\leq\frac{h(\ell-h+2)}{4}
, Robber wins the game
\CR_{q}^{h+1}(G_{h\times\ell})
.

The proof of
Lemma

4.15
builds upon
[
19
]
.
The winning strategy of the robber is to always stay in the component with the most vertices.
To prove that this strategy benefits Robber, we first make some structural observations on the grid
G_{h\times\ell}
with
h>1
rows and
\ell
columns and its separators of size
\leq h+1
.
For
X\subseteq V(G_{h\times\ell})
, we call a connected component of
G_{h\times\ell}\setminus X

good
if it contains at least one full column of
G_{h\times\ell}
.

Observation 4.16
.

For
1<h<\ell-1
and
X\in\binom{V(G_{h\times\ell})}{\leq h+1}
, there exists a good component in
G_{h\times\ell}\setminus X
.

Proof.

Since there are at most
h+1
vertices in
X
and the grid has at least
h+2
columns, there exists a column which does not contain any vertices from
X
. This column is contained by a good component of
G_{h\times\ell}\setminus X
.
∎

The next lemma shows that
G_{h\times\ell}\setminus X
can never contain more than two good components if
X\in\binom{V(G_{h\times\ell}}{\leq h+1}
.
And if it does contain two good components, there can only be one single vertex that is in any other component.

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
h,\ell>1
and let
X\in\binom{V(G_{h\times\ell})}{\leq h+1}
. If there are two distinct good components in
G_{h\times\ell}\setminus X
then there is at most one additional component and this component has size 1.

Proof.

Let
C_{1},C_{2}
be the two good components.
Since
X
separates
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
,
X
must contain at least one vertex from every row. Thus, at most one row contains two vertices from
X
.
Let
C_{3}
be the set of vertices that are not contained in
X\cup C_{1}\cup C_{2}
.
Assume
C_{3}
contains a vertex from row
i\in[h]
.
Then row
i
contains vertices from
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
.
Since these are all distinct components of
G_{h\times\ell}\setminus X
, row
i
also contains at least two, thus exactly two, vertices of
X
.

Hence,
C_{3}
intersects at most one row, say row
r
.
As
C_{3}
cannot contain a vertex of any other row, all neighbours of
C_{3}
that are not in row
r
need to be in
X
.
As
h>1
, at least one of
r-1
or
r+1
is in
[h]
.
Thus there is a row in
[h]\setminus\{r\}
where at least
|C_{3}|
vertices are contained in
X
.
Since all rows besides
r
have exactly one vertex in
X
, we conclude that
|C_{3}|\leq 1
.
∎

We use
Lemma

4.17
to show that Robber can find some large component and that Cops can never remove more than two vertices from this component as long as his component is good.
Next we show that a large component is always good.

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
1<h<\ell-2
and let
X\in\binom{V(G_{h\times\ell})}{\leq h+1}
.
A connected component
C
of
G_{h\times\ell}\setminus X
that contains more than
\frac{(h-1)(h+2)}{2}
vertices is good.

Proof.

Let
C
be a connected component of
G_{h\times\ell}\setminus X
which is not good.
We prove that
C
contains at most
\frac{(h-1)(h+2)}{2}
many vertices.

By
4.16
, there exists a good component
C^{\prime}
in
G\setminus X
containing some column
j\in[\ell]
. Without loss of generality, all columns intersecting
C
lie left of
j
, i.e. have index strictly smaller than
j
.

Claim 1
.

For
1\leq i<h
,

C
contains at most
i+1
columns with at least
h-i
vertices.

Proof.

Assume
C
would contain at least
i+2
columns with at least
h-i
vertices.

X
contains at least one vertex of each of these columns, as
C
is not good.
Let
j^{\prime}
be the largest index of a column intersecting
C
and let
I\subseteq[h]
be the set of rows that intersect
C
in column
j^{\prime}
, that is
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in C
, for all
i^{\prime}\in I
.
We define vertex disjoint
C
-
C^{\prime}
-paths by
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}+1),\ldots,(i^{\prime},j)
for all
i^{\prime}\in I
.
By Menger’s Theorem,
X
needs to contain at least one vertex of each of these paths and all those vertices are in some column that is strictly larger than
j^{\prime}
.
All in all,
X
contains at least
i+2+|I|\geq i+2+h-i=h+2
vertices, which contradicts the choice of
X
.

\blacksquare

For
1\leq i\leq h
, write
m_{i}
for the number of columns in
C
which contain at least
i
vertices. Then
m_{i}-m_{i+1}
is the number of columns in
C
which contain exactly
i
vertices.
By
1
,
m_{i}\leq h+1-i
for all
1\leq i\leq h-1
.
Hence,

|C|=\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}(m_{i}-m_{i+1})i=\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}m_{i}\leq(h-1)(h+1)-\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}i=\frac{(h-1)(h+2)}{2},

as desired.
∎

We observe that this bound is tight as one can realize a component
C
that is not good and contains exactly
i+1
columns with at least
h-i
vertices, for all
1\leq i<h
, by
X=\{(1,1),(1,2),(2,3),\ldots,(h,h+1)\}
.
We are now ready to prove
Lemma

4.15
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.15
.

The strategy of Robber is to always move to the largest component.

Claim 1
.

After
q^{\prime}<\frac{\ell h-h^{2}+2h}{4}
cop introductions the size of the robber component is at least
\frac{\ell h-h-2}{2}-2(q^{\prime}-h)>\frac{(h-1)(h+2)}{2}
.

Proof.

Since
h<\ell-2
, for every Cop position
X\in\binom{V(G_{h\times\ell})}{\leq h+1}
, there are at least two columns where no cop is positioned.
In order to catch the robber, the cops have to move to a position such that these two columns lie in distinct good components of
G_{h\times\ell}\setminus X
.
Since such an
X
contains a vertex of every row, Cop needs to introduce at least
h
cops.

By the pigeonhole principle and
Lemma

4.17
, there is some component
C
of size at least
\frac{\ell h-h-2}{2}
and it can be reached by Robber.
Before Robber reaches this component, the largest component of the graph contained even more vertices.
If the cops move back to a position
X
with only one good component, the escape space of the robber is again larger than
\frac{\ell h-h-2}{2}
.
If the cops move outside of the robber components, its size does not change.
The only way to shrink the size of the escape space is to move the next cop into the robber component.
Such a move could, in addition to the vertex where the cop is placed onto, remove some connected component from the robber escape space.
But we know from
Lemma

4.17
that this component can only be a singleton as long as the larger remaining component is good.
Thus from this point onward the size of the robber component shrinks by at most two per round as long as the robber is still in a good component.
Since

\frac{\ell h-h-2}{2}-2(q^{\prime}-h)>\frac{\ell h-h-2}{2}-2\left(\frac{\ell h-h^{2}+2h}{4}-h\right)=\frac{(h-1)(h+2)}{2},

Robber can choose a good component by
Lemma

4.18
.

\blacksquare

As
\frac{(h-1)(h+2)}{2}>1
, for
h>1
, Robber is not caught after
q\leq\frac{\ell h-h^{2}+2h}{4}
rounds and he wins the game.
∎

The bound of
Lemma

4.15
turns out to be tight up to an additive term that only depends on
h
, for all
h>3
.

Lemma 4.19
.

For
3<h<\ell-3
and
q\geq\frac{\ell h}{4}+h+1
,
Cop wins the game
\CR_{q}^{h+1}(G_{h\times\ell})
.

Proof.

In the first
h
rounds, Cop places the cops on the following vertices in arbitrary order
X\coloneqq\{(1,\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{h}{2}\rfloor+1),(2,\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{h}{2}\rfloor+2),\ldots,(h,\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\rfloor+\lceil\frac{h}{2}\rceil)\}
.

We assume that the robber is in position
\escape(X,{(h,1)})
.
In the next round Cops places the last cop on
(h,\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\rfloor+\lceil\frac{h}{2}\rceil-2)
.
In round
r
, as long as
(1,1)\notin X_{r}
, there is some
i\in[h]
and
1<j\leq\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{h}{2}\rfloor+1
such that the cops are in some position
X_{r}=\{(1,j),(2,j+1),\ldots,(i,j+i-1),(i,j+i-3),(i+1,j+i-2),\ldots,(h,j+h-3)\}
.
The robber is either still in position
\escape({X_{r}},{(h,1)})
or he moved to the singleton component
\{(i,j+i-2)\}
.
If the robber moves to the singleton component, he can be caught in the next round as
h+1>4
and the singleton has at most four neighbours.
Thus assume that the robber is in position
\escape({X_{r}},{(h,1)})
.
Then the cop player moves the cop on position
(i,j+i-1)
to
(i-1,\max(1,j+i-4))
if
i>1
, or to position
(h,j+h-5)
.
That is in
h
rounds the cops move iteratively to a new diagonal that is two columns closer to vertex
(h,1)
.
Else if
(1,1)\in X_{r}
, Cop continues this strategy of iteratively moving the (now partial) diagonal two columns closer to the vertex
(h,1)
until the robber is caught.
All in all this strategy places
\lceil(\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{h}{2}\rfloor+i)/2\rceil
times a cop in row
i\in[h]
, if the robber avoids singleton components as long as possible.
Therefore the number of rounds done by Cop is

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{i=1}^{h}\lceil(\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{h}{2}\rfloor+i)/2\rceil

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{h}(\frac{\ell}{2}-\frac{h-1}{2}+i+1)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\ell h-h^{2}+3h}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{h}i=\frac{\ell h}{4}+h+1

If the robber starts in position
\escape(X,{(1,\ell)})
the strategy for the cops is symmetric, but since
\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{h}{2}\rfloor+1\geq\ell-\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\rfloor-\lceil\frac{h}{2}\rceil
, Cop does not need longer to catch the robber.
∎

For
h=1
, the proof idea of
Lemma

4.15
is not applicable as on a path there are separators of size two that separate the path into three components of roughly equal size.
Despite that, one may observe that such a separator does not benefit Cop as from such a position he would always have to combine two of these components into a larger one.
Thus the cop player can only move along the path and shrink the escape space of Robber by one vertex.
This case is covered in the original proof of
[
19
]
.

Lemma 4.20

(
[
19
, Theorems 5 and 7]
)
.

Let
\ell\geq 1
. Robber wins the game
\CR_{q}^{2}(G_{1\times\ell})
if and only if

.

Using this we can construct a graph
G\in\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
such that Robber wins
\CR_{q}^{k}(G)
, if
q
is sufficiently larger than

.

Lemma 4.21
.

For
q\geq 3
, there exist a connected graph
G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{TW}_{1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
such that Robber wins
\CR_{q}^{2}(G^{\prime})
and for
k,q\geq 1
such that
2\leq k-1\leq\frac{q-4}{1+\log q}
, there exist a connected graph
G\in\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
such that Robber wins
\CR_{q}^{k}(G)
.

Proof.

We first construct
G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{TW}_{1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
.
Consider the path
G^{\prime}\coloneqq G_{1\times 2^{q}-1}
.
It is well known that
\tw(G_{1\times 2^{q}-1})=1
and
\td(G_{1\times 2^{q}-1})=\lceil\log(2^{q}-1+1)\rceil=q
, thus
G_{1\times 2^{q}-1}\in\mathcal{TW}_{1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
.
By
Lemma

4.20
, Robber wins against two cops if the game is played for
q\leq\lceil\frac{2^{q}-1-1}{2}\rceil=2^{q-1}-1
rounds.
Thus Robber wins
\CR_{q}^{2}(G^{\prime})
since
q\geq 3
by assumption.

Next we construct
G\in\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
.
Since
q-\frac{q}{\log q}>1
, we may pick an integer
\ell
such that
q(\frac{4}{k-1}+\frac{1}{\log q})\leq\ell\leq q(\frac{4}{k-1}+1)
.
Define the graph
G
as the grid
G_{k-1\times\ell}
.
Then

, cf.
Figure

1
.
Using that

and by a careful analysis of the rounding, we get that

\displaystyle(k-1)(\lceil\log(\ell+1)\rceil)

\displaystyle=(k-1)(\lfloor\log(\ell)\rfloor+1)

\displaystyle\leq(k-1)\left(\log\left(q(\frac{4}{k-1}+1)\right)+1\right)

\displaystyle\leq(k-1)\left(\log(q)+\frac{4}{k-1}+1\right)

\displaystyle=4+(k-1)\left(\log(q)+1\right)\leq q.

Hence,

and clearly

.

A tree-decomposition of

that has width

can be found by combining an optimal tree-decomposition for the grid and the binary tree with an edge that connects one bag of each decomposition that contains

.

.

Observe that every graph that contains

and every graph that has a complete binary tree of depth

. Hence, the above bounds are tight.

The tree added to the grid does not change the lower bound we found on the number of rounds in a Cops
-
and
-
Robber game with

Thus in a game with

.
Using that

, we get that

\displaystyle\frac{(k-1)(\ell-(k-1))}{4}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(k-1)(q(\frac{4}{k-1}+\frac{1}{\log q})-(k-1))}{4}

\displaystyle=q+\frac{(k-1)(\frac{q}{\log q}-(k-1))}{4}

\displaystyle>q+\frac{(k-1)(\frac{q-4}{1+\log q}-(k-1))}{4}

\displaystyle\geq q+\frac{(k-1)((k-1)-(k-1))}{4}=q.

\CR_{q}^{k}(G)
.
∎

5
Homomorphism indistinguishability and logical equivalence

In this section, we turn to investigating
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
in terms of homomorphism indistinguishability.
It turns out that the representation of graphs in
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
in terms of construction trees offers a perspective suitable for obtaining characterisations of logical equivalence.
The general idea will be to use these trees to inductively construct
\mathsf{C}
-formulae that capture homomorphism counts.
Not only does this approach generalise results from
[
15
,
22
]
, it also yields an intuitive characterisation of
\mathsf{C}^{k}_{q}
-equivalence and thereby provides a more elementary proof of a result from
[
12
]
.

Moreover, the constructive nature of our proof strategy proves fruitful in obtaining additional characterisations of fragments of
\mathsf{C}
.
The general idea is to impose natural restrictions on the construction trees, such that a fragment
\mathsf{L}\subsetneq\mathsf{C}
already suffices to capture homomorphism counts.
By choosing these restrictions carefully, the resulting subclass of
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
is then still large enough to capture
\mathsf{L}
-equivalence.
We illustrate this point by giving a characterisation of
guarded
counting logic
\mathsf{GC}
.

We conclude by
semantically
separating
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
and
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
.
More formally, we show
Corollary

1.5
, i.e. that, for
q
sufficiently larger than
k
, there exist graphs
G
and
H
which are homomorphism indistinguishable over
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
but have different numbers of homomorphisms from some graph in
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
.

5.1
Homomorphism indistinguishability over
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
is
\mathsf{C}^{k}_{q}
-equivalence

In his 2010 paper
[
15
]
, Dvořák showed that
\mathsf{C}^{k}
-equivalence is equivalent to homomorphism indistinguishability over
\mathcal{TW}_{k}
.
It turns out that his techniques generalize remarkably well to construction trees.
To begin with, we make a few observations on how the operations that make up construction trees interact with homomorphism counts.

The following is not hard to see.

Observation 5.1
.

For labelled graphs
F_{1},F_{2},G
, it holds that

\hom(F_{1}F_{2},G)=\hom(F_{1},G)\cdot\hom(F_{2},G).

This is because any two homomorphisms
g\colon F_{1}\to G
and
h\colon F_{2}\to G
must agree on vertices with the same label, so
g\sqcup h
is well-defined and a homomorphism from
F_{1}F_{2}
to
G
.
Moreover, for
h\in\HOM(F_{1}F_{2},G)
the restrictions
h|_{V(F_{1})}
and
h|_{V(F_{2})}
are homomorphisms.
We can also relate the homomorphism counts from graphs
F
and
F^{\prime}
, whenever
F^{\prime}
is obtained from
F
by removing some label
\ell
.
Then in any homomorphism
h\colon F^{\prime}\to G
the image of
\labfkt_{F}(\ell)
is no longer necessarily
\labfkt_{G}(\ell)
.
Hence, we can obtain
\hom(F,G)
by moving the label
\ell
to different vertices in
G
and tallying up the homomorphisms from
F
to those graphs.

Observation 5.2
.

Let
F^{\prime}

Observation 5.3

Let

The product of two linear combinations is defined in the natural way, where the graph products distribute over the sum.
We also remove any graphs with loops that might have been created from the resulting linear combination, to ensure that the resulting linear combination only contains simple graphs.

This definition preserves the property on homomorphism counts of products, that is

Given the constructive nature of these proofs, it is interesting to investigate whether the same strategy can be used to obtain results for different fragments of
\mathsf{C}

An example where this works well is guarded counting logic

In the guarded fragment

Formally, we require that quantifiers only occur in the form

One option is to pair graphs together with a distinguished vertex and write

.
This works, and we obtain, in fact, a characterisation for precisely this relation.
However, we would prefer to study the landscape of homomorphism indistinguishability relations on graphs without restrictions.
The following natural definition of

-equivalence allows us to lift our result to graphs without a dinstinguished vertex.

Lemma 5.14

For unlabelled graphs

Proof.

We thus get

\displaystyle\hom(F,G(\ell\to v))=m

\displaystyle\iff G,v\models\varphi^{F}_{m}(x_{\ell})

\displaystyle\iff H,f(v)\models\varphi^{F}_{m}(x_{\ell})

\displaystyle\iff\hom(f,H(\ell\to f(v)))=m.

or equivalently

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\hom(F,G(\ell\to v))

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\hom(F,H(\ell\to f(v)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V(H)}\hom(F,H(\ell\to w))

7
Conclusion

We study the expressive power of the counting logic fragment
\mathsf{C}^{k}_{q}
with tools from homomorphism indistinguishability.
After giving an elementary and uniform proof of theorems from
[
12
,
15
,
22
]
, we show that the graph class
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
, whose homomorphism indistinguishability relation characterises
\mathsf{C}^{k}_{q}
-equivalence, is a proper subclass of
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
.
Finally, we show that
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
and
\mathcal{TD}_{q}
are homomorphism distinguishing closed which implies that homomorphism indistinguishability over
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{k}
is not the same as homomorphism indistinguishability over
\mathcal{TW}_{k-1}\cap\mathcal{TD}_{q}
.

One key ingredient to the above results is finding a node searching game that can characterise the graph classes.
Exploring whether intertwining node searching and model comparison games can help to confirm Roberson’s conjecture in other cases seems a tempting direction for future research.

With slight reformulations, our results might yield insights into the ability of the Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm to determine subgraph counts after a fixed number of rounds
[
36
,
42
]
.

To use this connection of the games it is important to characterise the graph class using a node searching game that allows non-monotone moves.
