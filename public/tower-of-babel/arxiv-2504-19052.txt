Our approach can be summarized as follows.

(a)

We utilize lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers to derive an upper estimate for

in terms of the parameters

. The analysis is more straightforward when

Methods

2.1
Some identities on
k
-Pell numbers

We begin with an identity from
[
9
]
, which relates
P_{n}^{(k)}
with the
m
-th Fibonacci number. It states that

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(k)}=F_{2n-1},

for all
1\leq n\leq k+1
, while the next term is
P_{k+2}^{(k)}=F_{2k+3}-1
. The characteristic polynomial of
\{P_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is given by

\Psi_{k}(x)=x^{k}-2x^{k-1}-\cdots-x-1.

This polynomial is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
and it possesses a unique real root
\alpha:=\alpha(k)>1
. All the other roots of
\Psi_{k}(x)
are inside the unit circle, see
[
4
]
. The particular root
\alpha
can be found in the interval

\displaystyle\varphi^{2}(1-\varphi^{-k})<\alpha<\varphi^{2},\qquad\text{for}\qquad k\geq 2,

(2.1)

as noted in
[
4
]
. As in the classical case when
k=2
, it was shown in
[
4
]
that

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-2}\leq P_{n}^{(k)}\leq\alpha^{n-1},\qquad\text{holds for all}\qquad n\geq 1\quad\text{and}\quad k\geq 2.

(2.2)

Next, for
k\geq 2
, we define

\displaystyle f_{k}(x):=\dfrac{x-1}{(k+1)x^{2}-3kx+k-1}=\dfrac{x-1}{k(x^{2}-3x+1)+x^{2}-1}.

(2.3)

In Lemma 1 of
[
3
]
, Bravo and Herera showed that

\displaystyle 0.276<f_{k}(\alpha)<0.5\qquad\text{and}\qquad|f_{k}(\alpha_{i})|<1,

(2.4)

holds for all
2\leq i\leq k
, where
\alpha_{i}
for
i=2,\ldots,k
are the roots of
\Psi_{k}(x)
inside the unit circle. So, the number
f_{k}(\alpha)
is not an algebraic integer. Indeed, if we suppose that
f_{k}(\alpha)
is an algebraic integer, then we know that
f_{k}(\alpha)\neq 0
and

N_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}}(f_{k}(\alpha))\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
N_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}}(f_{k}(\alpha))
is the norm of
f_{k}(\alpha)
from
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
to
\mathbb{Q}
. Therefore, we obtain

1\leq\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}}(f_{k}(\alpha))\right|=f_{k}(\alpha)\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{k}\left|f_{k}(\alpha^{(i)})\right|<0.5,

which is absurd. So
f_{k}(\alpha)
is not an algebraic integer. Moreover, it was also shown in Theorem 3.1 of
[
4
]
that

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(k)}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{k}(\alpha_{i})\alpha_{i}^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|P_{n}^{(k)}-f_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}\right|<\dfrac{1}{2},

(2.5)

hold for all
k\geq 2
and
n\geq 2-k
. The first part of (
2.5
) provides a Binet-type representation for
P_{n}^{(k)}
. In addition, the second inequality in (
2.5
) indicates that the impact of the roots located inside the unit circle on
P_{n}^{(k)}
is small.

Next, let
n\geq 4
and

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(k)}=p_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdot p_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\cdots p_{s}^{\beta_{s}},

(2.6)

be the prime factorization of the positive integer
P_{n}^{(k)}
, where
2=p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{s}
is the increasing sequence of prime numbers and the integers
\beta_{i}
, for
i=1,2,\ldots,s
, are non-negative integers. By the right hand side of relation (
2.2
), we have

\log P_{n}^{(k)}\leq(n-1)\log\alpha<2(n-1)\log\varphi,

since
\alpha<\varphi^{2}
, for all
k\geq 2
in (
2.1
). Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}\log p_{i}=\log\prod_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}^{\beta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\log P_{n}^{(k)}<2(n-1)\log\varphi,

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}\log 2

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}\log p_{i}<2(n-1)\log\varphi,\quad\text{since}\quad 2\leq p_{i},\quad\text{for all}\quad i=1,2,\ldots s.

Dividing both sides by
\log 2
, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}<1.4(n-1)<1.4n.

In particular,

\displaystyle\beta_{i}<1.4n,

(2.7)

for all
i=1,2,\ldots s
.

2.2
Linear forms in logarithms

To establish our result, we apply a lower bound of Baker type for non-zero linear combinations of logarithms of algebraic numbers. Although various versions of such bounds exist in the literature, we make use of a result due to Matveev as presented in
[
11
]
. Before stating the relevant inequality, we first recall the definition of the height of an algebraic number.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\gamma
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\gamma^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive. Then, the logarithmic height of
\gamma
is given by

h(\gamma):=\dfrac{1}{d}\Big(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\max\{|\gamma^{(i)}|,1\}\Big).

In particular, if
\gamma
is a rational number represented as
\gamma=p/q
with coprime integers
p
and
q\geq 1
, then
h(\gamma)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
.
The following properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
will be used in the rest of the paper without further reference:

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2})+\log 2;

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2});

\displaystyle h(\gamma^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\gamma)\quad{\text{\rm valid for}}\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}.

With these properties, it was proved in
[
3
, Lemma 3]
that

\displaystyle h\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)<4k\log\varphi+k\log(k+1),\qquad\text{for all}\qquad k\geq 2.

(2.8)

A linear form in logarithms is an expression

\Lambda:=b_{1}\log\gamma_{1}+\cdots+b_{t}\log\gamma_{t},

(2.9)

where for us
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t}
are positive real algebraic numbers and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
are integers. We assume,
\Lambda\neq 0
. We need lower bounds
for
|\Lambda|
. We write
{\mathbb{K}}:={\mathbb{Q}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t})
and
D
for the degree of
{\mathbb{K}}
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
We give Matveev’s inequality from
[
11
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Matveev,
[
11
]
)
.

Put
\Gamma:=\gamma_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{t}^{b_{t}}-1=e^{\Lambda}-1
. Then

\log|\Gamma|>-1.4\cdot 30^{t+3}\cdot t^{4.5}\cdot D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

where
B\geq\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|\}
and
A_{i}\geq\max\{Dh(\gamma_{i}),|\log\gamma_{i}|,0.16\}
for
i=1,\ldots,t
.

During the calculations, upper bounds on the variables are obtained which are too large, thus there is need to reduce them. To do so, we use some results from
approximation lattices and the so-called LLL-reduction method from
[
10
]
. We explain this in the following subsection.

2.3
Reduced Bases for Lattices and LLL-reduction methods

Let
k
be a positive integer. A subset
\mathcal{L}
of the
k
-dimensional real vector space
{\mathbb{R}^{k}}
is called a lattice if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}b_{i}\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

We say that
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
form a basis for
\mathcal{L}
, or that they span
\mathcal{L}
. We
call
k
the rank of
\mathcal{L}
. The determinant
\text{det}(\mathcal{L})
, of
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathcal{L})=|\det(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})|,

with the
b_{i}
’s being written as column vectors. This is a positive real number that does not depend on the choice of the basis (see
[
6
]
, Section 1.2p).

Given linearly independent vectors
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we refer back to the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization technique. This method allows us to inductively define vectors
b^{*}_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
) and real coefficients
\mu_{i,j}
(for
1\leq j\leq i\leq k
). Specifically,

\displaystyle b^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}b^{*}_{j},~~~\mu_{i,j}=\dfrac{\langle b_{i},b^{*}_{j}\rangle}{\langle b^{*}_{j},b^{*}_{j}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Notice that
b^{*}_{i}
is the orthogonal projection of
b_{i}
on the orthogonal complement of the span of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}
, and that
\mathbb{R}b_{i}
is orthogonal to the span of
b^{*}_{1},\ldots,b^{*}_{i-1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that
b^{*}_{1},b^{*}_{2},\ldots,b^{*}_{k}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Definition 2.2
.

The basis
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
for the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called reduced if

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i,j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|b^{*}_{i}+\mu_{i,i-1}b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<i\leq n,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length. The constant
{3}/{4}
above is arbitrarily chosen, and may be replaced by any fixed real number
y
in the interval
{1}/{4}<y<1
(see
[
10
]
, Section 1).

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a
k-
dimensional lattice with reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
and denote by
B
the matrix with columns
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
.
We define

l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\min_{x\in\mathcal{L}}||x-y||\quad;~~y\not\in\mathcal{L}\\
\min_{0\neq x\in\mathcal{L}}||x||\quad;~~y\in\mathcal{L}\end{array}\right.,

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. It is well known that, by applying the
LLL-algorithm, it is possible to give in polynomial time a lower bound for
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)
, namely a positive constant
c_{1}
such that

holds (see
[
13
]
, Section V.4).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

and

with

(i)

In our application, we are given real numbers

# Dictionary to memoize computed values
memo = {}

def k_generalized_pell_iterative(n, k):
# Handle trivial cases
if n < 2 - k:
return 0
elif n == 1:
return 1

# Check if already computed
if (n, k) in memo:
return memo[(n, k)]

# Initialize the list of Pell numbers
pell_nums = [0] * (2 - k) + [0, 1] + [0] * (n - 1)

# Compute iteratively
for i in range(2, n + 1):
pell_nums[i] = 2 * pell_nums[i - 1] + sum(
pell_nums[i - j] for j in range(2, k + 1) if i - j >= 0
)
memo[(i, k)] = pell_nums[i]

return pell_nums[n]

def is_of_form_2a_3b_5c_7d(val):
for prime in [2, 3, 5, 7]:
while val % prime == 0 and val > 1:
val //= prime
return val == 1

# Search for valid Pell numbers with prime factors <= 7
for k in range(2, 2500):
for n in range(k+2, 1214):
pell_val = k_generalized_pell_iterative(n, k)
if pell_val > 0 and is_of_form_2a_3b_5c_7d(pell_val):
print(f"For k={k}, n={n}: P_n^({k}) = {pell_val}")
