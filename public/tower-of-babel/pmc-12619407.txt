Is conformal thyroidectomy feasible? A 5-10-year follow-up study of residual thyroid in 2512 cases after thyroidectomy
Authors: Lisen Zhu, Yigang Zhang, Fuzhi Zhu, Weijia Wang, Jingxin Jiang, Gongsheng Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPapillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most common thyroid malignancy, and the balance between surgical extent (particularly non-total thyroidectomy [non-TX]) and postoperative recurrence risk in the residual thyroid remains a key clinical concern. This single-center retrospective study analyzed 2512 patients with unilateral PTC who underwent non-TTx between January 2014 and December 2024 and completed 5–10 years of follow-up evaluations, aiming to determine the recurrence rate in residual thyroid and related risk factors.MethodsThe Kaplan-Meier method was used to calculate cumulative recurrence-free survival (RT-RFS) in the residual thyroid. Independent risk factors were identified through univariate χ² tests and multivariate logistic regression analysis. The effect size for between-group differences in continuous variables was calculated using Cohen’s d.ResultsThe overall recurrence rate was 5.8% with 5- and 10-y RT-RFS rates of 95.4% and 94.2%, respectively. Univariate analysis demonstrated significant associations between recurrence and age > 55 y, increased tumor size (T1a-T3), multifocality, preoperative subclinical hypothyroidism, and postoperative TSH > 2.0 mIU/L (all P < 0.05). Multivariate regression analysis confirmed tumor size (T1b/T2/T3 vs. T1a: OR = 2.253/5.053/19.452), multifocality (OR = 1.908), and postoperative TSH > 2.0 mIU/L (OR = 2.193) as independent risk factors for residual thyroid recurrence (all P < 0.05). However, neither the surgical extent of non-TTx, nor age, gender, preoperative subclinical hypothyroidism, and coexisting Hashimoto’s thyroiditis are independent driving factors, and confirmed that there is no significant association between residual thyroid volume and postoperative recurrence risk.ConclusionFor patients with unilateral, unifocal PTC classified as T1a, we propose that conformal thyroidectomy represents a clinically appropriate and feasible alternative to unilateral thyroid lobectomy.

Introduction

Thyroid carcinoma is a common malignant tumor of the endocrine system with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) being the most prevalent subtype accounting for approximately 84% of all thyroid malignancies [1], PTC is associated with an excellent long-term prognosis with a 10-y survival rate as high as 98% [2, 3]. However, clinical strategies for the management of PTC are controversial, especially regarding the delineation of surgical extent and the impact on postoperative residual thyroid function [4, 5]. Although the global incidence of thyroid carcinoma has been increasing annually, overdiagnosis has led to unnecessary surgical interventions in some patients with low-risk thyroid carcinoma [6]. In recent years multiple clinical studies have systematically evaluated the long-term efficacy of non-total thyroidectomy (non-TTx) for PTC. Matsuz et al. [7] demonstrated through long-term follow-up studies that patients undergoing contralateral thyroid lobectomy achieved a higher remnant thyroid recurrence-free survival (RT-RFS) rate with a 10-y cumulative RT-RFS rate of 97.4%. Díez et al. [8] further analyzed postoperative completion thyroidectomy cases, which revealed that only 4.9% of patients required secondary surgery due to suspicious nodules or lymph nodes with recurrence in residual thyroid parenchyma occurring in as few as 1.8% of these cases. Similarly, a cohort study by Merten et al. focusing on patients with low-risk PTC (1–4 cm) reported that while 6.0% of patients underwent total thyroidectomy due to nodule enlargement or multifocality, only 1 case exhibited residual thyroid recurrence [9]. Notably, Xu et al. [10] detected no significant differences in recurrence rates when comparing different surgical approaches. Recent comparative studies from China and South Korea on conformal thyroidectomy versus lobectomy for PTC have demonstrated no significant difference in postoperative recurrence rates between the two surgical approaches [11, 12]. Taken together, this evidence-based data demonstrated that non-TTx meets the therapeutic needs of most PTC patients.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the recurrence rate in residual thyroid among patients with unilateral PTC undergoing non-TTx through long-term (5–10 y) follow-up at a single center. We sought to provide scientific evidence for the feasibility of conformal thyroidectomy.

Materials and methods

Patients

This single-center retrospective study enrolled 2512 patients with PTC who underwent surgical treatment in the Department of Surgical Oncology of the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University (Bengbu City, Anhui Province, China) between January 2014 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) confirmed diagnosis of unilateral PTC via postoperative pathologic examination; (2) surgical intervention limited to non-TTx with preserved observable residual thyroid tissue; (3) All patients underwent central compartment lymph node dissection of the thyroid gland, regardless of whether they underwent lateral neck lymph node dissection or not.; (4) Regardless of the subtype of PTC and whether the BRAF gene is mutated or not; (5)completion of systematic postoperative follow-up for 5–10 y and adhering to clinically standardized intervals; and (6) availability of complete preoperative laboratory test results, imaging data, surgical records, pathologic reports, adjuvant therapy documentation (e.g., thyroid stimulating hormone [TSH] suppression therapy), and regular postoperative ultrasound examinations and thyroid function tests.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) previous thyroid surgery performed at other institutions (non-primary surgical cases); (2) co-existing follicular, medullary, or anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, or other thyroid malignancies; (3) presence of extrathyroidal extension involving the thyroid capsule and surrounding soft tissues; (4) pre-existing distant metastases (e.g., pulmonary or osseous) confirmed radiologically or pathologically prior to surgery; (5) loss to follow-up postoperatively or incomplete regular follow-up (< 5 y) in recurrence-free patients based on the last valid follow-up documentation; and (6) diagnosis of other malignancies during initial diagnosis/treatment or the follow-up period.

Non -TTx

Non -TTx includes Unilateral thyroid lobectomy, Unilateral thyroid lobectomy with isthmus resection, and A unilateral thyroid lobectomy with isthmus and contralateral partial thyroidectomy.

A unilateral thyroid lobectomy with isthmus and contralateral partial thyroidectomy. Excision of the contralateral lesion-affected area was extended when non-suspicious nodules or occupying lesions are identified in the contralateral lobe via preoperative ultrasound or intraoperative exploration. Meticulous dissection techniques are used to remove the contralateral lesions and ensuring no gross residual disease intraoperatively and preserving normal thyroid tissue. For such patients, the preoperative aspiration result of the contralateral nodule has been clearly confirmed as benign; thus, our preoperative recommendation is long-term monitoring of the contralateral thyroid gland rather than surgical resection. Despite our detailed explanation to patients of the specific characteristics of thyroid cancer (such as biological behavior and progression pattern), patients still have persistent concerns about the risk of malignant transformation of the contralateral nodules, and ultimately insist on intraoperative resection of part of the contralateral thyroid tissue while explicitly refusing to accept the aforementioned monitoring plan.

Conformal thyroidectomy

Conformal thyroidectomy is a modified partial thyroidectomy for low-risk papillary thyroid microcarcinoma, which involves locating the tumor margin via preoperative and intraoperative ultrasound, ensuring a margin of more than 5 mm from the tumor, confirming negative margins through intraoperative frozen biopsy.

Criteria for recurrence determination

Residual thyroid recurrence specifically refers to the development of new cancerous foci within the residual thyroid tissue in patients with PTC following non-TTx. The diagnosis must meet the following criteria: (1) no residual lesions are detected on the first imaging evaluation (ultrasound) performed 3 months postoperatively; (2) suspicious malignant nodules identified within the residual thyroid tissue during follow-up imaging (ultrasound) after the second postoperative evaluation with confirmation via fine needle aspiration cytology (FNA) or postoperative histopathology as PTC; and (3) cervical lymph node metastasis, soft tissue recurrence in the thyroid bed, or distant metastasis must be ruled out. RT-RFS is defined as the interval from 6 months postoperatively to the detection of new lesions within the residual thyroid tissue.

Postoperative treatment and follow-up

All patients underwent postoperative TSH suppression therapy. Follow-up care commenced on the day of surgery and was scheduled every 6–12 months, with the final follow-up date set at 31 December 2024. The primary endpoints were 5- and 10-y RT-RFS. The median follow-up time was 79.5 months. Postoperative TSH levels were collected under standardized criteria, as follows: for patients with recurrence, TSH measurements obtained prior to secondary surgery were used; for recurrence-free patients, the last available TSH measurement before December 31, 2024, was utilized. Follow-up data were retrieved via hospital electronic medical records (EMRs) or structured telephone interviews.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses in this study were performed using specialized software. Data processing and univariate analysis were performed with SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Bengbu, Anhui, China), while survival curve visualization was generated using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Bengbu, Anhui, China). The Kaplan-Meier method was used to calculate the RT-RFS rate, and survival curves were dynamically generated to illustrate patient prognosis.

Observational indicators were standardized according to clinical guidelines, as follows: age was categorized into a younger group (≤ 55 y) and an older group (> 55 y) based on the AJCC staging criteria using 55 y of age as the cut-off; tumor diameter was classified into 4 groups per the TNM staging system (T1a, ≤ 1 cm; T1b, >1 cm to ≤ 2 cm; T2, >2 cm to ≤ 4 cm; and T3, >4 cm; categorical variables, including gender (male/female), tumor focality (unifocal/multifocal), residual thyroid volume(residual half of the thyroid/residual partial thyroid), Hashimoto’s thyroiditis [HT] (present/absent), preoperative subclinical hypothyroidism (present/absent), surgical approach, the extent of surgical resection, and postoperative TSH levels. Potential factors were initially screened via univariate analysis and differences in recurrence rates among variable groups were compared using the χ² test with the statistical significance level set at an α = 0.05.

Variables identified as statistically significant in the univariate analysis were further subjected to multivariate analysis using binary logistic regression models to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for each risk factor. All hypothesis tests were two-tailed and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The effect size for between-group differences in continuous variables was calculated using Cohen’s d.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study enrolled a total of 2512 patients with PTC. As shown in Table 1, the patients’ ages ranged from 16 to 81 years with a mean age of 45.87 ± 11.82 years. Among the 2512 patients, 2011 (80.06%) were ≤ 55 years of age, the majority of whom were female (n = 1981 [78.86%]). Tumor size was categorized as follows based on TNM staging criteria: T1a (n = 1594 [63.46%]); T1b (n = 666 [26.51%]); T2 (n = 230 [9.16%]); and T3 (n = 22 [0.88%]). Two hundred seventy-nine patients (11.1%) exhibited multifocal lesions. Concurrent HT was identified in 16.68% of the cohort, while preoperative subclinical hypothyroidism was noted in 4.86% of cases. A unilateral thyroid lobectomy was performed in 25.0% of patients, 38.3% underwent unilateral thyroid lobectomy with isthmus resection, and 36.7% underwent unilateral thyroid lobectomy combined with isthmus and contralateral partial thyroidectomy. All surgical approaches were only implemented after the following protocols in strict compliance with medical ethics: healthcare providers fulfilled the obligation of comprehensive informed consent with surgical plans and risks explicitly acknowledged via patient-signed informed consent documents; or patients proactively requested specific surgical approaches based on personal preferences, which were then rigorously evaluated and approved by the surgical team. Final surgical plans were confirmed and executed preoperatively under these dual ethical frameworks. In our institution thyroid function monitoring adheres to a TSH reference range of 0.3–5.0 mIU/L. The final TSH measurements of patients ranged from 0.00 to 26.36 mIU/L with a mean value of 1.21 ± 1.78 mIU/L. At the last follow-up evaluation, 81.09% of patients maintained TSH levels ≤ 2.0 mIU/L through TSH suppression therapy. Among patients with a TSH > 2.0 mIU/L, 96 (20.2%) self-discontinued medication, 257 (54.1%) exhibited poor medication adherence, and 122 (25.7%) received insufficient therapeutic dosages. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the extent of surgical resection: the Residual Half Thyroid Group (n = 1,590): Underwent unilateral lobectomy (628 cases) or lobectomy with isthmusectomy (962 cases), defined anatomically by preservation of the entire contralateral thyroid lobe with residual volume ≥ 50%; the Residual Partial Thyroid Group (n = 922): A unilateral thyroid lobectomy with isthmus and contralateral partial thyroidectomy, with residual volume < 50%.

Univariate analysis of factors influencing recurrence

The surgical approach had no significant impact on RT-RFS, as shown in Table 2. This conclusion is consistent with clinical data reported in prior studies. Ma et al. [16] reported that patients with a unilateral PTC and contralateral thyroid nodules (CN group) had a 5-year contralateral lobe recurrence-free survival (RFS) rate of 97.4%, while patients without contralateral nodules (UN group) had a rate of 96.8%. Statistical analysis demonstrated no significant differences in recurrence or survival rates between the two groups (P >0.05). As shown in Table 2; Fig. 3, Residual Half Thyroid Group: 97 recurrences (6.1%), and Residual Partial Thyroid Group: 49 recurrences (5.3%)。The recurrence event ratio was 1.98:1, approximating a 2:1 proportionality. This stratification strictly adhered to international thyroid surgery classification criteria, utilizing an anatomical volume threshold (≥ 50% vs. <50%) to define residual functional thyroid units. The recurrence percentage data further supported the correlation between residual tissue volume and recurrence risk. Chi-square test showed no statistically significant difference in recurrence rates between groups (P >0.05). Patients >55 years of age exhibited a significantly higher recurrence rate (7.8%) compared to patients ≤ 55 years of age (5.3%; Table 2; Fig. 1b). This phenomenon may be attributed to the senescent microenvironment, which promotes tumor progression and alters therapeutic responses through mechanisms, such as inducing stromal fibroblast senescence with secretory phenotype alterations, remodeling extracellular matrix structure and function, systemic chronic inflammation (inflammaging), and immunosenescence [17]. Studies have shown that age influences sodium-iodide symporter (NIS) expression, thereby affecting radioactive iodine uptake and therapeutic responses [18–20]. Molecular studies have revealed that genetic alterations, such as BRAF and TERT, are not only closely associated with tumorigenesis and progression but also exhibit significant correlations with age-related factors. Notably, older patient populations harboring the BRAF V600E mutation demonstrate higher cancer-specific mortality rates [21–23]. In the 8th edition of the AJCC staging system for differentiated thyroid carcinoma [DTC] [24], the age stratification threshold was elevated from 45 to 55 years. This revision underscores the critical role of age in the survival prognosis and recurrence risk assessment framework for patients with DTC. The recurrence rates were 7.3% and 5.4% in male and female patients, respectively (Table 2). Although males exhibited a slightly higher recurrence rate than females, the difference was not statistically significant (P >0.05). This finding suggests that gender may not be a critical factor influencing residual thyroid recurrence after PTC surgery, a finding consistent with results reported in a study [7]. However, Siraj et al. [25] reported that male patients presented with more advanced disease, exhibited greater tumor aggressiveness, and that male gender served as an independent prognostic factor for RFS.

Larger tumor diameter was associated with a significantly higher recurrence risk in this study (Table 2; Fig. 1d). These findings align with results from several studies. Choi et al. [26] revealed a significant positive correlation between tumor size and postoperative recurrence risk and demonstrate that contralateral lobe recurrence rates could reach 8.7%, further confirming that tumor volume serves as an independent risk factor for predicting disease recurrence. In this study patients with T1a PTC exhibited a significantly lower recurrence rate compared to patients with T1b, T2, and T3 tumors (3.4% vs. 7.4% vs. 14.8% vs. 40.9%, respectively). Patients with tumors >4 cm in size demonstrated a markedly higher residual thyroid recurrence rate than patients with tumors ≤ 4 cm in size (40.9% vs. 5.5%; Fig. 1e). Moreover, in alignment with the ATA [15] and NCCN guidelines [5]. Patients with multifocal tumors exhibited a significantly higher recurrence rate (11.1%) compared to patients with unifocal tumors (5.3%; Table 2; Fig. 1c). These findings align with current ATA guidelines [15], which classify multifocality as an intermediate risk factor, as well as numerous studies emphasizing the prognostic significance of multifocality in recurrence risk stratification [27, 28]. Interestingly, Omi et al. [29] found that patients with pathologically confirmed multifocal PTC undergoing non-TTx achieved a 5-year RT-RFS rate of 97.1%, demonstrating that the prognosis of multifocal PTC patients treated with non-TTx is not inferior to patients with unifocal PTC. Similarly, Huang et al. [30] reported a 10-y recurrence rate of 7.0% in the contralateral residual thyroid following lobectomy with isthmusectomy for unilateral multifocal PTC. And they suggested that lobectomy with isthmusectomy is sufficient for most patients with unilateral multifocal PTC with no need for total thyroidectomy.

HT had no significant impact on postoperative recurrence rates, as shown in Table 2. This observation aligned with previous research findings. Lau et al. [31] also reported that HT had no clinically significant effect on recurrence rates or overall survival in patients. A large retrospective study with 9 years of follow-up demonstrated that patients with co-existing PTC and HT exhibited significantly lower cancer-specific mortality and recurrence rates compared to patients without HT [32]. In contrast, patients with preoperative subclinical hypothyroidism demonstrated a significantly higher recurrence rate (Fig. 1f). Preoperative subclinical hypothyroidism was defined as preoperative TSH levels exceeding the institutional upper reference limit (>5.0 mIU/L) with free triiodothyronine (FT3) and free thyroxine (FT4) levels remaining within normal reference ranges. However, Ahn et al. [33] performed a retrospective analysis and concluded that elevated preoperative TSH levels were not an independent predictor of tumor aggressiveness or poor prognosis in PTC.

Patients with postoperative TSH suppression therapy levels ≤ 2.0 mIU/L exhibited a recurrence rate of 4.9% compared to 9.9% in patients with TSH levels >2.0 mIU/L, demonstrating a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05; Table 2; Fig. 1g). TSH suppression therapy reduces recurrence risk by lowering TSH levels, thereby diminishing the stimulatory effects on thyroid cancer cells [34] Patients with TSH suppression therapy levels ≤ 2.0 mIU/L achieved better TSH control, effectively inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and exhibiting a relatively lower recurrence risk. Among 475 patients with TSH levels >2.0 mIU/L, 14 had recurrences due to treatment discontinuation, 21 due to irregular medication adherence, and 12 due to insufficient medication dosage. Therefore, PTC management should be individualized, integrating recurrence risk stratification, patient age, and co-morbidities to optimize TSH targets, thereby balancing tumor control and treatment-related toxicities [35].

In this study (Table 3), the recurrent laryngeal nerve injury rate was calculated to be 0.6%, a result comparable to those reported in previous studies [36, 37]. Notably, the rate of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury in reoperative surgeries was 1.4%. Additionally, hypocalcemia is one of the most common complications following thyroidectomy. This study revealed a permanent hypocalcemia rate of 0.8%. It is noteworthy that most studies in the literature [38] have found that hemithyroidectomy and isthmusectomy, which preserve most of the thyroid and parathyroid glands, carry no risk of hypocalcemia. In contrast, the rate of permanent hypocalcemia in reoperative surgeries was 4.1%. Multivariate analysis of factors influencing residual thyroid recurrence

Variables demonstrating statistically significant impact in the univariate analysis were selected for multivariate analysis using a logistic regression model.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that tumor size, multifocality, and elevated postoperative TSH levels were independent risk factors for residual thyroid recurrence following PTC surgery (Table 4). A stepwise increase in recurrence risk was observed for T1b, T2, and T3 tumors (OR = 2.253, 5.053, and 19.452, respectively; all P < 0.001), indicating a positive correlation between tumor volume and recurrence risk. Patients with multifocal tumors had a significantly elevated recurrence risk (OR = 1.908; P = 0.005), while patients with postoperative TSH levels > 2.0 mIU/L had a 2.19-fold increased risk (P < 0.001), which was likely associated with inadequate TSH suppression. Although preoperative subclinical hypothyroidism did not reach statistical significance (OR = 1.84; P = 0.059), the potential risk necessitates clinical consideration. Notably, age > 55 y showed no independent impact (P = 0.165), as shown in Table 5, the two groups were well-matched in terms of age, with no significant difference observed. This favorable outcome indicates that the potential confounding effect of age had been effectively controlled for prior to comparing “size” and “TSH”. Discussion

This single-center cohort study confirmed that multifocality, tumor size, and postoperative TSH suppression levels are independent drivers of residual thyroid recurrence, which aligns closely with the ATA guidelines [15] consensus on “intrathyroidal dissemination risk” associated with multifocality. However, neither the surgical extent of non -TTx, nor age, gender, preoperative subclinical hypothyroidism, and coexisting Hashimoto’s thyroiditis are independent driving factors, and confirmed that there is no significant association between residual thyroid volume and postoperative recurrence risk. In global studies on conformal thyroidectomy, Chen et al. [11] found that for low-risk papillary thyroid microcarcinoma, conformal thyroidectomy and unilateral lobectomy showed no significant difference in 10-year event-free survival rates (87.9% vs. 91.7%, P = 0.776). Hyun Joo Nahm et al. [12], showed that for papillary thyroid microcarcinoma (PTMC), conformal thyroidectomy was superior to traditional unilateral lobectomy in terms of oncological safety and postoperative complications, and could be used as an alternative treatment option for selected patients. Based on this, in the present study, for unilateral, unifocal PTC with tumor size T1a, the 10-year residual thyroid recurrence rate in T1a patients was only 3.4%, significantly lower than that in larger tumors (T1b/T2/T3 recurrence rates were 7.4%/14.8%/40.9%, respectively). Additionally, the recurrence rate of unifocal tumors (5.3%) was significantly lower than that of multifocal tumors (9.7%), and multifocality was an independent risk factor (OR = 1.908). The surgical extent was not an independent driver of recurrence, and there was no significant correlation between residual thyroid volume and recurrence, indicating that preserving part of the thyroid does not increase the risk of recurrence. The study data also showed that TSH suppression therapy (≤ 2.0 mIU/L) after surgery could further reduce the recurrence risk in T1a patients (recurrence rate 4.9% vs. 9.9%). In summary, given the indolent biological behavior of unilateral unifocal T1a papillary carcinoma, non-TTx can ensure tumor control (10-year RT-RFS rates reaches 96.6%). Conformal thyroidectomy, as a refined practice of non-TTx, can control recurrence through local resection, and preserving thyroid tissue can optimize patients’ quality of life. Therefore, this study concludes that conformal thyroidectomy is feasible for patients with unilateral unifocal T1a PTC. Other stages, due to high tumor burden and recurrence risk, unilateral thyroid lobectomy with isthmus resection alone may suffice to control local recurrence risks and total thyroidectomy should not be performed unless necessary, thus conformal thyroidectomy is not indicated. Additionally, rigorous management of postoperative TSH suppression therapy is critical to avoid inadequate suppression, which may elevate recurrence risk.

This study revealed an overall residual thyroid recurrence rate of 5.8% in patients after PTC surgery with a long-term follow-up of 5–10 y, including a 5-y recurrence rate of 4.6% and a 10-y recurrence rate of 5.8%. Of note, discrepancies in recurrence outcomes were observed compared to relevant studies [7, 8, 13, 14, 16, 26, 30]. According to analyses of studies on PTC of varying scales, the residual thyroid recurrence rate ranges from 1.8% to 8.7%. The current study specifically confined the surgical variable analysis to non-TTx cases, excluding those patients undergoing total thyroidectomy within a framework integrating traditional clinicopathologic factors and postoperative therapeutic parameters. To mitigate confounding effects from lymph node metastasis status and conventional risk stratification systems, lymph node metastasis was excluded from the observation parameters with a dedicated focus on local recurrence within the residual thyroid parenchyma as the primary endpoint. This design enables a more precise evaluation of recurrence-related factors following thyroid parenchyma-preserving surgical approaches. Through a 5–10-year follow-up period, the current study provides an accurate assessment of long-term recurrence risks in residual thyroid tissue after PTC surgery.

The current study had several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study, there was a higher risk of case selection bias compared to prospective research designs. Patients in this study were restricted to the Northern Anhui region of Anhui Province, China, resulting in a relatively homogeneous distribution of living environments, economic conditions, and cultural backgrounds, limiting the generalizability of findings to PTC populations from diverse regions and socioeconomic backgrounds. Variations in regional healthcare standards, diagnostic protocols, and patient-specific factors, such as genetic predispositions and lifestyle habits, may influence postoperative recurrence rates and associated risk factors but were not comprehensively addressed in this cohort. Although the sample size was substantial, the cohort was insufficient to fully represent the heterogeneous clinical spectrum of PTC patients. This limitation may restrict the ability to account for rare clinical subtypes or atypical presentations, thereby constraining the broader applicability of the results. Although the follow-up duration in the current study exceeded 5 y, PTC recurrence may still occur beyond this timeframe. Studies have reported cases of recurrence 10–20 y postoperatively, highlighting the importance of extended surveillance for delayed recurrence [7]. The limited follow-up duration in the current study may have resulted in missed detection of some long-term recurrence cases, potentially leading to an underestimation of recurrence rates and an incomplete analysis of factors influencing long-term recurrence.

Conclusion

This single-center retrospective study of 2512 PTC patients with 5–10 y of follow-up yielded the following conclusions. The overall residual thyroid recurrence rate after PTC surgery was 5.8% with a 5-y recurrence rate of 4.6% and a 10-y recurrence rate of 5.8%. The analysis confirmed that multifocality, tumor size, and postoperative TSH suppression levels are independent drivers of residual thyroid recurrence. However, neither the surgical extent of non -TTx, nor age, gender, preoperative subclinical hypothyroidism, and coexisting Hashimoto’s thyroiditis are independent driving factors, and confirmed that there is no significant association between residual thyroid volume and postoperative recurrence risk. This study concludes that conformal thyroidectomy represents a clinically appropriate and feasible alternative to unilateral thyroid lobectomy for patients with unilateral unifocal PTC at tumor size T1a.
