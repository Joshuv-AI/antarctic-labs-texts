Labor migration in rural Nepal Arghakhanchi communities: impacts on left-behind caregivers and children
Authors: Sirjana Adhikari, Mahesh Prasad Joshi, Hari Rana, Sabrina Cheng, Theresa P Castillo, Peter Navario, Michelle Boyd, Keng-Yen Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren from migrant families with absent parents are more likely to have poorer physical and mental health than children from non-migrant families. The impact of labor migration on left-behind family members in South Asian countries is not well-known. This study aimed to examine the patterns of labor migration and its impact on the health and development of children and their caregivers in rural Nepal.MethodsBaseline family data collected from a school-based violence prevention program were utilized. Parents/caregivers (N = 346) with school-aged children (aged 3 to 15 years attending nursery to primary grades) from the rural Arghakhanchi district of Nepal were included in the study. A series of descriptive and chi-square analyses were carried out to explore the pattern of labor migration and differences between labor-migrant and non-labor-migrant families. Multivariate linear and logistic regression analyses were applied to explore the correlates and moderators involved.ResultsLabor migration has been a common practice in rural Nepal, with an estimated 49% of families having parents working overseas, mostly in India (57%) and Gulf countries (39%) on low-skill labor jobs. Labor migration was significantly associated with left-behind caregivers’ and children’s mental health. Left-behind caregivers in father-only labor-migrant families reported higher levels of depression than did parents in non-labor migrant families and left-behind children from labor-migrant families reported greater anger than did children from non-labor migrant families. The impact of labor migration on families was moderated by social class. For low social-class father migrant families, left-behind children were at greater risk for developmental delay and behavioral problems, but there seems to be a protective effect for high social-class father migrant families (with lower risk of developmental delay and problem behaviors compared to all other groups).ConclusionsLabor migration has a substantial impact on the mental health of left-behind families and children. The impact of labor migration may vary by living social-cultural context. Understanding the complex dynamics of labor migration has important implications for local and global migration-related health service planning.Keywords: Labor migration, Left-behind children and family, Mental health, Child development, Nepal, LMICs

Introduction

Labor migration in global contexts

Labor migration is a global phenomenon. Annually, millions of individuals relocate internationally in pursuit of enhanced social and financial security for themselves and their families. Approximately 184 million people —2.3% of the world’s population live outside of their country of nationality, and almost half of them are in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. A combination of factors, such as poverty, structural inequalities, lack of employment opportunities, and the need for higher wages, better economic opportunities, and recognition of skills abroad causes migration. Many individuals migrate to overcome adversity, natural disasters, violent conflict, and persecution to improve their quality of life [2].

Labor migration in Nepal

Migration is common in Nepal, occurring both from rural to urban areas and to foreign countries [3]. Nepal is one of the major labor-exporting countries in the world [4]. Labor migration to foreign countries has been an integral aspect of the socioeconomic status of Nepali communities since the Nepali economy has remained mostly based on remittances gained abroad by Nepali youth [5]. The international migration of low-skilled foreign labor plays a major role in Nepal’s economy, contributing to more than one-fourth of its total national income [6]. The 2021 census revealed that almost one in every four households (23.4% of total households/families) had someone living abroad [7]. Furthermore, more than 2.1 million (7.4% of the national population) Nepali citizens live outside the country. Analyzing the 2021 census using the average family size (4.37), it can be estimated that 5,257,413 people are left behind and most of them are women, children, and the elderly population, while a substantial portion of the working-age population, particularly men (82.2%), are absent from their home [7].

Nepal has experienced a significant change in its migration pattern in the last three decades [8]. Previously, migration was confined mainly to India, however, Nepali migrants have gone beyond India. There are now three broad migration streams: migration to India, migration to the Gulf States and Malaysia, and migration to developed countries [8, 9]. Currently, the second stream is dominant, which generates substantial remittances for Nepal [8]. Labor migration from Nepal is still a phenomenon dominated by men, with women making up less than 10% of the workforce [5]. However, with changing cultural norms, more women are joining the labor force. Poverty, unemployment or low wages, political instability, social pressure to earn more, and the desire to live a better quality of life are key factors driving labor migration in Nepal [6, 10–12].

Labor migration and impacts on Left-Behind family members

Although labor migration contributes to reducing unemployment and increasing economic efficiency in migrants’ home countries, transnational families are formed with vulnerable groups of left-behind children (LBC), women, and older people. Transnational families have problems with normal family functioning, often leading to family conflict, misbehaviors, and child neglect or abuse that has adverse psychological impacts on LBC and left-behind family members [13, 14]. These families, where family members of migrant workers are left behind, are becoming common in many LMICs [15]. The health and well-being of left-behind families may differ across contexts and are influenced by factors such as the gender of the migrant parent, culture, and family characteristics [15].

Moreover, labor migration has both positive and negative effects on left-behind family members [11]. Remittances sent by migrants directly support the survival of left-behind families providing them with better access to resources, improvements in the standard of living, and poverty reduction [16]. In Nepal, it is evident that the labor migration and inflow of international remittances have brought social and economic benefits to the country and the families of migrants [17].

Despite the economic benefits of labor migration for migrant workers, there is growing concern about the social and psychological consequences experienced by families who are left behind in their home countries. Labor migration substantially stresses the entire family [18], leading to significant disruptions in family structure and dynamics that raise concerns about the long-term well-being of left-behind spouses, children, and other dependents [10, 13]. Left-behind wives experience complex lives in the absence of their husbands along with increasing roles and responsibilities beyond the gender stereotypes [19, 20], likely leading to higher levels of stress. Left-behind family members are more likely to suffer from mental disorders than non-migrant family members [21, 22]. Studies from Nepal have also reported higher rates of mental health problems in left-behind wives than in those living with their husbands [23]. Furthermore, separated family members are more likely to experience loneliness, caregiving stress communication problems, and marital conflict [18, 23, 24], which remains underexplored in the context of labor migration despite its potential implications for mental health outcomes. Various factors such as frequency of communication, home return patterns, family support, and challenges influence the mental health and well-being of left-being wives [25, 26].

The international migration of parents also raises questions about the health impacts on LBC. These children are likely to experience both positive and negative impacts on their health and well-being. Remittances that are received from migrants serve as a direct support system for access to necessities such as nutritious food, education, and healthcare facilities, thereby leading to good physical health. However, the indirect impact of migration on LBC is predominantly negative [27]. Studies have reported poor physical health conditions, such as growth retardation in LBC [27, 28].

Parental migration negatively impacts LBC, leading to loneliness, isolation, sadness, and diminished academic motivation [29, 30]. The absence of either one or both parents adversely affects children’s social-emotional competence [31], increasing the risk of socio-emotional maladjustment and behavioral problems [28]. Compared to children of non-migrant parents, LBC are at increased risk of emotional and behavioral difficulties such as depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, conduct disorders, and substance use [15, 21, 22, 27, 32, 33]. These effects are more pronounced when both parents are absent [34, 35]. The absence of parents due to migration often leads to adverse childhood events that correlate positively with worse educational outcomes, feelings of abandonment, adoption of risky behavior, and mental illness. After parental migration, even though other family caregivers are present, the emotional needs of children are unmet [29]. More specifically, the absence of a mother disrupts children’s sense of safety, security, and sense of belonging [36], and maternal migration is notably associated with children’s depressive experiences than paternal migration [34]. Prolonged separation often disrupts parent-child relationships leading to psychosocial difficulties, especially for those facing multiple adversities like parental divorce, poverty, and grandparents’ frailty at home [37]. The impact of migration on the mental health and well-being of the LBC can vary based on several factors, including the age and gender of the children, the gender of the migrant parent, the stability of their care, and the overall living conditions [15].

Amidst the growing trend of labor migration, especially prevalent in developing countries with inadequate social support systems, understanding the intricate challenges faced by left-behind families remains a critical yet understudied area. Limited evidence exists on the specific impact encountered by these families, particularly in contexts like Nepal, where migration rates are high and the consequences on left-behind families remain ambiguous. The present study aims to fill this knowledge gap by investigating the impacts of labor migration on left-behind family members in rural Nepal.

Conceptual framework

This study was guided by the theoretical framework proposed by Schmeer [38], which suggests that the impacts of a parent’s absence on child outcomes can occur through four pathways related to family resources and functioning. These factors include economic resources, social resources, social organization, and emotions/health, which further influence child development and school functioning in either positive or negative ways. This study adopted this framework and examined the impacts of labor migration on families and their children in a rural context in Nepal (Fig. 1).

Guided by the conceptual framework, the present study included three research objectives.

To describe the patterns of labor migration in rural Nepal communities located in the Arghakhanchi rural district.

To assess the association between labor migration and four areas of family resources/functioning (economic resources, social resources, social organization, and health) and child developmental outcomes (Fig. 1 framework).

To examine whether the associations between parental labor migration and child development outcomes were moderated by child gender, child age (early vs. middle childhood), or social-cultural class.

To examine whether the associations between parental labor migration and child development outcomes were moderated by child gender, child age (early vs. middle childhood), or social-cultural class.

Methods

Study design and sampling

The study utilized evaluation data collected from a school-based mental health intervention study aimed at improving teachers’ capacity to support students’ social-emotional learning and mental health in the Arghakhanchi district, a hilly district that lies in western Nepal. Primary schools were identified through a regional school list. In collaboration with the District Education Office, 24 diverse primary schools from five municipalities (Panini Rural, Malarani Rural, Sitganga, Sandhikharaka, and Bhumekastan Municipality) in the region were selected for the evaluation study. A random subsample of about 15 families from each school (from preprimary to 6th grade) was selected for family data collection. The evaluation utilized a pre-post evaluation design. Baseline data were collected before the intervention which was carried out at the beginning of the school year, and the post-intervention data were collected about 6–8 months after the intervention. The present study utilized data collected at baseline (including all the study constructs listed in Fig. 1). To better understand labor migrant context and family connectedness patterns, we collected additional retrospective data about communication frequency and home-returning patterns of labor migrant families at post-intervention assessment. These data were used to characterize families.

Participants

The full study sample included 359 families. Children were not the subject of this study. Parents in this study were defined as biological birth parents or nonbirth adult primary caregivers who lived with target children and played a major caregiver role. For families with migrant-worker parents (who work overseas or out-of-town), nonbirth adult primary caregivers were recruited. Social parenthood, a practice of providing care, nurturing, and socializing the child in the absence of the child’s biological parent, often by relatives, is common in agricultural societies and is a widely accepted practice in Nepali labor-migrant families.

Of the full sample, 177 had at least one migrant parent, and 182 were non-migrant families. Among the 177 labor-migrant families, most were father-only migrant families (n = 171), and a minimal number of families had mother-only (1.7%, n = 3) or both-parent migrant families (1.7%, n = 3). Given our interest in studying the impact of labor migration on left-behind family members’ outcomes, when examining Objectives 2 and 3, we limited our study sample to families with labor-migrant fathers only (and whose left-behind caregivers provided data) and families without labor-migrant parents. Among the 171 father-only migrant families, 7 had demographic but did not have other-caregiver-reported child outcome data. Thus, we further exclude these 7 families from the Objectives 2 and 3 analyses. In summary, the full sample, 359 families were included in the Objective 1 analysis (to study rate and patterns of labor migration), and 346 families (father-only labor migration families and non-migration families) were included in the Objectives 2 and 3 analyses (study the association between labor migration on child outcomes).

Both analytical samples had similar overall characteristics. Overall, the mean age of the parents in the study sample was 35.88 years (SD = 11.45 years, range 18 to 78 years), and the majority were mothers (78.7%). Most parents (92.1%) were married with one-half of parents (51.5%) married at or before age 18. 67.9% identified as the child’s mother (14.6% fathers, 9.3% grandparents, and 8.2% relatives), and more than 64.0% had primary or less education. Most families (99.4%) were Hindu and 17.6% experienced food insecurity. On average, the number of household members was 5.76 (SD = 1.98), and they had diverse ethnic/caste backgrounds (33.3% Dalit and 28.3% Janajati or Indigenous groups considered lower social order; 16.5% Brahmin and 15.2% Chhetri considered higher social order). On average, the children were 8.85 years old (SD = 2.45 years, range 3 to 15 years), 52.7% were male, and all were enrolled in preprimary or primary school in the Arghakhanchi district. Procedure

As described above, parents were recruited from 24 schools as part of a school-based mental health intervention study in Arghakhanchi district in Nepal. Families were randomly selected from the student list from each school. School authorities and teachers facilitated linking the study team with the selected families/parents and supported the engagement of parents in the assessment and interview process. Primary caregivers who agreed to participate in the study provided verbal or written informed consent. For literate parents, written consent was sought, and a signed consent form was provided. For illiterate parents, a written consent form was given, and a literate witness (e.g., research staff, community guide) signed the consent form on behalf of the participant, which was then documented. After obtaining consent, the parents were scheduled for an interview in their children’s school by a trained bachelor’s or master-level social science researcher. Consent and interviews were conducted in the Nepali language (the mother tongue of Nepal). The Nepali version of the measures was translated and back-translated from the English version, and a team review approach was used to resolve any discrepancies between the versions. The study procedures and method of consent were approved by the Ethical Review Board of the Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) (Ref. 58, Regd. No. 64/2016).

Measures

The measures included in this study have been demonstrated to be reliable and valid for the Nepali population. The scale reliability (assessed using Cronbach’s alpha/α internal consistency based on the current study sample) for all the study measures is reported below.

Family resources and functioning measures

Economic resource-family hunger scale (using the Household Hunger Scale; 3 items; α = 0.88 for the study sample; if any item was rated as “yes” was considered food insecurity) [39] was assessed using the Household Hunger Scale, which evaluates family food resources and hunger status over the past 4 weeks (e.g., no food to eat because of lack of resources to purchase food; any household member going to sleep at night hungry, any household member going a whole day and night without eating anything at all because there was not enough food in the home). If any food resources or hunger issues were reported, the family was considered to have food insecurity.

Social support (4 items; α = 0.97 for the study sample) was evaluated using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support [40, 41]. The scale evaluates perceived support for comfort, sharing emotion, and needing help on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).

Perceived social class was assessed using the MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status [42, 43]. It has a numbered stepladder image that allows parents to rate how they think about their family on the social ladder/social hierarchy on a 1–10 point scale (1 = the people who are the worst off, have the least money, little or no education, a job that no one wants or respects; 10 = people who are the best off, have the most money, the highest amount of schooling, and the jobs that bring the most respect). Perceived social class is a valid measure, with supported construct validity. Specifically, we found low social class was significantly associated with poorer parental mental health (e.g., PHQ4 total scores, anxiety, and depression scores, with correlations ranging from rs = 0.13 to 0.26, p <.05), and higher food insecurity (rs = 0.15, p <.01) in the same Nepali sample [44].

Family Functioning

Four self-report measures were used to assess family functioning. Parental health was assessed based on parents’ perceptions of overall health, physical health, and quality of life (3 items; α = 0.88 for the study sample) on a 5-point scale (1 = poor, 5 = excellent) [40]. Parental mental health was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4; α = 0.89), which is an ultra-brief self-report consisting of a 2-item anxiety scale (GAD-2; α = 0.85) and a 2-item depression scale (PHQ-2; α = 0.87) that has been widely used and validated in many countries [45–48]. Parents rated symptoms on a 4-point scale (0 = not at all; 3 = nearly every day). Given the strong correlation between PHQ-2 and GAD-2 scores (rs = 0.68, p <.001), only the PHQ-4 total sum score was used for model testing.

Child social emotional health, development, and school functioning

Four tools were used to assess the children’s outcomes. The Social Competence Scale (12 items, e.g., “shares things with others,” “copes well with failure”; α = 0.76; [49, 50] evaluates children’s positive social behaviors, including emotion regulation, prosocial behaviors, and communication skills. Parents were asked to rate how well the statements described their child on a 5-point scale (0 = not at all to 4 = very well).

Three Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Pediatric Short Forms (PROMIS-Anger, Anxiety, Depression) were used to assess early symptoms of externalizing and internalizing behavioral problems. Parents rated how well each statement described their child on a five-point scale from 1 (never) to 5 (almost always). The anger (5 items; α = 0.78; e.g., “my child felt mad” and “When my child got mad, he/she stayed mad”) [51] anxiety/fear (8 items; α = 0.90; e.g., “My child felt worried” and “scared easily”) [52], and depression (6 items; α = 0.87; e.g., “Could not stop feeling sad” and “hard for my child to have fun”) [53] were used.

School functioning was assessed using two global parent rating items (i.e., complaints about school and having trouble with school) (2 items; α = 0.60).

Child developmental delay was assessed using 3 items from the Ten Questions Questionnaire (TQQ) (3 items, α = 0.61 using this Nepal sample). The TQQ was initially published as part of an international pilot study of severe childhood disability [54, 55] and it has now been widely used as a screening tool for childhood disability in LMICs [56]. Parents were asked to compare themselves with other children of his/her age and report whether the child has any serious delay in sitting, standing, or walking; has fit, becomes rigid or loses consciousness sometimes; or appears mentally backward, dull, or slow on yes/no options. For this study, we included 3 items (i.e., Did your child have any serious delay in sitting, standing, or walking? Did the child sometimes have fits, become rigid, or lose consciousness? Did the child appear mentally backward, dull, or slow?). Each item is scored as “Yes” or “No”. The screening was considered positive if any of the questions were answered with a positive response. The TQQ is valid for screening for disabilities [57].

Demographics

Information about parental gender (1 = female), parental age, education (1 = primary or less education), marital status (1 = married), employment status (1 = employed), early marriage status (1 = married before age 18), number of family members, number of children in the home, child age, child gender (1 = female), demographic factors, food insecurity, social support, perceived social class, and child developmental delay status was included.

Statistical analyses

To understand demographic differences between labor migrant and non-labor migrant families (i.e., whether labor migrant families have lower odds of being employed, married, receive higher education than non-labor migrant families), logistic regression analyses were conducted. To study the prevalence of labor migration and understand differences in demographic characteristics between labor-migrant families and non-labor-migrating families, we carried out a series of descriptive and chi-square analyses. To study whether fathers’ labor migration has an impact on left-behind family members’ health, mental health, family resources, and children’s health and school functioning, we carried out a series of regression analyses (logistic regression for binary outcomes and linear regression for continuous outcomes) that included parental labor-migration status as the predictor. We examined associations with and without adjustment for confounders (i.e., caregiver gender, caregiver age, and child age). We also considered potential moderation effects and examined whether the links between parental labor migration and left-behind children’s health and school functioning differed by children’s age, sex, or social class. For the moderation analysis, we built on the labor migration main effect model and added moderator and migration-by-moderator interaction terms in the regression model. We test moderation effects separately for each moderator.

Results

Labor migration patterns and contexts

To characterize labor migration rate and patterns in Nepali parents, we used the full sample (n = 359). Among the full sample, 177 (49.3%) families reported having at least one labor migrant parent, and 182 (50.7%) were non-labor migrant families. Among the 177 labor-migrant families, most were father-only migrant families (96.6%, n = 171), and a minimal number of families had mother-only (1.7%, n = 3) or both-parent migrant families (1.7% n = 3). To understand patterns and contexts of migration, we examined migration location and length of migration (using baseline data). We found that most migrant parents worked in India (57%), followed by Gulf countries (39%). Most migrants were working as Labor workers (28%), Hotel workers/cleaners and waiters (18%), cooks/bakery (19%), drivers (5%), security or police (6%), and company workers (9%), and others (15%). For the length of migration, 33% of labor migrants return home at least within a year, 37% of migrants, return home after a year but within two years of migration, and 30% of migrants’ families mention their migrant member returned after three or more than three years. The average migration duration was 2.51 (SD = 3.37) years.

To understand communication and home-returning patterns, we included additional questions in the post-intervention study assessment, and a subset of labor-migrant families (n = 99; 46.9% of the original sample) were able to provide data. Among these father-only labor migrant families, 20.0% reported returning home every 6 months, 36.8% reported returning home every year, and 30.5% reported returning home every 2–5 years. Most of the migrants stayed at home for more than 60 days (40.9%), 26.1% stayed at home for 31–60 days, and 18.2% stayed at home for 16–30 days.

As shown in Table 1, most children were cared for by female caregivers in labor migrant families, had younger caregivers (lower mean age in labor migrant families), and tended to have younger children. There were no significant differences in other demographic factors (e.g., religion, level of parental education). As shown in Table 2, we found that labor-migrant families also differed from non-labor migrant families in terms of parents’ perceived social class, level of depression, and children’s anger. As hypothesized, we found that labor migrant families perceived themselves as having a greater social class than typical non-labor migrant families. Left-behind caregivers in labor migrant families reported greater levels of depression than did caregivers of non-labor migrant families (β = 0.42, SE = 0.22, p <.05). LBC from labor migrant families reported greater anger than did children from non-labor migrant families (β = 0.92, SE = 0.45, p <.05). These findings were consistent in the non-adjusted and adjusted analyses (adjusted for the confounder caregiver gender, age, and child age, as identified in Table 1). Somewhat different from our expectation, we did not find a difference between migrant and non-migrant families in terms of food insecurity status, social support, parental physical health, or in terms of children’s social competence, school functioning, or developmental delay.

Moderation effects

To further understand whether the impacts of fathers’ labor migration on children’s development were influenced by moderators i.e. family social class, the child’s gender, or the age of the children (early childhood vs. later childhood), we carried out three sets of moderation analyses using regression models (controlling for the confounders of caregiver age, gender, and child age). We consider these moderators because previous literature has suggested these factors might influence the impacts of migration on health. In each regression model, migration status, specific moderators, migration-by-moderator interaction term, and 3 confounders (caregiver gender, caregiver age and child age as continuous variables) were included as predictors. In the child age moderation analysis, the dichotomized child age (2 groups) was included both as confounder and moderator. Table 3 summarizes the moderation effects for this analysis (only estimates for the migration-by-moderator variables were reported). Overall, we did not find child gender differences or age group differences, indicating similar impacts of parental labor migration on children’s outcomes for boys and girls or early childhood and middle/late childhood students. However, we found two social class moderation effects. Specifically, we found that the impacts of father migration on children’s school functioning and developmental delay differed by social class (see Fig. 2 for the moderation effect patterns). Specifically, we found that fathers’ migration had a more negative impact on children’s development (more likely to be delayed) among families who perceived themselves as having a low social class than among families who perceived themselves as having a high social class. The risk for developmental delay in low social class father-migrant families also appeared to be greater than that in children from non-migrant families (Fig. 2(a)). In examining school outcomes, we also observe similar patterns in labor migrant families. Fathers’ migration had a more negative impact on children’s school outcomes (more school problems) among families with low social class than among families with high social class (Fig. 2 (b)). However, contrary to our expectations, we found that among non-labor migrant families, children from high-social-class families tended to have more severe school problems than children from low-social-class families.

Discussion

This study explored the status of labor migration in rural communities and its impacts on left-behind children and families in the Arghakhanchi district of Nepal.

Labor migration patterns and contexts

Labor migration is a complex and multifaceted phenomenon in Nepal that is shaped by historical, economic, social, and political factors. Understanding the patterns and context of migration is crucial for developing policies and interventions to promote the well-being of migrant workers and their left-behind families. In the present study, nearly half of the families (49.3%) within the rural Arghakhanchi district were migrants. This figure underscores the substantial impact of migration on the community and highlights the importance of understanding its implications. In addition, one notable aspect revealed by the study is the disparity in migration patterns between fathers and mothers. Father migration was overwhelmingly more common, with 98% of the migrant families having fathers as migrants. This finding is consistent with Nepal’s most recent Labour Migration Report [5]. We found that most migrant parents worked in India, followed by Gulf countries on low-skill labor jobs. This is in line with the general trend of migration in Nepal [8, 9], reflecting broader patterns of labor migration observed globally, where individuals seek opportunities abroad, often in neighboring countries or regions with higher demand for low-skill jobs. The average migration duration was 2.51 (SD = 3.37) years. We found that the patterns of returning home and staying home were inconsistent among the labor-migrant families. Most of the migrants would return every 2–3 years, consistent with the previous study’s findings [25]. When they returned, most would stay for more than two months. Evidence suggests that return migrants alleviate the harm caused by migration and remediate the damage through various ways [58, 59]. The return of migrants positively impacts families by allowing them to spend more quality time together and communicate more frequently, which strengthens relationships and fosters a sense of security. Understanding these migration patterns is essential for developing targeted interventions aimed at promoting the well-being of migrant families and facilitating reintegration.

Labor migration and its influence on family resources, functioning, and child outcomes

Among the father-only migrant families, most children were cared for by female caregivers, particularly mothers. This observation highlights the important influence of the migration of men on reshaping families and the roles of left-behind spouses. Left-behind wives often take the bulk of caregiving responsibilities to maintain family structure and functioning. However, in addition to childcare, left-behind wives are expected to participate more in other activities outside the home [19, 20]. Compared to women in households with no migrants, women in households with migrants in Nepal have more responsibilities and decision-making [1].

Furthermore, as we expected, father-only migrant families have younger caregivers than non-labor migrant families and tend to have younger children. Younger caregivers may face many challenges in managing childcare responsibilities in the absence of their husbands or adult men in the family which could impact family dynamics and child outcomes. Studies have suggested that younger caregivers are associated with poor child outcomes in terms of grade repetition [60]. Younger caregivers are more likely to experience mental health challenges that are linked to adverse childhood outcomes such as developmental delays and physical and mental problems. Hence, it is imperative to focus on the needs of LBC and promote their well-being.

The findings of our study revealed significant differences between labor migrant families and non-migrant families in parents’ perceived social class, levels of depression, and anger in children. As hypothesized, compared to non-migrant families, labor migrant families perceived themselves as belonging to a higher social class. One potential explanation for this could be related to the positive effects of labor migration. The remittances sent by migrants support their left-behind families, contribute to better access to resources, and ultimately improve their standard of living [16, 17]. Left-behind families may compare themselves to non-migrant families and may perceive that their current situation has improved compared to their previous circumstances, leading to a sense of belonging to a higher social class.

We also observed differences in levels of depression between labor-migrant families and non-migrant families. Consistent with the literature, we found that left-behind caregivers reported higher levels of depression than did caregivers of non-labor migrant families [21–23]. Higher levels of depression in labor migrant families could be related to the unique stressors and challenges faced by left-behind families. Migration may affect a family’s economics, structure, and function [13]. It disrupts existing social networks and adds more responsibilities to left-behind families, increasing their vulnerability to mental problems.

In the present study, compared with children from non-labor migrant families, left-behind children from father-only labor-migrant families reported heightened levels of anger even after adjusting for parental age and other demographic confounders. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting greater levels of emotional and behavioral problems among left-behind children [15, 21, 22, 27, 33]. In addition, it highlights the intergenerational impacts of labor migration. The reason behind this could be related to the feelings of loneliness among LBC [29], as they struggle with the emotional impact of separation from their parents and experience anger and resentment as a natural response to family disruptions. Moreover, LBC experience abrupt disruptions in parent-child communication and disturbed emotional bonds leading to stress, anxiety, sadness, frustration, loss of motivation, and isolation [61]. This finding underscores the importance of having a comprehensive support system that prioritizes the emotional well-being of children and fosters a supportive environment that can mitigate the negative impact of parental migration on children’s mental health.

The results of our study revealed unexpected findings regarding several key variables, including food insecurity status, social support, parental health, children’s social competence, school functioning, and developmental delay, indicating no significant differences between labor migrant families and non-migrant families. This finding suggests that factors beyond migration status, such as family dynamics, social support systems, and diverse cultural beliefs and practices, may play a more significant role in the lives of left-behind families, which needs to be explored in future studies.

Moderation effects

We examined whether the influence of labor migration on family resources, functioning, and child development is moderated by child gender, child age (early vs. middle childhood), or social-cultural class. As we hypothesized, we did not find child gender differences or age group differences, indicating similar impacts of parental labor migration on children’s outcomes for boys and girls and early childhood and middle/late childhood students. However, we found social class moderation effects. As we expected, we found that fathers’ migration appears to be negatively associated with their children’s school functioning and development, as evidenced by a greater likelihood of developmental delay among families that perceive themselves as belonging to a lower social class than among those who perceive themselves as belonging to a higher social class. Specifically, the risk for developmental delay in low-social-class father-migrant families appeared to be greater than that among children from non-migrant families. The observed moderation effect highlights the complex interplay between socioeconomic status, family dynamics, and migration in shaping children’s developmental outcomes. The findings suggest that parental migration status may affect children’s development through family socioeconomic status and the home environment. Parental migration is high in families with low socioeconomic status [13, 62, 63], which eventually affects early childhood development [64, 65]. When the family’s socioeconomic background is low, it will be less stimulating and less favorable for children, resulting in detrimental effects on the child’s development [62]. Furthermore, the absence of a trusted caregiver, i.e., the father, may be less stimulating for a child’s growth and development. Socioeconomic disadvantages can exacerbate the adverse effects of parental migration on children’s well-being and development [66]. Families perceiving themselves as belonging to a lower social class may face heightened economic vulnerabilities and social stressors, which can amplify the negative impact of fathers’ migration on children’s development.

We also found a similar effect of social class on school outcomes in children from father-migrant and non-migrant families. Fathers’ migration had a more negative impact on children’s school functioning (such as complaints about school and having trouble with school) among families with low social class than among families with high social class. This finding is consistent with the literature showing that left-behind children are more likely to have poor educational attainment [63, 67]. These findings suggest that the social costs of family separation are largely reflected in school functioning. Educational attainment is closely linked to parental support and school engagement [68, 69]. Children with migrant parents are more likely to receive less adequate supervision and academic assistance and live in a less conducive home environment. Furthermore, parental migration can exacerbate the existing educational challenges faced by children in lower-class families. These families may already experience barriers to accessing quality education and limited parental involvement in school activities. The absence of a parent due to migration further disrupts children’s educational experiences, leading to irregular school attendance, decreased academic motivation, and lower educational attainment. Additionally, the stress and emotional burden associated with parental separation may exacerbate the risk of emotional and behavioral problems in children. The cumulative effects of all these factors might manifest in school-related problems.

Contrary to our expectations, we found that among non-labor migrant families, children from high-social-class families tended to have more complaints and/or trouble in school than children from low-social-class families. Typically, studies suggest that children from higher social class backgrounds tend to perform better academically [70, 71] due to various factors, such as access to resources, cognitive stimulation, and parental involvement. However, our findings revealed that among non-labor migrant families, children from high social class backgrounds exhibit higher rates of school problems than their counterparts from low social class families. One potential explanation could be related to heightened parental expectations toward their children performing better academically in higher-social-class families. This pressure may manifest in emotional and behavioral difficulties, negatively impacting school functioning. However, future studies are warranted to explore this phenomenon further and confirm these findings with better school functioning measures that capture more diverse domains of school functioning.

Strengths and limitations

In the present study, we explored the consequences of labor migration on left-behind families, addressing a critical social issue in Nepal. We compared the findings of the labor migrant and non-migrant families from a diverse socio-economic context in rural Nepal, keeping the vulnerable populations of children and families in focus which is often overlooked.

However, there are some inherent limitations worth noting. First, this was a cross-sectional study that cannot explain how the trajectories of left-behind families are affected over time by varying social and economic conditions. Second, the study measures were based solely on parental self-reports. There is a lack of alternate/secondary caregiver ratings or child self-reports. Some inconsistent findings might be due to reporter bias. In addition, most of our respondents comprise left-behind wives, so other caregivers such as grandparents are underrepresented in the sample. Caution for the interpretation of the results is warranted. Future longitudinal and qualitative studies could provide deeper insight into the lived experiences of left-behind children and families affected by labor migration, providing more insights into their support, coping mechanisms, and unmet needs. Third, a predominant migration pattern to India and other Gulf countries was observed, which could impact caregivers and children differently. For instance, migrants to India may visit home more often, while those who migrate to the Gulf countries tend to return less frequently. However, this has not been explored in the study. Moreover, comparative studies across different cultural and geographical contexts would enrich our knowledge of the diverse impacts of migration on left-behind families.

Study implications

This study highlights the multifaceted impact of labor migration on left-behind families and young children. This calls for comprehensive attention from policymakers, healthcare professionals, and community stakeholders. First, our findings highlight the need for targeted mental health interventions and support services aimed at mitigating the challenges faced by LBC and their caregivers. Additionally, our findings underscore the importance of considering family dynamics and socioeconomic context when designing policies and services to address the consequences of labor migration for left-behind families. It is essential to prioritize the mental health needs of left-behind women and children from low socioeconomic backgrounds and design mental health programs that address the unique challenges faced by this vulnerable population. Collaboration between government agencies, nonprofit organizations, and educational institutions is essential for developing comprehensive and culturally sensitive approaches to support children and families affected by migration.

Conclusion

The present study contributes to the growing body of evidence highlighting the negative impact of parental migration on child development and school-related outcomes. By recognizing the complex interplay of social, economic, and psychological factors, we underscore the importance of adopting a holistic approach to address the needs of left-behind families in the context of labor migration. Through targeted research, policy initiatives, and community engagement, we can strive to foster resilient and thriving environments for left-behind families to promote mental and physical well-being. Moreover, it is recommended to explore factors beyond migration, such as social support systems, cultural beliefs, and practices, that may have moderated the impact of labor migration.
