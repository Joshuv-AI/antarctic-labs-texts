Reconstruction of 2,965 Microbial Genomes from Mangrove Sediments across Guangxi, China
Authors: Ying Liu, Songze Chen, Huiquan Li, Nazia Mahtab, Yue Sun, Yunru Li, Jingjing Song, Dan Sun, Mingzhong Liang, Jianqing Chen, Jing Sun, Bin Gong, Jiaojiao Jing, Rongping Bu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMangrove sediments, being organic-rich and anoxic, host diverse and functionally important microorganisms that play crucial roles in global biogeochemical cycling. In order to characterize this diversity at the genome-resolved level, we collected 38 sediment samples encompassing both surface (0–5 cm) and core (up to 90 cm) depths from six representative mangrove sites across Guangxi Province, China. Using a standardized pipeline for assembly, binning, and dereplication, we reconstructed 2,965 non-redundant metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), comprising 2,383 bacterial and 582 archaeal genomes spanning 78 microbial phyla. This dataset captures the high microbial diversity and functional potential within mangrove sediments under variable environmental conditions. It provides a valuable genomic resource for investigating the structure, metabolism, and ecological roles of sediment microbial communities in intertidal, nutrient-rich ecosystems, supporting future studies on microbial adaptation and biogeochemical cycling in global blue carbon environments.Subject terms: Soil microbiology, Microbial ecology

Background & Summary

Mangrove wetlands are among the most productive ecosystems and serve as “blue carbon” sinks, extensively found throughout tropical and subtropical regions1–4. These ecosystems play a significant role in carbon sequestration, preventing coastal erosion, and enhancing water quality5,6. Characterized by rich nutrient availability, high salinity, hypoxia, low pH, and high redox potential, mangrove environments provide unique niches for the growth and metabolic activities of diverse microbial communities4,7–9. Microbial communities in mangrove sediments are important contributors to biogeochemical processes involving carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur3,10. However, the compositional architecture of microbial communities and their metabolic potentials across different mangrove ecosystems remain poorly characterized.

This study involved a comprehensive metagenomic analysis of surface (0–5 cm) and core (0–90 cm) sediment samples collected from six representative mangrove ecosystems across Guangxi Province, China (Fig. 1; Table S1). Through deep metagenomic sequencing integrated with genome-resolved approaches (Fig. 2), we reconstructed 2,965 non-redundant metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) after applying stringent quality filtering (≥50% completeness and <10% contamination). This dataset encompasses 2,383 bacterial and 582 archaeal MAGs (Fig. 3; Table S2), of which 27 satisfy the high-quality criteria (completeness > 90% and contamination < 5%, presence of the 23S, 16S, and 5S rRNA genes and at least 18 tRNAs) and 2,938 meet the medium-quality criteria (completeness 50–90% and contamination < 10%) as specified by the MIMAG (Minimum Information about a Metagenome-Assembled Genome) standard11.

The MAGs spanned 78 phyla, revealing remarkable microbial diversity within mangrove sediments. Bacterial genomes were predominantly comprised of Chloroflexota, Desulfobacterota, Pseudomonadota, Acidobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Planctomycetota, and Nitrospirota, while archaeal MAGs were primarily affiliated with Thermoproteota, Thermoplasmatota, Asgardarchaeota, Nanobdellota, and Halobacteriota (Fig. 4; Table S2). This high-resolution genomic dataset serves as a valuable resource for exploring the structure, metabolic potentials, and ecological functions of microbial communities within mangrove environments. It will be useful to decipher how microbial assemblages mediate elemental cycling processes (e.g., C, N, and S cycles) and respond to environmental gradients in intertidal, organic-rich ecosystems. Moreover, our results offer valuable insights into microbial variation and ecosystem functioning across different mangrove environments.

Methods

Sample collection and physicochemical analyses

A total of 38 sediment samples from 11 surface samples (0–5 cm) and 27 core samples (0–90 cm) were obtained across six distinct mangrove regions from August to September 2023. These mangrove sites mainly included Fangcheng Port Mangrove Natural Reserve (FCG), Maowei Sea Mangrove Natural Reserve (MWH), Shajing Port mangrove wetland (SJG), Xiandao Park mangrove wetland (XD), Sanniang Bay mangrove wetland (SNW), and Bei Sea Mangrove Natural Reserve (BH). Sampling locations are shown in Fig. 1. After sampling, the samples were immediately kept in a portable cooler and transported to the laboratory. Sediment samples were separated into two sets: one was stored at −80 °C for DNA extraction, while the other was stored at −20 °C for analysis of physicochemical properties.

A soil multiparameter recorder (GT-TRJCY; Guangtai Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.) with integrated sensors was employed to measure temperature, pH, salinity, and moisture. The particle size of the sediment was determined with a laser particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000; Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK). The CHNOS elemental analyzer (Vario EL-II; Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Germany) and an isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Delta XL Plus; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were utilized to determine total organic carbon (TOC), total nitrogen (TN), δ¹³C, and δ¹⁵N values. Following the protocols described by Liu et al. (2024), the concentrations of total phosphorus (TP), inorganic phosphorus (IP), and organic phosphorus (OP) were measured7 (Table S3).

DNA extraction and sequencing

DNA was extracted from 38 sediment samples using the FastDNA SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA). The extracted DNA was subjected to whole-genome shotgun metagenomic sequencing on the DNBSEQ-T1/MGISEQ-2000RS platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China), generating 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads. This sequencing effort yielded a total of 10.3 terabytes (TB) of metagenomic data across 38 samples, and the corresponding results are presented in Table S2.

Sequence quality control and assembly

All in-house codes used in this paper are available through a GitHub repository at https://github.com/SongzeCHEN/MetaGenome-MAG-Analysis, along with the workflow as shown in Fig. 2. Raw sequencing reads from metagenomic libraries were subjected to a series of sequential quality control (QC) steps. Initially, adapter removal and quality control were executed using fastp (v0.19.5) with the following parameters: -n 0, --detect_adapter_for_pe, --qualified_quality_phred 30, --unqualified_percent_limit 20, --length_limit 152, --length_required 70, --cut_tail, --cut_window_size 4, --cut_mean_quality 30, --correction12. Human contaminant sequences were subsequently identified and removed using BBMap (v38.92) with the parameters: minid=0.95, maxindel=3, bwr=0.16, bw=12, quickmatch, fast (http://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap), and the common sequence contaminants database used for filtering was hg19_main_mask_ribo_animal_allplant_allfungus obtained from https://zenodo.org/records/1208052#.X1hBFWf7SdY. High-quality clean reads were then assembled using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9)13. For individual samples, assembly was performed with the --presets meta-sensitive parameter. In contrast, for co-assemblies of samples derived from the same station, the --presets meta-large setting was employed to enhance the contig continuity of metagenomes from complex microbial communities. Contigs < 1 kbp were filtered and kept for further analysis using seqkit (v2.8.0)14. The contig continuity was subsequently evaluated using QUAST (v5.0.2)15, and the corresponding results are presented in Table S4.

MAGs generation, refinement, and analysis

Metagenomic contigs (≥1 kb) from individual DNA samples were binned using MaxBin2 (v2.2.7; --prob_threshold 0.3/0.5/0.7/0.9)16, MetaBAT2 (v2.15; --minContig 200/300/400/500)17, CONCOCT (v1.1.0; 2–3 flexible parameters adjusted based on MetaBAT2 results)18, and SemiBin219 (v1.5.1; single_easy_bin, --self-supervised) via BASALT (v1.1.0; --autopara more-sensitive -q checkm2)20,21. For co-assembled contigs, coverage depths were calculated using CoverM (v0.3.1; https://github.com/wwood/CoverM)22, followed by binning with SemiBin (v2.2.0; single_easy_bin)19, Vamb (v5.0.2; default parameters)23, and MetaBAT2 (v2.9.1) with varied parameter sets (--maxP 95 --minS 60 --maxEdges 200 -m 1500/ --maxP 60 --minS 60 --maxEdges 200 -m 1500/ --maxP 60 --minS 95 --maxEdges 200 -m 1500/ --maxP 60 --minS 60 --maxEdges 500 -m 1500/ --maxP 95 --minS 60 --maxEdges 500 -m 1500/ --maxP 60 --minS 95 --maxEdges 500 -m 1500/ --maxP 95 --minS 95 --maxEdges 200 -m 1500/ --maxP 95 --minS 95 --maxEdges 500 -m 1500)17, and MetaBAT2 bins were consolidated using DAS_Tool24 to improve bin quality25.

Metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were purified using MDMcleaner (v0.8.7)26, with their quality further validated via CheckM218. Dereplication was performed using dRep (v3.4.0)27 with dual quality thresholds (-comp 50 -con 10 -sa 0.99) during 99% average nucleotide identity (ANI) clustering. After dereplication, 2,965 microbial genomes were reconstructed; their genomic characteristics and chimerism were further determined using prok-quality28, incorporating three software tools: GUNC (v1.0.5; database version: v2.1)29, Barrnap (v0.9, https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap), and tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.6)30.

Based on the MIMAG standards11, the quality of the MAGs was categorized into two groups: high-quality (completeness > 90% and contamination < 5%, Presence of the 23S, 16S, and 5S rRNA genes, and a minimum of 18 tRNAs) and medium-quality (completeness 50–90% and contamination < 10%). Taxonomic profiling was conducted using GTDB-Tk (v2.4.0; https://github.com/Ecogenomics/GtdbTk) with reference database release 22631. For abundance analysis, metagenomic reads were aligned to quality-controlled MAGs using BWA (v2.2.1)32 and CoverM (v0.3.1)22 to calculate reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) and Transcripts per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads (TPM). RPKM was selected over TPM for MAG abundance quantification based on three key considerations: first, our study only generated DNA sequencing data (with no transcriptomic data), making RPKM a more appropriate choice given TPM’s inherent association with transcript-level quantification; second, MAGs are often draft-quality, containing missing genomic regions or chimeric contigs—this violates the core assumptions of TPM, which relies on accurate estimates of “total mapped reads” and “complete feature lengths” for reliable standardization; third, comparative analysis of RPKM and TPM profiles revealed a strong linear correlation between the two metrics (R² = 0.98), indicating highly consistent expression trend patterns that further support the validity of using RPKM for our abundance quantification.

Protein-coding sequences within the MAGs were predicted using Prodigal software (v2.6.3) with the parameter “-p meta”33. Subsequently, a set of 30 marker genes was employed for downstream analyses; this set comprises 27 ribosomal proteins and 3 RNA polymerase subunits. Notably, these proteins have been previously validated and benchmarked for application in concatenated phylogenetic analyses34,35. For the phylogenetic workflow, ribosomal marker genes were first aligned and then concatenated using the MarkerFinder (-m ribo_rnap, -n, -c)34,36. The alignment was refined with trimAl (-automated1)37 to eliminate less conserved residues. Phylogenomic inference was performed using IQ-TREE v3.0.0 (-m MFP -wbt -bb 1000)38, and the Tree obtained was visualized through Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL; https://itol.embl.de)39.

Technical Validation

Through deep metagenomic sequencing integrated with genome-resolved approaches (Fig. 2), we reconstructed 2,965 non-redundant metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) following a rigorous processing pipeline: first, MAGs were filtered using MDMcleaner (v0.8.7)26 to remove low-quality contigs, followed by quality validation with CheckM221—a tool specialized in assessing MAG completeness and contamination via lineage-specific marker genes—then dereplication was performed using dRep (v3.4.0)27 at 99% average nucleotide identity (ANI) with dual quality thresholds, retaining only non-redundant MAGs that meet criteria of ≥50% completeness and ≤10% contamination, and post-dereplication, taxonomic assignment of these 2,965 microbial genomes was conducted using GTDB-Tk (v2.4.0; reference database release 226)31 (which leverages a set of ubiquitous genes within the phylogenetic lineage of prokaryotic MAGs) while their genomic features and chimerism were analyzed using prok-quality28, with the final catalog including only genomes that satisfied the ≥50% completeness and <10% contamination criteria; these MAGs, capturing taxonomic and functional diversity across the sampled microbial communities, encompass 2,383 bacterial and 582 archaeal MAGs (Fig. 3; Table S2)—a distribution that reflects the typical prokaryotic community structure in the target environment, with bacterial lineages dominating while archaeal MAGs represent key taxa potentially involved in specialized biogeochemical processes—among which a subset of 27 meets the high-quality criteria defined by the MIMAG standard11 (exhibiting completeness > 90% and contamination < 10%, plus the presence of the full set of 23S, 16S, and 5S rRNA genes critical for phylogenetic classification and ribosomal function, and at least 18 tRNA genes essential for translational machinery) and the remaining 2,938 conform to MIMAG’s medium-quality criteria (completeness 50–90% and contamination < 10%); while these medium-quality MAGs may lack some structural genes (e.g., partial rRNA operons or fewer tRNAs), they still retain substantial genomic information to support analyses of taxonomic affiliation, functional potential, and ecological roles of the corresponding microbial taxa, and collectively, this MAG dataset—spanning high- to medium-quality tiers and covering both bacterial and archaeal lineages—provides a comprehensive genomic framework for investigating the functional ecology and evolutionary relationships of the microbial communities under study.
