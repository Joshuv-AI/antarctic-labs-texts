Pulse methylprednisolone-induced sinus bradycardia: A rare but must know association
Authors: Ashna Kumar, Pradeep Kumar Gunasekaran, Sarbesh Tiwari, Daisy Khera, Lokesh Saini
Published: 2024

Sir,

Pulse methylprednisolone is used as an anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive agent in a wide range of neurological, cardiopulmonary, hematological, and rheumatological disorders in children. The common acute-onset side effects are hypertension, arrhythmias, hyperglycemia, gastritis, psychosis, and raised intraocular pressure. Corticosteroid-induced sinus bradycardia is rarely encountered despite being widely used in children. Only a few pediatric cases have been reported in the literature.[12] We describe a case of pulse methylprednisolone-induced sinus bradycardia in a child.

A 10-year-old boy presented with altered sensorium and dystonic posturing 10 days after a nonspecific viral infection. At arrival, due to a poor Glasgow Coma Scale score of 5, he was intubated and mechanically ventilated. His serum inflammatory markers were elevated, and cerebrospinal fluid examination revealed lymphocytic pleocytosis with slightly elevated protein and normal sugar levels. Magnetic resonance imaging suggested patchy demyelination bilaterally, and the possibility of postviral acute disseminated encephalomyelitis was considered. He was initiated on intravenous pulse therapy with methylprednisolone. Thirty-six hours after the initiation of pulse methylprednisolone, he had bradycardia and hypertension. The raised intracranial pressure was ruled out by lack of clinical signs, no associated papilledema, and variability of heart rate (HR) with stimulation. His HR fluctuated between 35 and 50/min. A 12-lead electrocardiogram was suggestive of sinus bradycardia. Two-dimensional echocardiography was normal. His clinical status improved postpulse methylprednisolone therapy, but sinus bradycardia and hypertension persisted. After ruling out all other possibilities, including other medications that could cause bradycardia, these changes were attributed to administering pulse methylprednisolone. The score on the Naranjo algorithm (adverse drug reaction probability scale) was nine, indicating a “definite” reaction to pulse methylprednisolone.

The common corticosteroid-induced arrhythmias in adults are atrial fibrillation, premature atrial contractions, sinus tachycardia, and premature ventricular contractions. Although the exact mechanism of corticosteroid-induced sinus bradycardia is not established, the postulated mechanisms based on animal studies are direct action on the electron exchange in the myocardial membrane, alterations in sinoatrial node sensitivity to catecholamines, sudden electrolyte shifts, and changes in sodium and water physiology.[234] This side effect is well tolerated and benign without significant consequences, unlike the common effects of corticosteroids.[5] The predisposing factors are rapid infusion, underlying kidney, and heart disease.[3] The bradycardia risk is similar to both oral and intravenous administration of pulse corticosteroids.[2] The familiarity with corticosteroid-induced sinus bradycardia is clinically significant and must be considered a differential in patients treated with pulse corticosteroids to avoid unwarranted investigations and overtreatment.
