Evaluation of the efficacy of amendment types and rates in reducing ammonia emissions from broiler litter
Authors: Sabina Poudel, Debolina Chakraborty, Rishi Prasad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Several amendments have been used to reduce ammonia (NH3) emissions from broiler litter (BL); however, a comparative study between amendments and their application rates has not been fully explored. This study evaluated the potential of biochar (B), zeolite (Z), Flue Gas Desulphurization-Gypsum (FGD-G), and sodium bisulfate (S) at four application rates in reducing NH3 emissions from BL. The treatments comprised of amendment types (4) and their application rates (4), and a control with no amendment for a total of 17 treatments replicated twice and arranged in a completely randomized design. The treatments were incubated at 30 °C for 40 days at a moisture content of 40% (w/w), and NH3 emissions were measured every day for the first 10 days and 3 days intervals afterward for 40 days. Results showed that the application of 13 and 17% B (w/w) reduced cumulative NH3 emissions by 41 and 46%, respectively, compared to control over a 40-day period. Zeolite application at 8 and 11% reduced NH3 by 20 and 33%, respectively. There was no significant difference between the different rates of FGD-G, and they were generally less effective; however, a 15% FGD-G rate reduced NH3 by 9.1%. Application of S at rates of 2, 4, 6, and 7% significantly reduced NH3 emissions by 91, 99, 100, and 100 %, respectively. The effectiveness of amendments to reduce ammonia emissions followed the order: S > B > Z > FGD-G. These findings contribute to an ongoing effort to identify non-acidic amendments to minimize NH3 emissions in broiler houses.

Introduction

The United States (US) is the largest poultry producer, consumer, and exporter of poultry products (meat and eggs) (USDA-ERS, 2023). The US poultry industry produced about 9.17 billion broilers in 2022 (USDA-NASS, 2023) and generated approximately 12.6 million metric tons of broiler litter (BL) (Qian., 2019). A major challenge poultry producers face within broiler houses is the excessive production of ammonia (NH3). An increase in NH3 concentration inside the poultry houses causes a negative impact on the chick growth rate, watery eyes, respiratory infections, poor feed efficiency, and smaller egg sizes (David et al., 2015; Swelum et al., 2021; Yi et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2020). Additionally, the NH3 emissions during manure storage reduce the manure nutrient quality and have negative environmental consequences (Kangas and Syri, 2002). Anderson et al. (2020) reported that NH3 emissions from BL accounted 27% of the total atmospheric NH3 emissions in the United States, while Moore et al. (2011) reported 46g of NH3 is produced per broiler chicken. Ammonia concentrations above 25 ppm are detrimental to poultry production and the environment (Liu et al., 2021). Therefore, NH3 concentration should be reduced during production inside the broiler houses as well as during storage of BL (Bist et al., 2023; Carlile, 1984; Miles et al., 2004; Rylander and Carvalheiro, 2006).

Several amendments have been investigated for their ability to decrease NH3 formation and volatilization losses from BL or other manures. Previous research has reported the use of alum, alum mud, aluminum sulfate, aluminum chloride, phosphoric acid, ferrous sulfate, and potassium permanganate in the poultry house to reduce NH3 emissions (Moore Jr., 2016; Do et al., 2005; Choi and Moore, 2008). However, most of these amendments have acidifying nature and cause foot lesions in the bird as well as behave as inhibitors of microbial enzymes (Carlile, 1984; Moore Jr. et al., 1995). There is a need to identify new amendments that have better emission reduction potential and are less acidic. Here, we researched three non-acidic and one acidic amendments, namely Zeolite (Z), Flue gas desulfurization gypsum (FGD-G or G), biochar (B), and Sodium bisulfate (S), and investigated the role of their application rates in reducing ammonia emissions.

Zeolite is a non-acidic mineral compound known for its ion adsorption properties, hygroscopic nature, high ion exchange capacity, microscopic pore cavities, and large specific surface area (Šubová et al., 2021; Burt et al., 2017). These properties make Z a valuable mineral for various applications such as industrial and municipal wastewater treatments (Liu et al., (2021); facilitating the anaerobic digestion of animal manure (Wijesinghe et al., 2019; Ziganshina et al., 2015), and as a feed additive (Papaioannou et al., 2005). Additionally, Z has a higher affinity for NH4+ ions, and hence, we hypothesize that it can be used as an effective amendment for reducing NH3 emissions from BL.

Similarly, Flue gas desulfurization gypsum (FGD-G) is an industrial by-product produced at coal-fired power plants through the flue gas desulfurization process (Koralegedara et al., 2019; Kun and Xiaoping, 2019). The hygroscopic nature of FGD-G causes the absorption of excess moisture from litter (Watts et al., 2019) retarding the microbial activity and hence lowering the rate of NH3 formation (Tubail et al., 2008). While a few researchers have studied the impact of FGD-G application to BL on NH3 emissions, further investigation is needed to assess the effects of different application rates (Oliveria et al., 2003; Koenig et al., 2005; Mishra et al., 2013).

Recently, B has been introduced as a promising soil and manure amendment and has shown the potential to reduce NH3 emissions from manure (Janczak et al., 2017). Biochar is a carbon-rich substrate produced through the pyrolysis of plant biomass in the absence of oxygen (Dias et al., 2010; Janczak et al., 2017). The distinct physicochemical properties of B, including high pore volume, large specific surface area, and oxygen-containing functional groups, promote the adsorption of NH3/NH4+, thereby reducing NH3 emissions (Shin et al., 2024). Several researchers have reported on the effectiveness of B in reducing NH3 in manure and sewage; however, limited information is available regarding its application rate for reducing NH3 emissions from BL (Steiner et al., 2010; Janczak et al., 2017; Malin ska et al., 2014; Chung et al., 2021; Agyarko-Mintah et al., 2017).

Lastly, sodium bisulfate is a mineral-based acidifier and helps in pathogen reduction, NH3 control, and on-farm pest management (Pope and Cherry, 2000).

We found a limited number of comparative studies on non-acidic amendments and their appropriate application rates to control NH3 emissions. Therefore, our study aims to evaluate the potential of B, Z, FGD-G, and S at varying application rates to reduce NH3 emissions from BL. It is hypothesized that the effectiveness of amendments in reducing NH3 emissions will vary with the application rates. We tested this hypothesis by applying these four amendments at varying application rates on BL and measured the NH3 emission under controlled temperature and moisture conditions for 40 days.

Materials and methods

Broiler litter and amendments used in the incubation study

The BL used for the experiment was collected from a commercial poultry farm in Alabama. The farmer applied six inches of fresh bedding material (pine shaving) after a total cleanout and placed the chick. The broiler litter was occasionally de-crusted to prevent the build-up of moisture and pathogens. The farm had a grow-out cycle of thirty-three days for 2.2 kg birds. The BL was collected after two years of bedding material installation. Broiler litter samples were collected at several places inside the poultry house, including the waterlines and walls by using a shovel and bucket. The BL was finally dumped into a big tote and mixed well prior to collecting a representative sample for the incubation study. The moisture content of BL was determined by oven-drying samples for 6 h at 105 °C (Wolf et al., 1997). The selected characteristics of amendments used in the study are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The B was 100% wood biochar made from pine hardwood. The Z used in the study had 97% purity. The pH of B, Z, FGD-G, and S were 6.5, 7.0, 7.6, and 0.1, respectively.

Laboratory incubation

One hundred grams of homogenized BL was taken in an 8 cm diameter mason jar. The treatments comprised of four amendment types, four application rates, and a control (BL only) for a total of 17 treatments replicated twice and arranged in a completely randomized design. We were limited in number of replication due to the size of our incubator. Different rates of amendments were evenly spread on the surface of BL inside the jar prior to placing the jars inside the incubators. The treatment names are presented in Table 1. The incubator [KB240 UL (E6), Binder Inc] was maintained at 30 °C for 40 days. The BL moisture was maintained at 40% (w/w) to mimic the moisture content in the broiler house. The weight of the jar with BL and amendments at 40% moisture was noted prior to placing the jar inside the incubator. On each sampling day, the weight of the jars was measured immediately after removing the sponge. The weight difference was calculated, and an equal amount of water was uniformly sprayed into each jar to maintain the moisture level. Ammonia emission was captured by acidified sponge cubes (2.5 cm) hung inside the mason jar with thread without touching the sides of the jar (Fig 1). The acidified sponge to trap NH3 was prepared following the method by Paramasivam et al. (2009). Commercial quality foam material (thickness = 2.5 cm; Jo Ann Fabrics in Savannah, GA) was cut into 2.5 cm side cubes. These sponges were thoroughly washed using deionized water (DI) before proceeding to the acidification stage. The sponges were sequentially soaked in the following solutions: 1M H3PO4 followed by 1M KOH and DI. After soaking in each solution, the sponges were squeezed thoroughly to remove the excess solution. Finally, they were soaked in a mixture of 1M H3PO4 + Glycerol and squeezed. In the final step, 3 mL of mixture solution (1M H3PO4 + Glycerol) was added with a pipette to retain the solution in a sponge to trap NH3 produced inside the jars. To avoid contamination of acid with the BL, the sponge was hung inside instead of placing it directly above the BL surface. The lid was kept closed throughout the experiment and was opened only when the sponge cube was removed. The acidified sponges were replaced daily for the first 10 days, and afterward, they were changed every three days for the next 30 days, totaling 40-days incubation period. The NH4-N captured in an acidified sponge was extracted individually with 40 mL of 2M KCl solution and kept in a reciprocating shaker for 20 minutes, squeezed, and stored in 50 mL centrifuge tubes. The NH4-N was analyzed using an autoanalyzer (FIAlab Instruments Inc, Seattle, WA). Characterization of pre- and post-incubation treatments

The analysis was performed for pre- and post-incubated samples. The pre-incubated samples refer to the BL samples immediately after the application of amendments and post-incubated samples refer to the samples after 40 days of incubation studies. The pH was determined using a 1:2 ratio of sample and DI (Mitchell & Donald, 1999). Total nitrogen (TN) and total carbon (TC) content were analyzed using the thermal combustion method using a CNS Analyzer (Vario Max, Elementar Americas). The pre- and post-incubation BL samples were digested to determine the total digestible elements following Huang and Schulte (1985), where 0.5 g of dry sample was digested with 5 ml of HNO3 and 3 mL of H2O2 and analyzed in Inductively Coupled Plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES).

Re-verification incubation experiment

After completing the first set of experiments, we performed a re-verification trial on selected amendments. Biochar at a 17% application rate and S at a 7% rate, along with the control, were re-tested over a 55-day period, with samples collected at weekly intervals. The sample collection and analysis were performed following the same protocol stated above.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using repeated measurement, and Toeplitz was selected based on the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) value by the PROC GLIMMIX procedure in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute), where the treatment and days were considered fixed effects, and replication was considered as random effects. The mean separation was performed using TUKEY test in SAS. For all analysis, significance was given as P < 0.05. The graphs were prepared using Microsoft Excel 365.

Results and discussions

Characterization of the pre- and post-incubated treatments

The pH of the amendments used in this study is presented in Supplementary Table 1. The differences in pH immediately after the application of amendments (pre-incubation) and after 40 days of incubation (post-incubation) experiment are presented in Fig. 2. The range of pH for all treatments immediately after the application of amendments was between 5.8 and 8.2, whereas the pH after the 40 days of the experiment ranged from 6.7 to 8.2. For pre-incubation, all four rates of S significantly reduced pH (P < 0.05) compared to other treatments (Fig. 2). Similarly, for post-incubation, the treatments 6% S and 7% S significantly reduced pH compared to other treatments. Among the four rates of biochar, there was no statistical difference in pH between treatments and between pre- and post-incubation. Similar results were observed for Z and FGD-G. Among all the treatments, pH was significantly different (P < 0.05) for 3% Z, 6% Z, 8% Z, 11% FGD-G, 2% S, 6% S, and 7% S between pre- and post-incubations. The S application lowered the pH in pre-incubations. The primary factors contributing to NH3 formation are temperature, moisture, pH, and N content of BL (Moore et al., 1995; Ritz et al., 2004; Swelum et al., 2021). In our study, moisture and temperature were kept constant. The pH was the primary driver governing NH3 production (Merl and Koren, 2020) and therefore, the acidifying nature of S helped lower the pH significantly in our study. A study by Wood and Heyst, (2016) also reported similar results for poultry litter treatment (PLT), where pH was reduced from 8 to below 5 in BL. The study conducted by Janczak et al. (2017) stated that the application of B increased pH in BL during composting and was mainly due to the decomposition of organic matter, releasing CO2 and NH3. However, in our study, treatment with B had no effect on lowering the pH. This discrepancy in pH values between B can be explained by the B manufacturing process, primarily by the pyrolysis temperature, the presence of oxygen, and the feedstock used for the preparation of B.

We also measured the total N, C, P, and Al of treatments pre- and post-incubation (Table 2). The total nitrogen (TN) in pre-incubated samples ranged from 2.8 to 3.4%, whereas for post-incubated samples, TN ranged between 2.3 - 3.5% (Table 2). The total carbon (TC) content in pre-incubated treatments ranged from 17.3 to 28.3%, and for post-incubation TC ranged from 22.4 to 29.4%. The total phosphorus (TP) content ranged between 12.3 - 21.5 mg kg-1 for pre-incubation and 12.9 - 21.2 mg kg-1 for post-incubation. The total Aluminium (Al) content was higher in treatments with Z compared to other treatments as Z contains Al. The total iron (Fe) content ranged between 0.5 to 1.1 g kg-1 for pre- and 0.6 to 1.1 g kg-1 for post-incubation. The total calcium (Ca) concentration in FGD-G amended litter was higher (up to 86.7 g kg-1) than other treatments as it contains Ca. The total Mg content ranged between 8.6 and 11.4 g kg-1 for pre-incubation and 8.7 to 10.9 g kg-1 for post-incubation BL samples. Effect of biochar on NH3 emission

The cumulative ammonia emission from BL treated with four different rates of B, including control is presented in Fig. 3a. Over the 40-day incubation period, the average cumulative NH3 produced from control was 1.42 mg kg-1, which is the highest among all the treatments. The cumulative NH3 emissions over 40 days from treatments with 5% B was 1.2 mg kg-1, 9% B was 1.0 mg kg-1, 13% B was 0.8 mg kg-1, and 17% B was 0.8 mg kg-1. There were significant differences (P < 0.05) in cumulative NH3 emission between treatments on each sampling day over the 40 days. Higher rates of B (13% B and 17% B) significantly reduced NH3 emissions compared to control; however, there was no significant difference in the percent reduction between the two rates (Fig. 4a). Janczak et al. (2017) conducted an experiment to evaluate the effectiveness of wood B (0%, 5%, and 10% wet weight) in reducing NH3 emissions with the addition of wheat straw during composting poultry manure. They reported that the addition of 5% and 10% B to pine shaving during composting reduced NH3 emission by 30% and 44%, respectively, compared to control. Furthermore, Malinska et al. (2014) studied the effect of B addition on composting of sewage sludge (SS) mixed with straw and found that the addition of 4% B (by wet weight) + SS + woodchips reduced NH3 volatilization during the first week of composting. Steiner et al. (2010) conducted research on reducing nitrogen loss during composting of poultry litter (PL) by using biochar rates: (PL) + 0% BC (BC0), PL + 5% BC (BC5), and PL + 20% (BC20), (by weight) and observed a reduction of NH3 by 60% from BC5 and 64% from BC20, which indicates that higher rates of B application have higher efficacy in reducing NH3 emissions compared to control. Similar observations align with our findings, showing that higher application rates of biochar (13% B and 17% B) resulted in greater reductions in ammonia emissions by 41% and 46%, respectively.

Alarefee et al. (2023) conducted a study using rice husk B in composting PL with C/N ratios of 20, 25, and 30, resulting in NH3 emission reductions of 78%, 95%, and 97%, respectively, compared to the control. Their NH3 reduction percentages were very high compared to our study. The findings suggested that the higher rates of B are effective in reducing NH3 emissions from BL. Ammonia emissions from manures are reduced by B because of its adsorption properties and the presence of carboxylic groups (-COOH) formed by bio-oxidation at higher temperatures, which binds NH4+ and reduces volatilization (Dias et al., 2010). The properties of B and its potential to reduce NH3 emission from manures differ largely in literature and depend on the feedstock used to prepare B as well as the process involved in manufacturing it (Shrestha et al., 2023).

Effect of zeolite on NH3 emission

The cumulative ammonia emission from BL treated with Z at four different rates is shown in Fig. 3b. The cumulative NH3 emissions from different rates of Z were 1.3, 1.3, 1.1, 1.0 mg kg-1 from 3% Z, 6% Z, 8% Z, and 11% Z, respectively (Supplementary Table 2). Higher rates, 11% Z and 8% Z have higher efficacy in reducing NH3 by 32.5 and 19.6% as compared to control, whereas the lower rates 3% Z and 6% Z reduced NH3 emissions by 6 and 7%, respectively (Fig. 4a). There was a significant difference (P < 0.01) in cumulative NH3 emission between treatments with respect to control. However, there was no significant difference between the 9% Z and 11% Z treatments.

A study by Li et al. (2008) used 2.5, 5.0, and 10.0 % Z (by weight) in hen manure and reported NH3 volatilization decreased by 36, 62, and 92%, respectively, within seven days from laying hen manure storage compared to control. Furthermore, in the same experiment with the same application rate over 14 days, the NH3 emission was reduced by 20, 50, and 77% compared to control (no zeolite). Wang et al. (2024) conducted an experiment with chicken manure mixed with straw at a ratio 5:2 (chicken manure: straw by wet weight) with the three application rates of Z, i.e., 0% (control), 5%, and 10%. They reported that the addition of 5% and 10% Z had a reduction of NH3 by 15% and 28%, respectively, compared to the control during composting of chicken manure. Three different rates of zeolite, low (183 g m-2), medium (366 g m-2), and high (732 g m-2) were applied to PL for a three-week experimental period. The result showed a reduction in NH3 emissions by 28%, 39%, and 62% from low, medium, and high rates, respectively, compared to control (Lin, 2014). Research conducted by Awasthi et al. (2016) indicated that combining Z with 1% lime can lower pH and significantly decrease NH3 emissions. In addition to this, Z (88 g by weight), when mixed with chopped straw and pig slurry, reduced greater than 90% of N loss during composting of pig slurry (Bernal et al., 1993). In a separate study by Wang et al. (2012), the impact of various amendments on gas emissions from duck manure was examined, revealing that adding 10% zeolite (on a dry weight basis) led to a 36.0% reduction in cumulative NH3 emissions over a 46-day study period compared to the control. The differences in results could be mainly due to differences in manure types, moisture levels, and application rates. The mechanism by which Z works is due its hygroscopic nature, microbial inhibiting properties, and chemical three-dimensional tetrahedral structure with four oxygen atoms shared by two tetrahedra (Schneider et al., 2017). The shared oxygen carries a partial negative charge and exhibits a greater affinity for NH4+ ions (Baur and Fischer, 2023; Li et al., 2008), which minimizes the formation of NH3. This property allows it to be used as an amendment to reduce NH3 emissions from manure (Kithome et al., 1999).

Effect of FGD-gypsum on NH3 emission

The trend of cumulative NH3 emission from four different rates of FGD-G, along with control, is presented in Fig. 3c. Application of G at 6%, 11%, 15%, and 19% (w/w) reduced NH3 emission by 3.3, 1.1, 9.1, and 6.4%, respectively (Fig. 4a), with respect to control, but there was no significant difference in cumulative NH3 emission between the different rates of gypsum and control for all sampling days. Cumulative NH3 emissions were recorded at 1.4 mg kg⁻¹ for 6% G, 1.4 mg kg⁻¹ for 11% G, 1.3 mg kg⁻¹ for 15% G, and 1.3 mg kg⁻¹ for 19% G (Supplementary Table 2). The efficacy of G in NH3 emissions has shown mixed results in previous findings. A study by Oliveira et al. (2003) examined the effect of G and other amendments on NH3 volatilization from BL and found that G treated BL had the lowest NH3 emission (the quantity of emitted ammonia was 11.3 mg kg-1) during 42 days of the study period compared to other treatments. The addition of gypsum to dairy manure compost at a dry weight rate (w/w) of 0, 6, 13, and 23% showed that 13% gypsum was found most effective in reducing NH3 by 12.3% compared to the control (Tubail et al., 2008). Similarly, Iowa State University Extension Service (2004) reported that gypsum amendment in dairy manure storage reduced ammonia loss by 14.0%. A laboratory study was conducted by Mishra et al. (2013) by applying FGD-G on the soil where broiler litter (BL) + 0 FGD-G g-1 and BL + 0.25 g FGD-G g-1 litter applied on the soil surface at a constant temperature of 25 °C throughout the 63 days of the experiment period. The results from this study reported that G has no significant effect on NH3 emission compared to the control. However, the study by Burt et al. (2017) used 0, 20%, and 40% of G in 10 g of BL based on BL wet weight in an incubation experiment for 21 days and found a noticeable reduction in NH3 emission between 22 to 28% compared to control. Although there was no significant reduction in NH3 using G in many of the findings, the differences were due to the different compositions of regular agricultural gypsum and FGD-G. The addition of gypsum to litter leads to the formation of calcium carbonate precipitates through the reaction between gypsum-derived calcium and bicarbonates in the litter, which results in an immediate decrease in pH that can hinder NH3 formation (Burt et al., 2021). Additionally, the hygroscopic nature can absorb moisture from manure and retards microbial activity associated with nitrogen mineralization, thus potentially lowering NH3 emissions (Mishra et al., 2013; Oliveira et al., 2003). Additionally, further research is needed to explore the potential characteristics of FGD-G as an amendment for reducing NH3.

Effect of sodium bisulfate on NH3 emission

The trends of cumulative ammonia emission from the four different rates of S and control are presented in Fig. 3d. For the 40-day experiment, the cumulative NH3 emissions for 2% S, 4% S, 6% S, and 7% S were 0.13, 0.02, 0.01, and 0.003 mg kg-1, respectively (Supplementary Table 2). We found that all four rates of S significantly reduced NH3 emissions and were highly effective compared to control and other treatments. Higher application rates were more effective in reducing NH3 loss, with 2%, 4%, 6%, and 7% (w/w) of S resulting in reductions of NH3 emissions by 90.6%, 98.7%, 99.7%, and 99.8%, respectively. (Fig. 4a). This indicated that even the lower rate of S has the potential to reduce NH3 emissions above 90%. The lower rate of 2% S showed the potential to reduce NH3 initially for up to a week and afterward, the emission started increasing. However, the higher application rates have the potential to inhibit NH3 production throughout the study period. There was a significant difference (P < 0.05) in cumulative NH3 emission between treatments compared to control. However, there was no statistical difference between 6% S and 7% S (w/w). A study by Pope and Cherry (2000) found that S applied at a rate of 0.24 kg m-2 lowered NH3 concentrations by up to 55% over a 2-week study period. Similarly, another research by Hong Li et al. (2006) reported that S applied at rates of 0.5 to 1.5 kg m-2 reduced NH3 emission by 74 to 92% during 7 days. The NH3 emission immediately after applying S dropped to 72%, and the overall reduction was 57%. However, the effectiveness of S after 11 days was reduced (Wood and Heyst, 2016). This indicates that S reduced NH3 emission largely in the initial days, and the potential to reduce NH3 is reduced with an extended period of the experiment.

The mechanism of S in reducing NH3 emission from manure is mainly governed by its chemical composition and capacity to lower pH. Sodium bisulfate, which is an acidifier, breaks into sodium, hydrogen, and sulfate ions when applied to litter. The hydrogen ions help to lower the pH and bind ammonium ions, thereby preventing the formation of NH3 (Li et al., 2008; Terzich et al., 1998). Previous research has reported that the concentration of NH3 is low when pH is lower than 7 (Blake et al., 2009; Reece et al., 1980). Li et al. (2013) studied the effectiveness of treating S at two rates, 183 g m-2 and 366 g m-2 applied on BL on a weekly basis and found a reduction in cumulative NH3 emission ranging between 14% to 65% and 0 to 55%, respectively, in laboratory conditions. Further, they conducted a field experiment to quantify and delineate the efficacies of frequent litter amendment application with 244 g m-2 per 2 weeks in a three-flock period. The cumulative NH3 emission was reduced by 52% in a field setup. Sahoo et al. (2017) found that the addition of S at the rate of 25 g per sq. ft. was found more effective in lowering pH, microbial load, and ambiance in broiler houses compared to alum (at the rate of 90 g alum per sq. ft.). Additionally, Blake et al. (2009) also reported that using S as a litter amendment at three different application rates namely 24.4, 48.8, and 73.2 kg per 100 m², effectively reduced NH3 emissions, particularly at higher rates. Fairchild et al., (2006) experimented with six commercial broiler houses during colder months, applying S at rates of 50, 100, and 150 lbs. per 1000 ft2. Their findings indicated that the broiler house treated with 100 lbs. per 1000 ft2 experienced a 50% reduction in ammonia concentration compared to the house treated with 50 lbs. per 1000 ft2. A study by Lin (2014) experimented on mitigating NH3 emissions from BL in an emission vessel with the application of S at four rates ranging from 0, 183 (low), 366 (mid), and 732 g/m2 (high), and found that all rates of S reduced NH3 emission more than 90% on the first day for all the moisture levels (20%, 30%, and 40%) and from different application rates. Similarly, higher application rates of S (732 g/m2) at three different moisture levels reduced cumulative NH3 by more than 95.0% compared to the control.

Overall, our study showed different efficacies of amendments in reducing NH3 emissions from BL. The research conducted by Wang et al. (2017) studied the effects of B, Z, and their combination on nitrogen conservation and organic matter transformation during pig manure (PM) composting. They mixed PM with wheat straw (WS) at a ratio of 2:1 on a dry weight basis. They reported that among the three treatments, PM + WS + 10% B, PM + WS + 10% Z, and PM + WS + 10% (B + Z) decreased ammonia emissions by 35.8%, 31.1%, and 63.4%, respectively with respect to control. This indicates that the combination of amendments, B + Z, enhanced the potential to reduce NH3 emissions. Moreover, Febrisiantosa et al. (2018) had a similar finding where B alone reduced NH3 emission by 22.2%, whereas B combined with FGD-G reduced NH3 emission by 58.9% at the time of composting of livestock waste. Therefore, future studies with different combinations of amendments are needed to develop and identify an effective combination of amendments to reduce NH3 emissions.

Re-verification trial

We performed another set of experiments with selected amendments in similar settings to develop more confidence and draw conclusions from our 1st incubation study. Comparison between the 1st and re-verification trials for those selected amendments showed no significant difference between the treatments from the 1st and re-verification trials for 17% B but showed a significant difference for 7% S. However, the percent reduction by 7% S was 97.8%, which is very close to the value of the 1st experiment (99.8%) (Fig. 4b). The validation experiment showed similar results to those of the 1st experiment, providing us with additional evidence to support our results for the potential use of amendments to reduce NH3 emissions from BL.

Conclusions

This study revealed notable differences in the efficacy of amendments and their rates in NH3 emission from BL. We observed a general pattern of decreased NH3 emission with the increased rates of amendments. Among the B, Z, G, and S, all four rates of S demonstrated the highest potential for reducing NH3 emissions above 90% compared to control, followed by B, Z, and G. Although G reduced NH3 emissions up to 9%, the cumulative NH3 emission values were not significantly different from control, indicating its ineffectiveness in reducing NH3 emissions with respect to other amendments. This study has provided valuable insights into the selection of amendments and their rates for future investigations on amendments. These findings contribute to ongoing efforts to identify amendments of choice for poultry producers to minimize losses in poultry production systems and their environmental impacts benefiting poultry, human health, and the ecosystem. However, more investigation and implementation of findings at various scales are needed to address issues associated with NH3 emissions from manure.
