Accessibility to antimalarials in Spanish hospitals: analysis of a national survey
Authors: José I Bretones-Pedrinaci, María A Castro-Vida, Joaquín Urda-Romacho, Ana B Lozano-Serrano, Joaquín Salas-Coronas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the eradication of malaria as a locally transmitted disease in Spain, the incidence of imported cases continues to rise, with Plasmodium falciparum representing the majority of infections. Prompt access to effective antimalarial treatment, particularly artemisinin-based therapies, is crucial to prevent severe outcomes. However, the unguaranteed and irregular availability of these drugs poses a potential threat to patient management. The objective of this study was to evaluate the availability of antimalarial drugs and their procurement times in Spanish hospitals.MethodsA nationwide cross-sectional survey was conducted between September 2023 and January 2024. Hospital pharmacists were invited via the Spanish Society of Hospital Pharmacy to report on antimalarial stock levels and procurement times. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics and chi-square tests, stratified by hospital size and the presence of malaria treatment protocols.ResultsA total of 46 hospitals participated. Intravenous artesunate, the first-line treatment for severe malaria, was stocked in 74% of hospitals, but only 60% could acquire it within 24 h on weekdays if not already in stock or more doses were required. Availability was higher and acquisition times were shorter in large hospitals (> 500 beds) and in institutions with established protocols for malaria treatment. Artemisinin-based combinations, such as piperaquine-dihydroartemisinin, and atovaquone-proguanil were more accessible, but weekend acquisition remained limited.ConclusionsThe study highlights substantial variability and critical delays in antimalarial drug availability across Spanish hospitals. Enhancing access requires national stockpiling strategies, broader implementation of standardized treatment protocols, and improved procurement systems. These measures are vital to ensure timely treatment and reduce morbidity associated with imported malaria.Keywords: Antimalarials, Malaria, Hospital, Availability, Procurement time, Artemisinin-based combination therapies

Background

Malaria is the most frequent vector-borne infectious disease worldwide with an estimated 263 million malaria cases in 83 malaria endemic countries and areas, and 597,000 deaths in 2023 [1]. Malaria transmission occurs predominantly in Africa, Central and South America, Asia, and Oceania. Infections with P. falciparum and P. vivax represent the major part of the global burden of malaria. During 2022, 6131 confirmed malaria cases were reported in the EU/EEA. France reported the highest number of cases, followed by Germany, Spain, Italy, and Belgium. Since the eradication of malaria in Western Europe in the 1970 s, most of cases are imported from endemic areas, but sporadic locally acquired cases are reported every year [2].

Although malaria was officially eradicated as a locally transmitted disease in Spain in 1964, it remains a significant public health concern due to the rising number of imported cases [3, 4]. According to the 2024 report from the National Epidemiology Center, Spain reported 761 cases of imported malaria, the majority occurring between June and October. These cases predominantly originate from Africa, with 93.3% of infections imported from West and Central Africa. P. falciparum, the most virulent species, accounted for 92.5% of infections. Overall, 80.9% of patients were hospitalised, and six deaths were reported, yielding a case fatality rate of 0.8% [5].

The World Health Organization (WHO) and the main European guidelines recommend artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) as the first-line treatment for severe and non-severe P. falciparum malaria. For severe cases, intravenous artesunate is the preferred therapy [6–9]. The effectiveness of these therapies is closely linked to the prompt initiation of treatment, particularly in severe malaria, where the disease can progress rapidly and lead to life-threatening complications such as cerebral malaria, acute respiratory distress syndrome, severe anemia, and multi-organ failure. Given the potential severity of P. falciparum infections, timely intervention is critical to prevent complications and reduce mortality [10], underscoring the importance of robust malaria management systems in endemic and non-endemic countries. Early treatment is therefore crucial in reducing both morbidity and mortality, especially in people who lack semi-immunity. In non-endemic countries like Spain, where immunity to malaria is low or absent, delays in treatment can result in poor patient outcomes and a higher risk of fatality.

However, despite the established treatment protocols, Spain faces significant challenges in the timely acquisition and availability of essential antimalarial medications. Many of the first-line treatments recommended by the WHO, including artesunate and several ACTs, are not commercially available within the country. Hospitals must often rely on international procurement processes, which can result in substantial delays. These delays are particularly critical in cases of severe malaria, where every hour without treatment increases the risk of complications. The logistics of obtaining these medicines, especially in emergency situations, further exacerbate the problem.

Moreover, the lack of alternative therapeutic options adds to the complexity of malaria management. When first-line treatments are unavailable or delayed, healthcare providers have limited options to manage severe cases effectively. This shortage of alternatives can lead to suboptimal care, increasing the burden on healthcare systems and endangering patient lives. Ensuring a streamlined and efficient supply chain for these life-saving medications is paramount to addressing the challenges posed by imported malaria cases.

The aim of this study is to describe and analyse the availability of antimalarial medicines and their acquisition times in Spanish hospitals.

Methods

A cross-sectional survey was conducted to assess the availability of antimalarial medications in hospitals across Spain. The survey aimed to gather demographic hospitals data, current stock of antimalarial medicines, and the time required by hospitals to acquire these medications when they did not belong to hospital stocks. The study targeted hospitals in all Spanish regions. Hospitals that completed the survey were included in the analysis.

Data were collected from September 2023 to January 2024 using a structured questionnaire designed with Google Forms. The questionnaire was disseminated through the Spanish Hospital Pharmacist Society (SEFH), targeting those hospital pharmacists´ responsible for medication procurement. The survey was developed jointly by the Tropical Medicine Unit and the Hospital Pharmacy Service, agreeing on survey questions about key hospital and antimalarial data based on our national environment.

Antimalarial accessibility survey

Data were analysed using statistical software R Statistical v4.1.2; R core Team 2021. Descriptive statistics summarized the availability of antimalarial medicines and the average procurement time. Data were stratified by the number of beds and the presence of a malaria management protocol to identify factors associated with antimalarial availability. The chi-square test was used to compare hospital antimalarial stock and procurement times between different hospital categories, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 46 hospitals across Spain completed the survey and were included in the analysis. Since the Spanish Society of Hospital Pharmacy (SEFH) did not provide data on the total number of hospitals initially contacted or receiving the survey, it was not possible to determine a response rate. Notably, Spain has 258 public hospitals (excluding nursing homes and specialized clinics) and 241 private hospitals [11], resulting in an approximated response rate of 15.5% for public hospitals (40/258) and 2.5% for private ones (6/241). Figure 1 illustrates the geographical distribution of the participating hospitals, while Table 2 summarizes their main characteristics. Regarding hospital size, 20 (43%) had more than 500 beds, 13 (28%) had between 251 and 500 beds, and 13 (28%) had fewer than 250 beds. In terms of specialized services, 17 (37%) of hospitals reported having an International Health Unit or a specialized clinic for tropical diseases.

Hospitals´ characteristics

Concerning malaria admissions, the majority of hospitals reported having treated malaria cases in the last five years. Specifically, 12 (29%) of hospitals reported admitting more than 15 cases, 9 (22%) treated 11–15 cases, 9 (22%) reported 6–10 cases, and 11(27%) treated five or fewer cases.

A malaria management protocol was available in 22 of hospitals (52%) that responded to this question, indicating that slightly over half had established guidelines for the treatment and management of malaria cases.

Availability of antimalarial medicines

The stocking patterns of antimalarial medications varied significantly across surveyed hospitals. For non-severe malaria, the most relevant treatments included piperaquine-dihydroartemisinin, available in 70% of hospitals, and atovaquone-proguanil (74%). Overall, a total of 42 hospitals (91.3%) had in stock one of the first-line drugs such as dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine or atovaquone-proguanil (either one of them or both). Artemether-lumefantrine, recommended for pregnant women, was available in only 24% of hospitals.

Intravenous (IV) artesunate, the first-line treatment for severe Plasmodium falciparum malaria, was stocked by 74% of hospitals. Conversely, intravenous quinine was only stocked by 17% of hospitals. All centres that had IV quinine also had IV artesunate in stock. Additional details on other medications for severe and non-severe malaria are provided in Table 3.

Logistical characteristics and procurement times

Procurement times for key antimalarial medications, if not already in stock or more doses were required, varied between weekdays and weekends. For non-severe malaria, piperaquine-dihydroartemisinin was accessible within 24 h in 73% of hospitals on weekdays, compared to 56% on weekends. Atovaquone-proguanil followed a similar trend, with 75% availability within 24 h on weekdays, dropping to 60% during weekends. Artemether-lumefantrine was less accessible, being supplied within 24 h to only 33% of hospitals on weekdays and 25% during weekends.

For severe malaria, IV artesunate was available within 24 h in 60% of hospitals on weekdays, slightly decreasing to 56% during weekends. IV quinine exhibited lower accessibility, with procurement within 24 h reported by 33% of hospitals on weekdays and only by 27% during weekends. For non-severe malaria, piperaquine-dihydroartemisinin (320/40 mg) was stocked in 65% of the small hospitals (< 500 beds) and in 75% of the large hospitals (> 500 beds) (p = 0.48). Similarly, atovaquone/proguanil (250/100 mg) was available in 73% of the small hospitals and in 75% of the large ones (p = 0.88). Artemether/lumefantrine (20/120 mg), however, showed lower availability, with 27% of small hospitals and 20% of large ones reporting it in stock (p = 0.73).

For severe malaria, stocking of artesunate IV was significantly different between small and large hospitals: 58% compared to 95% respectively (p = 0.004). Quinine (500 mg IV) showed a less consistent pattern, being available in 27% of small hospitals and only in 5% of large ones (p = 0.11).

Regarding procurement times, for non-severe malaria, piperaquine-dihydroartemisinin (320/40 mg) could be obtained within 24 h on weekdays by 72% of the small hospitals and 70% of the large ones (p = 0.71). On weekends, figures decreased to 47% for small hospitals but increased to 75% for large hospitals (p = 0.10). Atovaquone/proguanil (250/100 mg) was supplied within 24 h on weekdays to 76% of small hospitals and 70% of the large (p = 0.48), while weekend availability dropped to 47% for small hospitals and remained stable for larger hospitals (75%), (p = 0.26). Artemether/lumefantrine (20/120 mg) was available within 24 h on weekdays for 35% of small hospitals and 25% of large ones (p = 0.27), with weekend availability further decreasing to 32% and 18% respectively (p = 0.68).

Artesunate IV was available within 24 h on weekdays for 46% of small hospitals and 65% of large hospitals (p = 0.49), with weekend availability reported by 53% of small hospitals and 67% of large ones (p = 0.36). Quinine (500 mg IV) could be procured within 24 h on weekdays in 35% of small hospitals and 25% of large hospitals (p = 0.27), while weekend availability was similar, 26% in small hospitals and 27% in large hospitals (p > 0.99).

Stratification by malaria management protocol

The availability of antimalarial medications was analysed based on whether hospitals had an established consensus malaria management protocol in place. Significant differences were observed in the stockage of certain antimalarials between hospitals with and without such a protocol.

For non-severe malaria, piperaquine-dihydroartemisinin (320/40 mg) was available in 55% of hospitals without a protocol and in 77% of hospitals with a protocol (p = 0.13). Atovaquone/proguanil (250/100 mg) was stocked in 70% of hospitals without a protocol and 82% of those with a protocol (p = 0.48). Artemether/lumefantrine (20/120 mg) showed a striking difference, not being available in the stockage of any hospital without a malaria protocol compared to 50% of hospitals with a protocol (p < 0.001).

For severe malaria, 50% of hospitals without a protocol had artesunate IV in stock compared to 95% of hospital with a malaria management protocol (p < 0.001). Quinine IV (500 mg) was available in 10% of hospitals without a protocol and 27% of hospitals with a protocol (p = 0.24). There was also a direct relationship between the number of malaria cases managed in the hospital and the existence of a diagnostic and treatment protocol for the disease. In our study, the 22 centres with such a protocol had attended at least between 6–10 malaria cases over the past five years. Discussion

This study underscores the significant variability in the availability and stoking pattern of antimalarial treatments across those Spanish hospitals responding to the survey. Treatments based on artemisinin derivatives such as piperaquine-dihydroartemisinin are in stock in vast majority of the large hospitals, whereas just over half of the small hospitals reported immediate availability of these therapies. This disparity highlights the influence of hospital size on preparedness. Furthermore, almost less than half of the small hospitals lacked atovaquone-proguanil for immediate use. While the rapid procurement of both artemisinin derivatives and atovaquone-proguanil is generally possible during weekdays, the situation becomes critical on weekends. Only one half of the hospitals could obtain piperaquine-dihydroartemisinin within 24 h, and a similar pattern was observed for atovaquone-proguanil. This limitation is particularly concerning, as delayed administration of appropriate treatments increases the risk of progression of Plasmodium falciparum malaria to severe forms, especially in non-immune patients.

This survey shows considerable variability in the availability of different antimalarial drugs. Regarding hydroxychloroquine, nearly all hospitals reported having the drug in stock. Although this drug still shows good sensitivity against non-falciparum Plasmodium species in large regions of the world, the widespread resistance of P. falciparum to these agents renders them ineffective as antimalarial treatments in the majority of malaria cases treated in our country. This underlines the importance of ensuring access to effective antimalarial therapies over those rendered obsolete by resistance [12, 13]. The availability of hydroxychloroquine is primarily attributed to its use in treating systemic autoimmune diseases, and this is probably the main reason why this antimalarial is so widely available.

The lack of a readily available stock of artesunate IV and artemether-lumefantrine, compounded by reliance on international procurement processes, further amplifies the weakness of malaria management in non-endemic settings [14]. In the case of artesunate IV, a request to the Ministry of Health is needed in order to get the drug or the patient must be transferred to another hospital where the drug is already available. In both cases, a delay in starting treatment is expected, which could have consequences for the patient. Artesunate IV, stocked by three-quarters of the surveyed hospitals, is particularly critical, as there are no viable alternatives currently available; intravenous quinine, once a secondary option, is no longer commercialized or accessible in international markets, due to resistance and the existence of newer treatments. Similarly, artemether-lumefantrine, a treatment for non-severe malaria in pregnant women [6], is in stock in a very limited number of the surveyed hospitals. As it is not commercially available in Spain, timely treatment initiation is difficult to achieve.

Procurement time data reveal additional concerns. Artesunate IV demonstrated critical gaps in timely acquisition, with only slightly over half of hospitals being supplied within 24 h during weekdays and no clear differences between weekdays and weekends. Artemether-lumefantrine followed a similar pattern, with just one third of hospitals reporting availability within 24 h on weekdays and just some less on weekends. The reliance on international procurement processes can result in critical delay and could increase morbidity and mortality [14]. It is very difficult to determine if the delay in administering proper malaria treatment, due to its lack of availability, is among the factors associated with malaria-related mortality in Spain. Although malaria is a notifiable disease, the standard reporting form that physicians are required to complete does not include relevant information, such as data on disease severity, treatments administered, or causes of death, even though this information would be of great importance.

The availability of antimalarial drugs is closely linked to hospital size, as demonstrated in this study. Larger hospitals (> 500 beds) are significantly better equipped, with the majority of them stocking artesunate IV, compared to just slightly more than half of the small hospitals. This disparity can be attributed to the fact that larger hospitals often have more specialized medical departments and are frequently designated as reference centres for tropical diseases. This role requires the availability of a broader range of treatments and the capacity to handle severe cases. Similar patterns have been observed in endemic settings [15], although the disparities in drug availability are driven less by specialization and more by financial and logistical constraints. Smaller facilities in endemic regions often struggle with inadequate funding, fragmented supply chains, and reliance on local suppliers, which limits their access to high-quality artemisinin-based combinations. In contrast, larger centres, particularly urban hospitals, benefit from centralized procurement systems and economies of scale, leading to more consistent availability.

Hospitals with a standardized and consensus-based malaria treatment protocol in place demonstrated improved stock availability and reduced procurement times. Virtually all hospitals with a specific protocol had artesunate IV in stock, in contrast to those without a protocol, where the drug was available in only 50% of cases. Protocols guide efficient resource allocation and ensure preparedness for imported malaria cases, a practice validated in endemic settings where adherence to national treatment guidelines has enhanced antimalarial accessibility [16]. This has also been observed in other contexts, where clear treatment protocols are crucial for the rational use and consistent availability of ACTs [17].

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are no similar national studies in non-endemic countries. In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis on the availability of malaria diagnostic tests and antimalarial drugs [16], all the studies included were from endemic countries and none of them in countries with low malaria transmission or non-endemic countries. Comparing our results with data from endemic countries reveals shared challenges, such as delays in accessing ACTs and limited availability of artesunate IV [18, 19]. However, the nature of these challenges differs; endemic countries often face stock-outs due to funding constraints, supply chain inefficiencies, lack of regulation in the private sector, high prices, the presence of illegal market and dependency of external donors [20], while non-endemic countries like Spain struggle with bureaucratic hurdles and reliance on international procurement. These findings also echo experiences in other non-endemic settings, such as the United States, where artesunate IV is accessed via investigational drug protocols, causing treatment delays of up to 7 h [14].

Not all hospitals, particularly those that have managed only a small number of malaria cases in their historical series, need to maintain antimalarial drugs permanently in stock. It would be sufficient to have a nearby reference centre with an adequate supply of antimalarial drugs, specifically intravenous artesunate. At present, the Spanish Society of Tropical Medicine and International Health (SEMTSI) is developing a national registry of centres with available stocks of specific drugs for the treatment of imported and tropical diseases, including malaria. This registry will be of great help in saving hospitals time when urgently seeking treatments and in optimizing healthcare resources. The study confirms that 29 of the 46 hospitals that responded to the survey (63%) are located in the four autonomous communities that account for 69% of malaria cases in Spain [5]. Among these, at least 3 centres in each region have urgent access to the required medication when needed.

Based on the data from this study and the deficiencies detected in the availability of antimalarials, it would be important to consider the following strategies aimed to improve access to antimalarials in Spain:

Domestic stockpiling: Establishing national or regional reserves of artesunate IV could reduce procurement delays and improve response times for severe malaria cases.

Hospitals collaboration: Lessons from endemic regions, such as real-time stock monitoring systems like “SMS for Life in Tanzania” [20] or from collaborative virtual network like the one used by the “Antidote Network” in Spain [21] that allow immediate location of available stocks, could assist a more robust supply chain management in Spain.

This study has some limitations. The main limitation is the sample size. Only 46 hospitals responded to the survey despite multiple calls for participation. Additionally, private hospitals were included but were underrepresented, constituting only 13% of the total sample, which may not fully reflect the accessibility and stock levels in this sector. Furthermore, the distribution of responding hospitals across regions of Spain is heterogenous. This is relevant as the incidence of imported malaria is different throughout the territory and may influence the stocking pattern of antimalarial drugs in each hospital.

The survey's cross-sectional design provides a snapshot of antimalarial availability at a specific point in time, potentially missing seasonal variations or changes in the stocks influenced by procurement cycles. The self-reported nature of the data introduces the possibility of reporting bias, particularly in aspects such as procurement times or adherence to treatment protocols. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights into the current state of antimalarial availability in Spain and underlines critical areas for improvement.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study reveals significant gaps in antimalarials availability among the Spanish hospitals responding to the survey, with possible implications for both patient outcomes and healthcare system reliability. Addressing these gaps requires coordinated efforts to strengthen procurement processes, implement standardized treatment protocols, and ensure the domestic availability of essential antimalarial drugs. By adopting best practices from both endemic and non-endemic regions, Spanish hospitals can improve their preparedness for managing imported malaria cases, reducing delays in treatment and ultimately saving lives.
