Non-Archimedean Neumann problem: weak and strong solutions
Alexandra V. Antoniouk, Anatoly N. Kochubei

Abstract
We consider the Neumann problem for the equation with the Vladimirov-Taibleson fractional differentiation operator over a non-Archimedean local field. We study weak solutions following the method by Dipierro, Ros-Oton and Valdinoci (2017). Our investigation of strong solutions is based on the ultrametric identities for the operator under consideration.



1
Introduction

Let
K
be a non-Archimedean local field,
\Omega\subset K^{n}
be a bounded open set. A model example of a pseudo-differential operator acting on real- or complex-valued functions on
\Omega
is the Vladimirov-Taibleson fractional defferential operator

\ \big(D^{\alpha,n}u\big)(x)=c_{n,\alpha}\int\limits_{K^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dy,\quad x\in\Omega,

(1.1)

where
\|z\|_{K^{n}}=\max\limits_{j=1,\ldots,n}|z_{j}|_{K}
for
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in K^{n}
,

c_{n,\alpha}=\frac{q^{\alpha}-1}{1-q^{-\alpha-n}},\quad\alpha>0,

q
is the residue cardinality of
K
, and the precise understanding of the integral in (
1.1
) depends on smoothness properties of a function
u
.

The operator (
1.1
) and related equations have been studied by many authors, see the monographs
[
21
,
16
,
1
,
20
,
12
,
22
]
and many recent papers, such as
[
5
,
3
,
4
,
18
,
13
]
and many others. This operator is obviously nonlocal; its form resembles that of the fractional Laplacian of real analysis. Both for the latter and
D^{\alpha,n}
, the Dirichlet problem assign the values of solution not on the boundary of
\Omega
, but on its complement, in our case
\Omega^{c}=K^{n}\backslash\Omega
. Note that the most important open subsets of a non-Archimedean local field, like balls and spheres, are simultaneously open and closed, thus have empty boundaries. The classical notion of "boundary value problem" does not make sense in this situation.

Nevertheless a version of the Neumann problem introduced in
[
7
]
for the fractional Laplacian can be used in our situation. Namely, denote

\ \big(\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}u\big)(x)=c_{n,\alpha}\int\limits_{\Omega}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dy,\quad x\in K^{n}\backslash\Omega.

(1.2)

The non-Archimedean Neumann problem is formulated as follows:

\begin{cases}D^{\alpha,n}u=f&\text{on $\Omega$}\\
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}\ u=g&\text{on $\Omega^{c}$}\end{cases}

(1.3)

where
f,g
are given functions. Note that a solution
u
should be defined on the whole space
K^{n}
though the first equation in (
1.3
) is checked only on
\Omega
.

In this paper we develop two alternative settings to understand solution of (
1.3
). While weak solutions are defined and investigated in a way similar to the one developed in
[
7
]
for real pseudo-differential operators, strong solutions are constructed under the stronger assumptions, using specific ultrametric properties of non-Archiemedean objects.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section 2, we present main definitions and facts regarding non-Archimedean local fields. Section 3 is devoted to appropriate analysis of fractional Sobolev spaces, while in Section 4 we deal with some integration by parts formulas. Sections 5 and 6 are devoted to weak solutions. Finally, in Section 7 strong solution are considered.

2
Local fields

A (non-Archimedean) local field is a non-discrete totally disconnected locally compact topological field (see for details, e.g.
[
16
,
19
,
23
]
).

An important construction related to local fields is that of field extension. If a field
k
is a subfield of field
K
, then
K
is called an extension of
k
. This relation is often denoted as
K/k
. In the above situation, the field
K
is a vector space over
k
, and we speak about a finite extension
K/k
, if this vector space is finite dimensional.

A non-Archimedean local field
K
is isomorphic either to a finite extension of the field
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of
p
-adic numbers (where
p
is a prime number), if
K
has characteristic zero, or to the field of formal Laurent series with coefficients from a finite field, if
\text{\it char}\,K>0
.

Note that by Ostrowski’s theorem,
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is the only possible alternative to
\mathbb{R}
as a completion of the field of rational numbers. This shows the importance of non-Archimedean mathematics as one of the principal branches of mathematical science.

Any local field is endowed with absolute value
|\cdot|_{K}
, such that: 1)
|x|_{K}=0
, if and only if
x=0
; 2)
|xy|_{K}=|x|_{K}\cdot|y|_{K}
; 3)
|x+y|_{K}\leq\max(|x|_{K},|y|_{K})
. The last property called the ultrametric inequality, implies the equality
|x+y|_{K}=|x|_{K}
, if
|y|_{K}<|x|_{K}
.

Note that the ring of integers
O=\{x\in K\colon|x|_{K}\leq 1\}
contains an ideal
P=\{x\in K\colon|x|_{K}<1\}
, which in turn contains such an element
\beta
that
P=\beta O
. We call the absolute value normalized, if
|\beta|_{K}=q^{-1}
, where
q
is the cardinality of the finite field
O/P
. We always assume this property of our absolute value. A normalized absolute value takes exactly the values
q^{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The additive group of a local field
K
is self-dual, so that the Fourier analysis in
K
is similar to the classical one. Let
\chi
be a fixed non-constant additive character having rank zero, that is
\chi(x)\equiv 1
as
x
belongs to the ring of integers, while
\chi(x_{0})\neq 1
for some
x_{0}\in K
with
|x_{0}|_{K}=q.

The Fourier transform of a complex-valued function
f\in L^{1}(K)
is defined as

(\mathcal{F}f)(\xi)\equiv\widehat{f}(\xi)=\int\limits_{K}\chi(x\,\xi)f(x)\,dx,\quad\xi\in K,

where
dx
is the Haar measure on the additive group of
K
normalized in such a way that the measure of
O
equals 1.
If
\mathcal{F}f=\widehat{f}\in L^{1}(K)
, then the inversion rule

f(x)=\int\limits_{K}\chi(-x\,\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)\,d\xi

holds. We will also denote
\widetilde{f}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}f
. Sometimes it is convenient to write
\mathcal{F}_{x\to\xi}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\xi\to x}
specifying the arguments of appropriate functions.
This Fourier analysis extends easily to functions on
K^{n}
.

Note that the Haar measure on
K
or on
K^{n}
is unique (up to a multiplicative coefficient) on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Meanwhile it can be extended onto a wider
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{M}
with the following completeness property: if
M\in\mathfrak{M}
has the extended measure zero, then every subset of
M
belongs to
\mathfrak{M}
. For the proof see Lemma 16.4 in
[
11
]
. Below we assume that the Haar measures have undergone the above extension procedure.

The Fourier transform preserves the Bruhat-Schwartz space
\mathcal{D}(K)
of text functions, consisting of locally constant functions with compact supports. The local constancy of a function
f\colon K^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
means the existence of such an integer
\ell
that for any
s\in K^{n}

f(x+x^{\prime})=f(x),\quad\text{whenever}\ \ |x^{\prime}|_{K}\leq q^{-\ell}.

The Fourier transform extends to the dual space
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(K^{n})
called the space of Bruhat-Schwartz distributions.

For
\Omega=K^{n}
the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator (
1.1
) admits, if
u\in\mathcal{D}(K^{n})
, the representation

\big(D^{\alpha,n}u\big)(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\xi\to x}\Big(\|\xi\|^{\alpha}_{K^{n}}\mathcal{F}_{x\to\xi}u\Big),

where
\|\cdot\|_{K^{n}}
is the natural non-Archimedean norm on
K^{n}
:

\|(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\|_{K^{n}}=\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq n}|x_{j}|_{K}.

Below we will need some integration formulas. For methods of calculating some integrals ove subsets of local fields see
[
20
,
21
,
16
]
. We have

\displaystyle\ \int\limits_{\|x\|_{K^{n}}\leq q^{N}}\,dx

\displaystyle=q^{nN},\quad N\in\mathbb{Z};

(2.1)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\|y\|_{K^{n}}>q^{N}}\frac{dy}{|y|_{K}^{\alpha+1}}

\displaystyle=\big(1-\frac{1}{q}\big)\sum\limits_{j=N+1}^{\infty}q^{-j(\alpha+1)}q^{j}=\frac{1-\frac{1}{q}}{1-q^{-\alpha}}q^{-(N+1)\alpha}=\frac{q-1}{q(q^{\alpha}-1)}q^{-N\alpha};

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\|x\|_{K^{n}}\leq q^{N}}\chi(ax)\,dx

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}q^{N}&\text{if}\quad|a|_{K}\leq q^{-N};\\
0&\text{if}\quad|a|_{K}\geq q^{-N+1}.\end{cases}

(2.3)

3
Fractional Sobolev Space

From now on, we assume that
\Omega
is a non-Archimedean ball

B_{N}=\{x\in K^{n}\colon\|x\|_{K^{n}}\leq q^{N}\}.

Let us introduce a Hilbert space corresponding to the problem (
1.3
).

Suppose that
\rho\in L^{1}(K^{n}\backslash\Omega)
;
u,v\colon K^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. Denote

\displaystyle\ \|u\|_{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,\rho}}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\,|\rho|^{1/2}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(K^{n}\backslash\Omega)}+

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy\Bigg\}^{1/2};

(3.1)

\displaystyle(u,v)_{{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,\rho}}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega}uv\,dx+\int\limits_{K^{n}\backslash\Omega}|\rho|\,uv\,dx+

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}\frac{\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy.

(3.2)

Correspondingly, the space
{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,\rho}}=\big\{u\colon K^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\ \text{measurable};\|u\|_{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,\rho}}<\infty\big\}
. Our definitions are similar to that given in
[
7
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,\rho}}
is a real Hilbert space with the inner product (
3.2
).

Proof.

The nontrivial part of the proof is that of completeness. The scheme of proof follows
[
7
]
.

Let
u_{k}\to u
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
be a Cauchy sequence with respect to the norm (
3
). Taking, if necessary a subsequence we may assume that
u_{k}\to u
almost everywhere on
\Omega
. Therefore there exists such a subset
Z_{1}\subset\Omega
that
\text{mes}\,(Z_{1})=0
and
u_{k}\to u
for all
x\in\Omega\backslash Z_{1}
.

Denote by
1\!\!\mathbb{I}_{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}(x,y)
the indicator function of the domain of integration in (
3
).
Let us write

E_{u}(x,y)=\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{(n+\alpha)/2}}\,1\!\!\mathbb{I}_{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}(x,y).

Then

E_{u_{k}}(x,y)-E_{u_{h}}(x,y)=\frac{\big(u_{k}(x)-u_{h}(x)-u_{k}(y)+u_{h}(y)\big)}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{(n+\alpha)/2}}\,1\!\!\mathbb{I}_{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}(x,y).

For each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists such a natural number
N_{\varepsilon}
that if
k,h\geq N_{\varepsilon}
, then, since
\{u_{k}\}
is the Cauchy sequence,

\|E_{u_{k}}-E_{u_{h}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(K^{n})}\leq\int\limits_{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}\frac{|(u_{k}-u_{h})(x)-(u_{k}-u_{h})(y)|^{2}}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

Taking a subsequence, if necessary, we find that
E_{u_{k}}\to E_{u}
in
L^{2}(K^{n})
and almost everywhere on
K^{n}
. The latter means the existence of such a subset
Z_{2}\subset K^{n}\times K^{n}
that
\text{mes}\,(Z_{2})=0
and

\ E_{u_{k}}(x,y)\to E_{u}(x,y)\ \text{for all}\ (x.y)\in(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash Z_{2}.

(3.3)

For
x\in\Omega
, let us set
S_{x}=\{y\in K^{n}\colon(x,y)\in K^{n}\backslash Z_{2}\}
. Denote

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in K^{n}\times K^{n}\colon x\in\Omega,y\in K^{n}\backslash S_{x}\};

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\{x\in\Omega\colon\text{mes}\,(K^{n}\backslash S_{x})=0\}.

We have

\ W\subseteq Z_{2}.

(3.4)

Indeed, if
(x,y)\in W
, then
y\in K^{n}\backslash S_{x}
, that is
y\notin S_{x}
, so that
(x,y)\in Z_{2}.

Due to the completeness property of the Haar measure on its extended
\sigma
-algebra (see Section
2
above), it follows from (
3.4
) that the set
W
is measurable and
\text{mes}\,W=0.
By Fubini’s theorem

0=\text{mes}\,W=\int_{\Omega}\text{mes}\,(K^{n}\backslash S_{x})\,dx,

so that
\text{mes}\,(K^{n}\backslash S_{x})=0
for almost all
x\in\Omega
. This implies the equality
\text{mes}\,(\Omega\backslash V)=0
.
Therefore

\text{mes}\,\big(\Omega\backslash(V\backslash Z_{1})\big)=\text{mes}\,\big((\Omega\backslash V)\cup Z_{1}\big)\leq\text{mes}\,(\Omega\backslash V)+\text{mes}\,Z_{1}=0,

so that
V\backslash Z_{1}\neq\emptyset
.

Let us fix
x_{0}\in V\backslash Z_{1}
. Then
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}u_{k}(x_{0})=u(x_{0}).
In addition, since
x_{0}\in V
, we have
\text{mes}\,(K^{n}\backslash S_{x_{0}})=0.
This means that for all
y\in S_{x_{0}}
(thus for almost all
y\in K^{n}
) we get
(x_{0},y)\in K^{n}\backslash Z_{2}
and by (
3.3
),

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}E_{u_{k}}(x_{0},y)=E(x_{0},y).

Note that
\Omega\times\Omega^{c}\subset(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})
and

E_{u_{k}}(x_{0},y)=\frac{u_{k}(x_{0})-u_{k}(y)}{\|x_{0}-y\|_{K^{n}}^{(n+\alpha)/2}}.

Therefore for almost every
y\in\Omega^{c}

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}u_{k}(y)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\Big\{u_{k}(x_{0})-\|x_{0}-y\|_{K^{n}}^{(n+\alpha)/2}E_{u_{k}}(x_{0},y)\Big\}=

\displaystyle=u(x_{0})-\|x_{0}-y\|^{(n+\alpha)/2}E(x_{0},y),

so that in addition to its convergence almost everywhere on
\Omega
, the sequence
\{u_{k}\}
converges almost everywhere on
K^{n}\backslash\Omega
, thus on
K^{n}
. Changing the notation, we may assume that
u_{k}
converges almost everywhere on
K^{n}
to some function
u
. Using Fatou’s lemma, for any
\varepsilon>0
we find
N_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for any
h\geq N_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}\|u_{h}-u_{k}\|^{2}_{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,\rho}}\geq\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}\int\limits_{\Omega}(u_{h}-u_{k})^{2}\,dx+\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}\int\limits_{\Omega^{c}}|\rho|\,(u_{k}-u_{h})^{2}dy\,+

\displaystyle+\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}\int\limits_{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}\frac{|(u_{k}-u_{h})(x)-(u_{k}-u_{h})(y)|^{2}}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy\geq

\displaystyle\geq\int\limits_{\Omega}(u_{h}-u)^{2}dx+\int\limits_{\Omega^{c}}|\rho|\,(u_{h}-u)^{2}dy\,+\int\limits_{\int\limits_{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}}\frac{|(u_{h}-u)(x)-(u_{h}-u)(y)|^{2}}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy=

\displaystyle=\|u_{h}-u\|^{2}_{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,\rho}},

that is
u_{h}\to u
in
{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,\rho}}
and this space is complete.
∎

Remark that even for
\rho=0
we should not write
H^{\alpha}(\Omega)
instead of
{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,\rho}}
, since the functions from the latter space are defined on
K^{n}
.

We will need the following analog of the classical embedding theorem.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
F
be a bounded subset of
{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,0}}
,
\Omega=B_{N}
, consisting of real-valued functions. If

\ \sup\limits_{f\in F}\int\limits_{\Omega}\int\limits_{\Omega}\frac{|(f(x)-f(y)|^{2}}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy<\infty,

(3.5)

then the set
F
is precompact in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

(Compare with
[
6
]
). We will show that
F
is totally bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, that is for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exist such
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{M}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
that for every function
f\in F

\ \|f-\beta_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{for some}\ j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}.

(3.6)

Let us consider a finite covering of
\Omega
by non-intersecting small balls
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{\ell}
of a radius
\gamma=q^{-\nu}
:

\Omega=\bigsqcup\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}Q_{j},\quad\text{mes\,}Q_{j}=q^{-\nu n}=\gamma^{n}\ \text{(see \eqref{2-1}).}

\displaystyle\text{For each}\ x\in\Omega\ \text{denote by}\ j(x)\ \text{the unique choice of}

\displaystyle\text{a number from the set}\ \{1,\ldots,\ell\}\ \text{ such that}\ x\in Q_{j(x)}.

(3.7)

Let

P(f)(x)=\frac{1}{\text{mes\,}Q_{j(x)}}\int\limits_{Q_{j(x)}}f(y)\,dy.

On each ball
Q_{j}
,
P(f)(x)
is constant and will be denoted
r_{j}(f)
. Note that
P(f+g)=P(f)+P(g)
for all
f,g\in F
. Denote also

R(f)=\gamma^{n/2}(r_{1}(f),\ldots,r_{\ell}(f))\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}.

We have
R(f+g)=R(f)+R(g)
,

\|P(f)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\,\int\limits_{Q_{j}}|P(f)|^{2}dx\leq\gamma^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}|r_{j}(f)|^{2}=|R(f)|^{2}\leq\frac{|R(f)|^{2}}{\gamma^{n}},

because
\gamma<1
. Next,

\displaystyle|R(f)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\gamma^{n}|r_{j}(f)|^{2}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{n}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\Big|\int\limits_{Q_{j}}f(y)\,dy\Big|^{2}\leq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\int\limits_{Q_{j}}|f(y)|^{2}dy=

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{Q}|f(y)|^{2}dy=\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

so that the set
R(F)
is bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, thus totally bounded. Therefore for any
\eta>0
there exist such
b_{1},\ldots,b_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, that

R(F)\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}B_{\eta}(b_{i}),

where
B_{\eta}(b)
is a ball of the radius
\eta
centered at
b
. For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
we write the coordinated of the point
b_{i}
as
b_{i}=(b_{i,1},\ldots,b_{i,\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. For any
x\in\Omega
we set

\beta_{i}(x)=\gamma^{-n/2}b_{i,j(x)},

where
j(x)
was defined in (
3
). If
x\in Q_{j}
, then

P\big(\beta_{j}(x)\big)=\gamma^{-n/2}b_{i,j(x)}=\beta_{j}(x),

so that
r_{j}(\beta_{i})=\gamma^{-n/2}b_{i,j}
, and

\ R(\beta_{i})=b_{i}.

(3.8)

On the other hand

\displaystyle\|f-P(f)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\,\int\limits_{Q_{j}}|f(x)-P(f)(x)|^{2}dx=

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\,\int\limits_{Q_{j}}\Big|f(x)-\frac{1}{\text{mes\,}Q_{j}}\int\limits_{Q_{j}}f(y)\,dy\Big|^{2}dx=

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\,\int\limits_{Q_{j}}\frac{1}{(\text{mes\,}Q_{j})^{2}}\,\Big|\int\limits_{Q_{j}}\big[f(x)-f(y)\big]\,dy\Big|^{2}dx\leq

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma^{n}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\,\int\limits_{Q_{j}}\,\int\limits_{Q_{j}}|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}\,dy\,dx.

Since every point of an ultrametric ball is its center, we find that for
x,y\in Q_{j}
we have
\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}\leq\gamma
, so that

\displaystyle\ \gamma^{-n-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\ \text{and}

\displaystyle\|f-P(f)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{\alpha}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\,\int\limits_{Q_{j}}\,\int\limits_{Q_{j}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dy\,dx\leq

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{\alpha}\int\limits_{Q}\,\int\limits_{Q}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dy\,dx.

(3.9)

Next,

\ \|f-\beta_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|f-P(f)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|P(\beta_{j})-\beta_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|P(f-\beta_{j})\|_{L^{2}.(\Omega)}

(3.10)

It follows from (
3.5
) and (
3
) that choosing
\gamma
sufficiently small we find that the first summand in (

) does not exceed
{\varepsilon}/{2}
for all

. By (

), the second summand equals zero. The third one equals

and we can find such

that

. Here also

Taking
\eta
sufficiently small, we obtain that the third summand in (
3.10
) does not exceed
\varepsilon/2
, which proves (
3.6
).
∎

4
Integration by Parts

Let us give several useful formulas explaining why the operation (
1.2
) can be seen as a analogue of the classical normal derivation. For further details see
[
7
,
8
]
.

1)

Suppose that
\dfrac{u(x)-u(y)}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\in L^{1}(B_{N}\times B_{N})
, (for example, if
u\in\mathcal{D}(K^{n})
). Then

\ c_{n,\alpha}\int\limits_{B_{N}}\int\limits_{B_{N}}\dfrac{u(x)-u(y)}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy=c_{n,\alpha}\int\limits_{B_{N}}\int\limits_{B_{N}}\dfrac{u(y)-u(x)}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dy\,dx=0

(4.1)

for the symmetry reasons.

2)

Let
u
be a bounded locally constant function on
K^{n}
. Then

\ \int\limits_{\Omega}\big(D^{\alpha,n}u\big)(x)\,dx=-\int\limits_{K^{n}\backslash\Omega}\big(\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}u\big)(y)\,dy.

(4.2)

This follows from (
4.1
), see
[
7
]
.

3)

Let
u
and
v
be bounded locally constant functions on
K^{n}

\displaystyle c_{n,\alpha}\!\!\!\!\int\limits_{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}\frac{\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy=

Let

Proposition 5.1

Under the above assumptions regarding

\displaystyle{\bf J}[u+\varepsilon v]

\displaystyle={\bf J}[u]+\varepsilon\Bigg(\frac{c_{n,\alpha}}{2}\int\limits_{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}\frac{\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)}{\|x-y\|_{K^{n}}^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy-

The following theorem is an analog of the main result from

First we consider the case where

, and

for all

and by the Riesz theorem, the problem (

) with a given

has a unique solution
v\in{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,0}}
.
Setting
\varphi=v
in (
6.2

\ \|v\|_{{H^{\alpha}_{\Omega,g}}}\leq C\|h\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(6.3)

since
\Omega\times\Omega\subset{(K^{n}\times K^{n})\backslash(\Omega^{c}\times\Omega^{c})}

Define the operator

Let us prove that

constant everywhere except a set of measure zero. Indeed, such a function

. Conversely, let

\ D^{\alpha,n}\big(T_{0}v\big)+T_{0}v=v\quad\text{on}\ \Omega.

(6.4)

Since also

on

:
