Detecting Periodicity of a General Stationary Time Series via AR(2)-Model Fitting
Jens-Peter Kreiss, Panagiotis Maouris and Efstathios Paparoditis

Abstract
Estimating the periodicity of a stationary time series via fitting a second order stationary autoregressive (AR(2)) model has been initiated by the seminal paper of ? . We investigate properties of this procedure when applied to a general stationary processes possessing a spectral density with a dominant peak at some frequency λ 0 ∈ ( 0 , π ) \lambda_{0}\in(0,\pi) . We show that if the peak of the spectral density is sharp enough (in a way to be specified) then the AR(2) model, which best (in mean square sense) approximates the underlying process, correctly identifies the frequency λ 0 \lambda_{0} . To investigate consistency properties of the AR(2) based estimator of λ 0 \lambda_{0} , a near to pole framework is adopted. Triangular arrays of stationary stochastic processes are considered that possess a spectral density the peak of which at λ 0 \lambda_{0} becomes more pronounced as the sample size n n of the observed time series increases to infinity. It is shown in this set up, that the AR(2) based estimator achieves a rate of convergence which is larger than the parametric n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} rate and which can be arbitrarily close to n − 2 / 3 n^{-2/3} , the best rate that can be achieved by this estimator.

The aim of this paper is to thoroughly investigate the capability and the consistency properties of such an AR(2) model

Inspired by the set up in

\lambda_{0}\in(0,\pi)
. Note that the standard asymptotic set up is not capable to properly resemble the inference problem we have in mind. To elaborate, it is well known that if an AR(2) model is fitted to a general
stationary time series by means of minimizing the mean square error, then the Yule-Walker parameter estimators converge to their theoretical counterparts at
n^{-1/2}

impacts the corresponding inference problem and the rate of convergence of the estimator. Recall that the situation we consider is that where the spectral density of the underlying stationary process has
a strong and dominant peak at some (unknown) frequency
\lambda_{0}
.

To develop a more appropriate mathematical framework for our investigations,
we consider a class of stationary
processes possessing a spectral density that can be expressed as

f_{\delta}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{|1-(1-\delta)e^{-i(\lambda-\lambda_{0})}|^{2}|1-(1-\delta)e^{-i(\lambda+\lambda_{0})}|^{2}}f_{U}(\lambda),

(8)

where
\delta
is a parameter taking values in the interval
(0,1)
. The class of spectral densities given in (
8

f_{\delta}
has a peak at frequency
\lambda_{0}
which becomes the more pronounced the closer
\delta
is to zero. In fact the parameter
\delta
allows to control within the stationary process class, the
strength of the peak of
f_{\delta}
at
\lambda_{0}
. For
\delta
tending to zero, the behavior of
f_{\delta}
at
\lambda_{0}
gets closer to that of a pole, that is
f_{\delta}

resembles in the limit, the behavior of the spectral density (
3
). Apart from the (long) memory properties described through the parameter
d
and which are not the focus of the current paper, the pole behavior of
(
3
) at
\lambda_{0}
can therefore be considered as a limiting case for
\delta\rightarrow 0
of the class of spectral densities given in (
8
). On the other hand, for
\{U_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
a white noise process and for
a_{1}=2(1-\delta)\cos(\lambda_{0})
and
a_{2}=-(1-\delta)^{2}
, the spectral density
f_{\delta}
coincides for any fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
, with that of a pure AR(2) model as given in (
5
). Notice that if the autoregressive polynomial
a(z)=1-a_{1}z-a_{2}z^{2}
has two complex roots
z_{1,2}=x\pm iy
, then
a_{1}=2(1-\delta)\cos(\lambda_{0})
and
a_{2}=-(1-\delta)^{2}
, where
\delta=1-\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
and
\lambda_{0}=\tan^{-1}(y/x)
. As we will see, the framework defined by the spectral density class (
8
), also enables us to investigate the effects of the sharpness of the peak on the rate of convergence of the AR(2)-based sample estimator of
\lambda_{0}
. To achieve this we will allow
\delta
to depend on the length
n
of the time series at hand and will let
\delta
getting closer to zero, that is, we will make the peak of the spectral density sharper, as
n
increases to infinity.

A special case of (
8
), in which
\delta=0
and
f_{U}
is the spectral density of a linear process driven by i.i.d. innovations, has attracted more interest in the econometric time series literature. In this case,
the corresponding process is a non-stationary, linear process which possesses two (conjugate) complex roots on the unit disc; see
?
,
?
and
?
.

For general stationary processes possessing the spectral density (
8
) and to the best of our knowledge, the effectiveness of the AR(2) model to correctly identify the periodicity and of the corresponding sample estimator to consistently estimate the peak frequency
\lambda_{0}
, are largely unknown in the literature. More specifically, it is not known if and under what circumstances
an AR(2) fit to the spectral density class (
8
) correctly identifies the frequency
\lambda_{0}
. An affirmative answer to this question turns out to be interesting especially when taking into account the fact that the effectiveness of such an AR(2) fit has been largely questioned in the literature. In particular,
?
have shown, that in the context of the non stationary in the mean model (
1
), a pure AR(2) fit, succeeds in correctly identifying the frequency
\lambda_{0}
, if and only if the process
\{U_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
in (
1
) has variance zero.

Apart from this, also the consistency properties of an AR(2) based estimator of
\lambda_{0}
for general stationary processes possessing a spectral density like in (
8
) are not known. The setting of an early work by
?
where the authors consider fitting a general
p
th order autoregression for peak estimation, is far too restrictive for our purposes. In particular, the authors assume that
\{X_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
follows a finite or infinite order, (linear) Gaussian autoregression and the
n^{-1/2}
-consistency rate of the corresponding frequency estimator has been derived under the assumption that the order
p
of the underlying process is finite and known. This rate has been weakened to
p^{3/2}n^{-1/2}
, in case
\{X_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is an infinite order autoregressive process and the order
p
of the autoregressive model fitted, increases to infinity with the sample size
n
. As already mentioned, we do not aim to impose structural assumptions on the stationary process
\{U_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
involved. Moreover, in the context considered in this paper, the AR(2) model is solely used as a vehicle in order to identify and infer periodicity, i.e., to identify and estimate
\lambda_{0}
and not as a procedure to parametrize the dependence structure of the underlying process.

In Section 2 we investigate within the process class (
8
), the effectiveness of the AR(2) model to correctly identify the frequency
\lambda_{0}
of interest. For this we consider the case where the peak of the process at
\lambda_{0}
is sharp enough and this is achieved by allowing the parameter
\delta
to converge to zero. We show that if
\delta
is close enough to zero and despite the properties that the spectral density
f_{U}
may have,
the frequency
\lambda_{0,\delta}
where the spectral density of the best (in mean square sense) approximating AR(2) model has its peak, is close enough to
\lambda_{0}
. In fact the difference between
\lambda_{0,\delta}
and
\lambda_{0}
can be arbitrary small and in the limit this discrepancy converges to zero for
\delta\rightarrow 0
. In Section 3 we investigate the consistency properties of a sample estimator of
\lambda_{0,\delta}
obtained by fitting an AR(2) model to a time series
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
stemming from a stationary process with spectral density in the class (
8
). The estimators of the autoregressive parameters are obtained via solving the system of Yule-Walker equations.
To develop a theory capable to investigate properties of this estimator, we adopt a “close to pole” asymptotic framework. For this,
we allow the parameter
\delta
to depend on the sample size
n
such that
\delta=\delta(n)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. That is, we allow for the peak of
f_{\delta(n)}
at
\lambda_{0}
to become more and more pronounced the larger the sample size gets. By properly controlling the rate at which
\delta(n)
approaches zero with respect to
n
, we show that the AR(2)-based estimator is consistent and that the rate of convergence of this estimator can be arbitrarily close to
n^{-2/3}
.

Before proceeding with our investigations, we fix some notation. For two sequences
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
, we use the notation
a_{n}\asymp O(b_{n})
if constants
k_{1}>0
and
k_{2}>0
exist such that
k_{1}b_{n}\leq a_{n}\leq k_{2}b_{n}
.

2
Effectiveness of the AR(2) Model in Detecting Periodicity

We start with the following assumption which describes our requirements on the underlying process
\{X_{t,\delta},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

Assumption 1
.

For
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\lambda_{0}\in(0,\pi)
,
\{X_{t,\delta},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is a zero mean stationary stochastic process which possesses a spectral density
f_{\delta}(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, as given in (
8
).

f_{U}
is the spectral density of a stationary process
\{U_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, which is continuous everywhere in
[0,\pi]
and
therefore also bounded, and satisfies
\inf_{\lambda\in[0,\pi]}f_{U}(\lambda)>0
.

Remark:

(i)

Assumption
1
is fulfilled if
X_{t,\delta}
can be expressed as

(1-(1-\delta)e^{-i\lambda_{0}}L)(1-(1-\delta)e^{i\lambda_{0}}L)X_{t,\delta}=U_{t},

(9)

where
L
is the shift operator defined as
L^{k}X_{t,\delta}=X_{t-k,\delta}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\{U_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is a zero mean stationary process possessing the spectral density
f_{U}

Notice that

|(1-\delta)e^{\pm i\lambda_{0}}|<1

\{X_{t,\delta},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}

\{U_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}

X_{t,\delta}=A_{\delta}(L)^{-1}U_{t}

A_{\delta}(L)=(1-2(1-\delta)\cos(\lambda_{0})L+(1-\delta)^{2}L^{2})
.

(ii)

The assumptions imposed on the spectral density
f_{U}
imply that
\{U_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
has a so-called autoregressive representation, that is, a sequence of coefficients
\{c:j\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
exists with
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|c_{j}|<\infty
and
c(z)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}z^{j}\neq 0
for
|z|\leq 1
, such that
U_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}U_{t-j}+e_{t}
. Here
\{e_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is a white noise process, that is,
{\rm E}(e_{t})=0
and
{\rm Cov}(e_{t},e_{s})=0
for
t\neq s
and
{\rm Var}(e_{t})=\sigma^{2}_{e}
. Hence

A_{\delta}(L)C^{-1}(L)X_{t,\delta}=e_{t}
, where
C^{-1}(z)
denotes the inverse power series of
C(z)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}z^{j}
,
|z|<1
.

For the stationary process
\{U_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
we additionally assume that its second and fourth order cumulants
satisfy the following summability conditions.

Assumption 2
.

For the autocovariance function of
\gamma_{U}(h)={\rm Cov}(U_{t},U_{t+h})
,
h\in\mathbb{Z}
, and the cumulant function

\textrm{cum}(U_{0},U_{h_{1},},U_{h_{2},},U_{h_{3}})
,
h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
, of the stationary process
\{U_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
,
it holds true that

(i)

\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}|h||\gamma_{U}(h)|<\infty
.

(ii)

\sum_{h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}=-\infty}^{\infty}(1+|h_{1}|+|h_{2}|+|h_{3}|)|\textrm{cum}(U_{0},U_{h_{1},},U_{h_{2},},U_{h_{3}})|<\infty

As already mentioned, the spectral density
f_{\delta}
has a peak at frequency
\lambda_{0}
which is the sharper the closer
\delta
is to zero. Let
\gamma_{\delta}(h)={\rm Cov}(X_{\delta,t},X_{\delta,t+h})

\rho_{\delta}(h)=\gamma_{\delta}(h)/\gamma_{\delta}(0)

, be the autocovariance and the autocorrelation function, respectively, of
\{X_{t,\delta},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}

The following lemma deals with the behavior of these second order functions as well as with some basic properties of the model class (
8
).

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, it holds

(i)

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}f_{\delta}(\lambda_{0})=+\infty\ \mbox{and}\ f_{\delta}(\lambda)\asymp{\mathcal{O}}(\delta^{-2})\ \ \mbox{as}\ \ \lambda\rightarrow\lambda_{0}

(ii)

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\gamma_{\delta}(0)=+\infty\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \gamma_{\delta}(0)\asymp{\mathcal{O}}(\delta^{-1})\ \ \mbox{as}\ \ \delta\rightarrow 0

(iii)

\rho_{\delta}(h)\ =\cos(\lambda_{0}h)+{\mathcal{O}}(\delta)
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
for any
h\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

(iv)

X_{t,\delta}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j,\delta}U_{t-j}+U_{t},\ \mbox{where for}\ K=\lfloor j/2\rfloor-1
,

b_{j,\delta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}(1-\delta)2\cos(\lambda_{0})&&j=1\\
(1-\delta)^{j}\big(1+2\cos(j\lambda_{0})-\frac{\displaystyle 2\sin((K+1)\lambda_{0})\cos(K\lambda_{0})}{\displaystyle\sin(\lambda_{0})}\big)&&j>1\ \mbox{even}\\

In particular,

|b_{j,\delta}|\leq C_{\lambda_{0}}(1-\delta)^{j}\ \mbox{for all}\ j\in\mathbb{N}\ \mbox{where the constant}\ C_{\lambda_{0}}>0,\\
\ \mbox{only depends on}\ \lambda_{0}

At the same time and as

The following result answers the question about the effectiveness of the best (in means square sense) fitting AR(2) model to detect the peak frequency
\lambda_{0}
of interest.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that the AR(2) model
X_{t,\delta}=a_{1,\delta}X_{t-1,\delta}+a_{2,\delta}X_{t-2,\delta}+v_{t}
is fitted to the process
\{X_{t,\delta},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, where the unique parameters

(a_{1,\delta},a_{2,\delta})
are those obtained as
{\rm argmin}_{c_{1},c_{2}}{\rm E}(X_{t,\delta}-c_{1}X_{t-1,\delta}-c_{2}X_{t-2,\delta})^{2}

If
a_{2,\delta}<0
define
\lambda_{0,\delta}=\arccos\big(a_{1,\delta}(1-a_{2,\delta})/(-4a_{2,\delta})\big)
.
Then,

(i)

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\ (a_{1,\delta},a_{2,\delta})=(2\cos(\lambda_{0}),-1)

(ii)

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\lambda_{0,\delta}=\lambda_{0}
.

Recall that the parameters
a_{1,\delta}
and
a_{2,\delta}
of the best AR(2)-fit are functions of the first and second order autocorrelations and they are given by

(a_{1,\delta},a_{2,\delta})=\frac{1}{1-\rho^{2}_{\delta}(1)}\big(\rho_{\delta}(1)(1-\rho_{\delta}(2)),\ \rho_{\delta}(2)-\rho^{2}_{\delta}(1)\big).

(10)

a_{1,\delta}
and
a_{2,\delta}
are obtained by solving the corresponding two-dimensional system of

Lemma

justifies, under certain conditions, the use of an AR(2) model in order to detect the dominant periodicity of a time series at hand.
Recall that the closer is

at frequency
\lambda_{0}

2
states that if the peak of the spectral density is strong enough, the frequency
\lambda_{0,\delta}
where the best (in mean square sense) fitting AR(2) model has its peak is close enough to the frequency
\lambda_{0}

3
Consistency of the AR(2)-Based Estimator of Periodicity

Suppose a time series
X_{1,\delta},X_{2,\delta},\ldots,X_{n,\delta}
stemming from the process
\{X_{t,\delta},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is observed. Since in our consistency considerations, we do not want to impose any structural assumptions on the dependence structure of the process
\{U_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
beyond the summability conditions stated in Assumption
2

\widehat{\gamma}_{\delta}(h)=\frac{2\pi}{n}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{G}}(n)}I_{n,\delta}(\lambda_{j,n})\cos(\lambda_{j,n}h),\ \ \ {\mathcal{G}}(n)=\{-N,\ldots,-1,1,\ldots,N\},

of
\gamma(h)
for
|h|<n

I_{n,\delta}

given by

I_{n,\delta}(\lambda_{j,n})=\frac{1}{2\pi n}\big|\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t,\delta}e^{-i\lambda_{j,n}t}\big|^{2},

with
\lambda_{j,n}=2\pi j/n
,
j\in{\mathcal{G}}(n)
. Notice that the estimator
\widehat{\gamma}(h)
is closely related to the commonly used sample autocovariance

\widetilde{\gamma}_{\delta}(h)=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n-|h|}X_{t,\delta}X_{t+|h|,\delta}
,
|h|<n

.

Suppose that Assumption
1
is satisfied and that
\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}|\gamma_{U}(h)|<\infty

|h|<n

\widehat{\gamma}_{\delta}(h)=\widetilde{\gamma}_{\delta}(h)+O_{P}(n^{-1}\delta^{-2}).

(11)

|\widehat{\gamma}_{\delta}(h)-\widetilde{\gamma}_{\delta}(h)|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\rightarrow}}0

the difference between the two estimators, which are denote in this case by

\widehat{\gamma}(h)=\widetilde{\gamma}(h)+O_{P}(n^{-1}).

Let
(\widehat{a}_{1,\delta},\widehat{a}_{2,\delta})
be the well-known Yule-Walker estimator of

For

.

Suppose that Assumptions

(i)

(ii)

\widehat{\lambda}_{0,\delta_{n}}=\lambda_{0,\delta_{n}}\ +\ {\mathcal{O}}_{P}(n^{-(1+\alpha)/2})

Remark.

Recall that
\lambda_{0,\delta_{n}}
is the location of the peak of the spectral density of the AR(2) model which
best (in mean square sense) approximates the second order structure of the underlying process
\{X_{t,\delta_{n}},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
;
see Lemma
2
. By Theorem
1
, the sample estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{0,\delta_{n}}
of the peak
location converges at a rate which is larger than the parametric rate

and
this rate is the closer to

the stronger the peak of the underlying spectral density is.

Giraitis et al. (2001) obtained for the maximizing frequency
\lambda_{0}
of their objective function over a discrete set of frequencies and for
infinite order moving average models with ergodic innovations which satisfy some conditional moment assumption up to
order four, a convergence rate of

.
Hidalgo and Soulier (2004) considered the maximum of the periodogram of all Fourier frequencies as an estimator for
the spectral peak. They were able to show for linear time series, i.e. moving averages of infinite order driven by i.i.d. innovations,
a convergence rate of their estimator which almost reaches
n^{-1}
.
The rate we obtained for the AR(2) based estimator is slower. However, on the one hand we think that it is worth investigating the ability
of the simple AR(2)-based procedure to estimate the peak frequency under rather mild assumptions, an issue which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been addressed so far in the literature.
On the other
hand, we conjecture that an asymptotic distribution theory may be possible for the AR(2)-based estimator of the
spectral peak, a task which so far, is open for further research. This could be an advance of the AR(2)-based estimator taking into account
the fact that for the estimator based on the maximum of the periodogram over the Fourier frequencies, a distribution theory is not
possible; see Giraitis et al. (2001), p. 997.

\hat{\rho}_{\delta_{n}}({h})-\rho_{\delta_{n}}({h})
. We have

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\delta_{n}}({h})-\rho_{\delta_{n}}({h})\

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\gamma}_{\delta_{n}}({h})}{\hat{\gamma}_{\delta_{n}}({0})}-\rho_{\delta_{n}}({h})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hat{\gamma}_{\delta_{n}}({0})}(\hat{\gamma}_{\delta_{n}}({h})-\rho_{\delta_{n}}({h})\hat{\gamma}_{\delta_{n}}({0}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\frac{2\pi}{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}I_{n,\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j,n})}\Big\{\frac{2\pi}{n}\sum_{\lambda_{j,n}\in\mathcal{G}(n)}I_{n,\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j,n})\cos(\lambda_{j,n}h)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ -\frac{2\pi}{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}I_{n,\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j,n})\rho_{\delta_{n}}({h})\Big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}({0})}{\hat{\gamma}_{\delta_{n}}({0})}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}({0})}{\frac{2\pi}{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}(\cos(\lambda_{j,n}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h))\ I_{n,\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j,n})}.

From Lemma
5
, we have that
\gamma_{\delta_{n}}(0)\big/\hat{\gamma}_{\delta_{n}}(0)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\rightarrow}}1
. Consider

R_{n}:=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}({0})}{\frac{2\pi}{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}(\cos(\lambda_{j,n}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h))\ I_{n,\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j,n})}.

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(R_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}({0})}{\frac{4\pi^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}\big(\cos(\lambda_{j,n}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h))^{2}\ {\rm Var}(I_{n,\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j,n})}\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}(0)}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{k,n}\in\mathcal{G}(n)\\
|j|\neq|k|\end{subarray}}\big(\cos(\lambda_{j,n}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)\big(\cos(\lambda_{k,n}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \times{\rm Cov}(I_{n,\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j,n}),I_{n,\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{k,n})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}({0})}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}(\cos(\lambda_{j}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h))^{2}\Big(f^{2}_{\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ +\mathcal{O}\big(|A_{\delta_{n}}(e^{-i\lambda_{j,n}})|^{-2}n^{-1}\delta_{n}^{-2}+n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-4}\Big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}(0)}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathcal{G}(n)\\
|j|\neq|k|\end{subarray}}\big(\cos({\lambda_{j,n}}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)\big(\cos({\lambda_{k,n}}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \times\Big(\frac{2\pi f_{4,U}(\lambda_{j,n},-\lambda_{k,n},\lambda_{k,n})}{n|A_{\delta_{n}}(e^{-i\lambda_{j,n}})|^{2}|A_{\delta_{n}}(e^{-i\lambda_{k,n}})|^{2}}+\ \mathcal{O}(n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-4})\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}({0})}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}(\cos(\lambda_{j}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h))^{2}f^{2}_{\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j})+{\mathcal{O}}\big(n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-1}+n^{-3}\delta_{n}^{-2}\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}(0)}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathcal{G}(n)\\
|j|\neq|k|\end{subarray}}\big(\cos({\lambda_{j,n}}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)\big(\cos({\lambda_{k,n}}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \times\frac{2\pi f_{4,U}(\lambda_{j,n},-\lambda_{k,n},\lambda_{k,n})}{n|A_{\delta_{n}}(e^{-i\lambda_{j,n}})|^{2}|A_{\delta_{n}}(e^{-i\lambda_{k,n}})|^{2}}+\ \mathcal{O}\big(n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-2}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}({0})}\frac{2\pi}{n}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(\cos(\lambda h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h))^{2}f^{2}_{\delta_{n}}(\lambda)d\lambda+{\mathcal{O}}\big(n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-1}+n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-1}+n^{-3}\delta_{n}^{-2}\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}(0)}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big(\cos(\lambda_{1}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)\big(\cos(\lambda_{2}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \times\frac{2\pi f_{4,U}(\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2})}{n|A_{\delta_{n}}(e^{-i\lambda_{1}})|^{2}|A_{\delta_{n}}(e^{-i\lambda_{2}})|^{2}}d\lambda_{1}d\lambda_{2}+\ \mathcal{O}\big(n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-1}+n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-2}\big)

\displaystyle=R_{1,n}+R_{2,n}+{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-2}),

with an obvious notation for
R_{1,n}
and
R_{2,n}
. Note
that the equality before the last one holds true since the error made in the corresponding terms
by replacing the
Riemann sums over the grid
{\mathcal{G}}(n)
with the integrals is of the order
\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-1})
. To elaborate, note the error

\big|\frac{2\pi}{n}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{G}}(n)}\big(\cos(\lambda_{j,n})-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(1)\big)^{2}f^{2}_{\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j,n})-\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\underbrace{\big(\cos(\lambda)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(1)\big)^{2}f^{2}_{\delta_{n}}(\lambda)}_{=:g(\lambda)}d\lambda\big|

is bounded by
2\pi\max_{\lambda}|g^{{}^{\prime}}(\lambda)|/n
. The important part in evaluating
\max_{\lambda}|g^{{}^{\prime}}(\lambda)|
is the term

\frac{\big(\cos(\lambda)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(1)\big)^{2}}{\big(\delta_{n}^{2}+2(1-\delta_{n})(1-\cos(\lambda-\lambda_{0}))\big)^{2}}=\frac{\big(\cos(\lambda_{0}+t)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(1)\big)^{2}}{\big(\delta_{n}^{2}+2(1-\delta_{n})(1-\cos(t))\big)^{2}}=:\frac{h^{2}_{1}(t)}{h^{2}_{2}(t)}=:h(t),

where the first equality follows using the substitution
\lambda-\lambda_{0}=t
. Differentiating
h(t)
and using the decomposition

\cos(\lambda_{0}+t)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(1)=\big(\cos(\lambda_{0}+t)-\cos(\lambda_{0})\big)+\big(\cos(\lambda_{0})-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(1)\big),

we can split the derivative
h^{{}^{\prime}}(t)
in the four terms

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big(\cos(\lambda_{0}+t)-\cos(\lambda_{0})\big)}{h^{2}_{2}(t)},\ \ \ \ \ T_{3}=\frac{\big(\cos(\lambda_{0}+t)-\cos(\lambda_{0})\big)^{2}\sin(t)}{h^{3}_{2}(t)},

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big(\cos(\lambda_{0})-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(1)\big)}{h^{2}_{2}(t)},\ \ \ \ \ T_{4}=\frac{\big(\cos(\lambda_{0})-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(1)\big)^{2}\sin(t)}{h^{3}_{2}(t)}.

Obtaining the maximum of the terms
T_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,4
via differentiation we get using the approximations,
\sin(t)\approx t
,
\cos(t)\approx 1-t^{2}/2
and
\cos(\lambda_{0}+t)-\cos(\lambda_{0})\approx-t\sin(\lambda_{0})
, after some algebra that all four terms are of the same order
{\mathcal{O}}(\delta_{n}^{-3})
. This implies,

\big|\frac{2\pi}{n}\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{G}}(n)}\big(\cos(\lambda_{j,n})-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(1)\big)^{2}f^{2}_{\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j,n})-\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big(\cos(\lambda)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(1)\big)^{2}f^{2}_{\delta_{n}}(\lambda)d\lambda\big|={\mathcal{O}}(n^{-1}\delta_{n}^{-3})

and therefore,

\displaystyle\big|\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}({0})}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}(n)}(\cos(\lambda_{j}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h))^{2}f^{2}_{\delta_{n}}(\lambda_{j})-\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}({0})}\frac{2\pi}{n}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(\cos(\lambda h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h))^{2}f^{2}_{\delta_{n}}(\lambda)d\lambda\big|

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}\big(n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-1}\big).

Consider next the terms
R_{1,n}
and

. Using Lemma
6
(i) we get that

and

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\delta_{n}}^{2}(0)}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big(\cos(\lambda_{1}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)\big(\cos(\lambda_{2}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \times\frac{2\pi f_{4,U}(\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2})}{n|A_{\delta_{n}}(e^{-i\lambda_{1}})|^{2}|A_{\delta_{n}}(e^{-i\lambda_{2}})|^{2}}d\lambda_{1}d\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}(n^{-1}\delta_{n}^{2}).

Hence,
{\rm Var}(R_{n})={\mathcal{O}}\big(n^{-1}\delta_{n}+n^{-2}\delta_{n}^{-2}\big)
, that is,

\hat{\rho}_{\delta_{n}}({h})-\rho_{\delta_{n}}({h})={\mathcal{O}}_{P}\big(n^{-(1+\alpha)/2}\big),

for

Assertion (i) of the theorem states that the above convergence rate of the autocorrelation estimators
\widehat{\rho}_{\delta_{n}}(1)

\widehat{\rho}_{\delta_{n}}(2)

, are different from the zero vector

.
Assertion (ii) of the theorem, follows by a linearization argument again, the observation that
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}=g(\widehat{a}_{1,\delta_{n}},\widehat{a}_{2,\delta_{n}})

with

for every

Proof of Lemma

:

Consider (i). Note that

|A_{\delta}(e^{-i(\lambda-\lambda_{0})}|^{2}=\delta^{2}\Big(1+2\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}(1-\cos(\lambda-\lambda_{0})\Big),

and that it suffices to consider the integral

\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\big(\cos(\lambda h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)^{2}}{\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{4}\Big(1+2\frac{1-\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n}^{2}}(1-\cos(\lambda-\lambda_{0})\Big)^{2}}d\lambda.

We first split this integral in two parts, the one for
|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|\in[0,\delta_{n}^{r}]
and the other for
|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|\in(\delta_{n}^{r},\pi]
for some
r\in(0,1]
. For the first part we get

\displaystyle\int_{\lambda_{0}-\delta_{n}^{r}}^{\lambda_{0}+\delta_{n}^{r}}

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\big(\cos(\lambda h)\pm\cos(\lambda_{0}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)^{2}}{\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{4}\Big(1+2\frac{1-\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n}^{2}}(1-\cos(\lambda-\lambda_{0})\Big)^{2}}d\lambda

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{O}}(\delta_{n}^{2r-4}+\delta_{n}^{-2})\int_{\lambda_{0}-\delta_{n}^{r}}^{\lambda_{0}+\delta_{n}^{r}}\big(1+2\frac{1-\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n}^{2}}(1-\cos(\lambda-\lambda_{0})\big)^{2}d\lambda

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{O}}(\delta_{n}^{2r-4})\int_{0}^{\delta_{n}^{r}}\Big(1+\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1-\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n}^{2}}t^{2}\Big)^{-2}dt

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}(\delta_{n}^{2r-3}).

Consider next the second part of the integral. We have,

\displaystyle\int_{|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|>\delta_{n}^{r}}

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\big(\cos(\lambda h)\pm\cos(\lambda_{0}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)^{2}}{\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{4}\Big(1+2\frac{1-\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n}^{2}}(1-\cos(\lambda-\lambda_{0})\Big)^{2}}d\lambda

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{O}}(\delta_{n}^{-4})\int_{|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|>\delta_{n}^{r}}\frac{(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{2}+\delta_{n}^{2}}{\displaystyle\Big(1+2\frac{1-\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n}^{2}}(1-\cos(\lambda-\lambda_{0})\Big)^{2}}d\lambda.

After some algebra we get that

and

\int_{|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|>\delta_{n}^{r}}\frac{\delta_{n}^{2}}{\displaystyle\Big(1+2\frac{1-\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n}^{2}}(1-\cos(\lambda-\lambda_{0})\Big)^{2}}d\lambda={\mathcal{O}}(\delta_{n}^{3}).

That is,

\int_{|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|>\delta_{n}^{r}}\frac{\displaystyle\big(\cos(\lambda h)\pm\cos(\lambda_{0}h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)^{2}}{\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{4}\Big(1+2\frac{1-\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n}^{2}}(1-\cos(\lambda-\lambda_{0})\Big)^{2}}d\lambda={\mathcal{O}}(\delta_{n}^{-1}).

Hence,

\int_{0}^{\pi}\big(\cos(\lambda h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h)\big)^{2}f_{\delta_{n}}^{2}(\lambda)d\lambda={\mathcal{O}}\big(\delta_{n}^{2r-3}+\delta_{n}^{-1}\big)={\mathcal{O}}(\delta_{n}^{-1}),

for

Consider (ii). Since
f_{4,U}
is bounded it suffices to evaluate the integral

T_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{(\cos(\lambda h)-\rho_{\delta_{n}}(h))}{|A_{\delta_{n}}(e^{-i(\lambda-\lambda_{0})})|^{2}}d\lambda\Big)^{2}.

Arguing as in the proof of (i) and using

.
