Antioxidant activity of ultrasound-assisted extracts of elephant garlic and their effects on the quality of pork patties during refrigerated storage
Authors: Jun Ho Park, Koo Bok Chin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the antioxidant activity of elephant-garlic (Allium ampeloprasum var. ampeloprasum) ultrasound-assisted extracts obtained with water (APW), 50% ethanol (APE50) and 100% ethanol (APE), and evaluated their effects on the physicochemical properties and microbial counts of pork patties during 14 d of refrigerated storage (4 °C). Total phenolic contents are followed APE > APE50 > APW > unextracted powder (AP) (p < 0.05). APE exhibited the highest 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity and reducing power, but the lowest iron-chelating ability relative to AP. Pork patties were prepared by incorporating 0.5% AP, APW, APE50, or APE, while patties without any additive served as the control and those containing 0.1% ascorbic acid (AA) served as the reference treatment. pH and lightness (L*) values did not differ among treatments. APE50 maintained redness (a*), whereas APE reduced it (p < 0.05). During storage, patties containing AP, APW, APE50 or APE had lower thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS) and peroxide values than control, with APE showing the greatest inhibition of lipid oxidation (p < 0.05). Volatile basic nitrogen (VBN) decreased with APW, APE50 and APE, while APE50 or APE also suppressed total plate counts and Enterobacteriaceae (p < 0.05). These results indicated that APE50 and APE effectively retard lipid oxidation and microbial growth in pork patties which might be better than the typical drying powder.Keywords: Elephant garlic extract, Ultrasound-assisted extraction, Antioxidant activity, Pork patty, Lipid oxidation

Introduction

Meat and meat products are important components of the human diet because they provide essential nutrients such as protein, vitamins, and minerals (Pereira & Vicente, 2013). However, due to their high moisture and nutrient contents, they are highly susceptible to microbial growth and oxidative deterioration, leading to quality degradation such as discoloration, rancidity, and nutrient loss, ultimately reducing consumer acceptance (Domínguez et al., 2019). Synthetic antioxidants such as butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA) and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) are widely used in meat products to retard oxidative deterioration and extend shelf life. Nevertheless, their safety continues to be discussed, particularly with regard to potential adverse effects associated with excessive or improper use, which has encouraged interest in natural alternatives (Xu et al., 2021). Accordingly, natural antioxidants, such as ascorbic acid, tocopherols, and phenolic compounds, have been increasingly investigated for application in meat products, particularly in studies using plant-based antioxidative materials (Lee et al., 2020).

Plants of the genus Allium, among plant-derived materials, are known to possess potential health benefits including antioxidant, antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and antihypertensive properties, which are attributed to the bioactive compounds they contain (Rose et al., 2005). These bioactive compounds mainly consist of organosulfur compounds (OSCs) and polyphenols. OSCs are primarily responsible for the characteristic flavor of Allium species, whereas both OSCs and polyphenols contribute to antioxidant activity by inhibiting oxidative processes (Modesti et al., 2024; Phan et al., 2019). Garlic (Allium sativum L.) has been extensively studied as a natural antioxidant in meat systems, and its solvent extracts have been shown to effectively inhibit lipid oxidation (Park & Chin, 2010). However, certain OSCs are highly reactive and may interact with protein thiol groups within meat matrices, potentially promoting oxidative reactions or altering protein functionality (Dursun & Güler, 2023; Nieto et al., 2013). These interactions highlight the complexity of applying garlic-derived compounds in real food systems.

Elephant garlic (Allium ampeloprasum L.), although morphologically similar to common garlic, is taxonomically distinct and has been reported to contain relatively lower levels of sulfur compounds and higher levels of phenolic compounds (Modesti et al., 2024). These compositional differences suggest that elephant garlic may exhibit different antioxidant behaviors and matrix interactions compared to common garlic. In particular, phenolic compounds have been reported to show stronger correlations with DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) than several OSCs, indicating their major contribution to commonly used antioxidant assays (Furdak et al., 2024). Therefore, elephant garlic, characterized by a relatively higher phenolic content, may function differently in food systems compared to OSC-dominant garlic materials. However, most previous studies on elephant garlic have focused primarily on compositional profiling or in vitro antioxidant activity. In actual food matrices, the functionality of bioactive compounds may be altered due to complex interactions with proteins, lipids, and moisture. Therefore, whether the antioxidant activity observed in vitro can be effectively translated into meat products requires further validation.

Previous work demonstrated that the incorporation of dried elephant garlic powder into pork patties partially suppressed lipid oxidation during refrigerated storage, as evidenced by reductions in TBARS and POV values (Park & Chin, 2025). However, the crude powder form may limit the release and accessibility of bioactive compounds within food matrices, thereby restricting their functional effectiveness. This highlights the need for extraction strategies to improve the functional utilization of elephant garlic-derived bioactive compounds.

Green extraction techniques, such as microwave-assisted extraction (MAE) and ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE), have been proposed as efficient alternatives to conventional solvent extraction methods, which often require prolonged extraction times and high solvent consumption (Rodriguez-García & Raghavan, 2022). In particular, UAE enhances mass transfer through cavitation-induced cell disruption, thereby facilitating the release of intracellular compounds (Shen et al., 2023). Indeed, UAE has been reported to be effective in inducing cell wall disruption and microcrack formation in Allium species, and has been shown to increase polyphenol content and enhance antioxidant activity (Medina-Jaramillo et al., 2022). Furthermore, such extraction processes may also influence the quantity and functional characteristics of the bioactive substances obtained. Differences in solvent polarity may influence the extraction efficiency of bioactive compounds with different physicochemical properties, potentially contributing to differences in antioxidant and antimicrobial effects in meat systems. Although solvent composition has been reported to influence phenolic extraction and antioxidant activity in Allium species (Cavalcanti et al., 2021), limited information is available regarding how solvent-dependent UAE affects the functional properties of elephant garlic extracts in meat systems.

Therefore, this study aimed to bridge the gap between in vitro antioxidant activity and meat product application by evaluating elephant garlic extracts obtained through UAE using solvents of different polarities. Specifically, the objectives of this study were to (1) evaluate the antioxidant properties of elephant garlic extracts obtained under selected UAE conditions and (2) assess how solvent polarity-dependent extract characteristics influence oxidative stability, microbial growth, and physicochemical quality attributes of pork patties during refrigerated storage.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Elephant garlic (Allium ampeloprasum L.) used in this study was harvested in July in Haenam-gun, Jeollanam-do, South Korea, and purchased from Hyeonseoun Co., Ltd. Elephant garlic powder was prepared according to the method described by Park and Chin (2025). After removal of inedible parts, the garlic was thoroughly washed and dried in a hot-air oven at 100 °C. After dried garlic was pulverized for 30 s using a grinder (HMF-3260 S, Hanil Electric Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea), it was passed through a 500 μm sieve to obtain a fine powder. The powder was then vacuum-sealed in polyethylene bags and stored at −70 °C until use.

Ultrasonic extraction from elephant garlic

To select appropriate ultrasonic extraction conditions for elephant garlic (Allium ampeloprasum L.) powder, the method described by Qin et al. (2021) was used as a reference, the following procedure was performed. UAE conditions used in the present study were experimentally selected based on antioxidant activity results obtained under different extraction conditions, including solvent type, ultrasonic power, and extraction time. Ultrasonic treatment was carried out using a probe-type ultrasonic homogenizer (STH-750 S, Sonictopia, Seoul, Korea) with a maximum power output of 750 W, operating at a constant frequency of 20 kHz and power inputs of 250 or 500 W. The extraction temperature was maintained at 4 ± 2 °C using a thermo-circulator (LCB-R08, Lab Tech, Namyangju, Korea). Elephant garlic powder (10 g) was mixed with each solvent (dd-water, 50% ethanol, or 100% ethanol) at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:20 (g/mL). The mixtures were sonicated at 4 °C for a total of 20 min, with 5 min intervals, using ultrasonic power outputs of 250–500 W. The extracts were filtered through Whatman No. 41 filter paper (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) and concentrated at 60 °C using a vacuum rotary evaporator (VE-11, Jeio Tech, Daejeon, Korea). The concentrates were subsequently freeze-dried at − 50 °C under 7 mm Torr using a freeze dryer (Ilshin Lab. Co. Ltd., Seoul, Korea) and stored at − 70 °C until further analysis. The extraction yield was calculated as the percentage ratio of the final dried extract powder to the initial elephant garlic powder, resulting in yields of 32.8%, 36.5%, and 11.6% for water, 50% ethanol, and 100% ethanol extracts, respectively.

Evaluation of the antioxidant activity of elephant garlic extract powder

Determination of total phenolic content

The total phenolic content of elephant garlic (Allium ampeloprasum L.) extracts obtained with different solvents (dd-water, 50% ethanol, and 100% ethanol) was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method (Lin & Tang, 2007). A 0.1 mL of 1% (w/v) extract solution was mixed with 2.8 mL of dd-water, 2 mL of Na₂CO₃ (2%), and 0.1 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (50%). After the mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 30 min, the absorbance was measured at 750 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-1601, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The results were expressed as g gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per 100 g of extract, based on a gallic acid standard curve (r² = 0.99).

2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity

The 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity of elephant garlic (Allium ampeloprasum L.) extract was determined according to the method of Huang et al. (2006). Sample solutions were prepared in methanol at concentrations of 0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1.0% (w/v). A 1 mL aliquot of each sample solution was mixed with 1 mL of methanol and 0.25 mL of 0.2 mM DPPH solution (prepared in methanol). The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-1601, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), with ascorbic acid as the reference standard. Ferrous ion chelating activity

The ferrous ion chelating activity of elephant garlic (Allium ampeloprasum L.) extracts was determined according to the method of Le et al. (2007). Elephant garlic powder extracted with various solvents was diluted in dd-water to different concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1.0%). Then, 0.5 mL of the diluted sample was mixed with 0.5 mL of dd-water, 0.1 mL of FeCl₂ (0.6 mM), and 0.9 mL of methanol, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of ferrozine (5 mM) was added and incubated for an additional 10 min at room temperature. The absorbance of the Fe²⁺–ferrozine complex was measured at 562 nm using a UV/VIS spectrophotometer (UV-1601, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Reducing power

The reducing power of elephant garlic (Allium ampeloprasum L.) extracts was determined according to the method of Huang et al. (2006). Elephant garlic extracts obtained with different solvents were diluted in dd-water to various concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1.0%). A 2.5 mL of sample was mixed with 2.5 mL of 200 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.6) and 2.5 mL of potassium ferricyanide (10 mg/mL), and the mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 20 min. After incubation, 2.5 mL of trichloroacetic acid (TCA, 100 mg/mL) was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 740×g for 10 min. A 2.5 mL of the supernatant was transferred to a test tube and mixed with 2.5 mL of distilled water and 0.5 mL of ferric chloride (1 mg/mL). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min, and the absorbance was measured at 700 nm using a UV/VIS spectrophotometer (UV-1601, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Ascorbic acid (AA) was used as the reference.

Incorporation of elephant garlic extracts in pork patties

Fresh pork ham (Grade 1+) and back fat were obtained from a wholesale meat market in Gwangju, South Korea. The ham was trimmed to remove connective tissues and visible fat, and the back fat was used for the experiment. Both lean meat and back fat were ground using a meat chopper (M-12 S, Hankook Fujee Plant Industries Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea). The formulation of pork patties shown in Table 1 was developed based on Park and Chin (2025), in which 0.5% elephant garlic powder was identified as an effective incorporation level for inhibiting lipid oxidation in pork patties during refrigerated storage. The treatments consisted of pork patties containing 0.5% elephant garlic powder or 0.5% elephant garlic extract powder, according to this formulation, and the mixtures were prepared using a mixer (EC20, Crypto Peerless, Birmingham, UK). The reference group was prepared with 0.1% ascorbic acid (AA). The mixtures were then re-ground, portioned into 60 g patties, and formed into shape using Petri dishes. These pork patties were stored in polystyrene containers at 4 ± 1 °C for 14 days, during which physicochemical and microbiological analyses were conducted on days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14. The entire experiment was independently repeated three times on different days using different batches of pork ham and back fat.

Physicochemical properties of pork patties

pH values and color measurement

pH and color measurements were conducted according to the method described by Kim and Chin (2020). The pH of pork patties was measured with a pH meter (Model 340, Mettler-Toledo, Switzerland) by inserting the electrode into the surface of the patties. Measurements were taken in five replicates, and the mean values were reported. The color of the patties was determined using a color reader (CR-10, Minolta, Japan). Color L*, a*, and b* values, representing lightness, redness, and yellowness, respectively, were recorded from the patty surface, and the mean values were calculated from six replicate measurements after calibration of the instrument.

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS)

The extent of lipid oxidation in pork patties was determined by measuring thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) according to the method of Sinnhuber and Yu (1977). Two grams of homogenized patty sample were mixed with 3 mL of 1% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) solution and 17 mL of 2.5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution. The mixture was heated in a water bath at 90 °C for 30 min and then cooled to room temperature. A 5 mL of the supernatant was collected and combined with 5 mL of chloroform, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged at 740×g for 5 min. Subsequently, a 3 mL of the resulting supernatant was mixed with 3 mL of petroleum ether, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged at 740×g for 10 min. The absorbance of the lower layer was measured at 532 nm using a UV-1601 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Peroxide values (POV)

The peroxide value (POV) of pork patties was determined according to the method of Shantha and Decker (1994) with slight modifications. Each 0.3 g sample was placed in a glass tube, and 10 mL of chloroform–methanol (1:1, v/v) was added and mixed to homogenize. The homogenate was transferred to a centrifuge tube, mixed with 3.08 mL of 0.5% NaCl, and vortexed for 30 s, followed by centrifugation at 930×g for 5 min. A 2 mL aliquot of the lower phase was transferred to a 15 mL glass tube, and 1.33 mL of chloroform–methanol (1:1, v/v), 25 µL of 30% ammonium thiocyanate, and 25 µL of iron (II) chloride solution were added. After incubation at room temperature for 20 min, the absorbance was measured at 500 nm using a UV/VIS spectrophotometer (UV-1601, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). POV was expressed as milliequivalents of peroxide per kilogram of sample (meq/kg).

Volatile basic nitrogen (VBN)

The volatile basic nitrogen (VBN, mg%) content of pork patties was determined using the microdiffusion method described by Conway (1962). One gram of sample was homogenized with 9 mL of distilled water for 30 s and then filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. A 1 mL of the filtrate and 1 mL of 50% K₂CO₃ solution were placed in the outer chamber of a Conway dish, while 1 mL of 0.01 N boric acid and VBN indicator (0.066% bromocresol in 100% ethanol + 0.066% methyl red in 100% ethanol) were added to the inner chamber. The dish was incubated at 37 °C for 120 min. The incubated solution was titrated with 0.01 N HCl, and the VBN value was expressed as mg%.

Microbial counts

Microbial counts were conducted according to the method described by Kim and Chin (2020). Ten grams of homogenized pork patty sample was aseptically mixed with 90 mL of sterilized dd-water and serially diluted. A 0.1 mL of each appropriate dilution was spread in duplicate onto total plate count (TPC) agar for enumeration of total bacteria and violet red bile (VRB) agar for enumeration of Enterobacteriaceae. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h and results were expressed as log10 colony forming units per gram (CFU/g).

Proximate composition analysis

The proximate composition of pork patties containing elephant garlic was determined according to the AOAC method (2000). Moisture content was analyzed using the oven-drying method, protein content by the Kjeldahl method, fat content by the Soxhlet extraction method, and ash content by the muffle furnace method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The effects of extraction solvent and ultrasound extraction condition (time–power combinations) on UAE extraction were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. The data were pooled if there was no interaction between extraction solvent and time-power combinations. The antioxidant activity of elephant garlic powder was analyzed by two-way ANOVA with treatment and concentration as factors, while the pork patty experiment containing elephant garlic powder was analyzed by two-way ANOVA with storage period and treatment as factors. The data were pooled if there was no interaction between treatment and storage time. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Duncan’s multiple range test at a significance level of P < 0.05. All experiments were independently repeated three times (n = 3).

Results and discussion

Effect of ultrasonic extraction parameters on the antioxidant activity of elephant garlic powder

As shown in Table 2, across all solvents (water, 50% ethanol, and 100% ethanol) and ultrasonic powers (250–500 W), total phenolic content, ferrous-ion chelating capacity, and DPPH radical-scavenging activity increased with extraction time up to 15 min (p < 0.05), while no further significant differences were observed thereafter (p > 0.05). This result may be associated with the saturation of cell-wall disruption and internal–external mass transfer caused by cavitation within 15 min, resulting in limiting the additional release of bioactive compounds. These findings are consistent with Qin et al. (2021), who reported that the increase in total polyphenols in Allium senescens L. extracts was attributed to cavitation and vibration effects of ultrasonication, and that the yield of phenolic compounds increased up to 150 W for 15 min. In the ultrasonic extraction of elephant garlic powder, no significant difference was observed between the 250 W and 500 W groups when the extraction time was fixed at 15 min (p > 0.05). However, higher ultrasonic power generates more microbubbles, elevating the temperature and pressure at the cavitation site. This process promotes microbubble growth and collapse, which may lead to excessive -OH radical formation, thereby oxidizing and degrading phenolic compounds and reducing their diffusion into the solvent (Yin et al., 2019). Similarly, Chemat et al. (2017) pointed out that prolonged high-power ultrasonication can increase temperature and, through cavitation bubble collapse, degrade heat-labile bioactive compounds. Since no further improvement in antioxidant activity was observed between 250 W and 500 W at 15 min (p > 0.05), the lower power (250 W) was selected to reduce energy consumption while maintaining comparable extraction efficiency. Therefore, extraction at 250 W for 15 min was selected for subsequent experiments based on the antioxidant activity results and energy efficiency considerations.

Evaluation of the antioxidant activity of elephant garlic extract powder

Total phenolic content

The total phenolic content of elephant garlic powder (AP) and its extracts obtained with water (APW), 50% ethanol (APE50), and 100% ethanol (APE) was measured, and the results are presented in Fig. 1A. The total phenolic content of AP was 0.61 g/100 g, while that of APW and APE50 was 0.78 g/100 g and 0.75 g/100 g, respectively, showing significantly higher values of ultrasonic treated samples as compared to the AP samples (p < 0.05). In particular, APE exhibited the highest total phenolic content of 2.22 g/100 g, which was significantly greater than all other treatments (p < 0.05). As the ethanol concentration increased (50 ~ 100%), the extraction efficiency of phenolic compounds from elephant garlic powder also increased, resulting in approximately 3.5-fold higher total phenolic content. This ethanol concentration-dependent increase in total phenolic content was attributed to differences in the extraction efficiency of phenolic compounds caused by solvent polarity. According to Naczk and Shahidi (2004), ethanol and ethanol–water mixtures have been widely recognized in many studies as suitable solvents for extracting phenolic compounds. Tomšik et al. (2016) reported that, in ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) of wild garlic (Allium ursinum), extraction temperature was the most dominant factor influencing total phenolic content (TP), and ethanol concentration (30–70%) also had a significant effect through its interaction with temperature. For flavonoid compounds (TF), ethanol concentration (30–70%) played the most important role, and 70% ethanol was found to be the optimal extraction condition, confirming that the lower polarity at higher ethanol concentrations was suitable for extracting bioactive compounds. Kang et al. (2015) measured the total phenolic and flavonoid contents of yellow garlic extracts with different ethanol ratios and found that the 100% ethanol extract contained about three times more phenolic compounds and about 40 times more flavonoids than the water extract, while the contents of alliin and S-allyl cysteine also increased about threefold with 100% ethanol extraction. These findings indicate that extraction solvent is one of the key factors influencing the total phenolic content of plant materials. Moreover, total phenolic content can be affected by multiple variables, including pretreatment methods (such as blanching or drying of the raw material), extraction techniques (e.g., Soxhlet extraction, maceration, and ultrasound-assisted extraction), extraction temperature, and the maturity stage of the plant material (Nantitanon et al., 2010). In the present study, the phenolic content increased with increasing ethanol ratio in the extraction solvent, suggesting that ethanol was more effective than water for recovering phenolic compounds from elephant garlic powder under the present extraction conditions.

DPPH radical scavenging activity

The results for the DPPH radical-scavenging activity of the water extract (APW), 50% ethanol extract (APE50), and 100% ethanol extract (APE) of elephant garlic powder are presented in Fig. 1B. Ascorbic acid (99.6%, w/w) was used as the reference standard, and all treatments exhibited high electron-donating activity of more than 90% at concentrations ranging from 0.1% to 1.0%. The electron-donating activity of AP, APE50, and APW increased with increasing concentration (p < 0.05). In particular, APE showed high activity of more than 90% at concentrations above 0.25%, with no difference from the reference (p > 0.05). In contrast, APE50 exhibited no difference from AP at concentrations above 0.25% (p > 0.05), while APW showed lower activity than AP (p < 0.05). Kang et al. (2015) reported that the DPPH radical-scavenging activity of ethanol extracts from yellow garlic increased with higher ethanol ratios, with 100% ethanol extracts showing 93.45% activity compared to 28.76% for water extracts. This finding is consistent with the present study and is likely due to the reduced solvent polarity at higher ethanol concentrations, which may be associated with enhanced extraction of antioxidant compounds under lower solvent polarity conditions. Similarly, Shin et al. (2008) observed that the DPPH radical-scavenging activities of ethanol extracts from fresh and steamed garlic were three- to four-fold higher than those of water extracts across different temperatures. This trend agreed with the results of total phenolic content in the present study, indicating that increasing ethanol content and lowering solvent polarity may be effective for enhancing the extraction of antioxidant compounds from elephant garlic powder.

Iron chelating ability

The iron chelating ability of elephant garlic powder (AP) and its extracts obtained with water (APW), 50% ethanol (APE50), and 100% ethanol (APE) is presented in Fig. 1C. Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was used as the reference standard. AP, APW, and APE50 exhibited significantly higher chelating ability with increasing concentration (p < 0.05), reaching more than 80% chelation at concentrations of 0.5% and above. In contrast, APE showed substantially lower chelating ability, ranging from 40% to 60%, which was significantly different from the other treatments (p < 0.05). These results of iron chelating ability contrasted with those of total phenolic content, DPPH radical-scavenging activity, and reducing power. Similar findings have been reported previously. Elosta et al. (2017) observed that aged garlic, despite having higher total phenolic content and radical-scavenging activity than fresh garlic, exhibited lower iron chelating capacity, which they attributed to the absence of suitable hydroxyl groups for metal ion binding. Iron chelating activity inhibits metal-catalyzed oxidation by binding Fe²⁺ ions, thereby preventing their participation in the Fenton reaction that generates highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (Le et al., 2007). Mira et al. (2002) demonstrated that only flavonoids with both 3′,4′-catechol and 3-OH groups in the B-ring, such as myricetin and quercetin, exhibited superior Fe³⁺ reduction and chelating activity. Park and Chin (2010) reported that the chelating capacity of garlic extracts was influenced by the extraction solvent and heat treatment, with aqueous extracts of fresh and heated garlic showing 4.8- and 2.8-fold higher activity, respectively, than methanol extracts at equivalent concentrations. In addition, Kittisakulnam et al. (2017) reported differences in antioxidant activity between aqueous and ethanol garlic extracts, suggesting that solvent type may influence antioxidant mechanisms differently. Therefore, the ferrous ion chelating capacity of elephant garlic extracts did not show the same trend as the other antioxidant activity parameters, suggesting that hydrophilic constituents possessing structural features capable of binding metal ions may have contributed to the observed activity.

Reducing power

The reducing power of elephant garlic powder (AP) and its extracts with water (APW), 50% ethanol (APE50), and 100% ethanol (APE) is presented in Fig. 1D. Ascorbic acid (AA, 99.6%, w/w) was used as the reference standard and exhibited a reducing power of 1.5 or higher across all concentrations. All samples showed increased reducing power with increasing concentration (p < 0.05). Reducing power reflects the electron-donating ability of antioxidant constituents and measures their capacity to reduce Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺, thereby stabilizing oxidized intermediates formed during oxidation reactions (Gülçin, 2014). Among the treatments, APE demonstrated higher reducing power than AP at all concentrations (p < 0.05), whereas APW and APE50 showed no differences from AP at any concentration (p > 0.05). These results indicate that as the ethanol ratio in the extraction solvent increased and the polarity decreased, the extraction of antioxidant compounds contributing to reducing power became more effective. The reducing power of elephant garlic extracts showed a trend similar to that observed for total phenolic content and DPPH radical-scavenging activity, suggesting that higher ethanol content enhanced the dissolution of lipophilic antioxidants, thereby increasing reducing power. Alothman et al. (2009) reported that banana and guava extracts prepared with different solvents showed the highest antioxidant recovery with 90% acetone for bananas and with 90% ethanol or 90% acetone for guava. They further concluded that low polarity solvents are suitable for extracting lipophilic phenolic compounds. In line with these findings, APE exhibited the highest reducing power in the present study, suggesting that lower-polarity ethanol conditions may facilitate the extraction of antioxidant constituents from elephant garlic. Therefore, the higher reducing power observed in APE may indicate enhanced electron-donating capacity of antioxidant constituents extracted under lower-polarity solvent conditions.

pH and color values

The pH and color values of pork patties containing 0.5% elephant garlic powder (AP) or its extract powders (APW, APE50, and APE) are shown in Table 4. The pH values of the treated patties ranged from 5.73 to 5.78, with no difference from the CTL (p > 0.05). Kim et al. (2019) reported that the addition of 1% fresh garlic powder (FG) to pork patties increased pH values compared to the control. However, low concentration of 0.5% FG did not affect the pH values of pork patties. During refrigerated storage, pH values increased after three days (p < 0.05), reaching up 5.91 by day 14. This increase might be considered to result from microbial proliferation within the patties. Pork provides a favorable environment for the growth of aerobic microorganisms such as Pseudomonas fragi which might produce alkaline metabolites, including histamine, and generate biogenic amines through protein degradation, resulting in the elevation of pH values (Zhou et al., 2024). These results were consistent with the microbial test results observed during storage in this study. Therefore, the increase in pH values during storage can be attributed to microbial growth and the accumulation of alkaline metabolites and biogenic amines.

The color attributes of pork patties with 0.5% elephant garlic powder (AP) or its extract powders (APW, APE50, and APE) are presented in Table 4. Lightness (L*) values showed no differences among treatments or across storage periods (p > 0.05). Compared with the CTL, the addition of APE decreased redness and increased yellowness values, respectively. The CTL exhibited a redness of 6.52, while patties containing APE showed the lowest value of 5.64 (p < 0.05). The CTL had a yellowness of 11.02, whereas APE increased yellowness to 12.98 (p < 0.05). Park and Chin (2014) reported that adding homogenized garlic at 1.4% and 2.8% to pork patties decreased redness and increased yellowness compared to the control as the inclusion level rose, attributing these changes to the inherent color of garlic extracts. Consistent with previous finding. In the present study the reduced redness and increased yellowness observed with APE were attributed to the inherent dark-brown color of the APE powder.

For the storage effect, redness (a*) decreased with increased storage time, lightness and yellowness values were observed during storage (p > 0.05). These results suggested that the natural color of elephant garlic extract influenced pork patties. Mancini et al. (2019) reported that rabbit patties showed increased yellowness, from 6.55 in the control to 8.43 and 7.03 when 0.25% garlic powder (G) and 0.25% garlic powder with 1.0% salt (GS) were added, respectively, indicating the effect of the natural color of garlic powder. Generally, the redness of meat decreases as myoglobin and oxymyoglobin oxidize to form metmyoglobin, which imparts a brown color. Dursun and Güler (2023) reported that freshly crushed garlic promoted the oxidation of ferrous ions in myoglobin, thereby accelerating metmyoglobin formation and causing discoloration. They observed a decrease in redness from 16.0 in the control to 13.4 and 11.6 with the addition of 1% and 2% crushed garlic, respectively, to ground beef. In the present study, color changes varied depending on the treatment group, which may be associated with differences in extract characteristics depending on the extraction solvent used for elephant garlic. These differences likely affected the rate of myoglobin oxidation and metmyoglobin formation. In particular, patties with APE tended to exhibit discoloration, likely attributable to the inherently dark-brown color of the APE extract powder. These color changes may represent a limitation for the practical application of APE in meat products, despite its antioxidant effectiveness. Therefore, future studies should explore strategies to improve the color stability and consumer acceptability of elephant garlic extract powder.

TBARS

The TBARS values of pork patties with 0.5% elephant garlic powder (AP) or its extract powders (APW, APE50, and APE) are presented in Fig. 2A. Unlike CTL, patties containing elephant garlic extracts (APW, APE50, APE) or REF showed no differences in TBARS values from the initial storage period to 7 days of storage (p > 0.05), indicating that elephant garlic extracts effectively suppressed early lipid oxidation. Among the treatments, APE exhibited the strongest antioxidant effect, similar values to those of REF up to day 10 (p > 0.05). Both APE50 and APW also lowered TBARS compared with CTL up to day 14 (p < 0.05). TBARS values increased in all treatments as storage progressed (p < 0.05). Kang et al. (2018) reported that 75% ethanol extraction maximized the recovery of diallyl trisulfide and ABTS radical-scavenging activity in garlic, supporting the superior lipid oxidation inhibition observed for APE and APE50 in this study. These antioxidant effects may be associated with phenolic compounds previously reported in elephant garlic (Emir et al., 2022). Overall, the inhibitory effect on lipid oxidation appeared to vary depending on extraction solvent polarity. Among the treatments, APE showed the highest antioxidant activity, and APE50 and APW also showed lower TBARS than the control in the late storage period. This pattern is consistent with prior reports that higher ethanol fractions tend to favor the extraction of relatively lower-polarity constituents in Allium extracts, which may contribute to the stronger TBARS suppression observed with ethanol extracts. These antioxidant mechanisms, including radical scavenging activity, electron-donating capacity, and metal-chelating effects, may contribute to inhibition of lipid peroxidation chain reactions in meat systems (Park & Chin, 2010). Considering the antioxidant activity results obtained in this study (Fig. 1), including radical-scavenging activity, electron-donating capacity, and metal-chelating ability, the addition of ethanol-based elephant garlic extract powder may have contributed to stabilization of lipid radicals and suppression of lipid peroxidation chain reactions during refrigerated storage of ground pork patties. Generally, TBARS values exceeding 0.5–1.0 mg MDA/kg have been associated with the development of rancid odor and oxidative deterioration in meat products (Tarladgis et al., 1960). In the present study, CTL reached this range at approximately day 7, whereas APE-treated patties reached comparable TBARS levels at approximately day 11, suggesting that APE supplementation may have delayed lipid oxidation in pork patties by approximately 4 days during refrigerated storage.

POV

The peroxide values (POV) of pork patties with 0.5% elephant garlic powder (AP) or its extract powders (APW, APE50, and APE) are presented in Fig. 2B. Patties containing elephant garlic extracts (APW, APE50, APE) or REF didn’t increase POV values from the initial storage period to day 7 (p > 0.05), indicating that elephant garlic extracts might effectively suppress lipid oxidation during the early storage period. This suggests that the accumulation of primary lipid oxidation products was not pronounced during the early storage period in patties containing elephant garlic extracts. In particular, patties containing APE showed similar values to those of REF up to 10 days of storage (p > 0.05), demonstrating strong inhibition of the formation of primary lipid oxidation products. Both APE50 and APW reduced POV compared with CTL at day 14 of storage (p < 0.05). POV values increased in all treatments as storage progressed. Zhang et al. (2016a) reported that beef patties with garlic extracted using 50% ethanol exhibited lower POV values than untreated patties (CTL) after 14 days of storage. A similar inhibitory effect on lipid oxidation was observed in the treatments of APE50 and APE in this study. This observation is consistent with the antioxidant activity results (Fig. 1), where treatments showing higher antioxidant activity exhibited lower TBARS and POV values, suggesting that the radical scavenging activity and reducing power of the extracts may have contributed to the inhibition of lipid oxidation in pork patties. A similar reduction in peroxide value was also reported by Lishianawati and Yusiati (2022), who observed that increasing the addition level of black garlic powder in duck nuggets enhanced total phenolic content and radical-scavenging activity while reducing peroxide values during refrigerated storage. This trend is consistent with the present study and may suggest that antioxidant constituents present in elephant garlic extract powder contributed to the inhibition of lipid oxidation through radical-scavenging activity and electron-donating capacity. Phenolic compounds are known to act as hydrogen and electron donors, thereby contributing to free radical stabilization and inhibition of oxidation reactions (Falowo et al., 2014). POV reflect the formation of lipid hydroperoxides as primary oxidation products, whereas TBARS indicates secondary oxidation products generated from the decomposition of these compounds. Therefore, combined interpretation of POV and TBARS is necessary to better understand the progression of lipid oxidation during refrigerated storage (Domínguez et al., 2019). As both TBARS and POV values showed similar trends, the APE treatment appears to have effectively delayed lipid oxidation in pork patties during refrigerated storage. These results suggest that antioxidant constituents present in elephant garlic may have contributed to the inhibition of lipid oxidation, and that ethanol-based extracts may serve as more effective natural antioxidant sources for controlling lipid oxidation in pork patties compared with water extracts under the present experimental conditions.

Volatile basic nitrogen (VBN)

The volatile basic nitrogen (VBN) values of pork patties with 0.5% elephant garlic powder (AP) or its extract powders are presented during storage in Fig. 2C. No differences were among treatments observed up to day 7 (p > 0.05), however, patties with APE50 showed lower VBN values than CTL thereafter (p < 0.05). On day 14, VBN values of patties with AP exhibited lower than those of control, whereas those containing APW, APE50, and APE (p < 0.05). This reduction may be associated with antimicrobial constituents present in elephant garlic extracts, which may have contributed to inhibition of microbial growth during storage. VBN values increased in all treatments as storage time progressed (p < 0.05). This trend was consistent with the microbiological analysis results obtained in this study, suggesting that amino acid degradation by proteolytic gram-negative bacteria, such as Pseudomonas spp. and Salmonella spp., may have been associated with the increase in VBN values during storage (Alur et al., 1994). Zhang et al. (2016b) reported that pork loin treated with 0.1% and 0.5% black pepper essential oil (BPEO) at 4 °C for 9 days had lower total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) values than the control, an effect attributed to terpenoid mediated suppression of Pseudomonas spp. and Enterobacteriaceae. Similarly, Lee et al. (2019) found that pork patties supplemented with 0.5% fermented garlic extract or aged garlic extract and stored at 4 °C exhibited lower VBN values than the control up to 21-days of storage. Organosulfur compounds previously reported in Allium species are known to react with bacterial sulfhydryl groups or proteins to form disulfide bonds, thereby limiting microbial proliferation during storage (Bhatwalkar et al., 2021). These findings are consistent with the present study, suggesting that elephant garlic extracts may have partially delayed VBN accumulation through inhibition of proteolytic microbial activity during refrigerated storage (Fig. 3).

Microbial counts

The microbiological analysis of pork patties with 0.5% elephant garlic powder (AP) or its extract powders is presented in Fig. 3. Addition of the elephant garlic extracts tended to suppress total bacterial counts during refrigerated storage. Although no changes were observed up to day 3 (p > 0.05), they increased progressively until the end of storage. On day 7, patties treated with APE50 exhibited lower total bacterial counts than CTL (p < 0.05). By day 14, patties containing APW, APE50, or APE all showed lower total bacterial counts compared with CTL (p < 0.05). The microbial counts of Enterobacteriaceae were lower in the APW, APE50, and APE treatments, as compared with CTL on day 7 (p < 0.05). By day 14, patties treated with APE50 and APE recorded 4.98–5.04 log CFU/g, suggesting inhibition of microbial growth compared with CTL (p < 0.05). As storage progressed, total bacterial counts and Enterobacteriaceae counts increased overall, during storage time (p < 0.05). Similar to previous studies, patties with AP did not exhibit inhibition of microbial growth compared with CTL. However, APE50 and APE showed stronger antimicrobial effects, resulting in the suppression of microbial growth in pork patties. The antimicrobial activity of garlic has been widely reported in various foods. Sallam et al. (2004) observed that chicken sausages with fresh garlic (FG), garlic powder (GP), or garlic oil (GO) exhibited lower aerobic plate counts (APC) than untreated sausages over 21 days, with 2% FG and 0.6% GP showing the greatest reductions. Park and Chin (2010) reported that pork patties with 0.5% water extract (WEHG) or methanol extract (MEHG) of garlic, both heated at 100 °C for 30 min, exhibited lower total bacterial counts and Enterobacteriaceae counts than the control over 28 days. These findings suggest that differences in antimicrobial activity among the extracts may be associated with variations in the composition of bioactive compounds depending on the extraction solvent. In the present study, APE50 and APE showed stronger inhibition of microbial growth than APW (p < 0.05). These results were consistent with Yin and Cheng (2003), who demonstrated that hydrophobic sulfur compounds such as diallyl sulfide and diallyl disulfide effectively suppressed pathogenic bacterial growth in meat products, whereas hydrophilic sulfur compounds such as S-ethyl cysteine and N-acetylcysteine exhibited relatively lower antibacterial activity. Therefore, the addition of APE50 and APE prepared by ethanol extraction with elephant garlic powder may be associated with extraction of relatively hydrophobic antimicrobial constituents under ethanol-based solvent conditions, which may have contributed to suppression of microbial growth in pork patties.

Conclusion

Among the tested solvents (water, 50% ethanol, and 100% ethanol) under the ultrasonic-assisted extraction conditions used in this study (20 kHz, 250 W, 15 min), differences in antioxidant activity of elephant garlic extracts were observed. Among the ultrasonic-assisted extracts, the 100% ethanol extract (APE) showed the highest TPC, DPPH radical-scavenging activity, and reducing power, indicating superior antioxidant capacity (p < 0.05). When applied to pork patties, all solvent extracts (APW, APE50, APE) lowered TBARS, POV, and VBN during storage, with APE providing the greatest overall suppression (p < 0.05). APE50 (0.5%) maintained redness (a*) and color stability relative to AP (p < 0.05). Patties with APE50 or APE exhibited lower Enterobacteriaceae counts than the control (p < 0.05). Thus, APE50 and APE, particularly APE, may serve as potential natural antioxidant and antimicrobial sources under the conditions tested in this study, improving the oxidative and microbiological stability of pork patties during refrigerated storage and suggesting a potential shelf-life extension of approximately 4 days compared with the control. The present study was conducted to evaluate the potential application of elephant garlic extracts as natural functional ingredients in pork patties during refrigerated storage and to provide fundamental information regarding their functionality in meat product systems. However, the addition of elephant garlic extracts affected color characteristics compared with the control, which may represent a limitation for commercial application. Therefore, further studies are needed to develop additional processing strategies for practical application, as well as to characterize the bioactive compounds associated with the functional properties of elephant garlic extract powders and evaluate sensory properties and consumer acceptability in meat products.
