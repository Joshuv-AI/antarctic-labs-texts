Non-suicidal self-injury and digital ostracism: a novel smartphone-based paradigm capturing real-time responses to social media exclusion
Authors: Carlos Schmidt, Stella Nicolaou, Daniela Otero, Josep Marco-Pallarés, Diana Castilla, Irene Zaragoza, Joaquim Soler, Juan Carlos Pascual, Daniel Vega
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOstracism on social media is a common and emotionally salient stressor. However, its moment-to-moment psychological and behavioral consequences, remain poorly understood in individuals with Non-Suicidal Self-Injury (NSSI) and Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD).MethodsUsing an Instagram-mimicking smartphone platform (Socializapp) coupled with Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA), we examined responses to experimentally manipulated peer feedback across 7 days in three groups: healthy Controls (HC; n = 20), NSSI without BPD (NSSI; n = 18), and comorbid BPD+NSSI (n = 17). Participants were exposed to Inclusion, Partial Exclusion, and Exclusion conditions. Linear and generalized linear mixed-effects models tested within-person effects on perceived exclusion and approval, affective reactivity, objective engagement (Likes and Comments) and NSSI urges, with borderline traits (BPQ) modeled dimensionally.ResultsCompliance was high, supporting feasibility. The manipulation was perceived as intended, with perceived exclusion increasing and perceived approval decreasing stepwise from Inclusion to Partial and Full Exclusion across participants. Exclusion also increased negative affect, reduced positive affect, and suppressed effortful interaction (fewer comments). In exploratory analyses, Exclusion was associated with more than a threefold increase in the odds of reporting an NSSI urge, after adjusting for borderline severity. While the data more clearly supported a baseline negative appraisal bias associated with borderline severity (even under objectively favorable feedback) evidence for cross-level moderation effects remained inconclusive.ConclusionsSocial media ostracism emerges as a relevant interpersonal stressor for individuals with NSSI, producing detectable shifts in online engagement and, in exploratory analysis, increases in self-harm urges and changes. Borderline severity tracked a sustained negative interpretation of social feedback, although potential moderation effects regarding exclusion reactivity remained inconclusive. These findings establish Socializapp as a feasible paradigm and highlight everyday digital social experiences as meaningful targets for understanding short-term fluctuations in NSSI vulnerability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40479-026-00364-0.Keywords: Non-suicidal self-injury, Borderline personality disorder, Social media ostracism, Young women

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), the deliberate, self-inflicted damage of body tissue in the absence of suicidal intent, represents one of the most pressing yet insufficiently understood challenges in contemporary youth mental health [1, 2]. NSSI is common in community samples of adolescents and young adults [3, 4], with even higher prevalence rates in clinical populations [5]. Importantly, NSSI indexes broader emotional and functional vulnerability [6]. Beyond its cross-sectional associations with greater psychological distress and impairment, NSSI prospectively predicts elevated risk for subsequent psychopathology and suicidal thoughts and behavior [7, 8]. The increasing prevalence of NSSI over recent decades underscore that it is an escalating and increasingly urgent public health concern. Notwithstanding considerable advances in NSSI research, fundamental questions remain unresolved. For example, one central question is determining how contemporary sociocultural environments, particularly the pervasive digital ecosystem in which youth development is now embedded, modulate NSSI vulnerability [2].

The widespread adoption of social media has reshaped how young people connect, seek support, and evaluate their social standing. While these environments can facilitate connection, their design can also intensify interpersonal stress, potentially producing experiences that may differ in salience, persistence, and psychobiological impact from offline equivalents [9]. These structural transformations may hold particular significance for young people with NSSI, who appear to exhibit differential vulnerability to the features of digital social environments [10, 11]. Many young people who self-harm report using social media platforms to seek support or communicate distress [12], yet greater engagement may also increase contact with cyberbullying, rejection, and NSSI-related material, which can contribute to the normalization and social reinforcement of NSSI [13]. Converging neurobiological findings further suggest altered valuation of social media feedback. In a recent fMRI study using participant’s own Instagram photos, Nicolaou et al. [14] found that young women with NSSI (particularly those with higher clinical severity) rated positive comments as less pleasant and negative comments as more unpleasant than controls, alongside blunted activation in core reward regions when processing positive versus negative feedback. These patterns are consistent with heightened sensitivity to negative evaluation and diminished reward responsivity to positive social cues, suggesting that the same platform features that facilitate connection for most users may create an asymmetric risk-reward profile for NSSI-vulnerable individuals, amplifying the impact of negative feedback while diminishing the protective value of positive social engagement.

Prior work has identified perceived social exclusion as a key contributor to mental health issues [15, 16]. Studies using laboratory paradigms of ostracism (i.e., being ignored and/or excluded), most notably Cyberball [17, 18], provide experimental evidence that even brief episodes of exclusion elicit immediate increase in negative affect and impaired self-regulatory function, and engagement of neural systems implicated in affective distress and salience processing [19, 20]. Consistently, converging evidence suggests that sensitivity to social exclusion is elevated among individuals with emotional dysregulation and NSSI. Thus, individuals with borderline personality disorder (BPD; a condition marked by severe NSSI and interpersonal hypersensitivity) exhibit heightened neural and affective reactivity to induced ostracism [21–23]. Similarly, adolescents with NSSI history show divergent neural processing of exclusion, stronger emotional responses to rejection, and slower recovery from exclusion-induced distress relative to depressed and healthy peers [24, 25]. These patterns converge with clinical evidence identifying social exclusion as a major proximal risk factor promoting NSSI [26, 27].

Although online ostracism may elicit effects comparable to face-to-face exclusion [28, 29], studies using experimental paradigms that model digitally mediated social exclusion remain scarce. This gap is particularly relevant because digital platforms, unlike transient offline interactions, transform social feedback into signals that are quantifiable [30]. To address the limited ecological validity of classic paradigms, more naturalistic experimental approaches have emerged. The Ostracism Online paradigm, for instance, integrates controlled inclusion and ostracism manipulations within a simulated social media interface by varying contingent peer feedback (e.g., ‘likes’) on participants’ posts [31]. Similarly, O-Cam simulates a web-conference interaction in which participants are ostracized or included by partners whose pre-recorded actions appear spontaneous and authentic [32]. Across studies, these paradigms suggest that ostracism in social networking contexts impairs perceived social connectedness and increases negative affect, including feelings of rejection, sadness, and anxiety [33–37]. However, they are unused in NSSI populations and, given that they are typically implemented as desktop-based laboratory tasks, they may not adequately capture the immersive, mobile, and context-dependent nature of smartphone-mediated social media use in daily life. Moreover, single-session laboratory designs cannot capture how responses to digital exclusion fluctuate within individuals in daily life, where exclusion is most proximal to behaviors such as NSSI.

In the current study, we address this gap by introducing and providing an initial validation of a smartphone-based paradigm for studying ostracism within social networking contexts. First, we developed ‘Socializapp’, an innovative smartphone App that emulates core Instagram-like functionalities and integrates an experimentally controlled inclusion versus exclusion manipulation, analogous to established paradigms such as Cyberball. Rather than limiting assessment to a single laboratory session (as in previous studies), the same App integrates an Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) component to capture real-world within-person dynamics. This EMA protocol allowed us to capture, within individuals and in daily life, the temporal coupling between perceived social exclusion, the emergence of negative affect, and subsequent urges to NSSI while using the app and interacting with other users. EMA is particularly well-suited to this aim because experiences of exclusion and urges to self-injure can fluctuate substantially over short time windows, and momentary sampling improves ecological validity and reduces retrospective bias [38, 39].

To provide an initial validation of this paradigm, we recruited three groups of young women spanning a clinically meaningful continuum of severity: (i) a clinical BPD+NSSI group, (ii) a subclinical NSSI group without other mental disorders, and (iii) healthy controls. Sampling across this continuum allowed us to evaluate the paradigm across increasing levels of clinical vulnerability and to explore whether responses to digital exclusion were more closely linked to self-injury, borderline pathology, or their co-occurrence. We therefore evaluated feasibility through EMA compliance and app engagement, and within-person sensitivity to the feedback manipulation through momentary changes in perceived exclusion, perceived approval, affect, engagement, and NSSI urges. We hypothesized that reduced social feedback would increase perceived exclusion and negative affect, decrease perceived approval and engagement, and, among participants with NSSI, increase momentary urges to self-injure. We also explored whether these responses scaled across the NSSI-BPD clinical continuum, expecting stronger effects among participants with comorbid BPD, although these between-person analyses were considered hypothesis-generating.

Methods

Participants and procedures

We recruited 65 participants, divided into three groups. Two groups presented recurrent and recent NSSI (engagement in NSSI on ≥ 5 days in the past year). One of them was clinical and the other subclinical. The clinical group (NSSI + BPD) included 21 women with BPD, recruited from the Hospital of Igualada and the Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau (Barcelona, Spain), where they were receiving psychological and/or psychiatric treatment. In the BPD+NSSI group, BPD diagnoses were confirmed using the Revised Diagnostic Interview for Borderlines (DIB-R), the structured diagnostic instrument routinely employed in the recruiting clinical units. In this group, additional exclusion criteria included active psychotic symptoms, bipolar disorder, active substance use disorder and cognitive impairment. The subclinical group (NSSI) included 21 women who did not meet the diagnostic threshold for BPD or any other current psychiatric condition. They were recruited in the community and had not received psychological or psychiatric treatment previously. The third group was a healthy control group (HC), which included 23 women with no history of NSSI, other psychiatric disorders, or psychological/psychiatric treatment. All participants were women, aged 18–30, with an active Instagram account. Specifically, only participants with Android smartphone were included in this study due to technical characteristics of the app used.

All participants completed a self-report assessment and a clinician-administered interview. They were then introduced to ‘Socializapp’, a smartphone application developed to model ostracism within social networking environments (see below).

The final sample of study comprised 55 participants who completed ≥ 80% of EMA assessments (NSSI + BPD = 17; NSSI = 18; HC = 20). Consequently, 10 participants were excluded from the analyses because they did not complete the minimum number of responses per experimental condition required. All participants signed an informed consent form and received monetary compensation for their participation in the study. Upon completion of the 7-day protocol, all participants received a full debriefing, including disclosure that the other user profiles were simulated and that all feedback was experimentally predetermined by the research team. All procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Bellvitge University Hospital [PR074/23 (CSA PR9/2023)] and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measures

Experimental paradigm

We developed Socializapp, a new smartphone paradigm modeled after the Cyberball procedure but implemented in an Instagram-like environment. Participants were informed that, over the subsequent 7 days, they would interact with ten other study participants by posting photographs and exchanging likes and comments. Participants were unaware that the remaining profiles were simulated, and the interaction dynamics (including feedback and comments) were predefined by the research team. This feedback was co-developed between research team and a youth advisory panel (Table S1).

In each interaction with the app, participants viewed others’ posts and the ongoing exchanges between users (likes and comments), and accessed the feedback received on their own posts (Fig. 1A). The experimental manipulation systematically varied the amount of incoming feedback to participants’ content (Inclusion, Partial Exclusion, Exclusion; Fig. 1C) while keeping the wider social context visible. Immediately after viewing the feedback environment, participants completed a brief EMA (see below). Next, participants were instructed to post a new photo (Fig. 1A). Sessions were prompted via app alerts twice a day to support adherence (Fig. 1B). Each session comprised exposure to the feedback environment, EMA completion, and the posting of new content. Prompts were therefore event-locked rather than intended for intensive random sampling of spontaneous states. Twice-daily sessions balanced repeated within-person assessment with ecological plausibility, as more frequent posting would be inconsistent with typical social media use (see Supplementary Material).

We implemented a three-level feedback manipulation (Inclusion, Partial Exclusion, Exclusion) rather than Cyberball’s original binary design to better approximate how exclusion manifests on social media, where ostracism typically involves reduced engagement (fewer likes and comments) rather than complete absence of feedback [33, 40]. A partial exclusion condition is well-established in modified Cyberball paradigms and has been shown to capture dynamic, time-varying behavioral responses that are not observable in an inclusion-exclusion comparison [41, 42]. In our smartphone protocol, the intermediate condition also served a feasibility and adherence function. The graded design further enables dose-response analysis.

After installation, Socializapp was active for one week. For ethical reasons, participants were instructed to avoid uploading identifiable personal photographs (e.g., selfies). In addition, the app included a risk alert system whereby predefined triggers generated a notification to the study principal investigator, alongside a help icon directing participants to a dedicated webpage with risk-management resources.

Ecological momentary assessment (EMA)

The app used for the current study (Socializapp) includes an EMA assessment (Fig. 1A). It aims to capture real-time data on dynamic psychological processes within naturalistic contexts, including affect, social interactions, and NSSI features [43], which are not fully captured by baseline self-reports due to their fluctuating nature. EMA assessment was done after interaction with other (fictitious) users by using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 10 (very much).

The first EMA item was ‘To what extent do you think others liked your photo?‘. Adapted from prior social-media ostracism paradigms [35], this item indexes perceived peer approval of participants’ own post. Because it was completed after viewing both own likes and comments and peer’s exchanges, the item reflects how approved they felt on the feedback they just observed, including how they compared with peers in the same feed. The variance structure of this EMA construct was examined using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) derived from an unconditional multilevel model. The item yielded an ICC of 0.266, indicating that a substantial portion of the variance (73.4%) was driven by momentary, within-person fluctuations. This supports the item’s sensitivity to momentary fluctuation in perceived approval.

Second, perceived social exclusion was assessed to capture momentary experiences of ostracism. Three EMA items were used, adapted from prior work [44] and consistent with methodological recommendations for assessing social exclusion [45]. These items were: (i) ‘To what extent do you feel ignored?‘, (ii) ‘To what extent do you feel excluded?‘, and (iii) ‘To what extent do you feel rejected?‘. The three items demonstrated good internal consistency in the present sample (α = 0.89).

Third, negative and positive affect were assessed to index participants’ immediate emotional responses to social interactions. Following established protocols investigating real-world reactions to social exclusion [44], and in order to minimize participant burden during intensive sampling, discrete state emotional items were utilized instead of lengthier global affect scales. Negative affect was measured using two items: (i) ‘To what extent do you feel sad?’ and (ii) ‘To what extent do you feel angry?‘. Positive affect was measured using the following two items: (i) ‘To what extent do you feel happy?’ and (ii) ‘To what extent do you feel calm?‘. These items have been previously employed in EMA research [44]. The within-construct correlations were high (r = 0.61) for negative affect and moderate (r = 0.59) for positive affect, indicating adequate reliability for the combined measures.

Finally, NSSI urge was assessed with the item: ‘Are you thinking about self-injuring right now?‘, while NSSI engagement was evaluated with the question: ‘In the last 24 hours, have you self-injured?‘. Both items were assessed using a binary outcome (Yes/No). These prompts have been previously used in EMA research (e.g [26]).

Self-report measures

The Non-Suicidal Self-Injury Disorder Scale (NSSIDS; [46]) is a self-report measure designed to assess proposed criteria for Non-Suicidal Self-Injury Disorder. In this scale, the items 3 (“to obtain relief from negative feelings or thoughts”) and 4 (“to cope with problems with other people”) were used to assess intrapersonal and interpersonal motivations, respectively. The Spanish version of the NSSIDS shows good internal reliability (α = 0.88). In the present sample, the NSSIDS showed good internal consistency (α = 0.80).

The Borderline Personality Questionnaire (BPQ; [47]) assesses BPD traits or symptoms. It consists of 80 statements that assess nine subscales based on the DSM-IV criteria: Impulsiveness, Affective Instability, Abandonment, Relationship, Self-Image, Suicide/Self-Mutilation, Emptiness, Intense Anger, and Quasi-Psychotic States. The total BPQ score was used as a measure of general BPD traits. The Spanish version of the BPQ presents moderate to high internal reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.78 to 0.93) through the nine scales. The internal consistency of the total BPQ scale in the present sample was excellent (α = 0.90).

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21; [48]) assesses psychological distress through the frequency of 21 negative emotional symptoms during the week prior to the evaluation. DASS-21 consists of 3 subscales: Depression, Anxiety, and Stress. The Spanish version presents moderate to good internal reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.84, 0.70 and 0.82) for each of the subscales, respectively. In the present sample, the total DASS-21 score demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.94).

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; [49]). The Spanish version of the MSPSS was used, which is one of the most broadly used scales to rate perceptions of social support. It consists of a 12-item scale, which assesses three sources of perceived social support: (i) family, (ii) friends, and (iii) significant others. The Spanish version of the MSPSS shows good internal reliability (α = 0.88) [50]. In the present sample, the MSPSS showed excellent internal consistency (α = 0.90).

Statistical analysis

Group differences in sociodemographic and clinical variables were tested using one-way ANOVA for continuous outcomes when assumptions were approximately met. Otherwise, Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied. Significant group effects were further examined using post-hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction to account for multiple testing. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests.

Feasibility was evaluated via compliance (proportion of completed EMA prompts) and in-app behavioral indicators (daily counts of likes and comments produced), described overall and compared across groups (Kruskal–Wallis test), experimental conditions (Friedman test), and study days.

To quantify the proportion of between- versus within-person variance across all intensive longitudinal outcomes, Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICCs) were computed utilizing Nakagawa’s approach via the performance package in R. For continuous psychological and affective dimensions, ICCs were derived from three-level unconditional models specifying nested random intercepts for days within participants (1 | ID/Day). Conversely, for digital engagement and clinical outcomes, two-level generalized linear mixed models with random intercepts at the participant level (1 | ID) were employed. Crucially, all baseline mixed models included the experimental Condition as a fixed effect; this approach ensures that the resulting ICC estimations accurately reflect stable, natural individual differences and daily variations rather than variance artificially induced by the smartphone-based paradigm manipulation.

Our primary analysis tested the within-person effects of feedback manipulation. For continuous outcomes, each EMA dimension was regressed on Condition (Inclusion, Partial Exclusion, Exclusion) and Time of Day (morning vs. afternoon) as fixed effects, with borderline personality traits (BPQ total score, grand-mean centered) included as a person-level covariate, using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs). To evaluate differences in app engagement (Likes and Comments), Negative Binomial Generalized Linear Mixed Models (NB-GLMM) with a log link function were employed. This distribution was selected to account for the count-based nature of the engagement variables, which exhibited significant overdispersion and a non-normal distribution. Finally, for the binary outcome (NSSI urges), a Logistic GLMM was used to analyze the likelihood of occurrence. The LMMs for continuous outcomes (perceived exclusion, approval, and affect) utilized 667 assessment-level observations contributing from 55 participants who provided valid EMA records. The NB-GLMMs for digital engagement metrics included 770 observations from the same sample. Finally, the Logistic GLMM for NSSI urges (restricted to the NSSI-only and BPD+NSSI groups) utilized 423 assessment-level observations from 35 participants.

In a separate and complementary set of models aimed at exploring between-person moderation dynamics, cross-level interactions were examined utilizing both dimensional and categorical approaches. First, moderation by baseline borderline traits was evaluated by entering the centered BPQ score and its interaction with Condition into the primary models. Second, categorical diagnostic effects were tested entering Group (HC, NSSI, BPD+NSSI) and its interaction with Condition as fixed effects. These full interaction models are reported in the Supplementary Material.

To account for individual baseline differences and temporal clustering, a specific random-effects structure was defined. All categorical predictors (Condition and Group) were entered using dummy coding, with Inclusion and HC serving as the respective reference categories. For the LMMs, a three-level nested random intercept structure was retained (1 | ID/Day), where assessment days were nested within participants, allowing us to control for day-specific variations without encountering convergence anomalies. Conversely, for the NB-GLMMs (Likes and Comments) and the Logistic GLMM (NSSI Urges), a parsimonious two-level random intercept for each participant (1 | ID) was strictly maintained. This simpler structure was necessary to prevent the severe model non-convergence. Owing to the absence of variance in NSSI urges among healthy controls, urge models were restricted to participants with NSSI (the NSSI and BPD+NSSI groups).

Model parameters were estimated using Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) for LMMs and Maximum Likelihood (ML) for both NB-GLMMs and Logistic GLMMs. Results for the NB-GLMM are reported as incidence rate ratios, while Logistic GLMM results are reported as Odds Ratios (OR), all with 95% confidence intervals.

Lastly, a series of retrospective power and sensitivity analyses was conducted via Monte Carlo simulations (1,000 iterations per model) using the simr package in R. This simulation-based approach was executed to quantify the exact statistical power available to detect within-person main effects versus cross-level interactions given our achieved sample size and data structure. Furthermore, these simulations evaluated the intrinsic sensitivity of our design by determining whether our empirical framework possessed sufficient capacity to reach the conventional 80% power threshold across the distinct level predictors.

Formal Monte Carlo simulation-based power analyses revealed high power to detect within-person effects of Condition for perceived social exclusion and approval, both reaching 100.0% power (95% CI [99.63, 100.0]). Power was also high for negative affect (98.8%, 95% CI [97.91, 99.38]) and digital comments (99.7%, 95% CI [99.13, 99.94]), and more moderate for positive affect (87.9%, 95% CI [85.72, 89.86]), NSSI urges (80.2%, 95% CI [77.59, 82.63]), and the overdispersed social media likes metric (70.5%, 95% CI [67.57, 73.31]).

In contrast, sensitivity was limited for cross-level interactions. For Condition × BPQ models, adequate power was reached only for digital comments (82.0%, 95% CI [79.48, 84.33]), whereas power was lower for negative affect (45.8%) and likes (32.9%), and markedly limited for NSSI urges, perceived exclusion, perceived approval, and positive affect (< 27%). Similarly, Group × Condition interactions remained below the 80% threshold across all outcomes, ranging from 13.3% for negative affect (95% CI [11.26, 15.56]) to 57.4% for likes (95% CI [54.27, 60.49]). This pattern reflects the expected asymmetry of the design, with extensive within-person sampling but limited between-person power.

Consequently, to align the manuscript’s theoretical weight with the empirical capacity of our data, the primary focus of this paper is centered on the validation of the smartphone-based paradigm and its momentary within-person processes. All dimensional (BPQ) and categorical (Group) cross-level interaction analyses are explicitly treated as secondary and exploratory. The full between-group interaction models are provided in the Supplementary Material.

All analyses were performed in RStudio using the lme4, lmerTest, and MASS packages.

Results

Demographic and psychological variables: between-group comparisons

Groups did not differ in age or daily social media use (Table 1). The NSSI groups showed higher borderline traits (BPQ; BPD+NSSI > NSSI) and general distress (DASS-21), as well as lower perceived social support (MSPSS), relative to HC (all p<.001). Within the self-injury groups, NSSI methods, functions and age of onset did not significantly differ.

Feasibility: EMA compliance and app use

Adherence to the EMA protocol was high, with 667 of 770 scheduled assessments completed (86.62% overall compliance). At the individual level, participants completed a mean of 12.13 assessments (SD = 2.25; median = 13, corresponding to a 92.86% individual compliance rate). Compliance did not differ significantly across groups (HC: 87.14%; NSSI: 89.29%; BPD+NSSI: 79.29%; x2(2) = 4.25, p = 0.11), Condition (Inclusion: 87.88%; Partial Exclusion: 84.55%; Exclusion: 86.82%; x2(2) = 1.27, p = 0.52) or study days (ranging from 90.91% on Day 1 to 87.27% on Day 7; x2(6) = 4.66, p = 0.58). The absence of differential missingness across conditions and days supports the plausibility of the missing-at-random assumption underlying likelihood-based estimation. In addition to protocol adherence, participants remained behaviorally active throughout the study, demonstrating sustained digital engagement within the application. Overall, participants produced a mean of 10.36 likes (SD = 10.53) and a mean of 2.04 comments (SD = 4.34) per assessment.

Variance decomposition and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC)

Adjusted ICCs, controlling for experimental conditions, revealed substantial clustering across all intensive longitudinal outcomes, supporting the multilevel modeling framework (Table 2). For the psychological dimensions, modeled with a three-level structure (assessments within days within participants), perceptions of social exclusion and social media approval showed total adjusted ICCs of 0.470 and 0.479, leaving over half of the variance at the momentary level. Affective dimensions were more trait-like (negative affect: 0.593; positive affect: 0.615), though momentary fluctuations still accounted for 38.5%-40.7% of the total variance. Taken together, these results indicate that a substantial share of the psychological processes under study unfolds within days, supporting the use of ecological momentary assessment over retrospective measures.

Digital engagement, modeled at two levels (daily observations within participants), showed diverging patterns. On one hand, roughly half of the variance in likes reflected stable individual differences (ICC = 0.520) whereas, on the other hand, commenting was almost entirely person-dependent (ICC = 0.932), with day-to-day fluctuations accounting for only 6.8% of the variance.

Finally, among participants with a history of self-injury, NSSI urges manifested substantial between-person stability (adjusted ICC = 0.756). This coefficient suggests that urge endorsement operated as a persistent baseline vulnerability throughout the assessment period rather than a transient state, while the remaining 24.4% of the variance occurred at the momentary level, potentially reflecting contextual fluctuations.

Perceived ostracism and approval on social media

Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were conducted to examine the effects of experimental conditions on perceived social media exclusion throughout the assessment period (Table 3, Model 1). A significant main effect of Condition emerged, with higher exclusion ratings under both Partial Exclusion and Exclusion relative to Inclusion (see Fig. 2A). Borderline personality traits (BPQ) were positively associated with social exclusion ratings (b = 0.02, p = 0.04). Perceived approval showed the opposite pattern (Table 3, Model 2; Fig. 2B). Both Partial Exclusion and Exclusion significantly reduced approval ratings relative to Inclusion. Approval was also lower in afternoon assessments (b=-0.35, p = 0.01), and higher BPQ scores predicted lower overall approval ratings (b=-0.03, p <0.001).Together, Models 1–2 indicate that the manipulation was perceived as intended, with effects scaling with the intensity of the exclusion condition; the role of borderline traits in these perceptions is examined in detail below.

Positive and negative affectivity

Affective responses were driven specifically by full Exclusion (Table 3, Models 3–4). Negative affect increased under Exclusion but not under Partial Exclusion. Accordingly, positive affect decreased under Exclusion, with non-significant trend under Partial Exclusion. BPQ scores were associated with higher negative affect and lower positive affect. Thus, perceptions were sensitive to both exclusion conditions, while affective responses were specifically elicited by complete exclusion.

Behavioral engagement: likes and comments

Beyond subjective perceptions, we evaluated the direct impact of the experimental manipulation on participants’ digital behavior by analyzing the number of likes and comments generated (Table 3, Models 5 and 6; Figs. 3A-B). For likes, a significant reduction in behavioral engagement was observed specifically under the Partial Exclusion condition relative to inclusion, whereas full Exclusion did not yield a significant change. For comments, a decline in engagement emerged across both experimental conditions, with both Partial Exclusion and Exclusion leading to a decrease in comment production relative to Inclusion. This dampening effect on behavioral engagement was driven by the experimental manipulation and was independent of BPQ scores (both p >0.15). Taken together, these findings highlight a distinct behavioral dynamic between likes and comments. While liking may function as a low-effort, potentially compensatory response under full exclusion, comments—which represent a more effortful form of digital interaction—are systematically suppressed during social ostracism.

NSSI urge and behavior

To evaluate momentary self-injury risk, the presence of NSSI urges was analyzed using a logistic generalized linear mixed model (GLMM; Table 3, Model 7) among participants with a history of NSSI. Given the relatively low overall frequency of these clinical events during the 7-day protocol, this multi-level estimates should be interpreted with caution and treated as exploratory. A significant main effect of Condition emerged, indicating that social exclusion amplified the risk of experiencing self-injury urges. Specifically, relative to the Inclusion baseline, participants under Exclusion exhibited more than a threefold increase in the odds of reporting an NSSI urge (OR = 3.53, 95% CI: [1.52, 8.18], p = 0.003). The Partial Exclusion condition showed only a marginal trend towards higher risk (OR = 2.28, 95% CI: [0.96, 5.42], p = 0.06). Urge reporting was unrelated to BPQ scores (p= 0.65).

Descriptive tracking further highlighted that these momentary NSSI urges did not consistently translate into behavior (Table 4). Across the 7-day protocol, NSSI behavior was reported by 7 participants in the NSSI group and 5 in the BPD+NSSI group. Despite the overall increase in urges under exclusion conditions observed in the logistic mixed model, NSSI engagement showed marked heterogeneity at the individual level. Together, these results suggest that although exclusion conditions were associated with higher urge reporting at group level, the urge-to-behavior transition varied substantially across individuals.

The role of borderline personality traits

As reported above, borderline traits were consistently associated with more negative perceptions and affect across all experimental conditions (i.e., higher perceived exclusion, lower perceived approval, higher negative and lower positive affect). To determine whether this pattern reflected amplified reactivity to exclusion or a tonic interpretation bias, we tested cross-level Condition × BPQ interactions for the perception outcomes (See supplementary material; Table S2). Interactions were non-significant for both perceived exclusion (all p-values > 0.25) and perceived approval (all p-values > 0.27). Given the limited statistical power available for these cross-level tests, these null findings should be regarded as inconclusive rather than as evidence of comparable responding across the severity continuum. In contrast, when analyses were restricted to the objective Inclusion condition, categorized BPQ levels (+ 1 SD, moderate, and − 1 SD) significantly predicted higher perceived exclusion and markedly lower perceived approval, despite the feedback being objectively favorable (Fig. 4). Specifically, individuals with high BPQ levels (+ 1 SD) perceived significantly greater exclusion than those with low levels (-1 SD; b = 1.39, SE = 0.54, t(53.66) = 2.55, p = 0.01), whereas those with moderate levels showed only a marginal trend in the same direction (b = 0.75, SE = 0.39, t(52.80) = 1.91, p = 0.06). Regarding perceived social approval, both the high BPQ group (b = -2.44, SE = 0.77, t(52.66) = -3.19, p = 0.002) and the moderate BPQ group (b = -1.54, SE = 0.55, t(51.78) = -2.80, p = 0.007) reported significantly lower approval scores compared to the low BPQ reference group. Taken together, the present data provided clearer support for a baseline, sustained negative interpretation bias of social media feedback associated with borderline pathology than for amplified reactivity to actual ostracism, the latter remaining inconclusive given the limited power for cross-level interactions.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess ostracism sensitivity in young women with NSSI using an ecologically social media context by combining an Instagram-mimicking smartphone app (Socializapp) with EMA to capture immediate responses to everyday app engagement. Importantly, we included clinical (BPD+NSSI), subclinical (NSSI), and healthy control groups to examine NSSI vulnerability along the severity continuum. Experimentally reduced peer feedback has induced change in momentary experiences across all participants, indicating that digitally mediated exclusion serves as a salient interpersonal stressor. Among participants with NSSI, the increase in NSSI urges under exclusion remained after adjusting for borderline severity (BPQ), suggesting that heightened self-injury motivation under digital social threat was observed across participants spanning the severity continuum.

Congruently with our first hypothesis, the perceived exclusion increases across all groups, showing that participants consistently detected ostracism cues within the task independently of the presence of NSSI. Full Exclusion produced robust effects, while Partial Exclusion elicited only modest trend-level changes, suggesting that graduated reductions in engagement may be experienced as a more ambiguous social signal than complete disengagement. This distinction may mirror real-world social media dynamics where users must interpret subtle engagement fluctuations [51, 52]. Converging with these subjective appraisals, perceived social media approval decreased stepwise from Inclusion to Partial Exclusion and Exclusion in all groups, suggesting that as exclusion cues intensified, participants increasingly inferred that their posts were less liked. This convergence between perceived ostracism and perceived social approval across conditions supports the paradigm’s internal consistency.

Consistently, the exclusion manipulation was accompanied by changes in momentary affective and behavioral responses. Relative to Inclusion, reduced peer feedback (especially under Exclusion and more modestly under Partial Exclusion) was associated with a change in affective valence (i.e., higher distress and lower positive affect) and a reduction of social engagement, particularly in comments (i.e., more effortful, self-exposing engagement). These results support the paradigm’s ecological relevance and converge with prior findings that social media ostracism acutely impairs affect and suppresses interaction when social reward cues are attenuated [33, 52, 53].

Beyond these condition effects common to all participants, we explored whether responses to exclusion varied across the clinical severity continuum. Categorical between-group comparisons were underpowered (see Methods) and were therefore treated as exploratory. Given this limited power, neither the observed differences nor the null results can be considered conclusive. These analyses are reported in the Supplementary Material and interpreted only as hypothesis-generating. Here we focus on the dimensional association with borderline severity, for which the design was adequately powered.

This dimensional profile (whereby greater borderline severity was associated with reduced perceived approval even under objectively favorable feedback) may reflect converging processes. First, positive cues may carry reduced subjective reward value and/or require a higher evidential threshold to be interpreted as genuine endorsement. Second, visible peer activity may amplify upward social comparison, biasing self-appraisal toward “insufficient” even when one’s own feedback is objectively favorable. Congruently, prior evidence suggests weaker reward updating and diminished formation of positive reward expectations in BPD and NSSI, which may translate into reduced perceived value of positive social feedback in social-media contexts [54–57]. Neurobiological evidence further supports this interpretation. In an Instagram-like fMRI paradigm, Nicolaou et al. reported that individuals with NSSI (specifically those with comorbid BPD) rated positive social feedback as less pleasant and exhibited blunted activation in striatal reward regions when processing positive versus negative feedback [14]. Finally, these findings converge with rejection-sensitivity accounts in BPD, including elevated rejection anticipation [58], negatively biased evaluation of affiliative (positive) cues [59], and a tendency to perceive less acceptance alongside more perceived rejection in daily social interactions [60].

Finally, full Exclusion exerted a proximal effect on NSSI urges relative to Inclusion, with Partial Exclusion showing a non-significant trend in the same direction, indicating that markedly reduced social media feedback functions as a proximal trigger for self-injury motivation. This effect remained when adjusting for borderline severity (BPQ), although the design was not powered to test whether severity moderated it. Notably, urges increased even though Partial Exclusion produced only minimal effects on explicit distress; while this dissociation should be interpreted cautiously given that the partial-exclusion effect did not reach significance, it tentatively raises the possibility that self-injury motivation may be activated by interpersonal threat cues at lower thresholds than consciously experienced distress, consistent with models of NSSI as an automatized regulatory response to such cues [61]. Importantly, this increase in urges did not consistently translate into NSSI behavior, which showed marked individual heterogeneity, underscoring that the urge-to-behavior transition is governed by factors beyond the immediate social-evaluative context. Together, these patterns tentatively suggest a partial dissociation, with exclusion-related increases in NSSI urges observed across the severity continuum and holding after adjusting for borderline traits, whereas the negative appraisal bias was more closely tied to borderline severity. However, whether borderline severity moderates reactivity to exclusion remains inconclusive because of the limited power to detect cross-level interactions. This finding has implications for understanding NSSI vulnerability in digital social contexts. Given continuous feedback fluctuations on visual-based platforms (e.g., Instagram, TikTok), these environments may constitute potential high-risk contexts for young women with NSSI.

Despite the ecological validity of using a smartphone-based experimental design, several limitations must be considered. First, conditions were presented in fixed multi-day blocks, following the sequential structure of established ostracism paradigms such as Cyberball. Although this enabled dose-response analysis and approximated how exclusion unfolds naturalistically over time, condition effects cannot be fully disentangled from time-related processes such as habituation or expectancy. Future studies should explore pseudo-randomized or counterbalanced sequences to strengthen internal validity. No formal suspicion probe or credibility questionnaire was administered after the study. Although the observed manipulation effects indirectly support the credibility of the paradigm, future studies should include explicit credibility checks. Second, while our intensive longitudinal design yielded robust statistical power to detect within-person experimental shifts in response to digital ostracism, the overall between-person sample size introduces constraints that must be acknowledged. Formal Monte Carlo simulations revealed a clear divergence in our capacity to test cross-level interactions. Specifically, the framework was structurally underpowered to reliably detect categorical between-group dynamics (Group × Condition). Similarly, moderation analyses by baseline dimensional borderline traits (Condition × BPQ centered) remained largely underpowered and exploratory across most psychological and clinical dimensions, achieving full statistical adequacy exclusively for digital comments. Consequently, these power constraints prevent us from drawing definitive conclusions regarding between-person differences as a function of the experimental condition, meaning that all cross-level interactions must be treated as strictly preliminary. Third, the recruitment was restricted to Android users due to application compatibility. While this might introduce device-selection bias in some contexts, Android devices are widely used in the local market where the study was conducted. Fourth, the sample comprised exclusively young women, limiting generalizability to men and other age groups. Fifth, the 7-day protocol may have been too brief to reliably capture low-frequency clinical events like NSSI urges and behaviors. Although post-hoc power simulations indicated adequate power to detect within-person shifts, the sparsity of these events requires that the results be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future EMA studies should monitor participants over extended periods to yield more stable event rates and incorporate multi-item scales, as the current single-item measures may not fully capture complex, multifaceted clinical mechanisms like baseline rejection sensitivity. Lastly, while ethically valid, the paradigm may underestimate real-world effects as participants could not post personal photos (e.g., selfies), and feedback was standardized, reducing the identity-relevant threat. Similarly, experimental comments were mild compared to real interactions on social media, which tend to include explicit criticism and ambiguous feedback. This may have limited detection of group differences under Exclusion.

Conclusions

This study provides an initial validation of Socializapp, the first smartphone-based paradigm to combine an experimentally controlled social-media ostracism manipulation with EMA in young women along the NSSI–BPD severity continuum. Reduced peer feedback rapidly shifted momentary states across participants, heightening perceived exclusion and negative affect, lowering perceived approval, and, among those with a history of self-injury, amplifying NSSI urges (an exploratory finding, given the sparsity of these events). Greater borderline severity was further associated with a sustained negative appraisal of social-media feedback, with reduced perceived approval even under objectively favorable conditions. Together, these findings indicate that small, everyday fluctuations in online social feedback can act as proximal triggers for affective distress and self-injury urges, identifying visual-based platforms as potential high-risk contexts for emotionally vulnerable young women. Although preliminary, these results support the development of just-in-time interventions delivered around moments of perceived online exclusion, as well as community-driven guidelines for safer digital social engagement.
