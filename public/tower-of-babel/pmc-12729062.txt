Dynamic conversion and associated risk factors between mutually exclusive muscle health states in geriatric population
Authors: Wen-Hui Fang, Tao-Chun Peng, Yuan-Ping Chao, Li-Wei Wu, Hui-Fang Yang, Tung-Wei Kao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe loss of muscle mass plus a decrease in muscle function is composed of sarcopenia. Before entering this muscle health status, individuals experience either low muscle quantity or poor muscle quality. The natural course of the dynamic transition to sarcopenia from robust through these two mutually exclusive muscle health statuses is presently unclear.MethodsAdults aged 65 years and older living in the community and who received annual health examinations, including muscle function, body composition and other measurements, were enrolled. Participants with normal muscle mass and function were considered robust. Presarcopenia is defined as the sole loss of muscle mass; dynapenia is indicated as poor muscle function but reserve normal muscle mass, and these two statuses are mutually exclusive to muscle health. Sarcopenia is characterized as a double reduction in muscle mass and muscle function. We investigated the trajectory of muscle health transition to sarcopenia from dynapenia and presarcopenia and estimated the possibility of recovery from dynapenia and presarcopenia. The relationships between muscle health transition and different determinants among older adults were examined.ResultsIn total, 296 participants were included in the analysis. Older adults with dynapenia have more chronic diseases and higher obesity parameters. Individuals with presarcopenia tended to have a higher hazard ratio (HR) for progression to sarcopenia (HR = 4.20, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 2.59–6.81). A higher body fat percentage and fat-to-muscle ratio are associated with the transition to sarcopenia. Dynapenic individuals had a higher HR for recovery to a robust status (HR = 4.18, 95% CI = 2.13–8.19). Greater muscle mass, greater handgrip strength, and higher albumin levels were associated with a greater likelihood of recovery to a more normal muscle health.ConclusionsDynapenia and presarcopenia, which are mutually exclusive muscle health conditions, tended to be associated with different muscle health transitions. Early recognition of and intervention in vulnerable elderly individuals are necessary to prevent the progression of sarcopenia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06756-1.

Introduction

Muscle health is comprised of muscle mass and function. Robust, a status of double reserve in muscle mass and function, is the initial stage prior to healthy muscle aging. In contrast, sarcopenia, a status of double loss in muscle health, is the endpoint of muscle health deterioration, and is associated with advanced unfavorable clinical outcomes, including falls, disability, frailty, and mortality [1–3]. Before progressing to the sarcopenia stage, some older adults may enter one of two mutually exclusive intermediate states, either muscle mass loss or muscle function decline, which is defined as presarcopenia or dynapenia.

During the aging process, muscle function decline precedes muscle mass loss, which leads to a detrimental clinical prognosis [4–6]. A longitudinal study also suggested that restoring muscle mass could not ameliorate age-related muscle function decline [7]. Furthermore, older women with poor muscle function were more prone to fall than those with a loss in muscle mass [8]. Dynapenic older adults with obesity have increased incidences of metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes [9, 10]. In line with the findings of previous studies, we reported that when different muscle health statuses were compared, dynapenic older people had the highest risk of falls and metabolic syndrome; conversely, presarcopenic older adults had the lowest risk of these clinical events [11]. Collectively, the impact of decreased muscle function on the risk of adverse clinical outcomes is more critical than that of muscle mass loss. Although the clinical impacts of these mutually exclusive muscle health statuses are quite different, there are few studies examining the trajectory potential of the dynamic transition from dynapenia or presarcopenia to sarcopenia. On the other hand, the possibility of recovery to a robust status from these intermediate muscle health statuses also warrants longitudinal observation.

We hypothesized that older adults with mutually exclusive muscle health status, either dynapenia or presarcopenia, have the potential for either loss or recovery of muscle health due to their specific muscle characteristics. The purpose of this study was to demonstrate the natural time course of progression to sarcopenia and recovery to a robust status from dynapenia or presarcopenia state among community-dwelling older people. Specific modifiable factors between the different muscle health transitional pathways were also estimated.

Methods

Participants

Community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older who underwent annual health examinations at a medical center in northern Taiwan were enrolled. Participants were excluded if they had cognitive impairment, congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease under regular hemodialysis, malignancy that required medical therapy, or had ever experienced chest pain or bone pain while exercising. All participants were requested to complete a documentation of informed consent if they agreed with enrollment. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Tri-Service General Hospital. An investigator used a structured questionnaire to collect information regarding the demographic profile, overall health condition, and daily physical activities of all the participants.

Participants’ personal information, including age, sex, cigarette smoking status, alcohol consumption status, and history of medical illness, was ascertained from a self-completed questionnaire and health insurance card. Previous medical histories were obtained through self-reports of a doctor’s diagnosis. The Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used [12]. The participants’ total cholesterol and serum albumin levels were collected and analyzed after they had fasted for eight hours.

Measurement of body composition

Participants’ body height was measured by a stadiometer, and their body weight was measured by a digital scale. Body weight in kilograms divided by the square of body height in meters was indicated as the body mass index (BMI). Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the mid-level between the lower border of the 12th rib and the iliac crest while the participants were standing with their feet 25 to 30 centimeters apart. The appendicular skeletal muscle (ASM) contains the lean muscle mass of the upper and lower limbs, which was measured by an eight-electrode bioelectronic impedance analyzer (BIA) (InBody 720, Biospace, Seoul, South Korea). ASM adjusted by the square of body height in meters (ASM/ht2) was defined as skeletal muscle mass index (SMI). We also used BIA to measure body fat mass and body fat percentage (BFP). The fat-to-muscle ratio (FMR) is defined as body fat mass divided by body muscle mass. In accordance with the consensus from the Asia Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) in 2019, the cutoff value for low SMI was 7.0 kg/m2 in men and 5.7 kg/m2 in women [13].

Measurement of muscle function

We used an analog isometric dynamometer (Exacta™ Hydraulic Hand Dynamometer; North Coast Medical Inc., Gilroy, CA) to measure the participants’ handgrip strength three times, and the best value was recorded. Walking for a six-meter distance at the usual pace was performed for all participants, and walking distance divided by walking time was indicated as gait speed. On the basis of the sarcopenia consensus of the AWGS in 2019, low handgrip strength was defined as lower than 28 kg in older male adults and less than 18 kg in female participants. A usual pace of walking slower than 1.0 m/sec is indicated as a low gait speed in both sexes.

Definition of muscle health status

Participants with normal muscle health based on SMI, handgrip strength, and gait speed were defined as having a robust status. Older adults have low handgrip strength and/or low gait speed, but a normal SMI is indicated as dynapenia. Individuals with normal handgrip strength and/or normal gait speed but low SMI were categorized as having presarcopenia. Sarcopenia is indicated by a low SMI with either low handgrip strength or low gait speed, or both. Among these four muscle health statuses, dynapenia and presarcopenia were mutually exclusive.

Data collection and statistical analysis

The participants’ assessments at baseline and annual follow-up were performed by well-trained research assistants. Participants with dynapenia or presarcopenia at baseline who either transitioned to sarcopenia or recovered to robust status during the observation period were treated as censoring indicators.

Student’s t-test was performed to examine the differences in covariates between dynapenia and presarcopenia participants. The associations between dynapenia or presarcopenia and the cumulative hazard of progression to sarcopenia or reversion to robust were estimated using Kaplan–Meier analysis. Cox regression analysis was applied to examine the risk of progression to sarcopenia or reversal to robust from dynapenia and presarcopenia. The extended model approach with stepwise variable adjustment by Cox proportional hazard regression was conducted: Model 1 = age, sex, smoking status, and alcohol use; Model 2 = Model 1 + hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke, coronary artery disease, and arthritis; and Model 3 = Model 2 + physical activity, FMR, total cholesterol, and serum albumin levels. Potentially modifiable covariates associated with the progression from presarcopenia to sarcopenia were examined. We also estimated the hazards of possible confounders implicated in dynapenia reverting to the robust stage by using Cox regression analysis. All analyses were conducted using Statistics Package for Social Science version 18.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

After the research purpose was explained, there were 1514 older adults were enrolled in this study. In addition, 125 participants had incomplete data on muscle health parameters; therefore, 1389 participants had available baseline data. We categorized the participants into four groups: robust, dynapenia, presarcopenia, and sarcopenia. There were 579 participants with dynapenia or presarcopenia muscle health status. We further excluded 283 participants because they did not visit at least twice in the seven year periods. Finally, data from 296 participants were used for the analysis of the dynamic transition from either dynapenia or presarcopenia (Fig. 1). The participants with dynapenia tended to be older and had lower physical activity and poor muscle function. Dynapenic older adults had higher obesity profiles, including BMI, WC, body fat mass, BFP and FMR. They were also more likely to have hypertension, diabetes mellitus, arthritis, and metabolic syndrome than presarcopenic participants were. Determinants of dynamic transition among different muscle health statuses

Cox regression analysis showed that presarcopenic participants with a higher BFP and FMR were more likely to progress to sarcopenia (HR = 1.05, 95% CI = 1.01–1.09; and HR = 3.24, 95% CI = 1.27–8.24, respectively) (Table 4). Presarcopenic older adults with higher skeletal muscle mass, handgrip strength, and gait speed were less likely to progress to sarcopenia (all p < 0.05). Dynapenic individuals with higher skeletal muscle mass, handgrip strength, and serum albumin levels were more likely to recover to a robust status (HR = 1.05, 95% CI = 1.01–1.10; HR = 1.06, 95% CI = 1.03–1.08; and HR = 2.90, 95% CI = 1.01–8.34, respectively) (Table 5). Discussion

Participants with low muscle function, represented as dynapenia in our study, were characterized by a much older age, reduced physical activity, inadequate handgrip strength, and slower gait speed. Furthermore, older dynapenic adults had more pronounced obesity profiles. In addition, they are more likely to have chronic diseases than presarcopenic participants are. Previous studies have suggested that muscle function decline occurs earlier and faster than muscle mass loss during muscle health deterioration [4, 5]. Numerous longitudinal cohort studies have demonstrated that poor muscle function contributes to worse clinical prognoses, including falls, cognitive impairment, frailty, hospitalization, nursing home admission, and even mortality, than muscle mass loss among older adults does [14–17].

We assumed that sarcopenia was the endpoint in the natural course of deterioration in muscle health. Although low muscle function is associated with more adverse clinical outcomes than low muscle mass is, studies on the dynamic transition to sarcopenia or recovery to robust muscle health conditions from dynapenia and presarcopenia are relatively rare. From this research, we disclosed that older adults with different muscle health statuses have the potential to transition to another status and that their specific muscle characteristics are important.

Dynapenia and presarcopenia have different potentials and characteristics for muscle health transitions. Dynapenic older adults with higher muscle mass, greater handgrip strength, and elevated albumin levels tended to return to a robust status, whereas presarcopenic participants with higher BFP or FMR were more likely to develop sarcopenia. A 9-year longitudinal study showed similar results. Intermediate subclinical states (either poor handgrip strength or slow gait speed) and low muscle function (poor handgrip strength plus slow gait speed) were categorized as dynapenia in our study. Among elderly people aged 70–79 years, 21% had presarcopenia, and 7% developed sarcopenia after 9 years, whereas 6% of those in the intermediate subclinical state and low functional state transitioned to sarcopenia [18]. A prospective population-based study revealed that participants with probable sarcopenia had a 10.3% probability of developing sarcopenia at 5 years and a 10.7% chance of recovering [19].

Advanced muscle mass loss is the key factor for the progression of dynapenia toward sarcopenia. In our study, dynapenic participants tended to have a greater fat mass and lower physical activity. This condition may attenuate the loss of muscle mass and result in the dynapenia stage rather developing sarcopenia. In contrast, a decrease in muscle function is critical for the further transition from presarcopenia to sarcopenia. Adiposity is thought to interfere with low muscle strength and slower gait speed among older people [11, 20–22], which could explain why presarcopenia with a higher obesity profile is more likely to progress to sarcopenia.

A previous study revealed that increasing BMI was associated with a greater likelihood of transition from no sarcopenia to probable sarcopenia and a lower probability of progression to sarcopenia [19]. Notably, obesity is a key factor that mediates muscle function decline to determine muscle health transitions. However, it has been suggested that BMI is not an appropriate fat parameter for studies on the aging process among older people [23]. The use of these measurements as obesity parameters may be masked by the transformation of body composition during aging, and these measurements are not as accurate at representing body fat mass increase [20]. Alternatively, our study revealed that presarcopenic older people with a higher BFP or elevated FMR were more likely to develop sarcopenia. This finding is consistent with a previous study addressing the association between fat indicators and muscle function, which revealed that only BFP and FMR, but not BMI or WC, were adversely associated with muscle function [20]. Obesity accelerates muscle weakness and functional decline through chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and intramuscular fat infiltration. Adipose-derived cytokines activate proteolytic pathways and impair myogenesis [24, 25]. Insulin resistance attenuates IGF-1/PI3K–Akt–mTOR signaling, resulting in anabolic resistance and reduced protein synthesis [26, 27]. Myosteatosis generates lipotoxic intermediates that impair mitochondrial function and contractile quality [28]. These mechanisms explain why BFP and FMR, but not BMI, were strong predictors of presarcopenia progression in our cohort.

In our study, participants with dynapenia were more likely to recover to a robust status. In another study, the reversal rate was 3.9% for probable sarcopenia, which was defined as dynapenia in our study, to recovery to no sarcopenia at the 1-year follow-up [19]. There was a 10.7% reversion rate from probable sarcopenia to no sarcopenia in the following 5-year and 10-year observations. Nevertheless, recovery data from presarcopenia to the robust stage in that study were not available because of the lack of this muscle health definition. In comparison, restoring muscle mass is a potential factor for muscle health reversibility, as explored by Murphy et al.; nevertheless, in our study, restoring muscle function was more important for recovery to a robust status. The differences in race, adiposity, muscle mass, physical activity, and nutritional condition may explain this discrepancy.

The major process through which older people with dynapenia recover to a robust status is to promote muscle function regain. In the present study, greater handgrip strength and elevated albumin levels increased the recovery process from dynapenia to robustness. The generation of muscle strength depends on protein supplementation and synthesis for energy transformation. This consistent relationship was supported by the results of previous studies. Schalk et al. reported that a low serum albumin concentration was inversely related to handgrip strength at baseline and further longitudinal follow-up [29]. Lower serum albumin levels increase the risk of older people transitioning from robust to dynapenia [30]. It was hypothesized that nutritional supplementation by increasing serum albumin levels may be a feasible method for reversing muscle function from dynapenia to a more normal status. However, albumin is affected by various non-nutritional factors, such as inflammation, hydration status, and chronic disease; therefore, it should not be regarded as a direct causal pathway whereby nutritional intervention improves albumin levels and subsequently enhances muscle strength. Future studies should incorporate more comprehensive nutritional assessments, such as the Prognostic Nutritional Index, the Controlling Nutritional Status score, and dietary intake evaluations, to better elucidate the clinical significance of albumin in relation to muscle recovery.

In this study, older dynapenic people with higher FMRs were more likely to stay at baseline. Dynapenic participants were more likely to be obese, have insufficient physical activity, and have more chronic illnesses in our cohort. This phenomenon is congruous with the status of chronic inflammation, which may detrimentally influence muscle function reserves, which resulted in maintenance in the dynapenia stage rather than recovery to a robust muscle health. In summary, adiposity seems to be a critical factor that causes older people with dynapenia to favor stasis in the current stage rather than further progressing or recovering to another muscle health status.

The strength of this study is that we disclosed that dynapenia and presarcopenia have different effects on the transition to other muscle health conditions. In contrast to previous studies, we defined low muscle strength and low muscle mass from the cutoff points according to the 2019 AWGS consensus. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal study to compare the transition trajectories between mutually exclusive muscle health statuses during muscle aging. Obesity and other nutritional factors have been implicated in this transition. Although few studies have reported the detrimental impact of adiposity on muscle health [31], our study presents the associations between different fat indicators and the possibility of muscle health transition. We also present some feasible and practical surrogates to predict the trajectory pattern from dynapenia or presarcopenia transition to other muscle health statuses in clinical practice.

Our study enrolled community-dwelling older adults with a relatively healthier status, which may restrict the generalizability of these findings to other individuals with different characteristics and settings. The substantial attrition from the screened cohort to the analytic sample may have introduced selection bias. The dynamic balance between muscle production and degradation is a critical determinant of muscle mass maintenance [32]. In older adults, muscle mass and strength may become uncoupled, questioning whether dynapenia and presarcopenia are distinct entities or thresholds along a shared continuum. Our study focused on transition events, limiting our ability to robustly estimate longitudinal slopes of muscle mass and function. Future trajectory or growth curve analyses are needed to clarify their clinical significance. In addition, the anabolic process through muscle production from protein digestion and amino acid absorption depends on the type of protein source [33]; nevertheless, the source of the dietary formulation and further nutritional analysis were not recorded in our study. Finally, some goodness-of-fit model are not directly available within the analytical environment employed in this study. Importantly, covariates were selected a priori based on established literature and clinical plausibility rather than automated statistical procedures, in order to preserve interpretability and to align with known risk factors for sarcopenia progression and recovery. This approach, while potentially limiting the discriminative performance of the models, provides a framework that is both clinically meaningful and consistent with prior research.

Conclusion

Presarcopenic older adults with higher obesity profiles had a greater risk of progression to sarcopenia. Conversely, dynapenic individuals with greater muscle mass, greater handgrip strength, and higher serum albumin levels were more likely to recover to a robust status. Early recognition of these modifiable factors and the performance of interventions for vulnerable older people are necessary to decelerate deterioration of muscle health.
