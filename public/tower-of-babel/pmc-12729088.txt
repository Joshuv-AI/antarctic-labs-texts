Psychological and socio-contextual factors influencing preventive healthcare decision-making: evidence from IT and ITES companies in three cities of India
Authors: Jangili Siva Rama Krishna, Md Asadul Haque, Karthikeya Gattupalli, Ashrafunnisa Mohammed, Kirubaharan Boobalan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe health of employed individuals has micro- and macroeconomic implications for individuals, nations, and economies. Therefore, the importance of healthy human capital for sustained growth deserves more attention. Preventive healthcare is crucial for maintaining a healthy and productive workforce, as it can mitigate health risks and the impact of chronic health conditions and reduce healthcare costs. This study examined the factors that influence preventive healthcare decisions among IT and ITES working-age adults. This study established a conceptual model using health consciousness, social interaction, public service advertisements, risk perception, perceived value, and health governance as antecedents; attitude as a mediator; and intention to spend as an outcome.MethodUsing an online survey, this study gathered data from 601 working-age participants of IT and ITES companies from Hyderabad, Visakhapatnam and Chennai via purposive sampling in a cross-sectional design. Subsequently, analysed the data using R. Lavaan and reported via structural equation modelling, measurement model comparison, and mediation analysis.ResultsThe results showed that health consciousness, social interaction, risk perception, public service advertisement, and perceived value exhibited favourable influences on attitudes, whereas health governance negatively affected attitudes but positively affected intention. The implications of these findings indicate a nuanced understanding of beneficial interventions, that shall extend beyond merely fostering positive attitudes via health education, boosting social support, and utilizing promotional strategies. They must also tackle issues related to health governance, potentially by enhancing communication and transparency, to facilitate the primary goal of promoting behavioural change.ConclusionIn order to promote preventive healthcare behaviours among individuals of working age, this study emphasizes the necessity for customized policies and interventions that deal with health consciousness, social interaction, and risk perception. The study demonstrated that enhancing perceived value (benefits outweighing barriers) in preventive healthcare services can increase self-efficacy and, consequently, foster stronger intentions. The study also highlighted the impact of health governance, focusing on policy development and regulation, community engagement and participation, and building trust in health systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13722-5.Keywords: Psycho-social-contextual factors, Health governance, Perceived value, Preventive healthcare, Working-age adults, Public service advertisement, Health services

Introduction

Chronic diseases, sedentary lifestyles, and substance use pose significant threats to global health, with nations showing a lack of preparedness as indicated by the Global Health Security Index (GHSI) ratings, where no country reached the highest tier (tier 5) [1]. This scenario highlights an opportunity to strengthen health-system, investment, and workforce capabilities through preventive healthcare, which is crucial for enhancing health emergency preparedness [2]. Preventive healthcare is emphasized by both the GHSI and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) as a key aspect of health readiness [1–3]. However, many countries face challenges in vital health metrics, such as prevention, detection, rapid response, and compliance with international health standards due to challenges intensified by the COVID-19 pandemic, conflicts, food crises, and climate change [3–6].

With the world population expected to reach 10 billion by 2050, the need for preventive health measures is more critical than ever [7, 8]. Chronic diseases account for 71% of global deaths, underscoring the need for coordinated preventive health strategies at individual, national, and international levels [2]. Such strategies not only improve population health but also boost productivity and economic growth by enhancing employee efficiency and lowering healthcare costs [9, 10]. The relationship between health and economic growth is complex and varies by income and region, requiring tailored health policy approaches. For instance, in South Asian countries, health and education have a significantly positive influence on productivity, emphasising the need for policies that enhance both sectors [11]. So, investments in preventive healthcare can lead to significant economic benefits, particularly in chronic conditions and ageing societies. Subsequently, a 1% increase in preventive health expenditure can significantly reduce the prevalence of ill health, thereby fostering sustainable economic growth [10]. Countries that have achieved specific health benchmarks, such as survival rates and life expectancy, as well as investments and health expenditures, experience a positive impact on economic growth, highlighting the importance of preventive health investments for long-term benefits [12]. Therefore, it is believed that an efficient preventive public health system can stabilise economic growth by enhancing the emotional, social, and financial well-being of the human capital and reducing macroeconomic fluctuations [13, 14].

Preventive healthcare has a significant impact on the health and well-being of the working-age demographic, defined as individuals aged 15 to 64 who contribute substantially to society and the economy [14]. In accordance with international labour standards, any individual under the legal age of 18 is considered a child [15]. Additionally, any occupation or line of work that could put the well-being, safety, or morals of minors at risk, either directly or indirectly, requires a minimum age of 18 years [16]. The Indian Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016 [17], and the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 [18], support the above claims, allowing working-age individuals aged 18 and above to comply with these protections.

Preventive healthcare encompasses actions designed to protect and enhance employees’ health, including establishing favourable working conditions, promoting physical activity, encouraging nutritious diets, and maintaining personal hygiene [10, 19–21]. These steps can help reduce the risk of illness and improve the overall well-being of the working-age population. So, determining the variables that impact individuals’ attitudes and intentions towards preventive healthcare is crucial. According to González-Rodríguez et al. [22], individual, societal, contextual, environmental, and psychological factors, together with socio-demographics such as age, education, income, occupation, residence, and gender [23], influence preventive health behaviours. Despite the increase in workplace wellness programs, spending on preventive healthcare services remains low, underscoring the need for research on how these variables influence attitudes and spending intentions [24, 25]. Additionally, the complex nature of preventative health behaviours necessitates a multi-perspective theoretical framework for its development within its context [25, 26]. For this purpose, the study draws upon the Socio-Ecological Model (SEM), which highlights the multi-level nature of health behaviour, including individual cognitions as well as societal and policy contexts [27], as its guiding framework to capture these dynamics [28, 29]. Health consciousness, risk perception, and perceived value are examples of psychological determinants; social interaction, public service advertising, and health governance are examples of socio-contextual factors. These two categories are our primary emphasis within this paradigm. Furthermore, psychological factors also correlate with prevalent health behaviour theory, such as the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [30]. This integration enables us to capture individual decision-making and socio-contextual elements [25, 26, 31] that influence the preventive healthcare spending intentions of working-age adults, particularly in the IT sector.

In India, around 65% of the population is of working-age, and over 70% of employees face at least one lifestyle-related health risk, with nearly 43% employed in high-stress and predominantly sedentary industries such as Information Technology (IT) and Information Technology Enabled Services (ITES) [32–36]. Although industry reports highlight the growing burden of chronic and lifestyle-related illnesses among corporate employees [34, 35, 37–41], much of this evidence remains descriptive, emphasizing prevalence rates, costs, or organizational impacts rather than attitudes and spending intentions regarding preventive healthcare services. A critical gap in the scientific knowledge of how psychosocial and contextual factors directly influence preventive healthcare decision-making among working-age adults in noteworthy sectors (like IT and software) exists, despite the extensive literature on health-related behaviours, disorders and concerns among Indian workers in particular [36, 38–41]. Among economically active cities in India (such as Hyderabad and Chennai), there is an alarming rise in working-age adults leading more sedentary lifestyles, which is leading to an increase in lifestyle diseases, according to the Associated Chambers of Commerce and Industry of India (ASSOCHAM) [36, 42]. Despite this challenging backdrop, preventive healthcare is on the rise, indicating a shift toward proactive health management [43]. This requires research on psychologically and socio-contextually customized preventative healthcare frameworks that reduce illness burden, increase efficiency, and sustain workforces.

Theoretical reasoning

The model centralizes attitude as a mediator influenced by six constructs: Public Service Advertisement (PSA), Health Consciousness (HC), Social Interaction (SI), Risk Perception (RP), Perceived Value (PV), and Health Governance (HG), all contributing to spending intentions for preventive healthcare services. Specifically, PSAs, grounded in SEM, shape societal norms through media and policy campaigns [44]; HC aligns with TPBs attitude construct, reflecting the prioritization of health [45]; SI integrates TPBs subjective norms with SEMs emphasis on interpersonal networks and workplace interactions [46, 47]; RP links to TPBs perceived behavioural control, assessing vulnerability and risk management capacity [48]; PV enhances TPBs attitude framework by highlighting perceived benefits versus barriers, while SEM situates value at community levels [46]; and HG reflects SEMs policy dimension, emphasizing institutional and governance mechanisms that support preventive behaviours [49]. Together, these constructs provide a theoretically grounded framework to explain how attitudes mediate preventive healthcare spending intentions among IT working-age adults. Further, the hypotheses are discussed in the following paragraphs

PSA → attitude → intention to spend

Exploring the mediating role of attitude in the relationship between Public Service Advertisement (PSA) and the intention to spend on preventive healthcare is crucial. PSAs have been recognized for their ability to shape public intentions and social behaviours by emphasizing ethical considerations [50]. Their success in promoting healthcare is contingent upon factors such as attractiveness, context, and strategic appeals [51–53]. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of message credibility, frequent exposure, cultural customization, and explicit calls to action in enhancing persuasive effectiveness [54–56]. Digital PSAs, especially those on social media, have greatly influenced health attitudes and behaviours [57–59]. For example, a World Bank study in India illustrated that Facebook PSAs, targeted based on location, enhanced malaria prevention behaviours and health-seeking activities [57]. These findings suggest that well-crafted PSAs can effectively influence attitudes towards preventive healthcare. Hence, we posit:

H1:

Attitude mediates the relationship between PSAs and intention to spend on preventive healthcare services.

HC → attitude → intention to spend

Health consciousness (HC) reflects the degree to which individuals value their health and it has a significant influence on attitudes and intentions towards preventive healthcare services [60–62]. It correlates with increased health knowledge, social involvement, and life satisfaction [63], promoting positive lifestyle choices and disease prevention [60]. Studies conducted in India indicate a positive association between health consciousness—relating to the benefits of good health and occupational risks—and the adoption of health-promoting activities among working-age individuals across various sectors [64–69]. This leads to a favourable preference for preventive healthcare services and increased spending on health-related products and wellness services, commonly connected with the Theory of Planned Behavior model, in which attitude is a crucial determinant [64, 66, 70]. HC has also been identified as a predictor of consumers’ inclination to acquire health-related products or services [71]. Hence, we propose:

H2:

Attitude mediates the relationship between health consciousness and intention to spend on preventive healthcare services.

SI → attitude → intention to spend

Social interaction (SI), a process wherein individuals acquire knowledge by observing, imitating, and modeling behaviours, greatly influences attitudes toward preventative healthcare services [72–74]. Active social involvement and perceived neighbourhood conditions (for example communal events, informal and office gatherings etc.) influence the utilization of preventive services [73]. Social communication influences attitudes through factors such as perceived access and illness-related fears [75]. In India, recent studies find that physician recommendations, support groups and family encouragement strongly influence attitudes towards preventive healthcare [76–78]. Social interactions often catalyse the conversion of positive attitudes into concrete spending intentions, moderated by affordability and trust [70, 77]. Therefore, we propose:

H3:

Attitude mediates the relationship between social interaction and intention to spend on preventive healthcare services.

RP → attitude → intention to spend

The relationship between risk perception (RP) and intention to spend on preventive healthcare services is likely mediated by attitude. Higher risk perception tends to foster more positive attitudes towards preventive measures [79, 80]. This mediation effect has been observed in various health contexts, including HIV prevention [80] and COVID-19 compliance [81–84]. Research conducted among the public and healthcare providers during the pandemic revealed that Indian individuals demonstrated increased risk perception and favourable attitudes towards spending on preventive healthcare services (such as disease screening, vaccination drive participation etc.) [82–84]. Corporate initiatives focused on health and wellness greatly influence the risk perception of working individuals, encouraging them to participate in preventive healthcare measures [85]. However, this relationship is complex and bidirectional [86, 87], further influenced by demographics, knowledge, financial consequences of healthcare decisions and personal experiences [88]. Hence, we hypothesize:

H4:

Attitude mediates the relationship between risk perception and intention to spend on preventive healthcare services.

PV → attitude → intention to spend

Perceived value (PV) represents the state of balance between perceived benefits and perceived barriers [89, 90] and significantly influences attitudes toward preventive healthcare [91]. The Theory of Planned Behavior and the Health Belief Model fundamentally suggest that individuals are more inclined to engage in preventive health activities when they perceive fewer barriers and greater benefits [46, 92, 93]. Wang et al. [93] found that perceived benefits were directly correlated with individual attitudes and significantly influenced preventive measures for noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) [71]. Odunsi and Farris [94] and Drolet and Lucas [95] found that perceived barriers limited individuals’ preventive efforts and decreased perceived behavioural control, impacting their attitudes to perform a behaviour. To encourage a positive attitude and increase the use of preventive healthcare services, interventions should focus on effectively balancing the benefits and barriers, thereby increasing perceived value [71, 75]. Mixed-methods and quantitative studies reveal that key benefits (clinician recommendations, provision of employer insurance, and explicit communication regarding clinical benefits) enhance employees’ positive spending attitudes and intentions, while financial and logistical obstacles reduce favourable attitudes [77, 96, 97]. Additionally, field evidence from India indicates that perceived cost barriers hinder the transformation of attitude into actual financial commitment [98]. Accordingly, we propose:

H5:

Attitude mediates the relationship between perceived value and intention to spend on preventive healthcare services.

HG → attitude → intention to spend

Recent studies on health behaviour have shown sensitivity to reward or punishment as a significant psychological aspect affecting individual responses to health policies and activities [98–100]. Based on this assumption, the variable health governance has been defined to measure the degree to which policies and regulations within a specific setting and/or system effectively promote individuals’ engagement in preventive health behaviours [93, 101–104]. Health governance encompasses mechanisms such as wellness initiatives, incentive frameworks, compliance oversight, and communication strategies that influence employees’ motivation to prioritize their health [105–107]. This study conceptualizes HG as shaping individuals’ attitudes toward preventive healthcare services, as supportive governance can foster positive intentions reflecting their readiness and commitment to acquire such services [108, 109]. For instance, the implementation of Village Health, Sanitation, and Nutrition Committees in India increased maternal healthcare service utilization, highlighting the role of public health systems [110]. However, the mediating role of attitude between HG and spending intentions, particularly among working-age individuals in sector-specific settings, remains understudied [111, 112]. Hence, we propose:

H6:

Attitude mediates the relationship between health governance and intention to spend on preventive healthcare services.

According to Fava et al. [113], motivating individuals to modify their behaviours for better health outcomes remains a significant challenge in healthcare today. Hence, addressing attitudes is essential for influencing an individual’s health intentions and behaviours [114, 115]. Health attitudes refer to an individual’s beliefs, perceptions, and behaviours concerning health and disease, which significantly impact several aspects of healthcare decisions. Positive attitudes, driven by psychological and socio-contextual elements, can significantly increase the willingness to allocate resources [116]. Limited research has demonstrated a positive correlation between attitude and the intention to spend on preventive healthcare services, which may vary based on context, determinants, and demographics, thereby substantiating the preceding claims [34, 35, 64, 87, 96, 112, 113, 117–119].

A few influential theories [117, 120, 121] in the field of health behaviour prediction emphasise the importance of behavioural intention as a prime predictor of engagement in certain behaviours [117, 121]. It signifies an individual’s deliberate and rational choice to engage in a behaviour [122]. It can be further classified as an individual’s intention or desire to utilize and pay for health-related products [123]. The difference between intention as an outcome and health-related factors can be explained by broadening the research to look at a new set of important health factors [124–126]. This research focuses on working-age preventive healthcare spending intentions, which lack direct evidence [127–129]. In order to describe the expected relationships among the study’s variables, the research builds a conceptual framework (Appendix A - Fig. 1).

Methods

Participants and procedure

The purposive sampling method was used to recruit participants from IT companies in the three major cities (Hyderabad (n = 257), Visakhapatnam (n = 203), and Chennai (n = 141)) of India. The researchers purposefully selected them due to their well-established reputation and strategic importance within the Indian IT sector. Indian IT and ITES companies employ a significant portion of the working-age population, often in high-pressure environments (for example, long sedentary hours, irregular shifts, and high mental stress) [130]. These factors increase lifestyle-related NCDs such diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and mental illness. Employer-provided insurance is common in this sector; however, research indicates low uptake of preventive health services, such as screenings, counselling, and wellness programs, due to workplace culture, awareness gaps, and legislation and regulation [130, 131]. The IT and ITES workforce predominantly comprises young to middle-aged professionals (i.e., 20–55 years) who are knowledge-driven [130, 132], making them the ideal cohort for studying preventive health within this working-age demographic. Hyderabad is a major IT hub, with exports worth 2.68 lakh crore and 9.46 lakh jobs [132, 133]. Chennai has also earned the name Software as a Service capital of India and accounts for roughly 15% of the country’s total IT workforce, with IT players such as TCS, HCL, ADP, Honeywell, Wipro, Infosys and Cognizant [127]. Additionally, Visakhapatnam’s IT sector is experiencing rapid growth, driven by companies such as Infosys and TCS [132, 134].

The inclusion criteria for the study are as follows: (a) participants must be employed as engineers, administrative executives, supervisors, or managers; (b) they must have regular access to the internet and possess either a smartphone or a computer; and (c) they should be able to read and understand the English version of the questionnaire to ensure the validity of their responses. This method matches the goal of gathering useful information from a specific group and is suitable for research aimed at acquiring a deep and relevant understanding of the topic [135–137]. Participants completed an online Qualtrics survey (http://www.qualtrics.com), which began with an informed consent form outlining the study’s purpose and confidentiality assurances. They then provided demographic data (gender, age, monthly income, basic health savings, health emergency savings and family size) before completing measures. The questionnaire was customised for mobile devices to enhance accessibility and facilitate participation among working-age adults, and it was circulated through professional networks and industry contacts. Following integrity and consistency checks, the survey was made available and received 700 responses. A final analysis was conducted on 601 survey entries (85.85% response rate) after 99 were removed for quality reasons [138]. Participants were free to participate in the study based on their own interests, and no personally identifiable information was collected unless it was directly related to the study’s objective. The Institutional Ethics Committee approved this study.

The pilot test (n = 57) assessed item clarity and construct validity. Scale-level statistics (e.g., ω, α, Guttman’s λ2), item-rest correlations, and factor loadings confirmed validity and reliability, indicating strong internal consistency across all constructs (Appendix A – Table 1) [139, 140]. Despite three items (HC_2, RP_2, and INTEN_3) exhibiting low item–rest correlations and factor loadings, they were retained due to their theoretical significance and content validity. Item retention, as per psychometric criteria, should be based not only on statistical indices but also on conceptual and theoretical rationale [141–144]. Furthermore, prior research indicates that items with loadings marginally below standard thresholds may still be deemed acceptable provided they hold theoretical significance or contextual relevance [145–150]. Subsequently, the research indicates that larger sample sizes produce more reliable values by reducing the variance associated with smaller samples [151, 152]. Items demonstrated satisfactory performance in the final investigation.

Measures

To ensure the study’s validity, variables were operationalized by adopting measurement items from previous research [153, 154]. These items were tested using a 7-point Likert scale (where 1 indicates ‘strongly disagree’ and 7 indicates ‘strongly agree’), a common approach in the social sciences [155]. Public service advertisement was measured by adapting four items (∝ = 0.82) from Chan et al. [156], focusing on prevention and promotion aspects. Five items derived from Gould [157] were used to measure health consciousness (∝ = 0.89), which is a psychological concern for health that influences behaviour. Four items, from Guo et al. [158], measured social interaction (∝ = 0.83) to assess respondent agreement on discussing preventive healthcare services with their social networks. The six items employed to measure risk perception, defined in the study as the subjective evaluation individuals make regarding the characteristics and severity of a risk (∝ = 0.89), were adapted from Paek and Hove [159]. Perceived value was measured using six items derived from Al-Metwali et al. [160] and operationalised as an individual’s trade-off between the offering’s benefits and barriers. Five items of health governance (∝ = 0.87) adapted from Torrubia et al. [161], encompassing policy and regulatory aspects within a specific context, aimed to evaluate an individual’s receptivity to positive or negative reinforcement that influences behavioural intentions. The attitude scale (4 items, ∝ = 0.83) was developed by Dawi et al. [162], and intention towards spending on preventive healthcare services comprised 5 items (∝ = 0.87), adapted from Mamun et al. [163].

Data analysis

We conducted the data analysis using JASP software version 0.19.3.0, which employs the Lavaan package developed for R. Lavaan serves a broad range of applied researchers who need structural equation modeling (SEM) to explore new methodological concepts. The confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and SEM processes used each item as an observable variable and modelled the latent constructs based on the shared variance among the items. Measurement validity and potential bias were evaluated through a combination of exploratory factor analysis (EFA), CFA, Harman’s one-factor test and common latent factor (CLF) technique. Harman’s one-factor test showed a variance of 9.7%, indicating that there was no common method bias [164]. Furthermore, a common latent factor (CLF) analysis was conducted to support this claim, confirming that the differences between estimates (with and without CLF) were all below the 0.2 threshold (Appendix A – Table 2). This implied the absence of significant method bias in the study constructs [165, 166]. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed which resulted in 8 factors (PSA, HC, RP, SI, HG, PV, ATT, INT) explaining 66.4% of the total variance. Based on the cutoff criteria established by Hu and Bentler [167], a good model fit was produced by the CFA of hypothesized model (ꭕ2 (674) = 809.446, p < 0.001, = 1.20; CFI = 0.987, GFI = 0.980, TLI = 0.986; RMSEA = 0.018, SRMR = 0.031). All the fit indices met recommended thresholds and the χ2/df ratio remained well below the conservative upper limit of 3.0, indicating acceptable relative model fit [168, 169]. Since the factor analysis was satisfactory, and the Cronbach’s alpha reliability value across all measurement scales indicated that the study met the criteria for both discriminant and convergent validity [167–169].

The model fit values for the conceptual framework are displayed in Table 1. Within the context of absolute fit indices, the Chi-Square statistic serves as a metric for assessing the congruence between the proposed model and the collected data [170]. Subsequently, Root Mean Square Error Approximation was employed to evaluate the model’s fit to the data, providing a more reliable metric of fit, particularly in structural equation modelling. The analysis of the study yielded favourable fit values (Table 1) as indicated by the Chi-Square test and RMSEA, which adhered to the cutoff criteria established by [167, 171]. To measure the average discrepancy between the observed correlations and model-predicted covariances, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) was employed in the study (a value less than 0.08 considered to be a good model fit) [167, 172, 173]. Furthermore, the study introduced additional indicators utilized in the assessment of structural equation modelling. Use of alternative fit indices in SEM assessment is advantageous, as each index provides definitive evidence of model fit by contrasting the null model with the specified model. For instance, a Comparative Fit Index (CFI) greater than or equal to 0.95 signifies superior fit, while Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) values exceeding 0.95 indicate a favourable fit [167, 173, 174]. Incremental measures, such as the Bentler-Bonett Non-Normed Fit Index (NNFI) and the Normed Fit Index, between 0.95 and 1, reflect the model’s alignment with the observed data [169, 174]. On the contrary, a value exceeding 0.90 is considered acceptable in certain cases, Bollen [175], and the majority of studies utilize TLI and GFI to evaluate and decide on model fit [176]. Additionally, the Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI) value greater than 0.90 is an acceptable fit [167, 169, 172, 174]. Subsequently, McDonald Fit Index (MFI) was calculated and exhibited in Table 1, and it is based on the fit ( > 0.90) of the obtained covariances to the implied covariances from the proposed model [174]. The study analyses and presents Bollen’s Relative Match Index (RFI) and Relative Incremental Fit Index (RIF), with values over 0.90 indicating a satisfactory fit, to compare the model’s fit to the null model [174, 177]. The indices demonstrate that the SEM assessment for the study’s model is both theoretically and statistically valid.

Results

Descriptive statistics of respondents

The participants were male (54.7%) and female (45.3%). Respondents aged 29–38 years represented the largest group of participants (26.1%), followed by those aged 18–28 (25.3%), 39–48 (21.5%), 49–58 (16.1%), and 59 and above (11%). 203 respondents (33.8%) mentioned an income between ₹20,000 and ₹50,000 INR. 319 respondents (53.1%) indicated that their monthly basic health savings for immediate health expenses are below ₹5000 INR, while 50.6% revealed that their health emergency savings for future health requirements amount to ₹5000 INR. Subsequently, 191 (31.8%) participants indicated a household consisting of four members.

Structural relationships and hypothesis testing

The SEM analysis indicated that the antecedents, specifically public service advertisement, social interaction, health consciousness, risk perception, and perceived value, are positively associated with attitude; however, health governance exhibited a negative relationship with the attitudes of working-age adults towards preventive healthcare services (ATT~0.109PSA + 0.116SI + 0.139HC + 0.127RP + 0.126PV - 0.101HG). The relationship among attitude, health governance, and intention was expressed by the equation INT~112HG + 0.236ATT, demonstrating a considerable positive relationship. Indirect Effects

Mediating role of attitude

The study’s hypotheses (H1 to H5) showed that five predictors (PSA, HC, RP, SI and PV) affect intention via attitude. A bootstrap analysis with 5000 iterations was performed to obtain robust regression coefficients, and the resulting significant indirect and direct estimates, including the upper and lower bound bias-corrected confidence intervals, are presented in Table 4. Figure 1 represents the mediation model with standardised estimates. The mediation analysis indicates that the attitude serves as a significant mediator between predictors and IT and ITES working-age adults’ intention to spend on preventive healthcare services. Specifically, the indirect effects (Table 4) of psychological variables—HC, RP, PV and socio-contextual variables—PSA, SI on spending intention through attitude were all positive and statistically significant, as their values were below 0.05 threshold and confidence intervals did not include zero. Results show that working-age adults in the IT and ITES verticals are more likely to have a positive attitude towards preventive healthcare services if they are: exposed to public service advertisements (like Ayushman Bhava or the Stop Diarrhoea campaign), actively participate in social groups (like HCLs interactive content on physical activity and mindfulness), show better health consciousness, perceive higher health risks for sedentary lifestyles, and strongly value the benefits of living fit and healthy. Positive attitudes subsequently increase their willingness to invest in services such as annual check-ups, gym memberships, workplace consultations, screenings, and stress reduction services provided by employers or healthcare providers. HG exhibited a negative indirect effect via attitude (H6), despite its good direct effect on intention. This is a suppressing effect, wherein health-related policies and regulations may directly stimulate spending by offering practical benefits, such as the inclusion of preventive services in medical insurance and compulsory employer-sponsored primary healthcare initiatives. Furthermore, this suggests that when governmental or corporate health policies are viewed as restrictive, uncertain, or excessively administrative, they can adversely affect attitudes. Consequently, effective health governance can directly promote preventive care spending by enhancing accessibility, while maintaining transparency in policies and regulations is essential to cultivate favourable attitudes. Contrary to the study’s expectation of full mediation, the mediation model showed that PSA, SI, HC, RP, and PV indirectly affect intention through attitude, while HG directly affects intention through pathways other than attitude (Appendix A - Figure 2).

Discussion

We will first discuss the direct effects. Preventive healthcare has gained prominence in the Indian IT and ITES sector, where employees encounter distinct occupational health hazards associated with prolonged sitting, elevated workplace stress, and unpredictable working hours. Research demonstrates that IT professionals are especially susceptible to lifestyle-related ailments, including obesity, musculoskeletal problems, hypertension, and mental health concerns [14, 19, 180, 181]. The prevalence of a youthful yet inactive workforce highlights the need for organized preventive measures, including routine health assessments, ergonomic education, stress management seminars, and encouragement of physical activity [182]. Indian IT companies are becoming increasingly aware of preventive healthcare practices, but they aren’t consistently implementing them, often due to organizational goals and a lack of policy-level mandates [10, 183]. For the IT sector’s workforce to remain stable over time, it’s essential to integrate preventive healthcare into the workplace culture through a long-term company commitment and policy support. The results showed that five predictors (HC, SI, PSA, RP and PV) had a positive effect on intention through attitude, except for health governance (HG), which had a direct impact.

We now discuss the mediating effects in detail.

First, the results support the hypothesis that attitude mediates the relationship between health consciousness and intention to spend on preventive healthcare services among IT and ITES working-age adults (estimate = 0.022, p < 0.010) [70, 184–189]. Prior findings highlight the significant impact of health consciousness on developing preventive health attitudes towards intention of preventive healthcare services within the workplace and recommend the need for organisational health initiatives, including lifestyle enhancement, nutritional guidance, targeted health communication campaigns, personalized checkups and diagnosis, incentives for healthy choices, modifications to the work environment, mental health training, and subsidized health services [70, 88, 185–194]. Examples of such initiatives include Infosys’s Health Assessment and Lifestyle Enrichment (HALE) and Tata Consultancy Services’ Fit4Life programs [85, 195]. However, 30 to 40% of the employees are unaware of the negative consequences of lifestyle illness; therefore, 71% pay out-of-pocket for their health issues [24]. This suggests that health consciousness alone is insufficient without a positive attitude towards the intention of preventive healthcare services that correlates with spending intentions.

Second, social relationships are identified as one of the six pillars of lifestyle medicine [196], and multiple studies have substantiated the structural and functional dimensions of social interactions in shaping attitudes and spending intentions towards preventive healthcare services [197–201]. The study’s findings support the hypothesis that attitude positively mediates the relationship between social interaction and intention to spend on preventive healthcare services (estimate = 0.023, p = 0.017). The interpersonal communication and social networks (family, friends, peers, and doctors) function as cues to action in shaping attitudes towards intention of preventive healthcare services through social norms, modelling, and reinforcement [197–201]. Individuals who participate in such interactions contribute to a more favourable mindset towards preventive healthcare services and increase an individual’s intention to spend on such services. For IT professionals frequently engaged in collaborative, peer-visible settings, social interaction serves as a particularly potent cue [197, 198].

Third, Public Service Advertisements (PSAs) have become an essential tool in promoting healthy preventive health attitudes towards the intention of preventive healthcare services among the working-age population of IT and ITES (estimate = 0.020, p = 0.033). The results support the study’s assumption that PSAs have a positive influence on intention via attitude towards preventive healthcare services. Researchers examined the efficacy and reach of such advertisements in enhancing health knowledge and influencing attitudes towards intention of preventive healthcare services across various contexts, including demographics and delivery methods [54, 202–209], thereby substantiating our claim. During the COVID-19 pandemic, public service advertising effectively raised awareness and countered misconceptions [202, 210]. Research indicates that, within IT and ITES organizations, visual and digital health advertisements particularly on social media and corporate wellness platforms enhance employee knowledge of preventive measures, leading to more effective engagement in such services [182, 211]. In India, nationwide initiatives such as Fit India, TB screening programs, and the COVID-19 vaccination drive exemplify PSA-led efforts to change public attitudes towards the adoption of preventive healthcare services [212–214]. From a practical standpoint, developing evidence-based, emotionally resonant, culturally and locally relevant public service advertisements targeting the working-age population of IT and ITES may serve as a highly effective strategy for increasing favourable attitudes towards the intention of preventive healthcare services that drive spending intentions [215, 216].

Fourth, the findings support the hypothesis that attitude mediates the relationship between risk perception and intention to spend on preventive healthcare services among working-age adults in IT and ITES (estimate = 0.018, p = 0.018). Research indicates that individuals with an increased risk perception are more likely to demonstrate positive attitudes towards the intention of preventive healthcare services and engage in preventive activities [80, 217, 218]. This research emphasised the essential importance of perceived vulnerability, perceived severity, and threat appraisal in shaping risk perception within health behaviour models [117, 219]. Working-age individuals in IT and ITES evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of financial, physical, professional, ethical, and emotional factors, where a positive risk perception may enhance productivity and behaviour [218, 220]. Consequently, organisations must prioritise the establishment of healthy workplaces and formulate effective preventive health, risk management, and communication strategies [221, 222].

Fifth, perceived value additionally influences attitudes towards intention to spend on preventive healthcare services (estimate = 0.017, p = 0.016), supporting the study’s hypothesis. This outcome highlights the critical importance of benefit appraisal in shaping preventive health attitudes towards the intention of preventive healthcare services and intentions, as emphasized in cognitive-behavioural models of health decision-making [117, 120, 219]. Proactively promoting preventive services through leadership endorsement, incentives, on-site screenings, and reduced financial barriers alters the benefit-barrier calculus, creating a practical perceived value that influences employee health attitudes and intentions towards preventive healthcare services [211]. Numerous studies have identified perceived value as a crucial and extensively employed predictor in behavioural research that can influence positive health attitudes towards the intention of preventive healthcare services [171, 185, 223–229]. Research indicates that individuals’ perceptions of health-related matters, whether beneficial or detrimental, can significantly influence their behaviour and overall quality of life [187]. For example, the Health Report of Corporate India, 2023 [24, 230] stated that the low adoption rate of wellness programs, particularly in the post-COVID period, is due to constraints such as a lack of comprehensive and flexible benefits, especially for those with chronic illnesses, and insufficient access for dependents. Some practical solutions include all-inclusive preventive healthcare insurance, flexible appointment options, integration of preventive care into standard workflows, and focused communication to reduce emotional and informational barriers.

Sixth, the study’s findings indicated that, among working-age individuals in IT and ITES, health governance had a negative influence on intention to spend through attitude (estimate = −0.015, p = 0.040); hence, the hypothesis was rejected. The dual finding, specifically a direct positive impact and a negative indirect effect via attitude, represents a fresh perspective particularly concerning attitudes and spending intentions on preventive healthcare services among working-age adults in the IT and ITES. Health governance encompasses policies and regulations within a particular setting that convert favourable health attitudes towards the intention of preventive healthcare services into actionable measures. The individual’s reactivity to positive or negative reinforcement influences behavioural intentions [102–104, 216, 231]. Supportive health governance directly fosters spending intentions, but opaque and restrictive laws and regulations could cause distrust, resulting in unfavourable attitudes. When employees are sensitive to institutional policies or regulations, they anticipate that government or employer programs will deliver necessary preventive services, functioning as a crowding-out mechanism that alleviates out-of-pocket expenses for IT working-age adults [232, 233], such activities are a prevalent phenomenon within institutional practices, particularly in the context of health insurance. The mandatory inclusion of preventive services within this health insurance framework is advantageous; however, it remains inadequate as present [234]. Working-age individuals in IT and ITES may resist policies that they perceive as misaligned with their interests, resulting in non-compliance [235]. The effective execution of the Occupational Safety, Health, and Working Conditions Code, Employee State Insurance regulations, and corporate social responsibility mandates can generate a direct compliance incentive, encouraging employees to invest in preventive services. Furthermore, organizations are encouraged to integrate preventive health into their fundamental human resource management to enhance health outcomes [236–238]. Research indicates that a more thorough understanding of access to preventive care as a ‘human fit’ between population demands and health professionals’ capabilities may result in improved results [239]. Additionally, health governance (HG) directly influences intention, unlike other antecedents, demonstrating that health governance affects individuals’ intentions by altering their perceptions of behavioral accessibility, benefits, or societal significance [109, 232, 240, 241]. The study asserts that health governance indicates that institutional or governmental changes in the workplace or public health systems promptly influence behavioural intentions, irrespective of attitudes. According to the World Health Organization, measures such as universal health coverage, robust infrastructure, substantial health expenditure, low out-of-pocket costs, effective health policy and regulation, health innovation, and public-private reforms can influence preventive healthcare decision-making [242]. Countries such as India are relentlessly striving to implement these principles through Universal Health Coverage, Ayushman Bharat-Health and Wellness Centers, the National Health Policy, and the Mental Healthcare Act, among others, irrespective of their geographical dimensions, demographic attributes, and various infrastructural and manpower constraints encountered in achieving the Sustainable Development Goals [243]. To turn preventive healthcare into actionable intentions among IT and ITES personnel, it is vital to promote positive attitudes towards health through targeted health communication, supportive management practices, and incentive-based interventions.

In conclusion, these findings collectively highlight that attitude towards intention of preventive healthcare services plays a significant mediating role in translating organizational initiatives, social interactions, personal health consciousness, risk perception, and perceived value into actionable intentions to invest in preventive healthcare among IT and ITES employees – which is in line with the previous study observations that - mediating role of attitude plays a vital role in shaping intentions toward healthcare spending [52, 53, 93, 117, 120, 227, 230, 231, 244–248], except for health governance. The results underscore the importance of designing targeted interventions, evidence-based communication, and supportive organizational practices to strengthen positive attitudes, ultimately fostering greater engagement with preventive healthcare services.

Implications and recommendations

Theoretical implications

This study aims to examine how attitudes mediate employees’ preventive healthcare spending intentions. The study extends the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and integrates it with the Social Ecological Model (SEM) by making attitudes the central mechanism linking cognitive (HC, RP, and PV) and social-contextual factors (PSA, SI and HG) to attitude and spending intentions. Attitudes are the main link between various antecedents and behavioural intentions, supporting TPB [249, 250]. Using the SEM, the study shows that individual cognition and external triggers influence attitudes, emphasizing the importance of multilayer influences on preventive healthcare behaviour. Public service advertisements and social interactions show ecological layers of effect, extending TPB to include social and informational aspects in attitude formation [251, 252]. Health governance is a key contextual component that impacts attitudes and behavioural intentions, demonstrating the interaction between structural and individual-level determinants of preventive healthcare behaviour.

Practical implications

At the individual level, working-age adults, such as IT working-age adults, should be encouraged to develop a stronger health consciousness and personal responsibility through accessible digital micro-learning initiatives and public service messages [253]. Educational initiatives can highlight the long-term benefits of preventive care, address barriers such as cost and time, and increase awareness of the risks associated with sedentary lifestyles, excessive screen time, and work-related stress [254]. The utilization of calibrated risk communication tools, such as online risk calculators and biometric assessments, renders susceptibility more tangible without fostering helplessness, employing the risk perception-attitude pathway to enhance spending intentions towards preventive healthcare services [255, 256]. Peer interactions within IT communities via social media platforms can further reinforce positive attitudes toward preventive health practices [257, 258]. Together, these micro-level behavioural supports enhance perceived value and normalize preventive action among time-constrained IT working-age adults.

At the organizational level, several Indian IT companies such as Infosys, Wipro, Cognizant and Tata Consultancy Services have implemented preventive healthcare mechanisms (examples: digital tools, workshops and partnerships with health organizations, vaccination programs etc.), aimed at addressing specific health issues (NCDs, mental stress, vision problems etc.) [85, 195, 259–261]. Building on this evidence, IT firms should invest in targeted knowledge campaigns, accessible preventive options such as on-site screenings and telemedicine vouchers, and low-friction, employer-sponsored services to enhance both perceived benefits and convenience. Such initiatives directly strengthen attitudes and intentions toward preventive spending. Additionally, social-norm engineering through team challenges, leader endorsements, and anonymous uptake dashboards can normalize preventive behaviour and reduce stigma [262, 263]. Behavioural nudges, such as default opt-in slots or co-funded screening incentives, have been shown to effectively convert intentions into concrete participation by leveraging psychological and situational cues that facilitate action without removing autonomy [264]. Privacy protection and non-penalisation must be guaranteed to preserve trust. Organizational provisioning should also consider multi-component approaches that combine education, on-site screening, leadership endorsement, and incentives, as evidence from workplace trials shows that combined interventions produce the strongest gains in uptake and sustained behavioural change [265, 266]. The interplay between organisational responsibilities and government initiatives (such as Ayushman Bharat-Health and Wellness Centers, Digital Health Mission, and Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana) via strategic memoranda of understanding diminishes out-of-pocket expenses, enhances the utilization of preventive services, and fosters continuity.

At the governance level, fiscal measures such as tax rebates or subsidies for preventive health expenditures, health screenings, and wearable health devices can reduce financial barriers and incentivize proactive health behaviours [267, 268]. Governments can encourage collaboration between IT firms, insurance providers, and healthcare organizations to co-create sector-specific preventive health frameworks, ensuring interventions are culturally and contextually appropriate. Legal and ethical considerations must underpin all initiatives, ensuring equitable access, voluntary participation, and non-penalization for non-participation to foster widespread adoption and trust [269]. By focusing on these systemic and regulatory strategies, health governance can create an enabling environment that supports organizational and individual efforts, embedding preventive health as a societal and industry-wide priority.

Future research and limitations

The survey tool employed in this research was administered in English, which may have posed challenges for participants with varying levels of language proficiency and educational backgrounds. Future studies should consider incorporating translations into regional languages to address inclusivity and diversity of the sample. The study sample primarily included working-age adults from a specific region. Subsequent research can incorporate a more heterogeneous sample to enhance the generalizability of the findings. As with any purposive sampling method, there exists a likelihood of selection bias that may influence the sample’s representativeness. However, the selected methodology aligns with the objective of obtaining broad perspectives from a certain demographic. Future research may improve representativeness by employing stratified or random sampling techniques, encompassing a broader demographic spectrum, or contrasting results across different locations or industries. The study employed a cross-sectional approach; therefore, longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to establish effective causation among the variables. The selection of three cities was chosen to facilitate feasible data collection and comprehensive analysis within the established time and resource limitations. Further research should broaden its focus to encompass new cities or regions. A more complete understanding of preventive healthcare decisions could be achieved by considering self-efficacy, responsibility, motivation, access-to-care, attitudes(instrumental/affective), and health status. This approach helps address the limited explanatory power of mediator and outcome variables. Health behaviour theories emphasize intentions, but researchers have not investigated the gap between preventive healthcare intentions and actual behaviour. Examining this relationship rigorously evaluates theoretical assumptions and shows where models fall short in practice.
