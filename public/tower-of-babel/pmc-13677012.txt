A single-center before-and-after study of physiology-first algorithmic surveillance versus standard observation for arteriovenous fistula maturation
Authors: Ari D. Kramer, Tyler Cunningham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate whether a structured, physiology-first surveillance protocol improves arteriovenous fistula clinical maturation rates and reduces time to cannulation readiness compared with standard postoperative observation.MethodsTwo consecutive cohorts of surgically created arteriovenous fistulas by a single surgeon at a community-based center were compared: a standard observation cohort (January-May 2025; 64 created, 58 analyzable) and a physiology-first cohort (June 2025-March 2026; 52 created, all analyzable) managed with a structured protocol featuring standardized 2.7-mm anastomosis, day-of-creation baseline flow documentation, structured ultrasound surveillance at weeks 2 and 4, and defined failure-to-progress intervention triggers and procedure. Salvage intervention for both cohorts included kinetic endovascular micro-incision creation followed by angioplasty with a drug-coated balloon.ResultsOverall maturation increased from 75.9% (44/58) to 92.3% (48/52; P = .022). Unassisted maturation rose from 53.4% (31/58) to 69.2% (36/52). The overall median time to clinical maturation decreased from 49 days (interquartile range, 42-68) to 30 days (interquartile range, 26-43; P < .001). Salvage intervention success was comparable between cohorts. Day-of-creation flow documentation increased from 0% to 100%, and no steal syndrome was observed (0/52) with the new physiology-first protocol.ConclusionsAs a quality-improvement evaluation, the physiology-first protocol generated a clear and consistent signal of improved maturation outcomes: access failure decreased, unassisted maturation increased, overall maturation increased, and median time to cannulation readiness decreased. These findings support adopting the protocol as a meaningful adjunct to care and warrant multicenter evaluation.

Arteriovenous fistula (AVF) maturation remains one of the most unpredictable steps in the hemodialysis access continuum. Despite decades of guideline development, including the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) and Fistula First Catheter Last, maturation failure rates persist in the range of 20% to 60%.1 The Hemodialysis Fistula Maturation (HFM) study demonstrated that even among fistulas achieving physiologic maturation criteria, functional failure occurs in a meaningful subset driven by cannulation variables outside the surgeon's control, suggesting that physiologic and functional maturation should be analyzed as distinct outcomes.2,3

Current practice relies on time-based postoperative observation, with maturation typically assessed at 4 to 6 weeks using KDOQI criteria (vein diameter ≥6 mm, flow ≥600 mL/min, and depth ≤6 mm). This paradigm treats maturation as a binary end point rather than a physiologic process with identifiable inflection points. Fistulas that fail to progress go unrecognized until clinical failure is apparent, at which point salvage procedures add cost, complexity, and might be limited in options.

Contributing factors to failed maturation include variable anastomotic technique, absent day-of-creation (DOC) flow documentation, convenience-based surveillance scheduling, and reactive rather than protocol-driven management.

We incorporated a structured physiology-first protocol, built around standardized anastomosis, DOC documentation, and defined surveillance with failure-to-progress triggers, to improve AVF maturation rates and reduce time to cannulation readiness.

Methods

Cohort populations

This is a single-center, single-surgeon quality improvement (QI) initiative at a regional medical center. This evaluation of clinical care changes is exempt from institutional review board review and follows Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.4 All AVFs were created by a single clinician (senior author). Two consecutive AVF cohorts were compared based on prespecified treatment windows defined by surgical creation date: standard observation (January 7-May 29, 2025) and physiology-first (June 2, 2025-March 3, 2026). All patients undergoing primary surgical AVF creation within these windows were eligible. Each patient was followed until clinical maturation, access failure, death, or loss to follow-up. The data lock occurred on April 28, 2026, when the last enrolled physiology-first patient (DOC: March 3, 2026) reached clinical-maturation clearance, closing the cohort with complete outcome ascertainment for all enrolled AVFs.

The unit of analysis is the individual AVF access. Access types included brachiocephalic (BC), distal radiocephalic (DRC), proximal radiocephalic, and brachial vein transposition.

Standard observation protocol cohort

Anastomotic size was determined by surgeon preference. Preoperative vein mapping was performed, but intraoperative measurements were not recorded as baselines. Follow-up occurred at ∼2 and 5 to 6 weeks. DOC flow metrics were not routinely documented; failed maturation triggered case-by-case angiographic referral. If necessary, targeted interventions included inflow angioplasty (brachial artery flow <1000 mL/min), coil embolization or banding (competing outflow), kinetic endovascular micro-incision creation (KEMIC) (Flex Vessel Prep, Flex Vascular) followed by drug-coated balloon (DCB) (IN.PACT AV DCB, Medtronic) maturation (vein <5 mm),5, 6, 7 and lipectomy/transposition (depth >6 mm).

Physiology-first protocol cohort

Operative anatomy selection and targeted intervention options were unchanged between cohorts. Standardized anastomosis

All AVFs were created using a standardized 2.7-mm arteriotomy via aortic punch. This fixed dimension established a reproducible inflow variable for serial comparison while limiting initial hemodynamic demand to mitigate steal physiology. The rationale follows the small-arteriotomy precedent of Crane et al,8 who reported routine use of a 3-mm arteriotomy for BC fistula formation as a strategy to limit steal while preserving patency.

DOC documentation

Intraoperative Doppler ultrasound under regional anesthesia established postcreation baselines (artery/vein flow, diameter, blood pressure, waveform quality, depth, and collateralization). These measurements were intended to reflect true postanastomotic physiology under vasodilation, providing a reproducible reference for all subsequent comparisons.

Structured surveillance at weeks 2 and 4

The 2-week visit became the primary physiologic decision point. Failure-to-progress criteria included flow plateaued or decreased from DOC baseline, vein flow failing to increase by ≥30% from DOC, weakening or loss of palpable thrill/audible bruit, development of high-resistance Doppler waveform, or imaging-confirmed stenosis or hemodynamically significant collateralization. Patients meeting full cannulation criteria at week 2 (brachial artery flow ≥1000 mL/min, vein flow ≥500 mL/min, diameter ≥5-6 mm, depth ≤4-5 mm, and consistent thrill) were cleared after 1 additional week of arterialization (∼21 days). Others returned at week 4. The composite criteria were designed to capture failure signals that a single fixed threshold may miss based on geography.

Defined intervention triggers

When failure to progress was identified (at 2 or 4 weeks) during structured surveillance, angiographic evaluation was scheduled within 48 hours to determine appropriateness for targeted interventions (as described above).

Definition of maturation domains

Clinical-maturation declaration was determined by the criteria in Table II at 1 of 2 time points: (1) intraoperatively at the time of an assisted-maturation procedure when intraoperative duplex ultrasound and volumetric flow assessment was performed or (2) at an outpatient surveillance visit when all criteria were met. The on-table pathway is a methodological clarification of when the cannulation-readiness criteria were ascertained, not a relaxation of the criteria themselves; the criteria in Table II were applied identically regardless of setting. Outcomes

The primary outcomes reviewed were the maturation access rate, defined as an AVF that achieved cannulation readiness; median time to clinical maturation; and safety. The secondary outcomes reviewed were unassisted clinical maturation rate, defined as achievement of cannulation readiness criteria without intervention; assisted maturation rate (clinical maturation achieved only after targeted intervention); and functional maturation, defined as documented initiation of 2-needle cannulation for hemodialysis as captured by a recorded cannulation date in the analyzable cohort. Access-related steal syndrome was monitored as an exploratory safety outcome.

Statistical analysis

This is a QI evaluation without planned statistical evaluation. However, post hoc statistical comparisons were conducted to characterize the magnitude and direction of protocol-associated changes and to support a decision about whether the protocol should be retained as standard practice; they are not framed as formal confirmatory hypothesis tests. The statistical analysis used for each categorical or continuous outcome is listed accordingly. All analyses were performed using SPSS, version 32.0 (IBM).

Secondary outcomes

Unassisted maturation increased from 53.4% (31/58) to 69.2% (36/52) in the physiology-first protocol cohort, a 15.8% improvement. Rates were broadly consistent across access types: BC 19/26 (73.1%), DRC 13/21 (61.9%), and proximal radiocephalic 4/5 (80.0%).

Assisted maturation rates were stable between cohorts (22.4% [13/58] vs 23.1% [12/52]). Swing-segment stenosis was the predominant lesion (7/13 procedures). Among the 13 physiology-first assisted-maturation procedures, 12 (92.3%) achieved clinical maturation; 6 of those 12 successful cases were cleared intraoperatively on the basis of duplex ultrasound and volumetric flow assessment confirming Table II criteria immediately postprocedure (with ≥21 days of arterialization elapsed), and the remaining six were cleared at the next outpatient surveillance visit. The standard observation on-table-clearance pattern was similar (5 of 13 successful assisted maturations, 38%), reflecting a consistent surgeon practice independent of cohort era.

Functional maturation was achieved in 44 of 52 physiology-first patients (84.6%) vs 40 of 58 (69.0%) of standard observation patients.

Sex-stratified analysis

Within-sex unassisted-maturation rates moved in the same direction in both cohorts (males: 65.6% [21/32] pre to 73.7% [28/38] post, P = .600; females: 38.5% [10/26] pre to 57.1% [8/14] post, P = .330), suggesting that the cohort-level protocol effect is not driven by the sex-distribution difference. Discussion

This evaluation of a QI initiative generated a clear signal that a structured physiology-first surveillance protocol meaningfully improves AVF maturation in community practice. The protocol bundle, not the procedure, was the intervention. The bundle included four simultaneous changes: standardized 2.7-mm anastomosis, DOC documentation, structured 2/4-week surveillance, and defined intervention triggers, and the design cannot disaggregate the contribution of any individual element. Access failure decreased from 24.1% to 7.7%, a 68% relative reduction; overall maturation increased from 75.9% to 92.3%; and median time to cannulation readiness decreased from 49 to 30 days—all without changes in operative anatomy selection, patient selection criteria, access type distribution, or target intervention options. The unassisted-maturation comparison was directionally consistent (53.4%-69.2%) but did not reach conventional statistical significance at the sample size. Collectively, these findings are consistent with a meaningful protocol effect supporting the value of the protocol as an adjunct to care.

Published maturation failure rates range from 20% to 60%, yet KDOQI 2019 provides no mandated surveillance protocol.1 For context, the HFM study reported approximately 50% unassisted maturation failure.2,3 Huber et al9 reported 24% maturation failure with a 37% prematuration intervention rate. A meta-analysis of 318 studies (>60,000 accesses) found pooled primary failure rates of approximately 23%.10 The physiology-first failure rate of 7.7% in the present study compares favorably with these published benchmarks, and the overall maturation rate of 92.3% compares with the HFM study's overall maturation rate of approximately 75%,2,3 though direct comparison is limited by differences in population demographics, maturation definitions, and follow-up duration across studies. The magnitude of improvement suggests that structured surveillance can meaningfully shift outcomes relative to observation-based management.

Three design elements appear to contribute to the observed improvement. First, the standardized 2.7-mm arteriotomy creates a fixed hemodynamic reference point that makes serial flow measurements interpretable, whereas the modest fixed inflow appears sufficient to mitigate steal physiology (0 of 52 cases of access-related steal). The small-anastomosis principle is well supported in the literature.7,11,12 Furthermore, the observation of no occurrences of steal syndrome is consistent with the hypothesis that a standardized 2.7-mm anastomosis limits initial inflow sufficiently to mitigate steal physiology. Second, DOC documentation under regional anesthesia establishes reproducible postanastomotic baselines that did not exist in any standard observation patient. Third, failure to progress as a defined construct shifts the clinical question from “has this fistula failed?” to “is it progressing as expected?”; a fistula with stagnant flow at the 2-week visit now triggers referral within 48 hours rather than waiting for clinical failure to declare itself.

Assist rate and salvage conversion

The assisted-maturation rate was similar across the two cohorts (22.4% pre, 25.0% post). This is consistent with the interpretation that the protocol does not eliminate the underlying need for intervention; rather, it shifts when intervention occurs. Under standard observation, fistulas that fail to progress often present at the first 4- to 6-week assessment with anatomy that has already begun to remodel adversely, narrowing the salvage window. Under physiology-first surveillance, failure-to-progress signals are detected at the 2-week visit and trigger angiographic evaluation within 48 hours, whereas the fistula remains anatomically and physiologically salvageable. The downstream effect is reflected in the salvage-success comparison and in where salvage attempts fall along the failure trajectory. In the standard observation cohort, all 14 fistulas that ultimately failed progressed to access loss without an angiographic salvage attempt; failure was apparent only retrospectively. In the physiology-first cohort, the structured surveillance flagged 13 fistulas meeting failure-to-progress criteria, 12 of which (92.3%) were converted to successful clinical maturation. The protocol therefore converts what would otherwise have been failures into successful clinical maturations rather than reducing the underlying intervention burden, and this conversion accounts for a substantial portion of the observed improvement in overall maturation rate. The implication for resource planning is that a physiology-first program does not reduce procedure volume; it relocates procedures to a window in which they are far more likely to succeed.

The most common assisted maturation procedure was endovascular lesion preparation followed by DCB. The endovascular lesion preparation was performed using a Flex Vessel Prep device (Flex Vascular), which delivers KEMIC that modifies the stenotic lesion. This mechanism increases vessel compliance while preserving adventitial integrity, creating a conditioned surface optimized for subsequent DCB deployment. KEMIC-conditioned compliance facilitates predictable paclitaxel transfer, reducing variability in drug delivery against unprepared lesions.7

Unassisted maturation

The largest between-cohort improvement occurred in unassisted maturation (53.4% vs 69.2%), a pattern not well explained by earlier intervention timing alone. Stratification by anastomosis site (Table V) showed this gain was concentrated almost entirely in RC fistulas, whereas BC fistulas changed minimally. Because the standardized 2.7--mm anastomosis was applied equally at both radial and brachial inflow, its expected mechanical effect should not be geography specific, suggesting the improvement is better explained by earlier and more frequent postoperative duplex surveillance allowing RC fistulas—which mature along a slower, lower-inflow trajectory and sit closer to fixed maturation thresholds—to be correctly recognized as progressing rather than prematurely classified as failed. This classification effect cannot be distinguished from a true ascertainment effect, since pre-protocol failures were identified retrospectively via chart review with no documented salvage attempts, whereas postprotocol surveillance was prospective.

Venous outflow pathology

Venous neointimal hyperplasia within the juxta-anastomotic segment and draining vein is the most observed barrier to fistula maturation.13 Progressive neointimal hyperplasia, driven by uremia and turbulent shear stress, impedes venous outward remodeling,14 compounded by pre-existing intimal thickening in CKD patients.15 The clinical consequence is that endovascular intervention for the failing-to-mature AVF is directed almost exclusively at the venous outflow tract,16 making the choice of lesion preparation strategy at this site critical. In this cohort, swing-segment stenosis was the predominant lesion across geographies (7/13 procedures), consistent with published data identifying the juxta-anastomotic and perianastomotic venous segment as the principal site of maturation-limiting disease. Conventional angioplasty is limited by elastic recoil, particularly where neointimal hyperplasia drives early restenosis. The Flex Vessel Prep device is uniquely suited to venous outflow lesion preparation.5, 6, 7 Unlike conventional cutting or scoring balloon technologies, which rely on high-pressure inflation to indent the vessel wall, the Flex Vessel Prep device uses endovascular microblades operating at low radial force (1.0-1.5 atm) to selectively incise the intimal stenosis without barotrauma or deep vessel wall injury. The microblades can be deployed focally or along the entire treatment zone, maximally preparing the vessel for destination therapy.

Quality improvement

The 19-day reduction in time to cannulation carries practical significance. This reduction in time to cannulation most plausibly reflects earlier physiologic clearance under structured 2- and 4-week surveillance, rather than any change in the clinical criteria required for cannulation, since fistulas were assessed against the identical Table II thresholds in both cohorts. Each day of catheter dependence increases infection risk and costs. Thamer et al17 estimated annual Medicare costs from $7871 for uncomplicated AVFs to substantially higher amounts when patency is lost. Across the physiology-first cohort, the 19-day reduction translates into approximately 988 cumulative catheter-days avoided (19 × 52), and approximately nine additional fistulas matured rather than failed at the physiology-first rate vs the standard observation rate. Though formal cost-effectiveness analysis was not performed, these projections suggest substantial resource savings.

Limitations

Several limitations exist in this evaluation. First, the cases describe RC and BC fistulas and not brachiobasilic fistulas, thereby limiting broader application of protocol outcomes. As a single-center QI initiative, this evaluation was designed to generate a signal of a potential protocol effect. The statistical comparisons should be interpreted as descriptive evidence supporting a decision about protocol adoption rather than as confirmatory tests. While the before-and-after evaluation of interventions done by a single surgeon lacks the rigor of a randomized controlled trial, it suggests that introduced practice changes may improve patient outcomes. In addition, pre-protocol failures were identified through retrospective chart review, whereas postprotocol outcomes were captured via prospective structured surveillance, so differences in ascertainment intensity between periods cannot be fully separated from a true protocol effect. In addition, the algorithm (Fig 1) was not prospectively validated; ongoing data collection will refine the specific intervention triggers and their thresholds. Given the senior author's prior career volume and the absence of uniform improvement across access configurations, a surgeon-experience effect is unlikely to account for the magnitude of improvement observed over this 14-month creation interval. However, further evaluation is warranted across multiple centers and multiple clinicians.

Future directions

Further prospective, proactive investigation is warranted to evaluate long-term patency and cost outcomes across multicenter settings.

Conclusions

A physiology-first protocol built on standardized anastomosis, DOC flow documentation, and structured surveillance was associated with a clear and consistent QI signal: access failure decreased, overall maturation increased, and time to cannulation readiness decreased, with no steal syndrome observed. The unassisted-maturation rate moved in the same direction consistent with the protocol effect. These results support adopting algorithmic maturation management as a meaningful adjunct to care in community vascular access programs. Prospective multicenter evaluation is needed to characterize reproducibility across practice settings and surgeon experience levels and to disaggregate the contribution of individual protocol components.
