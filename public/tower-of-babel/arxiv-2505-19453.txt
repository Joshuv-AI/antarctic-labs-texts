Approximately Optimal Mechanism Design for Competing Sellers
Brendan Lucier, Raghuvansh R. Saxena

Abstract
Two sellers compete to sell identical products to a single buyer. Each seller chooses an arbitrary mechanism, possibly involving lotteries, to sell their product. The utility-maximizing buyer can choose to participate in one or both mechanisms, resolving them in either order. Given a common prior over buyer values, how should the sellers design their mechanisms to maximize their respective revenues? We first consider a Stackelberg setting where one seller (Alice) commits to her mechanism and the other seller (Bob) best-responds. We show how to construct a simple and approximately-optimal single-lottery mechanism for Alice that guarantees her a quarter of the optimal monopolist’s revenue, for any regular distribution. Along the way we prove a structural result: for any single-lottery mechanism of Alice, there will always be a best response mechanism for Bob consisting of a single take-it-or-leave-it price. We also show that no mechanism (single-lottery or otherwise) can guarantee Alice more than a 1 / e 1/\mathrm{e} fraction of the monopolist revenue. Finally, we show that our approximation result does not extend to Nash equilibrium: there exist instances in which a monopolist could extract full surplus, but neither competing seller obtains positive revenue at any equilibrium choice of mechanisms.

Introduction

Sellers nearly always face competitive pressure, especially in a digital economy where potential buyers can find identical products being sold by multiple sources.
In such oligopolistic environments,
there are many ways that sellers can potentially compete, such as via price, quality, quantity, timing, and more
[
Hic35
,
VSPSH53
,
Cou20
,
CR89
]
.
Motivated by the theory of approximately optimal mechanism design, we consider yet another way that sellers could compete: by introducing randomness, in the form of lotteries, into their sales protocols.
In so doing, we view competing sellers as mechanism designers and consider how the class of mechanisms from which they choose might influence the outcome of perfect competition.

Imagine the following simple duopoly scenario: two identical sellers wish to sell identical products to a single potential buyer. The buyer can approach either of the sellers first, and in case he does not obtain the item from this seller, can then approach the other seller. The setting is Bayesian, with the buyer’s value for the product drawn from a commonly known distribution. How should revenue-maximizing sellers design their sales mechanisms? In the monopolist setting with only one seller, the theory of screening provides a classic solution: it is revenue-optimal to sell the good using a deterministic take-it-or-leave-it price
[
MR78
,
Mye81
]
. In duopoly, however, a restriction to posted prices leads to classic Bertrand competition, with disastrous results for the sellers. Indeed, any price
p>0
posted by one seller would simply be undercut by the other, leading ultimately to revenue
0
at equilibrium.
1
1

1

Throughout this paper we normalize the marginal cost of production to
0
.

This outcome occurs both
at Nash equilibrium, where prices are chosen simultaneously, and also at Stackelberg equilibrium, where one seller commits to their price first and the other seller responds.

The Bertrand analysis above applies when sellers compete on price, but in principle sellers need not restrict themselves to posted-price mechanisms. For example, a seller could offer a
lottery
in which the buyer pays a price for a
chance
at obtaining the good. It is known that even a monopolist seller may benefit from the use of lotteries for certain screening problems, such as when allocating multiple goods to a single buyer
[
HN13
,
DDT14
,
HR15
]
.
Returning to the case of just a single good but multiple sellers, we ask: is the ability to offer random lotteries likewise helpful for generating revenue in a duopoly?
Can fully general mechanism designers in duopoly approximate, at equilibrium, the revenue obtainable by a monopolist?

Model

To answer this question, we first require a formulation of sales protocols under competition. In the setup we consider, each of two sellers can design an arbitrary sales mechanism. There is a single buyer over which the sellers compete, with value drawn from a known prior. After observing the proposed mechanisms and their realized value, the buyer can choose the order in which to interact with the sellers. The buyer interacts with the selected first seller to the conclusion of their mechanism. The buyer is then free to interact with the other seller, if desired, using that seller’s chosen mechanism. We note that Bertrand competition corresponds to the special case where each seller is restricted to posting a take-it-or-leave-it price.

Under this model, we note that the
taxation principle

[
Ham79
,
Gue81
]
applies: it is without loss for each seller to describe their mechanism as a
pricing function
that assigns a price
p(x)
to each probability of allocation
x\in[0,1]
. Each seller’s protocol then has the buyer select their preferred allocation probability, pay the corresponding price, and receive the good with the chosen probability.
One can therefore model our competitive scenario as a game between sellers in which the actions are pricing functions over lotteries. Once the lottery menus have been selected, the buyer can first select any one entry from either one of the sellers. Then, after the randomness of the chosen lottery has been resolved, the buyer is free to purchase from the other seller, if desired.

Results

We begin our study by considering Stackelberg equilibria of this mechanism design game. One seller (Alice) is the principal who can commit to her menu in advance, and the other seller (Bob) best-responds to the principal’s choice. In our main result, we show that the sellers can obtain positive revenue at Stackelberg equilibrium. Moreover, the expected revenue obtained by the principal, Alice, is always at least a quarter of the expected revenue that she would obtain if she were a monopolist. This bound applies to any value distribution that satisfies either of two constraints: decreasing marginal revenue (DMR) or regularity (see Definition
2.4
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider any instance of the duopoly game in which the buyer’s value is drawn from a distribution
D
that is either regular or DMR. There exists a Stackelberg equilibrium in which the expected revenue of the principal is at least
\textsc{Rev}(D)/4
, where
\textsc{Rev}(D)
is the maximum expected revenue obtainable by a monopolistic seller.

As it turns out, this
4
-approximation is achievable by a simple menu for Alice in which she offers only a single lottery to the buyer. In our main technical lemma we show that, against any such single-lottery menu, Bob’s best response is a posted price. This simplifies Alice’s problem of optimizing over single-item menus, and we find that the optimal such menu always achieves the desired approximation bound.

Lemma 1.2

(Key Lemma)
.

For any value distribution satisfying the conditions of
Theorem

1.1
, any single-lottery mechanism admits a best response that is a posted-price mechanism.

We also provide a lower bound: there are instances of the duopoly game in which Alice cannot obtain more than a
1/\mathrm{e}
fraction of the monopolist revenue at any Stackelberg equilibrium.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider an instance of the duopoly game in which the buyer’s valuation distribution is a point mass.
At every Stackelberg equilibrium, the expected revenue of the principal is at most
\textsc{Rev}(D)/\mathrm{e}
, where
\textsc{Rev}(D)
is highest expected revenue obtainable by a monopolistic seller.

As partial progress toward closing the gap between our upper and lower bounds of
4
and
\mathrm{e}
,
we note a connection between these bounds and our approach of focusing on single-item menus by Alice and posted-price menus by Bob. First, we show that if Alice is restricted to only using single-lottery mechanisms, then our
4

On the other hand,
if Bob is restricted to only using posted-price mechanisms, then Alice can always obtain a
1/\mathrm{e}
fraction of the monopolist revenue.
This latter approximation result applies to
any

The fact that Alice can approximate the monopolist revenue at Stackelberg equilibrium raises a natural follow-up question: can such an approximation result also be supported at Nash equilibrium, where the sellers choose their mechanisms simultaneously and then the buyer interacts with them as before? One observation is that our game always supports at least one pure Nash equilibrium, in which each seller simply offers the good for free. But are there other equilibria with better revenue outcomes?
We show that there exist instances where the answer to this question is negative. Specifically, we provide instances in which
\textsc{Rev}(D)
is positive and yet both sellers obtain
0
revenue at every pure Nash equilibrium, and hence no non-trivial approximation to the monopolist revenue can be obtained.
Furthermore, in these instances, the buyer’s type distribution is a point mass meaning that the buyer’s value is fixed and known to both sellers.

Theorem 1.4
.

Consider an instance of the duopoly game in which the buyer’s valuation distribution is a point mass. At every pure Nash equilibrium, both sellers obtain revenue
0
.

Taken together with the classic analysis of Bertrand competition, these results suggest that a duopolist can approximate a monopolist’s revenue if they have (a) the use of general randomized mechanisms, plus (b) the power to commit as a first-mover. However, neither of these two – randomization nor commitment – is sufficient on its own to guarantee positive revenue.

Proof Techniques

The key lemma that drives our main approximation result is that, against any single-lottery mechanism chosen by Alice, Bob’s best response is a posted-price mechanism.
This conclusion is reminiscent of Myerson’s characterization of the optimal mechanism in monopoly,
but the duopoly setting introduces new challenges. For one thing, recall that the buyer can choose which seller to purchase from first. Depending on the menu selected by Bob, some buyer types may be better off buying from Alice first and others will prefer to buy first from Bob. From Bob’s perspective, this behavior introduces distortion into his perceived distribution over buyer values. One might therefore be tempted to think of Bob as a monopolist who faces a distorted value distribution. Crucially, however,
this distortion is endogenous to Bob’s choice of mechanism
. This endogeneity complicates the design problem faced by Bob.

To address this issue, we analyze the structure of buyer selections when Alice commits to a single-lottery mechanism. For any mechanism chosen by Bob, all buyer types can be grouped into three categories (see Section
3.2
). The lowest buyer types are unwilling to buy Alice’s lottery, and will only consider Bob’s mechanism. Intermediate types are willing to purchase from either mechanism, but choose to interact with Bob first. The highest types choose to purchase Alice’s lottery first, and only if they lose do they consider buying from Bob. The threshold between the second and third categories is endogenous to Bob’s choice of mechanism.
2
2

2

Alice’s lottery determines the threshold between the first and second categories.

Our approach to Bob’s design problem is therefore to fix an arbitrary threshold type
s
, then consider Bob’s optimal mechanism subject to being consistent with this choice of
s
. This fixes the distortion of buyer values perceived by Bob (see
Lemma

3.5
), leaving us with a more traditional mechanism design problem but with an additional self-consistency constraint. While the distorted value distribution may no longer be regular, a virtual value analysis allows us to conclude that Bob’s optimal mechanism (a) has allocation supported on
\{0,x\}
, for some
x\geq 0
, for all types below the threshold
s
(
Lemma

3.8
), and (b) generates revenue at most that of the best posted-price mechanism for types above the threshold
s
. It is here where we use our assumption that the value distribution is either regular or DMR: either of these conditions suffices to control the virtual values of the distorted distribution, which drives our analysis. This partial characterization is enough to let us bound the revenue of the optimal mechanism by a convex combination of posted-price mechanisms, from which we conclude that the optimal revenue (over all possible thresholds) is realized at some posted-price mechanism.

There is also an economic literature on competition between mechanism designers who compete to attract bidders from a potential pool. Champsaur and Rochet

study a model of sellers who compete for a single buyer via quality differentiation, in the style of Mussa and Rosen

Alice and Bob can each choose a

The buyer selects a seller

For the buyer’s equilibrium play, note first that if

For the sellers’ equilibrium play, we consider two different equilibrium concepts that correspond to subgame perfect equilibrium under two different timing models. At
(pure) Nash Equilibrium
, Alice and Bob choose their mechanisms simultaneously and in best response to each other. Formally, the pair of mechanisms
(\mathcal{M}_{A},\mathcal{M}_{B})

\mathcal{M}_{A}\in\argmax_{\mathcal{M}}\mathsf{rev}_{\mathsf{A},\left\lparen\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}_{B}\right\rparen}\left\lparen D\right\rparen

At

from Alice’s choice of mechanism to a mechanism for Bob, and Alice’s strategy is a mechanism

\mathbb{M}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\in\argmax_{\mathcal{M}}\mathsf{rev}_{\mathsf{B},\left\lparen\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\mathcal{M}\right\rparen}\left\lparen D\right\rparen

Pricing Functions and the Taxation Principle.

We observe an analogue of the taxation principle: any mechanism can equivalently be defined by a pricing function.
Formally, a
pricing
mechanism is described by a pricing function
M\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
. The input to the mechanism is a choice of
x\in[0,1]
, which results in payment
M(x)
and the buyer receiving the good with probability
x

proper
if (a)
M(0)=0
, (b)
M
is weakly increasing, and (c)
M
is continuous and weakly convex on interval
[0,\bar{x}]

\bar{x}=\sup\{x\colon M(x)<\infty\}
is the maximum probability offered at a finite price.

(Taxation Principle)
.

For any mechanism
\mathcal{M}
for either seller there is a strategically equivalent proper pricing mechanism. In the corresponding pricing rule,
M(x)
is the minimum expected payment over any distribution of buyer messages that results in an allocation with probability at least
x
(or
M(x)=\infty
if no such message exists).
6
6

6

That

follows because of the assumed null message. That

is non-decreasing follows from free disposal. That

is continuous and weakly convex follows because, for any

with

to obtain expected allocation

at an expected price of

5
Conclusions and Open Problems

In this paper we studied a game played between two competing mechanism designers with the ability to randomize allocations. When competition is perfect and each seller is selling an identical good, there are instances where only the trivial pure Nash equilibrium exists. However, in the Stackelberg setting where one seller can commit to her choice of mechanism before the other responds, it is possible to approximate the revenue raised by a monopolist, for any buyer value distribution that satisfies either the regularity condition or the decreasing marginal revenue condition.

Many questions and future directions are left open. On a technical level, there is a gap left between our upper and lower bounds on the leader’s Stackelberg payoff; the approximation factor versus the monopolist revenue lies in
\left[\mathrm{e},4\right]
. It appears that resolving this gap will require the use of multi-lottery mechanisms: we show that the factor
4
is tight if Alice is restricted to using a single-lottery mechanism, and the factor
\mathrm{e}
is tight if Bob is required to use a posted-price mechanism. Beyond resolving the exact approximation factor, it would be interesting to resolve the structure of the exact optimal mechanisms chosen at Stackelberg equilibrium, and/or derive conditions under which simple mechanism structures are optimal.

There are also numerous extensions to our model that would be interesting to explore. Sellers could have multiple items to sell to a buyer with combinatorial preferences, the buyers may not be risk neutral, or there could be multiple buyers interacting with the competing sellers. The latter could involve either synchronous or asynchronous timing of interactions. Also related to timing, we assume in our model that the buyer visits the mechanisms in sequence, resolving one before choosing whether to visit the other. One could alternatively consider a setting where a buyer can participate in multiple mechanisms simultaneously, partially resolving one and then switching to another. In such an environment, could sellers benefit by designing multi-round protocols that (at equilibrium) account for the possibility of a buyer leaving partway for a competitor and returning later?

Appendix A
Missing Proofs: Taxation Principle

In this section we provide a proof of
Observation

2.1
. Recall the statement:

Observation 2.1
(Taxation Principle).
For any mechanism
\mathcal{M}
for either seller there is a strategically equivalent proper pricing mechanism. In the corresponding pricing rule,
M(x)
is the minimum expected payment over any distribution of buyer messages that results in an allocation with probability at least
x
(or
M(x)=\infty
if no such message exists).

To see why
Observation

2.1
holds intuitively, note that it simply replaces each mechanism with an equivalent pricing rule assuming the mechanism was run by a monopolist. What
Observation

2.1
claims is that, in our duopolist setting, the original joint mechanism is also equivalent to the resulting joint pricing rules. The reason is that since the buyer participates in any mechanism to completion before engaging with the other mechanism, only the outcome of each mechanism (
i.e.
, its distribution over allocation and payment) can impact buyer behavior in the other.
Moreover, as the buyer is risk-neutral with quasi-linear utility, only the resulting probability of allocation and
expected
payment can influence buyer behavior. By definition, the constructed pricing rules do not change allocation probabilities or expected payments, so
Observation

2.1
follows.

More formally, suppose the two sellers select mechanisms
\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}
. For any undominated strategy
(i,\sigma_{i},\sigma_{-i})
for the buyer, let
a_{i}
and
t_{i}
denote the resulting allocation probability and expected payment from mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{i}
, and let
a_{-i}
and
t_{-i}
denote the allocation probability and expected payment from
\mathcal{M}_{-i}
conditional on not obtaining the item from mechanism
i
. Then the buyer’s expected utility from this strategy is

va_{i}-t_{i}+(1-a_{i})(va_{-i}-t_{-i}).

In particular, the buyer’s expected utility is entirely determined by
(a_{i},a_{-i},t_{i},t_{-i})
.

Let
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
be the corresponding proper pricing rules described in
Observation

2.1
for mechanisms
\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Consider what would occur if seller
1
deviated from mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{1}
to the pricing mechanism
M_{1}
.
By construction of
M_{1}
, any profile of allocation probabilities and expected transfers
(a_{i},a_{-i},t_{i},t_{-i})
is attainable by the buyer from the profile of mechanisms
(M_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})
if and only if they are obtainable via an undominated strategy for the mechanisms
(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})
. Thus the corresponding obtainable expected utilities for the buyer are identical as well. The correspondence of buyer-optimal behavior thus results in the same buyer utility and transfers to the sellers, and in particular for seller
1
, so it is without loss for seller
1
to employ pricing mechanism
M_{1}
instead of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
. By symmetry, the same is true for seller
2
. Thus each seller has a strategically equivalent proper pricing mechanism, regardless of the choice of the other seller, as claimed.

\displaystyle<\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,1,v\right\rparen.

Proof.

For the first one, note using
Equation

1
that:

\displaystyle\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,x,v\right\rparen-\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,1,v\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq B\left\lparen 1\right\rparen-B\left\lparen x\right\rparen+\left\lparen 1-x\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen zv-a-v\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq B\left\lparen 1\right\rparen-v\cdot\left\lparen 1-x\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen

\displaystyle<0.

(As
x\leq 1-\epsilon
and
v>\frac{B(1)}{\epsilon\cdot\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen}
)

Similarly, we have using
Equation

2
that:

\displaystyle\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,x,v\right\rparen-\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,1,v\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen xv-B\left\lparen x\right\rparen-v+B\left\lparen 1\right\rparen\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen B\left\lparen 1\right\rparen-v\cdot\left\lparen 1-x\right\rparen\right\rparen

\displaystyle<0.

(As
x\leq 1-\epsilon
and
v>\frac{B(1)}{\epsilon\cdot\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen}
)

∎

We now establish some properties about the buyer behavior in our duopolist setting that will be useful throughout
Section

3
. First, we show that a buyer only buys from Alice first if his value exceeds the bang-per-buck price
p
offered by Alice.

Lemma B.2
.

For all mechanisms
B
and all
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
, we have
\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)>0
and
v>p
. It follows that
p\leq\mathsf{AB}\text{-}\mathsf{start}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
.

Proof.

Note that
v\cdot\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)>B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen
because if not, the buyer can get the same utility by purchasing from Bob first, and our tie-breaking would contradict
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
. It follows that
\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)>0
. To show that
v>p
, note that
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
and our tie-breaking implies that
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}(v),v\right\rparen<\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}(v),v\right\rparen
. Simplifying using
Equations

1
and
2
, we get:

z\cdot\left\lparen v\cdot\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)-B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen\right\rparen<\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\cdot\max\left\lparen 0,zv-a\right\rparen.

As the left side is positive, we get
zv>a
and we are done.
∎

Next, we show that the option
\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen
chosen by the buyer is non-decreasing in
v
.

Lemma B.3
.

For all mechanisms
B
and all
0\leq v\leq v^{\prime}
, we have
\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\leq\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen
.

Proof.

To show that
\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen
is non-decreasing, it suffices to show that it is non-decreasing in the interval
[0,p]
and also in the interval
\left[p,\infty\right)
. We prove both these parts separately. For the first part, note that
v\leq v^{\prime}\in[0,p]
implies from
Lemma

B.2
that
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
. This means that
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen,v\right\rparen\leq\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen,v\right\rparen
and
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen,v^{\prime}\right\rparen\leq\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen,v^{\prime}\right\rparen
. Adding and using
Equation

1
, we get:

\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen-\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen v^{\prime}-v\right\rparen\geq 0.

The result follows. For the second part, for any
v\leq v^{\prime}\in\left[p,\infty\right)
, consider the case
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
and
v^{\prime}\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
(the other cases are similar). This means that
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen,v\right\rparen\leq\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen,v\right\rparen
and
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen,v^{\prime}\right\rparen\leq\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen,v^{\prime}\right\rparen
. Adding and using
Equations

1
and
2
, we get:

\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen-\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen v^{\prime}-v\right\rparen\geq 0.

As
z<1
, the result follows.
∎

The next lemma shows that the bang-per-buck price paid by the buyer to Bob is at most his valuation, and is at most
p
if and only the buyer goes to Bob first.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
B
be a mechanism and
v\geq 0
. If we have
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
and
\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)>0
, then
\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}\leq\min\left\lparen p,v\right\rparen
. Else, if
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
, we have
p<\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}<v
.

Proof.

For all
v>p
such that
\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)>0
, note by our tiebreaking that we have
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
if and only if
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}(v),v\right\rparen\leq\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}(v),v\right\rparen
. Using
Equations

1
and
2
, we get that
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
if and only if
\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}\leq p
. Next, note that for all
v\leq p
such
\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)>0
, we have
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
by
Lemma

B.2
which means that
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,0,v\right\rparen\leq\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}(v),v\right\rparen
. Using
Equation

1
, we get that
\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}\leq v
. Finally, for all
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
which implies
v>p
and
\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)>0
by
Lemma

B.2
, we have by our tie breaking that
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,0,v\right\rparen<\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}(v),v\right\rparen
which from
Equations

1
and
2
gives
\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}<v
as
z<1
. Combining gives the lemma.
∎

Now, we show that the bang-per-buck price paid by the buyer is also non-decreasing in
v
, by first showing a more general inequality.

Lemma B.5
.

Let
B
be a mechanism and
v\geq 0
satisfy
\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)>0
. For all
0<\delta<v
and
\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)-\frac{\delta\cdot\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}{2v}<x^{\prime}\leq 1
, we have
\frac{B\left\lparen x^{\prime}\right\rparen}{x^{\prime}}\geq\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}-\delta
. Combining with
Lemma

B.3
, for such
v
, we have for all
v^{\prime}\geq v
that
\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}\leq\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen}
.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)>0
implies that
x^{\prime}>0
. By setting
v^{\prime}=v
if
v\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
or if
v\leq p
and
v^{\prime}=a+\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen\cdot v
otherwise, note that we have from
Equations

1
and
2
that:

x^{\prime}\cdot\left\lparen v^{\prime}-\frac{B\left\lparen x^{\prime}\right\rparen}{x^{\prime}}\right\rparen\leq\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\cdot\left\lparen v^{\prime}-\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}\right\rparen.

Note from the definition of
v^{\prime}
and
Lemma

B.4
that the last factor is non-negative. Using the bounds
v^{\prime}\leq v
and
\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\leq x^{\prime}+\frac{\delta\cdot\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}{2v}
, this rearranges to:

x^{\prime}\cdot\left\lparen v^{\prime}-\frac{B\left\lparen x^{\prime}\right\rparen}{x^{\prime}}\right\rparen\leq x^{\prime}\cdot\left\lparen v^{\prime}-\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}\right\rparen+\frac{\delta\cdot\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}{2}.

As
\frac{\mathtt{x}_{B}(v)}{2}\leq x^{\prime}
, we can divide by
x^{\prime}
to get the lemma.
∎

Now, we show that a buyer with type
\mathsf{AB}\text{-}\mathsf{start}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
always buys from Bob first, unless
\mathsf{AB}\text{-}\mathsf{start}\left\lparen B\right\rparen=\infty
.

Lemma B.6
.

For all mechanisms
B
, if
\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen\neq\emptyset
(that is, if
\mathsf{AB}\text{-}\mathsf{start}\left\lparen B\right\rparen<\infty
), then, we have
\mathsf{AB}\text{-}\mathsf{start}\left\lparen B\right\rparen\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
.

Proof.

The high-level reason this is true is that we tiebreak in favor of Bob. Formally, let
s=\mathsf{AB}\text{-}\mathsf{start}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
and observe that we can assume
s>p
as otherwise we are done by
Lemma

B.2
. Also, note that
s-\delta\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
for all
\delta>0
. This means that, for all
\delta>0
, there exists
x_{\delta}\in[0,1]
such that
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}(s),s-\delta\right\rparen\leq\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen s-\delta\right\rparen,s-\delta\right\rparen
. Using
Equations

1
and
2
, we get:

\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(s)\cdot s-B\left\lparen x\right\rparen\right\rparen-\delta\leq\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen s-\delta\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen a+\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen\cdot s\right\rparen-B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen s-\delta\right\rparen\right\rparen.

Define
s^{\prime}=a+\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen\cdot s
and observe that the right hand side is upper bounded by
s^{\prime}\cdot\mathtt{z}_{B}\left\lparen s^{\prime}\right\rparen-B\left\lparen\mathtt{z}_{B}\left\lparen s^{\prime}\right\rparen\right\rparen
. As this bound holds for all
\delta>0
, we have
\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}(s)\cdot s-B\left\lparen x\right\rparen\right\rparen\leq s^{\prime}\cdot\mathtt{z}_{B}\left\lparen s^{\prime}\right\rparen-B\left\lparen\mathtt{z}_{B}\left\lparen s^{\prime}\right\rparen\right\rparen
. Adding
zs-a
on both sides and
Equations

1
and
2
, we get
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{x}_{B}(s),s\right\rparen\leq\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B,\mathtt{z}_{B}\left\lparen s^{\prime}\right\rparen,s\right\rparen
. This is impossible if
s\in\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen
, and we are done.
∎

Finally, we show a lemma to handle the corner case mentioned in
Lemma

3.8
.

Lemma B.7
.

Let
B
be a mechanism for Bob with
\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen=\emptyset
. Define the mechanism
B^{\prime}
as:

B^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}B(x),&\text{~if~}x<1\\
\sup_{v:\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen>0}\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen},&\text{~if~}x=1\end{cases}

Then, we have
\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B^{\prime}\right\rparen=\emptyset
and
B^{\prime}(1)=\sup_{v:\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen>0}\frac{B^{\prime}\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen}
and
\mathsf{rev}_{\mathsf{B},\left\lparen A,B\right\rparen}\left\lparen D\right\rparen\leq\mathsf{rev}_{\mathsf{B},\left\lparen A,B^{\prime}\right\rparen}\left\lparen D\right\rparen
.

Proof.

We start with the following helper claim.

Claim B.8
.

Let
B^{\prime\prime}
be a mechanism for Bob with
\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B^{\prime\prime}\right\rparen=\emptyset
and let
\nabla^{\prime\prime}=\sup_{v:\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen>0}\frac{B^{\prime\prime}\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen}
. Then,
\nabla^{\prime\prime}\leq p
and the supremum in
\nabla^{\prime\prime}
is attained at
v
if and only if
\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen=1
.

Proof.

That we have
\nabla^{\prime\prime}\leq p
is from
Lemma

B.4
. Observe that the “if” direction follows easily from
Lemma

B.5
. For the “only if” direction, assume for contradiction that the supremum is attained at some point
v
for which
\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen<1
. Then, we have from
Lemma

B.5
that it also attained at
v^{\prime}
for all
v^{\prime}\geq v
. Moreover, for a large enough
v^{\prime}
,
Lemma

B.1
says that
\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen<\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen
. By our tie-breaking, we must have that
\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B^{\prime\prime},\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\left\lparen v^{\prime}\right\rparen,v\right\rparen<\mathsf{util}_{\mathtt{BA}}\left\lparen B^{\prime\prime},\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen,v\right\rparen
. Using
Equation

1
, and the fact that the supremum is attained at both
v
and
v^{\prime}
, this gives a contradiction.
∎

Let
\nabla=\sup_{v:\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen>0}\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen}
and
\nabla^{\prime}=\sup_{v:\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen>0}\frac{B^{\prime}\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen}
for convenience. If the supremum in
\nabla
is attained we have
B=B^{\prime}
from
Claim

B.8
and there is nothing to show. Thus, we assume throughout that the supremum in
\nabla
is never attained.

For this case,
Claim

B.8
says that
\nabla\leq p
and
\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen<1
for all
v\geq 0
. As the only option that can possibly change between
B
and
B^{\prime}
is the option corresponding to
x=1
, it follows that for all
v\geq 0
, we have
\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\in\left\{\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen,1\right\}
. Again using the fact that
\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen<1
and
B^{\prime}
matches
B
at every point but
x=1
, it follows that
\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen>0
implies that
\frac{B^{\prime}\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen}\leq\nabla\leq p
. From
Lemmas

B.2
and
B.4
, this is possible only if
\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B^{\prime}\right\rparen=\emptyset
, showing the first part. For the second part, from
Claim

B.8
on
B^{\prime}
that it is non-trivial only if
\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen<1
for all
v\geq 0
. However, this would mean that
\nabla^{\prime}=\nabla
and the second part follows.

For the third part, we show that any buyer pays at least as much to Bob when his mechanism is
B^{\prime}
as he pays when his mechanism is
B
. Fix a buyer with valuation
v\geq 0
. As
\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B^{\prime}\right\rparen=\mathsf{V}_{\mathtt{AB}}\left\lparen B\right\rparen=\emptyset
, we have the buyer pays
B^{\prime}\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen
to Bob when his mechanism is
B^{\prime}
and
B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen
when his mechanism is

. Using the fact that
\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen<1
and
\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\in\left\{\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen,1\right\}
, we get that
B^{\prime}\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B^{\prime}}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen\in\left\{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen,\nabla\right\}
. Now, either
\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen=0
implying
B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen=0
and we are done or
\nabla\geq\frac{B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen}{\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen}\geq B\left\lparen\mathtt{x}_{B}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\right\rparen
, and we are done.

∎

B.2
An Auxiliary Distribution

In this section, we show some lemmas about our auxiliary distribution
D_{s}
defined in

, for an arbitrary

. In particular, we prove

3.5
. Throughout,
f_{s}
and
F_{s}
and probability density and the cumulative probability functions for
D_{s}
and

Lemma B.9
.

Let
s\geq p
. We have:

F_{s}\left\lparen v\right\rparen=\begin{cases}z\cdot\left\lparen 1-F(s)\right\rparen+F\left\lparen v\right\rparen,&\text{~if~}v\leq p\\
z\cdot\left\lparen 1-F(s)\right\rparen+F\left\lparen\frac{v-a}{1-z}\right\rparen,&\text{~if~}p<v<a+\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen\cdot s\\

z+\left\lparen 1-z\right\rparen\cdot F\left\lparen v\right\rparen,&\text{~if~}s\leq v<\infty\\
\end{cases}.

Proof.

We only show the second case as the others are direct from

\displaystyle F_{s}\left\lparen v\right\rparen

\displaystyle=z\cdot\left\lparen 1-F(s)\right\rparen+F\left\lparen p\right\rparen+\frac{1}{1-z}\cdot\int_{p}^{v}f\left\lparen\frac{y-a}{1-z}\right\rparen~\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=z\cdot\left\lparen 1-F(s)\right\rparen+F\left\lparen p\right\rparen+\int_{p}^{\frac{v-a}{1-z}}f\left\lparen y\right\rparen~\mathrm{d}y

(Reparametrizing

)

\displaystyle=z\cdot\left\lparen 1-F(s)\right\rparen+F\left\lparen\frac{v-a}{1-z}\right\rparen.

∎

Lemma B.10
.

Let
s\geq p
