Differences in Early Tacrolimus Exposure in Kidney Transplant Recipients With and Without CYP3A5 Genotype‐Guided Tacrolimus Dosing
Authors: Amy L Pasternak, Jenna Schwartz, Brett Vanderwerff, Sebastian Zöllner, Danielle J Haakinson, Mona Doshi, Jeong M Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT CYP3A5 genotype is a known predictor of tacrolimus dose requirements. This single‐center retrospective study evaluated the impact of CYP3A5 genotype‐guided dosing, implemented at our institution in 10/2023, on early tacrolimus exposure. Patients were included if they were adult kidney transplant recipients between 7/2014 and 10/2024; patients in the weight‐based cohort had research CYP3A5 genotypes in the institutional research biorepository and the genotype‐guided cohort had a clinical CYP3A5 genotype available at transplant. The primary outcome was time to therapeutic steady‐state (TSS) and secondary outcomes included classification of trough concentration on postoperative‐day (POD) 2, tacrolimus dose at TSS, and tacrolimus intrapatient variability (IPV) at 14‐ and 30‐day post‐transplant. Outcomes were also evaluated within each CYP3A5 phenotype and a per‐protocol evaluation. Outcomes were compared via Mann–Whitney U, Chi‐squared, or Fischer's exact tests. The median TSS was decreased in CYP3A5 normal metabolizers (6.0 days (3.0–6.0) vs. 16.0 days (8.0–16.0); p = 0.01) and CYP3A5 intermediate metabolizers had significantly fewer subtherapeutic troughs on POD2 (25.8% vs. 61.1%; p = 0.01) in the genotype‐guided cohort. CYP3A5 poor metabolizers were more likely to have a subtherapeutic trough on POD2 in the genotype‐guided cohort. The time below therapeutic range in the first 14 days was significantly reduced for CYP3A5 intermediate metabolizers who received per‐protocol, genotype‐guided dosing. The TSS dose did not differ between the cohorts but was lower than the genotype‐guided protocol dosing. CYP3A5 genotype‐guided dosing reduced the likelihood of early subtherapeutic exposure in CYP3A5 normal and intermediate metabolizers. Refinement of the genotype‐guided dosing strategy may help further improve early tacrolimus exposure.Keywords: CYP3A5, implementation, personalized dosing, pharmacogenetics, tacrolimus

Introduction

Kidney transplantation improves patient survival compared to dialysis in patients with end stage renal disease [1]. However, the success of kidney transplantation relies in part on appropriate immunosuppression with tacrolimus as the mainstay of post‐transplant immunosuppression regimens [2]. Tacrolimus has a narrow therapeutic index and large pharmacokinetic variability requiring extensive therapeutic drug monitoring, most often through trough concentrations which are titrated to a target exposure. Achieving therapeutic tacrolimus concentrations quickly is desired to minimize this risk of subtherapeutic or supratherapeutic exposure, which can contribute to increased rejection risk or adverse events such as nephrotoxicity, respectively [3, 4].

Genetic polymorphisms in CYP3A5 have been identified as a primary contributor for the interpatient pharmacokinetic variability of tacrolimus. Individuals carrying one or more CYP3A5*1 alleles metabolize tacrolimus more rapidly and require higher doses than those without a CYP3A5*1 allele. The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) guidelines recommend empirically increasing the initial dose of tacrolimus in patients known to carry CYP3A5*1 [5]. A limited number of prospective randomized trials have been conducted evaluating CYP3A5 genotype‐guided dosing in adult kidney transplant recipients [6, 7, 8]. Two of these studies concluded that CYP3A5 genotype‐guided dosing improved early therapeutic tacrolimus exposure compared to standard weight‐based dosing [6, 8]. None of the prospective clinical trials identified any impact on clinical outcomes such as graft rejection, graft loss, or mortality [6, 7, 9]. Despite limited data on clinical outcomes, some studies have suggested that CYP3A5 genotype may be a marker for healthcare resource utilization, and patients with CYP3A5*1 may have a higher healthcare resource utilization burden secondary to increased monitoring or dose adjustment requirements, although again this data is mixed [10, 11, 12].

Intrapatient variability (IPV) in tacrolimus concentrations has been associated with clinical outcomes post‐transplant. Higher IPV, evaluated as the coefficient of variation (CV) or time in therapeutic range (TTR), has been associated with reduced graft function and higher risk of graft rejection, graft loss, or mortality post‐transplant [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Although most studies have evaluated outcomes around 12 months post‐transplant, early IPV has been associated with early rejection risk [3, 18]. The impact of CYP3A5 genotype on IPV is less well defined. Most available studies to date have shown no association; however, these studies evaluated IPV at 6 months or greater post‐transplant [19, 20, 21]. One study that evaluated IPV within the first 6 months post‐transplant did identify an association between CYP3A5 genotype and tacrolimus CV [18]. Additionally, one study has demonstrated CYP3A5 genotype‐guided dosing improved the TTR in the early post‐transplant period [22]. Given the consistent association between IPV and clinical outcomes, further evaluation of the impact of CYP3A5 genotype, especially related to CYP3A5 genotype‐guided dosing and early IPV, warrants additional investigation.

Genotype‐guided tacrolimus dosing has increased over the last decade, with approximately 15% of US transplant centers reporting that they utilize this strategy in at least some transplant patients [23]. Our center, and others, have published implementation workflows demonstrating the feasibility of integrating genotype‐guided prescribing; however, to date, few outcomes assessments have been reported from clinical programs [22, 24, 25]. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the real‐world impact of implementing a CYP3A5 genotype‐guided tacrolimus dosing protocol on early therapeutic drug monitoring outcomes and IPV for immediate‐release tacrolimus in adult kidney transplant recipients.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This was a single‐center, retrospective study of adult (≥ 18 years old) kidney transplant recipients at the University of Michigan. This study was reviewed and approved by the University of Michigan IRB (HUM00161844, HUM00143486). Subjects were divided into two cohorts based on the timing of their kidney transplant and source of CYP3A5 genotype data. The weight‐based cohort included all kidney transplant recipients who received their transplant between 7/1/2014 and 10/10/2023, received weight‐based tacrolimus dosing (Table 1), and had research CYP3A5 genotypes available from the Michigan Genomics Initiative (MGI) biorepository [26, 27]. MGI participants are recruited primarily during surgical visits, although no participants were targeted for recruitment specifically because they were undergoing a kidney transplant. As the MGI genetic results are not clinical grade tests, the genotype results are unknown to all healthcare providers providing care to the participant. The genotype‐guided cohort included kidney transplant recipients who received their transplant between 10/11/2023–10/10/2024 and underwent clinical CYP3A5 genotype testing per institution protocol prior to the time of transplant [25]. Both MGI and clinical genotyping evaluated the CYP3A5*3 (rs776746; 6981A>G), CYP3A5*6 (rs10264272; 14685G>A), and CYP3A5*7 (rs41303343; 27126_27127insT) alleles to determine CYP3A5 genotype; a CYP3A5*1 inferred none of the evaluated variant alleles were identified. CYP3A5 genotypes were translated into phenotypes: normal metabolizer (i.e., *1/*1), intermediate metabolizer (i.e., *1 heterozygous), and poor metabolizer (i.e., no *1 allele), per the CPIC guidelines [5]. The recommended initial tacrolimus doses in the two cohorts are described in Table 1. The doses for the genotype‐guided cohort were established through an evaluation of current clinical practice guidelines and internal data and automatically adjusted according to the CYP3A5 phenotype in the post‐operative medication order set, as described previously [5, 25]. Patients were excluded if they had previously undergone a liver transplant, received multiple organs during the kidney transplant surgery, participated in a clinical trial at the time of transplant, received a formulation other than immediate‐release tacrolimus following kidney transplant, had a tacrolimus dose adjustment prior to the first tacrolimus trough, had taken oral tacrolimus prior to transplant, or were prescribed a CYP3A inducer or inhibitor at the time of transplant.

Immunosuppression and Therapeutic Drug Monitoring

According to the institutional protocol, the following patients received rabbit antithymocyte globulin induction (total of 5 mg/kg in 3 divided doses): steroid‐avoidant maintenance immunosuppression, African‐American race, living unrelated kidney transplant, positive crossmatch, panel reactive antibody > 20%, presence of donor‐specific antibody, and marginal graft function within 24 h post‐transplant defined as urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h, a decline in serum creatinine < 10% from baseline, or a need for hemodialysis. The standard maintenance immunosuppression consisted of tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil 1000 mg orally every 12 h, and a steroid taper. Immediate‐release tacrolimus was initiated within 24 h post‐transplant and administered orally every 12 h (Table 1). The first tacrolimus trough concentration was typically measured prior to the morning dose on post‐operative day (POD) 2. Subsequent dose adjustments were guided by therapeutic drug monitoring with a tacrolimus trough goal range of 8–12 ng/mL for the first 90 days post‐transplant. Standard steroid taper was administered according to the following schedule: methylprednisolone 500 mg intraoperatively followed by oral prednisone starting at 100 mg on POD1 with gradual taper to 10 mg by POD30. Further reduction of prednisone to 5 mg was done at the discretion of the transplant nephrologist. In the steroid‐avoidant maintenance immunosuppression, prednisone was discontinued on POD5.

Data Collection

All data were extracted from the electronic health record and included patient demographics, such as age at transplant, weight, sex, and race, CYP3A5 genotype, and transplant characteristics, such as donor type, post‐transplant graft function, and immunosuppression regimen. Patients were considered to have immediate graft function (IGF) if their urine output was ≥ 0.5 mL/kg/h on POD1 and serum creatinine was decreased ≥ 10% from baseline. The initial tacrolimus dose and all tacrolimus trough concentrations with corresponding doses were collected for the first 90 days post‐transplant.

IPV was calculated as TTR and CV for the first 14‐ and 30‐day post‐transplant as described previously [28] Time above and time below therapeutic range were also calculated via the same method. Patients who had < 10 days of tacrolimus follow‐up were excluded from all IPV assessments, and patients who had < 21 days of tacrolimus follow‐up were excluded from the 30‐day IPV evaluations. As early graft dysfunction was identified as a significant predictor of increased tacrolimus exposure, there has been an internal protocol change in tacrolimus dosing for patients with non‐IGF during the study period that may have changed the target trough concentration [27, 28]. Therefore, only patients who had IGF were included in the IPV analyses.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the time to first therapeutic steady‐state (TSS) tacrolimus trough concentration. Steady state was defined as the tacrolimus trough level measured following at least five unchanged doses, and a therapeutic tacrolimus concentration was between 8 and 12 ng/mL. Secondary analyses included the percentage of patients whose first tacrolimus trough concentration, measured on POD2, fell above, within, or below the therapeutic range, the tacrolimus dose (mg/kg/dose) at the first TSS trough concentration, and the TTR and CV at the specified time points.

Statistical Analysis

All comparisons were made between the weight‐based and genotype‐guided cohorts. Baseline characteristics were compared using Chi‐squared or Fischer's exact test or Mann–Whitney U test due to the non‐normal distribution of data. The primary outcome and secondary outcomes of dose at first TSS trough and IPV at 14‐ and 30‐day post‐transplant were also compared via Mann–Whitney U test. The secondary outcome of the proportion of patients within therapeutic range on POD2 was evaluated with Chi‐squared or Fischer's exact test. The primary analysis followed an intent‐to‐treat (ITT) design. A sensitivity, per‐protocol analysis was conducted and included only patients who received dosing that matched the protocol‐based tacrolimus dosing for their cohort, as described in Table 1. Finally, subgroup analysis evaluated the same outcomes within CYP3A5 normal metabolizers, intermediate metabolizers, and poor metabolizers, respectively. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 27. IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

Results

A total of 332 patients were included in the study. As shown in Figure 1, 212 patients were included in the weight‐based cohort and 120 patients were included in the genotype‐guided cohort, although not all patients had data for all outcomes. Patient demographics and transplant‐related characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Patients in the genotype‐guided cohort were significantly older than those in the weight‐based cohort (median age 57.7 vs. 51.0 years, p < 0.001). There was a higher proportion of Black or African American patients in the genotype‐guided cohort (34.2% vs. 13.2%) and CYP3A5 normal and intermediate metabolizers were observed more frequently in the genotype‐guided cohort (32.5% vs. 20.3%, p = 0.04). Patients in the weight‐based cohort had living kidney donation more frequently (57.5% vs. 30.8%, p < 0.001), while the genotype‐guided cohort had a higher proportion of donation after cardiac death (25.0% vs. 12.7%, p < 0.001). IGF was more common in the weight‐based cohort (76.4% vs. 59.2%, p = 0.005).

When including all patients in the ITT analysis, the median time to TSS did not differ between the cohorts (p = 0.94, Table 3), nor did the initial trough concentration classification or tacrolimus dose at TSS. In the planned subgroup analyses by CYP3A5 phenotype, there was a significant reduction in the time to TSS in CYP3A5 normal metabolizers in the genotype‐guided cohort compared to the weight‐based cohort where the genotype‐guided cohort achieved TSS approximately 10 days faster (p = 0.01, Table 3). CYP3A5 intermediate metabolizers in the genotype‐guided cohort were less likely to have a subtherapeutic initial trough concentration compared to the weight‐based dosing (25.8% vs. 61.1%, p = 0.01, Figure 2A) while CYP3A5 poor metabolizers in the genotype‐guided cohort were more likely to have an initial subtherapeutic trough concentration (45.7% vs. 23.5%, p = 0.002, Figure 2A). There were no differences in the TSS dose (data not shown). A total of 232 patients were included in the ITT IPV analysis. No differences were observed for the 14‐ or 30‐day TTR measures for any comparisons. The CV was significantly higher in the genotype‐guided cohort in all patients, CYP3A5 intermediate metabolizers, and CYP3A5 poor metabolizers at 14‐day and in all patients and CYP3A5 poor metabolizers at 30‐day post‐transplant (Table 5).

One patient in the weight‐based cohort received a non‐standard tacrolimus dose, while 28 patients in the genotype‐guided cohort received a clinician adjusted dose that did not correspond to the genotype‐guided dosing protocol and were excluded from the per‐protocol analysis, leaving 303 unique patients. The findings for the primary outcome were similar to the ITT analysis, with a significant reduction in the TSS for CYP3A5 normal metabolizers (Table 3). Discussion

This study provides real world evidence that CYP3A5 genotype‐guided dosing can improve early tacrolimus exposure in CYP3A5 normal and intermediate metabolizers, the patients at highest risk for early and potentially prolonged subtherapeutic tacrolimus exposure. Despite a very small sample size of CYP3A5 normal metabolizers, the time to achieve therapeutic tacrolimus exposure was significantly decreased in those who received genotype‐guided dosing. Although the tacrolimus dosing strategy for CYP3A5 poor metabolizers did not differ between the weight‐based and genotype‐guided cohorts, a higher proportion of CYP3A5 poor metabolizers in the genotype‐guided cohort experienced initial subtherapeutic tacrolimus concentrations.

The findings of this study on the primary outcome are consistent with previously published studies in adult kidney transplant that have evaluated genotype‐guided tacrolimus dosing, where time to therapeutic exposure and risk of initial subtherapeutic tacrolimus trough in CYP3A5 expressers (i.e., normal and intermediate metabolizers) was decreased. Two randomized clinical trials identified that the proportion of patients with initial therapeutic tacrolimus troughs significantly improved in the genotype‐guided group [6, 8]. Unlike these studies we did not observe a difference in these outcomes in the overall population; however, we did observe a lower risk of subtherapeutic exposure in the CYP3A5 normal and intermediate metabolizers, where tacrolimus dose adjustments were made. Additionally, CYP3A5 normal metabolizers receiving CYP3A5 genotype‐guided dosing in this study achieved therapeutic exposure approximately 1.5 weeks faster, which is consistent with the findings of Anutrakulchai et al. [8]. Overall differences in outcomes between this and prior studies may be partially due to differences in overall tacrolimus dosing strategies.

CYP3A5 normal and intermediate metabolizers may have a higher risk for a prolonged time below the therapeutic range and have been reported to have higher IPV, as defined by CV, in early tacrolimus trough concentrations compared to poor metabolizers [18]. Studies have linked early subtherapeutic tacrolimus concentrations to higher rejection risk [29, 30]. Although not all comparisons in this study reached statistical significance, the median time spent below therapeutic range was consistently lower in CYP3A5 normal and intermediate metabolizers who received the genotype‐guided dosing. Wolesensky et al. also determined that genotype‐guided dosing of extended release (LCP) tacrolimus significantly reduced the time when trough concentrations were within a concerning nontherapeutic range, defined as < 4 or > 12 ng/mL [22]. The finding that the genotype‐guided dosing strategy decreases the time below therapeutic range in CYP3A5 normal and intermediate metabolizers supports that CYP3A5 genotype‐guided dosing can identify patients at risk for and reduce the chance of the patient experiencing prolonged subtherapeutic exposure. Further study of the impact that genotype‐guided dosing has on clinical outcomes is warranted.

It is important to recognize that almost 50% of the CYP3A5 normal and intermediate metabolizers who received the genotype‐guided dosing in this study experienced a supratherapeutic (> 12 ng/mL) trough concentration on POD2. This indicates a need to further assess the genotype‐guided dosing strategy and determine characteristics that may contribute to supratherapeutic exposure. Heavier patient weight has been associated with higher tacrolimus exposure [31]. Post hoc we determined that the median weight (kg) for patients with supratherapeutic POD2 concentrations was higher than patients within or below therapeutic range (median (IQR): 88.0 kg (75.7–102.2) vs. 74.9 kg (65.4–90.5) vs. 71.0 kg (63.5–89.0); p < 0.001). Post‐transplant graft function also significantly impacts tacrolimus exposure, where patients with non‐IGF experienced higher exposure at the same tacrolimus dose [32, 33]. We also observed that the median doses at TSS for the evaluated cohorts were numerically lower than the designated genotype‐guided protocol dosing (Table 1). Overall, this suggests that a reduction in the genotype‐guided dosing and consideration of a maximum initial dose, especially for overweight patients, is warranted to minimize the risk of early supratherapeutic concentrations. The utilization of a “too‐high” initial dose may also have contributed to the lack of an association for some of the study outcomes, such as TSS or TTR. Although the strategy was successful at reducing subtherapeutic exposure in the most at‐risk patients, overshooting the target concentration likely resulted in the patients requiring additional monitoring and dose adjustments increasing the time to achieve the target range and increasing early IPV.

It was also unexpected that there were significant differences between the weight‐based and genotype‐guided cohorts when comparing only CYP3A5 poor metabolizers as the initial tacrolimus dose did not differ between these groups. Interestingly, Anutrakulchai et al. also observed an increase in the proportion of CYP3A5 poor metabolizers who experienced subtherapeutic tacrolimus troughs on POD3. In their study, the CYP3A5 poor metabolizers did receive a lower dose than their control group (0.08 mg/kg vs. 0.1 mg/kg). The exact reason for the difference in this population is less clear; there were significant differences in the demographics between the cohorts (Table 1), with the genotype‐guided cohort being older, more likely to have donation after brain death, and more likely to receive standard corticosteroids post‐transplant. These differences can be explained by the increased acceptance of marginal organs for transplants performed in the time period after we implemented the genotype‐guided dosing protocol. How this change in population demographics may contribute to the differences observed for CYP3A5 poor metabolizers is unclear.

This study is limited by a small sample size, particularly for the subgroup comparisons which may have limited the ability to detect differences between groups. The weight‐based cohort was obtained from a convenience sample of the institutional biorepository and may not be fully representative of all adult kidney transplant recipients. There were significant differences in the characteristics of the weight‐based and genotype‐guided dosing cohorts which may have introduced additional unintended confounding variables impacting tacrolimus exposure outcomes. These differences may be related to the biorepository recruitment or changes in the transplant center's acceptance of marginal organs, which occurred at the same time as the genotype‐guided dosing protocol implementation. This study did not consider other known sources of tacrolimus exposure variability, such as hematocrit changes in the early postoperative period and the CYP3A4*22 variant, which have been associated with decreased tacrolimus clearance [9, 34, 35]. Additionally, this study did not evaluate clinical outcomes such as rejection, graft loss, or survival or economic outcomes associated with tacrolimus therapeutic drug monitoring, both of which could provide additional evidence for the utility of genotype‐guided dosing.

Conclusions

A CYP3A5 genotype‐guided tacrolimus dosing strategy reduced the rate of early subtherapeutic tacrolimus concentrations for CYP3A5 normal and intermediate metabolizers and shortened time to TSS in CYP3A5 normal metabolizers. However, observed dose requirements at TSS were lower than the protocol‐based dosing. Reducing the initial dose for all CYP3A5 phenotypes and potential consideration for other non‐genetic characteristics, such as graft function, may help to decrease the risk of early supratherapeutic trough concentrations, which could further improve TSS and TTR in CYP3A5 normal and intermediate metabolizers.
