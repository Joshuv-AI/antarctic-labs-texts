Synthesis of hydrazine-functionalized Polyrotaxane and formation of adaptable sliding hydrogels
Authors: Sarah J. Loveland, Xinming Tong, Hung-Pang Lee, Christine L. Zhou, Fan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work details the synthesis and characterization of Polyrotaxane-Hydrazine (PR-Hyd), a polyethylene glycol-based polymer utilized in the formation of Adaptable Sliding Hydrogels (ASG). ASG is a viscoelastic 3D cell culture system with reversible hydrazone bonds that can be leveraged to present cells with tunable stress relaxation and mechanical plasticity. ASG builds upon our lab’s previously reported Sliding Hydrogel (SG) platform which is formed via irreversible covalent crosslinking between norbornene functionalized Polyrotaxane (PR-NB) and a dithiol crosslinker. To generate PR-Hyd, we adapted and optimized the synthesis protocol for PR-NB and used proton NMR spectroscopy to quantify polymer functionality and reduce batch-to-batch variability. In addition, this work outlines our protocol for facile ASG scaffold formation using aldehyde functionalized polyethylene glycol macromers for crosslinking. Taken together, these methods enable reproducible synthesis of PR-Hyd and the fabrication of reversibly crosslinked ASG scaffolds that can be leveraged as a 3D cell niche for applications including regenerative medicine and in vitro disease modeling.•Synthesis method for Polyrotaxane-Hydrazine, a polyethylene glycol-based polymer precursor designed for forming Adaptable Sliding Hydrogels (ASG).•Characterization of polymer functionality via proton NMR spectroscopy.•Reproducible fabrication of ASG hydrogels.

Background

The Adaptable Sliding Hydrogel (ASG) is a novel biomaterial scaffold designed to probe interactions between local hydrogel mechanics and cell behavior [1]. Distinct from conventional static hydrogels, ASG integrates two dynamic polymer architectures: sliding rings and reversible crosslinks. Both features can independently enhance local matrix reorganizability and facilitate nascent protein deposition, local remodeling, and mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) differentiation [[2], [3], [4]].

Polyrotaxane backbone dynthesis

The foundation of the ASG platform is a Polyrotaxane (PR) backbone composed of mechanically interlocked macrocyclic rings thread along a linear polymer chain [5,6]. Much like beads on a necklace, these rings slide dynamically along the polymer backbone, and bulky end-capping groups are used to prevent rings from unthreading. While PR can be generated from a range of different linear backbones such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), polyurethane, or polyamide and ring structures including cyclodextrins (CDs), cucurbiturils, or crown ethers, the increased solubility and facile assembly of PEG and α-CD based PR make it the most common choice for biological applications [[7], [8], [9]]. PR generated from PEG and α-CD also displays excellent biocompatibility, resists cell-based degradation, and contains functional group handles for crosslinking motif incorporation. Once end-capped, PR can be modified to improve aqueous solubility and to add functional groups that incorporate biochemical ligands or facilitate interchain crosslinking [2]. Specifically, we demonstrated that alkyne handles and hydroxypropyl groups can be conjugated onto the α-CD rings to disrupt crystalline packing and enable downstream click chemistry [10,11].

Evolution from permanent to adaptable covalent crosslinks

Previous work from our lab on sliding hydrogels (SG) utilized copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) click chemistry to add norbornene functional groups to alkyne-functionalized α-CD rings thread on PEG chains (PR-Alkyne) to form PR-Norbornene (PR-NB) [10,11]. PR-NB was subsequently crosslinked via thiol-ene click reactions with linear PEG-dithiols. The resulting SG provided cells with a more permissible local microenvironment when compared to conventional static hydrogels by facilitating cell-mediated local niche reorganization and cytoskeletal network reorganization [11]. To further enhance network mobility and permissibility, we sought to replace the irreversible covalent crosslinks of SG with reversible hydrazone bonds.

By grafting hydrazine onto the PR-alkyne backbone, we obtained PR-Hyd which can be crosslinked with aldehyde-functionalized macromers. The resulting hydrogels exhibit time- and strain-dependent behaviors including stress relaxation and irreversible plasticity which are characteristic properties of viscoelastic hydrogel systems such as collagen, alginate and reversibly crosslinked hyaluronic acid hydrogels [4,[12], [13], [14], [15]].

In the ASG system, the chemical structure of the aldehyde crosslinker can be tuned to modulate bond kinetics and to modulate bulk hydrogel mechanical properties such as stress relaxation and plasticity [3,16]. ASG serves as a versatile and unique 3D cell niche for probing the effects of sliding rings and tunable bond kinetics on cell fate. In this work, we provide detailed step-by-step methods for the synthesis and characterization of PR-Hyd, enabling reproducible fabrication of hydrazone-based hydrogels for 3D cell culture with highly tunable mechanical properties.

Oxidation of 35kDa-PEG to 35kDa-PEG-COOH (Fig. 1A)

First, 20 g of 35kDa-PEG was dissolved in 200 mL DI water. In 1 mL acetonitrile, 150 mg TEMPO was dissolved and added to the PEG solution. 150 mg of NaBr was then added and fully dissolved. Next, 30 mL of NaClO was added to the mixture, and the reaction was left stirring for 30 minutes at room temperature (RT).

10 mL of ethanol was added to quench the oxidation reaction, and the pH was adjusted to 1 using concentrated HCl. The combined organic phase was dried with Na2SO4 and concentrated using a rotary evaporator. Polyrotaxane (PR) (Fig. 1E)

24 g of lyophilized PPR was added to a large beaker and broken apart until light and fluffy (Fig. 2D). In a separate flask, 4 g of 1-adamantanamine hydrochloride was dissolved in 120 mL DMF. Next, 3 mL of DIPEA was added and stirred until almost completely dissolved. Finally, 10 g of PyBOP was added and stirred until dissolved. The solution was then poured over the PPR solid, and a large stir bar was added. The resulting mixture should be thick (will not be flowable) and was packed down into the bottom of the flask and stirring was set to the slowest speed possible overnight (Fig. 2E). The stir bar should begin to move in the solution overnight. If the mixture is still very dry the next day, around 4-10 mL of DMF can be added, the solid can be patted down gently, and the reaction can be left again overnight.

The solution in this case was found to become thin and flowable but not completely clear, and the PR product was then collected as follows (Fig. 2F). First, the suspension was poured into a 4-5x volume of acetonitrile to precipitate the PR product. This mixture was aliquoted into 50 mL Falcon tubes and centrifuged at 600g for 3 minutes. The white solid settled at the bottom of the tube, and the supernatant was poured off the top. The solid was then mixed with hot (∼80-90°C) 200 mL DI water (Fig. 2G). If the polymer mixture fully dissolves in the hot water, the endcapping reaction was unsuccessful: PR is insoluble in water and can be collected as a solid via centrifugation while PEG and free α-CD will dissolve during washing. The washing step was repeated two more times, and the solid was frozen and lyophilized (Fig. 2H). Validation was conducted using 1H-NMR spectroscopy in DMSO (Fig. 1F-G). 1H-NMR spectroscopy was also used to quantify α-CD ring coverage along the PEG backbone, as detailed in the method validation section. Under these reaction conditions, the final PR-Hydrazine (PR-Hyd) product had a ring coverage of approximately 8-10%. Of note, ring coverage after initial PR assembly was typically higher at 15-20%, as quantified by 1H-NMR. The decrease in coverage is likely due to residual free α-CD rings that were not fully removed during water washing.

Step-by-step methods

PR-Alkyne

5 g of PR were dissolved in 250 mL of 1M NaOH and cooled on ice. 2.5 mL of glycidyl propargyl ether were added to the solution and stirred for at least 3 hours until the solution became clear. The ice bath was refreshed, the solution was once again cooled, and 1.25 mL of glycidyl propargyl ether were added and left overnight to react. The next day, the solution was cooled on ice before 100 mL cold propylene oxide were added and left to react for at least 3 hours. The solution was once more cooled, and an additional 100 mL cold propylene oxide were added and left to react overnight.

The solution was then neutralized and dialyzed against DI water in 12-14 kDa molecular weight cutoff dialysis tubing then frozen and lyophilized. Validation was conducted via 1H-NMR spectroscopy in D2O (Fig. 3C).

Synthesis of PR-Hydrazine (Fig. 4A-B)

PR-Hydrazine (PR-Hyd) is a novel advancement to the sliding hydrogel polymer that allows for reversible crosslinking. To functionalize PR-Alkyne, copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) click chemistry was used to attach a tri-boc-hydrazine-azidopropyl amide bifunctional small molecule. After deprotection of the boc-protected PR-Hyd, the final polymer is characterized and can be used for hydrogel formation.

Step-by-step methods

Tri-boc-hydrazine-azidopropyl amide

Together, 2 g of tri-boc-hydrazinoacetic acid and 1.34 g of 3-azido-1-propanamine were combined in 100 mL DCM. Next, 2.93 g of EDC HCl were added before 0.065 g of DMAP. The solution was left stirring overnight. To purify the product, the solution is washed with 100 mL of 0.1 N HCl twice and with 100 mL of brine once. After drying with Na2SO4, the solvent was evaporated and a short silica gel column with 1:1 EtOAc:hexanes was used to purify the product.

Boc-protected PR-Hyd

First, 1 g PR-alkyne was dissolved in 18 mL of a 1:1 mixture of MeOH and DI water. In 1 mL of MeOH, 0.47 g of tri-boc-hydrazine-azidopropyl amide were dissolved and subsequently added to the PR-alkyne solution. 1.5 mL of a 10 wt% sodium ascorbate solution in DI water and 50 µL of a 10 wt% stock solution of CuSO4 in PMDETA (0.48 M) were also added to the reaction mixture and stirred at R.T. overnight. The next day, 1 mL of EDTA (1 M) was added and stirred for 5 minutes. The resulting solution was then dialyzed against DI water for at least 24 hours. PR-Hyd

In DI water, 1 g of boc-protected PR-Hyd, 1 mL of EDTA (1M), and 6 mL of 12 N HCl were combined and stirred at 40°C for at least 30 minutes. The polymer became soluble as deprotection occurred. When the solution was clear and all polymer had dissolved, the reaction was stopped. The solution’s pH was brought to 5-6 with HCl before dialysis against water for at least 24 hours. After freezing and vacuum drying, the product was validated via 1H-NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 4D, G). A ratio of 0.47 g of tri-boc-hydrazine-azidopropyl amide to 1 g of PR-alkyne with a functionality of 1-2 alkyne per α-CD ring and 8-10% coverage yielded PR-Hyd with approximately 0.5 hydrazine per ring, as quantified using 1H-NMR spectroscopy.

Formation of PR-Hyd-based hydrogels

Using 1H-NMR spectroscopy, the functionality of PR-Hyd can be quantified as the weight of polymer per one mol of hydrazine functional groups. The calculations are summarized in Table 1. To form hydrogels, 10kDa-8-arm-PEG aliphatic aldehyde (AA) and benzaldehyde (BA) are used as crosslinkers. As outlined in previous work, tuning the ratio of AA:BA crosslinks allows for control over the stress relaxation and plasticity of the resulting hydrogels [1,3,16]. AA-based crosslinks are more dynamic than BA-based crosslinks and result in faster stress relaxing hydrogels due to more rapid molecular reorganization under applied stress. For gel formulation, the hydrazine:aldehyde ratio was set to a 1:1 molar ratio to ensure stable network formation. In our work, 10kDa-8-arm-PEG-aliphatic aldehyde (8-arm-PEG-AA) and 10kDa-8-arm-PEG-benzaldehyde (8-arm-PEG-BA) crosslinkers were used and synthesized as previously described [3,18]. 8-arm-PEG-AA synthesis [18]

To a solution of 100 mL DCM, 5 g 8-arm 10kDa PEG, 3.87 g DAIB (3 eq), and 0.125 g TEMPO (0.2 eq) were added. This reaction was stirred overnight at room temperature. The solution was then added dropwise into cooled diethyl ether, and the resulting precipitate was collected, dissolved in deionized water, and dialyzed against DI water for 2 days.

8-arm-PEG-BA synthesis [3]

In 100 mL DCM, 5 g of 8-arm 10kDa PEG was dissolved and combined with 1.2 g 4-formylbenzoic acid (2 eq), 0.147 g DMAP (0.3 eq), and 1.5 g DIC (3 eq). The reaction was left stirring overnight before the product was precipitated in cooled diethyl ether and dialyzed against deionized water for 2 days.

B) Hydrogels are formed by pipetting an 8-arm-PEG-aldehyde solution onto a Teflon-coated glass slide and adding 2 spacers that are 1 mm (stacked height is 2 mm) on each side (step 1).
