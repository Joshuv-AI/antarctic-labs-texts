New genotyping method for Escherichia coli using RSXC repeat polymorphism and gene-neighborhood architecture
Authors: Ravi Jothi, Kyung Min Park, Soobin Yim, Kwang-Kyo Oh, Injun Hwang, Myeong-In Jeong, Dong Suk Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReliable genotyping tools are essential for understanding genomic diversity and tracking strain variation in Escherichia coli. Traditional typing approaches, including SNP and MLST based methods, focus mainly on conserved regions of the genome and may overlook potential changes in the unconserved gene content. To address this gap, this study proposed a new genotyping approach that combines diversity profiles observed from repeat-rich protein sequence and genomic neighborhood structural organization.ResultsComparative genomic analysis was conducted on 83 publicly available E. coli genomes. Sequence comparison identified a repeat hotspot within the RSXC protein, while gene neighborhood (synteny) analysis revealed three variable genomic regions (hotspots A–C). Differences in RSXC repeat copy number and terminal motifs allowed the strains to be classified into seven repeat types (R7a, R7b, R6a, R6b, R5a, R5b, and R4a). Structural variation within genomic hotspots A, B, and C was characterized by the presence, absence, and arrangement of intermediate genes and classified into 7, 8, and 4 structure types, respectively. Integration of RSXC repeat profiles with genomic hotspot patterns generated 14 distinct sequence types (ST1–ST14). RSXC repeat patterns and the combined genotyping scheme both showed good discriminatory power (Simpson’s diversity index of 0.726), which is comparable to MLST typing (0.723) among the 83 E. coli strains analysed. Virulence and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profiling of the examined strains further supported biological relevance of the proposed method. Strains with higher RSXC repeat numbers (R7 and R6) positively linked with virulence genes presence and negatively associated with AMR gene counts as supported by a significant positive correlation with virulence genes (ρ = 0.323, p = 0.0026) and a negative correlation with AMR genes (ρ = −0.276, p = 0.0105).ConclusionsThis repeat–synteny based genotyping approach captures genomic diversity in the 83 E. coli strains that is often overlooked by conventional typing approaches and provides a practical framework for discriminating E. coli strains. The strong association with MLST, combined with meaningful relationships to virulence and AMR profiles, suggests that this approach may serve as a useful marker for epidemiological identifications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12847-z.Keywords: E. coli, Amino acid repeats (AAR), Repeat hotspots, Genotyping, MLST, pathogen surveillance

Introduction

Escherichia coli is a Gram-negative bacterium that normally inhabits the human intestinal tract as part of the normal microbiota. Although most strains exist harmlessly as commensals, others have evolved into significant human pathogens capable of causing a wide range of illnesses, from diarrheal disease to urinary tract and systemic infections [1, 2]. Pathogenic strains are broadly categorized into intestinal pathogenic E. coli (IPEC) and extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli (ExPEC) [3]. IPEC includes several well-defined pathotypes such as enteroaggregative, enterohemorrhagic, enteroinvasive, enteropathogenic, enterotoxigenic, diffusely adherent, and adherent-invasive strains, whereas ExPEC includes uropathogenic, neonatal meningitis-associated, septicemia-associated, and avian pathogenic variants [4, 5].

The worldwide rise in antimicrobial resistance E. coli strains, particularly extraintestinal isolates, underscores effective strain typing methods for outbreak investigation, epidemiological tracking, and guiding infection control [6, 7]. However, the high genomic plasticity of E. coli caused by frequent acquisition of virulence and resistance genes, makes strain typing challenging.

Over the years, a variety of genotyping strategies have been developed. Classical methods such as pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), multilocus sequence typing (MLST), and rep-PCR provided high discriminatory power for studying population structure and tracking outbreaks [8, 9]. However, they are labor-intensive, difficult to standardize, and not well suited for large-scale routine use. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) now represents the most comprehensive strategy for strain comparison and has become increasingly accessible [10, 11]. With the increasing availability of WGS, it is now possible to examine additional layers of diversity, such as repeat polymorphisms and rearrangements within specific genomic regions that may provide insight into strain typing and adaptive processes. In this study, we comparative analysis the RSXC repeat sequence and gene-neighborhood organization pattern across three genomic hotspots to investigate structural diversity among E. coli genomes. We found out variations in the copy number and motif composition of RSXC repeats and gene organization within genomic hotspots A–C, and integrated these features to develop a composite genotyping framework. The resulting profiles were then compared with MLST and evaluated in relation to virulence and antimicrobial resistance gene content. By linking repeat polymorphism, structural variation, this work provides a complementary perspective on E. coli genome evolution and offers a practical approach for improving strain typing beyond conventional methods.

Materials and methods

Genome collection and curation

A total of 83 complete E. coli genomes were obtained from the NCBI Genome database. Strain identity was verified by cross-checking NCBI taxonomy and ANI in the Assembly records. When multiple assemblies existed for a strain, we used the RefSeq representative or the highest-quality GenBank entry. MLST profiles (7 housekeeping genes) were available for 83 strains and used for concordance analyses.

Target genes and sequence retrieval

Protein coding sequences were extracted from annotated proteomes. Multiple sequence alignments were run with MUSCLE (MegAlign Pro v15.1.0, DNASTAR). The collected sequences were analyzed to mine the target sequence, particularly those exhibiting more than five amino acid variations per gene through comparative analysis [12, 13].

Genomic neighborhood analysis

Comparative genomic analysis was performed to identify variable genomic regions flanked by conserved boundary genes. The protein data from the 83 E. coli strains imported into excel and converted the gene regions into their corresponding protein sizes. Following this, a synteny analysis was performed. Specifically, we investigated regions where the amino acid sequence length differed by 30 or more, even among homologous genes. Gene organization, including gene presence or absence, orientation, and insertion/deletion events, was compared across all genomes.

Development of combined genotyping panel

To create an integrated typing scheme, RSXC repeat groups were combined with genomic hotspot patterns using the format: RSXC – Hotspot A – Hotspot B – Hotspot C types. Each unique combination was assigned a Sequence Type (ST), resulting in 14 final genotypes (ST1–ST14). Grouping of individual strains to each ST is provided in Tables 2 and 3.

Simpson’s index of diversity analysis

The discriminatory power of the proposed typing method was analysed by Simpson’s index of diversity. The analysis was performed based on the distribution of the RSXC sequence types (ST1–ST14) and MLST numbers across the 83 E. coli strains. Simpson’s diversity index was calculated in R software (version 4.5.2) using the vegan package.

Detection of virulence genes and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) determinants

Virulence-associated genes of analyzed strains were detected using the AMRFinder databases in NCBI. Virulence and AMR gene counts were summarized for each RSXC group and ST to assess biological relevance of the typing panel. To statistically evaluate the relationship between RSXC repeat numbers and gene content, Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed. Correlation coefficients (ρ) and corresponding p-values were calculated, and statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R software.

Results

RSXC repeat region shows high repeat polymorphism

Comparative analysis of the protein sequence revealed amino acid repeat variation in RSXC protein. The repeat consisted of 32 amino acid units, but with notable variation observed in the copy numbers and terminal motifs across the 83 strains examined. Although each repeat starts with conserved DPRK motif, end motif varied among strains i.e., QQV, EPV, EQV, ETV, EQI, EKV across strains. A representative repeat unit is shown in Fig. 1A.

RSXC repeat profiles in the examined 83 E. coli strains. A Representative RSXC repeat sequence showing conserved and variable regions. B Schematic of repeat organization across strains. Strains were grouped into R7a–R4a based on repeat number and end-motif variation.

On the basis of repeat copy number and end motif composition, strains were grouped into seven repeat categories (R7a, R7b, R6a, R6b, R5a, R5b, and R4a). The detailed classification rules is described in the (Fig. 1B). The majority of strains carried six or seven repeat number, whereas fewer strains possessed five or four repeat numbers. These findings indicate that the RSXC region represents a repeat hotspot that contributes to structural diversity among the 83 examined E. coli strains.

Three genomic hotspot regions exhibit distinct gene-organization patterns

Gene neighborhood analysis identified three varied genomic regions (hotspots A–C) flanked by conserved boundary genes. Although the boundary genes remained stable, intermediate regions showed noticeable structural variation, including gene gain/loss, insertion events, and rearrangements. Based on these patterns of gene organization, strains were further grouped into different structural categories. Among the 83 genomes analyzed, hotspot A showed seven structural categories (A1–A7), hotspot B eight (B1–B8), and hotspot C four (C1–C4).

Hotspot A (glp–glg region) organization

The hotspot A located between glp and glg boundary genes. Across the analyzed strains, the glp/glg locus showed a predominantly conserved organization, despite variation observed in the number and orientation of neighboring genes. All the examined strains (hotspot A) consisted of glpE–glpD at the upstream end, followed by a series of conserved hypothetical genes, and terminating with the glycogen biosynthesis region comprising glpP and glgA (Fig. 2). Variation in gene arrangement allowed the strains to be divided into seven structural groups (A1–A7), with representative maps shown in Fig. 2.

Gene organization of Hotspot A (glp–glg region) across the 83 E. coli strains. Strains were grouped into structural types A1–A7 based on differences in gene arrangement within the region between glp and glg boundary genes. Conserved genes are shown with consistent colors and arrows indicate gene orientation

Among these, the A1 and A3 were most common, whereas the remaining pattern (A2–A7) contained only one or two strains each and represented rare variants characterized by insertions, deletions, or rearrangements within the region separating glpD from the downstream glp/glg genes. Notably, in the A3 pattern group, glgC appeared downstream of glgA, suggesting an extended glycogen operon. Despite these structural differences, the downstream cluster containing yzgL–glpP–glgA remained intact across all the analyzed strains. An occasional gene replacement of glpG was observed in A7 group, without disrupting of core metabolic structure. Together, these findings indicate that the strain having hotspot A retains a highly conserved glp/glg core, while diversification in the surrounding genomic region likely reflects recombination and horizontal gene transfer. Additional information on hotspot A is available in Supplementary File 1.

Hotspot B (elaB to nuoK region) organization

The hotspot B located between the elaB and nuoK boundary genes. The most of strains displayed a characteristic arrangement composed of upstream elaB–elaA–rbn–elaD, followed by members of the yfb cluster and downstream with NADH dehydrogenase subunits (nuoN–nuoM–nuoL–nuoK) (Fig. 3). Based on differences in center regions, strains were subdivided into 8 structural groups, designated B1 to B8. Representative architectures are shown in Fig. 3.

Gene organization of Hotspot B (elaB–nuoK region) across the 83 E. coli strains. Strains were grouped into structural types B1–B8 based on variation in gene content and arrangement between the elaB and nuoK boundary genes. Conserved genes are shown in the same colors across panels, and arrow direction indicates gene orientation

The B1 pattern was the most frequent and retained complete ela region together with multiple yfb genes. Subsequent groups (B2–B8) showcasing intact ela region, but varied center region mainly due to deletion or rearrangement of individual yfb genes. Despite these differences, at least one yfb gene and the downstream nuo cluster were preserved across all the analyzed strains, indicating that the hotspot B maintains a conserved functional core while undergoing localized modifications.

Hotspot C (trmH to yicH region) organization

The hotspot C was located between the trmH and yicH locus. It divided into 4 structural types C1 to C4. The C1 was the most widespread structural organization among the 83 examined strains. This arrangement began with trmH, followed by recG, and a variable intervening gene and terminated with the gltS, yicE, and yicH cluster (Fig. 4). The remaining structures (C2 to C4) exhibited only minor deviations between recG and gltS. Despite these variations, the downstream gltS–yicE–yicH cluster was remained intact in all strains. Overall, hotspot C reflects a highly stable structure with limited recombination compared with the other hotspots analyzed in this study.

Integration of RSXC repeat and hotspot structure profiles defines 14 sequence types

To create integrated typing scheme, RSXC patterns were combined with hotspot A–C structural types (Table 2). Based on these combined features, 83 strains were grouped into fourteen unique genotypes and designated ST1–ST14 (Table 3). Several sequence types contained multiple strains, whereas others were represented by single or few isolates. The grouping of genotype combinations to ST assignments is presented in Tables 2 and 3.

Strong agreement between the genotyping panel and MLST

Comparison of the newly developed genotyping scheme with MLST showed strong overall concordance between RSXC repeat profiles alone and in combination with genomic hotspot patterns. Multiple ST categories clustered consistently within the same MLST clades, suggesting that the developed scheme captures evolutionary relationships similar to MLST (Table 2). Notably, the discriminatory power of the proposed RSXC–hotspot typing method was evaluated using Simpson’s diversity index. The RSXC-based classification (D = 0.726) exhibited a discriminatory capacity comparable to MLST typing (D = 0.723), indicating that repeat–hotspot structural variation provides a resolution similar to that of MLST for the analyzed E. coli strains.

Association between genotyping patterns, virulence, and AMR genes profiles

Screening for virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes of strains demonstrated biologically meaningful associations with the presented genotyping scheme. Strains carrying higher RSXC repeat numbers (R7 and R6), corresponding to ST1–ST10, generally carried larger sets of virulence-associated genes. Most of these isolates belonged to the EHEC pathotype (stx⁺/eae⁺), while only one strain was classified as EPEC (eae⁺ stx⁻) (Table 4). Interestingly, ST1–ST7 possessed the stx2 operon together with eae (γ), whereas ST8–ST10 carried the stx1 (a) operon and eae (β). These groups also showed consistent enrichment of the AMR genes mdtM, blaEC, and acrF (Table 4). By contrast, strains with lower repeat counts (R5 and R4), corresponding to ST11–ST14, lacked major virulence determinants and instead resembled commensal pathotypes. These isolates possessed broader AMR genes, often exceeding 15 genes per genome. Notably, mdtM, blaEC, and acrF were detected across nearly all strains examined. This was confirmed by spearman rank correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between RSXC repeat numbers and virulence gene counts (ρ = 0.323, p = 0.0026) and a negative correlation with AMR gene counts (ρ = −0.276, p = 0.0105) (Table 5). Notably, the gene patterns represent observation rather than definitive biological differences as the plasmid and chromosomal locations of AMR genes were not distinguished. These findings suggest that RSXC repeat expansion may be linked more strongly to virulence potential, whereas reduced repeat copy number may be associated with resistance-adapted backgrounds.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated genomic diversity in E. coli using a combined framework based on RSXC repeat polymorphism and three genomic hotspots organization patterns across 83 E. coli strains. Together, these analyses revealed substantial structural diversity, providing insights into both evolutionary dynamics and strain typing of E. coli. In this study, RSXC gene was selected to investigate its structural variation in E. coli based on its prior identification and characterization within the Enterobacteriaceae family in our earlier studies [14, 15]. Analysis of the RSXC protein showed clear repeat polymorphism driven primarily by a conserved 32-amino-acid motif (Fig. 1). Although the structural core of the repeat was maintained, copy number and terminal sequence differences produced seven distinct repeat groups. Most strains carried six or seven repeats copy numbers, suggesting that longer repeat arrays are more common, while the shorter versions likely formed later by losing repeats (Fig. 1). So, this typing scheme may contribute for typing of strains that has been recently get changed or evolved. In parallel, investigation of three genomic hotspots demonstrated that structural rearrangements occur in predictable patterns rather than randomly across the genome. Each hotspot was anchored by conserved flanking genes, while the intermediate segments displayed variable gene content. In hotspot A, the glp/glg metabolic region remained intact despite substantial reorganization around it, indicating strong selective pressure to retain carbohydrate metabolizing functions (Fig. 2). Hotspot B exhibited a similar pattern, with the ela module forming a stable backbone and the yfb region undergoing progressive gene gain and loss (Fig. 3). Hotspot C appeared more stable overall, with only minor differences between recG and gltS, suggesting that some loci experience greater structural constraints than others (Fig. 4).

When RSXC repeat types were combined with genomic hotspot structural categories, fourteen distinct sequence types (ST1 to ST14) were identified (Tables 2 and 3). This integrated scheme resolved both dominant clonal groups and small variant clusters, demonstrating an ability to discriminate strains with greater precision than either repeat typing or hotspot analysis alone.

The high degree of agreement with MLST indicates that the approach reflects underlying evolutionary relationships (Table 2), while also offering additional structural information not captured by traditional housekeeping-gene typing.

Biological patterns associated with the genotyping profiles were also evident. Strains carrying higher RSXC repeat copy numbers tended to harbor more virulence-associated genes (Table 4). In contrast, strains with fewer repeats copy numbers frequently carried complex of antimicrobial resistance determinants (> 15 genes), including multiple efflux transporters and β-lactam resistance genes. These associations suggest that RSXC repeat pattern and hotspot remodeling may accompany ecological specialization toward either virulence-driven or resistance-adapted lifestyles.

Conclusions

Taken together, our findings highlight RSXC repeats and hotspot gene architecture as dynamic genomic features that can serve as descriptive marker for differentiating the 83 analyzed E. coli strains. Integrating these markers yields a robust and discriminatory typing framework that complements MLST and provides additional genomic insights into variation. Functional effects of RSXC repeat differences and hotspot variation were not experimentally validated, and broader sampling may reveal additional structural patterns. Nevertheless, the findings provide a repeat and genomic structure based framework for further comparative genomic and epidemiological studies of E. coli. Future studies incorporating gene expression assays, mutational experiments, and epidemiological datasets will be valuable to clarify how RSXC repeats and hotspot rearrangements shape adaptation and pathogenic potential of E. coli.
