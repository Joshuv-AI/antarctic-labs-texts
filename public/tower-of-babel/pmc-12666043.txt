Pregnant women’s knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding hypothyroidism during pregnancy: a cross-sectional study at the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University
Authors: Yajie Gan, Qin Wan, Hairui Lin, Yueyang Zhang, Xue Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study examined the knowledge, attitudes and practices of pregnant women regarding hypothyroidism during pregnancy.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingConducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University, Luzhou, Sichuan Province, China, between 10 March 2024 and 15 May 2024.ParticipantsData were obtained from 411 valid questionnaires (valid response rate: 74.46%). The average age of participants was 31.43±5.42 years.InterventionsData were collected using a specifically designed knowledge, attitudes and practices questionnaire.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe median (IQR) scores for knowledge, attitude and practice were 14 (10, 17), 30 (23, 35) and 41 (33, 47), respectively. Awareness of thyroid hormone replacement safety during pregnancy, postpartum hypothyroidism risks and potential intellectual development issues in newborns was less than 50%.ResultsMultivariable logistic regression indicated that knowledge score (OR=1.068, p=0.008), attitude score (OR=1.075, p<0.001) and having a high school/vocational education (OR=0.406, p=0.025) were independently associated with practice. Structural equation modelling revealed significant direct effects of knowledge on attitude (β=0.416, p<0.001) and practice (β=0.296, p<0.001), as well as attitude on practice (β=0.483, p<0.001).ConclusionThe study finds that pregnant women generally possess good knowledge, positive attitudes and proactive practices regarding hypothyroidism. It emphasises the need for healthcare providers to implement educational interventions to improve understanding and health outcomes for pregnant women with hypothyroidism.

Introduction

Hypothyroidism is the second most prevalent condition during pregnancy after diabetes mellitus, affecting 3%– 5% of pregnant women. This immune-related dysfunction manifests in various forms, including overt hypothyroidism, subclinical hypothyroidism and isolated hypothyroxinaemia.1 2 Hypothyroidism is linked with multiple adverse outcomes for both the mother and foetus. Pregnant women with this condition, including those with subclinical types, face a heightened risk of complications such as recurrent pregnancy loss, neonatal death, pre-eclampsia, placental abruption, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, low birth weight, preterm birth, foetal distress, intrauterine foetal demise and impaired intellectual function of the newborn.3 4 Consequently, it is imperative that women with hypothyroidism undergo thyroid hormone replacement therapy during pregnancy to mitigate these risks.5

The knowledge, attitudes and practices survey is a critical research tool used predominantly in health literacy to evaluate the understanding, beliefs and actions of individuals related to a specific health topic. It operates on the premise that enhanced knowledge positively impacts attitudes, thereby shaping behaviours.68 Recent findings from a study in the USA highlight a significant communication gap, with nearly half of surveyed pregnant women with hypothyroidism reporting that healthcare providers did not adequately explain the risks associated with uncontrolled hypothyroidism or the benefits of its proper management.9

Given this backdrop, the current study seeks to investigate the knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding hypothyroidism during pregnancy among Chinese pregnant women—a group previously unstudied in this context. Therefore, this study aimed to (1) assess the knowledge, attitudes and practices of pregnant women regarding hypothyroidism during pregnancy, and (2) determine the associations among knowledge, attitudes and practices using statistical and structural equation modelling (SEM) analyses. These findings will help identify key areas for improvement and inform the design of targeted educational interventions for pregnant women.

Methods

Study design and subjects

This cross-sectional study was carried out from 10 March 2024 to 15 May 2024 at the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University, Luzhou, Sichuan Province, China, and included a cohort of pregnant women. The hospital is a tertiary general hospital and clinical teaching centre directly administered by the Sichuan Provincial Health Commission. The Department of Endocrinology, where this study was implemented, has long operated a specialised clinic for endocrine disorders during pregnancy and has extensive experience in the diagnosis and management of gestational thyroid diseases. In addition, the hospital’s Department of Obstetrics handles over 110,000 outpatient visits and more than 4500 inpatients annually, providing a solid and reliable source of eligible pregnant participants for this study. Approval for the study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (Approval No. KY2024110). Verbal consent was obtained prior to questionnaire distribution, with electronic informed consent embedded on the first page, which required an affirmative selection to proceed with data collection.

Questionnaire distribution

The online questionnaire was developed through the Wenjuanxing WeChat mini-programme, which also generated the corresponding QR code for access. Participants were recruited by displaying these QR codes in obstetrics and endocrinology clinics. Through face-to-face interactions with patients, we encouraged them to scan the QR code and proceed to complete the questionnaire. Participants could access and complete the questionnaire simply by scanning the provided QR code. To prevent duplicate submissions, each IP address was limited to one response. The research team carefully reviewed each questionnaire to ensure its completeness, internal consistency and logical coherence.

Questionnaire introduction

The questionnaire was developed based on relevant guidelines and literature and refined through feedback from three experts in endocrinology and metabolism. After an initial draft, a pilot test involving 34 respondents was conducted. The feedback from this pre-test yielded a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.922 for the overall scale, with the knowledge dimension scoring 0.836, the attitude dimension 0.863 and the practice dimension 0.898. The final version of the questionnaire comprised four sections: demographic information (including age, education level, permanent residence, family income, gestational weeks, etc), and separate sections for knowledge, attitude and practice dimensions (online supplemental appendix).

The knowledge section consisted of 10 questions addressing the risks of hypothyroidism and its treatment and prevention. Scoring was as follows: 2 points for each correct answer, 1 point for unsure responses and 0 points for incorrect answers, resulting in a total score range of 0–20 points. The attitude section included eight questions, using a 5-point Likert scale from very positive (5 points) to very negative (1 point), leading to a total score range of 8–40 points. Higher scores indicated a more positive attitude towards the prevention and treatment of hypothyroidism. The practice section contained 11 questions, also using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from always (5 points) to never (1 point), with a total score range of 11–55 points. Higher scores reflected more proactive practices in preventing and managing hypothyroidism. Adequate knowledge, positive attitudes and proactive practices were defined as scores exceeding 70% of the maximum possible score in each respective dimension.10

Sample size calculation

The calculation of the sample size was conducted as follows:

N = (Z_(1−α/2)/δ)2×p×(1−p)

where N denoted the sample size, and p was assumed to be 0.5 to ensure the maximum sample size. The type I error (α) was set to 0.05. In this case, Z_(1−α/2)=1.96. The SE (δ) was assumed to be 0.05. The final target is to collect at least 387 completed valid questionnaires.

Statistical methods

Descriptive analysis was applied to the demographic information of respondents and the scores for each dimension. The normality of data distribution for each dimension was first tested. Normally distributed data were represented by means and SD, whereas non-normally distributed data were presented using the median and the 25th and 75th percentiles. Categorical data from responses to different questions were expressed as n (%). For comparisons of scores across different dimensions among subjects with varying demographic characteristics, the t-test was used for two groups with normally distributed continuous variables. For non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney test was used. In cases involving three or more groups, analysis of variance was used for normally distributed data with uniform variance, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to non-normally distributed data. Correlation analysis of the scores for each dimension was performed using Pearson correlation coefficients for normally distributed data and Spearman correlation coefficients for non-normally distributed data. In this study, as the knowledge, attitude and practice dimension scores were not normally distributed, Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships among knowledge, attitude and practice scores. Univariate and multivariable regression analyses were carried out with the practice dimension score as the dependent variable to explore the relationships between demographic information and practice dimension scores. Scores were classified according to 70% of the maximum possible score for each dimension. Participants with scores of 14 or higher for knowledge (70% of 20), 28 or higher for attitude (70% of 40), and 38.5 or higher for practice (70% of 55) were regarded as having adequate knowledge, positive attitudes and proactive practices. The practice dimension was divided at this cut-off point for multivariable logistic regression analysis. Variables with p<0.1 in univariate analyses were included in the multivariable regression. P values were reported to three decimal places, and a two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. SEM was used to analyse the relationships among the dimensions of the questionnaire.

Patient and public involvement

No patient was involved.

Results

Basic information of respondents

We contacted 552 pregnant women to participate. Among them, 72 individuals refused to participate. After data cleaning, 69 questionnaires were excluded for the following reasons: (1) completion time less than 60 s or more than 1800 s (4 cases); (2) duplicate IP addresses (48 cases); and (3) identical responses across all knowledge, attitude and practice dimensions (17 cases). Consequently, 411 valid questionnaires remained for the final analysis, yielding a valid response rate of 74.46%. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the overall scale in the formal experiment was 0.934 (knowledge dimension α=0.899, attitude dimension α=0.941 and practice dimension α=0.941); Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value=0.951 (p<0.001). Of the 411 pregnant women who participated in this study, the participants had an average age of 31.43 years (SD=5.42). Most had a junior college or bachelor’s degree (38.44%) and were in the late stage of pregnancy (38.93%). Nearly half had one pregnancy (43.80%). A total of 41.85% reported a history of hypothyroidism or other thyroid-related diseases. More than half (59.61%) had a regular hospital or doctor for prenatal visits, and 44.77% had a history of miscarriage. As the data were not normally distributed, the median and IQRs were used to describe the scores. The median (IQR) knowledge, attitude and practice scores were 14 (10 to 17), 30 (23 to 35) and 41 (33 to 47), respectively. Analyses of demographic characteristics found that participants’ knowledge, attitude and practice scores (in that order) varied across education (p<0.001, p<0.001, p<0.001), type of permanent residence (p=0.005, p<0.001, p=0.002), family history of hypothyroidism or other thyroid-related diseases (p=0.003, p<0.001, p<0.001), regular hospital or doctor (p=0.016, p<0.001, p=0.043), and history of miscarriage (p=0.019, p<0.001, p=0.008). Meanwhile, participants who differed in age (p<0.001) and annual income (p=0.044) were more likely to have different knowledge scores. The attitude scores of participants varied significantly across several demographic variables, including annual income (p<0.001), pregnancy stage (p=0.002), number of pregnancies (p=0.010), and history of hypothyroidism or other thyroid-related diseases (p=0.004; online supplemental table 1).

Knowledge, attitudes and practices

The analysis of knowledge regarding thyroid function in pregnancy among the participants indicated that less than half (48.18% being the highest) correctly recognised changes in thyroid hormone levels during pregnancy (K1). Only 34.55% of participants were aware that pregnant women may develop postpartum hypothyroidism (K7), 35.52% understood the safety of using thyroid hormone replacement therapy during pregnancy for both mother and foetus (K5), and just 41.61% recognised the risk of intellectual developmental issues in newborns due to maternal hypothyroidism (K8; table 1).

Attitude dimension of the participants

The survey responses regarding practices for managing thyroid health during pregnancy revealed some notable areas of non-compliance. Concerning proactive thyroid health management, 24.09% of respondents indicated that they were less likely or not at all likely to regularly check thyroid function early in pregnancy or even before conception (P1). Similarly, 17.28% were less likely or not at all likely to focus on iodine sources in their diet to prevent hypothyroidism (P3). Additionally, 16.66% were less likely or not at all likely to actively engage in understanding and managing the impact of hypothyroidism on foetal health (P4; table 3).

Discussion

Pregnant women demonstrated generally adequate knowledge, positive attitudes and active practices regarding hypothyroidism during pregnancy. This result may be attributed to the increased emphasis on maternal health education in China in recent years, as well as the relatively high prevalence of thyroid disorders in the general population, which has raised public awareness of thyroid health. In addition, the widespread implementation of prenatal examinations provides healthcare professionals with more opportunities to deliver relevant health education to pregnant women. However, despite the overall good knowledge, attitude and practice levels, continued efforts are still needed to strengthen understanding and promote more consistent preventive behaviours. It is recommended that healthcare providers implement targeted educational programmes to further enhance knowledge about hypothyroidism, as increased awareness can directly contribute to more proactive attitudes and practices, thereby improving pregnancy outcomes.

Significant associations and differences were observed across various demographics. Education emerged as a significant determinant of knowledge, attitude and practice scores. Participants with higher education levels demonstrated better knowledge and more positive attitudes, which have been found to influence their health management practices positively. This was evident from both univariate differences and multivariable associations where educational attainment was a predictor of improved practices. The impact of education on health literacy is well-documented,11 12 emphasising the need for targeted educational interventions that consider varying educational backgrounds.

Geographical differences also revealed that women from urban areas exhibited higher knowledge, attitude and practice scores compared with those from rural settings. Women from urban areas exhibited relatively higher knowledge, attitude and practice scores than those from rural areas, which might be related to differences in health literacy, educational background and access to health education programmes that are more commonly available in cities.13 14 However, our study did not directly measure healthcare accessibility, so this explanation should be interpreted with caution. Similarly, higher household income was associated with better knowledge and attitudes, a reflection of the broader social determinants of health that include access to resources and information.15 16

The positive correlations between knowledge, attitudes and practices, supported by both correlation analysis and SEM, suggest that increased awareness about hypothyroidism directly enhances the attitudes and practices surrounding its management. This aligns with findings from other studies indicating that higher health knowledge tends to improve both attitudes and health behaviours among patients with chronic conditions.17 18 Notably, the multivariate logistic regression also affirmed that higher knowledge and attitude scores are predictive of better practices.

In examining the knowledge dimension, it becomes apparent that while a moderate proportion of pregnant women understand the basic changes and complications hypothyroidism can bring during pregnancy, there are notable deficiencies. The relatively low awareness of treatment safety, postpartum hypothyroidism and potential effects on newborn development highlights specific gaps that may influence effective prevention and management strategies. Particularly, less than half of the participants recognised the safety of using thyroid hormone replacement therapy during pregnancy and the risks of postpartum hypothyroidism and intellectual development issues in newborns due to maternal hypothyroidism. These findings are concerning given the importance of these aspects in managing hypothyroidism effectively during and after pregnancy. To address these gaps, healthcare providers could develop targeted educational materials that outline thyroid function changes, treatment safety and long-term impacts on both the mother and the child.19 20 Workshops or informational sessions within prenatal care settings could reinforce these topics, ensuring that women receive accurate and comprehensive information throughout their pregnancy.21 22

Attitudes towards managing hypothyroidism during pregnancy were generally positive but showed room for improvement in specific areas such as the recognition of the relationship between thyroid function and foetal health, and the impact of dietary iodine intake. While the majority express a belief in the importance of learning about hypothyroidism, the neutral to negative responses regarding specific preventive actions indicate a potential disconnection between knowledge and perceived personal relevance or efficacy. This suggests that some pregnant women still lack sufficient awareness and proactive behaviour regarding hypothyroidism prevention, emphasising the need for continued education and engagement. Studies have shown that personalising the information about health risks increases patient concern and proactive health behaviour.23 To enhance attitudes, healthcare providers might consider personalised counselling sessions that directly relate thyroid health to individual pregnancy outcomes and emphasise actionable steps that can be taken. Additionally, involving partners in these educational efforts could help reinforce the importance and improve supportive behaviours at home.24 25

Regarding practices, there is a significant variation in how consistently pregnant women apply their knowledge and attitudes towards managing hypothyroidism. Specifically, areas like regular thyroid function checks, maintaining a balanced diet and mental health management show a mix of consistency. These variations imply that although pregnant women generally understand the importance of thyroid health, gaps remain in adhering to recommended practices, possibly due to limited motivation or insufficient follow-up support. The relative inconsistency in these practices might be improved through structured follow-up programmes that provide regular reminders and support. For example, mobile health applications could be used to remind women of thyroid function tests and provide tips on diet and stress management, which have been effective in similar contexts.26 27 Furthermore, establishing support groups within the community that allow sharing experiences and strategies could foster a more proactive approach to managing hypothyroidism during pregnancy. Engaging healthcare providers in actively recommending these resources and supports during routine visits could also enhance practice adherence.28 29

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the data were collected from a single centre, the hospital, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other regions or populations. Second, the sample is drawn from pregnant women visiting the endocrinology department, predominantly through QR codes in obstetrics and endocrinology outpatient clinics; this introduces a selection bias as the participants are more likely to have endocrine-related issues, which might not represent the broader population of pregnant women. Finally, the use of a self-designed questionnaire, while tailored to the specific needs of this study, may not comprehensively capture all relevant aspects of knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding hypothyroidism, potentially affecting the external validity of the study. Furthermore, the generalisability of the findings of this study may be limited by regional and cultural factors. Differences in healthcare systems, cultural backgrounds, health education levels and governmental policies—such as iodised salt fortification programmes and thyroid disease screening guidelines—across countries and regions could lead to variations in knowledge, attitudes and practices of pregnant women toward hypothyroidism during pregnancy. Therefore, the results of this study are primarily applicable to populations with similar sociocultural and healthcare contexts, and caution should be exercised when extrapolating the findings to other settings.

In conclusion, pregnant women demonstrated adequate knowledge, positive attitudes and active practices concerning hypothyroidism during pregnancy, revealing significant positive correlations and direct effects between these factors. Given these findings, healthcare providers should focus on enhancing educational programmes that target hypothyroidism awareness and management during pregnancy, which could further improve health outcomes for both mothers and their unborn children. Future studies may consider adopting qualitative approaches, such as in-depth interviews or focus group discussions, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying reasons and influencing factors behind knowledge, attitudes and practices of pregnant women regarding hypothyroidism during pregnancy. Given these findings, healthcare providers should focus on enhancing educational programmes that target hypothyroidism awareness and management during pregnancy, which could further improve health outcomes for both mothers and their unborn children.
