Persistent systemic interleukin-6 elevation after community-acquired pneumonia is associated with one-year mortality
Authors: Mónica Piqueras, Paula González-Jiménez, Ana Latorre, Noé Mengot, Jordi Tortosa-Carreres, Álvaro Gimeno, Belén Picazos, Miguel Porcar, Rosario Menéndez, Raúl Méndez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunity-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains a leading global cause of mortality and the foremost infectious cause of death. Emerging evidence suggests that its impact extends beyond the acute phase. In this context, long-term complications involving the cardiovascular system and other organs, as well as increased mortality lasting up to 10 years, have been reported. The aim of our study was to assess systemic inflammation at day 30 after CAP through readily available biomarkers (interleukin-6 [IL-6] and C-reactive protein [CRP]) and its association with long-term (one-year) mortality. We performed a post-hoc analysis of a prospective observational study including 268 patients discharged alive after hospitalisation for CAP, along with 15 healthy controls. The first follow-up took place 30 days after discharge at outpatient clinics, where blood samples were collected. One-year follow-up data were obtained from electronic medical records and telephone interviews. Our findings showed significant higher IL-6 and CRP levels at day 30 after CAP compared to controls, especially, in those who died during the following year. This elevation reflects ongoing low-grade systemic inflammation due to incomplete or slow resolution, or from an inability to restore homeostasis.

To the Editor:

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide and is, in fact, the first cause of death due to infection. Recent evidence indicates that its consequences extend beyond the acute phase, changing the previously accepted paradigm of a merely acute and confined infection [1, 2]. Long-term complications, affecting the cardiovascular system and other organs along with increased mortality that persists for up to 10 years, have been described [3–6]. Among the possible mechanisms involved, persistent inflammation, which can further accelerate the progression of chronic conditions and/or atherosclerosis, appears as a crucial component [5]. Lung inflammation has been demonstrated up to 45 days after hospital discharge using ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT [7]. Furthermore, the resolution of systemic inflammation may be incomplete or resolve slowly, leading to subsequent complications. Thus, patients with higher levels of inflammatory cytokines at hospital discharge had a poorer outcome [8, 9], although there is scarce information addressing systemic inflammation thereafter [10]. The aim of our study was to assess systemic inflammation at day 30 after CAP through readily available biomarkers (interleukin-6 [IL-6] and C-reactive protein [CRP]) and its association with long-term (one-year) mortality.

We conducted a post-hoc analysis of a prospective observational study involving 268 patients discharged alive after CAP hospitalization [11]. Additionally, fifteen controls were included. This study was conducted in accordance with the amended Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee Hospital La Fe (2013/0204). All participants provided written informed consent. Initial follow-up assessment was conducted at day 30 post-discharge at outpatient clinics, where blood samples were collected and processed to be stored frozen at − 80 °C until analysis. One-year follow-up was conducted through electronic medical records and telephone calls.

IL-6 levels were measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Elecsys IL-6 test) on the Cobas 8000 e602 analyser (Roche Diagnostics). CRP was measured by using a microparticle-enhanced turbidimetric assay (CRP Gen.3, Cobas 8000, c701; Roche Diagnostics). For values below the limit of detection, the lower limit of quantification was assigned as 1.5 pg/mL for IL-6, and 0.3 mg/L for CRP.

The statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statics version 26.0 software, RStudio v2025.05.0, and GraphPad Prism 8.0. Inflammatory biomarkers IL-6 and CRP are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). The areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROCs) were calculated to assess the accuracy of each biomarker in predicting one-year mortality, and the optimal cut-off points (OCP) were determined using the Youden index. A Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using IL-6 and CRP levels dichotomized according to their OCP and only significant results were found for IL-6. A Cox proportional hazards regression analysis using IL-6 dichotomized by OCP was conducted to calculate the Hazard Ratios (HR) with 95% of Confidence Intervals (CI) for time to death within one year as the primary outcome. The model was adjusted by age and sex. Statistical significance was considered if the p-value was ˂ 0.05.

In the univariate analysis based on ROC curves, the OCP for one-year mortality was 10.0 pg/mL for IL-6, and 2.4 mg/L for CRP, with corresponding AUC of 0.77 (CI 0.64–0.90; p-value: 0.015) and 0.64 (CI 0.46–0.82; p-value: 0.291), respectively. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated a significant increased one-year mortality in those with serum IL-6 > 10.0 pg/mL. No significant differences were found in survival for CRP (Fig. 1).

Kaplan–Meier survival curves for patients stratified by IL-6 and CRP levels relative to their optimal cut-off points

In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, IL-6 levels above the dichotomized OCP were independently associated with increased risk of one-year mortality (HR 7.04; CI: 1.30–38.21; p = 0.024) after adjusting for age and sex.

Our findings showed significant higher IL-6 and CRP levels at day 30 after CAP compared to controls, especially, in those who died during the following year. This elevation reflects ongoing low-grade systemic inflammation due to incomplete or slow resolution, or from an inability to restore homeostasis. In fact, the time to resolve CAP inflammation and how it impacts outcome is incompletely known. Yende et al. reported that patients with higher IL-6 levels at hospital discharge were more likely to die within the following three months [9], as it occurs with haemostasis markers -D-dimer and TAT levels- and one-year mortality [12]. However, there is limited information regarding systemic inflammation later; although raised cardiac biomarkers at day 30 after CAP have been related to long-term CVE [11]. Boczar et al. through ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT found persistent vascular inflammatory activity, higher than age- and sex matched controls, in the convalescent phase around 45 days after CAP [13].

A worse one-year outcome was found in those exhibiting serum IL-6 levels >10.0 pg/mL at day 30 (HR: 7.04; p = 0.02) and a trend in those with CRP >2.4 mg/L. Although CRP did not reach statistical significance, non-survivors tended to have higher CRP levels than survivors. IL-6 acts as an upstream inducer of CRP, which may explain why IL-6 appears more sensitive in reflecting persistent inflammation [14]. Another possible explanation is that CRP levels in both groups were relatively low and within the reference range for healthy individuals. Future studies could consider high-sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) measurements, which may provide a more precise assessment of endothelial damage and low-grade inflammation [15]. This finding supports the potential relevance of inflammatory biomarkers in identifying patients with incomplete inflammatory resolution susceptible to have a prolonged health decline and eventually die [5]. In this regard, sustained elevation of IL-6, a molecule with proinflammatory effects, has been associated with an increased likelihood of cardiovascular events, possibly due to endothelial inflammation, multiorgan dysfunction related to oxidative stress, and immunological alterations [14]. This subset of patients could constitute a target population for trials aimed at improving long-term CAP survival and for evaluating different therapeutic and monitoring approaches. The need for identifying biomarkers that aid long-term patient stratification has previously been emphasized as a key focus in CAP research [16].

The relatively small number of deaths between day 30 and one year represents the main limitation of this study. This limited number of events reduced statistical power and restricted our ability to adjust the model for additional variables potentially associated with long-term mortality after CAP, such as previous comorbidities, CAP severity, and functional status of the patient. However, statistical significance for IL-6 was still achieved, underscoring the strength of the results. Our findings suggest the need for further investigation ideally through studies with larger sample sizes that can adjust for additional relevant factors. Another limitation of the study is the small number of healthy controls and their age difference compared with the patients. Nevertheless, the IL-6 and CRP ranges in our control group are consistent with those reported in larger cohorts of healthy individuals [17, 18]. The strength of the current study is that IL-6 is a useful biomarker, easily available in hospitals, for assessing long-term inflammation leading to increased risk of death.

Persistent systemic inflammation after CAP that might lead to prolonged health decline, due to a steady downward decline of chronic diseases or organ dysfunction, remains a neglected clinical feature and research area. Our results highlight the importance of paying attention to hidden persistent inflammation that may be associated with an increased risk of long-term mortality. Serum levels of IL-6 could serve as a useful biomarker for recognizing a subset of more vulnerable subjects to optimize therapeutic and preventive measures directed to improve long-term prognosis.
