Kac’s Program and Relative Entropy Decay for Nonlinear Spin-Exchange Dynamics
Pietro Caputo, Mario Morellini

Abstract
. We introduce and analyze a nonlinear exchange dynamics for Ising spin systems with arbitrary interactions. The evolution is governed by a quadratic Boltzmann-type equation that conserves the mean magnetization. Collisions are encoded through a spin-exchange kernel chosen so that the dynamics converge to the Ising model with the prescribed interaction and mean magnetization profile determined by the initial state. We prove a general convergence theorem, valid for any interaction and any transport kernel. Moreover, we show that, for sufficiently weak interactions, the system relaxes exponentially fast to equilibrium in relative entropy, with optimal decay rate independent of the initial condition. The proof relies on establishing a strong version of the Kac program from kinetic theory. In particular, we show that the associated Kac particle system satisfies a modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constants uniform in the number of particles. This is achieved by adapting the method of stochastic localization to the present conservative setting.



1.
Introduction and main results

We consider Boltzmann-type evolution equations of the form

(1.1)

\frac{dp_{t}}{dt}\,=\,p_{t}\circ p_{t}\,-\,p_{t},

where
p_{t}
is a probability distribution on a configuration space
\Omega
, and the
collision product

p_{t}\circ p_{t}
is defined through a probability kernel
Q(\tau\,|\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
.
The kernel takes as input two independent configurations
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Omega
, each sampled from
p_{t}
, and produces an output configuration
\tau\in\Omega
, with law

(1.2)

p_{t}\circ p_{t}(\tau)=\int p_{t}(d\sigma)\int p_{t}(d\sigma^{\prime})\,Q(\tau\,|\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}).

A classical example is Kac’s caricature of a Maxwellian gas
[
28
]
, which captures essential features of the Boltzmann equation in a simplified setting; see also
[
6
]
for a discrete version of Kac’s model.
Such nonlinear equations admit a natural probabilistic representation in terms of random binary trees
[
39
,
30
]
.
A key structural property is the existence of
conservation laws
, associated to quantities that remain invariant under the collision product.
Each conservation law gives rise to a family of stationary measures, which determine the possible limiting distributions of the dynamics.

For instance, in Kac’s model, where
\Omega=\mathbb{R}
is the set of one-dimensional velocities and collisions conserve the kinetic energy, for any initial distribution
p_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}
with mean zero and finite variance one has convergence to the centered Gaussian distribution with the same variance as
p_{0}
.
Understanding the
rate of convergence to equilibrium
has been a longstanding question, and even in this simple setting it presents significant challenges
[
27
,
37
,
38
,
19
,
23
]
.

In this work we focus on a
combinatorial counterpart
of the Boltzmann equation, where
\Omega
is the Boolean cube, and the analogue of the Maxwellian equilibrium is played by a high-dimensional Gibbs measure, such as the Ising model on a graph.
At infinite temperature, the model reduces to a version of the recombination equations commonly studied in population genetics
[
35
,
34
,
14
]
.
When interactions are present, however, the system becomes substantially richer, and analyzing its convergence to stationarity raises new challenges.

We consider a family of models in which a collision between two spin configurations consists of exchanging spin values at a pair of sites, selected according to a prescribed transport kernel.
The conservation laws correspond to the magnetization profile of the distribution along the irreducible components of the transport kernel.
By tuning the transport kernel, one can realize any desired magnetization profile.

Our first result is a general convergence theorem, valid for arbitrary interaction strength and for all choices of transport kernel.
We then turn to the question of quantitative convergence rates.
A discrete-time version of the model, in the special case where the conservation law is given by the single-site magnetization profile, was recently analyzed in
[
16
]
, providing convergence rates in total variation distance at high temperature.
Here we consider arbitrary magnetization profiles and obtain finer quantitative bounds in terms of relative entropy rather than total variation distance.

Our main tool is an adaptation of the
Kac program
to this combinatorial setting, in the spirit of what has been developed for Kac’s original model of a Maxwellian gas
[
19
,
32
]
.
In contrast to that classical setting, we show that for the present model one can fully implement Kac’s program in a strong quantitative sense.
Specifically, under a suitable high-temperature assumption, we establish a uniformly positive rate of exponential decay of relative entropy, independent of the initial condition.
To our knowledge, this provides the first example of a high-dimensional system with genuine interactions where such a strong form of Kac’s program can be implemented.

We now proceed to a detailed description of the models and our main results.

1.1.
Spin system

Let
\Omega=\{-1,+1\}^{n}
, for some positive integer
n
, denote the set of spin configurations.
We consider Ising systems defined through the Gibbs measure

(1.3)

\mu_{J,h}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{Z_{J,h}}\exp\left(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}}J_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\textstyle{\sum_{i\in[n]}}h_{i}\sigma_{i}\right),\qquad\sigma\in\Omega,

where
J=(J_{i,j})_{i,j\in[n]}
is the interaction matrix,
h=(h_{i})_{i\in[n]}
is the external magnetic field, and
Z_{J,h}
is the partition function ensuring normalization, so that
\mu_{J,h}
is a probability over
\Omega
. Throughout, we use the notation
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
We assume that
J
is a real symmetric matrix, and
h
is a real vector. When
h=0
we will simply write
\mu_{J}
.
When
J=aA_{G}
, where
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
A_{G}
is the adjacency matrix of an undirected graph
G
with
n
vertices, then
\mu_{J,h}
represents the Ising model on the graph
G
with external field
h
, and with interaction strength
a
. If
a>0
the model is called
ferromagnetic
, while it is called
antiferromagnetic
if
a<0
.
A classical example is the Curie-Weiss model (or mean-field Ising model), where the interaction matrix is given by
J_{i,j}=\beta/n
for all
i,j\in[n]
, with
\beta>0
denoting the inverse temperature, which corresponds to the Ising model on the complete graph with interaction strength
a=\beta/n
. Other examples include disordered systems such as the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model, where
J_{i,j}=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{n}}g_{ij}
, where
g_{ij}=g_{ji}
,
i<j
, are i.i.d. standard normal random variables, or the Ising model on a random
d
-regular graph with interaction strength
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where
d\geq 3
is a fixed integer, in which case
J=aA_{G}
, where
A_{G}
is the adjacency matrix of a random
d
-regular graph with
n
vertices.

1.2.
The collision kernel

A collision between two spin configurations
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\Omega
consists in the exchange of the
\ell
-th spin of
\sigma
with the
k
-th spin of
\sigma^{\prime}
, that is

(1.4)

(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\mapsto\left(S_{\ell}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}_{k}),\ S_{k}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma_{\ell})\right),

where
S_{\ell}:\Omega\times\{-1,1\}\to\Omega
is the configuration defined as

S_{\ell}(\sigma,a)_{i}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{i}&\text{if }i\neq\ell,\\
a&\text{if }i=\ell.\end{cases}

The sites
(\ell,k)
at which the exchange occurs are chosen by picking
\ell
uniformly at random in
[n]
and then by picking
k
according to a prescribed
symmetric stochastic
n\times n
matrix
, denoted
\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)
, which we refer to as the transport kernel. In order to guarantee the reversibility of our system with respect to the Ising measure, the proposed exchange (
1.4
) is accepted with probability

(1.5)

p_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{\mu_{J}(S_{\ell}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}_{k}))\,\mu_{J}(S_{k}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma_{\ell}))}{\mu_{J}(\sigma)\,\mu_{J}(\sigma^{\prime})+\mu_{J}(S_{\ell}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}_{k}))\,\mu_{J}(S_{k}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma_{\ell}))},

and is otherwise rejected.
Summarizing, the pair
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\Omega
produces the new pair
(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\Omega
according to the probability kernel on
\Omega\times\Omega
given by

(1.6)

\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell,k=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)\ Q_{J}^{\ell,k}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime}),

where, for each pair of sites
\ell,k\in[n]
,
Q_{J}^{\ell,k}
is the probability kernel on
\Omega\times\Omega
defined as

\displaystyle Q_{J}^{\ell,k}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=p_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\mathbf{1}(\tau=S_{\ell}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}_{k}),\tau^{\prime}=S_{k}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma_{\ell}))\;+\;

(1.7)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\;(1-p_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}))\mathbf{1}(\tau=\sigma,\tau^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}),

and we use the notation
{\bf 1}(E)
for the indicator function of the event
E
.
If
\mathcal{A}
denotes the set of equivalence classes corresponding to the irreducible components of the kernel
\mathcal{K}
,
it follows that
\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}
satisfies the detailed balance condition with respect to
\mu_{J,h}\otimes\mu_{J,h}
, for any choice of external fields
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
h
is constant over every subset
A\in\mathcal{A}
:

(1.8)

\mu_{J,h}(\sigma)\mu_{J,h}(\sigma^{\prime})\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\mu_{J,h}(\tau)\mu_{J,h}(\tau^{\prime})\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}),

for arbitrary
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}),(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\Omega
and
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
h_{i}=h_{j}
for all
i,j\in A
, for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
; see Lemma
2.1
.

In conclusion, for any given interaction
J\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
, and any transport kernel
\mathcal{K}
, we have constructed a probability kernel
\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}
on
\Omega\times\Omega
, which satisfies the detailed balance condition with respect to
\mu_{J,h}\otimes\mu_{J,h}
, independently of the choice of
h
, provided
h
is constant over the irreducible components of
\mathcal{K}
.

1.3.
Nonlinear dynamics

Let
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
denote the set of probability measures on
\Omega
.
Given the collision kernel
\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}
, the nonlinear evolution on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)

is defined by the dynamical system

(1.9)

\frac{dp_{t}}{dt}=p_{t}\circ p_{t}-p_{t},\qquad p_{0}=p,

for some initial value
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, where
p\circ q\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, for
p,q\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, denotes the commutative collision product defined as

(1.10)

p\circ q\,(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\in\Omega}\big(p(\sigma)q(\sigma^{\prime})+p(\sigma^{\prime})q(\sigma)\big)\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime}).

Note that this has the form (
1.2
) with

Q(\tau\,|\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\textstyle{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\Omega}}\,\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})\,.

For any
J\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
and any stochastic matrix
\mathcal{K}
, the Cauchy problem (
1.9
)
has a unique solution
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
,
t\geq 0
, for any initial state
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
. This can be seen by standard existence and uniqueness results, see e.g.
[
2
]
for related statements. Moreover, a representation of the solution based on random binary trees can be obtained, see e.g.
[
18
,
14
,
12
]
for similar constructions.
Here, we are interested in the structure of the stationary states of (
1.9
), that is the set of
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
such that
p_{t}\equiv p
, and in the analysis of the convergence to stationarity.
As we will see, the set
\mathcal{A}
of irreducible components of the kernel
\mathcal{K}
determines the conservation laws of the dynamical system (
1.9
) and the set of stationary states is fully determined by
J
and
\mathcal{A}
. The following are some examples of kernels
\mathcal{K}
to keep in mind.

Example 1.1

(Single-Site Dynamics)
.

If
\mathcal{K}(i,j)={\bf 1}(i=j)
for all
i,j\in[n]
, exchanges are allowed only between spins at the same site. With the notation (
1.2
), the collision kernel is given by

(1.11)

\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}Q_{J}^{\ell,\ell}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})\,.

In this case the partition associated to
\mathcal{K}
is made of singletons:
\mathcal{A}=(\{i\},i\in[n])
. The system (
1.9
) is the continuous-time counterpart of the nonlinear Single-Site Dynamics introduced in
[
16
]
.

Example 1.2

(Mean-Field Exchange Dynamics)
.

If
\mathcal{K}(i,j)=1/n
for all
i,j\in[n]
, the proposed exchanges are uniformly chosen between all pairs of sites.
The collision kernel is given by

(1.12)

\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{\ell,k=1}^{n}Q_{J}^{\ell,k}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})\,.

In this case the partition
\mathcal{A}
consists of a single component
\mathcal{A}=\{[n]\}
, and the process (
1.9
) is a nonlinear analogue of a mean-field Kawasaki dynamics.

The two examples discussed above are both covered by the general class of examples defined as follows.

Definition 1.3

(Multi-component Mean-Field Exchange Dynamics)
.

Fix an arbitrary partition
\mathcal{A}
of
[n]
, and define the matrix
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{A}}
by

(1.13)

\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{A}}(\ell,k)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|A|},&\text{if }\ell,k\in A\text{ for some }A\in\mathcal{A},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

In this case, the nonlinear dynamics associated to the kernel
\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}
in (
1.6
) is referred to as the Nonlinear Multi-component Mean-Field Exchange Dynamics.

Definition 1.4
.

Given a partition
\mathcal{A}
of
[n]
, and
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, we define the
\mathcal{A}
-
magnetization profile
as the vector

(1.14)

m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)=(m(p,A)\,,\,A\in\mathcal{A})\,,\qquad m(p,A)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{\ell\in A}p[\sigma_{\ell}]\,,

where
p[\sigma_{\ell}]
is the expected value of the spin at site
\ell
according to
p
.
The vector
m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)
is in
[-1,1]^{\mathcal{A}}
, and we say that
p
is
\mathcal{A}
-regular if
m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)\in(-1,1)^{\mathcal{A}}
, that is if
|m(p,A)|\neq 1
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
. We write
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
for the set of
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
that are
\mathcal{A}
-regular.

We note that in Example
1.1
, specifying the vector
m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)\in[-1,1]^{n}
is
equivalent to giving all the single-site marginals of
p
, while in Example
1.2
,
m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)\in[-1,1]
is just the global mean magnetization of
p
.

The fist result is the following general convergence theorem, which summarizes the main properties of our nonlinear dynamical system.
We use the notation

\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for the set of external fields that are constant over each element of the partition
\mathcal{A}
:

\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}=\{h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\;h_{i}=h_{j},\;\forall i,j\in A\,,\;\forall A\in\mathcal{A}\}.

It can be shown that for each
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
there is a unique vector
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
depending only on
J
and
m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)
, such that

m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)=m_{\mathcal{A}}(\mu_{J,h})
; see Lemma
2.2
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Fix a symmetric matrix
J\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
and a symmetric stochastic
n\times n
matrix
\mathcal{K}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the partition of
[n]
induced by the irreducible components of
\mathcal{K}
.
A probability
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
is a stationary state if and only if
p=\mu_{J,h}
for some
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Moreover, the expected values

m(p_{t},A)
,
A\in\mathcal{A}
,
are independent of
t\geq 0
. Finally, for any initial state
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
, one has the convergence

(1.15)

\|p_{t}-\mu_{J,h}\|_{\rm TV}\;\longrightarrow\;0\,,\qquad t\to\infty\,,

where
\|\cdot\|_{\rm TV}
denotes the total variation distance and
h
is the unique vector
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
such that
m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)=m_{\mathcal{A}}(\mu_{J,h})
.

Remark 1.6
.

We note that the requirement that
p
be
\mathcal{A}
-regular in Theorem
1.5
is not essential, as it can be removed if one allows the external fields
h
to take the value
\pm\infty
.

1.4.
Entropy decay

The above theorem establishes convergence to stationarity, but it says nothing about the speed of the convergence. The next results address this question, under a high-temperature assumption on the interaction matrix
J
. Moreover, we restrict to the case of Multi-component Mean Field Dynamics, that is we take
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{A}}
as in Definition
1.3
with an arbitrary choice of the partition
\mathcal{A}
.

Given
\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, we write
H(\nu\,|\,\mu)
for the relative entropy, or KL-divergence, of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
, that is

(1.16)

H(\nu\,|\,\mu)=\begin{cases}\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\nu(\sigma)\log(\nu(\sigma)/\mu(\sigma))&\text{if}\ \ \nu\ll\mu,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\nu\ll\mu
denotes absolute continuity of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
.

Our main quantitative result is formulated as follows.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
J\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be a nonnegative definite matrix with largest eigenvalue
\lambda(J)<\frac{1}{2}
, and let
\overline{J}=\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n]}|J_{i,j}|
.
Then, for any partition
\mathcal{A}
of
[n]
, the nonlinear system (
1.9
) defined by
J
and
\mathcal{K}
, with
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{A}}
given in (
1.13
), satisfies

(1.17)

H(p_{t}\,|\,\mu_{J,h})\leq H(p\,|\,\mu_{J,h})\,e^{-\alpha\,t}\,,\qquad t\geq 0,

for any
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
, where
h
is the unique vector
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
such that
m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)=m_{\mathcal{A}}(\mu_{J,h})
, and the positive constant
\alpha
satisfies

(1.18)

\alpha\geq\tfrac{1}{4n}(1-2\lambda(J))^{2}\,e^{-16\overline{J}}\,.

Remark 1.8
.

We note that the estimate (
1.17
) implies exponential decay in total variation as well. Indeed, by Pinsker’s inequality,

\|p_{t}-\mu_{J,h}\|^{2}_{\rm TV}\leq\frac{1}{2}H(p_{t}\,|\,\mu_{J,h})
. Using
H(p\,|\,\mu_{J,h})\leq\max_{\sigma\in\Omega}\log(1/\mu_{J,h}(\sigma))
, which can be bounded by
Cn
, where
C=\lambda(J)+2\overline{h}+\log(2)
and
\overline{h}=\max_{i\in[n]}|h_{i}|
, it follows that, in the setting of Theorem
1.7
, for all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
, one has

(1.19)

\|p_{t}-\mu_{J,h}\|_{\rm TV}\leq\,\sqrt{\frac{C\,n}{2}}\,e^{-\alpha\,t/2}\,.

In particular, Theorem
1.7
is a quantitative version of Theorem
1.5
under the additional assumption on the spectral radius of
J
, which can be regarded as a high temperature requirement. It is worthy of note that the rate
\alpha
is bounded below independently of the choice of partition
\mathcal{A}
. In the single-site case of Example
1.1
this reproduces essentially the same rate that was obtained, under a more restrictive high temperature constraint, for the discrete-time version of the single-site model in
[
16
]
.

By comparison with the non-interacting case
J=0
, we note that the rate
\alpha
has the optimal dependence with respect to the dimension
n
, up to a constant factor. For instance, in the single-site case, one can use a coupon collector argument to show that if
J=0
and the initial distribution
p
is all
+1
with probability
1/2
and all
-1
with probability
1/2
, then the left hand side in (
1.19
) is at least
c\,e^{-\frac{t}{c\,n}}
,
t\geq 0
, for some absolute constant
c>0
; see
[
16
]
.

The estimate in Theorem
1.7
is equivalent to a certain nonlinear logarithmic Sobolev inequality, which provides a lower bound on the relative entropy dissipation in terms of the relative entropy; see Section
4.1
below. Rather than establishing such a bound directly, to prove Theorem
1.7
we follow the strategy introduced by Mark Kac in his seminal 1956 paper
[
27
]
, that is we
approximate the nonlinear evolution of one particle by a linear mean-field type Markov
process involving a large number of particles.

1.5.
Kac’s program

We consider a system of
N\in\mathbb{N}

particles

\{\sigma(i),i=1,\ldots,N\}
, where each
\sigma(i)=(\sigma_{1}(i),\dots,\sigma_{n}(i))
is a spin configuration in
\Omega=\{-1,+1\}^{n}
.
In words, the process is described as follows. Independently, each ordered pair of particles
(i,j)
, including
i=j
, is updated at the arrival times of a Poisson process with rate
1/N
. When an update occurs at particles
(i,j)
, a site
\ell
is picked uniformly at random, a site
k
is picked according to the transport kernel
\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)
, and then with probability
\hat{p}_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(i),\sigma(j))
the
\ell
-th spin of the
i
-th particle is exchanged with the
k
-th spin of the
j
-th particle,
while with probability
1-\hat{p}_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(i),\sigma(j))
the particle configuration is left unchanged.
Using the notation (
1.4
), the exchange can be written as

(\sigma(i),\sigma(j))\mapsto(S_{\ell}(\sigma(i),\sigma(j)_{k})\,,\,S_{k}(\sigma(j),\sigma(i)_{\ell}),

with the understanding that when
i=j
this is replaced by
\sigma(i)\mapsto\sigma(i)^{\ell,k}
, where
\sigma(i)^{\ell,k}
denotes the configuration
\sigma(i)
after the exchange
(\sigma_{\ell}(i),\sigma_{k}(i))\mapsto(\sigma_{k}(i),\sigma_{\ell}(i))
. Here the probability

\hat{p}_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(i),\sigma(j))
is defined by

(1.20)

\hat{p}_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(i),\sigma(j))=\begin{cases}p_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(i),\sigma(j))&i\neq j\\
\frac{\mu_{J}(\sigma(i)^{\ell k})}{\mu_{J}(\sigma(i))+\mu_{J}(\sigma(i)^{\ell k})}&i=j\end{cases}

where we use the notation (
1.5
). This defines a continuous-time Markov chain with state space
\Omega^{N}=\{-1,+1\}^{Nn}
, which is described by the infinitesimal generator

(1.21)

\mathcal{L}_{N}F(\sigma)=\frac{1}{Nn}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell,k\in[n]}\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)\,\hat{p}_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(i),\sigma(j))\,\nabla^{i,\ell;j,k}F(\sigma),

where
\sigma\in\Omega^{N}
,
F:\Omega^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, and the gradient operator
\nabla^{i,\ell;j,k}
is defined by

(1.22)

\nabla^{i,\ell;j,k}F(\sigma):=F(\sigma^{i,\ell;j,k})-F(\sigma),\quad\text{with }\sigma^{i,\ell;j,k}(u):=\begin{cases}\sigma(u)&u\neq i,j,\\
S_{\ell}(\sigma(i),\sigma(j)_{k})&u=i,\\
S_{k}(\sigma(j),\sigma(i)_{\ell})&u=j,\end{cases}

with the convention that, when
i=j
, then
\sigma^{i,\ell;i,k}(u)=\sigma(u)
if
u\neq i
, and
\sigma^{i,\ell;i,k}(i)=\sigma(i)^{\ell,k}
.

We may picture the system as a spin exchange dynamics in a
N\times n
grid, with exchanges occurring between sites
(i,\ell)
and
(j,k)
. By construction, the Markov chain is reversible with respect to the product measure
\mu_{J}^{\otimes N}
.
Since only pairs
(\ell,k)
belonging to sites within the same equivalence class are exchanged, to turn the Markov chain into an irreducible process we need to impose a constraint of fixed magnetization in each block
B_{N,A}:=\{(i,\ell)\,,\;i\in[N],\ell\in A\}
,
A\in\mathcal{A}
. To this end, we need the following definitions.

Definition 1.9
.

Given a partition
\mathcal{A}
of
[n]
, we write
D_{\mathcal{A},N}
for the set of all vectors
\rho=(\rho(A),\,A\in\mathcal{A})\in[0,1]^{\mathcal{A}}
such that
N|A|\rho(A)
is a nonnegative integer. Given
\mathcal{A}
,
D_{\mathcal{A},N}
is called the set of
\mathcal{A}
-
density profiles
. Given
\rho\in D_{\mathcal{A},N}
, we define

(1.23)

\Omega_{N,\rho}=\left\{\sigma\in\Omega^{N}:\;\textstyle{\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{\ell\in A}}\sigma_{\ell}(i)=(2\rho(A)-1)N|A|,\;\;\forall A\in\mathcal{A}\right\}\,.

It follows that the Markov chain with generator (
1.21
) is irreducible in the state space
\Omega_{N,\rho}
, for every choice of
\rho\in D_{\mathcal{A},N}
. Moreover, the probability measure

(1.24)

\mu_{N,J,\rho}:=\mu_{J}^{\otimes N}(\cdot\,|\,\Omega_{N,\rho})

obtained as tensor product of
\mu_{J}
restricted to
\Omega_{N,\rho}
is the associated unique invariant distribution. We refer to
\mu_{N,J,\rho}
as the multi-canonical Ising measure.

Our main result concerning the particle system is the following modified log-Sobolev inequality (MLSI), with bounds that are uniform in
N
and in the density profile
\rho\in D_{\mathcal{A},N}
.
To introduce the relevant quantities, observe that the Dirichlet form associated to the reversible pair
(\mathcal{L}_{N},\mu_{N,J,\rho})
is given by

(1.25)

\mathcal{E}_{N,J,\rho}(F,G):=\frac{1}{2Nn}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell,k=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)\mu_{N,J,\rho}\left[\hat{p}_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(i),\sigma(j))\nabla^{i,\ell;j,k}F\,\nabla^{i,\ell;j,k}G\right],

for functions
F,G:\Omega_{N,\rho}\to\mathbb{R}
. Recall that the
entropy
of a function
F\geq 0
with respect to a probability measure
\mu
is given by

\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu}F:=\mu[F\log F]-\mu[F]\log\mu[F]
.

Theorem 1.10

(MLSI for the particle system)
.

Let
J\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be a nonnegative definite matrix with largest eigenvalue
\lambda(J)<1/2
, and let
\overline{J}=\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n]}|J_{i,j}|
.
Then, for any partition
\mathcal{A}
of
[n]
, for any
N\geq 2
, and
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A},N}
, the Markov chain with Dirichlet form (
1.25
) defined by
J
and
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{A}}
given in (
1.13
), satisfies

(1.26)

\mathcal{E}_{N,J,\rho}(F,\log F)\geq\alpha\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu_{N,J,\rho}}F,

for all
F\geq 0
, with constant
\alpha
such that

(1.27)

\alpha\geq\tfrac{1}{4n}(1-2\lambda(J))^{2}\,e^{-16\overline{J}}\,.

It is well known that a MLSI of the form (
1.27
) is equivalent to an exponential decay statement for the relative entropy, with decay rate given by the same constant
\alpha
. That is, for any initial distribution
\nu
on
\Omega_{N,\rho}
, the distribution
\nu_{t}:=\nu e^{t\mathcal{L}_{N}}
of the particle system at time
t
satisfies

(1.28)

H_{N}(\nu_{t}\,|\,\mu_{N,J,\rho})\leq H_{N}(\nu\,|\,\mu_{N,J,\rho})\,e^{-\alpha\,t},\qquad t\geq 0,

where
H_{N}
denotes the relative entropy for distributions on
\Omega^{N}
.
The key feature of the bound in Theorem
1.10
is the independence on
N
and
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A},N}
. This, together with so-called Entropic Chaos and Fisher Chaos results for our system, will allow us to transfer the result (
1.28
) on the linear particle system to the desired entropy decay estimate (
1.17
) for the nonlinear system, as stated in Theorem
1.7
; see Section
4
.

1.6.
Related work

Boltzmann-type equations of the form (
1.1
) have been extensively studied across a wide range of fields.
Besides being a classical topic in kinetic theory
[
37
]
, they have been systematically investigated in the framework of
mass-action kinetics
; see
[
25
]
for a recent survey.
Moreover, such equations capture a variety of phenomena, from models in population genetics and genetic algorithms in combinatorial optimization
[
26
,
33
]
, to random sampling
[
35
,
34
,
36
]
.
The sampling perspective is particularly relevant in our setting, since, as shown in Theorem
1.5
, the nonlinear dynamics can be used to approximate a system with a prescribed interaction structure (determined by the matrix
J
) and a specified magnetization profile (determined by the initial condition and the partition
\mathcal{A}
).
We note that these types of dynamical systems can also be formulated within the broader framework of
nonlinear Markov chains
, introduced in
[
31
]
, which provides a convenient setup for the analysis of mean-field models.

The specific spin-system framework adopted in this paper was introduced under the name of
Reversible Quadratic Systems
in
[
15
]
, where the decay of relative entropy was analyzed for a class of recombination models at infinite temperature.
Further developments on nonlinear recombination models were obtained in
[
14
,
12
]
.
More recently,
[
16
]
established the first quantitative results for an interacting quadratic system by proving exponential decay of the total variation distance at high temperature, for a model in which exchanges occur at coinciding sites in the two spin configurations.
This yields a process conserving the full single-site magnetization profile.
In the present work, we considerably strengthen the results of
[
16
]
by allowing arbitrary magnetization profiles, and by upgrading the convergence to exponential decay in relative entropy.

Proving exponential decay of relative entropy for kinetic equations is a notoriously difficult problem; see
[
38
,
19
,
32
,
7
,
24
]
and references therein.
The investigation of this question has driven substantial advances in the field, with the concept of
Propagation of Chaos
, introduced by Kac in his foundational paper
[
27
]
, playing a central role.
We refer to
[
32
]
for a detailed analysis of Kac’s program in the context of the Boltzmann equation, and to
[
20
]
for a recent broad survey.

As shown in Theorem
1.10
, our analysis relies on proving that the relative entropy of the corresponding Kac particle system decays exponentially, with rates that are uniform in both the number of particles
N
and the conserved quantities
\rho
.
Equivalently, this amounts to establishing a uniform
Modified Logarithmic Sobolev Inequality
(MLSI).
The validity of such a strong functional inequality may come as a surprise, as it stands in sharp contrast to the classical Kac-Boltzmann setting; see
[
38
,
19
,
32
,
7
]
for related discussions.

Functional inequalities for conservative spin systems have been widely studied; see
[
40
,
11
]
for the case of lattice spin systems under a spatial mixing condition.
Although these results hold uniformly in the conserved parameters
\rho
, the techniques developed in those works do not provide the uniformity in the number of particles
N
required here.
To prove Theorem
1.10
, we instead adapt the framework of
stochastic localization
to our setting.
We refer to
[
21
]
for an introduction to stochastic localization, and to
[
5
]
for an alternative, essentially equivalent formulation based on renormalization group ideas.
In particular, inspired by the recent developments in Kawasaki dynamics
[
4
]
, we exploit a reduction to the case of independent spins with fixed magnetization and arbitrary external fields, for which a modified log-Sobolev inequality follows from a bound on the so-called
Down-Up walk
on matroid bases.
The latter, in turn, rests on important recent advances concerning the entropy of particle systems with log-concave generating polynomials
[
22
,
1
]
.
Our approach follows closely the formulation proposed in
[
13
]
, which is based on the
entropy factorization
inequality rather than the modified log-Sobolev inequality.
This perspective is crucial for handling arbitrary magnetization profiles, as it allows us to obtain flexible factorizations of the multi-canonical measure (
1.24
).

1.7.
Plan of the paper

General structural properties of the dynamical system are discussed in Section
2
, which also contains the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
The analysis of the associated Kac particle system, together with the proof of Theorem
1.10
, is presented in Section
3
.
Finally, in Section
4
we show how to transfer these results from the Kac particle system to the nonlinear equation, thereby completing the proof of Theorem
1.7
. We conclude with some open problems about establishing exponential decay to equilibrium without the high temperature assumption.

2.
Structural properties of the nonlinear equation

The statements in this section hold for any integer
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, for any fixed choice of the symmetric matrix
J\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
and the symmetric stochastic
n\times n
matrix
\mathcal{K}
.

2.1.
Preliminary facts

Recall the notation
\mathcal{A}
for the partition of
[n]
induced by the irreducible components of
\mathcal{K}
and the set
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
of external fields associated to
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The kernel
\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}
defined by (
1.6
) satisfies

(2.1)

\mu_{J,h}(\sigma)\mu_{J,h}(\sigma^{\prime})Q_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\mu_{J,h}(\tau)\mu_{J,h}(\tau^{\prime})Q_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}),

for all
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, and
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
p_{J,h}(\ell,k|\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
denote the quantity defined in (
1.5
) with the difference that
\mu_{J}
is replaced by
\mu_{J,h}
, with
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. By the definition of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, it follows that, whenever
\ell,k
belong to the same irreducible class,

p_{J}(\ell,k|\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=p_{J,h}(\ell,k|\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\,,\qquad h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}.

In particular, if
(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\left(S_{\ell}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}_{k}),\ S_{k}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma_{\ell})\right)
, and
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, then

\mu_{J,h}(\sigma)\mu_{J,h}(\sigma^{\prime})p_{J}(\ell,k|\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\mu_{J,h}(\tau)\mu_{J,h}(\tau^{\prime})p_{J}(\ell,k|\tau,\tau^{\prime}).

Since only pairs
(\ell,k)
such that
\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)>0
enter the definition (
1.6
) of
\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}
, this shows that the kernel
\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}
satisfies the claimed detailed balance (
2.1
).
∎

Recall the Definition
1.4
of the magnetization profile
m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix a partition
\mathcal{A}
of
[n]
. For any vector
v=(v_{A},\,A\in\mathcal{A})
, with
v_{A}\in(-1,1)
, for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists a unique choice of
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
such that

m_{\mathcal{A}}(\mu_{J,h})=v
.

Proof.

We adapt the argument from Lemma 1 in
[
10
]
. We need to show that the map

h\mapsto m_{\mathcal{A}}(\mu_{J,h})

is a bijection between
\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
and
(-1,1)^{\mathcal{A}}
. Using the change of variables
\theta_{A}:=\sum_{i\in A}h_{i}
,
A\in\mathcal{A}
, this is equivalent to showing that the map

(2.2)

\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{A}}\;\;\longmapsto\;\;f(\theta):=\nabla\varphi(\theta)\in(-1,1)^{\mathcal{A}}

is a bijection, where

\varphi(\theta)=\log\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}e^{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\theta_{A}s_{A}(\sigma)}\mu_{J}(\sigma)\right)\,,\qquad s_{A}(\sigma):=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}\,.

The Jacobian
\nabla f=\nabla^{2}\varphi
coincides with the covariance matrix of the random vector
(s_{A})_{A\in\mathcal{A}}

when
\sigma
is distributed according to
\mu_{J,h}
, which is easily seen to be strictly positive definite for all
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{A}}
. Therefore,
\varphi
is strictly convex and
f=\nabla\varphi
is injective.
To show that it is also surjective,
it is sufficient to show that the image of
f
,
\mathrm{Im}(f)
, satisfies

\partial\mathrm{Im}(f)\subset\partial(-1,1)^{\mathcal{A}}.

Indeed, suppose by contradiction that there exists
y\in(-1,1)^{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\mathrm{Im}(f)
, and consider the line segment
[f(0),y]
. Since
\mathrm{Im}(f)
is open, this segment must intersect
\partial\mathrm{Im}(f)
at some point
z
. If
\partial\mathrm{Im}(f)\subset\partial(-1,1)^{\mathcal{A}}
, then
z\in\partial(-1,1)^{\mathcal{A}}
, which contradicts the fact that
(-1,1)^{\mathcal{A}}
is convex.

To prove
\partial\mathrm{Im}(f)\subset\partial(-1,1)^{\mathcal{A}}
, take
w\in\partial\mathrm{Im}(f)
. Then there exists a sequence
(\theta_{k})\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}f(\theta_{k})=w
.
By continuity of
f
, the sequence
(\theta_{k})
must be unbounded, since otherwise one could extract a converging subsequence
\theta^{\prime}_{k}\to\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{A}}
which would imply that
w=f(\theta)\in\mathrm{Im}(f)
.
Therefore, there exists a subsequence
(\theta^{\prime}_{k})
and some
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
(\theta^{\prime}_{k})_{A}\to+\infty
or
(\theta^{\prime}_{k})_{A}\to-\infty
.
It follows that

w_{A}=\lim_{j\to\infty}f(\theta^{\prime}_{k})_{A}\in\{-1,1\},

and therefore
w\in\partial(-1,1)^{\mathcal{A}}
.
∎

2.2.
Entropy and stationary states

A distribution
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is called
stationary
if
p\,\circ\,p=p
, where the collision product “
\circ
” is defined in (
1.10
). Equivalently,
p
is stationary iff
p_{t}=p
, for all
t\geq 0
. It follows from the reversibility in Lemma
2.1
that
\mu_{J,h}\circ\mu_{J,h}=\mu_{J,h}
, for any
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, and thus all measures
\mu_{J,h}
,
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, are stationary. To characterize all stationary states we use the relative entropy functional.

The following statement is a version of Boltzmann’s H-theorem from kinetic theory.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
p_{t}
denote the solution of (
1.9
) with initial datum
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, and let
\mu=\mu_{J,h}
for some arbitrary, fixed
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
. Then,

(2.3)

\frac{d}{dt}H(p_{t}\,|\,\mu)=-\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f_{t}),\,\qquad t\geq 0,

where
f_{t}:=p_{t}/\mu
, and the entropy dissipation functional
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
is defined by

(2.4)

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\mu(\tau)\mu(\tau^{\prime})\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\left(f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})-f(\sigma)f(\sigma^{\prime})\right)\log\left(\frac{f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})}{f(\sigma)f(\sigma^{\prime})}\right).

In particular,
H(p_{t}\,|\,\mu)
is non-increasing in
t\geq 0
. Moreover,
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is stationary iff the density
f=p/\mu
satisfies
f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})=f(\sigma)f(\sigma^{\prime})
whenever
\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})>0
.

Proof.

For any
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, setting
f=p/\mu
, and using Lemma
2.1
, for any
\tau\in\Omega
,

(2.5)

p\circ p\,(\tau)-p(\tau)=\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}}\mu(\sigma)\mu(\sigma^{\prime})\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})\left(f(\sigma)f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})\right).

Therefore, differentiating the relative entropy,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}H(p_{t}\,|\,\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}\left[\frac{d}{dt}\,p_{t}(\tau)\right]\log\frac{p_{t}(\tau)}{\mu(\tau)}

(2.7)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\mu(\tau)\mu(\tau^{\prime})\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\left(f_{t}(\tau)f_{t}(\tau^{\prime})-f_{t}(\sigma)f_{t}(\sigma^{\prime})\right)\log f_{t}(\tau).

Symmetrizing first with respect to
\tau\leftrightarrow\tau^{\prime}
and then with respect to
(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\leftrightarrow(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
, Lemma
2.1
shows that the above expression can be rewritten as (
2.3
).
Note that
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f)\geq 0
, for any function
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, since every summand in its definition is nonnegative. Now, if
p
is stationary, then the density
f=p/\mu
satisfies
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f)=0
, and therefore, by positivity of
\mu
,
f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})=f(\sigma)f(\sigma^{\prime})
whenever
\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})>0
.
Conversely, if
f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})=f(\sigma)f(\sigma^{\prime})
whenever
\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\mathcal{K}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime};\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})>0
, then (
2.5
) shows that
p\circ p=p
.
∎

We turn to a characterization of the stationary states. Let
\mathcal{P}_{+}(\Omega)
denote the set of positive distributions, that is the set of
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
such that
p(\sigma)>0
for all
\sigma\in\Omega
. Clearly,
\mu_{J,h}\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(\Omega)
for any
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Lemma 2.4

(Stationary measures)
.

A probability measure
p\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(\Omega)

is stationary if and only if
p=\mu_{J,h}
for some
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Proof.

We already know that
\mu_{J,h}
is stationary, for any
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
. It remains to show that these are the only positive stationary distributions.
Fix
\mu=\mu_{J,h}
for some
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, and let
p\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(\Omega)

be a stationary measure. From Lemma
2.3
,
f=p/\mu
satisfies

(2.8)

f(\sigma)f(\sigma^{\prime})=f(S_{\ell}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}_{k}))f(S_{k}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma_{\ell})),

for all
\ell,k\in[n]
such that
\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)>0
, for all
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Omega
.
For any
\ell
, fix some
k=k(\ell)\in[n]
such that
\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)>0
.
For any
\sigma\in\Omega
and any
j\in[n]
, let
\sigma[j]\in\Omega
denote the configuration equal to
+1
on all sites
\{1,\dots,j\}
and equal to
\sigma
on
\{j+1,\dots,n\}
. Setting
\sigma[0]=\sigma
, we have:

f(\sigma)=f(+)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{f(\sigma[j-1])}{f(\sigma[j])},

where
\sigma[n]=+
is the all
+
configuration.
Define
\varphi_{j}(a):=\frac{f(+^{k(j),a})}{f(+)}
, where
+^{k,a}
denotes the configuration equal to
a
at site
k
and
+1
elsewhere.
Taking
\sigma=\sigma[j-1]
,
\sigma^{\prime}=+
, from (
2.8
) with
j=\ell
and
k=k(j)
,

\frac{f(\sigma[j-1])}{f(\sigma[j])}=\frac{f(+^{k(j),\sigma_{j}})}{f(+)}=\varphi_{j}(\sigma_{j}).

Since
\varphi_{j}
is a positive function on
\{-1,+1\}
, we can write
\varphi_{j}(a)=c_{j}e^{h_{j}a}
,
a=\pm 1
, for some constants
c_{j}>0,h_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. It follows that

f(\sigma)=C\exp\left(\textstyle{\sum_{j\in[n]}}h_{j}\sigma_{j}\right),

for some
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where
C>0
is a constant.
It remains to prove that
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
. To see this, note that by (
2.8
), whenever
\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)>0
one has

(2.9)

(h_{\ell}-h_{k})(\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\sigma_{\ell})=0\,,

for all
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
. It follows that
h_{\ell}=h_{k}
if
\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)>0
. Therefore
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
.
∎

2.3.
Conservation laws

Recall the Definition
1.4
of the magnetization profile
m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)=(m(p,A),A\in\mathcal{A})
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For all
p,q\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
,

(2.10)

m_{\mathcal{A}}(p\circ q)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,m_{\mathcal{A}}(q).

In particular, if
p_{t}
is the solution of (
1.9
) with initial datum
p
, then

\frac{d}{dt}m_{\mathcal{A}}(p_{t})=0

Proof.

By definition,

m(p\circ q,A)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{i\in A}p\circ q\,[\tau_{i}],

where

p\circ q\,[\tau_{i}]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}p(\sigma)q(\sigma^{\prime})\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{K}}_{J}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})+\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{K}}_{J}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma;\tau,\tau^{\prime})\right)\tau_{i}.

Moreover,

\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{K}}_{J}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})\,\tau_{i}=\sigma_{i}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}(i,k)p_{J}(i,k\,|\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})(\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\sigma_{i}).

Therefore,

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{K}}_{J}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};\tau,\tau^{\prime})+\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{K}}_{J}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma;\tau,\tau^{\prime})\right)\tau_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{i}+\sigma^{\prime}_{i})+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}R_{i,k}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})

where

R_{i,k}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\mathcal{K}(i,k)\left[p_{J}(i,k\,|\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})(\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\sigma_{i})-p_{J}(k,i\,|\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})(\sigma^{\prime}_{i}-\sigma_{k})\right],

and we have used the symmetries
p_{J}(i,k\,|\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=p_{J}(k,i\,|\,\sigma^{\prime},\sigma)
.
Since
\mathcal{K}(i,k)=\mathcal{K}(k,i)
,
R_{i,k}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
is antisymmetric in the exchange
i\leftrightarrow k
. Therefore, for any fixed
A\subset[n]
,

\sum_{i,k\in A}R_{i,k}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=0.

In particular, using that
\mathcal{K}(i,k)=0
when
i,k
do not belong to the same irreducible component, this proves that for any fixed
A\in\mathcal{A}
,

\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{i\in A}p\circ q\,[\tau_{i}]=\frac{1}{2}m(p,A)+\frac{1}{2}m(q,A)\,.

∎

2.4.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

The key to the proof of Theorem
1.5

is the following statement about positivity of solutions, that we refer to as the irreducibility lemma. Recall the notation
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
for the set of
\mathcal{A}
-regular distributions.

Lemma 2.6

(Irreducibility)
.

For any
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
, there exists
t_{0}>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
t\geq t_{0}
the solution
p_{t}
of (
1.9
) satisfies

(2.11)

p_{t}(\tau)\geq\varepsilon_{0},\qquad\forall\tau\in\Omega.

The proof requires several steps and is given in the next subsections. Here we show how to prove Theorem
1.5

once we have Lemma
2.6
.
If
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
, then Lemma
2.6
ensures that
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(\Omega)
eventually. Therefore, if
p

The strategy is based on ideas introduced in

The proof is broken down into several steps. We start with a graphical representation needed to take care of the continuous time nature of the process.

2.8.
Marked Partition Process

We prove Lemma
2.10
by deriving an explicit representation of the distribution
\Phi^{0}_{u}(p_{1},\dots,p_{2^{u}})
via a suitable discrete time fragmentation process, augmented with randomly walking labels, which we call the
Marked Partition Process
(MPP). Let
X=\{0\}\cup[n]
denote the space of marks. At each time
u\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the marked partition process takes a value
\mathcal{C}^{u}
of the form

\mathcal{C}^{u}=(C_{1}^{u},\dots,C_{2^{u}}^{u})
, with each fragment
C_{i}^{u}
given by

is called the

is called the

. The spatial parts are such that
\{A^{u}_{i},\,i=1,\dots,2^{u}\}
form a partition of
[n]
, for each
u\in{\mathbb{N}}
, that is
A^{u}_{i}\cap A^{u}_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
and
\cup_{i}A^{u}_{i}=[n]
(the sets
A^{u}_{i}
can be empty).
To define the evolution of the process we introduce some more notation. For any
x\in[n]
we let

denote the Markov chain with initial state
x
and transition matrix
\mathcal{K}
. Similarly, we let

, for
u\in\mathbb{N}
, be the Markov chain with initial state
x\in[n]
, and transition matrix given by the lazy kernel

\bar{\mathcal{K}}=\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{K}+\frac{n-1}{n}I,

where

is the identity

\mathcal{C}^{0},\mathcal{C}^{1},\dots
is defined as follows.
At time
0
, we start with the single fragment
\mathcal{C}^{0}=(C_{1}^{0})
, where
C_{1}^{0}=([n],0)
. Recursively, the process is defined by the following splitting and marking rules.
If
\mathcal{C}^{u}=(C_{1}^{u},\dots,C_{2^{u}}^{u})
, then, independently for every
i=1,\dots,2^{u}
, sample a uniform random variable
U\in[n]
and a uniform random variable

, and set
\mathcal{C}^{u+1}=(C_{1}^{u+1},\dots,C_{2^{u+1}}^{u+1})

(1)

if

, then
(C_{2i-1}^{u+1},C_{2i}^{u+1})=(C_{i}^{u},(\emptyset,0))
,

(2)

if

(C_{2i-1}^{u+1},C_{2i}^{u+1})=((\emptyset,0),C_{i}^{u})
,

(3)

if

,

and

, then
(C_{2i-1}^{u+1},C_{2i}^{u+1})=((A\setminus\{U\},0),(\{U\},X^{U}(1))
,

(4)

if
B=3
,
C_{i}^{u}=(A,0)
and

, then
(C_{2i-1}^{u+1},C_{2i}^{u+1})=((A,0),(\emptyset,0))
,

(5)

if
B=3
,

,

, then
(C_{2i-1}^{u+1},C_{2i}^{u+1})=((\{i\},Y^{x}(1)),(\emptyset,0))

(6)

, repeat the

.

We remark that, since we start with
\mathcal{C}^{0}=(C_{1}^{0})
, where
C_{1}^{0}=([n],0)
, the above rules are such that a nonzero mark can only be assigned to a spatial part consisting of a single site. Thus, the above rules
yield a well defined evolution. Note that, by construction, the spatial parts are such that
(A^{u}_{i},\,i=1,\dots,2^{u})
forms a partition of
[n]
, at any time
u
. We remark that the partition
(A^{u}_{i})
is monotone in the sense that it can only become finer with time, until it consists only of empty sets or single sites with nonzero marks. One can define the
fragmentation time

(2.29)

H=\min\left\{u\in\mathbb{N}:\text{all nonempty sets have nonzero marks in }\mathcal{C}^{u}\right\}.

For each time
u
, there is at most one spatial part

which can have cardinality larger than

, and, with probability
1/2
it loses one element every time the uniform random variable
U\in[n]
belongs to it. Therefore, the erosion of the largest spatial part can be described by a simple coupon collecting process, where at each step, with probability
1/2
, a coupon is chosen uniformly at random, while with probability
1/2
no coupon is collected. The fragmentation time
H
coincides with the first time where
n

(2.30)

\mathbb{P}(H\geq u)\leq n\,\left(1-\tfrac{1}{2n}\right)^{u}\leq n\,e^{-u/2n}\,.

We note that there is a natural representation of the Marked Partition Process in terms of a regular binary tree. Namely, we can assign to each node of the rooted regular binary tree with depth
u\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\Gamma_{u}=\{\emptyset\}\cup_{k=1}^{u}\{0,1\}^{k}

C_{1}^{0}=([n],0)
, and if the nodes with depth

(C_{1}^{k},\dots,C_{2^{k}}^{k})

(C_{1}^{k+1},\dots,C_{2^{k+1}}^{k+1})
, with the pair
(C_{2i-1}^{k+1},C_{2i}^{k+1})

C_{i}^{k}
. In this way, starting from the root, the marks along any path is always

until the first time where it takes some value

, after which it evolves, along the unique branch with nonempty fragments, with marks given the lazy Markov chain
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
. In particular, any marked set of the form

.

\Phi^{0}_{u}(p_{1},\dots,p_{2^{u}})

For any

, and any realization of

, for any vector

by the following rule. Given

and some nonzero mark

. For any

,

,

\psi(p,C_{i}^{u})(\tau)=\begin{cases}p_{A}(\tau_{A})&\text{ if }C_{i}^{u}=(A,0)\\

\{x\}
of the measure
p

Note that
\Psi(\vec{p}(u),\mathcal{C}^{u})\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, for any

In words,

Lemma 2.11
.

For any
u\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\tau\in\Omega
, any

,

(2.33)

\Phi^{0}_{u}(p_{1},\dots,p_{2^{u}})={\mathbb{E}}\left[\Psi(\vec{p}(u),\mathcal{C}^{u})(\tau)\right]\,,

where the expectation is over the MPP.

Proof.

We use induction over
u
. Note that it is true at

is given by the single marked set

and
\Phi^{0}_{u}(p)=p
. Let us check that for all

,

\Phi^{0}_{1}(p,q)(\tau)=p\circ q\,(\tau)={\mathbb{E}}[\psi(p,C_{1}^{1})(\tau)\psi(q,C_{2}^{1})(\tau)]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\Psi(p,q,\mathcal{C}^{1})(\tau)\right]\,,

which is the identity (
2.33
) for

.
To see this, note that
by definition of the collision kernel
\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{\mathcal{A}}
, in the case

one has

\displaystyle(p\,\circ\,q)(\tau)=\frac{1}{4}\,p(\tau)+\frac{1}{4}\,q(\tau)\;+

(2.35)

\displaystyle\;\;\;+\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{\ell,k\in[n]}\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)\left[p_{[n]\setminus\{\ell\}}(\tau_{[n]\setminus\{\ell\}})\,q_{k}(\tau_{\ell})+p_{k}(\tau_{\ell})\,q_{[n]\setminus\{\ell\}}(\tau_{[n]\setminus\{\ell\}})\right],

where
p_{A}
denotes the marginal of
p
over a subset

, and we use
p_{k}
for the marginal of
p
over a single site
\{k\}
.
Points (1) and (2) in the definition of the MPP take care of the terms

and
\frac{1}{4}\,q
in (

Moreover, points (3)-(4)-(5) provide the term

\frac{1}{4}\,p_{[n]\setminus\{U\}}(\tau_{[n]\setminus\{U\}})\,q_{X^{U}(1)}(\tau_{U})

in (

\frac{1}{4}\,p_{X^{U}(1)}(\tau_{U})\,q_{[n]\setminus\{U\}}(\tau_{[n]\setminus\{U\}})

).
Taking the expectation over

yields (

).

Reasoning in the same way, one obtains, for any realization of

, for all
\tau

(2.36)

\displaystyle\psi(p\circ q,C^{u}_{i})\,(\tau)={\mathbb{E}}\left[\psi(p,C^{u+1}_{2i-1})(\tau)\psi(q,C^{u+1}_{2i})(\tau)\,|\,C^{u}_{i}\right],

where

denotes the expectation over one step of the MPP

Assuming the validity of (

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Psi(\vec{p}(u+1),\mathcal{C}^{u+1})(\tau)\,|\,\mathcal{C}^{s},\,s\leq u\right]

\displaystyle\;=\prod_{i=1}^{2^{u}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\psi(p_{2i-1},C_{2i-1}^{u+1})(\tau)\psi(p_{2i},C_{2i}^{u+1})(\tau)\,|\,\mathcal{C}^{s},\,s\leq u\right]=\Psi(\vec{q}(u),\mathcal{C}^{u})(\tau)\,.

Taking the expectation, and using the inductive assumption, one finds

Since

2.9.
Proof of Lemma
2.10

Once the representation in terms of the MPP is established we turn to the proof of Lemma

. To prove (

), we observe that for any
u_{0}<u
, using Lemma

, Jensen’s inequality and the fact that
\{H\leq u_{0}\}
is measurable with respect to

, one has

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{u}^{0}(p_{1},\dots,p_{2^{u}})-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq{\mathbb{P}}(H>u_{0})+

(2.38)

\displaystyle\qquad+\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Psi(p_{1},\dots,p_{2^{u}},\mathcal{C}^{u})\,|\,\mathcal{C}^{\leq u_{0}}\right]-\pi\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}};H\leq u_{0}\right]\,.

We take
u_{0}=u_{0}(\varepsilon,n)
such that
n\,e^{-u_{0}/2n}\leq\varepsilon/2
, so that by (
2.30
) the first term above is at most
\varepsilon/2
.
To estimate the second term, the key observation is that conditionally on
\mathcal{C}^{\leq u_{0}}
, on the event
H\leq u_{0}
, one has the non-homogeneous product of Bernoulli measures

(2.39)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Psi(p_{1},\dots,p_{2^{u}},\mathcal{C}^{u})\,|\,\mathcal{C}^{\leq u_{0}}\right]=\textstyle{\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}}{\rm Be}(\xi_{j})\,,

where, for each
j\in[n]
, the parameter
\xi_{j}=\xi_{j}(p_{1},\dots,p_{2^{u}},\mathcal{C}^{\leq u_{0}})
is obtained as follows. Let
C_{i_{*}(j)}^{u}=(\{j\},x_{*}(j))
denote the unique marked set with spatial part given by
\{j\}
, and let
W_{j}=p_{i_{*}(j)}(\sigma_{x_{*}(j)}=+1)
denote the probability of the event
\sigma_{x_{*}(j)}=+1
, when
\sigma
is distributed according to
p_{i_{*}(j)}
. Then

\xi_{j}={\mathbb{E}}\left[W_{j}\,|\,\mathcal{C}^{\leq u_{0}}\right]\,.

Since the total variation distance between two Bernoulli variables is the distance between the parameters, one has

\left\|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Psi(p_{1},\dots,p_{2^{u}},\mathcal{C}^{u})\,|\,\mathcal{C}^{\leq u_{0}}\right]-\pi\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq n\,\max_{j}|\xi_{j}-\alpha_{j}|\,.

An important feature is that the random site
x_{*}(j)
, conditionally on
\mathcal{C}^{\leq u_{0}}
, on the event
H\leq u_{0}
, has the distribution of
Z^{j}(u-u_{*}(j))
, where

denotes the first time the site

receives a nonzero mark in the MPP, and

stands for the distribution after
k

\widehat{}\mathcal{K}:=\frac{1}{2}I+\frac{1}{2}\bar{}\mathcal{K}
, with initial state

. On the other hand we note that the parameter

, by our assumption

for all

, satisfies
\alpha_{j}={\mathbb{E}}[p_{i}(\sigma_{y_{*}(j)}=+1)]

, where

is distributed uniformly in the irreducible component

containing

. Call
u(j)
the uniform distribution over

. As a consequence, we may estimate the difference

by using the optimal coupling between the chain with transition
\widehat{}\mathcal{K}
and the uniform distribution over

. Since

, we can estimate

(2.40)

\displaystyle\max_{j}|\xi_{j}-\alpha_{j}|\leq\max_{j}\|\widehat{}\mathcal{K}^{u-u_{0}}(j,\cdot)-u(j)\|_{\rm TV}\,.

By elementary mixing time theory, for any
\varepsilon>0

such that the above is at most

3.
Relative entropy decay for the particle system

This section is concerned with the proof of Theorem
1.10
. We start with a version of that theorem that covers the case of the mean field exchange dynamics in Example
1.2
, that is for the kernel
\mathcal{K}(\ell,n)\equiv 1/n
, and then turn to the proof for the general multi-component case in Definition
1.3
. A key intermediate step in these proofs will be a modified log-Sobolev inequality for a version of the so-called Down-Up walk for the canonical Ising measure.

3.1.
Mean field exchange dynamics

Here we state the main result for the kernel
\mathcal{K}(\ell,n)\equiv 1/n
. In order to prepare for later applications to multi-component systems we need to extend the setup from Section
1.5
to include arbitrary external fields. Thus, for
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Nn}
, we write
h=(h(i),i\in[N])
, and
h(i)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for all
i\in[N]
, and define

(3.1)

\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}:=\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{J,h(i)}(\cdot\,|\,\Omega_{N,\rho}),

the measure obtained by taking products of
\mu_{J,h(i)}
and then restricting to
\Omega_{N,\rho}
.
Recall the notation
\sigma^{i,\ell;j,k}
for the configuration
\sigma\in\Omega^{N}
obtained by exchanging the spins
\sigma_{\ell}(i)
and
\sigma_{k}(j)
; see (
1.22
). We use the notation

(3.2)

r_{J,h,i,j}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(i),\sigma(j))=\frac{\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}(\sigma^{i,\ell;j,k})}{\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}(\sigma)+\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}(\sigma^{i,\ell;j,k})}\,.

Note that, because of the product structure, this quantity depends on
\sigma
only through
\sigma(i)
and
\sigma(j)
. Moreover, it coincides with (
1.20
) when
h\equiv 0
. We define the Dirichlet form

(3.3)

\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{N,J,h,\rho}(F,G):=\frac{1}{2Nn}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell,k=1}^{n}\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}\left[r_{J,h,i,j}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(i),\sigma(j))\nabla^{i,\ell;j,k}F\nabla^{i,\ell;j,k}G\right],

for functions
F,G:\Omega_{N,\rho}\to\mathbb{R}
. As usual, we let

\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}}(F)=\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}\left[F\log(F/\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}[F])\right]

denote the entropy of
F\geq 0
with respect to
\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Nn}
, let
J\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be a nonnegative definite matrix with largest eigenvalue
\lambda(J)<1/2
, and let

\overline{J}=\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n]}|J_{i,j}|
,
\overline{h}=\max_{\ell\in[n],i\in[N]}|h_{\ell}(i)|
.
For any
N\geq 2
, and
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A},N}
,

(3.4)

\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{N,J,h,\rho}(F,\log F)\geq\alpha\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}}(F),

for all
F\geq 0
, with constant
\alpha
such that

(3.5)

\alpha\geq\tfrac{1}{4}(1-2\lambda(J))\,e^{-8(\overline{J}+\overline{h})}.

We note that Theorem
3.1
implies Theorem
1.10
in the special case
\mathcal{K}(\ell,n)\equiv 1/n
, since
(1-2\lambda(J))\leq 1
and for this choice of kernel, taking
h\equiv 0
, one has

\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{N,J,0,\rho}(F,G)=n\mathcal{E}_{N,J,\rho}(F,G)\,,

where
\mathcal{E}_{N,J,\rho}(F,G)
is given in (
1.25
) with
\mathcal{K}(\ell,n)\equiv 1/n
.
The proof of Theorem
3.1
will be derived as a consequence of the following more general estimate for mean field exchange systems.

3.2.
Down-Up Walk for Conservative Particle Systems

We introduce some new notation. Consider a generic Ising spin system, with
L
spins, for some
L\in\mathbb{N}
, with interaction given by an
L\times L
symmetric matrix
\Lambda
and with external field
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
, that is

(3.6)

\mu_{\Lambda,w}(\eta)=\frac{1}{Z_{\Lambda,w}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\langle\eta,\Lambda\eta\rangle+\langle\eta,w\rangle\right),\qquad\eta\in\{-1,1\}^{L}\,,

where
\langle\eta,\Lambda\eta\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{L}\eta_{i}\Lambda_{i,j}\eta_{j}
and
\langle\eta,w\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{L}w_{i}\eta_{i}
. Given an integer
M\in\{-L,\dots,L\}
, consider the state space

(3.7)

\Omega_{L,M}=\left\{\eta\in\{-1,1\}^{L},\ \textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{L}}\eta_{i}=M\right\}.

We will assume that
L+M
is even, so that
\Omega_{L,M}
is not empty.
We view a configuration
\eta\in\Omega_{L,M}
as composed of
K
points in the set
[L]=\{1,\dots,L\}
, corresponding to the sites
i
where
\eta_{i}=+1
. Thus,
\eta
is a configuration of
K=(L+M)/2
elements from
[L]
, which we view as sites occupied by “balls”. The remaining sites are such that
\eta_{i}=-1
and will be seen as empty or “holes”. The Down-Up Walk on
\Omega_{L,M}
for the Ising system
\mu_{\Lambda,w}
is the continuous-time Markov chain where a ball at site
i
, with rate 1 is moved to a hole at
j
, chosen among all possible vacant locations (including going back to
i
itself) with probability proportional to
\mu(\eta^{i,j})
, where
\eta\to\eta^{i,j}
is the configuration obtained by swapping the spins values at sites
i
and
j
. Using the notation
\nabla^{i,j}F(\eta)=F(\eta^{i,j})-F(\eta)
, the infinitesimal generator of the process is

(3.8)

\mathcal{L}^{\rm du}F(\eta)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{L}c^{\rm du}(\eta,\eta^{i,j})\nabla^{i,j}F(\eta),

with
F:\Omega_{L,M}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, and
c^{\rm du}(\eta,\eta^{i,j})
is the transition rate

(3.9)

c^{\rm du}(\eta,\eta^{i,j})=\mathbf{1}(\eta_{i}=+1,\eta_{j}=-1)\frac{\mu_{\Lambda,w}(\eta^{i,j})}{\sum_{k\in U_{i}(\eta)}\mu_{\Lambda,w}(\eta^{ik})},

where
U_{i}(\eta)=\{j\in[L]:\eta_{j}=-1\}\cup\{i\}
denotes the set of available sites. This generator defines a conservative dynamics, reversible with respect to
\mu_{\Lambda,w}
.
It is not hard to check that, for any
M
such that
L+M
is even, the process is irreducible in the state space
\Omega_{L,M}
, and the restricted measure

(3.10)

\nu_{L,M}=\mu_{\Lambda,w}\left(\cdot\,|\,\Omega_{L,M}\right)

is reversible, and thus the unique invariant measure. Let
\mathcal{E}^{\rm du}(F,G)
denote the associated Dirichlet form,

(3.11)

\mathcal{E}^{\rm du}(F,G)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{L}\nu_{L,M}\left[c^{\rm du}(\eta,\eta^{i,j})\nabla^{i,j}F(\eta)\nabla^{i,j}G(\eta)\right]\,,

for
F,G:\Omega_{L,M}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Let also
{\rm Ent}_{\nu_{L,M}}(F)
denote the entropy with respect to the canonical Ising measure
\nu_{L,M}
.
The key to prove Theorem
3.1
will be the following MLSI for the Down-Up walk.

Theorem 3.2

(MLSI for the Down-Up walk)
.

Suppose that
\Lambda
is a nonnegative definite matrix with largest eigenvalue
\lambda(\Lambda)<1/2
. Then for all values of
L,M
, with
L+M
even, for all
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
,

\mathcal{E}^{\rm du}(F,\log F)\geq(1-2\lambda(\Lambda))\,\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu_{L,M}}(F)\,,

for every
F:\Omega_{L,M}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

It is crucial that the estimate is uniform over the parameters
L,M
and the external fields
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
. The above theorem can be proven using the arguments in Theorem 2.2 and Lemma 2.7 in
[
4
]
. Moreover, following
[
4
]
it can be shown that if the largest eigenvalue of
\Lambda
is associated to a constant eigenvector, then one can impose the condition
\lambda(\Lambda)<1/2
on the second largest eigenvalue. This is natural, in light of the global constraint
\sum_{i}\eta_{i}=M
. Indeed, if e.g.
\Lambda_{i,j}\equiv c
, then the canonical measure
\nu_{L,M}
becomes uniform and one might as well take
\Lambda=0
. However, for the applications to our setting, the removal of the eigenvalue associated to constant eigenfunction will not be relevant. Below, we prove Theorem
3.2
with a slightly different argument, which will allow us to introduce the relevant tools to prove the more general statements for multi-component mean field exchange models, which are not covered by the results in
[
4
]
. Our proof will follow the factorization technique developed in
[
13
]
, which in turn was based on the stochastic localization approach from
[
21
]
.
The proof of Theorem
3.2
will be given in the following subsections. Here we observe that it implies Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
assuming Theorem
3.2
.

Let
L=Nn
, where
N
is the number of particles and
n
the number of spins per particle. We use the correspondence

(3.12)

\eta\in\{-1,1\}^{Nn}\longrightarrow\sigma=(\sigma(i),i=1,\dots,N),\quad\sigma(i)=(\eta_{u},\,u=n(i-1)+1,\dots,ni),

that is the spins in the block of sites
\{(i-1)n+1,\dots,ni\}
are associated with the
i
-th particle
\sigma(i)=(\sigma_{1}(i),\dots,\sigma_{n}(i))
.
The
L\times L
matrix
\Lambda
and the
L
-vector
w
are taken such that

(3.13)

\langle\eta,\Lambda\eta\rangle=\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{N}}\langle\sigma(i),J\sigma(i)\rangle,\qquad\langle\eta,w\rangle=\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{N}}\langle\sigma(i),h(i)\rangle

where the scalar products on the left hand side are over
{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
while each of the scalar products on the right hand side is over
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. In this way, taking
M=(2\rho-1)Nn
, the canonical measure
\nu_{L,M}
in (
3.10
) coincides with the probability measure
\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}
. Since
\Lambda
consists of
N
blocks given by the
n\times n
matrix
J
, one has
\lambda(\Lambda)=\lambda(J)
.
Therefore, to prove (
3.4
) it remains to establish the comparison

(3.14)

\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{N,J,h,\rho}(F,\log F)\geq\tfrac{1}{4}\,e^{-8(\overline{J}+\overline{h})}\mathcal{E}^{\rm du}(F,\log F),

for all nonnegative functions
F
. To prove this, observe that, if
M\leq 0
, that is
\rho\leq 1/2
, then
|U_{i}(\eta)|\geq L/2=Nn/2
, for all
\eta\in\Omega_{L,M}
, and therefore

\displaystyle c^{\rm du}(\eta,\eta^{i,j})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{Nn}\,e^{8(\overline{J}+\overline{h})}\frac{\mu_{\Lambda,w}(\eta^{i,j})}{\mu_{\Lambda,w}(\eta)+\mu_{\Lambda,w}(\eta^{i,j})}

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{Nn}\,e^{8(\overline{J}+\overline{h})}\,r_{J,h,C_{i},C_{j}}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(C_{i}),\sigma(C_{j}))\,,

where
C_{i},C_{j}\in[N]
, and
\ell,k\in[n]
, denote the labels such that
\sigma_{\ell}(C_{i})=\eta_{i}
and
\sigma_{k}(C_{j})=\eta_{j}
in the correspondence (
3.12
). Using this pointwise estimate in (
3.11
) we obtain (
3.14
). This ends the proof in the case
M\leq 0
. The case
M\geq 0
can be handled in the same way with the change of variable
\eta\mapsto-\eta
. Indeed, this global flip changes the measure from
\mu_{N,J,h,\rho}
to
\mu_{N,J,-h,1-\rho}
. Thus we may apply the previous argument to the new variables.
This ends the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
3.2

Let
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a standard Brownian motion in
{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
, adapted to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and set
\tilde{W}_{t}=\Lambda^{1/2}W_{t}
. The stochastic localization process for the canonical Ising measure
\nu_{L,M}
is given by

(3.16)

\varrho_{t}(\eta)=\frac{1}{Z_{t}}\exp\left(-\frac{t-1}{2}\langle\eta,\Lambda\eta\rangle+\langle y_{t}+w,\eta\rangle\right)\mathbf{1}(\eta\in\Omega_{L,M}),\quad t\geq 0\,,

where
Z_{t}
is the normalization so that
\varrho_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{L,M})
, and the random field
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{L},t\geq 0
evolves according to the stochastic differential equation

(3.17)

dy_{t}=d\tilde{W}_{t}+\Lambda m(y_{t})\,dt,\qquad y_{0}=0,

with
m(y_{t})
given by the mean vector
m(y_{t}):={\varrho_{t}}[\eta]
, that is for all
i\in[L]
,

(3.18)

[m(y_{t})]_{i}={\varrho_{t}}[\eta_{i}]=\textstyle{\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{L,M}}}\eta_{i}\varrho_{t}(\eta).

That the process is well defined can be checked by standard methods. Moreover,
(\varrho_{t},t\geq 0)
is an
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
-martingale and therefore
\nu_{L,M}={\mathbb{E}}[\varrho_{t}]
for all
t\geq 0
, where
{\mathbb{E}}
denotes the expectation with respect to the stochastic localization process. We refer to
[
21
]
; see also Lemma 3.1 in
[
13
]
.

At time
t=1
, the measure (
3.16
) is a random product distribution restricted to
\Omega_{L,M}
,

(3.19)

\varrho_{1}(\eta)\propto\prod_{i=1}^{L}\exp\left(y_{1,i}\eta_{i}+w_{i}\eta_{i}\right)\,\mathbf{1}(\eta\in\Omega_{L,M}),

where
y_{1,i}
denotes the
i
-th component of the vector
y_{1}
.
That is,
\varrho_{1}
is a Bernoulli product measure conditioned on having total magnetization
M
. Such measures are known to satisfy the Strong Rayleigh property; see
[
8
]
. This implies strong log-concavity of its generating polynomial, which in turn yields a modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality (MLSI) for the associated Down-Up walk
[
22
]
; see also
[
1
,
4
]
. We shall actually establish a stronger estimate, namely entropy factorization. This allows us to use the arguments in
[
13
]
which yield a simple proof of Theorem
3.2
.

To introduce the factorization estimate, we formulate the Down-Up walk for an arbitrary measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{L,M})
as follows.
As above, we say that there is a ball at
j\in[L]
if
\eta_{j}=+1
. Thus, every
\eta\in\Omega_{L,M}
has
K:=(L+M)/2
balls. According to the distribution
\mu
, balls are not distinguished. However, we artificially distinguish them by taking a uniformly random labeling of the
K
balls, and write
X_{k}\in[L]
for the position of the
k
-th ball. With slight abuse of notation we call again
\mu
the induced distribution of the vector
X:=(X_{1},\dots,X_{K})
.
Let
\mu_{i}=\mu(\cdot\,|\,X_{j},\,j\neq i)
denote the distribution of
X_{i}
conditionally on the position of all remaining balls. Now, it is not hard to see that the Down-Up walk for the original measure
\mu
, defined by the rates (
3.9
) with
\mu
in place of
\mu_{\Lambda,w}
, coincides with the Markov chain where with rate
1
independently each variable
X_{i}
is updated by replacing it with a sample from
\mu_{i}
, provided we observe the chain on functions that are symmetric under permutations of the ball labels. Thus, letting
\mathcal{S}
be the set of symmetric functions
X\mapsto F(X)
, the generator of the chain becomes

(3.20)

\mathcal{L}^{\rm du}F(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}(\mu_{i}[F](X)-F(X)),

for any
F\in\mathcal{S}
,
where
\mu_{i}[F](X)=\mu(F\,|\,X_{j},\,j\neq i)
is the conditional expectation of
F
given all
X_{j},\,j\neq i
. It follows that the Dirichlet form is given by

(3.21)

\mathcal{E}^{\rm du}(F,G)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu\left[{\rm Cov}_{\mu_{i}}(F,G)\right],

for any
F,G\in\mathcal{S}
, where
{\rm Cov}_{\mu_{i}}(F,G)=\mu_{i}[FG]-\mu_{i}[F]\mu_{i}[G]
denotes the covariance with respect to
\mu_{i}
.
Moreover,
let
{\rm Ent}_{\mu_{i}}(F)=\mu_{i}[F\log F]-\mu_{i}[F]\log\mu_{i}[F]
denote the entropy functional with respect to
\mu_{i}
. Note that this is a function of the variables
X_{j},\,j\neq i
and that for any realization of these variables Jensen’s inequality implies

(3.22)

\mathrm{Ent}_{\mu_{i}}F\leq{\rm Cov}_{\mu_{i}}(F,\log F)\,,

for all
F\geq 0
. In light of (
3.21
)-(
3.22
) the following factorization property implies the MLSI for
\mu
with constant
1
. We say that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{L,M})
is strongly log-concave
if its generating polynomial is strongly log-concave; see
[
22
]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{L,M})
is strongly log-concave. Then, for any
F\in\mathcal{S}
,

(3.23)

{\rm Ent}_{\mu}F\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{\mu_{i}}F\right].

Proof.

From
[
22
, Lemma 11]
, or equivalently
[
1
, Theorem 5]
, any strongly log-concave
\mu
satisfies the subadditivity inequality,

(3.24)

{\rm Ent}_{\mu}F\geq\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\rm Ent}_{\mu}F_{i},\qquad F_{i}=\mu[F\,|\,X_{i}]\,,

for any nonnegative symmetric function
F
. Note that by symmetry
{\rm Ent}_{\mu}F_{i}={\rm Ent}_{\mu}F_{1}
for all
i
. Moreover, it is known that the inequality (
3.24
) continues to hold with
\mu
replaced by the conditional distribution
\mu(\cdot\,|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
for any
k=1,\dots,K-1
and any realization of the variables
X_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k
.
The factorization statement (
3.23
) then follows from a simple recursive argument, see e.g.
[
17
, Section 4.1]

∎

Since the measure
\varrho_{1}
in (
3.19
) is strongly log-concave
[
8
]
, we may apply Theorem
3.3
with
\mu=\varrho_{1}
. Taking the expectation with respect to the stochastic localization process,

(3.25)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm Ent}_{\varrho_{1}}F\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varrho_{1}\left[{\rm Ent}_{\varrho_{1,i}}F\right]\right],

where
\varrho_{1,i}=\varrho_{1}(\cdot\,|\,X_{j},\,j\neq i)
.
We would like to turn the statement (
3.25
) into a factorization statement for our original measure
\nu:=\nu_{L,M}={\mathbb{E}}[\varrho_{1}]
.
The first step is a convexity estimate.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any nonnegative symmetric function
F
, for all
i\in[K]
, one has

(3.26)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varrho_{1}\left[{\rm Ent}_{\varrho_{1,i}}F\right]\right]\leq\nu\left[{\rm Ent}_{\nu_{i}}F\right]\,.

Proof.

Using the martingale property
\nu={\mathbb{E}}[\varrho_{1}]
,

(3.27)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varrho_{1}\left[{\rm Ent}_{\varrho_{1,i}}F\right]\right]=\nu[F\log F]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varrho_{1}\left[\varrho_{1,i}F\log\varrho_{1,i}F\right]\right]\,.

We write

(3.28)

\varrho_{1,i}F(X)=\frac{\varrho_{1}(F;X_{\neq i})}{\varrho_{1}(X_{\neq i})},

where we use the notation
X_{\neq i}=(X_{j},\,j\neq i)
,
\varrho_{1}(X_{\neq i})=\sum_{X_{i}}\varrho_{1}(X)
, and
\varrho_{1}(F;X_{\neq i})=\sum_{X_{i}}F(X)\varrho_{1}(X)
, and therefore

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varrho_{1}\left[\varrho_{1,i}F\log\varrho_{1,i}F\right]\right]=\sum_{X_{\neq i}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varrho_{1}(F;X_{\neq i})\log\frac{\varrho_{1}(F;X_{\neq i})}{\varrho_{1}(X_{\neq i})}\right]\,.

Using
\nu={\mathbb{E}}[\varrho_{1}]
, and the joint convexity of the function
(x,y)\mapsto x\log(x/y)
on
(0,\infty)^{2}
, by Jensen’s inequality

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varrho_{1}\left[\varrho_{1,i}F\log\varrho_{1,i}F\right]\right]\geq\sum_{X_{\neq i}}\nu(F;X_{\neq i})\log\frac{\nu(F;X_{\neq i})}{\nu(X_{\neq i})}=\nu\left[\nu_{i}F\log\nu_{i}F\right]\,,

where we use the notation (
3.28
) for
\nu
in place of
\varrho_{1}
. The desired bound (
3.26
) follows from (
3.27
).
∎

Next, we use the so-called entropic stability estimate from
[
21
]
, which allows one to compare relative entropies with respect to
\varrho_{1}
with relative entropies with respect to
\nu
by using a covariance estimate.
Let

(3.29)

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}[\mu](i,j)=\mu[\eta_{i}\eta_{j}]-\mu[\eta_{i}]\mu[\eta_{j}],

denote the
L\times L
covariance matrix of any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{L,M})
, and write
\lambda({\rm Cov}[\mu])
for its largest eigenvalue. If
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
, the tilted measure
\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{L,M})
is defined as

(3.30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu(\eta)\propto\mu(\eta)e^{\langle v,\eta\rangle}\,.

The next lemma is a version of
[
21
, Proposition 39]
; see also
[
13
, Lemma 3.2]
. The proof can be adapted to our conservative setting without modifications.

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose there exists a deterministic function
\kappa:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that
\lambda({\rm Cov}[\mathcal{T}_{v}\rho_{t}])\leq\kappa(t)
a.s., for all
t\in[0,1],
for all
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
.
Then, for all functions
F\geq 0
,

(3.31)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm Ent}_{\rho_{1}}F\right]\geq\exp\left(-\lambda(\Lambda)\int_{0}^{1}\kappa(s)ds\right)\,{\rm Ent}_{\nu}F\,.

We turn to a bound on the covariance. The following was established in
[
21
, Lemma 50]
for the non conservative setting, based on a decomposition introduced in
[
3
]
; see also
[
13
, Lemma 3.4]
. We adapt the proof given in
[
13
]
to the present conservative setting.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\nu=\nu_{L,M}
be the canonical Ising measure (
3.10
) with positive definite interaction matrix
\Lambda

and external fields
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
. If
\lambda(\Lambda)<1/2
, then the largest eigenvalue of
{\rm Cov}[\nu]
satisfies

(3.32)

\displaystyle\lambda({\rm Cov}[\nu])\leq\frac{2}{1-2\lambda(\Lambda)}\,.

Proof.

By gaussian integration, for any
\eta\in\Omega_{L,M}
,

(3.33)

\displaystyle\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\eta,\Lambda\eta\rangle)\propto\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{L}}dy\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle y,\Lambda^{-1}y\rangle+\langle\eta,y\rangle\right),

where the implicit positive constant is independent of
\eta
. Define the function

\displaystyle f(y):=\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle y,\Lambda^{-1}y\rangle+\log\left(Z\left(y+w\right)\right)\right)\,,

where
Z\left(v\right):=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{L,M}}\exp\left(\langle\eta,v\rangle\right)
, for any
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
.
From (
3.33
) we obtain

\displaystyle\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle\propto\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{L}}dyf(y)Z\left(y+w\right)^{-1}\exp\left(\langle\eta,y+w\rangle\right){\bf 1}(\eta\in\Omega_{L,M})\,.

If
\mu
denotes the uniform probability measure over
\Omega_{L,M}
then for all
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
,

\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu(\eta)=Z\left(v\right)^{-1}\exp\left(\langle\eta,v\rangle\right){\bf 1}_{\{\eta\in\Omega_{L,M}\}}\,.

Therefore,
\nu
is given by a mixture of tilted measures
\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu
,

(3.34)

\displaystyle\nu(\eta)=\int\mathcal{T}_{y+w}\mu(\eta)\hat{f}(y)dy\,,

where
\hat{f}:=f/\int dyf(y)
is a probability density on
{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
.
By the law of total covariance,

(3.35)

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}[\nu]

\displaystyle=\int dy\hat{f}(y){\rm Cov}[\mathcal{T}_{y+w}\mu]\,+\mathcal{C}_{\nu}\,,

where
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
stands for the
L\times L
matrix

\mathcal{C}_{\nu}(i,j):=\int\gamma_{i}(y)\gamma_{j}(y)\hat{f}(y)dy-\left(\int\gamma_{i}(y)\hat{f}(y)dy\right)\left(\int\gamma_{i}(y)\gamma_{j}(y)\hat{f}(y)dy\right),

and we use the notation
\gamma_{i}(y):=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{L,M}}\mathcal{T}_{y+w}\mu(\eta)\eta_{i}
,
i\in[L]
.
Next, we observe that
\lambda({\rm Cov}[\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu])\leq 2
for any
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
. Indeed,
\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu
is Strong Rayleigh
[
8
]
, which implies negative correlation, and thus for any
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
, letting
C={\rm Cov}[\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu]
, one
has

\sum_{j}|C_{i,j}|=C_{i,i}-\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}C_{i,j}=2C_{i,i}\,,

for any
i\in[L]
, where we use
C_{i,j}\leq 0
for
i\neq j
and the fact that
\sum_{j}C_{i,j}=0
. Since
\lambda(C)\leq\max_{i}\sum_{j}|C_{i,j}|
and
C_{i,i}\leq 1
for any
i\in[L]
one has the desired bound
\lambda({\rm Cov}[\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu])\leq 2
.

We turn to a bound on the matrix
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
. For any
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
we write

(3.36)

\displaystyle\langle u,\mathcal{C}_{\nu}u\rangle

\displaystyle=\textstyle{\sum_{i,j}}u_{i}u_{j}\mathcal{C}_{\nu}(i,j)={\rm Var}\left(g_{u}(Y)\right)\,,

where
Y
is the
{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
-valued random variable with density
\hat{f}
, and
g_{u}(y):=\sum_{i}u_{i}\gamma_{i}(y)
.
We are going to use the Brascamp-Lieb inequality (Theorem 4.1 in
[
9
]
). Namely, suppose that
-{\rm Hess}\log\hat{f}(y)
is positive definite, with lower bound
a\,{\rm Id}
where
a>0
and
{\rm Id}
is the
L\times L
identity matrix. Then,

(3.37)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\left(g_{u}(X)\right)\leq\frac{1}{a}\int dy\hat{f}(y)\|\nabla g_{u}(y)\|_{2}^{2}\,.

Note that,

\nabla_{i}g_{u}(y)=\sum_{j}u_{j}\frac{\partial\gamma_{j}(y)}{\partial y_{i}}=\sum_{j}u_{i}C_{i,j}\,,

where
C={\rm Cov}[\mathcal{T}_{y+w}\mu]
. As discussed above, one has
\lambda(C)\leq 2
.
Therefore, for all
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
,

\|\nabla g_{u}(y)\|_{2}^{2}=\langle Cu,Cu\rangle\leq 4\langle u,u\rangle\,.

From (
3.37
) we conclude

(3.38)

\displaystyle\langle u,\mathcal{C}_{\nu}u\rangle\leq\frac{4}{a}\langle u,u\rangle\,\,.

It remains to establish the bound
-{\rm Hess}\log\hat{f}(y)\geq a\,{\rm Id}
.
Since
{\rm Hess}\log\left(Z\left(y+v\right)\right)={\rm Cov}[\mathcal{T}_{y+w}\mu]
, one has

{\rm Hess}\log\left(Z\left(y+w\right)\right)\leq 2\,{\rm Id}
. Moreover,
{\rm Hess}\langle y,\Lambda^{-1}y\rangle\geq 2\lambda(\Lambda)^{-1}{\rm Id}
. Therefore we obtain the quadratic form bound

(3.39)

\displaystyle-{\rm Hess}\log\hat{f}(y))={\rm Hess}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\langle y,\Lambda^{-1}y\rangle-\log\left(Z\left(y+v\right)\right)\right)\geq\left(\frac{1}{\lambda(\Lambda)}-2\right){\rm Id}.

Summarizing, we may take
a=(1-2\lambda(\Lambda))/\lambda(\Lambda)
. Since
4/a+2=2/(1-2\lambda(\Lambda))
, the desired conclusion follows from (
3.35
).
∎

We now have all ingredients to complete the proof of Theorem
3.2
. From Proposition
3.6
applied with
\Lambda
replaced by
(1-t)\Lambda
,
t\in[0,1]
, and using the expression (
3.16
) for
\varrho_{t}
we see that the assumption of Proposition
3.5
holds with

\kappa(s)=\frac{2}{1-2(1-s)\lambda(\Lambda)},\quad\forall s\in[0,1].

Integrating,
\int_{0}^{1}\kappa(s)ds=-\frac{1}{\lambda(\Lambda)}\log(1-2\lambda(\Lambda))
. Therefore, Proposition
3.5
implies

(3.40)

\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu}F\leq\frac{1}{1-2\lambda(\Lambda)}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Ent}_{\varrho_{1}}F\right],

for all
F:\Omega_{L,M}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
This, together with (
3.25
) and (
3.26
), proves that

(3.41)

\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu}F\leq\frac{1}{1-2\lambda(\Lambda)}\,\sum_{i=1}^{K}\nu\left[\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu_{i}}F\right],

Using (
3.22
),
it follows from (
3.21
)
that

(3.42)

\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu}F\leq\frac{1}{1-2\lambda(\Lambda)}\,\mathcal{E}^{\rm du}(F,\log F)

This ends the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

3.4.
Multicomponent mean field exchange dynamics

We extend the previous analysis to the case where the system consists of several regions each of which is restricted to have a fixed magnetization. To this end, consider again the probability measure
\mu_{\Lambda,w}
on
\{-1,+1\}^{L}
given in (
3.6
).
Let
\mathcal{B}
denote a partition of
[L]
, that is
\cup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}B=[L]
and
B\cap B^{\prime}=\emptyset
for all
B\neq B^{\prime}
. Let also
\mathcal{M}=(M_{B},\,B\in\mathcal{B})
,
M_{B}\in\{-|B|,\dots,|B|\}
, denote the magnetization vector, and define

\Omega_{L,\mathcal{M}}:=\left\{\eta\in\{-1,+1\}^{L}:\textstyle{\sum_{i\in B}}\eta_{i}=M_{B},\quad\forall B\in\mathcal{B}\right\}\,,

and the multicomponent canonical measure

\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}:=\mu_{\Lambda,w}\left(\cdot\,|\,\Omega_{L,\mathcal{M}}\right)\,.

For every
B\in\mathcal{B}
we define a Down-Up walk with rates

(3.43)

c^{\rm du}_{B}(\eta,\eta^{i,j})=\mathbf{1}(i,j\in B)\mathbf{1}(\eta_{i}=+1,\eta_{j}=-1)\frac{\mu_{\Lambda,w}(\eta^{i,j})}{\sum_{k\in U_{B,i}(\eta)}\mu_{\Lambda,w}(\eta^{ik})},

where
U_{B,i}(\eta)=\{j\in B:\eta_{j}=-1\}\cup\{i\}
.
Consider now the Down-Up walk given by the generator (
3.8
) with rates

c^{\rm du}(\eta,\eta^{i,j}):=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}c^{\rm du}_{B}(\eta,\eta^{i,j}).

It is not hard to check that, for any choice of the magnetization vector
\mathcal{M}
, the process is irreducible in the state space
\Omega_{L,\mathcal{M}}
, with unique reversible and invariant measure
\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}
.
The Dirichlet form is given by

(3.44)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\rm du}_{\mathcal{B}}(F,G)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{i,j\in B}\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}\left[c^{\rm du}_{B}(\eta,\eta^{i,j})\nabla^{i,j}F(\eta)\nabla^{i,j}G(\eta)\right]\,,

for
F,G:\Omega_{L,\mathcal{M}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Let also
{\rm Ent}_{\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}}(F)
denote the entropy with respect to the multi-canonical Ising measure
\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}
.
The multicomponent version of Theorem
3.2
is as follows.

Theorem 3.7

(MLSI for Multicomponent Down-Up walk)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a fixed partition of
[L]
. Suppose that
\Lambda
is a nonnegative definite
L\times L
matrix with largest eigenvalue
\lambda(\Lambda)<1/2
. Then for all values of the vector
\mathcal{M}
, for all
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
,

(3.45)

\mathcal{E}^{\rm du}_{\mathcal{B}}(F,\log F)\geq(1-2\lambda(\Lambda))^{2}\,\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}}(F)\,,

for every
F:\Omega_{L,\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

The key to prove the above theorem is the following factorization statement. For any
B\in\mathcal{B}
let
\nu_{B}=\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}(\cdot\,|\,\eta_{j},j\notin B)
denote distribution inside
B
conditioned on the outside variables, and let

\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu_{B}}(F)=\nu_{B}(F\log F)-\nu_{B}(F)\log\nu_{B}(F)
be the associated entropy functional. Note that
\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu_{B}}(F)
is a function of the outside variables
\eta_{j},j\notin B
.

Proposition 3.8

(Approximate Factorization of Entropy)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.7
, for all
F:\Omega_{L,\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

(3.46)

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}\left[\operatorname{Ent}_{\nu_{B}}(F)\right]\geq(1-2\lambda(\Lambda))\,\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}}(F)\,.

Proof.

The proof can be obtained as in
[
13
, Theorem 2.8]
, which establishes a Shearer-type inequality for multicomponent systems. For the reader’s convenience we sketch the main steps. Consider the measure
\varrho_{t}
defined as in (
3.16
)
with the set
\Omega_{L,M}
replaced by
\Omega_{L,\mathcal{M}}
. A key feature of the stochastic localization process in this case is that the measure
\varrho_{1}
is now a product over
B\in\mathcal{B}
, namely

\varrho_{1}=\bigotimes_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\varrho_{1,B}\,,\qquad\varrho_{1,B}=\mathcal{T}_{y_{1,B}+w_{B}}\mu_{B}\,,

where
\mu_{B}
is the uniform distribution over
\eta\in\{-1,+1\}^{B}
such that
\sum_{j\in B}\eta_{j}=M_{B}
, and
y_{1,B},w_{B}
denote the projection of the
L
-vectors
y_{1},w
onto
B
. This can be seen from (
3.19
) where now
\Omega_{L,M}
is replaced by
\Omega_{L,\mathcal{M}}
.

By the tensorization property of product measures, see e.g.
[
17
]
, one has the factorization

(3.47)

{\rm Ent}_{\varrho_{1}}F\leq\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\varrho_{1}\left[{\rm Ent}_{\varrho_{1,B}}F\right]\,.

Moreover, we may apply the convexity argument in Lemma
3.4
to obtain, for any
B\in\mathcal{B}
,

(3.48)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varrho_{1}\left[{\rm Ent}_{\varrho_{1,B}}F\right]\right]\leq\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}\left[{\rm Ent}_{\nu_{B}}F\right]\,.

At this point we repeat the steps in Proposition
3.5
and Proposition
3.6
. The fact that
the measures
\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu_{B}
,
B\in\mathcal{B}
, are Strong Rayleigh for any
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{B}
implies that their product is also Strong Rayleigh, and thus the same covariance estimates can be used. In conclusion,

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\nu\left[\operatorname{Ent}_{\nu_{B}}(F)\right]\,\geq\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\operatorname{Ent}_{\varrho_{1}}(F)\right]\,\geq\,(1-2\lambda(\Lambda))\,\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}}(F)\,.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

For every
B\in\mathcal{B}
, the measure
\nu_{B}
is of the form of a canonical Ising measure as in (
3.10
) with
L=|B|
,
\Lambda
replaced by
\Lambda_{B}=(\Lambda_{i,j},\,i,j\in B)
, the principal submatrix corresponding to the set
B
, and
w
replaced by some new external field
w(\eta_{B^{c}})=(w_{i}(\eta_{B^{c}}),\,i\in B)
, which depends on the outside variables
\eta_{B^{c}}=(\eta_{j},\,j\notin B)
through

w_{i}(\eta_{B^{c}})=w_{i}+\sum_{j\notin B}\Lambda_{i,j}\eta_{j}\,.

Thus we may apply Theorem
3.2
to this system. Noting that the rates
c^{\rm du}_{B}(\eta,\eta^{i,j})
can be written as in (
3.43
) with
\mu_{\Lambda,w}
replaced by
\nu_{B}
, this gives, for all
B
, uniformly in
\eta_{B^{c}}
,

(3.49)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in B}\nu_{B}\left[c^{\rm du}_{B}(\eta,\eta^{i,j})\nabla^{i,j}F(\eta)\nabla^{i,j}\log F(\eta)\right]\geq\,(1-2\lambda(\Lambda_{B}))\,\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu_{B}}(F)\,.

Summing over
B\in\mathcal{B}
, taking the expectation with respect to
\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}
, and noting that
\lambda(\Lambda_{B})\leq\lambda(\Lambda)
,

(3.50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\rm du}_{\mathcal{B}}(F,\log F)\geq(1-2\lambda(\Lambda))\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\nu_{L,\mathcal{M}}\left[\mathrm{Ent}_{\nu_{B}}(F)\right].

The statement in Theorem
3.7
then follows from Proposition
3.8
.
∎

3.5.
Proof of Theorem
1.10

We apply Theorem
3.7
with the notation given by the correspondence in (
3.12
), and with the following choice of blocks
B
.
Given a partition
\mathcal{A}
of
[n]
, we define the associated partition
\mathcal{B}=(B(A),\,A\in\mathcal{A})
of
[Nn]
where, for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
,

B(A)
is the stripe
[N]\times A
, or more formally

B(A):=\{i\in[Nn]:i=\ell+qn,\ \ell\in A,\ q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\ 1\leq\ell+qn\leq Nn\}.

Note that
\{B(A),A\in\mathcal{A}\}
forms a partition of
[Nn]
, and if
B=B(A)
, then
\eta_{B}
corresponds to the spins
(\sigma_{\ell}(i),\ell\in A,i\in[N])
. Let
\mu
be the canonical Ising measure defined in (
1.24
), where
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A},N}
, and for
B=B(A)
, write
\mu_{B}
for the measure

\mu_{B}=\mu(\cdot\,|\,\sigma_{\ell}(i),\ell\notin A,i\in[N]),

that is
\mu_{B}
is the measure obtained by conditioning on all variables outside of
B
.
The factorization in Proposition
3.8
is equivalent to

(3.51)

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\mu\left[\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu_{B}}(F)\right]\geq(1-2\lambda(\Lambda))\,\mathrm{Ent}_{\mu}(F)\,.

Recalling that
\mathcal{K}
is now given by
\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)=\frac{1}{|A|}
if
\ell,k\in A
and
\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)=0
otherwise, the Dirichlet form
(
1.25
) becomes

(3.52)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N,J,\rho}(F,G)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left[\mathcal{E}_{B(A)}(F,G)\right]\,,

(3.53)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{B(A)}(F,G):=\frac{1}{2N|A|}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell,k\in A}\mu_{B(A)}\left[\hat{p}_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(i),\sigma(j))\nabla^{i,\ell;j,k}F\nabla^{i,\ell;j,k}G\right],

for functions
F,G:\Omega_{N,\rho}\to\mathbb{R}
.
We note that every
\mu_{B}
is of the form (
3.1
), with
(n,J,h)
replaced by
(|A|,J_{A},h_{A})
, where
J_{A}=(J_{i,j},\,i,j\in A)
, and
h_{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N|A|}
is the external field induced by the boundary condition outside of
B
, which is given by
[h_{A}(i)]_{\ell}=\sum_{k\notin A}J_{\ell,k}\sigma_{k}(i)
,
i\in[N]
, for every
\ell\in A
.
Since
\bar{h}_{A}\leq\bar{J}
, and
\lambda(\Lambda)=\lambda(J)\geq\lambda(J_{A})
, for all
A\subset[n]
, by Theorem
3.1
, for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
,

(3.54)

\mathcal{E}_{B(A)}(F,\log F)\geq\tfrac{1}{4}(1-2\lambda(J))\,e^{-16\overline{J}}\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu_{B(A)}}(F),

for all
F\geq 0
.
From (
3.51
) and (
3.14
) we conclude

(3.55)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N,J,\rho}(F,\log F)\geq\tfrac{1}{4n}(1-2\lambda(J))^{2}\,e^{-16\overline{J}}\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu}(F)\,.

This ends the proof of Theorem
1.10
.

4.
Completing Kac’s program

In this section we are going to prove Theorem
1.7
. The main step consists in establishing a suitable nonlinear modified log-Sobolev inequality.

4.1.
Nonlinear MLSI

Consider the quadratic system with kernel
\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{K}}_{J}
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be the corresponding partition of
[n]
. Let also
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
denote the entropy dissipation functional from Lemma
2.3
.

Definition 4.1
.

For all
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, let
\mathcal{S}_{h}
be the set of
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that
\mu_{J,h}[f]=1
and such that
f\mu_{J,h}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
has the same magnetization vector of
\mu_{J,h}
, that is

m_{\mathcal{A}}(\mu_{J,h})=m_{\mathcal{A}}(f\mu_{J,h}).

The system is said to satisfy the Nonlinear MLSI with rate function
\mathcal{I}:\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
if for all
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
,

(4.1)

\mathcal{D}_{\mu_{J,h}}(f)\geq\mathcal{I}(h)\,\mu_{J,h}\left[f\log f\right]\,,\qquad f\in\mathcal{S}_{h}\,.

Recall the notation
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
for the set of probability measures with nondegenerate magnetization, see Definition
1.4
, and recall the uniqueness statement from Lemma
2.2
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Omega)
, let
h=h(m_{\mathcal{A}}(p))
denote the unique
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
such that
m_{\mathcal{A}}(\mu_{J,h})=m_{\mathcal{A}}(p)
, and let
p_{t}
denote the solution of (
1.9
) with initial datum
p
. If the system satisfies the Nonlinear MLSI with rate function
\mathcal{I}
, then

(4.2)

H(p_{t}|\mu_{J,h})\leq e^{-\mathcal{I}(h)t}H(p|\mu_{J,h})\,,\qquad t\geq 0.

Proof.

Let
\mu=\mu_{J,h}
and write
f_{t}=p_{t}/\mu
. From Lemma
2.5
it follows that
f_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
for all
t\geq 0
.
By Lemma
2.3
, and using the Nonlinear MLSI assumption,
the function
\varphi(t):=H(p_{t}|\mu)=\mu[f_{t}\log f_{t}]
satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\,\varphi(t)=-\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f_{t})\leq-\mathcal{I}(h)\,\mu\left[f_{t}\log f_{t}\right]=-\mathcal{I}(h)\varphi(t)\,.

Integrating, we obtain (
4.2
).
∎

To prove Theorem
1.7
it is then sufficient to prove the following
uniform
Nonlinear MLSI.

Theorem 4.3
.

In the setting of Theorem
1.7
, the system satisfies the Nonlinear MLSI with rate function
\mathcal{I}
such that

\mathcal{I}(h)\geq\alpha
, for all
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, where
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{4n}(1-2\lambda(J))^{2}e^{-16\bar{J}}
.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
4.3
. The estimate will be derived using Theorem
1.10
together with Entropic Chaos and Fisher Chaos estimates that are at the heart of Kac’s program. We start with some preliminaries concerning the local central limit theorem.

4.2.
Local Central Limit Theorem and Kac Chaos

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
be a probability measure on
\Omega=\{-1,+1\}^{n}
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be a partition of the set
[n]
. Define the
|\mathcal{A}|
-dimensional vector
M=(M_{A},\,A\in\mathcal{A})
, where

\displaystyle M_{A}=\sum_{\ell\in A}\sigma_{\ell},\qquad A\in\mathcal{A}\,.

Definition 4.4
.

We say that
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is irreducible if for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists an
|\mathcal{A}|
-dimensional vector
S
such that

\nu(M=S)>0
and
\nu(M=S+2\delta_{A})>0
, where

\delta_{A}
denotes the standard unit vector in the
A
-th coordinate.

Let
V_{\nu}^{\mathcal{A}}
denote the
covariance matrix
of the vector
M
,

(4.3)

V_{\nu}^{\mathcal{A}}(A,A^{\prime})=\nu(M_{A}M_{A^{\prime}})-\nu(M_{A})\nu(M_{A^{\prime}}),\qquad A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}.

If
\nu
is irreducible then

\displaystyle\det V_{\nu}^{\mathcal{A}}>0.

Indeed, suppose that
\det V_{\nu}^{\mathcal{A}}=0
. Then there exists
\overline{A}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
M_{\overline{A}}
is almost surely a non-trivial linear combination of
\{M_{A}:A\neq\bar{A}\}
under
\nu
. Consequently, no
S\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}
can exist such that both
S
and
S+2\delta_{A}
have positive probability under
\nu
, yielding a contradiction.
The following is a version of the local central limit theorem; see
[
14
, Proposition 2.2]
for a proof.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
be irreducible, and let
M^{(N)}
be the sum of
N
independent copies of
M
, each distributed according to
\nu
.
Then, there exists a constant
K(\nu)>0
such that for all
N\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\max_{S}\,\left|\,{\mathbb{P}}\big(M^{(N)}=S\big)-\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle z_{N},z_{N}\rangle}}{(2\pi N)^{|\mathcal{A}|/2}\sqrt{\det V^{\mathcal{A}}_{\nu}}}\,\right|\leq\frac{K(\nu)}{(2\pi N)^{|\mathcal{A}|/2+1}},

where
z_{N}
is the
|\mathcal{A}|
-dimensional vector defined as

(4.4)

z_{N}=\frac{(V_{\nu}^{\mathcal{A}})^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(S-\mathbb{E}[M^{(N)}]\right),

and the maximum is taken over all
|\mathcal{A}|
-tuples
S=(S_{A},A\in\mathcal{A})
such that for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
,
S_{A}=2X_{A}-N|A|
for some integer

X_{A}\in\{0,\dots,N|A|\}
.

We apply the above statement with
n
and
\mathcal{A}
fixed, while we take
N
arbitrarily large.
In particular, we use Proposition
4.5
to obtain a lower bound on the probability of the event
\Omega_{N,\rho}
defined in (
1.23
), for suitable sequences
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A},N}
, when the spin configuration
(\sigma(i),\,i\in[N])
is distributed according to the product measure
\nu^{\otimes N}
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
be irreducible and assume that
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A},N}
satisfies the condition

(4.5)

\displaystyle\langle\rho-\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(\nu),\rho-\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(\nu)\rangle=O\left(1/N\right),

where
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(\nu)=(1+m_{\mathcal{A}}(\nu))/2
is the density profile associated to
\nu
.
Then,

\displaystyle\nu^{\otimes N}\left(\Omega_{N,\rho}\right)\geq\frac{c}{N^{|\mathcal{A}|/2}},

where
c=c(\nu)>0
is a constant independent of
N
. In particular,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\nu^{\otimes N}\left(\Omega_{N,\rho}\right)\right)=0.

Proof.

The event
\Omega_{N,\rho}
can be written as
M^{(N)}=S
with
S_{A}=(2\rho(A)-1)N|A|
,
A\in\mathcal{A}
. Moreover, for each
A
, the
A
-component of
\mathbb{E}[M^{(N)}]
is given by
N|A|m_{\mathcal{A}}(\nu)
. The
results follow from Proposition
4.5
by noting that (
4.5
) is equivalent to
\langle z_{N},z_{N}\rangle=O(1)
.
∎

Given
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, it is always possible to find
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A},N}
such that (
4.5
) is satisfied. In fact, choosing

(4.6)

\displaystyle\rho(A)=\frac{1}{N|A|}\lfloor N|A|(1+m(A,\nu))/2\rfloor\,,\qquad A\in\mathcal{A}

one has
\rho(A)-\tfrac{1}{2}(1+m(\nu,A))=O(1/N)
, for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
,
and therefore (
4.5
) actually holds with
O(1/N^{2})
in place of
O(1/N)
.

Definition 4.7

(Canonical tensor product)
.

The choice (
4.6
) of
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A},N}
will be called the canonical density associated to
\nu
. We also define the canonical tensor product associated to
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
as the product measure

\nu^{\otimes N}
conditioned on
\Omega_{N,\rho}
, where
\rho
is the canonical density for
\nu
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\gamma_{N}(\nu)=\nu^{\otimes N}\left(\cdot\,|\,\Omega_{N,\rho}\right)\,.

Kac’s Chaos refers to the fact that marginals of the canonical tensor product converge as
N\to\infty
to independent copies of
\nu
. The next proposition quantifies this statement. For all fixed
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we write
P_{k}\gamma
for the marginal of a measure
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{N})
on the first
k
coordinates
\Omega^{k}
, for
k\leq N
. We refer to
[
14
, Theorem 2.6]
for a proof.

Proposition 4.8

(Kac Chaos)
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
be irreducible.
Then, for all
N\geq k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\|P_{k}\gamma_{N}(\nu)-\nu^{\otimes k}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{Ck}{N},

for some constant
C=C(\nu)
.

4.3.
Entropic and Fisher Chaos

We turn to the implications of the above estimates for the relative entropy and the Dirichlet form of the exchange dynamics.

Proposition 4.9

(Entropic Chaos)
.

Suppose
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
are irreducible measures such that
\nu_{1}\ll\nu_{2}
and
m_{\mathcal{A}}(\nu_{1})=m_{\mathcal{A}}(\nu_{2})
.
Then,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,H_{N}\left(\gamma_{N}(\nu_{1})\,|\,\gamma_{N}(\nu_{2})\right)=H(\nu_{1}\,|\,\nu_{2}).

Proof.

By definition of the canonical tensor products, calling
\rho
the common canonical density for
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
,

H_{N}\left(\gamma_{N}(\nu_{1})\,|\,\gamma_{N}(\nu_{2})\right)=\gamma_{N}(\nu_{1})\left[\log\left(\frac{\nu_{1}^{\otimes^{N}}}{\nu_{2}^{\otimes^{N}}}\right)\right]\;-\;\log\left(\frac{\nu_{1}^{\otimes^{N}}(\Omega_{N,\rho})}{\nu_{2}^{\otimes^{N}}(\Omega_{N,\rho})}\right).

Corollary
4.6
implies

(4.8)

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,\log\left(\frac{\nu_{1}^{\otimes^{N}}(\Omega_{N,\rho})}{\nu_{2}^{\otimes^{N}}(\Omega_{N,\rho})}\right)=0.

Moreover, by symmetry

(4.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\,\gamma_{N}(\nu_{1})\left[\log\left(\frac{\nu_{1}^{\otimes^{N}}}{\nu_{2}^{\otimes^{N}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=P_{1}\gamma_{N}(\nu_{1})\left[\log\left(\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{2}}\right)\right],

and the conclusion follows by Proposition
4.8
.
∎

Fisher Chaos is the convergence of the Dirichlet form (
1.25
) when the function
F
is the density of a canonical tensor product. Recalling the notation from Definition
4.1
, this can be formulated as follows.

Proposition 4.10

(Fisher Chaos)
.

Fix
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, and let
\mu:=\mu_{J,h}
. Suppose
f\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
is such that the measure
\nu:=f\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is irreducible.
Let
\rho
denote the common canonical density of
\nu
and
\mu
, and define

(4.10)

F_{N}:=\frac{\gamma_{N}(\nu)}{\gamma_{N}(\mu)}.

Then,

(4.11)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,\mathcal{E}_{N,J,\rho}(F_{N},\log F_{N})=2\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f).

Proof.

For any
F:\Omega^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
we write

\mathcal{E}_{N,J,\rho}(F,\log F)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell,k\in[n]}\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)\mathcal{E}^{\ell,k}_{N,J,\rho}(F),
where

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\ell,k}_{N,J,\rho}(F):=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mu_{N,J,\rho}\left[\hat{p}_{J}(\ell,k|\sigma(i),\sigma(j))\nabla^{i,\ell;j,k}F\log F\right].

Similarly,
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell,k\in[n]}\mathcal{K}(\ell,k)\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{\ell,k}(f)
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{\ell,k}(f)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Omega}\mu(\tau)\mu(\tau^{\prime})p_{J}(\ell,k|\tau,\tau^{\prime})\,\times\;

(4.13)

\displaystyle\qquad\;\times\,\left(f\big(S_{\ell}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}_{k})\big)f\big(S_{k}(\tau^{\prime},\tau_{\ell})\big)-f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})\right)\log\big(f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})\big).

Therefore,
to prove (
4.11
) it is sufficient to show that for all fixed
n
and
\ell,k\in[n]
, with
F_{N}
as in (
4.10
),

(4.14)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,\mathcal{E}^{\ell,k}_{N,J,\rho}(F_{N})=2\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{\ell,k}(f).

Since
F_{N}
and
\mu_{N,J,\rho}
are invariant under particle permutations,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{E}^{\ell,k}_{N,J,\rho}(F_{N})=\frac{N-1}{N}\mathcal{E}_{1,2}(F_{N})+\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{E}_{1}(F_{N})\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1,2}(F_{N})=-\mu_{N,J,\rho}\left[p_{J}(\ell,k|\sigma(1),\sigma(2))\nabla^{1,\ell;2,k}F_{N}\log F_{N}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(F_{N})=-\mu_{N,J,\rho}\left[\hat{p}_{J}(\ell,k|\sigma(1),\sigma(1))\nabla^{1,\ell;1,k}F_{N}\log F_{N}\right]\,.

Thus, it suffices to show that

(4.15)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{1,2}(F_{N})=2\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{\ell,k}(f)\,,

(4.16)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,\mathcal{E}_{1}(F_{N})=0\,.

For any configuration
\sigma\in\Omega_{N,\rho}
,

F_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{\mu^{\otimes N}\big(\Omega_{N,\rho}\big)}{\nu^{\otimes N}\big(\Omega_{N,\rho}\big)}\prod_{s=1}^{N}f\big(\sigma(s)\big),

so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1,2}(F_{N})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{N,\rho}}\frac{\mu(\sigma(1))\mu(\sigma(2))\prod_{s\neq 1,2}\nu\big(\sigma(s)\big)}{\nu^{\otimes N}\big(\Omega_{\rho_{N}}^{\mathcal{A}}\big)}\,p_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\sigma(1),\sigma(2))\,\log\left(F_{N}(\sigma)\right)\;\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[f(S_{\ell}(\sigma(1),\sigma(2)_{k}))f(S_{k}(\sigma(2),\sigma(1)_{\ell}))-f(\sigma(1))f(\sigma(2))\right].

We decompose
\Omega_{N,\rho}
by fixing the values
\tau=\sigma(1),\tau^{\prime}=\sigma(2)
,

(4.17)

\Omega_{N,\rho}=\cup_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Omega}\Omega_{N,\rho}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}),

where
\Omega_{N,\rho}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
denotes the set of configurations
(\sigma(s))_{s\neq 1,2}\in\Omega^{N-2}
such that

\sum_{\ell\in A}\sum_{s\neq 1,2}\sigma_{\ell}(s)=(N-2)|A|\left(2\widetilde{\rho}(A\,|\,\tau,\tau^{\prime})-1\right),\qquad A\in\mathcal{A},

where the adjusted density vector
\widetilde{\rho}(\cdot\,|\,\tau,\tau^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A},N-2}
satisfies

\left(2\widetilde{\rho}(A\,|\,\tau,\tau^{\prime})-1\right)=\frac{N}{N-2}(2\rho(A)-1)-\frac{1}{(N-2)|A|}\sum_{\ell\in A}(\tau_{\ell}+\tau^{\prime}_{\ell}).

Note that
\widetilde{\rho}(A\,|\,\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\rho(A)+O(1/N)
for all
A
and
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Omega
. In particular, by Proposition
4.5
, for all
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Omega
,

(4.18)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\nu^{\otimes{N-2}}(\Omega_{N,\rho}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}))}{\nu^{\otimes N}(\Omega_{N,\rho})}=1.

From the decomposition (
4.17
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1,2}(F_{N})=-\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Omega}\mu(\tau)\mu(\tau^{\prime})p_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\tau,\tau^{\prime})\left[f(S_{\ell}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}_{k}))f(S_{k}(\tau^{\prime},\tau_{\ell}))-f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})\right]\;\times

\displaystyle\;\times\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{N,\rho}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})}\frac{\prod_{s\neq 1,2}\nu\big(\eta(s)\big)}{\nu^{\otimes N}\big(\Omega_{N,\rho}\big)}\,\left[\log\frac{\mu^{\otimes N}\big(\Omega_{N,\rho}\big)}{\nu^{\otimes N}\big(\Omega_{N,\rho}\big)}+\log(f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime}))+\log\left(\prod_{s\neq 1,2}f(\eta(s))\right)\right].

Using reversibility as in Lemma
2.1
, we see that the function

\phi(\tau,\tau^{\prime}):=\mu(\tau)\mu(\tau^{\prime})p_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\tau,\tau^{\prime})\left[f(S_{\ell}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}_{k}))f(S_{k}(\tau^{\prime},\tau_{\ell}))-f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})\right]

is antisymmetric in the exchange
(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\leftrightarrow(S_{\ell}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}_{k}),S_{k}(\tau^{\prime},\tau_{\ell}))
. On the other hand,

\Omega_{N,\rho}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\Omega_{N,\rho}(S_{\ell}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}_{k}),S_{k}(\tau^{\prime},\tau_{\ell}))\,.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1,2}(F_{N})=-\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Omega}\frac{\nu^{\otimes{N-2}}(\Omega_{N,\rho}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}))}{\nu^{\otimes N}(\Omega_{N,\rho})}\,\phi(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\log(f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime})).

From (
4.18
) we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{1,2}(F_{N})=-\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Omega}\phi(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\log(f(\tau)f(\tau^{\prime}))=2\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f)\,.

This proves (
4.15
). To prove (
4.16
)
we proceed in a similar way. We decompose
\Omega_{N,\rho}=\cup_{\tau\in\Omega}\Omega_{N,\rho}(\tau)
by fixing
\tau=\sigma(1)
,
where
\Omega_{N,\rho}(\tau)=\Omega_{N-1,\widetilde{\rho}(\cdot\,|\,\tau)}
denotes the set of configurations
(\sigma(s))_{s\neq 1}\in\Omega^{N-1}
with fixed density
\widetilde{\rho}(\cdot\,|\,\tau)
such that

\widetilde{\rho}(A\,|\,\tau)=\rho(A)+O(1/N)
for all
A
and
\tau\in\Omega
.
Reasoning as above,
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(F_{N})=-\sum_{\tau\in\Omega}\frac{\nu^{\otimes{N-1}}(\Omega_{N,\rho}(\tau))}{\nu^{\otimes N}(\Omega_{N,\rho})}\,\phi(\tau)\log(f(\tau)),

where

\phi(\tau)=\mu(\tau)\hat{p}_{J}(\ell,k\,|\,\tau,\tau)\left[f(\tau^{\ell,k})-f(\tau)\right]\,.

Therefore,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{1}(F_{N})=-\sum_{\tau\in\Omega}\phi(\tau)\log(f(\tau))\,.

This implies (
4.16
).
∎

4.4.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

As we have seen in Section
4.1
, it is sufficient to prove Theorem
4.3
.
Take
f\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
, for a fixed
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, and write
F_{N}
for the function in Proposition
4.10
. Set

\mu=\mu_{J,h}
, and assume that
\nu=f\mu
is irreducible in the sense of Definition
4.4
. Therefore, we know that

(4.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,\mathcal{E}_{N,J,\rho}(F_{N},\log F_{N})\,.

On the other hand, by Theorem
1.10
,
\mathcal{E}_{N,J,\rho}(F_{N},\log F_{N})\geq\alpha\,{\rm Ent}_{N,J,\rho}F_{N}
, for all
N
, with
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{4n}(1-2\lambda(J))^{2}e^{-16\bar{J}}
.
By Proposition
4.9
with
\nu_{1}=\nu
and
\nu_{2}=\mu
, one has the convergence

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,{\rm Ent}_{N,J,\rho}F_{N}=\mu[f\log f]\,.

It follows that

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f)\geq\alpha\,\mu[f\log f]\,.

This establishes the desired bound for all
f\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
such that
\nu=f\mu
is irreducible. If
\nu
is not irreducible, we proceed by regularising it as follows.
For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, define

f_{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)f+\varepsilon.

Note that
f_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
whenever
f\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
. Moreover
\nu_{\varepsilon}:=f_{\varepsilon}\mu
is irreducible since
\nu_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\geq\varepsilon\mu(\tau)>0
for all
\tau\in\Omega
.
Thus, applying the above reasoning to
f_{\varepsilon}
we obtain

(4.21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(f_{\varepsilon})\geq\alpha\,\mu[f_{\varepsilon}\log f_{\varepsilon}]\,.

Taking the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, by continuity we obtain the desired inequality for
f
.
This proves
\mathcal{I}(h)\geq\alpha
for all
h\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{A}}
, which ends the proof.

4.5.
Open problem: exponential ergodicity at all temperatures?

We conclude with the discussion of some open questions.
The exponential decay established in Theorem
1.7
holds under the assumption that the interaction matrix
J
is nonnegative definite and has spectral radius
\lambda(J)<1/2
.
The positivity assumption is, in principle, not restrictive, since one may freely modify the diagonal entries of
J
without affecting the corresponding Ising measure.
The spectral radius condition, however, imposes that the interaction be sufficiently weak, which we interpret as a high-temperature assumption.

For instance, in the case of the Curie-Weiss model, where

J_{i,j}=\beta/n
for all
i,j\in[n]
, the condition
\lambda(J)<1/2
imposes
\beta<1/2
which is a factor
2
off with respect to the critical value
\beta_{c}=1
. It would be certainly desirable to have exponential decay with rate of order
1/n
throughout the whole region
\beta\in(0,\beta_{c})
in this case.
On the other hand, understanding the behavior of the system in the low temperature region
\beta>\beta_{c}
remains an open problem.
In particular, it is not even clear whether one should expect a slowdown or not for the nonlinear equation.

Nevertheless, by analogy with linear evolutions such as low-temperature Glauber or Kawasaki dynamics
[
29
]
, one may ask whether exponential decay to equilibrium still holds for every interaction
J
, possibly with rates that deteriorate exponentially in
n
.
Establishing such a result would amount to an exponential ergodicity property for the nonlinear equation, providing a quantitative counterpart to the plain convergence in Theorem
1.5
.

A possible strategy toward this goal is to revisit Kac’s program and show that a statement analogous to Theorem
1.10
remains valid for arbitrary interactions
J
, with some rate
\alpha\geq c(J)
for a constant
c(J)>0
that may depend on
J
, possibly exponentially small in
n
.
By repeating the reasoning in Section
3
, and in particular, invoking Proposition
3.5
, such a result would follow if one could prove that the tilted canonical Ising measure
\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu_{N,\rho}
satisfies a covariance bound of the form

\lambda\!\left(\mathrm{Cov}[\mathcal{T}_{v}\mu_{N,\rho}]\right)\leq C(J),

for some constant
C(J)
that may grow with
n
but remains uniformly bounded in both the linear tilt
v
and the number of particles
N
.
We conjecture that such a bound indeed holds for all interactions
J
. Using equivalence-of-ensembles estimates, as in Proposition
4.8
, one can obtain such bounds for each fixed choice of the tilt
v
, uniformly in
N
.
However, establishing estimates that are also uniform in the tilt parameter appears to be a rather challenging problem.
