The relationship between treatment methods and depressive mood in patients with low back pain: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Şeyma Ceylan, İsmail Arslan, Merve Koşucu, Duygu Yengil Taci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow back pain is widely recognized as a frequent health concern affecting many individuals in the community, and treatment options are wide-ranging. This study investigated the association of depressive mood with treatment approaches used or preferred by patients with low back pain.MethodsThe study included 233 patients who presented with low back pain to the Family Medicine and Traditional and Complementary Medicine (TCM) outpatient clinics of Ankara Training and Research Hospital. Data were collected through face-to-face questionnaires, obtaining information on sociodemographic characteristics, recent medication use for low back pain, non-pharmacological treatments and Visual Analog Scale (VAS). The assessment of depressive symptoms was conducted with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 was used for the analyses, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.ResultsOf the participants, 143 (61.4%) were women and 90 (38.6%) were men, with a mean age of 46.16 ± 13.99 years. For low back pain treatment, 45.1% of patients reported receiving pharmacological treatment, 32.6% reported receiving TCM treatment, and 21% reported receiving physical therapy. Those receiving pharmacological treatment had higher BDI scores (p = 0.006). Pharmacological treatment was more commonly used by women (p = 0.001) and by patients with lower education, poor health perception, chronic disease, inactive employment, and was significantly associated with VAS before treatment, older age and higher body mass index (BMI) (p < 0.001 for variables).ConclusionParticipants with low back pain who preferred pharmacological treatment were predominantly female, older, less educated, had higher BMI, greater pain severity, chronic diseases, and depressive symptoms. It is important to keep in mind that depressive symptoms may accompany low back pain, and patients should be evaluated from a holistic perspective. Assessing depressive symptoms in patients with low back pain may help clinicians develop more personalized and effective treatment strategies.

Background

Across the lifespan, nearly four in five individuals experience at least one episode of low back pain; this condition becomes chronic, leading to functional loss and disability [1]. Nonspecific low back pain, with a prevalence of 90–95%, is the most common form where the pathoanatomical cause cannot be determined, and treatment focuses on reducing pain and preventing disability [2]. Low back pain is categorized by duration into three groups: acute (< 6 weeks), subacute (6–12 weeks), and chronic (> 12 weeks) [3].

Biological factors such as age, gender, chronic diseases, and increased body mass index (BMI); Social determinants like marital status, level of education, employment status, occupation, health perception, and life quality, together with psychosocial aspects such as depression, anxiety, and stress, play a role in the development and continuation of low back pain [4, 5]. Depression and low back pain are common conditions in the general population and interact with each other [6]. Depressive symptoms are detected in approximately 20–25% of adults with low back pain, which is associated with increased pain severity, reduced quality of life, and a negatively affected recovery process [1]. Thus, concurrent evaluation and management of chronic low back pain and depression are important for optimizing patient outcomes [7].

According to national guidelines, first-line therapy for chronic low back pain consists of non- pharmacological approaches together with non-opioid medications [8, 9]. Pharmacological treatments include acetaminophen, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), muscle relaxants, opioids, and antidepressants; non-pharmacological treatments include exercise, physical therapy, acupuncture, massage, regional heat application, and TCM [10]. People with low back pain tend to use multiple therapeutic options.

A review of the literature revealed that while there are numerous studies on low back pain, low back function, and depression, there are only a limited number of studies comparing low back pain treatment methods and their relationship with depression. Therefore, our study aimed to compare the pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatment methods used among individuals with low back pain attending the clinic and to evaluate their association with depressive symptoms.

Methods

The study is cross-sectional, observational, and descriptive. Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling approach. Between 01.03.2023 and 31.08.2023, approximately 1500 patients with low back pain visited the Family Medicine and Traditional and Complementary Medicine outpatient clinics at Ankara Training and Research Hospital, and 245 patients presenting with low back pain complaints were assessed for eligibility.

The study included participants aged 18–65 years who had no communication barriers and provided informed consent. Cases of low back pain caused by infectious, inflammatory, visceral, tumoral, fracture, and metabolic causes based on prior medical diagnosis were excluded. In addition, patients diagnosed with depression and taking antidepressant medication, those who declined to participate, and those who did not fully complete the questionnaire were also excluded. The detailed recruitment and exclusion process, including the handling of incomplete data, is illustrated in the STROBE flow chart (Fig. 1). Consequently, the final statistical analysis was conducted with 233 participants.Fig. 1Flow diagram of participant recruitment and exclusion process (STROBE-Style)

Flow diagram of participant recruitment and exclusion process (STROBE-Style)

A literature-based questionnaire was used to collect participants’ basic characteristics such as age, sex, marital and educational status, occupation, current employment status, socioeconomic status, presence of chronic disease, subjective health perception, height, and weight. The body mass index was computed as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). The assessment of depressive symptoms was conducted with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). Regarding low back pain, the questionnaire inquired about previous visits to a physician, the length of pain history, use of medication (painkillers (NSAIDs) and muscle relaxants) in the last 3 months, and non-pharmacological treatment methods such as TCM (acupuncture, cupping therapy, prolotherapy), physical therapy, massage, exercise, balneotherapy, regional heat application, kinesiology tape treatments. The VAS score before any treatment was assessed. Data were collected via face-to-face interviews conducted by the researchers.The pharmacological treatment group included those receiving medication or non-pharmacological treatment in addition to medication, while the non-pharmacological treatment group consisted of those receiving treatments that did not involve medication.

The validated version of the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) translated into Turkish by Hisli in 1989 was used [11, 12]. In our study, participants who scored 10 or above were assessed as having 'depressive symptoms'.

The VAS is a 10-cm single line used to rate pain intensity, ranging from ‘no pain’ to ‘the worst pain imaginable’; higher scores indicate greater pain severity [13].

The research received ethical approval from the Ankara Training and Research Hospital Ethics Committee (Date: 22 February 2023, Decision No: 1210).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Group comparisons were carried out with the Mann–Whitney U test and the Kruskal–Wallis test. To identify factors associated with pharmacological treatment use for low back pain, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed. Age, BMI, VAS score before any treatment, presence of chronic disease, monthly income–expenditure status, and BDI total score were included as independent variables in the model. All variables were entered simultaneously using the enter method. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Model fit was evaluated using the Omnibus test of model coefficients and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The explanatory power of the model was assessed using the Nagelkerke R2 statistic, and the overall classification accuracy of the model was also calculated. A p value below 0.05 was accepted as statistically significant.

Results

A total of 245 patients were assessed for eligibility during the study period. After applying the exclusion criteria, 233 participants were included in the final analysis. The participant recruitment and exclusion process is presented in Fig. 1.

Of those comprising the study group, 38.6% were male (n=90) and 61.4% were female (n=143). Participants were 21–65 years old, and the average age was 46.16 ± 13.99 years. 77.3% of participants were married (n=180), and 50.2% were university graduates (n=117).

In 92.3% of the patients participating in the study (n=215), the pain had persisted for 3 months or longer. 62.7% of the patients (n=146) had previously consulted a physician for low back pain.

Regarding the treatments applied for low back pain, 45.1% reported using medication (pharmacological treatment group) (n=105), 32.6% reported using TCM (n=73), and 21% reported using physical therapy (n=49). Of the TCM methods, 59.2% stated that they had received cupping therapy (n=45), 21.1% acupuncture (n=16), and 15.6% prolotherapy (n=12). Depressive symptoms were more prevalent in the study group among those with chronic illness, women, primary school graduates, those with poor health perception, and those who had received pharmacological therapy for low back pain within the past three months (p< 0.05 for each).

Discussion

Our study revealed that patients with low back pain receiving pharmacological treatment were predominantly women, inactive (not actively working, not being actively employed), had a low level of education, and had chronic diseases and accompanying symptoms of depression. It was also observed that individuals who were older, had a higher VAS score and BMI preferred pharmacological treatment.

Low back pain relates to physical as well as mental well-being; chronic low back pain shows a strong association with limitations in physical function and depressive symptoms [14]. A recent study involving 7,550 adults aged over 50 reported that about 20–25% of those experiencing low back pain exhibited depressive symptoms or clinical depression, which may adversely influence health outcomes, healthcare utilization, and treatment processes [2]. The reason the rate of depressive symptoms was almost nearly double in our study may be due to the fact that most of the patients had chronic pain and it was a hospital-based study. When looking at studies using different depression scales, the rates are consistent with our study [6, 15, 16]. The increasing trend in depression rates in recent years should be taken into account.

In this study, the rates of patients using non-pharmacological methods in addition to drug treatment were high. Previous studies have shown that non-pharmacological methods are commonly preferred in chronic low back pain [17, 18]. This may be due to these treatments being reliable, easy to apply, and having low costs and side effects. It has been observed that patients do not settle for a single treatment method to alleviate their pain but instead try multiple treatment options [19].

In our study, the group receiving pharmacological treatment used NSAIDs and muscle relaxants as medication. No opioid treatment was observed in the patients. Although the percentage of the group receiving pharmacological treatment was similar to previous studies, the absence of any patients using opioid treatment may be due to our patients presenting as outpatients and not receiving inpatient treatment [5, 20]. Among non-pharmacological methods, the rates of massage and physical therapy were consistent with previous studies [15, 21, 22], while the exercise rate was higher and the use of heat application and balneotherapy was lower [5, 21, 23, 24].

The percentage of patients with low back pain using TCM treatments was higher in many studies than in our study [21, 23]. We believe that the low socioeconomic status of our patient population and the fact that these methods are fee-based may have had an effect.

Similar to previous studies, our study found that the group receiving pharmacological treatment was associated with factors known to pose a risk for low back pain, such as female gender, advanced age, primary education or below, chronic disease, and symptoms of depression. Unlike the literature, our study found that pharmacological treatment use was more prominent in participants with increased BMI, who were not actively working, and who perceived their health as poor [5, 24–27]. Considering risk factors for low back pain may contribute to improved clinical outcomes by enabling the planning of more effective, individualised treatment approaches.

In this study, the rate of depression among those using pharmacological treatment for low back pain was observed to be elevated, consistent with findings from recent studies [5, 25]. Studies conducted in adolescents have found that patients with depressive symptoms have a high use of analgesics due to pain [26–28]. Patients experiencing more severe pain or who have previously shown symptoms of depression may be more inclined to seek pharmacological treatment. This may be associated with learned helplessness and a pain-depression cycle. The observed relationship between the use of passive treatments and the exacerbation of the pain-depression cycle can be partly explained by decreased self-efficacy in patients who resort to passive strategies. Individuals with low self-efficacy are more likely to avoid activity and become reliant on medication rather than engaging in active coping behaviors. In contrast, active interventions such as exercise enhance self-efficacy by promoting patient participation and functional improvement [29, 30]. Consequently, depression should be considered in patients with excessive drug use related to low back pain. Family medicine is a speciality that can manage low back pain and depression simultaneously; therefore, evaluation and management of depressive symptoms should be considered in patients presenting with low back pain [31].

In current study, no relationship was observed between depression symptoms and non-pharmacological treatments (TCM, exercise, massage, physiotherapy, heat application, kinesiology tape) as seen in a recent study [15]. This may be because only non-depressed patients find sufficient effort and motivation within themselves to try and continue non-pharmacological treatments to find a remedy for low back pain. A study conducted in 2024 demonstrated that most non-pharmacological interventions contribute to alleviating depressive and anxious symptoms and enhancing psychological well-being in patients with chronic low back pain [32].

Limitations of our study include its single-centre nature, the use of only the BDI to assess depression symptoms, and the exclusion of severe cases due to the outpatient nature of the low back pain patients. In addition, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to infer causal relationships between depressive symptoms and treatment preferences. Because participants were recruited from a hospital-based outpatient population, selection bias may have occurred, and the findings may not be fully generalisable to the broader community. The use of self-reported questionnaire data may also have introduced recall bias. Furthermore, depression was evaluated using a screening scale rather than a formal clinical diagnostic assessment. Possible misclassification of low back pain etiology cannot be excluded, as detailed clinical or imaging-based evaluations were not performed for all participants. The lack of longitudinal follow-up also limits the ability to observe temporal relationships between depressive symptoms and treatment choices. Finally, the absence of a control group consisting of individuals without low back pain may restrict comparisons regarding the prevalence of depressive symptoms in the general population. In addition, the pharmacological treatment group included patients receiving medication alone as well as those receiving medication in combination with non-pharmacological treatments, which may have resulted in some conceptual overlap between treatment categories.

Future studies could adopt multicentre prospective observational designs. In addition, prospective cohort studies could help clarify the temporal relationship between depressive symptoms and pharmacological treatment use in patients with low back pain, particularly to determine whether depressive symptoms precede increased reliance on pharmacological treatment or emerge as a consequence of it. Furthermore, methods that confirm the diagnosis of clinical depression could be used to evaluate the effectiveness of pharmacological, non-pharmacological, and TCM treatments for low back pain.

Conclusion

Paying attention to low back pain risk factors may enable clinicians to develop more personalised and efficient management approaches for low back pain management, leading to better outcomes. Physicians should recognize that individuals with chronic low back pain accompanied by depression or severe pain may show limited response to both medication-based and non-drug interventions. Screening for depression and initiating treatment if necessary is recommended, especially in patients experiencing severe low back pain and receiving drug therapy.
