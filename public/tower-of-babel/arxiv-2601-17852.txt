1.
Introduction

A classical problem in the theory of
projective curves is to determine all their possible genera in
terms of the degree and the dimension of the space where they are
embedded
[
4
]
,
[
11
]
. Although a primary focus of interest
has been, and still is, on irreducible and reduced curves, there are
good reasons for studying more general curves, i.e. projective
schemes of dimension
1
. For instance, in the enumerative geometry
there is some interest, coming from theoretical physics, in certain
invariants of Calabi-Yau 3 -folds
X
, as Gromov-Witten invariants and
Gopakumar-Vafa invariants, involving
1
-cycles of
X

[
12
]
.

\bullet

Before stating our results,
we believe it is useful to briefly summarize some classical results
on the genus of a projective curve.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
be a projective curve of degree
d
. The
arithmetic genus

p_{a}(C):=1-\chi(\mathcal{O}_{C})

of
C
is
determined by the degree:

(1)

p_{a}(C)=\pi_{0}(d,2):=\binom{d-1}{2}=\frac{d^{2}}{2}-\frac{3}{2}d+1.

This is no longer true when
C
is not contained in a plane.
However, there is an upper bound for
p_{a}(C)
. More precisely,
assume that
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
,
r\geq 3
, is an
integral
,
i.e. reduced and irreducible, projective curve of degree
d
. Assume
moreover that
C
is non degenerate, i.e. not contained in a hyperplane.
Then the celebrated
Castelnuovo’s bound
states that:

(2)

p_{a}(C)\leq\pi_{0}(d,r):=\frac{d^{2}}{2(r-1)}-\frac{d}{2(r-1)}(r+1)+\frac{1+\epsilon}{2(r-1)}(r-\epsilon),

where
\epsilon
is defined by dividing
d-1=m(r-1)+\epsilon
,

0\leq\epsilon\leq r-2

[
4
, Theorem (3.7), p. 87]
.
Notice that for every
r\geq 3
and
d\geq r
one has

(3)

\pi_{0}(d,r)<\pi_{0}(d,r-1).

Castelnuovo’s bound is sharp, and there is a classification of the
extremal curves, the so called
Castelnuovo curves
. It turns
out that
the Castelnuovo curves must lie on quadric
hypersurfaces
, and that, at least when
d>2r
, must lie on
rational normal surfaces of degree
r-1
in
\mathbb{P}^{r}

[
4
, Theorem (3.11), p. 91]
.

Now assume that
C
is contained in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, i.e. put
r=3
.
We just said that if
C
is a Castelnuovo curve, it must lie on a
quadric. Therefore, the arithmetic genus of an integral curve of
degree
d

not
lying on a quadric should be strictly less than

\pi_{0}(d,3)
. So, it is natural to ask what is the sharp bound for a
curve not contained in quadrics. More generally, given an integer
s\geq 2
, one can ask
what is the sharp bound for the genus of a
curve in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
not contained in surfaces of degree
<s
. We
will call this question
Halphen’s problem
. When

s=2
, the estimate sought is precisely the Castelnuovo’s bound. The
celebrated Halphen-Gruson-Peskine Theorem gives an answer to this
problem
[
6
, Théorème 3.1]
,
[
5
, 10.8.Teorema]
:

Theorem 1.1

(Halphen-Gruson-Peskine)
.

Fix an integer
s\geq 3
. Let
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
be a projective
integral curve of degree
d>s^{2}-s
, not contained in a surface of
degree
<s
. Then one has:

(4)

p_{a}(C)\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+\frac{d}{2}(s-4)+1-\epsilon_{1},

where

\epsilon_{1}=\frac{(s-1-\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)(s-1)}{2s},

and

\epsilon
is defined by dividing
d-1=ms+\epsilon
,
0\leq\epsilon\leq s-1
.

The inequality (
4
) is sharp, and there is a description
of the extremal curves, which are necessarily contained in a surface
of degree
s

[
6
]
,
[
5
]
. Observe that

(5)

\frac{1}{2}(s-4)=\frac{1}{2s}(2\pi_{0}(s,2)-2-s).

Theorem
1.1
can be generalized. In fact, one may prove that if

C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
,
r\geq 3
, is a projective integral non degenerate curve of
degree
d\gg s
, not contained in a surface of degree
<s
, then one
has:

(6)

p_{a}(C)\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+\frac{d}{2s}(2\pi_{0}-2-s)+O(1),

where
O(1)
is a constant, which only depends on
r
and
s
, that
can be described explicitly, as is the hypothesis
d\gg s
.
Moreover,
\pi_{0}=\pi_{0}(s,r-1)
denotes the Castelnuovo’s bound for
curves of degree
s
in
\mathbb{P}^{r-1}
(see

[
4
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
1
, Main Theorem]
). The bound is sharp, and
the extremal curves must lie on surfaces
S
of degree
s
with
sectional genus
\pi_{0}(s,r-1)
(the sectional genus is the arithmetic
genus of a hyperplane section of
S
). Such surfaces are called
Castelnuovo surfaces
.
When
r=3
, (
6
) reduces to Halphen bound (
4
).

Halphen’s problem admits a weaker formulation. One can ask what is
the sharp bound for the arithmetic genus of an integral curve of
degree
d
, contained in a
fixed
integral surface of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
of degree
s
. This is
Noether’s problem
. This problem
can be seen as an extension to any surface
S
of what happens when

S
is a plane. If
d>s^{2}-s
, in view of Bezout’s theorem, a curve
contained in a surface of degree
s
cannot be contained in a
surface of degree
<s
, and so we fall back into Halphen’s problem.
And in fact, the answer given to this problem is exactly the same as
Halphen’s estimate
[
8
]
. Actually, one may examine Noether’s
problem also in the range
d\leq s^{2}-s

[
8
]
.

Noether’s problem can also be formulated for a fixed
surface in
\mathbb{P}^{r}
,
r\geq 3
. In this case, one knows the
following. If
C
is an integral curve of degree
d\gg s
, contained
in a given integral non degenerate surface
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
,
r\geq 3
, of
degree
s
and sectional genus
\pi
, one has

(7)

p_{a}(C)\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+\frac{d}{2s}(2\pi-s-2)+O(1),

where
O(1)
is a constant which only depends on
s
and
r
, but
not sharp
[
3
, Lemma]
. However, we notice that if
C=S\cap F_{m+1}
is a
complete intersection of
S
with a hypersurface
F_{m+1}
of degree

m+1=\frac{d}{s}
then (compare with (
21
) below):

(8)

p_{a}(C)=\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+\frac{d}{2s}(2\pi-s-2)+1.

This means that the previous estimate (
7
), excluding
the constant term
O(1)
, is sharp. Moreover, we also notice that,
based on an observation by Harris
[
8
, p. 196]
, the previous
bound (
7
) is in a certain sense predicted by the Hodge
Index Theorem. In fact, if
S\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
is a smooth
surface, then from the Hodge Index Theorem we know that

\left(C-\frac{d}{s}H_{S}\right)^{2}\leq 0,

H_{S}
= hyperplane section of
S
. If
S
is also subcanonical,
that is
K_{S}=qH_{S}
,
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, applying the adjunction formula
we deduce

(9)

p_{a}(C)\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+\frac{d}{2s}(2\pi-2-s)+1

for every closed subscheme
C
of
S
of dimension
1
and degree

d
. Here,
\pi
denotes the sectional genus of
S
. Notice that, in view of Castelnuovo’s bound,
if
S
is non degenerate in
\mathbb{P}^{r}
with
r\geq 4
, then (compare with (
3
) and (
5
)):

(10)

\frac{1}{2s}(2\pi-s-2)\leq\frac{1}{2s}(2\pi_{0}(s,r-1)-2-s)<\frac{1}{2s}(2\pi_{0}(s,2)-2-s)=\frac{1}{2}(s-4).

Halphen-Gruson-Peskine Theorem, and all the subsequent quoted
generalized results, apply to integral curves. Moreover, almost their proofs
are based on a property satisfied by the general hyperplane section

\Gamma
of an integral curve,
the uniform position principle

(see for example
[
8
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
5
]
). Simply
put,
it is not possible to distinguish two subsets of points of

\Gamma
having the same order
.

\bullet

Now let’s turn to more recent results, and those appearing in our
article.

Recently, Macrì and Schmidt observed that
Theorem
1.1

can be proved with a new method,
called
wall-crossing
, which uses the
Bridgeland stability
conditions

[
15
]
. In this new proof a generalized Bogomolov-Gieseker inequality,
concerning Chern classes of certain sheaves on

\mathbb{P}^{3}
and necessary to construct Bridgeland stability conditions on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, is fundamental
[
14
]
.

Next, based on the work of Macrì and Schmidt, Liu studies the genus
of a curve, even if it is not integral, on certain projective
3 -folds, obtaining a sort of relative Castelnuovo’s bound:

Theorem 1.2
.

[
12
, Corollary 4.15]

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
be a projective factorial variety of
dimension
3
, degree
n
, with at
worst isolated singularities. Assume that the Picard group of
X
is
generated by the hyperplane section class. Let
C\subset X
be a projective
subscheme of dimension
1
, and degree
d
. If
d\gg n
, then

(11)

p_{a}(C)\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2n}+\frac{d}{2}(n-4)+1-\epsilon_{1}.

The number
\epsilon_{1}
appearing in (
11
) is defined
as in (
4
) with
n
instead of
s
. And in fact, setting

n=s
, the bound (
11
) is equal to (
4
).
Actually, previous theorem is stated under slightly more general
hypotheses, and one may make explicit the assumption
d\gg n
, see

[
12
, Theorem 4.9, Theorem 4.10, and Corollary 4.11]
. Observe that when

X=\mathbb{P}^{3}
, the bound (
11
) reduces to
(
1
).

In short, the proof consists of a iterative method, whose purpose is to
reduce, via wall-crossing,
to a Noether’s problem for curves on a
fixed
surface of
X
of
small degree compared to
d
. Here, however, curves and surfaces
may not be integral. Therefore, to examine this case,
one still need to use the wall-crossing method.
Next, one reduces it to an
integral surface of
X
, and one may conclude with the generalized
Bogomolov-Gieseker inequality.
Note that, if the curve
C
is
integral, Theorem
1.2
can be easily proved with
classical methods by combining
[
1
]
with
[
3
]
(see
Remark
3.9
,
(iii)
, below).

To apply the wall-crossing method and the generalized
Bogomolov-Gieseker inequality, one must reduce the problem to a
curve problem in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. This can be done by projecting

C
onto a curve
C^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
of the same degree, such
that
p_{a}(C)\leq p_{a}(C^{\prime})
. Therefore, the problem reduces to

C^{\prime}
. This explains why the bound
(
11
) is equal to Halphen’s bound (
4
) in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

But Hodge Index Theorem suggests that, in general, the linear term
in
d
in the bound (
11
) should not be fine (compare
with (
9
) and (
10
)).

\bullet

Taking into account inequality (
9
),
our paper aims to refine
the bound (
11
) of Theorem
1.2
in the linear term in
d
.
As already noted in (
8
), the linear term in
d
suggested by the
Hodge Index Theorem is sharp.

Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
be a projective factorial variety of
dimension
3
, degree
n
, linear arithmetic genus
\pi_{X}
, with at
worst isolated singularities. Assume that the Picard group of
X
is
generated by the hyperplane section class. Set:

\nu=\frac{4}{3}n(n+1),\quad M(n)=\frac{n^{2}}{2}\left[\frac{2}{3}(n+1)+4+2\nu^{6}\right]^{2}.

Let
C\subset X
be a closed subscheme of
X
of dimension
1
, of degree
d>M(n)
, and arithmetic genus

p_{a}(C)
. Then one has:

(12)

p_{a}(C)\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2n}+\frac{d}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+4\nu^{6}.

The linear arithmetic genus
\pi_{X}
of
X
is the sectional genus
of its general hyperplane section. When
d\gg n
, the bound (
12
) is finer than the bound
(
11
) (compare with (
10
)).

The proof of Theorem
1.3
consists in showing that the
iterative method introduced in
[
12
, Proof of Theorem 4.9]
also
fits our estimate (
12
). However, once the problem is
reduced to the case where the curve is contained in a surface
S
of

X
of degree
s
small compared to
d
, our proof proceeds a little
differently. In fact, to solve this Noether’s problem (up to the
constant term), the wall-crossing method is not necessary. With
classical arguments, we reduce to the case where
S
is integral,
and we obtain the corresponding estimate by adapting the argument appearing
in
[
3
, Proof of Lemma]
. To this purpose, see Proposition
2.6

and Proposition
2.9
below, and their proofs. In the
subsequent Proposition
2.10
, Corollary
2.11
,
Corollary
2.13
and Corollary
2.14
, we refine the constant
term of the bound appearing in Proposition
2.9
under
the assumption
d\gg\deg S
, give some information on extremal
curves contained in
S
, and in certain cases we prove sharp bounds.
In particular, we prove that extremal curves are contained in
components of
S
of minimal degree.

Taking into account these Noether’s type results, when
d\gg M(n)
,
we are able to refine the constant term in (
12
), and
to prove that the extremal curves in
X
are contained in a
hyperplane section. For more precise statements, see Corollary

3.2
and Corollary
3.3
below.

As a consequence of Corollary
3.2
, we obtain the following
result, concerning Calabi-Yau varieties of dimension
3
:

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
be a smooth Calabi-Yau variety of
dimension
3
and degree
n
. Assume that the Picard group of
X
is
generated by the hyperplane section class. Let
C
be a closed subscheme of
X
of dimension
1

and of degree
d
with
d>M^{*}(n)=6n^{2}M(n)^{2}
. Then we have:

p_{a}(C)\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2n}+\frac{d}{2}+n^{4}.

Corollary
1.4
appears to us to be a significant step forward
for the Castelnuovo bound conjecture
stated in
[
12
, Conjecture 1.1]
.

As for the numerical assumption
d\gg n
in Theorem
1.3
,
and the constant term appearing in the bound (
12
),
they are certainly not the best possible. One might hope to do
better with a closer examination of the numerical functions arising
in the proofs. We decided not to push here this investigation
further. The same remark applies to the assumption that the Picard
group is generated by a
very
ample divisor, and to other
numerical assumptions appearing in our subsequent results.

In the sequel we use standard notations in Algebraic Geometry, see
e.g.
[
10
]
. Our base field is
\mathbb{C}
. By
curve
we mean a projective scheme of dimension
1
(in
[
12
]

curve
means a Cohen-Macaulay projective scheme of dimension

1
).

2.
Bound for the genus of a curve on a surface.

In this section we establish a Noether’s type bound for the arithmetic
genus
p_{a}(C)
of a projective scheme
C
of dimension
1
contained
in a projective scheme
S
of dimension
2
. When
S
is integral
(i.e. reduced and irreducible) we do not need any other hypothesis
on
S
. Otherwise, we have to assume that
S
is contained in an
integral projective threefold
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
, and that here
S

is a Cartier divisor whose irreducible components are linearly
equivalent to a multiple of the hyperplane section of
X
(see
Notations
2.8
below). The bound we prove, apart from the
constant term, is suggested by the Hodge Index Theorem (compare with
(
9
) and see also Remark
2.7
below), it is sharp (compare with (
8
)),
and it is more precise than the one that appears in
[
12
, Corollary 4.4]
(see (
10
)).
First, in Proposition
2.6
below, we examine the case
S

is integral (i.e. reduced and irreducible). The proof relies on the
same argument developed in
[
3
, Lemma]
.
This argument is classic in nature and does not require the wall-crossing method
(compare with
[
12
, Proof of Proposition 4.3]
).
Next, we argue by induction on the number of components of
S
.
Before stating our
result, we need some preliminaries.

Notations 2.1
.

(i)
By
curve
we mean a projective scheme of dimension
1
.

(ii)
If
C
is a curve, we define the
arithmetic genus

p_{a}(C)
of
C
as
p_{a}(C)=1-\chi(\mathcal{O}_{C})
. Observe that when

C
is integral then
p_{a}(C)\geq 0
.

(iii)
Let
Z\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
be a projective subscheme of

\mathbb{P}^{r}
. We denote by
h_{Z}(i)
the Hilbert function of
Z
in

\mathbb{P}^{r}
at level
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. In other words

h_{Z}(i)=\dim\left[{\text{Image}}\left(H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(i))\to H^{0}(Z,\mathcal{O}_{Z}(i))\right)\right].

Observe that
h_{Z}(i)=0
when
i<0
, and that if
Z\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r-1}
then the Hilbert function of
Z
in
\mathbb{P}^{r}

is equal to the Hilbert function of
Z
in
\mathbb{P}^{r-1}
.

(iv)
Assume that
\Gamma
is a zero-dimensional subscheme of

\mathbb{P}^{r}
of length
d>0
. We have
h_{\Gamma}(0)=1
. Moreover,

h_{\Gamma}(i)\leq d
for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, and

h_{\Gamma}(i)=d
for
i\gg 0
. Set

i_{0}=i_{0}(\Gamma)=\min\left\{i\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,h_{\Gamma}(i)=d\right\}.

Observe that
i_{0}\geq 0
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a zero-dimensional subscheme of
\mathbb{P}^{r}
of
length
d>0
. Then
h_{\Gamma}(i)<h_{\Gamma}(i+1)
for every
0\leq i<i_{0}
. And
h_{\Gamma}(i)=d
for every
i\geq i_{0}
. In particular,

h_{\Gamma}(d-1)=d
.

Proof.

When
r=2
, these properties follow from
[
11
, Proof of Proposition 1.1 and Corollary
1.2]
. The same argument of the proof applies also to the case

r\geq 3
.
∎

Next Lemma
2.3
appears in
[
4
, Corollary 3.2, p. 84]
. We
prove it for Reader’s convenience.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
be a curve of degree
d>0
, and let

\Gamma
be a general hyperplane section of
C
. Then we have:

p_{a}(C)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i).

Proof.

Fix an integer
j\gg 0
. We have:

1-p_{a}(C)+jd=h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(j))-h^{1}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(j))=h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(j))=h_{C}(j).

On the other hand, by
[
4
, Lemma 3.1, p. 83]
we know that for
every
i\in\mathbb{Z}

h_{C}(i)-h_{C}(i-1)\geq h_{\Gamma}(i).

Therefore, we have

h_{C}(j)=\sum_{i=0}^{j}h_{C}(i)-h_{C}(i-1)\geq\sum_{i=0}^{j}h_{\Gamma}(i).

Taking into account that
h_{\Gamma}(0)=1
and that

h_{\Gamma}(i)=d
for
i\geq j
, it follows that

p_{a}(C)=1+jd-h_{C}(j)\leq 1+jd-\sum_{i=0}^{j}h_{\Gamma}(i)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}d-h_{\Gamma}(i)=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i).

∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
be a curve of degree
d>0
. Then we
have:

p_{a}(C)\leq\binom{d-1}{2}.

Proof.

Combining Lemma
2.3
with Lemma

2.2
we get:

p_{a}(C)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}d-i-1=\binom{d-1}{2}.

∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
s\geq 2
,
\pi\geq 0
and
\varphi\geq 1
be integers. Then we have:

d\leq\sqrt{2\varphi}\quad\implies\quad\binom{d-1}{2}\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+\frac{d}{2s}(2\pi-2-s)+\varphi.

Proof.

Assume
d\leq\sqrt{2\varphi}
. Since
s\geq 2
and
\pi\geq 0
we
have

\frac{1}{2s}(2\pi-2-s)\geq-1.

Therefore, it suffices to prove that

\binom{d-1}{2}\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2s}-d+\varphi.

This is equivalent to prove that

(13)

d^{2}\frac{s-1}{2s}\leq\frac{1}{2}d-1+\varphi.

Therefore, when
d\geq 2
, it suffices to prove that

d^{2}\leq\varphi\frac{2s}{s-1}.

And this holds true because

2\varphi\leq\varphi\frac{2s}{s-1}.

If
d=1
then (
13
) follows by direct substitution, taking
into account that
\varphi\geq 1
.
∎

We are in position to prove our bound for the genus of a curve
contained in a reduced, irreducible and projective surface

S\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
. The
sectional genus
of such a
surface is the arithmetic genus of the integral curve
S\cap H
,
where
H\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
is a general hyperplane.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r}
be
a reduced, irreducible and projective surface, of degree
s
and
sectional genus
\pi
. Let
C\subset S
be a curve of degree
d>0
.
Then

(14)

p_{a}(C)\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+\frac{d}{2s}(2\pi-2-s)+s^{4}.

Proof.

If
s=1
, then (
14
) follows from Lemma
2.4
.
So, we may assume
s\geq 2
. We may assume that
S
is non degenerate
in
\mathbb{P}^{r}
. Therefore, we may also assume that
r\geq 3
.
Observe that if
s\geq 3
and
d\leq s^{2}+s
then

. Combining Lemma
2.4
with Lemma

we deduce

p_{a}(C)\leq\binom{d-1}{2}\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+\frac{d}{2s}(2\pi-2-s)+s^{4}.

By direct computation we see that previous inequality holds true
also when

and

. Therefore, we may assume also
that
d>s^{2}+s
. This will allow us to apply
[
3
, Lemma]
in a
little while.

Let
H\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
be a general hyperplane. Set:

\Sigma=S\cap H\quad{\text{and}}\quad\Gamma=C\cap H.

Observe that
\Sigma
is a non degenerate integral curve of degree

s
in
H\cong\mathbb{P}^{r-1}
, and that
\Gamma
is a
zero-dimensional scheme of length
d
. Since
\Gamma\subset\Sigma

we have
h_{\Gamma}(i)\leq h_{\Sigma}(i)
for every
i
. In view of
Lemma
2.3
it suffices to prove that

\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i)\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+\frac{d}{2s}(2\pi-2-s)+s^{4}.

To this purpose, divide

(15)

d-1=ms+\epsilon,\quad 0\leq\epsilon\leq s-1.

Since
\Sigma
is an integral curve, by degree considerations, we
have

(16)

h_{\Gamma}(i)=h_{\Sigma}(i)\quad{\text{for every $i\leq m$.}}

Therefore, we may split

(17)

\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}d-h_{\Sigma}(i)+\sum_{i=m+1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i).

The first summand depends only on
d
and
\Sigma
. By
[
3
, Proof
of Lemma, p. 795, line 8 from above]
we know that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}d-h_{\Sigma}(i)=\binom{m}{2}s+m(\epsilon+\pi)-\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}(i-1)(s-h_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(i))-\delta_{i}),

where
\Sigma^{\prime}
is a general hyperplane section of
\Sigma
, and

\delta_{i}
is the dimension of the kernel of the natural map

H^{1}(H,\mathcal{I}_{\Sigma,H}(i-1))\to H^{1}(H,\mathcal{I}_{\Sigma,H}(i))
. Replacing
m
with
\frac{d-1-\epsilon}{s}
, and
taking into account (
17
), we get

\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i)=\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+\frac{d}{2s}(2\pi-2-s)+\rho,

where

\rho=\frac{1+\epsilon}{2s}(s+1-\epsilon-2\pi)-\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}(i-1)(s-h_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(i)))+\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}(i-1)\delta_{i}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i).

So,

(18)

it suffices to prove that
\rho\leq s^{4}
.

The first three summands depend only on
\Sigma
, and by

[
3
, Lemma, (2.5), (2.7), (2.8)]
we can estimate their sum:

(19)

\left[\frac{1+\epsilon}{2s}(s+1-\epsilon-2\pi)-\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}(i-1)(s-h_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(i)))+\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}(i-1)\delta_{i}\right]\leq s^{3}.

It remains to estimate the last term, the tail:

\sum_{i=m+1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i).

Here we observe that, since
C
may be reducible or not reduced, one
cannot estimate the Hilbert function
h_{\Gamma}
via uniform
position as in the classical Castelnuovo Theory
[
4
]
,

[
1
]
. We may overlap this difficulty using Lemma

2.2
. Observe also that
\Sigma
is integral. Hence,
classical Castelnuovo Theory applies to
\Sigma
.

Since
d>s^{2}+s
, by Castelnuovo Theory we have
[
7
, Theorem,
(i)
, p. 492]
,

[
4
, Theorem (3.7), p. 87]
:

h^{1}(H,\mathcal{I}_{\Sigma,H}(m))=h^{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(m))=0.

Hence, we have:

h_{\Sigma}(m)=h^{0}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(m))=h^{0}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(m))-h^{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(m))=1-\pi+ms.

By (
16
) we deduce:

h_{\Gamma}(m)=h_{\Sigma}(m)=1-\pi+ms.

Therefore, by Lemma
2.2
we obtain

h_{\Gamma}(m+j)=d\quad{\text{for every $j\geq\pi+s$.}}

Hence,

\sum_{i=m+1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i)=\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+\pi+s}d-h_{\Gamma}(i).

On the other hand, again by Lemma
2.2
, for every

we have

d-h_{\Gamma}(m+j)\leq d-h_{\Gamma}(m)=\pi+\epsilon.

Hence, taking into account Lemma
2.4
and
(

\sum_{i=m+1}^{+\infty}d-h_{\Gamma}(i)=\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+\pi+s}d-h_{\Gamma}(i)\leq(\pi+s)(\pi+\epsilon)\leq(\pi+s)^{2}\leq\left(\binom{s-1}{2}+s\right)^{2}.

Combining with (
18
) and (
19
), we are done. In fact,
since
s\geq 2
, we have

\rho\leq s^{3}+\left(\binom{s-1}{2}+s\right)^{2}\leq s^{4}.

{C_{E}}

If

\pi_{1}=\pi_{l}

2.4

2.5

If

Taking into account (

are curves with the same degree. From

, and the claim
follows by induction.

Assume there is no such a component. If

is not a curve,
then

is a curve with the same degree of

) and (

. If

is a curve with the same degree of

, we

Taking into account (

), and that
s_{1}<s_{l}
and

, a direct elementary computation proves that:

\frac{d^{2}}{2s_{l}}+\frac{d}{2s_{l}}(2\pi_{l}-2-s_{l})+4r_{l}^{6}s_{l}^{6}\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2s_{1}}+\frac{d}{2s_{1}}\left(2\pi_{1}-2-s_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\right),

and we are done. Hence, we may assume that
C\cap E
and
C_{E}
are
curves. It follows that also
C\cap D
is a curve. If
C
and
C\cap D
have the same degree, then
p_{a}(C)\leq p_{a}(C\cap D)
, and we are
done by induction. Therefore, we may assume that
C_{E}
,
C\cap E
,

C_{D}
,
C\cap D

p_{a}(C)\leq\left[\frac{d^{2}}{2s_{1}}+\frac{d}{2s_{1}}(2\pi_{1}-2-s_{1})+4(\deg S)^{6}\right]+\left[-yd^{2}+2d+ds_{l}+r_{l}a_{l}d\right],

{\text{either}}\quad y=\frac{s_{l}-s_{1}}{2s_{1}s_{l}}\quad{\text{or}}\quad y=\frac{1}{6s_{1}}.

We may write

\left[\frac{d^{2}}{2s_{1}}+\frac{d}{2s_{1}}(2\pi_{1}-2-s_{1})+4(\deg S)^{6}\right]+\left[-yd^{2}+2d+ds_{l}+r_{l}a_{l}d\right]

\frac{d}{4s_{1}}+4(\deg S)^{6}-yd^{2}+2d+ds_{l}+r_{l}a_{l}d\leq 0.

If

, as in the proof of Proposition

2.9
, by (

p_{a}(C)\leq\left[\frac{d^{2}}{2s_{1}}+\frac{d}{2s_{1}}(2\pi_{1}-2-s_{1})+4(\deg S)^{6}\right]+d_{1}\left(-\frac{4}{9s_{1}}d+s_{l}+2+\alpha\right),

where

\left[\frac{d^{2}}{2s_{1}}+\frac{d}{2s_{1}}(2\pi_{1}-2-s_{1})+4(\deg S)^{6}\right]+d_{1}\left(-\frac{4}{9s_{1}}d+s_{l}+2+\alpha\right)

We are done because for

\frac{d}{4s_{1}}+4(\deg S)^{6}+d_{1}\left(-\frac{4}{9s_{1}}d+s_{l}+2+\alpha\right)\leq 0.

This concludes the proof of Proposition

.
∎

Proof of Corollary

.

In view of Proposition

, we only have to prove that if

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
be a Cohen-Macaulay curve of degree
d
.
Let
p
be an integer such that

0<\frac{4}{3}p\leq\sqrt{d}.

Suppose that for every integer
k
with
0<k\leq\frac{4}{3}p
, and for every surface

S\in|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(k)|
, one has
either
\dim S\cap C=0
, or
\mathrm{dim}\ S\cap C=1

and

(36)

\deg(S\cap C)\leq k\sqrt{2d}-\dfrac{k^{2}}{2}.

Then there is no actual wall for
\mathcal{I}_{C/\mathbb{P}^{3}}
in the range

a>0
and
-\frac{4}{3}p\leq b<0
.

Remark 3.6
.

Since
k\leq\sqrt{d}
, from assumption (
36
)
it follows that
\deg(S\cap C)<d
. Therefore,
C
cannot be
contained in
S
.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

By
[
12
, Proposition 3.2]
, an actual wall for

\mathcal{I}_{C/\mathbb{P}^{3}}
in the range
a>0
and
-\frac{4}{3}p\leq b<0
is given by an exact sequence of coherent sheaves in
\mathcal{A}^{b}
:

0\rightarrow A\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{C/\mathbb{P}^{3}}\rightarrow B\rightarrow 0,

where we have two possibilities for
A
. One of the possibilities is
that
A\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-S)
is a line bundle, for
a divisor
S\in|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(k)|
with

1\leq k\leq\frac{4}{3}p
. Since
A
is a subsheaf of

\mathcal{I}_{C/\mathbb{P}^{3}}
, the curve
C
would be contained in
S
. This
is in contrast with our hypotheses.

The other possibility is that there exist one dimensional subschemes

C_{1},\,C_{2}\subset C
and a divisor
S\in|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(k)|
such that
1\leq k\leq\frac{4}{3}p
,
A\cong\mathcal{I}_{C_{1}/\mathbb{P}^{3}}(-S)
,
C_{2}\subset S\cap C
,

, and

d-\deg C_{2}<\mathrm{min}\left\{d-\dfrac{k^{2}}{2},\,d+\dfrac{k^{2}}{2}-k\sqrt{2d}\right\}.

Since

, it follows that

\mathrm{min}\left\{d-\dfrac{k^{2}}{2},\,d+\dfrac{k^{2}}{2}-k\sqrt{2d}\right\}=d+\dfrac{k^{2}}{2}-k\sqrt{2d}.

Therefore, we would have

\deg S\cap C\geq\deg C_{2}>k\sqrt{2d}-\dfrac{k^{2}}{2}.

This is in contrast with our hypotheses.
∎

.

for any

Proof of Theorem

.

As in

Step 0
.

By
[

Set

By
[
12
, Section 3 and Lemma 3.14]

There are only three possibilities for

\bullet
Type I.

For every surface

Type II.

, we get a hypersurface
in
\mathbb{P}^{r}
intersecting

in a

\bullet

would be of Type

. Therefore, by

[
12
, Proposition 3.15 and proof]

, a

Set

d_{1}=\deg C_{1},\quad d^{\prime}_{0}=d_{0}-d_{1}=d-d_{1}=\deg(D_{1}\cap C_{0}).

Since we are interested to bound

from above, in the

C_{1}
is Cohen-Macaulay
[
13
, Lemma
4.3]
. As for
C_{0}
, denote by

. Set:

Notice that
M_{3}>M_{1}>M_{2}
.

Now, we are going to prove that in the following two subcases

,

.

To this aim, first we notice that, since

Set

=\frac{{d}^{2}}{2n}+\frac{d}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+4\nu^{6}+\left[-\frac{dd_{1}}{n}+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{2n}-\frac{d_{1}}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+p_{a}(C_{1})+k_{1}d_{1}\right].

Set:

In order to prove (

In the first case 1) we have

and

, we have:

p_{a}(C_{0})=p_{a}(C_{1})+p_{a}(C_{0}\cap D_{1})+k_{1}d_{1}-1

\leq\frac{{d^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}}{2n}+\frac{d^{\prime}_{0}}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+4\nu^{6}+[p_{a}(C_{1})+k_{1}d_{1}]

=\frac{({d-d_{1}})^{2}}{2n}+\frac{d-d_{1}}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+4\nu^{6}+[p_{a}(C_{1})+k_{1}d_{1}]

=\frac{{d}^{2}}{2n}+\frac{d}{2n}\left(2\pi_{X}-2-n\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d}+R^{*}_{1}

where

R^{*}_{1}=4\nu^{6}+\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d}-\frac{dd_{1}}{n}+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{2n}-\frac{d_{1}}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+p_{a}(C_{1})+k_{1}d_{1}.

It suffices to prove that
R^{*}_{1}\leq 0
. Let’s distinguish two
cases:

1)
d_{1}>M(n)
;

2)
d_{1}\leq M(n)
.

In the case 1), by Theorem
1.3
we have

p_{a}(C_{1})\leq\frac{{d_{1}}^{2}}{2n}+\frac{d_{1}}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+4\nu^{6}.

Inserting into
R^{*}_{1}
we have:

R^{*}_{1}\leq 4\nu^{6}+\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d}-\frac{dd_{1}}{n}+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{2n}-\frac{d_{1}}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+\left(\frac{{d_{1}}^{2}}{2n}+\frac{d_{1}}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+4\nu^{6}\right)+k_{1}d_{1}

\leq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d}-\frac{dd_{1}}{n}+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{n}+8\nu^{6}+k_{1}d_{1}.

Hence, it suffices to prove that

\frac{d_{1}}{n}(d-d_{1})\geq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d}+8\nu^{6}+k_{1}d_{1}.

Dividing by
\frac{d_{1}}{n}
, taking into account that
d_{1}>M(n)
and
that
d-d_{1}>k_{1}\sqrt{2d}-\dfrac{{k_{1}}^{2}}{2}
, it suffices that:

\sqrt{2d}\geq\sqrt{d}+2\nu.

This holds true when
d>M^{*}(n)
.

In the case 2), i.e.
d_{1}\leq M(n)
, using Lemma
2.4
and (
22
),
we have:

R^{*}_{1}=4\nu^{6}+\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d}-\frac{dd_{1}}{n}+\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{2n}-\frac{d_{1}}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+p_{a}(C_{1})+k_{1}d_{1}

=4\nu^{6}+\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d}-\frac{d^{\prime}_{0}d_{1}}{n}-\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{2n}-\frac{d_{1}}{2n}(2\pi_{X}-2-n)+p_{a}(C_{1})+k_{1}d_{1}

\leq 4\nu^{6}+\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d}-\frac{d^{\prime}_{0}d_{1}}{n}-\frac{d_{1}^{2}}{2n}+2d_{1}+\frac{1}{2}(d_{1}-1)(d_{1}-2)+k_{1}d_{1}.

It suffices to prove that

\frac{d_{1}}{n}(d-d_{1})\geq 4\nu^{6}+\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d}+2d_{1}+\frac{1}{2}(d_{1}-1)(d_{1}-2)+k_{1}d_{1}.

Dividing by
\frac{d_{1}}{n}
, taking into account that

d-d_{1}>k_{1}\sqrt{2d}-\dfrac{{k_{1}}^{2}}{2}
and
d_{1}\leq M(n)
, it
suffices that

\sqrt{2d}\geq\sqrt{d}+4n\nu^{6}+2n+\frac{n}{2}M(n)+n+\frac{2}{3}(n+1).

Hence, it suffices that

\sqrt{2d}\geq\sqrt{d}+nM(n).

This holds true for
d>M^{*}(n)
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
3.3
.

Let
C\in\mathcal{C}(X,d)

be a curve of maximal arithmetic genus
G(X,d)
. By the proof of
Corollary
3.2
and by our assumption on
p_{a}(C)
, it follows
that
C
is contained in a surface
S
of
X
of degree
\deg S\leq\frac{4}{3}n(n+1)
, with an integral component of minimal degree

n
, i.e. a hyperplane section of
X
(we may assume
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
and
h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))=r+1
). Therefore, our
claim follows by Corollary
2.11
.
∎
