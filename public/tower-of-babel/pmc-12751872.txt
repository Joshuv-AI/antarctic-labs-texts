Human herpesvirus 7 induced aseptic meningitis in an immunocompetent adult presenting with unusual neurological symptoms: a case report

Introduction

Viral meningitis is a common cause of central nervous system inflammation, typically presenting with headache, fever, photophobia, and neck stiffness [1]. While enteroviruses and herpes simplex virus are the most frequently identified pathogens, human herpesvirus 7 (HHV-7) is a rare and often overlooked etiology, particularly in immunocompetent adults [2].

HHV-7 is a T-lymphotropic, double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the herpesviridae subfamily, closely related to human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6). HHV-7 is a ubiquitous virus that usually causes asymptomatic or mild infections during childhood. By adulthood, over 95% of adults are seropositive for HHV-7, reflecting its global ubiquity. After primary infection, the virus establishes latency in CD4 + T cells and can reactivate under certain conditions [3]. Although HHV-7 is primarily associated with exanthematous disease and febrile seizures in children, emerging evidence suggests it can occasionally cause severe neurological complications - including meningitis, encephalitis, and myelitis - even in immunocompetent hosts [2, 4, 5].

We report a rare case of viral meningitis caused by HHV-7 in an otherwise healthy adult, whose initial symptoms were limited to lower limb pain and numbness without fever or classic meningeal signs. This case underscores the importance of considering viral meningitis in the differential diagnosis of unexplained neurological symptoms, even without typical systemic features, and highlights HHV-7 as a potential neurotropic pathogen in immunocompetent individuals.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old Italian female with a background of a 10-year history following a vegetarian diet and a 2-year history of HPV-associated low-grade squamous intraepithelial dysplasia presented at our hospital due to pain. Three days before admission, the patient noticed a sudden lower back pain radiating to the bilateral posterior thighs, calves, and ankles. She felt electric-shock tingling while sitting or lying down. The pain was relieved with over-the-counter Ibuprofen initially. On the following day, the pain increased and was associated with numbness in her soles. The patient preferred standing up and walking to relieve her pain, though movement generated needle-sharp tingling. She denied fever, headache, nausea, and photophobia. She practiced daily weightlifting and just recently paused due to the pain. Review of the system discloses negative findings for recent symptoms of the respiratory or gastrointestinal tract. She also denied illicit drug use.

On examination, the patient is alert, conscious, stable, but irritated by pain. There were some small bruises on her anterior thighs related to her weight-lifting practice. Muscle strength and sensation are intact. Bilateral ankle reflexes are absent, yet bilateral biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, and patellar reflexes remain intact (2+). Cranial nerves I–XII examination did not reveal focal deficits. The straight leg raise test produced severe electric-shock tingling at 45 degrees on both sides. Valleix sign is positive with tenderness upon palpation at bilateral central thighs, popliteal areas, posterior central calves, and inner ankles (Fig. 1).

Her plain lumbar MRI on admission

Initial workup includes routine serum laboratory testing, electromyography, and MRI. Complete blood count, basic metabolic panel, and vitamin B9 and B12 measurement indicated mild leukocytosis, while other parameters remained unremarkable. We also considered the potential involvement of vitamin B1 and/or B6 deficiency, but the testing was unavailable at our laboratory. On electromyography, signals indicated damage to muscle fibres in her lower limbs, but we attribute the damage to non-pathological consequences of weight-lifting practice, otherwise normal. Plain lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed mild disc bulging at the level of L4-L5 and L5-S1 without associated nerve root compression (Fig. 2).

Her brain MRI on day 2 of hospitalization

She was managed with fentanyl, etoricoxib, methycobalamin, and acetaminophen, but her pain was mildly relieved. One day after admission, the patient reported experiencing pinpoint pain in the medial arm bilaterally (more pain in the right side) when she stretched out her arms. Palpation in these areas revealed mild tenderness and numbness along the bilateral lateral upper arms, forearm, and fingers. She also noticed a 20-minute self-resolved numbing episode in the 1/2 upper half of her left lip and the 1/2 left maxilla (Table 1).

Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with meningitis. We carried out a lumbar puncture. Pharmacotherapy at this point consists of dexamethasone, ceftriaxone, and pain relievers. Discussion and conclusions

Human herpesvirus 7 (HHV-7) is a member of the Herpesviridae family, genetically similar to human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6), and primarily infects CD4 + T lymphocytes. It is acquired during early childhood—typically between ages 2 and 5—with a global seroprevalence exceeding 95% in adults. After primary infection, HHV-7 establishes latency in mononuclear cells and can reactivate, although such reactivation is often asymptomatic and rarely causes severe disease in immunocompetent individuals [3].

Despite its widespread prevalence, HHV-7 is an underrecognized cause of neurologic complications. While it has been associated with febrile seizures, encephalitis, and demyelinating disorders, particularly in pediatric or immunocompromised populations, its role in adult viral meningitis remains poorly defined. Among immunocompetent adults without fever, seizures, or headache, the prevalence of HHV-7 meningitis is unknown, and no cases describing this exact scenario have been documented in the medical literature [6, 7]. Systematic reviews and retrospective studies consistently report that affected adults present with at least one cardinal symptom—most commonly fever and headache, less often seizures—and no epidemiologic study or case series has identified or quantified patients lacking all three. Atypical viral meningitis without fever or headache is described—especially in certain populations—but remains uncommon; clinical features vary and may be influenced by age, sex, and immune status [8, 9]. Only a limited number of cases of HHV-7 - associated meningitis have been documented in the literature, and most involved immunocompromised hosts or co-infections with other herpesviruses [2, 4, 10]. For example, there is a case report about meningitis associated with HHV-7 in an Iranian immunocompetent adolescent girl. The 37-year-old female in our case presented with radicular pain, paresthesia, and bilateral reflex abnormalities, primarily affecting the lower extremities, along with back pain and sensory disturbances. In contrast, the 17-year-old adolescent presented with more typical meningeal symptoms such as fever, headache, and neck stiffness. Both patients had positive HHV-7 PCR in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Treatment for both patients included dexamethasone for inflammation and, in the adolescent’s case, acyclovir for viral management. The Iranian adolescent had a favorable outcome as she fully recovered after 10 days, while our patient improved after 5 days of treatment. Another example of HHV-7-associated meningitis was reported by Zhen et al. in a 12-year-old immunocompetent female who presented with high-grade fever, vomiting, headache, and optic disc edema—classic signs of meningeal irritation [11]. In contrast to our case, which lacked systemic symptoms and showed an atypical sensory-predominant presentation, the adolescent’s course was more consistent with textbook viral meningitis. Interestingly, both patients demonstrated pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and were confirmed to have HHV-7 via PCR. However, while the pediatric case required antiviral therapy with ganciclovir and mannitol for intracranial pressure management, our patient improved without antivirals, suggesting that disease severity and therapeutic needs may vary depending on host age, immune response, or viral load. These contrasting cases underscore the wide clinical spectrum of HHV-7 neurological involvement and highlight the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis, particularly when patients initial present with unexplained radicular pain or peripheral paresthesia without fever. Initially, it was challenging to determine whether our patient’s clinical presentation was due to bacterial or viral meningitis. Bacterial meningitis is extremely suggestive when CSF analysis shows a leukocyte count of ≥ 1000 cells/µL with a neutrophilic predominance [12]. However, pus meningitis can not only present with leukocyte counts markedly lower than 1000 cells/µL but also with normal counts on initial CSF examination [13]. Moreover, a lymphocytic predominance was observed in about 10% of cases. In our case, CSF analysis revealed 441 white blood cells/mm³, with 20% neutrophils and 30% lymphocytes, along with elevated protein levels (1.073 g/L). An increase in CSF protein is common in both bacterial and viral meningitis [12]. The increase is usually greater in bacterial and tuberculous meningitis, often reaching or exceeding 1 g/L [14]. Bacterial meningitis is a severe infectious disease that carries substantial mortality and a considerable risk of long-term disability in those who survive [12]. Steroids, as powerful anti-inflammatory drugs, may help by reducing damage caused by secondary inflammation [15]. Due to these concerns, she was given Ceftriaxone and Dexamethasone at the start of treatment. Timely CSF analysis and the inclusion of HHV-7 in multiplex PCR panels are essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions. In our case, a multiplex real-time PCR assay called AllplexTM Meningitis- V1 was requested to detect Herpes simplex virus (HSV) 1–2, Varicella zoster virus (VZV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), HHV-6, HHV-7. This PCR assay has 100% sensitivity for all pathogens except HHV-6. In term of specificity, 100% for CMV, VZV, EBV, HSV-2, HHV-7 and 98.7% for HSV-1 and HHV-6. Some others PCR panels on CSF were also conducted aim to search for the possible pathogens. AllplexTM Meningitis- V2, AllplexTM Meningitis- B Assay and AnyplexTM MTB/NTM Real-time Detection (V2.0) help detect other viral agents (including Parvovirus B19, Mumps virus, Parechovirus, Enterovirus, Adenovirus), bacterial pathogens (Neisseria meningitides, Listeria monocytogenes, Haemophilus influenza, Group B streptococcus, Streptococus pneumonia and Escherichia coli K1) and Mycobacterium tuberculosis/ Non-Tuberculosis Mycobacteria, respectively (Table 3).

The pathophysiology of HHV-7-associated CNS disease is not fully understood. Proposed mechanisms include direct viral invasion, immune-mediated injury, or reactivation in latently infected leukocytes that cross the blood-brain barrier. HHV-7 has been shown to have neurotropic properties and has been isolated from both glial and neuronal cells in experimental models. Additionally, it may act synergistically with HHV-6 or other herpesviruses in promoting CNS inflammation.

A vegetarian diet and a history of weightlifting could theoretically act as confounding factors that influence the clinical presentation or immune response in viral meningitis, but direct evidence for this is limited. Nutritional deficiencies associated with vegetarian diets—most notably vitamin B12, iron, and protein—can modulate immune function and cytokine responses, which may affect the severity or character of symptoms in CNS infections [16–18]. For example, vitamin B12 deficiency can impair both humoral and cellular immunity, potentially leading to blunted inflammatory responses and atypical symptomatology. However, the medical literature does not specifically link vegetarianism to altered clinical presentation in viral meningitis.

Intense physical activity such as weightlifting may transiently modulate immune function through exercise-induced changes in cytokine profiles and stress hormones, but there is no clear evidence that this specifically confounds the clinical presentation of viral meningitis [16]. The current consensus is that while these lifestyle factors may theoretically influence immune responses, there is no direct evidence that they systematically alter the clinical presentation of viral meningitis or account for the absence of classic symptoms such as fever or headache [8, 16, 18].

Diagnosing HHV-7 infection in the CNS relies on molecular techniques, as serological testing cannot distinguish between latent and active infection [7]. CSF PCR is the diagnostic standard, but is not universally included in routine viral meningitis panels, contributing to underdiagnosis [19]. In our case, a broad-range multiplex PCR panel was essential for identifying the causative agent.

There is currently no established antiviral therapy for HHV-7 [1, 20]. In immunocompetent patients, HHV-7 CNS infections often follow a self-limited course, and supportive care remains the mainstay of treatment.
