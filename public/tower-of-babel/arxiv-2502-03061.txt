Pure Exploration Beyond Reward Feedback: The Role of Post-Action Context
Mohammad Shahverdikondori, Amir Mohammad Abouei, Alireza Rezaeimoghadam, Negar Kiyavash

Abstract
We introduce the problem of best arm identification (BAI) with post-action context, a new BAI problem in a stochastic multi-armed bandit environment and the fixed-confidence setting. The problem addresses the scenarios in which the learner receives a post-action context in addition to the reward after playing each action. This post-action context provides additional information that can significantly facilitate the decision process. We analyze two different types of the post-action context: (i) separator , where the reward depends solely on the context, and (ii) non-separator , where the reward depends on both the action and the context. For both cases, we derive instance-dependent lower bounds on the sample complexity and propose algorithms that asymptotically achieve the optimal sample complexity. For the separator setting, we propose a novel sampling rule called G-tracking , which uses the geometry of the context space to directly track the contexts rather than the actions. For the non-separator setting, we do so by demonstrating that the Track-and-Stop algorithm can be extended to this setting. Moreover, in both settings, we theoretically and empirically show that algorithms that ignore the post-action context are sub-optimal. Finally, our empirical results showcase the advantage of our approaches compared to the state of the art.

Introduction

Multi-armed bandit (MAB) refers to a class of sequential decision-making problems, where a learner selects actions (arms) in order to maximize a reward.
MAB has widespread applications in various domains, such as clinical trials
(
Thompson, 1933
)
, dynamic pricing
(
Kleinberg and Leighton, 2003
;
Besbes and Zeevi, 2009
)
, recommender systems
(
Li et al., 2010
)
, and resource allocation
(
Gai et al., 2012
)
. Depending on the learner’s goal and constraints, different objectives may be pursued. For example, if the learner’s goal is to minimize cumulative regret, they must balance the exploration-exploitation trade-off
(
Auer, 2002
;
Garivier and Cappé, 2011
)
. Alternatively, in pure exploration problems, the learner aims to identify a desired object rather than maximize cumulative reward during learning. In the
Best Arm Identification (BAI)
setting, a canonical pure exploration problem, the learner seeks the arm with the highest expected reward and must minimize the sample complexity, i.e., the number of rounds needed to identify this arm
(
Mannor and Tsitsiklis, 2004
;
Audibert and Bubeck, 2010
;
Garivier and Kaufmann, 2016
)
. We focus on BAI with
fixed-confidence
, where the error probability
\delta
for identifying the best arm is fixed, and the objective is to minimize the sample complexity
(
Garivier and Kaufmann, 2016
;
Audibert and Bubeck, 2010
;
Kaufmann, 2020
;
Jamieson and Nowak, 2014
;
Mannor and Tsitsiklis, 2004
)
.

While the classic MAB model is suitable for a broad range of applications, additional side information about the environment can lead to more efficient algorithms. In the bandit literature, various types of side information have been considered. For example,
causal bandits

(
Lattimore et al., 2016
;
Lu et al., 2020
)
or
linear bandits

(
Auer, 2002
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011
)
impose specific structures on the actions, and
contextual bandits

(
Tewari and Murphy, 2017
;
Langford and Zhang, 2007
;
Russac et al., 2021
)
allow the learner to observe a context before choosing an action. In this work, we study a form of side information known as
mediator feedback
or
post-action context
, which has been considered in the setting of cumulative regret minimization by several recent studies
(
Mann et al., 2019
;
Metelli et al., 2021
;
Bilodeau et al., 2022
;
Esposito et al., 2023
;
Eldowa et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024
)
. Specifically, after choosing an action in each round, the learner receives intermediate feedback from the environment along with the reward. This post-action context can substantially accelerate the process of identifying the best arm. We consider this new problem in the fixed-confidence setting, aiming to show how leveraging post-action context can reduce the sample complexity for different environments.

Motivating Example.

Consider a healthcare scenario for diabetes management where
X
denotes a chosen treatment regimen (e.g., medication or dosage),
Z
is an intermediate biomarker such as short-term blood sugar levels, and
Y
is the clinical target of improvement (e.g., glucose stability and range). In each round, a clinician assigns a treatment
X
to a patient, measures
Z
after a short interval, and later observes
Y
. Tracking this intermediate observation may provide additional information about the alternative treatments. The distribution
P(Z|X)
can often be reliably estimated from electronic health records or prior clinical studies, making it valuable prior knowledge. By incorporating
Z
, clinicians can adapt treatment strategies more efficiently, reducing the number of trials needed to identify an optimal regimen. This scenario may fall into either non-separator or separator settings. In both cases, considering
Z
enhances learning compared to relying solely on target
Y
.

Comparison to closely related work.

Lu et al. (2020)
;
Eldowa et al. (2024)
and
Lattimore et al. (2016)
study similar settings with separating contexts, focusing on regret minimization and simple regret optimization, respectively.
Several works study best-arm identification (BAI) in contextual bandits
(
Li et al., 2022
;
Kato and Ariu, 2021
)
, including the proportional mode of
Russac et al. (2021)
. Although these problems are related to ours, there is a fundamental distinction: in contextual bandits, the context is generated before the action is selected, and the action is chosen based on the observed context.
Russac et al. (2021)
also considers more general scenarios, including active, agnostic, and oblivious modes. For example, in the agnostic mode, the context is independent of the action but is observed only after an action is chosen. However, none of these works captures the structure of our problem, where the context is generated as a consequence of the chosen action.
Finally,
Carlsson et al. (2024)
considers a similar problem; however, in their setting, the learner can choose
Z
in each round, whereas in ours the learner cannot directly choose
Z
. For a more detailed discussion of related work, see Appendix
A
.

The presence of post-action context can fundamentally alter the optimal strategy for selecting arms. In classic BAI problems, the optimal algorithms proposed by
Garivier and Kaufmann (2016)
focus more time on pulling the arms with the higher observed rewards, which is natural when no additional side information is available. However, in the presence of post-action context, collecting more samples from a certain context may expedite the identification of the best arm. In such scenarios, choosing a suboptimal arm that increases the probability of observing that context is more effective than selecting the arm with the maximum reward.
We show the suboptimality of the methods that ignore the post-action context both theoretically (Section
4
) and empirically (Section
5
).

Contributions.

We introduce
best-arm identification with post-action context
, a fixed-confidence pure exploration problem in which each action generates both a reward and an informative post-action context. We study two structural regimes: a
separator
setting, where the reward depends on the action only through the post-action context, and a
non-separator
setting, where the reward may depend on both the action and the context. For both regimes, we derive instance-dependent lower bounds and design algorithms that achieve the corresponding asymptotically optimal sample complexity. In the separator setting, the lower bound depends only on the frequencies with which contexts are observed; this motivates our new sampling rule,
G-tracking
, which tracks optimal context frequencies directly rather than tracking arm pull frequencies. In the non-separator setting, we show that the classical Track-and-Stop framework can be extended to exploit post-action contexts optimally. Finally, we show that algorithms that ignore the post-action context can be strictly sub-optimal and characterize this suboptimality.

Preliminaries and Problem Setup

Notations.
The set
[n]
denotes
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
\Delta^{n-1}
represents the
(n-1)
-dimensional standard simplex, defined as
\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid w_{i}\geq 0\wedge\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=1\}
. The notation
d(P,Q)
denotes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between two probability measures
P
and
Q
. Additionally,
d_{B}(\delta,1-\delta)
refers to the KL divergence between two Bernoulli random variables with parameters
\delta
and
1-\delta
. The convex hull of
n
vectors
\mathcal{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
\text{conv}(\{\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{n}\})=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}\mid\lambda_{i}\geq 0\ \wedge\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}
.

Best arm identification with post-action context problem.
In this problem, a player interacts with a multi-armed bandit environment
\mathcal{V}
with
n
arms. In each round
t
, the player selects an action
X=i_{t}\in[n]
and observes a pair
(z_{t},y_{t})
, where
z_{t}
is the value of the post-action context variable
Z
, and
y_{t}
is the realization of the reward variable
Y
.

We assume the context variable
Z
is discrete and takes values in
[k]
, depending only on the arm pulled. The context probability matrix
\mathcal{A}=[\mathcal{A}_{1}|\mathcal{A}_{2}|\ldots|\mathcal{A}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{j,i}=P(Z=j\mid X=i)
, encodes the probability of contexts given each action. We further assume that the reward distribution
P(Y\mid X=i,Z=j)
follows a Gaussian distribution with unit variance. The matrix
\boldsymbol{\mu}=[\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}|\ldots|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
represents the mean values of the reward distributions, such that
\mu_{j,i}=\mathbb{E}(Y|X=i,Z=j)
.

In the case of the separator context, the reward depends only on
Z
, that is,
P(Y\mid X=i,Z=j)=P(Y\mid Z=j)
,
for all
i\in[n]
and
j\in[k]
. This implies that all columns of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
are identical. We denote the joint distribution of the context and reward for a given action
i
by
P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}.\mathcal{A}}_{i}=P(Z,Y\mid X=i)
. The expected reward for a given action
i
is computed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=i]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}P(Z=j|X=i)\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=j,X=i]=\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}.

The best arm is the arm with the maximum expected reward, i.e.,
i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{i\in[n]}\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}
, where the mean matrix
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and the context probability matrix
\mathcal{A}
characterize the instance. In this work, we assume that
\mathcal{A}
is known to the learner and that the best arm is unique. Let
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
denote the set of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
matrices that imply a unique best arm for a given
\mathcal{A}
. For simplicity, as
\mathcal{A}
is known, we often use
\mathcal{I},i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu}),
and
P^{\mu}
instead of
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A}),i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}),
and
P^{\mu,\mathcal{A}}
, respectively.

We consider the best arm identification problem in the
fixed-confidence
setting, where the player aims to identify the best arm
i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
for any
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}
with a pre-specified error probability
\delta\in(0,1)
. A sequential learning algorithm for this setting consists of three main components: (i) A
sampling rule
(deterministic or stochastic) that determines which arm to pull in round
t
based on the history up to round
t-1
, denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}=(i_{1},(z_{1},y_{1}),i_{2},(z_{2},y_{2}),\ldots,i_{t-1},(z_{t-1},y_{t-1}))
, (ii) A
stopping rule

\tau
, based on
\mathcal{H}_{t}
, determines when to stop the process, and (iii) A
decision rule

\hat{i}_{\tau}
, which recommends the best action.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, an algorithm is
\delta
-correct
if for any
\mathcal{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A})
it satisfies
P_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}}(\tau_{\delta}<\infty,\hat{i}_{\tau}\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu}))\leq\delta
. The goal of the learner is to find a
\delta
-correct algorithm that minimizes the expected number of samples
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}}[\tau_{\delta}]
.

The sub-optimality gap of arm
i
is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})]-\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=i]=\mathcal{A}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}-\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}.

During the learning process, let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)
denote the matrix containing the empirical estimates of all entries of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
based on the samples collected up to round
t
and define

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{i}(t)\triangleq\mathcal{A}_{i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}(t)-\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}(t),

which is the gap of arm
i
relative to the arm with the best empirical mean up to round
t
estimated using
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)
. Finally,
N^{X}_{i}(t)
,
N^{Z}_{j}(t)
, and
N_{j,i}(t)
represent the number of times action
i
, context
j
, and joint action-context pair
(i,j)
have been observed up to round
t
, respectively.

Summary of Assumptions.

We assume that the set of contexts is finite. The context probability matrix
\mathcal{A}
is known, and the best arm is unique. In Appendix
H
, we provide a discussion on the complexity of the problem when
\mathcal{A}
is unknown. The reward distribution for each action-context pair follows a Gaussian distribution with unit variance; however, our results can be easily generalized to the one-parameter exponential family. Additionally, we assume positivity, meaning that for each action, each context occurs with positive probability, i.e.,
a_{\min}=\min_{i,j}\mathcal{A}_{i,j}>0
.

2.1
General Lower Bound

For the classical best arm identification problem,
Garivier and Kaufmann (2016)
established a lower bound on the expected sample complexity of any
\delta
-correct algorithm. Extending this lower bound to our problem setting is straightforward, as we show in Proposition
2.1
. Before stating the proposition, let us define Alt
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
as the set of alternative parameter matrices
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
such that
i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime})
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any bandit environment with parameters
\mathcal{A}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A})
and any
\delta
-correct algorithm,
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}}[\tau_{\delta}]\geq T^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})d_{B}(\delta,1-\delta)
, where
T^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
is

\displaystyle T^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})^{-1}\triangleq\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{n-1}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i},P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}_{i}).

(1)

This lower bound indicates that the optimal sampling strategy is to play each action
i
in proportions to
w^{*}_{i}
, a solution of Equation (
1
). Note that the bound is similar to a previous result
(
Garivier and Kaufmann, 2016
)
, with the key distinction that it depends on the context variable through
P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}=P(Z,Y\mid X=i)
.

We derive lower bounds for the BAI problem under both separator and non-separator post-action context settings by explicitly characterizing Equation (
1
). The key difference lies in the structure of the alternative set
\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
, which varies between the two settings. In the separator case, all
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}
vectors are identical, imposing additional constraints on
\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
and yielding a strictly smaller lower bound. This tighter bound motivates the design of a tailored sampling algorithm to achieve optimal sample complexity in the separator setting. The analysis for the non-separator case is deferred to Appendix
B
, as the derivation closely follows the separator case and known sampling rules such as D-tracking are sufficient to achieve optimality.

(2)

3.2
Learning Algorithm

Stopping Rule.
The stopping rule operates independently of the sampling rule and determines, at each step, whether sufficient information has been gathered to terminate the algorithm or if further sampling is necessary. A widely used approach for designing stopping rules in one-parameter exponential family bandits, including Gaussian bandits, is based on Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) tests
(
Garivier and Kaufmann, 2016
;
Kaufmann and Koolen, 2021
)
. We describe how we adapt these tests to our setting in Appendix
D
. The GLR statistic in this setting is defined as

\hat{\Lambda}(t)\triangleq\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})}\sum_{j\in[k]}N^{Z}_{j}(t)\frac{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j})^{2}}{2},

(4)

provided that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A})
and otherwise
\hat{\Lambda}(t)
is set to zero.
\hat{\Lambda}(t)
reflects the current confidence in the best arm identified based on the observations so far. A higher value of
\hat{\Lambda}(t)
indicates greater confidence. This definition can be simplified to

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}(t)=\min_{i\neq i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}\frac{\hat{\Delta}_{i}^{2}}{2\sum_{j\in[k]}\frac{(\mathcal{A}_{j,i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}-\mathcal{A}_{j,i})^{2}}{N^{Z}_{j}(t)}}.

Appendix
D
and
D.2
provide a proof of how to compute the GLR statistic and a justification of the above simplification.

To decide whether to terminate or continue playing,
\hat{\Lambda}(t)
is compared to a sequential threshold
\hat{c}_{t}(\delta)
, which determines if the confidence is sufficiently high. To design the sequential thresholds, we apply the result by
Kaufmann and Koolen (2021)
for adaptive sequential testing, which suggests

\hat{c}_{t}(\delta)=2\sum_{j\in[k]}\ln\left(4+\ln\left(N^{Z}_{j}(t)\right)\right)+kC^{g}\left(\frac{\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}{k}\right),

(5)

where
C^{g}
is the same function introduced in Equation (
11
). The stopping time
\tau_{\delta}
is then defined as

\tau_{\delta}\triangleq\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}\mid\hat{\Lambda}(t)>\hat{c}_{t}(\delta)\}.

(6)

At the stopping time
\tau_{\delta}
, the final suggestion
\hat{i}_{\tau}
is equal to the unique estimated best arm
i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\tau_{\delta}))
. The following lemma establishes the correctness of this stopping rule.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider a bandit instance with a separator context and Gaussian reward distribution with unit variance, parameterized by the matrix
\mathcal{A}
and the vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Any algorithm with the stopping rule of (
6
) is
\delta
-correct, that is,
P_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}}(\tau_{\delta}<\infty,\hat{i}_{\tau}\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu}))\leq\delta.

Sampling Rule.
As mentioned earlier, the lower bound and stopping rule depend solely on how often each context value is observed, regardless of the actions that result in these contexts. Motivated by this observation, we introduce our new sampling method, called
Geometric Tracking (G-tracking)
, which directly tracks the optimal frequency of observed contexts.

Note that this setting can be viewed as an active off-policy learning problem in
k
-armed bandit, where each arm corresponds to a context value. However, the agent cannot directly select a context to play. Instead, they have access to
n
distinct policies (the original
n
arms in our problem), where choosing policy
i
, probabilistically selects a context according to the known distribution
\mathcal{A}_{i}
. Each vector
\mathcal{A}_{i}
lies in the
(k-1)
-dimensional simplex and encodes the probability of selecting a context by playing policy
i
.
Under this interpretation, the policy space is the convex hull of the points
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{n}
, denoted by
\text{conv}(\mathcal{A})
. In each round
t
, choosing a policy
\mathbf{p}(t)\in\text{conv}(\mathcal{A})
is equivalent to fixing a distribution on context
Z
. Notably, there exists (although not necessarily unique) a distribution over arms
X
that is compatible with
\mathbf{p}(t)
, and the agent samples an arm at time
t
according to this distribution.

We now propose a new sampling rule that directly tracks the optimal frequency of observing contexts and asymptotically achieves optimal sample complexity when combined with the stopping rule in Equation (
6
). For any vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A})
(i.e., a unique best arm exists), we define the set
\mathbf{w}_{z}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
as the set of solutions to the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{\mathbf{w}_{z}\in\text{conv}(\mathcal{A})}\min_{i\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{2\sum_{j\in[k]}\frac{(\mathcal{A}_{j,i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}-\mathcal{A}_{j,i})^{2}}{w_{z,j}}},

(7)

which corresponds to the formulation in Equation (
3
).
Unlike the non-separator setting, in the separator setting, the optimal weights may not be unique, i.e.,
\mathbf{w}_{z}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
is not a singleton. The optimization problem (
7
) is convex for each
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}
and can be solved using numerical methods such as Frank-Wolfe
(
Jaggi, 2013
;
Wang et al., 2021b
)
. Although (
7
) is non-smooth, the objective is the minimum of a finite number of smooth functions. The standard Frank–Wolfe algorithm does not provide theoretical convergence guarantees in this case, but
Wang et al. (2021b)
establish such guarantees for optimization problems whose objectives admit this structure.

\text{conv}(\mathcal{A})
.

At each round
t
, our sampling rule first solves the optimization problem (
7
) using the current empirical estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)
to find a solution
\mathbf{w}^{*}(t)\in\mathbf{w}_{z}^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})
. This vector lies within
\text{conv}(\mathcal{A})
. Recall that vector
\frac{\mathbf{N}^{Z}(t)}{t}
denotes the actual frequency of observed contexts up to round
t
and it lies in the simplex
\Delta^{k-1}
. Our sampling rule, called G-tracking, determines the next policy
\mathbf{p}(t+1)\in\text{conv}(\mathcal{A})
by connecting
\frac{\mathbf{N}^{Z}(t)}{t}
to
\mathbf{w}^{*}(t)
and extending this line until it intersects the boundary of
\text{conv}(\mathcal{A})
. The algorithm then pulls arms according to a distribution
\pi\in\Delta^{n-1}
, where
\mathcal{A}\pi=\mathbf{p}(t+1)
. A compatible distribution
\pi
always exists because
\mathbf{p}(t+1)\in\text{conv}(\mathcal{A})
, but it may not be unique. For the formal mathematical formulation of G-tracking, see Appendix
F.1
.

Figure
2
illustrates the sampling rule for an instance with
k=3
and
n=5
. Note that while the policy points always lie within
\text{conv}(\mathcal{A})
,
\frac{\mathbf{N}^{Z}(t)}{t}
may lie outside this set (as shown in Figure
2
). In such cases, the policy point
\mathbf{p}(t+1)
is the intersection point of the line passing through
\frac{\mathbf{N}^{Z}(t)}{t}
and
\mathbf{w}^{*}(t)
with the boundary of policy space, ensuring that
\mathbf{w}^{*}(t)
lies between
\mathbf{p}(t+1)
and
\frac{\mathbf{N}^{Z}(t)}{t}
.

The main advantage of the G-tracking rule is that it ensures
\mathbf{w}^{*}(t)
is a convex combination of the observed context frequencies
\frac{\mathbf{N}^{Z}(t)}{t}
and the next action
\mathbf{p}(t+1)
. Thus, if a context
i
is under-explored
\left(\frac{N^{Z}_{i}(t)}{t}\leq w^{*}_{i}(t)\right)
, then
\mathbf{p}(t+1)
allocates a probability greater than
w^{*}_{i}(t)
to that context. Conversely, for each over-explored context
i

\left(\frac{N^{Z}_{i}(t)}{t}\geq w^{*}_{i}(t)\right)
, the next policy allocates a probability lower than
w^{*}_{i}(t)
. This “balancing" property holds for all contexts simultaneously.
Note that the balancing property holds for every point on the segment connecting
\mathbf{w}^{*}(t)
to
\mathbf{p}(t+1)
. Therefore, our proof remains valid for any algorithm that picks a point on this segment. In our algorithm, we choose the
\mathbf{p}(t+1)
on the boundary of the convex hull to achieve the fastest possible tracking of the optimal weights.

4
Sub-Optimality of Ignoring Post-Action Context

In this section, we explain why algorithms that ignore the post-action context are suboptimal in the separator setting. The same discussion for the non-separator setting is deferred to Appendix
B.3

4.1
Separator Context

In the non-separator setting, the sub-optimality of ignoring
Z
stems solely from the increased variance in the reward distributions (i.e., the increase in the sub-Gaussian parameter). In contrast, the separator setting involves more substantial structural differences.
The key distinction is that, in the separator setting, the action and reward are conditionally independent given the post-action context. As a result, any observation with context
Z=j
is informative about
\mu_{j}
, regardless of which action produced it. This property implies that algorithms that ignore
Z
can suffer arbitrarily large sub-optimality in the separator setting, even when the range of reward means is small. In the following, we describe two potential sources of sub-optimality in this case:

1.
In the separator setting with known context probability matrix
\mathcal{A}
, the learner only needs to estimate the means of
k
different unit-variance Gaussian variables (i.e., the entries of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
) with high confidence to identify the best arm. In cases where
n\gg k
, ignoring
Z
forces the learner to estimate the means of
n
different random variables (sub-Gaussian with variance greater than
1
) with high confidence, which can significantly increase the required sample complexity.

2.
The correlation among the arms in the separator setting implies that, even when the mean values lie within a small range and
n=k
, algorithms that ignore
Z
may still be highly sub-optimal. Consider the following example with
k=n=3
and the parameters:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0.5\\
1\end{bmatrix},\quad\penalty\ \mathcal{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon&2\epsilon&\frac{1}{3}\\
2\epsilon&\epsilon&\frac{1}{3}\\
1-3\epsilon&1-3\epsilon&\frac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix},

where
\epsilon
is a small positive number. In this example, the first arm is optimal, and the gap between arms 1 and 2 is
\Delta_{2}=\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}-\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. According to known lower bounds in the BAI literature, any algorithm solving this instance with confidence
\delta
must take more than
\frac{4}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln(\frac{1}{\delta})
samples
(
Kaufmann et al., 2016
)
.
However, by using the post-action context, repeatedly pulling the third arm (which has a large sub-optimality gap and is thus rarely played by algorithms ignoring
Z
) can stop after just
\mathcal{O}(\ln(\frac{1}{\delta}))
samples. This follows from a standard concentration argument, since the components of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
are separated by a constant gap,
\mathcal{O}(\ln(\frac{1}{\delta}))
samples are enough to recover their ordering with high probability. This example illustrates that the cost of ignoring
Z
in the separator setting can be substantial.

4.2
Reduction to Linear Bandit

A more sophisticated approach to solve the separator setting that ignores
Z
but takes advantage of knowing the context probability matrix
\mathcal{A}
is to reduce the problem to a linear bandit problem.

In this reduction, each arm
i
is mapped to
\mathcal{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then the reward from pulling action
i
can be written as

Y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathcal{A}_{j,i}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}+R+\varepsilon=\langle\mathcal{A}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle+R+\varepsilon,

where
\varepsilon
is a standard Gaussian, and
R
is a discrete variable over
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}-\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}\mid j\in[k]\}
such that
\forall j\in[k]:P(R=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}-\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathcal{A}_{j,i}.

If we assume the entries of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
are bounded from both sides, then similar to the non-separator case, we conclude that
R+\varepsilon
is a sub-Gaussian noise but with a variance greater than
1
(which means that the sub-Gaussian parameter is bigger than
1
). Note that a similar reduction also works for the non-separator setting by corresponding a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
to each action
X=i
, where positions from
ik+1
to
ik+k
are equal to
\mathcal{A}_{i}
and others are zero.

In the following analysis, we disregard the increase in variance due to the reduction to linear bandit, as discussed above, and assume
\sigma=1
. Even with this simplification, we show that by incorporating the post-action context, the sample complexity lower bound is always less than (or equal to) that of linear bandits on the same instance. This implies that the theoretical guarantees for linear bandit do not yield tight bounds in our setting, as we discuss below.

There is no sample complexity bound for BAI in linear bandits with sub-Gaussian rewards. However, if we approximate the sample complexity bound for the Gaussian case using the result of
Soare (2015)
, we obtain

\displaystyle T^{*}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mu},A)^{-1}\approx\sup_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\Delta^{n-1}}\min_{i\neq i^{*}}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{2(A_{i^{*}}-A_{i})^{\top}\left(\sum_{j}w_{j}A_{j}A_{j}^{\top}\right)^{-1}(A_{i^{*}}-A_{i})},

while for BAI with post-action context based on Equation (
2
), we have

T^{*}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu},A)^{-1}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\Delta^{n-1}}\min_{i\neq i^{*}}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{2(A_{i^{*}}-A_{i})^{\top}D^{-1}(A_{i^{*}}-A_{i})},

where
D=\sum_{j}w_{j}\text{diag}(A_{j})
. To show that
T^{*}_{1}\geq T^{*}_{2}
, it suffices to prove that

D-\sum_{j}w_{j}A_{j}A_{j}^{\top}=\sum_{j}w_{j}\left(\text{diag}(A_{j})-A_{j}A_{j}^{\top}\right)

is positive semi-definite. This follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\quad v^{\top}\text{diag}(A_{j})v=\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}A_{i,j}=\left(\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}A_{i,j}\right)\left(\sum_{i}A_{i,j}\right)\geq(v^{\top}A_{j})^{2}=v^{\top}A_{j}A_{j}^{\top}v.

Therefore, the linear bandit reduction necessarily increases the sample complexity.

To illustrate the difference numerically, consider the small instance

\boldsymbol{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}0.2\\
0.5\\
0.3\end{bmatrix},\quad A=\begin{bmatrix}0.9&0.2&0.5&0.1\\
0.05&0.4&0.4&0.8\\
0.05&0.4&0.1&0.1\end{bmatrix}.

In this case, we compute
T^{*}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu},A)\approx 165
, while the corresponding linear bandit lower bound is
T^{*}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mu},A)\approx 1054
, showing a substantial gap.

6
Conclusion

We introduced a new BAI problem with post-action context in the fixed-confidence setting. We considered two settings for post-action context. For both settings, non-separator and separator context, we derived lower bounds on sample complexity and proposed algorithms that achieve asymptotic optimal sample complexity.

An interesting direction for future work is to consider the setting where context probability matrix
\mathcal{A}
is unknown. In this case, as discussed in Appendix
H
, the optimization problem in Equation (
1
) becomes non-convex which renders designing a computationally efficient algorithm challenging.

Appendix A
Further Discussion on Related Work

Best Arm Identification.

The literature on pure exploration in multi-armed bandit problems is extensive
[
44
,
64
]
. Here, we provide a brief overview of the most closely related works. The standard best-arm identification problem is primarily studied in two settings: the fixed-confidence setting, where the probability of error is predetermined
[
26
,
6
,
40
,
29
,
51
]
, and the fixed-budget setting, where the time horizon is fixed
[
23
,
34
,
14
]
.

[
26
]
propose the first (asymptotically) optimal algorithm for the best-arm identification problem, known as track and stop, which attempts to track the optimal proportion of arm plays. The track-and-stop idea has since been widely adopted to design algorithms for various identification problems, including the algorithms presented in this paper.

In the fixed-confidence setting, other identification problems with different objectives have been explored in the literature, such as subset selection
[
33
,
37
]
, checking for the existence of an arm with a low mean
[
66
,
38
]
, and finding all the good arms
[
52
]
. The most general form of the identification problem with bandit feedback has also been studied under various assumptions and is sometimes called
sequential identification

[
39
]
,
General-Samp

[
15
]
,
partition identification

[
32
]
, and is typically encompassed by the term pure exploration
[
74
,
16
]
.
Our problem can be viewed as a general identification problem in which the agent cannot take arbitrary actions but can only choose probability distributions over them.

Best-arm identification in contextual bandits has also received considerable attention, with optimal algorithms and different constraints
[
46
,
35
]
. In these works, however, the context is assumed to be generated (independently) before taking an action, which differs from our setting.

S
Y

:

[
60
]
.

In particular,
[
60
]
introduce an additional observation, called a
sub-population
, and study four different modes. In the first mode, the
action mode
, the learner chooses both the action and the sub-population. In the other modes, however, the sub-population is sampled independently of the chosen action. The third mode, called the
agnostic mode
, is structurally related to the causal graph in Figure
4
: the learner chooses an action and observes both the sub-population and the reward afterward. However, since the sub-population distribution is fixed and independent of the chosen action, this mode corresponds in our framework only to the special case where all columns of
\mathcal{A}
are identical. Moreover, their objective is to identify the subset of actions that outperform a given control action. Therefore, their agnostic formulation only overlaps with our setting in the restricted two-action case with identical columns of
\mathcal{A}
. In contrast, our setting allows the post-action context distribution to depend on the chosen action and aims to identify a single best action. These differences make the two problems intrinsically distinct.

A more closely related line of research, recently explored, involves the bandit problem with mediator feedback. This setting is similar to our separator context setting but investigates different objectives
[
55
,
58
,
59
,
19
]
.

In the setting of BAI with fixed-confidence, deriving a closed-form expression for GLR requires that the reward distributions belong to a one-parameter exponential family, which includes the Gaussian distribution with known variance. This assumption is commonly found in most existing works. However, recently
[
70
]
extend the GLR-based approach to the more general class of sub-Gaussian distributions.
[

show that by adjusting the threshold in the stopping rule, one can adapt the GLR expression originally designed for Gaussian distributions to achieve an appropriate stopping rule for sub-Gaussian distributions (for more details, please refer to Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 in
[
70
]
). This result shows we can further relax our assumption from Gaussian distribution to sub-Gaussian distributions but for simplicity we consider Gaussian distributions in this paper.

Causal Bandits.

Another line of research in bandits, where the agent receives additional information beyond the reward, is the field of
causal bandits
, first introduced by
[
43
]
and subsequently explored in
[
49
,
30
,
27
,
75
,
61
]
. In causal bandit problems, a (known or unknown) causal graph is assumed among a set of variables, and the actions correspond to interventions on a subset of variables within the graph. After choosing each intervention (i.e., action), the agent observes the values of all nodes, with the goal of identifying interventions that maximize the expected value of a reward node.

In this paper, we study the best-arm identification problem for two particular graphs with three variables in a causal bandit setting.
[
11
,
47
]
introduce the notions of post-action context and
conditionally benign
property, which in absence of latent variables is identical to separator case in Section
3
. However, their main focus is on cumulative regret minimization in the minimax setting, aiming to design algorithms that perform well in both conditionally benign and non-conditionally benign environments.

Linear Bandits.

The separator setting of our problem can be reduced to a linear bandit problem through the following steps. We can disregard the context variable and assume that the reward value of action
i
is drawn from a distribution with mean
\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}
and a zero-mean noise
\epsilon_{i}
. Note that in this case, the mean of this new variable is the same as the mean reward for action
i
, and if the reward means are bounded, the noise becomes sub-Gaussian (for more details on this reduction, refer to Section
4.2
).

In this setting, action
i
corresponds to a point
\mathcal{A}_{i}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, resulting in a best-arm identification problem in linear bandits with finitely many arms. This problem was first introduced by
[
62
]
and has been widely studied. Optimal algorithms for best arm identification in linear bandits are provided in
[
31
,
17
,
56
]
.
[
31
]
propose an algorithm inspired by track and stop, called lazy track and stop, while
[
17
]
present an algorithm based on game-theoretic intuition. For additional algorithms on best arm identification in linear bandits, see
[
76
,
22
]
. The causal bandit with linear assumption on the causal model is also been studied recently
[
71
,
77
]
.

Best Policy Identification in RL.

Another direction related to our problem is the best-policy identification problem in reinforcement learning, which has gained considerable attention in recent years
[
53
,
3
,
72
,
5
]
. In best-policy identification, an agent interacts with an environment consisting of actions and states, and the goal is to select, from a given set of policies, the one that maximizes the expected reward. This problem generalizes best-arm identification in bandits, and many problems in the bandit literature can be viewed as special cases of this framework. For instance, in best-arm identification in contextual bandits, if we treat different contexts as distinct starting states, set the time horizon
H=1
, and consider the actions as the set of policies, the algorithms for best-policy identification can solve the problem; for a detailed discussion, see Section 6 of
[
57
]
.

One might consider reducing a bandit with post-action contexts to an RL setting by treating post-action contexts as states. However, this approach does not apply to either the non-separator or the separator settings. The key difference lies in how the reward is determined: in RL, the reward depends on the previous action and the state from which the action is taken. In contrast, in our problem, the reward depends on the last action and the new state reached after the action (non-separator) or only on the new state (separator). This fundamental difference prevents a direct application of RL algorithms to solve our problem.

Pure exploration with safety constraints.

The classic best-arm identification algorithm fails to address many problems in which constraints on action selection arise for critical reasons. Such constraints are often used to ensure safety in learning algorithms and can restrict different phases of learning—whether in exploration or recommendation. Furthermore, constraints can be known or unknown, depending on the application. Prior work explores best-arm identification under various settings
[
73
,
67
,
21
,
12
]
.

[
13
]
also tackles a related problem, but with the key distinction that the goal is to find an optimal policy, a distribution over actions, rather than identifying a single arm. In fact,
[
13
]
is the closest work to ours, although they can choose any action (i.e., setting
Z
to a specific value in our setting) during exploration. This flexibility is not available in our setting, constituting the primary difference from their work.

In this part, we introduce the components of our algorithm and show that it achieves optimal sample complexity.

Stopping Rule.
Similar to the separator setting, we use a Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) test for the stopping rule. The GLR statistic in this setting is defined as

\hat{\Lambda}(t)\triangleq\inf_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}\in\text{Alt}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})}\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[k]}N_{j,i}(t)\frac{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j,i}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j,i})^{2}}{2},

(10)

provided that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A})
(i.e.,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)
induces a unique best arm). Otherwise,
\hat{\Lambda}(t)
is set to zero.
We can simplify Equation (
10
) to

\hat{\Lambda}(t)=\min_{i\neq i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}\frac{\hat{\Delta}_{i}^{2}}{2\sum_{j\in[k]}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{j,i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}^{2}}{N_{j,i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}(t)}+\frac{\mathcal{A}_{j,i}^{2}}{N_{j,i}(t)}},

which allows
\hat{\Lambda}(t)
to be computed efficiently. Appendix
D.1
provides a detailed proof of this simplification.

To derive the sequential thresholds
\hat{c}_{t}(\delta)
, we use the deviation inequalities proposed by
[
39
]
, based on
mixture martingales
. For each
\delta\in(0,1]
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the threshold
\hat{c}_{t}(\delta)
is derived as

\hat{c}_{t}(\delta)=4k\ln\left(4+\ln\left(\frac{t}{2k}\right)\right)+2kC^{g}\left(\frac{\ln\left(\frac{n-1}{\delta}\right)}{2k}\right),

(11)

where
C^{g}(x)\simeq x+\ln(x)
(refer to Definition
E.1
). For more details on the derivation of the thresholds, see the proof of Lemma
B.2
in Appendix
E
. The stopping time
\tau_{\delta}
is then defined as

\tau_{\delta}\triangleq\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}\mid\hat{\Lambda}(t)>\hat{c}_{t}(\delta)\}.

(12)

At the stopping time
\tau_{\delta}
, the final suggestion
\hat{i}_{\tau}
is equal to the unique estimated best arm
i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\tau_{\delta}))
. The following lemma establishes the correctness of this stopping rule when combined with any sampling rule.

Lemma B.2
.

Consider a bandit instance with a non-separator context and Gaussian reward distribution with unit variance, parameterized by matrices
\mathcal{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Any algorithm with the stopping rule (
12
) is
\delta
-correct, that is,

P_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}}(\tau_{\delta}<\infty,\hat{i}_{\tau}\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu}))\leq\delta.

Sampling Rule.

We now propose a sampling strategy, which asymptotically achieves optimal sample complexity with the stopping rule in (
12
). Define
\mathbf{w}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
as the solution to the optimization problem

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{n-1}}\min_{i\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}w_{i}}{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}+w_{i}}\right).

For any
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}
, the optimal weights
\mathbf{w}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
are unique and can be efficiently estimated (see Appendix
G
). In our theoretical analysis, we assume the existence of an oracle that computes
\mathbf{w}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
exactly. However, in practice, we estimate the optimal weights
\mathbf{w}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
. These weights correspond to the fraction of times each arm is pulled by an optimal algorithm. Based on this intuition, various algorithms have been developed for different identification problems in the literature. These algorithms compute the optimal weights at each step given the estimated reward matrix
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)
and track these sequential weights. We adopt the
D-tracking
sampling rule first introduced by
[
26
]
. At round
t
of the algorithm, the D-tracking rule defines
\mathcal{U}_{t}=\{i\in[n]\ \big|\ N^{X}_{i}(t)\leq\max\left(\sqrt{t}-\frac{n}{2},\,0\right)\},
which contains the arms that are under-sampled. The algorithm then selects the next action
X_{t+1}
as

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=\begin{cases}\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{i\in\mathcal{U}_{t}}N^{X}_{i}(t)\hskip 71.13188pt\text{ if }\mathcal{U}_{t}\neq\emptyset,&\\
\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{i\in[n]}tw_{i}^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})-N^{X}_{i}(t)\quad\text{ o.w.}&\end{cases}

(13)

It is noteworthy that D-tracking operates correctly only when
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A})
, that is, a single optimal arm exists. If this condition is not satisfied at any step
t
, the next action is selected uniformly random. This scenario almost surely ceases to occur as the number of samples increases and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)
converges to
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A})
, which is assumed to imply a single best arm.

The pseudocode of our algorithm, called
Non-Separator Track and Stop (NSTS)
, is presented in Algorithm
2
. The following theorem proves its optimality.

B.3
Non-Optimality of Ignoring Non-Separator Context

By comparing the non-separator lower bound presented in Equation (
9
) with the lower bound for the BAI problem with unit-variance Gaussian rewards in
[
36
]
, we observe that the optimization problems characterizing the lower bounds are identical. At first glance, this may suggest that the post-action context is unnecessary. However, the key distinction lies in the reward distribution: when
Z
is ignored, the resulting reward distribution becomes a mixture of
k
unit-variance Gaussians, rather than a single Gaussian.

Since mixtures of Gaussians do not belong to the exponential family of distributions, algorithms developed for BAI within this family, like track and stop, are not directly applicable, and there are no theoretical guarantees for these distributions.
One possible way is to exploit the fact that a mixture of Gaussians is sub-Gaussian. More precisely, the reward distribution for each action
X=i
follows a sub-Gaussian distribution with
\sigma^{2}=1+\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{4}
, where
b-a
denotes the range of the reward means for
X=i
and different context values, i.e.,
\mu_{j,i}\in[a,b]
for all
j\in[k]
(by Lemma 4 of
[
60
]
). For BAI with sub-Gaussian rewards of parameter
\sigma^{2}
, the sample complexity can be upper bounded by the sample complexity of BAI with
\sigma^{2}
-variance Gaussian rewards.

Proof of General Lower bound: proposition
2.1

The proof employs the transportation lemma as outlined in Lemma 1 of
[
36
]
. The proof of the transportation lemma, which can be found in Appendix A and A.1 of
[
36
]
, relies on changes of distributions and the log-likelihood ratio expression. Extending this theorem to our setup is quite straightforward; we simply need to incorporate the context variables into the log-likelihood ratio formula, which means using

L_{t}=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{N^{X}_{a}(t)}\log\left(\frac{f_{a}(Z_{a,s},Y_{a,s})}{f^{\prime}_{a}(Z_{a,s},Y_{a,s})}\right),

instead of

L_{t}=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{N^{X}_{i}(t)}\log\left(\frac{f_{a}(Y_{a,s})}{f^{\prime}_{a}(Y_{a,s})}\right),

in Appendix A of
[
36
]
. All the proofs of the transportation lemma remain unchanged. Therefore, based on the transportation lemma for our setting, for each
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[N^{X}_{i}(\tau_{\delta})]d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i},P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}_{i})\geq\sup_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{\delta}}}d(P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\mathcal{E}),P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E})).

Define event
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\{\tau_{\delta}<\infty,\hat{i}_{\tau_{\delta}}\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\}
, then
P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})\geq 1-\delta
and
P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})\leq\delta
according to
\delta

\sup_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{\delta}}}d(P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\mathcal{E}),P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E}))\geq d(P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}),P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}))\geq d_{B}(\delta,1-\delta),

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[N^{X}_{i}(\tau_{\delta})]d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i},P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}_{i})\geq d_{B}(\delta,1-\delta).

Therefore,

\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[N^{X}_{i}(\tau_{\delta})]d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i},P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}_{i})\geq d_{B}(\delta,1-\delta).

Then, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[N^{X}_{i}(\tau_{\delta})]d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i},P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\delta}]\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}[N^{X}_{i}(\tau_{\delta})]}{\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\delta}]}d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i},P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}_{i})

which concludes the proof.

Appendix D
Generalized Likelihood Ratio Test

The Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) is used to measure our confidence in a hypothesis. Let have
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega_{1}
be two model spaces. We wish to know whether a model
\lambda
belongs to
\Omega_{0}
or
\Omega_{1}
. To do this, we can define two hypotheses: (i)
H_{0}:(\lambda\in\Omega_{0})
and (ii)
H_{1}:(\lambda\in\Omega_{1}
). The log-likelihood ratio of this test after
t
observations is defined as

\hat{\Lambda}(t)\triangleq\log\frac{\sup_{\lambda\in\Omega_{0}\cup\Omega_{1}}\mathcal{L}(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{t};\lambda)}{\sup_{\lambda\in\Omega_{0}}\mathcal{L}(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{t};\lambda)},

(14)

where
\mathcal{L}(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{t};\lambda)
is the likelihood function of observations (i.e.,
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{t}
) associated to parameter
\lambda
. If
\hat{\Lambda}(t)
has a high value, we could reject the hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

Our problem in both settings can be viewed as a
general identification

[
39
]
. In this problem, we assume
\mathcal{I}
is partitioned into
n
subsets
\mathcal{O}_{1},\mathcal{O}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{n}
, and the goal is to determine which subset contains vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. At round
t
, we have an estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)
and we need to decide whether we can confidently choose the correct subset with low risk. Refer to Section 4 of
[
39
]
for more details. In our problem, we can define the subsets

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{i}\triangleq\left\{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}\ \big|\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}>\mathcal{A}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j},\ \forall j\neq i\right\}.

Suppose that at round
t
of our problem,
i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))
is not unique, meaning
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)
is not in
\mathcal{I}
. In this case, we lack sufficient information to determine the best arm (or, find the correct subset in general identification problem), so we must continue sampling. If
i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))
indicates a unique arm, however, we need a measure to evaluate our confidence. We consider the following hypothesis test.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}:(i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\neq i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}:(i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))),

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}:(\boldsymbol{\mu}\notin\mathcal{O}_{i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))})=(\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\text{Alt}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}:(\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{O}_{i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}).

Hence, if
\hat{\Lambda}(t)
is high, we can reject the
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, indicating with high confidence that
i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))
is indeed the best arm

Note that at round
t
, the observed samples are
((Z_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(Z_{t},Y_{t}))
for the chosen actions
(i_{1},\dots,i_{t})
. Consequently,

\hat{\Lambda}(t)=\log\frac{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in I}\mathcal{L}((Z_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(Z_{t},Y_{t});\boldsymbol{\mu})}{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\text{Alt}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})}\mathcal{L}((Z_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(Z_{t},Y_{t});\boldsymbol{\mu})},

which expands to

\displaystyle\log\frac{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in I}\prod_{s\in[t]}P(Z=Z_{s}\mid X=i_{s})P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}(Y=Y_{s}\mid X=i_{s},Z=Z_{s})}{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})}\prod_{s\in[t]}P(Z=Z_{s}\mid X=i_{s})P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}(Y=Y_{s}\mid X=i_{s},Z=Z_{s})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\frac{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in I}\prod_{s\in[t]}\mathcal{A}_{Z_{s},i_{s}}P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}(Y=Y_{s}\mid X=i_{s},Z=Z_{s})}{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})}\prod_{s\in[t]}\mathcal{A}_{Z_{s},i_{s}}P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}(Y=Y_{s}\mid X=i_{s},Z=Z_{s})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\frac{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in I}\prod_{s\in[t]}P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}(Y=Y_{s}\mid X=i_{s},Z=Z_{s})}{\sup_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})}\prod_{s\in[t]}P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}(Y=Y_{s}\mid X=i_{s},Z=Z_{s})}

(15)

In the following two parts, for each case of context, we derive a simpler expression for
\hat{\Lambda}(t)
under the non-separator and separator context.

D.1
Non-separator context

Note that in this case,
P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(Y=Y_{s}\mid X=i_{s},Z=Z_{s})
is not necessarily equal to
P_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(Y=Y_{s}\mid Z=Z_{s})
. By assumption,
Y\mid X=i_{s},Z=Z_{s}
follows a Gaussian distribution with unit variance, which is a one-parameter exponential family. For this case, we can simplify Equation (
15

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})}\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[k]}N_{j,i}(t)d(\mathcal{N}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j,i}(t),1),\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j,i},1))

\displaystyle=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\text{Alt}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t),\mathcal{A})}\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[k]}N_{j,i}(t)\frac{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j,i}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j,i})^{2}}{2}

where
\mathcal{N}(x,1)
denotes Gaussian distribution with mean
x
and unit variance and we used the fact

which is equal to

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[k]}N_{j,i}(t)\frac{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j,i}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j,i})^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\mathcal{A}_{s}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{s}>\mathcal{A}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}.

for all

\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}}\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[k]}N_{j,i}(t)\frac{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j,i}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j,i})^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}}\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[k]}N_{j,i}(t)\frac{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j,i}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j,i})^{2}}{2}

(16)

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j,i}(t)&\text{o.w.}\end{cases}

Substituting

Hence,

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{s}}\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[k]}N_{j,i}(t)\frac{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j,i}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{j,i})^{2}}{2}=\frac{\hat{\Delta}_{s}^{2}}{2\sum_{j\in[k]}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{j,i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}^{2}}{N_{j,i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}(t)}+\frac{\mathcal{A}_{j,s}^{2}}{N_{j,s}(t)}},

Finally, the simplified form of
\hat{\Lambda}(t)
becomes

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}(t)=\min_{s\neq i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}\frac{\hat{\Delta}_{s}^{2}}{2\sum_{j\in[k]}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{j,i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}^{2}}{N_{j,i^{*}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t))}(t)}+\frac{\mathcal{A}_{j,s}^{2}}{N_{j,s}(t)}},

Lemma E.2
.

Consider a bandit instance with a non-separator context and Gaussian reward distribution with unit variance, parameterized by matrices
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\mathcal{A}
. For any weight vector
\mathbf{w}
such that
\forall s\in[n]:w_{s}>0
, the value of
f_{i}(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\sum_{s\in[n]}w_{s}d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{s},P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}_{s})
can be explicitly determined as

\displaystyle f_{i}(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}w_{i}}{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}+w_{i}}\right).

Proof.

First note that
P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{s}
and
P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}_{s}
are the distribution of random vectors
(Z,Y)
and
(Z^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
corresponding to bandit instances with context probability matrix
\mathcal{A}
and means matrix
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}
, respectively. Since reward distributions are Gaussian with unit variance, the KL divergence can be written as

\displaystyle d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{s},P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}_{s})=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[k]}P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{s}(Z=j)d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{s}(Y\mid Z=j),P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}_{s}(Y\mid Z=j))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{A}_{j,s}d(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{j,s},1),\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime}_{j,s},1))=\sum_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{A}_{j,s}\frac{(\mu_{j,s}-\mu^{\prime}_{j,s})^{2}}{2}.

Recall the definition

\mathcal{C}_{i}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}\big|\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i}>\mathcal{A}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}\right\}.

Note that

is continuous with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\mathcal{I}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
. Therefore, we can express
f_{i}(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
as the solution to the following optimization problem

\displaystyle f_{i}(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=

\displaystyle\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}}}\sum_{s\in[n]}w_{s}\sum_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{A}_{j,s}\frac{(\mu_{j,s}-\mu^{\prime}_{j,s})^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i}>\mathcal{A}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}.

The solution to this problem is equivalent to the solution to the following problem when
\alpha\rightarrow 0
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}}\sum_{s\in[n]}w_{s}\sum_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{A}_{j,s}\frac{(\mu_{j,s}-\mu^{\prime}_{j,s})^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i}\geq\mathcal{A}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}+\alpha.

To solve the second problem, we write the Lagrangian as

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\lambda)=\sum_{s\in[n]}w_{s}\sum_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{A}_{j,s}\frac{(\mu_{j,s}-\mu^{\prime}_{j,s})^{2}}{2}+\lambda(\mathcal{A}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}-\mathcal{A}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i}+\alpha).

By calculating the derivative of

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime*}_{j,t}=\begin{cases}\mu_{j,i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}-\frac{\lambda}{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}}\quad&t=i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu}),\\
\mu_{j,i}+\frac{\lambda}{w_{i}}\quad&t=i,\\
\mu_{j,t}\quad&o.w.\end{cases}

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}=\frac{\Delta_{i}+\alpha}{\left(\frac{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}+w_{i}}{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}w_{i}}\right)}.

By inserting these values into the objective function, we have

\displaystyle f_{i}(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\frac{(\Delta_{i}+\alpha)^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}w_{i}}{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}+w_{i}}\right)=\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}w_{i}}{w_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}+w_{i}}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

For a given

F.1
Mathematical Formulation of G-tracking

At round
t
, the G-tracking rule selects the next target point as the intersection of the ray from
\overline{N}(t)=\frac{N^{Z}(t)}{t}
toward
w^{*}(t)
with the boundary of the convex hull of the context probability vectors
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

This ray is given by the set of points of the form:

\overline{N}(t)+\alpha(w^{*}(t)-\overline{N}(t)),\quad\text{for }\alpha\geq 1.

We want the largest
\alpha\geq 1
such that this point still lies within
\text{conv}(\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{n})
, which is the set
\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}\,\middle|\,\pi\in\Delta^{n-1}\right\}
.

This gives rise to the following linear program:

\displaystyle\max_{\alpha,\pi}\ \alpha

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\ \overline{N}(t)+\alpha(w^{*}(t)-\overline{N}(t))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i},

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}=1,\quad\pi\geq 0,\quad\alpha\geq 1.

To implement the G-tracking rule, we solved the above linear program, and
\pi_{i}
is the desired policy.

Appendix G
Discussion on Solving the Optimization Problem of Lower bound

The optimization problem (
9
) can be solved efficiently and with arbitrary precision. Notably, the same optimization problem arises in the classic BAI problem with Gaussian rewards
[
8
]
. Although the definitions of
\Delta_{i}
differ between these two settings—and in this paper, these gaps depend on the context probability matrix—the optimization problem itself remains similar, allowing the use of similar techniques to solve it.

Lemma G.1

(
[
8
]
)
.

Consider the equation

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]\\
i\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{(w\Delta_{i}^{2}-1)^{2}}=1,

(26)

where
\Delta_{i}>0
. This equation has a unique solution
w^{*}
in the interval
\left[\frac{2}{\Delta_{min}^{2}},\frac{1+\sqrt{n-1}}{\Delta_{min}^{2}}\right]
. Define
\mathbf{u}=\left(\frac{w^{*}}{w^{*}\Delta_{1}^{2}-1},\frac{w^{*}}{w^{*}\Delta_{2}^{2}-1},\ldots,w^{*},\dots,\frac{w^{*}}{w^{*}\Delta_{n}^{2}-1}\right)
, where
i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
-th element is
w^{*}
. The normalized vector
\frac{\mathbf{u}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{1}}\in\Delta^{n-1}
is the unique solution of Equation (
9
).

As discussed in
[
8
]
, the left-hand side of Equation (
26
) is strictly decreasing over the interval
\left[\frac{2}{\Delta_{min}^{2}},\frac{1+\sqrt{n-1}}{\Delta_{min}^{2}}\right]
. Consequently, simple numerical methods, such as binary search, can be employed to approximate the solution efficiently. Leveraging this property, we can efficiently compute the weights for the optimization problem in Equation (
9
). This capability enables the design of an efficient learning algorithm, as discussed later.

Appendix H
Discussion on the Case of Unknown Context Probability Matrix

The assumption that the context probability matrix
\mathcal{A}
is known does not always hold in practice. A natural question, then, is whether the problem becomes fundamentally harder, in terms of minimum sample complexity, when
\mathcal{A}
is unknown. For instance, in the setting of contextual bandits,
[
57
]
showed that the sample complexity of BAI with unknown context distributions remains of the same order as in the case with known distributions. This indicates that, in that setting, not knowing the context distribution does not significantly increase the difficulty of the problem.

However, the following lemma shows that this is not the case in the separator post-action context setting. In this setting, the absence of knowledge about
\mathcal{A}
can arbitrarily increase the sample complexity lower bound, underscoring a fundamental difference from the contextual bandit setting.

Lemma H.1
.

Consider the instance with
n=k=3
and parameters

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon&2\epsilon&\frac{1}{2}\\
1-\epsilon&1-2\epsilon&\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix},

(27)

where
\epsilon
is a small positive real number. Then, we have

1.

On instance (
27
) with known
\mathcal{A}
, the characteristic
T_{S}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
defined in (
2
) is equal to
8
.

2.

For any
\delta
-correct algorithm
(P_{t},\tau,\hat{i})
interacting with instance (
27
) with unknown
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}}[\tau]\geq\frac{1}{4\epsilon}d_{B}(\delta,1-\delta)
.

Proof.

To prove the first part, recall that

\displaystyle T_{S}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})^{-1}=\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{2}}\min_{i\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{2\sum_{j\in[k]}\frac{(\mathcal{A}_{j,i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}-\mathcal{A}_{j,i})^{2}}{\sum_{l\in[n]}w_{l}\mathcal{A}_{j,l}}}.

Note that
i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=1
,
\Delta_{2}=\epsilon
, and
\Delta_{3}=\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
. Moreover, for each
i\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}),j\in[k]
,
\Delta_{i}^{2}=(\mathcal{A}_{j,i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}-\mathcal{A}_{j,i})^{2}
. After inserting and simplification, we have

T_{S}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})^{-1}=\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{2}}\frac{1}{2\left(\frac{1}{w_{1}\epsilon+2w_{2}\epsilon+\frac{w_{3}}{2}}+\frac{1}{w_{1}(1-\epsilon)+w_{2}(1-2\epsilon)+\frac{w_{3}}{2}}\right)}=\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{2}}\frac{w_{z,1}w_{z,2}}{2(w_{z,1}+w_{z,2})},

where
w_{z,1}=w_{1}\epsilon+2w_{2}\epsilon+\frac{w_{3}}{2}
and
w_{z,2}=w_{1}(1-\epsilon)+w_{2}(1-2\epsilon)+\frac{w_{3}}{2}
. Then setting
\mathbf{w}=(0,0,1)
implies
w_{z,1}=w_{z,2}=\frac{1}{2}
, then

T_{S}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})^{-1}\geq\frac{1}{8}.

On the other hand, since
w_{z,1}+w_{z,2}=1
, we have
w_{z,1}w_{z,2}\leq\frac{1}{4}
and
\frac{w_{z,1}w_{z,2}}{2(w_{z,1}+w_{z,2})}\leq\frac{1}{8}
, which implies

T_{S}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})^{-1}\leq\frac{1}{8}.

Therefore, we conclude that
T_{S}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=8
.

To prove the second part, note that Equation (
1
), by considering the instance
(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\in\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
with parameters

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\mu},\quad\penalty\ \mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}3\epsilon&2\epsilon&\frac{1}{2}\\
1-3\epsilon&1-2\epsilon&\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix},

implies that for any
\delta
-correct algorithm
(P_{t},\tau,\hat{i})
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}}[\tau]\geq T^{*}(\mu,\mathcal{A})d_{B}(\delta,1-\delta)
, where

T^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})^{-1}\leq\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{2}}\sum_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}w_{i}d\left(P_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}},P_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\right).

Since these two instances only differ in the first column of their context probability matrix, then

\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{2}}\sum_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}w_{i}d\left(P_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}},P_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\right)=\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{2}}w_{1}d(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{1}),

where

denotes the KL divergence between two binary variables and is equal to

d(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{1})=\epsilon\ln\left(\frac{\epsilon}{3\epsilon}\right)+(1-\epsilon)\ln\left(\frac{1-\epsilon}{1-3\epsilon}\right)=-\epsilon\ln(3)+(1-\epsilon)\ln\left(\frac{1-\epsilon}{1-3\epsilon}\right).

We can upper bound

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{1-\epsilon}{1-3\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{1-\epsilon}{1-3\epsilon}-1=\frac{2\epsilon}{1-3\epsilon}

\displaystyle{\mathrel{\to}^{1-\epsilon<2(1-3\epsilon)}}d(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{1})\leq-\epsilon\ln(3)+4\epsilon<4\epsilon

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow T^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})^{-1}\leq\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{2}}w_{1}d(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{1})\leq 4\epsilon,

Now, we discuss the main challenge in designing optimal algorithms for the case of unknown
\mathcal{A}
. When
\mathcal{A}
is unknown, the problem becomes more challenging because the set of alternative parameters (Alt) changes in structure. Recall that all tracking algorithms based on
[
26
]
must solve the optimization problem in Equation (
1
) to determine the optimal weights. Alternatively, one can use the formulation in Equation (
17
), but both approaches involve a minimization over some set. The main practical complexity of tracking algorithms lies in solving this optimization problem.

If an oracle were available to compute the optimal weights in Equation (
1
), one could still use tracking algorithms even when
\mathcal{A}
is unknown and achieve the optimal sample complexity. Note that the GLR statistic and the stopping rule need to be adapted to this setting.

When
\mathcal{A}
is unknown, both the matrices
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
should be learned by the agent during the learning process and if the error of estimation of each of them is high, the agent cannot stop. Let
\mathcal{I}^{u}
denotes the set of parameter pairs
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
such that the best arm
i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
is unique. Then for a problem instance parameterized by
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
, we define

\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=\{(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}^{u}\mid i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})\}.

Similar to the case of known
\mathcal{A}
,
\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
can be written as Alt
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=\cup_{i\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}\mathcal{C}_{i}
, where

\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}^{u}\mid\mathcal{A}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i}>\mathcal{A}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}^{{}^{\prime}\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}\}.

When
\mathcal{A}
is known, the sets
\mathcal{C}_{j}
or
Alt
are convex, so standard convex optimization tools can be applied. However, when
\mathcal{A}
is unknown, the sets
\mathcal{C}_{j}
or Alt, may not be convex, and thus finding optimal weights via convex optimization methods becomes infeasible. Similar issues have been noted in the reinforcement learning literature
[
4
]
.

As mentioned earlier, the case of separator context is a special case of non-separator setting, so it suffices to show non-convexity for the separator case. Note that if
\mathcal{A}
is unknown, the set
\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
changes because
\mathcal{A}
itself can vary within the alternative set. Formally, the new definition of

(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
for this setting is

\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=\{(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}\mid i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})\},

where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the set of all instances (parameterized by
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
) that have a unique best arm. Similar to known setting, we have Alt
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=\cup_{i\neq i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}\mathcal{C}_{i}
, and

\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}\mid\mathcal{A}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}>\mathcal{A}_{i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}\}.

As mentioned earlier, we need to solve

\inf_{(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\in\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})}\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}}_{i},P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}_{i}),

or equivalently, using Equation (
17
),

\displaystyle f_{j}(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})=\inf_{(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}d(P^{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A}}_{i},P^{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}_{i}).

(28)

For the case of known
\mathcal{A}
, each set
\mathcal{C}_{j}
is convex, so
f_{j}(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
reduces to a convex problem that can be solved via convex optimization methods. However, if
\mathcal{A}
is unknown, that convexity property no longer holds. The following example shows that neither
\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
nor
\mathcal{C}_{j}
is necessarily convex.

equals

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0.6&0.4\\

(32)

Now, we have
i^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(3)},\mathcal{A}^{(3)})=1
. It indicates that
(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(3)},\mathcal{A}^{(3)})
is
not
in
\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
, which shows that
\text{Alt}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{A})
is not a convex set.

Further Experiments

This section contains additional details on the experimental setup and the results of further experiments for both non-separator and separator settings.

I.1
Real-World Data Experiment

We also evaluate our methods on instances constructed from the
KuaiSAR
recommendation logs
[
65
]
, a real-world short video recommendation dataset containing user–item interaction records together with feedback signals such as clicks, likes, and watch behavior. The goal of these experiments is not to provide a large-scale benchmark, but rather to verify on real interaction data that the empirical behavior is consistent with the theoretical predictions of our framework.

For the non-separator setting, we construct a BAI instance directly from the real data. We use first level content categories as actions, resulting in
n=7
actions with sufficient support. Concretely, each action corresponds to recommending an item from one of these content categories, so pulling an arm means selecting a category and then observing the feedback generated by an interaction record associated with that category. Operationally, whenever the learner selects an action, one row of the dataset corresponding to that action is sampled uniformly at random, and the observed post-action context and reward are taken from that sampled interaction record.
As post-action context, we consider three discrete feedback outcomes observed after recommendation exposure:
(i) no click,
(ii) click only, and
(iii) click+like.
As reward, we use the clipped watch-ratio

R=\min\!\left(\frac{\text{playing time}}{\text{duration}},\,3\right),

where
playing time
is the total time the user spent watching the recommended video and
duration
is the nominal video length. This ratio is a standard normalized engagement measure; it can exceed
1
because users may replay or rewatch parts of a video, so the accumulated playing time can be longer than the video duration. We clip the ratio at
3
to control extreme values while preserving meaningful variation in engagement. To obtain a statistically meaningful BAI instance, we retain actions with sufficient support across all three context values and enforce a minimum gap between the best and second best arms. This yields a genuine non-separator instance: even after conditioning on the post-action context, the expected reward still depends on the action.

For the separator setting, we start from the same real interaction data and use it to estimate the post-action context distribution of each action. We then construct rewards so that they depend only on the realized context. More precisely, for each context value
z
, we compute its empirical mean reward
\mu_{z}
from the data and generate rewards as

Y=\mu_{z}+\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Hence, the action still influences which context is observed, but conditional on the context, the reward is independent of the action. Although this separator instance is not fully raw real-world data, it remains tightly grounded in the dataset because both the action-dependent context probabilities and the context level reward means are estimated from real user interactions.

For both settings, we report the average stopping time over
20
independent runs. For the TS baseline on the non-separator instance, since the reward is bounded in
[0,3]
, we use the Gaussian stopping rule with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}=(3-0)^{2}/4=9/4
, corresponding to the standard sub-Gaussian parameter guaranteed by Hoeffding’s lemma for bounded random variables. The results are shown in Table
2
. In the non-separator setting, NSTS significantly improves over TS. In the separator setting, STS dramatically outperforms context-ignoring baselines, which fail to stop within the horizon of
50{,}000
rounds. These results are consistent with the main message of the paper: exploiting post-action context leads to substantial gains when used in a manner aligned with the underlying problem structure.
