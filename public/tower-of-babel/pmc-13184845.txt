Early Healing After Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion Confirmed by Surgical Explantation
Authors: Kaoru Hasegawa, Minoru Yambe, Kazushi Kinebuchi, Kosei Matsuda, Yuhi Hasebe, Takahiro Ohara, Shunsuke Kawamoto, Koji Kumagai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealing of left atrial appendage (LAA) occlusion devices is believed to occur over several months; however, direct histological confirmation in humans remains limited.Case SummaryAn elderly patient underwent LAA occlusion and subsequently required total arch replacement 3 months later because of progressive aortic pathology. The device had previously been noted to have suboptimal positioning. Surgical explantation provided a rare opportunity for direct tissue evaluation. Histological examination demonstrated fibrous tissue coverage over the portion of the device in contact with the LAA wall, whereas the atrial-facing filter surface showed no tissue coverage.DiscussionThis case provides rare direct histological evidence of early fibrous tissue coverage after LAA occlusion and offers unique insight into the biological healing process after device implantation.Take-Home MessageEarly fibrous coverage after LAA occlusion may occur within 3 months, as demonstrated by surgical explantation.

History of Presentation

A 75-year-old man had been diagnosed with atrial fibrillation at the age of 71 and was treated with warfarin. At the age of 73, he developed a thalamic hemorrhage, and anticoagulation therapy was switched to edoxaban 15 mg. At the age of 75, in the setting of endovascular abdominal aortic aneurysm repair (EVAR), the cardiac surgery team recommended left atrial appendage (LAA) occlusion (LAAO) because of thrombocytopenia, prior intracranial hemorrhage, a CHA2DS2-VASc score of 6, and a HAS-BLED score of 3.Take-Home Message•Surgical explantation 3 months after left atrial appendage occlusion provided direct histological assessment of device healing.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and cough-variant asthma. He also had thrombocytopenia, which was under observation. At the age of 66, he underwent radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer. Atrial fibrillation had been present since the age of 71, and anticoagulation therapy with warfarin had been initiated. At the age of 72, he underwent thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) with axillo-axillary bypass and left subclavian artery embolization for a thoracic aortic aneurysm, during which a type IV endoleak was identified. In the same year, he was hospitalized for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction caused by tachycardia-induced atrial fibrillation. At the age of 73, coil embolization of the subclavian artery was performed for persistent endoleak, and later that year, he developed a thalamic hemorrhage. At the age of 75, he underwent EVAR for an abdominal aortic aneurysm.

Investigations

LAAO was performed using a Watchman FLX Pro device (Boston Scientific). Because the LAA was large and had thick pectinate muscles, device deployment was challenging. A 35-mm device was initially implanted; however, it became dislodged during the tug test. Therefore, the device was upsized to 40 mm and successfully implanted. The final device position after tug testing confirmed stable fixation (Figure 1). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) performed for device follow-up in conjunction with routine EVAR surveillance demonstrated medial shoulder protrusion of the device with contrast opacification of the LAA, consistent with a peridevice leak (Figure 3A). No thrombus was identified on the atrial surface of the device. However, contrast filling defects were observed within the LAA side of the filter, consistent with thrombus formation. Regarding the aorta, rapid expansion was noted at the prior TEVAR site, although a clear source of endoleak could not be identified. Equipment

TEE was performed using an EPIQ ultrasound system with an X8-2t transesophageal probe (Philips Healthcare). Contrast-enhanced CT was acquired using a 360-row multidetector CT scanner (Aquilion ONE, Canon Medical Systems). LAA closure was performed using a 40-mm Watchman FLX Pro device (Boston Scientific).

Management

Based on the TEE findings, continued observation of the device was considered reasonable. However, because of rapid enlargement of the aortic arch aneurysm, elective total arch replacement was planned.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Three months after implantation, while awaiting planned aortic surgery, the patient developed hemoptysis. Emergent contrast-enhanced CT revealed a frank endoleak (Figure 3B), and urgent total arch replacement was performed, along with LAA resection and surgical removal of the device.

No device-related thrombus was observed, and the peridevice leak was minimal. Although the device position was considered within acceptable limits, early imaging had demonstrated suboptimal positioning. While the risk of future complications, including device-related thrombus or device instability, was considered low, it could not be completely excluded. Because the patient required open aortic surgery under cardiopulmonary bypass and the additional procedural burden was limited, device removal and surgical excision of the LAA were performed after multidisciplinary discussion.

Surgery was performed under general anesthesia via median sternotomy. Upon incising the tip of the LAA, the device was directly visualized. Thick pectinate muscles were divided to expose the device. The device was firmly anchored by the distal anchors. Gross inspection suggested circumferential tissue coverage of the device struts (Figure 4A). After careful dissection, the device was explanted (Video 2). Notably, no tissue coverage was observed on the filter membrane, and organized thrombus was present on the inner (LAA) surface of the filter (Figure 4B). Histopathological Findings

Histological examination demonstrated thin fibrous and granulation tissue covering the device surface. The surface was partially covered by flattened cells (Figure 5A). Immunohistochemical staining for endothelial markers (CD31 and CD34) and mesothelial markers (calretinin and D2-40) was negative, and the cellular origin could not be definitively determined. These findings suggest early fibrous tissue coverage rather than mature endothelialization. In addition, thrombus containing fibrin was observed within the filter on the LAA side of the device (Figure 5B).Figure 5Histopathological Findings of Tissue Associated With the Explanted Device(A) Hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrating fibrous tissue obtained from the device surface. Discussion

Transcatheter LAAO has been established as an alternative to long-term oral anticoagulation for stroke prevention in patients with atrial fibrillation, with randomized data demonstrating noninferiority to warfarin therapy.1 Major late complications include device embolization and device-related thrombus.1 Although percutaneous or surgical retrieval of migrated devices has been reported,2,3 surgical explantation remains uncommon, and current guidelines provide limited guidance regarding device removal once implanted.4

Endothelialization of LAAO devices is generally believed to progress over several weeks to months. Experimental studies have demonstrated progressive neointimal coverage by 45 days, with more complete healing by 90 days.5 However, direct histological confirmation in humans remains limited, and most assumptions regarding healing are inferred from imaging findings.

In the present case, surgical explantation 3 months after implantation provided a rare opportunity for direct tissue-level evaluation. Gross inspection demonstrated circumferential fibrous coverage of the device struts, and histological assessment demonstrated fibrous and granulation tissue covering the device surface. These findings suggest early tissue coverage of the device struts within 3 months after implantation.

Importantly, healing appeared heterogeneous. While the struts demonstrated fibrous tissue coverage, the filter membrane showed no endothelialization, and organized thrombus was present on the LAA side. This regional variation indicates that endothelialization may not occur uniformly across all device components and that imaging findings may not fully capture microstructural healing differences.

Although small peridevice leaks (<5 mm) are generally considered acceptable, long-term data suggest a possible association with increased embolic risk.6 Meta-analyses have shown that CT is more sensitive than TEE for detecting residual leaks, although the clinical significance of CT-detected leaks remains uncertain.7 In this case, surgical explantation provided a rare opportunity for direct histological evaluation of device healing.

This case underscores the unique value of direct human histopathological evidence in understanding biological healing after LAAO. Further accumulation of similar cases may help clarify the relationship between device healing, thrombus formation, and long-term clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

This case describes a rare patient who developed rapid enlargement of a thoracic aortic aneurysm after LAAO device implantation and who underwent total arch replacement with concomitant LAA resection and device explantation. Intraoperative findings confirmed circumferential fibrous coverage of the device struts 3 months after implantation, along with thrombus formation inside the filter portion. This pathological evidence provides important academic and educational insight by validating the healing process previously inferred from imaging. The present case highlights the unique value of surgical explantation in enabling direct histological evaluation of device healing.
