Autism in adolescence and adulthood
Authors: Kristina Denisova, Catherine R. G. Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Landmark descriptions of autism in the 1940s were based on clinical work with children. Research over the subsequent 80 years has largely focused on the experiences and needs of autistic children, with recent neuroimaging and genetic studies contributing significant insights into the developmentally driven neurobiological underpinnings of this condition. However, autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition and adolescence and adulthood introduces new developmental transitions and life experiences that require specific consideration. Together, the articles in this Collection—Autism in Adolescence and Adulthood—highlight the need to understand autistic experiences and neurobiology in adolescence and adulthood through a context-sensitive lens that considers developmentally relevant experiences. Across diverse methodologies, including behavioural, physiological, qualitative, and computational (machine learning), a particular focus is on understanding and supporting emotion regulation, wellbeing and mental health. Collectively, the papers highlight that adolescence and adulthood are periods of autistic development that warrant focussed scientific attention. They also underscore that autistic flourishing in adolescence and adulthood depends on a society that embraces neurodiversity and is committed to meaningful change.

Across adolescence and adulthood, the brain remains highly dynamic, undergoing structural and functional changes that support learning, adaptation, and social development. Neurogenesis continues beyond childhood, with new neurons added in regions such as the hippocampus1, and neural circuits reorganising in response to shifting life stages and demands2. Particularly, changes in hemispheric organisation have been linked to major life transitions in adulthood, demonstrating that neurobiological development is not confined to early life2. These lifelong processes support individuals to adjust to new cognitive, social, and emotional challenges. Adolescence, beginning with puberty and extending until adult roles are assumed3–5, is a particularly dynamic period of neurobiological and social-cognitive change. During this period, young people develop capacities needed to navigate increasingly complex social environments, including peer influence and social evaluation6. Perhaps unsurprisingly, this developmental window is also associated with heightened vulnerability to mental health difficulties3.

Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition characterised by differences in social communication and the presence of restricted and repetitive behaviours7. It is considered to reflect a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors8, with a range of differences in neural functioning identified9. As autistic young people move through adolescence and adulthood they may encounter distinct challenges and opportunities that differ from those experienced in early life. Indeed, the myriad social and relational, educational and vocational, and societal and cultural demands of adolescence and adulthood, combined with evidence of ongoing brain plasticity, underscore the importance of studying autism beyond childhood. Despite this, autism research has continued to echo early conceptualisations of autism10 by focussing on child populations11. The autistic community has made clear that a key priority for autism research should be to understand and support autistic needs and differences across the lifespan12,13. Our Collection, Autism in Adolescence and Adulthood, presents new research from investigators tackling important questions that relate to autistic adolescents and adults’ lived experiences. These studies reflect ongoing global research efforts to build the knowledge base that can support all members of the autistic community to flourish.

The contributions to the Collection span diverse methodologies and questions and it is not possible to highlight them all in this brief Editorial. However, we take this opportunity to focus on three points of convergence across the articles. First, the Collection demonstrates that autistic adolescents and adults may differ from non-autistic peers in underlying bodily and neurophysiological processes, which may then shape behaviour. For example, Cook et al.14 found gradient differences in the tracing of simple shapes for autistic adults compared to non-autistic adults. These differences were underpinned by differences in speed modulation, which may reflect differences in the bodily filtering of outgoing movement signals. These distinct patterns of speed modulation may be relevant to a broad spectrum of everyday movements including social gestures. Social gestures have relevance to the contribution of Bierlich et al.15, who measured interpersonal synchrony. Interpersonal synchrony refers to the temporal co-ordination of verbal and non-verbal behaviours between social partners, which associate with a range of positive social outcomes16. In their study, which included both autistic and non-autistic adults, differences in interpersonal synchrony were not associated with differences in temporal perception. Bierlich et al. suggest that other processes, such as social attention, multisensory integration or higher order social-cognitive processes, may explain why interpersonal synchrony was attenuated in dyads that included an autistic person15. At a more basic physiological level, Ilen et al.17 measured heart rate variability in autistic adolescents and young adults, alongside self-reported mental health difficulties and momentary assessments of perceived stress and affect. They concluded that increased reactivity to stress may be a mechanism that links altered autonomic functioning, as indicated by heart rate variability, to mental health difficulties. Taken together, this work suggests distinct patterns of sensorimotor behavioural and physiological regulation in autistic adolescents and adults, which may interact with social and emotional functioning.

Second, building on Ilen et al.’s observations about the impact of daily fluctuations in stress on wellbeing, papers in the Collection also highlight that the experience of emotion regulation difficulties in autistic young people are highly contextual. As part of the Regulating Emotions—Strengthening Adolescent Resilience (RE-STAR) research programme in the UK, researchers18,19 interviewed adolescents with ADHD, autism, or both, asking how they experience distress in a variety of settings. Of the two studies, Pavlopoulou et al.18 reported findings based on reflexive thematic analysis while Lukito et al.19 applied a machine-learning topic modelling approach to these data. The adolescents described the importance of neurodivergent-affirming environments, trusted relationships and predictable environments in supporting emotional wellbeing18. Applying machine learning to the same dataset not only enabled an important comparison of human-led and machine-driven analyses of interview data, but highlighted that school was more difficult environment for regulating emotion compared to community settings19. The Collection therefore suggests that emotion regulation in autistic adolescents is not only biologically based but shaped by external contextual factors.

A key contextual factor that is relevant to mental health in autistic adolescents and adults is the implicit or explicit pressures that autistic people may feel to ‘fit in’ in a dominantly neurotypical world. This was also a third notable area of exploration within the Collection. Surveying a large sample of autistic adults from Turkey, Seçer et al.20 reported that social camouflaging, i.e. masking or compensating for natural autistic behaviours, often to avoid negative judgement21, mediated the link between elevated autistic traits and reduced mental wellbeing. Further, social exclusion and perceived public stigma both intensified this pattern of experience. Ai et al.22 explored the constructs of impression management, i.e. a person’s effort to positively influence how they are perceived23, and camouflaging in a representative sample of 972 US adults. The findings indicated that the concept of camouflaging can be considered within the broader framework of impression management. Further, autistic traits and ADHD traits both had distinct patterns of influence on the relation between impression management and mental health. Returning to Pavlopoulou et al.18, their work also suggests the relevance of social pressures as acceptance and stigma-free interactions were perceived by their participants as important to managing emotionally difficult situations. In summary, the Collection suggests that mental health difficulties in autistic people are embedded in social pressures, stigma, and expectations to conform.

Together, the articles in this Collection contribute to the positioning of autism in adolescence and adulthood as an important area of scientific enquiry, which has a distinct place in lifespan-oriented science alongside childhood autism research. The studies we have highlighted show that the physiological rhythms, sensorimotor patterns, and stress-response systems of autistic young people and adults differ in meaningful ways from non-autistic peers, shaping how social connection and emotional life unfold. However, they also make clear that these internal processes are only part of the story. Across studies, autistic participants described how their wellbeing was shaped by the environments around them. Environments that offer predictability, acceptance, and genuine understanding can have tangible positive impact on the experiences of autistic adolescents and adults. This Collection therefore highlights that autistic flourishing in adolescence and adulthood depends on a society that embraces neurodiversity and is committed to meaningful change.
