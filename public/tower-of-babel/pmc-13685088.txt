Anxiety in pregnancy complicated by gestational and pre-gestational diabetes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Piyanun Limruangrong, Uraiwan Ukhawounam, Prapaporn Pornsuriyasak, Alicia K. Matthews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and pregestational diabetes mellitus (pre-GDM) affect approximately 20% of pregnancies worldwide, requiring intensive management, alongside pregnancy-related concerns and broader psychosocial factors, all of which may contribute to increased psychological distress. While anxiety is associated with adverse maternal and child outcomes, evidence in women with diabetes during pregnancy remains fragmented. This systematic review and meta-analysis examined anxiety in pregnant and postpartum women with GDM and pre-GDM.MethodsThis study searched MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, EBSCO, Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, and WHO ICTRP from inception to April 2025. Observational studies reporting anxiety outcomes in pregnant or postpartum women with GDM or pre-GDM were included. Two reviewers independently screened articles, extracted data, and assessed quality using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed, with subgroup analyses by region and measurement instrument. Meta-regression explored heterogeneity sources. The study followed PRISMA 2020 and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251063824).ResultsThirty-four studies (n = 994,189; 38,584 GDM; 156 pre-GDM; 955,449 healthy controls) were included. Women with GDM had higher odds of antenatal anxiety than healthy controls (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.20–2.37) and higher mean anxiety scores (SMD = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.07–0.34). Among eight single-arm studies reporting the prevalence of screen-positive antenatal anxiety in women with GDM, all conducted in Asia, the pooled prevalence was 36% (95% CI: 22–50%); however, this estimate should be interpreted very cautiously because of extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 98.7%), with individual study estimates ranging from 12 to 61%. Subgroup analyses indicated stronger antenatal anxiety associations in Asian studies (SMD = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.27–0.55). Postpartum anxiety did not differ significantly between women with GDM and healthy controls (RR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.93–1.05). Evidence regarding pre-GDM was limited and should be considered preliminary.ConclusionsA high burden of antenatal anxiety was observed among women with GDM, particularly in Asian populations, although precise prevalence estimates remain uncertain due to extreme heterogeneity. Postpartum anxiety appeared comparable to that of healthy women. Evidence regarding pre-GDM was based on only two studies and remains insufficient to draw conclusions. Findings suggest that targeted assessment and increased clinical vigilance may be appropriate in higher-risk subgroups; however, further prospective studies are needed before routine screening can be recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09705-x.

Background

Hyperglycaemia in pregnancy is a primary global health concern, accounting for approximately 19.7% of live births worldwide and representing one of the most common medical complications affecting pregnant women. Regional differences exist, with the highest age-standardised prevalence observed in Southeast Asia (31.7%) and the lowest in Africa (13.8%) [1]. This condition is predominantly driven by Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM)—defined as glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy—which contributes to about 79.2% of cases. In comparison, Pre-Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (pre-GDM) refers to type 1 or type 2 diabetes diagnosed prior to conception—affects an additional 11% of pregnancies [1]. Both GDM and pre-GDM require intensive medical management, frequent monitoring, and significant lifestyle adjustments, all of which may elevate psychological distress during the antenatal and postpartum periods [2, 3].

Antenatal anxiety affects approximately 15–20% of pregnant women in the general population, providing an important baseline for understanding psychological vulnerability during pregnancy [4, 5]. In non-pregnant populations, individuals with diabetes consistently exhibit higher rates of anxiety than those without the condition [6]. During pregnancy, this relationship becomes even more complex due to physiological changes, concerns regarding fetal well-being, and the additional demands of diabetes management [7, 8]. Maternal anxiety has been associated with adverse outcomes such as preterm birth, low birthweight, and impaired neurodevelopment in offspring [9, 10]. For pregnant women with diabetes specifically, unique stressors—including fears about pregnancy complications, frequent glucose monitoring, dietary restrictions, potential insulin use, and heightened medical surveillance—may further amplify anxiety symptoms [11, 12].

Previous studies have suggested that women with GDM experience higher levels of anxiety during pregnancy compared with women without diabetes [8, 13]. However, evidence comparing anxiety in women with pre-GDM—relative to both GDM and healthy controls—remains limited. Although several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have explored psychological outcomes among women with GDM and pre-GDM, most have examined each diabetes type and perinatal period separately, leading to a fragmented understanding of comparative psychological outcomes across these groups [14–16]. Strengthening justification for examining pre-GDM as a separate category is essential, as pre-GDM represents a clinically distinct population characterised by pre-existing hyperglycaemia, longer disease duration, higher baseline medical risk, and a greater likelihood of obstetric and metabolic complications—all factors which may influence psychological outcomes. Although GDM and pre-gestational diabetes represent clinically distinct conditions, examining them within a single review allows for a direct comparison of psychological outcomes across these groups within a shared pregnancy context. This approach helps to identify whether differences in disease onset, duration, and baseline risk profiles translate into differential anxiety patterns, thereby addressing gaps in the existing literature, where these conditions have largely been studied in isolation.

The present study sought to address these gaps by conducting a joint meta-analysis of psychological outcomes across the antenatal and postpartum periods, with a specific focus on anxiety. Although the overall project examined three psychological outcomes—anxiety, depression, and stress—the current paper focuses exclusively on pooled evidence regarding anxiety. This review aimed to: (1) evaluate the prevalence and severity of anxiety in pregnant and postpartum women with current GDM diagnoses compared with healthy controls; and (2) examine anxiety outcomes in women with pre-GDM compared with both healthy controls and women with current GDM.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17], with the completed checklist provided in Table S1. They were prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251063824).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed and implemented across multiple electronic databases from inception to October 2024, with an updated search conducted in April 2025 before data extraction. The databases searched included MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, Embase, EBSCO, and the Cochrane Library. To enhance coverage, grey literature was explored through ClinicalTrials.gov and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP) to identify relevant ongoing or completed studies. Search terms encompassed pregnancy-related terms ("pregnant women," "pregnancy"), diabetes-related terms ("gestational diabetes mellitus," "pregestational diabetes," "type 1 diabetes," "type 2 diabetes"), and psychological terms ("anxiety," "depression," "stress"). Both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords were employed to maximise sensitivity. No language or date restrictions were applied (Table S2). Additionally, reference lists from included studies and relevant systematic reviews were manually screened to identify further eligible studies. Eligibility criteria

The criteria for selecting studies were explicitly defined using the PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome) framework. The population included pregnant or postpartum women diagnosed with GDM according to recognized clinical criteria (such as IADPSG, ADA, WHO, ADIPS, NDDG, national guidelines, and ICD-based clinical diagnoses) as well as women with pre-GDM (type 1 or type 2 diabetes diagnosed before pregnancy). Comparison groups consisted of healthy pregnant or postpartum women without diabetes or, when available, direct comparisons between GDM and pre-GDM groups. Single-arm studies were also included when they reported anxiety outcomes among women with diabetes during pregnancy, as these data contributed to estimating pooled prevalence and.

mean anxiety levels, but were not used for comparative analyses. For the purposes of this review, the antenatal period was defined as any assessment conducted during pregnancy, and the postpartum period was defined as any assessment conducted from delivery up to 24 months postpartum, consistent with the timepoints reported in included studies. The primary outcome was anxiety, measured using validated self-report instruments (e.g., STAI, DASS-Anxiety, HADS-A) or clinical diagnostic criteria (e.g., diagnostic codes). These measures were regarded as related, but distinct constructs—representing anxiety symptoms versus diagnosed anxiety disorders. Outcomes were assessed in the antenatal or postpartum period and reported as binary (presence/absence) or continuous scores.

Additional inclusion criteria required an observational design (cohort, case–control, or cross-sectional), availability of data for effect size calculation, and publication in any language. Studies were excluded if they were experimental, reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, qualitative studies, conference abstracts, theses, unpublished protocols, failed to distinguish between GDM and pre-GDM, focused on non-pregnancy diabetes, or were data-limited/duplicate publications.

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers (L.P. and U.U.) screened all records at the title and abstract levels, using the predefined eligibility criteria. Full-text articles of studies deemed potentially relevant were then retrieved and assessed independently by the same reviewers. To address potential overlapping samples or duplicate cohorts (e.g., multi-centre GDM cohorts or repeated analyses of the DIP studies), the researchers cross-checked authors, study settings, recruitment years, and cohort descriptions across all included records. When overlap was identified, the study providing the most complete or recent dataset was retained, and duplicates were excluded. Disagreements were resolved through consensus-based discussion, with recourse to a third reviewer for final adjudication when necessary.

Data extraction was performed independently and in duplicate by the two reviewers, employing a standardized, pre-designed extraction form. Key information systematically extracted included: study characteristics (first author, publication year, country, and design), participant characteristics (sample size, gestational age, and diabetes type), details concerning anxiety measurement (tool used and timing of assessment), and quantitative outcome data (means, standard deviations, proportions, effect sizes, and confidence intervals).

Outcome measures

Anxiety was conceptualized as encompassing general anxiety symptoms, pregnancy-specific anxiety, and clinically diagnosed anxiety, which were analysed separately to avoid conflating related but distinct constructs. Within each construct, subgroup analyses were performed based on measurement tool where applicable. Anxiety outcomes during the antenatal and postpartum periods were assessed using validated instruments. Studies reported either binary outcomes (presence/absence of clinically significant anxiety based on study-specific cutoffs) or continuous symptom scores. Since cutoff thresholds varied across instruments, values were extracted as originally reported without harmonisation. Multiple anxiety constructs were represented across tools. State/trait anxiety was measured using the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-S/T) [18]. General anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-A) [19], the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale—Anxiety subscale (HADS-A) [20], and the Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) [21]. Physiological or somatic anxiety was captured using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) [22]. Pregnancy-related anxiety was assessed using either the Pregnancy-related Anxiety Scale (PrAS by Brunton [23]; PrAS by Xiao [24]) or the Pregnancy Anxiety Scale (PAS) [25], depending on the study. Less commonly used tools included the Taylor Anxiety Scale (TAS) [26], which assesses test-related somatic tension, and the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) [27], which captures general self-rated anxiety symptoms. Clinically diagnosed anxiety disorders were identified using diagnostic codes from medical records [28, 29]. Full measurement details, scale ranges, and cutoffs for each included study are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists appropriate for the study design (e.g., cohort, case–control, cross-sectional) [30]. The percentage of criteria categorized studies met, with categories including high quality (≥ 75%), moderate quality (50–74%), or low quality (< 50%) [31]. Disagreements were resolved by discussion. The JBI tools were used because they are internationally recognized, validated instruments for critical appraisal.

Most high-quality studies generally demonstrated clear group definitions. They used validated anxiety instruments, whereas moderate-quality studies more often showed limitations in exposure or outcome reporting and incomplete adjustment for potential confounders. The single low-quality study included several unclear domains according to the JBI criteria. These methodological patterns were taken into account when interpreting the pooled results.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using Stata 18.0 [32]. Random-effects DerSimonian-Laird models were applied to address expected heterogeneity due to clinical differences across studies. For comparative studies, binary outcomes were synthesised using unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) or risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), extracted directly from study-reported crude data. Although adjusted estimates were reported in some studies, the covariates included in the adjustment models varied widely (e.g., age, body mass index, parity, and socioeconomic status), limiting comparability across studies. To ensure methodological consistency and maximise study inclusion, unadjusted estimates were prioritised for the primary meta-analysis, and adjusted values were documented descriptively, but not pooled quantitatively. It is acknowledged that unadjusted estimates do not account for potential confounders such as age, BMI, parity, and socioeconomic status, and therefore reflect crude associations rather than independent effects. Continuous outcomes from comparative studies were analysed using mean differences (MD) or standardised mean differences (SMD), each with 95% confidence intervals.

For single-arm studies, both binary and continuous outcomes were synthesised. For binary outcomes (e.g., prevalence of anxiety), pooled proportions were calculated using the number of events and the total sample size under a random-effects model with DerSimonian–Laird estimation. No transformation was applied to the proportion data, and 95% confidence intervals were estimated within the random-effects model. For continuous outcomes (e.g., mean anxiety scores), pooled mean values were estimated using a random-effects model with DerSimonian–Laird estimation. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane Q test and the I2 statistic, with values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [33]. Sources of heterogeneity were further explored using meta-regression. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted by geographic region and anxiety measurement tool. Sensitivity analyses were performed using leave-one-out procedures to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test when at least 10 studies were available. In cases of funnel plot asymmetry, contour-enhanced funnel plots were used to explore potential causes. Two-sided p-values of 0.05 were considered as the threshold for statistical significance, except for the heterogeneity assessment, where the Cochrane Q test’s p-values of 0.1 were used.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Database searches initially identified 5,261 records. An additional 239 articles were found through supplementary searches (218 via ClinicalTrials.gov and 21 via WHO ICTRP). After removing 1,727 duplicates, 3,295 titles and abstracts were screened, resulting in 230 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. In total, 80 studies were included in the quantitative synthesis across all outcomes. Of these, 34 studies reporting anxiety outcomes were included in the anxiety meta-analysis (Fig. 1). The main reasons for full-text exclusion were lack of relevant outcome data and population mismatch.

The 34 included studies were published between 1994 and 2025, with the majority (n = 29, 85%) published in the last decade. Studies were geographically distributed across four continents: Asia (n = 20, 58.8%), Europe (n = 8, 23.5%), North America (n = 4, 11.8%), and Australia and Oceania (n = 2, 5.9%). The total sample size was 994,189 participants, which comprised 38,584 women with GDM, 156 women with pre-GDM, and 955,449 healthy controls.

Study designs included cross-sectional (n = 22, 64.7%) and cohort (n = 12, 35.3%) studies. The most commonly used anxiety measurement instruments were the DASS (n = 8, 23.5%) and STAI-S (n = 8, 23.5%). These instruments capture different anxiety constructs: the DASS-Anxiety subscale reflects physiological hyperarousal, whereas STAI-S measures situational fear and worry. Therefore, pooled estimates should be interpreted with consideration of these conceptual differences.

Across most analyses, anxiety outcomes reflected self-reported anxiety symptom burden or screening-defined clinically significant anxiety symptoms, rather than formally diagnosed anxiety disorders. Although a small number of studies identified anxiety using diagnostic codes, these were analysed separately and were not pooled with symptom-based measures. Quality assessment results

Quality assessment using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist revealed that 23 studies (67.7%) were rated high quality, 10 studies (29.4%) were moderate quality, and 1 study (2.9%) was low quality. High-quality studies consistently met most JBI criteria, typically demonstrating clear participant selection, valid and reliable measurement of anxiety, and adequate statistical analysis. Moderate-quality studies often lacked adjustment for key confounders, did not clearly report recruitment methods, or provided limited detail on exposure and outcome measurement. The single low-quality study showed unclear sampling procedures and insufficient information for several methodological domains. Overall, strengths were most consistent in outcome measurement and participant selection, while limitations were most prominent in confounding control and methodological transparency (Fig. S1).

Antenatal anxiety outcomes

Anxiety outcomes were analysed separately by construct type: symptom-based measures using validated self-report instruments, and clinically diagnosed anxiety identified via administrative diagnostic codes. For the antenatal period, no comparative studies using diagnostic codes were identified; all analyses therefore reflect symptom-based measures.

GDM vs. Healthy controls

Binary outcomes were defined as presence versus absence of clinically significant anxiety symptoms, based on study-specific cutoff scores. Six studies (413 GDM and 431 healthy controls) examined GDM and anxiety during the antenatal period. For the comparison between pre-GDM and GDM groups, two studies (133 pre-GDM and 327 GDM) evaluated the risk of antenatal anxiety symptoms as a binary outcome. Publication bias

Some funnel plots showed asymmetry when assessing publication bias for the analyses of anxiety outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S2-S9). Egger’s tests were conducted for outcomes with sufficient study numbers (≥ 10 studies) and indicated no statistically significant small-study effects. Where visual asymmetry was observed—particularly in analyses with substantial heterogeneity—additional contour-enhanced funnel plots were examined. These plots demonstrated that any potentially missing studies were located within both significant and non-significant regions, suggesting that the observed asymmetry was more likely attributable to between-study heterogeneity, differences in psychological measurement tools, or geographical variation across study settings, rather than genuine publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S2-S9). Overall, despite asymmetry in some analyses, the combined evidence from funnel plot inspection, Egger’s tests, and contour-enhanced funnel plots indicated that publication bias was unlikely to have materially influenced the pooled estimates for the psychological outcomes included in this review. For outcomes with fewer than 10 contributing studies, including analyses of postpartum anxiety, Egger's test was not performed. However, contour-enhanced funnel plots suggested that missing studies were distributed across both significant and non-significant regions, and these findings should still be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis examined anxiety outcomes across the antenatal and postpartum periods in women with GDM and pre-GDM compared with healthy controls. During the antenatal period, women with GDM consistently reported higher anxiety symptoms than healthy controls, with pooled estimates indicating a modest, but statistically significant association (OR = 1.69, I2 = 14.48%; SMD = 0.21, I2 = 61.62%). In contrast, postpartum anxiety did not differ significantly between women with and without GDM. Evidence for pre-GDM was limited, and derived from only two studies, and largely non-significant. Overall, these findings indicated a modest association rather than a causal effect.

The observed association between GDM and antenatal anxiety likely reflects multiple stressors that accompany a new diagnosis during pregnancy. These include the need for rapid adjustments to glucose monitoring, dietary changes, and potential insulin initiation, as well as concerns about maternal–fetal complications and the psychological burden of lifestyle modification [2, 3]. Heightened illness-related uncertainty and increased cognitive and emotional demands of diabetes self-management may further contribute to anxiety symptoms. These interpretations align with broader evidence linking diabetes to psychological vulnerability and the challenges of managing medical complications during pregnancy [8]. However, given the observational nature of the included studies, these mechanisms should be considered plausible explanatory pathways rather than confirmed causal processes.

Geographical variation did not meaningfully contribute to heterogeneity in the pooled odds ratio analysis, where heterogeneity was low (I2 = 14%). The association between GDM and antenatal anxiety was relatively consistent across settings in dichotomous analyses, with minimal between-study variability. In contrast, geographical variation contributed substantially to heterogeneity in the continuous outcome analysis (overall SMD = 0.21, I2 = 61.6%). Standardized mean differences showed a clearer regional pattern, with significantly stronger effects in Asian studies (SMD = 0.41, I2 = 33.9%). Studies from Europe and North America demonstrated small, non-significant effects with low to moderate heterogeneity. However, substantial heterogeneity remained in regions with limited study numbers, such as Australia and Oceania, where between-study variability was high. These regional differences are unlikely to reflect inherent biological variation, but instead may arise from contextual and methodological influences. Cultural attitudes toward illness, pregnancy, and mental health vary substantially across regions and may influence the experience and expression of anxiety [8, 14]. Differences in healthcare system structures—including access to diabetes care, psychological support services, and insurance coverage—may also shape women’s stress experiences during GDM management [68, 69]. Differential stigma surrounding diabetes and mental health may further affect willingness to disclose symptoms or participate in research [70–72]. Socioeconomic status, education, and health literacy were inconsistently measured across studies (Table S3), limiting adjustment for these confounders and likely contributing to residual heterogeneity. Both continent and measurement tool were included as prespecified subgroup analyses, defined a priori to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.

Measurement instruments contributed meaningfully to heterogeneity. This was examined through prespecified subgroup analyses stratified by measurement tool. Studies using the STAI-S showed larger pooled odds ratios than those using the DASS-A (OR = 2.35, I2 = 0% in STAI-S; OR = 1.41, I2 = 0% in DASS-A). For continuous outcomes, DASS-A studies demonstrated a statistically significant mean difference (MD = 1.51) with moderate heterogeneity. In contrast, the pooled mean difference for the STAI-S was borderline and highly heterogeneous, becoming statistically significant only after leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (MD = 3.42). Taken together, these patterns likely reflect differences in the constructs measured. The STAI-S assesses acute, situational (“state”) anxiety in response to immediate stressors [18] and has been validated for use in perinatal populations [73, 74]. In contrast, the DASS captures broader and more persistent physiological or panic-related symptoms [19]. Such conceptual distinctions—together with differing heterogeneity profiles—likely explain the inconsistencies observed across studies rather than representing true differences in underlying anxiety severity. Therefore, the STAI-S may be particularly sensitive to acute anxiety immediately following a new GDM diagnosis. The use of heterogeneous anxiety measurement tools with varying cutoff thresholds across studies further complicates cross-study comparisons. For example, the STAI-S cutoff of ≥ 40 and the DASS-A cutoff of ≥ 8 reflect different constructs and different levels of clinical severity [15]. These inconsistencies preclude direct comparison of prevalence estimates across studies and likely contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Standardisation of anxiety measurement instruments in future GDM research—particularly using pregnancy-validated tools—would substantially improve the comparability of findings [75].

Given that only two studies contributed data for pre-GDM, the evidence base was extremely limited. Although a potential trend was observed, findings were inconsistent, non-significant, and highly heterogeneous. Therefore, no firm conclusions could be drawn regarding anxiety in women with pre-GDM, and further research is required.

Single-arm analysis of severity scales indicated that STAI-S and STAI-T scores approached clinical thresholds (≥ 40) [45, 76], suggesting anxiety levels near possible clinical significance. Pregnancy-specific measures such as PrAS showed expected normative patterns [65, 77], underscoring the distinctiveness of pregnancy-related anxiety as a construct [23]. These continuous measures complement prevalence estimates and highlight the importance of using pregnancy-appropriate instruments in clinical and research contexts [75]. Prevalence estimates ranged widely (12–61%), with a pooled estimate of 36% (I2 = 98.7%), indicating extreme between-study heterogeneity that substantially limits the reliability of this point estimate. As noted above, variation in measurement instruments, assessment timing, and broader contextual factors—including healthcare system differences and sociocultural influences across countries (China, Malaysia, Iran, India, and Turkey)—likely contributed to this variability [15, 68, 69, 71]. Notably, substantial heterogeneity persisted even though all eight prevalence studies were conducted in Asia, underscoring that Asian populations are not homogeneous, with substantial variation in healthcare systems and sociocultural contexts across countries. In the included evidence, prevalence estimates suitable for the single-arm meta-analysis were available only from Asian studies. We did not identify sufficient comparable prevalence data from Europe, North America, or other regions to support a reliable regional prevalence meta-analysis. This highlights an important evidence gap and limits the generalisability of the pooled prevalence estimate beyond Asian populations. Meta-regression did not identify any single explanatory covariate. Given these considerations, the pooled prevalence of 36% should be interpreted as a broad approximation rather than a single point-estimate, and caution is warranted when applying these findings beyond Asian populations.

Postpartum findings contrasted with antenatal patterns. Pooled estimates for both were developed, and symptom-based postpartum anxiety was close to null. This may reflect the reduction of diagnosis-related stressors after delivery, while postpartum challenges—such as infant care, sleep deprivation, and recovery—are shared across women regardless of diabetic status [78, 79]. Nevertheless, GDM is associated with substantial long-term health risks, including future type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease, which may confer ongoing psychological vulnerability beyond the immediate postpartum period [80, 81]. Although elevated postpartum anxiety was not observed at the population level, continued psychological monitoring may remain clinically relevant, particularly for women with persistent metabolic risk or prior antenatal distress.

From a clinical perspective, current guidelines emphasise glycaemic management with comparatively limited attention to mental health [82]. Given the substantial heterogeneity and limited evidence, universal routine screening cannot be recommended. Findings support heightened clinical vigilance and consideration of targeted assessment in higher-risk subgroups —particularly women with more severe GDM requiring insulin therapy, those with a prior history of anxiety or mental health disorders, those experiencing greater psychosocial or socioeconomic burden, and those reporting elevated distress at or shortly after GDM diagnosis—while stronger prospective evidence is needed before making population-level screening recommendations. Sensitive instruments such as the STAI-S may help identify acute distress. Brief validated screening tools—particularly the STAI-S and DASS, which were commonly used in the included studies—may help facilitate early detection of distress where resources allow [15]. Although pregnancy-specific anxiety scales exist, only a small number of studies have applied these instruments in GDM populations, and their comparative performance remains uncertain. Maternal anxiety has been associated with preterm birth and impaired diabetes self-management [9, 83], underscoring the importance of integrating psychological considerations within routine GDM care.

Evidence-based interventions such as cognitive–behavioural therapy [84], mindfulness [85], and psychoeducation [86] have shown effectiveness in reducing perinatal anxiety and may be adaptable for women with diabetes. Integrated care models embedding psychological support into diabetes and maternity care could improve accessibility [87]. Training providers to recognise anxiety symptoms and communicate empathetically is essential, as provider interaction strongly influences women’s psychological experiences [88].

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review has several strengths, including comprehensive searches across multiple databases, prospective registration, duplicate screening, validated quality appraisal, and inclusion of a large, geographically diverse sample. The analysis synthesised multiple outcome types, comparison groups, and time periods, providing a broad overview of anxiety outcomes across the perinatal continuum in women with diabetes.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Substantial heterogeneity persisted across multiple analyses despite extensive subgroup and meta-regression exploration, indicating that important sources of variability—such as differences in anxiety constructs, measurement tools, timing of assessment, and contextual factors—could not be fully accounted for. In particular, the pooled prevalence estimate of antenatal anxiety in GDM (36%) was associated with extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 98.7%), which could not be fully explained by the prespecified subgroup or meta-regression covariates, substantially limiting the precision and generalisability of this estimate. Variation in GDM diagnostic criteria across studies could not be formally explored due to incomplete reporting and may represent an additional source of residual heterogeneity. Although the study design was evaluated in meta-regression analyses, it was not identified as a significant contributor to heterogeneity.

All primary pooled estimates were derived from unadjusted effect sizes, as the covariates included in adjusted models varied widely across studies and precluded quantitative pooling of adjusted estimates. This represents an important limitation that restricts causal interpretation. Residual confounding by factors such as age, BMI, parity, socioeconomic status, and prior mental health history cannot be excluded (Table S3). Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal, and considered as risk markers rather than causal risk factors. The predominance of cross-sectional study designs (64.7% of included studies) fundamentally limits causal inference. Cross-sectional studies cannot establish whether GDM precedes anxiety, whether anxiety predisposes women to GDM, or whether both share common antecedents. The directionality of the observed association therefore remains uncertain, and the pooled estimates should be interpreted as reflecting co-occurrence rather than causal effect.

Furthermore, substantial variation and inconsistent reporting of postpartum assessment timing (ranging from 6 weeks to 24 months, with some studies reporting only a general postpartum period) may have contributed to heterogeneity and limited comparability across studies, and precluded a meaningful subgroup analysis by postpartum timing.

Most anxiety outcomes were based on self-report questionnaires rather than diagnostic interviews. In addition, the geographic concentration of included studies in Asia (58.8%) may limit the generalisability of the findings to other regions. Prevalence estimates and subgroup analyses based on anxiety severity are derived almost exclusively from Asian populations, and caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to European, North American, or other global contexts where healthcare systems, cultural attitudes toward mental health disclosure, and GDM management practices differ considerably. These limitations are inherent to meta-analyses of heterogeneous observational studies and necessitate cautious interpretation of pooled estimates.

The ability to assess publication bias was constrained for several outcomes due to the small number of contributing studies. For outcomes such as postpartum anxiety, where only two to five studies contributed data, Egger's test was not performed due to insufficient statistical power. Funnel plot asymmetry in these analyses should therefore be interpreted with considerable caution, and the possibility of publication bias cannot be excluded.

Although the two studies by Lydon [53, 54] originate from the same research group, differences in study populations, inclusion criteria, and psychological measures reported across the two publications suggest that they represent distinct data collection exercises, and participant overlap is considered unlikely.

Implications for research

Well-designed longitudinal studies with repeated assessments are needed to clarify temporal patterns of anxiety across pregnancy and postpartum. Research on pre-GDM remains insufficient; adequately powered comparisons between pre-GDM, GDM, and healthy populations using standardised measures are urgently needed. Future studies should incorporate diagnostic interviews alongside self-report tools, investigate biological, psychological, and social mechanisms, identify risk and protective factors, test tailored interventions, and explore cross-cultural experiences to inform culturally responsive care. Consistent reporting and application of standardized diagnostic criteria for GDM and pre-gestational diabetes are also needed to improve comparability across studies and enable more robust investigation of heterogeneity.

Conclusions

Pregnant women with GDM experience elevated antenatal anxiety compared with healthy controls, although the magnitude of this association remains uncertain due to substantial heterogeneity. Stronger associations were observed in Asian studies and in those using state anxiety measures. Evidence regarding pre-GDM, based on only two studies, is limited and inconclusive. Postpartum anxiety did not differ between groups. Given the substantial heterogeneity and limited evidence, universal routine screening cannot be recommended at this time. Screening recommendations in clinical practice typically require consideration of cost-effectiveness, availability of follow-up mental health services, acceptable test performance, and evidence that early identification improves outcomes—criteria that are not fully supported by the current evidence base. Nonetheless, the findings support heightened clinical vigilance and consideration of targeted assessment in higher-risk subgroups. Women who may be considered at higher risk include those requiring insulin therapy (reflecting more severe or poorly controlled GDM), those with a prior history of anxiety or depression, and those reporting elevated distress at or shortly after GDM diagnosis. Further research should employ adequately powered prospective designs and clearly differentiate between pre-GDM and GDM populations.
