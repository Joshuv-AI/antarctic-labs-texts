Electrostatic computations for statistical mechanics and random matrix applications
Sung-Soo Byun, Peter J. Forrester

Abstract
Although for the most part classical, the topic of electrostatics finds to this day new applications. In this review we highlight several theoretical results on electrostatics, chosen to both illustrate general principles, and for their application in statistical mechanics and random matrix settings. The theoretical results include electrostatic potentials and energies associated with balls and hyperellipsoids in general dimension, the use of conformal mappings in two-dimensions, and the balayage measure. A number of explicit examples of their use in predicting the leading asymptotic form of certain configuration integrals and particle density in particular statistical mechanical systems are given, as well as with regards to questions relating to fluctuation formulas and (conditioned) gap probabilities.

Scope of the review

1.1
Coulomb potentials and the one-component plasma model

Consider two unit charges in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at positions
\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime}
, and denote by
\Phi_{d}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})
the corresponding potential. We will take the latter as being specified by the solution of the particular
d
-dimensional, rescaled Poisson equation

\nabla_{\vec{r}}^{2}\Phi_{d}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})=-c_{d}\chi_{d}\delta(\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}),

(1)

where
c_{d}:=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)
is the surface area of the unit sphere
|\vec{r}|^{2}=1
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\chi_{d}=d-2
for
d>2
and equals unity otherwise.
Satisfying (
1
) are the so-called free boundary conditions solutions

\Phi_{d}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})=\begin{cases}-|x-x^{\prime}|,&d=1,\\
-\log|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}|,&d=2,\\
|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}|^{2-d},&d>2\end{cases}

(2)

(in the
d=1
case we have rewritten

\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime}
as
x,x^{\prime}
since the positions are on the real line).

Let
C(\vec{r}\,^{\prime})
denote a charge density with support
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(it is convenient to consider
\Omega
as being open and its closure

\bar{\Omega}
as being compact with a smooth boundary). The electrostatic potential at

\vec{r}
,
V(\vec{r})
say, of this charge density is given in terms of
\Phi_{d}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})
by

V(\vec{r})=\int_{\Omega}\Phi_{d}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})C(\vec{r}\,^{\prime})\,d\vec{r}\,^{\prime}.

(3)

Applying
\nabla_{\vec{r}}^{2}
to both sides of (
3
), commuting this with the integral on the right hand side (RHS) shows

\nabla_{\vec{r}}^{2}V(\vec{r})
is equal to the RHS of (
1
) with
\delta(\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime})

replaced by
C(\vec{r})
in the case that
\vec{r}\in\Omega
, and equal to zero for
\vec{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\bar{\Omega}
. This is another example of a Poisson equation. Suppose we now specialise to the choice that for

\vec{r}\in\Omega
,
C(\vec{r})=-\rho_{\rm b}
a constant. Then up to distinct additive harmonic functions
in the two cases, the solution of this new Poisson equation is

V(\vec{r})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\rho_{\rm b}\Big({c_{d}\chi_{d}\over 2d}\Big)|\vec{r}|^{2},&\vec{r}\in\Omega,\\[5.69046pt]
0,&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}

(4)

(the additive harmonic functions must be such that
V(\vec{r})
is continuous on
\partial\Omega
, the boundary of
\Omega
).

The potential
V(\vec{r})
is a key quantity in the specification of the one-component plasma model of the equilibrium statistical mechanics of a classical Coulomb system
[
29
]
. For a given domain
\Omega
in dimension
d
, this model consists of a smeared out, uniform, negatively charged background (charge density
-\rho_{\rm b}
say) such that

\rho_{\rm b}|\Omega|=N

(5)

(here
|\Omega|
denotes the volume of
\Omega
). Interacting with the background, and among themselves, are
N
mobile particles of unit positive charge.
One sees immediately that the potential energy experienced by a unit positive charge at point
\vec{r}
is given by the first case of (
4
), with the understanding that in the latter the additive harmonic function is still to be determined (and will depend on
\Omega
). Thus with the potential energy
U_{\text{p-b}}
due to the interaction of the
N
unit positive charges with the background we have

U_{\text{p-b}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}V(\vec{r}_{j}).

(6)

This potential is also relevant to the computation of the potential energy
U_{\text{b-b}}
of the self interaction of the background. Thus by (
3
) with
C(\vec{r})=-\rho_{\rm b}
,

U_{\text{b-b}}={1\over 2}\rho_{\rm b}^{2}\int_{\Omega}d\vec{r}\,\int_{\Omega}d\vec{r}\,\Phi_{d}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})=-{1\over 2}\rho_{\rm b}\int_{\Omega}V(\vec{r})\,d\vec{r}

(7)

(here the factor of one half is due to the double counting of the potential energy implied by the double integral).
Noting too that the total potential energy of the interaction between the
N
unit mobile particles at positions

\vec{r}_{1},\dots,\vec{r}_{N}
is

U_{\text{p-p}}:=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\Phi_{\rm d}(\vec{r}_{j},\vec{r}_{k}),

(8)

one has that the Boltzmann factor for the system is
e^{-\beta(U_{\text{p-p}}+U_{\text{p-b}}+U_{\text{b-b}})}
; see also
[
78
, §1.4.1]
,
[
114
, §III.A]
. Here
\beta
is the dimensionless inverse temperature.

In the formalism of equilibrium statistical mechanics one proceeds from knowledge of the Boltzmann factor to form the partition function

Z_{N,\beta}={1\over N!}\int_{\Omega}d\vec{r}_{1}\cdots\int_{\Omega}d\vec{r}_{N}\,e^{-\beta(U_{\text{p-p}}+U_{\text{p-b}}+U_{\text{b-b}})}.

(9)

From the partition function one computes the dimensionless free energy as
\beta F=-\log Z_{N,\beta}
. A significant property of this quantity is that
for
\Omega=N^{1/d}\Omega_{0}
with
|\Omega_{0}|=\rho_{\rm b}
(i.e.
\Omega
relates to
\Omega_{0}
by a dilation consistent with
(
5
)), the
large
N
limit of

\beta F/N
exists. Thus the dimensionless free energy is extensive, being proportional to
N
, and so has a well defined thermodynamic limit
[
115
]
,

[
133
]
,
[
114
, Th.23]
.

A useful viewpoint in understanding the role played by all terms in the total potential energy is the mean field heuristic
that for typical configurations the density of the particles will equal to that of the background and consequently to leading order
for typical configurations

U_{\text{p-b}}\sim\rho_{\rm b}\int_{\Omega}V(\vec{r})\,\,d\vec{r},\qquad U_{\text{p-p}}\sim U_{\text{b-b}}.

Accepting this, and recalling (
7
), one is lead to the conclusion that the leading order of the three terms making up the total energy cancels. An alternative viewpoint is that if (
9
) was to be considered without
U_{\text{b-b}}
present in the total energy, one could predict the leading large
N
form as being the leading large
N
form of
e^{\beta U_{\text{b-b}}}
, and thus as being entirely determined by electrostatics.

This latter conclusion is far from isolated. Asking the question as to what the leading order particle density of the one-component plasma must be, macroscopic electrostatics gives the answer as equal and opposite to the background charge density. If not an electric field would be created and the system would be out of equilibrium. In other directions, in the work
[
99
]
, macroscopic electrostatics was used to predict the leading form of the tails of the probability distribution for the random variable
N(R)
counting the number of particles in a ball of radius
R
for the two and three dimensional one-component plasma. Here it is assumed that the thermodynamic limit has already been taken, and that
R\gg 1
. Note that the mean of this distribution is
\rho_{\rm b}|D_{R}|
, where
|D_{R}|
is the volume of the ball of radius
R
, and the standard deviation is proportional to
|\partial D_{R}|^{1/2}
(i.e. the square root of that surface area of
D_{R}
)
[
120
]
; see also
[
47
,
71
,
6
,
7
,
10
,
122
,
126
]
for recent extensive studies on such a so-called hyperuniform phenomenon. The tail (large deviation regime) considered was the probability that the number of particles present in
D_{R}
is greater than
c|\partial D_{R}|^{\alpha}
for
\alpha>1/2
,
c>0
(for the details of this formula in the case
\alpha>2
, see (
99
) below).
Related electrostatic based hole probability calculations in the two-dimensional case, when there is an application to the complex Ginibre random matrix ensemble (also know as the GinUE — see the recent monograph
[
38
]
for more on this including the Coulomb gas analogy), can be found in
[
76
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
48
]
.

1.2
One-component Riesz potential models

Let
X
be an
N\times N
random matrix with independent standard real (complex) Gaussian entries, and form from this the Hermitian matrix
H={1\over 2}(X+X^{\dagger})
. A classical result in random matrix theory is that the joint eigenvalue probability density function (PDF) is proportional to

\prod_{l=1}^{N}e^{-\beta\lambda_{l}^{2}/2}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}|^{\beta},\quad\lambda_{l}\in\mathbb{R}\>(l=1,\dots,N),

(10)

where
\beta=1
(
\beta=2
) in the real (complex) case; see e.g.
[
78
, §1.2&1.3]
.
One sees that this has the functional form
e^{-\beta U}
for

U={1\over 2}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\lambda_{l}^{2}-\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\log|\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}|,

(11)

and thus has the interpretation as the Boltzmann factor for a one-component log-potential Coulomb systems with background-particle potential equal to
\lambda^{2}/2

(up to an additive constant). However, in distinction to the setting of Section
1.1
, the domain is now one-dimensional rather than two-dimensional, which results in different behaviours. As an example, we know that a constant background density is responsible for a quadratic background-particle potential in the latter setting (recall (
4
), whereas a result which can be traced back to Wigner
[
141
]
gives that a background with profile proportional to
(1-y^{2})^{1/2}\mathbbm{1}_{|y|<1}
,

y=\lambda/\sqrt{2N}
—
which is now referred to as the Wigner semi-circle — implies
the quadratic potential in (
11
). Most importantly from an electrostatics point of view is that this background can be determined by solving the potential theoretic Frostman-type
problem

{1\over 2}\lambda^{2}-\int_{-c}^{c}\mu(y)\log|\lambda-y|\,dy\quad\begin{cases}=C,&|y|\leq c,\\
\geq C,&|y|>c,\end{cases}

(12)

for the density
\mu(y)
and its support
(-c,c)
(
C
is sometimes referred to as Robin’s constant); see
[
132
]
.

This suggests introducing the family of potentials, known as Riesz potentials,

\Psi_{s}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})=\begin{cases}-|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}|^{-s},&s<0,\\
-\log|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}|,&s=0,\\
|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}|^{-s},&s>0,\end{cases}

(13)

defined in the first instance independent of the dimension. Further consideration does relate the exponent
s
to the dimension
d
. Thus, to be in a setting that a smeared out background is necessary for thermodynamic stability, we restrict attention to the cases that
\Psi_{s}
is not integrable with respect to
\vec{r}\,^{\prime}
at infinity and thus (as emphasised in
[
114
, §I]
) for typical configurations
with a constant density

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Psi_{s}(\vec{r}_{j},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})

will not converge as
N\to\infty
. Convergence then can only be achieved by the introduction of a smeared out background (recall the paragraph below (
9
)). This happens for
s\leq d
(note that the Coulomb case (
2
) corresponds to
s=d-2
).
We remark that for
d=1
, the short-range regime
s>1
has also been investigated (cf.
[
4
,
93
]
and related works), and in particular the case
s=2
exhibits connections with the Calogero–Moser model
[
5
]
.

There is still a further restriction to be placed on
s
. A general principle for statistical mechanical systems with a long range pair potential to be thermodynamically stable is that of screening (see e.g.
[
78
, §14.1]
). Use of linear response theory gives that for this to be well defined the reciprocal of the Fourier transform of the pair potential (interpreted as a generalised function
[
116
]
) must go to zero and be positive. This Fourier transform equals
[
114
, Eq. (5)]

2^{-s+d/2}(\Gamma((d-s)/2)/\Gamma(s/2))|k|^{s-d}
. Its reciprocal going to zero as
|k|\to 0
then requires
s<d
(and hence excluding the boundary case in the above paragraph) and
s
not equal to a negative even integer, while positivity requires
s>-2.

Outline of the remainder of the article

The first topic we consider, which forms the content of §

As is well known, going back to Wigner

2
Background as an analytic continuation

From the discussion of §
1.2
, the rationale for introducing the uniform background is so that the total potential energy will, for typical configurations, be of order
N
(i.e. extensive). A related viewpoint is that the potential at a point in the interior of the system will be of order unity.
For configurations forming lattices, and for particular geometries, there are circumstances when these expected consequences can be verified explicitly, and moreover can be shown to be an analytic continuation in
s
of the same quantity when no background is necessary.

2.1
Riesz gas on a circle

The one-dimensional domain of a circle of radius
R
lends itself to the simplest analysis.
Due to the rotational symmetry, the potential
V(\phi)
between a unit charge and the neutralising background charge density
-\rho_{\rm b}=-N/2\pi R
is independent of
\phi
. In the logarithmic case (
s=0
in (
13
)) one calculates
V(\phi)=N\log R
by using the classical Jensen’s formula. For the remaining allowed values of the exponent
s
in one-dimension
(the discussion below (
13
) gives
-2<s<1
) use of a trigonometric form of the
\beta
integral gives

V(\phi)=V_{0}={\rm sgn}\,(s)NR^{-s}{\Gamma(1-s)\over(\Gamma(1-s/2))^{2}}.

(14)

In terms of this
\phi
independent potential, one calculates from (
6
) and (
7
) that

U_{\text{p-b}}+U_{\text{b-b}}=NV_{0}/2.

(15)

As a lattice configuration, we suppose that the mobile charges are at their minimum energy positions, which on a circle corresponds to being equally spaced
[
142
,
36
]
.
1
1

1

As a side note, on the two-dimensional sphere the problem of finding an energy-minimising configuration is known as Smale’s 7th problem
[
136
]
; see also
[
30
]
for a recent account.
For the logarithmic potential, the simple identity
\prod_{l=1}^{N}(z-e^{2\pi il/N})=z^{N}-1
, together with the value of (
15
) give

U_{\text{p-b}}+U_{\text{b-b}}+U_{\text{p-p}}=-{1\over 2}N\log 2\pi\rho_{\rm b}
, exhibiting that it is linear
N
.
For
s\neq 0
the sum giving the potential energy of the equally spaced configuration on the circle has been analysed in

[
36
]
. It follows from this work that
U_{\text{p-b}}+U_{\text{b-b}}+U_{\text{p-p}}\sim N{\rm sgn}\,(s)\rho_{\rm b}^{s}\zeta(s)(1+{\rm O}(N^{-2})),
where
\zeta(s)
denotes the Riemann zeta function.
One again sees the expected linear dependence on
N
. In relation to the logarithmic potential case, one notices that

{d\over ds}\rho_{\rm b}^{s}\zeta(s)|_{s=0}=-{1\over 2}\log 2\pi\rho_{\rm b}
.

Following the theme put forward in
[
114
, §IV.A]
, the result so obtained for the leading order total energy of the Riesz gas on a circle of radius
R
with density
\rho_{\rm b}=N/2\pi R

and exponent
0<s<1

should be compared against that for the Riesz gas with the same density but exponent
s>1
. In the latter setting the Riesz potential is integrable at infinity, so one does without a neutralising background. For the particle-particle potential energy in the case of equal spacing we have

U_{\text{p-p}}={N\over 2R^{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}{1\over|1-e^{2\pi ij/N}|^{s}}\sim N\rho_{\rm b}^{s}\zeta(s),\quad s>1

(16)

which is same result found for the leading total potential energy for

. Thus one can interpret the background as providing an analytic continuation of the leading total energy from the regime where no background is required.

We consider next the potential energy
E_{N}(s,\phi)
say at an angle
\phi
due to the particles fixed to be equally spaced (at

with

for definiteness), and the uniform background. We have

E_{N}(s,\phi)=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Psi_{s}(Re^{2\pi ij/N},Re^{i\phi})\Big)-V(\phi).

(17)

In the case
s=0
, using the same identity as used below (
15
),
together with the value of
V(\phi)
as noted above (
14
) we find

E_{N}(s,\phi)\Big|_{s=0}=-\log|e^{iN\phi}-1|.

(18)

We remark that this is precisely the same logarithmic potential for unit charges on a circle, specifically at angles 0 and
N\phi
.
Setting
\phi=2\pi x/N
,
x\notin\mathbb{Z}
, and thus measuring
\phi
in units of the spacing between the lattice points, we arrive at the
order unity limiting quantity

-\log|e^{2\pi ix}-1|
.
For all other
s
in the range

-2<s<1
, repeating the calculation of
[
36
]
gives

\lim_{N\to\infty}E_{N}(s,2\pi x/N)=\zeta(s;x)+\zeta(s;1-x),\quad 0<x<1,

(19)

where
\zeta(s;a)
denotes the Hurwitz zeta function
(for
x\notin\mathbb{Z}
, the evaluation is deduced from this by the requirement of periodicty, period 1, in

).
The point to be made here is that this is the analytic continuation in
s
of the limiting large
N
form of

E_{N}(s,2\pi x/N)
for
s>1

17

V(\phi)
deleted.

2.2
Higher dimensions

For
d\geq 2
we restrict our attention to
a discussion of the analytic properties of the potential energy at a point for lattice configurations
when a background is required, with our presentation consisting mostly of quoting results from
[
114
]
.

Let
\{\vec{r}_{j}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
\inf_{j\neq k}|\vec{r}_{j}-\vec{r}_{k}|>0
, and let
B_{R}(\vec{r})
denote the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of radius
R

\vec{r}
.
In the
d

[

that

\Phi(s,\vec{r}):=\lim_{R\to\infty}\Big(\sum_{\{\vec{r}_{j}\}\in B_{R}(\vec{r})}{1\over|\vec{r}-\vec{r}_{j}|^{s}}-\rho_{\rm b}\int_{B_{R}(\vec{r})}{d\vec{r}\,^{\prime}\over|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}|^{s}}\Big)

(20)

in the range of exponents

(

),

d-2<s<d-2+2/(d+1)
(

)
is an analytic continuation of the same quantity defined without the integral (background) term.

Specialising to a lattice
\mathcal{L}

(integer span of a basis in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), more can be said
[
114
, §2]
.
Let us use the notation

\Phi(s,\vec{r})
for the corresponding lattice sum, which is well defined for

.
This a periodic function of

In the range

that

\Phi(s,\vec{r})

Moreover, if the domains of summation/ integration are appropriately modified to be consistent with the lattice structure, we have
[

\mathcal{L}
as zero quadrapole moment, the limit gives rise to an extra term

integrated over the cell
[

.

3
Explicit functional forms of electrostatic potentials due to a uniform background

3.1
The Coulomb potential with ball geometry

We first consider the case that
\Omega
is the (open) ball of radius
R
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, centred at the origin. Denote this

B_{R}
. For this
\Omega
we want to evaluate the potential
V(\vec{r})
as specified by (
3
) with the background charge density the constant
C(\vec{r})=-\rho_{\rm b}
. In this setting we know that up to additive harmonic functions,
V(\vec{r})
has the functional form (
4
), which is the solution of the Poisson equation
\nabla_{\vec{r}}^{2}V(\vec{r})=c_{d}\chi_{d}\rho_{\rm b}\mathbbm{1}_{\vec{r}\in\Omega}
. The simplifying feature of
\Omega
being
a sphere is that
V(\vec{r})
must be a function of only
r=|\vec{r}|
. We can then replace the operator
\nabla_{\vec{r}}^{2}
by its radial component
(we use the symbol
\doteq
to indicate this) in spherical coordinates,

\nabla_{\vec{r}}^{2}\doteq{1\over r^{d-1}}{d\over dr}r^{d-1}{d\over dr}.

(22)

The Poisson equation can now be solved to find missing harmonic functions in (
4
).

Proposition 3.1
.

For the ball geometry as specified above, with the ball filled by a uniform background charge density
-\rho_{b}
, total charge
-N
, the electrostatic potential
V(\vec{r})=V(r)
is given by

V(\vec{r})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\rho_{\rm b}\Big({c_{d}\chi_{d}\over 2d}\Big)(r^{2}-R^{2})-N\Phi_{\rm d}(R),&\vec{r}\in B_{R},\\[5.69046pt]
-N\Phi_{\rm d}(r),&{\rm otherwise},\end{cases}

(23)

where
\Phi_{\rm d}(r)=\Phi_{\rm d}(\vec{r},\vec{0})
for any vector
\vec{r}
such that

\vec{r}=r
.

Proof.

Consider first the potential outside of the ball. The RHS of the Poisson equation is then zero, and so restricting attention to radially symmetric solutions

V(\vec{r})=V(r)

as compatible with the symmetry of the ball, according to (
22
) our task is to solve

{d\over dr}r^{d-1}{d\over dr}V(r)=0.

(24)

This elementary second order linear differential equation has general solution

3.5.1
Non-intersecting Brownian particles on a line

We begin by considering not non-intersecting Brownian particles, but rather a classical gas of particles
\{x_{j}\}
on the real line with total energy

U={c\over 2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{2}-\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\log\Big|\sinh{\pi(x_{k}-x_{j})\over L}\Big|.

(53)

One sees that for large separations
|x_{k}-x_{j}|\to\infty
the sum over pairs has the leading order form
-(\pi/L)\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|x_{k}-x_{j}|,
and thus is proportional to the particle-particle potential energy for a one-component one-dimensional Coulomb system. Moreover, suppose that the one-dimensional Coulomb system has a uniform background in
[-R,R]
with
R=\pi N/cL
.
Then according to (
31
) the quadratic term in (
53
) can be interpreted as resulting from the interaction of the particles with this uniform background. With the particle density of a one-component Coulomb system in equilibrium matching that of the background (if not, there would be an electric field and the system would be out of equilibrium), it follows from these Coulomb gas considerations that for large
N
the particle density for the gas given by (
53
) will be uniform in the interval

[-\pi N/cL,\pi N/cL]
, and zero outside of this interval. We know from (
23
) that outside of the background, the Coulomb potential in the one-dimensional case is
N|x|
and thus the one-body term in (
53
) no longer matches. However the particles are outside of the region of the background (interior region) with low probability, so this has no effect on our conclusion.

Predictions can also be made for the large
N
form of the configuration integral

Q_{N,\beta}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx_{1}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx_{N}\,e^{-\beta(c/2)\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{2}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\Big|2\sinh{\pi(x_{k}-x_{j})\over L}\Big|^{\beta}.

(54)

From the viewpoint of a one-component Coulomb system,
missing from the implied Boltzmann factor in (
54
) is the terms independent of
\{x_{j}\}
in

e^{-\beta\pi(U_{\text{b-b}}+U_{\text{p-b}})/L}

(the factor of
\pi/L
is due to this multiplying the effective one-dimensional Coulomb potential; recall the text below (
53
)),
which according to (
10
) is equal to

\exp(-\beta\pi N^{2}R/6L)=\exp(-\pi^{2}\beta N^{3}/6cL^{2})
. The existence of the thermodynamic limit for a one-component Coulomb system as discussed below
(
9
) then gives that for large
N
, with
c,L
fixed,
-\pi^{2}\beta N^{3}/6cL^{2}+\log{1\over N!}Q_{N,\beta}
is of order
N
. We therefore conclude that the large
N
expansion of
\log Q_{N,\beta}
is
\pi^{2}\beta N^{3}/6cL^{2}+N\log N
, up to terms of order
N
. This can be checked in the case
\beta=2
, when we have the exact evaluation
[
138
]

Q_{N,\beta}\Big|_{\beta=2}=(\pi/c)^{N/2}N!e^{gN(N^{2}-1)/6}\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}(1-q^{j})^{N-j},

(55)

where
g=2\pi^{2}/cL^{2}
and

q=e^{-g}

Electrostatic potentials due to surface charges

4.1
Spherical and ellipsoid surfaces

A fundamental setting in electrostatics is the distribution of charge on a conducting surface — often referred to as the equilibrium charge distribution. As a concrete example, one can consider the latter as the shell formed by an ellipsoid surface, which in turn contains as a special case the surface of sphere. A fundamental consequence of the surface being conducting is that the potential on the surface is a constant. Here then one does not take the viewpoint of first imposing a uniform background on the surface — doing so is a different setting, albeit one which occurs in applications; recall the first paragraph of §
1.2
.

4.2
Projections of spherical and ellipsoidal uniformly charged bodies

As an introductory example, consider the ball

Let

There is further significance to both

(

d=2
), which after appropriate parameterisation can be checked to agree.

4.4
The Green function
g_{\mathbb{C}\backslash\bar{\Omega}}(z,w)

The viewpoint for our applications is that
\Omega
is a conducting domain (which may be infinite) with surface that is held at zero potential. A unit charge is placed at

w\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\bar{\Omega}
, and we denote by

g_{\mathbb{C}\backslash\bar{\Omega}}(z,w)
the electrostatic potential at
z
due to this charge and the effect if the conducting medium. More precisely,

g_{\mathbb{C}\backslash\bar{\Omega}}(z,w)
is the real valued function satisfying the two-dimensional Poisson equation
\nabla_{z}^{2}g_{\mathbb{C}\backslash\bar{\Omega}}(z,w)=-2\pi\delta(z-w)
,
outside of the conducting medium,
subject to the boundary condition that it vanishes on
\partial\Omega
. To make the Green function unique it is required that it be symmetric in
z,w
.

Well known and easy to verify is that for
\Omega=B_{R}
(the disk of radius
R
),

g_{\mathbb{C}\backslash\bar{B}_{R}}(z,w)=-\log{|z-w|\over|1-z\bar{w}/R^{2}|}.

(74)

Rearranging the denominator

\log|1-z\bar{w}/R^{2}|=\log|z-R^{2}/\bar{w}|+\log|\bar{w}/R^{2}|
, one sees that the effect of the metal disk to create an image charge of opposite sign at the inversion point
R^{2}/\bar{w}
within the disk.
The conformal map
\zeta(z)=(z+i)/(z-i)
maps to upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
to the exterior of the unit disk. Substituting in (
74
) with
R=1
gives the further well known result

g_{\mathbb{H}}(z,w)=-\log{|z-w|\over|z-\bar{w}|},

(75)

which is easy to independently deduce using the method of images (i.e. the effect of the metal half plane is to create a negative charge at
\bar{w}
in the half plane). Generally, if the conformal map from the outside of
\Omega
to the outside of the unit circle is
\zeta(z)
, it follows from (
74
) that

g_{\mathbb{C}\backslash\bar{\Omega}}(z,w)=-\log{|\zeta(z)-\zeta(w)|\over|1-\zeta(z)\overline{\zeta(w)}|}.

(76)

We refer the reader to
[
57
]
and
[
13
, § 2]
for further discussions on Green’s functions in various domains, particularly those beyond the simply connected case, where Jacobi theta functions and their extensions—such as the Schottky–Klein prime functions or Riemann theta functions—play a natural role in their representation.

Remark 4.2
.

Suppose the conducting medium is
B_{R}
in dimension
d=3
. Let
g_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\bar{B}_{R}}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})
denote the Green function at
\vec{r}

due to a point charge at

\vec{r}\,^{\prime}
and the response in the conducting medium. While complex variable methods are no longer applicable, the method of images can be used to show

g_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\bar{B}_{R}}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})={1\over|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}|}-{R\over|\vec{r}\,^{\prime}||\vec{r}-R^{2}\vec{r}\,^{\prime}/|\vec{r}\,^{\prime}|^{2}|}.

(77)

As another well known example in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for which the Green function can be computed using the method of images, let
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{3}_{-}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\,z<0\}
. For this conducting region we have

g_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\bar{\Omega}}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})={1\over|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}|}-{1\over|\vec{r}-\vec{r}_{-}^{\,\prime}|},

(78)

where
\vec{r}_{-}^{\,\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},-z^{\prime})
. Such results are covered in standard texts such as
[
96
]
.

4.5
Applications

4.5.1
Log-potential Coulomb gas on a closed contour — free energy expansion

Let
\partial\Omega
be a closed contour, corresponding to the outer boundary of a compact set
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(or in
\mathbb{C}
).
For
N
unit charges repelling via the logarithmic potential and contained in the closure of
\Omega
, it is a known theorem that the minimum energy configuration has each particle positioned on
\partial\Omega
— this follows from the maximum modulus principle for analytic functions; see e.g.
[
131
]
). In the large
N
limit the resulting normalised surface charge density must then be an equipotential and thus is identified as
\sigma(\vec{r})
from
§
4.3
. An alternative way to arrive at
\sigma(\vec{r})
starting with a statistical mechanical system is to confine the log-potential Coulomb gas to
\partial\Omega
, then to take the large
N
limit. In either case the circumstance is distinct from that of the one-component plasma as there is no background. Moreover we are interested in the large
N
limit with
\partial\Omega
fixed, which is distinct from the thermodynamic limit when both
N
and the volume go to infinity simultaneously, with the density constant.

For purposes of predicting the leading large
N
asymptotics, we propose to identify an equivalent system which does have a background and which furthermore permits a scaling allowing for the thermodynamic limit. This equivalent system starts by imposing a background surface charge density
-N\sigma(\vec{r})
on
\partial\Omega
. Since by the characterisation of
\sigma(\vec{r})
as implying that
\partial\Omega
is a equipotential, this does not alter the normalised charge density of mobile particles equaling
\sigma(\vec{r})
. With
V_{\partial\Omega}
defined as below (
70
), this implies the contribution to the Boltzmann factor from the background-background, and background particle interactions

e^{\beta N^{2}V_{\partial\Omega}/2}
. We furthermore scale
\partial\Omega\mapsto N\partial\Omega
so as to be considering the one-component log-gas plasma confined to this latter contour with Boltzmann factor

e^{\beta N^{2}V_{\partial\Omega}/2}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|z-z^{\prime}|
. For this we expect the corresponding free energy to be extensive. After rescaling the contour back to the original by changing variables in the particle coordinates, we are lead to the predicted large
N
asymptotic formula

\log{1\over N!}\int_{\partial\Omega}|dz_{1}|\cdots\int_{\partial\Omega}|dz_{N}|\,\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|z_{k}-z_{j}|^{\beta}\sim-{\beta N^{2}\over 2}\log N-{\beta N^{2}\over 2}V_{\partial\Omega}+\Big({\beta\over 2}-1\Big)N\log N,

(79)

up to terms of order
N
. This indeed agrees with a known rigorous result
[
100
]
,
[
54
]
(these references extend the asymptotic expansion to give too the order
N
and the constant term — see also
[
143
]
).
One remarks that the derivation of (
79
) applies equally as well in the circumstance that the closed curve
\partial\Omega
is replaced by an open contour
\gamma
say. The validity of the predicted formula is confirmed by the recent rigorous work
[
55
]
, which moreover extends the asymptotic expansion through to the constant term; see also
[
89
]
and references therein for related works.

4.5.2
Log-potential Coulomb gas on a closed contour — fluctuation formulas

In the case of
\partial\Omega=\partial B_{1}
and so equalling the unit circle in the complex plane, the Boltzmann factor of the corresponding log-gas model with
\beta=2
,
\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|e^{i\theta_{k}}-e^{i\theta_{j}}|^{2}
, is proportional to the joint eigenvalue PDF for Haar distributed unitary random matrices; see e.g.
[
62
]
. This is a determinantal point process
[
78
, Ch. 5]
and so has its correlation functions determined by a single kernel function

K_{N}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})={1\over 2\pi}{\sin N(\theta-\theta^{\prime})/2\over\sin(\theta-\theta^{\prime})/2}.

(80)

Specifically, we have for the so-called truncated two-point correlation function (see
[
78
, §5.1.1]
)
\rho_{(2),N}^{T}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=-|K_{N}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})|^{2}
. Generally the truncated two-point correlation is fundamental to fluctuation formulas for so-called linear statistics
[
82
, Prop. 2.6]

{\rm Cov}\,\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{N}f(\theta_{l}),\sum_{l=1}^{N}g(\theta_{l})\Big)=-{1\over 2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta^{\prime}\,(f(\theta)-f(\theta^{\prime}))(g(\theta)-g(\theta^{\prime}))\rho_{(2),N}^{T}(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

(81)

Squaring (
80
), noting that

(\sin N(\theta-\theta^{\prime})/2)^{2}={1\over 2}(1-\cos N(\theta-\theta^{\prime}))
and substituting as appropriate, we observe the limiting form (see e.g.
[
82
, Eq.(2.23)]
)

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\rm Cov}\,\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{N}f(\theta_{l}),\sum_{l=1}^{N}g(\theta_{l})\Big)={1\over(4\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta^{\prime}\,{(f(\theta)-f(\theta^{\prime})(g(\theta)-f(\theta^{\prime})\over(\sin(\theta-\theta^{\prime})/2)^{2}},

(82)

due to the oscillatory term causing cancellations in the integral to leading order.

It is instructive to compare (
82
) to the analogous result in relation to the
\beta=2
log-gas model with Boltzmann factor
\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|z_{k}-z_{j}|^{2}
. Up to normalisation, the latter arises in random matrix theory as the eigenvalue PDF of the top
N\times N
sub-block of an
(N+1)\times(N+1)
Haar distributed random matrix
[
145
]
. This is again a determinantal point process, now with kernel

K_{N}(z,z^{\prime})={1\over\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{N}j(z\bar{z}^{\prime})^{j-1}={1\over\pi}{d\over dw}{1-w^{N+1}\over 1-w}\bigg|_{w=z\bar{z}^{\prime}}\mathop{\sim}\limits_{N\to\infty}-{N\over\pi}{w^{N}\over 1-w}\bigg|_{w=z\bar{z}^{\prime}},

(83)

where the validity of the large
N
form requires
w\neq 1
.
From this functional form, it follows from integration by parts that

\int_{0}^{1}rdr\int_{0}^{1}r^{\prime}dr^{\prime}\,|K_{N}(re^{i\theta},r^{\prime}e^{i\theta^{\prime}})|^{2}\sim\Big({1\over 2\pi}\Big)^{2}{1\over|1-e^{i(\theta-\theta^{\prime})}|^{2}},

(84)

which we recognise as
1/2
of the smoothed part of
\rho_{(2),N}^{T}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
as implied by (
80
)
(by the smoothed part, we mean after application of the identity noted below (
81
) with the oscillatory term discarded). In particular, this provides another way to arrive at the limiting boundary fluctuation formula (
82
); albeit multiplied by
1/2

(see the subsection below for more on this modification).

There is a link between (
82
) and
g_{\mathbb{C}\backslash B_{1}}(z,w)
,

g_{\mathbb{C}\backslash B_{1}}(z,w)+\log|z-w|\\
=\beta\bigg(\lim_{N\to\infty}{\rm Cov}\,\Big(-\sum_{l=1}^{N}\log|e^{i\theta_{l}}-z|,-\sum_{l=1}^{N}\log|e^{i\theta_{l}}-w|\Big)+\log|zw|\bigg),\>\>|z|,|w|>1.

(85)

This follows from a linear response argument, with the charge at
z
regarded as having infinitesimal strength
\delta q
,
and considering the change in linear statistic

. From an electrostatics viewpoint, in the infinite density limit this should

to account for the test charge of strength
\delta q
at
z

\delta q
to arrive at the RHS (the factor of

\delta q
cancels out from both sides); see
[
98
]
,
[
78
, §14.3]
.
For a direct verification, one requires knowledge that the RHS of (

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}|n|f_{n}g_{-n}

f_{n}:={1\over 2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\theta)e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta

The identity (

|z|,|w|>1

From (

(86)

We refer to

With

The above considerations for the log-gas on a contour at

Balayage measure and the hole probability

5.1
Balayage measure

Let
\Omega
,
V(\vec{r})
be as in (
3
). Let
\mu_{\partial\Omega}(\vec{r})
be a charge density supported on
\partial\Omega
creating a potential
V_{\partial\Omega}(\vec{r})
. This charge density is said to correspond to a balayage measure if

V_{\partial\Omega}(\vec{r})=V(\vec{r})
for all
\vec{r}
outside of
\Omega
(continuity of the potential then gives that this equality holds true on the boundary as well). As a simple example, take
\Omega=B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is to be filled with a uniform charge density of total charge
Q
. Then for

\vec{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\bar{B}_{R}
we know from (
23
) that for
\vec{r}
outside of the ball
V(\vec{r})=Q\Phi_{d}(\vec{r})
, which in words is
Q
times the Coulomb potential.
On the other hand, with
c_{d}
specified as below (
1
), we know from (
59
) and (
60
) that the surface charge density
\sigma(\vec{r})=Q/(R^{d-1}c_{d})

Distinct from the ball geometry is an annulus, which has both an inner and outer boundary. Let us consider this geometry, specified by

5.2
Hole probability

Our viewpoint of a one-component charged system for the most part (an exception is §
3.4
) is that the background charge density is prescribed. From a physical perspective, the charge density of the mobile charges must be to leading order equal and opposite that of the background so that the net electric field is zero — if not the system would not be in equilibrium.

A possible circumstance is that there is a conditioning so that a region
\Omega_{0}
say inside the droplet is free of mobile charges. The ratio of partition functions
Z_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{0}}/Z_{\Omega}
then has the interpretation of the probability — known as the gap probability — that in the system without conditioning, there are no mobile charges in
\Omega_{0}
. We know that the leading large
N
form of the denominator
Z_{\Omega}
is given in terms of certain electrostatic energies determined from the potential due to the interaction of the background with a particle. To apply the same reasoning to
Z_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{0}}
, we need to be able to quantify, to leading order, the new particle charge density resulting from the conditioning. From an electrostatics viewpoint, a fundamental feature of interations via the
d
-dimensional Coulomb potential in a
d
-dimensional domain is that the displaced charge with accumulate on boundaries of a host conductor, which in the present situation must be
\partial\Omega_{0}
. Moreover, the rule that determines its distribution must be that the potential experienced at a point in

\Omega\backslash\Omega_{0}
due to the positive charge density of the displaced mobile charges, and the negative charge density of the background in
\Omega_{0}
, must exactly cancel (again, if not, the charges in this region would not be in equilbrium). These facts together tell us that the charge density on the boundary must correspond to the balayage measure with
Q=\rho_{\rm b}|\Omega_{0}|
(the latter being the expected number of charges in the region
\Omega_{0}
without conditioning).
