Cell wall-inhibiting antibiotics resistance in Streptococcus pneumoniae causing invasive diseases: a global systematic review and meta-analysis in pediatric populations
Authors: Laith B Alhusseini, Saeid Sadeghi Ghazi Chaki, Mohammad Ali Noshak, Khalil Azizian, Ebrahim Kouhsari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStreptococcus pneumoniae causes diseases such as meningitis and bacteremia, which lead to high morbidity and mortality rates among children under 5 years old. The invasive form of the disease, known as invasive pneumococcal disease, is a significant global health threat to children. Additionally, antimicrobial resistance in this bacterium has greatly hindered worldwide efforts to prevent and treat infections. In this meta-analysis, we reviewed published studies to provide a comprehensive overview of the global status of resistance to anti-cell wall antibiotics among S. pneumoniae strains causing invasive diseases. We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Embase for relevant studies published up to July 25, 2022. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R statistical software package. Our meta-analysis included 54 studies from 23 countries, spanning the period from 1994 to 2022. The average prevalence rates of antibiotic resistance were as follows: 30.1% for penicillin-non-susceptible pneumococci, 1.2% for amoxicillin, 2.5% for amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, 6.1% for ceftriaxone, and 0.6% for vancomycin. No significant differences were observed in resistance rates for vancomycin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, or amoxicillin. Additionally, no notable variations were found across age groups, over time, or based on different antibiotic susceptibility testing methods. The high prevalence of penicillin-non-susceptible pneumococci represents a significant challenge for global healthcare systems. Furthermore, our findings highlight considerable geographical variation in antibiotic resistance levels to cell wall–targeting antibiotics among S. pneumoniae isolates globally.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-025-02161-x.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Cell wall-inhibiting antibiotics, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Invasive diseases, Systematic review and meta-analysis

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae, often referred to as S. pneumoniae, is a Gram-positive bacterium that causes a wide range of infections, including meningitis, pneumonia, otitis media, and bloodstream infections. It poses a major global health threat to children under five years old, contributing significantly to morbidity and mortality in this vulnerable population [1]. When S. pneumoniae is found in normally sterile sites, such as blood or cerebrospinal fluid, it is classified as invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD). This condition most commonly affects infants under two years of age, older adults over 65, and people with weakened immune systems [2]. According to the World Health Organization, nearly half a million children under five are affected by pneumococcal disease every year, with most cases occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where access to healthcare is limited [3]. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become one of the biggest threats to treating bacterial infections globally. The first penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae strain was identified in Australia back in 1967, and since the 1990s, resistance rates have been rising steadily across the world [4–6]. Beta-lactam antibiotics, such as penicillins and cephalosporins, are the mainstay treatment for pneumococcal infections because they target the bacterial cell wall. Resistant strains, however, have developed changes in their penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)—especially PBP1a, PBP2b, and PBP2x—which reduce the drugs’ effectiveness.

As a result, clinicians increasingly rely on alternative treatments like macrolides, fluoroquinolones, lincosamides, and vancomycin [7]. The emergence of multi-drug resistant (MDR) strains has made managing serious infections, especially pneumococcal meningitis, even more challenging [2, 8–10].

Previous studies have documented highly variable resistance among S. pneumoniae isolates causing invasive disease in children. Resistance to penicillin-non-susceptible pneumococci (PNSP) has been reported from < 20% in many high-income countries to over 40% in several low- and middle-income settings [11, 12]. Zafar et al. (2025) [13] reported that while penicillin resistance in S. pneumoniae increased from 10% to 34.1% between 2002 and 2009 in Pakistani children, susceptibility to amoxicillin–clavulanate remained 99.4–100%, indicating continued effectiveness. Reduced susceptibility to ceftriaxone has been observed sporadically [11, 14, 15], while vancomycin resistance is extremely rare and limited to isolated case reports [11]. These resistance patterns have been largely driven by selective antibiotic pressure, altered penicillin-binding proteins, clonal expansion, and serotype replacement following pneumococcal conjugate vaccine introduction [11, 14, 15]. However, most prior reviews combine adult and pediatric populations or invasive and non-invasive isolates, leaving a major gap in global evidence focused on pediatric IPD, which this meta-analysis aims to address.

The introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) has had a major impact on reducing both disease incidence and antibiotic resistance by targeting the most common and virulent serotypes [16, 17]. However, the phenomenon of serotype replacement—where non-vaccine serotypes begin to dominate—threatens to undo some of these gains. In many LMICs, challenges such as limited vaccine coverage, poor sanitation, overcrowding, and inappropriate antibiotic use contribute to the persistence and spread of resistant strains [18, 19]. These factors highlight the urgent need for coordinated strategies that combine vaccination programs, improved diagnostics, and stronger antimicrobial stewardship, especially in resource-limited settings [16, 17].

This study focuses specifically on AMR to cell wall-inhibiting antibiotics, particularly beta-lactams and vancomycin, which remain key therapeutic options for treating invasive pneumococcal infections. These antibiotics are especially relevant in pediatric and hospitalized settings due to their safety profile and widespread use. This work is part of a larger research initiative examining resistance patterns across multiple antibiotic classes, including macrolides, fluoroquinolones, and lincosamides. Each component of the project addresses a distinct antibiotic group to allow for detailed analysis and interpretation. By narrowing the scope in this way, we aim to provide deeper insight into resistance trends and mechanisms associated with these critically important drug classes.

Existing studies have explored AMR patterns in S. pneumoniae, including global trends and their clinical implications [5, 20–22]. These reports highlight the evolving nature of resistance and underscore the importance of continued surveillance, especially in pediatric populations. However, a global analysis specifically focused on resistance cell wall-inhibiting antibiotics, such as beta-lactams and vancomycin—key first-line treatments for invasive pneumococcal infections—remains limited. Our study addresses this gap by providing a comprehensive assessment of resistance trends over time, focusing on pediatric isolates and the associated therapeutic challenges, particularly in LMICs.

Thus, this meta-analysis aims to provide a comprehensive global overview of resistance patterns to cell wall–targeting antibiotics among S. pneumoniae strains causing invasive disease in pediatric patients (0–18 years). Particular attention is given to trends observed in both high-income and low-resource regions.

Methods

he Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines are followed in the reporting of this review [23]. This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration ID CRD42024589444. The review protocol was developed in accordance with PRISMA guidelines to ensure methodological rigor and transparency.

Eligibility criteria

The meta-analysis included studies that met the following inclusion criteria: (1) publication in English; (2) Study population consisting of pediatric patients (0–18 years); (3) reporting of the total number of S. pneumoniae isolates; (4) provision of specific data on resistance to cell wall-inhibiting antibiotics among isolates causing IPD, including bacteremia/septicaemia, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, pneumonia, and meningitis; and (5) isolation of S. pneumoniae from normally sterile body sites such as blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and pleural fluid. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adult patients aged ≥ 18 years; (2) studies not focused on IPD; (3) lack of specific antibiotic susceptibility data; (4) duplicate or overlapping data; and (5) studies classified as reviews, lectures, editorials, cohort studies, pharmacokinetic studies, or conference abstracts.

Search strategy and study selection

We conducted a comprehensive and systematic search of multiple databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Embase, up to July 25, 2022. Our search strategy utilized specific keywords such as: “Streptococcus pneumoniae”, “S. pneumoniae”, “invasive pneumococcal disease”, “childhood pneumococcal disease”, “children” OR “school children” OR “0–18 years”, “antibiotic resistance”, “antimicrobial resistance”, “cell wall-inhibitors”, “beta-lactam antibiotics”, “penicillins”, “cephalosporins”, “monobactams”, “carbapenems”, and “glycopeptides”, focusing on the Title, Abstract, and Keywords fields. No restrictions were applied during the database search. The search strategy was designed and implemented by the study investigators. In addition, we carefully reviewed the reference lists of all included studies to identify any potentially relevant publications that may have been missed. To ensure data accuracy, all records retrieved from the databases were combined and duplicates were removed using EndNote X9 (Thomson Reuters, New York, NY, USA). As a quality control measure, one researcher independently assessed a random sample of the search results to confirm that no relevant studies were overlooked.

Data extraction

Two independent researchers (LBA and MAN) extracted the data, and a third researcher (KHA) verified its accuracy. Extracted information included: author, publication year, country, pediatric patients (0–18 years), number of S. pneumoniae isolates, antibiotic resistance rates, infection source (bacteremia/septicaemia, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, pneumonia, meningitis, or mixed), and AST method (MIC-based or disk diffusion). In our initial search, we included various keywords such as carbapenems. However, during the review process, no relevant studies were identified, or data related to these antibiotics were unavailable.

This review focused on peer-reviewed, published studies and did not include unpublished data or conference abstracts. Although this approach enhanced methodological rigor and allowed for traceable data collection, it may have introduced publication bias and restricted the comprehensiveness of our findings, especially regarding emerging or region-specific resistance trends that are often first reported in conference abstracts or grey literature.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers independently evaluated the quality of the studies included in the analysis. They employed a modified version of the Newcastle-Ottawa assessment scale, which was adapted for cross-sectional studies (Supplementary File) [24]. Each study was assigned a score ranging from 0 to 8 points, with a score of 6 or higher indicating high quality, while a score of 5 or lower indicated low quality (Table 2). In cases where there was a disagreement between the two reviewers, a third reviewer was consulted to resolve the discrepancy.

Proportion of resistance in S. pneumoniae isolates

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis included studies that provided raw data on cell wall-inhibiting antibiotics resistance in clinical isolates of S. pneumoniae. The analysis was conducted using the meta-prop [25] command in the R statistical software, considering prevalence statistics based on antibiotic, region (continents/countries), year, infection source, and AST. The results of the meta-analysis consisted of a prevalence statistic accompanied by 95% confidence intervals, which were calculated using weighted prevalence statistics from all the studies within the specified sub-group. A random effects model was used to fit the data. The amount of heterogeneity (i.e., τ²) was estimated using the DerSimonian-Laird estimator [26]. In addition to the estimate of τ², the Q-test for heterogeneity [27] and the I² statistic [28] are also reported. Any heterogeneity was detected (i.e., τ²>0, regardless of the results of the Q-test). Meta-regression models were used to evaluate potential changes in antibiotic resistance over time. To assess publication bias, the Egger and Begg tests were performed. All statistical results were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 3.6.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [29].

Study outcomes

The primary objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of resistance to cell wall-inhibiting antibiotics among clinical isolates of S. pneumoniae. To further explore the data, subgroup analyses were conducted based on the following factors: (1) year of publication (1994–2015 vs. 2016–2022), (2) geographical location (continent or country), (3) age groups (< 5 years and > 5 to < 18 years), and (4) AST method.

Results

Systematic literature search

The initial search retrieved a total of 4,580 records. Following an initial screening of titles and abstracts, 4,430 articles were identified as irrelevant or duplicated and were excluded. The full texts of the remaining 150 articles were then carefully reviewed. Of these, 79 were excluded for the same reasons outlined above. Eventually, this systematic review and meta-analysis included 54 eligible studies [7, 30–78] (Table 1). The screening and selection process were summarized in the PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1).

Penicillin-non-susceptible pneumococci (PNSP)

A total of 18,249 clinical isolates from 51 studies were analyzed for PNSP. The pooled resistance rate was 30.1% (95% CI: 23.4–37.6), with substantial heterogeneity among studies (I² = 98.4%, P = 0.001) (Table 2; Fig. 2). To analyze the trends in PNSP rates by age groups (< 5yrs and > 5 to < 18yrs), we performed a subgroup analysis for changes in the proportion of PNSP isolates in age groups (Fig. 3). The proportion of PNSP in < 5 yrs and < 18 yrs age groups was 25.9% (95% CI 14.2–42.5) and 31.5% (95% CI 23.5–40.7), respectively (Table 2; P = 0.557). The proportion of PNSP increased from 25.2% (95% CI 17.7–34.5) of 13,471 strains in 1994–2015, reaching 36% (95% CI 26.1–47.4) of 4778 strains in 2016–2022 (Table 2; Fig. 3; p = 0.119). According to the meta-regression, the PNSP rate increased over time (r = 0.014; P = 0.606; Fig. 4). Among 22 countries reporting PNSP, 14 countries (US, France, China, Taiwan, Argentina, Iran, Spain, South Korea, Saudi Arabia, Mozambique, Algeria, Turkey, Bulgaria, and Morocco) reported that > 25% of isolates had PNSP (Fig. 3). A statistically significant difference in PNSP rates was observed between different countries (P < 0.001). The subgroup analysis revealed no statistically significant discrepancy in the proportion of PNSP among various continents (P = 0.090). The highest recorded proportion of PNSP was found in Africa, with a rate of 56.6%, while Europe reported the lowest proportion, reaching 17.8% (Fig. 3). No significant difference was found in the AST methods used (P = 0.296).

Amoxicillin

A total of 1,539 clinical isolates examined across five studies were included in the analysis of amoxicillin resistance. The average resistance proportion, estimated using a random-effects model, was 1.2% (95% CI: 0.2–8.8), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 83.59%, P = 0.001) (Table 2; Fig. 2). The proportions of amoxicillin resistance in the under-5-years (< 5 yrs) and under-18-years (< 18 yrs) age groups were 0.9% (95% CI: 0.1–12.5) and 1.3% (95% CI: 0.1–12.8), respectively (Fig. 3; Table 2; P = 0.901). The proportion of amoxicillin resistance increased from 0.6% (95% CI: 0.1–6.0) among 1,401 strains isolated during 1994–2015 to 3.5% (95% CI: 0.2–36.7) among 138 strains isolated during 2016–2022 (Table 2; Fig. 3; P = 0.329). According to the meta-regression analysis, the amoxicillin resistance rate did not show a statistically significant increase over time (r = 0.075; P = 0.613). There was no statistically significant difference in amoxicillin resistance rates between countries (P = 0.433). Subgroup analysis also revealed no significant variation in resistance proportions across different continents (P = 0.329; Fig. 3). However, a statistically significant difference was found based on the AST methods used (P = 0.017).

Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (co-amoxiclav)

A total of 930 clinical isolates from 8 studies were included in the analysis of amoxicillin-clavulanic acid resistance. The estimated average proportion of resistance, based on a random-effects model, was 2.5% (95% CI: 0.1–6.3), with moderate heterogeneity observed between the included studies (I² = 65.53%, P = 0.005) (Table 2; Fig. 2). The proportions of amoxicillin-clavulanic acid resistance in the < 5 years and < 18 years age groups were 2.5% (95% CI: 1.0–6.3) and 4.1% (95% CI: 0.4–31.1), respectively (Fig. 3; Table 2; P = 0.505). Resistance increased from 2.2% (95% CI: 0.8–5.6) among 798 isolates collected during 1994–2015 to 3.3% (95% CI: 0.1–53.5) among 132 isolates from 2016 to 2022 (Table 2; Fig. 3; P = 0.447). Meta-regression analysis indicated no statistically significant increase in resistance over time (r = 0.109; P = 0.384). No statistically significant differences in amoxicillin-clavulanic acid resistance rates were observed between countries (P = 0.696) or across continents (P = 0.856) (Fig. 3). The highest resistance proportion was reported in the Americas (3.4%), while Europe had the lowest rate (0.9%). Additionally, no significant difference in resistance estimates was found based on the AST methods used (P = 0.933).

Ceftriaxone

A total of 2,442 clinical isolates from 24 studies were included in the analysis of ceftriaxone resistance. The estimated average proportion of resistance, based on a random-effects model, was 6.1% (95% CI: 3.9–9.4), with substantial heterogeneity observed between studies (I² = 84.78%, P < 0.001) (Table 2; Fig. 2). The proportions of ceftriaxone resistance in the < 5 years and < 18 years age groups were 7.0% (95% CI: 4.2–11.4) and 4.2% (95% CI: 1.9–9.3), respectively (Fig. 3; Table 2; P = 0.294). There was no significant change in resistance over time: the proportion remained stable at 6.1% (95% CI: 3.7–9.5) among 813 isolates collected during 1994–2015, compared to 6.4% (95% CI: 4.1–9.9) among 1,629 isolates collected during 2016–2022 (Table 2; Fig. 3; P = 0.729). Among 14 countries reporting ceftriaxone resistance data, five (the United States, Brazil, China, India, and Turkey) reported resistance rates exceeding 5% (Fig. 3). However, there was no statistically significant difference in resistance rates between countries (P = 0.452). Subgroup analysis also revealed no statistically significant differences in ceftriaxone resistance proportions across continents (P = 0.341). The highest resistance proportion was observed in Asia (7.6%), while Europe had the lowest rate (1.7%) (Fig. 3). No significant difference in resistance estimates was found based on the antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) method used (P = 0.50).

Vancomycin

A total of 6405 clinical isolates that investigated in 13 studies were included in the analysis of vancomycin resistance. The estimated average proportion based on the random-effects model was 0.6% (95% CI, 0.3, 1.2) was observed between included studies (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Publication bias

Funnel plots showed asymmetry in several cases, indicating possible publication bias in the pooled estimates. To quantify and adjust for this bias, the trim-and-fill method was employed across all antibiotic categories. The analysis identified that, for most antibiotics, accounting for missing or unpublished studies through the trim-and-fill method resulted in significant adjustments to the overall effect estimates. Thus, publication bias might have affected the results. However, for amoxicillin, the trim-and-fill analysis did not significantly alter the summary estimate (P = 0.239), suggesting a lower likelihood of bias affecting the findings for this antibiotic. Detailed results of these assessments, including the number of studies potentially missing and the adjusted effect sizes, are provided in the Supplementary File.

Implications of the findings and clinical significance

Based on our meta-analysis, the emergence of AMR in S. pneumoniae is likely to continue rising, particularly in LMICs, where high infection burdens, unregulated antibiotic use, limited surveillance, and frequent treatment failures drive resistance. S. pneumoniae’s capacity to acquire and develop resistance mechanisms further exacerbates this problem. The increasing AMR not only elevates the incidence of invasive pneumococcal disease but also leads to more severe complications, posing significant public health challenges. Clinicians must stay informed about current diagnostic guidelines, recommended treatment regimens, and options for managing multidrug-resistant strains. Regular antimicrobial susceptibility testing is essential to guide therapy and evaluate the impact of AMR on treatment efficacy. In most countries, penicillin—or amoxicillin, due to its reliable oral absorption and longer half-life—remains first-line therapy for susceptible infections; however, rising resistance, especially in LMICs, complicates clinical management. These findings underscore that pneumococcal infections remain a serious global health threat, with regional variations necessitating tailored interventions and stewardship strategies.

Discussion

In 2017, the World Health Organization listed S. pneumoniae among its top 12 priority pathogens due to the growing threat of antibiotic resistance and the substantial global burden of pneumococcal disease [79]. Despite the introduction of pneumococcal vaccines, the overuse and misuse of antibiotics persist, further fueling the development of resistant strains [80]. Therefore, it is essential to gather comprehensive data on antibiotic resistance patterns in clinical isolates of S. pneumoniae. This systematic review and meta-analysis examine the global prevalence of resistance to antibiotics that inhibit cell wall synthesis in S. pneumoniae, covering studies published between 1994 and 2022. The analysis provides a comprehensive overview of resistance trends across different regions, offering critical insights into the evolving challenge of antimicrobial resistance in pneumococcal infections. In general, penicillin has historically been the preferred treatment for pneumococcal infections [9].

Overall, our systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that the global prevalence of PNSP among clinical isolates is 30.1%. Among the 23 countries included in the analysis, PNSP rates varied substantially, with the highest prevalence observed in Mozambique (88%) and the lowest in the Philippines (4.6%). Regionally, the analysis revealed Africa as the continent with the highest PNSP prevalence at 56.6%, in contrast to Europe, which had the lowest rate at 17.8%.

Africa faces a critical antibiotic resistance crisis with a prevalence rate of 56.6%. Immediate coordinated action is needed, including establishing regional surveillance systems and expanding PCV13 vaccine coverage for vulnerable populations. We must implement strict antibiotic stewardship programs combined with comprehensive prescriber education and accelerate development of novel treatments through targeted research investment. Strengthening diagnostic capacity through enhanced laboratory networks remains equally vital. Success ultimately depends on fostering strong international partnerships that provide both funding and technical support. This multi-pronged approach demands urgent implementation to avert what could become a continent-wide public health catastrophe, requiring commitment from governments, healthcare providers, researchers, and global health organizations working in unison. The window for effective intervention is narrowing, making immediate action not just advisable but essential for preventing irreversible damage to Africa’s healthcare infrastructure and population health.

The emergence of resistance in PNSP is often associated with serotypes that are frequently implicated in clinical infections [81] Among the various serotypes and serogroups of S. pneumoniae, certain types—such as serotype 14 and serogroups 6, 19, and 23—are more commonly linked to higher rates of antibiotic resistance [79]. Geographical variations in the prevalence of PNSP can be influenced by regional differences in vaccine implementation and antibiotic use policies, which in turn affect the epidemiology and distribution of resistant isolates [82]. Our time trend analysis revealed a global increase in the prevalence of PNSP from 1994 to 2022, underscoring the need for effective antimicrobial stewardship strategies to mitigate further development of resistance. The primary mechanism of penicillin resistance in S. pneumoniae involves structural modifications in PBPs, particularly PBPs 1 A, 2X, and 2B. Other beta-lactam antibiotics, such as cephalosporins and carbapenems, are less affected by these changes and are therefore more commonly used in the treatment of infections caused by PNSP [9].

According to our analyses, the overall global prevalence of amoxicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae clinical isolates was 1.2%. Among the four countries included in the study, China had the highest prevalence of amoxicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae at 3.5%, while Germany reported the lowest rate at 0.1%.

Time trend analysis revealed an increase in amoxicillin resistance among S. pneumoniae clinical isolates, rising from 0.6% in 1994 to 3.5% in 2022. Amoxicillin, a semisynthetic penicillin, is commonly used in the treatment of respiratory tract infections caused by S. pneumoniae, including those caused by penicillin-resistant strains [83]. Amoxicillin can be used alone or in combination with clavulanic acid (a β-lactamase inhibitor). The mutations in Pbp1a, Pbp2b, Pbp2x, and MurM are responsible to the emergence of amoxicillin resistance in S. pneumoniae isolates [84]. The reduced susceptibility to amoxicillin is a major concern because it is the main antibiotic used by clinicians [85].

Epidemiological studies are essential for monitoring and detecting amoxicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae isolates globally. In our systematic review, the overall prevalence of amoxicillin-clavulanate-resistant S. pneumoniae was 2.5%. Among the five countries included in the analysis, Taiwan had the lowest prevalence of resistance at 0.2%. The Americas reported the highest prevalence at 3.4%, while Europe had the lowest at 0.9%. The use of combination therapy, such as amoxicillin-clavulanate, has several drawbacks. One major concern is the potential acceleration of antibiotic resistance development due to increased selective pressure. This can lead to higher consumption of alternative antibiotics, including cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and carbapenems. Additionally, clavulanic acid, a component of the combination, is associated with side effects such as diarrhea, which can complicate patient management [86–88].

Resistance to amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (2.5%) was slightly higher than to amoxicillin alone (1.2%), likely due to differences in AST methods, local prescribing habits, or specific resistance mechanisms in S. pneumoniae that affect beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations. Despite this small difference, resistance to both antibiotics remains very low globally, reinforcing their continued use as first-line therapy for susceptible pediatric infections. These findings highlight the importance of ongoing regional surveillance and antimicrobial stewardship to detect and mitigate emerging resistance trends.

Across 14 countries, ceftriaxone resistance among S. pneumoniae isolates was 6.1%. The United States had the highest prevalence of ceftriaxone resistance at 14.6%, while Iran reported the lowest at 0.7%. When analyzing resistance by continent, Asian countries showed the highest prevalence of ceftriaxone-resistant isolates at 7.6%, whereas European countries had the lowest rate at 1.7%. Despite increasing resistance trends, ceftriaxone remains a commonly recommended antibiotic for the treatment of IPD, emphasizing the need for continued surveillance and appropriate use to preserve its therapeutic efficacy [89]. The most significant risk factor for ceftriaxone resistance was infection with S. pneumoniae serotypes 6 A, 15B, 19 A, 19 F, or 23 F [90]. Notably, the majority of ceftriaxone-resistant isolates were found to belong to serotypes included in the 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13) [91, 92].

Due to the limited data available for certain antibiotics, including amoxicillin and vancomycin, sample sizes for these agents were small. Thus, findings on their effectiveness and resistance should be interpreted cautiously, and generalizations should be made carefully. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings.

Vancomycin resistance in S. pneumoniae is rare but may involve modifications of cell wall precursors that reduce drug binding or acquisition of resistance genes [93]. Monitoring these potential mechanisms is essential to track and prevent emerging resistance.

Compared to previous meta-analyses, such as Andrejko et al. (2021) [94], which primarily focused on broad resistance trends across global populations, our study specifically examines resistance to cell wall synthesis inhibitors, offering a more targeted insight into antimicrobial resistance patterns. Additionally, this study incorporates the most recent data up to 2022, providing an up-to-date picture of resistance trends. Unlike earlier reviews that often aggregate data from adult populations or older studies, our analysis emphasizes pediatric populations and explores resistance across different clinical specimens (e.g., blood, respiratory, meningitis). Furthermore, through regional and temporal stratification, we identify specific areas—such as parts of Asia and Africa—where resistance is rising, information that has been less emphasized in prior literature. These advancements make our study a critical resource for shaping current treatment guidelines and public health strategies to combat pneumococcal resistance more effectively.

Clinicians should note that S. pneumoniae resistance patterns vary by region and over time, especially for ceftriaxone and penicillin. These differences might be due to various factors, such as how labs perform susceptibility testing (like disk diffusion versus MIC methods), regional prescribing habits, or changes happening over the years. It’s important to keep these potential sources of variation in mind when looking at the data.

Because of this variability, choosing the right antibiotic and performing susceptibility testing are more important than ever to ensure effective treatment. Doctors should stay informed about local and regional resistance trends and be ready to adjust their treatment plans accordingly. It’s also helpful to do sensitivity analyses to see how outliers or specific studies might influence overall results. Regularly updating and sharing information on resistance patterns worldwide can truly help guide better treatment decisions and improve patient outcomes.

This study has limitations. Heterogeneity in study design, laboratory methods, and geographic distribution may introduce bias and complicate comparisons. Since our analysis is based on previously published data, it might not fully capture the current global resistance patterns. Additionally, incomplete reporting—such as missing age-specific resistance data or detailed information on susceptibility testing procedures—could impact the robustness of our findings. To address the high heterogeneity observed among the included studies, we performed sensitivity analyses, such as excluding outliers, to evaluate the robustness of our findings. Additionally, we systematically discussed potential sources of variability—such as differences in study design, laboratory methods, and geographic regions—in the results section.

Another limitation of this study is the inclusion of studies published exclusively in English, which may have introduced language-related selection bias and potentially excluded relevant regional data available in other languages. Additionally, our reliance on published literature may have led to an underrepresentation of unpublished resistance trends from certain geographic areas, particularly those with limited access to international publication platforms. Variability in laboratory methods, like MIC testing versus disk diffusion, could also affect the resistance rates reported across studies.

Given these limitations, it’s important to support the development of region-specific antibiotic guidelines based on local resistance patterns. Promoting the use of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) can help reduce the prevalence of resistant serotypes. Lastly, investing in rapid diagnostic tools can help clinicians swiftly identify resistance and tailor treatments more effectively.

We acknowledge that our exclusion of studies involving mixed-age populations may limit the generalizability of our findings. While this decision was made to ensure pediatric specificity and minimize bias, some studies may contain disaggregated pediatric data that could have been included. Future systematic reviews may benefit from adopting a more granular approach to extract and analyze pediatric subgroups from mixed-population studies when feasible.

Conclusions

In conclusion, although resistance to amoxicillin, amoxicillin–clavulanate, and ceftriaxone remains relatively low overall, PNSP continues to pose a significant global healthcare challenge. Our findings reveal considerable regional variability in resistance, with Europe generally showing low rates, whereas higher prevalence is observed in parts of Africa and Asia. Important research gaps remain, including limited longitudinal surveillance in many LMICs, insufficient data on resistance trends in children aged 5–18 years, and scarce information on emerging mechanisms of resistance to cell wall-inhibiting antibiotics. Future studies should prioritize standardized, region-specific antimicrobial susceptibility monitoring, evaluation of treatment outcomes in resistant infections, and assessment of the impact of vaccination and antibiotic stewardship programs on resistance trends. Addressing these gaps will enhance empirical therapy decisions, inform both global and local public health strategies, and optimize the management of pediatric S. pneumoniae infections.
