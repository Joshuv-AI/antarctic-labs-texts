Experimental study on the compression and shear deformation evolution of coal pillar dam samples
Authors: Yan Wang, Haoran Liu, Shuo Feng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
To understand the mechanical properties and deformation failure patterns of coal pillar dams in gently inclined coal seam mine underground reservoirs, compression-shear coupling tests with variable angles were conducted on coal pillar dam samples under different saturation states. The digital image correlation (DIC) technique was utilized to obtain the deformation patterns of the displacement field throughout the compression-shear failure process of the samples. Numerical simulations were used to perform a comparative analysis and validation of the experimental results. The results indicated that the load-bearing capacity of the coal pillar dam samples decreased by 12.8–17.4% under water-saturated conditions. For every 5° increase in the inclination angle, the load-bearing capacity of the coal pillar dam samples decreased by an average of 3.53%. The normal stress and shear stress on the shear surface of the saturated samples were both lower than those of the natural samples, and both exhibited a linear variation with increasing inclination angle. The deformation evolution of the coal pillar dam samples under different test conditions exhibited similarity. The initial positive and negative displacement extremities in the horizontal direction were located at the lower right and upper left corners of the samples, respectively. Ultimately, the displacement field of the samples showed a pattern of smaller displacements at the top and larger displacements at the bottom, with negative values at the top and positive values at the bottom, distributed along the direction of the sample’s bedding. The saturated state and the increase in inclination both had a weakening effect on the load-bearing performance of the samples but had a relatively small impact on the differences in the displacement field deformation patterns. The research results can provide a reference for the study of the stability of coal pillar dams under similar conditions.

Introduction

China is currently the largest coal consumer and producer in the world1. With the rapid development of the country’s economy, the demand for coal remains high. As coal resources in the eastern regions are exhausted, the main coal mining areas are shifting to the central and western regions of China2. The ecologically fragile areas in the western region are located in arid-water deficient and semi-arid-low water zones, with water resources accounting for only 5.96% of the national total and high surface evaporation rates3,4. Due to the shallow burial depth of coal seams, large coal seam thickness, and thin overlying bedrock in the central and western regions, coal mining inevitably connects with aquifers, causing significant underground water loss5. After coal mining, mine water is discharged to the surface, exacerbating the waste of water resources6. In response to these issues, a new concept was proposed to address the evaporation loss of water resources discharged from coal mining in western mining areas of China7,8. This concept involves constructing underground reservoirs in goaf areas to store and use mine water underground, representing a water resource protection technology characterized by “drainage.” The goaf areas formed after coal mining were used as water storage carriers, as shown in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the structure of coal mine underground reservoir.

Schematic diagram of the structure of coal mine underground reservoir.

The coal pillar dam body was designed and modified from the protective coal pillars left by the original longwall mining method. It was connected with the artificial dam body to form the dam structure of the underground coal mine reservoir, serving as a crucial component of the underground reservoir9,10. As a typical sedimentary rock layer, coal contains natural bedding planes and structural surfaces. During its service period, the coal pillar dam was subjected to overburden pressure, mining-induced stress, water pressure, mine seismic activity, and the immersion effects from reservoir water storage11,12. Therefore, the stability of the coal pillar dam under the complex stress state of hydraulic coupling was crucial for the operation of the underground reservoir and the safety of mine production13–15.

Many studies were conducted by scholars focusing on the stability of coal pillar dams in coal mine underground reservoirs under complex coupling conditions. The cumulative damage patterns of the samples under cyclic loading and unloading and different immersion times were studied through laboratory experiments by Wang et al.16. The progressive damage characteristics of the coal pillar dam under the influence of mining and water storage were proposed. The seepage-stress coupling mechanism of underground reservoirs was studied using computational fluid dynamics by Zhang et al. and Wang et al.17,18. Numerical simulations were performed to investigate the mechanical properties and seepage behavior of coal pillar dams, considering anisotropy. The long-term sealing performance of coal pillar dams under different operating conditions was obtained, providing theoretical support for evaluating the seepage stability of coal pillar dams in underground reservoirs. Chen et al.19 studied the reasonable design dimensions and influencing factors of coal pillar dams. A theoretical formula for calculating the width of the reservoir dam body was proposed, highlighting that mining height and coal pillar parameters were important influencing factors. The stability of coal pillar dam bodies in distributed underground reservoirs under different seismic intensity levels was studied using similar model tests by Zhang et al.20. The reasonable safety distance between the upper and lower reservoirs was determined, providing a scientific reference for the safe design of coal pillar dams. Chi et al.21 comprehensively analyzed the stress changes, fracture development and propagation, and displacement variations of coal pillar dams under roof rock pressure and water storage height using mechanical models, similar simulations, and numerical calculation methods. The precursory information of coal pillar dam failure was obtained, providing a methodological basis for the safety and stability monitoring and early warning of coal pillar dams in coal mine underground reservoirs. Yao et al.22–25 conducted a detailed study on the failure patterns of coal pillar dam samples under different water immersion conditions. They used a series of methods including uniaxial compression tests, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and acoustic emission (AE) to obtain the strength, microstructure, fracture propagation patterns, and failure characteristics of coal pillar dam samples under water immersion. Wang et al.26 conducted triaxial compression tests on coal pillar dams under repeated water immersion and proposed the deterioration characteristics of the mechanical properties of coal pillar samples as well as the water-rock interaction mechanism. Qian et al.27 performed uniaxial compression tests on coal pillar samples with different fracture angles and water contents, and used AE analysis to study the effects of fracture angles and water content on the mechanical properties and fracturing behavior of coal pillar samples. Regarding compression-shear coupling tests, He et al.9, Chen et al.28, and Wang et al.29 applied compression-shear coupling loads to coal pillar dam samples (cylindrical samples) with different water contents using AE and inclined uniaxial compression tests. The relationships between the inclination angle, water content, crack initiation and closure, and the peak stress that the coal samples could withstand were studied. Yao et al.30 conducted variable-angle shear compression-shear coupling tests to study the mechanisms of crack propagation and strength weakening in coal pillar dam samples (cubic samples). Infrared thermography was used to analyze the effects of different water contents on the heat variation patterns during sample failure. Tang et al.11 also conducted compression-shear coupling tests on cubic coal pillar dam samples. A detailed study was carried out on the relationship between the physical and mechanical parameters of the samples and their water content, and the Mohr-Coulomb constitutive model was modified for water-saturated conditions. Most of the existing studies are based on methods such as theoretical modeling on an engineering scale, numerical analysis, and similar model tests, focusing on the macro-level stability of coal pillar dam structures. The experimental studies that have been conducted primarily concentrate on the failure patterns and crack propagation patterns, with the inclination angles studied being horizontal. Currently, there are no engineering examples of underground reservoirs in coal mines within gently inclined coal seams. Engineering trials have only been conducted at the Lingxin Coal Mine in China. Therefore, experimental research on the stability of coal pillar dam structures in underground reservoirs in gently inclined coal seams is of practical significance. Understanding the displacement variation of coal pillar dam structures under these specific compression-shear coupling conditions provides valuable engineering insights for maintaining their stability.

Digital image correlation (DIC), as an advanced optical measurement technique, has been widely applied in the study of rock deformation, displacement field evolution, and jointed rock mass fractures. Munoz et al.31 proposed a non-contact optical method using 3D DIC technology for strain measurement in uniaxial compression. A series of uniaxial compression tests under quasi-static loading conditions were conducted on sandstone samples to study the strain characteristics of sandstone before and after the uniaxial compression peak. Asem et al.32 used DIC and AE to conduct three-point bending tests to observe I-shape fractures in a series of geometrically similar notched rectangular beams made of metaquartzite. The study provided insights into the crack propagation patterns and fracture characteristics of metaquartzite. Li et al.33 conducted impact tests on coal samples using a split Hopkinson pressure bar. DIC was utilized to analyze the evolution behavior of surface displacement in the coal samples, and the correlation between stress waves and displacement was explored based on multifractal theory. Shukla et al.34 used DIC to study the stress-strain behavior of coal samples from a depth of 100 m. The study found that compared to linear variable differential transformer (LVDT) and strain gauges, DIC exhibited higher sensitivity to deformation, providing better measurements of coal sample deformation during the tests. Li et al.35 employed fractal theory and DIC to study the pore and fracture characteristics of coal bodies with different impact tendencies. The differences in pores and fractures in coal were analyzed.

Most of the aforementioned studies focused on nearly horizontal coal seams with shallow burial depths. For coal seams under gently inclined conditions (8°~25°), the impact of high ground stress and shear force results in different stress characteristics, fracture development patterns, and seepage behavior of coal pillar dams under the combined effects of mining pressure and water pressure compared to shallow coal seams. Currently, there is no engineering experience for reference. Previous studies on compression-shear coupling tests mainly focused on the crack initiation and propagation patterns of coal pillar dam samples under load. However, the evolution patterns of the displacement field in samples under compression-shear coupling effects remain unclear. Therefore, clarifying the mechanical properties and deformation patterns of coal pillar dams under gently inclined compression-shear coupling effects, as well as understanding the mechanisms of instability and failure, is of great significance. In this study, the stability of coal pillar dam s in gently inclined coal mine underground reservoirs was addressed. Variable-angle shear compression-shear coupling tests were conducted on coal pillar dam samples under different water saturation conditions. The mechanical response, deformation characteristics, and displacement field evolution patterns of the coal pillar dam samples were analyzed. Numerical simulations were used to verify the experimental results. The research findings can provide a reference for stability analysis of coal pillar dams under similar conditions.

Compression-shear coupling test methodology

Sample Preparation

The coal samples for the test were selected from the Shendong mining area in China. According to the requirements of the Standard for Test Methods of Engineering Rock Mass and the methods and recommendations of ISRM, they were prepared into cubic samples with a side length of 70 mm36,37. The specific sample preparation process is as follows: (a) The obtained coal samples were cut into 70 mm × 70 mm × 70 mm cubes according to the standard procedure; (b) A portion of the samples was immediately sealed with plastic wrap after cutting to maintain the natural moisture content of the samples; (c) For comparison, another portion of the samples underwent saturation treatment using the immersion method, where the samples were immersed in water for a period of time, and then weighed. Saturation was considered achieved when the sample weight remained constant; (d) All samples were uniformly numbered. The sample preparation process is shown in Fig. 2. During preservation process, plastic foam was used to protect the samples from collision damage, preserving their original structure. This ensured the maximum integrity and consistency of the coal samples. A total of 18 coal pillar dam samples were used in this test, as shown in Fig. 3.

Experimental equipment and methods

During the operation of underground reservoirs, the side of the coal pillar dam facing the water was subjected to periodic immersion and dewatering due to drainage operations. The coal pillar dam was thus exposed to both saturated and natural water content states. Since the inclination range of gently inclined coal seams is between 8° and 25°, and the test clamps allows for angle adjustments in 5° increments, three test angles were selected: 10°, 15°, and 20°. This selection was made to balance experimental feasibility while closely replicating the actual service conditions of coal pillar dam structures. These angles were chosen to facilitate a comprehensive study of the mechanical behavior and deformation patterns of the coal pillar dam samples under compressive-shear loading in gently inclined conditions. Some samples were saturated to form a comparison group. To improve the accuracy of the experimental results and reduce their randomness, the experiment was repeated three times for each angle. The results of the three tests were then comprehensively analyzed. Based on the above plan, the 18 samples were divided into three groups according to the experimental inclination angles: A, B, and C. Within each major group, half of the samples were subjected to saturation treatment, while the other half remained untreated as natural samples. These were further divided into two subgroups: N (normal) and S (saturation). Each subgroup was then specifically numbered from 1 to 3. For example, a sample numbered C–S–1 indicates the first compression-shear failure test performed on a saturated sample at a 20° inclination. In this test, axial load was provided by a YA-300 pressure testing machine. The coal samples were secured using two L-shaped variable-angle shear clamps, with the short sides of the clamps perpendicular to the coal sample bedding planes. The samples were placed with the bedding direction parallel to the long edge of the clamp, meaning that the normal pressure was applied perpendicular to the bedding direction. The angle between the bedding direction and the horizontal direction closely matched the test inclination angle. By adjusting the loading angles of the clamps, loading at 10°, 15°, and 20° was achieved. During the test, the ratio of shear force to normal pressure was set to 0.176:1, 0.268:1, and 0.364:1. Although the shear stress on the coal samples gradually increased, it always remained less than the normal pressure. The samples exhibited partial shear failure, primarily manifesting as compressive failure. In this experiment, DIC technology was used to obtain the deformation contour maps of the samples. DIC, also known as digital speckle correlation method, compares two digital images before and after deformation to obtain data on changes and measure the actual displacement variations in the test area, thereby acquiring full-field deformation information38,39. As an advanced non-contact measurement technique, DIC provides full-field strain and displacement measurements without physical contact, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of material behavior across the entire surface. Unlike traditional point-based measurement systems, such as strain gauges, which only provide localized data, DIC can capture detailed deformation distributions with high spatial resolution, making it particularly suitable for detecting small-scale material deformations that are difficult to identify with other methods. When combined with the time dimension, DIC can also monitor displacement variations in real time, facilitating the study of dynamic processes during experiments. Since DIC technology reflects specific deformation information through changes in the speckle field of the sample, speckles of uniform size and density were created on the surface of the coal pillar samples to obtain distinct reference points in this experiment. Since the color of the coal sample was the same as the required black speckles, a layer of white primer was first sprayed evenly on the surface to be photographed. After the primer dried, a layer of black speckles was uniformly sprayed on the surface using black paint. After this treatment, the speckle field of the coal pillar dam sample appeared as shown in Fig. 4. The entire experiment was recorded using a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera. The camera’s shooting frequency was adjusted according to the different stages of the experiment, and the resolution of the captured images was 568 × 399. XTDIC software was used to post-process the experimental results and generate various contour maps based on inherent algorithms. Additionally, since DIC photography requires a stable and bright light source, two 2500 W LED lamps were used to provide illumination to ensure the quality of the images. The test and monitoring system is shown in Fig. 5.

Effect of peak load

The peak load of each sample in the test reflected the load-bearing capacity of the samples under different conditions. The comparison of the average peak load of coal pillar samples in different water content states and at different inclination angles is shown in Fig. 6. The average axial peak load of saturated samples was lower than that of natural samples. Under large inclination conditions, the average peak load of the samples was lower than that of samples with the same saturation state under small inclination conditions. Both saturation and increased inclination weakened the compression-shear resistance of the coal pillar samples. The weakening effect caused by saturation was between 12.8% and 17.4%, while the weakening effect caused by inclination was between 3.4% and 7.3%.

Fig. 6Axial peak load of the samples.

Axial peak load of the samples.

As the test inclination increased, a decrease in average peak load was observed for both types of water content samples. The average peak load of natural samples in the 15° and 20° inclination tests decreased by 3.4% and 7.3%, respectively, compared to the 10° condition. For saturated samples, the average peak load in the 15° and 20° inclination tests decreased by 7.6% and 14.5%, respectively, compared to the 10° condition. The results indicated that an increase in the test inclination weakened the load-bearing capacity of the samples. As the test inclination increased, the proportion of shear force applied to the samples gradually increased. The samples transitioned from being subjected to compression to compression-shear combined action. The coal pillar samples had transverse bedding, and their shear resistance in the horizontal direction was weaker than their compressive resistance. With increased inclination, more shear failures occurred along the bedding planes, leading to a reduction in the average peak load of the samples. When designing underground reservoirs in gently inclined coal seams, it is necessary to adjust the geometry and dimensions of the coal pillar dam structures based on the seam’s inclination angle. A larger inclination angle increases the shear force, so the design should appropriately increase the width or height of the coal pillars to compensate for the reduction in mechanical performance caused by the steeper angle. In seams with a greater inclination, a multi-stage dam structure can be considered to distribute the dam pressure more evenly, thereby reducing the risk of shear failure in individual coal pillars.

By comparing the average peak loads of samples with different saturation states at the same angles, it was found that water saturation reduced the load-bearing capacity of the samples. At the same angles, the average peak load of saturated samples decreased by 12.9%, 14.3%, and 17.4% compared to natural samples. The main reason for this phenomenon is that coal, as a typical sedimentary rock, usually contains clay minerals that easily expand and disintegrate when exposed to water. By using direct immersion to simulate the water injection in underground reservoirs, the interaction between the coal pillar dam body and water was replicated. Water infiltrated through the surface cracks of the sample, wetting all its free surfaces and mineral particles30. Hydrophilic or soluble substances within the coal sample were dissolved, and the introduction of water molecules altered the internal physical state of the sample, weakening or disrupting certain inter-particle bonds. Consequently, the mechanical properties of the coal sample were altered, leading to a reduction in its load-bearing capacity40. Therefore, when designing underground reservoirs, it is essential to account for the weakening effect of prolonged water saturation on the mechanical properties of the coal pillar dam structures. To ensure the stability of the dam, it may be necessary to increase the size of the coal pillars or implement additional reinforcement measures, such as combining artificial dam structures with coal pillars, applying material improvements, or employing waterproofing treatments. These methods aim to enhance the overall stability of the structure.

Load–displacement variation patterns

The typical load-displacement curves for each group under different conditions are shown in Fig. 7. The coal pillar samples under six different test conditions all exhibited distinct brittle characteristics, with the load-displacement curve rapidly dropping after reaching the peak value. The shapes of the curves in each group exhibited certain similarities, with only localized differences. All curves could generally be divided into four stages: compaction stage (ab), elastic stage (bc), plastic failure development stage (cd), and post-failure stage (de).

Fig. 7Load–displacement curve.

Load–displacement curve.

In the compaction stage (ab), the load-displacement curve exhibited an upward bending growth, with the curve slope initially increasing slowly and then sharply. The natural fractures and pores within the coal pillar samples were compressed and closed, and the water stored in the fractures and pores of the saturated samples was expelled41. In the elastic stage (bc), the load-displacement relationship was nearly linear. The internal fractures of the samples were completely closed, and water within the pores was expelled. At the beginning of this stage, the sample surfaces were generally intact with no significant new cracks. As the load continued to be applied, the original cracks in the samples began to develop, and new cracks gradually appeared. Due to the resistance to shear stress, cracking sounds were heard between the bedding planes of the samples. Most new cracks occurred at the ends of the samples near the clamps on the left and right sides. In the plastic failure development stage (cd), as the load increased, surface cracks on the samples continued to develop and expand, accompanied by cracking sounds and spalling. Internal cracks in the samples further expanded and interconnected. The load-displacement curves during this stage exhibited some differences with changes in the test inclination angle. When the inclination angle was small, the curve showed no significant fluctuations near the peak and appeared as a single-peak curve. As the inclination angle increased, one or more minor peaks appeared before reaching the maximum peak value, resulting in a multi-peak curve. The test results for the saturated sample under the 15° inclination condition (B–S) are shown in Fig. 8. It was found that certain internal structures or structural planes within the sample were first sheared, which was reflected in the graph as a decrease in load-bearing capacity. However, as the load continued to be applied, the damaged areas were recompressed, and the load-bearing capacity was restored. When the inclination angle increased, the proportion of shear force on the sample gradually increased, making the bedding planes more prone to slippage and failure, resulting in a decrease in load-bearing capacity and causing fluctuations in the curve. In the post-peak failure stage (de), due to the occurrence of macroscopic fractures and the accumulation of previous compression-shear damage, the sample suddenly failed at the peak, resulting in significant spalling on the surface, accompanied by pronounced bursting sounds and flying debris. The curve exhibited a sharp drop, and the sample lost its load-bearing capacity.

Displacement field evolution patterns

In this test, DIC technology was used to output displacement in the u and v directions, representing the horizontal and vertical displacements, respectively. The horizontal displacement contour maps at the points of maximum deformation for natural and saturated samples at different inclinations during the initial stage (stage I), middle stage (stage II), and final stage (stage III) of the test are shown in Figs. 10 and 11. Observing the displacement field contour maps reveals that the displacements of the samples at various stages and under different conditions exhibited significant non-uniform distribution.

By comparing the initial displacement contour maps at different inclinations, it was found that the maximum horizontal displacement for the samples under all three inclination conditions appeared in the lower right corner and gradually decreased towards the upper left corner. At 10°, the initial maximum horizontal displacement was more concentrated in the lower right corner, appearing as a clustered region. Under the conditions of 15° and 20°, the initial maximum horizontal displacement formed a belt-like region primarily extending from the lower right corner towards the lower left corner. The larger the test inclination, the more the shape of the initial maximum horizontal displacement tended to be band-like. This phenomenon and pattern were observed in the displacement field contour maps of both natural and saturated samples. The horizontal displacement contour maps from the middle stage of the test indicated that, under different inclinations, the maximum horizontal displacement was consistently located in the bottom region, forming a band-like shape extending from the lower right corner to the lower left corner. This developmental trend was most evident in the test with a 20° inclination. The overall horizontal displacement field began to form a distribution with smaller displacements at the top and larger displacements at the bottom. This phenomenon was observed in both natural and saturated samples. By comparing the contour maps at the point of maximum horizontal deformation at the end of the test, it was found that the displacement field also exhibited a highly non-uniform characteristic at this stage. The color bands representing different displacement magnitudes were distributed in a pattern of smaller displacements at the top and larger displacements at the bottom, parallel to the bedding direction of the samples. Since deformation is often concentrated in the lower part of the coal pillar, special attention must be given to the instability risk in this region. The design should consider reinforcing the lower section of the coal pillar, such as using localized support or grouting reinforcement, to prevent excessive shear displacement that could lead to instability. Over time, the accumulation of deformation may result in the overall failure of the coal pillar. Therefore, during reservoir operation, it is crucial to regularly inspect the deformation of the coal pillars, particularly focusing on shear deformation in the lower part of the dam structure.

Additionally, damage was observed in the horizontal displacement fields under all test conditions. The locations of damage and loss in the displacement field contour maps differed between natural and saturated samples. For natural samples, the damage in the horizontal displacement contour maps mainly appeared at the left and right ends of the samples, where the short edges of the upper and lower clamps contacted the samples. In contrast, for saturated samples, the damage in the horizontal displacement contour maps appeared as scattered points on the surface, gradually expanding and connecting into larger areas. The damage and loss in the displacement field contour maps were caused by the spalling of the speckle field attached to the surface of the samples. By observing the locations of speckle field spalling, it was found that this spalling occurred due to failure at those locations caused by loading, resulting in surface structure cracking and coal debris falling off. Therefore, the locations of displacement field loss can indicate the initial failure points of the samples during the test. By analyzing the failure locations, it was found that for natural samples, the cracking locations were near the upper and lower left corner regions at smaller test angles. As the angle increased, the samples failed under shear stress along the shear angle set by the clamps, causing the edge areas of the middle region to fail first. The failure range of the saturated samples was significantly larger than that of the natural samples. The general pattern showed shear failure along the direction of shear stress on the lower side of the inclined direction, displaying similar patterns across all three angles. Since DIC captures the overall horizontal displacement of the entire experimental setup, rather than the relative displacement between the samples, the area with the largest displacement in the lower region of the sample’s displacement field contour map reflects the magnitude of the overall horizontal displacement. Additionally, vertical displacement exists within the experimental system, but this is not represented in the contour map. Additionally, it is worth noting that because the deformation of the samples during the test was minimal, low-adhesion paint was used to minimize the impact of paint adhesion on the deformation of the speckle field. Consequently, the spalling of the speckle field due to sample failure during the test could cause additional paint to peel off, resulting in an expanded area of missing displacement contour maps.

Numerical simulation of compression-shear coupling test

Numerical model construction

To verify the accuracy of the experiment and eliminate the randomness of the experimental data, numerical simulations were used to compare the data results and changes in displacement contour maps42. Rhino three-dimensional solid modeling software was used to recreate the three-dimensional structures of the test clamps and samples. After meshing, the model was imported into FLAC3 finite difference software to establish and perform numerical calculations. The dimensions of the computational model were kept consistent with the actual dimensions, with the sample having a length, width, and height of 70 mm. The Mohr–Coulomb model was used as the constitutive model for the samples. The axial test load was applied through the ends of the upper and lower L-shaped variable-angle shear clamp models. To reduce deformation of the clamps under load and minimize its impact on the simulation results, an elastomer constitutive model was used. The model parameters were set to approximate a rigid body. Displacement field evolution patterns

After numerical simulation calculations, the displacement field distributions under different inclination angles and water content conditions were obtained, as shown in Figs. 13 and 14. The simulation results indicated that the displacement fields of the samples under all conditions exhibited non-uniform distribution. The overall evolution trends were similar, and the inclination angle affected the severity of horizontal displacement evolution. Observations revealed that the positive and negative horizontal displacements in the contour maps were separated by the midpoint line of the left and right sides of the sample. The right direction was positive, and the left direction was negative. The upper part of the contour map mostly showed negative values, indicating movement in the negative horizontal direction, while the lower part mostly showed positive values, indicating movement in the positive horizontal direction.

The evolution process of the contour maps from the numerical simulations can be divided into three stages. In stage I, the maximum positive and negative horizontal displacements were distributed in the lower right and upper left corners. Under the 10° inclination condition, the color blocks representing the maximum displacement clustered in the lower right corner. In the 15° and 20° conditions, the maximum positive and negative horizontal displacements were primarily in the lower right and upper left corners, extending into band-like regions toward the lower left and upper right. The color bands representing other displacement magnitudes decreased from the upper left and lower right corners toward the center, diminishing to zero displacement in the middle. In stage II, observations focused on the lower half of the sample, separated by the midpoint line of the left and right sides. In the 10° simulation, the color cluster representing the maximum positive horizontal displacement extended toward the lower left corner and gradually became band-like. This development process was observed in the 15° and 20° simulations, with the degree of development becoming more pronounced as the simulation inclination increased. The evolution of the upper half was symmetrical to the lower half, but in the opposite direction. In stage III, taking the lower half of the sample as an example, the color band representing the maximum displacement value completely connected the lower left corner and the lower right corner, with the left side curling upwards near the contact point between the short edge of the left clamp and the sample. Overall, the displacement exhibited a pattern of negative at the top and positive at the bottom, with smaller values at the top and larger values at the bottom, distributed along the bedding direction of the sample. The horizontal displacement contour maps of the coal pillar dam samples obtained from the experiment and numerical simulation both exhibited very similar band-like distribution characteristics.

Stress field evolution patterns

Numerical calculation results of the stress distribution in the coal pillar dam samples were also obtained. Since the vertical stress distribution pattern was not distinct, the horizontal stress distribution patterns at different test angles were analyzed, as shown in Fig. 15. Under both saturation conditions, the final horizontal stress magnitude of the samples was influenced by the test inclination angle. An increase of 5° in the inclination angle resulted in a 15% and 11% increase in horizontal stress, respectively. The increase in stress for the saturated samples was smaller than that of the natural samples, indicating that the saturated state weakens the load-bearing capacity of the samples. In terms of stress distribution patterns, the horizontal stress distribution under both saturation conditions exhibited very similar characteristics. The areas of stress concentration were located at the upper left and lower right positions of the samples, corresponding to regions not covered by the clamp. Overall, the horizontal stress was symmetrically distributed along the line connecting the upper and lower corners of the samples.

Comparison of displacement field evolution and error optimization

By comparing the displacement contour maps obtained from the experiment and numerical simulation, it was found that no negative values existed in the horizontal displacement contour maps under all test conditions. This indicates that there was no horizontal negative displacement during the test. The testing machine applied a load to the sample in the vertical direction, which was decomposed into normal and tangential loads through the test clamps. The test results should exhibit both positive and negative displacement directions. Additionally, there was a discrepancy of an order of magnitude in the absolute deformation values. Since the numerical simulation represented an ideal test state and many objective conditions, including boundary conditions, were simplified, the comparative analysis between the numerical simulation results and the experimental results was more focused on the evolution patterns of the displacement field of the samples. The absolute numerical results of the test were provided for reference only.

After analyzing the original DIC images, it was concluded that the primary reason for the presence of only positive values in the DIC horizontal displacement contour maps was the vibration occurring during the loading process of the testing machine. The variable-angle compression-shear test clamps contained 11 circular rollers at the top and bottom to provide a channel for horizontal movement. During the loading process of the testing machine, the clamps moved simultaneously in both axial and horizontal directions, with the axial displacement being much greater than the horizontal displacement. The movement of the rollers caused the clamps to displace, thereby affecting the displacement results of the samples. During the test, whenever the clamps were about to compress the sample, slight vibrations occurred due to the fact that the surfaces of the sample and the clamps could not be perfectly parallel because of the processing and manufacturing precision. As a result, the surfaces did not fit together perfectly, and as the load continued to be applied, the clamps experienced slight vibrations to conform to the sample. At the same time, the angle of the clamps was adjusted and fixed using pins. The pin structure also experienced slight shaking and displacement under the load. The vibrations and displacements of the aforementioned instruments had a significant impact on the compression-shear failure test results, where displacement results were less than millimeter scale. This was reflected in the displacement contour maps, where only positive values were observed in the horizontal displacement contour maps.

To reduce instrument errors and optimize the disturbed data, the horizontal displacement data obtained from the test needed to be adjusted by subtracting the amount of instrument vibration displacement. By observing the numerical simulation results, it was found that there were regions with zero displacement in the displacement contour maps. Under ideal test conditions, the DIC displacement contour maps should also have a zero-displacement zone in the same location. The region where the zero-displacement zone should exist was identified in the DIC displacement contour maps. The current displacement value in this region represented the displacement error caused by the instrument. By subtracting this value from the DIC contour map displacement values, the instrument-induced error could be optimized, resulting in horizontal displacement values closer to the actual values. A numerical simulation horizontal displacement contour map was selected and imported into image processing software. The color representing zero displacement was selected from the legend using the color picker. By comparing colors, zero displacement points on the boundary of the sample in the contour map were identified. The ruler tool was used to measure the distance from the zero displacement point to the top vertex and determine its proportional relationship to the sample’s side length.A DIC horizontal displacement contour map under the same test conditions was selected. Using the previously determined proportional relationship, the zero displacement point on the boundary of the sample in the DIC horizontal displacement contour map was identified. The color picker was used to select the color of this point and compare it with the legend on the right side to find the corresponding displacement value. This value represented the displacement error caused by the instrument.The data range of the DIC displacement contour map was adjusted by subtracting the error value determined in the second step, resulting in a range of horizontal displacement values closer to the actual test values.

Conclusions

In this study, variable-angle compression-shear coupling experiments were conducted on coal pillar dam samples from underground reservoirs in gently inclined coal seams. A detailed analysis was performed on the mechanical response, deformation characteristics, and displacement field evolution of the coal pillar dam samples under different saturation conditions. The experimental results were validated using DIC technology and numerical simulation methods, which also helped to reveal key phenomena during the compression-shear failure process. The main findings of the research are summarized as follows:

The weakening effect of saturation on the mechanical properties of the coal pillar dam is significant. Experimental results showed that the axial peak load-bearing capacity of the coal pillar dam in a saturated state decreased by 12.8–17.4% compared to the natural state samples. Both the normal stress and shear stress on the shear plane of the saturated coal samples were significantly reduced, exhibiting a linear decrease as the inclination angle increased. This result highlights the weakening effect of water saturation on the internal structure of the coal pillar dam, emphasizing the need to fully consider the impact of water infiltration on the stability of the coal pillar dam during the operation of underground reservoirs.Increasing the inclination angle leads to a gradual decrease in the load-bearing capacity of the coal pillar dam. The experiments showed that, on average, the load-bearing capacity of the coal pillar dam decreased by 3.53% for every 5° increase in inclination. The increase in inclination resulted in a higher proportion of shear forces, making the coal pillar samples more prone to shear failure along the bedding plane. This finding provides important theoretical support for the design of coal pillar dams in gently inclined coal seams, particularly highlighting the need to optimize the coal pillar structure under larger inclination angles to enhance stability.Both saturated and natural samples exhibited a similar evolution pattern in their displacement fields under compression-shear loading, with a distribution characterized by smaller displacements at the top and larger displacements at the bottom. Although the inclination angle and saturation state reduced the load-bearing capacity of the coal pillars, their impact on the deformation pattern of the displacement field was relatively minor. The final displacement field showed a banded distribution along the bedding direction, further confirming the critical role of the bedding plane in shear failure. The horizontal stress distribution for both saturated and natural samples followed similar patterns as well. The displacement field obtained through DIC technology was highly consistent with the numerical simulation results, validating the reliability of the experiments.This study primarily focused on the effects of inclination angle and saturation state on the compression-shear coupling behavior of coal pillar dam structures. Future research could introduce more complex stress conditions, such as mine pressure and seismic loading, to evaluate the failure mechanisms of coal pillar dams under multiple coupled stress conditions. Additionally, by utilizing DIC technology, future work could develop optical and digital real-time monitoring systems for coal pillar dams to enable early warning of deformation and failure. This would provide a basis for dynamic adjustments in the operation and maintenance of underground reservoirs.
