Right ventricular–pulmonary arterial uncoupling and venous excess in normotensive pulmonary embolism shock
Authors: José Carlos Gasca-Aldama, Karen Harumi López-Rodríguez, Mario Arturo Carrasco-Flores, Juan Ángel Morales-Ferrer, Osvaldo Alexis Marché-Fernández, Marcos Vidals-Sánchez, José Ernesto Juárez-León, Mónica Libertad Moya-Alfaro, Eduardo Alberto González-Escudero
Published: 2026

Main text:

To the Editor:

We read with interest the article by Hunsicker et al. [1] regarding the clinical and echocardiographic recognition of normotensive shock (Category D2) in acute pulmonary embolism (PE), as introduced by the 2026 AHA/ACC guideline [2]. The authors outline a multi-parametric diagnostic framework to identify right ventricular (RV) dysfunction. We entirely agree with their emphasis on early recognition and hemodynamically driven stratification to guide reperfusion therapies. However, we believe that integrating retrograde venous hemodynamics and addressing the clinical limitations of forward-coupling surrogates would substantially strengthen this framework for clinical decision-making.

This quantitative progression is vital. Historically, the seminal study by Kucher et al. [3] on 1,035 hemodynamically stable PE patients (systolic blood pressure [SBP] ≥ 90 mmHg) established that qualitative, binary RV hypokinesis independently predicts 30-day mortality (Adjusted HR: 1.94; 95% CI: 1.23–3.06), with a substantial 16.7% mortality rate in this normotensive subgroup. While their work proved that normal SBP can mask impending cardiovascular collapse, this qualitative assessment was highly subjective. To overcome this, quantitative forward-coupling surrogates like the tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion to pulmonary artery systolic pressure ratio (TAPSE/PASP) are preferred. Originally validated against invasive single-beat pressure-volume loops (Ees/Ea) by Tello et al. [4], this ratio was utilized in the largest multicenter acute PE cohort to date (N = 4,478 from the RIETE registry), where Lyhne et al. [5] demonstrated that a decreased TAPSE/PASP ratio is independently associated with 30-day mortality, predicting short-term death across the entire spectrum of PE severity.

As highlighted by Brusca and Holtzman [6], integrating this ratio into clinical risk assessment significantly refines risk stratification, yielding a Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) of 27.7% over European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines. However, this forward surrogate is highly load dependent. In cases of severe or terminal RV failure, a paradoxical decline in PASP due to forward flow failure can alter the ratio’s linear relationship with coupling. Consequently, a single forward ratio cannot differentiate whether uncoupling is driven by severe right ventricular afterload mismatch or has already progressed to secondary ischemic myocardial impairment. Furthermore, a major clinical limitation of TAPSE/PASP is its dependence on an adequate tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV) signal. In the National Echocardiography Database of Australia (NEDA) substudy by Bowcock et al. [7]., TRV was unmeasurable or inadequate in 39.7% of critically ill patients, rendering the forward assessment unfeasible. To bypass the subjective estimation of right atrial pressure (RAP) when TRV is available, the simplified TAPSE/TRV ratio has been proposed as a robust afterload-normalized metric. Yet, both TAPSE/PASP and TAPSE/TRV share this dependence on a high-quality tricuspid regurgitant jet. When TRV cannot be measured, right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) Doppler metrics serve as alternative forward surrogates. Specifically, a pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAAT) < 100 ms and midsystolic notching reliably detect increased pulmonary vascular resistance and impaired RV-PA coupling without relying on tricuspid regurgitation [8].

Nonetheless, in the acute phase of PE, this forward surrogate possesses substantial prognostic utility. As synthesized by Yuriditsky [9], a TAPSE/PASP ratio < 0.34 mm/mmHg effectively identifies right ventricular-pulmonary arterial (RV-PA) uncoupling, predicting an invasively measured low cardiac index (< 2.2 L/min/m2) with 97% sensitivity and 91% specificity. Furthermore, this threshold independently predicts a significant hemodynamic ‘super-response’ (defined as a ≥ 25% increase in cardiac index) after mechanical thrombectomy with an odds ratio: 6.5.

To understand how acute PE alters systemic perfusion and organ blood flow when the forward acoustic window is limited, the classic venous capacitance model of Tyberg [10] must be integrated with RV-PA coupling. At baseline, venous return (VR) is driven by the transmural pressure gradient (ΔP) between the peripheral venous reservoir and the right ventricle (RV) in diastole: ΔP = VR×Rvenous, where Rvenous represents venous resistance. In acute PE, sudden mechanical pulmonary arterial obstruction increases right ventricular afterload (arterial elastance, Ea). This acute afterload mismatch disrupts the Tyberg venous-cardiac interaction: first, the RV fails to eject efficiently, causing stroke volume (SV) to decline and end-systolic volume (ESV) to accumulate; second, operating at higher volumes, the RV shifts to the steep portion of its passive end-diastolic pressure-volume relationship (EDPVR), causing a severe rise in right ventricular end-diastolic pressure (RVEDP) that retrogradely elevates right atrial pressure (RAP); and third, as RAP rises, the pressure gradient (ΔP) between systemic capacitance veins and the right heart narrows critically.

This right ventricular claudication relative to persistent capillary inflow generates a highly positive venous accumulation rate (VAR = VR−CO, where VR and CO represent venous return and cardiac output, respectively), leading to physical volume accumulation within the central veins. Originally conceptualized by Reddi and Carpenter [11], venous excess (VE) represents a dynamic hemodynamic error signal reflecting this cumulative mismatch (VR − CO). Correspondingly, Guinot [12] and Rola et al. [13] emphasize that the Venous Excess Ultrasound Score (VExUS) reflects the interaction between VR and cardiac function. Rather than indicating passive volume overload, this score represents the altered right-sided cardio-venous pressure-volume relationships modeled by Tyberg.

Indeed, in a prospective cohort of patients with acute PE, Gimena-Rodríguez et al. [14] demonstrated that pathological Venous Excess Ultrasound Score (VExUS) grades are strongly associated with disease severity. Specifically, in their intermediate-high risk group—the clinical equivalent of normotensive shock (Category D2)—64.7% exhibited altered hepatic vein flow (52.9% with a systolic-to-diastolic ratio S < D and 11.8% with a reversed S-wave), 23.6% had discontinuous renal vein flow, and the mean portal vein pulsatility index was 37.5%. Notably, portal pulsatility was the strongest independent predictor of PE severity (adjusted OR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.07–1.28). A VExUS score > 1 exhibited excellent diagnostic accuracy in identifying European Society of Cardiology (ESC) high-risk PE (the clinical equivalent of Category E; AUC: 0.94; 95% CI: 0.89–0.99; sensitivity: 100%; specificity: 85%).

As reviewed by Huard et al. [15], pulsed Doppler analysis of hepatic venous flow patterns directly reflects right ventricular diastolic function and constitutes a core component of the VExUS evaluation. Consequently, bedside hemodynamic assessment in acute PE can no longer rely on isolated forward metrics. We invite the critical care community to adopt a multimodal, two-sided hemodynamic paradigm specifically in high-risk patients classified under Categories D2 and E. By integrating forward surrogates of RV-PA coupling (such as TAPSE/PASP, TAPSE/TRV, or PAAT) with a backward evaluation of venous excess (VExUS), clinicians can comprehensively map right ventricular pressure-volume disruption (Fig. 1). As suggested by Hunsicker et al. [1] regarding early shock recognition, larger prospective trials are warranted to validate this dual-sided approach. Ultimately, decoding both forward uncoupling and backward venous excess at the bedside will refine clinical decision-making, guiding timely interventions—such as early mechanical thrombectomy, precise vasoactive sparing, or extracorporeal life support—to improve patient outcomes in these critical categories.
