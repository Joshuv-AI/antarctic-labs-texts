Mobile intervention for emerging adults with regular cannabis use: a micro-randomized trial
Authors: Maureen Walton, Inbal Nahum-Shani, Maya Campbell, Devin C Tomlinson, Autumn Rae Florimbio, Susobhan Ghosh, Yongyi Guo, Pei-Yao Hung, Mark W Newman, Jeremy J Lin, Tianchen Qian, John Dziak, Huijie Pan, Kelly W Zhang, Lauren Zimmermann, Erin E Bonar, Susan Murphy, Lara N Coughlin

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCannabis use is prevalent among emerging adults, with regular use increasing risk for problems; yet few seek treatment. Using a micro-randomized trial (MRT), the study aims were to test a personalizing just-in-time adaptive interventions (pJITAI), which is powered by a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm to improve the JITAI in real-time.MethodsEmerging adults (N = 122) with regular cannabis use were enrolled in a 30-day MRT, and completed a 1-month post-test and 2-month follow-up (e.g., after MRT start; NCT05824754). During the MRT, participants were prompted to complete twice-daily surveys, followed by twice-daily micro-randomizations to intervention message or no message (probabilities determined by the RL algorithm). Primary outcomes were 1-month feasibility and acceptability; MRT results were examined for the effect of intervention message delivery (vs. not) on proximal intervention engagement and cannabis use (next decision point; intent-to-treat).FindingsThe app was feasible (89.1% (n = 122/137) downloaded; 77.1% (5642/7320) of twice-daily surveys completed, and acceptable (80.4% (n = 90/112) positively rated the messages for care, support, warmth, and respect). In MRT analyses, delivering a message (vs. not) increased proximal engagement (b = 0.044; 95% CI: 0.018, 0.070) but did not affect proximal cannabis use (b = 0.008; 95% CI: −0.003, 0.020). A clinically meaningful negative correlation was found between average engagement and cannabis use days at 2-months (b = −3.38; 95% CI: −8.68, 1.91).InterpretationThe pJITAI is promising among not-in-treatment emerging adults, with messages increasing engagement, warranting further refinement to optimize this intervention.FundingResearch was supported by the National Institute of Health [(P50DA054039; K23AA028232; T32AA007477; K23DA059484; P41EB028242] and the Brzoznowski family.Keywords: Mobile health, Cannabis, Emerging adults, Just-in-time adaptive intervention

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Cannabis use rates peak during emerging adulthood, which is important to address as regular use increases risk for problems and few emerging adults seek treatment. Mobile health (mHealth) just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs) offer potential for reducing cannabis use, with engagement being a key challenge. JITAIs leverage dynamic information about the person's state and context to decide whether and how to intervene via decision rules that link tailoring variables to intervention options (e.g., deliver vs. not deliver a message). In classical JITAIs, these decision rules are pre-specified prior to intervention deployment. In contrast, personalizing JITAIs (pJITAIs) integrate an RL algorithm that uses data accrued over time to learn whether the decision rules are performing well and modify them as needed during the intervention. This additional layer of adaptation ensures the JITAI can evolve alongside the participant, with the potential to increase engagement and responsivity.

PubMed and Google Scholar databases were searched using terms “mobile health”, “mHealth”, “digital health”, or “just-in-time adaptive interventions”, combined with “engagement”, “cannabis”, “marijuana”, “drug”, and/or “substance” in 2025 and 2026 to identify relevant prior research with mHealth apps for cannabis use and engagement in digital interventions. Areas for further research included conducting fully powered optimization studies to inform mHealth interventions for substance use.

Added value of this study

The objective of this study was to reduce harms from regular cannabis use among emerging adults by pilot testing MiWaves—a highly novel mHealth intervention deploying a pJITAI. Emerging adults with regular cannabis use, who were not in treatment for cannabis use were enrolled in a 30-day study testing MiWaves. Results indicated that the app was feasible and acceptable, delivering a message (vs. not) increased proximal mHealth engagement, and mHealth engagement was associated with fewer cannabis use days at 2-months. This research lays the foundation for the next generation of mHealth interventions to reduce the public health burden of cannabis use by adapting to the rapidly changing needs of underserved populations.

Implications of all the available evidence

This is the first study to test a mHealth intervention for cannabis use that applies a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm. Findings for this intervention were promising, including that it was feasible and acceptable, that message delivery increased proximal mHealth engagement, and that mHealth engagement was linked to cannabis use reductions. Together, these insights underscore the promise of a mHealth intervention leveraging RL algorithms to address the changing needs of emerging adults who might otherwise remain disconnected from traditional substance use services. Results warrant further testing in a fully powered trial.

Introduction

Cannabis use among emerging adults (age 18–25) continues to increase with escalating health consequences,1 including negative impacts on school/work performance and day-to-day decision making, impaired driving and injury, and the development of a cannabis use disorder.2,3 Emerging adulthood is a critical period for interventions, with mobile health (mHealth) delivery being particularly well-suited for this group given their high levels of digital literacy and tool access.4 Indeed, extant research underscores the promise of mHealth for reducing cannabis use among emerging adults.5, 6, 7 Despite data showing that only 11.3% of emerging adults in need of treatment actually receive care, most mHealth interventions continue to target individuals already in treatment.5, 6, 7 This leaves the vast majority of this population—those not currently in treatment—underserved. Given that emerging adults who use cannabis regularly (e.g., 3 times per week or more) face elevated risk for consequences and cannabis use disorder, there is clear potential for mHealth interventions to support those not in treatment.8 Moreover, while mHealth interventions are well-positioned for large-scale public health impact given their wide reach and low delivery costs, suboptimal mHealth engagement (i.e., energy investment in intervention-related stimuli and tasks) remains a barrier to effectiveness.9,10

Just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs) may promote mHealth engagement, as they are designed to address individuals' rapidly changing needs in daily life while minimizing habituation and burden.11,12 JITAIs deliver an intervention only when necessary (i.e., high risk for an adverse proximal outcome; opportunity for positive change) and when the individual is receptive (i.e., likely to engage effectively with intervention-related stimuli and tasks).11 This is done via ‘adaptation’, defined as the use of dynamic (i.e., time-varying) information about the individual to decide whether and how to intervene.11 This process is operationalized via decision rules, which are If-Then statements linking tailoring variables (i.e., self-reported and/or sensor-collected information used to make intervention decisions) to intervention options (i.e., different intervention types, tactics or intensities under consideration). The decision rules specify the conditions in which different intervention options should be delivered at each of several decision points, which are time points at which an intervention decision should be made. For example, consider the following hypothetical decision rule: twice daily, in the morning and in the evening, if the individual used cannabis recently, then deliver an intervention message supporting cannabis use reduction; otherwise, no message should be delivered. Classical JITAIs deploy decision rules that investigators pre-specify prior to intervention deployment based on prior evidence and practical considerations. These decision rules are implemented uniformly across individuals (i.e., they are fixed) and remain unchanged over time (i.e., they are static). This does not mean that the JITAI itself is fixed and static. Because the tailoring variables (e.g., recent cannabis use) fluctuate, the JITAI delivers different intervention options for different individuals and at different times, even though the decision rule governing those choices does not evolve during the intervention.12

While classical JITAIs have shown tremendous potential for overcoming barriers that have limited engagement with mHealth interventions,7 even highly sophisticated pre-specified decision rules with many tailoring variables, cannot anticipate the myriad of unexpected variables that influence participant engagement and responsivity. Sudden shifts in a participant's environment or the technological landscape can rapidly erode engagement and responsivity, rendering a priori decision rules obsolete. Personalizing JITAIs (pJITAIs) were developed to address this gap via the integration of a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm that improves JITAI decision rules during intervention deployment [preprint13], this algorithm uses data accrued over time as the participant experiences and interacts with the JITAI to learn whether the decision rules are performing well and iteratively modify them throughout the intervention, rather than waiting for post-deployment analysis. For example, if data from the first weeks indicate insufficient evidence that messaging following recent cannabis use is beneficial, the RL algorithm will reduce the likelihood of delivery under those specific conditions. The term “personalizing” here is used to highlight an added layer of adaptation, on top of the adaptation governing classical JITAIs. Specifically, the RL algorithm enables the decision rules that govern the adaptation to adapt—improve based on the person's engagement with and responsivity to the decision rules during the intervention. This additional layer of adaptation ensures the JITAI can evolve alongside the participant, amplifying the capacity of the decision rules to address the changing needs of the person, while minimizing burden and habituation.12

The development of a pJITAI for cannabis use in emerging adults represents a highly novel and previously unexplored application,14 placing this work at the forefront of intervention science. As a proof-of-concept, this study offers preliminary evidence of feasibility and acceptability in real-world settings. Reflecting the iterative development process central to intervention optimization,15, 16, 17 this preparation is essential for a subsequent fully powered optimization trial. Optimization trials, such as the micro-randomized trial (MRT)18 are highly efficient experiments aimed at empirically informing an effective and resource-efficient intervention before conducting an evaluation trial comparing the optimized intervention to a suitable control (e.g., comparing the optimized pJITAI to a classical JITAI without a learning algorithm).17 By ensuring that every intervention component provides sufficient benefit to justify its associated burden and costs, optimization increases the overall public health impact of interventions.17

Emerging adults with regular cannabis use, who were not in treatment for cannabis use and had diverse levels of motivation to change, were recruited from the community and enrolled in a 30-day study testing MiWaves—a novel mHealth intervention involving twice-daily self-monitoring and up to twice-daily intervention messages supporting cannabis use reduction. At each decision point, decision rules adapted the likelihood of message delivery based on information about the time of day (morning, evening), mHealth engagement, and cannabis use, all of which are indicators of risk for future use.10,19,20 An RL algorithm aimed at optimizing proximal mHealth engagement iteratively updated these decision rules during intervention deployment, determining the likelihood of message delivery at each decision point. Micro-randomized trial (MRT) design principles18 were leveraged to ensure the data can be used to answer exploratory causal scientific questions about the effects of delivering (vs. not delivering) a message, as well as the impact of message length and effortfulness of the message task on proximal mHealth engagement and cannabis use. Primary outcomes included feasibility and acceptability, with exploratory analyses investigating whether, what type, and under what conditions delivering an intervention message increases proximal mHealth engagement and reduces proximal cannabis use. The correlation between proximal mHealth engagement and cannabis use, as well as changes in cannabis use from the start of the 30-day MRT to 1-month post-test and 2-month follow-up, is also reported.

Thus, the objective of this study was to reduce harms from regular cannabis use among emerging adults by pilot testing a highly novel mHealth intervention designed to deliver timely therapeutic messages in a way that increases engagement and reduces burden. By laying the groundwork for the next generation of mHealth interventions, this research establishes a foundation for reducing the public health burden of cannabis use through high-reach, low-burden interventions that adapt to the rapidly changing needs of underserved populations.

Methods

Design

Emerging adults (ages 18–25), recruited nationally in the U.S. using social media ads (2/12/2024–3/25/2024), were invited to complete an online consent and eligibility survey. Those eligible were invited to complete an online consent for MRT participation and the baseline survey, followed by MiWaves app onboarding and the remote 30-day MRT, with four weekly online surveys (Sundays), and a 1-month post-test and 2-month follow-up (after the start of the 30-day MRT).21 We expected the MiWaves app to be feasible [at least 70.0% (e.g., n = 86/122) downloading the app] and acceptable [at least 70.0% (e.g., n = 86/122) rating the messages positively for care, support, warmth, and respect]. In the MRT, we hypothesized that sending a message (vs. not) would increase proximal mHealth engagement and cannabis use (at the next decision point). There was no public involvement in the study design, conduct or reporting.

Procedures

MRT enrollment occurred in three phases of different-sized cohorts (wave 1: n = 37, 2/19/2024–3/11/2024; wave 2: n = 67, 3/11/2024–3/25/2024; wave 3: n = 18, 3/25/2024–4/15/2024), given limited staffing. Eligibility criteria were: 18–25 years old, smartphone access, self-report of cannabis use 3 or more times/week in the past month (given evidence for more consequences20), and any motivation to reduce cannabis use (>0 on 0–10 scale ranging from not at all to very).21 Eligibility criteria were masked with other substance use questions. To increase representativeness, verification checks (i.e., CAPTCHA, IP address, social media checks, survey time duration20,22) were conducted prior to MRT enrollment, prioritized by sex [at least 50.0% (e.g., n = 61/122) female or non-binary] and race [at least 25.0% (e.g., n = 31/122) non-White]. App onboarding included selecting a morning and evening survey completion start time. Participants received gift cards for completing assessments: $0.50–3.00 via a “tap to reveal” reward card (twice-daily surveys; up to $60), $40 (baseline), $60 ($15/weekly), $40 (1-month), and $50 (2-month).

Intervention

The MiWaves app includes twice-daily self-monitoring surveys with personalized feedback graphs, reward cards showing incentives earned for completing surveys, intervention messages, and an online, real-time RL algorithm (Fig. 1).21 The self-monitoring survey assessed cannabis use, health behaviors (e.g., sleep/waking hours, alcohol use), mood, hopefulness, and mHealth engagement with behavior change strategies suggested in the intervention messages. Up to two daily intervention messages were delivered at each of two individual-specific decision points per day based on randomization probabilities determined by the RL algorithm (range 0.2–0.8). To explore if effortfulness was related to proximal mHealth engagement and cannabis use, six types of intervention messages were possible based on length (short, ≤150 characters; or, long, ≥200 characters) and effortfulness of the task in the message (acknowledge receipt by clicking “done”, access a resource link, or type a response, such as “fill in the blank with a cannabis-free activity”) (Table 1). Intervention messages were developed based on prior work and refined with feedback from emerging adults.20, 21, 22 Messages were framed using principles of motivational interviewing and focused on theory-based mechanisms of change for cannabis use (i.e., affect regulation, alternative cannabis-free activities, future/goal-oriented thoughts/activities).23 Staff could not manipulate randomization, and were blinded to randomization details. Human-touch contacts (via email, text or phone call) were delivered based on disengagement period (i.e., 72, 120, and 168 h [1 week]), summed over the past 4 decision points, and coded binary (no = 0, yes = 1).

Measures

Screen/Baseline, 1-month post-test, and 2-month follow-up

Characteristics assessed included: age, sex, gender identity, race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, marital status, school enrollment, education, employment status, household income (“including parents who support you”), and food insecurity.24

Past-month cannabis-related questions included a header, “This includes products that contain THC, including flower in joints, blunts, bowls; dabs/wax/shatter/budder or other concentrates; oil or flower in vaporizers; edibles and tinctures; and skincare products such as lotions.” “Do not include products that contain only CBD.”22 The survey assessed a) frequency (never, 1 time, 2–3 times, once a week, 2 times a week, 3–4 times a week, nearly every day, every day, and more than once a day)22; b) time used after waking (within 5 min, 6–30 min, 31 min to almost 1 h, 1 to almost 2 h, 2 to almost 4 h, and 4 or more hours)25; c) cannabis use days (range: 0–31); d) times used on a typical day (range: 0–24, recognizing 24 h/day); and, e) number of hours used on a typical day (range: 0–24).25,26

Motivation to reduce cannabis use was assessed: “Right now, how important is it to you to cut back your cannabis use?”, ranging from “not at all” (0) to “very” (10).26 Alcohol use severity was assessed with the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test-Consumption (AUDIT-C)27; frequency of illicit drug use28 and prescription drug misuse29 were assessed (never, 1 time, 2–3 times, once a week, 2 times a week, 3–4 times a week, nearly every day, every day, and more than once a day).

Open-ended questions in the 1-month post-test assessed: “What did you like most about the MiWaves app features and MiWaves messages?”, “What did you like least about the MiWaves app features and MiWaves messages?”, “What would you change about the MiWaves app?”.

Primary outcomes

Study outcomes were pre-registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05824754).21

Feasibility

The primary outcome is the percentage consenting and downloading the MiWaves app; additional participant and app performance measures included: a) the number of twice-daily surveys delivered on time (i.e., 15–30 min after the participant's chosen survey time ended or immediately after completing self-monitoring) and the percentage of twice-daily surveys completed over 30 days; b) the percentage of intervention messages delivered as planned and the percentage of intervention messages with which participants interacted; and c) whether the RL successfully updated the micro-randomization probabilities based on incoming data information [i.e., time of day (AM vs. PM), app related data (e.g., cannabis use based on twice daily survey, mHealth engagement with MiWaves; score (0–3)]) and the percentage of micro-randomization probabilities within the pre-specified boundaries of [0.2, 0.8].

Acceptability

The primary acceptability outcome was the percentage with a positive rating at 1-month post-test on a single item assessing care, support, warmth, and respect, defined as rating the messages and notifications at least a 3 (“somewhat or greater”) on a 1 (“not at all”) to 5 (“extremely”) scale.30 As shown in Table 2, participants rated other aspects of MiWaves.

Exploratory outcomes

Proximal cannabis use

Cannabis use was assessed twice daily by asking participants to select all hours that they used any cannabis product in the past 12 h and all hours of sleep in the past 12 h. The proximal cannabis use outcome was operationalized as the proportion of awake hours with cannabis use (i.e., number of awake hours with cannabis use divided by the total awake hours), following each decision point.

Proximal mHealth engagement

Engagement with MiWaves was assessed based on a score ranging from 0 to 3, between each decision point and the next: 0—the participant did not open the app or complete the self-monitoring survey (i.e., no interaction); 1: the participant opened the app for more than 10 s but did not complete the self-monitoring survey; 2: the participant completed the self-monitoring survey, but did not report engaging with the behavior change strategies suggested in the messages; 3: the participant completed the self-monitoring survey and reported engaging with the behavior change strategies suggested in the messages.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data are presented for the sample and for feasibility and acceptability, including qualitative data, which were coded using Rapid Qualitative Analysis (RQA).31 RQA is a pragmatic approach to rapidly gather qualitative information to inform intervention refinements in substance use research.31,32 Sample size justification is provided within the protocol paper.21 No interim analyses were conducted.

For the exploratory analyses, descriptive data are presented for mean motivation to change and mean past-month cannabis use at baseline, 1-month post-test, and 2-month follow-up. Then, we report the correlation between the average of proximal mHealth engagement over 30 days and follow-up reports of cannabis use, at 1-month and 2-months, using linear regressions, adjusting for baseline past-month days of cannabis use.

Finally, results for the effect of sending (vs. not sending) an intervention message on proximal mHealth engagement and cannabis use are presented. The MRTAnalysis R package was used to fit a generalization of a regression model that ensures unbiased estimates of causal effects of time-varying treatments.21,33,34 A similar approach was used to explore a set of time-varying and baseline candidate moderators for the effect of sending (vs. not sending) an intervention message (see Appendix; Table A-1). Analyses concerning the proximal cannabis use outcome adjust for covariates: time of day (AM vs. PM), day of week (weekend vs. weekday), prior cannabis use (average over past 4 decision points, i.e., approximately 48.0 h), prior mHealth engagement (average over past 2 decision points, i.e., approximately 24.0 h), baseline motivation to change, baseline cannabis use, and baseline time to cannabis use after waking. Covariates for analyses concerning the proximal mHealth engagement outcome include time of day (AM vs. PM), day of week (weekend vs. weekday), prior mHealth engagement (average over past 6 decision points, i.e., approximately 72.0 h), and prior human-touch engagement (sum over past 4 decision points, i.e., approximately 48.0 h and dichotomized; see Table A-1 for details). The covariate time-scale, for those with rolling averages, were selected by examining univariate associations between each covariate averaged over the past 24.0, 48.0, and 72.0 h with the relevant proximal outcome, using a linear mixed effects model with a random intercept and adjusting for time of day (see Table A-1). Analyses were performed in R Software Version 4.4.2; see the protocol paper for information on the power analysis.21

Results

Enrollment and sample characteristics

Recruitment ads were posted on social media platforms (e.g., Meta, Snapchat, TikTok). Overall, 1871 emerging adults completed the screening survey, with n = 907 (48.5%) eligible (Fig. 2). Given that social media recruitment poses a risk of encountering bots and/or fraudulent responses, verification checks (e.g., duplicate IP address, CAPTCHA) were employed to maintain sample and data integrity. To increase representativeness, verification and enrollment was prioritized based on sex and race. Verification checks were conducted with 573 individuals, 177 passed and were invited to consent, 137 consented and were invited to complete the baseline survey (77.4% of the 177 who passed), 125 completed the baseline survey (91.2% of the 137 invited) and were invited to download the app, and 122 downloaded the app and were enrolled. Regarding the 4 weekly surveys, 71.3% (n = 87), 74.6% (n = 91), 74.6% (n = 91), and 54.1% (n = 66) were completed, respectively (due to a technical error, some participants did not receive the Week 4 survey). Follow-up completion rates exceeded 90% (n = 112 at 1-month and n = 111 at 2-months).

Feasibility

Participant measures

Regarding the primary feasibility outcome, the percentage of participants who consented to the study and downloaded the MiWaves app, among those eligible, verified, and consented (n = 137), was 89.1% (n = 122/137); rates were similar by sex [89.0% (n = 65/73) female; 89.1% (n = 57/64) male] and race/ethnicity [85.6% (n = 45/47) Non-Hispanic White; 95.7% (n = 77/90) Hispanic/race other than White]. Among the 122 participants who downloaded the app, emerging adults completed 77.1% (5642/7320) of the twice-daily self-monitoring surveys; rates were similar by sex [81.3% (n = 3169/3900) among 65 females; 72.3% (n = 2473/3420) among 57 males] and race/ethnicity [78.6% (n = 3633/4620) among 77 participants who were Non-Hispanic White; 74.4% (n = 2009/2700) among 45 participants who were Hispanic/race other than White].

App performance measures

First, 7293 (99.6%) of the total (7,320) twice-daily self-monitoring surveys were delivered on time (i.e., at the participant's chosen survey time); 6 were not prompted on time due to participant travel, and 21 surveys were not prompted due to the backend system being at capacity. Second, the average number of messages delivered was 24.5 (SD = 4.0), with 99.2% delivered as planned; 21 expected messages were not delivered due to backend system downtime at 21 decision points, and 4 messages could not be confirmed as sent due to network or system issues (see Appendix Table A-2). Participants opened 1987 (66.5% of the 2990 intervention messages delivered) and actively interacted with 1693 messages (85.2% of those opened; 57.4% of the 1507 short and 55.8% of the 1483 long). Participants responded to 59.2% of the 1001 messages requesting acknowledgement of receipt only, 25.2% of the 1011 messages providing resource links, and 35.2% of the 978 messages requesting a written response. The RL algorithm informed the randomization probabilities (to deliver vs. not deliver an intervention message) for 94.8% of the decision points (6943/7320) (see Appendix Table A-2).

Acceptability

Regarding the primary acceptability outcome, 80.4% (n = 90/112) rated MiWaves messages and notifications at least a 3 (somewhat or greater) for care, support, warmth, and respect; rates were similar by sex [81.0% female (n = 51/63); 79.6% (n = 39/49) male] and race/ethnicity [79.2% (57/72) Non-Hispanic White; 82.5% (n = 33/40) Hispanic/race other than White]. Most (79.4%; n = 89/112) indicated they could envision using at least some of the activities after the intervention ended (see Table 4 for additional feedback).

Qualitative feedback

At the 1-month post-test survey, participants responded to open-ended questions regarding MiWaves (see Table 4), with some providing suggestions for likes (n = 98) and dislikes (n = 90), excluding those stating “none” (n = 14 and n = 22, respectively). Participants liked the messages (26.5%; n = 26; e.g., helpful, creative, kind), overall app helpfulness and/or ease of use (30.6%; n = 30), feedback graphs (27.6%; n = 27; e.g., trends), rewards for survey completion (14.3%; n = 14), and check-ins (9.2%; n = 9). Additional positive comments (11.2%; n = 11; e.g., timing of messages, resources, reminders, routine) were noted. Of all themes endorsed for dislikes (n = 90), the most common dislike was experiencing technical issues (38.9%; n = 35). Additional dislikes or suggestions for app improvement included the message content (25.6%; n = 23; e.g., perceived as generic, uninteresting, poorly phrased, or included unhelpful links), self-monitoring windows timing (21.1%; n = 19), and other features (14.4%; n = 13; e.g., trends graphs, message timing, phone usage demands).

Correlation between engagement in mHealth and cannabis use and 1- and 2-months

A clinically meaningful negative correlation was found between average mHealth engagement during the MRT and days of cannabis use at 2-month follow-up (b = −3.38 [95% CI: −8.68, 1.91]), but not at 1-month post-test (b = 0.35 [95% CI: −4.39, 5.08]), when controlling for baseline days of cannabis use for both. The former effect is considered meaningful, given its size, in that a 1-unit increase in average mHealth engagement during the study is associated with 3.38 fewer cannabis use days at 2-month follow-up, adjusting for baseline cannabis use.

Discussion

This study addresses a critical gap in intervention science by providing a proof-of-concept for a pJITAI targeting emerging adults with regular cannabis use who are not in treatment. Consistent with early-stage intervention development, we focused on feasibility and acceptability as pJITAIs add another layer of adaptation on top of the adaptation deployed by traditional JITAIs. Classical JITAIs adapt the delivery of interventions to the unique and rapidly changing needs of individuals via pre-specified decision rules that are applied uniformly across individuals and time. By contrast, pJITAIs introduce a more responsive approach, integrating an RL algorithm to improve the decision rules during intervention deployment, based on an individual's engagement with and responsivity to the decision rules. By enabling the decision rules to evolve alongside the participant to reduce burden and increase engagement, these algorithms help overcome key barriers to the public health impact of mHealth interventions. Overall, the results support the feasibility and acceptability of this innovative pJITAI. Specifically, the MiWaves app was feasible, with >80% (89.1%; n = 122/137) downloading the app and >75% of twice-daily surveys completed (77.1%; 5642/7320), and acceptable, with >80% (80.4%; n = 90/112) rating the messages positively for showing care, support, warmth, and respect. Importantly, findings that message delivery via a pJITAI increased engagement, a key prerequisite for behavior change, provide early empirical evidence that pJITAIs can meaningfully influence behavior. These results establish a foundation for next-generation interventions capable of improving public health outcomes through scalable, high-reach, and engaging digital care.

Days of cannabis use decreased between start of the trial, 1-month and 2-month assessments. Moreover, greater mHealth engagement over the intervention period was associated with meaningful reductions in cannabis use at 2 months, with every 1 unit increase in mHealth engagement linked to 3.38 fewer cannabis use days, even after adjusting for initial cannabis use, an effect that endured beyond the end of the intervention period. Whilst this association should be interpreted cautiously, as these analyses were exploratory, these findings are promising given the complexity of substance use behavior change. Likewise, although the average daily frequency and hours of cannabis use did not decrease from the baseline to 1- and 2-month assessments, the reliability and validity of retrospective, within-day panel survey items may be limited given the array of products, potency, and routes of administration available. These measurement challenges highlight the need for continued methodological development as pJITAIs are considered for broader population-health deployment. A key direction is to improve measurements of within-day, finer-grained cannabis use, including passive data, that are sensitive to risk reduction outcomes, and measurements of well-being outcomes.7

Importantly, delivering an intervention message (vs. not delivering) increased mHealth engagement, which is encouraging as engagement in mHealth interventions is essential for behavior change. Additionally, delivering a message in the morning (vs. the evening) was more beneficial in promoting mHealth engagement, and intervention messages containing a low-effort task were more beneficial than those with more effortful tasks (although message length did not appear to matter). Future work could investigate ways to incorporate message type into the pJITAI so that more effortful tasks, which are clinically important (e.g., planning future cannabis free activities) and support skill development (e.g., practice a strategy to manage cravings), are optimally introduced when the individual is most likely to engage with and benefit from them. Although further testing is needed, these findings advance the field by establishing a highly novel approach to boosting mHealth engagement—a pivotal prerequisite for improving intervention effectiveness and achieving public health impact. Finally, these findings are especially promising given that our study included emerging adults with regular cannabis use but who were not-in-treatment and had diverse motivation to change.

When examining the effect of delivering (vs. not delivering) an intervention message on proximal cannabis use, there was no effect observed, potentially reflecting the range of motivation to reduce cannabis use (e.g., based on an eligibility criterion that required motivation to change to be greater than 0) among emerging adults in our sample. Next, although there is conflicting evidence regarding reactivity effects for mHealth, in-the-moment assessments of substance use,35 we saw a potential indication of reactivity to messages among those with initial low motivation to change their use at baseline, with some increases in cannabis use. Prior work shows low motivation to change reduces treatment engagement and outcomes, with increases in motivation to change during treatment associated with better cannabis use outcomes.36 Although we did not assess motivation to change during the MRT intervention period, motivation to change decreased from start of the MRT to 2-month follow-up, despite the finding that on average cannabis use also decreased over time. Together, these findings may suggest that some emerging adults may perceive less need to reduce their use if they were already doing so. Future work should consider requiring greater motivation to change for study participation and assessing motivation to change rapidly (e.g., twice daily) during the MRT as a potential mechanism of change.

At a population health level, mHealth interventions such as MiWaves are potentially highly scalable and compatible with existing healthcare contacts, including routine primary care visits where cannabis use is commonly identified but options for early interventions and specialty treatment are limited. A low-burden, mHealth pJITAI offers a practical option that individuals can engage with as an early intervention to reduce use, or potentially alongside specialty treatment, depending on motivation and problem severity, extending the reach of care without replacing existing services.

Clinically, future optimization studies are needed to investigate how to best integrate pJITAIs with human-delivered support, as well as with other mHealth interventions for cannabis use reduction.7,37 For example, it may be beneficial to tailor the type and intensity of message delivery based on twice daily measures of motivation for cannabis use reduction, craving, and cannabis use severity (80.3% (n = 98/122) used almost daily or more; 25.6% (n = 31/121) used cannabis within an hour of waking, increasing risk19), as well as other risk and protective factors, such as other substance use, depression/anxiety symptoms, and social influences. The relatively low overall message exposure was an outcome of the RL algorithm's optimization process. To maximize engagement while minimizing participant burden and habituation, the algorithm adjusted message delivery probabilities: it reduced the likelihood of messaging when data accrued during JITAI deployment suggested a lack of responsiveness and increased it only when there was sufficient evidence of potential benefit. Thus, the observed delivery rate reflects the algorithm iteratively refining the JITAI decision rules to avoid over-burdening participants. It is possible that deploying this same algorithm in a population with greater clinical severity would have resulted in higher message frequency, as the ‘evidence’ for the utility of support would likely have been stronger. Moreover, prior mHealth studies have incorporated additional features, such as location alerts when entering a high-risk area for relapse, and features to facilitate human support, such as a clinician dashboard to share self-report data regarding progress.7

With the growth of generative AI, it may also be feasible to combine RL algorithms, which optimize JITAI decision rules (i.e., whether and when to send a message based on an individual's experiences), with automated content creation, which can instantly customize the framing and focus of the message itself to fit an individual's specific experiences. Exploring how these two technologies can work together to provide more relevant and timely support is a critical area for future research. Given that the development of mHealth apps requires significant funding and time, future work is needed to develop platforms that allow scientists to customize mHealth apps at a relatively low cost.38 In this regard, MiWaves was built on a platform called JustIn, which is a customizable, open-source platform that allows researchers to design, conduct, and analyze data from mHealth studies for optimizing JITAIs, enhancing scalability by reducing the time, effort, and funds needed to conduct iterative optimization studies and extending the shelf-life of mHealth apps in a resource-efficient manner.

It is important to note study limitations, which should be interpreted in the context of this study's aim: providing a proof-of-concept in preparation for a full-scale trial to optimize this highly novel intervention. Generalizability is limited based on the sample size and recruitment via social media, however, the study focuses on not-in-treatment emerging adults with regular cannabis use and any motivation to reduce cannabis use is strength, as this undertreated population may be amenable to low-barrier mHealth interventions deployed outside of specialty treatment. Although the sample included some diversity in sex and race/ethnicity, this pilot study was not powered to detect effects overall or within subgroups; such analyses should be prioritized in future research. The survey item assessing the acceptability of intervention messages was quadruple-barreled, and future measurement may be better achieved with single-topic questions. Next, the mHealth engagement definition assigned a value of “1” to those who opened the app but did not respond to the survey; it is unclear what content they viewed in that brief period (e.g., prior intervention message). Although compensation for completion of the twice-daily self-monitoring survey was low and there is precedent for compensation of mHealth interventions (e.g., contingency management, employment health stipends), such compensation limits scalability in practice, which is another key challenge in the field.39 Addressing these scalability considerations is essential for informing health-system and policy decisions about how pJITAIs could be incorporated into population-level behavioral health infrastructure. Finally, while the exploratory nature of testing message effects on proximal outcomes in this study requires replication in a fully powered optimization trial, these analyses offer early empirical insight into how message delivery via a pJITAI causally influences engagement—a clinically relevant intermediate outcome essential for the success of mHealth interventions.

In conclusion, these findings demonstrate that an innovative, risk-reduction–focused mHealth intervention leveraging a pJITAI is both feasible and acceptable for emerging adults with regular cannabis use who are not in treatment; a population who are unlikely to engage in specialty substance use treatment. Notably, this study delivers the first demonstration of an mHealth intervention for cannabis use that applies an RL algorithm to refine decision rules for mobile-based message delivery in real time as individuals interact with the intervention. The results indicate that message delivery meaningfully increases proximal mHealth engagement, a clinically important intermediate outcome for scalable digital interventions operating outside formal treatment settings, and that engagement is linked to reductions in cannabis use. Together, these insights underscore the promise of mHealth interventions leveraging pJITAIs to address the needs of young adults who might otherwise remain disconnected from traditional substance use services. Next steps require a fully powered trial to optimize this mHealth intervention and determine the added benefits of integrating human-delivered support and/or additional tailored mHealth features to improve health trajectories for emerging adults with regular cannabis use.
