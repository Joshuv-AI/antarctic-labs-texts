Spatiotemporal characterization of placental hypoxia and microvascular dysfunction in a preeclampsia rat model using photoacoustic and contrast-enhanced ultrasound
Authors: Bo Zhang, Houqing Pang, Haibo Yang, Hong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlacental ischemia and hypoxia are central drivers of preeclampsia (PE) pathogenesis. However, conventional Doppler ultrasound primarily evaluates uterine and umbilical arterial resistance to indirectly infer placental impedance, lacking the sensitivity to directly quantify intraplacental tissue oxygenation and microvascular perfusion dynamics. This study aimed to explore the feasibility of using a multimodal imaging approach, combining photoacoustic imaging (PAI) and contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), to characterize the longitudinal spatiotemporal features of placental dysfunction in an Nω-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME)-induced PE rat model.MethodsPregnant Sprague-Dawley rats were randomly assigned to control and PE groups (n=10 per group). PE was induced via daily L-NAME administration (250 mg/kg/day) from gestational day (GD) 7 to GD18. Longitudinal multimodal imaging was performed on the same cohort of rats at GD14, GD16, and GD18. PAI was utilized to quantitatively map hemoglobin oxygen saturation (sO2), with sub-regional analysis performed to evaluate the oxygenation gradient between the labyrinth and basal zones. CEUS was employed to quantify microvascular hemodynamic parameters. Imaging biomarkers were validated against histological markers of hypoxia (HIF-1α) and vascular density (CD31).ResultsPAI revealed a physiological oxygenation gradient (labyrinth > basal) in normal placentas. In the PE group, a progressive hypoxic shift was observed, with mean sO2 levels significantly reduced starting from GD14 compared to controls (52.2%±5.4% vs. 59.4%±5.1%, P<0.01), reaching a nadir at GD18 (49.4%±4.8% vs. 61.8%±5.4%, P<0.001). CEUS characterization revealed a hemodynamic phenotype characterized by “volume-velocity dissociation”: parameters reflecting microvascular blood volume [e.g., wash-in area under the curve (WiAUC)] significantly declined as early as GD14 in PE rats [6,203.9±2,358.1 vs. 10,956.1±4,166.1 arbitrary units (a.u.), P<0.05], indicative of vascular rarefaction, whereas flow velocity parameters [rise time (RT), time-to-peak (TTP)] remained preserved throughout gestation. Furthermore, sO2 was negatively correlated with HIF-1α (r=−0.73, P<0.005), whereas WiAUC was positively correlated with CD31 (r=0.68, P<0.005). A logistic regression model integrating metabolic (sO2) perfusion (WiAUC) metrics yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.89 for early disease prediction at GD14, and 0.93 for discriminating PE placentas at GD18.ConclusionsThis preliminary study demonstrates the feasibility of longitudinal multimodal imaging to characterize placental dysfunction. Specifically, PAI effectively captured the progressive hypoxic shift, complementing the CEUS-derived “volume-velocity dissociation”, which suggests that microvascular rarefaction precedes flow velocity alterations. By integrating metabolic and perfusion metrics, this platform holds promise as a quantitative tool for preclinical monitoring and therapeutic evaluation.

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) affects approximately 4–5% of pregnancies worldwide and remains a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity (1,2). The pathogenesis of PE is fundamentally driven by placental dysfunction, specifically impaired uterine spiral artery remodeling, which leads to placental ischemia and hypoxia (3,4). This hypoxic stress triggers the release of anti-angiogenic factors, culminating in widespread endothelial dysfunction and systemic hypertension (5,6). Importantly, although clinical symptoms typically manifest in mid-to-late gestation, the underlying microvascular and metabolic alterations within the placenta often precede or evolve concurrently with these systemic signs. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of these “invisible” placental pathologies is essential for understanding disease progression and evaluating potential therapeutic strategies.

Currently, placental function is assessed primarily via invasive procedures, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and ultrasonography (US). Cordocentesis carries significant risks of fetal loss (7). Advanced MRI techniques, expanding beyond blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) imaging to incorporate sophisticated diffusion and perfusion frameworks—such as two-compartment intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM)—have demonstrated substantial efficacy in non-invasively quantifying impaired trophoblast perfusion in placental pathologies (8,9). However, the widespread clinical adoption of these modalities is limited by high operational costs and prolonged multi-parametric data acquisition times, rendering them inherently susceptible to unpredictable fetal motion artifacts. Ultrasound remains the cornerstone of prenatal screening. However, conventional Doppler ultrasound primarily assesses placental vascular resistance indirectly through uterine or umbilical artery resistance indices (10). Critically, these methods lack the sensitivity to directly quantify tissue-level microvascular perfusion or regional oxygenation. Consequently, subtle microvascular deficits—such as capillary rarefaction—may remain undetected by standard clinical tools. There is a need for non-invasive, multimodal imaging strategies that can simultaneously characterize placental oxygenation and microvascular architecture in real-time (11).

Photoacoustic imaging (PAI) has emerged as a promising biomedical imaging modality that bridges the gap between optical contrast and ultrasonic resolution (12). Based on the photoacoustic effect, PAI utilizes pulsed laser irradiation to generate acoustic waves from biological tissues. A key advantage of PAI is its ability to spectrally differentiate endogenous chromophores, particularly oxyhemoglobin (HbO2) and deoxyhemoglobin (HbR), based on their distinct optical absorption coefficients in the near-infrared window (e.g., 750 and 850 nm) (13,14). This capability allows for the estimation of hemoglobin oxygen saturation (sO2), providing a functional readout of tissue oxygenation status. PAI has demonstrated significant potential in oncology for mapping tumor hypoxia (15) and has recently been explored in placental imaging (16). However, most existing studies have focused on cross-sectional observations, and the application of PAI for the longitudinal spatiotemporal monitoring of PE progression warrants further investigation.

To complement the metabolic information provided by PAI, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) offers high sensitivity in visualizing microvascular hemodynamics (17). By utilizing gas-filled microbubbles as intravascular tracers, CEUS enhances the backscatter signal, enabling the visualization of perfusion in capillary beds that are often invisible to conventional Doppler techniques. Through the analysis of time-intensity curves (TICs), CEUS allows for the extraction of critical perfusion parameters. Importantly, CEUS can differentiate between blood volume (reflected by the signal intensity of microbubble concentration) and flow velocity [derived from time-to-peak (TTP) kinetics], offering a unique opportunity to identify specific hemodynamic phenotypes, such as vascular rarefaction. Although CEUS has been validated in liver and tumor imaging (18,19), its potential to decipher the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of placental perfusion in PE remains to be fully realized (20).

Given that PE is fundamentally driven by placental ischemia and hypoxia, we hypothesized that a multimodal imaging approach combining PAI and CEUS could provide a comprehensive, longitudinal characterization of placental dysfunction. Theoretically, PAI-derived sO2 reflects the metabolic consequences of hypoxia, whereas CEUS-derived perfusion parameters reveal the structural and hemodynamic underpinnings. Clinically, early-onset PE is driven by severe placental ischemia, whereas late-onset PE relates to maternal metabolic factors. By inhibiting nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, the Nω-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME) model induces profound placental hypoperfusion, effectively mimicking this early-onset ischemic phenotype (5).

Therefore, in this study, we utilized an L-NAME-induced rat model of PE to: (I) longitudinally monitor the spatiotemporal evolution of placental sO2 and microvascular hemodynamics; (II) investigate the interplay between placental oxygenation and perfusion; and (III) validate these imaging biomarkers against histological evidence of hypoxia (hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha, HIF-1α) and vascular density (cluster of differentiation 31, CD31). Ultimately, this study aimed to provide a preliminary quantitative imaging framework that not only aids in understanding the underlying mechanisms of placental pathology but also explores the translational potential of these multimodal biomarkers for early clinical diagnosis. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2802/rc).

Methods

Animals and model establishment

All animal experiments were performed under a project license (No. 2023121) granted by the institutional ethics committee of West China Second Hospital, in compliance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals. Female Sprague-Dawley rats (200–250 g, 90–120 days old) were purchased from Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed under specific-pathogen-free conditions. Rats were housed in standard cages under a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 22±2 ℃, with ad libitum access to food and water. Prior to enrollment, all rats underwent a 1-week acclimatization period. Humane endpoints were established (e.g., >20% weight loss), and animals were monitored daily. Baseline tail-cuff blood pressure was monitored, and rats with unstable systolic blood pressure (SBP) or SBP >120 mmHg were excluded.

For mating, estrous females were caged with fertile males at a 2:1 ratio overnight. The presence of a vaginal plug was designated as gestational day 1 (GD1). Early pregnancy was confirmed by high-frequency ultrasound (HFUS) on GD7. Confirmed pregnant rats were then randomly assigned into two groups (n=10 per group) using the RAND() function in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA): a Control group (saline injection) and a PE group. The sample size was determined by referring to previous studies using similar experimental designs to ensure sufficient statistical power (15,16) and was further validated by a post-hoc power analysis, which yielded a statistical power of >0.99 (α =0.05) based on primary GD18 endpoints. To induce PE, L-NAME (250 mg/kg/day; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was administered intraperitoneally daily from GD7 to GD18 (21,22). On GD10, successful PE induction was defined as an SBP increase of >30 mmHg relative to the GD7 baseline, accompanied by significant proteinuria (>227 mg/L, exceeding mean + 2 SD of controls). Only rats meeting these criteria were included in the study. The validity of the PE model was further corroborated by placental histopathology at the study endpoint. All 20 animals successfully completed the longitudinal study protocol, and no animals or data points were excluded from the final statistical analysis.

Measurements of blood pressure and proteinuria

SBP was measured noninvasively using a tail-cuff system (Medlab; Nanjing Calvin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) at GD7 (baseline), GD10 (model validation), GD13, and GD18. To ensure accuracy, rats were pre-warmed at 37 ℃ for 30 minutes, and the mean of three consecutive measurements at 5-minute intervals was recorded. At the corresponding time points, 24-hour urine samples were collected in metabolic cages. After centrifugation (3,000 rpm, 5 min), supernatants were stored at −40 ℃. Protein concentration was quantified using a Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich), with absorbance measured at 595 nm.

PAI data acquisition

Longitudinal PAI was performed at GD14, GD16, and GD18 using a commercial hybrid imaging system (Vevo LAZR, Fujifilm VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada). The system integrates a tunable Nd:YAG-pumped OPO laser (680–970 nm, 20 Hz repetition rate, ~5 ns pulse width) with a high-frequency linear array transducer (LZ250; center frequency 21 MHz, 256 elements). The laser light was delivered via fiber optic bundles integrated into the transducer housing, ensuring co-registered photoacoustic and ultrasound signal acquisition.

Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction: 3–4%; maintenance: 1.5–2% in oxygen) and placed supine on a heated stage (37 ℃). Degassed coupling gel was applied to the depilated abdomen to ensure optimal acoustic coupling. To minimize optical attenuation variations, the PAI transducer depth was strictly fixed at ~9 mm from the skin surface across all animals. B-mode ultrasound was utilized to identify the umbilical cord insertion in the sagittal plane, determining the central imaging plane. For longitudinal monitoring, a single midline placenta with clear margins was selected per dam. Imaging parameters [gain, focus, time gain compensation (TGC)] were optimized and saved as standard presets applied consistently across all animals. Three-dimensional (3D) PAI scanning was performed in the Oxyhemo mode at dual wavelengths (750 and 850 nm). The optical fluence on the skin surface was maintained below 20 mJ/cm2, complying with the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) safety limits for laser exposure.

PAI reconstruction and analysis

PAI data were processed using VevoLab software (Fujifilm VisualSonics). Photoacoustic signals were reconstructed using a delay-and-sum beamforming algorithm. Based on the differential optical absorption spectra of HbO2 and HbR at 750 and 850 nm, the relative concentrations of chromophores were unmixed using a linear spectral unmixing algorithm. To quantify spatiotemporal heterogeneity, sub-regional ROIs were defined based on placental anatomical landmarks: the labyrinth zone (LZ) (inner vascular region on the fetal side) and the basal zone (BZ) (outer region on the maternal side) (Figure 1B). CEUS data acquisition

Longitudinal CEUS was performed on the same subjects immediately following PAI on GD14, 16, and 18, with each session involving an independent microbubble injection. The total duration of anesthesia was strictly limited to 25 minutes. Imaging was conducted on the identical maximal sagittal plane of the same placenta using a Samsung ultrasound system equipped with an LA2-14A linear probe (Samsung Medison, Seoul, South Korea). A bolus of sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles (SonoVue, Bracco, Milan, Italy; freshly prepared by dissolving 59 mg powder in 5 mL saline) was injected intravenously via the tail vein (1.0 mL/kg), followed by a 1.0 mL saline flush. To ensure quantitative consistency, imaging settings were strictly standardized and kept constant for all animals: depth 1.5 cm, gain 50 dB, dynamic range 50 dB, frame rate 10 fps, and focus positioned at the placental level. Real-time nonlinear contrast imaging was continuously acquired for 4 minutes using a low mechanical index (MI) (MI =0.07) to minimize microbubble destruction. Data were saved in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format for offline analysis.

CEUS quantification and analysis

The acquired cine loops were analyzed offline using VueBox software (Bracco Suisse SA, Plan-les-Ouates, Switzerland). Motion compensation algorithms provided by the software were applied to correct for respiratory motion artifacts. A perfusion model was fitted to the linearized echo power signal to extract quantitative hemodynamic parameters (Figure 2A). To characterize the hemodynamic phenotype, parameters were stratified into two physiological domains: (I) blood volume surrogates (intensity-related), including peak enhancement (Peak), wash-in area under the curve (WiAUC), wash-in perfusion index (WiPI), wash-in rate (WiR), and wash-out area under the curve (WoAUC), which reflect microvascular density; and (II) flow velocity surrogates (time-related), including rise time (RT) and TTP, which reflect blood flow transit kinetics. Additionally, color-coded parametric maps were generated to visualize the spatial heterogeneity of placental perfusion (Figure 2B). All CEUS cine loop analyses, including manual ROI tracking encompassing the entire placenta and quantitative data extraction, were performed by an investigator blinded to the group allocation.

Histopathology and quantitative assessment

At GD18, placentas were surgically harvested, weighed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Paraffin-embedded sections (4 µm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for structural evaluation. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to detect CD31 (marker for vascular density) and HIF-1α (marker for hypoxia) expression. Microscopic images were acquired using a light microscope with a ×40 objective and analyzed via the HALO image analysis platform (Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM, USA). For quantification, the Area Quantification algorithm was applied to five random fields per section to separate DAB (brown) signals. The percentage of DAB-positive tissue area (% DAB positive tissue) was quantified. All analyses were performed by an observer blinded to the experimental groups.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. If data violated the normality assumption, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was applied as a fallback strategy. Longitudinal data were analyzed using a two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test to control for the family-wise error rate. Endpoint comparisons between groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for key endpoint comparisons. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. To avoid overfitting due to the limited sample size, the combined diagnostic model was pre-specified as a dual-parameter logistic regression. Correlations were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r). All analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with a two-sided P<0.05 considered statistically significant. All data analyses were performed by an investigator blinded to the experimental group allocation.

Results

Successful establishment of the PE rat model

Longitudinal monitoring confirmed the successful induction of the PE phenotype. Although baseline (GD7) parameters were comparable between groups, L-NAME administration triggered a rapid and sustained hypertensive response. SBP in the PE group significantly exceeded controls by GD10 (P<0.001) and maintained an elevation of >30 mmHg throughout gestation (Figure 3A). This was accompanied by progressive proteinuria, which increased significantly from GD10 and peaked at GD18 with a ~4-fold rise compared to baseline (Figure 3B).

Spatiotemporal monitoring of placental sO2 via PAI

Regional PAI identified distinct physiological gradients of sO2 across placental subregions. Quantitative analysis revealed that the LZ exhibited the highest sO2 levels, followed by the mesometrial triangle (MT) and the BZ. This distribution pattern is consistent with the anatomical vascularization of the rat placenta (Figure 4A). Longitudinally, although the control group maintained stable oxygenation, the PE group exhibited a progressive hypoxic shift. Significant reductions in mean placental sO2 were observed as early as GD14 (52.2%±5.4% vs. 59.4%±5.1%, P<0.01). This hypoxic trajectory intensified throughout late gestation, reaching a nadir at GD18 (49.4%±4.8% vs. 61.8%±5.4%, P<0.001) (Figure 4B,4C). Notably, the magnitude of SBP elevation at GD10 correlated negatively with GD14 placental sO2 levels (r=−0.62, P<0.01), strengthening the link between systemic maternal disease and local placental hypoxia.

Placental perfusion dynamics assessed by CEUS

CEUS provided real-time visualization of placental hemodynamics. As illustrated in the sequential cine loops, microbubbles initially filled the maternal arterial channel and subsequently perfused the labyrinthine structures, reaching peak intensity at approximately 15 seconds before gradual washout (Figure 5A). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant compromise in placental perfusion in L-NAME-treated rats, characterized by a specific hemodynamic phenotype of “volume-velocity dissociation”. Perfusion metrics related to blood volume (WiAUC, WiR, and WiPI) were significantly reduced as early as GD14 and remained suppressed through GD18 (Figure 5B,5C; Table 1). Notably, peak enhancement (Peak) exhibited a delayed decline; it remained comparable to controls at GD14 but decreased significantly by GD16 and GD18 (P<0.05). In contrast, kinetic transit parameters (RT and TTP) showed no significant differences between groups at any stage.

Correlation between imaging biomarkers and pathological features

Histopathological analysis at GD18 substantiated the quantitative imaging findings. PAI-derived sO2 demonstrated a strong negative correlation with the hypoxia marker HIF-1α (r=−0.73, P<0.005) and a positive correlation with the vascular density marker CD31 (r=0.74, P<0.005), confirming that PAI effectively reflects both hypoxic stress and vascular remodeling (Figure 6A,6B). Regarding perfusion, CEUS-derived parameters showed significant positive correlations with CD31 expression. Notably, volume-related metrics, particularly WiAUC (r=0.68, P<0.005) and WiPI (r=0.65, P<0.005), exhibited the strongest associations, validating their utility in assessing microvascular density (Figure 6C-6G).

Cross-modality analysis further revealed a physiological coupling between oxygenation and perfusion. Placental sO2 was positively correlated with CEUS-derived perfusion volume metrics (WiAUC, WoAUC, Peak, WiR, WiPI; all P<0.05) (Table 2). This relationship supports the hypothesis that placental hypoxia in PE is mechanistically driven by compromised vascular perfusion.

Diagnostic performance of PAI and CEUS parameters

To evaluate the potential utility of these imaging biomarkers, ROC curve analysis was performed. Among individual parameters, placental sO2 at GD16 yielded high discriminatory capability [area under the curve (AUC) =0.91]. To leverage the complementary strengths of metabolic and perfusion imaging, a preliminary dual-parameter logistic regression model was constructed combining sO2 and WiAUC (selected based on their strong pathological correlations). This combined multimodal model demonstrated robust predictive value across different gestational stages. Notably, for early disease prediction at GD14, the model yielded a strong AUC of 0.89. This diagnostic performance peaked at the GD18 endpoint, yielding an AUC of 0.93 for discriminating PE placentas (Figure 7). Despite being derived from a preclinical cohort, these results provide a rationale for integrating functional and hemodynamic metrics to improve diagnostic precision.

Discussion

Placental dysfunction is widely recognized as a core driver of PE pathogenesis. However, current clinical management often relies on late-stage systemic symptoms rather than direct visualization of the underlying placental pathology. To address this gap, we employed a multimodal imaging strategy combining PAI and CEUS to systematically evaluate placental function in an L-NAME-induced rat model. Our findings provide four key insights: (I) PAI revealed a distinct physiological oxygenation gradient (LZ > BZ) and characterized the “progressive hypoxic shift” during PE development; (II) CEUS elucidated that hemodynamic impairment in PE is characterized by a “volume-velocity dissociation”, indicating that microvascular rarefaction may be a dominant feature preceding or accompanying flow velocity changes; (III) imaging biomarkers (sO2 and WiAUC) correlated with pathological features (HIF-1α and CD31), supporting their biological relevance; and (IV) the combined application of PAI and CEUS demonstrated the potential of this multimodal strategy for the longitudinal characterization of placental pathology.

Current non-invasive methods for assessing placental function have advanced significantly. Recent developments in diffusion and perfusion MRI have introduced the remarkable possibility of quantifying in vivo trophoblast perfusion and assessing villous microstructural changes (8,9). These advancements provide crucial insights into placental hemodynamics. Alongside perfusion, evaluating placental oxygenation remains equally critical for understanding PE pathophysiology. Traditional BOLD-MRI relies on the paramagnetic properties of HbR (23). Although effective, BOLD signals are indirect surrogates influenced by blood flow and hematocrit, and the technique can be limited by spatiotemporal constraints (24). To overcome these limitations and directly map tissue-level oxygenation with high spatiotemporal resolution, PAI leverages the intrinsic optical absorption contrast of hemoglobin (11,12). By utilizing multi-wavelength spectral analysis, PAI provides a functional estimation of sO2 (25). A major finding of our study is the visualization of intrinsic oxygenation heterogeneity within the rat placenta. We observed a consistent physiological gradient where the LZ exhibited higher sO2 levels compared to the BZ. This distribution aligns with the functional vascular architecture of the hemochorial placenta: the LZ contains the dense labyrinthine capillary network essential for maternal–fetal exchange, necessitating a high baseline oxygen supply (26,27). The ability of PAI to resolve these sub-regional differences highlights its enhanced spatial resolution compared to near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), which typically provides a global average of tissue oxygenation (28,29).

In the PE model, PAI detected a “progressive hypoxic shift” starting from GD14. This decline was negatively correlated with HIF-1α overexpression (r=−0.73). Unlike the physiological fluctuations seen in normal pregnancy, this sustained hypoxic trajectory suggests that the L-NAME-induced inhibition of NO leads to tissue-level ischemia (30). It is noteworthy that the sO2 values obtained in our study (baseline ~60%) are slightly lower than typical arterial saturation values. This likely stems from the volume-averaging of arterial and venous blood within the PAI voxel, as well as the systemic effects of isoflurane anesthesia (31). Additionally, L-NAME-induced hemoconcentration increases total hemoglobin (HbT) and blood viscosity, which may influence absolute quantification. Although these hemorheological changes and deep-tissue light fluence attenuation pose challenges, the ratiometric calculation of sO2 (HbO2/HbT) helps to minimize such concentration- and intensity-dependent artifacts. Furthermore, since both control and PE groups were imaged under identical protocols with consistent depths, the observed relative reduction serves as a robust indicator of the pathological trend (32,33).

Complementing the metabolic insights from PAI, CEUS provided a detailed characterization of microvascular hemodynamics (10,34). In clinical obstetrics, color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) and pulsed-wave Doppler are standard tools (35). However, these techniques rely on detecting the frequency shift of moving red blood cells. To eliminate tissue motion artifacts, Doppler systems employ “wall filters” that inadvertently suppress low-velocity signals from capillary beds. Consequently, Doppler is excellent for assessing large vessel resistance (e.g., uterine artery pulsatility index) but has limited sensitivity regarding microvascular perfusion (10). Our study overcame this limitation by using CEUS. The gas-filled microbubbles act as intravascular tracers that stay strictly within the vascular space, allowing visualization of flow independent of velocity.

This technical advantage allowed us to elucidate a specific hemodynamic phenotype in PE which we term “volume-velocity dissociation”. We observed that volume-related parameters (WiAUC, WiR, WiPI) decreased significantly as early as GD14, whereas kinetic parameters reflecting flow velocity (RT, TTP) remained unchanged. This dissociation is mechanistically insightful, implying that the primary vascular defect in this PE model is structural ‘vascular rarefaction’—a physical reduction in the density of the functional capillary bed—rather than a generalized slowing of inflow velocity. Theoretical principles of microbubble kinetics establish that the backscatter signal intensity (reflected by WiAUC) is linearly proportional to the fractional blood volume (36), corroborating that the observed signal loss reflects a reduction in vascular cross-sectional area. Functionally, this “pruning” of the microvasculature increases vascular resistance downstream, a hallmark of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, without necessarily altering the transit time in the remaining patent vessels (37). Importantly, because CD31 stains endothelial biomass rather than the lumen, the reduced CD31-positive area confirms true structural rarefaction rather than mere vasoconstriction, which positively correlated with WiAUC (r=0.68). Collectively, these findings suggest that clinical assessment strategies focusing solely on flow velocity (Doppler indices) may miss microvascular dropouts, whereas indicators reflecting “microvascular volume” (such as WiAUC) could serve as more sensitive indicators (24).

The integration of PAI and CEUS addresses the inherent limitations of single-modality imaging. PAI focuses on “functional oxygenation” (11), whereas CEUS characterizes “microvascular architecture” (vascular density) (38). Theoretically, tissue oxygenation and perfusion can uncouple; for instance, oxygenation may be preserved despite reduced flow if extraction efficiency increases. However, in our PE model, we confirmed a significant positive coupling between the two (sO2 positively correlated with WiAUC), suggesting that the observed hypoxia is indeed “supply-dependent”—driven primarily by the loss of vascular volume. ROC analysis demonstrated that the dual-parameter strategy achieved a robust AUC of 0.93 at GD18. By integrating a marker of hypoxic stress (sO2) with a marker of perfusion volume (WiAUC), this multimodal approach provides a comprehensive evaluation of placental health (39).

A consideration for translation is the safety profile of contrast agents. Unlike computed tomography (CT) or MRI contrast agents which can cross the placental barrier, the sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles (SonoVue) used in CEUS are large (2–10 μm) and strictly intravascular, theoretically preventing direct fetal exposure. Previous studies in primates have shown no adverse fetal hemodynamic effects, and retrospective analyses in humans support its safety profile in pregnancy (40,41). Although current guidelines urge caution, the diagnostic potential demonstrated in our study argues for the continued evaluation of CEUS in high-risk pregnancies, particularly as clearer safety data emerges. Regarding PAI translation, transabdominal access is most realistic for anterior placentas in low-body mass index (BMI) patients, where depth can be as shallow as 1–2 cm (42). Technically, shifting to NIR-II wavelengths (e.g., 1,064 nm) and advanced fluence compensation will be essential to ensure sO2 reliability despite maternal tissue attenuation. Future technical developments, such as optimized transvaginal or transabdominal hybrid probes, could facilitate the clinical translation of this method, providing a potential tool for the functional assessment of placental microcirculation in human pregnancies. Furthermore, advanced MRI techniques, particularly the diffusion-derived vessel density (DDVD) biomarker, have recently demonstrated high clinical performance in non-invasively assessing placental vascularity and distinguishing early PE from normal pregnancies (43,44).

Despite these promising results, this study has limitations. First, structural differences between the rat hemotrichorial labyrinth and human hemochorial villous placenta—particularly regarding the number of trophoblast layers and spatial organization—necessitate caution when extrapolating sub-regional oxygenation findings to clinical contexts (45). Second, although we standardized the PAI transducer depth (~9 mm), the lack of real-time fluence compensation—compounded by potential tissue edema—limits the absolute accuracy of deep-tissue sO2 measurements. Future studies should incorporate advanced algorithms, such as Monte Carlo-based light transport models or diffuse optical tomography, to ensure more precise fluence correction. Third, the L-NAME model may not fully recapitulate the complex immunological landscape of human PE. Fourth, the lack of significant differences in kinetic transit parameters (RT, TTP) may be jointly attributed to the capillary-preserving vasodilatory effects of isoflurane and the inherently lower temporal resolution of our clinical-grade ultrasound probe compared to dedicated preclinical systems. Finally, the absence of corresponding fetal outcome data (e.g., birth weight and viability) limits our ability to directly link these imaging biomarkers with perinatal morbidity.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that the combined application of PAI and CEUS provides a quantitative, non-invasive assessment of placental hypoxia and vascular dysfunction. We validated sO2 and WiAUC as sensitive biomarkers that accurately reflect the underlying histopathology. This multimodal imaging strategy not only deepens our understanding of PE pathophysiology but also serves as a valuable platform for the longitudinal monitoring of placental insufficiency and the objective evaluation of therapeutic efficacy in preclinical research.
