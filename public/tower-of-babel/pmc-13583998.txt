Retroperitoneal schwannoma: A rare entity with diagnostic challenges
Authors: Kays Chaker, Ayda Khadhar, Sadok Hmouda, Afif Limem, Ines Chelly, Yassine Nouira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetroperitoneal schwannomas are rare benign tumors arising from peripheral nerve sheath cells and are often diagnosed incidentally due to their nonspecific clinical presentation. We report the case of a 37-year-old woman presenting with isolated right-sided lower back pain caused by a compressive right retroperitoneal mass. The patient underwent complete surgical excision, and definitive histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of a retroperitoneal schwannoma.Keywords: Schwannomas, Retroperitoneal, Surgical excision

Introduction

Retroperitoneal schwannomas are rare benign tumors arising from Schwann cells of peripheral nerves, representing an uncommon subset of retroperitoneal masses and often presenting as incidental findings due to their slow growth and nonspecific symptoms. Their diagnosis remains challenging because imaging features are frequently nonspecific and may overlap with other retroperitoneal neoplasms, requiring histopathological confirmation for definitive diagnosis.1,2 Complete surgical excision is considered the treatment of choice, with careful dissection required to preserve adjacent vascular and neural structures and achieve favorable long-term outcomes.1,2

Case presentation

A 37-year-old woman, gravida 2 para 2, without significant medical history, presented with isolated right-sided lower back pain that had been evolving for 6 months. Clinical examination reveals a BMI of 25 kg/m2, mild right lumbar tenderness, and a palpable mass. The general examination found a blood pressure of 110/70 mmHg and a pulse of 80 bpm. The physical examination found a palpable mass in the right lumbar fossa, and no low limb edema. The urine cytobacteriological examination was negative. She had a hemoglobin level of 12.6 g/dl, a calcium level of 2.13 mmol/l, and a creatinine level of 7 mg/l. The results of a complete blood count and plasma levels of fasting glucose, cholesterol, sodium, potassium and phosphorus were unremarkable. The patient underwent a computed tomography scan, which revealed the presence of a heterogeneous right retroperitoneal soft-tissue mass with mild enhancement, exerting a mass effect on the adjacent structures measuring 130*78*71 mm. It exhibits regular, well-defined margins and exerts a mass effect on adjacent organs without evidence of direct invasion. It maintains close contact with the renal vein along the anterior aspect of its upper pole (Fig. 1). Urinary methoxylated catecholamine metabolites (metanephrines and normetanephrines) were within the normal range. The patient underwent a CT-guided core needle biopsy of the retroperitoneal mass using an 18-gauge coaxial needle. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of retroperitoneal schwannoma (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemical analysis showed that the tumor cells were negative for CD117 and DOG1, arguing against a gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST). The immunohistochemical profile, together with the morphological features, was consistent with a diagnosis of a retroperitoneal schwannoma. The case was discussed at the multidisciplinary team meeting. The patient underwent an open tumorectomy by a right subcostal (Kocher) incision. Exploration of the retroperitoneal space revealed a highly vascularized mass with intimate contact and dense adherence to the inferior vena cava. Given the significant vascularity of the lesion and its proximity to this major vessel, meticulous dissection was undertaken using a combination of sharp and blunt techniques. Sequential identification and secure ligation of the tumor's arterial and venous feeding branches enabled progressive mobilization while maintaining strict hemostatic control. Particular attention was paid to preserving the integrity of the inferior vena cava throughout the procedure. Following complete circumferential dissection, the mass was successfully excised en bloc with negative macroscopic margins, without vascular injury or excessive intraoperative bleeding. The right kidney was successfully preserved throughout the procedure. The surgical margins were clear. The definitive morphological examination of the surgical specimen was consistent with a schwannoma (Fig. 2). After 18 months of clinical and radiological check-up, there was no functional complaint or any sign of reccurrence.

Histological examination shows a benign spindle-cell tumor composed of alternating hypercellular Antoni A areas and hypocellular Antoni B areas. The Antoni A areas are composed of closely packed, slender spindle cells with scant cytoplasm and regular, monomorphic nuclei. The tumor cells are arranged in interlacing fascicles and show characteristic nuclear palisading, forming Verocay bodies (A). The Antoni B areas are more loosely cellular, with a less organized arrangement of spindle cells in a loose, myxoid or edematous stroma. The overall histological features are consistent with a benign retroperitoneal schwannoma (B).

Discussion

Retroperitoneal schwannomas are uncommon benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors, accounting for a very small proportion of retroperitoneal.1 They originate from Schwann cells and usually develop from autonomic or peripheral nerves located within the retroperitoneal space. Because of the large available space in this anatomical compartment, these tumors may remain asymptomatic for a prolonged period and are often diagnosed incidentally or when they reach a significant size causing compression of adjacent organs. Recent reports have emphasized the rarity of this entity and the frequent absence of specific clinical manifestations, which contributes to diagnostic challenges.2 Radiological evaluation plays a crucial role in the characterization of retroperitoneal schwannomas; however, preoperative diagnosis remains difficult because imaging findings may overlap with those of other benign or malignant retroperitoneal tumors. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging may demonstrate a well-circumscribed, encapsulated mass with heterogeneous enhancement, cystic degeneration, or areas of hemorrhage, particularly in large or ancient schwannomas.3,4 In the present case, the main diagnostic and therapeutic decisions were driven by the discrepancy between the radiological appearance and the final pathological diagnosis. Although the lesion was well circumscribed and appeared relatively well defined on imaging, its retroperitoneal location and relationship with adjacent structures made it difficult to establish a specific diagnosis preoperatively. This illustrates an important practical point: imaging can suggest a schwannoma but rarely provides sufficient specificity to reliably distinguish it from other retroperitoneal neoplasms, including sarcomas, neurogenic tumors, and metastatic lesions. Therefore, the decision to proceed with surgical excision was based not only on the presence of a large retroperitoneal mass but also on the need to obtain a definitive diagnosis while achieving local control. Another important consideration in this case was the surgical strategy. The close anatomical relationship between the tumor and surrounding vascular, ureteral, and neural structures required careful preoperative assessment and meticulous intraoperative dissection. When a retroperitoneal schwannoma is suspected, the surgical objective should be complete tumor excision while preserving adjacent organs and neurovascular structures whenever possible. In particular, attempting to obtain wide oncological margins in a lesion that ultimately proves to be a benign schwannoma may result in unnecessary morbidity. Conversely, incomplete excision may increase the risk of local recurrence. Thus, the operative approach should be individualized according to tumor size, anatomical location, relationship with major structures, and the surgeon's ability to achieve complete and safe resection. The pathological findings in this case were also critical in resolving the diagnostic uncertainty. The combination of characteristic Antoni A and Antoni B areas, Verocay bodies, and strong S-100 immunoreactivity supported the diagnosis of schwannoma and excluded several important differential diagnoses.3 This emphasizes the complementary role of radiology and pathology: imaging defines the anatomical and surgical problem, whereas histopathology and immunohistochemistry establish the definitive diagnosis. From an educational perspective, this case highlights that a benign-appearing retroperitoneal mass should not automatically be assumed to be a schwannoma, but that schwannoma should remain an important differential diagnosis when imaging demonstrates a well-circumscribed neurogenic-appearing lesion. Careful preoperative planning and a conservative, structure-preserving surgical strategy can allow complete excision while minimizing unnecessary morbidity. Surgical resection remains the treatment of choice for symptomatic or enlarging retroperitoneal schwannomas, as it provides both definitive diagnosis and effective local control. The main surgical challenge is the close relationship of these tumors with major vessels, ureters, and neural structures, requiring meticulous dissection to avoid neurological or vascular complications. Minimally invasive approaches, including laparoscopic or robotic surgery, have been increasingly reported in selected patients with favorable outcomes when complete resection is feasible.1,3,4 Although benign retroperitoneal schwannomas generally have an excellent prognosis after complete removal, long-term follow-up is recommended because rare cases of recurrence or malignant transformation have been described. It is important to distinguish conventional benign schwannoma from malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumor (MMNST), a distinct entity recognized in the current WHO classification.5 MMNST, historically referred to as melanotic schwannoma, is characterized by melanocytic differentiation and a distinct molecular profile and should not be considered synonymous with conventional schwannoma. This distinction is clinically relevant because MMNST carries a risk of local recurrence and metastatic dissemination, whereas conventional retroperitoneal schwannomas are generally benign and have an excellent prognosis following complete excision.5 Recent literature continues to highlight the importance of considering schwannoma in the differential diagnosis of retroperitoneal masses to avoid unnecessary aggressive procedures and optimize patient management.6

Conclusion

Retroperitoneal schwannoma is a rare benign peripheral nerve sheath tumor that should be included in the differential diagnosis of well-circumscribed retroperitoneal masses. Although imaging findings may suggest the diagnosis, definitive confirmation relies on histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation. Complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice, providing both diagnostic confirmation and favorable long-term outcomes with a low risk of recurrence.
