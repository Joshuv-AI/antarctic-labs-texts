On early-warning of full versus partial Atlantic overturning circulation collapse
Johannes Lohmann

Abstract
Climate models indicate a possible collapse of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) even for moderate climate change scenarios. There is considerable uncertainty in its likelihood for a given scenario and the critical global warming threshold. An alternative are early-warning signals (EWS) in AMOC fingerprints, which leverage generic statistical properties before destabilization of a steady state (a saddle-node bifurcation). But an AMOC collapse may be a sequence of partial collapses with shutdown of deep water formation in distinct regions. A conceptual model is presented featuring a sequential shutdown in two deep water formation regions. Multiple tipping points are present that do not follow the saddle-node normal form. As a result, the choice of the observable used to monitor EWS dramatically influences the prediction of the collapse via EWS. Various trends in EWS for different observables make it hard to determine what type of collapse (partial or full) will follow.



I
Introduction

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is a major pattern in the climate system and a large contributor to the global meridional heat transport, carrying 1.2 PW of heat northward across the subtropics
38
. A collapse of the AMOC would lead to dramatic decreases in temperature especially in Northern Europe, while warming the Southern Hemisphere and producing various cascading impacts
37
.
This possibility was pointed out by Stommel in 1961
73
, where a bistability of the AMOC was shown in a conceptual model as a result of the competing positive salt-advection and negative temperature-advection feedbacks. In the model, the strength of the circulation is determined by the opposing influence of atmospheric freshwater and temperature forcing on the meridional density gradient. As the forcing changes, a saddle-node bifurcation can occur where the present-day state loses stability and the circulation enters a weak salinity-driven state. This is a bifurcation-induced tipping point (TP)
6
.

Presently, the atmospheric forcing gradients onto the Atlantic are changing due to polar amplification of global warming, as well as increased glacial ice melt and changing precipitation patterns. Both increased warming and freshening of northern Atlantic surface waters could lead to a shutoff of convection and thereby the AMOC’s current downwelling path. Due the likely existence of the salt-advection feedback - consistent with the observation that the Atlantic imports salt
64
;
3
, whereby an initial AMOC weakening would be amplified in a self-reinforcing way by decreasing surface salinity in downwelling regions - this may happen abruptly and irreversibly.
The possibility for AMOC tipping is strongly supported by abrupt North Atlantic climate changes seen in paleoclimate records of the last glacial period
65
;
32
.

I.1
Observational and model evidence for AMOC slowdown and collapse

A direct and continuous monitoring of the strength of the AMOC has only been in place for two decades. The RAPID-MOCHA array (at 26.5
∘
N, since 2004) and the OSNAP array (55-60
∘
N, since 2014) show considerable variability (Fig.

Five hydrographic sections in the period 1957-2004 give snapshot measurements that do indicate an AMOC decline

, and neither does a model-assisted reconstruction based on air-sea heat fluxes of the past 60 years

Climate models show on average no AMOC weakening when simulating the historical period

Model averages remove internal variability, and it has been argued using ensemble simulations with a single model that individual simulations showing an AMOC weakening best match observational constraints, meaning that a weakening may have indeed occurred, but may have been pure centennial internal variability

Most model projections into the future show a significant AMOC decline as a result of accelerating global warming
85
. Due to model biases, which result in a spread in present-day AMOC strength ranging from 9 to 25 Sv

, models differ substantially in the magnitude of this decline

.
However, given the long time scales of ocean dynamics, the decline until 2100 in CMIP6 is still a transient response. It has become clear that models with extended simulations until 2300 or longer can in fact transition to a state where North Atlantic deep water formation ceases and the AMOC becomes very shallow and weak, with downwelling restricted to the subtropics
67
;
26

26
.
The uncertainty remains high even if all models would agree, as can be seen by ensemble simulations with a single model where some ensemble members in intermediate scenarios
reach a weak AMOC state and other do not
67
;
26
.

Thus, from current models one cannot exclude an AMOC shutdown for all emission scenarios due to global warming alone, where the shutdown of convection is likely initiated by the effect of thermal forcing on buoyancy and subsequent salt-advection feedback
26
.
The risk is increased by accerelating Greenland melt
8
, which is missing in most projections and comes with its own uncertainty as estimated by ice sheet models
4
;
34
.
In comparison to current and estimated future melt rates, the freshwater forcing needed for an AMOC shutdown are quite high and model dependent. Across models, a forcing of 0.3 Sv seems to initiate an AMOC shutdown while 0.1 Sv applied around Greenland does not
36
, even though simulation times are too short to assess long-term stability. Full collapses were identified in quasi-steady state simulations with different models using 0.2 Sv

and 0.5 Sv

;
83

Still, it is uncertain whether Greenland melt will reach these levels and can be sustained long enough to complete an AMOC shutdown. Neglecting changes in accumulation, at 0.1 Sv all of the 2.74 mio Gt Greenland ice would be melted after 870 years, and after 290 years at 0.3 Sv. Ice sheet model simulations forced with high-emission temperatures indicate a complete Greenland melt with average and peak discharge of about 0.07 Sv and 0.16 Sv, respectively
4
.
On the other hand, the true freshwater and global warming thresholds may be lower than indicated by current models. Models may be biased towards stability

, in particular since the salt-advection feedback may be weaker than in reality

;

;
79
;

I.2
Critical slowing down as alternative evidence for AMOC collapse

Overall, there is ample evidence for the possibility of an AMOC collapse under plausible future emission scenarios, but the abovementioned uncertainties make it difficult to give quantitative estimates of AMOC tipping thresholds and likelihoods. It is thus appealing to follow an independent line of evidence: Precursor signals of destabilization in dynamical systems. These arise due to critical slowing down (CSD) before bifurcations, and can be measured by statistical early-warning signals (EWS)
87
;
68
. If the AMOC system can be viewed as a dynamical system forced by stochastic atmospheric perturbations and driven towards a saddle-node bifurcation by anthropogenic emissions, then an impending AMOC collapse should be detectable by increasing variance and autocorrelation of fluctuations of an observable, given this observable measures the relevant AMOC fluctuations in a stationary way. The term observable is used here either loosely as any quantity that can be measured, or more specifically as a function of a subset of variables in particular governing equations.
Assessing the proximity of AMOC tipping by EWS would bypass the issues of climate model bias and resolution, and the unknown current trend in AMOC strength, which may not even be indicative of an upcoming collapse since from a dynamical systems view the AMOC strength can change in a non-linear way or may even increase
72
.

While this has been attempted before with the conclusion that the AMOC is indeed approaching a TP
14
;
52
;
25
, it comes with its own subtantial caveats. Since direct AMOC measurements are too short, fingerprints such as in Fig.
1
a must be used. For EWS these are analyzed not in terms of their decreasing trend, but in terms of increases in variability, and it is debated how well the fingerprints capture AMOC variability
75
. Biases in EWS can arise when the fingerprint recording is not stationary, for instance due to changes in measurement coverage and quality over time
11
. Since a whole spectrum of fingerprints may be used, inferred trends in EWS come with an uncertainty that is hard to quantify
10
. Further, assumptions on the noise process need to be verified and corrected for, in particular regarding its memory
55
, stationarity
56
and state-dependence
54
.

I.3
This paper

Here, more fundamental issues are discussed that arise for EWS even if perfect data, e.g., for the AMOC transport at a certain latitude, is available.
First, an observable that is good at summarizing the overall system state is not guaranteed to show significant EWS
12
;
45
. This also means that EWS-based extrapolations to predict the time of tipping
25
are highly dependent on the observable used, since the required scaling of variance or autocorrelation only arises when the observable obeys the saddle-node
normal form
, which may not happen until arbitrarily close to the TP.
Indeed, models of different complexity show an AMOC collapse where the AMOC strength does not display CSD
80
;
45
. Special observables, which may be counterintuitive, need to be obtained
either from physical
80
or dynamical systems arguments
47
;
44
, or by analyzing models
71
even though it remains to be seen how representative
individual models are.

Second, the interpretation of EWS is not straightforward.
While models and paleoclimate data show convincinly that the AMOC can collapse, it is less clear whether this is a result of a single TP corresponding to a saddle-node bifurcation due to the salt-advection feedback. Instead, a collapse may be initiated locally, e.g., in the Labrador sea, and by a different positive feedback, such as Welander’s convective feedback
86
.
As shown in Fig.
1
b, the convection that consitutes the AMOC downwelling path is distributed among different locations. These are subject to different evolving boundary conditions: There is larger Greenland meltwater input in the Irminger-Labrador seas compared to the Nordic seas
9
, and larger heat loss in the Nordic seas
24
.
Thus, a local shutdown of convection may happen at different global warming levels. The convection in the Labrador-Irminger seas is coupled to the subpolar gyre, which has been suggested to lead to yet another positive feedback mechanism
17
. Several climate models show abrupt transitions in the subpolar gyre
69
;
74
, which may be considered a separate tipping element. Prior to an AMOC collapse there can be further intermediate TPs related to changes in spatio-temporal patterns of the flow
43
.

As a result of such potential sequences of tipping events, EWS indicators may exhibit several jumps with non-monotonic behaviour in between
43
. Conversely, a monotonic EWS trend indicative of a TP generally does not inform whether it will be a full AMOC collapse, a localized transition, or a change in spatio-temporal mode. This may only be possible when there is a choice among a selection of possible TPs in isolated sub-systems, where the nature of the observables showing CSD distinctly points towards a particular sub-system. But in the present case the different types of possible TPs highlighted above should affect the variability in an overlapping set of observables, for instance the AMOC strength.

These difficulties in anticipating and quantitatively predicting an AMOC collapse from EWS, as well in distinguishing whether it is a complete or partial collapse are demonstrated here with a minimal conceptual model. A Stommel-type box model is presented, where a well-mixed subtropical Atlantic ocean box is connected to two distinct but coupled northern Atlantic boxes with heterogeneous forcing, representing the Irminger-Labrador and Nordic seas, respectively.
The structure of the paper is as follows. In Sec.
II
the model and its bifurcation structure is presented. Sec.
III
discusses the EWS found across observables for different TPs in the model. The possibility for quantitative prediction and its dependence on observables and a partial versus a full collapse is analyzed in Sec.
IV
. Discussion and conclusions are given in Sec.
V
.

III
Statistical early-warning signals

Statistical EWS arise due to critical slowing down, where before a saddle-node bifurcation the relaxation dynamics towards the stable fixed point become very slow along one particular degree of freedom.
This can be understood as a flattening of the potential
V_{\mu}(x)
(or more generally quasipotential
29
) governing the dynamics
\dot{x}=-\partial_{x}V_{\mu}(x)
, as visualized for one dimension in Fig.
5
.
A stable fixed point is a well/minimum in
V_{\mu}(x)
, and an unstable one a hill/maximum or more generally a saddle point. At the TP
\mu_{c}
, minimum and maximum collide and annihilate.
Far from the TP, the potential well is steep, and thus a relaxation towards the stable fixed point (e.g. after a small perturbation) is very fast. The well gets more shallow and flat as the bifurcation is approached, and accordingly the relaxation becomes slower. Shortly before the TP, the potential is almost perfectly flat, and the relaxation becomes arbitrarily slow with a restoring rate
\lambda
going to zero.

Figure 5:

Changing potential landscape
V_{\mu}(x)
for a one-dimensional system described by the saddle-node normal form
\dot{x}=-\partial_{x}V_{\mu}(x)=x^{2}+\mu
. As
\mu
is increased, the stable node (red) and the saddle point (green triangles) approach each other, and at the critical value
\mu_{c}=0
they collide and no more fixed point exists.
If the system is perturbed away from the stable node, it takes increasingly long to relax back (purple balls). When driven by small, random perturbations, the fluctuations around the stable node become increasingly large and correlated (see red timeseries).

This CSD may be detected in data, assuming that real-world complex systems are subject to random perturbations from their environment. These could be for instance small-scale atmospheric anomalies that perturb the ocean circulation. Their effect on the system state will grow as the TP is approached (red trajectories in Fig.
5

One can thus measure increases in the variance and autocorrelation of the fluctuations around the slowly evolving mean state in observational time series. These are statistical EWS.

For the conceptual model considered here this only happens on the PC branch and not on the ON branch serving as analogue of the present-day AMOC. But in general we cannot exclude that in the past centuries the Atlantic ocean circulation has experienced bifurcations, and hence that the present-day state is also bounded by a bifurcation going back in time (at reduced anthropogenic forcing). IV
Prediction of tipping times by extrapolation

The previous section showed that in principle - depending on the observable - any possible trend in EWS may be seen when approaching a TP. Next it is shown that this renders the extrapolation of a trend in variance or autocorrelation to predict when tipping will occur rather precarious.

To do such an extrapolation, one can exploit that towards a saddle-node bifurcation the restoring rate
\lambda
in the critical degree of freedom, with which the system relaxes to the fixed point, goes to zero. However, three conditions need to be met. First,
\lambda
needs to be reconstructed from statistical EWS in some observable. Second, the observable needs to project well on the critical degree of freedom.
Third, it needs to be known how
\lambda
scales as a function of the control parameter
\mu
, such that one can extrapolate to where
\lambda
reaches 0 as
\mu
crosses the critical value.
Previous studies, which predicted tipping of the Greenland ice sheet
13
and the AMOC
25
from observational data, implicitly assumed these three conditions to hold by positing that whatever observable is measured should obey the saddle-node normal form, i.e., obey the one-dimensional dynamics of the critical degree of freedom on the extended center manifold, and that it did so for the entire period of historical observations.
The saddle-node normal form (with additive noise) is given by

dx_{t}=(x_{t}^{2}-\mu)dt+\sigma dW_{t},

(8)

where
\mu=0
demarcates the bifurcation. The system can be linearized around the stable fixed point
x*=-\sqrt{\mu}
, and approximated by the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
d\tilde{x}_{t}=-\lambda\tilde{x}_{t}dt+\sigma dW_{t}
.
The linear restoring rate is
\lambda=-\partial_{x}(x^{2}-\mu)|_{x=x*}=2\sqrt{\mu}
.
This linearized, reversible stochastic process can be discretized, which gives the desired link
of the linear restoring rate and statistical EWS as measured from discrete time series samples.
Specifically, data sampled at small time intervals
\Delta_{t}
can be approximated by an AR(1) process

X_{k+1}=e^{-\lambda\Delta t}X_{k}+\epsilon_{k},

(9)

where
\epsilon_{k}
are Gaussian random variables with variance
\sigma^{2}(2\lambda)^{-1}(1-e^{-2\lambda\Delta t})
. For this process the lag-1 autocorrelation is given by
\rho_{1}=e^{-\lambda\Delta t}
. Since for the normal form
\lambda=2\sqrt{\mu}
, we can reconstruct
\mu
from data by

\mu=\lambda^{2}/2=\left(\frac{\ln\rho_{1}}{2\Delta t}\right)^{2}.

(10)

Thus, for a single time series one can estimate
\rho_{1}
in a sliding window as a function of time, and, assuming a linear trend in
\mu
, estimate with a linear fit to the function on the righthand-side at what time
\mu
will cross zero, which is exactly when the autocorrelation tends to 1 at the saddle-node bifurcation.

V
Discussion

This paper presents a conceptual model as a minimal example for how spatially distributed deep convection leads to different possible pathways of an AMOC collapse that depend on the heterogeneity of the atmospheric forcing, and how from EWS alone it is difficult to assess when a tipping will occur and what state it will lead to (partial or full AMOC collapse). The following was demonstrated:

1.

Existence and strength of EWS is observable-dependent. Observables that seem obvious (as shown here for the AMOC strength
q
) can perform poorly. Physical knowledge
80
;
71
or the design of observables from dynamical systems principles (edge states
45
) or operator-theoretic arguments
49
;
44
is required.

2.

The AMOC as a spatially extended, heterogeneous system may display higher multistability. The simplest case is tri-stability due to two convection sites, as shown here and similarly in
58
.

3.

Multistability can lead to intermediate TPs
43
with partial AMOC collapse. However, as in scenario II, an AMOC collapse can also bypass partially collapsed states. EWS signify loss of
local
stability (in phase space) and, unless special observables are known beforehand, lack global information to indicate whether the collapse is full or partial.

4.

A given branch of stable states can be bounded on either side by a TP, resulting in non-monotonic EWS. This cannot be excluded for the present-day AMOC.

5.

The normal form assumption, used for extrapolating EWS towards the expected time of tipping, may only hold when measuring (unknown) special observables or being very close to the TP.
Even if one knows roughly which variables are relevant (e.g. box 1), different observables give different estimates of the time of tipping. This structural statistical uncertainty on the estimate may be unbounded unless there are arguments to exclude certain types of observables.

The model is not intended to inform about the likelihood of future AMOC scenarios, or to argue for or against certain observables to be used as EWS. It serves to illustrate how going up one step in complexity from Stommel’s model already poses issues for the interpretation of EWS, should they be observed in data. While there is clear evidence for heterogeneity in atmospheric forcing
9
;
24
, it is not argued here whether scenario I or II is more likely in reality, since this depends on the interpretation of the model boundary conditions and the definition of box 1 and box 2.

AMOC complexity and associated challenges for the interpretation of EWS are plausibly further enhanced for several reasons. First, the array of convection sites that can collapse non-simultaneously could be further fragmented, noting, e.g., the different strenghts and mechanisms of deep-water formation in the Labrador and Irminger seas
61
;
60
.
Second, the presented model only encodes the salt-advection feedback, whereas other positive feedbacks such as the convective
86
and subpolar gyre feedbacks
17
may be present, which could yield further pathways to a partial or full AMOC collapse that are difficult to distinguish from EWS and naive observables.

Third, instead of collapsing, deep water formation could migrate
1
. It has been argued that downwelling in the Atlantic cannot fully collapse unless it is replaced by downwelling elsewhere to balance the (unchanged) Southern ocean wind-driven upwelling
7
. This could be by development of a Pacific meridional overturning, which in current climate models only happens to some degree under extreme global warming
7
.
Alternatively, deep water formation may move northwards, and there is observational support for this due to “Atlantification” of the Arctic
2
. This could represent tipping to a new state, comprising abrupt sea ice change and the onset of convection at new sites, or a more gradual process acting as stabilizing feedback to prevent an AMOC collapse. While CMIP6 models nevertheless show transitions to states with very weak AMOC and only rarely develop Arctic deep convection
33
, they may not represent the Atlantification well enough yet
57
.

Further factors may influence the tipping behaviour of the AMOC, which are not fully accounted for in most model projections. Increased Antarctic meltwater could weaken the effect of Greenland melting on the AMOC
70
. An AMOC slowdown
may also reduce freshwater forcing from Greenland due to the decrease in heat transport
62
. Further, Indian ocean warming may provide a stabilizing feedback for the AMOC
35
.
Taking together the various alternative feedbacks and possible alternative configurations of the AMOC mentioned above, it is not clear whether the AMOC collapses in CMIP6 models under freshwater and global warming forcing can be attributed to a singular TP due to the salt-advection feedback alone. As a result, it is unclear what kind of transition the detection of EWS in any real-world observable would indicate.

A recent study does provide an argument as to why the mean AMOC strength in a CMIP6-class model should approximately scale as the square-root of the freshwater forcing acting as control parameter
82
, which would be the hallmark (though not necessarily proof) of a saddle-node normal form. However, it is unclear whether it is consistent that the same observable in this model does not display CSD. It would be interesting to investigate whether other models support the scaling, and whether other arguments and approximations could support a different scaling, also when considering temperature as control parameter. It would also be helpful to clarify to what degree state-of-the-art models feature deterministic chaotic oscillations in the ocean dynamics (e.g. Atlantic multidecadal variability not driven by atmospheric variability), which would challenge the saddle-node bifurcation picture. Under chaotic oscillations the AMOC collapse TP would be a
boundary crisis

59
, which may still be associated with CSD, but one needs to take care in finding robust observables for EWS in this case
44
, especially since it is a priori unknown how oscillatory modes will change as an AMOC collapse is approached
43
.

In conclusion, EWS are an appealing option to complement the assessment of the risk and critical forcing for an AMOC collapse, given current observational and modeling uncertainties. Their interpretability is, however, limited since it is unclear which observables are robust and representative, and because they do not indicate what system state is reached at the bifurcation. One may attempt to rationalize which aspect of the circulation will collapse based on the difference in EWS of various observables, but there may nevertheless be situations (as in the scenario II here) where this can fail. The non-normal form behavior of observables and possible non-monotonicity of EWS challenges the possibility for quantitative prediction of an AMOC collapse via extrapolation of EWS.
