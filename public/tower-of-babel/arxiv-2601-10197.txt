On the average-case complexity of learning states from the circular and Gaussian ensembles
Maxwell West 1,2

Abstract
. Studying the complexity of states sampled from various ensembles is a central component of quantum information theory. In this work we establish the average-case hardness of learning, in the statistical query model, the Born distributions of states sampled uniformly from the circular and (fermionic) Gaussian ensembles. These ensembles of states are induced variously by the uniform measures on the compact symmetric spaces of type AI, AII, and DIII. This finding complements analogous recent results for states sampled from the classical compact groups. On the technical side, we employ a somewhat unconventional approach to integrating over the compact groups which may be of some independent interest. For example, our approach allows us to exactly evaluate the total variation distances between the output distributions of Haar random unitary and orthogonal circuits and the constant distribution, which were previously known only approximately.

Having introduced the ensembles we will consider, we can state our main result:

Theorem 1
.

(Informal).

The output distributions of circuits drawn from the circular and fermionic Gaussian ensembles are average-case hard to learn.

2.1.
Statistical query learning

As forewarned, our results are phrased within the framework of
statistical query learning
; we now briefly review the aspects necessary to understand the paper. We largely follow, though abridge, the treatment of Ref.
[
6
]
(to which the interested reader is referred for further details). Statistical query learning is concerned with the learning of distributions via access to the expectation values of functions on their support (as opposed, say, to access to samples from the distribution itself). Concretely, for a distribution
P
on a set
\mathcal{X}
and a tolerance
\tau\geqslant 0
, the
statistical query oracle

\mathsf{Stat}_{\tau}^{P}
is a function

(1)

\mathsf{Stat}_{\tau}^{P}:(\varphi:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1])\mapsto v

such that
\absolutevalue{\expect_{x\sim P}\varphi(x)-v}_{x\sim P}\varphi(x)-v\leq\tau
. That is, one gives the oracle a bounded function
\varphi
, and the oracle returns its average over the distribution, up to an additive tolerance
\tau
. Naturally, one is interested in the number of queries to
\mathsf{Stat}_{\tau}^{P}
necessary to identify
P
(perhaps given, say, that
P
belongs to some set
\mathcal{D}
of distributions on
\mathcal{X}
). Formally, given some fixed values of
\tau,\varepsilon
, we will consider the problem of
\varepsilon
-learning
\mathcal{D}
to be that of, given access to the
\mathsf{Stat}_{\tau}^{P}
corresponding to some unknown

, outputting a function

that is
\varepsilon
-close to
P
in
total variation distance
, where

(2)

{\rm d}_{\rm TV}(P,Q):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|P(x)-Q(x)\right|.

We will be interested in the (deterministic
2
2

2

As opposed to the situation where the learning algorithm itself may employ randomness

\mathcal{D}

of distributions and a success fraction

By the above discussion, it is evident that the integration of polynomial functions of the matrix elements of unitaries sampled from the classical compact symmetric spaces reduces to the integration of a polynomial (of doubled order) of matrix elements of unitaries sampled from the parent group; at which point one can rely on the well-known Weingarten calculus on the classical compact groups. As an explicit example of this that we will later use, one has:

Lemma 3
.

For any

\expect_{V\sim\mu_{\rm AI}}VAV^{\dagger}=\frac{\tr[A]\mathds{1}+A^{\mathsf{T}}}{d+1}

\displaystyle\expect_{V\sim{\rm AI}}VAV^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\expect_{U\sim\mathbb{U}}\left[U^{\mathsf{T}}UA(U^{\mathsf{T}}U)^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\expect_{U\sim\mathbb{U}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[height]{ai_1.pdf}}}}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\expect_{U\sim\mathbb{U}}\tr_{2,3}\left[(\mathds{1}\otimes U^{\otimes 2})(\outerproduct{\Om}{\Om}\otimes A)(\mathds{1}\otimes(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes 2})(\mathds{1}\otimes\outerproduct{\Om}{\Om})\right]

\displaystyle=d^{2}\expect_{U\sim\mathbb{U}}\tr_{1,2}\left[(J\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes J)(U^{\otimes 2}\otimes\mathds{1})(A\otimes\outerproduct{\Phi}{\Phi})((U^{\dagger})^{\otimes 2}\otimes\mathds{1})(J\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes J)(\outerproduct{\Phi}{\Phi}\otimes\mathds{1})\right]

∎

.

2.4.
Concentration of measure

An important property of sampling from the classical groups is their
concentration of measure
; informally, if
f:G\to\mathbb{R}
is “well-behaved” then with high probability evaluating
f
on a random
U\sim\mu_{G}
will produce a value extremely close to the average value
\expectationvalue{f}:=\expect_{U\sim\mu_{G}}f(U)
of
f
. More formally, say that
f
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous on
G
if, for all
U,V\in G
,
\absolutevalue{f(U)-f(V)}\leq L\|U-V\|_{2}
.
One then has
[
35
]
:

Lemma 6
.

Let
X\in\{\mathbb{U}(d),\mathbb{O}(d),\mathbb{SP}(d)\}
, and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be
L
-Lipschitz continuous, with
\expect_{U\sim\mu_{X}}f(U)=\expectationvalue{f}
. Then

(44)

\Pr_{U\sim\mu_{X}}[\lvert f(U)-\expectationvalue{f}\rvert\geq t]\leq 2e^{-(d-2)t^{2}/(24L^{2})}

Proof.

This is a (slight weakening of) Theorem 5.17 of Ref.
[
35
]
.
∎

Our primary purpose in this subsection is to adapt this result to apply to averaging over compact symmetric spaces. We find:

Lemma 7
.

Let
G/K
be a compact symmetric space with involution
\sigma
, and
f:G\to\mathbb{R}
be
L
-Lipschitz continuous, with
\expect_{U\sim\mu_{G/K}}f(U)=\expectationvalue{f}
. Then

(45)

\Pr_{U\sim\mu_{G/K}}[\lvert f(U)-\expectationvalue{f}\rvert\geq t]\leq 2e^{-(d-2)t^{2}/(96L^{2})}

Proof.

Note that by the discussion of Section
2.3
,

(46)

\Pr_{U\sim\mu_{G/K}}[\lvert f(U)-\expectationvalue{f}\rvert\geq t]=\Pr_{U\sim\mu_{G}}[\lvert f(\sigma(U)^{\dagger}U)-\expectationvalue{f}\rvert\geq t];

it then follows from Lemma
6
that we will be done if we can show that
g:U\mapsto f(\sigma(U)^{\dagger}U)
is
2L
-Lipschitz continuous. Indeed, note that by the assumed continuity of
f
,

(47)

\displaystyle\lvert g(U)-g(V)\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert f(\sigma(U)^{\dagger}U)-f(\sigma(V)^{\dagger}V)\rvert

(48)

\displaystyle\leqslant L\|\sigma(U)^{\dagger}U-\sigma(V)^{\dagger}V\|_{2}

(49)

\displaystyle=L\|\sigma(U)^{\dagger}U-\sigma(U)^{\dagger}V+\sigma(U)^{\dagger}V-\sigma(V)^{\dagger}V\|_{2}

(50)

\displaystyle\leqslant L\|\sigma(U)^{\dagger}U-\sigma(U)^{\dagger}V\|_{2}+L\|\sigma(U)^{\dagger}V-\sigma(V)^{\dagger}V\|_{2}

(51)

\displaystyle\leqslant L\|U-V\|_{2}+L\|\sigma(U)^{\dagger}-\sigma(V)^{\dagger}\|_{2}

(52)

\displaystyle=L\|U-V\|_{2}+L\|\sigma(U-V)^{\dagger}\|_{2}

(53)

\displaystyle=L\|U-V\|_{2}+L\|\sigma(U-V)\|_{2}

(54)

\displaystyle=2L\|U-V\|_{2}

where we have used that
\|AB\|_{2}\leq\|A\|_{2}\|B\|_{\infty}
, and that the spectral norm of a unitary is less than or equal to one. In the final line we have used that an exhaustive examination of the cases in Table
2.3
reveals that all of the involutions we consider are 2-norm-preserving.
∎

One of our primary applications of Lemma
7
will be to
f:U\in G\mapsto\langle 0|U^{\dagger}\Phi U|0\rangle
, which one observes is
2\|\Phi\|_{\infty}
-Lipschitz continuous:

Lemma 8
.

Let
f:U\in G\mapsto\langle 0|U^{\dagger}\Phi U|0\rangle
, with
\Phi\in\mathrm{End\ }\mathcal{H}
. Then
f
is
2\|\Phi\|_{\infty}
-Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

We directly calculate:

(55)

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{f(U)-f(V)}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\sbraket{0|U\ad\Phi U}{0}-\sbraket{0|V\ad\Phi V}{0}}

(56)

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\sbraket{0|U\ad\Phi U}{0}-\sbraket{0|V\ad\Phi U}{0}+\sbraket{0|V\ad\Phi U}{0}-\sbraket{0|V\ad\Phi V}{0}}

(57)

\displaystyle\leq\absolutevalue{\sbraket{0|U\ad\Phi U}{0}-\sbraket{0|V\ad\Phi U}{0}}+\absolutevalue{\sbraket{0|V\ad\Phi U}{0}-\sbraket{0|V\ad\Phi V}{0}}

(58)

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\tr[\ketbra{0} (U\ad\Phi U-V\ad\Phi U)]}+\absolutevalue{\tr[\ketbra{0} ( V\ad\Phi U-V\ad\Phi V)]}

(59)

\displaystyle\leq\|U^{\dagger}\Phi U-V^{\dagger}\Phi U\|_{\infty}+\|V^{\dagger}\Phi U-V^{\dagger}\Phi V\|_{\infty}

(60)

\displaystyle\leq\|U^{\dagger}-V^{\dagger}\|_{\infty}\|\Phi U\|_{\infty}+\|U-V\|_{\infty}\ \|V^{\dagger}\Phi\|_{\infty}

(61)

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\left(\|U^{\dagger}-V^{\dagger}\|_{\infty}+\|U-V\|_{\infty}\ \right)

(62)

\displaystyle=2\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\|U-V\|_{\infty}

(63)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\|U-V\|_{2}

where we have used Holder’s inequality, the submultiplicativity
\|AB\|_{\infty}\leq\|A\|_{\infty}\|B\|_{\infty}
of the spectral norm and that the spectral norm of a unitary is equal to one.
∎

3.
Distance to the uniform distribution in the group case

In this section we apply some of the tools of Section
2
to calculate
\expect_{U\sim\mu_{G}}\,{\rm d}_{\rm TV}(P_{U},\mathcal{C})
(recall Eq. (
2
)) exactly, for
G\in\{\mathbb{U}(d),\mathbb{O}(d)\}
; previously this quantity was known only up to an additive error of order
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{d})

[
6
,
24
]
(note that the unitary symplectic case is identical to that of the unitary case
[
20
,
24
]
, and so does not need to be handled separately).
We believe that this highlights the power of the slightly unconventional approach to integrating over the compact groups employed in this work.

3.1.
The unitary case

Let
U\sim\mu_{\mathbb{U}}
and
\ket{\psi}=U|0\rangle
. Equivalently, sample

g\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,\mathds{1}_{d})
and set
\ket{\psi}=g/\|g\|
.
Fix
a\in[d]
and decompose
g=\eta\,|a\rangle+g_{\perp}
, where
g_{\perp}
and
\eta:=\innerproduct{a}{g}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
are independent. Let

(64)

E:=|\eta|^{2}\sim{\rm Gamma}(1,1),\qquad T_{\perp}:=\|g_{\perp}\|^{2}\sim{\rm Gamma}(d-1,1);

then
p_{U}(a)=E/(E+T_{\perp})
is (independently of
a
) distributed as
V\sim{\rm Beta}(1,d-1)
, with density

(65)

f_{V}(v)=(d-1)(1-v)^{d-2}\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}(v).

At this point the problem is reduced to an elementary integral:

(66)

\displaystyle\expect_{U\sim\mu_{\mathbb{U}}}\,{\rm d}_{\rm TV}(P_{U},\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\expect_{V\sim{\rm Beta}(1,d-1)}\big|dV-1\big|

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1/d}(1-dv)f_{V}(v)\,{\rm d}v+\int_{1/d}^{1}(dv-1)f_{V}(v)\,{\rm d}v\right)

(68)

\displaystyle=\frac{d-1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1/d}(1-dv)(1-v)^{d-2}\,{\rm d}v+\int_{1/d}^{1}(dv-1)(1-v)^{d-2}\,{\rm d}v\right)

(69)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)^{d}

whence we immediately recover the known
[
6
]
result

(70)

\lim_{d\to\infty}\expect_{U\sim\mu_{\mathbb{U}(d)}}\,{\rm d}_{\rm TV}(P_{U},\mathcal{C})=e^{-1}

3.2.
The orthogonal case

This time let
O\sim\mu_{\mathbb{O}(d)}
and
\ket{\psi}=O|0\rangle
. Equivalently, sample

g\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{R}}(0,\mathds{1}_{d})
and set
\ket{\psi}=g/\|g\|
.
Fix
a\in[d]
and decompose
g=\eta\,|a\rangle+g_{\perp}
, where
g_{\perp}
and
\eta:=\innerproduct{a}{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

are independent. Consider the independent random variables

(71)

E:=\eta^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{1},\qquad T_{\perp}:=\|g_{\perp}\|^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{d-1}.

Then
p_{O}(a)=E/(E+T_{\perp})
is distributed as
V\sim{\rm Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)
. By the invariance of the Haar measure on
\mathbb{O}
,

(72)

\expect_{O\sim\mu_{\mathbb{O}}}\,{\rm d}_{\rm TV}(P_{O},\mathcal{C})=\frac{1}{2}\,\expect_{V\sim{\rm Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\big|dV-1\big|=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1/d}(1-dv)f_{V}(v)\,{\rm d}v+\int_{1/d}^{1}(dv-1)f_{V}(v)\,{\rm d}v\right),

where
f_{V}(v)=\dfrac{v^{-1/2}(1-v)^{(d-3)/2}}{B(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2})}
.
Since
\expect_{V\sim{\rm Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}[dV-1]=0
, we have

(73)

\displaystyle\expect_{V\sim{\rm Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\big|dV-1\big|

\displaystyle=2\int_{1/d}^{1}(dv-1)\dfrac{v^{-1/2}(1-v)^{(d-3)/2}}{B(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2})}\,{\rm d}v

(74)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{B(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2})}\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}

whence

(75)

\expect_{O\sim\mu_{\mathbb{O}}}\,{\rm d}_{\rm TV}(P_{O},\mathcal{C})=\frac{2\,\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi d}\,\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})}\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}};

standard limits then give

(76)

\lim_{d\to\infty}\expect_{O\sim\mu_{\mathbb{O}}}\,{\rm d}_{\rm TV}(P_{O},\mathcal{C})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi e}}

reproducing the asymptotic result of Ref.
[
24
]
. Similarly to the unitary case, we remark that previously only an approximation to additive error
\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2})
was known; in contrast the result of Eq. (
75
) is exact.

4.1.

We begin with:

5.
Discussion

In this work we have continued the recent trend
[
6
,
27
,
24
]
of establishing the hardness of learning within the statistical query model Born distributions of states resulting from various natural unitary ensembles. As in those works, we have found learning our target ensembles to be intractable, further highlighting the difficulty of the general problem. Similarly to Refs.
[
6
,
24
]
we have primarily relied upon a concentration of measure argument stemming from Levy’s Lemma, albeit combined with a slightly atypical approach to carrying out the corresponding integrations; in particular, our conclusions have turned out to be reasonably insensitive the differences between our considered ensembles.

We note that although the conclusions of Refs.
[
6
,
27
,
24
]
all mirror closely those of this work, the techniques employed in Ref.
[
27
]
to establish the difficulty of learning free-fermionic (matchgate) distributions are very different from those employed in the other three papers. Indeed, their proof proceeds by a reduction to a parity-learning problem which is known to be hard.
To see the correspondence between the matchgate case and our DIII analysis, we recall that a pure
d
-mode fermionic Gaussian state may be equivalently parametrised as
V=O^{\mathsf{T}}JO
, where
O\in\mathbb{SO}(2d)
and
J
is a (reference)
complex structure

[
28
]
, or as a matchgate circuit acting on a reference state
\ket{0}
. In either case there is a redundancy of description given by a stabiliser subgroup isomorphic to
\mathbb{U}(n)
; for example in the former case any
V^{\prime}
related to
V
by the transformation
O\mapsto O^{\prime}O
, where
O^{\prime}
is an orthogonal matrix which commutes with
J
, evidently leaves the Gaussian state unchanged. As (up to the unimportant linear action of another factor of
J
),
O^{\mathsf{T}}JO
was exactly the quantity we considered in our DIII analysis, we see therefore the correspondence with the learning free fermionic states problem of Ref.
[
27
]
; our result is then an (average-case) analogue of their result which relies only on carrying out conceptually straightforward (if admittedly somewhat tedious) calculations.
