Disseminated Ganciclovir-Resistant Cytomegalovirus in a Nontransplant Pediatric ALL Patient: A Case Report
Authors: Marielys Collazo-Roman, Jodi O’Connor, William S. Wilson, Andrew Smitherman, Thomas Belhorn, Zachary Willis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection is an underrecognized complication in children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). While CMV disease is well documented in transplant recipients, its clinical course in nontransplant settings remains poorly defined. Disseminated ganciclovir-resistant CMV infection is a rare complication in nontransplant children with ALL, and its optimal management remains uncertain.Observations:We describe an 8-year-old patient with T-ALL receiving maintenance chemotherapy who developed high levels of CMV viremia without initial end-organ disease and subsequent emergence of ganciclovir-resistant CMV infection. Despite prolonged valganciclovir therapy and the subsequent use of foscarnet and maribavir, the patient developed CMV-related complications, including CMV retinitis.Conclusion:This case underscores the potential severity of CMV in nontransplant pediatric ALL with prolonged lymphopenia, challenges of antiviral resistance, and limited treatment options for central nervous system involvement. The progression from asymptomatic viremia to clinically significant CMV disease remains poorly understood. Enhanced CMV surveillance protocols and resistance-guided treatment strategies may be warranted in selected high-risk populations.

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in immunocompromised patients, with infection rates ranging from 30% to 50% in allogeneic transplant recipients.1 CMV ranges from asymptomatic viremia to life-threatening end-organ diseases. Although it is well characterized in transplant patients, recent studies have shown that CMV viremia also occurs in nontransplant children with leukemia, particularly during maintenance or salvage chemotherapy, with retinitis being a rare but documented complication.1,2

A prospective study of 95 newly diagnosed acute leukemia (ALL or AML) patients found a high CMV IgG seroprevalence of 94.7%, suggesting a latent infection.3 CMV DNAemia was noted throughout all stages of chemotherapy, with the highest rates (51.7%) after salvage chemotherapy, compared with the pretreatment (8.4%) or postinduction (14.7%) phases.3 The role of CMV infection in nontransplant patients, especially in children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), remains unclear. In a retrospective study of children with ALL with CMV diagnosis who did not undergo hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), CMV viremia was detected in 13.6% of patients.2 The most common presenting symptom in Çağlar et al2 was fever in 64% of cases, elevated transaminases in 50%, and marked lymphopenia in 92%. In addition, in a case series of 14 pediatric ALL patients, several progressed to CMV end-organ diseases such as retinitis, hepatitis, colitis, and pneumonitis.

First-line treatment for CMV includes ganciclovir and valganciclovir. Alternatives for resistant variants or those intolerant to ganciclovir products include foscarnet and cidofovir.4 Maribavir, an UL97 kinase inhibitor that blocks CMV DNA replication via a mechanism distinct from that of ganciclovir,4 is primarily used for refractory or resistant CMV in stem cell or solid organ transplant recipients.4,5

This report describes a rare case of disseminated, treatment-resistant CMV in a non-HSCT pediatric ALL patient, highlights the use and limitations of maribavir, and summarizes gaps in CMV management in nontransplant immunocompromised patients.

CLINICAL OBSERVATION

An 8-year-old male with a history of acute T-cell lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) receiving maintenance chemotherapy presented to the clinic with fever, fatigue, and sleepiness for 2 days. The patient was alert and oriented, without complaints of headache, dizziness, confusion, seizure activity, or paresthesia. No bleeding, rash, respiratory symptoms, diarrhea, or visual changes were reported. Before his illness, he had a good energy level. Past medical history included recurrent otitis media during early childhood, requiring placement of pressure equalization tubes at 2 years of age. Family history was noncontributory. Recently, several family members have experienced infectious mononucleosis. No travel history has been documented. The patient resided in a household with 2 dogs and participated in outdoor activities. Relevant exposures included the use of well water at home and receipt of blood products, as expected for the patient’s disease state.

On physical examination, the patient appeared fatigued and pale but otherwise exhibited no abnormal findings. Laboratory evaluation revealed leukopenia with lymphopenia, anemia (hemoglobin 8.4 g/dL), and thrombocytopenia. These laboratory findings were mostly attributed to the patient’s current oral chemotherapy treatment with methotrexate and mercaptopurine. Owing to nonspecific symptoms and a recent history of viral exposure, CMV PCR testing was performed. CMV PCR was quantified as 72,700 IU/mL (4.86 log IU/mL). Serum immunoglobulin testing revealed hypogammaglobulinemia with a total IgG level of 412 mg/dL. CMV viremia was diagnosed, with no signs of end-organ injury and normal liver function tests. The initial assessment of no end-organ injury was based on a review of symptoms and objective data, with a formal ophthalmologic examination performed after diagnosis and confirmation of persistent viremia.

Valganciclovir therapy was initiated, with a plan to continue for at least 2 to 4 weeks, and discontinued treatment following 2 consecutive undetectable CMV viral loads. Persistent myelosuppression was observed, likely secondary to CMV treatment, necessitating careful risk assessment of continued therapy versus the potential development of CMV end-organ disease. Valganciclovir was continued for ∼3 months. The initial response was favorable, with viral loads declining to the 40 to 50 IU/mL range. Finally, the therapy was discontinued after 12 weeks of therapy, and 2 negative measurements were taken a week apart. However, therapy was resumed 2 weeks later because of a rebound viral load of 1710 IU/mL (>2 log increase). Approximately 10 weeks after reinitiation, the viral load began to increase despite ongoing valganciclovir. Subsequently, CMV resistance testing was performed. At that time, there was no evidence of cytomegalovirus (CMV) end-organ disease. Also, a dilated fundus examination and slit-lamp examination were both within normal limits, with no chorioretinal lesions, vascular abnormalities, or optic nerve abnormalities identified. While awaiting CMV resistance testing, the patient presented with a right-sided facial droop, headache, and right ear pain. No cough, congestion, visual disturbances, hearing loss, numbness, or recent trauma was reported. Otoscopy revealed a bulging right tympanic membrane and an erythematous but nonbulging left tympanic membrane with preserved light reflex. The pinnae were nontender, and no mastoid erythema or tenderness was observed. No otorrhea was observed, and the external ear tug test results were negative. Neurological examination revealed right-sided cranial nerve (CN) VII palsy with mild incomplete eye closure. Cranial nerves II to XII were otherwise intact. The extraocular movements were intact. The strength was 5/5 in both upper and lower extremities. Laboratory findings included continued leukopenia and lymphopenia, with an improved hemoglobin level of 11.2 g/dL. The CMV PCR result was 46,500 IU/mL (4.67 log IU/mL). C-reactive protein was <4.0 mg/dL, ANC 400 cells/μL, ALC 100 cells/μL, and total IgG was 871 mg/dL. Liver enzyme levels were within normal limits. An MRI showed diffuse bilateral enhancement of cranial nerves III to VIII, interpreted as most consistent with leukemic infiltration.

The differential diagnoses for cranial nerve palsy included complications of acute otitis media, CMV-related neuropathy, and leukemia relapse. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were then administered. Although the patient’s CMV viral load was concurrently rising and CMV-associated cranial neuropathy remained on the differential, a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed mild pleocytosis with 97% lymphocytes. The CSF cytomegalovirus (CMV) PCR results were negative. Resistance testing, obtained from a blood sample, revealed a mutation at A594V in the UL97 kinase gene, conferring ganciclovir resistance. Also, CSF analysis identified a CD7-positive lymphoid population, and FISH analysis found a TRB::LMO2 translocation, favoring CNS leukemic infiltration as the predominant etiology. Bone marrow evaluation at relapse did not confirm medullary disease, showing trilineage hematopoiesis with 4% blasts (within normal limits, ≤5%) and negative CMV and EBV immunostains. A sensitive T-ALL minimal residual disease (MRD) assay identified no discrete leukemic population. These findings indicate a predominantly extramedullary relapse involving the CNS and mastoid air cells; the inability to achieve complete remission, therefore, reflected refractory extramedullary disease rather than persistent medullary leukemia.

Due to the confirmed ganciclovir resistance, foscarnet was initiated at a dose of 180 mg/kg/day, divided every 12 hours. A partial virological response was achieved, although CMV viremia persisted. Therefore, the foscarnet regimen was adjusted to every-8-hour dosing at the same total daily dose. Subsequent resistance testing, prompted by a 1-log increase in viral load, indicated a mixed population of wild-type and ganciclovir-resistant CMV with preserved foscarnet sensitivity. A CMV-specific lymphocyte infusion was considered; however, after reassessment, maribavir 400 mg orally twice daily was added owing to the increase in viral load.

Further investigation confirmed that testicular relapse of T-ALL occurred during this period. An intensive reinduction chemotherapy and cranial radiation regimen was provided, weighing the risk of worsening CMV. Although HSCT was planned as a consolidative therapy, complete remission was not achieved, and ongoing CMV infection remained a major barrier. Upon combined foscarnet and maribavir therapy, CMV retinitis developed and was confirmed by PCR from an aqueous humor sample. Intravitreal foscarnet injections were initiated. However, his CMV retinitis progressed, and his right CN VII palsy persisted despite an undetectable serum viral load.

Six weeks after the initiation of maribavir, the patient developed encephalopathy. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed multifocal infarcts in the bilateral basal ganglia. At this time, it was unclear whether these radiologic findings were due to ALL relapse or CMV infection. Owing to poor prognosis, the patient was transitioned to hospice care and subsequently died from complications of progressive CMV infection and relapsed leukemia despite combination antiviral therapy.

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates a rare yet severe instance of disseminated CMV disease in a pediatric patient with T-cell ALL who had not undergone HSCT, underscoring the need for increased clinical awareness in nontransplant settings.2,3,6 The patient experienced resistance to first-line antivirals, narrowing of treatment options, persistent viremia despite treatment, precluding HSCT eligibility, and progression to end-organ disease. First, CMV encephalitis, a rare complication, was suspected, but confirmation was not obtained by either CSF or tissue PCR testing due to changes in goals of care at the time of imaging. Maribavir offers a novel mechanism of action and is effective against CMV strains with UL97 mutations that confer ganciclovir resistance. Its oral formulation and lack of myelotoxicity present significant advantages in pediatric patients with leukemia.7,8 However, its limited penetration into the CNS and ocular compartments poses challenges in the management of CMV encephalitis or retinitis.5,9 In contrast, the AURORA trial demonstrated that maribavir failed to achieve noninferiority to valganciclovir for confirmed CMV viremia clearance at week 8 (69.6% vs. 77.4%), with treatment-emergent resistance occurring in 8.8% of maribavir-treated patients, particularly among those with high viral load, acute GVHD, and T-cell depletion.10 Consistent with this, Kasriel et al11 found that among kidney transplant recipients with refractory CMV infection, only 42.1% maintained CMV clearance without recurrence within 16 weeks of maribavir initiation, with baseline CMV DNAemia ≥10,000 IU/mL independently associated with both treatment failure and the emergence of maribavir resistance mutations (OR=5.19; 95% CI: 1.85-61.22).11

At initial presentation, we faced a significant challenge in determining the appropriate valganciclovir dose because of his existing cytopenias. We consciously opted for a conservative, weight-based dosing strategy over a BSA-based regimen to minimize hematologic toxicity, a common dilemma in HSCT and SOT pediatric care.6,10,11 Underdosing with ganciclovir products is a well-recognized risk factor for the development of drug-resistant CMV strains, especially when combined with prolonged therapy.12,13,16 Antivirals were initiated because of the patient’s high viral load. Notably, the viral load did not decrease as promptly as expected, and the severity of the CMV-related morbidity was unforeseen. Despite treatment, CMV leads to major complications, underscoring the difficulty in managing nontransplant pediatric ALL. This suggests that some patients may face a higher risk of clinically significant CMV infection than is typically recognized.

It is well known in the literature that both subtherapeutic antiviral exposure and extended duration of ganciclovir treatment have been associated with the emergence of resistance in immunocompromised populations.12,13 In this case, maribavir combined with foscarnet led to improved CMV viral load. However, likely because maribavir did not penetrate the blood-brain barrier,14,17 cases of retinitis did not improve, and encephalitis with CMV as the suspected etiology developed while the patient was on combined therapy. Additional options include immune-based therapies (eg, donor-derived CMV-specific T cells) for refractory disease and investigational agents such as artesunate and sirolimus, although data remain limited.

Routine CMV monitoring is not recommended for nontransplant pediatric patients.6,15 However, profound immune suppression from lymphocyte-targeting regimens, especially hypogammaglobulinemia, may increase the risk of severe CMV disease. Because baseline CMV testing was not available, primary infection could not be excluded. This case supports the need for CMV surveillance in selected high-risk patients, particularly those with persistent lymphopenia or prolonged chemotherapy exposure.
