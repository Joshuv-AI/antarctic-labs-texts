Prognostic value of the Mayo-Fundación Jiménez Díaz classification for nonsurgical proximal humerus fractures: a morphological approach to predicting functional outcomes
Authors: Gustavo Armando Tafoya-Arreguín, José Rene Castillo-González, Carlos Omar Sepúlveda-Cadena, José de Jesus Martinez-Ruiz, Jaime Briseno-Ramírez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent classification systems for proximal humerus fractures (PHFs) often fail to provide a reliable functional prognosis. The Mayo-Fundación Jiménez Díaz (FJD) classification aims to address this by focusing on specific morphological patterns. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the prognostic value of the Mayo-FJD classification and determine if fracture morphology outweighs patient-specific factors like age, gender, and limb dominance in predicting clinical success.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 149 patients with PHFs initially managed nonsurgically (minimum 24-month follow-up). Fractures were categorized into 9 types based on the Mayo-FJD system. Clinical outcomes were assessed using University of California at Los Angeles, Constant, and Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand scores and range of motion. Multivariate regression analysis was performed to adjust for age, gender, and limb dominance.ResultsHighly significant differences were found across all functional scores and range of motion among classification groups (P < .0001). Type 5 fractures (valgus impacted) showed the best outcomes (Constant 76.7), while Type 9 (disengaged neck) represented the poorest results (University of California at Los Angeles 9.9) and a 100% conversion rate to reverse shoulder arthroplasty; this reflects the failure of initial nonoperative management. Multivariate analysis confirmed that the Mayo-FJD fracture type was the strongest independent predictor of outcome (P < .0001), whereas gender and limb dominance were not statistically significant (P > .05).ConclusionThe Mayo-FJD classification is a robust tool for predicting functional outcomes and surgical failure in PHFs. Fracture morphology is a more critical determinant of clinical success than patient-specific variables, providing surgeons with a reliable framework for therapeutic planning and patient counseling.Keywords: Proximal humerus fractures, Mayo-FJD classification, Fracture morphology, Functional outcomes, Reverse shoulder arthroplasty, Prognostic factors

Proximal humerus fractures (PHFs) remain one of the most common osteoporotic injuries in the elderly population, representing approximately 5% of all adult fractures.3,9 Despite their prevalence, the management of complex PHFs continues to be a subject of significant debate within the orthopedic community.2,4,8 Much of this controversy stems from the difficulty in accurately predicting clinical outcomes and the risk of surgical failure based on current radiographic assessment tools.1,5

Traditionally, the Neer and Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopaedic Trauma Association classification systems have been the cornerstones for describing these injuries.10,12,13 However, Foruria et al have questioned these classification systems, as they have been frequently criticized for their moderate interobserver reliability and, more critically, for their limited ability to correlate fracture morphology with long-term functional prognosis.6 While these classifications focus on the number of displaced fragments and anatomical locations, they often overlook specific mechanical and biological markers—such as the varus-valgus orientation of the head, the integrity of the medial hinge, and complex disengagement patterns—that are essential for determining the success of open reduction and internal fixation.

The Mayo-Fundación Jiménez Díaz (FJD) classification was developed as a response to these limitations, offering a more nuanced morphological framework.6 By categorizing fractures into 9 distinct types based on specific displacement and stability patterns, this system aims to bridge the gap between initial radiographic findings and final clinical performance. Nevertheless, there is a need for robust clinical data to validate whether this morphological stratification can reliably predict patient-reported outcomes, range of motion (ROM), and the risk of conversion to reverse shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA).11

Furthermore, while factors such as age and comorbidities are known to influence recovery, the relative impact of fracture complexity versus patient-specific variables like limb dominance and gender remains poorly defined in the context of advanced morphological classifications.6,7

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the prognostic value of the Mayo-FJD classification in a cohort of patients with PHFs. We primarily aimed to determine the correlation between fracture type and 24-month functional outcomes (University of California at Los Angeles [UCLA], Constant, and Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand [QuickDASH] scores) and ROM. Secondarily, we sought to analyze the influence of age, gender, and limb dominance on these outcomes to establish whether the fracture pattern itself remains the primary determinant of clinical success. We hypothesized that the Mayo-FJD classification provides a reliable hierarchical stratification of functional results and identifies high-risk patterns prone to surgical conversion.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

A retrospective cohort study was conducted on patients who sustained a PHF and were treated at our institution between 2015 and 2022. Inclusion criteria consisted of patients aged 55 years or older, with a minimum clinical and radiographic follow-up of 24 months. Patients with prior surgery on the affected shoulder, pathological fractures, or incomplete medical records were excluded from the study. The study was approved by the institutional review board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. All patients were initially managed nonoperatively with a standardized immobilization protocol. Conversion to rTSA was performed only in cases of secondary functional failure or intractable pain.

Fracture classification

All fractures were classified according to the Mayo-FJD system by 2 fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeons, based on the primary fracture pattern using X-rays and computed tomography scan. In cases of disagreement, a third senior surgeon was consulted to reach a consensus. This classification system stratifies PHFs based on specific displacement patterns and morphological characteristics (Types 1 through 9), focusing on predictors of functional prognosis and mechanical stability.

This hierarchical numerical framework allowed for a standardized correlation between the anatomical complexity of the fracture and the subsequent functional outcomes and conversion rates observed in the cohort. No cases of Type 7 (head dislocation) or Type 8 (head impression) were identified in this cohort during the study period.

Clinical and functional assessment

Clinical outcomes were evaluated at the 24-month follow-up mark. Functional status was assessed using the UCLA Shoulder Score, the Constant score, and the QuickDASH score. Satisfaction was primarily derived from the UCLA subscores. ROM was measured in degrees using a standard goniometer, recording active forward flexion (FF), abduction (ABD), internal rotation, and external rotation.

Demographic and surgical covariates

Data regarding patient age, gender, and limb dominance (dominant vs. nondominant extremity) were recorded. The incidence of conversion surgery, specifically the conversion to rTSA, was documented as a primary failure endpoint.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations (SDs). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test; due to the nonparametric distribution of the functional scores, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed for multigroup comparisons across the Mayo-FJD types. Correlation between ROM and functional scores was analyzed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (rs).

To evaluate the independent influence of age, gender, and limb dominance on clinical outcomes, a multivariate linear regression model was constructed, adjusting for the Mayo-FJD fracture type. Categorical data and conversion rates were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher's exact tests as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. All analyses were performed using Python (v3.9) with SciPy and Pandas libraries.

Results

Descriptive and demographic analysis

A total of 149 patients with PHFs, classified according to the Mayo-FJD system, were included. The cohort demonstrated a heterogeneous distribution across types: Type 6 was the most frequent (n = 38), followed by Type 4 (n = 29). Global mean age showed significant variations between groups (P < .001); notably, patients with Type 5 and Type 9 fractures were significantly younger (mean age 62 years) compared to those with Type 1 fractures (mean age 79.3 years).

Functional outcomes and range of motion

Highly significant statistical differences were identified across all functional parameters and ROM measurements between the classification groups (P < .0001).

Conversion analysis (reverse shoulder arthroplasty)

The global conversion rate for rTSA was critically dependent on the fracture type due to persistent pain and functional failure, primarily due to nonunion and malunion. Type 9 fractures exhibited a 100% conversion rate, followed by Type 6 (26.3%) and Type 2 (21.4%). Types 1 and 3 required no conversion procedures during the evaluation period.

Multivariate regression analysis was performed to adjust outcomes for demographic variables. The Mayo-FJD classification system proved to be the most robust independent predictor for all clinical outcomes (P < .0001).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that the Mayo-FJD classification for PHFs is a highly reliable predictor of both functional outcomes and the necessity for conversion surgery. Our results demonstrate that fracture morphology, categorized by the Mayo-FJD system, is the strongest predictor of clinical success, surpassing both age and gender. This aligns with the findings of Foruria et al (2022), who argue that the reliability of this system lies in the recognition of patterns that dictate mechanical stability and biological viability. While traditional systems, such as Neer, have focused on the number of fragments, our series validates that it is the “behavior” of the pattern (such as calcar displacement or loss of head-neck contact) that truly defines the functional prognosis at 24 months.

Functional outcomes and fracture morphology

The most striking finding was the significant disparity in functional scores across the Mayo-FJD types. Patients classified as Type 5 consistently achieved superior outcomes, reaching near-physiologic levels of function with a mean Constant score of 76.7. In contrast, Type 9 fractures represented the “functional failure” end of the spectrum, with UCLA scores (mean 9.9) and QuickDASH scores reflecting severe disability. This clear stratification supports the clinical utility of the Mayo-FJD system in setting realistic follow-up expectations for both surgeons and patients.

While traditional classifications, such as Neer's, focus on the number of displaced parts, the Mayo-FJD system's integration of specific displacement patterns and vascular risk—factors previously highlighted by Hertel et al.—appears to correlate more directly with the final ROM. Our data showed that FF and ABD were particularly compromised in Types 4 and 9. The strong correlation between FF and UCLA scores (rs = 0.896) reinforces the axiom that restoring the mechanical lever arm and tuberosity integrity is paramount for patient satisfaction.

The burden of conversion surgery

Our analysis revealed a critical “red flag” in Type 9 fractures, which exhibited a 100% conversion rate to rTSA. This suggests that, for these specific injury patterns, conservative treatment may prove insufficient, potentially exposing patients to conversion to a surgical procedure. The role of covariates: age, gender, and dominance

A noteworthy contribution of this study is the multivariate analysis of demographic factors. While age showed a moderate influence on functional scores—consistent with the general decline in musculoskeletal reserve—neither gender nor limb dominance reached statistical significance when adjusted for fracture type. The biological and mechanical “personality” of the fracture outweighs the patient's preinjury profile. Surgeons should prioritize the anatomical complexity of the fracture over concerns regarding whether the injury affected the dominant side when planning reconstruction.

Clinical implications

The Mayo-FJD classification facilitates a more nuanced approach to PHFs. By identifying groups with a high risk of failure (Types 6 and 9), surgeons can consider earlier intervention with arthroplasty or augmented fixation strategies. Conversely, the excellent results in Type 5 suggest that nonsurgical management for these patterns is highly effective.

Limitations

Despite the significance of the findings, this study is not without limitations. The retrospective nature of the analysis and the variable follow-up period may introduce selection bias. Additionally, while the total cohort (n = 149) is substantial, the specific sample size for Type 9 was small, reflecting its rarity but potentially limiting the generalizability of its 100% conversion rate. Future prospective multicenter trials are needed to validate these findings across different surgical expertise levels.

Conclusion

The Mayo-FJD classification is a robust tool for predicting clinical success in PHFs initially treated nonsurgically. It effectively stratifies patients into distinct prognostic groups, with Type 5 achieving optimal results and Type 9 representing a high-risk group for surgical conversion. Fracture type remains the most critical independent predictor of shoulder function and patient satisfaction.
