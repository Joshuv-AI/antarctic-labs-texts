Judgment behaviors differentiate depression from anxiety
Authors: Nicole L. Vike, Sumra Bari, Jeffrey R. Strawn, Felix Zhan, Byoungwoo Kim, Hans C. Breiter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Depression and anxiety are common mental health disorders that often share overlapping symptoms. This can limit the specificity of standard screening tools and complicate differential diagnosis. Changes in judgment can present for both conditions and can be mathematically extracted from a short picture rating task. The goal of this study was to determine if these judgment features can differentiate anxiety from depression. This cross-sectional population study surveyed 3476 adult participants (ages 18–70) from across the United States in December 2021. Anxiety (State Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI)) and depression (Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9)) scores categorized as moderate-to-severe were classified using random forest, with judgment variables and demographics used as predictors. Judgment variable differences were assessed across anxiety and depression symptom severities. The study cohort was predominately middle aged (mean 51), female (61%), white (84%), married (51%), with 5% self-reporting severe depressive symptoms and 28% reporting severe anxiety symptoms. The 15 judgment variables, with and without demographic variables, produced AUROCs of 0.798 and 0.793, respectively. Judgment variables, collectively, carried 83% of the predictive feature importance, whereas the seven demographics, apart from age, were the least important features (17% collectively). Judgment variables further differed across PHQ-9 and STAI severities. Judgment features from a picture rating task differentiated anxiety from depression and varied meaningfully across symptom severities, reflecting motivational deficits and emotional blunting. Given the prevalence of depression and anxiety, a quick, low-cost, and unbiased tool could improve diagnostic efficacy and patient well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48501-7.

Introduction

Depressive and anxiety disorders are among the most common psychiatric illnesses worldwide and are associated with substantial morbidity1. These disorders often share risk factors2 and overlap syndromically; however, they also have distinct neurobiological features3–5. While both disorders involve dysfunction in affective and salience networks, depression has been consistently associated with hypoactivity in regions subserving arousal and reward processing (e.g., parieto-temporal region and ventral striatum), contributing to anhedonia and amotivation6. Anxiety has been characterized by hypersensitivity to potential or sustained threats, with hyperactivation in amygdala-based circuits and dysregulation across systems involved in fear processing, cognitive control, and social evaluation7,8. These findings suggest that while depressive and anxiety disorders share some disruptions in large-scale brain networks, they diverge in the specific regions and patterns of connectivity, and in their clinical features.

Clinical approaches to assessing depression and anxiety often involve symptom rating scales, including the Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9)9 and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-710 or State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI)11. While these symptom inventories are widely used and accessible, they may not readily distinguish between overlapping presentations12. Many patients endorse symptoms of both anxiety and depression (e.g., fatigue, concentration difficulties, dyssomnia), which complicates diagnosis. Historically, efforts to differentiate these disorders have been limited. Clinical interviews often rely on bias-prone, subjective reporting that can miss symptom distinctions. Differentiating depression and anxiety using neuroimaging or other biomarker-driven approaches has shown promise but are cost-prohibitive and largely unavailable for routine clinical use13. As a result, clinicians often lack reliable tools to diagnose depression and anxiety, despite the importance of separating these disorders for selecting treatments and determining prognosis.

Reward and aversion processing (i.e., judgment) has been observed to differ for depression and anxiety14,15. Individuals may become more loss averse with anxiety16, more risk-seeking with substance use17, and more avoidant with depression18. Reward and aversion judgments can be mathematically described by a behaviorist framework that models rating (incenting reward) or operant keypress data (reinforcement reward) into 15 discrete judgment features connected to behavioral finance19,20, including analogs of risk aversion and loss aversion (Fig. 1)21–24.These features computationally define the complexity of human judgment – i.e., behaviors that precede decisions and frame our assessments of stimuli and internal states21. These 15 judgment features have been used to differentiate clinical substance use disorder from controls and depressives25, and to predict anxiety26, suicidal thoughts and behaviors27, addiction28, depression29,30, and vaccine adherence23. These judgment features also differ across condition incidence and severity, suggesting their potential for discriminating between anxiety and depression.

Given that judgment behavior profiles show unique alterations for different psychiatric illnesses, lending to the high accuracy prediction of them26,29,30, it was hypothesized that judgment variables would differentiate survey-based depression and anxiety metrics – which have been historically difficult to disentangle with surveys and symptom profiles. Such research is needed to aid clinical efforts at screening, triaging, and monitoring anxiety and depression accurately, ultimately improving patient outcomes. Identification of behavioral signatures of anxiety and depression could also yield targets for therapeutic intervention.

Methods

Participant recruitment

Recruitment procedures for this cross-sectional study followed previous publications23,26,27. Gold Research, Inc. sampled adults (aged 18–70 years) from the U.S. population in December 2021 using email survey databases. All participants followed a double opt-in, informed consent procedure and responses were anonymized before retrieval. The study was initially approved by the Northwestern University institutional review board and later by the University of Cincinnati institutional review board. Study approvals were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and participants were compensated US $10 for completing the survey. Specific language used by Gold Research for participant recruitment can be found in the Supplemental Material.

Participants were eligible if they could comprehend English and had access to a smart device (e.g., smartphone or laptop). Gold Research, Inc. administered the survey in December 2021 using their standard procedures: (1) customer databases from partner companies with revenue-sharing agreements, (2) social media outreach, and (3) mailings. The research team aimed for a final sample of 4000–4050 participants who met quality assurance standards (see Quality Assurance). From an initial pool of > 400,000 participants, 4019 completed the survey and passed quality assurance. Due to the research team’s focus on mental health, Gold Research, Inc. was asked to achieve a sample with 15% of participants indicating the presence of a mental health condition. Gold Research, Inc. used an initial screener to achieve a starting sample of 60,000 (out of 400,000), indicating a psychiatric disorder.

Survey questionnaires

Participants completed a comprehensive survey that included a demographic screener, nine mental health questionnaires, and a picture rating task (see Picture Rating Task). For this study, we utilized data from the (1) demographic screener (age, ethnicity, sex at birth, income level, employment status, marital status, and education level), (2) PHQ-9, and (3) state component of the STAI.

The PHQ-9 assesses depressive symptoms over the past two weeks,9 includes nine items rated on a 4-point scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), and yields a total score ranging from 0 to 27, where higher scores reflect more depressive symptoms.

The STAI is a validated measure of anxiety11. We used the state component, which assesses current anxiety across 20 items rated from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very much so) to produce scores between 20 and 80 with higher scores reflecting greater state anxiety.

Quality assurance

During recruitment, Gold Research, Inc. excluded participants who (1) gave inconsistent responses to repeated questions or (2) selected the same response throughout (e.g., choosing only “1”). During data collection, additional exclusions were made for participants who: (1) did not complete the survey, (2) reported 10 or more clinical diagnoses (questionnaire not included in this study), (3) gave identical ratings during the picture rating task (see Picture Rating Task in the main text), or (4) completed the complete survey in under 800 s and had inconsistencies for ‘years of education’ and ‘education level.’ Following data collection, picture ratings were modeled using relative preference theory, and data were discarded based on graphical and computational integrity (see Judgment Feature Derivation in the main text). Following these quality assurance procedures, 3476 of the 4019 participants were retained for data analysis. The resulting cohort distribution was on average middle-aged (39–65 years), white (83.7%) females (61.5%) who were married (51.4%), employed full-time (35.8%), with some college education (29.6%) and a household income between $25-50k USD (26.1%) (eTable 1).

Picture rating task

Participants rated 48 images selected from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS)31, divided evenly across six categories: cute animals, aggressive animals, disasters, adrenaline sports, models, and landscapes. Images were shown in pseudo-random order on participants’ digital devices (max resolution: 1204 × 768 pixels), each accompanied by a rating scale from − 3 (strong dislike) to + 3 (strong like), with 0 indicating a neutral preference (Fig. 1A). Participants were instructed to rate each image quickly based on their immediate liking or disliking reaction; the next image appeared after each rating. The task was split into two sections containing 24 images. The IAPS is framed by the dimensional model of emotion from Lang, Bradley, and Cuthbert32,33. Images used in this study covered both positive and negative valence, and had mild intensity, allowing participants to express a broad range of liking or disliking to enable the derivation of both positive and negative preferences for judgment feature quantification.

Judgment feature derivation

The picture ratings were transformed into 15 discrete mathematical features of judgment behavior, including behavioral economic features like risk aversion34 and loss aversion19, following published methods (Fig. 1)24. These methods have been applied across five datasets and demonstrated to consistently produce these metrics for judgment21–27,35–40. Ratings from the picture task were converted into a set of mathematically defined judgment variables representing the location, dispersion, and pattern (i.e., entropy) of behavior as modelled in prior work21–23,26,27. PHQ-9 and STAI categorization

Responses from PHQ-9 and STAI questionnaires were summed and total scores were categorized into three severity classes (Fig. 2): (1) no or low PHQ-9 (0–9, n = 2710) and STAI (20–37, n = 2107) scores, (2) moderate PHQ-9 (10–19, n = 597) and STAI (38–44, n = 391) scores, and (3) severe PHQ-9 (20–27, n = 169) and STAI (45–80, n = 978) scores. Thresholds were established using questionnaire guidelines9 and clinical metrics followed at the University of Cincinnati Medical Center, College of Medicine Anxiety Disorders Research Program. Participants with scores categorized as (2) and (3) (moderate-to-severe) were selected for the discrimination analysis. Those with neither moderate-to-severe PHQ-9 and STAI scores were dropped (n = 2034). Participants with both moderate-to-severe PHQ-9 and STAI scores were also dropped (n = 693). The resulting sample size for participants with only moderate-to-severe PHQ-9 scores was 73 (minority class) and those with only moderate-to-severe STAI scores was 676 (majority class). This resulted in total sample size of 749 participants (see eTable1 for demographic distributions).

Random forest analysis

Random Forest analysis was used to differentiate participants with moderate-to-severe PHQ-9 from moderate-to-severe STAI (n = 749). This analysis was done in Python (version 3.10) using open-source packages. Due to the large class imbalance, both up- and down-sampling methods were implemented using RandomOverSampler (v.0.13.0) and RandomUnderSampler (v.0.13.0) from packages imblearn.over_sampling (v.0.13.0) and imblearn.under_sampling (v.0.13.0), respectively. The minority class (moderate-to-severe PHQ-9, n = 73) was randomly up-sampled to achieve a sample size of 100, and the majority class (moderate-to-severe STAI, n = 676) was randomly down-sampled without replacement to a sample size of 100. The random forest classifier, from sklearn (v.1.6.1), was trained on Gini criterion with bootstrapping and 100 trees were constructed. Ten-fold cross validation was repeated 10 times, and the output metrics were averaged. Due to the random up- and down-sampling, the entire procedure was repeated for Q = 100 iterations to achieve stable output metrics. For these analyses, the moderate-to-severe PHQ-9 was considered the ‘positive’ class and moderate-to-severe STAI was the ‘negative’ class. Averages across all Q iterations for eight performance metrics were reported: accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, negative predictive value (NPV), area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), false negative rate (FNR), and false positive rate (FPR).

Two random forest analyses were performed following the above-described procedure. Model 1 included 15 judgment and seven demographic variables as predictors and Model 2 included 15 judgment variables alone. To support the use of the implemented balancing techniques, random forest analyses were run without balancing for both models. The random forest classifier, from sklearn (v.1.6.1), was trained on Gini criterion with bootstrapping and 100 trees were constructed. Ten-fold cross validation was repeated 10 times and the output metrics were averaged.

Relative feature importance was based on normalized values of the mean decrease in Gini scores. The Gini score shows how large the discriminative value was for separating data points into different classes. The higher the mean decrease Gini score, the more important the feature was for the prediction. The mean decreases in Gini scores were normalized for all predictors so the sum of the normalized ranks was 1. The judgment and demographic variables were then sorted based on these normalized ranks and plotted by decreasing feature importance, with the most important features appearing on the top of the plot using ‘matplotlib’ (v.3.10.0).

While traditional regression modeling recommends 10–20 events per predictor variable, several factors support our analytical approach. Please see the Supplemental Material.

The random forest algorithm was performed twice, once with both judgment and demographic variables (Model 1) and once with judgment variables alone (Model 2) to classify moderate-to-severe PHQ-9 scores (n = 73) from moderate-to-severe STAI scores (n = 676). Averaged across 100 iterations for Model 1, the accuracy was 71.7%, sensitivity 73.0%, specificity 70.5%, precision 72.0%, NPV 73.2%, and AUROC 0.798 (Fig. 4A). Judgment variables carried 83% of the feature importance for differentiating depression from anxiety, and the seven demographics were the least important features (17% collectively) despite differing between PHQ-9 and STAI groups (eTable2). Reward aversion consistency (preference indifference versus conflict) and consistency range (variance in indifference versus conflict of preferences) were the most important prediction features (7.85% and 7.46%, respectively). Demographic variables were low in importance (Fig. 4A), with scores ranging between 0.68 and 5.69%. When only judgment variables were included (Model 2), random forest outcome metrics were very similar, with accuracy of 71.5%, sensitivity 72.7%, specificity 70.6%, precision 72.1%, NPV 73.1%, and AUROC 0.793 (Fig. 4B). The variables with the highest importance were again reward aversion consistency (9.18%) and consistency range (8.54%). Without implementation of balancing techniques, the random forest algorithm produced accuracies of 90.0%, sensitivities between 0.03 and 0.14%, specificities between 99.6 and 99.8%, precisions between 0.10 and 1.00%, NPVs of 90.2%, and AUROCs of 0.6 (eTable3).

Discussion

Anxiety and depression often present with overlapping symptoms, making them difficult to distinguish. This study tested if judgment variables based on reward and aversion processing may differentiate depression and anxiety disorders. The study used 15 judgment variables modeled from a brief picture rating task to distinguish anxiety from depression with an AUROC of 0.842 and other prediction metrics above 70%. When judgment and demographic variables were included, judgment variables had the highest feature importance. Demographic variables contributed little to prediction as evidenced by negligibly higher model performance metrics and low importance scores. The judgment variables were modeled from lawful relationships between mean, variance, and pattern measures produced by preference ratings of mildly emotional stimuli. These variables can be directly interpreted23,26,27,30 and are difficult to game, unlike symptom-based inventories which can be influenced by response bias. The framework used here can be readily implemented on any smart device, making it readily scalable.

Altered judgment profiles can be interpreted in the context of anxiety and depression symptoms. Differential profiles of these judgment variables have proven powerful in machine learning prediction of suicidal thoughts and behavior, anxiety, depression, and vaccine adherence23,26,27,29. Other studies have had success predicting depression and anxiety independently26,30,43,44 but there remains a gap in quantitively and efficiently differentiating the two conditions. Prior efforts to disentangle the disorders have mostly relied on costly imaging or investigative physiological biomarkers13. The simplicity of the framework presented here, and its horizontal success predicting other mental health conditions, suggests its utility for early assessment and patient triage, or as a behavioral biomarker for pharmacological studies. The interpretability of judgment variable differences between conditions potentially provides clinicians with behavioral targets for further studies and clinical intervention.

In this study, participants with moderate-to-severe anxiety symptoms had greater loss aversion, reflecting more sensitivity to negative outcomes. Risk aversion, peak negative risk, total aversion risk, and reward-aversion consistency were lower in those with more anxiety symptoms. Lower peak negative risk suggests that individuals face less uncertainty for avoidance. Lower total aversion risk signifies less interaction between negative value and its risk, indicating aversion has less importance and avoidance is easier. Lower reward-aversion consistency implies more indifference during judgment and less internal conflict. Together, these patterns suggest a negative stimulus orientation and more avoidance behaviors consistent with prior studies45. Clinically, these findings could help differentiate anxiety from depression and might guide psychotherapeuties that target avoidance and threat sensitivity.

Participants with more depressive symptoms had greater ante and total reward risk compared to lower severity groups. A higher ante indicates individuals are willing to pay more to enter a situation with potential reward while higher total reward risk suggests that the goal-objects need to be more rewarding for approach behavior. These findings align with altered reward sensitivity in depression and suggest an absence of feeling reward46. By contrast, insurance and consistency range were lower in participants with more severe depressive symptoms. Lower insurance reflects less effort to insure oneself against bad outcomes while a lower consistency range indicates a narrower gap between indifference and conflict; this is consistent with reduced variance measures with other illnesses47. These findings suggest a behavioral profile characterized by attenuated avoidance and more reward-driven judgments. Clinically, this may reflect compensatory approach behaviors in the context of anhedonia and may help differentiate depressive symptomatology from anxiety-driven avoidance.

In participants with more severe anxiety and depressive symptoms, peak positive risk was higher, while loss resilience and tradeoff range were lower. A higher peak positive risk suggests more uncertainty must be overcome to initiate approach behavior. Lower loss resilience indicates an attenuated ability to recover from loss, and a narrower tradeoff range implies participants had a restricted range in preferences. Clinically, these patterns may reflect motivational deficits and emotional blunting, which may be targeted to increase reward sensitivity (e.g., with behavioral activation-based therapies) and adaptive-decision making.

This study has several limitations. Clinical diagnostic assessments were not conducted to confirm the presence of depressive or anxiety disorders, limiting discriminating precision. The study utilized population survey data and surveys were not administered in controlled environments. Future research using large cohorts of individuals with clinically confirmed diagnoses are needed. Additionally, the large age range may have introduced developmental variability leading to potential cancellation of effects. Larger samples would allow age-stratified analysis and incorporation of comorbid illnesses (e.g., anxiety with depression). We also acknowledge that our cohort was drawn from a U.S. population and that cultural differences in emotional expression and symptom reporting could affect the generalizability of our findings48,49. Future studies that replicate these methods in culturally diverse or cross-national cohorts are needed to determine the cross-cultural stability of the judgment variables identified herein. Further work might also assess how constructs from behavioral economics, such as loss aversion, risk aversion, and loss resilience, might have conceptual overlap with temperament dimensions including harm avoidance and reward dependence50,51. Future studies that integrate computational judgment measures with measures of temperament could help clarify variance across cultures and clinical populations. Finally, the cross-sectional design of this study limits interpretation, as symptoms and functional impairments may have affected survey performance and response patterns.

This study reveals how judgment variables differentiated anxiety from depression. These findings suggest a route for rapid screening in cases where comorbidity is unlikely. Given the incidence of anxiety and depressive disorders52, quick, low-cost, and unbiased tools are essential to inform diagnostic approaches. Better diagnostic efficiency would likely improve access to effective treatment, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
