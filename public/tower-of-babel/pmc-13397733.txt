Mitochondria–ER contact sites (MERCS) in the cell cycle: molecular architecture and functional remodeling across cellular states
Authors: Eduardo Silva-Pavez, Bruno Torres-Gonzalez, Michelle Sotomayor, Benjamín Cartes-Saavedra, Dorian V Ziegler, Yessia Hidalgo-Fadic, Ulises Ahumada-Castro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondria–endoplasmic reticulum (ER) contact sites (MERCS) are nanoscopic, dynamic platforms integrating metabolism, signaling, and stress responses to regulate cell fate. These nanoscopic interfaces remodel continuously to meet the demands of proliferating, quiescent, and senescent cells. We synthesize evidence that MERCS actively coordinate local Ca2+ signaling, non-vesicular lipid transfer, and proteostasis to shape mitochondrial function and cellular homeostasis. We discuss how MERCS architecture changes across the cell cycle and during arrest, distinguishing adaptive from maladaptive remodeling, and consider therapeutic potential in aging and age-related disease.Keywords: Mitochondria–ER contact sites, Cell-cycle regulation, Calcium signaling, Lipid transfer, Cellular senescence

Membrane contact sites as key cellular signaling hubs

Membrane contact sites are regions where two subcellular organelles come into proximity without fusing, stabilized by specific tethering proteins and structurally modulated by lipid microdomains [1–3]. These junctions enable the non-vesicular exchange of ions (e.g., Ca2+), lipids (e.g., sterols, phospholipids), and metabolites [4, 5]. Their molecular features and extent of remodeling vary across cell types, tissues, physiological states, and pathologies. Membrane contact sites are heterogeneous and dynamic, comprising tethers, recruitment factors, regulators, and transporters, and they differ in spacing, membrane organization, and cargo to support signaling, metabolism, and organelle biogenesis [4]. Consequently, some authors have begun to refer to this integrated network as the “organelle contactome” [4]. This extensive contactome encompasses a diverse array of interfaces, such as those between the ER and the plasma membrane (ER-PM), which tightly regulate store-operated calcium entry and lipid homeostasis, or between the ER and endolysosomal compartments, which govern cholesterol transfer and organelle positioning [1, 2]. The coordination of cellular bioenergetics,mitochondrial dynamics, and stress signaling is paramount for determining cell cycle progression and arrest [6–8], our focus centers on the contact sites between mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (termed mitochondria–ER contact sites or MERCS). These specific interfaces have been extensively characterized [9, 10] and are widely regarded as signaling hubs that regulate processes ranging from mitochondrial network organization to cell death [11–14]. While this review focuses primarily on ER-mitochondria contacts, it is important to acknowledge that mitochondria do not operate in a bipartite vacuum. For example, the precise execution of mitochondrial division (a critical prerequisite for successful mitosis) involves a highly complex multi-organelle dance. Recent studies have demonstrated that the terminal steps of mitochondrial fission at ER-constriction sites require the recruitment of Golgi-derived phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate (PI4P)-containing vesicles and interactions with lysosomes [15]. Thus, while MERCS serve as the primary hubs for cell-cycle coordination, they frequently act in concert with a broader network of organelle interactions that collectively influence mitochondrial dynamics and, consequently, cell fate.

Among the critical decisions determining cell fate are cell cycle progression and its arrest. It has been suggested that MERCS organization, which facilitates Ca2+ transfer between organelles, lipid synthesis, and the regulation of proteostasis [9, 11, 16, 17], undergoes dynamic remodeling specifically to support cell cycle transitions. This hypothesis is supported by recent discoveries showing that MERCS density and topology shift markedly across cellular states [10]. Understanding the spatial and functional organization of MERCS during these states provides crucial insights into fundamental biological processes and potential therapeutic interventions. Below, we dissect the molecular architecture of MERCS, map their dynamics at key cell-cycle checkpoints, and examine how their rewiring contributes to arrest programs, ultimately exploring therapeutic avenues.

Architecture of mitochondria–ER contact sites

MERCS are specialized ultrastructural regions where the membranes of the ER and mitochondria are closely apposed, typically separated by a gap of 10–50 nm [11]. The structural and functional diversity of MERCS is maintained by a variety of tethering complexes and regulatory mechanisms, which are dynamically modulated across cellular states, including stress responses, metabolic transitions, and disease [9, 10]. Recent studies have highlighted the adaptive nature of MERCS, emphasizing their ability to remodel structurally in response to environmental and metabolic cues [17, 18]. Here, we focus on the potential of the cell cycle-associated aspects of MERCS, highlighting their structural tethering complexes alongside three key functional axes: Ca2+-regulated metabolism, lipid transfer, and proteostasis (Fig. 1).

A critical factor when interpreting MERCS dynamics is the methodological approach used for their quantification. Different techniques capture distinct features of these contacts, which can sometimes lead to apparent discrepancies in literature. Ultrastructural methods, such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM), provide high-resolution, static snapshots of physical proximity, gap width, and coverage. In contrast, fluorescent proximity reporters (e.g., SPLICS or split-GFP systems) dynamically measure the frequency and extent of specific gap distances in living cells. Conversely, functional assays, such as real-time inter-organelle Ca2+ imaging or lipid transfer tracking, capture the physiological throughput rather than purely physical tethering. Recognizing whether a study reports on a structural scaffold or a functional exchange is essential for reconciling heterogeneous findings across different cell-cycle phases and arrest states.

Ca2+ microdomains and mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake

Ca2+ signaling at MERCS primarily involves the directional transfer of Ca2+ from the ER lumen into the mitochondrial matrix. This localized flux is highly relevant for organelle function, as it stimulates mitochondrial bioenergetics to match cellular energy demands, while also dictating cell survival versus death thresholds. One of the earliest and best-characterized Ca2+ transfer modules at MERCS comprises ER inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3Rs) and mitochondrial voltage-dependent anion channels (VDAC1/2/3), which funnel Ca2+ to the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (mtCU) complex situated in the inner mitochondrial membrane [19–22]. Although the detailed molecular architecture of this module remains only partially resolved, IP3Rs are regarded as the predominant ER Ca2+ channels at MERCS [23], where they engage mitochondria via VDACs [21] and associate with adaptor proteins including transglutaminase 2 (TG2) [24], TOM70 [25], and the glucose-regulated protein 75 (GRP75) [26, 27]. While the mtCU is the best-described route for mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, additional pathways have been reported, such as leucine zipper–EF-hand-containing transmembrane 1 (LETM1), a protein localized to the inner mitochondrial membrane whose exact role as a Ca2+ rather than a K+/H+ exchanger remains a subject of ongoing debate [28]. Local, IP3R-dependent microdomains at the ER-mitochondria interface provide a so-called quasi-synaptic Ca2+ flux that overcomes “the low apparent affinity of the mtCU” [20, 29–32], thereby activating Ca2+-sensitive dehydrogenases to sustain oxidative phosphorylation [33]; excessive flux, however, predisposes to mitochondrial permeability transition and apoptosis [34–37].

Geometry is a key determinant of transfer efficiency, as synthetic linkers and engineered nanoscale proximity reporters show a bell-shaped dependence of transfer efficiency on ER-outer mitochondrial membrane spacing, with an optimum of ~ 20 nm, whereas both tighter (< 7 nm) and wider gaps diminish mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake and oxidative metabolism [38]. Tether availability and nanoscale organization further tune throughput. Two distinct mammalian tethering complexes operate at these interfaces: the well-established VAPB-PTPIP51 complex and the recently identified PDZD8-FKBP8 tether, which regulates mitochondrial complexity and morphology [39]. Within these contacts, VAPB molecules are highly dynamic, forming metastable subdomains characterized by millisecond-scale tether exchange [40]. This VAPB-PTPIP51 architecture is responsive to metabolic stress, expanding under starvation [41], and its integrity is disrupted by ALS-associated VAPB mutants [42, 43]. In contrast, PDZD8 dynamics are distinct from those of VAPB, showing longer dwell times at contact sites [40]. Under stress or specific physiological demands, additional recruiters enhance the Ca2+ module at MERCS: FUNDC1 binds IP3R2 in the heart [44], and the UPR sensor IRE1α, together with TOM70, promotes IP3R enrichment at contacts [25, 45], linking ER proteostasis with mitochondrial bioenergetics (see Proteostasis/UPR and stress signaling section).

Lipid transfer and membrane biogenesis

In mammals, MERCS enable non-vesicular lipid exchange essential for membrane biogenesis and mitochondrial function. Most prominently, they facilitate the delivery of phosphatidylserine (PS) synthesized in the ER to the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM), where it undergoes decarboxylation to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) by phosphatidylserine decarboxylase (PISD) [16, 46, 47]. This flow is mediated by complementary systems. Shuttle-type ORP5/ORP8 proteins localize at ER-mitochondria contact sites and interact with the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) protein PTPIP51. These proteins are physically coupled to the mitochondrial intermembrane bridging complex (MIB) and the mitochondrial contact site and cristae organizing system (MICOS), thereby establishing a continuous non-vesicular route for PS transfer from the ER, across the OMM, and through the intermembrane space to the IMM for PE synthesis [48–50]. Notably, because mitochondria lack PI4P, this ER-to-mitochondria PS transfer proceeds without the PI4P counter-exchange that characterizes ORP5/ORP8 activity at ER-plasma membrane contacts, a mechanism consistent with a down-hill lipid flow [51].

In parallel, SLC25A46 (the mammalian functional analogue of yeast Ugo1 at the OMM) participates in an ER-mitochondria pathway that facilitates lipid transfer [52]. Furthermore, bridge-like transporters (BLTPs) such as VPS13D span the inter-organelle gap to support bulk lipid flow. These transporters are recruited to the ER by VAPs via FFAT or phospho-FFAT motifs and to mitochondria by MIRO, effectively bridging the distance between membranes [53, 54]. Additional MERCS factors, such as Mitoguardin-2 (MIGA2), triage ER-mitochondria-lipid droplet interactions via a phospho-FFAT–VAPB interaction and can directly transfer lipids with a preference for PS, impacting mitochondrial morphology and adipogenesis [55–57].

Under mitochondrial oxidative stress, the VAPB-PTPIP51 tether is upregulated via phosphorylation of the PTPIP51 FFAT-like motif. Its TPR domain then mediates the transfer of lipid radicals from mitochondria to the ER, limiting mitochondrial lipid peroxidation and promoting cell survival, which highlights a protective lipid-handling role for this axis [58]. Finally, Mitofusin 2 (MFN2) can further modulate ER-mitochondria apposition to influence lipid exchange efficiency [59, 60]. Overall, MERCS integrate shuttle and bridge lipid-transfer machinery and adaptors to route lipids efficiently in space and time to meet the demands of membrane biogenesis and mitochondrial function.

Proteostasis/UPR and stress signaling

MERCS integrate ER proteostasis with mitochondrial function by positioning unfolded protein response (UPR) sensors and stress effectors at the ER–mitochondria interface [17]. One such stress sensor, PERK (protein kinase RNA-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase), is enriched at MERCS and, early during ER stress, forms a PERK–ERO1α complex that strengthens ER–mitochondria coupling and increases Ca2+ transfer [61, 62]; PERK also interacts with MFN2 in a manner essential for proper UPR signaling [63]. Furthermore, IRE1α recruits IP3Rs to MERCS through its C-terminal domain independently of its RNase/kinase activities [45], thereby modulating ER-to-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer and bioenergetics. With sustained stress, ERO1α-driven hyperoxidation augments IP3R activity, leading to redox “nanodomains” at MERCS. These nanodomains reinforce Ca2+–ROS crosstalk that can tilt the balance from adaptation to apoptosis [64]. Local high-concentration Ca2+ nanodomains, often reaching micromolar levels, form within tens of nanometers of open Ca2+ channels [65], and further tune Ca2+ flux through MERCS-resident proteins that influence SERCA activity [66]. These regulators include GPX8 [67] and TMX1 [68], which localize to the interface to modulate the local redox environment and indirectly regulate Ca2+ reuptake mediated by SERCA2b (one of multiple sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase isoforms). This downregulation of SERCA2b activity shapes basal ER Ca2+ levels and mitochondrial uptake. At the same time, the sigma-1 receptor (σ1R) dissociates from BiP/GRP78 to stabilize IP3Rs at contacts, and support survival signaling [69, 70].

MERCS also scaffold the proteostasis machinery, including ERAD E3 ligases (AMFR/gp78), linking degradation capacity to contact-site composition [71], and serve as platforms for autophagosome initiation [72], emphasizing their role in protein quality control. Under proteostatic stress, TBK1 is recruited and activated at MERCS via an AMFR–σ1R axis, coupling stress-granule and proteostasis responses to contact-site signaling [73]. Collectively, UPR nodes, ERAD/autophagy factors, and redox modulators at MERCS dynamically retune ER-to-mitochondria Ca2+ flux to balance ATP production, autophagy, and apoptosis across changing cellular states.

MERCS dynamics and roles in the cell cycle

While MERCS functions are associated with cell-cycle processes, the regulation of these functions across the cycle is also coordinated in part by cell-cycle kinases: Key cell-cycle kinases, such as Cdc2/Cyclin B1 and CDK5, can phosphorylate IP3Rs to tune inter-organelle Ca2+ throughput [74–76], while, reciprocally, disruption of mtCU-mediated Ca2+ uptake has been shown to perturb lipid metabolism and lead to PP4-dependent dephosphorylation of AMPK [77], revealing an indirect metabolic link between mitochondrial Ca2+ flux and energy-sensing pathways. This observation suggests that sustained Ca2+ transfer at MERCS may be required to maintain AMPK phosphorylation status, though whether this constitutes a direct feedback loop operating during normal cell-cycle progression remains to be established. Of note, CDK4 was recently shown to enhance MERCS formation and signaling in proliferative contexts, further linking cell cycle regulation to MERCs remodeling [78]. Alongside mitochondrial dynamics, MFN2 influences ER–mitochondria apposition [79–81], and DRP1-driven fission at ER-marked constriction sites ensures proper mitochondrial partitioning, coupling contact topology to organelle inheritance [82, 83]. This coupling is vital during the cell cycle, as the ER marks the mitochondrial division sites where fission factors accumulate to fragment the mitochondrial network, a process required for the segregation and inheritance of functional mitochondria in yeast and mammalian cells [84, 85]. Despite these insights, while MERCS have well-defined roles in processes required for cell-cycle control, their phase-specific behavior remains comparatively underexplored. In the following sections, we briefly summarize the available literature on how MERCS are dynamically remodeled, focusing on the G1/S boundary and mitosis where evidence is strongest, while explicitly acknowledging the G2 phase as a critical blind spot in our current understanding (Fig. 2).

MERCS expansion at the G1/S transition

The decision to transition from G1 into S phase is one of the most tightly regulated steps in the cell cycle, universally requiring a surge in biosynthetic activity and ATP production, alongside context-dependent adaptations in redox buffering [86]. Recently, a pivotal study identified the mitochondrial Rho GTPase MIRO1 as a critical regulator of MERCS, highlighting an unexpected role for this protein in their assembly and functional remodeling during early cell-cycle progression [87]. Specifically, in this study, the authors show that MERCS are more abundant during the G1/S transition than in quiescent (G0) cells. This increase in contact-site density correlates with enhanced mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake and elevated ATP levels, suggesting that MERCS remodeling serves to boost mitochondrial output at the onset of proliferation. Additionally, genetic deletion of MIRO1 completely abolished this increase in MERCS. MIRO1-deficient cells failed to enhance ER-mitochondria coupling upon serum stimulation. Together, these initial findings suggest a potential link between MIRO1-dependent MERCS formation, mitochondrial activation, and cell-cycle re-entry, though further studies across diverse models are needed to establish broad causality.

Mechanistically, MIRO1 has been shown to interact with GRP75 and the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (mtCU) [87, 88], and its presence is critical for the proper formation of the canonical IP3R/GRP75/VDAC1 tethering complex at MERCS. In line with a broader architectural role, MIRO proteins form nanometer-scale clusters on the OMM in association with the MICOS complex, which is required for normal cristae architecture and ER-mitochondria contact sites, and couple MICOS to TRAK adaptors to coordinate cristae distribution with mitochondrial transport [89]. MIRO1 function requires anchoring to the OMM and intact EF-hand domains, a calcium-binding structural motif present in many Ca2+ sensor/buffer proteins [90], suggesting that both mitochondrial localization and Ca2+ sensing are essential for promoting functional ER-to-mitochondria coupling.

The novelty of this mechanism lies in how MIRO1 couples Ca2+ sensing with bioenergetic activation by regulating the composition of MERCS [87]. Its identification as a mitochondrial-side regulator of the IP3R/GRP75/VDAC1 axis assembly reveals a new layer of control in the plasticity of contact sites during cell-cycle progression.

MERCS restructuring during mitosis

Although the evidence is still limited, recent work suggests that MERCS undergo distinct structural remodeling as cells enter mitosis. Zhao et al. reported that the inner nuclear membrane protein lamin B receptor (LBR), which typically resides at the nuclear envelope, redistributes to the ER during mitotic nuclear envelope breakdown [91]. At the ER, LBR engages VDAC2 on the OMM to form a transient tethering complex that markedly increases ER–mitochondria coupling during metaphase [91].

This LBR–VDAC2 complex mediates a distinct surge in mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, which temporally aligns with concomitant increases in oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production, both of which are required for spindle assembly and chromatid segregation. Interference with this complex, either genetic or pharmacologic, delays the metaphase-to-anaphase transition and can lead to incomplete cytokinesis [91]. Mechanistically, this response likely reflects both structural remodeling and the tuning of Ca2+ channel conductance by mitotic kinases such as CDK1, which phosphorylates IP3R [74, 75], and energy sensors like AMPK, which has been proposed to dampen mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake via mtCU regulation as a potential feedback mechanism to prevent ATP-consuming Ca2+ overload during mitotic stress [91].

Complementing these mechanistic insights, Yu et al. showed that MERCS also undergo structural expansion during mitosis across multiple cell types [92]. This lengthening of MERCS was associated with enhanced ER-to-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer, as confirmed by direct Ca2+ measurements and increased activity of Ca2+-dependent mitochondrial dehydrogenases [92]. Highlighting the evolutionary conservation of these structural principles, additional support comes from studies in yeast, where the tripartite mitochondria-ER-cortex anchor coordinates mitochondrial division and phosphoinositide signaling through its interaction with the ER protein Scs2, thereby linking MERCS integrity to cell-cycle progression [93].

Collectively, MERCS restructuring during mitosis may act as a tightly regulated bioenergetic amplifier and spatial coordinator that supports mitochondrial fission, helping to ensure faithful completion of mitosis and mitotic exit.

MERCS in arrest programs

Cell-cycle arrest is a fundamental biological response [94] in which cells actively halt their progression through the division cycle to repair damage, conserve energy, or prevent the propagation of genomic errors. Rather than being a single, static state, it encompasses a family of stress- and context-specific programs [95], such as hypomitogenic arrest, replication- and oxidative-stress arrest, quiescence, and senescence, each with distinct entry points and trajectories [96, 97]. Recent single-cell “arrest maps” underscore this diversity and the relative under-representation of arrest within canonical cycle frameworks [96]. Across stresses, cells diverge from the proliferative trajectory at different phases (e.g., after division or from G2), and many paths converge on a terminal senescent state with a G1-like molecular signature; in some contexts, mitotic skipping and polyploidy emerge as alternative outcomes, and partial re-entry from senescence can be driven by G1 cyclins [96, 98].

Distinct from these temporary or stress-induced cell cycle arrests, terminally differentiated post-mitotic cells—such as cardiomyocytes and mature neurons—represent an extreme state of permanent cell cycle exit where MERCS also play vital homeostatic roles. Unlike dividing cells (where MERCS must dynamically assemble and disassemble to coordinate fission, energy spikes, and mitosis), post-mitotic cells require highly stable and functionally specialized contact architectures to maintain lifelong homeostasis. For example, in adult cardiomyocytes, sarcoplasmic reticulum-mitochondria contacts are permanently maintained to continuously regulate excitation–contraction bioenergetics and massive Ca2+ fluxes without triggering apoptotic thresholds [99]. Similarly, in mature neurons, stable MERCS are required to support continuous lipid transport and local Ca2+ buffering along extended axonal distances, and their structural disruption has been increasingly implicated as a contributing pathogenic factor in several neurodegenerative diseases [100], though definitive causal evidence in human pathology remains limited. While senescent cells and post-mitotic cells both represent non-dividing states, senescent MERCS remodeling is largely driven by stress-response pathways and is associated with progressive organelle dysfunction. In contrast, post-mitotic MERCS are tightly regulated to preserve cellular longevity, highlighting the critical biological difference between a physiological terminal state and a pathological arrest.

Against this backdrop, MERCS, by coordinating Ca2+ flux, lipid routing, and stress signaling, are poised to modulate the depth, persistence, and potential reversibility of arrest programs. We therefore frame arrest as an integral module of the cell-cycle logic and examine how MERCS may be reconfigured to support quiescence-like states, defined by reduced coupling and bioenergetic downshift, and senescence, marked by sustained coupling and metabolic rewiring (Fig. 2).

MERCS in G0/G1 cell cycle arrest

Quiescence is typically characterized by markedly reduced mitochondrial activity [101, 102]. In this context, Endoni et al. described that fibroblasts synchronized in G0/G1 by serum deprivation exhibit a reduction in wide MERCS (≥ 40 nm, detected via the SPLICS-L sensor) [87]. These findings reflect a diminished functional interaction between the ER and mitochondria. Consistent with this, serum-starved cells display lower basal mitochondrial Ca2+ levels and reduced ATP production, suggesting a functional silencing of mitochondrial activation via MERCS [87]. Nevertheless, the distinction between G0 and G1 was not explicitly assessed, as the 72-h serum deprivation likely synchronized cells into a mixed G0/G1 state. Therefore, the term “quiescence” may not strictly describe the state of these cells.

In contrast, it has been described that enforced upregulation of MFN2 in serum-deprived vascular smooth muscle cells arrests the cell cycle, which correlates with mitochondrial fusion and an increased and sustained ER and mitochondria tethering. Specifically, MFN2 overexpression caused G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, whereas MFN2 knockdown arrested cells in S phase [103]. These seemingly contradictory findings, a reduction of wide MERCS during starvation-induced quiescence in fibroblasts versus hyper-tethering driving arrest in smooth muscle cells, likely stem from distinct experimental approaches. The former captures an adaptive structural response to nutrient deprivation [87], whereas the latter demonstrates the consequences of enforced molecular tethering [103]. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that the dynamic remodeling of ER-mitochondria contacts, both their assembly and disassembly, governs cell-cycle progression at specific phases. Expansion of MERCS at the G1/S boundary may support cell cycle progression, while failure to either establish or resolve these contacts appropriately at key checkpoints may trigger cell cycle arrest. Together, these context-dependent observations suggest a model in which MERCS dynamics operate as a bidirectional regulatory node, potentially contributing to either proliferation or arrest depending on the specific cellular phase, cell type, and the nature of the stress.

MERCS in senescence

Senescent cells exhibit a unique and sustained remodeling of MERCS, in contrast to the transient MERCS fluctuations observed during the cell cycle. These long-lived, non-dividing cells maintain elevated mitochondrial activity to support the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), and MERCS have emerged as central players in this metabolic reprogramming [104–106]. It has been reported that stress-induced senescent cells display a significant increase in both the length and frequency of MERCS [107], as well as an upregulation of the canonical IP3R/GRP75/VDAC1 tethering complex and MFN2 expression [108–112]. This structural remodeling promotes sustained ER–mitochondria Ca2+ flux, enhancing pyruvate dehydrogenase activation via PDP2 and fueling the tricarboxylic acid cycle [105, 106, 108, 113].

By contrast, in therapy-induced senescence (TIS), ER–mitochondria contacts expand, while ER-to-mitochondria Ca2+ flux decreases, consistent with reduced IP3R isoform expression and diminished IP3R1–VDAC1 proximity; TEM analysis confirms increased MERCS coverage with a modest widening of the ER–mitochondria gap in TIS [109]. This apparent paradox highlights a fundamental concept in contactology: an increase in the physical extent or number of MERCS does not automatically equate to increased Ca2+ transfer. Functional throughput is strictly dictated by the precise inter-organelle spacing, where even a modest widening disrupts optimal transfer [38], and the specific composition of the tethers, as structural expansion can occur independently of Ca2+ channel enrichment. These context-dependent findings are further supported by additional work identifying MERCS as essential components in the establishment and maintenance of cellular senescence.

For instance, transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily C member 3 (TRPC3) downregulation leads to enhanced ER–mitochondria Ca2+ transfer and mitochondrial Ca2+ overload, boosting oxidative phosphorylation and stabilizing the senescent state in fibroblasts [110]. Re-expression of TRPC3 diminishes this Ca2+ flux and allows escape from senescence, illustrating the feedback between MERCS remodeling and metabolic signaling. Notably, TRPC3 deficiency also stabilizes a pro-inflammatory and tumor-promoting secretome in senescent stromal cells, reinforcing the role of MERCS-driven Ca2+ transfer in sustaining the SASP [110].

Loss-of-function studies further underscore the role of Ca2+ signaling via MERCS in senescence regulation. Knockout of the ER Ca2+ channel IP3R2 or the mtCU blocks mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake and prevents the establishment of both oncogene-induced and replicative senescence [108, 114]. Conversely, artificially enforced ER–mitochondria tethering can trigger premature senescence, as shown by Ziegler et al. [108]. Consistently, pharmacological inhibition of IP3Rs with desmethylxestospongin B (dmXeB) selectively compromises TIS cell viability in vitro and reduces senescent burden in vivo in p16-3MR mice, highlighting ER–mitochondria Ca2+ flux as a senolytic vulnerability [109].

Environmental and chemical stressors have also been shown to induce MERCS-dependent senescence. For example, exposure to aflatoxin B1, a genotoxic molecule, causes premature aging of mouse skin by increasing ER-mitochondria contacts via the recruitment of the tethering factors MIGA and Vap33 (as demonstrated in Drosophila models), leading to Ca2+ overload, ROS generation, and impaired mitophagy [115]. Likewise, pharmacological activation of SERCA by CDN1163 promotes mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation, membrane hyperpolarization, and G1-phase cell-cycle arrest. These effects mirror senescence-related mitochondrial dysfunction [116].

Finally, mechanical factors, such as extracellular matrix stiffness, have been linked to MERCS regulation in vascular smooth muscle cells. High matrix stiffness enhances MFN2 expression and IP3R/VDAC1 proximity, increasing ER–mitochondria coupling and Ca2+ transfer, which promotes vascular smooth muscle cell senescence and contributes to vascular aging [117].

Altogether, these findings support a model in which MERCS function as persistent metabolic relays in senescent cells, sustaining their elevated energy demands and reinforcing cell-cycle arrest. Rather than merely being a consequence of senescence, emerging evidence suggests that MERCS remodeling acts as an active mechanism capable of inducing and maintaining the senescent state, highlighting these contact sites as promising senescence-modifying targets for therapeutic intervention.

Open questions and future directions as concluding remarks

MERCS are increasingly viewed as dynamic nodes that synchronize proliferation and arrest decisions [118]. However, while specific cell cycle phase-dependent components have been identified (Table 1), our molecular understanding remains fragmentary, and key causal links and shared standards have yet to be established. Crucially, the G2 phase currently represents a “dark matter” interval in the organelle contactome; decoding this blind spot is paramount, as it likely harbors the transient structural checkpoints that convert the metabolic momentum of replication into the physical readiness required for mitosis.

First, we lack definitive, phase- and arrest-resolved causality maps for specific tethers and channels. Dissecting when MFN2, VAPB–PTPIP51, or the IP3R–GRP75–VDAC–mtCU axis drive discrete outputs (ATP supply, Ca2+ thresholds, fate switches) will require minimal, reversible perturbations that avoid network rewiring. For example, synthetic linkers and engineered nanoscale proximity reporters could be used to titrate ER–OMM spacing, and inducible, low-expression linkers could be employed to test necessity and sufficiency across cell-cycle phases and arrest programs [4, 9].

The variability across cell types and diseases requires systematic mapping. The organelle contactome is likely tissue-tuned; in vivo atlases should resolve how MERCS differ in neurons, hepatocytes, immune, and stromal cells, and how they rewire in senescence and cancer, comprehensively. Interventions must also weigh benefits versus toxicity carefully. Because MERCS are kinetic assemblies, hyper-stabilization can misroute signaling or precipitate Ca2+ overload; dose-, time-, and kinetics-aware strategies with biomarker-guided gating are essential. Looking ahead, tool development should proceed on multiple fronts: small molecules [119], CRISPR–Cas9/base-editing to minimally retune tethers/channels/regulators [120, 121], and transient mRNA delivery in lipid nanoparticles to express spacers/linkers for titratable geometry control [112, 122]. Together with phase-specific biosensors and super-resolution/EM pipelines, these approaches could underpin spatiotemporal MERCS atlases across cycle stages and arrest programs, closing current gaps and de-risking translation at the MERCS–arrest interface, ultimately informing therapeutic design decisions.

Looking forward, a critical frontier lies in the targeted perturbation of MERCS to therapeutically dictate cell fate. Pharmacological or genetic interventions aimed at upstream regulators of these contact sites, such as modulating VDAC1/IP3R complexes or targeting specific tethering proteins, could potentially shift the balance between proliferation, arrest, and senescence. For instance, actively loosening persistent MERCS in senescent cells might alleviate the sustained Ca2+ influx and metabolic dysfunction that drive tissue aging and SASP. Conversely, given that artificially enforced ER–mitochondria tethering can trigger premature senescence [108], it is tempting to speculate that controlled stabilization of MERCS could be explored as an anti-proliferative strategy in oncology. However, this hypothesis requires careful consideration of the dual nature of senescence: while arrest itself is tumor-suppressive, the associated SASP can paradoxically promote tumor progression in neighboring cells [110]. Preclinical validation across multiple cancer models, with careful characterization of secretome consequences, would be essential before this concept could advance. Thus, viewing MERCS not merely as passive structural responders, but as druggable upstream hubs, opens novel translational avenues for managing age-related diseases and hyperproliferative disorders.

Finally, we hope this review serves as an ignition point: by outlining what is known, and largely unknown, about MERCS across the cell cycle and arrest, we open a broader problem space regarding how the spatial choreography of organelles and their contact-site geometry governs physiology, fate, and pathology.
