Prediction of high-quality human oocytes and embryos through the analysis of gene expression in cumulus cells
Authors: Saffet Ozturk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe selection of high-quality oocytes and preimplantation embryos is becoming increasingly important because of delaying childbearing due to socioeconomic and career planning reasons. Gene expression analysis of cumulus cells serves as a reliable indicator for assessing oocyte quality and resulting embryos owing to their close communication and bidirectional interactions with maturing oocytes. For this purpose, the expression of cumulus cell genes involved in cumulus expansion, extracellular matrix organization, intracellular signaling, cell proliferation, metabolism, and apoptosis has been evaluated to determine their associations with oocyte quality, embryo development, and pregnancy outcomes. Consequently, this review synthesizes current studies on the topic and discusses the potential of cumulus cell gene expression analysis in predicting oocyte competence and early embryonic developmental potential.Keywords: Oocyte competence, Embryo quality, Cumulus cells, Gene expression, Infertility

Introduction

The primary objective of assisted reproduction treatments is to enhance the likelihood of healthy birth rates. Selecting high-quality oocytes and embryos from a pool with the potential to produce viable blastocysts and ultimately a healthy baby is a crucial aim of assisted reproductive technology (ART) centers. In ART centers, competent oocytes and embryos can be identified based on several morphological and morphokinetic characteristics, including zona pellucida thickness, cytoplasmic granularity, cleavage rate, fragmentation degree, and polar body extrusion [1, 2]. The available evidence suggests that morphological evaluation is not a reliable indicator of oocyte and embryo health. Indeed, approximately 40% of morphologically normal embryos had abnormal chromosomes [3]. Morphokinetic analyses appear to be inadequate for precisely identifying oocytes and embryos with high developmental potential. Therefore, it is necessary to have objective, accurate, fast, consistent, and affordable assays to predict good-quality oocytes and embryos. This approach is anticipated to improve pregnancy success rates and increase the proportion of live births, while reducing the number of embryos transferred.

Frequent removal of oocytes and embryos from the incubator may adversely affect their developmental potential [4]. Furthermore, invasive procedures may compromise oocyte and embryo viability and increase procedural costs [5]. Consequently, non-invasive approaches-such as the analysis of follicular fluid, culture media, and cumulus cells-represent a rational strategy for assessing the developmental competence of oocytes and their resulting embryos. It is well-established that granulosa cells surrounding oocytes differentiate into two subtypes during follicular development: cumulus cells around oocytes and mural cells surrounding follicular antrum in preovulatory follicles [6]. The mural granulosa cells produce estrogen and progesterone, while cumulus cells contribute to oocyte maturation through both direct and indirect mechanisms. Given that oocytes tightly regulate the functional characteristics of granulosa cells during follicular development, the cumulus cells derived from these granulosa cells can serve as valuable biomarkers for predicting oocyte and embryo competence [7]. To this end, the expression of cumulus genes involved in cumulus expansion, extracellular matrix formation, intracellular signaling, proliferation, metabolism, and apoptosis has been evaluated to investigate their associations with oocyte-, embryo-, and pregnancy-related outcomes. In addition to gene expression, assessments of telomere length, DNA fragmentation, antioxidant capacity, and mitochondrial DNA content in cumulus cells have been employed to predict these characteristics.

The utilization of biomarkers for oocyte and embryo selection can facilitate the achievement of a successful pregnancy while minimizing the costs associated with ART. In addition, the transfer of several embryos during the early cleavage or blastocyst stage is known to raise the likelihood of multiple pregnancies, which can, in turn, result in elevated fetal morbidity and mortality [8]. The identification of a non-invasive biomarker that can predict competence oocytes and embryos has the potential to enhance the success of single embryo transfer procedures. This, in turn, could result in a reduction in the number of laboratory procedures and minimizing the costs associated with ART cycles. In the following sections of this review, after outlining the structural and functional characteristics of cumulus cells, various analytical approaches employed to assess their potential as biomarkers are described and critically discussed.

Functional importance of cumulus cells in oocyte maturation and onwards

In preovulatory follicles, during ovulation, and following ovulation, cumulus cells remain in close proximity to the oocyte and maintain continuous bidirectional communication (Fig. 1). These cells play roles in mediating oocyte maturation, fertilization, and early preimplantation embryo development [9, 10]. During the peri-ovulatory period, cumulus cells undergo expansion in response to the LH surge, forming a radiating layer around the oocyte [11].

The close interaction of cumulus cells with metaphase II (MII) oocyte using gap junctions (GJs). Oocyte-secreted factors (OSFs) play a role in regulating a number of cumulus cell-related processes, including differentiation, proliferation, expansion, steroidogenesis, apoptosis, and levels of luteinizing hormone (LH), cyclooxygenase 2 (COX2), and prostaglandin (PG) receptors. Similarly, cumulus-secreted factors (CSFs) modulate several oocyte-associated processes, including nuclear maturation, meiotic resumption and arrest, as well as metabolic support. cAMP, cyclic adenosine monophosphate; cGMP, cyclic adenosine monophosphate.

Bidirectional paracrine communication occurs between the oocyte and surrounding cumulus cells. Oocyte-secreted factors, including growth differentiation factor 9 (GDF9), bone morphogenetic protein 15 (BMP15), and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs), regulate cumulus cell proliferation, differentiation, and metabolic activity, particularly glycolysis [12] (Fig. 1). On the other hand, cumulus cells also secrete extracellular vesicles and exosomes containing microRNAs, mRNAs, and proteins, which contribute to the acquisition of oocyte competence [13]. Oocyte microvilli and cumulus cell membrane fusion, a form of cytoplasmic sharing, facilitate molecular exchange between these cell types and support the formation of trans-zonal projections (TZPs) [14]. It is important to note that TZPs extending from cumulus cells and oocytes through zona pellucida contribute to connecting cumulus cells with oolemma [15].

The gap junctions at TZPs connecting cumulus cells and oocytes facilitate a bidirectional exchange of signal molecules, including cAMP, cGMP, small proteins, and amino acids [9, 12]. These molecules, specifically cAMP and cGMP, regulate oocyte maturation by inhibiting spontaneous meiotic activation prior to receiving ovulatory signals. Cumulus cells also provide an energy source for the growth and development of oocytes through carbohydrate metabolism [16]. During the LH surge, cumulus cells facilitate the transmission of signaling molecules from the follicular environment to the oocyte, thereby promoting meiotic resumption [17].

Since cumulus cells are typically discarded following the retrieval of denuded oocytes, they represent a readily available source for various molecular and biochemical analyses. Given that cumulus cells share the same microenvironmental conditions as the oocytes, their evaluation may yield objective insights into oocyte quality. Therefore, cumulus cells are regarded as a primary target for non-invasive assessments of oocyte competence. This is particularly relevant in countries such as Germany and Poland, and formerly in Italy [18], where legal restrictions on embryo cryopreservation limit the number of embryos that can be produced in the ART centers. The following section describes gene expression analyses of cumulus cells aimed at identifying molecular markers associated with competent oocytes and embryos.

Cumulus expansion and extracellular matrix formation-related genes

The cumulus-expressing genes, including prostaglandin endoperoxide synthase 2 [PTGS2, also known as cyclooxinase 2 (COX2)], hyaluronan synthase 2 (HAS2), gremlin 1 (GREM1), and steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (STAR) to inhibit expression of the LH receptor (LHR) and urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA) gene are primarily implicated in the processes of cumulus expansion and extracellular matrix formation [19, 20]. The transforming growth factor β (TGFβ)-related GDF9 gene is responsible for stimulating expression of these genes and successful follicular development [21]. It is known that GDF9 takes part in regulation of cholesterol biosynthesis [22], cumulus expansion, follicle stimulating hormone (FSH)-mediated steroidogenesis as well as in increased expression of LH, COX2 and type 2 prostaglandin receptors in cumulus cells of mouse preovulatory follicles [19, 23]. Elevated expression of GDF9 in cumulus cells has been correlated with the development of high-quality oocytes and improved pregnancy outcomes [24]. Kahraman et al., (2018) also revealed an association between up-regulated GDF9 expression and blastocyst quality [25]. In parallel, a recent study demonstrated a positive correlation between oocyte quality and increased GDF9 mRNA expression levels [7].

The PTGS2 protein is involved in the synthesis of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which is a key factor in the process of cumulus expansion [26]. PTGS2 is also implicated in several biological processes, including development of lesions, inflammatory response, and cell proliferation [27]. The expression of PTGS2 in human cumulus cells was found to be positively correlated with oocyte nuclear maturation [28–30]. Moreover, it was higher in the cumulus cells of human oocytes that produced high-quality embryos than in those that produced low-quality embryos [7, 31]. However, Feuerstein et al., (2007) observed an inverse relationship between PTGS2 expression and developmental ability up to the embryonic stages of day 5 or 6 [28]. The cumulus cells of oocytes that resulted in pregnancy and live birth exhibited elevated PTGS2 expression levels [32] (Table 1). Similarly, in patients with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), the PTGS2 mRNA level was higher in the cumulus cells of mature oocytes than in immature ones [42]. PTGS2 expression elevated in the cumulus cells of high-quality embryos in comparison to those of low-quality embryos [42]. Consequently, PTGS2 expression may serve as a predictive marker of oocyte and embryo developmental potential in women undergoing in vitro fertilization (IVF) with intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) as well as in those with PCOS.

A similar relationship was also observed between PTGS2 and prostaglandin E receptor 2 (EP2) mRNA levels and the maturation time and quality of bovine oocytes [43]. Upregulation of PTGS2 gene expression in porcine cumulus cells was associated with an improvement in the oocyte competence [44]. The expression of Ptgs2 in mouse cumulus cells also correlated with the transition from GV to MII stages [45]. Expectedly, loss of Ptgs2 in mice has been demonstrated to impair ovulation and fertilization, as well as decidualization and implantation [46]. In the different species, PTGS2 expression is associated with oocyte maturation and cumulus expansion, which have been identified as a clinical pregnancy predictor [28, 29, 47]. However, there was a negative correlation between PTGS2 expression in cumulus cells and embryonic developmental stage on day 3 in ICSI patients [33]. All these studies indicate that altered expression of PTGS2 in cumulus cells influences oocyte maturation and onwards possibly by changing bidirectional interactions between oocytes and cumulus cells as well as impairing cumulus expansion.

The HAS2 gene encodes an enzyme responsible for the biosynthesis of hyaluronic acid, a critical component required for the expansion and structural organization of the cumulus cell matrix [35, 48]. In other words, HAS2 is involved in extracellular matrix remodelling essential for ovulation and fertilization, and its expression is related to embryonic development [10]. The expression of HAS2 is regulated through a protein kinase A (PKA)-dependent signaling pathway activated in response to follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) [49]. The HAS2 expression was observed to be at higher levels in the cumulus cells of human oocytes that produced embryos of a higher quality, as compared to those of a lower quality [31]. Furthermore, its mRNA level elevated in the cumulus cells of oocytes that produced high-quality embryos on day 3, in comparison to the cumulus cells of oocytes that could be fertilized or developed into poor-quality embryos [35] (Table 1). Similar to observations in humans, increased HAS2 expression in the cumulus cells of bovine oocytes has been associated with enhanced developmental competence of the oocytes [50].

HAS2 expression was significantly lower in the cumulus cells surrounding MII-stage oocytes compared with those associated with GV or MI oocytes in women undergoing IVF with single embryo transfer [34]. There was also a correlation between higher HAS2 expression and good-quality blastocyst compared with the embryos that could not progress to blastocyst stage [34]. Similarly, Kahraman et al., (2018) revealed a correlation between up-regulated HAS2 expression and blastocyst quality in the cumulus cells of cumulus oocyte complexes (COCs) obtained from infertile women [25]. A recently published study has revealed a positive correlation between oocyte quality and HAS2 mRNA level in women with PCOS [51]. Given that the HAS2 protein participates in cumulus expansion, regulation of cumulus cell apoptosis, and glucose metabolism [51], its expression appears to be closely linked to the generation of high-quality oocytes and subsequent embryo development.

The GREM1 protein acts as an antagonist of BMP and TGFβ signaling pathways, and in the context of ART, it regulates the balance between GDF9 and BMP15 signaling [20, 52]. GREM1 is also involved in the luteinization of mural granulosa cells and cumulus expansion [20]. Therefore, GREM1 expression may serve as a predictive biomarker for assessing oocyte maturity, fertilization potential, and the developmental quality of resulting embryos [31]. GREM1 expression was significantly higher in oocytes that yielded embryos of superior quality compared with those that produced embryos of lower quality [31, 35]. While Anderson et al., [29] reported a weak positive association between GREM1 expression in cumulus cells and embryo quality, Wathlet et al., [30] identified a robust positive correlation with both embryo quality and blastocyst morphology [7, 30]. Elevated GREM1 expression in cumulus cells is associated with rapidly developing embryos (≥ 7 cells) and is also observed more frequently in older women [33]. Notably, there was a positive correlation between GREM1 expression and the quality of oocytes from women with PCOS [51]. Collectively, these findings suggest that GREM1 expression may serve as a biomarker for predicting oocyte and embryo quality in women undergoing ART and in those with PCOS.

Versican (VCAN) is a principal component of the extracellular matrix in COCs, and its expression in cumulus cells is upregulated in response to elevated FSH levels [53]. VCAN has been shown to stimulate PTGS expression in both mouse embryos and cumulus cells [36]. A significant negative correlation was observed between gonadotropin-dependent VCAN expression in cumulus cells and oocyte maturity in women undergoing controlled ovarian stimulation with ICSI [33]. Wathlet et al., (2011) observed a reduction in VCAN expression of cumulus cells of mature oocytes in women undergoing ICSI at an ART Centre [30]. However, a positive correlation was identified between the quality of human oocytes and elevated VCAN expression as a result of epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like activity because VCAN includes an EGF motif [7, 36]. The VCAN expression increased in the cumulus cells of oocytes that produced high-quality embryos [37, 38]. However, Hammond et al., (2015) reported that lower VCAN expression is associated with high-quality embryos on day 5 [54]. It is important to note that VCAN expression exhibits variability according to a woman’s age and body mass index (BMI) [38]. In addition to oocyte and embryo quality, its expression in cumulus cells was found to be associated with an increased probability of pregnancy [7, 55] and live birth rates [32]. In women with PCOS, the VCAN expression in cumulus cells obtained from in vitro matured oocytes with amphiregulin (AREG) administration showed a positive correlation with embryo quality [56], as well as with quality of the oocytes obtained from these patients [51]. Taken together, the findings of these studies suggest that VCAN plays a role in enhancing the developmental potential of oocytes and embryos, as well as the rates of pregnancy and live births. This seems to be achieved by increasing EGF-like signaling pathway, which is recognized as essential for oocyte growth, maturation, and developmental competence [57].

Pentraxin 3 (PTX3) belongs to the superfamily of evolutionarily conserved proteins and contributes to maintaining the structural stability of the cumulus matrix, a process essential for normal oocyte development, ovulation, and fertilization [58]. The cumulus cells of the fertilized human oocytes exhibited a higher level of PTX3 mRNA than the cumulus cells from unfertilized oocytes [39]. These fertilized oocytes were able to produce normal embryos on day 3. The PTX3 expression was at high levels in the cumulus cells of women who had undergone an ICSI procedure and subsequently become pregnant [40]. In the PCOS patients, PTX3 mRNA level was found to be at higher levels in the cumulus cells of mature [42] and good-quality [51] oocytes than immature oocytes’ cumulus cells. However, cumulus cells surrounding oocytes from PCOS patients that were fertilized and exhibited two normal pronuclei showed lower PTX3 expression compared with those from oocytes that were fertilized and displayed more than two pronuclei [59]. Similarly, PTX3 expression was elevated in the cumulus cells associated with high-quality embryos compared with those related to low-quality embryos [42]. Overall, PTX3 expression in cumulus cells may be used to predict developmental competence of oocytes and quality of the resulting embryos even in women with PCOS.

The disintegrin and metalloprotease with thrombospondin type 1 motif 1 (ADAMTS1) protein belongs to the proteinase family and exhibits catalytic activity towards proteoglycans, including aggrecan and versican, which results in reorganization of extracellular matrix (ECM) [60–62]. The elevated ADAMTS1 gene expression in cumulus cells is positively correlated with mature oocytes, transferable embryos, and high-quality embryos [41]. The perlecan/heparan sulfate proteoglycan 2 (HSPG2) gene is responsible for the production of perlecan at high levels within ovarian ECM. Perlecan serves as a substrate for ADAMTS1 during ovulation and plays a role in molecular stabilization and cell adhesion [60, 63]. In contrast to ADAMTS1, lower HSPG2 gene expression in cumulus cells are positively correlated with oocyte maturity, transferable embryos, and good-quality embryos [41]. Expression of these two genes appears to be involved in the organization of ECM among cumulus cells, which is associated with molecular transport between oocyte and these cells. Expression of these genes may indirectly affect oocyte and embryo development.

The inflammation-associated protein TNFAIP6 (TNF alpha induced protein 6, also known as TSG-6) is involved in the covalent transfer of heavy chains of IαI proteins to hyaluronan in COCs [64]. This protein is produced by granulosa and cumulus cells following ovulatory stimulation [65]. Consistently, Tnfaip6-null mice exhibited impaired cumulus mucification, which ultimately led to female infertility [66]. Thus, TNFAIP6 functions in the formation and remodelling of cumulus extracellular matrix. Its gene expression was lower in fertilized oocytes than in unfertilized oocytes [38]. In parallel, reduced TNFAIP6 expression was observed in the cumulus cells of infertile women with PCOS [67].

The brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is involved in the process of cumulus expansion and embryonic development [29], in addition to follicle growing and primordial follicle activation [68]. Consistently, its deficiency resulted in impairments in follicular development and oocyte survival in the follicles [69]. Its expression in human cumulus cells is regulated by the reproductive hormone LH [70], and it contributes to improving oocyte maturation and embryo development [71]. In women undergoing ICSI, BDNF expression was lower in the cumulus cells surrounding oocytes that exhibited normal fertilization [29]. Its expression further exhibited a weak predictor of embryo quality [29]. The precise mechanism by which BDNF influences oocyte and embryo quality through the regulation of cumulus expansion remains unknown.

As discussed above, several genes play key roles in regulating cumulus expansion, as well as in the formation and remodeling of the ECM. The upregulation or downregulation of these genes in cumulus cells correlates positively with oocyte and embryo quality, as well as with pregnancy and live birth rates (Table 1). A deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying these associations will enhance the reliability and applicability of these expression patterns as potential biomarkers.

Intracellular signaling-related genes

Intracellular signaling under the actions of kinases, G-protein coupled, TGFβ, and Ca2+ molecules in cumulus cells closely regulates own cellular functions and their intercellular communications with oocytes. For instance, calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase ID (CAMK1D), a member of the Ca²⁺/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase 1 subfamily of serine/threonine kinases, has been identified as being expressed in cumulus cells [72]. CAMK1D is involved in the regulation or activation of genes having roles in cellular differentiation and apoptosis, as well as in the control of steroidogenesis during folliculogenesis [55, 72]. A recent study reported a positive correlation between oocyte quality and increased mRNA expression levels of the CAMK1D gene [7] (Table 2).

➤Control of cellular energy

Another steroidogenic activity-related gene, STAR is produced by granulosa and cumulus cells in response to LH surge [87]. STAR contributes to the regulation of cholesterol transport into the inner mitochondrial membrane, and is also involved in MAPK activity, controlling steroidogenesis in COCs [88]. Deficiency of Star gene in mice caused failure in follicular maturation and ovulation [89]. The STAR gene expression was reduced in the cumulus cells of high-quality oocytes and embryos compared to the cumulus cells of low-quality oocytes and embryos [37]. Given that STAR also participates in various cellular processes, including progesterone synthesis in granulosa cells [90] and the regulation of oocyte nuclear maturation [28], the observed association between oocyte or embryo quality and altered STAR expression may be attributable to these additional functions of this intracellular signaling protein.

The reproductive hormone progesterone plays a pivotal role in both the successful establishment and maintenance of pregnancy. It performs these functions by interacting with the intracellular specific receptor, designated as the progesterone receptor (PR). The ligand-receptor complex results in the regulation of transcription of target genes [91]. In preovulatory follicles, LH and FSH can stimulate PR mRNA expression via the cAMP-dependent signaling pathway [92]. The elevated secretion of progesterone and the presence of its PRs in cumulus cells promote meiotic resumption in porcine oocytes and enhance early embryonic development to the blastocyst stage following IVF [93]. The presence of PRs in cumulus cells leads to reduced proliferative activity, inhibition of apoptosis, diminished cumulus expansion, and the closure of gap junctions within these cells [94, 95]. Progesterone has also been shown to downregulate connexin 43 expression in cumulus cells via a PR-mediated pathway, an effect associated with the resumption of meiosis [94].

The PR mRNA level was at lower levels in the cumulus cells of human embryos on day 3 with good morphology in comparison to those with poor morphology [73]. However, no significant differences were detected in either fertilization or cleavage rates. Gene expression profiling of elevated PR levels in cumulus cells enabled the selection of MII oocytes of superior quality-reflected by enhanced blastocyst development and live birth rates-compared with randomly selected oocytes [34]. PR expression was detected in the dominant follicles in humans, and its increased expression in cumulus cells correlated with oocyte competence [74]. In addition to its genomic effects mediated through ligand-activated transcription factors for direct transcriptional regulation, progesterone can also exert non-genomic actions via membrane-bound PRs. Therefore, the functional impact of PRs in cumulus cells may, in part, be mediated by the non-genomic actions of progesterone.

Transient receptor potential cation channel, subfamily M, member 7 (TRPM7) plays a role in the activation of downstream G-coupled receptors and in responding to intracellular and extracellular Mg2+, pH, and phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) [96]. Furthermore, TRPM7 regulates Mg2+ levels, which are upregulated in the cumulus cells of MII oocytes [20, 30]. A significant positive correlation was identified between TRPM7 expression in cumulus cells and the development of early embryos displaying high quality and optimal blastocyst morphology [30]. It has also been demonstrated that TRPM7 expression can serve as a predictive marker for embryo development at the cleavage stage with improved accuracy The inositol-trisphosphate 3-kinase A (ITPKA) is involved in regulating TRPM7 protein level and ITPKA activity is controlled by the calmodulin-Ca2+ complex [97]. The ITPKA expression was elevated in the cumulus cells of MII oocytes, and this issue contributed to predicting oocyte maturity and developing better cleavage-stage embryos [30]. These findings indicate that the regulation of intracellular ionic concentrations, including Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺, within cumulus cells is critical for the generation of high-quality oocytes and embryos.

Syndecan 4 (SDC4) is expressed in mouse cumulus cells and plays a role in modulating signaling pathways associated with TGFβ, FGF, and VEGF [33]. The SDC4 expression in cumulus cells demonstrated a positive correlation with the development of high-quality embryos and favorable blastocyst morphology [30]. Furthermore, the same study indicated that SDC4 expression may serve as a predictive marker for pregnancy outcomes. SDC4 is involved in follicular development through regulating the AMH, inhibin, and activin receptors through acting on TGFβ pathway as well as in the regulation of cumulus cell development and function through FGF signaling [98, 99]. These effects appear to exert direct or indirect influences on oocyte maturation and early embryo development by controlling intracellular responses of cumulus cells.

Prokineticin 1 (PROK1, also named EG-VEGF for endocrine gland-derived vascular endothelial growth factor) exerts its effects through binding to two G protein-coupled receptors, namely prokineticin receptor 1 (PROKR1) and 2 (PROKR2) [100]. In addition to corpus luteum [101], PROK1 expression was detected in oocytes and granulosa cells from primordial to preovulatory follicles in human ovaries [102]. It is noteworthy that this protein is abundantly secreted by granulosa and cumulus cells, accounting for its elevated concentration in follicular fluid [103]. Altered PROK1 expression has been shown to be associated with the development of ovarian disorders [104]. The expression and secretion levels of PROK1 in cumulus cells may serve as indicators for predicting human oocyte maturity [75].

Another oocyte maturity-related factor, chemokine C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8, also known as IL8), expressed in ovarian cells and follicles and plays roles in follicular development and ovulation [105, 106]. CXCL8 is responsible for the activation and chemotaxis of granulocytes and leukocytes, thereby enabling the aforementioned functions [107]. CXCL8 expression at both mRNA and protein levels in cumulus cells showed an inverse correlation with oocyte maturation in women undergoing ICSI treatment for female factor infertility [76] (Table 2). However, no significant association was observed with embryo quality.The SplA/Ryanodine receptor domain and SOCS box containing 2 (SPSB2) is involved in intracellular signaling and homeostasis. Importantly, the SPSB2 gene exhibited markedly reduced expression in the cumulus cells surrounding oocytes with chromosomal aneuploidy [77]. Additionally, there was a pronounced tendency towards elevated SPSB2 expression in the cumulus cells of oocytes, accompanied by the production of a healthy live birth. Given that SPSB2 expression in cumulus cells has been identified as a potential biomarker for predicting oocyte aneuploidy, its expression may be further utilized to identify high-quality oocytes, particularly with respect to chromosomal integrity.

AREG and epiregulin (EREG) are two components of the EGF-like growth factor family. They are involved in the maturation and ovulation of oocytes [108, 109]. AREG mRNA levels in the cumulus cells of oocytes that developed into blastocysts on day 5 or 6 were significantly higher than those in oocytes that did not reach the blastocyst stage [28]. A positive correlation has been observed between the AREG level in follicular fluid and the number of available embryos [78]. Conversely, a negative correlation has been identified between AREG levels in follicular fluid and fertilization rates [110]. The AREG expression in cumulus cells and mural granulosa cells was observed to increase in pregnant women, with a positive correlation observed for the number of collected oocytes and the quality of embryos [79]. Findings from these studies suggest that assessing AREG levels in follicular fluid may represent a practical and cost-effective approach for predicting fertilization rates and embryo yield.

In addition to GDF9, the TGFβ-related gene BMP15 is involved in follicular development, ovulation, and corpus luteum formation [111]. Oocyte-secreted factors are known to promote granulosa cell proliferation and differentiation, as well as oocyte maturation, through paracrine and autocrine signaling mechanisms. Consequently, both their expression in cumulus cells and their concentrations in follicular fluid are associated with oocyte maturation and embryo quality [112, 113]. The expression of BMP15 in cumulus cells was found to be correlated with the quality of oocytes and an enhanced pregnancy rate [24]. A recent study reported a positive correlation between oocyte quality and elevated BMP15 mRNA levels [7]. Considering the absence of BMP15 receptors in oocytes [114], it can be postulated that this protein influences oocytes and the resulting embryos indirectly by modulating intracellular processes within cumulus cells.

The binding of anti-Mullerian hormone receptor type 2 (AMHR2) by AMH results in the activation of AMHR1, which in turn phosphorylates cytoplasmic proteins and transcription factors to regulate the expression of target genes [115]. An increase in mRNA levels of AMHR2 in cumulus cells has been observed to be positively correlated with embryo quality [7]. As AMH and AMHR2 expressions exhibit a positive correlation [116], it can be postulated that increased AMHR2 expression may result from elevated serum AMH concentration, which displays an increased follicular content including good-quality oocytes. As AMHR2 enables to influence estradiol and FSH levels [117], the association between embryo quality and AMHR2 mRNA level may derive from the indirect effects of these hormones on oocytes and subsequent embryo development.

The primary function of the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) is to facilitate the transfer of steroid precursors, including E2 and P4, which are essential for gonadotropin-dependent oogenesis, ovulation, and luteinization. Cumulus cells from women aged ≤ 33 years or ≥ 40 years who achieved pregnancy exhibited higher gene expression levels compared with those from women who did not conceive [80]. The LDLR gene expression was consistently observed to increase in the cumulus cells of the oocytes, indicating a higher degree of maturity in the pregnancy group in comparison to the non-pregnancy group [80]. This finding suggests that LDLR expression in human cumulus cells is not influenced by maternal age. Thus, the evaluation of this expression could represent a reliable approach for estimating oocyte competence and the likelihood of pregnancy among women undergoing ART across different age groups.

Myostatin (MSTN, also known as GDF8) can reduce PTX3 expression [118], and its overexpression leads to a decrease in the expressional control of the LH receptor and progesterone, and an increase in the FSH receptor and estradiol levels [119]. The MSTN gene expression decreased in the cumulus cells of high-quality oocytes and embryos when compared to the cumulus cells of low-quality oocytes and embryos in women [37]. As cumulus cells express estrogen [120], progesterone [73], FSH and LH receptors [121], MSTN appears to exert its functional effects, including the stabilization of the cumulus matrix, by regulating PTX2 expression through the modulation of these hormones.

Connexin 43 (CX43) is a pivotal protein in mouse granulosa cells and involved in the formation of gap junctions among these cells that function in follicular growth and meiotic competence in oocytes [122]. Decreased CX43 expression in human cumulus cells was found to be associated with the day 3 good embryos [81]. Wang et al., (2009) reported that CX43 protein level in human cumulus cells positively correlated with embryo quality, cleavage rate, and embryo morphology [82]. Furthermore, its expression was significantly higher in pregnant women than in non-pregnant individuals. As gap junctions facilitate the transfer of meiosis inhibitory signals from cumulus cells to oocytes [123], their potential additional roles, for instance in cytoplasmic maturation, may influence the quality of oocytes and embryos. Nevertheless, the conflicting results observed in cumulus cells regarding embryo quality may be attributed to the use of disparate cohorts of COCs in the aforementioned studies.

Glutathione peroxidase 3 (GPX3) plays a role in embryonic development and is modulated under hypoxic conditions through HIF-1 binding sites, which are exclusively found in this enzyme [124, 125]. In women undergoing assisted reproduction treatments, cumulus cells of oocytes that produced implanted embryos exhibited a lower GPX3 expression level than that of non-implanted embryos [83]. Furthermore, it reduced in cumulus cells of oocytes from pregnant women in comparison to those from non-pregnant women (Table 2). In patients with PCOS, oocytes surrounded by cumulus cells exhibiting lower GPX3 expression gave rise to blastocysts with higher developmental potential [59]. GPX3 participates in hypoxia-induced ROS detoxification, and its overexpression is known to be associated with the ROS production in cumulus cells, oocytes, and follicular fluid [126]. Therefore, maintaining GPX3 expression at an optimal level appears to support the generation of high-quality embryos and successful pregnancy outcomes.

The cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) is a cAMP-controlled and ATP-dependent anion channel that regulates the transfer of Cl− and HCO3−. It plays an important role in reproduction [127]. Furthermore, CFTR is involved in regulating the expression of aromatase and estrogen in mouse granulosa cells [128]. The CFTR mRNA level in cumulus cells is positively correlated with the number of recovered MII oocytes, antral follicle count, serum E2 levels, and expression of BMP15 and GDF9 in infertile women who underwent ovarian stimulation cycle [84]. Conversely, decreased CFTR mRNA level was found to be associated with a reduction in the number of oocytes and follicles, and reduced rates of fertilization, embryo cleavage, high-quality embryos, and implantation [84]. The controversy related to the correlation between CFTR mRNA levels and oocyte- and embryo-associated features can be attributed to the number of ovarian stimulation protocols. In contrast, opposite results were observed in women who received continuous stimulation with a constant dose of recombinant FSH. This finding suggests that gene expression analyses of cumulus cells should be conducted in women undergoing comparable ART protocols, particularly when investigating differentially expressed genes.

The olfactory receptor, family 10, subfamily h, member 2 (OR10H2) is known as a G-protein-coupled serotonin receptor and enables binding to secreted serotonin and supports oocyte development and fertilization by activating adenylyl cyclase type 3 (AC3) and cAMP production in cumulus cells [129]. The OR10H2 gene expression in cumulus cells showed higher expression in mature oocytes, suggesting that it could be used as an indicator for the selection of competent oocytes [85]. Creatine kinase B (CKB) plays a role in the reversible phosphorylation of creatine, which provides cellular energy deposition and transport [130]. The transcription and translation of the Ckb gene is exclusively and rapidly stimulated by estrogen [131]. The CKB expression was found to be higher in the cumulus cells of good-quality embryos from older women (≥38 years) in comparison to the cumulus cells of poor-quality embryos from younger women (≤28 years) [86]. Further studies on the OR10H2 and CKB gene expressions in cumulus cells would contribute to understanding their importance in predicting oocyte and embryo quality. Such studies can also be conducted on murine cumulus cells to elucidate their functional roles in oocyte and embryo development.

Taken together, the aforementioned genes, which play crucial roles in a number of intracellular processes, including steroidogenesis, differentiation, meiosis, cellular communication, and immune modulation, have been identified as being associated with the prediction of oocyte and embryo quality, as well as pregnancy and live birth outcomes (Table 2). Given that these genes participate in diverse cellular processes, elucidating their mechanistic roles in the generation of high-quality oocytes in coordination with cumulus cells is essential. Simultaneous evaluation of these genes using advanced techniques such as microarray analysis and RNA sequencing may provide insights into whether their expression profiles can serve as predictors of oocyte and embryo quality.

Proliferation- and metabolism-related genes

The connective tissue growth factor (CTGF, also known as CCN2) is known as a mitogen that is produced by vascular endothelial cells [132]. As a transcription factor, CTGF functions as an effector of the Hippo signaling pathway, thereby participating in the proliferation of intrafollicular cells and early embryonic cells [133]. By these ways, CTGF is involved in the processes of cell adhesion, migration, and survival as a matricellular protein in follicular development, ovulation, and corpus luteum formation [134]. Consistent with its essential role in reproductive function, inhibition of this factor results in loss of fertility in females.

CTGF is further expressed in cultured cumulus cells [135] and in follicles during development, with the highest expression in COCs [136]. Administration of FSH and hCG increased the expression of CTGF in the granulosa cells isolated from preantral and small antral follicles [137]. A reduction in the expression of CTGF gene was observed in the cumulus cells of high-quality oocytes and embryos in women [37]. Conversely, elevated CTGF expression in cumulus cells has been proposed as a potential biomarker for oocyte quality [135] (Table 3).

The stearoyl-CoA desaturase 5 (SCD5) enzyme in cumulus cells plays a role in the conversion of saturated fatty acids into mono-unsaturated fatty acids, thereby protecting oocytes from potential toxicity of saturated fatty acids [139]. A reduction in SCD5 expression was observed in the cumulus cells of oocytes and embryos exhibiting superior quality in women [37]. The gelatinases matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) is involved in mammalian fertility, as evidenced by the observation that its deficiency results in a reduction in the number of pups per litter [140]. In cases exhibiting a diminished ovarian response and fertilization rate, the cumulus cells exhibited elevated levels of MMP9 expression [138]. Uncovering potential effects of other MMPs in the functional properties of cumulus cells merit further studies.

The ATP synthase, H+ transporting, mitochondrial Fo complex, subunit E (ATP51) is a mitochondrial ATP synthase that catalyzes ATP synthesis. The ATP51 gene showed higher expression in the cumulus cells of the oocyte generated high-quality embryos on day 5 when compared to the ones with moderate quality in women who underwent ICSI treatment for male infertility [54]. Decreased ATP51 expression in the cumulus cells accompanying by maternal aging indicates potential relationship between this expression pattern and developmental competence of oocytes [141]. Consequently, expression of the ATP51 gene in cumulus cells may be used to identify high-quality embryos with regard to mitochondrial function.

The phosphofructokinase, platelet (PFKP) is involved in glycolysis regulation, cell proliferation, and metabolic reprogramming [142]. It is expressed in cumulus and mural granulosa cells in porcines, and their oocytes can promote the production of PFKP transcript levels in mural granulosa cells [143]. The glucose metabolism-related PFKP gene expression increased in the cumulus cells of mature oocytes when compared to immature ones [38]. Lower PFKP expression in human cumulus cells correlates with higher embryo quality on day 5 [54]. The pyruvate kinase isozyme type M2 (PKM2) is a phosphotyrosine-binding glycolytic enzyme, involving in glucose metabolism. PKM2 gene showed lower expression in implanted embryos but higher expression in mature oocytes [38]. Elevated expression was also detected in the cumulus cells surrounding oocytes with demonstrated developmental potential [32]. A comparable correlation was identified in relation to the glucose metabolism-related aldolase A (ALDOA) gene and the developmental potential of oocytes [32] (Table 3). As anticipated, these findings demonstrate the necessity of maintaining a balance in glucose metabolism-related gene expression to achieve the production of high-quality oocytes and embryos.

Apoptosis-related genes

Apoptosis is a key process in cumulus cells, which is essential for maintaining ovarian function. Oocyte and embryo quality is positively associated with reduced apoptosis in the surrounding cumulus cells [144]. The phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) is a tumor suppressor gene that is involved in apoptotic process. The PTEN mRNA level was found to be lower in the cumulus cells of oocytes that exhibited higher maturity and normal fertilization, as well as produced good-quality embryos, when compared to the oocytes whose cumulus cells exhibited higher expression [145] (Table 4). As PTEN promotes apoptosis by inhibiting phosphorylation of phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3) kinase that results in blocking AKT and its downstream activity [149], high PTEN expression shows higher rates of apoptosis in the cumulus cells that is associated with decreased oocyte quality [150].

The primary function of Golgi phosphoprotein 3 (GOLPH3, also known as GOPP1, GPP34, MIDAS and Vps74) is to suppress apoptosis, as well as to participate in cellular growth, proliferation, and differentiation [151]. Elevated GOLPH3 expression was significantly associated with increased rates of oocyte fertilization, embryo cleavage, high-quality embryo development, and blastocyst formation [146]. It would be reasonable to find the mechanistic background of promoting effects of increased GOLPH3 expression on fertilization and early embryo development.

An executioner caspase, caspase 3 (CSP3), is a member of conserved family of cysteine proteases that is involved in intrinsic apoptosis and its expression is increased by the actions of LH and FSH hormones [152]. CSP3 further participates in chromatic condensation, DNA fragmentation, nuclear collapse, and the regulation of apoptotic bodies [153]. The CSP3 expression in cumulus cells of PCOS patients is elevated when compared to the cumulus cells of control women [154]. This can be used as a predictor of embryo quality. Similarly, elevated CSP3 gene expression in cumulus cells of the poor ovarian response (POR) group is associated with a reduction in pregnancy rates [155]. Caspase 7 (CSP7) plays a role in the demolition stage of apoptosis, which results in the deposition of ROS and detachment of cells from extracellular matrix [156]. Its expression in cumulus cells is enhanced in PCOS patients when compared to the cumulus cells of control women [154]. This factor was associated with the prediction of embryo quality. Although comparing expression of these caspase types and developmental potential of oocytes and embryos has been studied in PCOS women, conducting similar comparisons in infertile women may aid in defining high-quality oocytes and embryos. The previously reported correlations between cumulus cell apoptosis and fertilization, as well as oocyte and embryo quality [157, 158], support the consideration of this potential association.

As miRNAs contribute to epigenetic control of target genes, a limited number of studies have evaluated their expressions in cumulus cells. miR-144 is expressed in ovulatory follicles and involved in the regulation of fas ligand (FASL), CSP3, tumor protein 53 (TP53), and B-cell leukemia/lymphoma gene-2-like 1 (BCL2L1) genes [159]. miR-224 is present in spermatozoa and growing follicles, and functions in granulosa cell development, enhancing folliculogenesis by interacting with SMAD4, controlling TGFβ1-activated granulosa cell proliferation, and inducing estradiol biogenesis through enhancing CYP19A1 expression [160, 161]. In patients with PCOS, the miR-144 and miR-224 RNAs were found to be expressed at lower levels in the cumulus cells of mature oocytes in comparison to immature oocytes’ cumulus cells [42]. Furthermore, miR-144 and miR-224 expression levels were decreased in cumulus cells associated with high-quality embryos compared to those linked to low-quality embryos [42]. In contrast, miR-21 is highly expressed in human cumulus cells and plays a role in regulating apoptosis and cell proliferation [162]. Its expression in cumulus cells of women with infertility was also found to be positively correlated with oocyte maturation [148]. However, no significant correlation was observed with embryo development. In this context, analyzing additional miRNAs involved in various cellular processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation [163], may provide insights when examined in cumulus cells surrounding oocytes that yield high-quality embryos.

The phosphoglycerate mutant enzyme family member 5 (PGAM5) is a mitochondrial phosphatase that functions in the stress responses related to apoptosis, mitophagy, necrosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction [164]. In addition to its role in mitochondrial fission, PGAM5 also plays a part in the dephosphorylation of DRP1S637 and the mitochondrial translocation of DRP1 protein [165]. The high expression of PGAM5 in cumulus cells was found to be negatively correlated with the pregnancy rate of infertile women [147]. The tumor protein P53 inducible protein 3 (TP53I3) contributes to controlling apoptotic process as well as carbohydrate metabolism. In human cumulus cells, TP53I3 expression was downregulated in association with oocytes exhibiting chromosomal aneuploidy [77] (Table 4).

Overall, as apoptosis provides elimination of defective cells, its increased rates among cumulus cells indicate possibility of poor oocyte quality reflecting to embryo development. Analogously, cumulus cells of oocytes that produced good-quality embryos showed lower apoptotic rate when compared to the poor-quality embryos [166]. Nevertheless, cumulus cells of oocytes exhibiting fertilization rates of ≥ 50% exhibited elevated apoptosis rates in comparison to cumulus cells of oocytes with fertilization rates of < 50% [166]. The apoptotic incidence of cumulus cells in fertilized oocytes and those of women who conceived following IVF application was found to be lower than in unfertilized and non-pregnant women. Co-culture of fertilized oocytes with cumulus cells exhibiting high proliferative activity has been demonstrated to enhance the implantation and pregnancy rates in comparison to the cumulus cells exhibiting poor proliferative activity [157]. The proliferative and apoptotic status of cumulus cells is closely associated with the developmental competence of oocytes and embryos, as well as with implantation and pregnancy outcomes.

Other function-related genes

Ubiquinone oxidoreductase subunit A1 (NDUFA1) is a mitochondrial antioxidant that is expressed in response to oxidative and endoplasmic stress. Its overexpression in cumulus cells has been demonstrated to result in an accelerated cellular aging in mice due to releasing more soluble Fas ligand [167]. NDUFA1 expression was significantly reduced in the cumulus cells associated with high-quality oocytes and embryos in women [37]. Another antioxidant enzyme peroxiredoxin 2 (PRDX2) plays a role in reducing hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and alkyl hydroperoxides [168] as well as in regulating intracellular H₂O₂ generation and H₂O₂-mediated apoptosis [169]. The PRDX2 gene expression was observed to be elevated in the cumulus cells of high-quality embryos in comparison to those of low quality [86]. A similar finding was reported by Hammond et al., (2015) [54]. Alterations in the expression of NDUFA1 and PRDX2 genes may occur in response to oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress within ovarian follicles, which appears to indirectly influence oocyte quality. As a biomarker of endoplasmic reticulum stress, the HSPA5 (heat shock 70 kDa protein 5, also known as glucose-regulated protein, 78 kDa) protein showed lower expression in the cumulus cells correlated with high-quality embryos on day 5 compared with the moderate quality ones [54]. Cumulus cells associated with lower-quality embryos may exhibit elevated HSPA5 expression as a result of increased oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress within ovarian follicles.

The simultaneous assessment of multiple gene expressions in cumulus cells

To improve the predictive value of gene expression in cumulus cells, many studies have simultaneously evaluated a number of genes to more reliably determine oocyte and embryo developmental potential. A ranking gene expression analysis of the HAS2, FSHR, VCAN, and PR genes in cumulus cells revealed that oocytes selected based on their expressions predicted good quality and subsequently resulted in more blastocyst formation and live birth rates when compared with randomly selected oocytes [34]. In the same year, Iager and colleagues identified a set of 12 genes whose expression in human cumulus cells was found to predict pregnancy outcomes with an accuracy of 78% [170]. These genes are involved in glucose metabolism, transcriptional regulation, gonadotropin regulation, and apoptosis. Microarray analysis revealed that while 48 candidate genes exhibited increased expression, 27 genes demonstrated decreased expression in the cumulus cells derived from the zona-good pregnant women when compared to those derived from the zona-bad non-pregnant women [171].

The cumulus cells of oocytes yielded embryos of moderate and poor quality expressed 37 downregulated and 106 upregulated genes when compared to the high-quality embryo group [172]. These genes are involved in a number of biological processes, including cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, cell signaling and communication, transcription, and cell metabolism. In the cumulus cells of women undergoing IVF, 5.739 genes were downregulated and 2.499 were upregulated depending on embryo quality [37]. As these genes participate in various cellular processes, evaluating multiple gene expressions in cumulus cells through advanced techniques such as microarray and RNA sequencing represents a promising approach for predicting oocyte and embryo potential.

High expression of the COL3A1, SFRP4, CTGF, HTR2B, VCAM1, and TNFRSF11B genes out of 133 genes was identified as a potential biomarker for predicting oocyte quality [135]. The collagen type III alpha 1 chain (COL3A1) plays a role in the assembly and organization of cumulus cells [173]. The tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily, member 11b (TNFRSF11B) protein is a member of the receptor TNF superfamily and is primarily involved in regulating cell death [174]. The secreted frizzled related protein 4 (SFRP4) protein contributes to controlling WNT signaling, and its expression in cumulus cells is higher than in mural granulosa cells [175]. Importantly, SFRP4 adversely influences development of ovarian follicles and their response to gonadotropins, ovulation, and female fertility [176].

The disheveled segment polarity protein 2 (DVL2) is involved in WNT signaling pathway, related to the regulation of cell proliferation and normal folliculogenesis. The DVL2 gene is known to be expressed in human cumulus cells [177]. The cellular communication network factor 1 (CCN1/CYR61) protein is involved in a number of cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix formation [178]. Expression analysis of cumulus cells revealed differential expression of 3.122 genes in embryos with monosomy 21 and 19 in those with trisomy 21 when compared to euploid cumulus cells [179]. Notably, thirteen of these genes such as DVL2, CCN1/CYR61 and serum response factor (SRF) exhibited different expression in both monosomy and trisomy 21 cumulus cells as compared to euploid ones. They are also involved in embryonic developmental competence [179]. The SRF protein, which localizes in the cell nucleus, is involved in a number of processes, including apoptosis, cell growth, cell cycle regulation, and cell differentiation [180, 181]. Additionally, SRF plays a role in embryonic development, with its function in mesoderm formation. The studies by Chermula et al., [135] and Tiegs et al., [179] found some genes impacting oocyte-quality in cumulus cells, and they may be used in creating a panel for predicting oocyte competence.

In addition to the simultaneous analysis of multiple genes, certain studies have focused on the evaluation of genes that predominantly share similar functional characteristics. The PVL index, which utilizes expression of the PTGS2, VCAN, and L19 genes in this formula (PTGS2 + VCAN)*L19normalized), was found to be helpful in predicting pregnancy outcome [182]. Although both PTGS2 and VCAN participate in extracellular matrix formation, the L19 gene encodes a ribosomal protein, which constitutes a component of the 60 S subunit of the ribosomal complex. Expressional analysis of the three predictive genes [EFNB2 (ephrin-B2), SASH1 (SAM and SH3 domain–containing protein 1), CAMK1D (calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase ID)] and two housekeeping genes (UBC and β2M), termed as corona test, in cumulus corona cells of human oocytes is implicated in the prediction of clinical pregnancy and live birth outcomes when compared to the single embryo transfer on day 3 without using this test [183]. The EFNB2, SASH1, and CAMK1D proteins are associated with cumulus expansion, the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling pathway, and calcium-mediated signaling pathways, respectively [184, 185]. More specifically, EFNB2 exhibits receptor tyrosine kinase activity [186]. SASH1 is a scaffold protein that plays a role in the assembly of a signaling complex at the downstream of TLR4 so that early endothelial response related to receptor activation is induced [185]. CAMK1D belongs to the calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase 1 family and is involved in many functions including regulatory or gene activation and apoptosis of erythroleukaemia [72]. Taken together, although the available evidence remains limited, the aforementioned studies support the potential utility of these tests in predicting pregnancy outcomes. However, additional studies involving larger cohorts of infertile women are required to obtain more definitive results.

The pro-apoptotic BCL2-associated X protein (BAX) establishes an interaction with BCL2, thereby inhibiting BCL2’s ability to suppress apoptosis [187]. The BCL2/BAX ratio plays a pivotal role in the regulation of apoptosis in human ovarian granulosa cells [188]. The anti-apoptotic BCL2 protein is involved in regulating mitochondrial pathway of apoptosis and participates in the effector stage of apoptosis [189]. The fertilization and subsequent development of 365 oocytes, which were examined in terms of their cumulus cells, did not appear to be associated with the expression of genes involved in apoptosis. A significant reduction in the BAX/BCL2 ratio was observed in cumulus cells of mature oocytes [155]. Rather than assessing the expression of multiple apoptosis-related genes, the utilization of this ratio may represent a more practical approach for predicting oocyte maturity.

The observed variations in the expression of cumulus cell gene(s) among women with infertility may be attributable to differences in ovarian stimulation protocols, including the use of distinct gonadotropin preparations [190]. Furthermore, additional biological factors such as age, serum FSH concentrations, ovarian response, and treatment regimens should also be taken into consideration [33]. The hyaluronidase enzyme, which is utilized for the dissociation of COCs, may possess the potential to influence gene expression in cumulus cells [191].

Although substantial progress has been made in elucidating the role of cumulus cell gene expression and its predictive value for oocyte, embryo, and pregnancy-related outcomes, certain limitations persist, and inconsistent findings have been reported across studies for the same gene expression analyses. Discrepancies in study design-including the use of diverse methodologies for gene expression measurement, variations in study populations arising from epidemiological differences, and the application of distinct statistical models may collectively contribute to the generation of inconsistent findings. Consequently, it is essential to consider the clinical characteristics of both infertile women and men when establishing study cohorts. In other words, the identification of a reliable biomarker in cumulus cells depends on multiple factors, including patient inclusion and exclusion criteria (such as maternal age and stimulation protocols), the timing of embryo transfer (day 3 or 5), outcome assessments (encompassing oocyte and embryo competence), and pregnancy outcomes. Furthermore, inconsistencies are evident in the experimental designs applied for RNA extraction and gene expression analysis, including microarray- and qPCR-based approaches, as well as in subsequent bioinformatics analyses [192].

Conclusion

Expression levels of the genes involved in various cellular processes-including cumulus expansion, ECM formation, intracellular signaling, proliferation, metabolism, and apoptosis-in cumulus cells contribute, to some extent, to defining high-quality oocytes and early embryos, as well as other pregnancy-related characteristics. Despite these significant findings, additional studies are warranted to develop comprehensive gene panels associated with oocyte competence, embryo quality, implantation potential, pregnancy outcomes, and live birth. In other words, the application of more advanced and high-precision techniques, such as next-generation sequencing, should be utilized to enable the simultaneous analysis of multiple gene expressions capable of predicting the associated biological features.
