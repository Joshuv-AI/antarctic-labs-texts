Andreev versus tunneling spectroscopy of unconventional flat-band superconductors
Authors: Sayak Biswas, Saurav Suman, Mohit Randeria, Rajdeep Sensarma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAndreev spectroscopy experiments can address important open questions about the nature of the superconducting state in moiré graphene. Their results have, however, been hard to interpret given their apparent tension with standard tunneling spectroscopy. In this paper, we show that previously proposed interpretations are not tenable, emphasizing the important role of the strong mismatch between the electronic structure of the metallic probe and the moiré graphene superconductor. We show that the puzzling aspects of the data can be understood in terms of Andreev bound states induced by the metallic tip in contact with the superconductor. Our results give strong evidence for the unconventional nature of the superconducting state, since only then would such Andreev bound states exist.

The finding of superconductivity (SC) in magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) (1–6) and related moire materials (7) raises many challenging questions These include: What is the symmetry of the SC order parameter? What is the mechanism of SC in topological flat bands? What role, if any, do nearby correlated insulating states play? Scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) spectra has given insight into these questions, indicating nodes in the SC gap in both TBG (8) and twisted trilayer graphene (TTG) (9). This is also consistent with power-law temperature dependence of the superfluid stiffness in TBG (10) and TTG (11).

While Andreev spectroscopy is one of the few phase-sensitive experiments done on TBG and TTG with the potential of elucidating the pairing symmetry, its interpretation is not clear. There are puzzling differences between STM results (8, 9) in the large resistance tunneling regime and the low resistance Andreev regime where the tip touches the sample. The two experiments reveal rather different characteristic energy scales, differing by as much as a factor of ∼4. The larger energy scale seen in tunneling has been tentatively identified with a pseudogap, while the one seen in the Andreev regime, has been associated with a coherent SC gap, an idea that traces its roots to early work on cuprates (12, 13).

Green’s Function Formalism

Our starting Hamiltonian is H=HN+HS+H′, where HN describes the normal (N) metal, HS the superconductor (S), and H′ couples the two. In 1D with a sharp N-S interface, or in a point contact regime without momentum conservation, H′=γ∑kk′σcN†(k,σ)cS(k′,σ)+h.c., where cj†(k,σ) creates an electron with momentum k, spin σ, and j=N,S. (We have generalized this to interfaces in 2D but the simplest analysis suffices here).

To obtain the first two results highlighted in the Introduction, we develop a Green’s function approach using the Schwinger–Keldysh technique to compute the differential conductance g=dI/dV of the N-S boundary as a function of the applied voltage V. Both N and S are in thermal equilibrium with N kept at a potential eV with respect to S. The final answer depends only on retarded/advanced (R/A) Green’s functions, because the N and S Keldysh Green’s functions are related to the corresponding GjR via the fluctuation–dissipation theorem.

It is convenient to work in the two-component Nambu basis for both j=N,S, and to define momentum-integrated Green’s functions ΛjRω=∑kGjRk,ωσz and ΛjA=ΛjR†, with a Nambu σz included for later convenience. At zero temperature we find[1]gV=e2hγ2∑s=±1trMΛSA−ΛSRM|ω=seV,

where the trace is in Nambu space and M is given by M=1−γ2ΛNRΛSR−1PsΛNR−ΛNA1−γ2ΛSAΛNA−1 with Ps=121+sσz. M incorporates virtual hopping processes across the N-S boundary to all orders in γ, essential to describe the ballistic Andreev limit.

Pseudogap versus SC Gap

The first question we address is whether we can identify two different energy scales; a pseudogap Epg in the large Z tunneling limit that is distinct from the coherent SC gap Δ in the Z=0 Andreev limit. Toward this end, we have used various Green’s functions with two distinct energy scales and always found that such an interpretation is not tenable. While the details depend upon the specific pseudogap model, the qualitative conclusions are the same. All regimes—from tunneling to Andreev—show characteristic features at the same energy scales arising from the singularities of the Green’s functions in Eq. 1.

First, consider GS(k,z)=(z2−ξk2−Epg2−Δ2)−1×z+ξkσz+Δσx, which describes a system with a spectral gap Eg=Epg2+Δ2 distinct from the SC gap Δ. The addition of the pseudogap and SC gap in quadrature is motivated by, e.g., ref. 28, 29, which analyze the appearance of superconductivity in a normal state with “hard” gap Epg. We can easily incorporate anisotropies in the SC gap and in the pseudogap, but it does not affect the qualitative results we want to emphasize. The unconventional nature of the SC gap will play a crucial role in the last part of the paper.

Using GS(R/A)(k,ω) obtained by z=ω±i0+ in Eq. 1, we obtain the conductance shown in Fig. 2 A and B. The red curves in panel (A) show the γ~=0.1 tunneling regime and (B) the γ~=1 Andreev limit result. In both cases, the conductance has a characteristic feature when the bias voltage matches the spectral gap Eg. (The dashed black curves in (A and B) are the standard SC state results without any pseudogap).

Another pseudogap model is one where SC develops in a normal state DOS ρpg(ϵ) with a “soft” suppression of spectral weight on the Epg scale; see SI Appendix for details. Here, we take ΛSR(ω)=∫dϵρpg(ϵ)z2−ξ2−Δ2−1z+ξσz+Δσxσz, with ξ=ϵ−μ and z=ω+i0+. This is inspired by and captures the basic idea in ref. 27, which analyzed a phenomenological model for underdoped cuprates.

We plot in Fig. 2 C and D the conductance obtained from this model in the (C) tunneling and (D) Andreev regimes. All curves are normalized by their high bias value gNN. The black dashed curves are the “normal” state results with a soft pseudogap but no SC, while the red solid lines show the effect of the SC gap Δ riding on top of the pseudogap DOS. Here, we see characteristic features in both the tunneling and Andreev spectra on the scale of Δ. In addition, the pseudogap shows up in tunneling as the remnant of the high energy scale at which the DOS is suppressed and in Andreev as the scale where the conductance approaches gNN.

Finally, we have also looked at the BCS-BEC crossover for a single band SC, where the spectral gap μ2+Δ2 is distinct from Δ once μ<0 in the BEC-like regime (30). We will report these results separately, but they lead to the same qualitative conclusion as the examples discussed above: The tunneling and Andreev spectra exhibit characteristic features at the same energy scales.

Velocity Mismatch and Self-Energy Effects

We next address how α=vFS/vFN, the Fermi velocity mismatch between the SC and the normal metal affects Andreev spectroscopy. Although the effect of the mismatch between bare (band structure) Fermi velocities has been addressed earlier (18), its importance in the context of the flat-band SCs has not been recognized.

This also raises the question of the role of interactions in renormalizing the vF that enters the interface conductance. Ref. 19 analyzed this using a nonstandard approach, introducing nonlocal self-energies into a wavefunction formulation. We provide more clarity on these issues using the Green’s function-based formulation of Eq. 1, where self-energy effects are transparent.

Let us consider the N-N′ interface where N′ is the normal state of the SC. It suffices to look at the zero bias conductance gNN′(0) as explained below. We will see that the key parameter that determines this is the DOS ratio ρN(0)/ρN′(0)=vFS/vFN≡α for the 1D or point contact model we are analyzing. Using ΛjR/A(0)=∓iπρα(0)σz for j=N,N′, we find that gNN′(0) has the same form as Eq. 2 with the crucial difference that now γ~2=π2γ2ρN(0)ρN′(0). Effectively this means that we must replace γ~2→αγ~2.

In the limit of small α this implies we are effectively in the tunneling regime. The upshot of this analysis is that with strong velocity mismatch one can never be in the ballistic Andreev limit. This result is not just valid for 1D or for a point contact, but also holds in the 2D case as we shall demonstrate in detail in the next section.

Next, we turn to self-energy corrections focusing, for the sake of simplicity, on a Fermi liquid normal state. Standard arguments then lead to a single-particle Green’s function GRk,ω≈Z/ω+i0+−Zξk+… for k near kF and small ω, where the quasiparticle weight Z=1/[1−(∂Σ/∂ω)kF,0] and ξk=vF(k−kF). Note that there are three “Fermi velocities”: i) the bare vF0 from band structure, ii) vF=vF0+(∂Σ/∂k)kF,0 which includes, e.g., Hartree–Fock corrections, and iii) the fully renormalized vF∗=ZvF.

The DOS at the chemical potential ρ(0)=−1π∑kImGR(k,ω=0)=∑kZδ(Zξk)=∑kδ(ξk) scales like 1/vF. Thus it is vF that enters the conductance calculated from Eq. 1. vF includes the effects of k-dependent corrections to the bare band structure vF0, but not the ω-dependent self-energy corrections arising from say electron–phonon or electron–electron interactions. In SI Appendix, we give a more general argument, without a quasiparticle pole assumption, that reaches the same conclusion.

We focused on the zero bias gNN′(0) that determines the scale of the conductance, which is sufficient to conclude that one cannot be in the ballistic Andreev limit for a strong velocity mismatch. At finite voltage, one may see self-energy effects beyond what we discuss here (31).

Metallic Disc in an Unconventional SC

Having concluded 1) that the two different energy scales seen in tunneling and Andreev spectroscopies cannot be attributed to the pseudogap and the SC gap, and 2) that a transparent barrier is renormalized toward the tunneling regime when the Fermi velocities are strongly mismatched, we must now ask how we can understand the observed Andreev spectra in TBG and TTG.

We model the STM tip that has crashed into the sample as a circular normal metal disc of radius R embedded in an unconventional 2D SC (r>R). As a concrete example, we consider a d-wave SC with Δ(ϕ)=Δ0cos(2ϕ) which also supports nodal quasiparticles as suggested by the experiments noted in the Introduction. The unconventional order parameter leads to the appearance of Andreev bound states (ABS) (20–22) at the N-S interface which will play a crucial role in our analysis.

In the spirit of looking at the simplest model that captures the qualitative physics, we describe the N and S electronic dispersion with parabolic bands with masses mN and mS, and Fermi wave-vectors kFN and kFS, We have found that our results depend weakly on ζ=kFS/kFN but, as emphasized below, exhibit a strong dependence on α=vFS/vFN=ζ(mN/mS).

We found it illuminating to analyze this 2D problem using the Bogoliubov-de Gennes (BdG) formalism. We consider a source at the origin that emits an outgoing wave with energy E incident upon the N-S interface with a bare Z=0. We work with partial waves labeled by angular momentum l0, determine the scattering amplitudes for each l0, and sum over l0 to obtain the conductance.

The unconventional order parameter Δϕ gives rise to interference effects that produce Andreev bound states (ABS) at the interface (20, 22). The ABS strongly modify the low-energy local DOS at r=R, which leads to the zero bias conductance peaks (ZBCP) seen in the α=0.1 (black) and α=0.01 (gray) results in Fig. 3B. We also clearly see that as the velocity mismatch grows the conductance evolves from the Andreev regime (α=1) toward tunneling (α≪1).

Tunneling signatures of ABS at planar junctions have been studied both experimentally (33, 34). and theoretically (20–22, 35) in the cuprates. These were analyzed in detail (21, 22) as a function of the angle β between the interface normal and the d-wave order parameter. The planar junction conductance averaged over all orientations 0≤β<2π shown in Fig. 3A is in semiquantitative agreement with the results of the kFR≫1 circular interface shown in Fig. 3B.

We turn to smaller values of kFR in Fig. 3 C and D, where we cannot approximate the results using known planar junction results. For equal velocities α=1, we see from panels (B–D) that the normalized g(0) decreases with decreasing kFR, and is smaller than unity for kFR=1. This trend is consistent with the vanishing Andreev signal for a point contact kFR≪1 in a d-wave SC (25).

In the regime of strong velocity mismatch relevant to flat-band SCs, however, the signatures of the ABS persist down to the kFR∼1 limit. The ABS give rise to the low energy scale observed in the Andreev experiments, which is much smaller than the SC gap seen in tunneling spectroscopy.

Numerical Estimates

We next show that our kFSR∼1−10 results are relevant for the Andreev experiments. Experimentally, the large bias conductance in the Andreev spectroscopy is gNN′≈100−160μS=(1.3−2)×2e2/h; see Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 in ref. 8. Using vFS∼1−4×104ms−1 (36) and vFN∼105−106ms−1, we obtain a rough estimate α=vFS/vFN∼0.01−0.25. We then use this α and the measured gNN′ in Eq. 8 to estimate the number of channels and find that kFSR∼1−20. This is reasonable, since for a density n∼2×10−2 nm−2, assuming two-fold valley and spin degeneracies, kFS=2πn∼0.35 nm−1, so that R∼3 nm leads to kFSR∼1.

Comparison with Prior Theoretical Studies

Andreev spectroscopy in moiré graphene was discussed in the context of constraints on the order parameter symmetry (23), and of the BCS-BEC crossover (24). In these papers, the 2D problem was effectively treated as a sum of independent 1D BTK channels, one for each ϕ. This approximation ignores the interference effects between sign-changing lobes of the SC order parameter that lead to the ABS, which are central to the results here. The Keldysh formalism in the first part of our paper is, however, equivalent to that in ref. 23 as shown in SI Appendix.

Refs. 23 and 25 correctly emphasized the complete suppression of the Andreev signal for a point contact (kFR≪1) in a d-wave SC. This is consistent with our analysis. In our Green’s function formalism, this arises from the vanishing of the off diagonal terms in the k-integrated SC Green’s function. We also see this in our BdG results where the zero-bias conductance decreases with decreasing kFR in the absence of any velocity mismatch; see Fig. 3. We emphasized in the previous Section, however, that the experiments are not in a literal point contact regime, but rather in regime with kFR∼1−10.

The DOS arising from ABS in d-wave SCs is well known. We note two limiting cases: the semiclassical analysis (kFR≫1) of a strong extended defect (37) and the T-matrix analysis of strong point impurity (38) (kFR≪1). The analysis presented here goes beyond these studies by analyzing the signatures of ABS in the differential conductance of a circular N-S interface for various kFR as a function of the velocity mismatch between tip and sample, which crucial for moire graphene SCs.

Comparison with Other Correlated Materials

It may be useful to briefly comment on spectroscopy in the ballistic Andreev limit or of ABS in various strongly correlated materials. In heavy fermion systems, the small Fermi velocity vF∗ (or large effective mass m∗) arises from the frequency-dependent self-energy due to electron–electron interactions. i.e., vF∗=ZvF. This does not lead to a large renormalization of the junction transparency, since it is vF, and not vF∗, that determines the effective transparency as discussed in detail above; see also ref. 19. Indeed, a ballistic Andreev signal is seen in experiments (39) on CeCoIn5. For Andreev data on UPt3, CeCu2Si2 and URu2Si2, see ref. 40 and references therein. In this sense, heavy fermions are quite different from moiré graphene which has a small vF even in the absence of correlation effects. On the other hand, heavy fermion superconductors with suitably oriented planar junctions host ABS, which have been observed in tunneling of CeCoIn5 (41).

We have discussed above theoretical studies of ABS induced by planar interfaces (20, 21) or defects (37, 38) in the d-wave cuprates. There is an equally large body of experiments probing ABS via zero bias peaks in the tunneling spectroscopy in the cuprates. Tunneling experiments (33, 34, 42) with suitable planar junction orientations have probed ABS in YBCO; see the review of ref. 22 for further references. In addition, STM studies (43–45) have probed the ABS induced by nonmagnetic impurities in BSCCO.

Conclusions

Our main conclusions (1) through (4) are already highlighted in the Introduction. Our results rule out earlier interpretations in terms of pseudogap/SC gap, clarify the role of vF mismatch between tip and sample, and highlight the role of ABS created at the N-S interface in an unconventional SC. We note that a conventional s-wave SC would not give rise to ABS and the low-energy scale observed in Andreev spectra. The singlet d-wave SC analyzed here is only an example of an unconventional order parameter. Elucidating the symmetry of the SC order parameter in TBG and TTG remains an important open question with a sharp answer independent of many microscopic details.
