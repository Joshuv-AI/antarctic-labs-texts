Magnitude and associated factors of elective surgical case cancellation in Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital hybrid retrospective and prospective cross-sectional study, Amhara, Ethiopia, 2025
Authors: Getachew Tesfaw Walle, Moges Beriye Rede, Simachew Asress Mekonin, Mengesha Alemu Seid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Elective surgical case cancellation is a pervasive global challenge, with rates ranging from 0.37 to 40% in developed nations and reaching up to 73% in low- and middle-income countries. In Ethiopia, while descriptive studies indicate high cancellation rates, there is a lack of analytical studies identifying the associated factors. This study aimed to determine the magnitude and associated factors of elective surgical case cancellations in the study area.Methods:This hybrid retrospective and prospective cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2024 to April 2025 among 378 systematically sampled elective surgical cases. Data were collected using pretested, structured questionnaires and medical record reviews. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 25. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were employed to identify associated factors, with an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) at a 95% confidence interval and a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.Results:The magnitude of elective surgical case cancellation was 25.6% (95% CI: 20.94, 30.26). The leading reasons for cancellation were time-related factors (28.7%, n = 27), including prolonged previous cases (17.0%, n = 16) and emergency case priority (11.7%, n = 11), as well as facility/management-related issues (23.4%, n = 22). Factors significantly associated with cancellation included age 61–70 years [AOR = 2.83; 95% CI (1.29, 6.24)], age 71 years and above [AOR = 3.68; 95% CI (1.37, 9.85)], and a monthly income less than 2000 Birr [AOR = 2.67; 95% CI (1.44, 4.98)].Conclusion:The cancellation rate exceeded the recommended benchmark. Advanced age and low income were significant risk factors. We recommend implementing thorough preoperative assessments, creating realistic surgical schedules, optimizing resource management, and establishing effective policies to mitigate this issue.

Introduction

Elective surgery refers to medically necessary, nonemergency procedures that can be delayed for at least 24 hours and are typically performed in an operating room under anesthesia[1]. The cancellation of these scheduled procedures, defined as an operation not being performed on its intended day after a patient has been formally listed, is a significant global challenge[2–4]. Efficient delivery of surgical services is a key indicator of hospital performance, reflecting the effectiveness of management systems and requiring substantial resource coordination[5]. While benchmarks suggest an acceptable cancellation rate of 5–10%, day-of-surgery (DOS) cancellations remain a pervasive issue, often stemming from preventable causes like inadequate preoperative assessment, administrative inefficiencies, and resource limitations[2,4,5]. This represents a major quality-of-care deficit worldwide. A recent systematic review estimated the global prevalence of DOS cancellation to be 18%[9]. The rates vary significantly, ranging from 0.37 to 40% in developed nations and reaching as high as 73% in low- and middle-income countries[4,9]. Specific national rates, such as 7.6% in China, 6.79% in Brazil, and 27.2% in India, underscore the variability of this issue[5,8,10,11]. In Africa, the problem is particularly acute, with cancellation rates ranging from 20 to 34%[12–14]. In Ethiopia, studies have consistently revealed high magnitudes of cancellation, with rates ranging from 32 to 34%, indicating a persistent local challenge[1,15,16].

The consequences of such cancellations are multifaceted and severe. They lead to the inefficient use of operating rooms (ORs), the highest cost and revenue centers in a hospital, wasting valuable resources and creating a substantial economic burden for both hospitals and patients through extended stays and repeated preparations[4,6]. Furthermore, cancellations detrimentally affect surgeon productivity, damage staff morale, and cause significant psychological distress in patients and their families[4,6].

Previous research, including recent descriptive studies in Ethiopia, has identified common contributing factors such as inadequate preoperative preparation, lack of operating room time or hospital beds, patient-related issues, and surgeon-related factors[1,3,4,15,17,18]. However, these causes are often context-specific and vary among institutions. Despite the high rates and severe implications, analytic studies in Ethiopia that quantitatively assess the magnitude and specifically identify the factors associated with elective surgical case cancellations are scarce.

Therefore, this study aimed to fill this gap by assessing the magnitude and identifying the factors associated with case cancellation among elective surgical patients at the hospital. This cross-sectional study has been reported in line with the STROCSS guidelines[19].

Methods and materials

Study area and period

The study was conducted at Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital between 25 September 2024 and 24 April 2025. The hospital has been serving nearly three million people from the Amhara, Oromia, Afar, and Tigray national regional states. Based on the human power structure, 387 professional health providers, along with 95 administrative workers, worked in the institution. It has four major operating theaters with a total of four functional operating rooms that are under renovation. Of the functional rooms, three of them provide services for elective surgical cases, and the other provides services for emergency operations. In the last 8 months, approximately 380 elective surgical cases were admitted for operation in different departments (general surgery, gynecology/obstetrics, and orthopedics).

Study design

This was an institution-based cross-sectional study combining a retrospective review of surgical records with prospective confirmation of cancellation reasons.

Source population

All elective surgical patients were scheduled for elective surgery at the hospital.

Study population

All elective surgical patients admitted to the hospital during the study period were included in the study.

Sample size determination

The study included a systematically sampled subset of all elective surgical cases scheduled during the study period, not the entire population. Sampling was necessary due to resource constraints. As described in the sampling procedure section, cases were stratified by department and selected using systematic random sampling with a random start. This approach was designed to ensure representativeness across surgical specialties. The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula by taking “p” proportion of surgical case cancellation, 33.9% from a study conducted on the magnitude and reasons of surgical case cancellation at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH)[15], confidence level of 95%, and 5% margin of error. n=(Za/2)2*p(1−p)d2

where n = the required sample size, p = proportion of surgical case cancellations, a = level of confidence, z = degree of accuracy at 95%, and d = margin of error. n=(1.96)2*0.339(1−0.339)0.052 n=344

To increase the power and compensate for the non-response rate, 344 cases with an additional 10% (totaling 378 elective surgical cases) were included in the study.

Sampling procedure

First, patients who were elective for surgery were identified in different departments (general surgery, gynecology, and orthopedic surgery) in the hospital. Because of the variation in the number of OR theaters and physicians among departments, the study subjects were divided into seven strata for each department. Elective surgical patients were then distributed to each department proportional to the average number of elective surgical patient admissions over an 8-month period for each department. Finally, participants were selected using a systematic sampling technique after identifying an initial starting respondent through a random (lottery) method.

Inclusion criteria

All patients scheduled for different elective surgical procedures at the hospital during the study period were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Patients scheduled in a minor operating room for minor surgery, who did not have a preoperative assessment sheet, were excluded. Patients listed for elective surgery but performed on the day of the schedule as an emergency were not included in the study.

Dependent variables

Elective surgical case cancellation.

Independent variables

Sociodemographic characteristics: Residence, occupation, age, sex, marital status, ethnicity, religion, educational status, and monthly income.

Department: General surgery, orthopedics, and gynecology/obstetrics,

Reasons for cancellation: Patient-related, medical-related, management-related, shortage of time, staff-related, and incomplete investigations.

Operational definitions

Elective surgery is a nonemergency surgery that is medically necessary but can be delayed for at least 24 hours[1].

Case cancellation: After a patient is admitted and/or informed about being operated on a specific day (a planned operation), and the procedure is not carried out on the scheduled day for any reason[1].

Data collection procedures

Data collection tools were pretested using an interviewer-administered questionnaire developed from different studies[9,15,16,20]. Data were collected by three Bachelor of Science in Nursing graduates and one Master of Public Health (MPH) supervisor, who received 2 days of training on study procedures, including standardized approaches to determining primary cancellation reasons. For each scheduled elective case, data collectors: (1) reviewed the medical record to extract sociodemographic and clinical information using a structured abstraction form; (2) confirmed the surgery status (performed vs. canceled) by directly observing theater lists and consulting with theater nursing staff at the end of each surgical day; and (3) for canceled cases, determined the primary reason for cancellation through structured discussion with the surgeon, anesthetist, and/or theater nurse involved in the case. When multiple contributing factors were identified, the data collectors, in consultation with the supervising MPH, assigned a single primary reason based on predefined prioritization criteria.

Data quality control

A structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire was prepared in English by adopting variables and terminologies from various studies. The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the total sample size at Dessie Hospital prior to the main study, and some modifications were made. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All variables retained in the final model had VIF values below 2.5. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (grouped by deciles of predicted risk), which yielded a chi-square value of 2.45 with 8 degrees of freedom (P = 0.964), indicating excellent model fit. The omnibus test of model coefficients (likelihood ratio chi-square = 28.47, df = 19, P = 0.043) demonstrated that the model with predictors was significantly better than the null model. Adequate orientation for data collectors and study participants was provided regarding the objective of the study and the data collection procedures. The principal investigator checked the completed questionnaires on the spot for consistency and completeness.

Data processing and analysis

After checking the completeness of the questionnaire, the data were coded and entered into Epi-Data version 3.1, and then exported to SPSS version 25 for further analyses. Frequency, percentage, mean, and variance were computed using descriptive statistics and are presented using frequency distribution tables, pie charts, and graphs. A purposeful variable selection approach was used for multivariable modeling. First, all variables with theoretical or empirical relevance to surgical case cancellation were considered based on a literature review. Bivariable logistic regression was then performed for each independent variable. Variables with P < 0.20 in bivariable analysis were entered into an initial multivariable model. Additionally, variables with established clinical importance (age, sex) were retained regardless of bivariable P-values. A backward stepwise elimination procedure was then applied, retaining variables with P < 0.05 in the final model or those whose removal substantially changed (>20%) the coefficients of other significant predictors.

Discussion

This study was conducted to assess the magnitude of elective surgical case cancellations and their associated factors among elective surgical patients at the hospital. The magnitude of elective surgical case cancellations in this study was [25.6% (20.94, 30.26)].

This result was similar to those of studies conducted in India (27.2%), Uganda (28.8%), and Nigeria (25.5%)[5,13,14]. However, as expected, it was higher than the studies conducted in China (7.6%), Brazil (6.79%), Dubai (6%), and Australia (14.3%)[8,10,11]. It is clear that this is due to the differences in sociodemographic and economic characteristics. Additionally, the possible reason for the higher rates compared to China, Brazil, Dubai, and Australia may be the difference in the availability of effective policies and awareness levels related to the impact of surgical case cancellations.

In contrast, the result was lower than the study conducted in TASH, Assella Teaching and Referral Hospital, and Debre Tabor General Hospital, which revealed that DOS cancellation was 33.9, 32.2, and 32.1%, respectively[1,15,16]. This may be due to the difference in the number of OR theaters (functional tables) and the variability in the total number of surgeons.

The most common reason for cancellation in this study was time-related, accounting for 27 cases (28.7%). A shortage of time due to the prolonged first case was the most common avoidable reason. This result is similar to studies conducted in Nigeria (32%)[3], Uganda (67.8%)[13], TASH (39.2%)[15], Debre Tabor General Hospital (42.2%)[16], and Assella Referral and Teaching Hospital (47.2%)[1].

This is due to the overbooking and the unknown exact time of the procedure, and the majority of the surgeries started after 2:30 a.m., which extended the time of the break between surgeries. In addition, the reason might be improper anesthesia setup and the unavailability of experienced surgeons on time.

The second cause for cancellations was due to management/facility-related reasons, which accounted for 22 (23.4%). This result was similar to a study conducted in America (19.9%), Saudi Arabia (20.3%), Nigeria (27.6%)[2], Jimma (21.87%)[20], and Hawassa (21.2%)[4]. However, this finding is lower than those reported in Bahrain (53%)[21] and Uganda (67.83%)[13]. The possible reason for this discrepancy may be the differences in sociodemographic and economic characteristics.

Study participants with advanced age and low income were at a higher risk of surgical case cancellation.

Respondents aged 61–70 and those older than 71 years were at 2.83 and 3.68 times higher risk of surgical case cancellation, respectively, compared to those less than 30 years old. This may be due to the fact that as respondents get older, they accumulate more coronary atherosclerosis, which causes NCDs, including cancer, cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory illness, and diabetes, compared to younger age groups[22]. Due to these medical illnesses, surgery might be canceled.

Another factor was low income. Respondents with a monthly income of <2000 Birr had an almost 2.67-fold higher risk of surgical case cancellation than those with a monthly income of >4000 Birr. The possible explanation for this association may be the inability to complete the necessary investigations because most of the medicine and laboratory investigations were outsourced from the hospital, leading to significant expenses that depleted their financial resources. As a result, the respondents could not complete all the necessary investigations, which were essential to decide on the operation, and their cases were canceled.

Limitation of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center design limits generalizability to other hospitals in Ethiopia or similar settings, though the detailed description of our context may allow readers to assess applicability to their own institutions. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between identified factors and cancellation outcomes; we can only report associations. Third, despite our efforts to capture all relevant variables, we cannot exclude the possibility of unmeasured confounding (e.g., surgeon experience, day-of-week effects, or patient health literacy). Fourth, some cancellation reasons were determined by operating theater staff on the day of surgery, which may introduce subjectivity, though we attempted to minimize this through standardized training and the use of predefined categories. Finally, the study period coincided with regional infrastructure challenges that may have influenced cancellation rates, potentially limiting temporal generalizability.

Relevance and implications

The findings of this study are highly relevant in highlighting the significant burden of elective surgical case cancellations at the hospital, with a cancellation rate of 25.6%. This proportion reflects a substantial waste of valuable health care resources, including operating room time, personnel, and hospital beds. The findings align with similar studies from other developing countries such as India, Uganda, and Nigeria, suggesting that elective surgical cancellations remain a pervasive issue in low- and middle-income settings. The study’s identification of time-related factors, such as prolonged duration of the first case and poor time management, as the leading causes of cancellation underscores the need for improved scheduling efficiency and resource allocation in surgical services. Furthermore, facility- and management-related factors highlight systemic inadequacies, including shortages of equipment, anesthetic setup delays, and lack of experienced surgeons’ issues that are characteristic of under-resourced health systems.

Importantly, the associations between older age, low income, and surgical case cancellation point to the role of socioeconomic and health status disparities in determining access to timely surgical care. These findings are crucial for policymakers, hospital administrators, and surgical teams striving to improve patient outcomes and operational efficiency in Ethiopia’s public hospitals.

Conclusions

This study revealed that the magnitude of surgical case cancellations was high among elective surgical patients within the hospital. The study also illustrated significant factors that contribute to surgical case cancellations, which include age and monthly income.

Recommendations

Based on the study’s findings, we propose the following integrated recommendations for the Woldia Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. At the administrative level, it is imperative to ensure a sustainable supply of blood and essential materials, implement a mandatory standardized preoperative assessment protocol, and develop clear policies for realistic surgical scheduling and resource management. For clinical departments, we recommend that the surgical department establish average procedure times, minimize anesthesia preparation time by ensuring equipment and senior staff are available, and implement weekly monitoring of cancellation rates. The anesthesia department should assign responsible personnel to specific surgical specialties for preoperative evaluation and ensure that essential supplies are readily accessible. Operative theater staff must focus on improving time management and communication, preparing all materials in advance, and minimizing gaps between surgeries. Finally, in collaboration with public media, community awareness should be raised regarding surgical preparations and potential needs, particularly among health insurance users.
