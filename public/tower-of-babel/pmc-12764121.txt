Prevalence and factors associated with small for gestational age births among women delivering at a tertiary hospital in South-Western Uganda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jonathan Mutumba, Musa Kayondo, Joseph Ssebanenya, Joan Abaatyo, Sharon Mutesasira, Gideon Akandwanaho, Julius Mugisha, Onesmus Byamukama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall for Gestational Age (SGA) is defined as birthweight of a baby below the 10th percentile for a given gestational age. A baby is born SGA if they are constitutionally small or if they had intrauterine growth restriction and this is associated with neonatal morbidity and mortality. When measuring birthweight alone to predict early adverse neonatal outcomes, babies born with a normal birthweight and are SGA go unnoticed yet they are at risk of morbidity and mortality. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and factors associated with SGA births among women delivering at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on mother-baby pairs at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH) from May to August 2023. Data on maternal, medical, socio-demographic and obstetric characteristics were collected. Babies whose birthweight was below the < 10th percentile for gestational age were classified as SGA using a WHO birthweight gestational age chart. The prevalence of SGA was the proportion of babies whose weights were less than 10th percentile expressed as a percentage of the total participants. A modified Poisson multivariable regression analysis was used to determine the factors associated with all SGA births. Variables with a p-value of < 0.05 were considered independently associated with SGA.ResultsA total of 427 mother-baby pairs were enrolled with a mean age of 26.6 (± 5.58) years. The prevalence of SGA was 21.8% (95% CI; 18.1% − 26.0%). Factors that were independently associated with SGA included; maternal > 34 years [aPR: 2.29; 95% CI (1.15–4.53), p = 0.018], maternal height < 150 cm [aPR: 2.18; 95% CI (1.26– 3.91), p = 0.005], history of a hypertensive disorder in current pregnancy [aPR: 2.18 (1.12–5.09), p = 0.039].Conclusion and recommendationsAt MRRH, the prevalence of SGA is high. Factors that are associated with SGA include; advanced maternal age, short stature and a history of any hypertensive disorder in the current pregnancy. We recommend routine use of birthweight gestational age charts to screen for SGA especially among women of advanced age, short stature and those who are hypertensive.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08528-6.

Introduction

A Small for Gestational Age (SGA) neonate is defined as one with a birthweight below the 10th percentile of an optimal standard reference population for the sex and gestational age [1]. Determination of SGA is done using the WHO birth weight-for-gestational-age chart that ensures standardized classification with reference. SGA encompasses neonates born with intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) as well as those who are constitutionally small. Of the neonates born SGA due to IUGR, 40% have no underlying pathology identified and of the known etiologic factors, one third has been attributed to genetic variables and two thirds to environmental factors [2].

Globally, about 16% of all births are SGA, ranging from 7% in industrialized countries to 41.5% in South Asia [3, 4]. The vast majority (27%) of SGA occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [5]. The prevalence of SGA in Sub-Saharan Africa is higher (25·5%) than the global prevalence and this could be the reason for the high neonatal mortality rates in the region. A baby born SGA is at risk of both immediate and long-term complications. Immediate complications include; hypoglycemia, neonatal asphyxia, hypothermia, polycythemia, sepsis, and death [6–8]. Compared to babies born appropriate for gestational age (AGA), the relative risk for neonatal mortality is about two times higher if born SGA and this risk increases 15-fold more if baby is born both SGA and preterm [9]. Long-term adverse health outcomes that have been documented in babies born SGA include; stunting, adult-onset metabolic and cardiovascular risks, reduced learning potential and productivity [10–12]. When birthweight alone is used as a predictor of negative outcomes, many newborns who have a normal birthweight and yet are SGA go unnoticed and may succumb to complications [9, 13].

The current global and regional estimates highlight the disproportionately high prevalence of SGA in LMICs, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa. South western Uganda, like many rural regions, faces multiple health system challenges, including limited access to quality antenatal care, high rates of maternal anemia, and socio-environmental risk factors—all of which may contribute to SGA births. Without reliable, context-specific prevalence data, it is difficult to design and implement effective, targeted interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence and factors associated with SGA births among women delivering at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH), to inform public health strategies, guide maternal and child health policies, enable clinicians and administrators to plan logistics, design evidence-based interventions for prevention and management, as well as identify women at higher risk for timely intervention.

Methods

Study setting, study design, and study population

This was a cross-sectional study conducted on the postnatal ward of MRRH from 20th April 2023 to 30th July 2023. The hospital serves as a regional Referral Hospital for southwestern Uganda and a teaching hospital for Mbarara University of Science and Technology (MUST). With a total bed capacity of 494 beds, 40 of which are on post-natal ward. The department of Obstetrics and Gynecology conducts approximately 600 deliveries per month [14]. Our study population comprised mother–baby pairs delivered at MRRH and admitted to the postnatal ward during the study period. We included all women who delivered at 26 weeks of gestation and above. We excluded mothers with missing birthweight records or uncertain gestational age when neither last menstrual period nor first trimester ultrasound data were available. Additionally, consecutive sampling was applied to all eligible mothers admitted to the postnatal ward during the study period.

Sample size and sampling

Sample size was calculated using the Kish Leslie formula (1965) commonly applied for prevalence and cross-sectional studies, n = Z2p q/d2 [15], where Z is the standard normal deviate corresponding to the desired confidence level (1.96 for 95% confidence), p is the estimated prevalence, q = 1–p, and d is the margin of error (precision) set at 0.05Estimated prevalence (p) of SGA was determined using a 50% prevalence because no study has used the WHO chart in screening for SGA using the same inclusion criteria in a similar setting. With a margin of error of 5%, at 95% level of confidence and adjusting for a 10% non-response rate, we estimated a sample size of 427 women. For objective 2, the sample size was calculated using OPEN EPI basing on a study done on prevalence, risk factors and consequences of newborns born SGA: A multi-center cross-sectional study across 12 public hospitals in rural and urban areas in Nepal south Asia in which the proportion of SGA among those whose time of first ANC visit was in first trimester (un exposed) and those whose first time was in third trimester (exposed) was 37.9% and 19.8% respectively, assuming a 95% confidence interval and a power of 80% at multivariable analysis [16]. This gave us a sample size of 212 participants which is lower than the 427 calculated for objective one. So, the final sample size considered for this study was 427.

We enrolled the mothers on the postnatal ward who met the inclusion criteria into the study through consecutive sampling. Written informed consent was obtained from each study participant before recruitment and participation in the study.

Data collection and study variables

A questionnaire was developed and pretested to capture socio-demographic, obstetric and medical characteristics. The data were collected by a team of four qualified midwives as research assistants who were trained on data collection procedures including proper administration of the questionnaires as well as plotting of the birthweight gestational age charts. Our dependent variable was SGA. This was dichotomized as SGA and No SGA. We defined SGA as a baby born with a birthweight below the 10th percentile according to WHO birthweight gestational chart for gestational age and sex. This chart was adopted for use in this study because no such chart has been developed and approved for use for the Ugandan population.

The socio-demographic characteristics included: age was categorized as < 20, 20–34, or > 34 years; education level was categorized as none, primary, secondary, or tertiary; marital status was categorized as single, married, cohabiting, divorced, or widowed; smoking status was categorized as never smoked, former smoker, or current smoker; alcohol intake during pregnancy was categorized as yes or no; and place of residence was categorized as urban or rural. Obstetric factors included: parity was categorized as 1, 2–4, or > 4 prior deliveries; inter-birth interval was categorized as < 33 or ≥ 33 months. The 33-month cut-off approximates the World Health Organization recommendation of at least 24 months between a live birth and the next conception (24 months plus ~ 9 months of gestation), and has been used in previous studies as a practical measure of optimal birth spacing [17, 18]. History of stillbirth was categorized as yes or no; hypertensive disorders in pregnancy was categorized as yes or no; trimester of booking ANC visit was categorized as first (< 12 weeks), second (12–27 weeks), or third (≥ 28 weeks); folic acid-iron supplementation (FIS) intake was categorized as yes or no; FIS adherence was categorized as good if folic acid was taken for more than 90 days/3 months and poor if taken for less than 90 days (among users); number of ANC visits was categorized as < 4 or ≥ 4; history of low birthweight neonates was categorized as yes or no; and maternal height was categorized as < 150 cm or ≥ 150 cm. Medical conditions included: malaria in pregnancy was categorized as yes or no; hypertensive disorders was categorized as yes or no; diabetes mellitus in pregnancy was categorized as yes or no; HIV status was categorized as positive or negative; MUAC was categorized as < 23 cm (undernutrition) or ≥ 23 cm (normal); diet during last month of pregnancy was categorized as adequate or inadequate. Diet was measured as “Adequate” if one had intermediate or high consumption of a combination of different dietary indicators (protein rich food e.g. meat, milk, fruits and carbohydrate foods e.g. rice, posho or bread) and “Inadequate” if the consumption was low as per the Food Frequency Questionaire (FFQ) (FIGO, 2023). A participant had low consumption if a combination of the dietary indicators was taken once a week, intermediate consumption if taken twice a week and high consumption if taken three or more times a week. Diet was determined in the month prior to conception and in the last month of pregnancy.

Data management and analysis

Following a completeness check of the questionnaires by the Principal Investigator (PI), data were coded and entered in RED-CAP software. Data was then exported to Stata Version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) for analysis. The baseline characteristics of participants were summarized using means ± SD, for continuous data and frequencies and percentages for categorical data. The prevalence of SGA was the proportion of babies whose weights were less than 10th percentile expressed as a percentage of the total participants.

To determine the factors associated with all SGA births, all independent variables were subjected to a modified Poisson regression with robust error variance and only variables with a p-value < 0.2 at bivariate analysis were subjected to a multivariable analysis. Variables with a p value < 0.05 were reported as significant. Adjusted prevalence ratios, 95% confidence intervals and p-values were reported.

Obstetric characteristics of study participants.

Majority of the women delivered at term (89.0%), were multiparous (2–4) (59%), had a maternal height of < 150 cm (63.7), attended at least 4 ANC visits (78.2%) with a booking visit in second semester (52.7%), took folic acid iron supplementation during pregnancy (56.4%); however, majority had poor adherence to it (68.8%). Significant differences were observed in several participants characteristics among women who delivered babies with SGA and those with No SGA, these included: parity, history of hypertension in pregnancy, maternal height, booking ANC visit and history of taking folic acid-iron supplements during pregnancy. The proportions among women who were of short stature (height < 150 cm), were higher in women who delivered SGA babies (75.3%) as compared to those that had No SGA (60.5%). Among women who had booked ANC in third trimester and those who never took FIS, the proportions of women who delivered SGA were however lower (2.1% and 33.3%) as who compared with No SGA (3.9% and 46.4%) respectively. Prevalence of SGA

Among the 427 infants included in the study, birthweight classification was significantly associated with gestational age-adjusted growth categories (Pearson’s χ² = 93.8, p < 0.001). Of the 49 infants with low birthweight, 69.4% were SGA (n = 34). Among the 378 infants with normal birthweight, 16% (n = 59) were SGA.

Overall, 21.8% of all infants were SGA (95%CI; 18.1% − 26.0%). The proportion of SGA babies among babies delivered with a normal birthweight was 16.0% (n = 59). See Fig. 2.

Discussion

This study determined the prevalence and factors associated with SGA births among women delivering at a tertiary hospital in southwestern Uganda. About one in every five babies delivered was small for gestational age. The prevalence of SGA was significantly higher among those aged > 34years, those having hypertensive disorder in pregnancy and those whose maternal height was < 150 cm.

The prevalence of SGA in this study is similar to that in studies done in a tertiary care hospital of Western Nepal (20.3%) [19] and another multinational study in sub-Saharan Africa (25.5%) [5]. Low income countries often struggle with food insecurity and limited access to a diverse and nutritious diet yet poor nutrition in pregnant women can result in restricted fetal growth leading to SGA births [20]. Lower prevalences have been reported in studies done in China (12.3%), Nepal (11.9%) and Korea (10.6%) [21–23]. The variation in prevalence may reflect contextual and methodological differences, including more accurate gestational age estimation using early ultrasound, better maternal nutrition, and stronger antenatal care systems in these countries. In contrast, our study relied partly on LNMP for dating pregnancies and was conducted in a setting with higher rates of maternal undernutrition, short stature, and hypertensive disorders, all of which are known risk factors for SGA. These factors likely contribute to the relatively higher prevalence observed in our study [24–27]. It is also possible that the study population in China consisted of individuals that have better access to healthcare, nutrition and education compared to our population [28]. Higher SGA prevalence had been reported in Ethiopia (46.14%) [29]. The higher prevalence in this study could be attributed to the fact that they included preterm babies who were < 2 kg compared to our study population which included both preterm and term deliveries.

In our study, women who had a history of hypertension were 2.18 times more likely to have SGA births compared their counterparts who were normotensive. This is consistent with a study done in the Canadian province of Nova Scotia and a tertiary public maternity hospital in the city of São Paulo in Brazil [30, 31]. This is explained by the fact that hypertension can lead to vascular changes in function and structure thus causing utero-placenta insufficiency which deprives the fetus of nutrients necessary for growth hence SGA.

This study also found that women who had a maternal age of > 34 years were 2.29 times more likely to have SGA births compared to those in the age range of 20–34. Similar findings were reported in studies done in Mexico in 2017 and USA [32, 33]. The observed difference could be explained by the fact that women of advanced age are more likely to have chronic health conditions like hypertension, diabetes, and obesity [34]. These conditions can impact the development of the placenta and delivery of nutrients and oxygen to the fetus thus increasing the risk of SGA [35, 36].

In our study, women who had a maternal height of less than150cm were 2.22 times more likely to deliver babies who were SGA compared to those who had a height of greater or equal to 150 cm. Consistent findings were presented in a cohort study done in LMICs and another study done in Tanzania [37]. Maternal height is influenced by genetics which play a role in determining the baby’s size at birth. Taller parents may have taller children, and shorter parents may have shorter children [38]. Also, maternal short stature may be associated with nutritional deficiencies or chronic health conditions that could potentially impact fetal growth [39].

Conclusion

One in every five babies delivered was SGA. The proportion of SGA babies among babies delivered with a normal birthweight is also high at 16.0%. The prevalence of SGA was significantly higher among those aged > 34years, those having hypertensive disorder in pregnancy and those whose maternal height was < 150 cm. Efforts should be made to strengthen the routine use of birth weight gestational charts to identify babies who are SGA for specialized neonatal care. Future studies need to explore outcomes of babies born SGA.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths that enhance its scientific contribution. It fills a crucial gap in data on SGA births in Southwestern Uganda, providing valuable insights for local health policy and intervention strategies. The use of WHO birth weight-for-gestational-age charts ensures standardized classification and improves comparability with international data [1, 40]. A relatively large sample size (n = 427) strengthens statistical power, and the rigorous methodology, including a clear definition of SGA and modified Poisson regression with robust error variance, enhances the validity of the findings. The study’s thorough data collection on socio-demographic, obstetric, and medical factors, along with the use of trained midwives, minimizes bias and supports reliable analysis of risk factors. Conducted in a busy referral hospital, it reflects real-world clinical settings. However, there are limitations: the cross-sectional design prevents causal conclusions, and the hospital-based, consecutively sampled population may not fully represent the broader community, particularly those giving birth outside of health facilities. The WHO growth charts, not validated locally, may misclassify infants due to genetic and environmental variations. Maternal self-report data are subject to recall and social desirability bias, and the short data collection period may miss seasonal variations in SGA prevalence. Despite adjustments, potential residual confounding remains, and the lack of information on missing data raises concerns about the study’s internal validity. Lastly, Gestational age was assessed using LNMP for those without first trimester USS. Although commonly applied in clinical practice in our setting, LNMP is less precise than first-trimester ultrasound, which is the gold standard. Prior studies have shown that LMP-based dating introduces greater variability in gestational age estimates and tends to overestimate the prevalence of preterm birth [24–27]. In comparative studies, misclassification due to LNMP corresponded to a mean error of approximately one week and overestimation of preterm birth rates by 2–4% points. Because our risk factor analysis included all participants, this imprecision may have slightly attenuated or distorted some associations between risk factors and SGA. Given that only a minority were dated by LNMP, any resulting bias is likely to have diluted true associations rather than created spurious ones, and we interpret our findings with this limitation in mind. At the same time, our use of LNMP reflects the realities of clinical care in resource-limited settings, where it remains the standard clinical method in our setting, and reporting these data together with sensitivity analyses provides valuable insight into the burden of SGA in this population.
