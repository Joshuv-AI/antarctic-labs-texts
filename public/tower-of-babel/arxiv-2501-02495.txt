Quantum noise in time-dependent media and cosmic expansion
Ziv Landau 1,2 and Ulf Leonhardt 1

Abstract
In spatially uniform, but time–dependent dielectric media with equal electric and magnetic response, classical electromagnetic waves propagate exactly like in empty, flat space with transformed time, called conformal time, and so do quantum fluctuations. In empty, flat space the renormalized vacuum energy is exactly zero, but not in time–dependent media, as we show in this paper. This is because renormalization is local and causal, and so cannot compensate fully for the transformation to conformal time. The expanding universe appears as such a medium to the electromagnetic field. We show that the vacuum energy during cosmic expansion effectively reduces the weights of radiation and matter by characteristic factors. This quantum buoyancy naturally resolves the Hubble tension, the discrepancy between the measured and the inferred Hubble constant, and it might resolve other cosmological tensions as well.

supports the idea that renormalization is local: each local region appears to have its own renormalizer

[

II
Fluctuation and dissipation

II.1
Green functions

The central quantity to be calculated in this paper is the correlation
K
defined in Eq. (
11
) and evaluated in the conformal vacuum. The energy densities are obtained in Eq. (
10
) by differentiating
K
with respect to the space–time coordinates
\{t_{1},\bm{r}_{1}\}
and
\{t_{2},\bm{r}_{2}\}
. In the fluctuation–dissipation theorem
[
57
]
we relate the correlation
K
of the fluctuations to the dissipation
\Gamma
defined as

\Gamma=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}c}{2i\hbar}\,\langle\widehat{A}_{1}\widehat{A}_{2}-\widehat{A}_{2}\widehat{A}_{1}\rangle=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}c}{2i\hbar}\,\big[\widehat{A}_{1},\widehat{A}_{2}]\,.

(14)

We removed the quantum average, as the commutator of the field is state–independent
[
26
]
. The commutator gives the retarded and advanced Green functions
G_{\pm}
such that
[
26
]

\Gamma=\frac{1}{2c}\,(G_{+}-G_{-})\,.

(15)

The retarded Green function
G_{+}
describes a classical light pulse emitted from a point source at
\{t_{1},\bm{r}_{1}\}
with unit strength, propagating in
\{t_{2},\bm{r}_{2}\}
coordinates. The advanced Green function
G_{-}
describes in
\{t_{2},\bm{r}_{2}\}
coordinates how a pulse is focused such that it becomes absorbed at the point
\{t_{1},\bm{r}_{1}\}
. The difference
G_{-}-G_{+}
satisfies the homogeneous wave equation without source or drain, and so it is neither absorbed nor emitted, but focused
[
87
]
. In our case, Eqs. (
1
) and (
3
), the field propagates as in Minkowski space with respect to conformal time, and so we have
[
1
]

G_{\pm}=-\frac{1}{4\pi r}\,\delta(\tau\mp r/c)

(16)

in terms of the radius

r=|\bm{r}_{2}-\bm{r}_{1}|

(17)

and the conformal–time difference

\tau=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{dt}{a}\,.

(18)

In time–independent media,
G_{\pm}
would only depend on the time difference
t_{2}-t_{1}
. There
G_{-}
is the time reverse of
G_{+}
and so the Fourier transform
\widetilde{G}_{-}(\omega)
equals
\widetilde{G}_{+}(-\omega)
, which implies
\widetilde{G}_{-}-\widetilde{G}_{+}=2i\mathrm{Im}\widetilde{G}_{+}
that corresponds to the local density of states
[
88
]
.

II.2
Fluctuation–dissipation theorem

How is the dissipation
\Gamma
related to the correlation
K
? We see from Eqs. (
11
) and (
14
) that
K
is the real part and
\Gamma
the imaginary part of a complex function
f
:

K=\mathrm{Re}f\,,\quad\Gamma=\mathrm{Im}f\quad\mbox{with}\quad f=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}c}{\hbar}\,\langle\widehat{A}_{1}\widehat{A}_{2}\rangle\,.

(19)

The function
f
is often called the Wightman function
[
29
]
. Let us analyze its properties. The field operator
\widehat{A}
can be decomposed into a superposition of modes
[
89
]
:

\widehat{A}=\sum_{k}\left(\widehat{a}_{k}A_{k}+\widehat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}A_{k}^{*}\right)

(20)

with the quantum annihilation and creation operators
\widehat{a}_{k}
and
\widehat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}
and the classical mode functions
A_{k}
. In our case, the mode functions can be taken as plane waves oscillating with positive frequencies
[
89
]

\omega_{k}
with respect to conformal time. In the conformal vacuum, where
\widehat{a}_{k}|0\rangle=0
, the Wightman function appears as a superposition of terms oscillating as
\exp(-i\omega_k\tau)
in the conformal–time difference, Eq. (
18
). As these are analytic functions decaying on the lower half plane of
\tau
the Wightman function
f
is also analytic and decaying for
\mathrm{Im}\,\tau<0
. From Cauchy’s theorem
[
63
]
follows that the real part and the imaginary part on the real axis, Eq. (
19
), are connected by the Hilbert transformation
[
63
]
:

K(\tau)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\tau_{0})}{\tau_{0}-\tau}\,d\tau_{0}\,.

(21)

This is the fluctuation–dissipation theorem
[
57
]
for our case of time–dependent media with equal electric and magnetic response: the correlation
K
is given in terms of the dissipation
\Gamma
by the Hilbert transformation (
21
) with respect to the conformal–time difference (
18
). Connections between real and imaginary part are familiar in physics from the Kramers–Kronig relations
[
64
,
65
]
between the real and the imaginary part of the Fourier–transformed susceptibility
\widetilde{\chi}(\omega)
. There causality requires that
\chi(t)=0
for
t<0
, which implies that
\widetilde{\chi}(\omega)
is analytic and decaying on the upper half
\omega
plane. This gives the same relation as Eq. (
21
) for
\mathrm{Re}\widetilde{\chi}
and
\mathrm{Im}\widetilde{\chi}
, apart from a different sign in the prefactor, because in the underlying Cauchy theorem
[
63
]
the integration contour runs mathematically–positive (counter–clockwise) on the upper half plane and negative (clockwise) on the lower half plane.

One might object that the imaginary part of
f
, the dissipation
\Gamma
of Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
), consists of delta functions that are not mathematical functions. However, they are associated with poles: the functions
f_{+}=i\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\omega\tau}\,d\omega
and
f_{-}=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega\tau}\,d\omega
are analytic on the upper (+) or lower (-) half plane, respectively, and the integrals give
f_{\pm}=\tau^{-1}\pm i\pi\delta(\tau)
. In the fluctuation–dissipation theorem, delta functions in
\Gamma
get translated into poles in
K
. If these delta functions were negative they would still produce the same poles, but should be read as analytic on the lower half plane, which will become important later on (Sec. IIIC).

To proceed, we insert Eqs (
15
) and (
16
) in Eq. (
21
) and obtain:

K=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}s^{2}}

(22)

in terms of the Minkowski metric

s^{2}=c^{2}\tau^{2}-r^{2}\,.

(23)

We see that the vacuum correlations are singular at the light cone (
s=0
). We also see that the correlations persist outside of the light cone (where
s^{2}<0
) in agreement with experiment
[
56
]
. They change sign: fields are correlated outside and anti–correlated inside the light cone. These correlations arise, because the randomness in wave noise stems from the coefficients
\widehat{a}_{k}
and
\widehat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}
in the mode decomposition of Eq. (
20
) whereas the mode functions are deterministic waves extending over space and time. Wave noise is organized
[
90
]
(Fig.
2
).

II.3
de Sitter space

An instructive example is the case of exponential expansion called de Sitter space
[
91
,
92
]
where the Hubble parameter of Eq. (
5
) is constant:

H=\mathrm{const}

(24)

and so
a(t)=a_{0}\,e^{Ht}
with
a_{0}=\mathrm{const}
. Let us express the two times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
of the Green function and correlation function in terms of their average
t
and difference
\theta
:

t=\frac{1}{2}\,(t_{1}+t_{2})\,,\quad\theta=t_{2}-t_{1}\,.

(25)

We obtain for the difference (
18
) in conformal time:

\tau=\frac{2}{aH}\,\mathrm{sinh}\,x

(26)

with
a=a(t)
and

x=\frac{H\theta}{2}\,.

(27)

Suppose we replace
\theta
by the complex

\theta_{*}=\theta-i\beta\quad\mbox{with}\quad\beta=\frac{2\pi}{H}\,.

(28)

We see from Eqs. (
26
) and (
27
) that
\tau(\theta_{*})
is real but changes sign. We obtain from Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
) that
K(\theta_{*})=K(\theta)
and from Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
) that
\Gamma(\theta_{*})=-\Gamma(\theta)
, and so

f(\theta_{*})=f^{*}(\theta)\,.

(29)

This is the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger relation
[
26
,
57
]
that characterizes a thermal state with temperature

k_{\mathrm{B}}T=\frac{\hbar H}{2\pi}

(30)

where
k_{\mathrm{B}}
denotes Boltzmann’s constant. The Kubo–Martin–Schwinger relation (
29
) suggests that the vacuum in the exponentially expanding universe appears as thermal radiation. Let us compare the de Sitter correlation function
K_{\mathrm{deS}}
with the correlation
K_{\mathrm{th}}
of thermal fluctuations in Minkowski space, with inverse temperature
\beta=\hbar/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)
. One obtains via conformal mapping
[
26
]
:

K_{\mathrm{th}}=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}c^{2}\rho^{2}}\,\partial_{\rho}\ln\left(e^{H\theta}-e^{H\rho}\right)\left(e^{H\theta}-e^{-H\rho}\right)

(31)

with
\rho=r/c
. For
r\rightarrow 0
one gets indeed
K_{\mathrm{deS}}=K_{\mathrm{th}}
with the temperature of Eq. (
30
) for
\beta=2\pi/H
. This is the Gibbons–Hawking effect
[
43
]
: an observer at rest with the exponentially expanding universe perceives the quantum vacuum as thermal relation. However, for
r\neq 0
(
i.e.

\bm{r}_{1}\neq\bm{r}_{2}
) the correlations functions of the vacuum in de Sitter space and the thermal correlation function of Eq. (
31
) are different. A nonlocal detector might not see thermal radiation at all or thermal radiation with a different temperature. Here is an intriguing example
[
93
]
: take the ionization of an atom as detection event of Gibbons–Hawking radiation. Note that the atom is not a point detector, as the ionized electron must get away from it, ideally to infinity. Use the coordinates
\bm{x}=a\bm{r}
where the exponential expansion appears as a stationary flow
[
94
]
. In these coordinates, the electron tunnels out of the atom through a potential barrier set by the ionization energy and gradually lowered with
|\bm{x}|
due to cosmic expansion. The tunnel rate suggests
[
93
]
that the atom detects thermal radiation of twice the temperature of Eq. (
30
). We interpret this beautiful result
[
93
]
as a feature of nonlocal detection: the expansion of the universe has helped the electron tunnel out of the atom to infinity, which raises the Gibbons–Hawking temperature, remarkably by exactly a factor of two
[
93
]
.

II.4
Energy densities

Return to the general case of arbitrary time evolution
a(t)
. We wish to compute the electromagnetic energy densities that according to Eq. (
10
) depend on the correlation function
K
given [Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
)] by the conformal time
\tau
defined in Eq. (
18
). We express the two times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
in terms of the time difference
\theta
and average
t
as

t_{1}=t-\frac{\theta}{2}\,,\quad t_{2}=t+\frac{\theta}{2}\,.

(32)

If we replace
\theta
by
-\theta
the times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are exchanged, and the defining integral (
18
) of
\tau
changes sign:
\tau
is an odd function of
\theta
. For small time differences
\theta
, we could regard the scale factor in Eq. (
18
) as a constant and get
\tau\sim\theta/a
.

Let us work out the higher terms in the power series of
\tau(\theta)
. We shall do this in a form that will be useful later on (Sec. III) for renormalization. We express the scale factor in terms of the Hubble parameter
H(t)
of Eq. (
5
):

a(t)=a(t_{1})\exp\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t}H\,dt\right)

(33)

and expand
H(t)
in a power series around
t_{1}
:

H=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{H^{(m)}}{m!}\,(t-t_{1})^{m}\,.

(34)

The next non–vanishing order in
\tau(\theta)
we obtain from the first–order term in the power series (
34
). We work out the integral (
18
) as a power series in
\theta
and get

\tau\sim\frac{\theta}{a}+\frac{H^{2}-\dot{H}}{24a}\,\theta^{3}\,.

(35)

Higher–order terms can be obtained from higher orders in the Hubble series (
34
) but expression (
35
) is already sufficient for calculating all divergent orders of the electromagnetic energy densities; higher orders just produce converging terms, as we will see next.

Formula (
10
) for the energy densities contains the derivatives
\partial_{t_{1}}\partial_{t_{2}}
and
\nabla_{1}\cdot\nabla_{2}
. The time derivatives we express as
\partial_{t_{1}}=-\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}
and
\partial_{t_{2}}=\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}
according to Eq. (
25
). As
K
is only a function of
r=|\bm{r}_{2}-\bm{r}_{1}|
we have for the spatial derivatives
\nabla_{1}\cdot\nabla_{2}=-\nabla^{2}=-\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{2}{r}\partial_{r}
. We thus get from Eq. (
10
):

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{E}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar a}{c}\,\left(\partial_{\theta}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\,\partial_{t}^{2}\right)K\,,

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{M}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar c}{a}\left(\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{2}{r}\partial_{r}\right)K\,.

(36)

These expressions involve maximally second derivatives of
K
with respect to
\theta
while
K
is given by Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
). Higher–order terms of
\tau(\theta)
will thus produce converging results in our limiting procedure [Eq. (
8
)] that corresponds to the limits
\theta\rightarrow 0
and
r\rightarrow 0
. In this paper we are solely interested in the divergencies, because only they can produce energies that matter on a cosmological scale for a cut–off on the Planck scale.

Note that the
u_{\mathrm{E}}
and
u_{\mathrm{M}}
are coordinate densities, not proper densities. During the cosmic expansion the volume element grows with
a^{3}
and so we must divide the coordinate densities by
a^{3}
to get the proper densities (Appendix A):

\epsilon_{\mathrm{E}}=\frac{u_{\mathrm{E}}}{a^{3}}\,,\quad\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}=\frac{u_{\mathrm{M}}}{a^{3}}\,.

(37)

These energy densities diverge in the limits
\theta\rightarrow 0
and
r\rightarrow 0
. Diverging expressions may depend on the order of limits. If we take the limit
r\rightarrow 0
first we get

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{E}}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{3\hbar}{2\pi^{2}c^{3}\,\theta^{4}}-\frac{\hbar\,(2H^{2}+\dot{H})}{8\pi^{2}c^{3}\,\theta^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{3\hbar}{2\pi^{2}c^{3}\,\theta^{4}}-\frac{\hbar\,(H^{2}-\dot{H})}{4\pi^{2}c^{3}\,\theta^{2}}\,,

(38)

whereas if we take the limit
\theta\rightarrow 0
first we have

\epsilon_{\mathrm{E}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}}=-\frac{\hbar c}{2\pi^{2}\ell^{4}}

(39)

in terms of the proper length

\ell=ar\,.

(40)

In the latter case (taking the limit
\theta\rightarrow 0
first) the energy densities do not depend on the Hubble parameter
H
at all. Note that this is an exact result valid for arbitrary
\ell
, not only in the limit
\ell\rightarrow 0
. So for
\theta\rightarrow 0
we get exactly the same energy densities as in empty Minkowski space: no trace is left of Gibbons–Hawking radiation. This illustrates again that the Gibbons–Hawking effect
[
43
,
90
]
requires
r\rightarrow 0
: a local observer at rest with the universe.

In our case, the different orders of limits encode different types of dispersion, as follows. We describe the dispersion by a cut–off, a smallest time or a smallest length. For ordinary dielectrics, the order of magnitude of the smallest time corresponds to the lifetime of the highest resonance, whereas the smallest length corresponds to the molecular or atomic size. Typical resonances have decay rates in the order of MHz and atoms are as small as a few
10^{-10}\mathrm{m}
. They are therefore much longer in time than in space, and so for ordinary dielectrics we should take the limit
r\rightarrow 0
first. For the “dielectric” of space the nature of dispersion is not known yet. Following Jacobson
[
81
]
we assume
spatial dispersion
where the medium of space–time consists of elements that are shorter in time than in space such that we take the limit
\theta\rightarrow 0
first. We shall see (Sec. III) that in this case we will get particularly simple and transparent expressions for the renormalized energies.

III
Renormalization

III.1
Preamble

The previous version
[
26
]
of the theory of quantum noise in the expanding universe has relied on the analogy between the expansion and moving media
[
94
]
that suggests the thermal radiation of cosmological horizons
[
95
]
. Horizons can radiate with excellent approximations to thermal Planck spectra
[
96
]
. However, we have seen (Sec. IIC) that even for the simplest case — exponential expansion — the quantum vacuum appears as thermal radiation only to local observers at rest, but not in the full correlation function of the electromagnetic field. While we are going to apply many of the ideas and the same mathematical technique as in the previous theory
[
26
]
we need a different physical starting point. Let us begin at the beginning.

III.2
Hubble truncation

Renormalization is local. For our case of time–dependent media, the renormalizing Green function
G_{0}
should only depend on the scale factor
a
and its first and second derivatives
\dot{a}
and
\ddot{a}
at the time of emission
[

,
76
]
, which according to Eq. (
5
) is equivalent to the scale factor
a
and the Hubble parameter
H
with its first derivative
\dot{H}
. For
G_{0}
we thus take
a
from Eq. (
33
) and truncate the Hubble series (

) at the first order:

H=H(t_{1})+\dot{H}(t_{1})(t-t_{1})\quad\mbox{for}\quad t_{2}>t_{1}

(41)

when
t_{1}
is the time of emission and
t_{2}
the time of reception. Consider the conformal–time difference
\tau
that determines the Green functions in Eq. (
16
). Following the same procedure as before (Sec. IID) we calculate
\tau
as a power series in the actual time difference
\theta

\tau\sim\frac{\theta}{a}+\frac{H^{2}-\dot{H}}{24a}\,\theta^{3}+\frac{\ddot{H}}{24a}\,\theta^{4}\quad\mbox{for}\quad\theta>0\,.

(42)

Due to the truncation of the Hubble series we have got a fourth–order term, but
\tau
must be an odd function of
\theta
. If we reverse the order of times and take
t_{2}
as the time of emission and
t_{1}
as the time of reception such that

H=H(t_{2})+\dot{H}(t_{2})(t-t_{2})\quad\mbox{for}\quad t_{1}>t_{2}

(43)

we replace
\theta
by
-\theta
[Eq. (
32
)] and get

\tau\sim\frac{\theta}{a}+\frac{H^{2}-\dot{H}}{24a}\,\theta^{3}-\frac{\ddot{H}}{24a}\,\theta^{4}\quad\mbox{for}\quad\theta<0\,.

(44)

Indeed,
\tau(\theta)
is an odd function, but it is no longer analytic
[
63
]
: it has a discontinuity in the fourth derivative. Consequently, for the correlation function
K
we must not use expressions (
22
) and (
23
), because the fluctuation–dissipation theorem (Sec. IIB) relies on analyticity. The correlation function
K
is the real part and the dissipation
\Gamma
the imaginary part of the Wightman function, Eq. (
19
). The dissipation
\Gamma
is proportional to the difference between the retarded and the advanced Green functions. For analytic functions, the real and imaginary parts are connected, but not for arbitrary functions with discontinuities in derivatives. For finding the correlation
K_{0}
of the renormalizer we thus need to re–express the problem in terms of analytic functions.

Here we will make another assumption on our renormailzation procedure that follows the same spirit as the truncation of the Hubble series, Eqs. (
41
) and (
43
). There we are fitting the evolution
a(t)
of the scale factor with nearly–exponential pieces at each moment of time: with local de Sitter spaces. Here we assume that each local de Sitter space also obeys a Kubo–Martin–Schwinger relation
[
26
]
. Specifically, we express the conformal–time difference
\tau
of Eqs. (
42
) and (
44
) in the form of Eq. (
26
) with
a=a(t)
and
H=H(t)
. Instead of the
x
of Eq. (
27
) we obtain from the series (
42
) and (
44
):

x=\frac{H\theta}{2\eta}

(45)

with

\eta\sim 1+\frac{\dot{H}}{24}\theta^{2}\mp\frac{\ddot{H}}{24}\theta^{3}\,.

(46)

So far, this is simply an equivalent re–expression of the series (
42
) and (
44
) around
\theta\sim 0
. Now, if we replace the
\theta
in Eq. (
45
) with

\theta_{*}=\theta-\frac{2\pi i\eta}{H}

(47)

such that

x=\frac{H\theta_{*}}{2\eta}

(48)

we get
\tau(\theta_{*})=-\tau(\theta)
and therefore
\Gamma(\theta_{*})=-\Gamma(\theta)
from Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
). We assume that
K(\theta_{*})=K(\theta)
such that for the Wightman function (
19
) the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger relation (
29
) holds. This is the assumption we make (Fig.
3
).

III.3
Geometrization

Assumption (
29

3

according to Eqs. (

for real

we have solved our problem: we have found an analytic representation for the Wightman function along the curve

and, via the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger relation (

K_{0}
from the Green functions via the fluctuation–dissipation theorem.

Consider a curve in the complex

(49)

Here and elsewhere the prime denotes the derivative of

with respect to the parameter, here

. The second invariant is the angle
\chi

(50)

The third invariant is the radius of curvature

with

We are also going to use the fourth invariant, the curvature change

having the same invariants. This function must have a branch point that matches the discontinuity in Eq. (

The imaginary part of the logarithm smoothly goes from zero to

on the upper half plane; it jumps from zero to
+i\pi
on the real axis if we take the logarithm as analytic on the upper half plane. On the lower half plane it goes or jumps from zero to

where we parameterize our curve, the poles of the Wightman function

with real constant

and complex constant

III.4
Renormalized energy density

In renormalization, causality causes discontinuities in the conformal–time difference, expressed in Eq. (
46
). These discontinuities prevent the direct application of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem for calculating the correlation function from the Green functions. We have extended the actual time difference
\theta
to the complex
z
plane and considered the curve where the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger relation (
29
) holds. There we have transformed
z
to
w
such that the discontinuity in the third derivative of the curve is described by an analytic function with a logarithmic branch point, Eq. (
58
). Now we return to the real axis. We use the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger relation (
29
) and the analyticity of
z(w)
to calculate the correlation of the renormalizer. We do this only to leading order, as all other terms will have negligible energies.

On the real axis
\theta=\mathrm{Re}\,z
. There we obtain from Eq. (
58
)
\theta\sim w+\frac{\delta}{6}\,w^{3}\ln w
neglecting the cubic term. Inverting this relationship:

w\sim\theta-\frac{\delta}{6}\,\theta^{3}\ln|\theta|\quad\mbox{for}\quad\theta\sim 0

(59)

and real
\theta
gives the transformed time
w
for which we then can apply the fluctuation–dissipation theorem. We transform the Green function from
\tau
to
w
and get from Eq. (
16
):

G_{\pm 0}=-\frac{1}{4\pi r}\left|\frac{dw}{d\tau}\right|_{w_{\pm}}\delta(w-w_{\pm})

(60)

where the
w_{\pm}
denote the zeros of
\tau=\pm r/c
as a function of
w
. In leading order,
\tau\sim\theta/a
and so
w_{\pm}=w(\pm ra/c)
with the function
w(\theta)
given by Eq. (
59
). As this is an odd function we have

w_{\pm}\sim\pm w(\theta_{0})\,,\quad\theta_{0}=\frac{ar}{c}\,.

(61)

We also have
dw/d\tau\sim aw^{\prime}(\theta)
and
w^{\prime}(-\theta)=w^{\prime}(\theta)
. The Green function
G_{\pm 0}
of Eq. (
60
) generates the dissipation
\Gamma_{0}
according to Eq. (
15
). Seen as a function of
w
, the dissipation is the imaginary part of an analytic function. The real part — the correlation
K_{0}
— we obtain by replacing the delta functions in
\Gamma_{0}
by poles:

K_{0}=-\frac{a\,|w^{\prime}(\theta_{0})|}{8\pi^{2}cr}\left(\frac{1}{w-w_{+}}-\frac{1}{w-w_{-}}\right).

(62)

Now turn to calculating the energies. We insert formula (
62
) with definitions (
59
) and (
61
) in Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
) and take the limit

. This gives the energy densities of the renormalizer as a function of the cut–off

that we express as the proper length

. We obtain up to order

\epsilon_{\mathrm{E}0}\sim\epsilon_{\mathrm{M}0}\sim-\frac{\hbar c}{2\pi^{2}\ell^{4}}+\frac{\hbar c}{2\pi^{2}\ell^{2}}\,\frac{\delta}{6c^{2}}\,.

(63)

Higher–order terms make negligible contributions to the energy. The dominant term of order

matches exactly the bare energy densities, Eq. (

). In renormalization, by taking the difference between
\epsilon
and
\epsilon_{0}
it removes the contribution that disagrees
[

]
with the real vacuum energy
[

]
by 120 orders of magnitude for

in the order of the Planck length

. The term of order

comes from causality in renormalization. We obtain from Eq. (
57
) for the sum of the electric and magnetic energy densities of Eq. (
63
):

\epsilon_{\mathrm{vac}}=\epsilon-\epsilon_{0}\sim-\frac{\hbar c}{\pi^{2}\ell^{2}}\,\frac{\ddot{H}}{6c^{2}H}\,.

(64)

This energy density is of exactly the right order of magnitude to influence the expansion of the universe (Sec. IV). But it is not the only vacuum energy present, as we shall see next.

III.5
Anomaly

The first law of thermodynamics requires that
dE=-pdV
for an adiabatic process, such as the cosmic expansion, that does not change the entropy,
dS=0
. Here
V
denotes the volume of an arbitrary region of space,
p
is the pressure and
E
the energy in this volume. The energy is given by the energy density
\epsilon
times the volume, which implies
d\epsilon=-(\epsilon+p)\,dV/V
. During cosmic expansion
V
grows with
a^{3}
and so we have
dV/V=3\,da/a
. We thus obtain
[
98
]
from the first law and the definition (
5
) of the Hubble parameter:

\dot{\epsilon}=-3(\epsilon+p)H\,.

(65)

This is one of Friedmann’s equations
[
99
,
100
]
. Note that this simple consequence of thermodynamics follows from the conservation of the energy–momentum tensor
[
101
]
. Note also that both energy–momentum conservation and thermodynamical arguments apply to each one of the cosmic fluids individually
[
94
]
. In our case, we are interested in the “fluid” of vacuum fluctuations. For those, the pressure makes up the energy density acting in all three spatial dimensions such that
\epsilon_{\mathrm{vac}}=3p_{\mathrm{vac}}
(Appendix A) or

p_{\mathrm{vac}}=\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{vac}}}{3}\,.

(66)

However, with this pressure the energy density
\epsilon_{\mathrm{vac}}
of Eq. (
64
) does not satisfy the Friedmann equation (
65
): it would violate the first law. We need to supplement the energy with another energy density
\epsilon_{\Lambda}
. As it only takes care of the mismatch in energy–momentum conservation, it must not contribute to the right–hand side of Friedmann’s equation (
65
):

p_{\Lambda}=-\epsilon_{\Lambda}\,.

(67)

We thus obtain for the total energy density

\partial_{t}(\epsilon_{\mathrm{vac}}+\epsilon_{\Lambda})=-4\epsilon_{\mathrm{vac}}H\,.

(68)

Consider now the corresponding energy–momentum tensor [Eq. (
112
)]
\mathrm{diag}(\epsilon_{\Lambda},-p_{\Lambda},-p_{\Lambda},-p_{\Lambda})=\epsilon_{\Lambda}\mathbb{1}
according to Eq. (
67
). This tensor has the trace
4\epsilon_{\Lambda}
, whereas for classical electromagnetic fields the trace of the energy–momentum tensor is zero
[
98
]
. Renormalization causes a
trace anomaly

[
78
]
.

Formula (
68
) is the defining equation of the anomalous energy density
\epsilon_{\Lambda}
. We see from our result (
64
) for
\epsilon_{\mathrm{vac}}
that we can directly integrate it:

\epsilon_{\mathrm{vac}}+\epsilon_{\Lambda}=\epsilon_{\infty}+\frac{2\hbar}{3\pi^{2}c\ell^{2}}\,\dot{H}\,.

(69)

The integration constant
\epsilon_{\infty}
is all that is left in the infinite future when the universe settles to exponential expansion
[
27
]
and
H=\mathrm{const}
. This integration constant is the cosmological constant
[
102
]
.

IV
Cosmic expansion

IV.1
Gravity

The only force acting over the vast distances of space is gravity. The content of the universe — radiation, matter and the quantum vacuum — generates a gravitational background field that, in turn, decelerates the cosmic expansion — if the force is attractive. This background field is weak and almost non–relativistic
[
98
]
. We can use Newton’s law to work it out. Let us briefly review the known theory
[

,

before we show how the quantum vacuum enters the picture.

Space is flat to an excellent approximation. This concludes our miniature review of cosmic dynamics

Quantum buoyancy

Consider now the influence of the vacuum energy on the cosmic dynamics. We have derived formula (

\epsilon_{\mathrm{vac}}+\epsilon_{\Lambda}

Radiation and matter are characterized by a simple equation of state

Let

(80)

This is the effective gravitational density of the fluid. There the original density
\rho_{m}
is reduced by the characteristic factor

as if the fluid were partially buoyant in the quantum vacuum.

V
Conclusion

We have considered spatially uniform, time–dependent media with equal electric and magnetic response, Eq. (
1

[

to the conformal time
\tau

\tau

\tau

[

76

74
]
. Renormalization is local
[

— in our case, local in time.

Causality implies that the dielectric environment of the renormalizing Green function depends on the earlier of its two times. Causality combined with locality, Eqs. (
41

[

64

Such a dispersionless medium with equal electric and magnetic response is the “medium” of space in cosmology
[

[

to the medium of Eq. (

Thermodynamics or, equivalently
[

[

Cosmic expansion, or equivalently, the time–dependance of the medium, Eq. (

Appendix A
Brief excursion into general relativity

While the main body of this paper does not rely on general relativity, but applies to spatially uniform dielectric media with equal electric and magnetic response, and negligible dispersion, it is instructive to see where the “medium of space” comes from
[
15
]
. Modern cosmology
[
27
,
94
]
starts from the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric with zero spatial curvature:

ds^{2}=c^{2}dt^{2}-a^{2}d{\bm{r}}^{2}\,.

(91)

The metric (
91
) describes the infinitesimal increment
ds/c
of proper time
[
98
]
perceived along any timelike trajectory. Space is assumed to be flat such that it enters with Euclidean line element
d{\bm{r}}^{2}=dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}
in Cartesian coordinates, but the measure of length is multiplied by the time–dependent scale factor
a
. The metric (
91
) is a quadratic form
g_{\alpha\beta}\,dx^{\alpha}dx^{\beta}
of the coordinate increments
dx^{\alpha}=(cdt,dx,dy,dz)
. Throughout this Appendix we adopt Einstein’s summation convention over repeated upper and lower indices. The matrix
g_{\alpha\beta}
is called the metric tensor with, in our case:

g_{\alpha\beta}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-a^{2},-a^{2},-a^{2})\,.

(92)

We get for the matrix inverse
g^{\alpha\beta}
of the metric tensor

g^{\alpha\beta}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-a^{-2},-a^{-2},-a^{-2})

(93)

and for its determinant

g=\det(g_{\alpha\beta})=-a^{6}\,.

(94)

The determinant of the metric accounts for the space–time volume element as
\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x
(with
\sqrt{-g}=a^{3}
in our case).

The classical electromagnetic field is described by the four–potential
A_{\alpha}
that comprises the electric potential
V
and the magnetic vector potential
{\bm{A}}
as
A_{\alpha}=(V,-c{\bm{A}})
. The potentials generate the electric field strength
\bm{E}=-\nabla V-\partial_{t}\bm{A}
and the magnetic induction
\bm{B}=\nabla\times\bm{A}
that constitute the field–strength tensor

F_{\alpha\beta}=\partial_{\alpha}A_{\beta}-\partial_{\beta}A_{\alpha}

(95)

which reads in matrix form:

F_{\alpha\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}0&E_{x}&E_{y}&E_{z}\\
-E_{x}&0&-cB_{z}&cB_{y}\\
-E_{y}&cB_{z}&0&-cB_{x}\\
-E_{z}&-cB_{y}&cB_{x}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(96)

If we replace the four–potential
A_{\alpha}
by
A_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}\varphi
in Eq. (
95
) we get the same

, which means the field–strength tensor is gauge–invariant. Being constructed from potentials, the fields naturally satisfy the first group of Maxwell’s equations
[
1
]
:

\nabla\cdot\bm{B}=0\,,\quad\nabla\times\bm{E}+\partial_{t}\bm{B}=\bm{0}\,.

(97)

The second group of Maxwell’s equations turns out to follow from minimising the action
[
