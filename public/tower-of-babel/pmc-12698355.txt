Corticosteroid infiltration in partial distal biceps ruptures
Authors: Elisabeth A Wörner, Elisa L Zwerus, Ante Prkic, Femke MAP Claessen, Bertram The, Denise Eygendaal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLittle is known about the potential negative or positive effects of peritendinous infiltration with corticosteroids in the non-surgical treatment of partial distal biceps tendon tears. Peritendinous fluid, synovitis, and bursitis often accompany partial tears and can be a source of persistent pain. We hypothesize that peritendinous corticosteroid infiltration is a safe non-surgical treatment option for complaints related to a partial distal biceps tendon rupture.MethodsA single-center retrospective analysis was performed on a cohort of 52 patients with partial distal biceps tears (<50% of the footprint involved), as confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging. All patients received an ultrasound-guided intrabursal peritendinous infiltration with 1 mL of triamcinolone acetonide (10 mg/mL) and 4 mL of 2% lidocaine HCl. Patient files were reviewed for demographic information, date of injury, injury mechanism, treatment modality (operative or nonoperative), clinical follow-up (2011–2021), and complications, including progression to a complete rupture.ResultsThe median duration of follow-up after infiltration was 15 months (1.2–45 months). No infiltration-related complications were observed following infiltration therapy. Surgical reconstruction was ultimately performed in 65% of the patients with a partial tear. One patient sustained a trauma to the elbow in the weeks following infiltration, resulting in a complete tear.ConclusionsInfiltration with corticosteroids is a safe treatment option for patients with a partial tear of the distal biceps who failed progressive exercise therapy. Surgical reconstruction was avoided in 35% of patients following infiltration treatment.Level of evidenceIV.Keywords: Distal biceps, Tendon, Rupture, Infiltration, Corticosteroid

INTRODUCTION

Distal biceps tendon injuries encompass a spectrum of pathologies, ranging from tendinopathy to partial and complete ruptures (Table 1) [1,2]. Partial distal biceps tendon ruptures are most commonly caused by ongoing chronic repetitive microtrauma. Repetitive supination, pronation, and flexion stresses, as well as changes in tendon vascularity and impingement at the radial tuberosity, have been shown to contribute to tendon pathology [3-5]. Distal biceps tendon pathology typically presents with an increased fluid signal on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). This ranges from small effusions around the tendon insertion to collections within the tendon sheath and cubital fossa [6].

Bauer et al. [7] demonstrated that failure of nonoperative treatment strongly correlates with partial tears involving more than 50% of the tendon footprint. Gradual exercise therapy can optimize tendon load capacity while minimizing the strain on the healing tendon and could improve symptoms [9]. Nevertheless, some patients continue to have complaints, and up to 50% of patients ultimately require surgery [7].

Peritendinous fluid, synovitis, and bursitis are common conditions that can accompany partial tears and may be the sources of persistent pain [2,10]. We hypothesize that ultrasound-guided intrabursal corticosteroid infiltration is a safe treatment for patients with a partial distal biceps rupture, potentially reducing the need for surgical intervention. The primary aim of this retrospective study was to assess the number of patients with partial distal biceps ruptures who proceeded to surgical treatment at least 6 weeks following ultrasound-guided corticosteroid infiltration following conservative treatment. The secondary aim was to investigate the prevalence of adverse events associated with infiltration.

METHODS

The Institutional Review Board of Amphia Hospital (MEC-U, reference number W19.223) determined that this study does not fall under the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO); therefore, ethical approval was not required, and the need for individual informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design of the study.

Patient Selection

This retrospective study included patients diagnosed with a partial distal biceps tendon rupture, defined as involvement of less than 50% of the footprint, who underwent ultrasound-guided intrabursal peritendinous corticosteroid infiltration. Patients were identified through a query of our institutional database for diagnoses of partial rupture of the distal biceps, using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, codes for “distal biceps rupture” and “infiltration therapy.” All cases were seen at a tertiary referral center for elbow pathology during 2010–2021. Study exclusion criteria were a history of elbow injury, surgery, rupture involving more than 50% of the footprint based on MRI findings, systemic steroid use, and follow-up less than 6 weeks (per the criteria used by Bauer et al. [7]).

Data Collection

A chart review was conducted to extract relevant data, including demographic information (age, sex, smoking status), injury date, initial physician evaluation, mechanism of injury, details of nonoperative treatments, surgical reports, and clinical follow-up results. Patients were categorized according to occupational demands, with those requiring heavy lifting (foreseeably >25 kg) or repeated elbow flexion and supination classified as “high-demanding” and all other occupations classified as “low-demanding.”

All included patients had been injured more than 4 weeks before infiltration. There was no standardized physical therapy protocol; each patient was treated according to the individual approach of their physical therapist. MRI was used to differentiate between distal biceps tendinopathy and (partial) tears. Partial tears were identified on MRI scans, which were reviewed and interpreted by both a radiologist and an orthopedic surgeon with specialized training in elbow surgery. All patients received an ultrasound-guided intrabursal infiltration of 4 mL of local anesthetic (lidocaine HCL, 2%) and 1 mL of corticosteroid (10 mg/mL) (Kenacort-A; Bristol Myers Squibb).

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel 2010 (Microsoft Corp.) and the SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp.). Median and range were used for non-normally distributed continuous variables, while mean and standard deviation were used for normally distributed continuous variables. Normality was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Frequencies and percentages are reported for categorical variables. A paired t-test was used to compare normally distributed data between the two groups, while a Mann-Whitney U-test was used to compare skewed data. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare proportions. The level of statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 90 patients received an infiltration for distal biceps pathology. Patients with tendinopathy without a tear on MRI were excluded from the study. Additionally, 22 patients were excluded due to insufficient follow-up (<6 weeks), primarily as a result of disruptions during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic. The remaining 52 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 2. The mean age at infiltration was 51 years (standard deviation, 12.0). The median follow-up was 15 months (range, 1.2–45); more specifically, the follow-up duration was 6 weeks in four patients, more than 3–5 months in six patients, 6 months in 12 patients, and at least 1 year in the remaining 30 patients. Work intensity was documented in 43 of 52 cases; 31 patients (72%) had high-demanding occupations, while 12 patients (28%) had low-demanding ones. A single infiltration was administered to 39 patients (75%), two infiltrations were administered to 12 patients (23%), and three infiltrations were administered to one patient (2%). No complications (such as neurovascular damage, skin thinning, loss of skin color, or post-infiltration flare) were reported after the infiltration.

Six weeks after infiltration, patients were assessed at the outpatient clinic to evaluate the clinical effect. Patient-reported outcome measurements (PROMs) were available in only 23 patients. As incomplete PROMs are not a useful addition, we refrained from analyzing these data. Several patients still had complaints at least 42 days after the corticosteroid infiltration. Eventually, surgical reconstruction of the distal biceps tendon was performed in 34 patients (65.4%) (Table 3). No statistical relationship was found between the number of administered infiltrations and the need for surgical repair (P=0.2). The median time from infiltration to surgery was 7.7 months (range, 2.3–24). One patient sustained a new trauma to the elbow (collision) 4 weeks after the infiltration, resulting in a complete rupture of the distal biceps as confirmed by MRI. The patient opted for nonoperative treatment and achieved a satisfactory outcome. No other adverse events were reported. Following surgery, two patients (6%) experienced a re-rupture of the distal biceps tendon. No neurovascular complications were observed following surgery.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to evaluate the number of patients with partial distal biceps tendon ruptures who ultimately required surgery following ultrasound-guided intrabursal peritendinous corticosteroid infiltration, as well as the complication rate associated with this treatment. Our primary finding is that 35% of patients with a partial rupture were successfully treated with infiltration, eliminating the need for surgery. Unfortunately, we did not include a control group in this study, so it was not possible to compare the infiltration to a structured physical therapy rehabilitation program. We hypothesize that the beneficial effect of intrabursal corticosteroid infiltrations can be attributed to the anti-inflammatory effect on concomitant bicipitoradial bursitis.

One patient sustained a complete rupture following a trauma to the elbow 4 weeks after the infiltration. Although this event occurred after the injection, it remains unclear whether the rupture was causally related to the infiltration or whether it would have occurred regardless of the previously compromised tendon. Notably, this patient declined surgical treatment and was successfully managed conservatively.

Current literature lacks comprehensive information regarding the optimal treatment for partial distal biceps ruptures [11]. With limited sample sizes, it remains unclear which patients with a partially ruptured distal biceps tendon may derive success from nonoperative treatment and which need surgery. The natural course of a partial distal biceps rupture is unknown; no prior studies have reported relevant data, and this study lacks a control group to monitor the natural course of the partially torn tendons. Bauer et al. [7] reported a surgical conversion rate of 56%, which encompassed a period of rest and avoidance of aggravating activities, along with braces and corticosteroid infiltration in certain cases. Interestingly, the duration of nonoperative treatment did not negatively affect the surgical outcomes. The percentage of conversion to surgery in our study (65%) is in line with these findings. In other tendinopathies, corticosteroid infiltrations have been more extensively studied. Especially in Achilles tendinopathy, new findings suggest benefit of infiltration therapy combined with physical therapy [12].

The current study is the first to describe a relatively large cohort of patients treated with corticosteroids for partial distal biceps tendon ruptures. As it was a single-center study, all outpatient clinic and surgical reports of the patients were accessible and uniform. However, this study also has some limitations, including its retrospective design and lack of a control group. We were also not able to monitor the “natural” course of a partial distal biceps rupture, and this remains unclear in the current literature, although it was reported that a rupture of more than 50% of the footprint is best reconstructed [6]. Not all demographics were documented in the physician notes; characteristics such as smoking or subjective effects after the infiltration were not described in all patients. Additionally, it would have been beneficial to administer questionnaires with PROMs to evaluate the perceived outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

Ultrasound-guided intrabursal infiltration is a possible treatment option for partial distal biceps ruptures involving less than 50% of the footprint if progressive exercise therapy fails. In this study, surgery was avoided in 35% of patients. We hypothesize that the beneficial effect of intrabursal corticosteroid infiltration can be attributed to its positive impact on concomitant bicipitoradial bursitis. Future research, ideally incorporating PROMs and randomized comparisons with a placebo and physical therapy, is warranted to validate these findings and further guide clinical decision-making.
