Identification of two point mutations associated with inherited antithrombin deficiency
Authors: Shiue-Wei Lai, Chia-Yau Chang, Hwei-Jen Lee, Yeu-Chin Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntithrombin (AT) is a serine protease inhibitor which exerts its anticoagulant effect through binding to serine residues in the active centers of procoagulant serine proteases. Its deficiency is associated with increased risk of venous thrombosis. We aim to investigate the pathogenic mechanism of two natural mutants (W221C and M284R) in inherited AT deficiency.MethodsWe analyzed 9 unrelated patients with inherited AT deficiency by extracting peripheral blood DNA and sequencing the SERPINC1 gene after amplification by polymerase chain reaction. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and heparin affinity chromatography were used to assess AT secretion and purification efficiency. The mutant AT models were evaluated via computational simulations.ResultsAmong the 9 patients with inherited AT deficiency, 8 patients had type I AT deficiency, and one patient had type II AT deficiency with subtype of reactive site mutation. Seven of them experienced venous thrombotic events and all patients were found genetic mutations including missense (n = 6), deletion (n = 2) and insertion (n = 1). Two point mutations, W221C and M284R, were identified and were hypothesized to affect AT by destabilizing the central β-sheet. Based on immunoassays and heparin purification, the W221C mutant may impair AT secretion, whereas M284R mutant decreased the total AT production (696.8 ± 151.6 ng/ml versus 3833.72 ± 315.4 ng/ml, p = 0.029). Both mutants delayed the peak of AT release in heparin affinity chromatography.ConclusionsOur study demonstrates that two mutations in SERPINC1 gene altered the production and structure of AT by in vitro protein expression and functional studies, including protein secretion and production. These findings enhance our understanding of the genetic basis of AT deficiency and its possible clinical implications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12959-024-00677-6.Keywords: Inherited antithrombin deficiency, Thrombosis disorder, SERPINC1

Introduction

Antithrombin (AT) serves as a crucial regulator of blood clotting by inhibiting key enzymes involved in the coagulation cascade. Classified as a serine protease inhibitor (SERPIN), it targets thrombin, factor (F) IXa, FXa, FXIa, and FXIIa for degradation. Together with the tissue factor pathway inhibitor, AT also acts as an inhibitor of FVIIa; however, FVIIa only becomes sensitive to inhibition by AT when it is bound to tissue factor [1]. The SERPINC1 gene, comprising 7 exons and spanning over 13 kb, encodes the AT protein [2]. While AT exhibits inherent activity, its activation can be enhanced by the presence of heparan sulfate or the administration of heparins [3].

Considering the structure of AT, three intramolecular disulfide bonds are formed by six of the amino acids: Cys8-Cys128, Cys21-Cys95, and Cys248-Cys430. Most of the circulating AT undergoes post-translational modifications, leading to the four asparagine (Asn)-linked oligosaccharides at positions 96, 135, 155, and 192. In the predominant form of human AT, α-antithrombin, covalently attached oligosaccharide sidechains occupy all of these sites, resulting in a molecular weight of 58,200 Da [4]. However, it is worth noting that the potential glycosylation site at Asn 135 remains unoccupied in a minor form of AT, known as β-antithrombin, which constitutes approximately 10% of the total [5].

AT deficiency can be categorized as either hereditary or acquired, both increase the risk of thromboembolism [6–8]. It is characterized by consistently low levels of AT activity, typically below 80% of normal or the lower limit of the assay’s reference range [9]. Inherited AT deficiency, primarily caused by mutations in the SERPINC1 gene, follows an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance and is a rare thrombotic disorder [1]. Its prevalence in the general population ranges from 0.02 to 0.2% (1 in 5000 to 1 in 500 individuals). This condition is associated with a significantly increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE), up to 16 times higher than in healthy individuals [10–12]. Based on the circulating antigen level and functional activity of AT, type I AT deficiency is characterized by low levels of both AT activity and antigen, typically below 70 IU/dL. In contrast, type II AT deficiency represents a qualitative defect, producing a variant protein with decreased function despite normal antigen levels [13]. The functional defect in type II variants involves either the heparin binding site (type II HBS), the reactive site (type II RS), or both (pleiotropic effects in type II PE) [13]. Since progressive AT activity, assessed through a functional assay without heparin, is normal only in type II HBS deficiency, this second-line test serves as an effective tool for distinguishing these patients from others with AT deficiency [14].

The clinical manifestations and severity of this disease vary based on the specific type of deficiency and the mutation site within the SERPINC1 gene. Type I deficiency typically arises from small deletions or insertions, larger deletions, or single base substitutions in the genetic sequence. These alterations can lead to decreased protein production, while changes in the signal peptide may impair post-translational processing or alter AT protein stability [15, 16]. Type II inherited AT deficiency results from various mutations affecting different aspects of protein function. Type II RS deficiency is characterized by damage to the thrombin-binding domain, resulting in low AT activity alongside normal AT antigen levels. Type II HBS deficiency, the most common subtype, is caused by defects in the heparin-binding domain. Most frequent mutations sites of this subtype are AT Basel (p.Pro73Leu), AT Padua (p.Arg79His), and AT Budapest 3 (p.Leu131Phe) [17–19]. Type II PE involves defects in the reactive loop site, leading to conformational changes in the protein. This subtype exhibits reductions in both heparin binding and progressive AT activity [9].

Patients with AT deficiency most commonly present with deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism; however, phenotypic variations exist among different classifications of AT deficiency. Notably, type II HBS is associated with a relatively lower risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) compared to other AT defects. Various abnormalities affecting the heparin binding site have been identified, leading to isolated reductions in heparin cofactor activity. Individuals with this subtype typically show plasma AT-heparin cofactor activity measurements around 50% while maintaining normal progressive AT activity [20, 21].

In cases involving the homozygous variant of the Budapest 3 mutation (ATBp3), arterial thrombotic events such as myocardial infarction, stroke, and abdominal arterial thrombosis have been reported, along with juvenile thrombotic incidents. Regarding pregnancy complications linked to AT deficiency, the risk of pregnancy-related VTE is similar across both types of AT deficiency, except for the heterozygous variant of ATBp3. Adverse pregnancy outcomes are particularly associated with type II deficiencies, especially in the homozygous variant of HBS in ATBp3 [22]. The effectiveness of direct oral anticoagulants for secondary prevention of VTE in patients with severe inherited thrombophilia varies significantly. Treatment failures may be more pronounced in type II AT deficiency, particularly in patients with the homozygous variant of the HBS mutation in ATBp3 [23]. Therefore, genetic analysis is crucial for distinguishing between different types of AT deficiency and is essential for assessing risk and tailoring appropriate management strategies for individuals affected by this condition.

Based on literature review, approximately 400 distinct mutations in the SERPINC1 gene have been documented in the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) [24, 25]. Given that inherited AT deficiency can result in significant morbidity and even potentially fatal events, the identification of mutations associated with this condition can be of substantial scientific and clinical importance by providing insights into disease mechanism and potential targeted therapeutic strategies. The objective of this study was to validate the pathogenic impact of SERPINC1 mutants in inherited AT deficiency through in vitro protein expression and functional analyses.

Materials and methods

Patients, blood samples and genetic sequencing

Between August 2009 and April 2014, a cohort of 9 patients with inherited AT deficiency was identified based on clinical presentation and low AT activity assessed using a chromogenic method reliant on FXa inactivation (Liquid Antithrombin, HemosIL). Informed consent was obtained from each patient, and DNA from peripheral blood leukocytes was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was employed to amplify seven exons, including the intron/exon boundaries, of the SERPINC1 gene, followed by direct sequencing for mutation analysis.

Cell line and culture

Human embryo kidney 293 (HEK293) cell lines were maintained in DMEM medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium; Gibco, Invitrogen) and all cells were incubated at 37℃ in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 in air.

Construction of recombinant wild-type and mutant AT expression vectors

The pReceiver-M13 expression plasmid clone containing the full-length of SERPINC1 gene was purchased from GeneCopoeia™, Rockville, USA. The mutants c.663G > T, p.Trp221Cys(W221C) and c.851T > G, p.Met284Arg(M284R) were constructed with Quick Change site-directed mutagenesis kit (Stratagene, California, USA) using the pReceiver-M13 expression plasmid clone containing the full-length of SERPINC1 gene. A primer pair used to create a mutant clone is described in Table 1. We also used restriction enzymes (BamH I and SacII) and sequencing to confirm that the created mutant clones were correct.

Transient expression of the recombinant ATs

All chemicals used were of analytical grade. Transient gene expression in HEK293 cells is an established approach for the rapid production of large amount of recombinant ATs proteins (r-proteins). HEK293 cells (ATCC® CRL1573™) were cultured in DMEM medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium; Gibco, Invitrogen), supplemented with 10% FBS (peak serum, Nicaragua) in adherent format. Cells were plated at least 24 h prior to transfection when adherent cells reached about 70–80% confluency. We prepared the PolyJet-DNA Complex (SignaGen Laboratories, MD, USA) by transiently transfecting with 5ug plasmid DNA following the manufacturer’s manual. After incubation for 48 h, we collected the cell culture medium and cell lysate respectively. The cell lysate was dissolved in m-PER lysis buffer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and centrifuged at 1500 g for 20 min, subsequently prepared as Western blot samples. The cell culture medium was concentrated by Vivaspin 6 collection tubes (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB, Sweden), and prepared as Heparin experiment samples or Western blot samples.

Affinity chromatography of recombinant ATs on heparin-sepharose

Affinity chromatography of each recombinant AT molecule was performed on a heparin–sepharose column by Amersham Biosciences AKTA FPLC System with UPC-900. Concentrated media containing wild type (WT), W221C and M284R were subjected to column chromatography on the HITRAP Heparin HP 1mL affinity column (GE Healthcare, Sweden). The HITRAP Heparin HP column was equilibrated with 10 mmol/l sodium phosphate buffer (pH7) before injecting the concentrated media. The column was washed with five-column volumes of the equilibration buffer, and the bound AT was then eluted by a gradient stepwise increase in the concentration between 0 and 2 M saline. The concentration of AT in each fraction was determined by Human Serpin C1/Antithtombin-III Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, USA) following the manufacturer’s manual.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The ELISA method was used to determine the concentration of the target AT protein. Human Serpin C1/Antithtombin-III Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, USA) were used per the manufacturer’s manual. A mono-clonal antibody specific for human Serpin C1 was pre-coated onto a microplate. Standards and samples were pipetted into the wells and any Serpin C1 was bound by the immobilized antibody. After washing unbound substances, the detection antibody was added to form a complex. After washing to remove any unbound antibody enzyme reagent, a substrate solution was added to the wells. The color development was stopped, and the intensity of the color was measured by ELISA reader (BioTek Instrument, USA) with the wavelength of 450 nm.

Western immunoblotting analysis

Total cellular protein was extracted from HEK293 cells after 48 h of transiently transfected plasmid DNA. Western blotting was performed using standard methods. Cells were washed with PBS and then lysed in m-PER Mammalian Protein extraction reagent (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Total protein was determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Pierce Biotechnology). 10ug of total cell lysate protein were prepared as western blot samples. The proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane, followed by blocking with Tris-buffered saline plus skimmed milk. These PVDF membranes were then probed by using a AT primary antibody (polyclonal Human Serpin C1/AT antibody (R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA) or AT antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), followed by secondary antibody (anti-goat IgG horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibody (R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA) or anti-rabbit IgG horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibody (Cell Signaling, Massachusetts, USA)). The internal control antibody that used was monoclonal anti-ß-actin antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA). Images were captured and analyzed using the SPOT IdeaTM Analysis System (USA).

Structural models

The mutant antithrombin models were generated based on the antithrombin-thrombin-heparin mimetic ternary complex (pdb code: 1TB6) using the Built Mutants protocol in the program of Discovery Studio (version 4.5) (Accelrys Inc, San Diego, CA) [26]. The geometries of the models were optimized using the algorithm of smart minimization in CHARMm force field including the implicit solvent model of generalized born in the calculation.

Statistical analysis

All assays were performed in quadruple. Reported data results were expressed as means ± S.E.M. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad prism (v.5.01 GraphPad Software Inc, CA, USA). The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons among more than two groups, while the Mann-Whitney U test was employed for comparisons between two groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Transient expression analysis confirmed M284R mutant decreased total production of AT

To investigate whether point mutations in AT affect the intracellular (cell lysate) and extracellular (culture medium) expression, recombinant mutants W221C and M284R AT were transiently expressed in HEK293 cells. As shown in Fig. 2, the AT protein expression of W221C mutant is mildly decreased and M284R mutant is markedly decreased in culture medium (Fig. 2a). The AT protein expression of W221C and M284R mutant are increased compared to WT in cell lysate (Fig. 2b). The quantitative analyses of AT protein by ELISA showed similar quantities with W221C mutant and WT combined in culture medium and cell lysate (p = 0.343). The total AT protein concentration of M284R mutant was significantly lower than that of WT (p = 0.029, Fig. 3).

On the other hand, HEK293 cells expressing M284R mutant had significantly lower total AT production than that in WT (696.8 ± 151.6 ng/ml versus 3833.72 ± 315.4 ng/ml, Fig. 3). These results were compatible with the phenotypes: patient 3 with W221C had type II RS AT deficiency and patient 5 with M284R had type 1 AT deficiency.

Discussion

Natural variants of antithrombin have been instrumental in understanding its role in blood clot prevention and its structural characteristics, as well as the processes that result in AT deficiency and the related risk of thrombosis. Antithrombin’s structure comprises three highly conserved β-sheets and nine α-helices, making it both highly adaptable and susceptible to the impacts of genetic mutations [28]. Due to the rarity of AT deficiency, research on the mutation profiles in the SERPINC1 gene, which encodes AT, is scarce. Nevertheless, while numerous mutations and various genetic changes have been identified in this gene [2], the direct causative relationship of these mutations has rarely been explored or verified in living organisms, particularly within the Asian population. In the present study, we described both the genetic basis and clinical thrombotic profile of AT deficiency in 9 unrelated Chinese patients. A total of 8 mutations were identified and 4 novel mutations of c.539delG、c.992delTCAC、c.1148insC and W221C were reported first. The mutation of M284R in exon 4 had been reported but the causative effect was not confirmed by functional study [27].

The SERPINC1 gene, situated on chromosome band 1q23-q25, consists of 7 exons (numbered 1, 2, 3a, 3b, 4, 5, 6) and 6 intervening introns [29]. The primary functional areas of AT include the active site, which exon 6 encodes, and the heparin binding domain, which is encoded by exon 2. The majority of AT mutations are either point mutations or minor insertions/deletions [30]. To assess the potential pathogenic impact of a missense point mutation, functional analyses were conducted on the mutations of W221C in exon 3b and M284R in exon 4.

In our cohort, the patient with W221C mutation had a phenotype of type II RS AT in which the defect disrupted the enzyme-inhibitor interaction. Most cases are caused by mutations that impact the crucial P1 position at the reactive site, specifically Arginine 425 and Serine 426 [1]. Additionally, mutations in the conserved alanine residues, numbers 414 and 416, located in the stalk of the reactive center loop of antithrombin, have been identified [1].

According to predictions from structural modeling, the W221 mutation is positioned in an α-helix and could destabilize the central β-sheet, mainly by forming hydrophobic interactions with residues of A175, R177, F179 and K225. Sonia and colleagues noted that the mutants of D174T, N144T, G328T, and G328N located within the A-sheet, exhibited similar secretion levels comparable to those of WT [31]. The observation differed from our findings, where the W221C mutation influenced AT secretion, suggesting that various point mutations affecting the α-helix could alter physiological functions. Moreover, point mutations in reactive center loop (RCL) may lead to pathophysiological consequences. For example, the mutations at positions of Gly-392, Ala-384, Ser-382, Gln-381, and Ser-380 would affect RCL by altering its conformation. This alteration in conformation makes it difficult to trap the acyl-intermediate, causing the serpin to act variably as a substrate for the target protease [32].

We sought to prove our concept by fundamental tools in protein studies and translational biology research. The in vitro study utilizing western blot analysis and measuring the total concentration of AT in both cell lysate and medium indicated that the AT protein production was comparable between the W221C mutant and WT. However, a lower percentage of AT protein was found in the cell medium for the W221C mutant compared to the WT. This finding suggests that the missense mutation at position 663 in exon 3b of AT may impair its secretory function, rather than affecting its overall production.

Type II RS deficiency has traditionally been considered as a qualitative defect in AT antigen. However, our experimental results demonstrated that the W221C mutant exhibited a secretory defect, which was not compatible with AT type II RS deficiency. This discrepancy may suggest that the underlying mechanisms of type II RS deficiency may be more complex than previously thought, involving not only functional impairments but also issues related to the secretion of mutant AT proteins. Puurunen et al. found that in populations with a strong founder effect, type II AT deficiency is primarily attributed to a single point mutation, p.Pro73Leu. This mutation is significantly linked to an elevated risk of thrombosis. Additionally, the decrease in AT activity caused by this mutation may not be detectable using all available screening methods [17]. The regulation of AT antigen in the human body is complex and cannot be fully addressed through limited cellular experiments alone. This complexity arises from various factors, including the intricate interactions between AT and other components of the coagulation system, the influence of genetic variations, and the multifaceted nature of post-translational modifications. Additionally, the physiological context in which AT functions, such as its interactions with heparin and other glycosaminoglycans, further complicates our understanding. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of AT regulation requires a combination of in vitro studies, in vivo models, and clinical observations to capture the full spectrum of its biological activity and regulatory mechanisms.

In type I AT deficiency, missense mutations have been identified that lead to abnormal glycosylation and intracellular retention of AT or are linked to its reduced conformational stability [33, 34]. Corral et al. reported two missense mutations, Pro80Ser and Gly424Arg, which impact the stability of the AT shutter region. They suggested that these mutations could induce a conformational effect, highlighting the potential of missense mutations to affect protein structure and function [35]. In our study group, the individual with M284R mutant exhibited a type I AT phenotype. Western blot analysis and measurement of total AT concentration (from both cell lysate and medium) revealed that the AT protein production in the M284R mutant was lower compared to that of WT (p = 0.029). Thus, it appears that the M284R mutant may impact the production of AT protein within cells.

However, to evaluate the secretion efficiency and intracellular degradation of the AT mutant, conducting pulse-chase experiments in the presence of a transport inhibitor and protease inhibitors is crucial [36, 37]. This approach can help identify intracellular degradation occurring during endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-Golgi transport or within the pre-Golgi apparatus. In addition, to investigate the intracellular localization of the AT mutant, endo H digestion followed by SDS-PAGE analysis and immunofluorescence staining of cells transiently transfected with the W221C and M284R mutants are of importance [36, 37] as this can identify secretion-defective mutants of AT that are predominantly localized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Therefore, the intracellular degradation and potential secretion defect associated with the W221C and M284R mutants requires further studies.

Heparin affinity chromatography is utilized to purify AT and to distinguish between various inactive forms of AT. This method leverages the strong binding affinity between heparin and AT, allowing for the selective separation and purification of AT from a mixture of proteins based on their interaction with heparin [38]. Using a linear gradient elution with an increasing salt concentration, our study found that both the W221C and M284R mutants exhibited a delayed release peak of AT protein. This observation suggests that the mutations in the β-sheet regions may alter the dynamics of AT protein’s interaction with heparin.

Due to its highly specific binding site for heparin, AT undergoes a complex series of conformational changes upon binding to heparin, leading to enhanced activity against FXa and FIXa [39–41]. Heparin chains can also enhance the activity of AT against its targets by forming ternary complexes, which further increases AT’s inhibition of thrombin [26]. Among the type II HBS mutations, AT Padua has the most significant effect, showing the slowest formation of the AT-heparin complex and the weakest interaction, as supported by molecular modeling. ATBp3displayed the strongest heparin interaction while also destabilizing AT, indicating a quantitative aspect to its pathogenicity [42]. To evaluate the molecular mechanisms underlying impaired heparin pentasaccharide interactions in AT HBS mutants, enhanced sampling molecular dynamics (MD) can be employed. Recent studies have utilized enhanced sampling MD simulations to investigate various AT mutations, including R47H, and their interactions with heparin. This approach provides a detailed atomic-level understanding of how these mutations affect conformational changes and binding affinities, shedding light on the relationship between the W221C and M284R mutants and heparin. Enhanced sampling techniques like GaMD and LiGaMD2 have been particularly effective in revealing the distinct molecular mechanisms behind altered AT–heparin interactions in these mutants [43].

Structural mutations impacting residues buried within the protein core lead to modifications in amino acid size, charge, and hydrogen bonding capabilities [44]. Regarding the allosteric mechanism, such mutations induce structural alterations in AT protein, affecting the RCL, the heparin-binding site, and the hydrophobic core that are integral to the AT protein’s structure and function [45]. The native tertiary structure of this protein encompasses a domain that specifically interacts with the heparin pentasaccharide ligand. The coordination involves the protein’s N-terminal region, the N-terminal end of helix A, and the entirety of helix D, which together constitute the binding site [39]. In this study group, the W221C mutation was found in α-helix 7, while the M284R mutation occurred in β-strand 6, both of which are adjacent to the central β-sheet. Analysis of the AT-thrombin-heparin mimetic ternary complex structure indicates that these mutations, W221C and M284R, could destabilize the central β-sheet, potentially resulting in a loss of function. Furthermore, they might disrupt the interactions between the RCL and thrombin. These results demonstrated that changes in the AT protein may influence both its secretion and overall production, establishing a connection between specific point mutations and impaired AT function. The study of the AT protein emphasized the relationship between genetic variability and its biological function, particularly in the context of hereditary AT deficiency. Consequently, a thorough understanding of AT regulation necessitates an integrated approach that combines in vitro studies, in vivo models, and clinical observations to encompass the complete range of its biological activity and regulatory mechanisms.

Conclusion

Our research represents the initial attempt to clarify the pathogenic effects of AT mutations on protein expression and functionality in vitro. Through ELISA and Western blotting, it was determined that the W221C and M284R mutations in AT may impact both protein secretion and production. These mutations likely alter the protein’s capacity to bind to heparin and cause a delay in peak AT release. The findings enhance our comprehension of the potential molecular mechanisms and could lead to innovative treatment approaches for inherited AT deficiency.
