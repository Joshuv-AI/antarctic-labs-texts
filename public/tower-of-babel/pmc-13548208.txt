Quantitative evaluations of time-course and effectiveness of systemic treatments for atopic dermatitis: protocol for a pharmacodynamic model-based meta-analysis
Authors: Huan He, Boran Yu, Zigang Xu, Peng Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTreatment responses to systemic therapies for atopic dermatitis (AD) may vary significantly based on medication types, treatment duration, concomitant topical corticosteroid use and specific patient subgroups. Evidence regarding the optimal medication selection and predictors of treatment response is warranted. A systematic approach to studying the effectiveness of systemic treatments from multiple perspectives may generate previously unreported evidence. We aim to quantitatively evaluate the time-course and effectiveness of various systemic treatments for AD, and to identify associated factors that significantly affect their effectiveness, using a pharmacodynamic model-based meta-analysis (MBMA). This protocol describes the methods for this analysis.Methods and analysisData for the analysis will mainly originate from the database of a living network meta-analysis. We will include randomised controlled trials examining systemic treatments against placebo or any active comparator in patients with moderate-to-severe AD. The Investigator Global Assessment Scale, the Eczema Area and Severity Index Scale, the Patient Oriented Eczema Measure Scale and scales measuring quality of life or itch will be used as outcome measures. Study screening, data extraction and quality assessment will be conducted by two investigators independently. Where available, we will extract efficacy outcome data at each follow-up time point. We will use MBMA, the core principle of which involves developing a pharmacodynamic model to synthesise these longitudinal trial-level data. Whenever possible, modelling and simulation will be conducted for both binary and continuous outcomes. This MBMA will be performed using hierarchical models with non-linear mixed-effects methods including structural, random-effects and covariate models with the maximum likelihood estimation method. Response to systemic treatments will be analysed by conducting 1000 Monte Carlo simulations. Potential influencing factors will be tested using covariate modelling and subgroup analysis.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required for this study. Results from our analyses will be published in a peer-reviewed journal.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251068535

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common chronic inflammatory skin disease,1 2 with a worldwide prevalence of approximately 20% among children1 and of 2%–7% among adults.3–6 It results in impaired quality of life (QoL) and a substantial burden of disease.7–9 For patients with more severe AD and patients whose AD is refractory to topical therapy, systemic treatment may be warranted to achieve adequate disease control.10 Currently, there has been rapid development of novel systemic immunomodulatory therapies. The approvals of the anti-interleukin 4Rα (IL-4Rα) antibody dupilumab, the anti-IL-13 antibody tralokinumab and the anti-IL-31Rα antibody nemolizumab have provided first-in-class representatives of different therapeutic strategies for moderate-to-severe AD treatment.11–13 Other targeted agents include lebrikizumab, stapokibart, abrocitinib, upadacitinib and baricitinib, which were approved in various jurisdictions.14–17 In addition, several clinical trials of other biologics and small molecules are underway and results will also be announced. Various new biologics and small molecules provide much better levels of evidence than conventional systemic treatments and are strongly recommended as core systemic therapies in clinical guidelines.10 18 However, treatment responses may vary significantly based on medication types, treatment duration, concomitant topical corticosteroid (TCS) use and specific patient subgroups. The complexity in selecting optimal systemic AD therapies stems from two unresolved issues: unquantified benefits of concomitant TCS use and lack of standardised comparison for peak efficacy magnitude and time to peak response across therapeutic agents. In addition, the recommendation on patient subgroups which can benefit more from systemic therapies is lacking.

In clinical practice, most patients will use evidence-based topical therapies, including emollients and topical anti-inflammatory medications, concomitantly with systemic therapies.10 Although the effectiveness of combination treatments has been proven in some clinical trials, it is still unclear how significant the difference is between monotherapy and combination therapy with TCS. To date, there are no head-to-head trials to make direct comparisons. Whether a background TCS is needed remains to be answered as the novel targeted agents showed a very high therapeutic effect.

In clinical therapeutics, the onset time of action and magnitude of peak effectiveness are two issues of concern. European guidelines (EuroGuiDerm) on atopic eczema reported time-to-response estimates for immunosuppressants, biologics and Janus kinase inhibitors. However, these recommendations are based on expert opinion rather than formal quantitative evidence synthesis.18 Therefore, updated evidence regarding the time course of response for different systemic therapies is warranted. Existing evidence on comparative efficacy was generated by conducting a network meta-analysis (NMA). A previously published living NMA contains the most abundant data in the field so far, and enables comparisons between systemic medications, most of which have not been compared in head-to-head trials.19 Although informative, this study has limitations created by the NMA methodology. The efficacy data were obtained at different endpoints (ranging from 8 weeks to 24 weeks) and were pooled for analysis, neglecting the effect of time on treatment efficacy. Moreover, an important difference in the design of the included trials is in the use of concomitant topical anti-inflammatory therapies along with systemic medications. This analytical approach cannot eliminate heterogeneity among studies. Thus, the most effective treatment option for systemic therapies can only be determined through more comprehensive analyses.

Model-based meta-analysis (MBMA) is an efficient technique that can integrate time-course with dose-response metrics of medications by making full use of each longitudinal data and establishing a pharmacodynamic model.20 This model can explain, adjust and estimate heterogeneity in the observed efficacy data between the different intervention arms.21 22 It can also identify associated factors potentially influencing efficacy parameters and quantify correlations such as concomitant background TCS therapy, baseline severity and disease duration by applying a covariate model.23 24 Thus, specific participant subgroups which may benefit more from systemic therapies can be recommended. In terms of these functions, MBMA is better than conventional meta-analysis. Therefore, considering bridging these evidence gaps, we propose to develop a pharmacodynamic model that will quantify peak effectiveness magnitude and time to peak response across distinct therapeutic regimens, assess the synergistic effect size of concomitant TCS therapy, and ultimately enable head-to-head efficacy comparisons of systemic immunomodulatory agents in moderate-to-severe AD. Furthermore, we aim to delineate predictors (population-level and intervention-level) affecting the efficacy of systemic therapies. These findings will provide evidence-based guidance for individualised AD management. To date, MBMA remains a novel methodology in dermatology, with no prior applications in AD research.

Methods and analysis

This protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols guideline and has been registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251068535).

Information sources and literature search

This study focuses on the interventions of systemic immunomodulatory treatments for AD, which is highly consistent with a living systematic review and NMA led by Dr Drucker.19 Iterations of that systematic review have been previously published, with a comprehensive search strategy and clear screening process.19 26–29 We will search the studies identified in the newly updated NMA of systemic therapies in AD.29 The living systematic review uses electronic searches every 4 months in the following databases: Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, MEDLINE, Embase, Latin American and Caribbean Health Science Information database, Global Resource of Eczema Trials database and trial registries from inception. The full search strategy can be found in the supplements of the published papers.28 Ongoing updates of the search results will be available on http://www.eczematherapies.com/research. If the living review has been updated within the past 4 months at the time of our search, we will use its most recent study list as the foundation and will not perform an independent de novo search. Instead, we will supplement this with manual checks for recently published trials to capture any studies published after the living review’s last update. When the living NMA stops updates or some landmark clinical trial results have been published before the website update, we will also carry out an updated search. For update search, the databases MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and ClinicalTrials.gov will be searched again independently.

This study is not restricted to trials included in the living NMA. We will also conduct a manual search from reference lists of relevant systematic reviews, literature reviews and included articles to identify additional eligible trials or search the websites of ClinicalTrials.gov for more information of studies that have been retrieved. The data from peer-reviewed publications, trial registries, conference abstracts and product monographs will be included in our study. Only studies published in English will be selected.

Screening process

This study will be carried out based on results of the living NMA.29 We will import the retrieved items from the NMA and results from other sources into citation manager EndNote V.X8 and remove duplications. First, titles and abstracts of studies will be screened by two independent investigators to identify those that potentially meet the inclusion criteria. Then, potentially eligible studies will be further reviewed in the full text and independently evaluated for eligibility by two reviewers according to inclusion and exclusion criteria as above. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion with one of the senior team members.

Data extraction and literature quality assessment

Aggregate longitudinal data (means or proportions at reported time points) are used in our analysis, which is the standard data source in published MBMA studies.

Non-linear mixed-effects modelling accommodates sparse and irregular longitudinal data by borrowing information across studies. Accordingly, trials will not be excluded based on the number of time points per se. All included trials will contribute to the model, regardless of their individual time point count.

Predesigned standardised forms in Excel sheets will be used to extract the following information: literature characteristics (authors, year of publication, clinical trial location, registration number and sponsor), trial design (sample size, study population, background TCS therapy, whether placebo controlled), intervention (mechanism of action, intervention category, treatment regimen and dose), baseline characteristics of participants (age, proportion of male participants, duration since onset of AD, percent of body surface area affected, EASI Score, proportion of participants having an IGA Score of 4, mean IGA Score, PP-NRS Score, POEM Score, SCORing Atopic Dermatitis Score and score related to QoL parameters) and clinical outcomes (IGA-success, EASI-50, EASI-75, EASI-90, ∆EASI, ∆POEM, ∆QoL and ∆PP-NRS) together with the time point of measurement.

We will define the use of concomitant TCS therapy as a binary variable (yes or no) by whether background TCS are permitted. ‘Concomitant TCS use’ refers to any protocol-permitted use of TCS (any frequency or potency) during the placebo-controlled period, as explicitly reported in each included trial. If TCS is only permitted as rescue therapy, we categorise the study as not using concomitant TCS therapy. When available, we will extract efficacy outcome data at each follow-up time point during the initial randomised controlled treatment period. The treatment period is defined as the time from baseline to the last dose, plus one dosing interval. Data of all the dose regimens will be extracted. We do not impose a uniform treatment period across studies. Instead, we will extract all available data points reported during the treatment period as defined by each study. For binary outcomes, we will extract the number or percentage of patients achieving the event and the total number included in the analysis. For continuous outcomes, we will extract change from baseline directly, a measure of variance (eg, SE) and total number of patients included in the analysis. If data are not reported as expected, we will use the per cent change and baseline value to calculate it. Alternatively, if data are not reported as change or per cent change from baseline, we will get the outcome measures by subtracting follow-up values from baseline values. When SD/SE data are missing, we will impute the missing SD using the mean of the known SDs derived from the group of included studies, consistent with the approach adopted in the living systematic review by Drucker et al. This is a well-established approach in meta-analysis.

Two investigators will extract the aforementioned data independently, and conflicts will be adjudicated by a senior researcher. The outcome data will be extracted from articles as well as from tables. If results are presented as a graph, the data will be captured by using the GetData Graph Digitizer (V.2.25). When the extraction errors of the graphics data between two researchers exceed 2%, data extraction will be repeated, and the mean values will be used as the final data.

The quality of included trials will be assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool 230 by two independent researchers. Any difference will be resolved through discussion with a senior team member. We will report the results of risk of bias in the form of tables and diagrams, and compare them with published studies.

Statistical analyses

We will synthesise longitudinal trial-level data using MBMA. This approach, which leverages the advantages outlined in the Introduction, differs fundamentally from both pairwise meta-analysis and NMA. Its core principle involves developing a pharmacodynamic model that integrates longitudinal response data with dose-response metrics.21 All pharmacodynamic models will be fitted using non-linear mixed-effects methods, with parameters estimated via maximum likelihood using NONMEM. This approach allows flexible handling of between-trial random effects and provides full posterior distributions for parameter uncertainty. Separate MBMA models will be fitted for each outcome.

Model development

MBMA will be performed using hierarchical models with the maximum likelihood estimation method. Individual-level parameters will subsequently be obtained using empirical Bayes estimation. Both binary and continuous outcomes will include structural, random-effects and covariate models.22 31

Structural model

The building of the structural model is data-driven. It aims to capture the temporal characteristics of response in the treatment groups. We will first conduct an exploratory analysis (eg, plotting) of data to examine the characteristics of its variations. Then, different structural models (eg, linear, exponential, sigmoid Emax, Bateman) will be tested to describe the longitudinal effect data if possible. If complex models (eg, sigmoid Emax with free Hill coefficient) are unidentifiable, then simpler models (eg, exponential, linear) will be accepted. However, polynomials or regression splines will not be employed for data-fitting to avoid overfitting and unexplainable parameters. Random-effects model

The random-effects model includes interstudy variability and residual error.33 The variability in the systemic therapy interventions and placebo effect between different studies can be described as interstudy variability. Exponential and additive models will be explored to describe the interstudy variability if possible. In our MBMA framework, multiarm trials will be handled by incorporating interarm variability (IAV) as a random effect to describe the between-arm correlation structure within the same study. However, we acknowledge that the inclusion of IAV increases model complexity. When the available data are insufficient to support estimation of all variance components simultaneously, we will simplify the random-effects structure as appropriate (eg, by omitting IAV) to ensure model convergence and stable parameter estimation. Covariate model

The covariate models are used to evaluate the factors (prespecified study-level aggregate covariates) that potentially affect the pharmacodynamic model parameters. The potential factors may include age, proportion of male participants, duration since onset of AD, per cent of body surface area affected, baseline EASI Score, proportion of participants having an IGA Score of 4, mean IGA Score, PP-NRS Score, POEM Score, SCORing Atopic Dermatitis (SCORAD) Score, score related to QoL parameters, background TCS therapy, intervention category, application method, study population and year of publication. We will also conduct a comprehensive literature review to identify potential covariates with prior biological or clinical plausibility. Continuous covariates will be tested by a linear or power function, and categorical covariates will be tested using an additive shift function. Collinearity diagnostics will be performed. After that, the covariate selection is based on the stepwise forward addition (p<0.05) and backward elimination (p<0.01) approach. Subsequently, the selected covariates will be reviewed in consultation with clinical experts to determine whether the associations are clinically interpretable. Only clinically and statistically reasonable covariates will be included in the final model.33 34 The covariate model results reflect between-trial differences rather than individual-level predictions. For any study-level covariate with a missing proportion of 30% or less, the missing values will be imputed using the median of the observed values for that covariate. The median of the observed covariate values represents the central tendency of the actual measured data from the included studies, and is therefore a reasonable and conservative estimate for missing values. Covariates with a missing proportion exceeding 30% will not be considered in the covariate evaluation to ensure model stability and result reliability.

The treatment effect in the systemic medication groups (Edrug) is a function dependent on dose, time, fixed-effects model parameters (θ), random-effects model parameters (η and ε) and trial covariates (X) (equation 5).32 Initially, we will estimate shared fixed-effects pharmacodynamic parameters for systemic treatments and get the typical value of effectiveness. Then, we will further refine the model by separating parameters for each drug or mechanism of action, if better model fit and reduced interstudy variability is achieved. The model development handling of MBMA is governed by an iterative process of model comparison, parameter plausibility and clinical interpretability. Whenever possible, the potential dose-response relationship function will be described by a sigmoid Emax model.

Model evaluation

Following final model development, model diagnostic plots, visual predictive check (VPC) plots and the non-parametrical bootstrap method will be employed to evaluate model performance, encompassing goodness of fit, predictive ability and stability. To verify that our model captures between-trial heterogeneity across the observed time course, we will perform VPCs stratified by the irregular but commonly reported assessment time points (weeks 2, 4, 8, 12, 16 and 24).

Systemic treatment response analysis

We will simulate the responses of placebo and various systemic treatments at different levels of covariates by conducting 1000 Monte Carlo simulations based on the final model parameters, and will generate time-course profiles.

We will simulate the typical response and 95% uncertainty interval (UI) for systemic treatments versus placebo and targeted systemic therapies versus broad-acting immunosuppressive therapies using 1000 Monte Carlo simulations based on pharmacodynamic model parameters.

According to the strategy, we will first divide the targeted systemic treatments into targeting adaptive immune response, targeting innate immune response, targeting itch and inhibiting Janus kinases. Then, based on the pathways of highly complex pathophysiology, the type of targeting adaptive immune response will be subdivided into antigen presentation and TH2 cytokines and their receptors. With regard to target of action, type of systemic treatments will be mainly subdivided into OX40-OX40L inhibitor, IL-4Rα inhibitor, IL-13 inhibitor, JAK1 inhibitor, JAK1/2 inhibitor, IL-31RA inhibitor and IL-33 inhibitor. The effectiveness of the above systemic treatment types will also be simulated by using the pharmacodynamic model.

We will conduct subgroup analyses of all potential influencing factors (eg, children vs adults) when they were not included in the covariate model. The variables may include age, proportion of male participants, duration since onset of AD, per cent of body surface area affected, baseline EASI Score, proportion of participants having an IGA Score of 4, mean IGA Score, PP-NRS Score, POEM Score, SCORAD Score, score related to QoL parameters, background TCS therapy, intervention category, application method, study population and year of publication. Subgroup categories for continuous variables will be determined according to clinically meaningful cut-offs where these are well established. In the absence of such thresholds, exploratory categorisation based on the observed data distribution will be applied.

The analysis method for different groups consists of empirical Bayesian post hoc estimation and a meta-analysis with random effects, which has been described in published studies.31 33 The primary quantity for comparison across subgroups is the predicted treatment response trajectory over time, derived from the pharmacodynamic parameters. The analytical procedure consists of two steps: first, individual model parameters and their SEs are obtained via empirical Bayesian post hoc estimation; second, a random-effects meta-analysis is used to summarise the typical parameter values for each subgroup. Based on these summary estimates, we will generate the typical response trajectories using 10 000 Monte Carlo simulations.

Sensitivity analysis

In model evaluation, the robustness of the model will be evaluated by the bootstrap method. We will perform 1000 bootstrap resamples to evaluate model robustness. For each resample, the final model will be refitted and parameter estimates recorded. The bootstrap results will be summarised as the median and 95% CI. Model stability will be confirmed if the original parameter estimates are close to the bootstrap-derived median and fall within the 95% CI. Apart from that, we will estimate model parameters excluding trials not from peer-reviewed publications, including only trials with low risk of bias, and excluding trials of one of the agents, respectively. Sensitivity analyses will also be performed to evaluate the impact of missing data imputation on the parameter estimates.

Analysis software

The model development and bootstrap procedures will be performed by NONMEM, V.7.5 (ICON Development Solutions, USA) with the first-order conditional estimation method. Model simulation, meta-analysis and plotting will be carried out with R software, V.4.3 or higher (R Foundation for Statistical Analysis) and R Studio, V.1.1.453 (Posit Software). Quality assessment of the studies will be performed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool 2 for randomised trials. Microsoft Excel V.2010 (Microsoft) will be used during data extraction and processing.

Updating

There are still numerous new systemic medications under development in clinical trials for AD.35 36 The living network meta-analysis with several major updates, have produced robust and timely evidence for comparisons of systemic treatments.19 26-29 This model-based analysis will be updated following the steps of that living network meta-analysis. If it ceases updates, we will conduct independent systematic searches every 12 months. However, update may be frequent in the two conditions:

Landmark clinical trial results of a new systemic medication have been published.

2.Results of new head-to-head studies are inconsistent with the results of our analysis.

SUMMARY

MBMA is quite different from conventional meta-analysis. Previous MBMAs for psoriasis have provided a quantitative framework for efficacy comparisons across relevant therapeutic options for clinicians.22 37 We will conduct the first MBMA that comprehensively evaluates systemic treatments for AD. This study will uncover previously unreported time-course and predictors of treatments effect, and provide a comparison of new and developed therapies in a rigorous manner. This MBMA approach provides valuable information for decision making in clinical practice.

Our analysis relied on published aggregate data rather than individual patient data, which limits our ability to estimate inter‑individual variability and may introduce bias when interpreting covariate effects. A further limitation will be the binary classification of background TCS use. The definition does not capture the substantial clinical heterogeneity in TCS regimens across trials, including frequency, potency, and quantity.
