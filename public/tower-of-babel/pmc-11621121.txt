Effects of surfactin stress on gene expression and pathological changes in Spodoptera litura
Authors: Feiyan Zhang, Qiuyue Liu, Yana Wang, Jialu Yin, Xianghe Meng, Jiangping Wang, Wenya Zhao, Hongwei Liu, Liping Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Spodoptera litura (S. litura) is a polyphagous pest of the family Lepidoptera, which causes damage and yields losses to many crops. The long-term use of chemical pesticides for control not only seriously threatens environmental health, but also causes S. litura to develop drug resistance. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop environmentally safe and friendly biogenic pesticides. However, the mechanism of action of the secondary metabolite (surfactin) of Bacillus Vélezensis (B. vélezensis) on lepidopteran pests (S. litura) has not been reported yet. We found that several metabolites and genes in S. litura were affected by surfactin exposure. The expressions of the metabolites (protoporphyrinogen (PPO), gluconolactone (GDL), and L-cysteate) were significantly down-regulated while glutamate and hydroxychloroquine were significantly up-regulated. The expression levels of genes related to drug metabolism and detoxification, include the glutathione s-transferase (GST) gene family and acetaldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH), and apoptosis-inhibiting genes (seven in absentia homolog 1(SIAH1)) were significantly decreased. In addition, pathological changes occurred in intestinal wall cells, Malpighian tubule cells, and nerve cells of S. litura under surfactin stress. Conclusively, our results suggest that surfactin induces an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and damages S. litura cells. Furthermore, based on the integrated analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic data, it is hypothesized that surfactin may also trigger neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity in S. litura while hindering the insect’s detoxification processes. This study lays a foundation for further exploration of surfactin as a potential biopesticide.

Introduction

S. liturais a globally distributed, polyphagous insect pest1. S. lituracan damage more than 389 plant species, including plants from 109 families. Host plants range from ferns and gymnosperms to dicots and monocots2. S. liturahas 4–5 generations per year in North China and 6–9 generations yearly in South China, with a large overlap of generations and no obvious diapause2. Females can lay about 1000–2000 eggs in their lifetime. Under favorable environmental conditions, S. liturapopulations can increase rapidly, which can lead to outbreaks3. There has recently been an increase in the frequency and area of S. litura outbreaks. S. lituraposes a threat to large-scale crops such as tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, watermelons, bitter gourds, cowpeas, and cabbage and has a serious impact on crop yield and quality4.

The primary control method for S. liturais by the use of chemical pesticides. However, the excessive and instandard use of these pesticides has led to pesticide resistance. Pesticide resistance contributes to the increasing rampant of this pest and is a key factor affecting pesticide effectiveness5. S. litura populations in China have developed resistance to many insecticides. These include Bacillus thuringiensis (BT) and avermectin, with resistance multiples of 196.15 and 183.28, respectively. High levels of resistance were also observed for chlorpyrifos, cypermethrin, and indoxacarb, with resistance multiples of 114.89, 85.88, and 73.20, respectively. Moderate levels of resistance were also found for ethyl spinosad, with a resistance multiple of 10.566,7. The resistance ratios for metaflumizone and emamectin benzoate were 6.95 and 6.21, respectively. The sensitivity to chlorfenapyr and methoxyfenozide has decreased, with resistance multiples of 4.96 and 4.37, respectively. S. liturahas now developed high levels of resistance to most insecticides6,7. In addition to the insecticide resistance of S. litura, long-term use of chemical pesticides can pollute the environment and pose a threat to human food safety. Therefore, the development of new bio-insecticides is important for the safety of foods, humans and other non-target species, and the environment.

Surfactin is a surface-active microbial-derived metabolite produced by some species of Bacillus. It is a cyclic lipopeptide with both hydrophilic and lipophilic properties. It is highly effective against pathogenic bacteria, and has low non-target toxicity and a low environmental risk. Surfactin has an inhibitory effect on common pathogenic bacteria found in food, medical facilities, and livestock and poultry. The minimum inhibitory concentration for Listeria monocytogenesATCC 19,115 is 4 μg/mL. Surfactin did not show hemolysis to mouse erythrocytes within the effective inhibitory concentration range. Surfactin also has good thermal stability (20–100 °C) and wide pH (2.0–10.0) adaptability8. Qi et al. (2010) found that surfactin produced by Bacillus amyloliquefaciens WH1 can inhibit the growth of Candida albicans and Rhizoctonia solani, based on the induction of cell membrane perforation at high concentrations and the induction of apoptosis at low concentrations9. Vollenbroich et al. (1997) found that the surfactin at a 25 μM, it caused the rupture of the particles and the mycoplasma in the cell membrane capsule, and the gap began to decompose. When the concentration was 50 μM, the phospholipid bilayer was completely disrupted, leading to the rupture of all microbial cell structures10. Long et al. (2017) found that surfactin at the concentration of 15 mg/L can reduce the surface tension of water from to 27 mN/m. Therefore, at a certain concentration, surfactin can invade the capsules of bacteria. This invasion can cause the disintegration of the lipids of the outer membrane layer of organisms, such as viruses, bacteria, and fungi, and kill them11. Ghribi et al. (2023) found that a lipopeptide biosurfactant that product from a Bacillus subtilis strain SPB1, has a insecticidal activity on the Egyptian cotton leaf worm (Spodoptera littoralis)12 The histopathological changes occurred in the larval midgut of S. littoralis treated with it were vesicle formation in the apical region, cellular vacuolization and destruction of epithelial cells and their boundaries. Xia et al. (2024) by successfully constructing the surfactin deletion mutant YZ-14srfAA, verified that the surfactin produced by B. subtilis YZ-1 has insecticidal activity against T. molitor, Plutella xylostella and Achelura yunnanensis13. Assié et al. (2002) found that surfactins with long fatty acid chain (C and Crs) showed insecticide effect on the fruit fly D. melanogaster14.

Our laboratory isolated surfactin from the metabolite of B. vélezensis and found that it has a insecticidal activity on S. litura, and the insecticidal mechanism has not been reported. We studied the reasons why surfactin kills S. litura through metabolomic and transcriptomic methods. The killing method of surfactin against S. litura provides a theoretical basis for the development of surfactin as a new biopesticide.

Materials and methods

Insects

Third-instar larvae (3rd larvae) of S. litura were maintained in an incubator (the temperature is 26–27 °C, the relative humidity is 50–60%, the light-night ratio is 14:10, and the stocking density is 1/1.5–2.0 cm2). The S. lituras are purchased from Henan Jiyuan Baiyun Industry Co., Ltd (Henan, China).

Ethics statement

The animal subjects used in the present study are S. lituras, which are arthropoda and are exempt from this requirement.

Chemical reagents and equipment

All chemicals and solvents were analytical or HPLC grade. Methanol, diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC) water, purchased from CNW Technologies GmbH (Düsseldorf, German). Multi-sample cryogenic grinder (Model wonbio-E) was purchased from Shanghai Wanbai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The ultra-low temperature refrigerators are purchased from the Iceberg Pine Ocean Biotechnology Dalian Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). (Beijing, China).

Preparation of surfactin solution

Amberl XAD7HP macroporous resin was added to the sterile supernatant after centrifugation of B. vélezensisZLP-101 fermentation broth and shaken at 4 °C for 12 h. The resin was washed three times with distilled water and then washed with 60% ethanol. The eluent was concentrated by rotary evaporator to obtain the crude extract. The crude extract was loaded onto WondaSil C18 column (4.6 mm × 150 mm) with a particle size of 5 μm by semi-preparative liquid chromatography, and eluted with 10% ~ 90% acetonitrile as a linear gradient. The flow rate was 1 mL/min and the detection wavelength was 214 nm, the column temperature is 30℃.Then, the components with insecticidal activity were identified by mass spectrometry and stored in freeze-dry.The active components were identified by AB Triple TOF 5600 mass spectrometry system to be mainly C14-C15 surfactin homologuesABC15. The collected surfactin was concentrated by rotary evaporator and then freeze-dried to obtain a pure surfactin, which was then prepared into a surfactin solution of 120mg/L with water.

Design of the surfactin exposure experiment

Surfactin exposure assays were performed on 3rd larvae of S. lituras that were free of pesticides at the laboratory of the Institute of Biology, Hebei Academy of Sciences (China). The feeding conditions were presented in Fig. 1. 300 3rd larvae of S. lituras were allocated to two groups (n = 150/group), and set up three independent biological replicates (n = 50/replicate). In our study, to find the LC50 concentration of surfactin was 119.25mg/L. Therefore, one group was labelled as the experimental group (SUR group), and spray 120 mg/L (closest to LC50) surfactin solution. The other group was labelled as the control group (CK group), and spray water. The spraying dose is 250 mg/kg. Metabolomics

When the mortality rate of S. litura reached 50% under surfactin exposure, two groups of S. litura samples were collected. To carry out widely targeted metabolomics, we utilized eight separate biological replicates, each consisting of an entire S. litura specimen. For both the control group (CK) and the treatment group (SUR), one S. litura specimen was ground into a powder and thoroughly mixed with 1000 µL of a cold methanol/water solution (80% v/v). After being placed on ice for 15 min, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at a temperature of 4 C. The supernatant was collected for liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis.

The samples were detected by both positive and negative ion modes, and electro spray ionization (ESI) was used. The samples were separated by UHPLC and then analyzed by Tiple-TOF 5600. The ESI process settings of the sprayer were as follows: source gas 1(GAS1) : 40, gas curtain gas (CUR) : 30, ion source temperature: 500℃, ion source floating voltage (ISVF): + 5500 V for positive mode and −4500 V for negative mode, TOF MS scan m/z range: 25–1250 Da, production scan m/Z range: 25–1250 Da, TOF MS scan accumulation time 0.15 s/spectra, production scan accumulation time 0.03 S/spectra. The secondary mass spectrometry process was set to: information dependent acquistion (IDA), high sensitivity mode, deceustering poetal (DP): 80V (positive and negative ion mode), collision energy: 40V. The QCs were injected at regular intervals (every 8 samples) throughout the analytical run to provide a set of data from which repeatability can be assessed.

The MS data was analyzed using Metaboanalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca)16. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was carried out using MetaboAnalystR in the R package. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on a covariance matrix using the base package in R. The differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were identified based on FC > 1.5, P< 0.05, and variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1. Additionally, range scaling was used to normalise the abundance of some DEMs17. The KEGG database was used to annotate the DEMs. The P-value obtained from the KEGG enrichment analysis was calculated and corrected by hypergeometric tests and Benjamini–Hochberg tests, respectively.

Transcriptomics

Transcriptomics was performed with four independent biological replicates of surfactin-treated S. lituras, with untreated S. litura used as controls. A pool of one whole S. litura was included in each replicate. Total RNA was isolated using the Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies), after which the concentration, quality and integrity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Three micrograms of RNA were used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated according to the following steps. Firstly, mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations under elevated temperature in an Illumina proprietary fragmentation buffer. First strand cDNA was synthesized using random oligonucleotides and Super Script II. Second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Remaining overhangs were converted into blunt ends via exonuclease/polymerase activities and the enzymes were removed. After adenylation of the 3′ ends of the DNA fragments, Illumina PE adapter oligonucleotides were ligated to prepare for hybridization. To select cDNA fragments of the preferred 400–500 bp in length, the library fragments were purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter,Beverly, CA, USA). DNA fragments with ligated adaptor molecules on both ends were selectively enriched using Illumina PCR Primer Cocktail in a 15 cycle PCR reaction. Products were purified (AMPure XP system) and quantified using the Agilent high sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). The sequencing library was then sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Cp. Ltd.

RT-qPCR

First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using RNA with HiScript® II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (TaKaRa, China) as described by the manufacturer. The individual RT-qPCR (25 µL) contained 9.5 µL of sterile double-distilled water, 12.5 µL of SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, China), 2 µL of cDNA and 0.5 µL of each primer (10 µM). The sequences of the primers used in the RT-qPCR assay are provided in Table S25. A CFX96 Real-Time Detection System (BioRad, USA) was used to analyse the samples. At least three independent biological replicates were included in all RT-qPCR experiments. GPADH was used as a housekeeping gene to normalise the expression of other genes. Alterations in the mRNA abundance of each gene were analysed according to the 2-ΔΔCT method.

Integrative analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic data

In our experiment, transcriptome analyse were performed using four independent biological replicates, and metabolome analysis was carried out using eight independent biological replicates. Integrative transcriptome and metabolome analyses needed to be performed using the same number of biological replicates. We thus used the data from samples from which all transcriptome, and metabolome analyses were carried out to perform integrative analysis. The correlation between key differentially expressed metabolites (DEGs) and DEMs was assessed using the criteria of Pearson correlation coefficients (r > 0.6 and P < 0.05).

Quantification and statistical analysis

The statistical significance of differences between two groups was analysed using T-tests, with a significance level set at P < 0.05 (*).

Results

Surfactin exposure increases the mortality of S. litura

To explore the potential of surfactin to develop bioinsecticides, the contact killing effects of surfactin and other commercially available chemical insecticides against 3rd larvae of S. lituras were initially compared. A total of 210 3rd larvae of S. lituras were collected and divided into seven groups (n = 30/group). Three independent biological replicates were set up (n = 10/replicate). The survival rate of S. lituras within 24 h was recorded. The larvae were touched with the tip of a brush. If the larva did not move, it was considered dead. When 250 mg/L of surfactin was sprayed on the S. lituras, the mortality rate reached 100% within 24 h. When 125 mg/L of surfactin was sprayed, the mortality rate reached 56.67%. When 62.5 mg/L of surfactin was sprayed, the mortality rate was 30%. When 31.25 mg/L of surfactin was sprayed, the mortality rate was 10%. Using the linear regression model (R2 = 0.988) method, the LC50 of surfactin was calculated to be 119.25 mg/L, and the LC100 was 242.81 mg/L. In contrast, the neonicotinoid insecticide clothianidin has an LC100 concentration closest to 800 mg/L and an LC50 concentration closest to 600 mg/L. The 24-h LC50 concentration of avermectin was closest to 400 mg/L, while the 24-h LC50 concentration of pyrethrin was 450 mg/L (Table 1). All spray doses are 250 mg/kg. These findings suggest that surfactin shows potential as a biopesticide. In our study, it was found that the LC50 concentration of surfactin was closest to 120 mg/L, and the mortality of S. litura was closest to 50% after being exposed to surfactin for 6 h. Metabolite alterations of S. litura by surfactin exposure

LC–MS/MS was utilized to detect alterations in metabolites of S. litura in the CK group and SUR group. Total ion chromatograms (TIC) of each group of samples in positive ion ESI ( +) (Fig. S1A) mode and each group of samples in negative ion ESI (-) (Fig. S1B) mode indicated that the instrument analysis of all samples has strong signal strength, large peak capacity and good reproducibility of retention time. The XCMS plus software was employed to extract ion peaks of metabolites, resulting in the detection of 15,880 mass spectrum characteristics. Out of these, 8,110 metabolites were successfully identified. Furthermore, the OPLS-DA analysis indicated that all samples fell within a 95% confidence interval, as in ESI ( +) (Fig. S1C) and ESI (-) (Fig. S1D). The OPLS-DA model showed a significant difference between the two groups (X-axis direction). All of the above results showed that the metabolites of S. litura in SUR group have changed obviously comparing to CK group.

We conclude that surfactin affected many important pathways in S. litura.The path enriched by DEGs in KEGG shows that, SUR group significantly decreased the gene expression levels in drug metabolism (glutathione s-transferase (GST), hematopoietic prostaglandin D synthase (HPGDS), glutathione S-transferase 1 kappa (GSTK1), Isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH1)), Vitamin Metabolism (retinol dehydrogenase (RDH), UDP-glycosyltransferase (UGT), FABF, aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH)), glucose metabolism (long-chain triglycerides (LCT), aldose reductase(AKR1B), UGT), amino acid metabolism (ALDH) and genetic information processing (seven in absentia homolog 1(SIAH1), WNT2, WNT5) compared with those in CK group. However, the expression levels of genes involved in fatty acid metabolism (hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase (HMGCS), mevalonate kinase (MVK), farnesyl diphosphate synthase (FDPS), farnesol dehydrogenase (FOHSDR), decaprenyl diphosphate synthase subunit 1 (PDSS1), fatty acid synthase(FASN)), glucose metabolism (protein O-mannosyltransferase 1 (POMT), high-molecular-weight kininogen (HK)), longevity regulation (Insulin receptor (INSR), heat-shock proteins (HSPs), Naga), genetic information processing (mannosidase 1A (MAN1A), alphaB-Crystallin (CRYAB), carbonyl reductase 1 (CBR1), endoplasmic reticulum oxidoreductase 1L (ERO1L), HSPA1s) were significantly increased. Additionally, the expression levels of environmental information processing (protein-tyrosine phosphatase, nonreceptor type 11 (PTPN11), docking protein 2 (DOK2), laminin gamma 1 (Lamc1), Integrin beta 4 (ITGB4), DUOX) were also significantly increased.

After 6 h of surfactin stress, we measured the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and observed morphological changes in the cells. Our findings revealed that the cells exposed to surfactin exhibited significantly higher levels of ROS compared to the CK group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7A, Fig. 7B, Fig. 7K, Table S27). Cytological analysis showed that the ventral nerve cords in the CK group displayed a well-organized and regularly shaped structure, with even staining (Fig. 7C). Discussion

Chemical pesticides have historically played a key role in reducing pest populations and increasing crop yields. We found that exposure to the secondary metabolite (surfactin) from Bacillus velezensis increased S. litura mortality. We also determined the mode of action of surfactin on S. litura at the metabolome and transcriptome levels.

The S. litura cells were damaged by surfactin exposure and increased the risk of neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity in S. litura

The study results show that many metabolites and metabolic pathways in S. liturathat were affected by surfactin exposure. PPO is a key enzyme in the heme synthesis pathway in some organisms18. Reduced PPO activity in the S. liturawill lead to a large accumulation of protoporphyrinogen IX18. Protoporphyrin IX can stimulate the production of high ROS, which can destroy important macromolecular structures such as lipids and proteins and cause cell death18. The results of this study show that the protoporphyrinogen (PPO) of the porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism pathways was significantly down-regulated. This might be the crucial reason for cell damage in S. litura. This is similar to the research findings of many scholars. Wang et al. (2022) showed that ROS are highly reactive and that appropriate levels of ROS are required for cell proliferation, signal transduction, and apoptosis19. In a study of ROS in leukemia, Romo-González et al. (2022) showed that reactive oxygen species (ROS) are toxic by-products of aerobic metabolism and are also regulators of cell signaling20. The self-renewal ability of hematopoietic stem cells (hscs) is protected when ROS is maintained at low levels and inhibited when ROS is activated to maintain high levels of hematopoietic stem cells (hscs) renewal and proliferation20. Ling et al. (2022) showed that some secondary metabolites can influence complex cellular signaling pathways by upregulating ROS levels, leading to cytotoxicity (apoptosis, autophagy, and necrosis) and cell death21. Our paraffin sections and frozen sections results also confirmed that surfactin stress can aggravate oxidative stress and destroy the structure of S. litura cells (Fig. 7). In the pentose phosphate pathway, the expression level of GDL was significantly down-regulated. It may have a toxic effect on the heart of Spodoptera litura and influence the heart function of S. litura. This is similar to the research results of many scholars. Qin et al. (2022) study showed that GDL is a free radical scavenger, which can induce myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury in mice and hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/r) in neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMS)22. Intraperitoneal injection of 5 mg/kg of GDL reduced myocardial I/R injury and reduced both reperfusion-induced arrhythmias and oxidative stress. GDL can resist arrhythmias and oxidative stress, and it can also activate the Erk signaling pathway and provide cardioprotection23. The expression level of GDL was significantly down-regulated, suggesting that surfactin has a harmful effect on the heart of S. litura. In the taurine and hypotaurine metabolism pathway, the expression of L-cysteate was significantly down-regulated. Bansal et al. (2018) showed that cysteine is an antioxidant with multiple functions, most of which maintain cellular redox homeostasis and are involved in detoxification23. Kang et al. (2019) also showed that L-cysteate can block toxin-induced ROS production and thus attenuate toxin-induced apoptosis and inflammation24. Across the board, the expression levels of PPO, GDL, and L-cysteate in SUR group were significantly lower than those in the CK group, indicating that surfactin could induce an increase in ROS, which would lead to cell damage by decreasing the activities of PPO, GDL, and L-cysteate. This reduces the functions of antioxidation and detoxification and ultimately reducing insect survival rates.

In addition, among the (DEMs) with up-regulated expression in the SUR group, phospholipids such as phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidic acid (PA), and phosphatidylserine (PS) were the most diverse when compared with the CK group. These phospholipid metabolites are key components of the cellular lipid bilayer25. Among these, PC is an important membrane component involved in immune regulation and a repair agent for cell membranes, repairing damaged cells, restoring tissues and organs function and enzyme activity, regulating energy metabolism, and promoting tissue regeneration26. PE is a major component of cell membranes. It is a non-bilayer phospholipid that contributes to organelle shape and function and is involved in many membrane functions27–29. PA is an intracellular messenger that regulates several cellular processes. It helps balance body metabolism and physiological functions according to changing growth conditions30. The phagocytosis mediated by PS is key to the removal of apoptotic cells during development, tissue homeostasis, and infection. Although these phospholipids perform different functions in cells, they represent an important class of messenger molecules in cellular and physiological processes and play crucial roles in signaling pathways. They contribute to the regulation of stress responses by binding to target proteins and modulating their activity31. The expression levels of PC, PE, PA, and PS of SUR changed significantly, which indicated that the cells of S. litura undergo a stress response under the stress of surfactin. S. litura resists the surfactin exposure by increasing the expression level of phospholipid molecules that make up the cell membrane.

The results of this study show that glutamate and pantothenic acid were metabolites with significantly up-regulated expression levels. This indicates that surfactin affects the nerve cell function of S. litura. This is consistent with the research conclusions of many scholars. Iovino et al. (2020) showed that glutamate is the main excitatory neurotransmitter in the nervous system, but that glutamate-induced excitotoxicity occurs when it is exposed to mechanical, chemical, and toxic substances32. Glutamate appears to be released in large quantities and accumulates in the synaptic space, causing glutamate receptor hyperactivation and leading to cell death of neuronal or other tissues32. Riche et al. (2022) found that glutamate excitotoxicity occurs when neurons are overstimulated by the neurotransmitter glutamate, which causes multiple forms of neuronal cell death33. Examples include apoptosis, mitochondrial permeability transition, phagocyte death, lysosomal cell death, and ROS-induced death33. In addition, the expression level of hydroxychloroquine was also significantly increased. Bansal et al. (2020) showed that hydroxychloroquine had immunomodulatory and antiviral effects34. However, Martinez et al. (2020) showed that hydroxychloroquine can also disrupt lysosomes, inhibit ion channel activation and protein expression, reduce proinflammatory cytokine, and induce apoptosis35. Stremmel et al. (2020) also found that the side effects of hydroxychloroquine can lead to cardiac death due to severe arrhythmias36. Across the board, the expression levels of glutamate and hydroxychloroquine in SUR group were significantly higher than those in CK group, indicating that surfactin can increases the risk of neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity in S. litura.

The detoxification function of S. litura was affected by surfactin exposure

The transcriptome results of S. lituraindicated that surfactin elicited a wide-ranging genetic response. The majority of DEGs were associated with drug metabolism and detoxification metabolism. For example, GST, GSTP, HPGDS, GSTK1, ALDH, ALDH7A1, and ALDH9A1 are all related to detoxification metabolism37–39. It showed that the detoxification function of S. liturawas affected by surfactin exposure. The functions of these genes are similar to those found in other studies. Zhang et al. (2020) showed that GST is an important metabolic enzyme involved in the biotransformation of the body. It is the main detoxification system of cell anti-injury and anti-cancer and may be involved in the detoxification of chlorimuron-ethyl40. Zhang et al. (2023) showed that GST is involved in the detoxification of aflatoxins B1 (AFB1) in the primary hepatocytes of ducks41. Liu et al. (2022) showed that GSTK1 can combat oxidative stress to minimize liver damage42. Xin et al. (2010) demonstrated that GST exerts its antioxidant effects by repairing membrane phospholipid damage induced by free radicals and inhibiting microsomal peroxidation43. Wang et al. (2020) showed that ALDH is a key enzyme for acetaldehyde metabolism in the liver44. Munukutla et al. (2019) found that ALDH detoxifies aldehydes in the body and reduces heart damage45. Andrejeva et al. (2018) showed that ALDH7A1 expression is a prognostic marker in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma and that a low expression level is correlated with decreased survival in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma46. The expression levels of IDH1 and SIAH1, two genes related to insect survival, also decreased significantly in S. litura. The decreased expression of IDH1 suggests extensive necrosis of hepatocytes, although this result has not been reported. SIAH1 has the function of inhibiting apoptosis, and its decreased expression may indirectly accelerate the apoptosis of cells47..

In addition, the expression levels of the genes HMGCS, FDPS, CRYAB, and CBR1 were significantly increased. HMGCS is one key enzyme controlling the rate of cholesterol synthesis48. FDPS can assist in the biosynthesis of cholesterol49, which is most abundant in the brain and nerve tissue. CRYAB reduces cellular inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress50, and CBR1 provides neuroprotection from brain injury by reducing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation51. The expression levels of these genes were significantly increased, suggesting that S. litura increased the functions of brain or neural tissue protection, anti-oxidation, and repair of damaged cells under surfactin stress. Across the board, transcriptome analysis showed that surfactin could decrease detoxification and induce cell death of S. litura by down-regulating the expression levels of genes involved in detoxification, drug metabolism, and inhibiting apoptosis. These changes increased larval mortality. S. litura resisted surfactin stress by enhancing the repair of damaged cells, reducing oxidative stress, and increasing the expression of central nervous system genes. The results of transcriptomics are consistent with those of metabolomics.

Combined metabolome and transcriptome analysis

To determine the mechanism of surfactin on S. litura, the metabolome and transcriptome data of S. litura were analyzed. There was a strong correlation between DEM and DEG in both groups (P < 0.05). For example, the metabolite PPO was positively correlated with ALDH (P = 0.007). The lower the activity of PPO, the lower the expression of ALDH, so we inferred the weaker the detoxification function. The joint analysis of the metabolome and transcriptome once again demonstrated that surfactin can induce an increase in ROS and damage the cells of S.litura. It causes neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity in S.litura and inhibits its detoxification function.

Surfactin enhances intracellular oxidative stress, inhibits antioxidant function, and increases S. litura mortality

The metabolomics results showed that the expression levels of PPO and GDL in SUR group were significantly lower than those in the CK group. Decreased PPO activity can lead to the production of highly reactive oxygen species (ROS) in cells18. The GDL is a free radical scavenger against oxidative stress22. The expression levels of both DEMs were significantly decreased, indicating that surfactin can induce higher levels of ROS and lead to cell injury or death by decreasing the activities of PPO and GDL. The transcriptomic results showed that the SUR group had a significantly lower glutathione of glutathione s-transferases (GST) than CK group. GST is one of the antioxidant enzymes, and antioxidation can be achieved by repairing membrane phospholipid damage caused by free radicals and inhibiting microsomal peroxidation43. The expression level of GST was significantly decreased, which indicated that the surfactin inhibited GST activity and the level of anti-oxidation. It then destroyed the somatic cells and led to larval mortality. PPO was positively correlated with GDL (P = 0.139) and GST (P = 0.004). The metabonomics results were consistent with those of transcriptomics and sections of larval tissues and cells. This indicated that surfactin can increase oxidative stress and inhibit antioxidant function, leading to larval mortality.

Surfactin can cause neurotoxicity in S. litura

The metabolomics results showed that the expression level of glutamate in the SUR group treatment was significantly higher than that in CK group. Excessive glutamate induces excitotoxicity in S. litura, which kills the neuronal cells due to strong oxidative stress32,33. Surfactin appears to induce excitotoxicity by enhancing glutamate activity. The transcriptomic results showed that the expression levels of FDPS, CBR1, and CRYAB in SUR group were significantly higher than those in the CK group. FDPS is involved in cholesterol synthesis in the brain49. CBR1 provides neuroprotection from brain injury by reducing oxidative stress51. CRYAB reduces apoptosis and oxidative stress50. Glutamate was positively correlated with FDPS (P = 0.030) and CRYAB (P = 0.010).

The HE staining results showed damage to neuron morphology under the stress of surfactin (Fig. 7). The combination of histopathology analysis (HE staining) with the metabolomics and transcriptomics results showed that the surfactin could destroy S. litura nerve cells.

Surfactin can inhibit the detoxification function

The metabolomics results showed that the expression level of L-cysteate in SUR group was significantly lower than that in CK group. It is involved in a variety of redox functions in the body, has protective effects on detoxification cells, and has a variety of detoxification effects23. The expression level of L-cysteate was significantly decreased, suggesting that the surfactin interfered with the detoxification function. The transcriptomic results showed that the expression of ANPEP, a gene related to detoxification function in humans, was also significantly decreased. The combined analysis of metabolomics and transcriptomics showed that surfactin could inhibit the detoxification functions of S. litura.

Surfactin can cause adverse effects on the heart of S. litura

The metabolomics results showed that the expression of hydroxychloroquine was significantly increased and the expression of GDL was significantly decreased under surfactin stress compared with the CK group. Hydroxychloroquine is a common agent causing human cardiomyopathy33. As a potent activator of PKC ε, GDL can provide cardioprotection by activating ERK signaling22. The up-regulation of hydroxychloroquine and the down-regulation of GDL suggest that surfactin can induce cardiotoxicity and decrease cardioprotection. The transcriptomic results showed that the expression level of the gene ALDH was significantly decreased in treated larvae compared with controls. ALDH detoxifies harmful substances in the body and alleviates heart damage45. There was a significant negative correlation between hydroxychloroquine and ALDH (P = 0.028), and a significant positive correlation between GDL and ALDH (P = 0.004). The combined analysis of metabolomics and transcriptomics showed that surfactin can induce cardiotoxicity and decrease the protective function of the heart.

In addition, Wang et al. (2022) showed that surfactin can trigger ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction leading to programmed apoptosis52. Danevčič et al. (2021) showed that surfactin can promote cell lysis and DNA release, but the specific mechanism was unclear53. Xiang et al. (2021) showed that oxidative stress can cause apoptosis, autophagy, and inflammation, leading to cell damage and tissue dysfunction54. Vo et al. (2020) showed that surfactin can induce cellular mitochondrial ROS production with JNK1/2 activation, leading to oral squamous cell (OSCC) apoptosis55.

Conclusions

Based on the integrated analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic data, indicate that surfactin is toxic to S. litura. It can influence the metabolite and gene expression of S.litura. Its mode of action involves inducing an increase in ROS in somatic cells, leading to cell damage, inducing neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity in S. litura, and inhibiting detoxification of the insect.
