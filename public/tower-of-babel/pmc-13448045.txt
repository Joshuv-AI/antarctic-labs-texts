Spinopelvic Parameters, Degree of Correction, and Proximal Junctional Complications After Adult Spinal Deformity Surgery: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: HyungSub Jin, Kyung-Soo Suk, Byung Ho Lee, Si Young Park, Hak-Sun Kim, Seong-Hwan Moon, Sub-Ri Park, Namhoo Kim, Jae Won Shin, Ji-Won Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveProximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) is a frequent postoperative complication following adult spinal deformity (ASD) surgery, whereas proximal junctional failure (PJF) is a less common but clinically more severe complication. Spinopelvic radiographic parameters have been associated with the development of these complications, yet existing studies have reported inconsistent results. This study aimed to investigate the association between spinopelvic parameters and proximal junctional complications (PJK/PJF), including analysis based on age-adjusted pelvic incidence (PI)–lumbar lordosis (LL) mismatch categories. MethodsThis review was conducted according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) 2020 guidelines. PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Embase were comprehensively searched from inception through March 2025. Adults (>18 years) who underwent surgery for ASD or long-segment (≥4 levels) spinal fusion were included. Studies were independently selected by 2 reviewers based on preset criteria. Random-effects model was used to calculate mean differences/standardized mean differences for continuous variables and odds ratios (ORs) for categorical outcomes. ResultsA total of 47 retrospective studies were included in the meta-analysis. Preoperatively, the PJK group had lower LL and sacral slope (SS) but had higher pelvic tilt (PT), T1PA, and C7-sagittal vertical axis than the non-PJK group. Postoperatively, the PJK group showed significantly lower PI–LL mismatch values and SS and higher PT, thoracic kyphosis, thoracolumbar kyphosis, and T1 pelvic angle. When stratified by the age-adjusted PI–LL formula, overcorrection was associated with significantly higher odds of PJK (OR, 2.67; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.61–4.42), whereas no significant association was observed for PJF. Using age-specific thresholds, overcorrection was significantly associated with higher odds of PJF (OR, 1.67; 95% CI, 1.10–2.52). ConclusionPreoperative sagittal imbalance and postoperative spinopelvic differences were associated with proximal junctional complications, whereas associations for PJF were more limited. Age-adjusted overcorrection showed outcome-specific associations with proximal junctional complications, suggesting that age-tailored alignment may be clinically relevant. Given the retrospective aggregate evidence and limited age-adjusted data, future prospective studies with standardized radiographic timing and separate outcome assessment for PJK and PJF are needed to validate these findings.Keywords: Proximal junctional kyphosis, Proximal junctional failure, Adult spinal deformity, Spinopelvic parameters, Degree of correction

INTRODUCTION

Adult spinal deformity (ASD) encompasses a range of conditions characterized by abnormal alignment of the thoracolumbar spine, predominantly arising during aging [1]. Surgical treatment provides superior outcomes compared to nonoperative care with respect to pain relief and functional improvement [2,3]. Correspondingly, there has been a rise in surgical cases, complexity of procedures, and hospital admissions [4,5]. Although surgical correction offers advantages over nonoperative care, it carries substantial risks of complications and morbidity, particularly in elderly patients [6]. Proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) and proximal junctional failure (PJF) are among the most notable complications, especially after long-segment spinal fusion. PJK and PJF are reported in approximately 20%–40% [7-9] and 2%–18% [10-12] of patients, respectively.

PJK and PJF are part of a pathological spectrum involving the proximal junction after ASD correction surgery, with PJF considered a severe form of PJK with symptomatic presentation [8,11,13]. The risk factors of proximal junctional complications are multifactorial and include old age, osteoporosis, surgical techniques, and devices [14]. Spinopelvic parameters associated with sagittal alignment are also known to be related to PJK and PJF occurrence. Therefore, achieving appropriate sagittal alignment is a critical surgical goal to minimize junctional complications [9,15]. Malalignment in spinopelvic parameters, particularly pelvic incidence–lumbar lordosis (PI–LL) mismatch, has been highlighted as a key modifiable risk factor for junctional complications [16].

However, the optimal correction target remains debated. The parameter to be prioritized and the most appropriate correction target (undercorrection, matched correction, or overcorrection) are still unclear [8,17]. Existing evidence is conflicting. For example, while some studies have suggested that overcorrection leads to favorable surgical outcomes without increasing the prevalence of PJK [18], others have reported that overcorrection may actually predispose patients to higher PJK rates [19]. Thus, more individualized alignment goals are needed.

The concept of age-related sagittal balance has recently gained increasing attention [20-22]. Age itself is a risk factor for proximal junctional complications [23]. As degenerative deformities of the spine progress, compensatory mechanisms such as pelvis retroversion and knee flexion help maintain global balance [24]. Consequently, efforts have been made to propose age-adjusted correction targets. Some studies have suggested reference values stratified by age categories [20,21]. Others have introduced formulas to calculate an ideal value and by calculating offset [20,22,25]. categorizing patients into overcorrection, matched, or undercorrection groups. In contrast to earlier concepts that proposed a uniform target of PI–LL mismatch regardless of age, these approaches acknowledge the influence of age on sagittal balance and propose more individualized targets.

This meta-analysis aimed to clarify the relationship between spinopelvic radiographic parameters and the risk of proximal junctional complications (PJK and PJF). Specifically, we synthesized evidence by comparing spinopelvic parameter values between patients with and without PJK or PJF and by assessing complication rates across degree of correction groups, defined according to age-adjusted PI–LL mismatch.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Search Strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) 2020 guidelines [26]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251107159). PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Embase were comprehensively searched to identify studies published from inception through March 2025. To ensure broad coverage and minimize the risk of omitting relevant studies in this relatively limited research field, the search strategy deliberately employed wide-ranging keywords, including “proximal junctional kyphosis,” “proximal junctional failure,” and “spine disease”. The full search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Methods. Eligible studies were restricted to full-text articles published in nonpredatory journals and written in English, without limitations on study design. Studies published in journals identified as potentially predatory according to Beall’s list were excluded. This assessment was performed independently by 2 reviewers during full-text screening, and any disagreements were resolved by consensus.

2. Study Selection

Two reviewers independently screened studies according to predefined eligibility criteria. Studies enrolling adult patients (>18 years) who underwent surgery for ASD or long-segment (≥4 levels) fusion were evaluated. Among these, those that stratified patients into PJK versus non-PJK, PJF versus non-PJF, or combined PJK/PJF versus non-PJK/PJF groups and reported spinopelvic parameter and sagittal alignment measures values for each group were eligible. Specifically, studies were included if they evaluated the following spinopelvic parameters preoperatively and/or postoperatively: PI, LL, PI–LL mismatch, pelvic tilt (PT), sacral slope (SS), thoracic kyphosis (TK), thoracolumbar kyphosis (TLK), T1 pelvic angle (T1PA), C7-sagittal vertical axis (C7-SVA), and lower lumbar lordosis (LLL).

In addition, studies that stratified patients according to ageadjusted PI–LL mismatch (overcorrection, matched, or undercorrection groups) and reported the corresponding rates of PJK and/or PJF in each subgroup were eligible. Both approaches to defining age-adjusted PI–LL mismatch targets were considered. The first approach applied the formula age-adjusted target PI– LL=(age–55)/2+3. For each patient, the offset was calculated as (age-adjusted target PI–LL)–(postoperative PI–LL). Patients were then categorized into the undercorrection group when the offset was less than -10°; matched correction, offset between -10° and +10°; and overcorrection, offset exceeding +10° [20,25]. The second approach adopted the age-specific alignment reference values proposed by Lafage et al. [20,21] that defined normative PI–LL mismatch ranges for distinct age categories: younger than 35 years: >-10.5°; 35–44 years: >-4.6°; 45–54 years: >0.5°; 55–64 years: >5.8°; 65–74 years: >10.5°; and >74 years: >17°). According to this classification, patients with PI–LL mismatch values below the normative threshold for their respective age group were categorized into the overcorrected group, whereas those with values greater than or equal to the normative threshold were categorized into the aligned group.

Studies that included patients without surgical intervention were excluded. Studies limited to specific populations such as those with Scheuermann kyphosis, early-onset scoliosis, adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, or congenital scoliosis were also excluded. Furthermore, articles not published in English or those appearing in predatory journals identified by Beall’s list were excluded [27]. Finally, studies that did not report the above outcomes of interest were also excluded. Any discrepancies between the 2 reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

3. Data Extraction

The extracted variables included the first author, publication year, journal, study design, patient diagnosis, surgical procedure, definition of PJK or PJF used in the study, methods used to measure spinopelvic parameter, and follow-up duration. For quantitative synthesis, 2 categories of data were extracted. First, in studies that stratified patients into PJK versus non-PJK, PJF versus non-PJF, or combined PJK/PJF versus non-PJK/PJF groups, we extracted the number of participants (n), means, and standard deviations for spinopelvic and sagittal alignment parameters (e.g., PI, LL, PI–LL mismatch, PT, SS, TK, TLK, T1PA, C7-SVA, LLL, C2-SVA, global tilt, and upper LL) based on both preoperative values and the earliest available postoperative measurements reported in each study. Second, in studies that stratified patients according to age-adjusted PI–LL mismatch (overcorrection, matched, or undercorrection), we collected the reported incidence rates of PJK and/or PJF in each subgroup, as well as information on which method of age-adjusted PI–LL mismatch definition was applied (formula vs. age-specific reference tables). To prevent duplication bias when overlapping patient populations were identified across studies within the same category, preference was given to the study with the most comprehensive data or the largest sample size for inclusion in the final analysis.

PI–LL mismatch was standardized as PI minus LL, with LL expressed as a positive value for lumbar lordosis. Negative PI– LL values were retained as valid values and were not converted to absolute values, as they indicate that LL exceeds PI. When studies used different sign conventions, the reported values were reviewed and harmonized to this convention before quantitative synthesis.

4. Study Quality Assessment

All included studies were observational in nature (e.g., retrospective or cohort designs). Hence, their methodological quality was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), a validated tool specifically designed for assessing non-randomized studies in meta-analyses [28]. The NOS examines 3 domains: selection, comparability, and outcome assessment. In line with commonly accepted thresholds, scores of 7–9 were categorized as high quality; 4–6, moderate quality; and 0–3, low quality. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger test when 10 or more studies were available, in accordance with the recommendations by Egger et al. [29]

5. Statistical Analysis

A random-effects model with inverse-variance weighting was employed to account for heterogeneity across the included studies. The between-study variance was estimated using the restricted maximum-likelihood estimator. PJK and PJF are often regarded as conditions within the same pathological spectrum involving the proximal junction. Hence, the meta-analysis was performed using the reported incidence rates of PJK and PJF based on the definitions provided in the original studies, despite variations in those definitions.

For quantitative synthesis, 2 analytical approaches were applied. First, in studies that compared spinopelvic parameter values between groups (PJK vs. non-PJK and PJF vs. non-PJF), mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed to estimate the effect size for both preoperative and postoperative measurements. In addition, TK was variably defined across studies, and standardized mean differences were used to account for this heterogeneity. For consistency, C7-SVA measurements reported in centimeters were converted to millimeters prior to meta-analysis. Subgroup analyses were additionally conducted for postoperative periods explicitly described as within 3 months, early, immediate, or first standing radiographs after surgery. Second, in studies that stratified patients according to age-adjusted PI–LL mismatch, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated to estimate the effect size and compare the incidence of PJK and/or PJF across subgroups. No zero-event cells were present in the final OR analyses; therefore, continuity correction was not required or applied. Formal leave-one-out or influence analyses were not routinely performed across all comparisons. Instead, targeted sensitivity analyses were performed for specific methodological concerns, including exclusion of the study that reported a mixed PJK/PJF endpoint from the PJK analysis.

Heterogeneity was evaluated using I2 values, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively, in accordance with the guidelines proposed by Higgins et al. [30] All data analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software (v4.4.2; R Core Team 2024), primarily utilizing the meta (v8.0.1) packages [31].

RESULTS

1. Selected Studies

A total of 58 studies [9,15,18,19,21,22,32-83] were included in the data extraction process. Among them, 7 studies [18,38,41,42,48,52,68] were excluded because of overlapping patient populations, resulting in 51 studies [9,15,19,21,22,32-37,39,40,43-47,49-51,53-67,69-83] included in the final systematic review. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. During full-text screening, 1 article was excluded because it was published in a journal identified as potentially predatory according to Beall’s list. No additional studies were excluded solely on the basis of journal indexing status or editorial standards. Among the studies on spinopelvic parameter analysis included, 4 studies [50,59,65,72] reported mixed rates of PJK and PJF and were not included in meta-analysis owing to heterogeneity. One study [53] was excluded for reporting data in median and interquartile range form. However, the study by Tian et al. [72] was included in the meta-analysis of PJK rates associated with degree of correction according to age-adjusted PI–LL mismatch values. Thus, 4 studies [50,53,59,65] were excluded, and 47 studies [9,15,19,21,22,32-37,39,40,43-47,49,51,54-58,60-64,66,67,69-83] were included in meta-analysis. Although there was no restriction for study design, all screened studies were retrospective. 2. Study Quality

The quality of the 51 included studies in the systematic review, as evaluated using the NOS, is summarized in Table 1. Most studies scored between 7 and 9 points, indicating generally high methodological quality. Three studies [43,81,83] scored 6, reflecting moderate quality. Specifically, the study by Hiyama et al. [43] was limited by its design restricted to female patients, lack of adjustment for confounders, and absence of information regarding non-respondents. The study by Yasuda et al. [81] defined PJF as revision surgery performed for PJF without providing explicit criteria, undermining the clarity of outcome definition. Meanwhile, Zhang et al. [83] did not specify the definition of PJK, resulting in ambiguity in outcome classification. Across the body of evidence, the inclusion criteria and radiologic outcomes were generally well defined among the studies, and measurement methods were clear in most studies. Information on blinding of outcome assessors was typically lacking due to the retrospective design, but this was considered less critical for radiologic outcomes. Although most studies achieved acceptable NOS scores, the absence of consistent multivariable adjustment for important patient- and surgery-related confounders should be con-sidered when interpreting the pooled estimates.

5. Proximal Junctional Complication Rates Associated With Degree of Correction According to Age-Adjusted PI–LL Mismatch Values

Although Tian et al. [72] reported proximal junctional complications as a mixed outcome (PJK/PJF), the study was included in the PJK analysis. This is because PJF lies on the pathologic spectrum with PJK and represents structural failure at the proximal junction. The patients were stratified into 3 groups using the age-adjusted target PI–LL=(age–55)/2+3 formula: undercorrection (offset <-10°), matched correction (-10° to +10°), and overcorrection (>+10°) [20,25]. No significant differences were found in PJK rates (Fig. 2A) and PJF rates (Fig. 2B) between the undercorrection and matched group. However, the overcorrection group showed a significantly higher risk of PJK than the matched group (OR, 2.67; 95% CI, 1.61–4.42; I2=0%) (Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, PJF rates did not differ significantly between the overcorrection and matched groups (Fig. 3B).

The second approach applied the age-specific alignment reference values proposed by Lafage et al. [20,21], which defined normative PI–LL mismatch thresholds for discrete age categories (<35 years: >-10.5°; 35–44 years: >-4.6°; 45–54 years: >0.5°; 55–64 years: >5.8°; 65–74 years: >10.5°; ≥75 years: >17°). Patients with PI–LL mismatch values below the age-adjusted threshold were categorized into the overcorrection group, while those with values greater than or equal to the threshold were classified into the aligned group. When analyzed under this framework, only PJF outcomes could be compared. The overcorrection group demonstrated a significantly higher risk of PJF than the aligned group (OR, 1.67; 95% CI, 1.10–2.52; I2=0%) (Fig. 4).

6. Sensitivity Analysis

To examine the robustness of pooled estimates and the potential influence of underlying bias and individual studies, we conducted funnel plots and Egger test for analyses including at least 10 studies that yielded statistically significant results. Across all eligible parameters comparing patients with and without PJK, Egger tests indicated no evidence of publication bias when setting a threshold of p=0.05 (preoperative LL, p=0.996; postoperative PI–LL mismatch, p=0.124; pre- and postoperative PT, p=0.297 and p=0.453; pre- and postoperative SS, p=0.116 and p=0.083; postoperative TK, p=0.057; pre- and postoperative T1PA, p=0.636 and p=0.576; preoperative C7–SVA, p=0.333).

Although the result for postoperative TK (p=0.057) was marginal and close to the significance threshold, it did not meet the conventional cutoff. Similarly, visual inspection of the corresponding funnel plots did not suggest substantial asymmetry (Supplementary Figs. 7, 12, 17, 18, 23, 24, 29, 34, 35, 40). Considering that fewer than 10 studies were available, Egger test and funnel plot inspection were not performed for the analyses showing statistically significant MDs in postoperative TLK between the PJK and non-PJK groups and in preoperative PI–LL mismatch and pre- and postoperative C7-SVA between the PJF and non-PJF groups.

Because one included study reported a mixed PJK/PJF endpoint, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding this study from the PJK analysis. After exclusion of Tian et al. [72] 2024, ageadjusted PI–LL overcorrection remained significantly associated with PJK, with a pooled OR of 3.11 (95% CI, 1.67–5.78; I2=0%; Fig. 5), compared with the original pooled estimate of 2.67 (95% CI, 1.61–4.42; I2=0%; Fig. 3A). These findings suggest that the observed association was not driven by the inclusion of the mixed-outcome study.

DISCUSSION

This meta-analysis evaluated the association between spinopelvic parameters and proximal junctional complications (PJK and PJF) after ASD surgery. Complication rates across age-adjusted PI–LL mismatch categories, using both the age-based formula (age–55)/2+3 and the age-specific normative thresholds proposed by Lafage et al. [20,21], were also evaluated. Preoperatively, the PJK group showed significantly lower LL and SS and higher PT, C7–SVA, and T1PA than the non-PJK group. Postoperatively, the PJK group showed lower PI–LL mismatch and SS and higher PT, TK, TLK, and T1PA than the non-PJK group. These patterns, especially the lower postoperative PI–LL mismatch, could suggest relative overcorrection in patients who subsequently developed PJK. For PJF, patients exhibited higher preoperative PI–LL mismatch and higher preoperative and postoperative C7–SVA values compared with non-PJF patients. No other spinopelvic parameters showed significant differences, suggesting that PJF may be less predictable based on spinopelvic parameters alone.

Composite predictive tools such as the global alignment and proportion (GAP) score have been proposed to integrate multiple radiographic factors for estimating the risk of mechanical complications after ASD surgery [84]. Although promising, these models have shown variable performance, and external validation across heterogeneous patient populations remains limited. Only 2 meta-analyses have been conducted, and they report conflicting results regarding the predictive value of the GAP score [85,86]. Our findings that PJF is not consistently associated with individual spinopelvic parameters reinforce that single angular measures are insufficient. Future studies should therefore aim to develop new multifactorial models that incorporate radiographic alignment; surgical strategy; instrumentation characteristics; and patient-specific factors that increase the risk of PJF, such as bone quality and frailty [11].

For preoperative parameters, the observed differences may reflect underlying modifications in sagittal balance associated with spinal deformity and age-related degeneration [24]. Patients with lumbar degeneration typically demonstrate sagittal imbalance, characterized by a loss of LL, increased PT, and anterior displacement of the C7-SVA [87]. During aging and degeneration, progressive loss of LL and development of lumbar kyphosis contribute to global sagittal imbalance [88,89]. These changes are compensated through mechanisms such as pelvic retroversion, knee flexion, and reduction of TK to preserve sagittal balance of the spine [24]. Given that PI is the sum of SS and PT, pelvic retroversion is reflected as an increase in PT and a decrease in SS. Although a larger PI allows greater potential for pelvic compensation [24,90], our results did not show significant differences in PI values between the proximal junctional complication and noncomplication groups. However, once the compensatory capacity is exceeded, global balance is disrupted, as indicated by increased C7–SVA and T1PA [24,91,92]. Consistently, we found that compared with those without PJK, patients with PJK exhibited significantly lower LL and SS and higher PT, C7–SVA, and T1PA. Therefore, careful preoperative assessment of global balance (e.g., LL, PT, SS, C7–SVA, T1PA) is essential. Additional preventative strategies such as hooks or tethers93,94 should be considered for patients with decompensated sagittal alignment as they are at higher risk of developing proximal junctional complications.

The meta-analysis results of postoperative parameters can be interpreted in 2 complementary ways. First, the postoperative parameters that showed statistically significant MDs between groups, including PI–LL mismatch, PT, SS, TK, TLK, and T1PA, were associated with the presence of PJK. Notably, T1PA differed significantly between groups, whereas C7–SVA did not, suggesting that T1PA may capture postoperative global alignment differences not fully reflected by C7–SVA. However, because the timing of postoperative radiographic assessment varied across the included studies, these findings should not be interpreted as causal predictors or validated markers for early detection of PJK. In addition, the clinical applicability of these postoperative differences remains limited by the absence of clear cutoff values that define when radiographic changes become clinically meaningful.

Second, the significant MDs in PI–LL mismatch values observed in the subgroup analysis of early postoperative periods (early, immediate, or within 3 months) may reflect the degree of surgical correction. Our findings suggest that in comparison with non-PJK patients, PJK patients tend to have overcorrection as reflected by lower postoperative PI–LL mismatch values. However, even within the early postoperative period, the precise timing of radiographic assessment varied, and early junctional changes may already have been present in some patients. Therefore, these postoperative parameters should be interpreted cautiously as associations rather than definitive evidence of overcorrection as a causal factor. Future prospective studies with standardized and long-term follow-up are needed to establish more reliable thresholds and to clarify how postoperative radiographic parameters can be both used to assess surgical correction and to monitor the early development of PJK.

However, the analysis of MDs in postoperative PI–LL mismatch values did not account for age. In our meta-analysis according to degree of correction by age-adjusted PI–LL, overcorrection was associated with higher PJK rates when assessed by the agebased formula. Meanwhile, it was associated with higher PJF rates when assessed by Lafage’s age-stratified thresholds. Although the association of PJF with individual spinopelvic parameters was inconsistent, it became significant when assessed using age-adjusted PI–LL mismatch criteria. This supports that alignment targets need to be tailored according to patient age rather than applied uniformly.

Importantly, PJK and PJF were analyzed separately whenever extractable from the original studies, because radiographic PJK and clinically more severe PJF are related but distinct outcomes. One study in the age-adjusted PI–LL analysis reported a mixed PJK/PJF endpoint; therefore, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding this study. The association between age-adjusted PI–LL overcorrection and PJK remained significant after exclusion, suggesting that the observed association was not driven by the inclusion of the mixed-outcome study. Nevertheless, given the limited number of retrospective studies, this finding should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence of prediction or causality.

Extensive deformity correction can sometimes result in sagittal overcorrection, such as excessive LL restoration or marked C7-SVA reduction [95]. Such alignment may be physiologically unsustainable as the body attempts to preserve balance within Dubousset’s cone of economy, where posture is maintained with minimal muscular effort [96,97]. After correction surgery, the compensatory capacity is largely confined to the mobile segments above the fusion. This often leads to an increase in the proximal junctional angle, thereby predisposing patients to PJK.

Degenerative changes in the spine progress, and compensatory mechanisms are activated with aging [24]. Additionally, the surrounding muscles and joints also deteriorate, further altering the ability to maintain sagittal balance. This in turn increases the risk for proximal junctional complications [59,98]. However, during surgery, the only values that can be directly corrected are spinal angles. Therefore, to account for the condition of the surrounding muscles and joints that cannot be surgically corrected, age-adjusted reference values are required to account for these aging-related physiological factors. Our findings suggest a possible association between age-adjusted PI–LL overcorrection and proximal junctional complications. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously because the formula-based offset method and the Lafage age-specific threshold method are related but not identical, and each analysis was based on a limited number of retrospective studies. Therefore, age-adjusted alignment targets may be useful for individualized surgical planning, but they require further prospective validation before being applied as definitive thresholds.

Classification systems such as the SRS–Schwab classification [16] and the GAP score [84] have been recently introduced in planning deformity corrective surgery to integrate both global and regional alignment. For example, in the SRS–Schwab classification, of the sagittal modifiers, C7-SVA represents global alignment, PT reflects compensatory mechanisms, and PI–LL mismatch reflects regional alignment [16,99].

However, the clinical application of age-adjusted alignment targets during actual surgery remains challenging. Patients are typically placed in the prone position rather than standing during surgery, which leads to alterations in spinopelvic parameters [100]. In addition, it is often difficult to obtain full-spine images intraoperatively owing to radiographic limitations. Even when obtained, the absence of gravitational loading makes the values less representative of true standing balance [100,101] despite some studies attempting to correlate intraoperative values with postoperative alignment [102,103]. Therefore, surgeons must estimate postoperative standing alignment based on intraoperative findings obtained under non-weight-bearing condition. As such, PI–LL mismatch has been classically used to assess intraoperative correction, and careful integration of preoperative age-adjusted planning, intraoperative assessment of directly modifiable parameters such as LL, and postoperative standing radiographs may be necessary when applying age-adjusted alignment targets. This explains why we evaluated degree of correction based on the age-adjusted PI–LL mismatch values in the metaanalysis.

In addition, LL, particularly the lower lumbar segments, is the most reliable parameter that can be directly visualized and corrected intraoperatively. Some studies have accordingly used LL as a key parameter for predicting postoperative sagittal alignment [104]. SS and PT are positional parameters that change secondarily in relation to LL and PI as a compensatory mechanism to maintain sagittal balance [104,105]. Therefore, their intraoperative use is limited because accurate assessment requires consideration of the effect of gravity and patient posture. Additionally, TK is difficult to directly modify unless instrumentation extends into the thoracic spine.

Across comparisons with statistically significant results that included more than 10 studies, Egger tests did not indicate publication bias, and funnel plots did not reveal substantial asymmetry. There was moderate to high heterogeneity for several parameters (e.g., postoperative PI–LL mismatch, PT, TK). Restricting to early postoperative assessments (≤3 months/early/immediate) reduced I2 in some subgroup analyses but increased it in others, indicating that timing alone cannot account for the observed variability. It is likely that between-study differences in age distributions, osteoporosis, measurement methods and inter-rater variability, surgical strategy (e.g., upper instrumented vertebra [UIV] level), and implant techniques contributed to heterogeneity. Given that this study relied on aggregate data rather than individual patient data, we could not adjust these confounders via meta-regression or patient-level stratification. Therefore, the pooled MDs should be interpreted primarily as summary associations rather than as directly actionable thresholds. Because the analyses were based on heterogeneous aggregate study-level data, we did not derive practical cutoff values or definitive correction targets from these estimates.

As such, the results of this meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution. Standardizing the timing of radiographic assessments—for example, consistent definitions of “early postoperative” or “first standing” imaging—would reduce variability and improve comparability. Although consistent MDs were observed, clinically useful cutoff thresholds could not be identified. Future large, prospective cohort studies are needed to evaluate timedependent changes in alignment, validate potential thresholds, and determine whether they can be translated into actionable surgical targets. Finally, although several spinopelvic parameters were linked to PJK, PJF appeared less predictable on the basis of angular measures alone, highlighting the importance of incorporating surgical technique, construct design, and bone health into predictive models.

This study has limitations. First, although statistically significant differences were detected for several parameters, the pooled MDs were generally small. These differences may fall within the expected range of radiographic measurement error and therefore may not necessarily represent clinically meaningful differences. Accordingly, the statistically significant MDs should be interpreted cautiously and should not be used to define practical cutoff values or directly actionable correction targets. Although the consistency of these findings across multiple studies supports the presence of statistical associations, their clinical significance remains uncertain.

Second, the absence of prospective or randomized studies and the retrospective design of all included studies limited the ability to control for confounding variables. Moreover, variability in the timing and methods of radiographic measurements, as well as interobserver differences, may have introduced bias in the reported spinopelvic parameters. Immediate postoperative radiographs, first standing radiographs, and radiographs obtained within 3 months after surgery may not be strictly equivalent, and this variability should be considered when interpreting the early postoperative subgroup analyses.

In addition, several major confounders could not be sufficiently adjusted across the included studies. These included UIV level, fusion extent, osteoporosis, frailty, bone quality, use of prophylactic techniques such as tethering or hooks, overall surgical strategy, and radiographic measurement timing. Because this meta-analysis was based on aggregate study-level data, we could not consistently account for these factors, and they may have influenced the observed associations between spinopelvic parameters, degree of correction, and proximal junctional complications. Therefore, despite generally acceptable NOS scores, the pooled estimates should be regarded as associations rather than independent causal effects, because residual confounding could not be fully addressed. Furthermore, the definitions of PJK and PJF varied across the included studies. PJK was generally defined using radiographic proximal junctional angle criteria, whereas PJF definitions more often incorporated clinically severe structural failure, symptoms, implant-related failure, or revision surgery, depending on the original study. This heterogeneity in outcome definitions may have contributed to inconsistency in the reported complication rates and should be considered when interpreting the pooled estimates. Finally, some studies may have included overlapping patient populations despite our efforts to minimize duplicate inclusion from overlapping patient populations, especially from the same institutions or authorship groups. This could have introduced unintended duplication in the pooled estimates.

CONCLUSION

Preoperative sagittal imbalance, characterized by reduced LL, increased PT, decreased SS, and elevated C7–SVA and T1PA, is associated with PJK, underscoring the importance of thorough preoperative assessment of global balance. Postoperatively, several parameters including PI–LL mismatch, PT, SS, TK, TLK, and T1PA are significantly different between those with and without PJK. However, because the timing of postoperative radiographic assessment varied across the included studies, these postoperative differences should be interpreted as associations rather than causal predictors or validated markers for early detection. Notably, T1PA differed significantly between groups whereas C7–SVA did not, suggesting that T1PA may capture postoperative global alignment differences not fully reflected by C7–SVA, although further validation is required. For PJF, only limited associations are observed, primarily involving higher preoperative PI–LL mismatch and higher C7–SVA values. This suggests that spinopelvic parameters alone may be insufficient for reliable prediction. Finally, age-adjusted PI–LL overcorrection showed significant associations with proximal junctional complications, although the specific association with PJK or PJF varied according to the classification method. These findings support the potential value of tailoring alignment targets to patient age rather than applying uniform criteria, but require further prospective validation.
