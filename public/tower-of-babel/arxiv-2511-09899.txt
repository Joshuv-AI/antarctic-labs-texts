Beyond Born-Oppenheimer Time-Dependent Density Functional Theory
Chen Li, Ryan Requist, E. K. U. Gross

Abstract
We formulate a time-dependent density functional theory for the coupled dynamics of electrons and nuclei that goes beyond the Born-Oppenheimer (BO) approximation. We prove that the time-dependent marginal nuclear probability density | χ ⁡ ( 𝐑 ¯ ¯ , t ) | 2 |\chi({\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}},t)|^{2} , the conditional electronic density n 𝐑 ¯ ¯ ​ ( 𝐫 , t ) n_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\mathbf{r},t) , and the current density 𝑱 𝐑 ¯ ¯ ​ ( 𝐫 , t ) \bm{J}_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\mathbf{r},t) are sufficient to uniquely determine the full time-evolving electron-nuclear wave function, and thus the dynamics of all observables. Moreover, we propose a time-dependent Kohn-Sham scheme which reproduces the exact conditional electronic density and current density and the exact N-body nuclear density. The remaining task is to look for functional approximations for the Kohn-Sham exchange-correlation scalar and vector potentials. Using a model driven proton transfer system, we numerically demonstrate that the adiabatic extension of a beyond-BO ground state functional captures the dominant nonadiabatic effects in the regime of slow driving.

Many fundamental processes in physics, chemistry, and materials science involve the coupled dynamics of electrons and nuclei, representing a challenging quantum many-body problem that requires methods balancing computational efficiency with accuracy.
Within the Born-Oppenheimer (BO) approximation, density functional theory (DFT) has emerged as a highly successful framework for treating the many-electron problem by reformulating the Schrödinger equation (SE) in terms of the electron density as reduced fundamental variable.
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
;
Kohn and Sham 1965

Using the standard density functional approximations (DFAs),
one can reliably obtain an accurate ground-state potential energy surface (PES), which forms the basis for simulations of quantum or classical nuclear dynamics. Although this approach neglects the back-reaction of nuclear motion on the electronic state, it offers a reasonably accurate description for a wide range of adiabatic chemical processes that do not involve significant electronic excitations out of the ground state.

The coupled motion between electrons and nuclei, however, could induce electronic excitations, whose description requires time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT).
Runge and Gross 1984
One common approach is to combine real-time TDDFT with Ehrenfest molecular dynamics, where nuclei evolve on a mean-field PES.
Andrade et al. 2009
;
You et al. 2024
.
However, in photochemical processes, strong nonadiabatic coupling (NAC) such as that occurring at conical intersections drives significant transitions between adiabatic electronic states with substantially different PES landscapes, leading to dynamics which cannot be captured by a single BO or mean-field PES. A rigorous treatment of the full electron-nuclear wavefunction can be pursued within the Born–Huang (BH) expansion
Born and Huang 1954
, wherein the nuclear wavefunction evolves over multiple static BO PESs, with population transfer between them mediated by NACs. A standard way to use DFT/TDDFT in this context involves ground state and linear-response calculations of adiabatic PESs and NACs. While the BH framework is well-suited to molecules, whose spectrum is dominated by a finite number of electronic states, it becomes challenging to implement when the spectrum is continuous, as in metals or materials with electrons excited to a conduction band. Examples include electron-phonon energy transfer, as occurs in the dynamics of coherent phonons,
Dekorsy et al. 2000
;
Caruso and Zacharias 2023
chiral phonons, excitons and polarons,
Corby et al. 2021
and laser-induced structural phase transitions in solids.
Frigge et al. 2017
;
Nicholson et al. 2018
.

In such cases, it is natural to hypothesize that the net effect of occupying a distribution of excited-state PESs can be represented by a single effective time-dependent PES governing nuclear motion. This concept can be formally realized through the exact factorization approach.
Hunter 1975
;
Gidopoulos and Gross 2014
;
Abedi et al. 2010
;
Abedi et al. 2012
.
In particular, it has been shown that the exact total wave function
\Psi(\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
can be factorized into a marginal nuclear wave function
\chi(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
and a conditional electronic wave function
\Phi_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},t)
; here
\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}=(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{r}_{N_{e}})
denotes the electronic coordinates and
\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}=(\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{R}_{N_{n}})
denotes the nuclear coordinates.
The full TDSE for
\Psi
is equivalent to the following set of coupled equations for
\chi
and
\Phi_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}
:

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\Phi_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},t)

\displaystyle=\Big[\hat{H}_{el}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},t)-\epsilon(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Big]\Phi_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},t),

(1)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\chi(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)

\displaystyle=\Big[\sum_{a=1}^{N_{n}}\frac{1}{2M_{a}}\Big(-i\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{a}}+\bm{A}_{a}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+V_{\rm ext}^{n}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)+\epsilon(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Big]\chi(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t).

(2)

Here we have set
\hbar=1
and
V_{\rm ext}^{n}
is the external potential acting on the nuclei.
The equation for
\chi
is a simple TDSE with time-dependent scalar and vector potentials
(\mathcal{E},\bm{A}_{l})
that are unique up to a gauge choice; this is very different from the BH formalism where the nuclear wave amplitude evolves on all static BO PESs and the population on different surfaces is exchanged through the nonadiabatic couplings. The equation for
\Phi_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}
is reminiscent of the electron-only problem at each nuclear configuration
\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}
but with
\hat{H}_{el}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},t)=\hat{H}_{\rm BO}+\hat{U}_{en}
containing both the BO Hamiltonian and a
\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{l}}
-gradient correction
\hat{U}_{en}
(see
Abedi et al. 2010
;
Abedi et al. 2012
for the formula).
Eqs. (
1
)–(
2
) provide an ideal starting point for
density functionalization. In particular, through replacing
\Phi_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}
by some electronic densities and reformulating Eq. (
1
) as a time-dependent conditional Kohn-Sham scheme,
we aim at reproducing the exact time-evolving densities.

Given the inherently conditional nature of this quantity, a natural choice is to introduce
n_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\mathbf{r},t)
, the conditional electronic density, as the fundamental variable.
Besides
n_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\mathbf{r},t)
, we note that there is another quantity, the conditional electronic current density
\bm{J}_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\mathbf{r},t)
, which deserves consideration as an additional fundamental variable. This is because electrons typically follow nuclear motion in a coupled electron-nuclear dynamics, yielding a nontrivial electronic current.
Schild et al. 2016
Formulating the theory with
\bm{J}_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\mathbf{r},t)
extends time-dependent current density functional theory (TDCDFT) beyond purely electronic systems, which is the approach adopted in this Letter.

This formulation involves reducing the fundamental variable from the full wavefunction
\Psi
to the set
(|\chi|^{2},n_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}},\bm{J}_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}})
, a reduction that entails a significant loss of information. To justify this choice, it is essential to establish an extension of the Vignale theorem
Vignale 2004
for the coupled electron-nuclear problem.
The key lies in establishing a one-to-one mapping between
(n_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}},\bm{J}_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}})
with some scalar and vector potential, which is not trivial for a two-component system.
Instead of using conditional densities, we show that a better starting point is using joint electronic densities and current densities, defined by

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)

\displaystyle=N_{e}\int|\Psi(\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)|^{2}d\mathbf{r}_{2}\cdots d\mathbf{r}_{N_{e}},

(3)

\displaystyle\bm{J}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{-iN_{e}}{2m_{e}}\int\Big(\Psi^{*}\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\Psi-\Psi\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\Psi^{*}\Big)d\mathbf{r}_{2}\cdots d\mathbf{r}_{N_{e}},

(4)

which are 1-body quantities in electrons and N-body in nuclei.
One can verify that our fundamental variables are derivable from
\rho
and
\bm{J}
through the following relations:
|\chi(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)|^{2}=\frac{1}{N_{e}}\int\rho(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)d\mathbf{r}
,
n_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\mathbf{r},t)=\rho(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)/|\chi(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)|^{2}
and
\bm{J}_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\mathbf{r},t)=\bm{J}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)/|\chi(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)|^{2}
. Thus, the joint densities carry exactly the same information as {
|\chi|^{2}
,
n_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}
,
\bm{J}_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}
}. Using
\rho
and
\bm{J}
, we state our theorem regarding a density-to-potential mapping as follows.

Theorem.

Consider an electron-nuclear system described by the time-dependent Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{a}\frac{1}{2M_{a}}\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{a}}^{2}+\sum_{k}\frac{1}{2m_{e}}\Big(\hat{\bm{p}}_{k}+\bm{A}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{a<b}U(R_{ab})+\sum_{j<k}W(r_{jk})+\sum_{k}V(\mathbf{r}_{k},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t).

(5)

Here
\hat{\bm{p}}_{k}=-i\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_{k}}
is the canonical momentum operator of electron
k
,
R_{ab}=|\mathbf{R}_{a}-\mathbf{R}_{b}|
and
r_{jk}=|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{k}|
.
V(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
and
\bm{A}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
are the time-dependent electron-nuclear scalar and vector interaction potentials, respectively, and assumed to be analytic functions of
t
at
t=0
. We show that under reasonable assumptions, i.e., the Taylor series expansion of the potentials have finite radius of convergence, the joint 1-electron N-nucleus density
\rho(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
and the current density
\bm{J}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
can be obtained under the influence of
\hat{H}
from a given initial state
\Psi(0)
and can also be obtained under the influence of
\hat{H}^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{a}\frac{1}{2M_{a}}\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{a}}^{2}+\sum_{k}\frac{1}{2m_{e}}\Big(\hat{\bm{p}}_{k}+\bm{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{a<b}U(R_{ab})+\sum_{j<k}W^{\prime}(r_{jk})+\sum_{k}V^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}_{k},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t),

(6)

starting from an initial state
\Psi^{\prime}(0)
that gives the same
\rho
and
\bm{J}
as
\Psi(0)
at
t=0
. The potentials
V^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
and
\bm{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
are uniquely determined by
V(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
and
\bm{A}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
,
\Psi(0)
and
\Psi^{\prime}(0)
, up to gauge transformations of the form

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)

\displaystyle\rightarrow V^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)-\partial_{t}\Lambda(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t),

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{}}A(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\bm{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)+\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\Lambda(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t).

(7)

Proof.
Given potentials
V(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
and
\bm{A}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
, one can always make a gauge transformation such that
the scalar potential vanishes. This can be achieved by setting

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Lambda(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)=V(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)

(8)

with initial condition
\Lambda(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},0)=0
. The same argument also applies to the primed potentials. Therefore, for the convenience of derivation, let us assume
V
and
V^{\prime}
have been gauged away. Then it suffices to construct the differential equation that uniquely determines
\bm{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
.

We begin by denoting
\bm{\hat{v}}_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{m_{e}}\Big(\bm{\hat{p}}_{k}+\bm{A}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Big)
and rewriting
\rho(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)=\langle\Psi|\hat{n}(\mathbf{r})|\Psi\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}
and
\bm{J}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)=\langle\Psi|\hat{\bm{j}}(\mathbf{r},t)|\Psi\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}
, where
\hat{n}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{k}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{k})
and
\bm{\hat{j}}(\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\{\bm{\hat{v}}_{k}(t),\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{k})\}
are electron density and current density operators, respectively. Here
\{\hat{A},\hat{B}\}=\hat{A}\hat{B}+\hat{B}\hat{A}
is the anticommutator. Using Heisenberg equation of motion, we can deduce the time derivative of the current density,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{J}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi|i[\hat{H},\bm{\hat{j}}(\mathbf{r},t)]|\Psi\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}+\langle\Psi|\partial_{t}\bm{\hat{j}}(\mathbf{r},t)|\Psi\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}.

(9)

By straightforward algebra, one can show
\langle\Psi|\partial_{t}\bm{\hat{j}}(\mathbf{r},t)|\Psi\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}=\frac{1}{m_{e}}\rho(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\partial_{t}\bm{A}
.
To evaluate the first term on the right hand side (RHS) of Eq. (
9
), we decompose
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{n}+\hat{H}_{\rm BO}
, where
\hat{H}_{n}
is the nuclear kinetic energy operator. Invoking Vignale’s result in Ref
Vignale 2004
, we arrive at (details can be found in the supplemental information
sup
)

\displaystyle\langle\Psi|i[\hat{H}_{\rm BO},\bm{\hat{j}}(\mathbf{r},t)]|\Psi\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m_{e}}\Big[-\bm{J}\times(\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\times\bm{A})+\bm{F}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Big]+\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t).

(10)

Here

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)=-\langle\Psi|\sum_{k}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{k})\sum_{l\neq k}\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_{k}}W(|\mathbf{r}_{k}-\mathbf{r}_{l}|)|\Psi\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}},

(11)

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)=-\frac{1}{4}\langle\Psi|\sum_{k}\{\hat{v}_{k}^{\beta},\{\hat{v}_{k}^{\alpha},\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{k})\}\}|\Psi\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}

(12)

is a stress tensor, with
\alpha
and
\beta
referring to the Cartesian indices. Denoting
\bm{S}=\langle\Psi|i[\hat{H}_{n},\bm{\hat{j}}(\mathbf{r},t)]|\Psi\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}
, we have

\partial_{t}\bm{J}=\frac{1}{m_{e}}\Big[\rho\partial_{t}\bm{A}-\bm{J}\times(\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\times\bm{A})+\bm{F}\Big]+\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\bm{\sigma}+\bm{S}.

(13)

Since
\partial_{t}\bm{J}
is identical for the unprimed and primed system, it follows

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{m_{e}}\Big[\rho\partial_{t}\Delta\bm{A}-\bm{J}\times(\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\times\Delta\bm{A})\Big]+\Delta\bm{Q}=0.

(14)

Here
\Delta\bm{A}=\bm{A}^{\prime}-\bm{A}
and
\Delta\bm{Q}=\bm{Q}^{\prime}-\bm{Q}
, with

\bm{Q}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)=\frac{\bm{F}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)}{m_{e}}+\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)+\bm{S}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t),

(15)

and
\bm{Q}^{\prime}
being the counterpart of the primed system.

At
t=0
, using the equality of the current densities, i.e.,
\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{\bm{j}}(\mathbf{r},t)|\Psi^{\prime}_{0}\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}=\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{\bm{j}}(\mathbf{r},t)|\Psi^{\prime}_{0}\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}
, we can deduce

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},0)\Delta\bm{A}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},0)=\langle\Psi_{0}|\bm{\hat{j}}_{p}(\mathbf{r})|\Psi_{0}\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}-\langle\Psi_{0}^{\prime}|\bm{\hat{j}}_{p}(\mathbf{r})|\Psi_{0}^{\prime}\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}.

(16)

Here
\bm{\hat{j}}_{p}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{k}\{\bm{\hat{p}}_{k},\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{k})\}
is the paramagnetic current density operator. Eq. (
14
) along with Eq. (
16
) define a partial differential equation for
\Delta\bm{A}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)
. By our assumption,
\Delta\bm{A}
is Taylor expandable with respect to
t
with nonzero radius of convergence. Then following Vignale’s constructive proof in
Vignale 2004
, we can plug the Taylor series formula
\Delta\bm{A}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)=\sum_{k}\Delta\bm{A}_{k}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}})t^{k}
into Eq. (
14
), which leads to a recursive relation for
\Delta\bm{A}_{k}
.
sup
By Eq. (
16
),
\Delta\bm{A}_{0}
is known. Therefore, the recursive relation shall uniquely determine all the
\Delta\bm{A}_{k}
, and hence
\Delta\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}^{\prime}
. This completes both the existence and uniqueness proof of our theorem.

By our theorem, the mapping between the potentials
\Big(V(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t),\bm{A}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Big)
and the densities

is invertible up to a gauge transformation, allowing us to use these densities as the fundamental variables to reproduce the exact dynamics.
Additionally, by choosing
W^{\prime}=0
we can introduce an auxiliary Kohn-Sham system with scalar and vector potentials
V^{\prime}
and
\bm{A}^{\prime}
that reproduce the actual densities. Similar idea has been exploited for the ground state problem in
Fromager and Lasorne 2024
.
Applying exact factorization to the full wave function for this Kohn-Sham system, i.e.
\Psi^{\prime}(\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)=\chi^{\prime}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Phi^{\prime}_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},t)

\hat{H}_{el}^{\prime}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},t)=\hat{H}_{\rm TDKS}^{\rm BO}+\hat{U}_{en}
, where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm TDKS}^{\rm BO}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{2m_{e}}\Big(\hat{\bm{p}}_{k}+\bm{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Big)^{2}+\sum_{a<b}U(R_{ab})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k}V^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}_{k},\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t),

(17)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{en}[\Phi^{\prime},\chi^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\frac{1}{M_{a}}\Big[\frac{(-i\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{a}}-\bm{A}_{a}^{\prime})^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\quad+(\frac{-i\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{a}}\chi^{\prime}}{\chi^{\prime}}+\bm{A}_{a}^{\prime})(-\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{a}}-\bm{A}_{a}^{\prime})\Big].

(18)

The Hamiltonian governing the nuclear dynamics reads

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{n}^{\prime}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\frac{1}{2M_{a}}\Big(-i\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{a}}+\bm{A}_{a}^{\prime}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)\Big)^{2}+\epsilon^{\prime}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t),

(19)

with
\bm{A}_{a}^{\prime}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)=\langle\Phi^{\prime}|-i\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{a}}|\Phi^{\prime}\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}
,
\epsilon^{\prime}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},t)=\langle\Phi^{\prime}|\hat{H}_{el}^{\prime}(\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}},\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},t)-i\partial_{t}|\Phi^{\prime}\rangle_{\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}}}
.

The equation for
\Phi^{\prime}_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},t)
has two unusual, but highly desirable, properties: (i) Owing to its
\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{l}}
dependence,
\hat{U}_{en}
acts like a non-Hermitian operator in the electronic Hilbert space. Consequently, the resulting time-propagation of

is non-unitary despite being norm-conserving by construction. We emphasize that it is precisely this non-unitary evolution that enables the description of electronic decoherence within a single-trajectory approach.
Tu and Gross 2025a
(ii) Even if the initial state
\Phi^{\prime}_{\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}(\underline{\underline{\bf{r}}},t_{0})
is a determinant of single-particle orbitals, the

creates correlations among the electrons, preventing

Ruan and Baer 2018

For real calculations, because

is inversely proportional to the nuclear mass, it can be treated perturbatively.

;
Tu and Gross 2025a
;
Tu and Gross 2025b
Alternatively, in the spirit of traditional KS TDDFT, we adopt, as a working hypothesis, the assumption that the nuclear observable quantities, namely, the N-body density

;
Agostini et al. 2013

In the following, we apply our beyond-BO TDDFT formalism to a model driven proton transfer process, demonstrating that
\epsilon
and
v_{s,\underline{\underline{\bf{R}}}}
can be accurately approximated by the adiabatic extension of a ground state functional. In order to compare with an exact solution which is unavailable in more realistic systems, we restrict the nuclear configuration space to one dimension and consider a double well model, mimicking a hydrogen transfer reaction through the tautomerism of two enol structures of acetylacetone driven by a bias potential (see Fig.
1
). Furthermore, we effectively truncate the electronic Hilbert space by using an
R

Li et al. 2018

Wang et al. 2025

In the basis of the three singlet states, namely,
\varphi_{1}=|1_{\uparrow}1_{\downarrow}\rangle
,
\varphi_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|1_{\uparrow}2_{\downarrow}\rangle-|1_{\downarrow}2_{\uparrow}\rangle
), and
\varphi_{3}=|2_{\uparrow}2_{\downarrow}\rangle

\bm{H}_{e}(R)=\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}+\Delta\epsilon(R),&-\sqrt{2}\tau(R),&0\\
-\sqrt{2}\tau(R),&0,&-\sqrt{2}\tau(R)\\
0,&-\sqrt{2}\tau(R),&U_{2}-\Delta\epsilon(R)\end{pmatrix}.

(21)

Here
U_{i}
are the on-site Hubbard parameters;
\tau(R)
is the electron hopping energy; and
\Delta\epsilon(R)
is the on-site energy difference. The electron density operator is defined as
\hat{n}=\rm{diag}(-1,0,1)
. Moreover, to avoid possible numerical difficulties at the boundary during the time propagation, we choose periodic functions for
\tau(R)
and
\Delta\epsilon(R)
with period
L=3
Bohr. We choose the nuclear mass

to be about the hydrogen mass, and choose the parameters in the Hubbard model such that the barrier in the ground state PES roughly corresponds to a weak hydrogen bonding energy.
To describe the external driving field, which effectively simulates a nearby polar solvent molecule,
Hanna and Kapral 2005
we introduce the following time-dependent driving potential,

\bm{V}(R,t)=V_{0}\sin(\frac{2\pi R}{L})\hat{n}\cos\omega t,

which couples to both the electrons and nuclei. Here
V_{0}
is the amplitude and
\omega\equiv\frac{2\pi}{T}

The total time-dependent electron-nuclear Hamiltonian is then given by
\bm{\hat{H}}=-\frac{1}{2M}\nabla^{2}+\bm{H}_{e}(R)+\bm{V}(R,t)
.

Here we also present the results of the BO approximation without the geometric correction, named DFTBO in the figure. Although the PES is well reproduced by DFTBO, we note that this is due to our use of the exact density as input and that the KS potential is drastically wrong. In an actual time evolution, the large errors in the KS potential would lead to a completely incorrect density, which would then feed back into the PES, affecting the nuclear wave function. This comparison between DFT and DFTBO thus indicates the vital role of the geometric correction in the adiabatic regime.
