A case of recurrent pleural effusion: tuberculous pleurisy or other disease? A case report
Authors: Yuanhao Ji, Jinsun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPOEMS syndrome (polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, M-protein, skin changes) is a paraneoplastic syndrome caused by a plasma cell proliferative disorder. Although POEMS syndrome can cause polyserous effusions, the report of pleural effusion as the main manifestation is not common. Here, we describe a case of POEMS syndrome misdiagnosed as tuberculous pleurisy due to massive pleural effusion.Case presentationA 70-year-old man was admitted with chest tightness and was diagnosed with tuberculous pleurisy. Despite receiving anti-tuberculosis treatment, patient exhibited ineffective responses and developed numbness in his lower limbs, indicative of an adverse drug reaction.Adjustment of medication and nutritional nerve treatment yielded no significant improvement. Then he was admitted for the second time three months later. Subsequent auxiliary examinations revealed positive M protein, elevated VEGF(vascular endothelial growth factor), peripheral neuropathy, and endocrine disorders, ultimately confirming the diagnosis of POEMS syndrome.ConclusionsPOEMS syndrome can be mainly characterized by pleural effusion. When the cause of pleural effusion is unknown, POEMS syndrome should be suspected.

Introduction

Pleural effusion is a common clinical problem with a broad differential diagnosis. In tuberculosis-endemic areas such as China, tuberculous pleurisy is frequently considered in clinical practice. According to the World Health Organization, the tuberculosis incidence rate in China decreased from 55 per 100,000 population in 2021 to 49 per 100,000 population in 2024 [1]. However, the early symptoms and imaging findings of tuberculous pleurisy are often nonspecific, and the pleural fluid is typically paucibacillary, which may hinder bacteriological confirmation and increase the risk of misdiagnosis without pathological examination [2].

POEMS syndrome (polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, monoclonal gammopathy, and skin changes) is a rare plasma cell disorder, but extravascular volume overload is common and can manifest as peripheral edema, ascites, and pleural effusions [3]. Notably, in a retrospective analysis of 282 patients, pleural effusion and pleural thickening were observed in 56.4% and 46.5% of cases, respectively [4]. Therefore, pleural effusion may be an early or dominant presentation and can lead to diagnostic anchoring toward tuberculous pleurisy.

In this report, we describe a patient with recurrent pleural effusion who was initially managed as tuberculous pleurisy but was ultimately diagnosed with POEMS syndrome, aiming to highlight diagnostic pitfalls and to improve the evaluation of unexplained pleural effusion/pleurisy in TB-endemic settings.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old man presented to the Department of Infectious Diseases with “chest tightness”. He had no history of close contact with infectious diseases such as tuberculosis but had an 1-year history of diabetes mellitus and hypothyroidism. A complete review of symptoms, including chills, fevers, weight loss, and night sweats were unremarkable.We performed a systemaitc physical examination upon his admission. Dullness on percussion and diminished breath sounds were noted in both lower lungs, more pronounced on the right side. Chest computed tomography(CT) showed nodular hyperdense images in the upper lobe’s apical segment of the left lung, bilateral pleural effusions (predominantly right-sided) with pericardial involvement, and passive collapse in the lower lobe of the left lung(Fig. 1A-D). A laboratory examination was performed once the patient was admitted to our hospital. The interferon-gamma release assay was positive. Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear of pleural fluid was negative.Pleural effusion assay showed a leukocyte count of 2 × 109/L with 30% polymorphonuclear cells and 70% monocytes, a total protein of 24.1 g/L, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 56 U/L, adenosine dehydrogenase (ADH) of 9 U/L, and adenosine deaminase (ADA) of 8 U/L.

Despite recommending further pleural biopsy, the patient declined, leading to a clinical diagnosis of tuberculous pleurisy.

Although the pleural fluid profile (low ADA and low protein) was atypical for tuberculous pleurisy, TB was still considered because the patient lived in a TB-endemic setting, had a positive interferon-γ release assay, and showed an apical pulmonary nodule on CT; moreover, pleural biopsy was declined, limiting pathological confirmation. Transudative causes (e.g., heart failure) and endocrine causes (e.g., hypothyroidism) were also included in the initial differential diagnosis, but were not established at that time and the case was managed empirically.

With the diagnosis of tuberculous pleurisy, the patient was given the anti-tuberculosis treatment(isoniazid 0.3 g/d, rifampin 0.6 g/d, pyrazinamide 1 g/d, ethambutol 0.75 g/d) .In addition, prednisone (starting dosage 30 mg/day, tapered by 10 mg every 10 days) was administered as an adjunctive therapy to relieve severe pleuritic pain and inflammation. The patient showed clinical improvement, with relief of chest tightness and reduction of pleural effusion, and was discharged on medication.

Although treated consistently, the patient continued to experience recurrent pleural effusion. By mid-August 2023, the patient reported numbness in both lower extremities, which was considered a side effect of isoniazid. Then isoniazid was discontinued, and the treatment was supplemented with methylcobalamin and Vitamin B6 for neurotrophic benefits. However, the numbness in the lower limbs persisted.

The patient was admitted for a second time on 10 December 2023. Hyperpigmentation of both hands was detected (Fig. 2A) and bone pain appeared. Subsequently, additional examinations were performed. Electromyography and nerve conduction studies demonstrated a peripheral polyneuropathy.Serum protein electrophoresis revealed the presence of M protein bands. The bone marrow biopsy showed clonal plasma cell hyperplasia (Fig. 2B).His hormone tests showed free thyroid hormone 8.71 pmol/L (reference range: 11.61–21.41 pmol/L), ultrasensitive thyroid-stimulating hormone 43.549 mIU/L(reference range: 0.51–4.94 mIU/L), follicle-producing hormone 16.02 mIU/L(reference range: 1.5–12.4 mIU/L), progesterone P 0.70 ng/ml(reference range: 0.10–0.20 ng/mL), and pituitary prolactin 34.53 ng/ml(reference range: 4.0–15.2 ng/mL).His plain bone film showed the presence of marginal osteophytes in both the thoracic and lumbar regions of the spine. (Fig. 2C).Serum VEGF level was significantly increased (400pg/ml) (reference range: <115 pg/mL).

Based on the results of his report above, he was finally diagnosed with POEMS syndrome and was subsequently referred to the Haematology Department for treatment. The patient was treated with the “RD” regimen (lenalidomide 40 mg/week + dexamethasone 25 mg/day) and was discharged from the hospital with significant improvement (Table 1). Three months later his symptoms improved significantly and his bilateral pleural effusions had disappeared (Fig. 3).

Discussion and conclusion

Tuberculosis (TB) poses a significant threat to global health as a chronic infectious disease, leading to the highest number of deaths worldwide caused by a single pathogen.China is one of the 22 high-burden TB countries in the world, and ranks third in the world [1]. Tuberculous pleurisy is prevalent in China, often leading to exudative pleural effusion. Considering the widespread prevalence of tuberculosis and its vague initial symptoms, tuberculous pleurisy frequently presents as the preliminary diagnosis of pleural effusion. Notably, a short course of prednisone was co-administered, and the early clinical improvement could have been driven by corticosteroid effects rather than anti-tuberculosis therapy per se.

In retrospect, our patient’s pleural fluid findings (total protein 24.1 g/L, LDH 56 U/L, ADA 8 U/L) were not typical of tuberculous pleurisy, which is usually an exudative, lymphocyte-predominant effusion with elevated ADA; an ADA level < 40 U/L substantially lowers the likelihood of tuberculous pleural effusion. AFB smear of pleural fluid was negative, which is common given the paucibacillary nature of the disease. We therefore emphasize that paired serum and pleural samples for applying Light’s criteria, systematic microbiological studies (smear/culture and nucleic acid amplification when available), cytology, and consideration of common transudative etiologies (especially heart failure) and endocrine causes (such as hypothyroidism) are essential in the evaluation of unexplained pleural effusion in TB-endemic settings. The absence of pleural biopsy/pathology in our case contributed to diagnostic anchoring and delayed recognition of POEMS syndrome.

The diagnosis of POEMS syndrome poses a challenge due to its rare occurrence and complex clinical symptoms.And its diagnostic criteria are based on the current Dispenzieri diagnosis [3]. The main manifestation of POEMS syndrome is peripheral neuropathy, which may be accompanied by multiple plasma cavities, sclerotic bone damage, and organomegaly [3]. POEMS-associated neuropathy may resemble chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy; however, the two conditions can be distinguished by their clinical profiles and characteristic patterns of nerve conduction abnormalities [5]. However, POEMS syndrome may also be characterized by a variety of atypical presentations, among which polyserous effusions is commonly reported [6–10]. Wang W [8] reported a 69-year-old woman whose condition mirrored our patient’s, initially misdiagnosed as tuberculous peritonitis because of abdominal swelling and previous tuberculosis exposure. After the anti-tuberculosis therapy proved ineffective, further detailed investigations ultimately confirmed a diagnosis of POEMS syndrome.She was treated with a combination of lenalidomide (25 mg/day) and dexamethasone (40 mg/week), leading to significant improvement and her subsequent discharge.A retrospective study found that of 282 cases of POEMS syndrome, 40.1% presented with respiratory symptoms, of which 56.4% presented with pleural effusion [4]. So patients with POEMS syndrome do not often present with a pleural effusion as their first manifestation, but the presence of a pleural effusion is not an uncommon condition.

The patient presented with diabetes and hypothyroidism one year ago, but it was not taken seriously by the physician. Endocrine disruption can be a major manifestation of POEMS syndrome. Zhou GX [11] reported a case of a 54-year-old woman with diabetic foot as her primary presentation.Before being admitted to the hospital for the second time, the patient experienced bone pain. Likewise, bone lesion warrants serious consideration. Wang Y [12] reported a case of a patient with POEMS syndrome with bone lesions as the main presentation.

This case emphasizes that in the diagnostic process of tuberculosis, we cannot rely on clinical diagnosis alone.Pathological analysis is paramount for making a definitive diagnosis. Peripheral neuropathy warrants broad diagnostic consideration beyond drug reactions to avoid misdiagnosis. In the diagnosis and treatment of diseases, a “monistic” mindset is essential, as we should look at the disease from a holistic perspective and consider multiple symptoms in order to improve the efficiency of diagnosis. POEMS syndrome can be mainly characterized by pleural effusion. When the cause of pleural effusion is unknown, POEMS syndrome should be suspected.These observations underscore the need for a structured diagnostic approach integrating pleural fluid interpretation, microbiology/cytology, and pathology when feasible.
