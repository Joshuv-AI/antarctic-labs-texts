Exposure of unmonitored workers to ionizing radiation due to portable X-ray machine use and area dose mapping in an emergency department in King Abdulaziz Medical City, Jeddah
Authors: Ahmad Subahi, Anas Bokhari, Atheer AlSulami, Ajwad Alwadani, Mashael Alqurashi, Rania Al-Sharif, Mohamed Allabban, Khalid Alshamrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo quantify the exposure of unmonitored workers to radiation and perform dose mapping in selected emergency room (ER) department areas during portable X-ray examinations at King Abdulaziz Medical City, Jeddah.MethodsThis prospective observational study was conducted over a 3-week period in the ER at King Abdulaziz Medical City, Jeddah. In total, 39 unmonitored healthcare workers comprising 18 nurses and 21 respiratory therapists (RTs) were monitored using optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dosimeters. Additional OSL dosimeters were placed in fixed ER locations to assess scattered radiation in selected areas. Net badge values were obtained after subtracting available baseline or background readings before annualization. Annualization was conducted to allow comparisons with previous studies that reported annual dose estimates.ResultsThe mean estimated annualized worker dose was 0.50 ± 0.21 mSv/year for nurses and 0.40 ± 0.30 mSv/year for RTs, and there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.51). The mean estimated annualized area dose across the monitored ER locations was 0.70 ± 0.38 mSv/year.ConclusionIn the present study, measurable but modest annualized dose estimates were found in unmonitored workers and similarly low annualized area dose estimates in monitored ER locations. These findings support the value of periodic local dose audits, staff education, reinforcement of safe positioning during portable imaging, and continued monitoring to strengthen radiation protection practice in emergency care settings. These estimates were derived based on a relatively short monitoring period, so they should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Introduction

Portable X-ray imaging remains an essential diagnostic tool in acute and critical care because it enables bedside assessment for patients who are unstable, immobile, or dependent on continuous monitoring.1, 2, 3, 4 The use of portable X-ray imaging is especially common in emergency room (ER) department and intensive care settings, where timely imaging is often required during active clinical management.1,2,5 In contrast to fixed radiography rooms, portable X-ray examinations are performed in open clinical environments where the shielding conditions are less controlled, and healthcare workers, nearby patients, and accompanying persons may remain close to the patient during image acquisition.1,6, 7, 8 These settings lead to radiation protection concerns that differ from those in conventional radiology settings.

In general, diagnostic radiography involves relatively low radiation doses, but ionizing radiation remains a recognized occupational and environmental hazard because the interaction between the primary beam and patient generates scattered radiation.2,6,9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Scattered radiation may contribute to exposure for radiographers but also for personnel who are not formally classified as radiation workers, including nurses, respiratory therapists (RTs), ER staff, and other unmonitored workers who may be present in the examination area.1,5,14, 15, 16 In the present study, the term unmonitored worker refers to a member of healthcare staff who is not formally classified as an occupational radiation worker but whose routine clinical duties may place them near portable radiographic examinations. These workers may remain close to the patient to provide clinical support, maintain monitoring equipment, or assist with urgent care, thereby increasing the possibility of repeated low level exposure during bedside imaging.1,5,14,16

Previous studies have examined radiation exposure associated with mobile radiography in ERs, adult intensive care units (ICUs), neonatal ICUs, pediatric units, and post-anesthetic care areas.1,3, 4, 14,7,7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13,17, 18, 19, 20, 16, 21, 22, 23 Most of these studies indicate that staff and nearby occupant doses are generally low when standard radiation protection principles are followed, particularly when adequate distance from the patient is maintained.3,9,11, 12, 13,18,20 However, exposure may vary according to the workload, staff position, distance from the source, examination frequency, room geometry, and local work practices.1,6, 14, 5,14,15,19,21,24 Therefore, recent studies have emphasized the value of measuring the scatter distribution and staff position-related exposure during portable radiography and bedside chest imaging.25,26

In addition to personal exposure monitoring, area dose assessment has become increasingly important for understanding how scattered radiation is distributed within real healthcare environments.4,4, 7, 8,1924 Spatial dose mapping studies have demonstrated that scattered radiation is not uniform and may differ substantially depending on the beam orientation, patient size, bed position, examination type, and layout of the surrounding space.4,7, 8,24 This issue is particularly important in ERs, where staff workstations, corridors, patient bays, and adjacent occupied areas may be located close to the site of exposure.1,5 Therefore, evaluating both worker exposure and the area dose distribution can provide a more complete assessment of radiation safety than considering either in isolation.4,6, 4, 7, 8,19

Despite the growing number of studies in this area, important gaps remain. Much of the available evidence was acquired from ICU, neonatal, pediatric, or simulation based settings rather than routine adult ER practice.2, 3, 4,14, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 17,18, 8, 20,20 In addition, few studies have combined staff category dose assessment with fixed area dose mapping in the same ER environment, even though this approach can help identify exposure patterns and support local radiation protection measures.1,6, 4, 5, 7, 8,19,24 Recent review evidence also indicates that staff knowledge, safety practices, and workflow influence exposure patterns, reinforcing the need for setting specific assessments.2 In the present study, a novel approach was employed by combining unmonitored worker dose assessment with fixed area dose mapping in a real ER setting during routine portable X-ray practice.

This issue is particularly relevant at King Abdulaziz Medical City, Jeddah (KAMC-J), where local data are limited regarding radiation exposure among unmonitored workers and surrounding ER areas during portable X-ray use. The distribution of radiation may be influenced by the local room design, workflow, patient throughput, and staff proximity to bedside imaging, so findings obtained in other hospitals cannot be assumed to apply directly to this setting. Accordingly, it is important to measure dose levels in both personnel and nearby areas in order to provide local evidence, evaluate exposure patterns within departments, and support radiation protection practice.

Thus, the aims of the present study were to assess radiation exposure among unmonitored workers in the ER at KAMC-J and perform area dose mapping associated with portable X-ray machine use, thereby providing local evidence for improving radiation protection, supporting safer clinical workflow, and identifying practical areas where staff positioning and local monitoring practices may be strengthened.

Materials and Methods

This study was conducted in the ER at KAMC-J, KSA. The overall project lasted from August 2020 to August 2021, but radiation dose data were collected prospectively over a 3-week monitoring period beginning in November 2020. The study was designed as a prospective observational study to evaluate radiation exposure among unmonitored healthcare workers and assess scattered radiation levels in selected ER areas during portable X-ray examinations, in line with previously published approaches used for staff exposure assessment and environmental scatter evaluation during mobile radiography.4,5,7,8,14,15,19,24

The study population comprised unmonitored healthcare workers in the ER, specifically nurses and RTs who worked 12-h shifts. Other staff categories were excluded. In total, 39 eligible unmonitored healthcare workers were included in the final analysis comprising 18 nurses and 21 RTs. Participants were recruited using consecutive sampling according to their availability and eligibility during the monitoring period.

Radiation dose measurements were obtained using optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dosimeters. In total, 18 OSL dosimeters were used in the study, where 13 dosimeters were used for worker monitoring and five were allocated for fixed area monitoring. The 13 dosimeters for monitoring workers were shared between nurses and RTs during portable X-ray examinations, where six were used for nursing staff and seven for RTs. These dosimeters were not assigned permanently to individual participants throughout the study period, but instead they were used during scan related duties and handed over between teams when required. Therefore, the resulting worker dose values represent staff category exposure during scan-related duties rather than exact individual cumulative occupational exposure amounts. During use, workers were instructed to wear the dosimeter on the left upper side of the chest. The use of personal dosimeters to assess staff exposure during mobile radiography has been reported in previous hospital based studies of occupational and nearby staff exposure.4,5,14,15,19,24

For environmental monitoring, five OSL dosimeters were placed in fixed locations within the ER for the full 3-week period to assess scattered radiation levels. These locations were corridor 1, corridor 2, the reception area, toilet area, and hand washing area. As shown in Figure 1, the fixed OSL monitoring locations were distributed across selected occupied and nearby ER spaces during portable X-ray practice. Each dosimeter was labeled with a unique identification number to facilitate tracking and analysis. This fixed location approach was selected to characterize the spatial distribution of scattered radiation within the department, consistent with previously reported methods for area dose mapping and scatter distribution assessment in clinical settings.4,6, 7, 8,19

The dosimeters on workers were read weekly for 3 consecutive weeks by the Radiation Safety Office at KAMC-J. The area dosimeters were collected and read at the end of the 3-week monitoring period beginning on November 12, 2020. Measured doses obtained during the monitoring period were annualized based on the observed frequency of portable X-ray procedures during the 3 weeks. Net badge values were calculated after subtracting available baseline or background readings before annualization. Background correction was performed using the available baseline or pre-use badge readings, and the corrected net badge values were then annualized using the same procedure applied throughout the study. Annualization was performed to facilitate comparisons with previous studies and radiation protection frameworks that report annual dose estimates. These values were extrapolated from a 3-week monitoring period, so they should be interpreted as approximate annualized estimates rather than direct annual measurements.

The estimated annualized dose values for unmonitored workers were interpreted descriptively within the study context and alongside relevant international radiation protection principles. Area dose values were also interpreted as estimated annualized levels for the monitored ER locations rather than as direct long-term measurements.

Data were entered and analyzed using JMP software. Continuous variables were summarized as the mean and standard deviation when normally distributed, and median and interquartile range when distributional assumptions were not met. Categorical variables were represented as frequencies and percentages. The main study variables were the estimated annualized worker dose among unmonitored workers and estimated annualized area dose in the selected ER areas. Distributional assumptions were assessed before statistical testing. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test before conducting independent t-tests. An independent t-test was conducted to compare worker dose values between nurses and RTs, and the result was interpreted with caution because of the small number of category level badge readings. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Discussion

In the present study, radiation exposure was evaluated among unmonitored workers in the ER at KAMC-J, and area dose mapping was conducted in selected locations exposed to scatter from portable X-ray examinations. After subtracting baseline or background readings before annualization, the mean worker dose was 0.50 ± 0.21 mSv/year for nurses and 0.40 ± 0.30 mSv/year for RTs, and there was no statistically significant difference between categories (p = 0.51). The mean estimated annualized area dose across the monitored ER locations was 0.70 ± 0.38 mSv/year.

These findings indicate that both worker and area dose estimates were low in the ER setting in the present study, and they confirmed the presence of measurable exposure associated with portable radiography. This pattern is important because fixed area measurements reflect environmental scatter within selected ER locations, whereas the worker badge results represent staff category exposure during scan-related duties rather than exact individual occupational exposure amounts. In the present study, nurses and RTs worked 12-h shifts and may have remained near patients during image acquisition or preparation. Accordingly, workers may have experienced exposure patterns that differed from those indicated by the fixed area measurements because of their closer and more variable proximity to patients during portable imaging. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted descriptively within the study context, particularly because international radiation protection frameworks distinguish different exposure categories.25

The small, statistically non-significant difference between nurses and RTs may reflect differences in their clinical roles, proximity to patients, time spent at the bedside, and frequency of involvement during portable X-ray procedures. Nurses had a slightly higher estimated mean dose, but the spread of values was greater for RTs. Worker dosimeters were shared during scan-related duties, so this comparison should be interpreted as based on a staff category estimate rather than precise personal dosimetry.

The area mapping results showed that the estimated annualized area dose was highest for corridor 1 at 1.33 mSv/year, followed by the hand washing area and toilet at 0.67 mSv/year, reception at 0.50 mSv/year, and corridor 2 at 0.33 mSv/year. The variation across sites indicates that scattered radiation was not distributed uniformly throughout the department. In addition to the local room geometry and arrangement of surrounding spaces, this pattern may reflect the ER workflow, patient movement, staff traffic, and frequency of portable imaging near each monitored location. Corridor 1 may have been more exposed because it functioned as a common movement pathway near areas where portable imaging was repeated, whereas corridor 2 may have experienced lower exposure because of less frequent traffic or greater separation from typical examination positions. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies that found variations in the scatter distribution according to the examination setting, distance, and spatial position within the clinical environment.6, 7, 8,19,24

The findings obtained in the present study can be interpreted in the context of previous hospital-based studies of portable radiography scatter exposure. Fernández et al. reported patient and staff dosimetry during radiographic procedures in an ICU and found dose levels below the limit discussed in their study.4 Divatia et al. evaluated radiation exposure among medical professionals working in an ICU and reported a mean annual exposure of 0.4 mSv among resident doctors, and area readings were described as almost immeasurable.14 In an ER based cohort study, Kim reported radiation doses of 0.36 mSv for nurses, 0.25 mSv for doctors, and 0.85 mSv for emergency medical technicians during the study period.5 Mourão et al. also assessed personal doses associated with mobile X-ray use in a Brazilian hospital and emphasized the importance of monitoring hospital personnel exposed during bedside radiography.15 More recent studies have continued to show that the scatter distribution and staff exposure during portable radiography depend greatly on the staff position, distance, and local workflow.26,27 Compared to these studies, the corrected worker and area dose estimates in the present study were within a similarly low range overall, although direct comparisons should still be made with caution because the monitoring duration, badge allocation, correction methods, and reporting units differed across studies.

Several factors may explain the variations in the corrected dose estimates observed in the present study. First, ER workflow may involve bedside imaging under crowded and time-sensitive conditions, which could influence the proximity of staff to patients during exposure. Second, the physical design of the ER and arrangement of the work areas may influence the scatter distribution in a different manner compared with ICUs or other hospital settings.4,5,14,15 Third, the annualized estimates were derived over a 3-week monitoring period in the present study. This monitoring period reflected routine clinical activity without any known changes to portable imaging practice, but its representativeness of the full annual workload could not be independently verified. Finally, the use of shared worker dosimeters during scan-related duties reflected category-based exposure during imaging tasks rather than individual occupational patterns.

The interpretation of these findings should be treated with appropriate caution. In particular, the results reflect measurable annualized dose estimates for unmonitored workers and selected ER areas within the study setting. In addition, these estimates were derived over a relatively short monitoring period and based on shared OSL dosimeters used during scan-related duties rather than permanently assigned individual badges. Accordingly, it is most appropriate to interpret these findings as staff category exposure estimates and fixed location environmental scatter measurements within the study setting, rather than exact personal annual dose values.

The present study has several strengths. In particular, this study addressed a clinically relevant and under-examined issue in a real ER environment, included both worker monitoring and area dose mapping, and used OSL dosimeters processed by the in-house Radiation Safety Office at KAMC-J, thereby supporting the technical reliability of the measurements. The novel aspect of this study is that it combined assessment of unmonitored worker exposure and fixed area dose mapping during routine portable X-ray practice in an adult ER setting. However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. The monitoring period was relatively short, the sample size was modest, the annualized dose estimates relied on short term measurements, and worker monitoring was based on shared rather than individually assigned dosimeters. The statistical comparison between nurses and RTs should also be interpreted with caution because it was based on shared category-level badge readings rather than repeated measurements from individually assigned dosimeters. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Overall, the results obtained in the present study suggest that scattered radiation from portable X-ray examinations in the ER resulted in measurable but low dose estimates in both workers and monitored ER areas. These findings support continued attention to radiation protection practice in the ER, including staff awareness, optimization of positioning during portable imaging, periodic local dose audits, reinforcement of appropriate staff positioning during image acquisition, and consideration of targeted monitoring in high use emergency areas. Recent evidence continues to support the value of position-specific scatter assessment and dose optimization during portable radiography.26,27

Conclusion

In the present study, evaluations were conducted to assess radiation exposure among unmonitored workers in the ER at KAMC-J, and area dose mapping associated with portable X-ray examinations. After correcting for baseline or background readings, the findings showed that dose estimates for nurses and RTs were low during the monitoring period and dose levels were also low in the monitored ER areas. However, these estimates were derived from a relatively short monitoring period and shared category level dosimetry, so they should be interpreted with caution rather than as exact individual annual occupational dose measurements. Overall, the findings obtained in this study highlight the importance of monitoring both worker exposure and the environmental dose distribution in emergency care settings. These findings support periodic local dose audits, reinforcement of appropriate staff positioning during portable imaging, and consideration of targeted monitoring in high use emergency areas to strengthen radiation protection practice.
