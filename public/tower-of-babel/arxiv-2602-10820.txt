Adaptive Sampling for Private Worst-Case Group Optimization
Max Cairney-Leeming, Amartya Sanyal, Christoph H. Lampert

Abstract
Models trained by minimizing the average loss often fail to be accurate on small or hard-to-learn groups of the data. Various methods address this issue by optimizing a weighted objective that focuses on the worst-performing groups. However, this approach becomes problematic when learning with differential privacy, as unequal data weighting can result in inhomogeneous privacy guarantees, in particular weaker privacy for minority groups. In this work, we introduce a new algorithm for differentially private worst-case group optimization called ASC (Adaptively Sampled and Clipped Worst-case Group Optimization) . It adaptively controls both the sampling rate and the clipping threshold of each group. Thereby, it allows for harder-to-learn groups to be sampled more often while ensuring consistent privacy guarantees across all groups. Comparing ASC to prior work, we show that it results in lower-variance gradients, tighter privacy guarantees, and substantially higher worst-case group accuracy without sacrificing overall average accuracy.



1
Introduction

Making machine learning systems
trustworthy
requires effort in many different directions, including
privacy
and
fairness

(
Tabassi 2023
)
.
Privacy means that the individuals who contribute
data to the training set should have a guarantee that their data is not
disclosed to others, not even indirectly through model weights or model outputs.
Such guarantees can be provided by enforcing
differential privacy (DP)

in the training process
(
Dwork 2006
)
.

Fairness means that the trained system should not show discriminative behavior.
In particular, it should provide as-high-as-possible utility for any
demographic subgroup, not just on average across the whole population.
In this work, we adopt Rawls’ max-min criterion
(
Rawls 1974
)
,
under which the goal of training is to maximize the minimum accuracy
across groups
(
Martinez et al. 2020
)
.

Methods for differentially private model training are well developed, with
the algorithm of choice usually being DP-SGD
(
Abadi et al. 2016
)
. Training for the max-min criterion has also received ample attention,
specifically by employing
distributionally robust optimization (DRO)

(
Sagawa et al. 2020
;
Duchi & Namkoong 2021
;
Levy et al. 2020
)
.
Unfortunately, in practice both techniques have been found to be in
conflict with each other
(
Bagdasaryan et al. 2019
;
Sanyal et al. 2022
)
.
DRO assigns higher weights to data points in worse-performing
groups to steer the training process in their favor.
Differentially private learning hides the specific contribution of
individual data points by adding noise at a scale proportional to
their contribution to the model updates. Combined, this scheme causes more noise to be added to the gradient
signal of (higher-weighted) low-accuracy groups than for the (lower-weighted)
high-accuracy groups.
This increased noise makes it even harder to train a good model
for low-accuracy groups, especially if these are
minorities
,
i.e.
only make up a small fraction of the full training set.

Zhou & Bassily 2024
proposed an algorithm
to mitigate this problem by sampling data batches in a non-uniform way.
However, their focus was theoretical, and they did not study the
algorithm’s practical applicability.
In this work,
we propose ASC (Adaptively Sampled and Clipped Worst-case Group Optimization), a practical solution
for learning max-min fair classifiers
that
overcomes the limitations above by data-dependent sampling
and gradient clipping.
Specifically, our contributions are the following:

•

we introduce ASC as a practical learning algorithm for
differentially private DRO that consists of easily implementable (DP-SGD-style) steps,

•

we prove that ASC can provide equally strong differential
privacy guarantee for all participating groups, regardless of how
much training data they contribute,

•

we prove that ASC’s gradient updates have
lower expected variance than those of previous works, thereby
making optimization easier,

•

we show experimentally that ASC yields models with higher worst-case group accuracy than several baselines.

Background

We study a
supervised learning task with group information
, i.e.
each data point is also annotated with a group label at training time.
Formally, for an input set
\mathcal{X}
, an output set
\mathcal{Y}
, and a set of
groups,
[G]=\{1,\dots,G\}
, we assume a data distribution
P_{g}

over
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
for each group.
At training time, a dataset
D_{g}\sim P_{g}
of size
n_{g}
is given
for each
g\in[G]
, from which we form an overall training set,

D=\bigcup_{g=1}^{G}D_{g}
, of size
n=\sum_{g=1}^{G}n_{g}
.
At prediction time, group labels are not available to the model.

Worst-case group optimization.
For a class of models parametrized by
\theta\in\Theta
, let

\operatorname{acc}(\theta,S)
denote the accuracy of a model
1
1

1

For
conciseness, we will identify models and their parameters.

\theta
on a dataset
S
.
For any model
\theta
we are interested not only in its quality on
the whole dataset,
\operatorname{acc}(\theta,D)
, but also in the lowest accuracy
it achieves on any group,

\operatorname{WGA}(\theta,D):=\min_{g\in[G]}\operatorname{acc}(\theta,D_{g}),

(1)

which we call the
worst-case group accuracy (WGA)
.

To train models for best
\operatorname{WGA}
, we change the maximization of
\operatorname{acc}
, which is a discrete, binary function, into a minimization over a continuous loss
function,
\ell(z,\theta)
, and we relax the minimization
over groups into a linear program over per-group weights. The final optimization problem is then given by:

\min_{\theta}\max_{\lambda\in\Delta}\quad\sum_{g=1}^{G}\Big[\frac{\lambda_{g}}{n_{g}}\!\sum_{z\in D_{g}}\ell(z,\theta)\Big],

(2)

where
\Delta=\{\lambda\in[0,1]^{G}:\sum_{g}\lambda_{g}=1\}

is the probability simplex.
Note that solving
Eq.

2
will
identify the worst-case (highest loss) group because the objective function is a linear program in
\lambda
, so its optimum will
be attained at a corner of the simplex.

This approach is also known as
group distributionally
robust optimization (group DRO)

(
Sagawa et al. 2020
)
, as it is equivalent to minimizing the
training loss not for a fixed data distribution, but across
all mixture distributions with components
(P_{g})_{g\in[G]}
, parameterized by
\lambda
.

Differentially private training.

Following the seminal work of
Dwork 2006
,
we consider an algorithm differentially private, if one cannot determine from its
output if any specific data point was part of the input or not,
except with a certain probability that quantifies the privacy level.

Formally, let
\mathcal{M}
be a randomized mechanism that takes as input
a dataset,
S
, and outputs
o\in\mathcal{O}
.
For any
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\in[0,1]
,
\mathcal{M}
is
called
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private (DP)
,
if
\Pr(\mathcal{M}(S)\in O)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr(\mathcal{M}(S^{\prime})\in O)+\delta

for every
O\subset\mathcal{O}
and any two datasets
S
and
S^{\prime}

that are
neighboring
under the ‘replace one’ definition, i.e.
they are identical except for one element.

In this work, we additionally make use of

Rényi Differential Privacy (RDP)

(
Mironov 2017
)
:
For any
\alpha>1
and
\varepsilon>0
, a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is called

(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-RDP if
D_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(S)\|\mathcal{M}(S^{\prime}))\leq\varepsilon

for any two neighboring datasets
S
and
S^{\prime}
, where
D_{\alpha}(P,Q):=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{o\sim Q}\big(\frac{P(o)}{Q(o)}\big)^{\alpha}

is the Rényi divergence of order
\alpha
.
For this work, we will generally treat the value
\alpha>0
as
arbitrary but fixed.further on.
Rényi DP makes it possible to prove tighter bounds for algorithms which compose many mechanisms, and also offers simpler characterization of the privacy guarantee of the commonly used Gaussian mechanism. Specifically, if
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
are two mechanisms that
are
\varepsilon_{1}
-RDP and
\varepsilon_{2}
-RDP, respectively, then
their composition
(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})
is
(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
-RDP,
even if
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is chosen only after the output of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is
known. It is common practice to report final guarantees in terms of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, and RDP guarantees can be easily converted to this. Further details can found in
Appendix

A
.

Analogously to
Jorgensen et al. 2015
,
we also consider a more refined notion of group-specific privacy:
for any
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{G}>0
, a mechanism
\mathcal{M}

is
(\alpha,(\varepsilon_{g})_{g\in[G]})
-GroupRDP, if
D_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(S)\|\mathcal{M}(S^{\prime}))\leq\varepsilon_{g}
,
for any two neighboring datasets
S
and
S^{\prime}
, for which the
element they differ in belongs to group
g
.

3
Adaptively Sampled and Clipped Worst-case Group Optimization

In this section, we introduce our main contribution,
the ASC algorithm, and we establish its privacy guarantees.

ASC combines design elements of DRO and DP-SGD:
it minimizes an adaptively reweighted average loss,
and it enforces privacy by appropriately clipping gradients and adding noise.

Algorithm

2
depicts ASC in pseudocode, which we will now discuss in detail.
A more detailed comparison to the prior work of

Zhou & Bassily 2024

will follow in
Section

4
.

At its core, ASC follows the DRO scheme of
optimizing over a mixture of per-group losses,
where the weights are chosen adaptively to give more
importance to the currently worst performing group.
In non-private algorithms, this is done by simply summing the losses with larger weights for worse-performing groups.
Within a private algorithm, this approach would require stronger additive noise.
We avoid this by a combination of adaptively reweighting the sampling scheme, and balancing the clipping threshold to stay within the privacy budget.

Adaptive Sampling.

Given the target size
M
of a training batch, the algorithm splits
M
into integer batch sizes
m=(m_{g})_{g\in[G]}
for each group, with the goal of adjusting the learning objective to focus on harder groups by sampling more data points from them.
Line
5
performs stratified sampling according to these batch sizes, by taking
m_{g}
examples without replacement from
D_{g}
for each group
g
.

Balanced Clipping.

For each group
g
, the gradients on batch
B_{g}
are clipped to a group-specific threshold

\xi_{g}=\sigma/\kappa_{\alpha}(m_{g}/n_{g},\varepsilon^{s}_{g})

in line
6
. This threshold is set so that the noise multiplier on these gradients,
\sigma/\xi_{g}
increases (resp. decreases) when
m_{g}
increases (decreases), so that the contribution of
m_{g}
and
\xi_{g}
to the privacy loss balance out to give a consistent
(\alpha,\varepsilon^{s})
-RDP privacy guarantee, regardless of how
m_{g}
changes.

These two concepts form the core of our algorithm, and both adaptive sampling and clipping are necessary together to be able to prove that this algorithm is private.

The clipped gradients from all of the groups are summed with Gaussian noise (variance
\sigma^{2}
) in line
8
to update the model weights.
Note that the noise is added to the sum of the group-wise updates,
instead of to each group separately, reducing the total amount of DP noise needed. This is possible because the
clipping thresholds of the update vectors are designed such that
all group updates require the same strength noise to achieve
privacy.

The rest of the algorithm deals with adjusting the per-group
batch sizes.
Line
13
estimates the average losses on the training dataset using batches sampled in line
12

for each group. These losses are computed on sensitive data, so if they were used to reweight
\lambda
as-is, privacy leakage would occur. They are privately released using clipping to a threshold
\zeta
and Gaussian noise of variance
\tau^{2}
. By the post-processing property of DP, the losses
\{L\}_{g\in[G]}
can be used to exponentially re-weight the group batch sizes (line 12).
These are then normalized and rounded to integer values (detailed in
Appendix

B
),
m_{g}
, that sum up to
M
(line
16
).
Since these steps are computationally demanding, and also involve private
information, we execute them only every
k\in\mathbb{N}
steps (e.g.
once per epoch). Note, also, that the reweighting is less sensitive to the scale of
\tau^{2}
than the model update is to
\sigma^{2}
, so
\tau^{2}
can be set significantly higher than
\sigma^{2}
to reduce the privacy cost of the reweighting.

4
Relation to Prior Work

Several works in the literature have studied the
problem of training models with max-min fairness
using distributionally robust optimization, e.g.

Sagawa et al. 2020
;
Duchi & Namkoong 2021

introduce algorithms and
Barocas et al. 2023

discusses the broader relation to algorithmic fairness.
Learning with differential privacy has found even
more attention in the literature, both in classical machine learning
(
Ji et al. 2014
)
, and deep learning
(
Ponomareva et al. 2023
;
El Ouadrhiri & Abdelhadi 2022
;
Demelius et al. 2025
)
.
However, research at their intersection is still at its infancy.

The prior work most related to ours is

Zhou & Bassily 2024
,
where three possibilities to perform differentially
private DRO are discussed: two of these are not
applicable to the setting we study, because they
rely on techniques that are impractical for
general-purpose (deep) learning tasks:
output
perturbation

(
Chaudhuri et al. 2011
)

and
private online convex optimization

(
Jain et al. 2012
)
.
The third,
NoisySGD-MGR
, is based on DP-SGD and
therefore readily applicable to deep learning.
We compare it to ASC conceptually in this section, and experimentally in Section
6
.

Like ASC,
Zhou & Bassily 2024
’s
algorithm rely on the general DRO strategy of optimizing over a
mixture of per-group losses, adaptively giving more emphasis to
data from currently low-accuracy groups.
In contrast to ASC, it does not vary the composition of
batches, but instead maintains a set of per-group sampling
probabilities
\lambda=(\lambda_{g})_{g\in[G]}
. In every training
step
t
, it randomly chooses one group,
g
according
to a categorical distribution
\operatorname{Categorical}(\lambda)

and samples a fixed-size batch from the group’s dataset
D_{g}
.
It then computes the model updates by clipping the gradients
to a fixed threshold
\xi
and adding noise of strength

\sigma^{2}
.

Like in ASC, the weights are derived by an exponential
weighting of the per-group losses. However, no clipping is
performed, and Laplacian noise is used to privatize the
weights instead of Gaussian.
Because of the lack of clipping, the original
NoisySGD-MGR

algorithm is only private for bounded loss functions.
This rules out many common choices, such as cross-entropy.
However, this shortcoming is easily overcome by introducing
a clipping step, as we do in ASC.
The Laplacian noise, rather than Gaussian, gives
pure

(\varepsilon,0)
-differential privacy, but this is less easily combined with RDP guarantees of the module updates, and has heavier tails.
In this work, we therefore discuss an adapted
version of the algorithm that uses the subsampled Gaussian mechanism with
clipping for the weight update step. We refer to it as
aZB (for
adapted

Zhou & Bassily 2024
).

For practical use, a problem of the algorithm is that it is derived
and analyzed under the unrealistic assumption of
balanced
groups
, i.e.
all groups have identically sized datasets ,
n_{g}=n/G
.
Re-analying the method for an unbalanced setting (i.e.
different
group sizes), results in the conclusion that each model update step
would achieve per-group RDP guarantee of
\varepsilon_{\alpha}(M/n_{g},\sigma/\xi)
, i.e.
groups of different sizes get guarantees of different strengths, as
\sigma
and
\xi
are fixed.
The overall privacy guarantee is
\varepsilon
-RDP with

\varepsilon=T\varepsilon_{\alpha}(M/n_{\text{min}},\sigma/\xi)+\lfloor\frac{T}{k}\rfloor\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\gamma^{\ell},\tau/\zeta)

for
n_{\text{min}}=\min_{g}n_{g}
, i.e.
the overall
privacy guarantee is determined by the group with
the smallest dataset size. A formal statement is proved in
Appendix

C
.

We also introduce two variants of this model for use as baselines. Firstly, aZB-weak sets the parameters such that the targeted guarantee
only
holds for the
largest groups
, because the noise multiplier needed to achieve privacy protection for the smallest group can be too high to learn effectively.
Clearly, one can expect aZB-weak to achieve
higher utility than aZB, but at the
expense of violating the privacy of minority
groups.

Secondly, we re-design the algorithm to use
a specific batch size
M_{g}
when sampling from group
g
, instead of
using
M
for all groups. Note that these batch sizes do not change over time.
Setting
M_{g}=M\cdot\frac{n_{g}}{n}
(up to rounding)
for all
g\in[G]
, i.e.
proportional to the groups’ dataset
sizes, ensures that the
sampling proportions
remain approximately
equal between groups, giving equal privacy guarantees.
Besides the increased implementation effort of having randomly
determined batch sizes in each step, another downside of this
approach is that small groups receive update vectors computed
from fewer data points, which results in increased variance.
We refer to this variant as aZB-prop, and compare ASC to it, aZB-weak, and aZB in
Sections

5
and
6
.

Sampling Variance

All training algorithms we discussed follow an
SGD-style

2

2

All algorithm could readily be adapted

scheme. As such, their convergence properties

of their respective

(

Chen et al. 2020

which are stochastic due to the sampling of data batches and the
added random noise.

The additive noise contribution is a direct consequence of the
assigned privacy budget, and can be directly read off from the
algorithm.
In contrast, the variance due to sampling depends on the specific
batch construction, so it requires a more detailed analysis.

In the following, we study the sampling variance of ASC in
relation to aZB and its variants. For this, we consider the
algorithms’ behaviors at an arbitrary point in training, with
model weights
\theta
, and group probabilities
(\lambda_{g})_{g\in[G]}

(for aZB, aZB-weak, and aZB-prop) or batch sizes
(m_{g})_{g\in[G]}
for ASC.
To allow comparability, we assume all algorithms currently have the
same per-group weighting, i.e.

m_{g}=M\cdot\lambda_{g}
.
For any
S
, we denote by
\mathcal{L}(S):=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{z\in S}\ell(z,\theta)
the
average loss over the data points in
S
with respect to the current model parameters
\theta
,
and by
U(S):=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{z\in S}\nabla\ell(z,\theta)
its gradient.

The target objective of the next DRO optimization step is
\mathcal{L}^{\text{DRO}}:=\sum_{g\in[G]}\lambda_{g}\mathcal{L}(D_{g})
,
i.e.
the weighted average of the average loss value on the individual
groups’ dataset. Consequently, the optimal (non-stochastic) gradient update
would be

U^{\text{DRO}}:=\sum\nolimits_{g\in[G]}\lambda_{g}U(D_{g}).

(4)

For any dataset
S
and
m\leq|S|
, let
\sample(m,S)

be an operator that samples a batch of size
m
from
S

without replacement.
ASC’s stochastic update can be written as

\displaystyle U^{\text{ASC }}

\displaystyle=U\Big({\textstyle\bigcup\nolimits_{g\in[G]}}\sample(m_{g},D_{g})\Big).

(5)

For aZB and aZB-weak, the updates are

\displaystyle U^{\text{aZB }}

\displaystyle=U(\sample(M,D_{g}))\text{ for $g\sim\text{Cat}(\lambda)$}

(6)

(these are identical, because both methods rely on the same update vector
and differ only in the amount of Gaussian noise that is added to it). For aZB-prop the

\displaystyle U^{\text{aZB-prop }}

\displaystyle=U(\sample(m_{g},D_{g}))\text{ for $g\sim\text{Cat}(\lambda)$}.

(7)

It is easy to check that all of these updates are unbiased estimates of the DRO update, i.e.

\mathbb{E}[U^{\text{ASC }}]=\mathbb{E}[U^{\text{aZB }}]=\mathbb{E}[U^{\text{aZB-prop }}]=U^{\text{DRO}}.

(8)

\operatorname{Var}(U)=\mathbb{E}\big\|U-U^{\text{DRO}}\big\|_{2}^{2}
.

Theorem 2

(name=Comparison of sampling variances,store=samplingVarianceThm)
.

For any group
g\in[G]
, let
\operatorname{Var}_{g}:=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim D_{g}}\big\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z,\theta)-U(D_{g})\big\|_{2}^{2}

be the within-group variance of the loss gradients. Then the sampling variance of ASC fulfills

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(U^{\text{ASC }})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{g\in[G]}\lambda_{g}\frac{n_{g}-m_{g}}{n_{g}-1}\operatorname{Var}_{g}.

(9)

In contrast, aZB and aZB-weak have sampling variances:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(U^{\text{aZB }})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{g\in[G]}\lambda_{g}\frac{n_{g}-M}{n_{g}-1}\operatorname{Var}_{g}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{g\in[G]}\lambda_{g}\|U(D_{g})-U^{\text{DRO}}\|_{2}^{2},

(10)

and aZB-prop has sampling variance:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(U^{\text{aZB-prop }})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in[G]}\frac{1}{m_{g}}\lambda_{g}\frac{n_{g}-m_{g}}{n_{g}-1}\operatorname{Var}_{g}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{g\in[G]}\lambda_{g}\|U(D_{g})-U^{\text{DRO}}\|_{2}^{2},

(11)

The proofs can be found in

Comparing the results in

its sampling variance is simply an importance-weighted

An analog term also appears for aZB in

For aZB-prop,

7
Conclusion and future directions

We have proposed ASC, a new algorithm for training differentially
private models with max-min fairness by means of distributionally robust
optimization.
ASC’s central idea is to sample data points from groups according to
their intended importance, while ensuring consistent privacy across all
group by clipping the resulting gradients with suitably-constructed thresholds.
By using group-mixed batches, the variance of the update steps is kept low
enough to make the algorithms practical for real-world learning tasks.
Our experiments on three datasets showed that this mechanism is able to clearly
improve the accuracy of the model for the worst-case group compared to
standard DP-SGD training and alternative private DRO algorithms.

In future work, we plan to generalize the mechanism to other forms of
distributionally robust optimization algorithms, e.g.
in the
context of multi-task or meta-learning, where multiple models are learned,
and different tasks can vary greatly in terms of their dataset
sizes and desired privacy guarantees.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine
Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none
which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Imbalanced groups are a natural phenomenon, as e.g.
it is expected that a small proportion of the population commits bank fraud, even though high accuracy on this group is actually very important.

We can see the problem in more detail if we turn to the the CelebA dataset, which has four groups, ranging in size from
1387
to
22880
images.
For all methods, we used a consistent batch size
b=256
(and also tried a smaller
b=36
specifically for aZB to help address this problem), giving a sampling rate
\gamma_{\text{min}}=256/1387=0.186
for the minority group. The noise multiplier
\sigma/\xi
is automatically calculated, like in Opacus
(
Yousefpour et al. 2022
)
, to give an
\varepsilon=1
guarantee for all groups. The result,
\sigma/\xi=570
, (respectively
\sigma/\xi=214
for
b=36
), is over
100
(resp.
40
) times higher than the noise multiplier for DPSGD,
5.08
, or that for our method and aZB-prop,
5.59
. This is extremely high, and has a severely negative effect on the training process.

One approach to fix this is aZB-prop, which shows much better results in experiments (
Section

6
), but has the highest sampling variance of all methods (
Theorem

2
).

We start with the more complicated derivation, for aZB and aZB-weak, beginning with the covariance matrix of
U^{\text{aZB }}
. Given group
g
was selected, define the update that would be performed by
U_{g}^{\text{aZB }}=U(\sample(M,D_{g}))
.

The following holds by the law of total covariance, applied to the fact that this sampling scheme is defined by a mixture distribution, with weights
\{\lambda_{g}\}_{g\in[G]}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(U^{\text{aZB }})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g}\lambda_{g}\operatorname{Cov}(U_{g}^{\text{aZB }})

(25)

\displaystyle+\sum_{g}\lambda_{g}(U(D_{g})-U^{\text{DRO}})(U(D_{g})-U^{\text{DRO}})^{\top}.

Next, we turn to the covariance of the average update on the sampled batch,
\operatorname{Cov}(U_{g}^{\text{aZB }})
, where the samples are drawn without replacement. This is covered by a simple lemma:

Lemma 3
.

Consider a batch of size
m
, represented by indices
\{I_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
, which is sampled without replacement from a population size
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
.

The population covariance matrix is given by
\operatorname{Cov}(v_{I})=\mathbb{E}[(v_{I}-\mathbb{E}[v_{I}])(v_{I}-\mathbb{E}[v_{I}])^{\top}]
for
I\sim\mathrm{Unif}[n]
, and the cross-covariance matrix is
K_{IJ}=\mathbb{E}[(v_{I}-\mathbb{E}[v_{I}])(v_{J}-\mathbb{E}[v_{J}])^{\top}]
for
I\neq J
drawn uniformly without replacement from
[n]
.

The covariance matrix of the batch sum
\operatorname{Cov}(\sum_{i\in[m]}v_{I_{i}})
is given by

\operatorname{Cov}\left(\sum_{i\in[m]}v_{I_{i}}\right)=m\cdot\frac{n-m}{n-1}\cdot\operatorname{Cov}(v_{I}).

(26)

Proof.

We start by expanding the batch sum’s covariance over the sum, giving us

\operatorname{Cov}\left(\sum_{i\in[m]}v_{I_{i}}\right)=m\cdot\operatorname{Cov}(v_{I})+(m^{2}-m)\cdot K_{IJ}.

(27)

K_{IJ}
is eliminated by considering a batch which is the same size
m=n
as the whole dataset, which obviously has no covariance as the only randomness is over the sampling scheme. Solving
\operatorname{Cov}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{I_{i}}\right)=\mathbf{0}
leads to
K_{IJ}=\frac{-1}{n-1}\operatorname{Cov}(v_{I})
and the result in the lemma statement.
∎

Thus,
\operatorname{Cov}(U_{g}^{\text{aZB }})=\frac{1}{M}\frac{n_{g}-M}{n_{g}-1}\operatorname{Cov}_{g}
, where
\operatorname{Cov}_{g}
is shorthand for
\operatorname{Cov}_{z\sim D_{g}}(\nabla_{\theta}(\ell(z,\theta)))
. Together, this gives us

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(U^{\text{aZB }})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g}\lambda_{g}\frac{1}{M}\frac{n_{g}-M}{n_{g}-1}\operatorname{Cov}_{g}

(28)

\displaystyle+\sum_{g}\lambda_{g}(U(D_{g})-U^{\text{DRO}})(U(D_{g})-U^{\text{DRO}})^{\top}.

\operatorname{Var}(U^{\text{aZB }})
and
\operatorname{Var}(U^{\text{aZB-weak }})

Eq.

28

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(U^{\text{aZB }})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g}\lambda_{g}\frac{1}{M}\frac{n_{g}-M}{n_{g}-1}\operatorname{Var}_{g}

(29)

\displaystyle+\sum_{g}\lambda_{g}\left\|U(D_{g})-U^{\text{DRO}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Note that the variance reduction given by using batch sizes larger than
1

The same proof holds for aZB-prop, except for the replacement of
M

m_{g}
throughout.

U^{\text{DRO}}
into the sums over the batch parts, which is possible as the sub-batches
B^{\text{ASC }}_{g}:=\sample(m_{g},D_{g})

M\cdot\lambda_{g}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(U^{\text{ASC }})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\|U^{\text{ASC }}-U^{\text{DRO}}\right\|^{2}

(30)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\|\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{g\in[G]}\sum_{z\in B^{\text{ASC }}_{g}}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z,\theta)\right)-\sum_{g\in[G]}\lambda_{g}U(D_{g})\right\|_{2}^{2}

(31)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{g\in[G]}\sum_{z\in B^{\text{ASC }}_{g}}\left(\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z,\theta)-U(D_{g})\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(32)

By the definition of
U(D_{g})
, the per-group sums
\sum_{z\in B^{\text{ASC }}_{g}}\left(\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z,\theta)-U(D_{g})\right)
have zero expectation, and are independent as they are sampled from different groups:

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{g\in[G]}\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{z\in B^{\text{ASC }}_{g}}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z,\theta)-U(D_{g})\right\|_{2}^{2}

(33)

Applying
Lemma

3

\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\|X\|_{2}^{2}=\operatorname{trace}(\operatorname{Cov}(X))
and the linearity of
\operatorname{trace}
, this simplifies to

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{g\in[G]}m_{g}\cdot\frac{n_{g}-m_{g}}{n_{g}-1}\operatorname{Var}_{g}

(34)

E.2
Choosing Hyperparameters and Seeds

Experimental baselines were not available for all of these datasets and methods under privacy, meaning that prior information on what parameters represent a reasonable learning scenario was lacking.
Thus, we designed a small sweep of plausible hyperparameter combinations (e.g.
learning, rate, momentum,…), and tested each method on all combinations, and selected the best combination for each method, measured by the worst group accuracy on the validation set (which was not used at any other point in our experiments).
Note that the hyperparameter sweep for aZB and aZB-weak also included an extra, smaller batch size for the Unbalanced MNIST and CelebA datasets, to include some runs with somewhat smaller noise multipliers. As the number of steps per epoch is calculated by taking
\text{dataset size}/\text{batch size}
, this results in more steps being taken overall, but with the same final privacy guarantee.
This hyperparameter sweep is not accounted for in our privacy guarantees, as it would incur the same privacy cost for all methods, and was not performed privately, as this would reduce the accuracy of our comparisons, if noise was introduced into selection. This means that our results leak the privacy of the validation set, which would be unacceptable if applied to a real learning problem, but is permissible here to be able to demonstrate all of the methods fairly.

For DP methods,
\delta
was set to
\frac{1}{2N}
, where
N
is the
total number of datapoints in the dataset. The error bars in all figures and tables are the standard
deviation over the seeds used.
