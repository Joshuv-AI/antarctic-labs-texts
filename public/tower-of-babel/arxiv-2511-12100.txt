Did Models Sufficient Learn? Attribution-Guided Training via Subset-Selected Counterfactual Augmentation
Yannan Chen, Ruoyu Chen, Bin Zeng, Wei Wang, Shiming Liu, Qunli Zhang, Zheng Hu, Laiyuan Wang, Yaowei Wang, Xiaochun Cao

Abstract
In current visual model training, models often rely on only limited sufficient causes for their predictions, which makes them sensitive to distribution shifts or the absence of key features. Attribution methods can accurately identify a model’s critical regions. However, masking these areas to create counterfactuals often causes the model to misclassify the target, while humans can still easily recognize it. This divergence highlights that the model’s learned dependencies may not be sufficiently causal. To address this issue, we propose Subset-Selected Counterfactual Augmentation (SS-CA), which integrates counterfactual explanations directly into the training process for targeted intervention. Building on the subset-selection-based LIMA attribution method, we develop Counterfactual LIMA to identify minimal spatial region sets whose removal can selectively alter model predictions. Leveraging these attributions, we introduce a data augmentation strategy that replaces the identified regions with natural background, and we train the model jointly on both augmented and original samples to mitigate incomplete causal learning. Extensive experiments across multiple ImageNet variants show that SS-CA improves generalization on in-distribution (ID) test data and achieves superior performance on out-of-distribution (OOD) benchmarks such as ImageNet-R and ImageNet-S. Under perturbations including noise, models trained with SS-CA also exhibit enhanced generalization, demonstrating that our approach effectively uses interpretability insights to correct model deficiencies and improve both performance and robustness.

3
Preliminary and Formulation

This section establishes the theoretical groundwork and formalizes the problem. We first review the fundamentals of visual attribution and then define the attribution-guided training framework that addresses shortcut learning.

3.1
Preliminary of Subset-based Attribution

A central challenge in visual attribution is to identify the most critical input regions that determine the decision of models. One effective paradigm for this task is subset-based attribution. This approach, notably formulated as a submodular optimization problem within the LIMA framework
7
, seeks to find an optimal subset of image regions that best explains the model’s output. This formulation provides a structured alternative to standard pixel-level attributions.

Given an input image
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w\times 3}
and a classification model
f(\cdot)
producing class-score pairs
f(\mathbf{I})=\{(c_{i},s_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the goal is to find the smallest ordered subset of image regions. This subset, with incremental insertion, should most efficiently recover the model’s confidence on a target
(c_{t},s_{t})
.

To formalize this, the image
\mathbf{I}
is first partitioned into
m
disjoint sub-regions
\mathcal{V}=\{\mathbf{I}^{s}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{I}^{s}_{m}\}
. An ordered subset is defined as
S=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
, where
s_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
. The optimization objective is structured to reward ordered subsets that yield high confidence scores early in the insertion sequence, while also considering the area of the regions being added. This dual consideration ensures that the most salient regions are prioritized. The optimal ordered subset
S^{*}
is therefore identified by maximizing this cumulative, area-weighted contribution:

S^{*}=\arg\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}S=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\\
S\subseteq\mathcal{V}\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{|s_{j}|}{A}f(S_{\cdot,j}),

(1)

where
|s_{j}|
denotes the pixel area of the
j
-th region
s_{j}
,
A
is the total image area, and
S_{\cdot,j}
represents the cumulative subset containing the first
j
elements of
S
. This formulation explicitly casts the task as an ordered subset optimization.

3.2
Problem Formulation

Let
f_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a deep learning model parameterized by
\theta
. Given a training dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
is an input (e.g., an image) and
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
is the corresponding label.
The standard training paradigm of Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) seeks the optimal parameters
\theta^{*}
by minimizing a task-specific loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}
over the dataset.

Models trained via standard ERM, however, often learn to exploit spurious correlations or “shortcuts” present in the data.
This reliance on non-causal features results in poor out-of-distribution generalization.
Furthermore, it creates a model rationale, or attribution, that fails to align with the true causal features of the task.

To address this fundamental limitation, we formulate an “attribution-guided” training framework.
This proposed approach actively leverages the model’s own explanations to dynamically generate challenging training samples.
Such a process is designed to compel the model to refine its internal logic and learn more robust, generalizable features.
Our method introduces a joint optimization to achieve this, which is formulated as:

\mathcal{L}=\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(f_{\theta}(X,Y))}_{\text{Task Supervision}}+\lambda\underbrace{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(f_{\theta}(T_{\text{Aug}}(\mathcal{A},X,Y)),Y)}_{\text{Explainable Data Augmentation}},

(2)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the attribution map derived from the current model
\theta
.
T_{\text{Aug}}
is the augmentation function that uses
\mathcal{A}
to generate a counterfactual sample.
\mathcal{E}\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}
is the loss computed on this new sample. Finally,
\lambda
is a hyperparameter that balances the standard supervision and the attribution-guided augmentation.

This formulation creates a dynamic feedback loop. The model’s explanations (captured in
\mathcal{A}
) are used to create challenging new data, which in turn refines the model and its future explanations. This process promotes enhanced robustness, generalization, and a decision-making process that is more aligned with the desired rationales.

Methodology

This section details our approach to integrating counterfactual explanations directly into the training loop, with the goal of mitigating reliance on spurious correlations. Figure
2
illustrates the overall framework of our closed-loop methodology, which implements this core integration.

4.1
Counterfactual Attribution Objective

To implement the
Explainable Data Augmentation
term from Equation. (
2
), a method is needed to identify the spurious features
\mathcal{A}
for the augmentation function
T_{\text{Aug}}
. This identification is formulated as a combinatorial optimization problem, designed to find the minimal feature set responsible for the model’s reliance on shortcuts.

Counterfactual Utility Function:

Instead of standard attribution (i.e. “Which regions support
y_{\text{gt}}
?”), this framework answers a more profound counterfactual question:
“What is the minimal set of regions
S
that must be removed to flip the prediction from
y_{\text{gt}}
to a specific
y_{\text{counter}}
? ”

This problem is modeled by designing a submodular utility function
F
, which is optimized via a greedy algorithm. The algorithm iteratively selects the next optimal region
v^{*}
at each step
t
by maximizing the marginal gain
\Delta F(v|S)=F(S\cup\{v\})-F(S)
:

v^{*}_{t}=\underset{v\in V\setminus S_{t-1}}{\arg\max}\left[\Delta F(v|S_{t-1})\right],

(3)

where
V
is the set of all regions and
S_{t-1}
is the set of regions selected so far.
The marginal gain
\Delta F
is defined by a utility function
\mathcal{F}(S)
composed of two complementary objectives:

Deletion Score:
This objective quantifies the impact on model confidence when excluding the selected regions
S
. It is designed to be twofold: to actively drive the prediction towards the counterfactual class
y_{\text{counter}}
(a), while simultaneously suppressing any remaining confidence in the ground-truth class
y_{gt}
(c).

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{Deletion}}(S)=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\lambda_{1}\cdot f_{y_{\text{counter}}}(I(V\setminus S))}_{\text{(a) Counterfactual Driver}}

(4)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\underbrace{\lambda_{2}\cdot(1-f_{y_{gt}}(I(V\setminus S))).}_{\text{(c) GT-Class Suppression}}

Insertion Score:
This component quantifies model confidence based only on the selected regions
S
. It serves as a critical fidelity regularizer, ensuring
S
is faithful to the original
y_{gt}
prediction (d) while penalizing any support for the counterfactual class
y_{\text{counter}}
(b).

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{Insertion}}(S)=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\lambda_{1}\cdot(1-f_{y_{\text{counter}}}(I(S)))}_{\text{(b) Counterfactual Consistency}}

(5)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\underbrace{\lambda_{2}\cdot f_{y_{gt}}(I(S)).}_{\text{(d) GT-Class Fidelity}}

In these equations,
f_{c}(X)
represents the model’s predicted confidence for class
c
(e.g.,
counter
or
gt
class) on input
X
. The “deletion” operation
I(V\setminus S)
is defined as the input
I
with regions
S
masked (e.g.,
I\odot(\mathbf{1}-M_{S})
). Similarly, the “insertion” operation
I(S)
is the input
I
with only regions
S
visible (e.g.,
I\odot M_{S}
).

The marginal gain function is derived from this utility function
\mathcal{F}(S)
, which is a weighted sum of these two scores. This objective, controlled by hyperparameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, can be defined first by combining the scores and then expanding into the full four-term formulation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(S)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{\text{Deletion}}(S)+\mathcal{F}_{\text{Insertion}}(S)

(6)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\lambda_{1}\cdot f_{y_{\text{counter}}}(I(V\setminus S))}_{\text{(a) Counterfactual Driver}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\lambda_{1}\cdot(1-f_{y_{\text{counter}}}(I(S)))}_{\text{(b) Counterfactual Consistency}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\lambda_{2}\cdot(1-f_{y_{gt}}(I(V\setminus S)))}_{\text{(c) GT-Class Suppression}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\lambda_{2}\cdot f_{y_{gt}}(I(S)).}_{\text{(d) GT-Class Fidelity}}

Utility Function Analysis:

This dual-objective design, fully expanded in Equation. (
6
), is critical. The terms driven by
\lambda_{1}
(a, b) actively identify regions that, when removed, flip the prediction to
y_{\text{counter}}
. The terms driven by
\lambda_{2}
(c, d) act as a regularizer, ensuring these regions are precisely the “shortcuts” the model actually used for its original
y_{\text{gt}}
decision. The greedy search terminates upon reaching a budget
k
or a confidence threshold
\tau_{\text{cf}}
, yielding the final counterfactual attribution map
\mathcal{A}=M_{S}
. Specific details of the hyperparameter experiments are provided in the supplementary materials.

Counterfactual Target Selection.

Attribution-Guided Counterfactual Augmentation

Random Background Padding.

The final step of Algorithm
1
creates the augmented sample
I_{\text{aug}}
. This utilizes a “donor” image
I_{\text{donor}}
. Critically, this donor is not sampled from a generic external dataset, but is rather drawn from a curated, in-distribution collection of background patches derived from ImageNet and its variants. The identified mask
M_{\text{base}}
(referred to as
M_{\text{cf}}

I
with corresponding content from
I_{\text{donor}}
:

I_{\text{aug}}=I\odot(\mathbf{1}-M_{\text{cf}})+I_{\text{donor}}\odot M_{\text{cf}},

(8)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication. This “Background Refilling” strategy is critically different from simpler erasure-based augmentations, which introduce OOD artifacts (e.g., black regions or random noise) that the model can exploit as a new shortcut. This refilling strategy, by contrast, forces the model to re-evaluate the remaining (non-shortcut) features of
I
within a new, plausible context.

Attribution-Guided Hard Mining.

We apply our augmentation Algorithm
1
to a candidate subset
\mathcal{B}_{\text{cand}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\text{orig}}
. This hard mining process then filters the results, retaining only “successful” counterfactual explanations where the resulting confidence
c_{\text{max}}
exceeds a predefined threshold
\tau_{\text{aug}}
. This step constructs the final “hard batch”
\mathcal{B}_{\text{aug}}
, formally defined as:

\mathcal{B}_{\text{aug}}=\{(I^{\prime}_{j},y_{j})\ |\ (I_{j},y_{j})\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{cand}}\ \land\ c_{\text{max}}>\tau_{\text{aug}}\}.

The core inductive bias of our method lies in the labeling of this hard batch. Each hard sample
I^{\prime}_{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{aug}}
is explicitly assigned its original ground-truth label
y_{j}
(e.g. “Robin”). From a Causal Inference perspective, this step performs a crucial “intervention” by forcibly breaking the spurious correlation

. By preserving the original label, we provide an distinct supervisory signal: “Even if your most relied-upon features (
M_{\text{cf}}
) are removed, the true identity of this sample is still
y_{\text{gt}}
.” This compels the model to learn the more robust, causal relationship
P(y_{\text{gt}}|I(V\setminus M_{\text{cf}}))
.

Finally, the generated samples are used for training. At each training step, an original batch

\mathcal{B}_{\text{orig}}

M\leq N
.
These batches are then merged,
\mathcal{B}_{\text{joint}}=\mathcal{B}_{\text{orig}}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\text{aug}}

\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}
is computed over this merged batch:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(I_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{orig}}}\mathcal{L}_{CE}(f_{\theta}(I_{i}),y_{i})

(10)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{(I^{\prime}_{j},y_{j})\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{aug}}}\mathcal{L}_{CE}(f_{\theta}(I^{\prime}_{j}),y_{j}),

where

\theta
and
\mathcal{L}_{CE}
is the standard cross-entropy loss. This Joint Optimization strategy is vital. The “easy” samples in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{orig}}
ensure the model does not forget standard data (preventing catastrophic forgetting), while the “hard” samples in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{aug}}
apply continuous “robustness pressure”. This joint objective, which combines standard ERM with counterfactual samples, is designed to reduce the model’s generalization error, forcing it to discover a generalizable feature representation that performs well in both standard and debiased scenarios.

Ablation Studies

To deconstruct the efficacy of the components within our SS-CA framework, we conducted a comprehensive series of ablation studies on ImageNet-100. The results are presented in Table

. These results allow for a critical analysis of the guidance strategy.

Comparison between Attribution Guidances.

This analysis commences by evaluating guidance using standard (factual) attribution methods, such as Grad-CAM
36
and LIMA
7
.
Figure
6
provides a clear visual explanation for their inherent limitations, showing how standard Grad-CAM produces a diffuse map that incorrectly highlights spurious background regions.
Table
5
provides the quantitative confirmation for this visual finding. These factual methods (e.g., LIMA,
90.41\%
ID) indeed yield only limited performance improvements over the baseline.

Importance of Counterfactuals.
The analysis then demonstrates that switching to a counterfactual-based objective provides a significant performance leap.
However, the data also reveals a critical insight that not all counterfactual approaches are equal.
For instance, the naive Counterfactual Grad-CAM (
89.14\%
) actually performs worse than the baseline (
89.50\%
).
This finding strongly indicates that a poorly designed or naive counterfactual objective, perhaps one based only on gradients, can actively mislead the model rather than enhancing its robustness.

This superior attribution fidelity provides the underlying rationale for the quantitative performance gains documented in our results.

Advantage of SS-CA (Ours).

The full SS-CA framework, which integrates our novel submodular search with “Additional Background” (Background Refilling), achieves the peak performance of
91.18%
(ID),
62.64%
(ImageNet-R), and
59.11%
(ImageNet-S).
These robust results confirm that our success stems not merely from the general concept of “counterfactuals,” which, as we have shown, can be ineffective in isolation.
Instead, our superior performance is attributed to the unique submodular optimization objective formulated in our algorithm.
This specific formulation is critical, as it strategically balances the counterfactual drive with the ground-truth fidelity term, thereby ensuring the model is guided by precise, meaningful explanations rather than misleading artifacts.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we revisited visual model training from a causal and interpretability perspective, showing that conventional models often rely on limited sufficient causes and fail under distribution shifts or missing key features. To tackle this, we proposed Subset-Selected Counterfactual Augmentation (SS-CA) and designed a new Counterfactual LIMA specifically for training-time counterfactual attribution, which identifies minimal decision-changing regions and turns them into attribution-guided augmentations. Experimental results show that SS-CA effectively improves model performance, enhances generalization, and strengthens robustness, indicating that coupling counterfactual interpretability with training is a promising path toward more reliable and transparent visual models.
