Tensionable Knotless Tendon‐Sparing Biceps Rerouting Technique for Irreparable Rotator Cuff Tears
Authors: Ignancio Pérez, Pia Stefano, Hady Ezzeddine, Alex Campagnoli, Xavi Rius, Abdul‐ilah Hachem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis technique describes a tensionable knotless tendon‐sparing biceps rerouting for superior capsular reconstruction using the long head of the biceps in irreparable supraspinatus tears. The long head of the biceps is arthroscopically released from the bicipital groove, rerouted over the greater tuberosity footprint, and secured with 2 self‐punching, double‐loaded knotless all‐suture anchors, creating tensionable loops that stabilize both the long head of the biceps and infraspinatus or anterior cuff remnants. Medial side‐to‐side sutures further enhance reduction and stability. This technique preserves the tendon, avoids perforation of the tendon tissue, and eliminates the need for graft harvesting or bone tunnels. The approach is reproducible, minimally invasive, and suitable for degenerative or inflamed tendons, providing biomechanical stability and improved functional outcomes. Diligent patient selection remains critical for optimal results.

This video presents a saw‐bone model summary as well as the surgical technique for tensionable knotless tendon‐sparing biceps rerouting. The patient is set in an oblique lateral decubitus position, with the arm in 30° to 40° of abduction with an axial traction of 3 to 3.5 kg. Anterior, anterolateral, posterolateral, and posterior standard arthroscopic portals are used. Tear recognition is made from the subacromial anterolateral portal. In this case, the anterior portion of the supraspinatus tendon was found to be intact. From the anterior portal, an arthroscopic probe is used to pull and tense the LHB, facilitating its release. The transverse ligament is then cut from the anterolateral portal using low radiofrequency intensity until the LHB moves freely and without tension out of the groove. The footprint is then prepared, and the first all‐suture, self‐punching, double‐loaded tensionable knotless 2.6 mm FiberTak is positioned near the cartilage where the infraspinatus and the LHB reduction converge. Pulling of the sutures confirms correct deployment under the cortex. Next, the first repair suture is passed underneath the LHB using a suture retriever from the anterior portal. The second repair suture is transported and passed through the IST using a SutureLasso from the posterior portal. In this case, the anterior portion of the supraspinatus was added to the LHB repair suture to enhance anatomical restoration. A second FiberTak anchor is placed approximately 5 mm lateral to the tip and at least 1 cm away from the bicipital groove, aligned with the first anchor. The same steps previously explained are then repeated. The repair sutures and the shuttle link strand are retrieved together through an accessory portal for loading. Once loaded, by pulling the tail strand of the shuttle link suture, the repair sutures are transported inside the all‐suture anchor, creating 2 tensionable knotless suture loops across the tendons. The same steps are then repeated for the second anchor. A knot pusher is used to tense each repair suture individually, and finally, the sutures are cut flush to the bone. After finishing the rerouting, the bicipital groove is noted to be devoid of tendon, and the LHB assumes a new posterolateral position. Several techniques have been developed to address function and pain in patients with irreparable rotator cuff tears, including partial repair, biceps tenotomy/tenodesis, subacromial balloon, tendon transfers, reverse shoulder arthroplasty, and superior capsular reconstruction (SCR); none of which are exempt from its own set of complications.

Mihata et al. described the use of fascia lata autograft for superior capsular reconstruction. 1 Since then, this technique has evolved to incorporate different types of grafts, such as extracellular matrix, dermal or tendon allografts, and the long head of the biceps (LHB) tendon. 2 , 3 , 4 Boutsiadis et al. pioneered the use of LHB as an autograft for SCR. 4 Fixation methods and configurations have also evolved alongside the development of anchor technologies. 5 , 6 In the following technique, we describe a tensionable knotless tendon‐sparing biceps rerouting method for SCR using the LHB in irreparable supraspinatus (SST) tears.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The surgical technique is shown in Video 1.

Patient Positioning and Arthroscopic Evaluation

The patient is positioned in a beach chair or oblique lateral decubitus position, with the arm at 30° to 40° of abduction and placed in a traction foam sleeve (3‐Point Shoulder Distraction System; Arthrex) with an axial traction of 3 to 3.5 kg (Figure 1).

Patient positioning, posterior view of a right shoulder. Oblique lateral decubitus position, with 30° to 40° of abduction, and the arm placed in a traction foam sleeve (3‐Point Shoulder Distraction System; Arthrex) with axial traction of 3 to 3.5 kg.

First, glenohumeral arthroscopy is performed through a standard posterior portal using a 30° scope and an anterior portal through the rotator interval. All potential intra‐articular lesions are examined, including the labrum, subscapularis, cartilage, capsule, and the articular LHB quality. Next, through the posterior portal, the scope is switched to the subacromial space, which is evaluated, with particular attention to the conditions of the SST tear, extra‐articular LHB, and infraspinatus (IST).

An anterolateral portal is usually created in the middle of the SST tear, and an anterolateral subacromial and subdeltoid bursectomy is performed using a shaver and a radiofrequency device. The view is then shifted to this portal to assess the tear and LHB properly. Next, the arthroscope is moved from the anterolateral to the posterolateral portal, established near the posterolateral corner of the acromion, and is maintained there throughout most of the procedure (Figure 2). Then, an evaluation of the SST and IST tears is done to determine if full or partial repair is possible.

Viewing through the posterolateral portal, using an arthroscopic probe, the LHB is pulled and tensioned in an anterosuperior direction. This facilitates biceps release from its groove allowing complete section of the transverse ligament inferiorly with low radiofrequency intensity from the anterolateral portal (Figure 3 and 4). The SS footprint is then debrided with a shaver, without compromising the greater tuberosity (GT) cortex. Then, the LHB is mobilized posteriorly over the vacant footprint, and its position is evaluated for subsequent fixation.

LHB Fixation on GT

This fixation method employs 2 all‐suture, self‐punching, double‐loaded tensionable knotless 2.6 mm FiberTak anchors (Arthrex, Naples, FL) (Figure 5). The first anchor is inserted percutaneously through an accessory portal near the anterolateral side of the acromion, maintaining an alignment as vertical to the bone as possible. This anchor is positioned near the cartilaginous margin (Figure 6A). All sutures are then gently pulled. This anchor features 2 nonsliding #2 repair sutures and 1 shuttle link suture for transport. The first repair suture is passed underneath the LHB (Figure 6B). The second repair suture is transported and passed through the IST using a SutureLasso (Arthrex, Naples, FL), and then all 4 sutures are retrieved from the same portal (Figure 7). Next, the same procedure is repeated with a second 2.6 mm FiberTak anchor, placed approximately 5 mm lateral to the GT tip and at least 1 cm away from the bicipital groove, at the center of the GT aligned with the first anchor (Figure 8).

The 4 suture limbs of the first FiberTak anchor are then retrieved through the accessory portal, and the 2 repair sutures are loaded into the shuttle link suture strand with a loop (Figure 9). Two loops are then created, 1 loop encircles the biceps, while the other loop simultaneously reduces the IST (Figure 10). Finally, a knot pusher is used to tension each repair suture individually, ensuring they are tight enough to secure the LHB and IST over the footprint. The sutures are then cut flush to the bone. The same steps are repeated with the sutures of the lateral anchor to ensure a strong double‐row fixation and rotational stability (Figure 11).

Suture between LHB and IST or Remnant Anterior Cuff

In the final step, 1 or 2 medial side‐to‐side sutures are placed between the LHB and IST. This technique involves a traditional side‐to‐side repair using 1.3 mm SutureTape (Arthrex, Naples, FL), passing the suture underneath the LHB rather than through it, and tying the knots over the rotator cuff (Figure 12).

Postoperative Care

Starting day 1 postoperative, free movement of the elbow, hand, and wrist is permitted. During the first week, the patient may electively wear a sling based on comfort, starting with periscapular muscle strengthening and complete passive or active‐assisted range of motion without restrictions. Office work is allowed immediately without weightlifting. Stretching exercises, with particular focus on the anterior and inferior capsular tissue, are encouraged from the third to fourth week onward. All free active range of motion is permitted as well; however, lifting weights overhead is not allowed until 8 weeks postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

Superior capsular reconstruction is a feasible option for active patients with an isolated irreparable SST tear. Biomechanically, it functions by restoring superior shoulder stability when the upper parts of the SST and IST are absent, preventing the humeral head from translating superiorly. 7 , 8 , 9 Recently, numerous studies have shown promising results of SCR and LHB tenodesis in active patients, leading to improved pain relief and shoulder motion outcomes. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13

We describe the tensionable knotless tendon‐sparing biceps rerouting technique for SCR using the LHB as a graft, employing 2 knotless all suture anchors for double row fixation avoiding biceps tendon perforation. This approach can be beneficial when encountering a degenerative, delaminated, and inflamed biceps. It prevents sutures from tearing the weakened tendon and may lower the risk of short‐term surgical failure.

Various techniques have been developed, involving different fixation methods: single‐ and double‐bundle configurations, bone tunnels, and tendon graft harvesting distal to the pectoralis major insertion. Using this approach, Fandridis and Zampeli described the “box” technique, which offers increased graft length and enables triple fixation: 1 on the glenoid rim and 2 on the GT, placed at the cartilaginous margin. 5 Similarly, Brandão et al. described the “biceps loop technique,” with a long graft, in which a transosseous bone tunnel is created from the bicipital groove to the posterior aspect of the GT. 6 The graft is passed through this tunnel and fixated to the posterosuperior glenoid. Norton and Field described redirection of the long head of the biceps to augment repair of anterosuperior rotator cuff tears, using the biceps as a biologic convergence post to offload the SST‐subscapularis repair. 14

A dynamic biomechanical study compared 3 different configurations for SCR with LHB: a V‐shaped, a box, and a single‐strand configuration. All 3 configurations improved shoulder function by preventing superior humeral migration, decreasing deltoid forces, and subacromial contact pressure. 15 Another biomechanical study aimed to evaluate the optimal fixation location and glenohumeral fixation angle for a single‐strand LHB for SCR. It concluded that at a 30° glenohumeral angle and with posterior fixation, the proximal migration of the humeral head is effectively reduced. 16 These studies align with our study, with the setup of the patient's arm in 30° of abduction and the posterior fixation of the LHB in the GT.

The main limitation of this technique is in cases of LHB absence, which is why it is essential to assess the presence of the intraarticular LHB using MRI and clinically using the “Popeye” sign. Unlike other published techniques, where degenerative and inflammatory conditions are contraindications due to significant manipulation, multiple fixation points, and perforations of the LHB, this method avoids such issues. Furthermore, it is a reproducible technique that does not require various complex steps like other methods; the anchors are self‐punching, with no drilling or bone socket preparation required. Additionally, partial or complete repair of the IST tear and side‐to‐side points between the LHB and the remaining rotator cuff improves stability and biological conditions.

In summary, our technique for SCR with LHB using a double‐loaded tensionable knotless suture anchor offers the benefits of using a local autograft, avoiding damage to a weakened tendon, and complex fixation on the GT. Studies support the use of a single‐strand approach, which improves pain relief and functional outcomes. The success of this method relies on proper patient selection to achieve optimal results.
