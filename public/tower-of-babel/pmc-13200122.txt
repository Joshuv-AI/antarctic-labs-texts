Influence of Tectona grandis leaf supplementation on growth, nutrient digestibility, serum biochemistry, hepatic antioxidant status, and cecal microbial profile in rabbits
Authors: Saheed A Ahmed, Kazeem D Adeyemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Potential of Teak leaves (TL) in rabbits’ diets was investigated.•10 % TL did not affect growth or nutrient digestibility, whereas 20–30 % reduced both.•TL reduced serum alanine aminotransferase and enhanced hepatic antioxidant enzymes.•TL reduced ceca Lactobacillus, Streptococcus aureus and Escherichia coli in rabbits.Keywords: Alanine aminotransferase, Catalase, Feed efficiency, Lactobacillus, Protein digestibility

Introduction

The demand for animal-derived foods in developing regions continues to outpace supply, largely because conventional feed resources are expensive and scarce (Adesogan et al., 2020; Henchion et al., 2021). Feed represents the most expensive input in livestock production, and dependence on cereal- and legume-based ingredients has increased production costs, thereby making animal protein less affordable (Pexas et al., 2023). This situation has intensified the pursuit for alternative and sustainable feed resources that can support efficient animal production while reducing reliance on conventional feedstuffs (Alagawany et al., 2022; Salem et al., 2020; Simbaya et al., 2020).

Tree leaves offer a practical strategy for achieving feed sustainability as they serve as valuable sources of nutrients and bioactive compounds for livestock (Adeyemi et al., 2022; Omokore & Alagbe, 2019; Simbaya et al., 2020). Leaf meals derived from trees contain phenolic acids, flavonoids, tannins, and saponins, which exhibit antioxidant, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory activities that can enhance animal health and productivity (Alagawany et al., 2022; Hou et al., 2018; Osman et al., 2024; Salem et al., 2020).

Rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus) are efficient converters of leaves into meat and can utilize diets including as much as 30 % crude fiber, compared to the 10 % limit for poultry species (Safwat et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2021). This reduces competition with humans for cereal grains and enhances the sustainability of smallholder production. It has been demonstrated that adding leaf meals to rabbit diets can improve growth performance, carcass yield, and health indicators (Adeyemi et al., 2022; El-Adawy et al., 2020; Omokore & Alagbe, 2019; Osman et al., 2024). However, the availability of green forages is often restricted to the rainy season, resulting in feed shortages during dry periods (Salem et al., 2020; Simbaya et al., 2020). This highlights the need for tree species capable of providing nutritious foliage throughout the year.

Tectona grandis Linn. (family Verbenaceae), commonly known as teak, is a widely cultivated multipurpose tree valued for its durable timber and ecological adaptability (Moya et al., 2014; Palanisamy et al., 2009). Beyond its economic importance, the leaves and other plant parts contain phytochemicals with bactericidal, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties (Daramola, 2022; Ghareeb et al., 2014; Krishna & Jayakumaran, 2010; Kusnadi & Budyanto, 2015). In Nigeria, teak plantations cover approximately 70,000 hectares—more than half of Africa’s total teak cultivation area (Dantani et al., 2019; Shamaki et al., 2011). The University of Ilorin Teak Plantation alone spans 616 hectares (Amusa & Adedapo, 2021), producing substantial quantities of leaves that remain largely underutilized.

The abundant availability of teak leaves, coupled with their rich phytochemical profile, presents an opportunity for their inclusion in livestock diets to reduce feeding costs and enhance productivity. Previous studies have examined the effects of teak leaves on in vitro fermentation (Abd-El Rahman et al., 2024) and growth performance in cattle (Kasihandari et al., 2025), as well as teak leaf extract on egg quality in hens (Edi et al., 2018). In addition, the impact of dietary teak leaves on carcass and sensory characteristics of rabbit meat has been reported (Ojali et al., 2020). However, information on the impact of dietary teak leaves on growth, nutrient digestibility, blood chemistry, hepatic antioxidant status, and cecal microbial profile of rabbits remains scarce.

Given the nutritional and phytochemical attributes of teak leaves, it was hypothesized that their dietary inclusion would affect growth performance, nutrient utilization, antioxidant capacity, and cecal microbiota in rabbits. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of dietary Tectona grandis leaf meal supplementation at 0, 10, 20, and 30 % on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, serum biochemistry, hepatic antioxidant status, and cecal microbial profile in growing rabbits.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

The experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of University of Ilorin (Approval No. UERC/ASN/2023/2634).

Leaf collection and processing

Mature Tectona grandis leaves were collected from 10-year-old T. grandis trees at the University of Ilorin Teak plantation. The leaves were air-dried at 34 ± 3 °C for 5 d and milled using a locally fabricated attrition mill to pass through a 2.0 mm sieve. The Tectona grandis leaf meal (TGLM) was packaged in air-tight container until use.

Rabbit feeding and husbandry

Seventy-two 42-day-old New Zealand White rabbits (1130 ± 43 g) were randomly allocated to 24 wire mesh cages (70 × 50 × 45 cm), with three rabbits per cage. Using a completely randomized design, the cages were assigned at random to either: TGLM-0 (diet without TGLM), TGLM-10 (diet including 10 % TGLM), TGLM-20 (diet including 20 % TGLM), or TGLM-30 (diet including 30 % TGLM), as detailed in Table 1. Each dietary group had six replicates. The experiment lasted 56 days. Diets were prepared in accordance to the National Research Council (NRC, 1977) requirements for rabbits and were offered as pellets. Feed and water were offered ad libitum. Rabbits were weighed at the start of the trial and subsequently every week. Weekly records of feed offered and refused were recorded, and the feed conversion ratio (FCR) was computed.

Chemical and phytochemical composition

The chemical composition of the dietary treatments and TGLM was determined according to standard methods of AOAC (2000), including dry matter (Method 934.01), crude protein (Method 984.13), ether extract (Method 920.39), crude fiber (Method 973.18), and ash (Method 942.05). Phytochemical constituents, including total phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, steroids, glycosides, triterpenes and alkaloids, were quantified following the procedures described by Makkar et al. (1993) and Yadav and Agarwala (2011). The chemical and phytochemical compositions of the experimental diets and TGLM are presented in Table 1.

Apparent nutrient digestibility

Apparent nutrient digestibility was assessed by the total fecal collection method (Perez et al., 1995). On day 43, six rabbits were selected at random from each dietary group and housed individually in metabolic cages for a 9-day period, comprising 5 days of adaptation followed by 4 days of fecal collection. A known quantity of feed was offered daily, and feed refusals were recorded. Fecal samples were collected quantitatively once daily at 08:30 h prior to feeding.

Collected feces from each rabbit were weighed, pooled over the collection period, and representative samples were oven-dried at 60 °C for 24 h to constant weight. Dried samples were ground and analyzed for dry matter, crude protein, ether extract, and crude fiber using AOAC (2000) methods. Apparent nutrient digestibility ( %) was calculated as:

Digestibility ( %) = [(Nutrient intake − Nutrient in feces) / Nutrient intake] × 100

Serum biochemical analysis

On day 53, 5 mL blood was collected through ear vein, from each of two rabbits per replicate into plain sample bottles and centrifuged at 3500 × g for 18 min to separate the serum, and kept at -20 °C until assayed. Serum total protein (TP) was determined by Tietz (1995) method. Albumin, total cholesterol, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and triglycerides were measured using commercial assay kits (Randox Laboratories, WV, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Hepatic antioxidant enzymes

On day 56, the rabbits were given free access to water and fasted for the entire night. Subsequently, two rabbits per replicate were humanely slaughtered by severing the carotid artery and jugular vein. Liver samples were immediately excised, rinsed in ice-cold saline to remove blood residues, and homogenized in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The homogenates were centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were used for enzymatic assays. Activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and catalase (CAT), and total nitric oxide were determined using commercial assay kits (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Cecal microbial population

Cecal digesta from two euthanized rabbits per replicate was collected into sterile bijou bottles (Thermo Scientific™, Waltham, MA, USA). One gram of each sample was aseptically mixed with 9 mL of sterile distilled water, vortexed, and serially diluted to 10⁻¹⁰. From the appropriate dilution, 1 mL was dispensed into sterile petri dishes, after which sterile molten agar was poured in Plates were incubated at 37 °C, and colony counts were obtained using a colony counter. Lactobacillus spp. was cultured on De Man Rogosa Sharpe agar under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 h, whereas E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus were cultured on eosin methylene blue agar and Baird–Parker agar, respectively, under aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h. All media were prepared according to the manufacturers’ guidelines.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design. Data were analyzed using the general linear model procedure of SAS (Version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The statistical model used was:

where Yijis the observed response, μ is the overall mean, Tiis the fixed effect of dietary treatment, and eijis the random error term.

The experimental unit varied according to the parameter measured. For growth performance, a replicate (cage) consisting of three rabbits served as the experimental unit, and data were averaged per replicate prior to analysis. For apparent nutrient digestibility, the individual rabbit was considered the experimental unit. For serum biochemical parameters, hepatic antioxidant enzymes, and cecal microbial population, the mean value of two rabbits sampled per replicate was used, and this average constituted the experimental unit. When a significant treatment effect was detected (P < 0.05), treatment effects were further evaluated using orthogonal polynomial contrasts to determine linear and quadratic responses to increasing inclusion levels of TGLM.

Results

Chemical and phytochemical composition of dietary treatments

The dietary treatments had comparable chemical compositions (Table 1). Nonetheless, as dietary TGLM levels increased, so did the phytochemical constituents.

Growth performance

Dietary inclusion of TGLM significantly affected growth indices in rabbits (Table 2). There was a significant linear decrease (P < 0.001) in final body weight (BW), body weight gain (BWG), average daily gain (ADG), feed intake, and FCR with increasing levels of TGLM. In addition, quadratic effects (P < 0.05) were observed for final BW, BWG, feed intake, and FCR. Rabbits in the TGLM-0 and TGLM-10 groups had greater final BW and lower FCR than those in the TGLM-20 and TGLM-30 groups (Linear, P < 0.001). Similarly, rabbits fed TGLM-0 and TGLM-10 diets exhibited comparable BWG and feed intake, which were higher than those of rabbits in the higher inclusion groups. Among all treatments, rabbits fed the TGLM-30 diet had the lowest feed intake, BWG, and ADG.

Nutrient digestibility

Nutrient digestibility was variably affected by TGLM supplementation (Table 3). There were significant linear effects (P < 0.05) on dry matter digestibility (DMD), crude protein digestibility (CPD), and ether extract digestibility (EED), while a quadratic effect (P = 0.003) was observed only for EED. DMD was significantly higher in TGLM-10 rabbits than in TGLM-30 rabbits. However, there was no significant difference in DMD between TGLM-0, TGLM-20, and the other treatment groups. CPD was higher in the TGLM-0 rabbits compared to the TGLM-20 and TGLM-30 rabbits, while the TGLM-10 group showed no significant differences compared to TGLM-0 and TGLM-20 rabbits. EED was highest in TGLM-10 rabbits and lowest in TGLM-30 rabbits. The TGLM-20 group showed EED value that was lower than those of TGLM-0 and TGLM-10 rabbits but higher than that of TGLM-30 rabbits. Crude fiber digestibility was unaffected by the diets.

Serum biochemistry

Serum biochemical parameters were variably affected by TGLM supplementation (Table 4). There were significant linear effects on serum albumin, ALT, ALP, and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL)-cholesterol. Serum albumin levels were significantly higher in TGLM-10 rabbits compared to all other groups. Alanine aminotransferase level was higher in TGLM-0 rabbits compared to other dietary groups. Alkaline phosphatase levels in TGLM-10 and TGLM-20 rabbits was comparable to those in other dietary groups, but TGLM-0 rabbits showed lower ALP levels compared to TGLM-30 rabbits. Triglycerides and total, HDL, and LDL-cholesterol levels were not significantly influenced by dietary TGLM. VLDL-cholesterol levels were higher in TGLM-0 rabbits than in TGLM-30 rabbits, with no significant differences among TGLM-10, TGLM-20, and other groups.

Hepatic antioxidant enzymes

Dietary TGLM affected liver antioxidant enzyme activity in rabbits (Table 5). As dietary TGLM levels increased, so did hepatic glutathione peroxidase, catalase, and superoxide dismutase activities (Linear, P < 0.05). Hepatic SOD activity was higher in the TGLM-supplemented rabbits compared to the TGLM-0 rabbits (Linear, P = 0.014). SOD activity in TGLM-10 and TGLM-20 rabbits did not differ significantly but was lower than that of TGLM-30 rabbits. Hepatic GPx activity was highest in TGLM-30 rabbits, whereas it was lowest in TGLM-0 and TGLM-10 rabbits (Linear, P < 0.001). Catalase activity increased proportionally with higher levels of TGLM in the diet (Linear, P < 0.001). Hepatic nitric oxide levels were unaffected by the diets.

Discussion

This study provides comprehensive dose–response data on the dietary inclusion of TGLM in rabbit diets and represents the first systematic assessment of its effects on multiple aspects of rabbit physiology, including growth performance, nutrient digestibility, serum biochemical indices, hepatic antioxidant status, and cecal microbiota. The findings therefore offer valuable evidence supporting the potential utilization of this locally abundant and underutilized plant resource in rabbit nutrition.

The supplementation of 10 % TGLM did not alter feed intake, BWG, and feed efficiency in rabbits relative to the control diet. The low inclusion level of TGLM likely supported adequate nutrient availability and palatability, thereby maintaining normal growth performance compared with the higher TGLM levels. This observation supports previous findings in which supplementation with 10 % Kigelia pinnata leaves (Adeyemi et al., 2022), mulberry leaves (Hou et al., 2018), Alchornea cordifolia leaves (Ayodele et al., 2016), and Leucaena leucocephala leaves (Adedeji et al., 2013) did not significantly affect feed intake or growth indices in rabbits.

In contrast, supplementation with 20 and 30 % TGLM reduced feed intake and BWG, with the TGLM-30 group exhibiting the lowest values. This decline in performance may be associated with the higher concentrations of phytochemicals quantified in the experimental diets (Table 1), including tannins, alkaloids, glycosides, triterpenes, and steroids, which increased progressively with increasing TGLM inclusion. Among these compounds, tannins are known to bind dietary proteins and digestive enzymes, thereby reducing nutrient digestibility and utilization. In addition, alkaloids and certain triterpenoid compounds may impart bitter or astringent characteristics to feed, potentially reducing palatability and voluntary feed intake. The combined effects of reduced feed intake and impaired nutrient utilization may have contributed to the lower growth performance observed in rabbits fed diets containing higher levels of TGLM. Similar reductions in growth performance have been reported in rabbits fed high inclusion levels of leaf meals such as Leucaena leucocephala and Moringa oleifera (Safwat et al., 2014), mulberry leaves (Hou et al., 2018), and Paulownia leaves (Al-Sagheer et al., 2019).

Dry matter digestibility was not different between the control and the TGLM-supplemented rabbits. However, TGLM-30 exhibited a lower DMD compared to the TGLM-10 rabbits, likely due to the higher phytochemical contents that are capable of reducing digestibility with increased TGLM levels. This observation aligns with Osman et al. (2024), who found a lower DMD in rabbits fed diets containing 30 % Bridelia ferruginea leaf meal compared to those fed 10 % or 20 %.

The TGLM-10 showed CPD values comparable to TGLM-0, indicating that a 10 % inclusion level does not significantly impair protein digestibility. However, CPD decreased in TGLM-20 and TGLM-30 groups, highlighting the potential anti-nutritional factors in higher TGLM inclusion on protein utilization. This may be due to the higher tannins in the TGLM at these levels. Tannins can bind to dietary proteins and digestive enzymes, lowering protein digestibility and nutrient absorption (Chisowa, 2021). Consistently, the inclusion of Leucaena leucocephala leaves beyond 10 % reduced CPD in rabbits (Adedeji et al., 2013). Safwat et al. (2015) found a reduction in CPD in rabbits fed diets supplemented with 30 % and 40 % Moringa oleifera or Leucaena leucocephala leaf meals compared with the control group. Moreover, CPD was lower in rabbits receiving 40 % leaf meals than in those fed 30 %.

TGLM-10 rabbits recorded the highest EED. This finding suggests that moderate inclusion of TGLM enhanced lipid digestibility, possibly due to improved digestive enzyme activity or better nutrient utilization at lower inclusion levels. This finding corroborates that of Ewuola et al. (2012) who found improved EED in rabbits supplemented with 10 % Moringa leaf meal compared with those fed 0, 5 and 15 % Moringa leaf meal. However, the marked decline in EDD at 20 % and 30 % inclusion indicates that higher levels of TGLM may have exerted inhibitory effects on lipid digestion. The reduction may be due to the presence of anti-nutritional factors such as alkaloids or tannins, which can bind to lipids or interfere with lipolytic enzymes, reducing fat digestibility. Similar trends have been reported in studies where excessive inclusion of tannin-rich leaf meals reduced EED in rabbits (Abejeshi et al., 2018; Idowu et al., 2013).

Serum total protein was comparable among the dietary groups, reflecting the isonitrogenous nature of the dietary treatments. Similarly, dietary neem leaves (Ali et al., 2025) and Paulownia leaves (Al-Sagheer et al., 2019) did not affect serum total protein in rabbits. The elevated albumin level in the 10 % TGLM group corresponds with its higher crude protein digestibility, indicating optimal protein metabolism at this inclusion level. The higher ALT levels in rabbits fed 0 % TGLM likely result from the absence of liver-protective phytochemicals found in TGLM. These compounds, at moderate to high inclusion levels, help reduce oxidative stress, inflammation, and liver cell damage (Atta et al., 2018), resulting in lower ALT levels in the TGLM-supplemented groups. This observation is akin to that of Oloruntola and Ayodele (2017), who reported lower ALT in rabbits supplemented with 15 % pawpaw leaf meal compared to those fed the control diet.

The higher serum alkaline phosphatase levels in rabbits fed 30 % teak leaves compared to 0 % likely indicate the impact of excessive antinutritional factors, such as tannins, and alkaloids, which can induce mild hepatobiliary stress or disrupt mineral metabolism by binding essential nutrients like calcium and phosphorus. These disruptions may stimulate ALP activity, a marker for liver and bone metabolism (Fernandez & Kidney, 2007). Consistently, increasing level of dietary Langucularia racemosa leaves increased serum ALP in rabbits (Yahya et al., 2015). The lack of significant differences in ALP levels among rabbits fed 10 % and 20 % TGLM compared to 0 % and 30 % suggests that moderate inclusion rates provided a balance between beneficial phytochemical effects (antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties) and the minimal impact of antinutritional factors, preventing marked physiological disruptions. The serum biochemical indices were within the normal physiological range for New Zealand White rabbits (Özkan et al., 2012).

Serum HDL, LDL and total cholesterol and triglycerides were comparable among the dietary groups, indicating a limited influence on lipid metabolism and circulation. This observation aligns with Hou et al. (2018), who found no effect of dietary supplementation with mulberry leaves on total cholesterol, HDL, and LDL-cholesterol or triglycerides in rabbits. Nonetheless, the TGLM-30 rabbits had lower VLDL cholesterol than the TGLM-0 rabbits. VLDL transports endogenous triglycerides from the liver to peripheral tissues (Van Zwol et al., 2024); therefore, reduced VLDL levels may indicate decreased hepatic triglyceride synthesis or secretion. This impact may be due to the myriad phytochemicals in TGLM, which have been reported to inhibit lipid absorption or modulate lipogenic enzyme activity in the liver (Liu et al., 2022). The lack of significant differences in VLDL cholesterol among TGLM-10 and TGLM-20 groups and other treatments implies a dose-dependent and nonlinear response to TGLM inclusion. The cholesterol and triglyceride levels were within the normal physiological range for rabbits (Jenkins, 1993).

The higher hepatic SOD, GPX, and CAT activities in the TGLM-supplemented rabbits suggest that TGLM enhanced the hepatic antioxidant defense system. This effect may be partly attributed to the phytochemicals present in TGLM, which are known to modulate antioxidant pathways and stimulate the activity of endogenous antioxidant enzymes (Cheng et al., 2017). The gradual increase in hepatic GPX and catalase activities with increasing TGLM levels indicates a dose-dependent modulation of the liver’s enzymatic antioxidant system. These enzymes function synergistically to neutralize reactive oxygen species and protect hepatocytes from oxidative damage (Lee et al., 2016). The enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities may therefore reflect a direct stimulatory effect of bioactive compounds in TGLM that promote antioxidant defense. However, it is also possible that the increased enzyme activities represent a compensatory adaptive response to mild oxidative stress associated with higher concentrations of phytochemicals at elevated TGLM inclusion levels. Such adaptive upregulation of antioxidant enzymes helps maintain redox homeostasis in the liver. Similar improvements in antioxidant enzyme activities have been reported in rabbits supplemented with mulberry leaves (Hou et al., 2018) and Kigelia pinnata leaves (Adeyemi et al., 2022).

The decrease in cecal Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, and E. coli counts with dietary supplementation of TGLM suggests that the phytochemicals in TGLM, such as tannins, and flavonoids, exert antimicrobial effects that inhibit the growth of these bacteria. Tannins, for instance, can disrupt bacterial cell membranes and inhibit enzyme activity, thereby reducing microbial populations in the gut (Abass et al., 2024; Sweet et al., 2024). The antibacterial properties of teak leaves and extracts have been documented (Daramola et al., 2022; Kusnadi & Budyanto, 2015; Younis et al., 2023). The concurrent reduction in all microbial groups suggests that TGLM may exert a broad-spectrum antimicrobial effect rather than a selective modulation of the cecal microbiota. In addition, the relatively high fiber and tannin contents of TGLM may alter cecal fermentation patterns by reducing the availability of fermentable substrates and inhibiting microbial enzymatic activity, which could suppress overall microbial proliferation. This effect may also partly explain the lower nutrient digestibility observed at higher levels of TGLM inclusion, as reduced microbial activity in the hindgut can limit fermentation and nutrient utilization. Similar reductions in microbial populations have been reported in rabbits fed diets containing Paulownia leaf meal (Al-Sagheer et al., 2019). In contrast, supplementation with mulberry leaf meal selectively decreased E. coli while increasing Lactobacillus counts in rabbits (Hou et al., 2018), suggesting that the microbial response may vary depending on the phytochemical composition of the leaf meal.

Limitations

This study quantified several phytochemical groups in Tectona grandis leaf meal, including total phenolics, total flavonoids, steroids, glycosides, alkaloids, tannins, and triterpenes. However, the specific types and individual compounds within these phytochemical classes were not characterized, and therefore their precise contribution to the observed physiological and microbial responses could not be directly determined. In addition, the microbial analysis focused on selected bacterial groups (Lactobacillus, Streptococcus aureus, and E. coli) using culture-based methods and did not assess the overall cecal microbial community structure, which could be more comprehensively evaluated using molecular approaches such as 16S rRNA sequencing. Furthermore, the experimental period lasted 8 weeks, and therefore the long-term effects of dietary TGLM supplementation, particularly at higher inclusion levels, on rabbit health, productivity, and gut microbial ecology remain to be elucidated.

Conclusion

Dietary supplementation with varying levels of TGLM significantly influenced growth performance, nutrient digestibility, serum profiles, hepatic antioxidant enzyme activity, and cecal microbial populations in rabbits. TGLM-10 supplementation emerged as the optimal level, showing feed efficiency and final BW comparable to the control diet, while enhancing ether extract and crude protein digestibility and increasing serum albumin levels. In contrast, TGLM-20 and TGLM-30 negatively affected feed intake and growth performance, with TGLM-30 showing the poorest results for growth and nutrient digestibility. TGLM supplementation improved hepatic antioxidant enzyme activities, with higher inclusion levels associated with increased activity, suggesting a dose-dependent antioxidant effect. Dietary TGLM also reduced the counts of Lactobacillus, Streptococcus aureus, and E. coli in the ceca of rabbits. Although the inclusion of 10 % TGLM can be recommended without compromising growth performance and nutrient digestibility, the observed reduction in Lactobacillus populations suggests that the effects of TGLM on beneficial gut microbiota warrant further investigation. Future studies should therefore examine the implications of TGLM supplementation on gut microbial balance, fermentation characteristics, carcass traits, and meat quality in rabbits.
