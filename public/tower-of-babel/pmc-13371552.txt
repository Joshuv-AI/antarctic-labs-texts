Multi-omics elucidation of temporal patterns in tanshinone accumulation in Salvia miltiorrhiza
Authors: Mingzhi Zhong, Fang Peng, Jian Yang, Can Yuan, Hailang Liao, Xiufu Wan, Long Zhou, Changqing Mao, Xinyi Zhao, Song Chen, Chao Zhang, Shan Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalvia miltiorrhiza is valued for tanshinones, but the dynamics and regulatory mechanisms of tanshinone accumulation from root expansion to harvest are unclear. This study investigated temporal changes in tanshinone content and their molecular basis in five germplasms. Tanshinone levels were monitored from June 2024 to January 2025. Phenotypic correlation, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA), and RT-qPCR validation were applied. Tanshinone content initially decreased then stabilized. Root branch number positively correlated with Tanshinone IIA (Tan IIA) and (Tan I) content, while root diameter negatively correlated with tanshinone IIA. Downregulation of key biosynthetic genes (SmHMGR3, SmMK, SmDXS1, SmDXS2, SmCYP76AK1) from late August was linked to reduced tanshinone accumulation. WGCNA identified a transcript–metabolite network with bHLH transcription factors as potential regulators. The temporal progression of tanshinone accumulation is associated with specific root morphological traits and coordinated downregulation of biosynthetic genes. These findings provide a theoretical basis for selecting high-yielding genotypes and optimizing harvest timing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08975-z.Keywords: Salvia miltiorrhiza, Tanshinones, Temporal dynamics, Germplasm resources

Introduction

The formation of quality in traditional Chinese medicinal herbs is a complex process driven by the accumulation of active pharmaceutical ingredients. Both genetic background and environmental factors affect the biosynthetic pathways of these compounds [1, 2]. Environmental conditions, including light, temperature, water and soil nutrition, can modulate plant secondary metabolism and lead to phenotypic plasticity in chemical composition [3]. However, the genetic makeup of a plant, represented by its germplasm, provides the primary blueprint that defines its inherent capacity for synthesizing and accumulating specific constituents [4]. Superior medicinal quality is therefore largely determined by access to elite germplasm resources, and the identification and selection of such genetic material remain essential for ensuring the consistency and efficacy of herbal medicines [5, 6].

S. miltiorrhiza, a perennial herb of the family Lamiaceae, is an important traditional Chinese medicine with a long history of clinical use [7]. Its medicinal properties arise mainly from two groups of bioactive compounds: water-soluble phenolic acids (e.g., salvianolic acid B) and lipophilic tanshinones. The tanshinones, a class of diterpenoid quinones, have attracted considerable research attention because of their pharmacological activities [8, 9]. Major bioactive tanshinones include tanshinone I, tanshinone IIA, cryptotanshinone and dihydrotanshinone I. Among these, tanshinone IIA is widely regarded as a principal quality marker for S. miltiorrhiza in several pharmacopoeias [10, 11]. Extensive studies have shown that tanshinones have broad therapeutic effects, particularly in preventing and treating cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, such as angina pectoris, myocardial infarction and atherosclerosis. Their mechanisms include anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-apoptotic and microcirculation-improving effects [12, 13]. These applications account for much of the high economic and medicinal value of S. miltiorrhiza in the global herbal market.

Tanshinones are diterpenoid quinones synthesized through two partially interconnected pathways: the plastidial 2-C-methyl-D-erythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway and the cytosolic mevalonate (MVA) pathway (Fig. S1). The MEP pathway provides isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP), the basic five-carbon units for the early steps of diterpene synthesis. Evidence also supports limited exchange of IPP/DMAPP between the MVA and MEP pathways. These precursors condense to form the universal diterpene intermediate geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP) in plastids [14, 15]. The committed step in tanshinone biosynthesis is the cyclisation of GGPP by a class II diterpene cyclase, copalyl diphosphate synthase (CPS), to yield copalyl diphosphate (CPP). A class I diterpene synthase, kaurene synthase-like (KSL), then converts CPP to miltiradiene, the central hydrocarbon scaffold for all tanshinones. Miltiradiene is subsequently exported to the cytoplasm, where it undergoes a series of oxidative reactions largely catalysed by cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (CYP450s), including CYP76AH1, yielding intermediates such as ferruginol [16, 17]. Further oxidation, rearrangement and quinonisation mediated by additional CYP450s and dehydrogenases ultimately produce the bioactive tanshinones, including tanshinone I, tanshinone IIA and cryptotanshinone [18, 19]. The final biosynthetic steps, including specific enzyme identities and reaction order, remain incompletely resolved.

S. miltiorrhiza is widely distributed across China, with populations extending from northeastern to southwestern regions, reflecting strong environmental adaptability. Traditional genuine producing areas include Shandong, Henan, Sichuan and Shaanxi provinces [20]. This broad distribution has facilitated the development of abundant germplasm resources and substantial genetic diversity. However, this genetic richness also results in considerable variation in quality, especially in tanshinone content, across germplasms and geo-ecotypes. For instance, ecotypes from Shandong province generally have higher tanshinone levels, whereas those from Sichuan province often show lower levels [21, 22]. This variation affects market circulation and clinical application and contributes to price disparities, with materials from genuine areas commanding higher prices, while materials with insufficient active constituents are often devalued or discarded. These disparities can create a disruptive “bad money driving out good” effect, in which inferior products displace higher-quality ones. Quality inconsistency also encourages adulteration, where roots of other Salvia species are mixed, intentionally or unintentionally, with genuine S. miltiorrhiza. Such practices compromise the stability, efficacy and safety of the traditional Chinese medicine supply chain.

To address these challenges and support sustainable development of the S. miltiorrhiza industry, two priorities are essential. First, the selection and breeding of elite cultivars with high and stable tanshinone content are required. Second, a detailed understanding of tanshinone accumulation dynamics throughout plant development is needed. This requires a systematic investigation of temporal patterns of tanshinone products and key intermediates, as well as expression profiles of the major enzyme genes (e.g., SmCPS, SmKSL, CYP450s) that control biosynthetic flux. Clarifying the relationships between gene expression, metabolite accumulation and tanshinone yield will reveal the regulatory mechanisms underlying tanshinone biosynthesis. This knowledge provides a scientific basis for optimizing agricultural practices, including determining suitable harvest time, and for accelerating molecular-assisted breeding of high-yielding S. miltiorrhiza varieties. Previous studies have shown that tanshinone levels increase from the seedling stage (January–February) to the root expansion phase (July–August) [23, 24]. However, few studies have examined tanshinone accumulation from the root expansion phase to maturity and harvest (January of the following year), limiting understanding of complete accumulation patterns.

We collected five S. miltiorrhiza germplasms from major producing regions across China. All germplasms were grown under uniform conditions in a controlled field at the Industrial Crops Research Institute of the Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences beginning in January 2024 to reduce environmental variation. From June 2024 to January 2025, samples were collected and analyzed for phenotypic traits, tanshinone content and integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic (multi-omics) analyses. This study aims to clarify the molecular basis of tanshinone biosynthesis during the critical period from root expansion (July–August) to maturity and harvest (January), and to provide a foundation for breeding high-tanshinone S. miltiorrhiza varieties.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

As shown in Table 1, root segments of one-year-old S. miltiorrhiza were collected from five locations: Gaodu Town, Mengyin County, Shandong (35.72°N, 117.95°E); Zhongcun Town, Pingyi County, Shandong (35.53°N, 117.70°E); Baoding City, Hebei (38.87°N, 115.48°E); Hexing Town, Zhongjiang County, Sichuan (30.99°N, 104.68°E); and Jiqing Town, Zhongjiang County, Sichuan (30.86°N, 104.65°E). All plant materials were cultivated varieties collected from local cultivation bases, not from wild sources. All materials were formally identified by Professor Chao Zhang of the Industrial Crops Research Institute, Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences, as Salvia miltiorrhiza Bunge (Lamiaceae). Voucher specimens have been deposited at the Herbarium of the Industrial Crops Research Institute, Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (specimen accession numbers: G97-2025-001 to G392-2025-005). The collection and use of plant materials complied with institutional, national and international guidelines. No specific permissions were required because all samples were obtained from cultivated fields with the permission of the local farmers and the research institute. The field studies were conducted in accordance with local legislation, and all necessary permissions and licences were obtained. All root segments were propagated vegetative and cultivated under uniform field conditions at the Industrial Crops Research Institute, Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Qingbaijiang District, Chengdu, Sichuan (30.70°N, 104.25°E). One hundred plants of each germplasm were established. The germplasms, designated G97, G161, G353, G210 and G392, were managed using consistent conventional field practices.

Source of the samples

We measured the tanshinone content of the experimental materials in January 2025 (the harvest period). The phenotypic traits and tanshinone contents of all experimental materials are presented in Figs. S2, S3 and Table 1. G97 exhibited lush above‑ground stems and leaves, prostrate growth, red roots, a high number of evenly thick root branches, and a tanshinone content of 0.87 ± 0.12%, which is 3.48‑fold higher than the minimum requirement (0.25%) specified in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025 edition) [25]. G353 showed low above‑ground biomass, semi‑erect growth, late shoot withering, numerous fibrous roots, red roots, and a tanshinone content of 1.13 ± 0.18%, equivalent to 4.52 times the pharmacopoeia standard. G161 displayed lush above‑ground stems and leaves, prostrate growth, relatively thick roots, high yield, and a tanshinone content of 0.61 ± 0.07% (2.44‑fold the pharmacopoeia limit). G210 had small leaves, thick stems, tall plants, easy lodging, early shoot withering, and a tanshinone content of 0.32 ± 0.03%. G392 exhibited low above‑ground biomass, semi‑erect growth, late shoot withering, thick and brittle roots with low lignification, high yield, light red roots, and a tanshinone content of 0.41 ± 0.05%. Among the five germplasms, G353 had the highest tanshinone content, significantly surpassing the other four materials. G97 ranked second, significantly higher than the remaining three. G161 had significantly lower tanshinone content than G97 and G353 but significantly higher than G210 and G392. No significant difference in tanshinone content was detected between G210 and G392. It is worth noting that all five germplasms have been continuously cultivated and evaluated for four years, during which their phenotypic traits and tanshinone contents remained stable, suggesting that these characteristics are primarily influenced by genetic effects.

Sample collection and size

All materials were cultivated under uniform conditions beginning in January 2024. Random sampling was performed using a five-point method on 26 June, 26 July, 26 August, 26 September, 26 October, 26 November and 26 December 2024, and 26 January 2025. At each time point, fifteen plants per germplasm were collected. The samples were used for phenotypic trait assessment, tanshinone quantification and transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses.

Phenotypic trait evaluation

For samples collected on 26 June, 26 July, 26 August, 26 September, 26 October, 26 November and 26 December 2024, and 26 January 2025, the following agronomic traits were measured: plant height, canopy spread, number of root branches, root length, number of shoot branches, shoot fresh weight, root fresh weight, root diameter and root dry weight. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 15).

Quantification of tanshinones

S. miltiorrhiza enters the root expansion phase in June and July, a stage associated with significant increases in tanshinone accumulation in the roots [26]. Quantification of tanshinone content therefore began on 26 June. Root samples collected on 26 June, 26 July, 26 August, 26 September, 26 October, 26 November and 26 December 2024, and 26 January 2025, were processed as follows. For each germplasm at each time point, fifteen biological replicates were used. Roots were dried at 50 °C to constant weight, then ground and passed through a 50-mesh sieve. For extraction, 0.3 g of powder was weighed accurately and transferred to a sealed conical flask, followed by addition of 50 ml methanol. After sealing and weighing the flask, the mixture was sonicated for 30 min (140 W, 42 kHz). The flask was cooled to room temperature and re-weighed, and any weight loss was compensated with methanol. The solution was mixed thoroughly and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. The filtrate was used as the final extract for analysis [27, 28].

Tanshinone levels were quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an octadecylsilyl (ODS) silica gel column. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (A) and 0.02% phosphoric acid solution (B), and a gradient elution programmer was applied as described in Table S1. The column temperature was maintained at 20 °C, and the detection wavelength was set at 270 nm for quantification of tanshinone IIA, tanshinone I and cryptotanshinone [24].

RNA-Seq and LC-MC analysis

Given the documented accumulation of tanshinones in the root bark of S. miltiorrhiza [29, 30], root bark samples of germplasm G353 collected on 26 June (G353B-01), 26 August (G353B-03), 26 November 2024 (G353B-06) and 26 January 2025 (G353B-08), each with three biological replicates, were selected for transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. Samples were flash-frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. Total RNA was extracted within 48 h. RNA purity and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (OD260/280 ratio between 1.9 and 2.1) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RNA Integrity Number, RIN ≥ 8.0). Subsequently, ribosomal RNA depletion and mRNA enrichment were performed, followed by reverse transcription to construct cDNA libraries. The cDNA libraries were prepared with an insert size of 300 bp, quantified to a concentration of 10 nM, and a volume of 20 µL per library was used for sequencing. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (150 bp paired-end reads).

Raw reads were filtered to remove reads containing adapters, reads with > 10% ambiguous bases (N), and low-quality reads (Q-score ≤ 20 for > 50% of bases). The cleaned high-quality reads were aligned to the S. miltiorrhiza reference genome (accession number: GWHAOSJ00000000) using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1) software. Gene expression levels were quantified, and genes with counts per million (CPM) ≥ 1 in at least three libraries were retained for further analysis. The cleaned data from the 26 September time point were used as the control group. Data from 26 June, 26 July and 26 October were compared individually with this control using a negative binomial generalized linear model (DESeq2). Genes with |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). DEGs were annotated using the Swiss-Prot database, and Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using a hypergeometric test with expressed genes as background. Terms with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant [31, 32]. RNA sequences data are available from the NCBI SRA under accession numbers PRJNA1371962.

Untargeted metabolomics profiling was conducted using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC® BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) at a column temperature of 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase B) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 95% A; 0.5–7 min, 95% A to 0% A; 7–10 min, 0% A; 10–10.1 min, 0% A to 95% A; 10.1–12 min, 95% A. The injection volume was 2 µL. Mass spectrometry was performed using a Q-Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in positive ion mode. The ion source parameters were set as follows: spray voltage, 3.5 kV; capillary temperature, 250 °C; sheath gas, 50 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas, 10 arbitrary units; and mass scan range, m/z 70–1000 [33]. Metabolites were annotated using the KEGG database. Using the 26 September samples as the control, comparisons were made with data from 26 June, 26 July and 26 October. Differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were defined using |log₂FC| ≥ 1, variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1 from OPLS-DA and p < 0.05 [34]. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was then employed to identify correlations between DEGs and DEMs. Association networks were visualized using Cytoscape to depict major molecular interactions [35, 36].

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the root bark of germplasm G353 collected on June 26, August 26, November 26, 2024, and January 26, 2025 using a Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (LABGENE, China). RNA purity and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer; only samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.9 and 2.1 and an RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 8.0 were used for subsequent analysis. The extracted RNA was subsequently reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using the TransScript® First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, China). Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis was performed on a CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad), and raw data were acquired using Bio-Rad CFX Maestro (version 2.2). Using SmActin (accession number: HM231319) and SmGAPDH (accession number: KF234408) as reference genes [37] and the June 26 samples as the control, relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [38]. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 9, representing three technical replicates for each of three biological replicates). Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t-test, with * indicating p < 0.05 and ** indicating p < 0.01.

Results

Phenotypic changes in S. miltiorrhiza at different time points

Plant height across the germplasms generally increased initially and then decreased. G161 showed greater plant height than the other germplasms from 26 June to 26 October, whereas G392 displayed higher plant height from 26 November to 26 December (Fig. 1A). Canopy spread increased progressively, with G161 consistently exhibiting the largest canopy at all sampling points (Fig. 1B). The number of shoot branches increased initially and then gradually declined (Fig. 1C). Shoot fresh weight rose initially and then decreased; G161 maintained higher values than the other germplasms from 26 September 2024 to 26 January 2025 (Fig. 1D), indicating an advantage in above-ground traits.

Statistical analysis of agronomic traits in S. miltiorrhiza at different time points. A Plant height. B Canopy spread. C Number of branches. D Shoot fresh weight. E Number of root branches. F Root diameter. G Root length. H Root fresh weight. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 15)

The number of root branches showed no clear temporal trend and generally fluctuated between 10 and 26 across all germplasms from 26 June onward (Fig. 1E). Root diameter increased gradually, with a more pronounced rise after 26 August. G392 showed larger root diameter from 26 September 2024 to 26 January 2025 (Fig. 1F). Root length also increased steadily (Fig. 1G). Root fresh weight increased initially and then declined, with G392 and G161 showing higher values from 26 October 2024 to 26 January 2025 (Fig. 1H). These observations indicate that G392 displays favourable root morphological traits during growth and development.

Temporal variation in tanshinone content among different S. miltiorrhiza germplasms and their correlation with phenotypic traits

Quantification of tanshinones in the root bark of different S. miltiorrhiza germplasms across multiple time points revealed an initial decline followed by stabilization for tanshinone IIA, tanshinone I, cryptotanshinone, dihydrotanshinone I and danshenxinkun A from 26 June 2024 to 26 January 2025. Among the germplasms, G353 consistently showed relatively high levels of these tanshinones. In G353, tanshinone IIA, tanshinone I, cryptotanshinone and dihydrotanshinone I decreased significantly from 26 August, with tanshinone I reaching its lowest level on 26 January 2025 (Fig. 2A–D). In contrast, danshenxinkun A showed a significant decrease from 26 July, and levels from 26 August 2024 to 26 January 2025 were significantly lower than those from 26 June to 26 July 2024 (Fig. 2E). Representative chromatograms are presented in Fig. S4. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between phenotypic traits and tanshinone content. No significant correlations were identified between above-ground traits and any tanshinones. Among root traits, neither root fresh weight nor root length showed significant correlations with tanshinone content. A significant positive correlation was identified between the number of root branches and the contents of tanshinone IIA and tanshinone I, whereas root diameter showed a significant negative correlation with tanshinone IIA content. These findings suggest that a higher number of thinner roots favours tanshinone accumulation in S. miltiorrhiza (Fig. 2F).

Because the contents of tanshinone I, cryptotanshinone, and dihydrotanshinone I in G353 were higher than those in the other germplasms at all time points, and its tanshinone IIA and danshenxinkun A contents were also higher than those in the other germplasms at most time points (Fig. 2), and considering that materials with high tanshinone content offer greater advantages for subsequent screening of elite germplasms and breeding of new improved varieties, we selected G353 for further investigation. The samples of G353 collected from 26 June 2024 to 26 January 2025 were designated G353B-01 to G353B-08, respectively. Based on the observed trends in tanshinone levels, four critical time points were selected for integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses: 26 June (G353B-01), 26 August (G353B-03), 26 November (G353B-06) and 26 January 2025 (G353B-08).

Clustering of transcriptome and metabolomics expression trends in G353 across four time points

To investigate the molecular basis of temporal changes in tanshinone accumulation, root bark samples of G353 were collected at four time points: 26 June 2024 (G353B-01), 26 August 2024 (G353B-03), 26 November 2024 (G353B-06) and 26 January 2025 (G353B-08). Each time point included three biological replicates. These samples were used for RNA-Seq and LC-MS analyses. Principal component analysis (PCA) of both transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets showed clear group separation and high intra-group consistency (Fig. S5), confirming high data quality.

Based on the temporal trends of tanshinone content, the expression profiles of 25,226 genes were clustered into six groups. Among these, Cluster 1, containing 3,228 genes, showed an expression pattern characterized by an initial decline followed by stabilization (Fig. 3A, B). This pattern paralleled the observed decline in tanshinone accumulation and suggests potential regulatory roles for these genes in tanshinone biosynthesis and deposition.

Similarly, 1,717 metabolites were grouped into six clusters according to their accumulation patterns. The 331 metabolites in Cluster 1 also showed an initial decline followed by stabilization (Fig. 3C, D), consistent with tanshinone trends. Integrating these transcriptomic and metabolomic components provides a foundation for constructing a transcript–metabolite network related to tanshinone biosynthesis.

Further analysis of the metabolome revealed the accumulation levels of metabolites and their derivatives involved in the tanshinone biosynthetic pathway across four time points. Metabolites including Phosphoenolpyruvic acid, Geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate, Mevalonic acid, Neocryptotanshinone, Nortanshinone, Tanshinone I, Sugiol, Cryptotanshinone, Tanshinone IIA, and Dihydrotanshinone I exhibited relatively high accumulation at the G353-01 time point (June 26), and their levels gradually decreased over time at the G353-03 (August 26), G353-06 (November 26), and G353-08 (January 26 of the following year) time points (Fig. 4). This trend is consistent with the changes in tanshinone components measured by HPLC at different time points described above.

Regulatory network and pathway enrichment of genes and metabolites in transcriptome- metabolomics cluster 1 across four time points

A correlation network was constructed using the genes and metabolites in Cluster 1 from both datasets, establishing a potential transcript–metabolite regulatory network. This network comprised 485 genes and 94 metabolites, forming 1,039 regulatory relationships, including 647 positive and 392 negative correlations. As tanshinones are terpenoid compounds, the network showed predominant enrichment in categories such as terpenoids, phenolic acids, lignans/coumarins and flavonoids (Fig. 5A), suggesting involvement of these genes and metabolites in tanshinone metabolism. KEGG enrichment analysis of these genes and metabolites revealed significant associations with pathways including “Metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides”, “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis”, “Transcription factors”, “Sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis” and “Terpenoid backbone biosynthesis” (Fig. 5B). These findings further support a regulatory role for this transcript–metabolite network in tanshinone biosynthesis, particularly in downstream terpenoid pathways.

Differentially expressed genes and metabolites between two time points of extreme tanshinone levels

To identify gene modules associated with tanshinone metabolism, two sampling points with the most contrasting tanshinone levels were selected from the four stages: 26 June 2024 (highest tanshinone content) and 26 January 2025 (lowest tanshinone content). A heatmap illustrated the gene expression profiles of three biological replicates at these two time points (Fig. 6A). Differential expression analysis, visualized using a volcano plot with thresholds of |log₂FC| > 2 and p < 0.05, identified 1,845 significantly down-regulated and 3,144 up-regulated genes (Fig. 6B). GO enrichment analysis showed significant associations with processes related to secondary metabolism, growth and development, and transcriptional regulation (Fig. 6C).

A corresponding metabolomic analysis was conducted for the same two time points. A heatmap showed clear differences in metabolite accumulation across biological replicates (Fig. 6D). Differential accumulation analysis using |log₂FC| > 2 and p < 0.05 identified 198 significantly up-regulated and 233 down-regulated metabolites (Fig. 6E). KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted pathways such as diterpenoid biosynthesis, biosynthesis of terpenoids and steroids, flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and ubiquinone and other terpenoid–quinone biosynthesis (Fig. 6F).

Mapping the differentially expressed genes and metabolites onto the tanshinone biosynthetic pathway revealed significant downregulation of key enzyme genes, including 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase 3 (SmHMGR3), Mevalonate kinase (SmMK), 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate synthase 1 (SmDXS1), 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate synthase 2 (SmDXS2) and Cytochrome P450 monooxygenase CYP76AK1 (SmCYP76AK1) (Fig. 7A, B). Several pivotal metabolites and derivatives, such as methyltanshinoate, 15,16-dihydrotanshinone I, dihydroisotanshinone I, 1,2,15,16-tetrahydrotanshinone I and tanshinone IIA, also showed significant decreases (Fig. 7C). These results indicate that the initial decline followed by stabilization in tanshinone levels from 26 June 2024 to 26 January 2025 is primarily explained by coordinated downregulation of key enzymatic genes and associated metabolites.

WGCNA reveals a transcript-metabolite regulatory network underlying tanshinone biosynthesis

To refine the identification of key modules related to tanshinone biosynthesis, we integrated the genes and metabolites in Cluster 1 across the four time points with those identified in the comparison between the two extreme time points, 26 January and 26 June. The overlap included 1,141 genes and 129 metabolites, suggesting a strong potential regulatory relationship. Among these genes, 7.2% were transcription factors (e.g., bHLH, C2H2, MYB), which may regulate 8.5% of the oxygenated organic compounds among the shared metabolites (Fig. 8A).

A transcription factor-mediated transcript–metabolite regulatory network was subsequently constructed. bHLH transcription factors exhibited the highest connectivity. In particular, EVM0017292 (SmbHLH87), EVM0026409 (SmbHLH139) and EVM0007220 (SmUPBEAT1) were predicted to regulate the largest number of metabolites in the tanshinone pathway, including tanshinone I, cryptotanshinone, tanshinone IIA and shikimic acid (Fig. 8B), suggesting essential regulatory roles for these genes in tanshinone biosynthesis.

RT-qPCR validation of key gene expression patterns

To validate the transcriptome data and further assess temporal regulation of tanshinone biosynthesis, RT-qPCR was performed on root bark samples of G353 collected on 26 June, 26 August and 26 November 2024, and 26 January 2025. Using the 26 June sample as the control, SmHMGR3, SmMK, SmDXS1, SmDXS2, SmbHLH87, SmbHLH139 and SmUPBEAT1 were significantly down-regulated on 26 August, 26 November and 26 January. For SmCYP76AK1, no significant difference was detected on 26 August, whereas its expression was significantly lower on 26 November and 26 January (Fig. 9).

RT-qPCR analysis of genes involved in tanshinone biosynthesis

Overall, the RT-qPCR results were consistent with the transcriptome data. The progressive decrease in expression of key biosynthetic genes, including SmHMGR3, SmMK, SmDXS1, SmDXS2 and SmCYP76AK1, corresponded to the observed decline in tanshinone content. These findings indicate that the initial decline followed by stabilization in tanshinone accumulation from 26 June 2024 to 26 January 2025 is likely regulated by the temporal expression patterns of these genes.

Discussion

Temporal variation in tanshinone accumulation and the influence of yield-related traits

Tanshinone accumulation exhibited a clear temporal pattern characterized by an initial decline followed by stabilization from 26 June 2024 to 26 January 2025. This trend is partially consistent with previous findings. For instance, a study of S. miltiorrhiza from Hebei Province reported that tanshinone IIA content showed an increase–decrease–increase fluctuation, with a marked decrease during the late growth stage approaching harvest (October–November) (Liu et al., 2012). Other research showed that tanshinone IIA reached its peak approximately 150 days after transplantation, which corresponds to June–July. It then declined gradually during the subsequent growth period (Guo et al., 2005). In contrast, a two-year cultivation study in Australia found that tanshinone levels reached their maximum during winter in both the first (day 290) and second (day 580) years, then decreased as temperatures rose and plants resumed active growth [39]. Together, these observations indicate that the trajectory of tanshinone accumulation varies across geo-ecotypes and developmental timelines.

The decreasing trend in tanshinone content observed in G353 from the root expansion phase (around August) to maturity (January) may reflect developmental adjustments in resource allocation. As plants shift from vigorous growth into dormancy preparation, photosynthetic carbon may be directed towards storage reserves or structural components rather than specialized metabolites such as tanshinones [40–42]. The gradual decline may also result from a dilution effect, as rapidly expanding root biomass reduces metabolite concentration or from degradation and conversion of tanshinones in older tissues [43, 44]. Furthermore, the progressive decrease in ambient temperature and shortening of day length during late autumn and winter may directly suppress the catalytic activity of enzymes involved in tanshinone biosynthesis, thereby reducing the rate of de novo synthesis [45].

Correlation analysis showed that root morphological traits exert a significant effect on tanshinone accumulation. A significant positive correlation was observed between the number of root branches and the contents of tanshinone IIA and tanshinone I, whereas root diameter showed a significant negative correlation with tanshinone IIA content (Fig. 3F). These findings suggest that denser and finer root systems favour tanshinone accumulation. This can be attributed to the spatial localization of tanshinones, which are predominantly synthesized and stored in the root periderm [46]. Finer roots possess a greater surface area-to-volume ratio and a larger proportion of bark tissue relative to the central xylem, resulting in greater biosynthetic and storage capacity per unit biomass [47]. Previous research has shown that tanshinone levels increase from the seedling stage to the root expansion phase [24, 26]. During this peak period, the root system is not yet fully expanded, resulting in a relatively larger bark area per unit weight, correlating with higher tanshinone accumulation. As root diameter increases, tanshinone content declines correspondingly [27, 48]. These results highlight the importance of root architectural traits in breeding programmers and indicate that selecting genotypes with finer and more highly branched root systems may enhance tanshinone yield.

Molecular mechanisms of tanshinone biosynthesis revealed by key genes and metabolites across time points

Key enzymes in the tanshinone biosynthetic pathway are recognized as central regulators of tanshinone production. Extensive studies have shown that genes such as SmHMGR3, SmMK, SmDXS1, SmDXS2 and SmCYP76AK1 promote tanshinone biosynthesis. Overexpression of SmHMGR or SmDXR in S. miltiorrhiza hairy roots increased total tanshinone production, and combined overexpression produced a larger enhancement [49]. Precision editing of functional sites in SmHMGR3 using prime editing resulted in an approximately twofold increase in tanshinone I content, together with higher levels of downstream diterpenoids [50]. SmMK and SmCYP76AK1 have also been identified as positive regulators of tanshinone biosynthesis [51–53]. In this study, the expression levels of these key genes began to decrease significantly from 26 August relative to 26 June (Figs. 7 and 9), likely contributing to the decline in tanshinone content after 26 July. This developmental downregulation may be influenced by several factors. Seasonal cues such as decreasing day length and falling temperatures can affect secondary metabolic gene expression [54, 55]. Internal developmental programmers may also redirect transcriptional activity away from specialized metabolite pathways towards storage, defense and stress-related processes as the plant prepares for dormancy [56].

Transcription factors also play essential regulatory roles in tanshinone biosynthesis. Overexpression of SmbHLH10 and SmbHLH148 enhances tanshinone production. SmbHLH10 activates key MEP pathway genes (DXS2, DXS3, DXR) as well as downstream genes such as CPS1, CPS5 and CYP76AH1. SmbHLH148 similarly activates many core pathway genes, promoting tanshinone accumulation [57, 58]. In contrast, SmbHLH3 and SmbHLH92 function as negative regulators. SmbHLH3 suppresses genes such as KSL1 and CYP76AH1, reducing tanshinone accumulation, while RNAi-mediated inhibition of SmbHLH92 leads to upregulation of multiple key biosynthetic genes and increased tanshinone levels [59, 60]. In the present study, the bHLH transcription factors SmbHLH87, SmbHLH139 and the subfamily gene SmUPBEAT1 showed significant co-expression with tanshinone IIA, tanshinone I and dihydrotanshinone I (Fig. 9), suggesting that these transcription factors may participate in regulating tanshinone biosynthesis. These observations warrant further experimental investigation to clarify their molecular roles.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the dynamics of tanshinone accumulation in the roots of S. miltiorrhiza beginning from the expansion phase. Across the investigated germplasms, tanshinone levels showed a gradual decline followed by stabilization. A significant positive correlation was identified between the number of root branches and the contents of tanshinone IIA and tanshinone I, whereas root diameter exhibited a significant negative correlation with tanshinone IIA content. Multi-omics analyses demonstrated that the significant downregulation of key biosynthetic genes, including SmHMGR3, SmMK, SmDXS1, SmDXS2 and SmCYP76AK1, beginning in late August likely contributed to the progressive decrease in tanshinone content observed from late July onward. These findings provide practical insight for agricultural production, indicating that genotypes with more numerous and finer roots may display higher tanshinone levels. Overall, this work improves understanding of the temporal patterns of tanshinone accumulation and offers a theoretical basis for breeding new S. miltiorrhiza varieties with enhanced tanshinone content.
