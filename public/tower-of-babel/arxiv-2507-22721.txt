Continuity of critical points for 1-dimensional non-local energies
D. Carazzato, N. Fusco, A. Pratelli

Abstract
. In this paper we deal with the bounded critical points of a Riesz energy of attractive-repulsive type in dimension 1. Under suitable assumptions on the growth of the kernel in the origin, we are able to prove that they are continuous inside their support.



1.
Introduction

In the recent years the mathematical community has been more and more interested about energies related to attractive-repulsive forces. To be more specific, we are interested in a functional of convolution type, that in its maximal generality can be defined in the class of probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\mathcal{E}(\mu)=\iint g(x-y)d\mu(x)d\mu(y)\,,

(1.1)

where
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,+\infty]
is the kernel describing the interaction. For instance, when
g
is radial and it is not radially monotone, then the minimization of
\mathcal{E}
is a non-trivial problem, and can lead to a large variety of behaviors. Notice that in several models a part of the total energy
\mathcal{E}
is given by a monotone kernel. See for example some works about different versions of the Gamow model
[
17
,
18
,
13
,
4
,
3
]
. We also mention
[
15
]
for a comparison between different attractive-repulsive models containing the Riesz energy. The prototypical kernel
g
that describes an attractive-repulsive interaction is of the form

g(x)=\frac{|x|^{\alpha}}{\alpha}-\frac{|x|^{\lambda}}{\lambda}\,,

where
\alpha>0
and
-d<\lambda<\alpha
, and many questions are still open even for these simple functions. This kind of energies appear in many different models, and they have been intensively studied both from the analytical and the numerical point of view. In fact, some very general existence result is available (see Section
1.2
), whereas only in some very special cases a precise formula for the minimizers has been found (see
[
10
, Section 5]
,
[
14
,
16
]
). Their shape changes even more dramatically when the kernel is not singular in the origin (see
[
7
,
11
,
12
,
6
]
). Instead, some regularity results valid for singular kernels are contained in
[
8
]
, where they exploit the specific singularity of the kernel, related to the fractional Laplacian, to address the problem via PDE techniques. Our starting point is
[
5
]
, where, loosely speaking, the authors assume that
g
satisfies a differential inequality and obtain an
L^{\infty}
estimate for the minimizers of
\mathcal{E}
(see Theorem
1.2
in Section
1.2
for the precise result).

In this article, we obtain the internal continuity of the bounded local minimizers of
\mathcal{E}
; more precisely, we consider measures which are not necessarily minimizers, but which have constant potential inside their support (see (
1.3
) for the definition of potential). This is more general, since the constancy of the potential is ensured for all the local minimizers by the Euler-Lagrange conditions.

We stress that we are interested in the
inner regularity
, i.e. we will deal only with points in the interior of the support of a measure. In fact, in some cases minimizers are known to be bounded away from
0
near boundary points of their support (see for example the discussion in
[
8
]
and the anisotropic version contained in
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
, which are related to kernels). We also refer to
[
16
]
for some explicit minimizers, that are unbounded in some cases. Some partial information about the boundary behaviour is available, only for minimizers, applying
[
5
, Lemma 3.11]
, where they exploit the complete Euler-Lagrange conditions (
1.4
). Our main theorem, namely Theorem A, regards a kernel
g
that satisfies some hypotheses, stated below, and considers optimal measures which are already known to be
L^{\infty}
. This extra assumption is in fact very mild, since optimal measures are actually known to be bounded under assumptions which are only slightly stronger, as we will describe in Section
1.2
. Moreover, it is known that some hypotheses about the behaviour of
g
near the origin are necessary to have some regularity. In particular, if the kernel
g
is not singular, then we may also have some minimizers that are not absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure (see for example
[
7
,
11
,
12
]
). We remark, however, that the singularity of the kernel is not sufficient to have the absolute continuity of the minimizers, as it is showed in
[
16
, Theorem 1]
. Furthermore, even if the minimizers admit a density with respect to
\mathscr{L}^{d}
, they may not be bounded, while the control in
L^{\infty}
is necessary in our proof (see
[
10
, Section 5]
and
[
16
, Theorem 2]
where they even find explicitly the minimizers for some special kernels). Notice, however, that their explicit minimizers are always bounded when the space dimension
d
is 1. In this sense, we observe that they are not an obstruction to our 1-dimensional study, while in principle it is possible that there exists a kernel
g
for which the energy
\mathcal{E}
admits unbounded minimizers in dimension 1.

1.1.
Setting

In this paper, we consider a kernel
g
satisfying the following assumptions:

(i)

g
belongs to
C^{1}\big(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\big)\cap L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R})
, it is symmetric, and it is decreasing in
(0,r)
for some length-scale
r>0
;

(ii)

g
is convex in
(0,r)
,
g^{\prime}
is concave in
(0,r)
,
g^{\prime}\in BV_{loc}((0,+\infty))
, and there exists
\Lambda\in(1,+\infty]
such that

\liminf_{x\to 0}\frac{|g^{\prime}(x/2)|}{|g^{\prime}(x)|}=\Lambda\,.

(1.2)

Remark 1.1
.

We notice that any prototypical kernel of the form

g(x)=\frac{|x|^{\alpha}}{\alpha}-\frac{|x|^{\lambda}}{\lambda}\,,

for
\alpha>0
and
-1<\lambda<\min\{1,\alpha\}
satisfies our hypotheses, where the expression
|x|^{\lambda}/\lambda
is intended to be
\log|x|
when
\lambda=0
.

Our main result is the following theorem (we refer to Section
1.2
for the definition of the potential
\psi_{f}
).

Theorem A
(Continuity of critical functions).
Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty]
be a function satisfying hypotheses
(i)-(ii)
. If
f\mathscr{L}^{1}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
is a finite measure with
{\rm spt}f=[a,b]
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}=M<+\infty
and
\psi_{f}
is constant almost everywhere in
[a,b]
, then
f
is continuous in
(a,b)
.

Structure of the paper

In Section
1.2
we introduce some notation and we expose some known results. We also provide the definition of essential limits in Definition
1.3
, that are the “Lebesgue compliant” version of the usual limits, which are necessary since we work with a density that is well defined almost everywhere.

In Section
2
we introduce the tools to look at the second derivative of a potential, since our strategy is to show that the second derivative of the potential cannot be
0
at some point
\bar{x}
if
f
does not admit essential limit at
\bar{x}
. This procedure needs a preliminary step, where we regularize
f
by convolution, and this is very convenient in our problem since the potential behaves well in terms of convolutions. In Section
2.1
, we use the hypotheses on
g
to show some cancellation phenomena, yielding to Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
. With these results at hand, it is not hard to prove Lemma
2.4
, that provides the leading term in our estimates of the main theorem. However, one has to be careful: the expression that we use for the second derivative is obtained from an integration by parts, and this is not always well defined even if the density is smooth because
g^{\prime}
is not locally integrable; more precisely, our formulas work only if one considers intervals starting at critical points of the fuction. As a consequence, we will estimate the potential only in some critical points of the regularized function
f_{\delta}
; Section
2.2
will be devoted to the careful choice of some critical points. The final Section
3
is then devoted to the proof of Theorem A, where we combine the various estimates found before to show that the second derivative of
f_{\delta}
cannot be constantly
0
if the convolution parameter is small enough.

1.2.
Preliminary results

This class of problems has been intensively studied, and some known results are the starting point for our analysis. To begin with, there exists a minimizer, and this was proved for example in
[
20
,
2
,
5
]
, where very weak assumptions are needed. Moreover, it was recently proved that any minimizer is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathscr{L}^{1}
and its density is an
L^{\infty}
function:

Theorem 1.2

(​​
[
5
, Proposition 3.4]
)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty]
be a function satisfying
(i)
and, in addition,
g
is strictly convex in
(0,+\infty)
. Then any minimizer
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
of (
1.1
) is of class
L^{\infty}
and
{\rm spt}\mu=[a,b]
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

We define now a fundamental tool, the so-called
potential
induced by a measure, which is defined pointwise by

\psi_{\mu}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x-y)d\mu(y).

(1.3)

Of course, this is nothing else than
g*\mu
, it is lower semicontinuous whenever so is
g
, and
\mathcal{E}(\mu)=\int\psi_{\mu}(x)d\mu(x)
. This function plays a crucial role since it appears in the Euler-Lagrange conditions satisfied by a minimizer
\mu
(see for instance
[
1
,
8
,
5
]
):

\begin{cases}\psi_{\mu}=\mathcal{E}(\mu)&\mu-a.e.\,,\\
\psi_{\mu}\geq\mathcal{E}(\mu)&in\ \mathbb{R}\setminus{\rm spt}\mu\,.\end{cases}

(1.4)

In the sequel, when working with an absolutely continuous measure
\mu=f\mathscr{L}^{1}
, we will write
\psi_{f}
in place of
\psi_{\mu}
.

Definition 1.3

(Essential directional limits)
.

Given a function
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}
, we say that
l\in\mathbb{R}
is the
essential liminf from the left
of
F
at
\bar{x}
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\eta>0
such that

\begin{cases}\mathscr{L}^{1}(\{F>l-\varepsilon\}\cap(\bar{x}-\eta,\bar{x}))=\eta\,,\\
\mathscr{L}^{1}(\{F<l+\varepsilon\}\cap(\bar{x}-\eta,\bar{x}))>0\,.\end{cases}

In this case, we write
l=\essliminf_{t\to\bar{x}^{-}}F(t)
. Similar definitions can be given for the essential limsup and for the limits from the right. If all of the four essential limits coincide, then we say that
F
admits essential limit at
\bar{x}
.

2.
Second derivative of the potential and selection of critical points

Roughly speaking, our goal is to study the second derivative of
\psi_{f}
, and to check that it cannot be
0
when
f
is not continuous. In order to make this in a formally correct way, we will regularize
f
so to work with a smooth potential. To do that, we fix a symmetric mollifier
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
such that
{\rm spt}\rho=[-1,1]
,
\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{1}=1
,
\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
, and
\rho^{\prime}<0
in
(0,1)
. Then, we set as usual
\rho_{\delta}(t)=\rho(t/\delta)/\delta
, and we consider the smooth function
f_{\delta}=f*\rho_{\delta}
, which has compact support; moreover, since the potential
\psi_{f}
is constant in
(a,b)
, then the potential
\psi_{f_{\delta}}
is constant in
(a+\delta,b-\delta)
. The proof will come by the fact that, if
f
is not continuous, then
\psi_{f_{\delta}}^{\prime\prime}
cannot be
0
at some critical point of
f_{\delta}
. The reason to consider a critical point is merely technical; in fact, we will use an alternative formula for
\psi_{f_{\delta}}^{\prime\prime}
obtained integrating by parts, that can be justified only if the point where we compute that derivative is a critical point of
f_{\delta}
. The contradiction will then provide the thesis.

Let us be more precise. Let
F\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be a generic smooth function, and let
x
be a critical point for
F
. Then, we have the following expression for
\psi_{F}^{\prime\prime}(x)
:

\psi_{F}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F^{\prime\prime}(t)g(x-t)\,dt=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}F^{\prime}(t)\frac{d}{dt}g(t-x)\,dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sign(x-t)F^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(|x-t|)\,dt\,.

(2.1)

The fact that the integrals containing first derivatives are well defined is true since
x
is a critical point of
F
(which implies
|F^{\prime}(t)|\lesssim|t-x|
since
F
is smooth), and in view of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty]
be a function in
L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
such that
g^{\prime}\leq 0
in an interval
(0,r)
. Then

-\int_{0}^{r}g^{\prime}(t)t<+\infty\,.

Proof.

For any
t\in(0,r)
we write
g(r)-g(t)=\int_{t}^{r}g^{\prime}(s)\,ds
. Then, since
g^{\prime}
has constant sign in
(0,r)
, we can apply Fubini Theorem and see that

\int_{0}^{r}g(r)-g(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{r}\,dt\int_{t}^{r}g^{\prime}(s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{r}\,ds\int_{0}^{s}g^{\prime}(s)\,dt=\int_{0}^{r}sg^{\prime}(s)ds\,.

This concludes the proof since
g\in L^{1}([0,r])
.
∎

2.1.
Cancellation lemmas

We are going to manipulate the expression (
2.1
), and we obtain some inequalities for the contribution due to the integral between two critical points in that expression.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty]
satisfy
(i)-(ii)
. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
be given, with
\alpha<\beta
and
\beta-\alpha<r
. Let
F\in C^{2}([\alpha,\beta])
be a function with
F(\alpha)=F(\beta)
, and such that
\alpha
and
\beta
are absolute minimum points of
F
in
[\alpha,\beta]
. If
F^{\prime}(\beta)=0
, then for every
x\geq\beta
with
x-\alpha<r
we have that

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}F^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(x-t)\,dt\geq 0\,.

If, instead,
F^{\prime}(\alpha)=0
, then for every
x\leq\alpha
with
\beta-x<r
we have that

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}-F^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-x)\,dt\geq 0\,.

Proof.

First of all, we observe that Lemma
2.1
guarantees that the integral is finite. It is also easy to check that the second inequality can be deduced from the first one considering the function
G(t)=F(-t)
defined in the interval
[-\beta,-\alpha]
, so we will just prove the first one. Moreover, it is sufficient to prove the result when
F^{\prime}
changes sign only once: the general result can be obtained approximating
F
with functions whose derivative has a finite number of sign changes (see the proof of Lemma
2.4
, where this procedure is slightly more complex). Therefore, we need to prove the result when there exists
\xi\in(\alpha,\beta)
such that
F_{1}=\left.F\right|_{(\alpha,\xi)}
is monotone increasing,
F_{2}=\left.F\right|_{(\xi,\beta)}
is monotone decreasing, and
F^{\prime}\neq 0
in
(\alpha,\beta)\setminus\{\xi\}
. With this reduction, we use the change of variables
z=F_{1}(t)
and
w=F_{2}(t)
to get that

\begin{split}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}F^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(x-t)\,dt&=\int_{\alpha}^{\xi}F_{1}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(x-t)\,dt+\int_{\xi}^{\beta}F_{2}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(x-t)\,dt\\
&=\int_{F_{1}(\alpha)}^{F_{1}(\xi)}g^{\prime}(x-F_{1}^{-1}(z))\,dz-\int_{F_{2}(\beta)}^{F_{2}(\xi)}g^{\prime}(x-F_{2}^{-1}(w))\,dw\\
&=\int_{F(\alpha)}^{F(\xi)}\left[g^{\prime}(x-F_{1}^{-1}(z))-g^{\prime}(x-F_{2}^{-1}(z))\right]\,dz.\end{split}

For every
z\in[F(\alpha),F(\xi)]
we have that
x-F_{2}^{-1}(z)\leq x-F_{1}^{-1}(z)<r
, and since
g
is convex in
(0,r)
, then the function inside the integral is non-negative, concluding the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty]
satisfy
(i)-(ii)
. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
be given, with
\alpha<\beta
and
\beta-\alpha<r
. If
F\in C^{2}([\alpha,\beta])
,
\alpha
is an absolute minimum point of
F
in
[\alpha,\beta]
and
F^{\prime}(\alpha)=0
, then for every
x\leq y\leq\alpha
with
\beta-x<r
we have that

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}F^{\prime}(t)(g^{\prime}(t-x)-g^{\prime}(t-y))\,dt\geq 0\,.

Proof.

We use a cancellation principle similar to the one of Lemma
2.2
, this time relying on the concavity of
g^{\prime}
. As before, by approximation it is sufficient to prove the result when there exists
\xi\in(\alpha,\beta)
such that
F_{1}=\left.F\right|_{(\alpha,\xi)}
is monotone increasing,
F_{2}=\left.F\right|_{(\xi,\beta)}
is monotone decreasing, and
F^{\prime}\neq 0
in
(\alpha,\beta)\setminus\{\xi\}
. Using the change of varibles
z=F_{1}(t)
and
w=F_{2}(t)
we arrive to

\begin{split}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}F^{\prime}(t)(g^{\prime}(t-&x)-g^{\prime}(t-y))\,dt=\int_{F_{1}(\alpha)}^{F_{1}(\xi)}g^{\prime}(F_{1}^{-1}(z)-x)-g^{\prime}(F_{1}^{-1}(z)-y)\,dz\\
&\qquad-\int_{F_{2}(\beta)}^{F_{2}(\xi)}g^{\prime}(F_{2}^{-1}(w)-x)-g^{\prime}(F_{2}^{-1}(w)-y)\,dw\\
&\geq\int_{F(\alpha)}^{F(\xi)}g^{\prime}(F_{1}^{-1}(z)-x)-g^{\prime}(F_{2}^{-1}(z)-x)\,dz\\
&\qquad-\int_{F(\alpha)}^{F(\xi)}g^{\prime}(F_{1}^{-1}(z)-y)-g^{\prime}(F_{2}^{-1}(z)-y)\,dz.\end{split}

For any
z\in[F(\alpha),F(\xi)]
we have that
F_{1}^{-1}(z)-x\leq F_{2}^{-1}(z)-x
and
F_{1}^{-1}(z)-y\leq F_{2}^{-1}(z)-y
, and since
g^{\prime}
is concave in
(0,r)
, with
\beta-x<r
, then

g^{\prime}(F_{1}^{-1}(z)-x)-g^{\prime}(F_{2}^{-1}(z)-x)\geq g^{\prime}(F_{1}^{-1}(z)-y)-g^{\prime}(F_{2}^{-1}(z)-y)\qquad\forall z\in[F(\alpha),F(\xi)],

and the inequality is proved.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty]
satisfy
(i)-(ii)
. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha<\beta
and
\beta-\alpha=\gamma<r
. If
F\in C^{2}([\alpha,\,\beta])
with
F^{\prime}(\alpha)=F^{\prime}(\beta)=0
and
\alpha
and
\beta
are respectively an absolute minimum and an absolute maximum of
F
in that interval, then

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}F^{\prime}(t)\big(|g^{\prime}(t-\alpha)|+|g^{\prime}(\beta-t)|\big)\,dt\geq(F(\beta)-F(\alpha))\big(|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|+|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\big)\,.

(2.2)

Proof.

First of all we observe that, since
F^{\prime}=0
in
\alpha,\,\beta
and
F^{\prime}
is Lipschitz, then the integrals are finite thanks to Lemma
2.1
. We can easily check that (
2.2
) is valid under the additional assumption that
F
is increasing. Indeed, keeping in mind that
g
is convex and
g^{\prime}\leq 0
in
(0,\gamma)
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}F^{\prime}(t)|g^{\prime}(t-\alpha)|\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\alpha}^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}F^{\prime}(t)|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\,dt+\int_{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}^{\beta}F^{\prime}(t)|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|\,dt\,,

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}F^{\prime}(t)|g^{\prime}(\beta-t)|\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\alpha}^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}F^{\prime}(t)|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|\,dt+\int_{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}^{\beta}F^{\prime}(t)|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\,dt\,,

and adding these two inequalities we get the thesis. Notice that this proof does not require that
F\in C^{2}
; indeed, it is sufficient that
F
is a Lipschitz function such that

|F^{\prime}(\alpha+s)|+|F^{\prime}(\beta-s)|\leq Cs\,,\qquad\hbox{for some $C>0$ and a.e. $\begin{aligned} 0\leq s\leq\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\end{aligned}$}\,.

(2.3)

Let us now consider the general case when

is not necessarily monotone. Assume first that, as in Figure

, and call
\widetilde{F}
the function, depicted in red in the figure, which equals
F
outside the interval
[\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}]
, and which is constantly equal to
F(\alpha^{\prime})=F(\beta^{\prime})
in

.

(2.4)

In the very same way,

\int_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{\beta^{\prime}}F^{\prime}(t)|g^{\prime}(\beta-t)|\,dt=\int_{F(\alpha^{\prime})}^{F(\gamma^{\prime})}|g^{\prime}(\beta-F_{1}^{-1}(s))|\,ds-\int_{F(\alpha^{\prime})}^{F(\gamma^{\prime})}|g^{\prime}(\beta-F_{2}^{-1}(s))|\,ds\,.

(2.5)

Let us now fix any
F(\alpha^{\prime})<s<F(\gamma^{\prime})
, and call for brevity
z=F_{1}^{-1}(s)
and

As a consequence, using again the convexity of

|g^{\prime}(z-\alpha)|-|g^{\prime}(w-\alpha)|+|g^{\prime}(\beta-z)|-|g^{\prime}(\beta-w)|\geq 0\,.

Inserting this inequality in (

\widetilde{F}^{\prime}\equiv 0

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}F^{\prime}(t)\big(|g^{\prime}(t-\alpha)+|g^{\prime}(\beta-t)|\big)\,dt\geq\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\widetilde{F}^{\prime}(t)\big(|g^{\prime}(t-\alpha)+|g^{\prime}(\beta-t)|\big)\,dt\,.

(2.6)

A simple approximation argument implies that (

\widehat{F}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\begin{aligned} \inf\bigg\{F(s),\,t\leq s\leq\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\bigg\}\end{aligned}&\hbox{if $\begin{aligned} t\leq\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\end{aligned}$}\,,\\

And then, repeating the very same argument in the second half of the interval

F^{*}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\begin{aligned} \inf\bigg\{F(s),\,t\leq s\leq\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\bigg\}\end{aligned}&\hbox{if $\begin{aligned} t\leq\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\end{aligned}$}\,,\\[10.0pt]
\begin{aligned} \sup\bigg\{F(s),\,\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\leq s\leq t\bigg\}\end{aligned}&\hbox{if $\begin{aligned} t\leq\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\end{aligned}$}\,.\end{array}\right.

3.
Proof of Theorem A

In this final section, we are going to prove Theorem A. To do so, it is enough to show that there exists an essential limit for
f
at any point of
(a,b)
; indeed, from the definition itself it follows that
f
is continuous. More precisely, if for any point
x\in(a,b)
an essential limit exists, and we call it
g(x)
, then from the definition we immediately get that
g
is continuous; and then, the functions
g
and
f
are readily seen to coincide a.e. thanks to the Lebesgue Differentiation Theorem.

Let us then take a generic point of
(a,b)
, and for simplicity of notation let us assume that this point is
0
. We start by showing that if
f
has left and right (essential) limit at
0
, then they must be equal. Then, a key idea to show that left and right limits actually exist is that we can basically reduce ourselves to consider the special cases when
f
is even, and when
f
is odd; indeed, if
f
has constant potential around
0
, then the same is true for the symmetric and antisymmetric parts
(f(x)\pm f(-x))/2
. To keep the presentation easier, we will consider all the different cases separately. Let us start with the case when left and right limits exist.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
g
and
f
be as in Theorem A, with
\psi_{f}
being a.e. constant in a neighborhood of
0
(and not necessarily on the whole
[a,b]
). Assume that both the left and the right limit of
f
at
0
exist. Then, they must be equal.

Proof.

We distinguish the proof in two subcases.

Case I.
If
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}g(t)=+\infty
.

Let us call for brevity
l_{L}
and
l_{R}
the left and the right limit of
f
at
0
, and let us assume by contradiction that they are not equal; without loss of generality, we can think
l_{L}<l_{R}
. We fix a positive constant

\varepsilon<\frac{l_{R}-l_{L}}{5}\,.

(3.1)

Then, there exists
\eta<r/2
such that
\psi_{f}
is a.e. constant in
(-2\eta,2\eta)
and

\displaystyle f(t)>l_{R}-\varepsilon\quad\hbox{for a.e. $t\in(0,\eta)$}\,,

\displaystyle\big|f(t)-l_{L}\big|<\varepsilon\quad\hbox{for a.e. $t\in(-\eta,0)$}\,.

(3.2)

For any positive, very small
\delta<\eta
, we know that

0=\psi_{f}(-\delta)-\psi_{f}(0)=\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\big(g(-\delta-t)-g(-t)\big)\,dt=\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\big(g(t+\delta)-g(t)\big)\,dt\,,

(3.3)

where the last equality comes since
g
is symmetric. We evaluate now the last integral in some subintervals. First of all, we have

\int_{\eta}^{b}f(t)\big(g(t+\delta)-g(t)\big)\,dt=\delta\int_{\eta}^{b}f(t)g^{\prime}(s_{t,\delta})\,dt\,,

where
s_{t,\delta}
is a suitable point in
(t,t+\delta)
for any
t\in[\eta,b]
. Recalling that
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
and that
f\in L^{\infty}
, we immediately deduce that if
\delta
is sufficiently small then

\int_{\eta}^{b}f(t)\big(g(t+\delta)-g(t)\big)\,dt\leq C_{1}\delta

(3.4)

for some constant
C_{1}
, which depends on
a,\,b,\,f,\,g,\,\varepsilon,\,\eta
but not on
\delta
. In the very same way, up to increasing the constant
C_{1}
and decreasing the value of
\delta
, we have

\int_{a}^{-\eta}f(t)\big(g(t+\delta)-g(t)\big)\,dt\leq C_{1}\delta\,.

(3.5)

Since
g
is decreasing in
(0,2\eta)
, then
g(t+\delta)-g(t)<0
for every
t\in(0,\eta)
, and thus by (
3.2
)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\eta}f(t)\big(g(t&+\delta)-g(t)\big)\,dt\leq\big(l_{R}-\varepsilon\big)\int_{0}^{\eta}g(t+\delta)-g(t)\,dt\\
&=\big(l_{R}-\varepsilon\big)\bigg(\int_{\eta}^{\eta+\delta}g(t)\,dt-\int_{0}^{\delta}g(t)\,dt\bigg)\leq C_{2}\delta-\big(l_{R}-\varepsilon\big)\int_{0}^{\delta}g(t)\,dt\,,\end{split}

(3.6)

for some constant
C_{2}
again depending on
a,\,b,\,f,\,g,\,\varepsilon,\,\eta
.

Finally, we have to evaluate the integral in
(-\eta,0)
. The situation is slightly different with respect to the one just considered. Indeed, for every
t\in(0,\eta)
we have used the fact that
g(t+\delta)-g(t)<0
, which comes since
g
is decreasing in
(0,2\eta)
. The opposite inequality
g(t+\delta)-g(t)>0
is not true for every
t\in(-\eta,0)
, but only for
t\in(-\eta,-\delta/2)
; instead, for
t\in(-\delta/2,0)
we have again
g(t+\delta)-g(t)<0
. As a consequence, by (
3.2
) the estimate now reads as

\begin{split}\int_{-\eta}^{0}f(t)\big(g(&t+\delta)-g(t)\big)\,dt=\int_{-\eta}^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}f(t)\big(g(t+\delta)-g(t)\big)\,dt+\int_{-\frac{\delta}{2}}^{0}f(t)\big(g(t+\delta)-g(t)\big)\,dt\hskip-20.0pt\\
&\leq(l_{L}+\varepsilon)\int_{-\eta}^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}g(t+\delta)-g(t)\,dt+(l_{L}-\varepsilon)\int_{-\frac{\delta}{2}}^{0}g(t+\delta)-g(t)\,dt\\
&=(l_{L}+\varepsilon)\int_{-\eta}^{0}g(t+\delta)-g(t)\,dt-2\varepsilon\int_{-\frac{\delta}{2}}^{0}g(t+\delta)-g(t)\,dt\\
&=(l_{L}+\varepsilon)\bigg(\int_{0}^{\delta}g(t)\,dt-\int_{\eta-\delta}^{\eta}g(t)\,dt\bigg)-2\varepsilon\bigg(\int_{\frac{\delta}{2}}^{\delta}g(t)\,dt-\int_{0}^{\frac{\delta}{2}}g(t)\,dt\bigg)\\
&\leq(l_{L}+3\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{\delta}g(t)\,dt+C_{2}\delta\,.\end{split}

(3.7)

Inserting (
3.4
), (
3.5
), (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) into (
3.3
), and recalling (
3.1
), we obtain

0\leq 2(C_{1}+C_{2})\delta-\big(l_{R}-l_{L}-4\varepsilon\big)\int_{0}^{\delta}g(t)\,dt<2(C_{1}+C_{2})\delta-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\delta}g(t)\,dt\,.

And in turn, this last inequality is impossible as soon as
\delta
is small enough, because the assumption that
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}g(t)=+\infty
implies that
\int_{0}^{\delta}g\gg\delta
.

Case II.
If
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}g(t)<+\infty
.

Let us now assume that the limit of
g
at
0
is finite; hence,
g
is continuous on the whole

. As a consequence, also recalling that
f\in L^{\infty}
, the potential
\psi_{f}

\psi_{f}^{\prime}(x)=\int_{a}^{b}g^{\prime}(x-t)f(t)\,dt\,.

Since the value of

is constant in a neighborhood of

\psi_{f}^{\prime}
is constant (and actually

) near

. We can then argue similarly to how we have done in Case I, being careful since
g^{\prime}
is odd, while

and find

) and (

) are in force together with the fact that
\psi_{f}^{\prime}
is constant in
(-2\eta,2\eta)
. As in (

), we have

0=\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\big(g^{\prime}(t+\delta)-g^{\prime}(t)\big)\,dt\,,

and since
g^{\prime}\in BV_{loc}((0,+\infty))
as in (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) we get

\bigg|\int_{[a,b]\setminus(-\eta,\eta)}f(t)\big(g^{\prime}(t+\delta)-g^{\prime}(t)\big)\,dt\bigg|\leq C_{1}\delta\,.

This time, for every
t\in(-\eta,-\delta)\cup(0,\eta)

g^{\prime}(t+\delta)-g^{\prime}(t)>0
, while the opposite inequality is true in

. As a consequence, arguing similarly to how done in (

) and (

), this time we get

\begin{split}\int_{-\eta}^{\eta}f(t)\big(g^{\prime}(t&+\delta)-g^{\prime}(t)\big)\geq(l_{L}-\varepsilon)\int_{-\eta}^{-\delta}g^{\prime}(t+\delta)-g^{\prime}(t)\,dt\\
&\quad+(l_{L}+\varepsilon)\int_{-\delta}^{0}g^{\prime}(t+\delta)-g^{\prime}(t)\,dt+(l_{R}-\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{\eta}g^{\prime}(t+\delta)-g^{\prime}(t)\,dt\\
&\geq-C_{2}\delta+(l_{L}-\varepsilon)\big(g(0)-g(-\delta)\big)+\Big(2(l_{L}+\varepsilon)-(l_{R}-\varepsilon)\Big)\big(g(\delta)-g(0)\big)\\
&=-C_{2}\delta+\big(g(0)-g(\delta)\big)\Big(l_{R}-l_{L}-4\varepsilon\Big)\,.\end{split}

Summarizing, we obtain

0\geq-(C_{1}+C_{2})\delta+\big(g(0)-g(\delta)\big)\varepsilon\,,

and this is again impossible because the assumptions imply that
g^{\prime}(t)\to-\infty
when

, and thus

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{g(0)-g(\delta)}{\delta}\to+\infty\,.

∎

Now, we consider the case when
f
is even.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
g
and
f
be as in Theorem A, with
\psi_{f}
being a.e. constant in a neighborhood of
0
(and not necessarily on the whole
[a,b]
). Assume that
a=-b
and that
f
is even. Then the limit of
f
at
0
exists.

Proof.

To get the thesis, it is enough to show that
h_{L}=0
; indeed, this means that the left limit of
f
at
0
exists, but since
f
is even then in that case also the right limit exists and they coincide. Let us then assume by contradiction that
h_{L}>0
. Let us define the parameters
\varepsilon,\,\eta
and
\delta
as in Section
2.2
, Case I, having possibily reduced
\eta
so that
\psi_{f}
is a.e. constant in
[-2\eta,2\eta]
. Then, consider the function
f_{\delta}
, define the corresponding points
p_{N}<p_{N-1}<\,\cdots\,<p_{1}<0<q_{1}
and fix the “good couple”
\{p_{j+1},p_{j}\}
.

We first observe that it is not restrictive to assume that
p_{j+1}
is a local minimum (or, equivalently, that
j

Remark 3.3
.

In the proof of Lemma
3.2
we have used the symmetry assumption only once, that is, to say that
f_{\delta}\geq l_{L}^{-}-\varepsilon
in
(0,\eta)
in the estimate of
K
. Indeed, in general we can only say that
f_{\delta}\geq l_{R}^{-}-\varepsilon
in
(0,\eta)
, but the symmetry of
f
clearly gives
l_{L}^{-}=l_{R}^{-}
. But then, the proof that
h_{L}=0
of Lemma
3.2
remains true also if

is not symmetric but
l_{L}^{-}=l_{R}^{-}
, provided the points

are defined as in Case I of Section
2.2
. However, the fact that

does not imply that

is continuos at

in a general case, while it clearly does so if

is even.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
g
and
f
be as in Theorem A, with
\psi_{f}
being a.e. constant in a neighborhood of
0
(and not necessarily on the whole
[a,b]
). Assume that
a=-b
and that
f
is odd. Then the limit of
f
at
0
exists.

Proof.

We are going to prove that
h_{L}=0
. Indeed, this guarantees that the left limit of
f
at
0
exists, and then also the right limit exists since
f
is odd. The fact that the two limits coincide, which completes the proof, is then ensured by Lemma
3.1
.

Let us assume that
h_{L}>0
, and let us seek for a contradiction. First of all, we notice that since
f
is odd, then
h_{L}>0
implies that
l_{L}^{-}\wedge l_{R}^{-}<0
. Moreover, as observed in Remark
3.3
, if
l_{L}^{-}=l_{R}^{-}
then the contradiction is directly given by the same argument as in Lemma
3.2
, so there is nothing to prove. Considering then the case
l_{L}^{-}\neq l_{R}^{-}
, we can assume without loss of generality that
l_{L}^{-}<l_{R}^{-}
, up to replacing
f(x)
with
f(-x)
. Summarizing, we have to find a contradiction assuming that
h_{L}>0
and
l_{L}^{-}<\min\{l_{R}^{-},0\}
.

In this case, we define the parameters
\varepsilon,\,\eta
and
\delta
as in Section
2.2
, Case II, in such a way that
\psi_{f}
is a.e. constant in
[-2\eta,2\eta]
; then, consider the function

, define the corresponding points

and fix the “good couple”. We divide our analysis in three subcases.

Case I.
The good couple is

.

This is the easiest case to deal with. Indeed, the antisymmetry of

guarantees that

\int_{p_{1}}^{q_{1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)|g^{\prime}(t-p_{1})|\,dt=\int_{p_{1}}^{q_{1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)|g^{\prime}(q_{1}-t)|\,dt\,,

and then (

\begin{split}-\int_{p_{1}}^{q_{1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{1})\,dt&=\int_{p_{1}}^{q_{1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)|g^{\prime}(t-p_{1})|\,dt\geq\frac{f_{\delta}(q_{1})-f_{\delta}(p_{1})}{2}\,\big(|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|+|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\big)\\
&\geq\frac{l_{R}^{+}-l_{L}^{-}-2\varepsilon}{2}\,\big(|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|+|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\big)>\frac{h_{L}-2\varepsilon}{2}\,\big(|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|+|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\big)\,,\end{split}

where
\gamma=q_{1}-p_{1}
and in the last inequality we have used the fact that
l_{R}^{+}>l_{L}^{+}
. Notice that this estimate is stronger than the estimate of
J
in (

exactly as in the estimate of

Putting the last three estimates together we get

Let us now seek for a contradiction in this last case. We are assuming that the good couple is

while arguing exactly as already done multiple times we obtain the estimates

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{-\eta}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(p_{j+1}-t)\,dt<\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\,,

\displaystyle\int_{\eta}^{b}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{j+1})\,dt>-\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\,,

\displaystyle\int_{-\eta}^{p_{j+1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(p_{j+1}-t)\,dt<2\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\,,

\displaystyle\int_{p_{j}}^{p_{1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{j+1})\,dt>-(h_{L}+2\varepsilon)|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|\,.

Notice that in order to obtain the third and fourth estimate we have used the fact that
l_{L}^{-}-\varepsilon<f_{\delta}<l_{L}^{+}+\varepsilon
in
(-\eta,p_{1})
, which is true thanks to (
3.19
). Notice also that the fourth estimate deals with the integral in
[p_{j},p_{1}]
: we would obtain the usual contradiction if we had the estimate in the interval
[p_{j},\eta]
, but this is not possible in this case using the techniques exploited before. However, adding the above estimates and using (
2.9
) and the first requirement of (
2.10
) we obtain

\begin{split}0&=-\int_{a}^{p_{j+1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(p_{j+1}-t)\,dt+\int_{p_{j+1}}^{b}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{j+1})\,dt\\
&>\frac{h_{L}-10\varepsilon}{2}\,|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|-\frac{h_{L}+6\varepsilon}{2}\,|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|+\int_{p_{1}}^{\eta}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{j+1})\,dt\\
&>\bigg(\frac{h_{L}-10\varepsilon}{2}-\frac{h_{L}+6\varepsilon}{2\overline{\Lambda}}\bigg)\,|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|+\int_{p_{1}}^{\eta}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{j+1})\,dt\\
&>4\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|+\int_{p_{1}}^{\eta}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{j+1})\,dt\,.\end{split}

Thus, the proof is immediately concluded if, by chance,

\int_{p_{1}}^{\eta}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{j+1})\,dt\geq-4\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\,.

(3.22)

If this inequality is not true, then we are not able to find a contradiction using
p_{j+1}
as “basepoint”; however, we will find a contradiction using instead
p_{1}
as basepoint. Let us be more precise. Applying (
2.1
) to the point
p_{1}
and keeping in mind as usual that
\psi_{f_{\delta}}
is constant in
(-\eta,\eta)
, we have

0=-\int_{a}^{p_{1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(p_{1}-t)\,dt+\int_{p_{1}}^{b}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{1})\,dt\,.

(3.23)

Now, we can notice that
p_{1}
is the minimum point of
f_{\delta}
in
[p_{1},\eta]
. Indeed, by construction it is the minimum point in
[p_{1},0]
; moreover, for every
t\in[0,\eta]
we have
f_{\delta}(t)>l_{L}^{-}+\varepsilon
by (
3.21
), while
f_{\delta}(p_{1})<l_{L}^{-}+\varepsilon
by the construction of Section
2.2
. As a consequence, we can apply Lemma
2.3
to the function
F=f_{\delta}
with
\alpha=y=p_{1}
,
\beta=\eta
and
x=p_{j+1}
to get that

\int_{p_{1}}^{\eta}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)(g^{\prime}(t-p_{j+1})-g^{\prime}(t-p_{1}))\,dt\geq 0\,,

and since (
3.22
) is false, this gives

\int_{p_{1}}^{\eta}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{1})\,dt<-4\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\,.

(3.24)

Arguing as usual, we know that

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{-\eta}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(p_{1}-t)\,dt>-\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\,,

\displaystyle\int_{\eta}^{b}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{1})\,dt<\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|\,.

(3.25)

Finally, arguing once again as in the estimate of
I
in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, and keeping in mind that
f_{\delta}\geq l_{L}^{-}-\varepsilon
in
[-\eta,p_{1}]
while
f_{\delta}(p_{1})\leq l_{L}^{-}+\varepsilon
, we get the estimate

\int_{-\eta}^{p_{1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(p_{1}-t)\,dt\geq-2\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})|\,,

where we have called
\gamma^{\prime}=p_{1}-p_{2}
. By the definition of
j
made in Section
2.2
, in particular by the fact that
j
is the smallest value such that (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) hold, we know that
\gamma^{\prime}\geq\gamma
, so in particular
|g^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})|\leq|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|
and the above estimate implies

\int_{-\eta}^{p_{1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(p_{1}-t)\,dt\geq-2\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|\,.

(3.26)

Inserting (
3.24
), (
3.25
) and (
3.26
) into (
3.23
), we obtain

0=-\int_{a}^{p_{1}}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(p_{1}-t)\,dt+\int_{p_{1}}^{b}f_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)g^{\prime}(t-p_{1})\,dt\leq-2\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma/2)|+2\varepsilon|g^{\prime}(\gamma)|<0\,,

which gives the desired contradiction, hence concluding the proof.
∎

We are finally in position to show our main result, which will be easily obtained by putting together the special cases already considered.

Proof of Theorem A.

As already observed at the beginning of Section
3
, a standard argument ensures that
f
is continuous in
(a,b)
as soon as it admits an essential limit at every point in that interval.

Let us then take a generic point
\bar{x}\in(a,b)
. We define the auxiliary functions
f_{S}
and
f_{A}
as

\displaystyle f_{S}(x)=f(\bar{x}+x)+f(\bar{x}-x)\,,

\displaystyle f_{A}(x)=f(\bar{x}+x)-f(\bar{x}-x)\,.

Notice that
f_{S}
is even and
f_{A}
is odd; moreover, since
\psi_{f}
is constant in
(a,b)
, then the potential of
f_{S}
and that of
f_{A}
are constant in
(-s,s)
, where
s=\min\{\bar{x}-a,b-\bar{x}\}
. As a consequence, Lemma
3.2
implies the existence of the essential limit of
f_{S}
at
0
, while Lemma
3.4
gives the same for
f_{A}
. The existence of the essential limit of
f
at
\bar{x}
is then obvious.
∎
