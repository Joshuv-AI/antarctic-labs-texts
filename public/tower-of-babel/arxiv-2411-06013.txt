Real randomized measurements for analyzing properties of quantum states
Jin-Min Liang, Satoya Imai, Shuheng Liu, Shao-Ming Fei, Otfried Gühne, Qiongyi He

Abstract
Randomized measurements are useful for analyzing quantum systems especially when quantum control is not fully perfect. However, their practical realization typically requires multiple rotations in the complex space due to the adoption of random unitaries. Here, we introduce two simplified randomized measurements that limit rotations in a subspace of the complex space. The first is real randomized measurements (RRMs) with orthogonal evolution and real local observables. The second is partial real randomized measurements (PRRMs) with orthogonal evolution and imaginary local observables. We show that these measurement protocols exhibit different abilities in capturing correlations of bipartite systems. We explore various applications of RRMs and PRRMs in different quantum information tasks such as characterizing high-dimensional entanglement, quantum imaginarity, and predicting properties of quantum states with classical shadow.

II
Real randomized measurements

Suppose that Alice and Bob share a two-qudit state
\varrho
. Following (i) in Assumptions, they apply orthogonal matrices
O_{A}
and
O_{B}
sampled via the Haar measure on the orthogonal group
\bm{\mathrm{O}}(d)
. The RRMs allow them to measure real observables
M_{A}
and
M_{B}
, while the PRRMs allow them to measure imaginary observables
\hat{M}_{A}
and
\hat{M}_{B}
. Here and hereafter, we use the notation
\hat{\square}
to denote quantities associated with imaginarity. Similar to
t
-order moments of RMs
[
13
]
, the
t
-order moments of RRMs and PRRMs are respectively defined as

\displaystyle Q_{AB}^{(t)}\!

\displaystyle=\!\!\int\!dO_{A}\!\!\int\!dO_{B}\,[{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho O_{A}M_{A}O_{A}^{\top}\otimes O_{B}M_{B}O_{B}^{\top})]^{t},

(2)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{AB}^{(t)}\!

\displaystyle=\!\!\int\!dO_{A}\!\!\int\!dO_{B}\,[{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho O_{A}\hat{M}_{A}O_{A}^{\top}\otimes O_{B}\hat{M}_{B}O_{B}^{\top})]^{t}.

(3)

Note that the moments
Q_{AB}^{(t)}
and
\hat{Q}_{AB}^{(t)}
are invariant under any local orthogonal operations, while the moment
R_{AB}^{(t)}
is invariant under local unitary operations. That is,
Q_{AB}^{(t)}(\varrho)=Q_{AB}^{(t)}(W_{A}\otimes W_{B}\varrho W_{A}^{\top}\otimes W_{B}^{\top})
for any
W_{A},W_{B}\in\bm{\mathrm{O}}(d)
, and similarly for
\hat{Q}_{AB}^{(t)}
, while
R_{AB}^{(t)}(\varrho)=R_{AB}^{(t)}(V_{A}\otimes V_{B}\varrho V_{A}^{{\dagger}}\otimes V_{B}^{{\dagger}})
for any
V_{A},V_{B}\in\bm{\mathrm{U}}(d)
. In other words, the moments
Q_{AB}^{(t)}
and
\hat{Q}_{AB}^{(t)}
characterize two-qudit states in a real-reference-frame-independent manner
[
63
,
64
]
.

Let us discuss the difference among RMs, RRMs, and RRMs. Recall that any two-qudit state
\varrho
can be written as

\varrho=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}\otimes\lambda_{k},

(4)

where
\lambda_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{d}
denotes the
d
-dimension identity and
\lambda_{j}
are the generalized Gell-Mann matrices (GGMs) satisfying
\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{{\dagger}}
,
{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k})=d\delta_{jk}
, and
{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j})=0
for
j>0

[
65
,
66
]
. Note that each of
(d^{2}-1)
GGMs can be seen as an observable, belonging to one of the two types: (a)
real
GGMs consisting of
L
real observables:
\lambda_{j}^{\top}=\lambda_{j}
, associating with condition (ii) in Assumptions; or (b)
imaginary
GGMs consisting of
\hat{L}
imaginary observables:
\lambda_{j}^{\top}=-\lambda_{j}
, associating with condition (iii) in Assumptions. Here, we let first
L=(d-1)(d+2)/2
GGMs be real GGMs and latter
\hat{L}=d(d-1)/2
GGMs be imaginary GGMs, where
L+\hat{L}=d^{2}-1
, see Appendix
A
.

The bipartite correlations in
\varrho
can then be divided into four types: real-real, imaginary-imaginary, real-imaginary, and imaginary-real ones, as shown in Fig.
1
. We have the following observation.

Observation 1
.

The RMs given by Eq.(
1
) extract all four types of correlations of the
\varrho
as a whole, while RRMs and PRRMs given by Eqs.(
2
) and (
3
) only extract the real-real and the imaginary-imaginary correlations of
\varrho
, respectively, as shown in Fig.
1
. The following relation holds for all
\varrho
,

Q_{AB}^{(t)}(\varrho)+\hat{Q}_{AB}^{(t)}(\varrho)\leq R_{AB}^{(t)}(\varrho),\quad\forall\varrho,

(5)

where the equality is attained when
\varrho
is real,
\varrho=\varrho^{\top}
.

To prove Observation
1
, we consider the simplest case of
t=2
. General cases for any
t
are given in Appendix
B
. Based on the Schur–Weyl duality in unitary and orthogonal groups (see Refs.
[
67
,
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
]
or Appendix
A
), the second commutant in the unitary group is spanned by identity
\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}
and the SWAP operator
\mathbb{S}=\sum_{j,k}|jk\rangle\langle kj|
. In contrast, the one in the orthogonal group is spanned by the identity, the SWAP operator, and additionally, the operator
\Pi=\sum_{j,k}|jj\rangle\langle kk|
, where
\{|j\rangle\}
denotes the computational basis in
d
dimensions. Hence, the Haar unitary and orthogonal integrals read, respectively,

\displaystyle\int dU\,U^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{A}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}=\alpha\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}+\beta\mathbb{S},

(6)

\displaystyle\int dO\,O^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{A}O^{\top\otimes 2}=\gamma_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}+\gamma_{2}\mathbb{S}+\gamma_{3}\Pi,

(7)

where the parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
, and
\gamma_{3}
depend on a two-qudit operator
\mathcal{A}
, see Appendix
A
. Thus, the three second-order moments of RRMs, PRRMs, and RMs are, respectively,
Q_{AB}^{(2)}(\varrho):=\sum_{j,k=1}^{L}T_{jk}^{2}
,
\hat{Q}_{AB}^{(2)}(\varrho):=\sum_{j,k=L+1}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}^{2}
, and
R_{AB}^{(2)}(\varrho):=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}^{2}
by ignoring normalization constants. Note that the real-imaginary correlations
\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=L+1}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}^{2}
and imaginary-real correlations
\sum_{j=L+1}^{d^{2}-1}\sum_{k=1}^{L}T_{jk}^{2}
are missing. Thus, Eq.(
5
) is true for any
\varrho
. Moreover, real states (i.e.
\varrho=\varrho^{\top}
) exhibit real-real and imaginary-imaginary correlations, which guarantees that equality in Eq.(
5
) holds for real states.

From Observation
1
, RRMs and PRRMs can extract and distinguish partial correlations. This feature determines their irreplaceable roles in quantum information tasks. We show in the following that real-real correlations are still useful for entanglement detection.

III
Entanglement detection via RRMs

We here employ RRMs to characterize high-dimensional bipartite entanglement. Recall that the Schmidt number (SN) of mixed state
\varrho
is defined by the Schmidt rank (SR) of pure state
|\psi_{j}\rangle

[
58
]
,
\textrm{SN}(\varrho)=\min\{r:\varrho=\sum_{j}p_{j}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|,\textrm{SR}(|\psi_{j}\rangle)\leq r\}
, where
p_{j}\geq 0
and
\sum_{j}p_{j}=1
. We aim to find an analytical criterion to detect SN
(\varrho)
using RRMs.

The first step is to evaluate the orthogonal integrals of the moments
Q_{AB}^{(t)}(\varrho)
in Eq. (
2
) and find their analytical expressions. For
t=2,4
, we obtain

\displaystyle Q_{AB}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\tau_{j}^{2},

(8)

\displaystyle Q_{AB}^{(4)}

\displaystyle=W\left[2\sum_{j=1}^{L}\tau_{j}^{4}+L^{4}\left(Q_{AB}^{(2)}\right)^{2}\right],

(9)

where
\tau_{j}
are singular values of the submatrix
T_{R}=(T_{jk})
of the correlation matrix with the entries given in Eq. (
4
);
j,k>0
running over all real GGMs;
L=(d-1)(d+2)/2
is the number of real GGMs; and the normalization
W=\frac{3\Gamma^{2}(\frac{L}{2})}{16\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{L+4}{2}\right)}
,
\Gamma(z)
denotes Euler’s gamma function with
\Gamma(z)=(z-1)!
for positive integer
z

[
72
]
. See details in Appendix
C
.

It is important to note that for higher-order moments, not all observables give rise to the same results, since orthogonal matrices do not imply sphere rotations, due to the lack of the Bloch sphere for
d>2
. Nevertheless, one can construct observables to obtain the moments in Eqs. (
8
and
9
) for
t=2,4
and any dimension
d

[
35
,
37
]
, for details see Appendix
C
. For example, the observables for
d=3
are
M_{A}=M_{B}=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{3/2},0,-\sqrt{3/2})

[
37
]
.

Next, we propose our entanglement dimensionality criterion by adopting the second and fourth moments of RRMs. Our approach to detect SN
(\varrho)
is to consider a plane spanned by
(Q_{AB}^{(2)},Q_{AB}^{(4)})
defined in Eqs. (
8
and
9
) by employing the strategies in Refs
[
34
,
35
,
37
,
36
]
. The task is to obtain the lower boundary on this plane, so as to reveal a hierarchy of Schmidt numbers. To do so, we use the criterion
[
37
,
36
]
that any bipartite quantum state
\varrho
with
\textrm{SN}(\varrho)\leq r
satisfies
\|T\|_{{\mathrm{tr}}}\leq rd-1
for
r=1,\cdots,d
, where
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathrm{tr}}}
denotes the trace norm, i.e., the sum of the singular values of
T
as in Eq. (
4
). In the case of
r=1
, the inequality reduces to the de Vicente criterion
[
73
]
. Recalling that
T_{R}
is the submatrix of
T
, we have that
\|T_{\mathrm{R}}\|_{{\mathrm{tr}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\tau_{j}\leq\|T\|_{{\mathrm{tr}}}\leq rd-1

[
74
]
. We summarize our criteria as follows:

Observation 2
.

Let
C_{2}
and
C_{4}
be the second and fourth moments of RRMs given in Eqs. (
8
and
9
) for a two-qudit state
\varrho
. Consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\tau_{j}}\quad

\displaystyle Q_{AB}^{(4)},\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\begin{cases}Q_{AB}^{(2)}=C_{2},\\
\sum_{j=1}^{L}\tau_{j}\leq rd-1,\\
\tau_{j}\in[0,d-1].\end{cases}

(10)

If
F_{\min}(r,C_{2})\leq C_{4}<F_{\min}(r-1,C_{2})
, then
\textrm{SN}(\varrho)=r
for
r=2,\cdots,d
. Here, we denote the minimum of the above optimization problem as

F_{\min}\left(x,y\right)\!=\!\begin{cases}\!WL^{3}y^{2}(2+L),&0\!\leq\!y\!\leq\!\frac{(dx-1)^{2}}{L^{3}},\\
\!F_{\min}^{(n_{g})}\left(x,y\right),&\frac{(dx-1)^{2}}{L^{2}\left(n_{g}+1\right)}\!\leq\!y\!<\!\frac{(dx-1)^{2}}{L^{2}n_{g}},\end{cases}

where
x=1,\cdots,d
,
n_{g}=1,\cdots,L-1
,
F_{\min}^{(n_{g})}\left(x,y\right)=2Wf\left(n_{g},y\right)/\left(n_{g}+1\right)^{4}+WL^{4}y^{2}
,
f\left(n_{g},y\right)=\left[\mathcal{B}-(dx-1)\right]^{4}+\left[\mathcal{B}+n_{g}(dx-1)\right]^{4}/n_{g}^{3}
, and
\mathcal{B}=\sqrt{n_{g}\left(n_{g}+1\right)L^{2}y-n_{g}\left(dx-1\right)^{2}}
.

IV
Detection and quantification of imaginarity

RMs are currently used to analyze entanglement
[
13
,
12
]
and magic resources
[
78
]
. Here, we show that RMs together with RRMs and PRRMs can be used to characterize imaginarity
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
, another important resource in channel discrimination
[
2
,
52
]
, weak-value theory
[
79
]
, and quantum multiparameter metrology
[
80
]
. From the imaginarity resource theory
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
, the resource states are identified as imaginary states that have imaginary entries on a given basis
\{|j\rangle\}
. Our task is to detect and quantify imaginary states without a common reference frame. However, this is impossible if one only uses the moments
R_{AB}^{(t)}
in Eq.(
1
), as illustrated in Fig.
1
. To proceed, we consider the combination of RRMs and PRRMs, and summarize our results below:

Observation 3
.

Consider the second moments
R_{AB}^{(2)}
of RMs,
Q_{AB}^{(2)}
of RRMs, and
\hat{Q}_{AB}^{(2)}
of PRRMs for a two-qudit state
\varrho_{AB}
. Define the gap

G_{AB}^{(2)}=(d^{2}-1)^{2}R_{AB}^{(2)}-L^{2}Q_{AB}^{(2)}-\hat{L}^{2}\hat{Q}_{AB}^{(2)},

(11)

where
L=(d-1)(d+2)/2
,
\hat{L}=d(d-1)/2
. Let
\hat{Q}_{X}^{(2)}
be the marginal second moments of the reduced states
\varrho_{X}

(X=A,B)
. We have the following:

(i)
\varrho_{AB}
is a real (free) state if and only if the following conditions hold:
\hat{Q}_{A}^{(2)}=\hat{Q}_{B}^{(2)}=G_{AB}^{(2)}=0
. Conversely,
\varrho_{AB}
is an imaginary (resource) state if and only if at least one of the following conditions holds: (a)
\hat{Q}_{A}^{(2)}>0
, (b)
\hat{Q}_{B}^{(2)}>0
, (c)
G_{AB}^{(2)}>0
.

(ii)
\hat{Q}_{A}^{(2)}
,
\hat{Q}_{B}^{(2)}
, and
G_{AB}^{(2)}
induce a lower bound of the robustness of the imaginarity measure
\mathcal{F}_{R}(\varrho_{AB})=\min_{\tau}\{\mu\geq 0:\frac{\varrho_{AB}+\mu\tau}{1+\mu}\in\mathscr{R}\}

[
49
]
, where
\mathscr{R}
denotes the set of all two-qudit real states and the minimum is taken over all quantum states
\tau

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{LB}}(\varrho_{AB})\leq\mathcal{F}_{R}(\varrho_{AB}),

(12)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{LB}}=\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\hat{L}\left(\hat{Q}_{A}^{(2)}+\hat{Q}_{B}^{(2)}\right)+G_{AB}^{(2)}}

The traditional characterization methods for measuring imaginarity involving complicated optimization and quantum state tomography are fragile to noise due to the requirement for well-controlled measurement settings

Classical shadows with RRMs

The classical shadow is a powerful tool in certifying properties of quantum systems

,

,

VII
Conclusion

We introduced the concepts of RRMs and PRRMs to characterize high-dimensional quantum states. We showed that partial correlations obtained by RRMs can detect bound entanglement and dimensionality, while the combination of RMs, RRMs, and PRRMs can characterize bipartite imaginary states. The latter allowed us to measure imaginarity without having shared reference frames. Furthermore, we extended the classical shadow by using random orthogonal matrices.

Several potential avenues for further investigation arise from our research. First, while we considered the orthogonal versions of RMs, developing RMs within different subsets of unitaries would be interesting. This could motivate further investigations of quantum resources beyond entanglement and imaginarity. Second, it would be worthwhile to find stronger entanglement criteria than the criterion by RMs itself, e.g., using linear or nonlinear combination in the space of
Q_{AB}^{(t)}
and
\hat{Q}_{AB}^{(t)}

[

[

[

.

Appendix A
Preliminaries and calculation of second-order moments

A.1
Moments over orthogonal group

Let us start by introducing the notation used in this work. We denote the set of linear operators that act on the
d
-dimensional complex vector space
\mathds{C}^{d}
as
\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})
. The identity operator with dimension
d
is represented by
\mathbbm{1}_{d}
. The orthogonal group, denoted as
\bm{\mathrm{O}}(d)
, consists of operators
O\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})
that satisfy the conditions
OO^{\top}=O^{\top}O=\mathbbm{1}_{d}
. It is worth noting that the orthogonal group
\bm{\mathrm{O}}(d)
is compact, which implies the existence of a unique probability measure
\mu
that remains invariant under left and right group multiplication. This measure is commonly referred to as the
Haar measure
, and we will denote integrals to the Haar measure as
\int dO
.

By introducing two important operators, the SWAP operator,
\mathbb{S}=\sum_{j,k=0}^{d-1}|jk\rangle\langle kj|
, and the operator
\Pi=\sum_{j,k=0}^{d-1}|jj\rangle\langle kk|
, we here present the calculation of the Haar integrals over the orthogonal group.

Lemma 1
.

(First and second moment). Given
\mathcal{A}_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d}\otimes\mathds{C}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\int dOO\mathcal{A}_{1}O^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})}{d}\mathbbm{1}_{d},

(14)

\displaystyle\int dO\,O^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{A}_{2}O^{\top\otimes 2}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}+\gamma_{2}\mathbb{S}+\gamma_{3}\Pi,

(15)

where the coefficients are

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A}_{2})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\mathcal{A}_{2})}{d(d-1)(d+2)},

(16)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A}_{2})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\mathcal{A}_{2})}{d(d-1)(d+2)},

(17)

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A}_{2})+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\mathcal{A}_{2})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}.

(18)

Proof.

The first-order moment is proportional to the identity operator, i.e.
\int dOO\mathcal{A}_{1}O^{\top}=\alpha\mathbbm{1}_{d}
with
\alpha\in\mathds{C}
. Taking the trace on both sides, we deduce that
\alpha=\frac{{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})}{d}
.

The second moment is a linear combination of three operators
\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}
,
\mathbb{S}
, and
\Pi

[
68
]
,

\displaystyle\int\!dO(O\!\otimes\!O)\mathcal{A}_{2}(O^{\top}\!\otimes\!O^{\top})\!=\!\gamma_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{d}\!\otimes\!\mathbbm{1}_{d}\!+\!\gamma_{2}\mathbb{S}\!+\!\gamma_{3}\Pi.

(19)

To find the coefficients, we left-multiply both sides by
\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}
,
\mathbb{S}
,
\Pi
respectively, and take a trace, which gives us the following linear system of equations

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})\\
{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A}_{2})\\
{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\mathcal{A}_{2})\end{pmatrix}=d\begin{pmatrix}d&1&1\\
1&d&1\\
1&1&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}\\
\gamma_{2}\\
\gamma_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

During the calculation, we use the fact that
{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d})=d^{2}
and
{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S})={\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi)=d
. Solving this linear system, we obtain the coefficients and complete the proof.
∎

In general, the
t
-th moment operator can be expressed as a linear combination of
\frac{(2t)!}{2^{t}t!}
operators, which are derived from the Brauer algebra
[
88
]
and the Schur-Weyl duality for orthogonal groups
[
69
]
. For instance, the fourth moment can be represented as a linear combination of
105
operators.

Lemma 2
.

For any two operators
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d})
, we have
{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B})={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}^{\top})
.

Proof.

Note that the operator
\Pi
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Pi

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=0}^{d-1}|jj\rangle\langle kk|=\sum_{j,k=0}^{d-1}|j\rangle\langle k|\otimes|j\rangle\langle k|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=0}^{d-1}|j\rangle\langle k|\otimes(|k\rangle\langle j|)^{\top}=\mathbb{S}^{\top_{2}},

(21)

where
A^{\top}
denotes the transpose of operator
A
and
\top_{2}
is the partial transpose for the second system. Thus, we complete the proof via

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}^{\top_{2}}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B})={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}^{\top}).

(22)

∎

A.2
Useful properties of the generalized Gell-Mann matrices

The generalized Gell-Mann matrices (GGMs) are extensions of the Gell-Mann matrices, which are originally defined for qutrit systems. The GGMs are denoted as
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}
, where
\lambda_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{d}
and
\lambda_{j}
are the normalized Gell-Mann matrices. These matrices satisfy the following properties,
\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{{\dagger}}
,
{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k})=d\delta_{jk}
,
{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j})=0
for
j>0

[
89
]
. The GGMs consist of three different types of matrices
[
66
]
,

(i)
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
symmetric GGMs,

\displaystyle\lambda_{jk}^{(s)}=\sqrt{\frac{d}{2}}\left(|j\rangle\langle k|+|k\rangle\langle j|\right),\!0\leq j<k\leq d-1.

(23)

(ii)
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
antisymmetric GGMs,

\displaystyle\lambda_{jk}^{(a)}\!=\!\sqrt{\frac{d}{2}}\left(-i|j\rangle\langle k|+i|k\rangle\langle j|\right),\!0\leq\!j<k\leq\!d-1.

(24)

(iii)
d-1
diagonal GGMs,

\displaystyle\lambda_{l}^{(d)}=\mathcal{K}\Big[-(l+1)|l+1\rangle\langle l+1|+\sum_{j=0}^{l}|j\rangle\langle j|\Big],

(25)

where
\mathcal{K}=\sqrt{\frac{d}{(l+1)(l+2)}}
and
0\leq l\leq d-2
.

It is important to note that all symmetric and diagonal Gell-Mann matrices have real elements and satisfy Assumption (ii) in the main text. On the other hand, all antisymmetric Gell-Mann matrices have imaginary elements and satisfy Assumption (iii) in the main text. As a result, we can divide the Gell-Mann matrices into two categories: real Gell-Mann matrices denoted as
\bm{\lambda}=\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{L}\}
and imaginary Gell-Mann matrices denoted as
\hat{\bm{\lambda}}=\{\lambda_{L+1},\cdots,\lambda_{d^{2}-1}\}
. Here,
L=\frac{(d-1)(d+2)}{2}
and
\hat{L}=\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
. It is worth mentioning that the total number of Gell-Mann matrices is given by
L+\hat{L}=d^{2}-1
. For the Gell-Mann matrices, we present the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

For two GGMs
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
in Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B})=\begin{cases}d,&\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}\in\bm{\lambda}\\
-d,&\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}\in\hat{\bm{\lambda}}\\
0,&otherwise.\\
\end{cases}.

(26)

Proof.

Based on Lemma
2
, we immediately have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\mathcal{A}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{B})\!=\!{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{B}^{\top})\!=\!\begin{cases}{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}),\!&\!\mathcal{B}\in\bm{\lambda}\\
-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}),\!&\!\mathcal{B}\in\hat{\bm{\lambda}}\\
\end{cases}.

(27)

The proof is completed by using
{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k})=d\delta_{jk}
for two GGMs
\lambda_{j}
,
\lambda_{k}
.
∎

A.3
Moments of RRMs and PRRMs

In this subsection, we derive the second moment for quantum states of single-qudit, two-qudit, and
N
-qudit systems.

A.3.1
Single-qudit systems

Consider a single qudit state
\varrho=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j}\lambda_{j}
. The second moment with real observable
M
, satisfying the conditions
{\mathrm{tr}}(M)=0
and
{\mathrm{tr}}(M^{2})=d
, is

\displaystyle Q^{(2)}

\displaystyle\!=\!\int\!dO\left[{\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho O^{\top}M\right)\right]^{2}\!=\!\frac{1}{d^{2}}\!\sum_{j,k=1}^{d^{2}-1}\!T_{j}T_{k}{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi),

(28)

where the quantity

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi)

\displaystyle\!=\!{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\int dO\left(O\otimes O\right)(\lambda_{j}\otimes\lambda_{k})\left(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top}\right)\left(M\!\otimes\!M\right)\right]

\displaystyle\!=\!\gamma_{2}{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M\otimes M)+\gamma_{3}{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi M\otimes M)

\displaystyle\!=\!d\left(\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}\right).

(29)

The third equation uses the fact that
{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M\otimes M)={\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi M\otimes M)={\mathrm{tr}}(M^{2})=d
. The coefficients

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d+1)d\delta_{jk}}{d(d-1)(d+2)}-\frac{{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\lambda_{j}\otimes\lambda_{k})}{d(d-1)(d+2)},

(30)

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d\delta_{jk}}{d(d-1)(d+2)}+\frac{(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\lambda_{j}\otimes\lambda_{k})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}.

(31)

Following Lemma
1
, the quantity

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=d\left(\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}\right)=\frac{d^{2}\delta_{jk}+d{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\lambda_{j}\otimes\lambda_{k})}{(d-1)(d+2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{2d^{2}\delta_{jk}}{(d-1)(d+2)},&\lambda_{k}\in\bm{\lambda}\\
0,&\lambda_{k}\in\hat{\bm{\lambda}}\\
\end{cases}.

(32)

Using Lemma
3
, we finally obtain the second moment of RRMs,

\displaystyle Q^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\times\frac{2d^{2}}{(d-1)(d+2)}\sum_{j=1}^{L}T_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(d-1)(d+2)}\sum_{j=1}^{L}T_{j}^{2}=L^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{L}T_{j}^{2}.

(33)

If we utilize an imaginary observable
\hat{M}
, such that
{\mathrm{tr}}(\hat{M})=0
and
{\mathrm{tr}}(\hat{M}^{2})=d
, the quantity

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=d\left(\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{3}\right)=\frac{d\delta_{jk}-{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\lambda_{j}\otimes\lambda_{k})}{d-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\lambda_{k}\in\bm{\lambda}\\
\frac{2d}{d-1},&\lambda_{k}\in\hat{\bm{\lambda}}\\
\end{cases}.

(34)

Using the same tricks as RRMs, the second moment of PRRMs is

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\int dO\left[{\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho O^{\top}\hat{M}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\times\frac{2d}{d-1}\sum_{j=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{d(d-1)}\sum_{j=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\hat{L}^{-1}\sum_{j=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{j}^{2}.

(35)

A.3.2
Two-qudit system

Consider a two-qudit state
\varrho=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\otimes\lambda_{k}^{B}
. The second moment with real observables
M_{A}
and
M_{B}
satisfying
{\mathrm{tr}}(M_{s})=0
and
{\mathrm{tr}}(M_{s}^{2})=d
for

\displaystyle Q^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\!\int\!dO_{A}\!\int\!dO_{B}\left\{{\mathrm{tr}}\left[(O_{A}\!\otimes\!O_{B})\varrho(O_{A}^{\top}\!\otimes\!O_{B}^{\top})(M_{A}\!\otimes\!M_{B})\right]\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{4}}\sum_{j_{1},k_{1},j_{2},k_{2}=1}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{A}){\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{B}).

(36)

where the quantity

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{A})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\int dO_{A}O_{A}^{\otimes 2}(\lambda_{j_{1}}^{A}\otimes\lambda_{j_{2}}^{A})O_{A}^{\top\otimes 2}M_{A}^{\otimes 2}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\mathbb{S}(M_{A}\otimes M_{A})\right]+\gamma_{3}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\Pi(M_{A}\otimes M_{A})\right]

\displaystyle=d(\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}).

(37)

A similar result for
{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{B})
can also be found. Following the derivation of the single-qubit case, the second moment is

\displaystyle Q^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{4}}\left[\frac{2d^{2}}{(d-1)(d+2)}\right]^{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{L}T_{jk}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{2}{(d-1)(d+2)}\right]^{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{L}T_{jk}^{2}

\displaystyle=L^{-2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{L}T_{jk}^{2}.

(38)

If we utilize imaginary observables
\hat{M}_{A}
,
\hat{M}_{B}
satisfying
{\mathrm{tr}}(\hat{M}_{s})=0
and
{\mathrm{tr}}(\hat{M}_{s}^{2})=d
for
s=A,B
, the second moment of PRRMs turns to

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\!=\!\int dO_{A}\!\int dO_{B}\left\{{\mathrm{tr}}\left[(O_{A}\!\otimes\!O_{B})\varrho(O_{A}^{\top}\!\otimes\!O_{B}^{\top})(\hat{M}_{A}\!\otimes\!\hat{M}_{B})\right]\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{1}{d^{4}}\left[\frac{2d}{d-1}\right]^{2}\!\sum_{j,k=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{jk}^{2}

\displaystyle\!=\!\hat{L}^{-2}\!\sum_{j,k=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{jk}^{2}.

(39)

Generalizing the above results to an

For a fixed

B.3

We express

\displaystyle\varrho_{AB}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\otimes\lambda_{k}^{B}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{d^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{A}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{B},

(42)

where the reduced states

\displaystyle\varrho_{A}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}_{B}(\varrho_{AB})=\frac{1}{d}\Big(\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{A}+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j0}\lambda_{j}^{A}\Big),

\displaystyle\varrho_{B}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}_{A}(\varrho_{AB})=\frac{1}{d}\Big(\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{B}+\sum_{k=1}^{d^{2}-1}T_{0k}\lambda_{k}^{B}\Big).

(43)

The imaginarity of

is characterized by the imaginarity of reduced states
\varrho_{A}
,
\varrho_{B}

(b)
\varrho_{B}
is imaginary,

(c) the correlation

contains imaginarity. One of

and
\lambda_{k}^{B}
in the correlation

, (b)
\hat{Q}_{B}^{(2)}>0
, and (c)

, respectively. Conversely,

is a real state if and only if all conditions are not true. Thus, the result (i) is proved.

Next, we prove the lower bound of the robustness of imaginarity of
\varrho_{AB}
. Based on Ref.
[
49
]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{R}(\varrho_{AB})

\displaystyle=\min_{\tau}\left\{\mu\geq 0:\frac{\varrho_{AB}+\mu\tau}{1+\mu}\in\mathscr{R}\right\}

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\varrho_{AB}-\varrho_{AB}^{\top}\|_{{\mathrm{tr}}}

(45)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\varrho_{AB}-\varrho_{AB}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{HS}},

(46)

where
\|A\|_{{\mathrm{tr}}}={\mathrm{tr}}\left(\sqrt{A^{{\dagger}}A}\right)=\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}
denotes the trace norm (the sum of singular values) of
A
and
\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sqrt{{\mathrm{tr}}\left(A^{{\dagger}}A\right)}=\sqrt{\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm (the square root of the sum of the squares of its singular values) of
A
. Note that the inequality
\|A\|_{{\mathrm{tr}}}\geq\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
holds, and the equality is true if and only if the operator
A
has all zero singular values.

Motivated by the Bloch decomposition of
\varrho_{AB}
, we express the correlations in
\varrho_{AB}
as four parts, including real-real, imaginary-imaginary, real-imaginary, and imaginary-real parts. Here, we assume the first
L
Gell-Mann matrices are real GMMs and next
\hat{L}
are imaginary GMMs. We then obtain the following equations,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\otimes\lambda_{k}^{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{L}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\otimes\lambda_{k}^{B}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\otimes\lambda_{k}^{B}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\otimes\lambda_{k}^{B}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\otimes\lambda_{k}^{B},

(47)

and its transposition

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{d^{2}}\!\sum_{j,k=1}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\!\otimes\!\lambda_{k}^{B}\Big)\!^{\top}

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{L}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\!\otimes\!\lambda_{k}^{B}

\displaystyle\!+\!\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\!\otimes\!\lambda_{k}^{B}

\displaystyle\!-\!\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sum_{k=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\!\otimes\!\lambda_{k}^{B}

\displaystyle\!-\!\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\!\otimes\!\lambda_{k}^{B}.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\!\otimes\!\lambda_{k}^{B}-\Big(\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}^{A}\!\otimes\!\lambda_{k}^{B}\Big)^{\top}

(50)

E.2

In the main text, we present the imaginarity detection and quantification of two-qudit states. Next, we first consider the single-qudit states.

Observation 6
.

Let
\varrho_{s}
be a single-qudit state and denote its second moments of RMs and RRMs as

is a real state and

Proof.

\varrho_{s}^{\top}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{L}T_{j}\lambda_{j}^{\top}-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{k}\lambda_{k}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{d}\sum_{k=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{k}\lambda_{k},

(94)

\displaystyle\|\varrho_{s}-\varrho_{s}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{2}{d}\sum_{j=L+1}^{L+\hat{L}}T_{j}\lambda_{j}\right\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle G_{s}

\displaystyle=(d^{2}-1)R_{s}^{(2)}-LQ_{s}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{R}(\varrho_{s})=\frac{1}{2}\|\varrho_{s}-\varrho_{s}^{\top}\|_{{\mathrm{tr}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\varrho_{s}-\varrho_{s}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{4G_{s}}{d}}=\sqrt{\frac{G_{s}}{d}}.

(97)

∎

E.3

The classical shadow is a method for predicting properties of quantum systems based on RMs
[
19
]
. One applies a random local or global unitary
U
on an
N
-qudit quantum state
\varrho
and then performs projective measurement in the computational basis

and

for

. The above procedure is repeated
N_{U}
independently unitaries
U
. One can use the obtained data to extract interesting information about quantum systems. The transformation
U
is picked from the Clifford group or the Pauli group for
d=2

[
12
]
. We here find that RRMs with global orthogonal matrices are sufficient for analyzing real states.

The classical shadow with RRMs employs a global orthogonal matrix
O
to rotate the state,
\varrho\rightarrow O\varrho O^{\top}
. After measuring the updated state in the basis
\{|b\rangle\}
, we store a classical snapshot
O^{\top}|b\rangle\langle b|O
whose expectation value over the random orthogonal matrix and probability distribution can be linked with the measurement channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\rho)=\sum_{b}\int dO\langle b|O\varrho O^{\top}|b\rangle O^{\top}|b\rangle\langle b|O.

(100)

Assuming that
\mathcal{M}
is invertible,
\tilde{\varrho}:=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O^{\top}|b\rangle\langle b|O)
is the classical shadow of
\varrho
and
\mathbb{E}(\tilde{\varrho})=\varrho
. Below, we observe that the measurement channel
\mathcal{M}(\cdot)
is related to the second moment of RRMs.

Therefore, the classical shadow for the
m
th measurement setting
O^{(m)}
with
K
outcomes
|b^{(m)}\rangle=|b_{1}^{(m)}\cdots b_{N}^{(m)}\rangle
, is

\displaystyle\tilde{\varrho}^{(m)}:=\frac{d^{N}+2}{2}O^{(m)\top}|b^{(m)}\rangle\langle b^{(m)}|O^{(m)}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{d^{N}}.

(101)

The collection
\{\tilde{\varrho}^{(m)}\}_{m=1\cdots,N_{O}}
is called a classical shadow of
\varrho
. We can further predict properties such as (i) the expectation value of observable
\mathcal{G}
,
g={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{G}\tilde{\varrho})
, (ii) quadratic functions of the quantum state.

Here, two questions arise. First, how about the upper bounds for the sample complexity of the above tasks? However, this problem involves the
3
-design of the orthogonal group. We will leave this question for future work. Second, does it still work for local orthogonal matrices in the classical shadow? The answer is case by case. The proof of this claim follows. Suppose we perform a local orthogonal matrix

. For elementary tensor products

and each

is a

matrix, we obtain the measurement channel is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\int dO\langle b|O(X_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes X_{N})O^{\top}|b\rangle O^{\top}|b\rangle\langle b|O

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{b_{j}\in\{0,\cdots,d-1\}}\int dO_{j}\langle b_{j}|O_{j}X_{j}O_{j}^{\top}|b_{j}\rangle O_{j}^{\top}|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|O_{j}

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{d(d+2)}\sum_{b_{j}\in\{0,\cdots,d-1\}}{\mathrm{tr}}_{1}\left[X_{j}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}+(X_{j}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d})\mathbb{S}+(X_{j}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d})\Pi\right]

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\frac{{\mathrm{tr}}(X_{j})\mathbbm{1}_{d}+X_{j}+X_{j}^{\top}}{d+2}.

(102)

It is clear to check that if

for all

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}\frac{{\mathrm{tr}}(X_{j})\mathbbm{1}_{d}+2X_{j}}{d+2}.

Extending linearly to all quantum states in

We estimate two expectation values
E_{k}={\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{k}O^{\top}M_{k}\right)
for
k=1,2
. Repeating the above procedure for different orthogonal matrices

, we find the quantity,
\mathcal{D}=\int dOE_{1}E_{2}
.

Similarly, we consider two imaginary observables

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{1}=\hat{M}_{2}=(-i|0\rangle\langle d-1|+i|d-1\rangle\langle 0|)^{\otimes 2},

(107)

and find another quantity
\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\int dO\hat{E}_{1}\hat{E}_{2}
, where expectation values
\hat{E}_{k}={\mathrm{tr}}(O\varrho_{k}O^{\top}\hat{M}_{1})
for
k=1,2
. Using
\mathcal{D}
and
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
, we present the following result:

Observation 9
.

Consider any two-qudit state
\varrho_{1}
and a real two-qudit state
\varrho_{2}
and apply RRMs and PRRMs. The overlap is estimated by

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2})=\frac{\eta^{2}\mathcal{D}+\gamma^{2}\hat{\mathcal{D}}}{4\gamma^{2}\eta^{2}},

(108)

where parameters
\gamma=\frac{-(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})+d(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2})}{d\left(d-1\right)\left(d+2\right)}
and
\eta=\frac{2}{d(d-1)}
.

Proof.

Consider two-qudit states
\varrho_{1}
,
\varrho_{2}
, we can estimate the overlap
{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2})
by RRMs and PRRMs with local orthogonal matrices. Construct real observables for real parameters
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
(determined later),

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=M_{1}^{A}\otimes M_{1}^{B}

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha_{1}|0\rangle\langle 0|+\alpha_{2}|d-1\rangle\langle d-1|\right)^{\otimes 2},

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=M_{2}^{A}\otimes M_{2}^{B}

\displaystyle=\left(\beta_{1}|0\rangle\langle 0|+\beta_{2}|d-1\rangle\langle d-1|\right)^{\otimes 2}.

(109)

We have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}),

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}(M_{1}^{k}M_{2}^{k})=\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2},

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbb{S}\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\left(\hat{M}_{2}^{k}\right)^{\top}\right)={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})=\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2},

(110)

for
k=A,B
. In this scenario, the integral is

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle=\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{1}O^{\top}M_{1}\right){\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{2}O^{\top}M_{2}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\int dO(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top})(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})(O\otimes O)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\int dO_{A}\int dO_{B}(O_{A}^{\top}\otimes O_{B}^{\top}\otimes O_{A}^{\top}\otimes O_{B}^{\top})(M_{1}^{A}\otimes M_{1}^{B}\otimes M_{2}^{A}\otimes M_{2}^{B})(O_{A}\otimes O_{B}\otimes O_{A}\otimes O_{B})(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\bigotimes_{k=A,B}\int dO_{k}(O_{k}^{\top}\otimes O_{k}^{\top})(M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})(O_{k}\otimes O_{k})(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right].

(111)

Using Lemma
1
, we obtain the following quantity,

\displaystyle\int dO_{k}(O_{k}^{\top}\otimes O_{k}^{\top})(M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})(O_{k}\otimes O_{k})

\displaystyle=c_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{d^{2}}+c_{2}\mathbb{S}_{k}+c_{3}\Pi_{k},

(112)

where the coefficients are

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d+1)(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})-2(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2})}{d(d-1)(d+2)},

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{-{\mathrm{tr}}(M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{-(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})+d(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2})}{d(d-1)(d+2)},

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{-{\mathrm{tr}}(M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}

\displaystyle=c_{2}.

(113)

Hence, the quantity
\mathcal{D}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\bigotimes_{k=A,B}\left(c_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{d^{2}}+c_{2}\mathbb{S}_{k}+c_{2}\Pi_{k}\right)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\bigotimes_{k=A,B}\left(\mathbb{S}_{k}+\Pi_{k}\right)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}+\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\Pi_{B}+\Pi_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}+\Pi_{A}\otimes\Pi_{B}\right)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right],

(114)

To remove the tensor item that includes single operators
\mathbb{S}_{k}
and
\Pi_{k}
, we let
c_{1}=0
and parameters
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
should satisfy

\displaystyle(d+1)(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})=2(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2}).

(115)

The parameter
\gamma=c_{2}
. From the calculation, we can conclude that the quantity
\mathcal{D}
is meaningful only when the parameter
\gamma
is nonzero. Thus the free parameters
\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
,
j=1,2
have another constraints such that

\displaystyle d(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2})\neq(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}).

(116)

Now we define two two-qudit imaginary observables,

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{1}=\hat{M}_{1}^{A}\otimes\hat{M}_{1}^{B}=(-i|0\rangle\langle d-1|+i|d-1\rangle\langle 0|)^{\otimes 2},

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{2}=\hat{M}_{2}^{A}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{B}=(-i|0\rangle\langle d-1|+i|d-1\rangle\langle 0|)^{\otimes 2}.

(117)

We have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}(\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\hat{M}_{2}^{k})=2,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbb{S}\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\left(\hat{M}_{2}^{k}\right)^{\top}\right)=-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})=-2.

(118)

In this scenario, the integral is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{1}O^{\top}\hat{M}_{1}\right){\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{2}O^{\top}\hat{M}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\bigotimes_{k=A,B}\int dO_{k}(O_{k}^{\top}\otimes O_{k}^{\top})(\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})(O_{k}\otimes O_{k})(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right].

(119)

Using Lemma
1
, we obtain the following quantity,

\displaystyle\int dO_{k}(O_{k}^{\top}\otimes O_{k}^{\top})(\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})(O_{k}\otimes O_{k})

\displaystyle=\hat{c}_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{d^{2}}+\hat{c}_{2}\mathbb{S}_{k}+\hat{c}_{3}\Pi_{k},

(120)

where the coefficients are

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}=0,

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{-{\mathrm{tr}}(\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}=\frac{2}{d(d-1)},

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{-{\mathrm{tr}}(\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi\hat{M}_{1}^{k}\otimes\hat{M}_{2}^{k})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}=\frac{-2}{d(d-1)}=-\hat{c}_{2}.

(121)

The integral turns to

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\bigotimes_{k=A,B}\left(\mathbb{S}_{k}-\Pi_{k}\right)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}-\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\Pi_{B}-\Pi_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}+\Pi_{A}\otimes\Pi_{B}\right)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right],

(122)

where the parameter
\eta=\frac{2}{d(d-1)}
.

Add the above two integrals with suitable coefficients, and we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{D}}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}{\eta^{2}}

\displaystyle=2{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}+\Pi_{A}\otimes\Pi_{B}\right)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=4{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}\right)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=4{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\right].

(123)

In Eq.(
123
), we use the fact that for any state
\varrho_{1}
and the real state
\varrho_{2}
,
\varrho_{2}=\varrho^{\top}
, the following equation is true

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\Pi_{A}\otimes\Pi_{B}\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}^{\top_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}^{\top_{2}}\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}^{\top_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}^{\top_{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{\bm{l},\bm{m},\bm{j},\bm{k}}\varrho_{1}^{\bm{l}\bm{m}}\varrho_{2}^{\bm{j}\bm{k}}|l_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{l}_{A}|\otimes|m_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{m}_{B}|\otimes|j_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}_{A}|\otimes|k_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{k}_{B}|\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}\right)\left(\sum_{\bm{l},\bm{m},\bm{j},\bm{k}}\varrho_{1}^{\bm{l}\bm{m}}\varrho_{2}^{\bm{j}\bm{k}}|l_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{l}_{A}|\otimes|m_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{m}_{B}|\otimes|j_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}_{A}|^{\top}\otimes|k_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{k}_{B}|^{\top}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}\right)\left(\sum_{\bm{l},\bm{m},\bm{j},\bm{k}}\varrho_{1}^{\bm{l}\bm{m}}\varrho_{2}^{\bm{j}\bm{k}}|l_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{l}_{A}|\otimes|m_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{m}_{B}|\otimes|j_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}_{A}|\otimes|k_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{k}_{B}|\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right].

(124)

We here expand
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}
in the computational basis of subsystems A and B, such that

\displaystyle\varrho_{1}=\sum_{\bm{l},\bm{m}}\varrho_{1}^{\bm{l}\bm{m}}|l_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{l}_{A}|\otimes|m_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{m}_{B}|,\varrho_{2}=\sum_{\bm{j},\bm{k}}\varrho_{2}^{\bm{j}\bm{k}}|j_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}_{A}|\otimes|k_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{k}_{B}|.

(125)

For the real state
\varrho_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\varrho_{2}^{\top}=\sum_{\bm{j},\bm{k}}\varrho_{2}^{\bm{j}\bm{k}}|j_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}_{A}|^{\top}\otimes|k_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{k}_{B}|^{\top}=\varrho_{2}=\sum_{\bm{j},\bm{k}}\varrho_{2}^{\bm{j}\bm{k}}|j_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}_{A}|\otimes|k_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{k}_{B}|.

(126)

Finally, we obtain the overlap

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\right]=\frac{\eta^{2}\mathcal{D}+\gamma^{2}\hat{\mathcal{D}}}{4\gamma^{2}\eta^{2}}.

(127)

∎

The proof indicates that our protocol can estimate the overlap
{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2})
where
\varrho_{2}
must be a real state and
\varrho_{1}
can be any quantum state.

E.4.2
Estimation of the overlap between multipartite states from global and local orthogonal matrices

We present protocols to estimate the overlap
{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2})
of two
N
-qudit states
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}
by RRMs and PRRMs with global and local orthogonal matrices.

Estimation from global orthogonal matrix

Given two
N
-qudit states
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}
, we first apply to both
\varrho_{1}
,
\varrho_{2}
the same random global orthogonal matrix,
O
. Here
O
is sampled via the Haar measure on the orthogonal group
\bm{\mathrm{O}}(d)
. We calculate the expectation value on both states with two
d^{N}\times d^{N}
diagonal observables

\displaystyle M_{1}=\alpha_{1}|0\rangle\langle 0|^{\otimes N}+\alpha_{2}|d-1\rangle\langle d-1|^{\otimes N},M_{2}=\beta_{1}|0\rangle\langle 0|^{\otimes N}+\beta_{2}|d-1\rangle\langle d-1|^{\otimes N},

(128)

where
\{|j\rangle\}
is the computational basis in a
d
-dimension space and nonzero real numbers
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
,
\beta_{1}
, and
\beta_{2}
satisfy

\displaystyle\left(d^{N}+1\right)(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})=2(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2}),

(129)

which enables the second moment operator to be only spanned by the operators
\mathbb{S}
and
\Pi
. We then find that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})=(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}),{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M_{1}\otimes M_{2})={\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi M_{1}\otimes M_{2})=\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2}.

(130)

Hence, the integral turns to

\displaystyle\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{1}O^{\top}M_{1}\right){\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{2}O^{\top}M_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(O\otimes O\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\left(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top}\right)\left(M_{1}\otimes M_{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top}\right)\left(M_{1}\otimes M_{2}\right)\left(O\otimes O\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\int dO\left(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top}\right)\left(M_{1}\otimes M_{2}\right)\left(O\otimes O\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right].

(131)

Using Lemma
1
, we find

\displaystyle\int dO(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top})(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})(O\otimes O)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{d^{N}}+c_{2}\mathbb{S}+c_{3}\Pi,

(132)

where the coefficients are

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d^{N}+1){\mathrm{tr}}(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M_{1}\otimes M_{2})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi M_{1}\otimes M_{2})}{d^{N}(d^{N}-1)(d^{N}+2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d^{N}+1)(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})-2(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2})}{d^{N}(d^{N}-1)(d^{N}+2)},

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{-{\mathrm{tr}}(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})+(d^{N}+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M_{1}\otimes M_{2})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi M_{1}\otimes M_{2})}{d^{N}(d^{N}-1)(d^{N}+2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{-(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})+d^{N}(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2})}{d^{N}(d^{N}-1)(d^{N}+2)},

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{-{\mathrm{tr}}(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}M_{1}\otimes M_{2})+(d^{N}+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\Pi M_{1}\otimes M_{2})}{d^{N}(d^{N}-1)(d^{N}+2)}

\displaystyle=c_{2}.

(133)

Let
c_{1}=0
such that
(d^{N}+1)(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})=2(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2})
. The integral is

\displaystyle\int dO(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top})(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})(O\otimes O)

\displaystyle=\gamma\left(\mathbb{S}+\Pi\right),\penalty\ \gamma=c_{2}.

(134)

Now, we have

\displaystyle\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{1}O^{\top}M_{1}\right){\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{2}O^{\top}M_{2}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\gamma\left(\mathbb{S}+\Pi\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]=2\gamma{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\mathbb{S}\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]=2\gamma{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\right).

(135)

The second equation holds since

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\Pi\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\mathbb{S}\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}^{\top}\right)\right]={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\mathbb{S}\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]

(136)

for the real state
\varrho_{2}=\varrho_{2}^{\top}
. Thus, the overlap is estimated by

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2})=\frac{d^{N}\left(d^{N}-1\right)\left(d^{N}+2\right)}{-2(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})+2d^{N}(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{2})}\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{1}O^{\top}M_{1}\right){\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{2}O^{\top}M_{2}\right).

(137)

In summary, the estimation from a global orthogonal matrix requires one of the quantum states to be a real state.

Estimation from local orthogonal matrices

Suppose we first apply to both
N
-qudit states
\varrho_{1}
,
\varrho_{2}
the same random local orthogonal matrix,
O=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}O_{k}
. We construct real local observables

\displaystyle M_{1}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}M_{1}^{k},M_{2}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}M_{2}^{k},

(138)

where
d\times d
real observables are

\displaystyle M_{1}^{k}=\alpha_{1}|0\rangle\langle 0|+\alpha_{2}|d-1\rangle\langle d-1|,M_{2}^{k}=\beta_{1}|0\rangle\langle 0|+\beta_{2}|d-1\rangle\langle d-1|.

(139)

Thus, the integral is

\displaystyle\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{1}O^{\top}M_{1}\right){\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{2}O^{\top}M_{2}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\int dO(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top})(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})(O\otimes O)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\int_{dO_{k}}(O_{k}^{\top}\otimes O_{k}^{\top})(M_{1}^{k}\otimes M_{2}^{k})(O_{k}\otimes O_{k})(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{N}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\left(\mathbb{S}_{k}+\Pi_{k}\right)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=(2\gamma)^{N}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{S}_{k}(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=(2\gamma)^{N}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\mathbb{S}(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=(2\gamma)^{N}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\right].

(140)

The fourth equation is true if and only if
\varrho_{2}
are partial transpose invariant (PTI) such that
\varrho_{2}=\varrho_{2}^{\top}=\varrho_{2}^{\top_{k}}
for
k=1,\cdots,N
. In this case, the overlap is

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\right]=\left[\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right]^{N}\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{1}O^{\top}M_{1}\right){\mathrm{tr}}\left(O\varrho_{2}O^{\top}M_{2}\right).

(141)

In conclusion, the estimation from the local orthogonal matrix requires one of the quantum states to be a PTI state. For example,
3\otimes 3
bound states such as chessboard and UPB states are PTI states.

Here, we prove the fourth equality of Eq. (
140
) for
N=2
and rewrite two systems as
A,B
. The proof for general
N
is similar to
N=2
. Using Eq. (
125
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\left[(\mathbb{S}_{A}+\Pi_{A})\otimes(\mathbb{S}_{B}+\Pi_{B})(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right]={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}+\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\Pi_{B}+\Pi_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}+\Pi_{A}\otimes\Pi_{B}\right)(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2})\right].

(142)

Without loss of generality, we calculate

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\Pi_{B}\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}^{\top_{2}}\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}^{\top_{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{\bm{l},\bm{m},\bm{j},\bm{k}}\varrho_{1}^{\bm{l}\bm{m}}\varrho_{2}^{\bm{j}\bm{k}}|l_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{l}_{A}|\otimes|m_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{m}_{B}|\otimes|j_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}_{A}|\otimes|k_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{k}_{B}|\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}\right)\left(\sum_{\bm{l},\bm{m},\bm{j},\bm{k}}\varrho_{1}^{\bm{l}\bm{m}}\varrho_{2}^{\bm{j}\bm{k}}|l_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{l}_{A}|\otimes|m_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{m}_{B}|\otimes|j_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}_{A}|\otimes|k_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{k}_{B}|^{\top}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}\right)\left(\sum_{\bm{l},\bm{m},\bm{j},\bm{k}}\varrho_{1}^{\bm{l}\bm{m}}\varrho_{2}^{\bm{j}\bm{k}}|l_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{l}_{A}|\otimes|m_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{m}_{B}|\otimes|j_{A}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}_{A}|\otimes|k_{B}\rangle\langle\tilde{k}_{B}|\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right],

(143)

where the fourth equality holds if
\varrho_{2}=\varrho_{2}^{\top_{B}}
. Similarly,
{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\Pi_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left(\mathbb{S}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{B}\right)\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\right]
means
