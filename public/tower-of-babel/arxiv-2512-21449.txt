Minimal Primes and Radicality of Ideals Generated by Adjacent 2-Minors
Takayuki Hibi, Francesco Navarra, Ayesha Asloob Qureshi, Sara Saeedi Madani

Abstract
. In this paper, we provide a complete description of the minimal primes of ideals generated by adjacent 2 2 -minors, in terms of the so-called admissible sets and associated lattice ideals. We prove that for these ideals, the properties of being unmixed, Cohen–Macaulay, level, Gorenstein, and complete intersection are equivalent. Moreover, we give a combinatorial characterization of all convex collections of cells satisfying any of these equivalent properties. Finally, we study the radicality of these ideals and derive necessary combinatorial conditions based on minimal non-radical configurations.

05B50, 05E40, 13F20, 13P10

Introduction

Determinantal ideals constitute a classical and extensively studied subject in commutative algebra and algebraic geometry (
[
2
,
3
,
12
]
). Among several generalizations of determinantal ideals, a prominent and widely studied class is given by ideals generated by
adjacent
2
–minors
.

Let
X=(x_{ij})_{i=1,\ldots,m\atop j=1,\ldots,n}
be an
m\times n
matrix of indeterminates over a field
K
. An
adjacent
2
–minor
is a binomial of the form
x_{i,j}x_{i+1,j+1}-x_{i+1,j}x_{i,j+1},
arising as the determinant of a
2\times 2
submatrix of
X
. Ideals generated by adjacent
2
–minors arise naturally in several contexts, including the study of lattice and toric ideals
[
7
]
and applications to algebraic statistics, particularly in the theory of contingency tables, that is, matrices of non-negative integers whose row and column sums, called
marginals
, are fixed, see
[
6
,
11
]
. From a statistical perspective, a fundamental problem is to understand the space of all contingency tables with given marginals and, in particular, to determine whether this space is connected under suitable local transformations. Such transformations, usually called
moves
, are integer-valued modifications of a table that preserve the row and column sums while keeping all entries non-negative. A basic class of moves arises from adjacent
2\times 2
sub-matrices: increasing the two diagonal entries
(i,j)
and
(i+1,j+1)
by one and simultaneously decreasing the remaining two entries by one yields a new contingency table with the same row and column sums, provided that all entries remain non–negative. Algebraically, such moves correspond to adjacent
2
–minors, and the ideal
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(X)
generated by all these binomials encodes the connectivity of the space of contingency tables. According to
[
6
, Section 4]
, two tables with the same marginals are connected by adjacent
2\times 2
moves if and only if the corresponding binomial belongs to
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(X)
.

Motivated by these connections, the algebraic structure of ideals generated by adjacent
2
–minors, particularly their primary decompositions and minimal primes, has been studied extensively. A complete combinatorial description of the minimal primes of
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(X)
was obtained by Hoşten and Sullivant
[
14
]
. Building on this perspective, Herzog and Hibi
[
9
]
initiated a systematic investigation of ideals generated by arbitrary sets of adjacent
2
–minors. By interpreting these ideals in terms of collections of cells, they developed a combinatorial framework that enabled the study of primality, radicality, and primary decomposition, especially in the convex case.

In this paper, we substantially extend the line of research initiated in
[
9
]
. We provide a complete description of the minimal primes of ideals generated by adjacent
2
–minors associated with an arbitrary collection of cells. As a consequence, we prove that every unmixed adjacent
2
–minor ideal is a complete intersection, and we obtain a combinatorial characterization of all convex collections of cells satisfying these equivalent conditions. Furthermore, we investigate the radicality of such ideals and establish several necessary conditions.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
1
, we introduce the necessary definitions used throughout the paper. Section
2
is devoted to the description, in full generality, of the minimal primes of ideals generated by adjacent
2
–minors associated with collections of cells. This description is formulated in terms of admissible sets and lattice ideals (see Theorem
2.6
). Our results extend previously known descriptions (see
[
9
, Lemma 3.1]
) and play a central role in proving one of our main results in Section
3
.

Theorem 0.1

(Theorem
3.1
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of cells, and let
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})\subset S_{\mathcal{C}}=K[x_{v}\mid v\text{ is a vertex of }\mathcal{C}]
denote its adjacent
2
–minor ideal.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is a complete intersection;

(2)

S_{\mathcal{C}}/I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is Gorenstein;

(3)

S_{\mathcal{C}}/I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is level;

(4)

S_{\mathcal{C}}/I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is Cohen–Macaulay;

(5)

I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is unmixed.

Moreover, if
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
satisfies above conditions, then
\mathrm{ht}(I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}))=|\mathcal{C}|
.

The reader may refer to
[
2
]
for further details on these properties. From a combinatorial–algebraic perspective, we investigate necessary conditions
on the shape of a collection of cells for the validity of the equivalent properties
listed in Theorem
3.1
. In
Lemmas
3.2
and

3.3
,
we show that to satisfy any of these properties, a collection of cells must avoid both a square tetromino
and an
X
–pentomino, see Figure
2
.
Moreover, when the collection of cells is
convex
,
the absence of these two configurations is not only necessary
but also sufficient for the properties in
Theorem
3.1
to hold.
This yields a complete combinatorial characterization,
which is stated in the following result.

Theorem 0.2

(Theorem
3.5
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a convex collection of cells. Then
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
satisfies the equivalent conditions in Theorem
3.1
if and only if
\mathcal{C}
contains neither a square tetromino nor an
X
-pentomino.

However, this result does not extend beyond the convex case, as shown by a counterexample in Remark
3.7
. Section
4
is devoted to the study of radicality, motivated by the combinatorial interpretation of the primary components given in Corollary
4.1
through the description of the minimal primes. From a computational perspective, we employ computer algebra systems (see
[
16
]
) to explore examples and verify conjectural patterns related to radicality. In particular, we prove in Theorem
4.5
that, among all collections of cells of rank greater than
6
, there always exists a collection of cells that is
minimally non-radical
, that is, not radical but with every weakly connected subcollection being radical (see Definition
4.2
). Finally we provide necessary conditions for radicality in Theorem
4.8
.

1.
Preliminaries

Let
(i,j),(k,l)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We define the partial order
(i,j)\leq(k,l)
if and only if
i\leq k
and
j\leq l
. Given

\mathbb{Z}^{2}

is called an
interval
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. If
i<k
and
j<l
, then the interval
[a,b]
is said to be
proper
; in this case, we refer to
a
and
b
as the
diagonal corners
of
[a,b]
, and to

and
d=(k,j)
as the
anti-diagonal corners
. If

(respectively,

), then
a
and

are said to be in a
horizontal
(respectively,
vertical
)
position
.
A proper interval
C=[a,b]
such that
b=a+(1,1)
is called a
cell
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. The points
a
,
b
,
c
, and
d
are respectively referred to as the
lower left
,
upper right
,
upper left
, and
lower right

corners
of
C
. We denote by

V(C)=\{a,b,c,d\}\quad\text{and}\quad E(C)=\{\{a,c\},\{c,b\},\{b,d\},\{a,d\}\}

the sets of
vertices
and
edges
of
C
, respectively.

For a non-empty collection of cells
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we define the sets of vertices and edges of
\mathcal{C}
, respectively, as

V(\mathcal{C})=\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}V(C),\qquad E(\mathcal{C})=\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}E(C).

The
rank
of
\mathcal{C}
, denoted by
|\mathcal{C}|
, is the number of cells contained in
\mathcal{C}
.

Given two distinct cells
C
and
D
in
\mathcal{C}
, a
walk
(respectively, a
weak walk
) from
C
to
D
in
\mathcal{C}
is a sequence of cells
\mathcal{C}:C=C_{1},\dots,C_{m}=D
in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
C_{i}\cap C_{i+1}
is a common edge (respectively, a common edge or vertex) of
C_{i}
and
C_{i+1}
, for all
i=1,\dots,m-1
. Moreover, if all cells in the sequence are distinct, i.e.,
C_{i}\neq C_{j}
for all
i\neq j
, then
\mathcal{C}
is called a
path
(respectively, a
weak path
) from
C
to
D
.

Two cells
C
and
D
are said to be
connected
(respectively,
weakly connected
) in
\mathcal{C}
if there exists a path (respectively, a weak path) from
C
to
D
in
\mathcal{C}
. A
polyomino

\mathcal{P}
is a finite collection of cells in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that any two cells in
\mathcal{P}
are connected in
\mathcal{P}
.
A finite, non-empty collection of cells
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
weakly connected
if any two cells in
\mathcal{C}
are weakly connected in
\mathcal{C}
.

A
connected component

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{C}
is a polyomino contained in
\mathcal{C}
that is maximal with respect to inclusion; that is, if
A\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, then
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cup\{A\}
is not a polyomino. For instance, the weakly connected collection of cells shown in Figure

(A) consists of four connected components. Observe that every polyomino is a weakly connected collection of cells. Moreover, for convention, we assume that the empty-set is also a collection of cells.

Having established the preceding combinatorial definitions and notation, we now turn to Commutative Algebra. In
[
9
]
and
[
17
]
, the authors introduced the notions of the
adjacent 2-minor ideal
and the

of a collection of cells, which we briefly recall here.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a finite and non-empty collection of cells, and let
S_{\mathcal{C}}=K[x_{v}\mid v\in V(\mathcal{C})]

be its polynomial ring, where
K
is a field. A proper interval
[a,b]
is called an
inner interval
of
\mathcal{C}
if all cells contained in
[a,b]
belong to
\mathcal{C}
.

If
[a,b]
is an inner interval of
\mathcal{C}
with diagonal corners
a
and
b
, and anti-diagonal corners
c
and

, then we consider the binomial
x_{a}x_{b}-x_{c}x_{d}\in S_{\mathcal{C}}
, which is called an
inner 2-minor
of
\mathcal{C}
. In particular, if
[a,b]
is a cell of
\mathcal{C}
, then
x_{a}x_{b}-x_{c}x_{d}
is called an
adjacent 2-minor
of
\mathcal{C}
.

The ideal

in
S_{\mathcal{C}}
, generated by all adjacent

-minors of
\mathcal{C}
, is called the
adjacent 2-minor ideal
of
\mathcal{C}
. We denote by
\mathcal{G}(I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}))
the set of all binomial generators of
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
of the form
x_{a}x_{b}-x_{c}x_{d}
where
[a,b]
is a cell.

Similarly, the ideal
I_{\mathcal{C}}
in
S_{\mathcal{C}}
, generated by all inner
2
-minors of
\mathcal{C}
, is called the
inner 2-minor ideal
of
\mathcal{C}
. Clearly, we have

.

.

Finally, let us recall an important property. If
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of cells that can be written as a union of collections of cells
\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{r}
such that
V(\mathcal{C})
is the disjoint union of the vertex sets
V(\mathcal{C}_{j})
, with
j\in[r]
, then the following isomorphisms hold:

S_{\mathcal{C}}/I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})\cong\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}\left(S_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}/I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}_{i})\right),\qquad S_{\mathcal{C}}/I_{\mathcal{C}}\cong\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}\left(S_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}/I_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}\right).

Most of the commonly studied algebraic properties (such as Cohen–Macaulayness and Gorensteinness) are preserved under tensor products. Hence, it is not restrictive to consider only weakly connected collections of cells. Throughout this paper, whenever we refer to a “collection of cells”, we mean a weakly connected collection of cells.

2.
Minimal primes of adjacent
2
-minor ideals

This section is devoted to studying the minimal prime ideals of the adjacent
2
-minor ideals of a collection of cells. In what follows, we generalize
[
9
, Theorem 3.2]
to a more general setting. Before doing so, we introduce some preliminary algebraic and combinatorial tools. We begin by recalling the definition of the lattice ideal of a collection of cells, as given in
[
17
]
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of cells. For each
a\in V(\mathcal{C})
, denote by
\mathbf{v}_{a}
the vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{|V(\mathcal{C})|}
having
1
in the coordinate indexed by
a
and
0
in all other coordinates. If
C=[a,b]\in\mathcal{C}
is a cell with
a,b
as its diagonal corners and
c,d
as its anti-diagonal corners, we set
\mathbf{v}_{[a,b]}=\mathbf{v}_{a}+\mathbf{v}_{b}-\mathbf{v}_{c}-\mathbf{v}_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{|V(\mathcal{C})|}
. We define
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
as the sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{|V(\mathcal{C})|}
generated by the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{C}
for all
C\in\mathcal{C}
. Observe that the rank of
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
is equal to
|\mathcal{C}|
.

Let
n=|V(\mathcal{C})|
. For
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we denote by
x^{\mathbf{v}}
the monomial in
S_{\mathcal{C}}
having
\mathbf{v}
as its exponent vector. For
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we denote by
\mathbf{e}^{+}
the vector obtained from
\mathbf{e}
by replacing all negative components with zero, and by
\mathbf{e}^{-}=-(\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{e}^{+})
the corresponding non-positive part.

Let
L_{\mathcal{C}}
be the lattice ideal of
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
, that is, the following binomial ideal in
S_{\mathcal{C}}
:

L_{\mathcal{C}}=(\{x^{\mathbf{e}^{+}}-x^{\mathbf{e}^{-}}\mid\mathbf{e}\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}\}).

Given a collection of cells
\mathcal{C}
, the ideal
L_{\mathcal{C}}
is a prime ideal, see
[
17
, pp. 288]
. Moreover, it is shown in
[
17
, Theorem 3.6]
that
I_{\mathcal{C}}
is a prime ideal if and only if
I_{\mathcal{C}}=L_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of cells. Then
L_{\mathcal{C}}
is a minimal prime of
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
. Moreover,
\mathrm{ht}(I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}))\leq|\mathcal{C}|
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
such that
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathfrak{p}\subseteq L_{\mathcal{C}}.
We show that
L_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
.
Let
f\in L_{\mathcal{C}}
. By
[
13
, Proposition 1.1]
, we have

L_{\mathcal{C}}=I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}):\left(\prod_{a\in V(\mathcal{C})}x_{a}\right)^{\infty}.

Hence, there exists a monomial
u\in S_{\mathcal{C}}
such that
uf\in I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
.
Since
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
, it follows that
uf\in\mathfrak{p}
.
If
f\notin\mathfrak{p}
, then by the primality of
\mathfrak{p}
we must have
u\in\mathfrak{p}
.
Again, by the primality of
\mathfrak{p}
, this implies that at least one variable appearing in
u
belongs to
\mathfrak{p}
.
However, since
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq L_{\mathcal{C}}
, this would mean that
L_{\mathcal{C}}
contains a variable, which is a contradiction, as
L_{\mathcal{C}}
does not contain any variable.

Now, it is well known from
[
7
, Theorem 2.1 (b)]
that, if
\Lambda
is a lattice, then its lattice ideal
L_{\Lambda}
has height equal to the rank of
\Lambda
. In our case, the rank of
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
is equal to
|\mathcal{C}|
. Then
\mathrm{ht}(I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}))\leq\mathrm{ht}(L_{\mathcal{C}})=|\mathcal{C}|
.

\square

We now recall the notion of an admissible set, introduced in
[
9
]
, which plays a central role in the description of the minimal primes of adjacent
2
-minor ideals.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of cells. A subset
W\subseteq V(\mathcal{C})
is called an
admissible set
of
\mathcal{C}
if, for every cell
C
of
\mathcal{C}
, either
W\cap C=\emptyset
or
W\cap C
contains at least one edge of
C
.

The following example illustrates the definition.

Example 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the polyomino shown in Figure
1
(B).
Consider
W=\{(1,3),(2,3)\}.
Then
W
is an admissible set of
\mathcal{P}
. Indeed, we have
W\cap A=W\cap B=\emptyset
, and
W\cap C
is an edge of
C
.
Now consider
W^{\prime}=\{(1,1),(2,1)\}.
This set is not admissible, since
W^{\prime}\cap B=\{(2,1)\}
.

Given an admissible set, we can associate to it a subcollection of cells and a naturally defined prime ideal, as follows.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
W
be an admissible set of
\mathcal{C}
. We define the following collection of cells

\mathcal{C}_{W}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}:C\cap W=\emptyset\},

and the associated ideal

P_{W}(\mathcal{C})=(x_{w}:w\in W)+L_{\mathcal{C}_{W}},

where
L_{\mathcal{C}_{W}}
is the lattice ideal attached to
\mathcal{C}_{W}
.

The next remark records two basic properties of the ideal
P_{W}(\mathcal{C})
that will be used repeatedly in what follows.

Remark 2.5
.

For any admissible set
W\subseteq V(\mathcal{C})
, the ideal

P_{W}(\mathcal{C})
is prime. This follows from the fact that

(x_{w}:w\in W)
and the lattice ideal
L_{\mathcal{C}_{W}}
are prime ideals involving
disjoint sets of variables. Moreover, by construction,

P_{W}(\mathcal{C})
contains
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
.
Finally, we have

\mathrm{ht}\big(P_{W}(\mathcal{C})\big)=|W|+|\mathcal{C}_{W}|,

since
\mathrm{ht}\big(L_{\mathcal{C}_{W}}\big)=|\mathcal{C}_{W}|
, as discussed
in the last part of the proof of Proposition
2.1
.

The following theorem shows that all minimal primes of

I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
arise from admissible sets.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of cells and
\mathfrak{p}
be a minimal prime of
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
. Then there exists an admissible set
W
of
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathfrak{p}=P_{W}(\mathcal{C})
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.1
,
L_{\mathcal{C}}
is a minimal prime ideal of
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
. If
\mathfrak{p}=L_{\mathcal{C}}
, then
\mathfrak{p}=P_{W}(\mathcal{C})
by setting
W=\emptyset
. We now assume that
\mathfrak{p}\neq L_{\mathcal{C}}
. Let

W=\{a\in V(\mathcal{C}):x_{a}\in\mathfrak{p}\}.

We first show that
W\neq\emptyset
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
W=\emptyset
, i.e., that
\mathfrak{p}
contains no variables. Let
f\in L_{\mathcal{C}}
. By
[
13
, Proposition 1.1]
, there exists a monomial
u
in
S_{\mathcal{C}}
such that
uf\in I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
, and thus
uf\in\mathfrak{p}
. If
f\notin\mathfrak{p}
, then
u\in\mathfrak{p}
by the primality of
\mathfrak{p}
, implying that
\mathfrak{p}
contains a variable, contradicting the assumption that
W=\emptyset
. Therefore,
f\in\mathfrak{p}
, i.e.,
L_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
. By Proposition
2.1
and the minimality of
\mathfrak{p}
, we must have
\mathfrak{p}=L_{\mathcal{C}}
, contradicting the assumption
\mathfrak{p}\neq L_{\mathcal{C}}
. Hence,
W\neq\emptyset
.

We now show that
W
is an admissible set of
\mathcal{C}
. Let
C
be a cell in
\mathcal{C}
with
V(C)=\{a,b,c,d\}
such that
a,b
and
c,d
are the diagonal and anti-diagonal corners, respectively, and
W\cap C\neq\emptyset
. Without loss of generality, assume that
a\in W\cap C
. Note that
x_{a}x_{b}-x_{c}x_{d}\in I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
, and since
a\in W
, we have
x_{a}\in\mathfrak{p}
, which implies that
x_{c}x_{d}\in\mathfrak{p}
. Since
\mathfrak{p}
is prime, either
x_{c}\in\mathfrak{p}
or
x_{d}\in\mathfrak{p}
, that is, either
c\in W
or
d\in W
. Thus, either
\{a,c\}\subseteq W\cap C
or
\{a,d\}\subseteq W\cap C
. This implies that
W\cap C
contains an edge, and hence,
W
is an admissible set of
\mathcal{C}
.

We now prove that
\mathfrak{p}=P_{W}(\mathcal{C})
. Since
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}_{W})\subseteq L_{\mathcal{C}_{W}}
, we have

I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq(x_{w}:w\in W)+L_{\mathcal{C}_{W}}=P_{W}(\mathcal{C}).

Since
P_{W}(\mathcal{C})
is prime, see Remark
2.5
, to conclude that
\mathfrak{p}=P_{W}(\mathcal{C})
, it suffices to show that
P_{W}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
, and in particular that

From Unmixedness to Complete Intersection for adjacent
2
-minor ideals

The description of minimal primes in Theorem
2.6
yields the following equivalence among several well-known algebraic properties of adjacent
2
-minor ideals.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of cells. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is a complete intersection;

(2)

S_{\mathcal{C}}/I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is Gorenstein;

(3)

S_{\mathcal{C}}/I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is level;

(4)

S_{\mathcal{C}}/I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is Cohen–Macaulay;

(5)

I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is unmixed.

Moreover, if
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
satisfies above conditions, then
\mathrm{ht}(I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}))=|\mathcal{C}|
.

Proof.

The implications
(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow(3)\Rightarrow(4)\Rightarrow(5)
always hold for graded ideals. We now prove the converse implication
(5)\Rightarrow(1)
. Suppose that
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is unmixed, that is, all its minimal primes have the same height. By Proposition
2.1
, we have
\mathrm{ht}(I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}))=|\mathcal{C}|
. This value coincides with
|\mathcal{G}(I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}))|
. Therefore,
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is a complete intersection.

Assume that
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
satisfies any of the given conditions. Then
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is an unmixed ideal and by Proposition
2.1
, it follows that
\mathrm{ht}(I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C}))=|\mathcal{C}|
.

\square

As a consequence of Theorem
3.1
, we derive two necessary combinatorial conditions on the shape of
\mathcal{C}
that must be satisfied whenever
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
has one of the equivalent properties listed therein.

We recall that the polyominoes shown on the left and on the right in Figure

are known in the literature as the

and the

, respectively.

We next introduce the following definition.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of cells. If

We prove that if
\mathcal{C}

is not unmixed. By Proposition

\mathrm{ht}(L_{\mathcal{C}})=|\mathcal{C}|

whose height is strictly smaller than

, which implies that
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is not unmixed. We begin by setting up some notation that will be used throughout the proof, with reference to Figure

4.
On the radicality of adjacent
2
-minor ideals

In this section, we aim to provide necessary conditions for the radicality of adjacent
2
-minor ideals. We begin with the following consequence of Theorem
2.6
, together with the fact that the radical of an ideal is the intersection of its minimal prime ideals.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of cells. Then

\sqrt{I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})}=\bigcap_{W\subseteq V(\mathcal{C})}P_{W}(\mathcal{C}),

where the intersection is taken over all admissible sets
W\subseteq V(\mathcal{C})
.

The above result yields a primary decomposition of
\sqrt{I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})}
, although it is not necessarily minimal. Indeed, not every admissible set of
\mathcal{C}
corresponds to a minimal prime of
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
, as illustrated in Remark
2.7
.

Given a collection of cells
\mathcal{C}
, our strategy is to identify subcollections of
\mathcal{C}
whose presence guarantees the non-radicality of
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
.

Definition 4.2
.

A collection of cells
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
minimally non-radical
if
I_{\mathrm{adj}}(\mathcal{C})
is not radical and, for every cell
C
of
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathcal{C}\setminus\{C\}
is weakly connected, the adjacent

We now turn to necessary conditions for radicality. The following lemma and proposition provides a useful tool for this purpose.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
J
be an ideal of
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}]
such that
J=I+L

I
and
L
ideals of
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
K[y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}]
, respectively. If
I
or
L
is not radical, then
J
is not radical.

Proof.

If
I
is not radical, then there exists
f\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that
f\notin I
but
f^{k}\in I
for some
k\geq 2
. Since
I\subset J
, we obtain
f^{k}\in J
.
Suppose that
f\in J=I+L
. Since
f\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, it follows that
f\notin L
and
f\in I
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
f\notin J
, and consequently
J
is not radical, as claimed. Same argument can be done if
L
is not radical.

\square
