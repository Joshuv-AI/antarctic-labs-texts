Parameter estimation from local measurements for a class of stochastic Burgers equations
Josef Janák, Enrico Priola

Abstract
We deal with a class of semilinear SPDEs driven by space–time white noise that includes the one dimensional stochastic Burgers equation. Such equations can have nonlocal and quadratic nonlinearities. We consider the problem of estimation of the diffusivity parameter in front of the second-order spatial derivative. Based on local observations in space, we study the estimator derived in ( Ann. Appl. Probab. 31 (2021) 1–38) for linear stochastic heat equation that has also been used in ( Bernoulli 29 (2023) 2035–2061) to cover certain class of semilinear SPDEs including stochastic Burgers equations driven by trace class noise. The space-time white noise case we consider has also relevant physical motivations. After we establish new regularity results for the solution, we are able to show that our proposed estimator is strongly consistent and asymptotically normal.



Keywords:

Generalized stochastic Burgers equation; space-time white noise; local measurements; diffusivity estimation; augmented MLE; central limit theorem.

Introduction

We consider estimation of the diffusivity parameter
\vartheta>0
in a class of generalized stochastic Burgers equation driven by space-time white noise:

\begin{cases}dX(t)&=\vartheta\partial_{xx}^{2}X(t)\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(X^{2}(t)\right)\,dt+F(X(t))\,dt+dW(t),\\
X(0)&=X_{0},\\
X(t)|_{\partial\Lambda}&=0,\quad 0<t\leq T.\end{cases}

(1.1)

Here
W
stands for a cylindrical Wiener process on
L^{2}(\Lambda)
, where
\Lambda=(0,1)\subset{\mathbb{R}}
,
dW(t)/dt
is also referred to as space-time white noise. The nonlinearity
F
is locally Lipschitz and can have a quadratic growth. When
F=0
we obtain the (classical) stochastic Burgers equation. We consider Dirichlet boundary conditions and a continuous and deterministic initial condition
X_{0}
defined on
\bar{\Lambda}
. Note that (
1.1
) is often written as

\begin{cases}dX(t,x)&=\vartheta\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}X(t,x)\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}X^{2}(t,x)\,dt+F(X(t,\cdot))(x)\,dt+dW_{t}(x),\\
X(0,x)&=X_{0}(x),\quad x\in(0,1)=\Lambda,\\
X(t,0)&=X(t,1)=0,\quad 0<t\leq T,\end{cases}

(1.2)

and the same equation with the nonlinear term
-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}X^{2}(t,x)\,dt
can be treated similarly. The stochastic Burgers equation has an important role in fluid dynamics and has been studied by several authors. We mainly refer to the works
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
, where the existence and uniqueness of the global solution as well as the existence of an invariant measure has been established; see also Chapter 14 from
[
15
]
. We also recall
[
12
]
, where moment estimates for the solution were established. Generalized stochastic Burgers equations like (
1.1
) are studied in the literature under different assumptions (see, for instance,
[
18
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
32
]
). Interesting applications of generalized Burgers equations are mentioned in
[
41
]
.

We first establish existence and uniqueness of regular solutions to (
1.1
) (see Proposition
2.7
) and prove moment estimates (or integrability properties) for the solution (see Proposition
4.7
). The following one is an example of
F
we can treat:

F(u)(x)=-c_{0}\,u(x)|u(x)|\,+\,u(x)\,h\big(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\big),\quad x\in\Lambda,\,a.e.,

u\in L^{2}(\Lambda)
,
h
is any bounded
C^{1}
-function,
c_{0}>0
(see also hypothesis (
F
) and Examples
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
). It seems that even well-posedness of (
1.1
) under our assumptions is not covered by the results in the literature. In particular our generalization of the stochastic Burgers equation leads in a different way than
[
18
]
; see also Remark
2.4
. We mention other types of generalized stochastic Burgers equations in Remark
2.6
.

The estimation of the diffusivity (or the drift part) has become a standard inference problem for stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) in both parametric and nonparametric setting, but it is worth noting that parameter estimation for the stochastic Burgers equation has not been much investigated. In that regard, we may mention the work
[
10
]
, where statistical inference for the very related Navier-Stokes equations is studied, and
[
38
]
, where stochastic Burgers equation driven by a trace class noise fits into a class of semilinear stochastic evolution equations.

In general, the classical methods mostly rely on the observations in the Fourier space over some time interval (with the number of modes
N\rightarrow\infty
in the so-called spectral approach, see, e.g.,
[
26
]
) or on the ergodic properties of the solution (with
T\rightarrow\infty
, see, e.g.,
[
34
]
) or on discrete sampling (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
24
]
). However in this paper we continue to expand the methodology that is based on local measurements. This novel approach was first introduced in
[
3
]
and relies on a local observation in space of the solution against some small “observational window”. That is represented by a function called kernel
K
that is scaled and localized around certain observation point
x_{0}\in\Lambda
. The observational data then comes in the form of convolution
\left\langle X(t),K_{\delta,x_{0}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}
and the asymptotics is studied for
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

The augmented maximum likelihood estimator (augmented MLE) of
\vartheta
was introduced in
[
3
]
for linear SPDEs in nonparametric setting. Since then, it was used in the parametric setting to experimental data from cell biology (see
[
1
]
and note that such usage is the practical motivation of local measurements) and from the theoretical point of view, the estimator is remarkably flexible and robust to some misspecifications. For instance,
[
29
]
studied the augmented MLE and its variants in the case of stochastic heat equation driven by the multiplicative noise, while the authors in
[
2
]
focused on a case of semilinear SPDEs. Their work covers a wide range of nonlinearities (such as stochastic reaction-diffusion equations) assuming that the SPDE is driven by additive noise
B\,dW(t)
, where
B^{*}
scales as the fractional operator
(-\partial_{xx}^{2})^{-\gamma}
. However, the classical stochastic Burgers equation with
F=0
in (
1.1
) is still in some sense a “borderline case” of their presented theory. The augmented MLE is strongly consistent, but the asymptotic normality needed to be analyzed separately and only in the case of a trace class noise:
(-\partial_{xx}^{2})^{-\gamma}\,dW(t)
for
\gamma>1/4
(that is the case when
B
is Hilbert-Schmidt). This was the starting point of our work.

In particular we study the augmented MLE for classical stochastic Burgers equation that is driven by space-time white noise (i.e., the case when
\gamma=0
). We believe that this case is challenging and from the theoretical point of view also very interesting (for physical motivations see
[
6
]
,
[
9
]
and
[
31
]
; for numerical approximations see
[
21
]
). When
F=0
our results and arguments can be further extended to cover the case of spatially “smoothed” additive noise
(-\partial_{xx}^{2})^{-\gamma}\,dW(t)
with
\gamma\in[0,1/4]
but, in fact, to cover all
\gamma\geq 0
. However, this would require extra effort. Among other things, one should revise all the proofs in Appendix for such general
\gamma
,
d=1
and
B=(-\partial_{xx}^{2})^{-\gamma}
.

At first, we consider the notion of mild solution to our equation (
1.1
) using so-called “splitting technique”, where we split the solution to the linear part
\bar{X}
and the nonlinear part
\widetilde{X}
. For the analysis of a (possible) asymptotic bias, we need to assert the regularities of both parts. The results on regularity of the stochastic convolution
\bar{X}
are basically known, but for the nonlinear part
\widetilde{X}
, the results seem to be new; see in particular Propositions
2.7
and
4.7
.

For the analysis of the error
\delta^{-1}\left(\hat{\vartheta}_{\delta}-\vartheta\right)
, we follow general ideas from
[
2
]
and its supplement, where many auxiliary lemmas (e.g., for scaling) and propositions do hold true even in our case
\gamma=0
(see Section
A.1
in Appendix). It must be noted though, that we use different proof techniques to adjust to our space-time white noise case and to clarify some points of the original paper; see also Remark
3.3
. We believe that Lemma
4.10
is pivotal to our parameter estimation. In its proof we show the expansion of the non-Gaussian process

\widetilde{X}(t,x_{0})=\widetilde{X}(t)(x_{0})
in the form
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}(t)\nu_{i}
. That requires subtle care, especially in building a separable Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}^{\prime},{\mathbb{P}})
with its orthonormal basis
(\nu_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
(we are considering a suitable
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{F}
). Note that we are not able to apply the classical Karhunen-Loève expansion in our case due to the low regularity of
\widetilde{X}
(see also Remark
4.11
).

In conclusion, we find that the augmented MLE is asymptotically normal, asymptotically unbiased and with the rate of convergence and the asymptotic variance that align to previous results from the theory of estimation using local measurements. The precise statement is formulated in Theorem
3.4
. Based on the asymptotic result, we can deduce (data-driven) confidence intervals for
\vartheta
. We mention that numerical simulations for stochastic Burgers equation (
1.1
) driven by space-time white noise are proposed in Section 4 of
[
2
]
(see Figure 1 therein, where the white noise is multiplied by a factor
\sigma=0.05
).

Our paper is organized as follows. The notation and the exact setting is introduced in Section
2
, where we also discuss the notion of mild and weak solution. The results on the regularity of the nonlinear part
\widetilde{X}
can be also found here. In Section
3
we construct the estimator, revisit assumptions of the model and present the main asymptotic results. The proofs and auxiliary results are delegated to Section
4
and Appendix, where they are assorted by the topic. For the sake of completeness, we also provide the most relevant results from
[
2
]
that are revisited and adjusted to our setting in Section

2
The model

2.1
Notation

Consider
\Lambda=(0,1)
and the space
L^{2}(\Lambda)
equipped with the usual
L^{2}
-norm
\|\cdot\|:=\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}
and the scalar product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle:=\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}
. Even though the set
\Lambda
is only one dimensional, we use the standard Laplace operator notation

\Delta u=\partial_{xx}^{2}u=u^{\prime\prime}

for a function
u
which is regular enough. We denote
(\lambda_{n},e_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
the eigensystem of the positive, self-adjoint operator
-\Delta
on
\Lambda
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. That is

\lambda_{n}=\pi^{2}n^{2},\quad e_{n}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin(nx\pi),\quad x\in(0,1).

To describe higher regularities, we consider for
s\geq 0
the fractional Laplacians
(-\Delta)^{s/2}
and denote their domains by
H^{s}=H^{s}(\Lambda)
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{s}:=\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}}:=\|(-\Delta)^{s/2}\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}
. The Hilbert spaces

H^{s}=\Dom\left((-\Delta)^{s/2})\right)=\Big\{u\in L^{2}(\Lambda);\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{s}u_{n}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{s}\left\langle u,e_{n}\right\rangle^{2}<\infty\Big\}

are equipped with the scalar product
\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{H^{s}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{s}u_{n}v_{n}
for
u,v\in H^{s}
. We also set

H:=L^{2}(\Lambda)

and by
B(0,n)
we denote the closed ball in the space
H
with the radius
n
, that is
B(0,n)=\{u\in H;\,\|u\|\leq n\}
.
Recall that
H^{1}=H_{0}^{1}
with equivalence of norms, where
H_{0}^{1}
denotes the space of all
u\in H
with weak derivative
u^{\prime}\in H
and such that
u(x)=0
for
x\in\partial\Lambda
(cf. Section 1.3 in
[
23
]
, or
[
21
]
).

For
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
p\geq 2
we consider also
W^{s,p}(\Lambda):=\{u\in L^{p}(\Lambda);\,\|u\|_{s,p}<\infty\}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{s,p}:=\|(-\Delta)^{s/2}\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Lambda)}
. These are fractional Sobolev spaces associated to
\Delta
defined as Bessel potentials spaces. The space
C(\bar{\Lambda})
of all continuous functions on
\bar{\Lambda}
is equipped with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. The spaces
C^{k,\alpha}(\Lambda)
for
k\geq 0
integer and
0<\alpha<1
are Hölder spaces equipped with usual norms.

We fix a constant
\vartheta>0
, that is the parameter of interest, and denote by
(S_{\vartheta}(t),t\geq 0)
the strongly continuous semigroup on
L^{2}(\Lambda)
generated by
\vartheta\Delta
.

Throughout this work we fix a finite time horizon
T>0
and let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a stochastic basis (satisfying the usual hypotheses) with a cylindrical Wiener process
W
on
L^{2}(\Lambda)
(cf.
[
16
]
). The process
W(t)
is formally given by “
W(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(t)e_{n}
” where
(\beta_{n})
are independent one dimensional Wiener processes adapted to the previous filtration; we are using the above orthonormal basis
(e_{n})
in
L^{2}(\Lambda)
.

Throughout, all equalities and inequalities, unless otherwise mentioned, will be understood in the
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s. sense. By
A_{\delta}\lesssim B_{\delta}
we mean that there exists some constant
C>0
such that
A_{\delta}\leq CB_{\delta}
for all values
\delta
under consideration. Here, we work with
\delta\in(0,1)
or with the convergence
\delta\to 0
. Convergence in probability and convergence in distribution are denoted by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}
and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}
, respectively. The symbol
A_{\delta}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(B_{\delta})
for random variables
A_{\delta},B_{\delta}
means that
A_{\delta}/B_{\delta}
is tight, that is,
\sup_{\delta}\mathbb{P}(|A_{\delta}|>C|B_{\delta}|)\rightarrow 0
as
C\rightarrow\infty
. The notation
A_{\delta}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(B_{\delta})
stands for
A_{\delta}/B_{\delta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
.

2.2
The generalized stochastic Burgers equation

We study the generalized stochastic Burgers equation (
1.1
) driven by a cylindrical
Wiener proces
W
on
H=L^{2}(\Lambda)
. Our nonlinearity is given by a function
F:L^{2}(\Lambda)\rightarrow L^{1}(\Lambda)
. We also assume the following conditions:

(a)

For any
R>0
there exists
C_{R}>0
such that for all
u,v\in B(0,R)=\{u\in H;\,\|u\|\leq R\}:

\|F(u)-F(v)\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda)}\leq C_{R}\|u-v\|.

(b)

There exists
q\geq 2,\,C_{q}>0
such that for all
u,v\in L^{2}(\Lambda)
for any
x\in\Lambda,\,a.e.:

F(u+v)(x)\cdot v(x)\leq C_{q}(1+|u(x)|^{q}+|v(x)|^{2}).

(F)

(c)

F
maps bounded sets of
L^{\infty}(\Lambda)
into bounded sets of
L^{p}(\Lambda)
for some
p>2
.

Note that hypotheses (a) and (b) are needed to prove well-posedness of our SPDEs as well as to establish the additional moment estimates of the solution (see Proposition
4.7
). On the other hand condition (c) is only needed to perform the parameter estimation (see in particular the proof of Lemma
4.12
).

The following stronger conditions imply the previous assumptions:
(i)
F:L^{2}(\Lambda)\to L^{2}(\Lambda)
is locally Lipschitz, (ii) there exists
C>0
such that for any
u\in L^{2}(\Lambda)
and for all
x\in\Lambda,\,a.e.
, we have:
|F(u)(x)|\leq C\left(1+|u(x)|\right)
. However these hypotheses prevent the study of nonlinearities which grow quadratically.

Now we provide examples of nonlinearity
F:L^{2}(\Lambda)\to L^{1}(\Lambda)
that satisfy our conditions (a), (b) and (c). We give more details in Section
A.2
in Appendix. The first examples are nonlocal nonlinearities.

Example 2.1
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be real functions such that
f_{1}
is bounded and locally Lipschitz and
f_{2}
is (globally) Lipschitz. For any
u\in L^{2}(\Lambda)
, we define

(F(u))(x)=f_{1}\left(\|u\|\right)\cdot f_{2}\left(u(x)\right),\quad x\in\Lambda,\,a.e..

Example 2.2
.

Let
g:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a real function such that
g
is bounded and locally Lipschitz. Let us fix a function
h\in L^{\infty}(\Lambda)
. For any
u\in L^{2}(\Lambda)
, we define

(F(u))(x)=g\left(\|u\|\right)\cdot h(x),\quad x\in\Lambda,\,a.e.

Similarly, one can consider examples like
(F(u))(x)=h(x)\int_{0}^{1}g(y)f(u(y))\,dy
,
x\in\Lambda,\,a.e.
, where
g,h\in L^{\infty}(\Lambda)
and
f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is Lipschitz and bounded.

Example 2.3
.

This class concerns with Nemytskii type nonlinearities. For any
u\in L^{2}(\Lambda)
, we define

(F(u))(x)=f(u(x)),\quad x\in\Lambda,\,a.e.,

(2.1)

where
f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a
C^{1}
-function such that the following conditions holds: There exists
C>0
,

,
C_{q}>0
such that

(i)

|f(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{2}),\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

(ii)

|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq C(1+|x|),\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

(iii)

f(x+y)\,y\leq C_{q}(1+|y|^{2}+|x|^{q}),\;\;\,x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

For instance, we can fix
\eta\in[0,1]
and consider
f(x)=-x|x|^{\eta}\,g(|x|^{1-\eta})

Remark 2.6
.

Generalized stochastic Burgers equations can also be studied when the nonlinear term

In this paper for the initial condition

.

\left\langle z,X(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle z,X_{0}\right\rangle+\vartheta\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\Delta z,X(s)\right\rangle\,ds-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\partial_{x}z,X^{2}(s)\right\rangle\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle z,F(X(s))\right\rangle\,ds+\left\langle z,W(t)\right\rangle.

The proof is not difficult, but rather lengthy. We give a sketch of proof in Section

in Appendix.

We finish the section with a result on well-posedness of (

. We stress that well-posedness of mild solutions for the Burgers equation (

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
X_{0}\in H
. Then there exists a pathwise unique mild solution
X
to the equation (

).

Proof.

Let
T>0
. For any
n\geq 1
, we introduce the projection
\pi_{n}:H=L^{2}(\Lambda)\to B(0,n)
, with
B(0,n)=\{u\in H;\,\|u\|=\|u\|_{H}\leq n\}
(here
\pi_{n}(u)=u
if
u\in B(0,n)
and
\pi_{n}(u)=\frac{nu}{\|u\|}
if
\|u\|>n
). We consider

X_{n}(t)={S_{\vartheta}(t)X_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)\partial_{x}\left([\pi_{n}(X_{n}(s))]^{2}\right)\,ds}+\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)F(\pi_{n}(X_{n}(s)))\,ds+\bar{X}(t),

(2.7)

for
t\in[0,T]
,
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s. Arguing as in
[

one can prove that (

) has a unique solution on a small time interval

by the contraction principle, using the space
Z_{R}

Z_{R}
is the Banach space of all continuous adapted
H

such that
\|u\|_{Z_{R}}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}[\sup_{t\in[0,{R}]}\|u(t)\|^{2}]<\infty
.

We stress that

is a

constant (when

exists and it is unique on
[0,T]
. Clearly, this solution is an adapted stochastic process.

We only show how to handle the additional term
\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)F(\pi_{n}(X_{n}(s)))\,ds
which is not present in
[
14
]
. Let
0<R\leq T
. For

, define the nonlinear operator

(M_{n}u)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)F\left(\pi_{n}u(s)\right)\,ds,\quad t\in[0,R].

Let

be such that

. We have,
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s.,

\displaystyle\left\|(M_{n}u)(t)-(M_{n}v)(t)\right\|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left\|S_{\vartheta}(t-s)\left(F(\pi_{n}(u(s)))-F(\pi_{n}(v(s)))\right)\right\|\,ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{(t-s)^{1/4}}\left\|F(\pi_{n}(u(s))-F(\pi_{n}(v(s)))\right\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda)}\,ds\leq C_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{(t-s)^{1/4}}\left\|\pi_{n}(u(s))-\pi_{n}(v(s))\right\|\,ds

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{(t-s)^{1/4}}\left\|u(s)-v(s)\right\|\,ds\leq\frac{4}{3}C_{n}\,R^{3/4}\sup_{t\in[0,R]}\|u(t)-v(t)\|,

where we have used the local Lipschitz property of

\|S_{\vartheta}(t)\phi\|\leq\frac{C}{t^{1/4}}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda)},\quad\phi\in L^{1}(\Lambda),\quad t\in(0,T],

that holds for some constant

(the proof is standard; it involves the explicit representation of the heat kernel). We find

\|M_{n}u-M_{n}v\|_{Z_{R}}\leq\frac{1}{6}\|u-v\|_{Z_{R}},

for small enough deterministic

and we can continue as in
[
14

. Indeed we can repeatedly apply the contraction principle on a finite number of closed time intervals

,

, with length less or equal than

(clearly,

). Thus on each

can be obtained,
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s., as limit on

Solutions of (

In

\tau_{n}=\inf\{t\geq 0;\,\|X_{n}(t)\|\geq n\},\quad n\geq 1.

(2.8)

Since

,

,

.
One can obtain a (global) mild solution by proving that

,
{\mathbb{P}}

[

(see also page 263 in

Following
[

]
we first consider a regular approximation of

. Note that
\bar{X}\in C([0,T];C(\bar{\Lambda}))
,
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s. (cf. Section 5 in

). With such approximation we can work with equation (

Note that the final obtained estimates do not require the presence of the regular approximation of

Multiplying both sides of equation (

, integrating with respect to

and using the integration by parts we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{X}^{2}(t,x)\,dx+\vartheta\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{x}\widetilde{X}(t,x))^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\widetilde{X}(t,x)+\bar{X}(t,x)\right)^{2}\partial_{x}\widetilde{X}(t,x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}F\left(\bar{X}(t,\cdot)+\widetilde{X}(t,\cdot)\right)(x)\,\widetilde{X}(t,x)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{X}^{2}(t,x)\partial_{x}\widetilde{X}(t,x)\,dx-\int_{0}^{1}\bar{X}(t,x)\widetilde{X}(t,x)\partial_{x}\widetilde{X}(t,x)\,dx

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{X}^{2}(t,x)\partial_{x}\widetilde{X}(t,x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}F\left(\bar{X}(t,\cdot)+\widetilde{X}(t,\cdot)\right)(x)\,\widetilde{X}(t,x)\,dx.

(2.9)

The first term on the right-hand side equals to zero due to the boundary conditions

\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{X}^{2}(t,x)\partial_{x}\widetilde{X}(t,x)\,dx=\frac{1}{3}\left[\widetilde{X}^{3}(t,x)\right]_{x=0}^{x=1}=0.

For the last term on the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}F\left(\bar{X}(t,\cdot)+\widetilde{X}(t,\cdot)\right)(x)\,\widetilde{X}(t,x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{q}\int_{0}^{1}\left(1+|\bar{X}(t,x)|^{q}+|\widetilde{X}(t,x)|^{2}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=C_{q}\left(1+\|\bar{X}(t)\|_{L^{q}(\Lambda)}^{q}+\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|^{2}\right),

(2.10)

so we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|^{2}+\vartheta\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{X}(t)\|_{C(\bar{\Lambda})}\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|_{1}\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|+\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{X}(t)\|_{C(\bar{\Lambda})}^{2}\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|_{1}+C_{q}\left(1+\|\bar{X}(t)\|_{C(\bar{\Lambda})}^{q}+\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\vartheta}{2}\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|_{1}^{2}+\frac{2}{\vartheta}\|\bar{X}(t)\|_{C(\bar{\Lambda})}^{2}\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|^{2}+\frac{\vartheta}{4}\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{\vartheta}\|\bar{X}(t)\|_{C(\bar{\Lambda})}^{4}+C_{q}+C_{q}\|\bar{X}(t)\|_{C(\bar{\Lambda})}^{q}+C_{q}\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|^{2}.

Therefore, we can easily apply the Gronwall lemma as in the proof of Lemma 3.1 in

and get

\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|^{2}\leq C(\mu+\|X_{0}\|^{2}+1)e^{T\mu}

(2.12)

with

,

,

. This finishes the proof.
∎

2.3
The observation scheme

As motivated in
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
29
]
, we observe the solution process
(X(t,x),\,t\in[0,T],x\in\Lambda)
only locally in space around some point
x_{0}\in\Lambda
. That point
x_{0}
as well as the terminal time
T>0
remain fixed.

More precisely, the observations are given by a spatial convolution of the solution process with a kernel
K_{\delta,x_{0}}
, localising at
x_{0}
as the resolution
\delta
tends to zero. This kernel might for instance model the
point spread function
in microscopy.

For
z\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\delta\in(0,1]
introduce the scalings

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\delta,x_{0}}

\displaystyle:=\delta^{-1}\left(\Lambda-x_{0}\right)=\{\delta^{-1}(x-x_{0});\,x\in\Lambda\},

\displaystyle z_{\delta,x_{0}}(x)

\displaystyle:=\delta^{-1/2}z\left(\delta^{-1}(x-x_{0})\right),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}},

and we also set
\Lambda_{0,x_{0}}:={\mathbb{R}}
. For
\delta\in(0,1]
, we denote by
\Delta_{\delta,x_{0}}
the Laplace operator on
L^{2}(\Lambda_{\delta,x_{0}})
with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Throughout this paper,
K\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
stands for a fixed function of compact support in
\Lambda_{\delta^{\prime},x_{0}}
, for some
0<\delta^{\prime}\leq 1
, called kernel (here
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is the usual
L^{2}
-Sobolev space on
{\mathbb{R}}
); note that
\delta^{\prime}
may depend on
x_{0}
. The compact support ensures that
K_{\delta,x_{0}}
is localising around
x_{0}
as
\delta\to 0
; moreover we have
K_{\delta,x_{0}}\in H^{2}=H^{2}(\Lambda)
,
0<\delta\leq\delta^{\prime}
. The scaling with
\delta^{-1/2}
simplifies calculations due to
\|K_{\delta,x_{0}}\|=\|K\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
, while the proposed estimator is invariant with respect to kernel scaling.

Local measurements of (
1.1
) at the point
x_{0}
with resolution level
\delta\in(0,1]
are described by the real-valued processes
(X_{\delta,x_{0}}(t),0\leq t\leq T)
and
(X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t),0\leq t\leq T)
given by

\displaystyle X_{\delta,x_{0}}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle X(t),K_{\delta,x_{0}}\right\rangle,

(2.13)

\displaystyle X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle X(t),\Delta K_{\delta,x_{0}}\right\rangle.

(2.14)

These two processes are the data for our estimation procedure. In fact, since
X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)=\Delta X_{\delta,\cdot}(t)|_{x=x_{0}}
by convolution,
X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)
can be computed by observing
X_{\delta,x}(t)
for
x
in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
.

3
Estimation method and main results

3.1
The estimator

We use the augmented maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{\delta}
of
\vartheta
introduced in
[
3
]
. This (nonparametric) estimator was derived for a linear stochastic heat equation with additive space-time white noise, but
[
2
]
studied it in an abstract nonlinear (and parametric) setting that also covers the stochastic Burgers equation with trace class noise.

Definition 3.1
.

The
augmented maximum likelihood estimator
(augmented
MLE
)
\hat{\vartheta}_{\delta}
of the parameter
\vartheta>0
is defined as

\hat{\vartheta}_{\delta}=\frac{\int_{0}^{T}X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)\,dX_{\delta,x_{0}}(t)}{\int_{0}^{T}\left(X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)\right)^{2}\,dt}.

(3.1)

As discussed in
[
3
]
, this estimator is closely related to, but different from, the actual MLE, which cannot be computed in closed form, even for linear equations and constant
\vartheta
.

By Lemma
2.8
the dynamics of
X_{\delta,x_{0}}
is given by

dX_{\delta,x_{0}}=\vartheta X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\partial_{x}\left(X^{2}(t)\right),K_{\delta,x_{0}}\right\rangle\,dt+\left\langle F(X(t)),K_{\delta,x_{0}}\right\rangle\,dt+\|K\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\,d\bar{w}(t),

(3.2)

where
\bar{w}(t):=\left\langle W(t),K_{\delta,x_{0}}\right\rangle/\|K_{\delta,x_{0}}\|
is a scalar Brownian motion. (Note that
\|K_{\delta,x_{0}}\|=\|K\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}>0
). Using (
3.2
) in the numerator on the right-hand side of (
3.1
), we obtain the fundamental error decomposition

\delta^{-1}(\hat{\vartheta}_{\delta}-\vartheta)=\delta^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{\delta}+\delta^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{\delta},

(3.3)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\delta}

\displaystyle:=\|K\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{-2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)\right)^{2}\,dt,

(observed Fisher information)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\delta}

\displaystyle:=\|K\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{-2}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle\partial_{x}\left(X^{2}(t)\right),K_{\delta,x_{0}}\right\rangle\,dt

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\|K\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{-2}\int_{0}^{T}X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle F(X(t)),K_{\delta,x_{0}}\right\rangle\,dt,

(nonlinear bias)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\delta}

\displaystyle:=\|K\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}X_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)\,d\bar{w}(t).

(martingale part)

The observed Fisher information
\mathcal{I}_{\delta}
does not correspond to the Fisher information of the statistical model, however it gives the quadratic variation of the martingale
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}
in time.

To prove consistency, it is enough to show that
(\mathcal{I}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
and
(\mathcal{I}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{\delta}
vanish, as
\delta\rightarrow 0
, and to prove asymptotic normality, we will show that
\delta^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{\delta}\rightarrow 0
, while
\delta^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{\delta}
converges in distribution to a Gaussian random variable.

To analyze these terms, we use the “splitting technique” of the solution (i.e., we write
X=\bar{X}+\widetilde{X}
as above) and we study separately the linear parts

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{\delta,x_{0}}(t):=\left\langle\bar{X}(t),K_{\delta,x_{0}}\right\rangle,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\bar{X}_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t):=\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\Delta K_{\delta,x_{0}}\right\rangle

and the corresponding nonlinear parts
\widetilde{X}_{\delta,x_{0}}(t)
,
\widetilde{X}_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)
. (Note that since the stochastic convolution
\bar{X}
is centered Gaussian process, the
\bar{X}_{\delta,x_{0}}
and
\bar{X}_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}
are also centered Gaussian processes.) We also define the observed Fisher information
\bar{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}
that corresponds to the linear part

\bar{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}:=\|K\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{-2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\bar{X}_{\delta,x_{0}}^{\Delta}(t)\right)^{2}\,dt.

For the analysis, we initially follow
[
2
]
, where the classical stochastic Burgers equation with spatially “smoothed” noise
(-\Delta)^{-\gamma}\,dW(t)
,
\gamma>1/4
is also discussed. (It is a borderline case of the presented more general nonlinear SPDE and as that, it is analyzed separately.) A lot of general and auxiliary results can be used also in our case
\gamma=0
, so first, let us revisit the assumptions of the model from
[
2
]
:

Assumption
B
:
Since we have
\gamma=0
,
B=I
,
B^{*}=I
,

Assumption

Since we have

.

Assumption

Since we have
\gamma=0
,
\lceil{\gamma}\rceil=0

Assumption

Since our nonlinearity

Assumption (L)

Remark 3.3

From the calculations in Lemma S.4 of the supplement of

Finally, when all convergences in (

Theorem 3.4
.

Grant Assumption
(L)
. Then the augmented maximum likelihood estimator

is strongly consistent and asymptotically normal estimator of the parameter

\delta^{-1}\left(\hat{\vartheta}_{\delta}-\vartheta\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N\left(0,\frac{2\vartheta\|K\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}}{T\|K^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}}\right),\quad\text{as $\delta\rightarrow 0$.}

(3.4)

The asymptotic normality of the estimator allows us to prescribe asymptotic confidence intervals for the parameter

.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let

. Based on the estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{\delta}

I_{1-\alpha}=\left[\hat{\vartheta}_{\delta}-\mathcal{I}_{\delta}^{-1/2}q_{1-\alpha/2},\hat{\vartheta}_{\delta}+\mathcal{I}_{\delta}^{-1/2}q_{1-\alpha/2}\right],

with the standard Gaussian

has asymptotic coverage

.

The next result is adapted from Theorem 6 in
[

]

]

\delta

Let
\Theta

\kappa=(\vartheta,a,F,X_{0})

), where we consider
\vartheta>0
,

, instead of

, the nonlinerarity

Denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{\kappa}

∎

4.5
Proofs of the main theorems

In this section we prove our main result, that is the central limit theorem (see Theorem

We consider the error decomposition (

\displaystyle\|K\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}X_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle X^{2}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}X_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle F(X(t)),K_{\delta}\right\rangle\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(U_{1,\delta}+U_{2,\delta}+U_{3,\delta})+U_{4,\delta},\quad\text{where}

\displaystyle U_{1,\delta}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{T}\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle\bar{X}^{2}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle\,dt,

\displaystyle U_{2,\delta}

\displaystyle\phantom{:=}+2\int_{0}^{T}\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle\bar{X}(t)(\widetilde{X}(t)-\widetilde{X}(t,x_{0})),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle\,dt=:V_{1,\delta}+V_{2,\delta}+V_{3,\delta},

\displaystyle U_{3,\delta}

\displaystyle:=2\int_{0}^{T}\widetilde{X}(t,x_{0})\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle\,dt,

Lemma 4.10
.

As

Proof.

We argue differently. The main point of the proof will be to represent

,

where

, with a
\sigma

Then we will show the desired convergence of

Step 1
. First, recall the formal representation of the cylindrical Wiener process

Therefore, we also have,
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s, for any

Y_{n}(t)=-\vartheta\lambda_{n}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\vartheta\lambda_{n}(t-s)}\,\beta_{n}(s)\,ds+\beta_{n}(t).

(4.12)

Step 2
. Let us fix
T>0
. Example 5.a in
[

(4.13)

,
n\geq 1
,

-a.s.,

\displaystyle Y_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-\vartheta\lambda_{n})\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\vartheta\lambda_{n}(t-s)}\,\Big(\int_{0}^{s}\varphi_{k}(u)\,du\Big)\,ds\int_{0}^{T}\varphi_{k}(u)\,d\beta_{n}(u)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{k}(u)\,du\cdot\int_{0}^{T}\varphi_{k}(u)\,d\beta_{n}(u).

Let us define

. Note that

. Let us define deterministic functions

,

,

,

.

Step 3
. We introduce the

,

,
n\geq 1
.

(4.15)

It turns out that by (

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
-measurable with values in

.

(4.16)

Step 4
. Using the approach of
[
14
]
as explained in the proof of Proposition

,

is
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
-measurable.

(4.17)

For instance, let us fix

\displaystyle Z_{k+1}(t)

. Arguing by induction, it is not difficult to prove that each

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}

Step 5
. Recall that Proposition
4.7

and therefore, for any

\widetilde{X}(t,x_{0})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}^{\prime},{\mathbb{P}}).

Recall the

is countably generated. This means that there exists a countable set

By Proposition 3.4.5 in

Hence there exists an orthonormal basis

(different from

) such that for any

(4.18)

Hence, for almost all

, we have the desired representation

\widetilde{X}(t,x_{0})=\sum_{i\geq 1}b_{i}(t)\nu_{i},

(4.19)

with

Step 6
. We finish the proof of the convergence in
L^{1}(\Omega)
of
\delta U_{3,\delta}
proceeding analogously as in the second part of the proof of Lemma S.8 in supplement of
[
2
]
. Consider the representation (
4.19
) and, for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, set

U_{3,\delta,N}:=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu_{i}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}b_{i}(t)\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle\,dt}_{=:s_{i,\delta}}=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu_{i}s_{i,\delta}.

Fix
\eta>0
and choose
N=N(\eta)\in{\mathbb{N}}
large enough such that

\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\widetilde{X}(t,x_{0})-\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}(t)\nu_{i}\Big)^{2}\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}b_{i}(t)\nu_{i}\Big)^{2}\,dt\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}b_{i}^{2}(t)\,dt<\eta.

We decompose
\delta{\mathbb{E}}|U_{3,\delta}|\leq\delta{\mathbb{E}}|U_{3,\delta}-U_{3,\delta,N}|+\delta{\mathbb{E}}|U_{3,\delta,N}|
and we handle both terms separately. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Gaussianity (recall that if
Z
is Gaussian, then
{\mathbb{E}}|Z|^{p}\lesssim\left({\mathbb{E}}Z^{2}\right)^{p/2}
), we obtain

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\left({\mathbb{E}}|U_{3,\delta}-U_{3,\delta,N}|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left|2\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}\nu_{i}b_{i}(t)\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle\,dt\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\delta^{2}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}\nu_{i}b_{i}(t)\right)^{2}\,dt\cdot\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle\right)^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{2}\eta\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\Delta K_{\delta}\right\rangle^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle=\delta^{-4}\eta\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle\bar{X}(t),(\Delta_{\delta}K)_{\delta}\right\rangle^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle\bar{X}(t),(\partial_{x}K)_{\delta}\right\rangle^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\eta\|(-\Delta_{\delta})^{1/2}K\|_{L^{2}(\Lambda_{\delta})}^{2}\|(-\Delta_{\delta})^{-1/2}\partial_{x}K\|_{L^{2}(\Lambda_{\delta})}^{2}\lesssim\eta,

using the lemmas of Section
A.1
for scaling and a uniform boundedness (in
\delta>0
) of the two terms on the last line. (Here we also use Assumption
(L)
.) As the result we obtain

\sup_{\delta\in(0,1]}\delta{\mathbb{E}}|U_{3,\delta}-U_{3,\delta,N}|\lesssim\eta^{1/2}.

(4.20)

Now we focus on
\delta{\mathbb{E}}|U_{3,\delta,N}|
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\delta{\mathbb{E}}|U_{3,\delta,N}|=2\delta{\mathbb{E}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu_{i}s_{i,\delta}\Big|\leq 2\delta\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}\nu_{i}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\cdot\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}s_{i,\delta}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

The first sum on the right-hand side in the above expression is of order

. Since
N
is fixed (because small
\eta
is fixed), this factor only contributes to a constant. Next, we will prove that

\delta^{2}{\mathbb{E}}s_{i,\delta}^{2}\rightarrow 0,\quad i\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(4.21)

We take advantage of the symmetry in

,

and we write

s_{i,\delta}^{2}=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(t)b_{i}(t^{\prime})\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t^{\prime})\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle\left\langle\bar{X}(t^{\prime}),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle\,dt^{\prime}\,dt.

To compute the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}s_{i,\delta}^{2}
, we use the Wick’s formula, noting that all four random terms
\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)
,
\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t^{\prime})
,
\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\bar{X}(t^{\prime}),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle
are Gaussian processes. If we denote the needed products of covariances of pairs in the altering partitions by

\displaystyle\rho_{1,\delta}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}[\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle]{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t^{\prime})\left\langle\bar{X}(t^{\prime}),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle],

\displaystyle\rho_{2,\delta}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}[\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t^{\prime})\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle]{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\left\langle\bar{X}(t^{\prime}),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle],

\displaystyle\rho_{3,\delta}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}[\left\langle\bar{X}(t),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle\left\langle\bar{X}(t^{\prime}),\partial_{x}K_{\delta}\right\rangle]{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t)\bar{X}_{\delta}^{\Delta}(t^{\prime})],

we arrive at

{\mathbb{E}}s_{i,\delta}^{2}=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(t)b_{i}(t^{\prime})(\rho_{1,\delta}(t,t^{\prime})+\rho_{2,\delta}(t,t^{\prime})+\rho_{3,\delta}(t,t^{\prime}))\,dt^{\prime}\,dt.

Now (
4.21
) follows from this and Lemma
A.11
(i)-(iii). Therefore, we deduce that
\delta{\mathbb{E}}|U_{3,\delta,N}|<\eta
for sufficiently small
\delta
(depending on
N
and thus on
\eta
). Together with (
4.20
) and since
\eta
was arbitrary, we obtain
\delta U_{3,\delta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}(\Omega)}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

We can compare our expansion (

Example A.13
.

Let
g:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a real function such that
g
is bounded and locally Lipschitz. Let us fix a function
h\in L^{\infty}(\Lambda)
. For
u\in H
, we define

(F(u))(x)=g\left(\|u\|\right)\cdot h(x),\quad x\in\Lambda,\,a.e.

It is not difficult to see that it satisfies our above assumptions. Since (a) and (c) are straightforward, we only check (b):

\displaystyle F(u+v)(x)\cdot v(x)

\displaystyle=g\left(\|u+v\|\right)\cdot h(x)\cdot v(x)\leq\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\Lambda)}\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\Lambda)}|v(x)|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+|u(x)|^{2}+|v(x)|^{2}\right),\quad u,v\in H,\,x\in\Lambda,\,a.e.

Similarly, one can consider examples like
(F(u))(x)=h(x)\int_{0}^{1}g(y)f(u(y))\,dy
,
x\in\Lambda,\,a.e.
, where
g,h\in L^{\infty}(\Lambda)
and
f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is Lipschitz and bounded.

Example A.14
.

This example concerns with Nemytskii type nonlinearities. Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a function such that
f\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
and the following conditions holds: There exists
C>0
,
q\geq 2
,
C_{q}>0
such that

(i)

|f(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{2}),\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

(ii)

|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq C(1+|x|),\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

(iii)

f(x+y)\,y\leq C_{q}(1+|y|^{2}+|x|^{q}),\,x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

We define
F:H\to L^{1}(\Lambda)
as follows:

(F(u))(x)=f\left(u(x)\right),\quad u\in H,\,x\in\Lambda,\,a.e.

Note that (iii) is stronger than the well-known one-side Lipschitz condition: There exists
C>0
such that

f(y)\,y\leq C(1+|y|^{2}),\quad y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(A.2)

This condition has been imposed in
[
19
]
to cover a large class of semilinear SPDEs. However such SPDEs do not contain the Burgers nonlinear term and do not treat nonlocal nonlinearities. Their approach uses comparison theorems for SPDEs.

We check that this nonlinearity
F
satisfies our initial assumptions.

Let us consider (a). By (i) we know that
F:H\rightarrow L^{1}(\Lambda)
is well-defined.

To show the local Lipschitz property of
F
, we use condition (ii). We fix
R>0
and the ball
B(0,R)
in
H
. Using the mean value theorem for
u,v\in B(0,R)
, we have

\displaystyle\|F(v)-F(u)\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\left|f(v(x))-f(u(x))\right|\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\left|\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(u(x)+s(v(x)-u(x)))(u(x)-v(x))\,ds\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Lambda}\int_{0}^{1}(1+|u(x)+s(v(x)-u(x))|)|u(x)-v(x)|\,ds\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Lambda}(1+2|u(x)|+|v(x)|)|u(x)-v(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{R}\left(\int_{\Lambda}|u(x)-v(x)|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2},

where we used that
u,v\in B(0,R)
on the last line. This gives (a). The point (b) follows easily from (iii).

Finally note that it is easy to obtain (c) by using only condition (i). Indeed if
K=D(0,M)
is a ball in
L^{\infty}(\Lambda)
, then

\|F(u)\|_{L^{p}(\Lambda)}^{p}=\int_{\Lambda}|f(u(x))|^{p}\,dx\leq C^{\prime}\int_{\Lambda}\left(1+|u(x)|^{2p}\right)\,dx\leq C^{\prime}(1+M^{2p}),

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
. It follows that
F(K)
is a bounded set in
L^{p}(\Lambda)
and this gives the assertion.

We have checked that (i), (ii) and (iii) imply our initial assumptions (a), (b) and (c).

It is useful to introduce some simple conditions on
f\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
which imply (i), (ii) and (iii). In the next classes of examples we always assume (i) and (ii). On the other hand, we will consider different conditions which imply (iii).

(I)

f
verifies (ii) and has at most a linear growth, i.e., there exists
C>0
such that

|f(x)|\leq C(1+|x|),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Clearly this condition implies (i). It is also not difficult to show that this condition implies (iii).

(II)

f
verifies (i) and (ii) and moreover there exists
M\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

f^{\prime}(x)\leq M,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(A.3)

In order to check (iii), we write

f(x+y)\,y=\left(f(x+y)-f(x)\right)y+f(x)\,y.

Now by (i) we have
|f(x)y|\leq C(1+|x|^{2})|y|\leq C^{\prime}(1+|y|^{2}+|x|^{4})
for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
and all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
. By the mean value theorem we get

\left(f(x+y)-f(x)\right)y\leq My^{2},

which shows (iii). An example of
f
in this class is

f(x)=-Cx|x|+Mx,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad C>0.

Note that the function
g(x)=-x|x|
is decreasing and has at most quadratic growth. Moreover its derivative has at most linear growth.

(III)

Consider
f\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
which satisfies (ii) and such that there exists
\eta\in(0,1)
and
C_{\eta}>0
with

|f(x)|\leq C_{\eta}(1+|x|^{1+\eta}),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(A.4)

(Clearly this condition is stronger than (i)). In addition we assume that
f
verifies the one-sided Lipschitz condition (
A.2
).

We prove under these conditions that hypothesis (iii) holds for
f
. We write

\displaystyle f(x+y)\,y

\displaystyle=f(x+y)\,(x+y)-f(x+y)\,x\leq C(1+(x+y)^{2})+C_{\eta}|x|(1+|x+y|^{1+\eta})

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}(1+|y|^{2}+|x|^{2})+C^{\prime}|x|^{2+\eta}+C^{\prime}|x|\,|y|^{1+\eta},

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
. By the Young inequality:
|x|\,|y|^{1+\eta}\leq\frac{|y|^{2}}{p}+\frac{|x|^{q}}{q}
with
p=\frac{2}{1+\eta}
and
q=\frac{p}{p-1}
. Hence (iii) holds.

For instance we can fix
\eta\in(0,1)
and consider any bounded
C^{1}
-function
g
which is Lipschitz and nonnegative. Then

f(x)=-x|x|^{\eta}\,g(|x|^{1-\eta}),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}},

verifies our conditions.

It is clear that (ii), (
A.4
) and (
A.2
) hold. Note that in the previous example we can also consider
\eta=0
and
\eta=1
(to treat these cases we use (I) and (II)).

A.3
The notion of weak solution

Proof of Lemma
2.8
.

For the nonlinear part
\widetilde{X}
we have by (
2.5
),
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s.,

\widetilde{X}(t)=S_{\vartheta}(t)X_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)\partial_{x}\left((\bar{X}(s)+\widetilde{X}(s))^{2}\right)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)F\left(\bar{X}(s)+\widetilde{X}(s)\right)\,ds.

(A.5)

Now we work with
\omega
fixed (i.e., we fix
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
for some event
\Omega^{\prime}
which verifies
{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega^{\prime})=1
). We first prove that

\left\langle z,\widetilde{X}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle z,X_{0}\right\rangle+\vartheta\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\Delta z,\widetilde{X}(s)\right\rangle\,ds-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\partial_{x}z,(\bar{X}(s)+\widetilde{X}(s))^{2}\right\rangle\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle z,F\left(\bar{X}(s)+\widetilde{X}(s)\right)\right\rangle\,ds

for any
t\in[0,T]
,
z\in H^{2}
(this is so-called weak formulation of (
A.5
)). To this purpose we argue as in
[
5
]
, see also Proposition A.6 in
[
16
]
.

Let us consider
q\geq 2
from our hypothesis (
F
). Let us choose
s\in[0,1/2)
such that
H^{s}
embedds into
L^{q}(\Lambda)
. We know that,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
\bar{X}(\cdot,\omega)\in C([0,T];H^{s})
by Proposition
4.2
. Setting

y(t):=\bar{X}(t,\omega),\quad t\in[0,T],

we choose
(\varphi_{n})\subset C([0,T];H^{1})
such that
\varphi_{n}(t)\rightarrow y(t)
in
H^{s}
, uniformly in
t\in[0,T]
(see, for instance, Corollary 0.1.3 in
[
37
]
).

Denote
B_{s}(0,N)
the closed ball in the space
H^{s}
with radius
N
, that is
B_{s}(0,N)=\{u\in H^{s};\|u\|_{s}\leq N\}
, and note that such ball is included in a ball of the space
L^{q}(\Lambda)
.

Note that there exists
N=N(\omega)>0
such that
y(t)\in B_{s}(0,N)
for any
t\in[0,T]
. Therefore, there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(\omega)>0
such that for any
n\geq n_{0}
:
\varphi_{n}(t)\in B_{s}(0,N+1)
, for any
t\in[0,T]
.

Then define

\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)=S_{\vartheta}(t)X_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)\partial_{x}\left((\varphi_{n}(s)+\widetilde{X}_{n}(s))^{2}\right)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)F\left(\varphi_{n}(s)+\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)\right)\,ds

and note that
\partial_{x}\left((\varphi_{n}(\cdot)+\widetilde{X}_{n}(\cdot))^{2}\right)\in C([0,T];H)
(see also Proposition
2.7
). Hence we have

\displaystyle\left\langle z,\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle z,X_{0}\right\rangle+\vartheta\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\Delta z,\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)\right\rangle\,ds-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\partial_{x}z,(\varphi_{n}(s)+\widetilde{X}_{n}(s))^{2}\right\rangle\,ds

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle z,F\left(\varphi_{n}(s)+\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)\right)\right\rangle\,ds.

(A.6)

Using the assumptions on the nonlinearity
F
and
n\geq n_{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}F\left(\varphi_{n}(s,\cdot)+\widetilde{X}_{n}(s,\cdot)\right)(x)\,\widetilde{X}_{n}(s,x)\,dx\leq C_{q}\int_{0}^{1}\left(1+|\varphi_{n}(s,x)|^{q}+|\widetilde{X}_{n}(s,x)|^{2}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{q}\left(1+\|\varphi_{n}(s)\|_{L^{q}(\Lambda)}^{q}+\|\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)\|^{2}\right)\leq C_{q}\left(1+(N+1)^{q}+\|\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{q,N+1}\left(1+\|\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)\|^{2}\right),

(A.7)

for some constant
C_{q,N+1}>0
.

Following the proof of Theorem 14.2.4 in
[
15
]
with the additional term handled as in (
A.7
), we use the Gronwall lemma to obtain an upper bound for
\|\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)\|^{2}
(analogous to (14.2.12) in
[
15
]
). We get

\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)\|=C_{0}<\infty,

(A.8)

where
C_{0}
depends on
\omega,T
,
\|X_{0}\|
,
N
and
\sup_{n\geq 1}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{C([0,T];H^{s})}
which is finite. Note that

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)\partial_{x}\left((\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)+\varphi_{n}(s))^{2}-(\widetilde{X}(s)+y(s))^{2}\right)\,ds

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)\left(F\left(\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)+\varphi_{n}(s)\right)-F\left(\widetilde{X}(s)+y(s)\right)\right)\,ds.

(A.9)

We first handle the
L^{2}(\Lambda)
-norm of the first term on the right-hand side of (
A.9
). Arguing as in the proof of Lemma 14.2.1 in
[
15
]
(see also page 393 in
[
13
]
) we obtain, for
0<s\leq t
,
t\in(0,T]
,

\left\|S_{\vartheta}(t-s)\partial_{x}\left((\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)+\varphi_{n}(s))^{2}-(\widetilde{X}(s)+y(s))^{2}\right)\right\|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{(t-s)^{3/4}}\left\|(\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)+\varphi_{n}(s))^{2}-(\widetilde{X}(s)+y(s))^{2}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda)}

for some constant
C_{1}>0
. We are using that

\|S_{\vartheta}(t)\partial_{x}\phi\|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{t^{3/4}}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda)},\quad\phi\in H^{1},\quad t\in(0,T].

Now it is not difficult to see that, using also the bound (
A.8
),

\left\|(\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)+\varphi_{n}(s))^{2}-(\widetilde{X}(s)+y(s))^{2}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda)}\leq C_{2}\|\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)-\widetilde{X}(s)\|+C_{3}\|\varphi_{n}-y\|_{C([0,T];H)},

where constants
C_{2},C_{3}>0
are independent of
n
(they depend also on
\omega
and
T
). Recall that
\bar{X}(t,\omega)=y(t)
.

Now we handle the
L^{2}(\Lambda)
-norm of the second term on the right-hand side of (
A.9
) using the local Lipschitz property of
F
. In order to do that, consider
n\geq n_{0}
and note that
\varphi_{n}(s)+\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)\in B(0,C_{0}+N+1)
,
y(s)\in B(0,N)
(we are using closed balls in
H
) and
\widetilde{X}(s)\in B(0,K)
for some
K>0
, since
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
is fixed and we know that
\widetilde{X}\in C([0,T];C(\bar{\Lambda}))
,
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s., by Proposition
2.7
.

\displaystyle\left\|S_{\vartheta}(t-s)\left(F\left(\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)+\varphi_{n}(s)\right)-F\left(\widetilde{X}(s)+y(s)\right)\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{(t-s)^{1/4}}\left\|F\left(\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)+\varphi_{n}(s)\right)-F\left(\widetilde{X}(s)+y(s)\right)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Lambda)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{C_{0}+N+1+N+K}}{(t-s)^{1/4}}\left\|\left(\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)+\varphi_{n}(s)\right)-\left(\widetilde{X}(s)+y(s)\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{4}}{(t-s)^{1/4}}\|(\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)-\widetilde{X}(s)\|+\frac{C_{5}}{(t-s)^{1/4}}\|\varphi_{n}-y\|_{C([0,T];H)},

for some constants
C_{4},C_{5}>0
independent of
n
(they depend also on
\omega
and
T
).

Using the above upper bounds, we receive

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{6}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{(t-s)^{3/4}}+\frac{1}{(t-s)^{1/4}}\right)\|(\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)-\widetilde{X}(s)\|\,ds+C_{7}\|\varphi_{n}-y\|_{C([0,T];H)},

\displaystyle\leq C_{8}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{(t-s)^{3/4}}\|(\widetilde{X}_{n}(s)-\widetilde{X}(s)\|\,ds+C_{7}\|\varphi_{n}-y\|_{C([0,T];H)},

C_{6},C_{7},C_{8}>0

n
(they depend also on
\omega
and
T
). Applying the Henry-Gronwall lemma (cf. Section 1.2.1 in

]
), we find, for any

\|\widetilde{X}_{n}(t)-\widetilde{X}(t)\|\leq C_{7}\,M\|\varphi_{n}-y\|_{C([0,T];H)},

where

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle z,F\left(\bar{X}(s)+\widetilde{X}(s)\right)\right\rangle\,ds.

On the other hand, we have,

A.4
Rate optimality

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

It is enough to find a lower bound for

\inf_{\hat{\vartheta}}\sup_{\kappa\in\{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\}}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\kappa}\left(\hat{\vartheta}-\vartheta\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

(A.12)

for two suitable admissible alternatives
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\Theta
. We fix
\vartheta_{0}>0
and consider for some
c>0
the alternatives
\kappa_{1}=(\vartheta_{0},0,0,X_{0}^{\vartheta_{0}})
and
\kappa_{2}=(\vartheta_{0}+c\delta,0,0,X_{0}^{\vartheta_{0}+c\delta})
, where both nonlinearities are nullified and the initial conditions are taken random as
X_{0}^{\vartheta}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}S_{\vartheta}(-s)\,dW(s)
with
\vartheta\in\{\vartheta_{0},\vartheta_{0}+c\delta\}
and the process
(W(s),s\in{\mathbb{R}})
that is now a two-sided cylindrical Wiener process.

It is not difficult to prove that the random variable

verifies the assumptions corresponding to (
2.2
). We only note that to prove that, for
\omega\in\Omega
,
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s.,

one can proceed with the same method of the proof of Lemma 5.1 in
[
16
]
which is based on the Kolmogorov-Chentsov test (see also Theorem 5.22 in
[
16
]
).

This choice leads us to a linear submodel of our model (
1.1
) that we consider from now on. That is

\begin{cases}dX(t)&=\vartheta\partial_{xx}^{2}X(t)\,dt+\,dW(t),\quad X(0)=X_{0}^{\vartheta},\\

(A.13)

with

X(t)=S_{\vartheta}(t)X_{0}^{\vartheta}+\int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)\,dW(s)=:\widetilde{X}(t)+\bar{X}(t).

(A.14)

A.5
The algebra property of Sobolev spaces

This section is used in the proof of Proposition
2.7
. It is well known that the space
H^{s}
is an algebra with respect to pointwise multiplication for
s>1/2
(in our case
d=1
). For bounded functions it holds true for any
s\geq 0
. We believe the next result is known, but we have not found a precise reference. We provide a proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition A.15
.

For any
s\in[0,1]
, we have

\|uv\|_{s}\leq\|u\|_{\infty}\|v\|_{s}+\|v\|_{\infty}\|u\|_{s},

(A.15)

for any
u,v\in H^{s}\cap L^{\infty}(\Lambda)
.

Proof.

We will use the theory of Dirichlet forms, see, e.g.,
[
17
]
.

Consider the negative definite, self-adjoint linear operator
A=\Delta
with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\Lambda
. It is well known that the semigroup
e^{tA}=T_{t}
is symmetric and Markovian on
L^{2}(X,m)=L^{2}(\Lambda)
(according to the definition used in Theorem 1.4.1 of
[
17
]
or the one in
[
25
]
). We will consider the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}
associated to
T_{t}
. By Theorem 1.3.1 in
[
17
]
, there is a biunivocal correspondence:
\mathcal{E}\leftrightarrow e^{tA}\leftrightarrow A
. Moreover,

\Dom(\mathcal{E})=\Dom(\sqrt{-A}),\quad\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\left\langle\sqrt{-A}u,\sqrt{-A}v\right\rangle.

Recall Theorem 1.4.2 in
[
17
]
: For any
u,v\in\Dom(\mathcal{E})\cap L^{\infty}(X,m)
(in our case
L^{\infty}(X,m)=L^{\infty}(\Lambda)
), we have

\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(uv,uv)}\leq\|u\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(v,v)}+\|v\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(u,u)}.

By Remark 1 on page 417 in
[
25
]
, we know that for any
0<s\leq 1
, the operator
-(-A)^{s}
is also the generator of a symmetric Markovian semigroup
T_{t}^{s}
on
L^{2}(\Lambda)
obtained by subordination of order
s
of
T_{t}
. (For more on subordination like
T_{t}^{f}=\int_{0}^{\infty}T_{s}\,\mu_{t}^{f}(ds)
, see for instance
[
4
]
.)

The Dirichlet form associated to
T_{t}^{s}
is
\mathcal{E}_{s}
with
\Dom(\mathcal{E}_{s})=\Dom\left((-A)^{s/2}\right)
. We will apply Theorem 1.4.2 of
[
17
]
to the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}_{s}
. We find

\sqrt{\left\langle(-A)^{s/2}(uv),(-A)^{s/2}(uv)\right\rangle}\leq\|u\|_{\infty}\|(-A)^{s/2}v\|+\|v\|_{\infty}\|(-A)^{s/2}u\|,

for any
u,v\in\Dom(\mathcal{E}_{s})\cap L^{\infty}(X,m)=H^{s}\cap L^{\infty}(\Lambda)
for
s\in(0,1]
. Since the inequality (
A.15
) holds elementarily for
s=0
, the proof is complete.
∎
