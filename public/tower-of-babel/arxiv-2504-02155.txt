The global attractor of the inelastic linear Boltzmann equation in a gravity field for Maxwell molecules
Théophile Dolmaire, Nicola Miele, Alessia Nota

Abstract
In this article we consider the linear inelastic Boltzmann equation in presence of a uniform and fixed gravity field, in the case of Maxwell molecules. We first obtain a well-posedness result in the space of finite, non-negative Radon measures. In addition, we rigorously prove the existence of a stationary solution under the non-equilibrium condition which is induced by the presence of the external field. We further show that this stationary solution is unique in the class of the finite, non-negative Radon measures with finite first order moment, and that all the solutions in this class converge towards the stationary solution in the weak topology of the measures.



Keywords:
Linear Boltzmann Equation;
Lorentz Gas;
Inelastic collisions;
Granular Media;
Acceleration Field;
Out-of-equilibrium Steady States;
Stationary Solutions;
Global Attractor

Introduction

1.1
The model

In the present article, we will consider a version of the inelastic Lorentz gas of Maxwell molecules evolving under the action of a uniform and fixed acceleration field, in dimension
d=2
or
d=3
. Considering a tagged particle of such a gas, that interacts only with a set of fixed and randomly distributed scatterers, the evolution of the tagged particle is described by the linear Boltzmann equation, with a collision kernel of the form:

\displaystyle B\left(\left|N\cdot\omega\right|,|v|\right)=b\left(\left|N\cdot\omega\right|\right)\hskip 8.53581pt\text{with}\hskip 8.53581pt\quad N:=\frac{v}{|v|}.

(1.1)

(
1.1
) is the Maxwell molecules collision kernel. The presence of the uniform acceleration field is expressed by the presence of a drift term in the equation. We will consider the space homogeneous situation, that is, the case when the distribution function
f
of the tagged particle is independent from the position variable, and depends only on the time variable
t
, and the velocity
v
of the tagged particle. We will therefore consider the equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(t,v)+a\cdot\partial_{v}f(t,v)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{r}b(|\hskip 1.42262pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptN\cdot\omega|)f(t,^{\prime}\hskip-2.84526ptv)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega-\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b(|N\cdot\omega|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\right)f(t,v),

where we used the notation
\hskip 1.42262pt{}^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptN=\hskip 0.0pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv/|^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv|
,

{}^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv

\displaystyle v=\hskip 0.0pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv-(1+r)(^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv\cdot\omega)\omega\hskip 8.53581pt\text{or equivalently}\hskip 8.53581pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv=v-(1+1/r)(v\cdot\omega)\omega,

(1.3)

where

is the

, which is assumed through all the paper to be a fixed real number (the case

We will also consider the following generalization of (

rehomogeneized collision operator
. Notice that this procedure will lead to a nonlinear version of the collision operator, satisfying however a physically relevant decay of the temperature. Eventually, the collision operator will be:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(t,v)

\displaystyle+a\cdot\partial_{v}f(t,v)

\displaystyle=T^{\mu}(t)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{r}b(|\hskip 0.7113pt^{\prime}N\hskip-1.42262pt\cdot\omega|)f(t,^{\prime}\hskip-2.84526ptv)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega-T^{\mu}(t)\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b(|N\cdot\omega|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\right)f(t,v)

We discuss the well-posedness theory and the long time behaviour of (

\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Finally, we provide a complete study of the system of the moments of zeroth, first and second order of (

1.2
Lorentz gas and granular material: a brief review of the literature

Existing results concerning conservative Lorentz gases.

Introduced by H. A. Lorentz in 1905, the Lorentz gas model has been originally proposed to analyze electron transport in metals. It describes the dynamics of non-interacting particles moving through a fixed random configuration of heavy scatterers. The interaction between the Lorentz particle and the scatterers is characterized by a finite-range central potential. Despite its simplicity, this model is profoundly important, since it is one of the few sources of exact results in kinetic theory, showcasing how microscopic reversibility can be reconciled with macroscopic irreversibility. Specifically, it allows for the demonstration of the validity of linear kinetic equations and, subsequently, diffusion equations under appropriate scaling limits. The first rigorous proof of the linear Boltzmann equation’s validity was established in
[
37
]
within the Boltzmann–Grad limit. For further related findings and developments, we refer to references
[
4
,
19
,
49
,
61
,
62
,
72
,
73
]
.
The rigorous derivation of hydrodynamic equations, specifically the heat equation, from the random Lorentz gas remains a challenging and unresolved problem. However, it is noteworthy that the rigorous validity of the diffusive limit has been established by Bunimovich and Sinai (see
[
24
]
) for periodically distributed scatterers. In the random case, the diffusion equation can be derived by relying on the kinetic approximation of the microscopic dynamics. Specifically, the heat equation can be obtained from the random Lorentz gas employing the kinetic equation that emerges in an appropriate kinetic limit as a bridge. This approach has been used in
[
18
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
, and also in
[
34
]
. Additionally, we refer to
[
49
]
for an alternative approach in this direction.

In most of the mathematical studies of the linear Boltzmann equation, it has been assumed that additional transport terms describing the effect of possible external fields are absent. Notably, the presence of an external field significantly influences the derivation of the linear Boltzmann equation in low-density regimes, as well as the properties of its solutions.
More precisely, in
[
13
]
and later in
[
14
]
(see also
[
44
]
where the model has been studied numerically), it has been shown that the motion of a test particle in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a Poisson distribution of hard disk scatterers and a uniform, constant magnetic field perpendicular to the plane formally leads to the generalized Boltzmann equation with
memory terms. A rigorous derivation of this linear Boltzmann equation has been recently obtained in
[
63
]
. We also refer to
[
50
]
,
where linear kinetic equations with a magnetic transport term have been derived, but the non-Markovian behavior of the limit process disappears. On the other hand we also mention
[
20
]
where the well-posedness of a slightly different linear Boltzmann equation with external field, specifically a space periodic electric field, has been studied in the time-stationary regime.

It is important to note that all the results mentioned above pertain to systems where the dynamics of the Lorentz particle is conservative, implying that each collision with the background obstacles is perfectly elastic. In contrast, issues concerning the well-posedness of solutions to linear kinetic equations in the presence of external fields, as well as the long-term behavior and thermalization of solutions, have been significantly less explored in non-conservative systems.

The inelastic collision model.

Turning to the case of dissipative gases, we first mention that considering inelastic collisions between the elementary components of a fluid is motivated by many physical situation: snow, sand or interstellar dust may be modelled by a very large number of particles that dissipate kinetic energy along collisions. Such a model has countless applications, in theoretical physics as well as in industry (see in particular
[
42
]
).

The question of modelling inelastic collisions is delicate. It may be performed by studying the deformation of colliding objects and the contact force that is generated by such a deformation, as it was first done by Hertz in the elastic case
[
40
]
, and later adaptated by Brilliantov et al.
[
22
]
in the inelastic case. Determining the contact force enables then to deduce the post-collisional relative velocity (right when the particles cease to be in contact) from the pre-collisional relative velocity (at the time the particles enter in contact). Since the time during which the particles are in contact and deformed is typically much smaller than the time scale used to describe the motion of the whole system, it is therefore usual in kinetic theory to consider that collisions take place instantaneously.

One major simplification of the previous description consists in considering interactions without friction, so that only the normal component
v\cdot\omega
(that is, along the line of contact between the particles, orientated by
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
) of the relative velocity
v
is modified during the collisions, so that the tangential component remains unchanged. As a consequence, to completely determine the law of collision in the frictionless case, it is enough to prescribe a single real constant, the so-called
restitution coefficient
(denoted by
r
), defined as the absolute value of the ratio between the normal component
v^{\prime}\cdot\omega
of the post-collisional relative velocity
v^{\prime}
and the normal component
v\cdot\omega
of the pre-collisional relative velocity
v
:

\displaystyle r=\left|\frac{v^{\prime}\cdot\omega}{v\cdot\omega}\right|\cdotp

(1.6)

In the physics literature, based on experiments (see
[
23
]
and the references therein) as well as on theoretical considerations (
[
69
]
,
[
67
]
,
[
23
]
), it is well-known that
r
depends on the norm of the relative velocity of the colliding particles, and tends to
1
when the relative velocity becomes small (
r=1
corresponds to an elastic collision). Particle systems with variable restitution coefficients are called
viscoelastic particle
systems. Nevertheless, assuming that the restitution coefficient
r\in[0,1]
is a fixed constant yields a much simpler model, which is still a good approximation of real particles (see the discussion in
[
23
]
).

We finally mention that more accurate descriptions taking into account friction exist, relying on another restitution coefficient used to model the change of the tangential component of the relative velocity during the collisions. The reader may refer to
[
6
]
,
[
70
]
, and again to
[
23
]
and the references therein.
In the present article, we will focus on the frictionless model, and although we will briefly discuss the literature including also the case of viscoelastic particles (variable restitution coefficient) in the introductory Section
1.3
, we will consider only with fixed (normal) restitution coefficient in our investigations.

Qualitative behaviour of granular gases.

Fluids or solids composed with a large number of particles that interact inelastically are called
granular media
. Such systems present a wide range of very interesting behaviours, and exhibit differences of fundamental nature with respect to conservative fluids, the evolution of which is governed by the classical, elastic Boltzmann equation. For a brief introduction to the peculiarities of the granular media, the reader may consult the surveys
[
42
]
and
[
43
]
. Let us present two of such specificities, which are maybe the most prominent.

Within granular media, the dissipation of kinetic energy along collisions induces a decay of the temperature in the case when there is no external source of energy injected in the system. In many cases, it is possible to prove that the temperature decays according to a power law: this is the celebrated Haff’s law
[
39
]
. Observe that the algebraic exponent of such a decay depends on the properties of the restitution coefficient (see
[
23
]
).

A second characteristic of granular media is the trend to create spontaneously stable clusters (or regular patterns in some particular cases), even when the gas departs from an initial state very close to be spatially homogeneous. This behaviour is widely documented (
[
38
]
,
[
43
]
,
[
55
]
,
[
23
]
), but still not completely understood.

In addition, in the case of a fixed restitution coefficient that is small enough, inelastic particle systems exhibit
inelastic collapse
: infinitely many collisions take place in finite time. At the level of the kinetic equation, this phenomenon can be identified by the convergence in finite time of the solutions towards Dirac masses, which represents a brutal concentration of the velocity profile of the distribution function. A vast literature exists about this phenomenon: see
[
54
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
66
]
,
[
30
]
and the references therein, and
[
31
]
for the
d
-dimensional case with
d\geq 2
. Concerning the one-dimensional case (which is not only a toy model but turns out to be of fundamental importance according to the observations of
[
54
]
, as well as a challenging mathematical problem), the reader may refer to
[
10
]
, and the more recent
[
32
]
and the references therein.

On the one hand, the inelastic collapse phenomenon can be seen as an extreme case of clusterization. Such a phenomenon constitutes a major obstruction to perform numerical simulations, and to provide a rigorous derivation of inelastic kinetic equations (no analog of Lanford’s theorem
[
45
]
exists for such models). On the other hand, it is still unclear if such a phenomenon exists for models different from the particular, not completely physical case of fixed restitution coefficient, nor if the dynamics of the particle system is globally well-posed, even when a collapse occurs.

1.3
Mathematical theory of the inelastic Boltzmann equation

In this section, we briefly review the state of the art concerning the inelastic Boltzmann equation. In order to insert the results of the present article in the broad context of the inelastic Boltzmann equation(s), we will consider the non-linear case in a first time, corresponding to a gas of particles that interact with each other. In a second time, we will turn to the linear case, corresponding to particle systems that do not interact with each other, but only with a background, that is either composed with another gas at thermal equilibrium (Rayleigh gas) or with fixed obstacle (Lorentz gas).

In order to establish inelastic versions of the Boltzmann equation, the starting point is the weak formulation (for instance, see
[
25
]
). The weak formulation is a consequence of the formal derivation of the Boltzmann equation, which is performed via a careful counting of the different ways to modify the number of particles of the system with a velocity that belongs to the infinitesimal volume
v+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v
of the phase space. In the linear case, which is the main focus of the present article, the equation reads (for a general collision kernel):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)f(t,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}B\left(|N\cdot\omega|,|v|\right)f(t,v)\left[\varphi(v^{\prime})-\varphi(v)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,

(1.7)

for any test function
\varphi
, and where
v^{\prime}
is the post-collisional velocity given by the expression:

\displaystyle v^{\prime}=v-\left(1+r\right)(v\cdot\omega)\omega.

(1.8)

We denote by
c
the scattering mapping (
1.8
) that to
v
associates
v^{\prime}
, and by
J_{c}
its Jacobian determinant:

\displaystyle J_{c}=J(v^{\prime})=J(c(v)).

(1.9)

In the case of a fixed restitution coefficient
r
we have:

\displaystyle\left|J_{c}(v)\right|=r.

(1.10)

The strong form of the inelastic, spatially homogeneous, linear Boltzmann equation with a general collision kernel
B
, is the following:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(t,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}B\left(|\hskip 1.42262pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptN\cdot\omega|,|v|\right)\frac{1}{|Jc(^{\prime}v)|}f(^{\prime}v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega-f(v)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}B(|N\cdot\omega|,|v|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega,

(1.11)

where
{}^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv
is the
pre-collisional velocity
obtained with inelastic collisions:

{}^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv=v-\left(1+1/r\right)(v\cdot\omega)\omega.

(1.12)

Contrary to the case of the elastic Boltzmann equation, a non-trivial Jacobian
J_{c}
appears in the gain term, when the collision operator is written in the strong form. This Jacobian corresponds to the change of variables from post- to pre-collisional velocities. In the elastic case we have
|J_{c}|=1
.

The non-linear inelastic Boltzmann equation.

In the non-linear case, the strong form of the inelastic Boltzmann equation reads in general:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)+v\cdot\partial_{x}f(t,x,v)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{B(|\hskip 0.7113pt^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv-\hskip 0.7113pt^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv_{*}|,\left|\hskip 1.42262pt{}^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptN\cdot\omega\right|)}{|Jc(^{\prime}v,^{\prime}\hskip-2.84526ptv_{*})|}f(t,x,^{\prime}\hskip-2.84526ptv)f(t,x,^{\prime}\hskip-2.84526ptv_{*})

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-B(|v-v_{*}|,\left|N\cdot\omega\right|)f(t,x,v)f(t,x,v_{*})\Bigg]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega,

(1.13)

where
\hskip 0.7113pt{}^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv
,
\hskip 0.7113pt{}^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv_{*}
denote the pre-collisional velocities, defined as:

\hskip 0.7113pt{}^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv=v-\frac{(1+1/r)}{2}(v-v_{*})\cdot\omega\omega,\hskip 14.22636pt\hskip 0.7113pt^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv_{*}=v_{*}+\frac{(1+1/r)}{2}(v-v_{*})\cdot\omega\omega,

(1.14)

or equivalently,
(v,v_{*})=c(\hskip 0.7113pt^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv,\hskip 0.7113pt^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv_{*})
, with
c(v,v_{*})=\left(c_{1}(v,v_{*}),c_{2}(v,v_{*})\right)
, and

\displaystyle c_{1}(v,v_{*})=v-\frac{(1+r)}{2}(v-v_{*})\cdot\omega\omega,\hskip 14.22636ptc_{2}(v,v_{*})=v_{*}+\frac{(1+r)}{2}(v-v_{*})\cdot\omega\omega,

(1.15)

with
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
the angular parameter (the orientation of the line of contact of two colliding particles in the case of hard spheres),
N
,
\hskip 1.42262pt{}^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptN
denoting:

\displaystyle N=\frac{v-v_{*}}{|v-v_{*}|},\hskip 14.22636pt\hskip 1.42262pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptN=\frac{\hskip 0.7113pt{}^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv-\hskip 0.7113pt^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv_{*}}{|\hskip 0.7113pt^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv-\hskip 0.7113pt^{\prime}\hskip-0.7113ptv_{*}|},

(1.16)

and
Jc
being the Jacobian of the mapping
(v,v_{*})\mapsto c(v,v_{*})
(considered at
\omega

Going back to the weak form, we recover directly the conservation of the total mass
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,x,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
and the momentum
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}vf(t,x,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
.

We refer to
[
76
]
for a very complete survey about the mathematical state of the art concerning (
1.3
), and to the more recent
[
27
]
, which addresses also the question of the numerical simulations related to (
1.3
).

Contrary to the elastic Boltzmann equation, the dissipative character of the collisions causes the solutions of (
1.3
) to develop singularities (concentrations of the velocity profile, explosion of the gradient
\partial_{x}f
…), and the trend to create clusters prevents to consider linearization around an homogeneous solution. For these reasons, the mathematical theory of (
1.3
) is still quite incomplete, and many results are obtained only in particular cases, or for related, simpler models.

Rigorous results were first obtained in the one-dimensional case (observe that, contrary to the elastic Boltzmann equation, the one-dimensional case yields a non-trivial collision operator), relying on the so-called quasi-elastic limit: a Boltzmann-like equation is derived using the BBGKY hierarchy for a system of
N
inelastic hard spheres (
[
7
]
, see also
[
53
]
and
[
74
]
for other derivations), assuming that the restitution coefficient
r
is a function
r(N)
of the number of particles
N
, such that
r(N)\rightarrow 1
, as
N\rightarrow+\infty
, which prevents in particular the inelastic collapse to take place (
[
10
]
). The kinetic equation that is obtained is called the non-linear friction equation (
[
74
]
), and can be written as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)+v\partial_{x}f(t,x,v)=-\partial_{v}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}(v_{*}-v)|v_{*}-v|f(t,x,v_{*})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}\right].

(1.17)

In the space-homogeneous case, the solutions can be studied completely (
[
7
]
): the equation is globally well-posed for any initial datum that is a Borel probability measure with finite moment of order
2
and finite entropy, and if the initial datum admits a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
density (with respect to the Lebesgue measure), then so does the solution. In addition, the asymptotic behaviour of the solutions can be precisely described: all the solutions converge towards a Dirac mass, and up to consider solutions obtained from
L^{1}
initial data, after a proper rescaling it is possible to show that the rescaled solutions converge towards the sum of Dirac masses
\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1/2}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1/2}
. Note that this last result reminds the spontaneous onset of shear flows in granular media observed in
[
55
]
when the restitution coefficient is chosen in between the elastic kinetic regime, and the typically inelastic regime of clusterization.

Later, Toscani
[
74
]
studied the one-dimensional inelastic Boltzmann equation with the hard sphere collision kernel, with general restitution coefficients:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\partial_{x}f=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\beta(\theta)\left[\frac{|\hskip 1.42262pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv-\hskip 0.28453pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv_{*}|}{J_{c}}f(t,x,^{\prime}\hskip-2.13394ptv)f(t,x,^{\prime}\hskip-2.13394ptv_{*})-|v-v_{*}|f(v)f(v_{*})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*},

(1.18)

where the restitution coefficient
r
, involved in the scattering formulas (
1.14
) and (
1.15
), is given by:

\displaystyle r=r(\theta,|v-v_{*}|)=\frac{1}{1+\theta|v-v_{*}|^{\gamma}},

(1.19)

with
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
a fixed constant and
\theta\geq 0
. This model covers therefore a particular case of viscoelastic particles.
\theta
measures the degree of inelasticity (
\theta=0
corresponds to an elastic collision, while
\theta=+\infty
corresponds to a perfectly inelastic collision), and
\beta(\theta)
is the probability that a collision with degree of inelasticity
\theta

\gamma=0
and
\beta=\delta_{(1-r_{0})/r_{0}}

, equal to

, so that (
1.18

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\partial_{x}f=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|v-v_{*}|\left[\frac{1}{r^{2}}f(t,x,^{\prime}\hskip-2.13394ptv)f(t,x,^{\prime}\hskip-2.13394ptv_{*})-f(v)f(v_{*})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}.

(1.20)

In
[
74
]
, it is in particular proved that the space-homogeneous version of (
1.18
) is well-posed, in the same spaces as for (
1.17
). Then, Benedetto, Caglioti and Pulvirenti obtained results in the non-homogeneous case. In
[
8
]
, it is proved that (
1.20
) (corresponding to the case of a fixed restitution coefficient) is well-posed close to the vacuum. More specifically here, it is assumed that the initial datum
f_{0}
belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, vanishes for large enough velocities, and such that
\|f_{0}\|_{L^{1}}
is small enough. Finally in
[
11
]
, considering the case
\gamma>0
(and
\beta
being a Dirac mass), the well-posedness of (
1.18
) is proved without assuming anything on the
L^{1}
norm of
f_{0}
(but
f_{0}
is still assumed to be vanishing for
|v|
large).

Later, many authors turned to the case of higher dimension, motivated to investigate a conjecture due to Ernst and Brito
[
35
]
. Such a conjecture postulated that, while the temperature of a granular gas decays according to the Haff’s law, the velocity profile
f(t,v)
should converge towards a self-similar solution of the inelastic Boltzmann equation:

\displaystyle f(t,v)\xrightarrow[t\rightarrow+\infty]{}\alpha(t)m(\beta(t)|v|),

(1.21)

where
\alpha,\beta:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
are two scaling functions. In addition, the conjecture postulated that the self-similar profile
m
, contrary to the Maxwellian equilibria of the elastic Boltzmann equation, should present overpopulated high energy tails, in the sense that there exists some
0<b<2
and
a>0
such that:

\displaystyle m(|v|)\sim e^{-a|v|^{b}}\hskip 8.53581pt\text{as}\ |v|\rightarrow+\infty.

(1.22)

At the same time, a simplified version of the inelastic Boltzmann equation (
1.3
) was introduced, corresponding to Maxwell molecules: in this case the general collision kernel
B(|\hskip 1.42262pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptN\cdot\omega|,|\hskip 1.42262pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv-\hskip 0.28453pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptv_{*}|)
takes the simpler form
b(|\hskip 1.42262pt^{\prime}\hskip-1.42262ptN\cdot\omega|)
. In such a case, the evolution of the moments can be determined. Nevertheless, as observed for instance in
[
76
]
, such a model would lead to an exponential decay of the temperature, which does not correspond to the algebraic decay prescribed the Haff’s law. For this reason, several authors (
[
26
]
,
[
15
]
) considered a rehomogeneization factor

in front of the collision operator (as in (
1.1
)) in order to restore the homogeneity of the collision operator, and to recover an ad hoc Haff’s law. Most of the references considered only the spatial-homogeneous case of (
1.3
), and important contributions concerning the Ernst-Brito conjecture for Maxwell molecules are
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
.

In order to restore also the existence of steady states, variations of the space-homogeneous version of (
1.3
) for Maxwell molecules were considered. For instance, it is possible to consider the inelastic particles in a thermal bath, reinjecting the energy that is lost during the collisions (first in
[
9
]
, then, for instance, in
[
26
]
). Another effect competing with the inelastic collisions that was considered is the presence of a shear
[
28
]
.

Finally, important progresses were completed in the series of articles
[
58
]
,
[
59
]
and
[
60
]
, in which the space-homogeneous, inelastic Boltzmann equation (
1.3
) is considered for the hard sphere collision kernel, which corresponds better to a physical model than the Maxwell molecule kernel. In particular, the Cauchy theory is established in a quite general setting, and the Haff’s law as well as the Ernst-Brito conjecture are proved for restitution coefficients close enough to
1
. We mention also
[
2
]
, in which the Haff’s law is proved for viscoelastic particles, with optimal lower and upper bounds.

To conclude this discussion concerning the non-linear inelastic Boltzmann equation, we emphasize that, to the best of our knowledge, the only reference that deals with the spatially non-homogeneous version of (
1.3
) (in general dimension) is
[
1
]
, where the well-posedness of the solutions is obtained only close to the vacuum.

The linear inelastic Boltzmann equation.

Linear inelastic models have been less discussed in the literature. Concerning the Rayleigh gas, that is, the evolution of a tagged particle within a background gas at thermal equilibrium, several directions were investigated. The existence of solutions, as well as long-time behaviour considerations are discussed in
[
65
]
. The discussion of the existence of an equilibrium, and the convergence of the solutions towards such an equilibrium can be found in
[
71
]
in the case of Maxwell molecules and in
[
48
]
in the case of hard spheres. In both cases, it is proved that the solutions converge towards a Maxwellian steady state (and so, contrary to the non-linear case, the tail of such a profile does not satisfy the Ernst-Brito conjecture).

In the case of a Lorentz gas, that is, when the tagged particles collide with fixed obstacles, there is no source of energy to compensate the inelastic collisions, and so the gas cools down. The behaviour of such a system was studied in
[
41
]
, relying on formal and numerical investigations. In particular, the diffusion of the particles in space is described, as well as the evolution of the velocity profile.

In order to have the existence of non-trivial steady states, the inelastic Lorentz gas was also considered when evolving under the action of an acceleration field (that can be seen as the gravity, or as an electric or magnetic field acting on charged particles). Such a situation was studied in
[
51
]
, where a Lorentz gas of inelastic hard spheres evolves under the action of a constant acceleration field. In this reference, the steady states of the linear inelastic Boltzmann equation are completely determined in dimension
d=1
, and a series expansion of a steady state is given in dimension
d=3
, in the limit of small inelasticity.

In
[
52
]
, the case of an inelastic Lorentz gas composed with Maxwell particles is considered. The existence of a steady state is obtained, based on the use of the Fourier transform. In particular, an explicit, integral formula is given for the steady state. Then, the formula of such a steady state is considered in the limit of small inelasticity.

We observe that in
[
51
]
and
[
52
]
, the uniqueness of the steady states and their stability are not discussed. In addition, the explicit formulas are good approximations only in the case when the particles interact almost elastically with the scatterers.

\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into itself. Indeed, since
|v^{\prime}|\geq r|v|
, we have
that for any
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\varepsilon>0
there exists
M\geq 0
such that, for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|v|\geq M
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

|f(v^{\prime})|\leq\varepsilon\,.

This implies, for
|v|\geq M
,

\left|\mathscr{L}^{*}_{+}[f]\right|(v)\leq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b(|N\cdot\omega|)|f(v^{\prime})|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\leq\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b(|N\cdot\omega|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega,

and so
\mathscr{L}^{*}_{+}[f]\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Observe that we used the assumption
r\neq 0
in a crucial manner.
It is also clear that
a\cdot\partial_{v}f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathscr{L}^{*}_{-}[f]\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if
f\in\mathscr{D}(T)
. As a consequence,
T
indeed maps
\mathscr{D}(T)
into
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we will show that the operator
T
defined in (
2.9
)-(
2.10
) generates a Markov semigroup. To do so, we will need the following technical results.

Lemma 2.2

(Test functions)
.

For any positive integer
n\geq 1
, the function
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as:

\displaystyle f_{n}(v)=\mathds{1}_{|v|<n}e^{-\frac{n}{n^{2}-|v|^{2}}}\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2.11)

satisfies:

•

f_{n}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
f_{n}
and its first derivative
\partial_{v}f_{n}
are compactly supported,

•

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}<+\infty
and
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|\partial_{v}f_{n}\|_{\infty}<+\infty
,

•

f_{n}\rightarrow 1
pointwise,
\partial_{v}f_{n}\rightarrow 0
pointwise as
n\rightarrow+\infty
,

•

f_{n}
is decreasing, in the sense that for
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|v|\leq|w|
, we have:

\displaystyle 0\leq f_{n}(w)\leq f_{n}(v)\leq 1.

(2.12)

Proof.

The fact that
f_{n}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
function is a classical exercise, and the fact that
f_{n}
and its derivative
\partial_{v}f_{n}
are compactly supported is a consequence of the definition.

As for a uniform bound, it is clear that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}=1
. Concerning its derivative, since we have:

\displaystyle\partial_{v}f_{n}(v)=\mathds{1}_{|v|<n}\frac{-2nv}{\left(n^{2}-|v|^{2}\right)^{2}}e^{-\frac{n}{n^{2}-|v|^{2}}},

(2.13)

observing also that
\displaystyle{\sup_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}}\rho^{2}e^{-\rho}}
is a finite, universal constant, we find for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
|v|<n
:

\displaystyle|\partial_{v}f_{n}|(v)\leq\frac{2n|v|}{\left(n^{2}-|v|^{2}\right)^{2}}e^{-\frac{n}{n^{2}-|v|^{2}}}\leq 2\left(\frac{n}{n^{2}-|v|^{2}}\right)^{2}e^{-\frac{n}{n^{2}-|v|^{2}}}\leq 2\sup_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}}\rho^{2}e^{-\rho},

(2.14)

so that we determined an upper bound on
\partial_{v}f_{n}
, uniform in
v
and
n
.

For
v
fixed and
n
large enough, we have
|v|<n/2
, and for such
v
and
n
:

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{n}{n^{2}-|v|^{2}}}\rightarrow 1\ \text{and}\ \left|\frac{-2nv}{\left(n^{2}-|v|^{2}\right)^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{n^{2}}{\left(3n^{2}/4\right)^{2}}\rightarrow 0\ \text{as}\ n\rightarrow+\infty.

(2.15)

Therefore,
f_{n}\rightarrow 1
and
\partial_{v}f_{n}\rightarrow 0
pointwise as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

Finally, the monotonocity of
f_{n}
follows directly from the definition.
∎

Finally, for
f\in\mathscr{D}(T)
,
g\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\lambda>0
given, we will consider the following equation

f-\lambda T[f]=g,

which, more explicitly, reads

f+\lambda\mathscr{L}_{-}^{*}[f]-\lambda a\cdot\partial_{v}f-\lambda\mathscr{L}_{+}^{*}[f]=g

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\left[1+\lambda C_{b}\right]f(v)-\lambda a\cdot\partial_{v}f(v)-\lambda\mathscr{L}_{+}^{*}[f](v)=g(v)

(2.16)

where
C_{b}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b(|N\cdot\omega|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega
. Introducing the characteristics
z_{v}
satisfying:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}z_{v}(t)&=&-\lambda a,\\
z_{v}(0)&=&v,\end{array}\right.

(
2.16
) can be rewritten, for any
\tau\leq s
, as:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\left[f(z_{v}(s))e^{\int_{\tau}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\right]-\lambda\mathscr{L}_{+}^{*}[f](z_{v}(s))e^{\int_{\tau}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}=g(z_{v}(s))e^{\int_{\tau}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}.

Integrating along the characteristics, that is, integrating the previous equation in
s
between
t_{0}\leq t
, we obtain:

\displaystyle f(v)

\displaystyle=f(z_{v}(t_{0}-t))e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}+\lambda\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathscr{L}_{+}^{*}[f](z_{v}(s-t))e^{-\int_{s}^{t}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 199.16928pt+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}g(z_{v}(s-t))e^{-\int_{s}^{t}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Being
t_{0}
and
t
arbitrary, we can choose
t=0
. We can also consider the limit
t\rightarrow-\infty
, which will make vanish the first term of the left hand side, using the fact that the exponential is smaller than
1
,
z_{v}(t_{0})=v-\lambda t_{0}a
and
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Therefore, we obtain (at least formally):

\displaystyle f(v)

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathscr{L}_{+}^{*}[f](z_{v}(s))e^{-\int_{s}^{0}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{-\infty}^{0}g(z_{v}(s))e^{-\int_{s}^{0}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathscr{L}_{+}^{*}[f](z_{v}(-s))e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{+\infty}g(z_{v}(-s))e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

where in the last step we performed the two changes of variables
u\rightarrow-u
and
s\rightarrow-s
.

We introduce then the two operators:

\displaystyle A[f]=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathscr{L}_{+}^{*}[f](z_{v}(-s))e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(2.19)

and

\displaystyle B[g]=\int_{0}^{+\infty}g(z_{v}(-s))e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(2.20)

We now show that the two operators
A
and
B
are well-defined on
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Lemma 2.3

(Domain and image of the operators
A
and
B
)
.

Let
\lambda>0
be a strictly positive number. The two operators
A
and
B
, respectively defined in (
2.19
) and (
2.20
), map
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into itself.

Proof.

We first show that, for any
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
A[f](v)
and
B[f](v)
are well-defined quantities.

We have:

\displaystyle\left|B[f]\right|(v)\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\|f\|_{\infty},

(2.21)

proving that
B[f]
is a uniformly bounded function. As for
A
, we have:

\displaystyle\left|A[f]\right|(v)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\frac{v+\lambda sa}{|v+\lambda sa|}\cdot\omega\right)|f\left((v+\lambda sa)^{\prime}\right)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\right)e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{b}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\lambda C_{b}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}[1+\lambda C_{b}]e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\lambda}\left[e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\right]_{0}^{+\infty}=\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\lambda},

(2.22)

using the fact that
e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\leq e^{-s}
, which is a vanishing quantity in the limit
s\rightarrow+\infty
.

Therefore,
A[f]
and
B[f]
are two continuous and bounded functions when
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

It remains to show that
A[f]
and
B[f]
are vanishing at infinity. In the case of
A
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
s_{0}=s_{0}(\lambda,\|f\|_{\infty},\varepsilon)>0
large enough such that:

\displaystyle\int_{s_{0}}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\frac{v+\lambda sa}{|v+\lambda sa|}\cdot\omega\right)|f\left((v+\lambda sa)^{\prime}\right)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\right)

\displaystyle e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\lambda}

\displaystyle e^{-\int_{0}^{s_{0}}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\lambda}e^{-s_{0}}\leq\varepsilon/2.

(2.23)

In addition, since
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists
M\geq 0
such that for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|v|\geq M
, we have for all
s\in[0,s_{0}]
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
:

\displaystyle|f((v+\lambda sa)^{\prime})|\leq\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{2}\cdotp

(2.24)

Therefore:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s_{0}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\frac{v+\lambda sa}{|v+\lambda sa|}\cdot\omega\right)|f\left((v+\lambda sa)^{\prime}\right)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\right)

\displaystyle e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{0}^{s_{0}}[1+\lambda C_{b}]e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdotp

(2.25)

We have shown therefore that for
|v|
large enough, we have
|A[f]|(v)\leq\varepsilon
, proving that
A[f]\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Similar arguments applied to the case of
B[f]
provides the same conclusion for this operator, which concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

In the end, we can consider a more general form of the equation
f-\lambda T[f]=g
, as summarized in the following result.

Proposition 2.4

(Generalized equation
f-\lambda T[f]=g
)
.

Let
\lambda>0
be a strictly positive number, and let
g\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be any continuous function vanishing at infinity.
If
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
belongs to
\mathscr{D}(T)
and solves the equation:

\displaystyle f-\lambda T[f]=g,

(2.26)

then
f
solves the equation:

\displaystyle f=\lambda A[f]+B[g],

(2.27)

where the mappings
A
and
B
are defined in (
2.19
) and (
2.20
) respectively.
Conversely, if
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
solves the equation (
2.27
) and if
f\in\mathscr{D}(T)
or
g\in\mathscr{D}(T)
, then
f
solves the equation (
2.26
).

The proof of Proposition
2.4
is obtained by a direct computation.

Finally, as a last intermediate step to establish the results we need to prove Theorem
1.1
, we will prove that the operator
A
is bounded, and determine its norm, as a linear operator acting on
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proposition 2.5

(Boundedness of the operator
\lambda A
)
.

Let the operator
A
be defined as in (
2.19
). Then we have that
\lambda\|A\|<1
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. A similar estimate as in the proof of Lemma
2.3
, slightly more precise, shows that, for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\left|A[f]\right|(v)

\displaystyle\leq C_{b}\|f\|_{\infty}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}_{=I_{\lambda}(v)}.

(2.28)

Observe that in the particular case
\gamma=0
, we have:

\displaystyle I_{\lambda}(v)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\int_{0}^{s}[1+\lambda C_{b}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-(1+\lambda C_{b})s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{1+\lambda C_{b}},

(2.29)

so that:

\displaystyle\left|\lambda A[f]\right|(v)\leq\frac{\lambda C_{b}}{1+\lambda C_{b}}\|f\|_{\infty},

(2.30)

which proves that
\lambda A
is bounded, with an operator norm strictly smaller than
1
for each
\lambda>0
. This improves the estimate obtained along the lines of the proof of Lemma
2.3
.
∎

2.2
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we first establish that the closure
\overline{T}
of the operator
T
, defined in (
2.9
), is a Markov generator. This will allow us to solve the adjoint equation (
2.4
) for the test functions
\varphi
in the weak form (
1.28
) of (
1.26
).

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We will check all the assumptions of Definition
3
in the case of the unbounded operator T defined in (
2.9
).

1.

Since the domain
\mathscr{D}(T)
contains the space
\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it is dense in
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Therefore the first point of Definition
3
holds for
T
.

2.

The sequence of functions
(\varphi_{n})_{n}
given by the expression (
2.11
) satisfy for
T
the second condition of Definition
3
, as a consequence of the different properties established in Lemma
2.2
for such functions. The second point of Definition
3
holds for
T
.

3.

We prove now that
T
satisfies the third point of Definition
3
. Let
\lambda\geq 0
be a non-negative real number, and let us consider the only two different cases that can occur.

Let
\varphi\in\mathscr{D}(A)
and let us first assume that
\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)
is not attained at infinity. Then there exists a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)=\inf_{v\in K}\varphi(v)=\min_{v\in K}\varphi(v)=\min_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)=\varphi(v_{0}).

(2.31)

Then let
g=\varphi-\lambda A\varphi
, we have that:

\displaystyle\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(v)\leq g(v_{0})=\varphi(v_{0})-\lambda a\cdot\partial_{v}\varphi(v_{0})-\lambda\mathscr{L}^{*}(\varphi)(v_{0})\leq\varphi(v_{0})=\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)

(2.32)

where we have used the fact that
\partial_{v}\varphi(v_{0})=0
and that
\mathscr{L}^{*}(\varphi)(v_{0})\geq 0
.

Let us now assume that
\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)
is attained at infinity. By the definition of the infimum, there exists a sequence of points
(v_{n})_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
|v_{n}|\rightarrow+\infty
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
and such that

\varphi(v_{n})\leq\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)+\frac{1}{n}.

Let
g=\varphi-\lambda A\varphi
, in this case for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
we have:

\displaystyle\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(v)\leq g(v_{n})

\displaystyle=\varphi(v_{n})-\lambda a\cdot\partial_{v}\varphi(v_{n})-\lambda\mathscr{L}^{*}(\varphi)(v_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)+\frac{1}{n}-\lambda a\cdot\partial_{v}\varphi(v_{n})-\lambda\mathscr{L}^{*}(\varphi)(v_{n}).

(2.33)

Taking the limit as
n\rightarrow+\infty
the right-hand side converges to
\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)
since
|v_{n}|\rightarrow+\infty
and
\partial_{v}\varphi,\mathscr{L}^{*}(\varphi)\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This gives

\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(v)\leq\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v).

Therefore
T
satisfies the third point of Definition
3
.

4.

As for the fourth point of Definition
3
, the surjectivity of
\text{id}-\lambda\overline{T}
, where
\overline{T}
is the closure of the operator
T
, is obtained in two steps. First, let us observe that the image of
\text{id}-\lambda T
is dense in
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Indeed, let us consider any
g\in\mathscr{D}(T)
. By Lemma
2.3
, the mapping
A
introduced in (
2.19
) is a linear mapping from
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to itself and, by Proposition
2.5
,
\text{id}-\lambda A
is invertible. Hence we can find a unique
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that solves the generalized equation (
2.27
). Since in addition
g
was assumed to belong to
\mathscr{D}(T)
, by Proposition
2.4
the regularity of
g
allows to deduce that
f\in\mathscr{D}(T)
as well.

Now,
T
being a Markov pregenerator, that is,
T
fulfills the three first conditions of Definition
3
(see
[
46
]
), we deduce that
T
is closable (relying for instance on Proposition 2.5 in
[
46
]
), and that the image of
\text{id}-\lambda\overline{T}
is the whole space
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(since the image of
\overline{T}
is closed, by Proposition 2.6 in
[
46
]
).

The fourth point of Definition
3
holds as well for
\overline{T}
.

To conclude, let us observe that
T
being a Markov pregenerator, then so is its closure
\overline{T}
. Moreover
\overline{T}
fulfills also the fourth condition of Definition
3
and we conclude that
\overline{T}
is a Markov generator. Therefore, by the Hille-Yosida theorem
2.1
, the operator
\bar{T}
generates a Markov semigroup
S(t)
.

Furthermore if
\varphi_{0}\in\mathscr{D}(T)
then
S(t)\varphi_{0}=\varphi(t,v)\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,+\infty),\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right))
can be chosen as a test function in Definition
1
. Now for every
f_{0}\in\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we define
f\in C([0,+\infty),\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right))
by means of the
duality formula

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)f(t,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)S^{*}(t)f_{0}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}S(t)\varphi(v)f_{0}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v).

(2.34)

Due to the existence of the semigroup
S(t)
this definition is well-posed.
Let now
u(t)\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,+\infty),\mathscr{D}(T))
. Notice that
\bar{T}u(t)=Tu(t)
and that
S(t)(Tu(t))=T(S(t)u(t))
since
u(t)\in\mathscr{D}(T)
for all
t\geq 0
.
Set

\Phi(t,v)=\partial_{t}u(t,v)+Tu(t,v).

(2.35)

One has that

S(t)\Phi(t,v)=\partial_{t}(S(t)u(t,v)).

(2.36)

Integrating (
2.36
) with respect to
f_{0}
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and integrating over
[0,\bar{t}]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
yields

\int_{0}^{\bar{t}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)S(t)\Phi(t,v)=\int_{0}^{\bar{t}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\partial_{t}(S(t)u(t,v))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)(S(\bar{t})u(\bar{t},v)-u(0,v)).

(2.37)

The above equation and the duality formula (
2.34
) give

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bar{t},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)u(\bar{t},v)

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)u(0,v)=\int_{0}^{\bar{t}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)S(t)\Phi(t,v)=\int_{0}^{\bar{t}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\Phi(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\bar{t}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)(\partial_{t}u(t,v)+Tu(t,v))

(2.38)

which proves that
f(t,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)
is a weak solution.

We turn now to the uniqueness of the weak solution of (
2.4
). Let
f_{1},f_{2}
be two weak solutions to (
2.4
), in the sense of Definition
1
, with the same initial datum
f_{0}
. Set
g=f_{1}-f_{2}
and notice that
g
also is a solution to (
2.4
), in the sense of Definition
1
, with initial datum
g_{0}=0
.
Let
u
be a non-zero test function such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\bar{t},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)u(\bar{t},v)\neq 0\;\text{for some $\bar{t}>0$}.

(2.39)

Then the function
u(t)=S(\bar{t}-t)u(\bar{t},v)
is the unique solution of

\begin{cases}-\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}(t)=a\cdot\partial_{v}u(t)+\mathscr{L}^{*}(u(t))&\\
u(\bar{t})=\varphi(\bar{t}),\;\;0\leq t\leq\bar{t}.\end{cases}

(2.40)

Equation (
1.28
) then yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\bar{t},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)u(\bar{t},v)=\int_{0}^{\bar{t}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\left[\partial_{t}u(t,v)+a\cdot\partial_{v}u(t)+\mathscr{L}^{*}u(t)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(2.41)

The right-hand side is identically zero by (
1.28
), hence
g(t,v)=0
for all
t
. We obtained therefore that
f_{1}=f_{2}
, hence the uniqueness.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is complete.

Proposition 3.1

Let

\displaystyle M_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=M_{0}(0),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}M_{1}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}

3.2
Existence of a steady state

To prove existence of a steady state we will use the result of Proposition
3.1
and Proposition
3.2
which allows us to use Schauder fixed point theorem to prove the existence of a stationary solution to (
1.11
) in the sense of Definition
2
. In fact, without loss of generality choose
M_{0}(0)=1
in (
3.9
)-(
3.17
) and define

\mathscr{U}=\left\{f\in\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)\,:\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)=1,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\leq aC_{0},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\leq|a|^{2}\hat{C}_{0}\right\}

(3.20)

with
C_{0},\hat{C}_{0}>0
. Notice that this definition of
\mathscr{U}
covers both cases of
d=2
and
d=3
. The following is the main theorem of this subsection.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
d=2,3
, let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let the restitution coefficient be
0<r<1
. Let
f_{0}\in\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
such that
\{|v|,|v|^{2}\}f_{0}\in\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
and let
f\in\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty),\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right))
be the unique weak solution obtained in Theorem
1.1
. Then there exists a stationary solution
f_{\infty}
of (
1.28
) in the sense of Definition
2
or equivalently a fixed point for the adjoint
S^{*}(t)
of the semigroup
S(t)
defined in (
3.23
).

Proof.

Due to Proposition
3.1
and Proposition
3.2
the set
\mathscr{U}
is convex and closed in the weak
*
topology of
\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
. In addition,
\mathcal{U}
is metrizable and hence sequentially compact and
\mathscr{U}
is also weak
*
compact. To see this notice that
\mathscr{U}
is contained in the unit ball of
\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the space
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is separable
d=2,3
. Since
\mathscr{U}
is weak
*
-closed by the Banach-Alaoglu’s theorem (cf.
[
21
]
) it follows that
\mathscr{U}
is compact. We have that for any
h\geq 0

S^{*}(h)\mathscr{U}\subseteq\mathscr{U}
, hence the operator
S^{*}(h)
is weak
*
compact. Therefore we can apply Schauder fixed point theorem to prove the existence of
f_{*}^{(h)}
such that
S^{*}(h)f_{*}^{(h)}=f_{*}^{(h)}
and by the semigroup property
S^{*}(mh)f_{*}^{(h)}=f_{*}^{(h)}
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Take a sequence
\{h_{k}\}_{k}
such that
h_{k}\rightarrow 0
and its corresponding sequence of fixed points
\left\{f_{*}^{(h_{k})}\right\}_{k}
. This sequence is compact in
\mathscr{U}
, since
\mathscr{U}
itself is compact, and taking a sub-sequence if needed we have
f_{*}^{(h_{k})}\rightarrow f_{*}
for some
f_{*}\in\mathscr{U}
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
. For any
t>0
there exists a sequence of integers such that
m_{k}h_{k}\rightarrow t
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
. On one hand this yields

S^{*}(m_{k}h_{k})f_{*}^{(h_{k})}=S^{*}(h_{k})f_{*}^{(h_{k})}=f_{*}^{(h_{k})}\rightarrow f_{*}
while on the other hand

S^{*}(m_{k}h_{k})f_{*}^{(h_{k})}=(S^{*}(m_{k}h_{k})f_{*}^{(h_{k})}-S^{*}(t))f_{*}^{(h_{k})}+S^{*}(t)f_{*}^{(h_{k})}.

(3.21)

By the weak
*
continuity of the semigroup
S^{*}(t)
it follow that the right-hand side converges to
S^{*}(t)f_{*}
which gives

\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}S^{*}(t)f_{*}=\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}S^{*}(m_{k}h_{k})f_{*}^{(h_{k})}=\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}f_{*}^{(h_{k})}=f_{*}

(3.22)

for every
t\geq 0
. Therefore
f_{*}
is a fixed point for
S^{*}(t)
and the proof is concluded.
∎

3.3
Uniqueness

In this subsection we prove the uniqueness of the stationary solution obtained in Theorem
3.3
. We will prove uniqueness expressing the stationary solution
f_{\infty}
by expanding the equation
f_{\infty}=S^{*}(t)f_{\infty}
by means of a Neumann series which we then prove to be convergent. This is a suitable adaptation of a well-known strategy, see e.g.
[
4
]
. The main theorem of this subsection is the following

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
d=2,3
, let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let the restitution coefficient be
0<r<1
. Let
f_{0}\in\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
such that
\{|v|,|v|^{2}\}f_{0}\in\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
. Then the stationary solution obtained in Theorem
3.3
is unique for every
t>0
.

Proof.

The semigroup
S(t)
introduced in Theorem
1.1
acts on
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

S(t)f=F(t)\varphi+\int_{0}^{t}F(t-s)(\mathscr{L}^{*}_{+}(S(s)\varphi))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(3.23)

where
F(t)
is the semigroup generated by the operator
S\varphi=-a\cdot\partial_{v}\varphi-\nu\varphi
with domain
\mathscr{D}(T)
. The explicit action of
F(t)
on
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is given by

F(t)f=e^{-\nu t}f(v-at).

(3.24)

From Theorem
3.3
there exists
f_{\infty}\in\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty),\mathscr{M}_{+}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right))
such that

f_{\infty}=S^{*}(t)f_{\infty}.

(3.25)

From (
3.23
) the adjoint semigroup
S^{*}(t)
acts as

S^{*}(t)f_{\infty}=F^{*}(t)f_{\infty}+(V(S(t))^{*}f_{\infty},\quad f_{\infty}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(3.26)

where we have set

V(S(t))\varphi=\int_{0}^{t}F(t-s)\mathscr{L}^{*}_{+}(S(s)\varphi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(3.27)

To compute the adjoint of
V(S(t))
take
f\in\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have that

\displaystyle\langle f,V(S(t))\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)F(t-s)(\mathscr{L}_{+}^{*}(S(s)\varphi(v))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)F(t-s)(\mathscr{L}_{+}^{*}(S(s)\varphi(v))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}S^{*}(s)(\mathscr{L}_{+}(F^{*}(t-s)f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)))\varphi(v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\langle(V(S(t))^{*}f,\varphi\rangle

(3.28)

where
\langle\,,\rangle
denotes the duality bracket. Hence from (
3.26
) we can write

f_{\infty}=R(1,F^{*}(t))\int_{0}^{t}S^{*}(s)(\mathscr{L}_{+}(F^{*}(t-s)f_{\infty}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(3.29)

where
R(1,F(t)^{*})
is the resolvent of
F(t)^{*}
at
1
. Notice that from (
3.24
) we have that

\|F^{*}(t)\|=\|F(t)\|\leq e^{-\nu t}<1

for every
t>0
, therefore

R(1,F^{*}(t))=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(F^{*}(t))^{n}

(3.30)

where the series is absolutely convergent for any
t>0
and therefore
R(1,F^{*}(t))
. Hence (
3.29
) reads

f_{\infty}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(F^{*}(t))^{n}(V(S(t)))^{*}f_{\infty}.

(3.31)

The general term of the series can be bounded in the operator norm as

\displaystyle\|(F^{*}(t))^{n}(V(S(t)))^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|F^{*}(t)\|^{n}\Big\|\int_{0}^{t}S^{*}(s)(\mathscr{L}_{+}(F^{*}(t-s)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\nu nt}\sup_{t\geq 0}\|S(t)\|\|\mathscr{L}_{+}\|\int_{0}^{t}\|F^{*}(t-s)\|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\nu nt}\nu\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\nu(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=e^{-\nu nt}(1-e^{-\nu t}).

(3.32)

where we used that fact that
\|S(t)\|\leq 1
for every
t\geq 0
since
S(t)
is a Markov semigroup and that
\|\mathscr{L}_{+}\|\leq\nu
. This give

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(F^{*}(t))^{n}(V(S(t)))^{*}\Bigg\|

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-\nu t})\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}e^{-n\nu t}=(1-e^{-\nu t})\frac{1}{1-e^{-\nu t}}

(3.33)

for any
t>0
and this shows that the series is convergent. Since the Neumann series (
3.31
) identifies a unique element we conclude that the stationary solution
f_{\infty}
is unique, see for instance
[
4
]
.
∎

4
Long time behaviour of the solutions: global attractivity of the steady state

4.1
Strategy to prove the long time behaviour

In this section, we will discuss the long-time behaviour of the measure-valued solutions to (
1.11
) obtained in Theorem
1.1
.
To this aim, we will rely on the well developed machinery available for the study of the Boltzmann equations in the case of Maxwell molecules by means of the Fourier transform.
Initially introduced by A. Bobylev in a nonlinear spatially homogeneous context, this approach has been recently applied in
[
17
]
to study homoenergetic solutions of the elastic, non linear Boltzmann equation and, specifically, to prove existence, uniqueness, and stability of a self-similar profile for the Boltzmann equation under the assumption of small shear deformations.

For this purpose, we will consider the Fourier transform with respect to the velocity
v
of such solutions, namely
\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,v)e^{-iv\cdot\xi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v
. Since the mass of the solutions is preserved along time, we can consider probability measures for all time, and therefore the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)
of the solution
f(t,v)
satisfies
\mathcal{F}(t,0)=1
and

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,v)e^{-iv\cdot\xi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v=1,

(4.1)

since
f\geq 0
. We recall that in probability theory the Fourier transforms of probability measures are often referred to as
characteristic functions
, see e.g.
[
36
]
. They are a
subset
\Phi
of the space of the complex-valued continuous functions
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
. First we consider the evolution equation of the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
, which reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)+\left(1+i(a\cdot\xi)\right)\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\widetilde{b}\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{F}(t,\overline{\xi})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma,

(4.2)

where

\displaystyle\overline{\xi}=\frac{1-r}{2}\xi+\frac{1+r}{2}|\xi|\sigma\,

and we refer to Appendix
A
and Appendix
B
for the detailed justification of the evolution equation for the Fourier transform (
4.2
).

We will then proceed according to the following steps.

•

We will prove that initial data
f_{0}
of the original evolution equation (
1.11
) can be properly compared to the steady state in Fourier variables. Namely, we will show that if
f_{0}
has finite first order moments, as it is the case for the steady state
f_{\infty}
, then, the respective Fourier transforms
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
can be compared as follows

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)-\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(v)\right|\leq C|\xi|,\quad\text{for all $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with $C>0$.}

(4.3)

•

Considering an initial datum
\mathcal{F}_{0}
for (
4.2
) satisfying

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\left|\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)\right|}{1+|\xi|}<+\infty,

(4.4)

we will prove that (
4.2
) is globally well-posed in
\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty),\mathcal{C}_{1})
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}=\{\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})\,:\,\ |\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}<+\infty\},

(4.5)

with

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}=\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\mathcal{F}(\xi)|}{1+|\xi|}\cdotp

(4.6)

•

In addition, we will prove a comparison principle for the evolution equation (
4.2
). More precisely, after defining an appropriate notion of super-solution for (
4.2
), we will prove that if
\mathcal{F}
is a solution of (
4.2
) with initial datum
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and if
\mathcal{G}
is a super-solution with initial datum
\mathcal{G}_{0}
with
|\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)|\leq\mathcal{G}_{0}(\xi)
for all
\xi
, then we have
|\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)|\leq|\mathcal{G}(t,\xi)|
, for all
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

•

We will finally conclude by proving that there exist super-solutions
\mathcal{G}
such that
|\mathcal{G}(t,\xi)|=\varphi_{p}(t)|\xi|^{p}
, where
\varphi_{p}(t)
is a function vanishing at infinity with
p>0
. This will allow us to deduce that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)-\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(v)|\leq\varphi_{1}(t)|\xi|,

(4.7)

hence the local uniform convergence of
\mathcal{F}
towards the steady state
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, and so, the weak convergence of the initial solution
f
of the equation (
1.11
) towards the steady state
f_{\infty}
we uniquely determined in Section
3
.

4.2
Comparison of the Fourier transforms of two functions with same mass and finite first moments

We first provide a general estimate concerning functions with finite zero-th and first moments. This estimate will be useful to compare the initial datum for (
4.2
) of the Fourier transform of a solution
f
of (
1.11
), with the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
of the steady state
f_{\infty}
determined in Section
3
. This result is a direct adaptation of a more general argument that the reader may find for instance in
[
17
]
(see Lemma 5.1 page 421).

First, we will rely on the following elementary result.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists a positive constant
C
such that, for any
v,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\frac{\left(-v\cdot\xi\right)^{n}}{n!}\right|\leq C\min\left(|v||\xi|,|v|^{2}|\xi|^{2}\right).

(4.8)

Proof.

The proof is obtained by distinguishing the case
|v||\xi|<1
and
|v||\xi|\geq 1

First we assume that
|v||\xi|<1
. In such a case, we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\frac{\left(-v\cdot\xi\right)^{n}}{n!}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|(-iv\cdot\xi)^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-iv\cdot\xi)^{n}}{(n+1)(n+2)n!}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|v|^{2}|\xi|^{2}}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\left(|v||\xi|\right)^{n}}{n!}=\frac{|v|^{2}|\xi|^{2}}{2}e^{|v||\xi|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e}{2}|v|^{2}|\xi|^{2}.

(4.9)

If now we assume that
|v|\cdot|\xi|\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\frac{\left(-v\cdot\xi\right)^{n}}{n!}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|e^{-iv\cdot\xi}-1+iv\cdot\xi\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2+|v\cdot\xi|

\displaystyle\leq 3|v|\cdot|\xi|,

(4.10)

where the first inequality we have used that
v\cdot\xi
is a real number so that
|e^{-iv\cdot\xi}|=1
and where we have used that
|v||\xi|\geq 1
in the second inequality.
Taking
C=\max\left\{\frac{e}{2},3\right\}
concludes the proof. ∎

Proposition 4.2

(Comparison principle)
.

Let
f,g\in\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)=1,

(4.11)

\displaystyle M_{1,f}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)<+\infty,\hskip 14.22636ptM_{1,g}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|g(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)<+\infty.

(4.12)

Then, there exists a positive constant
C
, depending on
M_{1,f},M_{1,g}
, such that for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi)-\mathcal{F}(g)(\xi)\right|\leq C|\xi|.

(4.13)

where
\mathcal{F}_{f}
and
\mathcal{F}_{g}
denote respectively the Fourier transforms of the measure
f
and
g
.

Proof.

By definition, we have:

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi)-\mathcal{F}(g)(\xi)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-iv\cdot\xi}f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-iv\cdot\xi}g(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\right|}_{=0}+|\xi|\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|g(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\frac{\left(-iv\cdot\xi\right)^{n}}{n!}\right)\left(f(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)-g(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\xi|\left[M_{1,f}+M_{1,g}+C\left(M_{1,f}+M_{1,g}\right)\right]

(4.14)

where
C
is the constant provided by Lemma
4.1
. The proof is then complete.

∎

4.3
Well-posedness of the evolution equation of the Fourier transform

This section is devoted to the study of the global well-posedness for (
4.2
) in the space
\mathcal{C}_{1}
. To this end we introduce an integrated in time version of (
4.2
). Let
\mathcal{F}
be a regular enough solution of (
4.2
), multiplying the equation by
e^{(1+i(a\cdot\xi))t}
and integrating with respect to the time variable, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{s}\left[\mathcal{F}(s,\xi)e^{(1+i(a\cdot\xi))s}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{F}(s,\overline{\xi})e^{(1+i(a\cdot\xi))s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

(4.15)

from which

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)=\mathcal{F}(0,\xi)e^{-\left(1+i(a\cdot\xi)\right)t}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{F}(s,\overline{\xi})e^{-(1+i(a\cdot\xi))(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.16)

Therefore, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, we say that
\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty),\mathcal{C}_{1})
is a
global solution
of (
4.16
) if for any
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)=\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)e^{-\left(1+i(a\cdot\xi)\right)t}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{F}(s,\overline{\xi})e^{-(1+i(a\cdot\xi))(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

(4.17)

where the space
\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty),\mathcal{C}_{1})
is equipped with the norm

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.42262pt|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}|\hskip-1.42262pt|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}=\sup_{t\geq 0}|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(t,\cdot)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}

(4.18)

and where the norm
\|\cdot\|_{1,\infty}
has been defined in (
4.6
).

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, then there exists a unique global solution to (
4.2
) in the sense of Definition
5
.

The proof of Proposition
4.3
follows the argument presented in
[
17
]
. The solution is constructed via an iterative scheme, which will also allow us to compare the solutions. The drawback of this approach is that the uniqueness has to be proved independently.

Proof.

Consider the following sequence of functions
\left(\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\right)_{n\geq 0}
, defined as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{F}^{(0)}(t,\xi)&=&0,\\
\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}(t,\xi)&=&\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)e^{-\left(1+i(a\cdot\xi)\right)t}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{F^{(n)}}(s,\overline{\xi})e^{-(1+i(a\cdot\xi))(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}.\end{array}\right.

Let
T>0
to be adjusted later. We introduce the Banach space

X=\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathcal{C}_{1})

(4.21)

equipped with norm

\|\mathcal{F}\|_{X}=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\mathcal{F}(t,\cdotp)\|_{1,\infty}

(4.22)

By induction we show that
(\mathcal{F}^{(n)})_{n\geq 0}\in X
. In fact, we observe that trivially
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}\in X
and that, since
\mathcal{F}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, we have
|\mathcal{F}^{(1)}(t,\xi)|\leq|\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)|
, and thus
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}\in X
. Assume now that
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}(t,\xi)}{1+|\xi|}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)e^{-(1+i(a\cdot\xi))t}}{1+|\xi|}\right|+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\frac{\mathcal{F^{(n)}}(s,\overline{\xi})}{1+|\xi|}e^{-(1+i(a\cdot\xi))(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}_{0}|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}+\|\mathcal{F^{(n)}}\|_{X}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\frac{1+|\overline{\xi}|}{1+|\xi|}e^{-(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.23)

We observe that

\displaystyle|\overline{\xi}|\leq\frac{1-r}{2}|\xi|+\frac{1+r}{2}|\xi|=|\xi|

(4.24)

from which it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}(t,\xi)}{1+|\xi|}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}_{0}|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}+\|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\|_{X}(1-e^{-t})\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\leq\|\mathcal{F}_{0}\|_{\infty,1}+\|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\|_{X}.

(4.25)

Thus, taking the supremum first for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and then for
t\in[0,T]
in the above equation implies that
\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}\in X
. By induction, then
(\mathcal{F}^{(n)})_{n\geq 0}\in X
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We show now that the sequence
\left(\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\right)_{n\geq 0}
is convergent in
X
, for
T
appropriately chosen. We first estimate the distance of two consecutive terms of
(\mathcal{F}^{(n)})_{n\geq 0}
. In fact, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}(t,\xi)-\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}(t,\xi)}{1+|\xi|}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\frac{\left[\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}(s,\overline{\xi})-\mathcal{F}^{(n)}(s,\overline{\xi})\right]}{1+|\xi|}e^{-(1+i(a\cdot\xi))(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}-\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}\|_{X}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\frac{1+|\overline{\xi}|}{1+|\xi|}e^{-(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-t})|\hskip-1.42262pt\|\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}-\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}\|_{X}\leq(1-e^{-T})\|\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}-\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}\|_{X},

(4.26)

which yields

\|\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}-\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}\|_{X}\leq(1-e^{-T})\|\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}-\mathcal{F}^{(n+2)}\|_{X}.

(4.27)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}-\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\|_{X}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\|\mathcal{F}^{(1)}-\mathcal{F}^{(0)}\|_{X}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\|\mathcal{F}^{(1)}\|_{X}

(4.28)

and so, for a given
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and any
p,q\geq n_{0}
such that
p\leq q
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}^{(q)}-\mathcal{F}^{(p)}\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=p}^{q-1}\|\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}-\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\|_{X}\leq\sum_{n=p}^{q-1}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\|\mathcal{F}^{(1)}\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{n_{0}}}\|\mathcal{F}^{(1)}\|_{X}\sum_{n=0}^{q-p-1}\frac{1}{2^{n}}=\frac{1}{2^{n_{0}-1}}\|\mathcal{F}^{(1)}\|_{X}

(4.29)

which proves that
\left(\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\right)_{n\geq 0}
is Cauchy in
X
and thus it is converging to a certain
\mathcal{F}\in X
.
Let us observe that the chosen
T
does not depend on
\mathcal{F}_{0}T
therefore, we can bootstrap the previous argument, and construct recursively a solution on
[nT,(n+1)T]
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover we notice that for
T=\log 2
, (
4.25
) implies that

\|\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}\|_{X}\leq\|\mathcal{F}_{0}\|_{\infty,1}+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\|_{X}

(4.30)

from which we immediately deduce by recursion

\|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\|_{X}\leq\|\mathcal{F}_{0}\|_{\infty,1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{2^{k}}=2\|\mathcal{F}_{0}\|_{\infty,1}.

(4.31)

This last estimate proves that the function
\mathcal{F}
belongs to the space
X
for any
T>0
.

Now, passing to the limit
n\mapsto+\infty
in (
4.3
), we find that
\mathcal{F}

is a solution in the sense of Definition
5
. It remains to prove the uniqueness of
\mathcal{F}
. To this end, let us assume that there exists another solution
\mathcal{G}\in X
, with the same initial datum
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. We begin by claiming that
\|\mathcal{F}\|_{X}\leq\|\mathcal{F}_{0}\|_{\infty,1}
. In fact, refining again (
4.25
) we get

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty,1}\leq\|\mathcal{F}_{0}\|_{\infty,1}e^{-t}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathcal{F}(s,\cdot)\|_{\infty,1}e^{-(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.32)

This is a Grönwall-type inequality, that we can study by noticing that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(s,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}e^{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)e^{-t}\right]=|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(t,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}-\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(s,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}e^{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)e^{-t}\leq|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}_{0}|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}e^{-t},

(4.33)

from which integrating in time

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(s,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}e^{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)e^{-t}\leq|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}_{0}|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}_{0}|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}\left(1-e^{-t}\right).

(4.34)

Estimating the integral term in (
4.32
) by (
4.34
), we find our claim

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(t,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}\leq|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}_{0}|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}.

(4.35)

We turn now to the question of uniqueness. Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\in X
be two solutions of (
4.16
) with the same initial datum
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. By definition we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)-\mathcal{G}(t,\xi)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\left[\mathcal{F}(s,\overline{\xi})-\mathcal{G}(s,\overline{\xi})\right]e^{-(1+i(a\cdot\xi))(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.36)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(t,\cdotp)-\mathcal{G}(t,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}\leq\int_{0}^{t}|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(s,\cdotp)-\mathcal{G}(s,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}e^{-(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.37)

This is another Grönwall-type inequality, that can be written as

\displaystyle\left(|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(t,\cdotp)-\mathcal{G}(t,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}e^{t}\right)e^{-t}-\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(s,\cdotp)-\mathcal{G}(s,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}e^{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)e^{-t}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(s,\cdotp)-\mathcal{G}(s,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}e^{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)e^{-t}\right]\leq 0.

(4.38)

Therefore we have that the function
\displaystyle{t\mapsto\left(\int_{0}^{t}|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(s,\cdotp)-\mathcal{G}(s,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}e^{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)e^{-t}}
is non-negative, decreasing, and initially zero, hence it is identically zero, as well as
t\mapsto|\hskip-1.42262pt|\mathcal{F}(t,\cdotp)-\mathcal{G}(t,\cdotp)|\hskip-1.42262pt|_{\infty,1}
. This proves the uniqueness of the solution in
X
for arbitrary
T>0
, concluding the proof.
∎

4.4
Comparison principle for (
4.2
)

Since we are considering Fourier transforms of the solutions
f
of (
1.11
), we are working with complex-valued functions in general. Nevertheless, there is an additional difficulty with respect to the situation studied in
[
17
]
: the drift term in the evolution equation (
1.11
) forces
f
to be complex-valued, in the sense that, even if we assume that the initial datum
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is real-valued, the associated solution of (
4.16
) will not be real-valued in general. This difficulty has to be overcome in order to define a notion of super-solution. In most cases (as it is the case in
[
17
]
), the solutions of evolutionary PDEs remain real-valued if the initial datum is real-valued. This leads to natural definitions of super-solutions, and which enables later to compare the solutions using the monotonocity of appropriate integral operators. In the present situation, it seems that there is no clear way to consider real-valued solutions of (
4.16
), neither to consider transformations of such solutions (such as multiplying the solutions by
e^{i(a\cdot\xi)t}
) that remain real-valued. As a consequence, we will be lead to consider a weaker notion of super-solutions. The notion we will introduce is weaker in the sense that a solution of (
4.16
) will not be a super-solution in the sense that will be introduced below (although our notion of super-solution requires to fulfill a more demanding inequality as the classical notion of super-solution). Another consequence of the impossibility to consider real-valued solutions is that the existence of a principle of comparison of two solutions, starting from two ordered initial data, seems to be lacking here. Nevertheless, we will be able to prove a comparison principle between a solution and a super-solution, assuming that the solution starts initially below the super-solution.

Definition 6

(Super-solutions for (
4.16
))
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}_{+})
. We say that
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{C}\left([0,+\infty),\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}_{+})\right)
is a
super-solution to the evolution equation (
4.16
) with initial datum
\mathcal{G}_{0}
if, for all
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(t,\xi)\geq\mathcal{G}_{0}(\xi)e^{-t}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{G}(s,\overline{\xi})e^{-(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(4.39)

We now prove the principle of comparison between a solution of (
4.16
), and a super-solution in the sense of Definiton
6
, assuming that the initial data are ordered.

Proposition 4.4

(Principle of comparison)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)\right|\leq\mathcal{G}_{0}(\xi).

(4.40)

Consider the solution
\mathcal{F}
to (
4.2
) obtained in Proposition
4.3
and assume that there exists a super-solution to the evolution equation (
4.16
) with initial datum
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.

Then, for all
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)\right|\leq\mathcal{G}(t,\xi).

(4.41)

Proof.

We consider again the sequence of functions
\left(\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\right)_{n\geq 0}
introduced in (
4.3
), defined recursively as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{F}^{(0)}(t,\xi)&=&0,\\
\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}(t,\xi)&=&\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)e^{-\left(1+i(a\cdot\xi)\right)t}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{F^{(n)}}(s,\overline{\xi})e^{-(1+i(a\cdot\xi))(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}.\end{array}\right.

According to the proof of Proposition
4.3
, we know that
\left(\mathcal{F}^{(n)}\right)_{n\geq 0}
converges towards the unique solution
\mathcal{F}
of (
4.16
) with initial datum
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is assumed to be non-negative, we have by assumption

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}^{(0)}(t,\xi)\right|\leq\mathcal{G}(t,\xi),

(4.44)

for all
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Now assume that for a certain index
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}(t,\xi)\right|\leq\mathcal{G}(t,\xi),

(4.45)

for all
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, using the definition of
\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}^{(n+1)}(t,\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)e^{-(1+i(a\cdot\xi))t}\right|+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{F^{(n)}}(s,\overline{\xi})e^{-(1+i(a\cdot\xi))(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)|e^{-t}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}(s,\overline{\xi})|e^{-(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{G}_{0}(\xi)e^{-t}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{G}(s,\overline{\xi})e^{-(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{G}(t,\xi),

(4.46)

where in the last line we have used the assumption that
\mathcal{G}
is a super-solution. Passing to the limit
n\rightarrow+\infty
in the last inequality, we obtain that the solution
\mathcal{F}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)\right|\leq\mathcal{G}(t,\xi).

(4.47)

for any
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

4.5
Exhibiting a super-solution to the evolution equation of the Fourier transform

In order to determine super-solutions of (
4.16
) in the sense of Definition
6
, that is large enough to estimate from above the solutions of (
4.16
) in
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\infty,1}
, and that at the same time have an interesting long-time behaviour, we will, once again, take inspiration from
[
17
]
, and consider functions of the form
u_{p}(t,\xi)=\varphi(t)|\xi|^{p}
.

Contrary to the situation in
[
17
]
, we will see that
u_{p}
decay and converge to zero as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, as soon as
p>0
, which will enable us to deduce the long-time behaviour of the solutions of (
4.16
).

Proposition 4.5

(Super-solutions of (
4.16
))
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{R}
be any real number. Then, the function:

\displaystyle u_{p}(t,\xi)=e^{(\lambda(p)-1)t}|\xi|^{p},

(4.48)

where

\displaystyle\lambda(p)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b(|\sigma_{1}|)\left(\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\sigma_{1}\right)^{p/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma,

(4.49)

and where
\sigma_{1}=\sigma\cdot e_{1}
is the first component of the vector
\sigma
, is a super-solution of (
4.16
) with initial datum
|\xi|^{p}
, in the sense of Definition
6
.

Proof.

The key observation is that the functions of the form
C|\xi|^{p}
are eigenfunctions of the gain collision operator
Q^{+}
defined in (
B.17
). In fact, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\left|\frac{1-r}{2}\xi+\frac{1+r}{2}|\xi|\sigma\right|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma=|\xi|^{p}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\left|\frac{1-r}{2}\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}+\frac{1+r}{2}\sigma\right|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma

(4.50)

Considering the rotation
R
that sends
e_{1}
to
\xi/|\xi|
, and the change of variables
\sigma=R(\sigma^{\prime})
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\left|\frac{1-r}{2}\xi+\frac{1+r}{2}|\xi|\sigma\right|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=|\xi|^{p}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(|(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})|\right)\left|\frac{1-r}{2}R(e_{1})+\frac{1+r}{2}R(\sigma^{\prime})\right|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=|\xi|^{p}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(|(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})|\right)\left|\frac{1-r}{2}e_{1}+\frac{1+r}{2}\sigma^{\prime}\right|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

(4.51)

Furthermore we compute

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1-r}{2}e_{1}+\frac{1+r}{2}\sigma^{\prime}\right|^{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\left|\frac{1-r}{2}e_{1}+\frac{1+r}{2}\sigma^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\frac{1-r}{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{(1-r)(1+r)}{2}\sigma^{\prime}\cdot e_{1}+\left(\frac{1+r}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-2r+r^{2}+1+2r+r^{2}}{4}+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\right)^{p/2},

(4.52)

hence

\displaystyle Q^{+}\left[|\xi|^{p}\right]=\lambda(p)|\xi|^{p},

(4.53)

where
\lambda(p)
is defined in (
4.49
), and
Q^{+}
denotes the gain term of the collision operator of (
B.15
).

To verify that
u_{p}
is a super-solution, we compute the right hand side of (
4.39
), with
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\xi)=|\xi|^{p}=u_{p}(0,\xi)
. Hence we have

\displaystyle u_{p}(0,\xi)e^{-t}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\omega\right|\right)u_{p}(s,\overline{\xi})e^{-(t-s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=e^{-t}|\xi|^{p}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(\lambda(p)-1)s}e^{-(t-s)}Q^{+}\left[|\xi|^{p}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=e^{-t}|\xi|^{p}+e^{-t}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(p)e^{\lambda(p)s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)|\xi|^{p}

\displaystyle=e^{-t}|\xi|^{p}+e^{-t}\left[e^{\lambda(p)t}-1\right]|\xi|^{p}

\displaystyle=u_{p}(t,\xi)

(4.54)

which proves that
u_{p}
is a super-solution in the sense of Definition
6
. ∎

To have complete understanding of
u_{p}
it only remains to study the constant
\lambda(p)
, in particular it remains to compare it with
1
. The following result provides the description of
\lambda(p)
we need.

Proposition 4.6

(Estimation of
\lambda(p)
)
.

Let
p>0
. Then, the constant
\lambda(p)
, defined in (
4.49
), satisfies

\displaystyle\lambda(p)<1.

(4.55)

Proof.

First, we observe that for
p=0
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda(0)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b(|\sigma_{1}|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma=1.

(4.56)

Consider now the derivative of
\lambda
with respect to
p
, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p}\lambda(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b(|\sigma_{1}|)e^{\frac{p}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\sigma_{1}\right)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b(|\sigma_{1}|)\frac{\ln\left(\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\sigma_{1}\right)}{2}e^{\frac{p}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\sigma_{1}\right)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b(|\sigma_{1}|)\ln\left(\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\sigma_{1}\right)\left(\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\sigma_{1}\right)^{p/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma.

(4.57)

We observe now that
-1<\sigma_{1}<1
for almost every
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, then for almost every
\sigma
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\sigma_{1}<\frac{1+r^{2}}{2}+\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}=1,

(4.58)

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p}\lambda(p)<0.

(4.59)

Therefore, we get that
\lambda(p)<1
, for all

. ∎

Remark 5
.

Observe that in the proof of Proposition

, we used in a crucial manner that

, in (

). Indeed, in the elastic case

,

is equal to the integral of the collision kernel

, and is therefore independent from

. In such a case, the super-solution

.

Conclusion of the long-time behaviour argument

We are now ready to prove the stability of the steady state

.

Theorem 4.7

(Long time behaviour of the solutions of (

))
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{0}
,

be the Fourier transforms

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v)

Let

In particular, if

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\left(\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)-\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(\xi)\right)\mathds{1}_{K}(\xi)\right|\underset{t\mapsto+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

By our assumption

we can apply Proposition
4.2
, which gives that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}_{0}(\xi)-\mathcal{G}_{0}(\xi)\right|\leq C|\xi|,

(4.63)

\mathcal{F}_{0}-\mathcal{G}_{0}

Using now classical results (see for instance

.

5
Long-time behaviour of the moments for general homogeneity
\mu\geq 0

In this section we will consider the evolution, and in particular the long-time behaviour, of the moments of the homogeneized version (
1.1
) of (
1.2
). We recall that the rehomogeneized homogeneous, inelastic linear Boltzmann equation, for Maxwell molecules, with a gravity field, is:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(t,v)

\displaystyle+a\cdot\partial_{v}f(t,v)=T^{\mu}(t)\Bigg[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{r}b(|\hskip 0.7113pt^{\prime}N\hskip-1.42262pt\cdot\omega|)f(t,^{\prime}\hskip-2.84526ptv)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega-f(t,v)\Bigg],

where
T=T(t)
is the temperature,
\mu\geq 0
and, without loss of generality, we have assumed in this section that
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b\Big(|N\cdot\omega|\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega=1.

As in Section
3.1
it is possible to derive the evolution equation for the moments of a solution of (
1.1
). Considering the weak formulation of the equation, and choosing
\{1,v,|v|^{2}\}
as test functions we obtain the evolution equations of the moments of order zero, one and two. These evolution equations are explicitly written in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
d=2,3
and let the restitution coefficient
r
be in
]0,1[
. Let
\mu>0
be a strictly positive real number. Let the angular collision kernel
b
satisfy Assumption
1
and suppose that
f
is a solution of (
1.1
). Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}M_{0}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=0,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}M_{1}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=a_{i}M_{0}-C_{d}(1+r)T^{\mu}M_{1},

(5.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}T}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=a\cdot M_{1}-\frac{C_{d}}{2}(1-r^{2})T^{\mu+1},

(5.3)

where
C_{d}
is constant depending only on
d
for which we have

\begin{cases}C_{d}=2\int_{-1}^{1}b(x)\frac{x^{2}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&\text{for $d=2$,}\\
C_{d}=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}b(x)x^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&\text{for $d=3$.}\end{cases}

(5.4)

The proof of the previous proposition, which we do not present here, follows the same lines of the ones provided in Section
3.1
.

5.1
Considerations on the phase space for the ODE system of the moments.

The system of equations obtained in Proposition
5.1
is closed. Nevertheless, the non-linear structure of the system in the case
\mu>0
prevents to solve it explicitly. Nevertheless, it is possible to obtain detailed information on the behaviour of the solutions to (
5.2
)-(
5.3
) with a qualitative study of the respective ODEs. Notice that independently on
\mu
we always have
M_{0}(t)=M(0)
. Since the case
\mu=0
was already addressed in Section
3.1
, we will focus on the case
\mu\neq 0
.

In order to study completely the evolution of the temperature, it is enough to understand the evolution of
a\cdot M_{1}
, the projection of the first moment along the direction of the acceleration field. We will denote such a projection by
x
:

\displaystyle x(t)=a\cdot M_{1}(t).

(5.5)

Denoting the temperature by
y
, we will obtain a two-dimensional system of ODEs of the form:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\vskip 5.69054pt\displaystyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}}x&=&f(x,y),\\
\displaystyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}}y&=&g(x,y),\end{array}\right.

with
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Namely, from (
5.2
)-(
5.3
), we deduce the following system of evolution equations for
x(t)
and
y(t)
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\vskip 5.69054pt\displaystyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}}x&=&|a|^{2}M_{0}-C_{d}(1+r)xy^{\mu},\\
\displaystyle{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}}y&=&2x-C_{d}(1-r^{2})y^{\mu+1}.\end{array}\right.

Since the temperature
T
is defined as

\displaystyle T(t)=y(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f(t,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,

(5.10)

such a quantity can never be negative. On the other hand, the projection
x
of the first moment can have both signs. We will therefore work in the phase space
y\geq 0
. In particular, it is important to ensure that the solutions
t\mapsto\left(x(t),y(t)\right)
never lead to unphysical situations where
y(t)<0
.

As a matter of fact, by considering some particular points
(x,y)
of the phase space of the form
(x,0)
(namely, choosing
x<0
), it is possible to show that the solution (
5.1
) to the moment system through such points are such that
y^{\prime}<0
, leading therefore to a negative temperature for arbitrarily small positive times.

Nevertheless, another important constraint has to be kept in mind, since the first and second moments are not independent, because they are both linked to the distribution function
f
. Since we have:

\displaystyle M_{1}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}vf(t,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,

(5.11)

we deduce that:

\displaystyle|x(t)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(a\cdot v)f(t,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\right|\leq|a|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|f(t,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\leq|a|\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v}\cdot\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f(t,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v}

\displaystyle\leq|a|\sqrt{M_{0}}\sqrt{y(t)}.

(5.12)

As a consequence, the relevant region in the phase space of the ODE system on
(x,y)
is the hypergraph of the function
\displaystyle{y=\frac{x^{2}}{|a|M_{0}}}
. We observe in particular that points of the form
(x,0)
, with
x<0
, do not belong to such a region, ruling out the only possibility to produce solutions with a temperature switching from positive, physical values, to negative values.

We can prove the following result, that can be seen as a test of consistency for the system of the moments (
5.1
).

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed vector, and let
C_{d}
be as in (
5.4
). Let
\mu>0
be a strictly positive real number.

Then, the region:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ /\ |a|M_{0}y\geq x^{2}\}

(5.13)

is an invariant region under the flow of the ODE system (
5.1
).

Proof.

At any point of coordinates
(x,y)
of the boundary
x^{2}=|a|^{2}M_{0}y
of the region
\mathcal{R}
, a normal, ingoing vector
n(x,y)
to the boundary at this point is given by:

\displaystyle n(x,y)=\left(-2x,|a|^{2}M_{0}\right).

(5.14)

Computing the scalar product between the ingoing normal vector
n
and the time derivative of the integral curve of the system (
5.1
) through the point
(x,y)
provides:

\displaystyle n(x,y)\cdot\left(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-2x,|a|^{2}M_{0}\right)\cdot\left(x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=-2x\left(|a|^{2}M_{0}-C_{d}(1+r)xy^{\mu}\right)+|a|^{2}M_{0}\left(2x-C_{d}(1-r^{2})y^{\mu+1}\right)

\displaystyle=2C_{d}(1+r)x^{2}y^{\mu}-C_{d}(1-r^{2})|a|^{2}M_{0}y^{\mu+1}

\displaystyle=C_{d}|a|^{2}M_{0}y^{\mu+1}\left[2(1+r)-(1-r^{2})\right]

\displaystyle=C_{d}|a|^{2}M_{0}y^{\mu+1}(1+r)^{2}.

(5.15)

We observe, on the one hand, that we can always assume
M_{0}>0
(if not, the situation is trivial, for
f
would describe void), and on the other hand that
a>0
. In the case
a=0
, that is, in the case of a Lorentz gas of Maxwell molecules, without any acceleration field, the projection
x
we introduced is zero by definition, and the evolution equation of the temperature becomes the simple one-dimensional ODE:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}y=-C_{d}(1-r^{2})y^{\mu+1},

(5.16)

which implies that a system starting from any positive temperature will have a positive temperature for any time (in the future, but also in the past).

Therefore, in the “non trivial” case
a\neq 0
,
M_{0}\neq 0
, we find that
n(x,y)\cdot(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})>0
, proving that the hypergraph of
x^{2}=|a|^{2}M_{0}y
is an invariant region under the flow of the ODE system (
5.1
). In other words, no solution of the ODE system starting in the hypergraph of
x^{2}=|a|^{2}M_{0}y
can leave this region
∎

Remark 6
.

The result of Proposition
5.2
can be interpreted as follows. Any physical choice of the initial data for the variables
(x_{0},y_{0})
(in the sense that the initial data satisfy the condition (
5.1
)) lead to an integral curve that will satisfy the same physical condition (
5.1
) for all positive times.

To conclude this preliminary discussion about the ODE system (
5.1
), let us observe that the only equilibrium
\left(x_{\infty},y_{\infty}\right)
of the system belongs to the invariant region
|a|^{2}M_{0}y\geq x^{2}
. Indeed, by assumption, we have:

\displaystyle|a|^{2}M_{0}=C_{d}(1+r)x_{\infty}y_{\infty}^{\mu}\hskip 8.53581pt\text{and}\hskip 8.53581pt2x_{\infty}=C_{d}(1-r^{2})y_{\infty}^{\mu+1}.

(5.17)

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle 2x_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle=C_{d}(1-r^{2})x_{\infty}y_{\infty}^{\mu}\cdot y_{\infty}

\displaystyle=C_{d}(1-r^{2})\frac{|a|^{2}M_{0}}{C_{d}(1+r)}y_{\infty},

(5.18)

that is

\displaystyle x_{\infty}^{2}=\frac{(1-r)}{2}|a|^{2}M_{0}y_{\infty},

(5.19)

so that

\displaystyle x_{\infty}^{2}<|a|^{2}M_{0}y_{\infty}.

(5.20)

Remark 7
.

We found that the parabola corresponding to the boundary of the region
\mathcal{R}
, and which describes the consistency relation between the first and second moment coming from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, cannot be crossed by solutions of the ODE system (
5.1
) evolving forward.

Nevertheless, it is possible to consider solutions of (
5.1
) starting exactly on such a parabola. For positive times, the solutions will remain always inside the hypergraph of the parabola, but for negative times, the solutions will leave this region, leading to solutions that cannot be associated to any distribution function
f
solving the inelastic Lorentz equation. Conversely, one can consider the solution of the ODE system (
5.1
) starting inside the region
\mathcal{R}
, for times both positive and negative. By doing so, we would observe that the solutions leave (for negative times) the region
\mathcal{R}
. This strongly suggests that (
1.1
) is not well-posed globally for negative times, and that the solutions of this equation develop singularity in finite, negative times.

5.2
Long time behaviour of the moments

In this section, we will carry out the study of the qualitative behaviour of the solutions of the system (
5.1
). In particular, we will prove that all the solutions (starting inside the physically relevant region
\mathcal{R}
) converge to the only equilibrium of the system.

First, we establish that all the solutions of (
5.1
) eventually enter the first quadrant in finite time.

Proposition 5.3

(Invariance and attractivity of the first quadrant)
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed vector, and let
C_{d}
be as in (
5.4
). Let
\mu>0
be a strictly positive real number.

Then, the region:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\cap\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ /\ x\geq 0,y\geq 0\},

(5.21)

where
\mathcal{R}
is defined in (
5.13
), is an invariant region under the flow of the ODE system (
5.1
). In addition, any solution of the system starting initially from a point of
\mathcal{R}
enters the first quadrant
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ /\ x\geq 0,y\geq 0\}
in finite time.

Proof.

We start to prove that the first quadrant
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ /\ x>0,y>0\}
is an invariant region under the flow of the ODE system (
5.1
).

If a solution
(x(t),y(t))
satisfies
x(t_{0})=0
for some time
t_{0}
, then
x^{\prime}(t_{0})>0
, so such a solution would be outside at
t_{0}-h
for
h>0
small enough. If
y(t_{0})=0
and
x(t_{0})>0
then
y^{\prime}(t_{0})>0
, and the same argument applies. If finally
x(t_{0})=y(t_{0})=0
, then the solution lies on the boundary of
\mathcal{R}
at time
t_{0}
, which is an invariant region under the flow of (
5.1
) by Proposition
5.2
.

We now prove that all the solutions enter eventually in
\mathcal{R}\cap\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ /\ x\geq 0,y\geq 0\}
. Let us assume that a solution
(x(t),y(t))
of the system (
5.1
) remains for all positive time in the quadrant
x\leq 0,y\geq 0
. Since this solution remains in
\mathcal{R}
by Proposition

) as

.
∎

6
Conclusion

In the present article, we studied the linear inelastic Boltzmann equation (
1.2
), in the case of a Lorentz gas, in the space-homogeneous case, for Maxwell molecules and in the presence of a gravity field.

We obtained well-posedness results for such an equation, proving the existence and uniqueness of measure-valued solutions that solve the linear inelastic Boltzmann equation in the weak sense, for a given measure-valued initial datum. In addition, we proved that a unique steady state exists in the class of the non-negative Radon measures with finite first moment, and we proved that such a steady state is attracting all measure-valued solutions with a finite first moment, relying on the Fourier transform approach developed by A. Bobylev.

We also provided a complete study of the system of moments associated to the rehomogeneized linear inelastic Boltzmann equation (
1.1
) for Maxwell molecules.

It remains in future works to establish the same sort of well-posedness results for the linear inelastic Boltzmann equation, considering more general collision kernels. In particular, it is desirable to extend the results to the more physical case of the hard sphere collision kernel. Nevertheless, most of the methods developed in the present articles seem to fail in such a case: the semigroup approach is more difficult to apply, and the study of the system of the moments is notably more difficult outside the case of the Maxwell molecules.

It would be also interesting to solve the rehomogeneized linear inelastic Boltzmann equation (
1.1
). Indeed, although this equation cannot be considered as a completely relevant model from the physical point of view, due to the homogeneity of the collision operator (
1.1
) the decay of the temperature of its solutions should reflect the behaviour of physical models.

Appendix A
\sigma

To obtain the equation satisfied by the Fourier transform

First, let us recall that the

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}v^{\prime}&=&\displaystyle{\frac{v+v_{*}}{2}+(1-r)\frac{v-v_{*}}{4}+(1+r)\frac{|v-v_{*}|}{4}\sigma},\\

Moreover we recall that the idea behind the

is to make explicit the conservation of momentum during collisions, so that the post-collisional velocities have the form

constraints

\omega
-representation. For example in the elastic case, we have

\displaystyle v^{\prime}-v_{*}^{\prime}=v-v_{*}-2((v-v_{*})\cdot\omega)\omega,

(A.3)

which is another way to see that the norm of

is the same as the norm of

up to choosing appropriately
\omega
. In other words, this last expression gives the connection between the
\sigma
- and the
\omega
-representations, since it gives

\displaystyle\sigma=\frac{v-v_{*}}{|v-v_{*}|}-2\left(\frac{v-v_{*}}{|v-v_{*}|}\cdot\omega\right)\omega.

(A.4)

In the inelastic case, the norms of the pre- and post-collisional relative velocities are not equal in general and so the link between

\omega

\displaystyle w=\frac{v^{\prime}-v_{*}^{\prime}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{v-v_{*}}{2}-\frac{(1+r)((v-v_{*})\cdot\omega)\omega}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{v-v_{*}}{2}+\frac{-2(1+r)(v-v_{*})\cdot\omega\omega}{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{v-v_{*}}{2}-\frac{(1+r)(v-v_{*})}{4}+\frac{(1+r)(v-v_{*})-2(1+r)((v-v_{*})\cdot\omega)\omega}{4}

\displaystyle=(1-r)\frac{v-v_{*}}{4}+(1+r)\frac{|v-v_{*}|}{4}\sigma.

(A.5)

In the case of the Lorentz gas, we only keep track of the velocity of the tagged particle moving in a background of fixed scatterers, hence the collision rule involves only one velocity
v
.
In such a case, decomposing to have an orthogonal symmetry to appear again, we obtain

\displaystyle v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=v-(1+r)(v\cdot\omega)\omega

\displaystyle=v-(1+r)\frac{v}{2}+(1+r)\frac{\left(v-2(v\cdot\omega)\omega\right)}{2}

\displaystyle=(1-r)\frac{v}{2}+(1+r)\frac{|v|}{2}\sigma.

(A.6)

To conclude, we notice that, in the elastic case, the
\sigma
-representation for the scattering relation of a Lorentz gas is simply

\displaystyle v^{\prime}=|v|\sigma.

(A.7)

), namely

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)=-i(a\cdot\xi)\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{\sigma}\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{F}(t,\frac{1-r}{2}\xi+\frac{1+r}{2}|\xi|\sigma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma-\mathcal{F}(t,\xi),

(B.14)

or rearranging the terms

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)+\left(1+i(a\cdot\xi)\right)\mathcal{F}(t,\xi)=Q^{+}(\mathcal{F})(t,\xi),

(B.15)

where
\overline{\xi}
is

\displaystyle\overline{\xi}=\frac{1-r}{2}\xi+\frac{1+r}{2}|\xi|\sigma.

(B.16)

and where
Q^{+}
is the gain operator acting on
\mathcal{F}

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{\sigma}\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{F}(t,\overline{\xi})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma

(B.17)

To conclude this section, let us observe that only very mild assumptions on the angular collision kernel
b
are necessary to obtain that the collision integral
\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{\sigma}\left(\left|\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\mathcal{F}(t,\overline{\xi})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma}
is continuous given
\mathcal{F}(t,\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty),\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. In fact, if we assume that
\mathcal{F}
is a continuous function, given
\xi,\xi_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}(\xi)-\mathcal{F}(\xi_{*})\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}b_{\sigma}\left(\left|e_{1}\cdot\sigma\right|\right)\left[\mathcal{F}(\overline{\xi}_{s})-\mathcal{F}(\overline{\xi_{*}}_{c})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\right|,

(B.18)

where
e_{1}
denotes the first vector of the canonical basis and

\displaystyle\overline{\xi}_{R}=\frac{1-r}{2}\xi+\frac{1+r}{2}|\xi|R_{\xi}(\sigma),\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pt(\overline{\xi_{*}})_{R}=\frac{1-r}{2}\xi_{*}+\frac{1+r}{2}|\xi_{*}|R_{\xi_{*}}(\sigma),

(B.19)

with
R_{\xi}
(respectively
R_{\xi_{*}}
) the orthogonal symmetry that sends
e_{1}
to
\xi/|\xi|
(respectively to
\xi_{*}/|\xi_{*}|
). The conclusion follows from the fact that

\displaystyle\left|\left(\frac{1-r}{2}\xi+\frac{1+r}{2}|\xi|R_{\xi}(\sigma)\right)-\left(\frac{1-r}{2}\xi_{*}+\frac{1+r}{2}|\xi_{*}|R_{\xi_{*}}(\sigma)\right)\right|\rightarrow 0

(B.20)

as
|\xi-\xi_{*}|\rightarrow 0
.
