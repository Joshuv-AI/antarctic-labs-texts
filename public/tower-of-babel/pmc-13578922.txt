Corporal Punishment and Associated Factors Among School‐Going Adolescents of Chandragiri Municipality, Kathmandu District, Nepal: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Sweta Adhikari, Susmita Thapa, Manish Rajbanshi, Sushila Kunwar, Shashi Yadav, Ashmita Maharjan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDespite legal prohibitions, corporal punishment remains widely practiced in schools and is associated with adverse physical and psychological outcomes among adolescents. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of corporal punishment and associated factors among school‐going adolescents of Chandragiri Municipality, Kathmandu District, Nepal.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted among 404 students in grades 6 to 10 selected using multistage sampling. Data were collected using a self‐administered questionnaire. Bivariate binary logistic regression, Chi‐square and Fisher's Exact Test were used to determine the factors associated with corporal punishment. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsA high proportion of students (84.2%) reported experiencing corporal punishment, with pulling and pinching being the most common. Among students who reported experiencing corporal punishment, the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress was 43.5%, 60.3%, and 14.7%, respectively. Corporal punishment was significantly associated with school type and academic performance. Students in private schools had higher odds of experiencing corporal punishment (OR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.26–4.82). Reported physical harm among students who experienced corporal punishment was significantly associated with age and academic performance. Students aged 10–13 years were less likely to experience physical harm compared to those aged 14–19 years (OR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.05–0.94). Students with low (OR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.48–5.49) and medium (OR = 7.12, 95% CI: 1.73–12.82) academic performance had higher odds of physical harm. Depression was higher among males (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.23–3.65) and private school students (OR = 2.67, 95% CI: 1.39–4.01). Anxiety was higher among older adolescents (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.28–0.97) and private school students (OR = 4.39, 95% CI: 1.81–6.74). Stress was also higher among private school students (OR = 2.25, 95% CI: 1.24–10.01).ConclusionCorporal punishment was highly prevalent among adolescents in Chandragiri Municipality. Physical effects of corporal punishment were common, with most students reporting pain and a smaller proportion reporting physical harm. Depression, anxiety, and stress were also common among students who reported experiencing corporal punishment. Targeted interventions are recommended, including teacher training on positive discipline, academic support for low‐ and medium‐performing students, age‐appropriate mental health services, and gender‐sensitive counseling to reduce the risk of corporal punishment and promote student well‐being.

Introduction

Corporal punishment refers to the intentional use of physical force to cause pain or discomfort as a method of discipline, often applied by parents, teachers, or caregivers to children and adolescents [1]. Exposure to such punitive discipline has been strongly linked to increased risks of anxiety, depression, aggression, poor academic performance, and impaired social relationships among adolescents [2]. Corporal punishment has also been associated with substance use and antisocial behaviors extending into adulthood [3]. However, corporal punishment is still widely accepted as a means to ‘tame’ children and shape their behavior [4]. Childhood and adolescence are critical developmental periods that require nurturing care and positive guidance [5]. Adolescents, in particular, undergo rapid physical, emotional, and social changes, making them vulnerable to harmful experiences such as corporal punishment [6].

Globally, about 1 billion children aged 2–17 years, experience some form of violence each year, with corporal punishment being a significant contributor [7]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately 1.2 billion children aged 2–17 years are subjected to corporal punishment by caregivers in the home each year [8]. A systematic review confirmed that corporal punishment remains common across families, schools, and communities, and is associated with a wide range of negative outcomes including physical, emotional, and social harm [9]. In Thailand, over 80% of children reported experiencing corporal punishment by age 10, which was later linked to poorer psychological health in young adulthood [10]. In the Czech Republic, 43% of 15‐year‐olds reported experiencing corporal punishment, with 9% exposed to more severe forms [11].

In South Asia, corporal punishment remains widely reported among children in home and school settings despite growing evidence of its harmful effects [12, 13]. More than 2,25,000 students endure corporal punishment every year in India, as many teachers still endorse the practice despite it being officially prohibited [14]. In China, 23.2% of adolescents reported experiencing corporal punishment, and 15.1% faced severe physical maltreatment within a 6‐month period [15].

In Nepal, the situation is particularly concerning. According to the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) 2019, 82% of Children aged 1–14 years were subjected to some form of violent discipline, where 20% experienced severe physical punishment and 77% faced psychological aggression [16]. Furthermore, United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) Nepal's 2018 report indicates that over 80% of children experience some form of violent discipline, including physical punishment and psychological aggression [17].

Despite International Human Rights (IHR) frameworks emphasizing the protection of children from physical violence, corporal punishment remains prevalent worldwide, including in home, school, and other settings [18, 19]. The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) upholds children's rights to dignity and physical integrity, explicitly calling for the elimination of corporal punishment in all environments [20]. The Constitution of Nepal, 2015 (Article 39) prohibits all forms of abuse and violence against children, including corporal punishment in homes, schools, and communities [21]. This was further reinforced by the Act Relating to Children (2018), which criminalized both physical and mental punishment [22]. However, despite these legal provisions, corporal punishment is often regarded as a necessary disciplinary tool to facilitate learning and instill discipline among children in Nepal [23]. The continued use of this practice has been associated with factors such as weak implementation of national policies, unhealthy academic competition among schools, insufficient teacher training, deeply rooted traditional beliefs, and hierarchical social structures [23, 24]. Enforcement of these laws remains inconsistent, particularly in rural and semi‐urban areas, where oversight is limited and traditional norms continue to dominate [17].

Addressing corporal punishment is critical to ensuring the physical, emotional, and educational well‐being of children, as well as upholding their fundamental human rights [18, 25]. Although national surveys such as MICS and UNICEF reports have documented the widespread occurrence of violent discipline among children in Nepal, these data primarily provide national‐level estimates and focus largely on younger age groups. As a result, there is limited evidence on its prevalence and associated factors among school‐going adolescents at the local level, particularly within specific municipalities and educational settings.

Chandragiri Municipality, a rapidly growing urban area with diverse socio‐cultural and educational characteristics, lacks municipality‐specific evidence on corporal punishment among school‐going adolescents. Generating local evidence is important because disciplinary practices, social norms, school environments, and the implementation of child protection laws may vary across settings. Understanding the prevalence and associated factors in this context is therefore essential for informing educators, policymakers, and child protection stakeholders to design targeted interventions and strengthen the enforcement of existing child rights policies. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of corporal punishment and associated factors among school‐going adolescents of Chandragiri Municipality, Kathmandu District, Nepal. The findings are expected to support local awareness, policy development, and broader efforts to eliminate corporal punishment and promote positive, non‐violent disciplinary practices.

Materials and Methodology

Study Design and Settings

This study employed a cross‐sectional design and was conducted in upper basic level (Grades 6–8) and lower secondary level (Grades 9–10) schools within Chandragiri Municipality, Kathmandu District, Nepal. According to the municipal profile of Chandragiri Municipality, the total population of Chandragiri Municipality was 85,198 [26]. The municipality has a substantial school‐age population, making it an appropriate setting for adolescent‐based research [26]. This municipality had 79 schools, including both public and private institutions [27]. Public and private schools may differ in their management systems, student–teacher ratios, disciplinary practices, and levels of monitoring, which may influence the prevalence and experience of corporal punishment among students. Therefore, both types of schools were included in this study for comparison purposes. Despite the municipality's proximity to the Kathmandu valley, there is limited evidence on the prevalence, effects, and associated factors of corporal punishment among school‐going adolescents in this setting.

Study Population

The students of grade 6 to 10 of both public and private schools were the population under study. Students who declined to provide consent or whose parents withheld assent were excluded from the study. Additionally, students who were absent during data collection were also excluded.

Sample Size and Sampling Technique

The sample size was calculated using the Cochran's probability proportionate formula (n = Z2pq/d2) [28]. Here, ‘z’ represents the standard normal deviate at 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), 'p' is the estimated prevalence of physical punishment (53%), and 'q' represents 1‐p. The prevalence of physical punishment among adolescents (p = 53%) was taken from the Nepal Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (NMICS) 2014 [29]. Assuming a 95% Confidence Interval (CI), 5% allowable error (d), and a 10% non‐response rate, the minimum required sample size was 404 for this study.

A multistage random sampling method was used to recruit participants. The list of schools was obtained from the Education Section of the municipality. There were a total of 79 schools in the municipality, of which 25 were eligible [27]. Schools that did not include academic sessions up to Grade 10, were closed during the data collection period, were conducting examinations, or did not grant permission to participate were excluded. Ten schools were then randomly selected from the eligible schools using the lottery method. Each selected school was considered a cluster. An equal probability proportionate sampling technique was applied to select the required number of participants from each selected school (n = 404/10 ≈ 40). All students from grades 6 to 10 in each school were invited to complete the questionnaire. Invalid questionnaires were excluded, and 40 valid responses were randomly selected from each school using the lottery method.

Tools and Measures

The study used a structured self‐administered questionnaire comprising both adapted and standardized instruments. The tool for assessing corporal punishment‐related information was adapted from previously published similar studies [10, 15, 25, 30]. Corporal punishment was operationally defined as any physical or verbal disciplinary punishment received by a student at school during the current academic year. Participants were classified as having experienced corporal punishment if they answered “yes” to the question, “Have you received corporal punishment at school during the current academic year?” Those who answered “no” were classified as not having experienced corporal punishment. Students who reported experiencing corporal punishment were subsequently asked about the type of punishment received, physical effects (pain or physical harm), and related characteristics. Physical effects were categorized as pain, physical harm, or other effects based on participants' self‐reports. For this study, pain referred to a painful or uncomfortable physical sensation experienced in relation to the punishment, whereas physical harm referred to a physical injury or bodily effect perceived and reported by the participant, beyond pain alone.

The questionnaire was translated into the Nepali language and back‐translated to ensure linguistic accuracy. Content validity was ensured through expert review by subject specialists from the Department of Public Health, Om Health Campus, Purbanchal University. Depression, anxiety, and stress were assessed using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale‐21 (DASS‐21), a standardized self‐report instrument consisting of 21 items with three subscales: depression, anxiety, and stress (7 items each) [31]. The DASS‐21 was administered only to students who reported experiencing corporal punishment (n = 340), as the study aimed to assess depression, anxiety, and stress specifically among students reporting corporal punishment. Each item is rated on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“did not apply to me at all”) to 3 (“applied to me very much or most of the time”) [31]. The Nepali version of the DASS‐21 has been previously validated and widely used among adolescent populations, demonstrating acceptable psychometric properties [32, 33].

The questionnaire, including both the adapted corporal punishment items and the standardized DASS‐21 scale, was pretested among 10% of the total sample size (n = 40) in non‐selected schools to assess clarity, feasibility, and consistency. Based on pretest findings and expert feedback, necessary modifications were made. The corporal punishment questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory reliability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.81). For the DASS‐21, Cronbach's alpha coefficients were 0.83 for depression, 0.87 for anxiety, and 0.89 for stress.

For DASS‐21 scoring, the final scores for each subscale were multiplied by two to obtain the final scores, as recommended for the DASS‐21. For the purpose of this study, the outcomes were dichotomized into “presence” and “absence” of psychological distress, where participants falling into mild and above categories were considered as having depression, anxiety, or stress, respectively. The questionnaire was divided into four sections.

Section I: Socio‐demographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, religion, school type, academic performance).

Section II: Corporal punishment–related characteristics (meaning, experience, reporting behavior, parental involvement, reasons, behavioral changes, and perceptions).

Section III: Types and physical effects of corporal punishment

Section IV: Psychological outcomes including depression, anxiety, and stress assessed using DASS‐21.

Data Collection Techniques

Data were collected from August 13 to September 8, 2019. The self‐administered questionnaire was used to collect the data. The Principal Investigator (PI) conducted the data collection in the classroom setting, while school teachers were physically present to assist with classroom management and logistical arrangements. Teachers were provided orientation on the study purpose and data collection techniques but did not participate in administering the questionnaire or access students' responses. The appropriate time for the data collection was arranged in coordination with the school administrations.

Before collecting data, the students were provided orientation and instructions on completing the questionnaire. To obtain parental consent, the consent forms were distributed to students before the day of data collection. On the following day, written consent and assent forms were obtained from each students.

Data Management and Analysis

The collected data were cleaned, coded, filtered, and cross‐checked using EpiData version 3.1, and then exported to IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 for analysis. Univariate analysis was performed using numbers and percentages. Bivariate binary logistic regression, Chi‐square and Fisher's exact test were used to determine factors associated with corporal punishment, physical effects, depression, anxiety, and stress. Multivariable logistic regression models were considered for physical harm, depression, anxiety, and stress among students who reported experiencing corporal punishment. Candidate variables included age, sex, ethnicity, religion, school type, and academic performance. However, the attempted multivariable model showed poor goodness‐of‐fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.02) and limited explanatory power (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.07). Additionally, the relatively small number of outcome events and sparse data across some categorical predictor levels resulted in unstable estimates. Therefore, the final analyses were based on bivariable logistic regression, and potential confounding could not be adequately controlled. Variables with a p‐value < 0.05 at the 95% CI were considered statistically significant. For analyses, the total sample (n = 404) was used to estimate the prevalence of corporal punishment and its associated factors. Subgroup analyses were then performed among students who reported experiencing corporal punishment (n = 340). Physical effects, depression, anxiety, and stress were assessed and described only within this subgroup. Participants who did not report experiencing corporal punishment (n = 64) were excluded from these subgroup analyses, as outcome variables were not applicable to them.

Ethical Consideration

This study was reviewed and approved by the Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) [Reference no: 402]. A letter of support was obtained from the Chandragiri Municipality Office to conduct data collection. For participants aged < 18 years, written parental consent was obtained prior to data collection, followed by written students' assent on the day of the survey. Participants were informed about the study objectives, procedures, risks, and their rights before participation. Confidentiality and anonymity were ensured by using self‐administered questionnaires without personal identifiers such as names or roll numbers. Data were collected in classroom settings by the research team, with teachers only assisting in logistical arrangements and not accessing responses. Students were seated to minimize visibility of others' responses.

Participation was voluntary, and students could refuse or withdraw at any time without consequences. Completed questionnaires were securely collected and handled only by the research team. Confidentiality was maintained throughout data processing, analysis, and reporting.

Discussion

In this study, a majority of participants (84.2%) reported experiencing corporal punishment, most commonly in the form of pulling and pinching (50.0%). These findings align with the studies conducted in Nepal [17, 25]. The persistence of such practices suggests that, despite legal prohibitions and awareness programs, traditional disciplinary methods continue to be normalized in school settings [34]. This high prevalence is concerning given the levels of depression, anxiety, and stress observed among students reporting corporal punishment.

In this study, school type was significantly associated with corporal punishment. Students in private schools were about 2 times more likely to report experiencing corporal punishment compared to students in government schools. This may be due to stricter disciplinary practices in some private schools to enforce rules, maintain academic performance, or meet parental expectations, which can sometimes lead to physical punishment [35]. Government schools may differ from private schools in their disciplinary approaches because of variations in school management practices and institutional contexts [23]. Similar differences in attitudes toward corporal punishment between public and private school settings have also been reported in the United States [36].

Academic performance was also significantly associated with experiences of corporal punishment. Students with low and medium academic performance were 2.3 and 1.5 times more likely, respectively, to report experiencing corporal punishment compared to students with high academic performance. This may be because teachers sometimes use physical discipline in response to poor academic performance [37]. However, such punishment has been associated with adverse learning, behavioral, and mental health outcomes, which may further affect academic progress [38]. These findings are consistent with those of a similar study conducted in the United States [30].

Participants' age was also significantly associated with reported physical harm among students who experienced corporal punishment. Students aged 14–19 years were more likely to report physical harm resulting from corporal punishment compared to students aged 10–13 year This difference may reflect variations in exposure to school settings, cumulative school experiences, or awareness and reporting of physical harm [39] [40]. Similar findings have been reported in studies from Tanzania [41] and the United States [42].

The findings of this study also showed that academic performance was significantly associated with the reported physical harm among students who experienced corporal punishment. Compared to students with high academic performance, those with low academic performance had twice the odds, while those with medium academic performance had 7.12 times the odds, of reporting physical harm. This may be because struggling students are punished more often, yet instead of improving learning, corporal punishment may increase stress, lower motivation, and reduce self‐esteem [1]. Such experiences may be associated with difficulties in concentration and academic progress, potentially contributing to continued poor academic performance [1]. In contrast, high‐performing students may be less exposed to such treatment, which may reduce their likelihood of experiencing physical harm [43]. These results are consistent with evidence showing detrimental effects of corporal punishment on children's cognitive and academic development [44].

Among students who reported experiencing corporal punishment, male participants had 1.49 times higher odds of depression than female participants. In many communities, boys are expected to perform well academically and carry future family responsibilities, which can increase pressure and psychological distress [45]. Additionally, boys may be less likely to share emotional difficulties due to prevailing gender norms that discourage expression of vulnerability, which may contribute to internalized stress and depressive symptoms [46]. This finding is in contrast to several previous studies, which have reported a higher prevalence of depression among female adolescents [45, 47, 48].

Among students who reported experiencing corporal punishment, school type was significantly associated with depression. Students in private schools reported a higher 2.7 times higher odds of depression compared to those in government schools. Although this finding appears counterintuitive given the generally perceived better resources in private schools, it may reflect higher academic pressure, competitive environments, and stronger performance expectations in private institutions [49, 50]. In addition, greater awareness and willingness to report depressive symptoms may have contributed to this association [51]. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies [52].

Among students who reported experiencing corporal punishment, age was significantly associated with anxiety. The adolescents aged 14–19 years were more likely to report anxiety compared to younger adolescents 10–13 years. Older adolescents face increased academic pressure, greater social evaluation, and more complex peer relationships [53]. Younger adolescents may have more parental support and less exposure to stressful social demands, which may contribute to the lower reported anxiety in this group [54]. These findings are similar to those of a study conducted in India [55].

Among students who reported experiencing corporal punishment, school type was significantly associated with anxiety and stress, with students in private schools having higher odds of anxiety and stress than those in government schools. This may be due to greater academic demands, frequent examinations, and high expectations in private schools, which may be associated with psychological distress [56]. In contrast, government schools may provide a relatively less competitive academic environment, which could explain the lower prevalence of anxiety and stress among their students [57]. It is also important to note that the study sample was predominantly composed of private school students, which may have influenced the observed associations.

Conclusion

Corporal punishment remains highly prevalent among school‐going adolescents in Chandragiri Municipality, despite existing legal prohibitions. The study found significant associations between reported corporal punishment and school type and academic performance, while physical harm and psychological distress were also reported among students who experienced corporal punishment. The findings underscore the need to address the high prevalence of corporal punishment among adolescents. Schools should strictly enforce existing bans on corporal punishment. Teacher training programs on positive and non‐violent disciplinary methods may help address practices such as pulling, pinching, and beating. School‐based mental health support should be provided to students experiencing depression, anxiety, or stress, with targeted support for vulnerable groups. Additionally, parental awareness and community sensitization programs are recommended to foster supportive, non‐violent environments both at school and at home.

Strengths and Limitations

This study provides important evidence on corporal punishment, and its associated factors among school‐going adolescents in Chandragiri Municipality. A key strength is the inclusion of both government and private schools, which enhances the comparability of findings across different school settings. The study used a standardized and widely validated instrument (DASS‐21) to assess depression, anxiety, and stress, which strengthen the reliability and comparability of the psychological measures.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, depression, anxiety, and stress were assessed only among students who reported experiencing corporal punishment; therefore, no causal relationship can be established. Second, as data were collected using a self‐administered questionnaire on a sensitive topic, there is a possibility of recall and social desirability bias, despite efforts to ensure confidentiality and provide adequate instructions to participants. Moreover, the physical presence of teachers in the classroom during data collection may have influenced students' willingness to report sensitive experiences related to corporal punishment. In addition, the study relied on self‐reported measures, including academic performance, which may be subject to reporting bias. Multivariable analysis was attempted but was limited by poor model fit, sparse data, and unstable estimates; therefore, bivariable analysis was retained, and potential confounding could not be adequately controlled. Furthermore, the sample included a higher proportion of private school students, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Finally, the tool used to assess corporal punishment was adapted and pretested, which may have affected measurement validity. The data were collected in 2019, and changes in school environments and disciplinary practices since then may limit the current applicability of the findings.
