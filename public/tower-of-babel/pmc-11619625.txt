Association between endothelial activation and stress index and 30-day mortality risk in acute myocardial infarction patients: a study based on the medical information mart for intensive care-IV database
Authors: Mingmin Sang, Xiaofeng Ma, Fangyi Zhu, Cunkui Zhu, Zuohua Ying
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the association between the Endothelial Activation and Stress Index (EASIX) and 30-day mortality risk in acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients.MethodsUsing a retrospective cohort design, data were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) database between 2008 and 2019. Patients diagnosed with AMI at intensive care unit (ICU) admission were included. EASIX score was calculated as follows: lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level (U/L) × creatinine level (mg/dL)/platelet count (109/L). Cox regression models assessed the association between EASIX and 30-day mortality, with subgroup analyses based on age, gender, AMI subtype, and sepsis status.ResultsA total of 1,036 patients were analyzed, among whom 323 did not survive beyond 30 days post-ICU admission. Higher EASIX scores were associated with increased 30-day mortality in AMI patients [Hazard ratio (HR): 1.70, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.17–2.46, P = 0.005). Subgroup analyses supported these findings and revealed significant interactions between EASIX and variables such as gender and AMI subtype (P < 0.05).ConclusionElevated EASIX scores are significantly correlated with increased 30-day mortality risk in AMI patients, suggesting EASIX as a valuable prognostic tool that may inform clinical management strategies to improve outcomes in AMI.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Association, Endothelial activation and stress index, Mortality, Acute myocardial infarction

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI), or heart attack, is characterized by myocardial necrosis (death of heart muscle cells) due to ischemia, which results from reduced blood flow to the heart muscle [1]. AMI is a major health concern, with a prevalence of 3.8% in individuals under 60 and 9.5% in those over 60 [2]. AMI is a common and critical condition in intensive care units (ICUs) [3], contributing significantly to high in-hospital mortality [4, 5]. In 2019, AMI accounted for approximately 8.9 million deaths worldwide, representing 16% of global mortality [6]. Consequently, identifying early biomarkers for mortality risk in AMI patients is essential to help reduce the disease burden and improve clinical outcomes.

Numerous studies have identified various biomarkers as significant predictors for the prognosis of cardiovascular diseases, aiding in the assessment of risk and patient outcomes [7–12]. In a study involving 1,912 ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients who underwent primary percutaneous coronary intervention (p-PCI), the SYNTAX Score II (SS-II) was shown to be a robust predictor of both in-hospital and long-term all-cause mortality [8]. Similarly, the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) score has demonstrated strong predictive performance in evaluating the complexity and severity of coronary artery disease in a cohort of 403 patients with stable angina [9]. A retrospective study found that the hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score is an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality in patients with STEMI undergoing p-PCI [10]. A study based on data from the Turkish Coronary Intensive Care Unit Mortality Study found that the glucose-to-potassium ratio (GPR) could serve as an important predictor of in-hospital mortality among coronary care unit (CCU) patients [12].

The Endothelial Activation and Stress Index (EASIX) has recently emerged as a promising biomarker for endothelial injury [13]. The vascular endothelium plays a crucial role in maintaining homeostasis and functions as a multifunctional organ that regulates vascular tone [14]. Endothelial dysfunction is commonly observed in individuals with cardiovascular risk factors and is a major contributor to the development and progression of atherosclerotic thrombosis, ultimately leading to AMI [15]. Based on this, we hypothesize that the EASIX index may be a valuable prognostic tool for AMI patients. Several studies have reported a significant association between EASIX and the short-term mortality risk in sepsis and Coronavirus Disease (COVID)-19 patients, demonstrating its potential predictive value [16, 17] Given these findings, this study aims to analyze the relationship between EASIX and 30-day mortality risk in AMI patients. Identifying high-risk patients through EASIX could enhance disease monitoring, inform therapeutic decisions, and improve prognostic outcomes for individuals with AMI.

Methods

Study design and patients

The study employed a retrospective cohort design and extracted data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) database. The study’s enrollment period spanned from 2008 to 2019. MIMIC-IV. The MIMIC-IV is a comprehensive, freely accessible database that contains de-identified health data associated with over 40,000 patients who were admitted to the ICUs at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts. MIMIC-IV is an updated version of the MIMIC-III database [18]. The data utilized in this study were sourced from a publicly accessible database. Given the de-identified nature of the data and the absence of any direct patient interactions, neither institutional review board (IRB) approval nor informed patient consent was necessitated for this research.

For this study, patient records were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) patients diagnosed with AMI upon ICU admission, and (2) patients aged 18 years or older. Exclusion criteria for this study included: (1) ICU stays of less than 24 h, (2) missing survival data, and (3) absence of key data such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatinine, and platelet count.

Data collection

The demographics and clinical data were collected including (1) Demographics: age (Years), gender (Female, and Male), race (White, and Other), insurance status (Medicaid, Medicare, and Other), marital status (Married, Single/Divorced/Widowed, and Unknown), (2) Clinical characteristics: ICU type [CCU, Medical intensive care unit (MICU), Surgical intensive care unit (SICU), Combined medical/Surgical ICU (MICU/SICU), Neurological intensive care unit (NICU), AMI type [STEMI, Non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and Other], sepsis, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), and PCI, (3) Vital signs and measurements: 24 h urine Output (mL), weight (kg), heart rate (bpm), systolic blood pressure (SBP, mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (DBP, mmHg), respiratory rate (insp/min), temperature (℃), (4) Laboratory data: white blood cells (WBC, K/uL), hematocrit (%), hemoglobin (g/dL), red cell distribution width-CV (RDW-CV, %), bilirubin (mg/dL), International Normalized Ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT, sec), blood urea nitrogen (BUN, mg/dL), glucose (mg/dL), anion gap (mEq/L), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL/min/1.73m2), (5) Scores: Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), (6) Medications and interventions: anticoagulation agents, antiplatelet agents, diuretic, statin, vasopressors, and mechanical ventilation.

Variable definitions

EASIX score was calculated based on the following formula: LDH level (U/L) × creatinine level (mg/dL)/platelet count (109/L). According to previous reports, the EASIX demonstrated a skewed distribution. To normalize this skewed distribution and make the data more suitable for statistical analysis, the EASIX was transformed into its logarithmic form using a base-2 logarithm and categorized into tertiles (three groups based on the distribution of the values) [13].

This study defined AMI in the MIMIC-IV database based on ICU admission diagnoses with International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 codes 41,000 to 41,092 or ICD-10 codes I21 (excluding I21A, I21A1, I21A9), with STEMI classified under ICD-9 codes 4100–4106, 4108 or ICD-10 codes I210-I213, and NSTEMI under ICD-9 code 4107 or ICD-10 code I214, including only the first ICU admission during the first hospitalization for patients with multiple admissions. Sepsis was diagnosed based on the Sepsis-3 criteria, which define sepsis as a condition in patients with a documented or suspected infection, accompanied by an acute increase of 2 or more points in the total Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [19]. PCI was identified using the following codes: ICD-9 procedure codes 0066, 3606, 3607, 3609, 3610, and ICD-10 procedure codes with the first five digits 02703, 02713, 02723, and 02733. CABG was identified using the following codes: ICD-9 procedure codes 3611–3619, 362, and ICD-10 procedure codes with the first four digits 0210–0213.

Anticoagulant medications were identified using specific item IDs for various drugs as follows: Warfarin (225913), Heparin (225152, 229597, and 225975), Bivalirudin (225148, 229781), Argatroban (225147), Lepirudin (221892), and Fondaparinux (225908). Antiplatelet medication was identified using the item ID for tirofiban (225157). Diuretics were identified using the item IDs for Furosemide (228340, 221794) and Bumetanide (229639). Statin use was identified from the prescription records for various statin medications, including Atorvastatin, Lipitor, ATOR, Caduet, Fluvastatin, Lescol, Pitavastatin, Lovastatin, Mevacor, Altoprev, Pravastatin, Livalo, Rosuvastatin, Crestor, Simvastatin, Zocor, and Vytorin. The eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula: eGFRCKD−EPI (mL/min/1.73m2) = 141×min(Scr/κ,1)α × max(Scr/κ,1)-1.029 × 0.993age×1.108 (if female), κ is 0.7 for females and 0.9 for males, α is -0.329 for females and − 0.411 for males, min indicates the minimum of Scr/κ or 1, and max indicates the maximum of Scr/κ or 1. Anion gap = (sodium + potassium) - (chloride + bicarbonate).

Outcome and follow-up

The outcome for this study was 30-day mortality. The follow-up endpoint was set at 30 days post-ICU admission. The median follow-up time was 30.00 days, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 11.93 to 30.00 days.

Statistics analysis

Continuous variables following a normal distribution were described using the mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), and differences between groups were compared using the t-test for equal variances. For data with unequal variances, the Satterthwaite approximate t-test was used for comparison. Continuous variables not following a normal distribution were described using the median and interquartile range M (Q₁, Q₃), and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for between-group comparisons. Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages [n (%)], and differences between groups were compared using the chi-square test. Variables with a missing rate greater than 20% were excluded from the analysis. Variables with a missing rate of 20% or less were subjected to multiple imputation. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to compare results before and after imputations.

Potential confounding variables were analyzed for their association with 30-day mortality and survival time using univariate Cox regression, selecting those with P < 0.05. Variables identified with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in a multivariable Cox regression model, employing forward stepwise regression for covariate selection. The multivariate model adjusted for these covariates to study the association between EASIX levels and the risk of 30-day mortality. Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed based on age, gender, AMI subtype, and sepsis status to explore potential interactions and ensure the robustness of the findings. Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves were also plotted to visualize the risk of 30-day mortality across different EASIX levels. The evaluation metrics included hazard ratio (HR) and confidence interval (CI). An alpha level of 0.05 was used for statistical significance. Data extraction was performed using PostgreSQL 14.7, and data cleaning, imputation of missing values, covariate selection, regression analysis, and subgroup analysis were conducted using R version 4.2.3.

Results

Characteristics of included patients

The study initiated with a total of 5,246 patients diagnosed with AMI and were admitted to the ICU. After applying the exclusion criteria, the final sample size was reduced to 1,036 patients. This study included 323 patients who unfortunately did not survive past the 30-day following their ICU admission. Figure 1 illustrates the study selection process. The mean age of the entire cohort was 67.15 ± 14.00 years. The cohort included 368 females (35.52%) and 668 males (64.48%). The insurance status of the patients was distributed as follows: Medicaid: 53 patients (5.12%), Medicare: 508 patients (49.03%), and Other: 475 patients (45.85%). Concerning the type of AMI, 328 patients (31.66%) experienced STEMI, 673 patients (64.96%) had NSTEMI, and 35 patients (3.38%) were classified as other types of AMI. There were significant differences in age, with the non-survivor group being older on average (70.96 ± 13.34 years) compared to the survivor group (65.42 ± 13.96 years) (P < 0.001). The non-survivor group had a lower median 24-hour urine output (900.00 mL) compared to the survivor group (1655.00 mL) (P < 0.001). The presence of sepsis was higher in the non-survivor group (40.56%) compared to the survivor group (33.52%) (P = 0.034). Non-survivors had a higher mean heart rate (91.56 bpm) compared to survivors (87.54 bpm) (P = 0.004), and lower mean systolic blood pressure (118.51 mmHg) compared to survivors (123.40 mmHg) (P = 0.003). Diastolic blood pressure was also lower in non-survivors (65.38 mmHg) compared to survivors (70.81 mmHg) (P < 0.001). Non-survivors had a higher mean respiratory rate (21.57 insp/min) compared to survivors (19.58 insp/min) (P < 0.001) and a lower mean temperature (36.46 °C) compared to survivors (36.73 °C) (P < 0.001). The median WBC count was higher in non-survivors (14.30 K/uL) compared to survivors (11.90 K/uL) (P < 0.001). The basic characteristics of the study sample are summarized in Table 1.

Association between the EASIX and the risk of 30-day mortality in patients with AMI

Table 2 illustrates the association between the EASIX and the 30-day mortality risk in AMI patients. The EASIX values were categorized into three tertiles: T1 (< 0.95), T2 ([0.95, 2.46)), and T3 (≥ 2.46), with T1 serving as the reference group. The results demonstrated that higher levels of EASIX were associated with an increased risk of 30-day mortality in AMI patients, with the HR being 1.70 (95% CI: 1.17–2.46, P = 0.005) in T2 group, and the HR being 2.24 (95% CI: 1.56–3.22, P < 0.001) in T3 group. Figure 2 presents the KM survival curves for the association between the EASIX and the 30-day mortality risk in AMI patients with different EASIX values. The KM curves show that patients with higher Log2 (EASIX) levels (T3) have a significantly lower survival rate compared to those with lower levels (T1 and T2).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we investigated the association between the EASIX and the risk of 30-day mortality in patients with AMI using data from the MIMIC IV database. The results demonstrated that higher EASIX levels were associated with an increased risk of 30-day mortality in patients with AMI. Subgroup analyses revealed significant multiplicative interactions between EASIX and variables such as gender and AMI type, suggesting that the combined effect of EASIX and these factors on mortality risk is more pronounced than their individual effects. This study revealed that EASIX may be a robust biomarker that helps identify high-risk AMI patients, potentially guiding more aggressive treatment strategies.

The EASIX has been identified as a significant prognostic factor for various conditions. A study on multiple myeloma patients found that those with high EASIX scores had significantly lower overall survival compared to those with lower scores [20]. Similarly, in patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, a higher EASIX score was linked to increased overall survival (OS) and overall survival (OS) [21]. A multicenter cohort study has found that patients diagnosed with COVID-19 and exhibiting higher EASIX scores at the time of diagnosis have significantly lower OS rates compared to those with lower EASIX scores [17]. In a prospective study, a pre-conditioning EASIX score of ≥ 3 has been identified as a predictor for a population of allogeneic stem cell transplant (alloSCT) recipients, indicating a more than twofold elevation in the risk of treatment-related mortality [22]. The pre-transplant EASIX score can predict the overall mortality in adult patients undergoing single-unit unrelated umbilical cord blood transplantation [23]. In a retrospective cohort study, elevated EASIX scores were significantly correlated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality at both the 28-day and 90-day marks in patients diagnosed with sepsis in the ICU [16]. Our study represents the first to identify the EASIX score was associated with 30-day mortality in patients with AMI.

The potential mechanisms underlying this association can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, EASIX is a marker of endothelial cell activation and damage [24]. Endothelial dysfunction plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of AMI by promoting atherosclerosis, inflammation, and thrombosis [25]. Secondly, creatinine, one of the components of EASIX, is an indicator of renal function [26]. Renal dysfunction is common in patients with cardiovascular diseases and may be associated with worse outcomes in AMI [27]. A study highlights that fluid overload due to impaired renal function contributes to heart failure exacerbation and increased mortality [28]​. Another study indicates that renal dysfunction is a major contributor to the development and worsening of heart failure, which substantially increases mortality risk in these patients ​ [29]. Thirdly, lower platelet counts, another component of EASIX, can be indicative of increased platelet consumption or destruction, which is often seen in severe systemic inflammation or disseminated intravascular coagulation [30]. This condition may lead to increased mortality in AMI patients [31]. In addition, LDH has been evaluated as prognostic factors in AMI in a previous study. Elevated serum LDH levels serve as a robust predictor of cardiac insufficiency in elderly patients following an AMI [32].

The stratified analysis showed the consistent trend of the effect size in all subgroups, indicating the robustness of the association between EASIX and mortality in AMI patients. A possible interaction was also found between EASIX and gender, and AMI types. Gender differences in AMI outcomes are well-documented, with women often experiencing worse long-term mortality compared to men [33, 34]​. Additionally, the type of AMI (STEMI vs. NSTEMI) can influence the prognosis, further affecting the predictive capability of EASIX [35]​. This interaction highlights the need to consider these factors when assessing the risk and tailoring treatment strategies for AMI patients based on their EASIX scores.

Our study elucidates the clinical significance of the EASIX as a novel predictive marker for 30-day mortality risk in AMI patients. This research provides a more refined understanding of risk stratification, enabling clinicians to identify high-risk patients who may benefit from more aggressive treatment strategies. By correlating EASIX scores with mortality outcomes, we offer a method to personalize treatment plans, potentially improving prognoses and reducing mortality rates in AMI patients. Our findings suggest that EASIX could guide the development of personalized treatment plans, including earlier interventional treatments, stricter antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapies, and more vigilant monitoring for patients with higher EASIX scores. This tailored approach may help mitigate mortality risk in high-risk patients. Furthermore, our study paves the way for future research into the role of endothelial cell activation and stress in cardiovascular diseases, potentially stimulating the investigation into the mechanisms by which EASIX influences prognosis and the development of targeted therapies to mitigate these effects. The integration of EASIX into the risk assessment toolkit significantly enhances our ability to predict patient outcomes and tailor treatments accordingly, transforming clinical management and potentially leading to better patient outcomes in the setting of AMI.

The study’s design as a single-center cohort introduces certain limitations. There is an unavoidable element of selection bias that may affect the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the reliance on data from the MIMIC database presents challenges in accessing comprehensive AMI characteristic indicators such as the location and size of the infarction. This limitation may introduce confounding biases that could influence the interpretation of the results. Despite these constraints, the study’s findings offer a significant contribution to the field, underscoring the need for further multicenter, large-scale studies to corroborate and expand upon these preliminary insights.

Conclusion

This study established a significant association between higher EASIX scores and increased 30-day mortality risk in AMI patients. These findings suggest that EASIX may serve as a valuable prognostic marker in clinical practice, potentially guiding more personalized treatment strategies to improve patient outcomes. While further research is needed to evaluate the direct impact of EASIX-based interventions, early identification of high-risk patients could help optimize therapeutic approaches and reduce mortality associated with AMI.
