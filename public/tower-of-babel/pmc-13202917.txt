Introduction

Intracranial Hypertension may be idiopathic (IIH) or due to some underlying cause, such as intracranial space-occupying lesion [1]. Classical clinical features are headache and papilledema. Delayed management can result in progressive, irreversible vision loss and optic atrophy. There are certain groups of drugs that are associated with IIH, such as vitamin A, steroids, thyroid hormone therapy, recombinant human growth hormone, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and antibiotics, such as tetracycline, sulfamethoxazole, gentamicin, cephalexin, and fluoroquinolones. The association of fluoroquinolones and raised intracranial pressure was first reported by Banzas et al. in a child using nalidixic acid [2]. Later on, the use of many other fluoroquinolones was found to be associated with raised intracranial pressure [3, 4]. Fluoroquinolones has bactericidal activity against mycobacterium tuberculosis and is an important drug in the treatment of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB). We report a case of MDR-TBon anti-tubercular therapy, who developed features of raised intracranial pressure as a rare side effect of Levofloxacin therapy.

Case report

A 14-year-old South Asian male presented with holocranial, throbbing headache, for 8 days, worse on lying down. Three days later, he developed bilateral horizontal diplopia with no photophobia, phonophobia, lacrimation, redness in eyes, ocular pain, or diminution of vision. He was diagnosed with multi-drug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) 3 months back and was on a longer regimen of anti-tubercular treatment since, which included Bedaquiline 200 mg (mg) thrice weekly, Linezolid 600 mg once daily (OD), Levofloxacin 1000 mg OD, Cycloserine 750 mg OD, Clofazamine 100 mg OD and Pyridoxine 100 mg OD.

His body mass index was 19.2 kg/m2. On neurological examination, his visual acuity was 6/6 in both eyes with bilateral lateral rectus palsy. Fundoscopy revealed bilateral papilledema (right eye grade 2 and left eye grade 3 according to Modified Frisén Scale) (Fig. 1A, B). The motor, sensory, and cerebellar examination was unremarkable, with no signs of meningeal irritation.

A Fundus photograph showing hyperemic optic disc with blurred, elevated nasal margin with venous engorgement along with surrounding retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) edema (Modified frisén grade 1 papilledema) in right eye. B Fundus examination revealed swollen, hyperemic optic disc with elevated and blurred margins all around, obscuration of segments of the major vessels leaving the disc because of intense RNFL edema and dilated tortuous vessels around the disc (Modified frisén grade 3 papilledema) in left eye. C, D Magnetic resonance imaging and venography revealing normal brain parenchyma and left transverse and sigmoid sinus stenosis. E Fundus photograph showing restoration of normal disc morphology of right eye suggesting resolution of papilledema. F Fundoscopy showing resolving papilledema in left eye with marked improvement in RNFL edema, restoration of disc margins except the presence of mild nasal blurring (Modified frisén grade 1 papilledema)

Laboratory tests showed normal complete blood counts, kidney, liver, thyroid function tests, and D-dimer levels with negative viral markers. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain with contrast and venography revealed normal parenchyma with left transverse and sigmoid sinus stenosis (Fig. 1C, D). His cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) study revealed an opening pressure of 36 cm of water with normal cells, protein, and glucose levels. There was also significant relief in the headache after 30 ml of CSF drainage. CSF Gene Xpert, fungal culture, cryptococcal antigen, and India ink studies were negative. In the absence of obvious underlying etiology and temporal relation to the initiation of anti-tubercular treatment, levofloxacin was suspected as a probable cause of raised intracranial pressure and was replaced by pyrazinamide 1250 mg daily with short course of acetazolamide 250 mg thrice daily for 5 days was given. After 1 week of cessation of levofloxacin, the patient fully recovered from the symptoms, and CSF opening pressure was also reduced to 18 cm of water. Two weeks later, fundoscopy also showed resolution of papilledema (Fig. 1E, F). Patient continued the regimen for MDR-TB with substitution of levofloxacin by pyrazinamide. There was no recurrence of symptoms during further follow-up.

Discussion

This case is about secondary intracranial hypertension, one of the rare side effects of Levofloxacin, in a young male who was receiving anti-tubercular therapy for multi-drug-resistant pulmonary tuberculosis. Initial differential diagnosis kept were meningitis or intracranial space-occupying lesion based on the underlying disease. Clinical examination showed features of raised intracranial pressure, such as papilledema and bilateral lateral rectus palsy. However, a detailed evaluation was unremarkable except for incidentally diagnosed left transverse and sigmoid sinus stenosis. Levofloxacin was thought to be the culprit and was stopped. This led to the improvement in the symptoms of headache and resolution of papilledema in the next 2 weeks.

Levofloxacin (FQ) is an important drug in MDR-TB treatment regimen apart from its routine use in treatment of respiratory and urinary tract infection. There are a dearth of literature showing the association between fluoroquinolones use and intracranial hypertension [2–4]. A recent review of literature by Rissardo et al. found total six cases (4 males, 2 females) with features of raised intracranial pressure, who received treatment with levofloxacin. Three of them had pneumonia and one had tuberculosis, orbital fracture and spondylodiscitis each. The duration between initiations of levofloxacin to onset of intracranial hypertension varied from 3 days to 12 weeks. The resolution of papilledema took from 2 weeks to 5 months. Imaging abnormality was reported in only one patient. Additional risk factors like increased Body Mass Index were present in only 2 cases [5].

The underlying mechanism for fluoroquinolone uses and secondary intracranial hypertension is not well-described. The hypothesis postulated is increased venous sinus pressure and decreased CSF absorption [6]. Fluoroquinolones’ interaction with the neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid, opioid, dopamine, and glutamate receptors is well-described [7]. The regulatory components of CSF production and absorption particularly mitochondrial metabolites (glutamate) and endocrinologic factors (insulin and steroid hormones) become dysfunctional in cases with elevated intracranial pressure [8, 9]. Hence, fluoroquinolones may have some direct effects on the neuro‑endocrinologic factor or in individuals with predisposing factors, leading to an indirect increase in intracranial pressure.

The onset of symptoms of raised ICP after fluoroquinolones use is variable. It ranges from days to weeks. In the present case, it was 9 weeks. The temporal relationship between the resolution of the headache and papilledema in our case further strengthens the causal association between the two. The dose of levofloxacin used in our case (1000 mg per day) was alsohigher than the usual prescribed dose considering the BMI of the patient, which might be the reason for development of intracranial hypertension.

Conclusion

Although rare, clinicians must be aware of this side effect of levofloxacin while evaluating a case with recent onset of features of raised ICP and taking this drug as part of some therapy. One must also consider the BMI of the individual while prescribing a particular dose of levofloxacin as higher dose may serve as a risk factor for development of intracranial hypertension.
