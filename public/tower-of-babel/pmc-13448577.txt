Barriers and facilitators to implementing continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) in primary hospitals in Ethiopia: a qualitative study of healthcare providers’ perspectives
Authors: Hana Abera Hailemariam, Endalew Gemechu Sendo, Abebe Gebremariam Gobezayehu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the barriers and facilitators influencing the implementation of continuous positive airway pressure from the perspective of healthcare providers (HCPs) in primary hospitals of four regions in Ethiopia.DesignA qualitative descriptive study employing thematic analysis of data collected through in-depth interviews from the perspective of HCPs.SettingThe study was conducted in twelve selected primary hospitals in four regions of Ethiopia: Oromia, Amhara, Tigray and Sidama.Participants28 HCPs who worked in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) and delivery rooms, including nurses, midwives, general practitioners, coordinators and medical directors, were interviewed in person and in-depth. Additionally, to ensure a diverse range of viewpoints, participants were identified in collaboration with hospital management and interviewed until data saturation was reached.Key areas of explorationThe study explored participants’ comprehensive experiences concerning the barriers to and facilitators of the implementation of CPAP, as well as their suggestions for enhancing its adoption.ResultsSeven all-encompassing themes emerged: (1) providers’ knowledge and experience, (2) training and mentorship, (3) communication, (4) perceptions of CPAP, (5) equipment availability and accessibility of CPAP, (6) management and infrastructure and (7) provider-driven recommendations. Significant barriers included inadequate and inconsistent training, staff rotation, limited mentorship opportunities, poor interdepartmental communication, misconceptions regarding oxygen requirements, equipment and supply shortages, unreliable power sources and inadequate managerial backing. Facilitators included improved provider familiarity with CPAP indications, structured mentorship initiatives, internal case discussions, backup power allocation to NICUs and prioritisation strategies for critically ill neonates.ConclusionsCPAP implementation in primary hospitals is influenced by interconnected individual, institutional and systemic factors. Sustainable scale-up in resource-limited settings requires practical training, mentorship, staff retention, dedicated funding and infrastructure and strong institutional and external partnerships.

Introduction

Neonatal mortality remains a major global public health concern, with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) accounting for nearly 99% of all neonatal deaths worldwide.1 Sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate share of this burden, largely driven by prematurity, low birth weight (LBW), RDS and neonatal infections.2 3 In Ethiopia, neonatal mortality has shown only a modest decline over the past two decades, remaining at 33 deaths per 1000 live births, reflecting persistent gaps in the availability and quality of inpatient newborn care.4 Prematurity and RDS are among the leading causes of neonatal mortality in Ethiopia, with RDS contributing to nearly half of all prematurity-related deaths.5 6

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is an evidence-based, noninvasive respiratory support intervention that reduces neonatal mortality, the need for mechanical ventilation and surfactant use.7 8 The WHO recommends CPAP as a core intervention for managing preterm and LBW infants with respiratory distress, including in resource-limited settings.9 Over the past decade, Ethiopia has expanded neonatal intensive care services and introduced CPAP at different levels of the health system, including primary hospitals. However, the translation of policy recommendations into routine clinical practice remains inconsistent.10 Primary hospitals are often the first referral point for rural and underserved populations and face unique challenges related to workforce capacity, infrastructure limitations, equipment availability and leadership support.11 12 Evidence from LMICs highlights that effective CPAP implementation is highly context-dependent and shaped by interactions between provider competency, institutional readiness and broader health system factors.13 Commonly reported barriers include inadequate and fragmented training, frequent staff rotation, unreliable power and oxygen supply, shortages of consumables and weak managerial prioritisation.14 15 Conversely, facilitators such as structured training programmes, mentorship, supportive supervision, interprofessional communication and partnerships with academic or non-governmental organisations (NGOs) enhance the uptake and sustainability of these programmes.16 17

Despite national efforts to scale up neonatal respiratory care, empirical evidence examining CPAP implementation in Ethiopian primary hospitals, particularly from the perspective of frontline healthcare providers (HCPs), remains limited. Understanding providers’ experiences is essential for identifying context-specific barriers and facilitators and informing pragmatic strategies for strengthening CPAP integration within routine newborn services. This study explored the barriers to and facilitators of CPAP from the perspective of frontline HCPs.

Methods

Study design and setting

A qualitative descriptive design was employed to capture HCPs’ experiences with CPAP implementation. The qualitative approach is widely regarded as one of the most appropriate methodologies for studies seeking to explore human experiences in depth and interpret individual perceptions within their natural contexts.18

The study was conducted in 12 purposively selected primary hospitals in four regions of Ethiopia: Oromia, Amhara, Tigray and Sidama, participating in the Saving Little Lives (SLL) project.

Participants and sampling

Purposive sampling with a maximum variation technique was employed to select participants with experience in CPAP implementation, ensuring diverse perspectives from HCPs. Maximum variation was maintained within each healthcare worker role, with participants selected based on diverse characteristics, including years of experience, level of education, profession, CPAP expertise, gender, age and hospital setting. At each facility, the principal investigator (PI) and a research assistant contacted the hospital administrator to identify potential participants. Subsequently, these individuals were contacted in person or by phone to explain the study objective, confirm their willingness to participate and schedule interviews.

We ensured that participation was completely voluntary for all HCPs involved. Before starting any interviews, every participant was given a detailed study information sheet (available as online supplemental document 3) and requested to provide written informed consent. Based on the number of healthcare workers involved with CPAP and the limited staff available for neonatal care, particularly in primary hospitals, an average sample size of 6–10 participants was included in each region, as needed to achieve data saturation and capture a variety of perspectives. In total, 28 HCPs were interviewed until data saturation was reached (defined as the point at which no new information emerged).

Interview guide development and validation

The semistructured in-depth interview (IDIs) guide was developed through a systematic, iterative process informed by a comprehensive review of the existing literature on CPAP implementation in LMICs. The PI (HAH), in consultation with the coinvestigators (EGS and AGG), drafted an initial pool of open-ended questions structured around six core domains: provider knowledge and clinical experience with CPAP; training and mentorship exposure; interdepartmental communication and referral practices; perceptions of CPAP among providers and caregivers; equipment availability and infrastructure; and management support and institutional context. The draft was subsequently reviewed by a panel of three content experts with experience in neonatal care and health systems research in Ethiopian primary hospitals, who assessed its relevance, completeness and contextual appropriateness. Revisions addressed terminology clarity, cultural sensitivity and the addition of probing questions in underdeveloped areas. The guide was originally developed in English, translated into Amharic and back-translated by an independent bilingual translator to ensure conceptual equivalence, with discrepancies resolved through team discussion.

Prior to fieldwork, the guide was piloted with three HCPs from primary hospitals outside the study sample. Pilot interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed and reviewed to assess question clarity, logical sequencing, interview duration and the adequacy of probing prompts, after which minor revisions were made; pilot data were excluded from the final analysis. The finalised guide was introduced to the eight research assistants during their 3-day training programme, which incorporated didactic instruction, role-play practice sessions and supervised mock interviews, resulting in minor phrasing refinements to improve accessibility for participants with varying educational backgrounds. The complete interview guide is provided in online supplemental document 1.

Data collection

Semistructured IDIs were conducted from 1 June to 30 June 2025. The flexible semistructured format facilitated open and in-depth expression of participants’ perspectives while ensuring alignment with the study’s central objectives, generating rich, contextually grounded data suitable for robust thematic analysis.19 To ensure high-quality data collection, the study engaged eight research assistants (two from each of the four regions) with a background in neonatal health. The team, comprising clinical nurses, midwives and public health specialists, completed 3 days of intensive training in qualitative research. The IDIs explored knowledge, training experiences, access to equipment, communication patterns, family engagement and the perceived effectiveness of CPAP. Interviews were conducted in private settings, averaged 41 min and were audio recorded with consent. Field notes and reflexive memos provided support for the contextual interpretation. The IDIs were conducted until thematic saturation was achieved, with data collection concluding after interviews with 28 HCPs (see online supplemental document 1).

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis framework.19 Primary coding was conducted by the PI (HAH), who performed open coding iteratively and inductively across all transcripts, followed by axial coding to develop broader themes. To enhance analytical rigour, a sample of the coded transcripts was independently cross-checked by a second analyst (EGS), who reviewed the coding structure and thematic categories for consistency, coherence and interpretive accuracy. Where discrepancies in coding or theme labelling arose between the two analysts, these were resolved through team consensus discussion involving all coinvestigators, without referral to an external arbitrator. Themes were further refined through constant comparison and ongoing dialogue with the advisory team. ATLAS.ti V.9 software was used for data organisation and management. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guideline was used to draft and edit this manuscript, and the completed SRQR checklist 20 is provided in online supplemental document 2.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was ensured by addressing credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability.21 Credibility was strengthened through member checking, transferability through detailed descriptions of the study context and participants, dependability through a comprehensive audit trail and confirmability through peer debriefing and reflexive bracketing to minimise researcher bias.

Research team and reflexivity

The PI (HAH) is a PhD candidate in Nursing at Addis Ababa University and an Assistant Professor of Neonatal Nursing at Saint Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College. She led and coordinated all aspects of this study. The second author (AGG) earned his MD and specialty training in paediatrics from Addis Ababa University and is currently a facultiy member at Emory University, USA. His expertise is in implementation science, particularly examining implementation factors, processes and outcomes. The third author (EGS) is an Associate Professor of Maternal and Reproductive Health and a Senior Lecturer at Addis Ababa University. All team members had prior experience in qualitative research. In this study, reflexivity was maintained through bracketing to prevent personal views and experiences from influencing data collection and analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 28 HCPs participated in the study. The majority were younger than 35 years (64.3%) and female (57.1%). Comprehensive nurses constituted the largest professional group (46.4%), followed by neonatal nurses (25.0%). In terms of position, most were nursing staff (64.3%), while coordinators and medical directors accounted for 25.0% and 10.7%, respectively. Participants were drawn from four regions, with the largest proportion from Oromia (35.7%) and equal representation from Amhara, Sidama and Tigray (21.4% each). Most participants had more than 2 years of clinical experience (71.4%) and the majority had received only informal training (60.7%), with the remaining 39.3% having completed basic NICU training (table 1).

Thematic analysis

Thematic analysis of the IDIs identified core themes that characterised the barriers and facilitators of CPAP implementation in primary hospitals. These themes reflect the complex interplay between individual provider capacity, resource availability and systemic support systems. Seven overarching themes emerged: (1) Knowledge and Experience of Providers, (2) training and mentorship, (3) communication, (4) perceptions of CPAP, (5) equipment availability and accessibility of CPAP, (6) management and infrastructure and (7) provider-driven recommendations (table 2).

Knowledge and experience of providers

Gaps in technical skills, clinical confidence and clarity regarding eligibility criteria were reported as barriers to timely CPAP initiation. Participants described delays in implementation that were linked to limited competence and operational challenges. As one provider stated, “limited technical skills among staff contributed to delays in CPAP initiation… the machine didn’t work at night…” In contrast, improved clinical understanding and familiarity with CPAP indications facilitated proactive use, particularly in high-risk neonates. One participant explained, “We administer prophylactic CPAP for very preterm infants…”

Training and mentorship

Limited, non-focused and non-inclusive training was a major barrier. Participants described insufficient depth and planning in training programmes, with one noting, “The training was not sufficient, lacked details and planning…” Mentorship was described as infrequent and unevenly distributed, particularly excluding night-shift staff: “Mentorship was inadequate and infrequent, often limited to once or twice a year and not reaching all staff, especially night-shift nurses…” In contrast, structured mentorship and peer learning strengthened confidence and competence. As reported, “With mentorship training from the SLL project … now we confidently use CPAP…”

Communication

Poor interdepartmental communication, particularly during referrals, hinders timely CPAP initiation. Participants reported that “babies were referred late and without communication…” However, internal team discussions and experience-sharing sessions facilitated problem-solving and motivation within the NICU. One provider described, “We held morning discussions to solve challenges…”

Perception of CPAP intervention

Provider concerns regarding oxygen toxicity and equipment limitations influenced the implementation. Providers expressed fear of potential ‘oxygen toxicity’, particularly in settings where oxygen regulators and blending systems were unavailable or unreliable. One participant explained that there were “concerns about oxygen toxicity due to absence of regulators…”

Family perceptions also significantly influenced CPAP use. Participants described widespread misconceptions among caregivers, who often associated CPAP with imminent death rather than with recovery. As one provider explained, “…families think oxygen or CPAP is for dying babies…”

Despite these concerns, observed clinical effectiveness strengthened provider trust in the intervention: “we observed its (CPAP) effectiveness in saving lives…” Family education and counselling were also essential in improving acceptance, with one participant noting, “After counseling, they fully accept the treatment (CPAP)…”

Equipment availability and accessibility

The limited availability of CPAP machines and accessories was a major constraint. One participant reported, “We admitted 20 preterm cases but had only one CPAP…” Human resource shortages and role confusion, particularly during night shifts, further limited implementation: “night-duty nurses from other units were unable to implement CPAP…” Facilitators included the allocation of backup generators and case prioritisation strategies. For example, “backup generators were specifically allocated to NICU…”, and “we prioritize neonates based on the ABC rule…”

Management and infrastructure

Weak leadership support, limited budget allocation and infrastructural gaps have been reported as systemic barriers. One participant stated, “There is a lack of support from higher officials…” The high cost of CPAP equipment was described as a substantial barrier to sustained implementation, particularly in resource-constrained primary healthcare facilities. Participants explained that CPAP machines, oxygen regulators, air–oxygen blenders, humidifiers, nasal prongs, circuits and other essential accessories require significant financial investments. However, these items are often not prioritised within the hospital’s annual budget allocation. As one provider stated, “…CPAP equipment is costly and not prioritized in the hospital budget, so necessary equipment and accessories are often unavailable when needed….”

Partnerships and quality improvement initiatives facilitate service strengthening. Participants emphasised, “forming partnerships and facilitating mentorship…” Collaborative efforts were perceived to improve outcomes, as illustrated by the statement: “Our partnership with Emory University improves care and lowers mortality despite limited feedback from officials…this collaboration benefits the hospital, NICU, and patients….”

Providers’ recommendations

Participants recommended strengthening staff capacity through practical, evidence-based CPAP training covering machine operation, parameters, alarms, oxygen use, scoring, cleaning and guideline development, to ensure confident bedside care. As one provider stated, “…NICU staff need practical, evidence-based CPAP training on machine operation, parameters, alarms, oxygen use, scoring, cleaning, and guideline development to ensure confident bedside care….”

They also emphasised the importance of retaining trained NICU staff, minimising unnecessary rotations and supporting career development to maintain expertise and motivation. “…retain trained NICU staff and avoid unnecessary rotation…” Additional recommendations included strengthening NICU autonomy, ensuring oxygen readiness, promoting mentorship, avoiding unnecessary referrals and pursuing partnerships and resource sharing. As one provider noted, “…It is recommended to avoid unnecessary referrals, strengthen NICU autonomy with dedicated resources, ensure oxygen readiness, promote mentorship, and pursue partnerships and resource sharing…”

Participants also highlighted that external academic partnerships strengthened CPAP service delivery and improved neonatal outcomes, as one provider noted: “…and our partnership with Emory improves care and lowers mortality despite limited feedback from officials…. This collaboration benefits the hospital, NICU, and patients….”

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive understanding of the multilevel factors influencing CPAP implementation in Ethiopian primary hospitals from the perspective of HCPs. Consistent with implementation research from other low-resource settings, CPAP implementation was shaped by provider competency, organisational processes, infrastructure readiness and leadership engagement.12 22 Limited provider knowledge, technical skills and uncertainty regarding CPAP eligibility criteria were the key barriers to timely initiation. Similar findings have been reported in Malawi and Kenya, where short, non-specialised training and high staff turnover reduced providers’ confidence in initiating and monitoring CPAP use.23 24 Inadequate competence may also delay the recognition of early respiratory distress, leading to missed opportunities for effective intervention.12 In contrast, familiarity with the eligibility criteria and clinical algorithms facilitated timely CPAP initiation, particularly among very preterm infants. This aligns with evidence demonstrating that standardised respiratory distress scoring systems empower providers and reduce delays in care.24

Providers’ awareness of potential CPAP-related complications further suggests the need for emerging clinical vigilance, which can enhance patient safety when supported by adequate mentorship and supervision.23 Training and mentorship were identified as central determinants of CPAP implementation. Participants described training as infrequent, insufficiently practical and inconsistently inclusive of all cadres and shifts, echoing findings from systematic reviews of LMICs.15 22 Conversely, structured mentorship and peer learning, particularly through project-supported and hub-and-spoke models, enhanced provider confidence and skill retention.16 25

Communication gaps within facilities and during referrals hinder consistent CPAP use, consistent with studies from Pakistan and Kenya reporting unclear role delineation and weak interprofessional coordination.24 26 However, internal case discussions and experience-sharing meetings facilitated collective problem-solving and motivation, reinforcing the evidence that multidisciplinary collaboration is critical for successful neonatal interventions.27

Provider and caregiver perceptions significantly influence CPAP uptake. Concerns regarding oxygen toxicity and the belief that CPAP requires higher-level facilities mirror findings from India and sub-Saharan Africa.28 29 Nevertheless, providers’ direct observation of improved neonatal outcomes reinforced trust in CPAP, consistent with studies demonstrating reduced mortality and length of NICU stay.7 8 Caregiver misconceptions associating CPAP with imminent death and concerns over additional costs limit acceptance, as reported in Malawi and other LMICs.23 30 Providers emphasised that counselling and family education prior to initiation improved acceptance, supporting the WHO’s recommendations for family-centred care for preterm infants.31

Equipment shortages, unreliable electricity and oxygen supply constraints were pervasive barriers, aligning with systematic reviews that highlighted supply chain and infrastructure deficits as major obstacles to neonatal respiratory support.12 32 Human resource shortages and role ambiguity, particularly during night shifts, further compromised service continuity, consistent with global newborn health frameworks.1 Leadership engagement and institutional prioritisation strongly influence sustainability. Weak managerial commitment and inconsistent budget allocation undermine CPAP services, whereas supportive leadership, quality improvement initiatives and partnerships enhance implementation.17 33

External collaborations with academic institutions provide critical training, mentorship and equipment support, consistent with evidence demonstrating improved neonatal outcomes through partnership-driven models.16 34

Overall, the findings underscore that effective CPAP implementation in primary hospitals requires a comprehensive, context-sensitive approach that addresses provider capacity, caregiver engagement, communication systems, infrastructure readiness and leadership commitment.

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, the qualitative descriptive design, while appropriate for exploring provider experiences, does not permit causal inference or generalisation to the broader population of primary hospitals in Ethiopia. Second, as participants were recruited exclusively from hospitals enrolled in the SLLs project, the findings may have limited transferability to facilities without access to structured external support, potentially overstating the facilitators associated with partnership-driven implementation. Third, the exclusion of caregivers, policymakers and supply chain stakeholders means important perspectives on CPAP implementation remain unexplored, thereby constraining the comprehensiveness of the findings. Fourth, although confidentiality measures were in place, social desirability bias cannot be entirely ruled out, as participants may have modified their responses in the presence of research assistants or in relation to a project with which their hospitals were affiliated. Finally, data were collected over a single 1-month period, which may not capture seasonal or longitudinal variations in implementation experiences, equipment availability or staffing patterns.

Implications for policy and clinical practice

These findings underscore the need for health authorities to prioritise CPAP as an essential neonatal intervention in primary hospitals through standardised, competency-based training, clear national clinical guidelines and strengthened human resource policies that support staff retention in NICUs. Dedicated budget allocation for CPAP equipment, consumables, oxygen systems and reliable power supply is critical to ensure sustainability beyond external projects, whereas structured mentorship and hub-and-spoke partnerships can support ongoing capacity building.

Clinically, improving provider competence, communication and family counselling can facilitate timely CPAP initiation, enhance caregiver acceptance, reduce unnecessary referrals and improve survival outcomes for preterm and LBW neonates, contributing to a reduction in preventable neonatal morbidity and mortality in resource-limited settings.
