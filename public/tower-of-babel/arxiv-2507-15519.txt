Switching States: Heteroclinic Cycles as Organising Centres of Neuronal Dynamics
Kateryna Nechyporenko, Peter Ashwin, Krasimira Tsaneva-Atanasova

Abstract
Neuronal networks alternate between high- and low-activity regimes, known as up and down states. They also display rhythmic patterns essential for perception, memory consolidation, and sensory processing. Despite their importance, the principles behind such state transitions remain elusive. We propose necessary conditions for the existence of a novel bifurcation structure as a universal organising centre governing these transitions. Bifurcation analysis and simulations of canonical mean-field network models, including Wilson–Cowan, Tsodyks–Markram, and Jansen–Rit frameworks, show that this bifurcation structure emerges robustly across models. We demonstrate that the interplay between external input and (synaptic) connectivity converges onto this shared mechanism, providing a fundamental principle for understanding how diverse brain states arise and are regulated. Beyond phenomenological mean-field models, we show that a shared mathematical structure of nonlinear input-output relationships, rather than model-specific details, preserves the organising centre across frameworks, revealing a general mechanism for dynamic state transitions.

Introduction

Neuronal activity often exhibits transitions between distinct, experimentally observable regimes, each characterised by unique dynamical properties. Understanding how such transitions arise and persist is central to deciphering brain function. Dynamical systems modelling and theory offer the tools necessary to investigate and interpret such complex dynamics. In particular, phenomenological mean-field models of neuronal network activity have proven valuable in exploring the mechanisms governing transitions between neural states, offering a bridge between observed time series data and the underlying physiological processes
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

Neuronal network dynamics arise due to broadly defined excitatory (positive feedback) and inhibitory (negative feedback) interactions. Such interactions have inspired the development of various phenomenological mean-field models that capture the collective behaviour of neural populations. The Wilson-Cowan model remains a foundational framework for cortical dynamics, describing interactions between excitatory and inhibitory populations via a reduction to firing rate equations
[
5
,
6
]
. In contrast, models based on Tsodyks-Markram framework shift the focus to the synaptic level, modelling short-term plasticity through neurotransmitter depletion (synaptic depression) and recovery (synaptic facilitation)
[
7
,
8
]
. Extensions such as the Mongillo-Barak-Tsodyks model incorporate calcium-dependent facilitation
[
9
]
, Markram-Wang-Tsodyks model refines the original framework’s dynamics of depression and facilitation
[
10
]
, while the Loebel-Silberberg incorporates multiple vesicle pool dynamics
[
11
]
.

Another widely used class of neural mean-field models includes Jansen-Rit framework, designed to reproduce features of electroencephalogram (EEG) and local field potential (LFP) recordings
[
12
]
. The Jansen-Rit model is structured as a network of pyramidal neurons receiving input from both inhibitory and excitatory interneuron populations, with model equations representing a realisation of Wilson-Cowan framework
[
13
]
. Other variations of the model include Robinson model
[
14
]
, Wendling model
[
15
]
, Liley model
[
16
,
17
]
and Freeman model
[
18
]
.

Across this range of modelling approaches and applications, a central challenge remains: Can one understand how network activity gives rise to metastable dynamics, transient yet structured states, without relying on chaotic mechanisms? Recent perspective
[
19
]
highlights two non-chaotic mechanisms underlying metastable dynamics: bistability, where the activity states switch between two coexisting attractors due to noise or external inputs; and heteroclinic dynamics, where the system moves along the trajectories connecting unstable attractors (saddles). While bistability provides robustness via sustained attractors
[
20
]
, heteroclinic dynamics supports order and timing through the sequential transitions
[
21
,
22
]
. Both approaches are, however, not mutually exclusive. Winnerless competition (WLC) is a dynamical systems concept that bridges bistability and heteroclinic dynamics, demonstrating how the system with nonsymmetric inhibition and competition can switch between winner-takes-all behaviour (bistability) and sequential switching among states
[
23
,
24
]
.

This duality plays a key role in modelling memory and cognition. Deco and Rolls proposed a model in which sequential memory arises from attractor dynamics and sequential transitions between neural states, driven by short-term synaptic depression
[
25
]
. In other work, Friston and Buzsáki argue that hippocampus-prefrontal system employs complementary stability mechanisms, namely, bistability, to maintain dynamical stability and sequential dynamics for temporal ordering
[
26
]
.

While bistability and heteroclinic dynamics are identified as key mechanisms in brain state transition, there is no unifying theory of non-chaotic metastability dynamics. To address this gap, we introduce a novel dynamic mechanism (SNIC
2
) that bridges bistable and sequential dynamics and explains transitions between different dynamic states such as the up and down states that are integral to how the brain processes information. We provide the necessary conditions that give rise to this phenomenon as well as demonstrate that SNIC
2
is present in canonical mean-field models, such as Wilson-Cowan, Tsodyks-Markram and Jansen-Rit. Our results offer a deeper understanding of the fundamental structures that shape the dynamics of mean-field neuronal network models.

2.1
Conserved bifurcation structure across phenomenological mean-field models of neural activity

Phenomenological mean-field models of brain dynamics can exhibit a range of dynamic behaviours, including high activity state (up state), quiescence (down state), oscillations (limit cycle), and bistability (co-existence of up and down states). We propose a new dynamical system mechanism, which we term SNIC
2
, that serves as an organising centre that gives rise to the transition between these states (
Figure 1
A

2

2

[

point out that a heteroclinic cycle involving two saddle nodes becomes codimension-two if it forms a hyperbolic invariant circle, which corresponds to the SNIC
2
scenario (see

A

\displaystyle\textbf{(NC1)}\quad\exists\,x_{\min},x_{\max}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ such that:}

J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}f_{x}(x,y)&f_{y}(x,y)\\

Wilson and Cowan demonstrated that their model is capable of supporting up to five equilibrium points, although these do not necessarily coexist simultaneously

. In the WC model the variables

In computational neuroscience, sigmoidal functions are commonly used to model the population-averaged response of neurons. These functions capture the nonlinear relationship between presynaptic input and the probability of neuronal firing, effectively translating input into bounded output
[
28

Phase space analysis of phenomenological mean-field models reveals that the nullcline associated with the excitatory population exhibits non-monotonic behaviour due to self-excitation, characterised by a local maximum and a local minimum. The combination of these two characteristics allows to provide the conditions that give rise to the SNIC

In the case of Wilson-Cowan model the presence of two saddle node equilibria also provides an equilibrium in between the two saddle nodes (

Given the SNIC

2

Mathematically, this corresponds to the inhibitory population’s response function having a larger maximal derivative compared to that of the excitatory population, resulting in a steeper input–output relationship and thus greater sensitivity to parameter variations. This configuration is characteristic of an inhibition-stabilised network (ISN) that is a recurrent neural circuit where the recurrent excitatory connections are so strong that, by themselves, they would drive runaway excitation and instability. Stability is then restored by equally strong (or stronger) feedback inhibition. This is typical in brain regions, such as the primary visual cortex and hippocampus, where excitation and inhibition dynamically track each other closely (often referred to as “balanced networks”), and hence the network operates in an ISN regime
[

31

2.3
Universality of the phenomenon

We have shown that the topology of the transitions between dynamical states that are driven by variations in connectivity (synaptic) strength and external input is preserved across the three phenomenological mean-field models (Wilson–Cowan, Tsodyks–Markram, and Jansen–Rit) and is structured by the underlying SNIC
2
bifurcation mechanism. A common feature of these modelling frameworks is the choice of a sigmoidal-function-type nonlinearity describing the interactions between network components (in our case neuronal populations). This is sufficient to satisfy the necessary conditions
NC1-NC2
for emergence of the SNIC
2
phenomenon, while capturing nonlinear input-output relationships in a biologically plausible way. However, the SNIC
2
necessary conditions are general and could be satisfied by other types of functions and modelling paradigms. For example, we are able to detect a SNIC
2
point in the Morris-Lecar model
[
38
]
that describes the electrical activity of excitable cells, such as neurons or muscle fibres, using a two-dimensional system of nonlinear differential equations that captures voltage dynamics and ion channel kinetics to reproduce spiking and bursting behaviours. This model features a hyperbolic tangent function that is akin to the sigmoid (see
Morris-Lecar model: electrical activity of a single neuron
and
Figure 6
). Here, we likewise obtain a 2-parameter bifurcation diagram with a topology similar to that of the phenomenological mean-field network models by varying the external input to the cell and the maximal potassium conductance, which effectively modulates the strength of the potassium current. This finding supports the universality of the phenomenon and the underlying dynamical systems mechanisms. In each of the four cases, a parameter that we vary represents the strength of an external drive to the system, such as afferent or inputs from other brain regions. The other parameter we vary in the mean-field models considered here stands for the average connectivity (synaptic) strength. In the single-cell model, we vary the strength of the potassium current, which modulates the repolarising influence exerted on the membrane potential. Therefore, in our analysis, one of the parameters we vary governs the intrinsic properties of the system, while the other modulates the extrinsic (or external) drive. It is the interplay between these intrinsic dynamics and external inputs that gives rise to the transition between the distinct dynamic regimes we uncover here.

3
Discussion

In this paper, we propose a novel organising mechanism (SNIC
2
) that governs a wide variety of transitions between up and down states, oscillatory dynamics, and bistability in neuronal networks, driven by changes in external input and connection (synaptic) strength. We identify the necessary conditions for its existence in two-dimensional systems and demonstrate its presence in three phenomenological mean-field models (Wilson-Cowan, Tsodyks-Markram, Jansen-Rit). These findings suggest that SNIC
2
is a central, model-independent organising principle in dynamical systems theory, offering a useful framework for state transitions in neuronal networks and beyond through low-dimensional bifurcation structures.

The introduced phenomenon builds on existing theory on non-chaotic mechanisms of metastability by proposing a unifying framework that connects noise-driven bistability with sequential transitions structured by heteroclinic cycles, all within a minimal two-dimensional model. For example,
[
19
]
employs different dynamical systems to illustrate bistability vs sequential transitions, notably, with a heteroclinic cycle realised by a three-dimensional dynamical system. However, SNIC
2
phenomenon also captures these transitions in an unifying manner. A related model of sequential transitions between neuronal network states was previously explored in research on winnerless competition, where the system exhibits a transition from sequential switching to winner-takes-all dynamics
[
23
,
24
]
; we note that winnerless competition phenomena are also of high codimension unless there are invariant subspaces imposed by modelling considerations.

In the dynamical systems literature, studies of heteroclinic phenomena in planar (two-dimensional) case have primarily focused on loops involving saddle equilibria, with considerably less attention given to the heteroclinic cycles between non-hyperbolic (saddle node) equilibria
[
39
,
27
]
, probably due to the need for extra parameters to make these persist in models. Moreover, many heteroclinic phenomena are yet to find application in the context of biologically or physically motivated models. Here, we address this gap by demonstrating the emergence of heteroclinic cycle phenomenon in two-dimensional phenomenological mean-field models (Wilson-Cowan
[
5
]
and reduced Tsodyks-Markram
[
7
,
20
]
) as well as in a single-neuron model (Morris-Lecar
[
38
]
). Our results highlight a previously unexplored connection between dynamical systems theory of heteroclinic cycles and its physiological relevance to understanding switches between activity modes in the brain.

From a dynamical systems perspective, the SNIC
2
phenomenon opens a new avenue for studying the interplay between excitability, multistability, and periodic behaviour in higher dimensions. Our analysis identifies both planar and higher-dimensional neuronal network models where centre manifolds of saddle nodes are otherwise stable. It is, however, of course possible that one or both saddle nodes could have unstable non-central eigendirections in which case we expect a much richer range of possibilities may arise. Previously, we have highlighted a ”SNICeroclinic bifurcation” heteroclinic bifurcation involving a saddle and a saddle node in the GTPase model, where the resulting loop was unstable
[
34
]
, suggesting that unstable SNIC
2
dynamics can play a role, for example, on basin boundaries. In higher-dimensional systems, SNIC
2
allows for even richer configurations. Here, we have identified a SNIC
2
phenomenon in the higher-dimensional Jansen–Rit model
[
12
]
, which mirrors the bifurcation topology found in the two-dimensional case, characterised by non-zero eigenvalues at the saddle nodes.

From an application standpoint, the significance of our findings is potentially far-reaching across several domains in neuroscience, medicine, and artificial intelligence. Disorders such as epilepsy
[
40
,
41
]
, schizophrenia
[
42
]
, and depression
[
43
,
44
]
are often marked by abnormal transitions between brain network activity states. Identifying an organising centre that governs these transitions could help in pinpointing what goes wrong in these conditions, potentially leading to biomarkers or targets for intervention. Since up and down states are associated with slow-wave sleep and memory consolidation
[
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
]
, insights into their control mechanisms could inform treatments for sleep disorders or cognitive decline (e.g., in Alzheimer’s disease). Understanding the dynamical principles organising transitions in neuronal networks can inform how deep brain stimulation (DBS), transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), or closed-loop neurofeedback protocols are applied, especially for conditions like Parkinson’s or treatment-resistant depression. Clinicians could tailor stimulation parameters to push the system toward desired activity states enabled through modelling patient-specific dynamics and identifying how close a network is to a bifurcation point. Techniques such as bifurcation analysis and mean-field modelling provide a theoretical framework to decode brain-wide dynamics from macro scale recordings
[
1
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
, potentially improving diagnostics or real-time brain monitoring (e.g., in ICU or surgery). This approach enables the connection of microscopic mechanisms (synapses) to macroscopic outcomes (cognitive state transitions), therefore helping to unify models across scales, which is a major challenge in systems neuroscience. Understanding how real neuronal networks switch states via global dynamic structures (rather than local rules) offers valuable insights for designing adaptive, energy-efficient neuromorphic hardware or AI models that can switch between exploration and exploitation modes, or between learning and inference. Embedding similar principles in artificial systems could lead to context-aware computation, where the “brain” switches into different operating modes depending on task demands or sensory context, just like biological systems.

In summary, we introduce a universally applicable organising principle that governs how the brain toggles between different modes of operation. This could find concrete applications in:

•

Diagnosing and treating brain disorders.

•

Developing more effective brain-computer interfaces and neuromodulation tools.

•

Enhancing our fundamental understanding of cognition and consciousness.

•

Designing smarter and more brain-like AI.
