Bioengineered titanium implants functionalized with aptamer-valproic acid conjugates orchestrate macrophage programming and mesenchymal stem cell homing for improved osseointegration
Authors: Danhe Sun, Quan Zhou, Xiaoling Guo, Hang Shi, Xiaoyan Wang, Xinkai Li, Zhihuang Zheng, Linfeng Zhong, Senhu Cao, Ruifan Shan, Zexu Su, Chufan Ma, Lingzhou Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional titanium implants often exhibit limited bioactivity, failing to adequately modulate the immune response and promote endogenous regeneration, which leads to suboptimal osseointegration. To address this, we developed a multifunctional implant (NTP/Apt-VPA) by conjugating Apt19s—an mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-specific aptamer—with valproic acid (VPA), anchored onto a polydopamine-coated TiO2 nanotubular surface. The NTP/Apt-VPA implant demonstrated exceptional capabilities in scavenging reactive oxygen species, programming macrophages toward the regenerative M2 phenotype, and enhancing the recruitment and osteogenic differentiation of MSCs. Through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo assessments, as well as RNA sequencing analysis, we observed that the modified implant was associated with accelerated early inflammation resolution, enhanced stem cell homing, and activation of key osteogenic pathways, including PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling. This study presents a bioengineering strategy that aims to synchronize immunomodulation with osteogenesis through an Apt19s-VPA conjugate, which may offer a promising platform for enhancing implant osseointegration.

Introduction

Rising life expectancy has increased the prevalence of dental and orthopedic disorders, driving demand for high-performance implants [1]. Titanium (Ti) and its alloys are widely used in orthopedic and dental applications due to their fatigue resistance, biocompatibility, and low modulus, but their bioinertness often limits osseointegration, particularly in patients with comorbidities like osteoporosis or diabetes [[2], [3], [4]]. To address this, surface modification strategies have been developed to enhance bioactivity. Osseointegration involves a complex interplay between the implant surface and the biological environment, including immune response, vascularization, and bone formation [5]. Macrophages play a key role: M1 cells exacerbate inflammation, while M2 cells promote tissue repair [6]. Meanwhile, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) recruitment and osteogenic differentiation are critical for successful osseointegration [7].

Early approaches focused on topographical modifications, such as TiO2 nanotubes (NTs), which can promote macrophage M2 polarization and MSC osteogenesis [8,9]. However, topography-only strategies lack biochemical specificity for directing endogenous MSC homing and offer modest, transient immunomodulation, especially in highly inflammatory microenvironments [10]. Thus, biochemical functionalization has emerged as a complementary strategy. Immobilized biomolecules enable precise receptor-mediated control over cell behaviors. For example, aptamer-functionalized surfaces can capture circulating stem cells via interacting with specific markers on cells [11]. Apt19s recognizes a membrane-associated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) isoform (ALPL) highly expressed on MSCs, enabling selective recruitment [12].

Valproic acid (VPA), a histone deacetylase inhibitor, shows dual therapeutic benefits when delivered locally: it promotes osteogenic gene expression in MSCs and drives macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. M2 macrophages further enhance MSC recruitment and differentiation via secreted factors, creating a positive feedback loop [[13], [14], [15]]. Critically, the local, sustained delivery system employed in our study—achieved via covalent immobilization of the Apt19s-VPA conjugates onto the polydopamine (PDA)-coated nanotubular surface—is designed to harness these therapeutic benefits while avoiding the systemic toxicities associated with chronic oral VPA administration.

Direct immobilization of Apt19s-VPA onto bare NTs suffers from low loading and poor stability. To address this, we introduced a PDA interlayer, which enables covalent immobilization of Apt19s-VPA. In addition, the PDA interlayer possesses intrinsic reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging properties that mitigate oxidative stress in the inflammatory microenvironment while enabling sustained, pH-responsive release of the Apt19s-VPA conjugates. Based on this design, we developed a biofunctionalized Ti implant combining TiO2 NTs, a PDA interlayer, and an Apt19s-VPA conjugate (Fig. 1). TiO2 NTs were first fabricated on Ti substrates by electrochemical anodization. The PDA interlayer then provides anchoring sites for covalent immobilization of the Apt19s-VPA conjugate via Michael addition and Schiff base reactions. This work presents the first covalent conjugation of the MSC-specific aptamer Apt19s with the epigenetic modulator VPA on a Ti surface, creating a multifunctional platform that concurrently achieves ROS scavenging, M2 macrophage polarization, and MSC recruitment, thereby offering a promising strategy to improve osseointegration.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the preparation and application of the NTP/Apt-VPA Ti implant. Results and discussion

Surface characterization

Surface morphology was characterized by field-emisson scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) (Fig. 2A). The Ti control group showed typical machining grooves from mechanical polishing. Electrochemical anodization produced highly ordered, vertically aligned TiO2 NTs with uniform pore openings (∼80 nm in diameter) and an average length of ∼238 nm (Fig. S1), confirming successful nanostructuring. Notably, 80 nm TiO2 NTs have been associated with enhanced bioactivity and reduced inflammation [16]. PDA modification (NTP group) reduced nanotube diameter and increased wall thickness, attributed to PDA's strong adhesion and uniform coating capability [17]. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) quantification (Fig. 2B and C) showed that all samples exhibited average surface roughness (Ra) below 10 nm. Although micron-scale roughness is low, the nanotubular surfaces possess distinct nanoscale features—∼80 nm pore openings (Fig. S1)—which are known to influence cellular responses independently of micron-scale topography [18]. Contact angle measurements (Fig. 2D, Fig. S2) revealed that the Ti group had moderate hydrophilicity (58.08°). After functionalization, contact angles ranged from 44.81° to 53.59°, a modest change expected to have minimal impact on biological responses.

Interfacial adhesion strength tests indicated that PDA functionalization slightly improved coating strength, supporting the robustness of the modified interfaces (Fig. 2E). The NT group showed a bonding strength of 27.65 ± 4.91 MPa, which increased to 33.93 ± 2.54 MPa after PDA modification (NTP). Further functionalization with Apt19s (NTP/Apt) or Apt19s-VPA (NTP/Apt-VPA) yielded slight additional improvements (34.12 ± 3.50 and 34.16 ± 2.19 MPa, respectively). This enhanced adhesion is attributed to covalent bonding via Michael addition and Schiff base reactions between the thiol-modified aptamer and the quinone/catechol groups of PDA. Micro-scratch tests (Fig. 2O, Fig. S3) revealed no coating delamination, cracking, or adhesive failure within the applied load range. Scratch tracks remained intact (Fig. S3), and friction force-displacement curves were smooth without abrupt jumps (Fig. 2O), confirming excellent adhesion stability. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the Apt19s-VPA conjugate is stably anchored onto the NTP surface via covalent bonds.

Chemical characterization was performed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Raman spectroscopy. XPS survey spectra (Fig. 2F) revealed a distinct N 1s peak and attenuated Ti 2p signals in NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA samples, indicating effective PDA coverage over TiO2 NTs. Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 2M) confirmed PDA modification via characteristic D (1396 cm−1) and G (1567 cm−1) bands. However, XPS and Raman showed limited sensitivity for detecting aptamer conjugation, likely due to surface densities below detection thresholds (<0.1 wt%).

High-resolution C 1s spectra (Fig. 2G–J) showed enhanced C-O/C-N signals at 286 eV and a negative shift (0.33 eV) in the quinone C=O peak for NTP versus NT, indicating PDA oxidation and substrate interaction. O 1s spectra (Fig. 2H–K) revealed a higher C-O/C=O ratio in NTP. N 1s spectra (Fig. 2I) identified three nitrogen species: -NH2+ (401.85 eV), C-N (400.11 eV), and C=N (398.77 eV). Additionally, a P-O signal at 133.51 eV in the P 2p spectrum (Fig. 2L) confirmed successful Apt19s-VPA deposition. These results support a two-step interfacial mechanism involving Michael addition and Schiff base condensation under alkaline conditions.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis (Fig. 2N) provided further evidence. NT displayed a Ti-O band at 1047 cm−1, while NTP showed PDA-specific signatures (1665, 1267, 1099 cm−1). NTP/Apt exhibited subtle variations at 1543 and 693 cm−1. Crucially, NTP/Apt-VPA displayed definitive VPA bands (3312, 2973/2884, 1659 cm−1), confirming successful Apt19s-VPA complexation. Second-derivative spectra (inset) reinforced the interaction sequence: Ti-O → PDA → aptamer → VPA.

Collectively, we successfully developed a multi-step biofunctionalization strategy that enables covalent immobilization of the Apt19s‑VPA complex via PDA while preserving the underlying nanotopography. No significant differences in Ra were observed among the four nanotubular groups (p > 0.05), whereas polished Ti exhibited slightly lower roughness. Given the comparable nanoscale topography across all functionalized surfaces (Ra < 10 nm, similar pore size), any biological differences observed among the NT, NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt‑VPA groups are attributed to their specific chemical modifications, not to differences in physical surface properties.

Analysis of Apt19s loading and release

The surface loading and release kinetics of the aptamer were quantified using fluorescence tracking with fluorescein amidite (FAM)-labeled Apt19s. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) confirmed successful immobilization, as evidenced by distinct green fluorescence on Apt19s-FAM-modified surfaces, whereas the control group showed negligible fluorescence (Fig. 2Q and R). The abundant catechol and amine groups on the PDA coating facilitate high loading capacity via π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding, while covalent conjugation through Michael addition and Schiff base reactions enhances binding stability [19].

A standard curve derived from FAM fluorescence was used to quantify the cumulative release of Apt19s under varying pH conditions (Fig. 2P, Fig. S4). The release kinetics exhibited strong pH dependence. Under physiologically neutral conditions (pH 7.4), a sustained and gradual release profile was observed, whereas under weakly acidic conditions (pH 6.0)—mimicking an inflammatory microenvironment—a significant burst release occurred within the first 4 days, followed by sustained release over 34 days. Notably, this release behavior aligns intelligently with the physiological stages of bone healing: the acidic inflammatory phase triggers an initial aptamer burst to enhance MSC recruitment, and the subsequent neutral reparative phase facilitates sustained release to support continued MSC homing and osteogenic differentiation. The initial loading density of Apt19s may influence the release kinetics, an aspect that warrants further investigation.

Macrophage reprogramming on modified Ti surfaces

RAW 264.7 viability, proliferation, and morphology

An integrated assessment of macrophage viability and proliferation on the engineered Ti surfaces was conducted using Live/Dead staining and Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays. Live/Dead imaging (Fig. 3A) demonstrated nearly 100% cell viability across all sample groups. CCK-8 proliferation kinetics (Fig. 3E) revealed a biphasic pattern: at 24 h, the NTP/Apt and NTP/Apt-VPA groups showed enhanced proliferative activity relative to Ti controls (p < 0.001). By 72 h, however, the NT, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA groups exhibited significant suppression of proliferation compared to the Ti control - a phenomenon potentially attributable to surface functionalization-induced early commitment toward differentiation [20]. Antioxidant performance of modified Ti surfaces

Following skeletal trauma, macrophages typically adopt a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and generate excessive levels of ROS. Uncontrolled ROS accumulation not only triggers apoptosis in osteoprogenitors and mature osteoblasts but also suppresses osteogenic gene expression. Such persistent oxidative stress ultimately compromises bone repair and poses a critical challenge to successful osseointegration. To address this issue, we modeled acute inflammation using lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages and examined the immunomodulatory and ROS-scavenging capacities of the engineered Ti surfaces.

ROS production was first assessed by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 3C) and flow cytometry (Fig. 3D). Consistent with the fluorescence images, flow cytometric analysis showed that NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA surfaces markedly suppressed LPS-induced ROS generation compared to Ti controls. To provide a quantitative comparison, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of DCFH-DA in the fluorescence microscopy images was analyzed using ImageJ software (Fig. S5). This quantitative analysis revealed that, relative to the Ti group, the MFI of the NT group showed no significant difference, whereas it was reduced by 88.3% in the NTP group, 84.4% in the NTP/Apt group, and 93.0% in the NTP/Apt-VPA group (all p < 0.001 vs. Ti). Moreover, the NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA groups all exhibited significantly lower intracellular ROS levels compared to the NT group (p < 0.01). These results suggest the potent ROS-scavenging capacity of PDA-containing coatings, with the NTP/Apt-VPA surface showing the most pronounced antioxidant effect. This antioxidant effect can be primarily attributed to the presence of PDA. The catechol groups within the PDA structure act as efficient radical scavengers, neutralizing free radicals and thereby mitigating oxidative stress and protecting surrounding cells from ROS-mediated damage [22].

Evaluation of macrophage polarization on modified Ti surfaces

In the local microenvironment following implant placement, macrophages act as central regulators, orchestrating inflammatory responses, wound healing processes, and both innate and adaptive immunity [23]. Depending on specific biochemical cues and microenvironmental signals, macrophages undergo polarization into distinct functional phenotypes—primarily the classically activated pro-inflammatory M1 type and the alternatively activated anti-inflammatory M2 type [24]. Under physiological healing conditions, macrophage polarization is dynamically and temporally regulated across successive stages of tissue repair, enabling them to perform phase-appropriate biological functions [25]. However, upon implant implantation, associated oxidative stress can disrupt local immune homeostasis, leading to abnormally sustained M1 polarization [26]. M1 macrophages secrete high levels of inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which, although beneficial for pathogen clearance, may also provoke chronic inflammation at the implant-tissue interface, thereby compromising osseointegration [27]. In contrast, M2 macrophages express abundant anti-inflammatory factors including transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and interleukin-10 (IL-10), which contribute to inflammation resolution, promote vascularization, facilitate extracellular matrix remodeling, and support regenerative tissue repair [28]. Therefore, strategies that promote a timely transition from the M1 to the M2 phenotype are crucial to mitigating prolonged inflammation and establishing a microenvironment conducive to accelerated osteogenesis and successful osseointegration.

Given the observed antioxidant capacity of NTP/Apt-VPA, this study further evaluated its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties. Macrophage polarization on different surfaces was analyzed via immunofluorescence (IF) staining using inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and CD206 as surface markers for M1 and M2 phenotypes, respectively. Phenotypic analysis (Fig. 4A–F) revealed that all modified surfaces (NT, NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA) promoted a shift from M1 to M2 polarization, as evidenced by pronounced downregulation of iNOS and marked upregulation of CD206. The most significant effect was observed on NTP/Apt-VPA. ELISA results (Fig. 4B–E) further corroborated this shift: NTP/Apt-VPA significantly suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6, p < 0.0001) while elevating anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β and IL-10, p < 0.0001). The greater roughness of NT relative to Ti may play a role in the baseline immunomodulatory effects of the nanotopography; however, given the comparable roughness across all nanotubular groups, the further improvements provided by PDA, Apt19s, and VPA are likely chemical in nature.

Gene expression levels of representative M1 (iNOS and TNF-α) and M2 (IL-4 and IL-10) markers were assessed by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR, Fig. 4G–J). All modified surfaces (NT, NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA) significantly downregulated M1 markers, with the strongest suppression observed in the NTP/Apt-VPA group. Specifically, TNF-α expression decreased to 28.8% of Ti control levels (p < 0.0001), and iNOS expression was reduced to approximately 19.7% (p < 0.0001). Conversely, M2 markers were substantially upregulated. The NTP/Apt-VPA group showed the highest induction of IL-4, while both NTP/Apt-VPA and NTP/Apt groups strongly promoted IL-10 expression. Compared to Ti controls, IL-4 and IL-10 expression increased by 15.0-fold (p < 0.0001) and 17.2-fold (p < 0.001), respectively, underscoring the superior efficacy of these functionalized surfaces to drive anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization.

The NTP/Apt-VPA surface may establish a tri-modal immunoregulatory axis through the concerted action of its three functional components: the underlying nanotopography, the intermediate PDA coating, and the sustainably released VPA. Specifically, the nanotubes transduce mechanical cues that dampen NF-κB nuclear translocation [29]; the PDA coating scavenges ROS and inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation [30]; and the sustainably released VPA enforces epigenetic reprogramming via HDAC inhibition and STAT6 phosphorylation [31]. This synergistic framework not only abbreviates the inflammatory phase but also fosters an osteogenesis-favorable microenvironment. Thereby, the NTP/Apt-VPA implant may function as an immune conductor, effectively redirecting the acute inflammatory response toward an osseointegration-forming outcome.

In vitro responses of MSCs to Apt19s-VPA conjugate functionalized implants

Evaluation of MSC viability on modified Ti surfaces

Live/dead staining suggested good cytocompatibility of all surfaces, with high cell viability across all groups (Fig. 5A, merge; full channel images in Fig. S6A). CCK-8 assays showed that while Ti supported the highest proliferation rate, the modified surfaces led to reduced proliferation (Fig. 5B) - a phenomenon similar to that observed in macrophage cultures. Cellular morphological adaptations were further assessed using dual-modality imaging with Alexa Fluor 488-phalloidin cytoskeletal staining (Fig. 5A, Fig. S6B) and high-resolution SEM (Fig. 5A, Fig. S6C). Both techniques revealed consistent trends: cells on polished Ti retained a spindle-like morphology with limited pseudopodia formation, whereas those on NT extended long, branched protrusions, indicating early cytoskeletal activation. Further biofunctionalization (NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA) progressively promoted morphological maturation. In particular, the NTP/Apt-VPA group exhibited a significant increase in cell spreading area and formed elaborate filopodial networks with secondary branching. These findings indicate that the synergy between nanotube topography and bioactive functionalization (PDA, aptamer, and VPA) engineers a cytocompatible microenvironment favoring MSC colonization and spreading. It is well established that stem cell morphology on biomaterials is closely linked to cytoskeletal tension and thus cell fate. Cells displaying extensive spreading and a polygonal shape are predisposed to osteogenic differentiation, while those with restricted spreading tend to adopt adipogenic fates. The enhanced cellular spreading may contribute to the osteoinductive properties of these surfaces, as it has been linked to osteogenic commitment in previous studies [32].

Evaluation of MSC recruitment

To assess the influence of surface engineering on MSC migration, complementary vertical (Transwell) and horizontal (wound healing) assays were employed. Transwell chemotaxis analysis (Fig. 5C and D) revealed a pronounced, time-dependent increase in migrated cells on NTP/Apt and NTP/Apt-VPA. After 12 and 24 h, the transmembrane cell densities on NTP/Apt and NTP/Apt-VPA were more than two-fold higher than those on Ti controls (p < 0.0001), suggesting the establishment of a robust chemotactic gradient by the aptamer layer.

This enhanced recruitment effect was further corroborated by wound healing assays (Fig. 5E and F), which showed distinct migration patterns across the different groups. Ti and NT surfaces achieved only 18.69 ± 0.30% and 20.57 ± 3.03% wound closure at 24 h, respectively, whereas NTP/Apt and NTP/Apt-VPA reached 65.97 ± 0.91% and 87.54 ± 0.30% closure under the same conditions (p < 0.0001). The stepwise improvement from NTP/Apt to NTP/Apt-VPA highlights the additive benefit conferred by VPA functionalization.

Mechanistically, Apt19s binds to the ALPL receptor on the MSC surface, which initiates PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling cascades that ultimately drive cytoskeletal remodeling and directed cell migration [33]. Importantly, covalent conjugation with VPA did not compromise aptamer affinity. Instead, VPA exerted an epigenetic priming effect by increasing histone acetylation at promoters of migration-related genes (e.g., CXCR4, MMP-2) [18], while simultaneously enhancing membrane receptor sensitivity to homing cues. The observed enhancement suggests a synergistic interaction between Apt19s and VPA, which may represent a dual-ligand paradigm for stem cell homing.

Collectively, these findings provide a rational blueprint for designing biomaterials whose spatiotemporal instructional cues can be precisely programmed. By integrating recruitment ligands with epigenetic modulators, the proposed platform enables on-demand MSC homing followed by osteogenic commitment—an orchestrated sequence that holds great potential for accelerating osseointegration.

Osteogenic differentiation on modified Ti surfaces

The osteoinductive trajectory of rat MSCs on modified Ti substrates was tracked through a time-dependent sequence of early and late osteogenic markers. ALP activity and staining (Fig. 6A and B) revealed an immediate, surface-dependent priming effect. By day 7, faint ALP signals were detectable on Ti and NT surfaces, while NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA displayed a stepwise intensification that peaked at day 14, following the hierarchy: NTP/Apt-VPA > NTP/Apt > NTP > NT > Ti. This gradient suggests that NT topography initiates early osteogenic commitment, Apt19s may augment the biotopographical cue, and VPA further enhances Apt19s-mediated priming over time. Terminal mineralization, evaluated by Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining at day 21, corroborated the early enzymatic trends (Fig. 6A–C). Dense, spatially organized mineralized nodules were evident on NTP/Apt-VPA, which showed a 1.4-fold increase in calcium deposition compared to Ti controls (p < 0.0001). qRT-PCR analysis further supported this chronological progression at the molecular level (Fig. 6D–F). Runx2 expression, indicative of early osteogenic commitment, showed a 1.8-fold increase on NT compared to Ti (p < 0.001), attributable to nanotopographical cues alone. PDA coating alone (NTP) showed a neutral effect, whereas Apt19s functionalization provided a 1.5-fold increase (p < 0.001). The Apt19s-VPA conjugate achieved a supra-additive 7.7-fold upregulation relative to Ti (p < 0.0001). Collagen I (COL-I) expression, associated with matrix deposition, was approximately 2.2-fold higher on NTP/Apt-VPA than on NT and Ti (p < 0.05). Osteopontin (OPN), a marker of late maturation, increased progressively across the groups (Ti < NT < NTP < NTP/Apt < NTP/Apt-VPA), culminating in a 3.2-fold elevation for NTP/Apt-VPA over Ti (p < 0.0001).

In summary, the combination of these cues appears to create a microenvironment favorable for osteogenic differentiation, as evidenced by the stepwise increase in ALP activity and mineralization. From a clinical perspective, the chromatin-remodeling capacity of VPA may be particularly beneficial in aged or metabolically compromised patients, whose MSCs often exhibit intrinsically blunted osteogenic potential. This strategy thus offers a promising translational avenue to rejuvenate endogenous bone repair.

Effect of macrophage-conditioned medium on the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs

Osseointegration relies on synergistic crosstalk between macrophages and MSCs. Upon implant placement, macrophages release various cytokines and chemokines to guide the homing of endogenous MSCs to the damaged site. Subsequently, M2-polarized macrophages secrete pro-regenerative factors that actively promote osteogenic differentiation. As our findings suggested that the modified Ti surfaces promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, we therefore sought to investigate the functional impact of this macrophage phenotypic shift on MSC behavior. We employed an indirect coculture model - treating murine MSCs (isolated from C57BL/6 mice) with macrophage-conditioned medium (MCM) from material-polarized murine RAW 264.7 macrophages - to test whether osteogenesis is promoted through macrophage modulation. This species-matched configuration ensures that any observed effects are attributable to conserved murine cytokine signaling. Specifically, we hypothesized that surfaces which mitigate oxidative stress and drive M2 polarization would enrich the MCM with anti-inflammatory and pro-osteogenic factors, thereby creating a microenvironment conducive to MSC osteogenesis.

Representative ALP and ARS staining (Fig. 6G) showed that after 7 days of culture, MSCs treated with MCM from NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA groups exhibited stronger ALP-positive staining and more extensive mineralized nodule formation compared to those cultured in Ti- and NT-derived MCM. Quantitative analyses (Fig. 6H and I) further suggested that mineralization levels were closely aligned with ALP activity. NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA significantly enhanced both ALP activity and mineralization capacity relative to Ti and NT controls (p < 0.0001), with the NTP/Apt-VPA group demonstrating the most pronounced effect. These results suggest that MCM from NTP/Apt-VPA enhances MSC osteogenic differentiation, an effect mediated at least in part through M2 macrophage polarization.

Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes were conducted using GO and KEGG databases. GO analysis showed that upregulated genes in the NTP/Apt-VPA group (Module C1) were mainly enriched in biological processes such as glycogen biosynthesis, hypoxia response, cellular response to TGF-β stimulation, regulation of neurogenesis, chemotaxis, regulation of vascular development and angiogenesis, monosaccharide metabolic process, and regulation of peptidyl-tyrosine phosphorylation (Fig. 7C). Among these, the regulation of angiogenesis helps construct the vascular network required for osseointegration, ensuring nutrient delivery and cell recruitment; chemotaxis guides stem cell migration and homing to the implantation site; the hypoxia response cooperatively creates a suitable microenvironment promoting cell adaptation and functional performance; and TGF-β-related processes play key roles in regulating cell function and osteogenic signal transduction.

KEGG pathway and GSEA further demonstrated that the PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways were significantly upregulated in the NTP/Apt-VPA group compared to the Ti group (Fig. 7D–F). The PI3K/AKT pathway, a classical osteogenic signaling cascade, promotes osteoprogenitor cell proliferation via mTOR activation and regulates transcription factors like FoxO 1 and Runx2, thereby enhancing expression of osteogenic genes including OCN and OPN. Concurrently, the MAPK pathway (particularly the ERK1/2 and p38 sub-pathways) activates transcription factors Elk-1 and ATF2, which bind to promoters of bone formation genes like COL-I and OPN. MAPK signaling also supports stem cell survival and migration to the osseointegration site. Focal adhesion and actin cytoskeleton regulation pathways were also upregulated in the NTP/Apt-VPA group, promoting actin reorganization and focal complex formation to enhance MSC anchoring and spreading on the implant surface. Additional pathways including Wnt, HIF-1, and AMPK signaling contribute to bone formation microenvironment regulation and stem cell differentiation: Wnt signaling directly induces osteogenic differentiation; the HIF-1 pathway activates angiogenesis-related genes under hypoxic conditions; and the AMPK pathway promotes autophagy and bone formation through ULK1 phosphorylation and mTOR regulation.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis based on RNA-seq data revealed distinct regulatory differences between the NTP/Apt-VPA and Ti groups (Fig. 7G). The network, centered around Egfr, integrated extensive interactions among metabolic enzymes, signaling kinases, and adhesion molecules, forming a comprehensive regulatory system encompassing signal transduction, metabolic reprogramming, and microenvironment interaction. Metabolic genes (e.g., Pkm, Tpi1, and G6pc3) were closely associated with Egfr-mediated cell proliferation and metabolic reprogramming; signal transduction genes (e.g., Jak2, Prkca, and Shc4) mediated downstream Egfr cascades regulating cell survival, proliferation, and migration; receptors and adhesion molecules (e.g., Erbb2, Cd44, and Itgav/Itgam) expanded Egfr functions in cell adhesion and microenvironmental response. Such multidimensional regulatory networks centered around Egfr are characteristic of tissue regeneration processes.

To explore whether the pathway activation suggested by transcriptomic data could also be observed at the protein level, we performed Western blot analysis to examine the phosphorylation status of AKT and ERK - key nodes in the PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways. MSCs were cultured on NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA substrates for 7 days, and the expression levels of total AKT, phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT), total ERK, and phosphorylated ERK (p-ERK) were measured (Fig. 7H–J). The NTP/Apt group showed elevated p-AKT and p-ERK levels compared to the NTP group, suggesting that Apt19s alone may activate both the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways. Notably, the NTP/Apt-VPA group exhibited a further increase in p-AKT and p-ERK levels relative to the NTP/Apt group, which may indicate a potential synergistic effect of VPA in combination with Apt19s. Together, these protein-level data are consistent with our RNA-seq findings and appear to support the idea that the combination of Apt19s and VPA may influence the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK pathways, potentially contributing to MSC osteogenic differentiation.

In vivo immunomodulatory and osseointegration evaluation

To evaluate the early immunomodulatory effects of the implants, we performed IF and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining on peri-implant tissues harvested at postoperative day 7. In rabbit femoral defect models, the acute inflammatory phase typically peaks within the first 3-5 days following implantation, after which the response transitions toward resolution and repair. By day 7, the inflammatory microenvironment has progressed to a stage where M2-biased macrophage polarization can be reliably assessed. Moreover, research indicates that cells exhibited a predominantly M2 macrophage phenotype by day 10, indicating that an M1-to-M2 shift may occur within the day 7-10 window [34].

Based on promising in vitro results demonstrating the exceptional osteogenic activity and immunomodulatory capacity of the NTP/Apt-VPA implant, we further evaluated its bone regenerative potential in vivo. In contrast, the NTP/Apt-VPA group showed reduced iNOS+ expression along with increased Arg-1, which may indicate enhanced M2 macrophage polarization and potentially faster inflammation resolution (Fig. 8B). Quantitative analysis of the Arg-1/iNOS fluorescence intensity ratio was consistent with this observation: the NTP/Apt-VPA group exhibited a ratio of 48.3%, representing an approximately 43.8-fold increase compared to the Ti group (1.1%) and a 7.6-fold increase compared to the NT group (7.6%) (Fig. 8C). IHC staining further supported this pattern: the Ti group showed relatively high TNF-α expression and low IL-10, whereas the NTP/Apt-VPA group was associated with lower TNF-α and higher IL-10 levels (Fig. 8E). Quantitative analysis showed that the NTP/Apt-VPA group had a TNF-α-positive area of 1.1%, compared to 5.5% in the Ti group and 4.9% in the NT group (Fig. 8D). Conversely, the IL-10-positive area in the NTP/Apt-VPA group was 69.8%, compared to 3.3% in the Ti group and 46.8% in the NT group (Fig. 8F). Taken together, these results suggest that the NTP/Apt-VPA implant may promote M2 macrophage polarization in vivo, potentially shorten the inflammatory phase, and help establish a more pro-regenerative immune microenvironment.

Bone regeneration was further analyzed using micro-CT, histology, and immunohistochemistry. Two-dimensional and 3D reconstructed micro-CT images (Fig. 8G) clearly distinguished the implant material (gray-white) from newly formed bone (red) at 4 and 8 weeks. The NTP/Apt-VPA group exhibited the most extensive bone deposition along the implant surface at both time points. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 8H–J) showed that at 4 weeks, the NTP/Apt-VPA group exhibited significantly higher bone volume fraction (BV/TV, %), bone mineral density (BMD, g·cm−3), and trabecular number (Tb.N, mm) than the Ti group, with no significant difference from the NT group. By 8 weeks, the NTP/Apt-VPA coating outperformed the Ti group in BMD (p < 0.01) and Tb.N (p < 0.01), while BV/TV significantly exceeded both the Ti (p < 0.001) and NT (p < 0.05) groups, suggesting an improved osseointegration capacity.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome (MST) staining (Fig. 9A and B) provided histological validation. At 4 weeks, the Ti group showed fibrous callus with sporadic immature trabeculae, while the NT group exhibited a thin layer of woven bone. In contrast, the NTP/Apt-VPA group presented dense, interconnected nascent bone well-integrated with the implant. By 8 weeks, the Ti group still contained fibrovascular tissue, and the NT group had formed discontinuous bony bridges, whereas the NTP/Apt-VPA group displayed mature, high-density trabecular bone with embedded osteoblasts, resembling native bone architecture. At 4 weeks, collagen fibers were sparse in the Ti and NT groups, while the NTP/Apt-VPA group exhibited well-aligned collagen supporting early bone formation. The recruitment of endogenous stem cells is crucial for bone formation during development and remodeling. IF staining of stem cell markers CD29 and CD90 at 7 days post-surgery revealed minimal stem cell aggregation in the Ti group and only sparse distribution in the NT group. In contrast, the NTP/Apt-VPA group recruited abundant endogenous stem cells around the implant (Fig. 9C). This effect is attributed to the aptamer Apt19s and the pro-healing M2 macrophage-enriched microenvironment, providing a cellular foundation for early osseointegration.

IHC and IF analyses of osteogenic markers—Runx2, BMP2, COL-I, and OPN—at 8 weeks post-implantation revealed significantly stronger expression in the NTP/Apt-VPA group compared to Ti and NT groups (Fig. 9D and E). Robust Runx2 expression suggested the activation of osteogenic lineage commitment in response to NTP/Apt-VPA [35]. Concurrent elevation of BMP2 suggests activation of SMAD-dependent signaling pathways that synergize with Runx2 to enhance osteogenic gene transcription [36], consistent with previous studies showing biomaterial-mediated BMP signaling promotes endochondral ossification and mineralized matrix deposition [37]. The spatial distribution of COL-I and OPN further elucidated the regenerative process. In control groups, expression was largely restricted to regions near the implant surface, indicating limited osseointegration. In contrast, the NTP/Apt-VPA group exhibited intense, widespread expression of both matrix proteins within well-developed bone tissue distant from the interface, reflecting successful osteoinduction and de novo bone formation beyond the inherent osteoconductive capacity of the implant surface. As the primary organic component of bone matrix, COL-I provides a structural scaffold for mineralization, while OPN regulates hydroxyapatite crystallization and osteoclast activity [38,39]. Their coordinated upregulation implies enhanced matrix synthesis and improved biomechanical and biochemical maturation of the newly formed bone. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the complete functionalization strategy (PDA + Apt19s + VPA) effectively upregulates osteogenesis-related protein expression. The NTP/Apt-VPA implant significantly enhanced osseointegration compared to the NT and Ti controls, indicating the overall efficacy of the combined functionalization strategy.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a biofunctionalized Ti implant designated NTP/Apt-VPA, which integrates TiO2 nanotube topography, a PDA interlayer, and an MSC-specific aptamer (Apt19s) conjugated with the epigenetic modulator VPA. This multi-component design aims to address key barriers to osseointegration: excessive inflammation, oxidative stress, insufficient endogenous stem cell recruitment, and impaired osteogenesis. The NTP/Apt-VPA implant scavenged ROS, promoted M2 macrophage polarization, and enhanced MSC recruitment and osteogenic differentiation. Transcriptomic analysis linked these effects to activation of PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways, as well as enhanced cell adhesion and extracellular matrix organization. In vivo evaluation in a rabbit bone implant model confirmed dual functionality of immunomodulation (macrophage M2 shift) and improved osseointegration. By integrating topographic and biochemical cues, this platform may facilitate implant osseointegration, particularly in clinically challenging settings with compromised bone regeneration.

Experimental methods

Sample preparation

Thiol-terminated Apt19s and Apt19s-VPA (each with a 3′-end thiol modification) were custom-synthesized by Hunan Accurete Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Commercially pure Ti substrates (Φ15 mm × 1 mm thickness) were sequentially polished with 400# to 1500# grit silicon carbide abrasive paper, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in acetone, ethanol, and deionized water (15 min per solvent) to remove surface contaminants. After drying, the polished Ti sheets were electrochemically anodized using a two-electrode configuration under a constant voltage of 20 V for 1 h using a DC power supply (Antaixin TPR6010T, China). The electrolyte consisted of 0.5 wt% HF aqueous solution. FE-SEM analysis (Hitachi Regulus 8230, Japan) revealed the formation of vertically aligned TiO2 NTs with an average pore diameter of approximately 80-100 nm and a depth of around 238 nm. Separately, cylindrical implants (4 mm diameter × 8 mm length) made of commercially pure grade 4 Ti were prepared for in vivo experiments.

To fabricate NTP, the NT samples were immersed in a freshly prepared dopamine hydrochloride solution (2 mg mL−1 in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer, pH 8.5) and continuously agitated for 24 h in the dark to prevent undesired photodegradation. Through this process, a uniform PDA coating was conformally deposited onto the NT surfaces.

Subsequent biomolecular immobilization was carried out by incubating NTP substrates in solutions of either Apt19s or Apt19s-VPA (8 μM in 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) under gentle stirring at room temperature for 6 h. The catechol/quinone-rich PDA surface facilitated covalent immobilization of the thiol-modified aptamers via Michael addition and Schiff base reactions. The resulting functionalized substrates were labeled as NTP/Apt and NTP/Apt-VPA, respectively. All samples were thoroughly rinsed three times with phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS) and stored under a dry nitrogen atmosphere prior to further use.

Sample characterization

Surface morphology was characterized by FE-SEM and AFM (Bruker Dimension ICON, Germeany). Wettability was assessed via sessile drop contact angle measurements (Dingsheng JY-82C, China) in triplicate. Chemical composition was analyzed using XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA), FTIR (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS20, USA), and Raman microscopy (Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution, Japan). Coating adhesion was evaluated using a universal testing machine (MTS CMT6103, USA) with a custom fixture. A shear force was applied parallel to the sample surface at a constant loading rate of 1 MPa s−1 until delamination. The critical load was recorded, and shear strength was calculated as the critical load divided by the coated area. Additionally, micro-scratch tests were performed using a scratch tester (Anton Paar RST3, Austria) with a Rockwell C diamond indenter (tip radius 200 μm). A linearly increasing normal load (0-30 N) was applied over 2 mm at a scratch velocity of 4 mm min-1.

The loading and sustained release profiles of aptamer conjugates on the biofunctionalized Ti surfaces were quantitatively investigated via fluorescence labeling. For loading analysis, FAM-labeled Apt19s (λex/λem = 490/520 nm) was grafted onto NTP following the established protocol. Unlabeled Apt19s were processed in parallel to exclude the autofluorescence of Apt19s. Aptamer loading was visualized using CLSM (Olympus FV1000, Japan).

To evaluate the release kinetics of Apt19s, a standard calibration curve was first established using Apt19s-FAM solutions at concentrations ranging from 2 to 10 μM. Fluorescence intensity was measured with a multimode microplate reader (Bio Tek Synergy H1, USA), and triplicate measurements per concentration were used to construct a linear regression model (R2 > 0.99). For release studies, Apt19s-FAM-grafted specimens (n = 3) were incubated under two pH conditions—acidic (pH 6.0) and neutral (pH 7.4)—at 37 °C under light-protected conditions. Each sample was immersed in 1 mL of PBS at the corresponding pH in a 24-well plate. The release medium was collected at predetermined time points (12 h, 1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 10, 14, 18, 22, 26, 30, and 34 days), and the cumulative release of Apt19s (nmol) was quantified based on fluorescence measurements against the standard curve. After each sampling, an equal volume of fresh pre-warmed PBS was replenished to maintain constant sink conditions throughout the release study.

Cell culture and osteogenic differentiation model

For most in vitro experiments (proliferation, migration, osteogenic differentiation on material surfaces, and RNA sequencing), bone marrow-derived MSCs were aseptically isolated from the femoral marrow cavity of 2-week-old female Sprague-Dawley rats (SPF Biotechnology, China). Primary MSCs were expanded in low-glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), with medium replacement performed every 48 h. Upon reaching 90% confluence, cells were harvested using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco, USA) and subcultured at a 1:3 ratio. Cells at passage 3 (P3) were used for subsequent biocompatibility assessment and osteogenic differentiation assays.

The murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 (Procell Life Science, China) was cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS and employed for immunomodulation studies. For the indirect co-culture experiments, bone marrow-derived MSCs were isolated from 6-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (SPF Biotechnology, China) following the same dissociation protocol as for rat cells. These murine MSCs were used exclusively for the indirect co-culture experiments to ensure species compatibility with RAW 264.7 macrophages. To model macrophage-MSC crosstalk, MCM was collected from RAW 264.7 cultures polarized by the modified substrates, centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, and subsequently applied to MSC cultures. All cells were maintained under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2, and 95% relative humidity).

In vitro biocompatibility assessment

Live/dead staining

Cellular viability and membrane integrity were assessed through a dual-fluorescence staining assay. Rat MSCs (2 × 104 cells cm−2) and RAW 264.7 macrophages (1 × 105 cells cm−2) were seeded onto the experimental substrates and cultured for 72 h under standard conditions. The samples were then rinsed with PBS (pH 7.4) and stained using a Calcein-AM/PI Double Stain Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Calcein-AM labels live cells via intracellular esterase activity, whereas propidium iodide (PI) enters cells with compromised membranes, indicating non-viable cells. Fluorescence images were acquired using an upright fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axioskop 40 FL, Germany).

Cell proliferation quantification via CCK-8 assay

Cellular proliferation was quantitatively analyzed using CCK-8 (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Rat MSCs were cultured on the experimental substrates for 1, 3, and 5 days, whereas RAW 264.7 macrophages were evaluated at 1 and 3 days to assess time-dependent proliferative activity. Before analysis, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM to minimize interference from serum esterases. A 10% (v/v) CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation under standard culture conditions for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using the multimode microplate reader.

Cell morphological characterization

Cytoskeletal organization analysis via fluorescence staining

Rat MSCs were seeded onto the experimental substrates at a density of 2 × 104 cells cm−2 and cultured for 72 h under standard conditions. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Solarbio, China) for 30 min at 25 °C, permeabilized and blocked simultaneously in PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Solarbio, China) and 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, China) for 1 h. Actin filaments were labeled with Actin-Tracker Green (Beyotime, China, 1:100 dilution in PBS with 1% BSA) for 60 min at 37 °C in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Beyotime, China) for 5 min. Between each step, the samples were washed three times with PBS. Imaging was performed using CLSM.

For RAW 264.7 macrophages, a pro-inflammatory microenvironment was first induced by priming the cells with 100 ng mL−1 LPS (Abmole, USA) in complete medium for 24 h. The primed macrophages were then transferred onto the substrates and cultured for an additional 96 h in standard medium before undergoing the same staining procedure described above.

Ultrastructural morphology via FE-SEM

Cellular adhesion and ultrastructural morphology were examined using FE-SEM. After fixation (as described in Section 4.5.1), the samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30% to 100%, 15 min per step) and subjected to critical point drying (Leica EM CPD300, Germany, 31 °C, 73.8 bar CO2). The dried specimens were sputter-coated with a 5 nm layer of Au/Pd (Quorum Q150T ES, UK) to enhance conductivity. High-resolution images were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV using a secondary electron detector.

In vitro assessment of macrophage polarization

Intracellular ROS detection

RAW 264.7 murine macrophages were seeded onto the substrates at a density of 1 × 105 cells cm−2 and polarized using 100 ng mL−1 LPS in complete medium for 24 h to establish a pro-inflammatory microenvironment. Afterwards, the cells were continuously cultured in fresh standard medium for another 96 h. For ROS detection, cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA (Beyotime, China) in serum-free DMEM at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 20 min in the dark. Fluorescence images were acquired using the upright fluorescence microscope with identical exposure parameters across all groups. For quantitative evaluation of ROS fluorescence intensity, ImageJ software was used to analyze the DCFH-DA single-channel images. Three random microscopic fields were selected for each group for statistical analysis. In addition, flow cytometry (Attune NxT, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was applied to provide auxiliary ROS distribution histogram results to further support the fluorescence quantification data.

Immunofluorescence staining for M1/M2 markers

After culture, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the M1 marker iNOS (SABbiotech, USA, 1:200 dilution) and the M2 marker CD206 (CST, USA, 1:200 dilution). Following PBS washes, Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (SABbiotech, USA, 1:500 dilution) were applied and incubated for 1 h at 25 °C in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Images were acquired using the CLSM.

Cytokine secretion analysis via ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were collected following 24 h of LPS stimulation and an additional 96 h of culture. The concentrations of key cytokines including TNF-α, IL-6, TGF-β, and IL-10 were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Jingmei Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 570 nm using the multimode microplate reader.

Inflammatory gene expression profiling via qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using the RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (ES Science, China), reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA), and amplified with SYBR Green Premix (Takara, Japan) on the CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA). qRT-PCR was performed with primers (Table S2) targeting iNOS, TNF-α, IL-4, and IL-10. The relative expression of each gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, normalized to GAPDH, and calibrated against the Ti control group.

MSC migration and recruitment

The chemotactic recruitment and migratory capacity of MSCs were systematically evaluated using standardized Transwell migration assays and scratch wound healing models.

Transwell migration assay

The Ti substrates were immersed in 500 μL of low-glucose DMEM containing 1% FBS in 24-well plates. Polycarbonate Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size, Corning, USA) were placed vertically above the substrates. Rat MSCs were resuspended in serum-free DMEM at a density of 1 × 105 cells cm−2, and 200 μL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber of each insert. After 12 and 24 h of incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed. Cells that migrated to the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, China) for 10 min. The membranes were imaged under a stereomicroscope (Cewei PXS6-T, China), and the number of migrated cells was quantified using ImageJ software for statistical analysis.

Scratch wound healing assay

Rat MSCs were seeded on the substrates at a density of 2 × 104 cells cm−2 and cultured until 90% confluency was reached. A uniform wound was created in each monolayer using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. The cultures were then maintained in low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 1% FBS for 24 h. Images of the wound areas were captured at 0 and 24 h using the stereomicroscope. The extent of wound closure was quantified with ImageJ software, and the scratch healing rate was calculated accordingly.

In vitro osteogenic potential evaluation

ALP activity analysis

Rat MSCs were seeded on thesubstrates at a density of 2 × 104 cells cm−2 and cultured in osteogenic induction medium, which consisted of low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 50 μg mL−1 ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 100 nM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The cells were induced for 7 and 14 days, with the medium refreshed every 48 h. At each time point, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature for 30 min.

ALP enzymatic activity was visualized using a BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Chromogenic Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. After staining, unbound dye was removed by triple rinsing with ultrapure water. The stained specimens were imaged under brightfield illumination using the stereomicroscope.

For quantitative analysis, ALP activity and total protein content were measured using an Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit and a BCA Protein Quantification Kit (Beyotime, China), respectively. Cell lysates were prepared using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, China) containing protease inhibitors. ALP activity was assessed by incubating the lysates with p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) substrate at 37 °C for 10 min, and the absorbance of the reaction product was measured at 405 nm using the multimode microplate reader. Total protein concentration was determined via the BCA assay, with absorbance read at 592 nm, based on a BSA standard curve (0–0.5 mg mL−1, R2 > 0.99). ALP activity was normalized to the total protein content and expressed as micromoles of p-nitrophenol (pNP) generated per minute per milligram of protein (U mg−1).

Mineralization analysis via ARS staining

Extracellular matrix mineralization was quantified after 21 days of osteogenic induction using ARS staining. Fixed cell-substrate constructs were incubated with 2% ARS solution (pH 4.2, Solarbio, China) for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by thorough rinsing with deionized water to remove non-specifically bound dye. Calcium nodule formation was visualized under the stereomicroscope. For quantitative analysis, bound ARS was solubilized with 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC, Solarbio, China) at room temperature for 20 min. The absorbance of the eluate was measured at 560 nm using the multimode microplate reader.

Osteogenesis-related gene expression profiling

The expression of osteogenesis-related genes was assessed using qRT-PCR. Rat MSCs were cultured on the Ti substrates in osteogenic induction medium for 7 days. Total RNA was extracted using an RNA Rapid Extraction Kit, and its purity was verified by A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0. cDNA was synthesized with a Fast Reverse Transcription System (ES Science, Shanghai, China). qRT-PCR amplification was conducted using SYBR Green Premix on the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System under standardized thermal cycling conditions. The relative mRNA expression levels of Runx2, COL-I, and OPN were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method, normalized to the endogenous reference gene GAPDH, and calibrated against the Ti control group. Primer sequences (Table S3) were validated to ensure amplification efficiency (90–110%) and specificity, as confirmed by single-peak melt curves.

Effect of MCM on MSC osteogenic differentiation

MCM was prepared as follows: RAW 264.7 murine macrophages were seeded on the Ti substrates at a density of 1 × 105 cells cm−2 and polarized by 24-h stimulation with complete DMEM containing 100 ng mL−1 ultrapure LPS under standard culture conditions. Following polarization, the cells were washed with PBS and further cultured in DMEM with 5% FBS for 96 h. The MCM was collected and subjected to sequential centrifugation (3000 ×g, 20 min, 4 °C, Eppendorf 5810R, Germany) followed by sterile filtration through 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The supernatant was then mixed 1:1 (v/v) with the osteogenic induction medium, aliquoted, and stored at −20 °C for ≤30 days.

For osteogenic induction, P3 murine MSCs were seeded at 2 × 104 cells cm−2 in 24-well plates and cultured in the MCM-supplemented osteogenic medium, which was refreshed every 48 h. ALP activity was assessed on day 7 using the BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Chromogenic Kit and quantified with the Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit. Mineralized matrix deposition was evaluated on day 14 via the ARS (pH 4.2) staining. Quantitative analysis was performed by measuring the absorbance of the reaction products at 405 nm (ALP) or 560 nm (ARS) using the multimode microplate reader.

RNA sequencing

Rat MSCs were cultured on NTP/Apt-VPA and Ti control in osteogenic induction medium for 7 days, with the medium replaced every 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were lysed using TRIzol™ Reagent (Beyotime, China). The lysates were cryopreserved at −80 °C in RNase-free cryovials (Corning, USA) and subsequently shipped on dry ice to Cosmos Wisdom Biotechnology Co. (Beijing, China) for transcriptomic analysis.

Western blot analysis

MSCs were seeded on NTP, NTP/Apt, and NTP/Apt-VPA at a density of 2 × 104 cells cm-2 and cultured in osteogenic induction medium for 7 days. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche, Switzerland). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA kit. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST (Servicebio, China) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against p-AKT (Thr308, #81232-10-RR, Proteintech, USA), AKT (#80816-1-RR, Proteintech, USA), p-ERK (Thr202/Tyr204, #80031-1-RR, Proteintech, USA), ERK (#83533-1-RR, Proteintech, USA), and GAPDH (#ab181602, Abcam, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Proteintech, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad, USA). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software, and the ratios of p-AKT/AKT and p-ERK/ERK were calculated after normalization to GAPDH.

In vivo osseointegration in New Zealand White rabbit femoral implant model

Establishment of bone implant model

Thirty-six 4-month-old male New Zealand White rabbits (SPF grade) were obtained from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Production License: SCXK (Beijing) 2024-0001) and randomly assigned to three experimental groups (Ti, NT, and NTP/Apt-VPA). All animals underwent a 7-day acclimatization period in individually ventilated cages under controlled temperature (22 ± 1 °C) and a 12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to autoclaved food and water. This experiment was approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Beijing MDKN Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and was conducted in strict accordance with the experimental animal care and use guidelines of Beijing Animal Control Committee (Approval No. MDKN-2025-82). Femoral implant sites were prepared under general anesthesia induced by 3% (w/v) sodium pentobarbital (30 mg kg−1) administered via the marginal ear vein. A cylindrical cavity (4 mm in diameter) was created in the femoral epiphysis using a surgical drill (NSK Surgic XT, Japan) under continuous saline irrigation. The designated cylindrical implants were aseptically inserted into the cavity. Wound closure was performed in layers using 4-0 vicryl sutures (Ethicon, USA). Postoperative care included subcutaneous injection of penicillin G (4 × 105 U/day) for 7 days to prevent infection, and oral administration of ibuprofen (5 mg kg−1 per day) for analgesia. The animals were euthanized at 1, 4, and 8 weeks post-implantation. At week 1, 9 rabbits (n = 3 for each group) were used for IF and IHC staining (iNOS/Arg-1, TNF-α/IL-10, CD29/CD90). At week 4, 9 rabbits (n = 3 for each group) were first subjected to micro-CT analysis, followed by hard tissue sectioning and H&E and MST staining. At week 8, 18 rabbits (n = 6 for each group) were used: 3 rabbits per group were randomly selected for micro-CT analysis followed by hard tissue sectioning and H&E and MST staining, and the remaining 3 rabbits per group were used for decalcified histology for IF and IHC staining of COL-I, OPN, Runx2, and BMP2.

Micro-CT analysis

The femurs harvested at the 4- and 8-week time points were fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h and scanned using a high-resolution micro-CT system (Bruker Y.Cheetah, Germany). Three-dimensional reconstructions were generated using NRecon software (v1.7.4.2) with standardized threshold values. Bone morphometric parameters, including BV/TV, BMD, and Tb.N, were quantified within cylindrical regions of interest (ROIs).

Histological analysis

Fixed bone specimens were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70% to 100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in poly (methyl methacrylate). Sections of 50 μm thickness were prepared using a hard tissue microtome (Leica SP1600, Germany) and subsequently polished to 30 μm. These sections were then stained with H&E to evaluate cellular architecture and with MST to assess collagen distribution. Finally, the stained sections were mounted with neutral balsam for observation under a brightfield microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan).

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining

For decalcified histology, specimens were fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h at 4 °C and decalcified in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4) with daily solution renewal over 28 days. Decalcification endpoint was confirmed using a needle puncture test. Tissues were then dehydrated in an ascending ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin (Leica EG1160, Germany). Serial sagittal sections (5 μm) were cut with a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235, Germany), mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0, Beyotime, China) at 95 °C for 20 min.

To evaluate the effects of NTP/Apt-VPA on the peri-implant bone microenvironment and new bone formation, IF and IHC staining were carried out using the following markers: double IF staining for iNOS (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated, 1:200 dilution) and Arg-1 (Cy3-conjugated, 1:150 dilution); IHC staining for TNF-α (1:200 dilution) and IL-10 (1:200 dilution); double IF staining for COL-I (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated, 1:200 dilution) and OPN (Cy3-conjugated, 1:200 dilution); and IHC staining for Runx2 (1:200 dilution) and BMP2 (1:200 dilution). All antibodies were purchased from Abcam (UK). For IF staining, tissue sections were co-incubated with the respective primary antibody cocktail overnight at 4 °C and then counterstained with DAPI. All stained sections were imaged using the CLSM. For quantitative analysis of IF staining, the ratio of Arg-1-positive cells to iNOS-positive cells was calculated by counting positively stained cells in three randomly selected fields per sample using ImageJ software. For IHC staining, the percentage of TNF-α-positive area and IL-10-positive area was quantified by measuring the area fraction of the 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining relative to the total tissue area within 200 μm of the implant surface using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.1 (GraphPad Software, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk testing (W > 0.9, α = 0.05), while homogeneity of variance was verified using Bartlett's test (χ2 p > 0.1). For datasets satisfying both normality and homoscedasticity assumptions, intergroup differences were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p < 0.05, with increasing levels of significance denoted as ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
