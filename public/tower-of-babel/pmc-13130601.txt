Reply to: ‘Mislabeling and inappropriate analysis render interpretations from “The effect of the participatory heat education and awareness tools (HEAT) intervention on agricultural worker physiological heat strain: results from a parallel, comparison, group randomized study” unreliable’
Authors: June Spector, Tamre Cardoso, Lianne Sheppard
Published: 2026

We offer this response to the “Matters Arising” article by Webster et al. referencing the manuscript, “The effect of the participatory Heat Education and Awareness Tools (HEAT) intervention on agricultural worker physiological heat strain: Results from a parallel, comparison, group randomized study” [1]. The corresponding author has been responsive to Webster et al.’s inquiries and shared the entirety of the content in this response, including sensitivity analyses performed by independent statisticians not involved with the study, with the inquiring individuals and with BMC Public Health in 2023. The original published manuscript does not indicate in its body or title that it is a cluster-randomized trial or a randomized controlled trial. The main scientific conclusions of sensitivity analyses, which address the questions posed by Webster et al., are unchanged from the original published conclusions. We appreciate the correspondence with Webster et al. and the additional thought they stimulated.

Study design & randomization

The title of the manuscript included the terms “parallel”, “comparison”, and “group randomized.” These terms were in the manuscript title because the BMC Public Health research article author guidelines indicate that the design should be mentioned in the title [2]. The design of this study was indicated to be parallel, because both the intervention and comparison groups participated in the study in parallel. The comparison designation refers to the comparison between the two groups. Group randomization refers to the intended design of randomization by crew, as described in the published methods. As transparently indicated in the original published methods and limitations, randomization was unable to be performed for the participating small company crews (referred to as ‘Small’) for logistical reasons [1]. Although the study meets the National Institutes of Health (NIH) definition of a ‘clinical trial,’ the NIH definition is broad and applies to a variety of different study designs [3]. The authors followed guidance from our institution’s Human Subjects Division and registered the study and additionally published the study protocol previously in a peer-reviewed journal [4]. The published manuscript does not refer to the study in its body or title as a cluster-randomized trial or a randomized controlled trial (RCT), and we would like to confirm that the study is not a cluster-randomized trial or an RCT.

Analyses

To address questions posed by the inquiring individuals, several (post-hoc) sensitivity analyses were conducted to further consider clustering, with individuals nested within crews, and considering the unexpected non-randomization of ‘Small’ company crews. These analyses were performed by independent statisticians (TC, LS) not involved with the study development or original analyses. First, a sensitivity analysis (Sensitivity Analysis 1) was conducted with the addition of a random effect for crew (to account for clustering), with individuals nested within crew, to the original adjusted model (Original Model 2), from which inferences were drawn in the published manuscript. As described in the original published paper, Model 2 accounts for repeated measures within individuals with a random effect for individuals and assesses the association of maximum work shift Physiological Strain Index (PSImax) with group status, accounting for potential confounders (age, sex, and Body Mass Index [BMI], effort level, maximum shift Heat Index [HImax], company), and including terms for the interaction of effort level with HImax and group [1]. For the original Model 2, Satterthwaite’s method from the lmerTest package [5] was used to adjust denominator degrees of freedom (ddf). For all sensitivity analyses reported here, the between-within version of ddf [6] implemented in the lme function in the nlme package was used [7]. Using simulation studies, between-within ddf have been shown to be robust for small numbers of clusters with variations in cluster sizes, while preserving the type I error rate [8]. Other simulation studies concluded that between-within ddf are a better option for small numbers of clusters [9]. The original Model 2 using between-within ddf gave the ddf shown for Sensitivity Analysis 1 in Table 1, and the scientific conclusions did not change. Conclusions also did not change using the Kenward-Roger method to adjust degrees of freedom. Second, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using the Sensitivity Analysis 1 model but dropping the crews from the non-randomized ‘Small’ company (Sensitivity Analysis 2) and third, reversing the intervention and comparison group designations for the ‘Small’ company crews (Sensitivity analysis 3). All models were run using R version 4.2.3 [10] and RStudio version 2023.03.0 Build 386 [11].

As shown in Table 1 (which corresponds to the original published Table 3 [1]), results of these sensitivity analyses are consistent with the main conclusions as reported in the abstract of the published paper: “We observed a higher PSImax with high versus low/medium-low effort and a lower PSImax with older age, after covariate adjustment.” These inferences were not intended or considered to be causal inferences. As in the primary published analyses and conclusions, there was no statistically significant effect of the intervention on the primary outcome (PSI). The focus of these sensitivity analyses was on the primary objective, which was to evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention (bolded in Table 1), though all results of sensitivity analyses corresponding to Model 2 are reported for completeness. Interpretation of coefficients and p-values that were not the focus of the primary objective is beyond the scope of the intended analyses and main published findings. Overall, the main scientific conclusions from these sensitivity analyses are unchanged from the original published conclusions.
