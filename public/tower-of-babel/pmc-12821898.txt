A novel nomogram for predicting stone-free status after retrograde intrarenal surgery in patients with kidney stones
Authors: Zufa Zhang, Li Chen, Kedi Niu, Wenkai Li, Bin Hou, Feng Tian, Sheng Guan, Sixiong Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to develop and validate a novel preoperative nomogram to predict stone-free status (SFS) in patients undergoing retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) for kidney stones.MethodsA total of 220 patients who underwent RIRS were retrospectively analyzed. Feature selection was performed using Boruta and LASSO algorithms, identifying six key preoperative predictors: inferior pole stone (classified by RIPA), calyx pelvic height (CPH), number of stones, maximum stone diameter, stone volume, and mean stone density. A nomogram was constructed using multivariable logistic regression and evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and clinical impact curves (CIC). Internal validation was performed with 1,000 bootstrap resamples.ResultsThe nomogram demonstrated strong discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.873 in the training cohort and 0.862 in the validation cohort. Calibration plots showed good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. DCA and CIC confirmed its superior clinical utility across a range of threshold probabilities. Notably, inferior pole stones with RIPA ≤ 30° and higher CPH were strongly associated with SFS failure. Compared to existing scoring systems, the new model achieved better predictive performance. Including both stone volume and maximum diameter offered a more accurate assessment of stone burden than using either metric alone.ConclusionWe developed and internally validated a nomogram that outperformed existing tools in predicting SFS after RIRS. It may assist clinicians in individualized risk assessment and preoperative planning. As this predictive model was developed based on a single-centre study, it will be necessary to conduct further multicentre or prospective studies in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-025-02022-z.

Introduction

Kidney stones are a common urological condition with a high recurrence rate [1, 2]. Kidney stones significantly impair patients’ quality of life and increase healthcare resource utilization and economic burden [3]. In recent years, technological advances and equipment improvements have expanded the use of RIRS in kidney stone treatment, owing to its minimally invasive approach and high stone clearance rate [4, 5].

Current guidelines from organizations such as the European Association of Urology (EAU) recommend PCNL as a first-line treatment for stones larger than 20 mm [6, 7]. However, growing clinical evidence indicates that the indications for RIRS are expanding, with its safety and efficacy in treating complex, multiple, or larger stones increasingly recognized [8, 9]. Several scoring systems, including the R.I.R.S. and RUSS scores, have been developed to help clinicians assess stone complexity preoperatively and predict postoperative outcomes [10, 11]. Although these scoring systems have enhanced the scientific basis of surgical decision-making to some extent, their predictive accuracy remains limited. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a more accurate and easy-to-apply forecasting tool.

This study aimed to develop and validate a novel preoperative model to predict stone-free status (SFS) in patients with kidney stones undergoing RIRS. We further compared the newly developed column-line diagram with the existing R.I.R.S score and RUSS score, thus providing a more accurate and intuitive aid for individualized preoperative decision-making in RIRS.

Materials and methods

Study population

The study was conducted by the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Zhongshan Hospital of Dalian University (No. KY2025-043-2). We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with kidney stones who received RIRS between August 2022 and December 2024 at the Affiliated Zhongshan Hospital of Dalian University. Initially, we included 316 patients. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1)We excluded 41 patients who underwent combined procedures including PCNL, phlebotomy for lithotripsy, or other adjunctive interventions. (2) We further excluded 47 patients with bilateral renal stones requiring concomitant RIRS, stones located in the ureter, staghorn calculi, abnormal renal function, combined urinary malformations, or genitourinary tumors. (3) Eight patients who did not undergo a non-contrast CT (NCCT) for SFS assessment one month after surgery were excluded. A total of 220 eligible patients who underwent RIRS were ultimately included (Figure S1).

Surgical techniques

All patients received antibiotic prophylaxis prior to surgery and adhered to current recommended standards [12]. All operations were performed by the same experienced urologist at the center participating in the study. All RIRS procedures were performed in the lithotomy position under general anesthesia. Preoperative D-J stent placement was applied in cases with pyelonephritis or/and a narrow ureter that could prevent access to the stone. An 8.5 F ureteroscope (Karl Storz®, Tuttlingen, Germany) was used in all cases to access the ureter and facilitate safe dilatation under guidewire guidance. The ureter was viewed to detect any abnormalities up to the ureteropelvic junction. A guidewire (COOK BWS-035150, 0.035’’ x 150 cm) was placed into the renal pelvis before insertion of a ureteral access sheath (12/14 F, INNOVEX; Shanghai, China) into the ureter. A 7.5 F flexible ureteroscope (DR060670, HAWK, Hangzhou, China) was used for the RIRS procedure. A 200 μm laser fiber (Ho: YAG Laser, HLumenis PULSE™ P120H; California, USA) was used for laser lithotripsy in all patients included in the study. Laser settings were adjusted to 0.5 J at 20 Hz for fine stone dusting or 1 J at 12 Hz for stone fragmentation. The fragmented stones were extracted by the nitinol basket catheter (Dakota®, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA). Finally, a D-J catheter was placed in all patients.

Data acquisition and processing

All patients underwent preoperative computed tomography of urography (CTU) to obtain data regarding stone characteristics. Based on a prior review of the literature, we identified anatomical factors associated with SFS [13, 14]. Renal Infundibulopelvic Angle (RIPA) is defined as the inner angle of the intersection of the ureteropelvic axis and the lower calyx axis. Infundibular length (IL) is the distance from the most distal point at the bottom stone-containing calix to the midpoint of the lower lip of the renal pelvis. Infundibular width (IW) is measured at the narrowest point along the lower-pole infundibular axis. The vertical distance between the horizontal line of the lowest point of the lower calyx, where the stone is located, and the highest point of the lower lip is the calyx pelvic height (CPH). CTU images were processed using the multi-planar reconstruction (MPR) technique. RIPA, IL, and IW were measured on the coronal reconstructed images. Renal structures were visualized using 3D volumetric reconstruction (VR) to measure CPH. The length, width, and height of the stone were measured in coronal, sagittal, and axial positions and the maximum stone diameter was determined. The maximum stone diameter was selected, and using the bone window condition, the mean stone density was determined by digitally hand-drawing along the edge of the stone by outlining it (avoiding the inclusion of the surrounding soft tissues) and thus determining the density of the stone [15, 16]. The standard deviation of stone density (SDSD) was used to assess stone fragility [17]. The most commonly used irregular ellipsoid/ellipsoid formula was used for estimating stone volume(SV) [18]. All measurements were made using the PACS Software Program System and transferred to a post-processing workstation for VR reconstruction.

To facilitate clinical interpretation and model construction, several continuous variables were categorized based on clinical relevance, literature evidence, and data distribution. The mean stone density was categorized into three groups based on Hounsfield units: <500 HU, 500–1000 HU, and > 1000 HU. To account for both the location of the lower pole and the RIPA, we adopted a classification method based on prior R.I.R.S scores and categorized inferior pole stones into three groups [11]. The number of kidney stones was recorded as a categorical variable: 1, 2, and ≥ 3. Stone volume was categorized as < 1000 mm³ and ≥ 1000 mm³. Stone localization was classified into five anatomical regions: upper calyx, middle calyx, lower calyx, renal pelvis, and multi-caliceal (stones involving more than one calyx). In addition, baseline demographic and clinical variables were collected, including age (years), gender (male/female), and body mass index (BMI, kg/m²). Preoperative D-J stent insertion was recorded as a binary variable (yes/no). The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification was categorized as class I, II, or III. Surgical characteristics such as duration of the operation (minutes) and laterality (left or right side) were also included in the analysis.

All anatomical parameters were measured on non-contrast CT (NCCT) by two experienced urologists who were blinded to surgical outcomes, following previously described methods [19, 20]. Discrepancies between the two observers were resolved by consensus discussion.

Definition of SFS

The outcome of interest in this study was SFS. Patients were assessed with an NCCT scan one month postoperatively. Residual fragments measuring ≤ 4 mm were considered clinically insignificant residual fragments (CIRFs), and patients with such fragments were defined as having achieved SFS post-RIRS [21, 22].

Variable screening and model construction and evaluation

To identify variables that significantly influence the outcome, we applied the Boruta algorithm and the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. The Boruta algorithm is a Random Forest-based method for evaluating the importance of all variables. It identifies variables that significantly contribute to model predictions by comparing them with randomly permuted noise variables. LASSO regression reduces the risk of model overfitting by applying L1 regularization, which promotes sparse variable selection while maintaining model performance, thereby eliminating redundant predictors.

The intersection of the results from both methods was used to identify the key variables for final model construction. Subsequently, the dataset was randomly divided into a training set and a test set in the ratio of 7:3. The training set was used for model building and the test set was used for internal validation of the model. Based on the screened variables, predictive models were constructed and visualized by column-line diagrams for easy clinical application and interpretation. The potential correlation between maximum stone diameter and stone volume was evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. Variance inflation factor (VIF) ≤ 5 is considered to be the absence of multicollinearity between the variables, which enhances the stability of our predictive model.

In this study cohort, the events-per-variable (EPV) for non-SFS events, calculated alongside six predictor variables, was slightly below the conventional standard of 10 but remains within the acceptable range for contemporary methodological studies [23, 24]. Furthermore, the application of LASSO regularisation with 1,000 bootstrap internal validations further mitigated overfitting risks and enhanced model parsimony.

This study was interested in predicting postoperative SFS in patients with RIRS, which means that we were concerned with model assessment, not with predicting significant differences between variables based on logistic regression. For model performance assessment, the discriminative ability of the model was evaluated using the subject’s work characteristic curve (ROC) and its area under the curve (AUC). The clinical utility value of the model was assessed by decision curve analysis (DCA). Calibration curves were plotted to assess the consistency between the predicted probability and the actual incidence of the model, thus examining the stability and generalization ability of the model. The model was internally validated using bootstrap 1000 times. Although we are concerned with model predictive performance, we still provide an important ranking of predictor variables for our model based on logistic regression coefficients. The Delong test was used to compare the consistency of ROC curves of different models. In addition, the clinical impact curve (CIC) was used to visualize the predictive effect of the model under different risk thresholds.

Statistical analysis

Normality was judged by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables were tested for normality and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for those that conformed to normal distribution and median (Q1, Q3) for those that did not. Normal and variance-aligned continuous variables were tested using Student’s t-test; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U-test was used. The chi-square test was used to compare qualitative data. Categorical variables are expressed as total (n) and percentage (%). Statistical methods regarding model construction and model evaluation were described previously. To facilitate clinical interpretation, total nomogram points were mapped to predicted probabilities of achieving SFS using the logistic regression equation derived from the final model. Predicted probabilities were computed across the full range of total points, and clinically relevant score thresholds were then defined according to the distribution of predicted probabilities. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

At this value of λ, the following eleven variables were determined to be the risk factors for SFS (Fig. 1A and B). Development of a novel nomogram

We developed a nomogram in the training set based on the above six preoperative characteristics for predicting SFS in kidney stone patients undergoing RIRS (Fig. 2). The total score (up to 350) derived from these predictive values was converted to a linear predictive value corresponding to the predicted probability of SFS. The nomogram shows that the lower the total score, the higher the probability of achieving SFS, while the higher the total score, the lower the probability of success.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and internally validated a nomogram for predicting SFS after RIRS. We first screened variables using both Boruta and LASSO algorithms, which converged on six preoperative predictors: inferior pole stone subclassified by RIPA, CPH, stone number, maximum stone diameter, stone volume, and mean stone density. Although maximum stone diameter and stone volume were moderately correlated (r = 0.486), they reflect distinct aspects of stone burden — the linear dimension versus overall three-dimensional mass. Retaining both variables in the model improved clinical interpretability and predictive precision without introducing multicollinearity. A multivariate logistic model was then fitted using these six predictor variables and visualized by a nomogram. The nomogram demonstrated excellent discriminative ability, achieving an AUC of 0.873 in the training cohort and 0.862 in the internal validation cohort after 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Calibration curves also indicated good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, with low mean absolute errors in both sets (MAE = 0.011 and 0.026, respectively). DCA and CIC curves confirmed greater net benefit across relevant thresholds than “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies. In direct comparisons, the nomogram outperformed the R.I.R.S score (AUC: 0.790 in training, 0.772 in validation), the RUSS score (AUC: 0.693 and 0.643), and all individual predictors. Standardized coefficients identified CPH of 30–50 mm and inferior pole stones subclassified by RIPA, especially those with RIPA ≤ 30°, as the most significant predictors of postoperative SFS after RIRS. These findings emphasize the critical role of anatomical features and stone location in determining successful stone clearance after RIRS.

Unlike the Guy’s Stone Score, which assesses stone burden mainly by anatomical complexity and general location without quantifying stone size [25], our model uniquely incorporates both stone volume and maximum diameter as independent continuous predictors. While prior models such as the S.T.O.N.E. score [26] and CROES nomogram [27], both originally developed for PCNL, have included either stone size or surface area, few have simultaneously integrated both stone volume and maximum diameter as independent predictors. Our study suggests that this dual-measurement approach offers a more nuanced assessment of stone load and may enhance the accuracy of SFS prediction after RIRS.

Current clinical guidelines such as the EAU and perspectives from PEARLS members continue to recommend stone diameter as the primary indicator for assessing stone compliance and guiding surgical planning [22, 28]. Stone diameter is easy to measure and correlates with fragmentation difficulty and clearance rates [29]. However, relying solely on diameter may oversimplify the complex three-dimensional nature of renal stones [30]. The R.I.R.S. score includes stone diameter as a categorical variable but does not consider stone volume [11], and the RUSS, which stratifies risk mainly by stone location and anatomy without quantifying stone burden [10], our model incorporates both maximum stone diameter and stone volume simultaneously within the same predictive model [31]. Stone diameter relates to fragmentation efficiency, while volume represents the overall stone burden to be cleared [29, 32]. The inclusion of stone volume as a predictive factor in our model is consistent with the nomogram developed by Ito et al. [33]. Our findings support the necessity of incorporating stone volume alongside diameter to better capture stone complexity and improve individualized risk prediction of stone-free status after RIRS.

The S.T.O.N.E. score was originally proposed by Okhunov et al. [26] who classified stone densities by 950Hu for the assessment of SFS after PCNL, and although they achieved a predictive performance of 0.831, there is still room for improvement. Subsequently, Molina et al. delineated stone density at 750 and 1000 HU to predict stone clearance after rigid and flexible ureteroscopy [34]. By grouping HU into three bands (< 500, 500–1000, ≥ 1000), our nomogram captured a finer gradient of risk, thereby improving the predictive accuracy to an AUC of 0.873. The RUSS score includes the location of the lower pole and poor anatomy as assessed by RIPA, yet the AUC from validation was only 0.707 [35]. Our model enhances this anatomical assessment by additionally incorporating CPH and a refined classification of RIPA angles, offering a more nuanced evaluation of anatomical complexity. Notably, inferior pole stones with a RIPA ≤ 30°were strongly associated with failure to achieve SFS, which aligns with previous anatomical research emphasizing the challenge posed by unfavorable drainage angles [36]. While direct evidence on CPH is limited, it may serve as a novel proxy for assessing vertical anatomical complexity within the collecting system [37]. This vertical dimension could affect the clearance of stone fragments post-RIRS, particularly in conjunction with other known unfavorable anatomical features.

The definition of SFS remains controversial [38]. Although a ≤ 4 mm threshold for residual fragments has been widely adopted to define CIRFs, accumulating evidence suggests that even small fragments may predispose to future stone events. Chew et al. demonstrated that residual fragments ≤ 4 mm are not always benign and can act as a nidus for recurrence or infection [39]. Similarly, Panthier et al. argued for more stringent cut-offs [22]. Nevertheless, the ≤ 4 mm definition remains the most commonly used operational standard in recent clinical series and guideline-based research. In this study, we adopted this conventional criterion for comparability with prior reports, while acknowledging that it may overestimate the true stone-free rate. Future studies should consider separate reporting of CIRFs and prospective evaluation using more restrictive thresholds.

The choice of laser settings and dusting strategy may influence postoperative stone-free outcomes. Recent studies have emphasized the lack of consensus in defining and standardizing “stone dusting.” Moretto et al. systematically reviewed dusting techniques and concluded that variations in pulse energy, frequency, and irrigation management could affect residual fragment size and clearance efficiency [40]. However, we did not perform a comparative analysis between dusting and fragmentation strategies, which represents a limitation of this study. Future work should explore how dusting settings and active dust evacuation impact residual fragments and long-term recurrence risk.

Our study developed a novel and practical nomogram that integrates key anatomical and stone-related variables identified through novel feature selection methods (Boruta and LASSO). Unlike traditional scoring systems such as the R.I.R.S and RUSS scores, our nomogram demonstrated superior discrimination and calibration performance. This graphical tool allows clinicians to intuitively calculate patient-specific probabilities by summing variable scores, facilitating preoperative counseling and decision-making. Moreover, the model was internally validated with strong generalizability and outperformed conventional tools and individual predictors in both training and validation cohorts. In clinical practice, the columnar charts we have developed additionally provide a threshold table for scores and probabilities. Clinicians can use this to aid decision-making by identifying high-risk patients who may benefit from close monitoring or additional interventions, while avoiding unnecessary examinations for low-risk patients. This approach translates statistical advantages into tangible clinical benefits. Meanwhile, we acknowledge that infundibular length and width have been reported as determinants of lower-pole stone clearance. In our retrospective dataset these parameters were considered but were not selected by our pre-specified feature selection pipeline, indicating limited incremental predictive value beyond the six final predictors. Retaining only predictors that add independent information improves parsimony and reduces the risk of overfitting, particularly in datasets with limited event counts. Nevertheless, IL and IW may still be clinically relevant, and future studies with larger sample sizes or standardized imaging protocols should re-evaluate their role.

However, there are some limitations of this study. First, the study is retrospective and single-center, which may limit generalizability. Although internal validation via 1,000-step guided resampling demonstrated robust performance, the absence of external validation limits its generalisability. We plan to conduct external validation through a multicentre prospective study in the future. Another potential limitation is that certain anatomical parameters require manual measurement and may be affected by inter-observer variability. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and standardized imaging protocols should incorporate formal inter-rater reliability assessment to enhance reproducibility and generalizability. Additionally, the classification of anatomical parameters relies on imaging quality and operator judgment, which may introduce bias. Future studies may explore integrating machine learning-based 3D reconstruction techniques and external prospective validation, which could further refine SFS prediction following RIRS.

Conclusions

We developed and validated a predictive nomogram for SFS after RIRS by integrating key anatomical and stone-related parameters. The model demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to existing scoring systems and may serve as a valuable clinical tool for individualized decision-making. However, further prospective validation is required.
