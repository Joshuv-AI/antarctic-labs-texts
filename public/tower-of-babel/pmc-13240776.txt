Balancing cancer recurrence risk and respiratory mortality: Immediate listing for lung transplantation following HCC therapy
Authors: Fadi Zaiem, Vladislav Muldiiarov, Sreeja Biswas Roy, Rajat Walia, Ashwini Arjuna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignancy usually requires a cancer-free interval before lung transplantation, although individualized exceptions are allowed based on tumor biology and recurrence risk. We report a case of urgent bilateral lung transplantation performed 30 days after curative-intent therapy for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in a 66-year-old man with rapidly progressive idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Over 52 months of MRI surveillance, there was no evidence of HCC recurrence. The patient ultimately died 4 years after transplantation from advanced chronic lung allograft dysfunction. This case illustrates that, in exceptionally selected patients with localized HCC and documented response to locoregional therapy, expedited lung transplant listing may be clinically and ethically justified when near-term respiratory mortality outweighs the anticipated risk of cancer recurrence. Such decisions should remain individualized and multidisciplinary rather than broadly generalizable.Keywords: lung transplantation, malignancy, hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

Pretransplant malignancy is generally considered a relative or absolute contraindication to lung transplantation, with most centers requiring a cancer-free interval before listing. Current guidelines, however, support individualized decisions based on tumor biology, recurrence risk, and transplant urgency.1 Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is particularly challenging because recurrence risk depends on tumor burden, vascular invasion, treatment response, and hepatic reserve.2 We report urgent bilateral lung transplantation shortly after curative-intent HCC therapy, highlighting the balance between posttransplant recurrence risk and imminent respiratory mortality.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old man with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis on nintedanib (150 mg twice daily) and remote hepatitis C infection (sustained virologic remission for 12 years) was referred for lung transplant evaluation after 11 months of progressive respiratory decline. During evaluation, pulmonary hypertension was identified, and his oxygen requirement increased from 4 to 10 L/min. Screening liver MRI showed a 2.1-cm segment VI lesion with arterial hyperenhancement and washout, consistent with LI-RADS 5, and a smaller LI-RADS 3 lesion in the setting of hepatitis C-related cirrhosis (Figure 1), without macrovascular invasion or extrahepatic disease. Biopsy confirmed poorly differentiated HCC. Hepatic reserve remained preserved (Child-Pugh A, 5 points; MELD-Na 11), without ascites, encephalopathy, or overt portal hypertension.

Histopathologic confirmation of hepatocellular carcinoma in a cirrhotic liver. (A) Glypican-3 immunohistochemistry (×20) demonstrating diffuse cytoplasmic positivity in tumor cells, supporting the diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma. (B) Hematoxylin and eosin staining (×12) showing hepatocellular carcinoma arising in a background of hepatic cirrhosis.

Six days after biopsy, he underwent transarterial chemoembolization, complicated by acute hypoxemic respiratory failure requiring intensive care and high-flow nasal cannula. Twelve days later, computed tomography (CT)-guided microwave ablation was performed (Figure 2). Given life-threatening pulmonary decline, localized tumor burden, preserved hepatic function, and absence of metastatic disease, the multidisciplinary transplant committee approved listing. Bilateral lung transplantation was performed approximately 30 days after chemoembolization. Explant lung pathology was free of malignancy.

Computed tomography (CT) before and during locoregional therapy for hepatocellular carcinoma. (A-B) Contrast-enhanced CT, axial and coronal, showing a right hepatic lobe lesion consistent with hepatocellular carcinoma in a cirrhotic liver. (C-D) Intraprocedural CT demonstrating the ablation zone at the treated site with expected postablation changes.

The donor was a 41-year-old nonsmoking man without major comorbidities; total ischemic time was 3.9 h. The postoperative course was prolonged and complicated by grade 3 primary graft dysfunction, pneumonia with septic shock, atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury, and tracheostomy followed by decannulation. One year posttransplant, he developed COVID-19 pneumonia and cellular rejection, followed by recurrent acute cellular rejection requiring belatacept. He subsequently developed restrictive chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD). Belatacept was discontinued 3 years after transplant because of leukopenia and a colonic tubulovillous adenoma. MRI continued to show a stable LR-TR nonviable treatment cavity without new hepatic lesions (Figure 3).

Axial (A) and coronal (B) magnetic resonance images show a smaller right lobe treatment cavity (2.2 × 2.0 cm; previously 2.5 × 2.1 cm) with no new suspicious lesions. A prior segment 5 finding represents a focally dilated bile duct with wall enhancement, consistent with cholangitis, with increased left-predominant intrahepatic ductal dilatation/periductal enhancement.

Four years after transplant, he developed antibody-mediated rejection. Despite intensive therapy, he progressed to advanced CLAD, entered hospice care, and died 4 years and 4 months after transplant without evidence of HCC recurrence during more than 52 months of MRI surveillance.

Although consensus statements generally re-- a malignancy-free interval before lung transplantation, ISHLT guidance supports individualized assessment based on tumor biology, treatment response, and expected survival without transplantation.3 This case represents a highly selected multidisciplinary exception rather than a generalizable strategy. The severe early graft dysfunction and complicated postoperative course were likely multifactorial, reflecting respiratory decompensation, pulmonary hypertension, cirrhosis, and limited physiologic reserve more than donor factors or recent HCC therapy alone.4, 5 Despite standard and augmented immunosuppression, MRI surveillance demonstrated sustained oncologic remission for more than 4 years.

In highly selected patients with localized HCC and imminent respiratory failure, expedited lung transplantation may be ethically and clinically justified.
