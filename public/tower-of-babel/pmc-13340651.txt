Eagle syndrome with bilateral stylohyoid ossification: a surgical case report with anatomical correlation
Authors: N. Fagni, G.M. Fiori, F. Paternostro, L. Livi, M. Mandalà, P. Ciabatti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Eagle syndrome is a rare clinical condition characterized by craniofacial pain and a wide range of associated symptoms. This syndrome is characterized by the elongation of the styloid process of the temporal bone and/or the calcification of the stylohyoid muscle tendon. The stylohyoid muscle elevates and retracts the hyoid bone during swallowing and contributes to mandibular depression through its anatomical interaction with the digastric muscle. In this case, clinical evaluation and anatomical knowledge led to the diagnostic suspicion and a minimally invasive and resolutive surgical approach.Case presentation:We report the case of a patient who had suffered for years from dental pain, odynophagia, temporal headache, neck pain, and dysphagia. Clinical and instrumental examinations were normal. CT imaging of the neck revealed elongation and ossification of the styloid processes, consistent with Eagle syndrome. A bilateral open transcervical surgical procedure was performed to remove the ossified segments. The surgery resulted in complete symptom resolution.Discussion:The correlation of symptoms, combined with in-depth knowledge of the functional anatomy of the stylohyoid muscle, enabled the identification of the cause of the symptoms through a thorough diagnostic evaluation. The procedure, aimed at interrupting the altered action of the stylohyoid muscle at its insertion on the lateral body of the hyoid bone, was sufficient, minimally invasive, and led to the complete resolution of the symptoms.Conclusion:The surgical technique adopted proved to be safe and effective, tailored to the patient’s clinical presentation and based on a detailed anatomofunctional correlation.

Introduction and importance

Eagle syndrome, also known as elongated styloid process syndrome, is a condition characterized by elongation of the styloid process of the temporal bone or by calcification of the stylohyoid muscle tendon. The styloid process is conventionally considered elongated when it exceeds 30 mm in length, whereas it falls within the normal range if it measures between 20 mm and 30 mm[1]. The syndrome was first described in the 1940s by Dr. Eagle, who identified two clinical forms: the classic form and the carotid or vascular form[2,3].

This condition is considered a rare cause of craniofacial pain. It has recently been associated with severe acute systemic sequelae, such as transient ischemic attacks when the head is rotated toward the symptomatic side, or carotid artery dissection[4]. Its estimated prevalence is approximately 4–6% in the general population, but only 4–10% of individuals are symptomatic; thus, it is often asymptomatic[5,6].

Eagle syndrome can affect both sexes, although a higher prevalence has been reported in women, especially in older female populations between the ages of 60 and 79[7].

An elongated styloid process may cause a range of symptoms, including neck pain, facial pain, migraines, difficulty chewing, trismus, dysphagia, pharyngodynia, referred otalgia, a globus sensation in the hypopharynx, and restricted neck movement. A case of progressive facial nerve (VII cranial nerve) palsy has also been described[8].

Although Eagle syndrome is considered a rare disease, prompt diagnosis and an individualized treatment plan are essential to alleviate symptoms and improve quality of life[9]. The stylohyoid muscle, along with its tendon, is innervated by the seventh cranial nerve and functions to elevate and retract the hyoid bone during swallowing; it also contributes to mandibular depression through its interaction with the digastric muscle at the lateral body of the hyoid bone. This muscle is part of the so-called styloid muscle group, which plays an essential role in the swallowing mechanism.

A transcervical surgical approach aimed at interrupting the altered action of this muscle at its insertion on the hyoid bone was sufficient to achieve complete regression of the described symptoms, demonstrating that this technique is safe and effective. Through this case presentation, we aim to advance the understanding of Eagle syndrome and provide additional clinical evidence to support the diagnosis and treatment of this rare condition.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[10].

Case presentation

In May 2024, a 58-year-old female patient presented to our department, referred by her family physician, reporting years-long dental pain associated with odynophagia, initially evaluated by a dentist due to the poor response to anti-inflammatory therapy. In the absence of signs of inflammation or malocclusion, a panoramic X-ray of the dental arches was obtained; it yielded inconclusive results.

Her symptoms were accompanied by a bilateral temporal headache, episodes of intense neck pain often associated with head movements, as well as dysphagia and a persistent sensation of a foreign body in the hypopharynx, without relief from the analgesic treatments previously attempted. The patient had no relevant risk factors in her medical history: no family history of head and neck disorders, no allergies, and an otherwise unremarkable past medical history.

During the ENT (otorhinolaryngology visit) physical examination, a flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy was performed to identify potential abnormalities of the hypopharyngeal-laryngeal tract. Still, the findings were within normal limits, showing preserved vocal cord mobility and no evidence of salivary pooling at the glottic plane or in the piriform sinuses. However, a reduced elevation of the larynx during swallowing was observed. The neck was supple and non-tender on palpation.

Specialist knowledge of cervical muscle function in otolaryngology proved essential for identifying the cause of the patient’s reported symptoms, given an otherwise unremarkable medical history. The resulting diagnostic suspicion of Eagle syndrome was confirmed following an appropriate request for contrast-enhanced and non-contrast CT scans of the neck, which revealed elongation of the styloid processes and ossification of the stylohyoid ligaments (Fig. 1A, Fig. 1B). Given the altered motor function and the distinctive CT presentation showing massive and bilateral ossification, it became clear that the best therapeutic choice was to interrupt the pathological muscular action through a surgical approach. A robotic surgical approach was excluded due to the patient’s age, the associated costs, the overall risk-benefit ratio, and the significantly longer operative time, which would have increased both surgical and anesthetic risk.

However, the patient’s good general clinical condition permitted an open transcervical surgical procedure under general anesthesia, which was esthetically optimized by identifying bilateral natural neck creases to provide an ideal access route. An experienced surgeon performed the procedure.

Surgeon’s procedure

The patient was positioned supine on the operating table with slight cervical extension, and general anesthesia with orotracheal intubation was administered. After standard sterile preparation and draping, the surgical field was disinfected with a chlorhexidine solution.

A bilateral external cervical approach was chosen. Two symmetric, minimally invasive horizontal skin incisions, approximately 3 cm, were made in the submandibular–suprahyoid region, following natural skin creases. Dissection was carried out through the subcutaneous tissue and platysma muscle, allowing access to the superficial cervical fascia.

The sternocleidomastoid muscle was identified and gently mobilized laterally, thereby exposing the suprahyoid compartment. Careful, blunt, and sharp dissection was performed to identify and protect adjacent neurovascular structures, including the hypoglossal nerve and the carotid sheath. The lateral portions of the hyoid bone were then exposed bilaterally and used as anatomical landmarks.

The ossified stylohyoid muscles were identified at their insertion onto the hyoid bone on both sides. The ossified stylohyoid tendons appeared as rigid, elongated structures extending cranially from the hyoid bone. After adequate isolation, the continuity of the ossified stylohyoid complex was interrupted by resecting approximately 2 cm of the ossified tendon bilaterally, using bone-cutting instruments. Complete release of the ossified insertion was confirmed visually and manually.

Hemostasis was achieved, and the surgical field was irrigated with saline solution. No drains were deemed necessary. The wound was closed in anatomical layers, and the skin was sutured with absorbable material. The patient tolerated the procedure well, with no intraoperative complications.

The patient was discharged the day after the transcervical surgical approach, showing complete resolution of the previously described symptoms. The result was consistent with the expected outcomes. At the follow-up visits, the first one after a week for suture removal and the second one at 1 month, no symptoms were reported. Visits were performed in an outpatient setting by the original operating surgeon, who performed a complete ENT physical examination, including flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy. In the postoperative course, no complications were observed.

This surgical choice proved safe and effective, allowing for a short operative time and hospital stay with no anesthetic or surgical complications.

Discussion

A negative medical history in a patient without risk factors or a family history of head and neck diseases, combined with long-standing and varied symptomatology, and the absence of a clear suspicion on fibrolaryngoscopy – which revealed an altered dynamic of laryngeal motility associated with odynophagia and a sensation of hypopharyngeal constriction – should raise a red flag in a patient who is otherwise in good general, neurological, and psychological health. This excludes differential diagnoses such as glossopharyngeal neuralgia, conversion disorder[11], or trigeminal neuralgia[12].

The most significant difficulty a physician may encounter in appropriately prescribing a neck CT scan with the correct clinical suspicion lies not only in recognizing Eagle’s syndrome clinically but also in understanding the functional anatomy of the stylohyoid muscle, which, in this atypical case, was largely ossified. This ossification explains the patient’s symptoms, which were caused by an inappropriate and altered motor dynamic resulting from this pathological muscular action. The underlying disease remains of unknown etiology.

Eagle’s syndrome is a rare clinical condition characterized by elongation of the styloid process or calcification of the stylohyoid ligament, which can cause cervicofacial pain, dysphagia, odynophagia, headache, and other symptoms that are often varied and apparently nonspecific. For this reason, diagnosis can be delayed and challenging, particularly when distinguishing it from other conditions[13].

Cases of unilateral and non-massive ossification of the stylohyoid muscle have been reported[14]. In other cases, symptoms were unilateral despite bilateral radiologic evidence of styloid process elongation of the temporal bone. This observation is not functionally related to the action of the stylohyoid muscle[15]. In even rarer cases, variants can lead to syncopal symptoms, vascular compression, and even cerebral ischemia or carotid dissection[16]. Other clinical cases of Eagle’s syndrome have been reported with varying prevalence and symptom profiles.

Both conservative and surgical treatments have been proposed[17]. Conservative treatment is often the first step, especially for patients who refuse surgery or present with mild symptoms, if symptom control is achievable[9]. Initial therapy includes basic analgesics, such as NSAIDs, to control pain. If ineffective, a more articulated pharmacological regimen can be implemented, involving drugs such as gabapentin, tianeptine, tramadol, and paracetamol[18]. This can be combined with local injections of corticosteroids and anesthetics to act directly on the painful area. In some cases, a stellate ganglion block has been added, performed weekly. This approach has led to significant symptom improvement and, in some instances, near-complete resolution[19].

Surgical treatment is generally considered the most effective and long-lasting, especially in patients with severe or persistent symptoms. The surgical techniques are classified into two main approaches: A transoral approach through the tonsillar fossa, either endoscopically[20] or robotically[21], as described in the literature, is mentioned, although there is no clear consensus on its effectiveness or complete symptom resolution. In some cases, the transoral approach was later converted to a transcervical approach due to persistent symptoms. Robotic surgery has shown promising results. An external cervical approach[22], involving an oblique incision near the angle of the mandible, provides access to the styloid process by navigating between the parotid gland and the digastric muscle, thereby offering optimal exposure and a safer, more complete resection. The literature on transcervical approaches, including styloidectomy, supports the effectiveness and safety of this technique[23].

Ultimately, the surgical choice depends on the severity of the symptoms and the response to medical therapy.

In this case, a unique anatomical finding was observed: massive bilateral ossification of the stylohyoid muscle and tendon, along with elongation of the temporal bone’s styloid process, as shown on CT. The symptomatology could not be managed solely through conservative means. Although our department is an accredited and recognized center for ENT robotic surgery, the surgical procedure was explicitly targeted at the tendinous ossification at the insertion of the stylohyoid muscle rather than at the elongation of the styloid process alone. This decision was based on the functional anatomical data and the clinical presentation.

Conclusions

This clinical case highlights the importance of considering Eagle syndrome in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with cervicofacial pain refractory to medical treatment and associated with dysphagia. Furthermore, it demonstrates that knowledge of anatomical and functional anatomy was pivotal in raising diagnostic suspicion, as confirmed by CT imaging showing extensive bilateral ossification of the stylohyoid muscle and tendon throughout their entire length. Due to the rarity of the disease, the diagnosis is unfortunately one of exclusion. The transcervical surgical approach, aimed at interrupting the altered muscular action, proved to be the most effective in this clinical scenario, given the extent of ossification and the reported symptoms attributable to the anatomo-functional correlation. This approach was also safe and effective. This case contributes to the existing literature by introducing noteworthy innovations and presenting an alternative operative strategy.
