Do Brain Extracellular Fluid Concentrations Predict PET‐Inferred Receptor Occupancy in Human CNS? A Mechanistic Model‐Based Analysis of Dopamine D2 Receptor Ligands
Authors: Divakar Budda, J.G. Coen van Hasselt, Elizabeth C.M. de Lange
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We previously developed a mechanistic model framework integrating physiologically based pharmacokinetic model (LeiCNS‐PK3.0), binding kinetics, and receptor dynamics, to predict regional human central nervous system (CNS) receptor occupancy (RO), using brain extracellular fluid (brainECF) concentrations. Positron emission tomography (PET)‐RO is a non‐invasive biomarker to estimate, and validate human CNS RO. However, for highly lipophilic drug buprenorphine brainECF concentrations‐based RO predictions deviated substantially from PET‐RO. This mismatch and the lipophilicity driven brain cell membrane (brainM) accumulation motivated us to investigate relevance of brainECF vs. brainM concentrations for PET‐RO. We identified human studies reporting plasma PK and CNS PET‐RO for D2 receptor ligands spanning lipophilicities. Population PK parameters, derived via model fitting, served as inputs to the mechanistic framework to simulate brainECF and brainM‐based regional RO and compared with PET‐RO. We systematically assessed the impact of lipophilicity, receptor dynamics, endogenous ligands, and schizophrenia‐related physiological changes on simulations accuracy. BrainM concentrations showed better agreement with PET‐RO than brainECF for five of eight D2 ligands. However, neither improvement nor brainM partitioning showed a clear trend with logP; KpM substitution yielded modest improvement. Receptor internalization reduced simulated RO and enhanced PET agreement (remoxipride/haloperidol), independent of lipophilicity. Endogenous dopamine and schizophrenia‐related changes had negligible effects. This study demonstrated that brainM concentrations offer improved PET‐RO alignment for a subset of compounds, independent of lipophilicity suggesting role of other drug‐specific factors in brainM accumulation or lack thereof. The integrated mechanistic framework offers potential to enhance CNS drug treatment, subject to further development via qMRI, hMRI, MRS, and controlled clinical studies.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models of the CNS, such as LeiCNS‐PK3.0, can predict regional brain pharmacokinetics from in vitro and in silico inputs. PET‐inferred receptor occupancy (PET‐RO) is an established clinical tool for quantifying CNS target engagement. For membrane‐bound receptors such as the dopamine D2 receptor, PET‐RO has conventionally been linked to unbound drug concentrations in brain extracellular fluid (brainECF). However, brainECF‐based predictions of PET‐RO showed substantial deviations from clinically observed data for lipophilic compounds such as buprenorphine, raising the question of whether brain cell membrane (brainM) concentrations represent a more appropriate biophase for membrane‐bound receptors.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

When brainECF‐based predictions of D2 PET‐RO show systematic deviations from observed clinical data, can brainM concentrations, simulated within an extended LeiCNS‐PK3.0 framework incorporating receptor binding kinetics, internalization, recycling, and endogenous dopamine competition, resolve these deviations across eight structurally diverse antipsychotics?

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

By comparing model‐predicted brainECF and brainM concentrations against clinical PET‐RO data from 66 participants across eight published studies, this work demonstrates that brainM concentrations provide an improved alignment with D2 PET‐RO than brainECF for five of eight drugs examined, independent of lipophilicity. This establishes brainM as a mechanistically relevant biophase for membrane‐bound CNS receptors and supports the LeiCNS‐PK3.0 framework extended with binding kinetics, receptor internalization and recycling, and endogenous dopamine competition as a quantitative NAM for CNS target engagement prediction.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

This framework enables CNS receptor occupancy prediction from in vitro and in silico inputs, reducing reliance on animal‐based biophase determination. By accounting for brainM partitioning within a mechanistic PBPK‐receptor dynamics model, it supports more accurate dose‐occupancy simulations through drug‐specific biophase selection, with implications for MIDD and rational dose optimization in CNS drug development.

Plasma pharmacokinetics (PK) is often used as the basis to model central nervous system (CNS) drug effects. However, multiple mechanisms separate plasma PK from CNS effects, with rates and extents that are context‐dependent. 1 Therefore, no general relationship between plasma PK and CNS effects can be assumed. To adequately predict CNS drug effects in humans, models must account for CNS drug distribution (including blood–brain barrier transport), target‐site exposure, receptor binding, and downstream transduction processes. CNS drug distribution cannot be directly assessed in humans because human brain sampling is invasive and associated with ethical constraints. 2 As such, there exists a need to infer CNS drug distribution in humans via other means. To this end, we developed the LeiCNS‐PK3.0 model, 3 a comprehensive general CNS physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model incorporating multiple physiologically relevant CNS compartments. These include microvascular space, brain extracellular fluid (brainECF), brain intracellular fluid (brainICF), brain cell membrane (brainM), lysosomal fluid, cerebrospinal fluid in the lateral ventricles, third and fourth ventricles, and subarachnoid space. LeiCNS‐PK3.0 has shown to adequately predict CNS drug concentrations in humans. 3

Once a drug reaches the CNS target site, it interacts with receptor target(s) to elicit an effect. Many receptors that face the brainECF; therefore, brainECF concentrations can provide insights into CNS drug effects. The subsequent drug‐receptor binding kinetics (BK) can be linked to receptor occupancy (RO). 4 A mechanistic approach integrating CNS‐PBPK with BK models enables prediction of CNS target site RO using plasma PK and BK parameters. 5 , 6 To validate such RO predictions, receptor binding can be determined non‐invasively using positron emission tomography (PET).

As part of the QSPainRelief consortium (qspainrelief.eu), 7 we developed the QSPainRelief in‐silico model platform to predict CNS action of drugs. Using our LeiCNS‐PK3.0 model intra‐consortium predicted brainECF concentrations and BK parameters, we simulated human CNS regional RO. When we validated our RO predictions against literature‐based PET‐RO values, 8 , 9 substantial discrepancies were observed for buprenorphine. This sparked the question of whether brainECF concentration is the primary driver for RO. Vauquelin 10 proposed that cell membranes play a central role in extending drug residence time at receptors via three micro‐PK/PD mechanisms: (1) acting as a sink/repository for local drug accumulation; (2) modulating drug conformation/orientation for easier receptor binding; and (3) facilitating rebinding through high local concentrations and proximity. This suggests membrane distribution is a key factor in receptor binding and RO. Buprenorphine is a highly lipophilic drug (logP = 5.0) that might distribute more extensively to brainM than in brainECF. We therefore hypothesize that for highly lipophilic compounds such as buprenorphine, brainM concentrations could represent/contribute as the driver to RO better than brainECF.

In this study, we examine how lipophilicity influences brainM vs. brainECF distribution and the resulting RO for dopamine D2 receptor (D2R) ligands. To this end, we compiled literature data to model plasma and CNS PK in brainECF and brainM using the LeiCNS‐PK3.0 model, predict regional RO, and compare these predictions with reported clinical PET‐RO values. This approach allows us to evaluate the contribution of factors such as logP vs. the microsomal membrane partition coefficient (KpM), receptor internalization, and disease‐related physiological changes to discrepancies between predicted and observed PET‐RO.

METHODS

An extensive literature search identified human PET studies with paired plasma PK and PET‐RO data for D2 ligands. Plasma PK parameters, physicochemical properties, unbound brainECF‐to‐plasma partition coefficient (Kp,uu,BBB), and BK parameters were input to the mechanistic model framework to simulate RO profiles. Mechanistic receptor‐level factors and disease‐related physiological changes were systematically evaluated. Methods follow the modeling workflow: (1) literature data extraction, (2) plasma and CNS PK predictions, (3) RO simulations, with subsections detailing derivations.

LeiCNS‐PK3.0 model inputs for brainECF and brainM concentrations

Plasma PK parameters (clearance, volume of distribution, absorption rate constants, etc.), physicochemical and biological properties of drugs (lipophilicity, ionization rate constants, etc.), unbound brainECF‐to‐plasma partition coefficient (Kp,uu,BBB) together with physiological parameters of human brain (regional blood flow, tissue volumes, brain cell membrane composition, etc.) 3 are used as input for the LeiCNS‐PK3.0 model to simulate human brainECF, brainM concentrations of drugs. Complete parameter values for all eight study drugs are provided in Supplementary S1 . Detailed information about each parameter is presented in the subsections below.

Plasma PK parameters

The plasma PK data for included studies (Table 1 ) were digitized for each dose‐group based on participants, followed by population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) modeling. Each individual PK profile was fitted using the stochastic approximation expectation–maximization (SAEM) algorithm implemented in MonolixSuite‐2024R1, 19 capturing subject‐level variability. Population‐level variability across dose groups was not modeled because brain PET‐RO profiles were reported at the dose‐subject level rather than as population estimates. Interindividual variability was not modeled, as each dataset was fitted independently due to the availability of only median data for a subject group. Residual variability was described using a combined additive and proportional error model; final PK parameters estimated (Supplementary S1 ) were used as input for the LeiCNS‐PK3.0 model.

Physicochemical and biological properties of drugs

Physicochemical properties required for LeiCNS‐PK3.0 were collected or computed from literature (Table S1 ). Octanol–water partition coefficient logP was used to predict the brainM concentrations in LeiCNS‐PK3.0, being the default. However, multiple studies have shown that the microsomal membrane‐water partition coefficient (KpM) is more relevant to predict a drug's membrane distribution. 20 Hence, we simulated brainM concentrations‐based on logP as well as KpM.

Unbound brainECF‐to‐plasma partition coefficient (Kp,uu,BBB)

Kp,uu,BBB is a key input parameter for LeiCNSPK‐3.0 to predict unbound drug concentrations in brainECF, brainM, and other compartments. Human Kp,uu,BBB (i.e., Kp,uu,BBBhumans) were not available for the drugs included in current study, hence Kp,uu,BBBrats were scaled to human using transporter expression at BBB (Supplementary S1 ). Exceptions to this approach were aripiprazole and brexpiprazole. Only unbound brain‐to‐plasma (Kp,uu,brain) values were available for aripiprazole in mice, 22 which were assumed to be equivalent to Kp,uu,BBB and used for scaling from mice to humans. Brexpiprazole Kp,uu,BBBhumans value was derived using steady‐state exposure in the brainECF relative to plasma. 23

Kp,uu,BBB values scaling from rats or mice to humans using BBB transporter expression

Kp,uu,BBB represents net transport across BBB reflecting both influx and efflux mediated by multiple drug‐specific influx/efflux transporters. Particularly, P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) are two major efflux transporters effecting drug concentrations in brainECF and brainM compartments. Therefore, species differences in the expression of active efflux transporters P‐gp and BCRP can be used to translate Kp,uu values using the relative expression factor (REF). 24 All the study drugs were checked for their interaction with P‐gp and BCRP (Table S1 ), which is used as a basis for scaling using REF calculated based on P‐gp, BCRP expression differences between species (Supplementary S1 ) Kp,uu,BBBhuman=REF×Kp,uu,BBBrator mice

(a) If a drug is substrate for only one transporter (either P‐gp or BCRP), REF is determined solely from species difference in expression of that specific transporter that is, REF=Expressionhumantransporter1Expressionrator micetransporter1

(b) If a drug is substrate for both transporters (P‐gp and BCRP), REF is calculated from the combined species differences in expression of P‐gp and BCRP, assuming additive effects on net efflux. Mechanistic model framework input for simulation of D2‐RO

Unbound brainECF, brainM, and their combined (brainC) concentrations predicted from LeiCNS‐PK3.0 (Section 2.2) were used together with D2R expression, dynamics, BK parameters, and mechanistic factors affecting RO to simulate dopamine D2‐RO.

D2R expression

D2R density values in putamen, caudate nucleus, and whole striatum from literature (Table S2 ) were used with anatomical weights of each region to calculate the total receptor expression for respective regions in human brain.

Binding kinetic parameters

Association rate constants (k on) and dissociation rate constants (k off) for all drugs were identified from literature (Table 2 ). The BK parameters used as input in the model were checked for their magnitude and alignment with historically reported k d values (Figure S5 ).

Mechanistic processes affecting D2‐RO

An additional 268 studies were analyzed to identify mechanistic factors affecting D2‐RO, including endogenous dopamine levels, receptor dynamics, and schizophrenia‐related physiological changes.

Endogenous dopamine competition

Endogenous ligands (such as dopamine) compete with exogenous D2R ligands, for binding to the same receptor, which can reduce the RO achieved by the drug. 6 Abi‐Dargham and colleagues 34 demonstrated that patients with schizophrenia have elevated baseline occupancy of dopamine D2 receptors by endogenous dopamine (i.e., higher tonic dopamine levels occupy more dopamine D2 receptors at baseline compared to healthy controls). To assess the impact of this competition on drug RO, simulations were performed in the presence and absence of endogenous dopamine, using literature‐reported dopamine concentrations in the striatum, putamen, and caudate extracellular fluid (Table 2 ). Results are presented for both scenarios: with endogenous dopamine competition and without. Tonic (steady state) dopamine levels were used for the main simulations, while phasic dopamine bursts were incorporated in a separate sensitivity analysis.

D2R internalization and recycling

Internalization and subsequent recycling of dopamine D2 receptors can be induced by ligand binding, thereby reducing the number of receptors available on the cell surface for subsequent binding. 35 This process applies to endogenous ligands (notably dopamine) as well as exogenous ligands (drugs). However, the capacity to induce D2 receptor internalization varies markedly between ligand classes. Full agonists are generally much more effective at promoting internalization, whereas partial agonists and antagonists exhibit only very limited or negligible ability to trigger this process. 36 All compounds included in the present analysis are either partial agonists or antagonists at the D2 receptor. This is further supported by experimental evidence for aripiprazole, cariprazine, and haloperidol showing minimal to no internalization. 37 , 38 Therefore, drug‐induced D2 receptor internalization was set to zero for all compounds, except endogenous dopamine in the current simulations. Internalization and recycling rates were implemented only for unbound D2R and dopamine bound D2R. Rate constants for internalization and recycling were calculated (Supplementary S1 ) from literature data identified (Table 2 ).

CNS physiology changes in schizophrenia

The clinical datasets used included participants with schizophrenia (N = 46) and healthy individuals (N = 20) with median age of 33 years. Literature has shown a reduction in brain intracellular pH and brain phospholipids content 32 , 33 (Table 2 ), which was implemented into the LeiCNS‐PK3.0 as an exploratory, post hoc analysis.

Integrated mechanistic model framework for RO simulations

For the analysis of objectives of the study, a mechanistic model framework was used. LeiCNS‐PK3.0 model 3 is extended with BK, receptor dynamics, and physiological processes using the ordinary differential equations (Supplementary S3 ).

Sensitivity analysis

Postmortem studies have consistently reported elevated striatal D2 receptor density in patients with schizophrenia. 26 In addition, increased dopaminergic transmission, including higher levels of dopamine in relevant brain regions, has been implicated in the disorder. To investigate the potential impact of such physiological variability on predicted RO, we performed a sensitivity analysis. This evaluated the influence of changes in striatal D2 receptor density (ranging from 25% to 200% of baseline values) and endogenous dopamine concentration (covering tonic to phasic ranges, i.e., 25% to 5,000% of baseline) on the model predictions. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was performed on Kp,uu,BBB values ranging between 25% and 400% of the Kp,uu,BBB values used in the simulation.

Software

Plasma PK profiles, and PET‐RO were digitized using Engauge digitizer v12.1. 39 Digitized plasma PK profiles were fitted with SAEM algorithm in MonolixSuite 2024R1. 19 Plasma PK parameters derived, together with physicochemical properties of drugs, Kp,uu,BBB and BK parameters are used as input to our integrated mechanistic model framework 5 , 6 to predict brainECF, brainM, brainC concentrations and resulting RO profiles. The LeiCNS‐PK3.0 and BK models are implemented using the rxode2 package 40 of the R statistical program. 41

Simulations

Doses of the corresponding clinical study designs (Figure S1 ) were simulated for whole time course of plasma sampling and PET image data. One of the participants (Patient 1) in haloperidol 30–50 mg from study 15 had poor model fitting for plasma PK, hence excluded from analysis in our study. Plasma, CNS pharmacokinetics and corresponding receptor occupancies were simulated for 30 virtual participants with inclusion of residual variability only (no IIV was estimated). An exploratory mechanistic analysis was conducted by systematically testing key physiological (schizophrenia‐related changes in brain pH and phospholipid content) and D2R trafficking (receptor internalization rate, recycling rate etc.). All parameters of concentrations, BK were converted to nanomoles (nM) for the simulations (Supplementary S1 ).

Data analysis and evaluation of simulation results

Simulated plasma PK and RO profiles were compared against digitized observed data. For each validation dataset, median of 30 stochastic simulations incorporating residual errors were shown. Predictions are displayed against observed data for brainECF, brainM, and brainC concentrations. Data are displayed by logP to investigate lipophilicity‐dependent membrane accumulation (primary objective of the study). Two metrics were mainly used throughout results one is prediction error (absolute deviation) and relative prediction error (RPE). Absolute deviations from PET observed RO are reported as percentage‐point differences from the line of unity, to enable direct comparison with the literature. A ± 10 percentage‐point deviation means the predicted occupancy differs from the observed PET‐RO value by up to 10 units on the 0%–100% scale (i.e., a predicted occupancy of 70% and an observed value of 60% represent a 10 percentage‐point deviation, not a 10% relative error). RESULTS

In this simulation study, plasma PK and PET data from 8 studies (Table 1 ) involving 66 participants (46 with schizophrenia, 20 healthy individuals) with a median age of 33 years (range: 28–38 years) involving D2R ligands were included for analysis. Results are presented in the order of workflow: first, evaluation of the developed PopPK models for their ability to describe plasma PK data (foundation for LeiCNS‐PK3.0 predictions); then, comparison of LeiCNS‐PK3.0‐based RO predictions against PET‐derived RO, including the influence of receptor‐level and physiological mechanistic factors. All the RO reported are from the human caudate, putamen, and striatum regions.

Plasma pharmacokinetic profiles were well captured with model fitting

PopPK models adequately described the plasma PK profiles of all eight included drugs with minor deviations, serving as reliable inputs for CNS concentration and RO predictions. Minor deviations from the line of unity were observed for aripiprazole and sulpiride (Figure 1 ), but predictions remained within acceptable limits (within twofold error of observed data). One‐compartment models were sufficient for sulpiride, aripiprazole, and remoxipride, whereas two‐compartment models were used for haloperidol, brexpiprazole, cariprazine, raclopride, and risperidone. Detailed plasma PK parameters are presented in Table S3 .

No lipophilicity‐based trend observed in brainM ‐based RO

To assess whether brainM concentrations better represent the biophase for PET‐RO than brainECF in lipophilic drugs, we compared predictions using brainECF, brainM and brainC concentrations for compounds spanning a range of lipophilicity values. No clear trend related to lipophilicity was observed in brainM accumulation or in the performance of brainM‐based RO predictions. Brexpiprazole being the highly lipophilic drug, which is expected to accumulate more in brainM, surprisingly shown inadequate brainM‐based RO whereas, brainECF‐based RO predictions were well aligned with the observed RO values in human caudate nucleus, and putamen (Figure 2 ). Remoxipride and brexpiprazole were best described using brainECF concentrations; risperidone, haloperidol, cariprazine, and aripiprazole using brainM concentrations. Raclopride predictions were similar across all concentrations that is, no differences were observed between brainM and brainC compartment predictions. When the brainM accumulation was modeled using KpM, we have seen the improvement of predictions for sulpiride, risperidone, cariprazine, aripiprazole (using brain cell membrane concentrations), brainECF concentrations for brexpiprazole (Figure S2 ). Hence here onwards we are using the KpM based results for visualization, of analysis of mechanistic factors.

D2R internalization inclusion resulted in improved predictions for remoxipride, haloperidol

To assess whether incorporating D2R internalization improves agreement with PET‐RO, we compared RO predictions with and without internalization across included D2R ligands. Endogenous dopamine competition did not influence RO predictions, hence not shown in main results (Figure S4 ). Inclusion of D2R internalization improved RO predictions alignment with PET‐RO for remoxipride and haloperidol (reduced prediction error) but worsened (increased prediction error) for sulpiride, risperidone, cariprazine, aripiprazole, and brexpiprazole (Figure 3 ). The change in prediction error is CNS compartment concentration‐dependent. Raclopride predictions were unaffected by inclusion of D2R internalization. No noticeable differences were observed between CNS locations caudate nucleus, putamen, and striatum in terms of prediction error with or without inclusion of internalization. Differences between prediction with and without D2R internalization showed inter‐individual variability within each drug as well (Figure S4 ).

Impact of D2 receptor internalization on the RO predictions in human caudate nucleus, putamen and striatum using LeiCNS‐PK3.0 (KpM based predictions). Remoxipride and haloperidol predictions were improved with inclusion of D2 receptor internalization, whereas sulpiride, risperidone, cariprazine, aripiprazole, and brexpiprazole predictions are worsened in a CNS concentration compartment dependent manner.

CNS physiological changes in schizophrenia did not affect predicted RO

To assess whether schizophrenia‐associated physiological changes (reduced intracellular pH and membrane phospholipid content) influence RO predictions agreement with PET, we tested these alterations independently and in combination using LeiCNS‐PK3.0, with RPE (%) as the metric. Schizophrenia‐related changes in physiology that is, intracellular pH, membrane phospholipid (PLs) content reduction shown negligible effect on predicted CNS receptor occupancies when tested independently or combined (pH + PLs) (Figure 4 ). The differences are primarily driven by the CNS compartment concentrations. Sensitivity analysis on top of CNS physiological changes (Figure S5 ) shown only concentration compartment, lipophilicity method, and internalization are important.

Impact of CNS physiological changes in schizophrenia on in human caudate nucleus, putamen and striatum using LeiCNS‐PK3.0 (KpM based predictions). Reduction in pH, phospholipids (PLs), or their additive effect did not impact RO predictions of any drugs across all CNS concentrations.

D2R internalization, KpM with brainM concentrations improved agreement with PET‐derived RO

To investigate discrepancies between model‐predicted and PET‐derived RO, we systematically evaluated the impact of receptor internalization, compartment concentration, and lipophilicity descriptor (logP vs. KpM) on prediction agreement, quantified by RMSE and bias. For most participants' RO predictions are within 10 percentage points from observed PET‐RO except for raclopride (percentage point deviation > 25) (Figure 5 ). The selected best fits showed good agreement for remoxipride, brexpiprazole, risperidone, and haloperidol (RMSE 1.6–8.7%, |bias| ≤ 4.8%), whereas raclopride, sulpiride, and cariprazine exhibited clear discrepancies (RMSE 23.4–27.9%, bias −10.7 to +27.9%). Overall, the combination of D2R internalization, concentration compartment and KpM gave best predictions, instead of logP‐based membrane accumulation modeling.

Representative best RO predictions for individual participants in human caudate nucleus, putamen and striatum using LeiCNS‐PK3.0 (KpM based predictions). D2 receptor internalization rate and brainM concentrations are better than brainECF concentrations as predictors of PET‐RO (as shown by RMSE and bias for selected best‐fit cases). Model predictions closely matched observed occupancy data for remoxipride, brexpiprazole, risperidone, and haloperidol, but showed systematic discrepancies for raclopride, sulpiride, and cariprazine.

DISCUSSION

The integrated approach combining mechanistic models 5 , 6 with non‐invasive imaging such as PET or SPECT 42 offers, unique advantage to improve CNS disorders treatment. BrainECF concentrations have traditionally been assumed to be relevant for PET‐RO, even for highly lipophilic drugs which might have brainM accumulation. 10 In the current study, we systematically investigated the CNS compartment relevant for PET‐RO together with the role of lipophilicity (logP vs. KpM) and mechanistic factors. We evaluated these questions using our mechanistic CNS‐PBPK model LeiCNS‐PK3.0 in healthy and schizophrenia participants (N = 66), and their corresponding (patho) physiological changes. BrainM concentrations provided better agreement with PET‐RO than brainECF for five of eight compounds, though no linear relationship with lipophilicity (logP) was observed. We modeled the brainM compartment as a reservoir (sink) that accumulates drug molecules, creating local concentration enrichment adjacent to transmembrane D2 receptors. This mechanism operates through concentrations gradient‐based PK and grounded in Vauquelin's proposed mechanisms of membrane mediated prolonged drug action. 10

The CNS compartment concentration relevance appears to be specific to drug and patient. Incorporating D2R internalization together with appropriate regional concentrations proved to be a key driver of accurate RO predictions for the ligands studied. Substituting logP with the microsomal membrane partition coefficient (KpM) further improved brainM‐based RO predictions for multiple compounds. Overall, this work presents an in‐silico mechanistic model framework that highlights the relevance of brainM concentrations for CNS PET‐RO. Previous translational PBPK/PD models for human CNS D2‐RO 43 , 44 predicted RO for several antipsychotics but relied solely on brainECF concentrations; with predictions deviating up to 20–40 percentage points from observed data. 43 By incorporating multi‐compartment CNS pharmacokinetics, drug ionization, pH‐dependent ionization, refined passive transport in LeiCNS‐PK3.0, 3 along with endogenous, and systems processes the current mechanistic model framework showed improved agreement with PET‐RO in humans. Overall, this work demonstrates that integrating mechanistic model framework with PET‐RO as a valuable tool that can enhance mechanistic understanding of CNS drug action, using non‐invasive modalities in humans. These findings warrant further controlled experimental studies and potential extension to other CNS therapeutic areas.

No clear trend of association between lipophilicity and BrainM RO was found in the study. Remoxipride and brexpiprazole receptor occupancies were better predicted with brainECF concentrations than brainM concentrations. Brexpiprazole, being the highly lipophilic drug, did not show brainM accumulation, which suggests a possible role of factors beyond lipophilicity alone. For brexpiprazole, Kp,uu value was used from in‐silico brain exposure efficiency model. 23 This limits the possibility to emphasize for brexpiprazole whether the brainECF relevancy observed is due to lipophilicity mechanics or Kp,uu,BBB value. Sulpiride predictions are deviating largely from the observed data based on both brainECF, brainM, or brainC. For risperidone, raclopride, haloperidol, cariprazine, and aripiprazole, predictions are deviating up to 25 percentage‐point deviation, which still offers valuable insights to monitor the therapeutic range in human CNS (65%–80%) for antipsychotics in schizophrenia. 45 Sensitivity analysis Kp,uu,BBB values ranging between 25% and 400% of the Kp,uu,BBB values used in the simulation showed maximally 20 percentage points deviation from the initial simulation results (Figure S7 ).

These results suggests that drug‐specific properties may play a key role in brainM accumulation despite the anticipated lipophilicity alone. When tested using KpM instead of logP an improvement of up to 10 percentage points were observed for haloperidol and sulpiride (Figure S2 ). Though KpM calculated based on logP the improvement in predictions highlights the physiological relevance of KpM to human brain than logP, as further supported by. 20 Overall, these findings demonstrate that lipophilicity alone is insufficient to fully elucidate the relationship between brainM concentrations and RO profiles. Rather, complex drug‐lipid interactions, specifically, membrane partitioning, antipsychotic‐induced structural reorganization, and alterations in membrane heterogeneity 46 modulate receptor accessibility and binding dynamics, representing a critical avenue for future investigation.

Endogenous dopamine competition showed negligible impact on predicted D2‐RO of tested drugs (Figures S3 ). Tonic dopamine levels in striatal extracellular fluid are extremely low compared to drug concentrations in brainECF or brainM, and even exaggerated phasic surges (up to 5000% of baseline) showed no noticeable effect (Figure S5 ). This insensitivity is possibly explained by dopamine's lower D2R affinity (K d = 6,369 nM; 33‐ to 296,000‐fold higher than the tested D2R ligands, Supplementary S1 ), resulting in its rapid dissociation relative to the slower‐off drugs. Sensitivity analysis with changes in D2R expression also did not influence predicted D2R occupancies (Figure S5 ). Incorporating constitutive D2R internalization and recycling reduced predicted occupancy in a CNS concentration‐dependent manner. This improved accuracy for remoxipride and haloperidol but worsened it for the other drugs (Figure 3 ). This observation suggests that drugs (though only partial agonists) might induce D2R internalization; thus, including drug‐specific internalization rates (currently set to zero) can improve model predictions. BK rate constants used in the current simulations were verified against the historical range of binding affinity values (K d) to ensure accuracy (Figure S6 ). It shows a huge discrepancy between used and reported raclopride K d value, which might explain the huge discrepancy between predicted and observed raclopride RO value.

Schizophrenia‐related changes (pH, phospholipids, dopamine levels) did not show any effect on predicted CNS concentrations (not shown) or RO profiles (Figure 4 ). This indicates the possibility that the changes are not big enough to affect the CNS concentrations, and receptor occupancies. Exploratory visualization of best individual participant predictions (Figure 5 ) showed that the combination of brainM concentrations, constitutive D2R internalization/recycling, and the membrane partitioning parameter KpM provided the best agreement with clinically observed D2R ligands PET‐RO data. Despite being only, a selective visualization of best fits, Figure 5 provides insights into the fact that even within the same drug, individual subjects might have different CNS concentration compartment that explains observed PET‐RO values. For most compounds, brainM outperformed brainECF, and the optimal parameter set yielded good predictions (RMSE < 10, bias < 5) for five of eight antipsychotics. Sulpiride, raclopride, and cariprazine retained larger discrepancies (RMSE > 23, bias −10 to +27) despite improved fits. Despite uncontrolled studies, and purely informed by literature data, the LeiCNS‐PK3.0 model predictions provided reliable estimated of RO with RMSE in the similar magnitude of literature D2‐RO models RMSE (0–15.1). 47 Based on these observations we speculate that accurate prediction of clinical D2 occupancy requires drug‐specific selection of CNS compartment, receptor trafficking dynamics, and refined lipophilicity descriptors (such as KpM) rather than logP alone.

The present study provides a strong foundation for integrating mechanistic CNS models with PET‐RO data by successfully simulating both plasma PK and PET‐measured CNS RO in humans. A key strength of this combined approach lies in the ability to systematically explore the impact of patient‐level physiological factors. Despite the strengths, the study overall has few limitations. LeiCNS‐PK3.0 relies on plasma PK parameters estimated from Monolix fits which show good agreement with observed data (Figure 1 ), thus improving the confidence for CNS target site concentration and RO simulations. All predicted drug concentrations are well within twofolds of observed data, except for aripiprazole. For all drugs (including aripiprazole) the SAEM algorithm has achieved full convergence; no improvement was seen with further iterations or model refinement; thus, the best possible model fits are used for parameter estimation and LeiCNS‐PK3.0 based simulations. Kp,uu,BBB values used in the simulations were scaled from rat Kp,uu values using major efflux transporters P‐gp, BCRP expression differences between rats and humans. This is an important data limitation; however, due to ethical, technical, and data constraints, the scaling factor is the best possible approach we can adopt to perform this study. Accurately measured Kp,uu,BBB values would improve the CNS concentrations' reliability, thus overall simulations' accuracy.

For compounds such as remoxipride and aripiprazole, only single measured RO data points were available per drug. This scarcity restricts our ability to assess model performance with respect to inter‐individual variability, which can substantially influence drug concentrations across CNS compartments. More observed data points can improve the strength of the conclusions made in the current analysis. Second, all participants included in the analyzed datasets had received chronic treatment (weeks to months) (Figure S1 ) with the corresponding antipsychotics. Prolonged exposure can alter underlying neurophysiology, including D2R density and endogenous dopamine levels. 26 Although we addressed this potential confounder through sensitivity analyses, the absence of longitudinal data limits solid conclusions.

Future refinements could incorporate serial measurements of physiological parameters via techniques such as non‐invasive in vivo histology using MRI (hMRI), quantitative MRI (qMRI), or MR spectroscopy 48 alongside non‐invasive biomarkers of dopaminergic tone and receptor expression. Such data would allow quantitative adjustment of model parameters and move the framework closer to true personalized prediction, particularly in treatment‐resistant populations such as those with chronic pain or treatment‐refractory depression, where inter‐individual variability in brain exposure is likely pronounced. 49 , 50 Additionally, while our mechanistic framework focused on the sink/reservoir mechanism, the additional two mechanisms proposed by vauquelin, that is, modulation of drug confirmation for receptor binding and facilitation of rebinding are important avenues to explore further in future studies. An important limitation of current investigation is that brainM concentrations are model‐derived and lack experimental measurement in human brain. However, for ethical reasons such experiments cannot be performed in humans. Therefore, we should rely on indirect information, including mathematical model approaches.

The current mechanistic framework is most appropriately applied to membrane‐embedded GPCRs such as dopamine D2 receptors, serotonin receptors (5‐HT), and adrenergic receptors, and considers the following key characteristics: (i) variable BBB transporter substrate status determining drug CNS penetration; (ii) moderate endogenous ligand concentrations; (iii) pronounced receptor internalization and recycling dynamics; and (iv) variable drug physicochemical properties that determine membrane partitioning. Consequently, for a number of CNS receptors the model framework is not applicable, which broadly would include: (i) ionotropic receptors (NMDA, AMPA, GABA‐A) that possess extracellular‐facing binding, for which brain cell membrane partitioning is not applicable; (ii) neurotransmitter reuptake transporters (DAT, SERT, NET) that are dominated by high endogenous substrate concentrations, which fundamentally alters the competitive binding landscape at target; (iii) neuropeptide receptors (NK1, opioid receptors in certain contexts) that maintain constitutively high occupancy from endogenous peptides, preventing significant drug‐occupancy; (iv) extracellular‐only targets (integrins, cell adhesion molecules) that lack membrane partitioning mechanisms entirely; (v) intracellular kinases (Src, JAK kinases) that require intracellular drug penetration independent of membrane partitioning.

In conclusion, our simulation study suggests the role of brainM concentrations and D2R internalization in PET‐RO, and improved predictive capacity of KpM for membrane partition instead of logP. This simulation study provides a strong basis for integration of mechanistic CNS models with non‐invasive neuroimaging, which can improve understanding of CNS drug action. Additional controlled experimental studies will further strengthen these findings, and support translation to clinical practice.
