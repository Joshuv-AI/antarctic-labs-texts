Temporal Exploration of Random Spanning Tree Models
Samuel Baguley, Andreas Göbel, Nicolas Klodt, George Skretas, John Sylvester, Viktor Zamaraev

Abstract
The Temporal Graph Exploration problem (TEXP) takes as input a temporal graph , i.e., a sequence of graphs ( G i ) i ∈ ℕ (G_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}} on the same vertex set, and asks for a walk of shortest length visiting all vertices, where the i i -th step uses an edge from G i G_{i} . If each such G i G_{i} is connected, then an exploration of length n 2 n^{2} exists, and this is known to be the best possible up to a constant. More fine-grained lower and upper bounds have been obtained for restricted temporal graph classes, however, for several fundamental classes, a large gap persists between known bounds, and it remains unclear which properties of a temporal graph make it inherently difficult to explore. Motivated by this limited understanding and the central role of the Temporal Graph Exploration problem in temporal graph theory, we study the problem in a randomised setting . We introduce the Random Spanning Tree (RST) model, which consists of a set of n n -vertex trees together with an arbitrary probability distribution μ \mu over this set. A random temporal graph generated by the RST model is a sequence of independent samples drawn from μ \mu . We initiate a systematic study of the Temporal Graph Exploration problem in such random temporal graphs and establish tight general bounds on exploration time. Our first main result proves that any RST model can, with high probability 1 1 1 In this paper “with high probability” refers to 1 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) 1-o(1) , where o ⁡ ( 1 ) o(1) is with respect to n n . (w.h.p.), be explored in O ⁡ ( n 3 / 2 ) O(n^{3/2}) time, and we show that this bound is tight up to a constant factor. This demonstrates a fundamental difference between the adversarial and random settings. Our second main result shows that if all trees of an RST are subgraphs of a fixed graph with m m edges then, w.h.p., it can be explored in O ⁡ ( m ) O(m) time.

Introduction

Many real-world networks, such as transportation systems, social interactions, and communication infrastructures, are inherently dynamic: their topologies evolve over time as connections appear or disappear
[

A temporal graph is a finite or infinite sequence of static graphs over the same vertex set, where each static graph, called a snapshot, represents the state of the network at a particular time step. Unlike static graphs, temporal graphs can capture crucial aspects of reachability and interaction patterns that depend on the time ordering of edges. This temporal dimension introduces new algorithmic, structural, and combinatorial challenges
[

, which have, in recent years, driven the rapid development of temporal graph theory towards various directions.

A central theme in this development consists of path-related problems: reachability in temporal graphs is defined via time-respecting paths (also called temporal paths), which must traverse edges in chronological order. This makes the reachability relation in temporal graphs very different from the one used in static graphs (in particular, temporal reachability is neither symmetric, nor transitive), resulting in a rich landscape of natural temporal graph problems, including, connectivity

Temporal Graph Exploration.

The goal of the
Temporal Graph Exploration problem
(TEXP) is to determine how quickly one can visit all nodes in a temporal graph starting from a given node using a temporal walk, i.e., using only the edges available at each time step. Note that the temporal explorer is ‘clairvoyant’ as they have knowledge of the snapshot of each time-step. This contrasts work on dynamic networks, where the agent only has information of the past and present set of edges, but not the future. TEXP was introduced by Michail and Spirakis
[
44
]
as a temporal analogue of the classic travelling salesperson problem.

The decision version of
TEXP
, in which one has to decide if at least one exploration schedule exists,
is an NP-complete problem
[
44
]
. In fact, this decision problem remains NP-complete even if the underlying graph (i.e., the static graph consisting of all edges that appear in at least one snapshot) has pathwidth 2 and every snapshot is a tree
[
13
]
,
or even if the underlying graph is a star and the exploration has to start and end at the centre of the star
[
5
]
.

Michail and Spirakis
[
44

proved that

admits no
(2-\varepsilon)

In other words, there is no polynomial time algorithm that outputs an exploration schedule whose arrival time is at most
(2-\varepsilon)
times the arrival time of an optimal exploration schedule.
This was substantially strengthened by Erlebach, Hoffman, and Kammer
[
29

who established NP-hardness of
n^{1-\varepsilon}

\varepsilon>0

In fact, the result was shown for
always-connected
temporal graphs, i.e., temporal graphs in which every snapshot is a connected graph.
This connectedness assumption makes the inapproximability result tight as any always-connected temporal graph can be explored in at most
n^{2}

The strong inapproximability result for

The aforementioned bounds on temporal exploration consider a worse-case analysis, in the sense that they assume that the snapshots are drawn adversarially. Despite the significant attention this setting has received, and the extensive progress made, several core cases remain unresolved and there are huge gaps between many of the best known lower and upper bounds. Arguably the most fundamental case is that of temporal graphs with bounded-degree snapshots, where the best known lower bound is

Motivated by the limited understanding of the Temporal Graph Exploration problem in the adversarial setting, we study the problem on random instances to shed light on its average-case behaviour. Following this line of inquiry, our central question is whether the quadratic bounds for temporal exploration also hold in the average case. To address this, one requires a random model for temporal graphs, and we propose the following.

Random Spanning Tree (RST) model

A
Random Spanning Tree model
is a pair
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
, where
\mathcal{T}
is a set of trees with vertex set
[n]
, and
\mu
is a probability distribution over
\mathcal{T}
.

A random temporal graph
\mathcal{G}=(G_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to be generated by the Random Spanning Tree model, denoted
\mathcal{G}\sim(\mathcal{T},\mu)
, if each
G_{i}
is a tree independently drawn from
\mathcal{T}
according to
\mu

We note that the focus on trees, rather than arbitrary always-connected graphs, is deliberate: trees are minimally connected graphs, and having more edges in snapshots can only facilitate exploration in temporal graphs. Thus, on the one hand, the model is minimal; on the other hand, it captures the core difficulty of temporal exploration.

The main goal of this paper is to comprehensively study the exploration time in random temporal graphs generated by Random Spanning Tree models.

1.1
Our results

We begin our investigation of the exploration time of random temporal graphs generated by RSTs by examining existing deterministic lower bound constructions and adapting them into suitable RSTs to see if comparable lower bounds can be achieved.

A natural starting point is the deterministic construction yielding a lower bound of

Techniques & Proof outlines

In this section we give a high-level outline of the proof strategies used in our main results. Note that some constants are simplified/neglected for ease of presentation.

Exploration in
O(n^{3/2})
time (
Theorem

1.1
).

We start by outlining our proof of explorability of
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
in time
O(n^{3/2})
for arbitrary set of trees
\mathcal{T}
over
[n]
and arbitrary distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{T}
.

The key property (
Theorem

3.2
) of RST that allows us to establish this upper bound is that

(
\star
) every vertex
v\in[n]
has a fixed set of at least
\sqrt{n}

close
vertices, i.e., vertices that
v
can reach in
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
time steps with probability at least 1/9.

This property is sufficient to derive the upper bound by constructing a straightforward exploration schedule in which, on average,
\sqrt{n}
time steps are spent to visit each new vertex.
This is done as follows:

1.

First we build an auxiliary
meta-graph
on
[n]
where two vertices are connected if and only if they are close to each other.

2.

Due to (
\star
), each vertex is in a connected component with at least
\sqrt{n}
other vertices, and therefore there are at most
\sqrt{n}
connected components in the meta-graph.

3.

Each connected component
C
of the meta-graph can be explored in
\Theta(|C|\sqrt{n})
time steps, where
|C|
is the number of vertices in
C
, by first fixing an arbitrary spanning tree of
C
and then traversing it along an Eulerian tour, spending
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
time steps for each edge of the tour.

4.

After exploring the current component, we go to an unexplored component, which can always be done in at most
n
steps (see
Lemma

2.1
).

5.

Summing over all connected components, the total time of this schedule is

\sum_{C}\Big(O(|C|\sqrt{n})+n\Big)=O(\sqrt{n})\cdot\sum_{C}|C|+\sum_{C}n=O(n^{3/2}).

The proof of (
\star
) forms the technical core of this upper bound. It proceeds by assuming the existence of a vertex
w
with fewer than
\sqrt{n}
close vertices and arriving at a contradiction by demonstrating the existence of a set of vertices that, in expectation, can reach more than
n
distinct vertices.

A key tool in the proof is a natural temporal analogue of the Depth-First search (DFS) algorithm, which, to the best of our knowledge, is introduced here for the first time (
Section

3.1
).
Roughly speaking, the
temporal DFS algorithm
discovers one new vertex in each snapshot
F
by revealing the first undiscovered vertex according to the (static) DFS order of
F
.

A more detailed outline of the proof of (
\star
) is as follows:

1.

We fix a set
B
of vertices that
w
can discover via the temporal DFS algorithm (and therefore can reach via temporal path) in
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
time steps with a constant probability.

2.

Each vertex in
B
is close to
w
, and thus, by our assumption,
|B|<\sqrt{n}
.

3.

This upper bound on
|B|
allows us to show (
Corollary

3.16
) that w.h.p. among
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
snapshots a constant fraction of them contain an edge that connects a vertex in
B
with a vertex which is far from (i.e. not close to)
w
.

4.

We show that each such far vertex
u
, in the subsequent
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
time steps, in expectation discovers via temporal DFS at least
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
vertices none of which can be reached from
B
during the same time interval (
Lemma

3.13
).

5.

We further show that all these sets of
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
vertices are disjoint, and thus they add up to more than
n
vertices, leading to the sought contradiction.

Lemma

3.13

Lemma

3.9
). Roughly speaking, for a fixed vertex
v
and two independent random temporal graphs, w.h.p., the sets of vertices discovered from
v

1/(2n)

O(m)

Recall, in

For each edge

Using this strategy, Erlebach, Hoffmann, and Kammer
[
29
]
showed that,
in expectation
,
\mathcal{G}

The main obstacle to exploring the graph in

Theorem

is to replace such high-weight edges with fast temporal paths. This substitution requires the explorer to have access to future snapshots.

We outline this strategy in more detail below:

1.

Building the backbone forest
:
Denote by
T_{\mathsf{min}}
the minimum-weight spanning tree of
G
.
Among the edges of
T_{\mathsf{min}}
, we select those with sufficiently low weight. These low-weight edges form a
backbone

F

2.

Partitioning backbone components into fast components
:
In order to establish fast connections between the connected components of the backbone forest
F
,
we partition each such component into subtrees, which we call
fast components
. These are constructed to have both small diameter and only ultra low-weight edges, ensuring that traversal within each fast component is fast.
We further guarantee that the total number of the fast components is not too large, which allows us to efficiently connect the backbone components together.

3.

Connecting backbone components via meta-edges
:
Using the bound on the total number of fast components, we show that in any partition of the fast components into two non-empty sets, there exists w.h.p. a pair of components—one from each set—such that an edge connects them during any short time interval. This property implies the existence of a fast temporal path between any source and destination vertices in these fast components, starting at any time step.

Specifically, by looking ahead into the future, we can identify a connecting edge between the two fast components within a short time interval. We then travel from the source vertex to an endpoint of the connecting edge before the interval begins, cross to the other component as soon as the edge becomes available, and continue to the destination vertex. Overall, the resulting temporal path is fast due to efficient traversal within fast components and the guaranteed presence of a connecting edge in every short time interval.

We mark the existence of such an efficient connection between the two fast components by creating a meta-edge between an arbitrary pair of vertices, with one vertex from each of the components.

4.

Building efficient exploration
:
Since any bipartition of the connected components of the backbone forest
F
induces a bipartition of the fast components, we can use meta-edges to greedily merge the connected components of
F
into a tree. We denote this tree by
H
and use it to construct an efficient exploration schedule.

To this end, we fix an Euler tour of
H
and define the exploration schedule by following this tour. We show that the total exploration time of this schedule is
O(m)
w.h.p., by separately bounding the total traversal time of the edges in the backbone forest and the total traversal time of the meta-edges in
H
.

Future directions and open problems

Our study of the Temporal Graph Exploration problem in Random Spanning Tree models suggests several interesting questions and research directions, some of which we outline below.

Our next conjecture concerns algorithmic aspects of temporal exploration of RSTs. A natural exploration strategy is greedy exploration, which starts from an arbitrary vertex, looks into the future to find the closest unvisited vertex, visits that vertex at the earliest possible time, and then repeats the process: looking ahead to discover the next closest unvisited vertex and visiting it as early as possible from the current position, and so on. While this is not always an effective strategy in adversarially constructed deterministic temporal graphs, we believe that in the RST models, this strategy performs within a constant factor of the optimal.

Beyond common temporal graph properties and algorithmic problems one can study on temporal graphs generated by Random Spanning Tree models, there are two natural ways to generalise the model.

First, one can consider arbitrary sets of
n
-vertex graphs as snapshots, rather than only spanning trees.
For example, in the context of temporal graph exploration, if there is at least one connected snapshot in the support of
\mu
, then the exploration time is almost surely finite. It is possible that the assumption of always-connectedness can also be relaxed in a more meaningful way by providing some conditions on the set of snapshots and
\mu
which guarantee ‘fast’ exploration times even when none of the snapshots are connected. A simple example here is the uniform measure over graphs consisting of a pair of cliques of size
n/2
, which is explorable (even online) in time
O(n)
.

Second, one can introduce dependencies between snapshots by replacing the distribution
\mu
with a Markov chain whose states are unique snapshots (note that the RST models would be a special case of this model). Such a generalisation would provide some interpolation between completely independent snapshots and deterministic temporal graphs; for example, it would capture all deterministic temporal graphs with pairwise distinct snapshots as well as periodic temporal graphs. It would be interesting to understand which properties of such Markov chains lead to fast exploration, where an obvious pair would be ergodic and rapidly mixing. Several specific instances of such random temporal graphs have appeared previously, such as edge Markovian
[
17
,
25
]
and mobile geometric
[
48
,
39
]
graphs. The flooding problem has been studied on more general Markovian models
[

]
.

2
Preliminaries

For a natural number
n
, we use standard notation
[n]
to denote the set
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

A
temporal graph

\mathcal{G}
is a finite or infinite sequence
G_{1}=(V,E_{1}),G_{2}=(V,E_{2}),G_{3}=(V,E_{3}),\ldots
of static graphs over the same vertex set
V
, which are called the snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
. The
lifetime
of
\mathcal{G}
is the number of snapshots it has. The static graph over the vertex set
V
that has an edge between two vertices if and only if these vertices are adjacent in at least one snapshot, is called the
underlying graph
of
\mathcal{G}
. A
time-edge
of
\mathcal{G}
is a pair
(e,t)
, where
t
is a natural number not exceeding the lifetime of
\mathcal{G}
and
e\in E_{t}
; the number
t
is called the time step of the time-edge
(e,t)
.
The temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
is
always-connected
if each of its snapshots is a connected graph.

A
temporal path
(resp.
temporal walk
) in
\mathcal{G}
is a sequence
((e_{1},t_{1}),\dots,(e_{\ell},t_{\ell}))
of time-edges of
\mathcal{G}
such that
(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{\ell})
is a path (resp. walk) in the underlying graph of
\mathcal{G}
and
t_{i}<t_{i+1}
holds for every
i\in[\ell-1]
.
A vertex
v\in V
can
reach
a vertex
u
in
\mathcal{G}
by time step
t
, if there exists a temporal path from
v
to
u
whose last time-edge has time at most
t
. Given a set of vertices
S\subseteq V
, we denote by
R_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}(S)
the set of vertices each of which can be reached by time
t
from some vertex in
S
. If
S
consists of a single vertex
v
, for brevity, we write
R_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)
as a shorthand for
R_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}(\{v\})
.

Given a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}=(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots)
and time steps
i,j
such that
i\leq j
and
j
does not exceed the lifetime of
\mathcal{G}
, we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{[i,j]}
the temporal graph
(G_{i},G_{i+1},\ldots,G_{j})
.

For a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
of finite lifetime, the
reverse
of
\mathcal{G}
, denoted
\overleftarrow{\mathcal{G}}
, is the temporal graph whose sequence of snapshots is the reverse of the sequence of snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
.

Temporal Exploration.

Given a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
, we denote by
\texp(\mathcal{G})
the minimum number
t
such that every vertex of
\mathcal{G}
can be visited via a temporal walk starting at some vertex of
\mathcal{G}
within the first
t
snapshots. If no such
t
exists, then we define
\texp(\mathcal{G})
to be
\infty
.
It is common to describe exploration schedules in terms of an explorer moving along a temporal walk. In this context, we use the terms
travel
and
traverse
to refer to the act of the explorer moving along the walk.

We also consider exploration of temporal graphs by an ‘online’ explorer, which can only see the current snapshot.
An online exploration algorithm for
\mathcal{G}
is a function
A
that, given a time step
t
, the partially revealed temporal graph
\mathcal{G}_{[0,t]}
, the current vertex
v
, and the set of unexplored vertices
U
, outputs the next vertex to visit, which is either the current vertex
v
or one of its neighbors at time step
t
.
We denote by
\Dexp_{A}(\mathcal{G})
the smallest time step by which every vertex of
\mathcal{G}
is visited by algorithm
A
.

We need the following standard yet useful lemma, which says that in any
n
-vertex always-connected temporal graph any vertex can reach any other vertex in at most
n
time steps.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
29
, Lemma 2.1]
,
[
41
, Observation 2.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an always-connected temporal graph on
n
vertices. Then for any two vertices
v
and
u
of
\mathcal{G}
, and any
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
t+n-1
does not exceed the lifetime of
\mathcal{G}
, the temporal graph
\mathcal{G}_{[t,n+t-1]}
has a temporal path from
v
to
u
.

Probability.

Let
X
and
Y
be random variables. We say that
X

stochastically dominates

Y
if
\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X\geq x\,\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,Y\geq x\,\right]
for all real numbers
x
, and denote this by
X\succeq Y
.
We will also make use of a number of concentration inequalities, which can be found in
Appendix

A
.

In this section we prove that for any RST model
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
,
a random temporal graph

\mathcal{G}\sim(\mathcal{T},\mu)
can be explored in time
\Theta(n^{3/2})
with high probability.
Throughout we assume that a set
\mathcal{T}
of trees on vertex set
[n]
and a probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathcal{T}
are arbitrary but fixed.

This result is based on the fundamental property of
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
that for every vertex
v\in[n]
in
\mathcal{G}\sim(\mathcal{T},\mu)
there are
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
vertices that are “close” to
v
.

To state this property formally, we first introduce a notion that measures closeness between vertices in an RST model.
Recall, given a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
, a vertex
v
of
\mathcal{G}
, and a natural number
t
, the set of vertices that
v
can reach in the first
t
snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
is denoted by
R_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
u,w\in[n]
,
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in[0,1]
. We say that
w
is
(t,p)
-close to
u
in
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
if

\underset{\mathcal{G}\sim(\mathcal{T},\mu)}{\mathbb{P}}\left[w\in R_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}(u)\right]\geq p.

Note, for a finite
T\in\mathbb{N}
, any
\mathcal{G}_{[1,T]}
and its reverse
\overleftarrow{\mathcal{G}_{[1,T]}}
have the same probability to be sampled in
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
. Therefore, a vertex
w
is
(t,p)
-close to a vertex
u
if and only if
u
is
(t,p)
-close to
w
. In other words, the
(t,p)
-closeness relation is symmetric, and we can talk about
u
and
w
being
(t,p)
-close to each other.
If
u
and
w
are not
(t,p)
-close to each other, then we say that they are
(t,p)
-far
from each other in
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
. When
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
is clear from the context we may say that
u
and
w
are
(t,p)
-close to each other or
(t,p)
-far from each other without specifying
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
.

Using the notion of
(t,p)
-closeness, the above-mentioned property of
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
can be stated as follows.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large,
\mathcal{T}
be a set of trees on vertex set
[n]
, and
\mu
be a probability distribution on
\mathcal{T}
.
Then, for every vertex
v\in[n]
there are at least
\sqrt{n}
vertices that are
(700\sqrt{n},1/9)
-close to
v
in
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
.

Most of this section is devoted to proving this result. Before proceeding to the proof, we first use
Theorem

3.2
to establish our main result.

See

Proof.

Given
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
, we define a
meta-graph

on

(700\sqrt{n},1/9)

\sqrt{n}

For traversing the spanning trees of the connected components, we observe that if two vertices
u

(700\sqrt{n},1/9)

Temporal Depth-First Search

The standard Depth-First Search (DFS) algorithm for a

Given a vertex

We introduce a natural temporal generalisation of DFS, which we call the

Given an always-connected temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
and a vertex

\mathcal{G}

\sigma_{v,F}

\sigma_{v,F}

.

the first vertex

under the order
\sigma_{v,F}
such that
w
is outside
D
, but every vertex preceding

in the order
\sigma_{v,F}
belongs to
D
.

The following pseudocode describes the temporal DFS algorithm formally: given

3.2
Temporal DFS over RST

In this section we analyse the behaviour of temporal DFS over the Random Spanning Tree model.
We start with some notation and basic facts that will be useful in proving the main statements
Lemmas

3.9
,
3.12
and
3.13
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
D_{1}\subseteq D_{2}\subseteq[n]
,
v\in D_{1}
, and
F
be a tree on
[n]
. If
\rho(v,D_{1},F)\in[n]\setminus D_{2}
, then
\rho(v,D_{1},F)=\rho(v,D_{2},F)
.

Proof.

Let
u:=\rho(v,D_{1},F)
.
By definition, all vertices smaller than
u
in
\sigma_{v,F}
are in
D_{1}
. Since
D_{1}\subseteq D_{2}
, all those vertices are also in
D_{2}
.
Therefore,
u
is the minimum vertex in
\sigma_{v,F}
that is outside
D_{2}
such that all vertices smaller than
u
in order
\sigma_{v,F}
belong to
D_{2}
. That is, by definition,
\rho(v,D_{2},F)=u
.
∎

Let
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
be an RST, and
v
be a vertex.
For a given set
D\subseteq[n]
such that
v\in D
, the distribution
\mu
naturally determines a probability distribution over the next vertices reached by the temporal DFS algorithm from vertex
v
. Formally, for every
u\in[n]
,

\nu_{v,D,\mu}(u):=\underset{F\sim\mu}{\mathbb{P}}[\rho(v,D,F)=u]

We say that a vertex
u
is
(v,D,\mu)
-good
if
\nu_{v,D,\mu}(u)\geq\frac{1}{2n}
.

The following observation follows from the fact that for any tree
F
, any sets
D_{1}\subseteq D_{2}\subseteq[n]
, and any vertices
v\in D_{1}
and
u\in[n]\setminus D_{2}
, we have that
\rho(v,D_{1},F)=u
implies
\rho(v,D_{2},F)=u
, which is due to
Lemma

3.4
.

Observation 3.5
.

Let
D_{1}\subseteq D_{2}\subseteq[n]
,
v\in D_{1}
, and
u\in[n]\setminus D_{2}
. Then
\nu_{v,D_{1},\mu}(u)\leq\nu_{v,D_{2},\mu}(u)
. In particular, if
u
is
(v,D_{1},\mu)
-good, then
u
is
(v,D_{2},\mu)
-good.

A key property of the
(v,D,\mu)
-good vertices is that they together carry at least half of the total probability mass of
\nu_{v,D,\mu}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
D\subseteq[n]
and
v\in D
. Then

\sum\limits_{u\text{ is }(v,D,\mu)\text{-good}}\nu_{v,D,\mu}(u)\geq 1/2.

Proof.

If
u\text{ is not }(v,D,\mu)\text{-good}
, then
\nu_{v,D,\mu}(u)<\frac{1}{2n}
. Thus,

\sum\limits_{u\text{ is }(v,D,\mu)\text{-good}}\nu_{v,D,\mu}(u)=1-\sum\limits_{u\text{ is not}(v,D,\mu)\text{-good}}\nu_{v,D,\mu}(u)>1-n\cdot\frac{1}{2n}\geq 1/2,

as
\nu_{v,D,\mu}
is a probability measure on
[n]
.
∎

Definition 3.7
.

Let
v\in[n]
,
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in[0,1]
. The
temporal DFS
(t,p)
-ball
of
v
, denoted
B_{t}^{p}(v)
, is the set of vertices that the temporal DFS algorithm reaches in
\mathcal{G}\sim(\mathcal{T},\mu)
in
t
steps with probability at least
p
, i.e.,

B_{t}^{p}(v):=\{w\in[n]\mid\underset{\mathcal{G}\sim(\mathcal{T},\mu)}{\mathbb{P}}\left[w\in D_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)\right]\geq p\}.

Since
D_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)\subseteq R_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)
, we have the following

Observation 3.8
.

Every vertex in
B_{t}^{p}(v)
is
(t,p)
-close to
v
.

We are now ready to prove the main property of the temporal DFS algorithm over RST. Informally, this property states that for a fixed vertex
v
and two independent random temporal graphs, w.h.p., the sets of vertices discovered from
v
via temporal DFS in the two temporal graphs either (1) overlap substantially, or (2) each contains a large subset of private vertices, each of which is good with respect to the opposite set, that is, it can be discovered from the opposite set in one step of the temporal DFS algorithm with probability at least
1/(2n)
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
v\in[n]
and
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}\sim(\mathcal{T},\mu)
be independent. Let
T\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
T\leq n
. Then, with probability at least
1-4e^{-T/500}
, we have

(i)

|D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)\cap D_{T}^{\mathcal{H}}(v)|\geq\frac{T}{50}
, or

(ii)

D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)
contains at least
\frac{T}{50}

(v,D_{T}^{\mathcal{H}}(v),\mu)
-good vertices, and
D_{T}^{\mathcal{H}}(v)
contains at least
\frac{T}{50}

(v,D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v),\mu)
-good vertices.

Proof.

To prove the lemma it is enough to show that with probability at least
1-2e^{-T/500}
we have

(i)

|D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)\cap D_{T}^{\mathcal{H}}(v)|\geq\frac{T}{50}
, or

(ii)

D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)
contains at least
\frac{T}{50}

(v,D_{T}^{\mathcal{H}}(v),\mu)
-good vertices.

The result then follows by applying the union bound.

To facilitate the argument, we sample
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}\sim(\mathcal{T},\mu)
in two phases:

(i)

first, we run for
T
rounds the coupling coin-flipping process defined below. This will generate the first
i
snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
and the first
j
snapshots of
\mathcal{H}
, where
i+j=T
;

(ii)

then, we continue sampling snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
independently (i.e. without using the coupling coin-flipping process).

The coupling coin-flipping process

Repeat the following steps in rounds:

1.

Sample snapshot
F\sim\mu
.

2.

Flip a fair coin and depending on the outcome add
F
to
\mathcal{G}
or
\mathcal{H}
.

This process results in temporal graphs
\mathcal{G}
,
\mathcal{H}
independently sampled from
(\mathcal{T},\mu)
. The first phase is a convenient coupling that will allow us to prove the target result.

To do this, we will reveal snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
iteratively and, abusing notation, we will denote by
D_{\mathcal{G}}
and
D_{\mathcal{H}}
the sets of vertices that are reached from
v
by the temporal DFS algorithm in
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, respectively, using the revealed snapshots.

We will show that by the end of phase (i), with probability at least
1-2e^{-T/500}
we have

(1)

|D_{\mathcal{G}}\cap D_{\mathcal{H}}|\geq\frac{T}{25}
, or

(2)

D_{\mathcal{G}}
contains at least
\frac{T}{25}
many
(v,D_{\mathcal{H}},\mu)
-good vertices.

We claim that this is enough to deduce the lemma. Indeed, if (1) is true at the end of the first phase, then it is also true for the second phase. Otherwise, if
u\in D_{\mathcal{G}}
is
(v,D_{\mathcal{H}},\mu)
-good by the end of the first phase, then, by the time
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
each acquire
T
snapshots,
either
u
will be reached by temporal DFS in
\mathcal{H}
, in which case it will belong to

, or, otherwise, by

, it will stay

-good.

, or

(v,D_{\mathcal{H}},\mu)
-good vertices.

Thus, in the rest of the proof, we prove the desired outcome of the first phase of the sampling process.

, if

, and, respectively,

, if

.

Claim 3.10
.

If

, then either
u_{\mathcal{G}}\in D_{\mathcal{H}}
or
u_{\mathcal{H}}\in D_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Proof of Claim.

If
\sigma_{v,F}(u_{\mathcal{G}})<\sigma_{v,F}(u_{\mathcal{H}})
, then, by definition,

u_{\mathcal{G}}\in D_{\mathcal{H}}
.
Similarly, if
\sigma_{v,F}(u_{\mathcal{H}})<\sigma_{v,F}(u_{\mathcal{G}})
, then
u_{\mathcal{G}}=\rho(v,D_{\mathcal{G}},F)
implies

.
∎

Let

be the number of rounds of the coin-flipping process in which the vertex

was

-good at the moment of drawing the corresponding snapshot

.

Claim 3.11
.

After

Proof of Claim.

By

, at every round, the probability of

to be

∎

By

, at the end of the coin-flipping process, with probability at least

we have

. Among the rounds in which

.
We split the analysis in two cases:

(i)

. In each of the rounds where

, the corresponding snapshot

, in which case

). Thus, as before, we can conclude from

, that with probability at least

the number of rounds when

is added to

contains at least
T/25

contains at least
T/25

(v,D_{\mathcal{H}},\mu)

(ii)

\tau_{2}\geq\tau/2\geq T/5
. In each of the rounds, in which we have

, by

, either

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be large enough,
v\in[n]
, and
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}\sim(\mathcal{T},\mu)
be independent. Let
T\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
20\sqrt{n}\leq T\leq n/2
.
Let
S\subseteq[n]
be any set that is independent of the snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
from
T+1
to
2T
, but may depend on the first
T
snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
. Then

(i)

\mathbb{P}[D_{2T}^{\mathcal{H}}(v)\cap S\neq\emptyset]\geq 1/3
; or

(ii)

\mathbb{P}[|D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)\setminus S|\geq T/100]\geq 1/3
.

Proof.

We start by introducing notation for the following events:

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=\{D_{2T}^{\mathcal{H}}(v)\cap S\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle:=\{|D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)\setminus S|\geq T/100\},

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{|D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)\cap D_{T}^{\mathcal{H}}(v)|\geq\frac{T}{50}\},

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{matrix}D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)\text{ contains at least }\frac{T}{50}\text{ $(v,D_{T}^{\mathcal{H}}(v),\mu)$-good vertices}\end{matrix}\right\}.

In this notation, our goal is to show that

\mathbb{P}[Q]\geq 1/3\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\mathbb{P}[W]\geq 1/3.

(1)

We will instead prove that

\mathbb{P}[Q\mid Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}]\geq 2/5\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\mathbb{P}[W\mid Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}]\geq 2/5.

(2)

Indeed,
Eq.

2
together with the law of total probability, and the bound
\mathbb{P}[Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}]\geq 1-4e^{-T/500}\geq 5/6
(by
Lemma

3.9
), imply
Eq.

1
.

To prove (
2
), we suppose that
\mathbb{P}[\lnot W\mid Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}]\geq 3/5
, and show that this implies
\mathbb{P}[Q\mid Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}]\geq 2/5
. Note that
\mathbb{P}[\lnot W\cap(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})]>0
, or else we contradict our assumption.
Observe that,

\mathbb{P}[Q\mid Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}]\geq\mathbb{P}[Q\mid\lnot W\cap(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\lnot W\mid Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}]\geq\frac{3}{5}\cdot\mathbb{P}[Q\mid\lnot W\cap(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})].

(3)

Notice that we cannot have both
\mathbb{P}[\lnot W\cap Q_{1}]=0
and
\mathbb{P}[\lnot W\cap(Q_{2}\setminus Q_{1})]=0
, since
\mathbb{P}[\lnot W\cap(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})]>0
. We now assume that

\mathbb{P}[\lnot W\cap Q_{1}]>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}[\lnot W\cap(Q_{2}\setminus Q_{1})]>0.

(4)

Then, by the law of total probability, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[Q\mid\lnot W\cap(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[Q\mid\lnot W\cap Q_{1}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[Q_{1}\mid\lnot W\cap(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{P}[Q\mid\lnot W\cap(Q_{2}\setminus Q_{1})]\cdot\mathbb{P}[Q_{2}\setminus Q_{1}\mid\lnot W\cap(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})]

\displaystyle\geq\min\{\mathbb{P}[Q\mid\lnot W\cap Q_{1}],\,\mathbb{P}[Q\mid\lnot W\cap(Q_{2}\setminus Q_{1})]\}.

(5)

We now estimate each of the terms in (
5
) individually. However, first observe that we can assume w.l.o.g. that (
4
) holds, as otherwise we just end up with a simpler expression for (
5
) only involving one of the two conditional probabilities.

Estimation of
\mathbb{P}[Q\mid\lnot W\cap Q_{1}]
. We claim that

\mathbb{P}[Q~|~\lnot W\cap Q_{1}]=1.

(6)

Indeed, assuming
|D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)\cap D_{T}^{\mathcal{H}}(v)|\geq\frac{T}{50}
(event
Q_{1}
) and
|D_{T}^{\mathcal{G}}(v)\setminus S|<T/100
(event
\lnot W
), it is easy to see that
D^{\mathcal{H}}_{T}(v)\cap S\neq\emptyset
, and hence, by the monotonicity of
D^{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(v)
, we have
D^{\mathcal{H}}_{2T}(v)\cap S\neq\emptyset
(event
Q
).

Estimation of
\mathbb{P}[Q\mid\lnot W\cap(Q_{2}\setminus Q_{1})]
. Assuming
D^{\mathcal{G}}_{T}(v)
contains at least
\frac{T}{50}
many
(v,D^{\mathcal{H}}_{T}(v),\mu)
-good vertices (event
Q_{2}\setminus Q_{1}
) and
|D^{\mathcal{G}}_{T}(v)\setminus S|<\frac{T}{100}
(event
\lnot W
), it follows that

D^{\mathcal{G}}_{T}(v)\cap S
contains at least
\frac{T}{100}
many
(v,D^{\mathcal{H}}_{T}(v),\mu)
-good vertices.
Then, at least one of these
(v,D^{\mathcal{H}}_{T}(v),\mu)
-good vertices will be reached by the temporal DFS algorithm in
\mathcal{H}
in the next
T
rounds, implying

, with probability at least

1-\left(1-\frac{T}{200n}\right)^{T}.

Recall that for

and

we have
(1-\frac{x}{n})^{n}\leq e^{x}
. Thus for

we have

\left(1-\frac{T}{200n}\right)^{T}=\left[\left(1-\frac{T}{200n}\right)^{200n}\right]^{T/200n}\leq\left[e^{-T}\right]^{T/200n}=e^{-T^{2}/200n}.

(7)

Returning to the main thread of the proof of (
2

), (

), (

), (

), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[Q\mid Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{5}\cdot\mathbb{P}[Q\mid\lnot W\cap(Q_{2}\setminus Q_{1})]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{5}\cdot(1-e^{-T^{2}/200n})

\displaystyle\geq 2/5,

establishing (

), where the last inequality follows since

.
∎

Given a star, we call the unique vertex with degree greater than one its

The first one is at the heart of our arguments and is used implicitly in

In this lemma, we use a slightly more general notion of “stars” that allows for isolated vertices. Let

Lemma 5.1

Time to visit non-centres.

According to

Roughly speaking,

(23)

The next lemma shows that for a fixed

concentrates around

Lemma 5.6
.

There exists
c>0
such that, for any

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\big|t_{\ell}-\ell\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right]\big|\geq t\,\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-ct^{2}}{\ell k}\right)}.

Proof.

For

the probability that no element of

appears twice in

is

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\tau_{1}>t\,\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}\left(1-{\frac{i}{k}}\right)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}\exp\Big(-\frac{i}{k}\Big)=\exp\Big(-\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\frac{i}{k}\Big)=\exp\Big(-{\frac{t(t-1)}{2k}}\Big),

(24)

where we have used the inequality

that holds for all

Observe, if

, then

; and if

From this and

It follows from the sub-Gaussian Hoeffding inequality

b\in(0,1/2)
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

(i)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\big|U(ak\ln k)\big|>0\,\right]\leq k^{-a/2}}
; and

(ii)

if we let
\vartheta=bk\ln k\geq 1
, then

k^{1-b}/2\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\,U(\vartheta)\,\right]=k(1-1/k)^{\vartheta}\leq k^{1-b},

and

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\big|U(\vartheta)-\mathbb{E}\left[\,U(\vartheta)\,\right]\big|>\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\left[\,U(\vartheta)\,\right]\,\right]\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}k^{1-2b}}{\ln k}\bigg).

Proof.

For
s=xk\log k
, and a fixed
i\in[k]
, the probability that
i
does not occur in
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{s}
is

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,i\in U(s)\,\right]=(1-1/k)^{s}=(1-1/k)^{xk\ln k}\leq k^{-x}.

(25)

Now, since
k^{-a}\leq k^{-a/2-1}
, Item
(i)
follows from
Eq.

25
and a union bound over all
i\in[k]
.

For Item
(ii)
, it follows from
Eq.

25
that
\mathbb{E}\left[\,U(\vartheta)\,\right]=k(1-1/k)^{\vartheta}
.
Furthermore,

k^{-b}\geq\Big(1-\frac{1}{k}\Big)^{\vartheta}=\Big(1-\frac{b\ln k}{bk\ln k}\Big)^{bk\ln k}\geq e^{-b\ln k}\Big(1-\frac{b\ln k}{k}\Big)\geq k^{-b}/2,

where the first inequality is by
Eq.

25
, and in the second inequality we have used the fact that
(1+x/n)^{n}\geq e^{x}(1-x^{2}/n)
holds for all
n\geq 1,|x|\leq n
(see, e.g.,
[
46
, Proposition B.3]
). Consequently,

k^{1-b}/2\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\,U(\vartheta)\,\right]\leq k^{1-b}.

Now, note that
U(\vartheta)=f(X_{1},\dots,X_{\vartheta})
for some function
f:[k]^{\vartheta}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Observe also that if two sequences of inputs to this function differ only in one place, then the outputs differ by at most one. Thus,
f
satisfies Condition (L) of
Lemma

A.3
, which we apply with
\lambda=\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\left[\,U(\vartheta)\,\right]
to derive

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\big|U(\vartheta)-\mathbb{E}\left[\,U(\vartheta)\,\right]\big|>\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\left[\,U(\vartheta)\,\right]\,\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2(\varepsilon k^{1-b}/2)^{2}}{bk\ln k}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}k^{1-2b}}{\ln k}\right),

as claimed.
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

There exists a universal constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that, for any
C:=C(k)\geq 2
, and
k
large enough, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\tfrac{1}{10}k\ln k\leq T_{\mathcal{G}}(K)\leq Ck\ln k\,\right]\geq 1-k^{-C/2}-4\exp\Big(-\frac{c^{\prime}k^{4/5}}{\ln k}\Big).

Proof.

For the upper bound, we follow the ‘coupon collector’ strategy of simply visiting the current centre in each snapshot (and staying put if the centre has not changed).
Thus, applying
Lemma

5.8

(i)
with
a=C
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,T_{\mathcal{G}}(K)>Ck\ln k\,\right]\leq k^{-C/2}.

(26)

For the lower bound, we fix
b=1/10
and let
\vartheta=bk\ln k
. Our idea is to show that within the first
\vartheta
many steps many vertices in
K
have not been picked as centres, an event we call
\mathcal{E}
, and then within this time it is unlikely for an explorer to have visited all of these vertices by considering the visited and unvisited certres in
K
as an RST model on a smaller set of stars.

Let
L^{\prime}\subseteq K
be the random set of vertices in
K
that are leaves in every snapshot of
\mathcal{H}:=\mathcal{G}_{[1,\vartheta]}
.
Denote
\ell^{\prime}:=|L^{\prime}|
, and
let
\Lambda=k(1-1/k)^{\vartheta}
and
\mathcal{E}:=\{\big|\ell^{\prime}-\Lambda\big|\leq\Lambda/10\}
. Then, by
Lemma

5.8

(ii)
, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\lnot\mathcal{E}\,\right]\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{k^{1-2b}}{100\ln k}\bigg).

(27)

Conditional on the vertices of
L^{\prime}
never being centres,
\mathcal{H}
picks a random centre in each step uniformly from
K\setminus L^{\prime}
. Thus, conditional on this event, the first
\vartheta
snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
, restricted to the vertex set
[k]
, have the distribution of an RST model
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\sim(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\unif)
on vertex set
[k]
, where
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
consists of
k-\ell^{\prime}
distinct
k
-vertex stars. Let
T_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}(L^{\prime})
be the time needed to visit the non-centres of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. Observe that
T_{\mathcal{G}}(K)\geq T_{\mathcal{G}}(L^{\prime})
, since
L^{\prime}\subseteq K
. Thus, by the coupling above

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,T_{\mathcal{G}}(K)<bk\ln k\,\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,T_{\mathcal{G}}(L^{\prime})<bk\ln k,\;\mathcal{E}\,\right]+\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\lnot\mathcal{E}\,\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,T_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}(L^{\prime})<bk\ln k\,\right]+\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\lnot\mathcal{E}\,\right].

(28)

We seek to apply
Corollary

5.7
to
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
to bound
\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,T_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}(L^{\prime})<bk\ln k\,\right]
. In this setting, the total number of vertices is
n^{\prime}=k
and the number of centre vertices is
k^{\prime}=k-\ell^{\prime}
. We denote the corresponding ‘birthday times’ by
\tau_{i}^{\prime}
, which are defined in the same way as
\tau_{i}
, i.e.,
\tau_{i}^{\prime}=t_{i}^{\prime}-t_{i-1}^{\prime}
, where
(t_{i}^{\prime})_{i\geq 0}
is defined for
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
in the same way as
(t_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is defined for
\mathcal{G}
.
Thus, by applying
Corollary

5.7
to
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and
L^{\prime}
, for any
t\geq 0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,T_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}(L^{\prime})\leq\ell^{\prime}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}^{\prime}\,\right]-t-k^{\prime}-1\,\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-ct^{2}}{\ell^{\prime}\cdot k^{\prime}}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-ct^{2}}{\ell^{\prime}\cdot k}\right).

(29)

Recall, by
Lemma

5.8

(ii)
,
k^{1-b}/2\leq\Lambda\leq k^{1-b}
, and, by (
23
),
\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}^{\prime}\,\right]\geq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}k^{\prime}}-\frac{2}{5}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}(k-\ell^{\prime})}-\frac{2}{5}
. Thus, if
\mathcal{E}
holds, then
\tfrac{9}{20}k^{1-b}\leq\ell^{\prime}\leq\tfrac{11}{10}k^{1-b}
, and so, for large
k
, we have

\ell^{\prime}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}^{\prime}\,\right]\geq\frac{9}{20}k^{1-b}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}\left(k-\tfrac{11}{10}k^{1-b}\right)}-\frac{2}{5}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}k^{3/2-b}.

Now, set
t=\frac{1}{10}k^{3/2-b}
.
Recalling that
b=1/10
, we have
k^{3/2-b}\gg k\ln k\gg k^{\prime}
, and thus, for any sufficiently large
k
, we have

bk\ln k<\frac{1}{2}k^{3/2-b}-t-k^{\prime}-1\leq\ell^{\prime}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}^{\prime}\,\right]-t-k^{\prime}-1
. Consequently, using
Eq.

29
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,T_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}(L^{\prime})<bk\ln k\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,T_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}(L^{\prime})<\frac{1}{2}k^{3/2-b}-t-k^{\prime}-1\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,T_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}(L^{\prime})\leq\ell^{\prime}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}^{\prime}\,\right]-t-k^{\prime}-1\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{c(k^{3/2-b}/10)^{2}}{\tfrac{11}{10}k^{1-b}\cdot k}\right)=2\exp\left(-\frac{ck^{1-b}}{110}\right).

(30)

Inserting (
27
) and (
5.2
) into (
28
) gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,T_{\mathcal{G}}(K)<\tfrac{1}{10}k\ln k\,\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{ck^{1-b}}{110}\right)+2\exp\left(-\frac{k^{1-2b}}{100\ln k}\right)\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{c^{\prime}k^{4/5}}{\ln k}\right),

(31)

where
c^{\prime}>0
is some sufficiently small universal constant.
Finally, the lemma follows by combining
Eq.

26
and
Eq.

31
.
∎

We now have all the ingredients we need to prove
Theorem

5.10
, the main result of this subsection.
We note that for fixed
k
,
Theorem

5.10
does not guarantee a probability tending to one. However, for any fixed constant
k
,
Theorem

1.2
implies that the exploration time of the corresponding RST is
O(n)
with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(n)}
.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of
k:=k(n)\geq 2
distinct
n
-vertex stars, and let
\mathcal{G}\sim(\mathcal{S},\unif)
. Then, there exists a universal constant
d>0
such that for any
C:=C(k)\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\max\Big\{f_{-}(k),\;\tfrac{1}{10}k\ln k\Big\}\leq\texp(\mathcal{G})\leq f_{+}(k)+Ck\ln k\,\right]\geq 1-3k^{-dC}-4\exp\Big(-\frac{dk^{4/5}}{\ln k}\Big).

where

f_{\pm}(k):=\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right](n-k)\left(1\pm\sqrt{\tfrac{C\ln k}{n-k}}\pm 2\sqrt{\tfrac{k}{(n-k)^{2}}}\right).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that the set
K
of centres of the stars in
\mathcal{S}
is
[k]
.

We will split the exploration into two tasks: exploring the non-centres in
L
, and exploring the centres in
K
. For these tasks the explorer may visit any vertices in the graph, but must visit all vertices in the target set.
Since any exploration schedule must explore both
L
and
K
, and the distributions of
T_{\mathcal{G}}(L)
and
T_{\mathcal{G}}(K)
do not depend on specific
\mathcal{G}\sim(\mathcal{S},\unif)
, we have the stochastic domination

\max\{T_{\mathcal{G}}(L),T_{\mathcal{G}}(K)\}\preceq\texp(\mathcal{G})\preceq T_{\mathcal{G}}(L)+T_{\mathcal{G}}(K).\

(32)

Thus, it remains to bound
T_{\mathcal{G}}(L)
and
T_{\mathcal{G}}(K)
.
Before doing so, observe that due to the presence of the non-explicit universal constant
d>0
in the statement of the result, we may assume that
k
is at least some sufficiently large constant throughout.

For centres, by
Lemma

5.9
, there exists a constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\tfrac{1}{10}k\ln k\leq T_{\mathcal{G}}(K)\leq Ck\ln k\,\right]\geq 1-k^{-C/2}-4\exp\Big(-\frac{c^{\prime}k^{4/5}}{\ln k}\Big).

(33)

For non-centres, by
Corollary

5.7
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\big|T_{\mathcal{G}}(L)-(n-k)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right]\big|\leq t+k+1\,\right]\geq 1-2\exp\left(\frac{-ct^{2}}{(n-k)k}\right).

(34)

From
Eq.

23
, for any sufficiently large
k
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right]\geq\sqrt{k/2}
.
Thus, for
t=\sqrt{C(n-k)k\ln k}
,

\displaystyle(n-k)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right]-t-k-1

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right](n-k)\left(1-\sqrt{\tfrac{Ck\ln k}{\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right]^{2}(n-k)}}-\frac{k+1}{\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right](n-k)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right](n-k)\left(1-\sqrt{\tfrac{C\ln k}{n-k}}-2\sqrt{\tfrac{k}{(n-k)^{2}}}\right)=f_{-}(k),

and, similarly,

\displaystyle(n-k)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right]+t+k+1

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right](n-k)\left(1+\sqrt{\tfrac{C\ln k}{n-k}}+2\sqrt{\tfrac{k}{(n-k)^{2}}}\right)=f_{+}(k).

Consequently, from
Eq.

34
, we derive

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,f_{-}(k)\leq T_{\mathcal{G}}(L)\leq f_{+}(k)\,\right]\geq 1-2\exp\left(\frac{-ct^{2}}{(n-k)k}\right)=1-2k^{-c\cdot C}.

(35)

Now, by setting
d:=\min\{1/2,c,c^{\prime}\}
, from
Eq.

32
,
Eq.

33
, and
Eq.

35
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\max\Big\{f_{-}(k),\;\tfrac{1}{10}k\ln k\Big\}\leq\texp(\mathcal{G})\leq f_{+}(k)+Ck\ln k\,\right]\geq 1-3k^{-dC}-4\exp\Big(-\frac{dk^{4/5}}{\ln k}\Big),

as desired.
∎

Theorem

5.3
now follows readily from
Theorem

5.10
.

Proof of

.

Let
w\leq k\leq n-w\sqrt{n}\ln n
, where we will be taking

to be a suitably large constant. Then, by (

) we have

\big(1-2w^{-1}\big)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\pi k}{2}}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\,\tau_{1}\,\right]\leq\big(1+2w^{-1}\big)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\pi k}{2}}.

(36)

We will apply
Theorem

5.10
with

, note that in this case, for large

,

\sqrt{\frac{C\ln k}{n-k}}\leq w^{-1},\quad 2\sqrt{\frac{k}{(n-k)^{2}}}\leq w^{-1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad 3k^{-dC}+4\exp\Big(-\frac{dk^{4/5}}{\ln k}\Big)\leq w^{-d}.

(37)

Recalling that
T=(n-k)\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}k}
, it follows from (

) and (

) that for large

f_{+}(k)\leq\big(1+2w^{-1}\big)(1+w^{-1}+w^{-1/2})\cdot T\leq\big(1+8w^{-1/2}\big)\cdot T,

\left|f_{\pm}(k)-T\right|\leq 8w^{-1/2}\cdot T,\qquad\tfrac{1}{10}k\ln k<f_{-}(k),\qquad\text{and}\qquad Ck\ln k\leq 2w^{-1}\cdot T.

Thus, using (

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\left|\texp(\mathcal{G})-T\right|\leq 10w^{-1/2}\cdot T\,\right]\geq 1-w^{-d}.

Thus if we choose

∎

5.4
Lower Bound for Online Exploration

In this section, we show that any online exploration algorithm requires
\Omega(n^{2})

(\mathcal{S},\unif)
with
\lceil n/2\rceil

Theorem

5.4
. For convenience, we assume
n
is even, but the result also holds for odd values of
n
.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be even and
n\geq 4
.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of
n/2
distinct
n
-vertex stars, and
\mathcal{G}\sim(\mathcal{S},\unif)
.
Then, any randomised online exploration algorithm
A
of
\mathcal{G}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\Dexp_{A}(\mathcal{G})>\frac{n^{2}}{15}\,\right]\geq 1-\exp\Big(-\frac{n}{100}\Big).

Proof.

Let
k=n/2
.
Let
K:=[k]
be the centres of the stars in
\mathcal{S}
,
and
L:=[n]\setminus K
to be the non-centres.

We represent the graph
\mathcal{G}
by a sequence
(c_{t})_{t\geq 1}
, where each
c_{t}\in K
denotes the centre of the star in the
t
-th snapshot of
\mathcal{G}
.
Furthermore, we interpret a randomised online exploration algorithm
A
as an explorer moving on the vertices of
\mathcal{G}
, and represent the explorer’s walk by a sequence
(a_{t})_{t\geq 1}
, where
a_{1}
is the initial position (chosen by the algorithm without seeing any snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
), and, for each
t\geq 2
, the value
a_{t}\in[n]
denotes the vertex to which the explorer moves at the end of time step
t-1
.
For every
t\geq 1
, the decision
a_{t}
at time step
t-1
is based on the explorer’s movement history
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{t-1})
, the revealed snapshots of
\mathcal{G}
up to time
t-1
, represented by
(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{t-1})
, and the random bits used by the algorithm.

Since no two vertices in
L
are connected by an edge in any snapshot of
\mathcal{G}
, the only way the explorer can visit a vertex in
L
at time step
t
is by being located at the star center of the current snapshot at the start of time step
t
. That is, the explorer must have moved to a vertex
v\in K
at the end of time step
t-1
, and the
t
-th sampled snapshot of
\mathcal{G}
must be the star centered at
v
. This condition is equivalent to the equality
a_{t}=c_{t}
.

Thus, if the explorer visits all vertices of
\mathcal{G}
by time
T
, then

c(A,T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}(a_{t}=b_{t})
is at least
|L|-1=n/2-1
(where the ”
-1
” accounts for the possibility that the explorer’s initial position lies in
L
).
This and the fact that
c(A,T)
is stochastically dominated by
Z\sim\operatorname{Bin}(T,1/k)=\operatorname{Bin}(T,2/n)
(by
Proposition

A.5
) imply

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\Dexp_{A}(\mathcal{G})\leq T\,\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,c(A,T)\geq n/2-1\,\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,Z\geq n/2-1\,\right].

To complete the proof, we choose
T=n(n-2)/6
and
\delta=1/2
so that
(1+\delta)\cdot\frac{2T}{n}=n/2-1
. Then, since
n^{2}/15\leq T
for
n\geq 4
, applying
Lemma

A.1
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\Dexp_{A}(\mathcal{G})\leq n^{2}/15\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\Dexp_{A}(\mathcal{G})\leq T\,\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,Z\geq(1+\delta)2T/n\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{-(1/2)^{2}}{3}\cdot\frac{2T}{n}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{n-2}{36}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n}{100}\right),

which implies the claim.
∎

Appendix A
Concentration and Auxillary Results

Concentration inequalities.

A random variable
X
is
binomially distributed
with parameters
n\geq 1
and
p\in[0,1]
, denoted
X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
, if
\mathbb{P}(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}
. We will make use of the following classic Chernoff bound for binomial random variables.

Lemma A.1

(
[
45
, Theorems 4.4 & 4.5]
)
.

Let
X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
p\in(0,1)
. Let
\mu:=\mathbb{E}[X]
. Then, for any
0<\delta<1
,

\mathbb{P}[X\leq(1-\delta)\mu]\leq e^{-\delta^{2}\mu/2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}[X\geq(1+\delta)\mu]\leq e^{-\delta^{2}\mu/3}.

A random variable
X
is
geometrically distributed
with parameter
p\in[0,1]
, denoted
X\sim\operatorname{Geo}\!\left(p\right)
, if
\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X=k\,\right]=(1-p)^{k-1}p
for any integer
k\geq 1
.

We will use the following Chernoff bound for sums of geometric random variables.

Lemma A.2

(
[
36
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

For any
n\geq 1
and
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in(0,1]
, let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, where
X_{i}\sim\operatorname{Geo}\!\left(p_{i}\right)
. Let
p_{*}=\min_{i\in[n]}p_{i}
and
\mu=\mathbb{E}\left[\,X\,\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p_{i}}
. Then, for any
\lambda\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X\geq\lambda\mu\,\right]\leq\exp(-p_{*}\cdot\mu\cdot\left(\lambda-1-\log\lambda\right)).

We will also use McDiarmid’s inequality.

Lemma A.3

(
[
37
, Corollary 2.27 & Remark 2.28]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}
be independent random variables, with
X_{i}
taking values in a set
\Lambda_{i}
. Assume that a function
f:\Lambda_{1}\times\cdots\Lambda_{\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

satisfies the following Lipschitz condition for some numbers
c_{i}
,
i\in[\ell]
:

(L)

If two vectors
x,x^{\prime}\in\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\Lambda_{i=1}^{\ell}
differ only in the
i
-th coordinate, then
|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|\leq c_{i}
.

Then, for any
\lambda>0
, the random variable
Y=f(X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell})

satisfies

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,|Y-\mathbb{E}\left[\,Y\,\right]|>\lambda\,\right]\leq 2\exp\Bigg(-\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}c_{i}^{2}}\Bigg).

We will also use a concentration result for sums of sub-Gaussian random variables
[
51
]
. The sub-Gaussian norm
||X||_{\psi^{2}}
of a random variable
X
is defined as follows,

||X||_{\psi^{2}}:=\inf\{K>0:\mathbb{E}\left[\,\exp(X^{2}/K^{2})\,\right]\leq 2\}.

(39)

We say that a random variable
X
is sub-Gaussian if
||X||_{\psi^{2}}
is bounded.
We will use a variant of Hoeffding’s inequality for sub-Gaussian random variables.

Theorem A.4

(
[
51
, Theorem 2.7.3]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be independent sub-Gaussian random variables with zero mean. Then, there exists a universal constant
c>0
such that for every
t\geq 0

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right|\geq t\,\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-ct^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}||X_{i}||^{2}_{\psi^{2}}}\right).

Lower Bound for Randomised Online Decisions.

The following proposition says that if are playing a game where you get to open one of
k
boxes in each round, where a single uniformly sampled box contains a gold bar, then you cannot do better than just opening the first box each time.
We need this for our lower bounds for online explorers. The proof of this is very basic, but we could not find it anywhere to cite, so we include it for completeness.

Proposition A.5
.

Let
(b)_{t\geq 1}
be i.i.d. with uniform distribution over
[k]
. Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
A
be any randomised algorithm which in step
t\geq 1
has access to
(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{t-1}
and outputs
a_{t}\in S
. Then
c(A,T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}(a_{t}=b_{t})
is stochastically dominated by a random variable with distribution
\operatorname{Bin}(T,1/k)
. Furthermore, if
S=[k]
then
c(A,T)
has distribution
\operatorname{Bin}(T,1/k)
.

Proof.

We begin by showing that for any algorithm
A
taking values in a set
S
, there exists an algorithm
A^{\prime}
taking values in
[k]
such that
c(A,T)\preceq c(A^{\prime},T)
. We can consider each
a_{t}
as a function
a_{t}:((a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{t-1},\omega_{t})\mapsto S
, where
\omega_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is an independent
0/1
-random string. We define a new algorithm
A^{\prime}
by setting each output
a_{t}
as follows

a^{\prime}_{t}=\begin{cases}a_{t}&\text{ if }a_{t}\in[k],\\
1&\text{ if }a_{t}\not\in[k].\\
\end{cases}

Observe that if
a_{t}\in S\backslash[k]
then, regardless of the value of
b_{t}
, we have
\mathbf{1}(a_{t}=b_{t})=0
. It then follows that for every sequence
\big((a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{t-1},\omega_{t}\big)_{t\in[T]}
we have

c(A,T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}(a_{t}=b_{t},a_{t}\in[k])=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}(a^{\prime}_{t}=b_{t},a_{t}\in[k])\leq c(A^{\prime},T),

giving
c(A,T)\preceq c(A^{\prime},T)
as claimed. Thus, we can assume from now on that
S=[k]
.

Now, for any such algorithm
A
taking values in
[k]
, since
b_{t}
is independent of
(a_{i})_{i\in[t]}
we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,a_{t}=b_{t}\,\right]=\sum_{c\in[k]}\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,a_{t}=b_{t}=c\,\right]=\sum_{c\in[k]}\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,b_{t}=c\mid a_{t}=c\,\right]\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,a_{t}=c\,\right]=\sum_{c\in[k]}\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,a_{t}=c\,\right]=\frac{1}{k}.

Thus, for each
t\geq 0
, the random variable
X_{t}=\mathbf{1}(a_{t}=b_{t})\sim\operatorname{Ber}\!\left(1/k\right)
is a Bernoulli random variable with probability
1/k
.

We now show that, for any
T\geq 1
, the random variables
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
are mutually independent. For
t\in[T]
,
\mathbf{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}^{t-1}
, and
Q\subseteq[t-1]
, we define the event
\mathcal{E}(Q,\mathbf{\alpha})=\bigcap_{q\in Q}\{X_{q}=\alpha_{q}\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X_{t}=1\mid\mathcal{E}(Q,\mathbf{\alpha})\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in[k]}\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,a_{t}=b_{t}=c\mid\mathcal{E}(Q,\mathbf{\alpha})\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in[k]}\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,b_{t}=c\mid a_{t}=c,\mathcal{E}(Q,\mathbf{\alpha})\,\right]\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,a_{t}=c\mid\mathcal{E}(Q,\mathbf{\alpha})\,\right].

Now, since
b_{t}
is independent of the sigma algebra generated by
\{(a_{i})_{i\in[t]},(b_{i})_{i\in[t-1]}\}
, which contains all events of the form
\{a_{t}=c\}\cap\mathcal{E}(Q,\alpha)
, and
\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,a_{t}=\cdot\mid\mathcal{E}(Q,\mathbf{\alpha})\,\right]
is a probability distribution on
[k]
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X_{t}=1\mid\mathcal{E}(Q,\mathbf{\alpha})\,\right]=\sum_{c\in[k]}\frac{1}{k}\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,a_{t}=c\mid\mathcal{E}(Q,\mathbf{\alpha})\,\right]=\frac{1}{k}=\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X_{t}=1\,\right],

and similarly
\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X_{t}=0\mid\mathcal{E}(Q,\mathbf{\alpha})\,\right]=\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X_{t}=0\,\right]
. Thus, for any
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{T}
and subset
I\subseteq[T]
with largest element
i^{*}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\cap_{i\in I}\{X_{i}=\alpha_{i}\}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X_{i^{*}}=\alpha_{i^{*}}\mid\cap_{i\in I\setminus\{i^{*}\}}\{X_{i}=\alpha_{i}\}\,\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\cap_{i\in I\setminus\{i^{*}\}}\{X_{i}=\alpha_{i}\}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X_{i^{*}}=\alpha_{i^{*}}\,\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,\cap_{i\in I\setminus\{i^{*}\}}\{X_{i}=\alpha_{i}\}\,\right]=\prod_{i\in I}\mathbb{P}\!\left[\,X_{i}=\alpha_{i}\,\right],

by induction. Thus
c(A,T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}
follows the Binomial distribution
\operatorname{Bin}(T,1/k)
.
∎
