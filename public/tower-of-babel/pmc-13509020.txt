RSV Monoclonal Antibody Implementation for At‐Risk Infants: Victoria, Australia's 2024 Experience
Authors: Jane Tuckerman, Daryl R. Cheng, Guillian Hunter, Sonja Elia, Tara Hearn, Deepali Verma, Susan Jacobs, Jeremy Carr, Nigel W. Crawford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNew respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) preventative programs are launching globally. Our Australian jurisdiction implemented RSV monoclonal antibody provision via pediatric and perinatal hospitals for highest risk neonates and infants. The aim of this study was to monitor the implementation and utilization of nirsevimab and palivizumab in a cohort of infants at increased risk who were program recipients in Victorian hospitals in 2024 and provide evidence and operational context for future programs.MethodsIn this observational cohort study, suitable participants at each site were identified by checking the drug administration records for receipt of nirsevimab and/or palivizumab. Follow‐up of all infants was via the hospital electronic medical record (EMR), with relevant administration information as well as any subsequent respiratory encounters (defined by testing for respiratory pathogens) at either Monash Children's Hospital or the Royal Children's Hospital, Melbourne. This study was descriptive and exploratory, and no formal effectiveness or comparative analyses were undertaken.ResultsIn total, 430 infants received RSV preventatives at five hospital sites throughout Victoria. In total, 54% of infants who received RSV preventatives were premature (< 37 weeks gestation); 45% were < 32 weeks gestation and four infants (2 pre‐term; 2 term) experienced breakthrough infection.ConclusionWithout a national program combining maternal and infant preventatives, hospital‐level targeted programs can protect infants at highest risk of severe RSV and reduce admissions; we recommend biopharmaceutical support for these targeted initiatives over large population‐wide programs that have not met cost‐effectiveness criteria—this 2024 approach informed Australia's 2025 RSV program. Findings will inform ongoing RSV special‐risk initiatives.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) causes a considerable disease burden globally. All infants are susceptible; however, risk factors that increase the risk of severe disease and hospitalization include premature birth (< 37 weeks gestation), age less than 6 months, and underlying medical conditions, such as congenital heart disease [1, 2].

Two products have recently been approved to protect infants against RSV—Abrysvo (Pfizer), a maternal RSV vaccine, and Beyfortus (nirsevimab), a long‐acting monoclonal antibody (mAb). During the 2024 RSV season in Australia, there was no national nirsevimab program, and Abrysvo was only available privately. However, all‐infant nirsevimab programs were employed across several Australian jurisdictions [3, 4, 5]. In Western Australia (WA) and Queensland, all newborn infants and children medically at‐risk were eligible, while in New South Wales, the program was solely for children medically at‐risk [3, 4, 5]. In jurisdictions without official nirsevimab programs in 2024, access to RSV mAb was limited to existing hospital‐funded palivizumab programs [6]. Palivizumab (Synagis) is an mAb used to reduce the risk of severe RSV illness. However, it has several limitations compared to nirsevimab: duration (short acting), dosing (requires monthly doses throughout the RSV season), and high cost. Due to these factors, palivizumab is typically reserved for individuals at highest risk of severe RSV illness and provided only through dedicated programs. In 2025, most countries have replaced palivizumab with the long‐acting nirsevimab in their immunization prevention programs.

Sanofi's allocation strategy for nirsevimab during 2024–2025 was shaped by global supply constraints and regulatory timelines. Sanofi faced limited manufacturing capacity in 2024 due to high global demand and production ramp‐up. Priority was given to countries with earlier regulatory approvals and national funding commitments, such as the United States and parts of Europe. In Australia in 2024, nirsevimab was not listed on the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS), meaning no Commonwealth purchasing power existed. The procurement model was fragmented, and states had to negotiate directly with Sanofi for limited stock, and the global supply shortages compounded the issue. Jurisdictions with confirmed funding and procurement contracts received stock earlier. In Australia, states that committed to full or partial programs (e.g., NSW, ACT) were prioritized over those still negotiating (like Victoria in 2024) or without the budget or mechanism to fund a full whole of target population jurisdictional based program.

Victoria, which encountered difficulties accessing products for a targeted program, secured limited nirsevimab doses from states running universal infant programs in 2024. Utilizing these doses, we implemented a targeted nirsevimab program to protect highest risk infants. For most eligible infants, nirsevimab replaced palivizumab as the RSV preventative offered. However, several factors impacted the dispensing patterns of RSV preventatives including supply, RSV season/program start dates, and medical comorbidities (e.g., infants undergoing cardiac surgery). In Victoria, the RSV season typically runs from mid‐April to the end of September. For transparency, we have reported data for both nirsevimab and palivizumab products.

The aim of this study was to monitor the implementation and utilization of nirsevimab and palivizumab in a cohort of infants at increased risk who were program recipients in Victorian hospitals in 2024 and provide evidence and operational context for future programs. Any infant follow‐up outcomes collected were exploratory in nature and not intended to assess effectiveness.

Hospital‐Lead Nirsevimab Program

At the Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne (RCH) and Monash Children's Hospital (MCH), hospital‐funded RSV mAb programs using palivizumab have operated annually for over a decade. On average, ~50% of participants are recruited at the start of the season (April 1: mid‐autumn), with the rest joining the program up until September (spring) for monthly doses (maximum six in total) [7]. In the RCH cohort, the Palivizumab Program is associated with fewer RSV hospitalizations (3 in 2023; 6 in 2022), hospital days, and ICU admissions [6]. While the majority of infants attended RCH and MCH, service delivery has included other hospitals in the state: Western Health, the Royal Women's Hospital (RWH), Bendigo Health, and Barwon.

In 2024, nirsevimab replaced palivizumab as the main RSV mAb administered as part of the RCH and MCH programs, with expansion to include additional sites, including five pediatric tertiary hospitals and three tertiary perinatal hospitals.

The dedicated high‐risk programs varied by site. At RCH, all eligible infants received nirsevimab mAb prior to discharge, while at MCH, all eligible premature infants received nirsevimab before discharge from the neonatal unit. The immunization teams at both hospitals coordinated administration for two groups of infants who were not currently hospitalized: premature infants who were born before 37 weeks gestation (with those < 32 weeks gestation prioritized) and were under 12 months of age and high‐risk infants who had comorbidities. Families were educated about RSV disease and the mAb program. The dosage of nirsevimab and/or palivizumab was based on body weight and administered as an intramuscular injection.

For this study, suitable participants at each site were identified by checking the drug administration records for nirsevimab and palivizumab. Eligibility data were not collected, so coverage estimates were not calculated. Follow‐up of all infants was via the hospital electronic medical record (EMR), with relevant information collected including demographics, service delivery of preventatives, and any subsequent respiratory (defined by testing for respiratory pathogens) and RSV encounters at MCH or RCH.

Study Characteristics

Demographics, service delivery, and follow‐up respiratory encounters were collected for a cohort of 430 infants (median age at first administration: 64 days, IQR: 23–149) from five hospital sites (RCH, MCH, RWH, and outreach through the RCH [Mercy, Sunshine and Regional Hospitals that acted as RCH satellite sites]) (Table 1). Over half (230/430; 54%) of infants were premature (median gestation 30 weeks, IQR 26–32). In total, 339 infants received only nirsevimab, 74 infants received only palivizumab, while 17 infants received a combination (Figure 1). For those that received both palivizumab and nirsevimab, all received palivizumab first. Overall, these 17 infants received either 1 (n = 16) or 2 doses (n = 1) of palivizumab prior to nirsevimab; the median interval between palivizumab and nirsevimab was 27 days. Figure 2a illustrates the distribution of age and timing of administration for infants receiving nirsevimab or palivizumab across five Victorian hospitals in 2024, showing delivery clustered within the RSV season with variability in age at dosing. The use of nirsevimab extends throughout the season especially for infants aged < 100 days, while palivizumab is used predominately at the start of the season and in older infants.

Follow‐up of Vaccinated Infants

Follow‐up data were collected for descriptive and exploratory purposes, to provide context around post‐administration outcomes during early program implementation. No formal effectiveness or comparative analyses were performed. Data for subsequent respiratory encounters were available for 414 infants, with follow‐up data we collected for these infants until the end of the 2024 calendar year. This included infants who had received their mAb therapy at RCH or MCH. Additionally, infants who had received their mAb therapy at RWH were included if they subsequently presented to RCH or MCH for a respiratory‐related encounter (Table 2). A third 132/414 (32%) of infants had a hospital presentation at MCH or RCH and were tested for respiratory pathogens. After rhinovirus and enterovirus, parainfluenza was the most detected pathogen.

Four infants developed RSV (50–91 days post nirsevimab), with two developing severe infections requiring intensive care unit admission and high‐level respiratory support (Table 2). While two of these infants were born premature (26 and 27 weeks), the other two had respiratory and/or cardiac conditions. Figure 2b presents age at administration among infants receiving nirsevimab, stratified by risk group and breakthrough RSV infection. Nirsevimab was administered across multiple risk categories and age groups, with few breakthrough infections observed and no clear clustering by age or risk group (Table 2).

Discussion

No RSV prevention products were funded in 2024 under Australia's National Immunisation Program (NIP). While both nirsevimab and Abrysvo were considered by Australia's Pharmaceutical Benefits Advisory Committee, nirsevimab was not recommended at the time of this program, and the funded maternal RSV vaccine program did not commence until February 3, 2025 [8].

Universal infant RSV mAb programs using nirsevimab are now implemented in several countries including Spain, the United States, Belgium, and China. WA implemented an all‐infant nirsevimab program in 2024, demonstrating 71% coverage during April–September 2024 [9]. However, this was a state‐based program unlike our implementation, which utilized established channels of care on a tailored scale.

Our statewide hospital‐based mAb program saw reassuringly few infants (1%; 4/414) who had subsequent RSV infections requiring hospitalization. In WA, statewide prospective surveillance using a test‐negative design found RSV hospitalization occurred in 42 (22.8%) children who had received nirsevimab [10].

A program limitation was that we could not offer nirsevimab to children during their second RSV season, nor capture all premature and high‐risk infants across the state. A limitation of this study is the absence of data on the eligible population, which prevents calculation of uptake or representativeness; the study cohort therefore comprises program recipients only and is not intended to estimate uptake, so findings should be interpreted as descriptive of recipients only (i.e., in the absence of denominators from neonatal units or risk‐condition registries).

We were also unable to capture all respiratory virus testing throughout the season. While most participants in this study are likely to have ongoing care in their place of administration due to the nature of their risk status, they may have presented elsewhere for respiratory PCR testing, such as community general practice. This may underestimate the full burden of RSV in this special‐risk cohort. Reassuringly, most infants who subsequently developed an RSV infection and required an ICU admission are likely to be captured. This is because neonatal units do not admit infants with RSV infection, and the only two hospitals in the state with pediatric intensive care units were study sites. While two of the four confirmed breakthrough cases were pre‐term and had bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD). In the overall cohort, we did not clarify how many of the pre‐term and/or respiratory groups had a background BPD. We were unable to report on parental acceptance for either mAb as we identified only infants with known administration, rather than all those that were offered. Previously, few parents declined palivizumab; with 5% declining in 2023 [11], this will be important to monitor moving forward.

A Maternal and Infant Immunisation Program for Australia was announced at the end of 2024, and after this, the program was completed [12]. Under the program, Abrysvo (the maternal vaccine) was made available free to pregnant women between 28 and 36 weeks' gestation on the NIP from February 3, 2025. In Victoria, funded Beyfortus (nirsevimab) was made available during the RSV season, from April 1, 2025, and restricted to infants with high‐risk conditions or whose mothers did not receive the maternal RSV vaccine or were vaccinated less than 2 weeks before delivery. High‐risk infants under 2 years of age are also eligible for a mAb dose in their second RSV season [12].

Conclusion

In the absence of a nationally funded program that includes both maternal and infant preventatives, targeted programs are likely to net those most at risk for severe RSV and offer protection against RSV‐associated hospital admissions. We demonstrate that a program implemented at the hospital level can identify and capture those most at‐risk. The authors suggest that the biopharmaceutical industry should aim to support the specific, targeted programs as outlined in this paper, rather than concentrating only on large, universal, population‐wide programs that are yet to reach health technology assessment cost‐effectiveness criteria. This targeted approach described from 2024 has helped guide the broader RSV prevention program in Australia in 2025.
