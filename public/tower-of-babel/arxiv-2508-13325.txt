An infinite library
Klaas Pieter Hart

Abstract
. The purpose of this note is to describe a space that is regular but not completely regular, but only barely so: all closed sets are G δ G_{\delta} -sets and every singleton is a zero-set.



Introduction

There are many examples of regular spaces that are not completely regular.
A particularly simple one is due to Adam Mysior, see
[
4
]

or
[
2
]
*Example 1.5.9.

What many of the examples have in common is that in order to ensure
their non-complete regularity most continuous real-valued functions are
constant on relatively large sets.
This is often ensured by having very few points of first-countability
and very few closed sets that are
G_{\delta}
-sets.

In this note we adapt a method due to F. B. Jones from
[
3
]

to produce an example that is first-countable and in which every closed
set is a
G_{\delta}
-set and every singleton set is a zero-set.
This method goes back to Tychonoff’s paper
[
6
]
where
it is applied to the top and right-hand lines of the Tychonoff plank.

We apply it to the sets
Q
and
P
of the rational and irrational points
of the
x
-axis in the Niemytzki plane respectively.

The note is organized as follows.
Section
1
contains a description of the Niemytzki plane
N

and an analysis of the behaviour of continuous real-valued functions on the

x
-axis.
In Section
2
we apply Jones’ method to the Niemytzki plane
and obtain an analogue of Tychonoff’s example, but with in which every
closed set is a
G_{\delta}
-set and in which all points, but one, are zero-sets;
in Section
3
we show how to make that one point a zero-set
as well.

1.
The Niemytzki plane and continuous functions

Let
N
denote the Niemytzki plane,
see
[
1
]
*§ 1, Nr. 6,
2^{\circ}
,
or
[
2
]
*Example 1.2.4,
or
[
5
]
*Example 82.

The underlying set of
N
is the closed upper half-plane in
\R^{2}
, that is,

N=\{\langle{x},{y}\rangle:y\ge 0\}
.
The topology is defined by specifying local bases at all points of
N
.

If
y>0
then the
n
th basic neighbourhood of
\langle{x},{y}\rangle
is

U(x,y,n)=\bigl\{\langle{u},{v}\rangle\in N:\mathopen{\|}\langle{u},{v}\rangle-\langle{x},{y}\rangle\mathclose{\|}<2^{-n}\bigr\}

and
\{U(x,y,n)n\in\N\}
is the local base at
\langle{x},{y}\rangle
.

If
y=0
then we denote, for
n\in\N
, by
U^{\prime}(x,n)
the open disc

\bigl\{\langle{u},{v}\rangle:\mathopen{\|}\langle{u},{v}\rangle-\langle{x},{2^{-n}}\rangle\mathclose{\|}<2^{-n}\bigr\}

and the
n
th basic neighbourhood of
\langle{x},{0}\rangle

then is
U(x,n)=U^{\prime}(x,n)\cup\{\langle{x},{0}\rangle\}
.

In
N
we have two nice disjoint closed sets that cannot be separated by
disjoint open sets nor, a fortiori, by continuous real-valued functions.
These sets are

•

P=\{\langle{x},{0}\rangle:x
is irrational
\}
, and

•

Q=\{\langle{x},{0}\rangle:x
is rational
\}
.

In
[
1
]
*§ 5, Nr. 5 this is part of an exercise with a hint
in a footnote, which comes down to: “Use the Baire Category theorem”.

We need some quantitative information about how close these two sets are
in the context of continuous real-valued functions.
To this end we formulate a few lemmas.
The first two are quite elementary, but useful.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
a\in\R
and let
\langle a_{k}:k\in\N\}
be a sequence in
\R
that
converges to
a
.
Then for every
n
we have
U^{\prime}(a,n)\subseteq\bigcup_{k\in\N}U^{\prime}(a_{k},n)
.

Proof.

Let
\langle{x},{y}\rangle\in U^{\prime}(a,n)
, then
\mathopen{\|}\langle{x},{y}\rangle-\langle{a},{2^{-n}}\rangle\mathclose{\|}<2^{-n}
.
Take
k\in\N
such that

\mathopen{|}a_{k}-a\mathclose{|}<2^{-n}-\mathopen{\|}\langle{x},{y}\rangle-\langle{a},{2^{-n}}\rangle\mathclose{\|}

Then
\mathopen{\|}\langle{x},{y}\rangle-\langle{a_{k}},{2^{-n}}\rangle\mathclose{\|}<2^{-n}
, and so
\langle{x},{y}\rangle\in U^{\prime}(a_{k},n)
.
∎

From this we deduce the following lemma.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
I=[a,b]
be a closed interval in
\R
, let
D
be dense in
I
,
and let
n\in\N
.
Then
\bigcup_{d\in D}U^{\prime}(d,n)=\bigcup_{x\in I}U^{\prime}(x,n)
.

Proof.

One inclusion is clear.

For the other let
x\in I
and let
\langle d_{k}:k\in\N\rangle
be a sequence
in
D
that converges to
x
.
Then by Lemma
1.1
we have

U^{\prime}(x,n)\subseteq\bigcup_{k}U^{\prime}(d_{k},n)\subseteq\bigcup_{d\in D}U^{\prime}(d,n)
.
∎

We apply Lemma
1.2
and the Baire Category Theorem to
establish the following two lemmas.

Lemma 1.3
.

If
f:N\to[0,1]
is continuous and
G
is a dense
G_{\delta}
-subset of
\R

such that
f\restr G\equiv 0
then for every open
interval
I
in
\R
we have
\inf\{f(q,0):q\in I\cap\Q\}=0
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that for every nonempty open interval
I
and
every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
q\in\Q
such that
f(q,0)<\varepsilon
.

Let
I
and
\varepsilon
be given.
For every
p\in G
we let
n_{p}
be the smallest natural number
for which
f[U(p,n_{p})]\subseteq[0,\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon)
.

By the Baire Category Theorem there are a nonempty open subinterval
J

of
I
and a natural number
n
such that
J
is a subset of the closure
of
A=\{p:n_{p}=n\}
.
Now both
A\cap J
and
\Q\cap J
are dense in
J
,
and so by Lemma
1.2
we have

\bigcup_{p\in A\cap J}U^{\prime}(p,n)=\bigcup_{q\in\Q\cap J}U^{\prime}(q,n)

This shows that
f[U^{\prime}(q,n)]\subseteq[0,\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon)
for
all
q\in\Q\cap J
.
But then for all
q\in\Q\cap J
we have

f(q,0)\le\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon<\varepsilon
.
∎

We also have the converse of this lemma.

Lemma 1.4
.

If
f:N\to[0,1]
is continuous and such that for every nonempty
open interval
I
in
\R
we have
\inf\{f(q,0):q\in I\cap\Q\}=0
then the set

\{x\in\R:f(x,0)=0\}
contains a dense
G_{\delta}
-set of
\R
.

Proof.

We show that for every
k\in\N
and every nonempty open interval
I

there is nonempty open subinterval
J
of
I
such that
f(x,0)<2^{-k}

for all
x\in J
.

This then implies that there is a sequence
\langle O_{k}:k\in\N\rangle

of dense open sets in
\R
such that
f(x,0)<2^{-k}
whenever

x\in O_{k}
.
The intersection
\bigcap_{k\in\N}O_{k}
is a dense
G_{\delta}
-set and
contained in
\{x:f(x,0)=0\}
.

Let
k
and
I
be given.
Let
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{3}\cdot 2^{-k}
and cover
[0,1]
by the
intervals
K_{i}=\bigl(i\cdot\varepsilon,(i+2)\cdot\varepsilon\bigr)
,
where
i=-1
,
0
, …,
3\cdot 2^{k}-1
.

For
p\in I
let
i_{p}
be minimal with
f(p,0)\in K_{i_{p}}
and let
n_{p}

be minimal such that
f[U(p,n_{p})]\subseteq K_{i_{p}}
.
By the Baire Category Theorem we have
i
and
n
and
a nonempty open subinterval
J

of
I
such that
A=\{p:i_{p}=i
and
n_{p}=n\}
contains the closure of
J
.

Again by Lemma
1.2
we have

\bigcup_{p\in A\cap J}U^{\prime}(p,n)=\bigcup_{q\in\Q\cap J}U^{\prime}(q,n)

This now implies that
f[U^{\prime}(q,n)]\subseteq K_{i}
, and hence that

i\cdot\varepsilon\le f(q,0)
, whenever
q\in\Q\cap J
.
But
\inf\{f(q,0):q\in\Q\cap J\}=0
and hence
i=-1
or
i=0
.

We find that
f[U(x,n)]\subseteq[0,2\varepsilon]
, and hence

f(x,0)\le 2\varepsilon<2^{-k}
, for all
x\in J
.
∎

2.
A Book

We apply Jones’ method from
[
3
]
to
N
to obtain a
regular, non-completely regular space, as follows.

We start with the product
N\times\N
, where
\N
carries its discrete
topology.

For every even
n\in\N
and
q\in\Q
we identify the points

\langle{\langle{q},{0}\rangle},{n}\rangle
and
\langle{\langle{q},{0}\rangle},{n+1}\rangle
, so that
Q\times\{n\}

and
Q\times\{n+1\}
become one copy of
Q
, which we denote
Q_{n}
.

Likewise for every odd
n\in\N
and
p\in\Irr
we identify

\langle{\langle{p},{0}\rangle},{n}\rangle
and
\langle{\langle{p},{0}\rangle},{n+1}\rangle
, thus creating out of

P\times\{n\}
and
P\times\{n+1\}
one copy of
P
that we denote
P_{n}
.

The resulting space we call
B
and we let
\pi:N\times\N\to B
denote
the quotient map.

Remark 2.1
.

In
[
5
]
*Examples 90 and 91 Tychonoff’s example, mentioned
in the introduction, is represented pictorially as a corkscrew.

Our space
B
looks more like a book made from infinitely many sheets
of paper sewn together alternately along the rational and irrational
points on the
x
-axis, hence the title of the present section.
We shall call
B
a
book
from now on.

Lemmas
1.3
and
1.4
above imply that
if
f:B\to[0,1]
is continuous and equal to
0

on the set
Q_{0}
then, by induction, the following holds for every
n
,
where we let
F:N\times\N\to[0,1]
be the composition
f\circ\pi
.

•

if
n
is even then
\inf\{F(x,0,n):x\in O\cap\Q\}=0

whenever
O
is an open interval in
\R
, and

•

if
n
is odd then there is a dense
G_{\delta}
-set
G_{n}
, such that

F(x,0,n+1)=F(x,0,n)=0
whenever
x\in G_{n}\cap P_{n}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that by the second item there is in fact a single dense
G_{\delta}
-set
G
,
to wit
\bigcap\{G_{n}:n
is odd
\}
, such that

F(x,0,n+1)=F(x,0,n)=0
whenever
x\in G\cap P_{n}
and
n
is odd.

This implies that if we were to add a ‘point at infinity’
\infty

to
N\times\N
with basic neighbourhoods

U_{m}=\{\infty\}\cup(N\times\{n\in\N:n\ge m\})

and apply the quotient operation above to the new space

(N\times\N)\cup\{\infty\}
,
then the resulting quotient space
B\cup\{\infty\}
is not completely
regular at
\infty
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The map
\pi:(N\times\N)\cup\{\infty\}\to B\cup\{\infty\}
is closed.

Proof.

If
F
is closed in
(N\times\N)\cup\{\infty\}
then

\pi^{\leftarrow}\bigl[\pi[F]\bigr]
is closed as well.
If
n\in\N
then its intersection with the sheet
N\times\{n\}

consists of three parts (two if
n=0
):

•

F\cap(N\times\{n\})
,

•

the set
\{\langle{\langle{x},{0}\rangle},{n}\rangle:\langle{\langle{x},{0}\rangle},{n+1}\rangle\in F\}
, and

•

the set
\{\langle{\langle{x},{0}\rangle},{n}\rangle:\langle{\langle{x},{0}\rangle},{n-1}\rangle\in F\}

if also
n>0
.

The union of these intersections is closed in
N\times\N
.

If
\infty\notin F
then
F
intersects only finitely many sheets and
so
\infty
is not in the closure of
\pi^{\leftarrow}\bigl[\pi[F]\bigr]
.

If
\infty\in F
then all is well.
∎

Lemma 2.4

(Exercise 1.5.H in
[
2
]
)
.

In the Niemytzki plane every closed set is a
G_{\delta}
-set.

Solution.

Let
F
be closed in
N
and divide it onto
F_{1}
, its intersection with the

x
-axis, and
F_{2}=F\setminus F_{1}
.

The set
F_{2}
is closed in the open subspace
\{\langle{x},{y}\rangle:y>0\}
, which carries
its Euclidean topology.
Hence
F_{2}
is a
G_{\delta}
-set in that subspace and hence in
N
.

Let
A=\{x:\langle{x},{0}\rangle\in F\}
; then

F_{1}=\bigcap_{k\in\N}\bigcup_{x\in A}U(x,k)
.

So
F
is the union of two
G_{\delta}
-sets.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

In the space
B\cup\{\infty\}
every open set is an
F_{\sigma}
-set.

Proof.

Let
O
be open in
B\cup\{\infty\}
, then
\pi^{\leftarrow}[O]
is open
in
(N\times\N)\cup\{\infty\}
and hence an
F_{\sigma}
-set:
By Lemma
2.4
its intersection with every sheet

N\times\{n\}
is an
F_{\sigma}
-set,
and, if
\infty\in\pi^{\leftarrow}[O]
then
\{\infty\}
is a contributing
closed set as well.

Say
\pi^{\leftarrow}[O]=\bigcup_{k\in\N}F_{k}
,
then
O=\bigcup_{k\in\N}\pi[F_{k}]
is an
F_{\sigma}
-set by
Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

This example is not quite the one that we want because although
\{\infty\}
is
a
G_{\delta}
-set it is not a zero-set.
For assume
f:B\cup\{\infty\}\to[0,1]
is continuous and such
that
f(\infty)=0
and
f\bigl(\pi(x,0,0)\bigr)>0
for all
x\in\R
.

Then by the method proof of Lemma
1.4
we find, natural
numbers
k
and
m
, an open interval
J
and a dense subset
A
of
J

such that for all
a\in A
we have
f\bigl(\pi(a,0,0)\bigr)\ge 2^{-k}

and
f\bigl(\pi(x,y,0)\bigr)>\frac{1}{2}f\bigl(\pi(a,0,0)\bigr)
for
all
\langle{x},{y}\rangle\in U^{\prime}(a,m)
.
As before this implies that
f\bigl(\pi(x,0,0)\bigr)\ge 2^{-k-1}

for all
x\in J
and that there is a single
G_{\delta}
-set
G
that is dense
in
J
and such that
f\bigl(\pi(x,0,n)\bigr)\ge 2^{-k-1}
for all
x\in G
and
all
n\in\N
.
This would contradict the continuity of
f
at
\infty
.

3.
A whole Library

We redo our construction.

We start with the product
N\times\N\times\N
and add a point
\infty
.
The basic neighbourhoods of
\infty
are the sets

V_{k}=\bigcup_{m\ge k}\bigcup_{n\ge k}N\times\{\langle{m},{n}\rangle\}

We apply the Jones construction to every column in this matrix, so that
we get a book
B_{m}
for every
m
.

The resulting quotient space, which we call
L
is the union of these books
and the point
\infty
(yes,
L
for Library).
We let
\pi
denote the quotient map.

Proposition 3.1
.

The space
L
is regular, but not completely regular.

Proof.

The space
L
is completely regular at all points except possibly
\infty
.

The space is regular at
\infty
by the same argument as for
B
:
for every
k
the closed neighbourhood
V_{k+1}
is contained in the interior
of
V_{k}
.

To see that
L
is not completely regular at
\infty
let
f:L\to[0,1]

be continuous and such that
f(x,0,m,0)=0
for all
x\in\R
and all
m\in\N
.
By Remark
2.2
applied to every column we obtain a single
dense
G_{\delta}
-set
G
in
\R
such that for all
x\in G

and all
m,n\in\N
we have
f(\pi(x,0,m,n))=0
.
By continuity this implies that
f(\infty)=0
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Every one-point set in
L
is a zero-set.

Proof.

We only need to check this for
\{\infty\}
.

But this is straightforward: let
f:L\to[0,1]
take on the value
2^{-m}

on book
B_{m}
, and let
f(\infty)=0
.
∎

To summarize in a theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

The space
L
is regular, but not completely regular.
Every one-point set is a zero-set of
L
, and every closed set is
a
G_{\delta}
-set.

Proof.

The only thing that still needs to be verified is the last statement.

But that is done almost verbatim as for
B\cup\{\infty\}
.
Adapt the proofs of Lemma
2.3
and
Proposition
2.5
to the present situation.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

This example also has an application in lattice theory.
The lattice
\operatorname{O}(X)
of open sets in a topological space is readily
seen to be complete: the union and the interior of the intersection serve
as the supremum and infimum, respectively, of a family of open sets.
An important sublattice of
\operatorname{O}(X)
is that of the cozero-sets:
\operatorname{Coz}(X)
.

It turns out that
\operatorname{O}(X)
is the Dedekind-MacNeille completion of
\operatorname{Coz}(X)

if and only if
X
is completely regular and each singleton set is a zero-set.

As shown by the present example this result is sharp in that complete
regularity cannot be replaced by regularity.

Thanks to Guram Bezhanishvili for supplying this information.
