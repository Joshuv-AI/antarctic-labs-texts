A Unified Symmetry Classification of Many-Body Localized Phases
Yucheng Wang

Abstract
Anderson localization admits a complete symmetry classification given by the Altland-Zirnbauer (AZ) tenfold scheme, whereas an analogous framework for interacting many-body localization (MBL) has remained elusive. Here we develop a symmetry-based classification of static MBL phases formulated at the level of local integrals of motion (LIOMs). We show that a symmetry is compatible with stable MBL if and only if its action can be consistently represented within a quasi-local LIOM algebra, without enforcing extensive degeneracies or nonlocal operator mixing. This criterion sharply distinguishes symmetry classes: onsite Abelian symmetries are compatible with stable MBL and can host distinct symmetry-protected topological MBL phases, whereas continuous non-Abelian symmetries generically preclude stable MBL. By systematically combining AZ symmetries with additional onsite symmetries, we construct a complete classification table of MBL phases, identify stable, fragile, and unstable classes, and provide representative lattice realizations. Our results establish a unified and physically transparent framework for understanding symmetry constraints on MBL.

Symmetry Constraints on Many-Body Localization.–

We consider a generic interacting lattice system with a finite-dimensional
local Hilbert space on each site
i
.
The operators
\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}
(
\alpha=x,y,z
) denote a set of microscopically local observables, such as Pauli matrices acting on a
physical spin-
1/2
degree of freedom or, more generally, an on-site
operator basis. These bare operators do not commute with the interacting Hamiltonian and
are therefore not conserved.
In a MBL phase, there exists a quasi-local unitary
transformation
U
that maps the microscopic operators to dressed, emergent
conserved operators,
\tau_{i}^{\alpha}=U\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}U^{\dagger}
.
Among them, the operators
\tau_{i}^{z}
form a complete set of independent
LIOMs, or
l-bits

;
IOMclassA
;
IOMSPT
,
satisfying
[\tau_{i}^{z},H]=0
for all
i
.
As a consequence, the Hamiltonian can be brought into a diagonal form,

H=U\tilde{H}U^{\dagger}
, where

\tilde{H}=\sum_{i}\tilde{h}_{i}\,\tau_{i}^{z}+\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}\,\tau_{i}^{z}\tau_{j}^{z}+\sum_{i<j<k}K_{ijk}\,\tau_{i}^{z}\tau_{j}^{z}\tau_{k}^{z}+\cdots.

(1)

This effective Hamiltonian is viewed as the universal form
characterizing the MBL phase.
Here,
\tilde{h}_{i}
denote renormalized on-site fields, while the
effective interactions decay exponentially with distance,

J_{ij}\sim J_{0}e^{-|i-j|/\kappa}
and

K_{ijk}\sim J_{0}e^{-|i-k|/\kappa}
,
where
\kappa
is the corresponding length scale.
Each many-body eigenstate is uniquely labeled by the configuration

\{\tau_{i}^{z}\}
, and its energy is a quasi-local function of these LIOM
eigenvalues. Consequently, in a stable MBL phase, the many-body spectrum is generically
resolved by the LIOM quantum numbers; away from fine-tuned points or phase
transitions, any robust residual degeneracies are enforced by global
symmetry constraints acting on the LIOM algebra.

Global symmetries act on the microscopic operators
\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}
and
are inherited by the LIOMs through the quasi-local unitary transformation
defining the MBL phase.
Accordingly, the stability of MBL is governed by whether a symmetry can be
implemented consistently on the LIOM algebra without destroying its
quasi-local structure or enforcing an extensive pattern of exact
degeneracies. We therefore say that a global symmetry group
G
is compatible with MBL
if there exists a quasi-local unitary transformation such that the
Hamiltonian is diagonal in a complete set of LIOMs
\{\tau_{i}^{z}\}
and the
adjoint action of any
g\in G
maps each LIOM to a quasi-local function of
nearby LIOMs,

g\,\tau_{i}^{z}\,g^{-1}=f_{i}\bigl(\{\tau_{j}^{z}\}\bigr),

(2)

with the function
f_{i}(\{\tau_{j}^{z}\})
having support concentrated near site
i
and
decaying exponentially with distance.
Crucially, the symmetry must not enforce an extensive number of exact
degeneracies in the many-body spectrum, since such degeneracies strongly
enhance resonant processes and destabilize localization.

This criterion immediately distinguishes different symmetry classes.
Abelian onsite symmetries, such as U(1) or
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, are generally
compatible with MBL.
For example, a U(1) symmetry generated by a conserved charge

Q=\sum_{i}q_{i}\tau_{i}^{z}

(3)

Symmetry Classification of Many-Body Localized Phases.–

Building on the symmetry constraints on LIOMs
established above, we now present a systematic
classification of MBL phases.
Our framework extends the AZ symmetry classification
to interacting systems by incorporating the operator-algebraic
structure of LIOMs, additional onsite symmetries, and the stability of localization against
symmetry-enforced degeneracies and resonant processes.
Each symmetry class is therefore characterized not only by its AZ
indices
(T^{2},C^{2},S)

algebra and whether it is compatible with a quasi-local conserved

We note that several AZ symmetry classes that are distinct in non-interacting systems,
including BDI, DIII, CI, and CII, do not give rise to independent entries
in the present MBL classification.
Once interactions are included and MBL is formulated in terms of a
complete set of LIOMs, these distinctions do not impose additional
constraints on the LIOM algebra beyond those already captured by the
classes A, AI, AII, AIII, D, and C (see the End Matter).

In the absence of symmetry constraints (class A), LIOMs are simple
scalar operators
\tau_{i}^{z}

parametrically suppressed at strong disorder, making this class the most
favorable setting for stable MBL.

Conclusion and Discussion.–

We have developed a comprehensive symmetry-based
classification of many-body localized (MBL) phases, providing a
systematic extension of the Altland–Zirnbauer framework to interacting
disordered systems.
Our approach is formulated at the operator level, where MBL is defined
by the existence of an extensive set of quasi-local LIOMs, and global symmetries act by constraining the algebraic
structure of these emergent conserved operators.
This perspective yields a unified and physically transparent criterion
for determining whether a given symmetry is compatible with stable
localization.
Within this framework, we constructed a classification covering all
combinations of time-reversal, particle-hole, and chiral symmetries,
supplemented by common onsite symmetries such as U(1),
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
,
and SU(2).
The resulting scheme organizes MBL phases into stable, fragile,
symmetry-protected, and unstable classes, and naturally incorporates
both conventional localized phases and symmetry-protected topological
MBL phases.
Embedding well-studied lattice models into this unified framework
clarifies their stability properties and reconciles previously
fragmented observations within a single symmetry-based picture.

More broadly, our results highlight symmetry as a primary organizing
principle for interacting localization phenomena.
By delineating which symmetry actions are compatible with a quasi-local
LIOM algebra and which inevitably destabilize it, the classification
provides a foundation for addressing open questions in driven and open
systems, in higher spatial dimensions, and in the interplay between
localization and topology.

Reduction of AZ Classes in the MBL Regime.—

In the AZ classification of non-interacting fermionic
systems, the ten symmetry classes are distinguished by different
combinations of time-reversal (
T
), particle-hole (
C
), and chiral (
S
)
symmetries. Classes such as BDI, DIII, CI, and CII are therefore genuinely distinct at
the single-particle level and play an essential role in determining the
structure of Anderson localization, nonlinear sigma models, and
topological band theory. In the interacting MBL setting considered in this
work, the organizing principle is fundamentally different.
MBL is defined by the existence of an extensive set of LIOMs, and the classification of MBL phases is governed
by how global symmetries constrain the algebraic structure of these LIOMs.
When formulated at this operator level, several AZ symmetry classes that
are distinct in the non-interacting limit no longer impose independent or
qualitatively new constraints.
In particular, the additional symmetry relations that distinguish the
BDI, DIII, CI, and CII classes do not enforce new local degeneracies,
Kramers-like multiplet structures, or symmetry-protected operator
multiplets beyond those already present in classes A, AI, AII,
AIII, D, and C.
As a result, they do not lead to distinct localization stability criteria
or separate MBL phases.

More concretely, in class BDI, the time-reversal and particle-hole
symmetries satisfy
T^{2}=+1
and
C^{2}=+1
, implying the presence of a chiral symmetry

S=TC
.
In the MBL setting, however, the absence of Kramers
degeneracies means that these symmetries constrain the LIOM algebra in a
much more limited way.
Generically, if the particle-hole symmetry does not induce a genuine
Bogoliubov-de Gennes (BdG) structure, the effective constraints reduce
to those of class AI, whereas when a BdG structure is present the system
falls into class D.
Accordingly, generic BDI Hamiltonians collapse to AI or D in the MBL
classification, depending on their microscopic realization.
A nongeneric but physically important exception arises when the chiral
symmetry
S
provides the only nontrivial constraint on the LIOM algebra,
while time-reversal and particle-hole symmetries do not enforce
additional local degeneracies or BdG structures.
In this case, the symmetry constraints are fully captured by the chiral
pairing structure alone, and the system is naturally described as
belonging to class AIII in the MBL regime.
A paradigmatic example is provided by the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH)
model. Although the noninteracting SSH model belongs to the BDI class in the AZ
classification, in the interacting MBL setting its LIOM algebra is
constrained only by chiral symmetry, so that the distinction between BDI
and AIII does not lead to qualitatively different localization
properties.
For this reason, SSH-type models are most naturally discussed as
representative examples of AIII-class MBL phases.

Class DIII is characterized by
T^{2}=-1
and
C^{2}=+1
, leading to Kramers
pairs enforced by time-reversal symmetry.
In the MBL setting, the essential consequence is precisely this
Kramers pairing, which is already fully captured by class AII.
The additional chiral symmetry does not impose further constraints on the
LIOM algebra, and therefore DIII reduces to AII.
Similarly, class CI, with
T^{2}=+1
and
C^{2}=-1
, typically arises in
systems with spin-rotation or charge conservation.
At the level of LIOMs, the relevant constraint is the presence of a
conserved U(1) charge together with a time-reversal symmetry that does
not enforce local degeneracies.
This situation is fully captured by the AI
+
U(1) class in our
classification.
Finally, class CII combines
T^{2}=-1
with
C^{2}=-1
and thus enforces
Kramers degeneracies together with a conserved U(1) charge.
In the MBL regime, these constraints reduce to those of the

Supplementary Material:

A Unified Symmetry Classification of Many-Body Localized Phases

This supplementary material presents the Schrieffer–Wolff (SW) transformation as a
constructive method for deriving local integrals of motion (LIOMs) in
strongly disordered systems. The discussion serves three purposes.
First, it illustrates through a concrete example how LIOMs emerge
order by order in perturbation theory.
Second, it provides a detailed explanation of the statement that if the
Hamiltonian
H
has a symmetry
G
, then each generator
S^{(n)}
and the
resulting LIOMs
\tau_{i}^{z}
transform covariantly under
G
.
Third, it clarifies the distinct roles played by different realizations
of U(1) symmetry within the LIOM framework, in particular explaining
why the disordered XXZ spin chain is classified in class A, whereas a
spinless fermionic system with particle-number conservation belongs to
class A
+
U(1), despite the fact that the two models can be related by mapping
transformations at the level of the microscopic Hamiltonian under
suitable conditions, such mappings do not imply an equivalence of the
resulting LIOM algebras or of the corresponding MBL symmetry
classification.

We consider a generic lattice Hamiltonian decomposed into a dominant diagonal part and a weak off-diagonal perturbation,

H=H_{0}+\lambda V,

where

H_{0}=\sum_{i}h_{i}\sigma_{i}^{z}

with strong random fields
h_{i}\in[-W,W]
and
W\gg 1
, while
\lambda\ll 1
controls the strength of interactions contained in
V
. We decompose the perturbation
V
into its diagonal and off-diagonal
parts in the eigenbasis of
H_{0}
,

V=V_{\mathrm{d}}+V_{\mathrm{od}},

where
V_{\mathrm{d}}
commutes with
H_{0}
and
V_{\mathrm{od}}
contains
all matrix elements connecting different eigenstates of
H_{0}
. The goal is to construct a quasi-local unitary transformation
U=e^{S}
, with
S^{\dagger}=-S
, such that the transformed Hamiltonian

\tilde{H}=e^{S}He^{-S}

is diagonal in the eigenbasis of
H_{0}
up to exponentially small corrections.

The generator
S
is expanded perturbatively as

S=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}S^{(n)}.

Using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff expansion,

\tilde{H}=H+[S,H]+\frac{1}{2}[S,[S,H]]+\frac{1}{3!}[S,[S,[S,H]]]+\cdots,

we impose that the off-diagonal part of
\tilde{H}
vanishes order by order in
\lambda
. At first order, the generator
S^{(1)}
is fixed by the condition

[S^{(1)},H_{0}]=-V_{\mathrm{od}},

which, in the eigenbasis of
H_{0}
,
H_{0}|\alpha\rangle=E_{\alpha}|\alpha\rangle
,
yields

\langle\alpha|S^{(1)}|\beta\rangle=\frac{-\langle\alpha|V_{\mathrm{od}}|\beta\rangle}{E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta}},\qquad E_{\alpha}\neq E_{\beta}.

For later convenience and to streamline the recursive construction at
higher orders, this solution can be written compactly as

S^{(1)}=\mathcal{L}_{H_{0}}^{-1}(-V_{\mathrm{od}}),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{H_{0}}(X)=[H_{0},X],

where the inverse Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{H_{0}}^{-1}
is understood to act only on off-diagonal
operators.
At second order,
S^{(2)}
is chosen to cancel the off-diagonal contributions generated by commutators involving
S^{(1)}
,

[S^{(2)},H_{0}]=-\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{od}}\left(\frac{1}{2}[S^{(1)},V]+\frac{1}{2}[S^{(1)},[S^{(1)},H_{0}]]\right),

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{od}}
projects onto the off-diagonal subspace. Higher orders follow recursively.

I.1
Example 1: Disordered XXZ Spin Chain (Class A)

To make the construction explicit, we first apply this procedure to the random XXZ spin chain (Eq.(5) in the main text),

H_{\mathrm{XXZ}}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}h_{i}S_{i}^{z}+\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}\left[J_{z}S_{i}^{z}S_{i+1}^{z}+\frac{J_{\perp}}{2}\left(S_{i}^{+}S_{i+1}^{-}+S_{i}^{-}S_{i+1}^{+}\right)\right],

where
S_{i}^{\pm}=S_{i}^{x}\pm iS_{i}^{y}
,
h_{i}
are independent random variables uniformly distributed in
[-W,W]
, and
J_{z},J_{\perp}\sim J
with
J/W\ll 1
. We identify

H_{0}=\sum_{i}h_{i}S_{i}^{z},\qquad V=\sum_{i}\left[J_{z}S_{i}^{z}S_{i+1}^{z}+\frac{J_{\perp}}{2}\left(S_{i}^{+}S_{i+1}^{-}+S_{i}^{-}S_{i+1}^{+}\right)\right],

and treat
\lambda\sim J/W
as the small parameter.

The off-diagonal part of
V
is

V_{\mathrm{od}}=\sum_{i}\frac{J_{\perp}}{2}\left(S_{i}^{+}S_{i+1}^{-}+S_{i}^{-}S_{i+1}^{+}\right).

Solving
[S^{(1)},H_{0}]=-V_{\mathrm{od}}
yields

S^{(1)}=\sum_{i}\frac{J_{\perp}}{2(h_{i}-h_{i+1})}\left(S_{i}^{+}S_{i+1}^{-}-S_{i}^{-}S_{i+1}^{+}\right).

The energy denominators
(h_{i}-h_{i+1})\sim W
ensure that
S^{(1)}
is parametrically small.

To first order in
J/W
, the transformed Hamiltonian becomes

\tilde{H}^{(1)}=\sum_{i}h_{i}S_{i}^{z}+\sum_{i}J_{z}S_{i}^{z}S_{i+1}^{z}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\frac{J_{\perp}^{2}}{2(h_{i}-h_{i+1})}\left(S_{i}^{z}-S_{i+1}^{z}\right)+\mathcal{O}(J^{3}/W^{2}),

where the commutator
\frac{1}{2}[S^{(1)},V_{\mathrm{od}}]
generates additional diagonal terms.

The LIOMs are obtained by dressing the bare operators,

\tau_{i}^{z}=e^{S}S_{i}^{z}e^{-S}.

To first order,

\tau_{i}^{z}\simeq S_{i}^{z}+[S^{(1)},S_{i}^{z}]=S_{i}^{z}+\frac{J_{\perp}}{2(h_{i}-h_{i+1})}\left(S_{i}^{+}S_{i+1}^{-}-S_{i}^{-}S_{i+1}^{+}\right)+\frac{J_{\perp}}{2(h_{i}-h_{i-1})}\left(S_{i}^{+}S_{i-1}^{-}-S_{i}^{-}S_{i-1}^{+}\right).

This expression explicitly shows how a strictly local operator acquires exponentially decaying tails due to virtual spin-flip processes.

For strong disorder, typical matrix elements satisfy

\frac{|V_{\alpha\beta}|}{|E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta}|}\sim\frac{J}{W}<1,

ensuring convergence of the SW expansion. Higher-order terms involve products of such denominators, leading to amplitudes bounded by
CJ(J/W)^{k-1}
for operators spanning
k
sites. As a result, the LIOMs are quasi-local, with a localization length
\xi\sim 1/\ln(W/J)
.

I.2
Example 2: Disordered Spinless Fermion Chain (Class A+U(1))

We now consider a disordered interacting spinless fermion chain to illustrate the class A+U(1):

H_{\mathrm{ferm}}=\sum_{i}\left[t_{i}(c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i+1}+\mathrm{h.c.})+V_{i}n_{i}n_{i+1}+\mu_{i}n_{i}\right],

where
n_{i}=c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i}
, and
t_{i}
,
V_{i}
,
\mu_{i}
are random with typical magnitude
|t_{i}|,|V_{i}|\ll|\mu_{i}|\sim W
. This Hamiltonian conserves total particle number
N=\sum_{i}n_{i}
, giving a U(1) symmetry.

We take
H_{0}=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}n_{i}
and

V=\underbrace{\sum_{i}t_{i}(c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i+1}+\mathrm{h.c.})}_{V_{\mathrm{od}}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i}V_{i}n_{i}n_{i+1}}_{V_{\mathrm{d}}}.

The first-order generator must satisfy
[S^{(1)},H_{0}]=-V_{\mathrm{od}}
. For a hopping term
t_{i}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i+1}
connecting states with energies
E_{\alpha}
and
E_{\beta}
, we have

E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta}=\mu_{i}(0)+\mu_{i+1}(1)-[\mu_{i}(1)+\mu_{i+1}(0)]=-(\mu_{i}-\mu_{i+1}).

Thus

S^{(1)}=\sum_{i}\frac{t_{i}}{\mu_{i+1}-\mu_{i}}(c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i+1}-c_{i+1}^{\dagger}c_{i}).

To first order, the transformed Hamiltonian is

\tilde{H}^{(1)}=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}n_{i}+\sum_{i}V_{i}n_{i}n_{i+1}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\frac{t_{i}^{2}}{\mu_{i+1}-\mu_{i}}(n_{i}-n_{i+1})+\mathcal{O}(t^{3}/W^{2}).

The LIOMs are constructed as
\tau_{i}=e^{S}n_{i}e^{-S}
. To first order:

\tau_{i}\simeq n_{i}+[S^{(1)},n_{i}]=n_{i}+\frac{t_{i-1}}{\mu_{i}-\mu_{i-1}}(c_{i-1}^{\dagger}c_{i}+c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i-1})-\frac{t_{i}}{\mu_{i+1}-\mu_{i}}(c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i+1}+c_{i+1}^{\dagger}c_{i}).

I.3
Comparison of Symmetry Realization in the LIOM Algebra

The two examples reveal fundamentally different ways in which a U(1) symmetry can be realized at the level of the emergent LIOM algebra. For the XXZ chain, the choice of
S_{i}^{z}
as the bare operators from which
LIOMs are dressed is a matter of convenience rather than symmetry
necessity.
One could equally well start from a different local basis, such as

S_{i}^{x}
or
S_{i}^{y}
, and carry out the SW construction, albeit with a more
involved bookkeeping of resonant processes. At no stage is the U(1) symmetry generated by

Q=S^{z}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{i}S_{i}^{z}
required to select the operator
content or to define the LIOM algebra. Indeed, the entire construction may be performed without reference to

Q
, and only a posteriori does one discover that a particular
linear combination of the resulting scalar LIOMs reconstructs the
conserved charge,
Q=\sum_{i}\tau_{i}^{z}
up to quasi-local corrections.
In this sense, the U(1) symmetry emerges within an already well-defined
scalar LIOM framework and does not constitute an independent structural
constraint.

By contrast, in an interacting spinless fermion chain, the conserved
particle number
N=\sum_{i}n_{i}
must be taken into account from the outset.
Before any LIOM construction can begin, the U(1) symmetry enforces a
superselection structure that restricts the admissible local operators:
one must decide whether the building blocks are
n_{i}
,
c_{i}
, or

c_{i}^{\dagger}
, and this choice is fixed by particle-number conservation.
Only charge-neutral operators can enter the LIOM algebra, and the dressed
LIOMs must be constructed so as to label eigenstates across distinct
particle-number sectors.
Here the relation
\sum_{i}\tau_{i}=N
is not an emergent observation but a
defining requirement for the completeness of the LIOMs.
Thus, the U(1) symmetry acts as a
genuine organizing principle that shapes the operator content and the
construction itself.

This distinction becomes especially transparent when the U(1) symmetry
is weakly broken.
In class A systems such as the disordered XXZ spin chain, breaking spin
conservation by adding generic local perturbations (e.g., transverse
fields) does not introduce new fundamental constraints on the LIOM
algebra.
The LIOMs remain scalar and quasi-local, and the system typically
remains within class A, with localization properties that are
continuously connected to those of the U(1)-symmetric limit.
By contrast, in class A
+
U(1) fermionic systems, breaking particle-number
conservation has a qualitatively different effect.
Introducing pairing terms forces a reformulation in terms of
Bogoliubov–de Gennes quasiparticles, enlarging the operator algebra and
changing the effective symmetry content.
As a result, the system generally transitions to a different AZ class,
such as class D or class C, depending on the presence or absence of
additional symmetries.
This symmetry-class transmutation can qualitatively modify localization
stability, spectral statistics, and topological characteristics.
These contrasting responses to U(1) breaking reflect the underlying
classification criterion: whether the U(1) symmetry merely emerges as a redundancy within a scalar LIOM algebra (class A), or instead defines the
operator framework and superselection structure in which localization is
formulated (class A
+
U(1)).

I.4
General Symmetry Considerations

We now turn to the general role of symmetry. Let
G
be a global symmetry group represented by unitary or anti-unitary operators
g
satisfying
gHg^{-1}=H
. The recursive equations defining each
S^{(n)}
are manifestly covariant under such transformations. Applying
g
to the defining equation for
S^{(1)}
gives

[gS^{(1)}g^{-1},\,H_{0}]=-V_{\mathrm{od}},

which is identical to the original equation. Provided the solution is chosen uniquely by fixing its diagonal part to zero,
which removes the freedom to add operators commuting with
H_{0}
,
this implies
gS^{(1)}g^{-1}=S^{(1)}
. The same argument applies recursively to all higher orders.

For anti-unitary symmetries such as time-reversal
T
or particle-hole symmetry
C
,
complex conjugation acts on the anti-Hermitian generators
S^{(n)}
.
With the standard choice that
S^{(n)}
has vanishing diagonal part,
one finds

TS^{(n)}T^{-1}=-S^{(n)},\qquad CS^{(n)}C^{-1}=\pm S^{(n)},

where the sign depends on the specific implementation of the symmetry.
In all cases, the transformed generator satisfies the same defining
equations as
S^{(n)}
.

Finally, the covariance of the LIOMs follows directly from their definition,

g\tau_{i}^{z}g^{-1}=ge^{S}g^{-1}\,gS_{i}^{z}g^{-1}\,ge^{-S}g^{-1}.

Since
gSg^{-1}=\pm S
, one has
ge^{S}g^{-1}=e^{\pm S}
, implying that
g\tau_{i}^{z}g^{-1}
is obtained by dressing the transformed bare operator in the same manner. This shows explicitly that the emergent LIOMs inherit the symmetry transformation properties of the microscopic degrees of freedom.

This order-by-order SW construction therefore provides a concrete and constructive demonstration of how global symmetries act on the LIOM algebra. It explains why certain symmetries (like the
S_{\mathrm{tot}}^{z}
conservation in the XXZ chain) are compatible with and can be absorbed into a scalar LIOM framework (class A), while others (like particle-number conservation in fermionic systems) impose genuine constraints that require a distinct classification (class A+U(1)).
The failure of the SW construction to converge for symmetries that enforce local multiplets (e.g., SU(2)) manifests the no-go theorems for MBL in those symmetry classes. In this case, the requirement that
S^{(n)}
transform as a vector under
SU(2) prevents the construction of a complete set of commuting,
quasi-local scalar LIOMs.
Equivalently, the SW generator cannot remain quasi-local order by order,
reflecting the no-go theorem for stable MBL in the presence of continuous
non-Abelian symmetries.
