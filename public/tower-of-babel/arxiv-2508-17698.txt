Indefinite structure of the Bergman kernel on the open unit disk
Kenta KOJIN

Abstract
In this paper, we focus on an indefinite structure lying behind the Bergman kernel on the open unit disk. In particular, an invariant distance, birational maps and an indefinite kernel are constructed from the Bergman kernel, and we deal with their interaction.



2010 Mathematical Subject Classification: Primary 46E22; Secondary 46C20

keywords: Bergman kernel, Kreĭn space

1
Introduction

Let
{\mathbb{D}}
denote the open unit disk in the complex plane
{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
k
denote the Bergman kernel on
{\mathbb{D}}
,
that is, we set

k(z,\lambda)=\dfrac{1}{(1-\overline{\lambda}z)^{2}}\quad(z,\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}).

Operator theory induced by the Bergman kernel has been studied in a number of papers,
for example,
Barbian
[
3
]
, Chailos
[
4
,
5
]
, McCullough–Richter
[
9
]
, Yang–Zhu
[
11
]
.
The purpose of this paper is to give a new point of view to functional analysis related to the Bergman kernel.
In order to state our main result, we need a map and a matrix.
Let
\Phi
denote the nonlinear transformation defined as follows:

\Phi:{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2},\quad\lambda\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}\lambda\\
\lambda^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

For any
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})^{\top}
and
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{\top}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, we set

\langle(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})^{\top},(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{\top}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=\lambda_{1}\overline{\mu_{1}}-\lambda_{2}\overline{\mu_{2}}

and consider the indefinite inner product space
{\mathcal{K}}=({\mathbb{C}}^{2},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})
.
Then, since

1-\langle\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\mu)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=1-(2\lambda\overline{\mu}-\lambda^{2}\overline{\mu^{2}})=(1-\lambda\overline{\mu})^{2},

we have that

k(z,\lambda)=\dfrac{1}{1-\langle\Phi(z),\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}.

Moreover, we set

M_{\Phi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(\mu_{1}),\Phi(\mu_{1})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{1}),\Phi(\lambda_{1})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}&\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(\mu_{1}),\Phi(\mu_{2})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{1}),\Phi(\lambda_{2})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\\[14.22636pt]
\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(\mu_{2}),\Phi(\mu_{1})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{2}),\Phi(\lambda_{1})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}&\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(\mu_{2}),\Phi(\mu_{2})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{2}),\Phi(\lambda_{2})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\end{pmatrix}.

As a part of the main result (Theorem
5.3
),
under the condition that
M_{\Phi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is positive semi-definite,
we prove not only that the two-point Pick problem
\lambda_{j}\mapsto\omega_{j}

(j=1,2)
is solvable,
but also that an indefinite two-point Pick problem is solvable as follows:
there exists a rational map
F(z_{1},z_{2})=(f_{1}(z_{1},z_{2}),f_{2}(z_{1},z_{2}))

such that

(i)

F(\Phi(\lambda_{j}))=\Phi(\omega_{j})
for any
j=1,2
,

(ii)

|f_{1}(z_{1},z_{2})|^{2}-|f_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})|^{2}<1

if
|z_{1}|^{2}-|z_{2}|^{2}<1
.

We note that the Bergman kernel does not have the two-point Pick property.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, an invariant distance on
{\mathbb{D}}
is introduced and
a Schwarz–Pick type inequality is discussed.
In Section 3, we deal with birational self-maps of a hyperbolic ball.
In Section 4, it is proved that
the Bergman space is isometrically embedded into a reproducing kernel Kreĭn space.
In Section 5, we discuss structure of Pick matrices and prove the main theorem.

2
Invariant distance

We set

\rho(\lambda,\mu)=\sqrt{1-\dfrac{|k(\lambda,\mu)|^{2}}{k(\lambda,\lambda)k(\mu,\mu)}}\quad(\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{D}}).

Then,
\rho
is a distance on
{\mathbb{D}}
by Lemma 9.9 of Agler–McCarthy
[
1
]
.
Moreover, a direct calculation shows that

\rho(\lambda,\mu)=\sqrt{2\left|\dfrac{\lambda-\mu}{1-\overline{\mu}\lambda}\right|^{2}-\left|\dfrac{\lambda-\mu}{1-\overline{\mu}\lambda}\right|^{4}}.

(2.1)

Hence
\rho
is invariant under the action of the automorphism group of
{\mathbb{D}}
.
Let
{\mathcal{S}}
denote the Schur class, that is, we write

{\mathcal{S}}=\left\{f\in\operatorname{Hol}({\mathbb{D}}):\sup_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}}|f(\lambda)|\leq 1\right\}.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f
be a function in
{\mathcal{S}}
that is not a constant with absolute value one.
Then, for any two points
z
and
w
in
{\mathbb{D}}
,

\rho(f(z),f(w))\leq\rho(z,w).

Moreover,
f
is isometric with respect to
\rho
if and only if
f
is an automorphism of
{\mathbb{D}}
.

Proof.

We set

X=\left|\dfrac{z-w}{1-\overline{w}z}\right|\quad\mbox{and}\quad X_{f}=\left|\dfrac{f(z)-f(w)}{1-\overline{f(w)}f(z)}\right|.

Then, by (
2.1
), we have

\displaystyle\rho(z,w)^{2}-\rho(f(z),f(w))^{2}

\displaystyle=(2X^{2}-X^{4})-(2X_{f}^{2}-X_{f}^{4})

\displaystyle=(X^{2}-X_{f}^{2})(2-X^{2}-X_{f}^{2}).

Since the Schwarz–Pick inequality means that
X_{f}\leq X
,
we conclude the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
be points in
{\mathbb{D}}
.
Then,

M_{\Phi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
is positive semi-definite
if and only if
\rho(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\leq\rho(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.

Proof.

We set
M_{\Phi}=M_{\Phi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
.
Since the diagonal entries of
M_{\Phi}
are nonnegative,
it suffices to show that

\det M_{\Phi}\geq 0
is equivalent to
\rho(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\leq\rho(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.
First, by definition, we have the following identity:

\dfrac{(1-\langle\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})(1-\langle\Phi(\mu),\Phi(\mu)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}{|1-\langle\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\mu)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}}=1-\rho(\lambda,\mu)^{2}

(2.2)

for any
\lambda
and
\mu
in
{\mathbb{D}}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\det M_{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(1-\langle\Phi(\mu_{1}),\Phi(\mu_{1})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})(1-\langle\Phi(\mu_{2}),\Phi(\mu_{2})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}{(1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{1}),\Phi(\lambda_{1})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})(1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{2}),\Phi(\lambda_{2})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}-\left|\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(\mu_{1}),\Phi(\mu_{2})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{1}),\Phi(\lambda_{2})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left|1-\langle\Phi(\mu_{1}),\Phi(\mu_{2})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}\right|^{2}\left(\rho(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})^{2}-\rho(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{2}\right)}{(1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{1}),\Phi(\lambda_{1})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})(1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{2}),\Phi(\lambda_{2})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

The following class denoted as
{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
is suggested by Proposition
2.2
:

{\mathcal{S}}_{B}=\left\{f\in\operatorname{Hol}({\mathbb{D}}):\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(f(z)),\Phi(f(\lambda))\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(z),\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\ \mbox{is positive semi-definite in ${\mathbb{D}}^{2}$}\right\}.

A characterization of
{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
is given in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3
.

{\mathcal{S}}_{B}={\mathcal{S}}\cup{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}
, where we set

{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}=\left\{f\in\operatorname{Hol}({\mathbb{D}}):\inf_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}}|f(\lambda)|\geq 1\right\}.

Proof.

First, we assume that
f
belongs to
{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
.
If
|f(\lambda)|=1
for some
\lambda
in
{\mathbb{D}}
,
then we have

\det\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(f(\lambda)),\Phi(f(\lambda))\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}&\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(f(\lambda)),\Phi(f(\mu))\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\mu)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\\[14.22636pt]
\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(f(\mu)),\Phi(f(\lambda))\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(\mu),\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}&\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(f(\mu)),\Phi(f(\mu))\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(\mu),\Phi(\mu)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\end{pmatrix}=-\left|\dfrac{(1-f(\lambda)\overline{f(\mu)})^{2}}{(1-\lambda\overline{\mu})^{2}}\right|^{2}\geq 0

for any
\mu
in
{\mathbb{D}}
.
This implies that
f({\mathbb{D}})
does not intersect with the unit circle whenever
f
is not a unimodular constant.
Hence
f
is in
{\mathcal{S}}\cup{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}
.
Next, we suppose that
f
is a function in
{\mathcal{S}}\cup{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}
.
If
f
is in
{\mathcal{S}}
, then, it follows from Schur’s theorem that

\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(f(z)),\Phi(f(\lambda))\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(z),\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}=\left(\dfrac{1-\overline{f(\lambda)}f(z)}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}\right)^{2}

is positive semi-definite. Hence,
f
belongs to
{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
.
If
f
is in
{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}
, that is,
1/f
is in
{\mathcal{S}}
, then we have

\dfrac{1-\langle\Phi(f(z)),\Phi(f(\lambda))\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(z),\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}=\left(\dfrac{1-\overline{(1/f(\lambda))}(1/f(z))}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}\overline{f(\lambda)}f(z)\right)^{2}

is positive semi-definite by Schur’s theorem.
Hence,
f
belongs to
{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
.
∎

3
Birational maps

In this section, we shall consider the unit ball of
{\mathcal{K}}=({\mathbb{C}}^{2},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})
.
In what follows, each element in
{\mathcal{K}}
is denoted such as
\boldsymbol{z}
in bold type.
Let
\Omega
be the unit ball in
{\mathcal{K}}
, that is, we set

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in{\mathcal{K}}:\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}<1\}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},z_{2})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:|z_{1}|^{2}-|z_{2}|^{2}<1\}.

A vector
\boldsymbol{a}
in
{\mathcal{K}}
is said to be neutral if
\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=0
.
Let
\boldsymbol{a}
be a nonneutral vector in
\Omega
.
Then, for any
\boldsymbol{z}
in
\Omega
, we set

P_{\boldsymbol{a}}\boldsymbol{z}=\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\boldsymbol{a}.

It is not difficult to see that
P_{\boldsymbol{a}}
is idempotent and self-adjoint with respect to the inner product of
{\mathcal{K}}
.
Moreover, we set
s_{\boldsymbol{a}}=(1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})^{1/2}
,
Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}=I-P_{\boldsymbol{a}}
and

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{\boldsymbol{a}-P_{\boldsymbol{a}}\boldsymbol{z}-s_{\boldsymbol{a}}Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}\boldsymbol{z}}{1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}

(cf. Section 2.2 in Rudin
[
10
]
, and see also K.D.–Kojin–Seto
[
8
]
).
Although
\boldsymbol{0}=(0,0)^{\top}
is a neutral vector, as an exception,
it is convenient to define
P_{\boldsymbol{0}}\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{0}}(\boldsymbol{z})=-\boldsymbol{z}
.
We set

\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}=\Omega\setminus\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega:\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=1\}.

A matrix
T
is said to be
\sharp
-unitary if
T^{\sharp}=T^{-1}
where
T^{\sharp}

denotes the adjoint matrix of
T
with respect to the inner product of
{\mathcal{K}}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}
be a nonneutral vector in
\Omega
.
Then,

(i)

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{0})=\boldsymbol{a}
and
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{a})=\boldsymbol{0}
.

(ii)

For any
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
in
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}
,

1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=\frac{1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{(1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})(1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}(1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}).

In particular, for any
\boldsymbol{z}
in
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}
,

1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=\frac{1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{|1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}}(1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}).

(iii)

For any
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
in
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}
,

1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=\frac{1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}(1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{w})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}).

(iv)

For any
\boldsymbol{z}
in
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}
,

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z})
belongs to
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}

and
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z}))=\boldsymbol{z}
. In particular,
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}|_{\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}}
is an automorphism of
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}
.

(v)

For any
\sharp
-unitary matrix
T
,

T\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}T^{\sharp}=\varphi_{T\boldsymbol{a}}
.

(vi)

For any
z
and
w
in
{\mathbb{D}}
,

\rho(z,w)^{2}=\langle\varphi_{\Phi(w)}(\Phi(z)),\varphi_{\Phi(w)}(\Phi(z))\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}.

Proof.

Routine calculation shows (i), (ii) and (iii).
We shall show (iv).
It follows from (ii) that
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}
maps
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}
to
\Omega
. Moreover,
Since

\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}},

we have that
\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}\neq 1
.
Hence
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}|_{\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}}
is a self-map of
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}
.
Further, direct calculation shows that
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z}))=\boldsymbol{z}
.
Thus, we have (iv).
It is not difficult to see (v).
By (
2.2
) and (ii), we have (vi).
∎

The following fact might be known. However, we have not found appropriate literature,
so that we include a proof here for the sake of readers.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{b}
be any nonneutral vector in
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}}
. Then,
there exists a
\sharp
-unitary matrix
T
such that

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\circ\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}=T\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}
.

Proof.

Since
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}
is a linear fractional map, making some calculations,
we obtain that the denominator of
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z}))
is

(1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})(1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})

and the numerator of
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{z}))
is a linear expression of
\boldsymbol{z}
.
By this observation, we have that

\displaystyle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\circ\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\text{a linear expression of $\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{z})$}}{1-\langle\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{z}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\dfrac{\text{a linear expression of $\boldsymbol{z}$}}{1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle}}{1-\left\langle\dfrac{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})-P_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}\boldsymbol{z}-s_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}Q_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}\boldsymbol{z}}{1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}},\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\text{a linear expression of $\boldsymbol{z}$}}{(1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})-(\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\text{a linear expression of $\boldsymbol{z}$}}{1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}.

Hence, there exist a matrix
T
and a vector
\boldsymbol{u}
such that

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\circ\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{z})=T\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{u}.

Further,
it follows from (i) and (iv) of Proposition
3.1
that

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\circ\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{0})=\boldsymbol{0},

that is,
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Moreover, by (iv) of Proposition
3.1
, we have that

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\circ\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z})=\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\circ\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{z})=T\boldsymbol{z}.

Thus we have
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\circ\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}=T\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}
.
Hence, applying (ii) and (iii) of Proposition
3.1
repeatedly, we have that

\displaystyle 1-\langle T\boldsymbol{z},T\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}

\displaystyle=1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\circ\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{z}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\circ\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{z})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\langle\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{b}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{|1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{b}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}}\cdot\frac{1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{|1-\langle\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\frac{1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{|1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}}\cdot(1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(1-\langle\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{b}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})|1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{a})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}|1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}}{|1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}|1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\dfrac{(1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})|1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}}{|1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}|1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{a})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\frac{1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{|1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}}\cdot(1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})(1-\langle\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{b}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}{|1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}|^{2}(1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}\cdot(1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})

\displaystyle=1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}.

The above calculations work if
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}
, where
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}
are suitable finite nonneutral vectors (e.g.,
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}=\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}_{2}=\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b}),\boldsymbol{a}_{3}=\varphi_{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{b})}(\boldsymbol{a})
).
Since each
\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega:\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=1\}
is a nowhere dense set, so is
\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega:\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=1\}
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}
is dense in
\Omega.

By the continuity of inner product and matrix of finite dimension,
for any vector
\boldsymbol{z}
in
{\mathcal{K}}
, we have that

\langle T\boldsymbol{z},T\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

4
Indefinite kernel

In this section, we study the structure of the indefinite kernel defined
on
\Omega\times\Omega

K(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\dfrac{1}{1-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\quad(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Omega).

The symbol
{\mathbb{B}}_{2}
denotes the open unit ball

{\mathbb{B}}_{2}=\left\{(z_{1},z_{2})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}<1\right\}.

Trivially,

{\mathbb{B}}_{2}

is included in
\Omega
. Further,
the following formal calculations make sense on

{\mathbb{B}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{B}}_{2}
:

\displaystyle K(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(z_{1}\overline{\lambda_{1}}-z_{2}\overline{\lambda_{2}})^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}(z_{1}\overline{\lambda_{1}})^{n-k}(z_{2}\overline{\lambda_{2}})^{k}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\{\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{2\ell}(z_{1}\overline{\lambda_{1}})^{n-2\ell}(z_{2}\overline{\lambda_{2}})^{2\ell}\right\}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lceil n/2\rceil}\binom{n}{2\ell-1}(z_{1}\overline{\lambda_{1}})^{n-2\ell+1}(z_{2}\overline{\lambda_{2}})^{2\ell-1}\right\}.

Indeed, if
|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2},|\lambda_{1}|^{2}+|\lambda_{2}|^{2}\leq r<1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(|z_{1}\overline{\lambda_{1}}|+|z_{2}\overline{\lambda_{2}}|)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2})^{n/2}(|\lambda_{1}|^{2}+|\lambda_{2}|^{2})^{n/2}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^{n}<\infty.

Setting

K_{+}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\{\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{2\ell}(z_{1}\overline{\lambda_{1}})^{n-2\ell}(z_{2}\overline{\lambda_{2}})^{2\ell}\right\}

and

K_{-}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{\sum_{\ell=1}^{\lceil n/2\rceil}\binom{n}{2\ell-1}(z_{1}\overline{\lambda_{1}})^{n-2\ell+1}(z_{2}\overline{\lambda_{2}})^{2\ell-1}\right\},

these
K_{+}
and
K_{-}
are positive semi-definite kernels defined on

{\mathbb{B}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{B}}_{2}
.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{-}
be the reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces generated by
K_{+}
and
K_{-}
, respectively.
Further, we consider the linear map
\Delta
defined as

\Delta:(F_{+},F_{-})\to F_{+}-F_{-}\quad(F_{+}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{+},F_{-}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{-}).

Note that representation
F=F_{+}-F_{-}
is unique, because
any function in
{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
is a power series which has no odd terms of
z_{2}
,
but every function in
{\mathcal{H}}_{-}
has odd terms of
z_{2}
.
Next,
we define an inner product on

\operatorname{ran}\Delta
, the range space of
\Delta
, as follows:

\langle\Delta(F_{+},F_{-}),\Delta(G_{+},G_{-})\rangle_{\Delta}=\langle F_{+},G_{+}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{+}}-\langle F_{-},G_{-}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{-}}

for any
F_{+},G_{+}
in
{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
and any
F_{-},G_{-}
in
{\mathcal{H}}_{-}
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{K}({\mathbb{B}}_{2})
denote the linear space
\operatorname{ran}\Delta
endowed with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Delta}
.
We see that the Bergman space
L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
is isometrically embedded into
{\mathcal{K}}_{K}({\mathbb{B}}_{2})
as a Kreĭn space in the following sense (cf. Theorems 7.31 and 8.2 in Agler-McCarthy
[
1
]
). We fix a sufficiently small positive number
r
so that
\Phi(\lambda)
falls in
{\mathbb{B}}_{2}
for any
\lambda\in r{\mathbb{D}}
. We note that
L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})|_{r{\mathbb{D}}}=\{f|_{r{\mathbb{D}}}:f\in L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})\}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space on
r{\mathbb{D}}
with kernel
k|_{r{\mathbb{D}}\times r{\mathbb{D}}}
. Then,

L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})\ominus I(r{\mathbb{D}})\rightarrow L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})|_{r{\mathbb{D}}},\;\;\;\;f\mapsto f|_{r{\mathbb{D}}}

is a unitary operator, where
I(r{\mathbb{D}})=\{f\in L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}}):f|_{r{\mathbb{D}}}=0\}.
Since
r{\mathbb{D}}
is open and
L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
is a holomorphic Hilbert function space, we have
I(r{\mathbb{D}})=0
. Thus,

L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})\rightarrow L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})|_{r{\mathbb{D}}},\;\;\;\;f\mapsto f|_{r{\mathbb{D}}}

is a unitary operator. Moreover, we can prove the following result:

Theorem 4.1
.

{\mathcal{K}}_{K}({\mathbb{B}}_{2})
is a reproducing kernel Kreĭn space on
{\mathbb{B}}_{2}
.
Moreover,
L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})|_{r{\mathbb{D}}}
is isometrically embedded into
{\mathcal{K}}_{K}({\mathbb{B}}_{2})
. More precisely, there exists a linear operator
V:L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})|_{r{\mathbb{D}}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{K}}_{K}({\mathbb{B}}_{2})
such that

(1)

Vk_{\lambda}=K_{\Phi(\lambda)}

(\lambda\in r{\mathbb{D}})
and
(2)

\langle Vf,Vf\rangle_{\Delta}=\langle f,f\rangle

(f\in L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})|_{r{\mathbb{D}}})
hold.

Proof.

By the definition of the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Delta}
,

{\mathcal{K}}_{K}({\mathbb{B}}_{2})=(\operatorname{ran}\Delta,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Delta})
is a Kreĭn space.
Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
be any point in
{\mathbb{B}}_{2}
.
Setting
K_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{z})=K(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
and
K_{\pm,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{z})=K_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, we have

\langle F,K_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\rangle_{\Delta}=\langle F_{+},K_{+,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{+}}-\langle F_{-},K_{-,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{-}}=F_{+}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})-F_{-}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

for any
F=\Delta(F_{+},F_{-})
.
Hence,
K_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is the reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{K}}_{K}({\mathbb{B}}_{2})
at
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.
Since
K(\Phi(z),\Phi(w))=k(z,w)
,
it is easy to see that the map
k_{\lambda}\mapsto K_{\Phi(\lambda)}

(\lambda\in r{\mathbb{D}})

extends to an isometric linear embedding of
L_{a}^{2}({\mathbb{D}})|_{r{\mathbb{D}}}
into
{\mathcal{K}}_{K}({\mathbb{B}}_{2})
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Our

{\mathcal{K}}_{K}({\mathbb{B}}_{2})

gives a concrete example to Theorem 3.1 in Alpay
[
2
]
.

5
Pick matrices

In this section, we shall discuss structure of Pick matrices.
It is well known that the Bergman kernel does not have the two-point Pick property
(see Example 5.17 in Agler–McCarthy
[
1
]
). However,
the following modified two-point interpolation theorem holds.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
be distinct two points in
{\mathbb{D}}
, and let
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
be arbitrary two points in
{\mathbb{D}}
.
Then,
M_{\Phi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is positive semi-definite if and only if
there exists a function
f
in
{\mathcal{S}}
such that
f(\lambda_{j})=\omega_{j}
for any
j=1,2
.

Proof.

We set
M_{\Phi}=M_{\Phi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
.
Suppose that
M_{\Phi}
is positive semi-definite.
Then, by Proposition
2.2
, we have that
\rho(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\leq\rho(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.
Moreover, by the same calculation as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we have that

\left|\dfrac{\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}}{1-\omega_{1}\overline{\omega_{2}}}\right|\leq\left|\dfrac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{1-\lambda_{1}\overline{\lambda_{2}}}\right|.

This concludes the only if part.
Since
M_{\Phi}
is the square of a Pick matrix with respect to the Schur product,
the if part is trivial by Schur’s theorem.
∎

A matrix
T
is called a contraction on
{\mathcal{K}}
if
\langle T\boldsymbol{z},T\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}\leq\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}
for any
\boldsymbol{z}
in
{\mathcal{K}}

(see p. 150 in Dritschel–Rovnyak
[
7
]
)
We note that
T
is a contraction on
{\mathcal{K}}
if and only if

\begin{pmatrix}\langle T\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i},T\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}\end{pmatrix}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\leq\begin{pmatrix}\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}\end{pmatrix}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

(5.1)

for any
n
in
\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}
in
{\mathcal{K}}
.
Further,
{\mathcal{R}}(\Omega)
denotes the set of all rational self-maps of
\Omega
.
A typical example of an element of
{\mathcal{R}}(\Omega)
is
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}
discussed
in Section 3.
We define the following class:

{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{S}}_{\Phi}(\Omega)=\left\{F\in{\mathcal{R}}(\Omega):\dfrac{1-\langle F\circ\Phi(z),F\circ\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(z),\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\ \text{is positive semi-definite in ${\mathbb{D}}^{2}$}\right\}.

Example 5.2
.

Let
T
be any contraction on
{\mathcal{K}}
.
Then,
T\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}
belongs to
{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{S}}_{\Phi}(\Omega)
.
Indeed,
it follows from
(
5.1
), Schur’s theorem and (ii) in Proposition
3.1
that

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{1-\langle T\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\Phi(\lambda_{i}),T\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\Phi(\lambda_{j})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{i}),\Phi(\lambda_{j})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\end{pmatrix}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

\displaystyle\geq\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{(1-\langle\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\Phi(\lambda_{i}),\varphi_{\boldsymbol{a}}\circ\Phi(\lambda_{j})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}{1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{i}),\Phi(\lambda_{j})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\end{pmatrix}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{(1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}{(1-\langle\Phi(\lambda_{i}),\boldsymbol{a}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})(1-\langle\boldsymbol{a},\Phi(\lambda_{j})\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}})}\end{pmatrix}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

The following is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
be distinct two points in
{\mathbb{D}}
, and let
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
be points in
{\mathbb{D}}
.
Then,
the following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

M_{\Phi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is positive semi-definite.

(ii)

P(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is positive semi-definite, where
P(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
denotes the Pick matrix with respect to
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
and
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
.

(iii)

\rho(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\leq\rho(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.

(iv)

There exists a map
F
in
{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{S}}_{\Phi}(\Omega)

such that
F(\Phi(\lambda_{j}))=\Phi(\omega_{j})
for any
j=1,2
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
and Lemma
5.1
, (i), (ii) and (iii) are equivalent.
We shall show that (i) and (iv) are equivalent.
Suppose that
M_{\Phi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is positive semi-definite.
We set
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\varphi_{\Phi(\lambda_{2})}(\Phi(\lambda_{1}))
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\varphi_{\Phi(\omega_{2})}(\Phi(\omega_{1}))
.
Then, by (vi) of Proposition
3.1
and Proposition
2.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\omega}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi_{\Phi(\omega_{2})}(\Phi(\omega_{1})),\varphi_{\Phi(\omega_{2})}(\Phi(\omega_{1}))\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}

\displaystyle=\rho(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\rho(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi_{\Phi(\lambda_{2})}(\Phi(\lambda_{1})),\varphi_{\Phi(\lambda_{2})}(\Phi(\lambda_{1}))\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}.

In what follows,
we write
[\boldsymbol{x}]^{[\perp]}=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathcal{K}}:\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=0\}
for any vector
\boldsymbol{x}
in
{\mathcal{K}}
.
We set

T\boldsymbol{z}=\dfrac{1}{\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}\boldsymbol{\nu},

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}
are chosen so that

\boldsymbol{\mu}\in[\boldsymbol{\lambda}]^{[\perp]}
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{[\perp]}
and
\langle\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}},\langle\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\nu}\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}=-1
.
Then,
T\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
T
is a contraction on
{\mathcal{K}}
.
Setting
F=\varphi_{\Phi(\omega_{2})}\circ T\circ\varphi_{\Phi(\lambda_{2})}
, we have that
F(\Phi(\lambda_{j}))=\Phi(\omega_{j})
for any
j=1,2
.
Further, it follows from (ii) of Proposition
3.1
and (
5.1
) that

\dfrac{1-\langle F\circ\Phi(z),F\circ\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(z),\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}=\dfrac{1-\langle\varphi_{\Phi(\omega_{2})}\circ T\circ\varphi_{\Phi(\lambda_{2})}\circ\Phi(z),\varphi_{\Phi(\omega_{2})}\circ T\circ\varphi_{\Phi(\lambda_{2})}\circ\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}{1-\langle\Phi(z),\Phi(\lambda)\rangle_{{\mathcal{K}}}}

is positive semi-definite (see also Example
5.2
).
Hence, we conclude that (i) implies (iv).
The converse is trivial by the definition of
{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{S}}_{\Phi}(\Omega)
.
∎

Diligent readers would have found already that

M_{\Phi}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
is the Schur product of two Pick matrices

P(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
.
Let
P
be a two-by-two Pick matrix. Then,
as a corollary of Theorem
5.3
, we obtain that

P\odot P
is positive semi-definite if and only if so is
P
,
where
\odot
denotes the Schur product.
However, the positive semi-definiteness of
P\odot P
of a three-by-three Pick matrix
P
does not imply that of
P
.
To see this, we consider the following example.

Example 5.4
.

We set
\lambda_{1}=2/3,\lambda_{2}=3/4,\omega_{1}=1/3,\omega_{2}=1/4
.
Then, the Pick matrix
with respect to
\lambda_{1}\mapsto\omega_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}\mapsto\omega_{2}
,
0\mapsto 0
is

\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{1-|\omega_{1}|^{2}}{1-|\lambda_{1}|^{2}}&\dfrac{1-\omega_{1}\overline{\omega_{2}}}{1-\lambda_{1}\overline{\lambda_{2}}}&1\\[14.22636pt]
\dfrac{1-\omega_{2}\overline{\omega_{1}}}{1-\lambda_{2}\overline{\lambda_{1}}}&\dfrac{1-|\omega_{2}|^{2}}{1-|\lambda_{2}|^{2}}&1\\[14.22636pt]
1&1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{8}{5}&\dfrac{11}{6}&1\\[14.22636pt]
\dfrac{11}{6}&\dfrac{15}{7}&1\\[14.22636pt]
1&1&1\end{pmatrix},

which is not positive semi-definite. In fact,

\det\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{8}{5}&\dfrac{11}{6}&1\\[14.22636pt]
\dfrac{11}{6}&\dfrac{15}{7}&1\\[14.22636pt]
1&1&1\end{pmatrix}=-\frac{11}{1260}<0.

However,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\left(\dfrac{1-|\omega_{1}|^{2}}{1-|\lambda_{1}|^{2}}\right)^{2}&\left(\dfrac{1-\omega_{1}\overline{\omega_{2}}}{1-\lambda_{1}\overline{\lambda_{2}}}\right)^{2}&1\\[14.22636pt]
\left(\dfrac{1-\omega_{2}\overline{\omega_{1}}}{1-\lambda_{2}\overline{\lambda_{1}}}\right)^{2}&\left(\dfrac{1-|\omega_{2}|^{2}}{1-|\lambda_{2}|^{2}}\right)^{2}&1\\[14.22636pt]
1&1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{64}{25}&\dfrac{121}{36}&1\\[14.22636pt]
\dfrac{121}{36}&\dfrac{225}{49}&1\\[14.22636pt]
1&1&1\end{pmatrix}

is positive definite.
Indeed,

\det\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{64}{25}&\dfrac{121}{36}\\[14.22636pt]
\dfrac{121}{36}&\dfrac{225}{49}\end{pmatrix}=\dfrac{29087}{63504}>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\det\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{64}{25}&\dfrac{121}{36}&1\\[14.22636pt]
\dfrac{121}{36}&\dfrac{225}{49}&1\\[14.22636pt]
1&1&1\end{pmatrix}=\dfrac{45119}{1587600}>0.
