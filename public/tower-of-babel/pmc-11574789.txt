The brain topological alterations in the structural connectome and correlations with clinical characteristics in type 1 narcolepsy
Authors: Huiqin Zhang, Lin Xu, Zhu Ai, Linlin Wang, Lu Wang, Lili Li, Ruilin Zhang, Rong Xue, Zuojun Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights:•Diffusion tensor imaging is a useful tool in exploring the brain structure networks.•White matter structural connectome related to cortical / subcortical regions has shown to be disrupted in type 1 narcolepsy.•The topological alterations in cingulate and relevant connections potentially affected their clinical manifestations.Keywords: Type 1 narcolepsy, Diffusion tensor imaging, Topological alterations, Structural connectome, Polysomnography

Introduction

The type 1 narcolepsy (NT1) is a sleep disorder characterized by excessive daytime sleepiness, cataplexy episodes, sleep paralysis or hypnagogic hallucinations, and nocturnal sleep disturbance. In NT1, a marked decrease in hypocretin-1 (Hcrt-1) level in cerebrospinal fluid (Toor et al., 2021, Peyron et al., 2000) and a selective loss of the sleep-wake producing neurons in the lateral hypothalamus would cause excessive sleepiness as well as anxiety, depression, cognitive impairment, or other comorbidities (Bassetti et al., 2019, Peyron et al., 1998). Hypocretin affects various neural networks; it maintains the stability of sleep-wake state, energy homeostasis, reward system regulation, and cognitive and mood control (Nishino and Sakurai, 2007). Regarding a variety of complains in narcolepsy, the disrupted local and global white matter (WM) structural integrity and connectives would worth exploring.

Last two decades witnessed emerging neuroimaging studies in investigating the pathophysiology of narcolepsy. Functional imaging analysis found metabolic (Joo et al., 2004) and perfusion (Joo et al., 2005) abnormalities in the hypothalamus–thalamus–orbitofrontal pathway and other brain areas, demonstrating the hypocretin pathway alterations in patients with narcolepsy. Moreover, impaired and abnormal functional connections were also revealed in the executive attention network (Rieger et al., 2003, van Holst et al., 2018) of patients with NT1 (Ponz et al., 2010). Alterations in brain topology and connectivity of some cerebral regions in NT1 patients were reported to be related to somnolence, impulsive behavior, and depression (Fulong et al., 2020). Of note, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) (Most-ley et al., 1991) is a powerful magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) technique in investigating WM structural changes in narcolepsy (Menzler et al., 2012, Nakamura et al., 2013, Scherfler et al., 2012). Park et al. (Park et al., 2016) found the widespread disruption of WM integrity and prevalent brain degeneration of frontal lobes related to depression. Recently, the importance of WM pathology was further demonstrated on H1N1-vaccinated hypocretin deficient patients with NT1 and their first-degree relatives (Juvodden et al., 2018). The widespread abnormalities of WM connectivity in many brain regions of patients with NT1 were also demonstrated through conjoint analysis of whole brain tract-based spatial statistics, regions of interest (ROI)-based analysis, and connectivity analysis (Gool et al., 2019).

Recent studies suggest that the human brain can be modeled as a network, referred to as a connectome (Sporns et al., 2005), and that it exhibits nontrivial topologic principles, such as small-world properties, rich club organization, and modular structure (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009). Meanwhile, the theoretical graphic method has been widely regarded as an effective method for assessing the whole-brain WM structure network using DTI (Caeyenberghs et al., 2012). The WM structure network analysis based on graph theory has reflected several topological characteristics of the network, and proved the relationship between development and intelligence and these topological measures in normal adults (Malpas et al., 2016, Zhao et al., 2015). Changes in the WM network topology has shown to be indicators of various illnesses, such as stroke, Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, and sleep disorder (Ballester-Plane et al., 2017, Shu et al., 2018, Ni et al., 2022). Recently, the disrupted topological structure networks were found to be closely related to not only narcolepsy symptoms but also glutamatergic signatures in narcolepsy (Chen et al., 2024).

However, seldom study has been involved in investigating relationships between the topologic alterations of WM structural connectome and psychophysiological features in narcolepsy. In this study, we focused on NT1 to explore the disrupted WM structural connectome using graph theory approaches, and their associations with clinical index as well as polysomnography (PSG) were further analyzed for exploring the neuropathophysiological features of NT1.

Methods and materials

Experiment design and participants

This study was approved by the institutional review board of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital, and written consent forms were fully informed and signed by all subjects or their families. Patients exhibited related symptoms of NT1 in our hospital enrolled from June 2018 to Aug 2023 were included, as per the International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD)-3. These patients exhibited drowsiness and sleep episodes that are difficult to resist during the day for at least 3 months. According to the standard techniques as American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommendation (Berry et al., 2017); overnight PSG and multiple sleep latency tests (MSLT) were performed. Patients with cataplexy showing an average sleep latency of ≤8 min and ≥2 sleep-onset rapid eye movement (REM) periods (SOREMPs) on MSLT were included in this study. A SOREMP (within 15 min of sleep onset) on the preceding nocturnal PSG could replace one of the SOREMPs in the daytime MSLT. All recruited participants carried out MRI acquisition and performed a standardized clinical evaluation protocol, including basic information recording, a medical history interview, neurologic examination, and a series of neuropsychologic tests.

Moreover, participants exhibited other sleep disorders, other mental disorders, a history of alcohol or drug abuse, organic lesions of the nervous system after MRI examinations, and serious internal medicine diseases were excluded. Hence, 46 patients with NT1 (PT; aged 12–59 years; mean ± standard deviation (SD) = 30 ± 12 years; males: 29) and 34 age- and sex- matched healthy control (HC; aged 12–63 years; mean ± SD = 30 ± 12 years; males: 24) were considered in this study. All participants are right-handed. Specifically, narcoleptic symptom was assessed by Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) (Epworth Sleepiness Scale, 2020, Wang et al., 2016); Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) (Mollayeva et al., 2016), and Narcolepsy Severity Scale (NSS) (van Holst et al., 2019) (PT only), which depicts the symptom severity in the past month measured by four indicators: daytime sleepiness, cataplexy, sleep paralysis and hallucinations, and nocturnal sleep disturbance. The neuropsychological scales include the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) (Thompson, 2015), the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) (Carney et al., 2017), Trail Making Tests A and B (TMTA and TMTB, respectively; the TMT score was recorded as the mean score of the TMTA and TMTB) (Llinas-Regla et al., 2017), the Digit Span Test (DST), which was recorded as the mean of the DST forward score and the DST reverse score (Tripathi et al., 2019), and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment Scale (MoCA) (Ciesielska et al., 2016). Furthermore, a wide range of PSG parameters, including total sleep time (TST), sleep latency (SL), sleep efficiency (SE) (with respect to both total registration and TST), percentage of sleep stages N1, N2, N3, REM efficiency, REM time, REM latency, Apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) and periodic leg movements (PLM), were also collected.

MRI data acquisition

All subjects were scanned using a 3.0 T MRI (GE Discovery MR750, USA) scanner equipped with an 8-channel phase array head coils. A series of standardized clinical protocols were applied for image acquisition, and DTI and 3D T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) were used here. During the acquisition, all subjects were instructed to remain still in the supine position, relax, maintain awake with eyes closed, and think of nothing. Tight but comfortable earplugs and foam padding were placed around their heads to isolate the surrounding noise and minimize head motion. DTI were collected along 64 noncollinear directions with b = 1000 s/mm2 and five b = 0 s/mm2. Other parameters were: repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) = 6000/62.3 ms, number of slices = 50, slice thickness = 3 mm, flip angle (FA) = 90°, matrix size = 128 × 128, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 3 mm3. 3D T1WI were acquired at the sagittal plane by using a magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient-echo sequence (MPRAGE) with parameters: TR/TE = 8.17/3.18 ms, slice thickness = 1 mm, FA = 12°, matrix size = 256 × 256, and voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3.

Network construction and graph-theory network analysis

The DTI data were preprocessed in FMRIB Software Library (FSL: https://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl) (Jenkinson et al., 2012), including obviously inspection, brain skull stripping, motion artifact and eddy current correction, gradient rotation, estimation of the diffusion tensor, and calculation of the fractional anisotropy (FA). The quality control and analysis of MR images were performed by an experienced neuroscientist (ZHQ, with 8 years of experience in MR image analysis). Then, WM structure network was constructed individually following the steps of node definition, WM tractography, edge definition, and network construction using the UCLA Multimodal Connectivity package (https://github.com/jbrown81/umcp). In this study, we defined the number of the connected fibers with two end points located in two regions as the weight of the edge between ROI i and j. To reduce false-positive connections caused by noise and the limitations of tractography, we used a minimum threshold of fiber number (wij = 10 (Shu et al., 2018, Yu et al., 2019), where wij is defined as the weight of the edge) between two regions. Furthermore, structural connections that were present in at least 80 % of the whole group was screened out for each group (Stellmann et al., 2017). The details are explained in Appendix A1.

To characterize the topologic organization of the WM structure networks, the following graph metrics were mainly assessed: global efficiency (Eg), local efficiency (Eloc), characteristic path length (Lp), average clustering coefficient (Cp), and small-worldness (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010) (More details about these measures are provided in Appendix A2). Meanwhile, rich club nodes in this study were selected based on the group-averaged backbone network. The backbone network was constructed by examining the significant nonzero connections across the subjects in each group by using a nonparametric one-tailed sign test (p < 0.05, FDR corrected). The node degree was calculated by the backbone network. The nodes with a nodal degree at least 1 standard deviation above the mean nodal degree were identified as the rich club nodes. Based on the classification of the nodes, the edges of the network were classified into rich club connections (linking rich club nodes to rich club nodes), feeder connections (linking rich club nodes to peripheral nodes), and local connections (linking between peripheral nodes). Furthermore, the connection strength of rich club, feeder, and local connections were calculated for each group. All network analyses were performed on in-house software Gretna (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/gretna/) (Wang et al., 2015) and visualized by using BrainNet Viewer (Xia et al., 2013) and Circos-0.69-6 (https://circos.ca/) (Krzywinski et al., 2009). The schematic plots for the whole procedure are illustrated in Fig. 1.

The flowchart of WM network construction and graph-theory network analysis. The imaging processing includes node definition (90 regions in standard MNI space from AAL atlas were wrapped into native diffusion space using SPM; firstly, the native T1 was normalized to MNI T1 and the transformation was denoted as M, the inverse transformation matrix M−1 was also adopted; secondly, the native T1 images were coregisted with native b0 images, and the transformation was denoted as N; thirdly, M−1 and N were used to warp the AAL atlas to native diffusion space), deterministic tractography using diffusion toolkit, network construction (UCLA based on Python was used) and network analysis (mainly including small-world analysis, rich club analysis, and NBS analysis).

To evaluate the effects of different atlas, repeated network analysis using a different atlas, Schaefer-400 (Schaefer et al., 2018) with more detailed sub-divisions on grey matter, were performed. See Appendix A4 and Fig. S1 for the detailed descriptions of the reproducibility analysis and findings.

Statistical analysis

The statistical results of demographics, PSG, and clinical index were first analyzed. The hypothesis of normality and homoscedasticity of continuous variables were tested by Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene test. Differences in age and questionnaire scores between PT and HC were compared by using independent Mann-Whitney U tests, whereas sex difference was compared using the chi-square test. Since some questionnaires were uncompleted during data collection, the missing data for certain indexes were neglected during the comparisons (the exact numbers of each index used were listed in Table 1). Meanwhile, a significance level P < 0.05 was used, and mean ± SD values were offered.

To determine the between-group differences in global network metrics and different types of connections, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) analysis was performed with age and sex as covariates. A series of correlational analysis were conducted between nodal measurements (nodal degree, nodal efficiency, nodal path length) for all the 90 nodes (details in Appendix Table S1) and clinical index or PSG parameters in PT group. The difference of WM structural connectivity in view of the number of fibers between nodes were compared between two groups using network-based statistical analysis (NBS) (Zalesky et al., 2010). Then, the affected nodes were selected by overlapping two groups’ rich club nodes, and the significantly weakened connections seeding from those nodes were correlated with clinical index or PSG parameters in PT using partial Spearman’s correlation analysis controlled for age and sex. FDR correction was conducted afterwards with a significant level PFDR < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed on SPSS 23.0 software and Matlab 2019b (Mathworks, Natick, MA, USA).

Results

Demographics and clinical assessment

In Table 1, basic information, clinical indexes, and PSG (PT only) were summarized. The age and sex were consistent within two groups as per no significant difference existed (P = 0.973; P = 0.481). The narcoleptic assessment showed that PT had significantly higher ESS and PSQI scores (Z = 6.363, P < 0.001; Z = 3.567, P = 0.02) compared to HC. The mean NSS score for PT group was 34.2 ± 10.7 points (mild: NSS ≤ 10; moderate: 11 < NSS < 16; severe: NSS ≥ 16). In PT, the HAMA, HAMD, and TMT scores were significantly increased (Z = 5.581, P < 0.001; Z = 3.612, P < 0.001; Z = 2.330, P < 0.001), and oppositely the DST and MoCA scores were significantly reduced (Z = -2.857, P = 0.004; Z = -3.655, P < 0.001) compared to HC. In addition, all statistical results for overnight PSG and MSLT listed in Table 1 further reflected impaired sleep structures of PT.

Altered topological features and disrupted structural network in PT

Both groups showed characteristic small-world organization (small-worldness > 1) of the WM networks. In PT group, the Eg (F = 1702.7; P < 0.001), Eloc (F = 316.4; P < 0.001) and Cp (F = 292.0; P < 0.001) were significantly lower, whereas Lp (F = 1699.5; P < 0.001) was greater compared with the HC group (in Fig. 2).

Correlations between structural topology and clinical characteristics of NT1 patients

In PT group, the partial correlation analysis between network nodal measurements of 90 regions and clinical data showed that nodal clustering coefficient NCp of left middle frontal gyrus (MFG) was positively correlated with PSQI scores (r = 0.575, P = 0.0009, with Bonferroni corrected, and all significant correlations were listed in Appendix Table S2) after controlling for age and sex (Fig. 5g). Meanwhile, the NCp of left MFG significantly increased in PT compared to HC. However, no significant correlations were found in view of PSG after Bonferroni corrections.

Of note, SFGdor.R-THA.R were positively correlated with HAMA scores (r = 0.482, PFDR = 0.032). The SFGdor.R-ACG.L (left anterior cingulate and paracingulate gyri) were negatively correlated with MoCA scores (r = -0.56, PFDR = 0.016).

Meanwhile, SFGdor.R-PoCG.R (postcentral gyrus) (r = -0.512, PFDR = 0.048) was in negative correlation with REM latency. The SFGdor.L-ORBmid.L (middle frontal gyrus of orbital) (r = 0.511, PFDR = 0.033), SFGdor.R-ORBsupmed.R (superior frontal gyrus of medial orbital) (r = 0.574, PFDR = 0.012) and SMA.R-ACG.R (r = 0.551, PFDR = 0.018) were positively correlated with SE.

Discussion

The WM structural connectome and clinical characteristics of NT1

In this study, the ESS and PSQI scores of PT significantly increased compared to those of HC, reflecting excessive daytime sleepiness and nocturnal sleep disturbance in PT group. The sleep disorders of PT have also been revealed from quantitative results of NSS, PSG and MSLT. Meanwhile, the significantly increased HAMA and HAMD scores of PT reflected their comorbidities of anxiety and depression. Furthermore, PT group showed significantly decreased MoCA and DST scores, whereas significantly increased TMT scores, suggesting the decline of executive attention and comprehensive cognitive abilities in PT.

In addition, the topological alterations and disruption of WM structural connectome, and their impacts on clinical characteristics of patients were observed. Firstly, characteristic small-world organization was detected in both groups. Of note, the global metrics Eg, Eloc, and Cp of PT group were lower than that of HC group, whereas Lp of PT was greater than that of HC. By overlapping rich club nodes of two groups, the disrupted structural connectives were primarily distributed in the bilateral SFGdor, SMA, left HIP and PAL. Meanwhile, the fraction of rich club connections decreased from 13 % to 10 % and the strength of rich club connections were also reduced in PT, affecting the mediation of complex neurologic function and efficient interregional brain communication and integration at the network level (van den Heuvel et al., 2013, van den Heuvel and Sporns, 2011). Furthermore, the significantly weakened connectives seeding from those selected nodes were found significantly associated with clinical index and sleep structures, demonstrating their involvements in the neuropathophysiology of PT.

The altered structural topology in frontal-parietal cortex connected with subcortical regions shaped characteristics of NT1 patients

In NT1 patients, the increased NCp of left MFG reflected their elevated synchronization in this region, and it also exhibited positive correlation with PSQI scores. Previously, certain studies showed that MFG was closely related to sleep quality. For example, reduced functional connectivity between the right precuneus and right MFG was brought on by sleep deprivation (Li et al., 2020). Furthermore, insomnia patients had a much lower gray matter concentration in the left MFG and PoCG compared with HC (Dai et al., 2014), and a lower fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF) values in right MFG (Li et al., 2014). Besides; it was found that psychological resilience mediated the association of the MFG functional connectivity with sleep quality (Shi et al., Dec 2022), as per psychological disorders were comorbidity of NT1 patients. The above findings would together implicate that the disrupted structural connectome in left MFG (i.e., greater NCp) would lead to bad sleep quality (i.e., higher PSQI scores) in PT.

Regarding those affected rich club nodes, prefrontal cortex and subcortical regions were mainly involved. Several studies in narcolepsy have shown reduced structure or functional activity in SFGdor (Joo et al., 2009, Kaufmann et al., 2002, Xu et al., 2024) and HIP (Joo, 2018, Kim et al., 2016, Nemcova et al., 2015). Huang et al. further found increased metabolism in HIP using positron emission tomography (PET) (Huang et al., 2016), and it was found long sleep duration is related to larger grey matter volume of regions like right PAL (Schiel et al., 2023), where has also been found reduce volumes in patients with iRBD (Rahayel et al., 2018). For SMA, the nodal topological properties of narcolepsy patients have been found changed (Xiao et al., 2019). Furthermore, the supplementary eye field, of which the SMA has been considered to be the oculomotor extension (Schall, 2002), showed greater activity during cognitively demanding saccades versus visually-guided saccades (Jamadar et al., 2013, McDowell et al., 2008). SAM region will be activated during an RBD episode in subject with RBD (Mayer et al., 2015), which has also been a comorbidity of some NT1 patients (Chen et al., 2024). Together, these were consistent with the results that there existed cognitive impairments and disrupted structural networks of bilateral SMA in NT1 patients.

Further in correlation analysis between structural connectives and clinical measurements, the altered structural topology in frontal-parietal cortex connected with subcortical regions was demonstrated to shape sleep characteristics of PT. Regarding the weakened connectives of structure networks, positive correlations between SFG.R-THA.R and HAMA, SFGdor.L-ORBmid.L and SE, and SFGdor.R-ORBsupmid.R and SE were found. However, negative correlations between SFGdor.R-PoCG.R and REM latency was found.

The SFGdor in prefrontal cortex, is the high-level center of brain cognition, decision-making, execution, learning and memory, and emotional regulation (Baddeley, 2003). The dysfunction of the prefrontal cortex was greatly associated with psychological trait, i.e., excessive worry (Baxter and Croxson, 2012). Recently, voxel-wise whole-brain correlational analysis showed a positive association between the cortical thickness of superior frontal gyrus and social anxiety levels (Kim et al., 2023). The THA (typically the paraventricular nucleus) is anatomically positioned to integrate threat- and arousal-related signals from cortex and hypothalamus. A review described the anatomical connections of the THA that support the view that the paraventricular nucleus may be a critical node in the brain anxiety network (Kirouac, 2021). Hence, the strengthened SFGdor.R-THA.R, as a part of the damaged frontal-thalamic circuits (Park et al., 2016); potentially elevate the anxiety mood in NT1 patients.

Looking into the REM latency associated results, we found those significant correlations involved with REM latency was dominantly formed by subjects with prolonged REM latency (see Fig. 5c). Regarding the PSG parameters, 1/3 patients showed extremely long REM latency (> 90 mins, 190 ± 75 mins), increasing the final mean REM latency in Table 1. Meanwhile, these patients exhibited reduced REM efficiency (15 ± 6 %) and SE (65 ± 13 %), and probably their sleep structures have been damaged along with a loss of the first REM sleep. Furthermore, these patients were reported with symptoms of frequent nocturnal awakening or having nightmares. The PoCG of the parietal cortex is the main receptive region for external stimuli as the location of primary somatosensory cortex. Recently, the alteration of grey matter in PoCG showed associations in sleep initiation in patients with insomnia (Fukunaga et al., 2006, Joo et al., 2013). Furthermore, frequent distressful dreams were associated with cortical surface reduction in right PoCG and SFGdor for Parkinson’s disease patients (Radziunas et al., 2018). Previously, deactivation in frontal gyrus during spontaneous arousals in non-REM sleep (Zou et al., 2020)was detected. These influences of disrupted frontal-parietal cortex on sleep manifestations may be supports for that the more impaired of SFGdor.R-PoCG.R would cause longer REM latency in PT.

Disrupted structure networks in prefrontal cortex, e.g., SFGdor.R-ORBsupmid.R and SFGdor.L-ORBmid.L, reflected their visual and somatosensory processing impairments, which in turn result in low SE (Yang et al., 2020). Meanwhile, our previous study (Xu et al., 2024) found functional deficits in the visual and somatosensory area of NT1. It has also been found that RBD patients show prefrontal cortex abnormalities, which may be related to more aggressive dreams and executive dysfunctions (Wakasugi et al., Apr 2021). Recently, left dorsal and medial frontal areas may be proved especially important in mediating sleep (Ioannides et al., 2009, Murphy et al., 2009). PSG-derived SE is the most used quantitative measure to objectively evaluate sleep quality (Jung et al., 2017), and these findings together could be evidences of the positive correlation between SFGdor.R-ORBsupmid.R/SFGdor.L-ORBmid.L and SE. In other word, the disrupted connection in SFGdor.R-ORBsupmid.R/SFGdor.L-ORBmid.L would likely cause bad sleep quality in PT.

The disrupted structure network of cingulate cortex greatly correlated with cognition and sleep structures of PT

Importantly, the cingulate involved connections in WM structure have widely correlated with cognition and sleep structures of PT. Of note, negative correlations were found between SFGdor.R-ACG.L and MoCA scores whereas positive correlations were found between SMA.R-ACG.R and SE.

The cingulate cortex has been identified as the backbone of the structural and functional brain networks. It is associated with multiple brain functions, such as sleep regulation, consciousness state, conscious sensory perception, and efficient executive control (Piantoni et al., 2013). Besides, an electroencephalogram (EEG) study found that sleep deprivation impaired the effective functional connectivity from posterior to anterior cingulate cortex, which can predict the individual differences in the performance of a vigilant attention task after sleep restriction (Piantoni et al., 2013). The ACG has been suggested to participate in cognitive and memory functions (Bubb et al., 2018) as well as emotional processing (as a component of the Papez circuit) (Maldonado et al., 2020).

Once the prefrontal cortex, including the SFGdor and the SMA, is dysfunctional and loses its ability in inhibition of subcortical emotion regulation pathways (limbic structures, such as the HIP and ACG), dysregulation of cortical-limbic system connectivity would occur, i.e., the core mechanism causing emotional, cognitive, and behavioral disorders would be triggered (Chen et al., 2018). Hence, the weakened SFGdor.R-ACG.L would probably affect the cognition of PT. Indeed, this connectivity was strengthened to compensate for the attention in the damaged cognition, i.e., the smaller MoCA scores in PT. Compensatory mechanism is normal for patients who aim to complete a task. For example, higher WM integrity in the cingulum has been associated with sleep and activity disturbances in insomniacs (Rostampour et al., Feb 2022), which has been further demonstrated in shift workers with altered WM integrity of ACG, suggesting their circadian adaption to shift work (Lee et al., 2022). Meanwhile, the SMA was suggested as a crucial region for sleep-dependent consolidation of the motor skill (Tamaki et al., 2013). Moreover, the intensified spontaneous brain oscillations found in the SMA may represent reorganization of the SMA to consolidate the motor-sequence memory. Therefore, we could speculate that the disrupted structure networks in frontal-limbic cortex, i.e. the weakened SMA.R-ACG.R, were highly related to the sleep efficiency of NT1.

In conclusion, we may speculate the cingulate cortex would be a key node linking subcortical regions and cortical regions shaping the sleep structures of NT1, and pertinently this disrupted circuit would be the pathogenesis of poor sleep quality.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered for our study. Long disease course of patients and the first-night-effect during PSG collection might lead to inaccurate clinical examinations. Although the sample size of patients has been increased, the wide age ranges for both groups (around 12–60 years) would likely cause bias in final analysis. During brain network construction, we did not evaluate the effects that different thresholds of fiber number and connection matrix with FA had on the findings, so more reproducibility analysis may be conducted for further investigations. In the future, multi-modality analysis will be conducted to evaluate the relationships between brain structural and functional networks. Furthermore, the biochemical examination of cerebrospinal fluid or blood collection from NT1 patients will be included to further exam the immunological mechanism and underlying pathogenesis of NT1.

Conclusions

In NT1 patients, topological alterations and disrupted WM structural connectome evidently impacted their clinical manifestations. In particular, the altered structural topology in frontal-parietal cortex connected with subcortical regions potentially shaped the sleep characteristics. Of note, the disrupted structure networks of cingulate cortex greatly affected cognition and sleep structures of NT1 patients.
