Role of psychosocial factors in muscle performance and response to outpatient supervised exercise-based physical therapy in individuals with chronic low back pain: a prospective cohort analysis
Authors: Ryan Lebens, Lara Havandjian, Armin Zavareh, Lissa Taitano, Connor Richards, Kamshad Raiszadeh, Bahar Shahidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe purpose of this study is to evaluate the relationship between psychosocial factors, muscle performance and treatment response in individuals undergoing an exercise-based physical therapy (EBPT) programme for chronic low back pain (CLBP).DesignA secondary analysis of a prospectively collected clinical registry involving participants with CLBP enrolled in an 8–10-week EBPT programme. Participants completed psychosocial questionnaires before starting EBPT for CLBP. Lumbar extensor muscle performance was assessed using an isokinetic dynamometer, which recorded absolute and age and sex adjusted torque. Differences in muscle performance (absolute and adjusted) were calculated and regressed against psychosocial factors and clinical outcomes.ResultsAbsolute and adjusted torque increased with treatment (33.33% and 82.84% respectively, p<0.01). On average, pain, disability and patient goal achievement improved with treatment (p<0.10). Improvements in muscle performance were associated with lower fear avoidance (ß=−0.29 to −0.31, p<0.04), whereas higher absolute baseline and final torque correlated positively with pain self-efficacy (ß=0.20, p=0.04; ß=0.27, p=0.02). No significant correlations were observed between changes in muscle performance and improvements in clinical outcomes, including pain, disability or functional status (all p>0.20).ConclusionIndividuals with CLBP demonstrated improved muscle performance and clinical outcomes after EBPT, though these improvements were independent of each other. Better absolute lumbar extensor muscle performance correlated with higher pain self-efficacy, and greater treatment response corresponded with lower fear-avoidance. These data suggest that pain coping strategies and addressing fear avoidance through educational and exposure-based interventions may be a target for modifying muscle performance in CLBP.Keywords: Back pain, REHABILITATION MEDICINE, Physical Therapy Modalities, Psychosocial Intervention

Introduction

Chronic low back pain (CLBP) is among the most common conditions faced today, with a prevalence as high as 577 million in 2017.1 Globally, it is the most common musculoskeletal problem, while also being the leading cause of activity limitation and work leave of absence.1 Given the high incidence, research on treatment optimisation remains imperative to reducing its global health burden.

Treatment recommendations for CLBP follow a consistent algorithm, starting first with pharmacological and educational care, along with exercise therapy. Surgical treatment is used occasionally, although surgical management is recommended only for patients with disabling CLBP for more than 1 year, per the American Pain Society.2 Exercise-based physical therapy (EBPT) is nearly always the first form of treatment, even when surgery may ultimately be necessary. EBPT has demonstrated efficacy in treating CLBP,3 and targeted strengthening of the lumbar paraspinal muscles has shown efficacy in reducing pain,4 5 likely due to reduced stress and strain on other pain-generating structures such as facet joints, discs and ligaments.

EBPT’s effectiveness depends on a patient’s ability to perform prescribed exercises. Exercise performance is influenced by muscle health, activation and capacity (eg, strength or endurance) on initiation of an exercise task, and exercise response represents changes over time in exercise performance and clinical symptoms, respectively. If individuals face physical, psychological or social barriers that hinder their ability to perform these tasks, it can adversely affect their physiological adaptation and clinical response to a treatment targeting these features. A significant body of research suggests that clinical responses to musculoskeletal rehabilitation across a variety of conditions are dependent on psychosocial factors, including anxiety, depression, kinesiophobia, pain self-efficacy and treatment beliefs.6,12 Similarly, studies have noted interactions between certain psychosocial factors and the magnitude of disability in individuals with CLBP.9 However, current literature lacks insight regarding the degree to which psychosocial factors can modulate muscle performance and response in individuals with CLBP. Without elucidating the relationship between psychosocial factors, muscle performance and clinical response, the benefit of EBPT may remain self-limiting.

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the cross-sectional relationship between psychosocial factors and lumbar extensor muscle performance in individuals with CLBP undergoing an EBPT programme. The secondary objective was to determine whether these relationships were associated with clinical outcomes, including changes in pain, disability and functional status following EBPT. Understanding these relationships will allow clinicians to provide more holistic and patient-specific treatment that may improve outcomes through optimising performance in exercise-based rehabilitation.

Materials and methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from a single centre between 14 May 2021 and 20 December 2024 and were eligible to enrol if they were prescribed an EBPT programme for their CLBP. Participants were included if they had symptoms for at least 3 months and were excluded if they had received prior surgical treatment for their back pain, were receiving treatment for non-lumbar body regions, were older than 85 years of age (due to safety concerns with high-intensity exercise programme) or did not attend an initial evaluation after receiving their prescription (figure 1). All participants provided informed consent under an approved Institutional Review Board protocol and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (figure 2).

Questionnaires

Prior to initiating the EBPT programme, participants completed a series of psychosocial questionnaires, including the Treatment Belief Questionnaire (TBQ), Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire (PSEQ), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK) and Pain Catastrophization Scale (PCS). The psychosocial questionnaires each assess a specific component of the participant’s psychological state. The TBQ assesses belief in the efficacy of exercise-based treatment to improve their LBP. TBQ has been proven to be an effective tool to assess an individual’s treatment beliefs, with higher scores indicating greater belief in the efficacy of exercise as a modality of treatment.13 The PSEQ is a 10-item questionnaire assessing the degree of confidence to carry out various tasks despite the participant’s pain state. Confidence is assessed on a scale of 0–6 for 10 tasks, with 6 being the most confidence and 0 being no confidence. Out of a maximum of 60, <20 is considered severe, 20–30 is considered moderate, 31–40 is considered mild and scores >40 are considered minimal impairment.14 PSEQ has excellent validity, reliability and responsiveness when used for patients with CLBP.15 The TSK represents fear of injury because of movement. TSK is a 17-item scale ranging from 17 to 68, with higher levels representing greater fear of movement. Scores of 37 or higher have been shown to be predictive of worse health outcomes in individuals with LBP.16 The PCS is commonly used to assess catastrophising thoughts related to pain and consists of three subscales: rumination, magnification and helplessness. PCS is a 13-question scale where participants indicate on a 0–4 point scale how frequently they experience various emotions or feelings when experiencing pain, with higher scores indicating greater pain catastrophising. Scores above 30 out of 52 are thought to represent clinically important levels of pain catastrophising.17 The PCS has demonstrated precision in assessing catastrophising thoughts in individuals with LBP.18 Depression and anxiety levels among participants were assessed through the Hostpital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Anxiety (HADSA) and Hosptial Anxiety and Depression Scale- Depression (HADSD). This questionnaire includes seven items related to either anxiety or depression, and ranges from 0 to 21. The higher the score, the higher the state of anxiety or depression. Scores from 8 to 10 are thought to represent mild symptoms, 11–15 are considered moderate and 16–21 are severe anxiety or depression.19 This questionnaire has also shown to be a valid tool for assessing anxiety and depression in individuals with LBP.20

In addition to psychosocial questionnaires, patient-reported clinical symptoms and functional status were collected. Pain severity (Visual Analogue Scale; VAS), LBP-related disability (Oswestry Disability Index; ODI) and patient-specific goals (Patient Specific Functional Scale; PSFS) were collected before and after treatment, and change scores were calculated for each clinical outcome, respectively (DeltaVAS, DeltaODI and DeltaPSFS). The VAS is a subjective measure representing magnitude of pain and is determined by the location of a handwritten mark on a 10 cm line, with higher scores indicating greater symptoms. Scores of <3.4 correspond to mild pain, 3.5–7.4 represent moderate pain and >7.5 represents severe pain.21 Studies have shown VAS to be a reliable and valid clinical rating of LBP.22 ODI is a 10-item questionnaire measuring perceived back pain-related disability and is a common outcome measure used in patients with LBP. Scores are calculated as a percentage out of 50 possible points, with percentages >40% indicating severe or crippling levels of disability. Percentages 20–40% are considered moderate disability and <20% is considered minimal disability.23 Studies have demonstrated repeated validity and reliability of ODI as a quantified measurement of disability in individuals with chronic LBP.24 Lastly, the PSFS assesses the degree of function for self-selected activities. Participants choose three important activities that are made difficult by their LBP and then rank the degree of difficulty of carrying out that task on an 11-point scale, with higher scores equating to a higher ability to perform the task. The PSFS has been shown to be a valid way to measure functional changes in individuals with LBP.25

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measures were lumbar extensor muscle performance variables, including baseline absolute torque, final absolute torque, adjusted torque and their respective changes following EBPT.

Secondary outcome measures included patient-reported clinical outcomes, pain severity (VAS), disability (ODI) and functional status (PSFS), along with their respective change scores following EBPT.

Spine-focused muscle performance assessment and EBPT programme

All participants initiated an 8–10 week supervised EBPT programme with a focus on machine-based lumbar extension resistance exercises aimed at increasing paraspinal muscle strength, as previously described in detail.26 On initiating the programme, lumbar extensor performance was measured using an isokinetic dynamometer (MedX Holdings, Cheyenne, Wyoming, USA). This device isolates the lumbar extensors using pelvic stabilisation and measures torque from a maximum voluntary contraction within a monitored patient-tolerated range of motion (absolute torque). Torque was measured and reported in foot-pounds (ft·lb); for reference, 1 n·m=1.356 ft·lb. It then provides a normalised output that compares an individual’s peak torque to age-matched and sex-matched normative values from pain-free individuals.27 This value represents the percent difference between the observed torque value and the expected age-adjusted and sex-adjusted normative value (adjusted torque). Differences in absolute and adjusted muscle performance were calculated between the post-treatment (final) and the pre-treatment (baseline) time points (delta absolute torque or delta adjusted torque). Because participant characteristics of the healthy pain-free individuals from which the adjusted torque population was normalised were unknown (beyond age and sex), we reported both the absolute performance values as well as the adjusted performance values.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons between baseline and final performance and clinical measurements were performed using paired Student t-tests. Changes in outcomes were additionally expressed as mean differences (delta values) between post-intervention and pre-intervention measurements.

Individual backward regression models were performed for each of the following outcomes: baseline performance, final performance and performance improvement for the absolute and adjusted measures. Demographic covariates (age, sex, height and weight) were included, and final variable selection was based on SPSS default backward elimination settings: predictors with p value <0.05 were retained, while those with p value >0.10 were iteratively removed. These final models were run separately using the previously stated psychosocial measures as predictors, and the model p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction. An a priori power analysis was performed to identify a sample size estimate for relationships between baseline muscle performance and neurophysiological, physical and psychosocial variables using an effect size of f2=0.26 based on preliminary data, alpha level of 0.05, power of 0.8 and k=13 predictors, which yielded an N size of 93 participants.

Results

7293 individuals who were prescribed EBPT for LBP were screened for possible inclusion from April 2020 through June 2024. Of those screened, 6.2% were excluded due to acute (<3 months) symptoms, 3.2% were excluded due to having a prior lumbar spine surgery, 9.7% were excluded due to being older than 85 years, 22.8% were excluded due to never attending an initial rehabilitation evaluation and 20.4% were excluded due to not being treated primarily for CLBP. Of the remaining 2823 who were deemed eligible on screening, 96% declined to participate in research. The remaining 109 participants were included in the study (figure 1). On average, participants were in their fourth decade of life. 40.2% were male and 59% were female. Baseline VAS and ODI scores revealed, on average, moderate pain and disability across the cohort. Psychosocial assessments revealed that the participants demonstrated generally high pain self-efficacy, low anxiety and depression and low levels of fear-avoidance and catastrophisation (table 1).

Secondary objective analyses evaluating associations between changes in muscle performance and clinical outcomes revealed weak, non-significant associations (r ranging from −0.18 to 0.12, p>0.2). These correlation analyses were distinct from the regression models described above, which evaluated the association between psychosocial factors and muscle performance outcomes. However, improvements in ODI were positively correlated with higher baseline TBQ exercise scores (r=0.31, p=0.01) and lower baseline HADSA and HADSD (r=−0.26, p=0.04; r=−0.27, p=0.03, respectively) scores. Additionally, improvements in pain (VAS) were correlated with lower PCS Rumination and PCS Helplessness scores (r=−0.29, p=0.01; r=−0.22, p=0.04, respectively). No other correlations were observed between clinical outcomes and psychosocial predictors (p>0.08) (figure 2).

Discussion

Clinical implications

This study provides novel data on relationships between psychosocial factors and spine muscle performance before initiating and in response to an EBPT programme. Our primary findings suggest that fear-avoidant behaviours are associated with attenuated improvements in muscle performance in response to treatment, regardless of absolute performance, whereas greater pain self-efficacy is associated with greater absolute performance. Our secondary analyses demonstrate that improvements in pain and disability are associated with positive treatment expectations, low anxiety and depression and low pain catastrophisation but are not associated with changes in muscle performance in response to EBPT. Although there is no prior literature evaluating the specific relationship between psychosocial factors and muscle performance in individuals with CLBP, our data are largely consistent with previous research relating psychosocial factors and symptom-based clinical outcomes.28 29 For instance, a 2018 meta-analysis by Alhowimel et al provides an overview of the psychosocial factors associated with change in pain and disability in individuals with CLBP, concluding that fear of movement, depression, pain self-efficacy and catastrophising are associated with clinical outcomes following physical therapy. However, this association is described as indirect, and the study calls for further research examining underlying mechanisms.29 Similarly, Varela et al found in a cross-sectional correlation study that there was a significant inverse relationship between pain self-efficacy and reported disability for patients with CLBP.28

Overall, these findings are important for psychosocially informed treatment of CLBP. Specifically, these results support the use of a more holistic treatment programme for CLBP, as previous research suggests that treatment modalities such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), yoga, mindfulness and education may positively impact fear avoidance and pain self-efficacy, thus improving clinical outcomes. The most studied of these interventions is CBT incorporating techniques like graded exposure, with many studies reaffirming its utility in the treatment of LBP and affecting pain self-efficacy and fear avoidance.30,33 A meta-analysis from 2022 by Yang et al investigated the effects of CBT on pain, disability, fear avoidance and pain self-efficacy in participants with CLBP and reported improved pain self-efficacy when compared with other non-CBT interventions. This study also found that fear avoidance could be reduced by CBT, although those results were less conclusive.31 Importantly, a systematic review of psychosocial interventions for CLBP by Vergeld et al identified 12 studies demonstrating significant improvements in fear avoidance for individuals with LBP who underwent CBT or psychoeducation.32

Other treatment modalities, such as yoga, mindfulness and education, have each been independently associated with improvements in pain self-efficacy for patients with LBP, with larger effects with combined treatments, particularly yoga combined with physical therapy.30 A randomised controlled trial by Turner et al in 2016 found that mindfulness-based stress reductions and CBT had overlapping effects in improving pain self-efficacy in patients with CLBP.33 Unsurprisingly, interdisciplinary treatments including CBT and psychosocial interventions combined with EBPT have shown promise. Davin et al directly compared the effectiveness of EBPT with an interdisciplinary pain programme that includes CBT, concluding that patients in the interdisciplinary programme demonstrated greater functional improvements compared with the group without.34 Many other studies have reaffirmed this, supporting the importance of psychosocially informed physical therapy offering heightened treatment efficacy for LBP.35 Overall, the established improvements in fear avoidance, pain self-efficacy and clinical outcomes through the incorporation of CBT and other psychosocial interventions support their use as effective treatments for CLBP and identify them as modifiable targets for improving clinical outcomes in CLBP.

Interestingly, our data were in contrast to previous literature suggesting relationships between clinical outcomes and performance of the lumbar extensor muscles or improvements in response to treatment.5 36 One explanation for the lack of relationship between performance and clinical measures may be that the baseline symptom severity and disability levels in our cohort were low, and the range of improvement in clinical variables was somewhat narrow. Although improvements in pain, disability and patient functional goals were statistically significant, their clinical significance is less clear. For example, the minimally important clinical difference (MCID) for ODI is the subject of ongoing debate, with changes ranging from 5 to 17 considered clinically relevant for LBP.37 38 We observed a mean ODI improvement of 6.20, placing it at the lower end of the MCID spectrum. Literature suggests the VAS MCID is a 2-point improvement.39 Finally, PSFS scores improved an average 1.62 points in our cohort, falling just short of the 2-point MCID for LBP.40 Given these narrow ranges of improvement, this relationship may be more evident in a more severely impacted population. Regardless, the mechanisms underlying these relationships require further exploration.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As previously mentioned, the narrow range of baseline pain and disability states among participants may limit the generalisability of our findings to broader LBP populations with more severe symptomology. This is of particular concern given that only 4% of the over 2000 individuals contacted and deemed eligible ultimately agreed to participate in this research study, and additional information on the population that declined is not available. This may have resulted in participation bias and may represent large scale research hesitancy in this population that is typically under-reported. Similarly, given the exploratory nature of the outcomes assessed, the small sample size may limit the power of the statistical analysis. However, our a priori power analysis suggests that we had sufficient sample size to evaluate relationships between our independent and dependent variables. Future studies on this topic may benefit from a more pragmatic design with psychosocial metrics integrated into the clinical workflow to better capture a broad range of representative data. A final limitation is that measurements of maximum muscle performance in participants with chronic pain are often confounded by symptom severity and may not reflect performance capacity, but rather pain tolerance. Indeed, baseline absolute torque and baseline pain (VAS) were negatively correlated (r=−0.23, p=0.02), suggesting this is a potential limitation of this study.

Conclusion

Our study found that greater pain self-efficacy was associated with better lumbar extensor muscle performance, whereas fear avoidance was associated with reduced improvement in lumbar extensor performance in response to an EBPT programme in individuals with CLBP. These data offer targetable psychosocial behaviours that may be addressed with psychosocially informed multimodal treatments such as CBT, specific patient education and graded exposure.
