Effect of a health belief model–based educational intervention on oral–dental health behaviors in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: an experimental study
Authors: Babak Pezeshki, Zahra Eskandari, Fatemeh Rezaei, Pooyan Afzali Harsini, Andishe Hamedi, Ali Khani Jeihooni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with type 2 diabetes mellitus are at increased risk for oral–dental complications, yet there is limited evidence from Iran on whether theory-based educational interventions can produce sustained improvements in oral–dental self-care behaviors in this population. This non-blinded experimental study was conducted in 2023–2024 among patients with type 2 diabetes registered at the Fasa Diabetes Center. A total of 220 participants were randomly allocated to an intervention group (n = 110) or a control group (n = 110). Sample size was calculated using the formula for comparing two independent means with a 95% confidence level and 80% power, with allowance for attrition. The intervention group received six Health Belief Model based educational sessions (50–55 min each) delivered through group discussions, question-and-answer sessions, practical demonstrations, and educational materials; the control group received routine care. Data were collected at baseline and six months post-intervention using a validated questionnaire assessing knowledge, Health Belief Model constructs, and oral–dental health behaviors. At six months, the intervention group demonstrated significant improvements compared with the control group in knowledge, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, self-efficacy, cues to action, and oral–dental health behaviors (all p < 0.05). These findings provide evidence that Health Belief Model-based education can improve oral–dental self-care behaviors in patients with type 2 diabetes and support using the Health Belief Model as a practical framework for designing integrated oral-dental health education programs within diabetes centers.Keywords: Knowledge, Educational intervention, Health belief model, Oral- dental health, Type 2 diabetesSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a metabolic disorder characterized by impaired regulation and utilization of glucose, resulting in chronic hyperglycemia1. It has emerged as a major global public health challenge and is often described as a silent epidemic of the present century2. The burden of type 2 diabetes mellitus is increasing worldwide, with a particularly rapid rise in emerging nations3. According to reports from 2016, approximately 422 million adults were living with diabetes globally4, In Iran, statistics released by the Iranian Diabetes Society indicate that nearly 6 million individuals (8.9% of the population) are affected by diabetes5, a figure projected to increase to 7 million by 20306. Diabetes leads to significant pathological changes across multiple body systems and is associated with acute and chronic complications, disability, substantial healthcare costs, and increased mortality7.

Among the complications of diabetes, oral–dental health problems are particularly prevalent, especially in patients with poor glycemic control. Numerous studies have shown that individuals with diabetes experience 2–4 times higher rates of chronic oral and dental disorders in terms of incidence, progression, and severity compared with non-diabetic individuals8. The risk of periodontitis is reported to be three times higher in patients with uncontrolled diabetes than in healthy individuals and periodontal disease tends to progress more rapidly and severely in this population9. In addition, poorly controlled diabetes increases susceptibility to opportunistic oral infections such as candidiasis and plays an important role in the development of xerostomia, which in turn contributes to dental caries, oral infections, and oral ulceration8.

Given these risks, acquiring adequate knowledge about susceptibility to periodontal disease, xerostomia, and preventive strategies for oral–dental disorders is as essential as effective diabetes management itself10. Research has demonstrated that the adoption of appropriate oral self-care practices is closely linked to increased oral–dental health knowledge11. Although awareness of correct behaviors does not always lead to behavior change, insufficient oral–dental health knowledge can increase exposure to oral health risk factors and result in neglect of oral self-care behaviors among diabetic patients12. Moreover, inaccurate beliefs regarding oral–dental health may promote harmful practices, and misconceptions can represent substantial barriers to the prevention and effective management of oral diseases, particularly in high-risk populations such as individuals with diabetes13.

Prevention is a practical and cost-effective approach to improving oral and dental health, and the World Health Organization emphasizes health education as a key strategy for promoting community health14. Many educational interventions are designed using behavioral change models, and evidence suggests that theory-based programs are generally more effective than non-theoretical approaches15. The Health Belief Model (HBM) is one of the most frequently used models in health promotion because it explains why individuals adopt or avoid recommended health behaviors based on perceived susceptibility to a condition, perceived severity of its consequences, perceived benefits of action, perceived barriers, self-efficacy, and cues to action16.

These constructs are highly relevant for oral–dental self-care in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Individuals with diabetes are more vulnerable to periodontal disease, dental caries, xerostomia, and delayed healing, yet preventive behaviors may be neglected due to low perceived risk, competing demands of diabetes management, limited access to dental services, or lack of confidence in performing oral self-care correctly. HBM-based education can directly address these issues by strengthening perceived susceptibility and severity, highlighting the benefits of preventive behaviors, reducing perceived barriers, and improving self-efficacy through skills-based training and practical guidance. Prior studies have reported that HBM-guided interventions can improve oral hygiene practices and reduce dental plaque by promoting consistent preventive behaviors17–19.

Although prior studies have shown that HBM–based education can improve oral–dental health behaviors, these studies largely focus on general or non-diabetic populations and do not account for the distinct oral-dental health risks, self-care barriers, and disease-management demands faced by patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Importantly, there is a lack of evidence examining whether HBM-based interventions can produce sustained improvements in oral–dental behaviors within diabetes care settings, where education is typically integrated with routine disease management. Moreover, no well-controlled experimental studies have specifically evaluated the effectiveness of HBM-guided oral-dental health education among patients with type 2 diabetes in Iran, where cultural norms, health literacy levels, and access to preventive dental services differ from those in high-income countries. Consequently, the existing literature does not provide sufficient evidence to guide the implementation of theory-based oral-dental health education within diabetes centers in this context. The present study was therefore conducted to fill this gap by rigorously assessing the long-term effectiveness of an HBM-based educational intervention on oral–dental health behaviors among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus in Iran.

Methods

Research design and participants

This quasi-experimental study with a parallel-group, pre–post design and a 6-month follow-up was conducted among adults with type 2 diabetes attending diabetes clinics in Shiraz, Iran, between August 2024 and March 2025. Although the study was not a fully randomized controlled trial, several methodological strategies were implemented to enhance internal validity, including baseline comparability assessment and statistical adjustment for potential confounders.

A six-month follow-up period was selected to allow sufficient time for changes in behavioral outcomes while acknowledging that clinical indices such as periodontal measures may require longer durations to demonstrate significant changes.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was calculated using the formula for comparing two independent means based on parameters derived from the study by Malekmahmoodi et al.20. Assuming a significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and a medium effect size of 0.5 according to Cohen’s criteria, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 126 participants. Considering possible attrition and feasibility, the final sample size was rounded to 120 participants (60 per group).

Sampling and participant flow

Eligibility screening was conducted before enrollment. Inclusion criteria included literacy, age between 30 and 50 years, having an active medical record, and absence of major physical or mental conditions affecting participation or oral health behaviors.

After screening and obtaining informed consent, 245 eligible patients entered the study. During baseline assessment, 25 participants were excluded due to withdrawal of consent, incomplete baseline questionnaires, or unavailability for follow-up. Ultimately, 220 participants completed the baseline assessment and were allocated to the intervention group (n = 110) and the control group (n = 110). The final sample size exceeded the minimum required number, thereby increasing statistical precision and study power

Allocation and control of confounding

Participants were allocated to study groups using a computer-generated random sequence with a 1:1 allocation ratio by an independent researcher not involved in recruitment or data collection. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The participant recruitment, randomization, and allocation process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The HBM

Health Belief Model (HBM).

The intervention was guided by the Health Belief Model (HBM), a widely used theoretical framework for explaining and predicting health-related behaviors. The HBM includes six key constructs: perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, perceived self-efficacy, and cues to action21. Perceived susceptibility and perceived severity reflect individuals’ beliefs about their risk of developing oral–dental diseases and the seriousness of potential consequences. Perceived benefits and perceived barriers represent individuals’ evaluation of the effectiveness of preventive behaviors and the obstacles to performing them. Self-efficacy refers to confidence in one’s ability to perform oral health behaviors, while cues to action are internal or external triggers that prompt behavior change.

These constructs were used both to design the intervention content and to assess changes in psychosocial determinants of oral–dental health behaviors.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured, validated questionnaire adapted from previous studies22,23. The instrument consisted of three sections:

Demographic and clinical characteristics (e.g., age, sex, education, occupation, marital status, smoking status, duration of diabetes, treatment type, HbA1c, and family history).

HBM constructs and knowledge

Knowledge: 9 dichotomous items (score range: 0–9).

Perceived susceptibility: 7 items (score range: 7–35).

Perceived severity: 10 items (score range: 10–50).

Perceived benefits: 8 items (score range: 8–40).

Perceived barriers: 7 items (score range: 7–35).

Self-efficacy: 11 items (score range: 11–55).

Cues to action: 9 items (score range: 9–45).

All items (except knowledge) were rated on a 5-point Likert scale.

Oral–dental health behaviors

Assessed using 10 items rated on a 5-point frequency scale (score range: 0–40), with higher scores indicating better self-care practices.

Validity of the tool

Face validity of the questionnaire was assessed by administering it to 40 patients with type 2 diabetes who had similar demographic, economic, and social characteristics to the study population. Their feedback was used to evaluate item clarity, relevance, and comprehensibility. Content validity was examined by an expert panel consisting of nine specialists in health promotion and education, one adult endocrinologist, one dentist, and one epidemiologist. Content validity was quantified using the Lawshe method, and items with a content validity ratio (CVR) of 0.78 or higher were considered essential and retained for further analysis24. The internal consistency reliability of the questionnaire was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The overall reliability of the instrument was 0.88, indicating good internal consistency. Cronbach’s alpha values for individual HBM constructs were also acceptable, with coefficients of 0.86 for self-efficacy, 0.85 for perceived susceptibility, 0.80 for perceived severity, 0.82 for perceived benefits, 0.83 for perceived barriers, and 0.81 for cues to action.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Fasa Diabetes Center and Fasa University of Medical Sciences (ethical code: IR.FUMS.REC.1398.152). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality of the collected data was strictly maintained. The validated questionnaire was completed by both the intervention and control groups before the intervention.

Protocol

Participants in the intervention group were organized into small groups of 25–30 individuals. The educational intervention consisted of six 55-minute sessions conducted twice weekly. All sessions were delivered by the research team, including a health education specialist and a dentist, who were trained in the HBM framework. The same educators delivered the intervention across all groups to ensure consistency in content and delivery.

The educational content was developed based on a review of relevant scientific literature, national diabetes care guidelines, and oral–dental health promotion resources. The materials focused on diabetes and its effects on oral and dental structures, oral–dental health risk factors associated with diabetes, prevention of dental caries and periodontal disease, proper oral hygiene practices, and the advantages and limitations of dental floss use. The content was reviewed and validated by a panel of experts consisting of a dentist, an endocrinologist, and a health education specialist prior to implementation.

The intervention was explicitly structured around the core constructs of the HBM. Perceived susceptibility was addressed by providing information on the increased risk of periodontal disease, xerostomia, and oral infections among patients with type 2 diabetes. Perceived severity was emphasized through discussions of the potential consequences of poor oral health, including its impact on glycemic control and diabetes-related complications. Perceived benefits were reinforced by explaining how regular tooth brushing, flossing, and dental visits can prevent oral complications and improve overall health. Perceived barriers were explored through group discussions in which participants identified personal, financial, or practical obstacles to oral self-care, and educators facilitated problem-solving strategies tailored to individual circumstances. Self-efficacy was strengthened through practical demonstrations of oral hygiene techniques, supervised practice, and positive feedback to build participants’ confidence in performing oral self-care behaviors correctly. Cues to action were provided through reminders, educational pamphlets, follow-up sessions, and ongoing communication via a WhatsApp group.

Educational methods included lectures, question-and-answer sessions, group discussions, practical demonstrations, PowerPoint presentations, and educational videos. To further enhance perceived susceptibility and self-efficacy, discussions addressed the importance of preventing dental caries, the increased vulnerability of individuals with diabetes to oral–dental health problems, and participants’ confidence in performing oral self-care behaviors. Participants also shared personal challenges related to oral–dental health practices, and tailored solutions were discussed based on their individual circumstances.

In one session, a patient with type 2 diabetes and oral–dental health complications was invited to share personal experiences, reinforcing perceived severity and cues to action. Additionally, a family member, a physician, and staff from the Diabetes Center participated to strengthen social support and external cues to action. At the end of the intervention, an educational pamphlet was distributed to participants, and a WhatsApp group was created to facilitate information sharing and ongoing engagement. Two follow-up sessions were conducted at two and four months after the intervention to reinforce key messages and maintain motivation.

Both the intervention and control groups completed the study questionnaire six months after the intervention. After data collection was completed, all educational materials were provided to the control group.

The control group received routine care provided by the Diabetes Center during the study period and did not participate in any of the educational sessions or receive study-specific materials before the six-month follow-up assessment. To ensure ethical comparability, after completion of the post-intervention data collection, the educational materials used in the intervention (including the pamphlets and supporting content) were provided to the control group.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22. Descriptive statistics, including mean and standard deviation, were used to summarize continuous variables, while frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables. The chi-square test was applied to compare categorical variables between the intervention and control groups. Independent t-tests were used to compare mean scores of continuous variables between groups at baseline and at the six-month follow-up, and paired t-tests were employed to assess within-group changes over time. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

220 people with diabetes participated in this study. The experimental group’s mean age was 48.14 ± 8.22 years, whereas the control group’s mean age was 47.26 ± 8.16 years (P = 0.224). Moreover, male patients made up 52.73% of the control group and 56.36% of the experimental group. In addition, 47.27% of patients in the control group and 43.64% of patients in the experimental group were female. The experimental and control groups had mean durations of diabetes of 14.33 ± 3.75 and 13.92 ± 3.81 years, respectively (P = 0.227), and the groups had mean HbA1C levels of 8.66 ± 1.80 and 8.74 ± 1.64, respectively. Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrated that an educational intervention grounded in the HBM significantly improved oral–dental health behaviors among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Improvements were also observed across key HBM constructs, indicating that the intervention effectively influenced patients’ perceptions, motivation, and self-care practices related to oral-dental health.

The HBM served as a practical and structured framework for designing and delivering the educational program. During the intervention, interactive educational sessions were developed to target the main HBM components, including perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy. Participants received information about the risks and complications of poor oral–dental health in the context of diabetes, were trained in proper oral hygiene practices, and engaged in discussions aimed at reducing perceived barriers and strengthening self-efficacy, consistent with the HBM framework16.

Poor oral hygiene is common among patients with diabetes and may contribute to worsening glycemic control and an increased risk of vascular and inflammatory complications. Chronic oral inflammation can exacerbate systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and the progression of atherosclerosis, while periodontal disease has been associated with impaired glycemic control and increased diabetes-related complications. Evidence suggests that oral-dental health education and structured oral care interventions can improve health outcomes in individuals with diabetes25.

The findings of the present study indicate that, following the intervention, the experimental group achieved significantly higher mean scores in knowledge related to oral–dental healthcare behaviors compared with the control group. This improvement may be attributed to the use of multiple educational strategies, including educational pamphlets, structured workshops, educational videos, and the establishment of a WhatsApp group, which facilitated continuous learning and reinforcement of key messages. Although increased knowledge alone does not necessarily result in behavior change, insufficient oral-dental health knowledge is known to increase oral–dental risk factors and may lead patients to neglect essential self-care behaviors26. Likewise, in the studies of Smith et al.27, Mohammadi Zeidi28, Poudel et al.29 Kandula, et al.30, and Askari et al.31, the education increased the knowledge of diabetic patients.

Beyond knowledge, the present study demonstrated improvements in clinical periodontal indicators, including plaque index, gingival index, and clinical attachment level, following the educational intervention. These results are particularly important given the well-established bidirectional relationship between periodontal health and glycemic control in individuals with diabetes. Poor periodontal status may worsen glycemic control, while improvements in oral-dental health can contribute to better metabolic outcomes. The current findings align with those of lifestyle modification and dental care programs that reported concurrent improvements in periodontal parameters and glycemic control after educational and behavioral interventions25. Similarly, the observed increases in oral-dental health behaviors such as regular tooth brushing and flossing are consistent with prior studies showing that targeted oral-dental health education can positively influence self-care practices among diabetic patients25. These parallels strengthen the evidence that educational interventions can produce meaningful behavioral and clinical benefits when integrated into diabetes care.

The present study also revealed a significant increase in perceived self-efficacy among participants in the experimental group six months after the intervention, whereas no significant change was observed in the control group. Within the Health Belief Model framework, self-efficacy is a pivotal determinant of behavior change because it directly influences individuals’ ability to translate knowledge and motivation into sustained action. The pronounced improvement in self-efficacy observed in this study may be attributed to the intervention’s strong emphasis on skill-based and experiential learning components, including practical demonstrations of oral hygiene techniques, supervised practice, problem-solving discussions, and individualized feedback. These strategies enabled participants to gain hands-on experience and confidence in performing oral–dental self-care behaviors correctly. In addition, repeated reinforcement through follow-up sessions, peer interaction, and social support further strengthened participants’ confidence in their ability to maintain these behaviors over time. Given that oral–dental self-care behaviors are habitual and require ongoing effort, directly targeting self-efficacy appears to have been one of the most effective components of the intervention32. Consistent with previous research, interventions that actively enhance self-efficacy tend to produce more durable improvements in oral–dental health behaviors than information-based approaches alone33. These findings underscore the importance of prioritizing self-efficacy–enhancing strategies when designing HBM-based educational programs for oral–dental health promotion.

In addition, this study found that perceived benefits significantly increased and perceived barriers significantly decreased in the experimental group six months after the intervention. According to behavioral theory, the likelihood of adopting a health behavior is influenced by the balance between perceived benefits and perceived barriers34. By providing tailored oral–dental health information, facilitating access to dental professionals, sharing patient experiences, delivering educational booklets, and sending motivational messages, the intervention helped participants overcome perceived obstacles and strengthen facilitating factors. Consistent with previous studies, perceived barriers emerged as one of the strongest predictors of oral–dental health behaviors, as barriers that outweigh perceived benefits can substantially reduce the likelihood of action35. These findings emphasize the necessity of designing educational interventions that actively reduce practical and psychological barriers to oral self-care.

The experimental group also demonstrated a higher mean score for cues to action following the intervention. Both internal and external cues to action play an essential role in initiating and sustaining oral–dental healthcare behaviors. Internal cues reflect personal motivation, while external cues involve social approval, professional advice, and environmental support36. In the present study, external cues were reinforced through the involvement of physicians, dentists, diabetes center staff, and family members, as well as through group discussions and follow-up communications. This approach aligns with evidence suggesting that supportive social environments and reinforcement of positive norms can enhance the effectiveness of educational interventions, particularly among individuals with chronic conditions such as diabetes.

Despite these positive findings, potential confounding factors should be considered when interpreting the results. Lifestyle characteristics such as dietary habits, physical activity levels, smoking status, and adherence to diabetes management may have influenced oral–dental health behaviors independently of the intervention. In addition, baseline oral-dental health status and the presence of diabetes-related comorbidities could have affected participants’ ability to adopt and maintain recommended self-care practices. Although random allocation was used to reduce baseline differences between groups, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, particularly as these variables were not objectively measured or controlled for in the analysis. Future studies should account for these factors through baseline clinical assessments and multivariable analyses to better isolate the independent effect of HBM-based educational interventions on oral–dental health outcomes.

Taken together, the findings of this study extend existing evidence by demonstrating that an HBM-based educational intervention can effectively improve knowledge, psychosocial determinants, self-care behaviors, and periodontal health outcomes among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Compared with previous studies conducted primarily in children, adolescents, or non-diabetic populations, the present study contributes novel evidence specific to adults with diabetes, highlighting the applicability and value of theory-driven oral–dental health education within diabetes care settings.

Strengths and limitations

The present study has important limitations that may substantially affect interpretation of the findings. The primary limitation is the reliance on self-reported measures to assess oral–dental health behaviors, which are inherently vulnerable to recall error and social desirability bias and may overestimate true behavior change. Therefore, future studies should incorporate objective oral-dental health outcomes—such as Plaque Index (PI) and Gingival Bleeding Index (BI)—to verify whether reported behavioral improvements translate into measurable clinical changes. A second major limitation is the absence of post-intervention clinical outcomes, including HbA1c and standardized periodontal assessments. Not measuring these outcomes is a significant weakness because it prevents determining whether improved behaviors were accompanied by better glycemic control and actual periodontal improvement, thereby limiting the strength and validity of conclusions about health impact. Finally, the follow-up period of six months is relatively short and may be insufficient to evaluate the long-term maintenance and sustainability of oral–dental behavior change. Longer follow-up periods with repeated objective assessments are needed to confirm durability of effects.

Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths. It employed a theory-driven, non-invasive educational intervention based on the Health Belief Model, which demonstrated effectiveness in improving oral–dental health behaviors among patients with type 2 diabetes. The structured and scalable nature of this framework supports its integration into routine diabetes care and suggests potential applicability to other chronic metabolic conditions.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that a HBM–based educational intervention can meaningfully improve oral–dental self-care behaviors among patients with type 2 diabetes, with sustained gains observed six months after the program. The findings underscore the practical value of using the HBM as a structured, theory-driven framework for designing and implementing integrated oral-dental health education within diabetes centers. Accordingly, incorporating HBM-guided educational programs into routine diabetes care, supported by interprofessional collaboration (physicians, dentists, nurses) and family involvement, and reinforced through accessible educational materials and media—may strengthen preventive oral-dental health practices and contribute to reducing diabetes-related oral complications.
