Interpretable machine learning for predicting one-year prognosis in acute ischemic stroke
Authors: Xing-Yu Zhu, Tian-Tian Jiang, Yan-Wei Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIschemic stroke is the most prevalent type of stroke worldwide, accounting for approximately 85% of all stroke cases and resulting in the deaths of about 6.4 million people annually. Among stroke survivors, more than 60% experience varying degrees of functional impairment within three months following the stroke. Therefore, early identification and prediction of the risk of adverse outcomes after stroke are crucial. This study seeks to create an intuitive and clinically relevant machine learning model to predict the risk of adverse prognostic outcomes within one year in patients suffering from acute cerebral infarction.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed using medical data from 888 patients at the Air Force Specialty Medical Center of the People’s Liberation Army of China to construct the model and evaluate its performance. Following screening, 771 patients were included and allocated into training and validation datasets in a 7:3 ratio. Patients were classified into poor and favorable prognostic groups based on the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores. The SHAP analysis method was employed to scrutinize the marginal effects of each feature on model outputs, delivering a nuanced analysis of the model’s structure from a global and local perspective. Furthermore, the LIME interpretation technique was utilized for an exhaustive dissection of the model’s local attributes.ResultsIn this study, the Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) algorithm was employed to identify 14 key variables, including D-dimer, HDL, and PNI, for constructing 11 machine learning models. Comparative analysis revealed that the Extreme Gradient Boosting model demonstrated optimal predictive performance, with its AUC value reaching 0.766 and accuracy as high as 0.835. Utilizing SHAP analysis, the study ranked the global importance of the 14 key feature variables, including D-Dimer. Through LIME analysis, the respective weights of different feature ranges within the predictive model were uncovered.ConclusionThe interpretable machine learning predictive model developed in this study exhibited commendable accuracy in predicting adverse outcomes within one year for patients with acute cerebral infarction. Introduction

Ischemic stroke is a clinical syndrome caused by various factors leading to cerebrovascular obstruction, inducing local cerebral tissue hypoxic necrosis and ischemia, resulting in corresponding neurological deficits [1]. It is a leading cause of disability and cognitive impairment, accounting for 5.2% of global deaths [2], imposing a significant burden on society [3]. Consequently, reducing the incidence, mortality, and disability rates of stroke has become a central objective in the medical field. Despite advancements in medical technology, stroke remains the third leading cause of death, following cardiovascular diseases and cancer [4]. Post-stroke, inflammatory factors in the damaged brain tissue are activated, exacerbating neural injury and leading to poor prognosis, with mortality and disability rates remaining high within one year [5]. Therefore, the development of convenient and rapid prognostic prediction tools to identify high-risk patients is of paramount importance.

Current traditional methods for predicting stroke prognosis primarily include risk-scoring systems and biomarker-based models [6, 7]. However, these approaches generally exhibit several limitations, such as inconsistent accuracy across diverse populations. This inconsistency arises from these models being typically constructed based on limited clinical features and demographic data, which fail to adequately capture individual variability. Although machine learning (ML) offers new directions for improving predictive accuracy, its application still faces significant challenges [8]. High-performance ML models, such as deep learning and boosting algorithms, are often viewed as “black boxes,” with decision-making processes lacking intuitive interpretability [9]. This opacity results in clinical practitioners and patients finding it difficult to comprehend the outcomes provided by the models, thereby leading to lower trust levels in practical clinical decision-making. This concern is particularly crucial, as in the medical domain, model interpretability may be more vital than accuracy, given that both physicians and patients need to clearly understand the decision-making process.

In dealing with clinical data, sample imbalance, and insufficient overall sample size also limit the effectiveness of existing predictive methods [10]. Various studies have shown that certain predictive models fail to be adequately trained or validated on small datasets, leading to overfitting or poor generalization capabilities. In the context of highly complex and high-dimensional clinical data, traditional feature selection methods may be inadequate, as they cannot fully capture the intricate interactions among different variables [11]. Although ML models possess unique advantages in handling high-dimensional data, overly complex models may overlook key clinical features or struggle to balance computational cost and interpretability [9]. This affects the practicality of models in actual applications. In summary, while existing predictive methods offer some assistance in stroke prognosis prediction, they continue to face challenges in terms of accuracy, interpretability, and applicability. Thus, developing an ML model that integrates interpretability with accuracy could help bridge the shortcomings of current methods, advancing the precision prediction of ischemic stroke prognosis.

This study introduces two advanced model interpretation techniques: SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), to address the shortcomings of traditional ML models in terms of interpretability. SHAP is a post-hoc explanation method based on game theory, employed to quantify the contribution of features to predictive outcomes. By calculating the marginal contribution of each feature within various feature combinations, SHAP provides comprehensive interpretative capabilities, allowing analysis of the model’s predictive mechanisms from both aggregate and individual perspectives [12, 13]. This approach offers researchers deep insights into how the model makes decisions and helps identify which features are most significant in model prediction [14]. LIME explains specific predictions by generating local linear models. It approximates individual predictions of any complex model, enabling users to understand the model’s local behavior. The advantage of LIME lies in its flexibility to adapt to various model types, offering straightforward and intuitive explanations for machine learning model decisions [15, 16]. By combining SHAP and LIME, we can analyze and validate ML model outputs at different levels, supporting the transparency of model decisions and laying the foundation for widespread application in diverse clinical scenarios. These interpretative techniques are poised to enhance the trust and adoption rate of models in medical decision-making, promoting their effective integration into practical clinical applications.

This study aims to develop an innovative interpretable machine learning model to enhance the predictive accuracy and interpretability of stroke prognosis. We selected XGBoost as the primary modeling algorithm, leveraging its exceptional performance in handling nonlinear relationships and improving predictive precision. Additionally, the study incorporates SHAP and LIME techniques to enhance the transparency of the model’s decision-making process and provide new perspectives to support medical applications, thereby more effectively aiding clinical decision-making. Through this exploratory research, we hope to promote the effective application of the model in clinical practice, better serving the needs of healthcare professionals and patients.

Methods

This retrospective observational study, conducted at the Air Force Specialty Medical Center of the People’s Liberation Army, included 888 patients who were consecutively admitted to the neurology department with a diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) from January 2021 to October 2023 (Fig. 1, Fig. 2 Abstract Map). Based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, 771 patients were ultimately included. These patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 535) and a validation set (n = 236) in a 7:3 ratio. Patients were categorized into a poor prognosis group (mRS ≥ 2) and a good prognosis group (mRS < 2) according to the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score [17]. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) complete medical records; (3) meeting the diagnostic criteria specified in the “Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke 2018” [18]. The exclusion criteria were: (1) lack of laboratory data within 24 h of admission; (2) absence of mRS data one-year post-hospitalization; (3) coexistence of neurological disorders such as intracranial malignant tumors, acute brain trauma, or dementia; (4) related hematological diseases, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; (5) severe cardiopulmonary insufficiency; (6) history of severe disability or stroke sequelae interfering with the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scoring [19]; (7) pre-existing swallowing difficulties and long-term nasogastric tube dependence; (8) pre-stroke mRS ≥ 2.

In the present study, a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of ≥ 2 was adopted as the threshold for defining unfavourable functional outcome. Although prior literature has employed alternative cut-offs of mRS ≥ 3 or mRS ≥ 4, applying these higher thresholds within the current study population would have substantially reduced the number of positive outcome events, compromising the stability of model training and estimation. An mRS score of ≥ 2 corresponds to any degree of functional dependency and carries direct clinical relevance in the context of stroke management, where restoration of functional independence is the primary therapeutic goal. Furthermore, this binary classification aligns with the clinical decision logic underpinning the initiation of intensified follow-up or rehabilitation interventions. It should be noted that the intended scope of application of the present model differs from that of studies employing higher mRS thresholds; accordingly, cross-study performance comparisons should account for differences in outcome definition to ensure interpretive validity.

Missing data handling

Missing clinical data are ubiquitous in retrospective studies. Restricting analyses to complete cases not only reduces the effective sample size but may also introduce selection bias when data are not missing completely at random, thereby compromising the validity of study conclusions [20]. In the present study, we systematically evaluated missingness across 40 clinical variables in all 771 patients (Supplementary Table 1). Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) had the highest proportion of missing values (29.18%), followed by high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP; 19.84%) and body weight (13.36%). Coagulation-related parameters (prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, international normalised ratio, and fibrinogen) were each missing in 2.72% of cases, lipid panel variables in 2.08%, and all remaining biochemical indices in fewer than 2% of cases. Demographic characteristics, medical history, and the primary outcome variable (modified Rankin Scale score) were complete for all patients.

Regarding the plausibility of the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption, the relatively elevated missingness rates for HbA1c and hsCRP were adjudicated through clinical chart review, which revealed that omission of these assays occurred predominantly among patients presenting with milder neurological deficits for whom attending clinicians did not routinely order these investigations [21, 22]. The propensity for missingness was therefore associated with observed covariates—specifically age, admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, and comorbidity burden—rather than with the unobserved values of the variables themselves, consistent with the MAR assumption. Under the MAR framework, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) leverages the information contained in all observed variables to generate principled estimates of missing values while formally propagating imputation uncertainty, affording superior statistical efficiency and reduced bias compared with complete-case analysis or single imputation.

Accordingly, multiple imputation in the present study adhered strictly to a data-partitioning protocol to prevent information leakage. Imputation models were fitted exclusively within the training set (n = 535); that is, all parameters of the chained-equation procedure—including donor pools for predictive mean matching and coefficients for logistic regression imputation—were estimated solely from training-set observations. Missing values in the validation set (n = 236) were subsequently imputed using the parameters derived from the training set, with validation-set data contributing to no stage of imputation model estimation. Analyses were performed using the mice package in R (version 4.2.3). Five imputed datasets were generated (m = 5); predictive mean matching was applied to continuous variables and logistic regression to categorical variables. Each imputed dataset was analysed independently, and parameter estimates were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. This workflow preserved the independence of validation-set evaluation and ensured that missing-data handling introduced no optimistic bias attributable to distributional information derived from the validation set.

Statistical analysis

Comprehensive statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3. In the preliminary phase of data analysis, we conducted normality tests. For continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, we described them using means and standard deviations (𝑥̅±𝑠) and compared intergroup parameter values using independent samples t-tests. For continuous variables that did not meet the criteria for normality, we presented them with medians (M (Q1, Q3)) and employed the Mann-Whitney U test for comparative analysis. Categorical variables were presented using percentages, and differences were explored using the Chi-square (χ²) test. This study utilized a systematic hyperparameter optimization strategy to define the search space tailored to the clinical data characteristics for the XGBoost algorithm. The hyperparameter ranges included: learning rate (η) [0.005, 0.2], balancing the exploration-exploitation tradeoff; maximum depth [3, 12], controlling model complexity; row subsampling ratio [0.6, 0.95], enhancing robustness to missing data; column subsampling ratio [0.7, 1.0], reducing feature collinearity; minimum child weight [1, 10], preserving minority subgroups; and Gamma [0, 0.5], regulating regularization strength. The parameter optimization was conducted using Bayesian optimization across 80 iterations integrated with three-fold nested cross-validation. An early stopping mechanism (patience value = 50 rounds) and a class-weighted objective function (scale_pos_weight = ratio of negative to positive samples) were employed. To address the temporal dependency inherent in clinical data, a modified time-series cross-validation protocol was designed. Samples were ordered by admission date, and data splitting was implemented through five incremental temporal splits, ensuring the test set always chronologically follows the training set. Class proportions were maintained stable (± 5% deviation), with each test fold containing at least 20 events. Additionally, an independent temporal validation set comprising the most recent 20% of samples was created. The evaluation framework included time-dependent AUC-ROC (primary metric) as the main measure, along with balanced accuracy and F1-score (secondary metrics). Stability across folds was assessed using inter-fold metric variability analysis.

Development and validation of the predictive model

Model development and validation in the present study adhered to a rigorous data-partitioning framework, implemented sequentially across three hierarchical stages.

The feature set comprised laboratory and clinical indices routinely collected at hospital admission. Variables including baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, stroke aetiology (TOAST classification), infarct volume, reperfusion therapy status, and onset-to-treatment time were not incorporated into the feature set. Documentation of these variables was inconsistent in the retrospective dataset, with standardised volumetric measurement of infarct size being particularly incomplete; their systematic inclusion would have introduced substantial structured missingness and selection bias. Methodologically, the present model was designed as a rapid risk-stratification tool derived exclusively from routinely available biochemical and clinical parameters obtainable at early admission, with the aim of investigating whether a composite index reflecting inflammatory, coagulation, and nutritional status retains independent prognostic value for one-year functional outcomes without recourse to neuroimaging quantification or specialist neurological assessment. The clinical niche of this model is therefore complementary to, rather than a substitute for, comprehensive prognostic frameworks integrating neuroimaging and standardised neurological scales. The absence of the aforementioned variables constitutes an important contextual factor limiting the ceiling of model discriminative performance and is discussed further in the Limitations section.

Data partitioning

Data from all 771 patients were stratified by functional outcome and randomly allocated in a 7:3 ratio to a training set (n = 535) and a validation set (n = 236). The validation set remained entirely withheld throughout feature selection and model training and was reserved solely for independent performance evaluation at the conclusion of the study, thereby precluding optimistic bias in performance estimation.

Feature selection

Recursive feature elimination (RFE) was executed independently within the training set using a cross-validation fold assignment distinct from that employed in the subsequent hyperparameter optimisation stage. Logistic regression served as the base estimator, and ten-fold cross-validation within the training set was used to assess classification accuracy across candidate feature subset sizes (Table 2). Accuracy first reached a plateau at 14 features (0.7981); although identical accuracy values were observed at 18 and 42 features, neither configuration yielded a meaningful reduction in standard deviation. In accordance with the principle of minimum sufficiency, 14 features were retained as the final selection to mitigate overfitting risk and preserve clinical interpretability.

Model training and hyperparameter optimisation

Eleven machine learning models were independently trained on the training set using the 14 selected features. Hyperparameter optimisation for each model was conducted via grid search combined with ten-fold cross-validation performed within the training set, using a fold assignment entirely independent of that used during feature selection. These two optimisation steps were therefore non-nested and mutually isolated, preventing the cross-stage accumulation of selection bias. To address class imbalance in the training set (favourable outcome: n = 429; unfavourable outcome: n = 106; ratio approximately 4:1), the scale_pos_weight parameter was applied in XGBoost for sample-weight correction, and class-weight adjustments were incorporated into the hyperparameter search for all remaining models.

Performance evaluation

Following determination of optimal hyperparameters, each model generated a single set of predictions on the validation set. Given that the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) may overestimate minority-class detection capability under conditions of class imbalance, the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC) was additionally reported as a more informative measure of the model’s capacity to identify patients with unfavourable outcomes. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and overall accuracy were systematically calculated, and confusion matrices were generated to provide transparent characterisation of minority-class prediction performance. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed for the final optimal model to quantify net clinical benefit across a range of decision thresholds [23].

Machine learning interpretability tools

SHAP, as an ex-post model interpretation method, is widely utilized as a general tool for assessing feature importance in machine learning models [12, 13]. Its primary essence lies in quantifying the marginal effects of features on the model’s final output, providing in-depth analysis from both global and individual perspectives for ‘black-box models [14].’ SHAP constructs an additive explanation framework where all features are assigned the role of ‘contributors.’ The sophistication of SHAP values lies in their ability to precisely reveal each predictor’s positive or negative contribution to the target variable [14]. LIME is an algorithm that can use an interpretable model to approximate any classifier or regressor locally, faithfully explaining its predictive outcomes. By training a local surrogate model, LIME offers explanations for individual samples. For elucidating black-box models, we randomly select instance samples, disturb them within their vicinity to generate new data points, and obtain the black-box model’s predictions for these new data. Subsequently, we train an interpretable model with these new data, achieving an accurate local approximation of the black-box model. To conduct an in-depth analysis of our risk prediction model, we leveraged the robust capabilities of SHAP and LIME to meticulously explore the contribution strength of different clinical variables. For each prediction sample, the model generates a corresponding prediction value, while SHAP values accurately reflect the specific numerical contribution allocated to each feature within that sample. LIME further provides insights into the contribution power of feature variable ranges within the predictive model.

Results

The study ultimately enrolled 771 patients, who were randomly allocated in a 7:3 ratio to a training set (n = 535) and a validation set (n = 236). Patients were classified as having a favourable outcome (mRS score < 2) or an unfavourable outcome (mRS score ≥ 2) based on their modified Rankin Scale score at one year. Baseline characteristics of the training and validation sets are presented in Supplementary Table 2. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two sets with respect to demographic characteristics, comorbidity profile, haematological indices, or the primary predictive variables of interest in this study—including D-dimer, systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), haemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet score (HALP), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP)—with all comparisons yielding p values exceeding 0.05. A borderline difference was noted for fibrinogen alone (p = 0.048). Collectively, these findings indicate that random allocation yielded well-balanced baseline distributions across the two sets, supporting the use of the validation set as an unbiased, independent benchmark for evaluating model generalisation. Baseline characteristics of patients with favourable and unfavourable outcomes within the training set are presented in Table 1. To enhance the predictive accuracy of poor prognostic events occurring within one year in AIS patients, this study employed the Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) algorithm, carefully selecting 14 significant feature indicators (Table 2; Fig. 3) that are deemed core elements for model construction. When examining models with 14, 18, and 42 feature variables, an accuracy value of 0.7981 was observed, leading to the decision to select 14 feature variables to prevent model overfitting. Specifically, these 14 feature variables include: D-dimer, HDL, uric acid, Age, total cholesterol, SII, HALP, LDL, fibrinogen, hsCRP, Fasting glucose, ALT, AST, and PNI. From the data presented in Table 1, it is evident that patients with elevated levels of hs-CRP, Age, D-dimer, fibrinogen, Fasting glucose, and SII face a significantly increased risk of poor prognosis. Conversely, lower levels of PNI and HALP are associated with a heightened risk of adverse outcomes in AIS patients.

Model performance and interpretability

Systematic evaluation of eleven machine learning models was conducted on the validation set; detailed performance metrics for each model are presented in Table 3; Fig. 4, and Supplementary Fig. 1. In terms of AUROC, XGBoost achieved the highest discriminative performance (AUROC = 0.766, 95% CI 0.690–0.843), followed by Random Forest (0.741) and Gaussian Process (0.736). Given the pronounced class imbalance in the training set (approximately 4:1), PR-AUC was reported as a complementary evaluation metric. Precision-recall curve analysis on the validation set similarly identified XGBoost as the top-performing model (PR-AUC = 0.421, approximately 2.1-fold above the random baseline), with Random Forest (0.371) and gradient boosting machine (GBM; 0.352) ranking second and third, respectively.

It should be noted that the sensitivity of XGBoost on the validation set was 0.21 (95% CI 0.10–0.36), indicating limited detection of patients with unfavourable outcomes at the current decision threshold. This finding is consistent with the pattern commonly observed across analogous studies employing class-imbalanced datasets, wherein high specificity is achieved at the expense of sensitivity, reflecting a model tendency to generate false-negative classifications. The high specificity (0.97, 95% CI 0.94–0.99) and positive predictive value (0.64, 95% CI 0.35–0.87) indicate that the model identifies high-risk patients with considerable precision when a positive prediction is made; however, the rate of misclassification among patients who are in fact at low risk remains elevated. On the basis of the dual evaluation framework incorporating both AUROC and PR-AUC, XGBoost maintained superiority across both metrics and was selected as the final predictive model. Nevertheless, its moderate overall discriminative performance warrants measured interpretation when considering its translational potential for clinical application.

For the test dataset, we plotted the Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) for the XGB machine learning model to thoroughly evaluate its net benefit performance. In a clinical context, net benefit is defined as the minimum probability at which a patient is considered to need further medical intervention [24]. Figure 4 displays three lines: the orange line, the black line, and the blue line. The orange line represents an extreme scenario where no individuals receive treatment, resulting in a net benefit of zero. The black line depicts another extreme where all individuals are treated, showing a declining net benefit as the threshold probability increases. The blue line illustrates changes in net benefit achieved by our decision model across varying threshold probability. Using the orange and black lines as reference standards, if the blue line closely aligns with these reference lines, it indicates a lack of practical utility for the model. Conversely, if the blue line consistently surpasses the reference lines over a broad range of threshold intervals, it demonstrates a favorable net benefit for the model. As observed in Fig. 5, our ML predictive model shows considerable net benefit in both the training and validation datasets, underscoring its potential clinical applicability.

Decision curve analysis of a clinical prediction model

SHAP is a highly versatile model interpretability method that provides both global and local explanations, allowing for the exploration of potential associations between model predictions and specific features at an individual sample level. In this regard, SHAP demonstrates a unique advantage. It considers the varying marginal contributions of a feature across all possible feature sequences and calculates the average, resulting in the SHAP baseline value for that feature. This process is both rigorous and comprehensive, ensuring that the contributions of each feature in the model are accurately assessed and explained.

Figure 6 A presents the SHAP value distribution across all validation set samples for the XGBoost model, quantifying both the direction and relative magnitude of each feature’s contribution to model prediction output. D-dimer exhibited the highest mean absolute SHAP value among the 14 features, with higher D-dimer values corresponding to greater predicted risk scores in the model. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) displayed an analogous pattern, with samples carrying elevated hsCRP values contributing predominantly positive SHAP values. In contrast, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol and prognostic nutritional index (PNI) demonstrated contributions in the opposing direction, whereby lower values were associated with a tendency towards higher predicted risk. The directional contributions of these features are broadly concordant with findings reported in the literature regarding the prognostic relevance of coagulation activation, inflammatory response, and nutritional-immune status in ischaemic stroke. It should nonetheless be emphasised that SHAP values reflect statistical associations between features and model output, and must not be interpreted as evidence of clinical causality.

Wide areas indicate a large number of samples clustered together. Figure 6 B presents the SHAP waterfall plot for a representative patient in the validation set with an unfavourable outcome (actual mRS ≥ 2), for whom the model-predicted probability (f(x) = 0.999) substantially exceeded the baseline expected value across all samples (E[f(x)] = 0.198). In this case, D-dimer (268 µg/mL; SHAP = + 0.263) and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII; 638; SHAP = + 0.175) were the dominant features driving the predicted probability upward. Although hsCRP (0.5 mg/L; SHAP = − 0.065) and HDL cholesterol (0.97 mmol/L; SHAP = − 0.065) exerted negative contributions, the cumulative weight of positive-contributing features predominated given the specific combination of feature values in this patient, yielding a final predicted probability approaching unity. The SHAP waterfall plot reflects a local decomposition of the model’s decision at this particular sample; the magnitude and direction of individual feature contributions are contingent on the sample’s position within the feature space and should not be extrapolated to other patients or interpreted as population-level risk factor estimates.

Figure 6 C presents the SHAP waterfall plot for a patient assigned low predicted risk by the model (f(x) = 0.000375). HDL cholesterol (1.23 mmol/L; SHAP = − 0.078) and PNI (59.2; SHAP = − 0.034) contributed negative SHAP values and carried comparatively prominent weight in this sample. SII was notably elevated (1026; SHAP = + 0.029), yet its positive contribution was modest in magnitude and was offset at the model level by the remaining negatively contributing features. Juxtaposition of these two cases (Fig. 5B vs. 5 C) reveals marked heterogeneity in the SHAP contributions of identical features across different patients, underscoring the sample-dependent nature of feature effects. This property is inherent to individual-level SHAP explanations derived from tree-based models, distinguishing them from population-averaged effect estimates.

Figure 7 presents local interpretable model-agnostic explanation (LIME) analyses for Case 1, with separate local linear approximations generated for each output label (“favourable outcome” and “unfavourable outcome”). The local fidelity for both labels was 0.40, indicating moderate approximation accuracy of the LIME surrogate model for this case and implying a degree of uncertainty in the estimated feature weights. Under the “favourable outcome” label, the local weights for D-dimer and age were directionally concordant with that label (i.e., negative weights); under the “unfavourable outcome” label, the weights for these two features reversed in sign. This reversal reflects a mathematical property of LIME within the multi-class local approximation framework, specifically the estimation of local linear coefficients within distinct label subspaces, and does not imply that the prognostic directionality of these features has changed. HDL cholesterol and PNI yielded weights approaching zero under both labels, suggesting that, given this patient’s particular combination of feature values, these two indices contributed limited discriminative influence to the model’s local decision boundary.

Interpreting individual forecasts

Taken together, SHAP and LIME provide complementary, quantitative characterisations of the model’s decision logic at the levels of global contribution distribution and individual local attribution, respectively. Their outputs may serve as auxiliary references for generating clinical hypotheses; however, both analyses are grounded in feature-output associations internal to the model, and their clinical significance requires independent validation in prospective studies.

Discussion

Ischaemic stroke, caused by cerebral arterial occlusion, is a leading cerebrovascular disease and represents the foremost cause of disability and the second leading cause of mortality worldwide [25, 26]. As population ageing accelerates, stroke incidence continues to rise, with the disease burden increasingly shifting towards younger populations, most notably in low- and middle-income countries [27]. Ischaemic stroke accounts for 75–80% of all stroke subtypes, and affected patients may sustain severe impairment across physical function, social participation, and cognitive capacity [28, 29]. Although advances in reperfusion therapy have improved acute-phase outcomes in a subset of patients, the accuracy and clinical applicability of existing tools for predicting one-year functional outcomes remain suboptimal [30–32]. Developing early prediction models based on routinely available clinical indices may facilitate timely identification and stratified management of high-risk patients.

Neurological injury and functional recovery following ischaemic stroke involve pathophysiological mechanisms operating at multiple levels. At the neural network level, functional recovery in patients with moderate-to-severe stroke does not depend exclusively on the classical interhemispheric inhibition model. Recent dynamic functional connectivity research has demonstrated that the contralesional dorsal premotor cortex (PMd) can compensatorily assume the modulatory role of the ipsilesional PMd by strengthening its functional connectivity with subcortical structures, thereby revealing the existence of state-dependent neural reorganisation pathways [33]. At the molecular level, precise regulation of alternative mRNA splicing in neuronal precursors is essential for maintaining long-term neuronal survival and functional adaptation, and its dysregulation is closely associated with multiple neurodegenerative pathological processes [34]. Collectively, these mechanisms suggest that systemic factors — including inflammatory activation, coagulation dysregulation, and nutritional imbalance — perturb the neuroinflammatory microenvironment and post-transcriptional regulatory networks, thereby influencing the molecular substrates of post-stroke neuroplasticity and determining long-term functional outcomes across multiple levels. This mechanistic framework provides a theoretical rationale for incorporating the relevant clinical indices into the predictive model developed in the present study.

In ischemic stroke, inflammation is considered a critical component in its development and progression. Vascular occlusion leads to the formation of necrotic cells, subsequently triggering an inflammatory response. In addition, poor prognosis in acute ischemic stroke has been associated with irregular blood coagulation, inadequate nutritional status, and the persistence of systemic inflammation [35]. Exploring new biomarkers holds the potential to improve early identification of patients at risk for adverse outcomes.

Elevated D-dimer levels serve as a marker of thrombin formation and fibrinolysis. Studies have demonstrated that D-dimer testing not only aids in identifying cryptogenic stroke etiologies and previously undetected sources of cardiac emboli but also provides important insights for therapeutic decision-making in secondary stroke prevention [36]. Previous research has found that elevated baseline levels of D-dimer, as well as increases in D-dimer levels within three months, are associated with poor outcomes in patients following ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack [37].

The immune-inflammatory response is not only involved in coronary artery disease but also plays a role throughout the entire course of acute stroke, including initial arterial occlusion, brain parenchymal injury, tissue repair, and infectious complications [38]. The systemic immune inflammation index (SII), a novel biomarker representing systemic immune-inflammatory levels, is derived from the counts of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and platelets. SII has been shown to be a significant prognostic predictor for a variety of inflammatory diseases and is closely associated with both short- and long-term prognosis in acute ischemic stroke. Higher SII levels are linked to an increased likelihood of adverse events, further underscoring its relevance in predicting stroke outcomes [39].

Moreover, we have identified that malnutrition is associated with poor prognosis across various diseases. For stroke patients, malnutrition poses a severe issue as it impacts bodily functions, leading to adverse prognostic events. Malnutrition is highly prevalent among elderly individuals with ischemic stroke and is linked to increased mortality [40]. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) serves as a valuable tool for assessing the nutritional status of these patients. Studies have shown that PNI is not only a more accurate prognostic marker of nutritional status in ischemic stroke patients, but it also helps identify those with compromised immune nutritional status who are at higher risk for recurrent stroke and major cardiovascular events [41, 42].

In recent years, the HALP score — a composite biomarker integrating nutritional and immune status — has been demonstrated to independently predict mortality in patients with acute ischaemic stroke [43]. The interactions observed in the present study between metabolic indices, including HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and fasting glucose, and inflammatory markers suggest that the metabolic–inflammatory axis participates in the pathological processes of post-stroke neurological injury through a “brain–body pathway.” Research integrating plasma proteomics with multimodal neuroimaging has confirmed that metabolic dysfunction can drive central nervous system pathological progression via structural alterations such as hippocampal atrophy and reduced cortico-sensorimotor network connectivity, with Mendelian randomisation analyses providing further support for the causal directionality of this pathway [44]. Moreover, cortical morphological features exhibit dose-dependent associations with transdiagnostic cognitive and psychopathological indices, with lower brain morphology scores conferring greater comorbidity burden and elevated risk of persistent psychopathology [45].

Collectively, these lines of evidence suggest that predictive frameworks for unfavourable post-stroke outcomes should not be confined to physical disability alone, but should systematically incorporate post-stroke cognitive trajectories and affective dysregulation. The multidimensional clinical index prediction system developed in the present study provides a methodological foundation for this multimodal integration approach.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, lipid metabolism-related features (HDL, LDL, total cholesterol) consistently demonstrate a positive contribution to adverse outcomes (SHAP values > 0). This aligns with the established pathological mechanism by which dysregulated lipid metabolism promotes atherosclerosis [1]. However, these biochemical markers are physiologically interconnected (e.g., HDL is involved in reverse cholesterol transport and is inversely regulated by LDL metabolism [2]), potentially leading to collinearity among features. Regression analysis indicates moderate collinearity between total cholesterol (VIF = 4.36) and LDL (VIF = 4.04), which corroborates their partially overlapping distribution regions in the SHAP plot (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p = 0.028). Notably, although HDL shows a relatively low VIF (1.24), its antagonistic physiological relationship with LDL may induce nonlinear interactions, which are challenging to fully capture through traditional collinearity assessments.Through multidimensional validation, the reliability of the current model’s interpretability has been established from several perspectives: (1) Consistency of Key Features: D-dimer consistently displays a significant positive association both in the SHAP plot (mean = 0.22) and in regression analysis (β = 8.93E-5, p = 0.001). (2) Differentiation of Collinear Features: High values of total cholesterol (orange) concentrate in the SHAP > 0.15 region, whereas LDL high values predominantly cluster within the 0.05–0.1 range. The width ratio of regression coefficient confidence intervals between total cholesterol and LDL (95% CI width ratio = 1.84) further suggests that the model can effectively distinguish their respective contributions. (3) Alignment with Stroke Pathophysiology: The elevated SHAP values of inflammatory markers (hsCRP, SII) and coagulation indicators (D-dimer, fibrinogen) correspond to the thromboinflammatory mechanisms, which are consistent with the known underlying pathophysiology of stroke.

Machine learning–based prediction models have been increasingly reported in the field of stroke prognostication; however, the opacity of model decision-making processes remains a significant barrier to clinical translation [46, 47]. In the present study, two complementary explainability frameworks — SHAP and LIME — were jointly applied to quantify the contribution of each feature variable to the predicted outcomes across both global and local dimensions. SHAP, grounded in Shapley value theory, provides marginal contribution estimates for each feature at the whole-sample level, enabling identification of the dominant factors driving unfavourable prognosis. LIME, by contrast, employs local linear approximation to decompose the prediction for a given individual into interpretable feature weight outputs, thereby rendering risk attribution at the patient level more clinically legible [48]. Regarding integration into clinical workflows, the explainability outputs described above are technically amenable to interfacing with electronic health record systems via standardised application programming interfaces (APIs), using routine laboratory and clinical indices collected at admission as inputs to automatically generate visualised risk reports for clinical reference. Nevertheless, the practical utility of such implementation requires prospective validation, including assessment of its effects on clinical decision-making behaviour and patient outcomes.

The present study has several methodological limitations. First, established strong predictors — including baseline NIHSS score, stroke subtype, infarct volume, reperfusion therapy, and onset-to-treatment time — were not incorporated into the model. This necessarily constrains the upper bound of discriminative performance and represents essential context for interpreting the current AUROC levels. Second, the study employed a single-centre retrospective design with internal holdout validation only; the stability of the model across different institutions, geographic regions, and ethnic populations therefore remains uncertain. Clinical deployment should be contingent upon multicentre prospective external validation. Third, both SHAP and LIME are post-hoc attribution methods whose stability may diminish in the presence of strong multicollinearity among features; the derived conclusions should accordingly be interpreted as statistical associations at the model level and do not support causal inference. Furthermore, although the present study followed the TRIPOD-AI framework in reporting data partitioning procedures and dual assessment of discrimination and calibration, several core recommended items — most notably prospective data collection and external validation — have not yet been fulfilled. The practical clinical utility of the model therefore warrants further evaluation in subsequent research.

Conclusion

The present study developed and internally validated an interpretable XGBoost prediction model for assessing the risk of unfavourable one-year outcomes in patients with acute ischaemic stroke, using routine clinical indices obtained at admission. The model demonstrated satisfactory discrimination and calibration in internal validation. Through complementary SHAP and LIME explainability analyses, the framework provides clinically referenced feature contributions at both the global distributional and individual attribution levels, supporting more transparent clinical decision-making. These findings suggest that a multidimensional predictive framework integrating inflammatory, coagulation, and nutritional indices holds potential clinical utility; however, its practical effectiveness requires further validation in multicentre prospective cohorts before clinical deployment can be recommended.
