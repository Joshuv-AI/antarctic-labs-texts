Smooth Muscle Cell Plasticity as a Central Determinant of Plaque Stability
Authors: Pei-Yu Chen, Michael Simons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewThis review examines how smooth muscle cell (SMC) fate transitions contribute to the central paradox of atherosclerosis: why thin-cap plaques that only mildly narrow the lumen cause most acute events, whereas many severely stenotic lesions remain stable. We propose that interactions between SMC developmental programming and local microenvironments determine whether SMCs build collagen-rich fibrous caps, undergo medial degeneration, or adopt phenotypes that weaken the vessel wall.Recent FindingsSMCs show marked plasticity, adopting phenotypes that either strengthen or weaken fibrous caps. Three axes appear central. First, developmental origin imprints durable differences in how coronary, ascending aortic, and abdominal SMCs respond to the same insults. Second, microenvironmental factors such as flow, vessel architecture, aging, and perivascular fat translate identical systemic risks into bed-specific disease patterns. Third, the “TGF-β paradox” reflects context-dependent signaling in which dose, cellular audience, matrix, and timing determine whether TGF-β supports cap formation or drives inflammatory and matrix-degrading programs. Mouse lineage-tracing and human pathology converge on the view that clinical risk correlates more robustly with plaque composition and cap integrity than with stenosis severity.SummarySMC plasticity helps explain why the same systemic risk factors produce different patterns of atherosclerosis in different vascular beds. Developmental imprinting and local cues shape whether SMCs maintain the media and build thick, collagenous caps or instead contribute to cap thinning, core expansion, and aneurysm formation. These insights argue that therapy should move beyond lumen stenosis and focus on preserving or restoring cap-stabilizing SMC states at high-risk sites. Introduction

The SMC Plasticity Principle: How Developmental Origins Create Predictable Responses

Rethinking Risk: From Stenosis to Stability

Many heart attacks and strokes come from sudden ruptures of thin-cap plaques that only mildly narrow the lumen. In contrast, severe stenoses usually have thick, fibrotic caps, present with stable angina, and are less prone to rupture [1–3]. This observation highlights limitations of traditional stenosis-focused risk assessment approaches and underscores the critical but underappreciated role of smooth muscle cells (SMCs) in fibrous cap-building and stabilization processes.

Accumulating data suggest that SMCs are active regulators of plaque stability rather than passive bystanders. Their phenotypic plasticity lets them adopt multiple fates: some of these fates support collagen-rich fibrous cap formation, whereas others drive foam cell–like, proteolytic, or osteogenic programs that thin and destabilize the cap [4–7] (Fig. 1). This plasticity appears to follow predictable patterns influenced by developmental programming and local microenvironmental factors [8–11]. Understanding how developmental origin, local cues, and TGF-β signaling shape these state transitions may clarify why identical systemic risk factors produce distinct regional plaque patterns and cap behaviors, and may highlight new ways to promote cap-stabilizing SMC phenotypes [11].

SMCs in Health and in Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is fundamentally an arterial disease: plaques develop mainly in large and medium-sized arteries exposed to high pulsatile pressure, complex shear stress patterns, and a wall architecture that favors lipoprotein retention and smooth muscle–driven remodeling [12, 13]. In contrast, low-pressure veins with thinner media, different extracellular matrix composition, and distinct endothelial identity rarely develop atherosclerosis under physiological conditions and only become plaque-prone when they are exposed to arterial hemodynamics, as in saphenous vein bypass grafts [14, 15].

In healthy adult arteries, medial smooth muscle cells (SMCs) maintain vessel tone and wall integrity by sustaining a low-proliferative, contractile phenotype marked by proteins such as smooth muscle myosin and calponin [4, 16]. They continuously renew the elastin- and collagen-rich extracellular matrix, preserving wall structure and stress resilience [17]. Atherosclerosis disrupts this balance by injuring the endothelium, increasing lipid entry, and inducing chronic inflammation. Coupled with the loss of nitric oxide production by the endothelium, this results in medial SMCs migrating into the intima and changing their phenotype, a process referred to as cell fate transition [10, 18]. Depending on context, SMCs can adopt fibromyocyte/synthetic, cap-building phenotypes that deposit collagen and support fibrous cap formation, or shift toward foam cell–like and osteochondral-like states that lay down pro-inflammatory, fibronectin-rich matrix and expand the necrotic core, thereby thinning and weakening the cap [19–25]. Because SMC-derived cells build most of the fibrous cap and help determine its mechanical strength, current work increasingly focuses on therapies that shift the SMC fate toward protective, matrix-stabilizing states rather than treating lumen stenoses alone [19, 26–28].

Importantly, SMC plasticity and fibrous cap remodeling differ across vascular beds [29] (Fig. 2). In the coronary circulation, atherosclerotic plaques range from relatively stable fibrocalcific lesions to thin-cap, lipid-rich fibroatheromas. Fibrocalcific plaques typically produce stable, flow-limiting stenoses and are the main lesion type underlying exertional angina. Thin-cap fibroatheromas are prone to rupture or erosion and are the dominant lesion type underlying acute myocardial infarction. These rupture-prone plaques most often develop at sites of disturbed flow, such as bifurcations and vessel curvatures [30], and in many patients myocardial infarction is the first clinical manifestation of coronary artery disease, without prior stable angina.

By contrast, disease in the proximal ascending aorta is often dominated by primary medial degeneration. In the infrarenal abdominal aorta, large intimal plaques with overlying thrombus are more common. Proteases and inflammatory mediators released from this thrombus diffuse into the underlying media, leading to progressive loss of smooth muscle cells, elastic fiber breakdown, and medial weakening [28, 31]. Experimental mouse models recapitulate these anatomical patterns, with hyperlipidemic mice developing plaques at predictable sites of flow disturbance, and additional experimental manipulations such as angiotensin II infusion or elastase treatment reproducing specific regional disease phenotypes [32, 33]. This geographic specificity reflects two key factors that determine regional SMC behavior: developmental origins that create distinct SMC populations across vascular beds, and microenvironmental influences that modulate their responses to pathological stimuli [21, 34–36].

Developmental Origins

Arterial SMCs arise from distinct embryonic sources that vary across the vascular tree [34, 37, 38] (Fig. 2). Coronary SMCs derive mainly from epicardial cells that undergo epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and migrate into the vessel wall [39, 40]. In the proximal thoracic aorta, cardiac neural crest– and second heart field–derived SMCs occupy defined domains within the medial layer [28, 35, 41]. Further downstream, the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta are built predominantly from mesodermal SMCs that populate the embryonic dorsal aorta and persist into adulthood [41, 42]. As a result, the same systemic atherogenic insult is imposed in parallel on multiple embryonic SMC lineages, each embedded in a distinct wall architecture and flow environment. This durable imprint of origin helps explain why SMCs from different vascular beds respond to pathological stimuli in region-specific ways throughout life [21, 34, 43]. These origin patterns are highly reproducible across lineage-tracing studies and are preserved in the standard hyperlipidemic mouse models used for atherosclerosis research such as Apoe⁻/⁻, Ldlr⁻/⁻, and PCSK9-based approaches [28, 41].

These developmental imprints act through three connected layers. First, smooth muscle cells in different vascular beds have distinct patterns of active enhancers and chromatin accessibility. These site-specific regulatory programs determine which genes each SMC population can induce in response to growth factors such as TGF-β, PDGF, and angiotensin II [28, 43]. For example, coronary artery SMCs and aortic SMCs express a similar core contractile program but possess different sets of open regulatory regions [44, 45]. As a result, the same upstream signal activates overlapping but not identical targets at each site and produces different phenotypic responses. Second, developmental origin shapes the baseline expression of key signaling components, including receptors, adaptor proteins, and mechanosensors [34, 37, 46]. SMCs from different vascular beds show different levels of PDGFRβ, TGF-β receptors, and specific integrins, which changes how strongly they respond to TGF-β, PDGF, angiotensin II, and mechanical cues [34, 46]. Third, lineage-associated transcription factors act as master switches that direct incoming signals into distinct state transitions [34]. The transcription factor TCF21 is enriched in epicardial-derived coronary SMCs and promotes fibromyocyte-like programs, whereas another transcription factor, Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4), is strongly induced in aortic SMCs in atherosclerotic plaques and after injury and drives broader remodeling responses [19, 27]. These master regulators sit on top of the other regulatory layers and help explain how identical stimuli can push different SMC populations toward different phenotypes.

The Local Milieu: Five Microenvironmental Factors

Beyond the developmental imprint, the local microenvironment constitutes a second critical regulatory layer. A number of microenvironmental factors determine how systemic risk factors such as cholesterol levels, blood pressure, inflammation, and age are interpreted in distinct local conditions to shape the vascular bed-specific pathogenic response [47–49].

Flow patterns and mechanosensing

The pattern of blood flow is an important regulator of endothelial function and a key determinant of which regions of the artery are atheroresistant or atheroprone [48, 50, 51]. In straight arterial segments with high, steady laminar shear stress, endothelial cells align with the flow, upregulate KLF2 and KLF4, produce nitric oxide, and keep expression of NF-κB–driven inflammatory genes such as VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 low [52–54]. These regions are relatively protected from plaque formation. At the inner curvature of the aorta and arterial bifurcations, the flow shear stress is low and disturbed. Here, endothelial cells fail to align, downregulate KLF2/4, activate NF-κB and AP-1, increase leukocyte adhesion, and release growth factors that promote smooth muscle cell migration and matrix remodeling; these atheroprone regions are the typical sites of early atherosclerotic lesions [55, 56].

Hemodynamic forces are sensed through several structures, including a junctional PECAM-1/VE-cadherin/VEGFR2–3 mechanosensory complex, integrins, mechanosensitive ion channels such as Piezo1, G-protein–coupled receptors, primary cilia, caveolae, and syndecans such as syndecan-4 (Sdc4) among likely others [57–60]. Work from Schwartz and colleagues identifies endothelial junctions as major shear sensors. In their model, PECAM-1 bears the mechanical load, VE-cadherin links the complex, and VEGFR2/3 provides the kinase activity [57–59]. Disrupting this complex prevents endothelial cells from activating NF-κB and upregulating adhesion molecules in response to disturbed flow in vivo. FRET-based tension sensors further demonstrate that fluid shear increases the force across PECAM-1 and VE-cadherin at atheroprone sites, directly linking local mechanical load at endothelial junctions to downstream signaling. In parallel, integrins link the contractile cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix, allowing cells to pull on their substrate and sense how stiff it is. Under physiological conditions, this feedback lets endothelial cells and underlying smooth muscle cells adjust matrix deposition and organization to maintain a compliant, well-ordered wall. Under chronic disturbed flow, the same machinery instead drives a drift toward a stiffer, less compliant intima with enhanced inflammatory signaling and maladaptive matrix remodeling.

The junctional and integrin-based sensors that report these mechanical changes also feed directly into TGF-β/BMP signaling and help set the balance between quiescent and activated endothelial states. Under laminar, atheroprotective shear, BMP9/10–ALK1–Smad1/5/8 signaling keeps ECs aligned and relatively quiescent; under disturbed flow, a shift toward TGF-β–ALK5–Smad2/3 signaling, amplified by lipoproteins and inflammatory cytokines, drives endothelial activation, endothelial to mesenchymal transition (EndMT), and plaque growth at disturbed-flow sites [61–64].

The endothelial glycocalyx is a carbohydrate-rich surface layer that contributes to barrier function and can participate in shear sensing at the luminal side of endothelial cells [65]. Glycocalyx thickness tends to be greater in straight arterial segments exposed to high, steady laminar flow and reduced in disturbed-flow regions such as bends and bifurcations, where enzymatic shedding is enhanced [48, 50, 66–68]. Proteoglycans such as Sdc4 and Sdc2 help organize this layer and have been implicated in endothelial alignment and atheroprotective signaling. The loss of Sdc4 increases disturbed-flow–induced inflammatory activation and atherosclerosis in experimental models [69, 70]. Available data suggest that the endothelial glycocalyx and proteoglycans modulate shear sensing and permeability rather than dictating plaque location; loss of this layer favors inflammatory activation [71, 72].

Together, these flow-dependent endothelial programs create reproducible “hotspots” of disease across the arterial tree. Inner curvatures and post-bifurcation segments, where disturbed flow suppresses KLF2/4 and enhances NF-κB, show higher expression of adhesion molecules and growth factors such as PDGF, along with reduced nitric oxide; this milieu promotes recruitment of medial SMCs into the intima and favors switching toward synthetic, matrix-producing states [25]. In contrast, outer curvatures and flow-dividing ridges at branch points retain a KLF2/4-high, NO-rich, low-inflammatory profile and are relatively protected from SMC activation and plaque formation [48, 73, 74].

Arterial architecture

The aortic wall is not uniform along its length. The thoracic aorta contains multiple elastin-rich lamellar units in the media, which give the vessel high compliance and spread cyclic pressure loads across the wall [75–77]. The infrarenal abdominal aorta has fewer elastic lamellae despite similar systolic pressures and, with aging, often remodels into a thicker, more collagen-dense and mechanically stiffer segment [75, 76, 78, 79]. These structural differences shape how disease evolves. In the infrarenal aorta, aneurysms usually arise on a background of atherosclerotic plaque, and progressive dilation slows luminal flow, favoring platelet deposition and intraluminal thrombus (ILT) formation [80–82]. The ILT forms a multilayered clot along the lumen that increases the diffusion distance for oxygen and nutrients and acts as a reservoir for proteases and inflammatory mediators that diffuse into the underlying media [83–86]. As the wall thickens in this proteolytic, relatively hypoxic environment, elastic fibers fragment, smooth muscle cells die or become dysfunctional, and the remaining cells cannot fully restore matrix integrity [83, 86, 87]. Thoracic segments, with greater elastic reserve and a different remodeling pattern, appear less susceptible to this ILT-driven mechanism and more often show slowly progressive matrix degeneration without the same degree of thrombus-associated medial injury [75, 86, 88].

Fat depots

Perivascular adipose tissue varies by location with opposing inflammatory effects on nearby SMCs [89–91]. Perivascular fat surrounding the thoracic aorta contains brown/beige adipocytes that produce anti-inflammatory mediators, promoting nitric oxide bioavailability and supporting SMC maintenance in the contractile phenotype [89, 90, 92–94]. In contrast, perivascular fat around the abdominal aorta secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines via white adipocytes, while epicardial fat represents a specialized depot with direct anatomical proximity to coronary vessels—both promoting SMC migration, proliferation, and synthetic phenotype switching through cytokine-mediated pathways, especially during obesity [91, 95–98]. Beyond cytokines and adipokines, free fatty acids released during adipose tissue lipolysis may also directly influence SMC phenotype. In experimental VSMC systems, oleic acid promotes proliferation and fibronectin synthesis through mitogenic and reactive oxygen species–dependent signaling, whereas palmitate exacerbates cholesterol-induced lipid accumulation and KLF4-associated foam cell transition. Thus, these effects depend on fatty-acid species and metabolic context [99–101].

Aging

Aging fundamentally alters vessel wall structure, with chronic low-grade inflammation (inflammaging) serving as the primary driver of coordinated changes in both matrix and cellular components [102–104]. Age-related inflammatory mediators, including IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, orchestrate a cascade of destructive processes [102, 105–107]. The extracellular matrix progressively stiffens as inflammation-induced enzymes fragment elastic fibers and advanced glycation end-products accumulate in collagen [108, 109]. Simultaneously, chronic inflammatory signaling drives SMC functional decline characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and altered gene expression that reduces their collagen synthesis capacity [110–112].

As inflammatory stress pushes SMCs into senescence, they shift from maintaining the wall to helping its breakdown: senescent SMCs lose normal matrix repair capacity, adopt a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) rich in cytokines and proteases that drive matrix degradation and increase matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity, and become more prone to osteogenic differentiation and calcification [113–115]. This senescent, inflammatory milieu amplifies the other aging-related stresses in the vessel wall and sets up a self-perpetuating cycle of stiffening and structural failure [116–118].

Cell contacts

Direct cell–cell contact creates highly localized signaling domains that are not visible in systemic measurements. Endothelial–SMC junctions activate Notch signaling pathways that help maintain arterial identity and restrain inappropriate proliferation [119–121]. At the same time, mechanosensitive integrins can activate latent TGF-β complexes at the cell surface, generating small pericellular zones with higher TGF-β availability than the surrounding tissue [122]. These contact-driven signals add a local layer on top of the overall cytokine and flow environment. They help explain why endothelial cells and nearby SMCs can sit in the same tissue yet experience very different TGF-β inputs, as discussed in more detail in the TGF-β paradox section.

The integration: When developmental programming meets environmental challenge

The developmental imprint of regional SMC populations integrates with environmental signals to determine cellular fate decisions that drive distinct disease progression patterns [123]. These interactions explain why patients with identical risk factors develop different diseases in different vascular beds. For instance, a patient with elevated cholesterol and blood pressure may develop coronary atherosclerosis in areas where disturbed flow patterns and inflammatory signaling predominate, or abdominal aortic aneurysms where architectural vulnerability and matrix degradation combine with atherosclerotic plaque formation [124].

The TGF-β Paradox: A Four-Variable Decision Framework

Investigations of inherited aortic aneurysm syndromes first highlighted the TGF-β paradox in the vessel wall. Studies of aneurysms in patients with Marfan syndrome, caused by heterozygous FBN1 mutations, showed that defects in the fibrillin-1 scaffold are sufficient to cause progressive dilation of the aortic root [125]. These patients develop aneurysms of the sinuses of Valsalva, medial degeneration, and a high risk of dissection in young adulthood. Patients with a similar vascular phenotype but no FBN1 mutation were later found to carry heterozygous pathogenic variants in core TGF-β signaling genes [126]. These include the receptors TGFBR1 and TGFBR2, the SMAD transcription factors SMAD2 and SMAD3, and the ligands TGFB2 and TGFB3, now grouped under Loeys–Dietz syndrome and related TGF-β vasculopathies (LDS1–LDS6) [126–128]. Clinically, these syndromes show early, aggressive aneurysms, diffuse arterial tortuosity, and distinctive craniofacial and skeletal features. In these heritable TGF-β vasculopathies, aortic dissections can occur when the ascending aorta diameter is only in the low-to-mid 4-cm range, whereas typical sporadic, age-related thoracic aortic aneurysms usually do not dissect until around 5–5.5 cm [129–131]. In simple cell-based assays, most of the receptor, ligand, and Smad variants reduce Smad2/3 activation after TGF-β stimulation and behave as loss-of-function or hypomorphic alleles, suggesting that “too little” canonical TGF-β signaling should be the primary problem.

Aortic tissue from patients with Loeys–Dietz syndrome tells a very different story. Histologic analyses of ascending aorta from individuals carrying mutations in TGFBR1, TGFBR2, TGFB2, TGFB3, SMAD2, or SMAD3 show strong nuclear phospho-Smad2/3 staining in medial smooth muscle cells and adventitial fibroblasts, along with increased expression of canonical TGF-β target genes in the aneurysmal wall [126–128]. Similar Smad2/3 hyperactivation is seen in mouse models carrying LDS-like mutations in Tgfbr2 or Tgfb2 that develop thoracic aortic aneurysms, and in Fbn1 mutant Marfan models, where aortic root dilation is accompanied by increased pSmad2 and upregulation of TGF-β–responsive transcripts. In several of these models, partial rescue of the aneurysm phenotype by TGF-β neutralization or by drugs that indirectly reduce TGF-β signaling supports the idea that excessive pathway activity, rather than simple loss, is driving disease [132]. This mismatch between reduced signaling capacity per allele and apparent overactivation of the pathway in the diseased aortic wall is the original form of the TGF-β paradox in aortic disease. This work, largely from the Loeys and Dietz groups, established the current view that TGF-β signaling in these syndromes is dysregulated rather than simply reduced.

Early mouse models used germline TGF-β pathway mutations or systemic TGF-β blockade, so signaling changed in all cells and the paradox was viewed as a whole-wall phenomenon rather than a cell-type–specific effect. Conditional, lineage-specific knockouts later showed that the key question is how individual cell types, especially endothelial cells and smooth muscle cells, handle the same TGF-β signal [133–135].

In smooth muscle cells, the protective role of TGF-β is most obvious in conditional knockout models. Postnatal, SMC-specific deletion of Tgfbr2 (Myh11-CreERT2; Tgfbr2fl/fl) lowers Smad2/3 phosphorylation, as expected for reduced canonical TGF-β input. In the simple “too much versus too little” view that grew out of the early Loeys–Dietz work, this should produce a relatively quiescent, contractile media with lower TGF-β target gene expression. Instead, these mice develop rapid medial degeneration, aortic dilatation, and dissection, with strong activation of ERK and other stress MAPKs in the aortic wall [136, 137]. SMC-specific deletion of Tgfbr1 produces an even more aggressive thoracic aneurysm phenotype that can be partially rescued by ERK inhibition or by blocking the angiotensin II type 1 receptor, indicating that loss of canonical TGF-β/Smad input shifts SMCs onto an AngII–ERK–dominated stress program rather than leaving them quiescent [138, 139]. Other SMC-targeted disruptions of Tgfbr2 or downstream Smads, including partial Smad loss or combined reduction of Tgfbr1 and Tgfbr2, all tell a similar story: once canonical TGF-β/Smad signaling falls below a certain range, the media thins and disorganizes, contractile SMCs are lost, wall stress rises, and aneurysms form instead of a stable, adaptive remodeling response.

In hyperlipidemic settings, loss of SMC TGF-β signaling does more than blunt contractile gene expression. Lineage tracing and single-cell profiling in SMC-Tgfbr2–deficient Apoe−/− mice show that medial SMCs first downregulate the contractile program, pass through an Sca1+/PDGFRα+ mesenchymal-like state, and then diversify into osteochondral-, adipocyte-, and fibroblast-like lineages within aneurysmal segments [140]. In the ascending aorta these reprogrammed cells remain largely within the media, replacing elastic lamellae with cartilage- and bone-like tissue, whereas in the abdominal aorta they form a thick neointima overlying a thinned, failing media. The same loss of canonical TGF-β/Smad input in SMCs therefore produces very different patterns of wall failure in different regions, reinforcing the idea that developmental origin and local mechanical context strongly shape how SMCs respond when this pathway is removed. Together, these data support a simple conclusion: in SMCs, physiological Tgfbr1/2–Smad signaling is required for normal postnatal wall homeostasis, and taking that signal away destabilizes the media even in settings where overall TGF-β activity in the wall is increased.

Endothelial cells behave very differently. In most cell types, TGF-β signals almost entirely through the type I receptor ALK5 (TGFBR1), but arterial ECs also express high levels of ALK1 and the co-receptor endoglin (TGFBR3), forming a BMP9/10-responsive complex that is largely restricted to the endothelium [141–143]. Under high, steady laminar shear, ECs maintain FGFR1–FRS2α signaling and receive a continuous BMP9/10 input via ALK1–endoglin. This keeps let-7 microRNAs high and restrains expression of TGF-β ligands, TGFBR1, and SMAD2 [63, 144]. Canonical ALK5–Smad2/3 activity stays low, ALK1/BMP9–Smad1/5/9 provides the dominant signal, and the monolayer remains aligned, barrier-competent, and relatively anti-inflammatory.

In regions of disturbed flow and chronic inflammation, the same wiring is used in the opposite direction. Cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ, together with oscillatory or low shear, downregulate endothelial FGFR1, weaken FGF input, and release the let-7 “brake” on the pathway [61]. TGF-β ligands, TGFBR1, and SMAD2 increase, ALK5–Smad2/3 signaling becomes dominant, and ECs switch from a quiescent state to an activated program with higher expression of adhesion molecules, chemokines, and fibronectin-rich matrix genes. Bulk RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, and single-cell RNA-seq in hyperlipidemic mice all point in the same direction: TGF-β strongly amplifies inflammatory and matrix-remodeling pathways in ECs, while in SMCs the dominant effect is stabilizing [145, 146].

Within this TGF-β–driven endothelial program, a major outcome is EndMT. Mouse and human studies of endothelial TGF-β signaling show that pathway activation drives a robust EndMT program in atherosclerotic lesions and that the extent of EndMT correlates with disease severity [61, 145]. ECs undergoing EndMT upregulate leukocyte adhesion molecules and deposit a pro-inflammatory, fibronectin-rich matrix, directly contributing to plaque growth and maintaining a chronically inflamed intima.

Single-cell analysis identified a distinct EC-derived, MSC-like cluster with a particularly strong inflammatory and matrix-remodeling signature, whose frequency dropped sharply when endothelial TGF-β receptors were deleted. Complementary work from Kovacic and colleagues extended this picture, showing that EndMT-derived fibroblast-like cells are common in murine and human plaques, and that higher numbers of “transitioning” EC/mesenchymal cells associate with complex, thin-capped, and ruptured lesions [146]. These EndMT-derived fibroblast-like cells produce less stabilizing collagen and much more MMP activity than resident fibroblasts, shifting the collagen–MMP balance toward cap weakening.

Genetic and therapeutic loss-of-function experiments confirmed that this endothelial TGF-β axis is disease-promoting. Endothelial-specific deletion of Tgfbr1 and Tgfbr2 in Apoe⁻/⁻ mice reduced vascular inflammation, lowered macrophage and T-cell content in plaques, and markedly decreased necrotic core size. The same intervention prevented progression of established lesions and supported regression under lipid-lowering conditions, and lipid nanoparticle delivery of Tgfbr1/2 siRNA produced a similar suppression of aortic plaque burden. In parallel, related models showed that EndMT in mouse and human plaques is driven by TGF-β together with oxidative stress and hypoxia, and that blocking this axis reduces EndMT and neointimal growth.

Taken together, these endothelial data, when placed next to the SMC knockout studies, give a simple rule of thumb: in medial SMCs, physiological Tgfbr1/2–Smad signaling is broadly protective, whereas in ECs exposed to disturbed flow and inflammatory cues, the same ligand becomes a chronic stress signal that drives EndMT, fibronectin-rich matrix deposition, and cap-destabilizing inflammation. This cell-type split helps explain why the same upstream change in TGF-β signaling can push one population toward repair and another toward breakdown, and why the field has moved away from global TGF-β inhibition toward strategies that preserve SMC protection while limiting harmful endothelial responses [147, 148].

In an intact atherosclerotic plaque, these cell-type–specific programs run in parallel. SMCs that remain in a relatively organized matrix interpret TGF-β as a stabilizing cue, maintain contractile and matrix gene expression, and can thicken and reinforce the fibrous cap [149]. Endothelial cells overlying disturbed-flow regions see the same TGF-β-rich environment as stress, reduce nitric oxide production, loosen their barrier, and induce adhesion molecules and pro-inflammatory genes [145]. In the immune compartment, TGF-β supports regulatory T cells and M2-like macrophages and can dampen inflammation [150, 151]. Bulk readouts of “TGF-β activity” in plaques or aneurysms therefore average across stabilizing SMC programs, destabilizing endothelial programs, and context-dependent immune effects [27, 145, 146]. As a result, an apparently “high” TGF-β signal in tissue lysates or histology is hard to interpret, because it does not reveal whether protective SMC pathways or harmful endothelial and inflammatory pathways are dominant at that time [27, 152], making it difficult to infer what the pathway is doing in any one cell type from a single composite measurement.

In practice, four variables determine the effects of TGF-β in the vessel wall: (1) cell type and developmental origin, (2) signal strength, (3) signal duration, and (4) matrix and flow context (Fig. 3). Medial SMCs, endothelial cells, adventitial fibroblasts, and lesional immune cells differ in receptor and co-receptor sets, chromatin accessibility, and preferred transcription factors, so they read the same ligand in different ways. Brief, moderate activation can support repair and quiescence, whereas chronic, high-level signaling—often driven by AngII, mechanical stress, or matrix injury—tends to recruit noncanonical MAPKs and drive fibrosis, EndMT, or SMC degeneration. Compliant, well-organized extracellular matrix and laminar flow favor protective SMC and EC responses, whereas stiff, disorganized matrix and disturbed flow push both cell types toward inflammatory and proteolytic programs. The dominant cell populations and lesion architecture also shift with disease stage, so the measured “TGF-β effect” reflects whichever cells are in play at that moment. Once these features are specified, the so-called TGF-β paradox becomes less a paradox than a predictable consequence of context.

The Translation Principle: Mouse Lineage Tracing and Human Cap Stability Metrics

As discussed above, coronary, thoracic, and abdominal arterial segments are built from different SMC lineages and sit in different wall and flow environments. Here the question is how well current mouse models and human pathology let us study these SMC pools and their roles in cap stability. In practice, mouse models with SMC lineage tracing tell us which SMC populations build or weaken the fibrous cap, and human tissue shows how those choices look in real plaques.

To test this principle in vivo, atherosclerosis models need to satisfy three basic requirements. They should form plaques with human-like biology at defined arterial sites, allow precise SMC lineage tracing, and be compatible with conditional alleles that perturb specific origins or pathways. Three hyperlipidemia platforms are used most often: Apoe⁻/⁻, Ldlr⁻/⁻, and AAV–PCSK9–driven LDLR knockdown.

All three produce sustained hypercholesterolemia and plaques at disturbed-flow sites, but they differ in lipid profile and the rate of disease progression. Apoe⁻/⁻ mice develop severe, VLDL remnant–rich hypercholesterolemia even on a chow diet and rapidly form inflamed lesions in the aortic root, arch, and brachiocephalic artery [153–155]. Ldlr⁻/⁻ mice, and AAV–PCSK9–treated wild-type mice, generate more LDL-predominant, diet-dependent hyperlipidemia with slower plaque growth that more closely resembles familial hypercholesterolemia [32, 156–159]. For questions that need rapid lesion formation with a strong inflammatory drive, Apoe⁻/⁻ backgrounds are efficient. For experiments that require tighter control over cholesterol levels and diet, or closer alignment with human LDL biology, Ldlr⁻/⁻ or PCSK9-based models are usually preferable.

As SMCs change their cell fate, they downregulate canonical markers such as smooth muscle myosin heavy chain, so antibody staining alone cannot reliably identify SMC-derived cells in plaques [4, 19, 21]. Lineage tracing is therefore essential [11]. Most studies use inducible Cre drivers that label medial SMCs at baseline, such as the autosomal Myh11-CreERT2, which avoids sex-linked expression and provides robust arterial specificity [160–162]. Integrin β8-CreERT2 is an emerging alternative with good arterial selectivity [163], whereas Acta2- and SM22α-based drivers are used less often because they label additional lineages or show unwanted developmental activity [164, 165]. These drivers are paired with bright reporters such as tdTomato to permanently mark labeled cells and their descendants [166], and multicolor systems can distinguish whether cap patches arise from single cells (clonal) or multiple cells (polyclonal) [167]. Combined with single-cell and spatial profiling, this toolkit allows classification of SMC-derived states (fibromyocytes, foam cells, osteogenic cells) and mapping of their positions within the plaque structure [27, 29, 152, 168, 169]. Layering conditional alleles for key pathways or developmental origins onto Apoe⁻/⁻, Ldlr⁻/⁻, or PCSK9-based backgrounds that form plaques at disturbed-flow sites then makes it possible to quantify SMC recruitment, state changes, and contribution to cap matrix in a controlled setting [19, 25, 170]. In practice, these model–tool combinations provide the in vivo backbone for dissecting how developmental imprint and local microenvironments shape SMC behavior during atherogenesis.

The main gap relative to human disease lies in coronary plaque composition and complications. Classical Apoe⁻/⁻, Ldlr⁻/⁻, and PCSK9-based systems are excellent for studies of SMC fate, cap building, and TGF-β biology in the aorta, but they show only modest coronary disease and do not naturally generate human-like coronary thin-cap fibroatheromas or spontaneous ruptures [171–173]. More extreme combinations of genotype and diet, such as HypoE/SR-BI⁻/⁻ mice on very high-fat diets, can produce spontaneous coronary occlusions and myocardial infarction, but at the cost of profound metabolic derangement [32, 171, 174]. These strains are therefore useful for targeted questions about coronary events, whereas the classical models remain the practical backbone for SMC fate and cap biology.

Human pathology specimens are our best direct evidence for how SMCs help stabilize, or fail to stabilize, the fibrous cap in people. Autopsy hearts and surgical coronary or carotid plaques show a consistent pattern. Stable plaques usually have a thick fibrous cap with many SMCs and collagen, smaller necrotic cores, fewer macrophages, and often larger areas of calcification. In contrast, thin-cap fibroatheromas (TCFAs), which are the main precursor of rupture, have a large necrotic core, a very thin cap (often <65 μm), heavy macrophage infiltration, and relatively few cap SMCs [175–177]. Some TCFAs undergo small, often silent ruptures that heal; over time, repeated healing can add layers of fibrous tissue and calcification and convert these lesions into more fibrocalcific, narrowed plaques with a thicker, SMC-rich cap that tend to cause stable angina rather than sudden rupture.

Conclusion

Smooth muscle cells sit at the center of plaque stability. The same cells can either build a thick, collagen-rich cap or contribute to cap thinning, medial loss, and rupture. Which way they go is not random. It reflects developmental origin, local microenvironments, and how signals such as TGF-β are delivered in each vascular bed.

Mouse lineage-tracing studies now map these SMC fate choices with high resolution, and human pathology shows how they play out in people: thick, SMC-rich caps over smaller necrotic cores versus thin, inflamed caps over large cores. In practical terms, stable disease is the state in which SMCs stay alive, maintain the media, and keep building the cap. Vulnerable disease is the state in which SMCs are lost or pushed into programs that erode the cap and expand the core.

A central challenge is to preserve the protective TGF-β signals that help SMCs maintain the wall while blocking the harmful endothelial and immune responses that promote inflammation, EndMT, and matrix breakdown.

The translational goal is straightforward but demanding. Future work needs to define SMC states across vascular beds in human tissues, build better models of coronary disease without extreme, non-physiologic conditions, and design interventions that support protective SMC programs without worsening endothelial or immune injury. If we can do that, risk assessment and therapy will move beyond lumen narrowing and toward what actually matters for patients: whether the fibrous cap holds or fails.
