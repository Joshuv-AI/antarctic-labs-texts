Privacy-Preserving Fully Distributed Gaussian Process Regression
Yeongjun Jang, Kaoru Teranishi, Jihoon Suh, Takashi Tanaka

Abstract
Although distributed Gaussian process regression (GPR) enables multiple agents with separate datasets to jointly learn a model of the target function, its collaborative nature poses risks of private data leakage. To address this, we propose a privacy-preserving fully distributed GPR protocol based on secure multi-party computation (SMPC) that preserves the confidentiality of each agent’s local dataset. Building upon a secure distributed average consensus algorithm, the protocol guarantees that each agent’s local model practically converges to the same global model that would be obtained by the standard distributed GPR. Further, we adopt the paradigm of simulation based security to provide formal privacy guarantees, and extend the proposed protocol to enable kernel hyperparameter optimization, which is critical yet often overlooked in the literature. Experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness and practical applicability of the proposed method.



I
Introduction

Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) is a Bayesian nonparametric regression method that effectively models complex functions from observed data
[
34
]
.
An appealing feature of GPR is its ability to provide practical and reliable regression error bounds
[
32
,
11
]
, which most machine learning methods lack.
Consequently, GPR has been utilized in various applications, including safe control
[
23
,
18
]
, computer vision
[
33
,
22
]
, and bioinformatics
[
19
,
40
]
.

In many real-world scenarios, data are naturally distributed across multiple agents, for example, across mobile devices where user information is stored locally.
This has motivated the development of distributed GPR algorithms
[
28
,
7
]
, in which each agent independently performs regression on its local dataset, and the resulting local models are combined by a central aggregator or through a distributed protocol to form a global model.
Such approaches can improve predictive accuracy by incorporating heterogeneous information in the local datasets, and achieve scalability by distributing computational burden across the participating agents.

Despite such advantages of distributed GPR, its collaborative nature introduces new privacy concerns.
For example, each agent’s local dataset may contain sensitive information that must remain confidential, either due to privacy regulations (e.g., patient health records or financial transaction data) or because the dataset is proprietary (e.g., industrial process data).
Even when raw data are not directly exchanged, the local models can inadvertently disclose some information about the underlying datasets during aggregation.

There have been attempts to address this issue by employing cryptographic techniques such as secure multi-party computation (SMPC) and homomorphic encryption (HE), or the notion of differential privacy (DP).
However, SMPC and HE based methods have assumed the presence of a trusted central server to perform aggregation, which may be unrealistic when participating agents mutually distrust each other.
On the other hand, DP based methods ensure privacy by deliberately injecting noise, which inevitably leads to a trade-off between privacy and accuracy.

To overcome these limitations of existing approaches, we make the following key contributions:

•

We propose a
privacy-preserving fully distributed GPR protocol
based on SMPC that preserves the confidentiality of each agent’s local dataset without relying on a trusted central server.
Leveraging a secure distributed average consensus algorithm, the protocol guarantees that each agent’s local model practically converges to the global model that would be obtained by the standard distributed GPR.

•

We establish formal privacy guarantees using the simulation based security paradigm of
[
13
]
, under the assumption that agents are semi-honest but may collude.

•

We extend the proposed protocol to enable kernel hyperparameter optimization, which is essential for prediction accuracy, but has not been explored within a privacy-preserving framework.

•

Experimental results on two real-world benchmark datasets show that our protocol outperforms the methods proposed in
[
25
]
and
[
26
]
, demonstrating its effectiveness and practical applicability.

I-A

Related Works

There has been a growing interest in developing privacy-preserving GPR algorithms, and existing works can largely be classified into methods based on SMPC, HE, and DP.
In
[
25
]
, multiple trusted central servers run an SMPC protocol to aggregate local datasets and train a single centralized GPR model.
This approach can suffer from scalability issues due to the substantial computational overhead imposed on the servers by costly operations required during training, such as matrix inversion and exponentiation.
In contrast, our approach alleviates this burden by aggregating locally computed models instead.

On the other hand,
[
10
]
and
[
26
]
proposed methods based on HE, a cryptographic primitive that enables direct computation on encrypted data without decryption.
In a setting where a server holds a private pre-trained GPR model and a client submits encrypted queries for prediction,
[
10
]
selectively applied HE to only the sensitive steps of the protocol to ensure both privacy and efficiency.
However, this approach is limited to outsourced prediction and does not support collaborative model training.
To address this limitation and improve scalability,
[
26
]
implemented a distributed GPR algorithm using multi-key HE.
Nonetheless, their framework relies on a trusted central server for secret key distribution and management, and the computational complexity of multi-key HE can limit its practical applicability.

Several works have applied DP to GPR by injecting carefully designed noise into intermediate computations or prediction outputs to guarantee privacy
[
15
,
31
,
16
]
, but this inevitably leads to a trade-off between accuracy and privacy.

Importantly, none of the aforementioned works have incorporated hyperparameter optimization into their privacy-preserving frameworks, even though it is essential for achieving accurate predictions
[
20
,
34
]
.

II-A

Distributed Gaussian Process Regression

We briefly introduce the distributed Gaussian process regression (GPR) algorithm of
[
7
]
.
Consider a network of
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
agents,
where each agent
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
is equipped with a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{i}
consisting of noisy observations of an unknown function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
:

\mathcal{D}_{i}\!:=\!\{(x_{i,l},y_{i,l})\mid y_{i,l}=f(x_{i,l})+\epsilon_{i,l},~l=1,\ldots,N_{i}\}.\!\!

(1)

Here,
N_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the size of agent
i
’s dataset, and measurement noise
\epsilon_{i,l}\in{\mathbb{R}}
follow an i.i.d. zero-mean Gaussian distribution with variance
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}>0
.

We assume that the agents share a prior Gaussian process (GP) specified by a prior mean function
m:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and a prior covariance function (or kernel)
k:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
Without loss of generality, we consider a zero prior mean function
m\equiv 0
in this paper, and assume that the kernel
k
is positive definite.
1
1

1

The kernel
k
is said to be positive definite if the matrix
\mathbf{K}_{i}
is positive semi-definite for any
N_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,N_{i}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

By conditioning the prior GP on its local dataset, each agent
i
obtains a posterior mean function
\hat{f}_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and a posterior variance function
V_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
These functions serve as the agent’s estimate of the unknown function
f
and an uncertainty measure of the estimate, respectively.
For a given test point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, they are explicitly computed as

\begin{split}\!\!\hat{f}_{i}(x)&:=\mathbf{k}_{i}^{\top}(x)(\mathbf{K}_{i}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\cdot I_{N_{i}})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{i},\\
\!\!V_{i}(x)&:=k(x,x)-\mathbf{k}_{i}^{\top}(x)(\mathbf{K}_{i}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\cdot I_{N_{i}})^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{i}(x),\end{split}

(2)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{i}(x)

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}k(x,x_{i,1})\\
\vdots\\
k(x,x_{i,N_{i}})\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{i}},~~\mathbf{y}_{i}:=\begin{bmatrix}y_{i,1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{i,N_{i}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{i}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}k(x_{i,1},x_{i,1})&\!\!\cdots&\!\!k(x_{i,1},x_{i,N_{i}})\\
\vdots&\!\!\ddots&\!\!\vdots\\
k(x_{i,N_{i}},x_{i,1})&\!\!\cdots&\!\!k(x_{i,N_{i}},x_{i,N_{i}})\end{bmatrix}\!\!\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{i}\times N_{i}}.

The product-of-experts variant
[
7
]
of the distributed GPR algorithm aggregates (
2
), and computes a collaborative estimate
\hat{f}(x)
and an uncertainty measure
V(x)
as follows:

\begin{split}\hat{f}(x)&:=V(x)\sum_{i=1}^{M}V_{i}^{-1}(x)\cdot\hat{f}_{i}(x),\\
V(x)&:=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}V_{i}^{-1}(x)}.\end{split}

(3)

It can be seen from (
3
) that an estimate
\hat{f}_{i}(x)
with smaller
V_{i}(x)
, i.e., when agent
i
is confident, is assigned a greater weight.
In particular,
V_{i}(x)
is strictly positive for all
x
since the kernel
k
is positive definite
[
34
, Section 2]
, ensuring that (
3
) is well-defined.

Distributed GPR alleviates the poor scalability of standard centralized GPR.
The biggest computational overhead lies in computing the matrix inversions associated with (
2
), whose computational complexity is given by
\mathcal{O}(N_{i}^{3})
.
When a central aggregator collects every agent’s dataset to compute (
2
), the computational complexity becomes
\mathcal{O}((\sum_{i=1}^{M}N_{i})^{3})
.
In contrast, the distributed counterpart using (
3
) incurs only
\mathcal{O}(\sum_{i=1}^{M}N_{i}^{3})
.

II-B

Problem Formulation

Although distributed GPR offers computational advantages over standard centralized GPR, its implementation introduces new privacy concerns.
For example, each agent’s local dataset may contain sensitive data that must be kept private.
However, because the aggregation step in (
3
) involves local posterior means and variances that are computed from these local datasets, it may lead to indirect leakage of such sensitive data.
We provide some practical scenarios in which such privacy concerns become relevant.

Example 1

Consider a group of hospitals that are connected by a network topology.
One of the hospitals receives a new patient and wishes to estimate the health risk or the effectiveness of a certain treatment based on the data of previous patients.
To improve the quality of the estimate, it queries other hospitals to collaborate using the distributed GPR algorithm.
However, due to privacy regulations, hospitals should not disclose any information about their patients to other hospitals.
In this setting, directly sharing the local posterior mean and variance may not be acceptable, as it may reveal some information about the local dataset
\mathcal{D}_{i}
.
Moreover, assuming the presence of a trusted central server could be unrealistic, as hospitals may belong to competing institutions or operate in different countries.
\square

Example 2

The principle of Model Predictive Control (MPC)—where a planning problem is solved over a finite horizon but only the current action is executed—has been widely used in financial applications such as dynamic option hedging
[
2
]
and multi-period trading strategies
[
3
]
.
Consider a scenario in which multiple firms collaborate using the distributed GPR algorithm to construct a model for such tasks.
Each firm may have specialized expertise in a particular asset class or uneven access to alternative datasets,
giving it unique advantages in forecasting future risks and expected returns.
However, in order to maintain their competitive advantages, the firms wish to keep their local datasets
\mathcal{D}_{i}
private.
\square

Motivated by such privacy concerns, we aim to design a privacy-preserving fully distributed GPR protocol based on SMPC, which preserves the confidentiality of each agent’s local dataset without relying on a central trusted server.
The problem setting is formalized below.

Let us consider a multi-agent system, where each agent is equipped with a dataset of the form (
1
).
The network topology of the system is modeled by a graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{V}:=\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
is a finite nonempty set of
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
agents, and
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
is an edge set of ordered pairs of agents.
An edge
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
indicates that agent
i
can directly send information to agent
j
.
The set of neighbors of agent
i\in\mathcal{V}
is defined as
\mathcal{N}_{i}:=\{j\in\mathcal{V}\mid(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}\}
.
We assume that the graph
\mathcal{G}
is undirected and connected—that is,
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
if and only if
(j,i)\in\mathcal{E}
, and for every pair of agents
i,j\in\mathcal{V}
there exists a finite sequence of agents
(v_{0}=i,v_{1},\ldots,v_{l}=j)
such that
(v_{r},v_{r+1})\in\mathcal{E}
for
r=0,1,\ldots,l-1
.

In this paper we focus on the simple aggregation rule in (
3
).
We emphasize, however, that the proposed privacy-preserving framework is not restricted to this choice, and can extended in a straightforward manner to more advanced schemes, such as the generalized product-of-experts and (robust) Bayesian committee machine in
[

]
.

III
Secure Distributed Average Consensus

We present a secure fully distributed average consensus protocol based on additive secret sharing, which is a widely used cryptographic technique for SMPC
[
6
]
.
Then, we formally establish the protocol’s security using the simulation based security paradigm of
[
13
]
.
This protocol will serve as a main building block for our privacy-preserving fully distributed GPR protocol.

III-A

Additive Secret Sharing

Additive secret sharing splits a given message into random-like shares that individually reveal no information about the message.
It is defined over the finite set of integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}:={\mathbb{Z}}\cap[-q/2,q/2)
, where
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the modulus.
The modulo operation mapping
{\mathbb{Z}}
onto
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
is defined as
a~\mathrm{mod}~q:=a-\lfloor(a+q/2)/q\rfloor q\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
for all
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
For vectors, the modulo operation is applied componentwise.

The share generation algorithm

(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\leftarrow\mathsf{Share}(m,n)

splits a message
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{p}
into
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
shares
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
, where
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{p}
are sampled uniformly at random from
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{p}
and
s_{n}:=m-\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}s_{l}~\mathrm{mod}~q\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{p}
.
By construction, the message
m
can be recovered as
m=\sum_{l=1}^{n}s_{l}~\mathrm{mod}~q
.
However, if
n-1
(or fewer) shares among
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
are given, no information about the message
m
can be inferred due to the randomness of the shares.

Average Consensus with Quantized Communication

Privacy Analysis

We adopt the paradigm of simulation based security
[

and formally establish the security of Protocol

Let
\mathsf{x}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{*}
denote the

Suppose that the objective of the agents is to design a protocol

In practice, however, such a trusted third party is typically unavailable, and the agents must jointly evaluate the functionality through a distributed protocol.

In this context, we say that a protocol
h

IV-B

Hyperparameter Optimization

A widely used positive definite kernel is the squared-exponential kernel, defined by

k(x,x^{\prime})=\theta_{s}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}}{\theta_{l}^{2}}\right),

(25)

where the hyperparameters
\theta_{l}>0
and
\theta_{s}>0
determine the length-scale and signal variance of
k
, respectively.
It is well recognized that the choice of kernel hyperparameters significantly affects the prediction accuracy of GPR
[
34
,
20
]
.
Thus, we demonstrate the applicability of Protocol
1
for privacy-preserving hyperparameter optimization.
It is emphasized that the choice of the squared-exponential kernel is solely for the simplicity of presentation, and does not restrict the generality of the proposed method.

A common approach for hyperparameter optimization is to maximize the log marginal likelihood of the entire dataset
\mathcal{D}:=\{\mathcal{D}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
conditioned on the hyperparameters.
However, this is not directly applicable in our setting, as the local datasets are considered private.
Therefore, we approximate the log marginal likelihood as the sum of the log marginal likelihoods of each dataset by neglecting cross covariances between them as in
[
7
]
, i.e.,

\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\Theta)\approx\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\log p(\mathcal{D}_{i}\mid\Theta),

(26)

where
\Theta:=[\theta_{l},\theta_{s}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
In fact, agent
i
can explicitly compute
\log p(\mathcal{D}_{i}\mid\Theta)
as

\displaystyle\log p(\mathcal{D}_{i}\mid\Theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}_{i}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\cdot I_{N_{i}}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{i}}{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{\log\det(\mathbf{K}_{i}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\cdot I_{N_{i}})}{2}-\frac{N_{i}}{2}\log 2\pi.

Note that the gradient of the right-hand-side of (
26
) is given by
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\nabla_{\Theta}\log p(\mathcal{D}_{i}\mid\Theta)
, where each term can be locally computed by the agents.
We therefore propose that agents alternate between performing a local gradient ascent step to maximize their respective log marginal likelihoods, and invoking Protocol
1
to reach a consensus in a privacy-preserving manner.
This alternating strategy is commonly known in the literature as the
consensus based gradient method

[
27
]
.

The overall procedure is as follows:
Each agent
i\in\mathcal{V}
maintains an estimate of the optimal hyperparameters denoted by
\Theta_{i}(t)=[\theta_{i,l}(t),\theta_{i,s}(t)]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
At each time step
t\geq 0
, agent
i
performs a local gradient ascent step, as

\Theta_{i}(t+1/2)=\Theta_{i}(t)+\eta\nabla_{\Theta_{i}(t)}\log p(\mathcal{D}_{i}\mid\Theta_{i}(t)),

(27a)

where
\eta>0
is the step size.
Subsequently, Protocol
1
is invoked with the initial value
z_{i}^{\mathsf{ini}}=\Theta_{i}(t+1/2)
and
T=1
.
The output is then used to update the local estimate, as

\Theta_{i}(t+1)=z_{i}(T).

(27b)

The numerical simulation results in the following section demonstrate that
\Theta_{i}(t)
reach a consensus, while increasing the sum of the local log marginal likelihoods.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we have proposed a privacy-preserving fully distributed GPR protocol that preserves the confidentiality of each agent’s local dataset.
By integrating additive secret sharing into a secure distributed average consensus, the protocol guarantees that each agent’s local model practically converges to a global model that would be obtained from the standard distributed GPR.
We have analyzed the convergence of the proposed protocol and provided formal privacy guarantees through a simulation based security proof.
Furthermore, we have introduced a privacy-preserving fully distributed method to perform hyperparameter optimization by extending the secure distributed average consensus protocol.
Experimental results support the effectiveness and practical applicability of the proposed protocol.
