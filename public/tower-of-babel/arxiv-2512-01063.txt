A Beginner-Friendly Note on Maximal Monotone Operators
Hikmatullo Ismatov

Abstract
We give a self-contained and introductory account of some basic functional analytic tools needed to understand maximal monotone operators in Hilbert spaces. We review domains of (possibly unbounded) operators, closed sets and closed operators, and provide concrete examples of bounded and unbounded operators in both finite and infinite dimensions. We then explain in detail a fundamental result of Brézis: if A A is a maximal monotone linear operator, then its domain is dense, A A is closed, and ( I + λ ​ A ) − 1 (I+\lambda A)^{-1} is a non-expansive mapping for every λ > 0 \lambda>0 . The Banach fixed point theorem (contraction mapping principle) is stated and used as a key ingredient in the analysis. The presentation is aimed at beginning graduate students and readers seeing these notions for the first time.



Mathematics Subject Classification (2020):

47H05, 47A06, 46N20.

Keywords:

maximal monotone operators, closed operators, contraction mapping principle, resolvent, Hilbert space.

1
Introduction

The aim of these notes is to give a first contact with
maximal monotone operators
in a real Hilbert space
H
. This topic appears very early in the study of:

•

linear and nonlinear evolution equations (heat equation, parabolic PDEs),

•

variational inequalities and gradient flows,

•

Mean Field Games and other PDE models with monotone structure,

•

the Hille–Yosida theorem and semigroup theory.

Even in the purely linear case, the basic properties of a maximal monotone operator
A
are already quite rich:

•

the domain
D(A)
is dense in
H
;

•

A
is a closed (in general unbounded) operator;

•

for every
\lambda>0
, the operator
I+\lambda A
is bijective and its inverse is a contraction:

\|(I+\lambda A)^{-1}\|\leq 1.

These are the key ingredients behind the abstract theory of (linear) contraction semigroups, and they also serve as a model for the fully nonlinear theory of monotone operators in Banach spaces.

The structure of the notes is as follows:

•

In Section
2
we review basic Hilbert space notions: closed sets, orthogonal complements, and density.

•

In Section
3
we discuss domains, graphs, boundedness and closedness of operators, with concrete examples (including unbounded operators).

•

In Section
4
we define monotone and maximal monotone linear operators.

•

In Section
5
we present the contraction mapping (Banach fixed point) theorem.

•

In Section
6
we prove the three basic properties of a maximal monotone operator
A
following Brézis.

•

In Section
7
we collect some simple examples and a PDE application, to indicate how these abstract objects arise in practice.

The level is deliberately elementary: we work only in Hilbert spaces and focus on the linear case, but the ideas already capture the core mechanisms used in more advanced settings.

2
Hilbert spaces, closed sets and orthogonal complements

Let
(H,\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle)
be a real Hilbert space, with norm

\left\lVert u\right\rVert=\sqrt{\left\langle u,u\right\rangle}
.

2.1
Closed sets

Definition 2.1

(Closed set)
.

A subset
C\subset H
is called
closed
if whenever

(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence in
C
with
x_{n}\to x
in
H
, then

x\in C
.

Equivalently,
C
is closed if
C
contains all its limit points, or
if
C
equals its closure
\overline{C}
.

Example 2.2
.

In
\mathbb{R}
with the usual norm:

•

The interval
[0,1]
is closed.

•

The open interval
(0,1)
is not closed, because

x_{n}=1/n\in(0,1)
converges to
0\notin(0,1)
.

2.2
Orthogonal complements and density

For a subspace
M\subset H
we define its orthogonal complement by

M^{\perp}=\{\,f\in H:\left\langle f,v\right\rangle=0\text{ for all }v\in M\,\}.

Proposition 2.3

(Density and orthogonal complement)
.

Let
M\subset H
be a subspace.
Then the following are equivalent:

1.

\overline{M}=H
, i.e.
M
is dense in
H
;

2.

M^{\perp}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
:
Let
f\in H
be such that
\left\langle f,v\right\rangle=0
for all
v\in M
.
Since
M
is dense, for every
w\in H
there exists a sequence

v_{n}\in M
such that
v_{n}\to w
.
By continuity of the inner product,

\left\langle f,w\right\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\langle f,v_{n}\right\rangle=0.

Thus
f
is orthogonal to every
w\in H
, in particular

\left\langle f,f\right\rangle=0
, so
\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}=0
and
f=0
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
:
Suppose
\overline{M}\neq H
.
Then there exists
x\in H\setminus\overline{M}
.
Since
\overline{M}
is a closed subspace, we have an orthogonal
decomposition
H=\overline{M}\oplus\overline{M}^{\perp}
, so we can
write
x=y+z
with
y\in\overline{M}
,
z\in\overline{M}^{\perp}
.
Because
x\notin\overline{M}
, we must have
z\neq 0
.
But
M\subset\overline{M}
implies
\overline{M}^{\perp}\subset M^{\perp}
,
so
z\in M^{\perp}
and
z\neq 0
, contradicting
M^{\perp}=\{0\}
.
Therefore
\overline{M}=H
.
∎

In particular, to prove that a domain
D(A)
is dense in
H
it is
enough to show that the only
f\in H
orthogonal to all
v\in D(A)

is
f=0
.

3
Operators, domains, boundedness and closedness

3.1
Domains and graphs

Let
A
be a (possibly nonlinear) operator from a subset of
H
to
H
.

Definition 3.1

(Domain and graph)
.

The
domain
of
A
is

D(A)=\{\,u\in H:Au\text{ is defined}\,\}.

The
graph
of
A
is

\mathcal{G}(A)=\{(u,Au):u\in D(A)\}\subset H\times H.

If
A
is a bounded linear operator on
H
in the usual functional
analysis sense, then by definition
D(A)=H
and people often do not
write the domain explicitly.
For unbounded or nonlinear operators the domain is essential.

3.2
Bounded and unbounded linear operators

Let
X,Y
be normed spaces and
T:X\to Y
linear.

Definition 3.2

(Bounded linear operator)
.

We say
T
is
bounded
if there exists
C>0
such that

\left\lVert Tx\right\rVert_{Y}\leq C\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{X}\quad\text{for all }x\in X.

Equivalently,
T
is continuous.

If no such
C
exists,
T
is called
unbounded
.

Example 3.3

(Finite dimension: all linear operators are bounded)
.

Let
X=Y=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with any norm.
Every linear map
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded.
Indeed, the unit sphere is compact and
x\mapsto\left\lVert Tx\right\rVert
is
continuous, so
\sup_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert=1}\left\lVert Tx\right\rVert<\infty
.

Thus truly unbounded linear operators appear only in infinite
dimensional spaces.

Example 3.4

(Bounded operators in infinite dimension)
.

•

Identity on
\ell^{2}
:
I(x)=x
,
\left\lVert Ix\right\rVert=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
.

•

Right shift on
\ell^{2}
:

S(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)=(0,x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
.
Then
\left\lVert Sx\right\rVert=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
, so
\left\lVert S\right\rVert=1
.

•

Multiplication by a bounded function:
let
H=L^{2}(0,1)
and
g\in L^{\infty}(0,1)
, define

(Tf)(x)=g(x)f(x)
.
Then

\left\lVert Tf\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}|g(x)|^{2}|f(x)|^{2}\,dx\leq\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Thus
T
is bounded with
\left\lVert T\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
.

Example 3.5

(Unbounded linear operators in infinite dimension)
.

•

Derivative on
L^{2}(0,1)
:

let
H=L^{2}(0,1)
and define

D:D(D)\subset H\to H,\quad Df=f^{\prime},

with
D(D)=H_{0}^{1}(0,1)
.
The operator
D
is linear and densely defined, but unbounded:
there exist
f_{n}
with
\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\to 0
and

\left\lVert f_{n}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\to\infty
.

•

Diagonal operator on
\ell^{2}
:

let
H=\ell^{2}
and define

(Tx)_{n}=nx_{n},\quad D(T)=\bigl\{x\in\ell^{2}:\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{2}|x_{n}|^{2}<\infty\bigr\}.

Then
T
is linear and densely defined, but not bounded:
for the unit vector
e_{n}
with
1
in position
n
we have

\left\lVert e_{n}\right\rVert=1
yet
\left\lVert Te_{n}\right\rVert=n\to\infty
.

3.3
Closed graph and closed operators

Definition 3.6

(Closed graph)
.

Let
A:D(A)\subset H\to H
.
We say that
A
has a
closed graph
if
\mathcal{G}(A)
is a
closed subset of
H\times H
, i.e. whenever

u_{n}\in D(A)
,
u_{n}\to u
in
H
, and
Au_{n}\to f
in
H
, then

(u,f)\in\mathcal{G}(A)
, or equivalently

u\in D(A)
and
Au=f
.

Definition 3.7

(Closed operator)
.

A linear operator
A:D(A)\subset H\to H
is called
closed
if
its graph
\mathcal{G}(A)
is closed in
H\times H
.

Example 3.8

(Closed unbounded operator)
.

The derivative
D:H_{0}^{1}(0,1)\to L^{2}(0,1)
defined above is closed:
if
u_{n}\in H_{0}^{1}(0,1)
,
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{2}
and
u_{n}^{\prime}\to f
in
L^{2}
,
then
u\in H_{0}^{1}(0,1)
and
u^{\prime}=f
in the distributional sense.
Hence
(u,f)
lies in the graph of
D
.

Example 3.9

(An operator which is not closed)
.

Let
B:C^{1}([0,1])\subset L^{2}(0,1)\to L^{2}(0,1)
be the classical
derivative
Bu=u^{\prime}
.
There exists
u\in H^{1}(0,1)\setminus C^{1}([0,1])
and a sequence

u_{n}\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
such that

u_{n}\to u
and
u_{n}^{\prime}\to u^{\prime}
in
L^{2}
.
Thus
(u_{n},Bu_{n})\to(u,u^{\prime})
in
L^{2}\times L^{2}
, but

u\notin D(B)
, so
(u,u^{\prime})
is not in the graph.
Hence the graph of
B
is not closed and
B
is not a closed operator.

4
Monotone and maximal monotone linear operators

4.1
Monotone operators

Definition 4.1

(Monotone linear operator)
.

A (possibly unbounded) linear operator
A:D(A)\subset H\to H
is
called
monotone
if

\left\langle Au-Av,u-v\right\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }u,v\in D(A).

If
A
is linear, this is equivalent to

\left\langle Au,u\right\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }u\in D(A).

Example 4.2

(Diagonal operator on
\ell^{2}
is monotone)
.

Let
T
be as in Example
3.5
.
For
x\in D(T)
we have

\left\langle Tx,x\right\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(Tx)_{n}\overline{x_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n|x_{n}|^{2}\geq 0.

Thus
T
is monotone.

4.2
Maximal monotone operators

Intuitively, a monotone operator is maximal if its graph cannot be
strictly enlarged while preserving monotonicity.

In this chapter Brézis uses the following equivalent definition
for
linear
operators.

Definition 4.3

(Maximal monotone linear operator)
.

A monotone linear operator
A:D(A)\subset H\to H
is called

maximal monotone
if, in addition,

R(I+A)=H,

i.e. for every
f\in H
there exists
u\in D(A)
such that

u+Au=f.

This condition says that the operator
I+A
is surjective.
It turns out to be equivalent (in the linear case) to the usual
“maximality of the graph” definition.

5
The contraction mapping theorem

We recall the Banach fixed point theorem, which plays an important
role in the proof that
R(I+\lambda A)=H
for all
\lambda>0
.

Definition 5.1

(Contraction)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
A map
T:X\to X
is called a
contraction
if there exists a
constant
k\in[0,1)
such that

d(Tx,Ty)\leq k\,d(x,y)\quad\text{for all }x,y\in X.

In a normed space we write this as

\left\lVert T(x)-T(y)\right\rVert\leq k\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert
.

Theorem 5.2

(Contraction mapping / Banach fixed point)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and let
T:X\to X
be a
contraction.
Then:

1.

There exists a unique fixed point
x^{\ast}\in X
such that

T(x^{\ast})=x^{\ast}
.

2.

For any starting point
x_{0}\in X
, the iterative sequence
defined by
x_{n+1}=T(x_{n})
converges to
x^{\ast}
.

Sketch of proof.

Fix
x_{0}\in X
and define
x_{n+1}=T(x_{n})
.
Then

d(x_{n+1},x_{n})=d(Tx_{n},Tx_{n-1})\leq k\,d(x_{n},x_{n-1}).

By induction,

d(x_{n+1},x_{n})\leq k^{n}d(x_{1},x_{0})
.
For
m>n
we estimate

d(x_{m},x_{n})\leq\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}d(x_{j+1},x_{j})\leq d(x_{1},x_{0})\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}k^{j}\leq\frac{k^{n}}{1-k}d(x_{1},x_{0}).

As
n\to\infty
the right-hand side goes to
0
, so
(x_{n})
is a
Cauchy sequence.
Since
X
is complete,
x_{n}\to x^{\ast}
for some
x^{\ast}\in X
.
By continuity of
T
(it is Lipschitz) we have

T(x^{\ast})=\lim T(x_{n})=\lim x_{n+1}=x^{\ast}
, so
x^{\ast}
is a
fixed point.

For uniqueness, suppose also
y^{\ast}
is a fixed point.
Then

d(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})=d(Tx^{\ast},Ty^{\ast})\leq k\,d(x^{\ast},y^{\ast}).

Since
k<1
, this implies
d(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})=0
, so
x^{\ast}=y^{\ast}
.
∎

6
A key result on maximal monotone operators

We now explain in detail the three properties proved in Brézis’
Proposition 7.1 for a maximal monotone linear operator
A
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
A:D(A)\subset H\to H
be a maximal monotone linear operator.
Then

(a)

D(A)
is dense in
H
;

(b)

A
is a closed operator;

(c)

For every
\lambda>0
the operator

I+\lambda A:D(A)\to H
is bijective,
its inverse
(I+\lambda A)^{-1}
is bounded, and

\|(I+\lambda A)^{-1}\|\leq 1
.

6.1
Part (a): the domain is dense

Proof of (a).

Let
f\in H
be such that
\left\langle f,v\right\rangle=0
for all
v\in D(A)
.
We claim that
f=0
.

Because
A
is maximal monotone, by definition

R(I+A)=H
, so there exists
v_{0}\in D(A)
such that

v_{0}+Av_{0}=f.

Taking the scalar product with
v_{0}
gives

0=\left\langle f,v_{0}\right\rangle=\left\langle v_{0}+Av_{0},v_{0}\right\rangle=\left\lVert v_{0}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\langle Av_{0},v_{0}\right\rangle.

Since
A
is monotone,
\left\langle Av_{0},v_{0}\right\rangle\geq 0
, hence

0\geq\left\lVert v_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
, which implies
v_{0}=0
.
Then
f=v_{0}+Av_{0}=0
.

We have shown that if
f
is orthogonal to all
v\in D(A)
then

f=0
, i.e.
D(A)^{\perp}=\{0\}
.
By the Hilbert space result proved earlier, this is equivalent to

\overline{D(A)}=H
, so
D(A)
is dense in
H
.
∎

6.2
Part (b):
A
is closed

Proof of (b).

We first show that for every
f\in H
there exists a unique

u\in D(A)
such that

u+Au=f.

Existence
follows from maximal monotonicity:

R(I+A)=H
.

Uniqueness
:
Suppose
u,\bar{u}\in D(A)
both satisfy

u+Au=f
and
\bar{u}+A\bar{u}=f
.
Subtracting gives

(u-\bar{u})+A(u-\bar{u})=0.

Taking the inner product with
u-\bar{u}
yields

\left\lVert u-\bar{u}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\langle A(u-\bar{u}),u-\bar{u}\right\rangle=0.

By monotonicity,
\left\langle A(u-\bar{u}),u-\bar{u}\right\rangle\geq 0
, so

\left\lVert u-\bar{u}\right\rVert^{2}\leq 0
and
u=\bar{u}
.

Thus the map
f\mapsto u
defined by

(I+A)u=f

is well-defined and linear, and we denote it by

(I+A)^{-1}:H\to H
.

Next we show
\left\lVert(I+A)^{-1}\right\rVert\leq 1
.
Let
f\in H
and
u=(I+A)^{-1}f
, so
u+Au=f
.
Taking the inner product with
u
we obtain

\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}+\left\langle Au,u\right\rangle=\left\langle f,u\right\rangle\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert\,\left\lVert u\right\rVert.

Since
\left\langle Au,u\right\rangle\geq 0
(monotonicity), we have

\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert\,\left\lVert u\right\rVert.

If
u\neq 0
, dividing by
\left\lVert u\right\rVert
gives
\left\lVert u\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert
;
if
u=0
the inequality is trivial.
So in all cases

\left\lVert(I+A)^{-1}f\right\rVert=\left\lVert u\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert.

Thus
(I+A)^{-1}
is a bounded linear operator with

\left\lVert(I+A)^{-1}\right\rVert\leq 1
.

We now prove that
A
is closed.
Let
(u_{n})
be a sequence in
D(A)
such that

u_{n}\to u
in
H
and
Au_{n}\to f
in
H
.
We must prove that
u\in D(A)
and
Au=f
.

Since both sequences converge, we have

u_{n}+Au_{n}\longrightarrow u+f\quad\text{in }H.

But for each
n
,

u_{n}=(I+A)^{-1}(u_{n}+Au_{n}).

Using the continuity of
(I+A)^{-1}
we get

u_{n}=(I+A)^{-1}(u_{n}+Au_{n})\longrightarrow(I+A)^{-1}(u+f).

On the other hand
u_{n}\to u
, so by uniqueness of limits

u=(I+A)^{-1}(u+f).

By definition of
(I+A)^{-1}
, this means that
u\in D(A)
and

(I+A)u=u+f.

Thus
u+Au=u+f
and therefore
Au=f
.
This shows that the graph of
A
is closed and hence
A
is a closed
operator.
∎

6.3
Part (c):
(I+\lambda A)^{-1}
exists for all
\lambda>0

Proof of (c).

We have already shown (with
\lambda=1
) that
I+A
is bijective and

(I+A)^{-1}
is bounded with norm at most
1
.
We now prove that the surjectivity of
I+\lambda_{0}A
for some

\lambda_{0}>0
implies the surjectivity of
I+\lambda A
for all

\lambda>\lambda_{0}/2
.

Step 1: set up a fixed point problem.

Assume
R(I+\lambda_{0}A)=H
for some
\lambda_{0}>0
.
Then as in part (b), we know that for every
g\in H
there is a unique

u\in D(A)
solving

u+\lambda_{0}Au=g,

and the map
g\mapsto u
defines a bounded linear operator

(I+\lambda_{0}A)^{-1}
with norm at most
1
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
f\in H
be given.
We want to solve

u+\lambda Au=f.

(6.1)

We rewrite this in terms of
I+\lambda_{0}A
.
Equation (
6.1
) can be rewritten as

u+\lambda_{0}Au=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}f+\Bigl(1-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}\Bigr)u.

Applying
(I+\lambda_{0}A)^{-1}
gives

u=(I+\lambda_{0}A)^{-1}\Bigl[\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}f+\Bigl(1-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}\Bigr)u\Bigr].

(6.2)

Define
T:H\to H
by

T(u)=(I+\lambda_{0}A)^{-1}\Bigl[\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}f+\Bigl(1-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}\Bigr)u\Bigr].

Then (
6.2
) is simply the fixed point equation

u=T(u).

Step 2:
T
is a contraction if
\lambda>\lambda_{0}/2
.

For
u,v\in H
we compute

\displaystyle\left\lVert T(u)-T(v)\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(I+\lambda_{0}A)^{-1}\Bigl(1-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}\Bigr)(u-v)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert(I+\lambda_{0}A)^{-1}\right\rVert\Bigl|1-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}\Bigr|\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl|1-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}\Bigr|\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert,

since
\left\lVert(I+\lambda_{0}A)^{-1}\right\rVert\leq 1
.
Thus
T
is a contraction provided

\Bigl|1-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}\Bigr|<1.

This inequality is equivalent to
\lambda>\lambda_{0}/2
.

Step 3: apply the contraction mapping theorem.

If
\lambda>\lambda_{0}/2
, then
T
is a contraction on the complete
space
H
.
By Theorem
5.2
, there exists a unique fixed point

u\in H
such that
T(u)=u
, i.e.
u
satisfies (
6.2
).
Tracing back the steps shows that
u\in D(A)
and solves

u+\lambda Au=f
.

Thus for every
\lambda>\lambda_{0}/2
the operator
I+\lambda A
is
surjective.
The estimate
\left\lVert(I+\lambda A)^{-1}\right\rVert\leq 1
follows from the same
inner product argument as in part (b), replacing
A
by
\lambda A
.

Step 4: all positive
\lambda
are covered by iteration.

For a maximal monotone operator
A
we know
R(I+A)=H
, i.e.
\lambda_{0}=1
is admissible.
Therefore
R(I+\lambda A)=H
for all
\lambda>1/2
.
Now repeat the argument with
\lambda_{0}=1/2
to obtain surjectivity
for all
\lambda>1/4
, then with
\lambda_{0}=1/4
to get surjectivity
for all
\lambda>1/8
, and so on.
After
n
steps we know that
R(I+\lambda A)=H
for all

\lambda>2^{-n}
.
Given any
\lambda>0
, we can choose
n
such that
2^{-n}<\lambda
,
and hence
I+\lambda A
is surjective.
This proves that
R(I+\lambda A)=H
for every
\lambda>0
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Part (c) also shows that
(I+\lambda A)^{-1}
is nonexpansive:

\left\lVert(I+\lambda A)^{-1}\right\rVert\leq 1
for all
\lambda>0
.
In the nonlinear (maximal monotone) case this family plays the role of
the resolvent and is fundamental in the theory of nonlinear semigroups.

7
Examples and applications

In this final section we illustrate the abstract theory with a few simple examples and one basic PDE application.

7.1
A finite-dimensional example

Let
H=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the standard inner product and let
A
be a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix, i.e.

A=A^{\top},\qquad x^{\top}Ax\geq 0\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then:

•

A
is a bounded linear operator with
D(A)=H
;

•

A
is monotone, because

\left\langle Ax-Ay,x-y\right\rangle=(x-y)^{\top}A(x-y)\geq 0;

•

since
D(A)=H
and we are in finite dimension,
A
is automatically closed.

The operator
I+\lambda A
is invertible for all
\lambda>0
, and its inverse is bounded. In fact, its norm is
\leq 1
if and only if all eigenvalues
\mu
of
A
satisfy
\mu\geq 0
, which is exactly positive semidefiniteness.

This trivial example shows that in finite dimension, maximal monotone linear operators are essentially the same as symmetric positive semidefinite matrices (possibly after a suitable choice of inner product).

7.2
The derivative with Dirichlet boundary conditions

Let
\Omega=(0,1)
and
H=L^{2}(0,1)
. Consider

Au=-\frac{d^{2}u}{dx^{2}}

with domain

D(A)=H^{2}(0,1)\cap H_{0}^{1}(0,1),

i.e. functions which are twice weakly differentiable, vanish at the boundary, and whose second derivative lies in
L^{2}(0,1)
.

Then:

•

A
is densely defined;

•

A
is symmetric and

\left\langle Au,u\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}-u^{\prime\prime}(x)\,u(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}|u^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\,dx\geq 0,

so
A
is monotone;

•

A
is closed (this follows from elliptic regularity and standard Sobolev space theory);

•

A
is in fact maximal monotone, and
-A
generates the heat semigroup on
L^{2}(0,1)
.

In this setting, solving

u+\lambda Au=f

for a given
f\in L^{2}(0,1)
and
\lambda>0
is the same as solving the boundary value problem

\begin{cases}u(x)-\lambda u^{\prime\prime}(x)=f(x),&x\in(0,1),\\
u(0)=u(1)=0.\end{cases}

The abstract theory tells us that this problem has a unique weak solution
u\in D(A)
and that the solution operator

(I+\lambda A)^{-1}:L^{2}(0,1)\to L^{2}(0,1)

is a contraction.

7.3
A resolvent as an elliptic PDE

More generally, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain and consider the operator

Au=-\Delta u

with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions and domain

D(A)=H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

Then
A
is maximal monotone on
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

For
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\lambda>0
, the abstract resolvent equation

u+\lambda Au=f

is equivalent to the elliptic problem

\begin{cases}u(x)-\lambda\Delta u(x)=f(x),&x\in\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The maximal monotone theory guarantees a unique weak solution in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and provides a clean functional analytic framework to study its dependence on
f
and
\lambda
.

7.4
Outlook

The linear theory presented here extends in several directions:

•

to nonlinear maximal monotone operators in Banach spaces, where the resolvent
(I+\lambda A)^{-1}
and its properties play a central role;

•

to abstract evolution equations of the form
u^{\prime}(t)+Au(t)=0
, whose solutions can be constructed using the resolvent and a nonlinear semigroup generated by
-A
;

•

to applications in PDE, including nonlinear diffusion, variational inequalities, Mean Field Games, and Hamilton–Jacobi equations with convex Hamiltonians.

Even in these more advanced settings, the basic mechanisms—monotonicity, closedness, density of the domain, and the contraction mapping theorem—are essentially the same as in the linear Hilbert space case treated in these notes.

Summary.

In this note we have:

•

recalled basic notions about closed sets, orthogonal
complements and density in a Hilbert space;

•

discussed domains, graphs, boundedness and closedness of
operators, with concrete examples;

•

defined monotone and maximal monotone linear operators;

•

stated and proved the contraction mapping theorem; and

•

given detailed proofs of the three key properties in
Proposition
6.1
, together with simple examples and a PDE application.

These tools form the linear functional analytic backbone of the
Hille–Yosida theorem and are also a model for the nonlinear monotone
operator theory used in PDE and Mean Field Games.
