A catalog of gene editing sites and genetic variations in editing sites in model organisms
Authors: Weilong Yang, Jian-Kang Zhu, Wenfei Jin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCRISPR-Cas systems require a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM), which plays an essential role in self/non-self discrimination in their natural context, to cleave DNA for genome editing. Unfortunately, common genetic variation is distributed throughout genomes, which can block recognition of target sites by Cas proteins. However, little information is available about the distribution of editing sites in model organisms and how often common variation overlaps with those PAM sites.ResultsHerein, we characterized six representative Cas proteins (Cas9, Cas12a, Cas12b, Cas12i, Cas12j and Cas12l) genomic editing sites in ten model organisms (yeast, flatworms, flies, zebrafish, mice, humans, rice, maize, Arabidopsis and tomato). We demonstrated that there were more than 34 editing sites per kilobase on average in these genomes. In each genome, 91.69–99.83% and 95.4–99.73% of genes had at least one unique editing site in exon and promoter, respectively. Depending on publicly available genomic diversity data, we identified the variations (SNPs and InDels) in editing sites in humans and rice, indicating the risk in the application of CRISPR/Cas technology. Finally, using CCR5 and BCL11A as examples, we revealed variation site was a factor that must be considered when designing sgRNA.ConclusionsOur findings not only revealed the distribution characteristics of editing sites of six representative Cas proteins in ten model organism genomes but also shed light on the adverse effect of variation sites on target site recognition. Our current work will serve as a reminder of the risks of CRISPR application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11073-9.

Background

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR) systems, coupled with CRISPR-associated (Cas) proteins, have a role as an adaptive immune system against invading nucleic acids in Archaea and bacteria [1]. The CRISPR system has been categorized into 2 classes (class I and class II) and 6 types (type I - type VI) according to the number and composition of Cas proteins participating in nucleic acid interference [2]. For class II, it is further subdivided into 3 types: type II (Cas9), type V (Cas12), and type VI (Cas13). Due to class II CRISPR systems requiring only a single effector protein for nucleic acid cleavage, it is an ideal and smart tool for genome editing [3]. Over the past years, Cas9 and Cas12a have been harnessed in gene editing, transcriptional regulation, base editing, nucleic acid detection, and so on [4, 5]. Furthermore, an increasing number of CRISPR-Cas effectors in class II have been found, such as Cas12b [6], Cas12c [7], Cas12d [8], Cas12e (CasX) [9], Cas12f (Cas14) [10], Cas12g [7], Cas12h [7], Cas12i [7], Cas12j [11], Cas12k [12], Cas12l [13], Cas12m [14], Cas12n [15].

The CRISPR targeting specificity hinges on base pairing between the RNA chimera and the target DNA, and the binding between the Cas protein and protospacer adjacent motif (PAM), which is a short DNA motif commonly found at the 3’ or 5’ end of the target DNA [16, 17]. In naturally occurring CRISPR systems, the PAMs of different Cas proteins vary greatly, with different sequences, lengths, complexities, orientations, and distances from the target [18, 19]. For example, the PAM of Cas9 is 3’ NGG [1]; the PAM of Cas12a is 5’ (T)TTV [20]; the PAM of Cas12b [21] and Cas12i is 5’ TTN [7]; the PAM of Cas12j is 5’ TBN [11]; the PAM of Cas12l is 5’ CCN [13]. Though PAM, as a gatekeeper, distinguishes between itself and invading DNA, it also limits the targeting scope. Therefore, lots of researchers try to reduce the limitation of PAM to editing sites by mining natural Cas orthologs for altered PAM recognition or applying protein engineering to alter PAM recognition [22].

With the development of sequencing technology and the reduction of costs, the genomes of more and more species are being assembled and made public. Furthermore, different types of population genetic variation have been cataloged for humans [23], rice [24], mice [25], Arabidopsis [26] and so on. These SNP and InDel locations are widely distributed in the genome, meaning they may alter genome editing recognition motifs and complicate the application of CRISPR genome editing. Within our understanding, there are no systematic studies on the distribution of common Cas protein editing sites and the effect of variation sites on editing sites [27].

In this study, we identified editing sites of six Cas proteins (Cas9, Cas12a, Cas12b, Cas12i, Cas12j and Cas12l) in ten model organisms (yeast, flatworms, flies, zebrafish, mice, humans, rice, maize, Arabidopsis and tomato). Subsequently, we exhibited the distribution characteristics of editing sites in model organism genomes and the preference of Cas proteins PAM sequences. Finally, we revealed that lots of variation sites are distributed in editing sites, which would cause the application risk of CRISPR.

Results

Distribution of editing sites for six Cas proteins

In order to reveal the distribution characteristics of potential Cas editing sites, six representative Cas proteins (Cas9, Cas12a, Cas12b, Cas12i, Cas12j and Cas12l) were selected to analyze their editing sites in ten common model species (yeast [Saccharomyces cerevisiae], flatworms [Caenorhabditis elegans], flies [Drosophila melanogaster], zebrafish [Danio rerio], mice [Mus musculus], humans [Homo sapiens], rice [Oryza sativa], maize [Zea mays], Arabidopsis [Arabidopsis thaliana] and tomato [Solanum lycopersicum]). Cas9 was selected to represent Cas12l because PAM sequences of SpyCas9 (3’ NGG) and Cas12l/Casπ (5’ CCN) were reverse complementary, and their editing sites were the same. Cas12i was selected to represent Cas12b because the PAM sequences of AaCas12b and Cas12i1 were the same (5’ TTN), and their editing sites were the same. Finally, a total of 590,426–1,353,088,315 editing sites were identified for each Cas in these model organism genomes, of which 22.19–94.34% were unique editing sites (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1 - S3). Interestingly, across the ten genomes, maize had the lowest percentage of unique editing sites (22.19–26.03%) and was much lower than other species (61.77 − 94.34%). Furthermore, we calculated the density of editing sites in the genome, finding it ranges from 34.01 to 518.72 /Kb and 8.86–456.67 /Kb in editing sites and unique editing sites, respectively.

Genomic distribution of Cas9 editing sites in 10 model organisms

To figure out the distribution characteristics of editing sites, the distribution of editing sites and unique editing sites along the genome was calculated and demonstrated (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. S1-S2), indicating its uneven distribution. Next, we got the location of the exon, intron and intergenic regions in each genome based on GTF files and searched the editing sites in them (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. S3a). We found there were significant differences in the distribution of editing sites in genomic features among different species (Fig. 1b). E.g., it was illustrated by about 75% unique editing sites of Cas9 were in exon in yeast, while there were only about 5% unique editing sites in exon in mammalians and plants. On the other hand, about 75% unique editing sites of Cas9 were in the intergenic region in tomato and maize (Fig. 1b). Finally, we explored the distribution of Cas protein editing sites in plus and minus strands in ten model organisms and found the editing sites did not show obvious preferences for plus and minus strands (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. S3b). Similarly, editing sites in exon did not show any bias (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. S3c).

Almost all exons and promoters have editing sites

Since the editing sites in exons and promoters contribute to the potential use of Cas to target genes of interest for editing, we analyze their quantity, distribution and other characteristics. About 93.27–100% of genes contained editing sites in exons, and 91.69–99.83% of genes contained unique editing sites in exons (Table 2, Supplementary Table S4 - S6 and Supplementary information 1). The average number of editing sites in exons per gene ranged from 39 to 1,172 across the tested genomes, with 32–996 unique editing sites in exons per gene. 99.88–100% of gene promoters contained editing sites and 95.40–99.73% of gene promoters contained unique editing sites. The average number of editing sites per gene promoter ranged from 38 to 818, with 28–646 unique editing sites per gene promoter.

The PAM motif was biased in model organism genomes

To investigate whether the PAM motif was biased in model organism genomes, all PAM sequences of six Cas proteins in ten model organisms were identified. Firstly, we analyzed the PAM motif in editing sites and unique editing sites in all tested genomes and found significant bias (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. S9 - S10). Moreover, the proportion of PAM varies greatly among ten model organism genomes. For Cas9 unique editing sites, less than 10% of PAM sequences were CGG in mice and humans, however, more than 20% of PAM sequences were CGG in flatworms, flies, rice and maize (Fig. 2e). When it came to Cas12a unique editing sites, TTTA was the type with the highest proportion (45.12%) in zebrafish, but TTTC was the type with the highest proportion (38.07%) in flatworms. Finally, we analyzed PAM motifs in editing sites in exons and promoters and similar phenomena had also been confirmed (Fig. 2f, g and Supplementary Fig. S9 - S10).

Genetic variations in editing sites

There are many common variants (SNPs and InDels) in the genomes of different individuals in the same species, which potentially would seriously affect the recognition of editing sites by Cas proteins, or even create off-target matches. To reveal the relative positions of variations and editing sites, the genomes of humans and rice were selected as representatives for subsequent analysis. Based on the data from 1000 Genomes Project and the 3 K Rice Project, there were 78,229,218 and 31,990,158 variations in humans and rice, respectively. In humans, about 108 variation sites (SNPs and InDels) overlap with editing sites or unique editing sites, and more than 106 variation sites overlap with editing sites or unique editing sites that overlap with exons or promoters (Fig. 3a). About 40% of editing sites contained variation sites (Fig. 3b). Compared to the human genome, variation sites were less distributed in editing sites in rice, but they were also widespread (Fig. 3c, d).

Then, we explored the number distribution of variation sites contained by editing sites in humans (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. S11a) and rice (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. S11b). The editing sites of all Cas proteins had similar trends: most editing sites contained only one variation site; with the number of variation sites increasing, the corresponding percentage of editing sites decreased. Guide sequences designed by CRISPick contain variation sites

To further analyze the application risk of CRISPR caused by variation sites, two representative genes, CCR5 and BCL11A, were selected. Based on CRISPick, which was widely used to design guide sequences, the top ten candidate Cas9 guide sequences of CCR5 and BCL11A were got and illustrated in gene diagrams (Fig. 4). We found that all designed guide sequences were targeted to unique editing sites. After analyzing the variation sites in these guide sequences, we also visualized them in gene diagrams. There were six guide sequences and two guide sequences in CCR5 and BCL11A containing variation sites, respectively. In CCR5, two of six guide sequences with variation sites even contained two variation sites. Notably, in two guide sequences of CCR5, the variation sites were located in PAM, which might make them ineffective.

Discussion

A large number of editing sites are distributed in the genome

Applying CRISPR for gene editing, “single-guide RNA (sgRNA)” is designed based on editing sites that can be recognized by Cas protein in the target gene region [3, 4, 28, 29]. In the present research, editing sites were widely distributed in the genome, which would facilitate genome-wide genome editing (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. S1 - S2). Fortunately, the exon and promoter regions of the vast majority of genes contained unique editing sites, indicating that they could be edited and regulated precisely (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. S6). What’s more, when the unique editing sites of six Cas proteins were combined, almost all genes were covered in ten model organisms.

In these model organisms, maize had the lowest percentage of unique editing sites (22.19–26.03%), which was much lower than other species (≥ 61.77%) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1 - S3). The above interesting results may be because the proportion of repeat sequences in the maize genome (83.94–85.56%) is much higher than that of other species [30]. However, we didn’t need to worry too much about the inability of maize genes to be specifically recognized by the Cas protein, and subsequent results showed that the percentage of genes that contain no unique editing sites was minimally different from that of other species (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1 - S3), which may be because repetitive sequences are mainly present in non-coding regions.

Variant site is a factor that must be considered in designing sgRNA

Generally, sgRNA designing is based on reference genome sequences, but CRISPR applications are performed in different individuals, which brings a serious challenge to Cas proteins in accurately recognizing target sites. It is well documented that the genomes of individuals of the same species are diverse and variation sites are widely present in genomes [23–26]. In this study, about 40% and 7.5% of editing sites contained variation sites in humans and rice, respectively (Fig. 3b, d). Seriously, variation sites even occur at the PAM sites, which will directly lead to the inability of Cas protein to recognize editing sites and prevent nucleic acid cleavage. Of course, there are also cases where the sites different from the target site in the reference genomes, become extremely similar or even identical due to variation. The analysis of the variation sites in target sites of CCR5 and BCL11A provided a medium to understand the risk in designing sgRNAs (Fig. 4). Therefore, to reduce off-target and avoid causing additional harmful gene editing, sgRNA should be designed based on the information of variation sites, especially in individuals whose genomes are seriously different from reference genomes. Specifically, the guide sequences with variation sites, designed by CRISPick, in CCR5 and BCL11A should be excluded.

How to minimize the risk of variation sites for CRISPR applications? We propose the following solutions: (1) Designing sgRNA should be based on a reference genome that most closely resembles the targeting genome. (2) The information about variation sites can be found through publicly available data, and target site selection should avoid them. (3) When it comes to clinical treatment, the sgRNA of CRISPR drugs targeting different populations needs to be designed individually. Absolutely, it is best to tailor proprietary CRISPR drugs for individuals, if possible.

PAM-free Cas protein needs to be developed

The ever-expanding set of CRISPR technologies and their programmable RNA-guided nucleases show extraordinary flexibility in DNA targeting [22, 31]. However, this flexibility comes with an ever-present fetter: the requirement for a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) flanking each target, which will severely narrow the targeting. Although exon and promoter regions of most genes have unique editing site distributions, all gene coverage cannot be achieved, even if all six Cas protein editing sites are included. Thus, it is necessary to launch a far-reaching expedition for nucleases with relaxed PAM requirements. PAM-free Cas protein can target any sequence in the genome, contributing to all gene regions having editing sites. The more editing sites exist, the easier it is to avoid variation sites while designing sgRNA. Furthermore, it is a well-known fact that select different editing sites in the target region and the results will vary greatly, such as different cutting efficiency, different degrees of gene activation/inhibition, and so on [32–35]. PAM-free will give us more options for editing sites, thus increasing the probability of achieving ideal results.

However, there are some potential drawbacks of a PAM-free nuclease worth considering. About gRNAs expressed from DNA constructs, self-targeting of this DNA would be immediate, unavoidable, and likely disastrous, which highlights the crucial reason why CRISPR-Cas systems evolved PAMs [36, 37]. In addition, a PAM-free nuclease would also be expected to interrogate every sequence in the genome. Such thoroughness in target scanning would cause two issues: extended timescales for the nuclease to find its target and an increased off-targeting possibility [38–40]. Fortunately, an alternative choice has been placed before us: a nuclease repertoire [22]. In short, a collection of nucleases, retaining recognition of a defined PAM, could be explicitly assembled to cover all possible sequences to achieve a collective PAM-free status. Although there are many obstacles to this method, researchers have begun their painstaking exploration [41–43].

Conclusion

In the present study, it was found that editing sites were widely distributed in the genome and most genes have at least one unique editing site. However, variant sites in the genome would seriously affect the recognition of target sites by Cas proteins, reducing the editing efficiency and even leading to off-target. Therefore, we need to be cautious in selecting the appropriate editing site for the application of CRISPR, especially in the clinic.

Methods

The data used in the paper

The genome sequences (FASTA format) and gene annotations (GTF format) files of ten model organisms were downloaded from Ensembl (https://asia.ensembl.org), and the details were listed in Table S7. The genetic variations (all sites where SNPs or InDels were detected) of humans and rice were obtained from the 1000 Genomes Project (https://www.internationalgenome.org) and the 3 K Rice Project (https://snp-seek.irri.org), respectively. In this study, we only analyzed the nuclear DNA.

Identification of editing sites

The editing target locus includes the guide sequence (twenty random base sequences; N20) and the PAM sequences of each Cas protein. Thus, the motifs of Cas9/Cas12l, Cas12a, Cas12b/Cas12i and Cas12j were NNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNGG, TTTVNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNN, TTNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNN and TBNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNNN, respectively. Motif Scraper (v1.0.2) [44], using default parameters, was used to identify the editing target locus of Cas proteins, based on their corresponding motifs. The editing sites, found by the above method, were defined as editing sites. Furthermore, the editing sites, whose sequence without PAM was unlike any other editing sites, were defined as unique editing sites.

Distribution of editing sites

When calculating the distribution density of genomic editing sites, the genomes of zebrafish, mice, humans and maize were segmented into contiguous 200 Kb windows; the genomes of flatworms, flies, rice, Arabidopsis and tomato were segmented into contiguous 50 Kb windows; and the genome of yeast was segmented into contiguous 5 Kb windows. Next, the number of variations in each window would be calculated and their corresponding density would be obtained. Finally, Circos (v0.69-8) [45] was used to display them.

Using the gene annotation files of ten model organisms, the bed files of exon, intron, intergenic and promoter regions were built by a personal Python script. In the study, the 1 Kb region upstream of a gene was considered as the promoter. Next, the bedtools (v2.30.0) [46] was used to identify the editing sites in exon, intron, intergenic and promoter regions.

For the distribution of editing sites on plus and minus strands, relevant information could be obtained directly from the result files generated by Motif Scraper (v1.0.2) [44]. When it came to the distribution of editing sites on coding and template strands, they were grouped by the genes associated with them.

Variation sites analysis

We built the bed files using the location of genetic variations. The bedtools (v2.30.0) [46] was used to identify the variations in editing sites and get the number of variation sites in each editing site. Moreover, a personal Python script was used to analyze the relative position distribution of variation sites in editing sites.

Design guide sequences by CRISPick

CRISPick was used (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/gppx/crispick/public) to design guide sequences of CCR5 and BCL11A. The relevant parameters were as follows: Human GRCh38 (Ensembl v.109), CRISPRko, SpyoCas9(NGG), Chen (2013) tracrRNA, CRISPick Quota = 10.
