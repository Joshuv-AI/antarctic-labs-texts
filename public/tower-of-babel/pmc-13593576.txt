Strengthening Clinical Epidemiology in Somalia: Challenges, Opportunities, and Future Directions
Authors: Ibrahim Abubakar Abdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical epidemiology constitutes a foundational pillar of evidence-based medicine and health system strengthening. This commentary examines the current state of clinical epidemiology in Somalia and is intended to stimulate scholarly dialogue and policy action among Somali and international health stakeholders, situating the discussion within the country’s broader trajectory of health system recovery and evidence-based decision-making. Despite Somalia’s substantial disease burden, clinical epidemiology remains critically underdeveloped, constrained by structural, infrastructural, academic, and governance-related challenges that we delineate in detail. Simultaneously, we identify emerging opportunities — including growing international partnerships, digital health innovations, diaspora engagement, and nascent academic infrastructure — that may catalyse progress toward locally generated research capacity. We argue that deliberate investment in epidemiological capacity, curriculum reform in health sciences education, strengthened national health information systems, and clearer research governance mechanisms are prerequisites for building a robust clinical epidemiology enterprise in Somalia, and we propose priority actions spanning postgraduate training programmes, health information system investment, and the development of a nationally owned research agenda.Keywords: clinical epidemiology, Somalia, health systems, capacity building, evidence-based medicine, public health, global health

Introduction

Clinical epidemiology — the application of epidemiological principles and methods to improve clinical practice and patient outcomes — has emerged as an indispensable discipline in contemporary medicine.1 It underpins diagnostic accuracy, treatment efficacy evaluation, prognosis determination, and health policy formulation, thereby forming the intellectual backbone of evidence-based medicine.2 In high-income countries, well-resourced clinical epidemiology infrastructure has enabled the systematic generation of clinical evidence, the conduct of large-scale clinical trials, and the development of clinical practice guidelines that inform care standards.3

In stark contrast, Somalia occupies one of the most fragile health system positions globally. Decades of civil conflict, institutional collapse, recurring humanitarian crises, and persistent poverty have decimated health infrastructure and derailed academic and research capacity.4 The country continues to grapple with high rates of communicable diseases, maternal and child mortality, malnutrition, and an emerging burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), all of which demand robust epidemiological inquiry.5 Within this fragile environment, clinical epidemiology has correspondingly failed to take root as an institutionalised discipline, a gap explored in detail in the following section.

Despite these challenges, Somalia is at a transformative juncture. The gradual restoration of federal governance, growth of private medical universities, renewed international engagement, and the proliferation of digital technologies present unprecedented opportunities for clinical epidemiology development.6 This commentary synthesises current knowledge, identifies systemic barriers, and proposes forward-looking strategies to strengthen clinical epidemiology in Somalia. It is directed at Somali health academics, policymakers, international partners, and the wider global health community.

This commentary draws on peer-reviewed literature indexed in PubMed and Scopus, Somalia-specific reports from WHO and UNICEF, Federal Ministry of Health strategic and policy documents, and grey literature from international agencies and Somali academic institutions. Sources were identified through targeted rather than systematic searches, and are synthesised narratively; where the underlying evidence is not Somalia-specific, this is noted explicitly to avoid overstating the strength of the available data.

Current State of Clinical Epidemiology in Somalia

Somalia’s epidemiological landscape is characterised by a critical paucity of locally generated clinical data. Most available health statistics are derived from humanitarian organisation reports, cross-sectional surveys conducted by international non-governmental organisations (NGOs), or modelled estimates produced by agencies such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNICEF.5,7 While these sources provide valuable population-level data, they inadequately substitute for institution-based clinical epidemiology research that captures longitudinal patient outcomes, disease natural history, and healthcare utilisation patterns.8

The few existing research outputs from Somalia are predominantly descriptive epidemiological studies — prevalence surveys of infectious diseases, malnutrition assessments, and maternal mortality analyses.9 Analytical epidemiological studies, including cohort studies, case-control designs, and clinical trials, remain scarce in the domestic literature. This impression is drawn from the authors’ review of Somalia-focused publications rather than a formal systematic review, and should be read as indicative rather than definitive. Regardless, the pattern reflects not an absence of scientific curiosity among Somali health professionals, but rather the systemic constraints — detailed in the following section — that prevent robust research conduct.

Academic institutions represent nascent but expanding nodes of potential. Universities such as Al Hayat Medical University, SIMAD University’s health sciences faculty, and Benadir University have inaugurated undergraduate and postgraduate health sciences programmes that incorporate elements of epidemiology and biostatistics.10 However, the depth and rigour of epidemiological training across these institutions remains variable, and faculty with advanced research training are in short supply. Publications emanating from Somali institutions in Scopus-indexed or PubMed-indexed journals remain limited, though the trend is gradually improving.11

Challenges to Clinical Epidemiology Development

These constraints operate across five interlocking domains — data infrastructure, human resources, funding and ethics governance, security, and equity of participation — each considered in turn below.

Health System Fragility and Data Infrastructure Deficits

Somalia’s health information system (HIS) remains one of the weakest in sub-Saharan Africa.8 Health Management Information System (HMIS) data coverage is incomplete, and vital registration systems — including civil registration of births, deaths, and causes of mortality — are largely non-functional in much of the country. Without reliable routine data, the foundational inputs for epidemiological analysis are absent. Clinical record systems in hospitals are predominantly paper-based, non-standardised, and poorly archived, rendering retrospective clinical epidemiology studies methodologically untenable.12

The lack of a functioning disease surveillance infrastructure further compounds the problem. Somalia has historically reported delayed detection of epidemic-prone diseases such as cholera, measles, and acute watery diarrhoea, partly because community-based and facility-based sentinel surveillance systems are inadequately operationalised.13,14 An evaluation of Somalia’s national Early Warning and Response Network (EWARN) between 2017 and 2020 found that alert verification improved from only 2.0% to 52.6% over the study period, illustrating both the scale of historical surveillance weaknesses and the trajectory of recent improvement; nonetheless, incomplete reporting and limited integration with other health information subsystems continued to constrain the timeliness of outbreak detection.14 These are not abstract system deficiencies: delayed alert verification and incomplete reporting translate directly into delayed clinical recognition of outbreak-related presentations, complicate case-management planning at facility level, and can worsen patient outcomes by postponing the mobilisation of isolation, rehydration, and vaccination resources.

Academic and Human Resource Constraints

The production of clinical epidemiologists requires sustained academic investment across multiple educational tiers. Somalia currently lacks a dedicated postgraduate programme in clinical epidemiology or biostatistics. The majority of health sciences graduates receive only introductory exposure to epidemiological methods during undergraduate training, without the advanced instruction needed to design, conduct, and analyse rigorous clinical studies.15

Faculty with doctoral-level training in epidemiology are exceedingly rare in Somali universities. Brain drain — both to conflict and to economic migration — has depleted the pool of qualified academic researchers. Many Somali health professionals who pursue advanced training abroad do not return, creating a persistent intellectual diaspora and a corresponding net loss of research capacity within domestic institutions.16 We return to the question of whether, and how, this loss might be partially offset through structured diaspora engagement in the Opportunities section below.

Research Funding and Ethics Infrastructure

Research funding within Somalia is almost entirely externally dependent. The government allocates negligible budgetary resources to health research, and domestic philanthropic funding for academic medicine is non-existent. Health research that does occur is typically grant-funded by international organisations such as the Wellcome Trust, the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation, or United States Government agencies, and is often driven by external research agendas rather than locally defined priorities.17

Ethical oversight of clinical and epidemiological research is similarly underdeveloped. While a national research ethics committee has been established under the Ministry of Health, institutional review boards (IRBs) at individual universities are either nascent or non-functional. The absence of robust ethics infrastructure creates barriers to conducting multi-site clinical studies and limits Somalia’s ability to participate as a full partner in international research consortia.18

Political Instability and Insecurity

Persistent political instability and intermittent armed conflict continue to disrupt academic activities and healthcare delivery. Research infrastructure is particularly vulnerable — data collection instruments may be destroyed, study populations displaced, and research personnel endangered during conflict episodes.19 The resulting discontinuity undermines longitudinal research designs and erodes institutional memory. Insecurity also deters international researchers and funding bodies from establishing long-term research partnerships in Somalia, limiting knowledge transfer and collaborative research.

Gender and Equity Disparities in Research Participation

Gender disparities significantly affect both the conduct and findings of clinical epidemiological research in Somalia. Women’s participation in research — both as investigators and as study participants — is constrained by sociocultural norms, restricted mobility, and limited access to higher education.20 This underrepresentation means that women’s health conditions, including maternal morbidity, reproductive health outcomes, and gender-specific NCD profiles, are systematically underinvestigated. Building a more inclusive research culture is essential for generating evidence that is representative of the entire Somali population.

Opportunities for Strengthening Clinical Epidemiology

Expanding Higher Education Ecosystem

The rapid proliferation of private health sciences universities in Mogadishu and other urban centres represents a significant structural opportunity. As of the mid-2020s, more than twenty accredited health sciences institutions operate in Somalia, serving a student population that has grown substantially over the preceding decade.21 These institutions constitute potential incubators of epidemiological capacity if curricular reform — specifically the integration of research methods, biostatistics, and evidence appraisal — is systematically pursued. The establishment of inter-university research consortia could pool scarce resources and facilitate multi-site studies.

Digital Health and mHealth Technologies

Somalia’s remarkably high mobile phone penetration rate — one of the highest in sub-Saharan Africa, driven in large part by the widespread use of mobile money platforms — offers a transformative entry point for digital epidemiology.22 Mobile health (mHealth) applications, electronic data capture systems using platforms such as ODK Collect and REDCap, and short message service (SMS)-based surveillance systems have demonstrated feasibility in comparable low-resource settings.23

The integration of digital tools into clinical data collection could substantially accelerate the transition from paper-based records to standardised electronic health records (EHRs). Telemedicine platforms simultaneously provide opportunities for clinical outcome tracking and patient follow-up, which are foundational requirements for prospective epidemiological studies.24

Diaspora Engagement and South–South Collaboration

The Somali diaspora, concentrated in North America, Europe, and the Gulf states, includes a significant cohort of health professionals and academics.25 As noted above, the outflow of this diaspora represents a substantial loss of domestic research capacity; it also constitutes a potential resource where diaspora engagement strategies are effectively implemented.16,25 Structured diaspora engagement programmes — such as short-term visiting faculty schemes, co-supervised postgraduate research, and diaspora grant programmes — have been piloted in analogous settings in Ethiopia and Ghana, where translocal academic linkages have been documented to help curtail the effects of brain drain on African higher education.26 The revived Somali–Swedish research cooperation, built on an alliance of Somali universities, Swedish universities, and Somali diaspora professionals, offers a Somalia-specific precedent for this model.16

South–South collaboration with African institutions that have achieved greater epidemiological capacity — such as those in Kenya, Ethiopia, Uganda, and South Africa — offers culturally proximate and contextually relevant models for capacity building. Regional bodies such as the African Field Epidemiology Network (AFENET) provide structured training pathways, including Field Epidemiology Training Programmes (FETP), which are directly applicable to Somalia’s epidemiological workforce development needs.27

International Research Partnerships

International academic partnerships present substantive opportunities for technology transfer, joint research funding, and capacity development. Several Somali universities have established memoranda of understanding (MoUs) with universities in Turkey, Malaysia, and the United Kingdom. Comparable joint-degree and twinning partnerships between universities in low- and high-income countries — such as the Karolinska Institutet–Makerere University collaboration in global health research — illustrate how such arrangements can be structured to build sustained local research capacity rather than one-off technical assistance.28 Deepening Somali partnerships to include joint research design, data-sharing agreements, and collaborative publishing in indexed journals would materially advance Somali epidemiological output. The African Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) and WHO Somalia Country Office are also pivotal institutional partners for disease surveillance capacity building.29

Post-Conflict Reconstruction and Governance Momentum

The gradual stabilisation of federal governance structures in Somalia has created enabling conditions for health sector reform. The Federal Ministry of Health has articulated health sector strategic plans that acknowledge the importance of health information and research.6 The Somali Health Sector Strategic Plan provides a framework within which clinical epidemiology capacity building can be embedded. International donors and development partners are increasingly channelling support through government systems rather than parallel structures, creating opportunities for sustainable HIS strengthening.30

Future Directions and Recommendations

Curriculum Reform in Health Sciences Education

A systematic revision of undergraduate and postgraduate health sciences curricula across Somali universities is urgently needed. Epidemiology and biostatistics modules should be restructured to emphasise analytical and clinical epidemiology, research design, critical appraisal of clinical evidence, and the use of statistical software such as STATA, SPSS, or R.31 These competencies form the minimum methodological foundation for producing graduates capable of conducting and appraising clinical epidemiological research. The Somali Higher Education Commission should play a coordinating role in harmonising epidemiology teaching standards across institutions.

Establishment of Postgraduate Epidemiology Programmes

The establishment of a dedicated Master of Science programme in Clinical Epidemiology or Public Health Research within at least one Somali university should be treated as a national priority. Such a programme — ideally developed in partnership with a peer institution in a higher-resource setting — would serve as the primary pipeline for producing domestic epidemiological researchers. The programme should incorporate thesis research requirements with fieldwork components to ensure graduates are capable of independent inquiry.32

Strengthening the National Health Information System

Sustainable clinical epidemiology requires reliable data infrastructure. Investment in the HMIS — specifically expanding facility reporting coverage, standardising data collection instruments, and transitioning to electronic platforms — is a prerequisite for analytical epidemiology at scale.33 Somalia’s Essential Package of Health Services and its current Health Sector Strategic Plan already provide a national policy framework that prioritises HMIS standardisation and the reuse of routine health data for research and evidence-based policymaking; the immediate priority is consistent implementation and financing of these existing commitments rather than the creation of new frameworks.6 The national disease surveillance system, including EWARN and the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) strategy, should be expanded to include sentinel hospital surveillance for priority conditions, including acute respiratory infections, viral haemorrhagic fevers, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), and NCDs, building on the documented improvements in reporting timeliness achieved since 2017.14 A functioning national death registry, linked to cause-of-death ascertainment, would provide invaluable longitudinal epidemiological data.34

Development of a National Research Agenda

Somalia currently lacks a nationally endorsed health research agenda. The articulation of a national health research framework — identifying priority research questions, allocating resources, and establishing accountability mechanisms — would galvanise domestic and international research investment.35 Such a framework should be developed through an inclusive consultative process that incorporates academic institutions, the Ministry of Health, civil society, and affected communities. Priority areas should include: the epidemiology of NCDs in urban populations; maternal and perinatal mortality determinants; antimicrobial resistance patterns; mental health burden in post-conflict settings; and the nutritional epidemiology of children under five.36 Comparable research agenda-setting exercises in other fragile and post-conflict African states have relied on phased implementation — beginning with a small number of costed priority questions before expanding scope — which may offer a more feasible starting model for Somalia than an immediately comprehensive agenda.

Strengthening Research Ethics Infrastructure

The operationalisation of functional IRBs at major Somali universities is essential for enabling ethically governed clinical research. IRB members should receive formal training in research ethics, Good Clinical Practice (GCP), and the specific ethical challenges of research in post-conflict and humanitarian settings.37 National research ethics guidelines should be developed and enforced, and Somalia should engage with the WHO’s ethical standards for research in developing countries to ensure international alignment.

Leveraging Open-Access Publishing and Scientific Communication

Somali researchers face significant barriers to publishing in high-impact indexed journals, including article processing charges (APCs), limited access to scientific literature, and inadequate academic writing support; these barriers disproportionately affect researchers across low- and middle-income countries more broadly and are not unique to Somalia.38,39 Open-access platforms — particularly those offering APC waivers for low-income country authors, such as those provided by Dovepress, BioMed Central, and PLOS journals — offer accessible publication pathways. University libraries should invest in institutional subscriptions to research databases such as Hinari, and academic writing workshops should be incorporated into postgraduate training programmes.

Conclusion

Clinical epidemiology remains a largely underdeveloped discipline in Somalia, constrained by intersecting structural, academic, and governance challenges that reflect the country’s prolonged experience of fragility. However, the emerging higher education ecosystem, digital health infrastructure, diaspora resources, and renewed international partnerships collectively constitute a foundation upon which a meaningful clinical epidemiology enterprise can be constructed.

Progress will require deliberate, coordinated action: curriculum reform to embed epidemiological competencies in health sciences graduates; investment in postgraduate research training; a functional national health information system built on Somalia’s existing EPHS and Health Sector Strategic Plan commitments rather than parallel new structures; a nationally owned research agenda, phased and costed to be implementable within current resource constraints; and an ethical oversight framework that meets international standards. These milestones require distinct but complementary contributions: government leadership in financing and policy coordination, universities in curriculum reform and postgraduate supervision, civil society in holding these commitments to account, and international partners in providing technical and financial support aligned with, rather than parallel to, Somali priorities.

As Somalia continues on its trajectory of recovery and institution-building, clinical epidemiology must be positioned not as a luxury of well-resourced health systems, but as a foundational necessity for understanding the country’s disease burden, guiding clinical practice, and building an evidence-based health system that serves all Somali people. This commentary is intended as a contribution to the scholarly conversation that must precede and accompany that transformation.
