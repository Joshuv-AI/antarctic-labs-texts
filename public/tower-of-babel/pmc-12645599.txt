Differences in contributing factors to diagnostic errors between physicians and allied health professionals: a nationwide analysis in Japan
Authors: Ikuo Shimizu, Kiyoshi Shikino, Yukinori Harada, Masaru Kurihara, Kazumi Tanaka, Yuichi Masuda, Satoshi Watanuki, Yuko Ohishi, Tomoko Nakazato, Kosuke Ishizuka, Mamoru Komatsu, Taiju Miyagami, Toshinori Nishizawa, Ryo Nishimura, Toshihiko Oshita, Tomoharu Suzuki, Taro Shimizu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDiagnostic errors significantly contribute to adverse events, patient harm and malpractice claims. While most research focuses on physicians, allied health professionals (AHPs) also play a critical role in diagnostic processes. Existing studies, primarily based on medical-record reviews, often overlook communication gaps and team dynamics affecting diagnostic accuracy. This study aims to (1) describe the overall characteristics and patterns of diagnostic errors reported in the national adverse-event database and (2) compare the clinical features, diagnostic processes and contributing factors between cases involving physicians and those involving AHPs.MethodsWe examined diagnostic-error reports from 1631 hospitals in the Japan Council for Quality Healthcare database (2010–2023). Cases were identified using predefined keywords, and two independent physicians assessed them using the Revised Safer Dx Instrument and Diagnostic Error Evaluation and Research (DEER) taxonomy. Contributing factors were analysed, and statistical methods explored error patterns and stakeholder comparisons.ResultsAmong 147 654 event reports, 445 cases involved diagnostic errors. The most common errors occurred in diagnostic testing, assessment and follow-up, particularly misinterpretation of pathological or radiological reports on new injuries during hospitalisation. Physicians were the primary responsible providers (79.3%), while nurses were involved in 19.3% of cases. DEER taxonomy analysis revealed that nurses were more associated with errors in presentation, history-taking and physical examination (p<0.001). Additionally, patient-related factors, observation, reporting and training played a significantly greater role in errors involving nurses than physicians (p<0.001).DiscussionBy leveraging expert-assessed unstructured data, this study provides a broader perspective on diagnostic safety. Findings highlight the critical role of AHPs in diagnostic errors. Our study underscores the need for targeted, profession-specific interventions embedded within broader interprofessional initiatives to effectively reduce diagnostic errors and enhance patient safety.Keywords: Diagnostic errors, Patient safety, Incident reporting

Introduction

Diagnostic error is defined as ‘diagnoses that are unintentionally delayed (sufficient information was available earlier), wrong (another diagnosis was made before the correct one) or missed (no diagnosis was ever made), as judged from the eventual appreciation of more definitive information.’1,3 It is one of the main contributors to adverse events in medicine and a contributing factor in serious events such as death or preventable intensive care admissions. Furthermore, diagnostic error has long been recognised as a major contributor to malpractice claims.4 5

The impact of diagnostic errors on patient harm and patient safety is greater than that of other types of errors.6 7 Although attention to diagnostic errors has increased in recent decades, research has mainly focused only on physicians’ diagnostic outcomes, and the impact on overall care has not been fully explored.8 This is largely because, historically and legally, diagnosis is considered the physician’s responsibility.9 Meanwhile, much of patient care—excluding treatment and invasive examination—has naturally been the responsibility of allied health professionals (AHPs) as part of the legal definitions of their respective scopes. Therefore, diagnostic errors cannot be separated from the responsibilities of other professions.9 This aligns with the need to recognise the diagnostic contributions in clinical practice situations.10 First, diagnostic reasoning is embedded in the routine tasks of many AHPs, even if framed as ‘triage’ or ‘functional assessment’.11 12 Second, AHPs gather and communicate clinical and laboratory data essential to diagnosis, and failures to integrate this information can lead to errors. Finally, AHPs act on diagnostic decisions; if those decisions are not accurately reflected in care delivery, patient outcomes may be compromised.

Most existing studies on diagnostic errors comprise cognitive autopsies, such as physicians’ medical record reviews.13 14 However, concerns have been raised that such extant studies do not adequately detect communication gaps and team dynamics issues. Therefore, these methods fail to provide a comprehensive understanding of diagnostic errors in the entire care process.15 16 Furthermore, a critical missing element for advancing such research is epidemiological data, which must be further developed and strengthened.17

Currently, major Japanese hospitals are required by law to analyse serious adverse events, identify their root causes and then register them in a national database.18 This database, managed by the Japan Council for Quality Healthcare (JQ), is available free of charge.19 A serious adverse event, in this context, refers to an unintended or unexpected event resulting from medical management that causes temporary or permanent disability, death or the need for long-term care. Furthermore, these hospitals are encouraged to proactively and voluntarily register minor events and near misses in this database. These reports include extensive analysis of the event’s background, causes and involved stakeholders. Previous studies using these reports have shown that these reports are suitable for analysing patient safety events, including diagnostic errors.20 21 Running in parallel with the JQ system, the Medical Accident Investigation System (MAIS) was introduced in 2015, mandating hospitals to report all unexpected deaths related to medical care to a separate third-party organisation, the Japan Medical Safety Research Organisation.22 Unlike the JQ system, which is publicly available and covers adverse events and near misses, MAIS focuses exclusively on unexpected fatal events, regardless of whether an accident was identified, and its database is not publicly accessible. For these reasons, MAIS was not used in the present study.

This study had two primary aims: (1) to describe the characteristics and contributing factors of diagnostic errors reported in a national adverse-event database and (2) to compare errors involving physicians with those involving AHPs, to clarify the role of AHPs in diagnostic safety.

Methods

Study design

The study included event and near-miss reports reported by Japanese hospitals. We included all case reports from the JQ database for the period available at the time of conducting the study (fiscal year (FY) 2010 to the first half of FY 2023).

Data sources

Under the Medical Care Act, advanced treatment hospitals (276 hospitals in total), such as university hospitals and hospitals managed by the National Hospital Organisation, are required to anonymously register serious adverse events with the Project to Collect Medical Near-Miss/Adverse Event Information organised by the JQ.19 In addition, 1631 hospitals that voluntarily participated in this project, including public and private hospitals, were also contributing reports at the end of March 202323 (63.8% of the 2555 member hospitals of the Japan Hospital Association). Participation in the project is mandatory only for designated advanced-treatment hospitals, while other institutions participate voluntarily. However, participation is generally encouraged as part of national quality and safety-improvement efforts and is expected in hospital accreditation processes in Japan. Participating facilities also voluntarily report near-miss cases. Registered case reports are prepared by certified patient safety managers with experience in the statutory training in event-analysis methods, based on event reports submitted at each institution. Although the specific analytical method is not standardised across institutions, hospitals are expected to conduct internal investigations, typically involving some form of root-cause analysis, to summarise events and contributing factors to submit case reports to the JQ database. Event reports are anonymised and registered in a database. Case reports in the JQ’s database comprise unstructured, free-text reports and structured information, the latter including patient gender, age, setting, patient conditions, primary responsible provider occupation, situation (eg, medication, blood transfusion, treatment, medical equipment, surgical drain, examination and medical care), severity, complications and contributing factors of error. Patient condition categories (eg, impaired consciousness, bed rest and limb impairment) are predefined by the JQ to reflect physical and cognitive vulnerability. The registered information is available on the JQ website.19 In Japan, hospital care includes both inpatient and outpatient services. Thus, in this study, hospitals are viewed as comprehensive systems including laboratory and imaging services as well as interprofessional collaboration, and the reports cover both inpatient and outpatient clinical scenarios.

We searched the free-text portions of the case reports for keywords agreed upon by the researchers and extracted those containing one or more of these keywords in Japanese. The translated keywords were as follows: misdiagnosis, miss, error, notification (of diagnosis), diagnostic error, diagnosis+delay, diagnosis+communication, diagnosis+report+check+lack, diagnosis+delay and examination/test+delay. While both systems contribute to patient safety reporting in Japan, only the JQ database was used in this study.

Diagnostic error evaluation

We conducted a two-stage analysis of the selected cases. Each case was assigned to two physicians (one with experience in patient-safety management and the other with expertise in diagnosis), who received instruction on the analysis method and then assumed the role of assessor. The use of assessments by two physicians is a common approach in patient-safety research and has proven to be sufficiently reliable in identifying diagnostic errors. The approach has been demonstrated to be sufficiently reliable in identifying diagnostic errors.1 2 To finalise the ruling, both assessors had to agree on the entire ruling assessment; in the event of disagreement between the two assessors, three researchers (IS, KS and TS) discussed the disagreement and decided thereon in a consensus meeting.

In the first stage, the assessors were screened for diagnostic errors based on the information registered and the free-text descriptions. The second stage was graded using established tools such as the Revised Safer Dx instrument and Diagnostic Error Evaluation and Research (DEER) taxonomy.

Revised Safer Dx instrument

The Revised Safer Dx instrument24 is an assessment tool used to determine whether a diagnostic error has occurred. It comprises 12 items that focus on specific aspects of the diagnostic process and one remaining (final) item to determine whether a diagnostic error has occurred. All items are rated on a seven-point scale (1=strongly disagree, 4=neutral, 7=strongly agree). Following the instrument’s recommendations and guidelines based on previous studies, we used the final item of the instrument, considering a score of 4 or higher to indicate a diagnostic error. If two assessors’ ratings differed on the final item, as in the first step, three investigators (IS, KS and TS) discussed the discrepancy and reached a consensus in a meeting.

DEER taxonomy

The DEER taxonomy2 classifies diagnostic errors based on seven commonly accepted diagnostic processes (ie access to care, history taking, physical examination, diagnostic examination, assessment, consultation/referral and follow-up). The DEER taxonomy is frequently used in various healthcare disciplines and settings to classify where errors occurred in the diagnostic process and what went wrong. We employed DEER for comparison with the original factorial data. We employed items where at least one or more people judged the defect to be suspicious.

Statistical analysis and reporting

Standard descriptive statistics were used to summarise the cases included in the reports, covering the year of occurrence, gender, age group, care status (inpatient or outpatient), patient condition, outcome, department of clinical specialties, disease, primary responsible provider occupation and factors. The designation of the data was based on structured data fields within the JQ database, which are determined by the internal analysis conducted at each reporting institution. Our study did not reinterpret or reassign provider responsibility beyond what was submitted in the original reports. In the Japanese speciality system, all physicians experience a basic speciality training before choosing a subspecialty. Therefore, in this study, subspecialties were integrated into their corresponding basic specialties for the purpose of department classification. Surgical departments were divided into general surgery, orthopaedics and specialised surgical fields (eg otolaryngology, urology and plastic surgery), which differ in clinical focus and training structure. These were separately analysed because of their distinct diagnostic workflows and potential error patterns. Standard descriptive statistics included counts, percentages and means for each data item, with means and percentages used for continuous or count data, respectively. Diseases were classified using the broad categories of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th revision (ICD-10).25 Medical or surgical complications were analysed separately from other external causes. The latter included events classified under ICD-10 Chapter XX, such as accidental falls, transport injuries and environmental incidents. In contrast, complications were classified under Chapter XIX or Y40–Y84. New-onset trauma during hospitalisation (eg, fracture, dislocation or head contusion due to a fall) and failure to review imaging or pathological reports were considered as specific events. Factors were divided by occupation and compared. Processes in which diagnostic errors occurred were explored in relation to background factors using the DEER taxonomy. Contributing factors such as knowledge, observation, reporting and patient-related issues were extracted from the structured data fields in the JQ database, which are classified by patient safety managers at each institution based on predefined categories. Because these contributing factors are based on institution-level analyses and are not systematically aligned with the DEER taxonomy categories, we analysed them separately rather than integrating them into the taxonomy-based evaluation.

A two-tailed criterion was applied to assess significance, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. A χ2 test was employed for categorical-data comparisons of the binary data to measure effect sizes. Cramér’s V was used to measure effect sizes, where values of 0.10, 0.30 and 0.50 corresponded to small, moderate and large effects, respectively. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was employed for correlation analysis of binary data. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (IBM) V.29. This manuscript follows the Reporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely collected health Data (RECORD) guidelines.26

Results

Patient characteristics and diagnostic outcomes

In the period available in the JQ database (2010 to the first half of 2023), 147 654 case reports were found. Of these, 1524 cases were extracted by keywords. After analysis by the assessors, 1060 cases were judged to be eligible for further review. After excluding cases in which the assessors’ average rating on the final item of the Revised SaferDx was below an average of 3.5 and the case was not judged as a diagnostic error in the consensus meeting, a final total of 445 cases was included in this study (figure 1).

When the main diagnoses were classified by ICD-10, malignant or benign neoplasms (33.3%) were the most common, followed by neurological (14.8%) and cardiovascular (10.8%) diseases. Inadequate reading of pathological or radiological reports was found in 67 cases (15.1%) and was related to tumours. New injuries such as fractures and dislocations following traumatic events such as falls were present in 80 cases (18.0%). Patient background comorbidities included physical impairment (32.8%), cognitive impairment (20.7%) and sensory impairment (3.1%).

The average age group of patients in diagnostic-error cases was 70–79 years, and 57.5% were male (table 2). The dominant care type was inpatient (71.7%). The most frequently cited factors were decision-making (56.6%), checking (46.1%), collaboration (37.5%), observation (29.7%) and reporting (28.5%). Ultimately, 14.6% of patients died as a result of events related to diagnostic errors, and many patients suffered permanent (44.5%) or temporary (17.1%) disability.

DEER categories and comparison between physician and nurse

DEER, Diagnostic Error Evaluation and Research.

Physicians (79.3%) were the most frequently identified main responsible providers in diagnostic errors, followed by nurses (19.3%), who constituted the majority among AHPs. The remaining professions (7.4%) comprised other medical professionals (therapists and technicians) (table 1). In the DEER analysis, cases in which nurses were the primary responsible providers showed a significantly greater impact on multiple items in the physical-examination and lab-tests phases compared with those in which physicians were the primary responsible providers (table 3). A comparison of circumstances and contributing factors between physicians and nurses revealed that significantly more nurses than physicians were involved in cases in which patient comorbidity was present. Among nurses, contributing factors were significantly more often related to observation, reporting, knowledge, training and patient-related issues (table 4). These domains were selected from predefined categories in the JQ database, as classified by patient safety managers at the reporting institutions. These patterns were also reflected in representative cases. For example, a hospitalised patient with dementia experienced a fall; however, due to limited physical assessment and failure to recognise pain behaviours, the nurse did not suspect a fracture, which was only diagnosed after a significant delay. In another case, a nurse observed clinical deterioration but delayed reporting it. These cases illustrate that errors arose from deficits in observation, knowledge and communication—rather than failures in diagnostic reasoning per se.

Comparison of prevalent contributing factors between physician and nurse

The correlation analysis between DEER scores and contributing factors did not reveal any relationships exceeding a moderate level (ρ>0.400) in the overall group or within the physician subgroup. However, in the subgroup analysis focusing on nurses, deviation from rules was moderately correlated to failed/delayed reporting of results to clinicians (ρ=0.593), failed/delayed follow-up of (abnormal) test results (ρ=0.494) and failure/delay in recognising or weighing complications (ρ=0.423).

Discussion

This study identified 445 diagnostic error cases among adverse event reports from a national database, revealing that nearly one-third of these errors resulted in moderate to severe patient harm. Notably, while physicians were responsible for the majority of cases, nearly one-fifth involved nurses, highlighting the need to consider their roles in diagnostic safety efforts. These errors frequently originated from failures in diagnostic testing and clinical assessment. Such patterns emphasise that diagnostic errors are not confined to decision-making by physicians alone but are deeply rooted in broader interprofessional workflows and system factors. Our results collect diagnostic-error cases from a different perspective than that adopted in these previous studies. First, a large database of unstructured event information has not previously been the subject of investigation in large-scale diagnostic-error studies, whereas the addition of experienced expert assessment makes this possible in this study. Second, more accurate information may have been collected as many institutions began mandatory reporting of serious complications under the 2015 MAIS policy. The increase in reports after 2015 likely reflects this mandate, though it does not necessarily imply better detection of diagnostic errors, since the system was not designed specifically to capture them. While the adverse event reporting system is not generally designed for diagnostic errors, it has advantages for collecting diagnostic safety information.27 Serious events are of critical importance in patient-safety measures, and our results provide immediately useful insights for facilities and administrative bodies focused on resolving these events. Third, by identifying diagnostic errors in which AHPs, especially nurses, were involved, we demonstrated that diagnostic errors are not limited to physicians’ decisions but emerge from team-based clinical practice. This allowed us to reconceptualise diagnostic errors as problems of the diagnostic process within interprofessional teamwork.

Building hereon, our findings suggest that diagnostic errors involving AHPs, particularly nurses, differ in meaningful ways from those primarily attributed to physicians. While physician-related errors were more frequently linked to cognitive processes such as faulty reasoning or premature closure, AHP-related errors more commonly stemmed from process-based factors, including missed monitoring, unreported changes in patient condition or delayed communication. These patterns reflect the distinct roles and responsibilities of AHPs in the diagnostic process, which in Japan are further shaped by legal and institutional constraints.28 Nurses are legally mandated to act under physician instruction, which restricts their independent decision-making and may limit their ability to escalate diagnostic concerns, even when abnormalities are recognised.

This distinction aligns with the broader concept that diagnostic reasoning is not solely an internal cognitive activity but one that is distributed across individuals, teams and systems.29 Previous studies have also noted that while physicians typically engage in hypothetical-deductive reasoning, nurses tend to use inductive reasoning.30 Furthermore, nurses have been shown to exhibit higher situational awareness compared with physicians, which may enhance early detection of subtle clinical deterioration when appropriate escalation mechanisms are in place.31

In this study, rule deviations were more frequently observed in cases involving nurses, particularly during the reporting and follow-up phases of the diagnostic process. These categories, based on internal hospital reviews, likely reflect local judgments concerning task omissions or unclear role assignments. Notably, failure or delay in scheduling referrals or follow-ups was the most common contributing factor in nurse-led diagnostic errors (36%) and demonstrated the largest effect size among all variables (V=0.216). Such delays may indicate missed escalation or communication failures.

This pattern suggests that system-level breakdowns in communication and coordination—rather than deficiencies in clinical reasoning—play a central role when nurses serve as the primary responsible providers. Such breakdowns may arise from ambiguous delegation of responsibility, inadequate escalation protocols or over-reliance on informal handoffs in inpatient settings, and they should not be viewed as isolated individual failures but as broader systemic challenges in communication pathways, role clarity and interdisciplinary supervision. Addressing these challenges requires structured follow-up systems, clearer task-ownership frameworks and diagnostic safety strategies that more effectively integrate AHPs into decision-making structures through escalation protocols, feedback mechanisms and interprofessional education. Interprofessional learning has been shown to enhance patient safety and support more effective team-based diagnostic processes.32

Additionally, diagnostic errors by AHPs frequently involved deteriorating patient conditions during hospitalisation or coordination failures across departments. These findings identify specific targets for intervention and reinforce the importance of recognising diagnostic responsibility as a shared endeavour. In Japan, interprofessional education remains constrained by rigid roles and hierarchical structures.33 Reforming both educational frameworks and curricular content may be essential to fostering more collaborative diagnostic practices and advancing diagnostic excellence.

The proportion of the ‘Big Three Diseases’ (vascular events, infections and cancers) was lower than previously reported34; infectious diseases were especially low. One reason may be that our dataset predominantly includes adverse events that occurred during hospitalisation rather than during the initial diagnostic workup. These conditions are often identified at the time of admission or in outpatient settings. They may have been underrepresented in the reporting system. By contrast, a larger percentage of our extracted case reports included radiology and orthopaedics than in previous reports,34 35 because we found distinctive types of diagnostic errors. We provided the most comprehensive insights into the role of unconfirmed imaging and pathology reports in diagnostic errors, highlighting them as a critical issue. Additionally, the lower representation of the Big Three may reflect the inclusion of surgical-service lines, which are less involved in diagnosing vascular events, infections and cancers. Prior studies on diagnostic errors have largely focused on internal medicine, where these conditions are more common. Moreover, the national reporting database emphasises adverse-event reporting during hospitalisation, regardless of disease category. These factors may collectively explain the discrepancy from earlier studies.

Errors in diagnosing complications following falls during hospitalisation are another key category identified in this study. While some adverse events during hospitalisation have been found to be related to nursing care,36 this study found that a part of them was attributable to diagnostic errors. Health professionals may primarily focus on treating the medical condition that led to admission, potentially overlooking new problems that arise during hospitalisation.37 38 Patients with dementia or mental illness may not present with typical complaints of pain, making it difficult to detect new injuries. Additionally, health professionals may not be fully aware of the increased fracture risk associated with certain conditions.39 40 Furthermore, patients whose mobility is improved by inpatient care are at increased risk of fracture. These cases are common in areas such as psychiatry and postoperative management, highlighting the need to raise awareness of diagnostic errors in practice areas that have not traditionally focused on them.41

Moreover, this study revealed difficulties in managing diagnostic errors in traditional event-reporting systems. We extracted diagnostic-error cases by noting specific words from unstructured descriptions because these errors could not be easily captured from the structured items of traditional event reporting.42 To achieve diagnostic safety through the traditional event-reporting system, the organisation managing the event report should actively collect information on the presence of diagnostic errors.23 For example, they should be able to register information related to diagnostic errors in the structured information collected by the system.43 44 As the existing classification tools in this study helped reveal aspects of diagnostic errors, the routine use of one or more of these in combination may be useful for improving and studying diagnostic errors.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this study is based on cases registered in existing event-reporting systems. It is well known that, on average, less than 10% of adverse events are reported in voluntary reporting systems.45,47 Furthermore, in Japan, mandatory reporting is limited to cases of serious impairment, which may be influenced by selection bias regarding more minor cases and the characteristics of near misses. Therefore, the results of this study do not directly reflect the incidence or severity of diagnostic errors in Japan as a whole. Diagnostic errors are less likely to be publicised for non-serious cases and near misses, and the impact of reporting bias is a concern.48 49 The low proportion of diagnostic errors (0.3%) among total reports may reflect the under-recognition of diagnostic-safety events, as the system was not specifically designed to capture them. This impact is probably important in Japan, as near misses are not legally mandated. Second, not all medical institutions register event reports in this database; thus, these results cannot be generalised to all hospitals. However, as high-volume hospitals are participating, it is sufficient to use them as representative estimates. Third, diagnostic errors can be wrong, delayed or missed; however, missed diagnoses may not be detected in all cases, as the event-reporting system verifies events retrospectively after they have been recognised by the stakeholders involved.

Conclusion

This study analysed cases of diagnostic errors registered in the event-reporting system and identified their key characteristics. In particular, failure to confirm imaging and pathology reports, complications after falls and falls during hospitalisation accounted for a significant proportion of diagnostic errors that could not be overlooked. This study’s findings can contribute to efforts in enhancing diagnostic safety. AHPs other than physicians also play a role as stakeholders in diagnostic errors, highlighting the need to redefine these errors as problems within the diagnostic process as part of a team practice. However, distinct improvement approaches are required for different professions. Nurses, in particular, may benefit from training to complement their knowledge and experience.
