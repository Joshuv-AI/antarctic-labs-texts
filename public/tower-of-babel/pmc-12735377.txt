Efficacy and Safety of Multiple Micronutrient (MMN) Supplementation on Anemia in Children and Adults (Except Pregnant Women): A Meta-Analysis
Authors: Rashmi Ranjan Das, Amit Kumar Satapathy, Krishna Mohan Gulla, Anju Sinha, Samarendra Mahapatro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Studies have shown that multiple micronutrient (MMN) supplementation may be equal or better than iron-only or iron-folic acid in the prevention and treatment of anemia in children and non-pregnant women. However, there is no updated evidence on this statement. We followed the Cochrane methodology and conducted a meta-analysis of the published literature with the application of GRADE. The searches were conducted till December 31, 2023. The primary objective was to compare the effect of MMN versus iron and/or folic acid alone on clinical and laboratory parameters of anemia in children and adults (except pregnant women). Review Manager software (version 5.3) was used for data analysis. Of 4640 citations, 12 studies were finally included. There was a significant decrease (20%) in the anemia prevalence in the 2–5 days/week MMN supplementation. A significant decrease (27%) in the prevalence of iron deficiency was noted in the daily MMN supplementation group but not in the weekly MMN supplementation group. No significant change in hemoglobin level was noted in any of the supplementation group. Regarding anthropometry parameters, a daily (not weekly) MMN supplementation led to a significant weight gain by 220 g without affecting the height gain in infants. The GRADE evidence varied from “very low to moderate certainty” for the outcome parameters. MMN supplements incorporating a high dose of iron and micronutrients when provided 2–5 days/week may lead to a significant decrease in the anemia prevalence. We need more evidence before firm conclusions can be made on MMN supplementation for prevention and/or treatment of anemia in children and adults (except pregnant women).

INTRODUCTION

Anemia is a pathological condition in which various tissues in the body suffer hypoxia. Anemia could result from a myriad of causes, including infestations, inflammations, genetic diseases, and vitamin and/or mineral deficiencies.[1] Of the nutritional causes, iron deficiency is the most common and can result from a long‐term problem related to dietary intake, absorption, utilization, or loss.

Due to the poor consequences of chronic anemia on health, iron and/or iron folic acid (IFA) supplementation is often a part of the preventive therapy across all age groups.[2] At the same time, multiple micronutrient (MMN) deficiencies are prevalent in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), and multiple clinical trials have shown varying benefits of MMN supplementation on anemia. In addition, MMN supplementation in different clinical trials has been shown to offer a wide range of benefits compared to IFA alone. However, at present, MMN supplementation is not recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a standard part of anemia management.[2]

The MMN supplements are standardized products provided by UNICEF/WHO/UNU international MMN preparation (UNIMMAP), and the details are available in a catalog (https://supply.unicef.org).[3]

Systematic reviews have been published on the role of MMN in women of reproductive age and pregnancy as well as in children.[456] While analyzing the role of MMN during pre-conception, we found no significant difference between MMN and IFA supplementation in improving newborn anthropometric parameters [very low certainty of evidence (CoE)].[4] In another systematic review, the authors found that the risk of anemia in children <5 years of age was reduced with any of the following interventions: iron alone, IFA, MMN supplementation, or targeted or large-scale fortification.[6] MMN has been tested in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for prevention and treatment of anemia, and the results have been variable.[789101112131415161718] A similar finding was reported by a recently published Cochrane review that collated the findings of all reviews.[19] The trials have been conducted in diverse settings, with some being multicentric. As these are clinical trials, the evidence generated is going to be robust (unless there are risks of bias), and it would be prudent to conclude regarding the benefit (if any) of the intervention proposed. The limitations (if any) can be identified that can give direction to future trials conducted on this topic.

The intervention group must have received the MMN supplements (≥3 micronutrients) consistent with the UNIMMAP (as mentioned above) plus iron and/or folic acid, either in tablet or sachet form. The control group must have received iron and/or IFA supplements.

Search strategy

Data collection

Two authors independently screened the retrieved search results for eligibility first at the title and then at the abstract and full text level (if required). This was followed by full-text screening when the title and abstract did not provide sufficient information on the eligibility criteria of the articles. Pre-designed data extraction forms were used to extract the data. Following data were extracted from each study: author, year, country, study setting, type of RCT (cluster or non-cluster), inclusion criteria, risk-of-bias domains, participant details (demographic data, number, and degree of anemia), details of the intervention and co-intervention (if any), details of the outcomes, attrition (%), and any important additional information. The data extraction form was pilot tested before data extraction.

Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

This was done by two independent authors using the Cochrane RoB (risk of bias) assessment tool.[20] We planned to consider Funnel plot and Egger’s test for publication bias if the latter was identified, and the outcome included at least 10 studies.

Assessment of reporting (publication) bias

This was carried out by checking the trial registry and whether a protocol is available elsewhere (in case the trial is not formally registered in any trial registry).

Measures of treatment effect

Meta-analysis was conducted as per the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.[2021] For dichotomous outcomes, risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MD) with 95% CI were calculated. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed among the included trials by using the Chi-square test and the I2 statistic. The I2 statistic cut-offs used to grade the heterogeneity were as follows: low (<50%), moderate (50%–74%), and high (≥75%). To explore the reasons for significant heterogeneity, sensitivity and/or subgroup analyses were conducted.

Certainty of evidence

The GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach was used.[22] We used GRADEproGDT software (GRADEpro 2016) to create a table summarizing the findings.[23]

Subgroup analysis

We did the following subgroup analyses to look for the reasons for the differential effects of MMN supplementation, if any.

We conducted sensitivity analysis to explore the effect of trial quality on the primary outcomes. The step included excluding trials at high risk of bias to assess for any substantive difference in the outcomes.

Description of studies

Of 4640 total citations retrieved, the full text of 54 papers was assessed for eligibility, and 42 were excluded for various reasons [Figure 1]. Finally, 12 studies (11 studies with data of 3192 children and adolescents, and 1 study with data of 760 non-pregnant women) were included [Table 1].[789101112131415161718] The MMN supplemented was provided as daily, weekly, or 2–5 days/week. In the 2–5 days/week group, the doses of various micronutrients including IFA were used in a very high dose, compared to the other two categories of supplementations.

Risk of bias in included studies

The details are provided in the Supplementary Figure 1. One study was assessed to be of high risk of selection bias.[8] Three studies were assessed to be of high risk of performance bias.[479] No study was assessed to be of high risk of detection bias. Five studies were assessed to be of high risk of attrition bias.[23478] No study was assessed to be of high risk of reporting or other bias. Overall, the bias was assessed to be of low to high risk among the included studies.

Heterogeneity was not significant for any of these categories. DISCUSSION

Summary of evidence

After an extensive search of the literature, we could find 12 studies eligible for inclusion (11 studies with data of 3192 children and adolescents, and 1 study with data of 760 non-pregnant women). There was a significant decrease (20%) in the anemia prevalence in the 2–5 days/week MMN supplementation. A significant decrease (27%) in the prevalence of iron deficiency was noted in the daily but not in the MMN supplementation group. No significant change in Hb level was noted in any of the supplementation group. Regarding anthropometry parameters, a daily (not weekly) MMN supplementation led to a significant weight gain by 220 g without affecting the height gain in infants. The GRADE evidence generated was “very low to moderate” for the primary and secondary outcomes.

Previously published systematic review and meta-analyses tried to analyze the results from published studies over time, but the results have been variable.[192425]

In a systematic review published in 2015, the authors evaluated the effects of MMN-fortified non-dairy beverages in the LMICs.[24] Ten RCTs (nine in school-aged children and one in pregnant women) were included in the analysis. Compared to the controls, children in the MMN group showed significant improvements in Hb (MD: 2.76 g/L, 95% CI: 1.19–4.33) and serum ferritin (MD: 15.42 pmol/L, 95% CI: 5.73–25.12), and reduced risk of anemia (RR: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.29–0.88), ID (RR: 0.34, 95% CI: 0.21–0.55), and IDA (RR: 0.17, 95% CI: 0.06–0.53). The authors concluded that MMN-fortified beverages have the potential to reduce the burden of anemia and ID in school-aged children in LMICs.

In an overview of systematic reviews published in Cochrane library in 2021, the findings across different groups were as follows: In infants (6–23 months), multiple-micronutrient powder (MMNP) increased Hb levels and reduced the risk of anemia; in preschool and school-aged children (2–10 years), MMN-fortified beverage or point-of-use fortification of food with iron-containing MMN powder increased Hb levels and reduced the risk of anemia and ID; in adolescent children (11–18 years), MMN fortification with no fortification exhibited no evidence of a difference in Hb levels; and in non-pregnant women of reproductive age (19–49 years), no review reported the results of MMN supplementation.[19]

A recent systematic review assessed the impact of iron and MMN supplementation on anemia and anthropometric outcomes among individuals aged 5–24 years in LMICs.[25] Compared to placebo, iron and MMN supplementation led to increases in Hb levels by 5.81 g/L (95% CI: 4.19–7.44 g/L) and 4.82 g/L (95% CI: 2.32–7.32 g/L), respectively. In populations of older children, adolescents, and young adults with anemia, both iron (10.24 g/L, 95% CI: 5.10–15.39 g/L) and MMN (9.3 g/L, 95% CI: 1.52–17.09 g/L) showed significant therapeutic effects on Hb levels. Stratified analyses revealed that higher iron doses (>30 mg/day) were associated with greater Hb improvements, and shorter intervention durations (<3 months) were more effective in raising Hb levels. While iron supplementation significantly improved ferritin concentrations, MMN did not show the same benefit. Regarding growth outcomes, iron was associated with an increase in weight (0.52 kg, 95% CI: 0.12–0.93 kg), whereas MMN supplementation contributed to a modest gain in height (0.87 cm, 95% CI: 0.16–1.59 cm). The authors concluded that both iron and MMN were similarly effective in improving Hb levels in older children and young adults with anemia, although they may have differing impacts on growth-related measures.

In this meta-analysis, a consistent effect of MMN supplementation was not found for some of the outcomes. Our findings are similar to those of the antenatal as well as pre-conception MMN supplementation findings.[46] This may be either due to the diversity in the MMN composition and supplementation, or the influence of effect modifiers.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of the present review are the following: (a) use of robust Cochrane methodology, including GRADE to rate the CoE, and (b) synthesized evidence on the use of MMN for anemia in a special and less explored group of population (i.e., children).

The limitations are: (a) inherent bias in the included studies with relation to the methods of randomization and blinding, and (b) differences in the dose, schedule, and duration of MMN supplementation. All these prevented generation of high-certainty (quality) evidence.

Implications of the findings

MMN can be more effective than iron supplementation alone in certain contexts—enhanced Hb response and improved anemia outcomes among children are some of the beneficial outcomes. In regions with coexisting MMN deficiencies, MMN programs may provide better returns than single-nutrient strategies. While results are promising, long-term outcomes and optimal formulations would require more study.

CONCLUSIONS

MMN supplements incorporating a high dose of iron and micronutrients, when provided 2–5 days/week, may lead to a significant decrease in the anemia prevalence. The prevalence of iron deficiency was significantly decreased in the daily MMN supplementation group, but not in the weekly MMN supplementation group. We need more evidence before firm conclusions can be made on MMN supplementation for prevention and/or treatment of anemia in children and adults (except pregnant women).
