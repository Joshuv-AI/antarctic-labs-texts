Approximating R1 and R2: A Quantitative Approach to Clinical Weighted MRI
Authors: Shachar Moskovich, Oshrat Shtangel, Aviv A. Mezer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWeighted MRI images are widely used in clinical as well as open‐source neuroimaging databases. Weighted images such as T1‐weighted, T2‐weighted, and proton density‐weighted (T1w, T2w, and PDw, respectively) are used for evaluating the brain's macrostructure; however, their values cannot be used for microstructural analysis, as they lack physical meaning. Quantitative MRI (qMRI) relaxation rate parameters (e.g., R1 and R2) do contain microstructural physical meaning. Nevertheless, qMRI is rarely done in large‐scale clinical databases. Currently, the weighted images ratio T1w/T2w is used as a quantifier to approximate the brain's microstructure. In this paper, we test three additional quantifiers that approximate quantitative maps, which can help bring quantitative MRI to the clinic for easy use. Following the signal equations and using simple mathematical operations, we combine the T1w, T2w, and PDw images to estimate the R1 and R2 maps. We find that two of these quantifiers (T1w/PDw and T1w/ln(T2w)) can approximate R1, and that (ln(T2w/PDw)) can approximate R2, in 3 datasets that were tested. We find that this approach also can be applied to T2w scans taken from widely available DTI (Diffusion Tensor Imaging) datasets. We tested these quantifiers on both in vitro phantom and in vivo human datasets. We found that the quantifiers accurately represent the quantitative parameters across datasets. Finally, we tested the quantifiers within a clinical context, and found that they are robust across datasets. Our work provides a simple pipeline to enhance the usability and quantitative accuracy of MRI weighted images.

Introduction

QMRI is a powerful tool for studying the brain's microstructure. It can be used to produce parametric maps, which allow for reliable comparisons across subjects, time points, and MRI scanners (Cercignani, Dowell and Tofts 2021). Recent studies have proposed that qMRI, when combined with appropriate biophysical models, can be used to obtain in vivo histological information about the human brain (Weiskopf et al. 2015; Filo et al. 2019; Lazari and Lipp 2021; Filo et al. 2023). The observed relaxation rates such as R1 (1/T1) and R2 (1/T2) are sensitive to myelin content, iron concentration, and water content (Laule et al. 2007; Whittall et al. 1997; Stüber et al. 2014; Wagstyl et al. 2018). Another qMRI parameter is proton density (PD), which is sensitive to the brain's water content (Filo and Mezer 2021).

While there are clear advantages in using biophysically meaningful qMRI, it requires long scanning times, which means it is rarely used in clinical settings. On the other hand, clinical MRI assessment requires relatively short scanning times, and typically rely on weighted images, which are not suited for quantitative biophysical analysis of tissue microstructure. These weighted images include T1w, T2w, and PDw. Additionally, many large open‐source datasets of neurological disorders (e.g., PPMI (https://www.ppmi‐info.org/access‐data‐specimens/download‐data), ADNI (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/), UK‐Biobank (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/), and ABCD (https://abcdstudy.org/)) contain weighted images as well as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data, yet do not contain quantitative maps. Therefore, there would be a great benefit in linking the widely available clinical MRI weighted data and open‐source databases to known qMRI parameters (e.g., R1 and R2).

In recent years, many studies have utilized the ratio of T1w and T2w images (T1w/T2w) as a semi‐quantitative measure (Glasser and Van Essen 2011). On one hand, it has some quantitative properties, as it minimizes the instrumental bias. On the other hand, it is non‐quantitative, as it was not fitted using the physical signal equation, nor is it a direct estimate of a physical quantity (Sandrone et al. 2023). As then, T1w/T2w became widely available, and has been suggested as an indicator of tissue integrity and myelination (Glasser and Van Essen 2011; Baum et al. 2022), and is often referred to as myelin marker, though its validity is still debated (Sandrone et al. 2023). The distribution of T1w/T2w in the brain has been linked to myelin expression (Ritchie et al. 2018; Uddin et al. 2018; Arshad, Stanley, and Raz 2017; Shams, Norris, and Marques 2019; Ganzetti, Wenderoth, and Mantini 2014; Schurr et al. 2018; Schurr, Zelman, and Mezer 2020) and brain development (Baum et al. 2022; Norbom et al. 2022, 2019; Grydeland et al. 2013). Abnormal changes in T1w/T2w have been associated with neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson's disease (Drori, Berman, and Mezer 2022), multiple sclerosis (Beer et al. 2016; Cooper et al. 2019; Nakamura et al. 2017), Alzheimer's disease (Pelkmans et al. 2019), and Huntington's (Rowley et al. 2018) disease, and even affective diseases such as schizophrenia (Iwatani et al. 2015). Recently, this approach was extended to use FLAIR to approximate myelin integrity, using T1w/FLAIR ratio instead of T1w/T2w (Cappelle et al. 2022). As T1w/T2w had become a popular method, many open‐source datasets now contain both weighted images. However, some datasets (e.g., PPMI, ADNI1) additionally contain PDw image, which is often scanned along with T2w image. Some datasets (such as UK‐Biobank) contain T1w and PDw images but not T2w.

Another previously suggested method to measure quantitative information using weighted images is the log‐linear approach, which utilized gradient‐echo sequences containing multiple echo times to quickly measure R2 (Otto et al. 2011; Luo and Collingwood 2022).

Weighted MRI data does not typically used for inferring microstructural information about the brain. The biological complexity of brain tissue ideally requires the use of multi‐exponential relaxation signal equations to capture its multi‐compartmental organization (Ben‐Eliezer et al. 2015). Nevertheless, it is a common practice in the qMRI field to represent the tissue relaxations using a single exponential rate constant (Dowell and Wood 2021; Weiskopf et al. 2015; Otto et al. 2011; Pei et al. 2015; Wood et al. 2005; Whittall, MacKay, and Li 1999). In this work, we aim to harness the rich and extensive existing weighted datasets by developing a framework that utilizes available weighted images. This framework is designed to enhance the sensitivity to the underlying microstructural features of the tissue, even though such approximations cannot fully capture of the comprehensive quantitative properties.

We test two weighted image quantifiers (i.e., semi‐quantitative combinations of weighted images) based on three common weighted images (T1w, T2w, and PDw) and using mathematical combinations such as ratios and logarithms. We call these two semi‐quantitative qualifiers T1w/PDw and ln(T2w/PDw), and we show that they can approximate the observed, single‐exponent qMRI parameters R1 and R2, respectively. We validate our results both on a controlled lipid phantom system and on two human datasets. Furthermore, we modify the existing T1w/T2w quantifier and propose a third quantifier (T1w/ln(T2w)), which can better approximate R1. We also show that these quantifiers can be estimated using widely available DTI sequences. Finally, we apply our analyses to a clinical dataset from the PPMI, compare it with our previous results, and find the quantifiers to be robust across datasets. In summary, we propose a way to utilize weighted images, whose common use is clinical, to approximate quantitative MRI parametrization.

Methods

Theory

In this section, we follow the MRI signal equations to identify quantifiers that can be approximated from weighted images.

The typical T1w signal S can be generated using a spoiled gradient echo (SPGR) sequence (Buxton et al. 1987): (1) SSPGR=M0·exp−TET2*·sinα·B11−exp−TRT11−cosα·B1exp−TRT1 where α is the flip angle, M0 is the magnetization at equilibrium, TR is the repetition time, B1 is the transmit sensitivity profile of the RF coil, and TE is the echo time.

For short echoes, we can assume exp−TET2*~1.

The typical T2w and PDw signals can be generated using a spin echo (SE) sequence (Dowell and Wood 2021): (2) SSE=M0·exp−TET2

By varying the echo time (TE), one can generate the PDw signal (short TE) and the T2w signal (long TE).

In both Equations (1) and (2), the PDw contrast dominates the M0 parameter (M0≈PD·G, where G is a scale factor that characterizes the receive‐coil inhomogeneity gain (Baum et al. 2022). When we divide Equation (1) by the PDw contributions, we get a pure T1w signal. Hence, we predict that T1wPDw will provide a better approximation of the R1 contrast: (3) T1wPDw=sinα·B11−exp−TRT11−cosα·B1exp−TRT1

Note that the signal equations for PDw and T2w differ only by the TE. As PDw entails a short TE, exp−TEPDT2~1, so T1w/PDw will not contain PD influences. On the other hand, as T2w has a long TE, exp−TET2T2≠1, and so the contrast T1w/T2w will be a mix of both R1 and R2 processes.

We note that T1w also can be generated using other sequences, such as MPRAGE (Mugler and Brookeman 1990), which we also deployed in this paper. The signal equation for MPRAGE is given by (adapted from Nöth et al. (2015)): (4) SMPRAGE=M0·sinα·B1·G·Q

Where Q is a complex term, which is affected by different scanning factors, including: T1 relaxation time, number of 3D pixels acquired, time constants of the acquisition, time between excitation pulses and number of echoes.

As T1w images typically contain a T1w term (Q) that is multiplied by a PDw term (M0), the same logic can be applied: (5) T1wPDw=sinα·B1·G·Q

A similar heuristic was shown to be useful to estimate R2. This method was termed as two points log‐linear method (Otto et al. 2011).

Following Equation (2) (keeping in mind that both PDw and T2w derive from the same sequence, and thus share a resolution): T2wPDw=exp−TET2T2exp−TEPDT2=exp−TET2T2+TEPDT2=expTEPD−TET2·R2

We denote TE~=TEPD−TET2. Applying the natural log (ln) on T2wPDw to obtain a linear function of R2 (Cappelle et al. 2022): (6) lnT2wPDw=TE~·R2

So, to achieve a direct correlation with R2 we divide lnT2wPDw by TE~.

It is worth noting that this method was previously shown to be influenced by the choice of TEs (Figure S1). However, in clinical dataset one can only use the available T2w and PDw images and calculate the effective TE~ for each particular dataset.

By these mathematical operations, we can test two quantifiers: T1w/PDw, which approximates R1 (Equations 3 and 5); and ln(T2w/PDw), which approximates R2 (Equation 6).

To account for datasets that do not contain PDw images, several other mathematical heuristics can be tested given an existing T1w and T2w images. As T2w image contains an exponential term (Equation 2), we propose to test two additional quantifiers: T1w/ln(T2w) and ln(T1w/T2w).

Finally, to generalize this framework to larger datasets that do not contain T2w images, but contain T1w and DTI data (e.g., UK Biobank), we rely on the fact that typical DTI aquation are SE‐based and the B0 images (non‐diffusion weighting images) are described by the SE signal equation (Equation 2). Hence, we propose using B0 images as T2w in all abovementioned quantifiers.

To summarize, we can test 4 quantifiers: T1w/PDw, ln(T2w/PDw), T1w/ln(T2w) and ln(T1w/T2w), and their parallel DTI quantifiers.

Datasets

In order to test the semi quantitative quantifiers, we examined four datasets. Three of them (A, B, and D) were pre‐existing, and dataset C was collected as a validation. Datasets A, B, and C Were collected at the same scanner at the hebrew university by our group, while dataset D was collected on multiple scanners elsewhere.

All datasets preprocessing and analysis were preformed using MATLAB (The MathWorks Inc. 2022).

Dataset A—Phantom Data

The lipid dataset was designed and collected in a previous work by our group (Shtangel and Mezer 2020).

In short, four types of lipid mixtures were produced (phosphatidylcholine—PC; phosphatidylcholine, and cholesterol—PC:chol; phosphatidylserine—ps; and sphingomyelin—spg), at different concentrations. All samples were scanned using a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Skyra scanner equipped with a 32‐channel head receive‐only coil. Data were collected at the Edmond and Lily Safra Center's Neuroimaging Unit at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Israel.

Data Acquisition

For R1 and PD mapping, 3D images were acquired using 3D SPGR images were acquired with four flip angles (α = 4°, 8°, 16°, 30°), TE = 3.91 ms, TR = 18 ms, field of view (FoV) of 205 mm2, and voxel size 1.1 mm × 1.1 mm × 0.9 mm. For bias estimation, single‐slice images were acquired using a spin echo inversion recovery (SEIR) sequence with an adiabatic inversion pulse and inversion times of TI = 2000, 1200, 800, 400, 50 ms, TR = 2540 ms, TE = 73 ms, FoV of 222 mm2, and voxel size of 1.2 mm × 1.2 mm × 2.0 mm.

For R2 mapping, images were acquired with multi‐spin‐echo sequences with 15 equally spaced echo times between TE = 10.5 and TE = 157.5 ms, TR = 4940 ms, FoV of 207 mm2, and voxel size of 2 mm isotropic.

The SPGR scans with α = 30° were used as T1w images. The shortest and longest TEs for the multi‐spin‐echo scans (TE = 10.5 and 157.5 ms) were used for the PDw and T2w images, respectively.

Estimation of qMRI Parameters and Analysis

The R1 map was estimated using the variable flip angle approach. The R1 and PD maps, as well as the B1+ excitation bias, were computed using mrQ software (Mezer et al. 2013, 2016). The PD values for all lipids were normalized to the value of the water in the cuvette. The mrQ software was modified to suit the phantom system: as the agar‐Gd filling the box around the samples is homogeneous, it therefore can be assumed to have a constant R1 value (Shtangel and Mezer 2020, 2024).

The R2 map was estimated from a multi‐spin‐echo sequence using the echo‐modulation curve (EMC) algorithm (Ben‐Eliezer et al. 2015).

We calculated the T1w/T2w and T1w/PDw values using voxel‐to‐voxel division of the contrasts' values. For ln(T2w/PDw), we applied two point log‐linear (Otto et al. 2011) fit of R2: we divided the contrasts' values, and then derived the natural logarithm, and multiplied the result by TE~=−147ms.

All phantom data underwent bias correction using a 3rd‐degree polynomial over the agar mask, as was done in a previous study (Filo et al. 2019).

Dataset B—HUJI Dataset 1

This human dataset was collected by Filo et al. (2019), using the same scanner as in Dataset A.

Participants

From Filo et al.'s original dataset of 41 subjects (Filo et al. 2019), we selected 22 individuals who had MPRAGE T1w scans, as these scans are considered to be the gold standard for T1w scans in clinical settings (Brant‐Zawadzki, Gillan, and Nitz 1992). The original study focused on aging, and thus our data contained two age groups: younger adults (n = 13, age 26.7 ± 2.4 years, 7 females) and older adults (n = 9, age 67.1 ± 5.5 years, 2 females). Of the 22 subjects, 20 also had B0 diffusion images. All study procedures were approved by the Helsinki Ethics Committee from Hadassah Hospital, Jerusalem, Israel. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the procedure.

Data Acquisition

For quantitative R1 and PD mapping, 3D SPGR echo images were acquired with different flip angles (α = 4°, 10°, 20°, and 30°). Each image included five equally spaced echoes (TE = 3.34–14.02 ms) and the TR was 19 ms (except for six younger subjects, for whom the scan included only one TE = 3.34 ms). The scan resolution was 1 mm isotropic. For calibration, an additional SEIR scan was acquired with an echo‐planar imaging (EPI) read‐out (SEIR‐epi). This scan was done with a slab‐inversion pulse and spatial‐spectral fat suppression. For SEIR‐epi, the TE/TR were 49/2920 ms. The TI were 200, 400, 1200, and 2400 ms. We used 2‐mm in‐plane resolution with a slice thickness of 3 mm. The SEIR‐epi read‐out was performed using 2 × acceleration. For quantitative R2 mapping, images were acquired with a multi spin‐echo sequence with 15 equally spaced spin echoes between 10.5 and 157.5 ms. The TR was 4.94 s. The scan resolution was 1.2 mm isotropic.

For the diffusion B0 images, whole‐brain DTI measurements were performed using a diffusion‐weighted spin‐echo EPI sequence with a resolution of 1.5 mm isotropic. Diffusion weighting gradients were applied at 64 directions, and the strength of the diffusion weighting was set to b = 2000 s/mm2 (TE/TR = 95.80/6000 ms, G = 45mT/m, δ = 32.25 ms, Δ = 52.02 ms). The data include eight non‐diffusion‐weighted images (b = 0).

T1w values, we used MPRAGE scans with a resolution of 1 mm isotopic. As a replication we also used additional T1w for each subject, the SPGR scans with α = 30° (from which we derive R1 mapping, for full scanning details see above). The shortest and longest TEs for the multi‐spin‐echo scans (TE = 10.5 and 157.5 ms) were used for the PDw and T2w values, respectively.

Estimation of qMRI Parameters and Analysis

The PD and R1 estimations were computed using the mrQ (Mezer et al. 2013, 2016) and Vista Lab software packages. The R2 map was estimated from a multi‐spin‐echo sequence using the EMC algorithm (Ben‐Eliezer et al. 2015).

All quantifiers (T1w/PDw, T1w/T2w, and ln(T2w/PDw)) were computed by voxel‐to‐voxel division. We calculated the ln(T2w/PDw) quantifier using TE~=−147ms (as shown in Equation 4), which we derived from the T1w and PDw TEs.

Brain Registration and Segmentation

We registered the diffusion‐MRI (dMRI), qMRI and weighted images to the lower‐resolution R2 space (2 mm3 isotropic). We used the SynthStrip toolbox (Hoopes et al. 2022) to create a brain mask. We then applied rigid‐body registration using SPM 12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/), with the lower‐resolution R1 map as a reference.

Brain segmentation was done using FreeSurfer (Fischl 2012), with the T1w image as a reference. All steps were inspected by eye to ensure image quality.

ANTs bias correction (Tustison et al. 2010; Klein et al. 2010) was applied to the T1w, T2w, and PDw images independently, and then all quantifiers were computed voxel‐to‐voxel as described previously.

As the ANTs bias correction algorithm tends to shift the overall values of the images, for each corrected image we calculated the ratio between the pre‐ and post‐correction image medians, and multiplied by this ratio to shift the values back to their original range.

Dataset C—HUJI Dataset 2 (Validation Dataset)

In this dataset, we acquired scans for weighted images and scans for quantitative mappings independently, using a separate sequence for each map or weighted image. We collected the data using the same scanner used for datasets A and B.

Participants

Three individuals (age 28.0 ± 1.5 years, one female) were scanned. All study procedures were approved by the Helsinki Ethics Committee from Hadassah Hospital, Jerusalem, Israel. A written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the procedure.

Data Acquisition

For quantitative R1 and PD mapping, 3D SPGR echo images were acquired with different flip angles (α = 4°, 10°, 20°, and 30°). The TE/TR were 8.68/19 ms. The scan resolution was 2 mm isotropic. For calibration, we acquired an additional SEIR‐epi scan. This scan was done with a slab‐inversion pulse and spatial‐spectral fat suppression. The TE/TR were 49/2920 ms, with an adiabatic inversion pulse and inversion times of TI = 2400, 1200, 400, and 200. The scan resolution was 2 mm isotropic.

For quantitative R2 mapping, images were acquired with a multi spin‐echo sequence with 10 equally spaced spin echoes between 12 and 120 ms. The TR was 4210 ms. The scan resolution was 2 mm isotropic.

For the T1w image, we scanned an MPRAGE sequence with a resolution of 2 mm isotropic. For the T2w images, we used a SIEMENS T2w Skipped phase spin‐echo (SK/SP SE) structural scan with TE/TR of 482/3200 ms and a resolution of 2 mm isotropic. For the PDw images, we scanned a SIEMENS T2 Spoiling spin‐echo (SP SE) scan with a single echo time, with TE/TR of 12/4210 ms and a resolution of 2 mm isotropic.

Estimation of qMRI Parameters

The PD and R1 estimations were computed using the mrQ (Mezer et al. 2013, 2016) and Vista Lab software packages.

The R2 map was estimated from a multi‐spin‐echo sequence using the EMC algorithm (Ben‐Eliezer et al. 2015).

The semi‐quantitative quantifiers (T1w/PDw, T1w/T2w, and ln(T2w/PDw)) were computed by voxel‐to‐voxel division. We calculated the ln(T2w/PDw) using TE~=−470ms (as in Equation 4).

Brain Registration and Segmentation

Image registration, segmentation, and correction was done as described in Dataset B.

Dataset D—PPMI

Participants and Data

For our fourth dataset, we used data from an open‐source dataset of the Parkinson's Progression Marker Initiative (PPMI). Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained on 03/25/21 from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) database (https://www.ppmi‐info.org/access‐data‐specimens/download‐data), RRID:SCR_006431. For up‐to‐date information on the study, visit https://www.ppmi‐info.org. We used a subset of the dataset, which was selected in a previous published work by our group (Drori, Berman, and Mezer 2022). Our dataset consists of 46 healthy control subjects (age 65 ± 6 years, 17 females).

Data Acquisition

MRI data were collected using 3T SIEMENS Trio scanners, in 8 different sites. T1w images were acquired using an MPRAGE generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisitions (MPRAGE‐GRAPPA) sequence, with a sagittal slice thickness of 1 mm and an in‐plane resolution of 1 mm2, and the flip angle was α = 9°. The T2w and PDw images were acquired using a turbo spin echo (TSE) sequence with an axial slice thickness of 3 mm and an in‐plane resolution of 0.94 mm2, with TR = 3000 ms and TE = 11 ms (for T2w) or 101 ms (for PDw).

Brain Registration and Segmentation

We registered, aligned, and resampled the T2w and PDw images to the T1w image space, using software packages from SPM and MRtrix3 (www.mrtrix.org/). All steps were inspected by eye to ensure image quality. Segmentation was done in the same procedure as in Datasets B and C (Fischl 2012).

Synthetic Data

To examine our approach from a theoretical perspective, we produced synthetic data.

Synthetic weighted data was produced by generating quantitative values within the range of human brain values of Dataset B (i.e., T1 values ranged between 0.8 and 2.7 s with 100 equally spaced jumps, and T2 values ranged between 0.05 and 1.03 s with 100 equally spaced jumps), plugging them into the relevant signal equations, to create T1w/PDw and ln(T2w/PDw), as well as R1 and R2 values. For the synthetic T1w images, we use Equation (1) and assume exp−TET2*~1, α=30°, and TR=18ms. For synthetic T2w images, we use Equation (2) with TET2=157.5ms.

We assume that the synthetic PDw contrast can be best represented by Equation (2) given a short TE (TEPD=10.5ms), so that TE~=−147ms.

For each of the synthetic semi‐quantitative contrast we used additive Gaussian noise of 3 levels (10, 15, and 20 dB) and produced noisy data using the following calculation: Noisy signal=Signal+noise

Where noise was generated for each synthetic contrast using: Noise=noise power·randN where N is the size of the synthetic weighted vector, and noise power is the vector power (median of the vector squared) divided by the noise level (in dB).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB (The MathWorks Inc. 2022).

Correlations between quantifiers were calculated using Pearson's correlation.

To quantify the differences between the correlations, we performed cross‐validation for the tested variables, for Datasets A and B. For each of the pairs we randomly selected 90% of the samples (Dataset A) or voxels (Dataset B) and used them to produce a linear fit, which we tested on the remaining 10%. We then calculated the root mean square error (RMSE): RMSE=y−y′2 where y is the real quantifier value, and y' is the predicted quantifier value. We repeated this process 1000 times for each pair, and then averaged the RMSE over these 1000 repetitions.

For single‐subject analysis, to quantify the corrlation differences for each pair of variables correlations, we used Wilcoxon sign‐rank test (Wilcoxon 1945). The p‐values were later corrected for multiple‐comparisons using Bonferroni correction.

Bland–Altman analysis (Altman and Bland 1983) was used to assess the agreement between the tested variable pairs, for Dataset B. To overcome inherent scale differences within each pair, we used Z‐score normalization for each variable (across subjects). Then we used the common upper and lower bias limits for 95% confidence (of 1.96 of the standard variation).

Results

In the following sections, we first test the theory introduced in Methods section on synthetic phantom data, and produce two semi‐quantitative maps. Next, we test the theory using biological source lipid phantoms, and in vivo using two human datasets.

Quantitative MRI Values Can Be Approximated Using Weighted Images

We used two vectors representing R1 and R2 values (ranged in typical human brain ranges, (y‐axes of Figure 1), as well as synthetic semi‐quantitative T1w/PDw and ln(T2w/PDw) (using Equation (1), (2), (3), (4), x‐axes of Figure 1). To account for noisy signal, we had also introduced additive Gaussian noise in four SNR values (0, 10, 15, 20 dB indicated by different shades of gray).

The synthetic T1w/PDw and ln(T2w/PDw) quantifiers yield a perfect correlation with synthetic quantitative maps R1 and R2 in a noise‐free environment. As predicted from the MR relaxation equations, the weighted images quantifiers are in perfect agreement with the quantitative relaxation rate in a noise‐free environment. As expected from Equations (3) and (4), without noise we produced perfect linear relationships between T1w/PDw and the R1 map, as well as a perfect relationship between ln(T2w/PDw) and R2 map (R2=1; Figure 1). These results indicate that weighted images theoretically can provide a perfect estimate of the single‐exponent, observed quantitative maps in a noise‐free environment. Naturally, when introducing noise, we found smaller linear correlations between the quantitative R1 and R2 and the semi‐quantitative T1w/PDw and ln(T2w/PDw), with lower correlations for lower SNR (as a function of the noise level). Hence, as expected, the level of agreement between the quantifier and the true qMRI value is noise dependent.

For a given noise level (e.g., 20 dB), it is expected that the scanning parameters will also influence the relevant R1 and R2 weighting. To evaluate it, we simulated the scanning parameters α and TR as commonly used example values (α=20°,10°,4° and TR = 20, 10, 4 ms) and found that the relationship between synthetic T1w/PDw and synthetic R1 remains linear (with different slope) even though the underlying signal equation (Equation 1) is not linear. However, we observe the variance is highly affected by the change of α and TR. For the same noise level we also changed TE~ values to match commonly acquired TEPD and TET2 values (TEPD=4,8,10.5 ms and TET2=120,100,157.5ms to give TE~=−92,−116,−147ms). As expected from Equation (6), the linear relationship between the synthetic ln(T2w/PDw) and synthetic R2 sustained (Figure S1).

To validate the utility of the semi‐quantitative quantifiers in biological source lipids, we first tested it using phantom data. Specifically, we evaluated the correlation of R1 with T1w, T1w/T2w, and T1w/PDw. We found significant correlations for all three comparisons (Figure 2A–C). T1w/T2w showed the weakest correlation (R2=0.642,p<10−7), and T1w/PDw showed the strongest (R2=0.901,p<10−7). To quantify these correlations, we performed 1000‐fold cross‐validation (CV) for each pair of variables: each time, we trained a linear model on 90% of the samples and tested it on the remaining 10%, and then calculated the RMSE. We found that T1w/PDw had the lowest mean RMSE (0.0316 s−1), compared to T1w (0.0447 s−1) and T1w/T2w (0.0614 s−1). Similarly, we calculated the correlations of R2 with T2w, T1w/T2w, and ln(T2w/PDw). We found that the strongest correlation was with ln(T2w/PDw) (R2=0.956,p<10−7; Figure 2D–F). We did the same CV analysis, and found the lowest error with ln(T2w/PDw) (0.4911 s−1), compared with R2w (0.8192 s−1) and T1w/T2w (0.8711 s−1). These findings highlight the benefit of the T1w/PDw and ln(T2w/PDw) quantifiers, as they robustly approximate quantitative relaxation values in phantoms.

To test the applicability of the biological source lipid phantoms results on in vivo human brain data, we used a human dataset of 22 subjects (Dataset B). We pooled whole‐brain images from all subjects, and calculated the correlations of the weighted images T1w, T1w/T2w and T1w/PDw with R1. Here we used MPRAGE images for T1w, as MPRAGE is the T1w image most commonly employed. As in the phantom data, here also T1w/PDw yielded the highest correlation with R1 (R2=0.851,p<10−30; Figure 3A–C). Silimar to the CV analysis done in phantoms, we quantified these correlations using RMSE. We found that T1w/PDw had the lowest mean RMSE (0.0734 s–1, compared to T1w (0.102 s–1) and T1w/T2w (0.105 s–1). In the single‐subject level, we found that both T1w and T1w/PDw show strong correlations with R1, which were higher than T1w/T2w correlations for every subject measured (tested with Wilcoxon sign‐rank test, see Figure S4A–D). For additional validation, we repeated this analysis with SPGR‐based T1w images. In our dataset, the SPGR images seem noisier and more affected by the scanner inhomogeneities (Figure S2). Importantly, when applying a bias correction algorithm (Tustison et al. 2010; Klein et al. 2010), the SPGR‐based T1w quantifiers show similar results to MPRAGE T1w quantifiers (Figure S3). Together, these results suggest that T1w/PDw is a better approximation for R1 compared to both T1w and T1w/T2w. We also used Bland–Altman analysis to visualize the degree of agreement between R1 and the three abovementioned semi‐quantitative measurements. We found the best agreement for R1 was with T1w/PDw, with the smallest spread and narrowest bias limits. (Figure S5A–C).

Similarly, for the R2 approximates, we replicated the phantom results in humans. We found that ln(T2w/PDw) had the highest linear correlation with R2, among all other tested quantifiers (R2=0.988,p<10−30; Figure 3D–F, also for single‐subject in Dataset B see Figure S2E–H). In the CV analysis we did to quantify these correlations, we found the lowest error with ln(T2w/PDw) (0.0003 s–1), compared to R2w (0.002 s–1) and T1w/T2w (0.002 s–1). To confirm that the “ln” operation itself was not solely responsible for this effect, we also examined ln(R2w) and ln(T1w/T2w), which showed weaker correlations with R2 than did ln(T2w/PDw) (tested with Wilcoxon sign‐rank test, Figure S4E–H). Bland–Altman analysis revealed a strong agreement between ln(T2w/PDw) and R2, with the narrowest bias limits. The other two comparisons show apparent patterns (Figure S5D–E), which may be attributed to the non‐linear patterns apparent in the correlation plot (Figure 3D,E).

Validating the Semi‐Quantitative Contrasts

In Figure S2 we noticed that SPGR T1w images quantifiers can be improved following inhomogeneity biases correction. To further test the residual contributions of spatial inhomogeneity biases to all tested quantifiers, we applied a bias correction algorithm (Tustison et al. 2010; Klein et al. 2010). This correction accounts for inhomogeneous bias fields that might not cancel out when dividing the weighted images (to produce T1w/PDw and ln(T2w/PDw)). We found similar results to the non‐corrected results (Figure S6) when pooling whole‐brain images from all subjects of Dataset B: that is, the highest correlation with R1 was with T1w/PDw (R2=0.483,p<10−30), and the highest correlation with R2 was with ln(T2w/PDw) (R2=0.941,p<10−30). However, in this analysis we detected a separation between subjects (apparent especially in Figure S6A). This separation suggests that the bias correction might introduce an additional shift in the range of values of the semi‐quantifiers, thus generating another source of variation. To overcome this shift, we calculated the correlations with the quantitative maps R1 and R2 for individual subjects (Figure S4B,E). Our results with the individualized correction are similar to those without the correction, suggesting this bias‐correction step is not beneficial in our dataset. Additionally, we normalized the values for each subject (see Section 2), and got similar trends as shown in Figure 3, where T1w/PDw best approximates R1 and ln(T2w/PDw) best approximates R2 (Figure S7, R2=0.84,p<10−30;R2=0.96,p<10−30, correspondingly).

This suggests that the typical MPRAGE T1w images are relatively free of inhomogeneity compared to the SPGR T1w data. Our T2w and PDw seem to slightly improve by the correction. The fact that ln(T2w/PDw) images do not improve suggest that most of the bias is multiplicative and removed by the suggested division operation.

In order to rule out that our observations are due to the dependency between commonly acquired images or resampling bias due to difference in images resolution, we scanned an additional independent dataset, such that each weighted image was acquired using a different scanning sequence but at the same resolution (Dataset C). We then pooled whole‐brain images from Dataset C's subjects (N = 3), and indeed we replicated the result in Dataset B. In particular, we note our finding that ln(T2w/PDw) is the best approximation of R2 in this dataset as well (R2=0.642,p<10−30; Figure S8). However, unlike our results from Dataset B, there was no advantage for T1w/PDw over T1w or T1w/T2w. We suggest this may be related to the small number of subjects in Dataset C.

Modification of T1w/T2w Reveals R1 Approximation

T1w/T2w is a commonly used semi‐quantitative quantifier (Glasser and Van Essen 2011; Baum et al. 2022; Ritchie et al. 2018; Uddin et al. 2018; Arshad, Stanley, and Raz 2017; Shams, Norris, and Marques 2019; Ganzetti, Wenderoth, and Mantini 2014; Norbom et al. 2022, 2019; Grydeland et al. 2013). Following our analysis, we ask whether T1w/T2w could more accurately approximate the quantitative maps R1 and R2. As the PDw image is less common than T1w and T2w throughout clinical datasets, we ask whether we can optimize the utilization of T1‐ and T2‐weighted images. In Figures 2B,E and 3B,E, we report that the T2w and T1w/T2w images show an exponential change with respect to R2 or R1. Hence, we calculated the quantitative maps' correlations with ln(T1w/T2w) for humans.

Based on Equation (2), we also suggest applying the natural logarithm on T2w alone, and therefore calculated T1w/ln(T2w) for Dataset B. Interestingly, when pooling whole‐brain images from all subjects, we observed high correlations between R1 and ln(T1w/T2w) and between R1 and T1w/ln(T2w) (R2=0.706,p<10−7;R2=0.797,p<10−7, respectively; Figure 4A–C). On the single‐subject level, R1 showed a high correlation with T1w/ln(T2w), though it did not appear different from R1's correlations with T1w and with T1w/PDw (Figure S4A). We replicated this results in Dataset C (Figure S9A–C, and single‐subject results in Figure S4C). Hence, we found T1w/ln(T2w) to be a better approximation of R1 than is T1w/T2w in these datasets. It is worth noticing that any shared bias between the images will not be cancelled when taking T1w/ln(T2w) instead of the other options (i.e., T1w/T2w or ln(T1w/T2w)).

Next, we tested the correlations of R2 with each of T1w/T2w, ln(T1w/T2w), and T1w/ln(T2w) in Dataset B. Of these three correlations, we found the highest to be with ln(T1/T2w) (R2=0.706,p<10−7, Figure 4D–F). This result was replicated in Dataset C (Figure S9). However, for the single‐subject analysis, none of the T1w/T2w variations was as good as ln(T2w/PDw) in approximating R2 (Figure S4E,F, also for Dataset C in the same figure, panels G–H).

Quantitative R1 Can Be Approximated Using DTI Data

One main goal of this work is to utilize existing non‐quantitative data to extract quantitative information, so we wanted to generalize our analysis to widely available datasets that contain T1w data and DTI data but not T2w data (e.g., UK Biobank). Therefore, we repeated our analysis in Dataset B, using the B0 images (i.e., non‐diffusion T2w images, hereafter T2wB0) that are collected with every DTI scan. Importantly, T2wB0 is a measure taken with an EPI readout, in different from the T1w, and a nonlinear image registration is needed (Andersson, Skare, and Ashburner 2003; Berman et al. 2018). We replicated the main T2w result for T2wB0: We compared the correlations between R1 and either T1w/T2wB0, T1w/ln(T2wB0), and ln(T1w/ T2wB0). We found that T1w/T2wB0 provides the best approximation of R1 (R2=0.801,p<10−30; Figure 5A). We also compared the correlations between R2 and these three quantifiers, and found that ln(T1/ T2wB0) can approximate R2 (R2=0.513,p<10−30; Figure 5E). Notably, in this dataset, using any quantifier with T2wB0 instead of T2w gives a better estimation of R1 and a worse estimation of R2.

The Semi‐Quantitative Quantifiers Are Robust Across Datasets

To test the robustness of the proposed structural quantifiers, we analyzed data of healthy subjects from open‐source Parkinson's disease data (PPMI (Marek et al. 2011)—Dataset D). We used a subset of Dataset B (9 subjects, age 67.1 + −5.5, 2 females), to match the age group of Dataset D. We compared between the quantifiers in Dataset D and Dataset B's quantitative maps R1 and R2. To compare between the datasets, we used the median values of 14 gray‐ and white‐matter ROIs across the brain (Figure 6). Similar to the result that we found within subjects in Dataset B (Figures 3 and 4), we also found high correlations between the quantifiers calculated for Dataset D and the quantitative maps calculated for Dataset B. The quantitative R1 map of Dataset B correlated strongly with Dataset D's T1w/PDw (R2=0.962,p<10−30, Figure 6A), T1w/T2w (R2=0.952,p<10−30, Figure 6B), and also with T1w/ln(T2w) (R2=0.961,p<10−30, Figure 6C). We also found a significant correlation between quantitative R2 map of Dataset B and the semi‐quantitative ln(T2w/PDw) of Dataset D (R2=0.879,p<, Figure 6D). These high correlations between semi‐quantitative quantifiers produced from clinical data and quantitative maps mean that semi‐quantitative approximation can be also done using weighted images originated from clinical data. Hence, these results pave the way for future semi‐quantitative analysis to be performed in clinical datasets.

Discussion

qMRI offers a reliable biophysical interpretation of microstructural changes in the human brain (Leutritz et al. 2020). Unfortunately, in clinical settings the use of qMRI is still rare, which limits quantitative comparisons and the interpretation of such neuroimaging datasets. In this work we test three quantifiers, which are calculated from weighted images commonly used in clinical settings. We show that specific quantifiers resemble qMRI metrics and enhance the applicability of clinical datasets for quantitative interpretation.

The foundational theory of MRI relaxation (which inspired this research) is well established (Cercignani, Dowell, and Tofts 2021). Given the known signal equations for single‐exponent R1 and R2, our efforts centered on uncovering the latent potential of weighted images to estimate the relaxation rates. We propose a novel way to utilize existing weighted data for quantitative approximations. First, we showed that the observed R1 can be approximated using the T1w/PDw quantifier. We show this using synthetic data, biological source lipid phantom data, and in vivo human brain data.

In radiological practice, T1w images are used to visualize the R1 contrast within the subject's individual brain. A major benefit of T1w/PDw over T1w is that it normalizes the image values across subjects, which allows it to better represent the R1 differences between subjects. Hence, this normalization likely removes biases that are common to both images (Glasser and Van Essen 2011).

Another benefit of T1w/PDw is that it allows for the approximation of a “pure T1‐weighted” image, reducing PD influences. This rationale also was proposed in Nöth et al. (2015), where an MPRAGE image was acquired along with a PDw image. In contrast to that earlier work, the focus of the current work is to combine T1w and PDw images that already exist.

We use synthetic data to show how does the R1 signal also depends on the TR and the flip angle, as shown in Equation (1). However, as most datasets use a specific TR and flip angle, we simulated the data using unique values that were derived from PDw scans and are common in the field. Differences in scanning parameters between and within datasets are likely to shift the observed values. Such contributions also may account for the fact that T1w/PDw is not a perfect linear function of R1, but rather is approximately linear in the range of relevant values across the gray‐ and white‐matter regions.

Furthermore, we acknowledge that this analysis may be influenced by the scanning sequence of T1w (Equations 1 and 4), and so we performed it both for SPGR and MPRAGE‐based T1w images. As expected, as both equations have an inherent PD contribution, the division by PDw image worked similarly for both T1w images, and enhanced the R1 approximation (Figure S3).

Next, we showed that R2 was best approximated by ln(T2w/PDw). Replicating previous study (Otto et al. 2011; Luo and Collingwood 2022), we found this result across all datasets (synthetic, phantoms, and human datasets). We test whether using the ln operation (according to the R2 single‐exponent signal equation) with the minimal two echo time images (i.e., T2w, PDw) will allow for a more accurate approximation of R2. Interestingly, this approximation seems to be insensitive to the sequences that were used to produce the PDw and T2w images (Dataset C results, Figure S7). This approximation may depend on the choice of TEs (see our simulations in Figure S1). However, as we use existing in vivo T2w and PDw images, we are limited to specific TEs. In general, as long as the mono‐exponential T2 equation holds (see below for further discussion), it is linearly dependent of the TE~ (Equation 6), and this approximation will likely hold for any combination of TEs.

In current practice, T1w/T2w has become a standard in large dataset repositories (HCP, ABCD). In many studies, it has been proposed as a marker for myelin (Glasser and Van Essen 2011; Ritchie et al. 2018; Arshad, Stanley, and Raz 2017; Shams, Norris, and Marques 2019; Ganzetti, Wenderoth, and Mantini 2014; Uddin et al. 2019). The interpretation of the T1w/T2w quantifier is still debated (Sandrone et al. 2023); however, as it has become a standard quantifier, both T1w and T2w images are commonly found. Here we test for the best way to use T1w and T2w images to approximate qMRI relaxation. We observed that when T1w/T2w is modified to be T1w/ln(T2w), it can better approximate R1 (Figure 5C). Similarly, ln(T1w/T2w) was a better estimate of R2 than T1w/T2w itself (Figure 5E). These nuanced adjustments hold significant potential for researchers using T1w/T2w, particularly in datasets (e.g., HCP, ABCD) when these are the only available weighted images (and there is no PDw image).

At the ROI level across databases, quantifiers that show strong agreement at the voxel level also demonstrate high and consistent alignment with qMRI parameters (Figure 6). This finding reinforces our initial observation across datasets, suggesting a reliable replication of our results. However, it also indicates that ROI summary statistics may be less sensitive than voxel‐based analyses, as all tested quantifiers show similarly strong agreement. In ROI‐based analysis, results are likely influenced by major differences across ROIs in factors such as myelin, water, and iron levels, which impact both R1 and R2 values. As a result, we observe a high correlation between T1w/T2w and R1 (Figure 6C), comparable to correlations with the proposed quantifiers. This outcome aligns with earlier findings by Drori et al. in the Striatum (Drori, Berman, and Mezer 2022). However, for similar resolutions analysis, such as within specific tissue types like the white matter corpus callosum (Sandrone et al. 2023), this relationship does not hold. Therefore, it is important to emphasize that these measurements are not directly interchangeable, and their agreement may vary depending on analysis resolution and ROI selection. Hence, we note that while the quantifiers presented in this study offer sensitivity to microstructural features, they do not serve as absolute histological markers.

Notably, A potential limitation of dividing T1w by ln(T2w) is the fact that it doesn't cancel out the shared bias between the original images. However, this limitation could potentially be addressed using a postprocessing image bias correction (Glasser et al. 2022).

Not all clinical datasets have T2w images, but many do contain diffusion MRI sequences (e.g., PNC (Satterthwaite et al. 2014), ISBI 2017 (https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds002738), Multimodal DTI of the Human Brain (https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds003027/versions/1.2.0), UK‐Biobank). We showed that the approximation of R1 also can be done using the T2w image that was acquired as part of the diffusion MRI sequence. Thus, our method can expand the semi‐quantitative approximation to additional datasets.

Finally, as we aimed to utilize available clinical datasets for quantitative MRI analysis, we tested the quantifiers on a clinical dataset from the PPMI (Marek et al. 2011). We find the semi‐quantitative contrasts in the PPMI data correlated with the quantitative maps of Dataset B, as measured across multiple brain ROIs, suggesting our approach to be robust across datasets.

Some of the limitations that we faced stemmed from our decision to work with pre‐existing datasets. One limitation of the comparison between quantitative and semi‐quantitative measurements in Datasets B is the fact that T2w and PDw are used both to estimate the R2 map and to calculate ln(T2w/PDw). This creates dependencies in the data, which means that correlating between these images and R2 has an inherent bias. Another limitation is that the T1w, T2w, and PDw images were not scanned at the same resolution, requiring down‐sampling of T1w and R1. These two limitations were addressed when we acquired Dataset C for replication. However, Dataset C has a small sample size (N = 3), which limits the statistical inference. Moreover, Dataset C is comprised of a younger group than Dataset B, which may influence the brain's morphology and relaxometry (Filo et al. 2019; Lorio et al. 2016).

Additional potential limitations are related to the transmit field (B1+). Specifically, when we divided T1w by PDw or T2w to produce T1w/PDw and ln(T2w/PDw), the transmit field cannot be accounted for, and may not be removed in the division process. However, this bias can be addressed using a non‐specific bias correction, for example, the ANTs bias correction (Tustison et al. 2010; Klein et al. 2010). Aiming to minimize such biases, in Figure S6,7 we show such example. Moreover, many scanners use a built‐in transmit field bias correction. However, in our SPGR‐based T1w Figure S2, the bias is apperant and may influence the relationship between T1w‐mectrics (such as T1w/PDw, T1w/T2w) and R1 relaxation parameter, as well as the need for additional bias field correction. This field bias is apparent in Figure S2C,D.

In this paper, we show ln(T2w/PDw) can approximate R2. This log linear method had been previously suggested as a quick way to measure R2, and was shown to be highly affected by iron concentrations (Otto et al. 2011) and other multi‐exponential R2 influences (Pei et al. 2015). Naturally, in this paper we compare only semi‐quantitative values to single‐exponent R1 and R2; however, multi‐exponential relaxation processes may contribute to the signal as well (Whittall, MacKay, and Li 1999). As we acknowledge that comprehensive quantitative mapping can be possible only using multi‐exponential mapping, here we highlight the semi‐quantitative usage of the existing weighted images, in a simple and fast analysis to approximate the single exponent observed R1 and R2. This method can allow researchers to explore the possibilities available for brain quantifications within existing datasets, where the scanning times and sequences are fixed.

To leverage existing datasets, we developed a semi‐quantitative approach to extract microstructurally relevant metrics across various pre‐existing collections. While datasets like HCP provide high‐resolution T1w and T2w images (1 mm or finer), others, such as PPMI and ADNI, offer both PDw and T2w images at a typically lower resolution (approximately 2 mm), with effective resolution defined by the T2w/PDw sequence. Interestingly, in the UK‐ Biobank, high‐resolution T1w and PDw images are available, but T2w is not included and must be approximated using the B0 image from the diffusion sequence. As a result, each quantifier in these datasets will inherently have a different effective resolution. In regions such as the cortex, lower resolution can impact analysis and interpretability due to partial volume effects. Thus, selecting quantification methods that align with each dataset's available image resolution is essential to ensure compatibility and to maximize the utility of each dataset's unique characteristics.

Recently, efforts have focused on reducing the lengthy scanning times needed for quantitative mapping through techniques like MR fingerprinting (Ma et al. 2013; Zhao et al. 2019). This method allows for faster acquisition of quantitative maps in clinically relevant data (Yablonski et al. 2024). However, it is incompatible with existing datasets and requires specialized scanning protocols. We hope that the semi‐quantitative approach presented here can act as a bridge, enabling comparisons between this novel method and the vast array of available clinical datasets.

Conclusions

Weighted images are commonly used in clinical settings and found in many open‐source datasets (such as PPMI, ADNI, UK‐Biobank and ABCD) that rarely contain quantitative MRI mapping. To bridge this gap, we tested three quantifiers, based on simple combination of weighted images. We found that T1w/PDw yields a satisfactory approximation of R1, and rectifies image biases. Similarly, ln(T2w/PDw) is an accurate approximation of R2. We also refined the well‐accepted T1w/T2w approach, offering better quantitative approximations (ln(T1w/T2w), T1w/ln(T2w)), and extended the analysis to databases with diffusion MRI data. When applied to a clinical dataset, we showed substantive correspondence between semi‐quantitative contrasts and quantitative maps. Our work, therefore, presents a pathway for harnessing widely available weighted images to offer approximate quantitative insights, paving the way for enhanced neuroimaging analyses in both research and clinical settings.
