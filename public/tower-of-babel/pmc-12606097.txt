Controllability preservation in complex networks via minimal edge configurations
Authors: Zhuo Sun, Yifan Liu, Rui Zhou, Rushi Li, Mincheng Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advances in networked control systems have resulted in linear time-invariant (LTI) networks of increasing scale and complexity, thereby presenting significant challenges for controllability optimization. This study proposes a novel optimization framework to enhance control efficiency and reduce network complexity in large-scale complex networks, leveraging exact controllability theory and matrix similarity transformations for both directed and undirected topologies. Firstly, a method is introduced for identifying node correspondences before and after similarity transformations of the network’s adjacency matrix. Subsequently, a minimal-edge connection framework is proposed to link Jordan blocks associated with distinct eigenvalues in the Jordan canonical form. Finally, building upon this method and framework, a specific optimization strategy is developed to minimize the number of inter-node connections while preserving network controllability. The proposed approach effectively simplifies the network structure and improves control efficiency.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

In the context of complex networked control systems, networks consist of interconnected nodes with intricate dynamical interactions. These systems are fundamental to modern infrastructure, where efficient control strategies are essential for stability and performance1,2. In urban planning, traffic congestion and energy consumption issues are becoming more prominent, which requires the design of optimal transportation networks3. In the power sector, an effective design of the power grid can reduce energy loss and operating costs4. The construction of communication networks must consider factors such as coverage area and transmission efficiency5,6. Supply chain management must ensure the efficiency of logistics processes and cost reduction7. In social networks, the connections between the nodes directly influence the dissemination of information and the spread of influence8,9. In these complex networks, effective control of the system to achieve specific objectives is a major focus of research. For complex linear time-invariant networks, optimizing controllability cost is a particularly challenging topic10.

One of the fundamental issues in networked control systems, controllability, has received extensive research over the past few decades due to its wide application in natural, social, and complex systems11–16.The concept of controllability originates from control theory. For a given system, if there exist external signals capable of driving the system from any initial state to any specified final state, then the system is considered controllable. In 2007, Lombardi et al.17, inspired by biological network systems, modeled network systems as linear time-invariant systems (LTI) and introduced the controllability problem for network systems. The authors first equated the state transition matrix of a linear system to the weighted adjacency matrix of a network system. They also equated the system state variables with the node states of the network system. This provided a graph-theoretical analysis of network systems. In 2011, Liu et al.18 combined traditional control theory with complex networks, proposing the well-known “minimum input theorem”, which addressed the selection of control nodes in large-scale network systems. However, the minimum input theorem only applies to directed graphs, and no discussion was provided for more general undirected graph scenarios. In 2013, Yuan et al.19 introduced the theory of exact controllability based on the PBH (Popov-Belevitch-Hautus) controllability criterion from control theory. For complex networks with known weights, the minimum number of driver nodes required to control the entire network should equal the maximum geometric multiplicity of all eigenvalues of the network adjacency matrix (with weights). They also provided a framework for selecting driver nodes.Similar works can also be referenced20,21.

Optimization of controllability costs in complex networks can be achieved in two ways. On the one hand, increasing the number of edges can reduce the number of control input nodes. On the other hand, optimizing the network structure can reduce the number of edges22–28. Wang et al.22 first proposed enhancing the controllability of complex networks through topological perturbations. Based on structural controllability theory and the minimum input theorem, they added edges to the original network. This connected the mutually independent matching paths in the state bipartite graph, forming a chain-like network. As a result, only one driver node is needed to control the entire network. Hou et al.29 introduced the concept of rewiring edges to improve the controllability of complex networks. The authors classified the edges in the network into three types: critical edges, normal edges, and redundant edges. They proposed a method to remove redundant edges and reconnect them randomly, effectively reducing the number of driver nodes in the network. However, random rewiring of edges is unproductive. The literature30 proposed achieving the optimal controllability of complex networks by selectively changing the direction of the edges in the network. The authors transformed the original problem into a maximum-independent set problem in a switch network. The literature31 indicates that finding the maximum weighted independent set in a weighted network is an NP-hard problem. For large-scale complex networks, their high computational complexity is intolerable. The paper suggests improving network controllability by reversing as few edges as possible in the network. Other similar works include32–37. Moothedath et al.38 treat complex networks as composite systems made up of multiple subsystems, and propose algorithms based on minimum-cost perfect matching and a combination of minimum-cost perfect matching with stub matching. These algorithms are used to find the smallest set of interconnections, ensuring the structural controllability of the entire composite system. The literature39 applied a similar approach considering homogeneous structured subsystems. Addressing the issue of unreachable nodes in the network, references40,41 propose a comprehensive optimization framework that utilizes graph theory and control theory to determine the minimum number of directed edges needed to be added to the network. This aims to eliminate unreachable nodes and avoid inflated subgraphs in the network, achieving network controllability while minimizing the required costs. Reference42 introduces a method for designing input structures that are tailored to the controllability of complex network structures. By defining redundant unreachable strongly connected components (RISCC) and intermittent unreachable strongly connected components (IISCC), they construct a subnetwork model called the driving network. Based on these definitions and models, the paper develops an efficient algorithm to determine the minimum set of controlled nodes required to achieve full controllability of the network structure. It also provides a range for the number of input nodes and a configuration method for input nodes and controlled nodes to optimize the number of input nodes required. Lastly, references43–45 propose a time-segmented algorithm to restore the controllability of networked control systems without altering the original network structure. This method converts a static uncontrollable network into a time network composed of multiple snapshots. Using a palm structure-based segmentation technique, it transforms the problem into a classic set-cover problem, although the implementation algorithm presented in the paper is quite complex.

Existing studies typically achieve network controllability by either adding edges or optimizing the number of input nodes. In theory, adding about 5% of edges can ensure controllability with a single driver node, but in real networks, edge expansion is constrained by various factors. Network complexity is closely related to the number of edges; more edges lead to a more complex structure. This not only increases the difficulty of visualization and understanding, but also raises computational and storage costs, especially for large networks. Moreover, complex networks are more prone to error propagation or misleading information spread, which affects network reliability. Therefore, effectively managing and optimizing network complexity is essential to ensure efficient operation and accurate information flow.

In this article, we proposed a method analogous to maximum matching to determine the correspondence between network nodes in the adjacency matrix before and after any similarity transformation. In order to transform the adjacency matrix of the complex network into a controllable canonical form, we introduced an optimization framework. Under the premise of complex network controllability, this framework minimizes the number of edges between nodes while ensuring that the minimum number of inputs obtained from exact controllability equals the minimum number of inputs required. It is worth highlighting that our framework has multiple implementation approaches and that we provide an implementation algorithm. Finally, we demonstrate the optimality of the proposed framework.

Results

Node matching between the original and transformed networks

Network structure similarity analysis is an important area in network science, as comparing changes in network structures can reveal patterns and characteristics of network evolution. In this context, similarity transformation is a common operation used to convert a network structure into a similar one for analysis, with the adjacency matrix playing a key role by recording the connectivity between nodes.

It has been observed that after a similarity transformation, the nodes corresponding to the adjacency matrix and the original network nodes exhibit a maximum matching relationship, meaning that the number of nodes remains the same and there exists a one-to-one correspondence between nodes before and after the transformation. Further investigation shows that this correspondence can be achieved using maximum matching. Maximum matching, a fundamental concept in graph theory, refers to finding the largest set of edges in a graph such that no two edges are adjacent. In this scenario, nodes before and after the transformation are treated as graph nodes, and their correspondences are treated as edges; applying a maximum matching algorithm yields the optimal node correspondence.

This approach establishes a robust correspondence between nodes before and after similarity transformation, enhancing our understanding of network structure changes and evolution. It is significant for exploring the structure and function of networks and for uncovering the underlying principles of complex network systems. The combination of similarity transformation and maximum matching thus provides an effective method for analyzing network structure similarity, offering new tools and insights for the advancement of network science.

We will introduce an optimization framework to match the nodes between the original network adjacency matrix and its counterpart after similarity transformation by proposing the following theorem.

Theorem 1

For the adjacency matrix of any given connected network, there exists a one-to-one correspondence between the nodes of the network before and after the similarity transformation.

Minimal connection of Jordan blocks with different eigenvalues

A method is subsequently presented to achieve minimal interconnections between Jordan blocks associated with distinct eigenvalues via similarity transformations.

The Jordan canonical form is an important concept in linear algebra, describing a specific structure of a matrix composed of several Jordan blocks. In control theory, exact controllability theory tells us that each Jordan block requires only one driver node to achieve full controllability. This means that to control a network system, we only need to focus on the driver node of each Jordan block, rather than controlling each individual node. However, in Jordan canonical form, multiple Jordan blocks may correspond to the same eigenvalue. According to exact controllability theory, the minimum number of inputs required by the system depends on the maximum geometric multiplicity of the network adjacency matrix. This is the number of Jordan blocks corresponding to the same eigenvalue in the Jordan canonical form of the network’s adjacency matrix.

Since each subnetwork corresponding to a Jordan block can be considered a chain, we give the definition of a head node (or tail node) according to this fact. The starting node of this chain is referred to as the head node of this Jordan block, and the terminating node is referred to as the tail node of this Jordan block. The above four connection methods are only effective for Jordan blocks with distinct eigenvalues. It is worth emphasizing that the adjacency matrix formed by these connection methods is similar to the original network’s adjacency matrix (it does not alter the properties of the original network). There are also no requirements for the weights of the connecting edges. Additionally, the network does not contain unreachable nodes, therefore, the exact controllability theory is applicable. The effectiveness of the connection methods is demonstrated in the “Methods” section.

To connect multiple Jordan blocks with distinct eigenvalues, specific conditions must be satisfied: any two Jordan blocks can be connected using any of the four specified methods, provided that the resulting network contains no unreachable nodes. This property allows for flexible connections between Jordan blocks in the Jordan canonical form, tailored to the requirements of the application scenario. Importantly, the resulting network requires only one driver node to achieve complete controllability. This conclusion is valid from both the graph-theoretic perspective and the standpoint of exact controllability theory.

The key is to ensure that each connection operation introduces only one edge between two Jordan blocks. This effectively simplifies the network structure and reduces the resources required for control. Through this approach, the network structure can be simplified without compromising controllability, thereby improving overall control efficiency.

In addition, we can incorporate machine learning and deep learning techniques, using large datasets to train models to better predict the optimal direction for network optimization.

Discussion

This paper addresses the controllability structure optimization problem of linear time-invariant complex networks and proposes an innovative optimization framework. The framework is based on the theory of exact controllability, which is used to determine whether the network remains controllable and whether the minimum number of input nodes changes before and after network transformation. It further incorporates the theory of matrix similarity transformations as a key tool and is applicable to both directed and undirected networks. By analyzing the controllability features of the network and leveraging matrix similarity transformations, the proposed approach aims to achieve efficient control of network nodes, thereby simplifying the network structure.

Firstly, the study introduces a method to match node correspondences before and after similarity transformations of the network adjacency matrix, along with a framework to optimize connections between Jordan blocks in the Jordan canonical form. These methods provide new perspectives and tools for the analysis and optimization of network controllability. Based on this, the study develops an optimization strategy. This strategy involves transforming the original network adjacency matrix using similarity transformations to obtain its Jordan canonical form and determining node correspondences before and after the transformation. This process helps to understand the structure of the network and sets the stage for further optimization steps. In addition, the study proposes a specific optimization framework based on the similarity transformation approach. The goal is to minimize the number of edges in the network while ensuring network controllability,thereby simplifying the network structure. The paper presents a possible algorithm to implement this optimization strategy. Implementing this optimization strategy can effectively reduce the resources required for network control, enhance control efficiency, and have significant implications for designing efficient control systems.

In this work, we focus on networks with pairwise connections and analyze their controllability within the framework of traditional graph models. However, we recognize that the proposed approach has certain limitations. First, higher-order interactions, such as those captured by hypergraphs or simplicial complexes, have recently attracted considerable attention, as they represent multi-node dependencies that cannot be reduced to pairwise links46,47. Extending the present method to higher-order networks is beyond the scope of this study, though it provides potential directions for future research. Second, while the method is theoretically applicable to both directed and undirected networks, the current analysis is restricted to adjacency matrices with known weights; networks with unknown or uncertain weights require further investigation. Finally, in numerical computations, similarity transformations and Jordan decompositions are inevitably affected by rounding errors, which may impact algorithmic stability, particularly in large-scale networks. Although numerically stable decomposition methods were employed in the implementation, this potential issue should be explicitly acknowledged.

Existing studies focus on different optimization objectives. For example, edge addition under single-input conditions40 has a time complexity of ; optimizing the number of inputs while preserving edges42 requires ; and transforming a static uncontrollable network into a structurally controllable temporal network without changing inputs or edges44 has complexity. Despite these differences, their computational costs are comparable to that of the method proposed here, which operates at .

In contrast, our approach achieves network controllability with minimal edge additions, maintaining manageable computational complexity, and demonstrates superior performance in edge reduction, as evidenced by the experiments.

In summary, this study’s optimization framework and algorithm provide an effective solution to the controllability cost problem in linear time-invariant complex networks. By applying exact controllability theory and matrix similarity transformation theory, this research not only enhances the efficiency of network control but also lays a new theoretical and practical foundation for optimizing network structure. This achievement holds significant theoretical and practical value in the advancement of networked control, as well as in the achievement of efficient network control in practical applications.

Methods

Structural controllability and exact controllability

In many complex networks, system parameters (such as elements in matrix A) are often not precisely known. In fact, we usually cannot accurately measure the weights of connections and can only determine whether a connection exists. Therefore, even though conceptually feasible, it is quite challenging to numerically verify the rank condition of Kalman using fixed weights. Lin proposed a framework in 1974 that systematically avoids this limitation48. If the entries in the matrices A and B are fixed at zero (indicating that there is no relationship between certain state variables of the nodes) or independent nonzero parameters (indicating a relationship between two corresponding state variables), then the LTI system (A, B) is a structured system network. Structured systems represent a large class of linear systems. If there exists a set of nonzero parameters that make the state of the system controllable, then the structured system (A, B) is said to be structurally controllable.

The structural controllability framework provides a new way to determine whether a system is controllable, but it does not provide a method to find the number of inputs required by the system or the positions of the driving nodes. Therefore, in 2011, Liu et al.18 proposed the “minimum input theory”. The proposed minimal edge configuration is grounded in exact controllability theory and employs similarity transformations as a tool. Since similarity transformations do not alter the maximum geometric multiplicity of the adjacency matrix, controllability of the network remains unchanged before and after the transformation under the framework of exact controllability theory.
