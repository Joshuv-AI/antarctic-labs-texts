Cerebral small vessel disease is an independent determinant of dysphagia after acute stroke
Authors: Janina Wilmskoetter, Heather Bonilha, Bethany J Wolf, Emma Tracy, Allen Chang, Bonnie Martin-Harris, Christine Anne Holmstedt, Leonardo Bonilha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Dysphagia after acute stroke is not only related to the stroke lesion but also to the individual's pre-stroke brain health.•Brain atrophy and white matter hyperintensities are related to more severe pharyngeal swallowing impairments.•Perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia are related to longer oral and pharyngeal transit times during swallowing.Keywords: Stroke, Small vessel disease, Dysphagia, Modified barium swallow study

Introduction

Dysphagia is commonly observed after a stroke, affecting the majority of individuals, particularly in the initial days after the stroke (Martino et al., 2005). Why some individuals present with dysphagia and others do not, is partly explained by stroke lesion characteristics. Stroke lesions affecting the wide-ranging neural network controlling swallowing can lead to a disruption of swallowing control and result in dysphagia (Babaei et al., 2013, Cola et al., 2010, Hamdy, 2006, Leopold and Daniels, 2010, Lowell et al., 2012, Martin et al., 2001, Wilmskoetter et al., 2019a, Wilmskoetter et al., 2018). Nevertheless, two patients with the same lesion location may present very differently, with only one of them having dysphagia. Thus, patient characteristics beyond the stroke lesion also contribute to the incidence of dysphagia after stroke. Rather recently, brain health – the tissue integrity outside of lesioned brain areas – has been identified as a major contributor to the incidence and recovery of post-stroke impairments. However, our knowledge of to what extent brain health impacts post-stroke dysphagia is scarce.

Brain health is an umbrella term for the structural and/or functional integrity of the brain. A healthy brain has presumably a better reserve and resilience when an injury occurs compared to a brain with compromised brain health. One commonly recognized structural marker of compromised brain health is cerebral small vessel brain disease (cSVD). cSVD is strongly linked to the aging process and is typically an accidental and subclinical finding in stroke MRIs. The occurrence of cSVD is linked to cerebrovascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension, diabetes), and while initially subclincial, cSVD is the leading cause of cognitive decline and functional loss in the elderly and is associated with a higher risk of dementia, stroke, and death (Debette et al., 2019, Duering et al., 2023, Hannan et al., 2023, Pantoni, 2010, Wardlaw et al., 2015, Wardlaw et al., 2013). Importantly, if cSVD pathologies are noted in a brain scan of an individual who just had an acute stroke, one can infer that these pathologies reflect cumulative cardiovascular changes over time prior to the stroke and are not a result of the stroke lesion. Thus, analyzing cSVD pathologies in individuals with acute stroke allows an assessment of their pre-stroke (baseline) brain integrity and its impact on functional impairments when an acute stroke occurs.

Besides other biomarkers, there are three main signs of cSVD in brain MRIs 1) brain atrophy, 2) white matter hyperintensities (WMH), and 3) perivascular spaces (Duering et al., 2023, Wardlaw et al., 2013). Brain atrophy manifests as an increase in cerebrovascular fluid (CSF) spaces and a decrease in gray matter volume (Luo et al., 2020, Scahill et al., 2003). These volumetric brain changes are especially prevalent in late adulthood after the 6th life decade when the slope for CSF volume increase is steepest (Bethlehem et al., 2022). While some brain atrophy with aging is common, the degree of atrophy is highly variable across individuals. This is the case even if no diseases are present that may explain accelerated brain atrophy, such as neurodegenerative diseases. Thus, while brain atrophy is not an exclusive sign of cSVD, it commonly occurs in cSVD and relates to an individual’s prognosis (Duering et al., 2023).

WMH are located in the white matter and affect white matter integrity (Gouw et al., 2011, Gouw et al., 2008). Notably, the size of WMH can increase over time and can have indirect effects on the surrounding tissue such as cortical atrophy. The underlying pathologies are often unclear, however, are presumed to vary. For instance, severe WMH have been linked to gliosis, axonal loss, demyelination, and vacuolation, whereas less severe WMH have been linked to endothelial and microglial activation (Fernando et al., 2004, Gouw et al., 2011, Munoz et al., 1993). Considering this continuum of severity and pathologies, it has been suggested that as the WMH lesions are still subtle, they might be reversible, which does not appear the case for more severe and progressed WMH lesions (Wardlaw et al., 2015). WMH have been isolated as a separate vascular precursor for dementia, stroke, and cognitive decline (Debette and Markus, 2010, Wardlaw et al., 2013).

Perivascular spaces, also called Virchow-Robin spaces, are fluid-filled circular, oval, or linear spaces following along the path of small perforating vessels in the brain (Duering et al., 2023). These spaces are most notable in regions such as the basal ganglia, centrum semiovale, hippocampus, and mesencephalon (Adams et al., 2013). While a few perivascular spaces may be considered physiological, a significant burden of these spaces is linked to common neurological conditions such as stroke, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, dementia, and Alzheimer's disease (Duering et al., 2023, Zhu et al., 2010). Notably, perivascular spaces often precede the appearance of WMH. They can increase in size over time and are indicative of impaired clearance of interstitial fluid and waste from the brain (Duering et al., 2023).

Our understanding of how structural brain health pathologies, i.e., atrophy, WMH, and perivascular spaces, affect swallowing is scarce. Few prior studies assessed the relationship between WMH or brain atrophy and dysphagia and revealed conflicting and inconclusive evidence. For example, Matevelli et al. (2023) assessed the predictive value of clinical, cognitive, and neuroimaging features for dysphagia in a cohort of 228 acute ischemic stroke patients. Neither WMH nor brain atrophy was significantly related to dysphagia at admission or discharge from the acute hospital stay (Mattavelli et al., 2023). Flowers et al. (2017) assessed the impact of WMH and brain atrophy on dysphagia in 160 individuals with acute ischemic stroke and found that atrophy but not WMH were predictive of dysphagia (Flowers et al., 2017). These results conflict with findings from other studies implicating WMH as a significant predictor of acute dysphagia in individuals with ischemic stroke (Fandler et al., 2017, Hu et al., 2023, Toscano et al., 2015, Zhang et al., 2021). However, these studies were limited by rather imprecise methods of identifying dysphagia, as they used the Functional Oral Intake Scale (Crary et al., 2005), clinical bedside swallow evaluations, or the presence of a feeding tube. Those few studies that used more precise swallow assessments, such as the modified barium swallow study (MBSS), focused on WMH and revealed a significant relationship between WMH and oral and pharyngeal components of swallowing. Moon and colleagues (2017) conducted a retrospective analysis of 63 individuals with an acute ischemic stroke who had completed an MBSS (Moon et al., 2017). They found worse oral swallowing impairments (oral transit time, swallowing response time) in people with more severe WMH compared to people with less severe WMH. In a follow-up study of 88 ischemic stroke patients, Moon and colleagues (2019) explored the impact of specific locations of WMH and found that WMH involving the corticobulbar tract were significantly related to impaired laryngeal elevation and delayed pharyngeal transit time (Moon et al., 2019). Further, Levine and colleagues (1992) assessed the swallow function of 49 neurologically normal adults and found a relationship between the severity of periventricular WMH (PVH) and the duration of oropharyngeal swallowing for semisolids (Levine et al., 1992).

Thus, the current state of evidence on the impact of brain health on dysphagia after stroke is underwhelming due to the vast majority of studies employing non-imaging-based dysphagia assessments and few studies assessing other brain markers than WMH. Further, it is unclear whether brain health carries a clinically meaningful value beyond stroke lesion characteristics to explain post-stroke dysphagia. Understanding to what degree post-stroke dysphagia can be attributed to the acute stroke lesion and to what degree brain health could guide clinicians in developing effective interventions and treatment strategies that address the broader neurological context beyond the stroke lesion. Thus, in this study, we sought to advance the evidence by assessing different cSVD brain markers (WMH, brain atrophy, and perivascular spaces) and their relationship with post-stroke swallowing impairments visualized by MBSS. Specifically, we assessed individuals with acute stroke to rule out stroke-related (remote) effects on the residual brain integrity allowing an estimate of individuals’ pre-stroke brain health. We hypothesized that worse brain atrophy, WMH, and more perivascular spaces are linked to worse acute swallowing impairments independent of the stroke lesion size and site.

Methods

Participants

We conducted a retrospective observational study on 40 individuals with an acute first-ever ischemic stroke who were admitted to the Medical University of South Carolina. Data was leveraged from a subgroup of participants whom we included in a previous research study (Wilmskoetter et al., 2019a). Participants were included if they had no history of dysphagia before the stroke, no other known diseases that likely cause dysphagia (e.g., head-neck cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, esophageal diseases), were at least 21 years old, had an MRI and MBSS during the hospital stay and no neurological worsening between the MBSS and MRI. For the current study, participants were also excluded if the T1-weighted MRI or tissue segmentation was of poor quality (e.g., motion-degraded). The participants were 21 females and 19 males, with a mean age of 67.15 years (SD 15.38, median 66, min–max 42–95). The MRI was completed on average 1.5 days after admission, and on average (median) 2 days were between the MRI and MBSS. Demographic and clinical data were extracted from medical records. The study was approved by the IRB committee of the Medical University of South Carolina.

MRI processing

Each participant received routine MRI scans as part of standard stroke diagnostic procedures. The neuroimaging data utilized in this study was sourced from these diagnostic assessments. Due to the span of admission years (2009–2017) for the included participants and the advancements in diagnostic radiology technology during this period, there were variations in the MRI sequence protocols. The MR DICOM images were converted into NIfTI files using the software dcm2niix (Li et al., 2016).

Lesion delineation

A trained study team member (JW) manually delineated the stroke lesions on the DWI using the software MRIcron (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricron). Then, a neurologist (LB) reviewed all lesion drawings for accuracy and precision. At the time of the lesion drawings, both team members were blinded to the MBSS results. Lesion maps in diffusion space were normalized into the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) 152 non-linear asymmetric standard brain template. First, smoothed the lesion maps with a 3-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel to remove uneven edges associated with the manual drawing of lesions. Then, we binarized the smoothed lesion maps (lesioned vs not lesioned tissue), and lastly, transformed the participant’s DW-MR image onto standard space. For the last step, the transformation into standard space, we applied an enantiomorphic approach (Nachev et al., 2008) where the stroke was replaced by the mirrored equivalent of the intact hemisphere using the nii_preprocess pipeline (https://github.com/neurolabusc/nii_preprocess) and SPM12′s unified segmentation-normalization. After transformation into standard space, the lesion was placed back into the normalized image (Rorden et al., 2012). The stroke lesion volume was calculated using the lesion maps in standard space to allow comparison between participants (see Fig. 1 for a lesion overlay). The mean lesion volume was 102.27 ml (SD 101.64, median 92.51, min–max 0.21–360.72) across participants.

Structural brain analysis

For each participant, we analyzed their structural brain MRI to assess brain atrophy, WMH, perivascular spaces, and stroke lesion damage to the right and left corticobulbar tracts.

Computation of brain atrophy

Because the goal was to quantify brain atrophy of the residual brain tissue, the stroke lesion was excluded from the atrophy estimations. Thus, for each participant, we segmented their chimeric structural T1-weighted image into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) using SPM12 (Fig. 2). Segmentation accuracy was verified through visual quality control. The segmented images were then affine registered into MNI152 space using a non-linear spatial registration and SPM12′s DARTEL toolbox. Gray matter, white matter, and CSF volume were separately computed for each participant.

Scoring of white matter hyperintensities

WMH were identified as an increased signal in the white matter on fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images. Typically, WMH are located periventricular (PVH) or in the deep white matter (DWMH). The presence and severity of WMH were scored using the 4-point ordinal Fazekas rating scale (Fazekas et al., 1987). We derived separate scores for PVH and DWMH. For PVH, scores of “0″ represent absent hyperintensities, scores of “1” caps or pencil-thin lining, scores of “2” smooth halo, and scores of “3” irregular periventricular signal extending into the deep white matter. For DWMH, scores of “0” represent absent hyperintensities, scores of “1” punctuate foci, scores of “2” beginning confluence, and scores of “3” large confluent areas (Fig. 3A). Because the Fazekas scale is an ordinal rating scale, we transformed the PVH and DWMH Fazekas scores into binary dummy variables to use them as independent variables in the regression models. The reference category for PVH was a score of 1 (because no individual had a PVH score of 0) and for DWMH a score of 0 or 1.

Scoring of perivascular spaces

The number of perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia was counted on T2-weighted MRIs following standard guidelines (Wardlaw et al., 2013). Structures that comprised the basal ganglia were the caudate, internal capsule, thalamus, insula, lentiform nucleus, and the external/extreme capsule. Perivascular spaces were counted in both hemispheres separately, and the highest score was then recorded (Fig. 3B). If a stroke lesion covered (part of) the basal ganglia on one side, the contralesional side was automatically used to count the perivascular spaces. The count was transformed into an ordinal score ranging from “0″ to “4”. A score of “0” represented no perivascular spaces (0), “1” mild (0–10), “2” moderate (11–20), “3” frequent (21–40), and “4” severe (>40) perivascular spaces. Same as for the Fazekas scores, we transformed the ordinal perivascular space score into binary dummy variables. The reference category was a score of 1 (no individual had a score of 0).

Corticobulbar tract damage

To control for a potential effect of the stroke lesion location on the relationship between cSVD and MBSS measures, we estimated the stroke-related damage to the right and left corticobulbar tracts. Because our sample size was relatively small (n = 40) we had to limit the number of control variables, and thus, sought to control for a brain area that is very commonly reported to contribute to swallowing control. We chose the corticobulbar tracts because they have been repeatedly identified to result in dysphagia when lesioned (Alvar et al., 2021, Im et al., 2020, Jang et al., 2020, Jang et al., 2021, Kim et al., 2022, Qin et al., 2023). The corticobulbar tract primarily connects motor areas in the cortex with nuclei in the brainstem which connect to face and neck muscles through cranial nerves. Thus, the corticobulbar tract contributes to the transmission of signals from the motor cortices to the swallowing muscles.

To estimate the integrity of the study participants’ corticobulbar tracts, we computed the intersection between the stroke lesions and the streamlines of the corticobulbar tracts in a standard brain template. For the template, we used the populations-based human connectome project tractography atlas derived from diffusion imaging from 1,065 healthy adults (HCP-1065) (Yeh, 2022). Using the software DSI Studio (http://dsi-studio.labsolver.org, developed by Frank Yeh), we overlaid each participant’s stroke lesion onto the right and left corticobulbar tract within the HCP-1065 template (Fig. 4). We calculated the percentage of streamlines of each of the two tracts intersecting the stroke lesions. We assumed that streamlines intersecting a stroke lesion will be (at least partly) damaged by the stroke and thus, will be an estimate for the integrity of the corticobulbar tracts. Across all participants, on average (mean) 34.45 % (SD, median 0 %, min–max 0 %-100 %) of the right and 46.32 % (SD, median 0 %, min–max 0–100 %) of the left corticobulbar intersected the stroke lesion.

MBSS analysis

All MBSSs were conducted as part of the participants’ standard clinical care and completed in tandem by a radiologist and speech-language pathologist (SLP). A pulsed x-ray beam was used at 30 pulses per second and images were digitally recorded at 30 frames per second. Bolus trials using standard consistencies of barium sulfate (Varibar®, Bracco Diagnostics; Monroe Township, NJ) as the contrast agent were administered following the standardized and validated Modified Barium Swallow Impairment Profile (MBSImP™©) (Clain et al., 2022, Martin-Harris et al., 2008). The protocol included the administration in the lateral projection of two teaspoons (5 ml), one single sip, and one sequential swallow of thin liquid barium (Varibar Thin Liquid 40 % which equals level 0 – thin of the International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI) (Steele, 2017)), followed by one teaspoon (5 ml), one single, and one sequential swallow of nectar thick liquid (Varibar Nectar 40 % which equals IDDSI level 2 – mildly thick), one teaspoon (5 ml) of honey thick liquid (Varibar Honey 40 % which equals IDDSI level 4 – extremely thick), one teaspoon (5 ml) of pudding (Varibar Pudding 40 % which equals IDDSI level 4 – pureed), and one bite of a shortbread cookie coated with pudding (which equals IDDSI level 7). Following the lateral projection, patients were positioned into a posterior-anterior projection with the administration of one teaspoon (5 ml) of nectar thick liquid (IDDSI level 2) and one teaspoon (5 ml) of pudding (IDDSI level 4). Depending on the severity of a patient’s swallowing impairment, the protocol was aborted early for some patients if the radiologist and SLP had concerns about the patient’s safety.

The digitally recorded MBSSs were then analyzed using a frame-by-frame approach to obtain MBSImP, and Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS) scores (Rosenbek et al., 1996), and measures of oral transit, pharyngeal transit, and hyolaryngeal transit time (Kendall et al., 2000, Logemann et al., 1993). All MBSSs were scored by one rater and 20 % of the MBSSs additionally by a second rater to establish inter-rater reliability for the MBSImP, PAS and timing measures. Both raters held a current MBSImP certification with > 90 % reliability. Intra-rater reliability was also established on 20 % of the MBSSs. At the time of the MBSS ratings, the raters were blinded to the MRI analyses.

Modified Barium swallow impairment Profile scores

The MBSImP is a standardized and validated tool to assess 17 different swallow components along the continuum of the swallow, with 6 components representing oral swallowing, 10 components representing pharyngeal swallowing, and 1 component representing esophageal swallowing. Each component is scored on a 3- to 5-point ordinal scale. The component scores were determined for each swallow task separately, followed by computing an overall impression score for each component, which is the worst score across all swallow tasks. The respective overall impression scores for the components were tallied to derive a summative score for oral and pharyngeal swallowing function, henceforth referred to as oral total and pharyngeal total scores. The oral total score has a minimum value of 0 (no impairment for any of the 6 components) and a maximum value of 22 (most severe impairment for all 6 components), whereas the pharyngeal total score has a minimum value of 0 (no impairment for any of the 10 components) and a maximum value of 29 (most severe impairment for all 10 components).

Penetration aspiration scale scoring

The PAS is an 8-point ordinal scale quantifying the depth of and response to bolus airway invasion. A score of “1″ represents no entrance of bolus material into the laryngeal vestibule, scores of “2”, “3”, “4”, and “5” represent penetration, and scores of “6”, “7” and “8” represent aspiration of bolus material. We calculated the worst PAS score (Steele and Grace-Martin, 2017)across all swallows and then dichotomized the score using a threshold of “5” (PAS ≤ 5 vs. PAS > 5). Our goal was to identify patients with clinically significant swallow safety impairments (aspiration) which would likely have clinical implications.

Calculation of swallow timing measures

We computed oral and pharyngeal transit times on the first swallow of the 5 ml thin liquid swallowing tasks. This task was used because it was the only task administered twice and thus, allowed to control for some degree of intra-task variability. Oral transit time (OTT) was defined as the time between the onset of posterior movement of the bolus head in the oral cavity during the first productive tongue movement (a movement that results in transporting at least part of the bolus into the pharynx) until the bolus tail passes the ramus of the mandible (Wilmskoetter et al., 2018). Pharyngeal transit time (PTT) was defined as the time between the bolus head’s arrival at the ramus of the mandible until the bolus tail passes through the pharyngoesophageal segment (Logemann et al., 1993). Of note, the bolus head was defined as the leading edge of the bolus, and the bolus tail as the finishing edge of the bolus or of the part of the bolus that was swallowed.

We obtained OTT and PTT for each of the two 5 ml thin liquid swallowing tasks and then averaged the two values for each timing measure. Only 24 of the 40 participants had two adequately visualized and recorded 5 ml thin liquid swallows, and thus, for OTT and PTT data from these 24 individuals were analyzed. Importantly, the group of 24 individuals did not statistically differ from the group of all 40 individuals regarding any of the demographic, medical, and brain structural measures (p > 0.05 using Mann-Whitney U Test or Pearson Chi-Square Test where applicable). OTT and PTT outliers (>4,000 ms) were identified through visual inspection of the data distribution.

Statistical analyses

We performed multiple linear or logistic regression to assess the relationship between brain structural measures (independent variables) and MBSS swallowing measures (dependent variables). For each swallow measure (MBSImP oral total, MBSImP pharyngeal total, PAS, OTT, PTT) and brain structural measure (CSF volume, PVH, DWMH, perivascular spaces) we performed separate models, resulting in 20 models in total. Separate models were performed to avoid multicollinearity among the independent variables, because brain atrophy, WMH, and perivascular spaces were significantly associated with each other.

Because the PVH, DWMH, and perivascular space variables each had three categories and were transformed into binary dummy variables, the models including these variables included two independent variables for each brain marker. Thus, while we computed 20 regression models, we assessed the predictive values of 35 predictors (5 of CSF volume, 10 of PVH, 10 of DWMH, and 10 of perivascular spaces).

In each of the 20 models, we controlled for the average stroke lesion overlap with the right and left corticobulbar tracts, stroke lesion volume, and the number of days between the MRI and MBSS. Further, for all significant models, we assessed the impact of chronological age on the relationships between cSVD biomarkers and swallowing measures to determine whether cSVD biomarkers provided an additional explanatory value beyond chronological age.

For all tests, we used a P-threshold of 0.05. One-tailed statistical tests were applied based on the assumption that cSVD pathologies (i.e., higher CSF volume, more severe WMH, more perivascular spaces) will lead to impairment and not improvement. To correct for multiple comparisons across the 20 regression models, we applied Bonferroni correction and set the Bonferroni-corrected significance level to 0.05/20 = 0.0025. We used IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28, released 2021, IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA)).

Results

Descriptive results of the structural brain markers

Brain atrophy

The 40 participants had a mean grey matter volume of 0.53 L (SD 0.10, median 0.54, min–max 0.28–0.73), mean white matter volume of 0.51 L (SD 0.10, median 0.50, min–max 0.25–0.72), and a mean CSF volume of 0.36 L (SD 0.12, median 0.33, min–max 0.10–0.61) (Fig. 5A). Thus, brain tissue volume varied across participants. As expected, chronological age was positively correlated with CSF volume (Pearson’s r = 0.527, 2-sided p < 0.001). Older participants had more brain atrophy.

White matter hyperintensities

On average (median), participants had a PVH score of “1″ (range 1–3) and a DWMH score of “1” (range 0–3) (Fig. 5B). Same as for brain atrophy, chronological age was positively correlated with WMH severity (PVH: Pearson’s r = 0.505, 2-sided p < 0.001; DWMH: Pearson’s r = 0.474, 2-sided p = 0.002). Older participants had more severe WMH.

Perivascular spaces

On average (median), participants had 15 (range 4–31) perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia which represented an ordinal score of “2″ (range 1–3) (Fig. 5C). Chronological age was also significantly correlated with the number of perivascular spaces (Pearson’s r = 0.366, 2-sided p = 0.02). Older participants had more perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia compared to younger participants.

Descriptive results of the modified barium swallow study measures

Modified Barium swallow impairment Profile oral and pharyngeal total scores

Across participants, the mean oral total score was 11.78 (SD 3.42, median 12.50, min–max 0–17.00), and the mean pharyngeal total score was 7.80 (SD 4.00, median 8.00, min–max 0–16.00) (Fig. 5D).

Penetration aspiration scale scores

Twelve participants (30 %) had worst PAS scores of “>5”, and 28 participants (70 %) had worst PAS scores of “≤5” (Fig. 5E).

Oral and pharyngeal transit scores

One outlier was identified in OTT and two outliers in PTT and excluded from further analyses (Fig. 5F). The mean OTT across participants was 1.53 s (SD 0.91, median 1.42, min–max 0.35–3.67), and the mean PTT was 1.75 s (SD 0.87, median 1.61, min–max 0.60–3.60).

Relationship between cSVD markers and swallow measures

MBSImP oral total scores

As tested using linear regression, there was no significant relationship between MBSImP oral total scores (dependent variable) and any MRI marker (atrophy, WMH, perivascular spaces) while controlling for the mean percentage of the right and left corticobulbar tracts intersecting the stroke lesion, stroke lesion volume, and the number of days between the brain MRI and MBSS (Table 1). Further, MBSImP oral total scores were not correlated with chronological age (Spearman’s Rho = -0.23, p = 0.886).

MBSImP pharyngeal total scores

Higher CSF volume was significantly associated with worse MBSImP pharyngeal total scores (p = 0.0280) while controlling for the average percentage of the right and left corticobulbar tracts intersecting the stroke lesion, stroke lesion volume, and the number of days between the brain MRI and MBSS (Table 1). CSF volume alone explained 11.2 % of the variance in pharyngeal total scores. An increase of 10 ml in CSF volume was associated with an average increase of 1.11 in the MBSImP pharyngeal total score.

In a separate regression model PVH scores of “3″ compared to PVH scores of “0” or “1” were significantly associated with worse MBSImP pharyngeal total scores (p = 0.0365) while controlling for the average percentage of the right and left corticobulbar tracts intersecting the stroke lesion, stroke lesion volume, and the number of days between the brain MRI and MBSS (Table 1). A PVH score of “3” explained 5.8 % of the variance in pharyngeal total scores. The presence of a PVH score of “3” was associated with an average increase of 2.64 in the MBSImP pharyngeal total score.

After correcting for multiple comparisons, neither CSF volume nor PVH remained significantly associated with the MBSImP pharyngeal total scores. MBSImP pharyngeal total scores were not correlated with chronological age (Spearman’s Rho = 0.19, p = 0.2330).

Penetration aspiration scale scores

There was a significant relationship between the PAS scores (dependent variable) and DWMH while controlling for the average percentage of the right and left corticobulbar tracts intersecting the stroke lesion, stroke lesion volume, and the number of days between the brain MRI and MBSS (Table 1). DWMH scores of “3″ compared to DWMH scores of “0” or “1” were significantly different between individuals with a worst PAS score of “>5” and individuals with a worst PAS score of “≤5” (p = 0.0375). The model had a classification accuracy of 75 % for PAS scores of “>5” compared to PAS scores of “≤5”.

After correcting for multiple comparisons, DWMH were not significantly associated with PAS scores. Participants with PAS scores of “>5” and participants with PAS scores of “≤5” did not differ in their age (Independent-Samples Mann-Whitney U Test: U = 141.0, p = 0.439).

Timing measures

More perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia predicted longer OTT. Perivascular space scores of “3″ compared to scores of “0” or “1” were significantly associated with longer OTT (p = 0.0015) while controlling for the average percentage of the right and left corticobulbar tracts intersecting the stroke lesion, stroke lesion volume, and the number of days between the brain MRI and MBSS (Table 1). Perivascular spaces remained a significant predictor of OTT even when controlling for chronological age in the same model (p = 0.007). Perivascular space scores of “3” alone explained 27.5 % of the variance in OTT. The presence of a PVH score of “3” was associated with an average increase of 1571 ms in OTT.

In a separate regression model, perivascular space scores of “3″ compared to scores of “0” or “1” were also significantly associated with longer PTT (p = 0.0050) while controlling for the average percentage of the right and left corticobulbar tracts intersecting the stroke lesion, stroke lesion volume, and the number of days between the brain MRI and MBSS (Table 1). Perivascular spaces remained a significant predictor of PTT even when controlling for chronological age in the same model (p = 0.0105). Perivascular space scores of “3” alone explained 25.1 % of the variance in PTT. The presence of a PVH score of “3” was associated with an average increase of 1435 ms in PTT.

After correcting for multiple comparisons, a perivascular space score of “3″ remained significantly associated with the OTT, but not with PTT. OTT and PTT were not correlated with chronological age (Spearman’s Rho = 0.15, p = 0.4930, and Spearman’s Rho = 0.20, p = 0.4080, respectively).

Discussion

The variability in post-stroke dysphagia cannot be solely attributed to stroke lesion characteristics, such as location, volume, or chronicity, as they only partially explain observed inter-individual differences. Instead, our study provides evidence that dysphagia severity is also linked to the overall health of the brain beyond the immediate acute stroke lesion. Indeed, pre-stroke aging effects on the brain, encompassing atrophy, WMH, and perivascular spaces, were significantly related to dysphagia severity after a first-ever ischemic stroke, regardless of the stroke lesion volume and location. Importantly, brain atrophy, WMH, and perivascular spaces were not associated with the stroke lesion volume (Spearman’s correlation, p > 0.05). The absent correlation between brain health markers and the stroke volume emphasizes the additional explanatory value derived from the brain health at the moment of the stroke and that the brain health markers are not simply a result of the remote effects of the stroke.

Specifically, our study revealed that higher levels of brain atrophy, quantified by CSF volume, and more severe PVH scores were associated with worse pharyngeal swallowing impairments, as measured by the MBSImP. Moreover, severe DWMH scores correlated with instances of aspiration during swallowing, and more perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia were associated with longer OTT and PTT. However, these associations did not retain significance after correcting for multiple comparisons, except for the relationship between perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia and OTT. Although atrophy and WMH did not maintain significance after multiple comparison corrections, their initial significance highlights their potential importance, especially considering the small sample size of our study.

Notably, perivascular spaces had a high level of explanatory value for OTT and PTT (27.5 % and 25.1 %, respectively) and remained significant predictors even when controlling for chronological age. This is important because it implies that perivascular spaces in the context of swallowing are not just a surrogate of chronological age. This inference is further supported by our finding that chronological age did not emerge as a significant predictor of any swallow variables. While the lack of a significant relationship between age and other swallow measures may seem surprising, it is consistent with previous research suggesting that age-related changes in swallow function are not a linear progression, but rather a complex interplay of factors influenced by age, health status, and environmental factors. The finding of brain structural markers, not chronological age, being related to swallow measures emphasizes the variability in the aging process among individuals. While brain atrophy, WMH, and perivascular spaces are more likely to occur in older compared to younger individuals, there is variability in the severity of these brain structural pathologies even among people of the same chronological age. The information we can derive from differences in brain aging has also been explored for other functions after a stroke. For example, advanced brain aging is a significant predictor of post-stroke aphasia recovery, independently of chronological age (Busby et al., 2023, Wilmskoetter et al., 2023). Thus, while older age may be a risk factor for certain aspects of swallow impairment, it is not necessarily a predictor for all aspects of dysphagia after stroke and may better be reflected in brain anatomical changes.

Research into the mechanisms underlying the impact of perivascular spaces on post-stroke impairments remains relatively nascent. Therefore, our study contributes valuable supportive evidence to advance this line of investigation. In contrast, WMH have received more extensive scrutiny in prior research. Our findings not only align with but also build upon existing literature regarding the significance of WMH in post-stroke impairments. For example, Moon and colleagues (2017) and Guanyabens and colleagues (2023) both found that more severe WMH were significantly associated with the presence of penetration or aspiration (Guanyabens et al., 2023, Moon et al., 2017) what we also confirmed with our analyses. Additionally, we revealed a relationship between WMH and pharyngeal swallowing impairments which makes sense because the presence of penetration or aspiration is typically a result of some degree of pharyngeal impairment (e.g., impaired laryngeal vestibular closure, pharyngoesophageal segment opening).

In our study, we found that WMH scores of “3,” but not “2,” were associated with more severe swallowing impairments. While this discrepancy may partly stem from limitations in statistical power, there are also plausible biological explanations for the stronger impact of more severe WMH. Previous research indicates that the underlying biological processes vary depending on the severity of WMH. Severe WMH, akin to a PVH score of “3,” have been linked to irreversible cell changes including gliosis, axonal loss, demyelination, and vacuolation (Fernando et al., 2004, Gouw et al., 2011, Munoz et al., 1993, Wardlaw et al., 2015). Over time, WMH may have progressive effects on the brain integrity including cortical thinning and cerebral atrophy (Wardlaw et al., 2015). We tested the impact of severe WMH on brain network integrity in individuals with chronic post-stroke aphasia and found that more severe WMH predicted a lower percentage of long-range white matter fibers in the brain, which in turn predicted worse language impairments after aphasia therapy (Wilmskoetter et al., 2019b). On the other hand, less severe WMH, akin WMH scores of “<3”, have been linked to endothelial and microglial activation which may still be reversible, and thus, may not have long-lasting effects on the brain integrity and functions, such as swallowing.

Whether our study participants with WMH scores of “3″ indeed demonstrated gliosis, axonal loss, demyelination, and vacuolation within these areas, remains speculative. Using the standard of care MRIs, it was not possible to determine the underlying pathology. Thus, the variation in the relationship between WMH and swallowing that we and other studies observed across cohorts and also within cohorts, may reflect the variation in pathologies associated with WMH (Wardlaw et al., 2015).

Further, we found that overall brain atrophy, measured as an increase in CSF volume, was associated with more severe pharyngeal impairment. While brain atrophy is commonly associated with cSVD, it can also be a manifestation of neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer’s disease (Duering et al., 2023). Whether participants in our study harbored covert neurodegenerative processes remains uncertain, as we only excluded individuals with diagnosed or suspected neurodegenerative diseases before their stroke. Our method for computing CSF volume assessed global brain atrophy rather than regional atrophy with the latter potentially being more indicative of specific neurodegenerative disease, such as hippocampal atrophy in Alzheimer’s disease. cSVD can lead to widespread brain tissue loss, including subcortical changes like enlarged ventricles and cortical alterations such as thinning and sulcal widening (Duering et al., 2023). Because swallowing is controlled by a wide-spread bilateral neural network, an overall brain tissue loss secondary to atrophy increases the likelihood of affecting critical swallowing areas and their orchestration which may explain our findings.

Our study has several limitations, most notably the small sample size, which impacts the strength and generalizability of our findings. For instance, the lack of significant results for the MBSImP oral total scores may be attributable to limited statistical power rather than the absence of a true relationship. The MBSImP oral total score is an aggregate of six individual components, and it is possible that cSVD markers are associated with specific oral components. However, these associations may not be robust enough to emerge in the overall total score. Larger studies with greater statistical power are needed to more accurately assess the relationships between cSVD markers and individual oral mechanics, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of how cSVD may impact specific aspects of oral function.

Additionally, the use of ordinal scales for WMH and perivascular space scoring may limit the precision of our findings. Notably, in the assessment of perivascular spaces, we did not account for their size, focusing solely on their quantity. Furthermore, our study did not investigate other cSVD pathologies beyond brain atrophy, WMH, or perivascular spaces, such as lacunar infarcts or microbleeds, due to the unavailability of appropriate MRI images or their underrepresentation in our cohort.

Considering the limitations of our study, our findings not only shed light on the contribution of brain health on post-stroke dysphagia but also suggest a possible link between structural age-related brain and swallowing changes. Older adults demonstrate a decline in their swallowing safety and efficiency, even without any underlying diseases (Garand et al., 2022, Garand et al., 2019, Labeit et al., 2022, Leslie et al., 2005, Mancopes et al., 2021, Ney et al., 2009). The severity and extent of these age-related swallowing changes can vary and affect various aspects of swallow physiology, including swallow reaction times, pharyngoesophageal segment opening, laryngeal vestibular closure, and pharyngeal contraction. Thus, pre-stroke brain health may contribute to the extent of age-related swallowing changes before the stroke, and thus, determines an individual’s susceptibility to decompensate and develop dysphagia when a sudden brain injury occurs.

In terms of clinical implications, recognizing pre-stroke cSVD markers holds potential for improving clinicians' ability to anticipate and manage post-stroke dysphagia. Early identification of these markers may help healthcare providers better assess a patient's risk for developing swallowing disorders, enabling more informed discussions with patients and their families about recovery challenges and long-term prognosis. Additionally, such insights could guide the implementation of early interventions and the development of personalized rehabilitation strategies to improve patient outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study suggests that pre-stroke brain health may influence dysphagia severity following a stroke, independent of the stroke lesion and chronological age. These findings provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying the variability of post-stroke dysphagia and underscore the significance of preserving brain structural integrity beyond the immediate stroke-affected area emphasizing the critical role of maintaining overall brain health in stroke management. However, it is essential to note the primary limitation of our study's small sample size, which warrants caution in interpreting the strength of our conclusions. Future studies are warranted with larger sample sizes to confirm and expand upon our findings, and to provide more definitive insights into the impact of brain health on post-stroke swallow impairment, ultimately enhancing stroke management strategies and improving patient outcomes.
