Neonatal mortality following referral from peripheral facilities to Kanifing General Hospital, The Gambia (2022–2024): a survival analysis approach
Authors: Amadou Barrow, Kalipha K. Kassama, Kalifa Sanneh Darboe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal mortality remains a critical public health challenge in Sub-Saharan Africa, with The Gambia reporting one of the highest rates in the region. Despite global progress, the neonatal mortality rate has shown limited decline, and context-specific data on delivery setting-related risk factors remain needed to inform health system strengthening in The Gambia. We aimed to identify risk factors for neonatal mortality at Kanifing General Hospital (KGH), and examine the association between place of delivery and survival outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 1,354 neonates admitted to KGH between January 2022 and December 2024. The primary outcome was neonatal death during admission. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression with robust standard errors clustered by facility were used to identify risk factors.ResultsDuring the study period, 137 deaths occurred (10.1% mortality; 95% CI: 8.6–11.9). Median follow-up was 96 h. Overall, survival at 7 days was 87.8%, but differed substantially between neonates delivered at KGH Labour Ward (91.3%) versus peripheral facilities (78.9%; log-rank p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, delivery at KGH Labour Ward was strongly protective (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 0.41; 95% CI: 0.28–0.58; p < 0.001), representing a 59% reduction in mortality risk. Higher birth weight was protective (aHR 0.58 per kg; 95% CI: 0.37–0.90; p = 0.016), while birth asphyxia increased mortality risk (aHR 1.30; 95% CI: 1.11–1.53; p = 0.001). The model demonstrated acceptable discrimination (C-statistic = 0.713). E-value sensitivity analysis indicated robustness to unmeasured confounding (E-value = 4.36 for facility effect). Study limitations include the retrospective design and single-center setting.ConclusionsNeonates referred from peripheral facilities experienced substantially higher mortality compared to those delivered at the referral hospital. Birth weight and birth asphyxia were independently associated with survival. These findings highlight the critical importance of strengthening referral hospital capacity and improving quality of care at peripheral facilities to reduce neonatal mortality in The Gambia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06569-0.

Introduction

The first 28 days of life, the neonatal period represent the most vulnerable time for child survival. Globally, an estimated 2.3 million neonates died in 2023, accounting for 48% of all under-five deaths [1, 2]. While substantial progress has been made in reducing child mortality over the past three decades, the decline in neonatal mortality has been slower than that of post-neonatal mortality, and the proportion of child deaths occurring in the neonatal period continues to increase [1, 3]. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) call for all countries to reduce neonatal mortality to at least 12 per 1,000 live births by 2030; however, current trajectories suggest that more than 60 countries will fail to meet this target without accelerated action [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate burden of neonatal mortality, with rates approximately 50% higher than the global average [2, 2]. The region accounts for 43% of global neonatal deaths despite representing only 14% of global births [4]. The leading causes of neonatal death in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) include preterm birth complications, intrapartum-related events (including birth asphyxia), and neonatal sepsis, which are largely preventable with known, cost-effective interventions [4, 5]. However, weak health systems, limited access to quality care, and inadequate referral networks continue to impede progress [6, 7].

The Gambia, a small West African nation, faces significant challenges in neonatal health. According to the 2019–2020 Demographic and Health Survey, the neonatal mortality rate was 29 deaths per 1,000 live births, contributing to nearly half of under-five mortality [8]. This rate represents an increase from 22 per 1,000 reported in 2013, indicating a reversal of earlier gains in neonatal survival [8]. Common causes of neonatal death in The Gambia include neonatal sepsis, birth asphyxia, and complications of prematurity [8, 9]. Despite these challenges, epidemiological data on facility-level risk factors for neonatal mortality remain scarce, limiting evidence-based planning for health system strengthening.

Place of delivery and level of facility care have emerged as important determinants of neonatal survival in low- and middle-income countries [10, 11]. A large ecological study across 37 countries in SSA and South Asia found that regions with higher proportions of hospital deliveries (compared to lower-level clinics) had significantly reduced neonatal mortality [11]. Similarly, systematic reviews have demonstrated protective effects of health facility delivery on neonatal survival, with the magnitude of benefit varying by context and quality of care [12, 13]. However, these associations are complex; in settings with poor quality of care, facility delivery may not confer the expected survival advantage, and referral from peripheral facilities often indicates underlying complications that themselves increase mortality risk [11, 14]. Birth asphyxia remains a leading cause of neonatal mortality in SSA, accounting for an estimated 23% of neonatal deaths in the region [7, 15]. A recent meta-analysis reported that neonates with birth asphyxia in SSA had nearly four-fold higher odds of early neonatal mortality compared to those without asphyxia [16]. The burden of birth asphyxia is particularly high in settings with limited access to skilled birth attendance, emergency obstetric care, and neonatal resuscitation [7, 17]. Understanding the contribution of birth asphyxia to neonatal mortality in specific facility contexts is essential for developing targeted interventions.

Kanifing General Hospital (KGH) is the largest referral hospital in the Greater Banjul Area of The Gambia, serving a catchment population of over 400,000 and receiving referrals from peripheral health facilities across the West Coast Region. The hospital’s neonatal unit provides both basic and comprehensive essential newborn care services. However, to date, no comprehensive survival analysis has examined risk factors for neonatal mortality at this facility or quantified the impact of referral patterns on neonatal outcomes. We conducted this study to: (1) describe temporal trends in neonatal mortality at KGH from 2022 to 2024; (2) identify demographic, clinical, and delivery setting-related risk factors for neonatal death using survival analysis methods; and (3) quantify the association between place of delivery (KGH Labour Ward versus peripheral facilities) and neonatal survival. These findings aim to inform evidence-based strategies for reducing neonatal mortality in The Gambia and similar resource-limited settings in West Africa.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of neonates admitted to Kanifing General Hospital (KGH), the largest referral hospital in the Greater Banjul Area of The Gambia, between January 2022 and December 2024. KGH serves as the primary referral center for the Kanifing Municipal Area and receives referrals from peripheral health facilities across the West Coast Region. The hospital’s neonatal unit provides both basic and comprehensive essential newborn care services. The Gambia’s health system is organized into three tiers: community-level health posts, health centers (minor and major), and hospitals. Peripheral health facilities in this study included 12 minor health centers, 4 major health centers, and one district hospital within KGH’s catchment area (Supplementary Table S1). Neonates delivered at home who were subsequently referred to KGH were included in the “peripheral facilities” category if the referring facility was documented; direct home-to-hospital admissions (n = 23, 1.7%) were also categorized under “peripheral facilities” for the primary analysis, as these neonates similarly lacked immediate access to hospital-level delivery care.

Study population

All neonates (aged 0–28 days) admitted to the KGH neonatal unit during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Neonates with missing data on survival time or vital status were excluded from the primary analysis. The final analytical sample comprised 1,354 neonates with complete data on the primary outcome and key covariates.

Data sources and variables

Data were extracted from hospital admission registers and patient case files. The primary outcome was neonatal death during hospital admission. Follow-up time was defined as hours from admission until death or discharge (administrative censoring), capped at 28 days (672 h) to capture the complete neonatal period. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature, including: sex; birth weight (kg): For neonates delivered at KGH Labour Ward, birth weight was recorded immediately at delivery. For referred neonates, birth weight was obtained from referral documentation when available (78% of cases) or measured at KGH admission (22% of cases); gestational age (term, preterm, post-term); place of delivery (KGH Labour Ward versus peripheral health facilities); mode of delivery (spontaneous vaginal delivery, cesarean section, vacuum-assisted); and clinical diagnoses at admission (neonatal sepsis, birth asphyxia, prematurity/low birth weight, congenital abnormalities). A symptom severity score was calculated as the sum of presenting danger signs documented at admission, including respiratory distress, poor feeding, lethargy, convulsions, fever, hypothermia, jaundice, and cyanosis, with each symptom scored as 1 if present and 0 if absent (range: 0–8).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables, stratified by survival status. Differences between survivors and non-survivors were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables.

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier methods were used to estimate survival probabilities at clinically relevant time points (24 h, 72 h, 7 days, 14 days, and 28 days). Survival curves were compared between groups using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for factors associated with neonatal mortality. Birth weight was modeled as a continuous variable (per kilogram increase) after confirming linearity using martingale residual plots. To account for potential correlation of outcomes within delivery facilities, we computed robust standard errors using the Huber-White sandwich estimator clustered at the facility level.

Variables associated with neonatal death at p < 0.25 in univariable analysis were considered for multivariable modeling. The final multivariable model included sex, birth weight, gestational age, place of delivery, mode of delivery, and clinical diagnoses (neonatal sepsis, birth asphyxia, prematurity). Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s concordance index (C-statistic). Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Python version 3.12. Survival analysis was conducted using the survival and survminer packages in R and the lifelines package in Python. Publication-ready tables were generated using gtsummary and flextable. All statistical tests were two-sided with significance set at α = 0.05.

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using scaled Schoenfeld residuals with both global and variable-specific tests. Complementary log-log plots were visually inspected to confirm parallel hazard functions across exposure groups. Variables violating the proportional hazards assumption (p < 0.05 on the Schoenfeld residual test) were addressed through stratification.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings: Complete case analysis: We restricted analysis to participants with complete data on all covariates to assess the impact of missing data. E-value calculation: To quantify susceptibility to unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values for statistically significant associations. Time-varying analysis: We examined risk factors separately for the early neonatal period (0–7 days) and late neonatal period (7–28 days) using episode splitting.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by Ministry of Health under FETP program (ref #: DHS/AD/2025/3/(MTN)). The study used de-identified secondary data from routine health facility records; individual informed consent was waived given the retrospective nature and minimal risk. All data were handled according to the Gambian Data Protection Act and international standards for data security. Patient information was confidential and de-identified using numeric codes. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. This study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Between January 2022 and December 2024, 1,393 neonates were admitted to KGH. After excluding 39 (2.8%) with missing data on survival time or vital status, 1,354 neonates were included in the analysis (Table 1). The median age at admission was 24 h (IQR: 4–72), and the median birth weight was 2.80 kg (IQR: 2.36–3.30). Males comprised 59.4% (n = 804) of the cohort. The majority of neonates (74.3%, n = 1,006) were delivered at the KGH Labour Ward, while 25.7% (n = 348) were referred from peripheral health facilities. Among neonates with documented gestational age, 78.8% were term (n = 767), 18.9% were preterm (n = 184), and 2.4% were post-term (n = 23). Low birth weight (< 2.5 kg) was present in 29.5% of neonates. The most common admission diagnoses were neonatal sepsis (47.3%), birth asphyxia (28.4%), and prematurity/low birth weight (14.9%). Neonates who died had significantly lower birth weight (median 2.50 vs. 2.90 kg), were more likely to have low birth weight (49.6% vs. 27.3%), and were more frequently referred from peripheral facilities (47.4% vs. 23.3%) compared to survivors. The median age at admission was lower among non-survivors (19.5 h) than survivors (24 h), though this difference was not statistically significant (Wilcoxon rank-sum p = 0.312). This pattern likely reflects confounding by indication, whereby neonates with severe illness at birth (such as severe birth asphyxia) require urgent admission and have inherently higher mortality risk regardless of admission timing.

Neonatal mortality and survival estimates

During the study period, 137 neonatal deaths occurred, yielding an in-hospital mortality rate of 10.1% (95% CI: 8.6–11.9). The median duration of admission was 96 h (IQR: 72–120) among survivors and 48 h (IQR: 13–72) among non-survivors. Most deaths (60.6%, n = 83) occurred within the first 24 h of admission. Kaplan-Meier survival estimates at key time points were: 95.0% at 24 h (95% CI: 93.8–96.2), 92.0% at 72 h (95% CI: 90.4–93.5), 87.8% at 7 days (95% CI: 85.9–89.6), 78.1% at 14 days (95% CI: 75.5–80.8), and 61.4% at 28 days (95% CI: 57.3–65.7). Survival differed substantially by place of delivery (Fig. 1A). Neonates delivered at KGH Labour Ward had significantly higher 7-day survival (91.3%, 95% CI: 89.4–93.1) compared to those referred from peripheral facilities (78.9%, 95% CI: 74.5–83.5; log-rank p < 0.001). Survival also varied by birth weight category (Fig. 1B), with 7-day survival of 63.2% for neonates weighing < 1.5 kg, 85.1% for 1.5–2.5 kg, and 93.4% for > 2.5 kg (log-rank p < 0.001). Among neonates delivered at KGH Labour Ward, 47 (4.7%) were initially discharged home and subsequently readmitted to the neonatal unit within the first 7 days of life. Mortality among these readmitted neonates was significantly higher than among those admitted directly from the Labour Ward (8/47 [17.0%] vs. 64/959 [6.7%]; chi-square p = 0.007), suggesting potential gaps in discharge assessment or postnatal care practices (Supplementary Table S2).

Risk factors for neonatal mortality

In univariable Cox regression (Table 2), factors significantly associated with neonatal mortality included male sex (HR 0.71, 95% CI: 0.51–0.99; p = 0.044), higher birth weight (HR 0.56 per kg, 95% CI: 0.44–0.71; p < 0.001), low birth weight (HR 2.13, 95% CI: 1.52–2.99; p < 0.001), and delivery at KGH Labour Ward versus peripheral facilities (HR 0.41, 95% CI: 0.29–0.57; p < 0.001). In multivariable Cox regression with robust standard errors clustered by facility, three factors remained independently associated with neonatal mortality: Higher birth weight was protective, with each kilogram increase associated with a 42% reduction in the hazard of death (adjusted HR [aHR] 0.58, 95% CI: 0.37–0.90; p = 0.016). Delivery at KGH Labour Ward was strongly protective compared to referral from peripheral facilities (aHR 0.41, 95% CI: 0.28–0.58; p < 0.001), representing a 59% reduction in mortality risk. Birth asphyxia was significantly associated with increased mortality (aHR 1.30, 95% CI: 1.11–1.53; p = 0.001). Sex, gestational age, cesarean delivery, neonatal sepsis, and symptom severity were not independently associated with mortality after adjustment. The model demonstrated acceptable discrimination with a concordance index of 0.713 (Fig. 2).

Model diagnostics and sensitivity analyses

The global test of the proportional hazards assumption was not statistically significant (p = 0.124), indicating no strong evidence of violation. Visual inspection of scaled Schoenfeld residual plots (Fig. 3) supported the proportional hazards assumption for all covariates, with no systematic departures from horizontal patterns over time.

Time-varying analysis stratified by neonatal period (Table 3) confirmed that the protective effect of KGH delivery was consistent in both the early neonatal period (0–7 days: aHR 0.38, 95% CI: 0.26–0.56; p < 0.001) and late neonatal period (7–28 days: aHR 0.78, 95% CI: 0.21–2.86; p = 0.705), though the latter estimate was imprecise due to fewer events (n = 12). Birth asphyxia showed a stronger association with mortality in the early neonatal period (aHR 1.29, 95% CI: 1.10–1.51; p = 0.001) compared to the late period (aHR 0.93, 95% CI: 0.14–6.02; p = 0.936), consistent with the acute nature of asphyxia-related mortality.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 1,354 neonates admitted to KGH, we observed an in-hospital mortality rate of 10.1%, with most deaths (60.6%) occurring within the first 24 h of admission. Three factors were independently associated with neonatal mortality: delivery at the referral hospital (protective), higher birth weight (protective), and birth asphyxia (harmful). Most notably, neonates referred from peripheral health facilities experienced a 59% higher hazard of death compared to those delivered at KGH Labour Ward, even after adjustment for clinical characteristics. This facility-level disparity persisted across both early and late neonatal periods and was robust to sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding.

Our finding that delivery at the referral hospital was strongly protective is consistent with ecological evidence from SSA. Gage et al. [10] reported that a 10-percentage point increase in the proportion of facility deliveries occurring in hospitals (versus lower-level clinics) was associated with 1.2 fewer deaths per 1,000 births across 37 countries. Our effect estimate (aHR 0.41) suggests an even stronger association in this single-facility context, which may reflect the substantial quality differential between KGH and peripheral facilities in The Gambia. The referral hospital has continuous access to skilled personnel, resuscitation equipment, and emergency interventions that may be intermittently available at peripheral sites.

The protective effect of referral hospital delivery likely operates through multiple mechanisms. First, neonates born at KGH benefit from immediate access to skilled birth attendants and neonatal resuscitation if required [17]. Second, complications identified during labor can be managed promptly without the delays inherent in referral processes. Third, the quality of postnatal monitoring and early intervention for deteriorating neonates is likely superior at the referral hospital. However, our findings also reflect selection effects: neonates referred from peripheral facilities may have experienced delays in recognition, referral, and transport that contributed to their poorer outcomes independent of facility quality [18, 19]. The inability to fully disentangle quality effects from referral selection represents an important limitation of observational studies in this context.

Our findings contrast with some studies from settings with poor quality of facility care, where facility delivery does not consistently confer a survival advantage. Lohela et al. found that in Malawi, increasing distance to a health facility was paradoxically associated with lower neonatal mortality in some areas, potentially reflecting poor quality of care at accessible facilities [14]. Similarly, Fink et al. observed that the protective effect of facility delivery was attenuated or absent in regions with weak health systems [20]. These contrasting findings underscore that the benefits of hospital delivery depend critically on quality of care, not merely location of delivery, and suggest that KGH may provide a meaningful quality advantage over peripheral facilities in our setting.

The association between birth asphyxia and neonatal mortality (aHR 1.30) aligns with regional evidence. Systematic reviews have consistently identified birth asphyxia as a major risk factor for neonatal death in SSA, with pooled odds ratios ranging from 2.6 to 3.9 [16, 21]. Our more modest effect estimate may reflect the specific hospital-based population, where asphyxiated neonates receive immediate resuscitation and supportive care. The stronger association observed in the early neonatal period (aHR 1.29 for 0–7 days) compared to the late period (aHR 0.93 for 7–28 days) is biologically plausible, as severe asphyxia-related mortality occurs predominantly in the first days of life [15, 22]. These findings underscore the need for strengthened intrapartum care and immediate newborn resuscitation capacity, particularly at peripheral facilities where birth asphyxia may go unrecognized or inadequately managed [7, 17].

The protective effect of birth weight (42% reduction in mortality per kilogram increase) is well-established in the literature. Low birth weight, whether due to preterm birth or intrauterine growth restriction, is the single strongest predictor of neonatal mortality globally [23]. Our finding that 49.6% of neonates who died had low birth weight compared to 27.3% of survivors highlights the vulnerability of this population. Interestingly, after adjustment for birth weight as a continuous variable, the categorical low birth weight variable and prematurity diagnosis were no longer significantly associated with mortality, suggesting that the continuous measure captures most of the prognostic information. This has implications for risk stratification in resource-limited settings where gestational age assessment may be unreliable [24].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, we employed rigorous survival analysis methods with appropriate handling of censored observations and time-varying effects. Second, the use of robust standard errors clustered by facility accounted for potential correlation of outcomes within delivery locations. Third, comprehensive sensitivity analyses, including E-value calculations and time-varying models, demonstrated robustness of our principal findings. Fourth, the study population represents the largest referral hospital in The Gambia, providing insights relevant to health system planning.

This study has some limitations for consideration. First, the retrospective design relies on routinely collected clinical data, which may be subject to measurement error and missing information. Second, as a single-center study, findings may not be generalizable to other facilities in The Gambia or the region. Third, we could not assess quality of care at referring facilities or details of the referral process (timing, transport conditions), which may confound the facility-mortality association. Fourth, unmeasured confounders such as maternal HIV status, socioeconomic factors, and antenatal care quality may have influenced our estimates. Additionally, we could not assess facility-specific risk factors at individual peripheral health facilities, as our data were limited to place of delivery categorization (KGH vs. peripheral facilities as a group) without information on quality-of-care indicators at referring sites. Fifth, birth weight for referred neonates was sometimes measured at admission rather than at birth, which may introduce measurement error due to physiological weight loss in the first days of life; however, sensitivity analysis restricted to neonates admitted within 24 h confirmed the robustness of our findings. However, E-value analysis suggested that substantial unmeasured confounding (risk ratio > 4.3) would be required to explain away the facility effect. Finally, we could not distinguish between neonates who were delivered at peripheral facilities and referred versus those born at home and subsequently referred, which may represent distinct risk profiles.

Policy implications

Our findings have important implications for health policy in The Gambia and similar settings. First, the substantial survival disadvantage among neonates referred from peripheral facilities highlights the urgent need to strengthen neonatal care capacity at lower-level health facilities. This includes ensuring availability of basic resuscitation equipment, training health workers in Helping Babies Breathe and essential newborn care, and improving early recognition of danger signs [25]. Second, strengthening referral systems including communication between facilities, transport infrastructure, and pre-referral stabilization may help mitigate the mortality risk associated with referral [26, 27]. Third, the strong association with birth asphyxia underscores the importance of quality intrapartum care, including partograph use, fetal monitoring, and timely intervention for prolonged labor [9].

At the referral hospital level, our findings support continued investment in neonatal intensive care capacity at KGH, which serves as the safety net for complicated cases from across the region. The concentration of early neonatal deaths in the first 24 h suggests that interventions targeting the immediate postnatal period including enhanced monitoring, early feeding support, and prompt sepsis recognition may yield the greatest impact [28]. Quality improvement initiatives focused on standardized admission protocols, handover procedures from referring facilities, and real-time mortality surveillance could help identify modifiable factors contributing to early deaths.

The higher mortality observed among neonates readmitted after initial discharge from KGH Labour Ward (17.0% vs. 6.7%; Supplementary Table S2) warrants attention to postnatal discharge practices. Readmitted neonates presented later (median 72 h vs. 6 h) and more commonly with neonatal sepsis (66.0% vs. 45.0%) and jaundice (38.3% vs. 9.3%) (Supplementary Table S3), suggesting that conditions developing after initial discharge were the primary reasons for readmission. This finding suggests that some neonates may be discharged without adequate assessment or that danger signs are missed during the immediate postnatal period. Strengthening pre-discharge newborn examinations, enhancing maternal education on danger signs, and implementing structured postnatal follow-up within the first 48–72 h could help identify at-risk neonates before clinical deterioration.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that neonates referred from peripheral health facilities to KGH experience substantially higher mortality compared to those delivered at the referral hospital. Birth weight and birth asphyxia are independently associated with neonatal survival. These findings highlight critical gaps in the continuum of care for newborns in The Gambia and point to opportunities for intervention at multiple levels of the health system. Achieving the SDG target for neonatal mortality will require concurrent investments in peripheral facility strengthening, referral system optimization, and sustained quality improvement at referral hospitals. Future research should examine specific components of the referral process, quality of care indicators, and community-level factors that may further explain facility-level disparities in neonatal outcomes.
