The compound of microencapsulated essential oils improves productive parameters, antioxidant status, meat quality, and both humoral and cellular immune responses in broilers
Authors: Hassan Safari, Fatemeh Mohammadpour, Omid Jafaryani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis experiment investigated the effect of the compound of microencapsulated essential oils (CMEOs) on productive parameters, antioxidant status, meat quality, and humoral and cellular immune responses in broilers. In a completely randomized design, a total of 640 (mixed-sex) one-day-old Ross 308 broiler chickens were divided into 4 treatments with 10 replicates (16 chicks/replicate) for 42 days. Treatments were basal diet supplemented with CMEOs at levels of 0 (control), 300 (CMEOs-300), 600 (CMEOs-600), and 900 (CMEOs-900) mg/kg of diet. The CMEOs contain equal weight ratios of eucalyptus, oregano, rosemary, and thyme essential oils (EOs). The results showed that, compared to the control and other groups, the CMEOs-900 treatment increased (ANOVA and linear, P = 0.001) total body weight gain and decreased (ANOVA, P = 0.001; linear, P = 0.04) feed conversion ratio throughout the entire experimental period. The CMEOs-900 treatment increased (ANOVA and linear, P < 0.05) total antioxidant capacity and the enzyme activity of glutathione peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and catalase in the serum of broilers, compared with the control and other treatments. Feeding CMEOs-900 increased (ANOVA and linear, P < 0.05) 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl and 2,2ʹ-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid) diammonium salt free radical-scavenging activities, as well as decreased (ANOVA and linear, and quadratic, P < 0.05) malondialdehyde levels in breast meat at various times of storage, and serum compared with the control and other groups. The group fed CMEOs-900 increased (ANOVA, P < 0.05) breast meat pH values and water-holding capacity at 7 and 14 days of storage compared to the control and other groups. Broilers fed CMEOs-900 showed a higher (ANOVA and linear, P < 0.05) immune response against phytohemagglutinin and 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene challenge, antibody titers against Newcastle disease virus, total anti-sheep red blood cell titer, immunoglobulin M, immunoglobulin G, as well as lower (ANOVA and linear, P < 0.05) heterophil count, and heterophil-to-lymphocyte ratio compared to the control and other groups. In conclusion, supplementing CMEOs-900 in the broilers' diet improves productive parameters, cellular and humoral immune responses, antioxidant enzyme activity, meat quality, and oxidative stability.Keywords: Broiler, Eucalyptus, Oregano, Rosemary, Thyme

Introduction

The use of antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) is being phased out in poultry due to the transfer of antibiotic-resistant bacteria to humans via poultry products (Cui et al., 2019). Thus, identifying an appropriate alternative that lacks the detrimental effects of AGPs may be beneficial. At the same time, many stress factors result in accelerated oxidative stress, imbalance of homeostasis, and lower meat quality. In this case, a higher level of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in broiler meat, as well as genetic selection for bigger breast muscles, higher total weight, intensive breeding environments, and faster growth rates, leads to the production of a lot of free radicals, which raises the sensitivity of lipid and protein oxidative degradation in meat (Safari et al., 2016a; Shen et al., 2019). Lipid oxidation in meat continues postmortem and decreases its shelf life during storage, resulting in loss of color, flavor, and taste, and finally has an impact on consumer preferences and product quality (Safari et al., 2016a; Shen et al., 2019). Therefore, to achieve overall health and higher performance, as well as increase the quality and shelf life of poultry meat, it is essential to support its antioxidant and free radical scavenging properties (Safari et al., 2025).

As an alternative to antibiotics, the essential oils (EOs) are known as volatile materials with odorous impact, hydrophobic, esters, short-chain, lipophilic compounds, low molecular weight aliphatic hydrocarbons like phenols and aldehydes, aromatic oily liquids extracted from various parts of the plant (Ebani et al., 2016; Cui et al., 2019). The EOs can be utilized as promising antibiotic alternatives due to their good biological activity, including antibacterial, antiviral, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant (Cui et al., 2019). However, supplementing plant EOs in a non-coated form at high doses because of active molecules and repellent smell, as well as bitter taste, may decrease the diet palatability and poultry feed intake (Farouk et al., 2020; Temiz and Özturk, 2021; Pliego et al., 2022; Paul et al., 2023). Thus, microencapsulation of EO is a technique used for targets including increasing the stability and bioavailability of bioactive components, solidifying liquids, avoiding the loss of volatile substances and evaporation, concealing offensive flavors and odors, extending the effect period, preserving their structure, reduce the effective dose, decrease oxidation, and release them in a controlled way to target tissues (Temiz and Özturk, 2021; Paul et al., 2023). Moreover, non-coated EOs are mainly metabolized in the proximal part of the digestive tract and rarely reach the distal part. While the microencapsulated EOs, without being impacted by the acidic and other special environmental conditions of the upper digestive tract, can reach the hindgut or intestine to slowly release, especially in the jejunum, ileum, and cecum (Temiz and Özturk, 2021; Paul et al., 2023). This results in better absorption, leading to products with high bioavailability, as well as improved intestinal health and performance, and a decrease in the survival rate of enteric pathogens (Temiz and Özturk, 2021; Paul et al., 2023).

Among the potential candidates are the EOs of the oregano (Origanum vulgare L.; main bioactive compounds: carvacrol, 62.11%; ρ-cymene, 7.23%; thymol, 5.41%; linalool, 3.52%; ethyl caprate, 3.56%; γ-terpinene, 3.74%), rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis L.; main bioactive compounds: 1,8‐cineol, 40.64%; α-pinene, 16.01%; borneol, 5.63%; camphor, 5.10%; verbenone, 4.75%; limonene, 4.15%; camphene, 3.14%), thyme (Thymus vulgaris L.; main bioactive compounds: carvacrol, 51.31%; thymol, 21.12%; ρ-cymene, 9.42%; γ-terpinene, 5.40%), and eucalyptus (Eucalyptus globulus; main bioactive compounds: 1,8‐cineol, 61.31%; α-pinene, 13.16%; γ-terpinene, 6.57%; ρ-cymene, 3.64%; α-terpineol, 3.01%). Previous studies have identified supplemental volatile EOs, microencapsulated and/or encapsulated EOs in broilers' diets, improved productive parameters, antioxidant status, humoral and cellular immune response, and meat quality of the broilers (Amiri et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021; Johnson et al., 2022; Moharreri et al., 2022; Pliego et al., 2022; Ghazanfari et al., 2023; Salama et al., 2023). To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no published study in the literature has been found on the formation of the compound of microencapsulated essential oils (CMEOs) from thyme, rosemary, oregano, and eucalyptus. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the use of CMEOs as a new natural feed additive. Additionally, we investigated the effects of CMEOs on productive parameters, antioxidant status, humoral and cellular immune responses, and meat quality in broilers. Finally, the identification of an optimal inclusion level of CMEOs in the diet of broilers was done.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All experimental protocols and sample collection methods used in the current study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Guilan (code H98718/S16), Rasht, Iran.

Preparation, chemical compositions, and microencapsulation of EOs

Four EOs, including thyme, rosemary, oregano, and eucalyptus, were bought directly from the Barij Essence Company (Kashan, Iran). We used the gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) (GC, Hewlett-Packard, HP-6890, Palo Alto, CA, USA; MS, Hewlett-Packard 7953, CA, USA) to determine the chemical compositions of EOs using the previously described method (Puvača et al., 2021), and the data can be found in Table 1. Briefly, the Agilent HP-6890 gas chromatography (GC) equipped with a 7953-electron ionization-chemical ionization mass spectrometer (MS) detector and flame ionization detector (FID) connected by a capillary flow technology 2-way splitter with make-up was used for the GC-MS analysis. The capillary column HP-5MS (30 m in length, internal diameter 0.25 mm, film thickness 0.25 μm) was used. The GC oven's temperature was set to vary from 60 to 300°C at a rate of 3°C per minute and maintained for a duration of 15 minutes. Helium was utilized as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min at 16.255 psi (constant pressure mode). Also, 1 μL of the EO sample was injected into the GC-MS using a split-splitless injector. The split ratio was 1:50. The splitless mode was used to analyze the sample. Both the injector and the detector were operating at 300°C. Data of MS were collected in electron ionization mode with a scan range of 30–550 m/z, an ionization source temperature of 230°C, and a quadruple temperature of 150°C; the solvent delay was 3 minutes. The EO compounds were identified in the analyses by comparing their mass spectra and linear retention indices (with respect to C8–C36 n-alkanes on the HP-5MSI column) with those of authentic standards from the NIST 21, NIST 11, NIST 107, and NIST 14 databases.

We microencapsulated the EOs of eucalyptus, oregano, rosemary, and thyme by an ionic gelation process, following the method described previously (Sawtarie et al., 2017). Briefly, deionized water was used for all steps of the EO microencapsulation. Chitosan (CS) with a medium molecular weight, used as a coating polymer, and sodium triphosphate pentabasic (TPP) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The CS was dissolved in 1% (w/v) acetic acid solution at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and then sonicated. The solution was stirred overnight at room temperature for complete dispersion of CS. Then, before being sterilized at 121°C for 15 min to remove impurities, the produced solution was filtered (filter paper; Calbiochem-Novabiochem Crop, USA). The dropwise addition of 10 mL TPP solution (1 mg/mL) to a 25 mL CS solution at pH 5, under constant stirring conditions at room temperature. This resulted in the formation of CS-TPP particles by the ionic gelation mechanism. For the production of CS-TPP particles loaded with EO, 20% (w/v) EO was added to the CS-solution before adding the TPP-solution. Microencapsulation efficiency was above 96%, and the size of microencapsulated EOs was 4 ± 0.3 μm. Each of the EOs was microencapsulated individually. Then EOs were mixed in equal proportions (1:1:1:1, w/w) before being added to the diet.

Broilers, diets, and management

The study was carried out at the poultry research farm and the animal nutrition laboratory of the University of Guilan in Rasht, Iran. A total of 640 1-day-old mixed-sex broiler chickens (Ross-308) with similar average initial body weight (42.3 ± 1 g) were obtained from a commercial hatchery. Birds were randomly allocated in a completely randomized design to 4 treatments of 10 replicate pens each containing 16 birds (8 male and 8 female; by feather sexing). Each pen (1 m wide, 1.85 m long, and 1 m high) was equipped with three nipple drinkers, a hanging feed dish, and a fresh wood shavings litter. All diets were fed in mash form, and the birds were allowed to consume feed and water ad libitum for a 42-day experiment. The guidelines of the Ross 308 Breeding Guidance Manual on temperature, relative humidity, photoperiod, and household environmental conditions are considered (Aviagen, 2019). Briefly, the ventilation was controlled by wall-mounted air inlets and wall-mounted extraction fans. The light schedule was as follows: on the first day of life, the chicks were subjected to 23 h of continuous light and only 1 h of darkness to allow them to easily find their feed and water. From days 2 to 7, the hours of darkness were gradually increased to 6 h per day. From day 8 until 3 days before the end of the experiment, the broilers were subjected to 18 hours of light and 6 hours of darkness per day, which complied with Aviagen guidelines of a minimum of 4 hours of continuous darkness per day. Finally, the 3 days before the end of the experiment, gradually provided with 23 h of light and 1 h of darkness per day. Humidity was maintained at 50 to 60% during the experiment period. The initial temperature was set at 32°C and decreased day by day to 22.5°C by day 28. Then the temperature was maintained at 21.5 to 22.5°C during days 28 to 42 of the experiment. The broilers received vaccinations against Newcastle (Lasota; days 1, 8, and 18), infectious bronchitis (H120; day 1), influenza (H5; day 1), and Gumboro (IBD 071 IR; day 16). The vaccines have all been acquired from the Razi Institute of Serums and Vaccines (Karaj, Iran). As shown in Table 2, the basal diets were formulated based on the Ross 308 broiler chicken nutrient requirements guideline (Aviagen, 2019) by using UFFDA diet writing software. According to the age of broilers, the ingredients of the diets were milled to the optimum particle size. Then, the diet ingredients were mixed and homogenized in a horizontal mixer with a 100-kg full-load capacity. The broilers received the experimental diets in three rearing periods: starter (1–10 days), grower (11–24 days), and finisher (25–42 days). The following is how the experimental dietary treatments were created. A basal diet containing the compound of microencapsulated essential oils (CMEOs) at a level of 0 (control), 300 (CMEOs-300), 600 (CMEOs-600), and 900 (CMEOs-900) mg/kg of diet. The CMEOs were uniformly mixed into each kg of diet daily. The CMEOs contain EOs of eucalyptus, oregano, rosemary, and thyme in equal weight ratios.

Productive parameters

Body weight (BW), feed intake (FI), BW gain (BWG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) of broilers of each pen within each treatment were recorded periodically for the starter, grower, and finisher periods, and the whole experimental period. Every day, the mortality rate in each pen was noted.

Antioxidant enzyme activities

At 24 and 42 days of age, 4 broilers (2 male and 2 female; closest to average BW) were randomly chosen per pen, and blood samples were taken from the wing vein four hours after feed withdrawal. Immediately, blood samples were centrifuged (4,000 × g) for 15 minutes at 4°C. After separation, the serum samples were kept at −20°C for further assay (Safari et al., 2025). Finally, the total antioxidant capacity (TAC) assay kit (Catalog no. A015-1-2), glutathione peroxidase (GPX) assay kit (Catalog no. A005), malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit (Catalog no. A003-1), superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay kit (Catalog no. A001-1-1), and catalase (CAT) assay kit (Catalog no. A007-1-1) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China, to measure TAC, GPX, SOD, and CAT activities, as well as MDA concentration according to the protocol of the kit manufacturer.

Meat quality measurements

At the end of the 42nd day of the study, 4 birds (2 male and 2 female; closest to average BW) per pen were chosen at random and slaughtered. Then, 60 grams of right-hand breast meat from similar locations was stripped and under modified atmosphere packaging (MAP; 40% N2 + 60% CO2) condition in special plastic bags stored at 4°C. After 0 (immediately after sampling and before storage at 4°C), 7, and 14 days of storage, the meat quality was determined by evaluation of lipid oxidation, water-holding capacity (WHC), and pH value (Shen et al., 2019); as well as 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), and 2,2ʹ-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS) free radical-scavenging activity assay (Wan et al., 2017).

Lipid Oxidation: To prepare the muscle homogenates, one gram of the meat sample was diluted with 9 mL of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (pH = 7.2). The mixture was then subjected to homogenization and centrifugation at 9,000 × g at 4°C for 15 minutes (Safari et al., 2025). The concentration of MDA was measured in the clear homogenate supernatant of the samples by a commercial MDA kit (Catalog no. A003-1; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's guidelines (Shen et al., 2019).

WHC and pH Value: The WHC of the meat samples was assessed using a centrifugation assay, as outlined in a prior study (Khalifah et al., 2021). Briefly, 1 g of the meat sample on paper tissue within a tube was centrifuged (2,500 × g) for 4 min. In the next step, the centrifuged samples were dried at 70°C for 24 hours to measure the amount of water remaining. The WHC was calculated as follows: [(centrifuged sample weight − dried sample weight) / (initial sample weight)] × 100. Five grams of breast muscle samples were homogenized in 45 mL of distilled water for 1 minute. After that, a pH meter (Inolab, Germany) calibrated at pH 4.0 and 7.0 was used to measure the pH of the filtered homogenate (Safari et al., 2016a).

DPPH and ABTS Free Radical-Scavenging Activity Assay: The DPPH and ABTS free radical-scavenging activity was determined by a commercial DPPH assay kit (Catalog no. BC4750, Solarbio Life Sciences Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and a commercial ABTS assay kit (Catalog no. BC4770, Solarbio Life Sciences Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the protocol of the kit manufacturer.

Anti-sheep red blood cell antibody analysis

To measure the primary and secondary antibody responses against sheep red blood cell (SRBC), at 28 and 35 days of age, 4 broilers (2 male and 2 female; closest to average BW) per pen were intravenously administered to the right wings with 1 mL of 10% (v/v) suspension of SRBC diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH = 7.5). Seven days following the first and second SRBC injections (days 35 and 42), 3 mL blood samples were taken from the brachial vein of the same birds for primary and secondary antibody responses, respectively. It is worth noting that the first blood sampling was before the second SRBC injection. Serum was obtained by centrifugation at 4°C for 15 minutes at 3,000 × g and stored at −20°C for further assay. Samples were analyzed for total antibody titers against SRBC (total anti-SRBC titer), immunoglobulin G (IgG), and immunoglobulin M (IgM) levels by the hemagglutination assay (HA) test, following the method described earlier (Qorbanpour et al., 2018). The log2 of the reciprocal of the highest dilution was used to express the antibody titers, resulting in visible agglutination.

Anti-newcastle disease virus antibody assay

The vaccination program is presented in the management part. On days 24 and 42, two mL of blood were drawn from the wing vein of 4 birds (2 male and 2 female; closest to average BW) per pen. Then, using the hemagglutination inhibition (HI) method, the antibody titer production response against Newcastle disease virus (NDV) was carried out (Hosseini-Vashan and Piray, 2021). Finally, the log2 of the reciprocal of the highest dilution was used to express the HI antibody titers, resulting in visible agglutination.

Phytohemagglutinin response assay

At the 31st and 39th days of the experiment, 0.1 mL of phytohemagglutinin (PHA) was injected into the skin between the 3rd and 4th digits of the right foot of 8 broilers (4 male and 4 female; closest to average BW) per pen. Each broiler was given a 0.1 mL injection of sterile physiological saline solution at the same location on its left foot as the control. Skin thickness was measured using an electronic digital caliper (Forbes Gokak, Mumbai, India) with a precision of 0.001 mm before injection and at 24 and 48 hours after injection to assess the cellular response caused by PHA (L-9017, Sigma Aldrich) injection on the cutaneous basophile hypersensitivity. The cellular response was determined by the difference in the thickness before and after injecting with PHA (Amiri et al., 2020; Ghazanfari et al., 2023).

2,4-Dinitrochlorobenzene response assay

On the 31st and 39th days of the experiment, 8 broilers (4 male and 4 female; closest to average BW) per pen were chosen at random, and the featherless skin under the right wing was sensitized by administering 0.25 mL of 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene (DNCB) (10 mg/ml) in a vehicle consisting of an acetone and olive oil (4:1) combination. The resulting reaction to DNCB (Sigma-DNCB, USA) was evaluated by measuring the increase in skin thickness before the challenge and at 24 and 48 h after the challenge by an electronic digital caliper (Forbes Gokak, Mumbai, India) with a precision of 0.001 mm. As the control, to correct the solvent effect, the same place on the left side was chosen for the challenge of only the vehicle (Verma et al., 2004; Amiri et al., 2020).

Heterophile and lymphocyte values

At 24 and 42 days of age, 4 broilers (2 male and 2 female; closest to average BW) per pen were chosen at random, and three mL of their blood samples were taken from the brachial vein in heparinized tubes. Heterophile (H) and lymphocyte (L) counts were measured by the stained-slide method as described earlier (Safari et al., 2025). Also, the H:L ratio was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Shapiro-Wilk’s test verified the distribution normality, and Levene’s test verified the variance homogeneity of the data. Data related to the productive parameters, antioxidant enzyme activities, meat MDA, pH, and WHC of meat, DPPH, and ABTS free radical-scavenging activity in meat, antibody response against SRBC and NDV, immune response to PHA, and DNCB, as well as heterophil and lymphocyte count, were analyzed using the repeated measures model with the mixed procedure (PROC MIXED) of SAS software (SAS 9.2; Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). This accounts for variation over time, dietary treatment as a fixed effect, and pen as a random effect. The one-way ANOVA model was used for analyzing the data of serum MDA in a completely randomized design by the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure in SAS (Statistical Analysis System) software (SAS 9.2; Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Each pen in each treatment served as the experimental unit for productive parameters, while the individual sampled broiler served as the experimental unit for statistical analysis of other parameters. Additionally, the influence of supplemental levels of CMEOs in the diet was determined using an orthogonal polynomial contrast analysis to examine the linear and quadratic effects. Significant differences among experimental treatment means were identified by post-hoc Tukey's multiple-range tests and declared at P < 0.05.

Results

productive parameters

As presented in Table 3, the broilers fed diets supplemented with CMEOs-900 showed a higher total BWG and a lower FCR compared to the control and other treatments in the starter and finisher periods (ANOVA, P < 0.05). In the whole period of study, compared to the control and other groups, the CMEOs-900 group increased total BWG (ANOVA and linear, P = 0.001), and decreased FCR (ANOVA, P = 0.001; linear, P < 0.04). In the grower period, feeding CMEOs-900 increased total BWG compared to the control and CMEOs-300 groups (ANOVA, P = 0.019). However, total FI during the various periods and the whole period of the experiment, and FCR during the grower period, were not influenced by various treatments (P > 0.05).

Results shown in Table 4 indicate that CMEOs-900 treatment increased (ANOVA and linear, P < 0.05) TAC and the enzyme activities of GPX, SOD, and CAT in serum at 24 and 42 days of age, compared with the control and other treatments.

Discussion

Productive parameters

In the current research, the diet containing CMEOs-900 significantly increased total BWG and decreased FCR compared to the control and other groups, while FI was not affected by treatments over the experimental period. The findings of the current study are consistent with those of earlier experiments, which have also shown that 1.125% blend of thyme and rosemary powders (Nameghi et al., 2022), 200 and 400 g/ton immobilized microspheres of EOs (Paul et al., 2023), and 300 mg/kg non-coated thyme EO (El-Ashram and Abdelhafez, 2020) increased BWG and decreased FCR of broilers. The current results are in agreement with the results of previous researchers showing that 200 mg/kg or 0.5, 1, and 2 kg/ton microencapsulated and/or encapsulated EOs oregano, thyme, and rosemary (Heydarian et al., 2020; Moharreri et al., 2022; Pliego et al., 2022) increased total BW and BWG, while decreasing FCR of broilers. Similarly, a 300 mg/kg mixture of non-coated EOs, including thyme EO, in the diet of broilers did not impact the FI (El-Ashram and Abdelhafez, 2020). In contrast to the findings of the present study, it has been reported that a 200 mg/kg mixture of non-coated thymol and carvacrol (main compounds of thyme and oregano EOs) in the diet of broilers decreased FI (Hashemipour et al., 2013).

This variability in results could be related to the dose, constituents of EOs, synergistic interactions between their components, compatibility with other feed components, as well as their metabolism and use in birds. The proposed mechanisms of action of CMEOs-900 for improving productive parameters of broilers are as follows. In the current study, since feeding broilers with CMEO-900 decreased FCR without affecting their FI level, the increased nutrient digestibility was probably the cause of the increased BWG. Additionally, bioactive compounds of the EOs (i.e., carvacrol, thymol, ρ-cymene, α-pinene, γ-terpinene, 1,8‐cineol, limonene, and borneol) can stimulate digestive and pancreatic enzyme secretion (digestive enzymes, mucin, saliva, bile acids, and mucus), improving gut morphology, elevating antioxidant activities, enhancing immune function, and reducing pathogenic bacteria (Nameghi et al., 2022). This increases metabolic processes, feed digestibility, and nutrient absorption. Moreover, the enhanced growth performance may result from higher secretion of growth-related hormones by feeding EOs (He et al., 2023a). Furthermore, the active and phenolic ingredients of the EOs, such as carvacrol, thymol, 1,8‐cineol, and α-pinene, increase feed digestibility and metabolic processes, which raises the levels of gluconeogenic enzymes (e.g., glucose-6-phosphatase and fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase) in the liver. This finally leads to increased muscle protein formation (El-Ashram and Abdelhafez, 2020). Additionally, the use of EOs increases the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) retention time of feed, thereby enhancing nutrient digestion and absorption (Islam et al., 2022). Furthermore, EOs, by reducing the intestinal pH, improve beneficial gut microbiota, and also, by their lipophilic property, modify the permeability of pathogenic bacterial cell membranes, which leads to the leakage of essential cations throughout the membrane and bacterial death (Seidavi et al., 2022). In addition, EOs can stimulate glycolysis and increase energy utilization for production and, ultimately, growth (Mpofu et al., 2016). However, measurement of other tests, such as evaluating nutrient digestibility and utilization, as well as evaluating higher doses of CMEOs from thyme, rosemary, oregano, and eucalyptus in future investigations, may provide useful supplementary information.

Antioxidant enzyme activities

The results from our study revealed that the diet supplemented with CMEOs-900 significantly increased TAC, as well as GPX, SOD, and CAT activities, compared to the control and other treatments. The findings of the current experiment are in line with those of previous studies, which have shown that in laying hens, a diet containing 275 mg/kg of oregano EO (Johnson et al., 2022), thymol, and carvacrol at 200 mg/kg of the broiler's diet (Hashemipour et al., 2013) and microencapsulated EOs in the diet of broilers (Pliego et al., 2022) increased TAC, as well as activities of SOD, CAT, and GPX in muscle, serum, and liver. In contrast to our study, a previous study found that a blend of non-coated rosemary and thyme EOs did not affect the serum TAC of broilers (Ashayerizadeh et al., 2025).

The body's antioxidant capacity is closely related to the animal's health. Within the body, three key antioxidant enzymes, namely, SOD, CAT, and GPX, are responsible for the first line of antioxidant defense and regulate the redox balance. In poultry, the redox balance regulates various physiological and biochemical functions, such as gene expression, cell signaling, and maintenance of homeostasis (Johnson et al., 2022). Among them, the SOD, which catalyzes the conversion of the superoxide radical into hydrogen peroxide and molecular oxygen, is the first line of defense against reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Johnson et al., 2022). The second line of antioxidant defense is CAT, which breaks down hydrogen peroxide into oxygen and water to prevent its buildup (Chowdhury et al., 2018). The reaction between hydroperoxides and reduced glutathione to produce glutathione disulfide is catalyzed by GPX (a selenoenzyme) (Johnson et al., 2022; Safari et al., 2024a). Finally, these antioxidant enzymes have cytoprotective effects against oxidative stress, which prevent lipid oxidation and shield the body from ROS-induced oxidative stress reactions (Johnson et al., 2022; Safari et al., 2024a). Therefore, increases in antioxidant enzyme activities caused by CMEOs-900 may be due to several of the following reasons. Oregano, thyme, eucalyptus, and rosemary EOs contain active components and phenolic groups, including carvacrol, thymol, 1,8‐cineol, and α-pinene, constituents that have a potent antioxidant effect, which could enhance the body's antioxidant capacity by stimulating antioxidant enzyme activities (Johnson et al., 2022). In addition, the antioxidant function of EOs is not only due to their capacity to delocalize the unpaired electron within the aromatic structure but also their capacity to donate a hydrogen or an electron to free radicals (Chowdhury et al., 2018). Moreover, differences in results might be associated with the species of animal, doses, variety of EOs, ages, and physiological status of animals.

pH Value

In the current experiment, the diet containing CMEOs-900 significantly raised the meat pH value during storage compared to the control and other groups. In agreement with our results, it has been reported that feeding red ginger as phytobiotics increased the meat pH of broilers (Herawati and Marjuki, 2011). In contrast with this study, it has been shown that 50 and 100 mg/kg of dietary encapsulated cinnamaldehyde did not affect the pH of meat in broilers (Yang et al., 2021). This study measured MDA, pH, and WHC values as some of the most critical post-slaughtering meat quality parameters influencing consumers’ health and satisfaction. The quality of the meat is directly reflected in the pH value, which affects its color, tenderness, flavor, and water retention (Safari et al., 2025). Low pH interferes with the space between myosin and actin filaments; it reduces the muscle protein's water-binding capacity (protein denaturation), resulting in degradation and shrinkage of myofibrillar protein (Safari et al., 2025). Additionally, increases in blood creatine kinase and post-mortem intracellular water loss might be caused by an alteration in the integrity of the muscle membrane, which is indicative of membrane injury and linked to pH reduction (Herawati and Marjuki, 2011). Also, anaerobic glycolysis in postmortem muscle produces lactate from glucose, glycogen, and glucose-6-phosphate. Therefore, a decrease in pH is caused by lactate buildup and the protons released from adenosine triphosphate hydrolysis (Safari et al., 2025). Furthermore, Lactic acid produced by anaerobic glycolysis from muscle glycogen causes a reduction in post-mortem muscle pH (Safari et al., 2025).

Improving meat pH through feeding CMEOs-900 may be caused by the following reasons. The antioxidant impact of bioactive compounds of EOs, such as carvacrol, thymol, 1,8‐cineol, ρ-cymene, limonene, and α-pinene, on the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, which results in the formation of acidic molecules, including hydroxyl acid and keto acid, is responsible for preventing the decrease of meat pH (Yang et al., 2021). In addition, it has been acknowledged that increased catecholamine release in response to acute and/or chronic stress before slaughter speeds up the breakdown of glycogen and the post-slaughter pH reduction (Herawati and Marjuki, 2011). Thus, higher pH values in the CMEOs may be due to the antioxidant effects of EOs, which can decrease the harmful effects of stress in animals.

Water-holding capacity

In the current study, the diet containing CMEOs-900 significantly increased the meat WHC during storage compared to the control and other treatments. The findings of our study are also similar to the findings of previous researchers, which illustrated that 1.0 and 1.5 g/kg of dietary Artemisia annua L. plant (Wan et al., 2017), and 600 μl of oregano EO, compared to 300 µl (Salama et al., 2023), increased the WHC of breast meat in broilers. Our findings do not agree with the observation of previous research showing that 50 and 100 mg/kg dietary encapsulated cinnamaldehyde did not affect the WHC of meat in broilers (Yang et al., 2021).

Evaporation, sublimation, and exudation are the methods by which the meat loses water during freezing, storage, and thawing (Safari et al., 2016a). As WHC is correlated with juiciness, flavor, and nutrition, and describes the capacity of the meat to hold onto water during processing, storage, and cooking (Salama et al., 2023). The WHC has a positive correlation with the meat's antioxidant potential; higher WHC of meat can reduce lipid oxidation (Salama et al., 2023). Also, WHC is positively correlated with the pH value. Low meat WHC has been associated with low pH, leading to increased drip and cook loss (Safari et al., 2025). Lipid and protein oxidation alter the structure of the membrane, solubility, gelation, and emulsification of meat cells, leading to an increase in the loss of WHC (Salama et al., 2023). Therefore, the positive impact of CMEOs on the WHC of breast meat might be associated with raising the pH level and/or protecting the cell wall, protein, and lipids from oxidative damage, which was obtained in the current experiment. The EOs in the broiler diet increase gastrointestinal tract enzyme activities, which causes an improvement in the proteinaceous tissue of the cell membrane and WHC of the muscle cell membrane as well (Gheisar et al., 2015).

MDA of breast meat and serum

In the current research, the diet containing CMEOs-900 significantly decreased meat and serum MDA levels compared to the control and other groups. Our results concurred with the findings of earlier studies that showed that a blend of thyme and rosemary EOs and/or powders (Nameghi et al., 2022; Seidavi et al., 2022), an encapsulated and non-encapsulated mixture of thymol and carvacrol (Hashemipour et al., 2013; Li et al., 2022), 0.5-2 kg/ton of microencapsulated EOs, containing thyme (Moharreri et al., 2022), as well as 1.0 and 1.5 g/kg of Artemisia annua L. plant (Wan et al., 2017) in diet of broilers decreased the concentration of MDA in the serum as well as the breast meat during the various times of storage. Contrary to our present findings, it has been shown that a blend of dietary EOs did not affect MDA levels in broilers (Kahiel et al., 2025). The most common chemical deterioration of meat and meat products is due to the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids. Meat oxidation occurs owing to the uneven creation of ROS, reactive nitrogen species, and free radicals. This results in a series of secondary reactions that, in turn, lead to damage to macromolecules, including oxidative deterioration of lipids and proteins and the development of oxidative rancidity (Safari et al., 2025). This causes alterations to the phospholipid bilayer of the cell membrane, damaging the membrane structure and, as a result, increasing permeability. Therefore, increases the leakage of sarcoplasmic fluid and decreases WHC (He et al., 2023b). Furthermore, MDA is the main product of the oxidation of membrane lipids and polyunsaturated fatty acids. It can indicate the level of oxidative and cellular damage caused by oxygen-free radicals in the body (Safari et al., 2016b; He et al., 2023b). In addition, meat oxidation may damage the mitochondria and promote the accumulation of metmyoglobin, resulting in reduced color stability and accelerated decomposition of the meat (Hashemipour et al., 2013).

The generally proposed modes of action in decreasing levels of MDA in the muscle and serum of broilers by CMEOs may be due to the following reasons. Carvacrol, thymol, flavonoids, limonene, 1,8‐cineol, α-pinene, ρ-cymene, camphor, verbenone, isoprenoid quinones, linalool, and other phenolic compounds, the active ingredients in EOs, could scavenge free radicals by chelating metal ions and donating an electron or hydrogen atom to promote endogenous antioxidant enzyme production in cells. This prevents lipid oxidation (He et al., 2023b; Safari et al., 2024b). Furthermore, lowering the pH makes meat oxidation easier because H+ can promote myoglobin's redox cycle and its pro-oxidant effects (Safari et al., 2025). As shown in this study, CMEOs prevent the lowering of the pH of meat. However, among the other factors mentioned in the previous sections, the duration and method of meat storage, as well as tissue, can be factors influencing different results of MDA content in meat.

DPPH and ABTS free radical-scavenging activity

In the current experiment, CMEOs-900 supplementation significantly increased DPPH and ABTS free radical-scavenging activity compared to the control and other treatments. The findings of the current experiment are consistent with those of earlier experiments, where 0.5, 1, and 2 kg/ton dietary microencapsulated EOs, including thyme (Moharreri et al., 2022), 300 mg/kg mixture of dietary non-coated EOs, containing thyme (El-Ashram and Abdelhafez, 2020), 1.0, and 1.5 g/kg of dietary Artemisia annua L. plant (Wan et al., 2017), and 150 and 300 mg/kg of encapsulated rosemary EO (Mousapour et al., 2022) increased the DPPH and ABTS free radical-scavenging activities in serum, breast, and thigh meat of broilers. In contrast with the findings of the current study, it has been shown that dietary supplementation with a microencapsulated blend of organic acid and EO did not affect DPPH and ABTS free radical-scavenging activities in the meat of broilers (Tellini et al., 2024).

Differences in the antioxidant activity of EOs are related to their chemical composition, functional groups, and levels of hydroxylated derivatives, including alcohols, phenols, α-pinene, carvacrol, thymol, and 1,8-cineole, which increase antioxidant activity by increasing concentrations of these components in higher doses (Mousapour et al., 2022). This could be the reason for the different results in different experiments. In our study, the DPPH and ABTS free radical-scavenging activity of the CMEOs can be related to the following reasons. The antioxidant activity of phytogenic feed additives is due to the presence of phenolic hydroxyl compounds such as carvacrol, thymol, α-pinene, ρ-cymene, γ-terpinene, and 1,8-cineole (Moukhfi et al., 2022). These are abundant constituents of EOs of rosemary, oregano, thyme, and eucalyptus. Peroxide radicals can be scavenged by the hydrogen atom of the hydroxyl group, which prevents other substances, such as lipids, from oxidation (Moukhfi et al., 2022). Additionally, EOs can influence antioxidant enzyme activity by regulating nuclear factor erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) (He et al., 2023a). Furthermore, the phenolic compounds of the EOs possess the redox property, as they can be hydrogen, electron, and oxygen donors (Moukhfi et al., 2022).

Anti-SRBC immune response

In our study, CMEOs-900 supplementation significantly enhanced the immune response of broilers, evidenced by increased total anti-SRBC titer, IgM, and IgG levels. Consistent with the outcomes of the current experiment, other experiments have also shown that 0.5, 1, and 2 g/kg of microencapsulated EOs, containing thyme EO (Moharreri et al., 2022; Pliego et al., 2022), a mixture of thymol and carvacrol at 0 to 200 mg/kg (Hashemipour et al., 2013), and a 200 mg/kg blend of encapsulated thyme and oregano EOs (Heydarian et al., 2020) in the diet, increased total anti-SRBC titer, IgG, and IgM levels in broilers. In contrast with the findings of the current study, it has been reported that 200 mg/kg of dietary cumin EO did not affect the IgG and IgM levels after the first SRBC injection in broilers (Amiri et al., 2020).

Improving immune response against SRBC indicates that broilers fed CMEOs-900 are more likely to protect against new infections. Differences in results might be associated with compromised health status, systemic immune dysregulation, oxidative damage, levels and forms of EO, among other factors. Several mechanisms have been suggested for CMEOs to improve humoral immune responses in broilers. For example, the active and phenolic compounds of EOs (carvacrol, thymol, flavonoids, limonene, 1,8‐cineol, α-pinene, ρ-cymene, camphor, γ-terpinene, verbenone, linalool) may act as additional ligands for immunoglobulin molecules' Fc protein receptors, which bind to IgG and enhance immune system function (Saei et al., 2021). In addition, phenolic compounds primarily affect the immune response by promoting phagocytosis, stimulating immunoglobulin and cytokine secretion, and enhancing interferon-γ release (Mahfuz et al., 2021). Furthermore, phenolic compounds may stimulate antioxidant enzymes, which boost immunoglobulin levels and decrease inflammation (Mahfuz et al., 2021). Moreover, these increased blood IgM and IgG immunoglobulin levels may be due to increased B-cell proliferation, which boosts the immune response (Saei et al., 2021). Also, some bioactive substances present in plants regulate the expression of multiple genes related to immune system function (Chowdhury et al., 2018).

Antibody titer against NDV

In the present study, compared to the control and other treatments, feeding CMEOs-900 significantly increased the antibody titer against NDV. The results of the current experiment are in agreement with those documented that dietary rosemary EO in broilers improved the humoral immune responses by increasing the antibody titer against NDV (Seidavi et al., 2022). Similarly, it has been proven that the use of a mixture of EOs, containing eucalyptus EO, increased the antibody titer against NDV (Nahed et al., 2020). In contrast to our study, a previous study found that thyme and turmeric infusions did not affect the antibody titer against NDV in broilers (Sadeghi et al., 2012).

Factors such as the variation in the amount of EO included, the sources of herbs used to make EO mixtures, the EO composition, functional groups, interactions of bioactive components, the basic diet of the birds, and the microbial environment in which the birds were reared might have influenced the variation in the results of various research. There are several potential methods by which CMEOs enhance the antibody titer against NDV in broilers. The 1,8‐cineol, carvacrol, camphor, thymol, limonene, α-pinene, γ-terpinene, borneol, ρ-cymene, verbenone, flavonoids, and linalool as active substances in EO stimulate the immune function by increasing the monocytes' phagocytic activity (Seidavi et al., 2022). Moreover, the 1,8‐cineol active substance in eucalyptus EO has been shown to regulate mucus secretion in the epithelial layer of air passages in the respiratory system (Barbour et al., 2015). Furthermore, the γ-terpinene and α-terpinene in eucalyptus essential oils have been shown in vitro and in vivo to have virulence and antibacterial properties against poultry viruses and bacteria (Barbour et al., 2015). In addition, bioactive ingredients in EO, such as flavonoids (thymol, carvacrol, 1,8‐cineol, and α-pinene) and coumarins, may stimulate macrophages and B-lymphocytes involved in the synthesis of antibodies, resulting in an increased humoral immune response (Seidavi et al., 2022). Moreover, due to the lipophilic nature of EO, antiviral activity may be mediated by disruption of the viral membrane or by interference with the proteins of the viral envelope involved in the attachment to the host cells (Zhang et al., 2022). Furthermore, antioxidant compounds such as carvacrol, thymol, linalool, 1,8‐cineol, α-pinene, and camphor, in EOs, may inhibit free-radical formation, which causes a physiological improvement in the humoral immune system activity (Zhang et al., 2022). Also, the effectiveness of EO in stimulating the immune response may be related to its beneficial effect on increasing the utilization of blood proteins (albumin to globulin ratio) (Seidavi et al., 2022).

PHA and DNCB response

Our results indicated that supplementing the diet with CMEOs-900 increased cellular immunity response regarding higher PHA and DNCB compared to the control and other treatments. Consistent with our findings, previous studies pointed out that the 200 and 400 g/ton immobilized EOs blend containing thyme EO and carvacrol (Paul et al., 2023), encapsulated thyme and oregano EOs at 200 mg/kg (Heydarian et al., 2020), and 200 mg/kg nano-encapsulated cumin EO (Amiri et al., 2020) in the broiler diet, increased cellular immunity response regarding higher PHA and DNCB. Contrary to the results of the current study, it has been shown that 150 mg/kg dietary encapsulated ajwain EO did not affect cell-mediated immunity response in terms of PHA in broilers (Ghazanfari et al., 2023).

Cell-mediated immunity is an immune response in which T-lymphocytes, also called T-cells, are activated. The T-cells are needed to detect and eliminate specific infections, tumor cells, and pathogens in the body (Ghazanfari et al., 2023). The PHA is a plant lectin obtained from red kidney beans, which stimulates T-cell proliferation by acting as a mitogen (Ghazanfari et al., 2023). The PHA skin test is used to evaluate the capacity of T-cells to divide and proliferate in response to an antigen or mitogen, making it a valid technique for assessing acquired immunity (Ghazanfari et al., 2023). The DNCB is a chemical compound that combines with skin proteins to form complexes. Moreover, when DNCB is administered, T-cells activate, leading to some immune responses, including T-cell proliferation and cytokine production (Ghazanfari et al., 2023). The substance acts as an immune modulator by modulating the activity of cells involved in the immune response, such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and lymphocytes (Amiri et al., 2020). Increased skin basophilic hypersensitivity reactions may indicate that EO may have a potential effect on antigen recognition by lymphocytes and provide a more potent response by these cells and basophils (Amiri et al., 2020). Therefore, using challenge agents such as DNCB and PHA to investigate cell-mediated immunity provides valuable information for developing vaccines and targeted therapies that target particular T-cell responses (Ghazanfari et al., 2023).

The CMEOs improved cellular immunity response in terms of higher PHA and DNCB, which might be due to the following reasons. Generally, EOs rich in flavonoids, thymol, carvacrol, 1,8‐cineol, α-pinene, ρ-cymene, and carotenoids enhance the function of the immune system by dynamic regulation of the molecule and increased phagocytic activity, modulation of cytokine secretion, histamine release, secretion of immunoglobulins, plasma myeloperoxidase, and an increase in lysozyme activity (Farouk et al., 2022). Also, active compounds of EOs, including flavonoids such as thymol, menthol, carvacrol, 1,8‐cineol, α-pinene, and ρ‐cymene, increase the activity of vitamin C, act as antioxidants, protect cells from oxidative damage, enhance cellular function and proliferation, and may, therefore, enhance immune functions (Hashemipour et al., 2013). Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory capacity of EO might be associated with its function in enhancing the nonspecific immune responses of host cells by nonspecifically eliminating pathogens (Farouk et al., 2022). Additionally, reports indicate that eucalyptus EO may stimulate both the morphological and functional activation of macrophages derived from monocytes (Placha et al., 2014).

Heterophils and lymphocyte values

In the current research, CMEOs-900 supplementation significantly decreased the H count and H:L ratio compared to the control and other treatments. In agreement with the results of the present study, previous research has demonstrated that 200 mg/kg of dietary nano-encapsulated cumin EO, as well as a mixture of thymol and carvacrol, decreased the H count and H: L ratio in broilers (Hashemipour et al., 2013; Amiri et al., 2020). In contrast to our study, a previous study found that a 200 mg/kg diet of nano-encapsulated cumin EO increased L counts in broilers (Amiri et al., 2020).

The differences in the results of various studies, in addition to the factors mentioned in the previous sections, might be affected by enhancing the immune system process, immune system development, and hematopoietic or lymphoid organ development. The possible mechanism of action proposed to CMEOs by which the decreased count of H and the H:L ratio is listed below. The lymphoid structures in the gut play a crucial role in regulating the systemic immune system. The EO and herbal substances such as ρ-cymene, 1,8‐cineol, thymol, α-pinene, carvacrol, and γ-terpinene can activate the lymphatic system indirectly through the modulation of microbiota in the GIT (Rafeeq et al., 2023). Besides, it is well established that EO, including eucalyptus EO, increases transepithelial electrical resistance in broilers and stimulates the immune system response by increasing monocyte phagocytic activity. These monocytes express splendid anti-inflammatory properties in the intestine (Xue et al., 2020). Additionally, the high biological activity of lymphocytes renders them highly susceptible to oxidative stress. The destruction of immune-related cells is done by oxidative stress, which in turn suppresses the immune system by producing prostaglandin E2 (Mousavi et al., 2018). Therefore, the antioxidant capacity of the 1,8‐cineol, thymol, ρ-cymene, carvacrol, α-pinene, and camphor as active substances in EOs prevents such damage. Furthermore, the bioactive ingredients of EO, such as flavonoids, thymol, linalool, carvacrol, 1,8‐cineol, α-pinene, and ρ-cymene, can enhance the function of macrophages, natural killer cells, and lymphocytes, increase phagocytosis, and stimulate the synthesis of interferons (Amiri et al., 2020). Moreover, as explained, properties of biologically active ingredients in EOs, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and GIT microbiota modulating activities, appear to contribute to the improvement of GIT health and intestinal integrity, which help provide the nutrients necessary for the development of the immune system response.

Conclusion

It is concluded that CMEOs-900 treatment improved the productive parameters, antioxidant capacity, and both cellular and humoral immune responses compared with the control and other treatments. In addition, supplementation of CMEOs-900 in broilers' diet improved the meat quality and oxidative stability at 0, 7, and 14 days of meat storage, and thus extended the shelf life of meat postmortem. Based on the results of the current experiment, the supplementation of CMEOs-900 in the diet of broilers as a valuable feed additive to improve productive parameters, immune response, antioxidant status, and meat quality is recommended.
