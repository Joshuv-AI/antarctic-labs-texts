Feasibility of reducing the contrast dose in infants and children with complex congenital heart disease using virtual monoenergetic images on dual-layer spectral detector computed tomography
Authors: Ximing Cao, Wenbin Hu, Jinglei Li, Xiaomin Liu, Hui Liu, Hongjun Liu
Published: 2026

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is one of the most common cardiovascular diseases in infants and children [1–3], with a broad spectrum of anatomical and functional abnormalities. Depending on the type and severity of CHD, patients may require surgery or interventional therapy [1].

Imagings provide important value in the diagnosis, preoperative preparation, surgical planning, surgical risk assessment, and postoperative follow-up of CHD [3, 4]. A variety of imaging modalities are available for CHD, such as echocardiography, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and cardiac catheterization intervention.

Echocardiography, as a non-invasive and widely accessible modality, is the first-line imaging tool for CHD evaluation due to its safety and reproducibility. However, its diagnostic performance varies significantly depending on the operator’s experience, and is limited by an acoustic window and hardware. Moreover, echocardiography is less effective for diagnosing extracardiac structures and great vessel anomalies compared to CT [5–7]. Cardiac catheterization intervention is typically utilized as the gold standard modality for diagnosing CHD, but it is invasive and relatively time-intensive, requiring general anesthesia [7]. MRI provides superior soft-tissue contrast without radiation, but it is constrained due to long scan durations and high acoustic noise levels, particularly in pediatric patients.

CT has become the imaging modality of choice for the comprehensive evaluation of complex CHD due to its high spatial resolution, rapid acquisition time, and minimal invasiveness. In particular, its superior capability in the visualization of extracardiac vascular anatomy makes it a valuable complement to echocardiography in clinical decision-making [8–11]. However, the kidney injury and adverse reactions when applying contrast agents must not be disregarded during CTA examinations, particularly in infants and children [4, 12–14].

Dual-layer spectral detector CT, a novel spectral imaging technique, employs a single X-ray source and two detector layers to simultaneously capture high-energy and low-energy projection data at the same spatial and angular position [12]. Dual-layer spectral detector CT can generate a diverse array of reconstructed images, such as virtual monoenergetic images (VMIs), iodine maps, and effective atomic number (Zeff) images [15]. Based on the increasing attenuation of iodine as photon energies approach the k-edge at 33.2 keV [16], VMI reconstructions at lower keV facilitate increased soft tissue, iodine contrast, and vessel attenuation, potentially allowing for diagnostic-quality imaging with a reduced dose of contrast media. Furthermore, dual-layer spectral detector CT effectively reduces image noise by leveraging the nearly perfectly aligned projection data from both detector layers and applying an anticorrelated noise reduction algorithm, thereby maintaining low noise levels across all VMIs [17]. Some studies have reported a reduction in contrast media in craniocervical, pulmonary angiography, lower extremity and superior mesenteric artery CTA by using VMIs [17–22]. However, to date, no studies have evaluated the clinical application of VMIs for reducing contrast media dose in dual-layer spectral detector CT imaging of infants and children with CHD. Therefore, the aim of this study is to investigate the feasibility of contrast media reduction using VMIs in dual-layer spectral detector CT and to determine the optimal keV of VMIs for infants and children with CHD.

Materials and methods

Patients

This prospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. A total of 100 consecutive infants and children (age under 5 years) with clinically suspected complex CHD were prospectively enrolled from June 2024 to April 2025. All patients underwent dual-layer spectral detector CT examination before surgery. They were randomly divided into two groups according to the contrast dose: group A (standard dose, 1.5–2.5 ml/kg of contrast media) and group B (low dose, 1 ml/kg of contrast media). Exclusion criteria: contraindication to iodinated contrast media, failed CT examination due to sedation, no surgical treatment, renal insufficiency, motion artifacts. Finally, 80 patients were included in this study, with 36 in group A and 44 in group B. The flow chart of CT data collection in this study is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Imaging protocols and reconstruction

All patients underwent CT examinations on a dual-layer spectral detector CT (Spectral CT 7500, Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) with the following parameters: detector collimation according to the scan range, 96/112/128×0.625 mm; gantry rotation time of 0.27 s; matrix of 512×512; tube voltage of 100 kVp; automatic tube current adapted to the patient size; slice thickness of 0.67 mm; slice increment of 0.34 mm. CT datasets were acquired using prospective electrocardiogram-gating (ECG-gating) acquisition mode, and reconstructed at 45% of the R–R interval, with a ±5% buffer zone for data acquisition.

The iodinated contrast media (iopromide 370 mgI/ml, Ultravist 370, Bayer HealthCare, Leverkusen, Germany) was injected through a 24-gauge intravenous access at the upper extremity or lower extremity using a dual-head power injector (Ulrich, CT motion, Ulm, Germany). The contrast dose was adjusted according to the body weight: 1.5–2.5 ml/kg for group A (standard dose) and 1 ml/kg for group B (low dose). Based on the contrast dose, injections were performed at a flow rate ranging from 0.5 ml/s to 2 ml/s for durations exceeding 10 s, followed by a saline flush of 5–15 ml at an identical flow rate.

A manual bolus tracking technique was used to visually monitor contrast passage at the four-chamber cardiac plane, where the region of interest (ROI) was placed outside of the body. The scan was initiated manually when the contrast of the four-chamber cardiac was deemed appropriate with a delay of 2 s by the radiologic technologist.

Images were reconstructed on a workstation (IntelliSpace Portal 10.0, Philips Healthcare, the Netherlands) using spectral base data. Conventional image and virtual monoenergetic images (VMIs) in the range of 40–85 keV with an interval of 5 keV were reconstructed in group B while only the conventional image was reconstructed in group A. The reconstruction parameters were as follows: a spectral reconstruction algorithm (Spectral, level 0) for VMIs, a hybrid iterative reconstructive algorithm (iDose4, level 3) for the conventional image.

Image quality

Objective evaluations of image quality

The quantitative evaluation included CT-value, standard deviation (SD), signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) for the pulmonary artery and ascending aorta.

The CT-value and SD were measured by placing a ROI of 5–10 mm2 drawn independently by two radiologic technologists and averaged (XM.C. with 10 and WB.H. with 3 years of experience in cardiovascular imaging and blinded to the details of surgery) on axial slices in the pulmonary artery, ascending aorta, and left pectoral muscle at the level of the main pulmonary artery carefully avoiding the inclusion of vessel walls and atherosclerotic plaques. The SNR and CNR were calculated using the following formula: SNR ascending aorta=attenuation ascending aorta/noise ascending aorta, SNR pulmonary artery=attenuation pulmonary artery/noise pulmonary artery, CNR ascending aorta= (attenuation ascending aorta−attenuation pectoral muscle)/mean noise, CNR pulmonary artery=(attenuation pulmonary artery-attenuation pectoral muscle)/mean noise where mean noise=(noise ascending aorta+noise pulmonary artery+noise pectoral muscle)/3.

Subjective evaluations of image quality

Axial images and reconstructed images in each group were independently scored using a 5-point scale by two senior radiologists (JL.L. with 19 and H.L. with 20 years of experience in diagnostic cardiovascular imaging) who underwent uniform and standardized training and had no knowledge of the grouping results. The scoring criteria were based on relevant studies [2, 18], and a 5-point Likert scale was used based on the overall clarity of vascular details (Fig. 2): 5 points, excellent anatomical structure and satisfactory detail, best image quality; 4 points, good anatomical structure and details; 3 points, confidence in the details declines, fair anatomical structures; 2 points, poor details and poor anatomical clarity; 1 point, unacceptable anatomical clarity and details. An overall image quality rating of 3 or greater was classified as diagnostic; a rating of 1 or 2 was non-diagnostic.

Representative images illustrating the 5-point Likert scale for image quality assessment based on the diagnostic clarity of vascular structures. a Very poor (score=1). b Poor (score=2). c Adequate (score=3). d Good (score=4). e Excellent (score=5)

Diagnostic accuracy

Assessment of diagnostic utility in the cardiac CT was performed by a senior radiologist (H.L., with 20 years of experience in cardiovascular imaging and blinded to the details of surgery). To evaluate the diagnostic accuracy, cardiac CT findings were compared to the subsequent intraoperative findings, and differences were documented.

Radiation dose

The CT dose index volume (CTDIvol, mGy) and dose-length products (DLP, mGy×cm) were recorded for all dual-layer CT examinations based on CT dose reports.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Continuous data were expressed as mean±SD if normally distributed, nonparametric data were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). For comparisons between two groups, normally distributed data were analyzed using the independent-samples Student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. For comparisons among more than two groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, followed by Dunnett’s test for post hoc analysis. Diagnostic performance was compared against surgical findings. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) assessed inter-reader agreement on quantitative indicators. The Weighted Cohen’s kappa test was used to test the inter-rater agreement for the qualitative analysis of subjective image quality scoring with kappa values: >0.80 indicating almost perfect agreement, 0.60–0.79 substantial, 0.40–0.59 moderate, 0.21–0.39 fair, and <0.20, none to slight agreement. A P-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 4.1.2) and MedCalc (version 20, MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium).

Results

Clinical characteristics and contrast dose

No significant differences in clinical characteristics including sex, age, weight, and heart rate were observed between the two groups. The contrast dose and injection rate in group B were reduced by 24% [8.0 (5.0; 11.0) ml vs. 10.5 (8.0; 21.0) ml, P<0.05] and 16% [0.8 (0.6; 1.0) ml/s vs. 1.0 (0.8; 1.4) ml, P<0.05], respectively, compared with group A. Despite the differences, all images from VMI 40–50 keV in group B and CI in group A were deemed diagnostically acceptable (all scores ≥3). In cardiac CT, the primary focus in evaluating CHD was on obtaining preliminary diagnostic information regarding the major great vessels. Radiation dose

No significant differences were observed in CTDIvol [group A: (3.6±1.1) mGy vs. group B: (3.5±1.0) mGy, P>0.05] and DLP [group A: (35.9±15.7) mGy×cm vs. group B: (38.2±13.6) mGy×cm, P>0.05] between the two groups.

Discussion

This study investigated the feasibility of reducing the contrast dose and the optimal keV of VMIs on dual-layer spectral detector CT in infants and children with complex CHD, without compromising image quality and diagnostic efficacy. Our results demonstrated that the low-dose group with optimized VMIs achieved a 24% reduction in contrast volume and a 16% reduction in injection rate, while maintaining superior image quality and diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of the standard-dose group.

The risk of contrast-related adverse events is strongly associated with both the iodine dose and injection rate. In pediatric imaging, especially in infants, reducing the contrast load is of clinical importance, as it can alleviate the renal burden of iodine excretion and minimize the risk of nephrotoxicity [12]. Moreover, a slower injection rate not only decreases the risk of contrast medium extravasation but also reduces vascular irritation, mitigates pain, improves patient comfort, and avoids examination failure or repeat examination. Furthermore, this approach has more benefits for children with small-caliber intravenous access due to the reduced injection pressure [23].

Previous studies have reported that low tube voltage (80–100 kVp) is the primary method to reduce contrast medium dose and concentration while maintaining image quality. Wang S.Y. et al. [4] reported that prospective ECG-triggered cardiac CT using 80 kVp, a low-concentration iodinated contrast agent (270 mg iodine/ml), and 70% adaptive statistical iterative reconstruction (ASIR) provided excellent image quality and accurate diagnosis for complex CHD in infants with reduced contrast medium dose and low radiation dose.

Recently, spectral imaging with VMIs has emerged as an effective strategy to enhance contrast conspicuity at lower iodine concentrations. In our study, a 24% reduction in contrast volume and a 16% reduction in injection rate were achieved, without compromising image quality or diagnostic performance. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have explored dose-reduction strategies in pediatric cardiac CT. For instance, Xie et al. [12] reported that the dual-energy CT angiography in children achieved a 40% reduction in iodine dose and a 16% reduction in contrast injection rate, with image quality comparable to that of single-energy CT.

In our study, VMIs were acquired on dual-layer spectral detector CT, which differs from other dual-energy technologies by utilizing a single X-ray source and a single detector composed of two layers: an upper yttrium-based scintillator for low-energy photons and a lower gadolinium oxysulfide scintillator for high-energy photons [24, 25]. This enables simultaneous acquisition of high- and low-energy datasets at identical spatial and angular positions, from which VMIs (ranging from 40 keV to 200 keV) are reconstructed. Notably, lower-keV VMIs enhance iodine attenuation due to their proximity to the k-edge of iodine (33.2 keV), thereby facilitating lower contrast medium dosage and injection rate while increasing the conspicuity of contrast material [25]. Moreover, because the high- and low-energy projection data in dual-layer spectral detector CT are inherently aligned in both space and time, the system enables effective noise reduction by leveraging the anticorrelated nature of photoelectric and Compton scatter signals [17].

In our study, although conventional images in the low-dose group exhibited suboptimal enhancement, VMIs reconstructed at 40–45 keV significantly improved objective parameters such as CT attenuation, SNR, and CNR of the pulmonary artery and ascending aorta—surpassing even those of conventional images in the standard-dose group. The noise of VMI 40 keV was not significantly different from that on a conventional image. Moreover, the subjective evaluation score on VMI 40–50 keV in the low-dose group was significantly higher than that of the conventional image in the standard-dose group. These findings are consistent with previous studies which demonstrated improved CT-value, SNR, and CNR at low keV VMI [26–29].

The primary role of CT in the evaluation of complex CHD is to provide a comprehensive assessment of the extracardiac vascular structure, coronary arteries, great vessel pathologies, and noncardiac structure, especially when echocardiographic findings are inconclusive [3]. In our study, diagnostic accuracy was evaluated based on extracardiac findings, including pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary artery stenosis, pulmonary atresia, patent ductus arteriosus, coarctation of the aorta, transposition of the great arteries, overriding aorta, and right-sided aortic arch. VMIs demonstrated superior diagnostic performance in extracardiac great vessels. Diagnostic accuracy for CHD at VMI 40–50 keV in low-dose group was 97.73%, significantly higher than conventional image in standard-dose group (93.40%). Notably, the NPV at VMI 40–50 keV in low-dose group was significantly higher than conventional image in standard-dose group (98.1% vs. 94.2%), while no significant differences were observed in sensitivity and specificity between two groups.

An important technical challenge identified in our study was that contrast dose reduction resulted in a narrower enhancement time window for infants weighing <3 kg, which demands higher technical expertise during scanning. Due to hardware limitations of the device, a minimum tube voltage of 100 kVp was required to generate VMIs. To mitigate radiation exposure, multiple dose-saving strategies were adopted, including automatic tube current adapted to the patient size, prospective ECG-gating acquisition mode and narrow data acquisition window in 40~50% phase of the R–R interval.

Our study has several limitations. First, the patient population in this study was limited to children under 5 years, which may affect the generalizability of the findings to older pediatric cohorts. Second, no additional stratification by patient weight was performed in this study, which could influence contrast distribution and imaging outcomes. In the future study, we will enroll older pediatric patients and adopt more precise weight-based stratification, particularly those weighing less than 3 kg.

Conclusions

Reducing contrast dose is especially beneficial in pediatric populations. Spectral CT with optimized VMI settings offers a promising approach for accurate preoperative evaluation of complex CHD with lower contrast burden.
