On the tightness of left-invariant contact structures
Eugenio Bellini 1

Abstract
. We prove that all left-invariant contact structures on three-dimensional Lie groups are tight. The argument is based on Riemannian methods and establishes a unique factorization property for any Lie group admitting a left-invariant contact structure, other than SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) . We then make use of such factorization property to construct embeddings of left-invariant contact structures into the standard contact structure on ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} .

53D, 22E, 53C.

1.
Statement of the results

A
3-dimensional contact manifold

(M,\xi)
is a smooth
3
-manifold
M
equipped with a contact structure
\xi
, that is, a completely non-integrable plane field
[
20
,
25
]
. A contact manifold is said to be
overtwisted
if it contains an embedded disk whose boundary is tangent to
\xi
; otherwise, it is called
tight
. This tight/overtwisted dichotomy, originating in the seminal work of Bennequin
[
3
]
, represents a turning point in the development of contact topology. While overtwisted contact structures on closed
3
-manifolds have been completely classified and tight contact structures have been the subject of extensive study (see for instance
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
5
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
8
,
21
]
), determining whether a specific contact structure is tight or overtwisted remains, in general, a non-trivial problem.

In this work, we address the tightness question in the setting of
left-invariant contact structures
. Recall that a contact structure
\xi
on a Lie group
G
is called
left-invariant
if it is preserved under left translations, i.e. for every
g\in G
we have
(L_{g})_{*}\xi=\xi
. Left-invariant contact structures are of independent interest in different areas of mathematics. For example, they appear in contact topology
[
19
,
18
]
, in higher-dimensional contact and Riemannian geometry
[
9
,
10
]
, and in sub-Riemannian geometry
[
1
,
17
,
4
]
. They also play a role in K-contact geometry and comparison theorems
[
2
, Sec. 6.3]
, as well as in models of the visual cortex
[
7
,
6
]
. A classification of three-dimensional Lie groups admitting a left-invariant contact structure can be deduced from the classification of three-dimensional Lie algebras (see, for instance
[
1
]
). In particular, there exist infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic simply connected Lie groups carrying a left-invariant contact structure.

Our main goal is to determine whether such left-invariant contact structures are tight or overtwisted. We obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

All left-invariant contact structures on three-dimensional Lie groups are tight.

The question of the tightness of left-invariant structures first emerged for the author in the study of contact metric three manifolds. Specifically, the motivation was to investigate whether left-invariant structures could serve as model spaces for Riemannian and sub-Riemannian comparison theories aimed at establishing quantitative tightness results, see
[
15
,
16
,
2
]
.
As far as the author is aware, no statement concerning the tightness of left-invariant contact structures can be found in the literature.

1.1.
Outline of the proof

We briefly describe the strategy of the proof in the simply connected case; the non-simply connected case follows because the property of being overtwisted lifts to the universal cover (see, for instance,
[
2
, Remark  2.6]
).

Let
(G,\xi)
be a simply connected Lie group endowed with a left-invariant contact structure. We distinguish two cases according to the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
G
.

Case 1:
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)
.

Lie’s theorem implies that
G
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{SU}(2)
. Moreover, all left-invariant plane fields on
\mathrm{SU}(2)
are related by a Lie group isomorphism, and are well known to be tight contact structures (see, for instance,
[
25
]
).

Case 2:
\mathfrak{g}\not\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)
.

In this case, the structure of
G
is described by the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(G,\xi)
be a three-dimensional simply connected Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}\not\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)
, endowed with a left-invariant contact structure
\xi
. Then there exist three
1
-dimensional Lie subgroups
H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}
of
G
, with
TH_{3}\subset\xi
, such that the map

(2)

p:H_{1}\times H_{2}\times H_{3}\longrightarrow G,\qquad p(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})=h_{1}h_{2}h_{3}

is a diffeomorphism.

Choose generators
v_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}
so that
T_{1_{G}}H_{i}=\mathrm{span}\{v_{i}\}
for
i=1,2,3
. Theorem
1.2
implies that the map

(3)

p:\mathbb{R}^{3}\longrightarrow G,\qquad p(x,y,z)=\exp(xv_{1})\exp(yv_{2})\exp(zv_{3}),

where
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G
is the Lie group exponential map, is a diffeomorphism. In particular,
G
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and admits global coordinates
(x,y,z)
such that the vector field
\partial_{z}
is tangent to
\xi
, since
v_{3}\in\xi
.
Applying the following lemma, proved in
Section

4
, immediately yields tightness of
\xi
.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
\xi
be a contact structure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If there exist global coordinates
(x,y,z)
such that the vector field
\partial_{z}
is tangent to
\xi
, then
\xi
is a tight contact structure.

It remains to justify Theorem
1.2
.
For
G=\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2)
, the factorization is provided explicitly.
For all other groups with
\mathfrak{g}\not\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)
, we first construct a left-invariant Riemannian metric on
G
together with a
2
-dimensional subgroup
H
and a totally geodesic subgroup
H_{3}
, which is tangent to
\xi
and orthogonal to
H
.
We then apply the following lemma.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
G
be a simply connected Lie group endowed with a left-invariant Riemannian metric. Assume the existence of an immersed codimension-one subgroup
H
and a totally geodesic subgroup
H_{3}
, orthogonal to each other. Then the map

(4)

p:H\times H_{3}\longrightarrow G,\qquad p(h,h_{3})=hh_{3}

is a diffeomorphism.

Since
H
is
2
-dimensional, the classification of
2
-dimensional Lie groups yields a further factorization
H\simeq H_{1}\times H_{2}
(cf.
Section

3.1
), where
\simeq
denotes a diffeomorphism of smooth manifolds (not a Lie group isomorphism).

2.
Two lemmas on Riemannian Lie groups

Given a Riemannian manifold
(M,\eta)
, we denote the normal bundle of an immersed submanifold
S\subset M
as
TS^{\perp}
.
We say that
S
is complete as a metric subspace of
M
if
(S,d|_{S})
is a complete metric space,
d|_{S}
being the restriction of the Riemannian distance.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(M,\eta)
be a Riemannian manifold and
S
be an immersed submanifold which is complete as a metric subspace. The following are equivalent:

i)

(M,\eta)
is a complete Riemannian manifold,

ii)

The normal exponential map
\exp^{\perp}:TS^{\perp}\to M
, i.e., the restriction of the Riemannian exponential map to the normal bundle
TS^{\perp}
, is well-defined on the whole
TS^{\perp}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Notice that for
S=\{p\}
we recover the classical statement of Hopf-Rinow theorem.

The proof of
Theorem

2.1
is analogous to the one of
[
11
, Thm. 2.8, Chap. 7]
, replacing the normal neighborhood of a point with a normal tubular neighborhood around
S
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a Lie group endowed with a left-invariant Riemannian metric. Let
H\subset G
be an immersed Lie subgroup. If the normal exponential map
\exp^{\perp}:TH^{\perp}\to G
is an immersion then it is a covering map.

Proof.

We denote with
\eta
the left-invariant Riemannian metric. Since the normal exponential map is an immersion,
(TH^{\perp},(\exp^{\perp})^{*}\eta)
is a Riemannian manifold.
Notice that
H
acts on
TH^{\perp}
:

(5)

\displaystyle H\times TH^{\perp}\to TH^{\perp},\qquad(h,v)\mapsto dL_{h}v,

where
L_{h}:G\to G
denotes the left translation
L_{h}(g)=hg
.
The action preserves the metric
(\exp^{\perp})^{*}\eta

(6)

dL_{h}^{*}(\exp^{\perp})^{*}\eta=(\exp^{\perp}\circ\,dL_{h})^{*}\eta=(L_{h}\circ\exp^{\perp})^{*}\eta=(\exp^{\perp})^{*}L_{h}^{*}\eta=(\exp^{\perp})^{*}\eta.

The zero section
s_{0}\subset TH^{\perp}
, is invariant under the action (
5
). Furthermore,
H
acts transitively on
s_{0}
. This implies that
s_{0}\subset TH^{\perp}
is complete as a metric subspace of
(TH^{\perp},(\exp^{\perp})^{*}\eta)
, because it is a locally compact metric space with transitive isometry group.
Moreover, the normal exponential map of the zero section of
TH^{\perp}
, which we denote
\exp^{\perp}_{0}:\left(Ts_{0}\right)^{\perp}\to TH^{\perp}
, is well-defined, the normal geodesics being the 1-dimensional subspaces of the fibres of the vector bundle
TH^{\perp}
. Theorem
2.1
implies that
(TH^{\perp},(\exp^{\perp})^{*}\eta)
is a complete Riemannian manifold. Therefore

(7)

\exp^{\perp}:(TH^{\perp},(\exp^{\perp})^{*}\eta)\to(G,\eta)

is a surjective local isometry of complete Riemannian manifolds and thus, according to
[
11
, Lem. 3.3, Chap. 7]
, a covering map.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a simply connected Lie group endowed with a left-invariant Riemannian metric. Assume the existence of an immersed codimension-one subgroup
H
and a totally geodesic subgroup
H_{3}
, orthogonal to each other. Then the map

(8)

p:H\times H_{3}\longrightarrow G,\qquad p(h,h_{3})=hh_{3}

is a diffeomorphism.

Proof.

Let
X
be a left-invariant vector field spanning
TH_{3}
. Since
H_{3}
is totally geodesic and orthogonal to
H
, the vector field
X
is geodesic and orthogonal to
H
. Therefore, the normal exponential map can be written as

(9)

\exp^{\perp}:TH^{\perp}\simeq H\times\mathbb{R}\to G,\qquad\exp^{\perp}(h,t)=e^{tX}(h).

Because
X
and
H
are transverse,
\exp^{\perp}
is an immersion. By
Lemma

2.3
, it is also a covering map. Since
G
is simply connected,
\exp^{\perp}
is a diffeomorphism. It follows that the map

(10)

\psi:\mathbb{R}\to H_{3},\qquad\psi(t)=\exp^{\perp}(1_{G},t)=e^{tX}(1_{G}),

where
1_{G}
denotes the identity element of
G
, is a diffeomorphism as well. Finally, the map
p:H\times H_{3}\to G
can be expressed as

(11)

p(h,h_{3})=hh_{3}=e^{\psi^{-1}(h_{3})X}(h)=\exp^{\perp}(h,\psi^{-1}(h_{3})),

where the second equality follows from left-invariance of
X
and definition of
\psi
. Since
\exp^{\perp}
and
\psi
are diffeomorphisms, we conclude the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(G,\eta)
be a Lie group with a left-invariant Riemannian metric
\eta
and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a left-invariant orthonormal frame with structure constants
c_{ij}^{k}
:

(12)

[X_{i},X_{j}]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{ji}^{k}X_{k},\qquad i,j=1,\dots,n.

Then, the vector field
X_{1}
is geodesic if and only if
c_{1j}^{1}=0
for all
j=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

Let
\nabla
denote the Levi-Civita connection. For a fixed
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we compute

(13)

\eta(\nabla_{X_{1}}X_{1},X_{j})=-\eta(X_{1},\nabla_{X_{1}}X_{j})=-\eta(X_{1},\nabla_{X_{j}}X_{1}+[X_{1},X_{j}])=\eta(X_{1},[X_{j},X_{1}])=c_{1j}^{1}.

Therefore
\nabla_{X_{1}}X_{1}=0
if and only if
c_{1j}^{1}=0
for all
j=1,\dots,n
.
∎

3.
A unique factorization property: proof of
Theorem

1.2

In this section we show that any contact group with
\mathfrak{g}\not\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)
satisfies a unique factorization property. We illustrate this fact in
Example

3.1
, and, after introducing a convenient basis for
\mathfrak{g}
in
Lemma

3.2
, we prove
Theorem

1.2
.

Example 3.1
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2)
be the universal cover of
\mathrm{SL}(2)
, the latter being the group of
2\times 2
real matrices with determinant
1
. A basis for its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2)
is given by

(14)

v_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad v_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad v_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Note that such basis of
\mathfrak{sl}(2)
satisfies

(15)

[v_{1},v_{0}]=-v_{2},\qquad[v_{2},v_{0}]=v_{1},\qquad[v_{2},v_{1}]=v_{0}.

We define the standard contact structure on
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2)
as
\xi=\mathrm{span}\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
, extended to the whole group by left translations. Given
A\in\mathrm{SL}(2)
, seeing its columns as vectors
(x,y)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there exists a unique
O\in\mathrm{SO}(2)
mapping the first column of
A
to a vector belonging to the positive
x
-axes. That is to say, for any
A\in\mathrm{SL}(2)
there exists a unique
O\in\mathrm{SO}(2)
such that
OA\in H
where

(16)

H=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
0&a_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2)\,:\,a_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\,a_{1}>0\right\}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}e^{a}&b\\
0&e^{-a}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2)\,:\,a,b\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Therefore any matrix
A\in\mathrm{SL}(2)
can be uniquely factorized as
A=O^{-1}B
, with
O\in\mathrm{SO}(2)
and
B\in H
. Another way to state this unique factorization property is saying that the following map

(17)

\mathrm{SO}(2)\times H\to\mathrm{SL}(2),\qquad(O,B)\mapsto OB,

is a diffeomorphism. Introducing the following subgroups

(18)

H_{2}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&v\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2)\,:\,v\in\mathbb{R}\right\},\qquad H_{3}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}e^{u}&0\\
0&e^{-u}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2)\,:\,u\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

one can check that the map
H_{2}\times H_{3}\to H
defined as
(h_{2},h_{3})\mapsto h_{2}h_{3}
is a diffeomorphism. We can rewrite the diffeomorphism (
17
) as

(19)

\mathrm{SO}(2)\times H_{2}\times H_{3}\to\mathrm{SL}(2),\qquad(O,h_{2},h_{3})\mapsto Oh_{2}h_{3}.

Passing to the universal covers, denoting with
H_{1}
the universal cover of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
, we find the following diffeomorphism

(20)

p:H_{1}\times H_{2}\times H_{3}\to\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2),\qquad p(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})=h_{1}h_{2}h_{3}.

Notice that
TH_{3}\subset\xi
. Indeed, from (
14
) and the definition of
H_{3}
in (
18
), we deduce that
TH_{3}=\mathrm{span}\{v_{1}\}
, while, by definition,
\xi=\mathrm{span}\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(G,\xi)
be a three-dimensional Lie group with a left-invariant contact structure. Let
\mathfrak{g}
denote its Lie algebra. Then there exists a basis
\{v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}\}
for
\mathfrak{g}
such that
v_{1},v_{2}\in\xi
, and whose Lie brackets satisfy

(21)

[v_{0},v_{1}]=c_{10}^{2}v_{2},\qquad[v_{0},v_{2}]=c_{20}^{1}v_{1},\qquad[v_{1},v_{2}]=c_{21}^{1}v_{1}+c_{21}^{2}v_{2}-v_{0},

for some
c_{ij}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, if
c_{10}^{2}=c_{20}^{1}=0
, then we can choose
v_{1},v_{2}\in\xi
so that

(22)

[v_{0},v_{1}]=[v_{0},v_{2}]=0,\qquad[v_{1},v_{2}]=c_{21}^{2}v_{2}-v_{0}.

Proof.

We fix a left-invariant contact form
\alpha
, i.e., a left-invariant differential form such that
\xi=\ker\alpha
. Let
R
denote the associated Reeb vector field, i.e., the unique vector field satisfying

(23)

\alpha(R)=1,\qquad d\alpha(R,\cdot)=0.

Let
\{w_{0},w_{1},w_{2}\}
be a left-invariant trivialization of
TG
satisfying

(24)

w_{0}=R,\qquad w_{1},w_{2}\in\xi.

Since the flow of the Reeb vector field preserves the contact structure, we have the endomorphism

(25)

\mathrm{ad}\,w_{0}:\xi\to\xi,\qquad v\mapsto[w_{0},v].

Assume first that
\mathrm{ad}\,w_{0}
is identically zero. Then we have constants
a_{21}^{1},a_{21}^{2},a_{21}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(26)

[w_{0},w_{1}]=[w_{0},w_{2}]=0,\qquad[w_{1},w_{2}]=a_{21}^{1}w_{1}+a_{21}^{2}w_{2}+a_{21}^{0}w_{0}.

If
a_{21}^{1}=a_{21}^{2}=0
, then the basis satisfying (
22
) is obtained setting
v_{1}=w_{1},\,v_{2}=w_{2},\,v_{0}=-a_{21}^{0}w_{0}
.
Otherwise, if for instance
a_{21}^{2}\neq 0
, then the basis
\{v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}\}
for
\mathfrak{g}
, with
v_{1},v_{2}\in\xi
, defined by

(27)

v_{1}=\frac{1}{a_{21}^{2}}w_{1},\qquad v_{2}=a_{21}^{1}w_{1}+a_{21}^{2}w_{2},\qquad v_{0}=-a_{21}^{0}w_{0},

satisfies (
22
). The case
a_{21}^{1}\neq 0
is analogous.

Assume that (
25
) is not identically zero. We claim that, nonetheless,
\mathrm{ad}\,w_{0}
is traceless. Let
\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
be a trivialization of
T^{*}G
dual to
w_{0},w_{1},w_{2}
, i.e.,
\theta_{i}(w_{j})=\delta_{ij}
. Being
w_{1},w_{2}
a basis for
\xi
, which is a contact distribution, the bracket
[v_{1},v_{2}]
is transverse to
\xi
. One has
\theta_{0}([w_{1},w_{2}])\neq 0
. Up to re-scaling
w_{0}
we may assume the existence of
c_{21}^{1},c_{21}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(28)

[w_{1},w_{2}]=c_{21}^{1}w_{1}+c_{21}^{2}w_{2}-w_{0}.

Since
\theta_{i}(w_{j})=\delta_{ij}
, an application of Cartan formula
d\theta_{i}(v,w)=v(\theta_{i}(w))-w(\theta_{i}(v))-\theta_{i}([v,w])
yields

(29)

d\theta_{k}=-\theta_{k}([w_{0},w_{1}])\theta_{0}\wedge\theta_{1}-\theta_{k}([w_{0},w_{2}])\theta_{0}\wedge\theta_{2}-\theta_{k}([w_{1},w_{2}])\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}.

Therefore, in light of (
28
), for
k=0
one finds

(30)

d\theta_{0}=\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2},

where we have used that
\theta_{0}([w_{0},w_{1}])=\theta_{0}([w_{0},w_{2}])=0
, since
\mathrm{ad}\,w_{0}
maps
\xi=\mathrm{span}\{w_{1},w_{2}\}
to itself.
From the identity
d^{2}\theta_{0}=0
we deduce

(31)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=d^{2}\theta_{0}=d\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}\wedge d\theta_{2}=-(\theta_{1}([w_{0},w_{1}])+\theta_{2}([w_{0},w_{2}]))\theta_{0}\wedge\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{trace}(\mathrm{ad}\,w_{0})\theta_{0}\wedge\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}.

Hence
\mathrm{ad}\,w_{0}:\xi\to\xi
is traceless. Therefore, there exists a basis
v_{1},v_{2}
for
\xi
and real numbers
c_{10}^{2},c_{20}^{1}
such that

(32)

[w_{0},v_{1}]=c_{10}^{2}v_{2},\qquad[w_{0},v_{2}]=c_{20}^{1}v_{1}.

Setting
v_{0}=w_{0}
, the above equation and (
28
) conclude the proof.

∎

Before proving
Theorem

1.2
, we introduce some terminology. We call the couple
(G,\xi)
, where
G
is a Lie group and
\xi
a left-invariant contact structure, a contact group. We say that two contact groups
(G,\xi)
and
(G^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})
are isomorphic if there exists a group isomorphism
\varphi:G\to G^{\prime}
such that
\varphi_{*}\xi=\xi^{\prime}
.

3.1.
Proof of
Theorem

1.2

Proof.

Having already discussed the case of
Example

3.1
, we assume throughout the rest of the proof that
(G,\xi)
is not isomorphic to the contact group described there.

According to
Lemma

3.2
there exist a basis of left-invariant vector fields
\{v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}\}
, with
v_{1},v_{2}\in\xi
, and real numbers
c_{ij}^{k}
such that

(33)

[v_{1},v_{0}]=c_{01}^{2}v_{2},\qquad[v_{2},v_{0}]=c_{02}^{1}v_{1},\qquad[v_{1},v_{2}]=c_{21}^{1}v_{1}+c_{21}^{2}v_{2}-v_{0}.

Let
\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}
be the trivialization of

dual to
v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}
, i.e.,
\theta_{i}(v_{j})=\delta_{ij}

\eta=\theta_{0}\otimes\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}\otimes\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}\otimes\theta_{2}.

Note that
v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}

There exist a 2-dimensional subalgebra

\theta_{i}(v_{j})=\delta_{ij}

We set

\xi
and orthogonal to
\mathfrak{h}
. Moreover, since

.

If
c_{02}^{1}=c_{12}^{1}=0
, then the structural equations (

) of
\mathfrak{g}
reduces to

(38)

[v_{1},v_{0}]=c_{01}^{2}v_{2},\qquad[v_{2},v_{0}]=0,\qquad[v_{1},v_{2}]=c_{21}^{2}v_{2}-v_{0}.

We set
\mathfrak{h}=\mathrm{span}\{v_{2},v_{0}\}
,

\mathfrak{h}
is a sub-algebra and that
X
is tangent to
\xi
and orthogonal to
\mathfrak{h}
. Moreover, since
c_{1j}^{1}=0
for

2.5

iii
.

If
c_{01}^{2}=c_{02}^{1}=0

, we can choose

4.
Proof of the main result

In this section we prove the main result. We need one preliminary lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\xi
be a contact structure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If there exist global coordinates
(x,y,z)
such that the vector field
\partial_{z}
is tangent to
\xi
, then
\xi
is a tight contact structure.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be a 1-form defining
\xi
, so that
\xi=\ker\alpha
. Since
\partial_{z}
is tangent to
\xi
, we have
\alpha(\partial_{z})\equiv 0
, and therefore

(43)

\alpha=\alpha(\partial_{x})\,dx+\alpha(\partial_{y})\,dy.

Because
\alpha
is a contact form, it never vanishes. Hence, there exists a smooth function
f
such that

(44)

\alpha=\sqrt{\alpha(\partial_{x})^{2}+\alpha(\partial_{y})^{2}}\,(\cos f\,dx+\sin f\,dy).

The contact condition
\alpha\wedge d\alpha\neq 0
gives

(45)

\alpha\wedge d\alpha=-(\alpha(\partial_{x})^{2}+\alpha(\partial_{y})^{2})(\partial_{z}f)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\neq 0,

so
\partial_{z}f\neq 0
everywhere. This implies that the map

(46)

\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\qquad\phi(x,y,z)=(u,v,w):=(x,y,f(x,y,z))

is a diffeomorphism onto its image. By construction,

(47)

\phi_{*}\ker\alpha=\ker\{\cos w\,du+\sin w\,dv\}.

The contact structure
\ker\{\cos w\,du+\sin w\,dv\}
is the standard contact structure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which is known to be tight
[
25
]
. Therefore,
\xi
is tight as well.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

All left-invariant contact structures on three-dimensional Lie groups are tight.

Proof.

Since the property of being overtwisted lifts to the universal cover (see for instance
[
2
, Remark  2.6]
), we may assume without loss of generality that
G
is simply connected.

Assume
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)
. Since
G
is simply connected,
G\simeq\mathrm{SU}(2)
. The automorphism group of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
acts transitively on planes. Therefore, up to group isomorphism,
\mathrm{SU}(2)
carries a unique left-invariant plane field, which is well known to be a tight contact structure
[
25
]
.

Next, consider the case
\mathfrak{g}\not\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)
, and let

(48)

p:H_{1}\times H_{2}\times H_{3}\to G

be the diffeomorphism provided by Theorem
1.2
. Since
G
is simply connected, each subgroup
H_{i}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
. Choosing generators
v_{i}\in T_{e}H_{i}
, the maps

(49)

\mathbb{R}\to H_{i},\qquad t\mapsto\exp(tv_{i})

are diffeomorphisms. It follows that the map

(50)

p:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to G,\qquad p(x,y,z)=\exp(xv_{1})\exp(yv_{2})\exp(zv_{3})

is also a diffeomorphism. Observe that the vector field
\partial_{z}
is tangent to
\xi
. Indeed, let
X
denote the left-invariant extension of
v_{3}
. Since
X
is left invariant, its flow acts by right translations. In particular

(51)

p(x,y,z)=e^{zX}(\exp(xv_{1})\exp(yv_{2})),

which implies
p_{*}\partial_{z}=X\in\xi
. Applying Lemma
4.1
, we conclude that
\xi
is tight.
∎
