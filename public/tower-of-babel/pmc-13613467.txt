Tailoring Phase-Dependent Energy Storage in Nonstoichiometric Tungsten Oxide Nanostructures for Enhanced Supercapacitor Performance
Authors: Krishna KC, Santiago J. Dopico, Janak Paudel, Assem Basurrah, Noureen Siraj, Shawn E. Bourdo, Fumiya Watanabe, John Nichols
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The multivalent states of tungsten oxide offer multiple stoichiometric (WO3 and WO2) and nonstoichiometric WO x (2.72 ≤ x < 3) crystal structures. As x decreases in WO x , the reduced oxygen stoichiometry coincides with enhanced electronic conductivity and the emergence of structural channels within the unit cell. Despite these promising properties, most research has focused on WO3 due to the significant challenges in precisely synthesizing the various WO x phases. This study overcomes this limitation by employing a versatile resistive hot-wire oxidation (RHWO) method, enabling the precise engineering of tungsten-to-oxygen ratios and morphologies in WO x phases. Electrochemical characterization of these compounds indicates that energy storage performance is strongly influenced by crystal phase, morphology, and electrolyte cation size. In particular, clear performance distinction emerged between WO2.90 which shows enhanced capacitance (electric double-layer capacitance (EDLC)) due to fine nanostructures, whereas WO2.72 exhibits both capacitive and diffusion-controlled energy storage behavior, attributed to its larger open channels, higher carrier concentration, and larger concentration of W5+ and W4+ oxidation states. These findings highlight the correlation between crystal structure, morphology, redox behavior, and ionic transport in WO x , providing a foundation for advanced energy storage systems and an eco-friendly pathway for practical supercapacitor (SCs) applications.

Introduction

Societal demand to utilize portable/miniature electronics, electric vehicles, and artificial intelligence is leading to a perpetually increasing demand for new energy technologies. Despite much recent research effort committed to society evolving to exclusively utilize sustainable energy practices, viable solutions to the climate and energy crises require significant advancement in energy storage devices. − Although metal ion batteries are currently a popular solution to problems requiring large energy densities, the bulk diffusion-controlled chemical redox reaction intrinsic to their function result in relatively long charge/discharge cycles when compared to supercapacitors (SCs) along with relatively short lifetimes. In addition, current practices of the mining industry for extracting metals required for metal ion batteries commonly have negative environmental impacts, further compounding the sustainability of this technology. , Although SCs have a modest energy density compared to ion batteries, their rapid charge/discharge rates and extended lifetimes make them ideal solutions for applications requiring high power density that are inaccessible to traditional dielectric capacitors that have significantly lower energy density than their SC counterparts. − Potential applications for SCs include regenerative braking in vehicles with an electric power train, power supplies for miniature consumer electronics, and grid stabilization for wind and solar energy generation. −

While SCs hold significant promises for next-generation energy storage, their commercial viability remains constrained by limitations in electrode performance and scalability. , A common example for a SC electrode is activated carbon. However, despite being low cost and offering a large surface area, it exhibits low specific capacitance because its capacitive properties arise solely from electric double-layer capacitance (EDLC) , For this charge storage mechanism, an electric dipole layer forms at the electrode/electrolyte interface similar to the arrangement in a conventional capacitor, where only a limited amount of electrostatic charge accumulates at the electrode/electrolyte interface, limiting the specific capacitance. Another approach to overcome this fundamental limitation is by utilizing pseudocapacitive materials, such as conductive polymers. An advantage of these materials is their utilization of fast and reversible Faradaic (redox) reactions occurring at or near the electrode surface alongside the EDLC. Although conductive polymers commonly yield greater electrical performance than activated carbon electrodes, they commonly have poor mechanical and electrochemical stability. Thus, there is demand to develop novel robust electrode materials that promote efficient redox reactions for next-generation SCs. Tungsten oxides have emerged as highly promising SC electrode materials to overcome these limitations. This is due to their open crystalline frameworks and abundant redox active sites, which enables high theoretical capacitance through combined EDLC with pseudocapacitance charge storage, excellent electrochemical stability, dual-functional electrochromic energy storage systems, and scalable synthesis. − Furthermore, since conventional SC devices which utilize binders such as Nafion or PVDF commonly suffer from reduced performance due to dead mass and resistive loss, we aim to overcome these challenges by focusing on binder-free SCs. ,−

We have identified nonstoichiometric tungsten oxide phases, WO x (2.72 ≤ x < 3) as a viable alternative to WO3, for high-performance SC applications. ,, These compounds offer unique crystal structures, enhanced electronic conductivity, and superior ion-transport characteristics. ,− For instance, increasing the degree of nonstoichiometry (decreasing x) within these intrinsic n-type semiconductors not only boosts carrier concentration but also introduces multivalent tungsten cations (W5+ and W4+). This facilitates structural rearrangements, that create larger open channels that promote ion-intercalation and redox reaction, thereby promoting significant enhancement in pseudocapacitance performance. − Despite this promise, realizing the full potential of these WO x phases is hindered by significant synthesis challenges. The stabilization of specific, narrow-phase WO x compositions requires meticulous control over synthesis parameters due to them only occupies a small region of phase space which primarily limited them to fundamental research. , Furthermore, most research on tungsten oxide SCs has largely concentrated on acidic electrolytes, where the narrow potential window results in a hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) that compromises efficiency. , We propose that neutral electrolytes can mitigate HER overpotentials, expand the operational potential window, and enhance both stability and capacitance.

In this study, we have systematically investigated SC properties of different WO x phases synthesized through versatile resistive hot-wire oxidation (RHWO). Here, we report on the carefully curated compounds, WO2.98, WO2.90 (W20O58), and WO2.72 (W18O49) (see Figure ), which represent the diverse crystal structures and electronic properties within the tungsten oxide system, with WO2.90 being that to the best of our knowledge has yet to be investigated for potential SC applications. This research serves as an ideal platform for exploring the correlation among crystal structure, morphology, stoichiometry, and electrochemical performance in both acidic and neutral electrolyte environments. By conducting a comprehensive electrochemical evaluation of these WO x phases, we aim to offer critical insights into the charge storage mechanism of tungsten oxide electrodes and elucidate the fundamental relationship between material properties and electrochemical behavior.

Experiments

A low-cost resistive hot-wire oxidation (RHWO) technique was employed to synthesize three distinct nonstoichiometric tungsten oxide phases WO2.98, WO2.90, and WO2.72 each exhibiting unique morphology. This method has been described in detail elsewhere. The synthesis process is schematically illustrated in Figure . To synthesize WO2.98 on W substrate, the growth was carried out at a filament temperature T f = 750 °C under standard temperature and pressure (STP) for 75 s. Both WO2.90 and WO2.72 were synthesized by reducing WO2.98 in an annealing process. For WO2.90 the annealing conditions are a filament temperature of T f = 750 °C and a rough vacuum of air P air = 65 mTorr for 10 min while the conditions for WO2.72 are a filament temperature of T f = 850 °C and a nitrogen atmosphere of P N2 = 600 Torr for 40 min.

Material Characterization

The surface morphology was observed using a JEOL JSM-7000F scanning electron microscope (SEM). An X-ray diffractometer Rigaku MiniFlex 600 (Cu Kα radiation at λ = 0.154 nm) and a Horiba LabRam 800 Raman spectrometer (512 nm wavelength at 10 mW) were employed to identify its crystal structure and chemical composition. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out on a Thermo Scientific Kα, reference to the binding energy of carbon 1s (C–C peak at 284.80 eV), and XPS data is fitted with Thermo Scientific Avantage software; transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were captured with a JEOL2100F TEM at 200k accelerating voltage. The TEM samples were mounted in 2 separate ways, WO2.98 in the form of a powder and the other a direct mount (WO2.90//W and WO2.72//W). The powder form of WO2.98 was created on several wire samples and then sonicated in ethanol to remove the oxide layer from the wire. This slurry was then drop cast onto a ultrathin lacey carbon grid.

Electrochemical Characterization

The electrochemical performance of the synthesized nonstoichiometric tungsten oxide samples was evaluated using a standard three-electrode setup. In this setup, a 3 mm diameter polished glassy carbon electrode loaded with tungsten oxide served as the working electrode, platinum (Pt) as the counter electrode, and Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode. We used two different electrolytes: 1 M H2SO4 and 1 M Na2SO4. Prior to each experiment, approximately 20 mL of the electrolyte was purged with nitrogen (N2) gas for 30 min to eliminate dissolved oxygen and ensure an inert chemical environment. To prepare the electrode material, 20 mg of WO x was dispersed in 8 mL of ethanol, without adding any binders, then sonicated for 1 h. A 3 μL aliquot of this binder-free slurry was then drop cast onto a glassy carbon electrode, achieving an active mass loading of 7.5 μg. Note that a single synthesis would yield roughly 1 mg of material with a mass/area ratio of roughly 80 g/mm2 which required roughly 20 samples to provide enough material for each SC test. Samples were adhered firmly to the electrode surface, ensuring stable contact during electrochemical measurements. Data was analyzed using custom Python 3.9 scripts that utilized the packages SciPy (1.13.1) for integration and nonlinear curve fitting along with NumPy (3.0.0) for basic mathematical operations.

We performed cyclic voltammetry (CV) using a Keithley 2450 source meter to assess the material’s redox behavior and capacitive properties. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed using a custom-built potentiostat in conjunction with a Stanford Research Systems SR860 lock-in amplifier.

Results and Discussion

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was utilized to investigate the sample morphology on all of the samples. Representative images under various magnifications are shown in Figure . The low-magnification insets highlight the large-scale uniformity of all three samples. We observed that WO2.98 (Figure a) displays a globular morphology with an average particle diameter of 300 ± 40 nm. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was conducted for all samples to investigate the crystal structures, and the results are presented in Figure a. The XRD of WO2.98 (brown) shows diffraction peaks at 23.30, 23.60, and 24.47°, corresponding to the (002), (020), and (200) reflections consistent with the monoclinic WO3 structure, where the (002) reflection is dominant. Distinguishing WO2.98 from WO3 with small volume samples is quite formidable utilizing XRD alone because WO3 can accommodate loss of roughly 1% of its oxygen without undergoing significant changes in its crystal structure. Turning to XRD spectrum of WO2.90 (green) as shown in Figure a, it exhibits distinct reflections at approximately 22.80° (302̅), 40.60° (413), and 53.80° (615̅), suggesting a monoclinic crystal structure. , Similarly, XRD pattern of WO2.72 exhibits distinct reflections at 26.10° (104̅) and 30.50° (501) as shown in Figure a, suggesting the monoclinic WO2.72 phase. , Furthermore, both WO2.90 and WO2.72 exhibit a pronounced preferential orientation along the (0h0) plane, as evident from the spectral shift to (010) reflections as shown in Figure a. These results, corroborated by our SEM observations (discussed above), provide strong evidence for nanostructure growth along the b-axis.

Raman spectroscopy in conjunction with XRD is a powerful tool to investigate these crystal systems. As illustrated in Figure , in order to accommodate the significant reduction in oxygen both WO2.90 and WO2.72 undergo significant structural rearrangements, which are intrinsically linked to variations in the local bonding environments. Raman spectroscopy is uniquely suited to probe the local bonding environment intrinsic to WO x , providing a powerful avenue to differentiate between WO x phases with varying oxygen-to-tungsten (O:W) ratios. The RS are presented in Figure b where features in the spectral range of 200–400 cm–1 are due to bending modes while those in the range 500–900 cm–1 are due to stretching modes. , The spectra were normalized to its absolute maximum; however, the signal strength of WO2.98 was roughly an order of magnitude larger than that for the other samples, which is consistent with its high degree of crystal symmetry. The RS of WO2.98 closely resembles that of monoclinic WO3, with intense, distinct peaks indicating uniform bond lengths and angles. However, upon closer inspection, a broad, weak feature appears around 600 cm–1, which is not typically observed in WO3. The presence of this feature suggests the existence of weaker W–O bonds consistent with the presence of oxygen vacancies in WO3. In contrast, both WO2.90 and WO2.72 show remarkable changes in RS due to a distribution of bond lengths and angles, which is supported by a change in their crystal structure (see Figure ). The RS of WO2.90 (green, Figure b) consists of two stretching mode features at approximately 810 cm–1, and addition 880 cm–1 along with a bending mode feature at approximately 378 cm–1. We interpret the shift to Raman features to higher energy as an indication of shorter or more rigid W–O bonds. − These bonds are likely associated with the edge-sharing octahedral structure of WO2.90, which occur through the rearrangement of WO6 octahedral to accommodate a lower O:W ratio compared to WO3. Further reduction in oxygen content like WO2.72 phase results in a broad Raman feature in both W–O stretching and bending mode as shown in Figure b. This result and its interpretation are consistent with that of WO2.76. Furthermore, these results are characteristic of increased structural disorder and a broader distribution of W–O bond lengths, which is consistent with our above discussion.

To further explore the electronic properties of WO x , XPS valence band spectra of WO2.98, WO2.90, and WO2.72 were analyzed as presented in Figure . A distinct feature was consistently observed near the Fermi level (0 eV) across all samples. This feature is attributed to in-gap states arising from inherent oxygen nonstoichiometry, specifically the formation of multivalent tungsten sites (W5+ and W4+). − These features are negligible or absent in both the stoichiometric WO3 and the metallic W substrate, confirming its characteristic nature for the nonstoichiometric phases. This finding suggests that the intrinsic conductivity is inversely proportional to the carrier concentration with subsequently scales with the W:O ratio. Note that this result is in good agreement with previous theoretical studies.

Electrochemistry

To evaluate the supercapacitive performance of WO x electrodes, cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were performed in a 1 M H2SO4, and representative CV profiles acquired at a scan rate of 100 mV/s are presented in Figure a. The CV curve for WO2.98 (brown) is similar to that of WO3, , while the CV results for WO2.72 (blue) and WO2.90 (green) are distinct. Both WO2.90 and WO2.72 shows an enhanced capacitive response [integral area under the current density (j)-potential (V) curve] as compared to WO2.98 with maximum values of 553 and 384 F/g (at 1 mV/s), respectively. Despite the simple synthesis process utilized here, our results are comparable to those on similar systems despite their utilization of more sophisticated fabrication techniques and material design. ,− We observe distinct reduction (oxidation) features when scanning to negative (positive) potential. More specifically, the oxygen reduction features in WO2.90 and WO2.72 near −0.19 V, are attributed to reduction from W5+ to W4+, , while the corresponding oxidation features in WO2.90 and WO2.72, observed at −0.10 and −0.13 V, respectively, are attributed to oxidation from W5+ to W6+. , This result shows that WO2.90 features a smaller redox peak separation which is an indication of higher cyclic reversibility and faster redox kinetics. Notably, WO2.90 exhibits the highest response indicated by the largest CV curve area, followed by WO2.72, when compared to WO2.98. Since WO2.72 has the largest carrier concentration and crystalline channel, we found this surprising, however when considering the specific surface area (SSA) which is estimated utilizing morphological data obtained from SEM results discussed above (Figure ) to be 5 and 27 m2/g for WO2.72 and WO2.90, respectively, we conclude that the supercapacitive response of tungsten oxide is highly sensitive to sample morphology. Quantitative analysis was performed by estimating specific capacitance by integrating the area under the CV curves at various scan rates as shown in Figure c (see Supporting Information for detail). Here, WO2.90 exhibited the highest specific capacitance across all scan rates, followed by WO2.72, while WO2.98 showed the lowest capacitance values compared to WO2.90 and WO2.72, yet still comparable to stoichiometric WO3 from other studies. , Although consistent with our interpretation discussed above, this result is at odds with our initial expectation that WO2.72 would exhibit the highest capacitive response due to larger open channels and higher carrier concentration. , However, this is clearly not the case. This discrepancy suggests each material has different charge storage mechanisms, as evident by the distinct shape of their CV curves. The quasi-rectangular shape of WO2.90 suggests a dominant EDLC capacitive behavior, while WO2.72 exhibits mixed contributions from EDLC and pseudocapacitive behavior, and WO2.98 is likely dominated with diffusion-limited without significant redox features. ,

To further support these findings, Dunn’s method was used to quantify the surface (capacitive) and diffusion (intercalation) controlled contributions for all three samples (see Supporting Information for detail). , The results for the scan rate of 1 mV/s are summarized in Figure e. The analysis reveals that the kinetics of WO2.98 are diffusion-limited, with surface-controlled processes contributing less than 10%. In contrast, WO2.90 and WO2.72 exhibit significant hybrid behavior, with surface-controlled kinetics accounting for approximately 65 and 55% of the total capacitance, respectively. This aligns with the CV curve analysis, which indicates substantial contributions from both surface and diffusion process for WO2.90 and WO2.72. Notably, among the three samples, WO2.90 possesses the highest surface-controlled contribution (a trend that becomes more pronounced at faster scan rates see Supporting Information for detail), which is likely due to substantial EDLC contributions. This large EDLC performance is attributed to its fine nanostructure morphology, which provides an exceptionally high surface-area-to-volume ratio compared to the larger crystallite sizes of the other samples. While the surface behavior differs significantly between WO2.90 and WO2.72, the diffusion contributions for WO2.90 and WO2.72 converge at lower scan rates, implying efficient cation intercalation kinetics within the lattice. This bulk diffusion of cations remains the intrinsic rate-limiting process in WO2.72 nonsurface charge storage. For, WO2.72 the unique structural phenomena, specifically large open channels, appear to mitigate diffusion limitations by facilitating faster ion transport through the bulk material. , These results are further supported by scan rate-dependent current density variation among different samples, and a log­(ν) versus log­(j) analysis (see Supporting Information for details). , These findings suggest that in 1 M H2SO4, WO2.90 is the best electrode material for SC applications, exhibiting enhanced EDLC contributions, while WO2.72 benefits from a balanced hybrid storage mechanism.

To isolate and evaluate the intrinsic pseudocapacitive behavior, all samples were characterized in a neutral 1 M Na2SO4 electrolyte, where the EDLC effect is significantly suppressed compared to an H2SO4 medium. , A key distinction emerged among the samples. While the energy storage response of WO2.98 remains consistent across both electrolytes, both WO2.90 and WO2.72 displayed a roughly 2-fold reduction in specific capacitance within the neutral medium (Figure b), underscoring a strong dependence of the pseudocapacitive charge storage mechanism on the electrolyte environment and the contributions of pseudocapacitive charge storage. As presented in Figure f, Dunn’s analysis was utilized to provide further insights into the charge storage mechanism showing that the pseudocapacitive response for all three materials are dominated by diffusion-controlled charge storage. While WO2.98 displayed negligible contributions for surface-controlled storage mechanisms, that for WO2.90 and WO2.72 was found to be roughly 15 and 6%, respectively. Furthermore, WO2.72 exhibited a uniquely enhanced diffusion-controlled energy storage contribution, particularly at the slowest scan rates, attributed to a facilitated bulk intercalation process. These insights were further supported by detailed scan rate-dependent current density and a log­(ν) versus log­(j) analysis (see Supporting Information for details). , The enhanced energy storage properties of WO2.72 highlight its positions for next-generation energy storage devices that demands both high energy and power density. This performance is directly linked to the material’s larger intrinsic channels, which facilitate superior Na+ intercalation kinetics compared to WO2.90. To further evaluate the robustness of the electrode materials for SC applications, cyclic stability tests were conducted on representative WO2.90 and WO2.72 samples in both H2SO4 and Na2SO4 electrolytes.

Cycling stability tests spanning 1000 cycles at 50 mV/s were performed in both H2SO4 (Figure ) and Na2SO4 (Figure ) electrolytes. The virgin (final) CV curve is represented by a solid (dotted) line. In the acidic medium, WO2.72 (blue) exhibited substantial degradation attributed to a prominent HER, driven by its high carrier concentration and structural openness. Conversely, WO2.90 demonstrated superior stability, retaining over 80% of its initial capacitance after 1000 cycles without any substantial changes. The modest reduction in WO2.90 performance correlates with the observed suppression of surface-controlled redox features in subsequent CV cycles (Figure ). Contour plot analysis provides further insights into electrode robustness. While WO2.90 maintained a consistent current response throughout cycling, WO2.72 showed a sharp change in current response (dark blue) after 800 cycles, confirming performance instability likely due to the HER onset. Since the HER instability does not emerge until after 800 cycles suggest that it is due to gradual proton insertion into the lattice rather than surface chemistry. In the neutral Na2SO4 electrolyte, both materials demonstrated excellent long-term stability with nearly overlapping CV curves. WO2.72 showed a steady ∼20% increase in specific capacitance over 1000 cycles, consistent with electroactivation via the gradual wetting of internal channels intrinsic to its crystal structure, resulting in enhanced charge intercalation. Furthermore, the capacitive performance of WO2.90 nanostructures, which possess smaller internal channels, remained largely constant after an initial stabilization period, which is consistent with this interpretation.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to further investigate the charge storage kinetics of the synthesized WO x materials (Figure ). Since our other electrochemical results discussed above clearly indicate contributions from surface-controlled and redox/diffusive processes, this necessitates the careful selection of a two-time-constant model that adequately accommodates this fact. Thus, we identified the circuit model illustrated in the right panel of Figure . Within this model, R 0 represents the electrolyte impedance, R 1 and C 1 account for high-frequency surface-controlled processes, while R 2, C 2, and the Warburg element (A W) model the low-frequency diffusion and redox dynamics. Here, C 1 and C 2 were modeled as constant phase elements due to the intrinsic roughness of our nanostructured samples. More specifically, we interpret C 1 to represent surface contributions to be in great agreement with CV discussed above. Furthermore, since R 2 is tied to redox reactions, we expect it to scale inversely with carrier concentration, which is consistent with our XPS analysis.

Conclusions

This study systematically elucidates the structural, oxidation state, and electrochemical evolution of nonstoichiometric tungsten oxides (WO x ) synthesized by RHWO to investigate their viability for SC applications. The chemical composition of nonstoichiometric tungsten oxides can be finely tuned, accompanied by distinct morphological transformations from globular particles to nanorods. These results demonstrate how powerful RHWO synthesis is for new materials investigations and functional applications. This arises from the scalability, time efficiency, and environmental friendliness of the sample synthesis technique. Furthermore, electrochemical measurements demonstrate that WO2.90 exhibits the highest specific capacitance of 583 F/g, attributed to its morphology of finely bundled nanostructures. In contrast, WO2.72 shows a hybride energy storage mechanism and enhanced performance at slow scan rates, particularly in neutral electrolytes, owing to its open-channel structure and multivalent redox activity. Analysis of the EIS data further supports these findings, revealing low charge-transfer resistance and favorable ion diffusion in reduced phases. Overall, the results highlight the critical influence of oxygen stoichiometry, nanostructure, and electrolyte environment on the charge storage mechanisms of WO x , offering valuable insights into the rational design of advanced tungsten oxide-based energy storage systems. Moreover, these results show that the intrinsic properties of tungsten oxides make them highly promising electrode materials for energy storage across a wide pH range.
