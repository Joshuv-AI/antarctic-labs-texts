Hydraulic characteristics and water immersion damage energy evolution mechanism of fractured rock repaired by microbial-induced calcite precipitation (MICP)
Authors: Bolin Jiang, Juan Feng, Song Chen, Shanshan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Based on microbial-induced calcium carbonate precipitation (MICP) technology, this paper systematically studies the influence of cementing fluid concentration and microorganism-to-cementing fluid ratio on the compressive properties of MICP-repaired fractured rocks through laboratory experiments and microscopic tests. The research examines the strength attenuation and energy dissipation characteristics under different immersion times using stress–strain curves and an energy damage model at the optimal ratio. Key results demonstrate: (1) At 1 mol/L cementing liquid concentration and 1:1 bacterial-to-cementing liquid ratio, the repaired samples achieve 15.47 MPa unconfined compressive strength and 2.5 × 10−5 cm/s saturation permeability coefficient; (2) Calcium carbonate deposition exhibits “denser at top, sparser at bottom” vertical differentiation, homogenizing over time. ImageJ binarization shows 34.4% greater coverage at 28 days versus 3 days, evolving from discrete particles to continuous cementitious phase; (3) As the number of dry–wet cycles increases from 0 to 15, the peak total energy linearly attenuates from 64.8 to 23.4 kJ/m3, a decrease of 63.9%. The proportion of dissipated energy increases from 83.1 to 88.7% and the energy storage efficiency decreases by 83.7%; (4) Cycling elevates initial damage to 0.38, reduces damage threshold by 31% and accelerates energy dissipation across compaction-elasticity-plasticity-failure stages. Post 10 immersions, stabilized energy loss accompanies 38.3% strength reduction and 5.6% lower dissipation rate. These findings establish design thresholds and life assessment criteria for MICP-reinforced fractured rock in reservoir drawdown zones.

Introduction

The Three Gorges Project, as one of the world’s largest water conservancy projects, features a water-level-fluctuating zone in its reservoir area that represents a critical ecological and geological security concern1,2. Persistent reservoir water level fluctuations subject rock slopes to continuous dry–wet cycling. These slopes contain fractured rock masses with extensive fissures and weak fillings, leading to compromised slope stability3,4. External forces including water infiltration, latent erosion, hydraulic scouring and wave action exacerbate rock mass deterioration, progressively expanding bank collapse areas5,6. Chronic strength reduction in the drawdown zone has created significant stability threats to upper slopes7,8. This situation demands immediate implementation of systematic anti-seepage measures and fracture reinforcement for drawdown zone rock masses.

Rock mass reinforcement technologies can be divided into two primary approaches: physical reinforcement and grouting techniques9,10. Physical reinforcement methods typically include anchor bolt support and vegetative slope stabilization11,12. Grouting methods are further categorized based on material properties into cement grouting and chemical grouting13,14. Conventional rock reinforcement technologies offer several advantages in engineering applications. These include well-established technical maturity, controllable construction costs and the ability to quickly form high-strength support structures15,16. Recent studies demonstrate these benefits clearly. Sun17developed a cement-based composite slurry where the clay-to-fly ash ratio significantly influenced slurry strength. The optimal mixture achieved a setting time of just 11 h and reached 18 MPa compressive strength after 28 days of curing. Similarly, Han18employed resin pre-coating technology to seal rock surface microfractures, resulting in specimens with 35% higher peak load and 118% greater fracture energy consumption compared to untreated granite.

However, with increasing emphasis on green and low-carbon development concepts, the limitations of traditional reinforcement technologies have become more apparent19,20. While conventional fracture repair methods can enhance rock mass performance, their high pollution levels and poor degradability contradict current green development principles21,22. Chemical grouting materials such as epoxy resin, acrylate, phenolic resin and polyurethane—besides water glass—typically carry potential toxic contamination risks23,24. These pollutants can enter reservoir ecosystems through river input, surface runoff and dry/wet deposition, accumulating in sediments. This accumulation ultimately threatens aquatic organisms and nearby residents’ health and safety25,26. Consequently, developing new reinforcement technologies with green, low-carbon and environmentally friendly characteristics has become particularly urgent.

Microbial-induced carbonate precipitation (MICP) technology utilizes microbial metabolism to produce carbonate ions that react with environmental calcium ions, forming carbonate precipitates for in-situ rock fracture repair27,28. For instance, Q Jia reported that multiple grouting cycles of calcium carbonate deposition significantly reduced crack leakage while improving compactness29. Liu D et al. confirmed that calcium carbonate precipitation formation substantially enhanced mechanical properties in reinforced granite fractures30. Xiao Yao observed effective fracture surface sealing in repaired samples, showing decreased permeability and 26–40% increased shear stress31. Simiao Zhang documented faster early-stage development of peak shear strength in MICP-reinforced specimens, with slower late-stage growth as curing time increased32. Nain et al. measured 15% improvements in both compressive and tensile strength for microfractured rocks treated with MICP technology33.

The natural calcium ion concentration in rocks is typically too low to support microbial-induced calcium carbonate precipitation (MICP)34,35. Additionally, microbial metabolism requires sufficient urea as a substrate to produce adequate carbonate ions36,37. For example, MO. Cuthbert injected a cementation solution containing Pasteuria, calcium chloride and urea into rock fractures, yielding approximately 750 g of calcium carbonate and significantly improved performance37. To achieve optimal reinforcement, a bacterial suspension must be co-injected with a cementation solution containing calcium and urea into rock fractures. The cementation solution concentration directly influences the precipitation rate and total calcium carbonate yield, while the bacteria-to-solution ratio affects reaction efficiency. Studies suggest that MICP performs best at cementation solution concentrations below 1 mol/L and high bacterial densities36. However, current research lacks clear guidelines on the optimal cementation solution concentration or bacteria-to-solution ratio. This knowledge gap hinders the advancement of MICP for rock fracture reinforcement.

Moreover, in the water-level fluctuation zone of the Three Gorges Reservoir, restored rock masses undergo long-term dry–wet cycling, requiring further study on the evolution of their mechanical properties38,39. For example, Gowthaman observed that dry–wet cycles degrade restored rock and soil, inducing microcracks and fractures40. J. Yu reported a gradual decline in silty mudstone’s compressive strength under dry–wet cycling, particularly after the first two cycles41. Y. Xiao found that dry–wet cycles dissolve and detach cementing materials, causing sand particles to loosen and shifting particle contacts from surface-to-surface to point-to-point42. However, research remains limited on the strength degradation and energy damage evolution of MICP-repaired fractured rocks under cyclic wet-dry conditions.

This study systematically examines how cementation solution concentration and microorganism-to-solution ratio affect the mechanical properties, permeability, and calcium carbonate precipitation in MICP-repaired soft mudstone fractures, combining laboratory experiments with microscopic analysis. Furthermore, considering the unique hydrological fluctuations of the Three Gorges Reservoir, we evaluate the durability and strength degradation of the repaired samples under repeated wet-dry cycles. By integrating energy damage theory, the research reveals the fundamental energy dissipation and storage characteristics of MICP-treated fractures during environmental loading. These findings provide theoretical and technical support for applying MICP technology to reinforce rock masses in the water-level fluctuation zones of reservoir environments.

Materials and methods

Materials

The test specimens consisted of soft argillaceous rock (grayish-red) collected from the water-level fluctuation zone of the Three Gorges Reservoir (Fig. 1). We used Bacillus pasteurii (ATCC 11859), which was activated and cultured in Petri dishes under conditions specified in Table 1. Microbial concentration (OD600) was monitored every 2 h using UV–Vis spectrophotometry (Fig. 2). The growth curve exhibited lag, exponential and stationary phases. Cultures reaching OD600 = 1.2 were stored at 4 °C to prevent genetic drift during prolonged cultivation.

Preparation and pretreatment of fractured rocks

Rock specimens were prepared as 50 mm diameter × 100 mm height cylinders following ASTM standards for physical and mechanical rock testing. We created 2 mm wide fractures using Brazilian splitting tests. Specifically, each specimen was confined with transparent adhesive tape prior to loading to prevent sudden brittle fragmentation. A multi-stage loading strategy was implemented during the Brazilian splitting tests: an initial uniform rate of 0.5 MPa/s was applied and then successively reduced to 0.05 MPa/s and 0.025 MPa/s once the load reached 80% of the estimated peak strength. To ensure a consistent 2.0 mm initial aperture across all specimens for subsequent MICP treatment, high-precision stainless steel spacers were symmetrically inserted into the induced fractures.

This study employed a bacterial-cementation composite grouting system. Bacterial and cementation solutions were mixed at varying ratios, reacted for 5 min, then injected via syringe into fracture tops. Specimens received two daily grouting cycles, with each injection volume being 1.5 × the fracture volume. Post-grouting, samples were cured at 20 °C for 24 h to stabilize the cemented structure. The pretreatment study examined three key parameters: (1) Cementation solution concentration (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25 mol/L); (2) Bacterial-to-cementation solution ratio (1: 0.25, 1: 0.5, 1: 0.75, 1: 1, 1: 1.25); (3) Repair duration (3, 7, 14, 28 d).

In addition, to ensure statistical reliability, at least three effective replicates were tested for each experimental condition. The results are presented as the mean values of these independent measurements. To maintain data integrity, any individual value deviating from the group mean by more than 15% was identified as an outlier and replaced by conducting additional tests.

Method

The amount of calcium carbonate produced

Pretreated specimens were mechanically separated along fractures after removing surface encapsulation. The separated blocks (initial mass M1) underwent dynamic washing with 1M HCl until bubble cessation, followed by deionized water rinsing and drying (final mass M2). Calcium carbonate adsorption was calculated from the mass difference (ΔM = M2–M1).

UCS

Unconfined compressive strength tests were conducted at an axial strain rate of 2 mm/min to evaluate specimens with varying cementation solution concentrations, bacterial-to-cementation solution ratios and repair durations.

Flexural strength

Brazilian splitting tests assessed flexural strength at an axial strain rate of 0.5 mm/min across varying binder concentrations, bacterial-to-binder ratios and repair times.

Permeability coefficient

Permeability tests were conducted using a 1.0 M cementation solution (1:1 ratio) with a variable-head permeameter. The initial water head was set at 100 cm. After flow stabilization, we measured the time required for the water head to decrease sequentially to 80 cm, 60 cm and 40 cm. The distribution of calcium carbonate deposition

Calcium carbonate deposition patterns (1.0 M cementation solution, 1:1 ratio) were characterized at different curing ages using split fracture surfaces imaged with a high-resolution camera. Deposition areas were quantified via ImageJ analysis.

Dry–wet cycle test

The performance degradation of fractured rocks repaired by microbe-induced calcium carbonate precipitation (MICP) was evaluated through dry–wet cycle tests. The experiment used treatment groups with a cementing liquid concentration of 1.0 M, a dosage ratio of 1:1 and a curing age of 7 days.

The dry–wet cycle procedure consisted of immersing the 28-day cured samples in water for 24 h, followed by drying in a constant temperature oven at 60 °C for 24 h. The samples were then naturally cooled at 20°C for 1 h before re-immersion in water for 23 h, completing one full dry–wet cycle. Tests were performed after 0, 5, 10 and 15 cycles, measuring the unconfined compressive strength, flexural strength and permeability coefficient after each cycle to systematically evaluate the long-term performance stability of MICP-repaired fractured rocks under dry–wet cycling conditions.

Results and analysis

The amount of calcium carbonate produced

Figure 3 presents calcium carbonate production amounts under different cementing conditions. When the cementing solution concentration was 0.25 mol/L, the low concentration resulted in insufficient urea and calcium chloride, yielding minimal calcium carbonate precipitation. Increasing the cementing fluid concentration enhanced calcium carbonate production. At a fixed 1:1 dosage ratio, raising the concentration from 0.25 to 1.0 mol/L increased calcium carbonate production by 48.0%. Conversely, with a fixed 1:0.5 dosage ratio, the same concentration increase only improved production by 2.3%. The bacterial-to-cementing solution ratio demonstrated comparable effects. Using a 1:0.25 ratio (low cementing proportion) produced minimal amounts (0.5 g at 0.25 mol/L). When maintaining 1 mol/L concentration but increasing the ratio from 1:0.25 to 1:1, production rose substantially by 53.7%. Further ratio increases showed diminishing returns, with only 6.3% additional improvement. These findings establish distinct optimal parameters: a cementing solution concentration of 1 mol/L combined with a 1:1 dosage ratio achieves maximum calcium carbonate production.

UCS

Figure 5 presents the unconfined compressive strength of repaired rock fractures under varying cementation conditions. Increasing cementation fluid concentration consistently improved compressive strength. At fixed 1:0.75 ratio, concentration increases from 0.25 to 1.25 mol/L enhanced strength by 5.3%, 12.0%, 16.7% and 18.8%. The content ratio exhibited a more pronounced effect on compressive strength than cementation concentration. When the cementation concentration increased from 0.25 to 1.25 mol/L, the compressive strength improved by 2.18 MPa for a 1:0.25 ratio, 2.82 MPa for 1:0.5, 2.20 MPa for 1:0.75 and 3.2 MPa for 1:1. Figure 6 shows the unconfined compressive strength of repaired rock fractures at different curing ages using the optimal ratio. The compressive strength initially increased then stabilized with curing time. From 3 to 28 days, strength increased by 16.4%, 22.2% and 26.6%. The growth rate flattened after 7 days, matching the calcium carbonate production trend observed previously. The observed behavior occurs because early-stage urease activity promotes rapid calcium carbonate formation on bacterial surfaces, while subsequent crystal growth coats bacteria and limits calcium ion access. Simultaneously, calcium carbonate fills microcracks and pores to reduce porosity, but uneven product distribution eventually hinders further pore penetration. These combined mechanisms explain the rapid early strength increase followed by progressively slower gains.

Further analysis reveals that while the 1:1.25 ratio yields a marginally higher absolute strength, the rate of strength enhancement diminishes significantly once the ratio exceeds 1:1. This trend indicates a plateau in reinforcement efficiency beyond the 1:1 threshold. Consequently, considering both mineralization patterns and engineering economy, the 1 mol/L concentration with a 1:1 ratio is identified as the optimal combination for maximizing flexural strength gain.

Permeability coefficient

Figure 9 presents the permeability coefficient evolution at different curing ages under the optimal conditions. The coefficient decreased from 8.03 × 10⁻4 cm/s at 3 days to 1.37 × 10⁻4 cm/s at 7 days, representing a substantial reduction of 82.9%. Subsequently, the coefficient continued to decline to 6.48 × 10⁻5 cm/s at 14 days and 2.48 × 10⁻5 cm/s at 28 days. This trend characterizes a transition from an initial rapid reduction to a gradual stabilization phase as the curing age progressed.

The influence of curing time.

In addition, the observed behavior occurs through two distinct phases. During the early stage (0–7 days), high microbial activity combined with abundant calcium and urea availability drives rapid calcium carbonate precipitation, leading to effective fracture sealing and a sharp permeability reduction. In the later stage (7–28 days), declining microbial activity and progressive coating of bacterial surfaces by carbonate deposits gradually limit further precipitation, resulting in slower permeability reduction as the primary flow paths become blocked. This biphasic pattern explains the initial rapid decrease followed by stabilization of permeability coefficients.

The distribution of calcium carbonate deposition

Figure 10 illustrates the spatial evolution of calcium carbonate deposition under optimal conditions. The initial fractured sample surface showed no deposition, consistent with natural fracture characteristics. After 3 days, discrete small particles and flocs appeared in patchy distributions, loosely bonded to the substrate with visible interparticle gaps. By 7 days, particles aggregated into localized continuous layers while maintaining interparticle gaps, showing progressive spatial expansion. At 14 days, deposition coverage increased significantly though loose particles persisted near fracture bases. After 28 days, nearly complete surface coverage was achieved, forming a dense, continuous cementitious layer with uniform structure.

Fig. 10Distribution of calcium carbonate deposition at different curing time.

Distribution of calcium carbonate deposition at different curing time.

Further analysis indicates that calcium carbonate deposition in restored rocks exhibits distinct vertical spatial distribution patterns. Specifically, the deposition area in the upper fracture sections is significantly larger than in the lower sections, particularly during early curing. This disparity arises from two factors: First, during grouting, slurry flows downward, allowing better filling and adhesion in upper fractures. Initial calcium carbonate precipitation thus occurs preferentially where slurry contact is more thorough—typically the upper regions. Second, in early curing, slurry injected at the top seeps through internal rock pores. More slurry remains and penetrates the upper pore structures, enhancing contact and reaction with the rock matrix and promoting upper-region deposition. In contrast, gravity reduces slurry retention in lower sections, shortening contact time. Additionally, as slurry infiltrates downward, partial loss occurs along mid-section fissures, weakening initial coverage and pore penetration compared to upper regions. Consequently, deposition density and area are markedly lower in lower sections. Over time, residual slurry in lower fractures gradually participates in reactions, reducing upper-lower deposition differences and leading to more continuous, uniform calcium carbonate distribution.

The distribution area of calcium carbonate deposition at different curing ages was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software through the following standardized procedure: (1) Original grayscale images of fracture surfaces were duplicated and imported into ImageJ; (2) Image binarization was performed by manually adjusting the threshold value while using the software’s default method, with careful verification through side-by-side comparison between processed binary images and original grayscale references; (3) Through iterative visual inspection and calibration, the optimal threshold value was determined to be T = 36, ensuring accurate differentiation between deposition areas and substrate; (4) Final quantification was achieved using the “Analyze Particles” function in ImageJ to calculate the precise deposition coverage area. As presented in Fig. 11, the measured data demonstrate progressive expansion of calcium carbonate deposition with curing duration, showing area increases of 15.9%, 24.2% and 34.4% at sequential time intervals compared to baseline values. This quantitative evidence confirms the temporal evolution of deposition patterns observed in microscopic analyses.

Fig. 11The distribution area of calcium carbonate at different curing time.

The distribution area of calcium carbonate at different curing time.

To verify the objectivity and robustness of the image segmentation, a sensitivity analysis was conducted on the segmentation threshold (T). In addition to the optimal threshold (T = 36), two contrast thresholds, T = 34 and T = 38, were applied for image binarization. The results indicate that as the threshold fluctuates within a range of $\pm 2$, the calculated distribution areas of calcium carbonate exhibit minor variations compared to those at T = 36. Specifically, the variation rates of the coverage area across different thresholds were found to be within a narrow and acceptable range. Despite these slight numerical fluctuations, the relative growth trends of the coverage area across all specimen groups remained highly stable and consistent.

Flexural strength

Figure 14 presents the flexural strength of repaired rock under varying dry–wet cycle counts. The results show a decelerating degradation trend: flexural strength decreases rapidly initially, then more gradually with additional cycles, exhibiting reductions of 15.2%, 20.8% and 24.1% at successive cycle stages. The data demonstrate that dry–wet cycling primarily affects strength during early and intermediate stages, with diminishing impact in later cycles.

Permeability coefficient

Figure 14 presents the permeability coefficients of repaired rocks under different dry–wet cycle durations. The results reveal a characteristic trend of rapid initial increase followed by slower growth in permeability with increasing cycle numbers, with measured values of 1.37 × 10⁻4, 2.00 × 10⁻4, 3.28 × 10⁻4 and 3.68 × 10⁻4 for successive cycles. This behavior results from water infiltration-evaporation cycles inducing expansion–contraction deformation in the calcium carbonate cementation layer during early stages, generating new microcracks and reopening existing interfaces between calcium carbonate deposits and rock surfaces. These changes create extensive flow pathways, significantly enhancing permeability. As cycling continues, damage accumulation in the cementation layer approaches saturation, markedly reducing the formation rate of new flow channels. Subsequent cycles produce only minor enlargements of existing fractures and pores, leading to the observed stabilization in permeability growth rate (Figs. 15 and 16).

Compaction Stage: External work is primarily consumed by the compaction of the repair interface and internal micro-fractures within the matrix. Elastic Stage: While the total energy (U) increases linearly, its accumulation slope diminishes significantly by 68.7% after 15 cycles. Concurrently, the accumulation rate of elastic strain energy (Ue) drops substantially, indicating that dry–wet cycling weakens the structural integrity of the MICP cementation layer. From the perspective of energy conversion, this manifests as interfacial debonding between CaCO3 crystals and the rock matrix, shifting the specimen from a “dense energy-storage state” to a “loose energy-dissipating state” and significantly reducing energy storage efficiency.

Plastic Stage: As the stress approaches the peak, internal damage evolves rapidly, with the Ue proportion dropping from 86.6% (0 cycles) to 47.8% (15 cycles). The strain value corresponding to the energy distribution turning point advances from 8.4 to 7.6. Consistent with established energy-damage logic, this confirms that the “pre-damage effect” caused by dry–wet cycles accelerates the unstable dissipation of energy. Water penetration triggers non-uniform swelling of clay minerals, inducing tensile stresses at the MICP-cemented zones, which leads to an early transition into the damage acceleration phase dominated by plastic energy consumption.

Failure Stage: Post-peak, Ue is abruptly released and converted into kinetic energy and dissipation energy associated with rock fragmentation. The release rate of Ue at 15 cycles decreases by 13.3% compared to 0 cycles, while the proportion of dissipated energy increment grows to 88.7%. These “low-release, high-dissipation” characteristics indicate that after repeated dry–wet cycles, the originally dense cemented structure has transformed into a fragmented structure with weak interfacial adhesion, resulting in a severe attenuation of energy-bearing and transmission capacity.

Figure 18 displays the energy damage curves of repaired rocks under different dry–wet cycles, which clearly reveal four distinct damage evolution stages. In the initial compaction stage, dissipation energy increases gradually as primary fractures compress and close, with initial damage and micro-fracture development becoming more pronounced with increasing cycle numbers. During the elastic stage, dissipation energy grows linearly but slowly while elastic strain energy storage dominates, accompanied by continuous steady damage development. The damage acceleration stage features a nonlinear surge in dissipation energy coupled with accelerated crack propagation and exponential damage growth. Finally, in the failure and penetration stage, elastic strain energy drops sharply while dissipation energy releases explosively, ultimately leading to macroscopic fracture penetration.

The analysis demonstrates that dry–wet cycling significantly influences damage characteristics by increasing the initial damage level (Dm) and reducing damage thresholds, thereby accelerating energy dissipation across all deformation stages. Compared to uncycled samples, specimens subjected to dry–wet cycling exhibit progressive increases in initial Dm by 0.16, 0.31 and 0.38 respectively, while showing substantially reduced load-induced damage and earlier initiation of dissipated energy accumulation. When comparing samples at equivalent strain levels, the deformation behavior is jointly determined by both cycling history and loading conditions, with cycled specimens consistently displaying lower load-induced damage than their uncycled counterparts. Notably, dry–wet cycling markedly reduces the proportion of energy loss attributable to vertical loading, as evidenced by measurements at the elastic stage endpoint: initial Dm values progress through 0.17, 0.29, 0.41 and 0.46 with increasing cycles, while the corresponding load damage contributions decrease progressively from 100% to 79.1%, 32.6% and finally 21.9%.

Discussion

This study applied MICP technology to repair fractured rocks. Experimental results demonstrated improved mechanical properties and reduced permeability, aligning with previous findings. For example, Deng et al. reported that MICP-treated rocks exhibited a 36.41% reduction in porosity, a 94.62% increase in impermeability and a 30.52% increase in compressive strength47. Similarly, Ling et al. observed 47.02% and 34.68% increases in uniaxial compressive strength and indirect tensile strength, respectively48. However, the unconfined compressive strength (UCS) values obtained in this study appear higher than those reported in some literature. This is attributed to the 2 mm/min loading rate employed during the UCS testing of the repaired fractured rocks, which led to an upward shift in the absolute strength values. Nevertheless, the growth trends and mechanical behaviors revealed across all specimens in this study remain consistent with established findings in the literature.

The effectiveness of MICP technology in rock restoration depends on factors such as cementation fluid concentration, the bacteria-to-cementation fluid ratio and curing age. This study identifies the optimal MICP combination for fractured rock repair. Previous research supports these findings. For example, Bao et al. eported that MICP-treated tailings reached a compressive strength of 1.01 MPa after 10 days of curing, with negligible improvement beyond this period49. Similarly, Shi et al. found that higher bacteria-to-cementation fluid ratios significantly enhanced permeability reduction in restored rocks50.

At the same time, During the MICP repair process, the distribution of CaCO3 exhibits spatial non-homogeneity, specifically characterized by vertical variations along the fracture surface. This spatial distribution characteristic is highly consistent with the findings described in existing literature. For example, Cui et al51. reported that the CaCO3 filling rate generally decreases along the MICP injection direction. Despite these spatial differences in internal mineralization, the macroscopic failure of a repaired fracture is fundamentally governed by the ‘weakest link’ principle. Therefore, the Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) test provides a representative measure of the systemic strength of the ‘MICP-Rock’ complex. The ultimate bearing capacity of the specimen is restricted by the weakest part of the repaired interface.

Furthermore, this study comprehensively analyzes how dry–wet cycles significantly impact both the mechanical properties and permeability coefficient of MICP-restored rocks, while also modifying the evolution patterns of energy characteristics during load-induced damage processes. These findings are strongly supported by previous research: He et al. systematically demonstrated that dry–wet cycles cause progressive reductions in compressive strength and water resistance capacity of biorepaired specimens52. Concurrently, Che et al. conducted detailed investigations showing that as cycle numbers increase, rock specimens exhibit continuous performance degradation with corresponding increases in damage variables. Their research specifically documented that during load failure processes, elastic strain energy undergoes characteristic changes—initially increasing before decreasing—with clearly observable decreases in energy storage limits accompanied by progressively higher proportions of dissipated energy53.

It should be noted that the present study primarily focuses on the overall mechanical performance and damage evolution of MICP-repaired fractured rocks. The individual contributions of rock matrix degradation and MICP cementation failure were not fully decoupled as an intact (unfractured) control group was not included in the current experimental program. Acknowledging this limitation, future research will be directed toward a comparative analysis involving both intact and repaired specimens, specifically focusing on the differences in energy evolution and dissipation mechanisms between the rock matrix and the MICP interface under cyclic environmental loading.

Conclusion

Through experimental investigations including compressive strength tests, flexural strength tests, calcium carbonate content measurement and permeability coefficient tests, this study comprehensively analyzed the effects of cementing liquid concentration, bacterial liquid dosage ratio and curing age on the properties of repaired rocks. The long-term durability characteristics were specifically evaluated through dry–wet cycle testing. This research systematically elucidates three key aspects: (1) the strengthening mechanism of MICP technology for fractured rock repair, (2) the evolution patterns of repaired rock performance and (3) the energy damage characteristics during the repair process.
