The LoaD study: the association between different types of physical activity and the progression of knee osteoarthritis—a cohort study protocol
Authors: Joris W van der Voort, Lennard Voogt, Sanne Huisken, Edwin HG Oei, Ben van Oeveren, Joyce BJ van Meurs, Babette M Pluim, Sita MA Bierma-Zeinstra, Marienke van Middelkoop
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global prevalence of osteoarthritis (OA) is expected to rise as the population ages, leading to increased physical impairments that often reduce physical activity. Knee OA, in particular, is a leading cause of chronic pain and disability in people over 40. OA progression varies among individuals, with factors such as higher age and body mass index contributing to a more rapid progression. However, research suggests that engaging in physical activity such as hiking and running may help alleviate pain and slow OA progression. However, current literature on the relationship between physical activity and knee OA is largely retrospective, lacks comparative analyses across different physical activity types and rarely considers the intensity of physical activity. To address these gaps, the LoaD study was designed as a prospective cohort study aimed at examining how different physical activity types (ie, hiking, running, cycling and tennis) and their intensities relate to knee OA progression over 24 months in physically active individuals with early signs of the condition.Methods and analysisThis prospective cohort study will include 300 participants (75 per physical activity group), who will be followed for 24 months. Eligible participants are aged 45–65, meet the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) criteria for clinical knee OA and engage in a minimum weekly activity level for either running (60 min), cycling (120 min), playing tennis (60 min) or hiking (1 hike of 10 km). Baseline and follow-up assessments will include MRI, physical examination (eg, biometrics, lower extremity strength) and blood sampling for molecular biomarker assessment (eg, genetics, inflammatory biomarkers). Participants will receive monthly online questionnaires (eg, demographics, lifestyle, general health, history of knee injuries) and their physical activities will be tracked continuously by global positioning system data from wearable devices. The study’s primary outcomes focus on knee OA progression and symptom changes. These will be assessed through (1) structural progression of knee OA, measured using the MRI Osteoarthritis Knee Score (MOAKS) and (2) clinical symptom progression evaluated by (a) the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score sport and recreation function subscale and (b) Visual Analogue Scale for pain during an activity nominated by the participant to be the most aggravating for knee pain in the past month (VASNA, 0–10). Differences between groups on the primary outcomes will be analysed using mixed-effects models to determine the impact of activity type and intensity on knee OA progression.Ethics and disseminationThe study is approved by the Erasmus MC Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC) (MEC-2022-0420). The results of this study will be made publicly available through peer-reviewed international open-access scientific journals and national and international conferences. Participants will be informed about the results as soon as they are published.

Introduction

Background and rationale

With the global population ageing, the prevalence of osteoarthritis (OA) is expected to rise substantially in the coming decades.13 Globally, OA is one of the leading causes of chronic pain and disability in individuals over the age of 40.3 OA can affect multiple joints, including the hip, hand and spine, but is most prevalent in the knee joint.3 Approximately 60% of all OA cases are attributed to knee OA.4 The pathophysiology of knee OA is complex and multifactorial, leading to degenerative alterations in multiple joint tissues such as cartilage, subchondral bone, synovium, ligaments and meniscal structures.5 These structural changes are often accompanied by inflammatory processes, resulting in joint pain, impaired mobility and functioning, and a decline in quality of life (QoL).3

Knee OA is a progressive disease, both clinically, in terms of pain, symptoms, impairments and QoL, and structurally, in terms of tissue degeneration. However, the progression of knee OA varies among individuals.6 Identified factors associated with accelerated clinical and structural progression of knee OA include older age, higher body mass index (BMI), varus alignment of the knee, more severe baseline OA and specific features observed on MRI (eg, infrapatellar synovitis, joint effusion).7 8 Mechanical loading of the knee joint through daily physical activities is also thought to influence the progression of knee OA.9

Current literature suggests that being physically active may confer benefits for individuals with knee OA.1013 In the Netherlands, for people aged 45 and older, an age typical for the onset of knee OA, it is common to be physically active through hiking, running, cycling or playing tennis.4 14 These activities impose different types of mechanical loading on the knee joint.1517 Hiking and running have not been linked to the progression of knee OA and are generally considered safe.11 12 While a single running session can temporarily affect the structural integrity of knee cartilage, it is not considered harmful.18 However, the long-term effects of repetitive strain from years of running remain uncertain. Notably, both hiking and running have been associated with reduced knee symptoms in individuals with knee OA.10 13 However, these studies are retrospective in nature and, as such, are unable to establish a causal relationship between physical activity and the progression of knee OA. Moreover, these studies lack comparative analyses across different activity types and intensities. The impact of cycling and playing tennis on the progression of knee OA is uncertain, though some evidence suggests that cycling might be beneficial, as it has been linked to lower prevalence of knee pain and knee OA.19

Despite evidence suggesting that physical activity does not contribute to knee OA progression, a significant proportion of the general public still perceives running as harmful for individuals with knee OA.20 Furthermore, due to the limited understanding of the causal effects of physical activity on knee OA, clinical guidelines do not provide specific recommendations regarding the most appropriate types and intensities of physical activity for affected individuals.

The LoaD study was established to enhance understanding of the relationship between physical activity and both structural and clinical knee OA progression. This knowledge aims to equip healthcare professionals with evidence-based guidance on optimal knee loading, helping them to advise physically active individuals who start experiencing knee OA symptoms.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study is to investigate the association between different types of physical activities (ie, hiking, running, cycling and tennis) and the structural progression and change in clinical symptoms of knee OA over 24 months in physically active individuals with early symptoms of knee OA.

Secondary objectives are to:

Investigate the association between intensity (measured by time, distance, speed and/or type of training) of physical activities and the structural progression and change in clinical symptoms of knee OA over 24 months in physically active people with early symptoms of clinical knee OA.

Investigate the association between intensity (measured by time, distance, speed and/or type of training) of physical activities and the structural progression and change in clinical symptoms of knee OA over 24 months in physically active people with early symptoms of clinical knee OA.

Investigate the association between daily activity level and sedentary behaviour on the structural progression and change in clinical symptoms of knee OA over 24 months in physically active people with early symptoms of clinical knee OA.

Investigate the association between daily activity level and sedentary behaviour on the structural progression and change in clinical symptoms of knee OA over 24 months in physically active people with early symptoms of clinical knee OA.

Explore the interactions between proteomics, genomics, microbiome composition and known risk factors for knee OA progression with different types of physical activities (hiking, running, cycling and playing tennis) and their associations with the structural progression and change in clinical symptoms of knee OA over 24 months in physically active individuals with early symptoms of clinical knee OA.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study is part of the LoaD (Healthy Loading to combat osteoarthritis: Leveraging molecular variations in load bearing capacity for individualized movement aDvice) consortium, which aims to develop personalised movement recommendations for patients with knee OA. The study follows a prospective cohort design with a 24-month follow-up and will be conducted at Erasmus MC University Medical Center, Rotterdam, The Netherlands.

Recruitment and participation

Participants will be recruited from September 2023 until October 2025 through an open recruitment campaign in collaboration with Golazo Sports (Organisation of the Marathon Rotterdam and several other Dutch running events), the Royal Dutch Hiking Association (KWbN), the Royal Dutch Lawn Tennis Federation (KNLTB) and the Dutch Tour Cycling Union (NTFU). These partners will promote the study via their media channels, social networks and sports events, ensuring outreach to individuals engaged in the four types of physical activities assessed in the study. Newsletters and social media posts from the partners will inform potential participants about the study and direct them to the study website.

The website provides detailed information and includes a registration form for individuals interested in participation. For specific running and cycling events, participants can indicate their interest in taking part in knee OA research on their online registration form. Those who express interest will receive an email from the research team, inviting them to complete the official registration form on the website. The online registration form will collect contact details (name, email address), age, duration of knee pain, primary type of physical activity and weekly minutes spent on this physical activity.

If participants meet the inclusion criteria listed in Box 1, the research team will contact them by telephone to explain study details, verify eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Box 1) and determine the index knee (ie, most affected or most painful during the last month). If both knees are equally affected, the right knee will be the index knee. Eligible participants will receive an email confirmation, along with a participant information form containing further details and a participant informed consent form (see online supplemental A).

Measurements

Baseline measurements will include MRI, physical examination, blood and faecal sample collection and an online questionnaire. These measurements, minus the faecal sample collection, will be repeated at the 24-month follow-up. Throughout the study, participants will be asked to complete monthly online questionnaires and continuously log their physical activities using a smartphone or a dedicated sports tracking device. Additionally, participants will be asked to wear an activity monitor for 1 week during both the first and last 6 months of the study period. MRI

An MRI scan of the index knee will be performed using a 1.5 T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Altea, Siemens Healthineers AG, Erlangen, Germany) with a dedicated eight-channel knee coil. The MRI protocol comprises sagittal, axial and coronal fat-suppressed fast spin-echo proton density-weighted sequences with 3 mm slice thickness, and a three-dimensional sagittal T2 fat-saturated sequence. All MRIs will be scored by a blinded researcher (trained by an experienced musculoskeletal radiologist (EO) using the semiquantitative MRI Osteoarthritis Knee Score (MOAKS).21 The MOAKS evaluates the presence of articular cartilage loss in conjunction with surrounding bony and soft tissue abnormalities, including bone marrow lesions, osteophytes, lesions of the menisci, ligaments and tendons, joint effusion/synovitis and peri-articular features. Structural progression of knee OA will be expressed using previously proposed definitions for longitudinal evaluation of OA MRI features.22 All subregional change scores (1 for progression, −1 for improvement and 0 for no change) will be summed over the different MOAKS subregions into an overall measure of change per feature. The summed change scores per feature will consequently be dichotomised into progression vs no progression (change score ≥1 = progression, change score <1 = no progression). Additionally, progression will be separately explored in the patellofemoral and the tibiofemoral joint.

Physical examination

A physical examination will be performed by members of the research team who are trained in the physical examination by a physiotherapist. Table 1 provides a detailed overview of all physical measurements. Height (centimetre), weight (kilogram) and abdominal circumference (millimetre) at the midpoint between the iliac crest and the lowest rib are assessed. Blood pressure is measured using an oscillometric device (Omron HBP-1320, Omron Health Care Inc., Illinois, USA). Moreover, knee function is assessed for both knees including stance, presence of joint line tenderness and crepitus,23 range of motion of knee and hip, effusion using the bulge sign,24 signs of hand OA25 and an assessment of the ACR criteria for clinical knee OA.26 Pain pressure threshold is tested using a hand-held dynamometer (HHD) (Biometrics MicroFET 2, Almere, The Netherlands). Physical function outcomes will include the 30 s chair sit-to-stand test27 and measures of quadriceps, knee flexor and hip abductor strength using a belt-stabilised HHD.28 For all measurements, the index knee is tested first.

Blood values, DNA and proteomics

Venous blood samples will be collected. Assessment of HbA1c (mmol/L) will be done before the remaining samples are aliquoted and stored at −80°C. All analyses will be done in a batch after all materials have been collected. DNA will be isolated from whole blood and genotyped using the Illumina genetic screening array or recent equivalent. Stored serum and plasma will be used to assess CRP, HDL-C, triglyceride, glucose, creatinine (mmol/L) (to estimate kidney function (eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate))) and relevant proteins will be measured using the metabolism and inflammation panels using Olink technology (Olink Biosciences, Uppsala, Sweden).

Microbiome composition

Participants will be asked to collect a faecal sample at home no more than 2 days prior to baseline visit, together with a form containing questions on medication use, probiotic use, trips to foreign countries in the last month and a Bristol Stool Chart. Participants will receive instructions to store the sample at −20°C and a container to store the faecal sample frozen during transport. When collected by the research team at baseline visit, the sample is labelled and stored at −80°C. The intestinal microbiome composition will be determined using 16S sequencing and the relationship between microbiome composition and pain and inflammation will be analysed.29

Questionnaires during study period

The research team actively monitors the completion of questionnaires. Participants who fail to submit a questionnaire are contacted to ensure timely completion and address any issues.

Sports exposure

In order to collect objective information on sport exposure, hikers, runners and cyclists will be asked to track and share GPS data of their activities, recorded with a wearable device or mobile phone. This includes data recorded via smartphone applications (eg, Strava or Runkeeper) or dedicated sports tracking devices (eg, Garmin, Wahoo, Polar). Extracted data include duration of each physical activity session, distance covered, speed and additional sport-specific metrics (eg, cadence) if available. Data collection will be managed through an online research platform developed by Move-Metrics, a company specialised in data analysis for sport and health. Participants will be asked to synchronise their Strava or Garmin accounts with the platform via an application programming interface or to manually upload their GPS data in various formats (ie, .tcx, .fit, .json, .gpx) . Synchronisation enables an automatic upload of recorded activities. The Move-Metrics research platform also facilitates monitoring of GPS data uploads by the research team, who will contact participants if no data have been uploaded for more than 4 consecutive weeks.

Tennis players will be asked to track average weekly playing time combined with game type(s) (ie, free play, training, competition, singles or doubles) and to report this monthly through an additional online questionnaire.

Daily activity level and sedentary behaviour

The level and intensity of daily activities of every participant will be measured with an activity monitor (ActiGraph GT3X, Pensacola, Florida). Participants will be asked to wear the ActiGraph twice during the study period: 1 week during the first 6 months of participation and 1 week during the last 6 months of participation. Each monitoring period will last for 7 consecutive days, including 5 weekdays and 2 weekend days. The ActiGraph is based on accelerometry and will provide objective information about the total physical activity and energy expenditure.30 Participants will receive an activity monitor with instructions to wear the monitor on the right hip, except for when they sleep, shower or swim. Data are collected at a sample rate of 100 Hz. Data on sedentary behaviour, moderate to vigorous physical activity and step count are extracted with an epoch length of 1 s.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes are (1) structural progression of knee OA measured by change in MOAKS score22 and (2) change in clinical symptoms of knee OA assessed through function (sport and recreation function subscale of the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS)) and Visual Analogue Scale for pain during an activity nominated by the participant to be the most aggravating for knee pain in the past month (VASNA, 0-10).

Secondary outcomes are (1) changes in pain at rest and during activity on 0–100 NRS, changes in subscales of the KOOS on pain, other symptoms, activities of daily living and knee-related QoL, changes in knee symptoms through PASS question and signs of flares; (2) changes in self-reported health through the Short form-12 and (3) changes in psychosocial factors through Self Efficacy for Exercise and 5-level EuroQol-5D (EQ-5D-5L) version.

Sample size

The sample size calculation is based on the primary endpoint: structural OA progression over 24 months. Two previous studies on early knee OA populations with a 24-month follow-up reported progression on MOAKS features ranging from 23% up to 40%.31 32 The progression in runners with knee OA is hypothesised to be 40% compared with 20% in hikers. Using a χ2 test simulation with these progression outcomes per group, a power of 80%, an alpha of 5% and an estimated dropout rate of 8%,31 32 a sample of 75 subjects per group is sufficient to show a significant difference between groups (p≤0.05). Therefore, a minimum of 300 participants will be recruited.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means, SD and frequency distributions, will be calculated to describe the population characteristics and baseline values of the outcome measures.

To assess differences in structural progression of knee OA between groups, a logistic regression model will be used. For the changes in clinical symptoms of knee OA between groups, a linear mixed-effects model will be used. Exploratory post hoc analyses will be performed when a significant group effect is found. A Bonferroni correction will be used to adjust for multiple comparisons. Potential confounders—including at minimum sex, BMI, age and history of knee injury—as well as baseline characteristics that show significant between-group difference, will be systematically added to the regression models one by one. If a potential confounder alters the unadjusted estimate effect by more than 10%, it will be retained in the regression model. The potential confounding effects of engaging in multiple or specific types of physical activities, measured by the Short QUestionnaire to ASsess Health questionnaire, will also be examined.

The effects of physical activity features, such as duration and intensity, on structural progression and change in clinical symptoms will be explored using regression techniques.

Various regression techniques (eg, linear and logistic regressions, machine learning algorithms) will be applied to explore relationships between proteomics, genomics, microbiome composition and known risk factors for knee OA progression with different types of physical activities (hiking, running, cycling and playing tennis) on the primary outcomes.

All analyses will be performed in RStudio (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and p values ≤0.05 will be regarded as statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patient representatives are involved in the LoaD study through an advisory board. During study development, patients were consulted to evaluate the study burden for participants and the relevance of the outcome measures. During the entire study period, patient representatives from this advisory board will advise on study progression, dissemination content and strategies for patients and the general public.

Data collection and storage

All data will be managed in strict compliance with the General Data Protection Regulations. Participant data will be pseudonymised with a unique trial number. Personal participant details (eg, name, address, contact details) and the link with the trial number will be stored separately from the collected data and can only be accessed by members of the research team.

The data collected through questionnaires and physical examinations will be collected in a secured online database (Castor EDC). These data, together with the data from MRI, blood and faecal samples, and sports data collected by Move-Metrics and ActiGraph, will be exported to a secured local research database of Erasmus MC and can only be viewed by the research team. All collected data will be kept for 15 years after the last study measurement. Data will be made publicly available upon request.

Monitoring

Monitoring will be performed to check if all study procedures are in accordance with the study protocol. The study will be monitored using the Monitorpool of the Erasmus MC. Monitoring visits are performed after the inclusion of the first five participants, when all participants are included and after the last follow-up visit.

Ethics and dissemination

The study is approved by the Erasmus MC’s Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC) (MEC-2022-0420). The study will be conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (75th version, date: October 2024) and in accordance with the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO) and other guidelines, regulations and acts.

The results of this study will be made publicly available through peer-reviewed international open-access scientific journals and national and international conferences. Participants will be informed about the results as soon as they are published.

Discussion

As knee OA continues to emerge as a major barrier to healthy ageing, the precise impact of physical activity on the structural and clinical changes of knee OA remains unclear. Existing research is primarily retrospective, lacking comparative analyses across different types of physical activity and their effect on knee OA progression at an individual level. The main objective of this prospective cohort study is to investigate the association between different types of physical activity and the structural progression and change in clinical symptoms of knee OA in physically active individuals with early clinical signs of the condition.

Strengths

The LoaD study will be the first prospective cohort evaluating the influence of different types of physical activity on the clinical and structural progression of knee OA. The cohort will consist of a large sample size of 300 people, 75 per sport.

This study is part of the LoaD consortium. The LoaD consortium consists of multiple academic medical centres, technical universities, universities of applied sciences, medical and technical companies and a patient organisation aiming to provide a tailored advice on knee loading for individuals with knee OA. The LoaD consortium integrates research across multiple domains, including cellular responses to mechanical loading, biomechanics of physical activity and behavioural approaches to physical activity modification in individuals with knee OA. The multidisciplinary nature of this consortium ensures that observations in the clinical cohort study can be supported and explained by insights from basic biomedical and biomechanical research. This will enlarge our understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of knee OA progression, enhance the internal validity of clinical findings in the LoaD study and help to explain observations in other clinical settings. The outcomes of this multidisciplinary collaboration will contribute to the development of an e-health tool designed to provide personalised movement advice, helping individuals with knee OA optimise their knee loading and maintain an active lifestyle.

Limitations

To assess differences between study groups, participants are expected to remain within a single group (ie, hiking, running, cycling and tennis). However, activity changes may occur—for example, runners experiencing persistent symptoms may transition to hiking. If a switch of physical activity occurs, participants are encouraged to remain in the study and continue tracking their activities.

It is likely that there will be significant heterogeneity within groups in terms of age and BMI, which may influence both the structural progression of knee OA and the change in clinical symptoms.33 34 Therefore, the study will also explore differences in level of physical activity within each group. The study permits the use of different types of wearables and smartphones to register GPS data. While the overall accuracy of wearables and smartphones in tracking distance via GPS has been shown to be reliable, slight variation may occur between different devices.3537 However, this variance is minor relative to the scale of the collected data over a 24-month period and therefore not expected to result in bias.
