Entanglement cohomology for GHZ and W states
Christian Ferko, Keiichiro Furuya

Abstract
Entanglement cohomology assigns a graded cohomology ring to a multipartite pure state, providing homological invariants that are stable under local unitaries and characterize inequivalent patterns of entanglement. In this work we derive exact expressions for the dimensions of these cohomology groups in two canonical entanglement classes, generalized GHZ and W states on an arbitrary number of parties and local Hilbert space dimensions, thus proving conjectures of Mainiero:2019enr . Using the additional structure of the Hodge star and wedge product operations, we propose two new classes of local unitary invariants: the spectrum of the natural Laplacian acting on entanglement k k -forms, and the intersection numbers obtained from wedge products of representatives for cohomology classes. We present numerical experiments which investigate these invariants in particular states, suggesting that they may provide useful quantities for describing multipartite entanglement.

Introduction

Entanglement is among the most fundamental features which distinguish quantum mechanics from classical physics. It is also an intrinsically
obstruction-theoretic
phenomenon, in the following sense. We say that a pure state in a multipartite Hilbert space,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{n}}}\,,

(1)

is a
product state
if it can be written as a tensor product of states in each space
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{i}}}
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi_{{\underline{1}}}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\ket{\psi_{{\underline{n}}}}\,,\qquad\ket{\psi_{{\underline{i}}}}\in\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{i}}}\,,

(2)

and we say that
\ket{\psi}
is
entangled
otherwise. More generally, for mixed states, one says that a density matrix
\rho
represents a
separable
state if it can be written as a convex combination of product states, and
\rho
defines an entangled state otherwise; separable states exhibit classical correlations but no genuine quantum entanglement.
1
1

1

However we note that, for mixed states, separability does not rule out other forms of nonclassical correlations such as quantum discord. See, for instance,
OllivierZurek2001Discord
;
HendersonVedral2001Correlations
;
ModiEtAl2012RMPDiscord
;
DakicVedralBrukner2010Discord
.
Although we will focus on pure states in this work, we note that both of these definitions of entanglement are phrased in terms of what entangled states are
not
. Entanglement is precisely the obstruction from realizing a pure state as a product, or a mixed state as a separable state.

A natural mathematical framework for studying obstruction-theoretic questions is that of homological algebra. Perhaps the most familiar example is that of de Rham cohomology on a smooth manifold
M
. Consider the spaces
\Omega^{k}(M)
of differential forms on
M
, along with the exterior derivative operator
d
which maps
k
-forms to
(k+1)
-forms. Every closed differential form is locally exact, by the Poincaré lemma. The de Rham cohomology groups

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{dR}}^{k}(M)=\frac{\mathrm{ker}\left(d^{k}:\Omega^{k}(M)\to\Omega^{k+1}(M)\right)}{\mathrm{im}\left(d^{k-1}:\Omega^{k-1}(M)\to\Omega^{k}(M)\right)}

(3)

measure the obstruction from promoting a locally exact form to a globally exact form.

An even simpler example

V_{0},V_{1},V_{2}

\displaystyle MN=0\,.

(4)

If a vector

\displaystyle\mathrm{im}(N)\subseteq\mathrm{ker}(M)\,.

(5)

\displaystyle H=\frac{\ker\left(M:V_{1}\to V_{2}\right)}{\mathrm{im}\left(N:V_{0}\to V_{1}\right)}\,.

(6)

In some cases, one can think of

as “local data” (e.g. values attached to patches or vertices),

6

) but which cannot be obtained by gluing any global choice of local data (
v\notin\mathrm{im}(N)
). Thus even in this finite-dimensional linear algebra setting, cohomology can be interpreted as measuring the failure of locally consistent data to come from a single globally defined object.

Such cohomological questions – attempting to characterize when one is prevented from gluing local data together to form a consistent global object – have a very similar flavor to the problem of understanding quantum entanglement. Owing to this suggestive similarity, in recent years several works have applied cohomological techniques in order to describe entanglement in quantum mechanical systems. For instance, in
Hamilton:2023klg
, the authors proposed a framework for measuring entanglement using the machinery of persistent homology, which was later extended in
Banka:2025yds
. Another approach was explained in the recent work
Ikeda:2025mgj
, which employed a sheaf-theoretic strategy, organizing states and entanglement witnesses into a presheaf and characterizing obstructions to gluing local data using Čech classes.

In this work, we will build upon a third proposal that was originally introduced in
Mainiero:2019enr
, and which we refer to as
entanglement cohomology
.

The idea of this formalism, which will be reviewed in greater detail in Section

4

We describe a global pure state interchangeably as a ket
\ket{\psi}

\rho=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}

One defines spaces of “entanglement

\Omega^{k}(\rho)

\displaystyle d^{k}:\Omega^{k}(\rho)\to\Omega^{k+1}(\rho)\,.

(7)

As we will see, the spaces
\Omega^{k}(\rho)
are simply tuples of finite-dimensional matrices. Therefore, every such space
\Omega^{k}(\rho)
can be viewed as a finite-dimensional vector space, and the derivative operators

\displaystyle d^{k+1}d^{k}=0\,,

(8)

Despite the fact that elements of
\Omega^{k}(\rho)
are mere matrices – and not, for instance, differential forms on a manifold – it may come as a surprise that the structure of entanglement forms shares many properties with that of differential forms defined on a manifold with a Riemannian metric

\displaystyle\ast:\Omega^{k}(M)\to\Omega^{n-k}(M)

(9)

on an
n

k
-forms to entanglement
(n-k)
-forms, in the setting of pure states on
n
-partite Hilbert spaces. Likewise, one can define a wedge product of entanglement forms, and therefore a natural inner product which takes the schematic form
\langle\omega,\eta\rangle=\mathrm{tr}(\omega\wedge\ast\eta)
. Continuing to take inspiration from Hodge theory, we can define the operator
\delta
which is adjoint to
d
with respect to this inner product, and from there construct the Laplacian

\displaystyle\Delta=d\delta+\delta d\,,

(10)

which we will revisit in Section
4.1
.

This rich structure suggests that one might learn more about entanglement in finite-dimensional quantum systems by further exploiting this analogy, computing quantities that are motivated by geometrical considerations and studying their physical interpretations. Such quantities carry more information than the
Betti numbers
,

\displaystyle b_{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})=\mathrm{dim}\left(H^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})\right)\,,

(11)

associated with a pure state. The purpose of this work is to continue this exploration of the use of entanglement cohomology as a tool for characterizing multipartite entanglement, both by establishing new results about the dimensions (
11
) and by introducing new geometrically-inspired entanglement measures. More specifically, our goals are twofold:

(i)

We will rigorously prove conjectural closed-form expressions, put forward in
Mainiero:2019enr
based on substantial numerical evidence, for the dimensions (
11
) of the entanglement cohomology groups in two canonical classes of states, namely generalized GHZ and W states. While filling a gap in the literature, the proof technique that we develop in order to establish these results also reveals an interesting connection between entanglement cohomology and ordinary simplicial homology. This method might be used to prove results about Betti numbers for other classes of quantum states, and it serves to further highlight which characteristics of entanglement (e.g. GHZ versus W type entanglement patterns) are captured by the mere
dimensions
of cohomologies, to be contrasted with properties that can only be probed by computing other quantities.

(ii)

We will introduce two new classes of local unitary (LU) invariants, the spectrum of the entanglement Laplacian (
10
) and the collection of
intersection numbers
, that can be computed from any multipartite pure state by leveraging the geometrical character of entanglement
k
-forms. The first of these, the spectrum of
\Delta
, contains strictly more information than the Betti numbers
b_{k}
: the dimension of
H^{k}
is identical to the number of zero eigenvalues of
\Delta:\Omega^{k}\to\Omega^{k}
, while the non-zero eigenvalues furnish us with new invariants. Likewise, we will show that double intersection numbers contain information about Schmidt coefficients of the parent pure state across bipartitions – additional data which is not contained in the dimensions of the cohomologies themselves – while higher intersection numbers capture still more information about the multipartite entanglement structure. We will also present the results of numerical experiments which investigate these new LU invariants in certain classes of states.

The study of entanglement cohomology is still quite young, and many basic questions remain to be explored. It is our hope that both sets of results
(i)
and
(ii)
will spur further progress in this area, leading to the development of additional mathematical tools that might eventually allow us to chart the space of possible patterns of entanglement in finite-dimensional quantum systems and thus better understand quantum information.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we review the aspects of entanglement cohomology which are relevant for later parts of this work. Section
3
presents the proofs of two conjectures of
Mainiero:2019enr
proposing exact, closed-form expressions for the dimensions of entanglement cohomologies of generalized GHZ and W states. In Section
4
, we define novel, geometrically-inspired local unitary invariants that can be constructed from the entanglement
k
-forms associated with a generic pure state, and study the structure of these new quantities in several examples. Section
5
summarizes our conclusions and describes a few areas for future research. We have relegated some standard material concerning simplicial homology to Appendix
A
for the benefit of the unfamiliar reader.

2
Generalities on Entanglement Cohomology

In order to make the present work self-contained, and to fix our conventions, we will begin by reviewing the formalism of entanglement cohomology. Although this subject was first introduced in
Mainiero:2019enr
, we will use notation which more closely follows that of
Ferko:2024swt
.

We will always work in a finite tensor product of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}=\bigotimes_{{\underline{i}}={\underline{1}}}^{{\underline{n}}}\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{i}}}=\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{n}}}\,.

(12)

Indices which label one of the
n
subsystems of the total Hilbert space will be decorated with an underline, to distinguish them from other integer labels that may appear. For instance, if
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}}
is
2
-dimensional, we might write basis states as
\ket{0_{{\underline{1}}}}
and
\ket{1_{{\underline{1}}}}
, where the non-underlined integers refer to the states in the computational basis. When no confusion is possible, we will omit subsystem labels and simply write
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
for these basis states.

When it is necessary to speak of the dimensions of the tensor product factors
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{i}}}
, we will denote the local dimensions by
d_{{\underline{i}}}=\dim\left(\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{i}}}\right)
, so that the total dimension is

\displaystyle\dim\left(\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\right)=\prod_{{\underline{i}}={\underline{1}}}^{{\underline{n}}}d_{{\underline{i}}}\,.

(13)

The starting point for defining the entanglement cochain complex is to select a pure state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}}\in\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\,,

(14)

to which we can associate the density matrix

\displaystyle\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}=\ket{\psi_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}}\bra{\psi_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}}\,.

(15)

Although it is also possible to directly study entanglement cohomology for mixed states, the resulting structure does not enjoy the same mathematical properties as for pure states. For instance, there is no well-defined Hodge star operation acting on mixed state entanglement
k
-forms, which precludes us from building the entanglement Laplacian to be introduced in Section
4.1
. For this reason, we will take the view that mixed states should always be studied by first purifying and then computing invariants using the entanglement cohomology of the parent pure states. We therefore focus entirely on pure states in what follows.

Given a parent density matrix
\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}
as in (
15
), its various reduced density matrices – obtained by tracing out some number of tensor product factors – will be denoted with the same symbol
\rho
but a different number of underlined indices. For instance,

\displaystyle\rho_{{\underline{2}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}=\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{1}}}\left(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\right)\,,\qquad\rho_{{\underline{3}}}=\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{1}}{\underline{2}}{\underline{4}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\left(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\right)\,,

(16)

and so on. Sometimes we will use a hat to indicate that a label has been omitted, as in

\displaystyle\rho_{{\underline{1}}\widehat{{\underline{2}}}{\underline{3}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}=\rho_{{\underline{1}}{\underline{3}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}=\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{2}}}\left(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\right)\,.

(17)

Here the partial trace is defined in the usual way. Given any linear operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}:V\otimes W\to V\otimes W

(18)

acting on a tensor product of vector spaces
V
and
W
, where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
is a basis for
V
and
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
is a basis for
W
, let
\mathcal{O}_{k\ell,ij}
be the matrix elements of this operator with respect to the basis
\{e_{k}\otimes f_{\ell}\}
, where
1\leq k,i\leq m
and
1\leq\ell,j\leq n
. The partial trace
\mathrm{tr}_{W}\left(\mathcal{O}\right):V\to V
is the operator whose matrix elements with respect to the basis
\{e_{k}\}
are

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{tr}_{W}\left(\mathcal{O}\right)\right)_{k,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{kj,ij}\,.

(19)

In addition to the lowercase underlined Latin indices that denote subsystems, we will use capital underlined Latin letters for multi-indices (collections of
k
subsystem indices) like

\displaystyle{\underline{I}}=\left({\underline{i}}_{1},\ldots,{\underline{i}}_{k}\right)\,,

(20)

which we always assume have been arranged in increasing order,

\displaystyle{\underline{i}}_{1}<\ldots<{\underline{i}}_{k}\,.

(21)

Given such a multi-index
{\underline{I}}
, we write
{\underline{I}}^{C}
for its complement in the full list of subsystem indices
{\underline{1}},\ldots,{\underline{n}}
, again taken to be sorted in increasing order.

There is a space of linear operators
\mathrm{End}\left(\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}\right)
acting on each reduced Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}
, for each length-
k

{\underline{I}}
and for each
k=1,\ldots,n
, and an associated reduced density matrix
\rho_{{\underline{I}}}
. We write
s_{\rho_{{\underline{I}}}}
, or simply
s_{{\underline{I}}}
for short, to denote the projection operator onto the image of this reduced density matrix (i.e. the span of its nonzero eigenvalues):

\displaystyle s_{{\underline{I}}}=\mathrm{proj}_{\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}})}\,.

(22)

Because the parent state is pure,
s_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}
will generically be a rank-one projector, but the other support projections
s_{{\underline{I}}}
may have different ranks.

Given an operator
\mathcal{O}:\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}\to\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}
, we denote its restriction to the image of the associated reduced density matrix with a vertical bar:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\big|_{\rho_{{\underline{I}}}}=s_{{\underline{I}}}\mathcal{O}s_{{\underline{I}}}\,.

(23)

Said differently, the bar notation indicates that the endomorphism
\mathcal{O}:\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}\to\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}
has been restricted to an operator
\mathcal{O}\big|_{\rho_{{\underline{I}}}}:\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}})\to\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}})
.

The key objects of our attention are
entanglement forms
which have a structure similar to that of differential forms on a manifold. There is one such space of entanglement forms
\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})
for each integer
k=0,\ldots,n
. The
0
-forms are simply complex numbers,

\displaystyle\Omega^{0}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})=\mathbb{C}\,,

(24)

and the top forms are just linear operators acting on the image of
\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}
,

\displaystyle\Omega^{n}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})=\mathrm{End}\left(\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})\right)\cong\mathbb{C}\,,

(25)

which is also one-dimensional because the parent state is pure.

For other values
0<k<n
, an entanglement form is a tuple of operators acting on images of reduced density matrices:

\displaystyle\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})=\bigtimes_{|{\underline{I}}|=k}\mathrm{End}\left(\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}})\right)\,,

(26)

where the Cartesian product
\bigtimes
runs over all increasing length-
k
multi-indices
{\underline{I}}=({\underline{i}}_{1},\ldots,{\underline{i}}_{k})
.

As a simple illustration, let us consider the case of a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. When studying Hilbert spaces with a small number of factors, such as two or three, we will sometimes use non-underlined capital early Latin letters (
A
,
B
,
C
) to label subsystems, rather than numerical labels
{\underline{i}}
. For a pure state
\ket{\psi_{AB}}
with associated density matrix
\rho_{AB}
, the entanglement one-forms
\alpha_{1}\in\Omega^{1}(\rho_{AB})
are
2
-tuples of the form

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\left(\mathcal{O}_{A},\mathcal{O}_{B}\right)\,,

(27)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{A}:\mathrm{im}(\rho_{A})\to\mathrm{im}(\rho_{A})\,,\qquad\mathcal{O}_{B}:\mathrm{im}(\rho_{B})\to\mathrm{im}(\rho_{B})\,.

(28)

For a tripartite system
\mathcal{H}_{ABC}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{C}
, entanglement
1
-forms are tuples

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{O}_{A},\mathcal{O}_{B},\mathcal{O}_{C}\right)\,,

(29)

with three elements, where again

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{A}:\mathrm{im}(\rho_{A})\to\mathrm{im}(\rho_{A})\,,\quad\mathcal{O}_{B}:\mathrm{im}(\rho_{B})\to\mathrm{im}(\rho_{B})\,,\quad\mathcal{O}_{C}:\mathrm{im}(\rho_{C})\to\mathrm{im}(\rho_{C})\,,

(30)

and
2
-forms are length-
3
tuples of operators acting on bipartite Hilbert spaces,

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{O}_{AB},\mathcal{O}_{AC},\mathcal{O}_{BC}\right)\,,

(31)

where now

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{AB}:\mathrm{im}(\rho_{AB})\to\mathrm{im}(\rho_{AB})\,,\quad\mathcal{O}_{AC}:\mathrm{im}(\rho_{AC})\to\mathrm{im}(\rho_{AC})\,,\quad\mathcal{O}_{BC}:\mathrm{im}(\rho_{BC})\to\mathrm{im}(\rho_{BC})\,,

(32)

and so on. In general,
\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})
is a tuple of operators of length
{n\choose k}
.

In order to define the
differential
, or
coboundary
operator, acting on entanglement
k
-forms, it will be necessary to introduce some notation to address ordering of tensor product factors. Let
{\underline{I}}=({\underline{i}}_{1},\ldots,{\underline{i}}_{k})
be a multi-index which is a strict subset of
{\underline{J}}=({\underline{j}}_{1},\ldots,{\underline{j}}_{m})
. There is a canonical identification of Hilbert spaces,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{J}}}\cong\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{J}}\setminus{\underline{I}}}\,,

(33)

which simply re-shuffles the tensor product factors on the right side of (
33
) to arrange them in increasing order. For instance, if
{\underline{I}}=({\underline{1}},{\underline{3}})
and
{\underline{J}}=({\underline{1}},{\underline{2}},{\underline{3}})
, then the isomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{J}}}=\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{2}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{3}}}\cong\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{3}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{2}}}=\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{J}}\setminus{\underline{I}}}\,,

(34)

permutes subsystems
{\underline{2}}
and
{\underline{3}}
.

We denote the re-shuffling operation which implements the identification (
33
) by

\displaystyle\Pi_{{\underline{I}},{\underline{J}}}:\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{J}}}\to\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{J}}\setminus{\underline{I}}}\,.

(35)

There is a natural inclusion of
\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}}))
into
\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{J}}}))
, where again
{\underline{I}}\subset{\underline{J}}
, which simply “tensors with the identity on missing sites” belonging to
{\underline{J}}\setminus{\underline{I}}
. Let
X\in\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}}))
. We define a corresponding linear operator on
\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{J}}})
by

\displaystyle\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(X)=s_{{\underline{J}}}\Pi_{{\underline{I}},{\underline{J}}}^{-1}\left(X\otimes\mathbb{I}_{{\underline{J}}\setminus{\underline{I}}}\right)\Pi_{{\underline{I}},{\underline{J}}}s_{{\underline{J}}}\,.

(36)

Here we write
\mathbb{I}_{{\underline{J}}\setminus{\underline{I}}}
for the tensor product of identity operators on all subsystems in
{\underline{J}}\setminus{\underline{I}}
. In words, the inclusion (
36
) first re-shuffles tensor product factors to put all sites in
{\underline{J}}\setminus{\underline{I}}
“at the end”, then tensors
X
with identity operators on each of these missing sites, and finally re-shuffles tensor product factors to return them to increasing order.

An example might make the construction (
36
) more transparent. Consider a tripartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{ABC}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{C}
. First suppose that
{\underline{I}}=(A,B)
and
{\underline{J}}=(A,B,C)
, and let
X=\mathcal{O}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}
be a linear operator on
\mathrm{im}(\rho_{AB})
. In this case, the inclusion (
36
) is trivial: one simply has

\displaystyle\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(X)=\mathcal{O}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C}\big|_{\rho_{ABC}}\,,

(37)

which merely tensors with the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}_{C}
.

However, now suppose that
{\underline{I}}=(A,C)
and
{\underline{J}}=(A,B,C)
with
X=\mathcal{O}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}
. Unfortunately, the tensor product

\displaystyle X\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}=\mathcal{O}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}

(38)

is a linear operator on
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{C}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, which has the local Hilbert spaces in the “wrong order”. The role of the re-shuffling operators
\Pi_{{\underline{I}},{\underline{J}}}
and
\Pi_{{\underline{I}},{\underline{J}}}^{-1}
in (
36
) is to interchange subsystems
C
and
B
and place the subsystems in the correct order:

\displaystyle\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(X)=\mathcal{O}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}\big|_{\rho_{ABC}}\,.

(39)

Similarly, for a linear operator
X\in\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{im}(\rho_{AC}))
which does not take the factorized form
\mathcal{O}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}
but is rather a linear combination of such operators, the action of
\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(X)
extends (
39
) by linearity.

We are now prepared to define the coboundary operator. Let
\omega\in\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})
, so that
\omega=(\omega_{{\underline{I}}})
where
{\underline{I}}
runs over all increasing multi-indices of length

. For a multi-index
{\underline{J}}\subset\{{\underline{1}},\ldots,{\underline{n}}\}

, again with elements listed in increasing order

\displaystyle{\underline{J}}=\{{\underline{j}}_{0}<\ldots<{\underline{j}}_{k}\}\,,

\displaystyle\left(d^{k}\omega\right)_{{\underline{J}}}=k\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{\ell}\,\iota_{{\underline{J}}\setminus\{{\underline{j}}_{\ell}\}}^{{\underline{J}}}(\omega_{{\underline{J}}\setminus\{{\underline{j}}_{\ell}\}})\,.

(41)

Thus the object

is a tuple of linear operators acting on images of reduced density matrices on
(k+1)

\omega
that have been “padded with the identity” on one missing site, each dressed with a sign

which is correlated with the ordering of the missing site.

in (
41
), which is unconventional in simplicial cohomology, is introduced for later convenience; in particular, it will ensure that the coboundary satisfies the Leibniz rule with respect to the wedge product to be introduced shortly. However, since this factor is invertible, it does not affect the kernels or images of
d^{k}
, and thus does not change its cohomology.

Some examples are again in order. For a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, and an entanglement
1
-form

\displaystyle\omega=(\mathcal{O}_{A},\mathcal{O}_{B})\,,

(42)

the coboundary operator acts as

\displaystyle d^{1}\omega=s_{AB}\left(\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}-\mathcal{O}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\right)s_{AB}\,.

(43)

In the case of a tripartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{ABC}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{C}
, an entanglement
1
-form

\displaystyle\omega^{(1)}=(\mathcal{O}_{A},\mathcal{O}_{B},\mathcal{O}_{C})\,,

(44)

is similarly acted upon by the differential as

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0ptd^{1}\omega^{(1)}=\left(\left(\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}-\mathcal{O}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\right)\big|_{\rho_{AB}},\left(\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}-\mathcal{O}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C}\right)\big|_{\rho_{AC}},\left(\mathbb{I}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}-\mathcal{O}_{B}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C}\right)\big|_{\rho_{BC}}\right)\,.

(45)

For an entanglement
2
-form

\displaystyle\omega^{(2)}=(\mathcal{O}_{AB},\mathcal{O}_{AC},\mathcal{O}_{BC})\,,

(46)

the expression for
d^{2}\omega^{(2)}
requires the action of the permutation operator in order to arrange the tensor product factors in increasing order,

\displaystyle d^{2}\omega^{(2)}=2\left(\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{BC}-\Pi_{AC,ABC}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{AC}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\right)\Pi_{AC,ABC}+\mathcal{O}_{AB}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C}\right)\big|_{\rho_{ABC}}\,,

(47)

where
\Pi_{AC,ABC}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{O}_{AC}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\right)\Pi_{AC,ABC}
produces a linear operator acting on
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{C}
.

We will sometimes suppress the index on the coboundary operator, writing
d
instead of
d^{k}
. As in the setting of ordinary differential forms on manifolds, we say that an entanglement
k
-form
\omega
is
closed
if
d\omega=0
, and we say that
\omega
is
exact
if
\omega=d\eta
for some entanglement
(k-1)
-form
\eta
. It was shown in
Mainiero:2019enr
that these differentials obey

\displaystyle d^{k+1}\circ d^{k}=0

(48)

for all
k
, due to a property of the projectors
s_{{\underline{I}}}
called
compatibility of supports
. One may therefore define the associated cohomology groups

\displaystyle H^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})=\frac{\ker(d^{k})}{\mathrm{im}(d^{k-1})}\,.

(49)

The dimensions of these cohomology groups, viewed as complex vector spaces, are denoted

\displaystyle b_{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})=\dim\left(H^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})\right)\,,

(50)

and called
Betti numbers
.

The generating function for these dimensions is referred to as the
Poincaré polynomial
,

\displaystyle P(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}};y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}b_{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})y^{k-1}\,.

(51)

We occasionally express the Betti numbers and Poincaré polynomials as functions of the pure state which defines the density matrix, writing
P(\ket{\psi_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}};y)
rather than
P(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}};y)
.

Mathematically, the definition (
41
) can be viewed as the usual simplicial coboundary on the
(n-1)
-simplex with vertex set
\{{\underline{1}},\ldots,{\underline{n}}\}
, but with values in a certain coefficient diagram. Let
\mathcal{C}
be the category whose objects are subsets
{\underline{I}}\subset\{{\underline{1}},\ldots,{\underline{n}}\}
, i.e. faces of the
(n-1)
-simplex
\Upsilon^{n-1}
, and whose morphisms are inclusions
{\underline{I}}\hookrightarrow{\underline{J}}
. Define a functor

\displaystyle F_{\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbf{Vect}\,,

(52)

where
\mathbf{Vect}
is the category of vector spaces, which acts on objects as

\displaystyle F_{\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}}({\underline{I}})=\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}}))\,,

(53)

and on morphisms as

\displaystyle F_{\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}}({\underline{I}}\hookrightarrow{\underline{J}})=\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}\,.

(54)

Then one can interpret the spaces of entanglement
k
-forms for
1\leq k\leq n
as

\displaystyle\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})\cong C^{k-1}(\Upsilon^{n-1};F_{\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}})\,,

(55)

where
C^{k-1}
denotes the usual space of
(k-1)
-cochains on the
(n-1)
-simplex. Of course, the
(n-1)
-simplex is contractible, so the cohomology groups of
\Upsilon^{n-1}
with
constant
coefficients are trivial in positive degree, but this is no longer true for cohomology with coefficients that take values in arbitrary diagrams. Nonetheless, there is a close connection between entanglement cohomology and the usual (co)homology of the
(n-1)
-simplex, which we will exploit in Section
3.1
to prove a lemma which is useful for computing the entanglement cohomologies of generalized GHZ and W states. A review of standard material about simplicial homology and the
(n-1)
-simplex
\Upsilon^{n-1}
can be found in Appendix
A
.

We assemble the spaces of entanglement
k
-forms into a cochain complex,

\displaystyle 0\to\mathbb{C}\xrightarrow{d^{0}}\Omega^{1}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})\xrightarrow{d^{1}}\Omega^{2}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})\xrightarrow{d^{2}}\ldots\xrightarrow{d^{n-1}}\Omega^{n}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})\xrightarrow{d^{n}}0\,,

(56)

where the
0
-th coboundary operator is defined to act on scalars
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
as

\displaystyle d^{0}\lambda=\bigtimes_{{\underline{i}}={\underline{1}}}^{{\underline{n}}}\lambda s_{{\underline{i}}}=(\lambda s_{{\underline{1}}},\ldots,\lambda s_{{\underline{n}}})\,.

(57)

As we alluded to in the introduction, the structure of entanglement
k
-forms shares several properties with that of differential forms defined on a manifold with a metric. For instance, by virtue of the Schmidt decomposition, one can define a version of the Hodge star operation on entanglement forms. For any multi-index
{\underline{I}}
, one can express the parent pure state (
14
) defining our entanglement complex as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{S}\lambda_{\alpha}\ket{\alpha_{{\underline{I}}}}\otimes\big|\alpha_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\big\rangle\,,

(58)

up to the action of a permutation operation which re-shuffles the subsystems in
{\underline{I}}\cup{\underline{I}}^{C}
into the order
\{{\underline{1}},\ldots,{\underline{n}}\}
, which we omit for simplicity. Here
S
is an integer called the
Schmidt rank
, not to be confused with the symmetric group
S_{n}
or a support projection
s_{{\underline{i}}}
, and the quantities
\lambda_{\alpha}
are numbers known as
Schmidt coefficients
. The states
\ket{\alpha_{{\underline{I}}}}
,
\big|\alpha_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\big\rangle
belong to
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}
and span the images of
\rho_{{\underline{I}}}
and
\rho_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}
, respectively. Therefore, given an entanglement
k
-form
\omega
with a component
\omega_{{\underline{I}}}\in\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}}))
, we can express the matrix elements of this operator in the Schmidt basis as

\displaystyle\omega_{{\underline{I}}}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{S}\left(\omega_{{\underline{I}}}\right)_{\alpha\beta}\ket{\alpha_{{\underline{I}}}}\bra{\beta_{{\underline{I}}}}\,.

(59)

We define the component of an entanglement
(n-k)
form
\ast\omega
which is associated with the complement
{\underline{I}}^{C}
by the expression

\displaystyle\left(\ast\omega\right)_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}=(-1)^{\sigma}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{S}\left(\omega_{{\underline{I}}}^{\ast}\right)_{\alpha\beta}\big|\alpha_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\big\rangle\big\langle\beta_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\big|\,,

(60)

where
\sigma\in S_{{\underline{n}}}
is the permutation of
\{{\underline{1}},\ldots,{\underline{n}}\}
given by
{\underline{I}}\cup{\underline{I}}^{C}
, and the collection of all such components as
{\underline{I}}
varies over length-
k
multi-indices defines
\ast\omega
. On the right side of (
60
), the symbol
\left(\omega_{{\underline{I}}}^{\ast}\right)_{\alpha\beta}
denotes the complex conjugate of
\left(\omega_{{\underline{I}}}\right)_{\alpha\beta}
. Like the Hodge star acting on differential forms, this operation on
k
-forms is an involution up to a sign:

\displaystyle\ast\ast=(-1)^{k(n-k)}\,.

(61)

Given an entanglement
k
-form
\omega
and an entanglement
p
-form
\eta
, with
k+p\leq n
, one can also define their wedge product
\omega\wedge\eta
. For any length
(k+p)
multi-index
{\underline{K}}
, the corresponding component of this wedge product is

\displaystyle\left(\omega\wedge\eta\right)_{{\underline{K}}}=\sum_{{\underline{I}}\sqcup{\underline{J}}={\underline{K}}}(-1)^{\sigma}\left(s_{{\underline{K}}}\Pi_{{\underline{I}},{\underline{K}}}^{-1}\left(\omega_{{\underline{I}}}\otimes\eta_{{\underline{J}}}\right)\Pi_{{\underline{I}},{\underline{K}}}s_{{\underline{K}}}\right)\,,

(62)

where the sum runs over all pairs of multi-indices
{\underline{I}}
,
{\underline{J}}
whose disjoint union is
{\underline{K}}
, and for each term the factor
(-1)^{\sigma}
is the sign of the permutation mapping
{\underline{I}}\sqcup{\underline{J}}
to
{\underline{K}}
.

For instance, on a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
with entanglement
1
-forms

\displaystyle\omega=(\omega_{A},\omega_{B})\,,\qquad\eta=(\eta_{A},\eta_{B})\,,

(63)

this wedge product acts as the usual wedge product on differential one-forms,

\displaystyle\omega\wedge\eta=(\omega_{A}\otimes\eta_{B}-\eta_{A}\otimes\omega_{B})\big|_{\rho_{AB}}\,.

(64)

Combining this definition of the wedge product with the Hodge star operation, one can define an inner product on
\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})
by

\displaystyle\langle\omega,\eta\rangle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(\omega\wedge\ast\eta\right)\big|_{\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}}\right)\,,

(65)

where the trace is taken in the total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}
, or more precisely in its one-dimensional subspace
\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})
.

The factor of
k
which was included in the definition (
41
) ensures that this notion of wedge product satisfies the Leibniz formula. If
\omega
is an entanglement
p
-form, one has

\displaystyle d\left(\omega\wedge\eta\right)=\left(d\omega\right)\wedge\eta+(-1)^{p}\omega\wedge\left(d\eta\right)\,.

(66)

Properties of the various operations on entanglement
k
-forms are discussed in
Ferko:2024swt
, to which we refer the reader for further details. We will review only one additional construction here. Using the inner product, we define the adjoint
\delta
of the coboundary operator,

\displaystyle\langle\omega,d\eta\rangle=\langle\delta\omega,\eta\rangle\,,

(67)

and the Laplacian operator given in equation (
10
), which we repeat:

\displaystyle\Delta=d\delta+\delta d\,.

(68)

3
Proofs of Conjectures for GHZ and W States

It has been known for decades that a collection of three qubits can be entangled in two inequivalent ways
Dur:2000zz
, exemplified by the GHZ state

\displaystyle\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{000}+\ket{111}\right)\,,

(69)

and the W state,

\displaystyle\ket{\mathrm{W}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\ket{001}+\ket{010}+\ket{100}\right)\,.

(70)

The states (
69
) and (
70
) exhibit qualitatively different physical features. For instance, the GHZ state is fragile to particle loss: upon tracing out a single qubit, the state (
69
) reduces to a classical mixture of product states with no bipartite entanglement between the remaining two qubits. In contrast, tracing out one qubit in the W state (
70
) gives a reduced system in which the two remaining qubits are still entangled.

One would expect that this qualitative difference is encoded in some feature of the entanglement
k
-forms associated with the states (
69
) and (
70
). Indeed, the distinction is already visible from the dimensions of their cohomologies, i.e. their Poincaré polynomials:

\displaystyle P\left(\ket{\mathrm{W}};y\right)=3+3y\,,\qquad P\left(\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}};y\right)=7+7y\,.

(71)

Because the dimensions of entanglement cohomologies are invariant under local unitary transformations, this result gives another way of understanding the LU inequivalence of the tripartite GHZ and W states.

It is natural to wonder about the mathematical structure of the cohomologies for generalizations of the states (
69
) and (
70
) to larger numbers of subsystems and different choices of local Hilbert space dimensions. Fix an
n
-partite Hilbert space of the general form (
12
), with dimensions described by the vector
\vec{d}=(d_{{\underline{1}}},\ldots,d_{{\underline{n}}})
where
d_{{\underline{i}}}\geq 2
for all
{\underline{i}}
, and define the generalized GHZ state

\displaystyle\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}_{n,\vec{d}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0}^{\otimes n}+\ket{1}^{\otimes n}\right)\,,

(72)

along with the generalized W state,

\displaystyle\ket{\text{W}_{n,\vec{d}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ket{0}^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-i)}\right)\,.

(73)

This notation (
73
) means that, in the
i
-th term of the sum, the
i
-th subsystem occurs with a state
\ket{1}
and all other subsystems appear with a state
\ket{0}
. Here we use the obvious shorthand
⊗k
to denote a
k
-fold tensor product,

\displaystyle\ket{0}^{\otimes k}=\underbrace{\ket{0}\otimes\ldots\otimes\ket{0}}_{k\text{ times}}\,,

(74)

and likewise for
\ket{1}^{\otimes k}
. To simplify notation, it is convenient to define

\displaystyle\ket{e_{i}}=\ket{0}^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes(n-i)}=\big|\underbrace{0\ldots 0}_{i-1\text{ times }}1\underbrace{0\ldots 0}_{n-i\text{ times}}\big\rangle\,,

(75)

which has a
1
in slot
i
and
0
elsewhere. In this notation,

\displaystyle\ket{\text{W}_{n,\vec{d}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ket{e_{i}}\,.

(76)

When
n=2
, both the GHZ and W states reduce to a Bell pair, so we will restrict attention to
n\geq 3
in what follows. Using numerical code to compute the dimensions of entanglement cohomologies for the first few non-trivial GHZ and W states, one finds the Poincaré polynomials listed in Table

\vec{d}

(77)

Based on this numerical evidence, in
Mainiero:2019enr
it was conjectured that the Poincaré polynomials for all generalized GHZ and W states take simple closed-form expressions.

Conjecture 1
.

For all
n\geq 3
and dimension vectors
\vec{d}
,

\displaystyle P^{\mathrm{GHZ}}_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=1+y^{n-2}+\frac{2}{y}\left(\left(1+y\right)^{n}-\left(1+y^{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(2n+1\right)+2\sum_{m=2}^{n-2}{n\choose m}y^{m-1}+\left(2n+1\right)y^{n-2}\,.

(78)

The coefficients in (
1
) are symmetric – as is required by Hodge duality – and grow linearly in
n
at the ends but as binomial coefficients in the interior. As was already pointed out in
Mainiero:2019enr
, these coefficients follow the structure of a modified Pascal’s triangle.

Conjecture 2
.

For all
n\geq 3
and dimension vectors
\vec{d}
,

\displaystyle P^{\mathrm{W}}_{n}(y)=3+3y^{n-2}\,.

(79)

That is, generalized W states are believed to have only two non-vanishing Betti numbers,
b_{1}=b_{n-1}=3
, independent of
n
and of the local dimensions. Again, this is compatible with Hodge duality, which requires that the top and bottom cohomologies are isomorphic.

There is strong numerical evidence for Conjectures
1
and
2
, but to the best of our knowledge, no explicit proof has yet been presented. The goal of this section is to rigorously establish these two results. The proof of each of these conjectures splits into two parts. The first part concerns the details of the structure of the states under consideration, and in particular the images of their reduced density matrices and the action of the operators
d^{k}
on tuples of operators acting on each space
\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}}))
. Once the explicit action of each such
d^{k}
has been determined, the second part of the proof is common to both conjectures, and simply involves a counting argument to determine the dimensions of the kernels and images of the operators
d^{k}
. Because this second step of the proof is shared in both conjectures, we will present it first as a lemma in Section
3.1
. Although this result is elementary, it clarifies how the structure of entanglement cohomology – at least for the special cases of generalized GHZ and W states – is related to ordinary simplicial homology, and in particular the contractibility of the
n
-simplex. After establishing this basic lemma, we apply it in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
to prove Conjectures
1
and
2
, respectively.

3.1
Simplicial Lemma

As we mentioned around equation (
41
), entanglement cohomology can be viewed as a certain modification of the simplicial cohomology of the
(n-1)
-simplex with coefficients involving endomorphisms of appropriate reduced density matrices. In our proofs of Conjectures
1
and
2
, we will “trivialize” these coefficients by working in explicit bases, which reduces the computation of the cohomologies to repeated application of standard facts about simplicial cohomology. The primary result which we will use is the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Fix positive integers
n
and
k
with
1\leq k\leq n-1
, and let
V_{n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For each
k
-element subset
I\subset V_{n}
, define a (real or complex) variable
x_{I}
. For convenience, we will always assume that subsets are sorted as
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
with
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}
. Before imposing any constraints, the (real or complex) dimension of the space of all
x_{I}
is
{n\choose k}
.

Now suppose that, for every
(k+1)
-element subset
J=\{j_{0},\ldots,j_{k}\}\subset V_{n}
, again assuming the sorting condition
j_{0}<\ldots<j_{k}
, we impose the alternating sum constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}x_{J\setminus\{j_{r}\}}=0\,.

(80)

Then the solution space of (
80
) has (real or complex) dimension
{n-1\choose k-1}
.

Lemma
1
is essentially the statement that the
(n-1)
-simplex is contractible. An intuitive way to think of the proof is that the collection of variables
(x_{I})
is uniquely determined by its values on
k
-element subsets that contain a single fixed element of
V_{n}
, which we may take to be
1\in V_{n}
without loss of generality. In particular, suppose that we know the values of the variables
(x_{I})
on all subsets of
V_{n}
containing
1
, and we wish to determine the value of
x_{I}
on some other subset
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
which does not contain
1
. Form the subset
J=\{1\}\cup I
, which is a
(k+1)
-element subset of
V_{n}
, and thus we can solve the constraint (
80
):

\displaystyle x_{i_{1}\ldots i_{k}}=\sum_{r=1}^{k}(-1)^{r-1}x_{1i_{1}\ldots\widehat{i}_{r}\ldots i_{k}}\,,

(81)

where we write a hatted index
\widehat{i}_{r}
to indicate that it is omitted from the sum. Every term on the right side of (
81
) involves a multi-index containing the entry
1
, so it is known by assumption. Furthermore, there is one such constraint (
81
) for each subset
I\subset V_{n}
which does not contain
1
, so we have the same number of unknown variables as equations. Thus we can solve for all of the variables
(x_{I})
given only the values of
x_{I}
on subsets
I
which contain a single fixed index like
1
, and the number of such values is
{n-1\choose k-1}
, which establishes the claim.

Although the above argument gives the idea of why Lemma
1
is true, we find it instructive to include a more formal proof, which also explains why this result is essentially an immediate consequence of contractibility of the simplex.

Proof.

Let
\Upsilon^{n-1}
be the
(n-1)
simplex on
n
vertices. A
(k-1)
-cochain
x\in C^{k-1}(\Upsilon^{n-1})
on
\Upsilon^{n-1}
is a function that assigns a scalar to each
(k-1)
-simplex, i.e. to each
k
-element subset
I\subset V_{n}
, which is precisely a collection of
{n\choose k}
variables
x\equiv(x_{I})
.

We choose the standard orientation convention where the oriented
k
-face with ordered vertices
j_{0}<\ldots<j_{k}
has boundary

\displaystyle\partial[j_{0}\ldots j_{k}]=\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}[j_{0}\ldots\widehat{j}_{r}\ldots j_{k}]\,,

(82)

where again a hat denotes omission in the sum.

The corresponding coboundary operator on cochains,
\delta:C^{k-1}\to C^{k}
, is
6
6

6

We trust that this operator will not be confused with the codifferential acting on entanglement forms, defined in equation (
67
), since the difference will be clear from context.

\displaystyle(\delta x)\left([j_{0}\ldots j_{k}]\right)

\displaystyle=x\left(\partial[j_{0}\ldots j_{k}]\right)=\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}x_{j_{0}\ldots\widehat{j}_{r}\ldots j_{k}}\,.

(83)

In this language, the alternating sum constraint (
80
) is simply

\displaystyle\delta x=0\,,

(84)

which means that
x
is a cocycle. The solution space is therefore

\displaystyle Z^{k-1}(\Upsilon^{n-1})=\ker\left(\delta:C^{k-1}\to C^{k}\right)\,.

(85)

We wish to show that

\displaystyle\dim(Z^{k-1})={n-1\choose k-1}\,.

(86)

To do this, we use the familiar fact from algebraic topology that
\Upsilon^{n-1}
is contractible, so

\displaystyle H^{p}(\Upsilon^{n-1})=0

(87)

for all
p\geq 1
, while
\dim\left(H^{0}(\Upsilon^{n-1})\right)=1
. Since
H^{p}=\frac{Z^{p}}{B^{p}}
, where
B^{p}=\mathrm{im}(\delta:C^{p-1}\to C^{p})
, this means that
Z^{p}=B^{p}
for
p\geq 1
and
\dim(Z^{0})=1
.

Letting
a_{k}=\dim(Z^{k-1})
, for
k\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle a_{k}=\dim(B^{k-1})=\mathrm{rank}(\delta:C^{k-2}\to C^{k-1})\,,

(88)

but by the rank-nullity theorem,

\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}(\delta)=\dim(C^{k-2})-\dim(\ker(\delta))={n\choose k-1}-a_{k-1}\,.

(89)

This means that we have the base case and recursion relation

\displaystyle a_{1}=1\,,\qquad a_{k}={n\choose k-1}-a_{k-1}\text{ for }k\geq 2\,.

(90)

The solution to this recursion is
a_{k}={n-1\choose k-1}
. The base case is clear:
a_{1}={n-1\choose 0}=1
. And assuming
a_{k-1}={n-1\choose k-2}
, we find

\displaystyle a_{k}={n\choose k-1}-{n-1\choose k-2}={n-1\choose k-1}\,,

(91)

by Pascal’s identity. This establishes the lemma for all
n
and
1\leq k\leq n-1
.
∎

3.2
GHZ States

We will now compute the entanglement cohomologies for the generalized GHZ states (
72
).

Proof.

As we mentioned above, the specific vector
\vec{d}
of local dimensions will not play any role in our discussion, so we will suppress it and write the associated density matrix as

\displaystyle\rho^{\text{GHZ}}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{0^{\otimes n}}\bra{0^{\otimes n}}+\ket{0^{\otimes n}}\bra{1^{\otimes n}}+\ket{1^{\otimes n}}\bra{0^{\otimes n}}+\ket{1^{\otimes n}}\bra{1^{\otimes n}}\right)\,.

(92)

To form the spaces
\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}}))
involving the reduced density matrices, we will need to evaluate various partial traces. Let
{\underline{I}}^{C}
be a length-
k
multi-index of subsystems, for
2\leq k\leq n-2
which we wish to trace out, retaining the subsystems associated with the length-
m
multi-index
{\underline{I}}
where
m=n-k
. The partial traces are trivial to compute,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\ket{0^{\otimes n}}\bra{0^{\otimes n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\ket{0^{\otimes n}}\bra{1^{\otimes n}}\right)

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\ket{1^{\otimes n}}\bra{0^{\otimes n}}\right)

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\ket{1^{\otimes n}}\bra{1^{\otimes n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\ket{1^{\otimes m}}\bra{1^{\otimes m}}\,,

(93)

where we use the notation
⊗m
to refer to tensor products of states in the
m
factors of
{\underline{I}}
. Explicitly, each of the operators in (
3.2
) act on

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}=\bigotimes_{{\underline{i}}\in{\underline{I}}}\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{i}}}\,,

(94)

and all of the reduced density matrices take the form

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{GHZ}}_{{\underline{I}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}+\ket{1^{\otimes m}}\bra{1^{\otimes m}}\right)\,,

(95)

so the support projections

\displaystyle s^{\mathrm{GHZ}}_{{\underline{I}}}=\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}+\ket{1^{\otimes m}}\bra{1^{\otimes m}}

(96)

are rank-
2
. Thus any operator
\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}}\in\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{im}(\rho^{\mathrm{GHZ}}_{{\underline{I}}}))
is determined by four matrix elements

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}}=a_{{\underline{I}}}\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}+b_{{\underline{I}}}\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{1^{\otimes m}}+c_{{\underline{I}}}\ket{1^{\otimes m}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}+d_{{\underline{I}}}\ket{1^{\otimes m}}\bra{1^{\otimes m}}

(97)

in this basis.

Now fix a subsystem
{\underline{j}}\notin{\underline{I}}
and consider the length
(m+1)
multi-index
{\underline{J}}=\{{\underline{j}}\}\cup{\underline{I}}
. We wish to compute the matrix elements of terms of the form appearing in the coboundary formula (
41
) which arise from the contributions of
\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}}
. Each such operator morally takes the form of a tensor product
\mathbb{I}_{{\underline{j}}}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}}
, up to a sign depending on the permutation and a re-shuffling of tensor product factors. By a direct computation one finds that

\displaystyle\bra{0^{\otimes(m+1)}}\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}})\ket{0^{\otimes(m+1)}}

\displaystyle=a_{{\underline{I}}}\,,

\displaystyle\bra{0^{\otimes(m+1)}}\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}})\ket{1^{\otimes(m+1)}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\bra{1^{\otimes(m+1)}}\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}})\ket{0^{\otimes(m+1)}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\bra{1^{\otimes(m+1)}}\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}})\ket{1^{\otimes(m+1)}}

\displaystyle=d_{{\underline{I}}}\,,

(98)

where we use the natural basis for
\mathrm{im}(\rho_{{\underline{J}}})
.

We may now use this fact to compute
\dim(\ker(d^{m}))
. Let
\omega=(\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}})
be an entanglement
m
-form whose components take the form (
97
) for each length-
m
multi-index
{\underline{I}}
. All of the components in the sum (
41
) vanish if and only if all four of the matrix elements (
3.2
) associated with each
(d\omega)_{{\underline{J}}}
vanish. We see that every entry
c_{{\underline{I}}}
and
b_{{\underline{I}}}
is in this kernel, since those matrix elements do not appear in (
3.2
) and thus are annihilated by the map
d^{m}
. There are also contributions to the kernel from the solution space arising from the
a_{{\underline{I}}}
and
d_{{\underline{I}}}
that are annihilated by the coboundary map. From the definition (
41
), we see that the conditions for a combination of
a_{{\underline{I}}}
or
d_{{\underline{I}}}
to lie in the kernel are

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{m}(-1)^{r}a_{{\underline{J}}\setminus\{{\underline{j}}_{r}\}}=0\,,\qquad\sum_{r=0}^{m}(-1)^{r}d_{{\underline{J}}\setminus\{{\underline{j}}_{r}\}}=0\,,

(99)

respectively. By Lemma
1
, the solution space of each of these two constraints is
{n-1\choose m-1}
, so

\displaystyle\dim(\mathrm{ker}(d^{m}))=2{n-1\choose m-1}+2{n\choose m}\,,

(100)

where the first term comes from the solution space of all of the
a_{{\underline{I}}}
and
d_{{\underline{I}}}
which are mapped to zero, and the second term comes from the
b_{{\underline{I}}}
and
c_{{\underline{I}}}
that are automatically in the kernel.

The dimension of the
m
-th cohomology group, where again
2\leq m\leq n-2
, is then

\displaystyle\dim(H^{m})

\displaystyle=\dim(\ker(d^{m}))-\dim(\mathrm{im}(d^{m-1}))

\displaystyle=\dim(\ker(d^{m}))-\left(\dim(\Omega^{m-1})-\dim(\ker(d^{m-1}))\right)

\displaystyle=2{n-1\choose m-1}+2{n\choose m}-4{n\choose m-1}+2{n-1\choose m-2}+2{n\choose m-1}

\displaystyle=2{n\choose m}\,,

(101)

where we have used the rank-nullity theorem, the fact that
\dim\left(\Omega^{m}\right)=4{n\choose m}
as there are
{n\choose m}
length-
m
multi-indices and each operator has four independent entries, and Pascal’s identity. This confirms the coefficients in the conjectured Poincaré polynomial on the second line of (
1
), except for the lowest and highest Betti numbers, which we have excluded by taking
2\leq k\leq n-2
. To complete the proof, we handle these edge cases directly. Since the image of the map
d^{0}
defined in equation (
57
) is clearly one-dimensional,

\displaystyle\dim(H^{1})

\displaystyle=\dim(\ker(d^{1}))-\dim(\mathrm{im}(d^{0}))

\displaystyle=2{n-1\choose 0}+2{n\choose 1}-1

\displaystyle=2n+1\,.

(102)

Similarly, as
\dim(\mathrm{im}(d^{n-1}))=1
, we have

\displaystyle\dim(H^{n-1})

\displaystyle=\dim(\ker(d^{n-1}))-\dim(\mathrm{im}(d^{n-2}))

\displaystyle=\dim(\Omega^{n-1})-1-\left(\dim(\Omega^{n-2})-\dim(\ker(d^{n-2}))\right)

\displaystyle=4{n\choose n-1}-1-4{n\choose n-2}+2{n-1\choose n-3}+2{n\choose n-2}

\displaystyle=2n+1\,.

(103)

These final two dimensions also agree with (
1
), establishing Conjecture
1
.
∎

We see that the proof of Conjecture
1
crucially relied on certain special properties of generalized GHZ states – namely, that all of their reduced density matrices are rank-
2
, and that the action of the coboundary operator in a natural basis takes a certain consistent structure across entanglement forms of different degrees.

3.3
W States

Next we apply a similar technique to prove Conjecture
2
regarding the dimensions of cohomologies for generalized W states. As we will see, the reduced density matrices are again all rank-
2
in this case, but the action of the coboundary operator will now involve all four matrix elements in an appropriate basis.

Proof.

Using the notation (
76
), the density matrix associated to the W state is

\displaystyle\rho^{\text{W}}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{\underline{i}},{\underline{j}}={\underline{1}}}^{{\underline{n}}}\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\,,

(104)

where again we suppress the vector
\vec{d}
of subsystem dimensions, since all states beyond
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
are spectators.

As before, it is convenient to develop some formulas for reduced density matrices. Suppose that we wish to trace out a length-
k
multi-index
{\underline{I}}^{C}
of subsystems, again with
2\leq k\leq n-2
, while keeping the subsystems labeled by the length
m=n-k
multi-index
{\underline{I}}
. In addition to the states
\ket{e_{{\underline{i}}}}\in\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}
, define analogous states

\displaystyle\ket{f_{{\underline{r}}}}=\ket{0}^{\otimes(r-1)}\otimes\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes(m-r)}\in\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}\,,

(105)

where we take indices
{\underline{r}},{\underline{s}}={\underline{i}}_{1}\ldots{\underline{i}}_{m}
to label subsystems in the reduced Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}
and non-underlined variables
r,s=1,\ldots,m
to label the integers associated with the corresponding tensor product factors.

Now consider a particular term in the partial trace

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\rho^{\text{W}}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{\underline{i}},{\underline{j}}={\underline{1}}}^{{\underline{n}}}\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)\,.

(106)

There are three possibilities giving three sets of contributions:

1.

Both
{\underline{i}}
and
{\underline{j}}
belong to
{\underline{I}}^{C}
. We first perform the partial trace over subsystem
{\underline{i}}
, giving

\displaystyle\sum_{{\underline{i}},{\underline{j}}\in{\underline{I}}^{C}}\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\underline{i}},{\underline{j}}\in{\underline{I}}^{C}}\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}\setminus{\underline{i}}}\left(\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{i}}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\underline{i}},{\underline{j}}\in{\underline{I}}^{C}}\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}\setminus{\underline{i}}}\left(\bra{0_{{\underline{i}}}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)\ket{0_{\underline{i}}}+\bra{1_{{\underline{i}}}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)\ket{1_{\underline{i}}}\right)\,.

(107)

Then the first term vanishes as it is proportional to
\braket{0_{{\underline{i}}}\mid 1_{{\underline{i}}}}
, and the second term also vanishes unless
{\underline{j}}={\underline{i}}
. Retaining this single non-zero contribution,

\displaystyle\sum_{{\underline{i}},{\underline{j}}\in{\underline{I}}^{C}}\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\underline{i}}={\underline{j}}\in{\underline{I}}^{C}}\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}\setminus{\underline{i}}}\left(\bra{1_{{\underline{i}}}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)\ket{1_{\underline{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle=k\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}\,.

(108)

2.

One of
{\underline{i}}
,
{\underline{j}}
belongs to
{\underline{I}}
while the other belongs to
{\underline{I}}^{C}
. Without loss of generality, assume that it is
{\underline{i}}\in{\underline{I}}^{C}
and first perform the partial trace over
{\underline{i}}
. Then both terms

\displaystyle\bra{0_{{\underline{i}}}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)\ket{0_{\underline{i}}}+\bra{1_{{\underline{i}}}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)\ket{1_{\underline{i}}}

(109)

vanish by orthogonality, the first because
\braket{0_{{\underline{i}}}\mid e_{{\underline{i}}}}=0
as before, and the second because
\braket{e_{{\underline{j}}}\mid 1_{{\underline{i}}}}=0
since by assumption
{\underline{i}}\neq{\underline{j}}
so
e_{{\underline{j}}}
has a tensor product factor of
\ket{0_{{\underline{i}}}}
on subsystem
{\underline{i}}
. Therefore all such terms vanish:

\displaystyle\sum_{{\underline{i}}\in{\underline{I}},{\underline{j}}\in{\underline{I}}^{C}}\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)=0=\sum_{{\underline{i}}\in{\underline{I}}^{C},{\underline{j}}\in{\underline{I}}}\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)\,.

(110)

3.

Both
{\underline{i}}
and
{\underline{j}}
belong to
{\underline{I}}
. Then neither
\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}
nor
\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}
has a tensor product factor of
\ket{1}
on any subsystem in the partial trace, and only the terms
\bra{0_{{\underline{k}}}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)\ket{0_{\underline{k}}}
contribute. Evaluating these partial traces has the effect of removing the extra
\ket{0}
factors in
\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}
and
\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}
, leaving the analogous states
\ket{f_{{\underline{r}}}}
in
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{I}}}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{{\underline{i}},{\underline{j}}\in{\underline{I}}}\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\ket{e_{\underline{i}}}\bra{e_{\underline{j}}}\right)=\sum_{{\underline{r}},{\underline{s}}\in{\underline{I}}}\ket{f_{{\underline{r}}}}\bra{f_{{\underline{s}}}}\,.

(111)

Combining the contributions from these three types of terms, we find

\displaystyle\rho^{\text{W}}_{{\underline{I}}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{I}}^{C}}\left(\rho^{\text{W}}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{n}\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{\underline{r}},{\underline{s}}\in{\underline{I}}}\ket{f_{{\underline{r}}}}\bra{f_{{\underline{s}}}}\,,

(112)

or in terms of the reduced W states

\displaystyle\ket{\text{W}_{{\underline{I}}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{{\underline{r}}\in{\underline{I}}}\ket{f_{\underline{r}}}\,,

(113)

one has

\displaystyle\rho^{\text{W}}_{{\underline{I}}}=\frac{k}{n}\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}+\frac{m}{n}\ket{\text{W}_{{\underline{I}}}}\bra{\text{W}_{{\underline{I}}}}\,.

(114)

As a check, the trace of this reduced density matrix is unity, since
m+k=n
.

It is then easy to read off the support projection for any such reduced density matrix:

\displaystyle s^{\text{W}}_{{\underline{I}}}=\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}+\ket{\text{W}_{{\underline{I}}}}\bra{\text{W}_{{\underline{I}}}}\,.

(115)

All that remains is to determine the dimensions of the kernels of the coboundary maps. A generic operator
\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}}
in
\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{im}(\rho^{\text{W}}_{{\underline{I}}}))
has four non-trivial matrix elements:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}}=a_{{\underline{I}}}\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}+b_{{\underline{I}}}\ket{0^{\otimes m}}\bra{\text{W}_{{\underline{I}}}}+c_{{\underline{I}}}\ket{\text{W}_{{\underline{I}}}}\bra{0^{\otimes m}}+d_{{\underline{I}}}\ket{\text{W}_{{\underline{I}}}}\bra{\text{W}_{{\underline{I}}}}\,.

(116)

Like in the preceding proof, choose a subsystem
{\underline{j}}\notin{\underline{I}}
and define the length
(m+1)
multi-index
{\underline{J}}=\{{\underline{j}}\}\cup{\underline{I}}
. We compute the matrix elements of terms relevant in the coboundary formula (
41
):

\displaystyle\bra{0^{\otimes(m+1)}}\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}})\ket{0^{\otimes(m+1)}}

\displaystyle=a_{{\underline{I}}}\,,

\displaystyle\bra{0^{\otimes(m+1)}}\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}})\ket{\text{W}_{{\underline{J}}}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{m}{m+1}}b_{{\underline{I}}}\,,

\displaystyle\bra{\text{W}_{{\underline{J}}}}\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}})\ket{0^{\otimes(m+1)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{m}{m+1}}c_{{\underline{I}}}\,,

\displaystyle\bra{\text{W}_{{\underline{J}}}}\iota_{{\underline{I}}}^{{\underline{J}}}(\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}})\ket{\text{W}_{{\underline{J}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{{\underline{I}}}}{m+1}+\frac{m}{m+1}d_{{\underline{I}}}\,.

(117)

Unlike in the case of GHZ states, all four matrix elements are now non-trivial, and are in fact linearly independent. A given tuple of operators
\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{I}}}
is therefore in the kernel of the coboundary map only if the alternating sum (
80
) vanishes for each of the
a_{{\underline{I}}}
,
b_{{\underline{I}}}
,
c_{{\underline{I}}}
, and
d_{{\underline{I}}}
separately. The dimension of each such solution space is
{n-1\choose m-1}
, per Lemma
1
, so

\displaystyle\dim(\mathrm{ker}(d^{m}))=4{n-1\choose m-1}\,,

(118)

while using the rank-nullity theorem,
\dim\left(\Omega^{m}\right)=4{n\choose m}
, and Pascal’s identity,

\displaystyle\dim(\mathrm{im}(d^{m}))=4{n\choose m}-4{n-1\choose m-1}=4{n-1\choose m}\,.

(119)

The dimension of the cohomology is

\displaystyle\dim(H^{m})

\displaystyle=\dim(\ker(d^{m}))-\dim(\mathrm{im}(d^{m-1}))

\displaystyle=4\left({n-1\choose m-1}-{n-1\choose m-1}\right)

\displaystyle=0\,.

(120)

This is sufficient to prove that all of the interior cohomologies for the generalized W states vanish, again with the exception of the top and bottom cohomologies where the argument fails because the dimensions of those spaces of entanglement forms are
1
-dimensional rather than
2
-dimensional, which is why we restricted to
2\leq k\leq n-2
above. We handle those remaining cases by hand. As
\dim(\mathrm{im}(d^{0}))=1=\dim(\mathrm{im}(d^{n-1}))
, we have

\displaystyle\dim(H^{1})

\displaystyle=\dim(\ker(d^{1}))-1

\displaystyle=4{n\choose 0}-1

\displaystyle=3\,,

(121)

and likewise

\displaystyle\dim(H^{n-1})

\displaystyle=\dim(\ker(d^{n-1}))-\dim(\mathrm{im}(d^{n-2}))

\displaystyle=\dim(\Omega^{n-1})-1-\left(\dim(\Omega^{n-2})-\dim(\ker(d^{n-2}))\right)

\displaystyle=4n-1-4{n\choose n-2}+4{n-1\choose n-3}

\displaystyle=3\,,

(122)

where we have again used the rank-nullity theorem. This agrees with the Poincaré polynomial proposed in Conjecture
2
.
∎

4
New Local Unitary Invariants

When entanglement cohomology was first introduced in
Mainiero:2019enr
, it was proven that the dimensions of these cohomologies are invariant under local unitary transformations acting on each of the tensor product factors
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{i}}}
of the total Hilbert space. Such LU transformations do not affect the structure of multipartite entanglement, so these Betti numbers are good quantities for characterizing inequivalent entanglement patterns, as we have seen in the case of the (unitarily inequivalent) generalized GHZ and W states.

However, two potential issues with the dimensions of entanglement cohomologies are:

(I)

The Betti numbers are
integers
, which therefore cannot directly capture continuous data such as entanglement entropies or Schmidt coefficients. In a sense, they are measuring certain coarse-grained information about multipartite entanglement.

(II)

The dimensions of cohomologies are not
complete
, in the sense that there exist unitarily inequivalent pairs of pure states whose Betti numbers all coincide.

The goal of this section is to introduce two new types of LU quantities, extending the collection of dimensions of cohomologies, which are constructed from the entanglement
k
-forms associated with a pure state. We take motivation from the close analogy between entanglement forms and differential forms on manifolds, defining quantities which are natural from a geometrical perspective. Both of the new classes of LU invariants are
continuous
quantities, which resolves issue
(I)
of the dimensions of entanglement cohomologies.

It appears unlikely that even our extended collection of LU invariants is
complete
, i.e. that this set of data is in one-to-one correspondence with unitarily inequivalent multipartite pure states, which would represent a solution to issue
(II)
. In general, performing a systematic classification of the possible invariants that can be constructed from objects transforming in representations of some group – and the relations between such invariants – is a difficult problem in the subject of invariant theory; see e.g.
derksen2002computational
for an introduction. We will comment briefly on this future direction in Section
5
. However, even if our set of new LU invariants is incomplete, these quantities still offer interesting new geometrically-inspired quantities which might be used to provide a partial classification of patterns of multipartite entanglement refining the one given by the Betti numbers.

4.1
Spectrum of Entanglement Laplacian

We have emphasized that, given a pure state density matrix
\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}
, the spaces of entanglement
k
-forms
\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})
are simply length-
{n\choose k}
tuples of finite-dimensional linear operators. In particular, each such
\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})
is itself a finite-dimensional vector space.
It was shown in
Mainiero:2019enr
that any local unitary transformation induces an isomorphism of the vector spaces
\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})
, which can simply be viewed as a change of basis. The key ingredients of entanglement cohomology are constructed so that they transform appropriately with respect to this change of basis. For instance, owing to the definition of the coboundary operators
d^{k}
, any local unitary transformation simply induces a cochain isomorphism between the original entanglement cochain complex and the transformed one obtained by enacting the LU transformation. As a result, the dimensions of entanglement cohomologies are LU invariants, as we have reviewed.

Similarly, the entanglement Laplacian
\Delta
is constructed only using the coboundary operators
d^{k}
and their adjoints
\delta^{k}
with respect to the LU-invariant inner product between entanglement forms. As a result, it is natural to conjecture that any collection of basis-independent data associated with the Laplacian – such as the eigenvalues of the linear operator
\Delta
acting on
\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})
, which are clearly basis-independent – also provides LU invariants. We will not give an explicit mathematical proof of this statement, although we provide experimental checks of LU invariance of these eigenvalues below.

First let us make some simplistic comments. Like the Hodge Laplacian acting on differential forms, the entanglement Laplacian
\Delta
is positive semi-definite, since

\displaystyle\langle\omega,\Delta\omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\omega,d\delta\omega\rangle+\langle\omega,\delta d\omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta\omega,\delta\omega\rangle+\langle d\omega,d\omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\|\delta\omega\right\|^{2}+\left\|d\omega\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 0\,,

(123)

since the inner product on entanglement
k
-forms obeys the usual property that
\langle\eta,\eta\rangle\geq 0
with equality only if
\eta=0
. Therefore, the eigenvalues of
\Delta
are non-negative: if
\Delta\omega=\lambda\omega
, then
\lambda\geq 0
, with
\lambda=0
only for harmonic forms.

Second, by virtue of its construction, the entanglement Laplacian commutes with differential and codifferential operators:

\displaystyle[\Delta,d]

\displaystyle=\left(d\delta+\delta d\right)d-d\left(d\delta+\delta d\right)

\displaystyle=d\delta d-d\delta d

\displaystyle=0\,,

(124)

using the property that
d^{2}=0
, and likewise

\displaystyle[\Delta,\delta]

\displaystyle=\left(d\delta+\delta d\right)\delta-\delta\left(d\delta+\delta d\right)

\displaystyle=\delta d\delta-\delta d\delta

\displaystyle=0\,.

(125)

Thus if an entanglement
k
-form
\omega
is an eigenform of
\Delta
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, there is typically either a corresponding
(k+1)
-form
d\omega
or a
(k-1)
-form
\delta\omega
with the same eigenvalue, except in the special case of harmonic forms
\omega
for which
d\omega=0=\delta\omega
. This is the same structure that underlies the pairing of energy levels in supersymmetric quantum mechanics
Witten:1982csb
;
Witten:1982mt
, which applies to excited states but not to zero-energy ground states.

Therefore, if we consider the collection of
all
non-zero eigenvalues of
\Delta
as it acts on entanglement
k
-forms across all choices of
k
, there will be a two-fold degeneracy, as each eigenvalue is paired with the corresponding eigenvalue for its superpartner.

Numerical Experiments

The goal of the present section is to numerically investigate the spectrum of the entanglement Laplacian in simple examples, including states of two qubits and generalized GHZ and W states on three qubits.

The simplest examples to begin with are a product state on two qubits in
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^{(P)}}=\ket{0_{A}0_{B}}\,,

(126)

and an entangled state of two qubits,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^{(E)}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0_{A}0_{B}}+\ket{1_{A}1_{B}}\right)\,,

(127)

Künneth Theorem

As a final application of the entanglement Laplacian, let us comment that it allows us to easily give a new proof of a (known) Künneth theorem for entanglement cohomology of pure states, using different techniques than the discussion in Section 7.6 of
Mainiero:2019enr
. In general, Künneth theorems relate the homology (or cohomology) of a product of objects to the homologies of the objects themselves. The most familiar case might be the formula for singular homology with coefficients in a field
F
, which relates the homology groups
H_{i}
of topological spaces
X
,
Y
, and their product
X\times Y
as

\displaystyle H_{k}(X\times Y;F)\cong\bigoplus_{i+j=k}H_{i}(X;F)\otimes H_{j}(Y;F)\,.

(141)

Taking dimensions on both sides of the formula (
141
) then gives relations for the dimensions of homologies, i.e. for their Poincaré polynomials.

We will focus on the dimensions of entanglement cohomologies associated with tensor products of pure states, giving the argument only in brief schematic form since it closely parallels the corresponding proof for manifolds. Consider two pure states
\ket{\psi}
and
\ket{\chi}
on disjoint collections of subsystems
{\underline{I}}=\{{\underline{1}},\ldots,{\underline{n}}\}
and
{\underline{J}}=\{{\underline{n+1}},\ldots,{\underline{n+m}}\}
, along with their associated pure state density matrices
\rho_{{\underline{I}}}
and
\rho_{{\underline{J}}}
. Let
\Delta_{\psi\otimes\chi}
be the entanglement Laplacian acting on
(k+p)
-forms associated with the pure state
\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\chi}
on the full set of subsystems
{\underline{1}},\ldots,{\underline{n+m}}
, and let
\Delta_{\psi}
,
\Delta_{\chi}
be the individual Laplacians acting on entanglement forms
\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}})
and
\Omega^{p}(\rho_{{\underline{J}}})
, respectively. Given two such entanglement forms

\displaystyle\alpha\in\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{I}}})\,,\qquad\beta\in\Omega^{p}(\rho_{{\underline{J}}})\,,

(142)

one can also naturally identify them as entanglement forms associated with the full tensor product system, simply setting components to zero if they involve any indices belonging to the complement system. Making this identification, one finds that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\psi\otimes\chi}\left(\alpha\wedge\beta\right)=0\,,

(143)

if and only if

\displaystyle\Delta_{\psi}\alpha=0=\Delta_{\chi}\beta\,.

(144)

This follows from the facts about the Hodge star, wedge product, and inner product introduced in
Ferko:2024swt
, such as the fact that the exterior derivative satisfies a Leibniz rule with respect to the wedge product.

We write this schematically as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Harm}^{q}(\psi\otimes\chi)=\bigoplus_{k+p=q}\mathrm{Harm}^{k}(\psi)\otimes\mathrm{Harm}^{p}(\chi)\,,

(145)

where
\mathrm{Harm}^{q}
denotes the space of harmonic entanglement
q
-forms, which is isomorphic to the
q
-th cohomology group by the Hodge isomorphism theorem of
Ferko:2024swt
, and thus

\displaystyle H^{q}(\psi\otimes\chi)=\bigoplus_{i+j=q}H^{i}(\psi)\otimes H^{j}(\chi)\,.

(146)

Taking the dimensions of these cohomology groups and defining the corresponding Betti numbers
b^{k}_{\psi\otimes\chi}
,
b^{i}_{\psi}
, and
b^{j}_{\chi}
, we also see that

\displaystyle b^{q}_{\psi\otimes\chi}=\sum_{i+j=q}b^{i}_{\psi}b^{j}_{\chi}\,.

(147)

Combining the result (
147
), proven either using the general homological arguments of
Mainiero:2019enr
or the use of the entanglement Laplacian, with the results of Section
3
, then gives a rigorous derivation of the Betti numbers for pure states that can be written as arbitrary tensor products of generalized GHZ and W states. For instance, states of the form

\displaystyle\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}_{n,\vec{d}}}\otimes\ket{\text{W}_{n^{\prime},\vec{d}^{\prime}}}

(148)

have Poincaré polynomials determined by (
147
) and the (now proven) expressions for the Betti numbers of GHZ and W states given in Conjectures
1
and
2
. Tensor products of three or more such states have Poincaré polynomials given by iteratively applying (
147
).

4.2
Intersection Numbers

We now turn to the second class of new invariants of interest in this work, which are inspired by certain integrals that measure intersections of submanifolds in geometry.

Review and Motivation

In general, the advantage of having a cohomology
ring
as opposed to mere cohomology
groups
is that the ring structure offers additional information that can sometimes be used to distinguish between objects which have identical cohomology groups. For instance, in singular cohomology, the ring structure is provided by the
cup product
: given a topological space
X
and commutative ring
R
, one can define a map

\displaystyle H^{p}(X,R)\times H^{q}(X,R)\to H^{p+q}(X,R)

(149)

that bilinearly maps a
p
-cochain and a
q
-cochain to a
(p+q)
-cochain. This product endows the collection of cohomology groups with the structure of a graded ring. In ordinary de Rham cohomology, the role of the cup product is played by the wedge product of differential forms, and in entanglement cohomology, the appropriate version of the cup product is defined in equation (
62
).

In the setting of manifolds, another useful feature of the cohomology ring is that it allows us to study
intersections
algebraically, using Poincaré duality. On an oriented closed manifold
M
of dimension
n
, for every closed
p
-form
\omega
there is an
(n-p)
-cycle
N
such that

\displaystyle\int_{M}\omega\wedge\eta=\int_{N}\eta\,,

(150)

for all closed
(n-p)
-forms
\eta
. This yields an isomorphism between the cohomology groups
H^{p}(M)
and the corresponding homology groups
H_{n-p}(M)
. Under this isomorphism, the wedge product (or cup product) is dual to the intersection of submanifolds. Consider two closed, oriented, embedded submanifolds
A
and
B
of the parent manifold
M
, with dimensions
p
and
q
, respectively, such that
p+q=n
. Under the technical assumption that
A
and
B
intersect transversally, which means that their tangent spaces obey
T_{x}A+T_{x}B=T_{x}M
at each point
x\in A\cap B
, the submanifolds
A
and
B
intersect at a finite collection of points
x
, and one can count each such point with a sign
\epsilon_{x}
that measures the orientation of the tangent spaces
T_{x}A
and
T_{x}B
at
x
. One then defines the intersection number,

\displaystyle A\cdot B=\sum_{x\in A\cap B}\epsilon_{x}\,.

(151)

As we mentioned, Poincaré duality gives a relationship between closed
p
-forms and
(n-p)
-cycles. If we wish to compute the intersection number of two cycles
A
and
B
– perhaps first deforming them to other cycles within the same homology classes, to ensure that they intersect transversally – we can first find a closed
q
-form
\omega_{A}
which is Poincaré dual to
A
(as
q=n-p
), and a closed
p
-form
\omega_{B}
representing the Poincaré dual of
B
. The interesting fact is that the intersection number is related to these forms as

\displaystyle A\cdot B=\int_{M}\omega_{A}\wedge\omega_{B}\,.

(152)

This allows us to relate a geometrical quantity, the intersection number, to an algebraic integral of the wedge product of differential forms, which is easier to compute.

Such intersection numbers appear in several contexts in physics. For instance, in string compactifications, one is often interested in Calabi-Yau
3
-folds, which are manifolds
M
of real dimension six. Given a collection of
4
-cycles (or divisor classes)
D_{i}
, one can likewise identify their Poincaré dual
2
-forms
\omega_{i}
, and compute the triple intersection numbers

\displaystyle\kappa_{ijk}=\int_{M}\omega_{i}\wedge\omega_{j}\wedge\omega_{k}\,,

(153)

which measures a signed count of the finite collection of intersection points
D_{i}\cap D_{j}\cap D_{k}
. In type IIA compactifications on a Calabi–Yau threefold,
\kappa_{ijk}
give the classical cubic terms in the holomorphic prepotential for the complexified Kähler moduli, and the corresponding Yukawa couplings are encoded by its third derivatives (up to instanton corrections).

Definition and Basic Properties

Motivated by the utility of this framework, we wish to define and study similar quantities in the setting of entanglement cohomology. We have already defined natural analogues of differential forms on manifolds and their wedge product, and the role of integration over the parent manifold
M
is played by a trace over the full multi-partite Hilbert space. Therefore, given a collection of closed entanglement
k_{a}
-forms

\displaystyle\alpha^{(k_{1})}\in\Omega^{k_{1}}\left(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\right)\,,\ldots\,,\alpha^{(k_{m})}\in\Omega^{k_{m}}\left(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\right)\,,

(154)

such that
\sum_{a}k_{a}=n
, we will define their intersection pairing by

\displaystyle I\left(\alpha^{(k_{1})}\,,\ldots\,,\alpha^{(k_{m})}\right)=\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\left(\alpha^{(k_{1})}\wedge\ldots\wedge\alpha^{(k_{m})}\right)\,.

(155)

In order to eliminate the ambiguity to shift the closed forms
\alpha^{(k_{1})}
by exact forms, we will typically choose them to be representatives of cohomology classes, and for concreteness it is often convenient to use the unique harmonic representative of each such class.

More specifically, we now take an indexed family of the collection of closed forms (
154
), where each one is harmonic and belongs to a distinct cohomology class:

\displaystyle\alpha^{(k_{a})}_{i_{a}}\in H^{k_{a}}\left(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\right)\,,\qquad\Delta\alpha^{(k_{a})}_{i_{a}}=0\,,

(156)

where now
i_{a}=1,\ldots,\dim\left(H^{k_{a}}\left(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\right)\right)
for each
a=1,\ldots,m
. Demanding that each
\alpha^{(k_{a})}_{i_{a}}
belong to a different class within
H^{k_{a}}
eliminates all ambiguity in the choice of differential form, and the value of the trace (
155
) is automatically unambiguous because each such form is closed. We now form the indexed collection of quantities

\displaystyle M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}=I\left(\alpha^{(k_{1})}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\alpha^{(k_{m})}_{i_{m}}\right)\,,

(157)

which we call the intersection numbers. We will sometimes adopt more specific terminology like intersection matrix when
m=2
, or triple intersections when
m=3
.

Although the quantities (
157
) are unambiguously defined for any fixed choice of basis for the cohomology classes, as
M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}
carries indices, it will transform covariantly under changes of basis for each of the vector spaces
H^{k_{a}}
. To eliminate this ambiguity and define fully invariant quantities, we should form singlets by taking products of the
M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}
and contracting all of the indices. For instance, one could compute the quadratic invariant

\displaystyle M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}M^{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}\,,

(158)

which is a scalar under rotations of the bases for any of the
H^{k_{a}}
. The collection of such scalar quantities like (
158
), which are constructed from intersection numbers but are singlets under rotations, are the new invariants that we propose to study.

For instance, in the case of the intersection matrix

\displaystyle M_{ij}=I\left(\alpha_{i}^{(k_{1})},\alpha_{j}^{(k_{2})}\right)\,,\qquad k_{1}+k_{2}=n\,,

(159)

an obvious collection of basis-independent scalars to study are its eigenvalues.

For general tensors
M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}
with many indices, it is not known how to systematically enumerate all independent invariants like (
158
) that can be constructed from
M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}
. This is the question addressed in the mathematical subject of invariant theory, which we have already mentioned. We will not attempt to address this difficult problem here, preferring instead to select a few simple invariants to compute in the examples below.

As in the case of the spectrum of the entanglement Laplacian, since the intersection numbers are constructed only from ingredients within entanglement cohomology that transform naturally under local unitary transformations, it is reasonable to expect that all scalars built from (
157
) are LU invariants. Indeed, an argument for this conclusion is the following. Consider a transformation by a tensor product of local unitary transformations,

\displaystyle U_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}=U_{{\underline{1}}}\otimes\ldots\otimes U_{{\underline{n}}}\,.

(160)

A local operator
\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{i}}}
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{i}}}
transforms as

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{i}}}\longrightarrow U_{{\underline{i}}}\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{i}}}U^{\dagger}_{{\underline{i}}}\,.

(161)

Now let
\alpha\in\Omega^{k}(\rho_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}})
be an entanglement
k
-form, which can be viewed as a tuple of operators acting on
k
-fold tensor products of the local Hilbert spaces (suitably restricted to images of reduced density matrices). Let
U_{{\underline{I}}}\cdot\alpha_{{\underline{I}}}
represent the action of the local unitary (
160
) on a component of
\alpha
, where
{\underline{I}}
is a length-
k
multi-index, which simply acts as (
161
) on each subsystem
{\underline{i}}\in{\underline{I}}
, and let
U\cdot\alpha
represent the full tuple of such transformed components as
{\underline{I}}
ranges over all length-
k
multi-indices. Because the transformation (
160
), by assumption, acts independently on each tensor product factor, by the definition of the wedge product (
62
) one has

\displaystyle U\cdot\left(\omega\wedge\eta\right)=\left(U\cdot\omega\right)\wedge\left(U\cdot\eta\right)\,.

(162)

Therefore, under the action of a local unitary, the intersection pairing (
155
) transforms as

\displaystyle I\left(\alpha^{(k_{1})}\,,\ldots\,,\alpha^{(k_{m})}\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow I\left(U\cdot\alpha^{(k_{1})}\,,\ldots\,,U\cdot\alpha^{(k_{m})}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\left(\left(U\cdot\alpha^{(k_{1})}\right)\wedge\ldots\wedge\left(U\cdot\alpha^{(k_{m})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\left(U\cdot\left(\alpha^{(k_{1})}\wedge\ldots\wedge\alpha^{(k_{m})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\left(U_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\left(\alpha^{(k_{1})}\wedge\ldots\wedge\alpha^{(k_{m})}\right)U_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\left(\alpha^{(k_{1})}\wedge\ldots\wedge\alpha^{(k_{m})}\right)

\displaystyle=I\left(\alpha^{(k_{1})}\,,\ldots\,,\alpha^{(k_{m})}\right)\,,

(163)

where we have used that the unitary transformation acts as
\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\to U_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}\mathcal{O}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}U_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}^{\dagger}
for an operator on the total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{{\underline{1}}\ldots{\underline{n}}}
, and cyclicity of the trace.

Below we will give further evidence for LU invariance by relating some scalars built from intersection numbers to other LU-invariant quantities such as Schmidt coefficients.

Numerical Experiments

As an example, we consider the general Schmidt form

Next let us consider more general multipartite systems. First, given an arbitrary pure state density matrix

5
Conclusion

In this work, we have explored and extended the formalism of entanglement cohomology in two primary directions. First, we have given explicit proofs of two conjectural closed-form expressions for the Poincaré polynomials of generalized GHZ and W states, which were proposed in
Mainiero:2019enr
. Second, we have expanded the class of local unitary invariants that can be computed using entanglement
k
-forms. In addition to the dimensions of the cohomology groups, which are invariants that capture some information about the entanglement structure of a state, we have also defined the spectrum of the entanglement Laplacian and a collection of intersection numbers. Both of these new invariants are motivated by analogous geometrical quantities and capture additional data about entanglement in pure states, such as Schmidt coefficients across bipartitions in the case of certain intersection numbers. We have also studied these new invariants numerically in several examples.

There are many interesting directions for future investigation. One is to study entanglement cohomology for other classes of states, such as the ground states of quantum spin systems.
9
9

9

Some results motivated by this direction will appear in
toappear
.
It would be very interesting if the structure of some unitary invariants constructed from entanglement
k
-forms admitted an interpretation in terms of physical data of such systems, such as the Hamiltonian defining the model. For instance, one could study free chiral fermions on a periodic chain, the SYK model, or the transverse field Ising model. Perhaps the proof technique developed in this work to establish Conjectures
1
and
2
could also be applied to prove exact expressions for the Poincaré polynomials of some such models, at least in certain special cases.

Another avenue for research is a more systematic investigation of the invariant theory of the local unitary invariants that we have constructed here. We have not attempted to find functional relations between, for instance, the data in the spectrum of the entanglement Laplacian and the intersection numbers, but it is possible that these LU invariants are dependent in some cases with party number
n\geq 3
. For instance, one might seek such relations using machine learning, e.g. by training a neural network to predict a subset of our LU invariants from the others, and investigate the model’s performance. Even if one were to sharply characterize a maximal independent subset of all the LU invariants that have been constructed from entanglement cohomology, we have commented that it seems unlikely that it would be complete. There have been many works studying various LU invariants that can be constructed from quantum states – see, for instance, the incomplete sampling of works
PhysRevLett.83.243
;
Makhlin:2000jhd
;
Sudbery:2001jxc
and references therein – and a complete classification of these invariants seems quite challenging. It would be interesting if enriching the formalism of entanglement cohomology with some additional structure, and using this to construct and categorize still more LU invariants, might facilitate further progress on this problem.

Finally, it would be quite intriguing to see whether entanglement cohomology could be generalized to characterize either entanglement in more general quantum systems (such as quantum field theories) or to describe other properties of quantum models. We have remarked that one of the fundamental objects in our construction, the coboundary operator (
41
), essentially takes the form of the simplicial coboundary associated with the
(n-1)
simplex of subsystems, but with coefficients that take values in endomorphisms of certain reduced density matrices, along with an appropriate “twisting” by an inclusion map. It is conceivable that a similar structure with coefficients that take values in a different algebra of operators might be used to classify other resources in quantum systems, such as magic
PhysRevA.71.022316
;
Emerson:2013zse
;
PhysRevLett.118.090501
. One could also replace the role of the
(n-1)
-simplex in this construction with other objects and investigate applications of the resulting formalism. We leave this important direction to future work.

Appendix A
Review of Simplicial Homology

As we have described in the body of this article, entanglement cohomology is closely related to ordinary simplicial cohomology, although with coefficients that take values in the endomorphism algebras of reduced density matrices. The proofs of the forms of the Poincaré polynomials for generalized GHZ and W states in Section
3
simply reduce the computation of cohomology groups to arguments about the
(n-1)
-simplex. In particular, the primary fact which we use to prove Lemma
1
is that the
(n-1)
-simplex is contractible. Here we collect a self-contained review of this statement and related definitions in simplicial homology (dual statements apply to cohomology). This appendix contains only standard material that can be found in any textbook on algebraic topology, such as
hatcher2002algebraic
.

It is convenient to introduce some terminology. Recall that a
homotopy
between a pair of continuous functions
f,g:X\to Y
from a topological space
X
to another topological space
Y
is a continuous function
F:X\times[0,1]\to Y
such that
F(x,0)=f(x)
and
F(x,1)=g(x)
for all
x\in X
. A subspace
Y
of a topological space
X
is called a
deformation retract
of
X
if there exists a continuous function
F:X\times[0,1]\to X
such that, for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
, we have
F(x,0)=x
,
F(x,1)\in Y
, and
F(y,1)=y
. The function
F
is a homotopy from the identity map on
X
to a function
f(x)=F(x,1)
which is the identity map on
Y
. Intuitively, such a homotopy continuously deforms the larger space
X
onto its subspace
Y
. A space
X
is said to be
contractible
if the identity map on
X
is homotopic to a constant map, or equivalently, if there exists a deformation retract from
X
to a point. A contractible space
X
has trivial homology groups
H_{k}(X)
for all
k>0
.

To build intuition, it may be useful to consider the
geometric

n
-simplex, defined by

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{n}=\left\{(t_{0},\ldots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\mid t_{i}\geq 0\,,\,\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1\right\}\,.

(171)

This subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is simply the convex hull of the unit vectors
e_{0}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
,
e_{1}=(0,1,0,\ldots,0)
,
\ldots
,
e_{n}=(0,\ldots,0,1)
. It is easy to see why the
n
-simplex (
171
) is contractible. Fix a point
p\in\Upsilon^{n}
and define the homotopy
F:\Upsilon^{n}\times[0,1]\to\Upsilon^{n}
by

\displaystyle F(x,s)=(1-s)x+sp\,.

(172)

We see that
F
is a continuous function of its arguments, and for any
x\in\Upsilon^{n}
and
s\in[0,1]
, the quantity
F(x,s)
belongs to
\Upsilon^{n}
because it is a convex combination of two points in a convex set. Furthermore, one has
F(x,0)=x
and
F(x,1)=p
, so the map
F
shows that the point
p\in\Upsilon^{n}
is a deformation retract of the entire space
\Upsilon^{n}
onto a point.
10
10

10

The same proof applies for any convex subset
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, since for any such space the quantity on the right side of (
172
) belongs to
X
by the convexity assumption, so all convex sets are contractible.
This establishes that the
n
-simplex
\Upsilon^{n}
is contractible and thus
H_{k}(\Upsilon^{n})
is trivial for
k>0
.

Let us now see how to rephrase this argument somewhat more abstractly in the language of simplicial homology. An
abstract simplicial complex

K
is a set of vertices
V
along with a set of finite subsets of
V
, called
simplices
, such that if
\sigma\in K
and
\tau\subseteq\sigma
then
\tau\in K
. For instance, let
V=\{1,2,3\}
and suppose that we form the complete simplicial complex which includes all simplices that can be built from
V
.

(a)

The
0
-simplices
\{1\}
,
\{2\}
, and
\{3\}
are points.

(b)

The
1
-simplices
\{1,2\}
,
\{1,3\}
, and
\{2,3\}
are line segments or edges.

(c)

The
2
-simplex
\{1,2,3\}
is a triangle, including its interior, with the given vertices.

Every abstract simplicial complex of this form can be realized as a geometric simplicial complex in some
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The subset
\Upsilon^{n}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
in equation (
171
) is an example of a geometric simplicial complex.

Given a simplicial complex
K
with
n
vertices, we can build a chain complex whose homology is called the
simplicial homology
of the simplicial complex. Fix an ordering of the vertices of
K
and write each
k
-simplex as a list of vertices in square brackets, like

\displaystyle[v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}]\,,\qquad v_{0}<v_{1}<\ldots<v_{k}\,.

(173)

The space
C_{k}(K)
of
k-chains
consists of finite linear combinations of such lists of vertices,

\displaystyle c=\sum_{i}a_{i}[v_{0}^{(i)},\ldots,v_{k}^{(i)}]\,,

(174)

where here we take
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and by convention we choose
C_{k}(K)=0
if
k<0
or
k>n
. One can also allow the coefficients

to take values in other fields, like
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
.

There is an induced ordering on the collection of vertices in any
k
-chain due to the ordering of the vertices themselves, and we choose to define a
k
-chain with opposite ordering to be the negative of its ordered version. For instance,

\displaystyle[v_{1},v_{0}]=-[v_{0},v_{1}]\,.

(175)

The
boundary
operator

\displaystyle\partial_{k}:C_{k}\to C_{k-1}

(176)

is defined on any

\displaystyle\partial_{k}[v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}]=\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}[v_{0},\ldots,\widehat{v}_{i},\ldots,v_{k}]\,,

(177)

where as usual the hat indicates that this vertex is omitted from the list.

We can then define homological notions in the usual way. Elements of
C_{k}
which are annihilated by
\partial_{k}
belong to

\displaystyle Z_{k}=\mathrm{ker}(\partial_{k})

(178)

and are called
cycles
. Elements of
C_{k}
which are themselves boundaries belong to

\displaystyle B_{k}=\mathrm{im}(\partial_{k+1})

(179)

and are thus called
boundaries
. The definition of the boundary operator ensures that
\partial_{k}\circ\partial_{k+1}=0
, which we sometimes write as
\partial^{2}=0
, suppressing indices. In particular,
B_{k}\subset Z_{k}
, so we can define the quotient as the

\displaystyle H_{k}=\frac{Z_{k}}{B_{k}}\,.

(180)

Let us now see how a homotopy like (
172
) is encoded at the level of this chain complex. We write
C_{\bullet}=\left(\ldots\to C_{k+1}\overset{\partial_{k+1}}{\to}C_{k}\overset{\partial_{k}}{\to}C_{k-1}\to\ldots\right)
to represent an abstract chain complex. A
chain map

f:C_{\bullet}\to C_{\bullet}
is a collection of functions
f_{k}:C_{k}\to C_{k}
which commutes with the boundary operation in the sense that

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\circ f_{k}=f_{k-1}\circ\partial_{k}\,\text{ for all }k\,.

(181)

Given two such chain maps
f,g:C_{\bullet}\to C_{\bullet}
, a
chain homotopy

h
between

and

is a collection of maps

such that

\displaystyle f_{k}-g_{k}=\partial_{k+1}h_{k}+h_{k-1}\partial_{k}

(182)

for each

. We suppress indices and write this formula as a single identity for the complex,

\displaystyle f-g=\partial h+h\partial\,.

(183)

The existence of such a chain homotopy can often be used to show that certain homology groups must vanish. At the level of abstract chain complexes – which need not yet correspond to the simplicial complex of a space – suppose that there exists a chain homotopy
h
between

and

. This means that

\displaystyle\mathrm{id}=\partial h+h\partial\,.

(184)

We wish to show that the homology of the chain complex is trivial in positive degrees, which means that every cycle is a boundary. Suppose that
z\in Z_{k}
so that
\partial z=0
. Using the identity (
184
), one finds that

\displaystyle z=\left(\partial h+h\partial\right)z=\partial hz+h\partial z=\partial(hz)\,,

(185)

where we have used
\partial z=0
to drop the second term in the identity. But then the result

immediately implies that
z\in B_{k}
is a boundary. So we conclude that

\displaystyle H_{k}=\frac{Z_{k}}{B_{k}}=0\,,

(186)

and the chain complex has trivial homology groups

for

.
