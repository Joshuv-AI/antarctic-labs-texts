A Graph-Based Test-Harness for LLM Evaluation
Jessica Lundin, Guillaume Chabot-Couture

Abstract
We present a first known prototype of a dynamic, systematic benchmark of medical guidelines for 400+ questions, with 3.3+ trillion possible combinations, covering 100% of guideline relationships. We transformed the WHO IMCI handbook into a directed graph with 200+ nodes (conditions, symptoms, treatments, follow-ups, severities) and 300+ edges, then used graph traversal to generate questions that incorporated age-specific scenarios and contextual distractors to ensure clinical relevance. Our graph-based approach enables systematic evaluation across clinical tasks (45-67% accuracy), and we find models excel at symptom recognition but struggle with triaging severity, treatment protocols and follow-up care, demonstrating how customized benchmarks can identify specific capability gaps that general-domain evaluations miss. Beyond evaluation, this dynamic MCQA methodology enhances LLM post-training (supervised finetuning, GRPO, DPO), where correct answers provide high-reward samples without expensive human annotation. The graph-based approach successfully addresses the coverage limitations of manually curated benchmarks. This methodology is a step toward scalable, contamination-resistant solution for creating comprehensive benchmarks that can be dynamically generated, including when the guidelines are updated. Code and datasets are available at https://github.com/jessicalundin/graph_testing_harness

Introduction & Background

1.1
Motivation

Rigorous evaluation of language models faces a critical challenge: the distribution gap between application-specific text and existing benchmark datasets. This gap encompasses both context (domain, localization, complexity) and the coverage (tasks, content). Current medical benchmarks rely on human curation, which is resource-intensive and typically results in incomplete coverage of specific medical guidelines.

MCQA Benchmark datasets serve dual purposes: training new models and evaluating across models. The test split has widespread utility as a yardstick for comparison across models. While vignettes and multi-turn conversation with evaluation rubrics

15

are critical for more-closely resembling the real-world scenarios; MCQA will always have a role, similar to the realities of teachers and students in classroom settings: MCQA is less ambiguous, easy to grade, and allows rapid evaluation.

While there are shifts in paradigms in the transformer model pipeline, the training and validation splits of MCQA benchmarks have continued to play an essential role. In post-training, supervised finetuning has undeniable utility in SOTA health domain models. More specifically within alignment, MCQA provides GRPO with naturally ranked outputs where correct answers serve as high-reward samples and incorrect options as progressively lower-reward samples, enabling the algorithm to learn from relative quality differences without expensive human ranking of responses.

The WHO guidelines are an ideal use case for creating specific benchmark datasets and testing methods because there is tremendous need for AI to support and supplement scarce healthcare workers in low and middle income countries (LMIC). WHO guidelines are often specific to the country, requiring custom evaluation to most accurately measure model performance.

Methods

2.1
Graph Construction

We transform the WHO IMCI handbook
[
17
]
into a directed graph structure. The handbook, an 80-page document containing flowcharts and checklists for childhood illness management, is parsed to extract medical entities and their relationships. The resulting graph contains 200+ nodes and 300+ edges in respiratory, gastrointestinal, nutritional, and infectious diseases.

The graph schema consists of five node types:

•

Condition
(31 nodes): Medical conditions with age range attributes (0-2 months for young infants, 2-60 months for children)

•

Symptom
(79 nodes): Observable clinical indicators (e.g., “fast breathing”, “convulsions”)

•

Treatment
(84 nodes): Medical interventions (e.g., “give oral Amoxicillin for 5 days”)

•

FollowUp
(15 nodes): Monitoring schedules (e.g., “3 days”, “7 days”)

•

Severity
(4 nodes): Triage classifications (severe, moderate, mild, none)

Four edge types connect these nodes:

•

INDICATES
: Symptom → Condition

•

TREAT
: Condition → Treatment

•

FOLLOW
: Condition → FollowUp

•

TRIAGE
: Condition → Severity

The pipeline dynamically generates questions using 4 templates for each of 5 question types that maintain clinical relevance while ensuring variability. The random age generation is within the condition’s valid range (e.g., 0-8 weeks for young infants, 2-60 months for children). Contextual distractors (Appendix
C.2
) are sampled from the same age range. The correct answer is shuffled each time the questions are generated.

The dynamic generation process creates novel questions, of 3.3+ trillion possible combinations (Appendix
E
), while maintaining consistent difficulty and clinical relevance. This addresses the contamination problem plaguing static benchmarks, where models may have seen evaluation questions during training—while enabling unlimited question variation for robust statistical analysis.

2.3
Evaluation Setup

We conduct baseline inference evaluation to assess out-of-the-box model performance for proprietary (GPT-o4-mini), open-weights (GPT-OSS-20B, Llama3.1-8B, Phi3-3.8B, Qwen3-1.7B), fine-tuned (MedGemma-4B) and open-source (OLMo2-7B) models. Models receive questions in a standardized format with explicit instructions to respond with only the letter (A, B, C, or D) corresponding to the correct answer. We measure accuracy per question type with uncertainty over the template variations.

Key Findings:
(1) The closed-source o4-mini achieves 67.5% overall accuracy, leading open-source and open-weights models across categories. (2) Symptom->condition questions show highest performance (40.9-81.9%), suggesting models better recognize symptoms than prescribe treatments. (3) Condition to treatment, severity, and follow-up questions prove most challenging (38.5-64.1%), indicating poor grasp of clinical protocols.

Unlike human-curated benchmarks, our dynamic graph-based method ensures complete coverage of all guideline relationships, consistent terminology from source documents, reduced data contamination through automated generation, and scalability to other medical guidelines.

Limitations of this work include the reliance on the graph as the source of truth. If there are inaccuracies in the graph these will be transferred to the benchmark questions. We consider here only text modality and MCQA questions, without patient vignettes and multi-turn conversation.

4
Conclusion

This is the first known systematic benchmark prototype of medical guidelines, utilizing a graph-based test harnesses to systematically evaluate LLMs. By transforming medical guidelines into queryable graphs, we achieve 100% comprehensive coverage that is impossible through manual curation. The dynamic nature of the testing harness allows that new questions (with different ages and distractors) can be generated, including for updates in the guidelines. While the baseline inference of models provides initial scores, the true value is in the granular performance among relationship types, revealing systematic capability and weaknesses in clinical protocol understanding.

In this introduction of the test harness, the methodology is applied WHO guidelines in the health domain; however, the graph-based approach is extensible, addressing the gap between general-purpose benchmarks and real-world applications.

The WHO IMCI handbook

is processed to extract structured medical knowledge. Initially we tested methods to extract the contents with pdf parsers and LLMs into a graph schema; however, we found that the automated pipelines missed information. We opted to manually annotate the graph components for this work. The handbook’s flowcharts (e.g., Figure

•

•

Treatment protocols including dosages and durations

•

•

B.2
Graph Schema Implementation

The graph is implemented using NetworkX’s MultiDiGraph structure, allowing multiple edges between nodes. Node attributes include:

•

type
: One of {Condition, Symptom, Treatment, FollowUp, Severity}

•

age_range
: For Condition nodes only, values are “0-2” or “2-60”

•

name
: The medical entity name as extracted from IMCI

Edge attributes consist solely of
type
indicating the relationship. The graph construction process ensures no orphan nodes exist and that each node participates in at least one relationship.

C.2
Distractor Generation Algorithm

This section describes the systematic approach for generating clinically appropriate distractors in the IMCI multiple-choice question assessment framework. The distractor generation algorithm prioritizes clinical validity through age-stratified selection. For each question requiring
k=3
distractors, the system first identifies all conditions sharing the same age range as the target condition, creating an age-appropriate candidate pool.

The distractor sampling process can be formally described using graph theory notation. Let
G=(V,E)
represent the IMCI knowledge graph, where
V
is the set of all nodes (conditions, symptoms, treatments, severity, follow-ups) and
E
is the set of directed edges representing clinical relationships.

For a given question with correct answer
v_{correct}
of type
\tau
and target condition
c_{target}
with age range
\alpha
, the age-appropriate distractor pool
P_{\tau,\alpha}
is defined as:

P_{\tau,\alpha}=\begin{cases}C_{\alpha}\setminus\{v_{correct}\}&\text{if }\tau=\text{Condition}\\
\bigcup_{c\in C_{\alpha}}N_{\tau}(c)\setminus\{v_{correct}\}&\text{if }\tau\in\{\text{Symptom, Treatment, FollowUp}\}\\
\{u\in V:\text{type}(u)=\text{Severity}\}\setminus\{v_{correct}\}&\text{if }\tau=\text{Severity}\end{cases}

(1)

where
C_{\alpha}=\{c\in V:\text{type}(c)=\text{Condition}\land\text{age\_range}(c)=\alpha\}
represents all conditions with age range
\alpha
, and
N_{\tau}(c)
denotes the neighborhood of condition
c
for node type
\tau
:

N_{\tau}(c)=\begin{cases}\{u\in V:(u,c)\in E\land\text{type}(u)=\tau\}&\text{if }\tau=\text{Symptom}\\
\{u\in V:(c,u)\in E\land\text{type}(u)=\tau\}&\text{if }\tau\in\{\text{Treatment, FollowUp}\}\end{cases}

(2)

The final distractor set
D
is then selected as:

D=\text{sample}(P_{\tau,\alpha},k)

(3)

where
k=3
and
\text{sample}(\cdot,k)
denotes random sampling without replacement. The IMCI knowledge graph demonstrates sufficient connectivity and node diversity within each age range such that
|P_{\tau,\alpha}|\geq k
for all generated questions, ensuring age-appropriate distractors are consistently available.

Appendix E
Question Generation Capacity Analysis

This appendix presents a comprehensive analysis of the theoretical question generation capacity of the IMCI clinical reasoning framework, examining the combinatorial possibilities when considering template variations, age-specific adaptations, and distractor combinations.

The current question generation system has produced 438 questions with the following distribution: Condition→Symptom (122), Symptom→Condition (122), Condition→Treatment (130), Condition→Severity (37), and Condition→Followup (27). The system employs 4 templates per question type, incorporates 35 unique age specifications, and covers 224 unique clinical conditions.

Based on the IMCI knowledge graph structure, the theoretical maximum question variations are calculated using:

V_{total}=\sum_{c\in\text{Conditions}}\sum_{t\in\text{Types}}Q_{c,t}\times T\times A_{c}\times D_{c,t}

(4)

Where
V_{total}
represents total possible variations,
Q_{c,t}
is base questions for condition
c
and type
t
,
T
is templates per type (4),
A_{c}
is age variations for condition
c
, and
D_{c,t}
represents distractor combinations calculated as
\binom{P_{c,t}}{3}=\frac{P_{c,t}!}{3!(P_{c,t}-3)!}
where
P_{c,t}
is the size of the age-appropriate distractor pool.

The analysis of 38 conditions reveals a theoretical maximum of 3,354,629,170,764 possible question variations (
3.35\times 10^{12}
). The current implementation utilizes only 0.0000% of this theoretical capacity (
1.31\times 10^{-8}
). Age range analysis shows significant differences in generation potential: 0-2 months contributes 62 base questions yielding 2,580,264 variations, while 2-60 months contributes 376 base questions yielding 3,354,626,590,500 variations, representing 99.92% of the total theoretical capacity.

Two illustrative examples demonstrate this variability. For Local Bacterial Infection (0-2 months): 11 base questions with 9 age variations and sample distractor pools of 12 symptoms and 18 treatments yield 563,400 total variations. For Very Severe Disease (2-60 months): 25 base questions with 25 age variations and sample distractor pools of 59 symptoms and 60 treatments yield 211,205,840,100 total variations.

The analysis reveals several clinical implications. The vast combinatorial space of
3.35\times 10^{12}
variations demonstrates the rich complexity inherent in pediatric clinical reasoning. Age-dependent complexity is evident as the 2-60 month range contributes the vast majority of variations, reflecting increased clinical complexity in older infants and children. The 4-template system multiplies variation potential by 400% compared to single-template approaches. Large age-appropriate distractor pools create exponential increases, with
\binom{60}{3}=34,220
combinations for treatment-rich conditions.

Methodological limitations include clinical appropriateness constraints that may reduce practically viable variations, age-specific symptom manifestations creating dependencies not fully captured in combinatorial calculations, and real-world clinical scenarios having probabilistic rather than uniform distributions. The current implementation achieving 438 questions from a theoretical maximum of
3.35\times 10^{12}
variations suggests substantial room for systematic expansion while maintaining clinical validity and educational value.
