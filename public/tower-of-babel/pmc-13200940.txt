Nomogram and machine learning models for predicting the risk of delirium in ICU patients with NSTEMI
Authors: Dan Zuo, Yunpeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Delirium is a frequent and clinically consequential complication among patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). Early risk stratification in patients with non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) remains challenging. We aimed to develop and validate prediction models for ICU delirium in NSTEMI patients using Boruta-based feature selection and machine learning (ML) approaches, and to compare ML performance with a nomogram model. We retrospectively identified adult ICU patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with NSTEMI in the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database, and included only those with no delirium within the first 24 hours of admission and no prior history of delirium. A total of 392 patients were included (mean age 72.2 ± 12.6 years; 64% male) and categorized into a delirium group (n = 201) and a non-delirium group (n = 191). Clinical characteristics and laboratory indices were extracted. Participants were randomly split into a training cohort (70%) and a validation cohort (30%). The Boruta algorithm was applied in the training cohort for feature selection and identification of relevant predictors. A nomogram was constructed using Boruta-selected variables. In parallel, the same features were incorporated into ML models; model discrimination and clinical utility were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis. SHapley Additive exPlanations were used to quantify and visualize feature contributions for the best-performing model. Boruta identified 9 key variables: systolic blood pressure, serum calcium, pulse oximetry, serum sodium, creatinine, serum chloride, anion gap, blood urea nitrogen, and body temperature. In the validation cohort, CatBoost achieved the best discrimination among ML models, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.743 (95% CI = 0.653–0.833). The nomogram demonstrated lower discrimination (validation area under the curve = 0.61). SHapley Additive exPlanations analyses indicated that the Boruta-selected variables contributed differentially to delirium prediction, supporting model interpretability. In ICU patients with NSTEMI, a Boruta-informed CatBoost model showed moderate predictive performance and outperformed a nomogram constructed from the same predictors. This interpretable ML approach may facilitate early identification of patients at high risk of delirium and support timely preventive strategies.

1. Introduction

Delirium is an acute disorder of attention and cognition that commonly complicates critical illness and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality.[1] In intensive care unit (ICU) cohorts, delirium prevalence remains high (particularly among patients requiring mechanical ventilation) and delirium independently predicts longer ICU and hospital length of stay as well as increased short and long-term mortality.[2–4] By contrast, delirium documented among patients admitted with acute coronary syndromes (ACS)/myocardial infarction is generally less frequent overall but not rare in high-risk or very elderly subgroups: for example, prospective data in octogenarians with non-ST-segment elevation ACS report incident delirium in up to ~7% (with higher rates in frail or comorbid patients).[5]

Patients admitted to cardiac ICUs after ACS, including non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), are not exempt from this risk. Observational studies on patients with ACS and myocardial infarction have reported a significant increase in delirium among those requiring long-term intensive care, which is associated with prolonged hospital stay and higher mortality rates.[6] Despite this, prognostic research dedicated specifically to delirium risk in NSTEMI patients admitted to the ICU remains limited.

Delirium in critically ill cardiac patients is multifactorial. Established precipitating and predisposing factors include advanced age, baseline cognitive impairment, hemodynamic instability, hypoxemia, metabolic and electrolyte disturbances, renal dysfunction, infection and sedative exposure; many of these factors are common in patients managed for NSTEMI in the ICU.[7–11] Traditional risk scores and isolated clinical predictors have modest discrimination for delirium when applied to heterogeneous ICU populations, which constrains early identification and targeted prevention in high-risk cardiac subgroups.

Recent methodological advances (particularly the application of rigorous feature-selection techniques and tree-based ensemble models) offer an opportunity to improve individualized risk stratification using routinely available clinical and laboratory data. Feature-selection algorithms such as Boruta can reduce dimensionality while preserving relevant predictors, and interpretable frameworks that combine high-performance learners with model-agnostic explanation tools permit both robust discrimination and transparent, clinically meaningful interpretation of variable contributions. Large contemporary efforts to predict ICU delirium and other acute brain-dysfunction outcomes have demonstrated that interpretable machine learning (ML) approaches can achieve improved performance relative to conventional methods while yielding actionable insights for clinicians.[12–15]

Against this background, there is a clear need for a validated, interpretable prediction tool tailored to NSTEMI patients admitted to the ICU that uses early, routinely collected data to identify those at greatest risk of developing delirium. The present single-center retrospective study therefore aims to use Boruta-guided variable selection and interpretable, tree-based modeling to develop and internally validate a delirium risk model for ICU patients with NSTEMI, and to compare the performance and clinical utility of the resulting ML model with a conventional nomogram constructed from the same predictors. Such a tool could facilitate proactive monitoring and timely implementation of delirium-prevention strategies in a high-risk cardiovascular population.

2. Methods

2.1. Data sources

This retrospective observational study was conducted using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, a publicly available, de-identified clinical repository comprising detailed inpatient and ICU data from a large tertiary academic medical center in Boston, Massachusetts, Unites States of America, spanning 2008 to 2019. MIMIC-IV contains high-granularity information for each hospital admission, including demographics, diagnoses, vital signs, laboratory measurements, medications, procedures, and ICU charted events, and has been widely used to support reproducible critical care research.

Database access was granted after completion of the required training and credentialing process; 1 author (Yunpeng Wang) obtained authorized access (certification No. 65133086). The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement.[16]

The MIMIC-IV project was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. Because the database is fully de-identified, the requirement for additional informed consent was waived by the overseeing ethics committees. Eligible admissions were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 9th and 10th Revision (ICD-9 and -10) diagnosis codes. Specifically, NSTEMI and delirium were ascertained using ICD-10 codes I214 and F05, respectively.

2.2. Participants and study variables

Adult ICU admissions with a diagnosis of NSTEMI were screened from MIMIC-IV. Patients were included if they met all of the following criteria: age ≥ 18 years at the time of admission; ICU length of stay > 24 hours; for patients with multiple ICU admissions during the study period, only the first ICU stay was retained to avoid within-patient correlation; absence of delirium event within first day; patients had no history of delirium; availability of sufficient clinical data for analysis.

Data were extracted from MIMIC-IV using structured query language in Postgre structured query language. Variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature and were obtained from the first 24 hours following ICU admission when applicable. The extracted covariates comprised: demographics: age, sex, marital status, and body weight; comorbidities: hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, heart failure, CKD, and ARF, identified using diagnosis codes recorded during the hospitalization; laboratory parameters: hemoglobin, white blood cell count, platelet count, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), and other routinely available biochemical indices; vital signs: heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure, body temperature, and peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2) by pulse oximetry.

Missing data handling: To mitigate bias and instability arising from incomplete data, variables with > 20% missingness were excluded from subsequent analyses. For the remaining variables (missingness < 20%), missing values were imputed using a k-nearest neighbors Imputer, thereby preserving the sample size for model development and validation.

Variables with > 20% missingness: creatine kinase-myocardial band and cardiac troponin T. Because of their high missingness these 2 cardiac-biomarker variables were excluded from downstream modeling (feature selection and predictive modeling), but they remained in descriptive Table 1 for transparency.

2.3. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between candidate predictors and the occurrence of delirium. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the independent-samples t test for continuous variables.

2.3.1. Feature selection

In the training cohort, the Boruta algorithm with 1500 trees was applied for feature selection. Briefly, Boruta is a random forest (RF)-based all-relevant feature-selection method that iteratively compares the importance of original variables with that of randomly permuted “shadow” variables; predictors that consistently outperform their shadow counterparts are retained as relevant features. All Boruta procedures were implemented using the “Boruta” package in R (version 4.5.1).

We assessed collinearity among the Boruta-selected predictors by computing a pairwise Spearman correlation matrix for continuous predictors and calculating variance inflation factors from the multivariable logistic regression model. In our analysis none of the Boruta-selected variables showed problematic multicollinearity: all pairwise correlations were moderate and all variance inflation factors were below 5. Therefore we retained all Boruta-selected predictors in the multivariable logistic regression and in subsequent ML models.

2.3.2. Nomogram development and performance assessment

For the nomogram, we then entered all Boruta-selected predictors simultaneously into a multivariable logistic regression model, and that the multivariable logistic model coefficients are adjusted for all Boruta-selected covariates. Model discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves. Model calibration was evaluated using calibration plots with 1000 bootstrap resamples. decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to examine clinical utility across a range of threshold probabilities. A 2-sided P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

2.3.3. ML model development and interpretation

In addition, predictors selected by the Boruta algorithm were entered into ML analyses. Integrating ML with conventional statistical approaches has become increasingly common in medical data analysis: traditional statistical methods offer theoretical grounding and interpretability, whereas ML methods excel in handling complex patterns and automated modeling.

Within the “mlr3” framework, multiple classification models were constructed, including logistic regression, support vector machine, gradient boosting machine, neural network, RF, XGBoost, k-nearest neighbors, AdaBoost, Light-gradient boosting machine, and CatBoost. The area under the curve (AUC) was used as the primary metric for selecting the optimal model, while other performance indicators provided complementary evaluations. To minimize evaluation bias, 10-fold cross-validation was used for resampling and model assessment.

To improve interpretability of the best-performing ML model, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to quantify global feature importance. SHAP, a state-of-the-art approach for interpreting tree-based models, leverages game theory to aggregate local feature contributions and explain model behavior at the global level. This method not only ranks features by their overall importance but also provides insight into how each feature influences individual predictions.

3. Results

3.1. Characteristics of participants

A total of 392 ICU patients with NSTEMI from the MIMIC-IV database were included and classified into the delirium group (n = 201) and the non-delirium group (n = 191) (Table 1). Overall, baseline comorbidities and demographic characteristics were largely comparable between the 2 groups, with no significant differences in hypertension, heart failure, CKD, diabetes, marital status, age, or body mass index (all P > .05).

Compared with the non-delirium group, patients in the delirium group had a lower prevalence of hyperlipidemia but a markedly higher prevalence of ARF. Regarding laboratory findings, the delirium group showed higher white blood cell, higher red cell distribution width, higher glucose, higher anion gap (AG), higher BUN, and higher Cr, while having lower calcium level and lower activated partial thromboplastin time.

For vital signs, the delirium group had lower SBP, higher SpO2, and slightly higher body temperature. No significant between-group differences were observed in body mass index, platelet, hemoglobin, sodium, potassium, chloride, prothrombin time, international normalized ratio, creatine kinase-myocardial band, cardiac troponin T, heart rate, diastolic blood pressure, or respiratory rate (all P > .05).

3.3. Construction of the nomogram and assessment of model performance

The 9 predictors retained by the Boruta selection procedure were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model and translated into a nomogram to estimate the probability of delirium in ICU patients with NSTEMI (Fig. 3). In the nomogram each predictor is assigned a point value proportional to its regression coefficient; the sum of these points corresponds to an individualized predicted probability of delirium, thereby enabling straightforward bedside risk stratification.

Nomogram for prediction of incident delirium in ICU patients with NSTEMI. Points are assigned for each of the 9 Boruta-selected predictors (SBP, Ca, SpO2, Na, Cr, Cl, AG, BUN, T); the sum of points maps to the predicted probability of delirium on the probability scale at the bottom. To use the nomogram, locate a patient’s value for each predictor, read the corresponding points, sum the points, and convert the total to a predicted risk of delirium. AG = anion gap, BUN = blood urea nitrogen, Ca = calcium, Cl = chloride, Cr = creatinine, ICU = intensive care unit, Na = sodium, NSTEMI = non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, SBP = systolic blood pressure, SpO2 = peripheral capillary oxygen saturation, T = body temperature.

In the training cohort the model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC = 0.794, 95% CI = 0.742–0.846), whereas performance declined in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.614, 95% CI = 0.51–0.718), indicating reduced discriminatory power when applied to the held-out data (Fig. 4). Bootstrap-corrected calibration curves (bootstrap = 1000) showed overall concordance between predicted and observed delirium risk, with only modest departures from the ideal line at higher predicted probabilities in both sets (Fig. 5). Taken together, these results suggest that the nomogram yields reasonable risk estimates within the derivation sample but that prediction accuracy is attenuated on internal validation, highlighting the need for further refinement and independent external assessment.

The nomogram provided greater net benefit than both “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies over a clinically relevant interval of threshold probabilities, with the net benefit being larger in the training set than in the validation set. This pattern indicates that the model may be useful for informing risk-stratified management decisions, although the reduced benefit in the validation cohort underscores that prospective or external validation is required before routine clinical application.

Comparison across the supplementary metrics in Table 3 shows that the CatBoost model yielded a more favorable overall balance: it achieved an accuracy of 0.709, sensitivity of 0.800, specificity of 0.614, precision of 0.686, F1 score of 0.738, and an AUC of 0.743. Although several alternative algorithms reached similar performance on 1 or 2 individual metrics, none matched CatBoost’s combination of high sensitivity and acceptable specificity. For example, some models traded off sensitivity for improved specificity, which would reduce false positives but increase the risk of missed delirium cases, while others attained marginally higher precision at the expense of recall. Taken together, these results suggest that CatBoost provides the most practical balance for identifying ICU patients with delirium in this cohort.

3.5. SHAP analysis

To increase transparency of the CatBoost model, we applied SHAP to quantify feature contributions and to visualize how individual predictors influence delirium risk (Fig. 10).

SHAP-based interpretation of the CatBoost model. (A) global feature importance (mean absolute SHAP values) ranking predictors by overall contribution to model output. (B) SHAP bee swarm plot showing the distribution and direction of each predictor’s impact on individual predictions (color indicates feature value). (C) example waterfall plot decomposing a single patient’s prediction into additive SHAP contributions (positive/negative). (D) force plot illustrating how individual features push the baseline probability toward higher or lower predicted delirium risk. SHAP = SHapley Additive exPlanations.

The global importance bar plot (Fig. 10A) identifies serum calcium as the single largest contributor to model predictions. Other influential variables included SpO2, BUN, body temperature, serum sodium, AG, SBP and Cr. These features produced the largest mean absolute SHAP values, indicating they were the most important drivers of predicted delirium probability across the cohort.

The bees warm plot (Fig. 10B) shows the distribution and directionality of each feature’s effect on individual predictions. For calcium, lower measured values tended to push the model output toward a higher predicted probability of delirium, whereas for BUN and Cr the effect was generally in the opposite direction (higher values increasing predicted risk). Reduced SpO2 and lower SBP were also associated with upward shifts in predicted probability. The wide vertical spread for several predictors highlights inter-patient heterogeneity and suggests that the model captures complex, nonlinear relationships rather than simple linear associations.

The waterfall plot (Fig. 10C) decomposes the prediction for an example subject, showing how the baseline risk is adjusted by positive and negative SHAP contributions from individual variables; in this example, low Ca, low SpO2 and elevated BUN were the dominant positive contributors. The force plot (Fig. 10D) presents the same information in an alternative layout, emphasizing how a few strong contributors can outweigh multiple smaller opposing effects to produce a net high-risk prediction.

In sum, SHAP analysis both prioritizes clinically plausible risk factors and provides case-specific explanations that may facilitate interpretation and acceptance of the model in practice. The results underscore the potential relevance of calcium homeostasis and cardiorespiratory and metabolic derangements in the algorithm’s delirium risk assignments and support further investigation into how these insights might inform bedside risk stratification.

4. Discussion

In this study we developed and interpreted a CatBoost ML model to predict delirium in ICU patients with NSTEMI (n = 392) and compared its performance with a traditional nomogram. The CatBoost model showed superior discrimination and calibration compared with the nomogram, with a higher AUC and better net benefit on decision analysis. Model interpretability was addressed using SHAP, which identified serum calcium as the single most influential predictor of delirium; arterial oxygen saturation, markers of renal dysfunction (BUN and Cr), and other indicators of physiological instability also contributed substantially. Patient-level SHAP force plots demonstrated how individual variable values shifted predicted delirium probability, supporting individualized risk explanation and potential bedside use.

We identified several plausible biological and clinical explanations for the model’s emphasis on serum calcium, oxygenation, and renal indices. Dysregulation of calcium homeostasis can alter neuronal excitability, intracellular signaling, and blood-brain barrier integrity and can modulate neuroimmune responses: mechanisms that plausibly lower the threshold for acute brain dysfunction.[17,18] Reduced arterial oxygenation (lower PaO2 or SpO2) reflects cerebral hypoxemic stress and impaired oxygen delivery, which have been associated with higher delirium incidence in critically ill patients.[19–21] Elevated BUN and Cr indicate renal impairment with accumulation of metabolic toxins, electrolyte and acid–base disturbances, and systemic inflammation, all of which can contribute to encephalopathy.[22–25] Collectively, these observations are consistent with prior clinical and mechanistic work linking metabolic, respiratory, and renal derangements to acute brain dysfunction in critical illness.

We note that several predictors retained by our modeling (for example, low serum calcium, reduced SpO2, and markers of renal dysfunction) are also commonly reported as delirium risk factors in broader ICU and medical populations. Therefore, these variables may reflect shared pathophysiologic pathways to acute brain dysfunction (such as metabolic derangement, hypoxemia and toxin accumulation) rather than being unique to NSTEMI. Nonetheless, the present model was derived specifically from an ICU cohort with NSTEMI and therefore captures how these risks present and combine in this clinical context. NSTEMI-related factors (for example, myocardial ischemia with attendant systemic inflammation, hemodynamic compromise or cardiogenic shock, urgent revascularization procedures, and exposure to antithrombotic or vasoactive medications) may modify both the prevalence and the prognostic weight of otherwise general predictors. Clarifying which associations are truly specific to NSTEMI will require direct comparison with noncardiac ICU cohorts and prospective external validation.

While the nomogram provides a familiar, transparent scoring tool that clinicians can apply at the bedside, our results indicate that the CatBoost model outperforms the nomogram in discrimination (higher AUC) and offers additional advantages. Crucially, SHAP analysis bridges the gap between predictive performance and interpretability: it surfaces which features drive risk both at the cohort and individual levels, allowing clinicians to see why the model assigns a high or low delirium probability for a given patient. Thus, the CatBoost model combines improved predictive accuracy with actionable explanations, addressing a key barrier to clinical adoption of complex ML models.

An accurate, interpretable delirium prediction tool could inform early preventive strategies (e.g., targeted monitoring, adjustments in sedation/analgesia, optimization of oxygenation and electrolytes, renal support considerations) in high-risk ICU patients with NSTEMI. The individualized explanations from SHAP plots may help clinicians focus on modifiable contributors in a specific patient, supporting precision preventive or diagnostic efforts. Given that the CatBoost model outperformed the nomogram, adopting such an ML approach (with appropriate integration into workflows and safeguards) has the potential to improve risk stratification beyond conventional score-based tools.

Major strengths include the direct comparison between a modern, high-performing ML algorithm and a conventional nomogram, and the use of model-agnostic explanation methods (SHAP) to render predictions transparent at both the cohort and patient levels. The relatively compact set of routinely measured predictors enhances potential clinical applicability, and demonstration of superior AUC supports the added value of the ML approach for delirium risk prediction in this population.

This study is retrospective and observational, so residual confounding cannot be excluded. The sample size is modest (n = 392), and the cohort derives from a single database, which may limit external generalizability; prospective, multicenter validation is needed. Some potentially important predictors for delirium (for example, detailed medication and sedative exposure, baseline cognitive status, and standardized delirium-prevention bundle adherence) were not included or may have incomplete capture, which could affect model performance and interpretation. We also acknowledge 2 additional limitations related to outcome ascertainment and cohort selection. First, delirium was ascertained using ICD coding in MIMIC-IV; administrative codes have limited sensitivity for delirium and the condition is frequently underdiagnosed and undercoded in routine practice. This may have led to outcome misclassification, underestimation of delirium incidence, and attenuation or distortion of some predictor–outcome associations. Second, we excluded patients with a documented delirium episode within the first 24 hours after ICU admission in order to model incident delirium. However, this approach may omit prevalent or very early-onset delirium cases, who may represent a particularly high-risk subgroup and whose exclusion could introduce selection bias and limit generalizability to all NSTEMI ICU admissions. Sensitivity analyses that include early delirium cases, or analyses stratified by time-to-onset, would help clarify how inclusion criteria affect predictor performance and overall incidence estimates. Finally, although SHAP improves interpretability, the causal relationship between identified predictors and delirium cannot be inferred from these associations.

5. Conclusions

The CatBoost model provided better delirium prediction than a traditional nomogram and, when combined with SHAP interpretation, offered transparent, individualized risk explanations. Serum calcium, oxygenation, and renal markers emerged as key contributors to predicted risk. Future work should focus on external and prospective validation, incorporation of additional clinically relevant variables (e.g., sedative exposure, baseline cognitive function), assessment of impact on clinical decision-making and patient outcomes, and pragmatic implementation studies that integrate model outputs into ICU workflows while monitoring for equity and safety.
