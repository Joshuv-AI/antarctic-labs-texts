Artesunate directly targeting MMP9 to reduce angiogenesis and increase CD8 + T cell infiltration inhibits hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Ning Wang, Liang Yao, Xiaoying Dong, Xiaoqian Man, Bo Liu, Huiling Li, Jie Sun, Rujiao Jiang, Meihua Guo, Jianlei Bi, Aiguo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtesunate (ART) is one of the semi-synthetic derivatives of artemisinin. It is currently mainly used in the clinic for the rescue of cerebral malaria and various critical malaria. Recent studies have shown that it has a wide range of anti-tumor effects. However, its treatment for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and its specific mechanism remain unclear.PurposeThis study aims to investigate ART therapeutic effects on HCC and elucidate its molecular mechanisms.MethodsThis study explored the inhibitory effect of ART on HCC through in vivo and in vitro experiments. Cellular experiments evaluated the effects of ART on HCC cell lines (HepG2, H22, Hepa1-6) using CCK-8, Annexin/PI staining, wound healing, and Immunofluorescence. In vivo, ART was tested in HepG2 xenografts and H-ras12V transgenic mice, with tumor growth monitored by Doppler ultrasound. Multi-omics (transcriptomics, network pharmacology, single-cell sequencing) identified MMP9 as a key target. Mechanisms were explored via molecular docking/dynamics, thermal shift assays, and Western blot analysis. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence confirm CD8 + T cell infiltration.ResultsART inhibited the proliferation of HCC cell lines and promoted its apoptosis. In vivo experiments showed that it had a certain inhibitory effect on the development of immune-deficient mice transplanted tumors. Continuous monitoring of tumor growth by Doppler ultrasound found that its effect was more obvious in the immune-competent spontaneous H-ras12V mouse model of liver cancer, especially for the development of small tumors. Mechanistically, ART can inhibit tumor growth by inhibiting the classic PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, which is consistent with the results in other tumor studies. At the same time, ART can directly target MMP9 and promote its degradation, thereby inhibiting tumor’s angiogenesis. To explain why its effect was more pronounced in immune-competent mice, we found ART can significantly increase the tumor infiltration of CD8 + T cells and its effect is also achieved by degrading MMP9.ConclusionOur findings reveal that ART inhibits HCC tumorigenesis and progression by targeting MMP9 to suppress angiogenesis and enhance CD8+ T cell infiltration. This study provides a mechanistic basis for the potential clinical application of ART in HCC therapy and identifies MMP9 as a promising therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04323-1.Keywords: ART, MMP9, CD8+ T, HCC, Angiogenesis

Introduction

HCC, accounting for 90% of primary liver cancers, represents a major global health burden, ranking as the sixth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Approximately 80% of cases occur in Asia and Africa, where hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is the predominant risk factor, while non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) drives HCC incidence in Western countries [1, 2]. By 2025, the annual number of new cases is projected to exceed one million [3]. Due to the lack of early symptoms, most HCC patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, rendering curative treatments ineffective. Current first- and second-line therapies, including anti-angiogenic agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), yield limited clinical benefits. Although the combination of ICIs and anti-angiogenic therapy extends median survival to 19.2 months, the objective response rate (ORR) remains modest (30–35%), and nearly 60% of patients experience disease progression within 12 months [4]. Thus, developing more effective therapeutic strategies for HCC patients refractory to standard treatments is an urgent unmet need.

ART, a semi-synthetic derivative of artemisinin, is the active component of the traditional Chinese herb Artemisia annua, which has been historically used to treat fever, malaria, and jaundice. ART exhibits outstanding pharmacological advantages including high efficacy, low toxicity, minimal drug resistance, and excellent blood-brain barrier permeability [5]. Beyond its antimalarial properties, ART exhibits broad pharmacological effects, including anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, antifibrotic, anti-angiogenic, and immunomodulatory activities [6–8]. A recent study also highlights the therapeutic benefits of ART in the treatment of obesity and related comorbidities [9]. ART targets MD2 for its antifibrotic and antihypertrophic effects [10]. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that ART inhibits the occurrence and development of various tumors. Artesunate can effectively block the DDX39B-TRIM28 interaction and inhibit EMT transformation to block the metastasis of small cell lung cancer [11]. Artesunate disrupts ribosomal RNA biogenesis and inhibits ovarian cancer growth by targeting FANCA [12]. Artesunate synergizes with sorafenib to induce ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma [7].Recently, A study also reported that artesunate inhibits the occurrence and development of liver cancer by inhibiting the TLR signaling pathway [13]. Therefore, the role of artesunate has been newly developed in various diseases. However, its mechanisms in HCC suppression remain incompletely understood.

Angiogenesis is a hallmark of cancer progression, providing oxygen and nutrients to support tumor growth while facilitating metastasis. Although anti-angiogenic therapies prolong progression-free survival, acquired resistance often limits their impact on overall survival [14]. Recent studies highlight ART’s anti-angiogenic potential [6, 15], but its precise mechanisms in HCC remain elusive. MMP9 plays an important role in angiogenesis [14].At the same time, Clinical data indicate that high MMP9 expression correlates with reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration in HCC [16], underscoring its dual role as a therapeutic target. Strategies targeting the tumor microenvironment to simultaneously inhibit angiogenesis and enhance T cell infiltration represent a promising avenue to overcome immunotherapy resistance.

Constitutive activation of the Ras signaling pathway is a common driver of HCC [17], which plays pivotal roles in regulating critical cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, and physiological functions [18]. Our group previously established an H-ras 12 V transgenic mouse model that recapitulates human HCC pathogenesis, providing a robust platform for mechanistic studies [19].

Here, we demonstrate that ART suppresses HCC cell proliferation, induces apoptosis, and inhibits angiogenesis in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, ART directly binds and degrades MMP9, thereby normalizing tumor vasculature and enhancing CD8+ T cell infiltration. Our work elucidates a novel anti-tumor axis of ART and positions MMP9 as a therapeutic target for HCC.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

HepG2 cell line (Cobioer Biosciences, China), Hepa1-6 cell line (Servicebio, China) and H22 cell line (Procell Life Science, China) were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. HepG2 and Hepa1-6 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), while H22 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Upon reaching 80–90% confluency, adherent cells (HepG2, Hepa1-6) were harvested using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, USA). Suspension H22 cells were collected by centrifugation (5 min, 1,000 rpm).

Plasmid and stable cell line construction

To investigate MMP9 function, MMP9-overexpressing (MMP9 OE) and MMP9-knockdown (MMP9 Sh) Hepa1-6 cell lines were generated using lentiviral vectors (GeneChem, China). For experimental treatments, cells were exposed to ART (Macklin, China) at concentrations of 10, 20, or 50 µM for 24–48 h prior to analysis.

MMP9 2.1kbp cDNA sequence were cloned into the pcDNA3.1-N-3xFlag vector. Constructs were confirmed by DNA sequencing. Short hairpin targeting sequences for MMP9 (5‘-AAGGACGGCAAATTTGGTTTC-3’) were cloned into the pGLVH6.1-puro vector. Vectors contain scramble shRNA (Genepharma #C06006) were used as negative controls.

Cell viability assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ to 1 × 10⁴ cells per well. Following attachment under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂), cells were treated with varying concentrations of ART for 24–48 h. One hour prior to the end of treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Abbkine, China) was added to each well. Cells were then incubated for 1–4 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Blank controls (cell-free medium) and negative controls (solvent-treated cells) were included. Cell viability was calculated using the following formula: (OD treatment/OD control) × 100%.

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis was analyzed by Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining. HepG2, H22, or Hepa1-6 cells treated with ART were washed with ice-cold PBS and resuspended at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Aliquots (100 µL) of cell suspension were stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL propidium iodide (PI; Servicebio) for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. Samples were immediately analyzed on a BD Accuri™ C6 Plus flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) with 488-nm excitation, detecting FITC emission at 530/30 nm and PI at 610/20 nm.

Flow Cytometric Analysis of Spleen/Tumor CD8⁺ T Cells. Fresh spleen and tumor tissues were mechanically dissociated through 70-µm strainers, washed with PBS, and subjected to erythrocyte lysis. After cell counting, aliquots of 3 × 10⁶ cells were stained with anti-CD4-FITC, anti-CD3-PE, and anti-CD8-APC antibodies (1 µL each; BioLegend) for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were then resuspended in 1 mL flow cytometry staining buffer, centrifuged, and resuspended in 200 µL PBS for acquisition on a CytoFLEX SRT flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). CD3⁺CD4⁻CD8⁺ T-cell populations were analyzed by sequential gating on FSC/SSC parameters followed by fluorescence channels.

Scratch wound healing assay

HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 10⁵ cells/well and cultured to 90–100% confluency. After serum starvation for 12 h in serum-free medium, uniform scratches were generated using sterile 200 µL pipette tips. Wound areas were documented at 0, 24, and 48 h using phase-contrast microscopy (100× magnification) with five fixed fields per well. Migration rates (%) were quantified by measuring scratch closure with ImageJ software. Data represent three independent experiments (n = 3 per group).

Cell climb assay

Hepa1-6 cells and HepG2 were seeded onto sterile coverslips placed in 24-well plates at 1 × 10⁵ cells/well and cultured for 24 h under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂) for adhesion. Experimental groups were treated with complete medium containing ART (10, 20, or 50 µM), while control groups received vehicle medium (equal volume with matched DMSO concentration ≤ 0.1%). After 24–48 h of incubation, experiments were terminated for subsequent analysis (n = 3 per group).

Proteasome inhibition experiment

Hepa1-6 cells were pretreated with MG132 (10 µM, MedChemExpress) for 1 h and subsequently exposed to ART (20 µM) for 12 h. MMP9 protein levels were then analyzed by Western blot as described above.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp) for 30 min at room temperature (tissue sections proceeded directly to staining). After three times of 5 min PBS washes, samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (150 µL/well): anti-MMP9 (1:500, Servicebio, GB11132), anti-CD31 (1:500, Servicebio, GB11063-2), anti-Ki67 (1:500, Servicebio, GB111499), anti-CD8 (1:500, Servicebio, GB15068), Caspase3 (1:500, Proteintech, 19677-1-AP), GZMB (1:200, Proteintech, 13588-1-AP), IFN-γ (1:200, Proteintech, 29788-1-AP), and TNF-α (1:500, Proteintech, 17590-1-AP). Following PBS washes, reaction enhancer (PV-9000; ZSGB-Bio) was applied (50 µL/sample) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescent secondary antibody (A23420, 1:200; Abbkine) was then added (50 µL/sample) for 1 h under light-protected conditions. After PBS washes, sequential staining was performed: actin cytoskeleton with IF488-phalloidin (G1248-100T; Servicebio) for 30 min, and nuclei with DAPI (BMU107-CN; Abbkine). Slides were mounted with antifade medium and imaged by fluorescence microscopy (n = 3 per group).

Cell viability assay (AO/PI staining)

HepG2, Hepa1-6 and H22 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well. Following adherence, cells were treated with varying concentrations of ART for 24–48 h. An aliquot of the cell suspension was then stained with AO/PI staining solution according to the manufacturer’s protocol, incubated protected from light at room temperature, and analyzed using a Countstar Mira BF/FL Plus cell counter. Viable cells (unstained, green) and dead cells (PI-positive, red) were quantified within defined fields; total cell concentration and viability percentage were calculated based on dilution factors and counting chamber specifications.

Animal studies

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Dalian Medical University (Approval No. AEE24341). Subcutaneous xenograft models were established by injecting 2 × 10⁶ HepG2 or Hepa1-6 cells in 100 µL PBS into the right flank of 6-8-week-old male BALB/c nude or C57BL/6J mice (SPF Biotechnology). Orthotopic models were generated via intrahepatic injection of 8 × 10⁶ similarly modified Hepa1-6 cells in 30 µL PBS into 6–8 week-old male C57BL/6J mice. One week post inoculation, tumor-bearing mice were randomized based on tumor volume and administered: (i) saline (vehicle control) or (ii) ART (50 mg/kg, i.p., daily for 28 days). Spontaneous hepatocarcinoma studies utilized 5-month-old male H-ras 12 V transgenic mice (Alb-EP-driven hepatocyte-specific expression) randomized to receive ART (50 mg/kg, i.p., BID for 37 days) or saline. Tumor progression was monitored twice weekly by caliper measurements (subcutaneous) or Doppler ultrasonography (spontaneous models). Body weight and survival were recorded throughout the study period.

Doppler ultrasonography and laser speckle doppler

H-ras 12 V mice were anesthetized and maintained at 37 °C on a thermostatic heating pad to minimize physiological variability. Laparotomy was performed to expose the liver, and tumor vasculature was imaged using a high-frequency linear probe (10–18 MHz) on an S7SCI ultrasound system (Mindray). B-mode imaging captured tumor morphology, while color Doppler mapping visualized vascular architecture and flow direction. Quantitative hemodynamic parameters (flow velocity/volume) were acquired in real-time with laser Doppler flowmetry (moorVMS-LDF2; Moor Instruments). Instrument settings (frequency/gain) were optimized during multiplanar scanning to ensure signal fidelity.

Aortic ring assay

Thoracic aortas harvested from adult male SD rats were immersed in ice-cold PBS containing penicillin-streptomycin. Under stereomicroscopic guidance, adventitial layers were dissected and vessels sectioned into 2–3 mm rings. Rings were randomized into three groups (n = 6 rings/group): (i) basal medium control, (ii) 20 µM ART, (iii) 50 µM ART. Rings were embedded in Matrigel (Servicebio) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (control medium contained equivalent DMSO). After 3 or 7 days cultured at 37 °C/5% CO₂, microvessel outgrowth was monitored by inverted microscopy (40×magnification).

HUVEC tube formation assay

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; Servicebio) were seeded at 1.5 × 10⁴ cells/well onto Matrigel-precoated 48-well plates maintained on ice. After polymerization at 37 °C/5% CO₂ for 30 min, cells were treated with: (i) vehicle control (0.1% DMSO), (ii) 10 µM, (iii) 20 µM, or (iv) 50 µM ART (n = 3 replicates/group). Capillary-like structures were documented at 3, 6, and 12 h post-seeding using inverted phase-contrast microscopy (100×). Five randomly selected fields per well were imaged, and tubular nodes quantified with ImageJ.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from cells (100 µL RIPA buffer per 1 × 10⁶ cells) or tissues (1 mL RIPA per 100 mg) using ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were quantified by Bradford assay (Beyotime). Aliquots (30 µg protein) were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h, membranes were probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: β-actin (1:1,000, Abmart, #M20011), anti-MMP9 (1:500, Servicebio, GB11132,), anti-CD31 (1:500, Servicebio, GB11063-2), AKT (1:600, Proteintech, 10176-2-AP), PI3K (1:1000, Proteintech, 20583-1-AP), Phospho-PI3K (1:1,000, CST, #4228), Phospho-Akt (Ser473) (1:1,000, Elabscience, #13038). Following TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature: Goat anti-rabbit (1:5,000, Bioworld, BS20241-Y), Goat anti-mouse (1:5,000, Bioworld, BS20242-Y). Protein bands were visualized by ECL and quantified using Image Lab™ software (Bio-Rad). Experiments were independently repeated three times.

In vivo imaging of Cy5.5-ART distribution

Cy5.5-ART in vivo imaging. Whole-body distribution kinetics of Cy5.5-conjugated artesunate (Cy5.5-ART) were monitored in H-ras 12 V transgenic mice using an IVIS Spectrum preclinical imaging system (PerkinElmer). Mice were depilated 24 h prior to imaging. Cy5.5-ART (2 mg/kg, dissolved in physiological saline containing 5% DMSO) was administered via tail vein injection. At serial time points post injection (0 min, 5 min, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 15 h, 24 h, and 36 h), animals were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane and positioned in the imaging chamber. Fluorescence signals were acquired using an excitation/emission wavelength pair of 675 nm/720 nm (Cy5.5 channel) with an exposure time of 500 ms.

Bioinformatic analysis

For bioinformatic analysis, MMP9 expression and survival in TCGA-LIHC were analyzed using the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/). Gene expression datasets were downloaded in MINiML format from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Non-normalized datasets underwent log2 transformation. All datasets were uniformly standardized using the normalize.quantiles function from the R (v4.0.3) preprocessCore package. Probe IDs were converted to gene symbols based on corresponding platform annotation files; probes mapping to multiple genes were excluded, and expression values for genes represented by multiple probes were averaged. For distinct batches within the same dataset and platform, batch effects were removed using the removeBatchEffect function from the R limma package.

Network pharmacology and molecular docking

To analyze the binding affinities and modes of interaction between the drug candidate and their targets, AutodockVina 1.2.2, a silico protein–ligand docking software was employed. The molecular structures of ENMD-2076 was retrieved from PubChem Compound (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The 3D coordinates of KDR (PDB ID, 5EW3; resolution, 2.5 Å) and BIRC5(PDB ID, 4AOI; resolution, 1.9Å) were downloaded from the PDB (http://www.rcsb.org/pdb/home/home.do). For docking analysis, all protein and molecular files were converted into PDBQT format with all water molecules excluded and polar hydrogen atoms were added. The grid box was centered to cover the domain of each protein and to accommodate free molecular movement. The grid box was set to 30 Å × 30 Å × 30 Å, and grid point distance was 0.05 nm. Molecular docking studies were performed by Autodock Vina 1.2.2 (http://autodock.scripps.edu/). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS. The Amber ff14SB force field was employed for the protein, and the TIP3P model was used for water molecules. Ligand parameters were generated using the GAFF force field via the ACPYPE tool. A production simulation of 100 ns was conducted. Trajectory analysis and visualization were performed using Molstar, and 2D ligand-protein interaction diagrams were subsequently generated using ProteinPlus.

Single-cell sequencing

The samples were downloaded from the GSE149614 series with 10× scRNA-seq data. We processed single-cell sequencing data of diabetes using the following methods. First, we transformed the 10× scRNA-seq data into a Seurat object using the “Seurat” R package, and excluded cells with poor quality by calculating the percentage of mitochondrial or ribosomal genes as quality control. The “FindVariableFeatures” program identified the top 2000 highly variable genes, and we performed dimensionality reduction and cell subpopulation identification using principal component analysis (PCA) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) with these 2000 additional genes. To find marker genes for each cluster, we used the “Find All Markers” tool. We annotated the cell subpopulations based on the marker genes identified in the study.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v9.5). Appropriate parametric tests (e.g., Student’s t-test, one-way or two-way ANOVA) were selected based on data distribution characteristics and experimental design. The software was also employed for correlation analyses and, where applicable, regression modeling. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with asterisks denoting significance levels (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001). Data visualization and presentation of statistical results were generated using Prism.

Results

ART suppresses viability, proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis in HCC cells

We first assessed the effect of ART on cell viability using the CCK-8 assay. Three HCC cell lines (HepG2, H22, and Hepa1-6) were treated with increasing concentrations of ART (10, 20, and 50 µM) for 24–48 h. ART inhibited the growth of all three cell lines in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 1A). AO/PI staining further confirmed the dose- and time-dependent suppression of HCC cell proliferation (Fig. 1B, C). Given its potent inhibitory effect at 20 µM, this concentration was selected for subsequent experiments. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that ART treatment for 48 h significantly increased the percentage of early and late apoptotic cells in HepG2, H22, and Hepa1-6 cultures (Fig. 1D, E, F, G). To evaluate ART’s impact on migration and proliferation, we performed a scratch wound assay and Ki67 staining in HepG2 cells. ART markedly inhibited cell migration (Fig. 1H, I) and reduced Ki67 expression (Fig. 1J, K), indicating suppression of proliferative activity. Collectively, these results demonstrate that ART exerts potent anticancer effects by inhibiting proliferation, inducing apoptosis, and impairing migration in HCC cells.

ART inhibits tumor growth in HepG2 xenograft and H-ras12 V spontaneous HCC models

To evaluate ART’s anti-tumor efficacy in vivo, we established a HepG2 subcutaneous xenograft model in nude mice (Fig. 2A). Mice were randomized into control and ART-treated groups (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal (i.p.), daily for 28 days). ART significantly suppressed tumor growth, achieving a tumor growth inhibition (TGI) rate of 28.2%, and reduced tumor weight (Fig. 2C and G). No significant body weight fluctuations were observed (Fig. 2B), indicating minimal systemic toxicity.

Given that spontaneous HCC models better recapitulate human disease complexity, we next employed H-ras 12 V transgenic mice. To track ART biodistribution, we synthesized an ART-Cy5.5 fluorescent probe. In vivo imaging revealed strong accumulation in the liver, kidneys, and tumor tissues, with clearance by 36 h (Fig. S1A–C). Five-month-old H-ras 12 V mice were stratified by baseline tumor volume (> 10 mm³ or < 10 mm³) and treated with ART (50 mg/kg, i.p., twice daily for 37 days). Tumor progression was monitored weekly via Doppler ultrasound. ART significantly inhibited the growth of large tumors (TGI = 67.6%; Fig. 2J and L) and exhibited even greater efficacy against small tumors (TGI = 79.3%; Fig. 2M and O). No adverse effects on body weight were noted (Fig. 2I). These findings demonstrate that ART effectively suppresses both established and nascent HCC tumors in vivo.

Transcriptomic profiling of tumor samples demonstrated ART-induced global gene expression changes, with principal component analysis (PCA) showing clear separation between control and ART-treated groups along PC1 (54.1% variance; Fig. 3E). Differential expression analysis identified 2,956 significantly altered genes (1,602 downregulated, 1,354 upregulated) (Fig. 3F, G). KEGG pathway enrichment highlighted PI3K-AKT signaling as the most suppressed pathway (Fig. 3H), corroborated by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 3I). Western blot validation in both cell lines and tumor tissues confirmed ART-mediated inhibition of PI3K-AKT signaling (Fig. 3J–M), establishing this pathway as a key mechanistic target.

ART directly binds MMP9, a highly expressed metalloproteinase in HCC

In order to explore the downstream pathways regulated by artesunate in addition to the classic PI3K/AKT, we used Integrated network pharmacology and transcriptomics identified nine putative ART targets (Fig. 4A, B), including MMP9, a metalloproteinase. Analysis of seven GEO datasets and TCGA-LIHC confirmed MMP9 overexpression in HCC patients versus healthy controls (Fig. 4C, D). Clinically, MMP9 expression stratified patient outcomes: low-MMP9 cohorts showed superior 10-year overall survival (log-rank p = 0.0054) and multivariate Cox regression confirmed MMP9 as an independent risk factor (HR = 1.6, p = 0.0059; Fig. 4E). Single-cell RNA-seq analysis further revealed MMP9 upregulation in tumors (Fig. 4F, S4), nominating it as a therapeutic target.

To elucidate the molecular interactions between ART and MMP9, we performed comprehensive molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Structural analysis revealed that ART stably occupies the MMP9 active site, forming a critical hydrogen bond and salt bridge with ARG424, while being further stabilized by surrounding hydrophobic residues (Fig. 5A-C). MD simulations demonstrated exceptional complex stability, as evidenced by minimal fluctuations in RMSD, RMSF, and Rg parameters, along with well-preserved secondary structure elements throughout the trajectory (Fig. 5D-E). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed concentrated conformational sampling, indicating system stabilization (Fig. 5F-G). Complementary thermal shift assays provided biochemical validation, with ART-treated MMP9 exhibiting significantly higher residual protein levels under thermal denaturation conditions compared to controls (Fig. 5H-I). These findings collectively demonstrate that ART binding confers structural stability to MMP9, thereby protecting against thermal-induced degradation or inactivation.

ART suppresses MMP9 expression both in vitro and in vivo

Our findings demonstrate that ART directly targets MMP9 for regulation. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed dose-dependent suppression of MMP9 in Hepa1-6 cells (Fig. 6A), while Western blotting confirmed time- and dose-dependent inhibition of MMP9 protein expression (Fig. 6B, C). Consistent results were obtained in H-ras 12 V model through immunofluorescence Notably, ART treatment reduced MMP9 expression, also upregulated Caspase3 while downregulating Ki67 in tumor tissues (Fig. 6D), Western blotting also confirmed the expression of MMP9 was downregulated in H-ras 12 V tumor (Fig. 6E, F). Mirroring our in vitro observations and confirming ART’s dual mechanism of MMP9 degradation coupled with pro-apoptotic and anti-proliferative effects. To further clarify whether ART reduces MMP9 protein abundance via proteasome-dependent degradation, we performed pharmacological inhibition experiments. Pretreatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 partially restored MMP9 protein levels in ART-treated cells (Fig. 6G, H), indicating that the ART-induced reduction of MMP9 is, at least in part, mediated through the ubiquitin–proteasome system.

ART directly targets MMP9 for degradation, effectively inhibiting angiogenesis both in vitro and in vivo

Given MMP9’s established role in basement membrane degradation, endothelial cell migration, and pro-angiogenic factor release, we investigated ART’s anti-angiogenic potential. HUVEC tube formation assays showed ART dose- and time-dependently inhibited lumen formation (Fig. 7A, B). MMP9 overexpression partially rescued this inhibition, while MMP9 knockdown phenocopied ART’s effects (Fig. 7C, D), suggesting MMP9-dependent mechanisms. Rat aortic ring assays also confirmed ART’s dose-dependent suppression of microvessel growth (Fig. 7E).

In H-ras 12 V models, ART significantly reduced tumor blood flow (Fig. 7F, G) and decreased CD31 expression (Fig. 7H, I). Similar results were observed in subcutaneous xenografts, with ART reducing blood flow and tumors are more likely to rupture due to lack of nutrition (Fig. 7J, K), increasing necrotic areas, and downregulating both Ki67 positive zone and CD31 positve zone (Fig. 7L). Flow cytometry confirmed higher baseline CD8+ T cell levels in C57BL/6J spleens (Fig. 8A), while immunofluorescence showed ART enhanced intratumoral CD8+ T cell accumulation (Fig. 8B).

To further determine whether the increased CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration induced by ART is associated with enhanced cytotoxic immune activity, we performed immunofluorescence staining for key cytotoxic effector molecules, including Granzyme B (GZMB), TNF-α, and IFN-γ, in tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S3, ART treatment markedly increased the expression of GZMB and IFN-γ within tumor tissues compared with the control group. In contrast, TNF-α expression did not show a substantial elevation following ART treatment.

To establish MMP9 as the mechanistic link between ART’s anti-angiogenic and immune-potentiating effects, we employed gain- and loss-of-function approaches. Orthotopic implantation of MMP9-modified Hepa1-6 cells revealed that: (i) ART treatment overcame the tumor-promoting effects of MMP9 overexpression (MMP9 OE), significantly reducing liver tumor weight (Fig. 8C, D); (ii) MMP9 knockdown (MMP9 Sh) phenocopied ART’s therapeutic effects; and (iii) these effects were attenuated in immunodeficient subcutaneous models (Fig. 8E, F). Flow cytometry of orthotopic tumors confirmed enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration in both ART-treated and MMP9 Sh groups (Fig. 8G, H), immunofluorescence assays for CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells in orthotopic tumors of Hepa1-6 consistent with tissue flow results (Fig. 8I), providing direct evidence that ART remodels the tumor immune microenvironment through MMP9 degradation.

Discussion

HCC, which originates from hepatocytes or intrahepatic bile duct cells, is predominantly diagnosed at advanced stages when surgical intervention is no longer viable. Consequently, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and combination regimens have emerged as primary treatment modalities for intermediate-to-advanced HCC. However, these approaches are often associated with significant systemic toxicity and, crucially, the development of therapeutic resistance during prolonged treatment courses.

Recent advances in traditional medicine have revealed numerous botanical agents with unique therapeutic advantages and promising clinical outcomes in HCC management. ART demonstrates superior preclinical safety profiles compared to conventional antiangiogenic agents - notably lacking the dose-limiting hypertension and proteinuria associated with VEGFR inhibitors [21]. The FDA-approved status of ART for malaria treatment provides distinct translational advantages, including well-characterized pharmacokinetics that could accelerate repurposing for HCC. Current mechanistic understanding remains incomplete, with proposed pathways including: (i) RP11/Smad3 axis blockade suppressing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [22]; (ii) synergistic ferroptosis induction when combined with sorafenib [7]; and (iii) Src/STAT3 inhibition coupled with ROS/STAT3 modulation to overcome sorafenib resistance in combination with ginsenosides [23]. Our study systematically elucidates a novel multimodal mechanism wherein ART targets MMP9 to simultaneously suppress tumor angiogenesis and enhance CD8+ T cell infiltration. These findings not only advance fundamental understanding of ART’s anti-tumor properties but also identify MMP9 as a promising therapeutic node for next-generation HCC treatment development.

ART exhibits broad-spectrum anti-tumor activity by suppressing invasion, metastasis, and proliferation of cancer cells, alongside activating ferroptosis, modulating immune responses, inducing apoptosis, and regulating cell cycle progression [24–26]. These mechanisms underlie its therapeutic efficacy in diverse gastrointestinal diseases. Consistent with prior studies, our in vitro data demonstrate that ART dose- and time-dependently inhibits the viability of HCC cell lines. Notably, ART not only triggers apoptosis in HCC cells but also markedly suppresses cell migration and proliferation, indicative of its multi-target anti-tumor properties. Strikingly, ART elicits comparable inhibitory effects across all three HCC models, suggesting its potential as a pan-HCC therapeutic agent—a finding with pivotal implications for clinical translation.

Our animal studies yielded even more compelling evidence. In the HepG2 subcutaneous xenograft model, ART achieved a tumor growth inhibition rate of 28.2% consistent with prior findings by Li [20]. However, conventional liver tumor models-limited to subcutaneous or orthotopic grafts-fail to recapitulate the chronic liver injury cascade inherent to HCC pathogenesis, a critical gap for investigating oncogenic mechanisms or prophylactic drug development. To address this, we employed the clinically relevant H-ras 12 V transgenic HCC model, stratifying mice by initial tumor volume (> 10 mm3, large tumors; <10 mm3, small tumors) for longitudinal monitoring. Strikingly, ART suppressed large tumor growth by 67.6%, while its efficacy was further enhanced in small tumors by 79.3% inhibition. This divergence likely stems from the transgenic model’s preservation of tumor microenvironment (TME) complexity, suggesting ART may exert potent anti-tumor effects via TME modulation.

Mechanistically, integrating transcriptomic sequencing and Western blot analysis, we confirmed that ART markedly suppresses the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, aligning with previous reports. The PI3K-AKT axis serves as a master regulator of cell growth, proliferation and survival, playing a pivotal role in HCC pathogenesis [27].

Building upon these findings, we identified MMP9 as a putative key target of ART. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations revealed stable binding of ART to the MMP9 active site, suggesting functional inhibition through conformational stabilization. These results not only validate MMP9 as a promising therapeutic target in HCC, but also provide a mechanistic foundation for the precision application of ART.

Guided by the ART-MMP9 binding dynamics revealed through molecular docking and simulations, we systematically validated MMP9 regulation by ART using both in vitro and in vivo models. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated significant suppression of MMP9 expression in Hepa1-6 cells and H-ras 12 V-derived liver tumors following ART treatment. Notably, our MG132 rescue experiment provides mechanistic support for the statement that ART promotes MMP9 degradation at the protein level. Together with the observed reduction of MMP9 in vitro and in vivo, these data strengthen the conclusion that ART suppresses MMP9 not only at the expression level but also by accelerating its proteasomal degradation, thereby contributing to impaired angiogenesis and downstream remodeling of the tumor microenvironment.

Tumor angiogenesis is orchestrated by hypoxia, EMT, and activation of tumor-associated macrophages and fibroblasts. This complex process is regulated by multiple molecular players, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), VE-cadherin, and non-coding RNAs. Previous studies have demonstrated ART’s anti-angiogenic effects through inhibition of STAT3 and Src activation in melanoma [28], while Ma. et, al. reported suppression of choroidal melanoma angiogenesis via modulation of the HIF-1α/VEGF/PDGF axis [29]. Chen and colleagues further showed that ART synergizes with sorafenib to inhibit both tumor growth and angiogenesis in non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma [30]. Our study reveals that ART’s anti-angiogenic activity is mediated, at least in part, through MMP9 regulation. MMP9 overexpression partially rescued ART-induced suppression of vascular formation, whereas MMP9 knockdown phenocopied ART’s effects. These findings carry significant clinical implications. Our work establishes that ART disrupts pro-angiogenic signaling through targeted MMP9 degradation, providing a mechanistic basis for its potent anti-tumor efficacy.

Angiogenesis in HCC is regulated by multiple pro-angiogenic mediators, including VEGF, FGF, and extracellular matrix–remodeling enzymes such as MMP9. Previous studies have shown that ART exhibits anti-angiogenic activity by suppressing VEGF expression and VEGFR signaling, thereby inhibiting endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation [31–33]. In addition, MMP9 plays an important role in extracellular matrix remodeling and can regulate the mobilization of matrix-bound pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF and FGF-2 [34, 35]. Therefore, the reduction of MMP9 may further limit angiogenic signaling within the tumor microenvironment.

Most previous studies investigating the anti-angiogenic effects of ART were primarily conducted in in vitro endothelial or tumor cell models. In contrast, our results provide in vivo evidence that ART markedly suppresses tumor angiogenesis in a hepatocellular carcinoma mouse model. Together, these findings suggest that ART inhibits tumor angiogenesis through multiple mechanisms beyond MMP9 alone.

A pivotal finding of our study is that ART-mediated MMP9 degradation concurrently modulates both angiogenesis and the immune microenvironment. This immunomodulatory effect may stem from: (1) MMP9 degradation preserving basement membrane integrity, leading to vascular normalization that facilitates T cell extravasation [36]; and (2) MMP9 inhibition potentiating IFN-γ signaling, thereby enhancing secretion of T cell chemokines such as CXCL9/10 [37]. These findings align with Cancer Research reports on vascular normalization potentiating PD-1 efficacy [38], providing mechanistic rationale for combining ART with immunotherapy. Furthermore, this discovery explains the comparatively weaker anti-tumor effects observed in immunocompromised animal models, underscoring the importance of an intact immune system for ART’s full therapeutic potential.

Importantly, increased immune cell infiltration does not necessarily indicate functional activation. To further evaluate the cytotoxic status of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, we examined the expression of key cytotoxic effector molecules within tumor tissues. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that ART treatment markedly increased intratumoral expression of Granzyme B (GZMB) and IFN-γ, two hallmark molecules produced by activated cytotoxic lymphocytes. GZMB mediates tumor cell apoptosis through perforin-dependent pathways, whereas IFN-γ enhances antigen presentation and promotes immune-mediated tumor elimination. These findings suggest that ART not only promotes CD8⁺ T cell recruitment but also enhances cytotoxic immune activity within the tumor microenvironment.

Interestingly, TNF-α expression did not show a significant increase following ART treatment. Because TNF-α can be produced by multiple immune cell populations, including macrophages and CD4⁺ T cells, its expression may not directly reflect cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cell activation. Therefore, the increased expression of GZMB and IFN-γ likely represents a more specific indicator of enhanced cytotoxic immune responses.

As an approved antimalarial drug with well-established safety profiles, ART offers accelerated clinical translation potential. Notably, accumulating evidence demonstrates synergistic effects when combined with sorafenib, providing novel strategies to overcome resistance in HCC targeted therapy. Our findings carry dual translational implications: (1) circulating MMP9 levels may serve as dynamic biomarkers for predicting ART response, and (2) ART’s unique dual capacity to normalize vascular barriers while enhancing T cell infiltration warrants prioritized clinical evaluation of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor combinations. Several study limitations should be acknowledged. First, while we focused on MMP9, tumor angiogenesis involves complex network regulation where other matrix metalloproteinases (e.g., MMP2) and angiogenic factors (e.g., FGF, PDGF) likely participate. Second, our cell line and animal model findings require clinical validation of MMP9-ART response correlations in patient specimens.

Limitations and future perspectives

Despite the mechanistic insights provided in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although our findings demonstrate that ART promotes proteasome-dependent degradation of MMP9 and consequently suppresses angiogenesis while enhancing CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, the precise molecular events linking ART–MMP9 interaction to ubiquitin-mediated protein turnover remain to be fully elucidated. Future studies will be required to determine whether ART directly promotes MMP9 ubiquitination or modulates upstream regulators of the ubiquitin–proteasome system.

Second, tumor angiogenesis and immune remodeling are regulated by complex signaling networks. While our data identify MMP9 as a central mediator of ART activity, other angiogenic factors and matrix remodeling enzymes may also contribute to the observed anti-tumor effects. Systematic multi-omics approaches and functional validation will therefore be necessary to further define the broader regulatory landscape of ART in the tumor microenvironment.

Finally, although the concentrations of ART used in vitro exceed clinically achievable plasma levels, the pronounced anti-tumor effects observed in multiple in vivo models support the biological relevance of ART in HCC. Future translational studies incorporating optimized drug delivery strategies, such as nanoparticle-based formulations or targeted drug carriers, may further enhance tumor-specific accumulation and therapeutic efficacy. Together, these efforts will help advance the clinical development of ART as a potential therapeutic strategy for hepatocellular carcinoma.

Conclusion

In summary, this study systematically elucidates the dual mechanism by which ART exerts its anti-tumor effects in HCC: through MMP9-targeted degradation that simultaneously suppresses tumor angiogenesis and promotes CD8+ T cell infiltration. These findings provide a robust experimental foundation for clinical translation while expanding our understanding of ART’s pleiotropic anti-cancer properties. Importantly, this work establishes MMP9 inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy for HCC treatment, offering new avenues for combination therapy development.
