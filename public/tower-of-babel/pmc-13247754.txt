D’Agoty’s early anatomical painting of the liver
Authors: Thomas M. van Gulik
Published: 2026

The cover image shows the liver as painted by the French artist and anatomist Jacques Gautier d’Agoty in 1765 (Figure 1). The image is a detail from a large anatomical oil painting in the Wellcome collection in London, depicting dissected male figures with exposed muscles and viscera (1). The liver is depicted detached from the abdomen and placed on the floor in the foreground, rendered with remarkable anatomical accuracy. Whereas most earlier depictions of the liver were printed in black and white, d’Agoty beautifully portrayed the liver in vivid colours. This representation of the liver in d’Agoty’s work is described here for the first time.

The liver depicted by the French artist and anatomist Jacques Gautier d’Agoty in 1765. Wellcome Collection, London (1).

Jacques Gautier d’Agoty (1716–1786) was born in Marseille, the son of a sculptor (2,3). He received his artistic training from Jean-Baptiste Oudry, a famous French painter known for his botanical illustrations and landscapes. The art of colour printing had only recently been developed when, in 1737, d’Agoty moved to Paris to work with Jacob Christoph Le Blon, the inventor of the first engraving and printing process using the primary colours blue, yellow, and red. Together with his sons, d’Agoty further developed and popularized colour engraving and published numerous coloured illustrations.

D’Agoty collaborated with the anatomist Joseph Duverney, renowned for his anatomical dissections (4). Together, they produced anatomical plates depicting the musculature with exceptional precision and vibrant colours. D’Agoty went on to combine detailed anatomical observation with refined artistic skill, creating anatomical illustrations that stood out for both anatomical accuracy and aesthetic quality. His fascination with anatomy—regarded by him as a divine creation—led him to perform his own dissections of human corpses. In 1754, he published the anatomical treatise Anatomie Général, illustrated with 18 plates executed in his sumptuous style, for which he conducted most of the dissections himself.

In the painting, the liver is shown as part of a cluster of upper abdominal organs, including (from posterior to anterior) the inferior vena cava alongside the abdominal aorta, the right and left kidneys with their vascular pedicles, the pancreas and spleen, and parts of the duodenum and stomach. The confluence of the mesenteric and splenic veins, and the right gastric vein which branches off more distally from the portal vein, are depicted. The celiac trunk as well as the splenic artery, the common hepatic artery and left and right hepatic arteries, are shown, with the right gastric artery arising as a branch of the right hepatic artery. The proper hepatic artery appears very short, with an early origin of the gastroduodenal artery.

An artery of unclear origin is seen crossing anterior to the duodenum and splenic vein toward the pancreatic head; this may represent an erroneous depiction of the gastroduodenal artery above the pancreas. The superior mesenteric artery (SMA) is correctly depicted running posterior to the splenic vein, alongside the superior mesenteric vein (SMV). Interestingly, a ‘middle mesenteric artery’ appears to arise from the celiac trunk, although this may be a misinterpretation by d’Agoty. The combination of a celiacomesenteric trunk in which the SMA and celiac trunk share a common origin, and the occurrence of a middle mesenteric artery, is an exceptionally rare anomaly.

The liver itself displays the gallbladder, while the common bile duct is depicted incorrectly as traversing on the anterior side of the duodenum, with an uncertain termination. The falciform and triangular ligaments are rendered as they would appear on dissection.

Although the external anatomy of the liver and upper abdominal organs had been described in detail by the physiologist Albrecht von Haller by the mid-18th century (5), d’Agoty’s work represents one of the earliest true colour renderings of the liver and its adjacent organs. Despite some inaccuracies in the depiction of arterial and biliary anatomy, d’Agoty succeeded in depicting the liver with striking anatomical fidelity and vivid coloration, establishing himself as both a skilled anatomist and a talented artist.
