Health information addiction and older adults’ health product purchases: the mediating role of perceived illness severity and social support
Authors: Xiaochen Wang, Zhongmei Li, Chang Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExisting literature has examined the connection between information addiction and purchase decisions. However, this is the first study to focus on the older adult population and health context. This study specifically investigated the mediating roles of perceived illness severity and perceived online social support in this relationship.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 358 individuals aged 60 years and older in China. Participants’ demographic characteristics were collected. Structural equation modeling was employed to assess health information addiction, perceived illness severity, perceived online social support, and health product purchase decisions.ResultsThe prevalence of health information addiction among the study participants was 42.7%. Perceived illness severity, perceived online social support, and health information addiction were significantly and positively correlated with health product purchase decisions (r = 0.539–0.622, p < 0.001). Health information addiction directly and positively predicted health product purchase decisions with a direct effect value of 0.147. Furthermore, perceived illness severity and perceived online social support played independent and serial multiple mediating roles in the association between health information addiction and health product purchase decisions, with indirect effect values of 0.231, 0.069, and 0.063, respectively.ConclusionHealth information addiction drives older adults’ health product purchases by heightening perceived illness severity and increasing reliance on online social support. To mitigate this effect, interventions should focus on developing senior-centric digital platforms with enhanced accessibility to reduce addictive consumption patterns, creating moderated online health communities to provide reliable social support, and implementing digital health literacy programs to temper excessive perceptions of illness severity. Such strategies would empower older adults to make healthier purchase decisions within digital health ecosystems.Keywords: Health information addiction, Health product purchase decisions, Perceived illness severity, Perceived online social support, Older adults

Introduction

The rapid penetration of the Internet into the daily lives of older adults in China has led to their increased exposure to online health information [1]. While beneficial, such exposure also carries risks such as health information addiction [2]. Characterized by excessive seeking and dependence on health-related information, health information addiction can disrupt daily life and decision-making processes [3, 4]. Older adults are particularly susceptible to being trapped in “information bubbles” created by big data algorithms, which may influence their health product purchase decisions [5]. However, the mechanisms by which health information addiction affects these decisions remain underexplored. This study specifically investigates how health information addiction influences health product purchase decisions among older adults, with a focus on the mediating roles of perceived illness severity and perceived online social support.

Previous studies present conflicting views on the impact of information addiction on purchase decisions. Some argue that it promotes purchase decisions [6], while others maintain that it overwhelms consumers, leading to constant evaluation and comparison, which reduces both the quality and speed of purchasing [5]. Despite these insights, existing research has largely overlooked or neglected several critical aspects that are pertinent to understanding the role of health information addiction in shaping health-related purchase decisions among older adults. First, the unique challenges faced by older adults in managing health information overload and its impact on decision-making remain underexplored [7]. Second, the mediating effects of perceived illness severity and perceived online social support in the context of health information addiction have not been adequately examined [2]. Third, the sequential stages through which health information addiction influences purchase decisions, particularly in health-related contexts, remain poorly understood [8]. For instance, while studies have examined the role of information overload in consumer decision-making [9, 10], few have explored how health information addiction, as a specific form of information overload, interacts with psychological states such as anxiety and risk perception to influence health-related purchase decisions. Additionally, while the role of perceived illness severity in shaping health product preferences has been studied in clinical settings [11], its interaction with health information addiction in the context of online decision-making remains underexplored. Similarly, while perceived online social support has been identified as a critical factor in health-related behaviors [12, 13], its role as a mediator between health information addiction and purchase decisions has not been thoroughly examined, especially among older adults.

To address these research gaps, this study adopts the previously proposed online purchase decision-making process (DMP) model as its core analytical framework. Rooted in consumer behavior theory and applied to general consumer decision contexts, the DMP model traces its theoretical foundation to the classic Engel-Kollat-Blackwell (EKB) model—a foundational framework dividing decision-making into five stages (need recognition, information search, alternative evaluation, purchase decision, post-purchase behavior) [14]—but adds a critical stage: Intention Identification. This addition stems from behavioral decision theory, which notes that, in health contexts, decision commitment is often mediated by cognitive-emotional states (e.g., information overload, anxiety) emerging after alternative evaluation but before final purchase commitment [15, 16]. The six-stage DMP model is defined as: (1) Need Recognition: identifying health needs to be met through purchase; (2) Information Search: actively acquiring health product information via online tools (e.g., search functions); (3) Alternative Evaluation: comparing product attributes using collected information before using shopping cart functions; (4) Intention Identification: forming/adjusting purchase intentions by reviewing cart product details, where information overload or anxiety may weaken decision commitment [17, 18]; (5) Purchase Decision: executing purchase if products meet expectations; (6) Post-Purchase Behavior: evaluating satisfaction and exhibiting behaviors (e.g., repurchasing if satisfied). This model guides the study by locating critical nodes where health information addiction (characterized by compulsive information seeking, therefore impairing decision clarity [19]) may disrupt decision-making. Notably, Intention Identification is a “vulnerable node”: individuals with high health information addiction often experience heightened anxiety or information overload at this stage, destabilizing purchase intentions through detail-reliant cognitive processes [20]. By integrating two context specific constructs - perceived illness severity (amplifying health information needs [21]) and perceived online social support (alleviating/exacerbating cognitive-emotional distress [22, 23]), this study develops a targeted perspective to explore how health information addiction influences purchase outcomes: not by uniformly altering all stages, but by disrupting this critical intention identification stage (where cognitive-emotional states and decision commitment converge). This approach also addresses a key gap: traditional DMP models focus on general consumption contexts and overlook health-specific cognitive-emotional factors [15]. By embedding the two constructs into the framework, this study validates the DMP model’s applicability in health information environments, expanding understanding of how information addiction affects decision-making processes.

Literature review and research hypothesis

Overreliance on health information can exacerbate concerns and perceptions of health risks, thereby prompting people to be more inclined to purchase products or services [24]. According to the theory of planned behavior, individuals’ information processing patterns directly shape their behavioral intentions [25]. Health information addicts often exhibit higher perceptions of health risks [26], which in turn drives them to purchase health products to avoid these risks [27]. For example, frequent exposure to health warnings may trigger changes in personal cognition, prompting consumers to view product purchases as a direct means of solving problems [28]. Therefore, health information addiction caused by information overload may lead to cognitive fixation, directly affecting purchase decisions. Thus, this study therefore proposes the subsequent hypothesis:

H1: Health information addiction is positively associated with health product purchase decisions.

According to protection motivation theory, threat assessment (e.g., perceived severity) serves as a key mediating variable linking information stimuli to protective behavior [29, 30]. Health information addicts may overestimate the severity of health risks owing to prolonged exposure to threat cues [31]. This heightened perception of threat triggers emotional anxiety, prompting consumers to alleviate psychological stress by purchasing health products [32]. Research has shown that health information overload significantly increases subjective assessment of disease severity [33], which has been identified as an important predictor of health product consumption behavior [34]. Therefore, perceived illness severity may act as a psychological pathway from information addiction to purchase decisions. Thus, this study therefore proposes the subsequent hypothesis:

H2: Perceived illness severity mediates the relationship between health information addiction and health product purchase decisions.

Perceived online social support refers to the emotional, informational, and resource support that individuals perceive they receive through their social networks [35]. In online health communities, the main forms of perceived social support are informational and emotional, exchanged through social interactions between members [36]. In health behavior research, perceived social support is often considered a key factor in healthy decision-making [37]. Perceived online social support is used to assess whether patients benefited from social relationships in the context of online health communities [38], as individuals who regularly encounter health information are more likely to seek advice or approval from others in their social circle [39], and this support can mitigate risk perception in decision-making and enhance purchasing confidence [40]. In particular, when it comes to health products, consumers tend to rely on recommendations from others or group consensus to confirm the rationality of their choices [41]. Therefore, health information addiction may stimulate individuals’ dependence on social support, thereby driving their willingness and behavior to purchase health products. Thus, this study therefore proposes the subsequent hypothesis:

H3: Perceived online social support mediates the relationship between health information addiction and health product purchase decisions.

While previous studies have examined several mediating variables between information addiction and product purchase decisions [17, 42], few have investigated the mediating roles of perceived illness severity and perceived online social support in the relationship between health information addiction and health product purchase decisions among older adults. When older adults are overexposed to health information, they may develop health anxiety, which intensifies their assessment of the severity of their illness and drives them to seek emotional support or solutions through social interaction [43]. Simultaneously, obtaining social support (such as advice from friends and family or feedback from online communities) can alleviate consumers’ decision-making anxiety and reinforce purchasing behavior [44]. By exploring the intrinsic relationship between perceived illness severity and social support in the context of health information addiction and product purchase decisions among older adults, this study aims to reveal the dual mediating mechanisms underlying irrational consumption driven by health information overload. This insight can help older individuals establish a scientific framework for processing health information, thereby reducing the risk of excessive consumption caused by cognitive biases, ultimately fostering the synergistic development of active aging and a healthy consumption ecosystem. Thus, this study therefore proposes the subsequent hypothesis:

H4: Health information addiction has indirect positive effects on health product purchase decisions through the sequential mediating effects of perceived illness severity and perceived online social support.

Materials and methods

Data and samples

This study employed a survey of people aged 60 years and above, living in different regions of China. It aimed to explore the relationship between older adults’ excessive reliance on health information (addictive behavior) and their decision-making process regarding the purchase of health-related products using a structured questionnaire that elicited their behavioral patterns and potential psychological mechanisms. Given resource constraints and transportation difficulties, we used a convenience sampling method, using personal networks to reach the target population.

Data collection was conducted through two channels: (1) Electronic questionnaires created on the sojump platform were distributed to the target population via social media platforms such as WeChat and QQ. This method was efficient for reaching participants comfortable with digital tools. (2) Paper questionnaires were administered to eligible elderly participants at their homes through their family members or close contacts (proxies), who were explicitly instructed not to influence the elderly participants’ responses. This approach was implemented because proxies reported some elderly individuals were unable to adeptly use smartphones for questionnaire completion. The proxies assisted by reading the questions aloud, while the elderly participants provided verbal responses that were transcribed onto the paper forms. Ultimately, the researchers collected all completed paper questionnaires, digitized the responses, and consolidated the data with those from the electronic questionnaires.

The sample size was determined using Kendall’s method, which recommends a size of 5 to 10 times the number of items in the scale with the highest item count [45, 46]. The Chinese Internet Addiction Scale Revised Version (CIAS-R), with 26 items, was used as the reference. Accounting for a 20% potential attrition rate, the minimum sample size required was 312. To account for potential incomplete or inconsistent responses, the total number of questionnaires distributed was 400. Invalid responses included those with incomplete items (missing values) and those with a pattern of consistently identical responses across multiple consecutive items (e.g., selecting the third option for multiple consecutive items). After excluding these invalid responses, the final valid sample comprised 358 participants, resulting in a valid response rate of 89.5%. This sample size was considered adequate to guarantee the robustness and reliability of the statistical analyses.

Approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Binzhou Medical University (No.2024-L063). All participants provided written informed consent prior to completing the survey, with participation being entirely voluntary and unincentivized to ensure the objectivity of the data. All data were anonymized and securely stored to protect participants’ privacy. The study data is available upon reasonable request in accordance with the ethical committee guidelines and relevant regulations.

Measures

Health information addiction

Health information addiction was assessed using the Chinese Internet Addiction Scale Revised Version (CIAS-R) developed in Taiwan [47]. As the original CIAS-R measures general internet information addiction, we adapted the items specifically to focus on “health information addiction” for the purpose of this study. Consequently, the questionnaire items (including the examples provided below) were modified to reflect addiction behaviors pertaining to online health information. The scale’s reliability in measuring internet information addiction among older adults has been confirmed [48]. The scale comprises 26 items organized into three aspects: (1) compulsive internet use (5 items), such as “I have tried to spend less time searching for health information online, but I cannot do it”; (2) internet withdrawal and detoxification reactions (5 items), such as “If I go without using the Internet to look for health information for a period of time, I feel uncomfortable”; (3) internet addiction tolerance reactions (4 items), such as “Compared to before, I need to spend more time searching for health information online to feel satisfied”; (4) time management issues (5 items), such as “I am accustomed to reducing my sleep time to have more time searching for health information online”; and (5) interpersonal and health issues (7 items), such as “I find myself spending more time looking up health information online and interacting less with friends around me.” A 4-point Likert scale was used for self-assessment, with “1” indicating “strongly disagree,” “2” indicating “disagree,” “3” indicating “agree,” and “4” indicating “strongly agree.” The maximum total score is 104. According to the diagnostic criteria, participants with a total score of 64 points or higher were diagnosed with health information addiction [49]. In the current study, the overall twenty-six-item scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.942.

Perceived illness severity

Perceived illness severity was measured using a scale to assess individual perceptions of the serious consequences of a disease or health problem [50]. The scale comprises four questions: “Having a serious health condition would be awful”; “It would be alarming if I experienced a significant health problem”; “Developing a health problem would be very distressing”; “If I were to experience a health problem, it would be dreadful.” For each item, participants rated how strongly they agreed with the statement on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). In the current study, this scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of 0.883.

Perceived online social support

Perceived online social support was measured using a scale that included two dimensions [51]: (1) information support (4 items) illustrated by items such as “On the online health communities, some people would offer suggestions when I needed help”; and (2) emotional support (3 items) illustrated by items such as “When faced with difficulties, some people on the online health communities are on my side.” All questionnaire items were measured on a five-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating “strongly disagree” and 5 “strongly agree.” In the current study, the overall seven-item perceived online social support scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.932.

Health product purchase decisions

Consumer health product purchase decision was measured based on [15, 52, 53], each measured using three items and a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). These stages include need recognition (e.g., “I decided to make a purchase out of necessity”), information search (e.g., “Search function in the online shopping platform helps you decide when buying health products”), evaluation of alternative (e.g., “Intending to buy health products before going to the shopping cart function in the online shopping platform helps you compare health products”), intention recognition (e.g., “I often get in the online shopping cart platform to check on product details”), purchase decision (e.g., “I will buy health products from the online shopping platform if the product satisfies me”), and post-purchase behavior (e.g., “I will make a repeat purchase if I am satisfied with the health product I bought”). In the current study, the overall eighteen-item scale measuring the consumer health product purchase decision process demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.966.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 26.0. Continuous data are reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as percentages. Prior to formal analyses, invalid responses were excluded, and t-tests (for continuous variables) and chi-square tests (for categorical variables) were employed to examine whether exclusions correlated with any demographics or key study variables. No statistically significant differences were found between excluded invalid responses and included analytical samples, indicating that exclusions were unrelated to other factors. Therefore, we used listwise deletion to exclude invalid responses. Reliability of the scales was evaluated using Cronbach’s α. Relationships between variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. The normality of the dependent variable (health product purchase decision) was confirmed using P-P plots. A chain mediation model, developed using AMOS version 24.0, positioned health information addiction as the independent variable, health product purchase decision as the dependent variable, and perceived illness severity and perceived online social support as mediators. Bootstrap analysis was conducted to assess the direct, indirect, and total effects within this mediation pathway.

Results

Common method bias testing

To evaluate common method bias, we employed the Harman single-factor method. The analysis showed that four factors had eigenvalues exceeding 1, with the factor that accounted for the highest variance explaining 23.03% of the total variance. As this percentage is below the 40% threshold, common method bias is not significant in our study.

Descriptive statistics

Demographic characteristics for the 358 Chinese older adults who participated in the survey show that 54.7% were female and 83.2% were under 70 years of age. More than half (76%) had an educational attainment of junior high school or below, and the majority were in a stable marital relationship (78.2%). Over half of the participants (87.9%) considered their health condition to be average or poor. Those who scored 64 points or higher on the Health Information Addiction Scale represented 42.7% of the sample. The mean and standard deviation of health information addiction, perceived illness severity, perceived online social support, and health product purchase decisions were 60.97 ± 21.89, 10.56 ± 4.70, 48.20 ± 19.97, and 48.20 ± 19.97, respectively. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the serial multiple mediating effects of perceived health risk severity and perceived online social support on the relationship between health information addiction and older adults’ purchase decisions regarding health products. The rate of health information addiction among the older adults who participated in this survey was 42.7%, which is slightly higher than the internet addiction rate reported among Chinese college students (36.52%) [54]. This may be attributed to older adults’ significantly increased attention to health issues with age, stronger health anxiety, and their tendency to alleviate disease-related worries by continuously accessing health information.

In addition, this study found a significant positive correlation between health information addiction among older adults and their purchase decisions regarding health products. This finding aligns with current research [6]; when older adults are overwhelmed by complex, confusing, and sometimes contradictory online health information, their cognitive resources and emotional states may be significantly affected [55], potentially leading to information overload, decision fatigue, and subsequent anxiety-driven impulse buying [56]. This direct correlation highlights the importance of understanding older adults’ behavior patterns in predicting and guiding their online health-related consumption behavior.

Perceived illness severity plays a key mediating role in the relationship between health information addiction and purchase decisions, which is consistent with the previous study [57]. Frequent exposure to health information online, especially on the consequences of disease and health risks, can easily be internalized by older adults as heightened awareness of their own health vulnerabilities and exaggerated threats [31]. This heightened perception of health threats (i.e., high perceived illness severity) drives individuals to develop strong risk-avoidance motives and a tendency to seek solutions [58]. Therefore, to alleviate health anxiety and insecurity caused by information overload, older adults tend to seek immediate psychological comfort and practical protection by purchasing health products that claim to have preventive or therapeutic effects [22]. Perceived health-risk severity is thus a critical psychological mechanism that transforms information overload into consumer action.

Perceived online social support is an important mediating pathway linking health information addiction and purchase decisions. Older adults who are immersed in online health information often face problems such as difficulty distinguishing between true and false information, and understanding technical terms [2]. This inevitably gives rise to a strong demand for information verification, emotional comfort, and specific advice. To compensate for the decision-making confusion caused by information uncertainty, individuals actively or passively seek support from members of online health communities [39]. Information interpretation, experience sharing, product recommendations, and even emotional encouragement from social circles (i.e., social support) can effectively reduce uncertainty in decision-making, enhance their trust in specific health products and confidence in purchasing, and ultimately lead to purchase behavior [40]. This finding indicates that perceived online social support plays a key catalytic role in converting information exposure into actual consumption.

This study’s value lies in revealing the sequential mediating path by which health information addiction influences purchase decisions, while also extending classic consumer decision-making frameworks to unpack nuanced behavioral mechanisms. Building on the decision-making process, we move beyond linear stage descriptions to operationalize sequential mediating processes that bridge information behavior and consumption decisions. The core chain unfolds as follows: health information addiction first heightens perceived illness severity (functioning as “need recognition” in traditional models), which in turn stimulates online social support seeking (a behavioral response to perceived threats). The supportive feedback obtained from this social interaction then reinforces the perceived rationality and urgency of purchasing health products, ultimately driving the final purchase decision. This sequential mechanism (health information addiction → perceived illness severity (need recognition) → online social support seeking → reinforced purchase intention) highlights two key contributions. First, it clarifies how information addiction reshapes the “need recognition” stage by amplifying threat perception, rather than merely triggering it. Second, it unpacks how social interaction (support seeking) acts as a critical mediator; rather than treating support as a static contextual factor, we show it emerges dynamically from perceived severity and actively reinforces purchase urgency. Collectively, this model demonstrates the synergistic interplay of perceived health-risk severity and online social support, revealing how information addiction cascades through cognitive (perceived severity), behavioral (support seeking), and evaluative (reinforced purchase intention) layers to shape consumption decisions.

This study has several limitations must be acknowledged. First, it used a cross-sectional research design that could not determine the direction of causality. Future research could adopt a longitudinal study design to better understand the causal relationships among health information addiction, perceived illness severity, perceived online social support, and decisions to purchase health products. Second, data were collected through self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Future research could combine objective data (e.g., purchase records, browsing behavior) to improve the reliability of the results. Third, participants in this study were from specific regions, limiting the representativeness of the sample. Future studies should expand the sample scope to include older adults from different social backgrounds and with varying health conditions to improve the universality of the research results. Finally, this study did not measure or include health anxiety, information overload, and other potentially relevant covariates (e.g., psychological factors beyond demographic characteristics) in the analysis. This may restrict the comprehensive understanding of the relationships among the studied variables, as these unmeasured variables could act as mediators or moderators affecting the associations between health information addiction, perceived illness severity, perceived online social support, and health product purchase decisions. Future research could consider incorporating covariates such as health anxiety and information overload into the analytical model to more precisely control for potential confounding effects and enhance the interpretability of the complex relationships among variables.

In summary, this study reveals how health information addiction influences older adults’ purchase decisions regarding health products through their perception of health risk severity and perceived online social support. The findings emphasize the need for interventions targeting health information addiction and enhancing social support for older adults, particularly in the digital age. Future research should focus on exploring these relationships in more diverse and longitudinal settings to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying these effects.

Implications

This research contributes to the theoretical understanding of online health behavior by establishing health information addiction as a potent antecedent that exacerbates perceived health threats among older adults. Importantly, it reveals that this amplified severity perception can dualistically trigger pathways leading to both increased social support seeking and health product consumption. This highlights the need for theoretical frameworks to account for such complex, multifaceted influences of maladaptive information use patterns, particularly within the context of aging populations navigating digital health environments. The identified chain mediation enriches our comprehension of the psychological mechanisms driving health-related decisions in later life.

The identified pathway underscores the vulnerability of older adults within online health information ecosystems that may inadvertently foster addictive usage patterns. The findings call for targeted, proactive measures to support older adults in navigating these spaces more effectively and healthily. First, the recognition that perceived online social support mediates the relationship between severity perception and purchase intent points to the potential of developing and promoting easily accessible, trustworthy, and senior-centric health information and social support platforms. Such platforms should prioritize user-friendly design (e.g., intuitive navigation, adjustable text size, simplified layouts, multimedia options) to reduce information overload and frustration. In turn, this potentially mitigates the drive toward addictive information seeking fueled by heightened severity perceptions. Second, there is a need to foster curated, evidence-based online health communities tailored for older adults. These communities can serve the dual purpose of providing reliable health information to counter misinformation and offering moderated, empathetic peer support to alleviate feelings of isolation and anxiety, which may otherwise be channeled into excessive information searching or impulsive product purchases. Finally, interventions should include targeted digital health literacy training programs for older populations. These programs should focus on equipping seniors with the skills to identify credible health information sources, critically evaluate health claims and persuasive marketing tactics, recognize signs of potential information addiction, and make informed decisions regarding both health information consumption and advertised health products.

By addressing these implications, stakeholders can work towards creating a more supportive and empowering digital health environment for older adults, one that harnesses the benefits of information access and social connectivity while mitigating potential risks associated with addictive behaviors and unwise consumer choices.

Conclusion

This study highlights the high incidence of health information addiction among the older population in China. Health information addiction has both direct and indirect effects on health product purchase decisions. Additionally, perceived health-risk severity and social support behavior mediate the relationship between health information addiction and health product purchase decisions. These findings suggest that health information addiction significantly influences older adults’ purchase decisions regarding health products by shaping their perception of health risk severity and received social support. The findings provide a theoretical basis for understanding health information addiction among older adults and its impact on consumer decision-making, while also offering guidance for developing targeted interventions.
