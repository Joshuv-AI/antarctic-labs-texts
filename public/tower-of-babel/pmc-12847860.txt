Impact of bed roughness configurations on flow dynamics and hydraulic resistance in open-channel flows
Authors: Hossein Sohrabzadeh Anzani, Sameh Ahmed Kantoush, Sohei Kobayashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBed roughness significantly influences open-channel flow dynamics, affecting velocity profiles, energy distribution, and hydraulic resistance in natural and engineered rivers. This study investigates the effects of four bed roughness configurations, smooth forward (S-FW), offset roughness (R-OFF), and forward-oriented roughness at 60° (R-FW60) and 90° (R-FW90), on flow characteristics in an experimental flume. Velocity measurements, energy calculations, spectral analysis, and statistical tests (ANOVA) were employed to quantify the impact of roughness on mean flow velocity, Chezy coefficient (C), energy dissipation, and turbulence. Results reveal that S-FW yields the highest mean velocity (1.1587 m/s) and C (51.62), indicating minimal resistance, while R-FW90 produces the lowest mean velocity (0.4422 m/s) and C (18.51), reflecting significant drag and turbulence. Energy profiles show pronounced dissipation near rougher beds, with R-FW90 exhibiting the lowest near-bed energy due to vortex shedding. Spectral analysis highlights amplified low-frequency velocity fluctuations in rougher configurations, particularly R-FW90. Linear mixed-effects modeling and post-hoc tests confirms significant velocity differences across configurations (p < 0.05). These findings offer insights for optimizing channel design, managing erosion, and enhancing flow efficiency in hydraulic engineering applications.Keywords: Bed roughness, Open-channel flow, Velocity profiles, Chezy coefficient, Energy dissipation, Turbulence, Flume experimentSubject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Rivers have long been the lifelines of the Earth, not only transporting water, nutrients, and sediments across natural landscapes but also serving as arteries of human civilization, playing an indispensable role in societal development. Despite the significant threats facing natural river waters, as noted by Abbott1, humans have consistently regarded these waterways and river valleys as vital sources for drinking water, agriculture, and industry, reliable transportation routes, and sites for energy production through hydropower2. More than one billion people live near rivers3, and the riverine areas, due to their natural attractions and recreational and tourism opportunities, have become ideal locations for urban settlement4.

Rivers are also dynamic and fragile ecosystems, whose delicate balance is influenced by hydro-morphological factors and human interventions5,6. This dynamism makes the study and modeling of river flows one of the most complex challenges in hydraulic engineering, where hydraulic resistance7 emerges as a key concept at the heart of these challenges8. Yet beneath this vital role lies a persistent engineering challenge. In steep mountainous rivers with gradients exceeding 1–3%, intense sediment transport and rapid reservoir infilling threaten infrastructure longevity and water security. In such environments hydraulic resistance becomes a controlling factor that governs flow velocity, energy dissipation, and sediment dynamics, making it a central element in river engineering.

Experimental investigations into the influence of artificial bed roughness on hydraulic resistance date back to the seminal work of Adachi4,9, who conducted controlled flume experiments using artificial strip roughness elements aligned perpendicular to the flow. His results demonstrated that hydraulic resistance transitions from isolated roughness (s/k > 40) to wake-interference (8 < s/k < 40) and finally to skimming flow (s/k < 8), with peak resistance occurring at s/k = 8. These findings established a foundational framework for understanding roughness spacing effects and remain a benchmark in open-channel hydraulics. Subsequent studies extended this concept to natural and quasi-natural beds. Türker and Valyrakis10 investigated gravel-bed channels with randomly distributed clasts and showed that changes in protrusion orientation can strongly modify near-bed turbulence and the thresholds for particle entrainment. Nepf11 evaluated drag partitioning in vegetated channels and demonstrated that the alignment of stems plays an important role in controlling form drag and the development of secondary currents. Stewart et al.6 examined self-affine bed topographies and reported that fractal roughness creates spatial variability in hydraulic resistance. More recently, Coscarella et al.12 and Zhang et al.13 investigated 3D bump-like protrusions, confirming that angled or offset roughness generates asymmetric vortex streets and enhances energy dissipation compared to transverse bars.

To better understand the underlying processes, it is essential to recognize that hydraulic resistance, the force that impedes the movement of flowing water, plays a central role in the analysis and design of riverine systems. This concept is widely applied in the mathematical modeling of free-surface flows, particularly within the framework of one-dimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) shallow water models based on the nonlinear Saint-Venant equations14. These models, implemented in advanced computational tools such as HEC-RAS, allow for the analysis of water surface profiles, sediment transport, and water quality under both steady and unsteady conditions15. However, complexities arising from bed irregularities, such as ridges, reefs, vegetation, suspended sediments, ice, and channel geometry variations, make hydraulic resistance a highly variable and challenging phenomenon11,16. These hydro-morphological factors, which can change randomly, seasonally, or locally6, can lead to significant variations in water levels and flow discharge, for example, in channel constrictions caused by ice jams, debris accumulation, or alluvial sediment deposits17. Such variations can complicate accurate flow prediction and flood risk management, as errors in estimating hydraulic resistance may result in catastrophic consequences18,19. In this context, shallow water models with advanced hydrodynamic solutions, derived from the Navier–Stokes equations and Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) formulations, serve as powerful tools for simplifying these complex problems and providing precise boundary conditions20.

Along this winding path, rivers serve not only as vital resources for life and development but also as natural laboratories for studying open-channel flow dynamics. Bed shear stress, as a key parameter in river engineering, plays a decisive role in the interaction between water and the riverbed, shaping flow behavior and boundary conditions21. The presence of bed irregularities, such as three-dimensional protrusions or roughness elements oriented perpendicular to the flow, intensifies flow turbulence and increases shear forces10,12. These disturbances not only accelerate bed and bank erosion but also threaten ecological balance and adjacent infrastructure22. Researchers, by studying simplified models such as bump-like protrusions, have been able to analyze the effects of these irregularities on hydraulic parameters including head loss, turbulent flow structures, and velocity distribution13,23–25. These findings, rooted in decades of hydraulic engineering research26,27, have provided valuable tools for designing water conveyance systems, sediment control, and erosion mitigation.

At the core of these analyses, the Chezy coefficient (C) emerges as a key empirical parameter that estimates the mean flow velocity and plays an indispensable role in hydraulic design28,29. Rooted in Antoine de Chezy’s pioneering work in 1775, this coefficient integrates the effects of bed roughness, channel cross-sectional shape, and flow conditions, providing a powerful tool for predicting flow and energy losses. Bazin30 took a significant step toward operationalizing it by demonstrating its dependence on the hydraulic radius and bed texture, while Carlier31 emphasized the role of surface roughness. Powell32 advanced the concept by linking the Chezy coefficient to roughness scale and flow intensity, and Swamee and Rathie33 contributed an explicit formula incorporating viscosity and slope. Nevertheless, modeling challenges in channels with complex geometries remain, as highlighted by Loukam and colleagues34,35, who stress the need for multipurpose equations for diverse configurations36–41.

This study builds upon controlled experimental tradition established by Adachi4,9 by exploring oriented bar roughness, contributing to a better understanding of roughness effects on flow resistance and turbulence.

and extending it into the previously unexplored domain of oriented bar roughness, thereby providing the missing link between laboratory-scale turbulence control and field-scale sediment management. Despite extensive research on the effects of bed roughness on open-channel flow dynamics, there is still a limited understanding of how specific roughness configurations influence velocity profiles, energy dissipation, and turbulence characteristics in a comprehensive manner. This study seeks to address these gaps by systematically analyzing the interplay between bed roughness, flow resistance, and energy distribution. The main aims of this research are: (i) to quantify the impact of varying bed roughness configurations (smooth, offset, and angled protrusions) on mean flow velocity and the Chezy coefficient; (ii) to evaluate energy distribution and dissipation patterns across different roughness setups; (iii) to investigate velocity fluctuations through spectral analysis to characterize roughness-induced turbulence; (iv) to assess the statistical significance of roughness effects on flow dynamics using ANOVA and advanced visualization techniques; and (v) to provide practical insights for optimizing channel design and managing erosion in hydraulic engineering applications.

Experimental setup

At Kyoto University’s Ujigawa Hydraulic Laboratory in Japan, experiments were conducted in a closed-circuit rectangular flume. The channel, constructed from Plexiglas, has dimensions of 0.5 m wide and 7 m long, with a longitudinal slope fixed at 2% (Fig. 1). A pump, regulated by a Mitsubishi FREQROL-A700 variable-frequency drive, circulated water from a constant-head tank through the system, and a calibrated gate valve enabled precise control of the flow rate. The laboratory maintained a consistent air temperature of 21 °C during all experiments. The discharge was set at Q = 15 L/s for all tests.

Schematic diagrams of the experimental flume: (a) plan view, (b) side elevation, and (c) isometric representation.

The study involved four distinct channel-bed configurations, S-FW, R-FW60, R-FW90, and R-OFF, as detailed in Table 1. Figure 2 illustrates the arrangement of roughness elements, while Fig. 3a shows the number of measurements taken for each setup. Velocity data collection followed the same methodology as used for the smooth-bed configuration. Velocity measurements were taken at a fixed streamwise location positioned midway between two consecutive roughness strips and at the channel’s transverse midpoint (B/2), where B denotes the channel width. All measurements were conducted at this single fixed cross-section to ensure consistency across configurations; longitudinal measurements along the channel were not performed, as the focus was on vertical effects and roughness configuration under steady, uniform flow.Importantly, the measurement points were located above the bed surface, in order to avoid geometric interference with the sensors. This position was kept identical for all roughness configurations and was also replicated for the smooth-bed case at the same longitudinal station. At each measurement point, a Kenek VP3000 electromagnetic flowmeter (Fig. 3b, and c) captured 60 samples of the three velocity components at a 100 Hz sampling rate. To ensure data consistency and repeatability, measurements were duplicated for each point. The minimum measurement height of z = 10 mm is dictated by the physical design of the Kenek VP3000 electromagnetic current meter. The probe has a sensing head (tip) approximately 10 mm in diameter that must be fully submerged in water for the electromagnetic field to function correctly (Fig. 3c). This is a fundamental limitation of electromagnetic current meters in shallow flows and is documented in the instrument manual.

The observed reduction in average flow velocity underscores the fundamental role of bed roughness in modulating hydrodynamic resistance. Such patterns align with established principles in open-channel flow, where roughness elements disrupt laminar layers, promoting energy dissipation via form drag and viscous effects. The computed energy profiles reveal distinct patterns across the roughness configurations, as illustrated in Fig. 6. This energy analysis underscores the role of bed roughness in modulating flow energetics, particularly within the boundary layer. The increasing E with z across all models aligns with theoretical expectations for turbulent flows, where kinetic energy recovery occurs as the influence of wall shear diminishes. However, the differences in curve slopes and intercepts reveal roughness-specific effects: smoother surfaces (S-FW) preserve higher energy levels, implying lower head losses and potential for greater conveyance efficiency in channels. Conversely, aggressive roughness like R-FW90 induces substantial near-bed energy dissipation, which could enhance mixing or sediment entrainment but at the cost of overall flow efficiency.

Although longitudinal energy loss was not measured, vertical energy dissipation (ΔE/Δz) clearly quantifies the effect of roughness elements within the boundary layer. For example, the R-FW90 configuration exhibits approximately 75% greater near-bed energy suppression compared to S-FW, consistent with increased form drag and vortex generation.

Spectral analysis of velocity fluctuations in open channel flow

The investigation of velocity fluctuations in open-channel flows plays a key role in understanding flow structures and the influence of bed roughness on turbulence. To investigate the fluctuating components of the flow, the velocity time series were first detrended and had their mean removed, isolating only the variations around the average. The power spectral density (PSD) of these fluctuations was then computed using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

The power spectral density (PSD) was calculated using the fast Fourier transform (FFT), with a spatial sampling frequency of fs = 1/5 mm− 1 = 0.2 cycles/mm. The single-sided PSD was derived for positive frequencies up to the Nyquist frequency (fs/2 = 0.1 cycles/mm), providing insights into the energy distribution across spatial scales in the flow.

The PSD plots in Fig. 8 indicate that velocity fluctuations are dominated by low-frequency components, corresponding to large-scale spatial variations driven by bed roughness. Due to the 5 mm sampling interval, the Nyquist frequency is 0.1 cycles/mm, and the PSD only captures macro-scale structures (wavelength > 10 mm). Thus, the observed low-frequency peaks reflect large-scale variations induced by bed roughness, not micro-scale turbulence. For the R-OFF configuration, the PSD peaks at approximately 8.22 10− 2, at 0.033 cycles/mm (30 mm wavelength) decaying to 1.70 10− 3 at 0.1 cycles/mm, indicating that angled roughness introduces moderate energy at larger scales. The R-FW90 configuration, with the broadest velocity range, exhibits the highest PSD, peaking at 0.276 at 0.018 cycles/mm (55 mm wavelength) and falling to 0.011 at 0.091 cycles/mm, suggesting significant low-frequency energy due to pronounced boundary layer disruptions from perpendicular roughness. The S-FW case shows intermediate behavior, with a PSD of 1.05 10− 2 at 0.05 cycles/mm (20 mm wavelength) and 5.40 10− 3 at 0.1 cycles/mm, consistent with a smoother flow regime.

PSD of velocity fluctuations for smooth and rough beds.

These spectral characteristics highlight the role of roughness geometry in shaping the velocity field’s spatial structure. Smoother beds (R-OFF, S-FW) produce lower PSD magnitudes and steeper decays, indicating velocity profiles dominated by large-scale, depth-driven variations, likely resembling logarithmic distributions. In contrast, rougher beds, particularly R-FW90, amplify low-frequency power, reflecting enhanced turbulent mixing and larger velocity gradients near the bed, as evidenced by PSD values up to two orders of magnitude higher than R-OFF. The 5 mm sampling interval limits the Nyquist frequency to 0.1 cycles/mm (10 mm wavelength), capturing macro-scale fluctuations. The mono-harmonic nature of the velocity data, inferred from the experimental setup, manifests as a lack of distinct high-frequency peaks, suggesting that fluctuations are primarily governed by a single dominant spatial mode tied to roughness-induced boundary effects.

Analysis of chezy C variation for different bed roughness configurations

The C serves as a key parameter in open channel flow hydraulics, quantifying the flow resistance imparted by the channel bed. Velocity profiles were obtained at multiple points vertically from the bed under four roughness setups. These point velocities were averaged to derive mean flow velocities, which inform the relative C values. A kernel density violin plot (Fig. 9) illustrates the distribution of these velocities, incorporating mean lines, ± 1 standard deviation markers, and individual data scatters, providing a comprehensive view of both central tendencies and variabilities influenced by bed roughness.

Mean Chezy’s coefficient for smooth and one-sided wart-type roughness beds.

The violin plot reveals distinct patterns in velocity distributions, reflecting the impact of roughness on flow resistance and thus C. For the S-FW configuration, the mean velocity is 1.1553 m/s with a standard deviation of 0.0631 m/s, exhibiting a narrow, symmetric distribution. This indicates minimal flow disruption, leading to the highest C among the tested cases, as the smooth forward bed minimizes frictional losses and promotes a more uniform velocity profile akin to fully developed flow in low-resistance channels. In contrast, the R-OFF setup yields a mean velocity of 0.7706 m/s and the lowest variability, suggesting a stable but moderately resisted flow due to the absence of oriented roughness, resulting in an intermediate C. The R-FW60 case shows a mean of 0.6622 m/s with increased spread (standard deviation: 0.0607 m/s), where the angled roughness introduces moderate turbulence near the bed, broadening the velocity distribution and reducing C further by enhancing drag without fully obstructing the flow.

The most pronounced effects are observed in the R-FW90 configuration, with the lowest mean velocity of 0.4034 m/s and the highest standard deviation of 0.1997 m/s (range: 0.041–0.664 m/s). The wide, skewed violin shape highlights a steep velocity gradient, with near-bed velocities approaching stagnation due to perpendicular roughness elements that generate significant wakes and eddy formations. This configuration yields the lowest C, underscoring how orthogonal protrusions maximize boundary layer thickness and frictional resistance, effectively dissipating kinetic energy across the flow depth. The broader distribution in R-FW90 compared to others illustrates the transition from near-laminar profiles in smoother beds to highly turbulent ones, where velocity fluctuations are amplified by roughness-induced separation zones.

These findings emphasize the sensitivity of C to roughness geometry in open channel designs. Smoother configurations (S-FW and R-OFF) favor higher C values, suitable for efficient conveyance in engineered channels, while angled and perpendicular roughness (R-FW60 and R-FW90) reduce C, which may be desirable for energy dissipation in erosion control or flood mitigation structures. The observed variabilities, particularly the increased standard deviations in rougher setups, suggest that C alone may not fully capture flow dynamics; incorporating dispersion metrics could refine resistance models.

Application of roughness in open channels

The findings from this experimental investigation into the effects of bed roughness on open-channel flow dynamics offer significant practical applications for hydraulic engineering and river management. The distinct flow behaviors observed across the four bed configurations, smooth forward (S-FW), offset roughness (R-OFF), and forward-oriented roughness at 60° (R-FW60) and 90° (R-FW90), provide actionable insights for optimizing channel design. The high mean velocity (1.1587 m/s) and Chezy coefficient (C = 51.62) of the S-FW configuration indicate its suitability for applications requiring efficient water conveyance, such as irrigation canals, drainage networks, or low-gradient streams. By minimizing frictional resistance and turbulence, smooth bed designs enhance flow efficiency, reduce energy losses, and support cost-effective water transport systems critical for agricultural and urban water management. These results align with established principles and can guide engineers in designing channels that prioritize high conveyance capacity while maintaining structural stability.

Conversely, the R-FW90 configuration, with its low velocity (0.4422 m/s) and C (18.51), highlights its potential in applications where energy dissipation is desirable. The significant drag and turbulence induced by perpendicular roughness elements make this configuration ideal for energy-dissipating structures, such as stilling basins, spillway aprons, or scour protection measures in flood control systems. The pronounced near-bed energy suppression (0.010 m at z = 10 mm) and elevated low-frequency velocity fluctuations (PSD peak of 0.276 at 0.018 cycles/mm) observed in R-FW90 suggest enhanced mixing and sediment entrainment, which can mitigate downstream erosion in high-flow scenarios. These findings are particularly relevant for designing hydraulic structures in flood-prone areas, where controlled energy dissipation can protect riverbanks and infrastructure from scour and degradation, as supported by prior studies.

In mountainous rivers (with steep gradients (S0 > 1–3%), sediment bypass tunnels (SBTs) are widely used to divert clear water around reservoirs and prevent sediment accumulation62,63. The high slope amplifies bed shear and turbulence. The high drag observed in the R-FW90 configuration suggests potential relevance to energy-dissipating entrance zones in sediment bypass tunnels (SBTs), where enhanced turbulence may promote particle separation. Deploying perpendicular roughness strips at the SBT inlet can enhance particle separation by promoting suspension in the bypass flow while reducing bed load entry into the tunnel that is critical for long-term efficiency in gravel-bed mountain streams.

The intermediate performance of R-OFF (C = 34.31) and R-FW60 (C = 29.07) offers a balanced approach for applications requiring controlled flow resistance, such as ecological restoration or sediment management in natural rivers. These configurations, with moderate velocity reductions (33% and 43% compared to S-FW), can be strategically employed to promote habitat diversity by creating varied flow patterns that support aquatic ecosystems without excessive energy loss. The statistical significance of roughness effects (ANOVA, p < 0.05) and the detailed visualization through heatmaps and ridgeline plots provide engineers with robust tools to predict and manage flow dynamics in complex channel geometries. By integrating these insights, hydraulic engineers can tailor bed roughness configurations to specific project goals, balancing conveyance efficiency, erosion control, and ecological considerations.

Discussion

Effect of channel slope on roughness-induced resistance

The experimental investigation into the effects of bed roughness on open-channel flow dynamics provides critical insights into how surface geometry influences hydraulic performance. The results demonstrate a clear hierarchy in flow behavior across the four bed configurations, smooth forward (S-FW), offset roughness (R-OFF), and forward-oriented roughness at 60° (R-FW60) and 90° (R-FW90). The S-FW configuration exhibited the highest mean velocity (1.1587 m/s) and Chezy coefficient (C = 51.62), reflecting minimal frictional resistance, while R-FW90 showed the lowest velocity (0.4422 m/s) and C (18.5), i1ndicating significant drag. These findings align with established hydraulic principles, where smoother surfaces reduce boundary layer turbulence, facilitating efficient momentum transfer46,59,60,64. Conversely, the pronounced velocity reduction in R-FW90, driven by perpendicular roughness elements, underscores the role of form drag and vortex shedding in amplifying energy dissipation. The variation in C across configurations highlights the sensitivity of hydraulic resistance to roughness geometry. The high C in S-FW corresponds to its streamlined flow, akin to idealized conditions in engineered channels. In contrast, the low C in R-FW90 reflects increased turbulence and shear stresses, which are exacerbated by the 90° orientation of roughness elements, creating wakes and recirculation zones. The intermediate C values for R-OFF (34.31) and R-FW60 (29.07) suggest a transitional effect, where offset or angled roughness introduces moderate resistance without the extreme drag of perpendicular features.

Energy distribution and dissipation analyses further elucidate the impact of roughness on flow energetics. The S-FW configuration’s high specific energy (0.067–0.102 m) near the bed indicates reduced frictional losses, while R-FW90’s suppressed energy (0.010 m at z = 10 mm) reflects significant dissipation due to turbulent structures. The steeper energy gradient in R-FW90, climbing to 0.077 m at z = 55 mm, suggests a thicker boundary layer and delayed velocity recovery, which aligns with the spectral analysis showing elevated low-frequency fluctuations (PSD peak of 0.276 at 0.018 cycles/mm). This contrasts with the smoother S-FW and R-OFF profiles, which exhibit lower PSD magnitudes and more uniform velocity distributions, indicative of thinner boundary layers and less turbulent mixing. The ANOVA results (p < 0.05) and Tukey-Kramer tests confirm the statistical significance of roughness effects on velocity profiles, reinforcing the deterministic role of bed configuration in flow dynamics. The wide velocity range in R-FW90 (0.041–0.664 m/s) and its broad IQR in the box plot analysis highlight the variability introduced by perpendicular roughness, which aligns with the violin plot’s skewed distribution and high standard deviation (0.1997 m/s). The present results are in agreement with earlier findings reported in65, indicating that roughness-induced turbulence leads to reduced mean flow velocity and enhanced velocity fluctuations.

From a practical perspective, these results have significant implications for hydraulic engineering. The high C and velocity of S-FW make it ideal for applications requiring efficient water conveyance, such as irrigation canals or low-gradient streams, where minimizing head losses is critical. Conversely, R-FW90’s low C and high energy dissipation suggest its suitability for energy-dissipating structures, such as stilling basins or scour protection measures, where enhanced turbulence can attenuate flood velocities. The intermediate profiles of R-OFF and R-FW60 offer a balanced approach, potentially applicable in scenarios requiring controlled resistance, such as habitat restoration or sediment management in natural rivers. The heatmap and cumulative C analyses further enhance the understanding of spatial and additive roughness effects. The stark velocity gradient in R-FW90’s heatmap (0.041–0.664 m/s) visualizes the pronounced boundary layer disruption, while the cumulative C plot (reaching 133.51 with S-FW) underscores the dominant contribution of smooth surfaces to overall flow efficiency.

Relevance to sediment bypass tunnels in steep mountainous rivers

The 2% slope tested aligns with headwater reaches and engineered SBTs in alpine environments, where S0 often exceeds 1.5%66. In such systems, bed roughness orientation governs not only hydraulic resistance but also sediment routing. The R-FW90 configuration-inducing near-stagnation velocities (0.041 m/s near bed) and elevated low-frequency turbulence-creates coherent recirculation zones ideal for trapping coarse bed load at the SBT intake. This supports field observations in the Asahi River SBT (Japan), where angled baffles reduced sediment ingress by 40%67. Conversely, the S-FW profile promotes rapid conveyance, suitable for flushing tunnels post-bypass. These findings suggest configurable roughness panels (90° for exclusion, 60° for partial entrainment) as adaptive tools for SBT design in high-gradient, sediment-laden rivers.

Although the results provide useful insights into the influence of bed roughness configurations on open-channel flow dynamics, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. All experiments were conducted at a fixed discharge (Q = 15 L/s) and channel slope (S₀ = 2%), which may not fully represent the wider range of hydraulic conditions encountered in natural rivers, particularly those with steeper gradients. In addition, the roughness elements consisted of idealized, uniform strip configurations rather than natural materials such as gravel or cobbles. Measurements were also confined to a single cross-section under steady and uniform flow conditions, without longitudinal profiling or consideration of developing flow regions. Moreover, sediment transport was not included in the experiments; therefore, potential feedback mechanisms between mobile bed particles and roughness-induced turbulence could not be evaluated.

Future research could address these limitations by exploring the effects of variable flow depths and sediment transport to enhance predictive models for real-world channels. Incorporating pressure measurements may refine energy calculations, particularly for rough beds. Additionally, investigating combined roughness patterns or natural bed materials could further bridge the gap between experimental and field conditions, improving applications in river management and erosion control. Furthermore, To generalize the results and develop scaling laws, future experiments will systematically vary the channel slope (0.5–5%), different flow depth, and different roughness height. In addition, intermediate roughness orientations such as 30° and 45° will be incorporated to examine potential non-linear variations of hydraulic resistance with respect to angle. Finally, multi-station longitudinal measurements will be included to quantify energy loss along the channel and provide a more complete understanding of roughness-induced dissipation.
