Novel vascular damage markers in SLE: insights from carotid colour Doppler ultrasound and aortic oscillometry in the LUPCARD cohort
Authors: Konstantinos Triantafyllias, Felix Tietze, George Bertsias, Jacques-Eric Gottenberg, Andreas Schwarting
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess for the first time a combination of new and established markers of macrovascular damage in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) using oscillometry and greyscale/colour Doppler ultrasound (GSUS/CDUS) and, additionally, to explore associations of these markers with traditional cardiovascular (CV) risk factors and patient and disease characteristics.MethodsCDUS was used to assess arterial compliance markers, such as the Resistance-Index (RI) and Pulsatility-Index (PI) of the common (CCA) and internal carotid artery (ICA) in SLE patients and healthy controls. Carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT), atherosclerotic plaque and total calcification area were evaluated by GSUS. Aortic stiffness was measured by carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV).ResultsWe recruited 185 SLE patients and 150 controls. After propensity-score matching, patients showed higher CCA-PI (padj=0.006), CCA-RI (padj=0.028), ICA-RI (padj=0.011) and carotid plaque (padj<0.001), compared with controls. Among patients, elevated C reactive protein was associated with higher ICA-RI (padj=0.016), and ANA positivity with higher CCA-PI (padj=0.009) and CCA-RI (padj=0.011). ICA-PI correlated with lung involvement (padj=0.011), whereas both cfPWV (padj=0.003) and total plaque area were inversely related with CO-diffusion (padj=0.018). cIMT and cfPWV both showed positive correlations with age (p<0.001) and mean arterial pressure (p<0.001), and inverse correlations with estimated glomerular filtration rate (both p<0.05).ConclusionsIn the first study of CV surrogate markers in SLE combining oscillometry and carotid CDUS/GSUS, patients showed increased carotid pulsatility and resistance compared with controls. Furthermore, several patient-related and disease-related factors were identified as angiopathy predictors. These results suggest that combined evaluation of CV surrogate markers may help detect subclinical organ damage and improve CV-risk classification in SLE.Keywords: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Cardiovascular Diseases, Atherosclerosis, Ultrasonography

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease with diverse manifestations including serositis, arthritis, cutaneous lesions, immunologic abnormalities and systemic organ involvement in 15%–60% of patients.1 The musculoskeletal system is affected in up to 90% of cases.2 Moreover, patients have a significantly elevated cardiovascular risk, with a 2–10-fold increased likelihood of developing cardiovascular (CV) diseases and higher overall mortality compared with the general population.3,6

A meta-analysis including 20 SLE studies showed higher relative risks (RR) for cerebrovascular (CVB) events (RR 2.30), CV diseases (RR 2.35), atherosclerosis (RR 2.31), myocardial infarction (RR 2.66) and hypertension (RR 2.31).5 Up to 50% of patients have positive antiphospholipid antibodies, while one-third develop an antiphospholipid syndrome with diverse clinical manifestations.7 There is a broad consensus regarding the importance of early CV risk detection and effective prevention strategies in patients with SLE.8,10 Beyond traditional risk factors such as obesity and hypertension, SLE is now recognised as an independent risk factor for CV disease.10 11 However, traditional CV risk assessment tools often fail to account for disease-specific factors such as chronic inflammation, disease activity, immunosuppressive medication use and organ involvement.4 12 Risk calculators like the Systemic Coronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE2) Score have been shown to underestimate actual CV risk in SLE patients, as they consider only traditional factors such as gender, age, lipid levels, systolic blood pressure and smoking status.10 A more specific risk assessment tool is QRESEARCH risk estimator version 3 (QRISK3), which incorporates additional factors such as an SLE diagnosis, chronic kidney disease and glucocorticoid use.13 It may predict CV events in SLE patients more reliably than the SCORE2 model, although it remains suboptimal and has not been validated in SLE patients yet.12 14 Consequently, there is a need for improved strategies to accurately assess true CV risk in SLE patients, as well as for additional markers to enhance individual risk stratification.4 9 As stated in the position paper by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Working Group on Peripheral Circulation, which was also endorsed by the Association for Research into Arterial Structure and Physiology (ARTERY) Society, the combination of vascular biomarkers is particularly valuable in selected high-risk populations—a category that clearly includes patients with SLE.15

One promising approach is the oscillometric measurement of carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV), a surrogate CV risk marker. As the current gold standard for assessing aortic stiffness, cfPWV reflects the cumulative impact of adverse factors such as hypertension, diabetes, chronic inflammation and genetic predisposition on the arterial wall.15 In the general population, it has been shown to have a high predictive value for future CVB and CV events as well as all-cause mortality.15 A meta-analysis with 17 635 participants from the general population found higher HRs in participants with increased cfPWV for coronary heart disease (HR 1.23 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.35); p<0.001), stroke (HR 1.28 (95% CI 1.16 to 1.42); p<0.001) and CV events (HR 1.30 (95% CI 1.18 to 1.43); p<0.001), even after adjusting for traditional risk factors.16

Another established screening tool for atherosclerosis assessment is carotid sonography. While it is not routinely used in CV risk assessment in the general population, it can help identify SLE patients at higher CV risk by detecting subclinical atherosclerosis early.6 11

According to the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR), sonographic examination of the carotid arteries may include measurement of carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) and plaque detection, while plaque presence seems to predict future CV events in a more reliable manner.3 10 Our research group and others have examined CV surrogate parameters in several rheumatic diseases like SLE,17 rheumatoid/psoriatic arthritis,18 antisynthetase syndrome/idiopathic inflammatory myopathies,19 20 mixed connective tissue disease21 and fibromyalgia22 over the last years. However, to our knowledge, there is currently no study focusing on colour Doppler ultrasound (CDUS) indices of carotid pulsatility and resistance in patients with SLE. CDUS can nevertheless provide information on carotid compliance and may serve as an indicator of ischaemic stroke risk.23 While cIMT has been extensively studied in SLE, carotid calcification burden, which has been shown to predict CV events in the general population, remains underexplored.

Thus, in the present study, we aimed to examine a wide spectrum of novel CDUS and greyscale ultrasound (GSUS) carotid indices alongside well-established macroangiopathy and CV risk markers like aortic stiffness, cIMT and calcification burden in a large cohort of SLE patients, compared with healthy controls. Furthermore, we aimed to evaluate relationships of CV surrogates with both patient and disease characteristics, as well as with traditional CV risk factors.

Methods

Study population

185 consecutive SLE patients treated at the Rhineland-Palatinate Rheumatology Center and the University Medical Center of Mainz were recruited in the context of the ‘Systemic LUPus erythematosus associated CARDiovascular risk’ cohort study, a part of our multicentre German CARDiovascular Disease (CARD) cohorts. An open call for study participation led to the recruitment of 150 hospital co-workers, free from chronic inflammatory diseases, as a control group. Both patients and controls underwent B-mode (GSUS) and CDUS examinations of the common carotid artery (CCA) and internal carotid artery (ICA), along with measurements of cfPWV. Exclusion criteria were age (<18 years or ≥ 85 years), pregnancy, malignancy and missing consent. All patients met the 2019 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/EULAR criteria for SLE.24

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of the Rhineland-Palatinate State Medical Council, Germany, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (approval number: 13762_2). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data collection

Epidemiological and anthropometric data were collected at the beginning of the hospital stay. Moreover, traditional CV risk factors like arterial hypertension, type II diabetes and nicotine abuse were documented in both groups. Laboratory examinations were additionally performed, including C reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), complement factors, differential blood counts, renal parameters (glomerular filtration rate, creatinine) and lipids. Immunoserological markers were investigated by use of established methods: indirect immunofluorescence was used to detect antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) on Hep-2 cells. Autoantibodies against double-stranded DNA and extractable nuclear antigen (ENA), Sjögren's-Syndrome-related Antigen A (SSA-Ro), Sjögren's-Syndrome-related Antigen B (SSB-La), to U1 ribonucleoprotein particle (U1-RNP) and smith antigen (Sm) were evaluated by Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA). Furthermore, we documented past CV events and known cardiac (cardiomyopathy/myocarditis and/or pericarditis/pericardial effusion) or cerebral involvement from the patient’s history. Pulmonary involvement was reported by patient history, and additionally assessed by chest X-ray in two planes and pulmonary function tests. In the case of pathological results in one or both diagnostic tests or respiratory symptoms, a high-resolution CT scan was additionally performed. Currently taken conventional and biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs were also documented, as well as current glucocorticoid therapy.

Carotid sonography

Carotid GSUS and CDUS were performed by an experienced examiner (KT, rheumatologist, certified trainer of the German Society of Ultrasound in Medicine). A linear transducer of a MyLab9 US device (Esaote) operating at a frequency of 15 MHz was used for the measurements. cIMT was evaluated in CCA on both sides at the end-diastolic phase at three subsequent spots approximately 1 cm proximal to the carotid bulb. The maximum measured value was taken for further analysis, as atherosclerosis may be distributed asymmetrically.25 Atherosclerotic plaque was documented if localised thickening in either carotid bulb was more than 1.2 mm.26 On both sides, total plaque area (TPA) was measured and registered. Patients and controls were classified as having a subclinical carotid atherosclerosis (SCA) if IMT in CCA was >0.9 mm or if at least one plaque was detectable.19

Power doppler ultrasound (PDUS) consisted of bilateral duplex assessments of mid-CCA and proximal ICA in two planes. As soon as multiple identical waveforms in the wave flow spectrum could be identified for 5 s, they were documented regarding peak systolic velocity (PSV) and end-diastolic velocity (EDV). From these parameters, the software calculated the Resistance-Index (RI) according to the Pourcelot formula (RI = (PSV − EDV) / PSV)27 and the Pulsatility-Index (PI) according to the Gosling formula ((PSV − EDV)/mean flow velocity (MFV)).28

cfPWV, SLE scores and SCORE2

The examination of cfPWV was carried out following the manufacturer’s instructions of a validated non-invasive oscillometric device (Vicorder, SMT medical, Wuerzburg, Germany) as described previously.4 Examinations were performed by trained medical staff. The travelled pulse wave distance was measured between the right CCA and right femoral artery and then divided by pulse transit time (m/s). The average value of three measurements was documented and finally multiplied by 0.8 as recommended by an expert consensus.29 The term ‘cfPWV’ was used to describe all cfPWV × 0.8 values. cfPWV >10 m/s was considered an indicator for increased CV risk.29 The current version of the traditional CV calculator ‘SCORE2’, of the ESC was moreover assessed.14 For evaluation of disease activity at hospital admission, the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index score was calculated, and for the assessment of SLE damage, we used the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC)/American College of Rheumatology Damage Index (SDI).30 31

Statistical analysis

The Gaussian distribution was evaluated by the Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q plots. Categorical variables were documented as absolute (n) and relative frequencies (%), while normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean (SD), and non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (25th/75th percentiles). Correlations between included CV surrogate markers (PI, RI, cIMT, cfPWV) and continuous variables such as laboratory values were examined by use of Spearman’s (rho) or Pearson’s (r) correlation coefficient. Eta coefficient was used to examine associations between binary variables and the mentioned markers.

Furthermore, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) for several traditional CV risk factors (age, gender, body mass index (BMI), mean arterial pressure, nicotine use, diabetes and hyperlipidaemia, defined as low density lipoprotein >116 mg/dL, total cholesterol >200 mg/dL or triglycerides >150 mg/dL) was performed and differences in PI, RI, cfPWV and cIMT between SLE and matched healthy control subgroups were assessed. The PSM tolerance was determined as 0.1.32 P values <0.05 were assumed to be statistically significant. An additional linear regression analysis for possible confounding factors between patients and controls was performed, and statistically significant key correlations in the patient group were controlled via adjustment for age and sex. All statistical calculations were done in IBM SPSS V.27 and V.29 software.

Results

A total of 185 patients and 150 healthy controls were included in the study. GSUS and CDUS examinations were performed in 163 patients and 87 controls, while 139 patients and 143 controls underwent cfPWV measurements. The median age of patients was 53 years (IQR 41–59), and the median age of controls was also 53 years (IQR 43–59) (p=0.68). In the patient group, 89.2% were female, compared with 84% in the control group (p=0.16). There were thus no statistically significant differences in age or gender between the two groups. Association between group status (SLE vs control): carotid sonography and cfPWV

Differences in the included surrogates were assessed after PSM, with 164 individuals (82 pairs) matched between patients and controls (figure 1). In these analyses, both the PI and RI of the CCA and ICA were significantly higher in the patient group compared with the control group. Specifically, CCA-PI was significantly higher in patients (1.75 (1.49–2.22, IQR)) compared with controls (1.54 (1.28–1.81), IQR); p=0.004), and CCA-RI was also higher in patients (0.77 (0.72–0.80, IQR)) versus controls (0.73 (0.68–0.76, IQR); p=0.004). Similarly, Doppler parameters for the ICA were significantly elevated in SLE patients, ICA-PI was 1.25 (1.02–1.53, IQR) vs 1.00 (0.80–1.28, IQR) in controls (p=0.012), and ICA-RI was 0.67 (0.60–0.73, IQR) vs 0.60 (0.53–0.68, IQR) in controls (p=0.006). A similar trend was noted for detected plaque in GSUS in SLE patients after PSM (25 (30.5%) vs 12 (14.6%), p<0.001). SCORE2 was significantly higher in patients compared with controls (2 (1–4, IQR) vs 1 (0–2, IQR); p=0.03).

Subclinical atherosclerosis was more common in patients than in controls (35 (42.7%) vs 14 (17.1%); p<0.001). However, absolute values of cIMT did not statistically differ between patients and controls (0.84 (0.7–0.97, IQR) vs 0.83 (0.71–0.88, IQR); p=0.283). Similarly, cfPWV was not statistically significantly different between patients and controls (7.2 (6.1–8.6, IQR) vs 6.7 (6–7.7, IQR); p=0.106) after matching. To control for the possible confounding effect of heart rate between both groups on Doppler parameters, we adjusted for this potential confounder by linear regression analysis, which confirmed the differences of CCA-PI (padj=0.006), CCA-RI (padj=0.028) and ICA-RI (padj=0.011), but not of ICA-PI (padj=0.074) (table 2 and online supplemental data S1–S4).

Briefly, correlations of Doppler parameters (RI and PI) of the CCA with ANA-positivity (CCA-PI (padj=0.009) and CCA-RI (padj=0.011)) and ICA-PI with lung involvement (padj=0.011) remained statistically significant. Associations of cfPWV with group and disease characteristics

Within the patient group, cfPWV correlated significantly with age (r=0.528, p<0.001), MAP (r=0.381, p<0.001) and ESR (r=0.244, p=0.012) and also inversely with eGFR (r=−0.183, p=0.034). Furthermore, higher cfPWV correlated inversely with lung CO diffusion (r=−0.323, p=0.003). Patients under statin therapy had significantly higher cfPWV (8.8 m/s (6.8–10.7, IQR) vs 7 m/s (6–8, IQR); p<0.001). Higher cfPWV was measured in patients with hyperlipidaemia than in those without; the same accounts for patients with detected plaques in sonography (8.1 m/s (7.1–9.4, IQR) vs 6.7 m/s (5.9–7.8, IQR); p<0.001 and 8.2 m/s (6.8–9.6, IQR) vs 6.8 m/s (5.9–7.7, IQR); p<0.001). Vice versa, patients under therapy with hydroxychloroquine showed lower cfPWV (6.7 m/s (5.9–7.9, IQR) vs 7.3 m/s (6.2–8.8, IQR); p=0.032) than those without.

After regression analysis for age and sex, the correlation of cfPWV with lung CO diffusion (padj=0.003), MAP (padj=0.001) and higher cfPWV in patients under statin therapy remained statistically significant (padj=0.004). However, correlation with ESR did not remain statistically significant (padj=0.271), but lower cfPWV in patients under HCQ therapy did (padj=0.015).

Discussion

Carotid sonography and cfPWV as surrogate markers of CV risk

In this study, SLE patients exhibited impaired carotid compliance, increased vascular resistance (as measured by CDUS) and a greater burden of subclinical atherosclerosis compared with matched controls. Moreover, both patient-related and disease-specific predictors of aortic and carotid macroangiopathy were identified within the SLE cohort. To our knowledge, this is the first study to combine GSUS and CDUS with oscillometric assessment of aortic stiffness in SLE patients, and one of the largest investigations of CV surrogate markers in this population.

Most studies conducted to date have focused on GSUS parameters, such as cIMT and plaque presence, often showing a higher prevalence of SCA in SLE cohorts.6 33 In our exploration, plaque presence and SCA were also more frequent in the patient group than in controls, even though absolute cIMT values did not differ between the two cohorts. In particular, SCA showed a threefold higher incidence in the SLE group, compared with the controls, and in nearly half of SLE patients. Interestingly, carotid plaque assessment has been shown to be a more accurate predictor of CV events, such as myocardial infarction, than cIMT measurements in the general population.33 34 Carotid plaque evaluation has also been shown to be superior to predict calcification in other parts of the arterial system, like the coronary arteries.35 Moreover, an increase in calcification areas can lead to carotid stenosis, resulting in CVB events and subsequently possibly worsening CV morbidity and mortality. In our cohort, SCORE2 identified 22% of SLE patients at a more than 5% risk of developing a first-onset CV disease within the next 10 years. However, combining results from SCA (cIMT/carotid plaque) and aortic stiffness assessment, we could identify 49.1%, nearly half of the examined patients at elevated CV risk, compared with one-fifth by SCORE2 alone. Thus, it can be postulated that the additional inclusion of carotid plaque evaluation and cfPWV and cIMT measurements in CV risk stratification has the potential to improve the prediction of CV events and may outperform traditional CVRF scores.36

Interestingly, the literature on cIMT in SLE cohorts yields mixed results, with some studies reporting increased cIMT in SLE patients and others finding no significant difference compared with controls.6 8 37 However, an overall higher atherosclerotic burden has been described in a plethora of SLE studies.6 11 38 We are not aware of studies examining CDUS indices of the carotid arteries in SLE. The only similar exploration was the one by Shehata et al, which, however, focused on the examination of intracranial arteries (middle cerebral artery, basilar artery, anterior and posterior cerebral artery) by transcranial Doppler-sonography.39 In this study, which included 26 SLE patients and 26 matched control subjects, the patient group exhibited lower PI and higher velocity indices. According to the authors, these findings represent intracranial arterial narrowing, constriction, stenosis or occlusion in pathologic conditions affecting large intracranial arteries.39 In our analysis, Doppler PI and RI of both the CCA and ICA were significantly higher in patients compared with healthy controls, even after adjustment for heart rate, which differed between the groups, except for ICA-PI. Both the PI and the RI can be of value during CV risk classification, as they have previously been shown to be associated with CV(B) events.25 In the general population, increased flow pulsatility has been shown to associate with an up to 13-fold increased risk of stroke as well as with cerebral microbleeds and lacunar infarcts.23 Elevated ICA-PI values have been associated with higher counts of white matter hyperintensities, which are indicators of cerebral small vessel disease.40 These findings suggest that an increased carotid PI can reflect greater stress on the cerebral microcirculation caused by pulsatile energy.23 40 Moreover, both CCA-RI and ICA-RI have been shown to be elevated in patients with ischaemic stroke, indicating a destructive effect on the arterial wall.41 The increased risk of stroke in SLE patients is well-documented, underscoring the potential utility of these markers in CV and vascular risk screening.5

Associations of CV markers with disease-associated characteristics

CCA-PI and CCA-RI were associated with TPA and were higher in patients with ANA positivity, even after statistical adjustment. The correlation with TPA and prednisolone was significant in unadjusted analyses and only tendentially significant after adjustment. Croca et al, who also evaluated TPA in SLE patients, found higher values in patients receiving prednisolone, supporting the well-established link between glucocorticoid use and increased CV risk.6 36 42 Furthermore, both CCA-Doppler parameters showed inverse correlations with lung vital capacity, which, however, did not retain statistical significance after adjustment. On the other hand, ICA-RI and ICA-PI showed correlations with lung involvement, and cfPWV as well as TPA correlated with impaired lung CO-diffusion. Given these inconsistent findings, the relationship between CV markers and lung involvement should be interpreted with caution. Interestingly, in a genetic epidemiological study from the general population, involving nearly 61 000 cases and more than 123 000 healthy controls, coronary artery disease has been independently associated with impaired vital capacity.43 Thus, further examinations of associations between CV risk and lung involvement are warranted. ICA-PI and ICA-RI showed correlations with BMI, corroborating higher CV(B) risk in obese patients and matching results of Chuang et al.23

Importantly, ICA-RI was found to correlate with elevated CRP levels, which can be pathophysiologically explained by the harmful effects of chronic inflammation on the vascular system.44 Swastini et al described a positive correlation of atherosclerotic burden with higher CRP levels in patients suffering from dyslipidaemia.45 A large meta-analysis found an independent association between elevated CRP levels and the risk of ischaemic stroke.46 Atherosclerosis is a major risk factor and cause for stroke and inflammation, which in turn is directly related to the development of atherosclerosis.47

Associations of CV markers with patient-associated characteristics and traditional CV risk factors

Regarding associations of surrogate markers with renal function, both cfPWV and cIMT were inversely associated with eGFR. In addition to the patient’s age, this finding can be attributed to several factors: impaired renal function is linked to vascular calcification, and increased aortic stiffness leads to higher pressure amplitudes in the renal microvasculature, resulting in vascular damage and further deterioration of renal function.48 The observations in this study, which included relatively young patients (median age 53 years) with renal involvement in 21.6% of cases, may suggest two possibilities: either early renal impairment resulting from increased vascular stiffness or the onset of vascular stiffening in the early stages of impaired renal function.17 Additionally, we found correlations between cfPWV/cIMT and factors such as patient age, arterial hypertension, carotid plaques or statin use. These findings emphasise the role of traditional CV risk factors in the development of aortic stiffness as well as carotid atherosclerosis/stenosis and highlight the importance of close monitoring in daily practice to improve patient outcomes.

Our study has several limitations. First, we only compared CV risk factors with atherosclerosis surrogates and incident CV events in a cross-sectional design, but not with longitudinal data of morbidity and mortality. Consequently, we cannot draw conclusions regarding long-term outcomes or the predictive accuracy of these parameters for future CV events. However, several studies have shown the predictive value of cfPWV and carotid sonography in the general population and are suggested as additional tools for CV risk stratification by the ESC Working Group on peripheral circulation and by the ARTERY Society (recommendation/level of evidence: IIa/A).15 Second, although we matched for multiple CV risk factors, the presence of immunosuppressive/glucocorticoid treatments in the patient group may have influenced our findings, so future control and confirmation of our results are essential. Third, the trained professionals conducting the examinations were aware of the control group’s case status, as they were recruited from hospital staff. However, they were blinded to laboratory values, individual medical histories and other surrogate markers. Additionally, most of the parameters examined, such as cfPWV and sonographic Doppler indices, were automatically calculated by the examination devices, minimising the potential for examiner bias. Fourth, our study population consisted predominantly of Caucasian SLE patients with a median age of 53 years. This demographic and their individual CV risk profiles may have influenced our findings, highlighting the need for further validation in more diverse populations. Finally, although correlations between CV surrogates and pulmonary parameters or lung involvement were observed, these associations were partly inconsistent. Despite additional adjusted analyses, these results should be considered exploratory, and further studies with larger cohorts and fully adjusted models are warranted to confirm these findings.

To conclude, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of CV risk surrogate markers of angiopathy and atherosclerosis in patients with SLE, incorporating carotid Doppler parameters, greyscale ultrasound and aortic stiffness alongside traditional CV risk factors. Our findings demonstrate, for the first time, impaired carotid compliance and resistance in SLE patients compared with healthy controls, and possible associations with organ involvement, SLE disease damage and impaired organ function. These results are of relevance, as specific CV screening protocols still do not capture the true CV risk in SLE. The non-invasive, radiation-free and simple nature of the applied diagnostic methods may render them as useful tools for identifying SLE patients at high risk for the development of CV(B) events. Nevertheless, further research and longitudinal studies are essential to validate these findings and expand the current understanding of CV risk stratification in SLE.
