Lipid membrane remodeling by myristic acid treatment reverses Parkinson’s disease α-synuclein phenotypes in patient neurons
Authors: Julian Avila Pacheco, Gleidia Sauli, Luis Fonseca-Ornelas, Haley Cirka, Deepa Kumari, Morgan Hazo, Sarah Jeanfavre, Pedro H. Vendramini, Christina R. Muratore, Clary B. Clish, Dennis Selkoe, Saranna Fanning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the lipid-rich nature of the brain, defining the role of lipid metabolism in neurodegenerative disease and targeting lipid metabolic pathways for disease modification are in their infancy. In Parkinson’s disease, Lewy body dementia, and other synucleinopathies, disease-associated forms of α-synuclein (αS) alter fatty acid (FA) metabolism, increasing monounsaturated FA-containing lipids. This disequilibrium in membrane fatty acyl composition results in aberrant αS:membrane interactions. We report treating PD patient neurons with myristic acid (C14:0) induces lipid metabolism modifications correcting abnormal PD-associated membrane composition and reversing PD-relevant phenotypes. C14:0 conditioning reduced Lewy-like αS inclusions; reduced abnormal pSer129 αS; corrected excess αS at membranes; and restored native αS tetramer:monomer homeostasis. Using nuclear magnetic resonance, we established C14:0 as correcting abnormal αS vesicle membranes dwell time in vitro, correlating with less pathogenic αS aggregation. Mechanistically, C14:0 rescued PD neuron phenotypes through remodeling the PD-associated lipidome, eliciting beneficial effects by increasing shorter, saturated fatty acyl-lipids.

Introduction

The genetically implicated cytoplasmic protein α-synuclein (αS) is the major proteinaceous component of Lewy bodies (LBs) and Lewy neurites, the hallmarks of Parkinson’s disease (PD), Lewy body dementia (LBD), and other synucleinopathies. In addition to αS, LBs also contain altered lipid membranes1,2. Normally, αS binds transiently to vesicle membranes and helps regulate vesicle trafficking and exocytosis in healthy neurons3–5. αS binds with highest affinity to small, highly curved membranes, i.e., those with lipid packing defects6–15, which are promoted by fatty acid (FA) unsaturation and increased membrane fluidity16. It is well established in vitro that synaptic vesicles with lipid packing defects attract and bind αS6–9. Such lipid packing defects are the result of altered fluidity caused, for example, by insertion of oleic acid (C18:1)17. Correcting this lipid metabolism dyshomeostasis through lowering the ratio of unsaturated to saturated fatty acids (i.e., reducing monounsaturated FA (MUFA)) lessens the degree of transient αS membrane association and correlates with the rescue of some PD-like phenotypes in vitro and in vivo18–22.

Expression of PD-mutant or excess wild-type (wt) αS increases MUFAs and the neutral lipid storage pathway (diglycerides, triglycerides, and lipid droplets (LDs))18,20. Genetically reducing or pharmacologically inhibiting the rate-limiting enzyme for MUFA biosynthesis, stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD), lowers MUFA incorporation into lipid membranes and decreases PD-like neuronal phenotypes in cultured neurons and motor deficits and neuropathology in multiple in vivo PD models18–25. An oral SCD inhibitor (YTX-7739) has entered clinical trials for PD (NL72549.056.20). A functionally distinct approach to correcting MUFA metabolism disequilibrium and balancing excess MUFAs in phospholipid membranes is regulation of hormone-sensitive lipase (LIPE), thereby targeting the degradation of lipids to free MUFAs for incorporation into membranes instead of the synthesis pathway. Inhibiting LIPE reverses abnormal PD-associated cellular phenotypes in patient neurons, including reducing accumulation of pSer129, increasing αS tetramer:monomer ratio, and decreasing αS:membrane interaction20. LIPE reduction also rescued PD-relevant phenotypes in male in vivo PD models26.

Here, we investigate a functionally distinct approach to correcting disease-associated membrane FA dyshomeostasis that ameliorates the PD-linked increase in αS:membrane interactions, resulting in fewer disease-associated αS aggregates.

αS protein and membrane interactions determine αS conformation. αS can exist in cells as an unfolded monomer that acquires α-helical structure upon binding to highly curved lipid vesicles27. Under non-denaturing conditions, αS has been found to also form an alpha helical tetramer, a form less prone to aggregation28. Natively unfolded monomers in the cytosol transiently bind to highly curved vesicle membranes and become α-helical29. Four α-helical αS monomers assemble into an energetically favored α-helical tetramer28. Destabilizing the αS tetrameric conformation is hypothesized to precede misfolding of the resultant free monomers and their subsequent aggregation30–33. Several familial PD-causing missense mutations reduce the native tetramers to aggregation-prone monomers in vitro and in vivo20,30,31,34–37. Stabilizing the αS tetramer is associated with reduction in disease-associated phenotypes in models of primary18–22,38 and non-primary35–37 synucleinopathies in vitro and in vivo. Hence, αS-membrane interactions have emerged as a key part of the pathogenic process. Increased membrane-αS interaction has been reported with the αS E46K mutation that causes PD and LBD11,39–43; as well as from elevated wt αS levels in αS duplication/triplication PD patients20,44. Further, increased αS membrane binding has been associated with altered cellular lipid/FA composition18–20. Decreasing excess membrane-associated αS can be achieved by altering fatty acyl membrane composition18–20.

In the current study, we present a distinct mechanistic approach to remodel lipid membranes as the first step to a new therapeutic strategy for synucleinopathies. Instead of reducing aberrant MUFAs in membranes (e.g., via SCD inhibition), we sought to balance high unsaturated FA levels by modestly elevating saturated FA incorporation into membranes, rewiring lipid membrane metabolism. We pursued initial evidence that conditioning neurons in the short-chain saturated FA, myristic acid (C14:0), could improve PD-like cellular phenotypes. Specifically, we had observed that conditioning mutant αS-expressing human neuroblastoma cells with saturated FAs of decreasing carbon chain length (i.e., C14:0 > C16:0 > C18:0) improved αS cellular phenotypes18,19. C14:0, the FA with the shortest chain length we studied, is found in coconut and palm oils. It has been proposed as a candidate treatment for other diseases, including diabetes45, Alzheimer’s disease46,47, and specific immune system dyshomeostasis48.

Here, we demonstrate that C14:0 reduces several PD-relevant αS abnormalities, specifically in αS:membrane interactions upstream of later stage consequences, including neuronal loss and inflammation. To wit, αS cytoplasmic inclusion formation and excess pSer129 αS are exacerbated by PD-associated MUFA increases (e.g., C18:1). Co-treatment with C14:0 and C18:1 results in C14:0 counteracting the negative impact of C18:1 on αS inclusion formation and pSer129 αS accumulation in an extensively characterized PD cellular model. More importantly, C14:0 conditioning of PD patient-derived neurons decreased the degree of transient αS binding to membranes, reduced the abnormal accumulation of pSer129 αS associated with PD, and restored the physiological αS tetramer:monomer equilibrium. Transient FA/lipid interactions with αS were probed directly using controlled FA composition and membrane curvature of small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs) coupled with nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). αS interaction with lipid membranes of SUVs comprised of C18:1 was greater than that of SUVs comprised of C14:0. Titrating C14:0 into C18:1 SUVs decreased αS membrane affinity and was associated with lower αS aggregation propensity. Using specialized 13C-labeled FA tracing, we determined that C14:0 was robustly incorporated into several lipid families, leading to shorter chain FA-containing lipids, specifically TGs, PCs, and CEs. We conclude that remodeling aberrant FA membrane composition by increasing shorter acyl chain lipids can mitigate αS-associated lipidome metabolism aberrations in patient-derived neurons. Future analyses will focus on determining the potential for C14:0 as a therapeutic treatment in vivo. The conceptual advancement herein that lipid membrane remodeling via FA side chain composition alters αS-relevant cellular phenotypes in neurons supports wider consideration of the role of FA homeostasis in αS:membrane interactions in disease.

Results

C14:0 treatment abrogates the impact of αS-associated C18:1 elevation on phenotypes associated with αS:membrane interactions in PD

PD is defined by the formation of pathological αS-containing Lewy bodies. We used a highly reproducible neuronal model of aggregated αS inclusion formation to assess the impact of FA balance on round, Lewy-body-like cytoplasmic inclusions. Amplifying the familial PD (fPD) E46K mutation (which changes KTKEGV in repeat motif #4 to KTKKGV) by additionally mutating analogous E → K residues in adjacent KTKEGV motifs #3 and #5 (à E35K + E46K + E61K; called αS 3 K) induces multiple, round αS+ cytoplasmic inclusions of clustered vesicles in neural cells30,31. These αS 3 K inclusions are decreased by known modifiers of wt αS neurotoxicity, including nortriptyline, isradipine, and tacrolimus (FK506)18–20,49. This expression system provided a model to analyze the impact of FAs on αS inclusion formation. αS dyshomeostasis (e.g., E46K mutant or excess wt αS) increases MUFA levels, principally oleic acid (OA, C18:1)18. Increased C18:1 exacerbates the formation of round, αS+, vesicle-rich cytoplasmic inclusions in neural cells18. Here, we tested whether C14:0 (a shorter chain, saturated FA) could reduce αS inclusion formation based on correction of αS-membrane dynamics. To simulate the PD-relevant increase in C18:1 observed in patient neurons, we first treated this cellular model with C18:1. In keeping with our previous publication18, C18:1 significantly increased αS inclusion formation. We hypothesized that if longer chain, unsaturated FAs exacerbated αS inclusion formation, shorter chain, saturated FAs may decrease αS inclusions. Treating cells with C14:0 significantly decreased αS inclusion formation without impacting cell viability (Fig. 1A). We then asked whether C14:0 treatment could ameliorate the impact of C18:1 on αS inclusion formation. We previously reported that C18:1 increases neuronal levels of pSer129 αS, a major constituent of LB and common marker of synucleinopathy, whereas C14:0 decreases pSer129 αS levels19. Here, we asked whether C14:0 could reduce the pSer129 αS elevation induced by C18:1 treatment in human M17D neuroblastoma cells expressing the fPD E46K αS mutation (cell line19,30). We confirmed that C18:1 increased and C14:0 decreased pSer129 αS levels (Fig. 1B). Importantly, C14:0 co-treatment with C18:1 negated the exacerbation of pSer129 αS accumulation relative to total αS or to GAPDH, without altering total αS (Fig. 1B). This finding was confirmed in neuroblastoma cells expressing 3 K αS, which have also been shown to robustly accumulate pSer129 αS (Fig. S1A).

A key part of constructing an integrated mechanistic αS:FA/lipid landscape is to delineate the interaction between αS and fatty acyl side chains in synaptic vesicle membranes at an early point in disease. αS is enriched at presynaptic terminals12,29,50, co-localizes with synaptic vesicles (SVs)34, and binds SVs in vitro51. αS contributes to SNARE assembly involving FA/lipid membrane interactions at pre-synaptic terminals52–60. αS membrane docking is the prerequisite step for the kinetics of the priming and fusion steps for neurotransmitter release in the active zone4,61–64. Importantly, the docking of αS to SVs is altered by membrane lipid composition65, hence our mechanistic focus on αS:membrane binding. αS has high affinity for small, highly curved membrane surfaces6,7,10–15. αS can alter synapse size, location, structure, and recycling of SVs under pathological circumstances66–72. This results in altered SV function and abnormal neurotransmitter release, a key feature of PD54,61,67,73.

Small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs) are an informative, condition-controlled (as regards FA composition and membrane curvature) reductionist model to interrogate specific transient FA/lipid:αS interactions impacting synaptic function. Decreasing excess membrane-associated αS can be achieved by altering fatty acyl membrane composition/dynamics through MUFA reduction18,20. These FA interactions are also important for “sporadic” PD or LBD patients, who develop Lewy bodies (LBs) containing αS and membrane fragments1,2. Here, using well-defined SUVs, we analyzed how αS:membrane interactions differ between SUVs constructed with C18:1, C14:0, or different percentages of both, enabling us to analyze αS:membrane interactions and secondary αS aggregation. To elucidate the biophysical mechanisms underlying the interaction between αS and phospholipid membranes having varying FA compositions, we employed a combination of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence assays. SUVs were prepared using C18:1 (phosphatidic acid, PA), C14:0 (PA), or C18:1 (PA) + C14:0 (PA) (66%:33%). The phosphatidic acid headgroup was chosen based on its biological significance in αS binding properties. Several studies have used this headgroup to analyze the impact of FA side chains on αS properties13,68,74–77. All SUVs were confirmed to have the same diameter by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. S1B). C18:1 SUVS under identical lipid and protein concentrations to the other SUVS exhibited a significantly reduced 1H–15 N amide signal of 15N-labeled α-synuclein, as measured from the first increment (t1 = 0) of an HSQC experiment (Fig. 1C, red line).

The diminished NMR signal intensity of 15N-labeled αS indicates an increased percentage of the protein adopting a membrane-bound state. In contrast, SUVs prepared with C14:0 demonstrated only a modest decrease in NMR signals, suggesting that a substantial portion of αS molecules remained free in solution, unbound to the SUVs (Fig. 1C, black line). Increasing C14:0 in the C18:1-containing SUVs increased NMR signal relative to C18:1 alone, denoting decreased αS-membrane interaction (Fig. 1C, blue line). Concurrently, increasing the C14:0 concentration in C18:1-containing SUVs reduced αS aggregation, as evidenced by thioflavin-T (ThT) fluorescence measurements (Fig. 1D). Increasing the % C14:0 from 33% to 66% significantly increased the aggregation lag time as determined by ThT fluorescence (Fig. 1D), indicating a reduced α-synuclein propensity to misfold and aggregate in a more solution-based state. These biophysical observations underscore the correlation between membrane FA composition, αS binding, and subsequent αS aggregation kinetics.

C14:0 treatment rescues aberrant αS:membrane interactions and resulting αS phenotypes by lipidome remodeling

To establish the mechanism for the beneficial effects associated with C14:0 exposure, we estimated both the changes in abundance of lipids and their lipid incorporation using 13C-labeled FA treatment of E46K αS expressing neuroblastoma cells at four time points of C14:0 treatment: 1, 3, 8, and 24 h. We first assessed the cellular uptake of C14:0 and C18:1 to confirm that both are incorporated with comparable efficiency over time. Using absolute quantification in both cells and media, we observed no significant differences in the depletion of either one from the media or cells, except for a subtle decrease in concentrations of C18:1 in cells at 8 h and media at 24 h (Fig. S2).

We monitored changes in lipid abundances and 13C stable isotope incorporation using a liquid chromatography tandem high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-MS) profiling method (Fig. S3). We detected 8 ceramides, 74 lyso-phosphatidylcholines and phosphatidylcholines (LPC, PCs), 12 lyso-phosphatidylethanolamines and phosphatidylethanolamines (LPEs, PEs), 59 ether or vinyl-ether containing lipids (PC P/O or PE P/O), 20 sphingomyelins (SM), 33 cholesteryl esters (CE), 3 monoacylglycerols (MG), 23 diacylglycerols (DG), and 80 triacylglycerols (TGs). Both C14:0 and C18:1 conditioning triggered complex rearrangements across all these lipid families detected (Fig. 2A). C14:0 triggers a larger number of lipid changes compared to C18:1 at the time points we tested (p: 0.02, Fig. 2A). The C14:0 vs. C18:1 treatment causes differential abundance changes within members of each of the above-cited lipid classes, particularly in TGs, PCs, LPCs, CEs, and DGs (Figs. 2B and S4A, S5 and Supplementary Table 3). C14:0 caused the most changes observed among all lipid classes in members of the TG lipid class, with a maximum 5.2 log-fold increase in the levels of TG 42:0 and a minimum 5.5 log-fold decrease in the levels of TG 46:5. C14:0 treatment increased the levels of shorter, saturated TGs while C18:1 treatments primarily increased the levels of TGs with acyl chains longer than 54 carbons and 2-8 double bonds. Conversely, C14:0 treatments lowered the abundances of longer, unsaturated TGs while C18:1 treatment decreased the abundance of shorter lipids without having significant impact on changes in saturation state compared to C14:0 treatment (Figs. 2B and S5). A similar pattern was observed in the levels of PCs, with C14:0 elevating shorter saturated PCs and decreasing longer unsaturated PCs (Figs. 2B and S5, S6A, Supplementary Table 3). As described above, co-treating cells with C14:0 with C18:1 rescued αS phenotypes (Fig. 1). The combined treatment of C14:0 and C18:1 resulted in increases in shorter saturated FAs mimicking those seen with C14:0 treatment alone (Figs. 2 and S5, S6). While C14:0 changes are more pronounced, they did not completely negate the lipid changes caused by C18:1, instead generating a hybrid state. For instance, treatment with both FAs caused CE 16:0 to follow an intermediate state between C14:0 and C18:1 (Fig. S4A). Conversely, C14:0 + C18:1 treatment restored CE 14:0 to the levels of C14:0 alone. Two of the most abundant PCs detected, PC 30:0 and PC 36:2, showed similar temporal trajectories in the C14:0 and C14:0 + C18:1 treatments. PC 30:0 increased under both C14 containing conditions, whereas PC 36:2, which C18:1 alone increased, was suppressed in the presence of C14:0, resulting in levels comparable to C14:0 treatment alone. (Fig. S4A). The combined treatment produced hybrid states on neutral lipids such DG 34:1, DG 36:2, TG 42:0 and TG 54:3 (Fig. S4A), most closely reflecting those of C14:0 treatment alone. The changes across multiple acyl chain lipids within individual lipid families confirm that FA modification (elongation, de-saturation) produces lipids with varying combinations of acyl lengths and saturation states79,80. C18:1 treatment (mimicking the PD-associated C18:1 increase) favored increased longer polyunsaturated FA containing lipids and C14:0 favored shorter saturated lipids (Figs. 2, S5, and Supplementary Table 3).

To evaluate whether the treatments with C14:0 and C18:1 directly impacted the abundance changes, we determined their incorporation using uniform stable-isotope 13C-labeled compounds and monitored the presence of various combinations of 13C14 and 13C18 mass shifts in all lipid families. Specifically, we calculated the ratio of each labeled lipid species to its corresponding unlabeled endogenous lipid. Since we monitored 13C14:0 and 13C18:1 incorporation, the naturally occurring levels of these isotopologues were assumed to be undetectable. We assessed the levels of each labeled lipid at different time points (requiring a ratio of at least 0.5 for detection) and across each FA treatment monitoring all possible 13C14:0 and 13C18:1 combinations. We then determined whether these incorporations were associated with the observed changes in lipid abundance caused by FA treatments. This approach enabled us to identify lipid changes triggered by FAs either independently of or dependent on incorporation. (Figure 2C, Supplementary Table 3 and Fig. S4B). The decrease in CE levels after FA treatments was independent of incorporation. In contrast, the increase in CE 14:0 and CE 14:1 was attributed to 13C14:0 incorporation under both 13C14:0-only and 13C14:0 + 13C18:1 treatments. Similarly, the increase in short-chain diacylglycerols (DGs; DG 32:0 and DG 32:1) under 13C14:0 treatment coincided with the incorporation of 13C14:0 or 13C14:0×2. Mid-chain DGs (e.g., DG 36:2) showed increases under 13C18 treatment linked to 13C18 incorporation (either single or double units). Several decreases in DG levels appeared independent of FA incorporation, as minimal or no 13C14:0 or 13C18:1 was detected across treatments. For LPCs, the increase in LPC 14:0 after 13C14 treatment was notable; however, all other C14- or C18:1 -induced LPC changes appeared independent of incorporation, suggesting they were driven by remodeling of unlabeled PCs. Similarly, reductions in PC and TG levels were largely independent of FA incorporation. Interestingly, the reversal of C18:1 treatment-induced changes under the 13C14 + 13C18:1 treatment was directly linked to 13C14:0 incorporation in several PCs (e.g., PC 29:0, 30:0, 30:1, 30:2, 30:3) and TGs (e.g., TG 42:0, 42:1, 44:1, 44:2, 45:0, 46:0, 46:1, 46:2, 47:1, 48:1, 48:2, 48:3, 48:4, 48:5, 50:2, 50:6). This suggests that the lipid remodeling machinery preferentially utilizes C14:0-labeled FAs when available, influencing the overall lipid composition and incorporation-independent lipid decreases (Fig. S4B, S7B).

To explore these incorporations in aggregate, we summed the total incorporation ratios for each FA treatment across different lipid families, categorized by acyl carbon number and labeled FA combinations. We also evaluated incorporation over time by lipid family and FA treatment (Fig. S7). These analyses revealed that 13C18:1 incorporation was generally lower than 13C14:0, occurring across a broad range of acyl chain lengths with a preference for longer chains. In contrast, 13C14:0 showed robust incorporation, particularly in lower acyl chain species such as C14, C16, and C30 (Fig. S7A). Incorporation occurred rapidly, with time-dependent increases observed predominantly in TGs, followed by PCs, CEs, DGs, LPCs, and PEs, with DGs. Except for MGs, DGs, LPEs, and PC plasmalogens, 13C14:0 incorporation was consistently higher across treatments compared to 13C18:1 (Fig. S7B).

C14:0 treatment corrects abnormal αS cellular distribution, increasing cytosolic αS and reducing Lewy-body associated pSer129 αS in PD patient neurons

The aforementioned data show that C14:0 treatment can rescue PD-relevant αS phenotypes associated with exogenous C18:1 treatment (which was used to simulate the increased PD-associated MUFA C18:1) (Fig. 1A, B) and mechanistically alters αS:membrane interactions (Fig. 1C), resulting in less αS aggregation (Fig. 1D). To advance understanding of C14:0 conditioning, we evaluated whether C14:0 could reverse αS phenotypes in patient-derived αS triplication human neurons. Patients with αS triplication have excess endogenous wt αS that results in increased C18:1 levels in their cultured neurons20 and early onset PD81,82. Hence, for these experiments, C18:1 treatment was not required to model the disease condition. Patient αS triplication neurons have been determined to have ~40% increased pSer129 αS:total αS relative to isogenic corrected neurons20. Conditioning triplication patient neurons in C14:0 significantly reduced disease-associated pSer129 αS relative to total αS or GAPDH without altering total αS (Fig. 3A). C18:1 is known to increase αS at membranes19. Reducing abnormal amounts of αS at membranes is associated with the rescue of certain PD-relevant phenotypes18–20. αS triplication patient neurons have increased αS at membranes due to overall increased αS from triplication of the αS locus20, and αS dwell time at membranes is a significant contributor to early disease. Hence, we assessed whether C14:0 could re-localize αS from membranes to cytosol. We performed in situ sequential extraction per83. C14:0 decreased membrane-associated αS and concurrently increased cytosol-localized αS (Fig. 3B). To assess whether this relative localization of αS away from membranes is associated with an alteration of cellular αS conformation, we performed intact-cell crosslinking per30,31,84. αS triplication patient neurons have decreased physiological αS tetramer:monomer ratios20. DSG crosslinking determined that C14:0 treatment rescued this αS phenotype, increasing the ratio of native αS multimers (60 kDa, 80 kDa, 100 kDa) relative to αS monomers (14 kDa) (Figs. 3C and S1C). Importantly, this change in ratio is primarily determined by the decrease in αS monomer (14 kDa), the species known to progress to aggregate formation (Fig. S1C)32. Unfolded monomeric αS binds membranes and forms an alpha-helical conformation. Alpha-helical αS monomers can then assemble to form multimers on membranes, with the process of folding and higher order multimer formation happening concurrently. These multimers have weak membrane association and thus are largely found in the cytosol and can disassemble and unfold to unfolded monomers. The unfolded monomers are aggregation prone (reviewed in ref. 85). The increased cytosolic αS correlates with increased tetramer:monomer αS ratio and decreased monomeric (aggregation-prone) αS. C14:0 treatment remodels lipid membranes in PD patient neurons elevating shorter saturated lipids

The impact of C14:0 treatment on the lipidome of PD patient αS triplication neurons was tested and compared to that observed in the E46K αS expressing neuroblastoma cells (Fig. 4A). Similar to results in E46K αS neuroblastoma cells, C14:0 increased lipids containing shorter FAs across TGs, PCs, and CEs. C14:0 decreased the levels of only a few specific lipids (CE 20:4, and CE 18:2) in patient-derived neurons, and these changes were independent of 13C14 incorporation (Fig. 4A). Some lipid changes were different between E46K expressing neuroblastomas and patient neurons, particularly in longer unsaturated TGs. The levels of TGs in untreated E46K neuroblastoma cells and patient-derived neurons are different, suggesting cell-specific lipid dynamics of longer TGs. In PD patient neurons, C14:0 increased the levels of abundant TGs that instead were reduced in E46K lines, specifically TG 58:5, TG 56:4, and TG 54:5. This supports C14:0 treatments consistently increasing the levels of shorter FA containing TGs, but not necessarily reducing the abundance of longer containing TGs. This finding highlights the importance of increased shorter chain fatty acyl groups in the rescue of PD-like biochemical phenotypes. Analysis of C14:0 incorporation demonstrated changes in lipid abundance were directly linked to the incorporation of C14:0 into lipid classes, including TGS, PC, and LPCs, with fewer of the abundance changes in CE resulting from C14:0 incorporation (Fig. 4B, C). Discussion

There is increasing evidence of the critical role of lipids in PD and other synucleinopathies. Excess or mutant αS results in FA metabolic disequilibrium18,19. This underlies our novel approach to correcting αS neuronal phenotypes by re-establishing FA homeostasis through C14:0 supplementation. If inhibitors of SCD (entered clinical trials) or of LIPE seem promising in cellular and mouse models of PD, why investigate the additional therapeutic approaches, such as enhancing neuronal C14:0 levels? Oral small molecule inhibitors can be associated with both on-target and off-target side effects. C14:0 supplementation could provide a dietary approach to membrane remodeling in PD and other synucleinopathies as an alternative to SCD or LIPE inhibitors. Given a wealth of evidence that αS binds transiently to the outer surface of synaptic vesicles physiologically and that Lewy bodies contain complex mixtures of αS aggregates and abnormal membranes, a rational mechanistic focus for PD is establishing treatments that can correct altered fatty acyl composition of cytoplasmic membranes, which bind αS. Lipid profiling in plasma, CSF, and brain tissues in PD has identified lipids that incorporate C18:1 as elevated, while those having C14:0 are decreased86–89. The interpretation of these fluid and brain lipid changes is limited by the lack of precise information on the fatty acyl composition of the measured lipids and difficulty in connecting the metabolic contributions of individual brain cell populations to the PD samples tested.

Here, we have established that C14:0 conditioning of patient neurons reverses PD-relevant αS cellular phenotypes. C14:0 supplementation corrected the aberrant PD-associated increase in chain length and unsaturation (e.g., C18:1 accumulation18), restoring αS:membrane interactions. This resulted in phenotype rescue, including αS cytoplasmic inclusion formation reduction, and reduced pSer129 αS in patient neurons. These experiments focused on αS:membrane interactions associated with FA regulation of αS properties, specifically modeling membrane interactions at synaptic vesicles. This paradigm was designed to focus on phenotypes upstream of the very pronounced neuroinflammation and neuronal loss consequences established later in the course of disease. FA-level resolution lipid profiling established a connection between corrected disease protein localization and aggregation with lipid membrane composition remodeling. The data presented herein support increased short chain lipid membranes correlating with restoration of αS:membrane interactions. Increased unsaturated FAs (e.g., C18:1) alter membrane dynamics (fluidity). Distorted membrane dynamics interfere with physiological protein:membrane (e.g., αS:membrane) dwell time. Correcting membrane dynamics corrects this dwell time18. Akin to the effects of inhibiting SCD to alter membrane composition, incorporation of a shorter chain saturated FA (C14:0) or increased abundance of shorter chain lipids modify membrane dynamics and can counteract the effects of abnormal unsaturated FA metabolism. We hypothesize that increased C14:0 incorporation, dependent and independent abundance in shorter chain lipids, corrects aberrant αS:membrane dynamics, decreasing the abnormally augmented αS:membrane interactions at vesicles induced by the increased αS at membranes in patient αS triplication neurons (e.g., Figs. 3B and S8). Future experiments to advance the importance of αS:membrane dwell time determined by membrane FA composition will include analysis of αS A30P and G51D mutations associated with lower membrane binding levels. Despite divergent membrane binding properties, correcting of membrane FA composition (using an SCD inhibitor) was determined to counteract neurotoxicity similarly for the E46K and G51D mutations, so we hypothesize C14:0 treatment may benefit the G51D and A30P mutations similarly to that of E46K44.

To advance these findings, extensive analysis of the dynamics of this lipid remodeling in vivo will be important. This will include examination of synaptosomes and synaptic vesicles reflecting physiological FA levels in the context of the cellular milieu in vivo. The extensive, statistically significant, quantitative cellular and lipidomic data in human neuronal cells reported here provide the foundational cell biological underpinning of nutritional lipid remodeling as an approach to correcting abnormal αS:membrane dynamics and assembly. There are many considerations for novel treatment approaches, one of which for neurologic diseases is efficiency crossing the blood brain barrier. C14:0 has already been shown to cross the blood brain barrier90,91, leading to proposals of C14:0 as part of lipid-based oral delivery systems92. As with any treatment, establishing the dose and side effects of C14:0 supplementation will be an important component of these studies.

Future in vivo experiments treating robust PD mouse models with C14:0 dietary supplementation will quantify in vivo effects on (i) their documented PD-like tremor and motor deficits22,34, (ii) brain lipidome metabolism changes including altered species and subspecies abundance; (iii) C14:0 incorporation/tracing specifically in midbrain, striatum, cortex, and other brain regions; and (iv) any impact on the regulation of endogenous brain and hepatic desaturases like SCD93. These and other in vivo measurements will be critical for advancing this novel approach. But the extensive, statistically significant, quantitative cellular and lipidomic data in human neural cells we report here provide the foundational cell biological underpinning of this new therapeutic concept. In summary, C14:0 supplementation may prove to be a novel functional approach to PD therapeutics for re-establishing αS:membrane homeostasis in neurons through targeted lipidome remodeling and thereby correcting disease-associated αS interactions at synapses (Fig. S8).

Human neuroblastoma cells

Human M17D neuroblastoma CRL-2267 (ATCC) cells were grown and maintained in DMEM 1x (Dulbecco’s Modification of Eagle’s Medium) with 4.5 g/L glucose, L-glutamine, and sodium pyruvate (Corning 10-013-CV) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma F0926).

Inclusions assays (human neuroblastoma cells)

Cells were plated at a density of 20 K cells/well in a 96-well plate. 200 µM fatty acids were added to wells for 6 h. Cells were induced using doxycycline for 24 h. Inclusions and mCherry signals were monitored for 24 h in the IncuCyte Zoom 2000 platform (Essen Biosciences).

13C isotope tracing

Cell lipids were extracted in isopropanol. Lipid extracts were centrifuged (10 min, 9000 × g, ambient temperature) and supernatants (10 µL) were injected directly onto a 100 × 2.1 mm ACQUITY BEH C8 column (1.7 µm; Waters; Milford, MA). The column was eluted at a flow rate of 450 µL/min isocratically for 1 minute at 80% mobile phase A (95:5:0.1 vol/vol/vol 10 mM ammonium acetate/methanol/acetic acid), followed by a linear gradient to 80% mobile-phase B (99.9:0.1 vol/vol methanol/acetic acid) over 2 min, a linear gradient to 100% mobile phase B over 7 min, and then 3 min at 100% mobile-phase B. MS analyses were carried out using electrospray ionization in the positive ion mode using full scan analysis over m/z 200–1100 at 70,000 resolution and 3 Hz data acquisition rate. Additional MS settings: ion spray voltage, 3.0 kV; capillary temperature, 300 °C; probe heater temperature, 300 °C; sheath gas, 50; auxiliary gas, 15; and S-lens RF level 60.

Identification of nontargeted LC-MS peaks was performed by matching observed retention times and m/z values to an internal library of reference compounds previously characterized under the same analytical conditions. This library includes experimentally validated retention times and MS/MS spectra of commercially sourced standards (Supplementary Table 5). For lipid species lacking available standards, we supplemented this approach with an in silico database containing predicted m/z and retention time values for additional lipids within each class. These predictions were based on known structural modifications, such as changes in chain length or degree of unsaturation, and their systematic effects on retention behavior. This combined strategy enabled confident annotation of both known and putative features across lipid classes. A compound database was created with the computed m/z of each compound containing combinations of oleic (13C18) or myristic (13C14) acids up to the maximum label-containing number of acyl or alkyl carbons: (13C14, 13C18, 13C28, 13C32, 13C36, 13C42, 13C46, 13C50). The abundances of each isotopologue were obtained by automated peak integration of the area under the curve, followed by manual inspection to ensure acceptable chromatography and peak quality using TraceFinder 3.3 (Thermo). The m/z deviation of each isotopologue in each sample was determined and integrated compounds with an m/z deviating over 5 ppm units from the theoretical m/z for the compound were removed. Additionally, random errors in the labeling pattern, particularly for metabolites with low abundance (i.e. measurement signal close to noise that may overlap with mass ions from sample matrix), were identified by inspecting the labeling patterns of all isotopologues in the BSA controls. Isotopologue signals deviating from the expected naturally occurring isotopic distribution in the BSA controls were flagged and removed across all experimental conditions.

Comparison of lipid abundances: Aggregated lipid abundances were computed by summing the abundances of all isotopologues detected for each compound. To analyze the changes in lipid abundance over time in E46K αS cells, any missing values were first imputed with ½ minimum abundance for each lipid. Subsequently, abundances for lipids under each treatment (13C18:1, 13C14, 13C18 + 13C14) and time point (1, 3, 8, 24 h98. Were divided by the average abundances of their respective BSAcontrol at each time point. These BSA-scaled abundance ratios were then log-transformed before generating line plots andabundance change heatmaps. T-tests were computed contrasting each time point against the 1-hour time point. Given themultiple hypotheses tested, resulting p-values were corrected using the Benjamini & Hochberg98 false discovery rate asimplemented in the R function p.adjust99.

Incorporation of labeled C14 and C18:1 in lipids ratios were calculated for each compound by dividing the signal for each 13C-containing isotopologue over the abundance of the non-13C- containing (M0) ion. Ratios for which one of the conditions was not measured because it was below detection limit were left as empty values. The incorporation heatmaps for each acyl/alky carbon length were generated averaging the incorporation for each carbon length lipid regardless of the family and log transforming the resulted average incorporation levels. Similarly, total incorporation plots for each family, FA treatment at each time point were generated by summing all the incorporation values.

Fatty acid profiling: Cell fatty acids were extracted in methanol containing 50 ng/mL 15-methyl PGE1, 15-methyl PGA2, 15-methyl PGE2. Resulting extracts were centrifuged (10 min, 9000 × g, 4 °C) and directly injected onto a 100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm ACQUITY BEH C18 column (Waters, Milford, MA), with injection volumes of 10 µL for cell extracts and 2 µL for culture media samples. Chromatographic separation was performed using a binary solvent system consisting of water with 0.01% formic acid (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile with 0.01% acetic acid (mobile phase B), at a flow rate of 600 µL/min. The gradient was initiated with 20% B and held isocratically for 1 min, followed by a linear increase to 100% B over 11 min. The composition was maintained at 100% B for 2 min, then returned to 20% B over 1 min, with a final re-equilibration at 20% B for 4 min, resulting in a total run time of 19 min. MS analyses were carried out using electrospray ionization in the negative ion mode using full scan analysis over m/z 70–850 at 70,000 resolution. Additional MS settings: ion spray voltage, 3.5 kV; capillary temperature, 320 °C; probe heater temperature, 300 °C; sheath gas, 45; auxiliary gas, 10; and S-lens RF level 60.

Methods per66. For expression of 15N-labeled αS, bacteria were grown in M9 complete medium + ampicillin, supplemented with 15NH4Cl and D-13C-glucose. M9 complete medium was prepared fresh from a 10x M9 medium stock (60 g L−1 Na2HPO4, 30 g L−1 KH2PO4, 5 g L−1 NaCl), 50 × 20% D-glucose, 1000 × 1 M MgSO4, 5000 × 1 M CaCl2, 1000 × 1 mg mL−1 thiamine and biotin, 100x trace element solution, 1000x vitamin solution and 1 g L−1 15NH4Cl. The 100x trace element solution was prepared by dissolving (in 1 L MilliQ water) 5 g ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.50 g FeCl3, 84 mg ZnCl2, 12 mg CuSO4, 10 mg CoCl2 · 6H2O, 10 mg H3BO3, 1.6 mg MnCl2 · 4H2O, 1.6 mg Na2MoO4 · 2H2O, and adjusting the pH to 7.0 (it was then filter-sterilized and stored at RT). The 1000x vitamin solution was prepared by dissolving (in 1 L 95% MilliQ water, 5% acetonitrile), 50 mg riboflavin, 0.5 g niacinamide, 0.5 g pyridoxine hydrochloride, and 0.5 g thiamine. After filtration with a 0.22-µm cutoff (to sterilize and remove the undissolved riboflavin), the solution was stored at 4 °C, protected from light. BL21(DE3) E. coli (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) were transformed with the pET21a-based constructs, and single colonies inoculated in 2xYT+ampicillin. Cultures were grown for 6-8 h at 37 °C under shaking, then used to inoculate an overnight pre-culture in M9 complete medium+ampicillin, which, on the following day, was diluted again 1:10 in M9 complete medium+ampicillin. Cultures were induced at an OD600 of ~0.5 with 1 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 4 h. The cell pellet, after harvesting, was resuspended in 20 mM Tris buffer, 25 mM NaCl, pH 8.0, and lysed by boiling for 15 min. The supernatant of a 20 min, 20,000×g spin of the lysate was then further processed. The sample was loaded on two 5 mL (tandem) HiTrap Q HP anion exchange columns (GE Healthcare, Pittsburgh, PA), equilibrated with 20 mM Tris buffer, 25 mM NaCl, pH 8.0. αS was eluted from the columns with a 25–1000 mM NaCl gradient of 20 mM Tris buffer, 1 M NaCl, pH 8.00. Peak fractions were pooled and further purified via gel filtration on a HiPrep Sephacryl S-200 HR 16/60 gel filtration column (GE Healthcare, Pittsburgh, PA) using 50 mM NH4Ac, pH 7.40 as running buffer. Peak fractions (>95% pure, by Coomassie Brilliant Blue-stained SDS-PAGE) were pooled, aliquoted, lyophilized, and stored at −20 °C. Purified samples were also analyzed by Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization-Time Of Flight (MALDI-TOF) Mass Spectrometry (MS) and trypsin digestion followed by MALDI-TOF MS, to confirm the intact mass and sequence of the proteins (Molecular Biology Core Facilities, Dana-Farber Cancer Institute). Lyophilized proteins were reconstituted fresh, before experiments, in saline buffer, pH 7.4, and not reused. Protein solutions were spun down every time after resuspension, before spectroscopic quantitation of the protein concentration, at 21,130 × g for 20 min. at 4 °C, to remove any large aggregates. Concentration was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm (ε = 0.412 mg mL−1 cm−1). HSQC NMR spectra were recorded per66 on a 600 MHz Bruker AVANCE II spectrometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA) equipped with a Prodigy CryoProbe, at 25 °C and using 25% NUS sampled using Poisson Gap distribution and reconstructed using the hmsIST protocol100. Resonances were assigned, where possible, by inspection and comparison with the previously assigned spectra of WT αS (BRMB 6968 and 5744). Data processing and analysis were performed with the Bruker TopSpin software and CcpNmr Analysis101. Spectra were extracted from the first increment (t1 = 0) of a [1H–15 N] HSQC experiment, using 32 scans, a 2048-point acquisition in the direct dimension, at 25 °C in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl. SUVs were present at a 1:50 protein-to-lipid molar ratio.

Small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs)

For vesicle formation lyophilized lipids C18:1, C14:0, C18:1 + C14:0 (Avanti Polar Lipids) were dissolved in a 4 ml mixture of chloroform/methanol (1:1 vol/vol) at the transition temperature, followed by evaporation of all solvents under a stream of N2 gas and lyophilized overnight. The resulting lipid film was hydrated in 50 mM Na-phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 100 mM NaCl to obtain a total lipid concentration of 12.5 mM. The suspension was then sonicated at 37 kHz (four times for 10 min with 5 min breaks at the transition temperature of the highest component until the sample was transparent) in a glass tube. SUVs were isolated using ultracentrifugation at 55,000 rpm in a Beckman TLA 100.3 rotor for 2 h at 298 K. The isolated SUVs had a hydrodynamic diameter of 25 ± 10 nm according to dynamic light scattering (DLS).

Thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence assay

ThT assays were performed per66. Solutions of αS at 50 μM concentration were incubated at 37°C in the presence and absence of SUVs at an excess of 60:1 lipid to protein ratio with 0.01% NaN3 as preservative. Aggregation was monitored using a continuous ThT assay. Six replicates (100 μL) were transferred to wells of non-binding, black, clear-bottom, nontreated 96-well plates (Corning Life Sciences, Corning, NY). Reactions prepared in the absence of recombinant protein were included as blanks to subtract basal ThT fluorescence. Plates were covered with adhesive plate sealers (VWR, Radnor, PA) and incubated at 37 °C in a POLARstar Omega plate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany) or Tecan, model Infinite 200 Pro with shaking at 100 RPM for 300 s before each reading. Fluorescence was recorded every 10 min (Ex440 and Em480), and initial reactions were carried out for at least 96 h.
