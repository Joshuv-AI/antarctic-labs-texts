Tailoring colorectal cancer screening: Knowledge, CRC screening acceptability, and practices among Jordanian and refugee populations
Authors: Raquel Sánchez Marqués, Abdallah Mazin Matouq, Anás Al Muhtaseb, Maria Falcón, Adela Castelló-Pastor, Beatriz Pérez-Gómez, Carmen Rodríguez-Blázquez, Maria João Forjaz, Seifeddin Hussein, Gabriel Díez, María Romay-Barja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is the second most prevalent cancer in Jordan and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Early detection through screening campaigns significantly improves survival. However, Jordan lacks a national screening program, and disparities in access persist, particularly among the refugee population. The aim of this study was to evaluate knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding CRC and CRC screening among Jordanian and Syrian refugee populations in Mafraq governorate and to determine the factors associated.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted between May and July 2024 to a sample of 897 individuals aged above 50 years old. Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire.ResultsThe findings revealed a limited level of CRC awareness overall, with a median knowledge score of 7 out of 32. Refugees showed a higher level of awareness regarding lifestyle-related risks, and lower awareness of CRC symptoms and screening methods. Both groups expressed high CRC screening acceptability, with refugees demonstrating higher willingness to participate if invited by the Ministry of Health. The screening facilitators identified included free treatment, dissemination of information by healthcare professionals, and accessibility of services. Preventive practices were moderate, though refugees reported lower physical activity, reduced fruit and vegetable intake, and greater economic constraints. Multivariate analysis identified urban residence and higher education as predictors of better CRC knowledge. Rural residence and the type of occupation were associated with healthy habits, while the age, being female, higher education and having a health insurance were associated with better health-seeking behaviors.ConclusionThese findings underscore the need for culturally adapted, equity-focused CRC education and screening initiatives in Jordan, taking into account the refugee populations.Keywords: Colorectal cancer; Knowledge, attitudes, and practices; Screening; Refugees; Jordan

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most prevalent cancer worldwide, accounting for nearly two million new cases annually and it is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality on a global scale (Bray et al., 2024).

One of the most effective strategies to reduce both CRC mortality and the progression to advanced disease stages is through systematic screening programs (IARC, 2019) that can reduce mortality rates by up to 85%, mainly through the early detection and timely intervention of precancerous lesions and early-stage tumors (Fitzpatrick-Lewis et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2017). Standard screening techniques primarily include stool-based diagnostic tests and endoscopic procedures such as colonoscopy (IARC, 2019).

In Jordan, a middle-income country with a population of approximately 11,302,000 individuals, CRC is the second most prevalent cancer and ranks third among cancer-related causes of death (Bray et al., 2024; Ministry of Health: Jordan Cancer Registry, 2022). Despite advances in cancer care within the country, only 26.5% of CRC cases are diagnosed at an early stage and survival rates fluctuate between 51% and 58% (Sharkas et al., 2017). These data underscore the critical need for early screening strategies and timely therapeutic intervention to improve clinical prognosis and reduce the burden of CRC. As Jordan currently lacks a national CRC screening program, the Ministry of Health (MoH) has expressed its intention to launch a nationwide screening initiative with special attention not only to the general population but also to Syrian refugees, who make up almost 12% of the country’s inhabitants (Department of Statistics of Jordan, 2022) and among whom CRC has a high prevalence, accounting for 11% of registered cancer cases (Spiegel et al., 2020).

Therefore, an effective screening campaign should be designed considering the population’s knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, concerns, heath practices, as well as structural, organizational, financial, and healthcare access factors (IARC, 2019; Jadallah et al., 2023; Jarab et al., 2024). This comprehensive framework is particularly relevant to populations with a culturally diverse background, as in Jordan, where a significant refugee population presents unique health care challenges.

As one of the most expensive non-communicable diseases, cancer requires complex, multidisciplinary care, posing significant financial and ethical challenges for health policy makers. The Jordanian Ministry of Planning and International Cooperation has reported that government spending has been substantially strained in efforts to meet the needs of Syrian refugees (Jordan Response Plan, 2015). However, Jordanian public health insurance currently covers approximately 55% of the population, limiting access to healthcare services for a substantial segment of households and individuals. As a result, various alternatives—including private insurance, coverage provided by nongovernmental organizations such as United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA) and United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), and out-of-pocket fee-for-service models—operate alongside public insurance (World Health Organization, 2019).

Evidence from Jordan suggests that awareness of CRC and screening has improved over time, yet screening participation remains low and substantial knowledge gaps persist (Jadallah et al., 2023; Jarab et al., 2024; Omran et al., 2015a). Existing studies have primarily focused on the general Jordanian population and have identified limited knowledge, concerns regarding screening procedures, financial barriers, and insufficient healthcare provider recommendation as key determinants of participation. However, little is known about how these factors differ between Jordanian citizens and Syrian refugees, despite the latter representing a substantial and potentially underserved segment of the population. Furthermore, evidence regarding CRC-related preventive practices and health-seeking behaviors among refugees in Jordan remains scarce.

In this context, the Jordanian MoH requested this study, which aimed to assess knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) of Jordanian and refugee populations in relation to CRC screening, and to determine the factors associated with these outcomes. The ultimate goal was to generate evidence to inform the design of an equitable national CRC screening strategy that addresses the needs of all population groups and helps ensure that no population, including refugees, is left behind.

Methods

This study was part of REAYAH Project, funded by the European Union through the Spanish Agency for International Development Cooperation and CSAI Foundation in collaboration with the Jordanian MoH to provide support to the Public Health System in Jordan for Jordanians and Syrian Refugees and in the prevention and management of Non-Communicable Diseases through Primary Health Care in the governorates of Mafraq, Ajlun and Tafila.

Study area and population

Jordan is a Middle Eastern country with a total area of 89,318 km2, divided into twelve administrative governorates. It has an estimated population of 11.7 million among which 48% are females and approximately 92% live in urban areas, with 427,351 registered refugees (Department of Statistics of Jordan, 2024; United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 2025). The Mafraq governorate, located in the northeastern part of the Kingdom, covers nearly 30% of Jordan’s territory while hosting only 6% of the national population (approximately 675,200 inhabitants). However, it provides shelter to one out of every four Syrian refugees in the country. According to 2025 data from the UNHCR, Mafraq hosts an estimated 107,339 registered refugees, including approximately 82,000 residing in the Zaatari refugee camp, the largest Syrian refugee camp in the country. Based on these figures, Syrian refugees represent approximately 20% of Mafraq’s total population.

CRC KAP study

Between May and July 2024, a cross-sectional survey was conducted in Mafraq. The sample size was calculated using the Jordan 2015 census as the sampling frame, assuming a confidence level of 95%, 80% statistical power, an expected response rate of 80% and an anticipated prevalence of CRC-related knowledge level of 50%. The final sample size for the study was determined to be 897 people.

Participants were selected through a three-stage sampling procedure. In the first stage, a total of 45 clusters were selected with a probability proportional to their size, defined as the number of residential households recorded in the 2015 Population and Housing Census. Sampling clusters included urban, rural, and refugee camp areas within Mafraq governorate. In the second stage, a fixed number of 20 households per cluster were chosen using systematic equal-probability sampling. Finally, in the third stage, one household member aged 50 years or older was selected according to predefined quotas. The inclusion criteria were: (i) being 50 years of age or older, (ii) residing in the selected household, and (iii) not having a prior diagnosis of CRC. The sample size and cluster selection were based on the Jordan 2015 Population and Housing Census, the most recent nationally available census providing the geographical detail required for sampling at the time of the study. Trained interviewers conducted face-to-face interviews, privately and participants were informed that their responses would remain confidential, would be used exclusively for research purposes, and would not affect their access to healthcare services, humanitarian assistance, or any other benefits. To facilitate data collection in refugee camps, the Jordanian Ministry of Health obtained the necessary permissions to access the camps and conduct household interviews. The age threshold of 50 years was selected because it represents the primary target population for average-risk CRC screening and future screening initiatives.

Questionnaire

A questionnaire (see Supplementary Material 1) was developed to evaluate KAP related to CRC disease and screening including: (a) demographic characteristics; (b) knowledge of CRC and screening procedures; (c) attitudes toward CRC screening, focused on the acceptability of CRC screening, willingness to participate, test preferences, willingness to recommend screening, and intended responses following a positive screening result; and (d) practices regarding CRC risk factors, preventive behaviors, and health-seeking behavior. The questionnaire was translated from English into Arabic and culturally adapted to ensure conceptual equivalence, cultural appropriateness, and comprehensibility for the target population within the Jordanian context. Prior to implementation, the questionnaire was pilot-tested in the same study setting among individuals who met the eligibility criteria but were not included in the final sample. Based on participant feedback and interviewer observations, minor wording changes were made to improve the clarity and comprehension of several items. The pilot confirmed that the questionnaire was acceptable and feasible for field implementation.

Variables

To provide an overall descriptive summary of the KAP domains, four study-specific indices were created: knowledge, CRC screening acceptability, healthy habits, and health-seeking behavior (Supplementary Material 2). Items were selected a priori according to their conceptual relevance to each domain, based on established CRC risk factors, preventive behaviors, screening-related knowledge, and expected engagement with health services.

For all indices, each item was given equal weight. This approach was chosen because no validated weighting scheme was available for this questionnaire or for this specific study population, and because the aim was to create simple and transparent summary indicators rather than formally validated psychometric scales. Responses considered correct or favorable were scored as 1, while incorrect, unfavorable, and “don’t know” responses were scored as 0. Open-ended responses recorded under “other” were reviewed and recoded as correct or favorable when they were conceptually consistent with an accurate answer for the corresponding domain. These indices were developed as pragmatic, study-specific summary measures rather than psychometric scales.

For each index a new variable indicating higher versus lower levels was generated using its median score as the cut-off as done in previous studies (Geng et al, 2026; Riccò et al, 2020; Sanjayanthan et al, 2026), given the absence of externally validated thresholds for this population. The median-based categories do not represent objectively adequate or clinically meaningful thresholds.

Knowledge

Participants were asked whether they had ever heard about CRC and CRC screening tests and, if so, about their sources of information. They were also asked about their preferences to gather such information. Thirteen questions addressed knowledge about CRC, including age- and sex-related risk (2 correct options), relevant risk factors (11 correct options), effective preventive behaviors (5 correct options), main CRC symptoms (5 correct options), screening techniques (2 correct options), and their interpretation, usefulness, and indication (7 correct options). A knowledge index was calculated by adding the values of the correct answers to these questions (Supplementary material 2, Table S2.1). The index ranged from 0 to 32 with higher values indicating greater CRC-related knowledge.

CRC screening acceptability

The CRC screening acceptability index included items reflecting willingness to participate in CRC screening, acceptance of screening procedures and preferences, willingness to attend free cancer detection tests, recommendation of screening to others, and intended action after a positive screening result (Supplementary material 2, Table S2.2). The index ranged from 0 to 6 with higher values, indicating better CRC screening acceptability.

Practices

Two complementary indexes were constructed to assess CRC-related preventive behaviors: a) the Healthy Habits Practice Index, developed by summing responses of the 14 items indicating favorable lifestyle habits associated with CRC prevention, including tobacco use, alcohol consumption, dietary patterns, sedentary behavior, and physical activity (Supplementary material 2, Table S2.3). This index captures general lifestyle behaviors and should be interpreted as reflecting the broader behavioral risk profile of the population. Score ranged from 0 to 14, with higher scores reflecting more beneficial practices for reducing CRC risk; and b) the Health-Seeking Behavior Index, which comprised items reflecting contact with healthcare services and proactive engagement with preventive or diagnostic care. (Supplementary material 2, Table S2.4). This index ranged from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating more favorable health-seeking behavior.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis by refugee status (Jordanian or refugee) was conducted for all variables. Data were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and median and interquartile range for continuous variables. Differences among groups samples were assessed using the rank-sum test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact (for frequencies below five) tests for categorical variables.

To avoid odds ratio overestimations for prevalence rates above 10%, Poisson regression models with robust variance were used to calculate prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) median-based categories of knowledge, CRC screening acceptability, healthy habit practices, and health-seeking behavior across different sociodemographic, clinical, reproductive, and health factors.. The cluster sampling design was incorporated to the four separate logistic regression models built using a forward stepwise procedure, each including one of the dichotomous adequacy variables. The selection of factors retained in the final models was based on the likelihood ratio test. This pragmatic approach was used to facilitate the interpretation of factors associated with relatively higher versus lower KAP levels.

Results

A total of 897 individuals living in Mafraq governorate were included in this study, of whom 30% were registered as refugees. Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants by refugee status are detailed in Table 1. Approximately 50% of the respondents were female in both groups, with refugees being slightly younger than non-refugees (p < 0.001). Primary education was the most reported level of education in both groups; however, education level was significantly lower among refugees (p = 0.010). Housewife was the most frequently reported occupation in both groups, although refugees were more likely to be unemployed or self-employed, and less likely to be retired (p < 0.001). An 88% of non-refugees had public insurance, compared to 82% of refugees who were insured through international organizations (p < 0.001), predominantly the UNHCR. Refugees significantly relayed more on walking and faced longer travel distances than non-refugees (p < 0.001).

Monthly household income was significantly lower among refugees, with 85% of refugees earning ≤250 Jordan Dinars (JD) monthly compared to 27% of non-refugees (p < 0.001).

CRC and CRC screening knowledge and awareness

The median knowledge index score was found to be low among all respondents, with a total score of 6 and 7 points respectively recorded among Jordanians and refugees, out of a possible total of 32 points (Table 2). 66% of non-refugees had heard of CRC compared to 56% of refugees and awareness about early detection tests was also lower among refugees (13% vs. 20%, p = 0.009). Regarding the knowledge about population at risk of CRC, refugees correctly identified individuals over 50 years old significantly more often than non-refugees (30% vs. 24%), while a greater proportion of refugees believed cancer is a matter of chance and cannot be prevented (22% vs. 14%, p = 0.032). Most respondents believed that CRC always manifests symptoms, stomachaches being the most frequently mentioned and blood in stools the least mentioned. The most widely recognized risk factor was an unhealthy diet, followed by smoking.

Knowledge and information sources regarding CRC and CRC screening by refugee status.

The majority of respondents said that screening is always recommended, even for people who are considered to be in good health, and that early diagnosis of CRC increases the chance of survival.

The sources of information about CRC and CRC screening most mentioned by all respondents were family and friends, and most expressed interest in learning more about early detection. The preferred source to receive information was through health workers.

CRC screening acceptability

Most respondents said that they will undergo CRC screening if they were invited by the MoH, being significantly more frequently among refugees (89% vs. 84%, p = 0.034). Refugees also showed a stronger preference for fecal-based techniques (65% vs. 55%) over endoscopy (10% vs. 19%, p = 0.004) (Table 3). Both groups showed similarly high CRC screening acceptability score toward CRC screening, with a median index score of 5 out of 6, and 62% of refugees and 57% of non-refugees categorized as having a higher CRC screening acceptability score (Table 3).

Health-seeking behavior and CRC healthy habits by refugee status.

The median practice index score was 8 out of 14 in both groups, with higher healthy habits reported by 72% of non-refugees and 75% of refugees. (Table 4). Almost two thirds (64%) of participants had never smoked and almost the 100% of participants reported they have never drunk alcohol. Regarding nutrition, refugees consumed fewer daily servings of fruits and vegetables (median 1.0 vs. 2.0; p < 0.001) and red meat (median 0.0 vs. 1.0; p < 0.001), but slightly more legumes (p = 0.010) and processed meats (p = 0.037). Refugees spent more time seated (median 6.0 hours/day vs. 5.0; p = 0.005) and exercised less frequently (36% vs. 46%; p = 0.005) than non-refugees. Refugees were also more likely to walk or cycle for transport (67% vs. 57%; p = 0.001).

Source of information

A significant proportion of respondents (63%) had heard about CRC, with family and friends representing their primary source of information on the subject, as indicated by 73% of the respondents. Healthcare workers (33%) and social media (29%) were the preferred sources for receiving information regarding CRC (Table 2). When asked how they would like to be contacted to be invited for CRC screening, telephone calls were the most preferred method (63%) (Table 3).

Discussion

The objective of the present study was to gather evidence to inform the creation of a tailored screening campaign that meets the needs of diverse demographic groups, including refugees. The findings highlight the need to incorporate the social and behavioral determinants associated with refugee or non-refugee status into effective CRC prevention and early detection strategies.

The Jordanian reality has undergone a significant social transformation in recent years. The marked demographic disparities between refugee and non-refugee groups have significant implications for the planning and implementation of CRC screening initiatives in Jordan. Refugees’ concentration in camps, lower educational attainment, and limited formal employment suggest potential barriers to accessing healthcare information, navigating the health system, and engaging with preventive services (El Muhtaseb et al., 2025; Mansour et al., 2024). Their predominant reliance on international health insurance may also limit continuity of care and access to national screening programs (Gougazeh et al., 2024).

Economic vulnerability is another critical factor: 85% of refugees report monthly incomes below 250 JD, and many rely on walking as their primary mode of transportation to healthcare centers. These logistical and financial constraints likely hinder attendance at screening appointments. In contrast, non-refugees —who report higher income levels and greater access to private transportation— may encounter fewer structural barriers.

These disparities underscore the importance of designing CRC screening campaigns tailored to the distinct realities of refugee communities. Strategies should include health education, mobile screening units, community engagement initiatives, and coordination with international organizations providing refugee healthcare (Batarseh et al., 2024). Moreover, addressing the low awareness is essential to improving screening uptake, especially among refugees (Balaw et al., 2024).

Equity-driven approaches are vital to ensuring that CRC screening efforts are inclusive, accessible, and impactful across diverse demographic groups. Without such measures, national screening programs risk perpetuating existing health disparities rather than alleviating them (Batarseh et al., 2024).

Research conducted in Jordan shows a progressive increase in population awareness regarding CRC. A study conducted before 2009 indicated that only 26% of individuals had ever heard of CRC (Omran and Ismail, 2010). By 2021, this figure had risen to 50% (Jadallah et al., 2023), and findings from our 2024 survey reveal a further increase to 63%. Despite this upward trend, previous studies —and our own results— consistently show that detailed knowledge of CRC remains limited among the general population (Jadallah et al., 2023; Omran et al., 2015a; Taha et al., 2019). For instance, the only study that previously assessed awareness of risk factors did not include diet as a recognized factor, and obesity was among the least cited (Omran et al., 2015a). In contrast, our data identified unhealthy diet as the most frequently reported risk factor, with differences in awareness between refugees and non-refugees. Refugees were more likely to associate CRC with lifestyle-related risks such as poor diet, while non-refugees demonstrated greater awareness of CRC as a disease but less clarity regarding its specific risk factors. These discrepancies suggest that while general awareness has improved across demographic groups, targeted health education initiatives must consider contextual factors —such as living conditions and access to health information— to effectively address knowledge gaps.

CRC screening awareness remained comparatively low in our study. Omran and Ismail (2010) reported that only 22% of individuals had heard of screening methods, a figure that remains largely unchanged in our current findings and CRC screening tests awareness was even lower among refugees.

Regarding the CRC screening acceptability, earlier studies in Jordan reported moderate willingness, with a majority of participants expressing openness to screening in 2014 (Ahmad and Al-Gamal, 2014; Omran et al., 2015a). However, subsequent research has shown a marked increase, with willingness rates consistently exceeding 80% and a predominance of higher CRC screening acceptability score toward screening (Al-Qadire et al., 2022; Jadallah et al., 2023; Jarab et al., 2024; Omran et al., 2015a; Taha et al., 2019). These trends suggest a progressive improvement in public disposition towards CRC screening, which most studies attribute to growing awareness of CRC and its preventability. In our study, refugees exhibited greater willingness to undergo screening when invited by the MoH compared to non-refugees. This suggests that, despite facing structural and informational barriers, refugees may be more receptive to formal health system engagement when offered culturally and logistically appropriate opportunities.

In relation to the preferred screening technique, the present study revealed that most respondents expressed a preference for fecal-based methods. In contrast, a previous study identified colonoscopy as the prevailing preference technique in Jordan (Omran et al., 2015a). The literature suggests that preferences for specific screening techniques vary considerably between countries due to numerous factors (Alharbi et al., 2023; Calderwood et al., 2011; Cho et al., 2017; Mansfield et al., 2018; Marshall et al., 2007). It is, thus, pivotal to identify the preferences of patients, since it is a crucial factor in determining attendance at screening procedures. The cost of the screening has been found to be also a key factor in being willing to be screened as evidenced by the findings of this study, which align with those of previous research (Jadallah et al., 2023; Jarab et al., 2024; Omran et al., 2015a). We also found that it had a greater impact in the decision-making of the refugees, what could be attributable to the low monthly income reported by this group. Therefore, it is necessary that screening is provided free of charge in order to ensure a high level of engagement, and to involve international organizations such as UNRWA and UNHCR, as they are the main healthcare providers for refugees.

Smoking, alcohol consumption, lack of exercise and an unhealthy diet are the main CRC risk factors (Haggar and Boushey, 2009). Our study found an average level of risk behaviors, with alcohol consumption being least frequent, likely influenced by religious norms and in line with the national survey regarding healthy habits (STEPS, 2019). Smoking and low physical activity, highlighted by Khatatbeh et al. (2018), remain dominant risks, though smoking rates in our sample were lower than national figures, possibly due to participants’ older age and shifting patterns to alternative tobacco products in younger population (STEPS, 2019). Despite age differences, physical activity and diet levels were similar to national data, yet most participants did not meet WHO health standards. Notably, while Jordanian population showed greater adherence to healthy diets, refugees face barriers such as high food costs and prioritization of survival, limiting lifestyle improvements (Dator et al., 2018). Most refugees included in this study resided in refugee camps, where dietary patterns, transportation options, opportunities for physical activity, and access to healthcare services may be strongly influenced by structural and environmental conditions (UNHCR, 2026). Consequently, some of the differences observed in the Healthy Habits Index may not solely reflect individual preventive choices but also contextual factors such as food availability, economic constraints, humanitarian assistance mechanisms, and camp infrastructure.

Despite increased awareness of CRC, participation in early detection testing remains limited in Jordan both for nationals and refugees. Previous studies consistently reported low uptake, with fewer than 17% of individuals ever undergoing a cancer screening test (Ahmad and Al-Gamal, 2014; Jadallah et al., 2023; STEPS, 2019). Our findings showed an even lower rate with only 8% of respondents ever participating in any cancer screening. Refugees were particularly underrepresented in screening engagement, reflecting additional structural and socioeconomic barriers compared to non-refugees. These persistent gaps highlight the need for targeted efforts to promote early detection, especially within displaced and vulnerable populations.

A lack of knowledge and experience regarding CRC screening, coupled with the absence of an established protocol has been highlighted as a major barrier preventing healthcare workers (HCWs) from recommending screening to patients (Omran et al., 2015b). This aligns with our study findings, where only 17% of respondents had received CRC-related information from HCWs, and none reported having been proactively advised about screening by them. Interestingly, HCWs were still cited as the most preferred source of trustworthy information. Notably, this gap in information was more pronounced among refugee respondents, who reported significantly lower levels of engagement with HCWs compared to non-refugees, suggesting additional layers of access and trust issues. These disparities reinforce the urgent need to equip Jordanian HCWs with comprehensive training on CRC screening and its protocol. The MoH finalizes the official guidelines, HCWs can more confidently and consistently inform both refugee and non-refugee populations, promoting broader screening uptake and addressing equity gaps in cancer prevention.

The multivariate results highlight the structural and behavioral disparities between refugees and Jordanian population that influence CRC prevention and screening. Refugees, who were more likely to report poor health, unhealthy lifestyles, and lower formal education, also demonstrated reduced CRC knowledge and lower engagement with preventive practices. The analysis suggested that higher knowledge level and higher CRC screening acceptability score were closely linked to factors more prevalent among non-refugees such as urban residence, higher education, and access to healthcare resources.

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, cluster selection was based on the 2015 Population and Housing Census. Although demographic changes may have occurred between 2015 and 2024, including population growth, urbanization, and population movements among Syrian refugees, Mafraq has remained one of the principal refugee-hosting governorates in Jordan throughout this period, consistently accommodating a substantial proportion of the country's Syrian refugee population. Second, the study relied on self-reported information collected through face-to-face interviews. Although interviews were conducted privately and participants were assured of the confidentiality of their responses and the absence of any consequences associated with their answers, some degree of social desirability bias may have remained. As a result, CRC screening acceptability and preventive or health-seeking behaviors may have been overreported. Finally, the dichotomization of KAP indices using the sample median, while consistent with common practice in similar studies (Geng et al, 2026; Riccò et al, 2020; Sanjayanthan et al, 2026), produces categories that are sample-specific and does not reflect an objectively validated level of adequate knowledge, CRC screening acceptability, or practice.

Conclusions and recommendations

The structural disparities between refugees and non-refugees in Jordan reveal significant gaps in access to colorectal cancer prevention. The study’s findings highlight that factors such as formal education, health insurance coverage, and available transportation options directly influence individuals' knowledge, CRC screening acceptability, and preventive practices. Given that refugees face disadvantages across these dimensions, it is necessary to design inclusive policies that specifically address their needs.

To facilitate the participation of Syrian refugees, the involvement of international organizations, such as UNCHR, which provide healthcare to this demographic group is crucial. HCWs should be trained in CRC screening and counselling and equipped with the necessary tools to ensure effective communication and guidance. This includes culturally sensitive awareness campaigns, community interventions targeting women and vulnerable populations, and improvements in medical coverage and transportation infrastructure, covering the KAP gaps found in this study. Only through tailored and equitable responses would it be possible to reduce health disparities and promote early and effective colorectal cancer screening among refugee populations.
