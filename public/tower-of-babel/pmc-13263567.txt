Real-world outcomes of minimally invasive posterior thoracic and lumbar spine fusion surgery versus open surgery in the Finnish Spine Registry
Authors: Jukka Huttunen, Pietari Kinnunen, Nikolai Klimko, Ville Leinonen, Corinne Schut, Ankita Kambli, George Wright, Saara Hiltunen, Suvi Pakarinen, Suzanne Battaglia, Cindy Tong, Annalisa Michielli, Marike Hempel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA growing body of evidence is accumulating for the benefits of minimally invasive spine fusion. This study aimed to assess the differences in clinical outcomes, safety outcomes, medical resource use, and patient-related outcomes between Finnish patients undergoing posterior lumbar spinal fusion using an open approach (Open) or a minimally invasive approach with the VIPER PRIMETM System (MIS).MethodsThis retrospective observational study utilized clinical data from the Finnish Spine (FinSpine) Registry, MyHealth service, and Social Insurance Institution of Finland (Kela) to evaluate spine fusion surgeries performed at Kuopio University Hospital (KUH) and Oulu University Hospital (OUH) in Finland. The primary outcome was total index length of stay (LOS) for MIS versus Open surgery. All other analyses were exploratory and included clinical, medical resource use, safety, and patient-reported outcomes. Propensity score matching was used to ensure balance across the Open and MIS groups, and a Poisson regression model was employed to adjust for residual imbalance.ResultsA total of 522 patients were included in this study (139 MIS and 383 Open). There was no evidence to suggest a difference in LOS between the MIS and Open surgery groups after adjustment (P=0.20). No overall difference was found between hospitals either (P=0.75). Mean blood loss and skin-to-skin procedure time were lower in the MIS group. Mean Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), European Quality of Life Visual Analog Scale (EQ-VAS), European Quality of Life Visual Analog Scale (EQ-VAS), European Quality of Life Visual Analog Scale (EQ-VAS), and European Quality of Life Visual Analog Scale (EQ-VAS) were numerically lower and higher, respectively, than baseline at all evaluated timepoints up to 2 years. Sample sizes for the safety outcomes in this study were too low to draw any definitive conclusions.ConclusionsIn the present study, MIS techniques showed favorable clinical and healthcare resource use profiles, further supporting its use in spinal surgeries and potential value to patients and the healthcare system in Finland. Given that the rates of spinal fusion surgeries, particularly minimally invasive surgery, are expected to increase, understanding the impacts of these procedures on clinical, safety, and patient-related outcomes will be important to improve patient prognosis, and understanding the economic outcomes associated with these procedures will aid healthcare administrators and decision makers in assessing and planning for required resources.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this study that compared resource use, clinical, and patient-reported outcomes between minimally invasive surgery with the VIPER PRIMETM System (referred to as MIS hereafter) and Open surgery for spine fusion, there was no evidence to suggest a difference in length of stay (LOS) between the procedure types. Blood loss and skin-to-skin procedure time were lower with MIS compared with the Open surgery.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior literature has highlighted the advantages of minimally invasive spine surgery over conventional open spine fusion surgery, particularly in terms of shorter hospitalization and recovery times, as well as improved peri- and post-operative outcomes such as less perioperative blood loss and lower Oswestry Disability Index score.

• This study is the first to report the clinical, resource use, and patient-reported outcomes of minimally invasive spine surgery using the VIPER PRIMETM System in Finland, and more broadly in Europe.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This real-world study showed that patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery with the VIPER PRIMETM System have similar LOS to patients undergoing Open surgery, with added benefits of shorter procedure times and lower intraoperative blood loss. Overall, the MIS approach demonstrated favourable clinical and patient-specific outcomes for spine surgery.

Introduction

Background

Spinal degenerative disease in adults, encompassing conditions such as degenerative disc disease, spine curvature deformities or scoliosis, spinal stenosis, and spondylolisthesis, arises from the gradual wear and tear of spinal discs and joints, and may lead to symptoms including pain, reduced mobility, and in some cases, severe neurological symptoms (1-3). Patients commonly experience radicular pain, often in the lower limbs, or low back pain (LBP) (4). The estimated annual prevalence of radicular pain, sometimes referred to as LBP with leg pain travelling below the knee, may vary from 9.9% to 25% in the general population and is a significant cause of disability (5,6). LBP is also a major source of disability across several countries and was estimated to affect over half a billion individuals worldwide in 2020 (7). Meta-analytic data from a search performed in 2016 found that amongst individuals with LBP in Europe, the incidence of degenerative spine disease was 5.7% (8). Spinal degenerative disease, radicular pain, and LBP significantly impact patient quality of life, often necessitating medical interventions ranging from physical therapy and pain management to surgical procedures such as spinal fusion (9).

Around 8,500 spinal surgery procedures are performed in Finland annually (9). In the period 1997–2016, the incidence of lumbar spine fusions increased by 289% (from 9 per 100,000 person-years to 35 per 100,000 person-years) in Finland, followed by a slight decrease in incidence in 2018 (30 per 100,000 person-years) (10). With an aging world population, the demand for spinal fusion procedures is expected to continue increasing, particularly in patients aged 75 years and older (11). Thoracic interbody fusion (TIF) and lumbar interbody fusion (LIF) are types of widely performed spinal fusions to treat degenerative diseases. These procedures involve the placement of an implant within the intervertebral space after discectomy and endplate preparation (12,13). The benefits of spine fusion include stabilization of the affected area of pain, indirect decompression of the neural elements, restoration of lordosis, and correction of deformities (12). Spine fusions may be performed using traditional open techniques or through minimally invasive approaches. Conventional open procedures require larger incisions, and extensive muscle dissection and retraction, contributing to muscle and tissue damage (14). A growing body of literature has been demonstrating the advantages of minimally invasive spine surgery over conventional open spine fusion surgery, particularly in terms of shorter hospitalization and recovery times, as well as improved peri- and post-operative outcomes such as less perioperative blood loss and lower Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores (3,15-17).

Introduction of all-in-one technologies such as the VIPER PRIMETM System may lead to increased efficiencies and decreased operative duration associated with minimally invasive surgery (MIS) (18-20). Conventional minimally invasive percutaneous pedicle screw insertion requires several steps, including skin incision and dissection of fascia, insertion of a probe or Jamshidi needle, removal of the probe’s inner sleeve for guidewire insertion, withdrawal of the probe jacket and tap, and finally, screw insertion. The use of guidewires in conventional MIS techniques carries a known risk of complications, including fracture and migration of the guidewire, breach of an anatomical structure, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, infection, and other spinal hardware failure (e.g., pedicle screw pull-out) (21,22). The all-in-one VIPER PRIMETM System for minimally invasive spine surgery (referred to as “MIS” hereafter) was the first system on the market to introduce a screw tip design and stylet fully controlled by the screwdriver, allowing surgeons to target the pedicles and insert the pedicle screw with a single instrument pass, thus eliminating the need for guidewires, Jamshidi needles, and pedicle preparation instruments (18). Moreover, usage of the VIPER PRIMETM system has been shown to decrease screw insertion time and reduce the number of surgical instruments used compared to conventional methods, while maintaining accuracy in pedicle screw placement (18-20).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Although the benefits and drawbacks of minimally invasive spinal fusion compared to open surgery have been reported previously (3,15-17), minimal findings have been published on the real-world outcomes of VIPER PRIMETM for minimally invasive surgery in Finland, and more broadly, in the European Union.

Objective

This study aimed to assess the differences in clinical outcomes, safety outcomes, medical resource use, and patient-related outcomes between Finnish patients undergoing posterior lumbar spinal fusion using an open approach or a minimally invasive approach with the VIPER PRIMETM System (MIS). The results of this study will provide a better understanding of the real-world impact of MIS devices such as the VIPER PRIMETM System on healthcare resource use and prognosis for patients undergoing spinal fusion procedures in Finland, as well as aid in decision-making to inform hospital guidelines that improve overall patient management. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-1-0025/rc).

Methods

Study design and outcomes

This study is a retrospective, comparative, observational study conducted using data collected over a 6-year period from May 2017 to August 2023 from patients in the Finnish Spine (FinSpine) Registry who underwent either conventional open spinal fusion (Open group) or minimally invasive spinal fusion with the VIPER PRIMETM System (MIS group). No other minimally invasive techniques were assessed. Patients ≥18 years who underwent spine fusion for one, two, or three levels of the thoracic or lumbar regions were included if they met the inclusion criteria (Table S1).

The primary outcome of this study was the total length of stay (LOS) (in days) of the index procedure for Open versus MIS procedures. LOS is a well-established outcome used to evaluate the potential benefits of minimally invasive procedures compared with open surgery (23-25). All secondary outcomes were exploratory and included: medical resource use outcomes (index procedure skin-to-skin time; in minutes), clinical outcomes (blood loss; in milliliters), safety outcomes [spinal surgery-related complications (i.e., complications directly related to the spinal fusion procedure such as vascular or spinal cord injury) and general surgery-related complications (e.g., infection); yes/no from baseline to 90 days, 90 days to one year, and one to two years post-procedure], patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) [European Quality of Life Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS), VAS for pain, ODI scores at baseline, 90 days, one year, and two years]; patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) (procedure satisfaction; at 90 days, one year, and two years post-index date), opioid medication use (number of patients utilizing opioids at 30 days and six months post-index procedure), and sick leave (number of patients on sick leave at 90 days, one year, and two years post-index procedure).

Data collection

Findata granted a data permit for combining data from the the two hospitals of KUH and OUH for this study (diary number: THL/1105/14.02.00/2022). An amended data permit was later granted with diary number THL/3796/14.06.00/2024. The FinSpine Registry was used to obtain pre-operative, intra-operative and post-operative data. This is a nationwide, computer-based spine registry with the goal of monitoring surgical activity, quality of surgery, long-term outcomes, and effectiveness of treatment (9). To complement the clinical registry data, MyHealth service, a digital platform for connecting patients and healthcare providers, was used to obtain PROM and PREM data. Data on medication use and sick leaves were collected from the Social Insurance Institution of Finland (Kela). Safety data (complications) were determined using specific complication fields in the FinSpine Registry during hospital stay and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes after hospital discharge (Table S2).

Electronic health records were obtained from two Finnish hospitals—Kuopio University Hospital (KUH) and Oulu University Hospital (OUH). KUH and OUH are key hospitals performing spine surgery in Finland, which have seen increases of 325% and 178%, respectively, in the annual rate of fusion surgeries performed between 1997 and 2018 (26). Data from KUH and OUH electronic health records were used to assess specific clinical variables. The date of the spinal fusion surgery was considered the index date (baseline). Data collection was initiated 90 days after the study period ended to ensure a standardized 90-day follow-up period post-surgery for all included patients.

Data security measures were taken to protect the confidentiality of the data and ensure patient information security. To comply with the Act of Secondary Use of Health and Social data in Finland, exact frequencies have not been reported for certain outcomes and cannot be inferred from other frequencies. These outcomes are reported in the following format: “<” followed by a number exceeding the number of patients for whom the outcome was reported (e.g., “<5” patients).

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Findata granted a permit for data use from the Finnish Spine Registry for this retrospective, non-interventional study. The permit exempted from the need to obtain informed consent as this study did not involve human subject research.

Sample size and power

Sample size and statistical power for this study were evaluated based on the testing of the primary endpoint of LOS with the Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test from the Lv et al., study (27) [mean ± standard deviation (SD) LOS of 7.1±3.3 days in the Open group and 5.4±2.8 days in the MIS group]. With Type 1 error at 5%, 10,000 simulations showed that 100 patients in the MIS group and 200 patients in the Open group would provide >90% statistical power for the primary endpoint.

Propensity score matching and Poisson regression analysis

Propensity score matching was performed to ensure balance across the two surgery groups. Patients from the MIS group were matched to patients from the Open group using caliper-based 1:2 nearest neighbor matching without replacement (R-package MatchIt) (28). The final key variables used in matching were age, body mass index (BMI), gender, smoking status, surgeon, hospital, instrumented levels, number of intervertebral levels, radiculopathy, opioid use 90 days before baseline, sick leave 90 days before baseline, surgery type, previous surgery within five years, ossification material, day of the week the procedure was performed, and ODI, EQ-VAS and VAS pain scores at baseline. Standardized differences were used to assess the post-match balance of variables used during matching. A balanced match was defined as an absolute value of standardized difference <0.1 for a matched variable. Several covariates were still unbalanced after matching including surgeon, hospital, instrumented levels, number of levels, gender, BMI, smoking, sick leave before baseline, surgery type, weekday of the procedure, and all PROM scores except VAS neck pain score. The largest difference between the comparison groups was caused by hospital imbalance as they mostly originated from different hospitals.

As several variables were unbalanced after matching, a multivariate Poisson regression model was employed to adjust for the residual unbalanced variables between the MIS and Open groups in the primary analysis and to enhance the robustness of the analysis. A multivariate Poisson regression model was employed for this as LOS by its nature is count data. The model incorporated an interaction term between the study groups (Open and MIS) and the hospital to account for potential variations in the effect across different hospitals.

The matched patient cohorts were utilized for explorative, descriptive analyses for all secondary outcomes.

Statistical analyses

All variables and outcomes were analyzed using standard descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are presented as means, SDs, and/or medians. Binary outcomes are presented as proportions with confidence intervals (CI). All analyses were conducted using R studio. A P<0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Note that this study was only powered to support statistical significance for the primary endpoint. All other analyses and reported values are considered exploratory.

Subgroup analyses

In consultation with key opinion leaders (KOLs) in Finland, any key variables that could have an impact on the primary and secondary outcomes were identified for subgroup analyses. Variables used to stratify the population into subgroups included: fusion type (fusion alone or fusion and decompression), surgery type (primary, new, or revision surgery), and the number of levels treated (one, two, or three). Primary surgery encompassed only the very first operation on the spine, new surgeries encompassed surgeries on a different level of a spine previously operated on, and revision surgeries (excluding re-instrumentation) encompassed patients undergoing spine surgery on the same level as a previous operation.

Many subgroups lacked sufficient sample sizes to draw meaningful conclusions. As such, secondary outcomes for subgroups were included in the main text only if the outcome was reported for more than 20 patients. Results for outcomes reported in less than 20 patients are included in the Appendix for transparency despite low frequencies. Due to the increased risk of Type I errors inherent in multiple testing, particularly in secondary and subgroup analyses, these outcomes were considered exploratory. As such, results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Sensitivity analysis

A post-hoc sensitivity analysis of the primary outcome (LOS) was performed to address potential hospital effects and allow for a more nuanced understanding of the heterogeneity between hospitals. Propensity score matching was applied to match patients in the MIS and Open group at KUH based on a 1:1 ratio. Similar to the main analysis, the primary outcome for this subgroup analysis for KUH was performed using multivariate Poisson regression to test for statistically significant differences in the outcome. This sensitivity analysis was conducted for KUH only due to the insufficient sample size of the MIS group in OUH.

Missing data and quality control

Missing data was incorporated as an additional factor level for smoking and as a value of zero for continuous variables (BMI, baseline ODI score, baseline EQ-VAS score, and baseline VAS pain) to avoid imputation bias and to study the association of missing values to outcome.

Quality control procedures were implemented to ensure the accuracy of the data. A systematic data cleaning process was conducted to identify and correct any outliers and errors in the dataset, such as erroneous dates and other recordings. Final validation checks were conducted to ensure the required standards were met.

Results

Patient demographics

Data from 522 adult patients [139 minimally invasive surgery with VIPER PRIMETM (MIS), 383 Open] who underwent one-, two-, or three-level posterior spinal fusion of the thoracic or lumbar spine were included in the study. A total of 414 patients were included in the propensity score-matched analysis: 138 patients in the MIS group and 276 in the Open surgery group (Table 1). The mean age ± SD at baseline in the matched population was 57.9±13.1 and 56.9±13.0 years in MIS and Open surgery groups, respectively. A notable hospital effect was observed with 89.1% of MIS patients treated in KUH and 66.7% of the Open surgery patients treated in OUH.

Procedural demographics

Of the 522 patients who underwent spinal fusion surgery, primary surgery was the most common surgery type conducted, with 51.4% and 60.1% of patients in the MIS and Open groups undergoing a primary surgery, respectively (Table 2). The MIS group has a higher proportion of revision surgeries (surgery to the same level as a previous operation) compared with the Open surgery group (39.1% vs. 29.7%). Approximately 30.4% (MIS: 32.6%, Open: 29.3%) of patients had a previous spine surgery within the five years prior to baseline. The majority (62.3%) of MIS surgeries were conducted on one intervertebral level. The number of intervertebral levels was more distributed in Open surgeries, with 35.9%, 40.2%, and 23.9% of surgeries being one-, two-, and three-level surgeries, respectively.

Primary outcome

LOS

After propensity score matching, 138 MIS patients and 276 open surgery patients were included in the primary outcome analysis (Table 3). The overall mean ± SD LOS for the propensity-matched cohorts was 3.26±1.88 days for MIS and 4.2±2.33 days for Open (Table 3, Figure S1). The median LOS was three and four days for MIS and Open surgery, respectively. Both the MIS and Open group showed a similar overall distribution in LOS (Figure S2). When further grouped by the number of levels fused (one, two, and three), mean LOS was 3.32±1.93, 2.91±1.61, and 3.65±2.09 days in the MIS group and 4.18±3.21, 4.19±1.68, and 4.26±1.64 days in the Open group.

As mentioned in the “Methods”, several covariates were still unbalanced after propensity score matching. Thus, Poisson regression analyses were conducted to adjust for the residual imbalance post-propensity score matching for the primary endpoint only. The results for this are presented in Table S3. There was no evidence to support a difference in LOS between the MIS and Open surgery groups after adjustment (P=0.20). No overall difference was found between hospitals either (P=0.75). The regression analysis found ossification material of own bone crista to be associated with a longer LOS compared with a combination of ossification materials (P<0.001). A higher EQ-VAS score at baseline was also found to be associated with a shorter LOS (P=0.001).

Secondary/exploratory outcomes

Blood loss

Mean blood loss was lower in the MIS group (mean ± SD: 312.3±239.0 mL) compared with the Open group (mean ± SD: 496.9±266.0 mL) (Table 4, Figure S3A; see Table S4 for full results). Additionally, in the subgroup analysis for surgery type, mean blood loss was 268.7±200.7 mL in the MIS group and 531.6±248.3 mL in the Open group for primary surgery.

Skin-to-skin time

The overall mean skin-to-skin (procedure) time was shorter in the MIS group (mean ± SD: 151.2±45.1 minutes) compared with the Open group (mean ± SD: 200.6±49.0 minutes) (Table 5 and Figure S3B; see Table S5 for full results). When assessed by the number of levels fused (one, two, and three), skin-to-skin time was 141.1±41.2, 152.3±37.0, and 199.9±49.5 minutes in the MIS group and 184.1±40.5, 206.3±48.9, and 214.6±54.2 minutes in the Open group. In the analysis of skin-to-skin procedure time by year of procedure, mean time for MIS was numerically longer than Open surgery in 2018, which coincided with when the VIPER PRIMETM was first introduced (Figure S4). In contrast to this, a trend for lower skin-to-skin time with MIS was observed, beginning in 2019 (Figure S4). In the subgroup analysis of surgery type, skin-to-skin time for primary surgery was 152.8±44.7 minutes for MIS and 202.6±49.6 minutes for Open surgery. Mean skin-to-skin time for revision surgery was 148.4±44.9 minutes for MIS and 197.9±50.2 minutes for Open surgery.

Complications (safety)

A total of 5.0% of patients in the MIS and 10.8% in Open surgery group experienced spinal surgery-related complications (i.e., complications directly related to the spinal fusion procedure) during their hospital stay (Table S6). Only dura lesions and perioperative fractures were reported in both groups. Additional spinal surgery-related complications were reported in the Open surgery group; however, due to low frequencies, exact ‘other complications’ are not reported to preserve patient confidentiality.

Approximately 7.2% and 5.7% of patients in the MIS and Open surgery groups, respectively, experienced general surgery-related complications during their hospital stay (Table S6). The most common general surgical complication in both groups was a new radicular finding or symptom. Additional general surgery complications including pneumonia, urinary tract infection, implant malposition, and wrong level were reported in the Open group but not in the MIS group; however, frequencies of these were low.

Few complications were reported at 90 days, 1 year, and 2 years post-index procedure. Less than 5 were reported within 90 days post-index procedure in the MIS group and 11 complications were reported in the Open surgery group. Between 90 days and one year, <10 and <5 complications were reported in the MIS and Open groups, respectively. Between 1- and 2-year post-index procedure, there were no additional complications reported in the MIS group, while <5 patients had general surgery-related infections in open surgery group.

Patient-related outcomes

The mean scores for the ODI questionnaire, EQ-VAS, and VAS pain scores (lower back, lower limbs, neck, and upper limbs) were similar between the MIS and Open surgery groups at baseline, 90 days, one year, and two years (Table S7).

The ODI questionnaire provides an assessment of the level of function (disability) in daily activities for on a scale ranging between 0 and 100, with 0 being no disability to 100 as the most severe disability (29). In the literature, minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs) reported for ODI vary across spinal pathologies and surgical techniques, with MCIDs ranging from 5 to 23 (30-35). Mean ODI scores were numerically lower than baseline at all timepoints. Mean ODI scores were 43.00±16.09, 28.40±16.00, 23.48±17.37, and 20.72±17.08 at baseline, 90 days (Δ −12.08±16.32), one year (Δ −16.94±16.04), and two years (Δ −19.65±18.30) post-index procedure in the MIS group, respectively. In the Open group, mean ODI scores were 43.78±16.52, 27.10±17.99, 19.43±17.78, and 21.65±18.92 at baseline, 90 days (Δ −13.89±18.60), one year (Δ −18.49±18.03), and two years (Δ −18.51±16.96) post-index procedure, respectively.

EQ-VAS incorporates a scale which records the patients’ self-reported assessment of their health on a scale from 0 (worst health imaginable) to 100 (best health imaginable) (36). In the literature, MCIDs reported for spinal pathologies and surgical techniques typically range from 8 to 15 for EQ-VAS (37). Mean EQ-VAS scores were numerically higher than baseline at all three timepoints for both MIS [mean ± SD: 42.97±22.39, 63.21±21.04, 61.76±22.99, and 64.69±24.53 at baseline, 90 days (Δ 17.00±25.09), one year (Δ 10.78±25.07), and two years (Δ 21.00±23.31) post-index, respectively] and Open surgery [mean ± SD: 41.68±21.74, 62.63±24.81, 61.49±28.02, and 63.62±27.82 at baseline, 90 days (Δ 16.52±29.19), one year (Δ 16.01±32.87), and two years (Δ 18.51±35.65) post-index, respectively]. VAS for pain is a commonly used measure of pain intensity on a 10-point (100 mm) scale from 0 (0 mm; no pain) to 10 (100 mm; severe pain) (38). MCIDs for VAS pain scores vary substantially across the literature, ranging from 5 to 46 mm for spine surgery and back pain (30,32-34,39). Mean VAS pain scores for the lower back were numerically lower than baseline at all three timepoints for both MIS [mean ± SD: 65.54±23.28, 32.78±24.61, 37.26±26.32, and 34.94±28.18 at baseline, 90 days (Δ −28.60±27.22), one year (Δ −24.07±28.18), and two years (Δ −21.27±31.16) post-index, respectively] and Open surgery groups [mean ± SD: 64.46±25.52, 30.58±25.98, 33.31±29.54, and 33.01±29.44 at baseline, 90 days (Δ −25.68±29.01), one year (Δ −22.37±33.63), and two years (Δ −21.60±36.38) post-index, respectively]. VAS lower limb and upper limb pain scores were numerically lower than baseline at all three timepoints across both surgery types. VAS neck pain scores were numerically lower than baseline at all three timepoints for MIS, while there was a small increase in scores from baseline in the Open surgery group at the 1-year and 2-year timepoints.

Most patients (>66%) were satisfied with their surgery at 90 days. After one year, approximately 85% of the patients in both groups indicated they would still get surgical treatment in a similar situation, knowing the results achieved based on their spine surgery (Table S8).

Sick leave

Between discharge and 90 days post-index procedure, approximately half of the patients took a general sick leave (51.4% MIS; 44.2% Open; Table S9). Around one-third of the patients took sick leave at some point between 90 days and one-year post-index procedure (28.3% MIS; 33.7% Open). The number of patients who took sick leave further decreased to <12.3% and 7.2% between one and two years after index procedure for MIS and Open surgery, respectively.

Opioid use

Compared with during the hospital stay (MIS: <84.1%; Open: 92.0%), there was a general trend for decreased strong opioid use at both one month (MIS: 20.2%; Open: 20.7%) and at six months (MIS: <27.5%; Open: 24.3%). Mild opioid use was increased at six months post-index procedure (MIS: 17.4%; Open: 16.3%) compared with during the hospital stay (MIS: <11.6%; Open: <6.2%). Tramadol and oxycodone were the most used mild and strong opioids, respectively. Fentanyl was used only in OUH Open surgery group.

Sensitivity analysis: KUH-only

After 1:1 propensity score matching in the KUH-only population, 93 MIS patients and 93 Open surgery patients were included in the primary outcome analysis (Table S10). After matching, mean LOS was 3.26±1.75 days for MIS and 3.82±3.32 days for Open surgery, whereas median LOS was 3 days for both surgery groups at KUH (Table 6 and Figure S5; see Table S11 for full results).

Discussion

Findings and comparison with similar research

Degenerative spine conditions and LBP result in significant healthcare resource use, which is expected to continue to increase with an aging world population (40,41). In Finland alone, the direct annual cost of lumbar spine surgery is over € 20 million, with around 8,500 spine surgical procedures performed each year (9,42). Increasing demands and resource constraints have highlighted the need for finding solutions to decrease the burden on hospitals without compromising patient outcomes. This analysis sought to understand the differences between MIS and conventional open surgery in economic (LOS, procedure time), clinical and safety (blood loss, complications, opioid use, and sick leave), and patient-reported (ODI, EQ-VAS, VAS Pain, PREM) outcomes.

In the main analysis of LOS between propensity score matched groups, MIS was associated with a median LOS of 3 days whereas Open surgery was associated with a median LOS of 4 days. Since several covariates were still unbalanced after propensity score matching, Poisson regression analysis was conducted, which found no evidence to support a difference in LOS between MIS and Open surgery. Subgroup analyses of the primary endpoint found LOS to be numerically lower for MIS for all surgery types (primary, new, and revision) and all number of levels (one, two, and three). The trend towards shorter LOS associated with MIS supports a growing body of evidence on the shortened LOS associated with minimally invasive spinal fusion procedures (29,30). LOS is a commonly reported endpoint in the literature on spinal fusion procedures. It is an important, objective measure that may be reflective of the overall speed of peri-operative morbidity and post-operative recovery. It is an especially important outcome for MIS, as these procedures are specifically designed to reduce tissue trauma, complications, and post-operative pain, which in turn can influence length of hospital stay. While LOS was not statistically significant in the present study, these findings may still be valuable for informing discharge protocols post-operation. It should be noted that patient clinical condition and readiness were the key drivers of discharge status; however, the authors acknowledge that post-surgical LOS may be impacted by factors including regional differences in population demographics, institution-specific allocation of resources, and other non-clinical factors (e.g., discharge criteria, rehabilitation access). Larger studies are needed to better understand the regional, population, administrative, and hospital factors that may influence LOS in Finland.

Results from the Poisson regression analysis indicated that own bone crista use as the ossification material for spine fusion was associated with significantly longer LOS. This is in line with the results of a systematic literature review which found that harvesting from the iliac bone crest for spinal fusions led to a slight increase in LOS compared with other graft substitutes or no grafts (5.93±2.09 vs. 5.51±2.05 days) (43), as well as another prospective study which found iliac crest bone grafts to be associated with significantly extended LOS and operative time (44).

Further, a sensitivity analysis with data from KUH only was undertaken to address the imbalance in the distribution of MIS and Open fusion surgeries between KUH and OUH. Similar to the main analysis, results from this sensitivity analysis showed no evidence to support a difference in LOS for MIS compared with the Open group. Regional variation in the rates of spinal surgeries across five University Hospital catchment areas has been previously reported in Finland (26). In 2018, there was greater than a two-fold difference in the regional rate of lumbar fusion surgery, ranging from a rate of 14 per 100,000 person-years for spinal fusion procedures in the Tampere area to 34 per 100,000 person-years in the Kuopio area (26). It was posited that this variation was due in part to the division of work between neurosurgeons and orthopedic spine surgeons, as well as regional differences in general morbidity, as resource allocation should be similar within a publicly funded healthcare system (26). As such, further research and data from more hospitals is needed to better understand the regional, population, administrative, and hospital factors that may influence LOS.

In the KUH sensitivity analysis, no difference in LOS was observed between primary and revision surgeries, which is in line with the findings of other published literature (45,46). In contrast, the Poisson regression results of the sensitivity analysis for KUH found primary surgery to be associated with significantly longer LOS compared with new surgery (surgery to a different level from one that was operated on previously; P=0.02); no such difference was found in the Poisson regression analysis for the main analysis involving both hospitals (P=0.05). At present, literature comparing LOS between primary and new surgery, and factors that may impact LOS following these procedures is limited. It is possible that patient education and expectations may differ for a patient who has previously undergone a spine surgery and result in a more streamlined hospital discharge procedure or improved home care; however, literature on the impact of patient education on LOS is mixed, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions (47-49).

In the Poisson regression analysis of both the main analysis and the sensitivity analysis, a higher EQ-VAS score at baseline was associated with a significantly shorter LOS. A higher baseline EQ-VAS score is indicative of improved self-perceived pre-operative patient health (36). A study of predictors for complications following adult spinal deformity surgery found that the patient group that experienced at least one intra- or peri-operative complication had significantly worse PROMs at baseline (50) compared with patients who did not experience any complications. While EQ-VAS was not assessed in the study by Scheer et al., it is possible that a higher baseline perceived health status may be correlated with a lower risk of complications and improved post-operative outcomes such as shorter LOS (50). While there is wide variability in patient outcomes following spine surgery, it is possible that patients with better general health or fewer comorbidities may be reporting higher EQ-VAS scores at baseline and showing improved operative outcomes and shorter LOS.

Reduced blood loss and shorter procedure times have been frequently reported as key benefits of minimally invasive surgery. In the current study, the MIS group showed a reduction in perioperative blood loss, which aligns with the trend of decreased perioperative blood loss reported across the literature for minimally invasive techniques (51-53). Additionally, this study found MIS to be associated with lower skin-to-skin procedure time, thus supporting the efficiencies associated with minimally invasive surgery. Furthermore, when stratified by the year during which procedures were performed, skin-to-skin procedure time in the MIS group was longer than in the Open group in 2018; however, beginning in 2019, a trend for decreased procedure time was observed in the MIS group. Procedure times for the Open group remained largely similar between 2019 and 2023. Surgeons first began exploring MIS procedures within orthopedics at KUH in 2018 and official MIS procedures started at both KUH and OUH in 2019 (54). The increased efficiencies for MIS over the period between 2019 and 2023 are likely attributable to surgeons obtaining experience and comfort with MIS. This is in line with the various previous reports of the learning curve associated with MIS spine fusions wherein decreases in procedure time have been observed as surgeons gain experience and proficiency with MIS techniques (55,56). Interestingly, KOLs who perform spine fusion procedures at KUH and OUH noted that the time savings from MIS often allow for the operating theater to be booked for additional surgeries, such as another fusion procedure, or lumbar decompression, one-to-two-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, or an intradural spine tumor operation. While anecdotal, this is an unanticipated benefit observed for MIS procedures that should be considered during treatment decision-making.

Previous literature has also reported that longer operative times for spinal fusion are associated with a substantially increased risk of complications including post-operative infections and thrombosis (57,58). Moreover, decreased procedure time and increased surgeon experience have been shown to be associated with decreased complication rates (55,56). Although sample sizes for the safety outcomes in this study were too low to draw any definitive conclusions, a trend for decreased spinal surgery-related complications during hospital stay was observed for MIS, with an overall complication rate of 5.0% for MIS and 10.8% for Open surgery.

In general, quality of life outcomes for ODI, EQ-VAS, and VAS Pain scores were improved from baseline in the two years following fusion surgery in both the MIS and Open groups. In line with other published literature, the majority of patients who underwent spinal fusion surgery were satisfied and would choose to undergo the procedure again, regardless of whether it is performed through MIS or Open techniques (53,59,60). Patterns of opioid use and sick leave taken by patients were similar between MIS and Open surgery for all timepoints analyzed.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several strengths. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to report on real-world outcomes and medical resource use in Finland, and more broadly in the European Union, with the use of the VIPER PRIMETM system for MIS. Additionally, this study reported on a larger patient population than previous studies, had a longer follow-up time, and assessed outcomes that have not been previously evaluated for the VIPER PRIMETM System such as LOS, PROMs, and complications in comparison with open surgery (18-20).

As with most retrospective studies, a key limitation of this study is missing data. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, loss of follow-up and missing data was unavoidable. Some baseline variables had varying degrees of missing data which may have affected the accuracy of propensity score matching and analyses. Data on surgery-related complications were missing for approximately 30% of patients in the registry. Importantly, data collection occurred during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, which likely impacted follow-up and data collection for long-term outcomes (i.e., complications, PROMs, sick leave, and opioid use) in some patients. The method for handling missing data in the analysis was decided based on patterns of missingness, which was assessed through a systematic examination of the dataset, focusing on variables with incomplete information. The pattern was found to be missing at random, with certain observed characteristics influencing the likelihood of data being missing. For example, PROM data was the most likely to be missing for older people. Finally, although measures were taken to address data quality, inaccurate or missing data, coding errors, or discrepancies in data entry can lead to potential misclassification of exposure or outcomes and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Propensity score matching was performed to isolate the true treatment effect, but several covariates still remained imbalanced. As such, to increase the robustness of the analysis for the primary outcome of LOS, a multivariate Poisson regression model was employed that adjusted for the residual imbalance between the MIS and Open groups. Although this is a well-established statistical method, regression adjustment after matching may not eliminate all covariate imbalance and may lead to overfitting, unstable estimates and additional residual imbalances. In addition, although outcomes for several subgroups were reported, small sample sizes hindered the capacity to draw any meaningful comparative conclusions. Due to these limitations, all secondary and subgroup outcomes are considered exploratory and should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Lastly, it is to be noted that the scope and findings of this study are specific to the VIPER PRIMETM System; however, the results are likely generalizable to minimally invasive spine instrumentation more broadly. While technical differences may exist between MIS systems, the underlying surgical principle of achieving intersegmental stabilization through fusion of the motion segment remains the same.

Implications and actions needed

Since minimally invasive surgical techniques were first introduced, data from literature has continued to support the value of them compared with conventional spinal fusion surgery, particularly in terms of resource use and patient outcomes. The VIPER PRIMETM System was the first system on the market to introduce a screw tip design and stylet fully controlled by the screwdriver, thus eliminating the need for guidewires, Jamshidi needles, and pedicle preparation instruments which have been associated with operative complications (18,19,61,62). This all-in-one approach reported significantly reduced pedicle screw placement time by 49% utilizing a transforaminal approach and by 48% with a lateral approach (61). Additionally, the VIPER PRIMETM has demonstrated patient benefits including reduced blood loss, reduced radiation exposure during insertion, and low rates of post-operative complications (15,18,19). Moreover, the use of fewer surgical trays with VIPER PRIMETM has been shown to translate to reduced waste and processing costs. In an economic assessment from a European hospital perspective, the use of the VIPER PRIMETM System was associated with significant cost savings compared to open surgery (63). These savings were reported to be driven by reduced hospital stay costs, lower costs for sterilizing fewer surgical trays, and fewer blood transfusions due to less intraoperative bleeding (63).

Overall, this study has highlighted the potential benefits of the VIPER PRIMETM System in Finland by leveraging real-world data from the FinSpine Registry. This real-world study demonstrated that MIS System offers comparable outcomes to open surgery in terms of LOS, with added benefits of shorter procedure times and lower intraoperative blood loss. These findings may be leveraged by hospital administrations to inform decisions on patient and resource management, guide hospital policies and surgeon workflow, and improve patient prognosis and satisfaction in Finland.

Conclusions

The prevalences of spine conditions and LBP are predicted to continue increasing with the rapidly aging global population (40,41). Moreover, there is a substantial burden associated with the decreased quality of life experienced by adults with spine conditions (9,41). Given that the rates of spinal fusion surgeries, particularly MIS, are expected to increase, understanding the impacts of these procedures on clinical, safety, and patient-related outcomes will be important to improve patient prognosis. As well, understanding the economic outcomes associated with these procedures will aid healthcare administrators and decision makers in assessing and planning for required resources. This real-world study demonstrated that there was no evidence to support a difference is LOS between minimally invasive spine surgery using the VIPER PRIMETM System (MIS) and Open surgery, but MIS may offer benefits related to reduced procedure times and decreased perioperative blood loss. The minimally invasive approach with VIPER PRIMETM has demonstrated clinical efficacy and economic benefits in prior studies (18-20,63). Further research into the benefits of MIS in additional hospitals in Finland, with larger sample sizes, as well as the impact of excluding three-level surgeries should be explored. In the present study, MIS techniques with VIPER PRIMETM offered comparable outcomes to open surgery in terms of LOS, with added benefits of shorter procedure times and lower intraoperative blood loss, supporting its use in spinal surgeries and potential value to patients and the healthcare system in Finland.
