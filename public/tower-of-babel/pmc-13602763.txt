Peptidomics-guided 3D structural-mechanistic investigation of bovine colostrum peptides reveals multi-targeted in silico and in vitro antidiabetic and antioxidant potential
Authors: Shashank M. Patil, Ramith Ramu, Kalirajan Rajagopal, Vivek Hamse Kameshwar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multi-target inhibition of α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and human aldose reductase can significantly address postprandial hyperglycemia and subsequent diabetic complications, yet bovine colostrum peptides remain largely unscreened against these enzymes. Using a peptidomics-guided, integrated in silico and in vitro approach, we identified three lead peptides and highlighted Peptide P2 (GPAGPQGPR) as a common lead inhibitor, showing favorable predicted binding and interaction profiles relative to the reference inhibitors. A dedicated 3D-structural analysis, including residue mapping, RMSF profiles, hydrophobicity surfaces, and intramolecular contact networks, suggested that the glycine-proline-rich scaffold of P2 may support predicted conformational adaptability and interactions with multiple enzyme targets. These findings were supported by in vitro enzyme inhibition and kinetic analyses, which indicated that P2 was consistent with a competitive inhibition model, while radical scavenging analysis indicated its antioxidant potential. These findings establish bovine colostrum as a potential multi-target antidiabetic and antioxidant peptide source, and present a structure-informed, reproducible framework for food-derived peptide discovery.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a major global health burden, and its progression is associated with oxidative stress and chronic complications, which continue to challenge the current pharmacological therapies (Mohd et al., 2025). Existing antidiabetic drugs often target a single metabolic pathway and may be associated with adverse effects, creating a need for safe, multifunctional alternatives (Artasensi et al., 2020). In this context, food-derived bioactive peptides have attracted considerable interest because of their low toxicity, favorable bioavailability, and ability to modulate multiple biological targets involved in glucose metabolism (Padhiyar et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023).

Bovine colostrum is an enriched source of diverse protein fractions including whey, casein, and cream-derived fat globule membrane proteins capable of yielding bioactive peptides with multifunctional properties, upon hydrolysis (Khan et al., 2024; Patil, Jayaram and Ramu, 2025). Although bovine colostrum is well recognized for its nutritional, antimicrobial, and antioxidant properties, its peptide repertoire is yet to be explored to its full potential for antidiabetic applications.

Inhibition of α-amylase (AM), α-glucosidase (AG) and human aldose reductase (HAR) plays a critical role, as these enzymes govern key steps in carbohydrate metabolism. AM and AG catalyze the breakdown of complex carbohydrates into glucose, HAR diverts excess glucose into sorbitol via the polyol pathway, a route implicated in diabetic complications (Ramu et al., 2014). Together, this combined enzymatic activity synergistically accelerates reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and oxidative stress, resulting in cellular damage associated with diabetes mellitus (Rebolledo & Dato, 2005; Yamaguchi & Nagai, 2025). This interdependence indicates a simultaneous need for enzyme inhibition and free radical scavenging, rather than addressing either mechanism in isolation.

Previous studies reporting antihyperglycemic effects of colostrum or milk-derived peptides have focused on individual inhibition of AM and AG, rather than multi-enzyme targeting approaches (Antony & Vijayan, 2021; Khan et al., 2024; Ramani et al., 2024). In addition, few studies using fermented milk products, or peptide sources other than bovine colostrum have also not highlighted the multi-target efficiency (Baba et al., 2021; Mohanty et al., 2016; Mudgil et al., 2024). Furthermore, studies solely highlight either in silico analyses or explore different food sources including camel milk, egg white, soy protein, and plants (Lu et al., 2023), which leaves a critical gap. Moreover, experimental evidence for peptides capable of simultaneously targeting AM, AG, and HAR is scarce. Therefore, identifying natural peptides with multi-enzyme inhibition potential may provide a comprehensive strategy to simultaneously control postprandial hyperglycemia, targeting pathways associated with long-term diabetic complications.

In this study, we investigated the antidiabetic potential of peptides previously identified from Indian bovine colostrum hydrolysates (Patil, Jayaram and Ramu, 2025) using an integrated computational and experimental workflow. Candidate peptides were initially screened based on their predicted bioactivity and physicochemical properties and subsequently evaluated using a combination of molecular modeling and computational approaches. Molecular docking was used to identify favorable binding interactions between the peptides and catalytic residues of the selected targets, followed by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to assess the persistence of peptide-target interactions and the stability of the resulting complexes based on trajectory-derived parameters. In addition, MM/GBSA analysis was used as an approximate computational estimation of binding energies to support comparative assessment of peptide–target interactions (Bhachoo & Beuming, 2017). The lead peptides were further evaluated in silico for pharmacokinetic properties, stability, and gastrointestinal susceptibility. They were subjected to 3D modeling, predicting hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity balance, steric compatibility, molecular flexibility, and electrostatic potential (Fetse et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2018), with the outcomes used to examine the molecular features that could govern peptide-enzyme interactions. Finally, the synthesized peptides were experimentally validated through in vitro enzyme inhibition and enzyme kinetic assays, complemented by antioxidant assays, thereby integrating computational predictions with experimental evidence to elucidate their potential bioactivity and the molecular features underlying peptide–enzyme interactions.

Materials and methods

Materials

Acarbose (≥95%, CAS: 56180–94-0), α-Glucosidase from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (≥10 units/mg, CAS: 9001-42-7) and α-Amylase from porcine pancreas (≥1000 units/mg) were purchased from Merck, Darmstadt, Germany. Zenarestat (CAS: 112733–06-9) was purchased from ChemSRC, Shanghai, China. Human aldose reductase assay kit (ab273276) from Abcam, Cambridge, Cambridgeshire, UK. Bioactive peptides (P1–P3) were custom-synthesized on an Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis platform by S Biochem, Thrissur, Kerala, India (≥98% purity, RP-HPLC purified, LC–MS confirmed). All peptides were received in lyophilized form and used directly for further analyses. All the other chemicals and reagents purchased were of analytical grade.

Colostrum fractionation and peptidomic profiling

Cow (Bos taurus) colostrum sample collected immediately after parturition was defatted (7500 ×g, 4 °C, 30 min) to separate liquid (whey) and solid (cream) portions. Whey was delactosed using a 3 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) membrane dialysis (72 h at 4 °C), with regular water changes. Casein was removed from this dialysate with acidic treatment (pH 4.6 with 0.2 N acetic acid, 7500 ×g, 4 °C, 30 min), yielding the final whey protein fraction. The pelleted casein was washed (PBS pH 7.2 and water; 5000 ×g, 4 °C, 10 min). Fat was washed under similar conditions with 0.01 M PBS (pH 7.2), and was frozen. It was churned using a blender, followed by serum recovery (4500 ×g, 4 °C, 20 min). Collected serum was defatted (7500 ×g, 4 °C, 30 min) and lipids were removed using ice-cold acetone (1,4 v/v) to retrieve fat-globule membrane proteins. The proteins from three fractions whey proteome (WP), colostrum fat globule membrane proteome (CFGMP) and casein proteome (CP) were dissolved in 3.5% KCl-HCl buffer (100 mM, pH 2), digested with pepsin (4% w/v) and pancreatin (4% w/v), subsequently at 37 °C for 4 h. Pepsin and pancreatin digestion model was used to physiologically represent in vitro model of human gastrointestinal proteolysis of colostrum proteins, and because pancreatin's multi-enzyme composition (trypsin, chymotrypsin, and accessory proteases) better co-relates with intestinal-phase digestion (Patil, Jayaram and Ramu, 2025; Patil, Ramu and Goh, 2025).

The resultant hydrolysates were filtered by Amicon® Centrifugal Filters (3 kDa MWCO, 12,000 ×g, 37 °C, 45 min), desalted via Empore® C18 Solid Phase Extraction (SPE) Discs (methanol conditioning, buffer equilibration). The hydrolysates were lyophilized and were subjected to Nano-LC-ESI-MS/MS on a Q-Exactive Plus Orbitrap (ThermoFisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) coupled to an Easy n-LC 1200. The LC and MS methods are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Fig. S2, respectively (Supplementary Material S1). Peptides were identified using MS/MS search with fixed-threshold auto-validation, enzyme specificity set to pepsin and pancreatin, with the reference Bos taurus used in the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1%. High-confidence identifications (q < 0.01, >95%) were validated in Proteome Discoverer 2.2, and intact mass/peptide mapping performed in BioPharma Finder 2.0 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) (Patil, Ramu and Goh, 2025). The identified peptides were used as a colostrum-derived, peptidomic-guided, drug discovery dataset.

Virtual screening and selection of lead bioactive peptides

Bioactive peptides were screened from the pooled peptidome through a stepwise in silico approach, retaining their original sequence as identified (Patil, Jayaram and Ramu, 2025). Initially, peptide sequences were filtered based on length (5–20 amino acids) and molecular weight (<3 kDa to improve absorption potential), while allergenic, toxic, and highly hydrophobic or aggregation-prone sequences were excluded (Zaky et al., 2021). In addition, repeated sequences within each hydrolysate fractions (WP, CFGMP and CP) were also removed. Filtered peptides were then analyzed using activity prediction tool PeptideRanker (http://distilldeep.ucd.ie/PeptideRanker/) (Mooney et al., 2012), where peptides with a score > 0.5 were prioritized as candidate bioactive peptides for subsequent computational and experimental screening. Peptides were sketched in Maestro 2D Sketcher (Schrödinger, LLC), and energy minimization was performed using the OPLS4 force field. This stepwise screening and multi-target prioritization are consistent with general interaction-landscape mapping frameworks used to support polypharmacology (Daina et al., 2019; Gfeller et al., 2013).

The protein 3D structures were retrieved from RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/) for the target enzymes AM (PDB ID: 1DHK), AG (PDB ID: 3A4A), and HAR (PDB ID: 1IEI). Protein preparation included removal of water molecules from ligand binding sites, addition of hydrogens, charge state assignment, and restrained energy minimization to the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) convergence of 0.30 Å using the Protein Preparation Wizard in Maestro 2022–1 (Sastry et al., 2013).

The docking grids for AM, AG, and HAR were generated to encompass the respective catalytic active-site regions, following the binding-site definition reported by a previous study (Maradesha et al., 2022). Receptor grid boxes were centered on key active-site residues to encompass the catalytic binding pocket. Molecular docking was carried out using the Extra Precision (XP) mode of Glide with a rigid receptor model, while allowing full conformational flexibility of the peptide ligands. This rigid-receptor, flexible-ligand docking approach was selected because, for short flexible peptide ligands such as P1–P3, ligand-side conformational sampling captures most of the relevant binding-mode diversity, consistent with prior peptide-enzyme docking studies. For each peptide, the top ten ranked poses were retained based on Glide XP scoring, and docking scores and binding interactions were analyzed using Maestro visualization tools in accordance with a previous study (Kumar & Piramanayagam, 2021). No induced-fit docking or explicit receptor side-chain flexibility was employed during the docking process, because of the substantial additional computational cost across three receptors and multiple peptide candidates. This is noted here as a direction for future refinement of the highest-priority complexes.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the Desmond module of Schrödinger to evaluate the dynamic stability and interaction profile of the peptide–protein docked complexes, with Acarbose and Zenarestat-bound complexes serving as controls (Rácz et al., 2022). The protein–peptide complexes obtained from molecular docking simulation were embedded in an orthorhombic box solvated with the TIP3P water model, and neutralized with appropriate counterions to mimic physiological conditions (Rey et al., 2023). The OPLS4 force field was employed for parameterization.

System minimization was done using the steepest descent method to remove clashes, followed by an 8-stage Desmond relaxation protocol. This included short restrained constant number of particles, constant volume, and constant temperature (NVT) and constant number of particles, constant pressure, and constant temperature (NPT) simulations to equilibrate solvent and ions around the solute, gradually releasing positional restraints. The production MD was then run for 100 ns in the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 bar pressure without restraints, ensuring stable and reproducible dynamics (Wróblewski et al., 2025). Trajectories were recorded at 100 ps intervals. Post-MD analyses, including RMSD, root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), protein–peptide contacts, and hydrogen-bonding profiles, were performed using Schrödinger Simulation Event Analysis and Simulation Interaction Diagram to evaluate structural stability and binding persistence throughout the simulations (Ivánczi et al., 2023). MM/GBSA analysis was performed using the Variable Dielectric Surface Generalized Born 2.0 (VSGB 2.0) implicit solvent model and the OPLS4 force field, using representative snapshots extracted from the MD trajectories of the peptide-enzyme complexes to estimate their binding free energies (Bhachoo & Beuming, 2017; Li et al., 2011). The calculated ΔGbind values were used to comparatively assess the predicted binding energetics of the candidate peptides, with more negative values indicating more favorable interactions. The resulting values were considered approximate computational estimates rather than experimental binding affinities (Genheden & Ryde, 2015).

Peptide 3D modeling and physicochemical analysis

Peptide sequences (P1: HMWPGDIK, P2: GPAGPQGPR, and P3: DKVLVFFK) were submitted to the PEP-FOLD 4.0 server (https://bioserv.rpbs.univ-paris-diderot.fr/services/PEP-FOLD4/) in a standard one-letter code to generate 3D structures. For each peptide, 100 independent simulations were performed using the server's default parameters, generating 100 putative conformational models. The default settings were selected to approximate physiological conditions for conformational sampling. Models were ranked by sOPEP energy and clustered to select the most probable conformations (Rey et al., 2023). Peptide RMSF and contact mapping of amino acid residues were identified using CABS-flex 3.0 server (https://lcbio.pl/cabsflex3/), which simulates peptide structure and dynamics by combining coarse-grained modeling with all-atom reconstruction. Out of the models generated, the “prime” model with highest accuracy was selected for further analysis (Wróblewski et al., 2025).

In silico prediction of pharmacokinetic properties, stability, and gastrointestinal resistance

To further evaluate the pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles (ADMET: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity), the peptide sequences were analyzed using the ADMETLab 3.0 server (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/) (Fu et al., 2024). Peptide stability and gastrointestinal resistance were subsequently predicted using DeepSTABp (https://csb-deepstabp.bio.rptu.de/) (Jung et al., 2023) and ExPASy PeptideCutter (https://web.expasy.org/peptide_cutter/) (Gasteiger et al., 2005), respectively.

Solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS)

The lead peptides (P1 to P3) were custom synthesized by S Biochem (Thrissur, Kerala, India) using standard Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis procedures (Selvam et al., 2015). Peptides were purified by RP-HPLC to at least 98% purity and identities were confirmed by LC–MS, as provided by the manufacturer.

In vitro multi-enzyme inhibition, kinetics, and free radical scavenging

The three leading bioactive peptides were subjected to in vitro multi-enzyme inhibition, targeting carbohydrate digestive enzymes (AM and AG) and HAR. P1 (HMWPGDIK), P2 (GPAGPQGPR), and P3 (DKVLVFFK) were prepared in sterile water and tested at final assay concentrations ranging from 0 to 50 μM (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 μM). Control inhibitors acarbose (AM, AG) and zenarestat (HAR) were prepared as stock solutions in 5% (v/v) aqueous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and tested at final assay concentrations ranging from 0 to 1000 μg/mL, with the final DMSO concentration in inhibitor-treated wells maintained at ≤1% (v/v). A vehicle control containing the same final concentration of DMSO (≤1% v/v) but without inhibitor was included in each assay to account for any solvent-related effects on enzyme activity. For consistency and ease of comparison, IC₅₀ values were calculated and expressed in both μM and μg/mL for all peptides and reference inhibitors.

The inhibition of AM (EC 3.2.1.1, type-VI B porcine pancreatic amylase), AG (EC 3.2.1.20, type-1 α-glucosidase), and HAR included 1% corn starch, 10 mM para-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG), and 5 mM D-L-glyceraldehyde as substrates, respectively. The enzyme inhibition was assessed by comparing absorbance change between the peptides and the control drugs. The results were reported as a percentage of enzyme inhibition using eq. 1 (Hamdi et al., 2023; Ramu et al., 2014).(1)Enzyme Inhibition%=Acontrol−AsampleAcontrol×100

Necessary blanks were included in each assay (see Section 2.8 for replicate design). The inhibitory activity of the peptides and control drugs was represented by a least-squares regression line of logarithmic concentrations plotted against per cent inhibition, resulting in half-maximal inhibitory concentration or IC50 values (μg/mL and μM). Compared to the control, the IC50 value indicates the concentration of test samples required to inhibit enzyme activity by 50%. The inhibition kinetics of AM, AG, and HAR were determined according to the previous study (Ramu et al., 2014).

The activity of AM, AG, and HAR was measured in the presence or absence of the peptides at various doses (IC20, IC40 and IC60) using the respective substrates mentioned above. Lineweaver–Burk plots (1/v versus 1/[S]) were generated for each enzyme to analyze the inhibition mechanism and to obtain the corresponding Vmax and Km values. The inhibition constant (Ki) was subsequently calculated from secondary replots derived from the double-reciprocal data (Ramu et al., 2014).

The in vitro free radical scavenging assays were conducted for bioactive peptides using DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picryl-hydrazyl-hydrate), superoxide, and ABTS (2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)), based on the previous study (Ramu et al., 2014). The positive control used was butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA). The following formula (eq. 2) was used to calculate the absorbance of the antioxidant activity in percentage (%).(2)Radical Scavenging%=Acontrol−AsampleAcontrol×100

The calculated EC50 values, expressed in both μM and μg/mL, for all peptides and BHA represent their potential radical-scavenging activity at the half-maximal effective concentration, corresponding to 50% scavenging of free cationic and anionic radicals.

To ensure consistency between concentration units, IC50, Ki and EC50 values have also been given in μM using the molecular weight of each peptide (HMWPGDIK: 982.470 g/mol, GPAGPQGPR: 835.430 g/mol, and DKVLVFFK: 994.590 g/mol). Statistical significance

In vitro enzyme inhibition, inhibition kinetics, and free-radical scavenging assays were performed in three independent assay runs (n = 3). For each assay run, the required reagents were freshly prepared from stock solutions, and the complete set of experimental groups, including P1, P2, P3, and the corresponding positive control, was evaluated on a separate 96-well plate. Replicate wells within each plate were used for assay measurements and were not considered independent experimental replicates. IC50 and EC50 values were determined by nonlinear regression from the concentration–response data obtained in each independent assay run and are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons among multiple groups were carried out using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Peptidomic library construction and lead peptide selection

From the initial peptide datasets (167 from WP, 64 from CFGMP, and 82 from CP) identified previously by the authors (Patil, Jayaram and Ramu, 2025), duplicate sequences were removed within each fraction, while peptides predicted to be of >3 kDa, 5–20 amino acid length, allergenic, toxic, highly hydrophobic, or aggregation-prone were also excluded. Sequence redundancy was addressed only on an intra-fraction basis; peptides occurring across different fractions were retained to enable independent evaluation of the bioactive potential of each fraction. Following this initial screening, 69 peptides from WP, 25 from CFGMP, and 34 from CP were retained, corresponding to the removal of 98, 39, and 48 peptides, respectively. The retained peptides were subsequently subjected to physicochemical property and PeptideRanker analysis, after which an additional 50, 12, and 20 peptides were excluded from the WP, CFGMP, and CP fractions, respectively (Patil, Jayaram and Ramu, 2025). The remaining 19 (WP), 13 (CFGMP), and 14 (CP) peptides with predicted bioactivity (PeptideRanker Score > 0.5) were then carried forward for molecular docking studies.

PeptideRanker uses an N-to-1 neural network (N1−NN) predictor, trained on 17,532 non-redundant peptide sequences from the BIOPEP, PeptideDB, Antimicrobial Peptide Database 2 (APD2), and Collection of Anti-Microbial Peptides (CAMP) databases (Mooney et al., 2012). The model assigns each peptide a bioactivity probability score ranging from 0 to 1, with scores ≥0.5 indicating predicted bioactivity. Two independent predictors are used for short (4–20 residues) and long (>20 residues) peptides to account for their distinct compositional characteristics. The long- and short-peptide predictors achieved Matthew's correlation coefficients (MCC) of 0.74 and 0.54 and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values of 0.94 and 0.83, respectively, on independent test sets. The false-positive rates (FPRs) were reduced from 11 to 16% to 2–6% for the long- and short-peptide predictors, respectively. Accordingly, the ≥0.5 threshold was retained as an initial screening criterion in the present study (Mooney et al., 2012). This threshold was used to identify candidates with predicted bioactive potential, rather than as definitive evidence of biological activity, as subsequent physicochemical filtering, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, and in vitro validation provided additional stages of candidate evaluation. This sequential approach enabled peptides identified by the initial prediction to undergo progressively more stringent computational and experimental assessment. Similar PeptideRanker-based screening has been applied in previous studies of bioactive peptides from buffalo colostrum (Ashok et al., 2017). No toxicity or allergenicity alerts were observed among these peptides, listed in Supplementary Table S1 (Supplementary Material S1). The corresponding LC-MS chromatograms are provided in Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4, while the peptide selection criteria are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S5 (Supplementary Material S1).

Similar peptidomic profiling studies have identified comparable peptide numbers, with one comprehensive investigation detecting 314 proteins in goat colostrum whey and 524 in mature milk (Sun et al., 2020), while another study on buffalo colostrum identified 49 peptides with dipeptidyl peptidase-IV (DPP-IV) inhibitory motifs (Ashok & Aparna, 2024). The dominance of whey-derived peptides observed in this work corroborates multiple reports emphasizing whey as the primary protein reservoir in colostrum, containing the most abundant bioactive peptide precursors (Brandelli et al., 2015; Quintieri et al., 2025). A peptidomics comparison of bovine colostrum and mature milk identified differentially expressed endogenous peptides (DEEPs) with predicted bioactivities, including angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibition, immunomodulation, and anticancer potential, supported by Gene Ontology analysis (Ning, Yang, Zhu, Luo and Yue, 2024). Similarly, comparative peptidomic profiling of human colostrum and mature milk revealed dynamic changes in the endogenous peptidome during lactation and identified multiple candidate bioactive peptides with potential physiological functions (Ning et al., 2024). However, these studies have not provided a comprehensive, multi-target applicable dataset evaluated for toxicity and allergenicity using standardized in silico tools. In this study, none of the selected peptides were predicted to be allergenic, in contrast to food-derived peptide reports where tools such as AlgPred, AllerCatPro, and AllerTOP predicted allergenic sequences, particularly in soybean and wheat oligopeptides (Xiong et al., 2023). These in silico predictions support peptide prioritization but do not replace experimental safety assessment.

To identify bioactive peptides, three selection parameters were assessed alongside toxicity and allergenicity. Peptides with a peptide rank greater than 0.5 were prioritized as bioactive candidates with a higher likelihood of meaningful biological target interaction (Bizzotto et al., 2024). Candidates with molecular weights up to 3 kDa were retained, with a preference for shorter sequences below 2000 Da. Additionally, peptides consisting of 5–20 amino acid residues were favored to ensure optimal size, bioavailability, and structural stability (Renukuntla et al., 2013). Studies on collagen hydrolysates have shown comparable bioavailability for peptides of 2000 and 5000 Da, confirming that the upper limit of 2000 Da supports efficient absorption without compromising activity (Virgilio et al., 2024).

The selected amino acid range aligns with structural patterns reported for biologically active peptides, with potential antidiabetic (6.94 ± 3.16 residues), antihypertensive (7.03 ± 4.11), and antioxidant (6.69 ± 5.18) peptides typically falling within this interval, whereas antimicrobial peptides tend to be longer (22.19 ± 14.75 residues) (Bizzotto et al., 2024). Peptides within 3–20 amino acids exhibit enhanced enzyme inhibitory, antimicrobial, and antioxidant activities due to their higher membrane permeability, structural integrity, and resistance to degradation (Fadimu et al., 2022; Fetse et al., 2023; Jakubczyk et al., 2020). All reported bioactive peptides were within this range and displayed favorable stability, absorption, and target-interaction profiles.

Among the screened peptides, P1 (HMWPGDIK from WP), P2 (GPAGPQGPR from CFGMP) and P3 (DKVLVFFK from CP) were predicted to have consistently high binding affinities across multiple targets. These lead bioactive peptides were prioritized for further analysis based on their multi-target potential and docking scores. The three lead bioactive peptides were predicted to exhibit potentially stronger binding interactions with AM than the control drug Acarbose. The relevance of this finding is supported by AM‘s crucial role in carbohydrate digestion and its hydrophobic core formed by catalytic residues Asp197, Glu233, and Asp300. Among the peptides, P2 was predicted to have the favorable binding affinity with a Glide Score of −12.82 kcal/mol, compared with −9.76 kcal/mol for the reference control Acarbose.

Comparing these outcomes with established dairy-derived AM inhibitory peptides from the literature provides a structural basis for their relative inhibitory potential. For instance, a camel milk-derived peptide, IMEQQQTEDEQQDK (−11.3 kcal/mol), was reported to interact with key active site residues Asp300 and His305 of AM (Mudgil et al., 2024). Similarly, a goat milk-derived peptide, DQHQKAMKPWTQPK (−11.3 kcal), interacted with residues including Trp58, Trp59, Tyr62, Asp300, and His305. In addition, VKETMVPKHK and HANAGAAGH from fermented sheep milk showed a binding energy of −9.6 and − 10.3 kcal/mol, respectively (Mudgil et al., 2024). In comparison, higher binding affinity was observed for the bioactive peptides. In contrast, Asp300 and Trp59 were identified as common interactions in this analysis, which closely mirrors the interaction profiles of these potent dairy-derived inhibitors. Residues Asp300 and Trp59 could act as key residues for AM inhibition in silico screening. The lower Glide scores of the three lead bioactive peptides compared to Acarbose highlight their enhanced binding affinity to AM.

The amphipathic nature of these peptides is integral to their inhibitory mechanism against AM. Their hydrophobic segments could penetrate deeply into the enzyme's hydrophobic catalytic core, while the hydrophilic and charged residues could engage in hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with critical active site residues such as Asp300 and Glu233 (Kabelka & Vácha, 2018). This dual-mode interaction may stabilize the peptide-enzyme complex and could further enhance binding affinity. Positively charged amino acids, including Arg and Lys, could play a significant role in forming these bonds, contributing substantially to the peptides' inhibitory efficacy. Additionally, residue position within the peptide sequence, particularly at the third and fifth N-terminal positions, has been reported to correlate significantly with AM inhibitory activity, indicating positional preferences that may contribute to inhibition (Li et al., 2024).

For AG, catalytic residues span Asp69 to Asp352 within Domain A, including Asp215, Glu277, and Asp352, and the pocket-shaped active site is formed by domains A and B, within which the peptides were predicted to bind efficiently and to occupy the same site as the co-crystallized inhibitor. P2 was predicted to have the highest affinity with a Glide Score of −13.21 kcal/mol, followed by P1 (−12.39) and P3 (−10.90 kcal/mol), whereas acarbose was predicted to have the Glide Score of −10.48 kcal/mol. All three peptides were indicated with lower Glide scores, hence higher binding affinity compared to Acarbose.

This finding aligns with previous research that identified 2 peptides YLGYLEQLLR (−9.6 kcal/mol) and TKVIPYVRYL (−9.6 kcal/mol) from buffalo casein as the potential inhibitors of AG (Zhao et al., 2022). Other food-derived peptides, such as those from hemp seed protein hydrolysates, where peptides like NPVSLPGR, LSAERGFLY, and PDDVLANAF were predicted to have lower binding energies (ranging from −8.4 to −8.7 kcal/mol) than acarbose (−8.1 kcal/mol), suggesting higher inhibitory potential (Mengyuan et al., 2025). Compared with previously reported peptides, those identified in the present study showed more favorable predicted binding energies, suggesting potentially stronger interactions with the target. The ability of the peptides to achieve lower binding energies than acarbose indicates their potential as more effective or safer alternatives to existing synthetic drugs, addressing the growing demand for peptide AG inhibitors (Akbarian et al., 2022; Mengyuan et al., 2025). This shows the importance of both hydrophobic interactions and residue-specific hydrogen bonds for AG inhibition (Akbarian et al., 2022). Studies have identified specific amino acids such as Pro and Leu as important for AG binding. Peptide inhibitors have been reported to occupy the active-site pocket or to engage residues involved in catalysis and substrate recognition, which can reduce substrate access and slow turnover (Asen et al., 2022; Asen et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023).

Docking analysis predicted that the peptides could bind within the AG active-site pocket, with an amphipathic binding mode. Hydrophobic side chains were predicted to orient toward non-polar subpockets, while polar and charged residues were positioned to potentially form hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with catalytic or substrate-recognition residues, which may contribute to favorable peptide–enzyme interactions (Caporale et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2007). For instance, the involvement of pocket-lining residues such as Asp68, together with the proximity of these contacts to the narrowed entrance region of the AG active site (including His280), suggests that the peptides may interfere with substrate access by occupying or perturbing the active-site region (Askarzadeh et al., 2022; Yamamoto et al., 2010). Docking studies of peanut meal hydrolysates reported peptide–AG complexes with binding energies of −4.4 to −8.6 kcal/mol, in which the peptides are accommodated within the catalytic pocket and stabilized by hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, salt bridges, and π–π interactions. By occupying the active site, these peptides may restrict substrate access and potentially interfere with enzyme–substrate complex formation (Yang et al., 2023). This detailed understanding of the specific interactions provides a foundation for further rational design of peptide-based inhibitors.

The catalytic site of HAR, which is precisely the binding site of the co-crystallized ligand Zenarestat, involves residues Trp20, Tyr48, His110, and Arg296. The peptides were predicted to occupy the same inhibitor binding site. P2, P3, and P1 were predicted to have Glide Scores of −12.73, −12.56, and − 10.51 kcal/mol, respectively, whereas Zenarestat, the control drug, was predicted to have a Glide Score of −9.89 kcal/mol. Arg296 was identified as the common residue bound by P1 and Zenarestat, as peptides could span over the catalytic site between Trp111 and Arg294. Similar to AG and AM, all three peptides were predicted to have lower Glide scores compared to Zenarestat, indicating their higher binding affinity. In the multiple protein-peptide docking scenarios, P2 was consistently identified as a common lead across the three enzyme targets, based on its predicted binding affinities (Fig. 1). Existing research on HAR inhibitors, including other peptides, confirms the importance of interactions with residues like Trp20, Tyr48, and His110 for inhibition (Antony & Vijayan, 2021; Gakpey et al., 2025). The active site of HAR is known to be a large, deep, ellipsoidal cavity that often requires an aromatic ring system for hydrophobic or pi-pi stacking interactions (Dréanic et al., 2017). The amphipathic character of the peptides may facilitate their orientation within this cavity, with hydrophobic moieties positioned toward the non-polar core and hydrophilic groups oriented toward polar residues at the periphery, potentially contributing to favorable peptide–enzyme interactions (Kabelka & Vácha, 2018). Although numerous natural compounds, including chlorogenic acid (Naeem et al., 2013), flavonoids (Umamaheswari et al., 2012; Yasir et al., 2024), ginsenosides (Ali et al., 2022), and synthetic drugs (Muhammad et al., 2020) have been reported as HAR inhibitors, the literature does not explicitly describe dairy-derived peptides that inhibit human aldose reductase.

Studies on milk peptides for other enzymes, such as cholesterol esterase or ACE, or general bioactive properties, were found (Chamata et al., 2020; Pipaliya et al., 2024), but no specific HAR inhibition data for dairy peptides were identified. This absence suggests a potential research gap in the existing literature regarding dairy-derived peptides as HAR inhibitors. Therefore, this work represents a significant contribution in this area, warranting further investigation into dairy-derived peptides as potential therapeutics for diabetic complications. Docking analyses of HAR inhibitors indicate that productive binding typically involves the catalytic pocket. Key contacts are frequently reported around Tyr48, His110, and Trp111, with additional contributions from nearby residues such as Thr113 (Naeem et al., 2013).

This study reveals favorable predicted binding affinities and interaction profiles of the peptides with AM, AG, and HAR, highlighting the combined contribution of hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and hydrophobic contacts within enzyme active sites. Across all three targets, the peptides were predicted to have lower Glide scores than the reference inhibitors (acarbose and zenarestat) and indicated comparable or, depending on the complex, higher numbers of stabilizing polar contacts. Binding patterns for AG and AM align with mechanisms reported for food-derived peptide inhibitors, supporting their antidiabetic potential. Notably, the predicted binding patterns of colostrum peptides to HAR, which is rarely discussed for dairy-derived peptides, highlight a relevant research avenue.

Molecular dynamics simulation and MM/GBSA analysis

RMSD profiles across AM, AG, and HAR systems consistently predicted P2 as potentially the most conformationally stable peptide, with favorable interactions across the systems. A complex was considered equilibrated once the backbone RMSD reached a plateau, with the least/zero fluctuations in the 100-ns trajectory. In the AM-P2 complex, both protein and peptide were predicted to exhibit coordinated stabilization during the simulation, indicating equilibration after 60 ns, maintaining closely aligned trajectories with notable convergence at 15–20 ns and 50–55 ns, a synchrony absent in AM-P1 and AM-P3, with equilibration indicated only after 70 ns. Acarbose was predicted to have equilibrated after 50 ns but indicated weaker protein–ligand RMSD correlation. In the AG systems, P2 indicated two stable regions (40–60 ns and 90–100 ns), with protein–peptide RMSD alignment indication after 90 ns, and was predicted to have cooperative stabilization. Although P2 and acarbose were predicted to reach equilibration at approximately 90 ns, the AG–acarbose complex indicated persistent protein RMSD oscillations (1.5–4 Å), and was predicted to have weaker dynamic stability, whereas P1 and P3 were predicted to reach equilibration after 50 ns and 70 ns, respectively. For HAR, P2 indicated early protein stabilization (post-10 ns) with three distinct RMSD alignments within 10–50 ns and was predicted to attain equilibrium after 75 ns, earlier than P3 (85 ns) and P1 (90 ns), while Zenarestat was predicted to get equilibrated after 70 ns, but indicated limited dynamic correlation. Collectively, P2 was predicted to have greater dynamic stability than P1, P3, and the control drugs (acarbose and zenarestat) across all three enzyme targets, indicating sustained protein-peptide RMSD alignment, frequent interaction events, and stable binding throughout the equilibrated trajectories. Its balanced hydrophilic–hydrophobic residue composition likely facilitates conformational adaptability within the enzyme active sites. Protein RMSF (P-RMSF) profiles predicted that peptide binding could broadly stabilize backbone flexibility across AM and AG targets relative to their reference inhibitors. In AM, peptide-bound complexes were predicted to have reduced residue-level fluctuations compared to the acarbose-bound form, with P-RMSF values largely remaining below ∼3.2 Å, while acarbose-bound AM reached peaks approaching ∼4.5 Å. Differences among the peptide-bound AM complexes were predicted to be modest, with elevated mobility confined primarily to terminal regions. A similar trend was observed in AG, where peptide-bound complexes were predicted to have low backbone fluctuations with increased values restricted to the N- and C-termini, whereas acarbose-treated AG displayed comparatively higher terminal flexibility. For HAR, zenarestat was predicted to have low backbone P-RMSF, making a less pronounced differentiation among peptide complexes. Collectively, the P-RMSF analysis suggested relatively favorable dynamic behavior of the peptide-AM and peptide-AG complexes, with P2 showing comparatively favorable predicted complex stability across the targets. However, inter-peptide differences in P-RMSF were subtle, and this metric alone should not serve as the sole criterion for ranking ligand stabilization across all three enzymes. Comparative P-RMSF profiles of P2 against control drugs are given in Fig. 2, with P1 and P3 comparisons in Supplementary Fig. S7 (Supplementary Material S1).

Protein-ligand contacts were predicted using interaction fractions, where values ≥0.7 indicate an interaction persisting for ≥70% of the simulation, and values >1.0 reflect multiple simultaneous contacts formed by a single residue (Bhachoo & Beuming, 2017). In AM, all peptides were predicted to have higher overall interaction fractions than in AG, reflecting a more hydrophobic and persistently interactive binding pocket. P1 and P2 were predicted to have the highest interaction fractions (2.5 each), indicating extensive and highly persistent contacts, while P3 and acarbose were predicted with 2.0 interaction fractions each. Notably, P3 was predicted to have a greater proportion of hydrophobic interactions than the other peptides, suggesting a distinct non-polar binding mode within the AM active site.

In AG, P1 was predicted to have predominant water bridges and hydrogen bonds with fewer hydrophobic and ionic contacts, yielding a modest interaction fraction of 0.4. P2, despite fewer total contacts, predicted to have a notably high fraction of 1.6, reflecting persistent hydrogen-bond interactions, thereby indicating a firm and stable binding. P3 was predicted to have broader contact diversity (water bridges, hydrogen bonds, limited hydrophobic and ionic interactions) with a fraction of 0.8. Acarbose surpassed all peptides with a predicted fraction of 1.75, mediated largely by persistent hydrogen bonds and water bridges. For HAR, P3 was predicted to have the highest number of contacts yet yielded the lowest interaction fraction (0.3), reflecting predominantly transient engagements. Zenarestat, conversely, was predicted to have a fraction of 1.75 through highly persistent hydrophobic contacts despite the fewest total interactions, underscoring the efficiency of rigid small-molecule binding. P1 and P2 were predicted to have intermediate fractions of 1.0 and 1.2, respectively, reflecting a balanced contact profile. Across all three targets, P2 was predicted to have the most consistent interaction profiles, supporting its selection as the lead peptide candidate. The stronger contacts observed for reference inhibitors in select complexes are expected given their optimized small-molecule character. Comparative contact profiles of P2 versus control drugs are shown in Fig. 2; P1 and P3 comparisons are provided in Supplementary Fig. S7 (Supplementary Material S1).

Ligand RMSF (L-RMSF) analysis predicted distinct flexibility profiles across AM, AG, and HAR complexes. Peptides were predicted to exhibit higher atomic fluctuations than small-molecule inhibitors, potentially owing to their greater internal degrees of freedom and capacity for conformational adaptation within the binding site (Abdulhakim, 2025; Tsomaia, 2015; Zech et al., 2025). This flexibility may enable peptides to explore broader interaction surfaces to reorganize local contacts as the protein–ligand interface evolves, and may produce a more dynamic interaction network (Abdulhakim, 2025; Dagliyan et al., 2011; Teilum et al., 2009). Critically, elevated L-RMSF does not necessarily reflect weak binding, particularly when accompanied by stable ligand RMSD behavior and persistent protein–ligand contacts, as documented across multiple peptide-binding systems (Ganoth et al., 2006; Johnston et al., 2006).

In AM, P1 was predicted to have high initial fluctuations (∼8 Å) that subsided to ∼1.5–2.5 Å, while P2 was predicted to have moderate fluctuations (∼0.7–3.6 Å) and P3 was expected to have broader mobility (∼0.8–6.2 Å). Acarbose was predicted as the most constrained ligand (∼1.1–2.7 Å). In AG, P1 and P3 were predicted to have comparably low fluctuations (∼2.1–3.0 Å and ∼ 2.3–3.4 Å, respectively), consistent with stable binding poses, whereas P2 was predicted to have substantially higher flexibility, reaching ∼9 Å. Acarbose was markedly constrained (∼0.6–1.6 Å). For HAR, P1 and P2 were predicted to have broad conformational sampling (∼4.5–9.5 Å and ∼ 2.0–9.5 Å, respectively), while P3 was more restrained (∼3.3–4.2 Å). Zenarestat was predicted as the least flexible ligand across all systems (<1 Å), reflecting its rigid small-molecule character. Multiple ligand properties were monitored throughout the 100 ns simulations to assess conformational stability and binding integrity, including RMSD, radius of gyration (rGyr), intramolecular hydrogen bonds (intraHB), molecular surface area (MolSA), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), and polar surface area (PSA). All investigated peptides were predicted to maintain stable and favorable profiles across these parameters. P2 was predicted to have the most robust behavior. Its RMSD was predicted to remain consistently below 3 Å against all three targets (AM, AG, and HAR), indicating a stable binding pose throughout the simulation. Correspondingly, rGyr, intraHB, MolSA, SASA, and PSA profiles for P2 were all predicted to remain stable up to 100 ns, indicating retention of compactness, internal hydrogen bonding, and solvent accessibility without significant conformational transitions.

Among the control drugs, zenarestat was predicted to no intramolecular hydrogen bonds throughout the simulation, suggesting limited propensity for stable internal hydrogen-bond formation under the simulated conditions. Nevertheless, it showed relatively consistent behavior across the other evaluated metrics, including RMSD, rGyr, MolSA, SASA, and PSA, and appeared to reach equilibration toward the end of the simulation. Acarbose was predicted to have stable equilibration across all evaluated properties when simulated against both AG and AM. The predicted absence of intraHB in zenarestat was the only notable distinction within this dataset; all other ligands were predicted to exhibit variable but stable intraHB profiles consistent with their conformational preferences in the binding environment. These simulations predicted that peptide P2 could have the most favorable dynamic behavior across AM, AG, and HAR, supported by stable RMSD profiles and persistent protein-ligand contacts relative to the other peptides. The RMSF trends indicate reduced backbone fluctuations for peptide-bound AM and AG compared with the acarbose reference, while the HAR indicated low backbone RMSF in the zenarestat complex. Overall, stable equilibration across all ligand-property metrics by 100 ns supports the reliability of the simulation results and indicates that all compounds reached a stable conformational state within their respective protein complexes. The MM/GBSA-derived ΔGbind values predicted for HAR-P2 complex are substantially negative (−90.93 kcal/mol) in comparison to Zenarestat control (−81.14 kcal/mol), and are best interpreted as relative rankings rather than precise absolute binding free energies. MM/GBSA calculations often overestimate the magnitude of protein-peptide binding free energies due to limitations in entropy calculations, the implicit generalized-Born solvation model, and the treatment of the dielectric environment (Genheden & Ryde, 2015). Therefore, the binding energy values from the MM/GBSA analysis of this study should be interpreted as computational estimates rather than precise measures of absolute binding affinity, as they may overestimate the magnitude of peptide-protein interactions. Taken together with the in vitro inhibition results, these outcomes support P2 as a promising multi-target peptide candidate, while avoiding overinterpretation of RMSF-based rankings across all targets.

A comparative survey of the literature reveals a near-absence of dairy-derived, multi-target bioactive peptides simulated against diabetes target proteins. For AG, MD simulations have been reported for tuna skeletal myosin-derived peptides LADW and EEAEGT, with stability rigorously assessed over 100 ns using RMSD, Rg, SASA, hydrogen bond number, RMSF, and Free Energy Landscape analyses (Zhao et al., 2024). In contrast, buffalo casein peptides (RNAVPITPTLNR, TKVIPYVRYL, YLGYLEQLLR, FALPQYLK) with strong AG inhibition and binding affinities up to −9.6 kcal/mol (Zhao et al., 2022), and Coix Seed Prolamin-derived peptides with in vitro and docking-based AG inhibition (Li, Zhang, Meng, Zhang and Zhang, 2023), were reported without MD validation. The same gap exists for AM: camel milk-derived IMEQQQTEDEQQDK, goat milk-derived DQHQKAMKPWTQPK (Mudgil et al., 2021), and casein-derived EDVPSER (Sumlang et al., 2025) all showed significant AM inhibition in vitro or in docking studies, yet none were subjected to MD simulation. For HAR, no significant efforts toward peptide inhibitor discovery have been reported to date. Beyond these three targets, goat milk peptides GPFPLL and LPYPY with DPP-IV inhibitory potential were supported by 80 ns MD simulations and in vitro validation (Dang et al., 2025), representing one of the few examples of simulation-supported dairy peptide studies. However, most of the studies on dairy peptides reported above have not performed post-MD analysis like MM/GBSA, which indicates a significant research gap.

Collectively, the literature reflects a persistent lack of consistency in integrating in silico and in vitro workflows, with most studies either omitting MD simulations or restricting analyses to single targets (Bellaver & Kempka, 2023). Comprehensive, multi-target, stability-validated investigations of dairy-derived peptides remain rare. Growing interest in non-bovine dairy sources is beginning to diversify the discovery landscape (Agoni et al., 2025) and identification of colostrum-derived peptides extends this area by applying a consistent multi-target in silico and in vitro workflow to multifunctional peptide development.

3D structural characterization of lead peptides

The 3D structural model of P1 was predicted to adopt a predominantly coil-like conformation (Fig. 4A, A1–C1), suggesting considerable structural flexibility that may facilitate induced-fit binding across diverse protein targets (Morris et al., 2021). Residue mapping (Fig. 4A-D1) indicated a balanced distribution of charged, hydrophobic, and flexible amino acids within the sequence: histidine and lysine could offer electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding potential, methionine, isoleucine, and tryptophan may contribute hydrophobic interactions, proline is expected to introduce local conformational restriction, glycine may enhance local flexibility, and aspartic acid provides a negatively charged residue available for interaction. Secondary structure analysis (Fig. 4A-E1) predicted dominant coil regions with short helical segments, while atom-type mapping suggested polar interaction hotspots around histidine, aspartic acid, and lysine residues (Caporale et al., 2021). P2 was predicted to adopt a more ordered structural organization than P1. The predicted structure (Fig. 4B, A2–C2) indicated a pronounced helical tendency, which may enhance structural stability and protein-binding specificity while increasing resistance to proteolytic degradation (Tran et al., 2024). Residue analysis (Fig. 4B-D2) indicated a moderately hydrophilic sequence composed of glycine, proline, alanine, glutamine, and arginine: glycine may contribute flexibility, proline is expected to introduce local conformational constraints, alanine is positioned to support structural packing, while glutamine and arginine could facilitate hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions (Foralosso et al., 2024). Secondary structure prediction (Fig. 4B-E2) indicated the plausible coexistence of α-helical and coil regions, with atom-type analysis (Fig. 4B-F2) predicting glutamine- and arginine-rich regions as the potential major interaction sites (Caporale et al., 2021). Hydrophobicity mapping (Fig. 4B-G2) predicted P2 to have an overall hydrophilic profile with potential extracellular target interactions (Balmert et al., 2015). RMSF analysis (Fig. 4B-H2) predicted P2 to have enhanced mobility at both termini, while the central GPAGPQ motif was expected to remain comparatively stable. Similarly, the contact map (Fig. 4B-I2) predicted it to have predominantly short-range contacts with few long-range interactions, suggesting a locally organized but conformationally adaptable peptide (Lange and Overall, 2013; Argudo & Giner-Casares, 2021).

Unlike P1 and P2, P3 was predicted to adopt a predominantly extended β-strand conformation (Fig. 4C, A3–C3), suggesting increased backbone organization and potential for stable intermolecular interactions (Nowick, 2008). Residue mapping (Fig. 4C-D3) indicated an amphipathic sequence comprising charged aspartic acid and lysine residues together with hydrophobic valine, leucine, and phenylalanine residues. This spatial distribution is expected to support electrostatic interactions through the charged residues and hydrophobic packing through the VLVFF core (Findlay et al., 2010; Zhao, X., et al., 2021). Secondary structure prediction (Fig. 4C-E3) suggested β-strand-rich regions with limited helical content, while atom-type mapping (Fig. 4C-F3) indicated polar interaction sites around aspartic acid and lysine and hydrophobic patches around leucine and phenylalanine (Dagliyan et al., 2011). Hydrophobicity analysis (Fig. 4C-G3) also predicted the plausible amphipathic nature of P3, with hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions spatially separated. RMSF analysis (Fig. 4C-H3) predicted P3 to have flexible terminal residues, yet with a relatively rigid VLVFF core stabilized by hydrophobic and aromatic interactions. Consistently, the contact map (Fig. 4C-I3) predicted concentrated local contacts within the hydrophobic core with limited long-range interactions, indicating a partially folded amphiphilic structure (Zhao, Chen, Wang and Zhang, 2021; Bhalani et al., 2022; Hawthorne et al., 2022).

Overall, P1, P2, and P3 were predicted to display distinct structural organizations that may facilitate diverse molecular interactions through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic, and hydrophobic interactions. Similar conformational flexibility has been observed in molecular recognition and signaling peptides that mimic intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) (Jorgensen & Chmielewski, 2022; Arriba & de Arriba & Granja, 2023; Wu & Collier, 2017), while amphipathic peptides are known to influence membrane interactions, stability, and bioavailability (El-Mowafi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022). Structurally similar stapled peptides targeting peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPAR-γ) and glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) have shown potential antidiabetic effects including improved glucose uptake, insulin sensitivity, and reduced body weight and lipid levels in animal models (Nielipińska et al., 2024). The predicted hydrophobicity profiles of the peptides may influence their biological interactions. In general, hydrophilic peptides tend to favor aqueous-phase targets such as enzymes and receptors, whereas hydrophobic and amphipathic peptides tend to support membrane association and broader target engagement (Cao et al., 2024; El-Mowafi et al., 2023; Hawthorne et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2012). Thus, the balance between hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains may contribute to peptide stability, bioavailability, and functional activity (Findlay et al., 2010; Pang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022), which was predicted for P1, P2 and P3 in this study. Although the 3D modeling provides structural evidence supporting the potential stability and interaction versatility of the peptides, experimental validation of their predicted conformations and molecular interactions using biophysical techniques (e.g., circular dichroism or nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy) and binding studies is required to confirm these computational predictions (Agoni et al., 2025; Goles et al., 2024).

Based on residue composition, VKETMVPKHK and HANAGAAGH differ notably in charge and hydrophobicity from each other and from previously characterized dairy peptides (Mudgil et al., 2024). VKETMVPKHK carries three lysine and one histidine against a single glutamic acid, giving it a net basic character, with valine, methionine, and proline contributing moderate hydrophobicity; broadly comparable to the mixed charged/hydrophobic composition of P1 and P3, though net cationic rather than anionic. Peptide HANAGAAGH lacks acidic residues and contains only two weakly basic histidines, with a glycine/alanine-dominated, largely neutral and hydrophobic-leaning profile, unlike the hydrophilic, glutamine/arginine-rich character of P2. Compared to the longer, acidic camel peptide IMEQQQTEDEQQDK (Mudgil et al., 2024) and the more hydrophobic buffalo peptides YLGYLEQLLR and TKVIPYVRYL (Zhao et al., 2022), both sheep milk peptides are shorter, less hydrophobic, and (for VKETMVPKHK) net positively charged rather than acidic. However, these comparisons reflect amino acid composition, as the above-mentioned studies have not performed 3D structural predictions.

In silico pharmacokinetic, stability and gastrointestinal susceptibility analysis

During the ADMET property analysis, all three peptides were predicted to violate Lipinski's rule of five due to higher molecular weight (835.43–994.59 Da), hydrogen donors and acceptors, along with large topological polar surface areas (356.36–374.74 Å2). Such deviations are commonly observed for bioactive peptides and do not necessarily influence the biological activity, as peptides rely on transporter-mediated uptake or parenteral administration rather than passive membrane diffusion (Di, 2015; Liu et al., 2025). Yet the peptides satisfied Pfizer's rule.

Limited passive intestinal permeability was predicted, reflected by low permeability values for colorectal adenocarcinoma (Caco-2) and (Madin-Darby Canine Kidney) MDCK cells for all the peptides. However, their short sequence lengths (<10 residues) may facilitate cellular uptake compared with larger peptides (Qian et al., 2017). All three peptides contain lysine and/or arginine residues, providing a net positive charge that may promote electrostatic interactions with negatively charged membrane components and contribute to cellular uptake (Yin et al., 2012). None of them were predicted to inhibit P-glycoprotein or major cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes, suggesting a low risk of transporter- or metabolism-mediated drug interactions. Distribution analysis predicted relatively low plasma protein binding (14.34–23.90%) and moderate volumes of distribution (0.497–0.613 L/kg), while all peptides were predicted to have negligible blood-brain barrier penetration, indicating their pharmacological effects would remain primarily peripheral. Clearance values ranged from 1.229 to 1.714, with predicted elimination half-lives between 0.673 and 0.941 h, consistent with the generally rapid systemic clearance observed for many naturally occurring peptides (He et al., 2023; Nordell et al., 2026).

In addition, all peptides were predicted to have a favorable toxicity profile, with no hERG blockade, AMES toxicity, carcinogenicity, or drug-induced liver injury predictions. However, peptide 3 was predicted to have a weak positive prediction for human hepatotoxicity, supporting its further experimental evaluation. Stability prediction estimated the longest half-life for P2 (30h), whereas P1 and P3 were estimated to have half-lives of 3.5 h and 1.1 h, respectively. However, the instability index predicted P1 and P3 to have comparatively higher structural stability than P2 (Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Material S1).

Gastrointestinal digestion analysis predicted that P2 was comparatively more resistant to pepsin and chymotrypsin than P1 and P3. It was predicted to have a single predicted trypsin cleavage site (9th Position), whereas P3 was predicted to have multiple cleavage sites, indicating lower digestive stability. P1 was predicted to have two cleavage sites (2nd and 8th Position, for chymotrypsin and trypsin, respectively). Therefore, P2 was predicted to have a balanced metabolic stability and gastrointestinal resistance (Supplementary Table S3; Supplementary Material S1). These predictions could support the favorable pharmacokinetic and safety profiles of the peptides, although experimental validation is required.

The present results align with a previous study that reported IC₅₀ values of 0.58–0.59 mg/mL for AM and 0.59–1.04 mg/mL for AG inhibition using camel and bovine casein hydrolysate fractions, where peptides FLWPEYGAL (AM) and LPTGWLM, MFE, and GPAHCLL (AG) were proposed as the likely contributors to inhibition based on in silico screening, without sequence-specific experimental validation (Mudgil et al., 2021). Another study reported camel whey protein hydrolysates with IC50 values of 0.29 to 3.69 mg/mL for AM inhibition, and 0.64–2.16 mg/mL for AG inhibition (Baba et al., 2021). In addition, Parmigiano-Reggiano cheese peptide extracts were also reported to have IC50 values ranging between 1.90 and 2.60 mg/mL for AM, and 2.74–3.50 mg/mL for AG (Martini et al., 2021). All the above studies did not report specific sequences with separate IC50 values. By contrast, three peptide sequences were reported together with in silico binding-pattern prediction, structural characterization, and reproducible in vitro IC50 values. Contrarily, one study with fermented rubing cheese reported peptide YPVEPF with IC50 of 3.52 mg/mL for AG, but with no in silico binding patterns available (Li et al., 2023). These inconsistencies and uncertainties were addressed by reporting sequence-resolved IC50 values together with in silico binding patterns and structural characterization. Research on HAR inhibitors has focused heavily on small molecules (synthetic and natural) rather than peptide-based inhibitors (Balestri et al., 2022). In addition, bioactive peptides exhibit greater enzyme inhibition than protein hydrolysates due to their precise amino acid sequences, whereas hydrolysates are complex mixtures with reduced specificity and activity (Asen et al., 2023; Tadesse & Emire, 2020).

Structural analyses of peptides P1, P2, and P3 indicate that the hydrophilicity and hydrogen bond-forming residues in P1 and P2 can promote binding to enzyme active sites through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and van der Waals forces, which may further contribute to local changes in enzyme conformation upon binding at the catalytic site (Heo et al., 2023; Kaewsahnguan et al., 2021). Proline in P1 and P2 could serve as crucial anchors by forming multiple hydrogen bonds with catalytic enzyme residues, while the hydrophobic nature of peptide P3 likely reduces its solubility and enzyme interaction, leading to lower activity. This observed structure–activity trend suggests that the greater hydrophilicity of P1 and P2 may contribute to their stronger enzyme inhibitory activity compared with hydrophobic counterparts. Notably, P2's multi-target inhibition of AM, AG, and HAR underlines its potential as a multifunctional antidiabetic candidate, capable of simultaneously modulating several pathways involved in glucose metabolism and diabetic complications. These findings support P2 as a priority candidate for further characterization, including studies on specificity, safety and efficacy in more complex in vitro and in vivo models.

Both AM and AG are extracellular digestive enzymes, allowing direct interaction with peptide inhibitors in the gastrointestinal lumen. In contrast, HAR is intracellular, requiring peptides to withstand digestion, undergo intestinal absorption, and achieve cellular uptake, which were not assessed in this study. Although short cationic peptides may possess permeability potential (Qian et al., 2017; Renukuntla et al., 2013), this requires validation. Further, this study evaluated in silico gastrointestinal resistance, stability, and permeability, and predicted P1 and P2 as the favorable candidates (section 3.5). However, the above-mentioned experiments need to be performed in vitro, to confirm the overall efficacy of the peptides in this regard.

Given its pronounced inhibitory potency, P2 was selected for detailed kinetic analysis against AM, AG and HAR (Supplementary Table S4). In the corresponding Lineweaver–Burk plots, the regression lines obtained at increasing P2 concentrations for AM (Supplementary Fig. S9-A), AG (Supplementary Fig. S9—B) and HAR (Supplementary Fig. S9—C) intersect at essentially the same y-axis value as the uninhibited enzyme, while displaying altered slopes and shifted x-intercepts. The slope increased when P2 concentrations increased, while the vertical axis intercept (1/Vmax) remained constant and the horizontal axis intercept (−1/Km) moved closer to zero. The kinetic data indicated that the Vmax of P2 reactions catalyzed by AM, AG, and HAR remained constant with increasing concentrations. The estimated Ki values for AM, AG and HAR were 1.34 ± 0.30 μM (1.12 ± 0.25 μg/mL), 0.85 ± 0.13 μM (0.71 ± 0.11 μg/mL) and 1.60 ± 0.19 μM (1.34 ± 0.16 μg/mL), respectively, for P2.

The progressive rightward shift in apparent Km with increasing P2 concentration (Supplementary Fig. S9) reflects increasing competition between P2 and substrate for the same catalytic pocket, consistent with the shared active-site residues identified by docking (Table 1); at the highest concentration tested, near-complete active-site occupancy is expected to account for the plateauing of inhibition observed in the dose-response curves. These findings suggest that the inhibition mechanism of AM, AG, and HAR with P2 was reversible, and the observed kinetic behavior was consistent with competitive inhibition model. However, direct peptide-HAR binding was not experimentally established. The kinetic profile of P2 is consistent with its predicted binding mode at the catalytic pocket of all three enzymes (Section 3.2, Table 1). The glutamine and arginine residues of P2 form the hydrogen bonds and salt bridges observed with catalytic residues (e.g., Asp300/Glu233 in AM, Asp68/Glu421 in AG, Glu120/His110 in HAR), and could mimic substrate-active-site contacts, while the Gly-Pro-rich backbone could provide the conformational flexibility needed to occupy the pocket without engaging non-catalytic (allosteric) surfaces. Together, these findings suggest the possibility of interactions within or near the substrate-binding region; however, direct peptide–HAR binding and the precise binding site remain experimentally unconfirmed. Direct binding confirmation (isothermal titration calorimetry, surface plasmon resonance, fluorescence quenching, and western blotting) represents the appropriate next step toward mechanistic validation.

Protein hydrolysates and peptides can exhibit competitive inhibition by binding to the enzyme active site in place of the natural substrate, thereby preventing substrate binding and reducing enzymatic activity (Okagu et al., 2022; Santos-Sánchez et al., 2024). This competitive binding is often observed when the hydrolysates or peptides have a similar structure or functional group to the substrate, allowing them to effectively compete for the active site (Okagu et al., 2022). The trend of competitive inhibition is largely observed in case of food protein peptides and hydrolysates (Awosika & Aluko, 2019; Tkaczewska, 2020). These reports are consistent with the present results.

Since the peptides and BHA significantly differ in terms of molecular weight, particularly P2 (835.43 g/mol) compared with BHA (180.24 g/mol), molar-based EC₅₀ values provide a more appropriate basis for comparing concentration-dependent activity between compounds with different molecular weights. For instance, although P2 showed a lower DPPH EC50 than BHA in mass concentration (14.21 ± 0.66 μg/mL vs. 37.23 ± 1.13 μg/mL), the molar comparison demonstrated substantially greater potency for P2 (17.00 ± 0.79 μM vs. 206.55 ± 0.36 μM). This corresponds to a lower molar concentration required to achieve the defined response in this assay. Similar results were also obtained in the ABTS and superoxide assays, indicating that P2 exhibited greater molar radical-scavenging potency than BHA under the conditions tested. Accordingly, comparisons of antioxidant potency between compounds with different molecular weights have been interpreted primarily using molar EC50 values, with μg/mL values provided as complementary mass-based measures.

The radical scavenging profiles of peptides P1, P2, and P3 reveal better antioxidant efficacy with lower EC50/IC50 values compared to many peptides reported in prior studies. For instance, a study reported bovine serum colostrum hydrolysate peptide fractions with EC50 values of 132.92 ± 0.06 μg/mL (ABTS cation-radical) and 405.96 ± 0.11 μg/mL (OH radical), which are considerably higher than the 14.21 μg/mL recorded for P2 in the present study (Hojahmat et al., 2022). Whey protein hydrolysates with up to 80% ABTS and DPPH scavenging were also reported, without specific IC50 values (Mehdipour Biregani & Ahari, 2021). The above-mentioned studies have not revealed specific peptide sequences responsible for the radical scavenging. While a casein-derived antioxidant peptide YFYPEL was reported with IC50 values of 98.0 μM and 251.0 μM for DPPH and OH radical scavenging activities (Suetsuna et al., 2000), respectively, a peptide from buffalo colostrum whey fraction (YVHPFHL) showed an IC50 of 339 ± 2 μM for ABTS scavenging (Ashok & Aparna, 2017).

These studies have several limitations including the lack of a multi-platform approach that integrates DPPH, ABTS, and superoxide assays within a single platform. Furthermore, they do not specify peptide sequences and often fail to report IC50 values, instead presenting percentage inhibition data, which lacks reproducibility. Consequently, these issues limit the robustness of antioxidant peptide research. Bioactive peptides show higher activity than their parent protein hydrolysates in antioxidant assays due to their defined amino acid sequences, which enable precise and efficient interaction with biological targets. In contrast, hydrolysates are complex mixtures of varying peptides, reducing specificity and activity (Akbarian et al., 2022; Tadesse & Emire, 2020). Additionally, bovine colostrum fraction peptides have not been examined in comparable detail in the available literature.

The antioxidant potency of P1 and P2 is likely associated with their amphipathic and hydrophilic profiles. Amphipathic peptides are structurally predisposed to interact with both aqueous and hydrophobic environments, enabling optimal contact with diverse radical species. This dual affinity improves the likelihood of scavenging various radicals across assay systems (Zou et al., 2016). For example, P2's structure contains both polar (G, Q, R) and nonpolar (A, P) residues, allowing it to efficiently interface with radicals in different solvent conditions. From the perspective of hydrophilicity, P1 and P2 tend to exhibit stronger radical scavenging in aqueous-based assays as their solubility ensures higher accessibility to hydrophilic radical species (Wu & Collier, 2017; Zou et al., 2016). The presence of multiple polar amino acids, including glycine (G), arginine (R), and glutamine (Q), facilitates electron or proton donation, contributing directly to free radical neutralization (Girgih et al., 2015). Unlike P1, P2 lacks aromatic (Tyr/Trp) and sulfur-containing (Cys/Met) residues typically associated with hydrogen atom transfer. Its antioxidant activity is therefore more likely due to the electron/proton-donating capacity of glutamine and arginine, or the flexible Gly/Pro-rich backbone that enhances solvent accessibility and metal-chelating potential. P2 was identified as a fragment of the Collagen α-1(I) chain (UniProt Accession No. P02453) (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Material S1), consistent with the prevalence of Gly/Pro-rich motifs in collagen-derived sequences. A recent study showed that the Gly/Pro-rich sequence of an antler residual peptide (ARP) contributed substantially to its radical-scavenging antioxidant activity (Xiao et al., 2025). In addition, residues proline (P), alanine (A), and glycine (G) have been associated with their antioxidant properties in studies that evaluated jinhua ham-derived (Zhu et al., 2013) and Scomberomorus niphonius (Japanese Spanish mackerel) protein-derived peptides (Huang et al., 2017). Further, the high aqueous solubility of P2 may enhance its interaction with hydrophilic radicals, further contributing to its antioxidant potential. Thus, the antioxidant activity of P2 is likely governed by a combination of composition, conformation, and solubility rather than the presence of specific antioxidant amino acids alone.

The scavenging of both DPPH and ABTS radicals by these peptides supports this interpretation. In contrast, the partly hydrophobic peptide P3, while still displaying notable activity, lagged behind its hydrophilic or amphipathic counterparts. Because highly hydrophobic peptides can exhibit reduced dispersion and solubility in aqueous assay media, their apparent scavenging activity may be underestimated in aqueous-phase tests, including ABTS• + and superoxide assays (Zou et al., 2016). However, such peptides may perform relatively better in lipid-rich or membrane-mimetic environments, which was not within the scope of the present investigation (Foralosso et al., 2024).

These findings collectively demonstrate that amphipathic and hydrophilic peptide sequences serve as highly effective radical scavengers in standard antioxidant assays, consistent with literature reports (Girgih et al., 2015; Zou et al., 2016). The lower EC50 values observed in our peptides, surpassing well-known milk-derived antioxidants, highlight the importance of designing peptides with targeted polarity patterns for food and nutraceutical applications (Akbarian et al., 2022; Tadesse & Emire, 2020). This is supported by extensive structure–activity reviews showing that polar and amphipathic residues correlate with strong radical scavenging activity and by mechanistic studies emphasizing the crucial influence of amino acid composition and sequence arrangement on antioxidant efficacy in vitro (Girgih et al., 2015; Wu & Collier, 2017).

Bioactive peptides show higher activity than their parent protein hydrolysates in enzyme inhibition and antioxidant assays due to their defined amino acid sequences, which enable precise and efficient interaction with biological targets. In contrast, hydrolysates are complex mixtures of varying peptides, reducing specificity and activity (Akbarian et al., 2022; Tadesse & Emire, 2020). Furthermore, peptides often exhibit enhanced stability, enabling them to maintain their functional properties longer during assays. In contrast, the variability and heterogeneity of hydrolysates dilute the concentration of active components, reducing their overall efficacy (Al Musaimi et al., 2022; Pihlanto, 2006; Rossino et al., 2023). These attributes make bioactive peptides particularly effective in inhibiting enzymes or scavenging free radicals in experimental settings.

The concurrent inhibition of AM, AG, and HAR, together with free radical scavenging, suggests that these activities may collectively target multiple biochemical processes associated with diabetes mellitus. In this study, P2 has shown significant potential to concurrently inhibit these enzymatic pathways and scavenge DPPH, ABTS, and superoxide radicals. These findings suggest that P2 may modulate both the upstream metabolic processes and downstream oxidative stress associated with diabetes mellitus. In summary, these outcomes indicate P2 as a potential multi-target directed ligand (MTDL) for its dual-acting profile, highlighting the need for future in vitro cellular- and animal model-based evaluations to elucidate its underlying mechanisms and biological effects. The use of three independent replicates represents a limitation in this study, and may influence statistical power; therefore, further validation with larger sample sizes is required in both multi-enzyme inhibition and free radical scavenging assays.

Conclusion

This study identifies bovine colostrum-derived peptides P1 (HMWPGDIK), P2 (GPAGPQGPR), and P3 (DKVLVFFK) from whey, fat globule membrane, and casein protein fractions, respectively, with multi-target antidiabetic and antioxidant potential. In silico molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations predicted favorable interactions of the peptides with residues from catalytic/substrate-binding regions of the three target enzymes (α-amylase, α-glucosidase, human aldose reductase) highlighting the contribution of hydrophilic and amphipathic motifs, particularly in P2, to favorable and sustained peptide-enzyme interactions. In addition, the peptides inhibited these enzymes in vitro and exhibited significant radical-scavenging activity in DPPH, ABTS, and superoxide assays, with P2 performing marginally better. Together, these data provide mechanistic insight into how colostrum-derived peptides may act as multi-target modulators of postprandial carbohydrate digestion and pathways linked to oxidative stress. These outcomes highlight the use of combined in silico and in vitro approaches to support prioritization for subsequent cell-based and in vivo investigations. In addition, screening against additional targets beyond metabolic enzymes can be supported by the peptide library. Further validation in cell-based systems and in vivo models is needed before health claims can be substantiated. Overall, the workflow provides a basis for prioritizing dairy-derived peptides for further investigation in functional-food and nutraceutical contexts, subject to validation in appropriate food matrices and biological models.
