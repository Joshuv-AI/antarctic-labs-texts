Timing of surgery for a melioidosis-associated abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm: a case report

Background

Melioidosis is a tropical infectious disease caused by Burkholderia pseudomallei, a Gram-negative environmental pathogen. The organism is widely distributed in soil and surface water, and human infection can occur through inoculation via disrupted skin, inhalation of contaminated aerosols, or ingestion of contaminated water [1]. Diabetes mellitus is one of the most important host risk factors for melioidosis [2]. Hainan Province in China is an endemic region for melioidosis, where many patients have a history of agricultural or environmental exposure [3]. Due to its diverse and nonspecific clinical manifestations, early recognition and microbiological confirmation of melioidosis remain challenging.

Melioidosis can involve multiple organ systems, including the lungs, liver, spleen, urinary tract, bones and joints, and central nervous system; however, vascular involvement is uncommon. Once infectious aneurysms or pseudoaneurysms develop, severe complications such as rupture and hemorrhage, sepsis, and aortoenteric fistula may occur [4, 5]. In endemic regions, B. pseudomallei should be considered an important potential pathogen in patients with infectious aneurysms [6]. Abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm represents a rare but potentially fatal vascular manifestation of melioidosis. Although several cases have been reported previously, there is currently no consensus regarding infection control strategies and the optimal timing of surgical intervention [7–9].

Definitive diagnosis of melioidosis relies primarily on the isolation of B. pseudomallei from clinical specimens, such as blood, pus, or tissue samples [1, 2]. Current treatment recommendations mainly focus on general antimicrobial strategies for melioidosis, including an initial intensive phase followed by oral eradication therapy [10, 11]. However, no specific guidelines or expert consensus currently exist for melioidosis-associated abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm, particularly regarding how to balance infection control with the timing of surgical intervention.

Here, we report a case of melioidosis-associated abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm in a patient from Hainan Province, China. Imaging demonstrated a localized lesion without definite contrast extravasation, and the patient remained relatively hemodynamically stable. After approximately 5 weeks of preoperative antimicrobial therapy, elective surgical repair was performed, with stable imaging findings during postoperative follow-up. This report aims to discuss the diagnostic evidence, antimicrobial management, and decision-making regarding surgical timing in this case, providing clinical insights for the management of similar patients.

Case report

A 62-year-old female farmer from Hainan Province, China, presented with persistent lower abdominal pain for more than 10 days, followed by progressive low back pain and fever. Her medical history was notable for chronic appendicitis, with no history of abdominal trauma or prior abdominal surgery. On admission, her maximum body temperature was approximately 38.0 °C, blood pressure was 149/91 mmHg, height was 160 cm, weight was 55 kg, and body mass index (BMI) was 21.5 kg/m2. Physical examination revealed lower abdominal tenderness, but no obvious pulsatile abdominal mass was detected.

The patient had no known history of diabetes mellitus. However, laboratory evaluation after admission revealed an HbA1c level of 14.8% with persistent hyperglycemia. Based on these findings and further evaluation, she was diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus and initiated on glucose-lowering therapy.

Emergency computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the chest and abdomen performed on the day of admission revealed a focal aneurysmal protrusion of the abdominal aorta at the level of the L3 vertebral body, accompanied by inward displacement of aortic wall calcification and periaortic inflammatory changes. These findings suggested an abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm with possible contained rupture or intramural hematoma formation (Fig. 1A–C).

Because the patient remained relatively hemodynamically stable, conservative management was initially adopted under close surveillance, including blood pressure control, analgesia, and empirical antimicrobial therapy with piperacillin/tazobactam (4.5 g every 8 h). On hospital day 3, the patient continued to experience mild abdominal pain and intermittent low-grade fever. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 11.30 × 109/L, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) level of 173.64 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 84 mm/h, hemoglobin level of 107 g/L, and hematocrit of 34.1%, indicating a significant inflammatory response.

On hospital day 5, blood cultures preliminarily indicated the presence of gram-negative bacilli. Considering the clinical suspicion of an infected abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm, antimicrobial therapy was adjusted to imipenem/cilastatin (1 g every 8 h).

On hospital day 6, blood cultures were definitively identified as Burkholderia pseudomallei. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed that the isolate was susceptible to imipenem, ceftazidime, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX). The available susceptibility report did not include results for piperacillin/tazobactam. On hospital day 8, the patient continued to experience abdominal pain, low back pain, and fever, with a maximum temperature of approximately 38.5 °C. After consultation with the infectious disease team, continued intensive therapy with imipenem/cilastatin for at least 4 weeks was recommended, together with close monitoring of body temperature, inflammatory markers, blood cultures, and abdominal imaging. Combination therapy with oral TMP-SMX was considered if clinically indicated. As the infection was not yet adequately controlled, the clinical team decided to defer surgical intervention while continuing antimicrobial therapy under strict surveillance for the risk of pseudoaneurysm rupture.

From hospital day 9 onward, the patient remained afebrile. On day 10, the dose of imipenem/cilastatin was adjusted to 2 g every 8 h. By day 13, the white blood cell (WBC) count decreased to 7.05 × 109/L, hs-CRP decreased to 18.92 mg/L, and procalcitonin (PCT) was < 0.02 ng/mL. Imipenem/cilastatin was subsequently adjusted to 1 g every 8 h, with plans to add oral TMP-SMX. By hospital day 15, the medical records documented the initiation of combination therapy with oral TMP-SMX. The patient’s abdominal pain had improved significantly, and continuous intravenous analgesia was discontinued. As the specific rationale for initiating TMP-SMX was not clearly documented, its timing of administration is reported based solely on available clinical records.

Follow-up CTA performed on hospital day 21 demonstrated substantial reduction in periaortic inflammatory exudation and surrounding soft tissue inflammation compared with baseline imaging (Fig. 2A–C). Given the unclear lesion margins, changes in pseudoaneurysm diameter or volume were not used as indicators of antimicrobial treatment response in this case. On day 24, hs-CRP further decreased to 1.07 mg/L, and ESR decreased to 45 mm/h.

Repeat blood cultures became negative on hospital day 28. On day 30, antimicrobial therapy was switched to ceftazidime every 8 h, with the dose documented as 2 g every 8 h by hospital day 33. The specific clinical rationale for switching from imipenem/cilastatin to ceftazidime was not clearly documented in the available records. At this time, the patient remained afebrile, with WBC decreased to 6.42 × 109/L and hs-CRP decreased to 0.59 mg/L. Considering the sustained hemodynamic stability, absence of definite active contrast extravasation, resolution of fever and pain, continued decline in inflammatory markers, negative blood cultures, and improvement of periaortic inflammatory changes, the clinical team considered the infection to be well controlled. On hospital day 37, although the abdominal aortic infection and systemic manifestations had improved, the persistent risk of pseudoaneurysm rupture prompted the decision to perform open surgical repair the following day.

On hospital day 38, the patient underwent open surgery under general anesthesia, including bilateral iliac artery endarterectomy, surgical management of the affected abdominal aortic segment, and aorto–bi-iliac prosthetic graft replacement. Intraoperatively, a pulsatile mass measuring approximately 3 × 5 cm was identified in the distal abdominal aorta. The lesion was densely surrounded by connective tissue and firmly adherent to a segment of the duodenum, making complete dissection technically difficult (Fig. 3A). No specimens from the affected aortic wall, aneurysmal thrombus, or surrounding tissues were collected for microbiological culture during surgery.

After vascular clamping, the abdominal aorta was transected approximately 2 cm proximal to the lesion and at the level of the bilateral common iliac arteries. The proximal and distal ends of the resected aortic segment were closed by suture ligation. The prosthetic graft was then anastomosed to the proximal aortic stump. Following bilateral iliac artery endarterectomy and removal of atherosclerotic plaques, the two iliac limbs of the graft were anastomosed to the corresponding common iliac arteries (Fig. 3B). Intraoperative assessment confirmed no obvious anastomotic leakage, and distal arterial pulsations were well preserved. The procedure was completed uneventfully, and antimicrobial therapy and supportive care were continued postoperatively.

Postoperative antimicrobial and supportive therapies were continued. CTA performed on postoperative day 10 demonstrated expected postoperative changes following abdominal aortic prosthetic graft replacement, along with abdominopelvic fluid accumulation. On postoperative day 15, the patient remained clinically stable. The surgical incision had healed, and the staples were removed. The vascular surgical management was considered essentially complete, and discharge was planned for the following day.

The discharge medication records did not include TMP-SMX or other oral antimicrobial agents targeting B. pseudomallei. Therefore, no standard oral eradication therapy after discharge was documented. The available medical records did not specify the reasons for discontinuation or omission of eradication therapy. Follow-up CTA performed on postoperative day 49 showed postoperative changes after abdominal aortic graft replacement, with reduced abdominopelvic fluid accumulation compared with the previous examination (Fig. 2D–F). At approximately 14 months of follow-up, CTA demonstrated continued patency of the prosthetic graft, without significant stenosis or contrast extravasation (Fig. 2G–I).

According to the patient’s report, fever, abdominal pain, and low back pain gradually resolved following antimicrobial therapy. The patient understood the importance of infection control before surgery and expressed satisfaction with postoperative recovery. At more than 1 year of follow-up, she reported no recurrence of clinical symptoms.

Discussion

We report a case of melioidosis-associated abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm in a patient from Hainan Province, China. The key clinical challenge in this case was determining the optimal timing of surgical intervention. After approximately 5 weeks of antimicrobial therapy, the patient underwent elective open vascular reconstruction. CTA performed approximately 14 months after surgery demonstrated continued graft patency without significant stenosis or contrast extravasation, indicating stable imaging outcomes during follow-up. This case highlights that, in melioidosis-endemic regions, patients presenting with fever, abdominal pain, or back pain accompanied by abdominal aortic abnormalities should be evaluated comprehensively by integrating epidemiological exposure, host risk factors, vascular imaging findings, and microbiological evidence. This case also provides clinical experience for understanding this rare manifestation and the decision-making process regarding surgical timing.

Diagnostic and etiological considerations

Melioidosis-associated infectious aneurysms often lack specific clinical manifestations and commonly present with fever, abdominal pain, or back pain, which may contribute to delayed diagnosis [4–6]. In this case, the diagnosis of melioidosis-associated infected abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm was supported by converging clinical, microbiological, and radiological evidence. Clinically, the patient was from Hainan Province, an endemic region for melioidosis, had a history of agricultural exposure, severe uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, and presented with fever, lower abdominal pain, low back pain, and marked systemic inflammation. Microbiologically, B. pseudomallei was isolated from blood cultures, providing direct etiological evidence; follow-up blood cultures became negative on hospital day 28 after targeted antimicrobial therapy. Radiologically, CTA demonstrated a focal pseudoaneurysmal protrusion of the abdominal aorta, inward displacement of mural calcification, and prominent periaortic inflammatory changes, with suspected contained rupture. Collectively, these findings supported the diagnosis of melioidosis-associated infected abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm.

To systematically assess the diagnostic evidence in this case, we referred to the international Delphi consensus framework for infective native aortic aneurysm (INAA) [12], integrating clinical, microbiological, and imaging evidence. In accordance with this consensus, INAA is used as the preferred disease-category term rather than the historically used term “mycotic aneurysm.” In the present case, “abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm” is retained where appropriate to describe the specific morphological manifestation of the lesion. The MAGIC criteria were developed for aortic graft infection [13], rather than infection involving the native aorta. Because the infection in this patient involved the native aorta before prosthetic graft implantation, the presenting lesion was not classified according to the MAGIC criteria.

Culture remains the primary method for microbiological confirmation of melioidosis, although its sensitivity is limited. A Bayesian latent class model study estimated the sensitivity of culture-based diagnosis to be approximately 60.2% [14]. Therefore, in patients with suspected melioidosis from endemic regions, early collection of blood, pus, or tissue specimens for microbiological testing is essential. In this case, cultures were not obtained from the affected aortic wall, aneurysmal thrombus, or surrounding tissues during surgery, resulting in a lack of direct microbiological evidence from the vascular lesion. In addition, the available medical records did not document culture results from urine, sputum, or other potential sites of infection; therefore, it remains unclear whether such investigations were performed.

Antimicrobial therapy

The treatment of melioidosis generally consists of an intravenous intensive phase followed by oral eradication therapy. For severe, deep-seated, or complicated infections, imipenem or meropenem are recommended as intensive-phase agents, while ceftazidime is also an important therapeutic option. TMP-SMX remains a key component of oral eradication therapy [10, 11, 15, 16].

In this case, antimicrobial therapy was switched to imipenem/cilastatin after blood cultures initially suggested gram-negative bacilli in the context of severe vascular infection. After confirmation of B. pseudomallei, intensive carbapenem therapy was continued according to infectious disease consultation, with subsequent addition of TMP-SMX during the treatment course, followed by transition to ceftazidime. During therapy, the patient demonstrated progressive improvement in clinical symptoms, inflammatory markers, blood cultures, and periaortic imaging findings.

Importantly, treatment response in this case was assessed primarily based on resolution of fever, improvement in pain, sustained decline in inflammatory markers, conversion of blood cultures to negative, and reduction of periaortic inflammatory changes, rather than morphological reduction of the pseudoaneurysm itself. These combined improvements suggested a reduction in infectious burden and supported subsequent elective vascular reconstruction.

Although TMP-SMX was added during the inpatient intensive treatment period, no anti-melioidosis oral antimicrobial agents were documented in the discharge medication records. Therefore, a standard post-discharge oral eradication regimen was not documented, and the reason for discontinuation or omission could not be determined from the available medical records.

Timing of surgery and clinical implications

Previous reports of melioidosis-associated infectious aneurysms suggest that antimicrobial therapy alone may not eliminate the risk of aneurysm rupture. Fatal cases due to aneurysm rupture during antimicrobial treatment have been reported [4–9, 17]. Conversely, emergency surgery for ruptured aneurysms also carries substantial risks, with some patients experiencing postoperative shock, sepsis, or multiple organ dysfunction despite vascular repair [4, 8, 18–20]. A case series from Hainan Province, China, further demonstrated that rupture, septic shock, and hemorrhage were major contributors to mortality among patients with melioidosis-associated infectious aneurysms [8]. In a recent report, a patient with melioidosis-associated infected aortic arch aneurysm without signs of impending rupture achieved a favorable outcome after blood culture clearance, 4 weeks of intravenous ceftazidime therapy, followed by open surgery and prosthetic graft reconstruction [21].

In the present case, CTA suggested an infected abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm with possible contained rupture; however, no definite active contrast extravasation was observed, and the patient remained hemodynamically stable. Under close surveillance, targeted antimicrobial therapy resulted in sustained clinical improvement: fever resolved after hospital day 9, abdominal and low back pain gradually improved, and WBC and hs-CRP levels continuously declined. Follow-up CTA on hospital day 21 demonstrated marked improvement in periaortic inflammatory changes, and blood cultures became negative on hospital day 28. By hospital day 33, the WBC count had decreased to 6.42 × 109/L and hs-CRP to 0.59 mg/L. These clinical, microbiological, laboratory, and radiological improvements collectively suggested adequate infection control and provided the rationale for elective open surgery. On hospital day 37, the clinical team considered the aortic infection and systemic manifestations to be controlled, and open vascular reconstruction was performed the following day.

The management strategy in this case suggests that, for carefully selected patients with melioidosis-associated abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm, suspected contained rupture, no evidence of active bleeding, and hemodynamic stability, elective surgery after achieving partial infection control under strict surveillance and with immediate access to emergency vascular intervention may represent a reasonable individualized approach. However, this strategy should not delay intervention in patients with clear indications for urgent surgery. Furthermore, this single case cannot demonstrate that delayed surgery is superior to early intervention or define the optimal waiting period. Therefore, surgical timing should be individualized based on comprehensive assessment of rupture risk, infection control status, and overall patient condition.

Conclusion

Melioidosis-associated infected abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm is a rare condition with a high risk of rupture, and the optimal timing of surgical intervention remains uncertain. This case suggests that, in highly selected patients with suspected contained rupture but no evidence of active bleeding, hemodynamic stability, and clinical, microbiological, laboratory, and periaortic imaging improvement after targeted antimicrobial therapy, elective open reconstruction after partial infection control may be feasible under strict surveillance and with immediate access to emergency vascular intervention.

However, this single case cannot demonstrate that this strategy is superior to early surgery or determine the optimal waiting period. Surgical timing should therefore be individualized based on comprehensive assessment of rupture risk, infection control status, and overall patient condition. This case provides clinical insights into surgical decision-making for similar patients and may contribute to the accumulation of evidence regarding the management of this rare but life-threatening condition.
