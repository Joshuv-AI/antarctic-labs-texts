Phenotyping and unmet need assessment for biological agents in uncontrolled asthma
Authors: Rahul Tyagi, Randeep Guleria, Pawan Tiwari, Karan Madan, Anant Mohan, Vijay Hadda, Ravindra Mohan Pandey, Saurabh Mittal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Setting:Uncontrolled asthma patients at a university teaching hospital in India.Objective:To determine the prevalence, endotypes, and need for add-on therapies in severe asthma.Methods:This prospective study screened all patients with uncontrolled asthma, and those with uncontrolled asthma while on moderate-to-high dose inhaled corticosteroids with long-acting beta agonists (ICS + LABA) were further assessed. Endotyping and optimisation of therapy for all potentially modifiable factors were performed, followed by reassessment at 3 months of optimised treatment. Uncontrolled patients at 3 months were classified as having severe asthma and assessed for eligibility for add-on therapies.Results:After screening 261 uncontrolled asthma patients, 160 patients with uncontrolled asthma on moderate-high dose ICS + LABA were included in the study (prevalence of difficult-to-treat asthma: 61.2%). On endotyping, 134 (83.75%) had T2-high endotype and 26 (16.25%) had T2-low endotype asthma. Severe asthma prevalence was 20% (32 out of 160) in this subgroup, and they were assessed for eligibility for guideline-directed add-on therapy: 12 (37.5%) were eligible for omalizumab; 18 (56.25%) were eligible for mepolizumab, benralizumab, or dupilumab; and seven (21.87%) were not eligible for any add-on therapy.Conclusion:T2-high asthma is five times more common than T2-low asthma, and the prevalence of severe asthma is 20% among uncontrolled asthmatics.

INTRODUCTION

Asthma is a disorder characterised by chronic airway inflammation leading to respiratory symptoms such as wheeze, shortness of breath, chest tightness and cough that vary over time and in intensity, and variable expiratory airflow limitation. Asthma is a common disease, and its prevalence ranges from 1% to 18% in various countries.[1] Over time, it has been realised that asthma is a heterogeneous disease with certain clusters showing observable characteristics. Clusters of these observable demographic, clinical, or pathophysiological characteristics are known as ‘asthma phenotypes’.[2] From the initial concept of extrinsic (allergic) and intrinsic (non-allergic) asthma as early as 1947,[3] various phenotypes have been identified by the Severe Asthma Research Programme[2] and other researchers.[456] Gradually, the strategy has evolved further to associate molecular mechanisms with phenotypes called endotypes.[7] These endotypes describe the pathophysiological mechanism at the molecular and cellular level. This knowledge helps identify targetable mechanisms providing precision medicine for asthma. The most well-established classification of asthma endotypes is Th2 high and Th2 low.[7]

Uncontrolled asthma is defined as having poor symptom control or frequent exacerbations.[8] Uncontrolled asthma may be ‘difficult-to-treat’ if it remains uncontrolled despite medium- or high-dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) with a second controller or requires high-dose ICS treatment to maintain good symptom control. Incorrect diagnosis, poor inhaler technique, inadequate treatment adherence, and unmanaged comorbidities may be reasons behind difficult-to-treat asthma. Asthma that remains uncontrolled despite high-dose ICS with a second controller and ensuring adequate compliance, proper inhaler technique, and management of comorbidities is known as severe asthma.[1] In a Dutch registry assessing the prevalence of severe asthma,[9] 17% of asthmatics had difficult-to-treat asthma and 3.7% had severe asthma. India is also expected to have around 20 million severe asthmatics.[10] Despite being such a common problem, there is only limited Indian data available on the phenotyping of asthma in Indian subjects.[1011] In a retrospective study of 100 subjects, 85% were classified as T2 high asthma.[10] Another study[11] from India evaluated inflammatory phenotypes in severe asthma by using sputum quantitative assay and reported the most common inflammatory phenotype to be neutrophilic (59%), followed by eosinophilic (26%), mixed pattern (11%), and pauci-granulocytic (3%).

There is limited data on eligibility for various biologicals among severe asthma patients. Knowing the prevalence of various endotypes of asthma is essential in this subset, as it can help guide the add-on therapy and identify the economic impact as well.

In view of the paucity of prospective data, we conducted this study to identify the prevalence of difficult-to-treat, severe, and T2-high asthma in Indian subjects with uncontrolled asthma. We also assessed the proportion of patients meeting the eligibility criteria for various biologicals.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a prospective study conducted for 2 years at a tertiary care university hospital in Delhi, India. Ethical clearance was obtained from the institute’s ethics committee prior to initiation. All participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

All adult patients presenting to the pulmonary medicine outpatient department with a diagnosis of bronchial asthma during the study period were screened for inclusion in the study. Patients having uncontrolled asthma while receiving moderate-to-high dose ICS + LABA were included. Patients with a diagnosis of COPD, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA), or any other respiratory disease were excluded from the study. Asthma control was assessed using the Asthma Control Test (ACT).[18] ACT is a validated numerical control tool that has four questions related to symptom/reliever use and one question for the patient’s self-assessed control.[1]

Study procedure

A detailed clinical evaluation, including assessment of symptom control, treatment history, inhaler technique, adherence, and exacerbation history, was done. Patients underwent blood investigations, including complete blood counts, absolute eosinophil count, serum total IgE levels, and Aspergillus-specific IgE and IgG levels. Assessment of lung functions (spirometry with reversibility testing) was done according to the American Thoracic Society guidelines. Patients were assessed for comorbidities causing worsening of asthma, including allergic rhinitis, gastroesophageal reflux disease, obstructive sleep apnoea, heart failure, environmental or allergen exposure, and anxiety, depression, or social difficulties. The STOP-BANG questionnaire was used to assess the risk of OSA, and patients with moderate-to-high risk underwent overnight in-lab polysomnography. A clinical assessment by the concerned specialty was also sought in case of other suspected comorbidities, and relevant investigations were undertaken. A reassessment was done at 3 months to assess the asthma control. Patients were labelled as uncontrolled, difficult-to-treat, or severe asthma based on characteristics as recommended by GINA guidelines.[18] Patients were classified as T2 high if they had absolute eosinophil count ≥150 cells/µL or more, FeNO ≥20 ppb, and clinically allergy-driven asthma.

Patients with asthma and features of atopy (atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis) with raised peripheral eosinophils or IgE were classified as early-onset asthma, late-onset asthma, and very-late-onset asthma based on onset before or after 18 years of age and onset after 50 years of age, respectively. Patients with adult-onset asthma and a BMI of 30 kg/m2 or more were classified as obesity associated asthma. Uncontrolled patients (ACT <20) were evaluated for biological therapy/bronchial thermoplasty (BT).

Criteria to assess eligibility for biologicals

Anti-IgE Therapy: Total serum IgE and bodyweight in dosing range along with sensitisation to inhaled allergen (Specific IgE) with ≥2 exacerbations in the last year requiring systemic steroids or hospitalisation.

Anti-IgE Therapy: Total serum IgE and bodyweight in dosing range along with sensitisation to inhaled allergen (Specific IgE) with ≥2 exacerbations in the last year requiring systemic steroids or hospitalisation.

Anti-IL5/IL5-R Therapy: Blood eosinophil count more than 300/µL with ≥2 exacerbations in the last year requiring systemic steroids or hospitalisation.

Anti-IL5/IL5-R Therapy: Blood eosinophil count more than 300/µL with ≥2 exacerbations in the last year requiring systemic steroids or hospitalisation.

Anti-IL4-R Therapy: Blood eosinophil >300/µL or FENO ≥25 ppb or requirement for maintenance OCS along with ≥2 exacerbations in the last year requiring systemic steroids or hospitalisation.

Anti-IL4-R Therapy: Blood eosinophil >300/µL or FENO ≥25 ppb or requirement for maintenance OCS along with ≥2 exacerbations in the last year requiring systemic steroids or hospitalisation.

For BT: Stage 4/5 GINA therapy for the previous year or oral steroids for >50% days in the previous year, FEV1 >60% predicted, and fit to undergo bronchoscopy.

For BT: Stage 4/5 GINA therapy for the previous year or oral steroids for >50% days in the previous year, FEV1 >60% predicted, and fit to undergo bronchoscopy.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated to determine the prevalence of T2 high phenotype in difficult-to-treat asthma patients based on the following assumptions: anticipated prevalence of 80%,[12] the absolute precision was 10%, and the level of significance was 95%; a sample size of 62 patients was determined. However, to improve the precision, we enrolled all consecutive subjects during the study period. Descriptive analysis was carried out by mean and standard deviation for quantitative variables, and by frequency and proportion for categorical variables. The association between categorical explanatory variables and quantitative outcomes was assessed by comparing the mean values. The mean differences, along with their 95% confidence intervals, were presented. ANOVA was used to assess statistical significance. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analysed using coGuide V.1.01 software.[13]

RESULTS

We screened 261 adult patients with uncontrolled bronchial asthma for inclusion. Of these, 110 were excluded for various reasons (43 were diagnosed as ABPA, 41 patients were using low-dose ICS, 1 had chronic pulmonary aspergillosis, and 16 did not consent). Finally, 160 patients with uncontrolled asthma on moderate- or high-dose ICS with long-acting beta agonist (ICS-LABA) were included to determine their phenotype and endotype. Thus, the prevalence of difficult-to-treat asthma in the uncontrolled asthma group was 61.3%. Baseline characteristics of the study population are given in Table 1. Most patients were using budesonide as the ICS (88.75%). The exacerbation history, comorbidities, and concomitant medication use are described in Table 2. Sixty percent of the patients were on GINA step IV treatment. The most common comorbidity was allergic rhinitis (n = 71, 44.37%), followed by hypertension (n = 22, 13.75%), hypothyroidism (n = 14, 8.75%), and diabetes mellitus (n = 7, 4.37%). Montelukast was the most common concomitant medication used (78.13%), followed by intranasal steroids (38.75%), proton pump inhibitors (36.88%), long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA) (33.13%), and theophyllines (30%).

DISCUSSION

In this study from our centre, the prevalence of difficult-to-treat asthma was 61.3% among uncontrolled asthmatics, while severe asthma was 20% of all difficult-to-treat asthma. In previous studies, the prevalence of difficult-to-treat asthma is approximately 17% among all asthma patients.[1] Although it may seem that the proportion of difficult asthma in our study is higher than that in previous studies, various factors were likely responsible for it. Firstly, the study was conducted at an apex centre, hence it was more likely that patients who attended the OPD were referred due to inability to control their asthma at various echelons of medical care before being referred. Hence, more patients with difficult asthma were likely to attend our OPD. Secondly, most of the study period was during various waves of COVID-19 in India. The OPD was shut down considerably during lockdown, and patients avoided visiting hospitals for less critical issues. Therefore, it is likely that the patients who attended the OPD during this period had more severe and uncontrolled symptoms.

The prevalence of T2 high asthma is higher than that of T2 low asthma. In our study, the prevalence of T2 high asthma was significantly higher than T2 low asthma (83.75% vs 16.25%). Similar results were reported by Talwar et al.[10] in a study from India. Oishi et al.[14] retrospectively analysed the data of 161 patients with severe uncontrolled asthma. They classified them into T2 high based on the presence of atopy (RAST positive) and/or FENO >25 ppb and/or blood eosinophils more than 300/µL. T2 low was considered when all the above parameters were absent. The authors found a prevalence of 89% of T2 high and 11% of T2 low asthma.[14] Among Indian studies, Dasgupta et al.[11] studied inflammatory phenotypes in severe asthma by using sputum quantitative assay (Hamilton protocol) and other clinical and blood parameters to phenotype 100 consecutive patients with severe asthma. The most common inflammatory phenotype was neutrophilic (59%) in this population. Our results are similar to most studies on the subject, which used serum biomarkers to classify endotypes. Phenotyping of patients in T2 high asthma showed that the majority (61.94%) had late-onset asthma. Similar results (late-onset asthma in 80.76% of patients) were seen in T2 low asthma. It is important to note that very-late-onset asthma was seen in around 7% of patients with T2 high and 15% of patients with T2 low asthma. This clearly indicates that even in patients presenting with the onset of asthma-like symptoms after 50 years of age, the diagnosis of asthma should not be rejected just based on the late age of symptom onset.

Among patients with T2 high inflammation, who, after careful evaluation, are considered eligible for targeted biologic therapy, there is considerable overlap, and an individual patient may be eligible for more than one biologic agent. Albers et al.[15] studied overlap in treatment eligibility for mepolizumab (anti-IL-5), omalizumab (anti-IgE), and reslizumab (anti-IL-5) in patients with severe asthma. Patients from clinical practice in six countries were recruited for this single-visit, cross-sectional observational study. Of the 670 patients who met the analysis criteria, 5% were eligible for reslizumab, 20% were eligible for mepolizumab, and 31%–41% were eligible for omalizumab based on the eligibility criteria used. In our study, 37.5% were eligible for omalizumab, while 56.25% were eligible for mepolizumab, benralizumab, or dupilumab. In our study, 21.87% of patients were not eligible for any add-on biologic treatment. This suggests that despite the availability of these drugs, there may still be a significant proportion of asthma patients who will not meet the criterion for eligibility. Upcoming agents may fill this void in the future.

Our study is the first prospective study from India evaluating endotypes and phenotypes in difficult-to-control asthma. It gives important information about the patient characteristics of uncontrolled asthma in India. It is also the first study in the country to evaluate the overlap among the present biologics for patients with severe asthma. The main limitation of our study is that it is conducted at an apex centre and hence may not truly reflect the prevalence of uncontrolled asthma in the general population.

CONCLUSION

In this study on phenotyping and endotyping of asthma, the prevalence of difficult-to-treat asthma was 61.2%, and severe asthma constituted 20% of the difficult-to-treat asthma group. Among these, 83.75% were T2 high subgroup of asthma. Among severe asthmatics, only 71.8% (23 out of 32) were eligible for an add-on biological agent, suggesting the need for future therapies for these patients.
