Ultrastructural characteristics and histochemical analysis of potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) foliar trichomes
Authors: Shuang Yang, Youhan Li, Fang Yang, Rui Zhang, Huachun Guo, Lei Bai, Jun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrichomes play important roles in improving plant environmental adaptation. Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) leaves have a dense distribution of trichomes, divided into two major categories: non-glandular trichomes (NGTs) and glandular trichomes (GTs). However, the ultrastructural features and GT secretion have not been adequately studied. This step of research is crucial in revealing the role of trichomes in defense.MethodsWe analyzed the ultrastructural characteristics of potato trichomes using transmission microscopy and the localization of GT metabolites using histochemical staining.ResultsThe results show that different types of potato trichomes have specific ultrastructural features: NGTs consist mainly of basal and stalk cells and have little internal cytoplasm, primarily large vacuoles, and thicker cuticles. GTs consist mainly of basal cells, stalk cells, and glandular head cells. Furthermore, the head cells of GTs exhibit dense cytoplasm. Glandular head cells have abundant organelles, such as mitochondria, chloroplasts, endoplasmic reticula, Golgi apparatus, and nucleus. Golgi apparatus multiple membrane sacs and active metabolism, generate numerous osmiophilic vesicles that migrate toward the cell wall, rupture, and release osmiophilic material. This material is stored in the space between the cell wall and the cuticle, and secreted from GT head cells when accumulated in excess. Histochemical staining of trichomes revealed that GTs secrete a variety of substances, and developing GT head cells accumulate secondary metabolites, such as phenolics, alkaloids, terpenoids, and flavonoids, while small quantities of these secondary metabolites are distributed in type VI glandular neck cells.ConclusionsNGT constituent cells lack organelles and secretory function and depend on thickened cuticles to remain upright and stiff. The organelles in GT head cells allow for active cellular metabolism, such as the synthesis of secondary metabolites like phenols and alkaloids. These findings, along with those of other plants of the genus Solanum (tomato), suggest that GTs may play important roles in potato leaf chemical defense. Overall, the type, structure, and secondary metabolite composition of potato trichomes demonstrate their constitutive defense functions and warrant further research. These phenotypes could provide additional options for breeding insect- and disease-resistant cultivars.Keywords: Potato, Trichomes, Ultrastructure, Secondary metabolites

Introduction

Plant trichomes are specialized leaf appendages composed of epidermal cells that evolved as an adaptive response to biotic and abiotic stresses [1]. Trichomes are widely regarded as the first line of defense against herbivores, pathogens, and abiotic stress in plants [2], and provide defenses similar to those of thorns, brambles, and wax. Plant trichomes are widely distributed in higher plants and exhibit diverse cell types upon full differentiation. They can be unicellular or multicellular, straight or branched, and living or dead [3]. The external morphology of trichomes varies significantly and is determined by organ and plant species [4]. Trichomes are found not only on plant leaves and stems, but also on flowers [5] and fruits [6]. In terms of plant morphology, trichomes are generally classified into two major types on the basis of their secretory capacity: nonglandular trichomes (NGTs) and glandular trichomes GTs [7].

NGTs without glandular head cells, and cannot secrete secondary metabolites. However, they can limit the feeding rate of phytophagous animals by forming physical defense structures while slightly damaging their digestive lining [8]. These features also play a key role in shielding against UV-B radiation and reducing the harmful effects of UV rays [9]. In potatoes, research has confirmed that trichomes can reduce the evaporation of plant water, affect the wettability of leaves, and improve the water retention capacity of the plant leaf surface, which improves plant drought resistance [10].

GTs are present on approximately 30% of vascular plant surfaces [11], and their most important feature is the presence of glandular cells, which can accumulate and release primary and secondary metabolites, are often referred to as “factories of phytochemistry” [12]. Secretions from GTs are generally classified on the basis of their chemical structure as phenols, alkaloids, terpenoids, and acyl sugars. These compounds not only function as chemical defenses but also serve as signaling molecules to regulate plant growth and development [13]. Furthermore, they act as pollinator attractants to facilitate fruit dispersal [14]. The GTs of cultivated tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) and its wild relatives biosynthesize and secrete diverse volatile and nonvolatile secondary metabolites, including terpenoids, flavonoids, and acyl sugars [15]. The vacuoles of glandular cells contain oxyphilic deposits, which may be phenolic compounds [16]. The diterpenoids, glycolipids, and resistance proteins in tobacco GTs collectively mitigate fungal infections in leaves [17]. O-acyl sugars are secreted by tobacco GTs when the plants are fed on by tobacco moth larvae and can alter the odor of their carapace and feces, which attracts ants to feed on them [18]. Plant flavonoids play pivotal roles in direct defense against both chewing and sap-sucking herbivorous insects [19]. The secondary metabolites of glandular trichomes and their functions have been thoroughly characterized in solanaceous plants such as tomato and tobacco. However, recent research has given limited attention to the internal architecture and metabolite profiles of potato trichomes, and their biological functions remain poorly characterized.

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) is an annual herbaceous tuberous plant of the genus Solanum in the family Solanaceae. It is the fourth largest food crop after rice, wheat, and maize [20] and is an important source of carbohydrates for humans [21]. However, phytophagous insects such as tuber moths, leafhoppers and beetles pose a significant threat to potato production [22–24]. Effectively controlling and managing pests through the large-scale use of pesticides is difficult and prone to problems such as drug resistance, food safety and environmental pollution. The fundamental solution to this problem lies in developing resistant crop cultivars through breeding programs. Studies on potato trichomes, when compared to those on crops such as tobacco [25] and tomato [26], still exhibit certain research gaps in their phenotypic and functional aspects. In wild potato (Solanum berthaultii Hawkes), glandular trichomes are classified into Type A (short-stalked with a 4-celled head) and Type B (long-stalked with a single-celled head) [27]. By comparison, reports characterizing the types of trichomes in cultivated potato varieties are limited. The results of our previous study on 30 different potato varieties revealed that the number of types of trichomes in potato is very similar to that in tomato. Therefore, we employed the classification criteria for tomato trichomes [11]. This system classifies trichomes based on the distinct characteristics of their basal, stalk, and head cells. Using this approach, we identified five types of trichomes (II, III, V, VI, and VII) in potatoes [28]. Given the multiple biological functions of trichomes, the aim of this study was to identify the substances involved in chemical defense within trichomes and elucidate how trichomes contribute to this mechanism, and provide a research basis for future study of their defense functions and mechanism. Also, this study can provide a new approach for potato defense against pests and diseases, and further apply it to the development of stress-tolerant varieties using various molecular and traditional breeding tools.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The plant materials selected were Ganba yangyu, Shishu No. 7, 110, Longshu No. 8, Lishu No. 6, Tianshu No. 12, Diancaishu No. 103, and Dianshu No. 1418. Potato seeds were provided by the Institute of Potato Crops, Yunnan Agricultural University. The plants were planted at Yunnan Agricultural University teaching internship farm on the back of the mountain. Which were planted using whole potatoes and potted plants. Ten pots were planted for each material, for a total of 80 pots, and the pots were planted with appropriate amounts of composite fertilizer and organic fertilizer. Field management, such as fertilization, watering, weeding and cultivation, was carried out according to the potato planting and cultivation process throughout the growing period.

Dimensional statistical analysis

For each potato variety, three replicates were taken. From each replicate, six to eight mature leaves that were free from disease and pests were selected, photographed, and observed under a Zeiss Smartzoom 5 digital microscope at 150 × magnification. From these leaves, 50 mature trichomes were randomly selected, and the diameters and lengths of the heads of the GTs, the lengths of the NGTs, and the lengths of the stripe cells were measured. A value of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed in SPSS using Tukey's multiple comparison test.

Morphological observations

During the growing, first flowering, and full flowering stages, basal Mature Leaves, middle Functional Leaves, and apical Juvenile Leaves were taken from the potato plants. The samples were washed twice with distilled water to remove surface dust and impurities, placed in an ice box and immediately sent to the Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Potato leaves were cut into 8 mm × 8 mm pieces with scissors and then observed and photographed using a Zeiss Sigma field emission scanning electron microscope. With reference to the classification criteria of tomato trichomes [29, 30], the potato trichome types were categorized, and the morphological characteristics of each type were analyzed.

Transmission electron microscopy

Materials: Fresh potato leaves at the seedling, bud and full bloom stages were selected. Fixation: Fresh potato leaves (avoiding the veins) were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 12 h. Following fixation, the samples were degassed under vacuum to ensure settling. Subsequently, they were washed three times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 15 min each, then post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, and finally washed again three times with PBS for 15 min each. Dehydration: Solutions of 30% ethanol, 50% ethanol, 70% ethanol, 90% ethanol, 100% ethanol twice (20 min each), 100% acetone twice (20 min each) were employed. Osmosis: The sequence acetone: Spurr resin = 3:1 (6 h), acetone: Spurr resin = 1:1 (3 h), acetone: Spurr resin = 1:3 (1 h), and pure Spurr resin for 12 h was employed. Embedding: Performed with Spurr resin at 60 °C for 48 h. Sectioning: Coarse blocking followed by fine blocking was performed, and a Leica uc7 ultramicrotome was used to generate 70-nm ultrathin sections. Staining: the sections were stained with 0.05% (w/v) borax in 1% toluidine blue for 5 min. Anatomical sections were observed with an AxioScope A1 light microscope. (85 nm) was stained with 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate for 5 min, followed by 2% (w/v) lead citrate for 5 min [31]. Ultrastructural observations and recordings were made using a Thermo Fisher Talos 120c transmission electron microscope.

Histochemical staining

Fresh potato leaves at the full bloom stage were selected and stained with different reagents and made into temporary water mounts for observation under a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse E600w). Phenolics were detected using toluidine blue O stain [31], alkaloids with Wagner's reagent [32], and terpenoids with the NADI reagent [33]. For flavonoid detection, autofluorescence was observed directly under 365 nm UV light after treatment with 5% aluminum trichloride (w/v) in ethanol for 5 min, which induced yellow-green fluorescence at 450 nm [31]. The types and distributions of secondary metabolites in the trichomes were determined by different colors, and the contents of the components was judged to be high or low on the basis of color intensity.

Trichome morphological analysis

The scanning electron microscopy results revealed that dense trichomes distributed on potato leaves. Through observation, a total of five types were identified: type II, III, V NGTs and type VI and VII GTs. Type II NGTs exhibit multicellular bases and stalks (Fig. 2a). Type III NGTs include the bases of single cells and the stalks of multiple cells (Fig. 2b). Type V NGTs include unicellular bases and multicellular stalks, which are mostly distributed on leaf veins; basal unicellular cells, which are mostly elevated and inflated, with slightly curved hooks at the end; and stalk cells, which generally have no more than three cells (Fig. 2c). Type VI GTs include unicellular bases and multicellular stalks with one neck cell and head cells of four equally distributed cells of the same layer. GTs are in an erect form (Fig. 2d and e), and metabolites gradually accumulate in the glandular head cell as the GTs mature (Fig. 2f). Type VII GTs include single-cell bases, multicellular stalks, no neck cells, and an unequal division of head cells. According to the number of head cells, these GTs can be subdivided into: two layers of multicellular head GTs with 4–6 head cells (Fig. 2g) and three layers of multicellular head GTs with more than 6 head cells (Fig. 2h). The head of the two layers of multicellular head GTs forms an inverted triangle overall, and the heads of the three layers of multicellular head GTs are cylindrical, which is bent and attached to the epidermis. The secretions of mature GTs adhere to the surface of glandular head cells (Fig. 2i).

Trichome ultrastructure

Ultrastructural characterization of NGTs

NGTs develop from epidermal cell protrusions, and their cell walls are relatively thick (Fig. 3a). Cell walls are not lignified. The basal cells of the NGTs have a large vacuole and a small number of chloroplasts that contain starch granules (Fig. 3b-d). The stalk cells of NGTs are composed of large vacuoles and small numbers of chloroplasts and mitochondria (Fig. 3e, i and j). Starch granules are contained within the chloroplasts (Fig. 3f), and the cell wall is covered with a cuticle (Fig. 3g and h). The cuticle gradually accumulates and forms verrucous protrusions on the surface of NGTs (Fig. 3k and l). NGTs lack head cells, and their ultrastructure differs from that of GTs (see Table 2 for a description of trichome ultrastructure).

Ultrastructural characterization of Type VI GTs

Early stage of type VI GTs development: consists of a basal cell and an apical cell (Fig. 4a). In this stage, there are few cells in the GTs, which are primarily composed of vacuoles, with less dense cytoplasm (Fig. 4b-f), a small number of visible mitochondria (Fig. 4c). The apical cells continued to grow and divide into GTs with complete structures (Fig. 5a). At this stage, the VI-type glandular trichomes are in the middle phase of development. The GT head cells are irregularly shaped. They gradually increase in size, and the intercellular space decreases as they develop and mature (Fig. 6a). At this stage, the glandular trichomes are in the late phase of development. GTs have secretory capacity and are rich in organelles. Following the completion of secretory activity, the glandular trichome head cells begin to shrink (Fig. 7a). At this stage, there are no gaps between the adjacent cells of the GT cells. The ultrastructure of glandular hairs also varies across different developmental stages (see Table 2 for a description of trichome ultrastructure).

Before secretion, the head cells are electron-dense and covered by a thin cuticle layer (Fig. 5i). The neck cells of type VI GTs contain predominantly vacuoles with few chloroplasts (Fig. 5b). The cytoplasm of the head cell is abundant and contains chloroplasts, mitochondria, the Golgi apparatus, the endoplasmic reticulum, vacuoles, and the nucleus (Fig. 5c-l). Chloroplasts and mitochondria are numerous, and the chloroplasts have many starch granules (Fig. 6i). As the cells within the glandular trichomes increase in size (Fig. 6a), the gaps between adjacent cells are reduced, and the number of organelles increases (Fig. 6b-h). The head cells are metabolically active, with increased numbers of endoplasmic reticula and Golgi apparatus. There are many Golgi membrane vesicles, and many vesicles containing osmiophilic substances are formed (Fig. 5j). These substances can subsequently be transported to the vacuoles (Fig. 6b and h), then converges toward the cell wall, fuses with the cytoplasmic membrane and is then transferred to the cell wall (Fig. 7d and e) where it accumulates. The secretions are released into the subcuticular space between the cell wall and the cuticle (Fig. 7d). Then secretory products are released, and the apical cells begin to invaginate and shrivel (Fig. 7a). At this stage, there are no gaps between the adjacent cells of the GT cells, and there are plasmodesmata between the cells (Fig. 7e). The GT head cells still contain abundant organelles (Fig. 7b-c and f-l). High number of Golgi bodies (Fig. 7f). The vesicular transport activity is active (Fig. 7d-e and g). GT cells continue to produce and release secretions.

Ultrastructural characteristics of Type VII GTs

Type VII GT can be further classified into two-layered multicellular head Type VII GTs (Fig. 8a) and three-layered multicellular head Type VII GTs (Fig. 9a). Like type VI GTs, all mature glandular trichomes exhibit secretory capacity. The two-layered multicellular head of type VII GTs (Fig. 8a), with cytoplasm-rich head cells, thick cell walls (Fig. 8k), contains mitochondria, Golgi apparatus, plastids, and the endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 8b-l). There are fibrous osmiophilic substances (Fig. 8l) in vacuoles. There are also some osmiophilic substances present near the plastids (Fig. 8h). There is a subcuticular space between the cell wall and the cuticle that can be used to store secretions (Fig. 8d and i). The secretions are eventually released and adhere to the surface of the head cells (Fig. 2i).

Composition and distribution of metabolites in trichomes

Distribution of phenolic substances in potato trichomes

After staining potato trichomes with toluidine blue O, it was revealed that in type VI GTs before and after maturation, phenol substances are present mainly in head cells. Before GT matures, phenolic substance content is low and staining is light (Fig. 10a). After reaching maturity, the content of phenolic substances increased and staining is increase, and more phenolic compounds were located near the head cells center (Fig. 10c). After staining, the phenolic substances adhered to the surface of the head cells could be clearly observed (Fig. 10d). Due to the downward accumulation of secretions, partial staining reactions will also appear in the neck cells (Fig. 10b). The phenols of type VII GTs were present only in the head cells. The content before maturation was low (Fig. 10e), while the content after maturation was high (Fig. 10g and h). The metabolites still exhibited a color reaction after being secreted by the head cells (Fig. 10f).

Distribution of alkaloids in potato trichomes

After Wagner staining, the results revealed that the alkaloids in type VI GTs were distributed mainly in the head cells (Fig. 11a-c). Due to the downward accumulation of alkaloids, a small amount of them is present in the neck cells (Fig. 11d). The alkaloids in type VII GTs are evenly distributed in each daughter cell of the head of the GTs, and no alkaloids were detected in the stalk cells (Fig. 11e-h).

Distribution of terpenoids in potato trichomes

Potato trichomes were also stained with NADI reagent. The terpenoids of type VI GTs are distributed mainly in the secretory cavity of the head cells, and a small quantity is found in the neck cells and stalk cells (Fig. 12a-c). As the GTs develop and mature, terpenes accumulate, staining intensifies (Fig. 12d). Before type VII GTs reach maturity, the daughter cells of the glandular head cells contain small quantities of terpenes (Fig. 12e and f). As the GTs mature, the glandular head cells become wrinkled, terpenes accumulate, and the color intensifies; notably, terpenoids are distributed predominantly in the head cells (Fig. 12g and h).

Distribution of flavonoid compounds in potato trichomes

The potato trichomes were observed by fluorescence microscopy. Flavonoid compounds in type VI GTs were abundantly distributed in head cells, which exhibited distinct fluorescence, while a small quantity of flavonoid compounds were detected in the neck cells (Fig. 13a and b). After the glandular head cells wrinkled, the flavonoids accumulated, and the fluorescence intensity increased (Fig. 13c). Flavonoid compounds in type VII GTs are distributed in head and stalk cells (Fig. 13d-h), with higher levels in the head cells.

Discussion

The group previously observed the cellular development process of potato trichomes using a light microscope. The results showed that leaf trichomes develop from plant epidermal cell protrusions, which are typical leaf appendages. Scanning electron microscopy revealed that trichomes are composed of four main types of cells: basal cells, stalk cells, neck cells, and head cells. Additionally, there are five main types of trichomes: types II, III, V, VI, and VII, of which types II, III, and V are NGTs and types VI and VII are GTs. NGTs consist mainly of basal and stalk cells. Type VI GTs consist mainly of basal, stalk, neck, and head cells, while type VII GTs consist mainly of basal, stalk, and head cells [28]. These results are similar to those of Kaplan et al. [34]. The development of NGTs in potato is relatively simple, as no branching structures are formed. After the protrusion forms the basal and apical cells, the apical cell divides and elongates to form a multicellular stalk. Our previous studies confirmed that the development of GTs in potato occurs in three main stages. In the initiation and expansion of GT primordia in epidermal cell protrusions, the GT primordial cells divide in the longitudinal direction by cytokinesis into one bottom cell and one apical cell, and these 2 cells divide and differentiate in different ways, with the bottom cells forming the stalk and the apical cells forming the head region. The development of type II, III, and V NGTs is a continuous process. The early development of type VI and VII GTs occur only during the formation of leaf primordia and young leaves, and has not been observed on mature leaves. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesize that GTs develop from leaf primordia, the epidermal cells of young leaves.

Dimensional statistical analysis revealed that type VI GTs were larger in length and diameter than type VII GTs. Additionally, type VI GTs exhibited an erect form, whereas type VII GT stalk cells were curved and overall close to the plant surface. This may be because type VI GTs have a neck cell that is not present in type VII GTs, which fixes the GT and provides support, and because type VI GTs have a thicker stalk cell. Thus, type VI GTs are able to stand upright on the plant surface with the support of both the stalk and neck cells.

An examination of the ultrastructure of potato trichomes revealed that NGTs as a whole presented thicker cell wall. The cuticle accumulates and forms wart-like projections on the surface of the NGT stalk cells, which may increase their mechanical strength, thus contributing to their elastic properties. Therefore, NGTs can impede the climbing of small phytophagous animals and even limit their mobility and feeding preferences, thus increasing physical defenses and leading to insect resistance. The cytoplasmic density of NGTs is low and the cytoplasm contains only a small number of chloroplasts and mitochondria, while a large portion consists of large vacuoles. It is hypothesized that only a small amount of energy is synthesized internally and that the substances used for development are more likely to be transported by the plant's epidermal cells. Inside GTs at the early stages of development, the cytoplasmic content is low, and cytoplasmic aggregation occurs. There is a small amount of cytoplasm and few organelles besides vacuoles, probably because the organelles in the GTs are also at an early stage of development. Thus, the organelles within the cell cannot be clearly distinguished by their features. As the gland cells divide in different ways, the GTs develop into type VI and type VII GTs. As the GTs become fully developed, the head cells grow larger. When the GTs are mature, they begin to secrete substances, and at this time, the glandular cells are rich in cytoplasm and contain a variety of organelles, such as chloroplasts, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticula, Golgi apparatus, cell nucleus, and vacuoles. As the GTs mature, the number of organelles increases. Vesicular transport activity is vigorous. There are many vacuoles containing osmiophilic substances at this time. Owing to vesicle fluidity, the osmiophilic substances are subsequently transferred to the cell wall after moving closer and fusing with the cytoplasmic membrane. The secretions eventually accumulate in the subcuticular space formed between the cell wall and the cuticle. Then, the secretions are released, marking completion of the secretory activity of the GTs. Different plants have trichomes that store secretions in different locations. For example, the secretions of Millingtonia hortensis (Bignoniaceae) are stored in a thick upper cell wall and a wide epidermal layer [35]. The secretions of Helianthus annuus (Asteraceae) accumulation between the parietal cuticle and the tip cell wall [36]. The present study confirmed that potato secretions accumulate in the subcuticular space between the cell wall and cuticle.

The metabolites in the secretions released from different plants vary. For example, the metabolic components of secretions in the glandular trichomes of C. gratissimus (Lavender croton) are alkaloids, phenolic compounds, and lipids [37]. A diterpenoid and two triterpenoids were isolated from the GT secretions of Salvia sclarea (clary sage) [38]. Additionally, the secretions of Citrus spp. contain many essential oils, including monoterpenes, fatty acids, and flavonoids [39]. Sunflower (H. annuus L.) GTs can produce sesquiterpenes [40]. The GTs of cannabis (Cannabis sativa L.) are important sites for the synthesis of terpenes and cannabinoids [41]. Tomato GTs contain large quantities of terpenes [42]. In the present study, the GTs of potato trichomes were found to contain phenolics, alkaloids, proteins, terpenoids and flavonoids. As GTs mature, the metabolite content in the GTs increases and are distributed primarily in the head cells. This confirms that GT head cells can synthesize, store, and secrete secondary metabolites. Additionally, partial substance exchange or osmosis allows a portion of the secondary metabolites to descend from the head cells and gradually accumulate in the stalk cells, thus resulting in a color reaction upon histochemical staining.

Terpenoids have significant antifeedant and insecticidal activities and toxicity [43]. Total phenolics are resistant to herbivores [44]. Flavonoids and tannins are strong contact deterrents [45]. Acylglycans are the main plant metabolites responsible for insect resistance [46]. The starchy secretions of GTs inhibit pathogenic fungi [30]. The volatile organic compounds produced on tomato leaves are effective against late blight [47]. Wild potato leaf surface metabolites, such as hydroxycinnamic acid amides, may contribute to the early defense response against pathogenic bacteria [48]. Given the diverse biological functions of the multiple metabolite components in trichomes, our results further confirm that potato trichomes contain a wide range of metabolite components, such as phenolics, alkaloids, proteins, terpenoids, and flavonoids. In the future, potato trichomes can be developed by means of molecular biology, increasing the content of metabolites to improve the potato resistance to pests and diseases. However, the intricate defense mechanism by which potatoes utilize these compounds to construct chemical barriers, regulate their own growth, enhance environmental adaptability, and simultaneously ward off insects and diseases remains to be studied.

Conclusions

The types of potato trichomes are as follows: type II, III and V NGTs and type VI and VII GTs. Ultrastructural studies showed that potato NGTs have thick cell walls, fewer organelles and thicker cuticles. GTs are rich in organelles, including many mitochondria, chloroplasts, endoplasmic reticula, Golgi apparatus, and vacuoles, with large and distinct nuclei and vigorous metabolic activity. The vesicular transport activity is active, the cell wall is externally coated by the cuticle, and secretions accumulate in the subcuticular space between the cell wall and the cuticle. Eventually, the secretions are released, completing secretory activity. Histochemical staining revealed the main components secreted by different types of GTs. GTs are rich in metabolites, including alkaloids, terpenoids, flavonoids, and phenols and are mainly concentrated in glandular head cells. As the GTs mature, their metabolite content increases. Analysis of the metabolite composition of trichomes could help elucidate the chemical functions of potato trichomes and provide a new avenue to improve plant resistance to disease.
