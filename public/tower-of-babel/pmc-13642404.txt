Cut through the noise: vocal signals and auditory processing vary along an urbanization gradient in Pacific treefrogs
Authors: Carolyn F. Schwartz, Tarah R. Haslett, Alejandro Vélez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
From dense assemblages of vocalizing organisms in neotropical rainforests, to fast-flowing streams and waterfalls in the Old World, Peter Narins’ work has been instrumental to our understanding of how animals, particularly frogs, communicate in noisy environments. With increasing levels of human-generated noise, there has been an explosion of studies on how vocal signals and signaling behaviors change in urban soundscapes. Surprisingly, we know little about changes in sensory-processing mechanisms of receivers. We asked whether vocal signals, hearing sensitivity, and the ability to extract signals from noise of Pacific treefrogs (Pseudacris regilla) vary in ways that facilitate communication among sites with different levels of urbanization and noise. We show that males from sites with high levels of urbanization and noise produce calls at higher rates and lower frequencies. Auditory brainstem responses obtained in the presence and absence of noise reveal that, at frequencies present in the vocalizations, subjects from urbanized and noisy sites are less sensitive and experience reduced masking. Our results suggest that lower auditory sensitivity may function as a gain-reduction mechanism that improves the signal-to-noise ratio in sites with high levels of ambient noise. We discuss our results considering Narins’ contributions to anuran auditory neuroethology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00359-026-01840-2.

Introduction

The world is inherently noisy and, over the last century, population growth, urban expansion, and growing transportation networks have led to a drastic increase in human-generated noise (Shannon et al. 2016). In fact, the World Health Organization (2011) recognizes anthropogenic noise as one of the major and most hazardous forms of pollution. Noise generated by human activity has led to changes in soundscapes with ecological and evolutionary consequences, affecting species that communicate acoustically (Barber et al. 2010; Swaddle et al. 2015). Nevertheless, animals have been exposed to noise from natural sources long before humans started altering the acoustic environment. Hence, selection pressures of anthropogenic noise may act on preexisting mechanisms that ameliorate the detrimental effects of noise (Gomes et al. 2021a). Population-level studies among natural and anthropogenic soundscapes are therefore expected to bring much needed light on the impacts of anthropogenic noise on animals that communicate acoustically (Francis and Barber 2013; Jerem and Mathews 2021). Peter Narins, to whom we dedicate this article, has been a leading authority on the mechanisms, function, and evolution of anuran acoustic communication in noise, from both natural and anthropogenic sources.

Animal communication mediates biologically important behaviors like mate attraction and territory defense (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011). Thus, communication signals, signaling behaviors, and signal-processing mechanisms are expected to coevolve in ways that optimize the transfer of information between signalers and receivers (Seyfarth and Cheney 2003). Our understanding of this relationship between signalers and receivers has been largely informed by tests of two influential hypotheses: the sensory drive hypothesis, which posits that physical properties of the environment shape sensory systems and communication signals (Endler 1992; Cummings and Endler 2018), and the matched filter hypothesis, which proposes that sensory systems reflect physical properties of species-specific communication signals (Capranica and Moffat 1983; Dooling et al. 2000; Simmons 2013). Narins and his collaborators provided us with a textbook example of how noise imposes selection pressures on both signalers and receivers, shaping the evolution of animal communication systems. With their work on species that breed in habitats adjacent to fast-flowing streams and waterfalls, environments characterized by high levels of low-frequency noise, they uncovered ultrasonic communication in anuran amphibians and its underlying anatomical and physiological mechanisms (Narins et al. 2004; Feng et al. 2006; Suthers et al. 2006; Arch and Narins 2008; Feng and Narins 2008; Shen et al. 2008, 2011; Arch et al. 2009, 2012; Cobo-Cuan et al. 2020). Together, their results show how high levels of natural noise may shape the behavior, physiology, and anatomy of both signalers and receivers in ways that facilitate communication.

Over the last few decades, we have gained significant knowledge about how vocal signals and signaling behaviors change in response to increasing levels of anthropogenic noise (Wiley 2015; Kunc and Schmidt 2021; Duquette et al. 2021; Derryberry and Luther 2021; Gomes et al. 2022). Signalers may cope with the detrimental effects of anthropogenic noise by altering components of the signals in ways that increase the signal-to-noise ratio and reduce masking. For instance, signal redundancy helps to maintain information transfer in noisy environments (Shannon 1948); accordingly, signalers of various species increase their signaling rate, signal duration, or both when exposed to high levels of natural or anthropogenic noise (Gomes et al. 2022; Zaffaroni-Caorsi et al. 2023). Because anthropogenic noise is biased towards low frequencies (Nemeth and Brumm 2010; Gill et al. 2015), another mechanism to reduce masking in urban settings is to shift the spectral content of signals towards higher frequencies, which decreases the amount of spectral overlap between the signal and noise (Derryberry et al. 2020; Gomes et al. 2022). In contrast, we are only starting to learn whether and how hearing capabilities in receivers also change in response to increasing levels of anthropogenic noise (Nduwimana et al. 2026). Indeed, recent reviews propose that the major gap in the field is our lack of understanding of how sensory-processing mechanisms, particularly the ability to extract signals from noise, are changing in urban soundscapes (Francis and Barber 2013; Gomes et al. 2021a; Derryberry and Luther 2021). The matched filter hypothesis, for instance, predicts a shift in hearing sensitivity that matches changes in the frequency content of vocal signals in urban environments. Furthermore, the sensory drive hypothesis predicts changes in the auditory system that facilitate hearing in novel environments with high levels of anthropogenic noise. Therefore, comparisons of vocalizations and hearing capabilities across soundscapes with different levels of natural and anthropogenic noise are imperative to fully understand how animals communicate acoustically in environments altered by human activity (Simmons and Narins 2018; Derryberry and Luther 2021; Lee et al. 2023; Zaffaroni-Caorsi et al. 2023).

We asked whether properties of the vocal signals and the auditory system of Pacific treefrogs (Pseudacris regilla) vary in ways that facilitate communication among sites with different levels of urbanization and ambient noise (Fig. 1, Table 1). During the breeding season, male Pacific treefrogs aggregate in suitable habitats and form dense choruses in which they produce two types of advertisement calls aptly known as diphasic and monophasic due to their overall temporal structure (Allan 1973). The diphasic call (Fig. 1a) is typically the most produced call in a chorus and functions to attract females and maintain space between males (Allan 1973; Whitney and Krebs 1975). With increasing levels of vocal competition, or when approached by another male or a female, males often switch to producing monophasic calls (Allan 1973; Whitney 1981). Male-male interactions may escalate to the production of aggressive calls and overt aggression (Whitney 1980). All three types of calls (i.e., diphasic, monophasic, and aggressive) are comprised of pulses with fundamental and dominant frequencies of approximately 1.1 and 2.2 kHz, respectively (Whitney 1980; Vélez and Guajardo 2021). The auditory system of Pacific treefrogs has two regions of enhanced sensitivity, one between 2.0 and 3.0 kHz that closely matches the dominant frequency of the three types of calls and corresponds to tuning of the basilar papilla, and one below 1.0 kHz that corresponds to tuning of the amphibian papilla (Fig. 1b) (Straughan 1975; Stiebler and Narins 1990; Vélez and Sandoval 2024).Fig. 1a Waveform of a representative example of one diphasic advertisement call. b Average audiogram (black line, left y axis; n = 20 subjects) generated from visually detected ABR thresholds in quiet conditions and average frequency spectrum (gray line, right y axis; n = 24 males) of Pacific treefrog diphasic advertisement calls (redrawn from Vélez and Sandoval 2024). c Map showing the location of the eight field sites in the San Francisco Bay Area, CA. To understand how urbanization and increasing levels of noise may influence vocal signals and auditory processing, we (1) characterized the soundscape of eight breeding sites of Pacific treefrogs along an urban gradient at the San Francisco Bay Area in California, USA, (2) compared physical properties of the advertisement call produced by males at these sites, and (3) compared hearing abilities of males and females from these breeding sites (Fig. 1, Table 1). We predicted that if high levels of urbanization and noise influence the advertisement call in ways that facilitate communication, calls would be longer, repeated at faster rates, comprised of more pulses, or contain higher frequencies at sites with high levels of urbanization and ambient noise. We used the auditory brainstem response (ABR) to estimate absolute (in quiet) and masked (in the presence of broadband noise) hearing thresholds for tones of different frequencies. ABRs are a type of auditory evoked potential (AEP) that represents synchronized neural activity at lower stages of the auditory pathway in response to the onset of short tone pips (Hall 2007). Theoretical models propose that lower hearing sensitivity (i.e., higher absolute thresholds) may facilitate signal recognition in noisy environments by filtering out unwanted noise and increasing the signal-to-noise ratio (Lugli 2015, 2019). Therefore, based on these models and the sensory drive hypothesis, we predicted an increase in absolute hearing thresholds with increasing levels of urbanization and ambient noise. According to the matched-filter hypothesis, if the spectral content of the call changes in urban soundscapes, we predicted a parallel change in the frequency region of best hearing sensitivity. Using ABR thresholds obtained in the presence of broadband noise, we calculated critical ratios as the difference between the spectrum level of the masking noise and the sound pressure level of the tone at masked threshold. Critical ratios are, essentially, a measurement of the auditory system’s efficacy to detect signals in noise; lower critical ratios represent higher efficacy and narrower auditory filters, while higher critical ratios represent lower efficacy and wider auditory filters (Fletcher 1940). We therefore predicted that if ambient noise shapes the auditory system in ways that facilitate signal detection, critical ratios would be lower at sites with high levels of urbanization and ambient noise. Critical ratios, however, are independent of absolute thresholds obtained in quiet conditions. Thus, to directly consider differences in hearing sensitivity, we measured the amount of masking as the difference between masked and absolute thresholds (Hawkins and Stevens 1950; Dooling and Saunders 1975; Ehret 1975). If reduced hearing sensitivity serves to filter out noise, then we predicted a lower amount of masking by noise in subjects from sites with higher levels of urbanization and noise. Together, our results suggest that a decrease in hearing sensitivity reduces the amount of masking at sites with high levels of urbanization and ambient noise.

Materials and methods

Study system and sites

We recorded male advertisement calls and collected individuals for hearing tests from ponds located at eight breeding sites of Pacific treefrogs exposed to different sources and levels of natural and anthropogenic noise around the San Francisco Bay Area in California, USA (Fig. 1, Table 1). All the sites sampled in the present study are within the range of the sierra mitochondrial lineage of Pacific treefrogs (Vélez and Ingram 2025).

Noise and urbanization levels

Quantification of noise levels

We recorded the soundscape at each of our field sites using calibrated autonomous recording units (ARUs; Song Meter Mini Acoustic Recorder, Wildlife Acoustics, Maynard, MA, USA). ARUs were calibrated before deployment using a modified version of a previously published method (Ripmeester et al. 2010). Inside an audiometric booth (40a-7.5 Classic Series Single-Wall, IAC Acoustics, Naperville, IL, USA; inside dimensions: Length x Width x Depth, 193 × 254 × 198 cm), we broadcast pink noise (22.05 kHz sampling rate, 16-bit resolution) through a speaker (ORB mod 1; Orb Audio, Sherman Oaks, CA, USA) located 1 m away from the tip of the microphone of the ARU and the tip of the microphone of a Larson Davis SoundTrack LxT1 Class 1 sound level meter (Larson Davis Inc., Depew, NY, USA). We broadcast noise at levels ranging from 25 to 90 dB sound pressure level (SPL, re 20 µPa, dB Zeq) in 5 dB steps, recorded the noise with the ARU, and used the sound level meter to measure the level of the noise in the 1/3-octave bands centered at 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 kHz. We then used a custom code in Matlab (Mathworks, Inc., version R2024b) to create calibration curves for each ARU by filtering the recordings of pink noise with 1/3-octave band filters centered at 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 kHz, calculating the root mean squared (RMS) values of the output of each filter, and regressing the RMS values with the decibel measurements obtained with the sound level meter.

We deployed the calibrated ARUs at each site from 9 March to 4 April and from 1 to 28 June of 2023 to record the soundscape during and outside of the Pacific treefrog breeding season, respectively. The ARUs were mounted on a T-post, 1 m above ground level, on the edge of the pond; the location of the ARU around the perimeter of the pond was selected at random. We set the ARUs to record 15 min of the acoustic environment every hour, at the hour, with a sampling rate of 22.05 kHz, 16-bit resolution, and a gain of 12 dB. Importantly, Pacific treefrogs, and frogs from the genus Pseudacris in general, are highly philopatric; adults do not move far from their breeding site during the breeding season, juveniles stay close to the pond for several weeks after metamorphosis, and overwintering often takes place very close to the breeding sites (Schaub and Larsen 1978; Ethier et al. 2021). Therefore, our recordings are likely representative of soundscapes experienced by frogs in our study sites at different times in their lives.

Using a custom code in Matlab (Mathworks, Inc., version R2024b), soundscape field recordings were first filtered with 1/3-octave band filters centered at 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 kHz. Then, the RMS value of the output of each filter was transformed to dB SPL values using the calibration curves created for each ARU. Finally, we calculated the spectrum level (dB/Hz) using the level obtained from the output of each filter and the equivalent rectangular bandwidth of each filter. The dB values of the spectrum levels we report here can be compared among our study sites but should not be compared to dB values obtained with sound level meters in other studies given the differences in microphone directionality between ARUs and sound level meters (Slabbekoorn et al. 2007).

To assess and control for daily rhythmic patterns in noise levels, we used cosinor analyses from the GLMMCosinor package v0.2.1 (Parsons et al. 2024) in R v4.4.1 (R Core Team 2024). For each site and frequency, we generated generalized linear models (GLMs) that incorporated the spectrum level for each hour of the day as the dependent variable, season (during the breeding season or outside of the breeding season) as the independent variable, and time of day with two components of rhythmicity: a 12- and a 24-h period. Our reasoning for including two components of rhythmicity was that anthropogenic noise might peak twice throughout the day, during rush hours, while noise from frog vocal activity might peak once at night during the breeding season. We derived five cosinor parameters from the coefficient estimates of these models and report them in Table S1. The first parameter is the midline estimating statistic of rhythm, or MESOR, which represents the average spectrum level while accounting for rhythm (Refinetti et al. 2007; Cornelissen 2014). We also obtained the amplitude, which describes half the variation (between the peak and the midline) of the 12- and 24-h periods, and the acrophase, which describes the time at which spectrum level is at its highest peak in the 12- and 24-h periods (Refinetti et al. 2007; Cornelissen 2014). We ensured model fit and assumptions following Cornlissen (2014). Since our main objective was to determine how noise levels affect call properties and hearing abilities among our populations, we focused on MESOR values in subsequent analyses.

Quantification of urbanization levels

We used data from the National Land Cover Database (NLCD) available through the Multi-Resolution Land Characteristics (MRLC) Consortium (https://www.mrlc.gov/) to characterize land cover at each site. We extracted land cover data for the year 2022, at a resolution of 30 m, for discs with a radius of 1 km centered at each of our field sites. For each disc, we calculated the percentage of land covered by 15 land-cover categories provided by the Land Cover tool: (i) open water, (ii) developed open space, (iii) developed low intensity, (iv) developed medium intensity, (v) developed high intensity, (vi) barren land, (vii) deciduous forest, (viii) evergreen forest, (ix) mixed forest, (x) shrub/scrub, (xi) grassland herbaceous, (xii) pasture/hay, (xiii) cultivated crops, (xiv) woody wetlands, and (xv) emergent herbaceous wetlands (Dewitz 2024).

Male advertisement calls

Acoustic recordings

At each field site, we obtained digital recordings of the advertisement calls produced by focal Pacific treefrog males using a TASCAM-DR 40 digital recorder (44.1 kHz, 16-bit resolution) coupled with a Sennheiser ME66 directional microphone equipped with a Sennheiser MZW66 foam windscreen and mounted on a handheld suspension grip (see Table 1 for sample sizes). The tip of the microphone was placed approximately 50–75 cm in front of the calling male, and eight to 15 vocalizations were recorded. After recording, we measured the snout to vent length (SVL) of the focal male to the nearest 0.1 mm using dial calipers. We also measured the water, air, and wet-bulb air temperature from the location of the calling male to the nearest 0.1 C with a high precision thermometer (H-B Instrument Enviro-Safe or Fisher Brand Traceable thermometers).

Acoustical analysis

In Raven Pro v 1.5, we used the waveform view to measure temporal properties and the power spectrum view to measure spectral properties of the advertisement calls. An example of a call waveform and power spectrum are depicted in Fig. 1. From each call, we counted the number of pulses in each phase and measured call duration as the time between the onset of the first pulse of the call and the offset of the last pulse of the call. Call rate was calculated as the inverse of the call period, which was measured as the time between the onset of the first pulse of the call and the onset of the first pulse of the following call. The dominant frequency was measured as the spectral peak of highest amplitude from the average power spectrum of the call (Hann windows, FFT size of 2048 points, 75% overlap). Agreement between the two observers who measured call properties was high (n = 18 calls, intraclass correlation r = 0.78–0.99).

Auditory evoked potentials

Subjects

Adult Pacific treefrogs in breeding condition were collected individually (vocally active males) or as pairs in amplexus at night during the breeding season, between 25 January and 19 April in 2022 and between 27 January and 18 April in 2023 (see Table 1 for sample sizes). Subjects were placed in plastic containers, transported to the laboratory at San Francisco State University, and maintained at approximately 2–4 °C in coolers filled with ice until the day of testing. We removed subjects from the cooler 40–60 min before testing to allow their body temperature to reach 14 ± 1 °C, and we measured their SVL to the nearest 0.1 mm with dial calipers and their mass to the nearest 0.1 g with a Pesola spring scale. After completion of all tests and full recovery from the procedure, frogs were released at their collection site within 5 days of collection.

Auditory test equipment and procedures

Our protocols for obtaining AEPs have been previously described (Vélez and Sandoval 2024), and readers are referred to those studies for details not included here. Briefly, we first immobilized subjects with an intramuscular injection of 0.4 or 0.5 mg/mL succinylcholine (dosage of 3ug/g–5ug/g) and wrapped them with a damp surgical gauze to facilitate cutaneous respiration. Subjects were then placed in a natural upright position on a table lined with acoustic foam inside a radio-shielded audiometric booth (40a-3 Classic Series Single-Wall, IAC Acoustics, Naperville, IL, USA; inside dimensions: Length x Width x Depth, 106.68 × 203.2 × 198.12 cm). We then applied 2.5% lidocaine HCl on the subject’s head and inserted three subdermal electrodes (Grass F-E2, West Warwick, RI, USA) to record AEPs. Electrodes were placed between the eyes (positive), above the left tympanum (negative), and above the right tympanum (ground); testing continued when electrode impedance was between 1 and 3 kΩ. We monitored the temperature during auditory tests by placing the probe of a digital thermometer (Zacro LCD digital aquarium thermometer) under the surgical gauze wrapping the subject. Testing temperatures ranged between 14 and 17 ºC, which are typical temperature during the breeding season of Pacific treefrogs at our study sites (Vélez and Sandoval 2024; Messersmith et al. 2024).

A Tucker Davis Technologies System III (Tucker Davis Technologies, Alachua, FL, USA) and a Dell PC running BioSigRZ software were used to coordinate stimulus presentation and response acquisition. We used a TDT RA4PA Medusa PreAmp linked to a low-impedance TDT RA4LI headstage to amplify (200 k) and digitize (24.41 kHz) AEPs recorded with the subdermal electrodes. With a TDT RZ-6 Multi-I/O Processor, biological responses were then resampled (48.8 kHz), filtered (bandpass 0.03–3 kHz, notch filtered at 60 Hz), and stored in the computer for offline analyses.

Acoustic stimuli

All acoustic stimuli were digitally generated using TDT SigGenRZ (tone pips) or TDT RPvdsEx (masking noise) with a sampling rate of 48.8 kHz and 16-bit resolution. Digital stimuli were converted to analog with the TDT RZ6 multi I/O processor, equalized with a dbx 231 s graphic eq (Professional Acoustics, Sandy, Utah, USA), amplified with a Crown XLi 15,000 amplifier (Crown International, Los Angeles, CA, USA), and broadcasted through an ORB Mod 1 speaker (Orb Audio, Sherman Oaks, CA, USA) mounted 0.75 m in front of the subject’s head. Sound levels were calibrated by placing the class 1 microphone of a SoundTrack LxT1 sound-level meter (Larson Davis Inc., Depew, NY, USA) at the approximate position of a subject’s head; the frequency response of our playback system was flat ± 2.0 dB between 0.25 and 10 kHz.

We estimated hearing thresholds for tone pips of different frequencies in three masking conditions: a no-noise condition, and two conditions in which noise was broadcast at two different levels. The masking noise was generated by filtering white Gaussian noise using a low-pass Biquad filter with corner frequency of 10.0 kHz and 4th-order Butterworth coefficients. In the two noise conditions, the masker was broadcast continuously at 65 or 71 dB SPL, corresponding to spectrum levels of 25 and 31 dB/Hz, respectively. We chose these levels because they span the range of environmental noise spectrum levels previously measured (Nemeth and Brumm 2010; Derryberry and Luther 2021) and are similar to levels used in previous studies of masked auditory thresholds (Moss and Simmons 1986; Simmons 1988; Southall et al. 2003; Noirot et al. 2011; Chou and Gall 2025). Tone pips were 5–ms long with 1–ms cos2 rise and fall ramps, had frequencies of 0.4, 0.5, 0.63, 0.8, 1.0, 1.25, 1.6, 2.0, 2.5, 3.15, 4, and 5 kHz, and were broadcast with alternating phase values of 90° and 270° at a rate of 31.1 stimuli per second. AEPs were recorded at 10 levels for each frequency, starting at 85 dB SPL down to 40 dB SPL in steps of 5 dB. Two AEP replicates, each one consisting of the average response to 400 stimulus presentations, were obtained for each combination of masking condition, tone frequency, and tone level. Both replicates were then averaged; therefore, all subsequent analyses are based on the average response to 800 stimulus presentations.

All subjects were first tested in the no-noise condition. Subjects were then tested in the noise conditions (noise spectrum levels of 25 and 31 dB/Hz), and the order of the noise conditions was randomly assigned to each subject. While most subjects completed all auditory tests, some subjects only completed tests of one or two conditions (see Table 1 for details). We ran a broadband click at 85 dB SPL before and after each masking condition. The stereotypical click ABRs allowed us to assess physiological consistency of the subjects over the course of the tests and to ensure our masking noises did not cause threshold shifts (Lucas et al. 2002; Vélez et al. 2015; Chou and Gall 2025).

Auditory brainstem response analyses

We analyzed the auditory brainstem response (ABR), which is a type of AEP that represents synchronous neural activity in the inner ear, auditory nerve, and auditory brainstem in response to the onset of short tone pips (Hall 2007). The ABR waveform of Pacific treefrogs is characterized by a series of peaks and troughs and resembles ABRs reported for other species of frogs (Fig. 2; Buerkle et al. 2014; Schrode et al. 2014; Yang et al. 2019; Vélez and Sandoval 2024). In frogs, the first ABR peak, with a latency of 3–7 ms, is generally assumed to reflect auditory nerve activity, whereas the neural origins of the remaining peaks are unknown (Buerkle et al. 2014; Lauridsen et al. 2021). Absolute and masked ABR thresholds were determined using a visual detection method (Brittan-Powell et al. 2002). For each tone frequency, the average AEP traces were plotted in descending stimulus level and the ABR threshold was defined as the level 2.5 dB (half step) below the lowest stimulus level at which a response with at least one peak was observed on the trace (Fig. 2). Given frequency- and level-dependent differences in ABRs to stimuli primarily encoded by the amphibian and basilar papillae (Buerkle et al. 2014; Schrode et al. 2014), visually detected thresholds were not based on a single ABR peak. Compared to automated detection methods, this visual detection method yields lower estimates of ABR thresholds while maintaining the same overall audiogram shape (Brittan-Powell et al. 2002; Schrode et al. 2014; Vélez and Sandoval 2024). Agreement between the three testers who conducted ABR experiments and determined ABR thresholds was high (n = 216 threshold estimates, intraclass correlation r = 0.96).

For both noise conditions, we calculated critical ratios (in dB) for each tone frequency by subtracting the spectrum level of the masking noise from the sound pressure level of the tone at masked ABR threshold. Similarly, we calculated the amount of masking at each frequency as the difference between the masked ABR threshold and the absolute ABR threshold obtained in the no-noise condition.

Statistical analyses

Urbanization and noise indices

We used a principal components analysis (PCA) with all 15 values of land cover and 10 MESOR spectrum levels (five frequencies during two seasons) and applied the broken-stick method (Jackson 1993) to retain principal components (PCs). We included all variables in the PCA to account for potential correlations between landscape properties and noise; for instance, because anthropogenic and surf noise are biased toward lower frequencies (Gomes et al. 2021a), sites with high coverage by developed land or open water may have higher MESOR values at low frequencies. Hereafter, the scores of the retained PCs are used as indices of urbanization and noise. Note that SFO, a breeding site by San Francisco’s international airport, is the only site predominantly covered by land developed at medium and high intensity and has extremely high MESOR values of spectrum levels at all frequencies (Table 2, Fig. 3). Such extreme values may influence the PCA and our interpretation of the results. Furthermore, animals may respond to, or be impacted by, the extremely high levels of noise typical of airports differently than to other less intense noises from natural and anthropogenic origins (de Framond and Brumm 2022). Sites with similar levels of urbanization and noise may be clustered in space, introducing potential geographical confounds to the analysis. To assess the extent to which sites with similar levels of noise and urbanization are geographically clustered or dispersed, we assessed spatial autocorrelation of the urbanization and noise indices (PC scores) by calculating Moran’s I using the ape package (Paradis and Schliep 2019) in R (R Core Team 2024). We also used the vegan package (Oksanen et al. 2024) in R to perform Mantel tests with the pairwise Haversine distances between sites and the pairwise Euclidian distances between PC scores of each site.

Advertisement calls

From each recording, we analyzed between 3 and 10 consecutive diphasic calls unobstructed by noise or by calls from nearby individuals and obtained the median value for each call property. These median values were used as the single data points contributed by each male for each call property in all subsequent analyses. In Pacific treefrogs, call duration and call rate are influenced by temperature, while call dominant frequency correlates with body size (Vélez and Guajardo 2021). Therefore, following previous studies (Messersmith et al. 2024; Vélez and Ingram 2025), we standardized these temporal properties to a common temperature of 14 °C and the dominant frequency to a standard SVL of 35 mm. We used water or wet-bulb air temperature for the linear regressions, depending on whether the male was in direct contact with the water while calling. To investigate the effects of our urbanization and noise indices on all call properties, we fitted generalized linear models using the glm function from the stats package in R v4.4.1 (R Core Team 2024) with Gaussian probability distribution for dominant frequency, call rate, and call duration, and Poisson distribution for the number of pulses. Model assumptions were checked by visual inspections of QQ plots, plots of residuals versus fitted values, and plots of residuals versus leverage. Since we examined 4 different call properties, we used a Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple hypothesis testing with a false discovery rate of 0.1.

Absolute and masked ABR thresholds, critical ratios, and amount of masking

We investigated the extent to which auditory function is influenced by our indices of urbanization and noise using linear mixed effect models with the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015) in R v4.4.1 (R Core Team 2024). For each of the four measurements of auditory function, we first created a maximal model that included tone frequency (12 levels), PC scores, and the two-way interactions between tone frequency and PC scores as independent variables. For models of masked ABR thresholds, critical ratios, and amount of masking, our maximal models also included masker spectrum level (two levels: 25 and 31 dB/Hz) and the two- and three-way interactions between masker spectrum level, tone frequency, and PC scores as independent variables. Because subject sex and size influence ABR thresholds in Pacific treefrogs (Vélez and Sandoval 2024), we controlled for their effects by including sex (2 levels), mean-centered SVL (Delaney and Maxwell 1981), their two-way interaction, and their two-way interaction with tone frequency as covariates in our maximal model (Yzerbyt et al. 2004; Keller 2014). We did not include higher order interactions of these covariates in our model because they are not relevant to our hypotheses and adding them unnecessarily reduces the degrees of freedom and power of our analyses. In our maximal model, we treated subject ID as a repeated factor and included tester (3 testers: CS, TH, AV) and subject ID nested within site as random effects. We then used the step function of the lmertest package (Kuznetsova et al. 2017) to perform backward elimination of non-significant random and main effects; random effects are eliminated first by likelihood ratio tests, followed by elimination of main effects by p-values and F-tests. For the resulting reduced model, we report results from type III analysis of variance tests, which test for the significance of one factor while controlling for all other factors and interactions, using degrees of freedom (df) calculated with the Satterthwaite's method. We used the emmeans package (Lenth and Piaskowski 2017) to obtain the estimated marginal means and standard error of the means for visualization. We also used the effectsize package (Ben-Shachar et al. 2020) to obtain partial η2 values, which represent the proportion of variance explained by each variable while ignoring the variance explained by other variables. Effect sizes were considered to be very small for partial η2 values less than 0.01, small for values between 0.01 and 0.06, medium for values between 0.06 and 0.14, and large for values higher than 0.14 (Field 2013). Model assumptions were checked using visual inspection of diagnostic plots; no serious departures of model assumptions were observed.

Results

Variation in levels of noise and urbanization across sites

Table 2 summarizes the MESOR spectrum levels at each of the five frequencies during and outside of the breeding season and the percentage of land covered by the different NLCD categories at each site; here, we describe the general trends. The percentage of land covered by low-, medium-, and high-intensity development was lowest at EBMUD (2.4%) and PORE (2.9%), and highest at the airport site (SFO, 88.1%) and the Presidio of San Francisco (PRESIDIO, 59.6%). Rodeo beach (RODEO) had the highest amount of open water (40%) followed by Mori Point (MORI, 18%). The percentage of land covered by the different forest categories, shrubland, and wetlands was lowest at PRESDIO (0.83%) followed by SFO (1.25%), and highest at EBMUD and PORE (70% and 69%, respectively). The HOTEL and LAWLER sites are mostly covered by grassland (60% and 23%, respectively) and have relatively high levels of developed land in the open space and low-intensity categories combined (HOTEL: 18%; LAWLER: 37%).

During the breeding season, Pacific treefrog vocal activity is evidenced by high levels of noise during the night, particularly at frequencies above 0.5 kHz (Figs. 3a, S1). Outside of the breeding season, noise levels across all frequencies are higher during the day (Figs. 3a, S1). Daytime levels of noise are similar during and outside of the breeding season across all frequencies at all sites (Figs. 3a, S1). In general, MESOR spectrum levels were biased toward low frequencies during and outside of the breeding season (Fig. 3b). Across sites, there was higher variability in MESOR spectrum levels outside of the breeding season, particularly at frequencies below 2.0 kHz (Fig. 3b). Overall, MESOR spectrum levels were higher during the breeding season at all sites, particularly at frequencies present in the advertisement call (Fig. 3b). Together with high levels of vocal activity and more frequent rain, lower scattering of noise due to the presence of less foliage and ground cover may account for the overall higher levels of noise at all frequencies during the breeding season (Grinfeder et al. 2022).

Indices of urbanization and noise

When all the sites are included in the analysis, the 10 MESOR values and 15 land-cover categories can be described by one PC that explains 50% of the variation and has an eigenvalue of 12.4 (Table S2). The retained PC (PC1) loads positively (> 0.25) on coverage by medium- and high-intensity developed land, all MESOR values outside of the breeding season except 4.0 kHz, and MESOR values at 0.5 and 1.0 kHz during the breeding season; PC1 also loads negatively (< − 0.15) on the percentage of land covered by deciduous forest, evergreen forest, shrub/scrub, and woody wetlands (Tables 2, S2). These results suggest that most of the variables, rather than just a few dominating variables, contribute information to PC1, and that PC1 describes a gradient from natural and quieter sites (negative values of PC1) to highly urbanized and noisy sites (positive values of PC1).

Note that the PC1 score for the airport site (SFO) is very high compared to the scores of all other sites (Fig. 3c; PC1 score at SFO = 7.2, at all other sites range from − 4.5 to 1.7). We ran a second PCA excluding the airport site to evaluate the extent to which its high levels of noise and urbanization influenced the analysis and results. The results largely parallel those obtained when the airport site is included in the analysis (Tables 2, S2, Fig. 3c). The broken-stick method retains two PCs that together explain 68% of the variance. The first principal component (PC1) explains 41% of the variance and loads positively (> 0.2) on percent cover by developed land (open, low, and medium intensity), all MESOR values outside of the breeding season except 4.0 kHz, and MESOR values at 0.5 and 1.0 kHz during the breeding season, and negatively (< − 0.2) on percent cover by deciduous and evergreen forests, pastures, and woody wetlands. PC2 explains an additional 27% of the variance and loads positively (> 0.3) on MESOR values at 2.0 and 4.0 kHz during the breeding season, and negatively (< − 0.3) on MESOR values at 0.25 kHz during the breeding season and on percent cover by open water and barren land. Thus, PC2 describes variation in noise levels mainly due to Pacific treefrog chorus activity and very low-frequency noise (Fig. 3), as well as the percent cover by open water and barren land. Together, these results suggest that PC1 describes a gradient from relatively quiet natural landscapes to urban landscapes with high levels of ambient noise from sources other than Pacific treefrog activity, regardless of whether the airport site is included in the analysis. Because of its high PC1 score, the airport site has high leverage and may be an influential data point that could make a weak relationship appear strong or a strong relationship appear weak. Hence, all further analyses were performed using PC values obtained both with and without the airport site.

No spatial autocorrelation among sites with similar values of urbanization and noise indices

Pairwise distances between sites range between 3.2 and 89 km. Sites with similar values of PC1 are not clustered together nor dispersed when the airport site was included or excluded from the analysis (all sites in analyses: Moran’s I = − 0.13, p = 0.924; excluding airport site: Moran’s I = − 0.18, p = 0.962). Likewise, we did not find evidence of clustering or dispersion among sites with similar values of PC2 (Moran’s I = − 0.21, p = 0.765). Moreover, Mantel tests reveal that geographic distance was not correlated with pairwise distances of PC1 scores (all sites in analyses: r = − 0.069, p = 0.568; excluding airport site: r = 0.156, p = 0.250) or PC2 scores (r = − 0.422, p = 0.954). These results suggest that, in our sample, sites with similar levels of noise and urbanization are not clustered together or dispersed in space, nor do they follow a geographic gradient.

No differences in body size among sites or levels of noise and urbanization

Because urbanization and time exposed to anthropogenic noise may influence body size (Meillère et al. 2015; Merckx et al. 2018; Rebrina et al. 2020), and body size influences hearing sensitivity and spectral call properties in Pacific treefrogs (Vélez and Guajardo 2021; Vélez and Sandoval 2024), we evaluated the extent to which body size varied among sites or correlated with levels of urbanization and noise. Body size did not vary among sites (males: F7,132 = 1.746, p = 0.104; females: F6,18 = 1.220, p = 0.341), nor did it correlate with our indices of noise and urbanization regardless of whether the airport site was included (PC1 – males: F1,138 = 2.6876, p = 0.103; females: F1,23 = 2.4074, p = 0.1344), or excluded from the analysis (PC1 – males: F1,120 = 0.0004, p = 0.985; females: F1,22 = 2.6446, p = 0.118; PC2 – males: F1,120 = 0.1072, p = 0.744; females: F1,22 = 0.986, p = 0.336).

We found a significant effect of PC1 on call rate; males from sites with higher levels of urbanization and ambient noise produce calls at faster rates (analysis including all sites: F1,136 = 6.04, p = 0.015, partial η2 = 0.04; analysis excluding airport site: F1,119 = 5.35, p = 0.023, partial η2 = 0.03; Fig. 4). Discussion

We asked whether properties of both signalers and receivers change in ways that facilitate communication among sites with different levels of urbanization and noise. With increasing levels of urbanization and noise, we predicted that calls would be longer, repeated at faster rates, comprised of more pulses, or contain higher frequencies. Our results show that male Pacific treefrogs from sites with high levels of urbanization and noise produce diphasic advertisement calls at faster rates and, in stark contrast to our predictions, with lower frequencies. We found that subjects from sites with high levels of urbanization and noise are less sensitive (i.e., have higher absolute thresholds) between 1.6 and 4.0 kHz, a frequency region important for communication. This result is consistent with our predictions based on theoretical models of signal recognition in noisy environments (Lugli 2015, 2019). Lower hearing sensitivity may act as a gain-reduction mechanism and filter out unwanted noise. Consistent with this hypothesis, we found that subjects from sites with high levels of urbanization and noise experience less masking in this range of frequencies important for communication. Finally, our prediction that the auditory system of subjects from sites with high levels of urbanization and noise would be more efficient at extracting signals from noise was not supported, as we did not find differences in critical ratios at frequencies important for communication. Note, however, that our experimental design does not allow us to establish causal relationships. With this in mind, we start our discussion by addressing the effects of the airport site, as the patterns we report here were stronger when the airport site was removed from the analysis.

Airport noise and implications for communication

Because noise characteristics, rather than the source itself, are what is expected to influence animal communication (Gomes et al. 2021b; Sweet et al. 2022; Reed et al. 2022), there is growing interest in moving from a dichotomous classification of rural vs urban to more systematic measures of noise (Pater et al. 2009; Gill et al. 2015; Francis et al. 2023). Our sample includes sites with high levels of urban development (like SFO and PRESIDIO) and sites very close to the Pacific Ocean (RODEO and MORI), two environments often characterized by high levels of low frequency noise (Gomes et al. 2021a). For these reasons, we created our indices based on properties of the landscape and the soundscape. Our analyses including and excluding the airport site (SFO) show that PC1 describes a gradient from relatively quiet and natural environments to environments characterized by high levels of urbanization and noise (Table 2), with the airport site earning an extremely high PC1 score (Fig. 3).

Consistent with previous studies showing unusually high levels of noise at or near airports (Gil et al. 2015; Dominoni et al. 2016; Wolfenden et al. 2019), the airport site in our study has extremely high levels of noise compared to all other sites both during and outside of the breeding season (Fig. 3, Table 2). Such extreme levels of noise are likely to impact animals differently than less intense noises from anthropogenic or natural origin. Indeed, studies suggest that signalers may use different strategies when coping with airport noise and other types of ambient noise (Sun and Narins 2005; Gil et al. 2015; Kruger and Du Preez 2016; Wolfenden et al. 2019; de Framond and Brumm 2022). In receivers, the extreme high levels of airport noise may also affect the auditory system differently. For instance, it is possible that the auditory system is malleable enough to adjust to moderate increases in noise levels within the range of typical noise levels from different natural sources, but not to extreme increases of noise from recently introduced sources, like those produced by aircrafts near airports. The ability of signalers and receivers to adjust within a certain range of noise levels, but not to extreme noise levels, could result in non-linear relationships between properties of the soundscape and properties of vocalizations and the auditory system. For instance, call properties (like the dominant frequency) and hearing capabilities (like sensitivity) may change linearly within a range of moderate noise levels, but not proportionally to extreme levels of noise. In such a scenario, unusually high levels of noise may be highly influential, making the relationship between ambient noise levels and properties of the vocal signals and the auditory system weak. Our results provide some support for this hypothesis. Compared to all other sites, very high levels of noise and urbanization at the airport site resulted in a very high PC1 score (Fig. 3) with high leverage, which made the relationship between PC1 and vocal and hearing properties weaker when included in the analysis. Future studies that include sites with a wide range of ambient noise levels, from very low to extremely high, are necessary to better model such potential non-linear relationships and test this hypothesis.

Variation in call properties may offer limited benefits at urban and noisy sites

Our results provide partial support for the hypothesis that signal properties vary in ways that increase the signal-to-noise ratio with increasing levels of urbanization and ambient noise. While males from sites with high levels of ambient noise deliver calls at faster rates than those from less noisy sites (Fig. 4; Table 3), the effect is small and the difference in predicted call rates between sites with highest and lowest levels of ambient noise is only about six calls per minute (~ 0.1 calls/sec). Furthermore, call dominant frequency decreases with increasing levels of ambient noise, with a difference of approximately 100 Hz between the sites with highest and lowest levels of noise (Fig. 4). While this result is against our expectations, it aligns with a prior study showing that dominant frequency decreases with increasing levels of anthropogenic noise in Pacific treefrogs (Nelson et al. 2017). The predicted differences in call rate and dominant frequency between the sites with highest and lowest levels of noise are within one standard deviation of these properties measured in several populations of Pacific treefrogs (Nelson et al. 2017; Vélez and Guajardo 2021; Messersmith et al. 2024; Vélez and Ingram 2025). In frogs, calling is one of the most energetically costly behaviors, entailing an increase in metabolism of up to 21 times the resting metabolic rate (Prestwich 1994), and the rate of oxygen consumption increases with increasing call rates (Taigen and Wells 1985; Taigen et al. 1985). Such high energetic costs may impose physiological constraints and limit the extent to which males can increase calling rates at sites with high levels of ambient noise. Morphological constraints, on the other hand, may impose limits on the extent of variation in call dominant frequency. As in many species of frogs (Gerhardt and Huber 2002), call dominant frequency is strongly correlated with body size in Pacific treefrogs (Vélez and Guajardo 2021). In our study, body size did not differ among sites, nor did it vary with our indices of urbanization and noise, constraining the range of variation for call frequency.

The extent to which such small differences in call rate and dominant frequency influence behavior in natural settings is currently unknown. Nelson and collaborators (2017) suggested that the downward shift in call frequency is not biologically significant; however, such a shift is expected to make the signal-to-noise ratio even lower at sites with high noise levels, potentially impairing communication even further. While we know little about call detection and recognition in noise in Pacific treefrogs, or about female preferences for call rate and dominant frequency, studies on other closely related treefrogs with pulsed calls suggest that female frogs favor faster call rates in the presence of background noise, even when the difference between alternatives is as small as one standard deviation (Ward et al. 2013). Conversely, females may not distinguish between calls varying by one standard deviation in frequency, even in quiet conditions (Gerhardt 2005; Schrode et al. 2012). The extent to which faster call rates and lower dominant frequencies influence signal detection, recognition, and discrimination at sites with higher levels of ambient noise is an open question that merits future investigation.

Hearing sensitivity, gain reduction, and reduced masking in noisy environments

Our results show that decreases in hearing sensitivity are associated with increasing levels of urbanization and ambient noise (Fig. 5a). A decrease in hearing sensitivity associated with urban living was first reported in a study comparing Easter Islanders who never left the relatively quiet island with those who left the island and lived in noisy continental cities; hearing sensitivity was negatively related to the time spent living in cities (Goycoolea et al. 1986). More recently, Nduwimana and collaborators (2026) showed that hearing sensitivity decreases with increasing levels of urbanization in house sparrows (Passer domesticus). While a loss of hearing sensitivity may be interpreted as detrimental, it could possibly benefit receivers that communicate in noisy environments. Acoustic communication occurs at signal levels much higher than those close to hearing thresholds. Ambient noise, however, can hinder signal recognition and discrimination by receivers. Theoretical models propose that reduced hearing sensitivity may act as a gain-reduction mechanism, filtering out undesirable noise and increasing the signal-to-noise ratio (Lugli 2015). We show that, in the region of frequencies used in communication, the difference in thresholds between the sites with highest and lowest levels of ambient noise is about 4 dB. We propose that lower auditory sensitivity at sites with high levels of ambient noise may function as a gain-reduction mechanism that filters out noise and improves the signal-to-noise ratio.

The amount of masking is the difference between masked and unmasked thresholds and increases linearly with the effective level of the masking noise, which describes the energy of noise passing through the auditory filter that just masks the signal (Hawkins and Stevens 1950; Dooling and Saunders 1975; Ehret 1975). Our results show that the amount of masking between 1.6 and 2.5 kHz is lower for subjects from sites with higher levels of urbanization and ambient noise (Fig. 8c). This is because, while absolute thresholds at these frequencies were higher at sites with high levels of noise (Fig. 5a), masked thresholds did not vary (Fig. 6c). These results suggest that reduced hearing sensitivity improves the signal-to-noise ratio by filtering out noise in a frequency region important for communication in Pacific treefrogs. Importantly, the ~4 dB difference in amount of masking in this frequency range may be biologically significant and sufficient to ameliorate some masking effects. In Cope’s gray treefrogs (Hyla chrysoscelis), for instance, females can perceive 2–4 dB differences in advertisement calls and discriminate against the lower-level alternative in the presence and absence of background noise (Bee et al. 2012). Therefore, a 4-dB reduction in masking may influence communication at sites with high levels of ambient noise.

Mechanisms that filter out noise at biologically important frequencies in noisy settings may not be restricted to the auditory system and communication. Funnel-weaving spiders (Agelenopsis pennsylvanica) use web vibrations during prey detection, recognition, and localization, and vibratory noise increases with increasing levels of urbanization (Mortimer 2019). Interestingly, under noisy conditions, urban spiders construct webs that attenuate low-frequency vibrations; this gain-reduction mechanism could enhance sensory reception of prey vibrations under noisy environments (Pessman and Hebets 2025). Together, these results suggest that animals may face a tradeoff and favor filtering out noise at the cost of reduced sensitivity in settings with high levels of ambient noise.

Critical ratios and hearing in noisy environments

Critical ratios are an estimate of the efficacy of the auditory system to extract a signal from noise, with lower critical ratios representing a higher efficacy (narrower filters). Accordingly, critical ratios have been deemed an essential measure to understand how receivers are impacted by, and may cope with, increasing levels of anthropogenic noise (Dooling and Leek 2018; Gomes et al. 2021a; Derryberry and Luther 2021; Fossesca et al. 2023; Francis et al. 2023). Because critical ratios are, essentially, a signal-to-noise ratio, they have been used to model and predict how communication distance or signal active space are influenced by anthropogenic noise (Lohr et al. 2003; Noirot et al. 2011; Gall et al. 2012; Chou and Gall 2025). Furthermore, recent studies have shown that differences in critical ratios among three species of birds align with species differences in antipredator behavior in the presence of anthropogenic noise (Damsky and Gall 2017; Chou et al. 2023; Chou and Gall 2025). The extent to which critical ratios vary within species, and the implications of such variation for communication in different soundscapes, have received little attention.

Our results show within-species variation in critical ratios associated with population-level differences in urbanization and noise (Table 4). Critical ratios varied the most at frequencies below 1.0 kHz (Fig. 7). Frogs from sites with high levels of urbanization and ambient noise have higher critical ratios between 0.5 and 0.8 kHz, while frogs from sites with high levels of chorus noise (high values of PC2) have lower critical ratios at the lowest frequencies measured (0.4 and 0.5 kHz; Fig. 7). Note, however, that PC2 also loads negatively with noise spectrum levels at the lowest frequency measured (0.25 kHz). Therefore, it is possible that higher critical ratios at the lowest frequencies are associated with high levels of low-frequency noise, instead of low levels of chorus noise. The frequencies at which critical ratios varied the most with noise levels are primarily encoded by the amphibian papilla in Pacific treefrogs (Stiebler and Narins 1990). Importantly, tuning of the amphibian papilla in Pacific treefrogs does not match the frequency content of the advertisement calls (Straughan 1975; Stiebler and Narins 1990; Vélez and Sandoval 2024). Differences in critical ratios at frequencies encoded by the basilar papilla, especially those in the advertisement call, are negligible among soundscapes (Fig. 7). Together, these results suggest that differences in critical ratios may have little effect on communication in soundscapes with different levels of natural and anthropogenic noise in Pacific treefrogs. Furthermore, our results suggest that changes in critical ratios associated with increasing levels of noise may worsen the detection of low-frequency sounds at sites with high levels of ambient noise. Future studies should investigate the behavioral implications of higher critical ratios at low frequencies in already noisy environments.

In most species tested, critical ratio functions show a monotonic increase of about 3 dB per octave that parallels the change in auditory filter bandwidth with increases in center frequency (Fay 1988). Some species, like the greater horseshoe bat (Long 1977) and budgerigars (Dooling and Saunders 1975; Noirot et al. 2011), have critical ratio functions with dips at some frequencies that deviate from this general monotonic trend, suggesting that their auditory system has high spectral resolution (i.e., narrow auditory filters) or is highly efficient at extracting signals from noise at those frequencies. The ‘W’ shape of the critical ratio functions we report here (Fig. 7) parallels the critical ratio functions reported by Moss and Simmons (1986) for green treefrogs (Hyla cinerea) and is in line with previous suggestions that the auditory system of frogs is specialized for detecting signals in noisy settings (Ehret and Capranica 1980; Ehret and Gerhardt 1980; Narins 1982; Moss and Simmons 1986). Our results of within-species variation in critical ratios, however, suggest that frogs from soundscapes with high levels of ambient noise may not be better at extracting signals from noise than those from less noisy soundscapes.

Two inner ear organs processing airborne sounds in noise

We start this section by addressing two results not strictly related to our overarching research question, but worth discussing. First, our results show that, in the 25 dB/Hz noise condition, the average amount of masking reached negative values at frequencies primarily encoded by the amphibian papilla (Fig. 8a, b). That is, in the presence of noise at the lower spectrum level, masked thresholds were, on average, lower than absolute thresholds. Other behavioral and physiological studies in frogs have shown improvements in signal detection and discrimination in the presence of low levels of noise. For instance, some male coqui frogs (Eleutherodactylus coqui) responded more strongly to playbacks of synthetic calls when low-level noise was also broadcast (Narins 1982). Similarly, female spring peepers (Pseudacris crucifer) show improved discrimination of call frequencies in the presence of noise (Schwartz and Gerhardt 1998). These results are supported by physiological data showing that stochastic resonance may improve the function of the anuran peripheral and central auditory system in the presence of noise at low or moderate levels (Ratnam and Feng 1998; Bibikov 2002). Our results add to growing literature showing that low to moderate levels of noise may facilitate signal detection in frogs.

Our results also reveal a significant interaction between masker level and tone frequency (Table 4), with critical ratios about 4 dB higher at frequencies primarily encoded by the amphibian papilla when the masker spectrum level was 25 dB/Hz (Fig. 7a, b). This result is somewhat puzzling as critical ratios have been shown to be independent of masker level in previous studies in vertebrates (Fay 1988). However, a closer look at behavioral and physiological studies of masked thresholds and critical ratios in frogs suggest that these differences might be common across species. For example, in an inventive field study with male coqui frogs, Narins (1982) showed that ‘effective critical ratios’ at 1.0 kHz, a frequency encoded by the amphibian papilla in this species (Narins and Capranica 1976), changed by 9 dB between noise spectrum levels of about 34 and 55 dB/Hz. Similarly, critical ratios derived from phonotaxis experiments with female green treefrogs at 0.9 kHz varied by up to 7 dB between noise spectrum levels of approximately 33 and 45 dB/Hz (Ehret and Gerhardt 1980). Psychophysical experiments with green treefrogs revealed that critical ratios were, on average, 4 to 5 dB higher at 0.6 kHz when the masker level was 25 dB/Hz compared to when it was 35 dB/Hz (Moss and Simmons 1986). Furthermore, physiological measures of critical ratios in auditory nerve fibers of bullfrogs (Lithobates catesbeianus) are not independent of noise level (Freedman et al. 1988). Together, these results suggest that critical ratios in frogs, particularly at frequencies processed by the amphibian papilla, may not be independent of masker level over a wide range of noise levels. Given the interdependence of critical ratios and noise levels, models and predictions of the effects of anthropogenic noise on communication and hearing based on critical-ratio measurements are expected to be more limited for frogs than other vertebrate taxa.

In the context of urbanization and noise, our results reveal contrasting patterns of signal processing at frequencies primarily encoded by the amphibian and basilar papillae. With increasing levels of ambient noise, masked thresholds and critical ratios increased in the amphibian papilla and varied little in the basilar papilla. On the other hand, absolute thresholds increased, and the amount of masking decreased, in the basilar papilla, but varied little in the amphibian papilla.

The amphibian and the basilar papillae differ in their physiology and structural organization, and some properties of the anuran auditory system are shared with other vertebrates while others are unique to this group reviewed in (Smotherman and Narins 2000, 2004; Simmons et al. 2006). For instance, the amphibian papilla acts as a bank of narrow band filters and is tonotopically organized, while the basilar papilla acts as one broad filter. Tuning of the amphibian papilla involves both mechanical and electrical mechanisms, while tuning of the basilar papilla is predominantly mechanical (reviewed in Smotherman and Narins 2000). Furthermore, spontaneous otoacoustic emissions have only been measured in the frequency range of the amphibian papilla, suggesting that amplification of sounds at or near threshold may occur in the amphibian, but not the basilar, papilla (reviewed in Simmons et al. 2006). Thus, differences in unmasked and masked thresholds associated with increasing levels of ambient noise may be mediated by changes in mechanical properties of hair cells in the basilar papilla and mechanoelectrical properties in the amphibian papilla.

Physiological studies on masking patterns in the anuran auditory nerve reveal that critical ratios in the frequency range of the amphibian papilla increase with tone frequency and are generally lower than those in the range of the basilar papilla (Freedman et al. 1988). Moreover, neural critical ratios in auditory nerve fibers are related to fiber absolute thresholds; fibers with high thresholds also have higher critical ratios (Freedman et al. 1988). Similarly, rate-level functions for auditory nerve fibers in response to tones in noise exhibit a right-ward shift that is inversely proportional to the fiber’s absolute threshold, particularly for low frequency sounds (Narins 1987). This relationship between absolute thresholds and critical ratios in auditory nerve fibers aligns with our findings of increased absolute thresholds and critical ratios with increasing levels of ambient noise in the frequency region of the amphibian papilla, but not in the frequency region of the basilar papilla.

Upward shifts in absolute thresholds may result from hair-cell damage or loss due to exposure to high levels of noise (Ryals et al. 1999; Simmons et al. 2014; Breitzler et al. 2020). In frogs, however, hair cell damage and temporary shifts in hearing thresholds have only been documented after exposure to sounds with levels much higher than the ambient levels found at our field sites (Zelick and Narins 1985; Simmons et al. 2014). Therefore, the differences we see in absolute thresholds are probably not due to hair-cell damage or loss at our sites with high levels of ambient noise. Changes in hearing sensitivity can also be due to changes in hormonal profile (reviewed in Arch and Narins 2009; Gall et al. 2021). Importantly, a recent study in túngara frogs (Engystomops pustulosus) showed that hormone levels vary among urban and rural frogs, and exposure to urban light and noise differentially affects hormone release in urban and rural frogs (Smit et al. 2024). The extent to which changes in hormonal profiles due to increasing levels of human activity influence hearing is not known.

Limitations and concluding remarks

We asked whether properties of the vocal signals and the auditory system of Pacific treefrogs vary in ways that facilitate acoustic communication among sites with different levels of urbanization and noise. We show that, as the levels of urbanization and ambient noise increase, male advertisement calls are delivered at faster rates and have lower dominant frequencies, while hearing sensitivity and the amount of masking at frequencies important for communication decrease. Such changes in auditory processing are expected to increase the signal to noise ratio and facilitate communication in noisy settings. A causal relationship, however, cannot be established because our study is not manipulative. Accordingly, while our indices consider levels of urbanization and noise, and our spatial analyses suggest the potential for geographical confounds is low, we cannot rule out the influence of other potential confounding variables, such as artificial light at night or water chemistry. In addition, extremely restrictive access to some of our field sites, with only a few nights over the breeding seasons, yielded small sample sizes of females. Such low number of females did not allow us to investigate differences among the sexes. Nevertheless, we controlled for the effect of sex statistically, and we believe the impact of the low number of females on the statistical analyses is limited, as we find the same overall patterns regardless of whether sex is included or excluded from the models (Tables 4, S3). Future studies with larger samples of males and females should investigate potential sex differences in auditory processing along gradients of urbanization and noise. Moreover, studies targeting specific stages of the ascending auditory pathway are necessary, as our methods for obtaining (AEPs) and measuring hearing thresholds (visual detection based on the waveform and not on single peaks) do not allow us to pinpoint where in the auditory system the observed differences in hearing arise. Finally, manipulative and common-garden experiments integrating acoustics, physiology, behavior, and anatomy are necessary to determine the extent to which the differences in vocal signals and signal-processing mechanisms we report here ameliorate communication in urban and noisy environments, and whether these differences represent adaptations to urban soundscapes, developmentally plastic properties of the auditory system, or short-term effects of noise exposure.

Understanding how sensory pollutants influence sensory-processing mechanisms is important and necessary to predict how animals respond to human-altered environments. With two inner ear organs specialized to detect airborne sounds, anurans offer a unique opportunity to understand how different auditory systems may respond to soundscape changes driven by human activity. Peter Narins laid a strong foundation for this line of research, and his work will guide and inspire future generations of neuroethologists to further our understanding of anuran acoustic communication in changing environments, at proximate and ultimate levels of analysis.
