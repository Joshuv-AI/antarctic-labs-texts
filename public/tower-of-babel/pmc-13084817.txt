From class consciousness to class suicide: considering community integration as a primary care ethic
Authors: Bilal Irfan, Aayesha Soni, Wei Meng Vincent Chia, Suha Quasmi Khan, Roberto Daniel Sirvent
Published: 2026

Community integration, including the choice of where physicians live, is rarely treated as an explicit concern of medical ethics—yet who one interacts with, what institutions and civic services one participates in and residence are all sites where professional identity, class position and the effects of a primary care practice can intersect. There are two interrelated ideas worth considering here. One is class consciousness, that is, the explicit awareness of one’s own social position within existing hierarchies of income, prestige and professional power, and, in turn, how this positioning can shape one’s own clinical encounters, institutional norms and moral codes. In contrast to this, class suicide refers to a voluntary posture one takes in which individuals who occupy a relatively privileged class position (thus maintaining class consciousness) go about relinquishing some of the material, spatial or symbolic benefits that are associated with their professional practice of medicine and that often insulate them from some of the structural inequities existing in society.1 We situate this discussion primarily in the context of contemporary primary care training and practice in high-income settings (especially the USA), where residential segregation and maldistribution of clinicians are persistent concerns.24 Our core claim is not that physicians must always live where they serve, but that community integration should be treated as an explicit ethical and educational domain. That domain includes residential, relational and institutional forms of embeddedness. Within that broader domain, residential proximity is one possible and, sometimes, powerful strategy, but not always necessary, nor is it inherently always sufficient on its own. We seek to posit a pedagogical claim, where physicians and trainees are made more aware of the ethical underpinnings of community integration (including the choice of residence) and how medical education can examine its successes, pitfalls and alternatives—rather than proscribing a universal mandate.

It is important to note that early motivations for entering the field of medicine may blend notions of prestige, favourable wages and job security with other altruistic and intrinsic commitments, interest in science or family expectations, and can vary across different contexts.59 These motivations and drivers to pursue a career in medicine can also be impacted by the opportunity structures in place and the setting, with substantial exposure to healthcare and access to mentors and internships being able to potentially strengthen intrinsic motivations, while the broader socioeconomic and cultural context being able to drive other motivating factors.1018 Understanding such motivations is relevant to considering how a profession’s workforce evolves that may later serve to shape specialty selection, choice of workplace and, ultimately, their residence in relation to it. The orientation towards prestige accumulation at the point of entry invites further inquiry into how such motivations evolve and sit uncomfortably in relation to professed commitments to underserved populations.19 20 The ‘lower’ prestige sometimes afforded to primary care does not necessarily insulate it from these dynamics; in fact, it could even intensify it by driving compensation-seeking behaviours that may widen the sociogeographic distance between clinicians and underserved (and likely low paying) neighbourhoods.21 22 Clinicians may be placed in incentive-related environments that can encourage revenue-protective decisions for practices (eg, payer-mix optimisation and location choices tied to reimbursement stability).2331 For example, US primary care practices with no Medicaid revenue are more likely to be located in higher income areas, which suggests that market and reimbursement incentives can actually shape where primary care capacity is built.32

Industry transfers to US physicians and hospitals in 2014 stood at approximately US$6.5 billion.33 In 2015, 48% of US physicians reported receiving US$2.4 billion in industry-related payments.34 From 2013 to 2022, over 85 million payments valuing over US$12.13 billion were made from industry to eligible physicians despite a decade of open payments transparency, which also impacts physician prescribing patterns.35 36 There is little evidence that patients routinely consult the database when selecting physicians. This persistent increase, coupled with limited patient scrutiny, suggests that disclosure alone has not restrained financially shaped professional self-interest. These dynamics contribute to the devaluation of primary care, where recruitment difficulties and reported ‘specialty disrespect’ have been documented, especially for family medicine trainees who absorb disparaging comparisons with higher paid procedural fields.37 38 Precisely because primary care physicians are structurally positioned closer to the everyday constraints that shape illness trajectories, they may be best situated to interrogate how their own residential choices either reproduce or resist material distance from low-income patients. This is precisely why the clinician’s geographic residence can be discussed as a part of one’s educational outlook, not as a hard rule to necessarily follow, but as an anchor for deliberative discussion that trainees are taught to notice. Residential distance may be best understood as one visible symptom of a broader problem of integration into community, that is, the degree to which primary care is organised around daily embeddedness and accountability to the communities most affected by structural determinants of health.

The stakes of these residential choices, and often relatedly the area of one’s clinical practice, can at times lead to pronounced segregation on socioeconomic lines (ie, physicians located in distinctly different and often further away neighbourhoods than the areas they primarily serve) and can lead to shortages of primary care distribution in key areas.39 For example, in the USA, tens of millions of people live in areas that are underserved by primary care, with only ~48% of needs met—requiring thousands of primary care practitioners to fill this gap.40 Although a lot of data pertaining to workforce captures the location of a physician’s practice, rather than where they live, ZIP-code-level analyses of primary care availability (per the American Medical Association) to census-informed data showcase that neighbourhoods that are classified as ‘Black or African–American’ in terms of ethnic/racial grouping have higher odds of being areas suffering from primary care shortage.41 This pattern can also appear as residential segregation increases across certain metropolitan areas.41

The moral vocabulary urging clinician engagement with disadvantaged communities often emphasises being ‘in touch’, cultivating cultural humility or episodic community visits.42 43 Yet evidence suggests intermittent contact, while valuable, may not close the social distance produced by residential segregation—a spatial pattern that erodes cross-class trust, fragments moral community and weakens solidaristic dispositions vital for addressing structural determinants of health.44 45 Short primary care visits (median ~18–19 min in a large US sample) are linked to higher rates of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing and opioid–benzodiazepine coprescribing.46 Modestly longer appointments, without deeper relationships beyond the clinic, may still fail to apprehend the cumulative burdens of food insecurity, precarious employment or substandard housing. Simulated pedagogies, such as poverty simulations, may temporarily shift attitudes and empathy,4750 but they cannot necessarily substitute for sustained relationships steeped in accountability and rapport with communities over time. Living in the same neighbourhoods as patients has the potential to reconfigure asymmetries of information and authority by embedding physicians in the similar school closures, transit delays, environmental exposures and policing patterns that contour their patients’ physiology and health-seeking behaviour. Residential proximity is one form of spatial integration, whose ethical value can depend on how it interacts with institutional forms. It situates primary care physicians within a broader repertoire of integrative practices that cultivate epistemic humility, accountability and sustained exposure to community conditions beyond episodic clinical encounters. However, geographic proximity does not automatically entail having the same shared lived experience as physicians may still retain some buffers for their income, mobility and institutional power that can change how adversity is actually experienced and navigated. After all, a specific choice of residence alone may not always erase the class boundaries that exist or produce identical challenges (which is not the goal), as clinicians typically retain some sort of income security, mobility and reserve of resources. They may very well also not face the same challenges of food insecurity, precarious employment or substandard housing and may be able to overcome challenges like environmental exposures and school closures easier than others. Residential proximity may, therefore, not be the end-all answer, or sufficient for that matter, in understanding patients’ lives—it can be viewed as one possible strategy within a broader continuum and framework of integrating with communities. The relevant educational aim may be a continuum of community integration practices that make structural determinants of health legible in daily clinical reasoning. Practical options can also deepen one’s lived understanding without necessarily requiring relocation and can include structured home-visit curricula, neighbourhood walking tour pedagogies to map local constraints and resources and longitudinal partnerships with reciprocal governance and feedback loops.51

Drawing on Amilcar Cabral’s notion of ‘class suicide’, we propose reframing community integration, including but not limited to the residential decision of a primary care physician serving low-income populations, as a site of voluntary partial relinquishment of professional class insulation in order to align daily life with the material realities that generate clinical caseloads.52 Class suicide in this context does not romanticise deprivation nor mandate a universal obligation that ignores debt burdens, caregiving responsibilities or safety concerns. Instead, it names a deliberate ethical stance that resists the centrifugal professional forces pulling clinicians towards enclaves structurally protected from the very inequities they document in charts. Living locally can expand moral perception, shifting structural determinants from abstract social history entries to actionable targets for advocacy and increasing the probability of solidarity when hospital support staff strike or tenants face eviction. Residential proximity can reframe counsel: nutritional advice that recognises actual food retail geography, physical activity prescriptions that account for local risks and adherence strategies that address firsthand transport instability. Residential proximity can deepen the perception of local constraints, but similar integrative gains could also be pursued through sustained civic participation, shared governance structures with community partners and long-term institutional commitments that redistribute agenda-setting power. The knowledge one develops and the time one spends, be it through residential, relational or institutional engagement, can be beneficial. For many clinicians, relocation may be difficult due to caregiving duties, spouse’s employment, schooling, certain resources for disability needs, immigration or legal realities, among other factors. To simply moralise, the choice of residence amid so much workforce shortage risks becoming counterproductive. A question at hand is dissecting what forms of situated presence are realistically available, be they joining local institutions (eg, schools, civic boards and tenant/worker health coalitions), building long-term community partnerships and advocating system-level supportive infrastructure (ie, safe transit, affordable housing and protected time off) that can keep clinicians involved in their local community needs.

There are other factors to consider when assessing the dynamic and relationship between primary care physicians and patients.53 Community integration can also be strengthened through models that make the clinic a sustained civic institution rather than a transient service site. A specific, intentional community-oriented primary care may serve to aid in encouraging a preventative and risk-reducing approach to health.5456 Furthermore, shared social groups and concordance in demographic backgrounds between clinicians and patients may also serve to improve trust and health-seeking behaviours among the latter.57 58

Therefore, we advocate for an exemplarist, not moralising, ethic, that is, one that highlights the multiple credible forms of community integration rather than treating relocation as the defining test of solidarity.59 Discussing class suicide may be less a demand for some sort of heroism, but rather a teachable moment for trainees to consider choices, be they residential, institutional commitments or personal advocacy, that can narrow the class distance. The key pedagogical question is less whether every primary care physician must relocate and more how medical education can highlight the narratives of clinicians who have integrated residence, practice and advocacy in pursuit of health justice. Exemplarist ethics begin with admiration for real moral exemplars whose lives recalibrate what trainees see as normal. In Cuba’s Family Doctor-and-Nurse programme, or consultorios, healthcare workers sometimes literally lived above offices in an attempt to make the clinical provider a civic neighbour rather than a commuting outsider.60 61 People may be a physician, a researcher and a neighbour all at once.62

Such exemplars can also complicate simplistic pipeline solutions that socioeconomic origins alone guarantee future solidarity, as upwards mobility may reduce sensitivity to persistent poverty.63 By curating and critically analysing the exemplars of class suicide in primary care, curricula can nourish a professional imagination that regards proximity not as a performative virtue but as a credible avenue for sustaining empathy, mitigating burnout through shared civic purpose and reanimating the countercultural roots of primary care as a movement for the common good. Community integration, residential proximity where feasible, but also sustained relational and institutional embeddedness, will not, on its own, dismantle structural inequity. However, ignoring community integration, including one’s choice of residence, as an ethical variable risks conceding an unexamined domain to the logics of careerism and segregation. Reclaiming it as a deliberative choice advances a thicker ethic of solidarity in primary care, one that binds clinical competence to situated presence and reorients professional identity towards the shared vulnerabilities that generate authentic trust.
