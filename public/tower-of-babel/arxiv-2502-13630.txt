New Quantum Algorithm For Solving Linear System of Equations
Nhat A. Nghiem

Abstract
Linear equations play a pivotal role in many areas of science and engineering, making efficient solutions to linear systems highly desirable. The development of quantum algorithms for solving linear systems has been a significant breakthrough, with such algorithms rapidly becoming some of the most influential in quantum computing. Subsequent advances have focused on improving the efficiency of quantum linear solvers and extending their core techniques to address various challenges, such as least-squares data fitting. In this article, we introduce a new quantum algorithm for solving linear systems based on the gradient descent method. Inspired by the vector/density state formalism, we represent a point, or vector, as a density state-like entity, enabling us to leverage recent advancements in quantum algorithmic frameworks, such as block encoding, to directly manipulate operators and carry out the gradient descent method in a quantum setting. The operator corresponding to the intermediate solution is updated iteratively, with a provable guarantee of convergence. The quantum state representing the final solution to the linear system can then be extracted by further measurement. We provide a detailed complexity analysis and compare our approach with existing methods, demonstrating significant improvement in certain aspects.



I
Introduction

Quantum computing holds great promise in solving problems that are beyond the reach of classical computers. Indeed, numerous examples across various domains have demonstrated quantum advantages. Grover’s algorithm
grover1996fast
achieves a quadratic speed-up in searching an unstructured database. Shor’s algorithm
shor1999polynomial
, along with recent improvements
regev2023efficient
, exhibits a superpolynomial speed-up in integer factorization. Deutsch’s algorithm
deutsch1985quantum
;
deutsch1992rapid
introduced a black-box computation model, demonstrating that a quantum computer can determine a black-box’s properties with minimal queries. In a similar vein, Jordan
jordan2005fast
showed that a single evaluation of a black-box function suffices to estimate its gradient. Other works
aharonov2006polynomial
;
lloyd2016quantum
;
ubaru2021quantum
;
nghiem2023constant
;
nghiem2023quantum
have explored various quantum speed-ups for computational problems in algebraic topology. In a more physically motivated setting, a series of studies have demonstrated quantum computers’ ability to efficiently simulate a variety of quantum systems
lloyd1996universal
;
berry2007efficient
;
berry2012black
;
berry2014high
;
berry2015hamiltonian
;
childs2010relationship
;
childs2018toward
;
gerritsma2010quantum
;
berry2020time
;
chen2021quantum
;
an2022time
;
low2017optimal
;
low2019hamiltonian
.

Among the many quantum algorithms that have been proposed—and many more that will certainly emerge—the Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) algorithm
harrow2009quantum
for solving linear systems stands out as one of the most influential. Beyond merely providing a quantum state corresponding to the solution of a linear system, the authors of
harrow2009quantum
demonstrated that solving linear systems is
BQP
-complete. This implies that, under widely accepted complexity-theoretic assumptions, classical computers cannot achieve a fundamentally faster solution. As shown in
harrow2009quantum
, the ability to solve linear systems in logarithmic time (with respect to problem dimension) opens up exciting practical applications for quantum computing, as linear systems are ubiquitous in science and engineering. The techniques introduced in the HHL algorithm have also inspired other quantum algorithms, such as quantum support vector machines
rebentrost2014quantum
and quantum data fitting algorithms
wiebe2012quantum
.

Building on the success of the HHL algorithm, several attempts
clader2013preconditioned
;
childs2017quantum
;
kerenidis2020quantum
;
huang2019near
;
bravo2023variational
;
wossnig2018quantum
;
subacsi2019quantum
have sought to improve it in various ways. The HHL algorithm relies on key subroutines, including Hamiltonian simulation techniques
berry2007efficient
;
berry2012black
and quantum phase estimation
kitaev1995quantum
;
abrams1999quantum
. However, as noted in
childs2017quantum
, quantum phase estimation leads to a linear dependence on the inverse of the error tolerance, making further optimizations necessary. To address this, the authors of
childs2017quantum
employed alternative approximation techniques—namely, Fourier and Chebyshev approximations—resulting in a quantum linear solver with polylogarithmic scaling in reciprocal error. The work in
clader2013preconditioned
tackled the issue of high condition numbers in real-world linear systems by incorporating a preconditioning technique, which reformulates the system into an equivalent one with a provably bounded condition number while maintaining the same solution. Ref.
subacsi2019quantum
introduced a quantum linear solver based on adiabatic quantum computing, achieving the same complexity as HHL. Meanwhile, Refs.
wossnig2018quantum
;
kerenidis2020quantum
developed quantum linear solvers leveraging the augmented quantum random access memory model, targeting dense linear systems and achieving a quadratic speed-up over both HHL and
childs2017quantum
in the same setting. Additionally,
bravo2023variational
;
huang2019near
proposed quantum linear solvers tailored for near-term quantum devices, bypassing the high-depth requirements of other approaches such as
harrow2009quantum
;
childs2017quantum
.

In this work, we introduce a new quantum algorithm for solving linear systems. Our input model is similar to that of the HHL algorithm, in which oracle access to the matrix entries is provided. Our approach reformulates the problem as an optimization task, which is then solved using gradient descent—a strategy explored in
huang2019near
;
kerenidis2020quantum
. However,
huang2019near
employs a variational method, which is more heuristic, while
kerenidis2020quantum
assumes a different input model based on augmented quantum random access memory. We show that, by defining an appropriate cost function, its gradient can be computed analytically. Furthermore, instead of representing a point in Hilbert space with a state vector (e.g.,
\mathbf{x}
), we represent it using a density matrix-like operator
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}
. This alternative representation enables us to leverage the recently introduced quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) framework
gilyen2019quantum
to perform gradient descent on
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}
. Although this representation deviates from the conventional notion of a state vector, we demonstrate that the quantum state corresponding to the solution of the optimization problem, and thus of the linear system, can still be recovered from the density operator representation. Thus, beyond providing a novel quantum approach for solving linear systems, our work also highlights the versatility of QSVT, adding to its growing list of applications
gilyen2019distributional
;
chakraborty2018power
. We note that while
gilyen2019quantum
also proposed using QSVT to solve linear systems, their approach relies directly on polynomial approximations, making it technically distinct from ours.

Our work is organized as follows. In the next Section
II
, we provide an overview, including the main objective, key input assumptions, summary of prior results and preliminaries for our method. Section
III
is devoted to outline our main proposal, which is a new quantum algorithm for solving linear system. A detailed analysis of algorithm, including error and time complexity is provided Section
IV
. Conclusion and outlook is left for Section
V
.

III
New Quantum Algorithm for Solving Linear Equations

Recall that the main objective is to find
x
such that
A\textbf{x}=\textbf{b}
, where
A
is a Hermitian
n\times n
matrix with sparsity
s
, and
b
is a unit vector (assumed without loss of generalization). For simplicity, we work in the real regime. An additional assumption is that the magnitude of the eigenvalues of
A
is between
(1/\kappa,1)
. As seen in the previous section, the direct solution is
\textbf{x}=A^{-1}\textbf{b}
. An alternative, indirect solution to solving linear system is given by:

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{x}}\frac{1}{2}||\textbf{x}||^{2}+\frac{1}{2}||A\textbf{x}-\textbf{b}||^{2}

(6)

where
||.||
refers to
l_{2}
Euclidean norm. Defining
f(\textbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}||\textbf{x}||^{2}+\frac{1}{2}||A\textbf{x}-\textbf{b}||^{2}
, a further expansion yields:

\displaystyle f(\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}||\textbf{x}||^{2}\frac{1}{2}\left(||A\textbf{x}||^{2}-\bra{\textbf{b}}A\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}+||\textbf{b}||^{2}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}||\textbf{x}||^{2}+\frac{1}{2}||A\textbf{x}||^{2}-\bra{\textbf{b}}A\textbf{x}+\frac{1}{2}

(8)

To find
x
so that
f(\textbf{x})
is minimized, a standard/textbook method is gradient descent. The algorithm proceeds by first randomizing a point
\textbf{x}_{0}
, then iterate the following procedure:

\displaystyle\textbf{x}_{t+1}=\textbf{x}_{t}-\eta\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x}_{t})

(9)

where
\textbf{x}_{t}
refers to the solution at
t
-th iteration, and
\eta
is the hyperparameter. In general, the total number of iterations
T
is typically user-dependent, and the convergence of the gradient descent algorithm depends on the behavior of the function. It turns out that the function
f(\textbf{x})
above is strongly convex, which means that the convergence of gradient descent algorithm is guaranteed. In particular, Ref.
nesterov2013introductory
;
nesterov1983method
;
boyd2004convex
;
garrigos2023handbook
proved that choosing
T=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{1}{\eta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\big)
suffices to secure that
x_{T}
is
\delta
-close to the true solution.

We remark that a crucial part of gradient descent method is the evaluation of the given function’s gradient
\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})
. In theory, if one can compute the function
f(\textbf{x})\equiv f(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})
at any point within the working domain, then the partial derivative of
f
, for example, with respect to
x_{1}
, can be numerically computed as:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})}{\partial x_{1}}\approx\frac{f(x_{1}+\delta,x_{2},...,x_{n})-f(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})}{\delta}

(10)

Similarly, the partial derivative with respect to remaining variables can be numerically computed. So, the gradient
\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})=\Big(\frac{\partial f(\textbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}},\frac{\partial f(\textbf{x})}{\partial x_{2}},...,\frac{\partial f(\textbf{x})}{\partial x_{n}}\Big)^{T}
can be constructed. We note that the first-order derivative approximation formula provided above is the simplest one, and there are more complicated yet more accurate ones. In principle, given oracle access to
A
, a means to prepare
\ket{\textbf{b}}
and suppose that we also have a means to prepare
x
, the function
f(\textbf{x})
can be estimated. A concrete procedure will be provided in Appendix
B
. Here, we point out that if we use such an approach to estimate the function
f
at some
x
plus nearby points
\textbf{x}+\vec{\delta}
, and use that information to estimate the entries of
\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})
, then constructing the state
\sim\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})
can be done by amplitude encoding
grover2000synthesis
;
grover2002creating
;
plesch2011quantum
;
schuld2018supervised
;
nakaji2022approximate
;
marin2023quantum
;
zoufal2019quantum
;
zhang2022quantum
. However, as the vector
\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})
is not sparse, it can be costly, hence prohibiting potential speedup in dimension
n
. Additionally, the error from estimation of the gradient’ entries accumulates, resulting in an overall error of order
n
in the gradient
\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})
.

It turns out that the function
f(\textbf{x})
admits an analytical form for the gradient as following:

\displaystyle\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=\textbf{x}+\big(A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}

(11)

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}

(12)

We remark that a technical aspect that distinguishes our work to existing ones, such as
harrow2009quantum
;
childs2017quantum
;
kerenidis2020quantum
;
huang2019near
;
subacsi2019quantum
, is that instead of vector/state representation, we use density operator representation. More concretely, instead of
x
, we use
\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{\dagger}
as a main working object. This choice allows us to leverage the block-encoding and related techniques mentioned in the previous section. Subsequently, we will show that from density operator, we can recover the quantum state corresponding to the solution of the linear systems.

In the new representation, the gradient descent updating step is:

\displaystyle\Big(\textbf{x}-\eta\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})\Big)\Big(\textbf{x}-\eta\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})\Big)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{\dagger}-\eta\textbf{x}\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})^{\dagger}-\eta\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})\textbf{x}^{\dagger}+\eta^{2}\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})\bigtriangledown f^{\dagger}(\textbf{x})

(13)

\displaystyle=\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{\dagger}-\eta\textbf{x}\Big(\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\Big)^{\dagger}-\eta\Big(\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\Big)\textbf{x}^{\dagger}

(14)

\displaystyle+\eta^{2}\Big(\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\Big)\Big(\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\Big)^{\dagger}

(15)

We expand term by term as follows.

\displaystyle\eta\textbf{x}\Big(\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\Big)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\eta\left(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{\dagger}\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)-\textbf{x}\bra{\textbf{b}}A\right)

(17)

\displaystyle\eta\Big(\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\Big)\textbf{x}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\eta\left(\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{\dagger}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\textbf{x}^{\dagger}\right)

(18)

\displaystyle\eta^{2}\Big(\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\Big)\Big(\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\Big)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}\Big(\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{\dagger}\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)-\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)\textbf{x}\bra{\textbf{b}}A

(19)

\displaystyle-A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\textbf{x}^{\dagger}\big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\big)+A^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}\bra{\textbf{b}}A\Big)

(20)

Our algorithm makes use of the following:

(1) Suppose there is a unitary
U_{b}
that generates
\ket{\textbf{b}}
, then Lemma
1
allows us to block encode the operator
\ket{\textbf{b}}\bra{\textbf{b}}

(2) Oracle access to entries of
A
can be combined with Lemma
4
to construct an
\epsilon
-closed block encoding of
A/s
, denoted as
U_{A}
. Then
U_{A}^{\dagger}
is the block encoding of
A^{\dagger}/s
. Use Lemma
2
to construct the block encoding of
\frac{1}{s^{2}}A^{\dagger}A
. Given that the block encoding of
\mathbb{I}
is trivial (see discussion below Definition
1
), Lemma
5
can be used to construct the block encoding of
\frac{1}{2s^{2}}\Big(\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A\Big)
.

(3) Suppose for now that we have a block encoding of operator
\alpha\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{\dagger}
(where
\alpha
is some factor). Then the inner product
\alpha\textbf{x}^{\dagger}\ket{\textbf{b}}
(including sign and magnitude) can be estimated to a known precision. A concrete procedure is provided in Appendix
C
.

Quantum Algorithm For Solving Linear Systems of Equations.
We proceed to describe our new proposal for solving linear systems of equations as follows. The aim is to construct the block encoding of operators in Eqn.
32
. First, we use a random unitary
U_{0}
to generate an arbitrary state
\ket{\phi}
(of dimension
>n
) that contains
\textbf{x}_{0}
as its first
n
entries. Using Lemma
1
, we can construct a block encoding of
\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}
. The top-left corner of such a matrix is
\textbf{x}_{0}\textbf{x}_{0}^{\dagger}
. According to Def.
1
, it is also a block encoding of
\textbf{x}_{0}\textbf{x}_{0}^{\dagger}

V
Conclusion

We have successfully developed a new quantum algorithm for solving linear systems of equations. Rather than directly inverting the given matrix, we reformulate the problem as an optimization task, providing an alternative pathway to the solution—specifically, via gradient descent. A key technical distinction of our approach is the mapping of a state vector
x
to a density operator-like representation
\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{\dagger}
. With an appropriately chosen cost function, we derive an analytical expression for the gradient, enabling a straightforward implementation of the gradient descent algorithm to iteratively update the density-operator representation.
We conduct a careful analysis of error accumulation across iterations and the overall time complexity of our framework, revealing that our approach achieves
\kappa
-free scaling, which is an unexpected result. As a trade-off, our method exhibits nearly linear scaling with respect to error tolerance, which appears difficult to improve due to the exponential dependence on T, the total number of iterations. The primary motivation for adopting a density-operator-like representation instead of the conventional vector state representation is to harness a broader range of tools from the quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) framework. Interestingly, we do not rely on any advanced techniques from QSVT but instead utilize only its fundamental definitions and basic block encoding operations. This work thus serves as another compelling example of how QSVT can enhance quantum algorithms, highlighting its vast potential for advancing quantum computing.

Appendix A
Strong convexity of
f(\textbf{x})

In this section we elaborate further the strong convex property of:

\displaystyle f(\textbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}||\textbf{x}||^{2}+\frac{1}{2}||A\textbf{x}||^{2}-\bra{\textbf{b}}A\textbf{x}+\frac{1}{2}

(85)

We first recall the following definition. A function
f(\textbf{x}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex if:

\bullet
For all
\textbf{x},\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

f(\textbf{x})\geq f(\textbf{y})+\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{y})^{T}(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y})+\frac{\mu}{2}||\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}||^{2}

\bullet
For all
\textbf{x},\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the gradient of
f(\textbf{x})
is Lipschitz with constant
L
:

||\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})-\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{y})||\leq L||\textbf{x}-\textbf{y}||

It turns out that the above conditions are equivalent to Hessian of
f(\textbf{x})
is bounded from below and above, respectively
nesterov1983method
;
nesterov2013introductory
;
boyd2004convex
. The gradient of function
f(\textbf{x})
is
\bigtriangledown f(\textbf{x})=\textbf{x}+A^{\dagger}(A\textbf{x}-b)
. The Hessian is
\bigtriangledown^{2}f(\textbf{x})=\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A
. It is apparent that:

\displaystyle||\bigtriangledown^{2}f(\textbf{x})||

\displaystyle=||\mathbb{I}+A^{\dagger}A||

(86)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\lambda_{\max}(A^{\dagger}A)

(87)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sigma^{2}_{\max}(A)

(88)

where
\lambda_{\max},\sigma_{\max}
refers to maximum eigenvalue of
A^{\dagger}A
and singular value of
A
, respectively. Thus, the Hessian of
f(\textbf{x})
is bounded from above, so it is
L
-smooth with
L=1+\sigma_{\max}^{2}(A)
. To show that the Hessian is
\mu
-strongly convex, we need to show that it is bounded from below. It is straightforward to see that:

\displaystyle||\bigtriangledown^{2}f(\textbf{x})||

\displaystyle\geq 1+\lambda_{\min}(A^{\dagger}A)

(89)

\displaystyle\geq 1+\sigma_{\min}^{2}(A)

(90)

Thus,
f(\textbf{x})
is
\mu
-strongly convex with
\mu=1+\sigma_{\min}^{2}(A)
