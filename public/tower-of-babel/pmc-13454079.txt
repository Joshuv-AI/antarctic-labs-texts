Association between serum chemerin and breast cancer mediated by lipid metabolism
Authors: Hui Li, Qunying Xu, Mingquan Huang, Jing Shu, Siyi Chen, Jia Xia, Ya Liu, Yunli Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between chemerin and breast cancer is unclear. We aimed to clarify the association between serum chemerin levels and breast cancer risk in Chinese women, as well as the effects of lipid metabolism on this association.MethodsA 1:1 matched case–control study of 160 pairs was conducted using questionnaires to collect data on breast cancer risk factors. Serum chemerin levels were determined. Conditional logistic regression was used to analyze their association with breast cancer risk. A subgroup analysis of menopausal status was performed using unconditional logistic regression. The non-linear dose–response relationship was analyzed using restricted cubic splines. The effect of blood lipids on chemerin and breast cancer were explored using a mediating effect model.ResultsSerum chemerin levels of breast cancer patients were higher than those of controls in the overall group and menopausal subgroup. Taking the median of the control group as the cutoff value, women with higher chemerin levels had an increased odds of breast cancer compared to that of those with lower chemerin levels (for overall group: OR = 2.217, 95%CI: 1.125–4.367; for pre-menopausal group: OR = 4.713, 95%CI: 1.454–15.276; for post-menopausal group: OR = 2.103, 95%CI: 1.123–3.936). A linear dose–response relationship between serum chemerin levels and breast cancer odds was identified. The model showed that triglyceride (TG) statistically accounted for part of the association between chemerin and breast cancer (b = 0.034, P = 0.003), explaining approximately 15.0% of the total association.ConclusionsThere is a positive association between chemerin and breast cancer, which may be partly mediated by TG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05350-0.Keywords: Breast cancer, Case–control, Chemerin, Adipokine, Triglyceride

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women worldwide. According to GLOBOCAN 2020, there were 2.3 million new cases of breast cancer worldwide [1]. In China, the incidence of breast cancer has rapidly increased in the past 20 years, particularly among the younger generations [2, 3].

Additionally, obesity is a serious public health problem worldwide that is closely associated with breast cancer, particularly in post-menopausal women [4, 5]. The biological mechanisms of obesity in breast cancer are complex and include changes in the body hormone system, chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, and oxidative stress caused by obesity [6]. Furthermore, as a metabolically dynamic secretory organ, the white adipose tissue secretes functional heterogeneous adipokines that regulate several physiological and pathological pathways, such as insulin sensitivity, appetite, inflammation, and angiogenesis [7]. Classical adipokines, such as leptin and adiponectin, are key in the genesis and progression of breast cancer [8].

Chemerin is a novel adipocytokine first identified in 1997 that is encoded by the RARRES2 gene [9]. Chemerin is abundant in the liver, adipose tissues, and placenta [9]. Chemerin has multiple biological functions, including the recruitment of innate immune defenses [10]. It is also involved in inflammation, acting as a pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory mediator depending on the specific tissue environment and inflammatory conditions [10, 11].

The association between chemerin and cancer has long been considered; however, its role remains controversial [12]. A prospective study reported that higher levels of chemerin are associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer, an obesity-related tumor [13]. However, studies on chemerin and breast cancer are limited. A study by Song et al. [14] is the only case–control study examining the association between serum chemerin concentrations and breast cancer, reporting higher chemerin levels in breast cancer patients than that in healthy women. However, the study did not adjust for potential confounders.

Chemerin is a multifaceted adipokine that is crucial in adipocyte differentiation [8, 15] and regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism by regulating the expression of enzymes [10, 16, 17]. In vivo animal studies have shown that exogenous chemerin treatment increases the levels of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) in mice [16]. Conversely, both adipose tissue-specific and global knockout of the RARRES2 gene significantly reduced these serum lipid levels in mice fed a high-fat diet [16–18]. Current epidemiological studies have consistently identified a positive correlation between blood TG and TC levels and serum chemerin levels [19].

Preclinical studies have shown that hyperlipidemia increases the occurrence, growth, and metastasis rates of breast tumors in mice [20–22]. Epidemiological studies have reported a significant association between blood lipid levels and breast cancer risk; however, these findings are inconsistent. Manjer et al. found that among women who quit smoking, those with high baseline TG levels had a significantly increased risk of breast cancer [23]. A large prospective study conducted in South Korea demonstrated that increased serum TC levels are significantly and positively associated with breast cancer risk in women [24].

Therefore, this case–control study aimed to evaluate the association between serum chemerin levels and breast cancer risk while controlling for important confounders. We further explored the mediating effect of lipid metabolism on the relationship between chemerin and breast cancer, which to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously examined. The findings of this study provide new insights into the mechanisms by which obesity contributes to breast cancer and identify potential biomarkers for obesity-related breast cancer.

Materials and methods

Selection of participants

This 1:1 matched case–control study was conducted between January and October 2024. New patients who were pathologically diagnosed with primary breast cancer were recruited from the Department of Breast Surgery at the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University. Patients with other tumors, a history of ovariectomy, hysterectomy, or mastectomy, and any breast cancer treatment before blood draw were excluded from the study. For each breast cancer case, one control was selected from the women who participated in physical examination or visited other departments (i.e., bone and joint surgery, endoscopy, and ophthalmology) in the same hospital. Controls were matched to cases on age (± 3 years). Women with a history of cancer, ovariectomy, hysterectomy, or mastectomy, or pathological diagnosis of breast dysplasia were excluded from this study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Clinical Trials at the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (number: KY2023423). Informed consent was obtained from all the participants. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations.

Sample size

In this study, the estimated odds ratio (OR) was set as 2.0, the estimated exposure rate among controls (P0) was set as 0.25, and the significance level (α) and statistical power (1-β) were set at 0.05 and 0.8 respectively. The sample size was calculated using the following formula.

The minimum target sample size for this study was 152 pairs. The study included 160 patients and 160 matched controls.

Information on breast cancer risk factors

The questionnaire was designed to obtain information on demographic factors (age, marital status, and education level), lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol consumption), menstrual and reproductive factors (menopausal status, age at menarche, menopausal age, number of live births, age at first live birth, number of abortions, and cumulative breastfeeding duration), usage of exogenous hormones (contraceptive use and hormonal replacement therapy [HRT]), family history of cancer, and personal disease history (benign breast disease, benign uterus or ovary disease, and other systematic diseases). Women who reported no experience of menarche in the past year or who were older than 55 years were considered post-menopausal. A trained health worker measured the height and weight of the participants. Height was measured to the nearest centimeter and weight to the nearest kilogram in light clothing without shoes. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the ratio of weight to height squared (kg/m2).

Laboratory tests

From each study participant, 2 mL of peripheral blood was collected after she had fasted for at least 8 h. The blood sample was centrifuged for 5 min at 1000 g RCF to separate the serum, which was immediately stored at -80 ℃. TG, TC, LDL-C, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels were enzymatically measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter, AU680) at the Medical Experimental Center of Southwest Medical University. Chemerin levels were measured in the serum samples obtained from the laboratory of the Public Experimental Technology Center of Southwest Medical University. An enzyme-linked immunoabsorbent assay (ELISA) kit provided by Elabscience Biotechnology China (catalog number: E-EL-H0698;detection range༚0.16-10 ng/mL) was used to determine the chemerin concentration according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples from cases and matched controls were analyzed in the same analytical batch. The blood samples were labeled only with numbers and randomly ordered within each case–control pair. Masked split specimens included in each batch were used to calculate the coefficient of variation (CV) within and between batches, which was less than 10%.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables that were not normally distributed were described as median (IQR). Quantitative variables are described as frequencies and proportions.

Univariate conditional logistic regression was used to assess the distribution differences in demographic and breast cancer risk factors between cases and controls. The serum concentration of chemerin was compared between cases and controls using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The association between serum chemerin levels and breast cancer risk was assessed using conditional logistic regression. In the models, chemerin concentration was divided into two categories according to the median of the control group. Both the univariate and multivariate models were fitted. In multivariable models, adjustments were made not only for demographic and breast cancer risk factors that were significantly unbalanced between case and control groups, but also for age and BMI, as these two factors were considered strong confounders in the association between chemerin levels and breast cancer risk.

Additionally, a subgroup analysis of the menopausal status was conducted. As some pairs had different menopausal statuses, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to explore the intergroup differences in chemerin levels between cases and controls. Unconditional logistic regression was applied to assess the distribution difference of demographic and breast cancer risk factors between cases and controls, as well as the association between serum chemerin levels and breast cancer risk in pre- or post-menopausal women.

We further used a restricted cubic spline (RCS) model to explore the dose-effect non-linear relationship between serum chemerin levels and breast cancer risk. The variables incorporated into the models were aligned with those in the multiple logistic regression models described above. Initially, 3–5 knots were used to fit a smooth curve. According to the lowest Akaike information criterion (AIC), the number of knots used to fit the smoothing spline is determined based on the lowest Akaike information criterion. The solid lines in the figures represent ORs and the shaded areas represent 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Correlations between chemerin levels and blood lipid indices among controls were tested using the Spearman correlation analysis. The Wilcoxon signed-rank sum test and paired t test was used to compare lipid indices between cases and controls. Based on the control group, the factors associated with blood lipids were identified using the Spearman correlation analysis, Mann–Whitney U test, Kruskal–Wallis test, or t test, which were adjusted when the association between serum chemerin and blood lipids was evaluated using the mediation effect model.

A mediating effect model was used to evaluate the effects of blood lipid levels on the relationship between chemerin and breast cancer. A hypothesis of action paths among the key variables was constructed and a bootstrapping approach with 5,000 resamples was applied to estimate the 95% CIs of the path parameters. If the 95% CI did not include zero, the corresponding effects were considered statistically significant.

Considering that metabolic, cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, endocrine, immune system, liver, and infectious diseases may affect chemerin or lipid levels, we conducted a sensitivity analysis based on the pairs without the diseases mentioned above.

RCS models were established using the R package “rms” in R version 4.4.2. The mediating effect model was established using Mplus8.3 software. Stata17.0 was used for all other analyses. The significance level for the analysis was set at P < 0.05 for two sides.

Results

Distribution of chemerin in cases and controls

Chemerin levels in the case and control groups were 28.08 (11.98) ng/ml and 23.84 (9.69) ng/ml, respectively. The distribution of chemerin was significantly different between the cases and controls (Z=-5.656, P < 0.001). In the pre-menopausal subgroup, chemerin levels were higher in the cases than those in the control group (Z=-4.484, P < 0.001). A similar result was observed among post-menopausal women (Z=-3.319, P < 0.001). In the case group, there was no association between chemerin levels and TNM stage (F = 1.172, P = 0.326) or molecular subtype (F = 0.145, P = 0.933; Table 1).

Sensitivity analysis

After excluding pairs with metabolic, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular, endocrine, immune system, liver, and infectious diseases, serum chemerin levels still showed a positive association with breast cancer odds in both univariate and multivariate regression models (Table S3). A positive dose-response relationship between serum chemerin levels and breast cancer odds was identified (P-overall = 0.0037), as well as a significant non-linear dose–response relationship (P-non-linear = 0.0481; Fig. S1); results of the mediation effect analyses were stable. TG mediated the relationship between serum chemerin levels and breast cancer, accounting for 20% of the total effect (Table S4).

Discussion

Chemerin displays tumor-inhibitory or tumor-promoting effects in different tumors [25]. However, evidence of the relationship between chemerin and breast cancer is scarce. In this case–control study, after adjusting for potential confounders, serum chemerin levels were significantly associated with an increased odds of breast cancer in the overall population, as well as in the both pre- and post-menopausal subgroups, agreeing with the findings of Song et al. [14].

An experiment study showed that MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 breast cancer cells treated with chemerin exhibited decreased viability and invasion capacity [26]. However, another study observed that the proliferation and migration abilities of breast cancer cells dose-dependently increased with recombinant chemerin protein [27]. Moreover, Pachynski et al. reported that high chemerin expression had no effect in EMT6 breast cancer cells in vitro [28]. These differences may be related to the source, isoform, and treatment concentrations of chemerin. Therefore, the role of chemerin in breast cancer development and progression, as well as its effective concentration, warrant further investigation in future studies.

In the present study, the RCS models showed a linear positive dose–response relationship between serum chemerin levels and breast cancer risk in the overall group and subgroups of menopausal status. These results further suggest that high chemerin levels may increase breast cancer risk. The role of chemerin in angiogenesis is a possible explanation. Angiogenesis is crucial in tumor growth and metastasis [29]. The binding of chemerin to CMKLR1 on endothelial cells activate key angiogenic and cell survival pathways [30]. Furthermore, it promotes angiogenesis in human umbilical vein endothelial cells by upregulating diverse pro-angiogenic factors, such as VEGF-A, MMP-9, MMP-2, and S100A9, in neutrophils [31].

In this study, we found that TG concentration mediated 15% of the association between chemerin and breast cancer risk. In a sensitivity analysis, the proportion of the mediation to the total effect was 20%. Consistent with previous studies, we observed a significant positive correlation between serum chemerin and TG in the control population [32–34]. In vitro studies revealed that chemerin significantly inhibits intracellular TG degradation and induced lipid accumulation [35, 36]. Adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) and lipoprotein lipase (LPL) are key enzymes involved in systemic lipid metabolism. ATGL catalyzes the hydrolysis of intracellular TG into glycerol and free fatty acids [37]. LPL is a key rate-limiting enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of chylomicrons and TG in blood, generating free fatty acids, which are then taken up and used by cells [38]. Chemerin reduced the expression of ATGL and LPL in the liver, gastrocnemius muscle, and epididymal fat of diabetic mice [16]. Reduced ATGL expression promotes ectopic TG deposition in the liver and skeletal muscle [37, 39], which, in turn, induces or exacerbates insulin resistance [40] and leads to increased serum TG levels. LPL knockout mice develop severe hypertriglyceridemia, whereas upregulation of LPL expression effectively reduces serum TG levels [41, 42].

In the present study, serum TG levels were higher in the case group than those in the control group, which is consistent with the findings of a recent case–control study conducted in China [43]. Residual cholesterol is the cholesterol of TG-rich lipoproteins (such as very low-density lipoproteins and chylomicrons) [44]. In our study, the serum levels of residual cholesterol in the case group were also significantly higher than those in the controls (data not shown). Nonetheless, the relationship between serum TG levels and breast cancer risk remains unclear. A cohort study reported that increased serum TG levels increased breast cancer risk in women [23]. However, another cohort study yielded the opposite conclusion [45], whereas other studies found no significant association between serum TG and breast cancer risk [46]. Current pooled analyses of cohort studies indicate that increased blood TG levels increase the risk of lung [47] and colorectal cancers [48]. Insulin resistance increases the risk of tumors including breast cancer [49]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that serum or visceral TG levels are independently and positively associated with insulin resistance indicators, including glucose infusion rate and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance, even among individuals without established risk factors for insulin resistance (those with normal BMI and glucose tolerance) [50–53]. These findings suggest that TG dysregulation may be crucial for developing insulin resistance and may serve as the most sensitive blood lipid marker for insulin resistance.

This study used a case–control design to assess the association between serum chemerin and breast cancer risk and to analyze the mediating role of serum TG in this association, which has not been previously reported. The results of this study provide new evidence to understand the relationship between obesity and breast cancer. However, our study has limitations. First, this was a single-center study in a leading tertiary hospital in the Luzhou area; therefore, our findings require replication in diverse populations to confirm their validity. Second, although the sample size was calculated in this study, subgroup analysis was still limited, particularly in the pre-menopausal group. Third, although this study adjusted for many important confounding factors, we cannot exclude potential residual confounders because of missing measurements of some factors (such as diet, physical activity and medication use). Fourth, we measured the chemerin and TG concentrations only once in the morning under fasting conditions, which did not reflect their in vivo fluctuations. Finally, the present study used a case–control design, which raises the possibility of reverse causation. However, the EPIC-Potsdam cohort study found that higher chemerin levels were associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer in both the overall population and subgroup analyses, with a linear dose–response relationship [13]. These findings suggest a potential positive causal association between chemerin and cancer. In the present study, no significant differences in serum chemerin levels were observed among breast cancer patients at different TNM stages, suggesting that breast tumor progression may not affect chemerin expression. Although tumor cells require more cholesterol as a raw material for growth, and tumor progression may thus influence lipid levels, we compared the distribution of lipid parameters among breast cancer cases across different clinical stages and found no significant differences (data not shown). Additionally, all patients included in this study were treatment-naïve, thereby excluding the potential interference of breast cancer treatment on lipid levels. Nevertheless, the present study design only demonstrates the associations between serum chemerin, TG, and breast cancer, and does not permit causal inferences among the three factors.

Future cohort studies with larger sample sizes and repeated measurement designs are needed to comprehensively evaluate the association between chemerin and TG levels and breast cancer risk. Additionally, basic experiments using breast cancer cells will help to validate the potential mechanisms identified in this study.
