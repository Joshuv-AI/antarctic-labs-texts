Asymmetric charge-polarization tailoring active hydrogen transfer for selective photoreduction CO2 to CH4
Authors: Chenxu Yin, Zhecheng Sun, Kunlin Tang, Weixin Zou, Haiqin Wan, Zhao-Qing Liu, Lin Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotocatalytic reduction of CO2 to natural gas using water vapor is a promising strategy for carbon recycling and renewable energy storage. However, the selectivity of current catalysts still remains a big challenge. Herein, we construct IrCu alloys on TiO2 nanosheets to promote photocatalytic CO2 to methane with 98.6% selectivity and 7.9% quantum efficiency at 365 nm under non-sacrificial ambient conditions. The performance is competitive with most other reported metal-based photocatalysts. Experimental and theoretical calculations demonstrate that the intensive H2O adsorption on Ir/TiO2 hinders *H transfer, inevitably generating the H2 by-product. Conversely, hydrophobic Cu effectively optimizes the interfacial hydrogen-bond network on IrCu/TiO2, predominantly in H-down configurations for H2O adsorption on the asymmetric charge-polarized Cuδ+-Irδ- structure, which facilitates the kinetic migration of dissociated *H to *CO-Cu sites, resulting in the reduced energy barrier for the key *CHO intermediate. This finding enables high CH4 selectivity on IrCu/TiO2, deepening our understanding of gas-solid interfacial water vapor in the enhanced natural gas synthesis.Subject terms: Photocatalysis, Photocatalysis, Photocatalysis

Introduction

The photocatalytic conversion of carbon dioxide (CO2) via water (H2O) vapor into methane (CH4) is an emerging technology for synthetic natural gas (SNG), which is able to achieve a sustainable energy economy, as it closes the carbon cycle and enables the efficient transportation of solar energy to valuable fuels1–5. Currently, the solar methanation process from CO2 reduction under non-sacrificial ambient conditions remains challenging, due to the low selectivity associated with the multi-step process involving eight-electron reduction and hydrogen proton migration (Fig. 1a)6,7. Titanium dioxide (TiO2), a classical metal oxide semiconductor, is widely used in photocatalysis owing to its stability, non-toxicity, and cost-effectiveness8–10. Nevertheless, the pristine TiO2 exhibits limited photocatalytic activity for the CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) to CH411,12. Accordingly, many works focus on interfacial engineering to regulate the charge distribution, modification of CO2 reactant, and key intermediates on the active sites for enhancing CH4 selectivity in TiO2-based catalysts13–19.

Water vapor (H2O) is an essential factor in CO2RR, serving as the hydrogen proton source and critically influencing product selectivity20–24. The hydrogen-bond network (HBN) formed from the interfacial water can affect intermediates and regulate the hydrogen proton transfer behavior25,26. For example, tuning water solvation could effectively modulate CO2RR performance25. And the co-assembly of molecular catalysts and HBN-rich polymers enhanced the *CO surface coverage, promoting C-C coupling to form C2+ selectivity27. However, achieving the selective reduction of CO2 to CH4 through the controlled behavior of interfacial water remains a significant challenge. Because proton-proton coupling to H2 is thermodynamically and kinetically favored over proton-to-CO2 hydrogenation, leading to the side reaction of the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER)28. Moreover, the reaction pathways and ultimate product selectivity are also affected by hydrogen proton coverage and distribution29,30. In order to balance hydrogen proton concentration and facilitate available *H transfer, constructing active sites in photocatalysts is a potential way of activating interfacial water and achieving selective CO2RR methanation. Notably, Iridium (Ir)-based catalysts are widely used in the HER and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) due to their ability to adsorb and dissociate H2O31–34. This prominent role stems from its electronic structure, characterized by unfilled d orbitals, which facilitates efficient hybridization with reactant molecular orbitals, enabling the adsorption of key intermediates such as *H and *O to be optimized32,35. However, excessive H2O adsorption and strong HBN around the Ir active sites would hinder the active hydrogen proton transfer to carbon-containing intermediates, resulting in the reduced CO2RR efficiency36,37 and undesirable by-products, including H2 or CO38. Therefore, it is imperative to develop Ir-based photocatalysts with moderated H2O adsorption, active hydrogen transfer, and intermediate binding for solar methanation.

Herein, we construct IrCu bimetallic nanoalloys supported on TiO2 nanosheets via an ethylene glycol-assisted reduction method. The dual-metal modulation strategy is proposed and illustrated in Fig. 1b; inferior CH4 generation efficiency can result from inappropriate HBN strength, such as localized hydrogen protons from the water dissociation at Ir sites on Ir/TiO2, or hydrophobic Cu sites on Cu/TiO2. In this work, we turn HBN moderately on the IrCu alloys to supply migratable hydrogen protons from the water dissociation at Ir sites to the adsorbed *CO intermediate at Cu sites, yielding high CH4 selectivity. Furthermore, the TiO2 support absorbs light for the photogenerated electrons and holes, and the alloy effect at the metal-support interface in IrCu/TiO2 generates oxygen vacancies (Ov), prolonging the lifetime of photoelectrons, which is critical for the sluggish eight-electron reaction in CH4 formation. The CH4 selectivity of 98.6% and quantum efficiency of 7.9% at 365 nm are achieved in IrCu/TiO2. In situ spectroscopic analysis and the density functional theory calculations reveal that a Cuδ+-Irδ− structure increases Ov at the support interface, and inhibits the recombination of photogenerated charges. Moreover, ab initio molecular dynamics simulations clarify the distinct adsorption structures of interfacial water molecules and the hydrogen proton transfer kinetics. Dual-functional IrCu/TiO2 effectively reconstructs the initial HBN, facilitating *H transfer and *CO reduction, which significantly improves CH4 selectivity. This work offers a fresh perspective on tuning the interaction between interfacial water molecules and the photocatalysts to achieve highly selective solar methanation.

Results

Rational design and synthesis of dual-metal IrCu/TiO2

Due to the different electronegativities of Cu (1.90) and Ir (2.20), constructing a dual-metal IrCu catalytic site can introduce local polarization to regulate intermediate formation. Theoretical calculations were performed to verify the effectiveness of this strategy. Bader charge analysis indicates that the electrons transfer from Cu (+0.092 e) to Ir (−0.302 e) in IrCu/TiO2, leading to a local polarized Cuδ+-Irδ− structure (Fig. 2a). The d-band center energy (ɛd) of Ir in IrCu/TiO2 (−2.488 eV) is closer to the Fermi level (EF) than that of Ir in Ir/TiO2 (ɛd,Ir = −2.704 eV). Similarly, the d-band center of Ti in IrCu/TiO2 (−4.802 eV) also presents an upshift compared to Cu/TiO2 (−5.033 eV) and Ir/TiO2 (−4.899 eV), suggesting that dual-metal IrCu/TiO2 can introduce intensive electronic interaction (Fig. 2b, c). Combined with the reduced Bader charge of the oxygen atoms in IrCu/TiO2 (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), the Ov is easier to generate in IrCu/TiO2 due to the intensive electronic interaction in Cuδ+-Irδ−, resulting in the enhanced photocatalytic CO2 reduction.

Under the guidance of dual-metal regulation strategy, IrCu nanoalloys supported on TiO2 nanosheets were prepared by an ethylene glycol-assisted reduction method (Fig. 2d)39. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES) proves successful introduction of Ir and Cu in a ratio of 1.1:1 in IrCu/TiO2 (Supplementary Table 3). Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of IrCu/TiO2 only exhibit the anatase phase of TiO2 (JCPDS No. 21-1272), indicating that there is no significant agglomeration of IrCu nanoalloys on the TiO2 substrate (Supplementary Fig. 1). Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM) images suggest that IrCu nanoclusters are uniformly distributed on the TiO2 surface, with an average particle size of ca. 1.3 nm, and lattice spacings of 0.189 nm and 0.235 nm correspond to the TiO2 (200) and IrCu (111), respectively (Fig. 2e–g and Supplementary Fig. 2). In addition, energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) element mapping and color-coded images of the ordered structure further confirm of the successful preparation of IrCu nanoclusters on TiO2 nanosheets (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 3).

Electronic structure and oxygen vacancy modulation

The electronic structure of the IrCu nanoalloys supported on TiO2 was investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) characterizations. The Ir 4f peak in the XPS spectra of IrCu/TiO2 shifts to a lower binding energy by 0.35 eV compared to that of Ir/TiO2, suggesting of electron-rich Ir species in the alloys (Fig. 3a). In the XPS spectra of Cu 2p (Fig. 3b), compared with Cu/TiO2, the Cu 2p1/2 and Cu 2p3/2 peaks of IrCu/TiO2 shift to a higher binding energy by 0.67 eV. The Cu LMM Auger electron spectra (AES) further confirm more Cu+ species in IrCu/TiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating the electrons transfer from Cu to Ir to construct the Cuδ+-Irδ− structure. To further elucidate the electronic and coordination properties of the IrCu structure, XANES spectroscopy was performed. The white line intensity and absorption edge of the IrCu/TiO2 are closely aligned to the metallic Ir foil (Fig. 3c), indicating the metallic state of the Ir. The Cu K-edge XANES spectra show that the absorption edge of the IrCu/TiO2 sample fell between the Cu foil and CuO (Fig. 3d), suggesting that the average valence state of Cuδ+ species is between 0 and +2 due to the electronic transfer from Cu to Ir. Furthermore, the coordination properties of Ir and Cu species were investigated by the extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectroscopy, and the corresponding structural parameters (CN: coordination numbers; R: the distance between absorber and backscatter atoms) were analyzed. The Ir L3-edge Fourier-transform (FT)-EXAFS spectrum of IrCu/TiO2 exhibits three coordination peaks with the bond distances (R) of 2.01–2.72 Å (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Three coordination structures are identified: Ir–Cu metallic bonding (CN = 2.3 at R = 2.53 Å), Ir–Ir coordination (CN = 4.9 at R = 2.72 Å), and Ir-O interactions (CN = 0.9 at R = 2.01 Å) (Supplementary Table 4). The lower coordination numbers of Ir-O (CN < 2) are attributed to surface-adsorbed oxygen species or interfacial interactions with the support. The Cu K-edge FT-EXAFS analysis of IrCu/TiO2 confirms the formation of Ir–Cu metallic bonding, with the reduced Cu-Cu coordination numbers. Above all, these observations suggest that Cu modulates the electron density of Ir, resulting in the formation of a polarized Cuδ+-Irδ− structure, consistent with the calculated results.

Moreover, the normalization XANES of Ti K-edge of Cu/TiO2, Ir/TiO2, and IrCu/TiO2 were determined in comparison. As shown in Fig. 3e, all the photocatalysts exhibit the TiO6 structure of TiO28. A reduced intensity of the white line peak indicates lower content, and the weakest peak intensity in IrCu/TiO2 indicates a diminished TiO6 structure, due to more Ov40,41. The changed local structure of the Ti–O bond was further probed by Fourier-transform (FT) and wavelet transform (WT) analysis. And the Ti–O bond length in IrCu/TiO2 is approximately 1.94 Å, longer than those in Cu/TiO2 and Ir/TiO2, likely due to the decreased Ti–O bond covalency (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 7). Moreover, the coordination numbers of the Ti–O bond follow the order IrCu/TiO2 < Ir/TiO2 < Cu/TiO2 (Supplementary Table 6), consistent with the trend in bond length and white line peak. These structural distinctions suggest that the IrCu nanoalloy effectively decreases the TiO6 structure and promotes Oᵥ formation in TiO2. The blue-shifted and broadened Eg vibration peak in Raman spectra and the negative-shifted peak in the Ti 2p XPS spectra indicate more Ov and Ti3+ species in IrCu/TiO2 (Supplementary Figs. 8–10)42. The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) signal intensity is significantly stronger in IrCu/TiO2 than in Ir/TiO2 and Cu/TiO2, confirming a higher Ov concentration (Supplementary Fig. 11). In situ EPR spectroscopy was further employed to investigate the role of Ov. The signals at g = 2.003 and 1.994 are assigned to Ov and Ti3+ species, respectively43. Under a CO2 atmosphere, the Ov signal decreased while the Ti³+ signal increased, confirming that Ov sites participate in the capture and activation of CO2, which is different from that under N2 conditions (Supplementary Figs. 12–15). Furthermore, IrCu/TiO2 exhibits a higher transient photocurrent density response and a smaller impedance (Fig. 3g, h), suggesting that Ov facilitates the separation and migration of photogenerated carries44,45. In addition, according to the calculated band energies from the UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) and the valence band maxima (VBM) from the VB-XPS results, all samples possess the suitable band-edge positions for CO2 reduction to CH4 or CO (Supplementary Figs. 16–18). Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra show that the emission peak intensity of IrCu/TiO2 is the weakest, compared to that of Cu/TiO2 and Ir/TiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 19), implying efficient charge recombination suppression and more electrons available for CO2RR. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra elucidate the carrier recombination kinetics, confirming the longer carrier lifetime of 36.93 ns for IrCu/TiO2 than those of Ir/TiO2 (34.18 ns) and Cu/TiO2 (6.07 ns) (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Table 7)46.

High selectivity of photocatalytic CO2 methanation

The photocatalytic performances of CO2 reduction for IrCu/TiO2, Ir/TiO2, and Cu/TiO2 were tested in a common gas-solid reactor under the conditions of CO2, H2O, and full-spectrum illumination at room temperature (25 °C), without any additional photosensitizers and sacrificial agents. The performance of photocatalytic CO2 reduction is presented in Fig. 4a. With the increased Cu loadings, the CH4 yield and selectivity increase and then decrease. The IrCu/TiO2 photocatalyst affords a great CH4 yield (100.3 μmol g−1 h−1), which is nearly 33 and 100 times higher than that of Ir/TiO2 and Cu/TiO2, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4b, the IrCu/TiO2 sample exhibits a CH4 selectivity of 98.6 %, which was nearly 3.7 and 4.5 times higher than that of the Ir/TiO2 and Cu/TiO2 samples, respectively. In addition, the CH4 yield shows a linear relationship with the amount of catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 20). Moreover, the yield of H2 by-product during the process of CO2 photoreduction was detected (Supplementary Fig. 21a and Table 8), which was only observed on the Ir/TiO2 sample, confirming that the excessive *H on the Ir sites promoted the HER, but was inhibited on the IrCu/TiO2 sample. Meanwhile, we conducted a quantitative analysis of O2 production from the H2O oxidation, which was highest on IrCu/TiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 21b). The apparent quantum efficiencies (AQE) of CH4 evolution for IrCu/TiO2 were measured under various light wavelengths of 365, 420, 450, 475, and 520 nm (Fig. 4c). For comparison, we also measured AQE of CH4 evolution for Ir/TiO2 and Cu/TiO2 at 365 nm, respectively (Supplementary Table 9). The IrCu/TiO2 sample exhibits an AQE of 7.9% at 365 nm, which is one to two orders of magnitude higher than the AQEs of Ir/TiO2 and Cu/TiO2, and competitive with most reported metal-based photocatalysts (Supplementary Table 10).

To evaluate the reliability of the current photocatalytic system, a series of systematic blank experiments was conducted. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 22, in the absence of light irradiation or photocatalysts or CO2 reactant, there are no products to detect, demonstrating that the determined products originate from the photocatalytic CO2 reduction. In order to further verify that the gas products were only from the CO2 feed, the 13CO2 isotopic labeling experiments were carried out. In Fig. 4d, only 13CO (m/z = 29) and 13CH4 (m/z = 17) are detected, which clearly rules out the contributions from other carbon-containing species such as ethylene glycol. In addition, the IrCu/TiO2 photocatalyst also exhibits a good catalytic stability, as demonstrated by its stable high methane yield and selectivity throughout five cycling tests (Fig. 4e). Moreover, the XRD, EPR, XPS, and STEM analyses were employed to investigate the structural, morphological and electronic properties of IrCu/TiO2 photocatalysts before and after the reaction (Supplementary Figs. 23–25). These analyses confirm that the IrCu/TiO2 remains stable during the catalytic process. Furthermore, the photocatalytic performance of the IrCu/TiO2 photocatalyst is compared with the recently reported CO2-to-CH4 photocatalysts under similar conditions (only CO2 and H2O reactants) (Supplementary Table 10)47–58. As a result, these findings suggest the great photocatalytic performance of CO2 methanation on IrCu/TiO2, where the IrCu dual-metal sites serve as the decisive active sites responsible for H2O dissociation and CO2 protonation.

In situ characterization and mechanism investigation

To deeply identify the active sites for H2O adsorption and dissociation, the water contact angle, H2O-TPD, and in situ DRIFTS experiments were carried out. The Ir/TiO2 presents the lowest water contact angles (<10°), suggesting of its hydrophilic characteristic, while Cu/TiO2 shows higher contact angle, indicating its decreased hydrophilicity59 (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 26). Clearly, the introduction of Cu modulates the surface hydrophilicity of IrCu/TiO2, indicating that the water adsorption on IrCu/TiO2 is moderate, compared with Ir/TiO2. The H2O-TPD profiles further display the distinct adsorption strength of H2O on Ir/TiO2 and IrCu/TiO2. In Fig. 5b, the desorption amount of H2O is significantly reduced on IrCu/TiO2, consistent with the water contact angles. In addition, three desorption peaks are observed, corresponding to physically and associatively adsorbed water, as well as structural hydroxyl clusters, and the associatively adsorbed water related to active *H and hydroxyl groups29. On IrCu/TiO2, the desorption peak of the associatively adsorbed water is shifted to a lower temperature, compared with Ir/TiO2, indicating that its active *H is easier to transfer, likely due to the moderate hydrophilic surface. In situ EPR conducted was used to further confirm this active *H around the IrCu/TiO2 photocatalyst interface. As shown in Fig. 5c, the EPR peaks belonging to DMPO-*H are observed, and the IrCu/TiO2 has a stronger signal intensity than the Ir/TiO2, indicating its higher abundance of available *H during the reaction process60. The active sites of *H migration on the photocatalysts were determined by in situ DRIFTS in the dark and in the light. The schematic diagram of the in situ infrared reactor is displayed (Supplementary Fig. 27). As observed in Fig. 5d, the peaks at approximately 2050 and 2100 cm−1 correspond to the characteristic vibration peaks of the Ir-H and Cu-H species, respectively61. On the IrCu/TiO2 photocatalyst, a weak peak is observed in the dark. Once light is introduced, a distinct Cu-H stretching signal at about 2060 cm−1 is observed, suggesting that the adsorbed H2O is easier to donate the *H from Ir to Cu. In contrast, the Ir-H signal on Ir/TiO2 shows negligible enhancement in light. Furthermore, the formed HBN from adsorbed H2O around the photocatalyst surface was analyzed to reveal the different abilities of *H transfer. The –OH stretching mode (νOH*) signal centers at around 3200, 3350, and 3600–3700 cm−1 in Fig. 5e, f, which are assigned to strong HBN, intermediate HBN, and free water, respectively25,62. An intensive and broad range of approximately 3400–3200 cm−1 is observed on the Ir/TiO2 surface, which may be attributed to the strong HBN. By contrast, for IrCu/TiO2, only the HBN (3450 cm−1) and free water (3590 cm−1) appear, but the strong HBN is absent, indicating that the strong HBN is broken down on IrCu/TiO2. These observations suggest that the moderate HBN on IrCu/TiO2 promotes H2O dissociation and accelerates *H migration compared to Ir/TiO2.

Furthermore, the reaction mechanism and active sites for CO2 protonation were revealed by in situ DRIFTS under CO2 and H2O conditions in the light. All samples exhibit the characteristic peaks for *COOH (1508, 1540, 1720 cm−1), *CH3O (1445 cm−1), m-CO32− (1455 cm−1), and b-CO32− (1280, 1298, 1310 cm−1) intermediates (Fig. 5g, h, and Supplementary Fig. 28)18,63,64. Interestingly, on the IrCu/TiO2 sample, the intensity of *CH3O is more intense than that of m-CO32− and b-CO32− intermediate, indicating that carbonate intermediates on IrCu/TiO2 are more prone to hydrogenation. The b-CO32− species on IrCu/TiO2 gradually convert to *CHO (1746 cm−1) and *CH3O in the process of reduction and protonation, while relatively stable on Ir/TiO2. Additionally, the normalized intensity of the *CH3O and b-CO32− species for Ir/TiO2 and IrCu/TiO2 are displayed (Supplementary Fig. 29). The formed *CH3O on IrCu/TiO2 is richer than that on Ir/TiO2, due to its sufficient *H for the protonation of b-CO32−, consistent with the in situ EPR results for *H species. The DFT calculations also confirm that the CH4 selectivity of IrCu/TiO2 is higher than that of Ir/TiO2, due to a lower energy barrier of the key *CO-to-*CHO step on IrCu/TiO2 (0.467 eV) than that on Ir/TiO2 (0.906 eV) thermodynamically (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Figs. 30–33). Therefore, the selectivity mechanism is proposed that Ir sites dissociate H2O to provide a rich *H environment, and Cu sites reconstruct HBN on the catalytic interface, thereby facilitating *H transfer to *CO-Cu for highly selective CH4 production on IrCu/TiO2.

To elucidate the mechanism of migratable hydrogen proton by the modulated HBN on IrCu/TiO2, the ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations and DFT calculations were further performed to investigate H2O adsorption orientation, thereby gaining insights into interfacial HBN microenvironments and dynamic proton transfer processes. AIMD simulations at room temperature (300 K) show the interfacial H2O molecules around Ir/TiO2 and IrCu/TiO2 (Supplementary Figs. 34 and35). The representative snapshots reveal that the interfacial H2O is farther from the surface of IrCu/TiO2 (2.12 Å) than that of Ir/TiO2 (2.06 Å), indicating that the hydrophilic Ir/TiO2 exhibits a stronger affinity for H2O than IrCu/TiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 36). However, no significant differences in radial distribution function (RDF) of O–H bonds are observed between IrCu/TiO2 and Ir/TiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 37). Interestingly, the orientation of H2O molecules was investigated and identified as the key factor. AIMD simulations show the concentration profiles of H and O atoms along the surface orientation (Fig. 6a). The H peak (L1) appears before that of O (L2), and the H peak density is approximately twice that of the O peak, suggesting a predominant H-down configuration of interfacial H2O on IrCu/TiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 38). In contrast, Ir/TiO2 exhibits overlapping and broadened L1 and L2 peaks (Fig. 6b), indicating disordered H2O orientations with both H-down and O-down configurations. These differences in orientation were further confirmed by the dipole angle θ between the water dipole vector and surface normal. As displayed in Fig. 6c, a value of cosθ = -1 corresponds to H-down orientation, which is more pronounced in IrCu/TiO2, reflecting a highly ordered H-down arrangement. Whereas Ir/TiO2 exhibits a broader distribution, consistent with a mixed H2O orientation.

A slow-growth AIMD simulation was conducted to confirm the facilitated hydrogen proton transfer on H-down configurations of IrCu/TiO2, compared with Ir/TiO2 (Fig. 6d, e). In the reconstructed HBN-mediated pathway on IrCu/TiO2, the hydrogen proton from H2O dissociation rapidly jumps to an adjacent H2O molecule, triggering a hydrogen proton transfer within the interfacial H2O (Supplementary Fig. 39). The hydrogen protons with H-down configurations of the interfacial H2O on IrCu/TiO2 could transfer directly to Cu atoms with an energy barrier of only 0.54 eV, which is much lower than 0.63 eV of Ir/TiO2. DFT calculations further show that the adsorption energy of H2O on Ir/TiO2 is −0.63 eV, lower than that of −0.58 eV on IrCu/TiO2, suggesting that Ir/TiO2 is more likely to absorb H2O (Supplementary Figs. 40–43), but the stronger adsorption of *H hinders its migration to *CO intermediate. Therefore, the H-down configuration of H2O adsorption accelerates *H transfer to enable efficient CO2 methanation on IrCu/TiO2 owing to the polarized Cuδ+-Irδ− electronic structure and reconstructed HBN in IrCu/TiO2.

Based on in situ experiments, DFT calculations, and AIMD simulations, the reaction mechanism of high CH4 selectivity mediated by interfacial H2O is proposed in Fig. 6f. The specific contents are as follows: (1) the H2O molecules are adsorbed on the alloys of hydrophilic Ir sites, generating localized hydrogen protons. Meanwhile, the hydrophobic Cu sites of the alloys modulate the hydrogen-bond network for H-down configurations, which significantly weakens the *H binding ability and facilitates *H migration to the Cu sites. (2) CO2 molecules are then adsorbed at the IrCu/TiO2 interface (on the alloy and OV), which are activated to form the *COOH intermediate. (3) Subsequently, the generated *CO intermediate on Cu sites undergoes continuous hydrogenation with its surface *H, ultimately producing CH4. From both kinetic and thermodynamic perspectives, the accelerated *H transfer to *CO-Cu reduces the energy barrier for key *CHO formation, promoting the continuous hydrogenation and reduction processes by utilizing the long-lived photogenerated electrons from the TiO2 semiconductor.

Discussion

In summary, we present a gas-solid interfacial water vapor strategy to enable the dual-functional alloy IrCu/TiO2 for highly selective photoreduction of CO2 to CH4. The obtained IrCu/TiO2 photocatalyst achieves CH4 selectivity of above 98% and quantum efficiency of 7.9% at 365 nm under the mild conditions without additional photosensitizers or sacrificial agents. Benefitting from the polarized Cuδ+-Irδ− structure in IrCu/TiO2, the stronger interfacial effect promotes Ov generation for CO2 capture and long-lived carrier formation, resulting in the accelerated eight-electron reaction in CH4 formation. Furthermore, the reconstructed HBN structure of the gas-solid interfacial H2O on the IrCu alloy breaks down the initial HBN of Ir/TiO2, enabling the predominant H-down configurations and reducing the adsorption energy of *H, which makes it easier to provide migratable *H for key intermediate *CHO generation. This work provides an effective perspective on tuning the interfacial water vapor for highly selective solar-energy methane production.

Methods

Materials and chemicals

All solvents and reagents purchased from commercial sources were used without further purification. Tetrabutyl titanate (C16H36O4Ti, 100%), ethylene glycol (EG, 99%), vupric chloride (CuCl2, 98%), and iridium trichloride (IrCl3, 99.8%) were purchased from Macklin. Hydrofluoric acid (HF, 50%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 96%), polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP, k-25) were purchased from Nanjing Chemical Reagent Co. Acetone (CH3COCH3, 99.8%) and n-hexane (C6H14, 99%) were purchased from Aladdin.

Preparation of catalysts

Anatase TiO2 nanosheets were prepared by a typical hydrothermal method. Tetrabutyl titanate was reacted with hydrofluoric acid solution in a Teflon-lined autoclave at 200 °C for 24 h. The resulting white precipitate was collected and washed alternately with ethanol and distilled water until the precipitate was pure white and dried at 110 °C for 24 h. Finally, the sample was obtained by drying at 400 °C for 2 h in a muffle furnace. The resulting sample was denoted as TiO2.

The composition-tunable IrCux nanoclusters on TiO2 nanosheets were prepared with ethylene glycol (EG) as a reducing agent through a one-step microwave reduction method, and the total metal loading was controlled to be 0.1 mmol. Depending on the real molar ratio of Ir and Cu by the ICP-OES analyses (Table S3), the catalysts were named as Ir/TiO2, IrCu/TiO2, Ir1Cu2/TiO2, Ir3Cu1/TiO2, and Cu/TiO2, respectively.

Synthesis of IrCux alloy samples

The 15 mL of glycol was placed in a three-necked flask with a magnetic stirring rotor, which was heated to 125 °C and held for 30 min in a microwave reactor. Then 1 mL of NaOH (0.25 M) and corresponding proportions of IrCl3 and CuCl2 were added to the three-necked flask. The mixture was held at 125 °C for an additional 30 min and then immediately immersed in an ice bath for 30 min. The colloid was then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min and the mixture was washed with acetone and n-hexane so that the PVP was completely washed out. The prepared colloid was redispersed in ethanol. A mixture of 300 mg of prepared TiO2 NS and ethanol was added dropwise. To remove the ethanol, the mixture was dried at 70 °C for 6 h.

Synthesis of Cu monometallic sample

The mixture of 15 mL of glycol and 20 mg of PVP was placed in a three-necked flask with a magnetic stirring rotor. Since Cu itself was more difficult to reduce by glycol, it is necessary to increase the temperature appropriately, and PVP needs to be added. The microwave reactor was adjusted to a temperature of 175 °C and held for 30 min with a maintenance temperature of 175 °C. The following steps were the same as those for the IrCux alloy samples. The obtained sample was denoted as Cu/TiO2.

Synthesis of an Ir monometallic sample

This sample was prepared in the same way as the previous IrCux alloy, except for the maintenance temperature of 150 °C. The sample was denoted as Ir/TiO2.

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on a Philips X’ Pert Pro diffractometer under Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm, 40 kV, 40 mA). Atomic-resolution aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopic (AC-HAADF-STEM) images were collected on a FEI Titan Cubed G2 60-300 aberration-corrected S/TEM instrument at 300 kV accelerating voltage. Imaging was conducted in HAADF mode to provide Z-contrast, with a probe convergence angle of 22 mrad and a detector inner angle of 79.5 mrad. Samples were prepared by dispersing the powder in ethanol and depositing it onto a TEM Mo grid. Elemental mapping was carried out via STEM-EDS using the SuperX detection system. An inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES) was recorded by Analytik Jena novAA350/ZEEnit650p (Analytik Jena). The catalysts were digested at 130 °C in a concentrated aqueous HCl/HNO3 and HF solution. After the solids were completely dissolved, concentrated sulfuric acid was added to remove the hydrofluoric acid. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were determined via PHI 5000 Versa Probe high-performance electron spectrometer, on monochromatic Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). The binding energies were corrected by the adventitious carbon (C 1 s peak at 284.6 eV). Raman spectra were collected on a Renishaw Laser Raman spectrometer (Renishaw plc) with an Ar+ laser beam. The emission line was 532 nm, and the output power was 10 mW. Samples were exposed to air at room temperature throughout the experiments. The in situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) tests were recorded by an ESR JES FA200 (JEOL) spectrometer at 77 K under the irradiation of a xenon lamp in N2 or CO2 atmosphere. In situ ESR of *H free radical using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) to capture the unstable hydrogen radical to form the DMPO-*H adduct to generate an EPR signal. The X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) measurement analysis was performed with Si(111) crystal monochromators at the BL11B beamlines at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) (Shanghai, China). Before the analysis at the beamline, samples were pressed into thin sheets with 1 cm in diameter and sealed using Kapton tape film. The XAFS spectra were recorded at room temperature using a 4-channel Silicon Drift Detector (SDD) Bruker 5040. The EXAFS spectra were recorded in fluorescence mode. The spectra were processed and analyzed by the software codes Athena and Artemis. UV–Vis diffused reflection spectra were obtained on a Shimadzu UV-2401 spectrophotometer. BaSO4 was used for the blank sample, and the measurement ranged from 200 to 1000 nm. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were carried out on a F-7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) with 365 nm excitation, and the slit in all measurements was 2 nm. Time-resolved photoluminescence (tr-PL) was measured by the FLS1000 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Edinburgh Instruments, UK) at room temperature.

Photoelectrochemical measurements

Photoelectrochemical tests, including electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and transient photocurrent response, were performed on a three-electrode system electrochemical workstation (CHI 660, CHInstrument, Shanghai) at room temperature (25 °C). Platinum wire electrode and Ag/AgCl electrode were used as counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. The catalyst-modified ITO glass (1 × 1 cm2) by the dip-coating method (5 mg of sample dispersed in 50 μL of ethanol) was used as the working electrode. A 300 W Xe lamp was used as the light source, and Na2SO4 (0.25 M) as the electrolyte solution.

In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier-transform spectra (DRIFTS)

In situ DRIFTS were determined by a Nicolet iS50FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo, USA) in a range of 3800–1000 cm−1, and the cell was equipped with a KBr window. The spectrometer was equipped with a specimen chamber and a highly sensitive mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector, cooled by liquid N2. In a typical process, 50 mg of catalyst powder was put in the DRIFTS cell (PIKE Technologies DiffusIR), pressed, and mounted. The catalysts were purged with N2 for 1 h at 300 °C. Afterward, the catalysts were cooled down step-wise to room temperature. During this process, the background spectra were collected at room temperature. For CO2 photoreduction experiments, the feed gas consisted of 15% CO2, respectively, using Ar as a balance. For the CO2 photoreduction test, the CO2 bubbles in water were switched into the system to collect the adsorption signal at an interval of 2 min until equilibrium. Finally, the 300 W Xe lamp was switched on to initiate the reaction, and the in situ DRIFTS spectra were collected by an MCT detector. The typical signals of diverse intermediates were detected subsequently under dark and light irradiation (0-30 min). All the spectra were collected from 1000 to 4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 for 100 scans.

Photocatalytic test of CO2 reduction

The photocatalytic reduction of CO2 was carried out under the irradiation of a 300 W xenon lamp in a gas-solid reactor made of stainless steel with a transparent quartz window at the top, and no additional photosensitizers and sacrificial agents were added. The procedures were followed: the sample powder (50 mg) and 5 mL water were added into a 100 mL Teflon-lined autoclave. The mixture was ultrasonicated for 20 min to make the sample fully disperse. Then, the container was sealed and purged by high-purity CO2 (0.4 MPa) for at least 30 min. Afterward, the gas in the container was analyzed by the gas chromatograph to confirm no air, and then the 300 W Xe lamp (Light intensity: 500 mW cm−2) was turned on. During the photoreaction process, the reactor temperature was maintained at 25 °C using a circulating water-cooling system. The mixture was irradiated for 8 h with stirring, and the gas products were analyzed using a gas chromatograph (GC-7920, CEAULIGHT), which was equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) for H2 and O2, and a flame ionization detector (FID) for CH4 and CO. 13CO2 isotopic labeling tests

In order to confirm the carbon source of products, the isotopic labeling experiments of the 13CO2 were used as the reactant to analyze the carbon source of CO and CH4 generated from the photocatalytic CO2 reduction. Recycling test

To determine the stability of catalysts, the recycling testing was conducted as follows. After the first reaction, the used catalyst was recovered by centrifugation. It was then used in the second reaction test, and then for the last test.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

All the first-principles spin-polarized calculations were performed by using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Program (VASP)65. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) in the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) form and a cutoff energy of 500 eV for the planewave basis set were adopted66. A 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k grid was used for sampling the Brillouin zones at structure calculation, whereas a denser mesh of 5 × 5 × 1 was used for the electronic structure calculations. The ion-electron interactions were described by the projector augmented wave (PAW) method. A vacuum space greater than 20 Ǻ was employed to avoid the interaction between two periodic units. The convergence criteria of structure optimization were chosen as the maximum force on each atom less than 0.02 eV/Ǻ with an energy change less than 1 × 10−5 eV. The Grimme’s DFT-D3 scheme of dispersion correction was adopted to describe the van der Waals (vdW) interactions in these systems.

Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulation

Both the AIMD and slow-growth approach simulations were sampled within the canonical (NVT) ensemble by Nosé-Hoover thermostats with a time step of 1.0 fs at a finite temperature of 300 K. First, an AIMD with a duration of 10 ps was applied to enable the system to reach a pre-equilibrium state. Then, we mainly adopted the Slow-growth method to obtain the activation energy barrier68. In the Slow-growth method, a geometric parameter λ, i.e., the collective variable (CV), is constrained during the dynamics and varied from the value characteristic of the initial state (λ0) to that of the final state (λ1) with a velocity of transformation ʋ. The Helmholtz free-energy difference ΔF can be computed as Eq. (6) by collecting the derivative of the potential energy with respect to lambda during the simulation, and by integrating over lambda afterward.

In the limit of infinitesimally small ʋ, ΔF corresponds to the free-energy difference ΔG between the final and initial state.
