PGC7 regulates maternal mRNA translation via AKT1-YBX1 interactions in mouse oocytes
Authors: Yingxiang Liu, Peiwen Feng, Xing Wei, Hongyu Xu, Mengying Yu, Lei Zhang, Weijie Hao, Zekun Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTimely and accurate translation of maternal mRNA is essential for oocyte maturation and early embryonic development. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of Primordial Germ cell 7 (PGC7) as a maternal factor in maintaining DNA methylation of maternally imprinted loci in zygotes. However, it is still unknown whether PGC7 is involved in the regulation of Maternal mRNA Translation. In this study, we have identified that PGC7-AKT1-YBX1 axis is involved in promoting the translation of maternal mRNAs. PGC7 not only sustains AKT1 activity by counteracting PP2A dephosphorylation and facilitating PDK1-AKT1 binding but also assists AKT1 in phosphorylating the translation inhibitor YBX1. In the absence of PGC7, despite increased PIK3CA expression and AKT1 phosphorylation, AKT1 is unable to phosphorylate YBX1. PGC7 facilitates the interaction between AKT1 and YBX1, enhancing YBX1-Serine 100 phosphorylation, which leads to YBX1 dissociation from eIF4E, thereby activating the translation of maternal Cyclin B1 and YAP1. The findings demonstrate the indispensability of PGC7 for translation activation in mammalian oocytes and provide a potential network regulated by PGC7 in early oogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-024-01976-1.Keywords: Oocyte, PGC7, AKT1, YBX1, Maternal mRNA translation

Introduction

In mammals, immature oocytes at the diplotene stage of meiosis-I produce and store abundant maternal mRNAs, which are translationally inactive during oocyte growth [1, 2]. Early embryo development, including meiotic spindle assembly, MII arrest maintenance, and mRNA clearance during MZT, is mainly regulated by proteins newly translated from these maternal mRNAs [3–6].

Maternal factors, abundant in oocytes, are crucial for oocyte maturation and early embryo development, participating in chromosome assembly, epigenetic regulation of the zygotic genome, and translation of maternal mRNA [7–9]. The primordial germ cell 7 (PGC7) protein comprises 150 amino acids and is encoded by a maternal effect gene that is specifically expressed in primordial germ cells, oocytes and pluripotent cells [10, 11]. PGC7 is an intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) that participates in the regulation of epigenetic modifications and the expression of imprinted genes through interactions with various proteins [12]. PGC7 contains a putative SAP-like domainn, which is not only involved in DNA binding, but also has substrate recognition and ligase activity [11, 13]. Investigation into the functions of PGC7 has predominantly concentrated on its regulatory influence on the process of DNA methylation [14]. In oocytes, PGC7 plays a crucial role in preventing abnormal DNA methylation by DNMT1 through the regulation of UHRF1 localization. In the absence of PGC7, both chromatin structure and cytoplasmic factors become defective, which hinders the development of preimplantation embryos [15]. Post-fertilization, PGC7 binds to H3K9me2 in the female pronucleus, safeguarding the maternal genome from the active demethylation process mediated by Tet methylcytosine dioxygenase3 (TET3), particularly in maternal imprint genes [16]. The study revealed that embryos lacking PGC7 often halt at the 2-cell stage and display abnormal expression of imprinted genes controlled by PGC7, but these defects only manifest post-implantation. This suggests that the 2-cell arrest in Pgc7 knockout embryos is not primarily due to imprinting issues [17]. In mice ES cells, the PGC7-interacted proteins were mainly participant in translation [18], but the precise mechanism behind this interaction remains unclear.

In the signaling pathways that regulate mRNA translation, AKT plays a role in controlling the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), which in turn regulates the activity of a translational repressor, the eukaryotic initiation factor 4E (eIF4E) binding protein 1 (4E-BP1) [19, 20]. FSH stimulation is crucial for activating signaling pathways and follicle development, with the PI3K/AKT pathway being preferentially activated before translation. This suggests that PI3K/AKT is involved in FSH-mediated oocyte maturation and is necessary for initiating maternal mRNA translation [21]. Inhibiting AKT activity can impair ZGA and cause embryonic arrest at the 2-cell stage [22], indicating a direct role of AKT in early protein translation regulation, although the exact mechanism remains unclear.

The oocyte-specific RNA-binding proteins have the ability to interact with eIF4E, and are involved in regulating the initiation of translation for maternal RNA [23]. YBX1 (Y-box-binding protein 1), a key translational repressor, was found within the PGC7 interactome and is highly conserved across species [24, 25]. YBX1 in the cytoplasm mainly participates in mRNA-related processes like translation and splicing [26, 27]. Maternal YBX1 suppresses overall translation to protect zebrafish oocyte maturation [28]. This inhibition doesn’t change ribosome transit time, so it doesn’t affect polypeptide chain elongation or termination, only the initiation phase [29, 30]. The function of YBX1 is mainly regulated by the PI3K-AKT1 signaling pathway. AKT1 directly phosphorylates Ser-100 of YBX1, causing its dissociation from target mRNA and releasing repression on eIF4E [31]. This effect is similar to that of 4E-BP1, where YBX1-shielded mRNAs are robustly translated upon activation of the PI3K-AKT1 signaling cascade.

In mice with double knockout of Pgc7, the decrease in fertility and oocyte developmental capacity is related to the translation defects of maternal mRNA. In this study, we confirms that PGC7 plays a role in the translation of maternal mRNA by promoting the AKT1-dependent phosphorylation of YBX1 through protein interactions. PGC7 maintains the activity of AKT1 and supports the phosphorylation of its target proteins. By maintaining the phosphorylation of YBX1-Ser100, PGC7 facilitates the translation of key proteins such as Cyclin B1 and Yap1, which are important for oocyte maturation and the transition to the 2-cell stage of development.

Results

Knockout of PGC7 blocks oocyte maturation and early embryonic development

The expression and localization of PGC7 in oocytes and early embryos were examined using immunofluorescent staining. The findings indicated that PGC7 is present in mouse oocytes and early embryos, with its levels peaking at the GVBD stage and then gradually declining from the MI stage to 8-cell embryos (Fig. 1A and B). Notably, PGC7 was concentrated around chromosomes in GVBD oocytes but exhibited a more diffused intracellular distribution during later stages, except for being predominantly localized to the two pronuclei at the zygotic stage (Fig. 1C). To investigate the role of PGC7 in early development, Pgc7-deficient mice (Pgc7+/−: heterozygous knockdown, Pgc7−/−: homozygous knockout) were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 technique to study PGC7’s function in vivo (sup Fig 1A-1 F, Fig. 1D). The impact of PGC7 loss on oocyte maturation was evaluated in vitro. After 14 h of in vitro maturation culture, the ratio of MII mature oocytes to GV oocytes was determined, and the morphology of MII oocytes was examined. The number of MII-stage oocytes from Pgc7−/− female mice did not significantly differ from WT mice, with approximately 58 oocytes in each group (Fig. 1E). However, Pgc7−/− mice showed around 60% fragmented oocytes at the GV stage and even in MII oocytes within the ampulla of the oviduct, compared to only about 1–2% in WT and Pgc7+/− mice (Fig. 1F and G). These oocytes displayed characteristics such as opaque cytoplasm or loss of the membrane-zona pellucida boundary, indicating that PGC7 deficiency hinders oocyte maturation.

Cumulus-enclosed oocytes with normal appearance were selected for in vitro maturation (IVM). It was observed that approximately 81% of WT oocytes and 74% of Pgc7+/− oocytes reached the MII stage after 14 h of IVM, whereas only 42% (P < 0.001) of Pgc7−/− oocytes matured to the MII stage. Further analysis revealed that Pgc7−/− oocytes were predominantly arrested at the GV (24.33%, P < 0.001) and GVBD (35.52%, P < 0.001) stages (Fig. 1H and I). Subsequently, approximately 84% of embryos derived from WT and Pgc7+/− female mice oocytes advanced to the 4-cell stage after 54 h of in vitro culturing of E0.5 embryos. In contrast, 84.2% of embryos from Pgc7−/− oocytes were arrested at the 2-cell stage, with a minor fraction (about 10.2%) reaching the 4-cell stage but lacking the potential for further development (Fig. 1J and K). Specifically, Pgc7−/− embryos exhibited significant growth retardation at the two to four-cell stages and their development was completely blocked before compaction (Fig. 1L). Additionally, after 24 h of in vitro culturing of E0.5 embryos, a substantial number of abnormal fragmented 2-cell embryos were observed in Pgc7−/− zygotes (Fig. 1M). This finding highlights the irreversible damage to oogenesis caused by Pgc7 deficiency, which impairs the post-fertilization development of oocytes lacking PGC7.

Deletion of PGC7 inhibits maternal mRNA translation

Given the abnormal maturation of Pgc7-/- oocytes, we hypothesized that the expression of Cyclin B1, a key component of mitosis-promoting factor (MPF) essential for MPF activity and meiotic resumption in oocytes, might be inhibited [33]. To test this, we used immunostaining to examine Cyclin B1 protein expression in GVBD oocytes from WT, Pgc7+/-and Pgc7-/- mice. The results showed that Cyclin B1 protein levels were unchanged in WT and Pgc7+/- oocytes but significantly decreased in Pgc7-/- oocytes (Fig. 2A and B), a finding confirmed by Western blotting (sup Fig 1G). Further analysis using qPCR revealed no significant differences in Cyclin b1 mRNA expression among oocytes from WT, Pgc7+/-and Pgc7-/- mice (Fig. 2C), indicating that PGC7 influences Cyclin B1 expression at the translational level.

The findings indicate that PGC7 is involved in the translation of maternal mRNA. To further validate this, an equal number of MII oocytes with a normal phenotype were collected from WT, Pgc7+/-and Pgc7-/- mice, and total maternal protein was extracted for silver staining. Figure 2D shows that the overall maternal protein content in MII oocytes from WT and Pgc7+/- mice was relatively consistent, but significantly reduced in Pgc7-/- oocytes. No defects or differences in maternal protein levels were observed between WT and Pgc7+/- mice, allowing them to be used interchangeably as littermate controls (LMC) in subsequent experiments. Similar results were observed in GVBD oocytes and zygotes from WT and Pgc7-/- mice (Fig. 2E). The translation activity of maternal proteins in GVBD and MII oocytes was also assessed using the Click-iT™ HPG Alexa Fluor™ 488 Protein Synthesis Assay Kit. The HPG incorporation assay revealed that protein translation activity remained unchanged in WT and Pgc7+/- oocytes but was significantly decreased in Pgc7-/- oocytes (Fig. 2F and G), consistent with the findings in Fig. 2D. In GVBD oocytes, chromosomal translational hotspots colocalizing with PGC7 were observed, with a significant decrease in Pgc7-/- oocytes (Fig. 2F). These results suggest a close relationship between PGC7 and protein translation around chromosomes in GVBD oocytes (Fig. 2H). To verify the role of PGC7, we restored its expression in Pgc7-/- GV stage oocytes by microinjection, and the results showed that overexpressed PGC7 could significantly restore protein translation activity (Fig. 2I and J). We also investigated whether PGC7 exhibits similar functionality in somatic cells. Results from sup Fig 1 H and 1I demonstrated that overexpression of exogenous PGC7 in HEK-293T cells (cells with low PGC7 expression) led to a significant increase in protein translation activity, as evidenced by elevated HPG fluorescence intensity in the cytoplasm. This suggests that PGC7 modulates mRNA translation activity not only in specialized germ cells like oocytes but also in somatic cells.

To further explore the impact of maternal mRNA translation on oocyte maturation and early embryonic development, we examined the fluctuation in maternal protein synthesis during these processes using silver staining. Figure 2K shows that maternal protein translation starts abruptly at GVBD and then gradually decreases after fertilization. This pattern is consistent with previous studies that have shown the activation of cap-dependent translation factors during this time [34]. Additionally, Fig. 1A reveals that PGC7 expression is highest at the GVBD stage. By integrating the data from Figs. 1A and 2H, we hypothesize that PGC7 is essential for the initiation of cap-dependent translation, especially during GVBD.

Next, GV-intact oocytes were cultured in cycloheximide (CHX) for 14 h. As expected, CHX significantly suppressed total maternal protein translation compared to DMSO, leading to evident abnormalities in oocyte meiotic progression (sup Fig 1 J). After 14 h of in vitro culture, a lower percentage of CHX-treated oocytes reached metaphase II (7.3%) compared to DMSO group oocytes (Fig. 2L). The majority of oocytes failed to expel the first polar body, with some arrested before GVBD (Fig. 2M, sup Fig 1 K). In zygotes, CHX resulted in complete failure of the first cleavage and subsequent cleavage to 2-cell stage (Fig. 2N, sup Fig 1 L). These findings indicate that global inhibition of translational machinery hinders oocyte maturation and early embryonic development, consistent with the observed phenotype in Pgc7−/− oocytes and zygotes.

Overall, the findings demonstrated the crucial role of PGC7 in facilitating oocyte maturation and early embryonic development by sustaining the translation activity of maternal mRNA.

AKT1 is an vital translation activator specifically regulated by PGC7

The initiation of maternal translation needs the activation of upstream signaling pathways, with the PI3K-AKT1-mTOR and ERK1/2 pathways being major contributors. To understand the impact of PGC7-dependent translational regulation, we analyzed the expression and activation of these pathways. Immunofluorescence and Western blotting showed that deleting PGC7 did not alter the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and mTOR (sup Fig 2 A-2E). This suggests that ERK1/2 and mTOR are not targets of PGC7, indicating another kinase might be involved. Previous studies linked AKT1 kinase to maternal mRNA translation [21]. Immunofluorescence data showed that in oocytes or zygotes, the phosphorylation of AKT1 (p-AKT-473) was reduced in Pgc7+/− oocytes compared to WT, but in Pgc7−/− oocytes, it showed a significant increase (Fig. 3A and B, sup Fig. 2 F). Additionally, fragmented Pgc7−/− oocytes also exhibited elevated AKT1 phosphorylation levels (sup Fig 2G, 2 H). This seemingly contradictory observation suggests that the novel function of PGC7 in maternal mRNA translation activity may be mediated by AKT1 kinase. Notably, p-AKT-473 expression increased from GV-stage oocytes to 8-cell embryos, peaked at the MII stage, and then declined in zygotes. In GVBD oocytes, p-AKT-473 distribution was no longer around chromosomes (Fig. 3C, sup Fig 2I, 2 J). In contrast, p-ERK expression did not change much from GV-stage oocytes to 8-cell embryos (sup Fig 2 K, 2 L).

We next examined the paradox between elevated AKTl activity and developmental defects in Pgc7−/− oocyts. Meiotically competent GV-intact oocytes (prophase I) and zygotes were cultured with varying doses of the AKT1 inhibitor MK2206, and development was monitored at different time intervals. The results showed that MK2206 significantly hindered oocyte maturation in a dose-dependent manner, with higher concentrations causing more severe impairment (Fig. 3D). Additionally, MK2206 delayed both the cleavage of zygotes into 2-cell embryos and blocked zygotic genome activity (ZGA) (Fig. 3E and F). These developmental abnormalities matched those seen in Pgc7−/− oocytes and embryos. To determine if the developmental defects caused by MK2206 were due to impaired maternal mRNA translation, we conducted HPG assays and silver staining. Results from HPG assays and silver staining demonstrated that inhibiting AKT1 activity significantly reduced overall protein translation in GVBD oocytes and embryos particularly in GVBD stage oocytes, treatment with MK2206 led to the disappearance of translational hotspots in the chromosomal region and the surrounding nuclear area, this phenotypic deficiency mirrored observations in Pgc7−/− mice oocytes again (Fig. 3G and I). In contrast, enhancing AKT1 phosphorylation with SC79, an AKT1 activator, significantly increased maternal mRNA translation activity (sup Fig 2 M, 2 N). We then tested whether reactivating AKT activity without PGC7 could rescue the inhibition of maternal translation. The results showed that sustained activation of AKT by EGF or SC79 significantly increased the maturation rate of WT oocytes, but had no effect on Pgc7−/− oocytes (sup Fig 2O). The regulatory role of AKT1 in translational processes was further confirmed in HEK-293T and F9 cells, as shown in sup Fig 2P, 2Q.

The findings collectively suggest that AKT1 is a key downstream kinase of PGC7, involved in the regulation of maternal mRNA translation activity. Although AKT1 itself is not directly involved in protein translation, its main role is to phosphorylate downstream translation regulators [35]. The results in Fig. 3A imply that in Pgc7−/− oocytes, despite increased AKT1 phosphorylation, its kinase activity was blocked, preventing further phosphorylation of AKT1 substrates. To confirm this hypothesis, an equal number of WT, Pgc7+/− and Pgc7−/− MII oocytes were pooled for analysis. Immunofluorescence and Western blotting analyses showed a significant decrease in the phosphorylation of RXRXXS*/T*, the universal substrate motif of AKT1, in Pgc7−/− oocytes (Fig. 3J and L). These results indicate that upon PGC7 knockout, AKT1 loses its ability to phosphorylate certain substrates. Notably, the phosphorylation of RXRXXS*/T* was also reduced in Pgc7+/− oocytes, although the decrease was less pronounced compared to Pgc7−/− oocytes. Additionally, Pgc7+/− mice exhibited normal reproductive capabilities, suggesting that oocytes contain sufficient levels of PGC7, and even half of the PGC7 protein amount is adequate for its full functionality.

Previous studies have characterized PGC7 as an intrinsically unfolded protein involved in various protein interactions [12]. The results in Fig. 3J suggest that PGC7 can enhance the interaction between AKT1 and its substrates. To test this, we performed a pulldown experiment where Flag-AKT1 or Flag-AKT1-T2A-PGC7 were overexpressed in HEK-293T cells. After 48 h, proteins were collected for immunoprecipitation, and the AKT1-interacting proteins (substrates) were identified using silver staining. Figure 3M shows that co-expression of PGC7 with AKT1 results in increased binding of substrate proteins, although this effect was not uniform across all substrates. This key finding underscores the role of PGC7 in promoting the binding of AKT1 to specific substrates.

PGC7 interacts with the CTD domain of AKT1 and mediates the interaction between PP2A, PDK1 and AKT1

We further investigated the intriguing observation that AKT1 phosphorylation showed an inverse pattern in Pgc7+/− and Pgc7−/− oocytes. In F9 cells, knockingdown Pgc7 with siRNA significantly reduced AKT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 4A, sup Fig. 3A), whereas overexpression of PGC7 increased AKT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 4B, sup Fig 3B). Importantly, AKT1 expression remained stable after Pgc7 knockdown or overexpression, indicating that PGC7 regulates AKT1 phosphorylation at the protein interaction level. To explore this, we transfected exogenous Flag-PGC7 into F9 cells for 24 h, then treated cells with LY294002 or MK2206 for 12 h, and assessed changes in endogenous AKT1 phosphorylation via Western blot. As shown in Fig. 4C, AKT1 phosphorylation was inhibited by LY294002 or MK2206, but this inhibition was rescued by overexpressed PGC7. Specifically, PGC7 restored AKT1 phosphorylation inhibited by LY294002 to control levels, and partially reversed the reduction caused by MK2206 (sup Fig 3D). Additionally, Western blot analysis demonstrated that Pgc7 knockdown in F9 cells inhibited IGF-induced AKT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 4D, sup Fig 3 C). These results strongly suggest that PGC7 maintains AKT1 phosphorylation through protein-protein interactions.

The Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) results clearly showed the interaction between AKT1 and PGC7 (Fig. 4E and F). To identify the key domains of AKT1 and PGC7 involved in this interaction, we generated a series of truncated mutants of both proteins (Fig. 4G). The results in Fig. 4H revealed that PGC7 interacts with the CDT domain of AKT1, a crucial kinase domain known for its role in directly phosphorylating downstream substrates. This finding is consistent with the results in Fig. 3L, confirming that PGC7 regulates AKT1 substrate phosphorylation. Additionally, both the CTD and NTD of PGC7 were found to interact with AKT1, suggesting that full-length PGC7 interacts with the CTD domain of AKT1 (Fig. 4I), which is characteristic of intrinsically unfolded proteins. Immunofluorescence results showed a clear colocalization of AKT1 and PGC7 in cells, with PGC7 localized in the cytoplasm and nucleus, and AKT1 primarily in the cytoplasm (Fig. 4J). Co-expression of wild-type PGC7 with AKT1 led to an enrichment of AKT1 around the nucleus. However, mutations in the nuclear localization signal (NLS) of PGC7 (PGC7-KRR) and the nuclear export signal (NES) of PGC7 (PGC7-44/46AA) did not affect the localization of AKT1 (sup Fig. 3E, 3 F). These results indicate that PGC7 not only interacts with AKT1 but also facilitates its perinuclear translocation.

The maintenance of AKT1 phosphorylation is a finely tuned process involving initial phosphorylation by upstream kinases and subsequent dephosphorylation by phosphatases [36]. Given the disordered structure of PGC7, it is hypothesized that PGC7 may influence the interaction between key kinases/phosphatases and AKT1. To test this, Co-IP experiments were performed again to examine the effect of PGC7 on the interactions of PDK1 (3-Phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase 1) and PP2A (Protein Phosphatase 2) with AKT1. The results in Fig. 4K and L show that overexpression of PGC7 in HEK-293T cells enhances the interaction between AKT1 and PDK1, while weakening the interaction between AKT1 and the core subunits of PP2ACa and PP2ACb. These findings suggest that PGC7 not only promotes the interaction between the upstream kinase PDK1 and AKT1 to enhance AKT1 phosphorylation, but also inhibits the interaction between PP2A and AKT1, thereby preventing dephosphorylation. Additionally, a competitive experiment demonstrated that the phosphorylation of AKT1 was significantly reduced upon overexpression of Flag-PP2ACa or Flag-PP2ACb compared to the empty vector (EV), but co-expression with PGC7 restored AKT1 phosphorylation levels (sup Fig. 3G). Overall, these results support the idea that PGC7 can counteract the PP2A-AKT1 interaction to maintain AKT1 activity, explaining the decrease in AKT1 phosphorylation observed in Pgc7+/- oocytes.

Loss of PGC7 activates PIK3CA (Phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha) and inhibits PP2ACb (protein phosphatase 2 catalytic subunit B) expression

In Pgc7−/− oocytes, the phosphorylation of AKT1 was increased, suggesting an unknown role of PGC7 in regulating AKT1 phosphorylation. Further investigation in F9 cells after Pgc7 knockdown revealed that silencing Pgc7 significantly enhanced Pik3ca transcription while inhibiting Pp2a-cb expression (Fig. 4M). Western blot analysis confirmed the correlation between the protein expression levels of PIK3CA and PP2ACB and their mRNA transcription levels (Fig. 4N, sup Fig 3 H). RT-qPCR analysis of Pik3ca and Pp2a-cb expression in WT, Pgc7+/− and Pgc7−/− MII oocytes yielded similar results to those observed in F9 cells (Fig. 4O). Immunofluorescence data also showed a gradual increase in PIK3CA levels and a decrease in PP2A levels with decreasing PGC7 protein abundance (Fig. 4P and Q).

PGC7 maintains phosphorylation of YBX1

AKT1 plays a central role in translation by phosphorylating downstream translation-related factors [31, 37, 38]. YBX1, an important translation inhibitor, was identified in the PGC7 interacting proteome and is a key substrate of AKT1. YBX1 is activated through AKT1-mediated phosphorylation at Ser102 in humans (Ser100 in mice) within the cold shock domain (CSD) [29, 31]. We speculated that PGC7 may regulate translation activity by maintaining the phosphorylation of YBX1.

Immunofluorescence analysis was employed to examine the expression patterns of YBX1 and its phosphorylated form, p-YBX1-Ser100, during oocyte maturation and early embryonic development. YBX1 expression levels increased significantly from the GV to the GVBD stage, remaining relatively constant until the 8-cell stage. Initially, YBX1 was distributed throughout the cytoplasm, but its nuclear presence decreased post-fertilization (sup Fig 3I). The expression of p-YBX1-Ser100 followed a similar trend to YBX1 during oocyte maturation. p-YBX1-Ser100 levels rose sharply after GVBD, reaching a peak at the MII stage, and then decreased following the 2-cell stage. In zygotes, p-YBX1-Ser100 was predominantly localized to the nucleoli, with reduced levels in the cytoplasm (Fig. 5A). Notably, the expression pattern of p-YBX1-Ser100 in MII stage oocytes was consistent with that of p-AKT1 but not p-ERK (sup Fig 2I and 2 K), suggesting that AKT1 is the primary activator of p-YBX1-Ser100 during oocyte maturation. Additionally, the distribution of p-YBX1-Ser100 shifted away from the chromosomes post-GVBD, which contrasts with the localization of PGC7 during this period, implying a role for PGC7 in enhancing YBX1 phosphorylation at this stage (Fig. 5B).

To understand how PGC7 regulates the phosphorylation of YBX1-Ser100, we analyzed the expression of YBX1 and p-YBX1-Ser100 in oocytes and F9 cells following Pgc7 knockdown or knockout. The results from Fig. 5C–E, showed that YBX1 expression remained unchanged in oocytes after Pgc7 manipulation. However, a partial decrease in p-YBX1-Ser100 levels was observed in Pgc7+/− oocytes, and a significant reduction was seen in Pgc7−/− oocytes, with almost no fluorescent signal detected. Additionally, in F9 cells, p-YBX1-Ser100 was also tightly regulated by PGC7. Knockdown of Pgc7 led to inhibition of p-YBX1-Ser100 (Fig. 5F), while overexpression of exogenous PGC7 resulted in enhanced phosphorylation of YBX1-Ser100 (Fig. 5G), but not in the YBX1 S100A mutant (Fig. 5H). These findings suggest that PGC7 plays a crucial role in maintaining the phosphorylation of YBX1-Ser100, primarily through post-translational mechanisms involving protein-protein interactions.

PGC7 promotes the interaction between YBX1 and AKT1 to maintain its phosphorylation

YBX1, a known interacting protein of PGC7 and a significant substrate for AKT1, was hypothesized to act as a downstream factor of PGC7 in the regulation of maternal mRNA translation. The interaction between YBX1 and PGC7 was confirmed through reciprocal Co-IP experiments in HEK-293T and F9 cells (Fig. 6A and B), and similar results were observed using endogenous YBX1 as the bait (Fig. 6C). YBX1 contains a conserved CSD domain that binds RNAs [39], and PGC7 also possesses RNA binding capabilities [40], suggesting that their interaction may be mediated by RNA. However, Co-IP experiments conducted in the presence of RNase A to deplete total RNA revealed that the interaction between YBX1 and PGC7 remained unaffected (Fig. 6D), indicating a direct interaction. To map the specific interaction domain with PGC7, truncated mutants of YBX1 were generated (Fig. 6E). Reciprocal Co-IP analysis revealed that both truncated mutants of YBX1 and PGC7 interacted with the full-length proteins (Fig. 6F and G), suggesting an integral interaction between PGC7 and YBX1. Confocal imaging in NIH-3T3 and F9 cells demonstrated co-localization of PGC7 and YBX1, with YBX1 predominantly localized in the cytoplasm and potentially involved in mRNA translation. Additionally, overexpression of PGC7 did not alter the intracellular distribution of endogenous YBX1 (sup Fig. 4A).

AKT1, a well-known upstream regulator of YBX1, interacts with PGC7, a protein that contains a putative SAP-like domain known for its substrate recognition and ligase activity. Non-substrate proteins such as PGC7 can also form tertiary complexes to direct the interaction between kinase and substrate. PGC7 interacts with YBX1 and the CTD of AKT1 (Figs. 4H and 6F), all above results indicating that PGC7 may promotes the interaction between AKT1 and YBX1. To further investigate this hypothesis, we conducted experiments in F9 cells, confirming that endogenous YBX1 can indeed be captured by AKT1 (Fig. 6H). Additionally, reciprocal Co-IP experiments in HEK-293T cells demonstrated that the PGC7-YBX1-AKT1 complex can be mutually captured. Notably, when immunoprecipitating with HA-tagged PGC7, both YBX1 and AKT1 were found to co-precipitate, providing further evidence for the interaction between these proteins (Fig. 6I).

Confocal microscopy was -utilized to identify the co-localization of YBX1-PGC7-AKT1 in F9 and NIH-3T3 cells. YBX1 exhibited a clear cytoplasmic distribution in both cell lines, while AKT1 was found in the inner membrane of F9 cells and uniformly distributed in NIH-3T3 cells. YBX1 and AKT1 co-localized in the cytoplasm, with PGC7 showing a similar distribution (sup Fig 4B). These results suggest that YBX1-PGC7-AKT1 co-localizes and functions in the cytoplasm, which is the site of mRNA translation.

We further explored the effects of PGC7 overexpression and the AKT1 inhibitor LY294002 on the YBX1-AKT1 interaction through Co-IP experiments. As expected, LY294002, which reduces AKT1 phosphorylation, enhanced the binding of un-phosphorylated AKT1 to YBX1. Additionally, the overexpression of GFP-PGC7 significantly strengthened the YBX1-AKT1 protein interaction (Fig. 6J).

Using NanoBRET™ technology, which facilitates sensitive and reproducible detection of protein interactions within native cellular contexts [41]. We further confirmed this pivotal discovery. The NanoBRET results demonstrated that PGC7 enhances the interaction between AKT1 and YBX1 in living cells (Fig. 6K), aligning with the findings presented in Fig. 6J. Conversely, knocking down Pgc7 in F9 cells led to a reduced interaction between YBX1 and AKT1 (Fig. 6L). Further experiments revealed that a modest overexpression of Flag-PGC7 increased the phosphorylation of YBX1-Ser100 without altering AKT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 6M). Moreover, the addition of LY294002 decreased YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation, but this effect was reversed by pre-treatment with overexpressed Flag-PGC7. This suggests that PGC7 can enhance YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation by strengthening YBX1’s interaction with a small amount of phosphorylated AKT1 in the presence of LY294002 (Fig. 6M).

In conclusion, our findings support the notion that PGC7 enhances the AKT1-YBX1 interaction and ensures YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation by AKT1. These results also confirm that YBX1 is a substrate involved in the regulation of maternal mRNA translation controlled by the PGC7-AKT1 axis.

Targeting YBX1-Ser100 with fisetin represses maternal mRNA translation

To elucidate the role of p-YBX1-Ser100 in maternal mRNA translation, we employed Fisetin, a specific inhibitor of YBX1-Ser100, which targets residues on the β1-β4 strands of the CSD, thereby disrupting the interaction between AKT1 and YBX1. Initially, we examined the effects of Fisetin and MK2206 on YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation in F9 cells. Western blot analysis revealed that inhibiting AKT phosphorylation with MK2206 significantly reduced YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation, while Fisetin also suppressed YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation without impacting YBX1 transcription (sup Fig 4 C). Moreover, we observed a substantial decrease in p-YBX1-Ser100 levels in Fisetin-treated oocytes (Fig. 7A and B). To investigate the impact of YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation on oocyte maturation, we inhibited p-YBX1-Ser100 with Fisetin and assessed the in vitro maturation rate. As expected, the maturation rate of oocytes significantly decreased after 14 h of treatment with 70 µM Fisetin, with the majority of arrested oocytes remaining at the GV stage (81.2% vs. 10.5%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 7C and D). Additionally, we also evaluated the effect of Fisetin on early embryonic development, which resulted in arrest at the 2-cell stage (98.73% vs. 0.13%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 7E and F). However, after removing Fisetin and switching to normal culture conditions after 6 h, some embryos that were initially blocked at the 2-cell stage were able to progress to the 4-cell stage (0.13% vs. 25.92%, P < 0.001), although not to the same extent as observed in the DMSO group (25.92% vs. 95.02%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 7G).

The developmental arrest of oocytes and embryos induced by Fisetin was found to be consistent with the knockout of Pgc7 and inhibition of AKT1. To further investigate the impact of Fisetin on maternal mRNA translation, HPG fluorescent staining was performed on oocytes and 2-cell embryos immediately after treatment with Fisetin at specified stages. The results revealed that compared to the DMSO group, Fisetin significantly hindered maternal protein synthesis, as indicated by HPG fluorescent staining (Fig. 7H and I). Interestingly, none of the oocytes treated with Fisetin underwent GVBD. Furthermore, the relevance of p-YBX1-Ser100 to protein translation was confirmed in different somatic cells. The results from sup Fig 4D and 4E demonstrated a notable reduction in nascent protein synthesis in HEK-293T and F9 cells after 12 h of Fisetin treatment.

Chemical small-molecule inhibitors may have non-specific and toxic effects. To address these limitations, YBX1-Ser100A mutants were generated using PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis. Mutations were introduced into the pcDNA3-mRuby2-YBX1 plasmid to create pcDNA3-mRuby2-YBX1-Ser100A. As expected, the YBX1-Ser100A mutants significantly hindered maternal protein synthesis in GVBD oocytes (Fig. 7J and K). Additionally, maternal protein synthesis was also inhibited in oocytes still in the GV phase (sup Fig 4 F, 4G). These findings were further confirmed in HEK-293T cells, showing similar results to those observed with Fisetin treatment in GV oocytes or somatic cells (sup Fig 4 H, 4I). Overall, the data presented demonstrate the critical role of the YBX1-Ser100 site in protein translation in oocytes and cells.

PGC7 disrupts the interaction between YBX1 and eIF4E

Previous studies have demonstrated that the interaction between YBX1 and translation initiation factor eIF4E inhibits cap-dependent translation initiation. Phosphorylation of YBX1-Ser100 by AKT1 negatively regulates its cap-dependent repressor activity, promoting translation initiation by the eIF4E complex on the mRNA [31, 42]. PGC7 facilitates the phosphorylation of YBX1 at Ser-100 through AKT1, indicating that disrupting the YBX1-eIF4E interaction is a pivotal mechanism by which PGC7 promotes translational activity. To confirm this hypothesis, a Co-IP assay was conducted to re-examine the YBX1-eIF4E interaction. The results demonstrated a direct interaction between YBX1 and eIF4E in vitro in HEK-293T and F9 cells (Fig. 8A and B). Subsequent Co-IP experiments confirmed the interaction following AKT phosphorylation or PGC7 overexpression. Notably, activation of AKT1 phosphorylation by IGF led to YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation and a subsequent decrease in the YBX1-eIF4E interaction (Fig. 8C). Further analysis revealed that PGC7 overexpression increased YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation and also inhibited the YBX1-eIF4E interaction, similar to the effects of IGF treatment (Fig. 8D and E). These findings support the idea that PGC7-induced phosphorylation of YBX1-Ser100 inhibits its interaction with eIF4E, highlighting the significance of YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation in this interaction.

PGC7 maintains the translation of YAP1 by promoting the YBX1 phosphorylation

Prior studies have highlighted the role of YAP1 in early embryonic development, emphasizing its importance during the oocyte stage for ZGA [43–45]. Analysis of RIP-seq data for YBX1 revealed Yap1 as a target mRNA [46], prompting the hypothesis that Pgc7 knockout might cause developmental arrest at the 2-cell stage by inhibiting Yap1 translation. This could represent a novel mechanism by which PGC7 influences ZGA. To investigate this, RIP-PCR was used to assess YBX1’s binding to Yap1 mRNA, confirming a direct interaction (Fig. 9A). YBX1 typically binds to mRNAs to prevent their association with the translation initiation complex, and the inhibition of YBX1 Ser100 phosphorylation disrupts the translation of YBX1-bound mRNAs. Examination of Yap1 expression in Pgc7−/− oocytes revealed that while Yap1 mRNA levels were unchanged, YAP1 protein expression was significantly reduced (Fig. 9B and C). Immunofluorescence studies further supported this observation (Fig. 9D and E). These results indicate that PGC7 regulates YAP1 expression at the translational level.

Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in YAP1 expression following treatment with MK2206 (Fig. 9F and G and sup Fig 5 A, 5B) and Fisetin (Fig. 9H and I). To examine whether the PGC7-AKT1-YBX1-YAP1 axis operates similarly in somatic cells, we knocked-down Pgc7 in F9 cells. Western blot analysis revealed a significant reduction in p-AKT-Ser473 and p-YBX1-Ser100 levels, as well as a marked decrease in YAP1 protein expression (Fig. 9J). Additionally, treatment of F9 cells with Fisetin or MK2206 for 24 h resulted in a significant inhibition of YAP1 protein expression (Fig. 9K). As shown in Fig. 2A, Cyclin B1 translation was inhibited in Pgc7−/− oocytes. sup Fig 5 C demonstrated that, similar to YAP1 protein, Cyclin B1 expression was also significantly suppressed by Fisetin in oocytes. This suggests that PGC7 regulates Cyclin B1 translation in a manner analogous to YAP1.

Discussion

The regulation of maternal mRNA during embryonic development involves various processes, including Poly(A) lengthening, translation, degradation and methylation modification. While transcriptional regulation is the primary mechanism for gene expression in most cells, oocytes, especially during maturation, rely heavily on translational regulation [47]. The timely and accurate translation of maternal mRNA is crucial, as it serves as the primary source of materials for oocyte maturation and early embryonic development [48, 49]. Maternal factors, maternal eukaryotic translation initiation factors (eIFs), and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) collaborate to regulate the translation of nascent proteins [8, 50–52].

The distribution of PGC7 in the cytoplasm in cells and/or oocyte cells suggests a different function (Fig. 1A). Given that protein translation occurs in the cytoplasm and PGC7 is also present in this cellular compartment, it is plausible that PGC7’s function in the cytoplasm is related to protein translation. Our study also revealed that, in contrast to WT and Pgc7+/- mice, approximately 65% of follicular oocytes in Pgc7-/- mice were fragmented, and Pgc7-/- embryos exhibited extensive apoptosis and 2-cell arrest (Fig. 1K and L). These findings suggest that the impaired oocyte maturation due to Pgc7 knockout is the primary cause of the 2-cell arrest.

The decreased expression of Cyclin B1 in Pgc7-/- oocytes indicates that meiotic abnormalities are partially caused by the inhibition of Cyclin B1 translation (Fig. 2A). Additionally, the developmental defects induced by CHX align with the phenotype observed in Pgc7-/- oocytes. Overexpression of exogenous PGC7 in HEK-293T cells significantly enhances translation activity, supporting the hypothesis that PGC7 plays a role in regulating the translation activity of maternal mRNA, which may not be limited to germ cells.

Notably, our study revealed a novel observation that PGC7 is positioned in close proximity to chromosomes during GVBD, aligning with HPG staining during this phase (Fig. 2H). Conversely, in Pgc7-/-oocytes, the translation of maternal protein was markedly suppressed, leading to the disappearance of the translation hotspot at the GVBD stage. This outcome reinforces the link between the specific localization of PGC7 during GVBD and protein synthesis, particularly within the chromosomal translational area (CTA). Research indicates the presence of translation hotspots within oocytes, known as the chromosomal translational area (CTA), primarily regulated by the mTOR-eIF4F pathway. This spatial organization of maternal mRNA translation enhances the production of specific proteins at their designated sites and minimizes errors in other cellular regions [20]. The above results indicate that PGC7 may play a crucial role in the initiation of maternal mRNA translation during the GVBD stage.

We have previously demonstrated that PGC7 does not interact with ERK1/2 [53]. The results from sup Fig 2 A and 2D also indicated that the phosphorylation levels of the vital kinases mTORC1 and ERK1/2 in Pgc7−/− oocytes did not significantly differ from those in WT and Pgc7+/− oocytes, while the phosphorylation of AKT1 exhibited significant fluctuations. Studies have shown that the deletion of Rptor, an essential mTORC1 component in oocytes, results in the loss of mTORC1 signaling, yet the follicular development and fertility of mice remain unaffected. This is attributed to the compensatory activity of PI3K-AKT1 signaling that maintains normal follicular development and fertility [54]. These findings suggest that AKT1, although one of the upstream kinases of mTORC1, has its unique pathway of action. The developmental defects observed with MK2206 were consistent with the phenotype of Pgc7−/− oocytes (Figs. 1E and H and 3H and J). HPG staining confirmed that inhibition of AKT1 significantly hindered maternal mRNA translation during GVBD in oocytes, leading to the disappearance of protein translation hotspots around chromosomes. This outcome aligns with the essential role of AKT1 activity in the formation of translation hotspots during GVBD.

The phosphorylation of AKT1 in oocytes from WT, Pgc7+/−, and Pgc7−/− mice exhibited distinct patterns (Fig. 3A). Despite the apparent complexity of these results, our study demonstrated that while p-AKT1 levels were significantly higher in Pgc7−/− oocytes, HPG staining was notably reduced. More importantly, the phosphorylation of AKT1’s common substrate (P-RXRXXS/T) was significantly lower in Pgc7−/− oocytes (Fig. 3J). This suggests a disruption in the transmission of AKT1 activity to translational regulators, leading to impaired translation of maternal mRNA. Substrate capture experiments with AKT1 revealed that PGC7 could enhance the enrichment of substrate proteins, although this effect was not universal and only targeted a specific subset of substrates (Fig. 3M). The influence of PGC7 on various substrates may depend on the structural characteristics of the substrate. Upon deletion of Pgc7, the phosphorylation of AKT1 itself increased; however, AKT1 lost its ability to continuously phosphorylate downstream substrates. This mechanism explains the contradiction between the significant increase in AKT1 phosphorylation in Pgc7−/− oocytes and the observed abnormalities in oocyte maturation and early embryonic development, which are consistent with the outcome of AKT1 inhibition.

In our study, we have confirmed that the entire region of PGC7 binds to the C-terminal domain (CTD) of AKT1, encompassing the critical phosphorylation sites T308 and S473. The reduction in AKT1 phosphorylation induced by LY294002 or MK2206 can be reversed by the overexpression of PGC7 (Fig. 4C). This suggests that PGC7 interacts with specific kinases or phosphatases that regulate AKT1 activity. The balance of AKT1 phosphorylation is maintained by both PDK1-mediated initial phosphorylation and PP2A-mediated dephosphorylation. Increased expression of PGC7 not only enhances the interaction between AKT1 and PDK1 but also inhibits the interaction between AKT1 and PP2A, thereby collectively sustaining AKT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 4K and L). While the AKT1 phosphorylation levels decreased in Pgc7+/− oocytes, but surprisingly, it significantly increased in Pgc7−/− oocytes. This suggests that the upstream regulatory factors of AKT1 were activated following the knockout of Pgc7.

Oocyte immunofluorescence results demonstrated that the expression patterns of PIK3CA and PP2A were consistent with those observed in F9 cells. These changes in expression levels of PIK3CA and PP2A appeared to “activate” AKT1. Our interpretation of this phenomenon is as follows: the decrease in PGC7 protein quantity led to a rapid decline in the phosphorylation of AKT1, which is mainly controlled by the level of interaction. As protein interaction-mediated regulation of AKT1 phosphorylation is more efficient than transcriptional regulation, the upregulation of PIK3CA could not promptly and adequately compensate for the decrease in AKT1 phosphorylation caused by the loss of interaction, leading to an overall decrease in AKT1 phosphorylation in Pgc7+/− oocytes. However, the complete deletion of PGC7 eliminates the maintenance effect of protein interaction, allowing the upregulation of PIK3CA to gradually activate AKT1 phosphorylation at the transcriptional level. Consequently, AKT1 phosphorylation ultimately increases in a reverse manner. But what we cannot ignore is that PGC7 also played a crucial role in facilitating the interaction between AKT1 and some of its substrates. As a result, while the phosphorylation of AKT1 increased in Pgc7−/− oocytes, the phosphorylation of its substrates was inhibited. In summary, PGC7 plays a critical role in modulating the function of AKT1, with the upregulation of AKT1 phosphorylation being null in Pgc7−/− mouse oocytes.

YBX1 exhibits a robust interaction with PGC7. This interaction not only strengthens the association between YBX1 and AKT1 but also elevates the levels of phosphorylated YBX1 at Ser100. PGC7 acts as a facilitator for the interaction between AKT1 and YBX1, thereby ensuring the effective transmission of AKT1 activity to YBX1. Our study also revealed that the YBX1 and AKT1 phosphorylation levels increase during oocyte maturation, peaking at MII stage (sup Fig 2I, Fig. 5A). Although ERK1/2 kinase can also phosphorylate YBX1-Ser100 [55, 56], immunofluorescence results suggest that ERK1/2 kinase activity remains stable during oocyte maturation, particularly from GVBD to MII (sup Fig 2 K). This implies that ERK1/2 kinase may not be responsible for YBX1-Ser100 phosphorylation during oocyte maturation. The concurrent phosphorylation of YBX1 and AKT1 indicates that AKT1 directly phosphorylates YBX1, establishing YBX1 as a downstream translational factor of PGC7. The p-YBX1-Ser100 is indicative of translation activity. During GVBD, the enrichment of p-YBX1-Ser100 around chromosomes diminishes, with PGC7 and p-AKT1 showing preferential distribution. The lack of colocalization of translation hotspots with p-YBX1-Ser100 suggests a close relationship between the occurrence of initial translation hotspots and YBX1.

Fisetin significantly impeded oocyte maturation, early embryonic development and maternal mRNA translation (Fig. 7D, F and H). This developmental anomaly was also observed in Pgc7−/− oocytes and those exposed to the AKT1 inhibitor MK2206. When the YBX1-Ser100A mutant was microinjected into GV oocytes or transfected into HEK-293T cells, HPG staining indicated a significant inhibition of mRNA translation. These findings further support the notion that YBX1 acts as a pivotal translational inhibitor downstream of the PGC7-AKT1 axis, with phosphorylation of YBX1 at Ser100 being crucial for the translational activity of maternal mRNA.

The cap-dependent repressor function of YBX1 is negatively modulated by AKT1-mediated phosphorylation of YBX1-Ser100. In its unphosphorylated state, YBX1 interacts with eIF4E to inhibit translation initiation, but phosphorylation of YBX1-Ser100 leads to the dissociation of this interaction and initiation of translation, a process requiring AKT1 phosphorylation [31, 57]. Activation of AKT1 by IGF or exogenous PGC7 disrupts the interaction between p-YBX1-Ser100 and eIF4E (Fig. 8C and D).

The LACE-seq analysis identified (Ccnb1) Cyclin b1 and Yap1 are common target of both eIF4E1B and eIF4E [58, 59], which are also targeted by YBX1. In Pgc7−/− oocytes, Cyclin B1 and YAP1 protein expression were significantly decreased. These results suggest that the reduced translation of these key proteins may contribute to oocyte maturation arrest and 2-cell stage arrest in the absence of PGC7.

PGC7, a protein that is prominently expressed in oocytes, also holds a pivotal role in the preservation of embryonic stem cell pluripotency. This function underscores the significance of maternal factors in early developmental processes. (Ref: Dppa3 Improves the Germline Competence of Pluripotent Stem Cells., 2023’ and ‘Maternal Factor Dppa3 Activates 2C-Like Genes and Depresses DNA Methylation in Mouse Embryonic Stem Cells. 2022) Although this study involves a large mechanistic studies, our work also has limitations. For example, there is no link between early reproductive defects and PGC7. Maternal obesity is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes and miscarriage. Researchers have discovered that the embryonic defects observed in obese mice are attributed to a deficiency of PGC7 in oocytes [60]. In light of the findings from our current study, we propose that the developmental anomalies induced by obesity may stem from disorders in maternal mRNA translation processes. In our subsequent research endeavors, we aim to delve deeper into the interplay between obesity and maternal mRNA translation, thereby elucidating the broader implications of PGC7 in the context of early embryonic development.

Conclusions

From the germinal vesicle (GV) stage to zygotic activity in the early embryo, oocytes decrease genome-wide transcriptional activity and rely on translational regulation to control gene expression. Understanding maternal mRNA translation in oocytes is key to comprehending oocyte maturation, essential for enhancing fertility and investigating infertility causes.

This study focuses on Pgc7 knockout oocytes and reveals that AKT1 operates via a PGC7-dependent pathway, distinct from the classical PI3K/AKT1/mTORC1 pathway. The interaction between PGC7 and AKT1 maintains AKT1 activity, enhances AKT1-YBX1 interaction, leading to increased p-YBX-Ser100 levels. This interaction also results in YBX1 dissociation from translation initiation factor eIF4E, activating the translation of maternal Cyclin B1 and YAP1.

In conclusion, the above findings uncover an oocyte-specific role of PGC7 in regulating the translation initiation of individual mRNAs. Moreover, this study not only expands the biological function of PGC7, but also provides new ideas for the treatment of fertility disorders. (Fig. 9L).

Materials and methods

Animals and ethics statement

Wild-type KM mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center, Air Force Medical University and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Animal Research Committee guidelines of Northwest A&F University, and housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility with a 12 h light/dark cycle at a controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C), humidity of 40–60%, and ad libitum access to water and food. Pgc7+/− and Pgc7−/− mice were generated using CRISPR-Cas9 system as illustrated in sup Figure 1A. The detailed process is shown in previous studies [61]. All animal protocols were performed in accordance with the approval of the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Northwest A&F University, Yang Ling, China (No. 20200523).

Superovulation, fertilization and Oocyte/embroys culture

The detailed operation steps have been described in our previous study [61]. Select GV stage oocytes that are not completely enveloped by granulosa cells, exhibit distinct nuclei, and maintain a uniform size. These oocytes should be matured in M2 culture medium following their removal from the ovaries. After dissecting the ampulla of the fallopian tube, transfer the cumulus-oocyte complexes to 0.1% hyaluronidase (HY) drops for the digestion of granulosa cells. Subsequently, the oocytes should be transferred to a new M2 culture solution. Select MII stage oocytes that are uniform in size, possess first polar bodies, and have clear cytoplasm for further culture. The procedure for obtaining fertilized eggs mirrors that of the MII stage oocytes. Following a 4-h maturation period in KSOM culture medium, the fertilized eggs with visible pronuclei are cultured to the early embryonic stage.

In vitro transcription and microinjection of oocytes

To prepare mRNAs pcDNA3-mRuby2-YBX1 or pcDNA3-mRuby2-YBX1S100A for microinjection, expression vectors were linearized and subjected to phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation. The linearized vectors were in vitro transcribtion and added with poly(A) tails using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE™ T7 ULTRA Kit, For specific steps, please refer to the instruction manual (Invitrogen, AM1345). The concentration of all injected RNAs was adjusted to 500 ng/uL, then the diluted RNA was prepared by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was aspirated at a working concentration of 20 µM. About 50 to 60 GV stage oocytes were placed in M2 medium with 2.5 µM milrinone. 2 µL of RNA was injected using a Microloader (Eppendorf) needle. After the injection, the oocytes were harvested and cultured in M2 medium containing 2.5 µM milrinone for 12–14 h. After that, the oocytes were washed 4 times in M2 medium without milrinone, and then cultured in M2 medium without milrinone for mature culture. HPG staining was performed 16 h later. The detailed operation steps of microinjection have been described in previous study [62, 63].

Cell culture and transfection

F9 EC cells and HEK-293T cells were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). All cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Transfections were performed by using Lipofectamine 2000 (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All cell culture reagents were purchased from Gibco (Carlsbad, USA) unless indicated.

Construction of plasmids

The coding sequences of the Pgc7, Akt1, Ybx1 and eif4E genes were amplified from F9 EC cells and then were cloned into pCMV-HA, pEGFP-C1 or p3*Flag-CMV-10 vectors (Lab preserved). The truncated Pgc7, AKT11 and Ybx1 vectors named HA-YBX1-CSD, HA-YBX1-CTD, Flag-PGC7-NTD, Flag-PGC7-CTD, HA-AKT1-CTD and HA-AKT1-PH were use full-length Pgc7, Ybx1 and Akt1 as templates. The mutation at amino acid of YBX1Ser100 was obtained by overlapping extended PCR. The mutation was also introduced into the pcDNA3-mRuby2-YBX1 plasmid to create pcDNA3-mRuby2-YBX1-Ser100A, an in vitro transcribed and polyadenylated mCherry mRNA was co-microinjected as a positive control. These vectors were constructed using standard molecular cloning methods and confirmed by sequencing. All the primers used for construction of the above plasmids are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA interference

Short interfering RNAs (siRNAs) that target mice Pgc7 and negative control siRNA-NC were purchased from Shanghai GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The RNA interference sequences were as follows: si-NC: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′, si-Pgc7-1: 5-GCACAACGAUCCAGAUUUA-3, si-Pgc7-2: 5-GCACAACGAUCCAGAUUUA. F9 ECs were transfected with the indicated siRNAs (50 nM final concentration) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Life Technologies Inc, Carlsbad, CA). The efficiency of transfection was confirmed by using Western blot analysis after 48 h.

Reverse transcription PCR and quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from treated cells using Trizol Reagent (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Please refer to our previous publications for the technical details [64]. All primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are shown in the Supplementary Table S2.

Western blot analysis

For a detailed procedure please refer to our previous publication [64]. The relative amount of each protein was quantified with Image J software. And GAPDH proteins were used for normalization. Details on the primary antibodies and secondary antibodies for WB are shown in the Supplementary Table S3.

Co-immunoprecipitation and pull-down experiments

For a detailed procedure please refer to our previous publication [64]. For the Co-IP experiment with RNase A treatment, 20 µg/mL of RNase A was added to cell lysis and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody. The protein molecular mass encoded by pCMV-HA-YBX1 could be about 50 kDa, HA-YBX1-CSD could be about 20 kDa. To avoid overlapping signals of IgG heavy chain or light chain, a HRP conjugated light-chain specific or heavy-chain specific secondary antibody was used for WB.

NanoBRET™ (Nano-bioluminescence resonance energy transfer) assay

Using the NanoBRET® Nano-Glo® Detection Systems (N2585) kit to detect protein interactions in living cells. Mouse AKT1 and YBX1 were cloned in NLuc and HaloTag vectors, respectively. HEK-293T cells, grown in six-well plates at a density of 2 × 106 cells/well. The two vectors were mixed at a ratio of 1:10 and transfected into HEK-293T cells with Flag-EV or plasmid Flag-PGC7, respectively. After 6 h, the cells were trypsinyzed and plated on black 96-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 104 cells/well for 24 h. One day before measurement, the cells were incubated in DMEM without Phenol Red. HaloTag NanoBRET 618 Ligand (100 nmol/l final concentration) were added 16 h before final determinations. Nano-Glo Substrate was added for 30 min and donor emission (460 nm) and acceptor emission (618 nm) were measured in a Tecan Infinite E Plex microplate reader (TECAN). The final values were calculated as ratio of 618 nm/460 nm after subtraction of the negative control values. Quantification of four independent experiments. The specific measurement and calculation methods refer to the product manual of the NanoBRET® Nano-Glo® Detection Systems (N2585) kit. See Supplementary Table S1 for details of primer sequence.

Confocal microscopy and immunofluorescence staining

The detailed operation steps have been described in our previous study [61]. The observation and photography were conducted using an Olympus multifunction microscope (Olympus BX51) or Olympus confocal microscope (FV1200). The antibodies utilized in this study have undergone validation through IF and have been confirmed as suitable for IF detection. Details on the primary antibodies and secondary antibodies for IF are shown in the Supplementary Table S3.

Protein synthesis efficiency

HPG signals were detected using a Click-iT®HPG Alexa Fluor®Protein Synthesis Assay Kit (Life Technologies). Mean intensity of the HPG signal was measured across the global of each oocyte and quantified using ImageJ software. At least 8 oocytes/embryos were counted in each experimental group and at least three mice were used in each group. For cells, at least 6 fields for each group were counted to Mean intensity of the HPG signal.

Silver staining

The protein samples were analyzed by 10% SDS-PAGE and visualized by Fast Silver Stain Kit (P0017s, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RIP-qPCR

F9 EC cells were removed from culture 100 mm plates and washed with cold RNase free PBS three times. The subsequent detailed experiment was performed according to the published literature [27, 65] and Magna RIP™ RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit instructions (Millipore, Billerica, MA). Co-precipitated RNAs were subjected to RT‐qPCR analysis.

Treatment with inhibitors

Six-well plate cells were used as an example. The cells were prepared for treatment when they reached 50–60% confluence. Two sterile 4 mL centrifuge tubes were prepared, 2 mL of fresh culture medium was added, and then the inhibitor and the same volume of control DMSO (unless otherwise specified, the inhibitor treatment control group was its diluent DMSO) were respectively blown and mixed. The medium in the culture plate was discarded, and the cells were washed once with PBS and replaced with medium containing inhibitors or DMSO. The cells were returned to the incubator for further culture. WB or IF tests were performed after 6–24 h of treatment according to the experimental purpose. Details on the inhibitors are shown in the Supplementary Table S4.

Statistical analysis

The data in graphs are expressed as the mean ± SD. Two-tailed student’s t-tests were carried out for the comparison of two groups using GraphPad Prism. A significance level of p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001 were denoted by one, two, and three asterisks, respectively, NS, not significant. The error bars on the Figures represent standard deviation (SD) of at least three biological replicates. Quantified data are normalized to the control group (the control group value is equal to 1).

Details on the abbreviations are shown in the Supplementary Table S5.
