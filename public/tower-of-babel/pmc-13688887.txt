Exploring the feasibility and educational value of an immersive situational teaching method in a preclinical pathology observation program
Authors: Chun-Hua Wang, Mei Li, Jing-Dun Xie, Li-Li Liu, Peng Sun, Jing-Ping Yun, Mu-Yan Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly exposure to pathology plays an important role in connecting foundational biomedical knowledge with clinical practice. However, traditional lecture-based teaching may provide limited opportunities for students to experience authentic diagnostic processes and appreciate the clinical relevance of pathology. This study aimed to explore the feasibility and perceived educational value of an Immersive Situational Teaching Method (ISTM)-based preclinical pathology observation program.MethodsA single-center, descriptive educational innovation study was conducted in the Department of Clinical Pathology at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. A total of 41 junior medical students participating in two consecutive summer preclinical observation programs (July–August 2022 and July–August 2023) completed a structured questionnaire evaluating their learning experiences and perceptions. The questionnaire was adapted from previously reported medical education instruments and demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.87). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize learner satisfaction and self-reported educational outcomes.ResultsThe ISTM-based program was highly accepted by participants, with 92.6% of students reporting satisfaction or high satisfaction. Students reported enhanced understanding of pathological diagnostic workflows, improved integration of basic medical knowledge with clinical practice, and increased awareness of the professional role of pathologists. Qualitative feedback further indicated that the immersive experience improved learning motivation and promoted appreciation of the relationship between pathology and patient care. No objective assessments of knowledge acquisition, diagnostic skills, or clinical competency were performed.ConclusionsImmersive situational teaching is a feasible and well-received approach for pre-clinical pathology education, offering a structured opportunity for early clinical exposure. Findings are limited by the single-arm, self-report design and lack of objective outcome measures. Further studies incorporating comparative designs and competency-based assessments are warranted to validate these preliminary observations.Keywords: Immersive situational teaching method, Medical students, Pre-clinical observation learning, Pathology education, Educational innovation, Questionnaire survey

Background

Pathology is a fundamental component of medical education, bridging basic biomedical sciences and clinical practice by providing essential knowledge for understanding disease mechanisms, diagnosis, and patient management [1]. However, traditional pathology education remains largely lecture-based, with limited opportunities for active engagement, contextual learning, and the development of clinical reasoning and problem-solving skills [2]. Innovative teaching approaches that promote experiential learning and facilitate the integration of theoretical knowledge with clinical practice are therefore needed.

Immersive and situational learning approaches have gained increasing attention in medical education by emphasizing active participation, contextual experience, and knowledge construction. The concept of deep engagement is closely related to flow theory proposed by Csikszentmihalyi, which describes focused involvement and intrinsic motivation during challenging activities [3]. Building on these principles, the Immersive Situational Teaching Method (ISTM) integrates authentic clinical scenarios and experiential learning to enhance learner engagement and facilitate knowledge application. Preclinical practice, as an early clinical exposure experience for junior medical students, provides an ideal context for applying immersive educational strategies and connecting foundational pathology with clinical reasoning [4].

Pathology is particularly suitable for immersive learning because accurate pathological diagnosis is central to clinical decision-making and patient care. Early exposure to diagnostic workflows enables students to appreciate that each pathology report represents an individual patient, thereby fostering an understanding of the pathologist’s responsibility to pursue diagnostic accuracy and provide timely, clinically meaningful information [5]. Contemporary medical education increasingly emphasizes the cultivation of professional values, including respect for life, empathy, and accountability [6].

Despite growing interest in immersive learning across medical disciplines, its application in preclinical pathology education remains underexplored. Few studies have investigated how immersive approaches can integrate pathological knowledge, clinical thinking, and professional development. Therefore, this study implemented an ISTM-based curriculum within a preclinical observation learning program in the clinicopathological department and evaluated its feasibility and educational value from the perspective of learner experiences and satisfaction.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-center, cross-sectional educational intervention study conducted at the Department of Clinical Pathology, Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC). The study aimed to evaluate the impact of an immersive situational teaching method (ISTM) on junior medical students’ learning experience and professional development during pre-clinical pathology practice.

Participants

Between July–August 2022 and July–August 2023, 41 junior medical students from the Zhongshan School of Medicine, Sun Yat-sen University, voluntarily participated in a summer pre-clinical pathology practice program. Participants included 23 males and 18 females, aged 19–22 years, comprising 19 students from the five-year clinical medicine program and 22 from the eight-year clinical medicine program. All students had completed basic pathology coursework but had limited prior exposure to clinical pathology workflows. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Intervention: immersive situational teaching method in pre-clinical pathology

The ISTM-based program was developed in accordance with the institutional educational philosophy of “Three Fundamentals, Three Stricts, and Three Early” [7, 8]. The curriculum integrated five core components: learners, instructors, learning tasks and resources, the teaching environment, and the pathology diagnostic process. Instructional strategies included scenario simulation, role-playing, interactive discussion, and real-time feedback.

Key learning stations encompassed gross specimen sampling, slide preparation, immunohistochemistry, microscopic slide interpretation, and intraoperative frozen section consultation. Students actively participated in daily pathology routines, including macroscopic specimen examination, microscopic analysis using multi-headed teaching microscopes, and correlation of morphological findings with clinical data. Teaching faculty consisted of board-certified attending pathologists with at least three years of diagnostic experience and formal training in medical education.

Instrument: questionnaire design and validation

A structured questionnaire was developed to assess students’ perceptions of the immersive pre-clinical pathology program. The instrument was adapted from previously validated questionnaires in medical education and aligned with the Kirkpatrick model for evaluating educational interventions [9, 10]. It comprised four domains:

Demographic information;

Demographic information;

Perceived quality of pre-clinical practice;

Perceived quality of pre-clinical practice;

Self-assessed knowledge and skill acquisition;

Self-assessed knowledge and skill acquisition;

Open-ended feedback on learning experience and suggestions.

Open-ended feedback on learning experience and suggestions.

Domains 2 and 3 employed a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Content validity was established through expert review by three senior pathologists and two medical education specialists, who evaluated item relevance, clarity, and appropriateness. A pilot test was conducted with 10 junior medical students not involved in the main study. Based on pilot feedback, ambiguous wording was refined, and redundant items were removed.

Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s α. For the final instrument, Cronbach’s α was 0.87 for the overall scale, with subscale values of 0.81 for practice quality, 0.85 for knowledge acquisition, 0.83 for skill development, and 0.79 for professional attitude items, indicating good to excellent reliability.

Data collection

A total of 41 questionnaires were distributed immediately after completion of the pre-clinical practice program. All questionnaires were completed and returned, yielding a 100% response rate. Responses were anonymized prior to analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and questionnaire responses. Results were expressed as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations where appropriate. The Kirkpatrick model provided the conceptual framework for interpreting outcomes across reaction (learner satisfaction), learning (knowledge and skill acquisition), and professional attitude domains.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to enrollment. Confidentiality of participant data was strictly maintained throughout the study.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 41 junior medical students participated in the immersive pre-clinical pathology observation program. Detailed demographic and academic characteristics of the participants are described in the Methods section.

Learner reactions (Kirkpatrick Level 1)

Overall, participants reported a highly positive learning experience. Among the 41 students, 32 (78.0%) reported being “very satisfied,” 6 (14.6%) reported being “satisfied,” and 3 (7.3%) rated their experience as “average.” No students reported dissatisfaction with the program.

Regarding the perceived achievement of the program objectives (multiple responses allowed), most students indicated that the immersive experience helped them gain a better understanding of hospital operations (38/41, 92.7%) and the daily workflow of pathologists (39/41, 95.1%). Furthermore, 40 students (97.6%) reported that the program provided a new perspective on pathology and enhanced their recognition of its important role in clinical medicine. In addition, 30 students (73.2%) expressed willingness to recommend the program to future student cohorts.

Learning outcomes (Kirkpatrick Level 2)

Students reported a range of perceived educational benefits following participation in the immersive situational teaching program (multiple responses allowed). The most frequently reported outcomes included improved integration of theoretical knowledge with clinical practice and increased interest in learning (37/41, 90.2%), enhanced understanding of clinical diseases and better integration of basic and clinical sciences (33/41, 80.5%), and increased awareness of the challenges and responsibilities associated with medical careers (38/41, 92.7%).

In addition, students reported a better understanding of the importance of communication between pathology and clinical disciplines (35/41, 85.4%) and strengthened professional identity and commitment to pursuing a medical career (35/41, 85.4%). A proportion of students also perceived improvements in clinical reasoning, humanistic awareness, and understanding of physicians’ social responsibilities in promoting public health (27/41, 65.9%).

Overall, these findings suggest that the immersive situational teaching approach was well received by students and was associated with perceived cognitive, professional, and affective learning benefits in preclinical pathology education. Consistently, the mean scores for perceived practice quality and self-assessed learning gains were 4.42 ± 0.51 and 4.28 ± 0.57 (on a 5-point scale), respectively, further indicating favorable learner perceptions of the educational experience.

Discussion

Pathology serves as the cornerstone of clinical decision-making; however, its inherent complexity and abstract nature present significant challenges for undergraduate medical education. Traditional didactic teaching often fails to translate theoretical knowledge into diagnostic competence, primarily due to limited opportunities for experiential learning. In contrast, ISTM leverages situated cognition theory to bridge this gap by placing students within authentic clinical workflows, thereby fostering both cognitive and affective learning outcomes [5, 11].

In this study, we implemented an ISTM-based preclinical pathology observation program and evaluated learner perceptions using the Kirkpatrick framework. Students reported high levels of satisfaction and perceived educational benefits, including improved understanding of pathological diagnostic processes, enhanced integration of basic and clinical knowledge, and increased awareness of professional responsibilities. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the value of immersive learning approaches in medical education, including applications in ultrasound [12], immunology [13], and emergency medicine [14]. Importantly, this study extends the application of immersive educational strategies to preclinical pathology education, an area that has received comparatively limited attention.

Several features of the ISTM program likely contributed to its effectiveness. First, the integration of multisensory learning experiences-ranging from gross specimen handling to microscopic interpretation—enabled students to construct a holistic mental model of the pathology report lifecycle. For instance, by participating in the entire workflow from tissue sampling to H&E staining and immunohistochemical interpretation, students developed a concrete appreciation for the precision required in diagnostic pathology. This hands-on engagement appeared to reinforce the concept of pathology as the “gold standard” in clinical diagnosis and cultivated an evidence-based mindset. Second, the program emphasized bidirectional integration between pathology and clinical medicine. Exposure to intraoperative frozen section consultations provided a vivid illustration of how rapid pathological diagnosis directly influences surgical decision-making. Such experiences not only enhanced students’ clinical reasoning but also fostered mutual respect between future clinicians and pathologists-a critical component of interprofessional collaboration. Third, the immersive approach facilitated the seamless incorporation of humanistic education and preventive medicine principles. Case-based discussions on colorectal adenomas, for example, enabled students to link morphological findings with long-term public health outcomes, such as the impact of early screening on cancer prevention. These narratives helped students recognize that behind every pathological specimen lies a unique patient, thereby strengthening their sense of professional responsibility and ethical commitment.

Student feedback also provided important guidance for further refinement of the program. Participants suggested extending opportunities for hands-on activities, providing preparatory learning materials in advance, and increasing exposure to technical procedures such as tissue embedding and sectioning. These recommendations highlight the importance of optimizing the balance between observational experiences and active participation to maximize the educational value of immersive pathology training.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the evaluation primarily relied on self-reported perceptions and satisfaction, without objective measurements of knowledge acquisition, diagnostic skills, or long-term professional outcomes. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence regarding the feasibility and perceived educational value of ISTM rather than definitive evidence of improved competency. Future studies involving larger multicenter cohorts, objective assessment tools, comparative study designs, and longitudinal follow-up are needed to further evaluate the educational impact of immersive pathology curricula.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the feasibility and acceptability of an ISTM-based preclinical pathology observation program. By embedding pathological knowledge within authentic diagnostic contexts, this approach may provide a valuable complement to traditional teaching methods and support students’ understanding of pathology, clinical integration, and professional development. Further systematic evaluation is warranted to determine its broader applicability and long-term educational benefits in medical training.
