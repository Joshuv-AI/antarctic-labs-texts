How are instant messaging applications used informally for paediatric health consultations in Pakistan? A qualitative study with caregivers and healthcare providers in Punjab
Authors: Andrea Bernasconi, Hélène Kirchhoffer, Muhammad Taimoor Raza, Umaima Butt, Qurat Ul-Ain, Muhammad Abdullah Khan, Muhammad Tabraiz, Marianne van der Sande, Muhammad Shakir Raza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe informal use of instant messaging applications for healthcare consultations is rapidly increasing in low- and middle-income countries. Despite its widespread adoption, there is limited research on how these tools are implemented, experienced by users and integrated into health systems. This study aims to provide insight into the practical functioning, perceived value and key challenges of WhatsApp-based paediatric consultations in Pakistan.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative phenomenological study using an interpretive approach across multiple healthcare settings in Punjab. In-depth, semi-structured interviews were held with 19 parents (13 mothers, 6 fathers) and 12 healthcare providers who actively used WhatsApp for paediatric consultations. Data were analysed thematically, guided by the newly developed ACCESS-Loop model.ResultsWhatsApp was used as a user-driven, informal digital channel for paediatric consultations. Access was mediated through social capital and provider altruism, compensating for systemic barriers. Clinical practice involved ‘digital auscultation’ via multimedia, with providers and parents jointly negotiating diagnostic uncertainty and informal triage thresholds. While this approach enhanced access and convenience, it was marked by a trade-off between interpersonal trust and data privacy and substantial provider burden.ConclusionWhatsApp-based paediatric consultations represent a complex, grassroots adaptation to healthcare system limitations. While enhancing access, this informal system poses significant challenges regarding clinical quality, patient safety, provider well-being, data privacy and equity. The findings underscore the need for contextually appropriate guidelines, training and policy interventions.Keywords: Health Services Accessibility, Community Health, Digital Health, Public Health, Qualitative Research

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Instant-messaging apps have become integral to clinical communication and remote consultations, a trend amplified and documented in telemedicine literature during the COVID-19 pandemic. Yet evidence remains largely descriptive, siloed by specialty or focused on feasibility, offering little understanding of lived experiences or pathways to system-level integration. Reviews highlight weak governance, inconsistent documentation and unresolved medico-legal risks. Despite their ubiquity, robust qualitative studies—particularly those capturing both caregiver and provider perspectives in routine paediatric care—are strikingly rare.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This is the first in-depth qualitative study in Pakistan to examine WhatsApp-based paediatric consultations from both caregiver and provider perspectives. Guided by the newly developed ACCESS-Loop model, we move beyond description to show how access, diagnostic reasoning and triage are negotiated in everyday practice. WhatsApp emerges as an informal, user-driven response to systemic healthcare gaps but with trade-offs between convenience, trust, privacy and provider workload. Our findings provide empirical evidence on the double-edged role of ubiquitous yet non-compliant platforms: extending access in resource-limited settings while posing risks to patient safety, equity and data governance.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Instant-messaging applications are embedded in healthcare delivery across many LMICs. Yet reliance on informal platforms risks undermining care quality, provider well-being and data security. Policymakers must recognise this grassroots reality and develop pragmatic, context-specific guidelines, training and regulation to ensure that digital communication is safe, equitable and sustainable. They should also acknowledge the role of instant-messaging applications in easing access to healthcare and close existing gaps—especially in disadvantaged areas—to help advance universal health coverage and achieve the health-related sustainable development goals.

Introduction

The rapid rise of Information and Communication Technology has irrevocably transformed global healthcare landscapes, with mobile health (mHealth) emerging as a particularly dynamic frontier.1,2 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), mHealth interventions offer unprecedented potential to bridge enduring gaps in healthcare access, affordability and quality, particularly in resource-constrained settings.3 Among diverse mHealth modalities, the adoption of ubiquitous instant messaging applications (IMAs) like WhatsApp,4 Telegram, Signal and WeChat for health-related communication has seen exponential, often informal, growth,5,6 enabling remote consultations through text, images and videos. In a 2019 Israeli study,7 approximately 87% of general practitioners and specialists reported daily use of WhatsApp for professional purposes.8 Similarly, in Italy in 2016, more than half of general practitioners (53%) used WhatsApp to communicate with patients, citing its effectiveness in exchanging data and images and its potential to avoid certain in-person visits.9 Previous studies—mostly conducted in high-income countries—have also shown that IMAs improve timely communication,8 chronic disease management by enhancing patient self-care,10 continuity of care11 and patient satisfaction.12 Evidence from Israel13 and Turkey14 further demonstrates that WhatsApp consultations can reduce in-person visits, shorten emergency department stays and accelerate specialist response times. Emerging evidence demonstrates potential benefits of IMA use also from LMICs. WhatsApp groups in Kenya and Malawi strengthened supervision, documentation and team cohesion at low cost.15 In Ghana, WhatsApp use was linked to higher antenatal care attendance and facility deliveries,16 while in Ethiopia, Telegram was preferred for triage and consultations due to its lighter bandwidth and group features.17 IMAs have also supported participant retention and data collection in health systems research.18

This trend is increasingly prominent in paediatric care, where timely communication and accessible advice are paramount for managing childhood illnesses and assuaging parental anxieties.19 Parents and healthcare providers are informally leveraging WhatsApp for a spectrum of paediatric needs, from seeking urgent advice and sharing multimedia for remote assessment to managing chronic conditions and clarifying medication regimens.2 However, while the convenience and potential benefits are anecdotally acknowledged, this informal integration occurs largely outside formal regulatory frameworks, raising significant questions regarding clinical governance, quality of care, diagnostic accuracy, data privacy and equity.5

In Pakistan, a country experiencing rapid growth in mobile telecommunication infrastructure and widespread WhatsApp adoption,20 the informal use of the platform for paediatric consultations is common, yet its implementation remains insufficiently studied. The nation’s healthcare system, characterised by resource limitations, geographical disparities and high paediatric morbidity burden,21,22 creates fertile ground for such informal digital health practices. Despite widespread use, significant knowledge gaps exist regarding implementation patterns, lived experiences and complex challenges within Pakistan’s socio-cultural context.

This study aims to comprehensively explore the implementation methods, user experiences and perceived healthcare delivery impact of WhatsApp-based paediatric consultations in Punjab, Pakistan. Specifically, we sought to: (1) identify perceived benefits and challenges from both providers’ and parents’ perspectives; (2) delineate workflows, communication patterns and decision-making processes; (3) understand parental perceptions of quality, convenience and trustworthiness; (4) analyse ethical and privacy concerns and (5) generate contextually relevant recommendations.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a qualitative research approach, guided by a phenomenological design using a thematic analysis, to explore lived experiences and operational dynamics of WhatsApp-based consultations in a paediatric context.

During the analytic process, our team developed the ACCESS-Loop model (figure 1), a novel, empirically grounded conceptual framework that synthesises recurring themes and interactions reported by participants. The model captures the cyclical and evolving nature of informal digital consultations across six interlinked stages: (A) access brokerage; (C) communication workflow and digital auscultation; (C) clinical decision-making and triage; (E) enacting care: responsiveness and management; (S) satisfaction, trust and relational dynamics and (S) sustainability pressures and ad-hoc safeguards.

The ACCESS-Loop: Operational Dynamics.

This model was developed inductively through constant comparison and collaborative coding and represents an original contribution of this study. It serves as a heuristic to describe and interpret the practical and relational dimensions of IMA paediatric consultations from the perspectives of both healthcare providers and parents.

Our research was conducted across five healthcare settings in Punjab province, including major urban centres, peri-urban towns and rural villages, capturing a range of socio-demographic contexts influencing mHealth adoption.

Participants and sampling

31 participants were purposively recruited until thematic saturation was achieved, comprising 19 parents (13 mothers, 6 fathers) and 12 healthcare providers (4 female, 8 male). Parents were identified through paediatric departments, local clinics and community health programmes. Healthcare providers—including public and private sector paediatricians and medical officers—were recruited via hospital rosters and snowball sampling.

Inclusion criteria for parents were as follows: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) being the parent or legal guardian of a child under 16 who had received at least one WhatsApp-based paediatric consultation within the past 12 months and (3) provision of informed consent. Healthcare providers were eligible if they had conducted five or more paediatric consultations via WhatsApp in the previous year.

Data collection

Data were collected between March and May 2025 through in-depth, semi-structured interviews conducted privately (Semi-structured Interview Guides for caregiver and healthcare providers are present in Supplementary File: Semi-structured Interview Guides), besides the health facilities, in Urdu, Punjabi or English. Only the child, the caregiver and members of the research team were present during the interview. Interview guides addressed experiences, access dynamics, communication, decision-making, trust, privacy and perceived benefits/challenges. All interviews (10–25 min) were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and translated by bilingual researchers. Five pilot interviews were conducted before the main study to standardise procedures and train the research team; pilot data were used only for procedural calibration and were not included in the analytic dataset.

Data analysis

A hybrid deductive–inductive approach was adopted for the analysis. Given that the phenomenon under investigation is relatively new and insufficiently explored in Pakistan, we first applied an inductive approach to identify patterns and derive preliminary themes from the data. These emergent themes informed the development of a codebook and guided the refinement of the ACCESS Loop Model, which was thus derived from data rather than imposed a priori. In a subsequent step, this refined framework was applied to deductively structure the data and guide their interpretation. The analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis.23 The iterative coding process was carried out in MAXQDA24 by one researcher, who combined inductive coding (to develop the codebook) with deductive coding, guided by the ACCESS Loop Model (to structure the data). The codebook was iteratively refined while capturing additional concepts emerging from the data, and themes were consolidated through team discussions. The ACCESS Loop Model was further supported by recurring patterns identified in the dataset. Methodological rigour was ensured through adherence to COREQ guidelines,25 triangulation, dual coding and audit trails.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Review Committee, Rai Medical College Sargodha (Approval No. RMCS/ERC/09/25, dated 17 March 2025). All participants provided written informed consent. Privacy and confidentiality were maintained through anonymisation.

Results

Participant characteristics

The caregiver group (n=19) included families from three setting categories: urban (n=8), peri-urban small towns (n=3) and remote villages (n=8). Educational backgrounds ranged from class-5 schooling to a bachelor’s degree, and reported occupations included farming, teaching, small-business ownership, driving and homemaking. Self-reported travel time to the nearest public healthcare facility, standardised to minutes by the participant’s primary mode of transport, varied across settings: urban participants reported 1–20 min (on foot, by rickshaw or by motorcycle); peri-urban participants reported intermediate distances with intermittent specialist availability (often requiring travel to a nearby city) and rural participants reported 20–30 min by foot, motorcycle or rickshaw to reach a government clinic or hospital. Participant-level demographics, distances and consultation indications are reported in supplementary table (see online supplemental table 1).

The healthcare provider group (n=12; 8 male, 4 female) comprised paediatric practitioners with 1.5–27 years’ experience. By sector, nine worked in public-sector facilities, two in mixed public–private settings and one in a public–private clinic. By level of care, the participating facilities included two public tertiary care hospitals, seven public secondary care hospitals (including one tehsil-level facility), one public primary care facility and two outpatient clinics with mixed public–private character. Patient-population characteristics reported by providers (predominantly lower socioeconomic status, mixed urban–rural catchment, varying parental literacy) are reported in the context-comparison sub-section of the results below and in supplementary table (see online supplemental file 1).

Thematic saturation was achieved; all participants expressed interest in the research topic, which they regarded as innovative, and none declined to participate. No interviews required repetition.

Access brokerage

A critical precursor to WhatsApp consultations, reported in 12 of 19 caregiver accounts, was an initial face-to-face interaction, establishing the digital trust forming the Informal Digital Paediatric Consultations (IDPC)’s relational bedrock. Access to a provider’s WhatsApp number did not, in any of the 19 caregiver accounts, originate through a formal institutional channel (no hospital appointment system, registered telemedicine service or insurance gateway). Three pathways recurred. The first was provider-initiated sharing, in which the provider gave out personal contact details at the close of an in-person visit, typically at the provider’s discretion based on perceived clinical need or geographical disadvantage of the family. The second was intermediary-mediated access, in which the caregiver obtained the number through a personal contact — a relative, neighbour or friend — who already had a prior relationship with the provider. The third was emergency ‘breaking-in’, in which the number was forwarded through a third party at night or when in-person care was otherwise inaccessible. As P15 (father) detailed: “My neighbour is friend with a doctor…He suggested I message the doctor on WhatsApp”.

Providers shared contacts selectively, particularly for vulnerable patients or those from remote areas. P1 (father) explained: “When he realised these are my patients from quite a distance, he said you can contact me whenever”. This ‘selective altruism’ responded to anticipated follow-up needs difficult to manage formally.

Systemic deficiencies catalysed WhatsApp adoption: geographical distance (P12, mother: “Doctors aren't available here all the time…so we keep their WhatsApp numbers”), temporal barriers (P4, mother: “It was out of necessity, especially late at night”) and financial constraints (P14, father: “if online advice works, why not keep that money?").

Communication workflow and digital auscultation

Consultations used WhatsApp’s multimedia capabilities. ‘Digital auscultation’ involved photographs of rashes, videos of breathing patterns and voice notes—particularly for less literate parents. Provider 12 noted: “Voice notes are most common. Many parents aren't comfortable typing”.

Urban-rural patterns varied significantly. Provider 11 observed: “Urban families send texts, photos, even PDFs. Rural families rely on voice notes”. Providers developed individualised methods, including photographed prescriptions and voice-guided dosages.

Clinical decision-making and triage

Both parties negotiated a ‘threshold of seriousness’ determining when remote consultation sufficed versus in-person examination necessity. Providers articulated boundaries—minor ailments managed remotely, serious conditions requiring physical assessment. Provider six stated: “Anything acute—high fever with lethargy, breathing problems—I ask them to come in person”.

Parents demonstrated awareness of limitations. P2 (mother) noted: “If serious, we take the child in person”. Despite informal protocols, absence of physical examination introduced diagnostic uncertainty. Provider four recounted: “Mother downplayed breathing issue…later turned out serious” highlighting high-stakes decision-making risks.

Enacting care: Digital responsiveness

WhatsApp facilitated rapid responses and ongoing management. P1’s child with acute breathing difficulties received immediate guidance: “Take the baby to clinic immediately”. For less severe conditions, parents received effective home management instructions. P3 (mother) stated: “Medicine from WhatsApp has always worked”.

Satisfaction, trust and relational dynamics

Successful interactions fostered high satisfaction and strengthened trust. P9 (mother) articulated: “Talking on WhatsApp quickly frees your mind…the longer it takes, more tension builds”. Trust remained contingent on illness severity, previous experiences and provider responsiveness.

Sustainability pressures and ad-hoc safeguards

The 24/7 accessibility created substantial provider burden. Provider 12 described: “It blurs the line. Messages late at night, weekends… adds to mental load”. Providers developed coping mechanisms—separate SIM cards, informal ‘off-hours’—though boundaries remained porous.

Privacy measures were improvised. Providers avoided saving full identifiers, regularly deleted sensitive content and used password-protected phones. The pervasive governance vacuum left providers relying on personal judgement and informal ethics discussions.

Despite the overall acceptance of digital consultations by parents, several critical issues emerged. While most parents expressed satisfaction with WhatsApp consultations, several participants articulated important reservations. P7 (mother) emphasised the irreplaceable value of in-person care: “Face-to-face is still better. On the phone the doctor has to deal with you hurriedly as other patients are waiting”. She further cautioned that while WhatsApp offered “excellent convenience,” major issues “shouldn't be solved solely through it; try to visit the doctor for a proper examination”.

Response delays emerged as another concern. P1 (father), whose child had cardiac problems, noted: “If we sent a message at night, we might get a reply in the morning”. This temporal uncertainty posed particular challenges for parents managing chronic conditions requiring timely interventions.

Even among satisfied users, endorsement was often qualified. P3 (mother) reported being “about 70–80% satisfied,” acknowledging that while “the medicines work,” the experience “is not the same as a clinic visit”. P11 (mother) similarly emphasised: “The real satisfaction lies in face-to-face consultation”.

While no participant reported direct harm from WhatsApp consultations, these accounts underscore persistent concerns about clinical attentiveness, response latency, and the platform’s limitations for managing acute or complex paediatric conditions.

WhatsApp consultations offered valued lifelines yet introduced new burdens. P2 (mother) summarised: “WhatsApp made life easier. We don't need hospital for small issues”. However, provider accessibility translated to uncompensated workload extension. Provider 3 described: “You might be at gathering, get calls about diarrhoea, give advice while eating”.

Although WhatsApp consultations were perceived as ‘good enough’ alternatives when optimal options were limited, a trust-privacy paradox emerged: strong interpersonal trust outweighed concerns about the risks of a commercial platform. However, both parents and healthcare providers acknowledged the potential for miscommunication and diagnostic errors arising from the absence of physical examination. Furthermore, IDPC can potentially exacerbate inequities. P18 (mother), unable to use WhatsApp, relied on her niece: “My niece suggested we message the doctor… I didn't know how, so she helped”. This ‘tech broker’ reliance indicated access mediated by household digital capabilities.

From the healthcare providers’ point of view, they articulated a clear need for formalisation. As provider 7 noted, “We need clear guidelines—what’s managed on WhatsApp, what needs a clinic visit, how to keep records”. Many healthcare providers recognised that their practices were grounded in informal learning, highlighting training needs on best practices for remote consultations. However, addressing systemic challenges, they admitted, will require stronger privacy safeguards, strategies to mitigate provider burden (eg, shared accounts, ‘quiet hours’ policies), and governance frameworks with clear ethical guidelines and liability provisions. At the same time, they stated, opportunities exist to build on existing trust by formalising triage pathways, enhancing digital health literacy and supporting ‘tech brokers’ to bridge digital divides.

Discussion

Our findings provide valuable insights into how WhatsApp has evolved from a tool for casual communication into a platform for IDPC in Pakistani Punjab, similar to experiences in other LMICs where mobile technologies have served as temporary solutions to bridge health system gaps.2,5,26 Unlike structured mHealth programmes, this IDPC arose organically from necessity, echoing user-driven digital health adaptation patterns.3,27

We found social capital played a pivotal role in brokering access, reflecting South Asian health-seeking practices that prioritise trust and community networks.28 Providers’ expressions of ’selective altruism’ expand existing observations on how telemedicine operates at the margins of formal healthcare.29 In our study, key drivers of IDPC included geographical distance, limited access to care outside regular hours and financial constraints.27,30 This aligns with evidence that trust in telehealth is strongly facilitated by prior in-person encounters.31

We use the term ‘digital auscultation’ to denote the set of remote, multimedia-mediated examination practices through which providers attempt to compensate for the absence of in-person physical examination, specifically, parent-recorded videos of breathing and chest movement, audio clips of cough, photographs of skin, eyes, throat or stool and structured verbal symptom reports — interpreted by the provider in the absence of direct sensory contact with the patient. The term is metaphorical: it captures the substitutive intent rather than implying acoustic equivalence to physical auscultation. Participants’ multimedia use for ‘digital auscultation’ aligns with telemedicine engagement improvements via IMAs.5 However, limitations—variable quality, context loss, diagnostic error risk—highlight informal digital care dangers, reinforcing standardised protocol needs.32

From this qualitative study, emotional reassurance and relational trust emerged as key components of perceived care quality, reinforcing existing literature on the importance of patient–provider communication.33 The trust-privacy paradox aligns with mHealth adoption findings where institutional trust is weak.34

Our study highlights often-overlooked provider burden in informal digital care. The 24/7 expectation drives digital health burnout.35 While providers devise coping strategies, these remain makeshift without institutional support.36 Ad-hoc privacy measures and governance vacuum parallel warnings about rapid innovation without regulatory scaffolding exposing substantial risks.5

Against this backdrop, our findings articulate a dual reality of clear benefits and substantive costs. Two features of the consultation workflow recurred across accounts and warrant attention. First, a default to asynchronous text and voice-note exchange was the most common modality across caregivers and providers, with synchronous calls reserved for urgent or follow-up cases. Second, an informal escalation pathway, in which providers prompted families to come in person when red-flag features were reported, was described by several providers with corresponding caregiver confirmations. Caregivers valued the speed and responsiveness of WhatsApp consultations, yet, in parallel, expressed concerns about uncertainty in clinical judgement and the limits of care without physical examination. Ethical and privacy concerns were raised explicitly by a subset of providers and a smaller subset of caregivers — principally around device security, accidental disclosure on shared family phones, and uncertainty about platform-level data handling. A small number of providers further raised medico-legal concerns about the absence of a recorded medical encounter. We characterise these as a salient but unevenly distributed set of concerns rather than a uniformly held position.

Drawing on these insights, we outline preliminary, locally grounded recommendations to mitigate provider burden, protect patients’ safety, clarify expectations and guide institutional engagement with informal digital care. Moreover, while improving access, persistent digital inequities may emerge: families without smartphones/literacy remain excluded or rely on ‘tech brokers’, aligning with calls ensuring digital health doesn’t reinforce gradients.3,37

Implications and future directions

In Pakistan, as in many other LMICs, there is a need for context-specific guidelines for the ethical use of IMAs in IDPC.5,38 Investment in digital literacy campaigns and equitable device/connectivity access is essential.27 Institutions must acknowledge digital care workload through compensation policies and shared duty arrangements.35 Robust privacy protections and secure platforms are needed to mitigate risks,5,34 given that IMAs are typically not compliant with regulatory frameworks such as HIPAA or GDPR.39 Moreover, end-to-end encryption alone is insufficient, as critical concerns persist regarding business associate agreements, device-level security, data exportability and long-term data retention.6 In 2019, WHO recommended digital client communication and other mobile interventions when benefits outweighed harms, highlighting data protection, acceptability and feasibility rather than app-specific guidance.1 Future research should quantitatively assess the benefits of informal consultations, develop and evaluate decision-support tools, conduct comparative studies across LMICs and explore the long-term sustainability of such practices.

Strengths and limitations

This study has an important strength as it includes hybrid inductive-deductive approach and the novel conceptualisation of the ACCESS Loop, which provides an empirical framework for understanding informal digital health practices. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Underage parents and potential parents without digital access were excluded, which may have limited the diversity of perspectives. The lack of participant feedback on the study’s findings may have limited their validation. Although the context-specific nature of the study may limit generalisability, the healthcare and digital infrastructure in Pakistani Punjab closely resembles that of many LMICs, supporting broader relevance of the findings. As data were collected over a relatively short period (March–May 2025), the study may not capture potential seasonal variations in healthcare utilisation or illness patterns. To counterbalance these limitations, the research team brought together medical and public health researchers with training in qualitative methods and prior experience in digital health studies. The lead coders (AB and MSR) are male physicians and epidemiologists with formal training in qualitative data analysis. This expertise under the supervision of a qualitative analysis expert (HK) combined with established collaborations with local institutions in Pakistan. This facilitated access to participants and contextual understanding, while regular team discussions, triangulation of disciplinary perspectives and the iterative refinement of the codebook served as safeguards to minimise bias and ensure that interpretations remained grounded in the data. Furthermore, to support the research team, female psychologists with prior research experience were recruited and trained to facilitate discussions with mothers in rural and more conservative settings.

The IDPC exemplifies ingenuity facing health system gaps, transforming WhatsApp into a paediatric care option for Pakistani families. Yet informality brings challenges such as digital divides, clinical uncertainty, provider burnout and ethical ambiguity as this study moves beyond simplistic mHealth narratives, revealing nuanced processes and ethical complexities inextricably linked to broader contexts.

In our qualitative study, healthcare providers themselves were calling for a shift from dismissal or critique toward constructive engagement with these evolving practices. They emphasised the need for proactive approaches. This entails developing contextually appropriate strategies that harness the benefits of user-driven innovation while systematically addressing risks through culturally sensitive guidelines, targeted training, secure low-cost solutions and policies that support provider well-being and promote equitable access. IDPC testifies to human adaptability when facing systemic constraints—a call for healthcare futures learning from, supporting and responsibly integrating grassroots innovations.

A 2022 analysis estimated that approximately USD 12.5 billion would be required to finance digital transformation across the health systems of 78 LMICs, including investments in digital infrastructure, health information systems, telemedicine and data governance.40 Yet, many of these initiatives have adopted top-down approaches, carrying the risk of limited uptake. It is therefore important that research and implementation also consider existing, field-tested solutions that are already in use by local stakeholders, ensuring that digital health investments are pragmatic, contextually appropriate and more likely to be sustained. In this regard, the widespread use of IMAs in routine communication and care delivery provides a salient example of bottom-up, organically adopted digital practices that merit attention. However, their full potential will only be realised if these shadow systems are formally integrated, supported and safeguarded as elements of an equitable paediatric healthcare ecosystem.
