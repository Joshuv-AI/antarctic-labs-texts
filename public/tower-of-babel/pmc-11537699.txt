A moment of mourning suspended in air
Authors: Amy LeBlanc
Published: 2024

I didn’t see the bird on the way up — I only arrive

later: the last Friday in June. I slotted myself

between neighbours standing twenty feet beneath

the tail. We didn’t plan a gathering, we left our homes

at the same moment to see a bird perpendicular to the

ground, feathers like dust from a construction zone. They say

this is the coolest summer of the rest of our lives,

and they say that the cicadas are burning in their tunnels. We

watched the bird. We watched smoke. We couldn’t hear cicadas,

but we knew they moved. The bird and I were in line

with freshly painted arrows on the road. A hand grazed

my shoulder and my neighbour (whose leg I cut

with a lawn mower) asked, have you ever seen anything like this?

I said no — like I was lying or surprised he’s speaking to me. I can still see him bleeding.

Was he asking about the bird, smoke, cicadas,

or the sun that hung in the sky like a raspberry.

Now the bird hovered, mid-air. And I looked for strings,

fishing line, anything that might have held it in a moment of mourning.

The scar winked with the sun

at this angle and I expected the bird to spin, to prove suspension.

No breath in that body, no pulse, no

half-digested dinner, only hollow bones that colluded

with wind for fifteen minutes on a Friday in June. The bird slipped,

crashed to pavement. I didn’t see it on the way up —

I saw the glitch, the nosedive instead. The collective

noun for hawks is a kettle. Inside, I dusted ash from my windowsill,

boiled water to wash the dust and bones away from the road.

My hands peeled. I had washed my neighbour’s

blood down the storm drain like a stream of red ants. We don’t talk about

the crash. Next year, on the last Friday in June,

we will all leave our houses and look up.
