Abstract
. We study the relationship between Sobolev extension domains and homogeneous Sobolev extension domains. Precisely, for a certain range of exponents p p and q q , we construct a ( W 1 , p , W 1 , q ) (W^{1,p},W^{1,q}) -extension domain which is not an ( L 1 , p , L 1 , q ) (L^{1,p},L^{1,q}) -extension domain.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
46E35, 30L99

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{X}({\Omega})
be a (semi-)normed function space defined on a domain
{\Omega}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
and
\mathcal{Y}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})
be a (semi-)normed function space defined on
{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
. We say that
{\Omega}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
is a
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
-extension domain, if there exists a bounded extension operator

E:\mathcal{X}({\Omega})\to\mathcal{Y}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})

in the sense that for every
u\in\mathcal{X}({\Omega})
, there exists
E(u)\in\mathcal{Y}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})
with

E(u)\big|_{\Omega}\equiv u\ {\rm and}\ \|E(u)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq C\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}}

for a positive constant
C>1
independent of
u
.

Partial motivation for the study of Sobolev extensions comes from PDEs (see, for example,
[
13
]
). In
[
2
,
19
]
, Calderón and Stein proved that for a Lipschitz domain
{\Omega}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
, there always exists a bounded linear extension operator

E:W^{k,p}({\Omega})\to W^{k,p}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}),

for each
k\geq 1
and all
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Here
W^{k,p}({\Omega})
is the Banach space of all
L^{p}
-integrable functions whose distributional derivatives up to order
k
belong to
L^{p}({\Omega})
. In
[
8
]
, Jones introduced the notation of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domains and generalized this result to the class of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domains. It was proved that for every
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domain, there always exists a bounded linear extension operator

E:W^{k,p}({\Omega})\to W^{k,p}({{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})

for each
k\geq 1
and all
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

By the results in
[
6
]
from Hajłasz, Koskela and Tuominen, for
1\leq p<\infty
, a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,p})
-extension domain must be Ahlfors regular in the sense that

(1.1)

|B(x,r)\cap{\Omega}|\geq C|B(x,r)|

for every
x\in\overline{{\Omega}}
and all
0<r<\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{4}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,diam\,}}({\Omega})\right\}
with a constant
C
independent of
x,r
. This excludes lots of domains, for example outward cuspidal domains which are domains with one singular point on the boundary. Sobolev extension for outward cuspidal domains has been studied in detail by several mathematicians like Maz’ya, Poborchi, Gol’dshtein and so on, for example, see
[
5
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Outward cuspidal domains are
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domains for some
q,p
with
q<p
depending on the orders of the cusps. Recently, Koskela and Zhu proved that for
1\leq q<n-1
and
(n-1)q/(n-1-q)\leq p<\infty
, an arbitrary outward cuspidal domain is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain. See
[
10
]
. This is a limit case of the results mentioned above.

In PDEs, people usually only care about the integrability of derivatives, for example, see
[
12
]
. Hence it is essential to study the homogeneous Sobolev space
L^{1,p}({\Omega})
. Here
L^{1,p}({\Omega})
is the semi-normed space of all locally
L^{1}
-integrable functions whose first order distributional derivatives belong to
L^{p}({\Omega})
. Obviously, the Sobolev space
W^{1,p}({\Omega})
is always a subspace of the homogeneous Sobolev space
L^{1,p}({\Omega})
. Conversely, for sufficiently nice domains, for example for balls, the homogeneous Sobolev space
L^{1,p}({\Omega})
coincides with the Sobolev space
W^{1,p}({\Omega})
as a set of functions. More generally, this holds for
p
-Poincaré domains. Recall that a bounded domain
{\Omega}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
is called a
p
-Poincaré domain for
1\leq p<\infty
, if the global
(p,p)
-Poincaré inequality

(1.2)

\int_{\Omega}|u(x)-u_{\Omega}|^{p}dx\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u(x)|^{p}dx

holds for every
u\in W^{1,p}({\Omega})
. For studies on
(L^{1,p},L^{1,p})
-extension domains, see e.g.
[
21
]
,
[
8
]
.

Based on the above, it is natural to inquire for the relationship between
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
and
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
extensions. In
[
7
, Section 4]
it was proved that for
1<p<\infty
, a bounded domain is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,p})
-extension domain if and only if it is a
(L^{1,p},L^{1,p})
-extension domain.

Towards the case
q<p
, let us define

(1.3)

q^{\star}:=\begin{cases}\frac{nq}{n-q},\ \ &\ {\rm if}\ 1\leq q<n,\\
\infty,\ \ &\ {\rm if}\ n\leq q<\infty.\end{cases}

In
[
11
]
, for a bounded domain
\Omega
, we showed that if
\Omega
is an
(L^{1,p},L^{1,p})
-extension domain, then it is also a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,p})
-extension domain and the converse holds if
q\leq p<q^{\star}
.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
1\leq q\leq p<q^{\star}
. Then a bounded
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain is also an
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
-extension domain. On the other hand, for
1\leq q<n
and
q^{*}<p\leq n,
there exists a bounded
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain, which is not an
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
-extension domain.

Towards the missing range
p>n
, let us analyze the positive result in more details. A detailed inspection on the proof of the first part of Proposition
1.1
gives the following conclusion.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
1\leq q\leq p<\infty
. If a
p
-Poincaré domain
{\Omega}
is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain, then it is also an
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
-extension domain.

Let
\psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a left-continuous and nondecreasing function with
\psi(0)=0
and
\psi(1)=1
. Define the corresponding outward cuspidal domain
{\Omega}_{\psi}
by setting

(1.4)

{\Omega}_{\psi}:=\left\{(t,z)\in(0,1)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}:|z|<\psi(t)\right\}\cup B^{n}\left((2,0,\cdots,0),\sqrt{2}\right).

The function
\psi
is called the model function for the outward cuspidal domain
{\Omega}_{\psi}
. The following picture explains the geometric shape of an outward cuspidal domain.

The outward cuspidal domain

In a series of papers
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
, Maz’ya and Poborchi found sharp conditions on the cuspidal function
\psi
to guarantee that the outward cuspidal domain
{\Omega}_{\psi}
is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain for
1\leq q<p<\infty
. In the paper
[
10
]
it was proved that for an arbitrary model function
\psi
, the corresponding outward cuspidal domain
{\Omega}_{\psi}
is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain for every
1\leq q<n-1
and
(n-1)q/(n-1-q)\leq p<\infty
. Furthermore, in the paper
[
3
]
, it was proved that for an arbitrary outward cuspidal domain
{\Omega}_{\psi}
, the Sobolev space
W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{\psi})
is isometrically equivalent to the Hajłasz-Sobolev space
M^{1,p}({\Omega}_{\psi})
for every
1<p<\infty
. Also see
[
20
]
for a more general result. Via this result, we can conclude that
{\Omega}_{\psi}
is a
p
-Poincaré domain for every
1<p<\infty
. Hence, for outward cuspidal domains, the two different Sobolev extension properties are equivalent.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
{\Omega}_{\psi}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
be an outward cuspidal domain. Then it is a
p
-Poincaré domain for
1<p<\infty
. It is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain for some
1\leq q<p<\infty
, if and only if it is also an
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
-extension domain.

Our final result addresses the case
p>n
which is not covered by the second part of the Proposition
1.1
. Notice that the condition
q^{\star}<p\leq n
in Proposition
1.1
requires that
1\leq q<\frac{n}{2}
and that no counterexamples can exist by the first part of the Proposition when
q\geq n
. Theorem
1.3
below will provide us with negative examples when
1\leq q<n-1
, but the case where
n-1\leq q<n
remains open. By Theorem
1.2
, outward cusps do not give examples for the missing case
n-1\leq q<n
. However, the construction for Theorem
1.3
is based on a somewhat related non-smoothness. Indeed, one relies on outward “mushrooms”, based on cylindrical stems. The idea behind constructions of this type goes back to Nikodým. The construction for the example referred to in Theorem
1.1
is also of Nikodým-type. However, the proof of the extension property heavily relies on the assumption that
p<n
. We prove the extension property of our example via ideas from
[
9
]
instead of using ideas from
[
11
]
. Analysis of our argument shows that the restriction
p>\frac{q(n-1)}{(n-1-q)}
cannot be avoided, see Section
6
. This suggest that perhaps no counterexamples for the coincidence exist when
q\geq n-1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n\geq 3
,
1\leq q<n-1
and
(n-1)q/(n-1-q)<p<\infty
. Then there exists a bounded domain
{\Omega}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
which is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain but not an
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
-extension domain.

2.
Preliminaries

We refer to generic positive constants by
C
. These constants may change even in a single string of estimates. The dependence of the constant on parameters
\alpha,\beta,\cdots
is expressed by the notation
C=C(\alpha,\beta,\cdots)
if needed.
First, let us give the definition of Sobolev spaces and homogeneous Sobolev spaces.

Definition 2.1
.

For
u\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}({\Omega})
, we say
u
belongs to the homogeneous Sobolev space
L^{1,p}({\Omega})
for
1\leq p\leq\infty
if
u
is weakly differentiable and its weak (distributional) derivative
\nabla u
belongs to
L^{p}({\Omega};{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}})
. The homogeneous Sobolev space
L^{1,p}({\Omega})
is equipped with the semi-norm

\|u\|_{L^{1,p}({\Omega})}:=\begin{cases}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u(x)\right|^{p}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&\ {\rm for}\ 1\leq p<\infty,\\
{\rm esssup}\left|\nabla u\right|,&\ {\rm for}\ p=\infty.\end{cases}

Furthermore, if
u\in L^{1,p}(\Omega)
is also
L^{p}
-integrable, then we say
u
belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,p}({\Omega})
. The Sobolev space
W^{1,p}({\Omega})
is equipped with the norm

\|u\|_{W^{1,p}({\Omega})}:=\begin{cases}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|u(x)\right|^{p}+\left|\nabla u(x)\right|^{p}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&\ {\rm for}\ 1\leq p<\infty,\\
{\rm esssup}\left(\left|u\right|+\left|\nabla u\right|\right),&\ {\rm for}\ p=\infty.\end{cases}

By the results due to Calderón
[
2
]
and Stein
[
19
]
, Lipschitz domains are
(W^{k,p},W^{k,p})
-extension domains for arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Later, Jones generalized this result to
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domains. Every Lipschitz domain is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domain for some
\epsilon,\delta>0
. However, the class of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domains is strictly larger than the class of Lipschitz domains. As we will use this result later, for the convenience of readers, we present the definition of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domains here.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
0<\epsilon<1
and
\delta>0
. We say that a domain
{\Omega}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domain, if for every
x,y\in{\Omega}
with
|x-y|<\delta
, there is a rectifiable curve
\gamma\subset{\Omega}
joining
x,y
and satisfies the following two inequalities that

(2.1)

{\rm length}(\gamma)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}|x-y|

and

(2.2)

d(z,\partial{\Omega})\geq\frac{\epsilon|x-z||y-z|}{|x-y|}\ {\rm for\ all}\ z\ {\rm on}\ \gamma.

By a classical mollification argument, for arbitrary domain
{\Omega}
, we always have that
C^{\infty}({\Omega})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega})
is always dense in
W^{1,p}({\Omega})
for every
1\leq p<\infty
. See
[
4
, Theorem 4.2]
. However, we cannot have that
C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega})
is always dense in
W^{1,p}({\Omega})
, unless the domain
{\Omega}
is sufficiently nice. For the following definition of segment condition, see e.g. the book
[
1
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
{\Omega}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
be a domain. We say it satisfies the segment condition if for every
x\in\partial{\Omega}
, there exists a neighborhood
U_{x}
of
x
and a nonzero vector
y_{x}
such that if
z\in\overline{{\Omega}}\cap U_{x}
, then
z+ty_{x}\in{\Omega}
for
0<t<1
.

The following lemma tells us that the Sobolev functions defined on a domain with segment condition can be approximated by functions smooth up to the boundary.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\Omega}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
be a domain which satisfies the segment condition. Then for every
1\leq p<\infty
, the subspace
C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega})
is dense in
W^{1,p}({\Omega})
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
{\Omega}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
be a bounded
p
-Poincaré domain and a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain. By
[
7
]
, we know that
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
coincides with
L^{1,p}(\Omega)
as a set of functions. So, for
u\in L^{1,p}(\Omega),
we have
(u-u_{\Omega})\in W^{1,p}(\Omega).
Let
B
be a ball with
\Omega\subset B
. We define a function on
B
by

T(u):=E(u-u_{\Omega})+u_{\Omega}.

Then we have
T(u)\in W^{1,q}(B)
with
T(u)\big|_{B}\equiv u
. By the fact
{\Omega}
is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain and by the
(p,p)
-Poincaré inequality, we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle\|\nabla T(u)\|_{L^{q}(B)}=\|\nabla E(u-u_{\Omega})\|_{L^{q}(B)}\leq\|u-u_{{\Omega}}\|_{W^{1,p}({\Omega})}\leq C\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}({\Omega})}.

This shows that
{\Omega}
is a
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
-extension domain, since
B
is such a domain.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
{\Omega}_{\psi}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
be an outward cuspidal domain. At first we show that
{\Omega}_{\psi}
is a
p
- Poincaré domain. Fix
u\in C^{\infty}({\Omega}_{\psi})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{\psi})
. From
[
20
, Lemma 4.1]
, there exists a non-negative function
g_{u}\in L^{p}({\Omega}_{\psi})
such that for almost every
x,y\in{\Omega}_{\psi}
, we have

(4.1)

|u(x)-u(y)|\leq C|x-y|(g_{u}(x)+g_{u}(y))

and

(4.2)

\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}g_{u}(z)^{p}dz\leq C\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}|\nabla u(z)|^{p}dz

with a constant independent of
u
.

By using
4.1
and
4.2
, we get that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}|u(x)-u_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}|^{p}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}|u(x)\rule[2.60138pt]{8.67204pt}{0.65062pt}\kern-11.56346pt\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}dy-\rule[2.60138pt]{8.67204pt}{0.65062pt}\kern-11.56346pt\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}u(y)dy|^{p}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}|\rule[2.60138pt]{8.67204pt}{0.65062pt}\kern-11.56346pt\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}u(x)dy-\rule[2.60138pt]{8.67204pt}{0.65062pt}\kern-11.56346pt\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}u(y)dy|^{p}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}\rule[2.60138pt]{8.67204pt}{0.65062pt}\kern-11.56346pt\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}dydx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}\rule[2.60138pt]{8.67204pt}{0.65062pt}\kern-11.56346pt\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}|x-y|^{p}(g_{u}(x)+g_{u}(y))^{p}dydx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,diam\,}}({\Omega}_{\psi})^{p}\int_{{\Omega}_{\psi}}|\nabla u(z)|^{p}dz

Hence,
4.3
shows that
{\Omega}_{\psi}
is a
p
-Poincaré domain for every
1\leq p\leq\infty
. By using Theorem
1.1
, we get that if
{\Omega}_{\psi}
is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain, then it is also an
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
-extension domain.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3

5.1.
The construction of the domain

Let
1\leq q<n-1
and
(n-1)q/(n-1-q)<p<\infty
be fixed.
We use the notation
\mathscr{Q}(x,r)
to denote an
(n-1)
-dimensional closed cube in the
(n-1)
-dimensional Euclidean hyperplane
{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
with the center
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
and the side-length
2r
. Let
\mathscr{Q}_{0}:=[0,1]^{n-1}
be the
(n-1)
-dimensional closed unit cube in the
(n-1)
-dimensional Euclidean hyperplane
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. We divide
\mathscr{Q}_{0}
into
2^{n-1}
equivalent
(n-1)
-dimensional closed cubes whose interiors are pairwise disjoint. Pick one of them and denote it by
\mathscr{Q}_{1}
. Denote by
z_{1}
to be the center of the cube
\mathscr{Q}_{1}
. So
\mathscr{Q}_{1}=\mathscr{Q}\left(z_{1},\frac{1}{4}\right)
. Define

\mathscr{B}_{1}:=B^{n-1}\left(z_{1},\frac{1}{2^{2}}\right)\ {\rm and}\ \mathcal{B}_{1}:={B^{n-1}}\left(z_{1},\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}+\frac{p}{q}}\right).

Define
\mathscr{C}_{1}:=\mathscr{B}_{1}\times\left(\frac{3}{2},2\right)
to be an open cylinder and
\mathcal{C}_{1}:=\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\left[1,\frac{3}{2}\right]
to be a cylinder which connects the unit cube
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
and the cylinder
\mathscr{C}_{1}
.

Choose one of the cubes different from
\mathscr{Q}_{1}
from the first step and denote it by
\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}_{1}
. Divide
\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}_{1}
into
2^{n-1}
equivalent
(n-1)
-dimensional closed cubes such that their interiors are pairwise disjoint. Pick one of them up and denote it by
\mathscr{Q}_{2}
. Then its edge length is
\frac{1}{4}
. Denote
z_{2}
to be the center of the cube
\mathscr{Q}_{2}
. So
\mathscr{Q}_{2}=\mathscr{Q}\left(z_{2},\frac{1}{8}\right)
. Define

\mathscr{B}_{2}:=B^{n-1}\left(z_{2},\frac{1}{2^{3}}\right)\ {\rm and}\ \mathcal{B}_{2}:={B^{n-1}}\left(z_{2},\left(\frac{1}{4^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}+\frac{p}{q}}\right).

Define
\mathscr{C}_{2}:=\mathscr{B}_{2}\times\left(\frac{3}{2},2\right)
to be an open cylinder and
\mathcal{C}_{2}:=\mathcal{B}_{2}\times\left[1,\frac{3}{2}\right]
to be a cylinder connecting the cube
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
and the cylinder
\mathscr{C}_{2}
.

We continue the construction by induction. For
k\in\mathbb{N}

(n-1)
-dimensional closed cubes
\mathscr{Q}_{k}
,
(n-1)
-dimensional balls
\mathscr{B}_{k}
and
\mathcal{B}_{k}
and the cylinders
\mathscr{C}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. Here
\mathscr{Q}_{k}
is one of
2^{n-1}
equivalent subcubes of the dyadic cube from
\mathscr{Q}_{k-1}
. Fix a remaining cube in
\mathscr{Q}_{k-1}
and denote it by
\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}_{k}
. Divide
\mathscr{Q}^{\prime}_{k}
into
2^{n-1}
equivalent
(n-1)
-dimensional closed cubes such that their interiors are pairwise disjoint. Fix one of them and denote it by
\mathscr{Q}_{k+1}
. Its edge length is
\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}
. Set
z_{k+1}
to be the center of
\mathscr{Q}_{k+1}
. Define

\mathscr{B}_{k+1}:=B^{n-1}\left(z_{k+1},\frac{1}{2^{k+2}}\right)\ {\rm and}\ \mathcal{B}_{k+1}:={B^{n-1}}\left(z_{k+1},\left(\frac{1}{4^{k+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}+\frac{p}{q}}\right).

Define
\mathscr{C}_{k+1}:=\mathscr{B}_{k+1}\times(2,3)
and define
\mathcal{C}_{k+1}:=\mathcal{B}_{k+1}\times[1,2]
to be a cylinder which connects
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
and
\mathscr{C}_{k+1}
. To simplify the notation, for every
1\leq k<\infty
, we use
\tilde{r}_{k}
and
r_{k}

\mathscr{B}_{k}

\tilde{r}_{k}=\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\ {\rm and}\ r_{k}=\left(\frac{1}{4^{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}+\frac{p}{q}}.

In conclusion, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have constructed two cylinders
\mathscr{C}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}
such that the cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{k}
connects the unit cube
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
and the cylinder
\mathscr{C}_{k}
. Then our domain
{\Omega}_{p,q}
is defined by setting

{\Omega}_{p,q}:=\mathcal{Q}_{0}\cup\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathscr{C}_{k}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}_{k}\right).

Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

(5.3)

{\Omega}^{m}_{p,q}:=\mathcal{Q}_{0}\cup\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\mathscr{C}_{k}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\mathcal{C}_{k}\right).

.

. Hence, by Lemma

.

Next, we show that
{\Omega}_{p,q}
is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain. The essential point is that every cylinder whose size is fixed from above, is a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain for
1\leq q<n-1
and
(n-1)q/(n-1-q)\leq p<\infty
. This observation was used in the paper
[
9
]
by Koskela, Ukhlov and Zhu to show that for
1\leq q<n-1
and
(n-1)q/(n-1-q)<p\leq\infty
, there exists a bounded
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain
{\Omega}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
whose boundary is of positive volume. The same observation was used in the paper
[
10
]
by Koskela and Zhu to show that every outward cuspidal domain in
{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
with
n\geq 3
is always a
(W^{1,p},W^{1,q})
-extension domain for
1\leq q<n-1
and
(n-1)q/(n-1-q)\leq p\leq\infty
. This result is a limit case for a sequence of results about Sobolev extension property of polynomial type outward cuspidal domains by Maz’ya and Poborchi in
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
.

5.2.
Cut-off functions

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}:=B^{n-1}(0,r)\times[0,1]
and
\mathcal{C}:=\overline{B^{n-1}}(0,\frac{r}{2})\times[0,1]
. Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
is a cylinder and
\mathcal{C}
is a closed sub-cylinder of
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
. We define
A_{\mathcal{C}}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\mathcal{C}
. We employ the cylindrical coordinate system

\left\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})=(s,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{n-2},x_{n})\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\right\}

where
\{(0,0,\cdots,x_{n}):x_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
is the rotation axis and
s:=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}}
. To simplify the notation, we write
\vec{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{n-2})
. In this cylindrical coordinate system, we can write

\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}=\left\{x=(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n})\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}:x_{n}\in[0,1],s\in[0,r),\vec{\theta}\in[0,\pi)^{n-3}\times[0,2\pi)\right\},

{\mathcal{C}}=\left\{x=(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n})\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}:x_{n}\in[0,1],s\in\left[0,\frac{r}{2}\right],\vec{\theta}\in[0,\pi)^{n-3}\times[0,2\pi)\right\}

and

A_{\mathcal{C}}=\left\{x=(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n})\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}:x_{n}\in[0,1],s\in\left(\frac{r}{2},r\right),\vec{\theta}\in[0,\pi)^{n-3}\times[0,2\pi)\right\}.

For
1<r<2
, we define two subsets
D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}
of the cylinder
\widetilde{C}
by setting

D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}:=\left\{x=\left(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n}\right)\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}:x_{n}\in\left(1-\frac{r}{2},1\right),s\in\left(\frac{r}{2},x_{n}+r-1\right),\vec{\theta}\in[0,\pi)^{n-3}\times[0,2\pi)\right\}

and

D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}:=\left\{x=\left(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n}\right)\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}:x_{n}\in\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right),s\in\left(\frac{r}{2},r-x_{n}\right),\vec{\theta}\in[0,\pi)^{n-3}\times[0,2\pi)\right\}.

The next lemma gives three cut-off functions towards the construction of the desired extension operator. It is almost the same as
[

, Lemma
7.2
]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

(1)
: There exists a cut-off function
L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}:\overline{A_{\mathcal{C}}}\to[0,1]
, which is continuous on
\overline{A_{\mathcal{C}}}\setminus\left(\partial B^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\times\{0,1\}\right)
, which equals
0
both on
\left(\overline{B}^{n-1}(0,r)\setminus B^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)\times\{0,1\}
and on the set
\partial B^{n-1}(0,r)\times(0,1)
, which equals
1
on
\partial B^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\times(0,1)
and which has the following additional properties. The function
L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}
is Lipschitz on
A_{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\left(\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\cup\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\right)
with

|\nabla L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{r}\ {\rm for}\ x\in A_{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\left(\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\cup\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\right)

and
L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}
is locally Lipschitz on
D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}
with

|\nabla L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+(1-x_{n})^{2}}}\ {\rm for}\ x\in D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}

and
L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}
is locally Lipschitz on
D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}
with

|\nabla L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+x_{n}^{2}}}\ {\rm for}\ x\in D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}.

(2)
: There exists a cut-off function
L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}}:\overline{A_{\mathcal{C}}}\to[0,1]
, which is continuous on
\overline{A_{\mathcal{C}}}\setminus\left(\partial B^{n-1}\left(0,r\right)\times\{0,1\}\right)
, which equals
0
on
\partial B^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\times[0,1)
, and which equals
1
both on
\partial B^{n-1}(0,r)\times(0,1)
and on
\left(B^{n-1}(0,r)\setminus\overline{B}^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)\times\{0,1\}
, and which has the additional following properties. The function
L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}}
is Lipschitz on
A_{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\left(\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\cup\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\right)
with

|\nabla L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{r}\ {\rm for}\ x\in A_{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\left(\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\cup\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\right)

and
L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}}
is locally Lipschitz on
D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}
with

|\nabla L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+(1-x_{n})^{2}}}\ {\rm for}\ x\in D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}

and
L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}}
is locally Lipschitz on
D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}
with

|\nabla L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+x_{n}^{2}}}\ {\rm for}\ x\in D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}.

Proof.

(1):
We define the cut-off function
L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}
on
\overline{A_{\mathcal{C}}}
with respect to the cylindrical coordinate system
\left\{x=\left(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n}\right)\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\right\}
by setting

(5.4)

L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{-2}{r}s+2,\ \ &\ {\rm if}\ x\in\overline{A_{\mathcal{C}}}\setminus\left(\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\cup\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\right),\\
\frac{x_{n}}{x_{n}+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)},\ \ &\ {\rm if}\ x\in\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\setminus\partial B^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\times\{0\},\\
\frac{1-x_{n}}{(1-x_{n})+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)},\ \ &\ {\rm if}\ x\in\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\setminus\partial B^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\times\{1\},\\
0,\ \ &\ {\rm if}\ x\in\partial B^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\times\{0,1\}.\end{cases}

Then, if
x\in A_{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\left(\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\cup\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\right)
, we have

\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial\theta_{1}}=\cdots=\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\theta_{n-2}}=\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial x_{n}}=0\ {\rm and}\ \left|\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial s}\right|=\frac{2}{r}.

If
x\in D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}
, we have

\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial\theta_{1}}=\cdots=\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial\theta_{n-2}}=0,

\left|\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial s}\right|=\left|\frac{x_{n}}{\left(x_{n}+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}\right|\leq\left|\frac{x_{n}+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)}{\left(x_{n}+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+x_{n}^{2}}}

and

\left|\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial x_{n}}\right|=\left|\frac{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)}{\left(x_{n}+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}\right|\leq\left|\frac{x_{n}+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)}{\left(x_{n}+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+x_{n}^{2}}}.

If
x\in D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}
, we have

\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial\theta_{1}}=\cdots=\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial\theta_{n-2}}=0,

\left|\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial s}\right|=\left|\frac{1-x_{n}}{\left(x_{n}+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}\right|\leq\left|\frac{(1-x_{n})+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)}{\left((1-x_{n})+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+(1-x_{n})^{2}}}

and

\left|\frac{\partial L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)}{\partial x_{n}}\right|=\left|\frac{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)}{\left((1-x_{n})+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}\right|\leq\left|\frac{(1-x_{n})+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)}{\left((1-x_{n})+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+(1-x_{n})^{2}}}.

Hence, we have

(5.5)

\left|\nabla L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)\right|\leq\begin{cases}\frac{C}{r},\ \ &{\rm for}\ \ x\in A_{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\left(\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\cup\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\right),\\
\frac{C}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+x_{n}^{2}}},\ \ &{\rm for}\ \ x\in D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}},\\
\frac{C}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(1-x_{n}\right)^{2}}},\ \ &{\rm for}\ \ x\in D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}.\end{cases}

(2):
We define the cut-off function
L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}}
on
\overline{A_{\mathcal{C}}}
with respect the cylindrical coordinate system
\left\{x=\left(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n}\right)\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\right\}
by setting

(5.6)

L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{r}s-1,\ \ &{\rm if}\ x\in\overline{A_{\mathcal{C}}}\setminus\left(\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\cup\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\right),\\
\frac{s-\frac{r}{2}}{x_{n}+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)},\ \ &{\rm if}\ x\in\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\setminus\partial B^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\times\{0\},\\
\frac{s-\frac{r}{2}}{(1-x_{n})+\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)},\ \ &{\rm if}\ x\in\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\setminus\partial B^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\times\{1\},\\
0,\ \ &{\rm if}\ x\in\partial B^{n-1}\left(0,\frac{r}{2}\right)\times\{0,1\}.\end{cases}

By similar computations, we have

(5.7)

\left|\nabla L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)\right|\leq\begin{cases}\frac{C}{r},\ \ &{\rm for}\ \ x\in A_{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\left(\overline{D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}}\cup\overline{D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}}}\right),\\
\frac{C}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+x_{n}^{2}}},\ \ &{\rm for}\ \ x\in D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}},\\
\frac{C}{\sqrt{\left(s-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(1-x_{n}\right)^{2}}},\ \ &{\rm for}\ \ x\in D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}}.\end{cases}

∎

5.3.
The extension operator

Towards the construction of our extension operator, we define piston-shaped domains
P_{k}
by setting

P_{k}:=2\mathcal{B}_{k}\times(0,1)\cup\mathcal{B}_{k}\times[1,2).

The class of piston-shaped domains
\{P_{k}\}
are pairwise disjoint. Given cylinders
\mathscr{C}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, to simplify the notation, we write
L_{k}=L_{\mathscr{C}_{k}},L^{i}_{k}=L^{i}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}
and
L^{o}_{k}=L^{o}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}
. We define cut-off functions
L
,
L^{i}
and
L^{o}
by setting

(5.8)

\mathscr{L}^{i}(x):=\sum_{k}L^{i}_{k}(x)\ {\rm for}\ x\in\bigcup_{k}\overline{\mathscr{A}_{k}},

(5.9)

L^{i}(x):=\sum_{k}L^{i}_{k}(x)\ {\rm for}\ x\in\bigcup_{k}\overline{\mathcal{A}_{k}}

and

(5.10)

L^{o}(x):=\sum_{k}L^{o}_{k}(x)\ {\rm for}\ x\in\bigcup_{k}\overline{\mathcal{A}_{k}}.

On
\mathscr{Q}_{0}\times[1,2]
, we define a cut-off function
L_{1}
by setting

(5.11)

L_{1}(x):=2-x_{n}\ {\rm for\ every}\ x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathscr{Q}_{0}\times[1,2].

On
\mathscr{Q}_{0}\times(1,2)
, we define a reflection
R_{1}
by setting

(5.12)

R_{1}(x):=\left(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n-1},2-x_{n}\right)\ {\rm for\ every}\ x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathscr{Q}_{0}\times(1,2).

Simple computations give the estimates

(5.13)

\frac{1}{C}\leq\left|J_{R_{1}}(x)\right|\leq C\ {\rm and}\ \left|DR_{1}(x)\right|\leq C,

for every
x\in\mathscr{Q}_{0}\times(1,2)
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define two cylinders by setting

\widetilde{\mathscr{C}_{k}}:=2\mathscr{B}_{k}\times(2,3)\ {\rm and}\ \widetilde{\mathcal{C}_{k}}:=2\mathcal{B}_{k}\times[1,2],

and define

\mathscr{A}_{k}:=\widetilde{\mathscr{C}_{k}}\setminus\overline{\mathscr{C}_{k}}\ {\rm and}\ \mathcal{A}_{k}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{C}_{k}}.

We also define

\mathscr{D}^{L}_{k}:=D^{L}_{\mathscr{C}_{k}},\mathcal{D}^{L}_{k}:=D^{L}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\ {\rm and}\ \mathcal{D}^{U}_{k}:=D^{U}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}.

On the set
\bigcup_{k}\mathscr{A}_{k}
, we define a mapping
\mathscr{R}
which is a reflection on every
\mathscr{A}_{k}
. With respect to the local cylindrical coordinate system on every
\mathscr{A}_{k}
, we write

(5.14)

\mathscr{R}(x):=\mathscr{R}\left(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n}\right)=\left(-\frac{s}{2}+\frac{3}{2}\tilde{r}_{k},\vec{\theta},x_{n}\right)

for
x=(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n})\in\mathscr{A}_{k}
. Simple computations give the estimate

(5.15)

\frac{1}{C}\leq\left|J_{\mathscr{R}}(x)\right|\leq C\ {\rm and}\ \left|D\mathscr{R}(x)\right|\leq C

for every
x\in\bigcup_{k}\mathscr{A}_{k}
. On the set
\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{A}_{k}
, we define a mapping
\mathcal{R}
which is a reflection on every
\mathcal{A}_{k}
. With respect to the local cylindrical coordinate system on every
\mathcal{A}_{k}
, we write

(5.16)

\mathcal{R}(x):=\mathcal{R}\left(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n}\right)=\left(-\frac{s}{2}+\frac{3}{2}r_{k},\vec{\theta},x_{n}\right)

for
x=(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n})\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
. Simple computations give the estimate

(5.17)

\frac{1}{C}\leq\left|J_{\mathcal{R}}(x)\right|\leq C\ {\rm and}\ \left|D\mathcal{R}(x)\right|\leq C

for every
x\in\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{A}_{k}
.

Define
\mathcal{C}:=\mathscr{Q}_{0}\times(0,3)
to be a cylinder which contains the domain
{\Omega}_{p,q}
. In order to explain the construction of our bounded linear extension operator, we need to define a subset
U_{1}
of
\mathcal{C}
. Define

U_{1}:=\mathscr{Q}_{0}\times[1,2]\setminus\bigcup_{k}{\mathcal{C}_{k}}

As we said,
{\Omega}_{p,q}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
satisfies the segment condition. Hence,
C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}_{p,q}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
is dense in
W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
. We begin by defining our linear extension operator on the dense subspace
C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}_{p,q}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
of
W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
. Given
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}_{p,q}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
, we define the extension
E(u)
on the rectangle
\mathcal{C}
by setting

(5.18)

E(u)(x):=\begin{cases}u(x),\ &{\rm for}\ x\in{\Omega}_{p,q},\\
L_{1}(x)\left(u\circ R_{1}(x)\right),\ &{\rm for}\ x\in U_{1}\setminus\bigcup_{k}\overline{\mathcal{A}_{k}},\\
\mathscr{L}^{i}(x)\left(u\circ\mathscr{R}(x)\right),\ &{\rm for}\ x\in\bigcup_{k}\overline{\mathscr{A}_{k}},\\
L_{1}(x)L^{o}(x)\left(u\circ R_{1}(x)\right)+L^{i}(x)\left(u\circ\mathcal{R}(x)\right),\ &{\rm for}\ x\in\bigcup_{k}\overline{\mathcal{A}_{k}},\\
0,\ &{\rm elsewhere}.\end{cases}

We continue with the local properties of our extension operator.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
E
be the extension operator defined in (
5.18
). Then, for every
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}_{p,q}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
, we have
E(u)\in C(\mathcal{C})\cap W^{1,q}(\mathcal{C})
and following properties:

(1)
:
E(u)
is differentiable almost everywhere on
\mathring{U}_{1}\setminus\bigcup_{k}\overline{\mathcal{A}_{k}}
with

(5.19)

\left|\nabla E(u)(x)\right|\leq\left|\nabla L_{1}(x)\left(u\circ R_{1}(x)\right)\right|+\left|L_{1}(x)\nabla\left(u\circ R_{1}(x)\right)\right|

for almost every
x\in\mathring{U_{1}}\setminus\bigcup_{k}\overline{\mathcal{A}_{k}}
.

(2)
:
E(u)
is differentiable almost everywhere on
\mathscr{A}_{k}
with

(5.20)

\left|\nabla E(u)(x)\right|\leq\left|\nabla\mathscr{L}^{i}(x)\left(u\circ\mathscr{R}(x)\right)\right|+\left|\mathscr{L}^{i}(x)\nabla\left(u\circ\mathscr{R}(x)\right)\right|

for almost every
x\in\mathscr{A}_{k}
.

(3)
:
E(u)
is differentiable almost everywhere on
\mathcal{A}_{k}
with

(5.21)

\left|\nabla E(u)(x)\right|\leq\left|\nabla\left(L_{1}(x)L^{o}(x)\right)\left(u\circ R_{1}(x)\right)\right|+\left|L_{1}(x)L^{o}(x)\nabla\left(u\circ R_{1}(x)\right)\right|\\
+\left|\nabla L^{i}(x)\left(u\circ\mathcal{R}(x)\right)\right|+\left|L^{i}(x)\nabla\left(u\circ\mathcal{R}(x)\right)\right|

for almost every
x\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
.

Moreover, with respect to the local cylindrical coordinate system
x=\left(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n}\right)
on
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}_{k}}
, for every
1\leq q<\infty
, we have

(5.22)

\int_{\widetilde{\mathscr{C}_{k}}}\left|E(u)(x)\right|^{q}dx\leq C\int_{\mathscr{C}_{k}}\left|u(x)\right|^{q}dx

and

(5.23)

\int_{\widetilde{\mathscr{C}_{k}}}\left|\nabla E(u)(x)\right|^{q}dx\leq C\int_{\mathscr{C}_{k}}\left|\nabla u(x)\right|^{q}dx\\
+C\int_{\mathscr{D}^{L}_{k}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{x_{n}^{2}+\left(s-\frac{\tilde{r}_{k}}{2}\right)^{2}}}\right)^{q}\left|u\circ\mathscr{R}(x)\right|^{q}dx\\
+C\int_{\mathscr{D}^{U}_{k}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\left(1-x_{n}\right)^{2}+\left(s-\frac{\tilde{r}_{k}}{2}\right)^{2}}}\right)^{q}\left|u\circ\mathscr{R}(x)\right|^{q}dx\\
+C\int_{\mathscr{A}_{k}\setminus\left(\overline{\mathscr{D}^{L}_{k}}\cup\overline{\mathscr{D}^{U}_{k}}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{r}_{k}}\right)^{q}\left|u\circ\mathscr{R}(x)\right|^{q}dx,

with some uniform positive constant
C
. With respect to the local cylindrical coordinate system
x=\left(s,\vec{\theta},x_{n}\right)
on
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}_{k}}
, for every
1\leq q<\infty
, we have

(5.24)

\int_{\widetilde{\mathcal{C}_{k}}}\left|E(u)(x)\right|^{q}dx\leq C\int_{P_{k}}\left|u(x)\right|^{q}dx

and

(5.25)

\int_{\widetilde{\mathcal{C}_{k}}}\left|\nabla E(u)(x)\right|^{q}dx\leq C\int_{P_{k}}\left|\nabla u(x)\right|^{q}dx\\
+C\int_{\mathcal{D}^{U}_{k}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\left(1-x_{n}\right)^{2}+\left(s-\frac{r_{k}}{2}\right)^{2}}}\right)^{q}\left(\left|u\circ R_{1}(x)\right|^{q}+\left|u\circ\mathcal{R}(x)\right|^{q}\right)dx\\
+C\int_{\mathcal{D}^{L}_{k}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{x_{n}^{2}+\left(s-\frac{r_{k}}{2}\right)^{2}}}\right)^{q}\left(\left|u\circ R_{1}(x)\right|^{q}+\left|u\circ\mathcal{R}(x)\right|^{q}\right)dx\\
+C\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}\setminus\left(\overline{\mathcal{D}^{L}_{k}}\cup\overline{\mathcal{D}^{U}_{k}}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{r_{k}}\right)^{q}\left(\left|u\circ R_{1}(x)\right|^{q}+\left|u\circ\mathcal{R}(x)\right|^{q}\right)dx,

with some uniform positive constant
C
.

Proof.

For every
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}_{p,q}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
, by the properties of our cut-off functions and reflections and the definition of
E(u)
, it is easy to see that
E(u)
belongs to
W^{1,q}_{loc}(\mathcal{C})
and differentiable almost everywhere both on
\mathring{U_{1}}
,
\mathscr{A}_{k}
and
\mathcal{A}_{k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The inequalities (
5.19
), (
5.20
) and (
5.21
) come from the chain rule.

By the definition of
E(u)
in (
5.18
), on every
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}_{k}}
, we have

(5.26)

\int_{\widetilde{\mathscr{C}_{k}}}\left|E(u)(x)\right|^{q}dx\leq\int_{\mathscr{C}_{k}}\left|u(x)\right|^{q}dx+\int_{\mathscr{A}_{k}}\left|\mathscr{L}^{i}(x)\left(u\circ\mathscr{R}(x)\right)\right|^{q}dx.

Since
0\leq\mathscr{L}^{i}(x)\leq 1
for every
x\in\mathscr{A}_{k}
, by (
5.15
) and the change of variables formula, we have

(5.27)

\int_{\mathscr{A}_{k}}\left|\mathscr{L}^{i}(x)\left(u\circ\mathscr{R}(x)\right)\right|^{q}dx\leq\int_{\mathscr{A}_{k}}\left|u\circ\mathscr{R}(x)\right|^{q}dx\leq C\int_{\mathscr{C}_{k}}\left|u(x)\right|^{q}dx.

By combining inequalities (
5.26
) and (
5.27
), we obtain the inequality (
5.22
).

By inequality (
5.20
), we have

(5.28)

\leq\left(\sum_{k}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}\setminus\left(\overline{\mathcal{D}^{L}_{k}}\cup\overline{\mathcal{D}^{U}_{k}}\right)}\left(\left|u\circ R_{1}(x)\right|^{p}+\left|u\circ\mathcal{R}(x)\right|^{p}\right)dx\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}\\
\times\left(\sum_{k}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}\setminus\left(\overline{\mathcal{D}^{L}_{k}}\cup\overline{\mathcal{D}^{U}_{k}}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{r_{k}}\right)^{\frac{pq}{p-q}}dx\right)^{\frac{p-q}{p}}.

By (

By (

(5.72)

\sum_{k}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}\setminus\left(\overline{\mathcal{D}^{L}_{k}}\cup\overline{\mathcal{D}^{U}_{k}}\right)}\left(\left|u\circ R_{1}(x)\right|^{p}+\left|u\circ\mathcal{R}(x)\right|^{p}\right)dx\\
\leq C\int_{R_{1}\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}\setminus\left(\overline{\mathcal{D}^{L}_{k}}\right)\right)}\left|u(x)\right|^{p}dx+\int_{\mathcal{R}\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}\setminus\left(\overline{\mathcal{D}^{L}_{k}}\cup\overline{\mathcal{D}^{U}_{k}}\right)\right)}\left|u(x)\right|^{p}dx\\
\leq C\int_{{\Omega}_{p,q}}\left|u(x)\right|^{p}dx.

By combining (
5.70
), (
5.71
) and (
5.72
), we obtain

(5.73)

\sum_{k}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{k}\setminus\left(\overline{\mathcal{D}^{L}_{k}}\cup\overline{\mathcal{D}^{U}_{k}}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{r_{k}}\right)^{q}\left(\left|u\circ R_{1}(x)\right|^{q}+\left|u\circ\mathcal{R}(x)\right|^{q}\right)dx\leq C\left(\int_{{\Omega}_{p,q}}\left|u(x)\right|^{p}dx\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}.

By combining (
5.63
), (
5.64
), (
5.68
), (
5.69
) and (
5.73
), we obtain

(5.74)

\int_{\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{A}_{k}}\left|\nabla E(u)(x)\right|^{q}dx\leq C\left(\int_{{\Omega}_{p,q}}\left(\left|u(x)\right|^{p}+\left|\nabla u(x)\right|^{p}\right)dx\right)^{\frac{q}{p}},

where the constant
C
is independent of
u
. Finally, by combining (
5.45
), (
5.46
), (
5.50
), (
5.62
) and (
5.74
), we obtain the desired norm control for the derivative of

. That is

(5.75)

\left(\int_{\mathcal{C}}\left|\nabla E(u)(x)\right|^{q}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq C\left(\int_{{\Omega}_{p,q}}\left(\left|u(x)\right|^{p}+\left|\nabla u(x)\right|^{p}\right)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

with a constant
C
independent of
u
.

By combining (
5.44
) and (
5.75
), we know that the extension operator
E
defined in (
5.18
) is bounded from the dense subspace
C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}_{p,q}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
of
W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
to
W^{1,q}(\mathcal{C})
. This means that for every
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}_{p,q}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
, we have
E(u)\in W^{1,q}(\mathcal{C})
with

\|E(u)\|_{W^{1,q}(\mathcal{C})}\leq C\|u\|_{W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})},

where the constant
C
is independent of
u
. Since
C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}_{p,q}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
is dense in
W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
, for every
u\in W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
, there exists a Cauchy sequence
\{u_{m}\}\subset C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}_{p,q}})\cap W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
which converges to
u
in
W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
. Furthermore, there exists a subsequence of
\{u_{m}\}
which converges to
u
almost everywhere in
{\Omega}_{p,q}
. To simplify the notation, we still use
\{u_{m}\}
for this subsequence. By (

),
\{E(u_{m})\}
is a Cauchy sequence in the Sobolev space

and hence converges to some function

with respect to
W^{1,q}
-norm. Furthermore, there exists a subsequence of
\{E(u_{m})\}
which converges to
v
almost everywhere in
\mathcal{C}
. On the other hand, by the definition of
E(u_{m})
and

, we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}E(u_{m})(x)=E(u)(x)

for almost every

. Hence
v(x)=E(u)(x)
almost everywhere. This implies that
E(u)\in W^{1,q}(\mathcal{C})
with

(5.76)

\displaystyle\|E(u)\|_{W^{1,q}(\mathcal{C})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|v\|_{W^{1,q}(\mathcal{C})}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\|E(u_{m})\|_{W^{1,q}(\mathcal{C})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\lim_{m\to\infty}\|u\|_{W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})}\leq C\|u\|_{W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})},

where the constant
C
is independent of

. Hence, the linear extension operator

defined in (

to

. The fact that

-extension domain.

Next, we show that
{\Omega}_{p,q}
is not a
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
-extension domain for

and

.

Theorem 5.2
.

Fix
1\leq q<n-1
and
(n-1)q/(n-1-q)<p<\infty
. Then
{\Omega}_{p,q}\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
defined in (
5.2
) is not an
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
-extension domain.

Proof.

Fix
1\leq q<n-1
and
(n-1)q/(n-1-q)<p<\infty
. Assume that
{\Omega}_{p,q}
is a
(L^{1,p},L^{1,q})
-extension domain, then for every
u\in L^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
, there exists a function
E(u)\in L^{1,q}(B(0,10))
with
E(u)\big|_{{\Omega}_{p,q}}\equiv u
and

\|\nabla E(u)\|_{L^{q}(B(0,10))}\leq C\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}({\Omega}_{p,q})}

with a positive constant
C
independent of
u
. By Theorem
1.1
, we have

L^{1,q}(B(0,10))=W^{1,q}(B(0,10))

as a set of functions. Hence we have
E(u)\in W^{1,q}(B(0,10))
and
u\in L^{1,q}({\Omega}_{p,q})
. Hence, to prove the theorem, it suffices to construct a function
u
which is in the homogeneous Sobolev space
L^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
but not in the Sobolev space
W^{1,q}({\Omega}_{p,q})
. Set
u\equiv 0
on
\mathcal{Q}_{o}
and
u\equiv(4^{k})^{\frac{n-1}{q}}
on
\mathscr{C}_{k}
. On every
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, we define
u
to be increasing linearly with respect to
x_{n}
from
0
in
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
to
(4^{k})^{\frac{n-1}{q}}
in
\mathscr{C}_{k}
. To be precise,

u(x)=(4^{k})^{\frac{n-1}{q}}x_{n}-(4^{k})^{\frac{n-1}{q}}

for every
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
. By some simple computations, we have

(5.77)

|\nabla u(x)|=\begin{cases}(4^{k})^{\frac{n-1}{q}},\ &\ {\rm if}\ x\in\mathcal{C}_{k},\\
0,\ &\ {\rm elsewhere}.\end{cases}

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{p,q}}|\nabla u(x)|^{p}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(4^{k})^{\frac{(n-1)p}{q}}\mathcal{H}^{n}\left(\mathcal{C}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(4^{k})^{\frac{(n-1)p}{q}}\left(\frac{1}{4^{k+1}}\right)^{1+\frac{(n-1)p}{q}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{k+1}}<\infty.

Hence
u\in L^{1,p}({\Omega}_{p,q})
. By a simple computation, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}_{p,q}}|u(x)|^{q}dx

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{Q}_{k}\cap{\Omega}_{p,q}}|u(x)|^{q}dx

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{4^{k+1}}\right)^{n-1}(4^{k})^{n-1}=\infty.

Hence, we also have
u\notin W^{1,q}({\Omega}_{p,q})
as desired.
∎

6.
Construction of an Example

In this section, we construct a domain

Let
