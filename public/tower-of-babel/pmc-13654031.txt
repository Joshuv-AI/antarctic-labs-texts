Incoming United States Army Basic Combat Trainees Report Lower Dietary Supplement Use than Active-Duty Soldiers☆
Authors: Maria C Canino, Stefan M Pasiakos, Joseph J Knapik, John A Caldwell, Meaghan E Beckner, Emily K Farina, Lauren A Thompson, Bridget A Owens, J Philip Karl, Lee M Margolis, Daniel W Trone, Ryan A Steelman, James P McClung, Harris R Lieberman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDietary supplement (DS) use by active-duty service members has been extensively studied, with >70% reporting use largely for purported effects on physical performance, weight management, and health. However, the prevalence of DS use among civilian incoming military trainees, before basic combat training, has not been extensively examined.ObjectivesThis study aimed to compare the prevalence of DS use among incoming United States (US) Army trainees with a cohort of active-duty soldiers.MethodsUsing a similar questionnaire, US Army male infantry soldiers (n = 451, mean time in service 9.2 y) reported DS use, and 112 incoming male infantry trainees reported use before starting basic combat training. Responses were classified into 9 predefined DS categories and were based on how many DSs a participant reported taking over a period of 1 wk. χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare frequencies of DS use between groups and across DS categories. The total number of categories from which participants reported using DSs was also calculated.ResultsPrevalence of DS use among soldiers was 81% compared with 59% for trainees (P < 0.001). The most frequently used DSs among soldiers were protein (58%), pre/post workout (48%), multivitamin/mineral (MVM; 48%), weight/fat loss (37%), and individual vitamin/mineral (IVM; 34%) products. The most frequently used DSs among trainees were protein (45%), pre/post workout (28%), MVM (24%), IVM (23%), and muscle-building (21%) products. Trainees used less DSs across all categories (all P < 0.05) and consumed DSs from fewer categories than soldiers (median: 1.0 compared with 2.0 categories; P < 0.001).ConclusionsDS use was higher in soldiers than in incoming US Army trainees overall and across all categories of DSs. These cross-sectional findings indicate that DS use may change between the start of army training and active-duty service. Longitudinal analyses should be conducted to assess changes in DS use over time.Keywords: military, multivitamin/mineral supplement, protein supplement, weight loss supplement, pre/post workout supplement

Introduction

Dietary supplement (DS) use among active-duty service members is well-documented. Studies have reported the overall prevalence of DS use [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]], factors contributing to DS use [[5], [6], [7], [8]], reasons why service members use DSs [4,6,8,9], and rates of adverse effects resulting from specific [7,[10], [11], [12]] or multiple categories [13] of DSs. In a large cohort of active-duty service members of all military branches, more than 70% reported regular or routine use of DSs at least once per week [2,14,15] to improve physical performance and health, support postexercise recovery, increase energy, and maintain body weight [4,6,8,9]. DS use is greater in females, older service members, those with more education, those who engage in resistance exercise training, and have higher BMI [2,7]. Heavy use of DS carries potential risk; approximately 20% of DS users report experiencing at least one seemingly related adverse event, such as heart palpitations, tingling/numbness, and sleep disturbances [7,10]. Despite these risks, DS use by service members remains high. Motivating factors contributing to high usage rates may, in part, include the mandatory physical performance and weight requirements necessary for military service to meet occupational demands and achieve a desired physical appearance [8]. Accordingly, products marketed to improve health and performance, including those promoted through marketing strategies directed toward military populations [[16], [17], [18]], appeal to service members seeking to enhance their ability to meet occupational requirements and personal goals rather than relying on exercise and healthy diet.

It has not been established whether military service status per se changes an individual’s use of DSs from preservice to active-duty service. Although the Department of War (DoW) has established policies to encourage safe DS use, including education, prohibited ingredient list, and adverse event monitoring [19], these policies do not apply to DS use among incoming trainees before they join the military. The absence of data on DS use among incoming trainees prior to beginning military service limits the ability to evaluate how DS use changes from preaccession to a long-term military career and identify potential safety risks associated with prior use. It has been reported that 50% of US Marine Corps [20] and 41% of US Air Force [21] incoming trainees have used DSs, which is markedly lower than the 70% of active-duty service members using DSs [[1], [2], [3]]. However, the prevalence of DS use among incoming US Army trainees has not been examined. As such, this cross-sectional study compared the prevalence of DS use and the types of DSs used between incoming US Army basic combat trainees and active-duty soldiers using a similar questionnaire. We hypothesized that the incoming trainees would have a lower prevalence of DS use overall and across several DS categories than active-duty soldiers.

Methods

US Army basic combat trainees

Incoming infantry trainees were enrolled in US Army one-station unit training (OSUT) at Fort Benning, GA, as part of training cohorts that began in September 2022 and April 2023. Although sex was not an exclusion criterion for study enrollment, only male trainees were in the cohorts available during the study period. Approximately 400 male infantry trainees attended briefings to recruit participants for the study. Of those, 116 trainees were enrolled, and the final analytic sample for this phase of the study included 112 trainees. Anthropometrics were collected on consented trainees after an overnight fast upon awakening (between 05:00 and 07:00) while wearing their physical training uniform (or similar clothing) and without footwear. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a stadiometer (SECA, Chino, CA); body mass was assessed using a digital weight scale (InBody 770, Cerritos, CA). Trainees were asked to report their age and highest education level attained (Table 1). The US Army Medical Research and Development Command Institutional Review Board approved this study. Investigators adhered to policies and procedures for protection of human subjects as prescribed by Department of Defense Instruction 3216.01, and the research was conducted in adherence with provisions of 32 Code of Federal Regulations Part 219.

Active-duty US Army infantry soldiers

To recruit participants for a survey of DS use, the Defense Manpower Data Center (DMDC) provided a list of 200,000 randomly selected service members from all services on active duty in 2018 with at least six months of service and an estimated one year remaining before separation from service [2]. Of those invited to participate, 18.2% responded (n = 26,681). Participants self-reported their age, height, body mass, highest education level attained, and time in service. Military occupational specialty was obtained from military records available from DMDC. To ensure an appropriate comparison with incoming infantry trainees participating in OSUT, the sample was limited to US Army male infantry soldiers (n = 451). The Naval Health Research Center Institutional Review Board approved the investigation, and participants signed an informed consent document. Investigators adhered to policies and procedures for protection of human subjects as prescribed by Department of Defense Instruction 3216.01, and the research was conducted in adherence to provisions of 32 Code of Federal Regulations, Part 219.

Dietary supplement survey

Active-duty soldiers completed an online questionnaire on DS use [22], and incoming trainees completed a similar version in a paper format because of logistical- and facility-related constraints of the military basic training environment, including the lack of available internet-connected devices. The DS categories defined [10,14] were mutually exclusive and included multivitamin/mineral (MVM), individual vitamin/mineral (IVM), protein, herbal, muscle building, weight/fat loss, pre/post workout, joint health, and fish oil/omega-3 . A product from the MVM category is a DS that contains a combination of essential vitamins and/or minerals, whereas a product from the IVM category contains a single vitamin or mineral supplement (e.g., vitamin D3, iron, calcium, and magnesium). For each category, an open-text field on the questionnaire was available for participants to include supplements not listed. These supplements were assigned to their proper DS category by trained investigators. Active-duty soldiers reported their frequency of use for the listed DS categories (Supplemental Figure 1) during the past six months using a five-point Likert scale (i.e., never, once a month, once a week, 2–6 days/week, or daily). Incoming OSUT trainees reported whether they had used these same DS categories at least once per week during the last 30 days before starting OSUT.

Data analysis

Active-duty soldiers and incoming trainees were classified as DS users if they reported using any DS category at least once per week; if not, they were classified as a nonuser. Similarly, participants were considered a user of a given category if they reported using at least one DS in that category at least once per week. The total number of DS categories used by each participant was calculated as the sum of DS categories for which they were classified as a user.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and visualized in RStudio software version 2024.12.0 + 467 (Posit Software, Boston, MA). Descriptive statistics were calculated and reported as means ± SDs or frequencies (percentages), unless otherwise indicated. Independent-sample t tests [t (degrees of freedom)] were used to compare group differences in age, body mass, height, and BMI. Cohen’s d effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for between-group comparisons. Mann–Whitney U tests were used to assess group differences in education and total number of DS categories used. Given that these variables were positively skewed and present with low frequencies in certain ordinal levels, rank-biserial correlation (rpb) [23] was calculated to quantify effect size. The χ2 or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the frequencies of DS users between the groups and across DS categories. Odds ratios with 95% CIs were calculated for significant associations. The significance level for all analyses was P ≤ 0.05.

Results

Active-duty soldiers were older [t (561) = 17.22; P < 0.001], heavier [t (561) = 6.95; P < 0.001], had higher BMIs [t (561) = 6.29; P < 0.001], and attained higher education (U = 34,249; P < 0.001) than incoming trainees (Table 1).

A significantly lower proportion of incoming trainees were users of any DS compared with soldiers (χ2 = 21.82; P < 0.001) (Table 2 and Supplemental Figure 2). Across all nine DS categories, the prevalence of use was lower among incoming trainees than among soldiers (Table 2; Supplemental Figure 2). Incoming trainees also used DSs from fewer categories than soldiers (median: 1.0 compared with 2.0 categories; U = 34,589; P < 0.001; Figure 1). For the cumulative number of categories used among DS users, 40% of soldiers (n = 146) reported taking DSs from at least five different categories compared with 15% (n = 10) of incoming trainees before starting OSUT. Of the nine DS categories assessed, seven were taken by ≥25% of soldiers in the past six months (i.e., protein, pre/post workout, MVM, weight/fat loss, IVM, muscle building, and fish oil/omega), whereas only two categories (i.e., protein, pre/post workout) were taken by ≥25% of incoming trainees in the prior 30 days before OSUT.

Discussion

This study assessed the frequency of use and DS categories consumed by incoming US Army trainees compared with US active-duty Army soldiers over a one week period. Compared with infantry soldiers, incoming infantry trainees had a lower prevalence of overall DS use, lower use across all DS categories, and used DSs from fewer categories. These findings extend prior research demonstrating the high prevalence and extensive use of purported performance-enhancing DSs and the broad range of DSs used by career soldiers [2,7] by comparing DS use between active-duty infantry soldiers and incoming infantry trainees before starting their US Army service.

The use of DSs by military personnel of other countries has also been studied [[24], [25], [26]]. For example, a North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) Research Task Group was established to study DS use among service members across member nations [4]. The panel concluded that DS use was higher in military populations than in the corresponding civilian populations [4]. The NATO nations do not have identical regulations on the use of DSs, nor do they permit the sale of the same extensive variety of DSs as the US. However, in studies of several NATO nations, DS use by service members was as high as 70%, similar to that in the US. The patterns of use and popularity of specific DSs also tended to be similar across the nations surveyed [4]. A recent survey of a small sample of British high-readiness personnel reported that 75% were using DSs, which is consistent with US reports of high DS use by Special Forces soldiers [4]. In studies of French service members, DS use was as high as 71% during missions abroad, and 68% of Slovenian service members surveyed reported using DSs [4]. On the basis of these findings, it appears that extensive DS use by service members from a number of countries is typical.

To our knowledge, only two studies have characterized DS use among incoming service members, and the methodological approaches used and, as would be expected, the results of those studies differ from this study. For example, 50% of US Marine Corps [20] and 41% of US Air Force [21] trainees reported using DSs; however, these surveys inquired about lifetime use, whereas this study’s survey assessed a finite duration of time, a single week of use. Use of the finite time frame of one week is a measure that aligns with most of the literature on DS use among service members [1,9,27,28] and better reflects habitual DS use [29]. Given that the US Marine Corps and Air Force data were collected more than a decade ago, and DS use has increased in both civilian [[30], [31], [32]] and military [2,5] populations, current rates among incoming trainees may have also increased and should be reexamined.

Although this study did not assess motivation for DS use, improving overall health and enhancing physical performance are commonly reported reasons for DS use among US adults [33,34], college students [35,36], athletes [37,38], and service members [8,9]. These attitudes appear to be amplified in the military environment, as it emphasizes health, readiness, and performance. Specifically, the occupational physical demands [39] and biannual requirements to meet body composition [40] and fitness [41] standards may result in the high prevalence of DS use among active-duty soldiers [8] due to the purported performance-enhancing and weight control/fat loss properties of many supplements. Service members are also expected to maintain their physical appearance, a factor associated with DS use [8,9], and that may contribute to the high prevalence of performance-enhancing and weight control/fat loss supplement use reported in the current and existing studies [1,9]. In addition, it has been documented that soldiers are directly targeted by marketing strategies that imply the advertised DSs enhance these factors [16,18]. The high prevalence of DS use reported among the infantry soldiers in this study (81% of users) may also reflect the physically demanding nature of this military specialty compared with less physically demanding job categories, such as 74% of combat support service members (e.g., engineers, intelligence and security, and chemical) reporting regular DS use [1,2,5,8,42]. The older age and higher education of career soldiers may also contribute to the higher DS use compared with incoming trainees, as age and education are associated with DS use [2,7].

In this study, protein, pre/post workout, and weight/fat loss supplements were among the most frequently used DS categories, with more than 37% of active-duty soldiers reporting using supplements in each of these categories (Table 2; Supplemental Figure 2). Pre/post workout and weight control/fat loss supplements have the highest prevalence of self-reported adverse events by service members, such as heart palpitations/racing heart and sleep disturbances [10]. Among active-duty DS users in this study, 40% reported concurrent use of at least five different DS categories compared with 15% of trainees. This prevalence of concurrent use among the soldiers, and to a lesser extent, trainees, may elevate risk of adverse events associated with using multiple DSs, as 23% of service members using at least five DSs per week reported at least one adverse event, most commonly heart palpitations, tingling/numbness, diarrhea, and sleep disturbances [13]. Given this pattern of use, targeted educational programs used in nonmilitary populations (e.g., athletes) [43] could be adapted and introduced in the early stages of service (e.g., during or immediately after basic combat training). The DoW does provide online guidance to service members on a website designed for this purpose (https://www.opss.org/). Additional nutrition and DS guidance provided by leadership [44] may be another approach to promote informed decision-making, encourage safe and effective use, and mitigate risk of DS-related adverse health outcomes that degrade occupational performance and health of service members.

Although DS use was lower among incoming trainees than active-duty soldiers in this study, 59% of the incoming trainees reported taking DSs in the month before basic combat training, demonstrating their extensive use even before enlisting. Because incoming trainees in this study appear to be reporting greater use of DSs than similarly aged civilians (46%) [32], their DS use may have increased in anticipation of the requirements for success in infantry training. This suggests a need to provide preaccession guidance for prospective recruits on safe DS use and to prevent potential adverse health effects, which frequently occur in association with certain types of DSs used by members of all military services [10]. The extensive use of DS by members of all the military services, including US Army infantry soldiers, even before their service, is a concern. It is suggested that longitudinal assessments of DS use and adverse events be collected from adolescence through adulthood and include clinical health and functional performance measures to characterize the extent of DS use and its potential consequences throughout a service member’s lifespan. We are not aware of any efforts in the US or other nations to address this gap, which would require a highly coordinated initiative across multiple federal agencies, including the National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the Food and Drug Administration in the US.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is that the two cohorts completed slightly different questionnaires, but the DS measures used in the analyses were comparable. Another limitation was the smaller sample size of incoming trainees relative to the active-duty soldiers, which limited the statistical power and the generalizability of the study findings. As only 5% of female service members serve in combat arms occupations (e.g., infantry) [45], and no females among the incoming trainees were available to participate, this study cannot be generalized to females and other military specialties. This issue should be addressed in future studies. Thefive-year gap between assessments of the active-duty soldiers and incoming trainees is also a limitation. However, recent work demonstrates soldiers’ DS use has been consistently high over this time (∼70% active duty are overall users) [15]. The five-year gap between assessments included the period when the COVID-19 pandemic was present. Overall DS use did not differ between prepandemic/early pandemic and postpandemic/later pandemic periods among US adults, but use of some individual vitamins and minerals, such as those promoted for immune health, increased [46]. Although the soldier cohort self-reported their height and body mass, male soldiers tend to accurately report their body mass and slightly overestimate their height [47]. Recall and participation biases are also limitations of the study [47]. In addition, thesix-month recall period of the active-duty soldiers compared with the 30-day recall of the incoming trainees prevented the comparison of DS intake stability between the two groups. However, reporting use as at least once per week, compared with chronic use, captures regular patterns of use [29], which were high in both groups. Finally, we cannot determine the causal relationships among military experience, career progression, or cultural influences and DS use. Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates, for the first time, that incoming US Army trainees exhibit lower overall DS use, lower category-specific usage, and consume supplements from fewer distinct categories compared with active-duty soldiers.

In conclusion, a considerable proportion of incoming US Army trainees reported extensive use of DSs because of basic combat training, but their use was significantly lower than that of active-duty soldiers. This suggests that US Army requirements to meet and maintain well-defined physical fitness and weight standards contribute to the heavy use of DSs by active-duty soldiers [8]. The findings of this study emphasize the importance of future longitudinal studies to establish a causal link between military service and DS use and the collection of longitudinal data to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of DSs.
