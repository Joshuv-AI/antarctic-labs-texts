Disentangling the association between chronic pain and sarcopenia-related traits: A bidirectional Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Weijie Wang, Qiankun Zhang, Zhenhua Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the potential causal relationships between chronic pain and three key sarcopenia-related quantitative traits: (a) hand grip strength; (b) usual walking pace; and (c) appendicular lean mass, using bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization.MethodsWe conducted bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization using summary-level data from large-scale genome-wide association studies to assess the genetically predicted associations between chronic pain, including multisite chronic pain and chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain, and the aforementioned sarcopenia-related traits.ResultsMendelian randomization revealed that multisite chronic pain was significantly associated with an increased risk of low hand grip strength (odds ratio = 1.70; p < 0.001) and decreased usual walking pace (odds ratio = 0.81; p < 0.001); chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain was also significantly associated with decreased usual walking pace (odds ratio = 0.15; p < 0.001). Additionally, higher left hand grip strength was significantly associated with a lower risk of multisite chronic pain (odds ratio = 0.90; p< 0.001) and chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain (odds ratio = 0.99; p = 0.002); higher right hand grip strength was significantly associated with a lower risk of multisite chronic pain (odds ratio = 0.91; p = 0.002); and higher usual walking pace was significantly associated with a lower risk of multisite chronic pain (odds ratio = 0.49; p < 0.001) and chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain (odds ratio = 0.92; p < 0.001). No significant causal associations were detected for appendicular lean mass in either direction (all p > 0.05).ConclusionThis study provides genetic evidence supporting potential causal links between chronic pain and key phenotypic components of sarcopenia.Keywords: Mendelian randomization, sarcopenia, chronic pain, widespread musculoskeletal pain, hand grip strength

Introduction

Sarcopenia, characterized by a progressive decline in skeletal muscle mass accompanied with reduced muscle strength and physical function, has been widely reported across various clinical settings, particularly among older adults. 1 In older adults, sarcopenia can lead to abnormal gait, impaired balance, fractures, falls, disability, and even death. 2 This condition is associated with reduced functional capacity, poorer physical performance, and an increased risk of mortality in older populations.3,4

Chronic pain refers to pain that persists beyond the normal healing period and is generally defined as pain lasting for ≥3 months. 5 It is highly prevalent among older adults, affecting approximately 30%–50% of community-dwelling individuals. 6 Chronic pain is associated with increased healthcare costs, social isolation, premature mortality, cognitive decline, and disability. 7 As individuals age, chronic health conditions become more common, further increasing the burden of pain. 8

Several studies have examined the association between chronic pain and sarcopenia. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis has reported that older adults who experience chronic pain have a significantly higher prevalence of sarcopenia than those without such pain. 9 Additionally, evidence has suggested that individuals with sarcopenia generally report higher levels of pain than those without sarcopenia. 10 Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether sarcopenia exacerbates pain, chronic pain increases the risk of sarcopenia, or the two conditions are bidirectionally associated.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is an analytical approach used to infer potential causal relationships between exposures and outcomes. Conventional observational studies are often affected by confounding factors and reverse causation, which may bias analytical results and compromise their validity. To address these limitations, MR has been widely applied in studies investigating the relationships between risk factors and disease outcomes. 11 In this study, we aimed to investigate the causal relationship between sarcopenia and chronic pain by conducting bidirectional two-sample MR using the latest genome-wide association studies (GWAS) datasets.

Materials and methods

Study design

To investigate the bidirectional relationship between sarcopenia and chronic pain, this study performed bidirectional two-sample MR using summary statistics from GWAS. Forward MR was performed to examine chronic pain as the exposure and sarcopenia as the outcome, whereas reverse MR was performed to evaluate sarcopenia as the exposure and chronic pain as the outcome. No additional ethical approval was required because all data used in this study were publicly available.

Genetic variants were used to evaluate the genetically predicted association between the exposure and outcome. The following were the essential assumptions underlying the validity of the genetic variants serving as valid instrumental variables (IVs) in this study:

1. The genetic variant must be associated with the exposure.

2. The genetic variant must not be associated with any confounders of the exposure-outcome relationship.

3. The genetic variant should affect the outcome only through the exposure (no pleiotropic effects).

Data sources

In this study, genetic associations for either exposure or outcome were extracted from two independent GWAS datasets. Summary statistics for both multisite chronic pain (MCP) and chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain (CWP) were obtained from large-scale biomedical GWAS databases. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with MCP were obtained from the study by Johnston K et al., which involved 387,649 individuals. 12 SNPs associated with CWP were obtained from the study by Rahman et al., which included 6914 cases and 242,929 controls. 13 Data on sarcopenia were derived from publicly available databases. The study evaluated four sarcopenia-related traits: (a) appendicular lean mass (ALM) in 450,243 patients; (b) hand grip strength (HGS; left/right) in 461,026/461,089 samples, with low HGS identified in 135,468 samples, according to the criteria established by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP, aged ≥ 60 years); and (c) usual walking pace (UWP) in 459,915 patients. IVs selection

SNPs showing genome-wide significant associations (p < 5 × 10−8) and independent inheritance (r2 < 0.001) without linkage disequilibrium (clumping distance = 10,000 kb) were used to identify IVs for sarcopenia. Furthermore, the F-statistic was used to assess weak instrument bias, and IVs with an F-statistic <10 were excluded. After rigorous filtering, the remaining SNPs were used as IVs for further analyses. The Bonferroni method was used to adjust for multiple testing. Based on the number of outcomes, the statistical significance threshold was set at 0.05 divided by 5 (i.e. 0.01) or 2 (i.e. 0.025).

MR and sensitivity analyses

The primary statistical method used for two-sample MR analysis was inverse variance weighting (IVW). Weighted-median and MR–Egger methods were also used to assess the consistency of the results. Cochran's Q-test (CQT; p < 0.05 indicating heterogeneity) was used to evaluate heterogeneity. The Mendelian randomization–Egger intercept test (MR–EIT; p < 0.05 indicating pleiotropy) and leave-one-out analysis (LOOA) were used to assess pleiotropy. Several potential sources of heterogeneity existed (e.g. population substructure, variant-specific pleiotropic effects, and varying linkage disequilibrium patterns), and heterogeneity-related bias was addressed using complementary sensitivity analyses, including the MR–EIT and LOOA.

To account for multiple testing, we applied a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of α = 0.05/k, where k denotes the number of independent MR tests performed (k = 10 for the forward direction and k = 10 for the reverse direction, corresponding to two chronic pain phenotypes × 5 sarcopenia-related trait instruments). Associations with p <0.005 were considered statistically significant, whereas associations with 0.005 ≤ p <0.05 were considered suggestive and interpreted with caution, warranting confirmation.

All MR and sensitivity analyses were performed using the “TwoSampleMR” package in R v4.2.2.

Results

Potential causal effect of chronic pain on sarcopenia

In total, 39 SNPs potentially associated with MCP were identified. After outcome data for low HGS, ALM, HGS (left), HGS (right), and UWP were extracted, 37, 37, 37, 35, and 35 SNPs, respectively, were selected as IVs for further analysis. The F values of all SNPs were >10, indicating that weak instrument bias was unlikely. IVW revealed that MCP was significantly associated with an increased risk of low HGS (odds ratio (OR) = 1.70; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.29 to 2.24; p < 0.001), decreased HGS (left) (OR = 0.91; 95% CI: 0.84 to 0.99; p = 0.025), and decreased UWP (OR = 0.81; 95% CI: 0.75 to 0.88; p < 0.001) (Figure 1). These results were supported by the weighted-median method (all p < 0.05; Figure 1). However, according to the Bonferroni-corrected threshold (p < 0.005), the association between MCP and HGS (left) did not reach statistical significance. In the sensitivity analysis, although CQT (all p < 0.05) indicated heterogeneity between these SNPs, both MR–EIT (all p > 0.05) and LOOA (Supplementary Figures S1–S3) indicated no evidence of directional pleiotropy between these SNPs. Thus, the sensitivity analysis supported the reliability of the MR results.

Three SNPs potentially associated with CWP were identified. After outcome data for low HGS, ALM, HGS (left), HGS (right), and UWP were extracted, all three SNPs were selected as IVs for further analyses. The F values of all SNPs were >10, indicating that weak instrument bias was unlikely. IVW revealed that CWP was significantly associated with decreased UWP (OR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.30; p < 0.001) (Figure 2). In the sensitivity analysis, CQT (p = 0.296), MR–EIT (p = 0.384), and LOOA (Supplementary Figure S4) indicated no evidence of heterogeneity or directional pleiotropy between these SNPs. Thus, the sensitivity analysis supported the reliability of the MR results. However, IVW revealed that CWP was not significantly associated with low HGS, ALM, HGS (left), or HGS (right) (all p > 0.05).

Potential causal effect of sarcopenia on chronic pain

In total, 13 SNPs potentially associated with low HGS were identified. After MCP or CWP outcome data were extracted, 13 and 5 SNPs, respectively, were selected as IVs for further analysis. IVW revealed that low HGS was significantly associated with an increased risk of MCP (OR = 1.06; 95% CI: 1.01 to 1.12; p = 0.019) (Figure 3). In the sensitivity analysis, although LOOA (Supplementary Figure S5) indicated no evidence of pleiotropy between these SNPs, CQT (p < 0.001) indicated heterogeneity, and MR–EIT (p = 0.043) suggested the presence of directional pleiotropy between these SNPs. Therefore, the sensitivity analysis indicated that the MR result was unreliable. Moreover, IVW revealed that low HGS was not significantly associated with CWP (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Chronic pain is highly prevalent among older individuals and negatively affects their quality of life, leading to serious outcomes such as disability and death. 14 Pain predicted the transition to sarcopenia in a prospective cohort study of health, aging, and body composition. 15 In their cross-sectional study, Tsekoura et al. found an association between sarcopenia and chronic pain among community-dwelling older adults in Greece. 16 One possible explanation for persistent pain is that it reflects inflammatory processes, such as elevated levels of circulating proinflammatory mediators and oxidative stress, both of which have been associated with muscle atrophy. 17 Furthermore, behavioral and functional mediators, including reduced physical activity, fear of movement (kinesiophobia), and muscle deconditioning, may represent potential mechanisms linking chronic pain to declines in muscle function and physical performance. In addition to systemic inflammation, pain-induced kinesiophobia and sedentary behavior trigger muscle disuse, creating a vicious cycle that weakens grip strength and slows walking speed. 18 In contrast, reduced muscle strength impairs joint stability and reduces the secretion of anti-inflammatory myokines, sensitizing nociceptive pathways and raising chronic pain risk. 19 However, these potential mechanisms require further investigation in large-scale clinical studies.

In this study, we also found that lower HGS and slower walking pace were significantly associated with an increased risk of chronic pain. This result is consistent with previous findings. In a study conducted among older adults in England, higher pain intensity was associated with a greater likelihood of sarcopenia. 20 In their study, Foley et al. found that over a follow-up period of approximately 3 years, reductions in muscle metrics were associated with lower extremity joint pain, stiffness, and functional impairment in women. 21 Older adults who reported pain were more likely to develop sarcopenia than those who did not report pain. 22 Furthermore, chronic musculoskeletal pain involving more painful sites and greater pain intensity was more common among older individuals with sarcopenia. 23 Sarcopenia may also contribute to the development of chronic pain through multiple endocrine, metabolic, and immunological pathways. 24 Conversely, this association may be cyclical, with persistent pain leading to reduced activity, which subsequently causes muscle weakness, further pain, and additional reductions in activity. 25 The exact duration required for pain to reduce physical activity and contribute to muscle loss remains unclear; therefore, further research is needed in this area.

The distinction between sarcopenia as a clinical syndrome and its individual functional components has been increasingly emphasized in recent literature. A systematic review by Ceolin et al. on spondyloarthritis revealed that “pre-sarcopenia” or “probable sarcopenia,” identified primarily by reduced muscle strength rather than low muscle mass, was substantially more prevalent than the full clinical syndrome, and this pre-sarcopenic state was closely associated with disease activity. 26 This observation is consistent with our decision to analyze grip strength, walking pace, and ALM as separate endophenotypes rather than treating sarcopenia as a single homogeneous entity. Furthermore, a 36-month longitudinal study on axial spondyloarthritis has demonstrated that greater muscle mass and better physical performance are correlated with lower disease activity and improved quality of life. However, the authors noted that causality could not be established from their observational design. 27 Collectively, these findings support the clinical relevance of muscle strength and physical performance as independent indicators of musculoskeletal health in chronic inflammatory conditions, and they highlight the value of MR approaches, such as ours, in complementing observational evidence to investigate causal directionality.

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that effective pain management serves as a preventive strategy against the progressive decline in muscle function that characterizes sarcopenia. This is consistent with current guidelines recommending physical activity for chronic pain management, and it adds a causal dimension to the rationale that pain relief, whether achieved pharmacologically (e.g. analgesics with minimal muscle toxicity) or through rehabilitation, may provide dual benefits for pain control and muscle preservation.28,29 Conversely, screening for pain in older adults presenting with reduced muscle strength may help identify individuals at risk of accelerated functional decline. Our findings underscore the importance of multidisciplinary management involving pain specialists, geriatricians, and rehabilitation therapists. A patient-centered care model, incorporating pain neuroscience education, graded exercise exposure, and nutritional support, is essential to break the cycle of pain-induced inactivity and progressive muscle deterioration.30,31

Although our study provides comprehensive and novel insights, certain limitations should be considered. First, we examined individual sarcopenia-related traits rather than the clinical syndrome of sarcopenia itself, which may not have fully captured the multidimensional nature of sarcopenia as defined by current consensus criteria. Second, the limited number of genetic instruments available for CWP represented a notable constraint as weak instrument bias may influence causal estimates and reduce statistical power. Significant associations for CWP were still detected in several analyses; however, the wide CIs around these estimates indicate considerable imprecision. Larger GWAS meta-analyses specifically designed for CWP are needed to provide more robust instruments and refined causal estimates. Third, our findings are constrained by the use of summary-level GWAS data, which cannot capture individual-level confounding or dynamic biological interactions, and the observed associations represent “genetically predicted” rather than “experimentally manipulated” effects.

Thus, this bidirectional MR study provides genetic evidence supporting causal relationships between chronic pain and key sarcopenia-related endophenotypes, specifically HGS and walking pace, in both directions. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted as associations with quantitative muscle function and physical performance traits rather than clinically diagnosed sarcopenia as our genetic instruments were derived from population-based GWAS of these individual components. Future studies using case–control cohorts with consensus-based sarcopenia diagnostic criteria (e.g. EWGSOP2) are warranted to validate whether these trait-level effects can be translated to the full clinical syndrome.
