A novel strategy for ultrasound-assisted immobilized enzyme hydrolysis for the preparation of yak bone collagen peptides: Process optimization, structure and antioxidant mechanisms
Authors: Yinjuan Cao, Zhaoyang Song, Yabin Ma, Yulong Luo, Jianrui Qi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Ultrasound-assisted, Immobilized enzyme, Yak bone collagen peptides, Process optimization, Structure, Antioxidant mechanisms

Introduction

Free radicals and reactive oxygen species (ROS) are natural by-products of metabolism. Excessive amounts of these can trigger oxidative stress, resulting in lipid peroxidation, DNA damage and abnormal protein metabolism, thereby causing various diseases [1]. Previous studies have shown that antioxidant peptides can effectively reduce ROS accumulation, protect biomolecules and regulate cellular signalling pathways, thereby promoting health [2]. According to Halliwell [3], higher blood levels of dietary antioxidants are linked to a decreased incidence of diseases induced by oxidative damage. Therefore, consuming antioxidant-rich foods is an effective way to counteract oxidative stress. Natural antioxidant peptides currently demonstrate superior digestibility, absorption efficiency and safety profiles (lower molecular weight and toxicity), making them highly effective in alleviating oxidative stress [4]. Collagen is a biological macromolecule synthesized by animal cells. The collagen peptides produced by its hydrolysis have antioxidant properties and are effective at eliminating free radicals in the body and reducing oxidative stress [5]. Furthermore, collagen peptides contain a variety of bioactive components, including growth factors, that play a significant role in delaying skin ageing, preventing atherosclerosis and promoting bone cell growth [6]. China has 85 % of the world's yak population. However, deep processing and high-value-added utilization of yak bones (accounting for around 20 % of their body weight) after slaughter is limited. This not only wastes valuable skeletal resources but also places significant pressure on the ecological environment of the plateau [7]. Yak bones are rich in collagen protein, making them a valuable source of bioactive peptides [8]. A multitude of studies have documented the preparation methods and biological activities of yak bone collagen peptides (YCP). These peptides have demonstrated benefits such as enhancing bone health, exhibiting anti-aging properties, and providing anti-diabetic effects [7,8]. Moreover, research has indicated that YCP can modify the composition of the intestinal microbiota in mice, leading to increased production of short-chain fatty acids. Furthermore, they effectively mitigate inflammation by modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway and regulating NO production [9,10]. Consequently, YCP hold potential as functional ingredients for improving human health.

Enzymatic hydrolysis represents a prevalent approach for producing bioactive peptides. Nevertheless, the entire process is time-intensive and typically yields relatively low quantities of the target peptide [11]. Previous studies have shown that by employing artificial techniques to extract enzymes from biological organisms and immobilize them on suitable carriers, it is possible to produce immobilized enzymes that exhibit catalytic activity within a defined space [12]. These immobilized enzymes demonstrate enhanced adaptability to varying temperatures and pH levels, as well as increased resistance to proteases and inhibitors, all while minimizing the loss of enzyme activity. This method is not only environmentally sustainable and safe but also offers maximum protection for amino acids against degradation. Moreover, it is especially well-suited for continuous and automated industrial production processes [13]. However, there have been few reports on the preparation of protein peptides using immobilized enzymes. In recent years, emerging technologies, including microwave, high-voltage pulsed electric fields, and ultrasound, have been employed to assist in enzyme hydrolysis [14]. Among the various auxiliary production technologies available, ultrasound technology is particularly notable for its environmentally friendly, clean, efficient, safe, and user-friendly attributes. During ultrasound processing, liquid vibrations give rise to microscopic bubbles. When these bubbles reach a critical size, they burst instantaneously, generating intense shock waves. As the bubbles rupture, there is a sharp increase in local pressure, resulting in powerful shear forces. This process is also accompanied by localized high temperatures, ultimately triggering the “ultrasonic cavitation” effect [15]. Owing to its cavitation effects, ultrasound has been demonstrated to enhance the extraction efficiency of bioactive compounds and reduce processing time [16]. Recent studies have confirmed that ultrasonic-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis technology demonstrates potential in enhancing the extraction rate of functional components, boosting protein conversion rates, and accelerating enzymatic hydrolysis rates [5]. He et al. [17] discovered that ultrasound-assisted processing not only enhanced the extraction rate and antioxidant activity of oxhide gelatin collagen peptides but also induced significant physical alterations in collagen, rendering its microstructure looser, more porous, and more uniform. However, to date, the effectiveness of ultrasound combined with immobilized enzymatic hydrolysis in the production of collagen peptides from yak bone remains unexplored.

This study presents a novel ultrasound-assisted immobilized enzyme hydrolysis method for extracting collagen peptides from yak bones. Using response surface methodology, key hydrolysis parameters were optimized to improve both process efficiency and the antioxidant activity of the peptides. To precisely separate and identify peptide fragments within the YCP, we implemented an integrated approach combining ultrafiltration with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Additionally, molecular docking technology was utilized to simulate the interactions between collagen peptides and the Keap1 protein, facilitating an in-depth evaluation of their biological properties and shedding light on their underlying molecular mechanisms. This study lays a foundation for the future development and application of YCP across diverse industries and fields.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Fresh yak leg bones were purchased from Qinghai Yutai Livestock Products Co., Ltd (Qinghai, China). Yak bones with marrow and surface fascia removed were cleaned, cut into 1–2 cm pieces with a bone saw (J280C, Zhejiang Shark Food Machinery Co. Ltd, Zhejiang, China), dried in a blast drying oven (101-2AB, Tianjin Tester Instrument Co. Ltd, Tianjin, China), pulverized in a pulverizer (1500C, Dongguan Fangtai Electric Co. Ltd, Dongguan, China), sieved through a 60-mesh sieve, and stored at −18 ℃ for spare use. All chemicals used were of analytical grade and obtained from China Pharmaceutical Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China).

Extraction and purification of yak bone peptide

This study employed covalent conjugation to prepare immobilized papain. The specific procedure is as follows: Chitosan microspheres, rich in surface amino groups, were selected as the carrier. First, under gentle stirring at 25 °C, the carrier reacted with a specific concentration of glutaraldehyde solution for 2h to introduce aldehyde groups onto its surface. Subsequently, the carrier was thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove excess glutaraldehyde. The activated carrier was then mixed with a solution of papain at a specified concentration and activity at pH 7.0 and 4 °C. The mixture underwent slow stirring for 12 h, allowing the amino groups of the enzyme molecules to form stable Schiff bases (covalent bonds) with the aldehyde groups on the carrier. Upon reaction completion, the immobilized enzyme was collected by filtration and repeatedly washed with phosphate-buffered saline until no detectable protein remained in the eluate, ensuring complete removal of unbound enzyme. The final immobilized papain was stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

The bone powder was homogenized with 0.5 mol/L glacial acetic acid at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio and then subjected to enzymatic digestion using 1.6 % pepsin (4000 U/g) under continuous agitation at 4 °C for 48 h. After centrifugation, collagen was recovered by isoelectric precipitation. The precipitate collected following centrifugation at 4 °C and 10,000 r/min for 20 min was freeze-dried. Collagen peptides were subsequently prepared via ultrasound-assisted enzymatic immobilization, where a 50 mL sample was treated for 30 min using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (Digital Sonifier 450, Branson, USA) operating at 20 kHz. The probe was immersed to a depth of 2 cm with a pulsed cycle of 10 s on and 5 s off.

Optimization of yak bone peptide extraction process

Enzymatic one- factor test

The DH was investigated with respect to enzyme digestion temperature (35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60 ℃), enzyme digestion time (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 h), ultrasonic power (100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600 W), and the amount of immobilized papain added (2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0, 4.5 %).

Response surface experiments

Based on the findings from the single-factor tests, enzyme digestion temperature, enzyme digestion time, ultrasonic power, and immobilized papain addition were identified as the key variables for further investigation. A four-factor, three-level response surface methodology (RSM) experiment was designed to optimize the conditions for collagen peptide preparation, with the DH serving as the response value. Particle size determination of collagen peptides

The collagen peptide-containing supernatant, obtained post-digestion, was diluted to 0.5 mg/mL using deionized water. Prior to analysis, the sample was equilibrated for 2 min, and its particle size was then determined at 25 °C with a laser diffraction sensor.

Structure determination of collagen peptides

Fluorescence spectrum

A fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-5301, Shanghai Prism Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was employed to record the fluorescence spectrum of YCP (1 mg/mL). An excitation wavelength of 275 nm was used, with an emission wavelength range of 280–400 nm and a slit width of 5 nm.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The lyophilized YCP powder was compressed into thin slices, and the spectra were recorded on a fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nicolet iS20, PerkinElmer, Waltham, USA) in the range of 4000–400 cm−1. Each sample was scanned 50 times.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

An X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean, Malvern Panalytical Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was employed to obtain the XRD patterns of YCP. The operating voltage and current were set at 40 kV and 30 mA, respectively. The scanning range was from 10° to 70°, with a scanning step size of 0.02° and a scanning rate of 2°/min.

Microstructure

The microstructure of the samples was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SU8010, Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Observations were conducted at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV and a magnification of 5000 × .

Thermal stability determination of collagen peptides

The thermal stability of the samples was analyzed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) using an ATS-DSC-500Q instrument (Etison Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), according to the protocol by Xu et al. [18]. Approximately 2 mg of each sample was accurately weighed into an aluminium crucible. The samples were equilibrated at 25 °C for 1 min before thermal analysis. They were then heated from 0 °C to 120 °C at a constant rate of 2 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 20 mL/min.

Peptide sequence identification

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was used for peptide sequence identification Song et al. [19]. A 5 μL sample was first subjected to reductive alkylation and then desalted. The desalted sample was loaded onto an analytical column (75 μm inner diameter, 150 mm in length, packed with Acclaim PepMap RPLC C18, 3 μm particle size, 100 Å). Mobile phase A consisted of an aqueous solution containing 0.1 % formic acid, while mobile phase B was an 80 % acetonitrile solution containing 0.1 % formic acid. The following elution gradient was applied: 0 min, 6 % B; 5 min, 9 % B; 50 min, 50 % B; 52 min, 95 % B; 56 min, 95 % B. The flow rate was maintained at 300 nL/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion mode with a capillary voltage of 3.6 kV, and scans were performed over m/z ranges of 100–1500 in MS mode and 50–1500 in MS/MS mode.

Antioxidant capacity determination of collagen peptides

The antioxidant activity of yak bone peptides was verified by DPPH radical scavenging and ABTS cation scavenging abilities Nan et al. [20]. Aqueous solutions of the peptide samples were prepared at varying concentrations (0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, and 1.0 g/L) using purified water.

Antioxidant stability study of collagen peptides

To intuitively reflect peptide stability, the antioxidant stability of YCP under varying pH and temperature conditions was assessed according to the method of Li et al. [22], using the retention rate of DPPH radical scavenging activity as the indicator.

Photostability was evaluated following a slightly modified procedure from Xu et al. [23].

Based on the approach of Guo et al. [9], a 1 mg/mL peptide solution was prepared, and the DPPH radical scavenging activity was used as the indicator to evaluate the effect of in vitro simulated gastrointestinal digestion on the antioxidant activity of YCP.

Molecular docking of the collagen peptides to the receptor Keap1

The molecular mechanism of YCP was investigated using a protocol adapted from Sun et al. [24]. The three-dimensional structures of the peptides were generated with Discovery Studio 2019 (Dassault Systèmes BIOVIA, USA) and followed by structural optimization using the Prepare Ligands module. The crystal structure of the receptor Keap1/Nrf2 complex (PDB ID: 6QMK) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). The Box centers of Keap1 was set at (5, 7, 2) with Box sizes of (15, 15, 15) [25], with the exhaustiveness set to “Higher” and the number of modes set to 3. Subsequent processing involved removal of the Nrf2 ligand and water molecules, followed by structural refinement using Clean Protein and Prepare Protein protocols to prepare the Keap1 receptor and characterize its binding site. The CHARMm force field was applied to the optimized peptide structures before performing CDOCKER-based docking against the Keap1 receptor, retaining the pose cluster radius at 0.5.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and data processing were conducted using Microsoft Excel 2010 (USA), and graphs were plotted with OriginLab Origin 2022 (USA). Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The normality of the experimental data was assessed with IBM SPSS Statistics 19. A one-way ANOVA followed by the LSD post hoc test was applied to normally distributed data to compare mean differences, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Extraction of yak collagen peptides (YCP)

Optimization via Single-Factor Experiments

Fig. 1A shows the effect of enzymatic hydrolysis temperature on the DH of yak collagen (YC). As the temperature increased, the DH value rose significantly. This could be attributed to the exposure of more enzymatic cleavage sites at higher temperatures, which accelerates the hydrolysis reaction and increases protein DH [26]. However, DH decreased when the temperature increased from 50 to 60 ℃. Previous studies suggest that excessively high temperatures may lead to the inactivation of alkaline trypsin, resulting in reduced DH [49]. Therefore, 50 ℃ was selected for the subsequent hydrolysis process.

Multiple means comparison results of the preliminary analyses of DH obtained from the various levels of Enzymatic hydrolysis temperature (A), Enzymatic hydrolysis time (B), Ultrasonic power (C) and the amount of immobilized enzyme (D). The error bar indicates ± standard deviation of mean (n = 3). Means that do not share the same letter are significantly different.

As shown in Fig. 1B, the DH of YC displayed a biphasic pattern with enzymatic duration, reaching a peak of 13.53 ± 0.38 % after 8 h of ultrasonication before decreasing under prolonged treatment. This trend may be attributed to the repeated collapse of cavitation bubbles during ultrasonication, which generates compressive shockwaves and elevated thermal conditions [27]. Thus, extending processing beyond 8–10 h induces thermal denaturation that compromises enzymatic activity, thereby lowering catalytic efficiency—a finding consistent with the report by Cao et al. [28].

The influence of ultrasonic power on the DH is shown in Fig. 1C. Ultrasonic power significantly affected the DH (P < 0.05), with the maximum hydrolysis efficiency (22.03 ± 0.35 %) achieved at 500 W. Studies suggest that lower power densities lead to a gradual temperature increase in protein solutions while reducing the cavitation threshold, thus promoting cavitation bubble formation [50]. However, beyond 500 W, a decline in DH was observed. This reduction may be attributed to protein thermal denaturation and increased vapor pressure under high-power conditions, both of which could reduce cavitation efficiency and ultrasonic effectiveness. Moreover, bubble clusters can shield interior bubbles from high-intensity ultrasound, thereby limiting further increases in cavitation intensity [29].

Fig. 1D illustrates the effect of enzyme immobilization on the DH for YC. The maximum DH (16.40 ± 0.52 %) was achieved at an immobilized enzyme concentration of 3.5 %. The increase in DH is primarily attributed to enhanced enzyme–substrate binding at lower enzyme concentrations [30]. Although enzyme dosage drives the reaction forward, excessively high levels may lead to mass transfer limitations and product inhibition, thereby reducing product yield and hydrolysis efficiency [31]. Therefore, an immobilized enzyme loading of 3.5 % was selected for response surface optimization.

The validity of the model was evaluated through ANOVA. The variance analysis of the response surface regression model for the DH value (response variable) is presented in Table 3. The model terms were statistically significant (P < 0.0001, F = 74.55), indicating that the independent variables strongly influenced the response. The non-significant lack of fit (P = 0.1509 > 0.05) confirms the model’s adequacy for predicting experimental outcomes. Statistical significance is generally indicated by low P-values together with high F-values, as reported in previous studies (Garcia et al., 2024). Moreover, high coefficient values (R2 = 0.9868, Adjusted R2 = 0.9735, Predicted R2 = 0.9303) attest to a strong agreement between experimental and model-predicted results. To visualize variable interactions, 3D response surface and contour plots were generated from the quadratic regression model (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). As shown in Fig. 3A, the nearly circular contour lines suggest a weak interaction between the two factors on DH. In contrast, elliptical contours and a steep response surface indicate a strong interaction between the factors affecting DH (Fig. 3C).

Based on the regression equation and DH analysis, the software-determined optimal processing conditions for the variables influencing YCP are as follows: enzymatic hydrolysis temperature of 49.22 °C, hydrolysis time of 8.41 h, ultrasonic power of 415.42 W, and immobilized enzyme dosage of 3.62 %. The predicted DH value was 22.37 %. After accounting for practical limitations, the refined optimal conditions were set at 49 °C, 8.4 h, 415 W, and 3.6 % immobilized enzyme. Under these conditions, three parallel tests yielded YC with a DH of 22.36 % ± 0.18 %, aligning well with model predictions. Therefore, ultrasound-assisted immobilized enzymatic hydrolysis was selected for YC processing, warranting further investigation into its structural modifications and antioxidant potential.

Structural characterization of YCP

Microstructure

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) serves as a valuable tool for characterizing microstructural changes in proteins and peptides [30]. As shown in Fig. 4, the physical structure of YCP under four different treatments was observed at 5,000 × magnification. Both ultrasonic-assisted and immobilized enzyme treatments significantly influenced YCP's morphological characteristics. Peptide segments from conventional enzymatic hydrolysis exhibited larger sizes, intact structures, and smooth surfaces. Conversely, those prepared by ultrasonic-assisted immobilized enzyme hydrolysis showed smaller sizes, more compact structures, and smooth surface textures. Ultrasonic treatment promoted the formation of reduced protein conformations, indicating surface-initiated proteolysis. This finding highlights the synergistic potential of ultrasound and immobilized enzymes in improving protein breakdown efficiency.

The effect of different extraction methods on the microstructure of YCP.

Particle size

The particle sizes of YCP treated by P-EH, US + P-EH, IP-EH, and US + IP-EH were measured as 438.2 ± 1.70 nm, 257.45 ± 1.56 nm, 294.45 ± 1.24 nm, and 217.45 ± 1.71 nm, respectively, as shown in Fig. 5A. The results demonstrated that the US + IP-EH treatment effectively reduced the particle size of the hydrolyzed products. This decrease may be attributed to the cavitation effect, which diminishes particle size by altering the physical properties of peptides. This observation aligns with previous findings [32], highlighting the capacity of ultrasound to significantly reduce the mean particle size distribution of collagen. The reduction in peptide particle size considerably influences their functional and conformational characteristics. Shear forces generated by ultrasound disrupt protein–protein interactions, such as hydrophobic and hydrogen bonds, leading to a decrease in particle size [33].

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The fluorescence spectrum of proteins or peptides changes significantly upon conformational changes [34]. As shown in Fig. 5B, YCP exhibited fluorescence emission intensities at 312 nm and 353 nm, indicating that the fluorescence mainly originated from tyrosine and tryptophan residues [51]. Meanwhile, a significant reduction in fluorescence intensity was observed when ultrasound was applied to assist enzymatic or immobilized enzyme digestion. This can be attributed to structural perturbations induced by ultrasonic cavitation, which promote the unfolding of collagen molecules. As a result, more chromophores become exposed to the solvent, thereby enhancing the fluorescence signal. Additionally, ultrasound altered the state of protein aggregation, affecting their local environment and leading to reduced fluorescence intensity [30]. Previous studies have reported ultrasound-induced fluorescence quenching in various protein systems, such as ovine bone collagen [29] and walnut protein [35].

XRD

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns can intuitively reveal the proteomic structure of proteins, thus reflecting differences in polypeptide crystal structures [52]. In this study, the crystalline nature of synthesized YCP was examined by X-ray diffraction analysis. The XRD pattern (Fig. 5C) exhibited a characteristic broad halo centered at 2θ = 19.82°, confirming the amorphous characteristics of YCP. However, the characteristic diffraction peak of the US + IP-EH group shifts to 22.68°, with a notable increase in intensity. This phenomenon may be attributed to ultrasound-induced structural reorganization, in which partial amorphous domains of YCP transition into crystalline phases, thereby enhancing overall crystallinity. Previous studies indicate that ultrasound treatment reduces collagen molecular spacing and enhances intermolecular interactions, leading to the formation of structurally ordered crystalline regions [53].

FTIR and secondary structure

FTIR spectroscopy detects molecular vibrational modes through characteristic infrared absorption, enabling quantitative assessment of protein secondary structure modifications and functional group changes essential for evaluating biomolecular properties [36]. The FTIR spectrum of YCP exhibits characteristic absorption bands, including the amide A vibration at 3243–3289 cm−1, regions corresponding to triple bonds and cumulative double bonds (within 3243–3289 cm−1), the amide I band at 1645–1650 cm−1, and the amide II band at 1553–1555 cm−1, as shown in Fig. 5D. The amide A band is primarily attributed to N–H stretching vibrations coupled with hydrogen bonding interactions in polypeptide chains, exhibiting characteristic frequencies between 3440 and 3300 cm−1. If the amide A peak shifts, it may move down to around 3300 cm−1 or lower, indicating that new hydrogen bond structures may have formed after ultrasonic treatment [37]. Moreover, the absorption peaks of the amide I band mainly arise from the C–O stretching vibration of the amide group, while those of the amide II band originate mainly from the deformation vibration of the amide group, N–H stretching, and C–N stretching vibrations. The amide III vibrational mode primarily involves coupled C–N stretching and N–H in-plane bending motions [30]. The observed spectral shifts reveal ultrasound-induced alterations in peptide secondary structure. After ultrasonic treatment, the α-helix content of YCP increased, whereas the content of random coil structures decreased (Fig. 5E). These results suggest that the microjets, turbulence, shear forces, and shockwaves produced by collapsing microbubbles during ultrasonication disrupted the connections between collagen subunits and the interactions among different regions or components within protein molecules [38]. Most β-turn structures are located on the surface of protein molecules, and their hydrophobicity helps maintain the structural stability and biological activity of proteins or peptide chains [30]. Similarly, Wang et al. [39] reported that cavitation treatment could unfold the molecular structure of samples, thereby exposing more active groups and significantly enhancing their hydrolysis efficiency and antioxidant properties.

DSC

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) is a key technique for assessing molecular thermal stability [52]. Fig. 5F presents the DSC thermogram of YCP. The thermal deformation temperatures of the four types of collagen peptides obtained from different treatments showed significant differences (P < 0.05). The DSC measurements indicated that the thermal deformation temperatures of YCP treated by P-EH, US + P-EH, IP-EH, and US + IP-EH were 60.98 °C, 67.01 °C, 61.28 °C, and 74.83 °C, respectively. These results indicate that appropriate ultrasonic treatment helps improve the thermal stability of collagen peptides. Zou et al. [40] reported that ultrasonic-assisted extraction of Chinese soft-shelled turtle skirt collagen effectively enhances collagen thermal stability, aligning with the findings of this study.

Antioxidant properties of YCP

As shown in Fig. 6A, this study preliminarily investigated the promoting effect of ultrasound-assisted immobilized enzyme hydrolysis on YCP production. Compared with other groups, the peptide content in the US + IP-EH group showed a statistically significant increase (P < 0.05). This result is attributed to the cavitation effect of ultrasound, which disrupts collagen cross-linking, reduces its stability, and enhances its solubility [8]. In addition, ultrasound treatment can loosen collagen structures, thereby exposing enzyme sites and improving peptide extraction efficiency [41]. Regarding protein recovery, ultrasound-assisted immobilized enzyme hydrolysis led to significantly higher recovery rates (P < 0.05). As illustrated in Fig. 6B, the YCP recovery rates for P-EH, US + P-EH, IP-EH, and US + IP-EH treatments were 25.23 %, 41.34 %, 38.25 %, and 58.91 %, respectively. Excessive ultrasound power may induce stronger cavitation, mechanical damage, and thermal effects, which could alter protease conformation, reduce hydrolysis efficiency, and impede yield improvement [42].

Effect of ultrasonic-assisted immobilized enzymatic hydrolysis on the extraction of bioactive peptide from yak bone. (A) Peptide content; (B) Recovery; (C) DPPH free radical scavenging rate; (D) ABTS free radical scavenging rate.

The antioxidant capacity of YCP obtained by different extraction methods (P-EH, US + P-EH, IP-EH, and US + IP-EH) was evaluated based on DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities, with results presented in Fig. 6, Fig. 6. Peptides from the combined ultrasound-immobilized enzyme method exhibited significantly higher antioxidant capacity than those from conventional enzymatic hydrolysis (P < 0.05). The DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging rates were 63.32 % and 75.43 % for the P-EH group, and 80.85 % and 89.42 % for the US + IP-EH group, respectively. This improvement may be attributed to ultrasonication-induced molecular agitation, which disrupts protein tertiary structures and enhances solvent exposure of hydrophobic, antioxidant-active, and aromatic residues [30].

Stability of YCP

Temperature variations are frequently encountered during food processing, and the effects of different temperatures on the antioxidant activity of YCP are shown in Fig. 7A. As the temperature increased from 25 ℃ to 70 ℃, the DPPH radical scavenging rates of all YCP groups remained stable. When the temperature was further raised from 70 ℃ to 100 ℃, the antioxidant capacity of all four treatments decreased significantly (P < 0.05). Among them, the US + IP-EH group maintained over 75 % antioxidant activity, which was significantly higher than the other three treatments. Previous studies have indicated that the amino acid residues involved in radical scavenging in proteins are mainly hydrophobic amino acids, which are usually buried inside the molecular core. High temperatures induce conformational changes in peptides, exposing these hydrophobic residues to the aqueous phase, thereby revealing more active sites and helping to preserve radical scavenging capacity [20,43].

Antioxidant stability of YCP. (A & B) Effect of different temperatures and pH on the antioxidant activity of YCP. (C & D) Effect of sunlight and digestion stages on the antioxidant activity of YCP.

The pH of the environment influences ionization behavior and electron transfer, thereby affecting the antioxidant capacity of peptides. Fig. 7B displays the pH-dependent antioxidant activity profile of YCP. At pH below 6 or above 8, the DPPH radical scavenging activity of YCP from all four treatments increased, with the US + IP-EH group exhibiting the highest increase of 6.05 %. The results show that the radical scavenging rate of the US + IP-EH group reached about 80 % at pH 6–8, significantly higher than that of YCP in other groups. It has been reported that acidic conditions may influence the accessibility of hydroxyl groups in peptide molecules and improve their hydrogen-donating ability. Moreover, at certain pH levels, amino acid residues in antioxidant peptides can achieve an optimal ionization state, thus enhancing their reactivity with free radicals [44]. This study demonstrates that YCP maintains good antioxidant stability under strongly acidic and alkaline conditions.

Prolonged exposure to sunlight can cause irreversible damage to the skin, making the development of natural antioxidants a key research focus for protecting the skin from photodamage [45]. Fig. 7C illustrates the effects of different light conditions on YCP. Under dark conditions, YCP demonstrates strong antioxidant properties, whereas exposure to either natural or ultraviolet light reduces its DPPH radical scavenging rates. Compared with other groups, the US + IP-EH group better mitigates the photodamage to the antioxidant activity of YCP. These results indicate that YCP prepared using ultrasound-assisted immobilized enzymes exhibits good light stability and holds potential for application in sunscreen cosmetics.

The absorption and utilization of nutrients depend on digestion and absorption through the gastric and intestinal tracts. Therefore, it is essential to examine the changes in the antioxidant activity of YCP during in vitro simulated gastrointestinal digestion. As shown in Fig. 7D, the DPPH radical scavenging capacity of YCP from the four treatments increased to varying degrees after gastric and intestinal digestion stages, consistent with the findings of Zhu et al. [46]. This enhancement may be attributed to gastric protease and pancreatic protease cleaving peptides into smaller fragments, thereby exposing more hydrophobic groups to the environment and enhancing the antioxidant activity of the peptides. This work establishes YCP as a promising functional ingredient for food industry applications.

Identification and screening of YCP by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

LC-MS/MS analysis demonstrated that the peptide segments in the YCP fraction exhibit significant antioxidant activity. Ultimately, 422 peptide segments were successfully separated and identified using Byonic software (Fig. 8A). As shown in Fig. 8B, the molecular weights of these peptides range from 558.2882 to 4066.98804 Da. Peptides in the 2–3 kDa range were the most abundant, accounting for 46.92 %, followed by those in the 3–4 kDa range at 30.33 %, which facilitates their transport across intestinal barriers to exert intracellular antioxidant activity. Based on abundance, ten collagen peptides were screened in this study (Fig. 8C), among which P1 exhibited the highest detection intensity of 3.1766 × 108. Molecular docking analysis

The Keap1/Nrf2 system acts as the master regulator of antioxidant defense mechanisms, with pathway activation conferring protection against exogenous oxidative insults [47]. To further investigate YCP's antioxidant mechanism, we performed molecular docking (AutoDock) of 10 candidate peptides with Keap1/Nrf2 (Fig. 9), identifying key binding interactions. The results showed that only P1, P4, and P10 successfully formed stable complexes with Keap1/Nrf2. Among these three antioxidant peptides, P1 exhibited the lowest binding energy with Keap1 (−4.64 kcal/mol), indicating a stronger binding affinity, followed by P10 (−3.11 kcal/mol) and P4 (−1.36 kcal/mol). The binding affinity between ligand and receptor is quantitatively evaluated by binding energy calculations, where more negative values correspond to stronger molecular interactions [48]. Additionally, P1, P4, and P10 formed multiple interactions with amino acids SER555, SER508, ARG483, ARG326, HIS516, VAL514, VAL561, VAL608, ASP422, and GLY423 of Keap1, including hydrogen bonds with the Keap1 protein, demonstrating good antioxidant potential. Consistent with prior reports [4], hydrogen bonds play a dominant role in mediating small molecule–protein interactions, as supported by our data.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed an ultrasound-assisted immobilized enzyme process (US + IP-EH) for extracting YCP. Optimization via single-factor tests and response surface methodology yielded the following optimal extraction conditions: enzymatic hydrolysis temperature of 49 ℃, time of 8.4 h, ultrasonic power of 415 W, and immobilized enzyme dosage of 3.6 %. Under these conditions, the hydrolysis degree reached 22.36 %. Compared with conventional methods, the US + IP-EH approach improved YCP yield, structural integrity, antioxidant activity, and stability. A total of 422 peptides were successfully isolated and identified from YCP. Using computer-aided screening, 10 novel antioxidant peptides were discovered, exhibiting favorable stability, non-allergenicity, and safety profiles. Among them, peptides P1, P4, and P10 were found to interact effectively with Keap1 by occupying its key binding sites, thereby disrupting the Keap1–Nrf2 complex. These findings confirm the strong antioxidant activity and application potential of YCP as a natural ingredient in functional foods. To further elucidate its antioxidant mechanism, future studies should focus on: (1) exploring the effect of YCP on oxidative stress-related genes and signaling pathways (e.g., Nrf2/ARE, NF-κB) at the cellular level; and (2) using animal models of oxidative damage to systematically evaluate YCP’s impact on antioxidant biomarkers, tissue pathology, and in vivo absorption and metabolism. Such investigations will provide a solid theoretical foundation for the further development and application of YCP.
