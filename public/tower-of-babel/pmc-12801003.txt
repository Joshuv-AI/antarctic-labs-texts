An All-Inside All-Suture Technique for Meniscal Allograft Transplantation: The Chinese Way
Authors: Hao Qin, Chengjie Lian, Wenlong Yan, Hao Tan, Zihang Xie, Aiguo Zhou, Hua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Current suturing techniques used in meniscal allograft transplantation pose risks of damaging blood vessels and nerves, and they conflict with patients’ esthetic needs, impacting the prognosis of the graft and patient satisfaction. This article introduces an all-inside all-suture technique for suturing and securing grafts during meniscal allograft transplantation procedures. This technique offers a more physiological alignment and fixation for the graft and joint capsule, ensuring graft stability while significantly reducing the risk of vascular and neural injuries in the knee joint and postoperative complications. Additionally, it eliminates the need for extra safety incisions, improving postoperative esthetics and patient satisfaction.

The meniscus serves as the functional core structure of the knee joint, with its primary roles including biomechanics and physiological functions. Regarding biomechanics, its concave-convex anatomic design enhances contact area while reducing pressure, contributing to knee joint stability. Furthermore, the circumferential alignment of its collagen fibers facilitates the transmission of axial loads and the dissipation of shock energy.1 Regarding physiological functions, the meniscus is involved in proprioceptive regulation, joint nourishment, and lubrication among other knee joint physiological activities.2 In young patients with meniscal destruction, meniscal allograft transplantation (MAT) is capable of reconstructing meniscal physiological functions, lowering the contact stress within the knee joint, delaying the advancement of osteoarthritis, and enhancing athletic performance.3,4

In arthroscopic meniscal transplantation, the inside-out (IO) suturing technique is regarded as the gold standard when fixing the graft to the recipient’s joint capsule. This technique results in less damage to the meniscus and provides greater mechanical strength. However, when the needle exits on the posterolateral side of the knee joint, it is easy to damage the popliteal artery or common peroneal nerve, typically necessitating an additional safety incision. Currently, in MAT surgery, the anterior horn of the graft is usually secured using an outside-in suturing technique, the body is typically secured with an IO technique, and the posterior horn is fixed with an all-inside suturing device. The combined use of the aforementioned techniques can reduce the incidence of complications and improve postoperative esthetics to some extent.5,6 However, there are still risks such as vascular nerve damage, ligament tethering, and anchor dislodgement, especially in the posterolateral area of the knee joint.7,8

This article presents a repair technique for suturing and fixing the meniscal graft to the recipient’s joint capsule during arthroscopic MAT. This technique uses an all-inside all-suture (AIAS) method, which minimizes trauma, ensures physiological alignment of the graft with the joint capsule, and significantly reduces the risk of damage to neurovascular structures.

This article uses lateral MAT as an example to illustrate the procedure; the same principles apply to the medial side (Video 1). The lateral MAT shown in Fig. 1 and Video 1 was performed by the experienced surgeon (H.Z.).

Surgical Technique

Patient Preparation

The patient is placed under general anesthesia without a tourniquet. The patient is supine with the leg either unsupported or with a lateral post placed just proximal to the knee; then an examination under anesthesia is performed to confirm ligament stability.

Meniscal Allograft Preparation

In this case of lateral MAT, meniscal grafts are preserved through deep freezing (–80°C) and low-dose irradiation (1.5 Mrad), and the bone plug technique is applied to achieve bony fixation of the graft. All non-meniscal soft tissues are cleared to accurately determine the anchoring points for the anterior and posterior horns. In the meniscal allograft, a cylindrical bone plug with a diameter of 6 mm and a depth of 5 mm is retained at both the anterior and posterior horns, and all soft-tissue attachments at the meniscal corners are also preserved. A 1.5-mm drill hole is made through each bone plug, and an Ethibond (No. 2) braided nonabsorbable polyethylene suture (Ethicon) is threaded through. Knee Evaluation and Preparation

Standard arthroscopic anterolateral and anteromedial approaches are established for a comprehensive examination to assess the condition of the meniscus, cartilage, and ligaments. The host’s remaining meniscus is removed, preserving a 1- to 2-mm bleeding edge of the meniscal rim to safeguard the meniscotibial ligament and prevent meniscal extrusion (Fig 1B).

Establishment of Tibial Tunnels and Pre-setting of Sutures

Electrocautery is used to mark the posterior and anterior endpoints of the recipient’s lateral meniscus. With an anterior cruciate ligament guide, tibial tunnels are sequentially drilled from the medial side of the tibial tubercle to the posterior and anterior endpoints of the lateral meniscus (Fig 1C). The 2 tunnels are expanded to 6.5 mm to facilitate bone plug insertion. With a meniscal suturing device (Knee Scorpion; Arthrex), 3 to 4 sutures (No. 2-0 FiberWire; Arthrex) are passed through the remaining posterior part of the lateral meniscus (Fig 1 D and E). All sutures located below the meniscus are then pulled out through the skin under the anterior horn of the lateral meniscus remnant with a spinal puncture needle and a PDS II traction stitch (Ethicon).

Graft Repositioning and Fixation

A suture passing wire is inserted through each tibial tunnel, and the traction stitches are pulled out through the tibial tunnels and the posterior lateral skin. With the guidance of the traction stitches, the allograft is inserted from the anterolateral portal using an incision dilator. Bone plugs are fully inserted into the tunnels to ensure that the graft is anatomically repositioned (Fig 1F). The AIAS technique is performed, with 8 to 10 vertical sutures (No. 2-0 FiberWire) used for peripheral fixation (Fig 1G). The suture hook (Spectrum; ConMed Linvatec) technique is used to pass 2 to 3 sutures through the residual tissue at the anterior horn (Fig 1H). The Knee Scorpion suture passer is used to vertically pass all sutures through the graft from the posterior to the anterior horn. Then, the peripheral sutures are tied under direct arthroscopic view, ensuring that the graft is well aligned and the tension is suitable. Finally, the anterior and posterior bone plug stitches are tied together firmly. The traction stitch at the posterior horn of the graft is removed (Fig 1I), and the graft’s stability is assessed.

Discussion

The posterolateral structure of the knee joint is complex, with the neurovascular structures of the popliteal fossa located adjacent to the posterior horn of the lateral meniscus.9 Any invasive procedure using sharp puncture instruments in this area carries a risk of injuring the popliteal vessels and the common peroneal nerve.10,11 All-inside suturing devices featuring anchors eliminate the need for safety incisions and deliver a degree of biomechanical strength.12 However, when anchoring through the meniscus’s posterior horn, they can still harm vascular and nerve bundles, and there is a risk of anchor dislodgement.13,14 Needle insertion using an outside-in technique at the anterior horn of the meniscus can potentially result in iatrogenic injuries to the lateral genicular artery and cartilage.15 Whereas IO techniques provide optimal biomechanical strength, knotting outside the joint capsule leads to significant surgical wounds and can entangle structures such as the iliotibial band and popliteal tendon, leading to pain during knee movement after surgery.16,17

With the AIAS technique, an all-inside suturing device (Knee Scorpion) is used to prearrange No. 2-0 sutures on the posterolateral aspect of the recipient’s joint capsule. Once the graft has been inserted into the knee joint, with the Scorpion suturing device, the sutures are threaded from beneath the graft to above the meniscus for knotting. Each threading of the sutures fully traverses the graft vertically, maximizing the contact area and achieving a more physiologically accurate alignment with the surrounding joint capsule. This contributes to the preservation of the meniscal synovial edge’s blood supply and facilitates healing of the graft with the joint capsule. Simultaneously, the pre-set sutures help maintain the normal shape of the meniscus, preventing flipping and folding. Furthermore, because AIAS procedures do not involve radial penetration of the meniscus and joint capsule, the risk of damaging posterior lateral knee neurovascular structures and ligating tendons is reduced. Advantages and Disadvantages of AIAS Technique

AIAS, all-inside all-suture.

Although the AIAS technique in MAT surgery substantially lowers the risk of postoperative complications, certain critical aspects and potential pitfalls must still be considered during surgery (Table 2). Because the AIAS technique involves pre-setting sutures on the joint capsule, when pulling in the graft, it is essential to meticulously organize various traction stitches and sutures and to properly adjust the sequence of tensioning the traction stitches. This step is crucial because if the sutures become entangled, this can prevent the graft from accurately reaching its optimal physiological position and can also create folds on the surface of the graft. Leveraging its physiologically aligned relationships, outstanding biomechanical properties, and minimal risk of complications, the AIAS technique has broadened the scope of repair techniques into the transplantation field, addressing the challenges of graft integration, and it is expected to have extensive application in arthroscopic MAT. It is necessary to conduct further clinical studies to compare the effects of the AIAS technique with traditional methods in MAT on patient outcomes to validate the technique’s clinical efficacy.
