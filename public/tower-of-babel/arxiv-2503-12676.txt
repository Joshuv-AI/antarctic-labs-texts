Redefining Orthogonal Co-Existence: A Mother Waveform Framework for DFT-Based Waveforms
Ayoub Ammar Boudjelal, Rania Yasmine Bir, Hüseyin Arslan

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce the concept of a ”mother waveform” to address key challenges in 5th generation (5G) and 6th generation (6G) networks, including spectrum efficiency, backward compatibility, enhanced flexibility, and the integration of joint sensing and communication (JSAC). We propose single- carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing (SC-IFDM) as the mother waveform and demonstrate, through rigorous mathematical modeling, that it can generate all discrete Fourier transform (DFT)-based waveforms without requiring structural modifications. Specifically, by selectively configuring lattice indices and phase adjustments, SC-IFDM enables seamless adaptation to diverse waveforms, such as orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM), orthogonal chirp division multiplexing (OCDM), orthogonal time-frequency space (OTFS), affine frequency division multiplexing (AFDM), and frequency modulated continuous wave (FMCW) within a unified framework. Critical aspects such as coexistence strategies, and resource allocation are thoroughly explored. Simulation results demonstrate the proposed framework’s ability to deliver superior communication performance, robust sensing capabilities, and efficient coexistence, surpassing traditional waveform designs in scalability and adaptability.

Introduction

As wireless communication progresses toward 6th generation, the expectations for network capabilities and performance have significantly broadened
[
1
]
. Beyond achieving higher data rates, 6th generation aims to support a wide range of advanced applications
[
2

, such as integrated artificial intelligence (AI) and communication, integrated sensing and communication (ISAC), and ubiquitous connectivity. Moreover, it seeks to enhance three existing scenarios: immersive communication, massive communication, and hyper-reliable low-latency communication. These scenarios are extensions of 5G’s established use cases, including enhanced mobile broadband, massive machine-type communications, and ultra-reliable low latency communications, as defined by IMT-2030
[
3

.

Addressing these diverse requirements demands a highly adaptable physical layer capable of accommodating varying operational conditions, such as high-mobility environments, ultra-low latency, and the need for enhanced spectral efficiency. However, developing such a flexible physical layer introduces considerable challenges, particularly in waveform design.

Orthogonal frequency division multiplexing, a foundational waveform for 4th generation and 5th generation systems, has been widely adopted due to its spectral efficiency, implementation simplicity, and compatibility with multiple-input multiple-output systems
[
4
]
. Despite these strengths, it has inherent drawbacks that become more pronounced as communication systems evolve. These include a high peak-to-average power ratio, leading to power inefficiencies in uplink scenarios, particularly for energy-constrained devices, and poor performance in high-mobility environments, such as vehicle-to-everything communication and high-speed railways
[
5
]

To address these limitations, several waveform modifications were introduced in 5th generation. One prominent example is discrete Fourier transform spread orthogonal frequency division multiplexing
[
2
]
, which has been widely adopted for uplink transmission in 5th generation due to its ability to reduce peak-to-average power ratio. Discrete Fourier transform spread Orthogonal frequency division multiplexing retains the spectral efficiency of orthogonal frequency division multiplexing while enhancing power efficiency, making it particularly well-suited for power-constrained devices. Additionally, variants such as zero-tail discrete Fourier transform spread orthogonal frequency division multiplexing
[
6

and unique word discrete Fourier transform spread orthogonal frequency division multiplexing
[
7

were proposed to address out-of-band emissions and guard interval limitations, offering increased robustness against timing mismatches and multipath effects. While these advancements improved flexibility and performance, 5th generation waveforms still fail to meet the diverse requirements anticipated for 6th generation networks.

With the transition to 6th generation, the need for new waveforms capable of addressing high-mobility scenarios, doubly-dispersive channels, and ultra-low latency demands has become evident. Emerging candidates such as orthogonal time-frequency space
[
8

, affine frequency division multiplexing

[

For instance, orthogonal time-frequency space operates in the delay-Doppler domain, offering exceptional resilience to Doppler effects, making it a strong candidate for high-mobility environments such as vehicle-to-everything and low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite communications
[
11
]
. On the other hand, affine frequency division multiplexing demonstrates robustness in doubly-dispersive channels
[
12
]
, where interference mitigation is critical. Similarly, orthogonal chirp division multiplexing, leveraging chirp signals, excels in combating inter-symbol interference, making it highly suitable for scenarios demanding high spectral efficiency under challenging channel conditions.

While each of these waveforms offers unique benefits, their coexistence within a single network introduces new challenges. 6Th generation networks are expected to simultaneously support multiple waveforms, each tailored to specific applications with distinct performance requirements
[

Without effective coordination, waveform coexistence risks increased interference, suboptimal resource utilization, and degraded network performance.

The challenge extends beyond ensuring waveform compatibility to achieving high spectral efficiency and low implementation complexity. For 6th generation networks to meet their potential, a unified waveform framework is required—one capable of dynamically generating multiple waveforms and ensuring their orthogonal coexistence within the same spectral resources. This framework would enable the network to adapt to the diverse needs of 6th generation applications, allowing different waveforms to operate seamlessly without mutual interference. Moreover, such a unified approach is critical not only for supporting the expansive range of 6th generation use cases but also for maintaining backward compatibility with existing 4th generation and 5th generation systems.

This paper addresses a critical challenge in 6th generation—the need for a unified waveform architecture that enables the seamless and orthogonal coexistence of diverse waveforms, particularly those based on discrete Fourier transform principles. We introduce the innovative concept of a ”mother waveform,” a foundational waveform designed to generate multiple specialized waveforms tailored for different applications, ensuring minimal interference and optimal resource utilization. This mother waveform framework not only accommodates 6th generation waveforms such as orthogonal time-frequency space, affine frequency division multiplexing, and orthogonal chirp division multiplexing, but also maintains backward compatibility with legacy 5th generation waveforms like discrete Fourier transform spread orthogonal frequency division multiplexing, offering a cost-effective and efficient solution for the seamless transition to 6th generation. This framework offers enhanced flexibility in spreading techniques, resource allocation, and ensures backward compatibility, supporting seamless integration across different wireless standards.

In addition, this paper introduces a novel flexible and sparse resource allocation scheme for orthogonal time-frequency space in the time-frequency domain, which allows a seamless coexistence scheme with other waveforms defined in the time-frequency domain.

PRELIMINARIES

In this section, we introduce the discrete Zak transform, followed by a discussion on key waveform models such as orthogonal frequency division multiplexing, single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing, orthogonal time-frequency space, frequency modulated continuous wave, orthogonal chirp division multiplexing, and affine frequency division multiplexing.

II-A

Discrete Zak Transform

The delay-Doppler domain, also known as Zak space, is an intermediate space between the direct signal space ”time” and the Fourier space ”frequency”. Assume the delay-Doppler grid is discretized into
M
delays and
N
Doppler bins, and consider an arbitrary discrete
MN
-point sequence
\boldsymbol{s}
. The discrete Zak transform of
\boldsymbol{s}
is an isometry
\mathbb{C}^{MN}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N\times M}
expressed as
[
18
]

\displaystyle X(k,l)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}s(l+nM)e^{-j2\pi\frac{k}{N}n},

(1)

\displaystyle\text{for}~0\leq l<M,0\leq k<N.

To compute
X(k,l)
,
M
discrete Fourier transform blocks of size
N
are required for the data sets
s(l),s(l+M),\ldots,s(l+(N-1)M),~0\leq l<M
. The original sequence
\boldsymbol{s}
can be recovered from its discrete Zak transform using the inverse discrete Zak transform, as follows

\displaystyle s(l+nM)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X(k,l)e^{j2\pi\frac{k}{N}n}

(2)

\displaystyle\text{for}~0\leq l<M,0\leq n<N.

The vectorized output of the inverse discrete Zak transform for a matrix
\mathbf{X}
can be written as

\mathbf{s}=\left(\mathbf{F}_{N}^{\mathsf{H}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{M}\right)\text{vec}(\mathbf{X}),

(3)

where
\mathbf{F}_{N}
is
N\times N
unitary discrete Fourier transform matrix and
\mathbf{I}_{M}
is the
M\times M
identity matrix.

II-B

OFDM Signal Model

Consider a wireless communication system where
N
data symbols are modulated using orthogonal frequency division multiplexing over a total bandwidth
B
, operating at the carrier frequency
f_{c}
. The orthogonal frequency division multiplexing modulator distributes the symbols
\{X(k),~k=0,\dots,N-1\}
in the frequency domain. Denoting the bandwidth of each of the
N
subcarriers as
\Delta f=B/N
, the time duration of one orthogonal frequency division multiplexing symbol is
T=1/\Delta f
. The frequency samples
X(k)
are then converted to the time domain using an inverse discrete Fourier transform operation. The discrete output of an orthogonal frequency division multiplexing modulator is

s^{\text{OFDM}}(l)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x^{\text{OFDM}}(k)e^{j2\pi\frac{kl}{N}},

(4)

or equivalently in matrix form is given as

\mathbf{s}^{\text{OFDM}}=\mathbf{F}_{N}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{x}^{\text{OFDM}}.

(5)

II-C

SC-IFDM Signal Model

In single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing,
M\times N
data symbols are modulated by mapping them to
N
discrete Fourier transform blocks, each of size
M
. The outputs of these blocks are interleaved before applying a final
MN
-point inverse discrete Fourier transform. The resulting time-domain signal
s^{\text{SC-IFDM}}(p)
, with the data symbols
{X^{\text{SC-IFDM}}(k,l)}
for
k=0,\dots,N-1
and
l=0,\dots,M-1
, is given for
p=0,1,\dots,MN-1
as
[
19
]

s^{\text{SC-IFDM}}(p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X^{\text{SC-IFDM}}(k,[p]_{M})e^{j2\pi\frac{k}{MN}p}.

(6)

By setting
p=l+nM
with
n=0,\dots,N-1
, we get

s^{\text{SC-IFDM}}(l+nM)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X^{\text{SC-IFDM}}(k,l)e^{j2\pi\frac{k(l+nM)}{MN}}.

(7)

To express the discrete time-domain single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing baseband signal in (
7
) in a matrix-vector form, we write the output of the
M
-point discrete Fourier transform block after interleaving as

s(k+mN)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{l=0}^{M-1}X^{\text{SC-IFDM}}(k,l)e^{-j2\pi\frac{l}{M}m},

(8)

which is then written in matrix form as

\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(\mathbf{I}_{N}\otimes\mathbf{F}_{M}\right)\mathbf{x}^{\text{SC-IFDM}},

(9)

where
\mathbf{x}^{\text{SC-IFDM}}=\text{vec}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{SC-IFDM}})
,
\mathbf{\Gamma}=[\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{\Gamma}_{M-1}]^{\text{T}}
is the interleaving matrix, where each
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{m}
is defined as
\text{CircShift}\left((\mathbf{I}_{N}\otimes\mathbf{\Upsilon}),m\right)
for
m=0,\dots,M-1
and
\mathbf{\Upsilon}=[1,\mathbf{0}_{M-1}]
.
Thus, (
7
) is reformulated as

\mathbf{s}^{\text{SC-IFDM}}=\mathbf{F}_{MN}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(\mathbf{I}_{N}\otimes\mathbf{F}_{M}\right)\mathbf{s}^{\text{SC-IFDM}}.

(10)

II-D

OTFS Signal Model

In orthogonal time-frequency space,
M\times N
data symbols are modulated over a total bandwidth
B
at the carrier frequency
f_{c}
. The orthogonal time-frequency space modulator maps the data symbols
\{X^{\text{OTFS}}(k,l),~k=0,\dots,N-1,~l=0,\dots,M-1\}
onto the delay-Doppler grid. Each subcarrier occupies a bandwidth of
\Delta f=B/M
, resulting in a time duration of
T=1/\Delta f
. Consequently, an orthogonal time-frequency space frame spans a total duration of
NT
. The mapped symbols are transformed into the time-frequency domain using the inverse symplectic finite Fourier transform, which is defined as

X^{\mathrm{TF}}(n,m)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\sum_{l=0}^{M-1}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X^{\text{OTFS}}(k,l)e^{j2\pi\left(\frac{nk}{N}-\frac{ml}{M}\right)}.

(11)

The time-domain orthogonal time-frequency space signal is then produced by converting the time-frequency samples to the time domain using the inverse discrete Fourier transform, i.e.,

s^{\text{OTFS}}(m+nM)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}X^{\mathrm{TF}}(n,m)e^{j2\pi\frac{m}{M}n}.

(12)

Note that the total length of the discrete sequences representing the frame is
M\times N
. Substituting (
11
) in (
12
) and using
(1/M)\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}e^{j2\pi m\left(l-l^{\prime}\right)/M}=\delta\left(l-l^{\prime}\right)
, we obtain

s^{\text{OTFS}}(l+nM)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X^{\text{OTFS}}(k,l)e^{j2\pi\frac{k}{N}n},

(13)

which is precisely the inverse discrete Zak transform described in (
2
).
As a result, the discrete Fourier transform-based cascaded inverse symplectic finite Fourier transform and orthogonal frequency division multiplexing modulation have a similar structure to the inverse discrete Zak transform. In order to obtain the discrete sequence without employing the inverse symplectic finite Fourier transform, one can use the inverse discrete Zak transform. Similar outcomes can be attained for the receiver when the discrete Fourier transform-based orthogonal frequency division multiplexing demodulation is coupled with the symplectic finite Fourier transform, which yields the discrete Zak transform, i.e.,

\mathbf{s}^{\text{OTFS}}=\left(\mathbf{F}_{N}^{\mathsf{H}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{M}\right)\text{vec}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{OTFS}})=\left(\mathbf{F}_{N}^{\mathsf{H}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{M}\right)\mathbf{x}^{\text{OTFS}}.

(14)

FMCW Signal Model

II-F

OCDM Signal Model

In orthogonal chirp division multiplexing, a set of
M\times N
orthogonal chirps are used to modulate
M\times N
data symbols

s^{\text{OCDM}}(p)=\frac{e^{j\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{MN}}\sum_{i=0}^{MN-1}x^{\text{OCDM}}(i)e^{-j\pi\frac{(p-i)^{2}}{MN}},

(17)

which can equivalently be expressed in matrix-vector form as

\mathbf{s}^{\text{OCDM}}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{x}^{\text{OCDM}},

(18)

where
\mathbf{\Phi}
is the unitary discrete Fresnel transform matrix, defined with the

\Phi(p,i)=\frac{e^{j\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{MN}}e^{-j\pi\frac{(p-i)^{2}}{MN}},[MN]_{2}=0.

(19)

The matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}
can be decomposed into a discrete Fourier transform matrix with additional quadratic phase shifts

\mathbf{\Phi}=\mathbf{\Theta}_{1}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{\Theta}_{2},

(20)

where
\mathbf{\Theta}_{1}
and
\mathbf{\Theta}_{2}
are diagonal phase matrices given by
\mathbf{\Theta}_{1}(p,p)=e^{-\frac{\pi}{4}}e^{j2\pi\frac{p^{2}}{MN}}
and
\mathbf{\Theta}_{1}(i,i)=e^{j2\pi\frac{p^{2}}{MN}}
.

In affine frequency division multiplexing, a vector of symbols

In a matrix form, (

\mathbf{\Lambda}_{c_{i}}\triangleq\text{diag}[e^{-j2\pi c_{i}(0)^{2}},\dots,e^{-j2\pi c_{i}(MN-1)^{2}}

III-F

Proposed Mother Waveform

We have shown that orthogonal frequency division multiplexing, orthogonal time-frequency space, frequency modulated continuous wave, orthogonal chirp division multiplexing, and affine frequency division multiplexing can be efficiently generated using the unified single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing framework. This insight extends to other advanced waveforms, such as generalized frequency division multiplexing, zero-tail orthogonal frequency division multiplexing, unique word orthogonal frequency division multiplexing, Enhanced unique word orthogonal frequency division multiplexing, and UW DFT-S-W-OFDM, which share similar structural foundations, as noted in
[
2
]
. These structural commonalities inspire the development of a generalized ”mother waveform” based on single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing, from which all other waveforms can be derived through simple phase adjustments and customized data allocation techniques. The primary advantage of this approach lies in its backward compatibility with existing systems, requiring no hardware modifications. This framework capitalizes on current infrastructure, offering a cost-effective pathway to introducing new waveforms without the need for substantial upgrades. By generating all discrete Fourier transform-based waveforms from a single versatile structure, the proposed framework provides a dynamic physical layer that can adapt to a wide range of applications, offering flexibility in both waveform design and resource allocation.

As illustrated in Fig.
5
,
this mother waveform framework systematically generates various waveforms by adjusting how data blocks are allocated, either through interleaving or direct mapping. It allows targeted waveform creation through specific mappings in the grid
k
and
l
indices. This flexibility enables seamless orthogonal coexistence of multiple waveforms, allowing 4th generation, 5th generation, and 6th generation systems to support diverse applications with minimal interference.
Moreover, this approach enhances the scalability and adaptability of communication systems, enabling the coexistence of multiple waveforms within the same time-frequency resources. It offers a robust solution that meets the evolving demands of future wireless networks, balancing high performance with the seamless integration of heterogeneous services. By facilitating smooth transitions between generations and efficiently managing spectrum resources, the proposed framework ensures that both current and future applications can coexist without requiring changes to the existing hardware infrastructure, making it a highly scalable and future-proof solution for next-generation networks.

IV
Practical Co-existence Strategies of The Proposed Approach for 6G

To emphasize the effective integration across the time-frequency domain discussed in the previous section, this work explores orthogonal co-existence capabilities between orthogonal time-frequency space or single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing and orthogonal frequency division multiplexing, as well as between single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing and affine frequency division multiplexing.
First, we introduce a novel resource allocation strategy for orthogonal time-frequency space, designed to facilitate its orthogonal co-existence with other waveforms. Second, we discuss a specific scenario demonstrating orthogonal co-existence between orthogonal time-frequency space and orthogonal frequency division multiplexing. Lastly, we propose a method for achieving orthogonal co-existence between single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing and affine frequency division multiplexing.
These discussions highlight the effectiveness of the mother waveform concept in supporting orthogonal co-existence while demonstrating its versatility to extend beyond the presented cases for diverse applications.

IV-A

OTFS and SC-IFDM Resource Allocation Scheme

Both orthogonal time-frequency space and single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing distribute data across the entire time-frequency domain to capture delay and Doppler information. However, this inherently prevents orthogonal coexistence with other waveforms within the same resources. Additionally, when an orthogonal time-frequency space or single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing user requires less data than the total grid, the data must still occupy the entire time-frequency resources to maintain delay and Doppler resolution, leading to reduced spectral efficiency.
To address this limitation, we propose a method to optimize resource allocation in the time-frequency domain without compromising the delay and Doppler representation or resolution. This approach leverages the inherent capability of the orthogonal time-frequency space system to map time-frequency resources using the inverse symplectic finite Fourier transform transform. The delay and Doppler resolution remain dependent solely on the system’s bandwidth and symbol duration, ensuring efficient and flexible allocation.

Consider an orthogonal time-frequency space system with
\frac{MN}{\alpha\beta}
data bins, represented as
x[k^{\prime},l^{\prime}]
, where
k^{\prime}=0,\dots,\frac{N}{\alpha}-1
and
l^{\prime}=0,\dots,\frac{M}{\beta}-1
. Our approach employs a Kronecker product with a precoding matrix to precisely allocate resources in the time-frequency domain, mathematically expressed as

\mathbf{X}_{pr}=\mathbf{P}\otimes\mathbf{X},

(39)

where the precoding matrix is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{q_{1},q_{2}}=\mathbf{f}_{q_{1}}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{f}_{q_{2}}.

(40)

Here,
\mathbf{f}_{q_{1}}^{\mathsf{H}}
denotes the conjugate of the
q_{1}
-th column of the
\alpha\times\alpha
discrete Fourier transform matrix, and
\mathbf{f}_{q_{2}}
represents the
q_{2}
-th row of the
\beta\times\beta
discrete Fourier transform matrix. The matrix in (
40
) can be written explicitly as

\displaystyle P_{q_{1},q_{2}}[a,b]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}e\left\{\frac{-aq_{1}}{\alpha}+\frac{bq_{2}}{\beta}\right\},

(41)

where
e\{\cdot\}=e^{j2\pi(\cdot)}
,
a=\lfloor{\frac{k\alpha}{N}}\rfloor
, and
b=\lfloor{\frac{l\beta}{M}}\rfloor
. Therefore, the precoded signal,
\mathbf{X}_{pr}
, is expressed as

{X}_{\text{pr}}[k,l]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}X_{s}([k]_{\frac{N}{\alpha}},[l]_{\frac{M}{\beta}})e\left\{\frac{-aq_{1}}{\alpha}+\frac{bq_{2}}{\beta}\right\}.

(42)

By substituting
k=k^{\prime}+a\frac{N}{\alpha}
and
l=l^{\prime}+b\frac{M}{\beta}
into (
11
), where
k^{\prime}=0,\dots,\frac{N}{\alpha}-1
and
l^{\prime}=0,\dots,\frac{M}{\beta}-1
, the time-frequency domain signal is given by

\displaystyle X_{\text{TF}}[n,m]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN\alpha\beta}}\sum_{a=0}^{\alpha-1}\sum_{b=0}^{\beta-1}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{N}{\alpha}-1}\sum_{l^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{M}{\beta}-1}{x}_{s}[k^{\prime},l^{\prime}]

(43)

\displaystyle\times e\left\{\frac{n(k^{\prime}+\frac{aN}{\alpha})}{N}-\frac{m(l^{\prime}+\frac{bM}{\beta})}{M}\right\}e\left\{\frac{bq_{2}}{\beta}-\frac{aq_{1}}{\alpha}\right\}.

After simplification,
X_{\text{TF}}[n,m]
can be written as

\displaystyle X_{\text{TF}}[n,m]=\frac{\psi}{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}\sum_{a=0}^{\alpha-1}\sum_{b=0}^{\beta-1}e\left\{\frac{a(n-q_{1})}{\alpha}-\frac{b(m-q_{2})}{\beta}\right\},

(44)

where
\psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{N}{\alpha}-1}\sum_{l^{\prime}=0}^{\frac{M}{\beta}-1}x_{s}[k^{\prime},l^{\prime}]e\left\{\frac{nk^{\prime}}{N}-\frac{ml^{\prime}}{M}\right\}

) can be expressed as a product of two Kronecker delta functions as follows

\displaystyle X_{\text{TF}}[n,m]=\psi\delta_{nq_{1}}\delta_{mq_{2}}=\psi\sum_{a=0}^{\alpha-1}e^{\left\{\frac{a(n-q_{1})}{\alpha}\right\}}\sum_{b=0}^{\beta-1}e^{\left\{\frac{b(q_{2}-m)}{\beta}\right\}}.

(45)

For
X_{\text{TF}}[n,m]
to be nonzero, both
\delta_{nq_{1}}
and
\delta_{mq_{2}}
must be nonzero, which occurs only when
[n-q_{1}]_{\alpha}=0
and
[m-q_{2}]_{\beta}=0
. Thus, the time-frequency grid for
X_{\text{TF}}[n,m]
can be controlled as

\displaystyle X_{\text{TF}}[n,m]=\begin{cases}\psi,&[n-q_{1}]_{\alpha}=0\,\text{and}\,[m-q_{2}]_{\beta}=0,\\
0,&\text{Otherwise}.\end{cases}.

(46)

This demonstrates that the orthogonal time-frequency space grid in time-frequency domain can be controlled by adjusting the precoding matrix
P_{q_{1},q_{2}}[a,b]
, as illustrated in Fig.
6
. The parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
define the spacing between active bins in the time and frequency domains, respectively, while
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
specify the starting indices of the active bins in these domains, enabling flexible and efficient resource allocation without degrading OTFS capabilities of representing delay and Doppler efficiently since the same bandwidth and symbol duration is preserved.

IV-B

SC-IFDM/OTFS and OFDM Co-existence

Building on the earlier discussion of waveform flexibility within the single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing framework, this section addresses the coexistence of single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing/orthogonal time-frequency space with orthogonal frequency division multiplexing in a multi-user scenario. Given that orthogonal time-frequency space and single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing are capable of handling high mobility challenges, they are ideal for mobile users. In contrast, orthogonal frequency division multiplexing remains highly effective for static users due to its low-complexity frequency-domain equalization and reduced system latency. Ensuring the seamless integration of these waveforms within the same system is critical for optimizing the overall performance. To achieve this, an orthogonal co-existence scheme is necessary, allowing orthogonal frequency division multiplexing to serve static users while single-carrier interleaved frequency division multiplexing or orthogonal time-frequency space addresses the needs of mobile users by operating efficiently in the delay-Doppler domain. In this context, we focus on orthogonal time-frequency space as the representative waveform for mobile users.
An orthogonal time co-existence approach, combined with the full cyclic prefix method described in
[
22
]
, is used to address interference between OFDM and OTFS.
In this scheme, orthogonal time-frequency space occupies

data bins after setting

and
q_{1}=0
, while orthogonal frequency division multiplexing utilizes the remaining
(MN-\frac{MN}{\alpha})
data bins. This configuration results in (
N/\alpha

M

7

\displaystyle X_{\text{TF}}[n,m]=\begin{cases}0,~~&[n]_{\alpha}=0,\\

(47)

Furthermore, the proposed schemes exhibit performance close to frequency modulated continuous wave with appropriate power allocation. In contrast, the existing OFDM-FMCW method exhibits inferior performance. This degradation is primarily due to the loss of orthogonality between the chirp signal and data, as well as among the data symbols themselves. The issue arises because the chirp is extracted from the diagonal elements of the DFT matrix, leading to interference. This interference limits sensing accuracy and highlights the importance of designing better waveforms for such systems.

The results demonstrate that the proposed SC-IFDM-Chirp, SC-IFDM-OCDM, and SC-IFDM-AFDM waveforms provide robust sensing performance comparable to OTFS-FMCW and FMCW, while addressing the limitations of existing methods such as OFDM-FMCW. The flexibility in power allocation and orthogonality preservation highlights their effectiveness for joint communication and sensing applications.

VI
conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a unified ”mother waveform” framework, capable of generating multiple discrete Fourier transform-based waveforms like OTFS, OFDM, FMCW, OCDM, and AFDM from a single SC-IFDM structure. We showed that these waveforms differ primarily in phase shifts and data allocation strategies, enabling efficient orthogonal coexistence within shared time-frequency resources. This solution supports diverse 4G, 5G, and 6G applications such as URLLC, mMTC, and high-mobility scenarios, while ensuring backward compatibility with existing infrastructure. Our simulations confirmed the framework’s effectiveness in achieving low BER, efficient resource allocation, and robust multi-user performance. Future work may focus on real-time implementation on SDR platforms and using artificial intelligence / machine learningfor dynamic waveform switching, advancing the adaptability and scalability of communication systems for 6G networks and beyond.
