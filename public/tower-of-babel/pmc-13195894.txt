Effectiveness and safety of SGLT2 inhibitors in aged patients (≥ 75 Years) with diabetes: a multi-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hsu-En Huang, Jeng-Yu Jan, Yu-Sheng Lin, Chun-Liang Lin, Meng-Hung Lin, Ting-Yu Kuo, Pei-Chun Yen, Chen-Yi Li, Shih-Tai Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) is rising globally, especially among older adults, creating unique management challenges due to increased susceptibility to adverse drug reactions and multiple comorbidities. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) have shown cardioprotective and renoprotective effects, but their safety and efficacy in aged populations, particularly those aged ≥ 75 years, remain under-explored.MethodsIn this multi-center retrospective cohort study, we analyzed data from the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital Medical System in Taiwan, focusing on diabetic patients aged ≥ 75 years between 2012 and 2021. Initially, 33,964 patients were screened. After serial exclusions, a total of 3000 patients were included, with 1,000 in the SGLT2i group and 2,000 in the non-SGLT2i group, matched 1:2 by key variables. Clinical outcomes, including all-cause mortality, cardiovascular (CV) death, heart failure (HF) hospitalization, worsening renal function, and urinary tract infections (UTIs), were compared between groups over a maximum 5-year follow-up, with a minimum follow-up of one year.ResultsThe final cohort had a mean age of 83.5 ± 4.2 years, with 78.7% of patients aged 75–85 and 6.6% aged > 90 years. After inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), SGLT2i use was associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality (6.1% vs. 11.3%, p < 0.05) and a lower incidence of worsening renal function (12.9% vs. 16.3%, p < 0.05). Cardiovascular mortality (1.6% vs. 2.4%, p = 0.08) and HF hospitalization rates (8.9% vs. 9.1%, p = 0.75) showed no significant differences between the groups. Notably, the reduced risk of renal decline was more pronounced in those aged 75–85 years, while benefits appeared to diminish in patients over 85 years. UTI incidence was similar between SGLT2i and non-SGLT2i users (5.7% vs. 6.3%, p = 0.64), suggesting no increased risk. Subgroup analysis highlighted that SGLT2i maintained benefits on all-cause mortality and renal outcomes in most aged subgroups, but careful consideration is needed for the very aged patients (> 85 years).ConclusionSGLT2i treatment in DM patients aged ≥ 75 years seems associated with improved all-cause mortality and renal outcomes without increasing the risk of UTIs. The effectiveness of SGLT2i may decrease in very aged patients (> 85 years), suggesting careful patient selection is required in this subgroup.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07446-2.

Introduction

The prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) continues to rise globally, with a notable increase among older adults. Managing hyperglycemia in this population presents unique challenges, as older adults are more susceptible to adverse drug reactions, have multiple comorbidities, and often exhibit greater heterogeneity in functional status. Therefore, therapeutic approaches must balance efficacy with safety, taking into consideration both glycemic control and the broader context of aging-related factors such as cardiovascular health and renal function [1, 2].

Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) represent a relatively novel class of antihyperglycemic agents that act by inhibiting glucose reabsorption in the proximal renal tubule, thereby promoting glycosuria and reducing plasma glucose levels. Beyond their glucose-lowering effects, SGLT2i have demonstrated significant cardiovascular and renal protective benefits, making them attractive therapeutic options for older adults who are at higher risk for cardiovascular disease and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [3–11]. Despite these promising benefits, the application of SGLT2i in the geriatric population remains under-explored, especially those with age ≥ 75-year-old. Older patients are often underrepresented in clinical trials, leading to uncertainties about the safety and efficacy of these agents in this age group [3–11]. Furthermore, concerns related to the potential side effects of SGLT2i, such as volume depletion, urinary tract infections, and genital mycotic infections, are particularly relevant for older adults who may be more vulnerable to these adverse events [1, 12].

The objective of this study is to assess the current evidence on the use of SGLT2i in older adults aged ≥ 75 years with DM, focusing on their efficacy, safety profile, and potential benefits beyond glycemic control. Special attention will be given to the cardiovascular and renal outcomes, as well as the potential risks associated with SGLT2i in this population. By synthesizing the available data, we aim to provide guidance on the clinical utility of SGLT2i in managing diabetes among those aged ≥ 75 years old, with a focus on optimizing therapeutic outcomes while minimizing risks.

Patients and methods

In this multi-center retrospective cohort study, patient data were obtained from the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (CGMH) Medical System, the largest healthcare provider in Taiwan. The system comprises four tertiary care medical centers and three major teaching hospitals, with a total of approximately 10,050 beds, admitting more than 280,000 patients annually and serving about one-tenth of Taiwan’s population [13]. The CGMH medical database includes comprehensive information such as diagnoses, laboratory results, medications, echocardiography, imaging data, and detailed chart records [14, 15]. These records are linked and continuously monitored using consistent data encryption. To protect patient privacy, each patient’s identification number was encrypted and de-identified, thereby waiving the need for informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (IRB No. 202401275B0).

Study design and population

This multi-center retrospective cohort study analyzed data from aged patients (aged 75 or older) diagnosed with diabetes and prescribed anti-diabetic medications between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2021. The sequential process of the study design is illustrated as Supplemental Fig. 1. The initial cohort included 33,964 patients. After excluding 84 patients whose administration of SGLT2i preceded their diabetes diagnosis, 33,610 patients were divided into two groups: 3,661 patients prescribed SGLT2i and 29,949 patients not prescribed SGLT2i. Further exclusion criteria for the SGLT2i group included a history of autoimmune disease or cancer, and lack of HbA1c testing in the peri-period of SGLT2i prescription. Additional exclusions were applied to match clinical trial criteria for patients prescribed with SGLT2i [3, 6, 11]: patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) less than 30 mL/min/1.73 m² or without eGFR testing during the peri-prescription period were excluded. To ensure adequate follow-up, we also excluded those who initiated SGLT2i after December 31, 2021, and those with follow-up periods of less than 90 days. The final cohort consisted of 2089 diabetic patients on SGLT2i. Patients were then matched 1:2 by sex, age, heart failure diagnosis, HbA1c, and eGFR group, resulting in 1,000 patients in the SGLT2i group and 2,000 patients in the non-SGLT2i group as illustrated in Supplemental Fig. 1. The sequential process of the study design is illustrated as Supplemental Fig. 2.

Definition of index date and landmark period

SGLT2i Group: The index date was defined as the date of the first SGLT2i prescription. To ensure consistent exposure and clinical stability, we included only patients who maintained continuous SGLT2i use for at least 90 days. Control Group: We assigned a “pseudo-index date” based on a matched medical visit within the same calendar year. Crucially, to ensure a fair comparison and eliminate immortal time bias, we applied the same 90-day survival requirement to the control group. Patients in both groups who died or were lost to follow-up within the first 90 days post-index were excluded from the primary analysis. This approach, often referred to as a Landmark design, effectively eliminates immortal time because: The “immortal” period (the 90-day window required for treatment definition) is applied symmetrically to both the treated and untreated groups. The follow-up period for the survival analysis effectively begins after this 90-day landmark. Therefore, no “immortal” person-time is contributed to the hazard ratio calculation in either group. While this design prevents immortal time bias, we acknowledge it may introduce a degree of selection bias by excluding the most fragile patients who died early (within 90 days).

In our study, the index date for the exposure group was defined as the date of SGLT2 inhibitor initiation. However, patients in the non-SGLT2i control group did not have a natural treatment initiation date that could serve as a comparable index date. To ensure temporal alignment and avoid immortal time bias, we first performed 1:2 matching based on sex, age, year of diabetes diagnosis, heart failure diagnosis, HbA1c category, and eGFR category. Through this step, each matched control was assigned the same index date as its corresponding SGLT2i initiator, thereby establishing a comparable baseline time point for follow-up. The purpose of this initial matching step was not to perform full confounding adjustment, but rather to create a clinically and temporally comparable risk set, ensuring that controls were at risk and eligible for SGLT2i initiation at the same time point as treated patients.

Clinical outcomes and definitions

The observation period was from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2022 to ensure the follow up period at least 12 months in alive patients, and the end of the follow-up period was defined as the maximum 5-year follow-up, the date of clinical outcome development, or mortality, whichever came first. The clinical outcomes in this study were all-cause mortality, cardiovascular (CV) death, heart failure (HF) hospitalization, worsening renal function, and urinary tract infections (UTIs). To guarantee precision and prevent incorrect classifications, the identification of all study outcomes was strictly based on discharge diagnoses. Initially, these codes followed the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) system. After January 1, 2016, the system transitioned to using the Tenth Revision (ICD-10-CM) codes. The specific ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes utilized to identify both the adverse cardiovascular events under study and the baseline covariates are detailed in Supplemental Table 1. Comorbidities were identified based on all available diagnostic records prior to the index date. To ensure diagnostic validity and filter out incidental coding, we applied a stringent criterion: a patient was classified as having a comorbidity only if they had at least two outpatient diagnoses or at least one inpatient diagnosis recorded. This approach balances the need for sensitivity in capturing chronic conditions with the necessity of diagnostic specificity. CV death was defined as death caused by acute myocardial infarction, sudden cardiac death, HF, stroke, CV procedures, CV hemorrhage, or other CV causes. Heart failure hospitalization was defined as an unscheduled hospitalization with new or worsening symptoms and diagnostic test results consistent with the diagnosis of HF, necessitating significant changes in HF treatment, such as significant change in oral diuretics or initiation of intravenous diuretic or initiation of intravenous vasoactive agents or receive mechanical ventilation or mechanical support [16]. Worsening renal function was defined as having at least two eGFR tests, with one of the following criteria met: (1) a decline in eGFR exceeding 50% compared to the previous test, or (2) a decrease of more than 30 mL/min/1.73 m² from the previous value, with the follow-up eGFR falling below 60 mL/min/1.73 m², or maintenance of dialysis. Maintenance dialysis was predefined as the requirement for dialysis for a continuous period of at least 3 months (90 days). This definition is consistent with established clinical practice guidelines and major cardiovascular/renal outcome trials to represent the progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). UTI was defined as hospitalization or emergency department visits due to UTIs.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables are expressed as mean ± SD and percentages. The inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) method was used to reduce confounding effects when comparing the groups. We used a multivariable logistic regression model to estimate the propensity score, which included selected covariates listed in Table 1 and Supplemental Fig. 3. IPTW created a new population where treatment was randomly assigned, allowing for an estimation of the average treatment effect. Stabilized weights were used to address extreme values in the propensity score. The balance of covariates between the groups before and after IPTW was checked using the absolute standardized mean difference (ASMD). A value of less than 0.1 indicated a negligible difference, while a value between 0.1 and 0.2 indicated a small difference. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to compare the risk of outcomes between the groups, with a P value of less than 0.05 considered significant. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to illustrate the cumulative incidence rates of study outcomes in the SGLT2i and non-SGLT2i groups, and the log-rank test was used to test differences between the two groups. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by treating age and HbA1c as restricted cubic splines with four knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles). All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort

The baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. In the unweighted cohort, a total of 1,000 patients were treated with SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), and 2,000 patients did not receive SGLT2i therapy. After applying IPTW to adjust for confounding variables, the weighted cohort included 998 patients in the SGLT2i group and 2,006 patients in the non-SGLT2i group. Before IPTW, the mean age of patients was 83.5 ± 3.8 years, with the majority of patients were between 80 and 90 years old (78.7% in both groups) and 6.6% of patients were 90 years or older. There were 47.8% being male in both the SGLT2i and non-SGLT2i groups. The eGFR categories showed consistent distributions between groups. In the SGLT2i group, 45.6% of patients had an eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m². These proportions were virtually identical in the non-SGLT2i group across eGFR categories (ASMD = 0.043). Glycemic control was also comparable, with mean HbA1c levels of 7.7% in the SGLT2i group and 7.6% in the non-SGLT2i group and major the patients (70.9%) were acceptable targeted sugar control according to diabetic control guideline in those aged ≧ 75 years [17]. In this study cohort, most patients have hypertension, chronic kidney disease and dyslipidemia. After adjustment, comorbidities such as atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were evenly distributed. In terms of medication use, the most patients accepted angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors / Angiotensin II receptor blockers and following drugs were statins, beta-blockers and Dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors. Furthermore, these medications were well-matched between groups following IPTW. Baseline laboratory results, including lipid levels and HbA1c, were also similar between the two cohorts, ensuring that the treatment groups were comparable for outcome analysis.

Cardiovascular outcomes between the cohorts with/without treating with SGLT2i

During the first-year follow-up period, the potential hypoglycemic effect was seemed better in non-SGLT2i group than that in SGLT2i group (Supplemental Table 2) (level of HBA1C at 6 months: SGLT2i group vs. non-SGLT2i: 7.6 ± 1.4% vs. 7.4 ± 1.3%, ASMD = 0.146; level of HBA1C at 12 months: 7.4 ± 1.3% vs. 7.2 ± 1.3%, ASMD = 0.171). However, in the 5-year follow up period, the incidence of all-cause mortality was 6.0% in the SGLT2i group and 10.6% in the non-SGLT2i group during follow-up (ASMD = 0.161). After IPTW adjustment, the mortality rates were 6.1% in the SGLT2i group and 11.3% in the non-SGLT2i group (ASMD = 0.164). It also showed a suggested a mortality benefit in SGLT2i users as illustrated in Fig. 1. To enhance transparency, a graphical representation (e.g., a Love plot) of covariate balance before and after weighting in the Supplementary Fig. 3. Several covariates exhibited imbalance (ASMD > 0.1) before weighting; after IPTW adjustment, all covariates achieved adequate balance with ASMD < 0.1.

As the trend of all-cause mortality, the similar picture also noted in terms of cardiovascular death. Cardiovascular death occurred in 1.6% of patients with treating SGLT2i and 2.3% of patients without treating with SGLT2i in the unweighted cohort, and similar results were observed after IPTW adjustment (SGLT2i group vs. non- SGLT2i group: 1.6 vs. 2.4%, ASMD: 0.054). It showed that treating with SGLT2i was potentially associated with better impact on cardiovascular death in diabetes patients although it did not reach the significant differences as illustrated in Supplemental Fig. 4. In terms of hospitalization for heart failure, the incidence of heart failure hospitalization was observed in 9.2% in the SGLT2i group and 8.5% of non-SGLT2i group in unweighted cohorts. However, after adjustment, the rates were 8.9% in the SGLT2i group and 9.1% in the non-SGLT2i group. As illustrated in Supplemental Fig. 5, the correlation between treating SGLT2i and heart failure hospitalization was no significant differences in the aged population (Supplemental Table 3).

The incidence of worsening renal function between the cohorts with/without treating with SGLT2i

Renal function is an important issue in diabetes patients and SGLT2i was documented good impact in avoiding progression in renal function [18]. In this aged diabetes patients with majority have chronic kidney disease, worsening renal function occurred in 12.9% of the SGLT2i group compared to 16.2% of non-SGLT2i group in the unweighted cohort (ASMD = 0.094). After IPTW adjustment, the rates were 12.9% and 16.3%, respectively (ASMD = 0.099), showing a significant renal benefit in SGLT2i users as it illustrated in Fig. 2. (Supplemental Table 3)

Discussion

This study evaluated the long-term impact of SGLT2i on clinical outcomes in aged diabetic patients, specifically those aged 75 years and older, over a 5-year follow-up period. After IPTW adjustment, SGLT2i use was associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality, highlighting the mortality benefit of SGLT2i in this high-risk population. Although cardiovascular mortality was lower in the SGLT2i group, the difference did not reach statistical significance. Hospitalization for heart failure showed minimal differences between groups, with adjusted rates of 8.9% for SGLT2i users and 9.1% for non-SGLT2i users. Notably, SGLT2i therapy was associated with a reduced risk of renal function decline, underscoring its potential for renal protection. The incidence of urinary tract infections was similar in both groups, with no significant difference observed. When stratified by age, the benefit in all-cause mortality and preserved renal function diminished with increasing age, particularly among patients aged over 85 years, suggesting that the effectiveness of SGLT2i may be less pronounced in the oldest age groups. One potential explanation for the attenuated associations observed in patients > 85 years is age-related loss of nephron mass and reduced tubular reserve, which may blunt the natriuretic and hemodynamic responses that contribute to the cardio-renal benefits of SGLT2i. In addition, very old adults frequently have diminished baroreflex sensitivity, impaired renal autoregulation, and reduced extracellular volume buffering capacity, which may limit the translational impact of SGLT2i-related osmotic diuresis in this subgroup. These pathophysiological changes may therefore partially explain why the effect estimates became smaller and crossed unity in the > 85-year group.

In this study, we observed ~ 50% reduction in all-cause mortality (aHR 0.51) is substantial and warrants a highly cautious interpretation, particularly when compared to the ~ 30% reductions reported in pivotal RCTs such as EMPA-REG OUTCOME, CANVAS, and DECLARE-TIMI 58. The fact that this marked reduction in all-cause mortality occurred without a corresponding statistically significant decrease in CV death or HF hospitalization further suggests that established cardioprotective mechanisms may not fully explain the signal in aged patients. Unfortunately, our database does not provide reliable granular data on non-CV causes of death, which limits our ability to pinpoint the specific drivers of this survival benefit.

Comparison to previous studies on aged populations

Previous research primarily focuses on individuals aged 65 years and older, with less emphasis on patients over 75 years, a demographic characterized by higher comorbidity and polypharmacy [1, 19]. For instance, in the DECLARE-TIMI 58 trial, which included over 17,000 patients, SGLT2i was shown to significantly reduce the risk of cardiovascular death or hospitalization for heart failure by 17%, but the mean age of participants was 64 years, with limited data available for those above 75 years (4). Similarly, the EMPA-REG OUTCOME trial demonstrated a 38% reduction in cardiovascular mortality in patients treated with empagliflozin, yet the mean age was 63 years [3]. In contrast, our study specifically targeted those over 75 years, a group underrepresented in clinical trials due to frailty and increased adverse event risk [3–11]. By focusing on this older subset, our study refines the understanding of SGLT2i’s safety and efficacy for patients beyond 75 years, emphasizing age-specific treatment considerations in this high-risk group. Additionally, our study’s subgroup analysis provided insights into age stratification that were not explored in previous studies, revealing a decline in benefit for those over 85 years. In our cohort, 60.6% were younger than 85 years and 39.4% were 85 years or older. Although the share of patients aged 85 and over is substantial, analyses restricted to this very old group can still be limited by low event counts, shorter follow-up, and greater clinical differences such as frailty, functional decline, and poor nutrition, which are not recorded in claims or registry data. Therefore, any weakening of the effect estimate or loss of statistical significance in those over 85 may reflect limited power and remaining confounding rather than a true lack of benefit.

Mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and heart failure hospitalization

Several prior studies have demonstrated SGLT2i’s efficacy in reducing all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with DM, with substantial benefits observed in both younger and aged populations [20–23]. For example, a meta-analysis by Aldafas et al. found that SGLT2i use in aged patients significantly reduced all-cause mortality by 19%, particularly highlighting benefits in patients aged between 65 and 80 years [24]. Additionally, the CANVAS program, which included a significant proportion of older patients, demonstrated a 14% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) among SGLT2i users compared to placebo [11]. Our findings align with these studies in terms of reduced all-cause mortality but differ as we observed no significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality or heart failure hospitalization [20–24]. The lack of statistical significance for CV death and HF hospitalization may be attributed to a Type II error. In our cohort, the absolute number of events for these specific outcomes was relatively low (e.g., only 16 CV death events in the SGLT2i group). Consequently, our study may have been underpowered to detect a statistically significant difference for these specific CV endpoints, despite the observed trend toward benefit. In contrast, all-cause mortality, which encompasses a broader range of events, provided sufficient statistical power to reach significance. In fact, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and do not supersede the results of established RCTs.

SGLT2i are traditionally recognized for their potent cardioprotective effects. The observation of a survival benefit without a corresponding significant CV signal is indeed “puzzling” from a purely mechanistic standpoint. This discrepancy stems from two main factors: (1) Competing Risks in the Geriatric Population: In very aged populations, mortality is often driven by a complex interplay of non-CV causes (e.g., sepsis, respiratory failure, or renal complications). The robust renal protection observed in our study may have contributed to overall survival, even if the CV-specific benefit did not reach significance. (2) Residual Confounding (Healthy User Bias), The magnitude of the 50% mortality reduction likely reflects some degree of “healthy user bias” that IPTW could not fully eliminate. This bias may disproportionately affect all-cause mortality compared to more specific clinical endpoints like HF hospitalization.

Renal outcomes in comparison with literature

SGLT2i are well-documented for their renoprotective effects in T2DM patients, with evidence showing reduced progression of renal impairment across different age groups [9, 10, 25–27]. Our study corroborates these findings, demonstrating a lower incidence of renal function decline in the SGLT2i group compared to the non-SGLT2i group. Unlike some studies that showed universal renoprotective effects across age groups [10, 25, 26], our age-stratified analysis indicated that this benefit may decrease with advancing age, particularly in those over 85. One possible reason is the small number of patients in the advanced age group, which could be a contributing factor to this observation, potentially limiting statistical power and influencing the observed efficacy. Additionally, this finding could be attributed to age-associated changes in kidney function, comorbidities, or a higher prevalence of polypharmacy in the oldest patients, which may limit the therapeutic potential of SGLT2i in preserving renal function (24). Nevertheless, it is presumed that SGLT2i remains a suitable treatment option in this advanced aged population (aged > 75), even though the potential benefits may be reduced in the very old population.

Urinary Tract Infection (UTI) incidence

The risk of UTI with SGLT2i use has been a noted concern, especially in aged diabetic patients [28, 29]. Prior studies, such as those by Takeuchi et al., have reported a slightly increased risk of UTI with SGLT2i use, showing an approximately 1.4-fold increased risk compared to non-users, with an incidence of about 7.2% in SGLT2i users versus 5.5% in non-users [30]. In contrast, our study found no significant difference in UTI rates between the SGLT2i and non-SGLT2i groups after IPTW adjustment, with an incidence of 5.7% in the SGLT2i group compared to 6.3% in the non-SGLT2i group. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the study design and population. For instance, Takeuchi et al. included a broader range of patients and captured both severe and mild cases of UTIs [30], while our study primarily defined UTI by hospitalization records, which may have excluded less severe or clinic-managed cases. Additionally, the lower incidence in our study might reflect differences in healthcare practices or patient characteristics, such as more rigorous preventive measures for UTIs among our study cohort. As a result, while our findings suggest no major increase in UTI risk, it is reasonable to conclude that symptomatic UTI is at least not a significant concern in this population when treating with SGLT2i, despite the differences in study design. Further research, including outpatient data collection, may provide a more comprehensive picture of UTI incidence among aged SGLT2i users.

Clinical implications

Our study provides compelling evidence supporting the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in aged patients with diabetes, particularly those aged 75 to 85 years, demonstrating significant benefits in reducing all-cause mortality and preserving renal function. However, in patients older than 85 years, these benefits appear to attenuate, as shown in subgroup analyses suggesting reduced efficacy in mitigating cardiovascular events and renal deterioration. This diminished effect may be attributed to age-related factors such as frailty, a higher burden of comorbidities, and polypharmacy. Given these considerations, clinicians should carefully weigh the potential risks and benefits when prescribing SGLT2 inhibitors to very aged patients. Overall, our findings add to the growing body of evidence supporting the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in older populations and highlight the importance of age-stratified data to inform individualized clinical decision-making.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the observational design poses an inherent risk of selection bias, although IPTW was used to adjust for confounding. Besides, it is important to note that this study does not constitute a prospective randomized controlled trial, therefore, the presence of channeling bias is presumed to exist between both groups. Second, our cohort data is derived from a single healthcare system, which may limit generalizability. Third, while we reported UTI hospitalizations, our approach may have excluded less severe UTI cases managed in outpatient settings, potentially underestimating the true incidence. Fourth, the retrospective cohort study does not examine or address the risks specific to the aged patients associated with SGLT2 inhibitors beyond cardiovascular events—namely, dehydration, hypotension, falls and fractures due to worsening frailty, subsequent deterioration in overall health. These outcomes are frequently not coded consistently or are managed in non-inpatient settings, and therefore are not robustly quantifiable in our EMR-derived cohort. A further limitation relates to data structure. BMI was not consistently available in our CGMH database, and therefore body composition could not be directly modeled. To partially compensate for this, the IPTW model incorporated age, glycemic status, cardiometabolic comorbidities, renal function strata, and relevant cardio-renal medications, which indirectly capture part of the frailty / sarcopenia spectrum. Nevertheless, the lack of anthropometric variables may lead to residual confounding, and this limitation should be carefully considered when interpreting the study findings. In addition, treatment adherence or discontinuation will effect to real benefits of SGLT2i. Furthermore, diabetes duration as a potential confounder for renal and mortality outcomes. In our study, direct data on the exact date of diabetes diagnosis was not available for all patients, which is a common limitation in electronic health record database studies where patients may have been diagnosed before entering the system. Therefore, diabetes duration was not explicitly included as a covariate in the IPTW model.

Besides, our study show a higher all-cause mortality reduction than previous RCTs. The magnitude of the 50% mortality reduction likely reflects some degree of “healthy user bias” that IPTW could not fully eliminate. This bias may disproportionately affect all-cause mortality compared to more specific clinical endpoints like HF hospitalization. Furthermore, our database does not provide reliable granular data on non-CV causes of death, which limits our ability to pinpoint the specific drivers of this survival benefit. Lastly, the relatively short follow-up period for some patients may limit the capture of long-term outcomes and adverse effects. Future studies with extended follow-up and broader data sources could help address these limitations.

Conclusion

Our study reinforces the cardiovascular and renal benefits of SGLT2i in aged patients aged 75 and above with DM, while also highlighting potential age-related variations in efficacy, particularly among those over 85 years. These findings support the integration of SGLT2i into clinical practice for aged DM patients, with careful monitoring to mitigate risks associated with frailty, polypharmacy, and advanced age. Future studies should explore longer follow-up periods and include diverse populations to validate these results and further inform the optimal use of SGLT2i in aged patients.
