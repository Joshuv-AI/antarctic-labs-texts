Protamine Utilization and Clinical Outcomes for Coronary Artery Perforation in Chronic Total Occlusion Percutaneous Coronary Intervention Procedures
Authors: Akash Kataruka, Kanwar Parhar, David Daniels, Charles Maynard, Kathleen E Kearney, Ahmed Mahmoud, Jacob A Doll, James M McCabe, William L Lombardi, Ravi S Hira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited evidence exists for the management of coronary artery perforation. We evaluated temporal trends and clinical outcomes associated with protamine use following coronary artery perforation in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for chronic total occlusions (CTOs).MethodsA single-center retrospective analysis was performed on coronary perforations in patients undergoing CTO PCI procedures between September 10, 2014, and December 31, 2018, encompassing a change in institutional practice regarding use of protamine at case conclusion. The primary outcome was a composite of in-hospital mortality and cardiac tamponade. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to assess the association between protamine use and clinical outcomes.ResultsIn total, 1454 patients undergoing CTO PCI, coronary perforation occurred in 160 (11.0%), of which 68 (42.5%) received protamine. Ellis perforation classification was similar in the protamine and non-protamine groups (P = .148). The primary outcome of in-hospital mortality or cardiac tamponade occurred less frequently in the protamine group compared with the nonprotamine group (11.8% vs 40.2%; P < .01). In-hospital mortality (1.5% vs 14.1%; P = .01) and cardiac tamponade (10.3% vs 26.1%; P = .02) occurred less frequently in the protamine group. In a multivariate logistic regression, protamine was independently associated with an improved primary outcome (OR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.10-0.65; P < .01).ConclusionsIn this retrospective analysis, use of protamine in patients with coronary artery perforation complicating CTO PCI was associated with a reduction in death and cardiac tamponade without evidence of increased stent thrombosis. Additional studies are warranted to establish optimal strategies for protamine use.Keywords: chronic total occlusion, clinical outcomes, percutaneous coronary intervention, protamine

Introduction

Coronary artery perforation in chronic total occlusion (CTO) percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) procedures occurs more frequently than non-CTO PCI and portends increased short-term and long-term adverse outcomes.1 In an analysis of 1000 consecutive CTO PCI from the OPEN-CTO registry, angiographic perforation occurred in 8.9% of cases, of which 48.3% were clinically relevant to require treatment.2,3 Occurrence of a perforation during CTO PCI has been associated with increased occurrence of death (7.1% vs 0.4%), tamponade requiring pericardiocentesis (14.1% vs 0.4%) and emergent surgery (1.2% vs 0.1%).4 In addition to poor clinical outcomes, complications increase financial burden on the health care system with longer length of stay and increased in-hospital costs.5 Therefore, management of perforations is of significant importance for clinical outcomes and health care utilization.

Common approaches to manage coronary perforations include clinical observation, prolonged balloon inflation, fat or coil embolization, thrombin injection, and covered stents.6 Reversal of anticoagulation by unfractionated heparin with intravenous protamine can be used to facilitate hemostasis, but must be weighed against the potential increased risk of adverse events, including acute stent thrombosis and protamine reaction. Expert opinion supports the use of protamine to treat coronary artery perforations, but limited data exists to support this strategy.6, 7, 8 The purpose of this study was to investigate the safety profile and clinical outcomes for patients treated with protamine after coronary perforations during CTO PCI. A single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted, which compared patients who did and did not receive protamine after CTO PCI procedures complicated by coronary perforation. We hypothesized that protamine utilization would be associated with lower rates of tamponade, in-hospital mortality without an accompanying increase in acute stent thrombosis.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

A retrospective analysis was conducted on coronary perforations in all CTO PCI procedures performed at the University of Washington Medical Center, Seattle, WA, between September 10, 2014 and December 31, 2018. Perforations were identified from the Cardiac Care Outcomes Assessment Program, a statewide registry which captures clinical and procedural data on all PCIs performed at nonfederal hospitals in Washington state.9

All cases were linked to the institutional pharmacy data to identify protamine administration. All angiograms were reviewed to determine Ellis classification by the senior author (R.S.H.) who has 10 years of interventional cardiology and CTO PCI experience and was blinded to the protamine status and outcome.10,11 Patient demographic characteristics and clinical data were obtained from the Cardiac Care Outcomes Assessment Program registry.

Perforation management strategy and use of protamine

Use of protamine in coronary artery perforations was incorporated into an institutional perforation management strategy starting January 1, 2016. This protocol change was encouraged but not mandated. The decision regarding timing and use during or after the procedure was at the discretion of the primary operator. Uptake increased gradually over the study period as operator experience and confidence grew.

All patients were on dual antiplatelet therapy (with aspirin and P2Y12 inhibitor) before the procedure and protamine administration occurred only after removal of all intracoronary equipment. Heparin was the only anticoagulant used for all CTO PCI procedures at our institution consistent with standard practice for CTO PCI.12 Protamine was dosed at 25 mg increments for a total of 50 mg to achieve activated clotting time of <180 seconds. Balloon tamponade was performed as the initial management strategy in all cases of coronary artery perforation with Ellis grade II or higher. If there was active free-flowing extravasation, the option of placing a covered stent was left to the discretion of the individual operator. If this led to complete control of the perforation, protamine use was deferred at the discretion of the primary operator in cases where immediate reversal was considered to pose an unacceptable risk of acute stent thrombosis. Routine protamine administration was not mandated throughout the study period and interventional cardiology operators did not change during this period. Protamine administration was confirmed by cross-referencing the institutional pharmacy database and the procedural record by the first author to ensure accuracy.

Outcomes

The primary outcome for the study was a composite of in-hospital death or cardiac tamponade between patients who received protamine and those who did not (nonprotamine group). If patients had competing outcomes of cardiac tamponade that resulted in death, they were only counted once with the adverse outcome of death. Primary end points were defined according to the NCDR CathPCI, version 4.4, data collection form.13

Secondary outcomes included acute stent thrombosis, postprocedural MI, and clinically relevant protamine reactions, including hypotension and anaphylaxis (rash, urticaria, and bronchospasm). Secondary outcomes were obtained from an individual chart review. Acute stent thrombosis was defined as angiographically confirmed thrombotic occlusion in a previously stented segment during the index hospitalization.

Statistical analysis

Baseline clinical and demographic variables were compared between the groups by using the Student t test and Wilcoxon tests for continuous variables and χ2 and Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. The Mann–Kendall test was performed for temporal trends in protamine utilization and clinical outcomes. Linear trends were also evaluated for annual CTO PCI volume and annual deaths among CTO PCI procedures for the full calendar years between 2015 and 2018. A multivariable logistic regression was performed for the primary outcome adjusted for previous coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), previous cerebrovascular accident (CVA), stent placement at the index procedure, and Ellis classification of the coronary perforation. Previous CABG was selected due to higher incidence of coronary perforations during PCI and worse outcomes.14 Previous CVA was selected as covariate due to imbalance between protamine and nonprotamine groups; stent placement and Ellis classification were selected to adjust for potential stent thrombosis and angiographic severity of the perforation. Two additional sensitivity analysis were performed for in-hospital mortality during the period between 2016 and 2018 to account for other possible changes in equipment and technique and risk adjustment using the J-CTO score and NCDR CathPCI mortality risk score.15,16 The study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Washington. Statistical analysis was conducted with R version 3.61 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Of 1454 patients undergoing CTO PCI procedures between September 10, 2014, and December 31, 2018, 160 patients (11.0%) with coronary artery perforation were included in our cohort. The average age was 69.8 ± 12.2 years and left ventricular ejection fraction was 47.2% ± 14.2% in the study cohort. Of these 160 patients, protamine was administered in 68 (42.5%).

Baseline demographic variables were balanced between the protamine and nonprotamine groups except for prior CABG (58.8% vs 39.1%; P = .014) and prior CVA (17.6% vs 5.4%; P = .013), which were higher in the protamine group than those the nonprotamine group (Table 1). No patients required emergent CABG for management of the perforation. Indicators of procedural complexity including J-CTO score, adjuvant device use (atherectomy and laser), and multivessel coronary artery disease were not statistically different between the 2 groups (Table 2).

There was no statistical difference in the proportion of patients across different Ellis perforation classification between both groups (P = .15). The placement of a drug-eluting stent (60.3% vs 68.5%; P = .32) as well as use of covered stents were similar between the protamine and nonprotamine groups (11.7% vs 10.8%; P = .99) (Table 2).

The primary outcome of in-hospital mortality or cardiac tamponade occurred less frequently in the protamine group than that in the nonprotamine group (11.8% vs 40.2%; P < .0001). Individually, in-hospital mortality (1.5% vs 14.1%; P = .005) and cardiac tamponade (10.3% vs 26.1%; P = .012) also occurred less frequently in the protamine group (Table 3). For secondary outcomes, no cases of acute stent thrombosis were observed in either group and no cases of severe protamine reaction were observed in the protamine group. When evaluating trends over the study period, use of protamine in patients with coronary perforations in CTO PCI increased from 0% in 2014 to 77.5% in 2018 (P = .04). In-hospital death or tamponade decreased from 33.3% to 8.2% (P = .09), in-hospital death decreased from 22.2% to 2.0% (P = .03), and tamponade decreased from 33.3% to 6.1% (P = .09) over the same period (Figure 1). In a sensitivity analysis looking only at the period between 2016 and 2018 when protamine management strategy was adopted, protamine was still associated with lower in-hospital mortality (OR, 0.11; 95% CI, 0.01-0.66; P = .04). Further sensitivity analysis with risk adjustment using J-CTO score and NCDR CathPCI mortality risk score showed that the primary composite outcome (OR, 0.24; 95% CI, 0.12-0.70; P < .001), in-hospital mortality (OR, 0.12; 95% CI, 0.06-0.66; P < .001), and cardiac tamponade (OR, 0.32; 95% CI, 0.12-0.78; P < .001) were lower in the protamine group.

Discussion

In our single-center retrospective analysis of consecutive coronary perforations in CTO PCI procedures, we demonstrated 2 important findings. First, protamine administration was associated with lower risk of in-hospital mortality and cardiac tamponade. Second, protamine administration increased through the study period without any events of acute stent thrombosis or protamine reaction. Importantly, these observations derive from a cohort of elective CTO PCI procedures in which every patient had been pretreated with dual antiplatelet therapy. The marked increase in protamine utilization from 0% in 2014-2015 to 77.5% by 2018 was directly from a deliberate policy change, yielding 2 chronologically distinct cohorts that serve as natural before-and-after comparators. To our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating outcomes and safety profile of protamine use after coronary perforations in patients undergoing CTO PCI.

Initially, reversal of heparin anticoagulation with protamine following PCI had been studied with transfemoral access, primarily as a means to reduce access site complications and facilitate vascular sheath removal.17 In one of the earliest reports of such a strategy, Colombo et al18 described 30 patients treated with protamine, none of whom developed stent thrombosis at 1 month. Subsequent studies have established a reasonable safety profile for protamine as a bleeding mitigation strategy for femoral access. In a large study following bare metal stent implantation involving 5322 patients, of which 4193 patients received protamine, occurrence of acute stent thrombosis was unchanged with the administration of protamine at 0.1%.19 In a meta-analysis of 6739 patients undergoing elective PCI, protamine administration following PCI was associated with a significant reduction in major bleeding without any increase in mortality or myocardial infarction.20 Similar safety and efficacy of protamine has also been demonstrated in the context of acute myocardial infarction.21 However, data specifically evaluating protamine use for coronary artery perforation remain scarce, particularly in the CTO PCI population.

Recent studies have highlighted the benefits of protamine in reducing postprocedural complications including lower rate of adverse outcomes (in-hospital death, myocardial infarction, stent thrombosis, stroke/systemic embolism, bleeding requiring blood transfusion, and vascular access complications) and significantly shorter hospital length of stay between patients who were and were not given protamine after complex PCI.22 Similar to our findings, they saw no incidence of stent thrombosis in either group. Kubota et al23 also showed no increased risk of ischemia-driven target-vessel revascularization or stent thrombosis with protamine immediately after elective PCI.23 Lastly, in a systematic review evaluating 11 studies, protamine use was associated with a decreased incidence of major bleeding complications but not with stent thrombosis or mortality.24 These findings, in conjunction with our data, suggest that protamine can be utilized safely and effectively in select cases of coronary artery perforation without an increased risk of stent thrombosis.

However, caution must be exercised with protamine use due to concern for an adverse drug reaction. Protamine can exert several undesirable hemodynamic effects including transient hypotension, anaphylactic reaction, and pulmonary vasoconstriction.25,26 The incidence of transient hypotension has been reported between 1.8% and 12.4% and anaphylaxis from 0.19% to 0.69%.27 Operators must be cognizant of these potential drug reactions while managing an unstable patient with a coronary artery perforation.

Our study evaluated the safety and efficacy of protamine following coronary perforation in patients undergoing CTO PCI. It should be noted that the proportion of coronary artery perforation in CTO PCI procedures was higher in our study (11.0%) compared with existing registries, which may reflect differences in anatomical and procedural complexity.2,28, 29, 30 As a nationally recognized CTO PCI institution, the majority of the CTO cases were referrals from outside our system. Objective measures of CTO PCI procedural complexity such as the J-CTO score may underestimate complexity and risk of complications as reattempt of CTO PCI may not be captured accurately in the medical documentation and referring centers may not attempt CTO PCI due to inherent complexity. The large effect size and absence of adverse outcomes seen in our study must be interpreted with caution because it reflects a small sample size, and additional studies are warranted to further evaluate the benefits of protamine administration. Previous studies on the management of coronary artery perforation have described using protamine in select cases.29,31 However, clinical outcomes of systematic treatment in a large cohort of patients with coronary perforation have not been previously described.

Our findings must be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. First, although high volume for CTO PCI, our study is supported by data from a single-center and is observational in nature, a multicenter study will be required to further validate these findings. Second, coronary artery perforations were identified from registry data, and therefore, the proportion of Ellis class I and nonclinically significant perforations were likely underestimated. Third, protamine administration remained at the discretion of the primary operator, which may have influenced patient selection, and its effects cannot be completely distinguished from other institutional efforts to improve complication management. However, sensitivity analysis demonstrated protamine remained independently associated with in-hospital mortality in the later period. Furthermore, there was no change in CTO operators through the study or change in in-hospital mortality following CTO PCI. Fourth, while no adverse events were seen with protamine (stent thrombosis or severe protamine reaction), mild reactions may not have been captured in medical documentation. Fifth, heparin was the only anticoagulant used and results do not apply to alternative options for anticoagulation. Sixth, angiographic review of Ellis class and J-CTO score were performed solely by the senior author. However, the operator has 10 years of independent experience performing CTO PCI and was blinded to the treatment strategy and outcome. Seventh, the study was based on retrospective angiographic review, and thus, the mechanism of perforation and duration of balloon tamponade could not be reliably ascertained and, therefore, is not reported. Eighth, the J-CTO score may be underestimated due to inaccurate or incomplete medical documentation regarding a prior attempt at CTO PCI. Ninth, precise inciting event resulting in perforation during the procedure was not always evident so time to development of tamponade could not be reliably measured.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in a large single-center observational study, protamine administration following coronary perforation in patients undergoing CTO PCI was associated with reduced mortality and cardiac tamponade without any attendant acute stent thrombosis or severe protamine reaction. Findings are important for the management of an uncommon but potentially fatal procedural complication. Additional studies are needed to evaluate the safety, optimal strategy, and timing of protamine administration to improve outcomes following coronary artery perforation in CTO and non-CTO PCI cases.
