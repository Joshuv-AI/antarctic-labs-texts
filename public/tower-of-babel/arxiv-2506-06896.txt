Simulating discrete-time quantum walk with urn model
Surajit Saha

Abstract
Urn models have long been used to study stochastic processes, probability distributions, and reinforcement dynamics. Meanwhile, discrete time quantum walks (DTQW) serve as fundamental components in quantum computation and quantum information theory. This work explores a novel connection between an urn model and discrete-time quantum walks, focusing on how urn-based stochastic processes can provide insights into quantum state evolution and algorithmic behavior. Importantly, our model operates entirely with real-valued quantities, without relying on complex amplitudes of the quantum walk. Every stochastic rule is derived from urn-based processes, whose parameters are governed by the coin and shift mechanisms of the quantum walk, maintaining the structural dynamics of the QW. Through this interdisciplinary approach, we aim to bridge classical and quantum frameworks, offering new perspectives on both quantum algorithms and stochastic modeling. This work connects the urn model to the broad range of applications where DTQWs are successfully employed.



Exploring the dynamics of a physical system often involves identifying suitably engineered dynamics in a potentially very different realm that exhibits analogous behavior. This approach not only enhances our understanding of the physical system but also enriches the dynamics of that different realm, making both perspectives more insightful and beneficial. A classic example emerged in 2002, when Grover’s quantum search algorithm was shown to correspond to the behavior of coupled pendulums
grov1
. Recent work
gog
clarified this connection further. It showed that the dynamics of any classical system of harmonic oscillators can be equivalently understood as the dynamics of a corresponding quantum system of exponentially smaller size. Conversely, every quantum algorithm can be equivalently understood as the propagation of a classical wave in a system of coupled oscillators, albeit one that may involve action at a distance and significant classical resources, which make the latter process impractical. But the important point is that such an analogy led to the discovery of an efficient quantum algorithm to simulate the classical dynamics of coupled oscillators.

This duality underscores the importance of systematically studying how useful classical systems—such as coupled oscillators, which can themselves simulate a wide variety of classical dynamics—can reflect the behavior of quantum algorithms. While such classical simulations might incur substantial computational costs or non-local characteristics, they can lead to the development of novel quantum algorithms for simulating intricate classical systems, as well as quantum-inspired classical strategies. In this spirit, we employ discrete-time quantum walks (DTQW) and construct an urn model as the corresponding useful classical system, offering a fresh route to replicate quantum dynamics.

Quantum walks on graphs are one of the few established design strategies for quantum algorithms. Despite their simple description, quantum walks are extremely powerful, as they are universal for quantum computation
love
;
qwu
. Owing to their non-classical spreading dynamics, localization property, and potential algorithmic advantages, quantum walks have been extensively explored both theoretically and experimentally
Portugal2013
;
Kadian2021
;
Yuan2023
.

On the other hand, Urn models—built on the simple act of drawing and replacing colored balls—serve as a versatile framework for modeling both random and self‑reinforcing dynamics across domains as varied as population genetics, network growth, statistical physics, and machine learning
Godreche2002
;
Bellina2025
.

Replicating quantum phenomena like DTQWs on useful classical platforms is inherently challenging. This difficulty stems from the fact that DTQWs rely on quantum superposition, where evolving states interfere—constructively or destructively—based on their relative phases. As a result, they produce probability distributions that differ from those of classical random walks.

A line of research on this topic has approached this challenge by constructing classical random walks (CRWs)
tomoki
;
yama
whose space and time-dependent transition probabilities in each step are extracted from the complex amplitudes of the DTQW. This approach yields a non-homogeneous CRW whose probability distribution replicates that of the corresponding DTQW. Although effective in reproducing certain statistics, these methods necessarily break the spatial and temporal structure of the DTQW and demand access to quantum amplitudes at each step, an approach analogous to Bohmian mechanics
durr
, where the quantum wave function guides particle trajectories.

In contrast, alternative formulations of quantum mechanics—akin to the hydrodynamic approaches of Holland and Poirier
holl
;
poir
;
parl
;
shiff
;
sebens
;
wiseman
—eschew direct reliance on the wave function, instead constructing real-valued fields or potentials that reproduce quantum evolution without amplitude-based guidance. Inspired by this distinction, we propose an urn‑model framework for simulating DTQWs that never invokes complex amplitudes. All transition rules arise from real‑valued urn processes whose parameters depend only on the QW’s coin and shift structure. Crucially, our model mirrors the structural properties of the original quantum walk system. When the QW has uniform coin and shift operations, our urn model remains homogeneous in space and time, employing identical urn parameters at each node and time step. If QW coin or shift operations vary across space or time, the corresponding urn model adapts its parameters in the same pattern, preserving the structural variations of QW. This yields two main advantages. First, preserving the structural variations of the QW simplifies both the analysis and the simulation, while allowing our method to be naturally extended to higher-dimensional graphs. Second, by avoiding amplitude-based guidance, the urn model brings into focus a fundamental probabilistic mechanism underlying quantum walk spreading and interference, insights that may carry foundational significance.

An urn is a container that holds a collection of objects, usually called balls. The contents of the urn can be altered by randomly removing or adding balls according to the rules given. The behavior of such a system is probabilistic in nature. In the literature, it has been noted earlier that urn models has the capability to describe various quantum logic

In this work, we have developed an urn model to simulate the dynamics of 1D discrete-time quantum walks with a generalized coin.
The urn model can be systematically generalized to higher-dimensional graphs. In the two-dimensional case, for example, the walker requires a
4\times 4
coin operation acting on a two-qubit state, representing four possible directions: up, down, left, and right. Consequently, the urn model must be extended to include a transformation operation
\mathbb{T}
that acts between four distinct urns, with each site in the two-dimensional lattice hosting four urns. By carefully extending these ideas, the urn model can be generalized to arbitrary higher-dimensional graphs or even complex networks, where each node may be associated with multiple urns corresponding to the available directions or connections. So, this approach offers a versatile and intuitive framework for simulating a broad range of quantum dynamical systems. Our future work will focus on formalizing this methodology and exploring its application to increasingly complex topologies.

The strength of the present model lies in its simplicity and the valuable insights it offers.
Successful extensions of this approach could enable the design of DTQW-based algorithms through purely urn-based reasoning and may open new pathways for the efficient quantum simulation of complex classical systems.

\rho_{0}=r_{0}^{2}
. Consequently, the urn occupation number
N_{0}
is chosen randomly as either

N_{0}=\lfloor r_{0}^{2}N\rfloor\quad\text{or}\quad N_{0}=\lceil r_{0}^{2}N\rceil

with equal probability
1/2
. Given that the total number of balls is
N
, the complement is

N_{1}=N-N_{0}.

We can always write:

r_{0}^{2}N=\lfloor r_{0}^{2}N\rfloor+\epsilon=\lceil r_{0}^{2}N\rceil-\epsilon^{\prime},

where
0\leq\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}<1
. Moreover, if
r_{0}^{2}N\notin\mathbb{Z}
, then
\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime}=1
. This implies:

N_{0}=r_{0}^{2}N+O(1),\quad N_{1}=r_{1}^{2}N+O(1).

Multiplying gives:

N_{0}N_{1}=(r_{0}^{2}N+O(1))(r_{1}^{2}N+O(1))=r_{0}^{2}r_{1}^{2}N^{2}+O(N).

We write
O(f(N))
to indicate any quantity bounded in absolute value by
Cf(N)
for some constant
C>0
.

Let us define:

A:=r_{0}^{2}r_{1}^{2}N^{2},\quad B:=O(N),

so that:

\sqrt{N_{0}N_{1}}=\sqrt{A+B}.

Now factor out the leading-order part:

\sqrt{A+B}=\sqrt{A}\cdot\sqrt{1+\frac{B}{A}}.

Since
B=O(N)
and
A=O(N^{2})
, we have:

\frac{B}{A}=O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

Using a Taylor expansion:

\sqrt{1+\frac{B}{A}}=1+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{B}{A}+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right),

which gives:

\sqrt{A+B}=\sqrt{A}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\right)=r_{0}r_{1}N+O(1).

From the Hadamard-like transformation, we know:

{r_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(r_{0}^{2}+r_{1}^{2}+2r_{0}r_{1}\cos(\phi_{0}-\phi_{1})\right),

so multiplying by
N
:

{r_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}N=\frac{1}{2}\left(r_{0}^{2}N+r_{1}^{2}N+2r_{0}r_{1}N\cos(\phi_{0}-\phi_{1})\right).

Substituting
N_{0}=r_{0}^{2}N+O(1)
,
N_{1}=r_{1}^{2}N+O(1)
, and
\sqrt{N_{0}N_{1}}=r_{0}r_{1}N+O(1)
, we obtain:

{r_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}N=\frac{1}{2}\left(N_{0}+N_{1}+2\sqrt{N_{0}N_{1}}\cos(\phi_{0}-\phi_{1})\right)+O(1).

(25)

If we define:

\tilde{N}_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(N_{0}+N_{1}+2\sqrt{N_{0}N_{1}}\cos(\phi_{0}-\phi_{1})\right),

then:

{r_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}N=\tilde{N}_{0}+O(1).

Hence, one can equivalently write:

\displaystyle{r_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}N=\lfloor\tilde{N_{0}}\rfloor+O(1)

(26)

\displaystyle{r_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}N=\lceil\tilde{N_{0}}\rceil+O(1)

(27)

\displaystyle{r_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}N=\frac{\lfloor\tilde{N_{0}}\rfloor+\lceil\tilde{N_{0}}\rceil}{2}+O(1)

(28)

Thus, in the asymptotic limit:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\lfloor\tilde{N}_{0}\rfloor+\lceil\tilde{N}_{0}\rceil}{2N}={r_{0}^{\prime}}^{2}.

(29)

Using a similar approach, one can show that in the limit
N\to\infty
,
\eta_{i}^{\prime}(N_{0},N_{1},\phi_{0},\phi_{1})
of Eq.
Simulating discrete-time quantum walk with urn model
approaches to
\phi_{i}^{\prime}
. Also one can prove using
\tilde{N_{0}}
and
\eta_{i}^{\prime}
of Eq.
Simulating discrete-time quantum walk with urn model
and
Simulating discrete-time quantum walk with urn model
respectively that the probability of obtaining 0 and 1 in the urn model
\mathbb{M}
process approaches corresponding probabilities in Quantum version for generalised
SU(2)
coin operation.
