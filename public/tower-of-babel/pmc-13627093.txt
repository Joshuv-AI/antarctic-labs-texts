Non-destructive identification of closely related japonica rice seeds using hyperspectral imaging combined with HHO-optimized XGBoost
Authors: Chunxu Jiang, Shixue Gu, Gang Liu, Yang Liu, Rongjie Huang, Shaozhong Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genetically closely-related japonica rice seed varieties are difficult to distinguish using conventional identification techniques. These traditional methods generally require time-consuming operations, complex experimental workflows, and seed destruction for sample testing. Hyperspectral imaging offers a feasible non-destructive way to screen crop germplasm resources. Nevertheless, raw hyperspectral signals are easily disturbed by instrument noise and external environmental interference; the dataset also suffers high dimensionality and redundant spectral features, which hinders model performance. Standard XGBoost models rely on manual hyperparameter adjustment, which frequently falls into local optimal solutions. Besides, poor model interpretability makes it hard to reduce classification errors between genetically similar rice materials. To address these practical problems, this work constructed an improved XGBoost framework combined with multi-step spectral preprocessing and Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO). SHAP analysis was further introduced to reveal classification rules of critical spectral bands and realize accurate, non-destructive identification of rice seeds. Five pixel statistical calculation strategies were compared, and mean spectral curves were chosen as model input data. Savitzky-Golay smoothing and Min-Max normalization were implemented to suppress spectral noise. The Successive Projections Algorithm (SPA) was then adopted to pick out 22 sensitive bands with low multicollinearity. This step preserved effective discriminative information while lowering feature dimensions. SHAP values were applied to quantify feature contributions from both global importance and single-sample perspectives, alleviating the black-box limitation of machine-learning classifiers. Nine different swarm intelligence algorithms were tested for automatic hyperparameter searching of XGBoost. The final HHO-XGBoost approach achieved 94.72% overall accuracy and a Kappa coefficient of 0.9389. Per-class F1-score results demonstrated that the established method could obviously decrease misclassification among nine japonica rice varieties. This technical workflow integrates spectral preprocessing, feature screening, interpretive analysis and intelligent parameter optimization. It can serve as a practical reference for non-destructive identification of crop germplasm with close genetic relationships.

Introduction

Japonica rice is one of the vital food crops worldwide, and the varietal purity of its seeds directly governs field agronomic traits, grain yield and agricultural product quality (Meng et al., 2022). Closely related japonica rice germplasms usually share highly similar genetic backgrounds with trivial morphological distinctions, which greatly increases the risk of variety adulteration (Yang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2022). Therefore, rapid and precise identification of closely related japonica rice seeds acts as a critical prerequisite for seed quality control, germplasm resource conservation and the popularization of elite varieties. Traditional seed identification methods mainly consist of field phenotypic observation, biochemical detection and molecular biological assays. Field identification requires a long experimental cycle, and the final results are susceptible to environmental and artificial interferences. By contrast, physicochemical and molecular detection methods are destructive, accompanied by complicated operational procedures and high testing costs, which are not suitable for high-throughput rapid screening of bulk seed samples (Frischie et al., 2020). Accordingly, it is urgent to develop a non-destructive, efficient and convenient seed variety recognition technique to provide technical support for quality inspection in the seed industry.

In recent years, with the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence technology, deep learning methods have been widely applied to hyperspectral image classification due to their powerful automatic feature learning capability (Song et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024; Bi et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). Relying on multi-layer nonlinear architectures, deep learning models can hierarchically extract abstract deep features without manual feature engineering and achieve excellent classification performance with large-scale labeled datasets (Bao and Bambil, 2021). However, such strategies still exhibit inherent limitations in the non-destructive detection of crop germplasm, especially for the identification of closely related rice germplasms. First, deep learning models contain massive parameters and require substantial annotated samples for adequate training. In practical breeding scenarios, the collection and labeling of closely related japonica rice seed samples are labor-intensive and costly (Bayazit et al., 2025; Rahaman et al., 2025). Under limited sample conditions, deep learning models are prone to overfitting, resulting in degraded generalization performance and poor adaptability to small-scale germplasm identification tasks. Second, mainstream deep learning frameworks are typical black-box models (Jin et al., 2025). Although they can yield high classification accuracy, they cannot explicitly screen critical sensitive spectral bands or establish interpretable correlations between spectral responses and seed physicochemical properties, including moisture, protein, and starch contents. This deficiency restricts the mechanistic interpretation of confusing spectral characteristics among genetically similar varieties. Moreover, deep learning training heavily relies on high-performance GPU resources and involves time-consuming iterative optimization, making lightweight deployment difficult for conventional field detection devices and limiting its applicability in large-scale, rapid seed screening scenarios (Akbarpour et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2025). In addition, the complex hyperparameter systems of deep learning models lead to experience-dependent tuning procedures and low experimental reproducibility (Huang et al., 2022). In contrast, machine learning algorithms represented by XGBoost possess simpler structures, lower data and hardware requirements, and superior stability for limited-sample tasks. Combined with spectral preprocessing, sensitive band selection, and SHAP-based interpretability analysis, XGBoost can not only maintain reliable classification accuracy for closely related japonica rice seeds but also quantitatively reveal the contribution mechanism of spectral features. Further integrated with swarm intelligence optimization for global hyperparameter searching, this framework is more suitable for the precise identification of highly similar crop germplasm in practical seed detection scenarios.

Hyperspectral imaging technology can simultaneously acquire spatial image information and continuous spectral data of samples within the visible and near-infrared bands (Fu et al., 2025; Tejasree and Agilandeeswari, 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). It is capable of capturing subtle spectral variations derived from internal physicochemical components of seeds, including moisture, protein, and starch, thereby enabling non-destructive, rapid, and multi-index synchronous detection (Zhao et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023). Benefiting from these superiorities, hyperspectral imaging has been widely adopted in crop seed quality evaluation, variety classification, and pest and disease identification (Gui et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). In the hyperspectral identification of japonica rice seeds, raw hyperspectral data typically suffer from high dimensionality, excessive redundant information, and prominent noise interference induced by instrumental errors and illumination fluctuations (Ge et al., 2024; Diao et al., 2023). Direct modeling using original spectral data not only increases computational consumption but also easily causes overfitting and misclassification of closely related varieties, severely restricting the recognition accuracy and generalization performance of classification models (Bi et al., 2024). Although conventional spectral preprocessing and feature selection methods can optimize datasets to a certain extent, comparative investigations on the effectiveness of different denoising and feature screening strategies for the identification of closely related japonica rice germplasms remain insufficient (Diao et al., 2023). Furthermore, traditional machine learning models generally rely on manual grid search for hyperparameter tuning, which hardly obtains optimal parameter combinations and tends to fall into local optima, resulting in a performance ceiling in germplasm classification tasks.

XGBoost has exhibited promising application potential in crop hyperspectral classification tasks due to its outstanding fitting capability and robust anti-overfitting performance (Xiao et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2024). Nevertheless, its core hyperparameters, including learning rate, maximum tree depth, and the number of base learners, substantially determine the final classification performance, and conventional manual tuning suffers from strong subjectivity and low optimization efficiency. As an advanced swarm intelligence algorithm, Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) dynamically balances global exploration and local exploitation based on prey escape energy, effectively avoiding the premature convergence defect of traditional optimization approaches and enabling efficient searching for optimal hyperparameter combinations in high-dimensional parameter spaces (Shehab et al., 2022). The Successive Projections Algorithm (SPA) can screen sensitive spectral bands with minimal multicollinearity, compress feature dimensions while retaining critical discriminative information for variety identification (de Souza et al., 2025). Combined with SHAP interpretability analysis, it further reveals the internal mechanism between spectral features and classification results, addressing the inherent black-box limitation of machine learning models (Liang et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025).

Considering the existing research limitations mentioned above, nine genetically similar japonica rice seed varieties were chosen as experimental materials in the present work. Such rice germplasms share high genetic homology and cannot be easily separated through ordinary phenotypic observation, which makes them a typical difficult sample set for germplasm preservation and seed identification tasks. Hyperspectral imaging was utilized to acquire spectral information from individual seed samples. We compared several pixel-based statistical approaches to identify the most suitable spectral representation. Different preprocessing routines were also tested to select an effective noise-removal solution, and normalization was subsequently applied to remove magnitude discrepancies across different spectral bands. After evaluating several dimension-reduction and band-selection techniques, SPA was used to obtain key sensitive spectral features. SHAP analysis was then carried out to clarify feature importance and uncover the underlying classification mechanism. Using XGBoost as the base classification model, we assessed the hyperparameter-searching capability of nine swarm intelligence algorithms. An HHO-tuned XGBoost model was ultimately built to distinguish these hard-to-separate japonica rice seeds. Step-by-step experiments were designed to quantify how much performance gain could be achieved via spectral denoising, normalization, feature selection and intelligent parameter optimization. This work develops a high-accuracy hyperspectral identification approach for genetically close japonica rice germplasm. It expects to offer new technical ideas and methodological support for large-scale non-destructive variety discrimination and fast purity testing of rice seeds.

Materials and methods

Data set preparation

Rice samples

In this study, nine varieties of closely related japonica rice seeds with high genetic similarity were selected as experimental materials, which were provided by the Rice Research Institute of Jilin Academy of Agricultural Sciences. A total of 1800 seed samples were collected, with approximately 200 grains for each variety, to construct the hyperspectral dataset. As shown in Figure 1, the nine japonica rice varieties exhibit extremely similar morphological characteristics in grain shape, color, and size (Chen et al., 2025). The subtle phenotypic differences lead to low distinguishability, resulting in low efficiency and high error rates in manual identification. Meanwhile, such high visual similarity also imposes great difficulties on accurate classification using conventional computer vision methods. Accordingly, hyperspectral imaging is adopted to explore inherent spectral differences hidden in visually indistinguishable seeds, enabling efficient and non-destructive identification of closely related japonica rice germplasms.

Hyperspectral image acquisition and calibration

The configuration of the hyperspectral imaging system used in this study is illustrated in Figure 2. The experimental setup mainly consists of a hyperspectral camera, a uniform illumination module, an electric linear translation stage, a power supply, and a computer terminal equipped with SpectrononPro acquisition software. The hyperspectral sensor employed is Specim FX10 (Specim, Oulu, Finland). The spectrometer covers an effective spectral range of 392.38–1011.01 nm with 462 continuous spectral bands. No spectral bands were removed at both ends of the spectrum; all acquired bands were retained for subsequent analysis after radiometric calibration. Radiometric normalization was implemented using a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) white reference panel with a nominal reflectance of 99%. The slit width was set to 50 μm, providing a spatial resolution of 0.15 mm per pixel and a spectral resolution of 1.3 nm. Such high-resolution spectral acquisition capability enables the system to capture subtle spectral differences among closely related japonica rice seeds, providing high-quality original data for subsequent feature mining and classification modeling.

All seeds were arranged without overlap or adhesion. The template was placed on a black electric translation stage, and hyperspectral images were acquired through uniform scanning. Following the identical acquisition procedure, hyperspectral data of all nine rice varieties were sequentially collected to construct the complete original dataset.

Hyperspectral feature extraction

Spectral information extraction techniques for hyperspectral images have been widely employed in the non-destructive detection of food and agricultural products. In hyperspectral data analysis, spectral features are generally extracted based on the manually delineated Region of Interest (ROI). The two most commonly used extraction strategies are pixel-level spectrum extraction and mean spectrum extraction, which exhibit distinct advantages and applicability for different analytical scenarios.

Pixel-level spectrum extraction acquires spectral information from all pixels within the ROI, retaining detailed spectral variations across the sample surface. This method is suitable for quantitative analysis and visual characterization of the spatial distribution of chemical components in heterogeneous samples and shows unique superiority in local feature detection. However, for crop seed classification tasks, rice seeds exhibit inherent structural and compositional heterogeneity, resulting in inconsistent spectral responses among different surface pixels of a single seed. Such pixel-level spectral discrepancies do not correspond to varietal differences and may introduce redundant feature interference, thereby increasing the risk of model misclassification and limiting its applicability for closely related seed identification.

In contrast, mean spectrum extraction treats each seed as an independent analytical unit by averaging all pixel spectra within the individual seed ROI. This approach fully accommodates the natural structural and compositional heterogeneity of seed grains and can stably and accurately represent the overall spectral characteristics of a single seed. Unlike traditional point-based near-infrared spectrometers, which only collect spectral information from limited surface points and fail to reflect comprehensive seed physicochemical properties, hyperspectral imaging combined with mean spectrum extraction can effectively capture holistic seed spectral features and overcome the limitations of conventional spectral detection.

Furthermore, pixel-level spectral data are highly susceptible to sensor noise, environmental interference, and seed surface morphological variations, including shape curvature and surface flatness, resulting in low signal-to-noise ratio and substantial random spectral fluctuations. In comparison, mean spectrum calculation suppresses random noise through multi-pixel averaging, smooths spectral profiles, and significantly improves data stability and effectiveness, which is beneficial for enhancing the final classification performance. Therefore, mean spectrum extraction has been widely adopted in agricultural spectral detection and classification research.

In this study, the detailed workflow of seed mean spectrum extraction was implemented as follows. First, image segmentation was performed on the calibrated hyperspectral images (Figure 3a) to eliminate background interference and accurately define the ROI of each individual seed. Through comparative analysis of multi-band grayscale images, the band at 656.1300 nm presented the most significant contrast between seed targets and the black background, which was selected to construct a binary mask (Figures 3b, c). Based on preliminary experimental validation, an optimal segmentation threshold of 0.4 was determined. Pixels with gray values lower than 0.4 were defined as the background (assigned to 0), whereas pixels greater than or equal to 0.4 were defined as seed target regions (assigned to 1).

Model building

Savitzky-Golay smoothing algorithm

Although the seed mean spectral data obtained by multi-pixel averaging can suppress partial random noise, the raw spectral curves still contain subtle high-frequency noise and burr interference caused by instrument precision limitations, ambient illumination fluctuations, and tiny morphological differences on seed surfaces. Such noise may mask genuine spectral differences and degrade the classification accuracy of subsequent models. Therefore, the Savitzky–Golay (SG) polynomial smoothing algorithm was employed in this study to preprocess the original mean spectra and eliminate high-frequency noise.

The SG algorithm is a time-domain smoothing and denoising method based on local polynomial least-squares fitting. Unlike conventional mean filtering and median filtering, the SG algorithm can effectively remove high-frequency random noise while maximally preserving spectral peak magnitudes, peak widths, and overall spectral trends. It avoids spectral distortion and feature offset, making it highly suitable for retaining subtle spectral variations in hyperspectral data. Owing to these advantages, SG smoothing has been widely applied in the spectral preprocessing of agricultural products.

The core principle of the SG algorithm is to perform high-order polynomial fitting on discrete spectral points within a fixed-size sliding window based on the least-squares criterion. The original spectral values are replaced by the optimal fitted values, thereby eliminating high-frequency noise while maintaining inherent spectral trends. Through comparative parameter experiments, the optimal SG parameters were determined as a window size of 5 and a second-order polynomial. A relatively small window size prevents over-smoothing and preserves subtle spectral features, while the second-order polynomial accurately adapts to the gentle variation characteristics of seed spectral curves, balancing noise suppression and feature integrity.

After SG preprocessing, the high-frequency burrs and random noise in the spectral curves of different japonica rice varieties were effectively eliminated. The continuity and smoothness of the spectral curves were significantly improved, and the subtle spectral differences among closely related varieties were further highlighted. The signal-to-noise ratio and data reliability were greatly enhanced, providing high-quality spectral datasets for subsequent feature selection and stable model construction.

Max-min normalization

Although the spectral data smoothed by the SG algorithm exhibit high stability, the reflectance values of different spectral bands present inconsistent numerical scales and overall spectral offsets caused by the physical properties of seed samples. These discrepancies may result in slow model convergence, imbalanced weight distribution, and degraded generalization performance during model training. To unify spectral dimensional scales and eliminate numerical differences across bands, Min-Max Normalization (MMN) was adopted for linear standardization of the denoised full-band spectral data. All spectral reflectance values were linearly mapped to the interval of [0, 1]. SPA feature selection algorithm

The original hyperspectral data employed in this study contain a total of 462 spectral bands, which suffer from high dimensionality, excessive redundant information, and severe multicollinearity among adjacent bands. Direct utilization of full-band spectral data for model training substantially increases computational complexity, reduces training convergence efficiency, and easily causes the curse of dimensionality, thereby inducing model overfitting and deteriorating generalization performance. To address these issues, the Successive Projections Algorithm (SPA) was adopted to screen optimal feature bands from the normalized high-dimensional spectral data, aiming to reduce data dimensionality, eliminate redundant variables, and enhance discriminative spectral features.

SPA is a forward iterative variable selection algorithm. Its core principle is to iteratively select feature bands with the minimum collinearity and lowest redundancy through vector projection analysis, so as to construct an optimal feature band combination. This algorithm can effectively eliminate multicollinearity interference among spectral bands and retain the most discriminative information using the fewest feature variables. Compared with other commonly used dimensionality reduction methods, such as principal component analysis and genetic algorithms, SPA selects physically interpretable spectral bands with superior targeted discrimination ability, making it highly applicable to spectral feature optimization and classification modeling of agricultural products. Finally, a set of representative, low-redundancy, and highly discriminative spectral bands were screened out. The dimensionality of spectral data was greatly reduced while the core varietal discrimination features of closely related japonica rice seeds were well preserved. The simplified input structure effectively improves the computational efficiency and recognition stability of the subsequent classification model, providing a compact and efficient feature dataset for the rapid and non-destructive identification of rice seeds.

XGBoost algorithm

To achieve high-precision automatic identification of nine closely related japonica rice seed varieties, an Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm was employed to construct the seed classification model. As an optimized ensemble learning algorithm developed based on Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), XGBoost builds a robust high-accuracy classifier by iteratively stacking and weighted fusing multiple weak decision trees. It exhibits prominent advantages in computational efficiency, generalization ability, and fault tolerance, making it highly suitable for hyperspectral classification tasks characterized by small sample sizes, high dimensionality, and subtle inter-class differences. Currently, XGBoost has been widely applied in the non-destructive identification and quality detection of agricultural products.

Different from conventional gradient boosting frameworks, XGBoost adopts a second-order Taylor expansion for loss function approximation to achieve more accurate gradient fitting of real errors. Meanwhile, regularization terms are integrated into the objective function to effectively alleviate overfitting. Compared with traditional machine learning models such as support vector machine and random forest, XGBoost presents superior stability and accuracy in fine feature discrimination and complex sample classification. Harris Hawks optimization

The performance of the conventional XGBoost model heavily relies on manually defined hyperparameters or grid search strategies. Such tuning approaches are highly subjective and tend to fall into local optimal solutions during the classification of closely related japonica rice seeds with subtle spectral differences, thereby limiting model generalization and restricting further improvements in classification accuracy. To overcome the drawbacks of manual parameter tuning and fully explore fine-grained spectral discrepancies among varieties, this study adopted Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) to achieve global adaptive hyperparameter optimization for XGBoost. An optimized HHO-XGBoost model was finally constructed to improve both stability and identification accuracy for closely related japonica rice seed recognition.

HHO is a novel bionic meta-heuristic optimization algorithm that simulates the cooperative hunting, tracking, and sudden attacking behaviors of Harris hawks in nature. The algorithm adaptively switches between global exploration and local exploitation through dynamic prey escape energy regulation. Compared with traditional optimization algorithms such as genetic algorithm and particle swarm optimization, HHO exhibits distinct advantages, including fewer adjustable parameters, faster convergence speed, stronger global searching capability, and effective avoidance of premature convergence, which make it highly suitable for hyperparameter optimization in high-dimensional spectral modeling tasks.

Model evaluation framework

Before implementing spectral preprocessing, feature screening and model optimization, raw hyperspectral data were divided via stratified random sampling at a 6:2:2 ratio into training, validation, and independent test sets. This stratified partitioning strategy ensured a consistent proportion of each japonica rice variety across all three datasets. To thoroughly eliminate cross-set information leakage, all data transformation and modeling fitting procedures were strictly limited to the training set. Specifically, the maximum and minimum spectral reflectance values for Min–Max normalization were exclusively calculated from training data and subsequently adopted to standardize validation and test samples. The Successive Projections Algorithm (SPA) for sensitive band selection was performed on the training set with 5-fold cross-validation, and the screened optimal spectral bands were directly extracted from validation and test datasets without secondary fitting. In addition, the Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) algorithm tuned XGBoost hyperparameters by taking the training set’s 5-fold cross-validation accuracy as the fitness function. Throughout the entire experimental optimization process, the validation set was only used for intermediate model performance comparison, while the independent test set was completely isolated and only utilized to obtain final unbiased classification evaluation metrics.

To quantitatively and objectively evaluate the identification performance of the proposed model for nine highly similar japonica rice seed varieties, a comprehensive multi-dimensional evaluation system was established to systematically assess model accuracy, stability, and generalization capability. To avoid evaluation bias caused by a single metric, multiple indicators, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, and Kappa coefficient, combined with a confusion matrix, were adopted for the multi-classification task. Considering the balanced sample distribution and the requirement of fair evaluation for highly similar germplasms, all metrics were calculated based on macro-average strategy (Trigka and Dritsas, 2025). This unbiased evaluation method equally quantifies the classification performance of each rice variety and eliminates evaluation distortion induced by sample size differences, thereby ensuring scientific and credible evaluation results.

As an intuitive visualization tool, the confusion matrix was employed to reveal the misclassification and omission distribution between true labels and predicted labels among nine japonica rice varieties. Four basic evaluation parameters were defined based on the confusion matrix: True Positive (TP) represents correctly predicted positive samples; True Negative (TN) denotes correctly discriminated negative samples; False Positive (FP) refers to negative samples incorrectly predicted as positive; and False Negative (FN) indicates positive samples misclassified as negative.

Experimental environment and parameter settings

All data preprocessing, feature screening, model training and hyperparameter tuning experiments in this study were implemented on a desktop computer equipped with an Intel Core Ultra 7 270K Plus processor and 32 GB dual-channel DDR5 RAM (16 GB × 2, 8000 MHz, C36). The stable computing performance of this hardware configuration fully supports high-dimensional spectral matrix operations and iterative optimization, ensuring reliable experimental efficiency and result repeatability. All experiments were conducted on the Windows 11 operating system using Python 3.9 in PyCharm 2024. Specifically, the numpy and pandas libraries were employed for spectral data processing and matrix calculation; scipy was used to perform SG smoothing and Min–Max normalization; scikit-learn undertook dataset partitioning, SPA feature selection, evaluation metric calculation and confusion matrix visualization; XGBoost was adopted to construct the multi-class classification model, while matplotlib and seaborn were utilized for generating all experimental visualization graphs. To guarantee stable and reproducible modeling results, all experimental parameters were finalized through repeated pre-experiments. For spectral denoising, SG smoothing was performed with a window size of 5 and second-order polynomial fitting, which eliminated random noise while retaining subtle spectral differences among rice varieties. The SPA algorithm selected optimal sensitive bands ranging from 5 to 40 dimensions based on five-fold cross-validation accuracy, effectively removing collinear redundant features from the original 462-dimensional spectral data. For XGBoost modeling, hyperparameters including learning rate (0.01–0.3), maximum tree depth (2–10) and base learner number (50–200) were tuned, and L2 regularization was enabled to alleviate model overfitting. Meanwhile, the HHO optimizer was configured with a population size of 30 and a maximum of 50 iterations. It took the macro-average accuracy of the validation set as the fitness function to balance global exploration and local exploitation, thereby searching for the optimal hyperparameter combination for the identification of genetically similar japonica rice seeds.

Spectral denoising

Figure 6 illustrates the identification performance of the XGBoost model adopting six preprocessing methods, namely raw spectrum, mean filtering, median filtering, Gaussian filtering, wavelet threshold denoising, and Savitzky–Golay smoothing. The raw spectral data without denoising achieved the poorest comprehensive performance with an accuracy of 76.39%, indicating that inherent spectral noise severely restricted the identification of different rice varieties.

Mean filtering slightly improved the model accuracy to 79.26% by reducing partial noise, yet it tended to blur detailed spectral features and only produced limited performance gains. Median filtering and Gaussian filtering achieved better noise suppression for impulse noise and Gaussian random noise, yielding accuracies of 80.53% and 80.95%, respectively. Wavelet threshold denoising realized effective separation of valid spectral signals and noise in the time–frequency domain, further increasing the accuracy to 81.47% and outperforming the above three filtering methods.

Savitzky–Golay smoothing contributed the optimal model performance across all evaluation metrics. The model accuracy reached 82.15%. The macro-average precision, macro-average recall, and macro-average F1-score were 81.73%, 81.46%, and 81.59%, respectively, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.7992. This method removes high-frequency noise based on sliding least-squares polynomial fitting while completely retaining spectral absorption characteristics and subtle spectral differences among japonica rice varieties. Therefore, SG smoothing was selected as the optimal spectral denoising preprocessing method in this study.

Discussion

To achieve accurate, non-destructive identification of japonica rice seeds with high genetic similarity, we built an integrated analytical framework based on hyperspectral imaging. This workflow combines multi-step spectral preprocessing, optimal feature selection, SHAP-based interpretive analysis, and swarm-intelligence-driven hyperparameter tuning. Through systematic comparisons among multiple algorithms, our final HHO-XGBoost model achieved high classification accuracy across nine closely-related japonica rice varieties. This framework mitigates well-known drawbacks of raw hyperspectral data, including noise contamination and severe feature redundancy. It also reduces the risk of local optimal solutions commonly encountered during manual hyperparameter adjustment. SHAP outputs further pinpointed key sensitive spectral bands and clarified how these features contribute to variety discrimination. These findings offer practical technical references for high-throughput, non-destructive screening of genetically similar crop germplasm.

Even so, the complete XGBoost-SG-MMN-SPA-HHO pipeline demands relatively high computational overhead during model training. Repeated SPA feature selection together with HHO-based hyperparameter searching extends training runtime when compared with hardware-accelerated lightweight CNN architectures. Despite higher computational cost, the compact feature subset generated by SPA creates favorable conditions for subsequent model interpretation. Beyond dimensionality reduction, our workflow delivers strong interpretability, which opens opportunities to infer physiological and biochemical mechanisms behind spectral responses for germplasm identification. Lightweight CNN models, by contrast, benefit from fast hardware-assisted training. Yet they generally need larger sample volumes and behave as black-box models; their internal decision logic is hard to unpack, which limits our ability to explore underlying spectral mechanisms. Reducing computation costs for swarm-intelligence optimization will be prioritized in our follow-up research.

While our model yields promising classification performance under controlled laboratory settings, this study still suffers from several quantifiable limitations.

First, constraints exist regarding sample size and stratified sampling design. We applied strict stratified random splitting following a 6:2:2 ratio, ensuring that each japonica rice variety maintains identical sample proportions within training, validation and independent test partitions. This approach guarantees fair macro-level performance evaluation. Each variety includes 200 seed samples in total. After stratified division, roughly 120 samples per class enter the training set, 40 go to the validation set, and another 40 are reserved for independent testing. For this nine-class classification task, only 40 independent test samples are available for each rice variety. Published chemometric and agricultural spectroscopy studies suggest that at least 80–100 samples per class are preferred to produce stable macro-F1 metrics with low variance. With merely 40 test samples for each class, classification metrics inevitably show certain random fluctuations. This represents a concrete quantitative constraint rather than a vague qualitative shortcoming.

Second, all spectral measurements were collected under stable, standardized laboratory conditions. Real-world field interference factors, such as variable illumination, ambient temperature and humidity, seed moisture fluctuation, and seed aging during long-term storage, were not incorporated into our dataset. Since samples come from a single data distribution without multi-scenario disturbance inputs, model robustness may decline, and classification accuracy could drop sharply when deployed for on-site seed detection.

Third, experimental germplasm covers only nine japonica rice accessions collected from one research institute in Jilin Province and harvested within a single year. Such limited sample coverage restricts our model to closed-set classification scenarios. It cannot handle unknown, unlabeled rice materials or support large-scale seed resource screening across multiple regions.

Fourth, only one-dimensional spectral reflectance signals were used for model construction. Spatial texture, grain morphology and color information embedded within hyperspectral images were not fused into modelling inputs. Missing spatial features may hinder further performance improvement for rice varieties with extremely subtle spectral differences.

Fifth, the proposed algorithm pipeline currently runs only for offline computer analysis. We have not completed model compression, embedded code transplantation or integration with portable detection hardware. These missing steps prevent direct deployment for real-time industrial seed purity inspection.

Future work will focus on dataset expansion, multi-source feature fusion, lightweight model optimization and practical engineering deployment.

To begin with, we will build multi-condition hyperspectral datasets that cover diverse production regions, harvest years and seed storage periods to enrich data diversity. More than 100 test samples for each variety will be collected to alleviate metric instability induced by insufficient test samples per category. Domain adaptation and transfer learning will be adopted to offset spectral shifts caused by environmental variation. Meanwhile, open-set recognition modules will be integrated, enabling both known-variety classification and discrimination of unknown germplasm.

Next, we will fuse spectral curves with hyperspectral spatial texture features to fully capture subtle phenotypic differences among genetically similar rice seeds. Model pruning and quantization will be performed on the HHO-XGBoost architecture to cut computational burden. The full analytical workflow will be packaged into graphical software compatible with portable field-testing devices, facilitating industrial adoption.

Additionally, we plan to quantify seed internal biochemical properties including moisture, protein and starch content. This allows us to establish explicit correlations between SPA-selected sensitive bands and seed physicochemical traits, further uncovering the biological spectral response mechanism of japonica rice seeds. Moreover, the multi-stage optimization pipeline established in this work will be adapted for seed identification of wheat, corn and other grain crops, to validate its general applicability. Overall, this research provides solid technical support for crop germplasm conservation, seed quality supervision and the scaled promotion of elite rice varieties.

Conclusion

This study constructed a streamlined non-destructive identification framework based on hyperspectral imaging for distinguishing nine genetically similar japonica rice seed varieties. The workflow integrates multi-stage spectral preprocessing, optimal feature screening, interpretable analysis, and intelligent parameter optimization to achieve accurate germplasm classification. Mean spectral curves were adopted as model inputs, and Savitzky–Golay smoothing combined with Min–Max normalization was applied to suppress spectral noise and unify feature scales. The Successive Projections Algorithm effectively reduced high-dimensional spectral redundancy by selecting 22 low-collinearity sensitive bands while retaining varietal characteristic differences. SHAP analysis was further introduced to quantitatively interpret feature importance and sample-level classification contributions, addressing the inherent black-box limitation of conventional machine learning models. Optimized by the Harris Hawks Optimization algorithm, the XGBoost model achieved superior classification performance with an overall accuracy of 94.72% and a Kappa coefficient of 0.9389. Validated by per-class F1-scores, the proposed multi-strategy optimization framework significantly reduced misclassification among genetically similar rice varieties and improved identification stability. This study demonstrates that the combination of hyperspectral detection, SPA feature optimization, and HHO-based model tuning provides an effective non-destructive solution for the identification of closely related japonica rice germplasm, which can serve as a reliable technical reference for rice seed purity detection and crop germplasm resource identification.
