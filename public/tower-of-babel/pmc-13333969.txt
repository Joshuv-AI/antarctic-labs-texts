Comparative effects of tillage methods and depths on soil aggregate stability and organic carbon in sugarcane fields
Authors: Zhenhong Luo, Lei Gan, Song Wang, Yu Zhang, JinSheng Huang, Taiqing Huang, Benhui Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To assess the effectiveness of Smash-Ridging Tillage in improving soil structure and fertility, this study compared the impacts of traditional rotary tillage at 20 cm depth and Smash-Ridging Tillage at depths of 20 cm and 40 cm (F40) on soil aggregate distribution and stability meanwhile with its soil organic carbon characteristics in sugarcane fields of Guangxi, China. The results demonstrated that Smash-Ridging Tillage significantly reduced soil bulk density and enhanced soil total nitrogen content. Notably, it markedly increased the proportion of water-stable macroaggregates (> 0.25 mm) (p < 0.05), with the F40 achieving the highest macroaggregate content (51.17–60.72%). Aggregate stability, as indicated by mean weight diameter (MWD) and geometric mean diameter (GMD), was also significantly improved under Smash-Ridging Tillage. Pearson correlation and redundancy analyses revealed that macroaggregate content and the contribution rate of organic carbon associated with macroaggregates were positively correlated with MWD and GMD, but negatively correlated with fractal dimension and soil erodibility factor. The F40 showed the highest contribution rate of macroaggregate organic carbon, which was approximately 20.58–32.57% higher than that under traditional rotary tillage. Overall, deep smash-ridging tillage at 40 cm significantly improved soil structural stability, which enhanced organic carbon stabilization within macroaggregates, providing a scientific reference for optimizing tillage practices to improve soil structure and organic carbon sequestration in subtropical sugarcane fields.

Introduction

Soil structure is a key indicator for assessing soil fertility and the environmental status of arable land, which plays an essential role in regulating physical, chemical, and biological soil processes1,2. Soil aggregates, as fundamental units of soil structure, contribute to fertility and are critical for maintaining soil system functionality3. Aggregates are commonly classified by a threshold size of 0.25 mm: those larger than 0.25 mm are termed macroaggregates, while smaller ones are microaggregates4. Aggregates of varying sizes perform distinct functions, which include the improvement of soil porosity, the enhancement of water retention, the stimulation of biological activity, and the maintenance of nutrient balance2. Primary indicators for assessing aggregate stability include mean weight diameter (MWD), geometric mean diameter (GMD), and fractal dimension (D)5. Specifically, MWD and GMD reflect aggregate size and distribution, while fractal dimension characterizes soil particle structural complexity. Mechanical macroaggregates enhance water-holding capacity and reduce surface runoff, thereby improving resistance to erosion6. In contrast, water-stable aggregates are widely recognized as reliable indicators of soil fertility7,8. The formation and stability of soil aggregates are not only determined by physical processes but are also strongly regulated by organic binding agents9. Among these, soil organic carbon plays a central role in mediating aggregate formation, stabilization, and turnover, thereby linking soil structure with nutrient cycling and ecosystem functioning10.

Organic carbon acts as the primary cementing agent in soil aggregate formation, which enhances particle cohesion11. Approximately 90% of the soil organic carbon in agricultural topsoil is stored within aggregates12. Stable aggregates protect mineralizable organic matter from microbial decomposition; however, organic carbon content varies significantly among aggregate sizes13. Tang et al.14 found that under various land uses, soil organic carbon (SOC) content generally decreases with increasing aggregate size, with larger aggregates containing more labile, newly formed organic matter than microaggregates. Zhang et al.15 reported that decomposition of organic carbon in large aggregates may form more stable soil fractions, which were significantly correlated with a higher proportion of large aggregates and greater SOC mass fraction. Similarly, Six et al.16 concluded that newly immobilized SOC is primarily stored in microaggregates (< 0.25 mm), with its mass fraction increasing as microaggregates coalesce into macroaggregates. This aggregation process improves soil structure and promotes carbon sequestration by slowing macroaggregate breakdown and facilitating the formation of new microaggregates within them. However, most studies on aggregate-associated organic carbon have been conducted in temperate croplands or upland soils, while evidence from sugarcane agroecosystems, particularly under deep tillage management, remains limited. Previous studies have shown that different tillage methods and tillage depths significantly alter soil pore number, size, and connectivity, thereby affecting soil water storage capacity in sugarcane fields in Guangxi17,18. Studies on the relationship between soil aggregates and organic carbon in sugarcane fields remain scarce. This indicates that further research is required to better understand their interaction.

Tillage is a critical practice influencing farmland soil structure and aggregate stability19,20. Appropriate tillage could improve soil structure, which is beneficial to optimize the soil environment and enhance soil fertility21. Tillage depth also affects soil structure and soil environment. Pinheiro et al.22 demonstrated that traditional tillage disrupted soil structure and reduced aggregate stability, which finally increased the erosion susceptibility. Wei et al.23 reported that tilled soils were more prone to aggregate breakdown and organic matter exposure than no-tillage conservation systems, with excessive tillage accelerating soil organic matter mineralization. Tian et al.24 found that continuous no-tillage combined with deep plowing significantly increases soil organic carbon and aggregate stability, thereby improving soil structure. Similarly, Zheng et al.25 observed that deep plowing notably increased water-stable macroaggregate proportion and organic carbon content during a long-term experiment in the black soil region of northeastern China. Yang et al.26 demonstrated that long-term deep plowing combined with organic fertilizer application significantly enhanced soil microbial biomass carbon and nitrogen. These findings indicated that tillage methods and depths distinctly affect soil aggregates and organic carbon content. Thus, investigating optimal tillage practices is essential for improving farmland soil quality and promoting sustainable agricultural development.

As the main sugarcane-producing region in China, Guangxi has seen widespread application of Smash-Ridging Tillage technology. This method uses a spiral auger to vertically cut and extensively loosen the soil, which breaks the plow pan layer to achieve deep spinning and loosening. This tillage suspends soil particles, forming ridge-like structures, with tillage depth tailored to crop growth requirements. Current research about such tillage methods in Guangxi primarily focuses on crop yield and pest control27, but limited studies have been conducted on soil aggregates and organic carbon fractions. However, the effects of Smash-Ridging Tillage on soil aggregate stability and aggregate-associated organic carbon in sugarcane fields remain poorly understood. In particular, how different Smash-Ridging Tillage depths influence water-stable aggregate formation, organic carbon storage within aggregates, and the contribution of different aggregate size fractions to aggregate-associated organic carbon has not been systematically evaluated. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the relationships among soil aggregate content, stability, organic carbon storage within aggregates, and the contribution of aggregate organic carbon under traditional rotary tillage and varying depths of Smash-Ridging Tillage. We hypothesize that Smash-Ridging Tillage promotes the formation and stabilization of soil aggregates and enhances aggregate-associated organic carbon compared with traditional rotary tillage, with these effects being strengthened as tillage depth increases.

Materials and methods

Study area

The research was conducted at the Lijian Scientific Research Base of the Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China (23°14′N, 108°02′E). The study area lies in a basin within Wuming District, mainly characterized by hilly terrain. The soil is derived from Quaternary red earth parent material, which is predominantly classified as red soil. Soil pH ranges from 5.0 to 6.5. Soil organic matter content varies between 15 and 30 g·kg−1. The region has a subtropical monsoon climate, with a mean annual temperature of 21.6 °C. Annual sunshine averages approximately 1800 h. The precipitation is abundant, averaging about 1300 mm/year. Notably, the rainy season lasts from May to September, contributing over 60% of the total annual precipitation.

Soil sample collection

The tested soil was collected during the elongation stage of sugarcane growth in April 2024 from the Lijian Scientific Research Base of the Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China. To investigate the effects of different tillage methods on soil properties, three treatments were established in the experimental area. Because traditional rotary tillage commonly used in the region is technically limited to shallow soil disturbance (approximately 20–25 cm), rotary tillage at 20 cm depth (X20) was selected as the representative conventional practice and compared with Smash-Ridging Tillage at the same depth (F20). In addition, because Smash-Ridging Tillage machinery is capable of loosening soil to depths of up to 40 cm, a deeper treatment at 40 cm (F40) was included to evaluate the effect of increased tillage depth within Smash-Ridging Tillage by comparison with F20. Three replicate plots were set for each treatment with covering an area of 50 m2 for each plot. The sugarcane variety was Guiliu 05–136. The irrigation and fertilization conditions were kept consistent across all treatments. To ensure standardized sampling, a cubical soil profile (50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm), considering the rooting depth and ridge formation characteristics of sugarcane, was excavated after removing surface litter from each plot. A ringknife sample with a volume of 100 cm3 was used to collect undisturbed soil samples. The soil profile was divided into 10 cm intervals, with three replicate samples collected per layer, covering the 0–40 cm soil depth. For each layer, approximately 200 g of undisturbed soil was sealed in bags for analysis.

Analysis of soil samples

The collected soil samples were air-dried naturally in a well-ventilated area. After removing visible roots, stones, and other debris, water-stable aggregates were separated into four particle-size classes using the standard wet-sieving method described by Elliott28. Briefly, a 50 g air-dried soil sample was placed on a 2 mm mesh sieve (20 cm in diameter) and soaked in distilled water at room temperature for 5 min. The sieve was then vertically shaken at an amplitude of 3 cm and a frequency of 50 cycles per 2 min. Aggregates retained on the 2 mm sieve were collected as macroaggregates (> 2 mm). The remaining suspension was sequentially passed through 0.25 mm and 0.053 mm sieves using the same method, separating the 2–0.25 mm, 0.25–0.053 mm, and < 0.053 mm fractions. All fractions were rinsed with distilled water to remove residual fine particles. The mass fraction of each size class was determined by drying the aggregates at 105 °C for 24 h and weighing. This process allowed quantification of aggregate distribution across different size classes.

Soil organic matter and organic carbon content in aggregates of different particle sizes were determined using the potassium dichromate dilution-heat colorimetric method29. This method utilized the heat generated from mixing concentrated sulfuric acid with aqueous potassium dichromate to oxidize soil organic carbon into carbon dioxide. Hexavalent chromium (Cr6+) in potassium dichromate was reduced to trivalent chromium (Cr3+), then the remaining dichromate was titrated with a standard ferrous sulfate solution. The change in dichromate concentration before and after oxidation was used to calculate soil organic matter content. The soil bulk density was determined by oven-drying the samples at 105°C30. The saturated hydraulic conductivity was measured using the double-ring method31. Soil texture was analyzed by the Malvern laser particle size analyzer32. Total nitrogen content was determined by the Kjeldahl digestion method33, while available phosphorus was measured by the sodium bicarbonate-molybdenum antimony inverse spectropho­tometry method34.

Data processing and statistical analyses were performed using Excel 2019 and SPSS 26.0, while graphs were generated using Origin 2021. Preliminary two-way ANOVA with tillage treatment and soil depth as fixed factors revealed no significant treatment × depth interactions (p > 0.05) and heterogeneity of variance across depths for most variables (Levene’s test, p < 0.05). Therefore, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied separately within each soil depth to evaluate differences among tillage treatments, followed by the least significant difference (LSD) test at a 5% significance level. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess relationships between aggregate content, organic carbon contribution rate, and aggregate stability. Additionally, redundancy analysis (RDA) was conducted using Canoco 5.0 to assess the relationship between soil aggregate stability and physicochemical properties, and finally to determine the extent to which these properties explained variations in aggregate stability.

Results

Soil physical and chemical properties

In the 10–20 cm soil layer, the clay content of the X20 was significantly higher than that of the F20 (p < 0.01) and F40 (p < 0.05), exceeding them by 124.35% and 38.62%, respectively. Meanwhile, the silt content of the F40 was significantly higher than that of the X20 and F20 (p < 0.05), surpassing them by 48.45% and 31.71%, respectively. Distribution of soil aggregates

Water-stable aggregates were predominantly composed of large aggregates (> 0.25 mm), as shown in Table 2, with contents ranging from 43.5% to 60.72% across treatments. Under the F40, the proportion of > 0.25 mm aggregates differed significantly from that in the X20 (p < 0.05) across all soil layers. Concerning tillage practices, the F20 exhibited higher proportions of > 0.25 mm aggregates than the X20 in each layer, with differences ranging from 6.86 to 10.46%. In the 10–20 cm layer, the content of 0.25–0.053 mm aggregates was significantly lower in the X20 than in the F20 (p < 0.05), by 30.38%. The content of < 0.053 mm aggregates in the 0–20 cm layer was higher under the F20 than under the X20, whereas the opposite trend was observed in the 20–40 cm layer without a significant difference. (p > 0.05). Regarding the effect of tillage depth, the F40 consistently yielded higher proportions of > 0.25 mm aggregates compared to the F20, with increases ranging from 9.39 to 14.66%. In the 0–10 cm layer, the content of 0.25–0.053 mm aggregates was 14.33% higher in the F40 than in the F20, while this trend was opposite in the 10-30 cm layers. The < 0.053 mm aggregate content in the 0–10 cm layer was significantly lower in F40 than in F20 (p < 0.05), with a reduction of 31.19%. Collectively, compared with X20, Smash-Ridging Tillage (F20 and F40) was characterized by a higher proportion of water-stable aggregates (> 0.25 mm) across soil layers. Discussion

The composition of soil aggregates plays a critical role in determining soil structural quality. A higher proportion of macroaggregates (> 0.25 mm) is generally associated with improved soil structural stability, while an excessive presence of microaggregates (< 0.25 mm) can impair aeration, permeability, and microbial function36,37. In this study, the higher proportion of > 0.25 mm aggregates under Smash-Ridging tillage than under traditional rotary tillage suggested that Smash-Ridging enhances the content of water-stable macroaggregates in sugarcane field soils, with the most pronounced effect observed under the F40 (Table 2). This improvement may be attributed to the ability of Smash-Ridging machinery, which effectively breaks down soil clods and stones while penetrating deeper into the soil38. This deep disturbance promotes better contact between fine particles and soil organic matter, which might facilitate aggregate formation and increase the proportion of larger water-stable aggregates1,39. Rational tillage practices have been documented to reduce soil bulk density and enhance microbial respiration in the rhizosphere, thereby promoting aggregate formation25,40. However, in this study, the highest stability of soil aggregates under the F40 (Figs. 1 and 2) but lowest under the X20 can be attributed to the fact that traditional rotary tillage involved intense soil disturbance, which led to the formation of a compacted plow pan at a depth of 20–25 cm 41. Such a layer has been reported to restrict root growth and water infiltration, which would disrupt soil structure and ultimately reduce aggregate stability in tilled soils 42. In contrast, Smash-Ridging tillage (F40), characterized by vertical deep loosening, effectively broke up the plow pan to create a deeper and more aerated tillage layer43. This environment favored aggregate stability by improving subsoil porosity and reducing structural damage44–46. Pirmoradian et al.47 reported that the fractal dimension (D) is better suited than the mean weight diameter (MWD) and geometric mean diameter (GMD) for characterizing soil aggregate stability. In contrast, Xie and Wei48 indicated that the indicator D, despite being influenced by soil particle distribution, remained relatively stable, even when different land use patterns affected the growth status of surface vegetation and organic carbon content. This result is consistent with the findings of the present study, in which D showed no significant variation among the three treatments, whereas MWD and GMD demonstrated a stronger capacity to reflect changes in soil stability. Typically, higher MWD and GMD values indicate a more stable soil structure and greater resistance to erosion49,50. Conversely, lower D and K values suggest looser soil with improved water retention and conservation capacity50,51. The result of this study confirmed that Smash-Ridging tillage effectively enhances soil structural stability, which is projected to enhance soil water-holding capacity, and thus has the potential to improve soil water conservation and erosion resistance. The results of the redundancy analysis (RDA) in this study (Fig. 7 and Table 3) showed that MWD and GMD were positively correlated with the content of 2–0.25 mm aggregates (p < 0.05). This indicated that MWD and GMD consistently reflect the stability characteristics of soil aggregates, aligning with the findings of Li et al.52. In contrast, D and K were negatively correlated with the content of 2–0.25 mm aggregates (p < 0.05), which further confirmed that macroaggregates played a key role in enhancing soil structural stability. These findings also suggested that Smash-Ridging cultivation improves aggregate stability by increasing the proportion of 2–0.25 mm water-stable aggregates52,53. Importantly, by extending the analysis to the 40 cm soil layer, this study further demonstrates that the improvement in aggregate stability under Smash-Ridging tillage is not confined to surface soil. Instead, vertical deep loosening reshapes aggregate size distribution and structural stability throughout the entire 0–40 cm profile, revealing a depth-dependent regulation mechanism that distinguishes Smash-Ridging tillage from traditional rotary tillage.

As a major storage form of soil organic carbon (SOC), soil aggregates not only protect SOC from microbial degradation through physical isolation but also serve as structurally stable sites for long-term carbon retention54,55. However, tillage practices can compromise aggregate integrity, which results in SOC reduction and increases the soil’s vulnerability to erosion56. In contrast, improved tillage methods are more favorable for enhancing SOC sequestration within aggregates57. The results of this study showed that Smash-Ridging Tillage significantly increased the organic carbon content in macroaggregates (> 0.25 mm), compared with traditional rotary tillage. This suggested that Smash-Ridging Tillage, as an improved tillage practice, enhances SOC sequestration in macroaggregates58. Furthermore, deeper tillage was more favorable for the incorporation of organic carbon into large aggregates59. These findings are consistent with those of Zhang et al.60, who reported that the organic carbon content of macroaggregates accounted for more than 50% of total SOC. Matus61 reported that silt and clay particles acted as binding agents to play a dominant role in the formation and stabilization of aggregates. Similarly, Li et al.62 demonstrated that higher silt content significantly increased the proportion of macroaggregates, which affects their stability and associated organic carbon. Consistent with these findings, the highest silt content combined with moderate clay content was observed in the sugarcane field under the F40. This particle composition contributed to the maintenance and promotion of macroaggregate formation and stabilization, while simultaneously reducing aggregate disruption and enhancing organic carbon storage, which ultimately improved soil structure. Six et al.55 concluded that macroaggregates had a stable structure with small internal pores and moderate permeability, which favored microbial activity and enabled the physical encapsulation of organic carbon, ultimately reducing its loss. This protective mechanism facilitated the accumulation of organic carbon within macroaggregates. These observations further suggested that Smash-Ridging Tillage promotes SOC enrichment in macroaggregates in this study. Tisdall and Oades4 reported that most of the organic carbon newly formed in macroaggregates was unstable transitional carbon, which was readily subject to mineralization and decomposition. They also noted that soil macroaggregates were formed from microaggregates through the cementation of organic carbon. Therefore, tillage practices exert substantial influence on the composition, transformation, and distribution of both macroaggregates and microaggregates. In this study, the organic carbon under traditional rotary tillage was primarily concentrated in macroaggregates within the 0–20 cm soil layer. This finding aligned with the results of Carter63, who reported that the surface layer(0–5 cm) that was subjected to short-term rotary tillage (3–5 years) in fields retained approximately 30–40% of macroaggregates (> 0.25 mm). They also noted that the disruptive impact of rotary tillage became more pronounced with increasing soil depth, which was associated with a marked reduction in the proportion of macroaggregates below 20 cm. These observations suggest that although traditional rotary tillage may partially disrupt aggregate structure, a considerable portion of macroaggregates can still be preserved over a limited period, particularly in surface soils. Lavallee et al.64 revealed a spatial conservation mechanism for SOC within structurally stable aggregates, where organic carbon storage is governed by structural integrity and volumetric factors, thereby explaining the discrepancy between SOC content and storage. Similarly, Wiesmeier et al.65 found that although SOC content varied across regions and soil horizons, these variations did not directly correspond to changes in SOC storage due to differences in soil bulk density and soil thickness. In the present study, we observed comparable results: the trends of organic carbon storage and organic carbon content in aggregates of different particle sizes varied under different treatments (Figs. 3 and 4). This variation may be attributed to the fact that organic carbon storage depends not only on organic carbon content but also on soil bulk density, soil texture, and other related factors.

The proportion of aggregates and the organic carbon content within each particle size class together determine the organic carbon contribution rate of soil aggregates66. However, this distribution is strongly influenced by soil conditions and tillage practices25. The present study demonstrated that Smash-Ridging Tillage significantly increased the organic carbon contribution rate of macroaggregates (> 0.25 mm), whereas traditional rotary tillage led to a higher contribution rate in microaggregates (< 0.25 mm). The reason for this result was that Smash-Ridging tillage significantly reduced soil bulk density while increasing total nitrogen content67. The reduction in bulk density enhanced soil porosity and aeration, which may stimulate microbial activity and root growth in previous studies, and that has the potential to promote the incorporation of organic matter into macroaggregates68. This process, coupled with improved physical protection of carbon within aggregates, likely contributed to the observed increase in SOC within macroaggregates under Smash-Ridging tillage. The decrease in bulk density enhanced soil aeration by improving pore structure, which is also expected to improve the soil’s water retention capacity, consistent with the finding of Tian et al.69. The increase in total nitrogen content suggested that Smash-Ridging tillage reduced nutrient loss and promoted the accumulation of organic carbon within macroaggregates, which enhanced the contribution of organic carbon from the macroaggregate fraction70. Conversely, traditional rotary tillage tended to produce alternating zones of compaction and loosened soil, which resulted in low porosity and suboptimal saturated hydraulic conductivity conditions71. This practice may also incorporate deeper, finer-textured soil layers into the surface layer72, leading to a relatively higher clay content in the tilled layer, which can enhance the adsorption of organic carbon within microaggregates. It might be unfavorable for the formation and stability of large aggregates4. Additionally, previous studies have reported similar findings that rotary tillage is often associated with higher clay content, enhancing the adsorption of organic carbon within microaggregates, thereby increasing their contribution rate59,72. This was similarly concluded in this study, where clay content was higher under traditional rotary tillage than under Smash-Ridging tillage. Fang et al.73observed that the contribution rate of organic carbon increased with decreasing aggregate size. Lehmann et al. 74reported the highest contribution in 0.5–1 mm aggregates, with lower values in both < 0.25 mm and > 1 mm fractions, forming a “∧”-shaped distribution. In contrast, Tisdall and Oades4 identified a “V”-shaped pattern, in which aggregates > 0.25 mm and < 0.053 mm contributed more significantly to organic carbon than the 0.25–0.053 mm fraction. These patterns are consistent with the findings of the present study, which showed that the contribution of organic carbon was consistently highest in large aggregates (> 0.25 mm) and lowest in the 0.25–0.053 mm fraction across all treatments (Fig. 5). Notably, the < 0.053 mm aggregates also accounted for a substantial proportion of organic carbon, further corroborating the “V”-shaped distribution reported by Tisdall and Oades4. Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 6) revealed significant positive correlations between the mass percentage and organic carbon contribution of large aggregates (> 0.25 mm) and the stability indices MWD and GMD (p < 0.05). In contrast, negative correlations were observed with D and K (p < 0.05). Higher values of both the mass percentage and organic carbon contribution rate of 2–0.25 mm aggregates were associated with increased MWD and GMD but decreased D and K values, reflecting that the greater soil aggregate stability could form a more rational soil structure to enhance erosion resistance. These findings were consistent with those of Yang et al.75, who reported that increasing the proportion of large aggregates (> 0.25 mm) significantly elevated MWD, GMD, and organic carbon contribution (p < 0.05), while reducing D and K values. Overall, these results were consistent with the central hypothesis of this study that Smash-Ridging Tillage, in comparison with traditional rotary tillage, significantly increased the proportion of water-stable macroaggregates (> 0.25 mm) and enhanced the organic carbon contribution rate in sugarcane soils in Guangxi of China. Among all treatments, F40 significantly enhanced aggregate stability in this study, which creates a favorable environment for soil hydrodynamics and sugarcane root development, and has the potential to support better crop growth in field production.

Although the present study was conducted over a relatively short experimental period, it provides insight into how different tillage strategies influence soil structural dynamics in sugarcane fields. Continuous traditional rotary tillage has been widely reported to induce soil compaction, promote plow pan formation, and disrupt aggregate structure under long-term cultivation76. Previous studies have shown that improved or intermittent tillage practices, which reduce tillage frequency, can alleviate subsoil compaction and enhance aggregate stability by allowing soil structure to recover between disturbance events77,78. Against this background, Smash-Ridging Tillage demonstrated clear advantages over traditional rotary tillage in the present study by improving aggregate stability and redistributing organic carbon toward more stable aggregate fractions, even under the same tillage frequency. Considering that sugarcane is a perennial ratoon crop, its tillage mode resembles intermittent tillage, which does not require annual tillage79. As a tillage practice superior to traditional rotary tillage in our study, Smash-Ridging Tillage may function similarly to other improved tillage practices that reduce tillage frequency over time. Under such management, the Smash-Ridging Tillage has the potential to promote gradual improvements in soil aggregate stability and aggregate-associated organic carbon by allowing partial structural recovery between tillage events. However, in addition to the long-term effects of Smash-Ridging Tillage on soil aggregate stability and organic carbon retention, its potential impacts on sugarcane root architecture, soil water transport dynamics, crop yield and comprehensive agronomic benefits also require further verification through long-term field experiments.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that Smash-Ridging Tillage significantly improved soil physical and chemical properties in sugarcane fields in Guangxi, China, compared with traditional rotary tillage, highlighting its effectiveness in improving soil structure and stabilizing soil organic carbon. Specifically, Smash-Ridging Tillage reduced soil bulk density and increased total nitrogen content, while substantially enhancing the formation of water-stable macroaggregates. Among all treatments, the F40 produced the highest MWD and GMD, but the lowest D and K, which together reflect an improvement in aggregate stability. Moreover, Smash-Ridging Tillage promoted the accumulation of organic carbon within water-stable macroaggregates and increased their contribution to total soil organic carbon. These improvements led to enhanced soil permeability and structural integrity, which may contribute to water transport and root development under field conditions. Overall, the F40 under Smash-Ridging Tillage exhibited the best performance in improving soil aggregate stability and aggregate-associated organic carbon sequestration in the present study, providing a scientific reference for optimizing tillage practices to improve soil structural quality and soil carbon sequestration capacity in sugarcane production systems. The broader benefits of this practice for mitigating land degradation, enhancing land productivity, and supporting sustainable agricultural development remain to be validated through long-term field monitoring.
