Engineering Ethics, Risk, and Islamic Values in Contemporary Technologies
Authors: Zohreh Shaghaghi, Shahram Hosseinzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDebates in science and engineering ethics increasingly focus on how professionals should act under conditions of uncertainty, especially when essential technologies may pose low-probability but non-negligible risks to human health or the environment. A normative framework for engineering decision-making is developed, grounded in Islamic ethical concepts, and applied to four contemporary technological domains: electromagnetic field (EMF) exposure from high-voltage power infrastructure, radiofrequency (RF) exposure from mobile phones, water use in hydrogen fuel production, and hazardous waste from end-of-life solar photovoltaic (PV) panels. Drawing on the Qur’an and Hadith literature, as well as contemporary discussions of the higher objectives of Islamic law and professional engineering codes, the moral significance of public interest, harm prevention, stewardship, justice, and trusteeship is elaborated. The analysis shows how these values support precautionary yet innovation-friendly responses to uncertain risk, emphasizing transparency, distributional fairness, long-term environmental responsibility, and accountability to affected communities. By articulating points of convergence and complementarity between Islamic ethics and mainstream engineering ethics, a faith-informed framework is contributed that can guide engineers working in Muslim-majority contexts and in culturally diverse societies toward more responsible design, regulation, and deployment of emerging technologies. Read through the lens of the five ethical principles articulated in this framework—harm prevention, public interest, stewardship, justice, and trusteeship—the hadith metaphor emphasizes that ethical engineering consists of small but responsible actions taken with awareness of risk, care for others, and accountability to both present and future generations.Keywords: Engineering ethics, Risk, Islamic values, Technologies

Introduction

Questions of risk, responsibility, and justice are central to contemporary debates in science and engineering ethics. Modern societies depend on complex energy and communication systems to sustain economic activity and everyday life, yet these same systems may generate uncertain or unevenly distributed harms. Engineering codes of ethics—such as the National Society of Professional Engineers (NSPE) Code—accordingly require practitioners to “hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public” and to act with honesty and impartiality in their professional judgments (National Society of Professional Engineers, 2019). How these obligations should be interpreted in concrete technological controversies, especially where scientific evidence is incomplete or contested, remains a pressing practical and theoretical question.

This article examines how an explicitly religious normative framework—Islamic ethics—can inform engineering decision-making in such contexts. Islam understands natural resources as a divine blessing and a trust delegated to human beings. The Qur’an states, “He brought you forth from the earth and settled you therein” (Qur’an 11:61) (The Holy Qur’an, 11:61), a verse interpreted by many scholars as charging humanity with responsible cultivation rather than reckless exploitation of the earth. Other verses warn against environmental and social harm: “And cause not corruption upon the earth after its reformation” (Qur’an 7:56) (The Holy Qur’an, 7:56), and condemn those who “destroy crops and cattle” in pursuit of mischief (Qur’an 2:205) (The Holy Qur’an, 2:205). Islamic legal and ethical literature extends these scriptural themes into general maxims. The well-known principle la darar wa la dirar (no harm and no reciprocating harm) is taken by jurists as a broad prohibition on inflicting or tolerating unjustified injury (Al-Sistani, 1414 AH), while Imam Ja’far al-Sadiq’s statement that life is not pleasant without clean air, abundant fresh water, and fertile land links human flourishing directly to environmental integrity (Abedi-Sarvestani & Shahvali, 2008).

Building on these sources and contemporary scholarship in Islamic jurisprudence and bioethics (Hathout, 1988; Daar & Khitamy, 2001; Kamali, 2005; Amanullah, 2012; Muhsin et al., 2019), this paper develops an Islamic engineering ethics framework organized around five key notions:

Harm prevention: the obligation to identify, minimize, and, where possible, remove harms, including uncertain or low-probability risks when potential consequences are grave (Al-Sistani, 1414 AH, Muhsin et al., 2019).

In each domain, existing empirical evidence on risk is briefly reviewed and then evaluated through the Islamic engineering ethics framework sketched above. The discussion demonstrates points of alignment with established professional norms while also drawing attention to distinctively Islamic emphases on accountability before God, the sacrality of natural resources, and the moral status of future generations.

While previous faith-based or culturally grounded approaches to engineering ethics have primarily emphasized moral motivation or value alignment, the present framework advances this literature by explicitly structuring Islamic ethical principles into a decision-guiding model for managing technological risk and uncertainty. In doing so, it moves beyond general moral exhortation in favor of a systematic framework applicable to concrete engineering judgments.

An Explicit Islamic Engineering Ethics Framework

The Islamic engineering ethics framework proposed in this article constitutes its central normative contribution and is presented here explicitly as a coherent and structured whole. The framework is constructed through a synthesis of Qur’anic ethical injunctions, Prophetic traditions (Hadith), widely accepted Islamic legal maxims, and contemporary Islamic ethical scholarship. Core principles are selected based on three criteria: their normative centrality within Islamic moral thought, their direct relevance to technological risk and uncertainty, and their applicability to real-world engineering decision-making. On this basis, five principles are identified as constitutive elements of the framework: harm prevention, public interest, stewardship, justice, and trusteeship. Conceptual structure of the Islamic engineering ethics framework guiding decision-making under conditions of uncertainty

The framework is designed to guide engineering judgment under conditions of uncertainty, where scientific evidence may be incomplete and potential harms may be uncertain or delayed. Harm prevention functions as a threshold principle, prioritizing the avoidance or mitigation of serious or irreversible damage even when causal certainty is lacking. Public interest and justice jointly structure the evaluation and the fair distribution of risks and benefits across affected communities, while stewardship extends ethical concern to long-term environmental and intergenerational impacts. Trusteeship frames the professional role of engineers as one of moral accountability, emphasizing transparency, integrity, and responsibility toward society and future generations. These principles operate collectively as a decision-guiding framework, directing engineers to ask systematic normative questions regarding harm, benefit, fairness, sustainability, and professional responsibility. In this way, the framework is distinguished from a thematic collection of ethical values and presented as a coherent tool for responsible engineering practice under uncertainty.

Complementarity with Professional Engineering Codes of Ethics

Professional engineering codes of ethics, such as the NSPE Code, emphasize the primacy of public safety, honesty, and professional responsibility. These principles provide essential baseline obligations for engineers but often remain procedurally open-ended when risks are uncertain, long-term, or unevenly distributed. The Islamic engineering ethics framework proposed in this article is not intended to replace such codes but to complement them by offering additional normative guidance in three key areas: acceptable risk, intergenerational responsibility, and resource stewardship (see Table 1).

Complementarity between professional engineering ethics and Islamic ethical principles

With respect to acceptable risk, mainstream engineering codes typically require engineers to protect public safety but do not specify how to act when scientific evidence is incomplete or contested. Islamic ethics adds a substantive precautionary orientation through the maxim, “which prioritizes” the avoidance of serious harm even under uncertainty. This principle strengthens existing safety obligations by supplying a moral justification for erring on the side of caution when potential consequences are severe.

Professional codes generally focus on present stakeholders, whereas Islamic concepts of stewardship and trusteeship explicitly extend moral responsibility to future generations. Engineering decisions are thus evaluated not only in terms of immediate compliance or benefit but also in terms of their long-term environmental and social consequences.

While resource stewardship appears in contemporary sustainability discourse, Islamic ethics frames natural resources such as water, land, and energy as moral trusts rather than merely economic inputs. The principle of public interest requires that resource use advance genuine public welfare and prohibits burden-shifting to vulnerable populations or future societies. In this way, Islamic ethics deepens and contextualizes existing professional norms, thereby transforming general commitments to safety and sustainability into a more explicit and culturally grounded framework for responsible engineering judgment under uncertainty.

EMF Exposure from Electric Power Infrastructure

Reliable access to electricity underpins virtually all modern infrastructure, and grid expansion is widely regarded as a driver of social and economic development. At the same time, the transmission and distribution of electric power inevitably introduce extremely low-frequency EMFs into residential and occupational environments. A large body of epidemiological and experimental research has investigated whether proximity to high-voltage power lines or distribution substations may increase health risks, particularly childhood leukemia. While many studies have not found clear causal links at typical exposure levels, some systematic reviews report a modest but consistent association between long-term exposure to strong magnetic fields and childhood leukemia (Seomun et al., 2021). Children living within approximately 50–60 m of major transmission lines, where magnetic field strengths often exceed 0.3–0.4 μT, have been found to exhibit about a two-fold increase in leukemia risk relative to those with lower exposures (Kheifets et al., 2010). Reflecting this mixed evidence, the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies extremely low-frequency magnetic fields as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B) (Copes & Barn, 2008).

Standard engineering ethics would treat such findings as grounds for precaution. Even if causal mechanisms are uncertain and absolute risk levels remain low, professional codes instruct engineers to protect public safety, to be transparent about potential risks, and to avoid exposing the public to avoidable hazards (National Society of Professional Engineers, 2019). In practice, this often translates into routing high-voltage lines away from dense residential areas, using undergrounding where feasible, and applying technical measures such as phase reconfiguration to reduce field intensities (California Public Utilities Commission, 2006). Engineering regulators thereby attempt to secure the benefits of electrification while limiting plausible harms.

The Islamic engineering ethics framework both supports and deepens this precautionary logic. The harm-prevention maxim obliges engineers and policymakers to take seriously even low-probability risks when the potential consequences are severe, such as childhood cancer. Juristic discussions of this maxim emphasize the active removal or mitigation of harms where plausible evidence exists (Al-Sistani, 1414 AH, Muhsin et al., 2019). The notion of public interest requires a holistic assessment of benefits and harms. Extensive electrification may yield substantial social benefits—from hospitals and schools to livelihoods—but these benefits cannot ethically be purchased at the expense of disproportionate risks to specific, often less powerful neighborhoods. Justice thus demands attention to distributional fairness: siting decisions should not systematically burden low-income or marginalized communities with higher EMF exposure. Stewardship and trusteeship emphasize engineers’ accountability not only to regulatory agencies and current residents but also to future generations whose health may be affected by present infrastructural choices (Abedi-Sarvestani & Shahvali, 2008; International Islamic Fiqh Academy, 2023).

These values favor what might be called Islamically grounded precaution: adopting technically and economically reasonable measures to reduce EMF exposure, prioritizing protective standards for children and other vulnerable groups, and ensuring transparent communication of risks and uncertainties to affected communities (Al-Sistani, 1414 AH). Such an approach resonates with secular risk ethics while providing an additional layer of motivation rooted in religious accountability and the moral significance of human life as a divine trust (National Society of Professional Engineers, 2019; Kazmi, 2023).

In regulatory and planning contexts, such Islamic precautionary reasoning could be operationalized through conservative siting policies, enhanced buffer zones around residential areas, the prioritization of exposure reduction for children, and transparent public communication where scientific uncertainty persists.

Mobile Phones, RF Exposure, and Responsible Use

Mobile phones and wireless networks have become indispensable tools for communication, education, and commerce. They also expose users and nearby populations to RF electromagnetic fields, prompting public concern about possible long-term health effects. Regulatory and scientific bodies, including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the National Cancer Institute, have repeatedly concluded that typical mobile phone use does not clearly increase the risk of brain or other cancers (National Cancer Institute, 2024; U.S. Food & Drug Administration, 2025). Although the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies RF fields as possibly carcinogenic (Group 2B) (2013), more recent large-scale prospective studies, such as the COSMOS cohort, have not found an increased incidence of brain tumors, even among high-use groups (Feychting et al., 2024). Nonetheless, some uncertainty remains, particularly regarding very long-term exposure or specific subpopulations, such as children (European Environment Agency, 2011; Meena et al., 2016).

Established engineering and public health guidance treats this as a case for “prudent avoidance”: complying with exposure limits, optimizing antenna placement, and encouraging simple exposure-reducing practices, such as using a speakerphone or hands-free devices (American Academy of Pediatrics, 2016; U.S. Food & Drug Administration, 2020; Federal Communications Commission, 2024). The underlying ethical reasoning emphasizes proportionality and informed consent: where evidence of serious harm is weak but not negligible, and where mitigation is inexpensive, individuals and institutions should reasonably reduce exposure while continuing to enjoy the benefits of communication technology.

From an Islamic perspective, several additional ethical dimensions arise. The body is understood as a trust from God, and individuals are morally obliged to protect their physical and mental well-being (Hathout, 1988). Classical and contemporary scholars stress that the body has rights over the person, including the right not to be subjected to avoidable harm (Daar & Khitamy, 2001). This supports public health advice that users—particularly parents and guardians—should take modest steps to reduce potential RF exposure for themselves and their children, even if the quantified risk remains uncertain.

Second, Islamic conceptions of reliance on God reject both fatalistic passivity and presumptuous risk-taking. Religious teachings cited in contemporary scholarship emphasize that true trust in God involves taking reasonable precautions and then accepting the outcome (Kettani, 1984; Sadar, 1984; Bucaille, 1993). Engineers designing wireless systems, and regulators setting exposure limits, must therefore avoid both extremes: they should neither exaggerate risks in ways that paralyze social benefit nor dismiss plausible concerns in a way that neglects their duty of care.

Third, the strong Islamic emphasis on knowledge and truthfulness prohibits the spread of unfounded rumors and demands intellectual integrity in technical communication (Kettani, 1984; Sadar, 1984; Bucaille, 1993; Fiqh Council of North America, “COVID vaccines statement,”, 2021). Engineers, health agencies, and industry actors share a responsibility to communicate RF risks in a way that is accurate, comprehensible, and free from both undue alarmism and conflicts of interest (Kipnis, 2005; National Society of Professional Engineers, 2019). In Muslim contexts, framing such communication in terms of stewardship and accountability before God may enhance public trust.

Consequently, an Islamic engineering ethics approach to RF technologies would affirm international exposure standards, prioritize vulnerable populations, promote prudent user practices, and insist on transparent communication of uncertainties. It thereby offers a culturally resonant justification for risk management strategies already recommended in secular engineering and public health ethics.

In this respect, Islamic ethical reasoning converges closely with mainstream public health ethics, particularly in its endorsement of prudent avoidance, proportional risk reduction, and clear communication of scientific uncertainty to the public.

Water-Intensive Hydrogen Energy and Global Water Stress

Hydrogen is widely promoted as a versatile energy carrier that can support the decarbonization of hard-to-electrify sectors. Green hydrogen produced by water electrolysis using renewable electricity is particularly attractive, as its use in fuel cells emits only water vapor (Jafari & Shaghaghi, 2025). Yet this technology is not water-neutral. Approximately 9 L of high-purity water are required per kilogram of hydrogen, and when ancillary processes such as cooling and purification are included, total water demand may reach 20–30 L/kg (Anaya et al., 2025). In water-rich regions, this may pose little concern. In arid and semi-arid regions already experiencing water scarcity—which include many Muslim-majority countries—large-scale electrolysis could compete with domestic, agricultural, and ecological water needs (Harhay, 2011; World Wildlife Fund, 2023).

Secular frameworks in environmental and energy ethics increasingly analyze such trade-offs through concepts of energy justice and water–energy nexus governance (Bigas, 2012; Banerjee et al., 2017). They emphasize distributional questions (who gains clean energy and who loses water security), procedural questions (who participates in decision-making), and recognition questions (whose values and vulnerabilities are considered). Engineering decisions regarding hydrogen deployment must therefore account for both climate benefits and local water impacts.

In Islamic ethics, water occupies a particularly central place. The Qur’an emphasizes that “We have made from water every living thing” (21:30) (Priantina & Saleem, 2021), and Islamic legal traditions treat access to water as a basic communal right, not a purely private commodity (I. et al., 2001; Priantina & Saleem, 2021). Scholarly studies on water in Islamic law note prohibitions on withholding surplus water from those in need and repeatedly condemn wasteful use even where supplies appear abundant (I. et al., 2001, The Holy Qur’an, 7:31). Water is thus both a material resource and a symbol of divine mercy; squandering it or diverting it unjustly is framed as a moral failing.

Applying the Islamic engineering ethics framework to hydrogen energy yields several implications. Harm prevention and justice require that hydrogen projects not undermine essential water access for households, agriculture, and ecosystems, particularly for poor and marginalized communities (Harhay, 2011; Amanah Lifeline Initiative, 2025). Public interest demands that climate benefits be weighed alongside local water impacts: using freshwater for hydrogen production in a region facing acute water stress may not qualify as a genuine public interest, especially when alternative decarbonization paths exist (Banerjee et al., 2017). Third, stewardship implies a duty to design and operate hydrogen systems in ways that maximize water efficiency and prioritize non-freshwater sources where feasible, such as desalinated seawater or treated wastewater (I. et al., 2001; Importance of Water in the Perspective of Islam and Science, 2023; Anaya et al., 2025; PatSnap, 2025).

Trusteeship explains the importance of inclusive decision-making processes. Engineers advising governments or industry should ensure that affected communities are informed about water–energy trade-offs and are able to voice their concerns. In Muslim-majority contexts, public engagement that explicitly articulates how proposed projects align with or challenge Islamic values surrounding water may help prevent conflicts and foster ethically robust policies.

A common counterargument is that large-scale desalination could offset freshwater demand for hydrogen production. Within the proposed framework, such solutions should be evaluated ethically in terms of their full environmental costs, energy intensity, equity implications, and potential secondary harms, rather than being treated as morally neutral technological fixes.

Solar Photovoltaics, Hazardous Waste, and Lifecycle Responsibility

Solar PV technology is often portrayed as paradigmatically “green”: it converts sunlight into electricity without operational emissions and plays a central role in most decarbonization scenarios (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 2024). Yet PV systems have finite lifetimes—typically 20–30 years—and their eventual retirement generates substantial quantities of electronic waste. Panels and associated components contain glass, polymers, and various metals, including toxic substances such as lead, cadmium, and selenium (IRENA, 2016). If these materials are landfilled or improperly handled, they can leach into soil and water, posing environmental and public health risks (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 2024; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2025). Projections suggest that by 2050, global PV waste could reach tens of millions of tonnes, making it a significant waste stream (Ghisellini et al., 2016; National Renewable Energy Laboratory, 2021; Nyffenegger et al., 2026; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2025).

From the perspective of science and engineering ethics, this raises classic questions of lifecycle responsibility. Engineers and manufacturers who design and deploy PV technologies to mitigate climate change must also consider end-of-life impacts, avoiding what has been described as “burden shifting” from operational emissions to future waste hazards (IRENA, 2016; National Renewable Energy Laboratory, 2021). Policy responses in several jurisdictions now include producer-responsibility schemes and recycling requirements, while technical research explores more recyclable module designs and improved recovery processes (Curtis et al., 2021; Snead, 2021; Gao et al., 2024; U.S. Department of Energy, 2024).

Islamic ethics reinforces the importance of such lifecycle thinking. The Qur’an warns that “Corruption has appeared throughout the land and sea by [reason of] what the hands of people have earned” (Qur’an 30:41)(The Holy Qur’an, 30:41), a verse interpreted by contemporary scholars as encompassing environmental degradation (Abedi-Sarvestani & Shahvali, 2008). Hadith and juristic discussions emphasize that causing unnecessary damage to the natural world—including cutting trees or polluting water sources—is prohibited, even in wartime (Abedi-Sarvestani & Shahvali, 2008; Geneva Environment Network, 2025). Furthermore, Imam Ali’s famous letter to Malik al-Ashtar instructs political leaders to fear God with respect to both people and land, emphasizing responsibility for the well-being of ecosystems as well as human subjects (Abedi-Sarvestani & Shahvali, 2008).

Within the Islamic engineering ethics framework, three obligations stand out. Harm prevention and stewardship require that PV deployment plans include credible strategies for safe end-of-life management, whether through recycling, secure containment, or design changes that reduce toxic content (IRENA, 2016; Gao et al., 2024; U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2025). Justice demands attention to where PV waste is processed and who bears the risks: exporting hazardous modules to countries with weaker regulatory capacity or siting disposal facilities near marginalized communities would conflict with the Qur’anic call to avoid fasād and with basic principles of fairness (Banerjee et al., 2017; Mongabay, 2025, The Holy Qur’an, 30:41). Trusteeship highlights the role of engineers as intermediaries between current beneficiaries of solar electricity and future generations who may inherit the associated waste. Transparent communication of lifecycle impacts and advocacy for robust recycling infrastructures can be seen as part of discharging this trust (Amanullah, 2012; National Renewable Energy Laboratory, 2021; U.S. Department of Energy, 2024).

In this way, an Islamic perspective pushes beyond narrow operational metrics of “green” technologies and supports emerging secular arguments for a circular economy in solar PV and other renewable technologies.

This perspective aligns with emerging circular economy and lifecycle governance frameworks, which similarly emphasize extended producer responsibility, design-for-recycling, and the avoidance of burden-shifting across time and geography.

Cross-Case Ethical Tensions and Trade-Offs

While the four case studies demonstrate the applicability of the Islamic engineering ethics framework across diverse technological domains, a cross-case analysis reveals important ethical tensions and trade-offs that arise when the framework is applied in practice. These tensions demonstrate that the framework is not merely confirmatory but requires contextual judgment and ethical prioritization.

One recurring tension concerns precaution versus social benefit. In the EMF and RF exposure cases, the principle of harm prevention supports precautionary measures in the face of uncertain health risks, while the principle of public interest emphasizes the substantial social benefits of electrification and wireless communication. The framework does not prescribe maximal risk avoidance but instead requires engineers to balance precaution with proportionality, avoiding both reckless exposure and undue technological restriction.

A second tension emerges between global environmental objectives and local resource constraints. In the hydrogen energy case, stewardship and climate-related public interest support low-carbon energy transitions, yet justice and harm prevention raise concerns where water scarcity threatens essential human and ecological needs. Here, the framework exposes a genuine ethical trade-off rather than a straightforward endorsement of technological deployment.

Finally, the solar photovoltaic case illustrates a temporal tension between present benefits and future burdens. While solar energy advances immediate climate goals, the principles of stewardship and trusteeship draw attention to end-of-life waste risks that may be transferred to future generations or vulnerable communities. Addressing this tension requires lifecycle-oriented decision-making rather than narrow assessments of operational sustainability.

Across these cases, the Islamic engineering ethics framework does not mechanically resolve ethical conflicts. Instead, it structures ethical deliberation by making trade-offs explicit and by guiding engineers to justify decisions in terms of harm, justice, public welfare, and long-term responsibility. This cross-case perspective enhances the analytical depth of the framework and demonstrates its capacity to support critical ethical judgment across heterogeneous technological contexts.

Conclusion: Integrating Islamic and Mainstream Engineering Ethics

The four cases analyzed in this article illustrate how Islamic values can enrich existing debates in science and engineering ethics about risk, responsibility, and justice in contemporary technologies. Across EMF exposure, RF radiation, water-intensive hydrogen production, and solar PV waste, engineers confront trade-offs between significant societal benefits and uncertain or deferred harms. Mainstream professional ethics offers important guidance—prioritizing safety, transparency, and public welfare (Kipnis, 2005; National Society of Professional Engineers, 2019)—but does not, by itself, resolve all questions concerning acceptable risk, intergenerational responsibility, or the moral significance of natural resources.

The Islamic engineering ethics framework developed here- organized around harm prevention, public interest, stewardship, justice, and trusteeship- converges with many secular principles while also supplying distinctive motivations and emphases. It grounds risk reduction not only in a social contract or professional duty but in accountability before God; it interprets water, land, and air as sacred trusts rather than mere economic inputs; and it emphasizes the moral standing of future generations whose voices are absent from present decision-making. For Muslim engineers and policymakers, these considerations can supply powerful reasons to err on the side of precaution where plausible evidence of harm exists, to resist burden-shifting toward vulnerable communities, and to adopt genuinely lifecycle-oriented approaches to design and regulation.

At the same time, the framework is not insular. Its core commitments—to avoid unjustified harm, to discourage waste, to ensure the fair distribution of risks and benefits, and to act as faithful stewards of a fragile planet—are readily intelligible within broader global conversations on engineering ethics and sustainability. Islamic sources thus provide one rich tradition through which to articulate and deepen concerns that are widely shared across cultures and worldviews. Interdisciplinary dialogue among religious ethicists, engineers, policymakers, and affected communities can help translate these values into concrete standards, design practices, and governance mechanisms.

A short narration from Hadith collections captures the spirit of this approach: the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) is reported to have said that even if the final hour is about to be established and one has a sapling in hand, one should plant it (Al-Tabrisi, n.d.). Read through the lens of engineering ethics, this narration encourages practitioners to take constructive, forward-looking action, however small, in the face of daunting environmental and technological challenges. Each effort to route a power line more safely, to minimize RF exposure, to conserve water in hydrogen production, or to recycle solar panels responsibly can be understood as a kind of “sapling” planted for the benefit of current and future generations.

The Islamic framework presented here offers both a case study in culturally situated engineering ethics and a set of conceptual resources that may inform comparative work across religious and secular traditions. Further research could extend this analysis to additional domains—such as artificial intelligence, biotechnology, or geoengineering—and empirically investigate how Muslim engineers actually interpret and apply these values in their professional practice. Such work would contribute to a more globally inclusive and normatively robust understanding of what it means to practice engineering ethically in an era of profound technological and environmental change.

Read through the lens of the five ethical principles articulated in this framework- harm prevention, public interest, stewardship, justice, and trusteeship-, the hadith metaphor emphasizes that ethical engineering consists of small but responsible actions taken with awareness of risk, care for others, and accountability to both present and future generations.
