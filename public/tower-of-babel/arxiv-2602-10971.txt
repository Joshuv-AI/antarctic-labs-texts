A Jointly Efficient and Optimal Algorithm for Heteroskedastic Generalized Linear Bandits with Adversarial Corruptions Equal contributions
Sanghwa Kim, Junghyun Lee, Se-Young Yun

Abstract
We consider the problem of heteroskedastic generalized linear bandits (GLBs) with adversarial corruptions, which subsumes various stochastic contextual bandit settings, including heteroskedastic linear bandits and logistic/Poisson bandits. We propose HCW-GLB-OMD , which consists of two components: an online mirror descent (OMD)-based estimator and Hessian-based confidence weights to achieve corruption robustness. This is computationally efficient in that it only requires 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) {\mathcal{O}}(1) space and time complexity per iteration. Under the self-concordance assumption on the link function, we show a regret bound of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d ​ ∑ t g ⁡ ( τ t ) ​ μ ˙ t , ⋆ + d 2 ​ g max ​ κ + d ​ κ ​ C ) \tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d\sqrt{\sum_{t}g(\tau_{t})\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}+d^{2}g_{\max}\kappa+d\kappa C\right) , where μ ˙ t , ⋆ \dot{\mu}_{t,\star} is the slope of μ \mu around the optimal arm at time t t , g ⁡ ( τ t ) g(\tau_{t}) ’s are potentially exogenously time-varying dispersions (e.g., g ⁡ ( τ t ) = σ t 2 g(\tau_{t})=\sigma_{t}^{2} for heteroskedastic linear bandits, g ⁡ ( τ t ) = 1 g(\tau_{t})=1 for Bernoulli and Poisson), g max = max t ∈ [ T ] ⁡ g ⁡ ( τ t ) g_{\max}=\max_{t\in[T]}g(\tau_{t}) is the maximum dispersion, and C ≥ 0 C\geq 0 is the total corruption budget of the adversary. Introduction

1.1
Problem Setting

Generalized Linear Bandits (GLBs).

In stochastic contextual bandits, the interaction protocol is as follows: at every time step
t
, the learner receives a potentially time-varying contextual arm-set
{\mathcal{X}}_{t}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(which can be adversarial) from the environment; the learner selects an action
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{t}
; and the environment generates a stochastic reward
r_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Depending on the distribution of
r_{t}
, various problem variants exist
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
.
Of particular interest are
parametric bandits

(
Filippi et al. 2010
)
, which assume the reward belongs to a known family of parametric distributions.
We focus on the
generalized linear model (GLM)
family
(
McCullagh and Nelder 1989
)
: for an arm
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the reward is distributed as
r\sim GLM(\cdot\mid{\bm{x}},\tau;{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})
. Here,
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
is a
dispersion parameter
,
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is an unknown parameter, and the probability density of
GLM(\cdot\mid{\bm{x}},\tau;{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})
is given by

dp(r\mid{\bm{x}},\tau;{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})\propto\exp\left(\frac{r\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle-m(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}{g(\tau)}\right)d\nu.

(2)

The only component unknown to the learner is
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The remaining components are known:
g:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is the dispersion function controlling the variability of the GLM, and
d\nu
is an appropriate base measure (e.g., Lebesgue or counting).

We impose the following assumption on the log-partition function
m(\cdot)
:

{assumption}

m(\cdot)
is three-times differentiable and convex.

We define the
(inverse) link function
as
\mu:=m^{\prime}
. This function is well-defined and non-decreasing, i.e.,
\dot{\mu}\geq 0
.
Consequently, we note two useful properties of GLMs that are frequently utilized throughout this work
(
Wainwright and Jordan 2008
)
:

\mathbb{E}[r_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},\tau;{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}]=\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle),\quad\mathrm{Var}[r_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},\tau;{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}]=g(\tau)\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle).

(3)

This family of parametric bandit problems constitutes
generalized linear bandits (GLBs)

(
Filippi et al. 2010
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Lee et al. 2024a
;
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
. This framework encompasses various specific stochastic bandits, such as Gaussian linear bandits
(
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
)
when
\mu(z)=z
, logistic bandits
(
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Faury et al. 2020
;
Abeille et al. 2021
)
when
\mu(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
, and Poisson bandits
(
Lee et al. 2024a
)
when
\mu(z)=e^{z}
.
Note that we do
not
explicitly impose boundedness on the reward distribution.

We adopt several standard assumptions from the
GLB
literature. Denoting
{\mathcal{X}}_{[T]}:=\bigcup_{t\in[T]}{\mathcal{X}}_{t}
,

{assumption}

{\mathcal{X}}_{[T]}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\triangleq{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(1)
and
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(S)
for some
S\in(0,\infty)
known to the learner.

{assumption}

There exists
L_{\mu}\in(0,\infty)
and
R_{s}\in[0,\infty)
such that for all
({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathcal{X}}_{[T]}\times\Theta
,
\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle)\leq L_{\mu}
and
|\ddot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle)|\leq R_{s}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle)
.

Here,
L_{\mu}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\mu(\cdot)
by Rademacher’s theorem
(
Federer 1996
, Theorem 3.1.6)
.
The second inequality is referred to as
(generalized) self-concordance

(
Abeille et al. 2021
;
Russac et al. 2021
)
, a condition originally utilized in the analysis of logistic regression by
Bach 2010
to avoid exponential constants.
Throughout, we will always assume that
\Cref
assumption:m,assumption:bound,assumption:link-function hold.

The goal of the learner is to minimize the cumulative pseudo-regret:

\textup{{Reg}}(T):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t,\star},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle),\quad{\bm{x}}_{t,\star}\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{t}}\mu(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle).

(4)

Because the regret is defined in terms of differences in
\mu(\langle\cdot,{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)
, the parameter
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
significantly impacts the overall regret depending on the choice of
\mu(\cdot)
. Consequently, a
locally
minimax viewpoint is required to precisely capture the statistical hardness of a given bandit instance
(
Simchowitz and Foster 2020
;
Abeille et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Lee et al. 2025
)
.
Technically, this is captured via Taylor’s theorem
(
Klambauer 1986
)
, a crucial tool we utilize throughout. At each time
t
:

\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t,\star},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)-\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t,\star},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t,\star}-{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle+\cdots,

(5)

where
\cdots
represents the Lagrange’s integral remainder to be controlled via self-concordance (
\Cref
assumption:link-function).
Note that the leading term essentially corresponds to a linear bandit problem weighted by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}\triangleq\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t,\star},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}
, the slope at the best arm with respect to the ground-truth
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
.

For instance, when
\mu
is logistic and
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
has a large norm, the regret should vanish as the local landscape becomes significantly flat once the algorithm reaches the local vicinity of the optimal arm. In such a flat landscape, even a large error in parameter estimation would not significantly affect the regret.
Intuitively, the regret should scale
with
the
slope at the optimal arm and parameter
, necessitating algorithms that actively utilize curvature information.
Indeed, algorithms that are agnostic to local curvature, such as early works on generalized linear bandits
(
Filippi et al. 2010
;
Li et al. 2017
)
, typically yield regret bounds scaling with
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa:=\left(\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{[T]},{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(S)}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle)\right)^{-1}},
which often scales as
e^{\|\bm{\theta}_{\star}\|}

(
Faury et al. 2020
)
.

In the seminal work of
Abeille et al. 2021
, the authors proved a
locally
minimax-optimal regret bound of
\widetilde{\Theta}(d\sqrt{T{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{\star},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}})
for logistic bandits with a fixed arm-set
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
In other words, the optimal regret should benefit from the slope at the optimal arm.
This result was subsequently improved, both statistically
(
Emmenegger et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2024b
;
Lee et al. 2024a
;
Lee and Oh 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024a
;
Clerico et al. 2025
;
Kirschner et al. 2025
)
and computationally
(
Faury et al. 2022
;
Zhang and Sugiyama 2023
;
Sawarni et al. 2024
;
Lee and Oh 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
.

GLBs with Time-Varying Dispersions.

We consider a general extension of
GLB
s as follows.
Inspired by the vast literature on online learning, we assume that an oblivious adversary chooses a sequence of
\tau
’s, denoted as
\{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}\tau_{t}}\}_{t\in[T]}
, in advance of the interaction, consistent with the probability density function in Eqn. (
2
).
The interaction protocol remains the same, except that at each time
t
, after the learner chooses
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the reward is sampled as
r_{t}\sim GLM(\cdot|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}\tau_{t}};{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})
, and the learner subsequently observes
{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}\tau_{t}}
.
This setting unifies and subsumes several known heteroskedastic contextual bandit problems:

Example 1

(Heterskedastic Gaussian Linear Bandits)
.

This corresponds to
\mu(z)=z
and
g(\tau_{t})=\sigma_{t}^{2}
.
This setting has been studied extensively in prior literature
(
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Zhou and Gu 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2023a
;
Jia et al. 2024
;
He and Gu 2025
)
.
A minimax optimal rate of
\tilde{\Theta}\left(d\sqrt{\sum_{t}\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)=\tilde{\Theta}\left(d\sqrt{\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}}\right)
is known. Such a regret bound is referred to as being “variance-aware”.

Example 2

(Logistic and Poisson Bandits)
.

This corresponds to
\mu(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
and
\mu(z)=e^{z}
, respectively, with
g(\tau_{t})=1
.
Logistic bandits have been studied extensively, whereas Poisson bandits have only been studied recently
(
Lee et al. 2024a
)
.
In both cases, as they are self-concordant, a minimax optimal rate of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d\sqrt{\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}}\right)
is known. For logistic bandits specifically, this rate is known to be instance-wise (
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
-wise) minimax-optimal
(
Abeille et al. 2021
, Theorem 2)
.

Note that prior analyses, including both upper and lower bounds, have been conducted separately: either
\tau
is time-varying with
\mu(z)=z
, or
\tau
is fixed with a self-concordant
\mu
.
Moreover, as we detail in Table
1
and
\Cref
app:comparison, adversarial corruptions have not been extensively studied in these combined settings, and, in particular, their leading (corruption-free) term is not instance-wise minimax-optimal.
Our goal is to treat these scenarios in a unified manner with a single, computationally efficient algorithm.

It is important to clarify the use of the term “heteroskedastic” in the bandit literature, which implies that the conditional variance of the reward
r_{t}
is not bounded by a constant but varies heterogeneously.
In the aforementioned line of work on heteroskedastic linear bandits (
\Cref
example:linear), the source of this heterogeneity is exogenous: an adversary explicitly assigns different levels of variance regardless of the learner’s choice of arm
{\bm{x}}_{t}
.
On the other hand,
GLB
s with nonlinear
\mu
with fixed
\tau
(e.g.,
\Cref
example:logistic) are endogenously heteroskedastic, as shown in Eqn. (
3
); for instance, in the Bernoulli case, the choice of
{\bm{x}}_{t}
directly affects the variance of
r_{t}
, even when
\tau_{t}=1
.
In this work, we consider both settings as a
heteroskedastic GLB
.

Adversarial Corruptions.

We further incorporate
adversarial corruptions
into the setting described above.
At each time
t
, once the learner has chosen arm
{\bm{x}}_{t}
and the corresponding reward
r_{t}
is sampled by the environment from
GLM(\cdot\mid{\bm{x}}_{t},{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}\tau_{t}};{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})
, an adaptive adversary (having observed both
{\bm{x}}_{t}
and
r_{t}
) chooses a corruption level
c_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and reveals the corrupted reward
\tilde{r}_{t}:=r_{t}+c_{t}
to the learner.
The learner observes neither the true reward
r_{t}
nor whether the observed
\tilde{r}_{t}
is corrupted (or by how much).
The
corruption budget
is defined as
C:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}|c_{t}|
as in
He et al. 2022
, and we distinguish between scenarios where the learner has knowledge of
C
and where they do not.

Remark 3

(Different Adversaries)
.

We emphasize the distinction between the two adversaries: the adversary controlling
\tau_{t}
is
oblivious
, meaning the sequence must be determined prior to the interaction, whereas the adversary controlling corruption is
adaptive
, capable of selecting the corruption level at each time step after observing the realization of the current round.
Moreover,
\tau_{t}
must be revealed at each timestep
t
, while the corruption
c_{t}
is never revealed to the learner.

The literature on bandits with corruption has grown steadily, ranging from unstructured multi-armed bandits
(
Lykouris et al. 2018
;
Guan et al. 2020
;
Gupta et al. 2019
)
to (generalized) linear bandits
(
Zhao et al. 2021
;
Bogunovic et al. 2020
;
Bogunovic et al. 2021
;
Ding et al. 2022
;
He et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2024a
;
Yu et al. 2025
)
, dueling bandits
(
Agarwal et al. 2021
;
Saha and Gaillard 2022
;
Di et al. 2025
)
, Lipschitz bandit
(
Kang et al. 2023
)
, graphical bandits
(
Lu et al. 2021
)
, Gaussian process bandits
(
Bogunovic et al. 2020
)
,
and contextual bandits with general function approximation
(
Ye et al. 2023
)
.
The primary theoretical objective is to additively separate the leading regret term and the corruption term
C
, targeting regret bounds of the form
\widetilde{\Theta}(d\sqrt{T}+dC)
.
Ideally, we seek additive dependencies on the corruption budget
C
(up to other dependencies such as
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}
) while simultaneously obtaining instance-wise optimal bounds (e.g.,
\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}
or
\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}
) when
C=0
.
We note that
none
of the prior works on corruption-robust GLM bandits achieve this “best-of-both-worlds” result.

From an algorithmic viewpoint, most prior algorithms require some form of batch-wise maximum likelihood estimator (MLE), which incurs
{\mathcal{O}}(t)
time and space complexity per time
t
.
Despite numerous algorithmic advances in
GLB
s that use online-learning-based estimators
(
Faury et al. 2022
;
Zhang and Sugiyama 2023
;
Lee and Oh 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
with sublinear complexity, these advances have not yet been extended to the adversarial corruption literature.

1.2
Contributions

The aforementioned discussions lead to the following central question:

Can we design a computationally
efficient
algorithm for (self-concordant)
heteroskedastic GLBs with adversarial corruptions
that
generically
attains instance-wise minimax-optimal regret bounds?

We answer this question affirmatively, almost.
Our contributions are as follows:

1.

We propose
HCW-GLB-OMD
, a Hessian-based confidence-weighted (HCW) version of the recently proposed
GLB-OMD

(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
.
Despite its simplicity, we show that leading-term-wise, it attains the following regret bound:

\textup{{Reg}}(T)\lesssim_{\log}d\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}}+(d^{2}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g_{\max}}+dC){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa},

(6)

for
any
self-concordant
heteroskedastic GLBs with adversarial corruptions

(
\Cref
theorem:regret)
.

•

As shown in Table
1
, where we collect prior arts and our results, it is clear that our regret bound
simultaneously
attains the tightest regret upper bounds for various instances (logistic/Poisson bandits, heteroskedastic linear bandits).

•

HCW-GLB-OMD
only requires
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
times and space complexities per round, making it the most efficient algorithm out of prior corruption-robust algorithms.

2.

We demonstrate the optimality of our regret upper bound by proving a unified lower bound of
\tilde{\Omega}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}}+dC\right)
for any self-concordant
heteroskedastic GLBs with corruptions

(
\Cref
thm:lower-bound,thm:lower-bound-corruption)
.
This shows that, up to logarithmic factors and a factor of
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}
in the corruption term, our algorithm is instance-wise minimax-optimal.
Our lower bound subsumes various prior problem-specific lower bounds, such as logistic bandits
(
Abeille et al. 2021
, Theorem 2)
, heteroskedastic linear bandits
(
He and Gu 2025
, Theorem 4.1)
, and corrupted linear bandits
(
Bogunovic et al. 2021
, Theorem 3)
.

1.3
Notation

For real numbers
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote
a\wedge b\coloneqq\min\{a,b\}
and
a\vee b\coloneqq\max\{a,b\}
.
For any integer
T\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote the set of integers up to
T
as
[T]\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,T\}
.
For a vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{2}
denotes its Euclidean norm. The
d
-dimensional Euclidean ball of radius
r>0
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}^{d}(r)\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{2}\leq r\}
.
The inner product of two vectors
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is written as
\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\rangle={\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}
.
Given a positive semi-definite (PSD) matrix
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, the Mahalanobis norm is defined as
\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{{\bm{A}}}\coloneqq\sqrt{{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}}
.

\succeq
denotes the Löwner ordering on the
d\times d
symmetric matrices, i.e.,
{\bm{A}}\succeq{\bm{B}}
if
{\bm{A}}-{\bm{B}}
is PSD.
Regarding asymptotic notation,
f(n)=\mathcal{O}(g(n))
or
f(n)\lesssim g(n)
denotes that there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that
f(n)\leq Cg(n)
.
We write
f(n)=\Theta(g(n))
when
f(n)=\mathcal{O}(g(n))
and
g(n)=\mathcal{O}(f(n))
; that is, there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that
C_{1}g(n)\leq f(n)\leq C_{2}g(n)
.
Finally, the notations
f(n)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(g(n))
(or
f(n)\lesssim_{\log}g(n)
) and
f(n)=\tilde{\Theta}(g(n))
hide polylogarithmic factors, meaning
f(n)=\mathcal{O}(g(n)\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(n))
and
f(n)=\Theta(g(n)\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(n))
, respectively.

2
Our Algorithm: HCW-GLB-OMD

We present our algorithm,
Hessian-Confidence Weighted GLB-OMD (HCW-GLB-OMD)
, whose pseudocode is provided in
\Cref
algorithm:CW-GLB-OMD.
This is a weighted variant of
GLB-OMD

(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
, utilizing a weighting scheme inspired by the
CW-OFUL
of
He et al. 2022
.
However, from a technical standpoint, several subtleties require careful attention to ensure the validity of the confidence sequence and the subsequent proof of the regret bound, which is our main technical contribution.
Throughout, we assume that
C
is known, and we defer discussion of the case where
C
is unknown to
\Cref
app:unknown-C.

2.1
Brief Description of the Algorithm

Algorithmically, the primary distinction from
GLB-OMD
of
Zhang et al. 2025b
lies in the Hessian-based confidence weighting.
Specifically, once
{\bm{x}}_{t}
is selected and a corrupted reward
\tilde{r}_{t}
is observed, the learner assigns a confidence weight
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{t}}=\min\{1,{\alpha g(\tau_{t})}/{\lVert{\bm{x}}_{t}\rVert_{{\bm{H}}_{t}^{-1}}}\}
to the negative likelihood function (Eqn. (
2.1
)), where
\lVert{\bm{x}}_{t}\rVert_{{\bm{H}}_{t}^{-1}}/g(\tau_{t})
quantifies the uncertainty in the direction of
{\bm{x}}_{t}
relative to the dispersion of reward distribution,
g(\tau_{t})
.
When
\mu(z)=z
,
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{t}}
is the same as that proposed by
He et al. 2022
for
linear
bandits with adversarial corruptions; for nonlinear
\mu
,
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{t}}
is curvature-dependent, following the standard recipe for obtaining curvature-dependent regret bounds
(
Abeille et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
.
By design,
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{t}}
adaptively discounts observations from directions with high uncertainty, which are more susceptible to adversarial noise.
The learner updates the online estimator
{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}
via online mirror descent (OMD)
(
Beck and Teboulle 2003
;
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Orabona 2025
)
, while the Hessian matrix
{\bm{H}}_{t+1}
is updated cumulatively and
weighted
by
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{t}}
.

{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{t}}\approx 1
indicates that the direction of
{\bm{x}}_{t}
has been explored sufficiently, implying the observation is reliable enough to be fully incorporated.
Indeed, when
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{t}}=1
, we recover the standard likelihood loss, implying that the observed corrupted reward is deemed trustworthy.
Conversely, a low
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{t}}
suppresses the influence of arm
{\bm{x}}_{t}
in directions where the learner remains uncertain.
The parameter
\alpha>0
determines the threshold for this trust, for which we provide a theoretically optimal choice to ensure the desired regret bounds (
\Cref
theorem:regret).
This mechanism ensures that the estimator remains stable under adversarial corruptions, while adapting to the problem’s curvature.

2.3
Proof Sketch of
\Cref
theorem:confidence-sequence: Corruption-Robust Confidence Sequence

Technical Challenges.

Given our algorithmic design, our analysis largely follows the proof framework of
Zhang et al. 2025b
. We begin with the standard one-step lemma for OMD
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
, Lemma 4)
, which isolates the so-called “inverse regret” term. This term is subsequently decomposed by adding and subtracting mix losses
(
Vovk 1998
;
Vovk 2001
;
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
)
; the first resulting term is bounded via Ville’s inequality
(
Ville 1939
)
, while the second term (the gap between the cumulative mix loss and the cumulative loss of the OMD estimators) is bounded by selecting an appropriate distribution for the mix loss, specifically a normal distribution with the estimator as mean and inverse Hessian as covariance, following
Zhang et al. 2025b
.

However, several subtleties prevent a trivial extension of existing proof techniques.
First, because our likelihoods are computed with respect to
corrupted
rewards, it is essential to meticulously track the corruption-related terms throughout the OMD analysis and ensure they remain additively separable.
Moreover, many of the aforementioned steps (e.g., the definition of the mix losses, the application of Ville’s inequality) require careful modification and verification to accommodate our
weighted
likelihood losses
\tilde{\ell}_{s}
.
We also note that prior work on corrupted bandits typically relies on exact MLE computation
(
He et al. 2022
;
Ye et al. 2023
;
Yu et al. 2025
)
, whereas our algorithm does not.
Finally, bounding the residual corruption term requires a technique not covered by
Zhang et al. 2025b
.
We achieve this by solving a maximum-involved self-bounding inequality (Eqn. (
16
)), a technique that may be of independent technical interest.

Proof Sketch.

We now provide a sketch of the proof, divided into three steps.

Step (i): Decomposing the estimation error term.

We begin by noting that, due to the specific form of the GLM (Eqn. (
2
)), the difference of weighted likelihoods can be decomposed as:

\tilde{\ell}_{s}({\bm{\theta}})-\tilde{\ell}_{s}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})={\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}\ell_{s}({\bm{\theta}})-{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}\ell_{s}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})+\frac{{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{s})}}c_{s}\big\langle{\bm{x}}_{s},{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\theta}}\big\rangle,

(12)

where
\ell_{s}({\bm{\theta}}):=\frac{m(\langle{\bm{x}}_{s},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle)-r_{s}\langle{\bm{x}}_{s},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle}{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{s})}}
is the negative
unweighted
log-likelihood of the
true
reward
r_{s}
.

Combining this with the standard one-step lemma for OMD and self-concordance control
(
\Cref
lemma:inverse-regret)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rVert_{{\bm{H}}_{t+1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\eta\underbrace{\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}{\ell}_{s}({\bm{\theta}}_{\star})-\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}{\ell}_{s}({\bm{\theta}}_{s+1})\right)}_{(*)}+4\lambda S^{2}-\frac{2}{3}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{s}-{\bm{\theta}}_{s+1}\rVert_{{\bm{H}}_{s}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\eta\underbrace{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{s})}}c_{s}\langle{\bm{x}}_{s},{\bm{\theta}}_{s+1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle}_{(**)}.

(13)

Note that the corruption-related term
(**)
is now completely additively separated from the true reward-related term
(*)
. In
Zhang et al. 2025b
, the term
(*)
(without the weights) is referred to as the “inverse regret.”

Step (ii): Bounding the inverse regret
(*)
.

Because the weights
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}
may be strictly less than
1
, we introduce a weighted version of the mix loss.
We define the weighted mix loss directly, rather than inside the exponential:

(*)=\underbrace{\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}{\ell}_{s}({\bm{\theta}}_{\star})-\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}m_{s}(P_{s})}_{\triangleq Y_{t}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}m_{s}(P_{s})-\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}{\ell}_{s}({\bm{\theta}}_{s+1})}_{\triangleq Z_{t}},

(14)

where the mix-loss is defined as
m_{s}(P_{s})=-\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim P_{s}}\left[\exp(-\ell_{s}({\bm{\theta}}))\right]\right)
, and we set
P_{s}=\mathcal{N}({\bm{\theta}}_{s},\gamma{\bm{H}}_{s}^{-1})
for some
\gamma>0
to be determined later.

Consider the stochastic process
M_{t}\triangleq\exp(Y_{t})
, which is essentially the likelihood ratio of the observations up to time
t
between the true model
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}
and a “mixture” distribution
{\bm{\theta}}\sim P
,
weighted (exponentiated)
by
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}
.
If
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}=1
, this is precisely a martingale, which is a well-known fact in the likelihood ratio-based confidence sequence literature
(
Emmenegger et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2024a
;
Kirschner et al. 2025
)
, dating back at least 50 years in the classical statistics literature
(
Darling and Robbins 1967a
;
Darling and Robbins 1967b
;
Robbins and Siegmund 1972
;
Lai 1976
)
.
However, in our case, it may be that
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}<1
, in which case the martingale property does not hold, which deviates from the original proof by
Zhang et al. 2025b
.
Here, the critical observation is that the mapping
x\mapsto x^{{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}}
is concave in the domain
x\in[0,\infty)
.
Thus, by the Jensen’s inequality
1
1

1

We consider its “expectation version”:
\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\leq f(\mathbb{E}[X])
for any concave
f
and random variable
X
.
and Fubini’s theorem to deal with the mixture distribution
P_{s}
, we show that
(M_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is a supermartingale!

(
\Cref
lemma:mixture)

Thus, by applying Ville’s inequality
(
Ville 1939
)
, we have that
{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{t}\leq\log\frac{1}{\delta},\ \forall t\geq 1)\geq 1-\delta
.
The term
Z_{t}
is bounded analogously to
Zhang et al. 2025b
, as their arguments easily extend to the case with weights
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}

(
\Cref
lemma:mixture-ineq)
.
Consequently, we show that
2\eta(*)+4\lambda S^{2}-\frac{2}{3}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{s}-{\bm{\theta}}_{s+1}\rVert_{{\bm{H}}_{s}}^{2}\leq\beta_{t+1}(\delta)^{2}
for all
t\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, where
\beta_{t+1}(\delta)
is defined in
\Cref
theorem:confidence-sequence.

Step (iii): Bounding the corruption term
(**)
via self-bounding inequality.

Substituting the bounds back into Eqn. (
13
), we have:

\displaystyle\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rVert_{{\bm{H}}_{t+1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{t+1}(\delta)^{2}+2\eta\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}}{g(\tau_{s})}c_{s}\left\lVert{\bm{x}}_{s}\right\rVert_{{\bm{H}}_{s}^{-1}}\left\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{s+1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\right\rVert_{{\bm{H}}_{s}}

(Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{t+1}(\delta)^{2}+2\eta\alpha\sum_{s=1}^{t}c_{s}\left\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{s+1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\right\rVert_{{\bm{H}}_{s}}

(Def. of
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}

2.1

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{t+1}(\delta)^{2}+2\eta\alpha\sum_{s=1}^{t}c_{s}\underbrace{\left\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{s+1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\right\rVert_{{\bm{H}}_{s+1}}}_{\triangleq X_{s+1}}

(
{\bm{H}}_{s+1}\succeq{\bm{H}}_{s}
)

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{t+1}(\delta)^{2}+2\eta\alpha C\max_{s\in[t+1]}X_{s}.

(15)

This yields a maximum-involved
self-bounding inequality

X_{t}^{2}\leq\beta_{t}(\delta)^{2}+2\eta\alpha C\max_{s\in[t]}X_{s}.

(16)

For each
t\geq 1

\zeta_{t}:=\argmax_{s\in[t]}X_{s}

Then:

X_{\zeta_{t}}^{2}\leq\beta_{\zeta_{t}}(\delta)^{2}+2\eta\alpha C\max_{s\in[\zeta_{t}]}X_{s}=\beta_{\zeta_{t}}(\delta)^{2}+2\eta\alpha CX_{\zeta_{t}}.

This is a quadratic inequality in

\beta_{\zeta_{t}}(\delta)\leq\beta_{t}(\delta)
(as

Remark 6

(Necessity of Online Estimator)
.

The proof sketch above underscores a critical design choice: the necessity of the online estimator, not only for computational efficiency but also for statistical validity in the presence of corruption.
If we were to use a batched MLE approach as in
Abeille et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2024a
;
Lee et al. 2024b
, we would analyze the difference
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t})
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}({\bm{\theta}})\coloneq-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\tilde{\ell}_{s}({\bm{\theta}})
.
This leads to a cross-term of the form
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}c_{s}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\bm{x}}_{s}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}}-{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle
, which mixes time steps
s
and
t
.
This “subscript disagreement” is problematic: while the weight
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}
is determined using local information at time step
s
(specifically
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\bm{x}}_{s}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{s}}
), the global estimate
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}}
has no explicit relationship with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\bm{x}}_{s}}
.
This disagreement prevents further analysis of the corruption’s impact on the global estimator; for instance, properties such as
{\bm{H}}_{s+1}\succeq{\bm{H}}_{s}
cannot be exploited.
By using an
online estimator
, we resolve this issue by replacing the global term with the local update
{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}c_{s}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\bm{x}}_{s}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\bm{\theta}}_{s}}-{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle
. This allows us to bound the corruption term locally while fully exploiting the curvature of the link function via the accumulated Hessian
{\bm{H}}_{s}
.

3
A Unified Regret Lower Bound

We now complement our regret upper bound with a unified lower bound for any instance of self-concordant
heteroskedastic GLB with adversarial corruptions
.
We denote
\textup{{Reg}}^{\pi}\left(T;\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\star},\{\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}\right)
as the regret of an algorithm
\pi
under the instance with
\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\star}
and arm-set sequence
\{\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
.

We begin by establishing the following instance-specific, corruption-free lower bound:

Theorem 7
.

Let
{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and a sequence of dispersion parameters
\{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}\tau_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
be given.
There exist two absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for any algorithm
\pi
, there exists a parameter
\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\star}\in{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\star})
where

{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\star})\triangleq\left\{{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(S):\left\lVert\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}_{\star}\right\rVert_{2}\leq c_{1}\sqrt{\frac{L_{\mu}\lceil\log T\rceil}{T}}\right\}
,
and a sequence of arm sets
\{\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
with
\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(1)
, such that the following holds:
denoting
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t,\star}}=\argmax_{{\bm{x}}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}}\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle
, provided that
\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}(\langle\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t,\star},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}\geq 4d^{2}L_{\mu}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g_{\max}}
, then

\textup{{Reg}}^{\pi}\left(T;\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\star},\{\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}\right)\geq\frac{c_{2}d\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}(\langle\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t,\star},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}}}{\lceil\log T\rceil}.

(18)

Proof Sketch.

We provide the full detailed proof in
\Cref
app:proof-lower-bound.
The key observation is that for each fixed
\tau
, the local minimax lower bound of
Abeille et al. 2021
extends to generic, self-concordant
\mu(\cdot)
, with the only difference being the bounding of the KL-divergence: instead of relying on the closed-form
\chi^{2}
-divergence specific to Bernoulli distributions, we utilize the local quadratic geometry of the KL-divergence for GLMs and generic self-concordance tools
(
Lee et al. 2024b
;
Lee et al. 2024a
)
.
Then, we apply the peeling technique recently introduced by
He and Gu 2025
to handle the time-varying dispersions
{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}
.
∎

We now establish the following corruption-dependent lower bound:

Theorem 8
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
C>0
.
There exists an absolute constant
c_{3}>0
such that for any bandit algorithm
\pi
, there exist
\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(1)
,
\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}_{\star}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(S)
, and an adaptive adversary with corruption budget
C
such that for

4
Conclusion and Future Work

Conclusion.

In this work, we investigated self-concordant,
heteroskedastic GLBs with adversarial corruptions
.
We proposed
HCW-GLB-OMD
, a computationally efficient algorithm requiring only
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
space and time complexity per round while achieving a regret upper bound of
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d\sqrt{\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}}+(d^{2}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g_{\max}}+dC){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}\right)
.
We complemented this with a unified lower bound of
\tilde{\Omega}(d\sqrt{\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}}+dC)
, demonstrating that our algorithm
simultaneously
attains optimality (up to
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}
in the corruption term) across various scenarios, including logistic, Poisson, and heteroskedastic linear bandits.

Future Work.

Beyond the directions discussed in the main text, several other avenues warrant further investigation.
First, relaxing our self-concordance assumption (
\Cref
assumption:link-function) to the generalized notion proposed by
Liu et al. 2024b
would extend our framework to a broader class of GLMs, including the Gamma and Inverse Gaussian distributions.
Second, our current framework relies on the assumption that the learner observes the exact dispersion parameter
{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}\tau_{t}}
, constituting a notable limitation.
This assumption is technically critical, as our current analysis hinges on the weighted true likelihood ratio forming a supermartingale (see
\Cref
lemma:mixture
in the proof of our confidence sequence).
Unknown or misspecified
{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}\tau_{t}}
’s result in (online) misspecified MLEs, necessitating distinct analytical tools
(
White 1982
;
Robins et al. 1994
;
Walker 2013
;
Fortunati et al. 2017
)
.
Consequently, developing algorithms that are robust to such misspecification, or agnostic to
{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}\tau_{t}}
– paralleling advances in heteroskedastic linear bandits
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2023a
)
– remains a significant open challenge.
We also hope that some of our technical insights will facilitate algorithmic and theoretical advances in
online
RLHF under adversarial corruptions, an area of increasing practical interest
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Entezami and Naseh 2025
;
Kusaka et al. 2025
)
.
Indeed, existing work on ’robust RLHF’ has largely been confined to random noise models
(
Chowdhury et al. 2024
;
Liang et al. 2025
)
, semi-adversarial corruption in the preference model (where the adversary perturbs the logits rather than arbitrarily flipping labels)
(
Bukharin et al. 2024
)
, or strong (Huber-type) adversaries in
offline
settings
(
Mandal et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2025
)
.

Appendix A
Dealing with Unknown Corruption Budget
C

In our main analysis, we assumed knowledge of the corruption budget
C
. Here, we briefly discuss the feasibility and limitations of relaxing this assumption.

Model Selection.

From a statistical perspective, the knowledge of
C
is not strictly necessary to achieve the regret bound in
\Cref
theorem:regret.
Our derived bound depends linearly on
C
(specifically, in the form
\sqrt{\beta_{1}T}+\beta_{2}C+\beta_{3}
), which satisfies the affine structure required by the model selection framework of
Wei et al. 2022
.
Therefore, by invoking
Wei et al. 2022
, one can employ a meta-algorithm to achieve the same regret guarantee—up to additional polylogarithmic factors—without prior knowledge of
C
.
However, we note that this statistical optimality comes at a significant computational cost, as their meta-algorithm
(
Wei et al. 2022
, Algorithm 2;
COBE
)
requires maintaining multiple parallel instances of the base learner.

Hardness of Unknown
C
.

Alternatively, one might attempt to set a conservative budget estimate, for instance,
\overline{C}\asymp{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{-1}}\sqrt{\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}}
.
While this suffices when the true corruption satisfies
C\leq\overline{C}
, handling arbitrary unknown corruption is fundamentally hard.
As established in the context of stochastic linear bandits
(
He et al. 2022
, Theorem 4.12)
, without knowledge of
C
, any algorithm must suffer
\Omega(T)
regret if the corruption budget is too large, e.g.,
C=\Omega(\textup{{Reg}}(T)/d)
.
We believe that a similar linear lower bound applies to our setting as well.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
be a filtration defined by
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(\{({\bm{x}}_{s},r_{s},c_{s},\tau_{s})\}_{s=1}^{t-1})
. Let
\{P_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
be a stochastic process such that the random variable
P_{t}
is a distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable. Moreover assume that the loss function
\ell_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t+1}
-measurable. For any
t\geq 1

(64)

Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1]
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\forall t\geq 1,\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}\ell_{s}({\bm{\theta}}_{\star})\leq\sum_{s=1}^{t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}w_{s}}m_{s}(P_{s})+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

(65)

Proof.

We first verify that
M_{t}
is a supermartingale, and apply Ville’s inequality to this supermartingale to complete the proof.
By the definition of
M_{t}
, it can be written as:

\displaystyle M_{t}

\displaystyle=M_{t-1}\exp\left(w_{t}\ell_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{\star})-w_{t}m_{t}(P_{t})\right)=M_{t-1}\left[\exp\left(\ell_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{\star})-m_{t}(P_{t})\right)\right]^{w_{t}}.

(66)

Note that
\exp(-\ell_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))=p(r_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}})
and
\exp(-m_{t}(P_{t}))=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim P_{t}}[p(r_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}})]
, where
p(r|{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})
denotes the conditional probability of reward
r
given selected arm
{\bm{x}}
and parameter
{\bm{\theta}}
(see Eqn. (
2
)).
Here, the crucial observation (as mentioned in the main text) is that the mapping
x\mapsto x^{w_{t}}
is concave in the domain
x\in[0,\infty)
as
w_{t}\in[0,1]
, and thus we can utilize the expectation version of Jensen’s inequality
(
Jensen 1906
)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]

\displaystyle=M_{t-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim P_{t}}[p(r_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}})]}{p(r_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})}\right)^{w_{t}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

(67)

\displaystyle\leq M_{t-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim P_{t}}[p(r_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}})]}{p(r_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right)^{w_{t}}

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle=M_{t-1}\left(\int\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim P_{t}}[p(r|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}})]}{p(r|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})}p(r|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})dr\right)^{w_{t}}

(68)

\displaystyle=M_{t-1}\left(\int\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim P_{t}}[p(r|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}})]dr\right)^{w_{t}}

(69)

\displaystyle=M_{t-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim P_{t}}\left[\int p(r|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}})dr\right]\right)^{w_{t}}

(Fubini’s theorem)

\displaystyle=M_{t-1}.

(
\int p(r|{\bm{x}}_{s},{\bm{\theta}})dr=1
)

The proof then concludes by applying Ville’s inequality
(
Ville 1939
)
to the supermartingale
(M_{t})_{t\geq 1}
.
∎

Appendix E
Proof of
\Cref
thm:lower-bound-corruption: Corruption-Dependent Lower Bound

This proof relies on the construction and reduction arguments established in
Bogunovic et al. 2021
, which dates back to
Lykouris et al. 2018
and
Auer and Chiang 2016
.

Construction.

We consider a set of
d
arms
\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{d-1}\}\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{d}(1)
, defined as
{\bm{x}}_{i}={\bm{e}}_{1}\cos\phi+{\bm{e}}_{i+1}\sin\phi
for
i\in[d-1]
for some small angle
\phi\in(0,\pi/2)
.
Note that for any distinct
i,j\in[d]
, the inner product is
\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j}\rangle=\cos^{2}\phi
.
We define the corresponding set of potential parameters
\Xi:=\{\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(1)},\dots,\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(d-1)}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(S)
, where
\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(i)}=S{\bm{x}}_{i}
for a large scalar
S>0
.

We construct
d
distinct bandit instances. In the
i
-th instance, the ground-truth parameter is
\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(i)}
and the arm set is
\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. The expected rewards satisfy:

•

The optimal arm
{\bm{x}}_{i}
yields
\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(i)}\rangle)=\mu(S)
.

•

Any suboptimal arm
{\bm{x}}_{j}
(
j\neq i
) yields
\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{j},\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(i)}\rangle)=\mu(S\cos^{2}\phi)
.

The instantaneous regret of pulling any suboptimal arm is
\Delta\triangleq\mu(S)-\mu(S\cos^{2}\phi)
.
Intuitively, for sufficiently large
S
and
\phi\approx 0
, we have
\Delta\approx\dot{\mu}(S)S\sin^{2}\phi\approx{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}\sin^{2}\phi
, which may be exponentially small in
S
(e.g., for the logistic link).
Following
Bogunovic et al. 2021
, we assume a noiseless scenario where the learner observes the expected reward directly.
3
3

3

For settings where a purely noiseless observation is invalid (e.g., Bernoulli or Poisson rewards), this argument proceeds identically by replacing the additive corruption with a coupling strategy that perfectly simulates the suboptimal distribution. Specific coupling strategies (of the adversary) for logistic and Poisson bandits are provided in
\Cref
app:logistic and
E.2
, respectively, for completeness.

Adversary’s Strategy.

We consider the following adaptive adversary: in the
i
-th instance, whenever the learner pulls the optimal arm
{\bm{x}}_{i}
, the adversary introduces a negative corruption of
-\Delta
. If the learner pulls a suboptimal arm, the adversary introduces no corruption.
Under this strategy, as long as the corruption budget permits, the observed reward for
any
arm
j\in[d-1]
is identically
\mu(S\cos^{2}\phi)
for
any
bandit instance.
Consequently, the learner’s observation history provides no statistical signal to distinguish the true instance
\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(i)}
from any other instance
\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{(j)}
.

Lower Bound.

The adversary possesses a total budget
C
. The cost to corrupt a single pull of the optimal arm is
\Delta
. Thus, the adversary can sustain this deception for up to
N=\lfloor C/\Delta\rfloor
pulls of the optimal arm.

This problem reduces to identifying the unique optimal arm among
d
candidates.
Crucially, until an arm is pulled
N
times, the adversary can perfectly simulate the suboptimal reward distribution, rendering the observation history statistically identical to that of a suboptimal arm.
Therefore, the learner cannot distinguish the hypothesis “arm
j
is optimal” from “arm
j
is suboptimal” without pulling it at least
N
times.
To identify the true instance with constant probability under a uniform prior, any algorithm must inherently “check” a constant fraction of the arms by pulling them
\Omega(N)
times each.

Since pulling a suboptimal arm incurs instantaneous regret
\Delta
, the total expected regret is lower bounded by:

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Xi)}[\textup{{Reg}}^{\pi}(T;\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}},\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}})]\gtrsim d\times N\times\Delta\approx d\times\frac{C}{\Delta}\times\Delta=dC.

(157)

Crucially, the curvature-dependent gap
\Delta
cancels out between the budget capacity (
N\approx C/\Delta
) and the instantaneous regret (
\Delta
), yielding a
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}
-free lower bound.
∎

E.1
Logistic Bandits with Adversarial Corruption

We adopt the same notations and instances as above, and we set
\mu(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
.

Adversary’s Strategy.

An adversary corrupts the stochastic, binary reward in a coupled manner as follows.
When the learner chooses the optimal arm and the corresponding sampled reward
r_{t}
is
1
, the adversary flips the reward to
0
with probability
q
, then reveals
0
to the learner; otherwise, the adversary does nothing.
The corruption level is
|c_{t}|=1
when corruption occurs (flipping 1 to 0).
With this adversary, the expected corrupted (observed) reward
\tilde{r}_{t}

when the optimal arm is pulled
becomes

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}_{t}]=1\cdot\mathbb{P}(r_{t}=1,\text{not flipped})=\mathbb{P}(\text{not flipped}\mid r_{t}=1)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(r_{t}=1)=(1-q)\cdot\mu(S).

(158)

Therefore, if we set
q=\Delta/\mu(S)
where
\Delta=\mu(S)-\mu(S\cos^{2}\phi)
, the reward distributions of the optimal arm (with corruption) and the suboptimal arms all become
\mathrm{Ber}(\mu(S\cos^{2}\phi))
, making them statistically indistinguishable.

Lower Bound.

Unlike the deterministic case, when we consider the randomness of the reward, the number of pulls needed to identify if the arm
j
is optimal or not is a random variable.
We denote this random variable by
N_{j}\coloneqq\inf\{t:\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}\geq C\}
, where
X_{s}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathrm{Ber}(q)
.
By definition, up to time
N_{j}
, the learner cannot determine whether arm
j
is optimal.
Let
T_{0}
be a deterministic threshold such that
N_{j}\geq T_{0}
with a non-vanishing probability, to be specified at the end.

Now, following the arguments in
Bogunovic et al. 2021
, after
(d-1)T_{0}/2
rounds, at least
(d-1)/2
arms are not pulled
T_{0}
times.
For an instance whose optimal arm is included in this group, suboptimal arms are pulled
(d-1)T_{0}/2-T_{0}
times, incurring instantaneous regret of
\Delta
.
Therefore, the regret is of order
\Omega(dT_{0}\Delta)
.

We set as
T_{0}=C/(2q)=C\cdot\mu(S)/(2\Delta)=\Omega(C/\Delta)
, which then implies that by Hoeffding’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(N_{j}\geq T_{0})\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{T_{0}}X_{s}\leq C\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{2(C-qT_{0})^{2}}{T_{0}}\right)=1-\exp(-Cq).

(159)

Here, we assume that
C\gtrsim\mu(S)/\Delta
, which is our region of interest.
Thus, with the same probability, the regret is lower bounded as

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Xi)}[\textup{{Reg}}^{\pi}(T;\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}},\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}})]=\Omega(dT_{0}\Delta)=\Omega(dC).

(160)

∎

F.1

In this section, we provide a detailed discussion comparing our results against the specific prior works listed in Table
1
.
We note that our
HCW-GLB-OMD
inherits all the computational benefits of using an online estimator
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
: it is one-pass, relying on recursive updates and a single projection onto the convex set
\Theta
.
Our algorithm only requires
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
space and time per iteration
t

heteroskedastic GLBs with adversarial corruptions
.

No Corruptions (
C=0
).

In the absence of corruption, we immediately recover the existing state-of-the-art regret bounds:
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d\sqrt{\sum_{t}\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)
for heteroskedastic linear bandits
(
Zhou and Gu 2022
, Theorem 4.1)
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d\sqrt{\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}}\right)
for self-concordant
GLB

(
Lee et al. 2024a
, Theorem 4.1)
.
Indeed, as reflected in the nomenclature, when

, our algorithm effectively reduces to the
GLB-OMD
algorithm of
Zhang et al. 2025b
.

With Corruption, Linear (
\mu(z)=z
).

In this setting, we obtain
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d\sqrt{\sum_{t}\sigma_{t}^{2}}+dC\right)
, which exactly matches the state-of-the-art result by
Yu et al. 2025
.
We note that
Yu et al. 2025
is the only prior work to achieve a variance-aware regret bound for linear bandits with adversarial corruptions.

With Corruption, Generalized Linear.

Our parametric setting (Eqn. (
2
)) generalizes the prior settings in the following sense.
Projecting the prior heteroskedastic generalized linear bandit literature (where the learner accesses the conditional variance of the played arm) onto our setting is equivalent to assuming that
{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)
is known to the learner.
Because we allow the learner to access
only

{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}
, our scenario is strictly weaker regarding the feedback model.
Furthermore, the literature on heteroskedastic linear bandits does not account for nonlinear
\mu
and, with the exception of
Yu et al. 2025
, cannot account for (adaptive) adversarial corruption either.
Despite these challenges, we show that our regret bound either subsumes or improves upon all known prior results, under self-concordance (
\Cref
assumption:link-function).

We compare our results against
Yu et al. 2025
in our parametric GLBs setting, i.e., assuming that the reward follows the following distribution:

dp(r\mid{\bm{x}},\tau;{\bm{\theta}}_{\star})\propto\exp\left(\frac{r\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle-m(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}{g(\tau)}\right)d\nu,

(163)

where the link function satisfies
\Cref
assumption:link-function.
In the setting of
Yu et al. 2025
, at each timestep
t
, the learner pulls an arm
{\bm{x}}_{t}
and observes a corrupted reward
\tilde{r}_{t}=\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)+\epsilon_{t}+c_{t}
, where
\epsilon_{t}
is a martingale difference noise with
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{t}^{2}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}]\leq\nu_{t}^{2}
and
c_{t}
is the adversarial corruption.
Similar to our setting,
Yu et al. 2025
assumes that the learner explicitly observes
\nu_{t}
at the end of each round
t
.
Their regret bound
(
Yu et al. 2025
, Corollary 5)
is of order
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}\sqrt{\sum_{t}\nu_{t}^{2}}+d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}C\right)
.
Here, the comparison is more nuanced due to the distinction between exogenous and endogenous heteroskedasticity, as elaborated in
\Cref
sec:setting.
Indeed, as
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{t}^{2}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}]={\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}
, we divide their regret bound into two scenarios depending on how much “information” available in
\nu_{t}^{2}
.

First, when the endogenous heteroskedasticity
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}
is completely unobserved, the only viable observed quantity is that the learner observes
\nu_{t}^{2}=L_{\mu}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}
, where we naïvely upper bound
\dot{\mu}
by
L_{\mu}
.
With this, their regret bound reduces to
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}\sqrt{\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}}+d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}C\right)
.
For heteroskedastic linear bandits (with linear
\mu
), this bound is tight.
However, for nonlinear
\mu
(e.g., logistic or Poisson bandits), this bound is quite loose; the leading term’s dependence on the true, instance-dependent variance
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}
is lost, and the leading term scales directly with
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}
.

Second, suppose that
\nu_{t}^{2}={\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)
is observed by the learner; we remark that we do
not
require this.
Then, their regret bound becomes
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}\sqrt{\sum_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)}+d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}C\right)
.
As
{\bm{x}}_{t}
’s are algorithm-dependent, this alone is not a proper regret bound.
Utilizing the self-concordance arguments as in
\Cref
app:proof-regret, we can further upper bound the algorithm-dependent term as
\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}+{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g_{\max}}R_{s}\textup{{Reg}}^{\mathrm{Yu}}(T).

This then yields the following recursive inequality:

\textup{{Reg}}^{\mathrm{Yu}}(T)\lesssim_{\log}d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}+{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g_{\max}}\textup{{Reg}}^{\mathrm{Yu}}(T)}+d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}C.

(164)

Solving the inequality for
\textup{{Reg}}^{\mathrm{Yu}}(T)
via
\Cref
lemma:solve-ineq yields

\textup{{Reg}}^{\mathrm{Yu}}\lesssim_{\log}d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\dot{\mu}_{t,\star}}}+d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}C+d^{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g_{\max}.

(165)

Note that the leading term directly scales with the global curvature
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}
, and we again remark that this is obtainable only when the learner observes
\nu_{t}^{2}={\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}g(\tau_{t})}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{\star}\rangle)
.
The dependency on
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}
in the leading term is due to the fact that
GAdaOFUL
constructs a curvature-agnostic confidence sequence whose radius scales with
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}
.

Remark 18
.

We acknowledge that the results of
Yu et al. 2025
apply to generic link functions without requiring self-concordance, assuming only that the noise has finite variance (allowing for heavy tails), and also that their work only requires an upper bound on the conditional variance of the noise.
Thus, their results are more general in scope.
However, we demonstrate that under the mild assumption of self-concordance (
\Cref
assumption:link-function), it is possible to achieve significantly tighter, instance-wise minimax-optimal bounds.
We also remark that a similar regret bound can be derived for
MOR-UCB

(
Zhao et al. 2023b
)
, although their algorithm requires that the reward (noise) is bounded a.s.

With Corruption, General Function Approximation.

Finally, we compare our results with
CR-Eluder-UCB
of
Ye et al. 2023
, which is designed for general function approximation
(
Foster and Rakhlin 2023
)
with adversarial corruptions.
While this setting is more general, the regret bound scales with the eluder dimension
(
Russo and Van Roy 2013
;
Osband and Van Roy 2014
)
, which represents the worst-case complexity of the function class.
When instantiated to GLMs, the eluder dimension scales with
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}
.
Consequently, the instantiated regret bound of
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}\sqrt{T}+d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}C)
(see
\Cref
app:ye for the derivation) fails to exhibit curvature/instance-wise optimality, or variance-adaptivity when
\mu(z)=z
and
{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.05,0.96}\tau_{t}}
varies, in the leading term.

F.2
Regret Bound of
CR-Eluder-UCB
of
Ye et al. 2023

Here, we instantiate the regret bound of
CR-Eluder-UCB

(
Ye et al. 2023
, Theorem 4.1)
within the context of
GLB
with adversarial corruption.
Consider the function class
{\mathcal{F}}=\{{\bm{x}}\mapsto\mu({\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}}):\lVert{\bm{\theta}}\rVert_{2}\leq S\}
, then this corresponds to our setting with a fixed dispersion parameter
\tau_{t}=\tau
.

While their upper bound may be optimal for some function classes, it becomes loose when applied to a parametric GLM class.
Specifically, the regret bound is give as:

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{T\dim_{E}\big({\mathcal{F}},\lambda/T\big)\log N(\gamma,{\mathcal{F}},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty})}+C\dim_{E}\big({\mathcal{F}},\lambda/T\big)\right),

(166)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
denotes infinity norm,
\lambda=\log N(\gamma,\mathcal{F},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})
and
\gamma=(TC)^{-1}
.
For GLM class, the eluder dimension and covering number scale as
\dim_{E}(\mathcal{F},\lambda/T)=\mathcal{O}(d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}\log T)
(
\Cref
lemma:eluder) and
\log N(\gamma,\mathcal{F},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty})=\mathcal{O}(d\log(TC))
(
\Cref
lemma:covering) respectively.

By substituting these values, the resulting regret order becomes
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}\sqrt{T}+d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}C)
.
This bound not only fails to exhibit instance-wise optimality as it scales linearly to the global curvature
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}
, but also suffers from a quadratic dependence on
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa}
of the corruption-dependent term.
In contrast, our approach leverages the specific parametric structure of GLMs to achieve tighter, curvature-dependent regret that is more robust to the underlying geometry of the link function.

Remark 19
.

Very recently,
Bakhtiari et al. 2025
proposed a localization approach to the eluder dimension, accompanied by a simple algorithm (
\ell
-UCB) that enables first-order regret bounds.
While extending their approach to the corrupted setting of
Ye et al. 2023
might theoretically yield desired first-order bounds, such an extension is non-trivial. Furthermore, a tractable version of
\ell
-UCB is currently only known for self-concordant
\mu
; see
Bakhtiari et al. 2025
.

Lemma 22

(Eluder dimension of GLM class)
.

Consider the GLM class
\mathcal{F}=\{{\bm{x}}\mapsto\mu({\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}}):\lVert{\bm{\theta}}\rVert_{2}\leq S\}
for
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(1)
, and let
0<\epsilon\leq 2\sqrt{2}SL_{\mu}
.
Then the
\epsilon
-eluder dimension
\dim_{E}(\mathcal{F},\epsilon)
is upper bounded as:

\dim_{E}(\mathcal{F},\epsilon)\leq 16d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}L_{\mu}^{2}\log\left(1+\frac{8S^{2}L_{\mu}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(167)

Proof.

Suppose that
\dim_{E}(\mathcal{F},\epsilon)=n
and let
\mathcal{Z}_{m}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2},\cdots,{\bm{x}}_{m}\}
be a set of
m
samples.
If
m\leq n
, then by the definition of eluder dimension,
{\bm{x}}_{m}
is
\epsilon
-independent of its predecessors.
Precisely, for any
{\bm{\theta}}_{1},{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\in\Theta
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\big(\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\rangle)-\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rangle)\big)^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2},

(168)

the vector
{\bm{x}}_{m}
satisfies
|\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{m},{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\rangle)-\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{m},{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rangle)|>\epsilon
.
Since
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{-1}}\leq\dot{\mu}\leq L_{\mu}
, it holds that
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{-1}}|\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rangle|\leq|\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\rangle)-\mu({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rangle)|
for
i\in[m]
.
Therefore, we have the inequalities

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{-2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rangle^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\big(\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\rangle)-\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rangle)\big)^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}

(169)

and

L_{\mu}|\langle{\bm{x}}_{m},{\bm{\theta}}_{1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rangle|\geq|\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{m},{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\rangle)-\mu(\langle{\bm{x}}_{m},{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rangle)|\geq\epsilon.

(170)

The two inequalities can be written as:

({\bm{\theta}}_{1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{2})^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}{\bm{x}}_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}\right)({\bm{\theta}}_{1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{2})\leq{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}\epsilon^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle{\bm{x}}_{m},{\bm{\theta}}_{1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rangle^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{L_{\mu}^{2}}.

(171)

Define
\bar{{\bm{V}}}_{t}=\lambda{\bm{I}}_{d}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}{\bm{x}}_{s}{\bm{x}}_{s}^{\top}
and note that
({\bm{\theta}}_{1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{2})^{\top}\lambda{\bm{I}}_{d}({\bm{\theta}}_{1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{2})\leq 4\lambda S^{2}
, then the first inequality becomes

\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rVert_{\bar{{\bm{V}}}_{m}}^{2}\leq{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}\epsilon^{2}+4\lambda S^{2}.

(172)

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality with respect to
\bar{{\bm{V}}}_{m}
, the second inequality becomes

\lVert{\bm{x}}_{m}\rVert_{\bar{{\bm{V}}}_{m}^{-1}}^{2}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{1}-{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rVert_{\bar{{\bm{V}}}_{m}}^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{L_{\mu}^{2}}.

(173)

Combining the last two inequalities, we have that for
m\in[d_{e}]
, where
d_{e}
is the eluder dimension, i.e., the largest number of samples that are
\epsilon
-dependent of its predecessors, the vector
{\bm{x}}_{m}
satisfies

\lVert{\bm{x}}_{m}\rVert_{\bar{{\bm{V}}}_{m}^{-1}}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{L_{\mu}^{2}}\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}+4\lambda S^{2}/\epsilon^{2}}.

(174)

Summing up for
m=1,\cdots,d_{e}
, we have

\sum_{m=1}^{d_{e}}\lVert{\bm{x}}_{m}\rVert_{\bar{{\bm{V}}}_{m}^{-1}}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{L_{\mu}^{2}}\frac{d_{e}}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}+4\lambda S^{2}/\epsilon^{2}}.

(175)

On the other hand, the elliptical potential lemma (
\Cref
lemma:epl) implies that

\sum_{m=1}^{d_{e}}\lVert{\bm{x}}_{m}\rVert_{\bar{{\bm{V}}}_{m}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 4d\log\left(1+\frac{d_{e}}{d\lambda}\right).

(176)

Since the lower bound grows linearly in
d_{e}
and the upper bound grows logarithmically in
n
, the eluder dimension
d_{e}
is bounded by the largest value of integer
x\in{\mathbb{N}}
that satisfies

4d\log\left(1+\frac{x}{d\lambda}\right)\geq\frac{1}{L_{\mu}^{2}}\frac{x}{\kappa^{2}+4\lambda S^{2}/\epsilon^{2}}

(177)

By Lemma
27
, such
x
is bounded as

x\leq 8dL_{\mu}^{2}({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}+4\lambda S^{2}/\epsilon^{2})\log\left(1+4L_{\mu}^{2}\frac{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}+4\lambda S^{2}/\epsilon^{2}}{\lambda}\right)

(178)

If we choose
\lambda=\frac{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}\epsilon^{2}}{4S^{2}}
, then we get the desired result

d_{e}\leq 16d{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\kappa^{2}}L_{\mu}^{2}\log\left(1+\frac{8S^{2}L_{\mu}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(179)

∎

(Covering number of GLM class)
.

Let
N(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty})
be the
\epsilon
-covering number of the class
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
. Then the
\epsilon
-covering number of the class
\mathcal{F}=\{{\bm{x}}\mapsto\mu({\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}}):\lVert{\bm{\theta}}\rVert_{2}\leq S\}

(180)

Proof.

{\bm{\theta}}_{1},{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\in\Theta
, by Lipschitz continuity of
\mu
and boundedness of
\Theta
and
\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{2}\leq 1

\displaystyle\left|\mu(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\rangle)-\mu(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\rangle)\right|

{\bm{x}}

\Theta

\displaystyle N(\epsilon,\mathcal{F},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty})

(183)

Lemma 24

(Self-Concordance Control, Lemmas 8 of
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
.

Let
\mu:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly increasing function satisfying
|\ddot{\mu}(z)|\leq R_{s}\cdot\dot{\mu}(z)
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, where
R_{s}\geq 0
and
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}
is a bounded interval. Then, for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
z\in\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}\dot{\mu}(z_{1}+v(z_{2}-z_{1}))dv\geq\frac{\dot{\mu}(z)}{1+R_{s}\cdot|z_{1}-z_{2}|}

(185)

and the weighted integral

\frac{\dot{\mu}(z)}{2+R_{s}\cdot|z_{1}-z_{2}|}\leq\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)\dot{\mu}(z_{1}+v(z_{2}-z_{1}))d\leq\exp\left(R_{s}^{2}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}\right)\cdot\ \dot{\mu}(z).

(186)

Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
)
.

Let
\{{\bm{x}}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{\infty}
be as sequence in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\lVert{\bm{x}}_{s}\rVert_{2}\leq X
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\lambda
be a non-negative scalar. For
t\geq 1
define
{\bm{V}}_{t}\coloneqq\lambda{\bm{I}}_{d}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}{\bm{x}}_{s}{\bm{x}}^{\top}
. Then,

\sum_{s=1}^{t}\lVert{\bm{z}}_{s}\rVert_{V_{s}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2d(1+X^{2})\log\left(1+\frac{tX^{2}}{d\lambda}\right).

(187)

)

Lemma 27
.

Let
a,b,c>0
with
ab\geq 1
.
If
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies the inequality
n\leq a\log(1+bn)
, then

n\leq 2a\log(1+ab).

Proof.

We define
f(x)=\frac{x}{\log(1+bx)}
, then

f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\log(1+bx)-\frac{bx}{1+bx}}{\log(1+bx)}.

(189)

Let
h(y)=\log(1+y)-\frac{y}{1+y}
, then
h(0)=0
and
h^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{1+y}-\frac{1}{(1+y)^{2}}=\frac{y}{(1+y)^{2}}>0
for
y>0
. Therefore,
f(x)
is increasing in
x>0
.
Since
\lim_{x\to 0+}f(x)=\frac{1}{b}
, if
1/b<a
, or
ab>1
, the existence of
n
satisfying the given inequality is guaranteed.
Now, we show that
f(2a\log(1+ab))>a
, then for
n>2a\log(1+ab)
,
n\leq a\log(1+bn)
does not hold, which implies that the maximum value of
n
satisfying the inequality is smaller than
2a\log(1+ab)
.

Substituting
x=2a\log(1+ab)
,

\displaystyle f(2a\log(1+ab))

\displaystyle=\frac{2a\log(1+ab)}{\log(1+2ab\log(1+ab))}

(190)

\displaystyle=a\frac{\log(1+ab)^{2}}{\log(1+2ab\log(1+ab))}.

(191)

If
2ab+a^{2}b^{2}>2ab\log(1+ab)
holds, we complete the proof.
If
ab>0
, the inequality is equivalent to
1+\frac{ab}{2}>\log(1+ab)
, and it holds for any
ab>0
.
∎
