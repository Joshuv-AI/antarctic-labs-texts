Discrepancies in VAS Pain Assessment Time Points and Adjustment for Multiple Testing

Trial Protocol-Specified Outcome Timings

The inconsistent wording describing VAS assessment across the Abstract, Methods and Results sections stems from editorial errors introduced during manuscript revision, rather than any deviation from the pre-specified trial protocol. We have revised the “Outcome Measurements” section as follows: “Visual analogue scale (VAS) pain scores were recorded at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 hours following femoral nerve block (FNB)”.

Outcome Measurement Schedule

Visual analogue scale (VAS) pain scores were recorded at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 hours following femoral nerve block (FNB). We additionally documented cumulative postoperative opioid consumption and all predefined adverse events, including hypotension, hypertension, respiratory depression, nausea, vomiting, and urinary retention.

Rationale for Omitting Multiplicity Adjustment

Correction for multiple comparisons is only necessary when conducting repeated pairwise analyses across multiple endpoints or time points within a single dataset. In our trial, separate independent between-group comparisons of VAS scores were performed exclusively at each individual time point, with no cross-timepoint pairwise comparisons carried out. Given the absence of repeated comparative testing, the statistical prerequisites for multiplicity correction were not satisfied, and we therefore did not implement such adjustments. Given that the primary outcome serves as the basis for definitive conclusion, all secondary endpoints should be regarded as exploratory in nature. Therefore, we designate the 10-hour VAS score as an exploratory finding.

Incomplete Reporting of Secondary Analgesic Endpoints and Statistical Reproducibility

The primary endpoint of our trial was cumulative 24-hour opioid consumption, which served as the core metric for evaluating overall postoperative analgesic efficacy. While segmented patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) interval data and the time to first rescue analgesic request were not presented in the main Results section, their omission does not compromise the validity or clinical interpretability of our key opioid-sparing findings.

Clarification of Predefined Statistical Methods

Our trial protocol pre-specified the Pearson chi-square test with Yates’ continuity correction for small-sample 2×2 categorical safety data, which was the analytical method implemented in SPSS 22.0 for all adverse event comparisons. Regrettably, we failed to explicitly state this analytical approach in the footnote of the original table, creating barriers to independent replication by readers.

Clinical Interpretation

Despite the updated corrected P value of 0.52, the comparison remains statistically non-significant. This aligns with our original safety conclusion that adjuvant perineural esketamine does not significantly alter the 24-hour postoperative incidence of PONV following total knee arthroplasty (TKA).

Safety Reporting, Blinding Design and Limitations of Mechanistic Inference

Full Disclosure of All Predefined Adverse Events

All adverse events outlined in our trial protocol were prospectively documented throughout the 24-hour postoperative observation window. Hypotension, hypertension, respiratory depression, urinary retention, nausea and vomiting were pre-specified as safety endpoints, with standardized case report forms used to record adverse events for every enrolled participant.

After full review of the complete raw dataset, no episodes of hypotension, hypertension, respiratory depression or urinary retention were observed in either study group within the 24-hour follow-up period. PONV was the sole adverse event recorded across all patients, which explains why only PONV event counts were displayed in Table 3 of the original manuscript.

Sample Size Justification

Cumulative 24-hour sufentanil consumption was designated as the primary outcome, and sample size calculations were performed based on this metric. Preliminary data indicated mean 24-hour sufentanil consumption of 99.00 (standard deviation = 22.5) in Group A. To detect a 20% intergroup difference in total 24-hour sufentanil consumption with α = 0.05 and β = 0.1, a minimum of 22 patients per group was required. Accounting for an anticipated 10% dropout rate, we initially planned to recruit 50 participants in total. To mitigate confounding factors inherent to clinical trials—including unforeseen patient dropout, interindividual variability, and intraoperative/postoperative confounders—we enrolled a larger cohort than the theoretical minimum sample size. This approach was adopted to strengthen methodological rigor and bolster the robustness of our trial conclusions.

Comprehensive Description of Double-Blind Procedures

We elaborate on the full blinding workflow to address the reviewers’ inquiries: Study drug preparation: A dedicated nurse uninvolved in clinical patient care prepared all injectable study solutions. The injectates for Group A and Group B were identical in volume, color, and physical appearance, with no visual markers to distinguish treatment allocation.Blinded study personnel: All clinicians performing ultrasound-guided FNB, ward nurses providing postoperative care, outcome assessors collecting VAS scores and adverse event data, and statistical analysts remained fully blinded to group assignment. Only the nurse responsible for drug preparation held access to the randomization envelopes and did not participate in patient follow-up or outcome assessment.

Limitations in Disentangling Peripheral versus Systemic Esketamine Effects

We fully acknowledge the critical limitation highlighted by the reviewers: without an intravenous esketamine control arm or measurements of plasma esketamine concentrations, our trial cannot definitively differentiate peripheral perineural analgesic effects from central analgesia secondary to systemic drug absorption. We plan to conduct dedicated follow-up trials incorporating an intravenous esketamine comparator and plasma drug concentration testing to separately characterize the peripheral and systemic analgesic mechanisms of perineural esketamine.

Furthermore, we recognize the absence of assessments of motor function, quadriceps muscle strength and early mobilization as a notable study limitation. These core Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) outcomes were not pre-specified for data collection. We recommend that future trials evaluating perineural adjuvants for TKA incorporate standardized assessments of motor block and postoperative mobilization.

Once again, we extend our sincere appreciation to the editorial board and Dr. Zhou’s research group for this rigorous peer review commentary. Their feedback has greatly assisted us in standardizing trial reporting and refining the interpretation of our results.
