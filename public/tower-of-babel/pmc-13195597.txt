Facilitators, barriers, and strategies for optimizing engagement in recovery community centers among sexual minority people in the U.S. Northeast
Authors: Déjà N. Clement, Lauren B. Bernier, Lauren Gulbicki, John F. Kelly, Bettina B. Hoeppner, Jillian R. Scheer, Abigail W. Batchelder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the U.S., sexual minority (SM; lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer, and other non-heterosexual) individuals experience disproportionate substance use disorder (SUD) disparities and encounter barriers to affirming treatment and recovery services. Recovery community centers (RCCs) offer peer-led and community-driven programs with prior research suggesting that RCCs may address these gaps, yet SM experiences within RCCs are underexplored.MethodsThe current study conducted semi-structured, one-on-one interviews with SM adults (n = 21) engaged with RCCs and other recovery services in the U.S. northeast. Interviews were analyzed using iterative, reflexive thematic analysis with double coding and consensus procedures.ResultsThree overarching themes characterized SM participants’ barriers and facilitators to RCC engagement and included needs related to: 1) programming structure and accessibility, 2) creating intersectional and safe community spaces, and 3) addressing stigma and bias. Participant-generated recommendations for optimizing recovery engagement included increasing SM specific and intersectional programming; increasing SM and racially diverse staffing with visible cues of inclusion, targeted outreach, and comprehensive training in SM cultural competence; and programming related to harm reduction.ConclusionsFindings indicate that RCCs could potentially strengthen SM engagement when programming is flexible, holistic, and recovery environments are visibly inclusive, intersectional, and non-stigmatizing. Implementation efforts could prioritize diverse staff hiring, cultural competence and harm-reduction training, and community outreach, to build recovery infrastructures that equitably support SM communities. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of these SM-affirming recovery practices.

Introduction

Globally, sexual minority (SM; lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer, and other non-heterosexual) individuals experience disproportionately high rates of substance use disorders (SUDs) compared to heterosexual individuals (Hughes et al., 2023, 2020; Kerridge et al., 2017; McCabe et al., 2009; Norman et al., 2024; Schuler et al., 2018). Although prevalence estimates vary internationally due to differences in surveillance systems and legal climates, research from North America and Europe consistently demonstrates elevated risk for alcohol and drug use among SM populations compared to heterosexual peers (Abrahão et al., 2022; Demant et al., 2016). These disparities have been largely conceptualized through the minority stress framework (Dyar, 2022, Krueger et al., 2020, Meyer, 2003, Meyer and Frost, 2012), which posits that stigma, discrimination, and other chronic social stressors contribute to increased vulnerability to SUDs and other mental health difficulties. In turn, these stressors create barriers to treatment engagement and recovery (Haik et al., 2022), highlighting the need for culturally responsive and affirming recovery services (Mayer et al., 2008, Mizock and Lundquist, 2016).

Within the United States (US), national data indicate that SM adults are nearly twice as likely to meet criteria for alcohol use disorder or other SUDs compared to heterosexual adults. Despite this need, few recovery models have been designed for SM populations and are often not tailored to address intersecting identities and minority stress (Levenson et al., 2023, Paschen-Wolff et al., 2023, Penn et al., 2013). Research suggests that SM individuals may be overrepresented in the recovery population (Haik et al., 2022), yet face greater challenges maintaining long-term recovery, including higher rates of psychiatric co-morbidities, psychological distress, and minority stress (Batchelder et al., 2024, Haik et al., 2022). Despite growing recognition of these disparities internationally, recovery programs and services have historically been modeled around the needs of white, heterosexual, cisgender men (Meyers et al., 2021, Stevens, 2012). Recovery, as a multidimensional process (Matthews and Natrajan-Tyagi, 2019), requires approaches that promote flexibility, inclusivity, and holistic support, underscoring the importance of expanding recovery frameworks to reflect diverse pathways and lived experiences.

Recovery community centers (RCCs) offer a promising mechanism to address these gaps. Emerging in the early 2000’s in the U.S., RCCs are peer-led, community-driven, programs that emerged alongside broader recovery-oriented systems of care, offering extended recovery support for those engaged in formal treatment and low-barrier support for those who are not (Cousins et al., 2012, Haberle et al., 2014). Unlike mutual-help organizations (e.g., Alcoholics Anonymous; AA, Narcotics Anonymous; NA), which are meeting-based fellowships organized around a single recovery pathway and generally do not provide ancillary services such as employment assistance, harm reduction, or social service linkage (Kelly et al., 2020a), RCC’s function as integrative community hubs staffed by individuals with lived experience of recovery. They integrate multiple recovery pathways (e.g., 12-step, harm reduction, medication for opioid use disorder), bundle traditional supports (e.g., relapse prevention, peer recovery support specialist services) and non-traditional services (e.g., housing, support, employment linkage, childcare, & recreational programming) within a single physical and social setting (Bernier et al., 2024, Cousins et al., 2012, DeCristofaro et al., 2025, Haberle et al., 2014, Hoeppner et al., 2024; Kelly et al., 2025). Although RCCs vary widely within the US in size, scope, and funding models, they share an emphasis on voluntary participation, mutual support, and ongoing community membership rather than time-limited service episodes (Hoeppner et al., 2024, Kelly et al., 2020a). Examples of this model include the Connecticut Community for Addiction Recovery (Valentine, 2011) in Connecticut and the Phoenix House Bronx Community Recovery Center (BCRC) in New York (Mericle et al., 2014), each of which exemplifies the integrative, community-embedded structure that distinguishes RCCs from meeting-based mutual-help organizations ((Haberle et al., 2014); for a comprehensive review of RCC models, characteristics, and preliminary evidence, see Kelly et al., 2025).

Grounded in the recovery capital framework (Cloud and Granfield, 2008), RCCs aim to strengthen the social, personal, and community-level resources that sustain recovery over time (Kelly et al., 2020b, Kelly and Hoeppner, 2015). While RCCs are not standardized nationally and operate with substantial local variation, emerging evidence suggests they are associated with increased recovery capital, reduced substance use, enhanced social connectedness, and improved linkage to treatment and community services. Emerging evidence suggests that SM adults engage with RCCs at similar or higher rates than heterosexuals (Bernier et al., 2024b; Kelly et al., 2020b, Kelly et al., 2021), potentially reflecting the appeal of RCC’s flexible, peer-driven, and community-based design (Penn et al., 2013). However, limited research has examined how SM individuals experience RCCs and how RCCs can be optimized to meet their unique needs (i.e., address minority stress). No qualitative studies have explored the experiences of SM individuals who access RCCs. To address this gap, the present qualitative study explores the experiences of SM individuals engaged with RCCs. Interviews inquired about participants’ experiences in RCCs as well as other recovery services (e.g., Alcoholics Anonymous (AA)) and solicited recommendations for enhancing inclusivity, affirmation, and equity within recovery-oriented systems of care.

Methods

Study design and setting

Semi-structured qualitative interviews (n = 26) were conducted to better understand the experiences of SM people in RCCs in service of improving recovery support services in the future. RCCs were identified across the Northeast US (i.e., Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, Vermont, and New York) to recruit SM individuals accessing RCCs to participate in one-on-one qualitative interviews. RCCs were defined as non-clinical, peer-driven organizations that provide communal space, services, and supports to people recovering from substance use disorders and related behavioral health conditions. To be considered an RCC for the purposes of this study, an organization had to (a) operate as a recovery-oriented “hub” with a dedicated drop-in physical space (or, for hybrid sites, a virtual analog) accessible to community members at no cost, (b) be staffed at least in part by individuals with lived experience of recovery, (c) bundle multiple recovery support services within a single setting (e.g., recovery coaching, mutual-help meetings, social/recreational programming, employment or educational linkage, MOUD support, harm reduction), and (d) embrace multiple pathways of recovery rather than a single ideology (e.g., 12-step only). Stand-alone meeting-based mutual-help organizations (e.g., AA, NA, Marijuana Anonymous (MA)) and peer recovery support specialist (PRSS) services that did not also offer the integrated, community-embedded structure described above were not classified as RCCs. Pre-screening procedures included a brief telephone pre-screen with a trained study team member that confirmed eligibility (age, recovery status, RCC engagement, sexual minority identity) and verified the consistency of self-reported demographic information against information provided at initial study contact (e.g., the email or phone number used to contact the study, the RCC through which the participant learned of the study, geographic region of residence). If eligible, a study team member continued with the informed consent process and relevant information (e.g., the date of verbal consent) were documented for each participant in Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) (Patridge and Bardyn, 2018), a secure online database. Consistent with previous qualitative methodology for preventing and handling imposter participation in incentivized qualitative research, interviews were removed if unique identity could not be confirmed (Mistry et al., 2024, Ridge et al., 2023). Specifically, at the start of each scheduled interview, the study team member confirmed identity by asking the participant to appear on Zoom video and confirm key demographic and recovery-related details reported in the screener, Additionally, participants were required to remain alert during the interview and email addresses and compensation information were cross-checked for duplicates. These strategies are consistent with strategies outlined in the imposter-participation literature (e.g., aiming to identify scripted or implausible responses, inconsistencies between the pre-screen and interview, multiple participants contacting the study from the same email address or IP address, identical compensation information across participants) (Mistry et al., 2024, Ridge et al., 2023). When concerns about a participant’s unique identity could not be resolved through these verification procedures, the interview was excluded from transcription and analysis. Five participants were excluded on this basis, yielding the final analytic sample of 21.

Participants and recruitment

Inclusion criteria were as follows: a) ≥18 years of age; b) currently seeking or in recovery from a drug or alcohol use disorder and attending an RCC; c) identify as a sexual minority individual; d) able to read and write in English; and d) willing and able to provide informed consent. Recruitment sites were identified through R21AA022693 (PI Kelly), R24DA051988; PI Hoeppner), as well as through direct outreach from the study team, supported by a R24-funded pilot study (PI Batchelder). Staff members at each RCC were invited to advertise the study as part of routine announcements, post flyers in their RCC and share the flyer on relevant social media platforms. Study team members also visited various RCCs remotely and in-person to share information about the project.

Data collection procedure

Interviews were conducted virtually via Zoom (Zoom Video Communications, Inc, 2025) between June 2022 and May 2023 until saturation was reached. Study team member (LB) trained in qualitative methods with PI (AWB), conducted all virtual visits. All interviews were video- and audio-recorded, although only the audio output was sent for transcription (TranscribeMe, n.d.) . Prior to the interview, participants completed a brief online survey via REDCap asking about demographics, substance use, recovery status, utilization of addiction services, RCC visits in the past 90 days, social support in recovery, and engagement and satisfaction with RCCs (Bernier et al., 2024b; Kelly et al., 2021a). Qualitative interviews followed a guide which included ten discussion topics targeting potential facilitators (e.g., recovery capital, self-efficacy, sense of empowerment, location of care) and barriers (e.g., stigma-related concerns about treatment, concerns about the treatment process, and logistical barriers) to engagement in RCCs and other addiction and recovery services. We further asked participants about the perceived benefits of RCCs and the unique needs of SMs based on various identities (e.g., race and ethnicity, gender, and sexual orientation).

Ethical considerations

All procedures were approved by the Massachusetts General Brigham institutional review board.

Positionality statement

The research team brought diverse perspectives to this project. With respect to identity, at least one member of the team identifies as a racial/ethnic marginalized individual and several members of the team identify as sexual and/or gender marginalized individuals. With respect to professional and lived experience relevant to the present study, the majority of authors have substantive research expertise in substance use and recovery science, including investigators with established programs of research focused on minority stress, sexual and gender minority health, and recovery community centers (RCCs). Several authors also have clinical experience working with people who use substances, including as licensed clinical and counseling psychologists providing care to individuals with co-occurring substance use and mental health concerns and as researchers embedded within community-based recovery and harm-reduction settings. This diversity of lived experiences informed our analytical approach, particularly in interpreting participant narratives related to minority stress, intersectionality, and barriers to engagement in recovery spaces. Throughout the analysis, we engaged in reflexive discussions to ensure our interpretations centered participant voices while acknowledging how our own positionalities shaped what we noticed and prioritized in the data.

Data analysis

After all interviews were transcribed, they were transferred to NVivo for analysis. The analytic approach was informed by iterative, reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2019), a theoretically grounded method for identifying patterned meaning across qualitative data while explicitly attending to researcher reflexivity and analytic context. Iterative, reflexive thematic analysis involves repeated engagement with the data, active construction of themes through interpretation, and ongoing reflexive consideration of analytic decisions, rather than pursuit of coding consensus as an endpoint or the treatment of themes as objectively discoverable entities(Braun and Clarke, 2019). This approach has been used in prior research(Batchelder et al., 2021a; Rice et al., 2018) is particularly appropriate for studies that seek to examine participants’ lived experiences in relation to existing theoretical frameworks and structured research questions, while allowing themes to be generated inductively from the data (Ahmed et al., 2025, Braun and Clarke, 2019, Swain, 2018).

Consistent with recommended practices for reflexive thematic analysis conducted within research teams, authors LB and LG first independently reviewed and read through the full set of interview transcripts for familiarization and completed open coding for identified themes. After discussing the open coding with PI (AB), each independently drafted a preliminary codebook and in collaboration with AB, produced a drafted codebook and determined coding conventions (LB, LG). Subsequently, two transcripts were double coded (LB, LG) and the team (AB, LB, and LG) met to discuss discrepancies, determine inter-rater reliability, and refine the codebook, and both transcripts were recoded and coding was checked for consistency. An additional two transcripts were then chosen for double coding, codebook revision, recoding, and codebook finalization. Four transcripts were then double coded twice to ensure consistency prior to assigning half of the remaining transcripts to each author (LB and LG).

Largely consistent with the discussion topics posed by the interview guide, parent codes were identified that emerged from the iterative, reflexive coding process. Next, content belonging to each parent and child code was extracted from NVivo and particularly exemplar quotes belonging to each theme were highlighted to capture representative sentiments across interviews. Consistent with qualitative best practices for thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006, Nowell et al., 2017), additional members of the research team (DC, SI) used the coded data to 1) combine codes into a smaller set of broader ideas, 2) identify codes that presented an idea that would not be sufficiently captured if combined with other codes, and 3) identify themes, cross-cutting ideas seen in all or many codes. The researchers iteratively returned to the original data to ensure that they represented the ideas of the participants.

Results

Participant demographics

Twenty-one participants completed one-on-one qualitative interviews and were included in the presented analysis. Participants ranged in age from 28 to 60 years of age (M = 41.1, SD + 9.1). Most participants identified as white. Just over half identified as cisgender SM woman, and most self-identified as gay, lesbian, or homosexual. Sexual orientation was assessed using a five-point scale derived from prior population-based research on sexual identity and behavior (e.g., Kinsey-derived self-identification items used in national health surveys (Kinsey et al., 2003; Savin-Williams and Vrangalova, 2013), with response options of “only gay/lesbian/homosexual,” “mostly gay/lesbian/homosexual,” “bisexual,” “mostly heterosexual,” and “only heterosexual,” with an additional “other” option for participants whose identity was not captured by these categories. For the purposes of this study, participants who selected “only gay/lesbian/homosexual” can be interpreted as being exclusively attracted to and identified with members of the same sex/gender, whereas those who selected “mostly gay/lesbian/homosexual” can be interpreted as those who are predominantly attracted to and identified with members of the same sex/gender but who also endorse some attraction to or experience with members of a different sex/gender. Length of time participants had been engaged with their RCC was not systematically captured in the present study; this is acknowledged as a limitation below. Summary of themes

Three overarching themes were identified in the analysis that capture both barriers and facilitators related to optimization of RCC and other recovery services for SM people. These themes reflect participants’ perspectives and include 1) RCC and other recovery service programming structure and accessibility, 2) the importance of creating intersectional and safe community spaces, and 3) the need to address stigma and bias within RCC environments. A summary of each theme is provided, along with participant narratives illustrating each theme. Additionally, we have aggregated the recommendations for optimizing RCCs for SM people related to increasing SM affirmation in RCC services, SM specific services and groups, staff hiring and diverse outreach, and staff training. Although discussed separately, these themes were interrelated and should be interpreted as overlapping components of participants’ broader experiences of RCC engagement. Programming structure and accessibility

All participants (n = 21) reported that having recovery programming that was consistent, structured, comprehensive, and flexible was critical to engagement in RCCs and other recovery services. For the purposes of this study, programming structure refers to any tangible aspects of infrastructure and accessibility that supported participants’ recovery, such as program design, resource availability, and service delivery. Participant narratives highlighted two key subthemes related to program structure and accessibility: 1) flexibility in modality and delivery, and 2) educational, occupational, and recreational resources that enhanced engagement and were particularly beneficial for SM individuals.

Range and accessibility in available services

Approximately half of participants (n = 12) reported that having a range in options – variation in how and when recovery services were delivered, was a significant and positive factor for engagement in RCCs. Several participants indicated that RCCs were more beneficial when there was a significant range in times that programs or classes were offered (e.g., before or after normative work hours), options for virtual and/or in-person services, and consistency in programming (e.g., having programs that were routinely offered) were important facilitators to engaging in RCCs. Many participants noted that when the programming was consistent and accessible, they felt more committed and engaged with recovery services and had a greater sense of accountability in their recovery journey. Several participants also described that they either started going or continued to go to an RCC because of the proximity of the RCC to their home (e.g., within walking distance or physically accessible). Across interviews, participants conveyed that flexible, consistent, and accessible programming, whether through varied modalities, scheduled, or proximity, was central to sustaining engagement with RCCs.

Educational, occupational, and recreational opportunities

Many participants (n = 9) indicated that having a variety of educational (e.g., financial education), occupational/professional (e.g., workforce development), and recreational opportunities (e.g., art and music classes) within RCCs fostered empowerment, autonomy, and motivation to engage in their recovery journey. Participants emphasized that access to diverse forms of programming, ranging from skill-building and creative outlets to health education, helped them view recovery as a holistic and continuous process of personal growth. RCCs that offered educational programming related to substance use and other risk behaviors (e.g., HIV risk behaviors) were described as particularly impactful, enabling participants to recognize maladaptive behaviors and acquire knowledge to improve both recovery and overall wellness.“Not just recovering from the alcohol, it's life changing, where it's continuous[ly] changing. And there's always something new to the table, always new information about health or knowledge or something. I feel like I'm in school and recovering and the library [at] the same time.” (Participant 24).

Across interviews, participants conveyed that RCCs which offered educational, professional, and recreational programming fostered not only recovery-related engagement but also broader personal transformation, helping them to rebuild purpose, identity, and community connection.

Creating intersectional and safe community spaces

Approximately half of participants (n = 12) described a need for RCC environments and recovery spaces that felt intersectionally inclusive and safe (i.e., spaces that recognize and address overlapping systems of power and privilege that impact individuals who hold multiple minoritized identities). Participant narratives highlighted two key subthemes: 1) perceived safety and acceptance in recovery spaces, and 2) limited representation and intersectional inclusion.

Perceived safety and acceptance in recovery spaces

Approximately half of participants (n = 12) described the RCC environment as a welcoming environment for people at any place in their recovery journey. Participants described this as a facilitator to RCC engagement for SM individuals and often made comparisons to other types of recovery support that they felt were not as supportive. One participant stated:“We respect the multiple pathways of recovery, so it's not just 12-step based. We recognize and celebrate all pathways of recovery, and I think that adds for a nice diversity in the recovery experience.” (Participant 1)

“We respect the multiple pathways of recovery, so it's not just 12-step based. We recognize and celebrate all pathways of recovery, and I think that adds for a nice diversity in the recovery experience.” (Participant 1)

Several participants (n = 7) described uncertainty about whether recovery spaces would be affirming or inclusive of sexual minority (SM) identities. Participants described that this uncertainty often created apprehension or discomfort, leading to sometimes concealing their identities or delayed engagement in RCC programming. Participants emphasized that feeling accepted and safe was a key determinant of whether they could fully participate in recovery spaces. One participant described:“If I were to bring a friend of mine who’s new to the [the RCC] who is, like me, a cis-gay, out male, I would caution them. I would say we’re going into a place where we’re a minority and that not all of the people there are receptive to our community.” (Participant 12)

The same participant also stated:“I've gone to AA meetings where I've felt, again, like I stick out like a sore thumb in the group because most of the people in the group are cisgender, straight male, female, and it's not a group that's specifically LGBTQIA+ , so members of our community oftentimes stick out like a sore thumb.”(Participant 12).

Across interviews, participants highlighted that perceptions of safety and acceptance within RCCs were central to engagement. While many described RCCs as affirming spaces that embraced diverse recovery pathways, others noted that uncertainty about inclusion, particularly for sexual minority individuals, sometimes limited their comfort and participation.

Limited representation and intersectional inclusion

Several participants (n = 6) also described a lack of representation of intersecting marginalized identities (e.g., race/ethnicity, age, and sexual minority status). Participants described this lack of representation and affirmation as a barrier to RCC engagement and other types of recovery programming. Two participants who identified as Black/African American SM individuals described discomfort with the lack of racial/ethnic diversity, both among clientele and staff, in RCC and other recovery spaces, describing that these spaces are predominately White:“When I was in the [RCC], I was the only Black person there, and it was a group of over 15 people. And so that was a kind of isolating experience; like, ‘Oh, not going to bring up the race card. This isn't the right space for it.” (Participant 2).

“When I was in the [RCC], I was the only Black person there, and it was a group of over 15 people. And so that was a kind of isolating experience; like, ‘Oh, not going to bring up the race card. This isn't the right space for it.” (Participant 2).

Overall, participants underscored that representation across race, ethnicity, age, and sexual identity influenced their sense of belonging and ability to discuss issues related to other marginalized identities within RCC spaces. Limited visibility of intersecting marginalized identities was described as contributing to feelings of isolation, and for some reduced engagement in recovery programming.

Addressing Stigma and Bias

Enacted stigma and bias from staff, and concerns about privacy and confidentiality within RCCs and other recovery spaces were described as significant barriers to engagement in recovery programming across interviews. Several participants (n = 7) reported experiencing judgment and stigmatizing attitudes from recovery staff, including RCC staff, particularly related to substance use and mental health. Approximately half of participants recounted instances in which RCC staff used stigmatizing language or expressed moralistic attitudes towards substance use, which contributed to feelings of mistrust and reduced comfort in engaging in services.

As one participant explained:“…Really stigmatizing in the language that they used and how they treated me or had a general lack of understanding that you can use alcohol and use drugs, but that doesn't make you a bad person.” (Participant 3)

“…Really stigmatizing in the language that they used and how they treated me or had a general lack of understanding that you can use alcohol and use drugs, but that doesn't make you a bad person.” (Participant 3)

Another participant described similar experiences related to medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD):“If you’re open about [being on a medication for opioid use disorder] and you talk about it, you're not always so welcome. And they don't always consider you to be, quote-unquote, ‘clean,’ which I hate that word.”(Participant 33)

“If you’re open about [being on a medication for opioid use disorder] and you talk about it, you're not always so welcome. And they don't always consider you to be, quote-unquote, ‘clean,’ which I hate that word.”(Participant 33)

Two participants described difficulties with experiencing mental health stigma in recovery programming more broadly. They reflected that insensitive language or framing around mental illness often created discomfort and reinforced shame. One participant highlighted the connection between mental health, substance use, stigma, and shame as they described their perception of Alcohol Anonymous’s emphasis on language related to personal responsibility for substance use:“I just don't really think that language is super helpful. And I think it just kind of perpetuates people not speaking about certain things that may be part of their life or have impacted their relationship with substances, and just kind of perpetuates that shame.” (Participant 13)

Across interviews, participants emphasized that experiences of stigma, whether related to substance use or mental health, often led to feelings of exclusion, undermined trust, and reduced engagement within RCCs and other recovery settings.

Privacy and confidentiality – inclusion and autonomy

Participants (n = 5) described challenges with privacy and confidentiality within RCCs and recovery spaces, particularly among peer staff (i.e., peer recovery specialists and peer advocates). While most participant narratives highlight the importance and benefit of having staff with lived experience who serve as peers (i.e., peer recovery specialists and peer advocates), this shared identity occasionally became a barrier due to concerns about privacy and confidentiality within the recovery community. One participant noted:“…[T]he people that work [at the RCC] and volunteer there are also members of our 12-step community. And so, you might not want the people to see you there or might not want to share because of who’s around.” (Participant 28)

“…[T]he people that work [at the RCC] and volunteer there are also members of our 12-step community. And so, you might not want the people to see you there or might not want to share because of who’s around.” (Participant 28)

Across interviews, participants described privacy and confidentiality concerns as a recurring challenge within peer-supported recovery settings. While shared lived experience among staff fostered inclusion and relatability, some participants noted that it could also compromise their sense of privacy and autonomy, leading to hesitation in fully engaging with RCC services.

Recommendations for optimizing SM engagement in RCC and other recovery services

Participants described specific recommendations for improving the inclusivity and responsiveness of RCCs and other recovery programming and services to better meet the needs of SM communities. Drawing from their lived experiences, participants emphasized strategies to enhance representation, safety, and affirmation within recovery spaces. The recommendations outlined in this section reflect participant-identified priorities for strengthening recovery programming, staffing, outreach, and training to foster more equitable and affirming recovery environments. Table 2 includes an outline of these recommendations.

Develop SM-specific and visibly affirming intersectional programming

Several participants (n = 7) suggested that RCCs offer SM specific programming that is uniquely tailored to SM communities (e.g., groups addressing minority stressors as barriers to recovery engagement, SM-only groups focused on understanding differences in substance use for SM individuals). Several participants also recommended that RCCs have visual cues to signal that RCCs are welcoming to SM people. For example, many participants noted that having a rainbow flag somewhere within the organization would help increase feelings of safety and visible SM support.

In describing a need for SM specific programming, one participant stated:“…having programming specific to queer folks would be a start. I think also just creating space where they could actually… create a dialogue where they’re allowing people who are part of that population to advocate for themselves and voice what they need or want…,” (Participant 13)

“…having programming specific to queer folks would be a start. I think also just creating space where they could actually… create a dialogue where they’re allowing people who are part of that population to advocate for themselves and voice what they need or want…,” (Participant 13)

Overall, participants recommended SM specific programming and affirming visual cues as key strategies for creating more inclusive and supportive recovery environments.

Diverse representation and outreach

Over half of the participants (n = 12) emphasized a significant need for diverse representation among RCC staff and clientele. Participants emphasized the need for SM-identified RCC staff and increased outreach to local SM communities to increase awareness about SM recovery programming. Participants explained that increased SM community representation would result in an increased sense of safety, community, and motivation to engage in recovery. A participant described this need by stating:“having staff and people working at RCCs who are either part of the community themselves or more legitimate allies at the very least.” (Participant 1).

“having staff and people working at RCCs who are either part of the community themselves or more legitimate allies at the very least.” (Participant 1).

Participants described a need for belonging beyond substance use identity, noting that meaningful engagement was fostered when spaces reflected intersectional representation across sexual minority, age, gender, and racial/ethnic identities. As one participant described:“…it’s kind of nice to have that one thing [shared SM identity] in common. Yes, we’re all there because we’re in recovery from drugs and alcohol, so everybody has that in common, the recovery community. But to have that additional thing in common, it just feels like – I don’t know. It’s like I can talk about more of that experience of what it was like growing up in that group, and these people get that.”(Participant 21).

“…it’s kind of nice to have that one thing [shared SM identity] in common. Yes, we’re all there because we’re in recovery from drugs and alcohol, so everybody has that in common, the recovery community. But to have that additional thing in common, it just feels like – I don’t know. It’s like I can talk about more of that experience of what it was like growing up in that group, and these people get that.”(Participant 21).

Taken together, participants recommended increasing diverse and intersectional representation in RCCs and other recovery services to facilitate increased feelings of safety, community, and sustained engagement for sexual minority individuals in recovery.

Staff hiring and training

Many participants (n = 10) emphasized the importance of recovery staff training and hiring practices that promote cultural competence and inclusion within RCCs. Participants recommended that staff receive sensitivity and cultural-competence training specific to working with SM clients, as well as ongoing education around SM identity and culture. One participant stated:“anybody that works in the recovery world should have knowledge of the SM community” (Participant 25).

“anybody that works in the recovery world should have knowledge of the SM community” (Participant 25).

Participants further recommended that recovery staff should reflect the diversity and lived experiences of the communities they serve and recommended hiring of recovery staff who “speak the language(s)” of those attending recovery services – both literally and culturally, to ensure that all participants fell understood and supported in their recovery journey. For example, one participant stated:“…but you're working in a very international city. I mean, when they're hiring people, they should hire people that are able to speak the language…because communication is essential for these people to get services.” (Participant 27).

“…but you're working in a very international city. I mean, when they're hiring people, they should hire people that are able to speak the language…because communication is essential for these people to get services.” (Participant 27).

Lastly, in addition to training specific to SM identity and culture, participants also recommended a need for updated training related to harm-reduction services. Participants underscored the importance of culturally competent and linguistically responsive staffing, coupled with training in SM and harm-reduction practices, to promote equitable engagement in recovery.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored barriers and facilitators to recovery community centers (RCC) and other recovery services engagement among sexual minority (SM) individuals, as well as participant-identified recommendations for optimizing RCC and other recovery services for SM individuals. Findings revealed that engagement was influenced by three overarching factors (themes): 1) the structure and accessibility of RCC programming and recovery services, 2) the creation of intersectional and safe community spaces, and 3) addressing stigma and bias within RCC and other recovery environments. These findings extend existing literature on minority stress, recovery capital, and culturally responsive- care by highlighting how these processes operate within peer-led community-based recovery settings. Across interviews, participants described both affirming experiences and areas needing improvement, highlighting opportunities to strengthen RCC inclusivity, representation, and responsiveness to SM communities.

Programming structure and recovery capital

Grounded in the recovery capital framework (Cloud and Granfield, 2008), RCCs emphasize that sustained recovery cannot solely address psychopathology but must also increase one’s recovery capital (i.e., the individual, interpersonal, social, environmental, and cultural resources that increase the likelihood that individuals will be able to mitigate stress associated with remission) (Kelly et al., 2021b). Consistent with prior research on recovery-supportive environments, participants emphasized that consistent, flexible, and diverse programming enhanced engagement by promoting accountability, autonomy, and holistic wellness. Educational, occupational (e.g., workforce development), and recreational offerings were viewed as not only recovery supportive but improved overall well-being, underscoring RCCs’ unique role as low-barrier spaces for building recovery capital outside formal treatment systems, particularly for SM individuals.

Unlike many formal recovery support settings that are structured around time-limited programming, predefined recovery pathways (often with abstinence as the goal or expectation), or clinical eligibility criteria, RCCs offer ongoing, voluntary, and community embedded support that is not contingent on treatment status or abstinence orientation (Kelly et al., 2020a, Owens et al., 2023). This structural flexibility may be particularly salient for SM individuals, who often experience fragmented care and heightened disengagement from formal systems due to prior experiences of identity-based stigma or invalidation, in addition to substance use stigma (Benz et al., 2019, Bernier et al., 2024b; Haik et al., 2022). Consistent with Haik and colleagues (Haik et al., 2022), although SM individuals report similar facilitator types as heterosexual individuals, they tend to endorse a greater number of facilitators alongside higher unmet support needs. Rather than reflecting better recovery outcomes, this pattern likely signals increased exposure to minority stress and a corresponding need for holistic, identity-affirming supports, which RCCs may be uniquely positioned to offer given their peer-led, resilience-oriented, and adaptable design (Haik et al., 2022).

Intersectional safety, belonging, and representation

Participants’ emphasis on safety, acceptance, and intersectional representation within RCCs and other recovery services underscores the central role of minority stress and belonging in shaping engagement in recovery supports and structurally affirming recovery environments. While many described RCCs as inclusive and non-judgmental, others reported uncertainty about whether their sexual, racial, or gender identities would be affirmed. This hesitancy reflects well-established findings about minority stress and belonging in recovery contexts, particularly anticipated rejection or stigma, in which individuals from marginalized groups understandably expect stigma, invalidation, or exclusion based on past experiences in healthcare or recovery settings (Nieder et al., 2025a). Previous research demonstrates that among individuals with concealable stigmatized identities (e.g., a person who uses substances, sexual behavior/orientation), anticipated stigma is associated with psychological distress, suggesting that SM individuals may face compounded stigma when considering whether to disclose their sexual orientation and substance use (Quinn et al., 2014, Quinn and Chaudoir, 2009). These findings are consistent with qualitative work that recovery environments can operate as both supportive and exclusionary spaces for individuals with intersecting marginalized identities, but especially SM individuals, particularly when intersectional identities are not explicitly acknowledged or represented (Nieder et al., 2025b). For example, recent work has shown that sexual and gender minoritized individuals in recovery settings often experience belonging as conditional, shaped by dominant norms around whiteness, gender conformity, and recovery ideology, which can produce broader systems of marginalization even within peer-led contexts (Nieder et al., 2025b) Importantly, while many participants described RCCs as more inclusive and nonjudgemental than other recovery settings, limited representation across intersecting marginalized identities contributed to feelings of isolation for some participants. These findings underscore the relevance of intersectional approaches to recovery programming such as diverse staffing and cultural competence trainings that address multiple layers of identity and social context. The vast majority of RCC staff are white (Kelly et al., 2020a, Kelly et al., 2020b), mirroring broader disparities in outpatient substance use treatment settings where over 70% of staff are white while more than half of the clients have intersecting marginalized identities (e.g., race, sexual orientation, gender, housing status) (Guerrero, 2013). Existing RCC demographic studies do not report data on sexual orientation, highlighting a gap in RCC workforce diversity data. Despite varied approaches, it is evidenced that culturally responsive substance use and recovery interventions and settings are acceptable and efficacious (Banks et al., 2023).

Although research on SM specific recovery spaces remains limited, existing evidence suggests that identity-tailored substance use treatment can enhance engagement and improve recovery outcomes (Harkness et al., 2024, Pachankis et al., 2020, Paschen-Wolff et al., 2022). However, barriers including limited funding, low or inconsistent attendance, and structural stigma, particularly in community-based settings, continue to constrain availability (Harkness et al., 2024, Pachankis et al., 2021). In rural or low-density areas, the small number of SM individuals and peer facilitators or providers may further limit feasibility of offering in-person SM-specific recovery groups, underscoring the value of virtual or hybrid models to foster connection and access to affirming recovery care.

Stigma and privacy in peer-led settings

Stigmatizing language and moralistic attitudes from staff and peers emerged as major barriers to engagement, consistent with literature showing that stigma undermines trust and deters help-seeking among individuals with substance use and mental health challenges (Batchelder et al., 2021a, Batchelder et al., 2021b, Benz et al., 2019, Luoma et al., 2007). Participants’ experience of enacted stigma, particularly regarding medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD; e.g., buprenorphine, methadone), suggests the persistence of abstinence-only attitudes within some recovery spaces and the negative ramifications of this messaging, such as internalized stigma and shame, delayed treatment MOUD treatment initiation, and premature discontinuation of MOUD (Hoeppner et al., 2024, Paquette et al., 2022, Wakeman and Rich, 2018). Additionally, concerns about confidentiality within small, peer-based networks complicated participants’ ability to engage authentically, echoing previous work identifying tensions between shared identity and privacy in peer-led recovery models (du Plessis et al., 2020, Olding et al., 2022, Scannell, 2021).

Recommendations and Implications for RCC Practice and Policy

Participants’ recommendations for optimizing RCC and other recovery services emphasized the need for programming and environments that affirm SM identities while addressing broader structural and cultural barriers to engagement. Critically, participants identified several features of RCCs that make them uniquely positioned to implement these recommendations compared to traditional treatment settings. Participants highlighted the importance of developing SM-specific and intersectional programming that acknowledges minority stress (Meyer and Frost, 2012) and fosters belonging across diverse identities, including race, gender, age, and language. These recommendations align with evidence supporting identity-affirming and peer-based approaches as effective strategies for improving engagement and recovery outcomes among SM populations (Cochran et al., 2007, Matthews et al., 2019, Senreich, 2010). The peer-based, non-clinical nature of RCCs may reduce power differentials and institutional stigma that often characterize traditional treatment settings, creating more authentic opportunities for SM individuals to shape programming and leadership.

Further, increased representation of SM staff and visible cues of inclusion (e.g., rainbow symbols, outreach to SM communities) were described as critical indicators of safety and belonging, while comprehensive staff training in SM cultural competence, harm reduction, and inclusive communication was viewed as essential to promoting equitable care. These findings echo earlier SAMHSA guidelines (2012), which outlined best practices for SM-affirming substance use treatment through inclusive environments, culturally competent staff training, and visible indicators of support. Notably, these guidelines have been removed under current federal policies, raising concerns about the de-prioritization of SM health equity. Importantly, SM participants of color noted that RCCs remain predominantly white spaces, contributing to additional isolation and underscoring the need for intersectional and culturally responsive approaches that recognize how overlapping identities shape recovery experiences and disparities (Dawes et al., 2023, DeCristofaro et al., 2025, Xin et al., 2023). Taken together, these findings suggest that building SM-affirming, intersectional, and culturally responsive recovery infrastructures is critical to advancing equity, engagement, and long-term recovery outcomes across diverse communities. Collectively, greater investment in SM-specific RCC programming could address critical gaps in peer-based, long-term recovery support that complement and extend beyond what clinical substance use treatment alone can provide, offering sustained community connection beyond time-limited therapeutic interventions, particularly for SM individuals.

Limitations

This study should be interpreted considering several limitations. Some participants recounted experiences from several years prior, including during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may not accurately reflect current RCC operations. The sample was small and predominantly white and cisgender, limiting the transferability of findings to SM individuals who identify as people of color, transgender, and gender diverse. Although participants were drawn from RCCs across the New England region, heterogeneity in settings (e.g., rural versus urban) may have introduced variability in participants’ experiences of accessibility and inclusivity, and findings may not be generalizable to RCCs in other regions. In addition, five interviews were excluded due to the inability to confirm unique participant identity, a methodological challenge that may be attributable to the broad geographic recruitment strategy, including sites where the research team had limited prior partnerships. While the approach to addressing potential fraudulent participation was consistent with established qualitative research practices, this issue underscores the logistical and ethical complexities of conducting community-based research across geographically dispersed recovery settings. Additionally, length of time participants has been engaged (e.g., months or years of attendance) was not systematically captured at the time of the interview, limiting our ability to characterize the sample by recovery and RCC engagement tenure or to examine whether facilitators, barriers, and recommendations differed across newer versus longer-term RCC members. Future qualitative and mixed methods work should explicitly assess length of RCC engagement to allow comparisons across stages of community membership and to identify needs that may be specific to early versus sustained engagement.

Implications

Findings from this study may have important implications for clinical practice, research, policy, and public health. Clinically, results highlight the need for recovery-oriented services that explicitly affirm SM identities, incorporate intersectional and culturally responsive frameworks, and ensure staff are trained in SM cultural competence and harm-reduction approaches. For RCCs and related recovery programs, adopting flexible delivery models, diverse staffing, and visible cues of inclusion may enhance engagement and retention among SM individuals, particularly those holding multiple marginalized identities.

From a research perspective, future studies should evaluate the implementation and effectiveness of SM-affirming and intersectional recovery interventions, including hybrid or virtual models that expand access for individuals in rural or low-density areas. Policy efforts should prioritize reinstating and strengthening federal guidance on SM-affirming substance use treatment (e.g., SAMHSA’s 2012 recommendations) and allocate resources to support workforce diversification and culturally competent training across recovery settings. At the population level, integrating these approaches within broader public health strategies can advance equity, reduce stigma, and promote sustained recovery outcomes among diverse SM communities.

Conclusion

This study contributes to a growing body of research highlighting the importance of inclusivity, identity-affirming, and structurally responsive approaches to substance use recovery. By centering the voices of SM individuals in addiction recovery, findings illuminate both the strengths and limitations of current RCC models and identify several clear suggestions that may enhance engagement and equity. Building RCCs and recovery services that are flexible, culturally competent, and intersectional in design may prove helpful in fostering safety and belonging and sustained recovery among diverse SM populations. Broader efforts to embed these principles into research, clinical practice, and policy will be critical for reducing disparities and ensuring that recovery-oriented systems of care equitably meet the needs of all individuals, regardless of identity and background. Investing in these recommendations has the potential to expand much needed recovery support to SM people, a population with substantial recovery needs, increasingly discriminated against, and underserved in the current political climate.
