Fairness-informed Pareto Optimization An Efficient Bilevel Framework Thanks: Corresponding author: yassine.laguel@univ-cotedazur.fr
Sofiane Tanji ∗, Samuel Vaiter ⋄, Yassine Laguel †

Abstract
. Despite their promise, fair machine learning methods often yield Pareto-inefficient models, in which the performance of certain groups can be improved without degrading that of others. This issue arises frequently in traditional in-processing approaches such as fairness-through-regularization. In contrast, existing Pareto-efficient approaches are biased towards a certain perspective on fairness and fail to adapt to the broad range of fairness metrics studied in the literature. In this paper, we present badr , a simple framework to recover the optimal Pareto-efficient model for any fairness metric. Our framework recovers its models through a Bilevel Adaptive Rescalarisation procedure. The lower level is a weighted empirical risk minimization task where the weights are a convex combination of the groups, while the upper level optimizes the chosen fairness objective. We equip our framework with two novel large-scale, single-loop algorithms, badr-gd and badr-sgd , and establish their convergence guarantees. We release badr , an open-source Python toolbox implementing our framework for a variety of learning tasks and fairness metrics. Finally, we conduct extensive numerical experiments demonstrating the advantages of badr over existing Pareto-efficient approaches to fairness.



1.
Introduction

Data-driven decision systems are now commonly used in high-stakes domains such as employment, education, law enforcement, and health care
[
20
]
.
Their widespread deployment which assists and sometimes takes over human decisions, has been accompanied by increased concerns about unfairness and bias based on sensitive attributes such as gender, race or religion
[
24
;
67
;
70
]
.

Motivated by evidence that learned models can perform unevenly across sensitive groups
[
67
]
, research on algorithmic fairness has proposed a range of formulations and metrics
[
47
;
6
;
39
]
to quantify and alleviate the bias of standard learning algorithms.
Yet, optimizing these criteria typically comes at a cost in predictive performance, inducing an inherent fairness-accuracy trade-off
[
68
]
.
This tension is particularly pronounced in in-processing methods that incorporate constraints or penalties into the learning algorithm
[
33
]
, often yielding models that are
inefficient
from a performance standpoint.
Simply put, it is often possible to improve the performance of one group without harming others, indicating that the model is not operating on the
Pareto front
of groups performances.

This limitation has spurred interest in group fairness formulations
[
36
]
that consider aggregations of group performance alternative to the standard empirical risk minimization (ERM) objective.
For instance, the minimax group fairness framework
[
64
]
minimizes the worst-group loss, ensuring that no group’s performance can be improved without degrading another’s.
More broadly, fairness risk measures such as the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR)
[
75
]
interpolate between the robust minimax approach and the expectation-based ERM loss.
While these methods guarantee Pareto efficiency, as detailed in Section
2.3
, their impact on specific fairness metrics remains unpredictable and uncontrollable.

In this work, we introduce a general framework –
badr
for Bilevel Adaptive Rescalarization – that learns on the Pareto front of group performance the model that optimizes a user-specified fairness metric.
We cast the task as a bilevel formulation: the lower level enforces Pareto efficiency across groups, and the upper level selects the point that minimizes the chosen fairness metric.
We propose
badr-gd
, a large-scale single-loop stochastic algorithm designed specifically for this bilevel problem, and prove convergence under standard assumptions.
We release an open-source Python toolbox that implements our approach for several fairness metrics.
Experiments on real-world datasets show improvements over existing methods on the targeted fairness metric while preserving Pareto-efficient group performance.

Related work

Multi-objective optimization

In this work, we tackle fair machine learning within a multi-objective optimization framework.
That is, group performances are measured through their respective losses and success is measured through Pareto efficiency
[
37
]
.
The set of Pareto efficient models, coined the Pareto front can be searched through different methods, including scalarization methods
[
37
;
38
]
, the
\varepsilon
-constraint method
[
65
]
, Particle Swarm Optimization
[
21
]
, etc.
Here, we build upon a standard scalarization method, the weighted sum approach
[
86
;
41
]
, which swaps the multi-objective formulation for an arbitrary convex combination of the objectives.
This approach is widely used in practice and is guaranteed under mild assumptions to output Pareto efficient solutions
[
41
;
37
]
.
Yet the interplay between the convex weights and the quality of the resulting solution remains poorly understood in general
[
27
;
37
]
.
Some methods attempt to explore the entire Pareto front by varying the weights
[
53
;
78
]
, while other methods compare weights based on their relative impact on the objective
[
4
]
.
In this work, we present a novel data-driven approach for the weight selection in the weighted sum method.
Our method optimizes a user-specified fairness metric over the Pareto front of group performances, thereby tailoring the solution to the stakeholders’ fairness concerns.
Beyond fair learning, our approach is applicable to general multi-objective problems whenever a utility function is given to measure the quality of pareto-efficient solutions.

Efficient Fair Learning

To mitigate unfairness, three families of interventions are common: pre-processing
[
90
;
85
;
77
;
58
;
14
]
, which modifies the data or labels before training (e.g., reweighting, resampling, or learning fair representations); in-processing see, e.g.,
[
88
;
89
;
35
;
25
;
23
;
16
]
, which
alter the training process itself to promote fairness in the learned model; and post-processing
[
60
]
, which adjusts scores or decision thresholds of a trained model to satisfy a target criterion.
Within in-processing, the arguably most standard approach is to incorporate fairness requirements in the optimization problem with either explicit fairness constraints or
penalization terms
[
33
]
.
By formulating the initial optimization problem to penalize violations of a chosen fairness criterion (e.g., adding a constraint on demographic parity or a penalty term for equalized odds), the model is encouraged to be fairer during learning.
However, from a Pareto perspective this approach cannot guarantee efficient solutions – see our discussion in Section
2
: a model trained with such penalties may still be improvable for some groups without harming others.

Most Pareto-efficient methods in the fairness literature rely on data reweighting and have been cast as both pre- and in-processing techniques.
Regarding pre-processing methods,
[
81
]
proposes for instance to reweight data per group with weights inversely proportional to group frequencies.
We note also several works consider reweighting jointly per group and class – e.g.
[
52
]
– to reduce certain fairness metrics such as demographic parity.
Yet allowing weight to depend on class takes the method out of our multi-objective framework : the resulting weighted loss is no longer a convex combination of group losses, and Pareto efficiency is not guaranteed.
Regarding in-processing methods, in the same vein as
[
52
]
, some training procedures dynamically estimate weights jointly on group and class to mitigate certain fairness metrics
[
56
;
50
]
.
Alternatively, several methods have considered adversarial reweighting schemes to promote fairness during training.
This line of works, designated as minimax fairness
[
64
;
48
;
31
;
1
]
, has expanded to distributionally robust optimization approaches
[
84
;
74
;
83
]
, and has the benefit of systematically yielding Pareto efficient models, see
Section

2.3
for details.
Yet, given a specific fairness metric, one can hardly guarantee the quality of these robust models.

Finally we note that several works have explored bilevel optimization to dynamically adjust group-wise weights.
For instance
[
71
;
76
]
consider a bilevel formulation where the upper level minimizes arbitrary fairness metrics but the lower level allows for weights to depend on both group and class, thereby losing Pareto efficiency.
Alternatively,
[
64
;
1
;
80
]
propose a bilevel framework where the lower level minimizes a weighted group loss, and the upper level optimizes the worst-case group loss.
Furthermore, some bilevel methods, such as those proposed in
[
51
]
, have been developed to address multi-objective problems that aim to balance different fairness metrics and accuracy.
These approaches lead to formulations and considerations that differ from ours : competing loss functions do not share the same regularity properties and are typically supported by weaker convergence guarantees.
Finally, we note none of these papers provide large-scale optimization methods with convergence guarantees that effectively solve their bilevel formulations.

Bilevel Optimization

Bilevel optimization
[
29
;
28
]
plays an increasing role in machine learning applications including including hyperparameter optimization
[
7
]
, meta-learning
[

]
, or fair machine learning, as discussed in the above paragraph.
Among existing approaches for bilevel learning problems, we note two main categories: penalization-based approaches
[
57
]
that transforms the bilevel problem into a single-level one by adding a penalty term to the upper-level objective, and implicit methods
[

that approximate hypergradients through implicit differentiation or unrolling techniques.
Early deterministic works relied on double-loop algorithms, where the inner loop iteratively solved the lower-level problem to near-optimality
[
73
;
43
;
44
]
.
More recent studies have proposed single-loop schemes that update both levels simultaneously
[
26
;
19
]
, thereby avoiding the need to tune the number of inner iterations.
To enable implicit differentiation and theoretical guarantees, these methods usually assume the lower-level problem is smooth (with Lipschitz-continuous Hessians) and strongly convex
[

;
43
;
26
]
, or at least satisfy the Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition
[
49
]
.
Yet, in our setting, the multiplicative interaction between model weights and group-level losses induces unbounded cross derivatives, violating the smoothness and convexity assumptions commonly required in the analysis.

Contributions

We have four sets of contributions.

The
badr
framework.

We introduce the
badr
framework for fair learning, which trains models to minimize a user-specified fairness metric subject to group-wise Pareto efficiency.

badr
treats group-wise empirical losses as multiple objectives, seeking solutions where no group loss can be decreased without increasing at least one other.
Within this efficient set,
badr
minimizes the selected fairness metric.
The key idea is to learn the scalarization parameters (group weights) via a bilevel optimization formulation.
The lower level enforces efficiency, whereas the upper level optimizes the fairness criterion, rather than fixing the weights a priori.
This perspective provides a unified basis to compare Pareto-efficient baselines for fair learning (e.g., balanced reweighting and minimax fairness) and enables metric-driven improvements in the learned model.

Large-scale bilevel optimization methods.

Our second contribution is the development of two single-loop bilevel algorithms,
badr-gd
and
badr-sgd
, tailored to our framework
badr
in the deterministic and stochastic settings, respectively.
These algorithms jointly update the model parameters, the dual variable associated with the lower-level optimality conditions, and the group-weight vector through coupled gradient steps. The proposed updates require only first-order information on the fairness metric and approximate second-order information on group losses.
In contrast to traditional bilevel approaches that rely on costly inner-outer loops or strong smoothness assumptions,
badr-gd
and
badr-sgd
operate under mild regularity conditions.
We establish theoretical convergence guarantees in this general bilevel setting, achieving complexity rates of
O(1/T)
for both settings.
Notably, our convergence metric jointly captures stationarity of the implicit upper-level objective and suboptimality of the lower-level solution.
A central challenge in our analysis arises from the absence of
global
smoothness of the lower-level objective with respect to the upper-level variable. In the deterministic setting, we overcome this difficulty by deriving a novel uniform bound on the dual variable generated by the algorithm. In the stochastic setting, such a uniform bound is no longer attainable. To address this issue, we introduce two novel technical arguments: (i) a clipping mechanism that controls the influence of the dual variable in the estimation of the implicit function, and (ii) a new Lyapunov function that extends those previously considered in the literature
[
26
]
by incorporating an additional term specifically designed to handle the unboundedness of the dual variable.
These results constitute a contribution to the bilevel optimization literature beyond the fairness application of this paper.

2.
Fairness through a Pareto lens

We consider a general prediction problem in which the data consists of triplets
(x_{i},y_{i},a_{i})
.
Specifically,
(x_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
represents the input-output pair for individual
i
, and
a_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}=\{1,\ldots,S\}
indicates their sensitive attribute (e.g. gender, race, or similar characteristics).
Traditionally, predictors
f(w,\cdot):{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
are estimated through the empirical risk minimization (ERM) principle. Given training data
(x_{i},y_{i},a_{i})_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}
, this entails solving

(1)

\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\ell(f(w,x_{i})),y_{i}\right],

where
w\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
parametrizes the predictor.
Notably, ERM disregards individuals’ sensitive attribute, potentially resulting in biases across the communities involved
[
9
]
.
In this work we seek to develop a novel learning method that reduces the disparity of performances across communities.
Our approach aligns with the framework of in-process fairness methods
[
15
]
.
In this section, we present an adaptive reweighting scheme to enhance fairness while guaranteeing efficiency across groups performance.

2.1.
Inefficiency of fairness by regularization approaches

Figure 1.
Illustration of the impact of fairness regularization on Pareto efficiency for a logistic regression task evaluated on three subsamples of the ACSEmployment dataset
[
32
]
.
The first row reports the group-wise losses for the two sensitive groups (male vs. female). The two curves correspond to the Pareto front and the regularization path induced by the fairness-by-penalization approach, respectively.
The second row reports the values of the considered fairness metric (individual fairness
[
6
]
) on the Pareto front, together with the performance of several fairness Pareto-efficient methods compared against
badr
.

A natural starting point for quantifying performance disparities across groups is to explicitly examine their respective losses.
Specifically, for each sensitive attribute
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we define the
group loss

F_{a}:w\mapsto F_{a}(w)=n_{a}^{-1}\sum_{i:a_{i}=a}\ell(f(w,x_{i}),y_{i})
, where
n_{a}=\#\{i:a_{i}=a\}
denotes the number of data points corresponding to the sensitive attribute
a
.
Group losses allow to extend the original empirical risk minimization problem (
1
) into a multi-objective formulation:

(2)

\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[F_{1}(w),\ldots,F_{S}(w)\right].

Considering fairness through group losses underpins many fairness-oriented learning methods.
A prominent strategy, often termed minmax fairness, reallocates sampling weight toward the worst-performing groups, thereby reducing (
2
) to a single-objective problem (see
Section

2.3
).
Nonetheless, the full multi-objective formulation in (
2
) admits a broader spectrum of solutions, offering greater flexibility in pursuing fairness.
Such solutions are typically characterized by the concept of Pareto efficiency:

Definition 1

(Pareto Efficiency)
.

A model
\bar{w}
is called
Pareto efficient
for the group losses
(F_{a})_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}
, if for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one of the two following assertions is satisfied

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle F_{a}(w)\geq F_{a}(\bar{w}),\quad\text{for all }a\in{\mathcal{A}},

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle F_{a}(w)>F_{a}(\bar{w}),\quad\text{for some }a\in{\mathcal{A}}.

The set of Pareto efficient point for (
2
), denoted subsequently
{\mathcal{P}}
, is called the
Pareto front
.

Intuitively, Pareto-efficient solutions characterize models for which performance cannot be improved for any group without adversely affecting at least one other.
While this requirement seems natural, it is not necessarily achieved by current in-processing fairness methods.
This can be observed for instance on fairness by constraint methods, which typically involve augmenting the empirical risk minimization (ERM) problem (
1
) with a fairness constraint,

(3)

\displaystyle\min_{w\in{\mathcal{X}}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(w,x_{i}),y_{i})\;\text{s.t.}\;{\mathcal{F}}\text{air}(w)\leq\varepsilon,

or its relaxation through penalization
[
33
]
,

(4)

\min_{w\in{\mathcal{X}}}\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(w,x_{i}),y_{i})+\frac{1}{\nu}\;{\mathcal{F}}\text{air}(w).

Here,
\varepsilon>0
(resp.
\nu>0
) is a user-defined tolerance level that governs the permissible degree of unfairness, and
{\mathcal{F}}\text{air}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbf{R}
denotes the fairness metric used to quantify model bias - we review a number of standard fairness metrics in Appendix
A
.

In Figure
1
we illustrate the effect of fairness regularization on Pareto efficiency across three classification tasks.
We train a logistic regression model on three distinct subsamples of the ACSEmployment dataset
[
32
]
.
In each case, gender (male vs female) is the sensitive attribute.
The model is augmented with an individual-fairness penalty
[
6
]
, and we vary the penalty weight
\nu
over a logarithmic grid from
10^{-6}
to
10^{-0.5}
.
The top row plots the Pareto front of group losses and the regularization path starting from uniform weights.
Both are colored by the individual fairness metric, with the color scale shown by the colorbar.
In the bottom row, we show how the fairness metric changes with the group weights.
This view makes changes in the metric more apparent than the color map.
Across all three datasets, the resulting trajectories significantly depart from the Pareto frontier displayed by the subgroup losses (in blue).
This demonstrates that naive penalization produces solutions that are dominated-and therefore inefficient from a Pareto perspective.

2.2.
Scalarization and the weighted-sum method

Problem (
2
) may be addressed by a broad literature of optimization approaches aiming to recover its Pareto front.
These include scalarization methods, a priori methods, or no-preferences methods - for general references, see
[
11
;
63
]
.
In this work, we will build upon a standard scalarization strategy in the multiobjective literature, the weighted-sum method
[
40
;
41
]
, which given a distribution of weights
\lambda_{a}\in\Delta_{S}
, turns (
2
) into the single optimization problem:

(5)

\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\lambda_{a}F_{a}(w)

Under mild conditions, solutions of (
5
) are guaranteed to be Pareto efficient.
Their closure also spans the entire Pareto front when the Group losses are strongly convex.

Proposition 2

(See
[
41
]
)
.

Let
\lambda\in\Delta_{S}
be fixed. Any solution
w_{\lambda}
of (
5
) is Pareto-efficient for the multi-objective problem (
2
).
Conversely, if the group losses
F_{a}
are convex and at least one of them is strictly convex, then any Pareto efficient point for (
2
) belongs to the closure of
\{w_{\lambda},\lambda\in\Delta_{S}\}
.

When the learning weights
\lambda_{a}
correspond to the proportion
n_{a}/n
of each group in the training sample, one recovers the standard ERM problem (
1
), which therefore outputs by the above Proposition a Pareto-efficient model.
Alternatively, the fairness literature has considered a broad family of data reweighting strategies to alleviate the unfairness of ERM’s output.
Yet these approaches are mostly restricted to binary classification tasks – e.g.
[
13
;
56
]
– and further require weights to jointly depend on sensitive attribute
a
and output label
y
.
This is for instance the case for
[
13
]
which adopts an importance sampling approach to achieve independence on training data between sensitive attribute and output variable.
As a consequence, group loss performance may no longer exhibit Pareto efficiency.

2.3.
Minmax fairness and fairness risk measures as robust scalarizations

From a group losses perspective, min-max fairness
[
64
;
48
;
31
;
1
]
constitutes a popular approach to fairness that focuses on improving performance over worst-performing groups

(6)

\min_{w}\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}F_{a}(w).

Solutions of (
6
) offer robust guarantees on the output model
w
by minimizing the worst-case group error, often resulting in a reduced performance gap across sub-groups.
This highlights their advantage over equal error rate approaches
[
31
]
, which may enforce parity by uniformly degrading performance-even at the expense of disadvantaged groups.

Finding such solutions also present important computational advantages over other in-processing methods, benefiting from the extended literature on stochastic min-max optimization
[
8
]
.
In particular, optimization algorithms for (
6
) are frequently supported with theoretical guarantees including the preservation of convexity for linear tasks (logistic or linear regression) and convergence rates toward optimal or critical point in the non-convex setting.
These perks have spurred the interest of min-max fairness approaches on a broad span of learning tasks, notably in federated learning
[
69
]
.

A recurrent reproach to formulation (
6
) is its potential over-emphasis on the performance of a single-group, which may reveal inadequate in situations where many groups are involved.
To this end, extensions of (
6
) have been considered to provide robustness on distributions over groups, leading to the min-max formulation

(7)

\min_{w}\max_{Q\in{\mathcal{D}}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim Q}[F_{\boldsymbol{a}}(w)].

where
{\mathcal{D}}
denotes a set of probability distributions supported on the simplex
\Delta_{S}
.
Benefits of these extensions have been studied in a number of papers and for various formulations of the ambiguity set
[
74
]
, bridging connections with the theory of risk measures
[
84
;
82
]
.

It turns out problem (
6
) and its extension (
7
) both output Pareto-efficient models when group losses are convex:

Proposition 3
.

Assume the group losses
(F_{a})_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}
are convex. Then any solution of (
6
) or (
7
) is Pareto-efficient.

Proposition
3
constitutes the basis of
[
64
]
to motivate for min-max fairness, although the formulation presented there remains expressed as
\min_{w\in{\mathcal{P}}}\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}F_{a}(w)
with an explicit restriction to
{\mathcal{P}}
.
Subsequent works
[
1
;
31
]
have relaxed the Pareto front constraint, which directly follows from Proposition
3
.
A proof of Proposition (
3
) is provided in
[
79
, Proposition 1]
for the analogous setting in which the ambiguity set
{\mathcal{D}}
consists of distributions supported on the training set, rather than on the set of sensitive attributes.

2.4.
badr
: an
optimal
Pareto-efficient approach to group fairness

In this paper, we propose to learn optimal Pareto efficient models for (
2
) given an arbitrary measure of unfairness
{\mathcal{F}}\text{air}:\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Leveraging the implicit characterization in (
5
), we cast this task as a bilevel program, which we term
badr
(Bilevel Adaptive Rescalarization), and formulate as follows:

(8)

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda~\in\Delta_{S}}\quad

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{F}}\text{air}(w_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle w_{\lambda}\in\arg\min_{w\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\lambda_{a}F_{a}(w)

When the group losses
F_{a}
are strongly convex, solutions of (
8
) are guaranteed to be Pareto-efficient.
Furthermore, provided the unfairness metric
{\mathcal{F}}\text{air}
is differentiable - or admits a tractable smooth approximation - problem (
8
) is amenable to standard bilevel programming methods for large-scale learning
[
73
;
26
]
.
In particular, for any initial point
\lambda
in the simplex, the unfairness of the associated model
w_{\lambda}
can be decreased until a critical point of the implicit loss
\lambda\mapsto{\mathcal{F}}\text{air}(w_{\lambda})
is reached.

Several bilevel approaches to in-processing fairness have been explored in the literature.
[
76
]
proposes to learn a sampling distribution depending jointly on sensitive attributes and labels.
In particular, the inner level objective may not correspond to a weighted sum of group losses, which can compromise Pareto efficiency of the output model.

[
64
;
80
]
study the particular instance of
(
8
)
where the unfairness metric
{\mathcal{F}}\text{air}
corresponds to maximum group loss.

[
71
]
studies a similar problem to (
8
), where the upper level additionally includes the population loss
w\mapsto\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{A}}}\frac{n_{i}}{n}F_{a}(w)
as a penalty term.
While several fairness metrics are considered therein, no connection to Pareto efficiency is established.
On a different note, multi-objective approaches for group fairness have also been considered in
[
59
]
, where the group losses and fairness metrics are taken as competing objectives.

Our general formulation (
8
) offers two key advantages over existing approaches in the literature.
To begin with, it is the first formulation that can accommodate a broad class of fairness metrics while simultaneously ensuring Pareto efficiency across groups.
Second, as shown in the following section, it enables efficient optimization with provable guarantees.

).
We present in Section

badr-gd
.
We further establish in Section

3.1.
Algorithms

We present in Algorithm
1
a simple method, we name
badr-gd
to solve the bilevel problem (
8
) for large-scale instances.
badr-gd
iteratively updates three variables : the learning model
w
, the dual variable
v

Specifically, at each iteration

Q_{t}\triangleq\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\lambda_{t,a}\nabla^{2}F_{a}(w_{t})

Finally, the fairness weights

\lambda\mapsto{\mathcal{F}}\text{air}(w^{\star}(\lambda))

3.2.
Convergence analysis

To analyze the bilevel formulation (
8
), we rewrite it in the form of a general bilevel optimization problem :

(9)

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{C}}}\quad

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{U}}(w(\lambda),\lambda)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle w_{\lambda}\in\arg\min_{w\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}{\mathcal{F}}(w,\lambda)

where
{\mathcal{C}}

denotes a convex compact subset,
{\mathcal{U}}:\mathbf{R}^{d}\times{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathbf{R}
and
{\mathcal{F}}:\mathbf{R}^{d}\times{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathbf{R}
are continuously differentiable functions.
Applied to Problem (
9
), our algorithm
badr-gd
reads :

(10)

\displaystyle w_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow w_{t}-\tau\,\nabla_{w}\,{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})

\displaystyle v_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow v_{t}-\rho\left(\nabla_{w,w}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\,v_{t}+\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\lambda_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\text{Proj}_{\Delta_{{\mathcal{S}}}}\left(\lambda_{t}-\gamma\left(\nabla^{2}_{w,\lambda}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{\top}\,v_{t}+\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\right)\right)

As such it can be seen as a variant of the SOBA method
[
26
]
, adapting its parameterization to the structure of (
8
).
Indeed, in addition to the novel constraint holding on the upper variable
\lambda
, the main distinction with existing works is the absence of global smoothness of the lower-level objective
{\mathcal{F}}
with respect to
\lambda
.
In fact, in the specific setting of Problem (
8
),
{\mathcal{F}}
admits the form
{\mathcal{F}}(w,\lambda)=\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{A}}}\lambda_{s}F_{s}(w)
.
This leads to crossed derivatives of the form:

\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w,\lambda)=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla F_{1}(w)&\nabla F_{2}(w)&\cdots&\nabla F_{S}(w)\end{bmatrix}.

Hence, uniform smoothness cannot hold, since the gradients of group losses
\nabla F_{s}
are unbounded over
\mathbf{R}^{d}
due to the strong convexity of the functions
F_{s}
.
This in turn invalidates the previous convergence analysis of SOBA
[
26
]
, in several places including the Lipschitz structure of the inner solution
w^{\star}:\lambda\mapsto\argmin_{w}{\mathcal{F}}(w,\lambda)
, the smoothness of the implicit function
\varphi:\lambda\mapsto{\mathcal{U}}(w^{\star}(\lambda),\lambda)
or the control of the error on the gradient of its gradient.
In contrast our approach makes use of weaker assumptions on the lower-level objective
{\mathcal{F}}
that remain midly acceptable in practice, and that we clarify below.

Assumption 1
.

The upper objective
{\mathcal{U}}
satisfies the following conditions :

(1)

{\mathcal{U}}
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbf{R}^{d}\times{\mathcal{C}}
with constant
L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}>0
, i.e. for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}
:

|{\mathcal{U}}(w_{1},\lambda_{1})-{\mathcal{U}}(w_{2},\lambda_{2})|\leq L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}\|(w_{1},\lambda_{1})-(w_{2},\lambda_{2})\|

(2)

\nabla{\mathcal{U}}
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbf{R}^{d}\times{\mathcal{C}}
with Lipschitz constant
L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}>0
, i.e. for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}
:

\|\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{1},\lambda_{1})-\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{2},\lambda_{2})\|\leq L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}\left(\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|+\|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\|\right)

Assumption
1
is standard in the bilevel optimization literature
[
43
;
3
;
26
]
and essential to ensure global smoothness of the implicit function
\varphi:\lambda\mapsto{\mathcal{U}}(w^{\star}(\lambda),\lambda)
.
We complement it with the following assumption on the lower-level objective.

Assumption 2
.

The lower objective
{\mathcal{F}}
is
{\mathcal{C}}^{2}
-differentiable and satisfies the following conditions :

(1)

{\mathcal{F}}
is strongly convex with respect to
w
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}\times{\mathcal{C}}
with strong convexity constant
\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}>0
. That is, for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
\lambda\in{\mathcal{C}}
:

{\mathcal{F}}(w_{2},\lambda)\geq{\mathcal{F}}(w_{1},\lambda)+\langle\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{1},\lambda),w_{2}-w_{1}\rangle+\frac{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2}\|w_{2}-w_{1}\|^{2}.

(2)

\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}
is Lipschitz with respect to
w
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
with Lipschitz constant
L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}\geq 0
, uniformly over
{\mathcal{C}}
. That is, for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
\lambda\in{\mathcal{C}}
:

\|\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{1},\lambda)-\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{2},\lambda)\|\leq L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|.

(3)

\nabla^{2}_{w,w}{\mathcal{F}}
is Lipschitz Hessian with respect to
w
on
\mathbf{R}^{d}\times{\mathcal{C}}
with Lipschitz constant
L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}\geq 0
. That is, for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
\lambda\in{\mathcal{C}}
:

\|\nabla^{2}_{w,w}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{1},\lambda)-\nabla^{2}_{w,w}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{2},\lambda)\|\leq L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}(\|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\|+\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|).

(4)

\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}
is uniformly upper bounded by a constant
M_{\mathcal{C}}>0
over the solution set

\{(w^{\star}(\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{2}),(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}\}
, i.e. for all
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}
,

\|\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}(\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{2})\|\leq M_{\mathcal{C}}.

(5)

\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}
is Lipschitz with constant
L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda}>0
. That is for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}
:

\|\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{1},\lambda_{1})-\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{2},\lambda_{2})\|\leq L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda}\left(\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|+\|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\|\right).

First three conditions of Assumption
2
are also common to ensure the well-posedness of the solution mapping
\lambda\mapsto w^{\star}(\lambda)
and the smoothness of the implicit function
\varphi
.
A notable distinction with existing works
[
3
;
61
;
26
]
is the absence of global smoothness of the lower objective with respect to
\lambda
.
Instead, we relax this assumption and only require an upperbound for
\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}
over the solution set
\{(w^{\star}(\lambda),\lambda),\lambda\in{\mathcal{C}}\}
.
Finally, we further require this crossed derivative to be Lipschitz continuous, which would directly follow from a smoothness assumptions on the gradients
\nabla F_{s}
in (
8
).
We note that these assumptions are typically satisfied in learning problems with strongly convex loss functions and standard regularization schemes, such as ridge regression or logistic regression, provided the data are bounded
3
3

3

Our extension to the stochastic setting in Theorem

relaxes this requirement by allowing data with only bounded second-order moments.

Our convergence rate is of order

[

Remark 6
.

An important component of our analysis is the control of the dual variable
v_{t}
along the iterations.
Specifically, we manage to upper bound
\|v_{t}\|
uniformly over time, using the strong convexity of the lower-level problem.
This in turn enables us to control the error
\|\nabla\varphi(\lambda_{t})-(\nabla^{2}_{w,\lambda}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{\top}\,v_{t}+\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t}))\|
on the gradient of the implicit function.

We complement our analysis with convergence guarantees for

laid down in Algorithm

allows for stochastic estimates of the oracles involved in (

Unlike in the deterministic setting, we cannot uniformly control the dual variable

along the iterations as in Lemma

Although uniform boundedness of the dual variable plays a key role in controlling the stochastic error of the implicit gradient estimate

Remark 8
.

In Theorem
7
, the overall convergence metric used also jointly quantifies optimality at the implicit and lower levels.
Regarding optimality at the implicit level, the metric used is the expected norm of the stochastic generalized gradient
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{D}_{\lambda,t}\|\right]
, which slightly differs from the standard metric in stochastic non-convex proximal optimization
[
42
]
, which would in place involve
\mathbb{E}\left[\|D_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}\right]
, the expected squared norm of the generalized stochastic gradient.
Besides having the expectation outside of the norm – which we argue allows for a better characterization of stationarity, as randomness only concerns the path taken up to
\lambda_{t}
and not the stochastic gradient at time
t
– we note note that our results present convergence in gradient norm instead of squared gradient norm, which is a common feature in analyses involving clipping
[
54
]
.

Remark 9
.

The right-hand side of our convergence rate involves three components. First two components upper bound respectively the speed at which we forget the initial conditions and the variance of our stochastic estimates.
We note that the matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
depends on the problem constants introduced in
1
, and does not vanish as
\gamma
goes to zero. Hence, to achieve an
\varepsilon
-stationary point, one may resort to mini-batching to reduce the noise vector
{\mathcal{V}}
to an appropriate norm.
In particular, setting both the minibatch size
m
and the number of iterations
T
to scale as
\varepsilon^{-2}
yields an overall complexity of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})
, which matches the known lower bound with respect to
\varepsilon
for stochastic bilevel problems
[
18
]
. We do not, however, investigate optimality with respect to the condition number
\kappa
, as our framework addresses a broader class of problems, and establishing corresponding lower bounds in this regime lies beyond the scope of the present work.

Remark 10
.

We prove Theorem
7
based on the derivation of a novel Lyapunov function
\psi_{t}
defined as

\psi_{t}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\lambda_{t})+\alpha\|w_{t}-w^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}+\beta\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}+\delta\|w_{t}-w^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}\|v_{t}\|^{2}]

where
\alpha,\beta,\delta>0
are carefully tuned parameters.
The decrease of
\psi_{t}
is made possible thanks to the clipping of the upper-level update, which allows specifically to control the drift on the fourth term
\mathbb{E}[\delta\|w_{t}-w^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}\|v_{t}\|^{2}]
of our Lyapunov function.
This term is responsible for the presence of the additional starting error
\varepsilon_{w,v,0}^{2}
in our convergence rate.

4.4.
Algorithms

The
badr
toolbox mainly includes an implementation of
Algorithms

1
and
2
, the large-scale algorithms
badr-gd
and
badr-sgd
to solve the bilevel problem (
8
).
We also allow the use of two-loop algorithms to solve small and medium scale instances of (
8
).

Two-loop methods for bilevel optimization work as follows: at each iteration, the lower-level problem is (approximately) solved using an iterative method (inner loop).
Its solution is used to compute an implicit gradient which is used to update the outer variable, also using an iterative method (outer loop).
This is a standard approach to solving bilevel optimization problems
[
34
;
43
]
, but it can be computationally expensive and less scalable than single-loop algorithms, as the lower-level must be solved multiple times, and solving the adjoint equation to compute the gradient of the solution mapping typically requires a matrix inversion.

4.4.1.
Default choices for the optimization method

In the remainder of this section, we present the optimization methods available in the toolbox for two-loop algorithms.
We outline the default parameters and stopping criteria for each method.
We identify the default two-loop method and justify this choice.
We discuss how the wall-clock time of each method scales with an increasing number of groups.
Finally, we compare the scalability of the default two-loop method with
badr-sgd
.

Implementation.

We implement the two-loop algorithms by (i) solving the lower-level problem with the

\ell_{2}^{2}
-regularized logistic regression model, and the
\ell_{2}^{2}

For logistic regression and smoothed SVM, we obtain the lower-level gradient and Hessian with

10

and solve the resulting linear system with

Available optimization methods.

As outlined in

[

Stopping criteria.

Each of the three optimization methods has its own stopping criterion, in addition to a maximum iteration limit of

5.1.2.
Learning Models

We consider three common models for supervised learning, which losses we describe below.

•

\ell_{2}^{2}
-regularized logistic regression:

\ell(f(w,x_{i}),y_{i})=\log\left(1+\exp(-y_{i}\langle w,x_{i}\rangle)\right)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|w\|^{2}

This model is used for binary classification tasks.

•

\ell_{2}^{2}
regularized smoothed SVM:

\ell(f(w,x_{i}),y_{i})=\max\{0,\,1-y_{i}\langle w,x_{i}\rangle\}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|w\|^{2}

This model is also used for binary classification tasks.

•

Ridge regression

\ell(f(w,x_{i}),y_{i})=\left(y_{i}-\langle w,x_{i}\rangle\right)^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|w\|^{2}

This model is used for regression tasks.

Note that

Methods

badr
outputs a set of group weights
(\lambda_{a}^{*})_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\in\Delta_{{\mathcal{S}}}

a)

b)

we train the model without group reweighting that is the group weights vector is the barycenter of the simplex

c)

Balanced sampling strategy:
we train the model using group weights that rebalance the dataset, assigning each group a weight inversely proportional to its sample size and normalizing them to unit sum.

d)

One-group fitting strategy:
we train separately one model per sensitive group and keep the fairest model, that is the model trained on group
\arg\min_{a\in|{\mathcal{A}}|}{\mathcal{F}}\left(\theta^{*}\left(\mathbf{e_{a}}\right)\right)

6.
Concluding remarks

In this work, we introduce
badr
, a bilevel framework for fair learning that selects, among group-wise Pareto-efficient models, the one that optimizes a user-specified fairness metric.
We achieve this by treating group scalarization weights as variables learned through a bilevel formulation: the lower level enforces Pareto efficiency of group losses, while the upper level optimizes the chosen unfairness criterion.
This perspective makes it possible to target a specific fairness metric without sacrificing efficiency, unlike fixed reweighting schemes or robust minimax objectives whose effect on a given metric can be difficult to control.
From an optimization standpoint, we develop scalable single-loop algorithms for solving this bilevel problem and establish convergence guarantees under mild regularity conditions, despite the lack of global smoothness induced by the coupling between weights and group losses.
We complement these theoretical results with an open-source toolbox,
badr
, that implements our approach for multiple fairness metrics and standard learning models, and we demonstrate on real datasets that
badr
improves the targeted metric over Pareto-efficient baselines while preserving Pareto-efficient group performance.

This framework opens several research directions at the intersection of multi-objective optimization, bilevel optimization, and statistical fairness. A particularly important direction concerns strengthening the statistical understanding of metric-driven selection over the Pareto front, especially the out-of-sample behavior of the selected fairness metric and the degree to which improvements observed on the empirical Pareto front translate into test performance.

corresponds to the variance of the group losses, that is

{\mathcal{F}}\text{air}_{\mathrm{GV}}(w)=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{S}}}\sum_{a=1}^{{\mathcal{S}}}\left(F_{a}(w)-\frac{1}{{\mathcal{S}}}\sum_{b=1}^{{\mathcal{S}}}F_{b}(w)\right)^{2}.

We generalizes the definition for two groups proposed in

\mathrm{IF}(w)\triangleq\frac{1}{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq a<b\leq{\mathcal{S}}}n_{a}n_{b}}\sum_{1\leq a<b\leq{\mathcal{S}}}\sum_{i\,:\,a_{i}=a}\sum_{j\,:\,a_{j}=b}e^{-|y_{i}-y_{j}|}\bigl(f(w,x_{i})-f(w,x_{j})\bigr)^{2}

13

{\mathcal{F}}\text{air}_{\mathrm{DP}}(w)=\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\left|p_{a}(w)-\overline{p}(w)\right|
, where

Lemma 11

( Lipschitz-continuity of the solution map)
.

Under
2

\lambda\mapsto w^{\star}(\lambda)
is
M_{{\mathcal{C}}}/\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}
-Lipschitz.
That is, for any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\lambda}}

\|w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})-w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})\|\leq\frac{M_{{\mathcal{C}}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\|
.

Proof.

By strong convexity of
{\mathcal{F}}
with respect to
w
, we have for any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\lambda}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2}\|w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})\!-\!w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}(\lambda_{2}),\lambda_{2})-{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}(\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\!\left\langle\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}(\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{2}),\;w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})\!-\!w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2}\|w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})\!-\!w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}(\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{2})-{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}(\lambda_{2}),\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\!\left\langle\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}(\lambda_{2}),\lambda_{2}),\;w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})\!-\!w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})\right\rangle.

Hence, summing the two inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}\left\|w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})-w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}\big(w^{\star}(\lambda_{2}),\lambda_{2}\big)-\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}\big(w^{\star}(\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{2}\big),w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})-w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})\right\rangle.

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}\big(w^{\star}(\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{2}\big),w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})-w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})\right\rangle.

where the equality follows from the optimality condition
\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}\big(w^{\star}(\lambda_{2}),\lambda_{2}\big)=0
.
Moreover, using
2
.4, we have

\|\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}\big(w^{\star}(\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{2}\big)\|=\|\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}\big(w^{\star}(\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{2}\big)-\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}\big(w^{\star}(\lambda_{2}),\lambda_{2}\big)\|\leq M_{{\mathcal{C}}}\|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\|.

and by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}\left\|w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})-w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})\right\|^{2}\leq M_{{\mathcal{C}}}\|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\|\cdot\left\|w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})-w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})\right\|.

Hence,
\left\|w^{\star}(\lambda_{2})-w^{\star}(\lambda_{1})\right\|\leq\frac{M_{{\mathcal{C}}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\|
.
∎

and Lemma
11
, we have :

\displaystyle\|w_{t+1}-w^{\star}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|w_{t+1}-w^{\star}_{t}+w^{\star}_{t}-w^{\star}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|w_{t+1}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}+2\langle w_{t+1}-w^{\star}_{t},w^{\star}_{t}-w^{\star}_{t+1}\rangle+\|w^{\star}_{t}-w^{\star}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(1-\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}\Bigr)\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}+\Bigl(\tfrac{M_{{\mathcal{C}}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\Bigr)^{2}\gamma^{2}\|D_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}+2\langle w_{t+1}-w^{\star}_{t},w^{\star}_{t}-w^{\star}_{t+1}\rangle.

Using Young’s inequality, and Lemma
14
, we obtain for any
\eta>0
:

\displaystyle\langle w_{t+1}-w^{\star}_{t},w^{\star}_{t}-w^{\star}_{t+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{2}\|w_{t+1}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\eta}\|w^{\star}_{t}-w^{\star}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{2}(1-\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\eta}\Bigl(\tfrac{M_{{\mathcal{C}}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\Bigr)^{2}\gamma^{2}\|D_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}.

Taking
\eta=\frac{\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2(1-\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})}
finally gives

\displaystyle\|w_{t+1}-w^{\star}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2}\Bigr)\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+\Bigl(\frac{2}{\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}-1\Bigr)\Bigl(\tfrac{M_{{\mathcal{C}}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\Bigr)^{2}\gamma^{2}\|D_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2}\Bigr)\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+\frac{2}{\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\Bigl(\tfrac{M_{{\mathcal{C}}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\Bigr)^{2}\gamma^{2}\|D_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}.

∎

B.1.3.
Dual control

In this paragraph, we derive several bounds on the dual iterates
v_{t}
.
We start with a boundedness result on the sequence
(v_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
This result is crucial for the control of the error made on the upper-level gradient estimate in Section
B.1.4
, as it allows to circumvent the issue of having a potentially unbounded Hessian
\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}
for the lower level objective.
In particular, prior analyses
[
43
;
26
]
do not address these issues.

Lemma 16

( Boundness of the dual variable)
.

Assume that the dual stepsize satisfies
\rho\leq\tfrac{1}{L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}}
.
Then, the sequence
(v_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies for all
t\geq 0
,

\|v_{t}\|\leq\frac{4L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\varepsilon_{v,0},

where
\varepsilon_{v,0}\triangleq\|v_{0}-v^{\star}(w_{0},\lambda_{0})\|^{2}
.

Proof.

Let us now show by induction that for all
t\geq 0
,

\|v_{t}\|\leq\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}+\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{s/2}\cdot\frac{2L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\right]+(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{t/2}\varepsilon_{v,0}.

First note that for all
t\geq 0
,
\|v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|=\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\leq\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}
.
Thus, for
t=0
, we obtain,

\|v_{1}\|\leq\|v_{1}-v^{\star}(w_{0},\lambda_{0})\|+\|v^{\star}(w_{0},\lambda_{0})\|\leq\varepsilon_{v,0}+\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}},

which confirms the base case (using the convention that empty sums are null).
Assume now the result holds for some
t\geq 0
. Then, we have by Lemma
14

\displaystyle\|v_{t+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{t+1}-v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|+\|v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{1/2}\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|+\|v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{1/2}\|v_{t}\|+(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{1/2}\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}+\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{1/2}\begin{aligned} &\left(\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{s/2}\cdot\frac{2L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}+(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{t/2}\varepsilon_{v,0}\right)\\
&+(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{1/2}\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}+\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{s/2}\cdot\frac{2L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}+(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{(t+1)/2}\varepsilon_{v,0},

This finishes the induction and we can conclude that for all
t\geq 0
,

\|v_{t}\|\leq\frac{2L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})^{t/2}+\varepsilon_{v,0}=\frac{4L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\varepsilon_{v,0}.

where we used the inequality
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(1-\alpha)^{t/2}\leq\tfrac{2}{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
∎

We now move to a bound on the drift of the dual solution
v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})
along the iterates, which will be useful to control the dual error
\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|
in the upcoming Lemma
19
.

Lemma 17

( Drift of the dual solution)
.

For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\|v^{\star}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})-v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\leq M_{v^{\star}}\left(\tau L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+\gamma\|D_{\lambda,t}\|\right)

where
M_{v^{\star}}\triangleq\Bigl(L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}\frac{L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\Bigr)
.

Proof.

Observe that,

\displaystyle\|v^{\star}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})-v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})^{-1}\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})\!-\!\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq\begin{aligned} &\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})^{-1}\!-\!\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\|\cdot\|\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})\|\\
&\!+\!\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\|\cdot\|\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})-\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\end{aligned}

Therefore, using similar arguments as in the proof of Lemma
12
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})^{-1}-\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}\left(\|w_{t+1}-w_{t}\|+\|\lambda_{t+1}-\lambda_{t}\|\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}\left(\tau\|\nabla_{w}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|+\gamma\|D_{\lambda,t}\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}\left(\tau L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+\gamma\|D_{\lambda,t}\|\right)

where last line follows from
2
.2.
Besides, using Assumptions
1
.2 and
2
.1, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\|\cdot\|\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})-\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\left(\|w_{t+1}-w_{t}\|+\|\lambda_{t+1}-\lambda_{t}\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\left(\tau L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+\gamma\|D_{\lambda,t}\|\right)

Hence, using one last time
1
.1, we get overall

\displaystyle\|v^{\star}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})-v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}\frac{L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}\left(\tau L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+\gamma\|D_{\lambda,t}\|\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\left(\tau L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+\gamma\|D_{\lambda,t}\|\right)

\displaystyle=M_{v^{\star}}\left(\tau L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+\gamma\|D_{\lambda,t}\|\right)

∎

To get a bound on the dual error
\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|
, we need finally to control the partial error stemming from the drift of the lower variable
w_{t}
with respect to the dual solution
\|v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|
.

Lemma 18

( Lower drift impact on the dual solution)
.

For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\|v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|\leq M_{v^{\star}}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\|v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|

\displaystyle=\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})-\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq\begin{aligned} &\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}-\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\|\cdot\|\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\\
&+\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\|\cdot\|\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})-\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\end{aligned}

and using the same arguments as in the proof of Lemma
12
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}-\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\|\cdot\|\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}\cdot L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{w,w}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})^{-1}\|\cdot\|\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})-\nabla{\mathcal{U}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|.

Summing the two above inequalities gives the result.
∎

Our recursive bound on the dual error
\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|
finally writes as follows.

Lemma 19

( Control of the dual error)
.

For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\|v_{t+1}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{aligned} \;&\left(1-\frac{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2}\right)\;\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}+\frac{107}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\cdot M_{v^{\star}}^{2}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t})\|^{2}\\
&+\frac{25}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\;M_{v^{\star}}^{2}\begin{aligned} \bigg[&\frac{2}{\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\left(\frac{M_{\mathcal{C}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\right)^{2}+3\bigg]\gamma^{2}\|D_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}.\end{aligned}\end{aligned}

Proof.

For any
\alpha,\beta>0
, we have by Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|v_{t+1}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha)\;\|v_{t+1}-v^{\star}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}+(1+\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\;\|v^{\star}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{aligned} &(1+\alpha)\;(1+\beta)\;\|v_{t+1}-v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|^{2}\\
&+(1+\alpha)\;(1+1/\beta)\;\|v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})-v^{\star}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}\\
&+(1+1/\alpha)\;\|v^{\star}(w_{t+1},\lambda_{t+1})-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}\end{aligned}

Thus, plugging Lemmas
14
,
17
, and
18
into the above inequality gives

(21)

\displaystyle\|v_{t+1}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha)(1+\beta)(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle+(1+\alpha)(1+1/\beta)\,2\tau^{2}(L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w})^{2}\Bigl(L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}\frac{L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\Bigr)^{2}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+(1+1/\alpha)\Bigl(L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}\frac{L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\Bigr)^{2}\|w_{t+1}-w^{\star}_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+(1+\alpha)(1+1/\beta)\,2\gamma^{2}\Bigl(L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}\frac{L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,w}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\Bigr)^{2}\|D_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}.

Leveraging one more time Lemma
18
together with Young’s inequality, we derive for any
\delta>0

\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|^{2}\leq(1+\delta)\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}+(1+1/\delta)\;M_{v^{\star}}^{2}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}

Plugging the above inequality together with Lemma (
14
) into (
21
) gives after some rearrangement:

(22)

\displaystyle\|v_{t+1}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{aligned} \;&(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})\;(1+\alpha)\;(1+\beta)\;(1+\delta)\;\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}\\
&+M_{v^{\star}}^{2}\begin{aligned} \bigg[&(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})\;(1+\alpha)\;(1+\beta)\;(1+1/\delta)\\
&+(1+\alpha)\;(1+1/\beta)\cdot 2\tau^{2}(L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w})^{2}\\
&+(1+1/\alpha)\;(1-\frac{\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2})\bigg]\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t})\|^{2}\end{aligned}\;\\
&+M_{v^{\star}}^{2}\begin{aligned} \bigg[&(1+1/\alpha)\;\frac{2}{\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\left(\frac{M_{\mathcal{C}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\right)^{2}\\
&+2(1+\alpha)\;(1+1/\beta)\Bigg]\gamma^{2}\|D_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}.\end{aligned}\end{aligned}

Finally, setting
\alpha=\beta=\delta=\frac{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{24}
, we observe that
\alpha\leq\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{F}}}{24L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}}\leq 1
. This first ensures that
(1+\alpha)^{3}\leq 1+\alpha\sup_{1\leq s\leq 2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}[t^{3}]\leq 1+12\alpha
,
which in turn implies

\displaystyle(1+\alpha)^{3}\cdot(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})

\displaystyle\leq(1+12\alpha)(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})=(1+\frac{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2})(1-\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})\leq(1-\frac{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2}).

And since
\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle(1+1/\alpha)

\displaystyle=1+24/(\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}})\leq\frac{25}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}

\displaystyle(1+\alpha)\cdot(1+1/\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq(25/24)\cdot(1+1/\alpha)\leq\frac{27}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}

\displaystyle(1+\alpha)^{2}\cdot(1+1/\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq(25/24)^{2}\cdot(1+1/\alpha)\leq\frac{28}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}

This allows to simplify (
22
) into

\displaystyle\|v_{t+1}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{aligned} \;&\left(1-\frac{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}{2}\right)\;\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}\\
&+\frac{107}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\cdot M_{v^{\star}}^{2}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t})\|^{2}\\
&+\frac{25}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\;M_{v^{\star}}^{2}\begin{aligned} \bigg[&\frac{2}{\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\left(\frac{M_{\mathcal{C}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\right)^{2}+3\bigg]\gamma^{2}\|D_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}.\end{aligned}\end{aligned}

where we also used the upper bounds
\tau\leq 1/L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}
and
(1-\tau\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}/2)\leq 1
.
∎

B.1.4.
Control of the error on implicit gradient estimation

We now turn to the control of the error on the implicit gradient estimate
\|\nabla\varphi(\lambda_{t})-D^{in}_{\lambda,t}\|
.
This proposition will be combined with the descent Lemma
13
on the implicit objective to derive our Lyapunov analysis in the upcoming Section
B.1.5
.

Lemma 20

( Error bound on the implicit gradient)
.

For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla\varphi(\lambda_{t})-D^{in}_{\lambda,t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}+L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda}\left(\frac{4L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\varepsilon_{v,0}\right)\right)\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+M_{\mathcal{C}}\;\|v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})-v_{t}\|.

Proof.

For any
t\geq 0
, we have by the triangular inequality:

\displaystyle\|\nabla\varphi(\lambda_{t})\!-\!D^{in}_{\lambda,t}\|

\displaystyle\!=\!\|\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})\!+\!\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})^{\top}v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\!-\!(\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\!+\!\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{\top}v_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})\!-\!\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\!+\!\|\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})^{\top}v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\!-\!\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{\top}v_{t}\|

The first term can be upper bounded using
1
.2:
\|\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})-\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\leq L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|
.
We now focus on the second term. First observe that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})-\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})v_{t}\|

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\leq\begin{aligned} &\|\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\cdot\|v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})-v_{t}\|+\|\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})-\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\cdot\|v_{t}\|\end{aligned}

By Assumptions
2
.4 and
2
.5, we note that
\|\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\leq M_{\mathcal{C}}
, and
\|\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})-\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|\leq L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|
.
Furthermore, Lemma
16
gives
\|v_{t}\|\leq\frac{4L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\varepsilon_{v,0}
, so that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})-\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})v_{t}\|\leq M_{\mathcal{C}}\;\|v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})-v_{t}\|+L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda}\left(\frac{4L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\varepsilon_{v,0}\right)\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|.

Hence, we have overall

\displaystyle\|\nabla\varphi(\lambda_{t})-D^{in}_{\lambda,t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}+L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda}\left(\frac{4L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\varepsilon_{v,0}\right)\right)\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+M_{\mathcal{C}}\;\|v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})-v_{t}\|.

∎

As a direct consequence of the above lemma and the non-expansiveness of the projection operator on the convex set
{\mathcal{C}}
, we immediately have the following corollary.

Corollary 21

( Error bound on the generalized gradient)
.

For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\|D_{\lambda,t}-\bar{D}_{\lambda,t}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}+L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda}\left(\frac{4L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\rho\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}}+\varepsilon_{v,0}\right)\right)\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|+M_{\mathcal{C}}\;\|v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})-v_{t}\|.

B.1.5.
Proof of Theorem
4

Proof.

Let us introduce the Lyapunov function
\psi_{t}=\varphi(\lambda_{t})+\alpha\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}+\beta\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}
for some
\alpha,\beta>0
.
By Lemma
28
, setting
\alpha
and
\beta
as

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=6\gamma\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}\left(L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}+L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda}\left(4\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}+\varepsilon_{v,0}\right)\right)^{2}+1284\gamma\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}^{3}M_{\mathcal{C}}^{2}M_{v^{\star}}^{2}

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=3\gamma\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}M_{\mathcal{C}}^{2},

together with the parameterization
\tau=\rho=\frac{1}{L_{{\mathcal{F}},1}^{w,w}}
, and
\gamma\leq\min(\frac{1}{\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}},\bar{\gamma})
where

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}^{-1}=

\displaystyle 2{L_{\varphi}}+48\;\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}\left(\tfrac{M_{{\mathcal{C}}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\right)^{2}\left(L_{{\mathcal{U}},1}+L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda}\left(4\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}\frac{L_{{\mathcal{U}},0}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}+\varepsilon_{v,0}\right)\right)^{2}

(23)

\displaystyle+10272\;\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}^{3}\frac{M_{\mathcal{C}}^{4}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}^{2}}}M_{v^{\star}}^{2}+300\;\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}M_{\mathcal{C}}^{2}\;M_{v^{\star}}^{2}\bigg[2\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}\left(\frac{M_{\mathcal{C}}}{\mu_{{\mathcal{F}}}}\right)^{2}+3\bigg].

ensures that
\psi_{t+1}\leq\psi_{t}-\frac{\gamma}{8}\|\bar{D}_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\alpha}{4\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}
for all
t\geq 0
.
Summing this inequality for
t=0,\ldots,T-1
gives

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\bar{D}_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{\gamma\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}\leq\frac{8}{\gamma T}(\psi_{0}-\psi_{T})\leq\frac{8}{\gamma T}(\varphi(\lambda_{0})-\inf\varphi+\alpha\varepsilon_{w,0}^{2}+\beta\varepsilon_{v,0}^{2}).

Setting
\bar{\alpha}=2\alpha/(\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}\;\gamma)
and
\bar{\beta}=2\beta/(\kappa_{{\mathcal{F}},w}\;\gamma)
concludes the proof.

∎

B.2.
The stochastic case

We now turn to the proof of Theorem

.

Lemma 26

( Stochastic error bound on the implicit gradient)
.

For any
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\varphi(\lambda_{t})-D^{in}_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}\right]\leq\begin{aligned} &2(L_{{\mathcal{U}},1})^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}]+4M_{\mathcal{C}}^{2}\;\mathbb{E}[\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}]+4(L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda})^{2}\;\mathbb{E}[\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}\cdot\|v_{t}\|^{2}]\\
&+{\mathcal{V}}_{{\mathcal{U}},1}^{\lambda}+{\mathcal{V}}_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda}\;{\mathcal{C}}_{v,\star}.\end{aligned}

and
\mathbb{E}\left[\|D_{\lambda,t}-\bar{D}_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}\right]
satisfies the same upper bound.

Proof.

First note that, by
3
, we have
\mathbb{E}[D^{in}_{\lambda,t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})+\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{\top}v_{t}

Hence, for any

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\varphi(\lambda_{t})-\mathbb{E}[D^{in}_{\lambda,t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 27.74982pt\leq\begin{aligned} &2\|\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})-\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|^{2}+2\|\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w^{\star}_{t},\lambda_{t})^{\top}v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})-\nabla_{w,\lambda}^{2}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})^{\top}v_{t}\|^{2}\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\hskip 27.74982pt\leq\begin{aligned} &2(L_{{\mathcal{U}},1})^{2}\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}+4M_{\mathcal{C}}^{2}\;\|v_{t}-v^{\star}(\lambda_{t})\|^{2}+4(L_{{\mathcal{F}},2}^{w,\lambda})^{2}\;\|w_{t}-w^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}\cdot\|v_{t}\|^{2}.\end{aligned}

Furthermore, we have by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbb{E}[D^{in}_{\lambda,t}]-D^{in}_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t,v}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})-\stonabla_{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}(w_{t},\lambda_{t})\|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t,v}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\varphi(\lambda_{t})-D^{in}_{\lambda,t}\|^{2}\right]
