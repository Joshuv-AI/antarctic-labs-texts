Pro-inflammatory cytokines moderated by visceral adiposity from a pragmatic randomized crossover trial of portable HEPA air purifiers
Authors: Doug Brugge, Misha Eliasziw, Lindsay Tallon, Chermaine Morson, Humza Rashid, Wig Zamore
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAirborne particulate matter (PM) increases the risk of cardiovascular disease. High efficiency particulate arrestance (HEPA) filtration can reduce indoor PM, but evidence is lacking as to whether HEPA filtration has an effect on pro-inflammatory cytokines.MethodsThis pragmatic crossover trial randomly assigned participants to 30 days of HEPA or sham filtration, followed by a 30-day washout, and then 30 days of the alternative. Concentrations of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin 6 (IL-6), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) were measured in quality-controlled peripheral blood plasma samples using ultra-sensitive assays. Linear mixed models compared HEPA to sham filtration. The moderating effect of visceral adipose tissue (VAT) was assessed using a dichotomized A Body Shape Index (ABSI) z-score and other measures of adiposity.ResultsAmong 116 participants, mean age was 41.5 years, 58.6% were female, and 72.4% were non-Hispanic White. No overall difference in mean IL-1β concentration was observed between HEPA and sham filtration. However, VAT significantly moderated the results (test for interaction p = 0.001). Among participants with excessive VAT (ABSI z-score ≥ +0.229, N = 12), HEPA filtration significantly reduced mean IL-1β concentration by 22.8% compared with sham filtration (0.138 versus 0.179 pg/mL, p < 0.001). No reduction was observed among participants with healthy VAT. Similar non-significant patterns were observed for IL-6, but little effect for hsCRP. Other measures of adiposity, including BMI, were less effective or did not moderate the results.ConclusionsVAT moderated inflammatory responses to HEPA filtration, with a significant reduction in IL-1β among participants with excessive VAT.

Introduction

Airborne particulate matter (PM), usually measured as particles <2.5 µm in aerodynamic diameter (PM2.5), is a leading cause of cardiovascular disease and mortality [1]. Major sources of PM2.5 in large cities include combustion sources, such as outdoor motor vehicle exhaust and indoor cooking fumes [2]. Numerous biological mechanisms have been proposed or implicated on the path from PM2.5 exposure to overt disease, including inflammation, oxidative stress, coagulation, and effects on the autonomic nervous system [3,4].

People living adjacent to roadways or highways with high traffic volume are also exposed to elevated concentrations of ultrafine particles (UFP, ≤ 0.1 µm in aerodynamic diameter) and black carbon [5]. In the absence of modern ventilation systems with high quality filtration, a substantial portion of these particles can penetrate the indoor living space [5,6], leading to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [7,8]. Given that large populations are exposed to near-source traffic pollution, it is important to evaluate interventions to reduce this risk [9].

In-home portable air purifiers, usually fitted with high efficiency particulate arrestance (HEPA) filters, have been evaluated in several clinical trials to reduce indoor PM exposures and cardiovascular risk factors, including blood pressure [10]. Randomized crossover trials have also examined the benefits of air purifiers on pro-inflammatory cytokines linked to cardiovascular disease, but most were small, and some had artificial constraints on participants’ activities of daily living [11].

Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) is the primary product of the NLRP3 inflammasome, a plausible pathway by which UFPs could initiate inflammation (Fig. 1). However, few randomized crossover trials have examined the effectiveness of air purifiers on IL-1β. Moreover, the trials that have measured IL-1β used older assays with high limits of detection (LOD) and did not report quality control measures for blood sample handling and processing. Nevertheless, two of these trials reported reductions in IL-1β concentrations of 68% [12] and 11% [13]. Other pro-inflammatory cytokines have also been examined in randomized sham-controlled trials. Here we report the secondary outcomes from the Home Air Filtration for Traffic-Related Air Pollution (HAFTRAP) trial whose primary outcome was reduction in blood pressure [15]. These secondary, inflammatory outcomes are based on strict exclusion of questionable blood samples and used ultra-sensitive ELISA assays for the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6, as well as a high-sensitivity assay for CRP (hsCRP). The study methods have been previously published [16] and the trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04279249) on February 19, 2020. The trial received approval from the UConn Health Institutional Review Board Human Subjects Protection Program and was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Methods

Trial design and participants

The HAFTRAP trial was a pragmatic randomized crossover trial of customized commercial air purifiers which contained only HEPA filters (HealthMate, Austin Air). Residences of participants who lived full time within 200 m of highways in Somerville, Massachusetts were randomized in a blinded manner to 30 days of either HEPA or sham filtration, during which air purifiers were placed in both the living room and bedroom. This was followed by a 30-day washout period, and then a subsequent 30-day period of the alternative intervention.

The trial enrolled individuals who were 30 years of age or older, had the cognitive ability to respond to questionnaires, and spoke English or Spanish. We excluded individuals who were taking anti-hypertensive medications or had a history of major cardiovascular events, currently smoked/vaped or allowed smoking/vaping inside the home. We also excluded individuals who had occupational or regular exposures to traffic pollution outside of the home, or significant combustion sources, other than cooking, inside the home. Participants were enrolled during the cooler months, between September and April 2020–2024, when traffic-related UFP concentrations have been shown to be elevated in this geographic area [17].

Although there is a large body of literature showing that HEPA air purifiers reduce PM indoors [18], we conducted indoor air monitoring of PM2.5 in 19 homes and of UFPs in 8 homes for one week each during both HEPA and sham filtration periods. Based on self-report and objective measures, participants had their air purifiers running virtually all the time [19]. As previously reported, there was a 52% reduction of indoor PM2.5 (mean 2.5 vs 5.2 μg/m3) and a 32% reduction (mean 4706 vs 6925 particles/cm3) of indoor UFP during HEPA versus sham filtration [15].

At the start and end of each intervention period, venous fasting blood samples were collected by a phlebotomist early on weekday mornings in the participant’s residence. Blood was drawn into 4 mL lithium heparin tubes which were stored on ice until delivery at the lab. Blood was then centrifuged to produce plasma, buffy coat, and red blood cells, and stored at −80 °C until lab analysis.

Laboratory methods

Blood samples that were not centrifuged within 24 h of arrival to the lab and those with gross hemolysis were excluded as these conditions can affect lab results. Concentrations of IL-1β and IL-6 were determined in plasma using ultra-sensitive ELISA assays from R & D Systems (Minneapolis, MN). The assays employ a quantitative sandwich enzyme immunoassay technique, whereby monoclonal antibodies specific for IL-1β or for IL-6 are pre-coated onto a microtiter plate. A color is generated that is proportional to the amount of IL-1β and IL-6 present in the sample. The IL-1β assay has a LOD of 0.063 pg/mL and the inter-assay precision (CV%) at concentrations of 0.27, 1.36 and 2.60 pg/mL are 10.7, 7.4 and 6.3%, respectively. The IL-6 assay has a LOD of 0.09 pg/mL and the inter-assay precision (CV%) at concentrations of 0.53, 2.75 and 5.58 pg/mL are 10.8, 4.9 and 3.9%, respectively. The concentration of hsCRP in plasma was determined using a particle enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay on the Roche Cobas 6000 system (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN). This assay uses an antigen-antibody reaction between CRP in the sample and an anti-CRP antibody that has been sensitized to latex particles, which causes a decrease in transmitted light detected spectrophotometrically. The magnitude of the change is proportional to the concentration of CRP in the sample. This high-sensitivity assay has a LOD of 0.15 mg/L, and the inter-assay precision (CV%) at concentrations of 0.53 and 13.30 mg/L are 8.4 and 2.1%, respectively.

Resulting lab values were excluded if they were below the LOD. Lab values corresponding to the start of the two intervention periods were also excluded if they exceeded generally accepted upper limits of normal for healthy individuals, defined as > 1 pg/mL for IL-1β, > 3.7 pg/mL for IL-6, and > 10 mg/L for hsCRP [20]. These thresholds were considered to be indicative of either a current acute or a chronic inflammatory illness, and not healthy baseline levels.

After accounting for all previously mentioned exclusions, final processing of the values was performed on participants who had a complete set of four values. Acute inflammatory processes during the intervention periods were identified by comparing the intra-individual variation (CVI%) among the participant’s four values to the inter-individual variation (CVG%), calculated from pooling all participant values corresponding to the start of the two intervention periods. Participants were excluded if CVI% > CVG%, as one or more within-participant values exceeded reasonable biological variation [21].

Statistical analyses

Linear mixed models were used to compare the pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations between the HEPA and sham filtration groups at the end of the intervention periods. As the distributions of the pro-inflammatory cytokines were right-skewed, their levels were natural log transformed (Supplementary Figure). The models included a random intercept to account for the within-participant correlation of repeated blood draws, and sequence and period effects. The corresponding pro-inflammatory cytokine concentration at the start of each intervention period was included as a covariate in the models, as well as two time-varying factors, the number of hours per day spent indoors at home a week prior to the blood draw and Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-4) score at the time of the blood draw. Adjusting the results for the number of hours per day spent indoors at home accounted for some participants spending more time inside the home and thus receiving potentially more benefit from air purification. Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-4) scores were included in the models as perceived stress has been linked to increased levels pro-inflammatory cytokines [22,23].

As adipose tissue, in particular visceral adipose tissue (VAT), is a major source of pro-inflammatory cytokines [24,25], the moderating effect of excessive VAT was assessed. In post hoc analyses, we considered a variety of adiposity measures for excessive VAT deemed to increase the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. A cross-product term consisting of the intervention (HEPA vs sham) and the binary cut point for excessive VAT was included in the models to assess the moderating effect. While Computed Tomography (CT), Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), and, alternatively, Dual-energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA), scans are considered standards for measuring VAT [26], there is currently no universally accepted standard for measuring VAT from anthropometric measurements [27].

Although body mass index (BMI) is widely used to assess excess body weight, with a cut point of ≥ 25 kg/m2 for overweight and ≥ 30 kg/m2 for obesity, BMI does not differentiate adipose tissue from muscle mass [28]. Waist circumference (WC) has been deemed to be a superior indicator of VAT than BMI [29,30], with disease risk cut points of ≥ 102 cm for males and ≥ 88 cm for females [31]. A limitation of WC is its high correlation with BMI [32]. Because WC is influenced by overall body size, waist-to-hip ratio (WHpR) with cut points of ≥ 0.90 for males and ≥ 0.85 for females [31] and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) with a cut point of ≥ 0.5 [33] have been proposed to overcome this limitation.

Recently, a new index has been proposed that further addresses the above-mentioned limitations. A body shape index (ABSI), defined as WC, relative to height and weight [34], has been shown to be an indirect measure of VAT [35,36] and not correlated with BMI [34,35]. The ABSI scores are converted to age and sex standardized z scores, yielding a cut point of ≥ +0.229 [34].

All statistical analyses were carried out using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC), and results with p-values < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Despite the small overall reductions in mean concentrations between HEPA and sham filtration that did not reach statistical significance, IL-1β was significantly moderated by the ABSI cut point (test for interaction p = 0.001, Table 3 and Fig. 2). Participants above the ABSI cut point (z ≥ +0.229) had a significant 22.8% reduction in mean IL-1β concentration (p < 0.001) after a month of HEPA filtration compared to a month of sham filtration. The mean IL-1β concentration was virtually unchanged (p = 0.75) among participants below the ABSI cut point (z < +0.229). A similar moderating effect was observed for IL-6 but did not reach statistical significance (test for interaction p = 0.12) because of a smaller sample size. A much less pronounced effect (test for interaction p = 0.57), was observed for hsCRP. Discussion

In this pragmatic randomized crossover trial of in-home HEPA filtration, with strict quality control of blood samples, we observed no overall benefit of HEPA filtration on pro-inflammatory cytokines. However, ABSI, a recently developed indirect measure of visceral adiposity, significantly moderated the results. A significant mean reduction in IL-1β concentration was observed among participants above the ABSI cut point for excessive VAT. Similar mean reductions for IL-6 and CRP were not statistically significant. While other adiposity measures showed similar mean reductions for IL-1β, only WHpR reached statistical significance, suggesting that ABSI was a better measure of excessive VAT.

VAT is increasingly considered to be an important contributor to cardiovascular disease risk and systemic inflammation [37]. The ABSI was specifically developed to be a measure of visceral adiposity in the absence of direct measures such as CT, MRI, or DXA. Importantly, ABSI is not correlated with BMI, which is a measure that fails to differentiate adipose tissue from muscle mass. Because excessive VAT triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines whereas lean muscle mass releases anti-inflammatory myokines [38], it’s possible that the inclusion of muscle mass in BMI dilutes the association between VAT and pro-inflammatory cytokines. Had we relied solely on BMI, which is the most commonly used measure of adiposity in air pollution research, we would not have uncovered the moderating benefit on IL-1β. This finding suggests that the benefits of air purification may be specifically related to visceral adiposity rather than overall adiposity.

IL-1β is a primary product of the NLRP3 inflammasome which has been hypothesized to be the inflammasome primed and activated by exposure to airborne PM [39]. While IL-1β has received less attention in air pollution research than IL-6 and CRP, IL-1β has the advantage of greater mechanistic specificity because it is a proximal product of NLRP3 inflammasome activation. In contrast, IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine and CRP is an acute phase reactant [40].

We identified only two randomized crossover trials of air purifiers that reported IL-1β as an outcome, both of which were conducted in China. The first focused on cardiovascular factors in healthy young adults under tightly controlled conditions and reported a large benefit for IL-1β [12]. However, IL-1β was measured using a multiplex assay that usually has frequent values below the LOD, and the number of values that were below the LOD were not reported. The second trial focused on respiratory health in children [13]. IL-1β was measured in exhaled breath condensate, rather than blood, with a small benefit reported. Like the first trial, a multiplex assay was used without documenting how values below the LOD were addressed. It is worth noting that the ultrasensitive assay we used has only recently become available. Despite meaningful limitations, these two trials are broadly consistent with our findings, suggesting that IL-1β is an important pro-inflammatory cytokine that deserves more attention in air pollution research.

Our study has several strengths. Because it was conducted under real-world conditions, without artificial constraints, the findings are more likely to reflect actual benefits to health in general populations. While residual confounding from time-varying factors cannot be ruled out, this problem is greatly reduced by the randomized crossover trial design, short timeframe, and adjustment for two time-varying potential confounders, the number of hours per day spent indoors at home and perceived stress. We also applied strict exclusion criteria for blood samples and lab results, substantially reducing the risk that errant or outlying values unduly influencing the findings.

Despite the substantial strengths, there remain some limitations. Most notably, moderation analysis was conceived after the trial was completed and therefore can only be considered as hypothesis generating. Because we did not anticipate in advance the role of the ABSI in moderating the benefits on IL-1β, we did not recruit participants based on ABSI score above the cut point for excessive VAT. This resulted in a small sample of 12 participants with an ABSI score above the cut point. Nevertheless, and perhaps remarkably, the reduction in IL-1β among these participants was statistically significant, suggesting that this signal of effect is quite strong. However, because only a small percentage of homes had indoor air monitoring, we were unable to clearly assess the association between the degree of concentration reduction and the degree of pollutant reduction.

Our findings highlight the importance of identifying subpopulations that may be particularly susceptible to the health effects of air pollution and most likely to benefit from the use of in-home air purifiers. The two other outcomes published from our trial, blood pressure and cognitive function, also uncovered benefits in subpopulations [15,41]. Taken together, these findings raise the possibility that exposure-reduction interventions may provide the greatest benefit among individuals with elevated underlying risk, even in populations that are generally healthy.

Conclusion

Air pollution research generally, and trials of HEPA air purifiers in particular, might benefit from greater emphasis on the possible role of the NLRP3 inflammasome and its primary product, IL-1β. Additionally, there is a need to move beyond BMI as the main or sole measure of adiposity in air pollution research. We recommend, based on our findings, adding the ABSI to future air pollution studies. Notably, ABSI can be calculated from readily available measures, namely height, weight, and WC.
