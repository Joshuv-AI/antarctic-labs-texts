Latent profile analysis of rotation stress and associated factors among nurses undergoing standardised training in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yue Peng, Weiping Yan, Li Yuan, Chaoqun Xie, Huayu Feng, Hu Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to identify potential categories of rotation stress among nurses undergoing standardised training and to explore the relevant factors associated with each profile.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingData were collected in November 2024 from three hospitals in Zunyi City, Guizhou Province, China.ParticipantsNurses undergoing standardised training were recruited for this study.MethodsConvenience sampling method was used to recruit standardised training nurses in November 2024 from three hospitals in Zunyi City, Guizhou Province. The survey instruments used included demographic characteristics questionnaire, the Nursing Job Rotation Stress Scale and the Maslach Burnout Inventory. Latent profile analysis method was used to analyse rotation stress characteristics of nurses during standardised training. Additionally, logistic regression was performed to identify the factors influencing different characteristics.ResultsA total of 493 nurses completed the questionnaires, of which 453 were valid, resulting in a validity rate of 91.88%. Rotation stress was classified into two profiles: ‘Low Emotional Response–Stress Adaptation Group’ (21.5%) and ‘High Emotional Response–Stress Distress Group’ (78.5%). Univariate analysis showed that highest degree (χ2=11.389, p=0.001), monthly night shifts (χ2=33.913, p<0.001), employment type (χ2=20.858, p<0.001), new graduate (χ2=12.319, p<0.001), pretraining work experience (χ2=35.754, p<0.001) and training duration (χ2=15.357, p=0.002) significantly influenced the two subgroups. Multivariable regression analysis revealed significant associations of monthly night shifts, pretraining work experience, training duration and burnout level (p<0.05).ConclusionsNurses undergoing standardised training exhibit two distinct rotation stress profiles. Monthly night shifts, pretraining work experience, training duration and burnout are significant factors. Nursing managers should implement targeted interventions such as mindfulness, laughter therapy and emotional freedom techniques to mitigate stress and thereby enhance the quality of standardised training.

Introduction

The National Nursing Career Development Plan (2016–2020), introduced by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China in 2016, clearly emphasised the importance of standardised training to enhance nursing specialisation.1 Standardised training is a continuing education that nurses receive after graduation. It is a crucial pathway for developing the nursing talent pipeline and enabling them to grow rapidly into qualified nurses.2 3 However, the high intensity of rotations, frequent changes in the environment and the constant need to update knowledge and skills impose significant stress on newly hired nursing professionals.4

Rotation stress was defined as perceived stress experienced by nurses in response to a manager-ordered transition to another job or department.5 Some studies6 7 reported that nurses undergoing standardised training experience high levels of rotation stress. Liu et al3 reported that the average score of rotation stress among nurses undergoing standardised training rotations was 27.09. Additionally, excessive stress impairs nurses’ physical and mental health, reduces work efficiency and increases the risk of turnover by lowering job satisfaction.810 Globally, the turnover rate of new nurses within their first year of employment reaches as high as 71.8%.11 In Turkey, 42.5% of newly graduated nurses consider leaving the profession within their first year.12 A longitudinal study in Korea found that the turnover rate for new nurses increased by over 10% in the second year compared with the first.13 National studies indicate that the turnover rate of newly graduated nurses is two to three times higher than in other Asian countries.14 One study showed a positive correlation between job stress and burnout in nurses.15 A study in Singapore found a burnout rate of 49%–66% among nurses,16 which can have a negative impact on nurses undergoing standardised training and hospitals, and even affect the future growth and development of nurses.17 Although the abundance of research on nurse stress, there is limited literature specifically addressing the rotation stress experienced during standardised training.

Despite growing attention to nurse stress, existing research suffers from notable gaps. First, there is inconsistency regarding factors influencing stress: some studies suggested sociodemographic factors, such as gender, age, night shifts, salary level, work environment, educational background and work experience, are associated with stress.21820 While others found no correlation between age, educational level and stress.21 22 Second, there is no research to explore the heterogeneous subgroups of rotation stress among nurses undergoing standardised training or clarify the relationship between these subgroups and burnout, leaving a gap in the identification of high-risk populations.

Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a widely used statistical method that classifies individuals based on latent traits as measured by specific items.23 Its core advantage lies in adhering to an ‘individual-centred’ principle, which fully captures individual differences, making it well-suited to address the gap in existing research that overlooks group differences. Therefore, this study aimed to assess rotation stress among nurses undergoing standardised training, thereby identifying distinct subgroups and analysing the key determinants of these profiles.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study recruited nurses undergoing standardised training from three hospitals in Zunyi City via convenience sampling in December 2024. The study design and reporting followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.24

The inclusion criteria of participants in our study were as follows: (1) registered nurses; (2) currently undergoing standardised training; (3) provided informed consent and volunteered to participate. Exclusion criteria: history of mental illness or on vacation during the survey period.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the empirical method (5–10 times the number of questionnaire items). The questionnaire included 44 items (12 for basic information, 32 for scales). Applying the 10-fold criterion times yielded an initial sample of 440 and accounting for a 10% invalid sample rate, the required sample size was 440/(1%–10%)=489.

Measurements

Demographic characteristic questionnaire

The demographic characteristic questionnaire included age, gender, highest degree, marital status, professional title, monthly night shifts, employment of type, department, monthly income, working experience and training duration.

Nursing Job Rotation Stress Scale

The scale was developed by Huang et al,25 it assesses nurses’ rotation-related stress. The scale includes 10 items across three dimensions: emotional reactions (items 1–4), daily life (items 5–7), and communication (items 8–10). Responses are scored on a five‐point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree, 5=strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 10 to 50 (higher scores indicate greater stress). The original scale has a Cronbach’s α of 0.870. In this study, the version adapted for nurses undergoing standardised training had a Cronbach’s α of 0.857.3

Maslach Burnout Inventory

The scale was developed by Maslach et al26 and translated into Chinese by Luo et al.27 It includes 22 items across three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (9 items), depersonalisation (5 items) and personal accomplishment (8 items). Responses are scored on a seven-point Likert scale (0=never, 1=several times a year, 2=once a month, 3=several times a month, 4=once a week, 5=several times a week and 6=every day), with higher scores indicating greater burnout. The Cronbach’s α of this scale was 0.823 in this study.

Data collection

Data were collected via an online questionnaire distributed through Wenjuanxing. After obtaining consent from the three participating hospitals, the purpose, significance and completion procedures of the survey were explained to nurses and hospital administrators.

To ensure questionnaire validity: all options were mandatory, each IP address could submit only one response (after completing all sections); questionnaires with a response time <300 s or regular reply patterns were excluded.

Statistical methods

Mplus V.8.0 was used to analyse potential rotation stress profiles, starting with a two-class model and increasing the number of classes until the optimal fit was achieved. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size-adjusted BIC (aBIC), Entropy, Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMR) and Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLTR).

In accordance with the recommendations put forth by Nylund et al,28 2–5 class models were compared. The optimal model was defined by lower AIC, BIC and aBIC values; Entropy >0.8; and significant LMR and BLTR values (p<0.05). These indicators suggest that the model possesses high explanatory power and good overall fit.

SPSS V.22.0 was used for descriptive statistics: normally distributed data were expressed as mean±SD, categorical data were expressed as counts and percentages, with intergroup comparisons using the χ² test. Logistic regression is used for the multivariate analysis of latent profile categories (α=0.05).

Patient and public involvement

Neither patients nor the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans associated with this research.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 493 questionnaires were distributed, with 453 valid responses (effective response rate is 91.88%). Among the 40 invalid questionnaires, 12 had a response time <300 s, 10 had irregular data completion and 18 had missing data. Of the 453 nurses surveyed, 220 (48.57%) were under the age of 25 years, while 233 (51.43%) were aged 25 or older. The sample included 78 males (17.22%) and 375 females (82.78%). In terms of education, 134 participants (29.58%) held a junior college degree or lower, while 319 individuals (70.42%) had an undergraduate degree or higher. A total of 253 nurses held the professional title of nurse (55.85%), and more than half were unmarried. Additionally, approximately 400 nurses (88.30%) were employed on a contract basis.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the heterogeneity of rotation stress among nurses undergoing standardised training using LPA. The results identified two distinct subgroups: the Low Emotional Response–Stress Adaptation Group and the High Emotional Response–Stress Distress Group. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study using LPA to explore the heterogeneous characteristics of rotation stress among nurses undergoing standardised training. The findings suggest that main factors, including monthly night shift, pretraining work experience, training duration and burnout levels, are significant predictors of rotation stress among nurses undergoing standardised training.

In this study, our findings revealed that 78.5% of nurses experienced high rotation stress, whereas 21.5% reported low levels, aligning with those reported by Unaldi Baydin et al,9 while exceeding the prevalence rates documented in previous studies.29 30 This discrepancy may stem from variations in research instruments and study settings and reflect the inherent heterogeneity among nurses. From a broader standpoint, it is clear that nurses undergoing standardised training generally face heightened rotation stress during their training. Given the high rotation stress reported by most participants, our findings underscore the urgency for nursing managers to implement targeted interventions for nurses in standardised training.

Our results showed that nurses with ≥5 monthly night shifts were significantly more likely to be in the High Emotional Response–Stress Distress Group. Frequent night shifts may impair sleep quality and endocrine function, leading to increased stress.31 Previous studies32 33 have shown that shift work is positively correlated with poor mental health. Meanwhile, work-related stress and night shift are recognised as primary drivers of deterioration in individuals’ quality of life. We recommend that nursing management implement flexible scheduling strategies to promote nurses’ mental health. Additionally, a tailored flexible scheduling system could be developed based on the specific characteristics of each department. Our results showed that nurses with fewer than 3 years of work experience reported higher stress levels. This finding presents a divergence from existing literature.34 Future studies should therefore examine the consistency of rotational stress in this nurse population across diverse geographic and professional backgrounds.

The results of our study showed that the training duration of nurses was associated with the highest stress levels during the initial months. Kim and Yeo35 conducted a longitudinal analysis of occupational stress among new nurses in their first year of employment, finding that stress peaked 4 months after entering the profession and began to decline by 8 months. Specifically, new nurses experienced the highest stress levels at four and 8 months postemployment, with the lowest levels observed at 12 months.36 This indicates that nurses’ stress levels fluctuate dynamically over time and are heterogeneous across individuals. Therefore, stress reduction interventions for nurses should be tailored to these temporal trends. While previous studies have identified factors like gender22 and department affiliation37 as correlates of nursing-related stress, our study did not support these findings. Future research should further explore other factors affecting rotation-related stress among nurses undergoing standardised training to inform targeted intervention strategies.

Burnout is recognised as a response to job-related stressors, and nurses are particularly vulnerable given the high-stress nature of their profession.38 Our study further confirmed the correlation between burnout and rotation-induced stress. Such an imbalance between increasing nursing responsibilities and coping with stressful working environments can commonly lead to burnout. 39This underscores the importance of nursing managers implementing early identification and effective interventions to mitigate nurse burnout, enhance teamwork and job satisfaction, reduce overall stress levels and ultimately improve patient care safety.40

This study showed that nurses undergoing standardised training generally experience a certain level of rotation stress. Our findings have several implications for future clinical practice and research. It is worth emphasising that stress relaxation and coping skills should be incorporated as one of the courses in standardised training. Nursing administrators should pay attention to this issue and implement targeted mental health support initiatives, such as laughter therapy,41 mindfulness42 and emotional freedom techniques.43

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, a cross-sectional design was used, which cannot establish causal relationships between variables. Future longitudinal studies are needed to explore how rotation stress and its influencing factors change over time. Second, the participants were recruited from three hospitals in Zunyi City, Guizhou Province, China, resulting in sampling bias. Nevertheless, the findings are representative of similar hospital settings in some cities in southwest China. At the same time, caution is needed when extrapolating the findings to other provinces or healthcare systems outside China. We recommend future multi-centre, large sample studies to further broaden external validity.

Conclusions

In this study, nurses undergoing standardised training in China can be classified into two distinct rotation stress subgroups. Monthly night shift frequency, pretraining work experience, training duration and burnout level are key factors influencing these subgroups. Nursing managers should conduct regular stress assessments to identify high-risk nurses, implement targeted interventions based on individual characteristics and monitor stress levels over time to adjust interventions. These measures will help reduce rotation stress and improve the quality of standardised training.
