A comprehensive dataset of the historic built environment risk estimates under different climate scenarios for two counties in southern Norway
Authors: Paloma Guzman, Ionut Cristi Nicu, Ole Fredrik Unhammer, Nils Aage Hafsal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis data article presents a geospatial dataset of multi-hazard exposure and climate-based decay indices for historic buildings in two counties in southern Norway (Agder and Innlandet). The dataset is provided as ESRI shapefiles (.shp), with one record per building point and associated identifiers (cadastre building number and municipality code) and flags indicating whether the building is registered in Askeladden and/or the SEFRAK registry. For each building, the dataset includes five binary hazard indicators (flood, rockfall, landslide, snow avalanche, overgrowing; 1 = within the mapped hazard extent, 0 = outside) derived from spatial overlays with national hazard layers, and a summed multi-hazard count (0–5). Climate-based decay pressure is provided as a continuous index and as reclassified decay classes (low/medium/high) for three climate periods: a baseline (1971–2000) and two projection periods (2031–2060; 2071–2100). A dimensionless combined screening indicator is included by combining the multi-hazard count with decay class for each period. The dataset supports GIS-based screening, comparative analysis across municipalities, and prioritization scoping for monitoring and maintenance planning for historic buildings, noting that hazard exposure is encoded as binary presence/absence and does not represent hazard intensity or probability.Keywords: Multi-hazard, Vulnerability, Decay, Material degradation, GIS

Background

The historic built environment in Norway is increasingly exposed to a combination of climate-driven decay processes and geohazards, including more intense precipitation, altered freeze–thaw regimes, rising temperatures and humidity, and associated increases in flood, landslide and biodeterioration risk [10]. Climate-based decay indices for timber and porous mineral materials indicate that southern Norway is projected to experience high levels of biological and chemical decay hazards under future climate scenarios, alongside changing patterns of frost-related damage [10,11]. National and regional studies on cultural heritage under climate change have therefore highlighted the need for building-scale, spatially explicit information that links individual historic buildings with multiple geohazards [12] and climate-based decay indicators, to support monitoring and adaptation planning for vulnerable heritage [13].

The objective of this dataset is to provide a spatially explicit representation of multi-hazard exposure and climate-based decay pressure for historic buildings in two counties in southern Norway (Agder and Innlandet), based on a historical baseline (1971–2000) and two projection periods (2031–2060; 2071–2100) under RCP4.5 and RCP8.5. The dataset supports comparative screening across time by enabling users to examine hazard overlaps and decay indices for individual buildings and across municipalities. Linking hazard overlays and decay indices to cadastre building identifiers and municipality codes makes the dataset readily joinable to Norway’s planning and permitting workflows and to county-level heritage management. Users can screen exposure patterns across territories, compare baseline and projected conditions, and scope prioritisation and coordination needs at the heritage–planning–adaptation interface. The dataset also supports follow-up research and potential extension to wider geographic coverage [14,15].

For each historic building, hazard indicators are binary (1 = presence, 0 = absence) and are derived by spatially intersecting building locations with national hazard maps. The haz_sum field is defined as the sum of the five binary hazard fields (multi-hazard), and it is used to indicate the number of simultaneous hazards for each building (from 0 to 5).

The decay fields (historic, r2031, r2071) contain a relative decay index calculated from temperature and precipitation data for each climate period, following the principles of the Scheffer climate index for timber decay and the frost decay exposure index for porous mineral materials [16]. Higher values indicate a higher climatic potential for material decay [14,17].

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Study area, hazard and heritage data

The study area comprises two counties in southern Norway (Agder and Innlandet), which are characterized by a high density of registered historic buildings and various geohazards. Historic building locations were obtained from the National Cadaster Buildings registry. There is additional info whether they are listed in Askeladden or SEFRAK. The buildings were exported as point features in a common projected coordinate system (ETRS 1989 UTM Zone 33 N). All the data processing was carried out in ArcGIS Pro v10.6.1, but all items have been produced in a format that can be imported into any GIS environment (.shp format).

For each hazard (flood, rock fall, landslides, snow avalanche, and overgrowing), we intersected building points with the corresponding hazard layer, as follows: if a building point fell within a delineated hazard zone, the respective hazard field was assigned value 1, otherwise 0; the multi-hazard field (haz_sum) is the sum of the five binary fields, having a value from 0 (no hazard) to 5 (exposed to all hazards) (Fig. 1a for Agder, Fig. 1b for Innlandet). This approach follows previous multi-hazard assessments for cultural heritage [[18], [19], [20], [21]].

Climate data and climate-based decay index

Climate data for the baseline period (1971–2000) were taken from long-term observational or reanalysis datasets for Norway, while future climate projections (2031–2060, 2071–2100) were obtained from regional climate model ensembles under selected emission scenarios. For each period and grid cell, the climate-based decay vulnerability index was computed that expresses the relative potential for material decay based on temperature and precipitation [17]. These concepts were adapted to derive a normalized decay index for each grid cell and period, using variables such as mean monthly temperatures, number of days above/below threshold temperatures, and cumulative precipitation. The resulting decay index was spatially joined to the historic buildings by nearest neighbor [15]. The above procedure was repeated for: baseline climate (1971–2000), mid-century climate (2031–2060), and late-century climate (2071–2100). The relative risk score is the sum of the multi-hazard indicator. This score is dimensionless and is not intended as an absolute risk, but as a relative indicator that allows users to rank historic buildings by combining multi-hazard exposure and climatic decay potential across various climate scenarios. Limitations

The dataset relies on nationally available hazard layers that vary in spatial resolution, modeling techniques, and update frequency. Hazard exposure is expressed as a binary intersection (0/1) between building point locations and mapped hazard extents; it does not represent hazard intensity, probability, depth, or return period. Climate-based decay indices are derived from downscaled climate projections under RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 and represent scenario-based averages for defined time periods; they do not account for local microclimate variation, building-specific material properties, maintenance condition, or adaptive interventions.

Buildings are represented as point geometries (cadaster centroids), which may introduce positional uncertainty when overlaid with hazard polygons, particularly for large or irregular footprints. Registry coverage depends on the completeness and accuracy of the National Cadaster, Askeladden, and SEFRAK databases at the time of extraction; undesignated or recently altered heritage assets may not be fully represented. The building stock is treated as temporally static across climate periods, and potential demolition, relocation, or adaptation measures are not modeled. Finally, the summed multi-hazard count assigns equal weight to all hazard types and does not model compound or cascading hazard interactions.
