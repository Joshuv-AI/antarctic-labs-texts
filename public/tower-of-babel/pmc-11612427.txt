Gridded, temporally referenced spatial information on soil organic carbon for Hungary
Authors: Gábor Szatmári, Annamária Laborczi, János Mészáros, Katalin Takács, András Benő, Sándor Koós, Zsófia Bakacsi, László Pásztor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil organic carbon (SOC), known as the most important soil attribute, affects various soil functions and services, essential for nutritious food and clean drinking water. Since recognizing its key role in many environmental challenges, there has been an increasing demand for spatial information on SOC. Our objective is to present the results of a mapping activity aimed at producing spatially exhaustive information on SOC content, density, and stock for the topsoils of Hungary for 1992 and 2000. A “time-for-space” digital soil mapping approach was pursued to predict and map these SOC properties, with the associated uncertainty, at a resolution of 100 × 100 m. Particular attention was paid to validating the accuracy of the maps and the reliability of the uncertainty quantifications. The published maps are recommended to be used as baseline maps for Hungary. The spatial resolution makes them suitable for various practical applications (e.g., GHG inventory, sustainable agriculture, carbon sequestration). The maps are of interest to researchers, practitioners, and policymakers, helping to achieve scientifically sound results and informed decision-making.Subject terms: Environmental monitoring, Environmental impact, Agriculture, Geography

Background & Summary

Soil is the largest terrestrial pool of organic carbon and thus is an active and unavoidable participant in the global carbon cycle1,2. Soil organic carbon (SOC) is known as the most important attribute of soils, influencing various soil related functions and services, such as agricultural productivity3, water retention and management4–6, buffering capacity against toxic elements and compounds7,8, which are essential for providing nutritious food and clean drinking water. Since the recognition of soils’ multifunctionality and the pivotal role of SOC in many environmental challenges and crises (e.g., climate change, land degradation, desertification)9–13, researchers from various fields (e.g., climatology, Earth system sciences, hydrology, conservation biology, and forestry), practitioners, stakeholders, and even policymakers have shown an increasing demand for spatial, and more often, spatiotemporal information on SOC.

The key SOC properties commonly required by end-users are SOC content (SOCc), SOC density (SOCd), and SOC stock (SOCs). SOCc expresses the mass of organic carbon stored per unit mass of soil material. SOCd and SOCs express the mass of organic carbon stored per unit volume of soil and per unit area, respectively. In fact, SOCd and SOCs are closely related; however, SOCs is a summary measure that always refers to a specific soil layer, while SOCd is a relative measure that can be associated with any soil layer. In recent years, numerous mapping activities have been conducted worldwide at various scales, ranging from global14,15 and continental16–18 to national19–22 and regional23–25 levels. These efforts highlight the need for and importance of comprehensive and accurate mapping of SOC properties.

It should be noted that soil varies not only in space but also in time26, and SOC is no exception. Hence, end-users often want to know which year, years, or time period the SOC maps refer to. For end-users, this information is indispensable for the suitable application of these maps, such as in greenhouse gas (GHG) inventory, soil health monitoring, or carbon farming. However, in our experience, it is rarely communicated in published SOC maps which year or period of time they represent the spatial distribution of SOC. Therefore, using these maps as a baseline for such purposes can cause difficulties and requires caution, if not clearly defined.

Digital Soil Mapping (DSM), a successful sub-discipline of soil science with practical research outputs27, aims to meet the growing demands for spatial soil information by producing predictive soil maps. Nowadays, advanced mathematical and statistical techniques, including geostatistics and machine learning, are used to find predictive relationship between soil observation data and environmental covariates, which can then be used to infer the spatial, and increasingly, the spatiotemporal variability of soils. Environmental covariates are proxies for soil forming factors (e.g., topography, climate, parent material, and biosphere) and widely used in DSM to obtain not just accurate but thematically detailed soil maps28. A major achievement in DSM is the standard practice of quantifying, assessing, and communicating the prediction uncertainty associated with the maps, which is essential for their appropriate use29. This is crucial, as no map is error-free, meaning that the predicted and the actual (or true) values at a geographic location may differ slightly; this difference is known as error. In fact, this error is not known spatially exhaustively29. However, it is necessary to provide information about the reliability of the map. In DSM, probabilistic approaches are commonly adopted to model and quantify the uncertainty associated with the resulting maps30,31.

Our objective is to present the results of a SOC mapping activity aimed at producing spatially exhaustive information on SOCc, SOCd, and SOCs for the topsoils (0–30 cm) of Hungary for the years 1992 and 2000. In the methodology developed and applied, a “time-for-space” approach was pursued, which uses soil data not only from the years of interest (i.e., 1992 and 2000) but also from other years14. By using all available soil data, it is expected to obtain more accurate predictions and reduce the uncertainty associated with these predictions, thereby improving the applicability of the maps. The resulting SOCc, SOCd, and SOCs maps with their prediction uncertainty are freely available for both years with a spatial resolution of 100 × 100 m32. This spatial resolution makes the published maps suitable for various practical applications at the regional and country levels, including land use planning, rural development, sustainable agriculture, soil health monitoring, GHG inventories, carbon sequestration, and more. Furthermore, the produced maps can be of significant interest for European or even global initiatives and programs, such as the European Union Common Agricultural Policy or meeting the Sustainable Development Goals. Since the years, to which the resulting SOC maps refer, are explicitly known, they can serve as baseline maps for Hungary. The published SOC property maps are expected to be of interest to researchers, practitioners, stakeholders, and policymakers, helping to reach scientifically sound results and conclusions, and to support informed decision-making and policy formulation.

Methods

Data on soil organic carbon

Soil observation data were derived from the Hungarian Soil Information and Monitoring System (SIMS) (Fig. 2), which collects geographically referenced physical, chemical, and biological information on the temporal change of the soils in Hungary. SIMS was established in 1992, often referred to as “base year”, with 1236 monitoring points. Three types of monitoring sites are distinguished: (1) I-points (number of monitoring points: 865) placed on agricultural areas (arable lands, pastures, orchards, vineyards, etc.), (2) E-points (number of monitoring points: 183) located in forests and woodlands, and (3) S-points (number of monitoring points: 188) located in areas with various types of environmental hazards (mining sites, landfills, industrial areas, etc.). Note that S-points were excluded as they are significantly influenced by anthropogenic activity. The monitoring sites are benchmark locations and are revisited every year to collect samples from each soil horizon. According to the operation protocol of SIMS, SOCc is measured every three years by wet oxidation of organic carbon using potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) catalysed by sulfuric acid (H2SO4). We used SOCc measurements from the following years: 1992, 1995, 1998, 2000, 2004, 2007, 2010, 2013, and 2016. Originally, the unit of SOCc measurements is weight percent [%]. To express SOCc data in SI unit, the original unit was converted to [g ∙ kg−1], which can be readily done by multiplying SOCc values by 10.

Map presenting the location of the monitoring points of the Hungarian Soil Information and Monitoring System (SIMS) over a digital elevation model of the country (Source: Szatmári et al.13). Note that soil organic carbon properties were harmonized for the topsoil (0–30 cm) at each monitoring point.

In addition to SOCc data, quantitative information on bulk density (BD) and the proportion of coarse fragments (CF) is needed to derive SOCd and SOCs. It should be noted that physical soil properties were only measured in the base year, thus we had to use BD and CF measurements from the base year for all years with SOCc measurements. Soil surveyors visually estimated CF in the field for each soil horizon at each monitoring point, and its unit is volumetric percent [vol. %]. BD was measured by collecting undisturbed soil samples from each soil horizon at each monitoring point using a sharp-edged metal cylinder (inner volume: 100 cm3). The undisturbed samples were oven-dried until achieving constant weight, and then BD was computed by dividing the dry weight of the soil material by the inner volume of the metal cylinder. It should be noted that SIMS is public and of public interest, and therefore, SIMS data can be requested from the Directorate for Plant, Soil and Agri-environmental Protection, National Food Chain Safety Office (Nébih), Hungary.

Environmental covariates

Environmental covariates are summarized in Table 1. Since the objective was to map SOC properties for 1992 and 2000 using all the available soil data derived from SIMS, it was necessary to distinguish the environmental covariates, whether they can be considered static or dynamic over time, and then collect them accordingly. Topography and parent material were assumed to be static over time. Topography was described by a digital elevation model of Hungary35 and many of its derivatives, such as slope, topographic wetness index, multi-resolution ridge top flatness, etc. (Table 1). Parent material was characterized by a digitized version of the geological map of Hungary36 (https://map.mbfsz.gov.hu/). In our previous research37,38, it was found that soil type is also informative in mapping SOC properties; thus, the soil type map of Hungary39 (https://dosoremi.hu/) was also used as a static environmental covariate. Environmental covariates related to climate and biosphere were considered to be dynamic in time, and therefore these covariates were collected in each year when SOC measurements were available (i.e., 1992, 1995, 1998, 2000, 2004, 2007, 2010, 2013, and 2016) to capture their spatiotemporal variability. Climate was characterized by national and international databases, including the meteorological data repository (https://odp.met.hu/) of the Hungarian Meteorological Service and E-OBS daily gridded meteorological data for Europe40 (https://surfobs.climate.copernicus.eu/) (Table 1). Climatic covariates were derived for each season (spring: March to May; summer: June to August; autumn: September to November; winter: December to February) in each year. The biosphere was characterized by the land cover map of each year41, and cloud-masked, median-filtered annual satellite image mosaics, acquired by Landsat-4, ‐5, ‐7, ‐8, and ‐9 (https://landsat.gsfc.nasa.gov/) from June to October in each year, and the spectral indices derived from these (Table 1). Formulas used to calculate spectral indices can be found in Supplementary Table 1. Note that these spectral indices are often used in DSM42–46.

Since a large number of environmental covariates from various data sources, with a wide range of spatial resolutions (from 25 × 25 m to 10 × 10 km), were used, it was necessary to resample them into a common geographic reference system with a spatial resolution of 100 × 100 m. For covariates related to soil type and parent material, a nearest neighbour resampling technique was applied. For climatic data layers, a cubic technique was employed. For the digital elevation model, spatial aggregation was performed for a spatial resolution of 100 × 100 m, and then geomorphometric parameters were derived. Note that this common geographic reference system was also used for mapping SOC properties.

Note that almost all the environmental covariates or data sources from which they were derived are freely available to the public. The only exceptions are the soil type map and the geological map; however, to the best of our knowledge, the data owners make them available upon request.

Digital soil mapping

As mentioned, a “time-for-space” approach was pursued to map SOC properties for both years, based on the spatially and temporally referenced SOC property data, and the spatially exhaustive static and dynamic environmental covariates. This means that SOCc, SOCd and SOCs data from all sampling years were used in DSM, not only those from the years of interest (i.e., 1992 and 2000). Subsequently, the methodological parts of DSM are presented.

Regression matrix

First, a regression matrix needs to be prepared, which is a table containing SOC property data in the rows and environmental covariate values in the columns. To obtain the matrix, it is necessary to overlay the spatially exhaustive static and dynamic environmental covariates with the spatially and temporally referenced SOC property data, and extract their values at the given spatiotemporal location where the SOC property data are located. Note that if a given environmental covariate was considered static over time, its value in the regression matrix remained constant regardless of the sampling year. To preserve information on the time and geographical location, the monitoring sites’ ID, the year of sampling and the geographical location (i.e., longitude and latitude) were also retained and attached to the matrix.

Quantile regression forest

A machine learning algorithm, namely the quantile regression forest47 (QRF), was used (1) to establish predictive relationship between the environmental covariates and the SOCc, SOCd and SOCs data, based on the prepared regression matrix, and (2) to obtain predictions and map the SOC properties for 1992 and 2000 with the associated prediction uncertainty. QRF is increasingly used for mapping the spatiotemporal variability of SOC properties37,48,49, and in brief, QRF can be considered as an extension of random forest50 (RF). For regression problems, RF generates multiple regression trees during training, each tree is trained on a random subset of the training data considering a random subset of environmental covariates at each split. The final prediction is the weighted average of the predictions. This ensemble approach helps improve generalization and robustness, making RF an outstanding machine learning algorithm. It should be added that RF keeps only the averages and neglects all other information from the regression trees. However, QRF retains not just the averages but also the observations for each node in each tree, making it possible to derive an empirical distribution function, which can be used to quantify the uncertainty associated with a prediction.

Hyperparameter tuning

The number of environmental covariates used at each split, often referred to as “mtry”, was fine-tuned for each SOC property (Fig. 3) to improve prediction performance. In practice, a sequence of possible “mtry” values were tested to find the value that gave the best prediction performance for a given SOC property. The root mean square error (RMSE) was the quality measure, quantifying the spread of the error distribution. The smaller the RMSE, the narrower the error distribution and hence the more accurate the prediction. It is important to highlight that 10-fold leave-location-out cross-validation51 was used to avoid over-fitting of the QRF models. The fine-tuned “mtry” values of 30, 10 and 5 were obtained for SOCc, SOCd and SOCs, respectively (Fig. 3, first row), and were used to fit the final QRF models.

Prediction and uncertainty quantification

The QRF models and the spatially exhaustive static and dynamic environmental covariates were used to predict and map SOCc, SOCd and SOCs across Hungary for the years 1992 and 2000 (Fig. 4). Note that for dynamic covariates, only those for the years 1992 and 2000 were used in mapping. The relative importance of the top 25 environmental covariates in predicting SOC properties is presented in Fig. 3 (second row). The covariates found to be the most informative are consistent with those reported in our previous studies21,37,38. In DSM, the uncertainty associated with a predictive soil map is commonly quantified by the lower and upper limit of the 90% prediction interval (PI)52, which can be interpreted as the range of values within which the true value is expected to occur in 9 times out of 10. This interval was obtained for each SOC property map using the 0.05 and 0.95 quantile of the distribution function derived from the QRF model. Uncertainty quantifications were also mapped and attached to each SOC property map. It should be added that all maps were masked with the category of open water bodies from the ecosystem map of Hungary53 (http://alapterkep.termeszetem.hu/), as these areas are not relevant for SOC properties.

The reliability of the uncertainty quantifications was also evaluated based on cross-validation. This step is crucial because unreliable uncertainty estimates can mislead decision-making. Commonly adopted validation techniques include the prediction interval coverage probability (PICP) plot and quantile coverage probability (QCP) plot31,54. A PICP plot graphically represents the expected and observed fractions of validation data falling within symmetric PIs. Intuitively, for a 90% PI, 90% of the validation data is expected to fall within the PI, while 10% should fall outside it. In general, this should hold true for any symmetric PI. When true and expected fractions for a series of symmetric PIs are computed and plotted, the resulting plot should ideally follow the y = x line. QCP relies on a similar theory but uses quantiles instead of symmetric PIs. This is because the PICP plot fails to detect one-sided bias, where the fraction outside the PI is not symmetric between the lower and upper tails. Therefore, deriving both plots is advisable for a reliable evaluation of uncertainty quantifications54. Figures 6, 7 show the derived PICP and QCP plots, respectively. Each plot aligns with the y = x line, indicating that the uncertainty quantifications are reliable estimates of the “real” uncertainty.

Usage Notes

It is important to emphasize that the uncertainty associated with the predictive maps of SOCc, SOCd and SOCs was not only quantified but also attached to each map, providing crucial information on the reliability of these maps. Therefore, all potential users are strongly advised to consider not only the SOC property maps but also their quantified uncertainty to achieve scientifically sound results and conclusions and to support informed decision-making or policy formulation. Before assuming that the published maps are flawed or unsuitable for use, it should be underscored that the quantification, assessment and communication of uncertainty have become standard practices in DSM, with numerous benefits, particularly for potential users29. Moreover, treating any map as if it were an error-free and perfect representation of reality is not only dangerous but also scientifically unsound.

As mentioned, the maps can serve as baselines, as they present the spatial distribution of SOC properties for the years 1992 and 2000, which are of particular interest. The year 1992 closely follows 1989, when communism ended in Hungary and triggered significant changes and transformations in the land use structure and land tenure system that continued until around 2000. Thus, the 1992 maps reflect the initial state of the topsoils immediately following the end of the communist era in Hungary. The year 2000, marking the beginning of a new millennium, is often used as a reference year for monitoring and assessing spatiotemporal changes in soils in national and international initiatives and projects. By this time, the major changes and transformations triggered by the end of the earlier regime had largely subsided, so the 2000 maps represent the state of the topsoils under the new land use and management structure.

As mentioned, the resulting maps were masked to exclude open water bodies, as these areas are not relevant for SOC properties per se. However, it should be noted that urban and industrial areas are similarly not relevant for soils, as they are permanently covered by artificial materials or objects (e.g., concrete, roads, railways, buildings, industrial complexes). Covering soil for housing, roads, or other construction work is an important soil degradation process known as soil sealing55. It is therefore recommended that these areas be masked out before using the published maps. However, the maps can also be used to quantify and assess the amount of organic carbon lost due to soil sealing.

At the country and regional levels, the spatial resolution of 100 × 100 m makes the published maps suitable for various practical applications, including rural development, land use planning, soil health monitoring, sustainable agriculture, evaluation of ecosystem condition, identification and delineation of areas for carbon sequestration, as well as GHG inventory. Additionally, this level of spatial resolution is sufficiently detailed to feed various process-based models, such as biogeochemical models, crop simulations, and organic carbon turnover models, at the country or regional level. It should be noted that the use of the published maps at local scales is limited due to their spatial resolution. However, they could still indirectly support or assist local land use planning, carbon farming or the establishment of local soil monitoring systems. At the international level, the produced maps are of significant interest for global and European initiatives and programs, such as the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification, the Sustainable Development Goals, the European Union Common Agricultural Policy, the European Green Deal, and the European Union Mission “A Soil Deal for Europe”, which aims to conserve SOC stock, among other objectives.

Custom code used for digital soil mapping and validation is available on GitHub (https://github.com/GaborSzatmari/HU-SOC-mapping).
