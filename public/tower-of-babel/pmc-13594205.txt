In vivo effect of anti-PD-1 on HIV-specific CD8+T-cells in men with HIV
Authors: Céline Gubser, Lakshmi Rajdev, Chris Y. Chiu, Shihan Li, Maëlick Brochut, Hannah King, Carolin Tumpach, Rachel D. Pascoe, Christine M. Durand, J. Judy Chang, Ajantha Solomon, Rosalyn Cao, Luciano Martelotto, Jeffery M. Bethony, Daniel T. Utzschneider, Axel Kallies, Miles P. Davenport, Jenny L. Anderson, Thomas A. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
One approach to achieve a cure for HIV infection or allow for people with HIV to safely stop antiretroviral therapy is to enhance HIV-specific immune function by reversing immune exhaustion. Immune checkpoint blockade with anti-PD1 can enhance virus-specific T-cell function ex vivo and in animal models but the effects of anti-PD1 on HIV-specific T-cells in vivo has not been explored. We examine samples from men with HIV and cancer on antiretroviral therapy who receive nivolumab (anti-PD1) every two weeks as part of a prospective clinical trial. We show that a single dose of anti-PD-1 increases HIV-specific CD8 + T cells in some but not all participants. Using single cell RNAseq of HIV-specific CD8 + T-cells, we show that anti-PD-1 significantly reshapes the T-cell landscape. Following anti-PD1, we see the emergence of a distinct 'progenitor' like central memory cluster characterized by an interferon-stimulated gene signature as well as an increase in expression of Granzyme-H in memory cells with expanded T-cell receptors, highlighting potential pathways to reinvigoration of cytotoxic function. We also demonstrate an inverse relationship between an increase in cell associated viral RNA following anti-PD-1 and the percentage of specific T-cell clusters prior to administration of anti-PD-1. These findings demonstrate the multiple effects of anti-PD-1 on both HIV-specific CD8 + T cells and HIV latency in PWH on ART.

Introduction

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has dramatically improved the life expectancy for people with HIV (PWH), but lifelong treatment is needed, and there is no cure1. The main barriers to a cure are long-lived and proliferating latently infected CD4 + T-cells containing intact integrated virus, referred to as the HIV reservoir2,3 and exhaustion of HIV-specific CD4+ and CD8 + T-cells, characterized by increased expression of immune checkpoint proteins, including programmed death (PD)−14–9. Whether HIV-specific T-cell exhaustion can be reinvigorated in vivo following anti-PD-1 in PWH on ART remains unknown.

Previous studies of T cells from people living with chronic viral infections indicated a persistent exhausted and dysfunctional phenotype despite the resolution of chronic viral replication, raising the possibility that T-cell exhaustion can never be fully reversed. For example, following elimination of hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection with direct-acting antivirals, abnormalities persisted in HCV-specific CD8 + T-cells whereby they retained a ‘molecular scar’, even with complete antigen clearance10,11. Irreversible ‘scarring’ of HIV-specific CD8 + T-cells has also been described in PWH who can naturally control HIV infection to low or undetectable levels in the absence of ART, called HIV controllers4,12. In a small subset of HIV controllers who eventually lose control of virus replication, there was a decrease in proliferative and cytolytic capacities of HIV-specific CD8 + T-cells prior to viral rebound12.

Analysis of the CD8 + T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoire in PWH over a period of 25 years of ART demonstrated that HIV-specific T cells can be rejuvenated, through ‘clonal succession’ whereby new dominant clonotypes exhibited stem-like profiles, while older clonotypes were associated with exhaustion and apoptosis13. However, while some viral replication may drive the priming or expansion of HIV-specific clonotypes with favorable functional properties14,15, this alone is likely insufficient to sustain long-term viral suppression, highlighting the need for additional therapeutic interventions, such as immune checkpoint blockade16,17.

Dysfunctional or exhausted CD8 + T-cells (TEX) are antigen-experienced and comprise a dynamic network of phenotypically and functionally distinct subpopulations18,19. CD8 + T cell exhaustion occurs through a stepwise process from naive T cells to exhausted precursors (Exh-Pre), progenitors (Exh-Prog) expressing high T-cell factor 1 (TCF1), exhausted intermediate (Exh-Int) cells expressing CX3CR1, and ultimately terminally exhausted T cells (Exh-Term)20. Studies in humans and mice with cancer or chronic lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) infection showed that Exh-Prog CD8 + T-cells can self-renew and proliferate in response to PD-1 blockade and give rise to TEX, which are critical for clearing cancer or infected target cells21–27. In clinical trials of anti-PD-1 for melanoma, proliferation of TEX-like CD8 + T-cells and a high ratio of TEX cells to tumor burden were associated with a favorable clinical outcome, but in these studies, it was not possible to separate cancer-specific from total CD8 + T-cells28,29. In separate studies of people with basal or squamous cell carcinoma, exhausted CD8 + T cells predominantly contained novel TCR clonotypes following anti-PD-1, consistent with a process of clonal replacement, whereby anti-PD-1 induced newly expanded TCR clonotypes, potentially with new antigen specificities, rather than reinvigoration of pre-existing clones30. There have been no studies to determine whether HIV-specific TCR clonotypes change as a result of anti-PD1.

The effects of immune checkpoint blockade in vivo in PWH on ART on immune function and the HIV reservoir, to date, have been limited. One small case series from our group showed that immune checkpoint blockade increased HIV-specific T-cells and/or TEX cells31 while other publications have not reported consistent changes in the HIV reservoir or T-cell responses following anti-PD-132,33. In a small prospective study of PWH on ART without cancer, a single low dose of antibody to the PD-1 ligand (PD-L1; BMS-936559) resulted in an increase in HIV-specific cytokine-producing T-cells in two of six participants34. Similar findings were also observed following cemiplimab, another anti-PD-1 antibody, in one of four PWH on ART35. A recent study in people with HIV and cancer treated with anti–PD-1 reported a rapid interferon-stimulated gene (ISG)-high CD8 + T cell signature within 24 h that correlated with HIV reservoir decay. Notably, the authors suggested this increase in ISG-high CD8 + T cell signature was driven indirectly by an innate myeloid response to PD-1 blockade, which triggered interferon production and latency reversal, rather than by direct PD-1 blockade on the CD8 + T cells alone36. Together, these data provide some limited evidence that PD-1 blockade can increase HIV-specific T-cells, but this only occurs in a subset of participants31. In addition, these findings are consistent with clinical experience with anti-PD-1 for malignancies, whereby only a subset of treated individuals will have a favorable response37.

The overall goal of this study was to determine the effects of anti-PD-1 in vivo on HIV-specific CD8 + T-cells in a phase one clinical trial of PWH on ART with cancer who received anti-PD-1 (nivolumab) every 2 weeks, until there was tumor progression or toxicity. Our findings provide insights into the mechanism of how anti-PD-1 can modulate both antigen-specific CD8 + T-cells and the HIV reservoir and have implications for the development of immunotherapeutic strategies for controlling HIV in the absence of ART.

Results

Single dose of anti-PD-1 increases HIV-specific CD8 + T cells in some participants

PBMCs from six participants with HIV on ART and with cancer (Table 1) were collected before and after at least one infusion of anti-PD-1, and timepoints were defined as baseline (B), single dose (S, comprising timepoints one or eight days after the first anti-PD-1 infusion), and multiple doses (M, including all timepoints collected after at least two anti-PD-1 infusions. One participant (P1) had received 22 infusions before the collection of the multiple timepoint sample (Fig. 1A). To isolate HIV-specific CD8 + T cells, we validated a panel of ten peptide:MHC-I tetramers recognizing dominant epitopes from HIV-1 Gag, Nef, Pol, and Vpr proteins in the context of highly prevalent HLA class I alleles (A02, A03, B07, and B08) (Fig. 1B, C and Supplementary Fig. 1A–E). We retrospectively identified HLA types by aligning gene expression (GEX) sequences to an HLA reference38 (Source Data file, tab 11). Notably, two participants (P4 and P6) carried the HLA-B51 allele, associated with long-term control of viral replication39. All participants expressed either the HLA-A02 or A03 allele, validating our tetramer selection. We observed an increase in tetramer+ CD8 + T-cells in four (P1, P4, P6, P8) of six participants following a single dose of anti-PD-1 (Fig. 1D). Transcriptomic characterization reveals diverse HIV-specific CD8+ cell types

We performed single-cell RNA/TCR sequencing of ex vivo, live sorted HIV tetramer+ CD8 + T cells from six PWH on ART and with cancer (Table 1) who received anti-PD-1 (P1, P2, P4, P6, P7, P8), and from five PWH on ART without cancer or anti-PD-1 treatment as controls (C, D, E, F, G) (Supplementary Fig. 2A). We did not expand cells in vitro but analysed tetramer+ cells directly ex vivo. Donors and timepoints were demultiplexed using a combination of hashing antibodies and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D). Inter-donor batch effects were corrected using Harmony40, preserving treatment-related biological signals while effectively removing donor variability (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 2E). After quality-control filtering, we acquired single-cell transcriptomes from a total of 3,574 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2B, D and Source data tab 12). A computational cellular trajectory reconstruction analysis (CytoTRACE)41 was used to predict the relative differentiation state of cells based on transcriptome size, with undifferentiated cells having larger transcriptomes (Fig. 2B). Unsupervised clustering identified ten main cellular clusters (Fig. 2C), which were manually annotated based on the difference in gene percentage expression between individual clusters versus other clusters (Source data file, tab 2). In addition, we used anchor genes described in the literature20,42–45 to help assign cell type identity (Fig. 2D, E and Supplementary Fig. 3A). C-C Chemokine Receptor Type 7 (CCR7), Selectin L (SELL, encoding CD62L), and Lymphoid Enhancer Binding Factor 1 (LEF1) were observed within the memory clusters 1, 5, and 9. While cluster 1 expressed high levels of T cell factor 1 (TCF1, encoded by TCF7), cluster 9 displayed an interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) signature as well as high levels of nuclear factor kappa B subunit 2 (NFKB2). The transcription factor myeloblastosis viral oncogene homolog (MYB), which was shown to govern the generation and function of mouse CD62L+ precursor exhausted T cells44 was expressed by cluster 1 and 9. Cluster 4 expressed high levels of interleukin-7 (IL-7) as well as granzyme K (GZMK), while cluster 3 was defined by expression of the mitochondrial protein CMC1 associated with T-cell activation and differentiation46 while still expressing TCF7, CCR7 and CD27. Clusters 0, 2, 6, 7, and 8 were classified as different subsets of T effector cells. Cluster 7 highly expressed killer cell lectin-like receptors (KLRs) as well as the transcription factor helios (encoded by IKZF2). Cluster 2 expressed B lymphocyte-induced maturation protein 1 (BLIMP1, encoded by PRDM1), granulysin (GNLY), CX3 chemokine receptor 1 (CX3CR1), a marker indicative of positive response to immune checkpoint blockade47,48, as well as cytotoxic molecules, perforin (PRF1) and granzyme B (GZMB). Cluster 8 expressed high levels of GZMK as well as genes involved in proliferation and cell activation (HLA-DRA, UBE2C, BIRC5). Cluster 6 also showed high levels of an ISG signature (STAT1, MX1, ISG15). Expression of exhaustion markers such as thymocyte selection-associated high mobility group box (TOX) and T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT) was observed in all effector cell clusters. In summary, we observed distinct transcriptional clustering of CD8 + T-cells in effector and memory subsets of tetramer-positive cells.

Distinct changes in HIV-specific effector and memory T-cell subsets and TCR clones following a single and multiple doses of anti-PD-1

We defined T cell receptor (TCR) clones via their complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) α and β nucleotide sequences (Supplementary Fig. 4A, Source data file, tab 4) and identified a total of 1828 unique TCR clonotypes, including 42 expanded clonotypes (633 cells) (Source data file, tab 4). To correct for cell numbers per sample, an expanded TCR (ETCR) clone was defined as a frequency of 2% of all TCR clonotypes, a higher frequency than the peak number of cells with a unique TCR in all donors (Supplementary Fig. 4B, C). For example, three cells with the identical TCR sequence in a sample of 100 cells would qualify as an ETCR, as it represents 3% of the total cells. This approach ensured that we were capturing only the most prominent and expanded TCR clones.

ETCR clones clustered primarily in the lower half of the UMAP (Fig. 3B), aligning with ‘effector’ cell clusters, and ETCR clones were absent from memory cluster 1, 9, and rare in cluster 5 (Supplementary Fig. 4D). While the proportion of cells containing ETCR clones did not change over the course of treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4E), we observed both quantitative and qualitative changes in ETCR clones in response to anti-PD-1 therapy. After a single dose of anti-PD-1, the density of ETCR clones shifted from cluster 0 (EFF) to clusters 6 (ISG-EFF) and 2 (GNLY-EFF) (Fig. 3C). Moreover, after multiple doses of anti-PD-1, the majority of ETCR clones resided in cluster 2 (GNLY-EFF), and some were observed in cluster 7 (KLR-EFF). ETCR clones trackable at both baseline and the single-dose time point demonstrated a bias toward either cluster 2 or cluster 6, suggesting distinct differentiation pathways rather than a simultaneous split (Supplementary Fig. 5E).

We observed a small, non-significant increase in the number of ETCR clones, which were proportionally larger following anti-PD-1 (Fig. 3D, E). However, when looking at the proportional abundance of all clones present across the different timepoints (shared clones), we found a significant decrease post anti-PD-1, where many clones that were more prevalent at baseline became proportionally less abundant after treatment (Fig. 3F). This indicates that while some new ETCR clones may have appeared in response to anti-PD-1, the previously dominant ETCR clones present at baseline were proportionally reduced after anti-PD-1.

Interestingly, Cluster 9 (MEM ISG) appeared exclusively following anti-PD-1 treatment (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). In prior studies of chronic LCMV infection in mice, anti-PD-1 was shown to accelerate the differentiation of exhausted progenitor-like (Exh-Prog) to intermediate (Exh-Int) states, which then diverged into either Exh-Terminal (Exh-Term) or Exh-KLR states as summarized in Fig. 3G20,49. We applied these published signatures20 of exhausted CD8 + T cells (Source data tab. 9) as a score to our dataset post anti-PD-1 and found that cluster 9 (MEM-ISG) had a prominent Exh-Prog phenotype (Fig. 3H, first UMAP plot). Furthermore, the mouse Exh-Int signature overlapped with human CX3CR1 expression (Fig. 3H, second UMAP plot and Fig. 2D top row, second UMAP from left) and the mouse Exh-KLR signature overlapped with KLR-EFF (cluster 7) in our human data (Fig. 3H, third UMAP plot and Fig. 2D third row, third UMAP from left). Finally, the mouse Exh-Term signature was highly expressed in cluster 6 (ISG EFF) (Fig. 3H, last UMAP plot and Fig. 2D third row, middle and last UMAP from left) that also showed a prominent ISG signature (Fig. 3I). Finally, pseudotime analysis demonstrated that cells in clusters 7 (KLR EFF) and 6 (ISG EFF) followed a different trajectory compared to cells remaining in cluster 0 (EFF) (Fig. 3J).

Collectively, these data showed that following anti-PD-1, there were dynamic changes in HIV-specific CD8 + T-cells, altering the proportions of cellular subtypes in both effector and memory compartments, including the emergence of a cluster 9 (MEM ISG) subtype that overlapped with an Exh-Prog signature described in mice with chronic LCMV infection20. Anti-PD-1 also altered the proportions of different effector subtypes within ETCR clones.

Expanded TCR (ETCR) clones display distinct gene expression profiles following anti-PD-1

ETCR clones exhibited differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with Gene Ontology (GO) pathways related to immune response and T cell activation (Fig. 4A, B and Supplementary Fig. 4F, Source data tab 6). To further identify qualitative differences in ETCR clones, we first determined the top 10 DEGs between ETCR clones and all other TCRs for each timepoint (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 4G). After a single dose of anti-PD-1, genes such as CCL5, NKG7, and GZMB were upregulated in ETCR clones, while ISGs STAT1, ISG15, and MX1 were upregulated in all other TCRs found after single dose (Fig. 4C). When comparing ETCR clones against all other TCRs across cell types, cluster 5 (MEM) showed striking differences and was the only cluster out of the 10 to produce a significant number of differentially expressed genes (Fig. 4D, E, Source data file tab 7). CCL5, APOBEC3G, CD8A, and GZMH all showed significant differences in expression in ETCR clones compared to all other TCRs within memory cluster 5, independent of anti-PD-1 administration. However, GZMH significantly increased within cluster 5 (MEM) following a single dose of anti-PD-1 (Fig. 4F, G, Source data file, tab 8). Finally, we investigated whether certain TCR clones had a different fate in response to anti-PD-1. By tracking unique TCR clones across various T cell types, we observed that PWH on ART without cancer (Controls C, D, E, F, G, Supplementary Fig. 2A) showed a high prevalence of ETCR clones with a cluster 3 (TTM) phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 5A).

Variable plasticity of ETCR clones following anti-PD-1

To determine whether the phenotype of ETCR clones changed following anti-PD-1, we quantified ‘fidelity clusters’ based on the frequency of an ETCR clone in specific cell types (Supplementary Fig. 5B, top panel). We calculated the proportion of ETCR clones localized to a single transcriptional cluster, where a value close to 1 was consistent with the majority of the ETCR clones residing in a single cluster, indicating a high degree of phenotypic stability (Supplementary Fig. 5B, lower panel). Conversely, a lower fidelity value was consistent with broader heterogeneity, with a unique TCR clone distributed across multiple transcriptional clusters.

We found that ETCR clones had differing levels of fidelity following anti-PD-1. For example, the low-fidelity clones in cluster E were found in multiple T cell types following anti-PD-1, exhibiting more stemness and were more commonly found in the memory compartment (Supplementary Fig. 5C). This would suggest that these ETCR clones continued to produce progeny in response to anti-PD-1, even after multiple doses. In contrast, certain clones with high fidelity scores, like in clusters A and B, maintained their original characteristics following anti-PD-1 (Supplementary Fig. 5D), exhibiting a more differentiated, exhausted phenotype. In summary, these data are consistent with TCR clones having different capacities to respond to anti-PD-1.

Given these findings, we identified two participants (P1, P4) in whom there was an increase in viral RNA following anti-PD-1 (viral responders). We compared DEGs between viral responders and non-responders (Fig. 5C, Source Data file, tab 10). DEG in cluster 5 (MEM) and cluster 6 (MEM-ISG) of viral responders mainly corresponded to ribosomal and HLA genes (Source data file, tab 10). However, TCF1+ memory T cells (Cluster 1) of viral responders expressed an ISG signature. Furthermore, effector T cells (Cluster 0) in the viral responders showed high levels of Hobit (encoded by ZNF683) and PTK2, suggesting that these T cells had enhanced capabilities for migration, adhesion, and tissue infiltration, potentially allowing them to form more effective immunological synapses to target HIV infected cells in response to anti-PD-1. Notably, MX1 was enriched in both TCF1+ memory T cells (Cluster 1) and effector T cells (Cluster 0) in the viral responders. Previous studies have shown that MX1 gene expression is higher in PWH with higher plasma HIV RNA53,54. Discussion

We describe the transcriptome and TCR clonotypes of HIV-specific T-cells following anti-PD-1 in PWH on ART with cancer. We found that a single dose of anti-PD-1 increased HIV-specific CD8 + T cells in some but not all participants. Transcriptomic characterization of HIV-specific CD8 + T-cells identified ten main cellular clusters, including memory and effector CD8 + T- cell subsets. Anti-PD-1 treatment induced dynamic changes in both HIV-specific effector and memory CD8+ compartments, with significant alterations in cluster frequencies and the emergence of 'progenitor'-like memory cells with an ISG signature. Following anti-PD1, there was also an increase in expression of Granzyme-H in expanded clones with expanded T-cell receptors (ETCR), highlighting a potential pathway to reinvigoration of cytotoxic function. Finally, we demonstrated a relationship between an increase in viral RNA following anti-PD-1 and T-cell phenotype prior to administration of anti-PD-1. These findings demonstrate the multiple effects of anti-PD-1 therapy on both HIV-specific CD8 + T cells and HIV latency in PWH on ART.

Following a single dose of anti-PD-1, within isolated HIV-specific tetramer+ CD8 + T-cells, we observed a reduction in cluster 0 (EFF) alongside the emergence of cluster 9 (MEM-ISG), a new population exhibiting features of ‘progenitor’-like memory cells with an ISG signature. Notably, cluster 9 was absent in tetramer+CD8 + T-cells from the five PWH on ART without cancer who did not receive anti-PD-1 (Supplementary Fig. 2A, green and Supplementary Fig. 5A), consistent with this distinct cell population being induced by anti-PD-1 treatment. Cluster 9 cells were also detected within total CD8 + T-cells (which included HIV tetramer-positive cells) following anti-PD1, but at much lower levels than what we found in HIV-specific tetramer+ CD8 + T-cells. We are unable to conclude if the changes in T-cell clusters observed only occurred in response to HIV, or whether similar changes also occurred in other antigen-specific cells, such as CMV-specific or cancer-specific T-cells. Furthermore, we only examined peripheral blood and therefore may have underestimated any impact of anti-PD-1 in lymph nodes. Features of ‘progenitor’ like memory cells arising post anti-PD-1 could potentially be desirable for control of the virus, based on recent reports showing that stemness and proliferative capacity of HIV-specific T-cells at the time of ART cessation were associated with viral control off ART55,56. Further work will be needed to determine the functional significance of this MEM-ISG CD8 + T-cell cluster.

While the cohort size and the absolute number of HIV-specific cells were relatively small, recent studies using even fewer cells showed that the extensive data collected per cell provided enough detail to generate reliable hypotheses57,58. In addition, limitations in blood sampling meant we could only evaluate small numbers of tetramer+ CD8 + T-cells, and this could lead to some sampling bias. Even amongst this small cohort, we saw significant differences between donors. We did not explore the potential variables that may have contributed to this difference, such as sample processing, ethnic background, or other factors. Instead, we focused on integrating the data to homogenize the cell phenotypes across the samples. We included control participants who didn’t have cancer, who were similar in age (median age 50 years) to the participants with cancer (median age 53 years), and all participants were male. Finally, this study primarily included Caucasian men and was, therefore, restricted to certain HLA types, specifically A2, A3, B7 and B8. Findings may well differ in people of other races and/or ethnic groups, women, those infected with other HIV subtypes and have other HLA types.

The effects of anti-PD-1 on T-cell expansion and diversity have been extensively studied in vivo in the context of cancer30,59–61 but less commonly in chronic viral infections62. Different phenotypic changes following anti-PD-1 have been described in CD8 + T-cells, including “cytotoxic revival” (whereby exhausted antigen-specific T-cells proliferate), “clonal replacement” (where new antigen-reactive clones migrate from the periphery to the tumor site) and “clonal revival” (where pre-existing clones revert to a more stem-like state that can potentially regenerate a progeny of effector cells)63. In our study in PWH on ART with cancer, while the overall proportion of cells with ETCR clones remained stable following anti-PD1, we observed a redistribution of ETCR clones across different cellular clusters, and pre-existing ETCR clones were proportionally less abundant. We removed cells with multiple chain contigs, which might be doublets, and we removed TCRs that were likely not HIV-specific, such as MAIT cell TCRs, to enhance the accuracy of TCR clonotype assignment. However, while our data handling was appropriate for the assignment, we didn’t perform further experimental validation of the TCR antigen-specificity. Taken together, our findings are consistent with anti-PD-1 inducing function-related transcriptional changes and differentiation of existing HIV-specific T cell clones, rather than inducing proliferation of existing or new clones.

Together with quantitative changes in T-cell subsets, we also observed the upregulation of genes such as CCL5, NKG7, and GZMB in ETCR clones after anti-PD-1, consistent with an enhancement of effector function. There was an increase in expression of GZMH in memory (Cluster 5) ETCR clones after a single dose of anti-PD-1, highlighting a potential pathway to reinvigoration of cytotoxic function in HIV-specific memory T cells. In addition to qualitative and function-related transcriptional changes in HIV-specific ETCR clones, we observed plasticity of some but not all ETCR clones following anti-PD-1. Lower fidelity ETCR clones showed more potential to retain memory and stemness capacity, while higher fidelity clones clustered as effector cells. Taken together, our findings are consistent with clear changes in the T cell repertoire following anti-PD-1.

In PWH on ART, there is minimal circulating viral antigen due to control of viral replication and minimal or no transcription in the HIV reservoir14,64,65. However, despite minimal viral antigen expressed in PWH on ART, the frequency of HIV-specific CD8 + T cells has been shown to be correlated with levels of cell-associated HIV RNA and DNA14,15, and the HIV-specific TCR repertoire can rejuvenate under long-term ART13. This suggests that the TCR repertoire and function can indeed evolve and change, even in a low antigen setting. Interestingly, even though anti-PD-1 was administered in the setting of stable ART in this study, we found a relationship between an increase in viral RNA following anti-PD-1 and the frequency of memory CD8 + T cells and ISG-effector (cluster 6) CD8 + T cells prior to the administration of anti-PD-1 (Fig. 5A, B). ISG-effector (cluster 6) CD8 + T cells may actively suppress viral transcription from the reservoir, while upregulation of an ISG signature in TCF1+ memory cells (cluster 1) could be a consequence of virus-driven inflammation. These data are consistent with the HIV reservoir being under active control of HIV-specific T-cells.

We and others have previously shown that in PWH on ART, anti-PD-1 increased HIV transcription52,66,67, however, to date, it has not been demonstrated that induction of transcription translates to the production of infectious virions in vivo. In this sub-study, we did not observe an overall significant increase in HIV RNA nor a decline in the HIV DNA reservoir following a single or repeated dosing of anti-PD-1. This may have several explanations. First, the size of this cohort, while sufficient for scRNAseq studies, was not powered to identify a relationship with reservoir clearance. Second, it is possible that newly revitalized T-cells were still not able to kill the HIV reservoir due to increased expression of pro-survival proteins such as B Cell Lymphoma-2 in latently infected cells68. Finally, we only assessed total HIV DNA and therefore could not specifically capture changes in cells carrying intact, replication-competent virus or other qualitative changes in the HIV reservoir. These experiments would require larger numbers of cells to perform either the intact proviral DNA assay69–71 or single cell analysis of the integration site of the provirus in the genome72, which was not feasible in this study. As it was not possible to stop ART in this participant cohort, we could not evaluate the potential synergistic effects of HIV antigen presence in combination with anti-PD-1 treatment16,66.

Multiple studies in humans and macaques have shown greater effects of anti-PD-1 on HIV control when anti-PD-1 was given in the presence of viral antigen. For example, in simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV)-infected non-human primates, anti-PD-1 had minimal effect on T-cell function, time to viral rebound and/ or subsequent viral set point after cessation of ART, when administered in macaques on ART73. However, the administration of anti-PD-1 prior to initiation of ART and again following ART in rhesus macaques infected with SIV resulted in faster viral suppression in plasma, enhanced CD4+ and CD8 + T-cell function, a significant delay in viral rebound following ART interruption and subsequent reduction of the viral setpoint74. The administration of anti-PD-1 to SIV-infected rhesus macaques at the time of stopping ART also improved HIV-specific CD8 + T-cell function and lowered levels of plasma HIV RNA off ART75,76. Consistent with these findings, a clinical trial of low-dose anti-PD-1 (budigalimab) in people with HIV given at the time of stopping ART, resulted in viral control off ART in a subset of participants77. In our study presented in this manuscript, a trial of stopping ART was not feasible, given that participants had cancer.

The use of anti-PD-1 therapy as a cure strategy in PWH without cancer requires a deep understanding of the effects of anti-PD-1 on both HIV-specific T-cells and HIV transcription. Two clinically relevant issues remain: the durability of the observed transcriptional and phenotypic changes, and the potential development of resistance to anti-PD-1, a well-described issue in cancer78 where compensatory coinhibitory pathways eventually dampen the therapeutic effect. To address these gaps, we are currently conducting a clinical trial evaluating low-dose nivolumab in PWH on ART without cancer (Clinicaltrials.gov NCT05187429). This prospective study is designed to assess the long-term safety and durability of checkpoint blockade in this population and will hopefully provide important longitudinal data to determine if therapeutic benefits are sustained.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that anti-PD-1 in PWH on ART induced multiple changes in HIV-specific CD8 + T cells, affecting their phenotype, function, and clonal distribution. These findings contribute to our understanding of how anti-PD-1 impacts HIV persistence and immune control. Whether a response to anti-PD-1 can ultimately result in reduction or control of the intact HIV reservoir remains to be determined in future clinical trials limited to PWH without cancer that can safely incorporate the interruption of ART.

Methods

Our research complies with all relevant ethical regulations. The Consortium for Advancing Management and Prevention of Cancer in People with HIV (AMC)−095 study was a prospective phase 1 clinical trial of either nivolumab (anti-PD-1) or nivolumab plus ipilimumab (anti-CTLA4) in PWH on suppressive ART with advanced HIV-associated solid tumors including uncontrolled Kaposi sarcoma and classical Hodgkin lymphoma (Tabe 1). The AMC is the only cooperative clinical trials group with this research focus. The AMC is supported by a UM1 Cooperative Agreement (CA121947). The study was approved by the institutional review boards at all recruiting sites; the NCI Central Institutional Review Board (CIRB) provides ethical review, acting as the IRB of record for NCI-sponsored clinical trials to ensure they meet the highest ethical and quality standards for protecting human participants as well as the University of Melbourne and was conducted in accordance with the principles described in the: Declaration of Helsinki (1996); Food and Drug Administration regulations; and the Department of Health and Human Services regulations for the protection of human participants. Each participant provided written informed consent, and enrollment and follow-up are ongoing.

Virological assays

The lower limit of detection of the HIV DNA assay was one copy per well, and given the increasing variation in threshold cycles at this low level, we included values down to 0.1 copies. Bioinformatic analysis

Raw sequencing data from six 10x runs (each run containing one timepoint sample from each of the 8 donors) were processed to sparse data matrices for gene expression and VDJ using the cellranger multi pipeline of Cell Ranger (v6.0.2) with the pre-built human reference transcriptome (GRCh38, GENCODE v32/Ensembl 98) and the pre-built human V(D)J reference (GRCh38, version 5.0) from 10x Genomics. Bam files were split by Vireo donors based on cell barcodes. Following this, each donor’s BAM file was filtered for reads mapped to chromosome 6, then input into arcasHLA38 to genotype HLA types for each donor. CITE-seq-Count (version 1.4.3) with the options (-cbf 1 -cbl16 -umif 17 – umil 28 –start-trim 10 –sliding window -cells presentBarcodeCount –whitelist presentBarcode) was used to obtain the count of unique molecular identifier (UMI) for the TotalSeq-C Hashtag-oligos (HTO) antibody from the raw sequencing data as count matrices. Sparse data matrices for gene expression were loaded as input into R and analysed for non-viable cells and likely doublets: All cells with less than 200 or greater than 4000 genes, plus cells with >6% mitochondrial RNA, were removed (thresholds informed by data distribution of each of these variables). Six runs of single-cell data were merged and further processed using Seurat (version 4.3.0)85–87. Across the merged aligned sequencing reads, we demultiplexed the data by first running vireo88 on each of the lanes to stratify the data into genetically distinct donors (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). We assign these donors to the original samples via their hashtag signal: some samples were tagged with single hashtags (e.g. P1), some with pairs of hashtags (e.g., P5) and some without hashtags (filler cells and control cells) (Supplementary Fig. 2D). After assigning donor identities, we merge the lanes into a single dataset and remove additional cells that are identified as doublets via vireo, contain more than one TCR beta chain, or belong to the PBMC filler cell sample (Supplementary Fig. 2A). We end up with 3574 cells including 415 tetramer+ control cells (Supplementary Fig. 2B, D and Source data file, tab 12).

We used SCTransform to normalize the count data and determine the top 3000 variable genes. We excluded T-cell receptor components (^TRA/^TRB) and ribosomal genes (^RPS/^RPL) from the variable genes as unwanted factors of variance and performed principal component analysis (PCA). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) reductions and cell neighbors were calculated using 25 dimensions from the Harmony reduction.

To address inter-donor variability in our dataset, we implemented a PCA integration approach using the Harmony algorithm40, with donors serving as the grouping variable. This method effectively corrected for batch effects associated with individual donors while preserving the biological signal related to treatment time points. We deliberately chose not to integrate across lanes to maintain the integrity of the treatment-related biological information. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2E, the even distribution of lanes across the UMAP plot demonstrates the absence of a significant lane-related batch effect. The unintegrated UMAP reveals substantial inter-donor variability (first panel) but minimal and confounded inter-batch variability (second panel). In contrast, the integrated UMAP (third panel) successfully mitigates the donor-related effects through the application of Harmony.

Ten cell clusters were detected with a resolution of 0.8. We then determined cell types within the data by assessing cluster markers (FindAllMarkers) and their overall expression (Source data file, tab 2). Genes from each cluster were ordered based on the difference in percentage expression between an individual cluster and the other clusters. We focused on genes that were highly expressed in each cluster to assign identity as well as anchor genes published in the literature (see also Results and Fig. 2). The nine longitudinal sample time points that were assessed [Baseline, i.e. cycle (C01) day (D)−1, C01D + 1, C01D + 8, C04D-1, C04D + 1, C13D-1, C13D + 1, C22D + 1] were further grouped as follows: Baseline (also C01D-1), single dose (C01D + 1, C01D + 8), and multiple dose (C04D-1, C04D + 1, C13D-1, C13D + 1, C22D + 1). Participants P3 and P5 were retrospectively excluded from longitudinal analysis as their baseline samples were not viable or retrievable post single-cell RNA sequencing. This left 6 participants (P1, P2, P4, P6, P7 and P8) for longitudinal analysis.

To compare HIV-specific to non-HIV-specific T-cells, we integrated scRNAseq data from HIV-specific tetramer+ T-cells and total PBMCs collected from participant P2 (Supplementary Fig. 2A). We merged the two datasets (total PBMC from P2 and tetramer+ cells from P1, P2, P4, P6, P7 and P8), normalized counts, scaled the data, ran PCA and UMAP algorithms for an overview of phenotypes. Next, we identified T-cells (clusters with CD4 or CD8 expression, as well as CD3 and CD45) and further refined these into CD8 cells only by repeating the above steps and selecting clusters of interest, i.e., CD8. We ran clustering on the final UMAP and then determined cluster phenotypes by selecting the tetramer+ phenotype with the most cells in each cluster (Supplementary Fig. 3D, E).

All further analyses and plots were generated in R (version 4.2) using tidyverse89 functions and ShinyCell90. Heatmaps were created with the pheatmap package (version 1.0.12). Differentially expressed genes were determined with the FindMarkers function using the Wilcoxon test. Enrichment of GO-terms and KEGG pathways was performed with the goana/kegga functions from the edgeR package91. Differential sample composition per donor was analysed using the propeller function from the speckle package (version 0.0.3)92. Signature scores for various gene modules, such as expanded clonotypes, were established using the AddModuleScore function. Cell differentiation levels were computationally estimated using CytoTRACE41. To find the differentially expressed genes between the six conditions: expanded clones (baseline, single dose, multiple dose) and non-expanded clones (baseline, single dose, multiple dose), we used the function rank_genes_group from scanpy version 1.11.1. Associations of viral levels and cell type composition at baseline were established using Pearson correlation (cor function) and linear models (lm function) as shown in Fig. 5A.

TCR analysis

Cellranger outputted filtered contig files were passed through the sc-dandelion package93 for pre-processing. Subsequently, cells that contained a single pair of TCRa and TCRb chains were kept. In cases where two TCRa contigs were detected, the contig with a higher UMI was selected. Clonally related cells (clonotypes) were identified by having matching CDR3a and CDR3b nucleotide sequences, and expansion was defined using a data-based frequency cut-off for expanded TCR clonotypes of 2% above the singleton peak in all the samples, to correct for population sampling and uneven cell numbers per donor (Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). Analysis and graphing of data were done using custom Python scripts. Computational analyses and plotting were performed in R (version 4.2), Python (version 3.11.2) and ShinyCell90. Heatmaps were created with the pheatmap package (version 1.0.12).

Statistics & reproducibility

This immunological sub-study utilized longitudinal samples from the AMC-095 clinical trial (NCT02408861). No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size; enrollment was based on the availability of viable specimens. Participants P3 and P5 were retrospectively excluded from longitudinal analysis due to non-viable baseline samples, leaving six participants for final analysis. Information on sex, gender, and race was self-reported and not considered in the study design. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were blinded to clinical outcomes as the trial is ongoing; however, they were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.

Data normality was assessed via frequency histograms and Q-Q plots. Parametric (t-test, ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc) or non-parametric (Mann-Whitney-U, Wilcoxon signed-rank) tests were applied as appropriate. Skewed data were log-transformed. Data collection and compilation were done using Excel (version 16.1073), Graphpad Prism v.10.2.1, R and Python. Statistical testing on analyte measurements and flow cytometry data was performed using FlowJo V10.10.0 and Graphpad Prism (v10.2.1). For scRNA seq analysis, we used Cell Ranger (v6.0.2), Seurat (version 4.3.0), speckle package (version 0.0.3), vireo (version 0.5.8), R (version 4.2), Harmony (version 1.1.0), edgeR package (version 3.40.2). Viral reservoir measurements were performed in triplicate (RNA) or quadruplicate (DNA). Due to the nature of the clinical trial, replication via scRNA-seq was not possible. Standardized protocols were used for PBMC isolation and processing. Illustrations were created by Gubser, C. w BioRender (2026). https://BioRender.com/guinpdh. The manuscript was refined for readability using Claude 3.5 Sonnet.
