Nuclear state and level densities of actinides with the shell-model Monte Carlo
D. DeMartini and Y. Alhassid

Abstract
The study of heavy open-shell nuclei requires model spaces that are too large for the direct diagonalization approaches of conventional shell-model calculations. The shell-model Monte Carlo (SMMC) method enables calculations of the thermal properties of heavy nuclei in very large model spaces. Here, we extend the SMMC method to the actinides. Fifteen even-even and odd-mass actinides 232 Th, 234-239 {}^{\textrm{234-239}} U, 240-243 {}^{\textrm{240-243}} Pu, 246-248 {}^{\textrm{246-248}} Cm, and 250 Cf are studied using a single-particle model space that is larger than one major shell each for protons and neutrons, with a total dimension of the many-particle space as large as 10 32 10^{32} . We use spin projection methods to calculate nuclear level densities and average s s -wave neutron resonance spacings, both of which are found to be in good agreement with recent experiments.



The nuclear level density (NLD) is a fundamental statistical property of nuclei with importance to compound nuclear reactions
Hilaire and Goriely 2021
, astrophysical abundances and processes
Arnould et al. 2007
, and more. Theoretical calculations of these properties in heavy open-shell nuclei have required either highly restricted model spaces, or the use of mean-field approximations (e.g., density function theory
Drut et al. 2010
) which miss important correlations between nucleons. Accurate calculations of statistical properties at finite temperature in the actinide mass region (
A\sim 240
) require model spaces larger than
10^{30}
, while computational costs currently limit conventional diagonalization methods to model spaces around
10^{11}

Tsunoda et al. 2017
;
Shimizu et al. 2019
. The shell-model Monte Carlo (SMMC)
Alhassid 2017
enables microscopic calculations of nuclear properties in very large model spaces. Previously, the SMMC method has been applied to nuclei as heavy as the lanthanides with
A\sim 160

Alhassid et al. 2008
;
Özen et al. 2013
;
Özen et al. 2015
;
Alhassid et al. 2014
;
Guttormsen et al. 2021
;
Mercenne et al. 2024
;
DeMartini and Alhassid 2025
.

Several experimental methods have been used to measure NLDs. The Oslo method
Schiller et al. 2000
;
Guttormsen et al. 2005
;
Larsen et al. 2011
has been used to measure the NLDs of nuclei across a broad region of the nuclear chart at excitation energies below their neutron separation energies
S_{n}
. In particular, the Oslo method has been used to measure NLDs in several actinides
Guttormsen et al. 2013
;
Tornyi et al. 2014
;
Laplace et al. 2016
;
Zeiser et al. 2019
.

Actinides can fission and the deformation dependence of their NLDs is an important input to nuclear fission calculations
Mazurek et al. 2011
;
Ward et al. 2017
. Additionally, the quadrupole deformation of
238
U, the largest nucleus collided at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider, can impact observables like the transverse momentum distributions of particles detected in relativistic heavy ion collisions
Adamczyk et al. 2015
;
Giacalone 2020
;
Jia et al. 2022
;
Magdy 2023
.

In this Letter we present the first applications of the SMMC method to actinides. The larger model space and need to calculate observables at very large inverse temperatures
\beta=1/T
(in order to cool the nucleus to its ground state) present additional technical challenges compared to previous applications of SMMC. The calculated NLDs are found to be in similarly good agreement with experiment as has been seen in the lighter lanthanides. This work constitutes the first step towards studying statistical properties of actinides that are much beyond the reach of conventional large-scale shell-model calculations.

The SMMC method is based on the canonical ensemble of a nucleus at finite temperature
T
. The Gibbs operator
e^{-\beta\hat{H}}
(where
\beta=1/T
) is transformed by the Hubbard-Stratonovich (HS) transformation
Stratonovich 1957
;
Hubbard 1959
into a functional integral of one-body propagators
\hat{U}_{\sigma}
that describe non-interacting nucleons in a set of external, imaginary-time-dependent fields
\sigma(\tau)
with
0\leq\tau<\beta

e^{-\beta\hat{H}}=\int D[\sigma]G_{\sigma}\hat{U}_{\sigma},

(1)

where
G_{\sigma}
is a Gaussian weight and
D[\sigma]
is the integral measure.

The thermal average of an observable
\hat{O}
is then given by

\langle\hat{O}\rangle=\frac{\textrm{Tr}(\hat{O}e^{-\beta\hat{H}})}{\textrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta\hat{H}})}=\frac{\int D[\sigma]G_{\sigma}\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\sigma}\textrm{Tr}\hat{U}_{\sigma}}{\int D[\sigma]G_{\sigma}\textrm{Tr}\hat{U}_{\sigma}}.

(2)

The integral over the auxiliary fields is evaluated using Monte Carlo methods, in which the fields
\sigma(\tau)
are samples with the positive-definite weight
W_{\sigma}\equiv G_{\sigma}|\textrm{Tr}\hat{U}_{\sigma}|
. The Monte-Carlo sign
\Phi_{\sigma}\equiv\textrm{Tr}\hat{U}_{\sigma}/|\textrm{Tr}\hat{U}_{\sigma}|
is always positive for grand-canonical ensembles with the good-sign interaction used in this work.

Quantities in the integrand of Eq. (
2
) can be calculated using the matrix representation
\boldsymbol{U}_{\sigma}
of the propagator
\hat{U}_{\sigma}
in the single-particle model space, reducing the matrix algebra to matrices the size of the single-particle space (
\sim 100
) rather than the combinatorially-large spaces required in direct diagonalization methods. For example, the grand-canonical trace of

\hat{U}_{\sigma}
is given by

\textrm{Tr}\hat{U}_{\sigma}=\det(\boldsymbol{1}+\boldsymbol{U}_{\sigma}).

(3)

The imaginary-time interval
0\leq\tau\leq\beta
is discretized into
N_{\tau}
time slices of width
\Delta\beta=\beta/N_{\tau}
. For any given value of
\beta
, thermal averages of observables are calculated for
\Delta\beta=1/32
MeV
-1
and
\Delta\beta=1/64
MeV
-1
. The continuous time limit
\Delta\beta\rightarrow 0
of the expectation value of an observable is then determined by linear extrapolation of the expectation values computed at these two values of
\Delta\beta
.

We work in the canonical ensemble with fixed proton and neutron numbers so the grand-canonical traces in Eq. (
2
) are replaced by canonical traces at fixed particle number
A
, i.e., we substitute
\textrm{Tr}\rightarrow\textrm{Tr}_{A}
.
This is accomplished by an exact particle-number projection onto
A
valence nucleons via a discrete Fourier transform
Ormand et al. 1994
;
Alhassid et al. 1999

\textrm{Tr}_{A}\hat{U}_{\sigma}=\frac{e^{-\beta\mu A}}{N_{s}}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{s}}e^{-i\phi_{m}A}\det(\boldsymbol{1}+e^{i\phi_{m}+\beta\mu}\boldsymbol{U_{\sigma}})\;.

(4)

In practice, we use two particle-number projections onto fixed proton and neutron numbers.

For good-sign interactions (in the grand-canonical ensemble), the projection onto an even number of particles keeps the good sign, but the projection on an odd number of particles leads to a sign problem at low temperatures. This makes direct calculations of the ground-state properties of odd-mass nuclei impossible. Additional extrapolations must be made to determine ground-state observables in these nuclei.

A configuration-interaction (CI) shell-model Hamiltonian has been developed to describe statistical properties of actinides. The model space consists of spherical orbitals with energies computed from a central Woods-Saxon potential with spin-orbit coupling. The doubly-magic
208
Pb is used as the inert core. For protons, the valence space consists of the 82-126 shell plus the
1g_{9/2}
orbital, while the neutron valence space consists of the 126-184 shell plus the
1h_{11/2}
orbital
Nak
. For the largest nuclei studied in this work, the total m-scheme model space dimension is greater than
10^{32}
.

The interaction Hamiltonian consists of a pairing-plus-multipole interaction which has a good Monte-Carlo sign and describes the dominant collective components of realistic nuclear interactions
Dufour and Zuker 1996
. The interaction Hamiltonian is given by

\hat{H}_{\textrm{int}}=-\sum_{\nu}g_{\nu}\hat{P}_{\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{P}_{\nu}-\sum_{\lambda}\chi_{\lambda}:(\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\lambda p}+\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\lambda n})\cdot(\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\lambda p}+\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\lambda n}):,

(5)

where
\nu=p,n
is the nucleon species,
\lambda=2,3,4
corresponds to the
2^{\lambda}
-pole interactions,
P_{\nu}^{\dagger}
is the monopole pair creation operator,
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\lambda\nu}
are the surface-peaked multipole operators, and
::
denotes normal ordering. The multipole strengths are defined as
\chi_{\lambda}=\chi k_{\lambda}
where
\chi
is determined self-consistently
Alhassid et al. 1996
and
k_{\lambda}
are renormalization factors that take into account the effects of the inert core. The interaction coefficients are parameterized by the following
N
-dependent functional forms:

\begin{gathered}g_{p}=0.111+0.00038(N-148)\\
g_{n}=0.0681+0.00047(N-148)^{5/4}\\
k_{2}=2.299-\frac{2.39}{(N-148)^{2}+21.206}-0.0149(N-148)\\
k_{3}=k_{4}=1.0\\
\end{gathered}

(6)

These coefficients were determined such that they reproduce known odd-even mass staggerings and models of nuclear state densities fitted to experimental data (See the Supplemental Material
sup
and references therein
Gilbert and Cameron 1965
;
Krieger et al. 1990
;
Cwiok et al. 1996
;
Donati et al. 2005
;
Goriely et al. 2008
;
Bonett-Matiz et al. 2013
for more details).

The nuclear state density (NSD) at excitation energy of
E_{x}=\langle\hat{H}\rangle-E_{0}
is the inverse Laplace transform of the nuclear partition function
Z(\beta)
. This transform is numerically ill-behaved, and instead we compute it in the saddle-point approximation where it provides the average NSD

\rho(E_{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-i\infty}^{+i\infty}d\beta e^{\beta E_{x}}Z(\beta)\approx\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\pi C}}e^{S(E_{x})}\;.

(7)

In Eq. (
7
)
S
and
C
are, respectively, the canonical entropy and heat capacity. The logarithm of the partition function
\ln Z(\beta)
is computed by integrating the thermodynamic relation
E=-\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta
for the thermal energy
E=\langle H\rangle
. The canonical entropy is then determined by
S(E_{x})=\ln Z+E_{x}\beta
, and the canonical heat capacity
C
is computed using the method of Ref.
Liu and Alhassid 2001
. Thermal energies
E(\beta)
are calculated up to
\beta=40
MeV
-1
for even-even nuclei and
\beta=8
MeV
-1
for odd-mass nuclei. For the even-even nuclei, this temperature is sufficiently low that
E_{0}
can be determined by fitting the thermal energy to a form assuming a ground-state rotational band. For odd-mass nuclei, this temperature is not low enough to directly extract
E_{0}
and we use the recently developed partition function extrapolation method
Alhassid et al. 2024
.

In comparing the SMMC NSDs with experimental data, we used the back-shifted Bethe formula (BBF)
Dilg et al. 1973

\rho_{\textrm{BBF}}(E_{x})=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{2\sqrt{a(E_{x}-\Delta)}}}{12a^{1/4}(E_{x}-\Delta)^{5/4}},

(8)

where
a
and
\Delta
are parameters determined by a fit to low-lying state counting and neutron resonance average spacing data

The contribution of these rotational states is quantified by the enhancement factor
K=\rho_{\textrm{SMMC}}/\rho_{\textrm{HFB}}
. The energy dependence of
K
, shown in Fig.
2
for the even-even uranium isotopes, displays some important features. For the energies of interest in this work, the enhancement factors are between 10 and 25, indicating a large number of rotational states missed in the HFB . The quick decrease in
K
starting around
E_{x}\sim 15
MeV is due to the transition from deformed to spherical shapes of the nuclei. At the highest excitation energies, when the nuclei are essentially spherical,
K\approx 1
indicating agreement between the HFB and the SMMC as the rotational bands have disappeared. The large enhancement at very low excitation energies is an unphysical artifact of the non-conservation of particle number in the HFB.

The NSD counts the total number of states including the
2J+1
magnetic degeneracy for each level of spin
J
. However, the NLD measured in experiments such as the Oslo method counts each level with spin
J
only once. The SMMC naturally includes contributions from all degenerate states, but the NLD can be computed through the use of spin projection
Alhassid et al. 2007
. For each level of spin
J
in an even-mass (odd-mass) nucleus there exists exactly one state with
M=0
(
M=1/2
), thus by projecting thermal observables onto
M=0
(
M=1/2
) the contribution of only one state from each level is counted. The trace over an observable
\hat{O}
at fixed spin component
M
is calculated using a discrete Fourier transform

\textrm{Tr}_{M}\hat{O}=\frac{1}{2J_{s}+1}\sum_{k=-J_{s}}^{J_{s}}e^{i\phi_{k}M}\textrm{Tr}(e^{i\phi_{k}\hat{J}_{z}}\hat{O}),

(9)

where
\phi_{k}=\frac{k\pi}{J_{s}+1/2}
are quadrature points with
k=-J_{s},...,J_{s}
where
J_{s}
is the maximum possible spin in the model space. By taking Eq. (
7
) and replacing the thermal excitation energy
E_{x}
with the thermal excitation energy projected onto
M=0
, we can calculate directly the NLD. Note that the term
e^{i\phi_{k}M}
leads to non-positive weights for non-zero
M
,resulting in a sign problem at very low temperatures.

The spin (as well as parity) distribution of level densities is another important input in the calculation of nuclear reaction rates
Rauscher et al. 1997
. This spin distribution is also necessary for calculating total level densities from the measured average neutron resonance spacings
Schiller et al. 2000
. Typically, it is assumed that at the neutron resonance energy the spin distribution follows the spin-cutoff model
Ericson 1960
.

\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{J}(E_{x})}{\rho(E_{x})}=\frac{2J+1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{3}}e^{-J(J+1)/2\sigma^{2}},

(10)

where
\sigma
is the spin-cutoff parameter, which depends on
E_{x}
and is related to a thermal moment of inertia
I
by
\sigma^{2}=IT/\hbar^{2}
.

Assuming that both parities have equal densities at
S_{n}
, the average s-wave neutron resonance spacing
D_{0}
of the target nucleus in the neutron resonance experiment (i.e., the nucleus with one neutron fewer than the nucleus whose NLD is being considered) can be related to the spin-dependent level densities by

\frac{2}{D_{0}}=\begin{cases}\tilde{\rho}_{1/2}(S_{n})&\textrm{if }J_{t}=0\\
(\tilde{\rho}_{J_{t}-1/2}(S_{n})+\tilde{\rho}_{J_{t}+1/2}(S_{n}))&\textrm{if }J_{t}\neq 0,\end{cases}

(11)

where
J_{t}
is the ground-state spin of the target nucleus. Further assuming that the spin distribution is well described by the spin-cutoff model (
10
) leads the the relation

\tilde{\rho}(S_{n})=\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{D_{0}((J_{t}+1)e^{-(J_{t}+1)^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}+J_{t}e^{-J_{t}^{2}/2\sigma^{2}})}.

(12)

In the SMMC, the spin-projected level densities are calculated from the
M
-projected level densities using
\tilde{\rho}_{J}=\tilde{\rho}_{M=J}-\tilde{\rho}_{M=J+1}

By computing the spin-projected NLDs, the spin-distribution
\tilde{\rho}_{J}/\rho
can be calculated at fixed excitation energy
E_{x}
. We find that at excitation energies near
S_{n}
, the spin distribution is very well described by the spin cutoff model. A fit is then performed to determine the spin-cutoff parameter
\sigma(S_{n})
with high accuracy and used to calculate the average neutron resonance
s
-wave spacing
D_{0}
.

The fitted values of
\sigma(S_{n})
shown in Table
1
are found to be within a few percent of the values from the empirical model of Refs.
Zhongfu et al. 1991
;
von Egidy and Bucurescu 2005
, which were used in the Oslo method analyses. For the even-even nuclei, we find the SMMC results for
\tilde{\rho}(S_{n})
and
D_{0}
to agree with their experimental values. For the odd-mass nuclei, the SMMC values of
\tilde{\rho}(S_{n})
and
D_{0}
are, respectively, lower and higher than their corresponding experimental values. We note however that the uncertainties in these quantities for the odd-mass nuclei are much larger due to the larger uncertainties in the determination of the ground-state energies.

In conclusion, we have carried out the first microscopic calculations of NSDs and NLDs in the actinide region using the SMMC. The SMMC is the only method currently able to handle the large model spaces required for this mass region. Our results are in agreement with experimental level counting data at low excitations, neutron resonance data, and recent Oslo method results. The SMMC spin projection enables predictions of the spin cutoff parameter and neutron resonance average spacing in nuclei that have not yet been studied experimentally.
