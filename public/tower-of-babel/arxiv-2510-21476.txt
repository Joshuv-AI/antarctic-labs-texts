From Discrete to Continuous-Variable Systems via Jordan–Schwinger Tomographic Transformation Preprint: APS/123-QED
Vladimir A. Orlov, Liubov A. Markovich, Alexey N. Rubtsov, Vladimir I. Man’ko

Abstract
Hybrid quantum systems that combine discrete-variable (DV) and continuous-variable (CV) architectures represent a promising direction in quantum information science. However, transferring concepts and methods between such fundamentally different platforms entails both practical and theoretical challenges. The formalisms of these two “universes” differ significantly, and many notions, although sharing the same names, possess distinct properties and physical interpretations. In this work, we construct a bridge between DV and CV systems by means of the tomographic probability representation of quantum states complemented by the Jordan–Schwinger map. We connect observable random variables such as spin projections, photon numbers, or quadratures arising in DV and CV architectures through their probabilistic representations, namely spin, photon-number, and symplectic tomograms, as well as the Wigner function. This makes it possible to directly obtain the tomogram in one architecture from measurement data in another, thereby reconstructing the corresponding state across representations realizing similar statistics. The proposed formalism enables a unified framework for comparing and transferring quantum information across different hardware platforms. This facilitates the design of hybrid protocols where, for example, spin-based quantum memories interface with photonic communication channels or CV bosonic codes. It also provides a practical tool for benchmarking quantum devices, validating algorithms across heterogeneous architectures, and exploring error correction schemes that rely on mappings between finite- and infinite-dimensional systems.



I
Introduction

Quantum information theory operates within two complementary paradigms:
the discrete-variable (DV) framework, describing finite-dimensional systems such as qubits and qudits, and the continuous-variable (CV) framework, describing infinite-dimensional systems such as optical modes
Chen et al. 2014
, microwave-cavity modes
Rosenblum et al. 2018
, and vibrational modes of trapped ions
Flühmann et al. 2019
.
Mapping between these different types of quantum objects helps to unify different physical platforms and enables
hybrid continuous variable-discrete variable
(CV-DV) architectures, exploring the best from both CV and DV worlds in one quantum device
Takeda et al. 2015
;
Andersen et al. 2015
;
He and Malaney 2022
;
Lu et al. 2025
. Classic examples include the Jordan–Schwinger mapping
Jordan 1935
;
Schwinger 1952
which connects Lie algebra generators to bilinear combinations of bosonic or fermionic operators, Jordan–Wigner transformation
Jordan and Wigner 1928
, which relates spin chains to fermionic systems, Holstein–Primakoff mapping
Holstein and Primakoff 1940
of spins to bosonic modes, and Dyson–Maleev transformation
Dyson 1956
;
Maleev 1958
providing a mapping of spin operators onto bosonic operators using a non-Hermitian representation.

Since the density matrix of a given state cannot be measured directly, alternative but equivalent representations are required. For CV systems, this led to
quasiprobability distribution functions
(QPDFs) such as the Wigner
Wigner 1932
, Husimi

Q-

Husimi 1940
, and Sudarshan–Glauber
P
-functions
Sudarshan 1963
;
Cahill and Glauber 1969a
.
All of these functions are QPDFs, since they can take negative values or the variables they depend on lack direct physical meaning, being not measurable experimentally. The development of
symplectic tomography
resolved this by representing quantum states through positive probability distribution functions (PDFs) of quadratures
Mancini et al. 1995
, a framework that generalizes optical homodyne tomography
Lvovsky 2004
;
Lvovsky and Mlynek 2002
;
Lvovsky and Raymer 2009
.
The tomogram is informationally complete
Amosov et al. 2008
;
Man’ko and Markovich 2020
and, owing to the new nonparametric kernel quantum state reconstruction (KQSE) method
Markovich et al. 2025
, allows one to estimate any state (even non-Gaussian) while avoiding the drawbacks of reconstruction techniques based on QPDFs and maximum likelihood estimator.

Similar to CV systems, DV spin states can be represented by probability distributions known as
spin tomograms

Dodonov and Man’ko 1997
, obtained from projection measurements along different axes. In quantum optics,
photon-number tomography
provides an analogous description by measuring photon statistics, where Fock states correspond to photon-number eigenstates
Banaszek and Wódkiewicz 1996
;
Wallentowitz and Vogel 1996
;
Zucchetti et al. 1996
;
Mancini et al. 1997
.

Tomograms thus emerge as genuine quantum objects: while they share the positivity and statistical interpretation of classical probabilities, their dynamics are inherently quantum and governed by star-product relations
Man’ko et al. 2000
;
Man’ko et al. 2002
. They have been used to study entanglement and Bell-type inequalities, further emphasizing their foundational role
Andreev and Man’ko 2001
;
Lupo et al. 2005
;
Chernega et al. 2022
;
Paul et al. 2023
.

Despite this common probabilistic nature, photon, optical, and spin tomography remain largely separate frameworks, differing in measurement strategies and the underlying Hilbert space structure. This raises a central question: can symplectic, photon-number, and spin tomograms be formally connected? Establishing such a link would enable protocol transfer across DV and CV architectures, with potential impact on CV-DV quantum computing
Lloyd and Braunstein 1999
;
Braunstein and van
Loock 2005
;
Adesso et al. 2014
;
Takeda 2023
;
Hanamura et al. 2024
;
Abel et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
.

In this work, we employ the Jordan–-Schwinger (JS) operator mapping to establish explicit correspondences between DV and CV state tomograms, thereby providing a unified probabilistic framework for comparing different quantum architectures. Using the quantizer–dequantizer formalism
Andreev et al. 2020
, we derive transition kernels enabling one-to-one transformations among symplectic, photon-number, and spin tomograms, as well as Wigner functions (see Fig.
1
). This framework allows one to start from a measured random variable in one architecture and directly obtain its tomogram in another, thus reconstructing the corresponding state across representations or perform further analyses on the tomogram itself. The approach is particularly advantageous because certain measurements and benchmarks are more accessible in either discrete or continuous settings
Gandhari et al. 2024
, while their counterparts can be consistently obtained through our method.

II
Probability Picture of Quantum Mechanics

The central object describing a quantum state is the density matrix
\hat{\rho}
, that is a positive semi-definite, Hermitian operator with unit trace acting on the complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Although the density operator
\hat{\rho}
provides a complete description of a quantum state, its matrix representation depends on the choice of basis, which reflects the physical observables relevant to a given experimental setup. In different literature the same symbol
\hat{\rho}
can hide important structural differences, and we annotate this operator with dimensional or contextual labels when clarity is needed, especially discussing hybrid quantum setups.

We consider a set of Hermitian operators
\hat{U}(z)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, called the
dequantizer
, where
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is the set of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
The dequantizer defines the
symbol
of an operator
\hat{\rho}
as a function:

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(z)=\tr{\hat{\rho}\hat{U}(z)}.

(1)

Here
z
is vector of parameters. This symbol establishes a linear mapping from the operator space to the functional space. Conversely, one can reconstruct

\hat{\rho}
from its symbol
f_{\rho}(z)
, using a set of
quantizer
operators
\hat{D}(z)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. If
z
is a vector of continuous parameters the reconstruction transformation is

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}=\int f_{\rho}(z)\hat{D}(z)dz,

(2)

while if
z
takes the discrete values
k
it is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}=\sum\limits_{k}f_{\rho}(k)\hat{D}(k).

(3)

In the continuous and discrete cases, quantizers and dequantizers are connected by the relations:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\tr{\hat{U}(z')^\dagger\hat{D}(z)}\!=\!\delta(z-z^{\prime}),\!\!\!\!\quad\tr{ \hat{U}(k)^\dagger\hat{D}(k')}=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}.

(4)

The quantizer/dequantizer pair associated with an operator
\hat{\rho}
is not uniquely defined. In general, the only requirement imposed on the dequantizer is hermiticity. However, in this case, the resulting symbol is not necessarily a PDF (for CV states) or a probability function (for DV states). Imposing the condition on the dequantizer
\hat{U}(z)
to be a positive semi-definite, complete on the
\mathcal{H}
operator, constitutes a positive operator-valued measure (POVM). Then Eq. (
1
) defines a valid PDF or probability function over the variable
z
. Further we consider the cases of DV and CV states on the finite and infinite Hilbert spaces and their tomographic representation to have a clear probabilistic picture of quantum states.

II.1
Discrete-Variable States in Finite Hilbert Space

If the Hilbert space is finite
\mathcal{H}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
, with
d<\infty
, then
\hat{\rho}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
and it is represented by a
d\times d
matrix with matrix elements

\hat{\rho}^{(d)}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\rho_{ij}\ket{i}\bra{j}
,
\rho_{ij}=\langle i|\hat{\rho}|j\rangle
,
expressed in an orthonormal basis
\{\ket{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
. The diagonal elements
\rho_{ii}
are the probabilities that a measurement of the observable associated with the basis
\{\ket{i}\}
yields the outcome corresponding to
\ket{i}
. An example of the latter case is a spin-
j
quantum system. Then we work on the
d=2j+1
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
and the natural basis is the eigenbasis of the spin operators
\hat{S}^{2}
and
\hat{S}_{z}
:

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{z}\ket{j, m}=m\ket{j, m},\quad\hat{S}^{2}\ket{j, m}=j(j+1)\ket{j, m},

(5)

denoted by
\{\ket{j,m}\}_{m=-j}^{j}
.
The density matrix
\hat{\rho}^{(j)}
is a
(2j+1)\times(2j+1)
matrix:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{(j)}=\sum\limits_{m,m^{\prime}=-j}^{j}\rho_{mm^{\prime}}^{(j)}\ket{j,m}\bra{j,m'},

(6)

where
\rho_{mm^{\prime}}^{(j)}=\langle j,m|\hat{\rho}^{(j)}|j,m^{\prime}\rangle
.
The diagonal elements
\rho_{mm}^{(j)}
are the probabilities of measuring projection

m
along the
z
direction.

Spin State Tomogram

A
spin tomogram

\omega_{j}(m|i)
is defined as the probability of obtaining the spin projection value
m
when measuring the spin-
j
system along the direction
i
:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\omega_{j}(m|i)=\langle j,m;i|\hat{\rho}^{(j)}|j,m;i\rangle,\!\!\!\!\!\!\quad\sum\limits_{m=-j}^{j}\omega_{j}(m|i)=1,

(7)

where the eigenstates
\{|j,m;i\rangle\}
of the spin projection operator
\hat{S}_{i}
are corresponding to outcome

m
.
To perform spin state tomography experimentally, one measures the spin projections along various directions
i
that form an informationally complete measurement set
Chen et al. 2019
;
Perlin et al. 2021
;
Stricker et al. 2022
. Hence for each direction
i
one obtains
2j+1
probabilities (spin tomograms).

The spin tomogram can be defined for the case of arbitrary rotating axes. To this end we use the general expression for a rotation operator
\hat{R}(\Omega)=e^{-i\alpha\hat{S}_{z}}\,e^{-i\beta\hat{S}_{y}}\,e^{-i\gamma\hat{S}_{z}}
in terms of Euler angles
\Omega\equiv(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. Let us also denote the density matrix composed of eigenvectors
|j,m\rangle
as
\hat{\rho}^{(j)}_{m}=|j,m\rangle\langle j,m|
.
Using (
1
), we define the tomogram for rotating axes as

\displaystyle\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr{\hat{\rho}^{(j)} \hat{\mathcal{U}}(m, \Omega)}\equiv\mathcal{S}[\hat{\rho}^{(j)}],

(8)

where we introduce the dequantizer as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)\equiv\hat{R}^{\dagger}(\Omega)\hat{\rho}^{(j)}_{m}\hat{R}(\Omega),

(9)

and
\sum_{m}\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)=\hat{1}
,
\forall\Omega
.
The tomogram for rotating axes satisfies the normalization conditions:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)=1,\quad\frac{2j+1}{8\pi^{2}}\int\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)d\Omega=1,

(10)

where
\int d\Omega\equiv\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\alpha\int_{0}^{\pi}d\beta\,\sin\beta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\gamma
.
The dequantizer operator can be written in the basis of irreducible tensor operators (ITOs)
\hat{T}^{(j)}_{LM}
, which carry definite rank
L\in[0,2j]
and projection
M\in[-L,L]
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{L=0}^{2j}\sum\limits_{M=-L}^{L}C_{L,M}(m,\Omega)\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L\,M},

(11)

where we denote

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!C_{L,M}(m,\Omega)\equiv(-1)^{\,j-m}\langle j,m;j,-m\mid L,0\rangle\,D^{(L)}_{M,0}(\Omega).

(12)

Here
D^{(L)}_{M,0}(\cdot)
is the standard Wigner
D
-matrix of spin
L

Edmonds 1957
. The quantizer can be found from the condition (
4
) to be:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}(m,\Omega)=\sum\limits_{L=0}^{2j}\frac{2L+1}{8\pi^{2}}\sum\limits_{M=-L}^{L}C_{L,M}(m,\Omega)\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L\,M}.

(13)

Then the state is reconstructed as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{(j)}=\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\int\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)\hat{\mathcal{D}}(m,\Omega)d\Omega.

(14)

The detailed derivations can be found in Appendix
A
.

II.2
Discrete-Variable States in Infinite Hilbert Space

If the Hilbert space is infinite, then
\hat{\rho}
is a trace-class, compact operator, and its spectrum consists of infinitely many eigenvalues. This density operator may be expressed in a CV basis (e.g., position or momentum) or in the DV Fock basis. For a single mode Fock basis
\{\ket{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, the density matrix operator is

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{(1)}=\sum\limits_{n,m=0}^{\infty}\rho_{nm}\ket{n}\bra{m},\quad\rho_{nm}=\bra{n}\hat{\rho}_{(1)}\ket{m}.

(15)

This matrix has a countably infinite entries. The diagonal elements
\rho_{nn}
are probabilities of measuring

n
photons in the system.

We can generalize this for an
M
-mode quantum system, where the natural basis is the
M
-mode Fock basis

\{\ket{\Vec{n}_M}\equiv\ket{n_1,n_2,\dots, n_M}|n_{l}\in\mathbb{N}_{0},l\in[1,M]\}
.
An arbitrary mixed state
\hat{\rho}_{(M)}
of such a system is:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{(M)}=\sum\limits_{\vec{n}_{M},\vec{n^{\prime}}_{M}=0}^{\infty}\rho_{\vec{n}_{M},\vec{n}^{\prime}_{M}}\ket{\Vec{n}_M}\bra{\Vec{n}'_M},

(16)

where the matrix
\rho_{\vec{n},\vec{n}^{\prime}}
is countably infinite-dimensional in
2M
indices
\vec{n}_{M}
and
\vec{n}^{\prime}_{M}
. Therefore,
\hat{\rho}_{(M)}
is a trace-class operator on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{(M)}=\bigotimes_{l=1}^{M}\mathcal{H}_{l}
, with the Fock space
\mathcal{H}_{l}=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{n_l}\}_{n_{l}=0}^{\infty}
of mode
l
.

Photon-Number Tomogram

Photon–number tomography is a quantum state reconstruction technique that relies on the statistics of photon numbers and uses photon‐counting measurements to determine the state of a light field
Banaszek and Wódkiewicz 1996
;
Wallentowitz and Vogel 1996
;
Zucchetti et al. 1996
;
Mancini et al. 1997
.
The
photon–number tomogram
is defined as
Man’ko 2012
:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)=\tr{\hat{\rho}_{(1)} \hat{\mathcal{U}}_n(\alpha)}\equiv\mathcal{P}[\hat{\rho}_{(1)}],

(17)

with the dequantizer
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(\alpha)=\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\ket{n}\bra{n}\hat{D}(\alpha)
. Here
\hat{D}(\alpha)=\exp\left(\alpha\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\,\hat{a}\right)
is the Weyl displacement operator,
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
is a field amplitude, while
n
is the non-negative integer photon number. The
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
are creation and annihilation operators, respectively.
The photon–number tomogram is the probability of detecting
n
photons in a state that has been displaced by
\alpha
. It is normalized with respect to the discrete variable
n
as
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)=1
. This comes from the resolution of the identity
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(\alpha)=\hat{1}
and the fact that the dequantizer
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(\alpha)
is a POVM.
The inverted transformation is

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{(1)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{\mathbb{C}}d^{2}\alpha\,\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}(\alpha)\equiv\mathcal{P}^{-1}[\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)],

(18)

where the quantizer operator is given by:

\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}(\alpha)=4(\pi(1-s^{2}))^{-1}g(s)^{(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\alpha^{*})(\hat{a}+\alpha)-n},

(19)

where
g(s)\equiv(s-1)/(s+1)
and
with the ordering parameter
s=-1,0,1
corresponding to antinormal, symmetric, and normal ordering of operator
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, respectively. One can see, that the full set of probabilities (
17
) for all displacement parameters

\alpha
and photon numbers
n
forms the tomogram sufficient to reconstruct the full quantum state.

Consider the state
\hat{\rho}_{(1)}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\ket{0}\bra{0}+\ket{1}\bra{1})
. Then the tomogram (
17
) is nonzero only for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
but its weight is concentrated on low photon numbers. Even though the state support is in
\{0,1\}
the displaced states
\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\ket{0}
,
\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\ket{1}
have infinite support in the Fock basis, so
\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)
can be nonzero for arbitrary large
n
.

One can notice, that the photon–number tomogram is also a PDF of the CV
\alpha
over the fixed DV
n
. It is normalized with respect to
\alpha
as
\int\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)d^{2}\alpha=\pi
.
Using this notion, an important connection of photon–number tomogram exists with the Husimi
Q
-function, that similarly to the tomographic symbol is non-negative and admits a dequantizer in the form of a POVM:
\hat{E}(\alpha)=1/\pi\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}
, where
\ket{\alpha}=\hat{D}(\alpha)\ket{0}
is a coherent state. For the fixed
n=0
the correspondence is
\mathsf{w}_{0}(-\alpha)=Q(\alpha)
,
where the state is displaced in the opposite direction compared to the coherent-state projection. The
Q
-function can be experimentally accessed via heterodyne detection, where the measurement outcome corresponds to an effective measurement of the POVM. Thus, heterodyne detection directly samples the
Q
-function over phase space, but not the corresponding random variable
\alpha
.
That is why
Q
-function must be classified as a QPDF.

The tomogram (
17
) can also be considered as a conditional PDF of photons, provided that the complex amplitude of the field
\alpha
is fixed. For example, the photon number tomogram
P(n)\equiv\langle n|\hat{\rho}|n\rangle=\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha=0)
gives the probability of
n
photons being in the state
\hat{\rho}
.

II.3
Continuous-Variable States in Infinite Hilbert Space

For the continuous position basis, the density matrix operator (
15
) can be represented by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{(1)}=\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(y,y^{\prime})\ket{y}\bra{y'}dydy^{\prime},

(20)

where
\rho(y,y^{\prime})=\langle y|\hat{\rho}_{(1)}|y^{\prime}\rangle
is an uncountable integral kernel.
The diagonal kernel elements are the positions PDF. Similar kernel can be introduced in the momentum basis.

Symplectic Tomograms

In quantum optics and CV quantum computing, a
mode
refers to a specific electromagnetic field configuration. When such modes are used to encode quantum information, they are often called
qumodes
. Qumodes are characterized by continuous degrees of freedom, typically the field quadratures. Unlike qubits, which are associated with discrete measurement outcomes, qumodes involve observables with continuous spectra.

We consider the quadrature operator
\hat{X}_{\mu,\nu}=\mu\hat{q}+\nu\hat{p}
,
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{R}
that is a linear combination of the coordinate
\hat{q}
and the momentum
\hat{p}
operator.
The parameters
\mu
and
\nu
specify the rotation and scaling of the reference frame in which the generalized quadrature operator
\hat{X}_{\mu,\nu}
is measured. This operator has a continuous spectrum and satisfies the eigenvalue equation

\hat{X}_{\mu,\nu}|x;\mu,\nu\rangle=x|x;\mu,\nu\rangle
,
where
x\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the measurement outcome of the quadrature observable in the rotated and scaled frame.

The
symplectic tomogram
of a quantum state (
20
) is given by
Mancini et al. 1995
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)=\tr{\hat{\rho}_{(1)} \hat{\mathcal{U}}(x, \mu, \nu)}\equiv\mathcal{R}[\hat{\rho}_{(1)}],

(21)

where
\hat{\mathcal{U}}(x,\mu,\nu)=\delta(x\hat{1}-\mu\hat{q}-\nu\hat{p})
is the dequantizer operator.
The delta function of an operator is defined via its Fourier representation as

\delta(\hat{a})=(2\pi)^{-1}\int e^{ik\hat{a}}dk
.
This transformation serves as a quantum analogue of the classical Radon transform. The inverse transformation, which reconstructs the density operator from its tomogram, is given by:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iiint\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)\hat{\mathcal{D}}(x,\mu,\nu)dx\,d\mu\,d\nu

(22)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{-1}[\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)],

where the corresponding quantizer operator in the symplectic-tomography framework reads as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}(x,\mu,\nu)=(2\pi)^{-1}\exp\left(ix\hat{1}-i\nu\hat{p}-i\mu\hat{q}\right).

(23)

Using the completeness relation

\int\ket{{x};{\mu},{\nu}}\bra{{x};{\mu},{\nu}}\,d{x}=\hat{1}
,
Eq. (
21
) can alternatively be expressed as
\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)=\bra{x;\mu,\nu}\hat{\rho}_{(1)}\ket{x;\mu,\nu}
, being the PDF of detecting the system in the state
\ket{x;\mu,\nu}\bra{x;\mu,\nu}
.
Symplectic tomogram satisfies all the properties of the PDF: non-negativity and normalization condition
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)dx=1
.
For parameter values
\mu=1
,
\nu=0
and
\mu=0
,
\nu=1
, the operator
\hat{X}_{\mu,\nu}
coincides with
\hat{q}
and
\hat{p}
, respectively. Then the tomogram is equal to the marginal PDFs corresponding to the coordinate or momentum random variable. Since the delta function is homogeneous,
\delta(\lambda y)=|\lambda|^{-1}\delta(y)
, where
\lambda
is a constant, the tomogram is homogeneous too

\mathcal{W}(\lambda x|\lambda\mu,\lambda\nu)=|\lambda|^{-1}\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)
.

For the pure state the tomogram can be expressed through the wave function
\Psi(y)
as follows:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2\pi\nu}\left|\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\Psi(y)\exp\left(i\frac{\mu y^{2}}{2\nu}-i\frac{yx}{\nu}\right)dy\right|^{2}.

(24)

A widely used special case is the
optical tomogram

w(x,\theta)\equiv\mathcal{W}(x,\cos\theta,\sin\theta)

where
\theta
is an angle at which the coordinate system in phase space is rotated.

Wigner Function

The Wigner function
\mathbb{W}(q,p)
is a QPDF with many applications in quantum optics and statistical physics. It is obtained using the Wigner-Weyl transform:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}(q,p)=\tr{\hat{\rho}_{(1)}\hat{\mathbb{U}}(q,p)}\equiv\mathcal{F}[\hat{\rho}_{(1)}],

(25)

where the dequantizer is

\displaystyle\!\!\!\hat{\mathbb{U}}(q,p)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\ket{q - \frac{\tau}{2}}\bra{q + \frac{\tau}{2}}e^{-i\tau p}d\tau.

(26)

The inverse transform is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{(1)}=\iint\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{W}(q,p)\hat{\mathbb{D}}(q,p)dqdp\equiv\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\mathbb{W}(q,p)],

(27)

where the quantizer is
\hat{\mathbb{D}}(q,p)=2\pi\hat{\mathbb{U}}(q,p)
.
It is more convenient to use an equivalent representation of the Wigner function through the complex-valued parameter
\alpha=(q+ip)/\sqrt{2}
.
Then, the dequantizer and quantizer of the Wigner function
\mathbb{W}(\alpha)
can be found through Glauber’s displacement-parity operator
Man’ko and Markovich 2020
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{U}}(\alpha)=(\pi)^{-1}\hat{D}(2\alpha)\hat{P},\quad\hat{\mathbb{D}}(\alpha)=2\hat{D}(2\alpha)\hat{P},

(28)

where
\hat{P}
is a parity operator.

Now we have a clear probabilistic picture of quantum mechanical DV and CV states, we will connect them, using the operator mapping below.

III
Jordan–Schwinger Map

In 1935, Jordan introduced a correspondence between irreducible representations of the symmetric group
\mathrm{S}_{n}
and the general linear group
\mathrm{GL}(n)
through bilinear operators obeying the commutation (bosonic) or anticommutation (fermionic) relations of
\mathfrak{gl}(n)

Jordan 1935
. His motivation was to clarify the relation between two formulations of many-particle quantum mechanics: the wave-function approach and the operator (second-quantization) approach, whose equivalence he had previously demonstrated at the matrix level
Jordan 1932
. Later, Schwinger proposed a unified oscillator-based method for angular momentum, reducing spin commutation relations to those of harmonic oscillators
Schwinger 1952
.

We consider a set of
n\times n
matrices
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
forming a representation of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with commutation relations
[A_{i},A_{j}]=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{ij}^{k}A_{k}
,
where
c_{ij}^{k}
are the structure constants of
\mathfrak{g}
. Denoting by
(A_{i})_{\alpha\beta}
, with
\alpha,\beta\in[1,n]
, the
(\alpha,\beta)
th entry of the matrix
A_{i}
, we define

\hat{A}_{i}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{n}\hat{a}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(A_{i})_{\alpha\beta}\hat{a}_{\beta}.

(29)

These operators also form a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
, preserving the commutation relations
[\hat{A}_{i},\hat{A}_{j}]=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{ij}^{k}\hat{A}_{k}
.
The homomorphism
A_{i}\mapsto\hat{A}_{i}
from matrices to operators on a bosonic Fock space is known as the JS map.

Let us concentrate on the angular momentum algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
.
Its fundamental representation is spanned by
S_{i}=\sigma_{i}/2
,
i\in\{x,y,z\}
,
with commutation relations
[S_{i},S_{j}]=i\varepsilon_{ijk}S_{k}
, where
\sigma_{i}
are the Pauli matrices.
The JS construction yields

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{x}=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{2}+\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}\right),\quad\hat{S}_{y}=\tfrac{i}{2}\!\left(\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}-\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{2}\right),

(30)

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{z}=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{1}-\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{2}\right).

The Casimir operator takes the form

\hat{S}^{2}=\hat{S}_{x}^{2}+\hat{S}_{y}^{2}+\hat{S}_{z}^{2}=((\hat{n}_{1}+\hat{n}_{2})/2)((\hat{n}_{1}+\hat{n}_{2})/2+1)
,
with
\hat{n}_{i}=\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}
.
Thus,
\hat{S}_{z}=(\hat{n}_{1}-\hat{n}_{2})/2
holds and the total occupation
\hat{n}=\hat{n}_{1}+\hat{n}_{2}
is conserved.
For fixed
n=2j
, the two-mode Fock space decomposes into invariant subspaces, carrying irreducible spin-
j
representations,
with magnetic quantum number
m=(n_{1}-n_{2})/2
(see Fig.
2
).
Without the constraint
n=2j
, the operators act on the entire Fock space, which contains a direct sum of all possible spin representations.

Figure 2
:

\hat{n}=\hat{n}_{1}+\hat{n}_{2}
, where

and
\hat{n}_{2}
denote the number of particles in each mode. Bold lines connect states with fixed
n=2j
, corresponding to spin-

representations. Dashed lines connect states with constant
\hat{S}_{z}=(\hat{n}_{1}-\hat{n}_{2})/2
, where

is the magnetic quantum number.

The normalized two-mode Fock states are

\ket{n_1,n_2}_{2}=\frac{(\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger})^{n_{1}}}{\sqrt{n_{1}!}}\,\frac{(\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger})^{n_{2}}}{\sqrt{n_{2}!}}\ket{0,0},\qquad n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N},

(31)

defined on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Fock}}=\operatorname{span}\{\ket{n_1,n_2}_{2}:n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
This space decomposes into subspaces of fixed total excitation:

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Fock}}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}S_{n},\qquad S_{n}=\operatorname{span}\{\ket{n_1,n_2}_{2}:n_{1}+n_{2}=n\},

(32)

with
\dim S_{n}=n+1
. Setting
n=2j
, the fixed total excitation subspace of the two-mode Fock space is

\!\!\!S_{2j}=\operatorname{span}\{\ket{j+m,\,j-m}_{2}\!\!:m=\!-j,-j+1,\ldots,j\},

(33)

where the Schwinger construction identifies these states with angular momentum eigenvectors
Schwinger 1952
,

\ket{j,m}=\frac{(\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger})^{j+m}}{\sqrt{(j+m)!}}\,\frac{(\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger})^{j-m}}{\sqrt{(j-m)!}}\ket{0,0}.

(34)

The parametrization
n=2j
,
m=(n_{1}-n_{2})/2

establishes the isomorphism

\ket{n_1,n_2}_{2}\;\longleftrightarrow\;\ket{j=\tfrac{n_1+n_2}{2},\,m=\tfrac{n_1-n_2}{2}},

(35)

between the Fock subspace
S_{n}
and the spin-
j
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{2j}
of dimension
2j+1
.
This mapping is precisely the JS map and it is one-to-one inside each fixed
n=2j
sector. We denote the unitary change of labels (isomorphism) as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{j}:S_{2j}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2j},\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{j}\ket{j+m,j-m}_{2}=\ket{j,m}.

(36)

This is not a physical operator connected to any observable, so we don’t put a hat on it.
This map is bijective inside each block
S_{2j}
. Using (
35
), we reparametrize
\hat{\rho}_{(2)}
, the two mode state analog of (
15
), with
n_{1}=j+m
,
n_{2}=j-m
,
n^{\prime}_{1}=j+m^{\prime}
,
n^{\prime}_{2}=j-m^{\prime}
. We call this rewritten state the
restricted two-mode Fock state
:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\hat{\rho}_{(2)}^{(2j)}\!=\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=-j}^{j}\!\!\!\!\!\!\rho^{(j)}_{m,m^{\prime}}\ket{j+m,j-m}_{2}\bra{j+m',j-m'}_{2},

(37)

where we denote the matrix elements as

\displaystyle\rho^{(j)}_{m,m^{\prime}}\equiv\bra{j+m,j-m}_{2}\hat{\rho}_{(2)}\ket{j+m',j-m'}_{2}.

(38)

In other words, we first project the two-mode density operator
\hat{\rho}_{(2)}
onto
S_{2j}
, getting (
37
). To treat it as a proper density matrix, one needs to renormalize it inside the block. Then we express this operator in the spin basis via (
36
) to get (
6
):

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{(j)}=\mathcal{E}_{j}\frac{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\rho}_{(2)}\hat{\Pi}_{2j}}{\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\rho}_{(2)}}}\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\dagger}\equiv\mathcal{JS}[\hat{\rho}_{(2)}].

(39)

where
\hat{\Pi}_{2j}=\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\ket{j+m,j-m}_{2}\bra{j+m,j-m}_{2}
is a projector onto
n=2j
subspace. The transformation is invertible in the following sense:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{(2)}^{(2j)}={\mathcal{E}}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}^{(j)}{\mathcal{E}}_{j}\equiv\mathcal{JS}^{-1}[\hat{\rho}^{(j)}].

(40)

Since we restrict to a single excitation number
n=2j
, this corresponds to selecting a
(2j+1)\times(2j+1)
block from the infinite-dimensional density matrix. This block is isomorphic to the spin-
j
density matrix in the finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{2j}
. The full two-mode Hilbert space decomposes as
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Fock}}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{2j}
,
so the reconstruction of
\hat{\rho}_{(2)}
requires all blocks
\{\hat{\rho}^{(j)}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
. From a single
j
block one recovers only the state restricted to that excitation sector, not the full infinite-dimensional operator. If the total number of excitations is fixed (i.e., no superpositions across different
n
), the corresponding block contains all relevant information: mode occupations as well as correlations and interference effects are encoded in the off-diagonal elements. In this case the two-mode density matrix is block-diagonal with only one nonzero block, while all elements
\rho_{n_{1},n_{2};n^{\prime}_{1},n^{\prime}_{2}}
vanish whenever
n_{1}+n_{2}\neq n_{1}^{\prime}+n_{2}^{\prime}=2j
. In contrast, a generic Fock state is a superposition of components with different total excitations. For example,
\frac{1}{2}\big(\ket{0,0}+\ket{1,1}\big)

contains contributions from
n=0
and
n=2
. Here
\ket{0,0}\in S_{0}
of dimension
1
, while
\ket{1,1}\in S_{2}
of dimension
3
. These subspaces are orthogonal, so no single
S_{n}
can accommodate both components. Thus, when the excitation number is not fixed, the full direct sum decomposition is required.

Extending the JS mapping to more than two modes is nontrivial, since spin quantum numbers no longer provide a complete labeling of states. An algorithmic construction of a bijective multimode correspondence was recently proposed in Ref.
Dubus et al. 2024
. In what follows, we focus on the connection between tomographic representations and restrict attention to the fundamental two-mode case.

IV
Connecting DV and CV States via the Tomographic JS Map

In this section we demonstrate the connection between the different tomograms of DV and CV states, using the JS map.

IV.1
Symplectic to Spin Tomogram Transformation

The transformation from two-mode bosonic system symplectic tomogram
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\equiv\mathcal{W}(x_{1},x_{2}|\mu_{1},\nu_{1};\mu_{2},\nu_{2})
to the spin-
j
tomogram
\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)
can be expressed as a composition of three maps:

\displaystyle\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)=\mathcal{S}\left[\mathcal{JS}\left[\mathcal{R}^{-1}\left[\mathcal{W}(\boldsymbol{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\right]\right]\right].

(41)

The first step reconstructs the two-mode density operator
\hat{\rho}_{(2)}
in the Fock basis from the symplectic tomogram, using the inverse Radon transform (
22
). The second step maps
\hat{\rho}_{(2)}
to the spin-
j
representation via the JS transformation (
39
). This defines an isomorphism between the
(2j+1)
-dimensional subspace of the two-mode Fock space with fixed total excitation number
n=2j
and the Hilbert space of a spin-
j
particle. Finally, the relation (
8
) between the spin tomogram
\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)
and the density matrix operator of the spin system is used.

As we mentioned before, the inversion of (
39
) is done throught the restricted state (
40
).
To this end, we introduce the
restricted symplectic tomogram
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(2j)}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}_{(2)}^{(2j)}\hat{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\right],

(42)

which can be considered as a projection of the full symplectic tomogram (
21
) to the
2j
excitation subspace.
While
\hat{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})
acts on the entire Fock space, the trace effectively restricts it to the
2j+1
dimensional sector.
For the states confined to
S_{2j}
the reverse transformation can be represented as a composition of three maps:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(2j)}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})=\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{JS}^{-1}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}[\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)]]].

(43)

We define
lifted spin dequantizer and quantizer
on Fock space

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{j}(m,\Omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{j}

(44)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{j}(m,\Omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathcal{D}}(m,\Omega)\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{j}.

(45)

They act as
\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)
,
\hat{\mathcal{D}}(m,\Omega)
on
S_{2j}
and zero otherwise.
Then the transition from the full symplectic tomogram to the spin tomogram and its partial inverted transformation to the restricted symplectic tomogram are

\displaystyle\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!d^{2}\mathbf{x}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}\;\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\,K^{(j)}_{\mathcal{W}\to\omega}(m,\Omega;\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!d^{2}\mathbf{x}\;\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mu,\nu)\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\nu})}},

(46)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(2j)}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\!\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)\,K^{(j)}_{\omega\to\mathcal{W}}(j,m,\Omega;\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\,d\Omega,

(47)

where the transformation kernels are the following

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\mathcal{W}\to\omega}(m,\Omega;\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})

\displaystyle\equiv\tr{\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\,\hat{\mathcal{U}}_j(m,\Omega)},

(48)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\omega\to\mathcal{W}}(m,\Omega;\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})

\displaystyle\equiv\tr{\hat{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\,\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j(m,\Omega)}.

(49)

The transition from the restricted symplectic tomogram to a spin tomogram can be deduced in the similar fashion, using the kernel (
48
). The explicit view of the latter kernels in terms of the Laguerre polynomials is provided in Appendix
B
.

IV.2
Spin to Photon-Number Tomogram Transformation

The transformation from the photon-number tomogram
\mathsf{w}_{\vec{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
,
\vec{n}=(n_{1},n_{2})
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
of the two-mode bosonic state
\hat{\rho}_{(2)}
to the spin-
j
tomogram
\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)
is

\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)=\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{JS}[\mathcal{P}^{-1}[\mathsf{w}_{\vec{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]]],

(50)

where
\mathcal{P}
denotes the map (
17
) from
\hat{\rho}_{(2)}
to the photon-number tomogram.
To write the reversed transformation we introduce the
restricted photon-number tomogram
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}_{\vec{n}}^{(2j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr{\hat{\rho}_{(2)}^{(2j)} \hat{\mathcal{U}}_{\vec n}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}.

(51)

Then the transformation from
\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)
to
\mathsf{w}^{(2j)}_{\vec{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
can be written as:

\mathsf{w}^{(2j)}_{\vec{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\mathcal{P}[\mathcal{JS}^{-1}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}[\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)]]].

(52)

This defines the connection of the spin and the photon-number tomograms:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\sum\limits_{\vec{n}=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{w}_{\vec{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})K^{(j)}_{\mathsf{w}\rightarrow\omega}(m,\Omega;\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\sum\limits_{\vec{n}=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{w}_{\vec{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec n}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}},

(53)

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}^{(2j)}_{\vec{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int d\Omega\sum\limits_{m=-j}^{j}\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)K^{(j)}_{\omega\rightarrow\mathsf{w}}(m,\Omega;\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),

where
\sum_{\vec{n}=0}^{2j}
denotes
\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{2j}
with condition
n_{1}+n_{2}=2j
.
The transition kernels are the following

\displaystyle K^{(j)}_{\mathsf{w}\rightarrow\omega}(m,\Omega;\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\equiv\tr{ \hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec n}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) \hat{\mathcal{U}}_{j}(m,\Omega) },

(54)

\displaystyle K^{(j)}_{\omega\rightarrow\mathsf{w}}(m,\Omega;\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\equiv\tr{ \hat{\mathcal{U}}_{\vec n}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j(m,\Omega)}.

(55)

The explicit view of the latter kernels is provided in Appendix
C
.

IV.3
Spin Tomogram to Wigner Function Transformation

The Wigner function
\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
of the two-mode bosonic state is defined by the dequantizer
\hat{\mathbb{U}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\equiv\hat{\mathbb{U}}(\alpha_{1})\otimes\hat{\mathbb{U}}(\alpha_{2})
. The transformation from Wigner functions to spin tomogram is:

\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)=\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{JS}[\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]]].

(56)

We introduce the restricted Wigner function as

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{(2j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}_{(2)}^{(2j)}\hat{\mathbb{U}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}))\right],

(57)

and the inverse transformation is

\mathbb{W}^{(2j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{JS}^{-1}[\mathcal{S}^{-1}[\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)]]].

(58)

Then the connection between the spin tomogram and the Wigner function is

\displaystyle\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})K^{(j)}_{\mathbb{W}\rightarrow\omega}(m,\Omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha})}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mathbb{W}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j} \hat{\mathbb{D}}( \boldsymbol{\alpha})}},

(59)

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{(2j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\Omega\sum\limits_{m=-j}^{j}\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)K^{(j)}_{\omega\rightarrow\mathbb{W}}(m,\Omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha}),

where the kernels are

\displaystyle K^{(j)}_{\mathbb{W}\rightarrow\omega}(m,\Omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\equiv\tr{ \hat{\mathbb{D}}( \boldsymbol{\alpha}) \hat{\mathcal{U}}_j(m,\Omega))},

(60)

\displaystyle K^{(j)}_{\omega\rightarrow\mathbb{W}}(m,\Omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\equiv\tr{ \hat{\mathbb{U}}( \boldsymbol{\alpha}) \hat{\mathcal{D}}_j(m,\Omega))}.

(61)

The explicit view of the latter kernels is provided in Appendix
D
.

IV.4
Photon-Number to Symplectic Tomogram Transformation

The connection between the symplectic
\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)
and photon-number
\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)
tomograms is

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)=\mathcal{P}[\mathcal{R}^{-1}[\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)]]].

(62)

This forward transformation was previously reported in the literature
Man’ko and Man’ko 2010
, whereas the corresponding inverse transformation had not been derived. The general procedure for obtaining the inverse transform is the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)=\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{P}^{-1}[\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)]]].

(63)

In contrast to the previous transformations, the one derived here is one-to-one, as no restriction on the Hilbert space is imposed. The integral form of the latter transformation can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)=\int\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)K_{\mathcal{W}\to\mathsf{w}}(x,\mu,\nu,n,\alpha)dXd\mu d\nu,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int\mathsf{w}_{n}(\alpha)K_{\mathsf{w}\to\mathcal{W}}(x,\mu,\nu,n,\alpha)d^{2}\alpha,

(64)

where the kernels are

\displaystyle K_{\mathcal{W}\to\mathsf{w}}(x,\mu,\nu,n,\alpha)

\displaystyle\equiv\tr{\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{ n}(\alpha) \hat{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}, \mu, \nu)},

(65)

\displaystyle K_{\mathsf{w}\to\mathcal{W}}(x,\mu,\nu,n,\alpha)

\displaystyle\equiv\tr{\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{ n}(\alpha) \hat{\mathcal{U}}(x, \mu, \nu)}.

(66)

The explicit view of these kernels is provided in Appendix
E
. Using the fact that the Wigner function is a Radon transform of the symplectic tomogram, the connection of the photon-number tomogram to the Wigner function is straightforward.

VI
Discussion and Conclusion

We have established a direct connection between tomographic representations of DV and CV quantum systems by employing the Jordan–Schwinger map along with the quantizer-dequantizer formalism.
This provides a practical framework for transforming measurement data across different physical platforms without the intermediate step of full density matrix reconstruction, which is often numerically expensive and prone to error accumulation. For example, one can start directly from the measured spin tomogram obtained in DV setup and, without reconstructing the density matrix, immediately obtain the tomogram of the corresponding CV state. The resulting data can then be used for state verification, error correction through the distance between the theoretical and reconstructed tomograms, recovery of trace characteristics of the state, and many other tasks.

Our analysis focuses on a fixed-excitation subspace (
n=2j
) , which is relevant for a wide class of systems where superpositions involving different total particle numbers are out of the primary interest. Within this regime, our approach enables a transparent mapping between spin and bosonic architectures, thereby facilitating hybrid processing protocols that combine the advantages of discrete and continuous-variables.

Looking ahead, extending the present method to multimode scenarios will enable the comparison and interfacing of more complex CV–DV architectures, including quantum memories and photonic communication links, on a fully tomographic footing. A detailed analysis of the estimation errors arising at each step of our method, combined with the KQSE approach, will be the subject of future research.

Dequantizer and Quantizer Derivation for the Spin Tomogram

Since the dequantizer operator (
9
) is the rotated projector, it can be written as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\!\!\!\!\!\!D_{m,m_{2}}^{(j)*}(\Omega)D_{m,m_{1}}^{(j)}(\Omega)|j,m_{2}\rangle\langle j,m_{1}|,

(70)

where
D^{(j)}_{m,m^{\prime}}(\Omega)=e^{-im\alpha}d^{(j)}_{mm^{\prime}}(\beta)e^{-im^{\prime}\gamma}
is the Wigner D-matrix
Edmonds 1957
.
For
m+m^{\prime}\geq 0
the
d
-matrix has the following form

\displaystyle d^{(j)}_{m,m^{\prime}}(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{(j+m^{\prime})!\,(j-m^{\prime})!}{(j+m)!\,(j-m)!}}\Bigl(-\sin\tfrac{\beta}{2}\Bigr)^{m-m^{\prime}}

(71)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\Bigl(\cos\tfrac{\beta}{2}\Bigr)^{m+m^{\prime}}P_{\,j-m}^{\,(m-m^{\prime},\,m+m^{\prime})}\!\bigl(\cos\beta\bigr),

where
P^{(a,b)}_{n}(x)
are Jacobi polynomials. Similar expression exists for the case of
m+m^{\prime}<0
.
The matrix elements of (
70
) are

\displaystyle U_{m_{2}m_{1}}(m,\Omega)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\bra{j,m_2}\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)\ket{j,m_1}

(72)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{m,m_{2}}^{(j)*}(\Omega)D_{m,m_{1}}^{(j)}(\Omega).

The Wigner
D
-matrix elements
form a set of orthogonal functions of the Euler angles:

\int d\Omega D^{(j)*}_{m,k^{\prime}}(\Omega)D^{(j)}_{m,k}(\Omega)=\frac{8\pi^{2}}{2j+1}\delta_{k^{\prime}k}.

(73)

In practice, while any operator on a spin-
j
Hilbert space can be expanded in the basis
\{\ket{j,m}\bra{j,m'}\}
, that representation is complicated when handling rotations. A more convenient choice is the basis of irreducible tensor operators (ITOs)
\hat{T}^{(j)}_{LN}
, which carry definite rank
L\in[0,2j]
and projection
N\in[-L,L]
:

\hat{T}^{(j)}_{LN}=\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=-j}^{j}\!\!\!\!\!\!(-1)^{j-m^{\prime}}\,\langle j,m;\,j,-m^{\prime}\,|\,L,N\rangle\;\ket{j,m}\bra{j,m'}\,.

(74)

The set
\{\hat{T}^{(j)}_{LN}\}
is complete and orthogonal under the trace inner product in the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
irreducible representation, satisfying

\displaystyle\tr{\hat{T}^{(j)\dagger}_{L'N'}\hat{T}^{(j)}_{LN}}=\delta_{LL^{\prime}}\delta_{NN^{\prime}}.

(75)

In this basis the expansion is

\ket{j,m}\!\bra{j,m'}\!=\!\sum_{L=0}^{2j}\!\sum_{N=-L}^{L}\!\!\!(-1)^{\,j-m^{\prime}}\!\!\langle j,m;\,j,-m^{\prime}\,|\,L,N\rangle\;\hat{T}^{(j)}_{LN},

(76)

where
\langle j,m;\,j,-m^{\prime}\,|\,L,N\rangle
is the Clebsch–Gordan coefficient.
Under rotation
\hat{R}(\Omega)
each
\hat{T}^{(j)}_{LN}
is carried into a linear combination of the same family of operators, mixing its original component
N
into new components
M\in[-L,L]
:

\displaystyle R(\Omega)\,\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L\,N}\,R^{\dagger}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{M=-L}^{L}D^{(L)}_{M,N}(\Omega)\;\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L\,M},

(77)

where the
D_{M,N}^{(L)}
are the Wigner
D
-matrices of spin
L
.
Taking
m=m^{\prime}
in (
76
), we get

\ket{j,m}\!\bra{j,m}\!=\!\sum_{L=0}^{2j}(-1)^{\,j-m}\!\langle j,m;\,j,-m\,|\,L,0\rangle\;\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L0},

(78)

where the Clebsch-Gordan coefficient enforces the selection rule on total projection

\displaystyle\bigl\langle j,m;\,j,-m\bigm|L,N\bigr\rangle\neq 0,

(79)

so the only surviving term is
N=0
.
Then the expansion of the dequantizer (
9
) in the irreducible-tensor basis is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)=\sum\limits_{L=0}^{2j}\sum\limits_{M=-L}^{L}C_{L,M}(m,\Omega)\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L\,M},

(80)

where we used the notation

\displaystyle C_{L,M}(m,\Omega)=(-1)^{j-m+M}\!\bigl\langle j,m;\,j,-m\!\bigm|\!L,0\bigr\rangle D^{(L)}_{0,-M}(\Omega).

Next, we seek the quantizer as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}(m,\Omega)=\sum\limits_{L=0}^{2j}\sum\limits_{M=-L}^{L}c_{L}C_{L,M}(m,\Omega)\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L\,M},

(81)

where
c_{L}
is the unknown coefficient. We expand

\displaystyle\tr{\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)\hat{\mathcal{D}}(m',\Omega')}

(82)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{L,L^{\prime}=0}^{2j}\sum\limits_{M=-L}^{L}\sum\limits_{M^{\prime}=-L^{\prime}}^{L^{\prime}}(-1)^{2j-m-m^{\prime}+M+M^{\prime}}c_{L^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\bigl\langle j,m;\,j,-m\!\bigm|\!L,0\bigr\rangle\bigl\langle j,m^{\prime};\,j,-m^{\prime}\!\bigm|\!L^{\prime},0\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle D^{(L)}_{0,-M}(\Omega)D^{(L^{\prime})}_{0,-M^{\prime}}(\Omega^{\prime})\tr{\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L\,M}\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L'\,M'}},

such that the condition (
4
) in the form

\displaystyle\tr{\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)\hat{\mathcal{D}}(m',\Omega')}=\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}),

(83)

holds. We use (
75
) with
(\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L,M})^{\dagger}=(-1)^{M}\hat{T}^{(j)}_{L,-M}
and
the Haar orthogonality to get
c_{L}=(2L+1)/8\pi^{2}
.

Appendix B
Transition Kernels for the Integral Transformation Between Symplectic and Spin Tomograms

The transition from a symplectic tomogram to a spin tomogram is written as:

\displaystyle\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)=\frac{\tr{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_j \hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\rho}_{(2)}\hat{\Pi}_{2j} \hat{\mathcal{E}}_j^{\dagger} \hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega))}}{\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\rho}_{(2)}}}.

(84)

We use the relation between the density matrix operator of the two-mode state with the symplectic tomogram to write:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\tr{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_j \hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\rho}_{(2)}\hat{\Pi}_{2j} \hat{\mathcal{E}}_j^{\dagger} \hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\!\!d^{2}\mathbf{x}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}\mathcal{W}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\nu})\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_j(m,\Omega)},

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\rho}_{(2)}}\!=\!\!\!\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\!\!d^{2}\mathbf{x}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}\mathcal{W}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\nu})},

where we use the cyclicity of trace and the lifted spin dequantizer (
44
) on Fock space,
that acts as
\hat{\mathcal{U}}(m,\Omega)
on
S_{2j}
and zero otherwise.

Let us take a generic two-mode bosonic Fock state
\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi_{2j}}+\ket{\psi_{\perp}}
, where
\ket{\psi_{2j}}\in\mathcal{S}_{2j}
and
\ket{\psi_{\perp}}
lies in all other sectors. Then

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{j}(m,\Omega)\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\ket{\psi}=\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{j}(m,\Omega)\ket{\psi_{2j}}.

(85)

Since
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{j}(m,\Omega)
acts nontrivially only inside the two-mode subspace with total number
2j
, we can write

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{j}(m,\Omega)\ket{\psi_{2j}}=\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{j}(m,\Omega)\ket{\psi_{2j}}.

(86)

Finally, one can achieve (
46
), using the transformation kernel
K^{(j)}_{\mathcal{W}\to\omega}(m,\Omega;\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})
given by (
48
). The transition from the restricted symplectic tomogram to a spin tomogram is derived in the similar way:

\displaystyle\!\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)\!=\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\!\!\!\!\!d^{2}\mathbf{x}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}d^{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}\mathcal{W}^{(2j)}\!(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})K^{(j)}_{\mathcal{W}\to\omega}\!(m,\Omega;\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}).

The transformation kernel is given by (
48
). Similarly, one can deduce the reversed transformation (
47
) from
\omega_{j}(m,\Omega)
to the restricted tomogram
\mathcal{W}^{(2j)}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})
, using the kernel (
49
).

Inserting the resolution of identity on
S_{2j}
, one can write

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{j}(m,\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\!\!\!\!\!\ket{j+m_2,j-m_2}_{2}\bra{j+m_1,j-m_1}_{2}

(87)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle U_{m_{2}m_{1}}(m,\Omega),

where the matrix elements
U_{m_{2}m_{1}}(m,\Omega)
can be written in terms of the Wigner D-matrix elements as in (
72
).
The symplectic quantizer (
23
) corresponding to the tomogram of the two-mode Fock state can be written as a direct product of the Weyl displacement
\hat{D}(\xi)
operators:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})=(2\pi)^{-2}e^{i(x_{1}+x_{2})}\hat{D}(\xi_{1})\otimes\hat{D}(\xi_{2}),

(88)

where we use the notation
\xi_{k}=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(\mu_{k}+i\nu_{k})
,
k=1,2
.
Since the operator factorizes over two modes, the matrix element factorizes too, using a standard property of tensor product:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!D_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\equiv(2\pi)^{-2}e^{i(x_{1}+x_{2})}

(89)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\bra{j+m_1}\hat{D}(\xi_{1})\ket{j+m_2}\bra{j-m_1}\hat{D}(\xi_{2})\ket{j-m_2}.

The matrix elements of
\hat{D}(\xi)
in the Fock state basis
\{|n\rangle\}
are given by
Cahill and Glauber 1969b
:

\!\!\!\!\!\!\langle n^{\prime}|\hat{D}(\xi)|n\rangle=\left(n!/n^{\prime}!\right)^{1/2}\xi^{n^{\prime}-n}e^{-|\xi|^{2}/2}L_{n}^{(n^{\prime}-n)}(|\xi|^{2}),

(90)

for
n^{\prime}\geq n
,
where
L_{q}^{(p)}(z)
is an associated (generalized) Laguerre polynomial. For the case
n^{\prime}<n
, one can use the relation
\langle n^{\prime}|D(\xi)|n\rangle=(\langle n|D(-\xi)|n^{\prime}\rangle)^{*}
. Thereby, we can write an explicit formula for the kernel (
48
):

\displaystyle K^{(j)}_{\mathcal{W}\to\omega}(m,\Omega;\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\!\!\!\!U_{m_{2}m_{1}}(m,\Omega)D_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{i(x_{1}+x_{2})}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}(|\xi_{1}|^{2}+|\xi_{2}|^{2})}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}D_{m,m_{2}}^{(j)*}(\Omega)D_{m,m_{1}}^{(j)}(\Omega)\mathcal{M}^{(j)}_{m_{1},m_{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}),

where we used the notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(j)}_{m_{1},m_{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})

(92)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{(j+\min\{m_{1},m_{2}\})!\,(j-\max\{m_{1},m_{2}\})!}{(j+\max\{m_{1},m_{2}\})!\,(j-\min\{m_{1},m_{2}\})!}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle(\xi_{1}^{*}\xi_{2})^{\max(0,m_{2}-m_{1})}\,(\xi_{1}\xi_{2}^{*})^{\max(0,m_{1}-m_{2})}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle L_{j+\min\{m_{1},m_{2}\}}^{(|m_{1}-m_{2}|)}(|\xi_{1}|^{2})\,L_{j-\max\{m_{1},m_{2}\}}^{(|m_{1}-m_{2}|)}(|\xi_{2}|^{2}).

The symplectic dequantizer can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk_{1}dk_{2}e^{i(k_{1}x_{1}\hat{1}_{1}+k_{2}x_{2}\hat{1}_{2})}

(93)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\hat{D}(k_{1}\xi_{1})\otimes\hat{D}(k_{2}\xi_{2}),

Then the kernel (
49
) can be written as follows

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\omega\to\mathcal{W}}(m,\Omega;\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk_{1}dk_{2}e^{i(k_{1}x_{1}+k_{2}x_{2})}

(94)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}(|k_{1}\xi_{1}|^{2}+|k_{2}\xi_{2}|^{2})}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\mathcal{D}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(m,\Omega)\mathcal{M}^{(j)}_{m_{1},m_{2}}(k_{1}\xi_{1},k_{2}\xi_{2}),

where the quantizer matrix element
\mathcal{D}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(m,\Omega)
can be found from (
13
) to be equal to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(m,\Omega)=\sum_{L}\frac{2L+1}{8\pi^{2}}\sum_{M}C_{L,M}(m,\Omega)

(95)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle(-1)^{j-m_{2}}\langle j,m_{1};j,-m_{2}|L,M\rangle.

To calculate these integrals, we use the series expansion of the associated Laguerre polynomial:

\displaystyle L_{q}^{(p)}(z)=\sum_{l=0}^{q}(-1)^{l}\binom{q+p}{q-l}\frac{z^{l}}{l!},

(96)

and the known integral

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{n}e^{-px^{2}-qx}dx=\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)^{n}\sqrt{\pi}p^{-(n+1)/2}e^{\frac{q^{2}}{4p}}H_{n}\left(\frac{iq}{2\sqrt{p}}\right).

We introduce the notations
\Delta=|m_{1}-m_{2}|
,
n_{1}=j+\min{\{m_{1},m_{2}\}}
,

n_{2}=j-\max{\{m_{1},m_{2}\}}
, to deduce:

\displaystyle\int dk_{r}e^{ik_{r}x_{r}-k_{r}^{2}\frac{|\xi_{r}|^{2}}{2}}k_{r}^{\max(0,m_{2}-m_{1})}

(97)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle k_{r}^{\max(0,m_{1}-m_{2})}L_{n_{r}}^{(\Delta)}(k_{r}^{2}|\xi_{r}|^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{|\xi_{r}|}\,\exp\!\Big[-\tfrac{x_{r}^{2}}{2|\xi_{r}|^{2}}\Big]\,\Big(\tfrac{i}{\sqrt{2}|\xi_{r}|}\Big)^{\Delta}\,

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{s_{r}=0}^{n_{r}}\binom{n_{r}+\Delta}{\,n_{r}-s_{r}\,}\,\frac{1}{s_{r}!2^{s_{r}}}\,H_{\Delta+2s_{r}}\!\Big(\tfrac{x_{r}}{\sqrt{2}|\xi_{r}|}\Big)\right],\quad r=1,2.

Using this result, we can rewrite (
94
) into the final form:

\displaystyle K^{(j)}_{\omega\to\mathcal{W}}(m,\Omega;\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\frac{1}{\pi}\exp\!\Big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{x_{1}^{2}}{|\xi_{1}|^{2}}+\tfrac{x_{2}^{2}}{|\xi_{2}|^{2}}\right)\Big]\!\!\!\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2|\xi_{1}\xi_{2}|}\Big)^{\Delta+1}\mathcal{D}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(m,\Omega)\,

(98)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{s_{1}=0}^{n_{1}}\binom{n_{1}+\Delta}{\,n_{1}-s_{1}\,}\,\frac{1}{s_{1}!2^{s_{1}}}\,H_{\Delta+2s_{1}}\!\Big(\tfrac{\,x_{1}}{\sqrt{2}|\xi_{1}|}\Big)\right]\,\left[\sum_{s_{2}=0}^{n_{2}}\binom{n_{2}+\Delta}{\,n_{2}-s_{2}\,}\,\frac{1}{s_{2}!2^{s_{2}}}\,H_{\Delta+2s_{2}}\!\Big(\tfrac{\,x_{2}}{\sqrt{2}|\xi_{2}|}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{(j+\min\{m_{1},m_{2}\})!\,(j-\max\{m_{1},m_{2}\})!}{(j+\max\{m_{1},m_{2}\})!\,(j-\min\{m_{1},m_{2}\})!}}(\xi_{1}^{*}\xi_{2})^{\max(0,m_{2}-m_{1})}\,(\xi_{1}\xi_{2}^{*})^{\max(0,m_{1}-m_{2})}.

Since (
88
) holds, we use (
89
) to write

\displaystyle\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\hat{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\nu})}=\sum\limits_{m=-j}^{j}D_{mm}^{(j)}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})

(99)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{i(x_{1}+x_{2})}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}(|\xi_{1}|^{2}+|\xi_{2}|^{2})}\sum\limits_{m=-j}^{j}L_{j+m}(|\xi_{1}|^{2})L_{j-m}(|\xi_{2}|^{2}).

Appendix C
Transition Kernels for the Integral Transformation Between Spin and Photon-Number Tomograms

With resolution (
87
) kernels (
54
) and (
55
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\mathsf{w}\rightarrow\omega}(m,\Omega;\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!D_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})U_{m_{2}m_{1}}(m,\Omega),

(100)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\omega\rightarrow\mathsf{w}}(m,\Omega;\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!U_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathcal{D}_{m_{2}m_{1}}(m,\Omega),

where we denote

\displaystyle D_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\!\equiv\!\bra{j+m_1,j-m_1}_{2}\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\!\ket{j+m_2,j-m_2}_{2},

\displaystyle U_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{(j)}(\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\!\equiv\!\bra{j+m_1,j-m_1}_{2}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{\vec{n}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\!\ket{j+m_2,j-m_2}_{2}.

Now we obtain an explicit form of the transition kernels from spin to photon number tomograms and vice versa, using the definition the photon-number tomogram (
17
). The photon-number dequantizer can be written as:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!U_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=M(j+m_{1},n_{1};\alpha_{1})M(n_{1},j+m_{2};-\alpha_{1})

(101)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j-m_{1},n_{2};\alpha_{2})M(n_{2},j-m_{2};-\alpha_{2}),

where we denote
M(a,b;\beta)=\bra{a}\hat{D}(\beta)\,|b\rangle
. We use (
90
) to deduce:

M(a,b;\beta)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\dfrac{b!}{a!}}\beta^{a-b}e^{-|\beta|^{2}/2}L_{b}^{(a-b)}(|\beta|^{2}),&a\geq b\\
\sqrt{\dfrac{a!}{b!}}(-\beta^{*})^{b-a}e^{-|\beta|^{2}/2}L_{a}^{(b-a)}(|\beta|^{2}),&a<b.\end{cases}

(102)

The expression for the quantizer (
19
) is not so straightforward. It is worth mentioning that a fraction with operators to the power can always be considered as an operator exponential:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!g(s)^{\left[(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\alpha^{*})(\hat{a}+\alpha)-n\right]}=e^{\left[(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\alpha^{*})(\hat{a}+\alpha)-n\right]\ln{g(s) }},

(103)

where
g(s)\equiv(s-1)/(s+1)
.
Then we use the unitarily equivalence of operators

\displaystyle f\left((\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\alpha^{*})(\hat{a}+\alpha)\right)=\hat{D}(-\alpha)f(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a})\hat{D}^{\dagger}(-\alpha),

(104)

where we take as the function
f(x)=g(s)^{x-n}
:

\displaystyle\!\!\!g(s)^{\left[(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\alpha^{*})(\hat{a}+\alpha)-n\right]}=\hat{D}(-\alpha)g(s)^{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-n}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(-\alpha).

(105)

Operator
g(s)^{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-n}
in the Fock basis is:

\displaystyle g(s)^{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-n}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}g(s)^{l-n}|l\rangle\langle l|.

(106)

Then the quantizer (
19
) for one mode can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}(\alpha)=\!\frac{4\hat{D}(-\alpha)}{\pi(1-s^{2})}\!\!\left[\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}g(s)^{l-n}|l\rangle\langle l|\right]\!\hat{D}^{\dagger}(-\alpha).

(107)

Using this, the matrix element of the two-mode quantizer has the form:

\displaystyle D_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{16}{\pi^{2}(1-s^{2})^{2}}\!\!\!\!\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}=0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!g(s)^{l_{1}-n_{1}+l_{2}-n_{2}}

(108)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j+m_{1},l_{1},-\alpha_{1})M(l_{1},j+m_{2},\alpha_{1})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j-m_{1},l_{2},-\alpha_{2})M(l_{2},j-m_{2},\alpha_{2}).

Thereby, we can immediately write the transition kernel from the spin tomograms to the photon-number tomogram as:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\omega\rightarrow\mathsf{w}}(m,\Omega;\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathcal{D}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(m,\Omega)

(109)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j+m_{1},n_{1};\alpha_{1})M(n_{1},j+m_{2};-\alpha_{1})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j-m_{1},n_{2};\alpha_{2})M(n_{2},j-m_{2};-\alpha_{2}),

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\mathsf{w}\rightarrow\omega}(m,\Omega;\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\!\frac{16}{\pi^{2}(1-s^{2})^{2}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!D_{m,m_{2}}^{(j)*}(\Omega)D_{m,m_{1}}^{(j)}\!(\Omega)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}=0}^{\infty}g(s)^{l_{1}-n_{1}+l_{2}-n_{2}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j+m_{1},l_{1};-\alpha_{1})M(l_{1},j+m_{2};\alpha_{1})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j-m_{1},l_{2};-\alpha_{2})M(l_{2},j-m_{2};\alpha_{2}).

We use (
107
), to calculate the denominator of (
53
):

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\,\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec n}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}=\sum_{m=-j}^{j}D_{mm}^{(j)}(\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})

(110)

The matrix diagonal element of the two-mode quantizer has the form:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!D_{mm}^{(j)}(\vec{n},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{16}{\pi^{2}(1-s^{2})^{2}}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}=0}^{\infty}g(s)^{l_{1}-n_{1}+l_{2}-n_{2}}

(111)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j+m,l_{1};-\alpha_{1})M(l_{1},j+m;\alpha_{1})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j-m,l_{2};-\alpha_{2})M(l_{2},j-m;\alpha_{2}).

To simplify, we can use the property
M(j+m,l_{1};-\alpha_{1})M(l_{1},j+m;\alpha_{1})=|M(j+m,l_{1};-\alpha_{1})|^{2}

that follows from the fact that
\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)=\hat{D}(-\alpha)
. In addition, the generating function for the squares of generalized Laguerre polynomials plays a key role:

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\!\!z^{m}|\langle n|\hat{D}(\alpha)|m\rangle|^{2}\!=\!z^{n}e^{-(1-z)|\alpha|^{2}}\!\!L_{n}\!\!\left(\!-\frac{(1-z)^{2}|\alpha|^{2}}{z}\right).

Then the explicit form of the trace is the following:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\,\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec n}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}=\frac{16}{\pi^{2}(1-s^{2})^{2}}g(s)^{|2j-n_{1}-n_{2}}

(112)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\,e^{-(1-g(s))(|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2})}\sum_{m=-j}^{j}L_{j+m}(-\frac{(1-g(s))^{2}}{g(s)}\,|\alpha_{1}|^{2})

\displaystyle L_{j-m}(-\frac{(1-g(s))^{2}}{g(s)}\,|\alpha_{2}|^{2}).

Collecting these results, one gets (

Appendix D
Transition Kernels for the Integral Transformation Between Spin Tomogram and Wigner Function

Using (
87
), the transition kernels (
60
) and (
61
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\mathbb{W}\rightarrow\omega}(m,\Omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\mathbb{D}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})U_{m_{2}m_{1}}(m,\Omega),

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\omega\rightarrow\mathbb{W}}(m,\Omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\!\!\!\!\mathbb{U}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathcal{D}_{m_{2}m_{1}}(m,\Omega),

(113)

where we use the notations

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\equiv\bra{j+m_1,j-m_1}_{2}\hat{\mathbb{D}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\ket{j+m_2,j-m_2}_{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{U}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\equiv\bra{j+m_1,j-m_1}_{2}\hat{\mathbb{U}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\ket{j+m_2,j-m_2}_{2}.

Let us derive the explicit form of the transition kernels linking the spin tomogram and the Wigner function. Using Eq. (
28
), the expression for the matrix elements of the Wigner quantizer can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbb{D}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\ =4\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\bra{j+m_1}\hat{D}(2\alpha_{1})\ket{n_1}

(114)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\bra{n_1}\hat{P}\ket{j+m_2}\bra{j-m_1}\hat{D}(2\alpha_{2})\ket{n_2}\bra{n_2}\hat{P}\ket{j-m_2}.

The formula for the matrix elements of the dequantizer is obtained in a similar way as for the quantizer:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbb{U}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\ =\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}\bra{j+m_1}\hat{D}(2\alpha_{1})\ket{n_1}

(115)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\bra{n_1}\hat{P}\ket{j+m_2}\bra{j-m_1}\hat{D}(2\alpha_{2})\ket{n_2}\bra{n_2}\hat{P}\ket{j-m_2}.

We use the fact that the matrix element of the parity operator is given by
\bra{m}\hat{P}\ket{n}=(-1)^{n}\delta_{n,m}
.
Using this relation, the transition kernel from the Wigner function to the spin tomogram can be written as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\mathbb{W}\rightarrow\omega}(j,m,\Omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha})=4\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}D_{m,m_{2}}^{(j)*}(\Omega)D_{m,m_{1}}^{(j)}(\Omega)

(116)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\!\!\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\delta_{j+m_{2},n_{1}}\delta_{j-m_{2},n_{2}}M(j+m_{1},n_{1};2\alpha_{1})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j-m_{1},n_{2};2\alpha_{2})=4\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}D_{m,m_{2}}^{(j)*}(\Omega)D_{m,m_{1}}^{(j)}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j+m_{1},j+m_{2};2\alpha_{1})M(j-m_{1},j-m_{2};2\alpha_{2}),

where the Kronecker delta functions have been used to eliminate the summation over
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
. The inverse transformation kernel is obtained in an analogous way:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!K^{(j)}_{\omega\rightarrow\mathbb{W}}(j,m,\Omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum\limits_{m_{1},m_{2}=-j}^{j}\mathcal{D}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{(j)}(m,\Omega)

(117)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle M(j+m_{1},j+m_{2};2\alpha_{1})M(j-m_{1},j-m_{2};2\alpha_{2}).

Next, we use Eq. (
114
) to calculate the denominator of Eq. (
59
):

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j}\,\hat{\mathbb{D}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}=\sum\limits_{m=-j}^{j}\mathbb{D}_{mm}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=

(118)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4e^{-2(|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2})}\sum_{k=0}^{2j}L_{k}(4|\alpha_{1}|^{2})L_{2j-k}(4|\alpha_{2}|^{2}).

Applying the identity for Laguerre polynomials,

\sum_{k=0}^{n}L_{k}(x)L_{n-k}(y)=L_{n}^{(1)}(x+y)
,
the result simplifies to

\displaystyle\tr{\hat{\Pi}_{2j} \hat{\mathbb{D}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}=4e^{-2(|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2})}L_{2j}^{(1)}\left(4(|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\alpha_{2}|^{2})\right).

Collecting these results, the Eq. (
59
) can be written in terms of the Laguerre polynomials.

Appendix E
Transition Kernels for the Integral
Transformation Between Symplectic and Photon-Number Tomograms

The transition kernels (
65
) and (
66
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle K_{\mathcal{W}\to\mathsf{w}}(X,\mu,\nu,n,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\langle n|\hat{D}(\alpha)e^{i(X-\mu q-\nu p)}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)|n\rangle,

\displaystyle K_{\mathsf{w}\to\mathcal{W}}(X,\mu,\nu,n,\alpha)=

\displaystyle=\tr{\frac{4}{\pi(1-s^2)} \left( \frac{s-1}{s+1} \right)^{[ (\hat{a}^\dagger+ \alpha^*)(\hat{a} + \alpha) - n ]}\!\!\!\! \delta(x \hat{1} - \mu\hat{q} - \nu\hat{p})},

that establishes a direct connection between the Wigner function and the photon-number tomogram. The explicit form of the symplectic-to–photon-number transition kernel can be immediately deduced

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!K_{\mathcal{W}\to\mathsf{w}}(X,\mu,\nu,n,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-\frac{\nu^{2}+\mu^{2}}{4}}

(119)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle e^{iX+\frac{\nu-i\mu}{\sqrt{2}}\alpha^{*}-\frac{\nu+i\mu}{\sqrt{2}}\alpha}L_{n}\left(\frac{\nu^{2}+\mu^{2}}{2}\right).

Using the integral representation of the delta function, the symplectic dequantizer can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}(x,\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\,e^{ikx}\hat{D}\left(\frac{k(\nu-i\mu)}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

(120)

Substituting this into the previous expression, the photon-number to symplectic kernel becomes

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!K_{\mathsf{w}\to\mathcal{W}}(X,\mu,\nu,n,\alpha)=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}(1-s^{2})}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\,e^{ikx}

(121)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\tr{\left( \frac{s-1}{s+1} \right)^{(\hat{a}^\dagger+ \alpha^{*})(\hat{a} + \alpha) - n} \hat{D}\left(\frac{k (\nu- i \mu)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}.

To evaluate the trace, we use the generating function identity for Laguerre polynomials,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)^{m}L_{m}(z)=\frac{s+1}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{s-1}{2}z\right).

(122)

This allows us to simplify the trace as follows

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\tr{\left( \frac{s-1}{s+1} \right)^{(\hat{a}^\dagger+ \alpha^{*})(\hat{a} + \alpha) - n} \hat{D}\left(\frac{k (\nu- i \mu)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}=

(123)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)^{m-n}e^{-ik\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(\nu\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)+\mu\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))}e^{-k^{2}\frac{\nu^{2}+\mu^{2}}{4}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle L_{m}\left(k^{2}\frac{\nu^{2}+\mu^{2}}{2}\right)=\left(\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)^{-n}e^{-ik\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(\nu\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)+\mu\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle e^{-sk^{2}\frac{\nu^{2}+\mu^{2}}{4}}.

Performing the integral over
k
and using the well-known identity

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\,e^{iak}e^{-bk^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{b}}\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{4b}\right),

(124)

with
a=X-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(\nu\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)+\mu\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))
and
b=s\frac{\nu^{2}+\mu^{2}}{4}
,
we obtain the explicit expression for the transition kernel

\displaystyle K_{\mathsf{w}\to\mathcal{W}}(X,\mu,\nu,n,\alpha)

(125)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}(1-s)\sqrt{s(\nu^{2}+\mu^{2})}}\left(\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)^{-n}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\left(X-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(\nu\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)+\mu\operatorname{Im}(\alpha))\right)^{2}}{s(\nu^{2}+\mu^{2})}\right).
