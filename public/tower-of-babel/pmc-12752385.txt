Clinical outcomes of navigation-assisted versus fluoroscopy-guided UBE-ULBD in single-level lumbar spinal stenosis: a retrospective study
Authors: Shi-Jie Liu, Si-Yuan Yao, Yao Zhang, Wan-Cheng lin, Li-Xiang Ding, Ji-Peng Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a common degenerative disorder characterized by neural compression, leading to radicular pain, neurogenic claudication, and lower limb dysfunction. Unilateral biportal endoscopic unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression (UBE-ULBD) has emerged as an effective minimally invasive surgical technique, but it requires advanced surgical skills and has a steep learning curve. The use of computer-assisted navigation has been increasingly adopted to improve surgical efficiency and safety; however, its clinical effectiveness and safety in UBE-ULBD for single-level LSS remain insufficiently investigated.MethodsA total of 119 patients undergoing UBE-ULBD were included and divided into Group A (navigation-assisted, n = 57) and Group B (fluoroscopy-guided, n = 62), with a minimum follow-up of 12 months. Perioperative outcomes and clinical efficacy were evaluated using operative time, fluoroscopy frequency, estimated blood loss, postoperative hospital stay, complication rates, as well as visual analogue scale (VAS) scores for back and leg pain, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and modified MacNab criteria.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the two groups. Group A had a significantly shorter operative time, fewer intraoperative fluoroscopies, and lower estimated blood loss compared with Group B (all P < 0.001), while postoperative hospital stay and overall complication rates did not differ significantly (P > 0.05). No surgical site infections or permanent nerve injuries occurred in either group. Both groups showed significant postoperative improvements in VAS and ODI scores relative to preoperative values; however, no intergroup differences were found at any follow-up point (P > 0.05). At the final follow-up, the excellent/good rate based on the modified MacNab criteria was 93.0% in Group A and 87.1% in Group B, with no significant difference (P > 0.05).ConclusionNavigation-assisted UBE-ULBD can significantly improve surgical efficiency and markedly reduce intraoperative radiation exposure for surgeons, while demonstrating comparable safety and clinical efficacy to conventional fluoroscopy-guided procedures. These advantages highlight its potential clinical value as a safer and more efficient alternative for minimally invasive decompression in lumbar spinal stenosis.Keywords: Navigation-assisted surgery, Lumbar spinal stenosis, Unilateral biportal endoscopy, Unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression

Introduction

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a common degenerative spinal disorder primarily caused by disc degeneration, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, and osteophyte formation. These pathological changes progressively reduce the spinal canal volume, resulting in neural compression and clinical symptoms [1]. Clinically, LSS is often characterized by radicular pain, neurogenic intermittent claudication, and varying degrees of lower limb dysfunction, which can severely impair patients’ quality of life [2, 3]. Although conservative treatments may provide symptom relief for some individuals, surgical intervention is considered a crucial therapeutic option for patients with persistent or progressive symptoms refractory to conservative treatment [4, 5].

Laminectomy has long been regarded as a classical surgical approach for the treatment of LSS, and favorable clinical outcomes have been achieved with this technique [6]. However, extensive soft tissue dissection and injury to the paraspinal musculature are commonly associated with this procedure, which often leads to significant postoperative pain, prolonged recovery, and potential compromise of spinal stability [7]. With growing recognition of the critical role of spinal stabilizing structures and a surgical trend shifting from laminectomy to laminotomy, minimally invasive techniques have gained increasing endorsement. These approaches offer significant advantages, including reduced tissue trauma, minimized intraoperative blood loss, and enhanced postoperative recovery [8, 9].

Among various minimally invasive techniques, unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression (ULBD) performed under unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE) has emerged as an important minimally invasive approach for LSS [10]. This technique utilizes separate portals for visualization and instrumentation, combined with continuous saline irrigation, which allows for a clear operative field and enhances the flexibility and efficiency of instrument manipulation [11]. However, as a technique that requires advanced spatial awareness and precise anatomical knowledge, UBE-ULBD is associated with a relatively steep learning curve [12, 13]. In the early stages of surgical training, frequent intraoperative fluoroscopic verification of portal placement and anatomical landmarks is often required, which not only prolongs operative time but also increases radiation exposure for both patients and surgeons. Furthermore, inaccuracies in localization may raise the risk of surgical complications and adversely affect clinical outcomes.

To address the aforementioned challenges, computer-assisted navigation systems have been introduced into UBE procedures in recent years, serving as an effective adjunct to enhance localization accuracy and optimize surgical trajectories [14]. Utilizing advanced technologies such as three-dimensional image reconstruction, intraoperative CT, O-arm scanning, and augmented reality (AR), these systems provide real-time, visualized spatial orientation during surgery, enabling precise portal establishment and accurate delineation of anatomical structures, significantly reducing reliance on conventional fluoroscopy, minimizing radiation exposure, and improving both surgical safety and efficiency [15–18]. these advantages, their clinical effectiveness and safety in UBE-ULBD for single-level LSS remain insufficiently investigated.

Therefore, this study aimed to retrospectively compare the clinical outcomes and safety profiles of navigation-assisted versus fluoroscopy-guided UBE-ULBD for single-level LSS, to provide theoretical support and practical reference for the broader clinical application of this technology.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This study, approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Shijitan Hospital, Capital Medical University (IIT2024-035-003) and conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki’s ethical principles, analyzed retrospectively 119 patients with lumbar spinal stenosis who had surgery at our hospital from January 2022 to June 2024. Divided into Group A (navigation-assisted UBE-ULBD) and Group B (fluoroscopy-guided UBE-ULBD), all patients received preoperative imaging, including anteroposterior and lateral lumbar X-rays, flexion-extension X-rays, CT scans, and MRIs. Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients with bilateral lower limb neurogenic symptoms due to single-level lumbar spinal stenosis, confirmed by physical and imaging evaluations; (2) no significant symptom relief or symptom worsening after at least three months of systematic conservative treatment; and (3) a minimum one-year postoperative follow-up. Exclusion criteria were: (1) multi-level lumbar spinal stenosis with unclear responsible segments; (2) imaging-confirmed lumbar instability (defined as ≥ 10° intervertebral endplate angle change or ≥ 4 mm vertebral translation on flexion-extension X-rays) [19], spondylolisthesis, or degenerative scoliosis; (3) previous surgery at the affected lumbar segment; and (4) presence of spinal tuberculosis, tumors, or other specific spinal pathologies.

Surgical methods

Conventional fluoroscopy-guided UBE-ULBD procedure

The patient was placed in a prone position under general anesthesia, with U-shaped pads supporting the abdomen to widen the interlaminar space. Under fluoroscopic guidance, we identified the target segment and marked the locations for a superior viewing portal and an inferior working portal. Following incision of the skin and fascia, the main dilator was used to bluntly dissect the paraspinal muscles and position itself at the spinous process-lamina junction. Following fluoroscopic confirmation of the position, separate cannulas were inserted to establish the viewing and working portals. A 30° endoscope was introduced through the viewing portal, and we utilized a plasma radiofrequency ablation probe with continuous saline irrigation to clear soft tissue, achieve hemostasis, and establish a clear operative field. Ipsilateral decompression commenced by resecting the junction of the lamina and spinous process with a high-speed burr and a Kerrison rongeur, which exposed the midline and the cephalad “V-shaped” structure of the ligamentum flavum. Subsequently, we detached the ligamentum from its cephalad and lateral attachments. Partial resection of the medial edge of the inferior articular process was performed to expose the ligament’s caudal attachment, which facilitated the complete removal of the ipsilateral ligamentum flavum. To ensure adequate decompression of the lateral recess, we resected the medial aspect of the superior articular process until the medial wall of the ipsilateral pedicle was fully visualized. For contralateral decompression, we first partially flattened the base of the spinous process using the burr. Employing an “over-the-top” technique, a blunt dissector carefully separated the contralateral ligamentum flavum from the ventral surface of the lamina to protect the neural elements. We then performed an undercutting decompression of the contralateral lamina using the burr and Kerrison rongeur. The decompression was progressively extended contralaterally until the medial wall of the contralateral pedicle was reached, achieving thorough bilateral nerve root release. After a final inspection confirmed adequate decompression and satisfactory dural sac pulsation, a 12-gauge drain tube was placed. A routine post-operative CT scan was performed to objectively evaluate the extent of decompression.

Navigation-assisted UBE-ULBD surgical procedure

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone position with a U-shaped soft cushion positioned beneath the abdomen to reduce intra-abdominal pressure and provide adequate exposure of the target intervertebral space. Two 2-mm Kirschner wires were percutaneously drilled approximately 2 cm into the cortical bone of the posterior superior iliac spine to secure a reference frame fitted with four infrared-reflective markers. A three-dimensional scan was then performed using a C-arm, and the images were automatically transmitted to the navigation system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). After automatic image registration, the surgeon used the real-time navigation display and a virtual probe. The upper viewing portal and lower working portal were then established accordingly. The surgery was performed under direct endoscopic visualization, with the surgeon simultaneously referencing the navigation monitor for guidance. The surgical procedure followed the same steps as the conventional fluoroscopy-guided UBE-ULBD, with intraoperative localization conducted using a probe. Upon completion of decompression, a nerve hook was used to assess nerve root mobility, and the navigation system was again utilized to confirm whether the decompression range met the predefined target area (Fig. 1).

All surgical procedures were executed by two experienced spinal endoscopic surgeons. Both surgeons possess independent operating experience of over 150 UBE-ULBD procedures, signifying the completion of the learning curve for this technique; furthermore, they both collaboratively underwent standardized training on the navigation system.

Data collection

Demographic data were systematically collected, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, symptoms duration, and Surgical segments. Perioperative parameters were assessed based on operative time (defined from the skin incision to wound closure), number of intraoperative fluoroscopy exposures, estimated blood loss, and length of hospital stay. Postoperative complications, including dural tears, epidural hematoma, inadequate decompression, and nerve root injury, were meticulously documented. The intensity of low back pain and lower limb pain was evaluated using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at multiple time points (preoperatively, and at 1 month, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively), while pain-related functional disability was assessed using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). At the final follow-up visit, surgical outcomes were evaluated according to the modified MacNab criteria.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 25.0; IBM, USA). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent samples t-tests were used to compare continuous data between groups, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a probability threshold of P < 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

This study included 119 patients in total. Group A had 57 patients (33 males and 24 females), while Group B had 62 patients (31 males and 31 females). Comparative analysis of baseline demographic characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, symptom duration, and surgical stage, showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups (P > 0.05, Table 1).

Perioperative parameters and complications

Perioperative parameters and complications are detailed in Table 2. Data analysis revealed that Group A outperformed Group B in several key indicators. Specifically, the mean operative time in Group A was 74.0 ± 10.1 min, which was significantly shorter than that in Group B (96.1 ± 8.8 min) (P < 0.001). Likewise, the intraoperative fluoroscopy frequency in Group A (2.9 ± 0.8 times) was significantly lower than that in Group B (6.9 ± 2.5 times) (P < 0.001). Additionally, Group A had significantly less estimated blood loss than Group B (P < 0.001). Significantly, no statistically significant difference was observed in the postoperative hospital stay between the two groups (P > 0.05).

Perioperative parameters and complications

The incidence of perioperative complications did not exhibit a statistically significant difference between the two groups of patients (P > 0.05). Within Group A, three cases of dural tear and two cases of transient lower limb neurological symptoms were observed. In Group B, four cases of dural tear, one case of wound hematoma, and three cases of transient lower limb neurological symptoms were documented. All cases of dural tear were successfully managed and healed through conservative treatment, without any residual sequelae. The single case of wound hematoma in Group B was effectively addressed and resolved following surgical debridement. Notably, within the 1-year follow-up period, three patients in Group B were found to require revision surgery due to inadequate decompression, while only one patient in Group A underwent a similar revision procedure. Throughout the study period, no cases of permanent nerve root injury or surgical site infection were reported in either group.

Clinical outcomes

Postoperative low back pain VAS, leg pain VAS, and ODI scores for both groups were significantly lower than preoperative baselines (Table 3). However, no statistically significant differences in these scores were observed between the groups at any assessment time point (preoperative, 1-month, 6-month, and 1-year postoperative; all P > 0.05, Table 3). At the final follow-up, clinical outcomes assessed by the modified MacNab criteria showed excellent/good rates of 93% in Group A and 87.1% in Group B. The difference in efficacy between the two groups was not statistically significant (P > 0.05, Table 3).

Discussion

With the rapid adoption of minimally invasive principles, UBE-ULBD has become a reliable option for treating LSS, owing to its advantages of smaller incisions and faster recovery [20, 21]. However, several challenges remain under conventional fluoroscopy guidance, including repeated fluoroscopic exposure, intraoperative localization errors, and difficulties in assessing the extent of decompression [22]. Therefore, ensuring adequate decompression while improving surgical efficiency and safety remains a major concern in clinical practice.

The results of this study demonstrated that navigation-assisted UBE-ULBD provided significant advantages in surgical efficiency. Compared with the fluoroscopy group, the navigation group had shorter operative time, less intraoperative blood loss, and fewer fluoroscopic exposures. These findings might be explained by several potential advantages of navigation technology. First, in conventional procedures, multiple fluoroscopic checks are often required to establish the working channel, which prolongs operative time, increases radiation exposure for both patients and surgeons, and may result in unnecessary muscle dissection [23]. Navigation technology overcomes these limitations by providing precise preoperative three-dimensional imaging and real-time guidance along a preset channel trajectory, allowing accurate and efficient channel establishment [24, 25]. Furthermore, once image registration is complete, the navigation system enables real-time spatial tracking of instruments without reliance on continuous intraoperative fluoroscopy, thus making nearly ‘radiation-free’ procedures possible. This feature not only optimizes the surgical workflow but also significantly minimizes the cumulative radiation dose received by the surgeon and assistants. Research indicates that orthopedic surgeons are frequently exposed to surgical radiation, with long-term cumulative exposure potentially leading to hidden health risks [26]. Therefore, the application of navigation technology, while ensuring or even enhancing surgical accuracy, also demonstrates significant occupational safety value, fundamentally helping to mitigate the radiation-related occupational risks for the surgical team. Second, in conventional fluoroscopy-guided UBE procedures, the limited field of view frequently leads to difficulties in intraoperative directional judgment, a problem particularly pronounced in patients with severe degeneration or anatomical abnormalities. Hypertrophied facet joints, ossified ligamenta flava, and distorted anatomical landmarks often obscure the correct operative path, making accurate determination of the decompression extent more challenging. Particularly in patients with severe lumbar degeneration, the visibility of the interlaminar space is further compromised; while in cases of deformity such as scoliosis, vertebral rotation alters the normal anatomical spatial relationship, significantly increasing the risk of localization error or incomplete decompression. Furthermore, the transforaminal approach frequently necessitates multiple fluoroscopic checks to establish a precise working channel, which not only prolongs the operative time but also increases radiation exposure for both the patient and the surgical team. In contrast, the navigation system provides real-time three-dimensional spatial tracking and localization capabilities, effectively assisting the surgeon in maintaining correct anatomical orientation and operative trajectory, even when confronted with complex or severely distorted anatomical regions.

Interestingly, although many studies have emphasized the role of navigation in improving the safety of spinal surgery, our results did not show a statistically significant difference between navigation and conventional fluoroscopy in reducing the overall complication rate. This may be attributable to the relatively small sample size of this study, and to the fact that our surgeons have already passed the learning curve and their techniques are relatively mature. Crucially, we must acknowledge that our current data do not strongly support navigation’s definitive benefits on safety and complication rates. Nevertheless, navigation technology is considered to have the potential to optimize decompression and to reduce complications associated with instability or intraoperative manipulation. Inadequate decompression remains one of the main reasons for reoperation after UBE-ULBD [27]. Although postoperative quantitative imaging was not performed in this study, prior reports have shown that navigation enables precise delineation of bony landmarks, facilitating more complete and symmetric decompression-particularly on the contralateral side. This may reduce the risk of residual stenosis and persistent symptoms [28]. Furthermore, navigation-guided trajectory planning supports precise intraoperative localization and may reduce iatrogenic complications caused by technical errors. Several studies have confirmed that navigation improves the accuracy of pedicle screw placement in minimally invasive surgery and reduces related complications [29–32]. In addition, real-time spatial localization enables precise control of the decompression extent and prevents excessive resection of the facet joints. Preservation of the facet joints is essential for maintaining spinal stability. Since postoperative instability may contribute to persistent low back pain, navigation-assisted decompression might offer important value for improving long-term outcomes [33].

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the follow-up period was only 12 months, which is insufficient to fully assess long-term outcomes, such as adjacent segment degeneration or recurrence of stenosis. Third, fluoroscopy frequency was used as an indirect measure of radiation exposure, lacking more precise dosimetric evaluation. Fourth, this study primarily relied on clinical efficacy indicators for assessment and did not utilize postoperative CT and MRI for quantitative analysis of the decompression scope and effect. Finally, hidden blood loss associated with intraoperative irrigation was not accounted for, potentially underestimating the actual blood loss. Future research should focus on further optimizing navigation systems, for example, by integrating high-resolution imaging and augmented reality (AR) technologies to enhance surgical precision and operational convenience. Furthermore, the inclusion of routine postoperative CT or MRI scans is necessary for the objective evaluation of the decompression extent and effect. Additionally, large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies are needed to validate the role of navigation in improving surgical efficiency, reducing radiation exposure, and minimizing complications, while long-term follow-up should be performed to evaluate sustained symptom relief, reoperation rates, adjacent segment degeneration, and spinal stability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the application of navigation technology in UBE-ULBD offers distinct advantages, including improved surgical efficiency through reduced operative time and decreased intraoperative radiation exposure for surgeons. More importantly, it addresses the difficulty in accurately evaluating the extent of decompression—a common limitation of conventional fluoroscopy-guided techniques. By providing real-time spatial orientation and visual guidance, navigation helps achieve more precise and complete decompression. These benefits may contribute to a reduced risk of major complications. Our findings support the view that navigation-assisted UBE-ULBD is a promising technique, with the potential to enhance the safety and effectiveness of surgical treatment for lumbar spinal stenosis. Further studies are warranted to validate its broader applicability.
