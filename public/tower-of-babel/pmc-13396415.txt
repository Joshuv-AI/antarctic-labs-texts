A data-driven framework for structural health monitoring using reinforcement learning and deep autoencoders
Authors: Amin Hadizadeh, Amir Tarighat, Abbass Malian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe importance of structural health monitoring, especially for bridges that have exceeded their design life, has increased significantly to reduce maintenance costs, improve safety, and optimize performance. This article proposes a combination of machine learning tools for automatic detection of anomalies in bridge structures from accelerometer data. The Z-24 bridge, which has a comprehensive database of accelerometer data, was considered as a case study. By extracting modal frequencies from accelerometer data using operational modal analysis, the proposed framework, which combines machine learning methods, identifies damage-sensitive features and provides a coherent framework for structural health monitoring. One of the main innovations of the proposed method is the integration of reinforcement learning with autoencoders. The proposed method, Reinforcement Learning Autoencoder Neural Network (RLAENN), is validated using real accelerometer data, and its performance is compared with the classical benchmark algorithms, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Kernel PCA (KPCA), Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), and Mahalanobis Square Distance (MSD). By eliminating the need to tune sensitive parameters, this method achieves significant improvements in accuracy and F1 score over conventional methods, confirming its performance and effectiveness.Keywords: Anomaly detection, Modal analysis, Machine learning, Structural health monitoring, Reinforcement learning, AutoencoderSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Civil infrastructure plays a fundamental role in the safety, sustainability, and continuity of transportation networks, and continuous assessment of their condition is an unavoidable necessity. With many existing structures, especially bridges, approaching the end of their service life, targeted planning for their maintenance and retrofitting is considered an essential and cost-effective solution. For example, over 2000 bridges in Italy, 4000 in France, and nearly 6.8% of 600,000 US bridges are in critical or deficient states1.

Therefore, accurately assessing the health status of bridge structures is of great importance, and structural health monitoring offers a range of methods to address this need. In extensive studies, the location and quantification of damage in structures have been investigated with different techniques in the field of structural health monitoring. One of the successful approaches in the field of structural health monitoring is vibration-based damage identification methods, which are themselves classified into two categories: data-driven and model-based2,3. Model-based methods rely on system identification and finite element model updating, and although these methods have high accuracy, they are usually time-consuming and expensive, and their performance depends on the correct definition of several parameters, including the behaviour of materials under environmental conditions and, in general, the modeling of the physics governing the problem. In contrast, data-based methods, using statistical techniques and pattern recognition, extract damage-sensitive features without the need for detailed modeling and are computationally more efficient and generalizable. In this context, and based on a data-driven approach, previous studies have shown that modal parameters, including natural frequencies and mode shapes, can play an important role as damage-sensitive features for accurate identification of structural failures4,5. In recent years, advances in directly extracting damage-sensitive features from vibration signals have led to the development and significant improvement of data-driven structural health monitoring methods. As these methods evolve towards more robust, automated frameworks, they play a more prominent role in supporting decision-making and improving the safety of critical infrastructure6. In the past year, several recent surveys have underscored how artificial intelligence and machine learning are transforming the field of structural health monitoring. These studies point to a distinct trend toward integrating deep learning frameworks into civil and bridge engineering practices, where automation and data-centric approaches are now reshaping how health assessments are performed7–10.

Datasets derived from bridge monitoring programs form a rich basis for data-driven structural assessments. When processed through advanced analytical tools, they enable the handling of complex and noisy signals with an efficacy rarely achieved by traditional physical models4,5,11. Various statistical tools, such as autoregressive modeling, Mahalanobis distance evaluation, and single‑class classifiers, have often been used to create representations of normal or undamaged structural conditions. When the new measurements deviate from that baseline, the change may suggest the beginning of damage4. Case studies have shown that data-driven methods can distinguish structural changes from environmental influences4,5,11,12. However, major challenges remain, including threshold selection, long-term sensor drift, and the scarcity of verified damage scenarios. These limitations continue to motivate the development of more sensitive and reliable SHM techniques4,11,12. Recently, advanced deep learning models have been introduced to handle common sensing challenges such as missing or corrupted signals. One effective approach is the MA-CNN-BiLSTM model, which reconstructs incomplete acceleration data and preserves the continuity of vibration-based monitoring, thereby improving reliability in long-term deployments13.

Unsupervised learning plays a central role in vibration-based SHM because labeled damage data are rarely available in real structures14. Novelty detection, statistical density estimation, and autoencoders can be used to detect anomalies by comparing signals to a baseline15,16. Advanced models, such as multi-head self-attention LSTM (MHSA-LSTM) autoencoders, have shown enhanced sensitivity in identifying small damage locations and multiple damage locations using the ambient vibration approach17. Hybrid methods have recently gained attention for their ability to reduce the impact of environmental factors, such as temperature and traffic, on model reliability in real-world conditions. Still, these systems face difficulties in harsh environments, where noise and fluctuations may hide damage-related signals and cause incorrect or missed detections18,19. Moreover, generalization across different bridge types remains constrained by structure-specific training, and comparative benchmarks against conventional SHM approaches are rare17–20. Despite these difficulties, unsupervised learning remains a scalable, adaptable solution for SHM, and future work should focus on generalization and field validation14,15.

Operational Modal Analysis (OMA), when combined with advanced feature-extraction techniques, provides highly damage-sensitive indicators that are essential for effective unsupervised SHM. Anomaly detection accuracy has been improved by reducing the dimensionality of modal data to a small number of informative features using PCA (Principal Component Analysis) or autoassociative neural networks. It is made even faster with deep stacked autoencoders, which reduce the number of modal parameters and increase their robustness to environmental changes21 .Multivariate autoregressive (AR) models also showed efficiency in damage localization using the time–frequency technique, particularly under acoustic excitation22,23. Using AR parameters and residuals, Entezami and Shariatmadar24 presented new collaborative damage-sensitivity features that precisely locate and measure damage. This was expanded upon by Römgens and Abbassi25, who used a time-series autoencoder to detect damage without the need for labels. PCA, in combination with FRFs, was shown by Siow and Ong26 to improve the identification of structural defects in flat-plate systems. Taken together, these works emphasize that strong feature extraction, consistent with OMA, is crucial for reliable and sensitive damage detection in unsupervised SHM settings.

In developing unsupervised structural‑health‑monitoring (SHM) systems, researchers inevitably rely on both real and simulated benchmark data. Over the years, the Z-24 Bridge campaign has become almost a laboratory icon; it holds years of accelerometer records gathered through changing seasons, both when the bridge was intact and when controlled damage was introduced. Because of that continuity, it has served as a testing ground for everything from transfer learning frameworks to Bayesian autoencoders and even generative adversarial networks27–29. Another long‑term source of insight is the S101 Bridge in Reibersdorf, a prestressed concrete structure whose dataset includes a remarkably dense archive of single‑parameter features used for benchmarking feature extraction30. At the opposite end of the spectrum, simulated datasets, such as those developed by Kullaa31,offer artificial but well‑controlled variations, including moving loads and breathing cracks, to evaluate an algorithm’s robustness under controlled conditions. Many of these repositories include standardized protocols and frequent cross‑checks, employing statistical and spectral feature analyses combined with stratified k‑fold validation to control bias30. However, the strong dependence of these datasets on specific structural configurations, loading conditions, and damage patterns restricts the broader applicability of the resulting SHM algorithms under diverse environmental or operational scenarios30,32. Even so, domain adaptation techniques and transfer learning help span this gap27, there is a need for more realistic and varied benchmark datasets to ensure the reliable operation of unsupervised SHM systems across various infrastructure types and scenarios.

In recent years, one‑class deep learning approaches have attracted considerable interest for identifying structural damage. However, in real environments, factors such as continuous traffic, temperature fluctuations, humidity, and aging materials make it difficult to distinguish genuine damage from ordinary variations, complicating detection efforts33. Conventional models built on autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, and one‑class neural frameworks often misinterpret these variations because the available training data are limited, generalization capacity is low, and creating representative pseudo‑negative samples remains challenging34. For instance, Tian (2025) introduced an autoencoder-based anomaly detection framework for bridge structural health monitoring, in which reconstruction error is used as the primary indicator for distinguishing abnormal states and issuing early warning signals35. Alongside reconstruction-based frameworks, more recent studies have explored time–frequency-domain methods such as short-time Fourier transform (STFT) for feature extraction. These approaches have been shown to capture subtle structural variations and improve the identification of early damage under varying operational conditions36. Researchers have introduced several strategies to reduce these difficulties. Hybrid models combining statistical methods with deep learning have been used to capture subtle relationships37. Numerical simulations have also assisted domain adaptation by enlarging the range of conditions38. Factor‑analysis‑based autoencoders were developed to manage variability without requiring data from known healthy states39,40. While spectral features-based population techniques achieved comparable reliability to traditional modal parameters41, semi-supervised learning approaches reduced false alarms by accounting directly for environmental influences42. These methods address environmental variability, but further advances are needed to improve robustness in damage detection across complex real-world scenarios.

To overcome the limitations of conventional unsupervised frameworks, recent paradigms have shifted toward self-supervised and open-set learning strategies to better handle the severe scarcity and imbalance of real-world damage data. For instance, contrastive learning-enhanced open-set methods have been introduced to establish robust decision boundaries capable of distinguishing not only between healthy states and known minor damages but also isolating entirely unknown or ambiguous anomaly types43,44. Concurrently, significant research efforts have focused on the practical deployment of unsupervised SHM within decentralized, self-powered wireless sensing networks. These approaches leverage multi-module neural networks—such as integrated reconstructor, classifier, and predictor frameworks—to capture intricate global spatial correlations and local temporal features directly at the sensor level, thereby minimizing communication energy while maintaining high diagnostic robustness against operational noise45,46.

Most studies based on the unsupervised vibration-based SHM strategy have primarily employed approaches tested in laboratory studies, numerical simulations, or structure-specific benchmark studies. However, these approaches are very challenging to apply in practice. Some deep learning approaches based on acceleration data have been applied, but the results in the field over the long term remain uncertain due to the models’ complexity, their sensitivity to environmental changes and operational variations, and their in-field implementation. In operational modal analysis, the use of natural frequencies is considered one of the best approaches for defining damage sensitivity. Moreover, their use, especially in unsupervised methods aimed solely at damage detection using recorded bridge acceleration data, is relatively underexplored.

In this study, an unsupervised framework is proposed to automatically detect structural anomalies in bridges by integrating operational modal features extracted from vibration data within a data-driven learning framework, using sensor-derived vibration data.

The proposed framework is organized as a coherent processing pipeline to ensure consistent anomaly detection under environmental and operational variability. First, an OMA-based stabilization diagram is established, after which clustering and time-domain tracking of the identified modal frequencies are performed. These modal features are then used as primary indicators within a one-class classification scheme. To further enhance anomaly discrimination, an autoencoder is trained to capture and reconstruct healthy structural response patterns.

Unlike conventional approaches with fixed or empirically selected thresholds, the decision boundary is adaptively optimized by a reinforcement learning agent based on the distribution of reconstruction errors. Within this unified workflow, we propose the Reinforcement Learning Autoencoder Neural Network (RLAENN), which explicitly links modal feature representations to a dynamically tuned decision mechanism for robust anomaly detection.

In this research, the performance of the proposed methodology is evaluated using real accelerometer data collected from the Z-24 Bridge. Detection performance is assessed in terms of accuracy, recall, precision, and F1 score, and compared against established benchmark methods, including PCA, KPCA, GMM, and MSD. A set of targeted experiments is conducted to evaluate robustness with respect to limited training data, sensitivity to varying damage levels, and progressive structural degradation, following standard SHM experimental practice. The results demonstrate the practical potential of RLAENN for real-world bridge structural health monitoring.

Description of the Z24 bridge and dataset

Description of the Z24 bridge

The proposed data-driven methodology is validated using measured vibration and environmental data from the Z24 bridge, a well-documented prestressed concrete box-girder bridge located in the canton of Bern, Switzerland. The bridge forms part of the A1 motorway connecting Bern and Zurich and consists of three spans: a central span of 30 m and two symmetric side spans of 14 m each, for an overall length of approximately 60 m. Owing to its statically indeterminate configuration, the bridge’s dynamic behaviour is particularly sensitive to changes in boundary conditions and environmental variations47.

Monitoring system and dataset

Within the framework of the European BRITE EURAM SIMCES project, the Z-24 bridge was equipped with an Environmental Monitoring System (EMS) during its final year of service, prior to demolition in 1998. The primary objective of the EMS was not direct damage detection but rather the systematic characterisation of environmental influences—most notably temperature—on the structure’s modal properties. To this end, accelerometers were strategically distributed along the deck, central pier, and supports to capture a set of dominant global vibration modes, rather than relying on the dense sensor arrays typically required for curvature-based damage indices. In parallel, an extensive temperature monitoring scheme was implemented. Temperature sensors were installed at selected cross-sections along the deck, at multiple locations within the box-girder cross-section, and in the surrounding soil near the abutments and pier at three different depth levels. This configuration enabled the observation of thermal gradients and soil–structure interaction effects, both of which are known to influence the dynamic response of prestressed concrete bridges significantly48.

Acceleration and environmental data were recorded continuously at hourly intervals, resulting in 24 measurement cycles per day. Each cycle consisted of environmental measurements acquired before and after the collection of acceleration time histories. All data were stored in ASCII format, providing a transparent, well-structured, and long-term dataset that is particularly suitable for data-driven and machine-learning-based structural health monitoring studies aimed at separating environmental variability from damage-sensitive features48.

Proposed methodology

In this research, we assessed the performance of the newly developed methodology using real accelerometer data collected from the Z-24 Bridge. The preprocessing step began with a careful inspection of the raw acceleration records. Typical issues such as sensor drift, short noise bursts, and minor discontinuities between successive segments were identified and corrected. Only minimal interventions were applied to ensure that the ambient‑excitation characteristics essential for subsequent operational modal analysis remained intact. Following denoising, normalization was performed so that the statistical properties of the signals, such as mean and variance, became comparable across sensors. Standardizing the data in this way ensured that later stages of the framework could operate reliably, without being biased by inconsistent measurement scales.

Environmental factors such as temperature‑induced variations, which are known to influence the dynamic response of the Z-24 Bridge, were also considered. When necessary, these effects were mitigated through filtering or by constructing normalization indices to prevent them from masking structural behaviour. After these corrections, the cleaned and standardized vibration segments were prepared for the modal‑processing phase, where the OMA‑based stabilization diagram and, later, clustering and time‑domain tracking were performed as part of the overall pipeline. These extracted modal frequencies subsequently serve as a compact feature set for the one-class classifier employed in the anomaly-detection stage of the framework. Validation on the Z-24 Bridge benchmark demonstrates high accuracy, notable noise robustness, and consistent performance across multiple evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1‑score (see Fig. 1). These results confirm that the preprocessing stage produced data of sufficient quality to support stable modal identification and reliable downstream analysis.

Since the proposed RLAENN and the comparative anomaly detection methods operate as unsupervised learning frameworks, the training phase is executed utilizing exclusively the healthy state data sequence. To preclude an optimistic bias in evaluating the performance of the anomaly detection algorithms, a strict partitioning scheme is enforced: a continuous sequence comprising 90% of the healthy state observations is allocated exclusively to the training set. The remaining 10% of the healthy baseline sequence is integrated with the entirety of the damaged state sequence to formulate the test set. Retaining a temporal sequence of healthy data within the test set is crucial; it ensures that the trained models are robustly evaluated on their ability to avoid misclassifying unseen normal environmental baseline fluctuations as structural degradation.

Environmental normalization strategy (black-box paradigm)

A fundamental challenge in implementing a reliable Structural Health Monitoring system is the mitigation of Environmental and Operational Variations (EOVs), such as severe temperature gradients, which can induce substantial shifts in dynamic features and consequently mask damage or trigger false alarms. Broadly, EOV normalization strategies fall into two categories: correlation-based models that require direct measurement of environmental parameters, and latent data-driven, or “black-box,” approaches.

The algorithms deployed in this study exclusively utilize the black-box approach. Status evaluation is conducted based solely on the measured structural responses. The foundational premise is that by training the Deep Autoencoder over a sufficiently long period encapsulating extreme environmental cycles (e.g., the full seasonal spectrum of the Z-24 bridge), the network’s latent space implicitly captures and models the non-linear manifold of the EOVs. Consequently, environmental fluctuations are recognized as normal variance within the operational baseline, eliminating the requirement for parallel environmental sensor networks.

Scope of identification and fundamental limitations

Following Rytter’s50 classical hierarchy for Structural Health Monitoring, the identification process is categorized into five progressive levels: detection, localization, classification, quantification, and prognosis. The framework introduced in this study (the RLAENN) is meticulously designed and validated exclusively for Level 1: Anomaly and Damage Detection. Advancing to higher-level diagnostics, such as damage localization or classification, using the current methodological configuration is mathematically ill-posed. The chosen condition indicators—natural frequencies—are global dynamic properties reflecting the integrated stiffness matrix of the entire structure. Relying solely on global frequency shifts to pinpoint local degradation lacks spatial determinism. Furthermore, while mode shapes could theoretically facilitate localization, the functional sensor topology of the Z-24 long-term monitoring system was constrained to only 8 asymmetrically distributed accelerometers along the deck. This severe sparsity introduces spatial aliasing, precluding the extraction of the high-resolution spatial derivatives (e.g., modal curvatures) mandated for precise localization. Consequently, the objective is constrained to providing a highly sensitive and reliable early-warning mechanism (Detection).

Operational modal analysis and frequency identification

Structural dynamic properties, specifically natural frequencies, are extracted herein using the Covariance-driven Stochastic Subspace Identification (SSI-COV) algorithm. This time-domain implementation operates exclusively on ambient vibration responses, obviating the necessity for input force measurement. Since SSI-COV is a well-established and robust algorithm within the field of Operational Modal Analysis (OMA), only a concise overview of its underlying mechanics is provided here.

Formulated by Van Overschee and De Moor51, the dynamic behavior of a linear time-invariant (LTI) structure under unknown ambient excitation is mathematically represented by a discrete-time stochastic state-space model:

where is the measured structural response output vector at the discrete time step , and denotes the state vector. The matrix characterizes the structural system dynamics as the state matrix, whereas serves as the output observation matrix, mapping the internal states to the external environment.

The terms and represent the unmeasurable process noise (arising from modeling inaccuracies and ambient inputs) and measurement noise (due to sensor inaccuracies), respectively. Both are assumed to be zero-mean white noise processes. The SSI-COV algorithm computes the output covariance matrices at various time lags to construct a block Toeplitz matrix (specifically, following established recommendations in the literature and considering the 8 active accelerometers available in the Z-24 long-term monitoring program, the time lag parameter was set to i = 50 and the maximum system order was chosen as n = 160). The application of Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to this matrix yields the extended observability matrix, from which the state matrix A is extracted. The eigenvalue decomposition of [A] subsequently provides the discrete-time poles, which are analytically transformed into the continuous-time domain to determine the structural natural frequencies52.

Following the extraction of initial modal candidates, a fully automated interpretation of the stabilization diagram was conducted to separate true physical modes from mathematical (spurious) modes caused by system order over-estimation or environmental noise. For this purpose, the modal purification and tracking process was implemented based on the unified methodology proposed by He et al. (2022)53, utilizing the Modified Modal Observability Correlation (MMOC) criterion. Unlike the traditional Modal Assurance Criterion (MAC), which frequently fails to distinguish modes under spatial aliasing or sparse sensor deployments—a characteristic condition of the Z-24 bridge dataset—MMOC incorporates normalized eigenvalues into the similarity computation, substantially enhancing identification accuracy.

The purification was executed in two systematic steps: initially, definitively non-physical modes were filtered via hard validation criteria. Subsequently, an adaptive MMOC threshold, dynamically determined by the maximum system order () and the anticipated number of physical modes (), was calculated to eliminate scattered spurious modes. Finally, the true natural frequencies of the structure were identified by selecting a representative for each physical cluster utilizing the box-plot method54,55.

Accordingly, the structural natural frequency identification process was performed across the entire health monitoring period. By retaining the first n modes (n ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5}), the Frequency–Condition Tensor (FCTₙ) was constructed to serve as the input for the subsequent deep learning-based anomaly detection framework. Variable retention of the modal order facilitates a systematic sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of modal truncation. Furthermore, while OMA processes inherently introduce minor extraction errors into the identified frequencies, the proposed RLAENN framework mitigates this through its Deep Autoencoder architecture. The autoencoder maps the multidimensional FCTₙ into a condensed latent space, effectively acting as a nonlinear noise filter that isolates robust structural condition indicators from transient OMA extraction errors.

Autoencoder architecture

To model the normal behaviour of the structure, an autoencoder neural network is used. The autoencoder is an unsupervised neural network consisting of two main components: the encoder and the decoder.

The encoder transforms high-dimensional input data, such as vibrational signals with a time length, into a lower-dimensional feature space (latent space), extracting important patterns and intrinsic features of the healthy data. This latent representation is stored in the encoder’s vector. The decoder then takes this lower-dimensional latent vector and attempts to reconstruct the original signal. In this architecture, the encoder and decoder jointly learn to preserve essential structural information. The goal of training the autoencoder is to minimize the reconstruction error between the input and output, typically using a mean squared error loss function57.

Thus, the network learns to reproduce only the common and repetitive patterns present in the healthy data. The autoencoder is trained on healthy, undamaged data to capture the structure’s normal features. In other words, the autoencoder is configured to reconstruct healthy data with very low error. However, when confronted with data related to a damaged structure or abnormal behaviour that was not encountered during training, the autoencoder will perform poorly in reconstruction, resulting in an increase in reconstruction error42.

A fully connected deterministic autoencoder is developed and trained in an unsupervised manner using only measurements acquired from the undamaged structural condition. Prior to training, all input features are standardized using Z-score normalization, with the normalization parameters computed exclusively from the intact training data and subsequently applied to the validation and test sets to avoid data leakage. The autoencoder adopts a symmetric architecture comprising two dense encoder layers with 16 and 4 neurons, respectively, defining a 4-dimensional latent space, followed by a mirrored decoder. ReLU activation functions are used in the hidden layers, while a linear activation is employed in the output layer. The network is trained by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 for 100 epochs and a batch size of 32. Reconstruction errors obtained from the trained autoencoder are used as damage-sensitive indicators for anomaly detection. To improve decision robustness, a Q-learning–based reinforcement learning strategy is incorporated as a post-processing decision layer. The reinforcement learning agent does not update the autoencoder weights; instead, it dynamically adjusts the decision threshold applied to the reconstruction error distribution based on discrete actions defined over percentile levels of intact-condition reconstruction errors. This hybrid framework enables adaptive damage detection while preserving the fixed autoencoder representation of the undamaged structural state.

The architecture of the autoencoder used can vary depending on the type of data. In this study, for simplicity, a fully connected autoencoder with several hidden layers is employed. In this setup, the encoder gradually reduces the dimensions of the input data, reaching a latent vector with fewer dimensions. Then, the decoder reverses this operation and reconstructs the data. All layers use appropriate nonlinear activation functions (e.g., ReLU or tanh) to model complex relationships between the data.

The number of neurons in the hidden layer (latent vector) is selected based on the problem; this number is smaller than the input dimension to enable compression. To prevent overfitting, techniques like batch normalization and dropout can be used in the layers.

It is worth mentioning that if the input data has specific temporal or spatial characteristics, advanced versions of autoencoders can also be used, such as convolutional autoencoders (for image data) or LSTM-based autoencoders (for sequential time-series data). However, the core idea across all these models is the same: learning a compressed representation of the normal behaviour and calculating the reconstruction error as an anomaly indicator.

Once training is complete, the autoencoder network is ready to receive new data and compute the reconstruction error for each sample. This reconstruction error will be very small for healthy data. However, if the structure is damaged or exhibits abnormal behaviour, the data will be reconstructed with lower accuracy, resulting in a reconstruction error that is higher than usual. In this way, reconstruction error is used as a metric for damage detection. Reinforcement learning for threshold adjustment

After training an autoencoder on healthy responses, each sample is mapped to a reconstruction error . The decision problem is to select a threshold τ that separates healthy from damaged responses. A fixed τ is difficult to tune and can become sub-optimal under drift in operating or environmental conditions. The proposed method therefore treats threshold selection as a sequential decision problem and learns τ automatically with tabular Q-learning.

Formulation and action set. Let ​ denote reconstruction errors on the healthy training split. A finite action set A = {a1,…,aK} is defined by mapping each action to a candidate threshold obtained from fixed percentiles of , that is τ(ak)= with ∈{95,96,…,99} in this study. At any learning step, choosing action aK ​ deterministically sets the working threshold to τ(ak); samples with e(x) ≥ τ(ak) are flagged as damaged.

State, reward, and learning rule. For computational parsimony and reproducibility, a single-state formulation is adopted, which reduces learning to estimating the long-run utility of each candidate percentile. The reward is designed to steer the procedure toward thresholds that better isolate anomalous behaviour while avoiding overly permissive choices. Training and inference procedure. First, the autoencoder is trained on healthy data (training split) using mean-squared reconstruction loss, after which reconstruction errors are computed for training, validation, and damaged test splits. Q-learning then proceeds for a fixed number of iterations. At each iteration: errors are recomputed with the current network, an action ak ​ is sampled via the ε-greedy policy, the corresponding threshold τ(ak) is applied to flag test samples, the reward r(τ(ak)) is evaluated, the Q-table is updated via the Bellman rule, and ε is decayed. Because the state is fixed, convergence reduces to identifying the action with the largest expected reward; the final decision threshold is τ⋆ = τ(arg Q(a)). This procedure yields an automatically tuned, data-driven threshold that avoids ad-hoc manual selection and adapts to the empirical error distribution induced by the training of the autoencoder.

Practical design choices and safeguards. Candidate thresholds are derived from ​ to anchor decisions to the distribution learned from healthy behaviour; standardization parameters are fixed from the training split and applied to validation and test splits to prevent leakage. To ensure numerical stability of the reward, when the flagged-set cardinality is zero a small fallback is used (e.g., assign a modest negative reward or evaluate the mean over the top-mmm errors), and rewards are clipped to a bounded interval. The single-state tabular design keeps the computation lightweight: the Q-table has size 1 × K, updates are O(K) per iteration, and no additional neural training is required during threshold learning.

Extension to richer decision processes. The same reinforcement-learning scheme can be generalized to a full Markov decision process by defining informative states and dynamic transitions. For example, the state can encode a discretized index of the current threshold together with short-term indicators from the validation split (recent false-alarm rate and detection rate computed over a sliding window). Actions can then be “decrease threshold by one bin,” “keep,” or “increase by one bin,” which induces non-trivial state transitions and enables adaptation under non-stationarity. If the state representation becomes high-dimensional or continuous, a function approximator (e.g., a deep Q-network) can replace the tabular update; however, the single-state tabular variant adopted here achieves stable behaviour with minimal complexity and aligns with the unsupervised nature of the anomaly-detection task.

After learning τ⋆, performance is summarized on the damaged test split. When ground-truth labels are available, standard detection metrics are reported, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. When labels are limited or only healthy labels are available, separation-oriented summaries such as the proportion flagged at τ ⋆ on healthy versus damaged data or distributional distance between their error histograms can complement the primary metrics and document robustness to distributional drift.

Performance evaluation and decision-making metrics

After training the autoencoder and adjusting the threshold by the reinforcement learning agent, the final step is evaluating the system’s performance in damage detection. For this purpose, test data, which includes labeled samples (healthy and damaged), are provided to the system for accuracy assessment. Each input sample first passes through the preprocessing stage and is then fed into the autoencoder to compute its reconstruction error.

In the decision-making stage, the reconstruction error of each sample is compared with the optimal threshold set by the reinforcement learning agent. If the error exceeds the threshold, the system will signal damage; otherwise, the sample is classified as healthy58.

Result and discussion

The performance of the RLAENN framework was evaluated against four well-known baseline methods, PCA, KPCA, GMM, and MSD, using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score as the primary evaluation criteria. The four figures summarizing these results guide the reader from a broad, high-level comparison to a more focused look at how each method behaves. As the experiments progress, a clear trend becomes evident: RLAENN remains steadier and frequently outperforms the other approaches across the full range of tests.

Under normal operating conditions, the autoencoder forms a compact internal picture of the structure’s healthy behaviour, which keeps reconstruction errors low. Once the structural response begins to drift, sometimes gradually, sometimes in the earliest stages of damage, these errors rise. This shift provides a direct signal for the Q‑learning agent, prompting it to adjust the decision threshold as new information arrives. In practice, this helps the system avoid unnecessary warnings while remaining attentive to the subtle signs that matter most for early detection.

The baseline methods face several familiar challenges under the same conditions. PCA and KPCA are restricted by their underlying assumptions, GMM is highly sensitive to changes in the data distribution, and MSD requires repeated tuning without offering a reliable rule for threshold selection. RLAENN avoids many of these limitations, maintaining a more dependable balance between precision and recall and consistently achieving higher F1-scores—even when environmental factors such as temperature or loading fluctuate over time.

Taken together, these results point to a clear conclusion: combining reconstruction-based feature-learning with a reinforcement-guided thresholding mechanism creates a flexible and dependable foundation for anomaly detection. This makes RLAENN particularly suitable for real structural health monitoring scenarios, where conditions may change gradually, unpredictably, or abruptly—and where timely, trustworthy detection is essential.

While the black-box approach implicitly neutralizes environmental factors, its efficacy must be quantitatively verified. In the absence of physically controlled in-situ experiments separating weather from damage, the algorithm’s performance is rigorously quantified via the Precision and Recall metrics. Precision measures the proportion of predicted positive cases that are truly positive, and therefore reflects the model’s ability to limit false alarms. The high precision values reported for the RLAENN framework suggest strong robustness against environmental interference; even under extreme diurnal temperature variations (e.g., the abrupt 19.8 °C change recorded in the asphalt layer on June 19, 1998), the model appears to maintain a low rate of false structural anomaly alerts. Likewise, Recall measures the proportion of actual positive cases that are correctly detected, and high recall indicates low missed-detection rates. The superior recall performance therefore suggests that the framework remains highly sensitive to genuine structural degradation, allowing damage-related signatures to be detected despite the presence of environmental noise.

High average accuracy, minimal dispersion across repeated evaluations, and stable behaviour under shifting conditions make the method suitable for continuous, real‑time structural monitoring where reliability over long horizons is essential.

Conclusions

This study presented an unsupervised framework for structural health monitoring that blends two complementary ideas: learning a stable representation of the healthy state through an autoencoder and adjusting the decision threshold with a reinforcement-learning agent. Instead of relying on a fixed, user-defined threshold, the model learns how to update its decision boundary as the data evolve, allowing it to adapt when environmental or operational conditions shift.

Across all experiments, the method showed a clear and repeatable pattern. The average F1-score remained close to 99%, and the variation across samples stayed below one percent. Such tight dispersion is a strong indication that the system can maintain consistent decisions even when the input distribution changes due to temperature, traffic, or noise. The trend analyses also revealed minimal temporal drift, suggesting that the RL agent succeeds in reshaping the threshold to preserve a balance between precision and recall without external tuning.

From a practical point of view, the framework offers a reliable pathway for real-time monitoring of civil structures. Because it operates without labeled damage data, it is suitable for real environments where anomalous states are rare or poorly documented. At the same time, its ability to adapt to non-stationary data makes it a strong candidate for long-term monitoring of bridges, large infrastructures, and vibration-based systems where decision stability is as important as accuracy.

Future work may expand this framework by combining it with probabilistic graphical models or distributed sensor-fusion strategies. These extensions could enhance the adaptive behaviour of the reinforcement-learning component and support multi-agent monitoring networks capable of operating reliably at larger scales.
