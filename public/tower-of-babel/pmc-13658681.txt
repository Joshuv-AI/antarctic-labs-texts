URICA Study: Diagnosis and Management of Recurrent Acute Bacterial Cystitis in Women With Urinary Behavioral Therapy
Authors: Tanguy Maurice, Orane Lemetayer, Aurélien Binet, Céline Bonduelle, Albert Sotto, Maxime Vallée
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRecurrent cystitis (RC) impacts both individual and societal levels due to the associated antibiotic prescriptions. However, the diagnosis is sometimes made incorrectly. The aim of this study was to evaluate the final diagnosis retained and the effectiveness of specific management through urinary behavioral therapy (UBT) in a cohort of women consulting for urinary tract infections (UTI).MethodsThis was an observational, retrospective, bi-centric study including women consulting for UTI between March 2019 and August 2024. A total of 255 patients were identified, excluding men and pyelonephritis cases, while including 176 women consulting for RC. In the event of an acquired functional lower urinary tract dysfunction, treatment was managed by means of UBT (voiding volume <400 ml, interval between two voids < 4 h, 24-h voided volume ≥ 1.5 L and ≤ 2 L).ResultsA total of 77 patients (45%) had a confirmed diagnosis of RC with no other associated diagnosis. Urethral burning during urination was found in 66 (86%) patients diagnosed with “isolated RC.” Among the patients diagnosed with isolated RC, 52 were followed for 4 months or more, and therapeutic UBT was proposed to 30 of these patients, with complete disappearance of RC in 17 (56.7%) and partial recovery in 11 (36.7%). Among the patients (n = 109) followed for at least 4 months (median: 7 months), regardless of diagnosis, 92 (84.4%) achieved complete or partial recovery after appropriate management of the pathology.ConclusionIn our cohort, 55% of patients consulting for RC ultimately had an alternative diagnosis. In cases of functional RC, UBT resulted in complete or partial improvement in 93% of the women followed for more than 4 months.Trial RegistrationApproved by the French Data Protection Authority (CNIL, Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés) and registered under number 30802498.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40121-026-01444-y.Keywords: Urinary tract infection, Recurrent cystitis, Antimicrobial resistance, Urinary behavioral therapy

Introduction

Recurrent urinary tract infections (rUTIs) constitute a public health issue at both the individual and societal levels. Each year, more than 150 million people worldwide experience at least one episode of UTI [1, 2]. UTIs predominantly affect women, with nearly one out of two experiencing at least one episode during their lifetime, and 25–30% develop recurrent infections (rUTIs) [3, 4], which negatively impact quality of life, generate substantial healthcare costs, and represent a potential driver of antimicrobial resistance.

Acute recurrent bacterial cystitis (RC) in women is defined as recurrent, according to the SPILF–AFU guidelines, when at least four episodes of cystitis occur per year [5]. If the patient experiences more than one episode per month, continuous antibiotic prophylaxis may be considered. Otherwise, management is individualized or based on patient-initiated therapy [5]. The situation frequently leads to repeated antibiotic prescriptions.

While the relevant pathophysiology remains poorly understood, repeated antibiotic prescriptions may induce long-term alterations of the microbiota, leading to dysbiosis that can contribute to various disorders, including functional gastrointestinal symptoms, food sensitivities, autoimmune diseases, and overactive bladder (OAB) [6–9]. Recurrent antibiotic use may also promote the development of multidrug-resistant microorganisms [10]. In a context where antimicrobial resistance has been identified by the WHO as one of the top ten global public health threats, with an estimated 1.91 million deaths projected by 2050 [11, 12], the need for alternative therapeutic strategies is becoming increasingly crucial.

Although all physicians learn about management of acute bacterial cystitis early in their medical training, the diagnosis is not always straightforward. Current guidelines provide limited specificity regarding initial management [5, 13, 14]. Diagnostic assessment can at times be challenging, and some patients may be incorrectly labeled as having recurrent cystitis [15].

Moreover, among women with true recurrent cystitis, a functional cause is often identified, frequently involving an acquired functional lower urinary tract dysfunction [15]. The normal voiding cycle implies regular bladder emptying while avoiding excessive bladder filling. However, many patients exhibit prolonged voiding intervals, unbalanced urine output (either low or, conversely, excessive), as well as large bladder capacities suggestive of reduced bladder sensation.

The objective of this study was to assess the final diagnosis established in patients initially referred for rUTIs, and to evaluate the effectiveness of urinary behavioral therapy (UBT) in this cohort when indicated.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective, descriptive observational, bicentric study (at the university hospitals of Poitiers and Nîmes) between March 2019 and August 2024. The inclusion criteria were all patients consulting a senior urologist or infectious disease specialist for a complaint of rUTIs. The exclusion criteria were men and persons with febrile UTIs.

We collected patient demographic data (age, comorbidities, medical history, etc.), along with the symptoms reported during cystitis episodes. Data from voiding diaries, clinical examination findings, and cystoscopy were also recorded when available.

Patients were classified according to the final diagnosis established after evaluation by a senior infectious disease specialist or urologist. Two groups were defined: patients with an isolated final diagnosis of recurrent cystitis, and patients with a diagnosis other than recurrent cystitis or with recurrent cystitis associated with at least one additional diagnosis. No specific or predefined criteria were established to assign patients to either group. Classification was based on the expert clinical judgment of the attending urologist or infectious disease specialist, after clinical and paraclinical analysis.

In cases of recurrent cystitis with an identified functional etiology, management consisted in UBT. Developed by one of the authors (MV), this therapeutic approach, aims to teach a new voiding pattern based on the following recommendations: a voided volume not exceeding 400 mL, a maximum daytime voiding interval of 4 h, and a target of 24-h voided volume between 1.5 L and 2.0 L [15].

We focused on the final diagnosis, in conjunction with patients’ symptoms and characteristics according to diagnosis. In addition, we evaluated the outcomes of UBT in cases of recurrent cystitis of functional origin. To optimally assess UBT and to ensure appropriate implementation of the intervention, the monitored patients had to use voiding diaries. Complete resolution was defined as the absence of cystitis episodes during follow-up, while partial improvement was defined as a reduction in cystitis episodes of at least 50%.

Quantitative data were described using means or medians, as appropriate. Qualitative data were expressed as percentages. For statistical analyses, Fisher’s exact test was used for qualitative variables, the Mann–Whitney U test for unpaired quantitative variables, and the Wilcoxon test for paired quantitative variables. The level of statistical significance was set at 5%.

Results

We identified 255 patients who met the inclusion criteria, among whom 79 were excluded due to their meeting the exclusion criteria (25 men and 54 patients consulting for recurrent acute pyelonephritis or another reason). In total, 176 patients consulting for recurrent acute bacterial cystitis were included, among whom 77 (45%) were ultimately diagnosed with isolated recurrent cystitis. The remaining patients received either an alternative diagnosis or a diagnosis of recurrent cystitis associated with another one (Fig. 1). The main identified differential diagnosis was OAB, with 36 patients (21%) having an isolated diagnosis of OAB and 14 (8%) having OAB associated with recurrent cystitis.

The median age at first consultation in our cohort was 62 years (range, 13–84). Patients sought medical attention after an average disease duration of 5 years. Diabetes was more prevalent in the recurrent cystitis group. Overall, among patients monitored for more than 4 months, 95 (85.6%) achieved either complete or partial recovery. In the recurrent cystitis group, 28 patients (93%) followed for more than 4 months and managed with UBT experienced either partial or complete recovery.

Discussion

Our study confirms our initial hypothesis. The diagnosis of recurrent acute bacterial cystitis is often incorrectly established. In our population, less than half of the patients had a final confirmed diagnosis of isolated recurrent cystitis (45%). In its 2019 guidelines [16], the AUA highlighted the difficulty of distinguishing true bacterial cystitis from lower urinary tract symptoms associated with asymptomatic bacteriuria. The presence of lower urinary tract symptoms in women, even when associated with bacteriuria, should not automatically lead to a diagnosis of cystitis. Numerous differential diagnoses can also explain these symptoms (OAB, CPPS, urethritis, and sexually transmitted infections …).

No symptom specific to RC was identified in our study. However, urethral burning during micturition was more frequently observed in patients with RC compared with the rest of the population. Nevertheless, this symptom is not pathognomonic for recurrent cystitis, as it was reported by more than half of the patients in the rest of the population.

We observed greater antibiotic efficacy in the RC group compared with the rest of the population, which tends to support the appropriateness of the patient classification into different groups. This may also reflect an interpretative bias, as the perceived efficacy of antibiotics could have influenced the clinician’s confirmation of bacterial cystitis.

The main differential diagnosis identified in our study was OAB. This can be explained by the fact that cystitis can induce transient bladder overactivity. The two pathologies share common symptoms, namely urgency and pollakiuria, although their prevalence is higher in OAB, which is now a well-recognized condition, but its initial management remains heterogeneous [17]. In their study, Gamé et al. [18] highlighted the challenges faced by physicians, particularly general practitioners, in managing this condition.

In cases of lower urinary tract symptoms associated with bacteriuria, many physicians conclude that an infection is present and prescribe antibiotics, fearing the development of secondary acute pyelonephritis in the absence of treatment. However, this situation is exceptional, and the absence of antibiotic therapy does not appear to increase the risk [19]. A positive urine culture often leads to a preference for a diagnosis of cystitis, even though the prevalence of urinary colonization is high in certain populations [14, 20]. Antibiotic treatment for cystitis has the purpose of reducing the duration of symptoms in an infection that resolves spontaneously within A0 days in nearly 90% of cases [21].

While observed greater antibiotic efficacy in the RC group, 49 patients (49%) in the other group likewise reported a response. It can be assumed that antibiotics transiently reduce local inflammation. Response to antibiotic treatment should not automatically lead to a diagnosis of RC, particularly when the reported efficacy is brief, lasting only a few days. Conversely, lack of response should prompt the clinician to investigate alternative diagnoses.

The complexity of diagnosing cystitis is also reflected in our study. Indeed, we observed differences between patients recruited in Poitiers and those included in Nîmes. The proportion of symptoms reported by patients diagnosed with RC varies by center, although antibiotic efficacy was similar. Thereby, a diagnosis of cystitis cannot be based solely on symptomatology but should be considered in the framework of a comprehensive clinical evaluation. It should be remembered that, even when recurrent, bacterial cystitis is an acute phenomenon. The primary symptom remains urethral burning during micturition. Except in rare cases, a diagnosis of cystitis should probably not be made in the absence of urethral burning.

In international guidelines [22], the diagnosis of CPPS requires a sterile urine culture. Our study shows that this diagnosis can exist in the presence of bacteriuria. Therefore, nuances should be introduced into the guidelines to clearly distinguish urinary colonization from UTI.

Among the defense mechanisms against infections, adequate fluid intake combined with regular bladder emptying appears to allow physiological functioning of the urinary tract, which may reduce UTIs by facilitating the elimination of non-adherent or weakly adherent microorganisms from the bladder mucosa [23]. Further studies are needed to confirm these hypotheses. In their study, Hooton et al. demonstrated that increased fluid intake (1.5 L in addition to usual fluid intake) reduced the risk of RC in women with a history of frequent cystitis who consumed less than 1.5 L of fluids per day [24]. They identified a population with insufficient urine output (mean, 900 mL/day), which was not the case in our study, as we sometimes included women with excessive urine output or with an insufficient number of voids relative to their level of hydration.

Voiding disorders (dysuria, reduced or absent bladder sensation) are consequently among the main risk factors for UTI [25]. UBT allows modification of voiding habits by promoting adequate hydration (1.5–2 L), more frequent voiding (not exceeding 4 h), and avoiding high bladder volumes (maximum 400 mL).

In the RC population managed with UBT and monitored for more than 4 months, we observed 57% complete recovery and 37% partial recovery. Analysis of voiding diaries after UBT shows that these results were achieved even though patients had not yet fully implemented UBT. In fact, despite improvements in voiding patterns, the UBT targets had yet to be fully reached. This may be explained by the relatively short follow-up and the difficulty patients experience in modifying voiding habits, which have been established since early childhood, making behavioral changes particularly challenging.

The socialization of voiding, particularly in young girls, appears to play a role in the establishment of these risky behaviors. Due to cultural and societal constraints, achieving physiological voiding in all circumstances is more difficult for women than for men. Women voiding requires a seated position to allow complete bladder contraction and full relaxation of the urethral striated sphincter and pelvic floor muscles. However, access to clean toilets is sometimes difficult or limited (e.g., during work shifts). From childhood, young girls learn to hold urine or to adopt a “skying” position to avoid touching the toilet seat. Several studies have reported that many adolescent girls avoid school toilets due to lack of hygiene, privacy, or fear of being stigmatized [26, 27]. This avoidance leads to voluntary retention, which, if chronic, can result in vesico-sphincter dysfunction, reduced or absent bladder sensation, or even chronic retention, all factors that increase the risk of UTIs. UBT allows modification of these risky behaviors by setting clear, easily measurable goals for patients, the adherence to which can be monitored through voiding diaries.

Our study demonstrates that management of patients consulting for recurrent cystitis can be achieved without repeated antibiotic prescriptions or antibiotic prophylaxis. All the patients managed with UBT were recruited in Poitiers by the same urologist (MV). When comparing the RC populations from Nîmes and from Poitiers, we observed an identical rate of complete recovery, despite a higher rate of antibiotic discontinuation in Poitiers. UBT allows for antibiotic sparing while achieving clinical recovery.

From an economic perspective, UBT appears attractive insofar as it reduces the need for antibiotic prescriptions. For instance, prophylaxis with fosfomycin-trometamol costs on average €292 per patient per year, while trimethoprim costs approximately €60 per patient per year.

Antibiotic use is not without consequences. Beyond short-term adverse effects (e.g., gastrointestinal disturbances, etc.), it may promote the development of conditions such as OAB, particularly through alterations of the urinary microbiota [28]. It is therefore important to promote antibiotic sparing as much as possible, particularly insofar as it contributes to the prevention of antimicrobial resistance. UBT permits the management of recurrent cystitis without antibiotic prescriptions.

Our study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective observational study. Longer follow-up could increase the recovery rate and confirm these results over the long term, including the maintenance of prolonged clinical recovery. Indeed, modification of voiding behaviors that have been established since childhood requires time. The findings are also limited by the small cohort, and the lack of a control group. In our study, UBT was managed predominantly by a single investigator without a comparator group. A multicenter study involving a larger number of urologists and infectious disease specialists would help reduce selection and interpretation biases, as well as center effects. In fact, we observed some differences between our two centers, both in the distribution of patients among groups and in the proportion of symptoms reported within each group. A prospective study using a standardized questionnaire to include patient-reported outcomes assessing the acceptability of UBT, its practical implementation challenges, and its impact on quality of life could be of interest.

Conclusions

The diagnosis of recurrent acute bacterial cystitis is often incorrectly established. In our cohort, only 45% of patients had a confirmed diagnosis of isolated RC. In most cases, a diagnosis of acute bacterial cystitis should not be made in the absence of urethral burning during micturition.

In cases of confirmed RC, urinary behavioral therapy seems to allow recovery in patients with an acquired functional lower urinary tract dysfunction. While this treatment requires consultation time and active patient participation in management, it has the advantage of being cost-free and without adverse effects, especially by avoiding repeated antibiotic prescriptions. Although these findings are encouraging, further prospective studies are warranted to confirm the efficacy of this therapeutic approach.
