Assisted Reproductive Technology as Predictor for Preeclampsia: A Systematic Review of Current Clinical Prediction Models
Authors: Géraldine C M Lafeber, Marie-Louise P van der Hoorn, Saskia le Cessie, Eileen E L O Lashley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssisted Reproductive Technology (ART) increases the risk of preeclampsia (PE), yet no specific prediction model tailored to the ART population exists. This review evaluates prediction models incorporating ART as a predictor, focusing on risk of bias, performance, generalizability and clinical applicability. A systematic MEDLINE search up to July 2023 identified models predicting PE that included ART as a predictor. Exclusion comprised studies on outcomes after PE onset, univariable models and validation studies of previously validated models. Risk of bias, applicability and generalizability were assessed using the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST) and the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis and Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guideline. Out of 15,305 records, 161 articles were assessed in full-text, yielding fifteen eligible studies. Eleven studies were added from a prior systematic review. Performance varied widely (AUC 0.58–0.99) and only few (7/26) models had been externally validated. Risk of bias was unclear in most studies (21/26), due to analytical issues. Despite ART being an established risk factor for PE, robust and generalizable models with proven external validity are lacking. Future research should prioritize development of a high quality prediction model including ART, or a model developed specifically within ART populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s43032-026-02168-7.Keywords: Assisted Reproductive Technology, Epidemiology, Preeclampsia, Prediction model, Risk stratification, Systematic review

Introduction

Infertility affects 8–12% of couples worldwide. Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART), posing these couples an opportunity to conceive [1], is becoming increasingly successful. Each year, over 3 million ART cycles are reported [1], reflecting an ongoing expansion of indications.

While ART has transformed the landscape of reproductive health, it has also introduced new challenges related to potential adverse obstetric outcomes. Studies indicate that pregnancies resulting from ART have a nearly doubled risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, including preeclampsia (PE) [2–4], compared to naturally conceived pregnancies. PE, characterized by hypertension accompanied by proteinuria, maternal end-organ dysfunction, or uteroplacental dysfunction [5], remains one of the leading causes of maternal mortality when left untreated or not recognized in time [6]. Moreover, hypertensive complications in pregnancy are associated with long-term maternal risks, including increased cardiovascular disease and decreased quality of life [7–9]. Additionally, severe PE increases the risk of fetal growth restriction and preterm birth due to underlying placental insufficiency [10–12], highlighting the need for timely identification and management, especially in ART pregnancies.

Several theories have been proposed to explain the increased risk following ART. The need for ART itself may reflect underlying reproductive characteristics associated with an increased risk of PE, including advanced maternal age, nulliparity after prolonged infertility, and infertility-related diagnoses [3, 4]. Moreover, recent evidence suggest that specific ART related factors, such as programmed frozen embryo transfer cycles lacking a corpus luteum and its vasoactive products, and multiple gestation resulting from multiple embryo transfers, may causally contribute to PE risk [1]. Finally, altered maternal–fetal immune interactions, as reflected in oocyte donation, have been hypothesized to play a role in PE development [8, 10, 12].

While many prediction models for PE exist, none specifically address ART populations [13]. Initially, this review aimed to assess models developed exclusively for ART pregnancies, but their absence required a broader approach. Therefore, this review identifies prediction models for preeclampsia incorporating ART as a predictor and evaluates their risk of bias, performance, generalizability, applicability and reproducibility using TRIPOD and PROBAST.

Materials and Methods

This study was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Statement guidelines [14]. This review is registered in PROSPERO (ID CRD420250477748). Registration was completed in 2025 after the process of study selection. No deviations from the registered protocol were made.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

A systematic literature search was conducted in MEDLINE, covering the period from January 2017 – July 31 st 2023, to update the search from De Kat et al. (2019) [13]. While De Kat et al. identified general prediction models for PE and selected only models with AUC ≥ 0.6, this review specifically focuses on models that included ART. The search strategy included free-text terms as ‘prediction model’ and ‘preeclampsia’. The complete search string is provided in Supplement 1.

Two researchers (GL and EELOL) independently performed the literature search. Results were exported into a citation management software (Endnote), and articles were subsequently screened by both researchers. The screening process involved two stages: first, titles and abstracts were reviewed; second, full-text articles were analyzed. Any discrepancies between the researchers were resolved through consensus meetings. Additionally, the full-text of the studies included in the review by De Kat et al. [13] were analyzed and incorporated into the final study selection if the prediction models presented included ART as predictor.

Eligibility Criteria

We included studies that developed (development only) or validated (validation only), or both developed and validated a prediction model for PE, with the definition as used by the authors and with ART as a predictor in the final model. If a validation study of a previously developed model within the inclusion period was available, we included both the development and corresponding validation study.

ART was defined as any fertility-related treatment in which hormonal stimulation takes place [15]. For the current review, studies that reported mode of conception rather than explicitly identifying ART as a predictor were also included. Studies published in English were eligible for inclusion. In the first stage, all prediction models for PE were considered. In the second stage, we determined whether ART was included in the final model. Timing of use of the prediction model was preconceptionally or in early pregnancy.

Exclusion criteria were studies focusing on outcome after PE onset, such as publications on the fullPIERS model [16]. We also excluded studies that focused solely on single variables, such as biomarkers [17], without formulating a prediction model. Furthermore, studies testing the performance of previously externally validated models, such as those from the Fetal Medicine Foundation, were excluded unless they involved adding at least one new variable or extended the original model. Systematic reviews, case reports, guidelines and letters were also excluded.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias assessment for this systematic review was performed using the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST) [18, 19], a framework specifically designed for evaluating prediction models. PROBAST involves risk of bias assessment across four domains: participants, predictors, outcome and analysis. In addition, it assesses applicability of prediction models. PROBAST does not address confounding, as it is not relevant in prediction studies.

Notably, De Kat et al. [13] employed the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool for their quality assessment. Although QUIPS evaluates six domains, it was not designed for scoring prediction models. Accordingly, the studies included from De Kat et al. [13] were also assessed on bias using PROBAST.

Risk of bias was rated low (+), unclear (?) or high (-). The overall rating reflects an aggregate assessment across all domains. For example, a study rated as “?” in the Analysis domain was still classified as overall “ + ” when the remaining domains were all rated “ + ”. Conversely, a study with “?” in the Analysis domain was rated overall “-” when the other domains were consistently “-”.

For the PROBAST tool, studies were classified having a low risk of bias if appropriate data sources, such as cohort studies were used, and included participants who corresponded to unselected participants representative of the target population. Predictors were required to have clear and consistent definitions, assessed uniformly across all patients. Continuous and categorical predictors had to be handled appropriately, by avoiding data-driven categorization and properly addressing non-linearity. Outcome information should not have been used during predictor assessment, and all predictors should have been available at the time the model was intended to be used. Outcomes were determined objectively, following established guidelines or prior publications on the topic and were assessed uniformly across all participants without knowledge of predictor information. The time interval between predictor assessment and outcome determination needed to be appropriate to ensure the recording of relevant and representative outcomes. Studies were also required to have an adequate sample size, with at least 20 events per variable and continuous variables could not be categorized into two or more groups when included in the model. Missing data for predictors or outcomes had to be appropriately handled, either through explicit reporting of minimal missing data or the application of multiple imputation. Predictor selection based solely on univariable analysis was considered inappropriate. Complexities in the data, such as non-linearity or interactions, had to be identified and addressed, and model performance was required to be evaluated using metrics like calibration and discrimination. Internal validation techniques, including bootstrapping or cross-validation, were expected, with adjustments for optimism or overfitting. Finally, predictors and their assigned weights in the final model needed to align with the results of reported multivariable analysis. Validation could not proceed if any predictor data required by the model are missing, as this compromises the assessment of the model's performance and reliability.

Applicability was rated as low (+), unclear (?) or high (-) concern regarding the relevance of the model to the review question. Judgements were based on whether the included participants and setting matched the review question, whether the definition, assessment or timing of predictors in the model match the review question, and whether the outcome, its definition, timing or determination match the review question. Detailed overview of domain specific assessments and derivation of the overall ratings are provided in Supplement 2.

Risk of bias and applicability were independently assessed by GL and EELOL. Any discrepancies in scoring were discussed with a third observer (MLvdH) in a consensus meeting.

Assessment of Reporting Quality

The quality of reporting in the prediction models was assessed using the TRIPOD statement [20], which includes a checklist of 22 items and 15 subitems focused on key reporting aspects of title/abstract, introduction, methods, results and discussion. GL and EELOL assessed each study, marking each item as ‘yes’ if it was sufficiently reported or ‘no’ if it was not. Any discrepancies in scoring were discussed with a third observer (MLvdH) in a consensus meeting. A reporting quality score was calculated based on the number of items that were met. Although this score is not a validated measure for prediction models, it has been used in other studies to summarize reporting quality [21–23].

Data Extraction

GL extracted data from all selected articles, using the Checklist for Appraisal and data extraction for systematic Reviews on prediction Modelling Studies [24]. Data extraction comprised study design, participants, predictors and outcome, the timepoint at which the prediction model is intended to be applied, model development methods, model performance and validation statistics, and means for clinical application. In studies with multiple models, only those with ART in the final predictor selection were extracted. Additionally, the impact of ART and its significance to the model's accuracy and predictive power were assessed. Data were synthesized narratively and presented in tables.

Model Performance

As model performance measures, metrics of discrimination and calibration were extracted. Discrimination measures the model’s ability to distinguish between patients who experienced the outcome and those who did not, typically assessed using the concordance statistic (c-statistic), which represents the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. A c-statistic of 1 indicates perfect discrimination, while a value of 0.5 indicates no better than random guessing. Calibration assesses the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcome frequencies, often visualized with a calibration plot that compares observed frequencies against predicted probabilities [25]. In case c-statistics and calibration slopes were not provided, detection rates (DR) and false positive rates (FR) were reported.

Results

Study Selection

The study selection process is illustrated according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) flowchart [26] (Fig. 1). The initial search yielded 15,305 records. After removing duplicates, 14,682 articles advanced to the first screening stage, where titles and abstract were assessed. This resulted in the exclusion of 14,521 articles, leaving 161 for full-text assessment in the second stage. Some studies proposed a model using Bayesian analysis [27] with prior risks based on ‘prior risk factors’ or ‘maternal factors’, though these factors were not always specified [28–32]. Following correspondence with the authors, studies in which ART was not considered a prior ‘maternal factor’, or where authors did not respond, were excluded [28–30]. Ultimately, 16 studies from this search met the eligibility criteria [31–46].

Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses flowchart

Additionally, full-text screening of the 70 studies included in the research by De Kat et al. [13] identified 10 extra studies with ART defined as predictor [47–56]. Consequently, a total of 26 articles were included in the current review.

Study Characteristics, Sample Size and Modelling Method

Studies were published between 2009 and 2023. Eleven studies were conducted across multiple centers [31, 34, 35, 39, 41, 45, 49, 52, 53, 55, 56]. The number of participants ranged from 94 [43] to 141.299 [42], with in total 625.001 participants of whom 20.142 developed PE. None of the studies performed a sample size calculation prior to model development. The majority of studies was conducted in Europe [32, 34, 35, 37, 38, 41–44, 47–56], six studies in Asia [31, 33, 36, 45, 46, 57] and one in North America [39]. Key elements of the included studies are summarized in Tables 1 and 2, which distinguish between Bayesian and frequentist models. In prediction model development and validation, frequentist methods estimate parameters based on observations only, assuming true parameters are fixed but unknown. These methods use metrics like confidence intervals and p-values. In contrast, Bayesian methods incorporate prior knowledge, updating beliefs as new data become available, resulting in probability distributions for model parameters [27].

Some studies applied machine learning such as random forest [37, 40], XGBoost [40] or support vector machines [40] to build prediction models.

Definition of Outcome

Definitions of PE varied among studies. While most studies defined PE as hypertension with proteinuria [37, 40, 56], several studies followed definitions based on different guidelines: Brown et al. (2001) [34, 35, 37, 49, 50, 55, 66], ISSHP (1988) [54, 67], Poon et al. (2019), FIGO [38, 68], Magee et al. (2014) [39, 59], and Brown et al. (2018) [44, 63]. The study by Hromadnikova [43] specified HELLP as primary outcome, following the Mississippi and Tennessee classifications [61, 62]. For participant definitions, some studies [31, 42, 43, 46, 51] provided only a limited definition of eligibility criteria, with little to no description of the study population.

Other studies developed competing risk models based on the hypothesis that PE can arise either before or after delivery. It is assumed that if the pregnancy were to continue indefinitely, all women would develop PE, resulting in a competition between the timing of delivery – whether it occurs before or after the onset of the condition. Consequently, some models used gestational age (GA) at the onset of PE as primary outcome [32, 34, 35, 48].

Timing at which the prediction model is intended to be used varied between studies and was not always clearly described [37, 40, 42, 44]. Most models were intended to be used at the end of the first trimester (GA 10–11 weeks) or the beginning of the second trimester (GA 12–13 weeks) [31, 34–36, 38, 39, 43, 47–53, 55, 69]. Models presented in two studies [41, 46] were intended for early first trimester. One model was intended for use between GA16-20 weeks [33], and one for 19–23 weeks [32]. One study published multiple models for different timepoints (GA 19–20, 23–24, 27–28) [56].

Predictors

Predictors included clinical parameters, biomarkers or a combination of both. Most commonly used biomarker predictors were Uterine Artery Pulsatility Index (UtA-PI), mean arterial pressure (MAP), placental growth factor (Pl-GF), pregnancy associated plasma protein A (PAPP-A). ART definition varied among different studies.

Bayesian models calculated the a-priori risk based on selected ‘maternal factors’, which – after clarification from the authors – consistently included maternal age, weight, height, racial origin, interpregnancy interval, GA at delivery of previous pregnancy with and without PE, method of conception, chronic hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)/anti-phospholipid syndrome (APS), dichorionic and monochorionic twins, and MoM biomarker values [31, 32, 34, 35] [58, 69]. These biomarker measurements were standardized to multiples of the expected normal mean (MoM) [70], adjusted for factors such as maternal age, BMI, smoking and racial origin. In these studies, method of conception was included in the a-priori risk assessment. After author inquiry for nine studies, method of conception was categorized in spontaneous, ovulation induction and IVF [31, 32, 34, 35, 47–50, 52, 53]. One study defined ART as conception by IVF (yes/no) [36]. For two studies ART definition could not be retrieved [33, 51].

In most frequentist models, ART was not specified further than requiring ART (yes/no) [37–40, 43, 44, 46, 52, 56]. In two models, ART was defined as requiring IVF (yes/no) [42, 45]. In two studies, ART was categorized in spontaneous, requiring IVF or ovulation induction [41, 54]. For categorical variables, details on coding were not specified (e.g. categorization or use of dummy variables).

Only seven out of 26 studies described the variable selection and model building process in detail [37, 38, 40, 45, 46, 52, 55]. Only three out of 26 papers provide the full model in their paper [48, 54, 55] with individual coefficients. Reporting of unadjusted associations between predictor and outcome was also limited [31–33, 35, 36, 38, 44, 56]. Only one study by Lewandowska et al. [38] reported the effect of ART on model performance. Here, performance did not improve by including ART to the model.

Performance, Generalizability, and Applicability

Due to substantial heterogeneity in study populations, predictors and outcome definitions, a meta-analysis was not performed. Instead, performance, generalizability and applicability are presented narratively. Performance of all included models is summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Model performance with regard to discrimination, including receiver operating curves and c-statistic values with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was provided in all studies. However, calibration information was limited. One study used Hosmer–Lemeshow test p-values to support model fit to observed outcomes [44], while another briefly mentioned calibration, but without supporting statistics [42]. O’Gorman et al. (2017) [53] and Hromadnikova et al. (2023) [43] reported highest AUCs (Tables 1 and 2). Model performance of Bayesian models improved with addition of biomarkers to maternal factors alone [31, 32, 34–36, 48, 49, 52, 53].

None of the studies presented prediction models as online calculators for health professionals or patients. The majority were ‘development only’ studies [32, 33, 35, 37–39, 41–44, 47–49, 54–56], with a few exceptions providing external validation. Park et al. (2013) [50] provided external validation of Poon et al. (2009) [47], Skråstad et al. (2015) [51] validated Wright et al. (2012) [48], O’Gorman et al. (2017) [53] validated Akolekar et al. (2013) [71]. The latter was not included in this review as it was omitted from de Kat et al.’s initial selection. After correspondence with the author [13], it was clarified that the exclusion was due to an incomplete description of the study population and an unclear overlap between populations of different studies. Fransisco et al. (2017) [34] validated Francisco et al. (2017) [35]. Of the validation studies (n = 7), only Zhou et al. [45] showed a comparison with the development data of the distribution of predictors and outcome.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The PROBAST tool comprises the following domains: participant selection, predictor assessment, outcome determination, and analytical methods [18]. Results for all included studies are summarized in Table 3. Many studies exhibited a moderate risk of bias, primarily due to analytical issues. For instance, some studies excluded patients with missing outcome data without motivation [31, 33, 38, 40, 41, 46, 47, 50]. Additionally, some studies reported a limited number of events per variable, raising the risk of model overfitting [31, 34, 35, 40, 41, 44–46, 50, 51, 56]. Concerns about applicability also arose, particularly where predictor definitions were unclear and required author correspondence for clarification of ‘maternal factors’ [31–36, 47, 48, 50–53]. Some studies lacked adequate outcome definitions [42, 43], and it was often unclear whether data-driven categorization was avoided or non-linearity of predictors had been addressed. Detailed overview of domain specific assessments and derivation of the overall ratings are provided in Supplement 2.

Quality of Reporting

Regarding reporting quality, each study was assessed using the TRIPOD checklist, with individual scores available in Supplement 3. Most studies were considered development-only [32, 33, 35, 37–39, 41–44, 46–49, 52, 54–56], hence nine subitems of the TRIPOD checklist were deemed non-applicable for these studies [23]. Additionally, as no therapeutic studies were included, the sub-item related to medication use was also non-applicable, for all studies. Therefore, the final quality of reporting score was calculated as the number of items met divided by the total number of scorable items. For development-only studies, the total number of items was 27, while for validation studies, the total number was 36. The percentage of TRIPOD checklist items met for each of the 26 included studies is shown in Table 4, ranging from 41% [43] to 89% [37]. The most frequently missing items included the identification of the study type in the title, blinding in outcome and predictor assessment, clarification of sample size, handling of missing data, handling of predictors in the analysis, description of model updating, identification of differences between the development and validation sets, comparison between these sets, presentation of the full model including coefficients, and providing context for the model’s use and clinical implications.

Discussion

This systematic review highlights the challenges in accurately predicting PE in ART pregnancies. Despite the well-established link between ART and increased PE risk [2–4], prediction models for PE rarely include ART as a predictor. We identified 231 prediction models in literature (161 records identified from Medline from 2017, 70 from the review by De Kat et al.), with none of the models specifically developed for the ART population. In the absence of these studies, we must rely on models that merely include ART as a predictor. We observed that in the 231 identified studies, only a remarkably small proportion of 26 included ART as a predictor. As previously noted by De Kat et al. (2019) [13], the quality of these prediction studies for PE varies widely, with few models fully available, let alone be externally validated or implemented in clinical practice [71]. With the introduction of PROBAST and the TRIPOD checklist to improve prediction model development and reporting, we expected higher-quality studies after the review by De Kat et al. in 2019 [13].

We identified several concerns regarding the methodological quality of the included studies. One significant issue is the inconsistency in definitions of PE across studies. While some of this variation can be attributed to historical shifts in clinical guidelines, many studies deviate from the contemporary definition of PE. Currently, PE is defined according to the ISSHP 2018 guideline, as gestational hypertension accompanied by ≥ 1 of the following new-onset conditions at or after 20 weeks’ gestation: proteinuria, other maternal organ dysfunction, including: acute kidney insufficiency, liver involvement with or without right upper quadrant or epigastric abdominal pain, neurological complications or hematological complications [7, 59]. The lack of outcome standardization complicates the comparability and generalizability of studies and emphasizes the need for a consistent approach to outcome definitions in future model development.

In addition, many studies had insufficient events per variable, which increases the risk of model overfitting and limits generalizability. To develop robust prediction models for binary outcomes like PE ‘yes’ or ‘no’ or time-to-event outcomes such as gestational age at PE onset, a minimum of 10 (preferably 20) events per predictor is often recommended [19]. When developing a prediction model, accurate estimation of predictor effects is crucial and can be supported by choosing the sample size taking the outcome proportion and expected performance into account [72].

In our study, we included both frequentist and Bayesian prediction models. Bayesian models incorporate prior knowledge, and use this knowledge to specify prior probabilities. These priors are then updated with new available data [73], whereas frequentist models calculate predictions solely based on the data at hand. This distinction makes direct comparison between studies using Bayesian and frequentist models challenging. In this review, studies using Bayesian models often lacked specification of maternal factors and standardization of biomarker values into multiples of the mean (MoM), with adjustments for unspecified maternal characteristics. These maternal characteristics were often used as priors, but the composition of these prior and their impact on the resulting models were not well defined. Additionally, it was unclear whether these models followed a two-step or single-step approach. Although we contacted authors of models included in our study to clarify the maternal factors and whether ART was incorporated, this lack of clarity impedes reproducibility of these studies.

Moreover, most studies (19/26) lack external validation, making interpretation of their generalizability difficult. It is widely recommended that a prediction model should not be used in clinical practice before being externally validated in other individuals than those in the training set [74–78]. Finally, proper handling of continuous and categorical variables also varied widely, which may contribute to inaccurate predictions in practice.

We also found significant reporting deficiencies in the included studies. For instance, only seven out of 26 studies detailed their variable selection and model building process. While all studies that specified candidate predictors included ART in the final model, only three reported full models with coefficients available. Reporting of unadjusted predictor-outcome associations was also limited.

Interestingly, the models with highest performance [43, 53] had the lowest reporting scores (Table 4). For these models, it remains unclear whether blinding of outcome and predictor assessment took place, how predictions were calculated, how the model was built, how missing data were handled, and how the prediction model should be used. These limitations in reporting compromise the transparency and reproducibility of the models, potentially hindering their accurate interpretation and appropriate application in clinical practice [77, 79]. Despite the TRIPOD statement [20] publication in 2015, reporting quality seems not improved over time. These findings underscore the need for future prediction studies to enhance transparency and compliance with the TRIPOD statement.

Our systematic review has some limitations. First, we relied exclusively on one database, MEDLINE, for study collection, which may increase the risk of selection bias. Although we screened over 15,000 articles, we acknowledge that due to absence of additional databases such as Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus and grey literature sources, relevant studies may have been missed. Notably, this approach is consistent with that of De Kat et al. [13], who also relied on MEDLINE as their only database.

Furthermore, the extensive screening and appraisal process is resource intensive which resulted in a time gap between completion of the search (July 31 st 2023) and manuscript finalization. As a result, more recent prediction models may have been published after the search end date, that were not included in this review.

Additionally, screening over 15,000 articles raises the potential for error during the selection process. Moreover, our search strategy was based on the approach used by De Kat et al. (2019) [13], but during data extraction, we identified some key studies such as Akolekar et al. [70] and Loughna et al. [80], which were development studies of prediction models that were later validated by O’Gorman et al. (2016) [52] and Skråstad et al. (2015) [51] respectively, and had been omitted by the authors [13]. Also, De Kat et al. included only papers with AUC > 0.6, a threshold which after correspondence with the authors [13] had been pragmatically chosen to ensure clinical relevance and keep the project manageable. This could also have contributed to the absence of abovementioned papers.

For our review,, we included only models in which ART was retained in the final model. Consequently, we did not capture models that initially considered ART as a candidate predictor but later excluded it through backward selection or LASSO. For most prediction models, assessing the effect of ART on performance was impossible, as it was embedded within maternal factors (Bayesian models) without detailed reporting.

Besides, model development and full coefficients were not provided (Supplement 3).

Finally, this systematic review does not assess whether the externally validated prediction models were presented as risk calculator, web tool or implemented in electronic health records.

Our study also has notable strengths, including it being the first review to comprehensively identify prediction models for PE that incorporate ART as a predictor. As we could not endorse a specific model to capture PE risk for ART pregnancies specifically, this study highlights the important gap in current risk assessment strategies for PE in ART pregnancies. The increased PE risk following ART likely reflects both underlying maternal reproductive characteristics and ART specific mechanisms, including programmed embryo transfer without a corpus luteum, multiple gestations, and altered maternal–fetal immune interactions in OD pregnancies [1, 3, 4, 8, 10, 12]. Omitting ART from prediction models may therefore compromise risk assessment in ART pregnancies. Remarkably, models that included ART as a predictor in our study generally treated it as a single entity and rarely distinguished between IVF, ICSI, frozen/thawed embryo transfer and OD, despite substantial variation in PE risk across these treatment modalities. For example, the risk of hypertensive complications in OD is more than doubled compared to conventional IVF and ICSI [11, 81–85]. Embryo donation, or double donation (DD), encompasses possibly an even higher risk due to the separate risk associated with sperm donation [60]. This study therefore provides an important starting point for future research.

Other strengths of this study are the extensive screening of over 15,000 studies, and adherence to the TRIPOD statement, and the use of PROBAST for accurate quality assessment of prediction models.

Future research should focus on developing and externally validating prediction models tailored to ART. In addition, we recommend future prediction models including ART as predictor to disaggregate ART types, such as OD and DD. Improved risk assessment could aid in disease management, support periconceptional counselling, and help minimize donor exposure to the risk associated with oocyte retrieval [86]. Understanding hypertensive risk in ART pregnancies could improve early recognition and insights into PE pathophysiology. Given the poor reproducibility and lack of external validation in these populations, we aim to eventually develop a dedicated prediction model for PE in OD/DD pregnancies, in accordance with to PROBAST and TRIPOD guidelines [64].

Conclusion

This systematic review illustrates the limited availability of robust, ART-specific models that predict the development of PE. Limitations in current studies, including inconsistencies in outcome definitions, insufficient events per variable, lacking external validation, varying approaches to handling continuous and categorical variables and limited reporting quality complicate PE risk assessment in the ART population. Based on current findings, we are unable to endorse any prediction model for clinical practice in the ART population at this time.

Future studies should focus on creating a high-quality prediction model, following PROBAST and TRIPOD guidelines, that incorporates ART as a predictor for PE. Ideally, this model should be developed and validated within ART populations specifically, to improve its accuracy for this high risk group.
