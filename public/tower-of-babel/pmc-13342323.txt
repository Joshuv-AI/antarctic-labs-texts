Risk factors for thrombosis in eosinophilia: A case-control study
Authors: Dongfeng Liang, Zhengyuan Hu, Wencheng Sun, Xiaohui Ma, Xuewen Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sustained eosinophilia can indicate a serious health issue and may elevate the risk of thrombosis. This study aimed to explore potential risk factors of thrombosis in eosinophilia. This case-control study enrolled patients with an absolute eosinophil count of ≥ 500 cells/µL at the First Medical Center of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital between January 2010 and January 2022. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify risk factors for thrombosis in eosinophilia. A total of 88 patients were included, with 40 (45.5%) experiencing thrombosis. The median age of patients with thrombosis was 42.5 years (range: 30.3 to 58.8), and 34 (85.0%) were male. In contrast, the median age of patients without thrombosis was 51.0 years (range: 30.3 to 66.0), with 34 (70.8%) being male. Peripheral neuropathy was significantly more prevalent in patients with thrombosis compared to those without (35.0% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.012) and was identified as an independent risk factor for thrombosis (odds ratio: 3.39; 95% confidence interval: 1.02–11.47; p = 0.045). Peripheral neuropathy may be a risk factor for thrombosis in patients with eosinophilia. This study highlights the importance of identifying patients at risk for thrombosis, providing valuable insights for their management.

Introduction

Eosinophils are a type of white blood cell that play a key role in the immune system, particularly in response to fungal and parasitic infections1. characterized by an elevated number of eosinophils in the peripheral blood, exceeding the normal upper limit of 5% of total leukocytes, with an absolute eosinophil count (AEC) typically ranging from 0.35 to 0.5 × 10⁹/L2,3. Eosinophilia occurs as a clinical manifestation of a wide array of diseases and disorders1. Hypereosinophilic syndromes (HES) are a diverse group of hematologic disorders defined by persistent eosinophilia (> 1.5 × 10⁹/L). Excessive eosinophil production causes tissue damage, particularly in the skin, nerves, lungs, and gastrointestinal system. Eosinophils release prothrombotic substances, increasing the risk of thrombosis4,5.Thrombotic complications are frequently observed in HES, with several studies documenting their prevalence4,6–9. In some studies, approximately 25% of patients with HES experienced thrombosis, and notably, 5–10% of these patients died as a result of this complication7,8.

The pathophysiological mechanism of thrombosis in eosinophilia has not been fully investigated. The release of major basic protein, eosinophilic cationic protein, and eosinophil peroxidase from granules may play a role10,11. Those molecules may predispose patients to thrombosis by acting as platelet agonists, increasing vascular permeability and stimulating the coagulation pathway via the activation of factor XII10. However, there remains limited knowledge regarding thrombosis in eosinophilia, and the risk factors for thrombosis are underreported.

While eosinophilia with identifiable clonal markers has been well documented, this study focuses on patients with eosinophilia lacking identifiable clonal markers to examine the clinical risk factors associated with thrombosis. Clonal eosinophilia is often associated with hematologic malignancies or clonal hematopoietic disorders, while non-clonal eosinophilia is generally linked to reactive or non-malignant conditions4. Methods

Study design and population

This case-control study enrolled patients at the First Medical Center of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital between January 1, 2010 and January 1, 2022. Patients who had absolute eosinophil count of 500 cells/µL (0.5 × 109/L) or greater on at least 1 occasion were enrolled12. The exclusion criteria were: (1) patients younger than 18 years; (2) patients without complete medical records; (3) patients whose eosinophilia was due to hematologic malignant or myeloproliferative neoplasms; (4) patients who had molecular aberration related with hematologic malignant neoplasms such as myelodysplastic syndrome, detected by fluorescent in situ hybridization assay (FISH); (5) patients present with positive antiphospholipid antibody; and (6) patients with other secondary venous thrombosis caused by surgery, infection, immobility and family history). Research ethics approval was granted by the Ethical Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (S2022-562-01) and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and definition

A comprehensive review of the patients’ medical records was conducted by a physician, to obtain demographic details, medical history, laboratory results, relevant images, medications, and treatment response. Comorbidities included atherosclerosis, diabetes, hypertension, infections, and autoimmune diseases, which were identified from the final diagnosis in the medical records. All venous thrombotic events (VTEs) and arterial thrombotic events (ATEs) were confirmed by imaging techniques like ultrasound or computed tomography (CT) angiography (CTA)4. Symptomatic and asymptomatic thrombosis were included. The ATEs included peripheral ATEs, stroke, and myocardial infarction. Stroke was diagnosed by a neurologist according to brain imaging and myocardial infarction was determined by the combination of clinical criteria, elevated serum biomarkers and electrocardiographic changes4. VTEs includes deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism events7.

Information on the multiple organ systems involved in eosinophilia was also collected, including dermatologic, peripheral neuropathy, cardiac, pulmonary, and gastrointestinal manifestations. Dermatologic involvement was defined as biopsy-proven eosinophilic infiltration of the skin or clinically diagnosed by dermatologist. Peripheral neuropathy was defined as particular clinical symptoms such as numbness, weakness, combined with neurogenic injury detected by needle electrode electromyography13. Cardiac involvement was determined by a cardiologist if patient manifested clinical symptoms of myocarditis or unexplained troponin elevation at the time of eosinophilia diagnosis4,14. Pulmonary or gastrointestinal involvement were defined as increased eosinophils on broncho-alveolar lavage fluid or biopsy-proven eosinophilic infiltration of the gastrointestinal tract, respectively15.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS Statistics (version 22; IBM Corp., NY, USA). Quantitative variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), or median (inter- quartile range, IQR) for normally distributed data, respectively. Categorical variables were described as number (%). Independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare quantitative variables between patients with and without thrombosis, while chi-squared tests and Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify risk factors for thrombosis in eosinophilia. Variables that had potential effects on thrombosis or were statistically significant in univariate analysis were included. All analyses were two-sided, with P values less than 0.05 regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 203 patients diagnosed with eosinophilia were considered for inclusion in the study. Patients who were younger than 18 years (n = 8); had incomplete medical records (n = 93); combination with neoplasms (n = 12, four patients had lung cancer, six patients had primary hepatocellular carcinoma, and two patients had brain tumor); and had molecular aberration related with hematologic malignant neoplasms (n = 2) were excluded. Finally, 88 patients were admitted into analysis, among whom 40 cases were complicated with thrombosis. (Fig. 1). Their median age was 42.5 (30.3 58.8), and 34 (85.0%) were male in patients with thrombosis. The median age of the patient without thrombosis was 51.0 (30.3, 66.0), 34 (70.8%) were male. Body mass index (BMI) and smoking status were similar between patients with and without thrombosis. Comorbidities including atherosclerosis (32.5% vs. 18.8%, P = 0.138), diabetes (7.5% vs. 6.3%, p = 0.571), hypertension (25.0% vs. 20.8%, p = 0.642), infection (30.0% vs. 27.1%, p = 0.763), and autoimmune diseases (17.5% vs. 31.3%, p = 0.138), had similar prevalence. Despite a similar prevalence of skin damage (40.0% vs. 52.9%, p = 0.320), cardiac (0% vs. 2.1%, p = 0.545), pulmonary (5.0% vs. 4.2%, p = 0.620), and gastrointestinal involvement (2.5% vs. 4.2%, p = 0.664) in patients with and without thrombosis, the rate of peripheral neuropathy was higher in the thrombosis group (35.0% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.012). Laboratory results

Patients with and without thrombosis shared a similar level of peak leukocyte count (p = 0.805) and AEC (p = 0.789), as well as immunoglobulins (Ig) M, IgG, IgE, creatine, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). However, patients with thrombosis had a borderline increased level of IgA than those without (median concentration 242.0 vs. 177.0 mg/dl, p = 0.054) (Table 1). Twenty-three and 28 patients in the groups with and without thrombotic, respectively, accepted bone marrow exams including aspiration cytology, biopsy, cytogenetic and gene rearrangement analysis. The morphology in both groups revealed normal cellularity with no increase in blast cells. Also, a similar extent of hypereosinophilia was found in both groups (18.4% vs. 20.5% in patients with and without thrombosis, p = 0.776). A normal karyotype was presented in both groups (Table 1).

Factors associated with thrombosis in eosinophilia

Univariate logistic regression analysis revealed that peripheral neuropathy is associated with a higher prevalence of thrombosis in eosinophilia (OR 3.77, 95% CI: 1.29–11.04, P = 0.015). The association between peripheral neuropathy and thrombosis remained significant in multivariate logistic regression analysis, even after adjusting for age, gender, smoking status, atherosclerotic disease, and serum IgA levels (OR = 3.39, 95% CI: 1.02–11.47, p = 0.045).(Table 2).

We also screened the 22 patients with peripheral neuropathy and found that 16 (72.7%) experienced thrombotic events, with 5, 2, and 7 patients present with ATE only, VTE only, and both ATE combined VTE. Given the substantial overlap of arterial and venous involvement, we did not perform separate analyses for ATE or VTE in relation to peripheral neuropathy. Nonetheless, the frequent co-occurrence of ATE and VTE in this subgroup suggests that peripheral neuropathy may reflect a systemic vasculopathic or prothrombotic state rather than a phenotype restricted to a single vascular compartment.

Thrombosis and clinical manifestations

Among the 16 patients with peripheral ATE, 3 (7.5%) and 8 (20.0%) cases were found with thrombosis in the upper and lower extremities, respectively and 5 (12.5%) patients had artery thrombosis in both upper and lower limbs. Furthermore, gangrene occurred in 8 of the 16 patients with peripheral ATE. One case of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), 1 case of superior mesenteric artery thrombosis, and 8 cases of stroke were also observed in patients with ATE. Thrombosis and eosinophilia treatments and response

All patients with thrombosis received anti-platelet/anticoagulation therapy as fundamental treatment, except one patient who had thrombocytopenia. Treatment with a glucocorticoid (GC), GC combined with antihistamine, or immunosuppressant were used in 16 (40.0%), 2 (5.0%), and 11 (27.5%) patients with thrombosis, respectively. For patients without thrombosis, 9 (18.8%) patients received GC monotherapy, 5 (10.4%) patients received antihistamine, 10 (20.8%) patients received GC combined with antihistamine, 19 (39.6%) patients received immunosuppressant, 3 (6.3%) patients received a triple therapy of GC, antihistamine, and immunosuppressant and 1 (2.1%) patient received GC, antihistamine, and anti-plate/anticoagulation. Imatinib was also administered to 1 patient without thrombosis (Table 4).

At least one follow-up visit was undertaken by 26 patients with thrombosis and 40 without thrombosis. Clinical manifestations of thrombosis such as limb edema and pain were alleviated to different extent while gangrene was little changed.

The peak leukocyte count (median count 13.7 vs. 12.0, p = 0.515) and AEC (median count 5.7 vs. 3.5, p = 0.248) were comparable in the two patient groups 1-month before treatment. At the 1-month follow-up visit, most patients had normal AEC (83.3% vs. 73.1%, p = 0.618) regardless of whether they had a thrombotic event or not (Table 4).

Discussion

The study demonstrated that thrombosis was a common complication in patients with eosinophilia. Importantly, multivariate analysis suggested that presence of peripheral neuropathy was associated with higher prevalence of thrombosis in patients with eosinophilia. Consequently, these findings suggest that patients presenting with eosinophilia and peripheral neuropathy should be closely monitored for the risk of thrombosis.

Thrombosis events have been reported to be common in eosinophilia, previous studies reported that perhaps a quarter of HES patients develop thromboembolic complications and that 5–10% died of them8,16. It has been shown that normalization of eosinophil count could prevent the recurrence of venous thrombosis in patients with eosinophilia17. In this study, the results suggested 45.5% of patients experienced a thrombotic event, this high rate may be due to differences in the patient population or in diagnosis methods. To measure the patient outcomes, the short-term prognosis and treatment response was investigated among eosinophilia patients and more importantly, whether the level of peak AEC affects patient response to treatments. Of the 26 cases with thrombosis and 48 controls without thrombosis that were followed for at least one month, there were comparable peak leukocyte count and AEC, and most patients received GC therapy. After 1 month, the treatment resulted in most patients achieving normal AEC, which indicated patients with thrombosis had a similar response to GC than those without thrombosis. However, due to the lack of long-term follow-ups, whether thrombosis in patients with eosinophilia were associated with worse prognosis or frequent flares were still undetermined.

Previous studies that have investigated risk factors for thrombosis in patients with eosinophilia are rare but a genetic basis for an increased risk was identified by Leiva et al.4. Variations commonly associated with clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) including DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and JAK2, and the spliceosome genes SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, and ZRSR2 were associated with high rates of thrombosis and death4. It has also been suggested that a common LNK variant (R262W, T allele) is related to eosinophilia and atherothrombotic disorders18. In this study, the aim was to identify clinical factors related to thrombosis in patients with eosinophilia, so that this might highlight patients at risk of thrombosis and guide their treatment.

In this study population peripheral neuropathy was associated with approximately 2-fold increased prevalence of thrombosis. Peripheral neuropathy is a common manifestation in several eosinophilia-related diseases such as eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA), polyarteritis nodosa (PAN) and other primary vasculitides19. Eosinophils contribute to nerve damage by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines and toxic proteins, leading to endothelial injury, vasculitis, and impaired blood supply to peripheral nerves. This process causes demyelination and axonal damage, resulting in peripheral neuropathy. Elevated eosinophil counts often correlate with disease activity in EGPA and may predict the severity of neurological involvement. Early recognition of this link is crucial for managing both the vasculitic process and associated neuropathy, improving patient outcomes20. It has been shown that neuronal damage could be derived from eosinophil infiltration and their cytotoxic action or vascular-mediated injury through endothelial cells21. However, the peak leukocyte count and AEC were comparable between the two groups with and without thrombosis in this study, which suggests that these factors were not involved in the mechanism of eosinophil infiltration that resulted in thrombosis or peripheral neuropathy. Additionally, ischemic nerve damage is caused by the involvement of epineurial and endoneurial vessels supplying peripheral nerves, so it should be associated with ATEs, not VTEs. In this study, participants with or without thrombosis who exhibited similar clinical characteristics (Age, sex, BMI, comorbidities, etc.) were included for analysis, still, such associations were also observed, indicating unknown mechanisms might exist in peripheral neuropathy and associated thrombosis in eosinophilia. Notably, patients with susceptibility genes were also excluded, which increased the generalization ability of study results for future studies.

The limitations of this study are largely due to the limited sample size and the retrospective nature of the study. In this study, it is not clear whether peripheral neuropathy precedes thrombosis or vice versa, the associations between peripheral neuropathy and ATE or VTE were also not analysed. Therefore, this requires further validation in prospective studies. Moreover, despite efforts to adjust for several significant confounding factors when evaluating the association between peripheral neuropathy and increased prevalence of thrombosis, the possibility of unknown factors influencing the results cannot be excluded. The rarity of eosinophilia and infrequent appearance of thrombosis impeded large-scale analysis. This is clear from previous reports on patients with thrombosis in eosinophilia mainly being case reports22–26. Consequently, knowledge about thrombosis in eosinophilia is quite limited and related risk factors are scarce, in the present study, borderline higher IgA was found in patients with thrombosis, but the underlying mechanism still required further exploration. Although we included several characteristics in the multivariable logistic regression, the results ascertain only one risk factor of thrombosis in patients with eosinophilia, however, the limited sample size and model results prevented us from exploring other potential factors, the relative small sample size may also yield unstable statistical performance, as well as curbing the confounding effects of other variables.

Conclusion

Thrombosis is common in eosinophilia, with deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and arterial thrombosis (ATE) being the most prevalent types. Additionally, peripheral neuropathy may be associated with thrombosis in eosinophilia. This study may help identify patients at risk for thrombosis and guide their treatment.
