Association Between Dietary Tryptophan Intake and Bone Health: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Shan Su, Limin Tian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe relationship between dietary tryptophan intake and the risk of low bone mineral density (LBMD) has not been thoroughly evaluated. This study aimed to examine the relationship between dietary tryptophan intake and LBMD. A total of 12,003 participants aged 50 years and older with complete data on bone mineral density (BMD) and tryptophan intake from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2005 to 2020 were included in this cross-sectional study. The median dietary tryptophan intake among the 12,003 participants was 1822.14 mg/day, with significantly lower levels observed in individuals with LBMD compared to those with normal bone mass (1740.45 mg/day vs. 2041.39 mg/day, p < 0.001). For every 2.7-fold increase in dietary tryptophan intake, the risk of low BMD decreases by 22%. When dietary tryptophan intake was categorized into quartiles, significantly lower risks of LBMD were observed in the third [Odds Ratio (OR) = 0.68, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.51–0.91] and fourth (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.49–0.87) quartiles compared to the reference group after multivariable adjustment. Moreover, the restricted cubic spline (RCS) results revealed a negative nonlinear relationship between dietary tryptophan intake and LBMD (p for overall < 0.001, p for nonlinear < 0.05), with this correlation remaining consistent across various population subgroups and exhibiting no significant interaction according to stratification variables. Sensitivity analyses further substantiated these findings. Overall, we found that increased dietary tryptophan intake may be associated with a lower risk of LBMD among individuals aged ≥ 50 years, highlighting the importance of optimizing tryptophan nutrition for reducing osteoporosis risk.Keywords: Dietary tryptophan intake, Low bone mineral density, Osteoporosis, NHANES

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a systemic bone disease characterized by decreased bone mass and density, leading to increased fragility of bones and a greater risk of fractures [1, 2]. With the aging of the global population, the prevalence of osteoporosis is increasing annually. In the USA, an estimated 10.2 million persons aged 50 years or older suffer from osteoporosis and around 43.3 million individuals (more than 40% of older U.S. adults) have LBMD, which is associated with a high risk of progression to osteoporosis [3]. Osteoporosis-related fractures pose significant hazards, and are among the leading causes of disability and mortality among the elderly [4]. Nutritional elements, food categories, and dietary patterns play crucial roles in maintaining bone health. Consuming a well-balanced diet rich in essential nutrients helps maintain bone homeostasis and prevents the occurrence of fragility fractures [5]. Despite this, clinical practice currently lacks specific dietary recommendations for osteoporosis patients.

Tryptophan, an indispensable and essential amino acid (EAA), relies on dietary intake to serve as a precursor for various bioactive compounds and influences numerous physiological processes, including neuronal function, metabolism, inflammation, oxidative stress, immune response, and gut homeostasis [6]. The level of tryptophan in the body depends on dietary intake and the activity of tryptophan metabolic pathways, such as the kynurenine pathway, serotonin pathway, and indole pathway. Increasing evidence indicates that the dysregulation of tryptophan metabolism plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of diseases, contributing to the progression of disorders in the digestive, urinary, nervous, and skeletal systems [7]. Targeted therapies focusing on tryptophan metabolism may offer novel treatment strategies.

The estimated average requirement (EAR) and recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for tryptophan, as determined by the National Academy of Sciences, are 4 mg/kg*day and 5 mg/kg*day, respectively [8]. Inadequate intake of tryptophan can adversely affect overall health, including bone health. Studies have shown that tryptophan deficiency exacerbates bone loss induced by movement restraint in both the femoral metaphysis and diaphysis, whereas a diet supplemented with tryptophan reversed these effects [9]. Additionally, metabolites derived from different tryptophan metabolic pathways exert varying effects on skeletal health; for example, those from the kynurenine pathway exert negative impacts on bone, whereas those from the indole pathway appear to have a protective effect [10–13]. Despite these findings, few population-based studies have evaluated the correlation between dietary tryptophan intake and the risk of LBMD. To address this research gap, the present study utilized NHANES data from 2005 to 2020 to explore the potential relationship between dietary tryptophan intake and the risk of LBMD, with the goal of better controlling osteoporosis through dietary guidance.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Population

NHANES is a comprehensive examination program that evaluates the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population. This nationally representative survey uses a multistage, stratified sampling method to conduct thorough household interviews and physical examinations. It further collects additional information on demographics, lifestyles, dietary intake, behavioral situations, and medical status from US civilians every two years [14]. Detailed information regarding the survey sampling method and laboratory examinations can be found on the official website (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes). The research protocol of NHANES was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and all participants provided written informed consent before participating in the study.

The present study was based on eight survey cycles of NHANES data from 2005 to 2020, encompassing a total of 76,496 individuals. Individuals who were younger than 50 years (n = 54,883), were corticosteroid users (n = 1085), did not undergo dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) examination (n = 7467), or had missing data on dietary intake (n = 1058) were further excluded. Ultimately, 12,003 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of participant selection from NHANES 2005 to 2020

Outcome Assessment

DXA is the gold standard for assessing bone density, with detailed examination protocols available on the NHANES website. We collected the DXA scan results of the participants’ femoral neck or lumbar spine from NHANES 2005 to 2020. T scores were calculated via the following formula: T score = (BMD participant—mean BMD reference)/SD reference.

Here, BMD participant represents the participant’s measured BMD, the mean BMD reference represents the average BMD of the reference population, and the SD reference represents the standard deviation of peak BMD in the reference population. In the present study, the latest BMD data updated by Xue et al. [15] were adopted as a reference. Osteoporosis was defined as a T score ≤ −2.5, and osteopenia was defined as a T score between −2.5 and −1 at the femoral neck or lumbar spine. LBMD includes both osteoporosis and osteopenia and is defined as a T score ≤ −1 at the femoral neck or lumbar spine.

Assessment of Dietary Tryptophan Intake

The dietary interview component of NHANES reports two 24-h dietary recall interviews for each participant. The first dietary recall is conducted in person at the Mobile Examination Center, whereas the second 24-h recall is collected via telephone interview 3–10 days later. The USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies was used to calculate the nutrient and food components of all foods. The mean of the participants’ two dietary recalls, or the value of one of them (if participants had only one recall), was used as the daily dietary tryptophan intake to mitigate potential recall bias in this study. Dietary tryptophan intake was natural log transformed to minimize the effect of outliers and improve the interpretability of the association results.

Covariates Assessment

Individual characteristics, such as sex (male and female), age (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years), race/ethnicity (Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other), educational level (below high school, high school or equivalent, and college education or above), marital status (single, widowed or divorced, and married), the family poverty income ratio (PIR ≤ 1.3, 1.3–3.5, and ≥ 3.5), physical activity level (inactive, moderate, and active), alcohol consumption (ever consumed 12 drinks), smoking history (ever smoked 100 cigarettes), and milk intake (never or rarely, sometimes, and often), were self-reported. The weight and height of each participant were obtained through physical examination, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m2). Medical history, including diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, stroke, and cardiovascular disease (CVD), was evaluated through self-reports, medication use, or dietary changes due to these conditions. Blood biochemical indicators such as alanine aminotransaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransaminase (AST), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), serum phosphorus, and calcium were also included, with detection methods available on the official website. Additionally, other dietary intake values, including energy, protein, carbohydrates, total fat, sugar, fiber, cholesterol, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, choline, folate, calcium, and phosphorus were considered continuous covariates in the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The complex weighting of NHANES data was performed using the “survey” package in R, with “WTDRD1” or “WTDR2D” used to calculate sample weights from 2005 to 2020. The participants were grouped on the basis of the presence of LBMD and quartiles of dietary tryptophan intake (natural log transformed). Continuous variables, if normally distributed, are represented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the Student’s t test (for two groups); otherwise, the median with interquartile range (IQR) and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test were employed. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (weighted proportions) and were analyzed using the chi-square test.

Weighted multivariable logistic regression models were used to investigate the link between dietary tryptophan intake (as a continuous variable or in quartiles) and the risk of LBMD, and odds ratios (OR) along with 95% confidence interval (CI) were reported. Four models were created: Model 1 did not make any adjustments; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity; Model 3 adjusted for age, sex, race and ethnicity, BMI, educational level, marital status, PIR, alcohol consumption, diabetes, FPG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, ALT, AST, and serum phosphorus; and Model 4 adjusted for the variables in Model 3 plus nutritional elements, including energy, protein, carbohydrate, sugars, fiber, fat, cholesterol, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, folate, choline, calcium, and phosphorus. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were applied to explore the potentially nonlinear relationship between dietary tryptophan intake and LBMD, with adjustments in Model 4.

In the primary analysis, multiple imputation was used to address missing covariates. To ensure the robustness of our study, sensitivity analyses were conducted. These included analyzing the remaining four imputed datasets, excluding individuals with missing covariates, excluding participants with only one dietary recall, and adjusting tryptophan intake using the nutrient residual method after controlling for energy intake. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2; https://www.R-project.org) and a two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic Characteristics of the Study Participants

A total of 12,003 participants aged 50 years and older were included in this study, representing 324,374,491 of the U.S. population. The mean age of our study participants was 60.29 years (SD 9.00), with 6084 males (weighted, 47.9%) and 5919 females (weighted, 52.1%), the majority of whom were non-Hispanic white (74.5%).

In Table 1, we showed the general characteristics of the study population based on whether they had LBMD. Compared with participants with normal bone mass, those with LBMD were older, more likely to be female, predominantly non-Hispanic white, single or divorced, and had lower family income, alcohol consumption, and prevalence of diabetes. They also exhibited lower BMI, FPG, ALT, and AST but higher TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, and serum phosphorus.

The daily dietary intake profiles of the participants are also detailed in Table 1. A comparison of nutrient intake, including total energy, protein, fat, carbohydrates, sugars, fiber, cholesterol, vitamins B6 and B12, folate, choline, calcium, and phosphorus, revealed that individuals with LBMD consumed significantly fewer of these nutrients than those with normal bone mass. Notably, dietary tryptophan intake was markedly lower in the LBMD group (1740.45 mg/day) than in the normal bone mass group (2041.39 mg/day).

Multivariable Logistic Regression Models for the Association Between Dietary Tryptophan Intake and LBMD

The participants were divided into four groups according to the quartiles of dietary tryptophan intake (natural log transformed): Q1 [≤ 7.005 mg/day], Q2 [7.005 mg/day −7.443 mg/day], Q3 [7.443 mg/day −7.869 mg/day], and Q4 [≥ 7.869 mg/day]. As dietary tryptophan intake increased, there was a significant decrease in the weighted prevalence of osteopenia and osteoporosis (p for trend < 0.001, Fig. 2).

Weighted prevalence of osteopenia and osteoporosis across dietary tryptophan intake quartiles

To further examine the relationship between tryptophan and LBMD, four models were constructed on the basis of weighted multivariable logistic regression. A 2.7-fold increase in dietary tryptophan intake was associated with a 22% (95% CI 0.67–0.92) reduction in the risk of LBMD. When tryptophan intake was categorized into 4 levels, higher dietary tryptophan intake was significantly associated with reduced odds of LBMD in the unadjusted Model 1. This association persisted in models 2 and 3 after further adjustment for various demographic characteristics and biochemical indicators. In fully adjusted Model 4, compared with the reference group (Q1), the adjusted ORs (95% CI) for LBMD were 0.68 (95% CI 0.51–0.91) in the third quartile and 0.65 (95% CI 0.49–0.87) in the fourth quartile, respectively (Table 2).

Dietary Tryptophan Intake and Odds of LBMD: a Restricted Cubic Spline Analysis

The dose–response relationship adjusted for all confounders is depicted in Fig. 3, revealing a nonlinear association between tryptophan intake and the risk of LBMD (p for overall < 0.001, p for nonlinear < 0.05). The OR declined steadily with increasing dietary tryptophan intake, reaching its lowest point at 8.12 mg/day (natural log transformed). Beyond this threshold, the OR for LBMD slightly increases with further increases in tryptophan intake.

Stratified and Sensitivity Analyses

No significant interactions were detected between dietary tryptophan intake and any stratification variables concerning the risk of LBMD (Fig. 4). These findings suggest that the association between dietary tryptophan intake and a lower prevalence of LBMD was consistent and robust across all the subgroups.

Subgroup analysis for the association between dietary tryptophan intake with risk of LBMD. Weighted univariate logistic regression was used for subgroup analysis. Adjustments were made for FPG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, ALT, AST, serum phosphorus, intake of total energy, protein, carbohydrate, sugars, fiber, fat, choline, cholesterol, Vitamin B6, Vitamin B12, folate, calcium and phosphorus

We conducted a sensitivity analysis on the association between dietary tryptophan intake and LBMD (Table 3). First, when excluding subjects with missing covariates, we found an inverse correlation between dietary tryptophan intake and LBMD risk across all the models. Specifically, in Model 4, which was adjusted for various covariates, the ORs for LBMD risk were 0.68 (95%CI 0.51–0.91) in the third quartile of dietary tryptophan intake and 0.67 (95%CI 0.50–0.91) in the fourth quartile, both compared to the reference group (p for trend < 0.05). Second, across the remaining imputed datasets, an association in which tryptophan intake was linked with a reduced risk of LBMD was consistently observed. Furthermore, nutrient intake is closely correlated with total energy intake. We further analyzed using the nutrient residual method adjusted for total energy intake, and the results were consistent with the above findings. Finally, the association between dietary tryptophan intake and LBMD risk did not substantially change after participants with only one dietary recall were excluded.

Discussion

Involving 12,003 participants, this study examined the relationship between dietary tryptophan intake and the risk of LBMD among Americans aged 50 years and older. Our findings revealed that increased dietary tryptophan intake was significantly associated with a decreased likelihood of low bone mass. This inverse correlation persisted even after adjusting for multiple confounders, suggesting that increased tryptophan intake may delay bone mass decline and the progression of osteoporosis. Furthermore, dose–response analysis revealed a negative nonlinear relationship, with higher intakes of tryptophan being particularly beneficial up to a certain point. Stratified and sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of our results.

The imbalance between osteoblast-mediated bone formation and osteoclast-mediated bone resorption is the primary mechanism underlying the development of osteoporosis. Amino acids, particularly EAAs, are widely recognized as critical for maintaining bone homeostasis by serving as both energy sources and molecular pathway modulators in bone [16]. In 2022, Lv et al. [17] summarized the role of EAAs in aging bone and reported that lysine, threonine, methionine, tryptophan, and isoleucine promote osteoblast proliferation, activation, and differentiation while reducing osteoclast activity. Additionally, animal studies have indicated that supplementation with methionine can alleviate bone loss induced by caloric restriction. L-arginine [18], on the other hand, enhances bone mechanical responsiveness in healthy, aged, and ovariectomized mice by activating the nitric oxide-calcium-positive feedback loop in osteoblasts and reprogramming osteoclast amino acid metabolism to suppress inflammatory bone loss [19, 20]. These findings have been partially validated in human studies. Mack et al. [21] demonstrated that preadolescent children who received lysine supplements experienced significant improvements in weight and height development, along with significantly higher mean density in the axial section of the radius. In a cohort study involving 111,257 participants and 901 hip fracture cases, lower valine levels were significantly associated with a higher risk of hip fractures [Hazard ratio (HR) 0.79, 95% CI 0.73–0.84, p < 0.001], highlighting that low circulating valine is a robust predictor of hip fracture risk [22]. Similarly, an observational study revealed that higher serum concentrations of valine, leucine, isoleucine, and tryptophan were associated with less decline in hip BMD four years later, and notably, elevated serum tryptophan levels were linked to reduced fracture risk after ten years (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.75–0.98) [23]. Collectively, both in vitro and in vivo studies, alongside population-based research, have consistently confirmed the crucial role of amino acids, especially EAAs, in maintaining bone health. Therefore, an adequate supply of amino acids is crucial to ensure optimal acquisition and remodeling of bone.

Tryptophan serves as a vital precursor for protein synthesis and the synthesis of numerous microbial metabolites within the host [7]. In experimental studies in which rats were given tryptophan-free diets, both decreases in bone formation and increases in bone resorption were observed [24]. Moreover, in a clinical cohort study (131 hip fracture patients and 131 controls), researchers reported a significant negative correlation between serum tryptophan levels and hip fractures, further substantiating the association between tryptophan and bone health [25]. In the body, tryptophan undergoes three main metabolic pathways: the kynurenine pathway, the serotonin pathway, and the indole pathway. Preclinical investigations have revealed that metabolites within the kynurenine pathway, such as kynurenic acid and 3-hydroxykynurenine, accelerate bone aging, diminish BMD, and increase fracture susceptibility, thereby exerting deleterious effects on skeletal integrity [11]. Conversely, other metabolites including 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid, xanthurenic acid, picolinic acid, and quinolinic acid have shown the capacity to enhance bone density and decrease fracture risk because of their ability to mitigate oxidative stress, modulate immune responses, and inhibit inflammatory cascades, among other possible mechanism [26]. Moreover, serotonin exerts a bidirectional influence on bone physiology, with central serotonin promoting bone formation, whereas intestinal serotonin impedes osteoblast proliferation [27]. Therefore, the specific effects of tryptophan on bone health may vary depending on its metabolic pathways and corresponding metabolites.

While evidence supports the protective role of serum tryptophan in bone health, few studies have explored the impact of dietary tryptophan intake on bone mineral density decline and osteoporosis, despite the close correlation between serum tryptophan levels and dietary intake. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the effect of dietary tryptophan intake on the risk of LBMD among middle-aged and older adults. A novel finding in this study was that participants with LBMD had significantly lower dietary tryptophan intake than did those with normal bone mass (1740.45 mg/day vs. 2041.39 mg/day), and there was an independent negative correlation between dietary tryptophan intake and the risk of LBMD. Specifically, higher dietary tryptophan intake was associated with a 28% reduced risk of LBMD. After adjusting for age and sex, the risk of low bone mass decreased by 20%, and this association persisted even after further adjustment for other potential confounding factors. Our findings are supported by several clinical studies. For example, an analysis using longitudinal cohort data from the Cardiovascular Health Study examined the relationships between tryptophan levels and the risk of hip fractures and mortality over a 12-year follow-up [25]. A significant association was revealed between higher serum tryptophan levels and a lower risk of hip fractures [HR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.57–0.99], suggesting potential benefits of increased tryptophan intake for osteoporosis patients. In another clinical study, Sabour et al. [28] investigated the dietary amino acid intake of 103 spinal cord injury patients and found a significant negative correlation between lumbar spine BMD and tryptophan intake (r = −0.31, p = 0.007) but no correlation with hip BMD. Our results contrast with those of Cleminson et al. [29]. Their study reported that higher tryptophan intake among men and women aged 45 and older was associated with higher hip BMD compared to those with lower tryptophan intake (p = 0.002 and p = 0.04, respectively). Interestingly, these relationships weakened with increasing age (all p > 0.05). In our study, however, high tryptophan intake was consistently associated with reduced LDMD risk, irrespective of age stratification or adjustment for covariates. This may be due to various factors, including cultural meals, eating habits, access to food, and potential under/overreporting of food intake in the food frequency questionnaire. Further long-term follow-up cohort studies are necessary to assess the relationship between dietary tryptophan intake and the risk of osteoporosis and fractures comprehensively.

Lean mass is a critical determinant for maintaining the functionality and structural integrity of the musculoskeletal system. Reductions in lean mass, as observed in conditions like sarcopenia, are associated with an increased risk of osteoporosis and fragility fractures [30], which highlights the interdependence between muscle and bone health. However, the relationship between tryptophan intake and lean mass remains unclear. Although a few preclinical studies showed a trend toward increased lean mass and decreased fat mass in mice or dogs supplemented with tryptophan, clinical evidence in this field remained limited [31, 32]. In the present study, we conducted additional analyses and found that participants with LBMD had significantly lower lean mass. In the fully adjusted model, which accounted for total fat mass and total lean mass, higher dietary tryptophan intake was significantly associated with a reduced risk of LBMD. Mediation analysis revealed that the contribution of total lean mass to the effect of tryptophan on LBMD was minimal, accounting for only 0.9% of the total effect. This finding further supports the direct impact of dietary tryptophan intake on bone health and strengthens the robustness of our results (data not shown). Nonetheless, this result piqued our interest in the potential relationship between tryptophan and lean mass, which will be a focus of our future research.

While further research is needed to fully understand the association between dietary tryptophan intake and the risk of low bone mass, our findings align with existing evidence and are biologically plausible, underscoring the role of optimizing dietary intake in managing osteoporosis risk. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although dietary data were obtained via two 24-h dietary recalls, the existence of recall bias could not be completely ruled out. Second, as our study design is observational, we cannot establish a causal relationship between tryptophan intake and low bone mass. Additionally, although we adjusted for a series of known risk factors for bone mass decline, we cannot exclude the possibility of unmeasured confounding factors. Finally, the analysis in this study did not account for the use of dietary supplements. According to NHANES 2007–2018 data, only 2.6% of dietary supplement users (n = 23,050) and 1.4% of the population aged ≥ 1 year (n = 44,000) consumed protein, amino acid, or sports supplements [33]. Owing to the low prevalence of nutritional supplement use, we infer that our findings are unlikely to be significantly impacted by this variable.

Conclusion

Findings from this cross-sectional study involving participants aged 50 years and above in the United States suggest that higher dietary tryptophan intake may be associated with a lower risk of bone loss, with a nonlinear negative correlation observed after adjusting for potential confounders. In addition to having major implications for clinical practice, our findings also have important public health implications as well. It is possible to prevent and reduce osteoporosis by encouraging a higher tryptophan intake diet or supplementing with tryptophan supplements because diet is a factor that can be changed. Future research urgently requires more randomized controlled trials or cohort studies to confirm these findings, ascertain optimal intake thresholds, and provide more precise and effective prevention and treatment strategies for osteoporosis.
