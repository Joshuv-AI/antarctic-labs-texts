Iodine levels in household and market salt, and associated knowledge, attitudes, and practices in Ethiopia: a nationwide cross-sectional study
Authors: Addisu Legesse Chaka, Dilnesaw Zerfu, Paulos Getachew, Dawd Gashu, Yoseph Demelash, Abebe Bitew Zegeye, Aditya Parmar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSalt iodization is a key strategy in the global effort to eliminate iodine deficiency disorders. In Ethiopia, iodized salt coverage has fluctuated over the years, potentially linked to consumers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP). This study aimed to evaluate iodine levels in salt samples from households and markets and to assess KAP related to iodized salt consumption.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted, collecting 1862 salt samples (931 from households and 931 from markets) from nine regions and two city administrations in Ethiopia. KAP data were collected via a questionnaire-based survey, and iodine concentration in the salt samples was determined using iodometric titration.ResultsThe study found that 88.1% of households consumed adequately iodized salt (≥ 15 ppm), with a mean iodine concentration of 43.9 ppm (95% CI: 42.4, 45.6). Coverage below the recommended level (11.3%) showed significant regional variation, with the lowest coverage observed in Tigray and Afar. Market coverage was slightly higher at 89.1% with a mean iodine concentration of 48.9 ppm (95% CI: 46.1, 49.9). Regarding KAP, 53.1% of women had heard of iodized salt, but awareness of iodine loss from improper storage and handling was low; 56.9% of households reported adding salt after cooking. In multivariable analysis, requesting iodized salt during purchase (AOR = 2.45; 95% CI: 1.82, 3.31), storing salt in a container with a lid (AOR = 1.92; 95% CI: 1.45, 2.54), adding salt after cooking (AOR = 1.68; 95% CI: 1.21, 2.33), and having heard of iodized salt (AOR = 1.34; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.76) were independently associated with having adequately iodized household salt.ConclusionEthiopia has high iodized salt coverage at household (88.1%) and market (89.1%) levels, but consumer knowledge and handling practices remain insufficient, risking iodine losses before consumption. The lowest household coverage and iodine concentrations were observed in Tigray and Afar. Public health actions should prioritize region-specific behavior change communication (e.g., low-coverage regions) focused on purchasing iodized salt, airtight storage, and appropriate use during cooking, alongside strengthened quality control in the supply chain.Keywords: Iodized salt; Iodine; Iodine deficiency disorders; Knowledge, attitudes and practices; Ethiopia

Introduction

Iodine is an essential micronutrient required for the synthesis of thyroid hormones, which regulate growth, neurodevelopment, and metabolic function across the life course [1, 2]. Because naturally occurring iodine content is low in many foods [3, 4], populations living in iodine-poor environments remain vulnerable to iodine deficiency and its consequences, collectively termed iodine deficiency disorders (IDD) [5, 6]. IDD includes goiter, hypothyroidism, impaired fetal and child neurodevelopment, growth retardation, and adverse pregnancy outcomes, with pregnant and lactating women and young children at greatest risk [6, 7].

Universal salt iodization (USI) is the globally endorsed, cost-effective strategy to prevent IDD by ensuring that all edible salt is iodized and that iodine content is monitored throughout the supply chain and at the point of consumption [8]. Globally, iodized salt remains a cornerstone intervention, yet gaps persist in coverage and quality, and inequities remain between and within countries [9–11]. In addition, iodine content can decline during transport, storage, and cooking, meaning that high coverage of iodized salt does not necessarily guarantee adequate iodine intake if handling practices are suboptimal [8, 12].

Ethiopia adopted USI in the 1990s and introduced mandatory salt iodization through the Salt Iodization Council of Ministers Regulation No. 204/2011, which prohibits processing, importing, distributing, or selling non-iodized salt for human consumption [13]. Since implementation of mandatory iodization and the expansion of centralized iodization facilities and monitoring, household coverage of iodized salt has improved substantially; however, evidence indicates that regional disparities and quality issues remain, particularly in settings affected by weak market access or disruptions in oversight [14, 15].

In this context, consumer knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) are important determinants of whether iodized salt is purchased, correctly stored, and used in ways that minimize iodine loss before consumption. KAP studies are widely used in public health to identify modifiable behavioral and information barriers and to design targeted behavior change communication; rigorous questionnaire development and validation strengthen the interpretability and reproducibility of KAP findings [16, 17]. Recent behavioral research during public health emergencies, including COVID-19, has also highlighted how information sources and beliefs shape preventive practices—an insight relevant to salt iodization programs that rely on sustained household behaviors [18, 19]. Therefore, pairing salt iodine measurements with KAP assessment can help distinguish supply-side gaps (availability/quality) from demand-side gaps (awareness/handling), informing more precise interventions.

Accordingly, this nationwide study assessed iodine concentration in salt samples collected from households and markets across nine regions and two city administrations in Ethiopia, and evaluated consumer knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to iodized salt purchase, storage, and use.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a community-based cross-sectional study conducted in nine regions and two city administrations across Ethiopia.

Sampling size

Utilizing data from the 2016 Ethiopian National Micronutrient Survey (ENMS), this cross-sectional study assessed iodized salt coverage at the household and market levels. A three-stage sampling design was employed: first, the country was stratified into nine regions and two city administrations. Second, 72 enumeration areas (EAs) were randomly selected, representing 20% of the 366 EAs utilized in the ENMS. Third, 13 households and 13 corresponding markets were selected per EA via systematic random sampling. The target sample size (N = 936) was powered to detect an anticipated 23% prevalence of adequately iodized salt, assuming a 5% margin of error, 95% confidence interval, and a design effect of 2. The final analytical sample comprised 931 units; exclusions were due to household vacancy (n = 2) and refusal of consent (n = 3). This negligible attrition rate (0.5%) preserves the statistical power and representativeness of the original frame.

Data collection

Participant selection and survey

Women aged 15–49 years from the selected households were the preferred respondents. Data collection teams underwent a two-day training session to ensure standardization of techniques. Information was gathered on participant demographics, knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding iodized salt, the retail price of iodized salt, and handling and packaging conditions.

Sample salt collection

For household samples, approximately 150 g of salt was collected from the resident, and a replacement portion (approximately 250 g) was provided. Samples were sealed in clean, airtight zip-lock plastic bags and shipped to the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) for analysis.

For the market survey, samples were collected from common grocery stores, open markets, supermarkets, and salt distribution points near the selected EAs. Selection within each EA involved random selection from a list of shops/markets: one distributor, one supermarket/large shop, six kiosks/small shops (six composite open salt and three packed salt; total of nine salt samples), and two open markets. When multiple brands were available, one brand was randomly purchased from each store. For unpacked salt, approximately 200–250 g was purchased, mixed well, and stored in labelled polythene zip-lock bags. A dedicated information sheet was used to record details, including region, type of shop, packaging information, production/expiry date, and unique sample code.

KAP questionnaire

The structured interview questionnaire used to collect Knowledge, Attitude, and Practices (KAP) data regarding iodized salt was adapted and developed for this study. The instrument was designed based on the core indicators and standardized methodology outlined in the WHO/UNICEF/ICCIDD guidelines for monitoring Universal Salt Iodization (USI) programs [8].

Laboratory analysis: salt-iodine determination

Each salt sample was quantitatively analyzed for iodine content using iodometric titration [20], at the EPHI Food Science and Nutrition Laboratory, using a known concentration of KIO3 for quality control.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and statistical analysis were performed using IBM SPSS version 20.0 via the Complex Samples module to account for the three-stage stratified cluster sampling design, ensuring that 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and standard errors were adjusted for the design effect and regional clustering. Regional differences in mean iodine concentrations were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc tests, using the harmonic mean of group sizes to accommodate unequal strata, while chi-square tests assessed associations between categorical variables. Furthermore, a multivariable binary logistic regression model, adjusted for region, was used to identify independent predictors of adequate household salt iodization (≥ 15 ppm), with results reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) at a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Operational definition iodine thresholds

In this study, salt iodization levels were evaluated against two distinct but complementary standards based on the point of assessment in the supply chain. While the Ethiopian National Standard (CES 70) mandates an iodine concentration of 20–40 parts per million (ppm) at the production and industry levels to account for anticipated iodine losses during distribution, this study utilized the WHO/UNICEF benchmark of ≥ 15 ppm for household and market-level assessments [8]. This threshold is the globally recognized standard for ensuring biological protection at the point of consumption. Accordingly, salt samples containing ≥ 15 ppm were categorized as adequately iodized, while those below this limit were categorized as inadequately iodized.

Results

Salt iodine coverage

Household iodized salt coverage by region

Nationally, 88.1% of households had adequately iodized salt (≥ 15 ppm) (Table 1), approaching the 90% USI monitoring target. Coverage varied markedly by region: Dire Dawa (100.0%), Amhara (98.3%), and Harari (96.9%) showed the highest adequacy, whereas Afar had the lowest adequacy (49.5%) and the highest proportion of inadequately iodized salt (48.4%). Mean household iodine concentration was 43.9 ppm nationally, with lower mean concentrations in Afar (25.9 ppm) and Tigray (26.9 ppm) than in higher-performing regions such as Harari (55.9 ppm).

The iodine content of market salt by region

Market salt iodine levels also varied by region (Table 2). Nationally, 89.1% of market samples were adequately iodized (≥ 15 ppm), with a mean iodine concentration of 48.9 ppm. Adequacy was highest in Harari (100.0%) and Addis Ababa (98.9%) and lowest in Afar (51.1%). Knowledge, attitudes and practices of iodized salt utilization in Ethiopia

To guide interpretation, we used a simple KAP logic model in which knowledge (awareness of iodized salt and iodine loss) influences attitudes (perceived importance and purchasing preference), which in turn shapes practices (asking for iodized salt, storage, and timing of salt addition). These practices determine the outcome of interest—whether household salt contains iodine at ≥ 15 ppm at the point of use.

Awareness and source of information

Table 3 summarizes women’s awareness and perceived benefits of iodized salt. Nationally, 53.1% of women reported having heard of iodized salt, and among those, 79.1% associated iodized salt with goitre prevention. In contrast, only 7.5% recognized its role in preventing iodine deficiency disorders (IDD) or supporting physical growth. Awareness varied by region, with the highest awareness in Tigray (92.3%) and the lowest in Somali and Harari (both 16.9%).

Knowledge and perception about iodized salt

As detailed in the provided Fig. 1, the survey reveals a significant national knowledge deficit regarding iodized salt properties and handling. Only 34.6% of households correctly recognize that iodine levels deplete in uncovered containers, while a 50.1% majority remain unsure of its stability. This gap is further evidenced by a lack of practical skill; only 14.2% of respondents can distinguish between iodized and non-iodized salt. Furthermore, market awareness is low, as only 41.9% correctly identify that not all salt is fortified, while 45.9% remain uncertain.

Where do people usually buy salt for household consumption?

Figure 2 shows that kiosks are the dominant national source for salt (70.04%), with the highest utilization in Addis Ababa (97.67%) and Dire Dawa (91.94%). A notable regional difference exists in the Somali region, where open markets (41.94%) surpass kiosks (37.1%) as the primary source. Nationally, supermarket utilization remains negligible at 2.51%, though it reaches a regional peak of 12.17% in Amhara. These findings suggest that public health interventions should prioritize small-scale retailers and open markets.

Type of salt people usually buy and reason for purchasing preference

According to Table 5 there is highly significant association between geographical region and the type of salt consumed in Ethiopia (χ2 = 243.98, df = 20, p<0.001). While coarse salt is the national standard (58.0%), there is extreme regional variation; for instance, exclusive coarse salt use ranges from a high of 90.7% in Harari to a low of 15.4% in Tigray. Conversely, exclusive use of fine salt is remarkably rare across the country, representing only 1.7% of the total sample and being completely absent in seven of the eleven regions studied.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the primary determinants of salt purchasing preferences among the surveyed households were centered on market accessibility and affordability. The most prevalent factor was availability, cited by 33.9% of households, followed closely by cheaper price, which influenced 30.3% of the sample.

Do people usually ask for iodized salt?

Nationally, only 29.6% of households specifically requested iodized salt during purchase, revealing significant regional disparities (Fig. 4). Tigray exhibited the highest demand at 80.3%, followed by Amhara (49.1%) and Benishangul-Gumuz (43.4%). Conversely, the lowest request rates were observed in the Somali (5.0%) and Harari (6.0%) regions, where over 90% of households did not specify salt type. Other regions, including SNNPR, Gambella, and Dire Dawa, also reported low engagement, with request rates falling below 20%.

Packaging indications for iodine fortification of household salt

As shown in Fig. 5, only 38.8% of households nationally retain original salt packaging, with significant regional disparities. While Tigray (83.3%) and Amhara (68.4%) show high retention rates, the vast majority of households in regions like Harari (96.2%) and Gambella (89.1%) prefer transferring salt to other containers. This regional preference for secondary storage drives the national trend, where 61% of households opt not to use the original packaging.

Analysis of packaging information: salt source, iodine fortification, and expiry date

Market salt packaging analysis (Fig. 6) indicates a strong reliance on domestic production, with 98.6% of samples originating from Ethiopia. Labelling compliance for fortification is high, as 89.5% of samples were explicitly marked and claimed as “Iodized”. Regarding product shelf life, expiry dates were predominantly concentrated in 2019 (50.3%) and 2020 (31.6%), with significantly lower proportions recorded for other years.

Predictors of household salt iodization adequacy

According to Table 8, a multivariable binary logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with household salt adequacy (≥ 15 ppm). After adjusting for region, several practices were independently associated with having adequately iodized household salt. Households that requested iodized salt at the point of purchase had higher odds of adequacy than those who did not (AOR = 2.45; 95% CI: 1.82, 3.31). Storage in a container with a lid (AOR = 1.92; 95% CI: 1.45, 2.54) and adding salt after cooking (AOR = 1.68; 95% CI: 1.21, 2.33) were also positively associated with meeting the ≥ 15 ppm threshold. Finally, having heard of iodized salt was associated with adequacy (AOR = 1.34; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.76).

Discussion

This nationwide survey indicates that Ethiopia has high coverage of adequately iodized salt at household (88.1%) and market (89.1%) levels, with mean iodine concentrations above the WHO consumption-point benchmark (≥ 15 ppm) [8]. This performance aligns with the scale-up of centralized iodization and strengthened enforcement following mandatory salt iodization [15], and approaches the ≥ 90% household coverage target used for USI monitoring [20]. However, high national coverage masks substantial subnational inequities and gaps in consumer awareness and handling that may reduce iodine delivered at the point of consumption.

National progress in context

Relative to earlier national estimates and to many Sub-Saharan African settings where coverage remains below the USI target, the current national estimate reflects substantial program progress. Improved market availability and more consistent iodization, supported by policy and industry investments, likely contributed to these gains [15]. Mean iodine concentrations above 40 ppm should also be interpreted against Ethiopia’s production standard (20–40 ppm) and the possibility of variable iodization across supply channels, underscoring the need for routine quality assurance from production through distribution [8].

Regional disparities and likely drivers

Despite high national coverage, household and market adequacy were lowest in Afar, and iodine concentrations were low in both Afar and Tigray. In Afar, proximity to production/mining areas may increase access to non-iodized “raw” salt that bypasses iodization facilities and regulatory oversight, consistent with national hotspot analyses showing uneven access to adequately iodized salt [14]. In Tigray, reduced coverage may reflect recent disruptions in market functioning and regulatory monitoring, which can shift purchases toward informal supply. Together, these patterns highlight the need for stronger last-mile distribution, enforcement, and routine market monitoring in low-coverage settings [21, 22].

The coverage–KAP gap

The study also shows a clear disconnect between supply-side coverage and consumer-side awareness. Although most households had adequately iodized salt, only 53.1% of women reported having heard of iodized salt, and knowledge beyond goiter prevention was limited. Similar gaps have been reported in Ethiopian subnational studies [23, 24], suggesting that market penetration can outpace consumer education. Because iodized and non-iodized salt often appear similar, households may purchase iodized salt without intent or knowledge and may be less likely to protect iodine through appropriate storage and cooking practices.

Linking KAP to household iodine adequacy

Several KAP-related factors were independently associated with having adequately iodized household salt. Requesting iodized salt at purchase more than doubled the odds of household adequacy (AOR = 2.45), suggesting that consumer demand can shift purchases toward better-labeled or higher-quality products. Storage in a container with a lid was also associated with adequacy (AOR = 1.92), consistent with evidence that humidity and air exposure accelerate iodine loss and that moisture-barrier packaging and closed storage improve iodine retention [25]. In addition, adding salt after cooking increased the odds of adequacy (AOR = 1.68), aligning with evidence that iodine loss increases with heat exposure time and cooking method [26]. These results identify practical, modifiable behaviors—asking, storing, and adding—that can improve the effective iodine dose delivered to households even when coverage is high.

Mechanisms of iodine loss and implications

While 56.9% of households reported adding salt after cooking or during serving, many also reported practices that can reduce iodine content, including transferring salt out of original packaging and storing salt in containers without lids. Cooking losses can be substantial (approximately 6%–51%) depending on heat intensity, cooking method, and exposure time [26]. Iodine stability during storage is also sensitive to moisture and air exposure; humid conditions and porous packaging can reduce iodine over time, whereas closed containers and moisture-barrier packaging improve retention [25]. Strengthening household handling is therefore a necessary complement to supply-side fortification.

Programmatic implications

Sustaining USI gains in Ethiopia requires a dual approach: continued regulatory monitoring and quality control to address regional supply gaps and iodization variability, and targeted communication to strengthen demand and protect iodine at the point of use. Multilevel evidence from East Africa similarly shows that purchasing and household handling practices influence whether salt remains adequately iodized when consumed [27, 28].In low-coverage regions, priorities should include strengthening availability, enforcement, and market surveillance; in higher-coverage regions, priorities should shift toward improving awareness beyond goiter prevention and reinforcing simple behaviors such as requesting iodized salt, storing it in closed containers, and adding it late during cooking [29].

Limitation of the study

First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; associations between KAP-related factors and household salt iodine adequacy should be interpreted as correlational and may be influenced by unmeasured confounding (e.g., household socioeconomic status, market access, and local supply conditions). Second, iodine was measured in salt samples but urinary iodine concentration was not assessed; therefore, we cannot directly infer population iodine status. Third, some KAP variables relied on self-report and may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Finally, although we used the Complex Samples procedures to account for the clustered survey design, residual measurement error in exposure (iodine loss during storage/cooking) cannot be excluded.

Conclusion and recommendation

Ethiopia has high iodized salt coverage at household (88.1%) and market (89.1%) levels, but knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to iodized salt remain limited, which may reduce iodine delivered at the point of consumption. Coverage and iodine concentrations were lowest in Afar and Tigray. In multivariable analysis, having adequately iodized household salt was independently associated with requesting iodized salt during purchase (AOR = 2.45; 95% CI: 1.82, 3.31), storing salt in a container with a lid (AOR = 1.92; 95% CI: 1.45, 2.54), adding salt after cooking (AOR = 1.68; 95% CI: 1.21, 2.33), and having heard of iodized salt (AOR = 1.34; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.76). These findings indicate that sustaining USI gains requires both continued supply-side quality control and targeted behavior-focused communication.

Target priority regions with tailored supply and communication actions. Prioritize Afar and Tigray for intensified market surveillance, enforcement of iodization requirements, and supply-chain strengthening (given the lowest household coverage and iodine concentrations). In parallel, prioritize Somali for behavior change communication because awareness of iodized salt was only 16.9% (Table 3) and only 5.0% of households reported asking for iodized salt at purchase (Fig. 4). Communication should use channels aligned with local purchasing patterns, with outreach through open markets (41.94% of household salt purchases) and kiosks (37.1%) (Fig. 2), supported by local-language radio and retailer engagement.
