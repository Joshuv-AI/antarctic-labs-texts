B7-H3 nanobody-based CAR T cells control multiple myeloma growth, while dual BCMA/B7-H3 CAR T cells overcome antigen escape
Authors: Arne Van der Vreken, Fien Meeus, Chenggong Tu, Lauren van den Broecke, Vincenzo Raimondi, Rosanna Vescovini, Heleen Hanssens, Fenja Watté, Dorien Autaers, Marta Marco Aragon, Janne Billiau, Kim De Veirman, Karin Vanderkerken, Yannick De Vlaeminck, Lorenzo Franceschini, Holly Lee, Paola Neri, Elke De Bruyne, Paola Storti, Nicola Giuliani, Karine Breckpot, Eline Menu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCAR T cell therapy targeting BCMA has shown remarkable efficacy in multiple myeloma (MM), but relapses occur due to T cell exhaustion and the emergence of BCMA-negative subpopulations. Novel targets are needed to overcome antigen escape.MethodsB7-H3 (CD276) expression was assessed on primary MM patient samples. We engineered nanobody-based CAR T cells (nanoCARs) targeting B7-H3 and evaluated their cytotoxicity and cytokine production in vitro, including against patient-derived myeloma cells. Anti-tumor activity was tested in two different MM xenograft models. Dual CAR T cells (BCMA/B7-H3) and CARpooling (mix of BCMA and B7-H3 CAR T cells) were also tested for efficacy in antigen escape models.ResultsB7-H3 expression was detected on plasma cells in 60% of MM patients. B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells exhibited strong antigen-specific cytotoxicity and effector cytokine secretion, including against primary MM cells. In vivo, they reduced tumor burden and improved survival. Dual (BCMA/B7-H3) CAR T cells and CARpooling effectively eliminated heterogeneous tumor populations with mutually exclusive BCMA or B7-H3 expression. These findings show that BCMA/B7-H3 targeting may be a strategy to overcome antigen escape mechanisms.ConclusionB7-H3 is a promising immunotherapy target in MM. B7-H3-specific and dual-targeting nanoCAR T cells could offer a strategy to prevent antigen escape and improve treatment durability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13045-025-01756-5.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a plasma cell malignancy characterized by the abnormal accumulation of plasma cells primarily in the bone marrow (BM) [1]. This hematological malignancy is still considered incurable, mainly due to cellular heterogeneity and the development of therapy resistance. Recent advances in antibody-based therapies and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell approaches have significantly improved patient outcomes and thereby revolutionized the treatment landscape of MM [2, 3]. Commercial CAR T cell products for refractory myeloma are targeting B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA). Among the two FDA-approved products, Idecabtagene vicleucel (ide-cel), a single chain variable fragment (scFv)-based CAR T-cell targeting BCMA, has demonstrated positive clinical outcomes [4]. The more recently approved BCMA CAR T-cell product is a bi-paratopic, single domain antibody (nanobody)- based (nanoCAR) known as ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel), which has demonstrated increased durable responses. However, CAR T cell therapy, though promising, is still confronted with challenges in MM. Relapse due to antigenic loss is observed in 4 to 33% of MM patients following BCMA CAR T-cell therapy [4–6]. Additionally, BCMA-targeted therapy may cause Darwinian selection of BCMAlow/negative MM cells. Next to selective pressure, BCMA expression is variable over time due to γ-secretase activity leading to shedding of soluble BCMA fragments capable of inhibiting CAR T-cell function [7, 8].

While BCMA can be considered a highly specific antigen for plasma cell malignancies, there is a need for alternative antigens. The search for these may mitigate the aforementioned problems and provide alternatives for patients with plasma cells expressing low or negative BCMA levels. To extend the antigen choice, we propose B7-H3 as a novel target for immunotherapy in MM.

B7-H3 or B7 homolog 3 (CD276) is a member of the B7 superfamily encoded on chromosome 15q24.1. It exists in two isoforms (2Ig and 4Ig form) due to exon duplication, with the dominant human isoform containing four immunoglobulin (Ig) like domains [9]. While B7-H3 mRNA is widely expressed across normal tissues, protein expression is generally low due to post-transcriptional and post-translational regulation, including miRNA-mediated repression and glycosylation. Among healthy tissues, the highest B7-H3 protein expression is observed in the prostate, albeit still lower than in tumor tissues. High expression of B7-H3 is associated with poor prognosis in various solid tumors, including glioblastoma and colon cancer [10, 11]. B7-H3 also has an immune-regulatory function. For instance, it has been shown that B7-H3 overexpression on tumor cells inhibits the cytotoxic function of CD8 + T cells and NK cells [12]. Current literature on its role in MM disease is limited. However, B7-H3 has been identified as a mediator of the Warburg effect and STAT3-driven proliferation, contributing to metabolic plasticity, tumor progression and drug resistance [13]. These features, combined with its tumor-selective protein overexpression and immunosuppressive functions, provide a rationale for investigating B7-H3 as potential target in MM immunotherapy [14].

In this study, we propose B7-H3 as novel antigen for CAR T-cell therapy in MM. We first examined B7-H3 expression in BM samples from MM patients, evaluating its expression in both tumor cells and healthy hematopoietic populations, present in the BM tumor microenvironment (TME). Next, we designed a nanobody-based second generation CAR, showing MM cell cytolysis of both primary cells and MM cell lines in vitro, and potent killing in vivo. Finally, we demonstrated that our CAR could overcome therapy resistance in case of BCMA antigen loss.

Methodology

Gene expression analysis

Gene expression profiling data are derived from the Heidelberg-Montpellier cohort and the Total Therapy 2 (TT2), both microarray-based gene expression datasets. The TT2 contains a dataset of 345 newly diagnosed MM patients (GSE2658) and 22 healthy donors, 44 MGUS and 12 cases of SMM (GSE5900) from the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences. The Heidelberg-Montpellier (E-MTAB-372) contains gene expression of selected CD138 BM plasma cells of previously untreated patients. Datasets were consulted and analyzed via Genomicscape (http://www.genomicscape.com) [15–17]. Data were normalized with the MAS5 algorithm.

Design and generation of CAR constructs

The design and generation of CARs are as previously described [18]. In short, variable fragments for B7-H3 were obtained from patent WO2020033837 (nanobody) and enoblituzumab (scFv). Sequences are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3A. The R3B23 nanobody was used to generate non-targeting control CARs [19, 20]. The extracellular domains were linked to the CD8α hinge and transmembrane domains and the cytoplasmic domains of 4-1BB and CD3ζ. Genetic codes were cloned in a pHR’ lentiviral transfer plasmid including the P2A linked Thy1.1 (CD90.1) reporter genetic code. VSV.G pseudotyped lentiviral particles were produced in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 T as described [21]. Harvests were collected after 48 and 72 h, filtered through 0.2 μm corning filters and centrifuged in the Beckman Coulter ultracentrifuge at 22,000 rpm for 1.5 h at 4 °C using the SW28 rotor. Pelleted lentiviral particles were dissolved in PBS with 10 µg/mL protamine sulphate (LeoPharma) and stored at −80 °C. Protamine sulfate, a polycationic peptide, was added to enhance transduction efficiency by neutralizing surface charges and promoting viral entry into target cells. Functional titers were estimated by titration series on HEK293T cells.

Cell culture

Human MM cell lines (HMCLs) RPMI-8226, LP-1, OPM-2, JJN-3, XG-2, XG-7, U266, MM1S and ANBL-6)(ATCC) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2D3 Jurkat cells were cultured in IMDM medium, and HEK293T cells in DMEM (high glucose). Medium was supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, TICO Europe), 2 mM L-glutamine, and 100 Units/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Short-tandem repeat analysis was performed to confirm cell line identity of MM cells, and used within 2 months post-thaw.

Generation of CRISPR/CAS mediated knock out cell lines

Doxycycline-inducible Cas9-expressing OPM-2 cells were generated by lentiviral transduction (Addgene) and selected using puromycin. To obtain a CRISPR/CAS9 knockout of B7-H3, cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding the following guide RNAs (gRNAs): TTCAGGGACCTGGACCTCCA and AGTGCCACCACTGGGTCTTC. Following transduction, cells were single-cell sorted by FACS and subsequently validated for B7-H3 expression on mRNA (qPCR) and protein level (Flow cytometry and western blot). BCMA knock out OPM-2 cells were kindly provided by the Bahlis lab (University of Calgary), using a sgRNA targeting GAAGAACATCGAAGTTGACA.

Generation of CAR-positive reporter cell lines

2D3 Jurkat cells were engineered to express GFP, under control of NFAT response elements. 2D3 cells were transduced at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) 5 with lentiviral particles encoding for one of the CARs. Cells were expanded and later labelled with magnetic beads coated with anti-Thy1.1 antibodies for magnetically activated cell sorting (MACS). Purified Thy1.1+ 2D3 cells were used in a 1:1 (E: T) ratio with different MM cell lines and analyzed after 48 h.

Generation of primary human CAR T cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of healthy donors were obtained from the hematology unit of the university hospital (UZ Brussels). Apheresis (Spectra Optia® apheresis system, TerumoBCT) were processed on the CTS Rotea™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for enrichment of peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs) and frozen in a 5% Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution with cryopreservation medium (CryoStor® CS5, Biolife Solutions). Transduction was performed according to the Miltenyi Biotec protocol. Briefly, PBLs were cultured in TexMACS medium and activated at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL with soluble anti-CD3/CD28 TransAct reagent (Miltenyi Biotec), supplemented with IL-7/IL-15 (Miltenyi Biotec) at 40U/mL each. After 24 h, CAR encoding lentiviral particles were added at a MOI of 2.5. Lentiviral particles and TransAct were washed away 24 h later. Expansion was done in TexMACS medium containing IL-7/IL-15 at 40U/mL each, replaced every 2–3 days. At day 7, Thy1.1 expression was evaluated as a measure for transduction efficiency, corresponding to CAR expression [18]. Dual CAR T cells were generated by co-transduction using an MOI of 2.5 and as described in [22].

Patient samples

A total cohort of 38 patients with MM was included in the study, comprising 25 patients with newly diagnosed MM (D) and 12 with relapsed MM (R). Patient characteristics are included in Supplementary Table 1. Mononuclear cells (MNCs) were isolated from the BM of MM patients using a Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation. CD138⁺ plasma cells were purified from isolated MNCs using an immunomagnetic method with anti-CD138 monoclonal antibody-coated microbeads (MACS, Miltenyi Biotec).

Killing assays

OPM-2 and RPMI-8226 cells were transduced with luciferase-encoding lentiviral particles and used to evaluate the killing capacity of CAR T cells. CAR T cells were co-incubated in RPMI-1640 medium with Fluc-positive MM cells at different effector: target ratios. Luciferase activity was determined 24 h later, using the Luciferase Assay System (Promega) and the Glomax plate reader, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For killing dynamics and antigen escape experiments, the indicated cell lines were transduced with either Katushka or GFP encoding lentiviral particles. GFP+ or Katushka+ MM cells were co-cultured in TexMACS medium with CAR T cells in 96 well U-bottom plates with cell-repellent surface (Greiner) at a 1:1 ratio. The fluorescent signal was monitored using IncuCyteZOOM system (EssenBio). Katushka/GFP intensity and area values were normalized to the first imaging time point (t = 0). Primary MM cells isolated from the BM were co-cultured at a 1:1 ratio with primary CAR T cells in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 20% FBS. Cell death was evaluated after overnight culture (18 h) by flow cytometry analysis using the BD Celesta.

Cytokine analysis

CAR T cells and tumor cells were co-cultured for 18 h (RPMI-8226 and primary samples) or 24 h (OPM-2) at a 1:1 ratio. Supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C for analysis. IL-2, IFN-γ and TNF-α levels were determined using ELISA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ThermoFisher Scientific, #88–7316−86, #88–7348−88 and #88–7025−88).

Flow cytometry

Cell acquisition was performed on the BD LSRFortessa or BD Symphony A1 flow cytometers at the Flowcore VUB, and the BD FACS Celesta (BD Biosciences) at the Hematology Laboratory in Parma. with data acquisition carried out using BD FACSDiva Software (version 8.02, BD Bioscience) and data analysis performed with FlowJo software (Tristar, inc.). Single-fluorochrome compensation was performed using BD Comp Beads (cat. 552843) and BM MNCs. Patient samples were washed with PBS containing 5% fetal calf serum (FCS) and stained using Brilliant Stain Solution for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. The following fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies were used: CD19 (BV785, #302239), CD38 (FITC, # 356610), CD138 (BV605, #356520), B7-H3 (PE and APC, #351004), BCMA (BV421, # 357519), CD14 (BV421, #325627), CD16 (FITC, #302005), CD15 (BV711, #323049), CD33 (BV785, #303428), CD45 (BV510, #368526), CD11b (BV605, #101257), HLA-DR (BV650, #307649), and CD3 (#300316) were all purchased from Biolegend. CAR expression was assessed using anti-Thy1.1 (PE-Cy7 or PE, #202523, Biolegend). CD34 (BV650, #743533), CD45RA (BV711, #563733) and cell viability dye 7-AAD (#559925) were purchased from BD Biosciences. After staining, samples were washed, resuspended in PBS with 5% FBS, and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry.

Western blot

Cells were lysed and blotted as previously described [23]. In short, cells were lysed with lysis buffer containing inhibitors for proteases and phosphatases. Isolated fractions were mixed with 2X Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad), and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were separated in sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels and transferred to Immuno-Blot PVDF Membranes (Bio-Rad). PVDF membranes were then blocked using 5% non-fat milk for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies. After washing the membranes with TRIS-buffered saline (0,1% Tween) Secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h. Visualization was done using West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific) and Li-Cor Odyssey Fc (Bad Homburg). The following antibodies were used: B7-H3 (#14058), α-tubulin (#2144) and horseradish peroxidase-coupled anti-rabbit (#7074), all Cell signaling Technology.

Animal models

Six to eight weeks old female NSG mice were purchased from Charles River. For the OPM-2 model, 1 × 106 OPM-2-fluc cells dissolved in RPMI-1640 medium were intravenously (i.v.) injected into the tail vein. Animals were imaged once a week for bioluminescent emission using an IVIS device following intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of D-luciferin (150 mg/kg). Images were analyzed using M3 vision software. For the RPMI-8226 model, 5 × 106 cells dissolved in PBS were subcutaneously (s.c.) injected in the flank. Tumor growth was monitored 2–3 times/week with caliper measurements. Tumor volume was calculated by the formula: (length x width x width)/2. A total of 4 × 106 CAR T cells were i.v. injected in 200 µL RPMI-1640 medium (no supplements), 14 days after tumor inoculation. Mice were sacrificed upon reaching a tumor volume of 2000 mm3 in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software). For comparisons across multiple groups, a one or two-way ANOVA (two-sided) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was applied where appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical tests, sample sizes (n), and exact p-values (where applicable) are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

B7-H3 is expressed on plasma cells of multiple myeloma patients

In evaluation of B7-H3 (CD276) as a potential new cell surface antigen in MM, we consulted the Heidelberg-Montpellier cohort. This data set contains Affymetrix data of BM aspirates of 206 MM patients and 5 BM samples from healthy donors. We observed that transcript levels of CD276 increased during differentiation from naive B cells towards plasma (Fig. 1A). We divided the MM patient cohort into two groups, using a cut-off value corresponding to the mean Affymetrix signal units (ASU) of the bone marrow plasma cell group (= 481.6). Elevated CD276 mRNA expression levels were found in more than one-third of the myeloma patients, indicating CD276 upregulation at the mRNA level in this cohort of MM patients (Fig. 1B). Next, we evaluated the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences – Total Therapy 2 TT2-cohort, to confirm these observations. Here we again observed statistically insignificant but lower CD276 mRNA levels in healthy plasma cells, compared to monoclonal gammopathy of unknown significance (MGUS) (P = 0.0532), smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM) (P = 0.3616) and MM (P = 0.2143), where heterogenous expression of CD276 was seen (Fig. 1C). MM patients were then classified in the classic seven molecular groups (Fig. 1D). A significant increase in CD276 mRNA expression was observed in the subgroup with MMSET overexpression compared to all the patients of the cohort (p < 0,0001). Next, we consulted DepMap to evaluate the dependency of MM cells on B7-H3 (Fig. 1E). Analysis showed that HMCLs were more dependent on B7-H3 compared to BCMA. We then evaluated total protein expression in PC from MM patients and HMCLs (Fig. 1F-G). A total of 3 out of 5 tested patient’s samples and 5 out of 7 cell lines showed B7-H3 expression evaluated by western blot (Fig. 1F). The OPM-2 cell line, which belongs to the MMSET molecular subgroup, was selected for further study and a CRISPR/CAS9 knockout was generated for subsequent experiments. Membrane expression of B7-H3 and BCMA was evaluated by flow cytometry in patients and HMCLs (Fig. 1G). We found that patient-derived MM cells were either high or low/negative for B7-H3. In the high population, we observed a more homogenous expression compared to BCMA levels, which is heterogeneously expressed on MM cells. B7-H3 and BCMA surface levels on CD138 cells were found to be independent of each other (P = 0.9813) (Fig. 1H). Detailed individual-level data, including flow cytometry histograms, quadrant plots, and patient background information, are available in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

Since B7-H3 is an immunomodulatory protein, it is not unlikely that it is also expressed on other cell types in the BM suppressive TME. Therefore, we further evaluated its expression in the BM of MM patients. Freshly isolated BM MNCs from newly diagnosed (NDMM) and relapsed/refractory MM (RRMM) were subjected to flow cytometry analysis for B7-H3 expression (Fig. 1I). Expression levels were low for hematopoietic precursor cells, NK cells, T cells and B cells. In the myeloid compartment, a patient-dependent heterogeneous expression for both healthy myeloid cells and suppressive cell types was observed. A detailed overview of the gating strategy used to identify these cell populations is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2. Altogether, B7-H3 is expressed at the surface of MM cells, and when present, more homogenously compared to BCMA, making it an attractive target for immunotherapy.

B7-H3 is a viable target for CAR T-cell therapy in multiple myeloma

To explore the potential of B7-H3 as target for immunotherapy in MM, we designed four CAR constructs of the second generation (Fig. 2A): a control CAR, with a nanobody or heavy chain variable fragment (VHH) for the 5T2 mouse idiotype (irrelevant), one with a nanobody against B7-H3, referred to as B7-H3 nanoCAR, and one with an anti-B7-H3 scFv sequence derived from enoblituzumab. Additionally, the BCMA CAR was included as benchmark (Idecabtagene vicleucel/Abecma scFv). The scFvs and nanobodies used in our CAR T cells have been previously reported to bind their respective antigens with affinities in the nanomolar range [18, 24, 25] (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

To assess the ability of these constructs to activate T cells, we first used Jurkat 2D3 reporter cells containing GFP downstream of NFAT response elements (Fig. 2B). CAR-expressing 2D3 cells were co-cultured with HMCLs at a 1:1 ratio (Fig. 2C). The GFP-signal was quantified in 2D3 cells using live imaging and after 48 h with flowcytometry, showing potent activation by B7-H3 CARs (Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). Notably, the nanoCAR targeting B7-H3 induced stronger activation compared to the scFv CAR. Based on these findings, we proceeded exclusively with the nanoCAR.

Next, primary T cells from healthy donors were transduced using lentiviral particles encoding for a second-generation CAR. Seven days after gene transfer, we evaluated Thy1.1 expression as measure for CAR expression (Fig. 2D). We then tested anti-B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells against luciferase-positive HMCLs and observed a E: T dependent killing of OPM-2 and RPMI-8226 cells (Fig. 2E). Killing dynamics were evaluated by incubating GFP+ MM cell lines with CAR T cells and monitoring the GFP signal via the IncucyteZOOM system (Fig. 2F), demonstrating a similar rate of target cell killing over time. Secretion of the effector cytokines IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in response to RPMI-8226 or OPM-2 cells was significantly increased compared to control CAR T cells (Fig. 2G-I).

B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells are efficacious in vitro against primary myeloma cells

To further gain preclinical evidence, CD138+ plasma cells were isolated from BM aspirates of MM patients. Purified BM-derived CD138+ cells were used for co-culture experiments with allogeneic nanoCAR T cells. Different E:T ratios were tested for 18 h and a 1:1 ratio was selected because of its low levels of non-specific killing (Fig. 3A). Pooled data showed significant killing by B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells compared to control nanoCAR T cells (Fig. 3B). T-cell activation was confirmed by IFN-γ secretion in the supernatant (Fig. 3C). To assess the safety of the B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells within the BM environment, when it is in close vicinity of progenitor cells and myeloid cells, for which B7-H3 expression was observed in some patients, we repeated the experiment in total BM MNCs from MM patients. Here, not only MM cell killing, but also the viability of non-malignant cells, such as hematopoietic stem cells (CD34+) was evaluated (Fig. 3D). In general, MM cells were killed by both BCMA and B7-H3 CAR T cells, while CD34+ and other BM populations were not affected by the presence of these nanoCAR T cells. However, in the BM MNCs of one patient, we observed a decrease in monocytic cell populations (Fig. 3E). IFN-γ secretion in supernatant confirmed T cell activation in total BM (Fig. 3F). Altogether, these data shows that B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells kill primary MM cells but cautions for off-tumor, on-target toxicity are warranted.

B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells are efficacious in two different mouse models

We next evaluated the efficacy of the B7-H3 nanoCAR-T cells in an orthotopic mouse MM model using OPM-2-fluc cells, which home to the BM upon intravenous injection. Tumor burden was monitored weekly through whole body bioluminescent imaging (Fig. 4A). Two weeks post-OPM-2-fluc injection, mice were treated with CAR T cells (Fig. 4B). After 34 days, we observed moderate limb paralysis across treatment groups and decided to sacrifice all mice simultaneously for analysis. Only B7-H3 nanoCAR T cell treated mice showed a significant reduction in tumor load in the BM (Fig. 4C), corroborating the bioluminescent imaging results and their quantification (Fig. 4D-E). In a second model, NSG mice were subcutaneously injected with RPMI-8226 cells (Fig. 4F). Two weeks after tumor injection, the mice were treated with CAR T cells (Fig. 4G). Both BCMA and B7-H3 CAR T cells delayed tumor progression (Fig. 4H-I). Individual tumor volumes are shown in (Fig. 4J). Overall, B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells exert anti-tumor effects in vivo, but are not curative.

Dual targeting increases the efficacy against wildtype multiple myeloma

We hypothesized that dual targeting might improve the therapeutic effects. Tandem CAR T cells targeting BCMA and a second antigen have been shown to have inferior cytotoxic capacity compared to T cells engineered to co-express two different CARs, so-called dual CARs [26, 27]. However, the concept of CARpooling, a strategy that combines two types of CAR T cells, has not been explored. To address this, we compared single BCMA or B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells with dual CAR T cells (co-expression of BCMA scFv CAR and B7-H3 nanoCAR), with CARpool T cells (Fig. 5A). CAR expression was confirmed using antigen-stainings (Supplementary Fig. 5A). In vitro results showed modest improvements in cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 5B–C), with the highest efficacy observed in the CARpooled and dual CAR T cell treatment groups, as demonstrated by the highest means. The dual CAR T cells demonstrated consistently the highest levels of cytokine secretion upon stimulation with OPM-2 and RPMI-8226 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5D). We repeated killing assays with primary MM cells and observed even more pronounced effects, confirming the highest levels of cytotoxicity and IFN-γ secretion by the dual CAR T cells (Fig. 5B–D). Next, we again used the orthotopic OPM-2 mouse model (Fig. 5E). Two weeks post tumor inoculation, mice were treated with either control CAR T cells, single BCMA CARs, B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells, a CARpool (mix) or dual CAR T cells (Fig. 5F). Tumor progression was weekly monitored by bioluminescent imaging (Fig. 5G-H), and CAR T cell persistence was assessed in the peripheral blood. Mean bioluminescence values revealed the strongest anti-tumor activity in the dual CAR T cell group, while single CAR T cells and the CARpool showed comparable tumor intensities at day 34 (Fig. 5H). Individual mouse quantifications are provided in (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Subsequent analysis at end stage of the BM (legs) showed residual tumor cells in B7-H3 nanoCAR T cell treated mice, whereas no tumor cells were detected in 50% of mice treated with the BCMA CAR T cells and 100% of mice treated with the CARpool or dual CAR T cells (Fig. 5I). Circulating CAR T cells were detected over time in the peripheral blood and at endpoint in the BM, confirming its circulation across treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 7B). Serum cytokine analysis at end point confirmed IFN-γ production, indicating ongoing CAR T cell activity (Supplementary Fig. 7C). IL-1β levels and body weights were measured as indirect indicators of toxicity, revealing no major differences across groups, except for a weight loss in control CAR T cell treated mice, likely due to disease progression (Supplementary Fig. 7D).

Dual targeting overcomes antigen escape

Since antigen loss is a known mechanism of relapse after BCMA-targeted therapies, we evaluated the potential of B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells and dual targeting strategies to overcome this limitation. CRISPR/Cas9 knock out (KO) cell lines for BCMA and B7-H3 were used (Fig. 6A-B). To mimic BCMA antigen escape in vitro, we mixed GFP⁺ (green) OPM-2 wild-type cells with Katushka⁺ (red) BCMAKO OPM-2 cells (Fig. 6C). As expected, B7-H3 CAR T cells and dual targeting conditions significantly reduced both populations (Fig. 6D-E). Notably, we also observed some level of killing of BCMAKO cells in the BCMA condition, which might be a result of local release of effector cytokines. Next, we also tested BM PCs from a MM patient lacking BCMA expression, showing resistance to BCMA CAR T cells, but sensitivity towards B7-H3 CAR T cells and the CARpool approach (Fig. 6F). To further explore the efficacy of dual targeting, we created a heterogeneous in vitro model using GFP⁺ OPM-2 B7-H3KO cells and Katushka⁺ OPM-2 BCMAKO cells (Fig. 6G). Once again, dual targeting approaches successfully eliminated both populations, whereas single-targeting CAR T cells were less effective (Fig. 6H–I). The most pronounced effects were again observed in the dual CAR condition. These findings demonstrate that dual targeting CAR T cells are capable of eliminating heterogeneous tumor populations, a key challenge in MM.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate plasma cell membrane expression of B7-H3 in MM patient samples and show for the first time that it can serve as a target for immunotherapy in MM by developing a second-generation CAR product. This CAR construct targets B7-H3 using a nanobody as antigen binding fragment and activates T cells using 4-1BB as co-stimulus. The use of a nanobody offers several advantages over conventional scFvs, including a smaller molecular size that facilitates more efficient CAR expression and cell surface trafficking, superior folding and stability, and potentially lower immunogenicity due to their single-domain nature [28]. Moreover, nanobody-based CARs appear less prone to antigen-independent clustering, which may reduce tonic signaling, a phenomenon linked to premature T cell exhaustion [29, 30]. This nanoCAR design aligns with the architecture of BCMA-targeting CARs that have shown the most favorable survival outcomes in clinical trials [31], supporting its potential as a next-generation therapeutic strategy for MM.

BCMA targeting has emerged as a highly successful approach for the treatment of MM [32–34]. The recent approval for Ciltacabtagene autoleucel as second-line treatment, along with the growing clinical adoption of the monoclonal antibody teclistamab, has established BCMA as a key therapeutic target. Yet, this increased focus also renders BCMA susceptible to mutational pressure, antigen escape and selection of antigen-negative clones [35, 36]. Although complete loss of BCMA is considered as relatively rare (4–33%), our data indicates that BCMA is not uniformly expressed across MM cells, which could provide a reservoir for BCMA-negative relapse.

To address these adaption mechanisms, novel strategies such as APRIL-based CARs, which is the natural ligand of BCMA and TACI, and GPRC5D targeting have been introduced. However, clinical results for APRIL-based CAR T cells have been disappointing [37] and cases of dual antigen loss following sequential targeting of BCMA and GPRC5D have been reported [38]. Therefore, adding B7-H3 to the antigen options might improve patient outcomes, as our data demonstrate that B7-H3 targeting can overcome BCMA antigen escape, while dual BCMA/B7-H3 targeting further prevents relapse driven by loss of either antigen.

For our in vitro tests, we did not generate bispecific tandem CARs (a single receptor carrying two antigen binding domains) since multiple studies have shown that they are outperformed by dual CAR T cells [26, 27]. Instead, we compared dual CAR T cells with a CARpooling strategy that involves mixing two types of CAR T cells, since no data on this approach was available. Dual CAR (BCMA/B7-H3) T cells consistently outperformed their single-CAR counterparts, whereas CARpooling showed less convincing results. This enhanced efficacy may be attributed to the higher initial CAR signaling per cell in dual CAR T cells, as each cell co-express both chimeric receptors, unlike single or mixed CAR T cell populations. In the OPM-2 mouse model, dual CAR T cells demonstrated superior antitumor activity and achieved curative responses, whereas neither B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells nor CARpooling achieved durable tumor clearance. One possible explanation for the suboptimal performance of the B7-H3 CAR and CARpool strategies is that, at the selected treatment timepoint and dosing, tumor burden may have already surpassed the threshold for efficient elimination. Future work should therefore explore strategies to further enhance B7-H3-directed therapy, either through optimized dosing and timing or by increasing B7-H3 expression on MM cells. It has been shown that the latter can be achieved using the FDA-approved compound ingenol-3-angelate, via PKCα activation [39]. Additionally, epigenetic drugs such as decitabine offer another promising strategy. Decitabine not only upregulates B7-H3 expression [40], but has direct anti-tumor effects and might also support the durability of CAR T cells via epigenetic reprogramming [41–43]. However, other combinations should be avoided since they might negatively impact B7-H3 expression. For instance, B7-H3 protein expression is regulated by mTORC1, a key player in MM cell biology [44]. Previous studies of our group have shown that targeting metabolic vulnerabilities in MM cells caused downregulation of mTORC1, and therefore might also affect B7-H3 expression [23, 45].

While combination strategies could modulate B7-H3 antigen expression, its expression on non-malignant cells must be carefully assessed to ensure a favorable safety profile. Therefore, we measured B7-H3 expression in BM aspirates. To rule out stem cell toxicities as observed after treatment with daratumumab (α-CD38), we evaluated its expression on hematopoietic progenitor cells. Low-to-negative expression was observed and after ex vivo treatment with B7-H3 nanoCAR T cells, progenitor cell numbers remained unchanged. In the myeloid-monocytic compartment of the BM, we did observe B7-H3 expression, resulting in monocyte and Mo-MDSC killing in one patient. This could initially make B7-H3 appealing since it also depletes immunosuppressive cells, but susceptibility to infections or other side effects related to decreased monocytes should be studied.

Current clinical trials involving B7-H3 CAR T cells in solid tumors showed tolerability with classic fever-like symptoms which might be rather CAR T related than target related [46–48]. A preclinical study by Meeus et al. demonstrated cross-reactive on-target, off-tumor toxicities upon tumor clearance [18]. In a recent clinical trial such on-target, off-tumor toxicities were not observed, but anti-tumor responses were limited as well [47]. During our in vivo evaluation, potential toxicities were assessed only through indicative parameters such as body weight and IL-1β levels [49]. B7-H3 nanoCAR T cell treated mice showed no signs of toxicity under these conditions. However, we cannot rule out such adverse events in case of complete tumor clearance. One of the limitations of this study is the lack of a fully humanized immune system in NSG mice [49]. Therefore, we cannot fully exclude the possibility of off-target effects, such as monocyte depletion or hematopoietic stem cell toxicities. To better evaluate safety in future studies, the use of humanized mouse models or xenograft bone marrow transplantation systems is recommended [49–51]. Potential toxicities could be mitigated by implementing strategies such as transient CAR expression (e.g. mRNA CARs) or including a suicide switch for controlled CAR T cell elimination in case of adverse events [52, 53].

B7-H3 is currently being explored as a target for immunotherapy in various cancers [9]. The development of a safe allogeneic B7-H3 CAR T cell product could provide an off-the-shelf treatment option with broad applicability across multiple cancer types, serving as a more universal CAR therapy. Furthermore, given the clinical success of bispecific T-cell engagers in MM and their promising preclinical outcomes in other cancers, our data may help accelerate the advancement of B7-H3-targeted bispecific T-cell engagers as well [54].

In conclusion, we provide robust evidence that B7-H3 is a viable target for immunotherapy in MM. CAR T cells that are either mono (α-B7-H3) or dual specific (α-B7-H3/BCMA) demonstrate potent killing capacity and are able to eliminate BCMA-negative clones. These insights could expedite the translation of α-B7-H3 therapies to the clinic and thereby be an additional therapeutic option for patients with MM.
