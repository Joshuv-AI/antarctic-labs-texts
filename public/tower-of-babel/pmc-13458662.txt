Differential reinforcement using matched examples and nonexamples expedites the practice needed to learn concepts
Authors: Catherine L. Williams, Rebecca E. Woodard, Mackenzie R. Fowler, Brooke A. Franks‐Jacobucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConceptual learning refers to correctly identifying novel examples and nonexamples of a concept. Concepts can be taught by presenting stimuli and differentially reinforcing responses to examples but not nonexamples. However, it is unclear which stimuli should be used and whether specifically designed stimuli can result in correct responding even in the absence of differential reinforcement. Stimulus selection can be informed by identifying features that a stimulus must have to be an example and can have without altering whether the stimulus is an example or a nonexample. Previous research suggests that using matched examples and nonexamples (the same can‐have features present) may expedite this learning. Experiment 1 compared acquisition and conceptual learning following practice with matched and unmatched examples and nonexamples lacking exactly one must‐have feature. Participants made fewer errors and completed practice faster with matched examples and nonexamples. Both practice conditions produced similar conceptual learning. Some participants also responded accurately during baseline. To evaluate whether the testing stimuli would produce conceptual learning in the absence of differential reinforcement, Experiment 2 compared conceptual learning without any practice. No conceptual learning was observed. The results support using matched examples and nonexamples for additional research on conceptual learning in classroom and laboratory settings.

Conceptual learning is defined as responding differently in the presence of novel examples than in the presence of novel nonexamples of a concept (Fleming & Levie, 1993; Markle & Tiemann, 1970; Mechner, 1965; Woolley & Tennyson, 1972; Zentall et al., 2002). Responding the same in the presence of novel examples as in the presence of previously learned examples is generalization, and responding differentially in the presence of novel examples than in the presence of novel nonexamples is discrimination (Mechner, 1965). Conceptual learning has occurred only when the learner reliably responds in the presence of examples but not of nonexamples. Concepts can be taught through rule‐governed behavior (such as instructions about what features make a stimulus a member of the concept class), differential reinforcement, or both (for reviews, see Williams & Roop, 2025; see also Khallad, 2004; Wasserman, 2016; Zentall et al., 2002). Identifying effective and efficient procedures to teach concepts through differential reinforcement alone facilitates access to these skills for nonhumans and human learners who may not demonstrate the prerequisite skills needed to follow multiple rules. Across all procedures used to teach concepts, a set of stimuli must be selected and shown across instruction, practice (i.e., differential reinforcement), and testing. However, the combination of stimuli that should be used during practice to most rapidly and reliably produce conceptual learning is still unclear.

One consideration for selecting stimuli to use during practice is the type of concept being taught. Conceptual learning has been divided into types based on the nature of the relation between stimuli that defines the conceptual class. Some concepts are defined in terms of shared function (i.e., equivalence relations; Sidman, 1997), others in terms of relative attributes between the stimuli (interdimensional concept; Layng, 2014), and others in terms of shared features that each stimulus has independently (i.e., intradimensional concept; Layng, 2014; i.e., natural concepts; Herrnstein et al., 1976). For the current experiment, we sought to identify variables that affect conceptual learning when the conceptual class is defined based on shared features because a wide range of concepts taught in formal and informal education are classified in this manner.

Conceptual classes defined in terms of shared features can be described in terms of these features. Features that are necessary to be an example of the concept are must‐have features, whereas features that can vary without affecting whether the stimulus is an example are can‐have features1 (Layng, 2019; Tiemann & Markle, 1990). A concept analysis (Tiemann & Markle, 1990) is a process through which the can‐have and must‐have features of a concept are identified. Once they are identified, they can be used to inform the selection of stimuli used during practice. Stimuli that have all the must‐have features are used as examples during practice. Stimuli that lack at least one must‐have feature are used as nonexamples during practice.

Williams et al. (2025) evaluated how the use of nonexamples during practice affected conceptual learning when teaching arbitrary concepts defined by three must‐have features to college students. Their results indicated that practice including nonexamples was necessary for conceptual learning. Furthermore, practice with nonexamples that lacked fewer must‐have features (i.e., close‐in nonexamples) facilitated conceptual learning relative to practice with nonexamples that lacked all must‐have features (i.e., far‐out nonexamples). However, practice typically took longer and resulted in more errors when close‐in nonexamples were used, indicating the need to refine these procedures to increase efficiency and reduce errors. In addition, Williams et al. demonstrated that adding can‐have features during the test that were not present during practice hindered conceptual learning, especially if the preceding practice used examples and far‐out nonexamples. Therefore, effective practice for conceptual learning will need to consider not only the relation between must‐have features but also the relation between can‐have features. Specifically, adding can‐have features during testing provides a more robust test of conceptual learning.

Whereas Williams et al. (2025) observed effects of can‐have features when they were added between practice and testing, the arrangement of can‐have features during practice may also affect conceptual learning. Song et al. (2021) taught college students three arbitrary concepts defined by one must‐have feature (the degree of rotation of an arrow). The stimuli had two can‐have features (color and dot quantity), each with three possible variations. Using a between‐groups design, they assigned participants to one condition of can‐have features. In the even condition, each variation of a can‐have feature was equally likely to be present. In the odd condition, some variations of a can‐have feature were present more often than others. Both groups learned the concepts similarly, but on average, participants in the even condition learned the concepts in about half the number of trials than did those in the odd condition. In addition, participants in the odd condition were more likely to demonstrate faulty stimulus control of a can‐have feature when asked to sort the features in terms of relevance.

Song et al.'s (2021) results also highlight the importance of considering multiple dependent measures when conducting research that informs teaching concepts in educational settings. Although two teaching procedures may produce similar conceptual learning, the pattern of acquisition (number of errors) and duration of time needed to learn the concept may differ. When a learner makes errors, they contact extinction, which can produce undesirable side effects for both the learner and teacher (Lattal et al., 2013). Learners need to acquire many concepts across their lifespan, so identifying methods that save even just a little time may have multiplicative effects on the amount of content instructors can cover. Efficient concept instruction is especially important given the shortage of teachers (Learning Policy Institute, 2025) and supervisors (Kranak et al., 2023) and increased practitioner demand (Behavior Analysis Certification Board, 2025).

Guidance about how to arrange can‐have features among examples and nonexamples has been provided by Tiemann and Markle (1990; see also Johnson & Bulla, 2021). They suggested selecting two examples in which all can‐have features differ (i.e., divergent examples). They also suggested using pairs of examples and nonexamples that have identical can‐have features presented (i.e., matched examples and nonexamples). Effects of matched examples and nonexamples were evaluated by Tennyson et al. (1972), who compared how well undergraduate students learned the concept of the trochaic meter between four groups. Each group was assigned to a different combination of three variables: (a) whether examples were divergent; (b) whether examples and nonexamples were matched; and (c) the range of question difficulty, as determined by testing completed before the experiment began. The lessons included only instruction (no practice or feedback). They found that participants learned the concepts best when examples were divergent, examples and nonexamples were matched, and all levels of difficulty were used. However, the comparison groups with unmatched nonexamples also differed in terms of the difficulty. Therefore, these results support that using all levels of difficulty as well as matching stimuli improves conceptual learning but does not allow for the interpretation of the effect of one of those changes in isolation.

In response to this limitation, Tennyson (1973) replicated this experiment to teach adverbs to seventh graders. He added experimental groups that isolated and demonstrated individual effects of using divergent examples, matched nonexamples, and varying difficulty among the questions. Both Tennyson (1973) and Tennyson et al. (1972) provide initial evidence that using matched examples and nonexamples improved conceptual learning. Yet, it is surprising that matched examples and nonexamples promoted better conceptual learning, not just faster acquisition. The same discriminations must occur to select a correct stimulus regardless of whether examples and nonexamples are matched. In addition, both experiments gave participants instructions that included a definition of the concepts being taught and their must‐have features. Therefore, it is possible that the stimuli needed to teach a concept using instructions (i.e., rule‐governed) are different than those needed to teach a concept using practice (i.e., contingency‐shaped). They also did a posttest‐only design, so it is possible that their participants had varying reinforcement histories related to the skill being taught and that these histories could differ systematically between groups. Therefore, it is unknown how these variables contribute to conceptual learning when the concept is taught through differential reinforcement alone (i.e., in the absence of instructions) and compared using a single‐subject design.

The primary purpose of the current study was to evaluate how well participants learned an arbitrary concept when practice stimuli included matched versus unmatched nonexamples. This comparison replicates and extends previous research demonstrating that practice stimuli consisting of examples and close‐in nonexamples (i.e., nonexamples that lack exactly one must‐have feature) facilitate conceptual learning (Williams et al., 2025). During Experiment 1, we evaluated conceptual learning during testing (no practice/differential reinforcement) before and after practice (including feedback and differential reinforcement). We also evaluated the efficiency of practice when matched or unmatched examples and nonexamples were used. This experiment extends previous research about can‐have features (Song et al., 2021; Tennyson, 1973; Tennyson et al., 1972) by using a single‐subject design, not providing any instructions related to the definition of the concept, and only varying whether the examples and nonexamples were matched between conditions. During Experiment 2, we evaluated conceptual learning during extended testing only (no practice/differential reinforcement). We compared conceptual learning when testing with some trials with some matched examples and nonexamples with that when testing with only unmatched examples and nonexamples. This experiment extends previous research by evaluating the role of repeated stimulus presentation alone when these testing arrangements are used.

Both experiments replicate and extend Williams et al. (2025) by evaluating two ways of presenting examples and close‐in nonexamples during practice and continuing to measure conceptual learning using multiple test‐trial arrangements. Most experiments arrange all test trials in the same way (e.g., Song et al., 2021; Tennyson, 1973; Tennyson et al., 1972; Williams & Roop, 2025), yet the stimuli shown during testing determine what faulty stimulus control can be detected. By contrast, in the current study, half of the test trials with untaught stimuli consisted of examples and far‐out nonexamples and the other half consisted of examples and close‐in nonexamples. Across practice conditions, we predicted higher accuracy on test trials with far‐out nonexamples based on the results of Williams et al. (2025). In addition, half used stimuli developed with the same number of can‐have features as the practice stimuli and half used stimuli with more can‐have features. Across conditions, we predicted lower accuracy on trials with additional can‐have features than other testing arrangements based on the results of Williams et al. (2025). Therefore, the outcomes of our test provide more detail about the conditions under which conceptual learning could be demonstrated.

Before beginning the experiment, all participants signed an informed consent form approved by the university's institutional review board. Participants were informed that they would earn between $10 and $20 per hour. If the earnings fell outside of this range, they were adjusted to the nearest value within the range. The session lasted up to 3 hr, and a 5‐min break occurred after the first block completed more than 50 min since the last break and when specified in the Procedure. To incentivize staying for the entire session, participants were given a bonus equal to 25% of their earnings if they stayed for the full duration or finished the experiment. Compensation was distributed via email in the form of a digital gift card rounded up to the nearest $5 increment.

Setting and apparatus

Participants completed the session alone in a space separated from the rest of the laboratory by walls on three sides and a foldable barrier on the fourth. The room contained a chair, table, tablet, and headphones. All personal items brought into the session were placed in a secure space on the other side of the foldable barrier, and these items were only available to the participants during breaks. The experimental task was presented using a custom Visual Basic program on a Dell Latitude 7320 Detachable tablet with a touch screen (33.02‐cm display; 1920‐ × 1280‐pixel resolution). No keyboard or mouse was available; responses were made by touching the screen. Noise‐canceling headphones were connected to the tablet, and sounds were played at 30% volume. If, at any point, a participant did not respond to the trial within 1 min, the stimuli disappeared from the screen and a prompt was provided saying, “please respond as fast as you can.” This only occurred for P5, who received two prompts during the posttest for the matched condition. Because our population was college students, sessions were conducted at a wide range of times from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m.

Experimental stimuli

We used a set of 72 stimuli: 24 stimuli that were used for practice and testing (i.e., practice stimuli) and 48 stimuli that were used only during testing to evaluate conceptual learning (i.e., untaught stimuli). Which stimuli were used during practice and testing are specified in the relevant sections of the Procedure. The kind of arbitrary stimuli, which differ in terms of the number of can‐have features, were labeled based on the names given in Williams et al. (2025; i.e., Arbitrary Stimuli B and Arbitrary Stimuli C). We taught college students arbitrary stimuli that mimicked the kind of concepts taught in education settings while ensuring that all participants had no history with these or related concepts.

Experimental design

The experiment used an A‐B design in which participants were taught two different concepts using two different practice conditions: matched and unmatched. During the matched condition, each practice trial consisted of one example and three matched (shared the same variation of each can‐have feature) close‐in nonexamples. During the unmatched condition, each trial consisted of one example and three unmatched (randomly mixed variations of can‐have features) close‐in nonexamples. Note that the nonexamples presented during practice in both conditions were created the same way. The pool of nonexamples contained three matched nonexamples for each example, each missing one of the must‐have features. What differed between conditions was whether the three matched nonexamples appeared during the same trial as each other and the example to which they were matched.

Stimuli in each condition were filled with a different color (blue or yellow). The concept assigned to each condition remained the same across all participants. The concepts should be equally difficult because they are based on three similar but independent features. Participants were assigned to a condition order, which was counterbalanced across participants in blocks of two. Participants completed a pretest, practice, and posttest for one condition before beginning the pretest for the next condition. Although sessions were divided into blocks of 24 trials for the purposes of data analysis, there was no indication to participants of the end of one block and the beginning of the next except when instructions or a break was presented between blocks.

Trial structure

Each trial consisted of the presentation of a trial initiation response, four stimuli, participant selection of one stimulus, and the delivery of feedback. At the start of each trial, a “start” button appeared in the center of the screen. Once touched, the button disappeared and four stimuli appeared on the screen equidistant from each other and the sides of the screen. When a stimulus was touched, all stimuli disappeared and feedback was immediately delivered. For correct responses, participants received the feedback “Right! + $0.10,” accompanied by a 1‐s trumpet sound. The total amount of money earned appeared on the screen for 0.25 s, disappeared for 0.25 s, and reappeared for 0.5 s, with $0.10 added to the total. For incorrect responses, participants received the feedback “Wrong. ‐$0.10,” accompanied by a 1‐s buzzer sound. The total amount of money earned appeared on the screen for 0.25 s, disappeared for 0.25 s, and reappeared for 0.5 s, with $0.10 subtracted from the total. All forms of feedback were followed by a 1‐s intertrial interval with a plain white screen after which the “start” button for the next trial appeared. If a participant responded incorrectly during a practice trial, a correction trial followed. The correction trial used the same four stimuli in shuffled locations and feedback procedures but was not counted as one of the 24 trials in a data block. Correction trials continued until a correct response was made. Earnings from each condition were carried over such that participants began the practice in each condition with the amount of money they had earned up to that point. Neutral feedback consisted of the word “Selected” without any auditory or monetary stimulus.

Practice

Before the first practice block in each condition, the following instructions were presented:You will be shown four images at a time. You will earn $0.10 each time you select a correct image and lose $0.10 each time you select an incorrect image. When you select an image, you will be told whether your selection is right or wrong. When you are done, a message will appear on the center of the screen telling you to knock on the door behind you.A start button was placed below the instructions; once touched, the practice block began.

You will be shown four images at a time. You will earn $0.10 each time you select a correct image and lose $0.10 each time you select an incorrect image. When you select an image, you will be told whether your selection is right or wrong. When you are done, a message will appear on the center of the screen telling you to knock on the door behind you.

After each response, participants received feedback indicating whether their responses were correct or incorrect as described in Trial structure.

Each practice block consisted of 24 trials, and each stimulus was shown an equal number of times during each block. All trials contained four stimuli: one example and three close‐in nonexamples (lacked exactly one must‐have feature). Whether the can‐have features of the stimuli were matched varied across conditions as described in the Experimental design. Practice ended once the participant demonstrated correct responding in 23/24 trials for three consecutive blocks, and a break was provided.

Posttest

After the final practice block and a break, participants completed one additional practice block. If they responded correctly in 23 out of 24 trials, they proceeded to the posttest. If they did not meet the mastery criterion, they continued additional practice blocks until the criterion was met. When the participant reached the mastery criterion, they received another break and the cycle repeated. Once the posttest began, it was identical to the pretest.

Dependent measures

To evaluate accuracy, we divided the number of correct responses on trials of that type by the total number of trials of that type in that block and multiplied by 100 to calculate the percentage of correct responses.

To evaluate efficiency, we used three interrelated measures to capture different attributes of responding. Duration provides a socially important measure of the temporal resources the practice required. We calculated duration as the total amount of time the participant spent in practice. We also measured the number of error correction trials needed during each block. More error correction trials will increase the duration but also increase contact with extinction. Practice that requires the same time but results in fewer errors is more desirable than practice that results in more errors. We also considered the number of blocks participants completed during practice. This indicates how many times participants saw each stimulus because each stimulus was presented the same number of times during each practice block. Latency or response time is the amount of time between the presentation of the stimuli and the response. Although data on latency for each trial were collected, we did not include these data because they are correlated with duration and duration is a more socially relevant measure.

During both conditions, accuracy for all participants was perfect by the second practice block and fewer than two incorrect responses were emitted during successive blocks. Responding for all participants met the mastery criterion during practice in both conditions. In general, few errors were made after the first practice block.

Relative to the pretest data, all participants increased their scores on at least one trial type during the posttest. All participants made few or no errors on trials with practice stimuli, demonstrating maintenance of these responses. Four of five participants (all except P5) made few or no errors on trials with untaught examples and nonexamples with Arbitrary Stimuli C with both types of nonexamples. Responding for P5 was consistently inaccurate on trials with untaught examples and far‐out nonexamples with Arbitrary Stimuli C in the matched condition. Regarding untaught examples and nonexamples with Arbitrary Stimuli B, the results were idiosyncratic across participants. In most cases, accuracy remained similar to baseline, as shown by frequently overlapping data. An exception was P5, who met 100% accuracy for untaught examples and far‐out nonexamples with Arbitrary Stimuli B in the unmatched condition.

Discussion

The practice arranged in this experiment (using examples and close‐in nonexamples) promoted conceptual learning and did so more efficiently when the examples and nonexamples were matched. Relative to the unmatched condition, the matched condition required fewer practice blocks, fewer correction trials, and less time to complete for four of the five participants regardless of the condition order. These findings complement those of Song et al. (2021), who found that equally presenting each can‐have feature variation expedited conceptual learning. In combination, there is continued support that the way can‐have features are presented for practice affects the efficiency of practice but not the conceptual learning outcomes.

The sensitivity of the measures of efficiency is limited by the mastery criterion and method. Recall that four practice blocks (three to meet the mastery criterion and one after the break and immediately before the posttest) must be completed with at least 23/24 correct for the posttest to begin. All participants responded correctly during at least 23/24 trials by the second block and continued to respond at this level for all remaining blocks. This meant that participants spent more practice time completing blocks to satisfy the mastery criterion than they did acquiring the discrimination. However, differences in efficiency are especially apparent in the first practice block, indicating these differences are not a product of the mastery criterion but rather the practice condition.

The magnitude of the difference in efficiency between matched and unmatched practice varied across measures. Whether the difference in efficiency is socially significant likely depends on the population and setting. For example, if a learner has a history of challenging behavior following incorrect responses, this decrease of 7.4 errors, on average, is likely substantial. In addition, in the matched examples and nonexamples condition, once a correct response was emitted once or twice, all other responses were correct. The social significance of this difference should be evaluated in future research. To further inform the decision about whether to use matched or unmatched practice, the social validity of each should be measured, as a strong preference may outweigh the difference in efficiency.

Conceptual learning outcomes were similar across the matched and unmatched practice conditions. All participants except one demonstrated conceptual learning (perfect accuracy across more than two blocks) on trials with Arbitrary Stimuli C. These trials represent typical procedures for testing conceptual learning during which the stimuli presented have the same number of can‐have features as those used during practice. P5 showed consistently low accuracy on trials with far‐out nonexamples in the matched practice condition. This result is unexpected because fewer discriminations are needed to select the correct stimulus between examples and far‐out nonexamples than with close‐in nonexamples. It is possible that responding was under faulty stimulus control of one of the replacement features, which would appear more frequently during trials with far‐out nonexamples than those with close‐in nonexamples. It is also possible that the concept itself came under stimulus control of the trial type such that the trial type was part of a four‐term contingency.

There were few systematic differences between test trials with Arbitrary Stimuli B and far‐out nonexamples compared with close‐in nonexamples, but when there was a difference, responding tended to be more accurate on trials with far‐out nonexamples (P4 matched practice condition; P5 unmatched practice condition). Evidence of conceptual learning during trials with Arbitrary Stimuli B only occurred for P2 and P5 and only following practice with unmatched examples and nonexamples. This suggests that lengthier practice or practice with unmatched nonexamples could facilitate conceptual learning when can‐have features are added. Future research could manipulate practice duration (i.e., overlearning; e.g., Dougherty & Johnston, 1996) or set mastery criteria based on rate of correct responding (e.g., Binder, 1993) to identify variables that affect when conceptual learning will be demonstrated when can‐have features are added.

The current results differ from those of previous experiments comparing matched and unmatched stimuli using group designs in classroom settings with instruction rather than practice (Tennyson, 1973; Tennyson et al., 1972). These experiments observed better conceptual learning following instruction with matched stimuli than with unmatched stimuli. However, the difference in findings is most likely due to three differences that resulted from their use of a between‐groups design and instructions rather than practice. First, they did not compare the skills prior to instruction, so there may have been group differences. Second, the skills they taught required several prerequisite skills (e.g., reading comprehension). These skills were not measured, and if they differed between groups, it would affect the results. We used a single‐subject design and arbitrary concepts based on visual properties to minimize the influence of prerequisite skills. Third, they provided rules with examples and nonexamples but no practice with participant responding. Therefore, it is possible that using matched examples and nonexamples only results in different conceptual learning when provided as part of rules/instructions but not when practice (responding and differential reinforcement) is used. Future research should repeat a classroom‐based evaluation of examples and nonexamples combining the design of the current experiment to address the limitations of these previous experiments. In addition, both Tennyson (1973) and Tennyson et al. (1972) supported the use of examples and nonexamples that get increasingly difficult rather than using practice questions that are all at the same level of difficulty. Future research could use the current procedure but gradually manipulate difficulty by adding can‐have features to see if this enhances conceptual learning with more can‐have features.

One limitation to the detection of conceptual learning in this paradigm is elevated pretest responding. One potential reason for high test scores is that the test (stimulus presentation) alone may teach the concepts. Recall that conceptual learning has been demonstrated after just instructions (Tennyson, 1973; Tennyson et al., 1972; Williams & Roop, 2025). However, in the current experiment, we intentionally minimized instructions. Still, it is possible that conceptual learning could occur through repeated exposure to the stimuli and recognition of patterns among the stimuli. These observations could interact with participant histories and self‐produced rules to result in accurate responding, and the type of practice trial stimuli used during testing (matched or unmatched) may alter the likelihood that accurate rules are generated.

EXPERIMENT 2

The results of Experiment 1 replicated previous findings that conceptual learning occurs after pretest, practice, and posttest with examples and close‐in nonexamples (Williams et al., 2025). The results also support that using matched examples and close‐in nonexamples expedited mastery when presented during practice. However, it is possible that the conceptual learning observed could be attributed to prolonged exposure to the stimuli, not to the differential reinforcement of selecting a stimulus with particular features provided during practice. Therefore, Experiment 2 evaluated conceptual learning during prolonged exposure to the stimuli without practice. If participants' responses were consistently accurate, this would indicate that conceptual learning can take place without differential reinforcement (i.e., feedback) and instructions.

Discussion

The purpose of Experiment 2 was to investigate the possibility that extended exposure to the stimuli in the test trial arrangements was sufficient to produce conceptual learning. If participants' responses were consistently accurate, this would indicate that differential reinforcement (i.e., feedback) is not necessary for conceptual learning. However, the results support that conceptual learning does require differential reinforcement. Although participants could select a correct stimulus repeatedly, indicating discrimination between stimulus feature patterns, they did not do so for prolonged periods, indicating that the selection of the stimulus was not reinforced, so they did not learn the concept.

Repeated blocks with accurate responding were most likely during whichever condition was experienced second, so it seems accurate responding is more likely the longer participants are exposed to the test. Patterns in accurate selection seemed to be more likely on trials with far‐out nonexamples than on those with close‐in nonexamples and on trials with Arbitrary Stimuli C compared with Arbitrary Stimuli B. Recall that the far‐out nonexamples lacked all three must‐have features, and each of those must‐have features was replaced with one of two replacement features. This means that during trials with an example and three far‐out nonexamples, the correct response likely differed more from the incorrect responses than the incorrect responses differed from each other. Therefore, depending on the stimuli assigned to the trial, the participant may select the correct stimulus by selecting the one that is most unlike the others. Recall also that Arbitrary Stimuli C had fewer features than Arbitrary Stimuli B. Therefore, it may be easier for participants to identify patterns.

In addition to occasional repeated selection of the correct stimulus, some participants also repeatedly selected the incorrect stimulus. The role of reinforcement in conceptual learning is also apparent. Responding was clearly sensitive to stimulus patterns. However, responding was exactly incorrect across more than two blocks five times (10%), which is more than responding was perfectly accurate (6%). Participants who respond repeatedly with perfect or exactly inaccurate accuracy may only differ from other participants in the extent to which they stuck with a particular response pattern. The relative likelihood of repeatedly selecting the correct stimulus (above‐chance responding) and an incorrect stimulus (below‐chance responding) illustrates the separate roles of feature pattern recognition and differential reinforcement. Both are necessary to produce conceptual learning.

GENERAL DISCUSSION

Robust and consistent conceptual learning was demonstrated with untaught stimuli with the same can‐have features as those presented during practice. Note that all test trials with untaught stimuli contained unmatched examples and nonexamples, and responding by participants who practiced (Experiment 1 only) with matched examples and nonexamples had never been differentially reinforced for responding in the presence of unmatched examples and nonexamples. In the case of Experiment 1, this means that practicing with examples and matched close‐in nonexamples still promoted discrimination between examples and unmatched nonexamples. In the case of Experiment 2, this means that just seeing more frequent trials with examples and matched close‐in nonexamples did not promote discrimination, as responding was not differentially reinforced.

There were no systematic differences in conceptual learning between the matched and unmatched conditions in either experiment. However, one should not expect a difference in conceptual learning outcomes between these conditions because the stimulus control required to master practice is identical across conditions. In both cases, the participants must discriminate between the presence and absence of three must‐have features to select the correct stimulus on practice trials. Doing so during the posttest will result in correct responding across all trials regardless of whether the can‐have features are matched. However, matching the can‐have features across examples and nonexamples makes the must‐have features salient because they are the only difference between stimuli, facilitating stimulus control by the must‐have features, without faulty stimulus control by any can‐have feature due to superstitious reinforcement.

There were also instances of accurate responding during the pretest, although the likelihood of these instances did not differ systematically between conditions or experiments. Experiment 2 was conducted to explore the possibility that accurate baseline responding is indicative of conceptual learning prior to differential reinforcement. The results of Experiment 2 provide clear evidence that transient, high pretest accuracies were not indicative of conceptual learning and were instead likely due to responding under the control of stimulus patterns. Responding to a particular stimulus needed to be differentially reinforced through the practice procedure to produce conceptual learning. However, the prolonged pretest resulted in participants having a history with the stimuli that may interact with differential reinforcement if or when it occurs. A shorter pretest and posttest may minimize this interaction.

Across both experiments, when accurate responding occurred during the pretest, it was likely due to self‐generated rules that governed responding based on stimulus patterns. When responding was consistently accurate (or inaccurate) across many pretest blocks, it simply means the participant did not change the rule instructing their responding. The remaining question is then to consider what aspects of the stimuli shown during those trials facilitated the development of a rule that resulted in consistently correct or incorrect responding, as these stimuli have resulted in similarly high pretest scores in previous research, too (Williams et al., 2025). Participants could select a correct stimulus based on any one feature. If they pick a feature that is a must‐have feature, then they will always select the correct stimulus during trials with far‐out nonexamples. If they pick a feature that is a replacement feature, then they will always select the incorrect stimulus on all trial types. These previous two rules can account for most of the pretest responding we observed but do not account for the occasional instances of accurate responding during trials with close‐in nonexamples. In this case, Williams et al. (2025) theorized that this effect could occur if participants develop a rule based on the assumptions that (a) there is only one correct stimulus, so the combination that indicates the correct stimulus should only occur in one stimulus, and (b) there is always a correct stimulus, so the feature(s) that indicate the correct stimulus should be present in every trial. Following this logic across trials could lead to the correct stimuli. This logic is like that taught for test taking strategies and puzzles, so it is reasonable for some participants to develop rules in this way. Accurate and inaccurate rules developed during the pretest will immediately be reinforced or punished, respectively, during the first trial of practice. For P1, accuracy on pretest trials with practice stimuli was perfect at the end of the pretest and remained perfect during the practice. The rules they developed during the pretest were reinforced during practice.

One other participant (P2) also responded accurately across all practice trials in the matched condition. Unlike P1, P2 was not responding accurately during pretest practice trials. This response is likely due to chance, which allowed it to then immediately contact reinforcement. However, because the stimuli in this trial were matched, the difference between the selected response and the unselected responses should be salient, making it clear what specific discriminations are reinforced.

In theory, conceptual learning is an all‐or‐nothing process. There is no learning curve (Sidman, 2010); once responding discriminates between must‐have features and generalizes across can‐have features, it will always be accurate. The key is identifying the fastest way to teach those features. This rapid acquisition in Experiment 1 demonstrates how effective the combination of using close‐in nonexamples (Williams et al., 2025) and matched examples and nonexamples is at teaching these concepts. The lack of acquisition in Experiment 2 demonstrates the role that differential reinforcement plays in producing discrimination. Future research could evaluate ways to facilitate initial selection of a correct stimulus, such as stimulus fading or prompting (Sidman, 2010; Terrace, 1963a, 1963b). Future research should also evaluate the degree to which this procedure facilitates conceptual learning for concepts in other formats or based on other kinds of relations.

While conceptual learning did not differ between conditions regardless of the inclusion of practice, it did differ based on the measure (test‐trial type) of conceptual learning. Accuracy was generally higher during test trials with Arbitrary Stimuli C (with both far‐out and close‐in nonexamples) than during test trials with Arbitrary Stimuli B during Experiment 1, but it was similar during Experiment 2. Recall that the difference between these stimuli is that Arbitrary Stimuli B have more can‐have features. However, in theory, the discriminations necessary to select a correct stimulus (presence or absence of each must‐have feature) did not change when can‐have features were added. These results strengthen evidence that even after a concept is learned based on the can‐have features with which it was instructed, adding can‐have features disrupts conceptual learning (Williams et al., 2025). Neither matched nor unmatched examples and nonexamples prevented this disruption. The addition of can‐have features is common between practice in structured classroom/clinical settings and use in naturalistic environments. Therefore, additional research is needed to identify ways to effectively and efficiently teach concepts such that conceptual learning occurs even when can‐have features are added. For example, one option could be evaluating conceptual learning when can‐have features are gradually added until as many can‐have features as feasible are present. Another could be evaluating conceptual learning when matched examples and nonexamples are presented first, then unmatched (or vice versa).

The unmatched condition included the same stimulus feature arrangements in the stimulus set as the stimuli in the matched condition. The main difference was that in the matched condition, the stimuli with the same can‐have features were presented together, whereas in the unmatched condition, they appeared in random mixes across trials. Therefore, it is not just the inclusion of matched stimuli in the stimulus set but the simultaneous presentation of matched stimuli in trials that expedites practice mastery and differential reinforcement that produces conceptual learning.

Overall, both practice procedures produced robust conceptual learning when no can‐have features were added during testing and differential reinforcement was in place. Practice with matched examples and nonexamples decreased the practice time needed to produce conceptual learning compared with practice with unmatched examples and nonexamples. In addition to its potential for practical application, this experiment provides a procedure (and stimuli, which are available upon request to the first author) that produces reliable, rapid conceptual learning. These procedures could then be used for evaluation of more complicated stimulus control, such as the acquisition, modification, and extinction classes consisting of multiple concepts, equivalence, and hierarchical learning.
