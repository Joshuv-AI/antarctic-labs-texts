Value of shear wave elastography in evaluating response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in breast cancer: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bin Lian, Chuanxin Ye, Fengjie Li, Huotang Qin, Lifang Zhou, Changyuan Wei, Huihui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo determine how well shear wave elastography performs as a diagnostic tool for determining the pathological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in breast cancer.MethodsThrough April 2025, a systematic search of PubMed, MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and SinoMed was used to perform a diagnostic meta-analysis. Included were studies of individuals with breast cancer receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy with shear wave elastography evaluation. The diagnostic performance of shear wave elastography was assessed using a hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic model and a bivariate random-effects model. The Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 was used to evaluate the quality of the study.ResultsThe study includes 15 trials with 1402 patients. For predicting pathological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the pooled sensitivity and specificity of shear wave elastography were 0.68 (95% confidence intervals: 0.49–0.86) and 0.73 (95% confidence intervals: 0.60–0.87), respectively. 13.06 (95% confidence intervals: 8.42–20.25) was the pooled diagnostic odds ratio. The hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic study showed a moderate diagnostic performance with an area under the curve of 0.85 (95% confidence intervals: 0.82–0.88).ConclusionsShear wave elastography may be a helpful supplementary imaging modality in clinical treatment monitoring and showed moderate diagnostic performance for assessing pathological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in breast cancer. To confirm its clinical value, more extensive prospective investigations are required. PROSPERO registration number: CRD420261435666.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors among women and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality. An estimated 1.67 million new cases are identified worldwide each year, and the disease is believed to cause 520,000 fatalities. 1 Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) was initially introduced for patients with advanced breast cancer who were no longer eligible for curative surgery. Preoperative NAC lowers the tumor burden, which may convert unresectable tumors into removable ones, thereby increasing the chances of a full pathological response. This strategy is currently used commonly and has greatly enhanced the quality of life and survival rates among patients with locally advanced breast cancer. 2 For certain individuals with breast cancer, NAC is now a part of standard treatment. 3 NAC can downstage the tumor and lessen nodal involvement, allowing breast-conserving therapy with negative surgical margins in cases wherein the original tumor is large and breast-conserving surgery would have otherwise seemed impractical. Usually, adjuvant radiation is administered postoperatively. The rate of breast conservation has significantly increased, with long-term survival results after breast-conserving surgery similar to those after modified radical mastectomy. 4

For customized therapy planning in breast cancer, early prediction of the pathological response to NAC is essential. 5 By calculating Young's modulus values and assessing tissue density, shear wave elastography (SWE) quantitatively evaluates tissue stiffness and offers an unbiased assessment of lesion elasticity. According to a recent study, SWE offers superior diagnostic specificity and interobserver repeatability than traditional ultrasound imaging. 6 The overall usefulness of SWE in tracking response to NAC in breast cancer has not yet been thoroughly assessed in a meta-analysis. In order to enhance therapeutic decision-making, the current study aimed to quantify the capability of SWE to predict NAC efficacy by examining changes in shear wave velocity before and after treatment.

Materials and methods

We conducted a systematic literature review in compliance with the Cochrane and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) criteria. 7 Since this meta-analysis was based on previously published research, patient permission and ethical approval were not necessary.

This study was registered post-study with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/), with registration number: CRD420261435666.

Literature search

A combined subject term and keyword search strategy was employed. Simple searches utilized Boolean logic to connect search terms, whereas advanced searches followed the strategy recommended in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Diagnostic Test Accuracy. Building upon an unpublished systematic review (with a search cutoff date of 2021), we performed a systematic update. We searched English databases, including the PubMed, MEDLARS Online (MEDLINE), Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (SinoMed) databases. The following search terms were used: (“preoperative therapy” OR “preoperative chemotherapy” OR “neoadjuvant chemotherapy” OR “NAC” OR “induction chemotherapy”) AND (“breast cancer” OR “breast carcinoma” OR “breast tumor” OR “breast neoplasms” OR “human mammary carcinomas”) AND (“shear wave elastography” OR “SWE” OR “real-time shear wave elastography”). We performed a manual secondary search of the references and similar documents included in the literature. All eligible studies published up to 10 April 2025 were considered for inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) prospective or retrospective diagnostic accuracy studies; (b) studies evaluating the diagnostic performance of SWE for predicting pathological response to NAC in patients with breast cancer; (c) pathological response after NAC used as the reference standard, regardless of the specific pathological response criteria adopted, including pathological complete response (pCR), major histological response (MHR), and other pathology-based response definitions; (d) full-text articles published in English; and (e) sufficient data to construct a 2 × 2 contingency table comprising true positive (TP), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), and false negative (FN) or calculate diagnostic accuracy indices, including sensitivity and specificity.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) studies unrelated to the research topic; (b) duplicate publications; (c) studies with insufficient data for diagnostic accuracy analysis; (d) commentaries; (e) letters; (f) conference abstracts; and (g) reviews.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted the following information from each eligible study: (a) first author; (b) publication year; (c) country; (d) study type; (e) number of patients; (f) patient characteristics; (g) reference standard; and (h) diagnostic accuracy data (TP, FP, TN, and FN). Any disagreements were resolved via discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2), a validated instrument created especially for assessing the risk of bias and applicability in diagnostic accuracy research. 8 Four domains were evaluated: (a) patient selection; (b) index test; (c) reference standard; and (d) flow and timing. Every domain was assessed for applicability issues and bias risk. Eleven signaling questions were used in all (Table 1). Quality assessment was performed separately by two reviewers; differences were settled via discussion or with the involvement of a third reviewer. Sensitivity analyses were informed by the quality assessment results.

Statistical analyses

Diagnostic meta-analysis was performed using Stata (version 17.0). A bivariate random-effects model was employed to jointly pool sensitivity and specificity across studies. The pooled diagnostic accuracy measures included sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (LR+), negative likelihood ratio (LR−), and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), together with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the chi-square (Q) test and quantified using the I2 statistic. In general, I2 values <30% were considered to indicate low heterogeneity, values between 30% and 70% indicated moderate heterogeneity, and values >70% indicated substantial heterogeneity. A p-value <0.1 for the Q test was considered to indicate statistically significant heterogeneity. In diagnostic meta-analysis, heterogeneity may also arise from threshold effects and differences in diagnostic cutoff values; therefore, heterogeneity was interpreted in conjunction with the hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic (HSROC) model and bivariate random-effects model. Given the presence of between-study heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used for all pooled analyses.

All the included studies used SWE-related elasticity parameters as diagnostic indicators but applied different cutoff values to define positive findings; therefore, a threshold effect was anticipated. Threshold effect and heterogeneity were evaluated by calculating the Spearman correlation coefficient between logit sensitivity and logit specificity, with a significant negative correlation indicating the presence of a threshold effect. Variances of logit sensitivity and logit specificity were also reported to quantify heterogeneity. In addition, the symmetry parameter beta in the HSROC model was examined, with p >0.05 indicating acceptance of the symmetry assumption.

Forest plots were generated to display the pooled sensitivity and specificity, whereas HSROC curves were constructed to summarize the overall diagnostic performance and assess model symmetry. When a significant threshold effect was identified, the HSROC model was considered the primary basis for interpretation.

Results

Literature search

In total, 176 possibly pertinent articles were identified (Figure 1). Among these, 15 studies examining the usefulness of SWE for assessing the effectiveness of NAC in breast cancer were included after excluding duplicate records, irrelevant studies, and non-data review articles.

Characteristics and quality assessment of the included studies

The analysis included 15 studies in total. The QUADAS-2 instrument was used to evaluate the study quality (Table 1). Most studies exhibited minimal risk of bias in the areas of patient selection, index test, reference standard, flow, and timing, indicating generally acceptable methodological quality. In some areas, a few studies indicated ambiguous or high risk of bias. The 15 included studies involved a total of 1402 participants; the sample sizes of the studies ranged from 28 to 255. Nine of the included studies were performed in China, two in South Korea, and one each in Egypt, Germany, India, and the United Kingdom (Table 2).

Bivariate model analysis

The 15 included studies were subjected to a diagnostic meta-analysis using a bivariate random-effects model, which effectively converged (log-likelihood = −87.22). SWE had a pooled sensitivity of 0.68 (95% CI: 0.49–0.86) and pooled specificity of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.60–0.87) for predicting the pathological response to NAC in breast cancer. Furthermore, the LR + was 3.97 (95% CI: 2.80–5.63), LR− was 0.30 (95% CI: 0.23–0.41), and pooled DOR was 13.06 (95% CI: 8.42–20.25), demonstrating a reasonable diagnostic performance of SWE in assessing NAC response.

Heterogeneity and threshold effect analysis

The logit sensitivity and logit specificity variances were 0.52 and 0.71, respectively, indicating a modest degree of study heterogeneity. There was a significant negative association between logit sensitivity and logit specificity, as indicated by the correlation coefficient of −0.79. These results, when combined with the symmetry parameter beta in the HSROC model (β = 0.15, p = 0.588), indicated the existence of a threshold effect, indicating that different studies used different SWE cutoff values to define positive results, resulting in a trade-off relationship between sensitivity and specificity.

Model diagnostics and robustness assessment

A substantial departure from the bivariate elliptical symmetry assumption was indicated by the elliptical symmetry assessment's QQ correlation coefficient of 0.97 and maximum absolute deviation of 3.84. One possible outlier study outside the outer ellipse was jointly identified using a bivariate box plot (Figure 2) and a Mahalanobis distance Q-Q plot (Figure 3). The diagnostic results were further supported by the Kendall plot (Figure 4), which demonstrated a degree of divergence from the desired bivariate normal distribution pattern between sensitivity and specificity. Overall, although there was some model deviation, it was not large enough to compromise the applicability of the bivariate random-effects model; therefore, this model was used for further investigations.

HSROC curve and forest plots analysis

The area under the curve (AUC) for SWE in predicting pathological response to NAC in breast cancer was 0.85 (95% CI: 0.82–0.88), indicating reasonable diagnostic performance, according to HSROC curve analysis (Figure 5). Sensitivity and specificity varied between trials, as observed on the diagnostic forest plots (Figure 6). Sensitivity and specificity ratings ranged from 49% to 86% and 60% to 87%, respectively, in most studies. Overall, there was moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 60%, 95% CI: 0.54–0.66). In line with the previously noted outlier research, a few studies significantly varied from the general trend; however, the overall direction of effect remained largely consistent, indicating that the pooled estimates were generally representative.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis was performed for the detected outlier study. Refitting the bivariate model after this study was excluded led to unstable model convergence, with a non-invertible covariance matrix, indicating that the outlier study was crucial for preserving model stability. Thus, the study was retained in the final analysis and carefully evaluated.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses based on several reference standard criteria, such as pCR, MHR, response, and significant response, were performed initially. Different outcome definitions resulted in variations in pooled sensitivity and specificity. However, standardized subgroup meta-analysis was not performed because the definitions of pathological response positive and grouping procedures were not identical across studies, and certain subgroups contained only a small number of studies.

Publication bias

Deeks’ funnel plot was used to evaluate publication bias (Figure 7). Regression analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant effect (p = 0.070 (>0.05)). There was no conclusive evidence of publication bias despite the funnel plot's slight asymmetry, pointing solely to a potential small-study effect.

Discussion

A multimodal treatment approach, which includes the administration of NAC followed by surgery and radiotherapy, is frequently used to treat locally advanced breast cancer. 24 NAC has been used more frequently in individuals with locally advanced illness in recent years. 25 The Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) recommends NAC for patients who require to undergo a mastectomy or have locally advanced breast cancer. 26 To guide the final surgical decision, accurate evaluation of remaining malignant lesions during neoadjuvant treatment is crucial. Currently, pathological investigation and clinical assessment, including physical examination and imaging evaluation, are the primary methods used to evaluate NAC response in breast cancer. 27 However, clinical palpation is very operator-dependent and may be influenced by subjective clinical experience, especially for the evaluation of deeply situated lesions.

Pathological testing remains the most dependable technique for assessing therapy response. 28 However, because pathological evaluation can only be performed postoperatively, it cannot offer real-time data for treatment modification during NAC. The use of imaging methods for NAC response evaluation has increasing significantly owing to their noninvasive, practical, and reproducible qualities. Currently, conventional ultrasonography, SWE, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are employed to evaluate NAC response.

The primary reliance of conventional ultrasonography on morphological features, such as lesion form, echogenicity, surrounding tissue alterations, and calcifications, is a significant constraint when assessing residual tumor burden. However, because residual fibrotic stroma may persist and resemble malignant tissue, NAC-induced tumor cell death does not always reflect as complete remission on imaging. After chemotherapy, it might be difficult to distinguish between fibrosis and residual tumor, which can lead to underestimation or overestimation of residual lesions on traditional ultrasonic imaging.

Another crucial imaging technique for assessing the NAC response in breast cancer is MRI. MRI offers high sensitivity in the evaluation of the size of residual tumors following chemotherapy and in the identification of initial invasive breast cancers. Additionally, MRI can assist in distinguishing between chemotherapy-induced fibrosis or necrosis and residual tumor tissue, making it easier to choose the best candidates for breast-conserving surgery after NAC. One of the best imaging techniques for identifying multifocal and multicentric breast cancer lesions is MRI. However, the comparatively high cost of MRI limits its extensive clinical application despite its excellent diagnostic accuracy.

With the advancement of multiparametric ultrasound techniques, elastography has become a viable noninvasive and repeatable imaging approach for evaluating NAC response in breast cancer. 29 SWE allows quantitative evaluation of tissue stiffness; therefore, it has emerged as a significant field of study in ultrasound imaging in recent years. The measurement of shear wave propagation velocity within tissues, which exhibits good reproducibility, is the basic concept of SWE. 30 The structural makeup and mechanical characteristics of tissues are intimately related. 31 The elastic properties of tissues and organs also alter when pathological alterations occur. Chemotherapy-sensitive tumors typically undergo macroscopic degeneration and necrosis after NAC, along with tumor-associated vascular atrophy and occlusion, which cause the tumor to shrink and soften. 32

SWE is a sophisticated ultrasonic elastography method that yields quantitative measurements of tissue elasticity in addition to standard B-mode pictures. In contrast to strain elastography, SWE employs low-intensity tracking pulses to measure tissue motion following localized tissue displacement caused by a short-duration, high-intensity acoustic radiation force impulse inside the region of interest (ROI). Deep tissue stiffness can be accurately measured using this method. 33 Shear wave propagation velocity is measured using SWE, which has good consistency and quantifies tissue stiffness in kilopascals (kPa) or meters per second (m/s).34–36 The ROI can be used to calculate a number of elasticity metrics, such as maximum elasticity (Emax), mean elasticity (Emean), minimum elasticity (Emin), SD, and lesion-to-background elasticity ratio (Eratio). Good diagnostic performance has also been reported for qualitative SWE pattern classification.37,38

With a pooled sensitivity of 0.68, a pooled specificity of 0.73, and an AUC of 0.85, SWE showed moderate diagnostic efficacy in predicting NAC response in breast cancer in the present study. These results imply that SWE could be a helpful supplementary method for assessing pathological response following NAC. Similar results have been observed in earlier studies. Gampenrieder et al. reported sensitivity and specificity values of 75% and 67%, respectively, for MRI in the assessment of NAC response in breast cancer. 39 Kontopodis et al. reported similar findings. 40 In general, these conclusions align with the findings of the present study. As reported by Lee et al., following NAC, the Emax value in patients with remaining malignancy was considerably higher than that in patients without residual lesions. 15 . Both SWE alone and SWE in conjunction with conventional ultrasonography demonstrated much greater sensitivity and specificity than two-dimensional ultrasound and exhibited diagnostic performance comparable with that of MRI when the highest elasticity threshold was set at >30 kPa. 41

Although the findings revealed strong diagnostic performance of SWE in predicting pathological response to NAC in breast cancer, a certain percentage of FN and FP still occurred. The false negative rate (FNR) and false positive rate (FPR) were 32.0% and 27.0%, respectively, based on the pooled sensitivity (68.0%) and specificity (73.0%). The intrinsic inaccuracy of the diagnostic test is reflected in these theoretical values. However, PPV and NPV are more important to doctors in clinical practice since they show how well SWE-based predictions match actual disease responses.

The NPV of the included studies ranged from 0.43 to 1.00, indicating a clinical FNR (1−NPV) of up to 57%, whereas the PPV ranged from 0.40 to 0.96, indicating a clinical FPR (1−PPV) of up to 60% (Table 2). Between-study variations in the prevalence (defined as the percentage of patients with a positive pathological response), study population characteristics, molecular subtype distribution, and definitions of pathological response—all of which significantly impact the PPV and NPV—are primarily responsible for the significant disparity between the theoretical FNR (24.7%) and the maximum clinical FNR (57%). FN results (SWE indicating “non-response” when the pathological response is positive) can cause doctors to change therapeutic regimens, prematurely switch to surgery, or stop effective chemotherapy, which lowers the pCR rate and negatively impacts patient prognosis. FP results (SWE showing “response” when the pathological reaction is actually negative) can lead to unnecessary drug-related harm, delayed surgical scheduling, and the continuation of ineffective treatment. Therefore, rather than being used as a stand-alone decision-making tool, SWE should be used as an auxiliary screening or dynamic monitoring technique for evaluating response to NAC in breast cancer. In clinical practice, in order to lower the risk of misclassification inherent to any one imaging modality, it is recommended to combine SWE with pathological assessment, biopsy, MRI, and conventional ultrasound.

Logit sensitivity and logit specificity showed a strong negative correlation in the current study (Spearman correlation coefficient r = −0.79), and the HSROC model's beta parameter was not statistically significant (β = 0.15, p = 0.588), suggesting the existence of a threshold effect. This result implies a trade-off connection between sensitivity and specificity because different studies used varied cutoff values for SWE elasticity parameters. The existence of a threshold effect was further corroborated by the shoulder-arm pattern observed on the HSROC curve. One of the main causes of heterogeneity in this study seemed to be the threshold effect. Crucially, the presence of a threshold effect does not contradict SWE's diagnostic utility; rather, it suggests that varying cutoff values affect how sensitivity and specificity are balanced in clinical practice.

The differences in reference standard definitions between research were another significant source of variability. pCR, MHR, response, and significant response were among the criteria utilized by the included studies to establish pathological response positive. The lack of residual invasive carcinoma in both original tumor and lymph nodes is necessary for pCR, as acknowledged by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration as a crucial objective for assessing NAC efficacy in breast cancer. However, MHR and associated definitions allow for a certain degree of residual tumor burden. Thus, although studies employing MHR-based criteria assessed significant but incomplete responses, those utilizing pCR as the reference standard needed diagnostic procedures to identify complete responders. These differences in response definitions could result in systematic variations in reported sensitivity and specificity.

The molecular subtype of breast cancer may also influence the diagnostic performance of SWE in evaluating NAC response. Previous studies have demonstrated that different molecular subtypes of breast cancer, including Luminal A, Luminal B, Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2)–positive, and triple-negative breast cancer, exhibit distinct responses to NAC. However, the included studies lacked uniform standards for molecular subtype classification and grouping strategies. Most studies classified patients according to conventional molecular subtypes, whereas some adopted alternative grouping methods based on immunohistochemical findings or SWE-related clinical characteristics. For example, Huang et al. 21 classified patients according to combinations of hormone receptor (HR) and HER2 status, whereas Wang et al. 18 stratified patients based on SWE-related characteristics, including HER2 positivity, skin involvement, retromammary space invasion, and muscle infiltration. Owing to substantial inconsistencies in the classification criteria used and outcome definitions across studies, it was difficult to establish a unified framework for subgroup analysis. Consequently, further subgroup analyses according to molecular subtype and outcome definition were not performed. These inconsistencies may also have contributed to inter-study heterogeneity and affected the accurate evaluation of SWE diagnostic performance. Future studies should adopt standardized molecular subtype classifications and pathological response criteria to further clarify the clinical utility of SWE in different breast cancer subtypes.

Additionally, variations in outcome criteria, the timing of SWE measurements during NAC; ultrasound equipment and imaging techniques used; and patient-related variables, including age, could have contributed to the variability. When interpreting the pooled results, several aspects should be considered.

Compared with previous meta-analyses, the present study focused exclusively on SWE, thereby reducing technical heterogeneity introduced by strain elastography. In addition, the literature search was updated till April 2025 to incorporate the most recent evidence. Furthermore, the HSROC model was used to account for threshold effects, and PPV and NPV were evaluated to obtain additional insights into the clinical applicability of SWE. These findings also highlight the need for future studies to standardize pathological response criteria, imaging protocols, and assessment time points to improve the comparability and clinical applicability of SWE.

This study has certain limitations that should be noted. First, very few studies with small sample sizes were included. Second, studies showed significant heterogeneity, partially owing to the threshold effects and inconsistent definitions of pathological response. Third, linguistic bias might have been introduced because only English-language studies were considered. Fourth, the retrospective registration of this review may have introduced bias in our results. Finally, a tiny asymmetry indicated the potential presence of a mild small-study impact, even if Deeks’ funnel plot analysis did not show statistically significant publication bias. The present results should be regarded with caution and more extensive prospective investigations should be conducted to confirm the diagnostic ability of SWE.

Conclusion

SWE demonstrated good diagnostic performance in predicting the pathological response to NAC in breast cancer and could be a helpful supplement to traditional imaging evaluation. The noninvasive, practical, and repeatable nature of SWE make it a potentially useful clinical tool for therapeutic monitoring and customized management, although it cannot currently replace pathological examination. To confirm its therapeutic utility, more extensive prospective trials with uniform diagnostic thresholds and pathological response criteria are necessary.
