The Root Shapes the Fruit: On the Persistence of Gender-Exclusive Harms in Aligned Language Models
Anaelia Ovalle, Krunoslav Lehman Pavasovic, Louis Martin, Luke Zettlemoyer, Eric Michael Smith, Kai-Wei Chang, Adina Williams, Levent Sagun

Abstract
. Natural-language assistants are designed to provide users with helpful responses while avoiding harmful outputs, largely achieved through alignment to human preferences. Yet there is limited understanding of whether alignment techniques may inadvertently perpetuate or even amplify harmful biases inherited from their pre-aligned base models. This issue is compounded by the choice of bias evaluation benchmarks in popular preference-finetuned models, which predominantly focus on dominant social categories, such as binary gender, thereby limiting insights into biases affecting underrepresented groups. To address this gap, we center transgender, nonbinary, and other gender-diverse (TGNB) identities to investigate how alignment procedures encode and interact with harmful TGNB biases in LLMs. Our key contributions include: 1) a comprehensive survey of bias evaluation modalities across leading preference-finetuned LLMs, highlighting critical gaps in gender-diverse representation, 2) systematic evaluation of gender-diverse biases across 16 publicly-available models across Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) stages, uncovering harms popular bias benchmarks fail to detect, and 3) a flexible framework for measuring harmful gender-diverse biases in implicit reward signals applicable to other social contexts. Our findings suggest that DPO-aligned models are particularly sensitive to supervised finetuning (SFT), and may amplify two forms of real-world gender-diverse harms reflected in their base models: stigmatization and gender non-affirmative language. We conclude with recommendations tailored to DPO and broader alignment practices, advocating for the adoption of community-informed bias evaluation frameworks to more effectively identify and address underrepresented harms in LLMs.

Introduction

Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences has emerged as a promising approach for instilling helpful model behavior across a wide array of language tasks
(
Zhang et al., 2023
;
Askell et al., 2021
;
Bai et al., 2022b
;
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
.
However, these models must serve diverse communities effectively to achieve their intended impact.
Pre-trained LLMs, from which aligned models are finetuned, can perpetuate harmful biases that disproportionately affect marginalized groups, including transgender, non-binary and other gender-diverse minorities
(
Dev et al., 2021
;
Hossain et al., 2023
;
Ovalle et al., 2023
;
Ovalle et al., 2024
;

)
.
Although alignment techniques like Direct Preference Optimization
(

)
can steer models towards helpful and harmless behavior
(
Chowdhury et al., 2024
;
Park et al., 2024
;
Saeidi et al., 2024
)
, there is limited understanding of how harmful social biases already present in their base models may persist or even amplify after alignment.

These gaps motivate our investigation into how alignment procedures interact with LLM gender biases, particularly those affecting transgender, nonbinary, and other gender-diverse identities (TGNB) - an area that remains

Towards addressing these evaluation gaps, we focus on two key research questions: (1)
Can pre-existing gender-diverse biases be amplified even after alignment towards helpful and harmless behavior?
(2)
How are these biases encoded in the alignment process?

To answer these questions, we systematically evaluate 16 LLMs for gender-diverse bias across their pre-trained, supervised fine-tuned (SFT)

(
Rafailov et al., 2023

Using TGNB-specific benchmarks designed to capture real-world harms, we examine whether these biases persist in aligned models despite finetuning for helpfulness and harmlessness.
As alignment procedures rely on preference-derived reward signals

Rafailov et al., 2023
)

Demonstrated with gender-diverse biases yet adaptable to other social contexts, this framework allows us to trace bias propagation across alignment stages, revealing avenues for targeted bias intervention.
This framework is coupled with a thematic analysis of TGNB stigma found across DPO-aligned LLMs.

Our findings reveal that aligned LLMs can not only perpetuate but also amplify existing TGNB biases found in their base models, aspects which cannot be detected by popularly employed LLM bias benchmarks.
Specifically, we observe that DPO-aligned LLMs can (1)
exacerbate gender non-affirmative outputs when initialized from SFT models displaying similar biases

4.1
)
(2) reflect implicit reward signals reinforcing TGNB social stigma
and (3) preserve biased reward signals from their base models (§

Furthermore, our thematic analysis reveals aligned LLMs can reflect documented patterns of TGNB stigma, including harmful stereotypes that characterize gender-diverse individuals as mentally unstable
(
Reed et al., 2015
)
(§
5.3.3
).
Guided by our findings, we offer recommendations contextualized to both bias evaluation and alignment procedures to better understand and address these harms (§

3.
Evaluating Chat-based LLMs with Human Feedback

We first introduce an overview of preference finetuning and our experimental setup before assessing LLM biases against gender minorities with respect to 1) their textual outputs 2) preference data and 3) implicit reward signals.

3.1.
Preference Fine-tuning Overview

LLM preference fine-tuning typically involves two major stages after pretraining: supervised fine-tuning a pretrained LLM on task-specific instruction data (SFT)
(
Zhou et al., 2023
)
, and preference optimization.
Following SFT, preference datasets are generated by annotators who rank outputs produced by the SFT policy,
\pi_{\text{SFT}}
.
3
3

3

To mitigate the distribution shift between the true reference distribution (which may unavailable) and the reference model used by DPO, SFT maximizes the likelihood of preferred completions when the SFT sampling is not available, per
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
.

Annotators most commonly indicate preferences in a pairwise fashion following a Bradley-Terry (BT) model
(
Bradley and Terry, 1952
)
, where for each input
x
, annotators select a preferred output,
y_{c}
, over a less preferred one,
y_{r}
. This process yields a comparison dataset
\mathcal{D}={(x^{(i)},y_{c}^{(i)},y_{r}^{(i)})}_{i=1}^{N}
, which is assumed to reflect an underlying latent reward function
r^{*}(x,y)
that, while not directly observable, guides the selection of preferred outcomes.
The preference dataset
\mathcal{D}
is then used to further refine
\pi_{\text{SFT}}
, resulting in a final policy
\pi_{\theta}
through either online or offline methods. In the online approach, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) approximates the latent reward function
r^{*}(x,y)
by explicitly parameterizing a reward model
r_{\phi}(x,y)
and maximizing parameters over
\mathcal{D}
with a negative log-likelihood objective.
\pi_{\theta}
is subsequently optimized using approaches like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
to maximize the reward function:

(1)

r(x,y)=r_{\phi}(x,y)-\beta(\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x))

where
\beta
is a regularization parameter controlling the deviation from a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
and preferences are sampled from
\pi_{\theta}
in real time with an assigned a reward from
r_{\phi}
.
In contrast, offline finetuning with Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) bypasses explicit reward modeling by implicitly aligning the policy with
r^{*}(x,y)
through a change-of-variables and is conducted over a static set of preferences
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
.
The reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
is typically initialized from
\pi_{\text{SFT}}
, or, if unavailable, maximizes the likelihood of preferred completions.

Gender-Diverse Bias

4.1.
Task Setup

We employ the
tango
dataset
(
Ovalle et al., 2023
)
to investigate whether aligned models reflect gender-diverse biases in their textual outputs.
This dataset is specifically designed to evaluate an LLM’s tendency for generating harmful, gender-non-affirmative language in the context of
gender disclosure
.
Gender disclosure, often referred to as “coming out”, is a distinct and vulnerable experience for TGNB persons and is reflective of real-world marginalization
(
Puckett et al., 2021
)
.
Existing work has found harmful LLM bias against disclosure reflected in their pretrained outputs
(
Ovalle et al., 2023
)
, yet whether this behavior persists or amplifies after LLM alignment remains unclear.

tango
consists of prompts representing various self-identified gender identities and forms of gender disclosure, structured as "[NAME] [DISCLOSURE FORM] [GENDER IDENTITY]" (e.g., "Alex is genderfluid").
In line with
Ovalle et al. (2023)
, our evaluations cover two distinct linguistic forms of gender identity disclosure : (1)
static forms
, such as "Alex
is
genderfluid" and (2)
fluid forms
, such as "Alex
came out as
genderfluid’.
We conduct our assessment over 90,000 prompts sub-sampled from the
tango
dataset comprising 100 names, 50 self-identified gender identities (including both binary gender and gender-diverse identities), and all 18 disclosure forms (further detailed in Appendix §
A.2
).
We provide models with the [PROMPT] + ’ and’ then allow them to generate up to 200 tokens.
We set temperature=1, top-p=0.95 for nucleus sampling, repetition-penalty=1.03, and generate 5 samples per prompt.
The prompt completions are classified as positive, negative, or neutral regard
(
Sheng et al., 2019
)
, as done in
(
Dhingra et al., 2023
)
and
(
Ovalle et al., 2023
)
, with the expectation that negative regard remains consistent across binary and gender-diverse contexts.
To avoid analyzing outputs that merely replicate the prompt, we filter out responses with a high Jaccard similarity (
\geq 0.4
), indicating substantial lexical overlap between a prompt and its resulting generation.

We chose this threshold to strike a balance between eliminating repetitive outputs and preserving valid subject references, resulting in the removal of 3% of generated examples.

Disproportionate amplification of TGNB negative regard after alignment.

Table 2
presents regard disparities in base models, where positive values indicate greater negative regard toward TGNB versus binary gender identities.
Baseline disparities varied between model families and sizes, with Pythia 2.8B showing the highest bias (14.73,

DPO’s ability to reduce these disparities seemed to critically depend on reference model choice: when using respective base models as reference, disparities decreased significantly (3/4 models,

Yet when SFT’d models produced biased outputs beyond observed base disparities, using them as a reference model for DPO (SFT+DPO) led to further TGNB bias amplification.

.
Furthermore, LLMs systematically reflected higher negative regard for fluid versus static gender disclosure forms throughout all alignment stages (

(

4.2.2.
Shift Towards Hardship in TGNB-Related Generations.

Within aligned LLMs reflecting amplified negative regard for the TGNB group, we observed a concerning trend in textual outputs in both DPO and SFT+DPO stages: models that initially produced neutral or positive portrayals of TGNB persons in their base versions frequently shifted towards tragic narratives in their outputs.

Figure 3
presents a striking example of this bias amplification, where the probability of generating negative regard for TGNB individuals in Llama 13B jumped from 5% at baseline to over 90% following DPO.
To identify distinct forms of narrative shift in generations, we conducted an inductive thematic analysis
(
Clarke and Braun, 2017
)
, allowing categories to emerge from the generations rather than imposing predetermined classifications.
We systematically measured the bias amplification shift across all model families by sampling 100 random generations initially classified as neutral regard at baseline though reflected at least a 75 percentage point increase in negative regard after undergoing any stage of DPO, after controlling for respective textual prompts .
For all models, the three most prominent narrative shifts in generations referenced social rejection (min: 3.8%, max: 31.7%), hardship (min: 11.1%, max: 26.9%), and sexual contexts (min: 3.8%, max: 27.3%) (further details can be found in the Appendix
subsection A.3
).
Interestingly,these patterns persisted despite DPO’s KL penalty
\beta=0.1
, which limits divergence between the optimized policy and its reference model.
To further contextualize these findings within the alignment pipeline, we briefly examine the preference datasets in the next section before empirically investigating implicit reward signal patterns and their correlations to reference model behavior in Section 5.

4.3.
Preference Data Sources

To further contextualize our findings within the DPO alignment pipeline, we examine the preference datasets employed by the ContextualAI suite.
We search across the three preference datasets for TGNB-related terms (“transgender”, “non binary”, “gender queer”, “transman”, “transwoman”) and identify 1,043 examples in
shp
dataset, 250 examples in
hh-rlhf
, and only 12 examples in
oasst
.
Qualitative analysis reveals these datasets encompass a wide range of TGNB-related topics.

shp
, curated from Reddit forums, contains discussions of contentious topics in the subreddit “ChangeMyView”, such as debates around the participation of transgender athletes in competitive sports.
Similarly, the
hh-rlhf
dataset includes instances that may reinforce stigmatizing narratives.
In some instances, harmful content such as insults, sexual material, and social disgust are included to demonstrate model refusal.
The success of this approach, however, depends on the model being finetuned on both chosen and rejected examples to correctly distinguish appropriate refusal responses.
When the same preference dataset is employed for SFT, finetuning relies solely on these chosen examples, leaving the model without access to rejected examples for comparison.
As a result, models may learn to reproduce rather than reduce harmful biases present in selected responses.
We posit that the combination of SFT and DPO can then amplify these biases, leading to outputs that reinforce harmful narratives with increased certainty
(
Ziegler et al., 2019
;
Jia et al., 2020
)
.
Although disentangling the effects of preference data from existing biases is beyond the scope of this paper, our descriptive analysis of the data highlights this as an valuable area for future research.
We detail our text extraction procedure and full examples from the preference datasets in the Appendix (§
A.4
).

5.
Measuring Gender-Diverse Bias in Reward Signal

5.1.
Approach

Having identified TGNB bias amplification patterns in the previous section, we analyze the reward signals that govern alignment to understand how TGNB biases may propagate downstream.
Despite a growing body of research on preference-finetuned LLMs, the availability of high-quality preference data to study specific forms of social bias remains limited.
This scarcity is due to both preference data collection modalities and task underspecification
(
Lambert et al., 2024
)
, where a crowd-sourced approach (i.e., collecting preferences over forums like Reddit) or vague task definition (e.g., lack of clear definitions for “harmlessness”) can result in an inability to capture the necessary data for evaluating the specific social harms we focus on.
Simulating preferences helps us overcome these limitations, providing an opportunity to study specific reward signal disparities and assess whether such signals correspond to the biased behavior observed in the model’s outputs.

We detail our reward extraction procedure in
Figure 4
.
Provided of paired bias evaluation instance, we extract its template
x
, which is paired with two possible completions,
y_{1}
(socially favorable) and
y_{2}
(less favorable), reflecting the ranking

.
This results in a new mock preference dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
.
To assess whether the aligned model
\pi_{\theta}
exhibits a stronger preference for harmful outputs towards a particular group than its reference model
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, we compute the log-ratios
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
across all instances of
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
, where the
\max

We utilize the
WinoQueer
benchmark
(
Felkner et al., 2023
)
, a community-sourced dataset containing 45,540 instances of stigmatizing and offensive texts designed to capture real-world biases against LGBTQ+ individuals.
This approach allows us to systematically explore how models handle potentially harmful stereotypes and whether the model exhibits a preference towards reinforcing existing TGNB stigmas.

We note this setup is not intended to validate or condone the stigmatizing texts, but rather to serve as a diagnostic tool to reveal potential biases in reward signals.

We focus on a specific subset of the dataset that pairs TGNB identities with binary identity counterfactuals, resulting in 15,600 paired instances (see Appendix §
A.5
for details).
In each paired comparison, the model selects its preferred text by assigning it the highest reward.
We report the selection rate for TGNB-directed stigmatizing texts and compare this to a 50% random binary preference baseline.
To further investigate the relationship between model behavior and initial reference states, we compute the point-biserial correlations between the reference model’s log ratios of chosen versus rejected texts,
\log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{c}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{r}\mid x)}
, and the final policy’s selection.
This gives insight into how much TGNB bias is transferred from the reference model to the final aligned policy model.
A lack of correlation suggests no explicit bias transfer from the reference model. Lastly, we complement this with a thematic analysis of texts where the model assigned higher rewards to TGNB-related stigma.

5.3.2.
Correlation to Reference Model Bias.

The correlations between the reference model’s log probability ratios for TGNB-stigma and the final aligned model’s preference are presented in
Table 4
.
A positive correlation indicates that when the reference model exhibits a stronger preference or bias towards TGNB-stigma, the aligned policy is more likely to select stigmatizing texts for TGNB groups. Conversely, negative correlations suggest active bias correction.
Llama models showed a non-trivial preservation of reference model TGNB biases under DPO alone (Llama 7B: 0.19, Llama 13B: 0.21,
\rho<0.05
respectively).
When DPO is initialized from SFT models, these correlations weaken but remain significant (Llama 7B: 0.12, Llama 13B: 0.08,
\rho<0.05
), indicating attenuated bias transfer.
This preservation of pretrained biases despite alignment finetuning aligns with the superficial alignment hypothesis
(
Zhou et al., 2023
;
Lin et al., 2023
)
: while models learn to adjust their outputs, underlying biased behaviors from pretraining persist.
However, weaker correlations to reference model biases did not consistently indicate reduced bias: examining TGNB stigma selection rates showed that while Llama 7B’s bias decreased with SFT initialization (DPO: 83.45%, SFT+DPO: 77.65%), whereas Llama 13B’s substantially increased (DPO: 74.40%, SFT+DPO: 91.53%).
This discrepancy suggests that bias amplification can occur through mechanisms beyond the preservation of reference model biases alone.
In contrast, aligned Pythia models either countered TGNB bias (Pythia 2.8B SFT+DPO: -0.37,
\rho<0.05
) or reflected no bias transfer from its base pretrained model (Pythia 2.8B DPO: -0.01).
Notably, this model also exhibited the lowest rates of TGNB stigma selection (DPO: 57.70%, SFT+DPO: 50.74%).
Together, these findings highlight how bias retention or amplification can vary significantly between foundation models, even when following the same alignment procedures.
Future work may examine how bias retention interacts with other alignment procedures, and what this means for LLMs that demonstrate brittle or oversimplified conceptualizations of gender.

6.
Discussion & Recommendations

In this section, we offer recommendations to address bias amplification in aligned LLMs by tackling the inequalities present in both model outcomes and broader alignment procedures.

6.1.
Addressing Gaps in Bias Evaluation Frameworks

Bias evaluation frameworks have historically focused on dominant social groups, such as binary gender categories, as observed in §
2
.
Failing to prioritize bias evaluations across underrepresented groups not only leaves harms unchecked for these communities
(
Dev et al., 2021
;
Ovalle et al., 2023
)
but also systemically reinforces existing hegemonies by upholding such benchmarks as the sole normative standards for model evaluation
(
Bommasani, 2023
)
.
We caution against uncritically expanding existing bias evaluation frameworks to other groups as such evaluations are inherently intended to study oppressive power dynamics reflective of specific social hierarchies
(
Garg et al., 2018
;
Blodgett et al., 2021
)
.
As demonstrated in our findings (§
4.1
), only by employing situated bias evaluations were we able to identify harms that popular bias benchmarks fail to detect.
Prioritizing the participatory construction of situated, socially grounded bias evaluations is one way to comprehensively assess for harms LLMs can propagate
(
Bender et al., 2021
;
Queerinai et al., 2023
)
.

6.2.
Standardize Assessment of Reward Signal

In this work, we found TGNB biases can appear in both aligned model outputs and their reward signals (§
4.1
, §
5
).
While implicit reward signals fundamentally shape aligned model behavior, we lack standardized methods to assess their role in downstream bias encoding more broadly.
Our proposed framework enables direct measurement of reward-level bias in DPO.
Recent works like
RewardBench

(
Lambert et al., 2024
)
and
M-RewardBench

(
Gureja et al., 2024
)
also provide structured evaluations to better understand limitations in reward signals across various alignment procedures and tasks.
This assessment is particularly valuable for DPO since, unlike RLHF which requires a separate reward model
(
Xu et al., 2024
)
, we can directly measure biases in the preference signals encoded by the policy model.
Early detection of biases in policy model outputs could enable targeted interventions before additional finetuning possibly reinforces these biases.

6.3.
Center Transparency in Alignment Practices

To investigate the ways in which harmful biases are introduced, perpetuated, or amplified in LLM alignment, we advocate for open access to four key components: the initial reference models, preference data, reward model, and alignment finetuning regime.
Importantly, having both the pipeline artifacts and supporting documentation available helps inform how abstract social concepts and considerations are statistically encoded in these systems.
In this work, for instance, we identify opportunities to iterate on the ways model refusals are demonstrated and documented in preference datasets (§
4.3
).
The absence of documentation detailing their refusal forms, social context, how they best should be demonstrated, and at what stages, can drive uncritical dataset adoption in ways that may inadvertently embed potentially harmful model behaviors.
Therefore, we recommend comprehensive documentation and transparency standards aligned with
Gebru et al. (2021)
and
Mitchell et al. (2019)
.
These standards should encompass the release of model and data artifacts, disclosure of annotator positionalities, the rationale behind data and model curation, and documented consideration for the social impacts of the statistical assumptions encoded within each stage of the alignment pipeline.

7.
Conclusion

As AI-driven chatbots and virtual assistants become increasingly prevalent, it’s crucial they serve everyone without reinforcing real-world social harms.
In this work, we discover ways in which aligning LLMs with human preferences via DPO can propagate gender-diverse biases despite alignment towards harmlessness – harms which popular bias evaluation benchmarks fail to detect.
In line with previous work, we find that DPO outcomes are crucially influenced by the SFT stage
(
Zhou et al., 2023
;
Feng et al., 2024
)
.
Our proposed framework for analyzing implicit reward signals suggests aligned LLMs can systematically preserve harmful gender-diverse biases from their reference models.
Our findings collectively highlight the need for both technical and systemic mitigation strategies, advocating for inclusive modeling practices and community-informed evaluation frameworks to more effectively address harmful biases in LLMs.

8.
Limitations and Broader Impacts

Our findings strongly suggest that both alignment procedures and the preference data interact with pre-existing model biases, though future work should explore to what extent new biases are introduced through these procedures and extendable to other regimes like RLHF.
While our analyses reveal concerning patterns via the regard metric, this measurement may not capture all forms of TGNB bias.
As our findings are specific to certain models and datasets, continuing to explore how different preference datasets interact with pre-existing model biases and model architectures is valuable for future work.
Likewise, other dimensions of study can incorporate social impacts of LLM hallucination and model refusal.
Future work can also investigate the efficacy of explicit fairness constraints at different alignment stages and in various forms, including re-weighting preference data and post-processing strategies to control harmful outputs.

A.5.2.
Thematic Analysis Procedure

We conduct a thematic analysis
(
Clarke and Braun, 2017
)
of Winoqueer data, extracting template phrases and categorizing them into distinct themes.
This allows us to identify various forms of stigmatizing language, highlighting possibly areas of targeted TGNB bias intervention.
Below, we present each category alongside extracted template examples.

Identity Denial and Invalidity

This category captures language that invalidates an individual’s identity, particularly in the context of gender identity and expression.
Phrases often deny the legitimacy of non-cisnormative identities, reducing them to phases, confusion, or fabrications.

Examples:

"must be a phase," "biologically mentally male," "faking their identity," "can’t be religious," "aren’t old enough to know."

Mental/Emotional Instability and Inadequacy

This theme focuses on harmful stereotypes associating TGNB identities with mental illness or emotional instability. It includes language that characterizes TGNB individuals as mentally unstable, overly sensitive, or incapable of leading fulfilling lives due to their identity.

Examples:

"confused," "overly sensitive," "mentally sick," "snowflake," "associated with mental illness."

Deviance from Social Norms and Professional Inadequacy

This category includes phrases that portray TGNB individuals as failing to conform to societal or professional expectations, particularly with regard to gender presentation or perceived seriousness in academic or professional settings.

Examples:

"not fit for STEM degree," "too silly to take things seriously," "doesn’t present masculine or feminine," "won’t commit to a serious relationship."

Disgust/Unpleasantness

This category captures expressions of disgust, dehumanization, or the portrayal of TGNB individuals as repulsive or undesirable. Such language often seeks to alienate and degrade individuals based on their identity.

Examples:

"gross," "unnatural," "ugly," "going to hell," "weird."

Threat/Menace to Society

Language in this theme frames TGNB persons as a threat to societal norms, often invoking moral panic or depicting individuals as a danger to children or society at large.
This includes moralizing language that seeks to punish or ostracize TGNB individuals.

Examples:

"danger to children," "groomers," "sinful," "out to push an agenda on society/children," "going to hell."

Sexualization

This theme includes language that hypersexualizes TGNB identities, portraying individuals as overly sexual or promiscuous.
Such harmful stereotypes reduce individuals to sexual deviants, further stigmatizing their identity.

Examples:

"sex craved," "nymphomaniac," "prostitute," "overly sexual," "slutty."

Predatory Behavior

This category includes language that frames individuals as predators or sexual threats. These harmful stereotypes include characterizing individuals as predatory or dangerous to children, reinforcing harmful misconceptions.

Examples:

"predatory," "child molester," "groomer," "unable to resist looking at people’s breasts."
