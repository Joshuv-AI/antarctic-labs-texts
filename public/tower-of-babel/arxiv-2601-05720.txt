Energy Bounds from Relative Magnetic Helicity in Spherical Shells Journal: ApJ
Anthony R. Yeates, Gunnar Hornig

Abstract
Relative magnetic helicity is commonly used in solar physics to avoid the well known gauge ambiguity of standard magnetic helicity in magnetically open domains. But its physical interpretation is difficult owing to the invocation of a reference field. For the specific case of spherical shell domains (with potential reference field), relative helicity may be written intrinsically in terms of the magnetic field alone, without the need to calculate the reference field or its vector potential. We use this intrinsic expression to prove that non-zero relative helicity implies lower bounds for both magnetic energy and free magnetic energy, generalizing the important Arnol’d inequality known for closed-field magnetic helicity. Further, we derive a stronger energy bound by spatially decomposing the relative helicity over a magnetic partition of the domain to obtain a new ideal invariant which we call unsigned helicity. The bounds are illustrated with analytical linear force-free fields (that maximize relative helicity for given boundary conditions) as well as a non-potential data-driven model of the solar corona. These bounds confirm that both relative helicity and the unsigned helicity can influence the dynamics in the solar corona.



I
Introduction

In solar physics, it is common to avoid gauge ambiguities in the classical magnetic helicity,

H=\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV,

(1)

by measuring instead the relative (magnetic) helicity of
4
, which we denote
H_{\rm R}
. This is most commonly computed from the
23
formula,

H_{\rm R}=\int_{V}(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p})\cdot(\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p})\,dV,

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{A}
is the magnetic field of interest, and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}
is a reference field matching
\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}|_{\partial V}=\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}|_{\partial V}
on the boundary
\partial V
. Using (
2
), the relative helicity is easily shown to be invariant under gauge transformations of either
\boldsymbol{A}
or
\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}
. It is conserved in ideal magnetohydrodynamics in line-tied situations where
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\partial V
, and – crucially – remains approximately conserved even under magnetic reconnection
(
6
;
12
)
. We use the notation
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}
because the reference field is almost universally taken to be the unique potential (current-free) field satisfying
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}=\boldsymbol{0}
in
V
as well as the boundary matching condition. Among all magnetic fields satisfying these boundary conditions,
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}
is well known to have minimum energy, or in other words to minimize the
L^{2}
-norm
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}\equiv\int_{V}|\boldsymbol{B}|^{2}\,dV
. By definition,
H_{\rm R}
vanishes if
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}
. The relative helicity
H_{\rm R}
has been widely used in solar physics to analyze magnetic activity
(
19
;
41
;
40
, e.g.,)
.

Oddly, much less attention has been paid in the literature to what
H_{\rm R}
is actually measuring, and why it can reasonably be expected to give us useful information about the dynamics. In particular, the fact that
H_{\rm R}
is conserved (or approximately conserved) in a line-tied evolution is not in itself a particularly compelling reason to study it, since any choice of classical helicity
H
is also conserved in such an evolution, provided
\boldsymbol{n}\times\boldsymbol{A}|_{\partial V}
is held fixed over time. Indeed, as long as
\boldsymbol{B}
contains at least one open magnetic field line (intersecting
\partial V
), one can choose the gauge of
\boldsymbol{A}
to give
H
an arbitrary numerical value, of either sign. This value will remain unchanged in the evolution provided that line-tying holds and that the gauge of
\boldsymbol{n}\times\boldsymbol{A}|_{\partial V}
is fixed. Thus although this
H
is conserved, its numerical value cannot tell us anything about the magnetic field, since it is arbitrary.

By contrast, in the magnetically-closed case, both the sign and magnitude of
H
are meaningful: it is gauge independent and measures the average pairwise linking between infinitesimal flux tubes within the magnetic field
(
31
;
10
;
30
, e.g.,)
. Moreover, for this case,
H
is well known to obey an inequality of the form

|H|\leq C\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2},

(3)

showing that a non-zero helicity puts a lower bound on the magnetic energy
(
2
;
16
;
11
)
. Our main aim in this paper is to show that an analogous inequality holds for
H_{\rm R}
, at least when
V
is a spherical shell (the region between two concentric spheres) and the reference field is potential.

In realistic magnetic fields, the bound
|H|/C
from (
23
) can be a significant underestimate for
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
. For example, it is certainly possible to have
H=0
even though the magnetic field has non-trivial topology
(
14
;
36
, e.g.,)
. Stronger energy bounds have been derived in terms of so-called crossing numbers, as reviewed by
11
. These apply to both magnetically-closed knotted fields
(
24
)
and “braided” fields between two boundaries
(
9
;
1
;
47
)
. However, these crossing numbers are not conserved in an ideal evolution. In this paper, we focus on obtaining energy inequalities where the left-hand side is also a conserved quantity. This is because our overall aim is not to generate the tightest possible bound on energy for a known magnetic field (indeed, if
\boldsymbol{B}
is known then one can compute the energy directly), but to (i) put robust bounds on how energy could evolve, and (ii) show that
H_{\rm R}
(and related conserved quantities) can constrain the dynamics. With the crossing number approach, it is a challenging problem to determine the minimum possible energy over all ideally accessible configurations
(
38
, but see, e.g.)
. Instead, we will show (Section
IV
) how to strengthen the inequality while retaining ideal invariance of the left-hand side, by partitioning
V
into magnetic subdomains.

The reason for restricting to a spherical shell is that we can take advantage of poloidal-toroidal decomposition of
\boldsymbol{B}

(
3
;
7
)
. As will be shown in Section
II
, the known Green’s function expressions for the poloidal and toroidal potentials give us an explicit expression for
H_{\rm R}
in terms of
\boldsymbol{B}
alone, with no reference to either
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}
or to any vector potentials
\boldsymbol{A}
or
\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}
. Analogous expressions are available in the planar slab (region between two parallel planes), but in other domains a generalized decomposition is required
(
5
;
54
)
; it is quite possible that
H_{\rm R}
may still give a lower bound for
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
, but our proofs do not apply directly.

In spherical shells, the simplification of
H_{\rm R}
has previously been exploited to relate energy to helicity for the specific case of force-free fields
(
8
, up to a boundary term;)
, to interpret
H_{\rm R}
as a sum of pairwise linking between elementary (open-ended) flux tubes
(
18
)
, and to relate helicity to pairwise winding numbers between field lines
(
45
)
. The latter two works provide physical interpretations of
H_{\rm R}
without recourse either to the reference field
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}
, or to fields external to
V

(
4
;
39
, unlike)
. This paper will provide further “intrinsic” evidence for the dynamical significance of
H_{\rm R}
in spherical shells.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
reviews the fundamental expression for
H_{\rm R}
on a spherical shell, on which the rest of the paper is based. The main inequality giving
|H_{\rm R}|\leq C\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
is presented in Section
III
, where we also obtain an analogous bound replacing
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
by free magnetic energy
\|\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\|^{2}
. Section
IV
then derives the improved, partition-based bound for
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
where
H_{\rm R}
is replaced by so-called “unsigned helicity”. The utility of this improved bound is illustrated in Section
V
using a numerical model of the solar corona, with conclusions in Section
VI
.

II
Spherical Shells

In this paper,
V
denotes a spherical shell with radius
r\in[r_{0},r_{1}]
. The spherical surfaces of constant
r
are labelled
S_{r}
, with
S_{0}\equiv S_{r_{0}}
denoting the inner boundary and
S_{1}\equiv S_{r_{1}}
the outer boundary. We assume no net magnetic flux or current through
S_{r}
for any
r\in[r_{0},r_{1}]
, meaning

\oint_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dS=\oint_{S_{r}}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B}\,dS=0.

(4)

II.1
Poloidal-Toroidal Vector Potential

In our spherical shell with (
4
), we can write
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
where

\boldsymbol{A}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times(P\boldsymbol{e}_{r})+T\boldsymbol{e}_{r},

(5)

which is the well-known poloidal-toroidal decomposition of
\boldsymbol{B}

(
3
)
, discussed in the helicity context by
7
and
5
.

To see that
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
exists, note that taking the curl of (
5
) once or twice gives

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{h}P

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{B},

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{h}T

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B},

(7)

on each
S_{r}
, in terms of the surface Laplacian

\nabla^{2}_{h}\equiv\frac{1}{r^{2}\sin\theta}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\phi^{2}}\right].

(8)

These two Poisson equations are solvable for
P
and
T
thanks to our assumptions (
4
). Note that the solutions are unique only up to additive functions of
r
. Neither of these ambiguities affect
H_{\rm R}
, however changing
T
does change
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
. To derive our results in Section
II.3
and beyond, we will need to fix
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
by choosing the particular
T
with

\oint_{S_{r}}T\,dS=0\quad\textrm{for each }r\in[r_{0},r_{1}].

(9)

II.2
Simple Expression for Relative Helicity

It is known that the expression (
2
) for
H_{\rm R}
simplifies considerably in our spherical shell geometry
(
8
;
21
;
20
)
. To see this, note that applying the equivalent decomposition (
5
) to the reference vector potential gives
\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times(P_{\rm p}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\big)
, because
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Then integrating by parts gives

\displaystyle\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\,dV

\displaystyle=\int_{V}\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\big(P_{\rm p}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\big)\,dV=0.

(10)

From (
2
), this leaves

\displaystyle H_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV+\int_{V}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p})\,dV

(11)

\displaystyle=\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV+\oint_{\partial V}\boldsymbol{n}\cdot(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\times\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{*})\,dS.

(12)

But since
P
and
P_{\rm p}
both solve the same Poisson equation (
6
), they can differ only by an additive constant on each of
S_{0}
,
S_{1}
, so that the boundary integral vanishes. Therefore

H_{\rm R}=\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV.

(13)

In other words, for the spherical shell,
H_{\rm R}
is equivalent to
H
in the poloidal-toroidal gauge. Note that this expression holds for any choice of
T
. It is the starting point for the energy bounds derived in this paper, for which we will use the particular choice (
9
) for
T
.

II.3
Intrinsic Expression for
\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{*}

Geometry for Sections
II.3
and
II.4
– in particular, the intrinsic expression (

) for
H_{R}
. Note that
\boldsymbol{x}=r\boldsymbol{e}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=r\boldsymbol{e}_{r}^{\prime}
.

To derive our energy bounds, we will express
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
in terms of
\boldsymbol{B}
. This can be done using Green’s functions to write the solutions to (
6
) and (
7
) as

\displaystyle P(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=-\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{e}_{r}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{B})(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,dS^{\prime},

(14)

\displaystyle T(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=-\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{e}_{r}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{B})(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,dS^{\prime}.

(15)

Here
\boldsymbol{x}=r\boldsymbol{e}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=r\boldsymbol{e}_{r}^{\prime}
are points on the sphere
S_{r}
and the spherical Green’s function is

G(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\log(1-\cos\xi),

(16)

with
\xi
the angle between
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
(see Figure
1
). The particular solutions (
14
) and (
15
) both have zero mean on every
S_{r}

(
3
, e.g.)
, so in particular (
15
) satisfies (
9
). Substituting into (
5
) gives

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\times\left(\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}B_{r}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}G\,dS^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{r}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}^{\prime}G\,dS^{\prime}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_{r}.

(17)

As observed by
45
, we have

\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}G=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}}{4\pi r^{3}(1-\cos\xi)},

(18)

and
\boldsymbol{e}_{r}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}^{\prime}G=-\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}G
.

II.4
Intrinsic Expression for
H_{\rm R}

As an aside, we can use (
17
) to derive a new expression for
H_{\rm R}
. Following
45
, we define the unit vector

\boldsymbol{e}_{t}=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}}{|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}|}=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}}{r^{2}\sin\xi},

(19)

which is orthogonal to the great circle connecting
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
(Figure
1
). Substituting (
17
) into (
13
) and defining
\Gamma(\xi)=\sin\xi/(1-\cos\xi)
leads to the alternative expression

\displaystyle H_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{0}}^{r_{1}}\int_{S_{r}\times S_{r}^{\prime}}\frac{\Gamma(\xi)}{4\pi r}\Big[B_{r}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})B_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\qquad-B_{r}(\boldsymbol{x})B_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\Big]\,dS^{\prime}\,dS\,dr,

(20)

where
B_{t}\equiv\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
. Note that
\Gamma(\xi)
has a singularity when
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{x}
, since
\sin\xi/(1-\cos\xi)\sim 2/\xi
as
\xi\to 0
. However, choosing coordinates for
S^{\prime}
where
\boldsymbol{x}
is the north pole makes
\xi
into colatitude, so the surface element
dS^{\prime}
contributes another
\sin\xi
, showing that the integrand remains finite. Expression (
20
) is “intrinsic” in the sense that it refers to neither any vector potential nor any reference field.

From Figure
1
, we see that
B_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the component of
\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x})
“rotating around”
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
, and
vice versa
for
B_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
. So (
20
) has the clear interpretation of measuring the average winding of
\boldsymbol{B}
between all pairs of points, with respect to the radial direction.
45
show that the weighting
\Gamma(\xi)
accounts naturally for the spherical geometry; this factor vanishes when
\xi=\pi
, meaning
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
are antipodal. By recasting (
20
) as an integral over field lines rather than height, they also show how to express
H_{\rm R}
in a spherical shell as a flux-weighted average of pairwise winding numbers between magnetic field lines. This is analogous to the topological interpretation of
H
in terms of linking numbers.

We could also view the expression (
20
) for
H_{\rm R}
as a two-point correlation function for
\boldsymbol{B}
, using off-diagonal components of the tensor
B_{i}B_{j}
. This is reminiscent of the well-known interpretation for
H
in periodic domains (using Coulomb gauge,
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{A}^{\rm c}=0
), used to evaluate helicity in studies of solar wind turbulence
(
29
;
33
)
.

III
Global Energy Bounds

In Section
III.1
we generalize the
2
inequality (
23
) to
H_{\rm R}
, for a spherical shell. Before doing so, recall the typical proof for (
23
) in a magnetically closed domain. This starts from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality

\left|\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV\right|\leq\int_{V}|\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}|\,dV\leq\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\|\boldsymbol{B}\|,

(21)

where
\|\cdot\|^{2}=\int_{V}|\cdot|^{2}\,dV
denotes the
L^{2}
-norm of a vector field on
V
. Inequality (
21
) holds in any gauge, but since the helicity is gauge independent in such a domain, we are free to choose a gauge where
\boldsymbol{A}
obeys a Poincaré inequality

\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\leq C\|\boldsymbol{B}\|

(22)

for some fixed constant
C
. For example, in a magnetically closed domain such an inequality is satisfied by the Coulomb gauge
\boldsymbol{A}^{\rm c}
with
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}^{\rm c}=0
in
V
and
\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}^{\rm c}=0
on
\partial V

(
55
, e.g.)
. Then we obtain the desired energy bound

\left|\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV\right|\leq C\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}.

(23)

Next consider our magnetically-open spherical shell.

III.1
Lower Bound for Energy

For
H_{\rm R}
, we are no longer free to choose the vector potential in (
1
), but must verify (
22
) for our particular gauge
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
from (
5
) and (
9
). We find that this can be done using the integral expression (
17
) for
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
in terms of
\boldsymbol{B}
alone, by adapting the argument of
16
. As detailed in Appendix
A
, this leads to

\|\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\|\leq\frac{\pi r_{1}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|,

(24)

and hence (
21
) gives

|H_{\rm R}|\leq\frac{\pi r_{1}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2},

(25)

or equivalently the energy bound

\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}\geq W_{1}\equiv\frac{2|H_{\rm R}|}{\pi r_{1}}

(26)

for our spherical shell.

B_{r}
from (

and

The bound (
26
) is not tight, in the sense that equality may be impossible to attain for any
\boldsymbol{B}
, at least for certain boundary conditions. To see this, note first that a magnetic field that maximizes the ratio
W_{1}/\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
– in other words, maximizes the tightness of the bound – must be a linear force-free field satisfying

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B}=\alpha\boldsymbol{B},\quad\alpha=\textrm{constant}.

(27)

This follows from Woltjer’s Theorem
(
44
)
, as extended by
6
to the case of
H_{\rm R}
with fixed
B_{r}|_{\partial V}
(we give an alternative proof in Appendix
B
using
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
).

Then consider a simple example where we fix
B_{r}
on both boundaries to match the dipolar “source surface” potential field,

\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}=B_{0}\left[\frac{r_{0}^{3}(r^{3}+2r_{1}^{3})}{r^{3}(r_{0}^{3}+2r_{1}^{3})}\cos\theta\boldsymbol{e}_{r}-\frac{r_{0}^{3}(r^{3}-r_{1}^{3})}{r^{3}(r_{0}^{3}+2r_{1}^{3})}\sin\theta\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}\right].

(28)

(This is the unique curl-free field with
B_{r}=B_{0}\cos\theta
on
S_{0}
that is purely radial on
S_{1}
.) There is a whole family of linear force-free fields with
B_{r}
matching (
28
) on both boundaries, having different values of
\alpha
(details in Appendix
C
), and different values of the ratio
W_{1}/\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
. This ratio is plotted in Figure
2
(a) as a function of (non-dimensionalized)
\alpha r_{1}
, with each curve corresponding to a different ratio
r_{0}/r_{1}
for the domain. For any
\alpha r_{1}
, the ratio
W_{1}/\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
for these linear force-free fields depends only on the “shape”
r_{0}/r_{1}
of the domain, and not the absolute “size”
r_{1}
. The dots indicate the solution that maximizes
W_{1}/\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
for each domain shape, and the field line topology of each of these solutions is indicated in Figure
2
(b). Here the contours show surfaces of constant
rR(r)\sin^{2}\theta
, where
R(r)
is defined in Appendix
C
. These are flux surfaces (tangent to
\boldsymbol{B}
) in these axisymmetric fields. Notice that the topology of the maximal-helicity solution changes depending on the domain: it is a sheared arcade for
r_{0}=0.8r_{1}
, but contains a disconnected “flux rope” when
r_{0}
is small enough.

Returning to the tightness of the bound (
26
), we see from Figure
2
(a) that it is tightest – meaning largest ratio
W_{1}/\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
– for larger
r_{0}/r_{1}
, but for the thinnest shell illustrated (
r_{0}=0.8r_{1}
), we reach only
W_{1}\approx 0.4\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
for the maximal-helicity solution. Conversely, as
r_{0}\to 0
, the maximal ratio tends to a limit of
W_{1}\approx 0.14\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
. In this limit, the corresponding
\alpha r_{1}
converges to the smallest curl eigenvalue for a unit ball, which is approximately
4.4934

(
17
, Table I)
.
For non-zero
r_{0}
, the ratio that is possible to attain will depend on the boundary conditions for
B_{r}
; more realistic configurations for the Sun will be considered in Section
V
.

III.2
Lower Bound for Free Energy

Since
H_{\rm R}=0
for a potential field, one might suspect that having a non-zero
H_{\rm R}
might imply not only a minimum amount of (total) magnetic energy,
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
, as shown in (
26
), but also some minimum amount of free magnetic energy,
\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|^{2}
, where

\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\|^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\|^{2}.

(29)

Here we prove this for our spherical shell.

To derive a bound for
\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|^{2}
in terms of
H_{\rm R}
, we recall the decomposition

H_{\rm R}=H_{\rm J}+H_{\rm PJ},

(30)

where the two terms

H_{\rm J}=\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm J}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\,dV,\quad H_{\rm PJ}=2\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\,dV

(31)

are individually gauge invariant
(
10
)
. They have been called the “current-carrying” and “volume-threading” helicities, respectively
(
25
)
. Choosing
\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm J}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm J}^{*}
allows us to immediately apply (
26
) to
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}
to see that

|H_{\rm J}|\leq\frac{\pi r_{1}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|^{2}.

(32)

This already gives us the free energy bound

\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|^{2}\geq W_{2}\equiv\frac{2|H_{\rm J}|}{\pi r_{1}},

(33)

but this only implies non-zero free energy in magnetic fields with non-zero
H_{\rm J}
.

To go further, we can bound
H_{\rm PJ}
by

|H_{\rm PJ}|\leq 2\int_{V}|\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}|\,|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}|\,dV\leq 2\|\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}\|\,\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|.

(34)

Choosing
\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{*}
shows from (
24
) that

\|\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{*}\|\leq\frac{\pi r_{1}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\|

(35)

and so combining we obtain the inequality

|H_{\rm R}|\leq\frac{\pi r_{1}}{2}\Big(\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|^{2}+2\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\|\,\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|\Big).

(36)

27
has recently published a tighter inequality of the same form, in the sense that the coefficient of
\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|^{2}
is smaller – this is derived by noting that
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}
is a closed magnetic field so that the estimate (
32
) can be replaced by the classical bound (
23
).

Note the appearance of
\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\|
on the right-hand side of (
36
). We could not expect to bound
|H_{\rm R}|
in terms of
\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|
alone, since in configurations where
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}
are interlinked, it is possible to increase
|H_{\rm R}|
without bound by increasing the strength of
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}
while keeping
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}
fixed. A simple example of this is the linear combination of a dipole and a toroidal field,

\boldsymbol{B}=ar_{0}^{3}\left(\frac{2\cos\theta}{r^{3}}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+\frac{\sin\theta}{r^{3}}\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}\right)+\frac{br}{r_{0}}\sin\theta\boldsymbol{e}_{\phi}.

(37)

Here
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}
is precisely the
\phi
-component, and it is straightforward to show that

\boldsymbol{A}^{*}=\frac{br^{2}}{r_{0}}\cos\theta\boldsymbol{e}_{r}+\frac{ar_{0}^{3}}{r^{2}}\sin\theta\boldsymbol{e}_{\phi}

(38)

so that

H_{\rm R}=\frac{8\pi abr_{0}^{4}}{3}\left(\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{r_{0}^{2}}-1\right).

(39)

We see that indeed
H_{\rm R}
can be increased arbitrarily by increasing the amplitude
a
of the
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}
component, while holding
b
fixed so that
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}
is unchanged.

By elementary calculus, we can solve (
36
) for
\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|
to see that

\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|\geq\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\|\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{2|H_{\rm R}|}{\pi r_{1}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\|^{2}}}-1\right),

(40)

and squaring gives the desired bound

\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}\|^{2}\geq W_{3}

(41)

for

W_{3}\equiv\frac{2|H_{\rm R}|}{\pi r_{1}}+2\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\|^{2}\left(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{2|H_{\rm R}|}{\pi r_{1}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}\|^{2}}}\right).

(42)

Observe that if
\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm p}
vanishes so that
\boldsymbol{B}\equiv\boldsymbol{B}_{\rm J}
, then
H_{\rm R}\equiv H_{\rm J}
and (
41
) reduces to (
33
). Also,
W_{3}
is always non-negative: this can be seen from (
40
).

IV
Local Energy Bounds

The bounds derived in Section
III
have been based on the global
H_{\rm R}
value, integrated over the whole of
V
. Here, we spatially decompose
H_{\rm R}
into a sum over more localized invariants, leading to stronger energy bounds. It would, of course, be possible to bound
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
based on
\int_{V}|\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}|\,dV
directly, bypassing the first inequality in (
21
). However, this integral is not an ideal invariant, and we prefer to bound the energy in terms of a conserved quantity that is likely to be more robust in simulation or measurement. To achieve this, we need to decompose
H_{\rm R}
not into arbitrary volumes
(
42
, cf.)
, but into subdomains separated by magnetic surfaces.

IV.1
Partitioning the Domain

Figure 4:
An example partition
\Pi
of the spherical shell
V
into magnetic subvolumes
V_{1},\ldots,V_{12}
, in this case for an axisymmetric magnetic field. Each subvolume
V_{i}
has an associated sub-helicity,
h_{i}
, with
\sum_{i=1}^{12}h_{i}=H_{\rm R}
.

The expression (
13
)
suggests – unlike the Finn-Antonsen formula (
2
) – how to spatially decompose
H_{\rm R}
into a sum of more localized “sub-helicities”,

H_{\rm R}=\sum_{V_{i}\in\Pi}h_{i},\textrm{ where }h_{i}=\int_{V_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV,

(43)

where
\Pi=\{V_{i}\}
is a disjoint partition of
V
, meaning
V=\bigcup_{i}V_{i}
and
V_{i}\cap V_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
. For an arbitrary such partition, each
h_{i}
may not be an ideal invariant. But if the
V_{i}
are magnetic subvolumes – meaning that
\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{i}=0
on the part of
\partial V_{i}
that is internal to the overall
V
– then the Reynolds Transport Theorem
(
30
, e.g.)
, with the uncurled ideal induction equation

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{A}^{*}}{\partial t}=\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi^{*},

(44)

gives

\frac{dh_{i}}{dt}=\oint_{\partial V_{i}\cap\partial V}\big(\phi^{*}+\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\big)\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\,\boldsymbol{dS}.

(45)

With the global line-tied boundary condition
\boldsymbol{v}|_{\partial V}=\boldsymbol{0}
, the second term in the parentheses vanishes, and it follows also that
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}\phi^{*}|_{\partial V}=0
. This means that we can take
\phi^{*}
outside the integral on each boundary surface
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
, so by (
4
) we have

\frac{dh_{i}}{dt}=0.

(46)

In other words, each sub-helicity is invariant under ideal motions that vanish on the global boundaries. Thus we have decomposed
H_{\rm R}
into more localized invariants, albeit that the computation of
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
is defined globally. An example of such a partition is shown in Figure
4
.

IV.2
Bound from a Magnetic Partition

We can exploit the partition (
43
) to give a stronger lower bound on the magnetic energy. To do this, define the unsigned helicity of the partition as

\overline{H}_{\Pi}=\sum_{V_{i}\in\Pi}|h_{i}|.

(47)

This is, again, an ideal invariant provided the
V_{i}
are magnetic subvolumes. It satisfies

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{\Pi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{V_{i}\in\Pi}\left|\int_{V_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV\right|

(48)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{V_{i}\in\Pi}\int_{V_{i}}|\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}|\,dV=\int_{V}|\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}|\,dV.

(49)

This recovers (
21
), so it follows from Section
III
that
\overline{H}_{\Pi}
for any disjoint partition
\Pi
also obeys

\overline{H}_{\Pi}\leq\frac{\pi r_{1}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}.

(50)

For the Sun’s corona, where there are positive and negative regions of helicity, a fine enough choice of partition will typically give a larger lower bound on the energy than (
26
).

IV.3
Bound From Field Line Helicity

To obtain the tightest possible bound for
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
using (
50
), we seek the finest possible partition
\Pi
. This is given by taking each
V_{i}
to be a thin flux tube around a single magnetic field line
L_{i}
, and taking the limit that these tube radii
r(V_{i})
go to zero. Since
h_{i}
would vanish in this limit, we normalize by the tube’s magnetic flux
\Phi_{i}
to obtain a finite value

\mathcal{A}(L_{i})=\lim_{r(V_{i})\to 0}\frac{1}{\Phi_{i}}\int_{V_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV.

(51)

Called field line helicity
(
50
)
, it was shown by
8
showed that this quantity has the simpler expression

\mathcal{A}(L_{i})=\int_{L_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot d\boldsymbol{l}.

(52)

Again, this is invariant for every field line under a line-tied ideal evolution. For a closed loop field line,
\mathcal{A}(L_{i})
is simply the enclosed magnetic flux. For an open field line, it still corresponds to a flux, although the interpretation is more nuanced
(
48
, e.g.,)
. We recover
H_{\rm R}
by replacing the sum in (
43
) by an integral of
\mathcal{A}(L_{i})d\Phi_{i}
over all field lines. In the (typical) case where all field lines intersect
\partial V
exactly twice, this may be written as

H_{\rm R}=\frac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial V}\mathcal{A}|B_{r}|\,dS,

(53)

where
\mathcal{A}
is integrated along the field line traced from each point on
\partial V
.

The corresponding unsigned helicity of this limiting “field line” partition is similarly given by replacing the sum in (
47
) with an integral, so that

\overline{H}=\frac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial V}|\mathcal{A}B_{r}|\,dS.

(54)

This was called “unsigned helicity” by
51
. From (
50
) this again obeys the inequality

\overline{H}\leq\frac{\pi r_{1}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}.

(55)

Since this is the finest possible (magnetic) partition, it gives us the tightest possible energy bound from the partition approach, namely

\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}\geq W_{4}\equiv\frac{2\overline{H}}{\pi r_{1}}.

(56)

It is clear that (
56
) will reduce to the original bound (
26
) precisely when
\overline{H}=H_{\rm R}
. This occurs when
\mathcal{A}
either vanishes or has uniform sign for all field lines. It is not so common for
\mathcal{A}
to vanish uniformly: as shown by
51
, even (non-axisymmetric) potential fields typically have non-zero
\mathcal{A}
values on individual field lines. But the second case – uniform sign of
\mathcal{A}
– can occur, at least in artificial configurations. An example is the family of linear force-free fields from Section
III
(Appendix
C
). Bound (
56
) therefore gives no new information for these fields. On the other hand, the more typical situation in the solar corona is to have mixed signs of
\mathcal{A}
, in which case
\overline{H}>H_{\rm R}

(
52
, e.g.,)
and bound (
56
) constrains
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
more strongly. This will be illustrated in the following section.

VI
Conclusion

The bounds derived above show that non-zero relative helicity
|H_{\rm R}|
implies non-zero magnetic energy, analogous to classical helicity
|H|
in a magnetically-closed domain. Similarly, we have shown that non-zero
|H_{\rm R}|
implies non-zero
free
magnetic energy. However, just as with
H
, magnetic fields can put non-trivial topological constraints on the energy even when
H_{\rm R}=0
. The unsigned helicity
\overline{H}
in (
54
) seems the most natural generalization, and we have shown that if this is non-zero then it implies non-zero magnetic energy even in fields with
H_{\rm R}=0
.

In the classical magnetically-closed case, the
\boldsymbol{B}
that maximizes helicity for a given magnetic energy depends only on the geometry of the domain
(
2
;
16
)
. In the case of
H_{\rm R}
, the helicity that can be reached
– and nature of the maximizing configuration – depend also on the boundary distribution of
\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}
. We illustrated the maximizing
\boldsymbol{B}
for the simplest case of a dipole boundary condition, but in future it could be computed (with care) for arbitrary distributions, including those from the numerical simulations in Section
V
. This could provide a normalized way to assess how “helical” is a given magnetic configuration in the solar corona, with possible application to predicting solar eruptions.

What about domains other than spherical shells? Firstly, we expect all of our results to extend (with small modification) to the case of an infinite plane slab (the volume between two parallel planes), since the poloidal-toroidal decomposition of
\boldsymbol{B}
also exists there. We omit this here since such a domain is of limited practical relevance in solar physics. More relevant would be a finite domain, such as a Cartesian box. For such a domain,
20
notes that we can again write
H_{\rm R}=\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}^{\rm c}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV
, by choosing
\boldsymbol{A}^{\rm c}
to match
\boldsymbol{n}\times\boldsymbol{A}^{\rm c}=\boldsymbol{n}\times\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{\rm c}
on
\partial V
, where the reference vector potential
\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{\rm c}
satisfies the Coulomb gauge conditions
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{\rm c}=0
throughout
V
and
\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{\rm c}|_{\partial V}=0
. However, except for spherical shells or infinite slabs, this does not yield an explicit expression for
\boldsymbol{A}^{\rm c}
in terms of
\boldsymbol{B}
, so that it is unclear how to derive an energy bound for
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
, although
27
has recently derived a free energy bound similar to Section
III.2
.

In certain other cases – for example, a doubly-periodic domain
(
46
)
, or a finite flux tube
(
37
;
15
)
– there are known
\boldsymbol{A}
with explicit expressions in terms of
\boldsymbol{B}
and associated interpretations of the corresponding
H
in terms of winding numbers. However, in these cases,
H
can differ from
H_{\rm R}

(
37
, an explicit example is given by)
. This raises the question whether
H_{\rm R}
is the most physically relevant helicity definition in general, or whether it coincides with such a definition only in more specialized domains such as spherical shells. Indeed,
26
have proposed how to extend
H_{\rm R}
to multiply-connected domains, but the relation to energy warrants further investigation.

Appendix A
Proof of Poincaré Inequality

To derive (
24
) for
\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm p}^{*}
, we adapt an argument of
16
which was applied originally to the Biot-Savart integral in a magnetically closed domain. We start from the expression (
17
) for
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
, using (
18
) to write it in the form

\boldsymbol{A}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{4\pi r^{3}}\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}\left[B_{r}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}}{1-\cos\xi}+\frac{\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}{1-\cos\xi}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\right]\,dS^{\prime}.

(A1)

Since
\boldsymbol{x}=r\boldsymbol{e}_{r}
, the two terms in the integrand are orthogonal vectors for any
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
, so

|\boldsymbol{A}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}=\frac{1}{(4\pi r^{3})^{2}}\left[\left|\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}B_{r}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}}{1-\cos\xi}\,dS^{\prime}\right|^{2}+\left|\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}\frac{\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}{1-\cos\xi}\,dS^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right].

(A2)

The first term in (
A2
) satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}B_{r}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}}{1-\cos\xi}\,dS^{\prime}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}\left||B_{r}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})|\frac{|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}|^{1/2}}{(1-\cos\xi)^{1/2}}\right|\frac{|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}|^{1/2}}{(1-\cos\xi)^{1/2}}\,dS^{\prime}

(A3)

\displaystyle\leq r^{2}\left(\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}|B_{r}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})|^{2}\Gamma(\xi)\,dS^{\prime}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}\Gamma(\xi)\,dS^{\prime}\right)^{1/2}.

(A4)

using Cauchy-Schwartz and the fact that
|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}|=r^{2}\sin\xi
. As in (
20
), we use the shorthand
\Gamma(\xi)=\sin\xi/(1-\cos\xi)
. Similarly the second term in (
A2
) satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}\frac{\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}{1-\cos\xi}\,dS^{\prime}\right|\leq r^{2}\left(\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{B}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})|^{2}\Gamma(\xi)\,dS^{\prime}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}\Gamma(\xi)\,dS^{\prime}\right)^{1/2}.

(A5)

By symmetry, the integral
\int_{S_{r}}\Gamma(\xi)\,dS^{\prime}
must be independent of
\boldsymbol{x}
, so can be directly evaluated by setting
\boldsymbol{x}
to be the north pole, giving

\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}\Gamma(\xi)\,dS^{\prime}=2\pi^{2}r^{2}.

(A6)

So overall (
A2
) gives

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{A}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{8}\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})|^{2}\Gamma(\xi)\,dS^{\prime}.

(A7)

Integrating over all
\boldsymbol{x}
in
S_{r}
then gives

\int_{S_{r}}|\boldsymbol{A}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}\,dS\leq\frac{1}{8}\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})|^{2}\left(\int_{S_{r}}\Gamma(\xi)\,dS\right)\,dS^{\prime}=\frac{\pi^{2}r^{2}}{4}\int_{S_{r}^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})|^{2}\,dS^{\prime}.

(A8)

Finally, integrating over
r
gives

\|\boldsymbol{A}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}\leq\int_{r_{0}}^{r_{1}}\frac{\pi^{2}r^{2}}{4}\int_{S_{r}}|\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})|^{2}\,dS^{\prime}\,dr\leq\frac{\pi^{2}r_{1}^{2}}{4}\|\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}.

(A9)

Appendix B
Woltjer’s Theorem for Relative Helicity

6
(in Appendix A) showed that the celebrated theorem of
44
applies equally to relative helicity: namely, the minimum energy state (if it exists) for a specified
H_{\rm R}
and
\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial V}
must be a linear force-free field satisfying (
27
).
Here we use (
13
) to give a simple alternative proof for our spherical shell domain.

Start by considering the first variation of
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
. Integration by parts gives

\delta\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}=2\int_{V}\delta\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B})\,dV-2\oint_{\partial V}\boldsymbol{B}\cdot(\delta\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\times\boldsymbol{n})\,dS.

(B1)

The surface term vanishes since
\delta\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
, because
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\times\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial V}
in the poloidal-toroidal gauge (
5
) depends only on
B_{r}|_{\partial V}
, which is fixed by assumption. On the other hand, and applying a similar boundary condition, we find

\delta H_{\rm R}=2\int_{V}\delta\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV.

(B2)

We then follow the original argument of
44
to minimize
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}
with fixed
H_{\rm R}
and fixed
B_{r}
on the boundaries, introducing a Lagrange multiplier
\alpha
to obtain

\int_{V}2\delta\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B}-\alpha\boldsymbol{B})\,dV=0.

(B3)

This holds for all allowable perturbations
\delta\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
if and only if (
27
) is satisfied.

Appendix C
Dipolar Linear Force-Free example

By separation of variables
(
7
;
22

matching (

\boldsymbol{B}=B_{0}\left[\frac{2R\cos\theta}{r}\boldsymbol{e}_{r}-\frac{(rR)^{\prime}\sin\theta}{r}\boldsymbol{e}_{\theta}+\alpha R\sin\theta\boldsymbol{e}_{\phi}\right],

(C1)

where the prime denotes differentiation with respect to

S_{1}

\displaystyle A=-\frac{r_{0}y_{1}(\alpha r_{1})-cr_{1}y_{1}(\alpha r_{0})}{2\lambda},\qquad B=\frac{r_{0}j_{1}(\alpha r_{1})-cr_{1}j_{1}(\alpha r_{0})}{2\lambda},

(C3)

where we have used the shorthand

\lambda\equiv y_{1}(\alpha r_{0})j_{1}(\alpha r_{1})-j_{1}(\alpha r_{0})y_{1}(\alpha r_{1}),\qquad c\equiv\frac{3r_{0}^{3}}{r_{0}^{3}+2r_{1}^{3}}.

(C4)

This solution exists for every value of

except for the discrete set of “resonant” values where

7

\alpha

\alpha

r_{0}

For a linear force-free field, we have
\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B}=\alpha B_{r}

P(r,\theta)=B_{0}rR\cos\theta,\quad T(r,\theta)=\alpha P(r,\theta),

(C6)

By (

(
7

H_{\rm R}=\int_{V}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dV=\frac{16\pi B_{0}^{2}\alpha}{3}\int_{r_{0}}^{r_{1}}(rR)^{2}\,dr.

(C7)

Similarly the energy reduces to the single integral

\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}=\frac{8\pi B_{0}^{2}}{3}\int_{r_{0}}^{r_{1}}\big[(2+\alpha^{2}r^{2})R^{2}+([rR]^{\prime})^{2}\big]\,dr.

To compute

for the reference field (

Finally, note that the field line helicity

\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}=\alpha B_{0}^{2}R^{2}(1+\cos^{2}\theta).

Appendix D
Numerical Method for Field Line Helicity

As with all calculations of magnetic helicity, care is needed to use a numerical method that does not introduce spurious
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}
contributions
(
43
)
. Here we summarize how the calculations of
\mathcal{A}
and hence
\overline{H}
in Section
V
were made
(
53
, code available at)
. As indicated by
52
, the simulations use a finite-volume method and provide
\boldsymbol{B}
components on the corresponding cell faces of a staggered mesh. Since neighboring cells share the face values, this ensures that
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}=0
is preserved in an integrated sense within each three-dimensional grid cell. To ensure that
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{B}
when computing
\boldsymbol{A}
, the components of
\boldsymbol{A}
are staggered on the cell edges, and computed in such a way as to enforce the discrete Stokes Theorem,
\int_{S_{ij}}\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,dS=\oint_{\partial S_{ij}}\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\,d\boldsymbol{l}
on every cell face
S_{ij}
.

D.1
Vector potential

Rather than computing
\mathcal{A}
directly from
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
using (
52
), we instead evaluate

\mathcal{A}(L_{i})=\int_{L_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}\cdot d\boldsymbol{l}

(D1)

where
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
is simpler to compute. To ensure that
\mathcal{A}
is unmodified for all field lines by this gauge transformation, we impose that (i)
\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\times\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\times\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
on both
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
, and (ii)
\oint_{S_{r}}A^{\prime}_{r}\,dS=0
on every
S_{r}
for
r\in[r_{0},r_{1}]
. See this by writing
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{A}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\chi
, so that condition (i) implies that
\chi(r_{0},\theta,\phi)\equiv\chi_{0}
and
\chi(r_{1},\theta,\phi)\equiv\chi_{1}
, both constant. Thus

\int_{L_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}\cdot d\boldsymbol{l}=\mathcal{A}(L_{i})+\chi_{1}-\chi_{0}.

(D2)

Then, noting from (
9
) that
\oint_{S_{r}}A^{*}_{r}\,dS=0
, condition (ii) implies that

\oint_{S_{r}}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial r}\,dS=0\quad\implies\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{1}{r^{2}}\oint_{S_{r}}\chi\,dS\right)=0\quad\implies\chi_{1}=\chi_{0}.

(D3)

D.2
Numerical algorithm

To compute a suitable
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
, we proceed as follows:

1.

Compute
\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}
in the DeVore-Coulomb gauge
(
21
;
32
)
,

\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}(r,\theta,\phi)=\frac{r_{0}}{r}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\big[\psi(\theta,\phi)\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\big]+\frac{1}{r}\int_{r_{0}}^{r}\boldsymbol{B}(r^{\prime},\theta,\phi)\times\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\,r^{\prime}dr^{\prime},\quad\textrm{where }\nabla_{h}^{2}\psi=-B_{r}(r_{0},\theta,\phi),

(D4)

choosing the solution
\psi
with zero mean over
S_{1}
. By definition,
A^{\dagger}_{r}\equiv 0
so
\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}
already satisfies condition (ii). In addition,
\psi
coincides with
P
from Section
II
, so
\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}
also satisfies condition (i) on
S_{0}
, but not (necessarily) on
S_{1}
. Both the Poisson equation and the radial integral are discretized so that the discrete Stokes Theorem is satisfied on each cell face, with
\psi
co-located with
B_{r}
on the radial cell faces.

2.

Change gauge to
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}g
satisfying both conditions (i) and (ii), while preserving
\oint_{\partial S_{ij}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}\cdot d\boldsymbol{l}=\oint_{\partial S_{ij}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\cdot d\boldsymbol{l}
on every cell face. The gauge function
g
is located at cell vertices, and determined as follows:

(a)

Compute the polar values
A^{*}_{\theta}(r_{1},0,\phi)
and
A^{*}_{\theta}(r_{1},\pi,\phi)
on
S_{1}
by solving
\nabla^{2}_{h}P=-B_{r}
. These will be used as a boundary conditions in the next step.

(b)

Compute the gauge function
g(r_{1},\theta,\phi)
on
S_{1}
by solving
\nabla^{2}_{h}g=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}
on
S_{1}
, choosing the solution with zero mean (this additive constant affects only
A^{\prime}_{r}
, not
A^{\prime}_{\theta}
or
A^{\prime}_{\phi}
). This will ensure that
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}=0
on
S_{1}
, and hence that
\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\times\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{e}_{r}\times\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
on
S_{1}
. Since the first and last rows of vertices are at the poles, we avoid numerical problems by solving for
g
with these two rows omitted, setting the values of
g
at the poles with the Neumann condition
\partial g/\partial\theta=A^{*}_{\theta}-A^{\dagger}_{\theta}
.

(c)

Set
g(r_{0},\theta,\phi)\equiv 0
so as to preserve condition (i) on
S_{0}
.

(d)

Fill in
g(r,\theta,\phi)
for intermediate
S_{r}
by linear interpolation between
g(r_{0},\theta,\phi)\equiv 0
and
g(r_{1},\theta,\phi)
. This will preserve condition (ii) since
g(r_{1},\theta,\phi)
has zero mean.

Once
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
has been computed, we trace field lines
L_{i}
using the (second-order) midpoint method, then evaluate (
D1
) by numerical integration of
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
along each field line. To compute
\overline{H}
, we assume that there are no closed field lines and approximate (
54
) by tracing field lines from grids at twice the original mesh resolution on both
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
.
