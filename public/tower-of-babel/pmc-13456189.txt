Demographic Analysis of the Sun Protective Behaviours and Skin Cancer Risk of Victorian Farmers
Authors: Eloise Violet Kwan, Jessie Adams, Joanna Macdonald, Susan Brumby, Tracey Hatherell, Feby Savira, Alison Kennedy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFarmers have an elevated risk of melanoma and non‐melanoma skin cancer due to their occupational exposure to high levels of ultraviolet radiation. The sun protective behaviours of farmers are poorly understood, limiting targeted interventions to this at‐risk population.ObjectivesThis study aimed to characterise sun protective behaviours among Victorian farmers and to assess whether demographic factors were associated with variations in sun protection uptake.MethodA cross‐sectional study of 124 Victorian farmers was completed using Health and Lifestyle Assessment data collected by the National Centre for Farmer Health. Associations between sun protective behaviours (sunscreen, sunglasses, long sleeve shirt, long trousers, broad brim hat) and demographic factors (gender, age group, rurality) were investigated with descriptive statistics and ordinal logistic regression.ResultsAge group was significantly associated with sunscreen use (p = 0.035). Participants aged 65 years and over were most likely to never use sunscreen, while those aged 50–64 years were most likely to always use it. No significant associations were found between gender or rurality and any sun protective behaviour. Overall, use of sun protection was suboptimal across all demographic groups.ConclusionThese findings suggest that Victorian farmers over 65 years of age may benefit from targeted public health messaging and education from primary care physicians and dermatologists. Further research is required to validate these results given the small sample size. A focus group of at‐risk farmers as identified in this study may be valuable to identify interventions that could increase the sun protective behaviours of Australian farmers.Keywords: farmers, melanoma, rural health, skin neoplasm, ultraviolet rays

Introduction

Ultraviolet radiation (UVR) from the sun is linked to skin cancer, photoaging, photo‐immunologic suppression and the exacerbation of photosensitive skin disorders [1, 2]. Even suberythemal UVR exposure causes DNA damage, which can result in malignant transformation of skin cells [2]. Coupled with its immunosuppressive properties, UVR exposure is a major risk factor for carcinogenesis [1]. Australia's UVR levels are among the highest in the world, due to its location near the equator, proximity to the sun in summer, and lower levels of air pollution. Consequently, Australia has the highest incidence rate of melanoma worldwide, and non‐melanoma skin cancers (NMSCs) are the most common cancers diagnosed [3, 4].

Despite poorer skin health, regional Australia has limited access to dermatology specialist care compared to metropolitan areas. According to Victorian Cancer Registry data from 2021 to 2023, melanoma diagnoses are over 50% higher in regional Victoria compared to major cities [5], yet only 6% of Australian dermatologists have their primary place of practice in a regional or remote area [6].

Skin cancers disproportionately affect outdoor workers like farmers [7]. Due to the nature of their work, Australian farmers are exposed to more UVR than the general population. Observational studies suggest that farmers have a higher likelihood of NMSC compared to non‐farmers and are at higher risk of dying from skin cancer [7, 8, 9]. Australia's skin cancer prevention campaigns have been widely influential, yet farmers still demonstrate suboptimal sun protective behaviours despite recognising the carcinogenic effects of UVR [9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

Primary prevention of skin cancer involves reducing UVR exposure through lifestyle modifications. These include wearing protective clothing, hats and sunglasses, correctly applying and reapplying sunscreen, seeking shade, and avoiding the sun when UVR is at its peak [1, 14]. These are life‐saving and cost‐effective interventions, especially in the workplace [14, 15]. Secondary prevention includes the early detection of skin cancers, and tertiary prevention involves treating skin cancers to prevent progression. However, primary prevention remains critical to prevent skin cancers from developing, progressing, or recurring. Reducing UVR exposure at any age reduces the risk of skin cancer [16].

Prior research has identified numerous barriers and enablers to using sun protection among Australian farmers. The sticky consistency of sunscreen, having to reapply sunscreen, and the inability to undertake farm work in the shade are common barriers [10]. Enablers of sun protective behaviours include UVR protective clothing, sun‐protected environments for farming activities, and workplace interventions that increase sun‐safety knowledge and practices [17].

Reducing skin cancer incidence in farmers requires locally implemented, targeted and evidence‐based workplace interventions and public health advocacy. A more detailed understanding of the highest risk groups of farmers is required to tailor these programs. This cross‐sectional study aims to identify target populations within a sample of Victorian farmers to guide skin cancer prevention interventions.

Method

Data Collection

The National Centre for Farmer Health's (NCFH) Health and Lifestyle Assessment (HLA) is a comprehensive questionnaire of farmers' overall health, safety, and wellbeing [18]. The questionnaire was conducted opportunistically at agricultural field days or rural events from April 2024 to January 2025, in conjunction with a metabolic health check, and was open to Victorian farmers aged 18 years and over. The HLA captures demographic data including gender, age, and postcode, and assesses sun protective behaviours and skin cancer history. If participants used sun protection, this was described as always, sometimes, or never wearing sunscreen, sunglasses, long sleeved shirts, long trousers, or broad brim hats. All participants of the assessment were provided with relevant, practical health resources. Once questionnaires were completed, the de‐identified data were recorded in IBM SPSS Statistics 30.0 [19].

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 30.0 [19] with descriptive and inferential statistics. Chi‐square tests were run to assess if sun protective behaviours differed by demographic factors (gender, age, or rurality). Ordinal logistic regression (OLR) tests were conducted to evaluate if demographic factors were associated with higher or lower frequencies of sun protection use. To analyse rurality, participant home postcodes were recoded into Modified Monash (MM) categories of remoteness using the Australian Health Workforce Locator [20, 21]. The 2023 Modified Monash Model (MMM) classifications are: metropolitan areas (MM 1), regional centres (MM 2), large rural towns (MM 3), medium rural towns (MM 4), small rural towns (MM 5), remote communities (MM 6), and very remote communities (MM 7). Of note, there are no MM 7 areas in Victoria. Due to small sample sizes, MM 1 to MM 3 were combined for analysis.

Results

Participant Demographics

In total, 124 responses were included for analysis (Table 1). All participants were Victorian residents aged 18 years or older and involved in the farming and agricultural industry. Participants were predominantly male (n = 87, 70.2%), consistent with Australia's farming population [22]. Participants were aged from 18 to 83 years, with the majority of participants over the age of 50 (n = 84, 67.7%). Most participants lived in regions classified as small rural towns (MM 5, n = 88, 71.0%) and medium rural towns (MM 4, n = 18, 14.5%).

Skin Health History

Nearly three quarters of participants had previously had a skin check (n = 88, 71%), of which 38.6% (n = 34) were in the last 12 months. Almost one third of participants reported a history of treated skin cancer (n = 36, 29.0%), including five who had a history of melanoma. Data on undiagnosed or untreated skin cancers were not captured, so these figures may be underestimates.

Sun Protective Behaviours

Almost all participants reported using sun protection (n = 121, 97.6%). Despite this, only 8.9% (n = 11) of participants answered ‘always’ to using sunscreen, sunglasses, sun protective clothing, and a broad brim hat at the same time. The most frequent response for all sun protective behaviours was ‘sometimes’ (Figure 1). The most commonly used items when ‘always’ and ‘sometimes’ responses were combined were broad brim hats (n = 102, 82.3%) and sunglasses (n = 102, 82.3%). The sun protective items most frequently reported as ‘always’ used were sunglasses (n = 47, 37.9%) and long trousers (n = 45, 36.3%). Using sunscreen (n = 31, 25%) and long trousers (n = 32, 25.8%) were most frequently reported as ‘never’ used.

Gender and Sun Protection Use

Sunscreen use differed by gender, but differences were not significant (p = 0.059). Females demonstrated a trend towards always or sometimes wearing sunscreen (Tables 1 and 2). Wearing long trousers also differed by gender but results were again not significant (p = 0.080), with males showing a trend towards always wearing long trousers. There were no statistically significant differences between gender and the use of sunglasses (p = 0.727), long sleeve shirts (p = 0.220), or broad brim hats (p = 0.607).

Age Group and Sun Protection Use

Sunscreen use differed by age group, and differences were statistically significant (p = 0.035) (Table 3). The 65+ age group was most likely to never wear sunscreen (n = 15, 44.1%) compared to other age groups. The 50–64 age group was most likely to always wear sunscreen (n = 18, 36.0%), while the 30–49 and 18–29 age groups were most likely to sometimes wear sunscreen (30–49 n = 18, 58.1%; 18–29 n = 6, 66.7%). OLR showed the effect of age group on sunscreen use was significant only for the 50–64 group compared to the reference group (65+) (OR = 2.435, p = 0.035, 95% CI [1.064, 5.573]). There were no statistically significant differences between age group and the use of sunglasses (p = 0.879), long sleeve shirts (p = 0.705), long trousers (p = 0.135), or broad brim hats (p = 0.303).

Discussion

This study sought to determine if demographic factors (age, gender, rurality) were associated with Victorian farmers' sun protective behaviours and identify populations that may benefit from targeted public health interventions. Farmers aged 65 and older were found to be significantly less likely to wear sunscreen than younger age groups. Gender was not found to be associated with sun protection use in this sample, though there was a trend among males to use sunscreen less frequently, and among females to wear long trousers less often. There were no differences in sun protection use when stratified by rurality.

Most participants reported using sun protection, which may demonstrate recognition of the carcinogenic properties of UVR; however, sun protection use was consistently inadequate. Sunglasses and long trousers were the most frequently used items; however, it is probable that these were utilised for practicality rather than strictly for sun protection. A quarter of participants reported never using sunscreen, highlighting a key focus area for public health advocacy: in particular, the need for sun protection products better suited to agricultural work environments, and more contextually appropriate sun protection education. The Australian Cancer Council recommends outdoor workers wear sun protection including sunscreen year round, even when the UVR index is below 3, highlighting the inadequacy of sun protection uptake in this cohort [14].

Female farmers showed a trend towards using sunscreen more consistently (p = 0.059), which is consistent with prior Australian research. Trenerry and colleagues [10] analysed a cohort of 498 South Australian farmers and found females were significantly more likely to wear sunscreen (p < 0.001), though the proportion of females was low (n = 57, 11.4%). Woods et al. [23] conducted a similar analysis of 243 farmers from New South Wales, whose population was 29.3% female, and also found that females were significantly more likely to wear sunscreen (p < 0.001). These trends are not unique to farmers, but are reflected in the broader Australian population: Lee et al. [24] analysed survey responses of 417 attendees (58.1% female) of a dermatology clinic in New South Wales, and females were twice as likely as men to wear sunscreen every day (23% of females compared to 11% of males) (p = 0.002).

Gender differences in sunscreen use have been attributed to differing motivations and perceptions. Abroms et al. [25] interviewed a cohort of 52 American adults aged 18 to 25 and found that males and females used sunscreen to prevent an uneven tan, peeling, or adverse health effects, but preventing the aging effects of UVR was only a predominant concern for females. Males were particularly concerned about the greasy nature and perceived feminine scent of sunscreen and conveyed that applying sunscreen was not masculine; however, they were motivated to use sunscreen if it resulted in prolonging outdoor activities such as sport. An Australian study of 62 Northern Queenslanders similarly found that females were strongly motivated by a desire to maintain a youthful appearance, while males perceived wrinkles and a weather‐beaten face to be more masculine [26]. Reframing sunscreen as a practical tool to prevent painful sunburn and avoid time lost to treatment, rather than as a cosmetic product, may therefore encourage uptake among male farmers.

Females demonstrated a trend against wearing long trousers compared to males in this cohort (p = 0.080). Females are known to have higher rates of melanoma on the lower limbs, while males are more likely to have melanoma of the head, neck, or trunk. This difference in melanoma distribution has been attributed to differences in clothing preferences and tanning behaviours, but is also hypothesised to be related to differing distributions of melanocytes in males and females [27]. Female farmers may be encouraged to apply sunscreen to all exposed skin and educated on the cosmetic and health consequences of UVR exposure to the lower limbs in order to reduce their risk of melanoma.

There was a statistically significant association between age and sunscreen use in this cohort (p = 0.035). The oldest population in this sample, and therefore the population most likely to develop melanoma, was the least likely to wear sunscreen. Similar results were found by Lee et al., where increasing age was associated with lower sunscreen use (p = 0.001) [24]. This may be due to older Australians being born prior to Australia's 1981 Slip‐Slop‐Slap campaign and consequently poorer skin‐health literacy, beliefs that UVR‐induced damage to their skin has already been done, or other competing health concerns that lower the priority of sun protection [24, 26]. While cumulative UVR‐induced damage accumulated in childhood does have a disproportionate impact on lifetime melanoma risk, sunscreen use at any age is known to reduce melanoma risk [16]. This message should be communicated opportunistically by all health professionals interacting with farmers, and especially those aged 65 and older.

Farmers in this cohort were predominantly from rural areas limiting comparison with metropolitan and regional areas. Consequently, few conclusions were able to be drawn about the effect of rurality on sun protection use, nor were any results statistically significant. These results may suggest that the nature of farming work, and subsequently the use of sun protection, is not greatly influenced by rurality. Future research investigating UVR exposure by farming industry may reveal higher risk sectors, depending on the feasibility of the farming or agricultural work to be completed in shade, enclosed areas, or within vehicles.

Numerous barriers have been identified for Australian farmers that prevent them from utilising adequate sun protection, requiring a multifaceted approach to intervention. Trenerry et al. [10] characterised the sun protection barriers, facilitators, and motivators of 498 South Australian farmers; the majority of study participants (73.8%) reported their main barriers were the perceived discomfort, practicality, or availability of sun protective equipment for farm work. Half of the participants (50.7%) reported that they would use sun protection if improvements were made to the protective items to make them more suitable for farm work. However, only 7.9% of farmers in this cohort reported that they would be motivated to use sun protection if they had access to better protective items. Rather, the majority of participants (53.1%) reported they would be motivated by a personal health scare or a health scare of a friend or relative. This demonstrates that the major barriers to sun protection use in farmers are multifactorial but ultimately stem from a lack of incentive and misunderstanding of skin cancer risk. In order to reach farmers and encourage behavioural change, a comprehensive and targeted public health approach is needed. Sun protective equipment that is suitable for farm work such as lightweight protective clothing and non‐sticky sunscreen already exist, but encouraging their use may require incentives such as tax deductions on purchase of sun protective equipment or coordination with agricultural companies who provide free branded merchandise to farmers. Interventions that address the motivation of a health scare, such as UV photography at agricultural field days, promotional campaigns using real‐life stories from farmers affected by skin cancer, or encouraging healthcare professionals to discuss the importance of skin cancer prevention opportunistically may motivate farmers to prioritise their skin health. Rye and colleagues [17] demonstrated that comprehensive workplace interventions can also increase the use of sun protection among outdoor workers; the implementation of sun safety policies, provision of sun protective infrastructure and PPE, education programs, role modelling from supervisors, and facilitating skin checks contributed to increasing outdoor workers' use of sun protective clothing and seeking of shade. Farm managers, regulatory bodies, and industry organisations have a responsibility to protect outdoor workers by ensuring effective and realistic sun safety interventions are implemented on Australian farms. A focus group of Australian farmers identified to be in higher risk demographic groups, based on results of this study, could be created to discuss these interventions and identify which would lead to behavioural change.

Limitations

Differences in sun protective behaviours were identified; however, due to a small sample size, effect sizes were small and consequently results require cautious interpretation. Furthermore, the HLA questionnaire is a survey completed by self‐selected participants, so volunteer bias cannot be excluded. Similar studies in larger cohorts of farmers are required to confirm trends.

No data were collected regarding participants' Fitzpatrick skin phototype, untreated skin cancers or lesions of concern, or if the individual had multiple melanomas. No analysis was completed to link sun protective behaviours with skin cancer history due to the likely unreliability of the results. These data would have added valuable insights to the analysis that was performed, in particular regarding the skin cancer risk profile of individuals in this cohort.

Due to the nature of the data collected in the HLA questionnaire, the ability to draw qualitative conclusions is limited. It is unknown whether participants who always wore a long sleeve shirt did so primarily to protect their skin from UVR, or to protect from physical hazards or environmental factors. Similarly, despite sunscreen being inadequately used in this sample, it is not known if this is because of discomfort, inconvenience, forgetfulness, a misunderstanding of UVR risk, or another reason. This type of analysis was beyond the scope of this study, but existing qualitative research on the values and attitudes that result in positive sun protective behaviours will be beneficial in tailoring health interventions [11, 28, 29, 30].

Conclusion

This cross‐sectional study of 124 Victorian farmers revealed a low uptake of adequate sun protection. Key target areas include promoting sunscreen use among males and individuals over 65, and increasing the use of lower limb sun protection among females. Overall, sun protection practices were suboptimal universally across this at‐risk cohort. While the small sample size and limited scope of the study constrain the generalisability of the findings, the results are consistent with prior research and highlight a clear need for continued advocacy by healthcare professionals and farmer groups to promote sun safety in this population. A future focus group of at‐risk farmers as identified in this study may be valuable to identify interventions that would increase sun protection use in this cohort.
