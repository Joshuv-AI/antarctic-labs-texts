The effect of different pretreatments and cleaning methods prior to adhesive cementation of lithium disilicate restorations. In vitro study
Authors: Abdulmajeed O Alotaibi, Ahmad Alghamdi, Hatem Alshammari, Christel Larsson, Evaggelia Papia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to evaluate the bond strength of adhesively cemented lithium disilicate restorations to dentin by applying different pretreatments and cleaning methods.Material and MethodsTwenty-five human posterior teeth were mounted in an acrylic mold, flattened to expose mid-coronal dentin, and divided into five groups based on different pretreatments and cleaning methods prior to adhesive cementation. After cementation, the teeth were sectioned to obtain 1 mm2 specimens, yielding 15 specimens in each group that were subjected to aging and thermocycling process, followed by a microtensile bond test and failure mode assessment.ResultsThe highest bond strength values were in the control group and in the group that was treated with fluoride and where the temporary cement was removed using pumice, whereas the lowest values were in groups that were cleaned by excavator irrespective of whether they were treated with fluoride or not. However, all groups showed mainly cohesive failure without any statistically significant differences.ConclusionsTo eliminate any possible negative impact of eugenol on adhesion to dentin, surface cleaning by rotational brush and pumice is recommended prior to cementing lithium disilicate restorations.Keywords: Adhesives, Ceramics, Dentin, Dental bonding, Eugenol, Fluorides, Lithium disilicate, Resin cements

Introduction

Nowadays, all-ceramic restorations have largely replaced previous metal-ceramic ones. The main driving force has been the demand for optimal esthetics. The survival rates of such restorations are good in general, but in order to achieve a high success rate with the highly esthetic glass–ceramic materials, a reliable bonding is important (Strasding et al., 2020).

Irrespective of the chosen material, fabricating indirect prosthetic restorations usually requires a temporary restoration. Meticulous cleaning is then required prior to bonding the final restorations to achieve optimal adhesion. Different protocols have been used to clean the abutment surface prior to final cementation. Cleaning protocols generally include manual removal using hand instruments, such as an excavator; removal by polishing the tooth surface with non-fluoridated pumice using a rotational brush; sandblasting with aluminum oxide particles; or using chemical cleaning agents, such as hydrogen peroxide and chlorohexidine solutions (Ganss and Jung, 1998, Abohamar et al., 2005). These protocols may have an effect on the bonding strength, but the studies that have evaluated the effect of different cleaning protocols on the final bond strength to dentin report contradictory findings. Furthermore, after tooth preparation, the exposed dentin has a risk of hypersensitivity (Bekes et al., 2009, Cartwright, 2014). Thus, desensitizing agents, which may or may not contain fluoride, are commonly used to reduce dentin hypersensitivity. However, these agents could adversely affect the bonding strength.

Evaluating the effect of temporary cementation (e.g., potential eugenol remnants) and cleaning protocols on the final bond strength is necessary to provide dentists with the knowledge to ensure the optimal longevity of ceramic restorations (Hansen and Asmussen, 1987). Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the bond strength of adhesively cemented lithium disilicate restorations to dentin by applying different pretreatments, cleaning methods, and desensitizing agents under similar conditions in vitro. The null hypothesis is that the bond strength between lithium disilicate restoration and tooth substance (dentin) will be the same when cemented to non-treated dentin (control group) or when dentin is treated with temporary eugenol containing cement with or without fluoride-containing desensitizer and subsequently cleaned manually by excavator or by rotational brush and non-fluoridated flour of pumice (test groups).

Temporization and pretreatment

Temporary restoration blocks were fabricated from auto-mixed self-curing composite resin material (Luxatemp, DMG Chemisch-Pharmazeutische, Germany). For DTE and DTP, one coat of fluoride varnish (Duraphat, 22,600 ppm, Woelm and Pharma, Germany) was applied with a micro-brush to the occlusal surface of the teeth and air dried for 10 s. The temporary restorations were then cemented using eugenol-containing temporary cement (TempBond, Kerr™ Restoratives, US), subjected to constant load, and subsequently kept in a 100 % humidity medium at 37 °C for seven days.

Cleaning methods

Dentin surfaces were cleaned as follows: DTE and TE were cleaned with a dental excavator; DTP and TP were cleaned with a rotating nylon midget pointed brush with firm bristles (Young™, Young Dental Manufacturing Co, US) and medium-grain-size non-fluoridated flour of pumice (Siladent, Dr. Böhme & Schöps, Germany) with a maximum speed of 5,000 rpm.

Ceramic block preparation and cementation

Twenty-five rectangular lithium disilicate blocks (IPS e.max CAD, Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Liechtenstein) were customized for each tooth. The bonding surfaces of the ceramic blocks were etched with 4 % hydrofluoric acid (IPS Ceramic Etching Gel, Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Liechtenstein) for 20 s, neutralized with IPS neutralizing powder, rinsed with water for 30 s, dried with air spray, primed with a silane-containing adhesive (Scotchbond Universal Plus, 3 M Deutschland Gmbh, Germany) for 20 s, and air dried for 5 s. Following the two steps etch and rinse protocol (ER), the bond surface of the tooth was etched with 32 % phosphoric acid (Scotchbond™ Universal Etchant, 3 M Deutschland Gmbh, Germany) for 15 s, rinsed with water spray, and air dried for 5 s. Thereafter, a universal adhesive (Scotchbond Universal Plus, 3 M Deutschland Gmbh, Germany) was applied to the dentin surfaces; it was rubbed on the dentin surfaces for 20 s and gently air dried for 5 s. RelyX™ Universal cement (3 M Deutschland Gmbh, Germany) was applied to the bond surface of the lithium disilicate using the 3 M™ RelyX™ Universal Micro Mixing Tip and loaded with a constant load of 1.1 kg to the dentin surface. Cement excess was removed by a dental explorer, and an oxygen-inhibiting gel (Liquid Strip, Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Liechtenstein) was applied on all bonding surfaces, which were cured using an LED curing light (Bluephase style, Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Liechtenstein).

Specimen preparation for micro-tensile bond strength test

Each tooth was sectioned using a low-speed diamond saw (IsoMet 5000 Linear Precision Saw, BUEHLER, US) perpendicular to the bonded interface (two directions) at a speed of 3,200 rpm (0.085 mm/s) under cooling. The final cut was done on the root surface horizontally and parallel to the bonded interface to release the specimens and obtain multiple beams (1x1x12 mm). The total number of included specimens after teeth sectioning was 75, as each group yielded 15 specimens (n = 15). All specimens were evaluated under a light microscope to exclude the specimens with signs of defect within the bonding area or with more than 120 μm cement thickness. The remaining specimens were subsequently inserted in a thermocycling device to complete 5,000 cycles between 5 °C ± 5 to 55 °C ± 5, with a dwell time of 20 s and transfer time of 10 s.

Microtensile bond strength test

The microtensile bond strength test was performed on each specimen using a universal testing machine (Instron®, US) with a crosshead speed of 1.0 mm/min until the interface of the specimen fractured, and the load at fracture was recorded in Newton as the peak load at failure. The cross-sectional area at the fracture sites was measured using a digital caliper, and the average surface area of each specimen was recorded. The microtensile bond strength values (in MPa) were calculated from the peak load at failure divided by the specimen surface area.

Assessment of failure mode

Each specimen was evaluated under a light microscope (Leica Wild M7A, Wild Heerbrugg, Switzerland) at a magnification of x 200 and failure mode was assessed as follows:

•1 = Cohesive when > 75 % within the dentin or the cement.

1 = Cohesive when > 75 % within the dentin or the cement.

•2 = Adhesive when > 75 % between the dentin and the cement.

2 = Adhesive when > 75 % between the dentin and the cement.

•3 = Mixed when 50 % within dentin or cement.

3 = Mixed when 50 % within dentin or cement.

Statistical analysis

The number of specimens was based on ISO 11405:2015. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test (with 95 % confidence interval) were used to determine statistical differences among microtensile bond strength values. The chi-square test was used for the failure analysis, utilizing IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28.0.1.1), and the level of significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Assessment of bonding microtensile bond strength

There were no pretest failures either in the pretest assessment or during the aging process. The highest bond strength values were found in the control group and the DTP group (32.19 ± 8.5 MPa and 32.52 ± 5 MPa, respectively), while the lowest values were in the manually cleaned groups DTE and TE (25.54 ± 9.1 and 24.26 ± 7.3 MPa, respectively). Furthermore, applying Tukey’s HSD test showed that the difference in bond strength results did not reach statistical significance (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, lower bond strength was noticed when the dentin was contaminated with eugenol and cleaned manually with an excavator. Nevertheless, the bond strength values did not show any statistically significant differences, possibly due to the small sample size. Since no statistical differences were found between the different cleaning methods, the null hypothesis is accepted. However, this study examined a flat smooth dentin surface in an in vitro setting, where accessibility for cleaning the surface, whether manually by excavator or by rotational brush and pumice, was much easier than in real clinical situations. Hence, a greater, perhaps even more impactful, negative effect on the bonding could be expected in the clinical setting. Interestingly, Grasso et al. (2002) reported that abutments with fine, comparatively smooth preparations, similar to those used in the present study, had consistently less residual temporary cement compared to coarse preparation in vivo when evaluated microscopically. Nonetheless, similar to the present study, their differences were not significant. Moreover, that could impact the residues of temporary cement or fluoride, as the surface roughness of the substrate also plays a major role when considering a proper cleaning method prior to final bonding.

Another possible explanation for our findings is that all the study groups were adhesively cemented following the ER protocol, which required a water-rinse step after the application of phosphoric acid (Pashley et al., 2011). This can change the concentration of eugenol present on dentin; Hume et al. (1984) reported the concentration to be higher at the dentin surface and decreasing toward the pulp. Furthermore, Carvalho et al. (2007) reported that the use of phosphoric acid as required in ER adhesive systems could remove the eugenol contaminated smear layer and that the reduction in the bond strength values was more pronounced in self-adhesive systems, which did not remove the smear layer. Moreover, Peutzfeldt and Asmussen (2006) reported that ER adhesive systems significantly yielded higher bond strength than all other adhesive systems, with or without the contamination of eugenol-containing cement.

The use of fluoride is expected to obliterate dentinal tubules and occlusion within tubule orifices and may negatively affect the adhesion between the ceramic restoration and dentin (Arrais et al., 2004, Dündar, Cal et al. 2010, Pinto et al., 2010). Interestingly, the results of our study did not show any statistically significance difference between fluoride-treated dentin groups and the non-treated group (control group). The results are in accordance with Dündar et al.’s (2010) research, which showed no adverse effect of fluoride-containing desensitizing agents on the final bond strength between lithium-disilicate restoration and dentin. The lack of difference between fluoride-treated groups and non-treated groups could be attributed to the use of the ER adhesive system, which includes the use of phosphoric acid and the water-rinse step that removes the superficial occlusion of dentinal tubules. Moreover, the superficial dentinal tubules occlusion following the fluoride treatment can be removed by daily tooth brushing or consumption of acidic beverages, which lead to a short-term desensitizing effect (Kerns et al., 1991, Kuroiwa et al., 1994, Suge et al., 1995).

The manually cleaned groups (DTE and TE) had a higher number of adhesive fractures, which may suggest that the interfaces were weaker than the other groups. However, the difference is not statistically significant, again possibly due to the sample size. Moreover, although these groups showed more adhesive fracture, the types of failure were mainly cohesive in all experimental groups.in the studies have reported that decreasing the cross-sectional area as in microtensile and microshear results in a more uniform stress distribution at the bonding area interface compared to their macro counterparts (Braga et al., 2010, Scherrer et al., 2010). Additionally, in the tensile bond test, stresses are more homogeneous across the interface than in a shear bond test (Braga et al., 2010). Therefore, this study was conducted using the microtensile bond strength test based on the ISO 11405:2015. Furthermore, although the ISO recommendation is 500 cycles, it is well documented in the literature that this is too low to evaluate bond strength durability. Consequently, the aging process using the thermocycling device was done for 5,000 cycles in this study.

This study was performed under in vitro conditions, and the findings should be carefully interpreted (Van Meerbeek et al., 2010). The tendency for statistical significance between the cleaning methods performed in the study might increase under clinical situations where accessibility is much more difficult. Therefore, the clinical use of a rotational brush and non-fluoridated flour of pumice as a cleaning method may perform better than manual cleaning methods. Additionally, only an ER adhesive system was used in this study. Other adhesive systems are available and widely used in clinics. Self-adhesive systems may be more adversely affected by the presence of eugenol. Further investigations evaluating the effect of eugenol-containing temporary cement, fluoride-containing desensitizing agent, and different cleaning protocols and comparing etch and rinse to self-adhesive systems are required to improve our understanding of their impact on the bonding system.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that the protocol for cleaning the tooth surface does not seem to have any impact on the final cementation of lithium disilicate restoration. However, performing a cleaning protocol using rotational brush and pumice rather than just an excavator is preferable to obtain a clean dentin surface and eliminate any possible adverse effect of eugenol-containing temporary cement remnants prior to final cementation.
