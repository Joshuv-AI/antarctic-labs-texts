Background

The pupillary light reflex (PLR), in which the pupil constricts in response to a single pulse of light, is a crucial biological reflex involving the retina, optic nerves, midbrain, brainstem, sympathetic, and parasympathetic nervous systems [1–3]. In dim light, the iris dilator muscle innervated by the sympathetic nervous system dilates the pupil, and when light enters through the pupil, the parasympathetic nervous system activates the circumferential sphincter muscle to constrict the pupil [4]. For PLR to properly function, the integrity of the reflex arc passing through the brainstem is vital. Naturally, the PLR serves as a key marker for neurological decline in disorders including stroke, infection, tumors, traumatic brain injury, and aneurysms due to increased intracranial pressure [5–17]. Moreover, recent studies have shown the degree of PLR reactivity reflects the prognosis of patients with intracranial hemorrhage and traumatic brain injury [8, 11–15]. It is therefore crucial to accurately examine the pupil and assess its reactivity. However, manual pupil assessment using a penlight is subjective, since pupil diameter is roughly estimated in millimeters and pupil reactivity is described in qualitative terms such as “prompt”, “sluggish”, or “fixed” [18]. In fact, the median error for manually measuring the pupil diameter was 0.5 mm, which was more than twice that of a pupillometer [3]. Moreover, accurately assessing pupil reactivity is rather challenging even to trained professionals due to factors such as varying levels of light stimulus and ambient light, leading to inter-examiner discrepancies [19, 20].

To overcome this inconsistency and inaccuracy of penlight manual pupil assessment, portable pupillometers have been developed, allowing objective and quantitative examinations of the dynamics of pupil response [21]. These noninvasive devices acquire images of the pupil using infrared energy, which allows accurate identification of iris and pupil borders even for dark colored irises and under varying illumination conditions due to the high degree of contrast between the iris and pupil that infrared imaging provides [22]. Pupillometers assess various parameters regarding pupil constriction dynamics, including initial and minimum pupil size, the size difference from initial to minimum (constriction amplitude), constriction and dilation velocity, and time to begin constriction (latency) [23]. Despite these pupillometers being the gold standard for measuring pupillary indices, their use is limited, especially in resource-limited environments or outside-laboratory home settings, due to issues of cost, availability, and convenience [24].

Hence, smartphones have recently been gaining attention as devices for pupil examination for their universal accessibility and convenience [25]. To date, several smartphone applications have been developed to measure pupil sizes and their changes in response to the phone’s built-in flash [21]. When compared with commercial infrared pupillometers, some studies [24, 26, 27] demonstrated high degree of accuracy, whereas others [21, 28] reported poor agreement. Even for applications with fair results, a major limitation was their use of visible light imaging, which complicates image processing for darker irises and prevents pupil measurement under dim light conditions. To overcome this shortcoming, some studies have incorporated infrared imaging with smartphones [29, 30]. However, one required an external camera with an infrared light source, and the other used the front-facing camera for facial recognition, meaning tests could only be performed by a conscious and tech-friendly self-examiner.

Therefore, we decided to keep the application simple by relying on flashlight imaging, but attempted to focus on improving measurement accuracy by applying deep learning computations. Moreover, we controlled the ambient light setting and designed the application so that it automatically acquires images when the eye is properly located in the frame and the illumination setting detected by the smartphone is adequate. We herein introduce our novel application for assessing PLR and evaluate its accuracy compared to the gold standard conventional pupillometer.

Methods

Study subjects

The study was approved by the institutional review board of Incheon St. Mary’s Hospital, The Catholic University of Korea and followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all subjects gave written informed consent before testing. 158 adult volunteers were recruited, and exams of 336 eyes, including some repeated exams, were undertaken. Exams were from eyes of 77 males and 81 females of Korean descent, aged 59.50 ± 13.67 years. Information regarding participants’ medical and ocular history, visual acuity, and intraocular pressure were recorded, but these factors were not used as exclusion criteria to fully reflect the real world.

Data capture

The S20 Fe (Samsung; Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea) was used in the present study. Images were taken using the built-in camera (12MP Ultrawide: f/2.2 aperture, 13 mm, 123º field of view).

All tests were conducted by a single investigator in a room with ambient light of 5 lx or less, which was measured by the smartphone’s built-in sensor. Subjects were placed in a chair and told to look far ahead, and one eye was tested at a time, with the other eye occluded (Fig. 1). First, the application identified the eye from the subject’s face; once the eye was situated in the center, the application automatically zoomed to the eye to acquire high resolution data of its PLR. For the first part of this process, the MediaPipe (MediaPipe by Google. Available from: https://mediapipe.dev.) [31] acquisition algorithm was used to identify landmarks of the eye. Next, we set a “trigger area” at the center of the screen at about 1/10 the image size, and pupil dynamic assessments were automatically conducted if (1) the iris was situated inside the trigger area, and (2) the iris filled 0.325 ~ 0.5 times the trigger area. This condition was met at approximately 10 cm distance from the eye to the camera, and the app audio guided the investigator to this spot. Afterwards, the camera was automatically digital-zoomed by 3x and the pupil testing was automatically initiated. Once the test was initiated on the app, images were acquired for 1 s before the flash, during the flash (1.5 s), and for 0.5 s more after the flash went off. All images were acquired at 30 Hz (1920 × 1080 resolution) for all phases. A distinguishing aspect of our application from others is that this procedure is automatically triggered, given the proper illumination settings and eye location at the center of the frame. After examination of the right eye, participants had their eyes closed for about 5 min in the dark before their left eye was measured.

Illustration showing the data capturing process

After the SmartPLR measurements, the participants had another 5 min of dark adaptation before the same investigator examined the right eye with a commercial pupillometer, the NPi-300 Pupillometer (NeurOptics Inc., Irvine, CA, USA). This device is placed directly over the eye and captures 90 images within 3 s with an infrared camera after LED light stimulus. After the automated measurement, it provides the pupil’s dynamic parameters such as maximum and minimum pupil diameter, constriction velocity (CV), percentage change in pupil size (CP), latency, and dilation velocity. In addition, the Neurological Pupil index (NPi), which is a score based on NeurOptics’ proprietary algorithm incorporating the aforementioned parameters, is displayed: NPi values of 3.0 to 4.9 are classified as normal or “brisk” pupillary response (termed class 0 in this study); less than 3.0 are assessed as abnormal or “sluggish” pupillary response (termed class 1 in this study); and 0 is classified as a non-reactive pupillary response [32]. After another 5 min of dark adaptation, NPi-300 measurement of the left eye was lastly conducted.

Development of the pupillometer software

Using the images acquired with the smartphone, the eye (Region of Interest; ROI) was cropped at 480 × 420, and the pupil and iris were annotated using the LabelMe [33] tool in the polygon annotation for ground truth measurements. With the automatic image capturing technique, the distance to the eye can vary, and therefore it is impossible to convert the measured diameter of the pupil in pixels to an absolute measurement in millimeters. Like other previous studies, we analyzed the pupil-to-iris ratio (PIR), which is dimensionless and theoretically unaffected by the distance between the camera and the subject, rather than an absolute pupil size in mm [26]. To emphasize the pupil edge, all images were processed using the CLAHE (Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization) method [34]. Once the images were annotated, we used various segmentation deep learning models including UNet [35], UNet++ [36], DeepLabV3 [37], DeepLabV3+ [38] and a major object detection/segmentation deep learning model, the Mask R-CNN [39] to analyze the images. All models were run under the following settings: 9 epoch, AdamW [40] as the optimizer, learning rate of 0.0001, MultiStepLR (milestones=[3, 6], gamma = 0.1) as the learning rate scheduler, Cross Entropy loss for loss, 0.05 for weight decay, 0.5 for the intersection over union (IoU) threshold, p = 0.5 random flip for data augmentation, and normalization with ImageNet [41] standards (mean=[0.485, 0.456, 0.406], std=[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]) with resizing to 480 × 420 for preprocessing. Each model’s performance on IoU for both the pupil and iris was compared, using backbones of ResNet50 [42] and ConvNeXt V2 [43]. For higher learning performance and faster convergence of learning abilities, the UNet, UNet++, DepLabV3, and DeepLabV3 + models used model weights pretrained by ImageNet in the encoder initialization process, and the Mask R-CNN used model weights fine-tuned for the COCO dataset [44] by utilizing a model backbone pretrained by ImageNet. After choosing the most suitable model, we then used this model as the base to further test different backbones of ResNet50, Swin Transformer [45], and ConvNeXt V2. Finally, we chose the best backbone to go along with the priorly chosen most suitable model and implemented this combination for our application.

Figure 2 shows the overview of the architecture of our application. The backbone network first extracts multi-level features from input images. The images are then refined by a neck module with Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN) to process different object scales. Afterwards, a Region Proposal Network (RPN) identifies candidate bounding boxes, which are standardized with ROI pooling. Finally, task-specific heads predict classifications, refine bounding boxes, and segment masks.

Data analysis

Before the data analysis, we excluded repeat exams and exams with blink artifacts or incomplete measurements. Afterwards, we filtered out images according to the (1) degree of eyelid opening and the (2) blurriness of the image. We determined if the eye was adequately opened by calculating , and only used images with an average of 0.75 or greater. In addition, the average blurriness of the images acquired for each eye was calculated using the Laplacian variance, and eyes with this value of 200 or more were used. For images with at least 75% of the iris visible, the iris was assumed to be a circle, and the horizontal diameter was determined in pixels for both the iris and the pupil. The PIR was calculated by dividing the pupil diameter by the iris diameter and dividing 10 from the value to normalize the data by distance. The maximum-minimum pupil size difference, CV, and CP calculated from the smartphone pupillometer measurements were compared to the conventional pupillometer. For NPi-300, the maximum and minimum pupil sizes were obtained, and CV was calculated by dividing the difference by 0.8 s []; CP was calculated by dividing the difference by the maximum pupil size []. For predicted CV values for the smartphone app data, the difference in maximum and minimum pupil size was multiplied by 30 and divided by the difference in image numbers for the respective states [i.e. ]. The values were plotted on a scatter plot to calculate the correlation value for each parameter. Lastly, we used the kernel density estimation (KDE) and Bhattacharyya Distance to qualitatively and quantitatively assess the degree of separation between the NPi classifications. KDE is a non-parametric method for estimating the probability density function of a dataset, which enables smooth visualization of data distributions without assuming any specific distributional form. The Bhattacharyya Distance provides a statistical metric of the overlap between two probability distributions, which allows quantitative measurement of the degree of separation between two distributions. We used the above methods to determine on which parameter to place more weight and to develop a scoring method to classify the pupils according to their reactivity: SmartPLR.

SmartPLR vs. NPi-300

After filtering the acquired data as outlined in the methods section, 203 eyes from 130 participants were available for analysis. Of the 203 eyes, 198 eyes were classified as NPi class 0 (3 ≤ NPi ≤ 4.9; normal/”brisk”) and 5 eyes as NPi class 1 (NPi < 3; abnormal/”sluggish”).

Discussion

In this study, we used machine learning to develop a smartphone application to assess pupil reactivity, without the need for any additional devices. After confirming its accuracy compared to the gold standard NPi-300 through Pearson correlation analysis, we developed a new scoring algorithm to classify pupil reactivity: the SmartPLR.

Commercial pupillometers are very expensive, inconvenient, and have restricted use in the clinical setting. To our knowledge, the SmartPLR is the first smartphone application to utilize the SOTA (State of the Art) algorithm, i.e. the Mask R-CNN with the backbone ConvNeXt V2, which can be operated on most modern smartphones with a FHD camaera and running Android 11 (released in 2020) or later. In addition, our application acquires images at high resolution by setting the smartphone camera’s magnification at 3x. This setting altogether provides accurate measurements of PLR, enough to fully replace the existing pupillometers, and allows usage either by health care professionals at the clinic or by patients at home, allowing for complete commercialization.

Previous efforts have been made to develop smartphone applications to examine the pupil. Some investigators tried using an infrared light source: Kim et al. used infrared LED add-ons [46]; Chang et al. used an externally powered infrared source and an infrared camera connected to a laptop [47]; and Solyman et al. used two synchronized minicameras connected to the smartphone [30]. Another application using the smartphone’s LED flashlight also required an additional 3D box to operate [18]. Regardless of their accuracy, the requirement of external devices mitigates the ultimate purpose of developing a convenient, smartphone-based application.

In contrast, Barry et al. were successful at developing an app that does not require additional devices [29]. They used the smartphone’s near-infrared camera for facial recognition to gather absolute measurements and achieved a mean absolute error in absolute pupil size of 0.39 mm when compared with a gold standard pupillometer. Despite this novel solution, the app can only function on a portion of available smartphones (i.e. iPhones), and the process of self-examination revealed limitations of inaccurate imaging angle and blinking issues.

Moreover, some applications focused on software development while using the visible light source from smartphones. Piaggio et al. and Mariakakis et al. used image post-processing and deep learning, respectively, to help the smartphone camera to acquire pupillary reaction data in dim settings [18, 27]. However, the former admitted they only tested subjects with light brown eyes, and the latter PupilScreen was built on the most basic convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture. Specifically, PupilScreen’s VGG16 backbone has limitations in performance and efficiency, with a top-1 acc of only 71.5% with the ImageNet dataset [48]. Conversely, we tested five different deep learning models, and all of them with the ConvNeXt V2 backbone showed high mIoU values. UNet, UNet++, DeepLabV3, DeepLabV3 + models were originally developed in the biomedical field specifically for segmentation tasks [35–38]. Therefore, they are capable of successfully identifying class labels at the pixel level, but their localization ability to track information about locations is fairly poor. On the other hand, Mask-RCNN is a form of object detection model, and it localizes the target object and performs segmentation at the same time [39]. Thus, this model was suitable for our app since, as the user performs the test, the software needs to correctly identify the eye and simultaneously analyze its iris and pupil through segmentation.

Furthermore, the backbone of our application, ConvNeXt V2, builds on the efficiency and adaptability of CNNs, providing an effective alternative to Transformer models such as the Swin Transformer, particularly in vision tasks [43]. Although Transformers are popular for their ability to capture global dependencies, they require extensive datasets and computational resources to demonstrate stable performance. ConvNeXt V2 leverages on Transformer-inspired innovations, such as large kernel convolutions that broaden receptive fields and inverted bottleneck blocks that modernize feature processing while sustaining representational power. In addition, incorporating LayerNorm within convolutional layers enhances training stability and allows more precise control over feature distribution. These enhancements make ConvNeXt V2 lightweight and efficient, demonstrating how CNNs modified with architectural insights from Transformer models can provide competitive and robust solutions for complex vision applications.

Another limitation of PupilScreen is its low resolution. Although the initial resolution was very high at 1920 × 1080, the images were cropped and magnified afterwards, resulting in a low resolution of images during the data analysis phase. Our application, on the other hand, provides higher resolution images for analysis, since the images are acquired at 3x magnification from the start. Finally, our application is unique in that it can be performed by a separate examiner or oneself with the help of audio guidance, and the exam is automatically initiated once the eye is properly situated and under an adequate illumination setting. This relieves the need for pressing a button on the screen, completely freeing one hand for lifting the eyelid especially for unconscious examinees.

Interestingly, some of the eyes classified as class 0 (normal) in the NPi-300 were categorized as sluggish in our new SmartPLR classification. NPi was primarily developed for neurologic patients, to identify definitive pupillary response defects [11]. It is crucial to identify sluggish or non-reactive pupils with high specificity in these critically ill patients. The SmartPLR classification, on the other hand, seems to provide greater sensitivity, and this may provide better screening value since PLR can also be diminished in ocular situations, namely glaucoma [49]. In fact, of the 11 NPi-300 normal eyes classified as sluggish in the SmartPLR, 9 were those diagnosed with glaucoma.

Limitations of our app include its lack of feasibility testing, especially for non-professionals. For this device to be ubiquitously used, its accuracy demonstrated by investigators needs to be replicated by ordinary people. Moreover, our app was only tested in a dim environment, which can be especially hard to control in a setting outside the clinic. Notably, Bogucki et al. used machine learning to develop the Pupil Reactivity (PuRe) score, which was accurate regardless of changes in ambient illumination magnitudes [23]. Future studies may involve testing our app in varying illumination settings, and possibly modifying the SmartPLR to function at varying lighting settings.

Conclusions

Nevertheless, our unique pupillometer app, which does not require any additional devices for pupil reactivity assessment, demonstrated promising results regarding its accuracy (Pearson correlation values 0.77 for pupil size difference, 0.77 for CV, and 0.74 for CP) compared to a commercial pupillometer in an Asian population, which consists mostly of dark irises.
