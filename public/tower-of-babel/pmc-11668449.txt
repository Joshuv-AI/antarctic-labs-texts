Overzealous over-the-counter cough syrup ingestion leading to serious manifestation in a child – A case report
Authors: Dinkar Yadav, Hunny Verma, Neha Yadav, Kapil Bhalla
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCough is a protective reflex that allows clearance of secretions from upper respiratory tract. It is not a disease by itself but a symptom of underlying disease. In a majority of cases, it is self-limiting and requires only supportive care. But over-the-counter cough syrups are inadvertently used to relieve cough in Indian settings. We hereby report a case of a 6-year-old girl child who presented to pediatrics emergency services with complaints of loss of consciousness and abnormal body movement after ingestion of dextromethorphan and chlorpheniramine containing cough syrup. All investigations including cerebrospinal fluid examination, neuroimaging, and electroencephalogram revealed no significant abnormality. The child was admitted for 5 days and discharged after that in stable conditions.

Introduction

Cough is a common symptom encountered in the pediatric age group. It is mostly caused by viral infection and self-limiting in nature. But an over-the-counter (OTC) irrational combination cough syrup was used for symptomatic relief. Several side effects have been reported with irrational use of these drugs, and the severity may even lead to mortality in a few cases.[1] In this index case, we describe the serious side effects after the ingestion of an OTC irrational combination cough syrup.

Case Report

A 6-year-old girl child was brought to Pediatric Emergency services with complaints of abnormal body movements in the form of increased tone of all four limbs, uprolling of eye balls, and frothing from mouth lasting for approximately 10–12 minutes, followed by loss of consciousness since last 1–2 hours. No other significant history such as fever and vomiting was present. There was no history of similar episodes in the past and no family history of similar illness. She was immunized for age and belongs to lower socioeconomic strata as per modified Kuppuswami classification. On examination, the child was in altered sensorium. The heart rate was 122 beats per minute, the respiratory rate was 24 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation was 85% on room air and 100% on face mask. Her blood pressure (BP) was 100/70 mmHg and RBS was 103 mg/dl. Central nervous system (CNS) examination showed that the child was in altered sensorium and bilateral pupils were sluggishly reactive to light with normal tone in all limbs and all superficial and deep tendon reflexes preserved. After reviewing the initial complaints, it was found out that the father had given a cough syrup containing dextromethorphan and chlorpheniramine (approx 2 teaspoons since 1 day) to relieve running nose and cough in the child, which was present since the night prior to the hospitalization. Then parents brought the child to the emergency department. We immediately secured two IV lines, and all base line investigations like complete hemogram, kidney function test, and liver function test with serum electrolytes were sent. On investigation, hemoglobin was 12.4 mg/dl, white blood cells were 6700/cumm, platelet count was 1.84 lacs, the erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 15 mm in the first hour, blood urea was 24 mg/dl, and serum creatinine was 0.4 mg/dl. Liver function test and serum electrolytes were normal. Cerebrospinal fluid examination was done, which revealed no cells; sugar and proteins were in normal limits. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) brain was done, and no significant abnormality was detected. Electroencephalogram done showed no epileptiform discharges. Quantitative dextromethorphan levels were not performed because of institutional nonavailability and financial constraint of parents.

Differential diagnosis: Sepsis, meningitis, encephalitis, CNS tumor, hypoglycemia

Final diagnosis: Cough syrup intoxication

Management and follow-up: The child was immediately shifted to Pediatric ICU. The child was managed with oxygen support, IV fluids, and injection levetiracetam with symptomatic and supportive care. After 24 hours, the child was conscious, oriented, and alert and hence shifted to a ward. The child was observed for 5 days and remained well without similar episodes, and all neurological findings were resolved. The antiepileptic was stopped, and the child was discharged after 5 days in stable conditions.

Discussion

OTC cough syrups are commonly used for symptomatic relief of cough and cold in Indian settings. Several fixed drug combinations are available as OTC drugs or nonprescription drugs for symptomatic cough relief, and they usually have irrational combinations of nonindicated drugs. Some health care professionals also prescribe these fixed drug formulations for symptomatic cough relief. Dextromethorpan, phenylephrine, and chlorpheniramine are common constituents of cough syrups.[2] Dextromethorphan is available in both prescription and nonprescription OTC formulations and is utilized as an antitussive agent.[3] Therapeutic activity of dextromethorphan is primarily due to its active metabolite dextrorphan. It mainly acts on NMDA receptors and antagonizes similar to ketamine and phencyclidine, but it differs biochemically from them. It also inhibits the reuptake of serotonin.[4] Chlorpheniramine is H1-histaminic receptor antagonist with potent antimuscarinic action. It also blocks serotonin reuptake.[5] Seltzer et al.[6] reported a case of 23-month-old male child presented with complaints of vomiting, progressive lethargy, apnea, and respiratory failure. He was intubated in view of continuous apnea episodes and extubated on the same day. Diagnosis was made on the basis of serum dextromethorphan level (110 ng/ml) and electroencephalogram. He was discharged on the next day. Monte et al.[7] reported a case report and review of literature to conclude chlorpheniramine and dextromethorphan have serotonergic effects and combination drugs should be avoided because cumulative serotonergic effects lead to serotonin toxicity. Hotha et al.[8] report a case series of three children presented with altered levels of consciousness even after the first dose of a dextromethorphan-containing cough syrup. Clinical presentation of dextromethorphan (toxic level 10 mg/kg) and chlorpheniramine toxicity includes confusion, nystagmus, disorientation, drowsiness, euphoria, hallucination, ataxia, seizures, and coma.[910] The index case presented with complaints of abnormal body movements (generalized tonic-clonic seizures) followed by loss of consciousness. Most patients with similar presentation are successfully managed with symptomatic and supportive care.[11] The index case was managed with antiepileptic with supportive care and showed full recovery on the same treatment. According to Indian Academy of pediatrics (IAP) Standard Treatment Guidelines 2022, antitussives should not be used in children under 6 years of age because of lack of demonstrated efficacy and potential adverse effects.

Conclusion

This case highlights life-threatening adverse reaction after ingestion of OTC dextromethorphan- and chlorpheniramine-containing cough syrups. Hence, health care professionals should prescribe dextromethorphan and chlorpheniramine with extreme caution in children. OTC antitussives should not be used in children less than 6 years of age because of documented serious adverse effects.
