Foundation Models for Logistics: Toward Certifiable, Conversational Planning Interfaces
Yunhao Yang, Neel P. Bhatt, Christian Ellis, Alvaro Velasquez, Zhangyang Wang, Ufuk Topcu {neurosymbolicintelligence}@gmail.com

Abstract
Logistics operators, from battlefield coordinators rerouting airlifts ahead of a storm to warehouse managers juggling late trucks, often face life - critical decisions that demand both domain expertise and rapid and continuous replanning. While popular methods like integer programming yield logistics plans that satisfy user-defined logical constraints, they are slow and assume an idealized mathematical model of the environment that does not account for uncertainty. On the other hand, large language models (LLMs) can handle uncertainty and promise to accelerate replanning while lowering the barrier to entry by translating free - form utterances into executable plans, yet they remain prone to misinterpretations and hallucinations that jeopardize safety and cost. We introduce a neurosymbolic framework that pairs the accessibility of natural - language dialogue with verifiable guarantees on goal interpretation . It converts user requests into structured planning specifications, quantifies its own uncertainty at the field and token level, and invokes an interactive clarification loop whenever confidence falls below an adaptive threshold. A lightweight model, fine - tuned on just 100 uncertainty - filtered examples, surpasses the zero - shot performance of GPT - 4.1 while cutting inference latency by nearly 50%. These preliminary results highlight a practical path toward certifiable, real - time, and user - aligned decision-making for complex logistics.

Problem Formulation

We consider a setting in which a user communicates a planning objective through a text-based or image-based prompt, describing goals and constraints in informal terms. The system must interpret this input and produce a structured representation of the problem—such as a planning domain and instance in PDDL or a JSON-based constraint specification—that can be executed or verified by downstream planning solvers.

Formally, let
\mathcal{X}
denote the space of user inputs (which could be text and/or images), and
\mathcal{Y}
denote the space of structured descriptions for planning problems (e.g., PDDL). A LLM defines a mapping
f_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
parameterized by
\theta
. However, this mapping is inherently stochastic and lacks guarantees of correctness. Our goal is to introduce a verification-guided framework that enhances
f_{\theta}
with uncertainty-aware inference and user clarification.

We define the problem as follows:

•

Input:
A user prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
describing a logistic goal under implicit or explicit constraints.

•

Output:
A structured representation
y\in\mathcal{Y}
(e.g., PDDL goal or JSON object) that encodes the inferred user objective.

•

Objective:
Maximize the probability that
y
correctly reflects the true intent of the user, subject to constraints inferred or retrieved from the user input.

To operationalize this, we introduce a confidence estimator
\hat{p}(y\mid x)
which provides a distributional estimate over the reliability of each part of the output. If the estimated confidence falls below a threshold
\tau
, the system issues a clarification query to the user. The final output
y^{*}
is either:

1.

Accepted if
\hat{p}(y^{*}\mid x)\geq\tau
, or

2.

Revised via interactive correction and re-prompting.

This formulation enables a tight integration between natural language understanding, structured representation, and user-in-the-loop validation. Furthermore, it lays the foundation for contrastive and curriculum-based training strategies that use confidence as a signal to improve
f_{\theta}
over time.

4
Vision–Language-Logistics Agents: High-Level Framework

The Vision–Language-Logistics agent (VLL agent), whose architecture is presented in Figure
1
, is a neurosymbolic system designed to transform free-form human instructions into certified, executable logistics plans. At its core, the agent follows a closed-loop architecture that integrates perception, reasoning, and symbolic verification, enabling real-time adaptability in dynamic and high-stakes operational settings.

The VLL agent begins at the
perception
stage, where vision models process multimodal sensor data—such as satellite imagery, drone feeds, or traffic cameras—to extract salient entities and attributes. These perceptual cues ground the referents in user commands, resolving ambiguity in expressions like “the nearest operational airbase” or “avoid flooded zones.” By tightly coupling language with perception, the system ensures that generated plans are contextually grounded and spatially coherent.

In the
reasoning
stage, a foundation model interprets the grounded natural language instruction and synthesizes a structured goal specification. This output may take the form of a PDDL instance, a SQL query, or a symbolic task graph, depending on the target planner. The foundation model functions as a semantic integrator, reconciling user directives with external databases, historical mission templates, and extracted perceptual entities. Once the structured goal specification is formatted, it will be sent to the target planner (e.g., a PDDL solver) for plan generation.

The final
verification
stage invokes a symbolic verifier that evaluates the generated plan against domain-specific constraints. These may include airspace regulations, cargo limitations, fuel availability, time windows, or cost budgets. If the plan is compliant and feasible, it is passed downstream for execution. Otherwise, the agent generates a clarification query for the user, enabling a feedback loop that corrects intent before execution. The results of the verification and clarification are then used as data to fine-tune the VLL agent to make it increasingly more effective at producing plans that satisfy the underlying constraints.

5
Uncertainty-Aware Intent-Verification

Overview of the intent-verification pipeline: The system extracts structured goals and constraints from user input using a foundation model. Confidence estimation is performed to identify uncertain outputs. When uncertainty is high, the system initiates user clarification.

This work focuses on the
reasoning stage
of the VLL agent, where a foundation model interprets user instructions. We develop an
intent–verification pipeline
that interprets user instructions, assesses confidence in the extracted goals, and proactively resolves ambiguity before plan synthesis. The pipeline, illustrated in Figure
2
, consists of three main stages: (1) user objective interpretation, (2) uncertainty estimation and clarification, and (3) verification-guided refinement. This section details each component.

5.1
User Objective Interpretation

The user provides a goal description in natural language, which may contain vague, implicit, or underspecified objectives. The system uses a foundation language model
f_{\theta}
to generate a structured representation
y
of the user’s goal. This representation can take the form of a JSON object, a PDDL goal statement, or another planning-compatible format. Additionally, the model extracts auxiliary information such as relevant constraints and entities (e.g., locations, deadlines) from connected databases.

5.2
Uncertainty Estimation and Clarification

To assess whether the generated output
y
faithfully represents the user’s intent, we introduce a confidence estimator
\hat{p}(y\mid x)
that computes either:

•

Token-level confidence scores for key phrases and values,

•

Or, a global confidence score over the entire structured output.

If any critical component of the output has confidence below a predefined threshold
\tau
, the system enters a clarification mode. In this mode, it issues targeted follow-up questions to the user, requesting confirmation or disambiguation on specific parts of the goal (e.g., “Do you mean to minimize cost or time?” or “Should weather conditions be considered?”).

5.3
Verification-Guided Refinement

Once high-confidence outputs are obtained—either directly or through clarification—they are passed through downstream solvers or verification tools to check for constraint satisfaction and domain feasibility. The resulting plan can be compared against cached solutions or checked for compliance (e.g., fuel, cost, regulatory constraints).

These verification outcomes are then used to iteratively refine the language model via fine-tuning. Specifically, our method supports:

•

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
using human-annotated and clarified examples,

•

Self-Supervised Contrastive Learning
by constructing high- vs. low-confidence pairs,

•

Self-Training
using pseudo-labels filtered by model uncertainty.

•

Reinforcement Learning
using the verification results as a reward signal for reinforcement fine-tuning.

This iterative refinement loop gradually improves the alignment between the model’s outputs and user goals, while preserving transparency through uncertainty metrics.

7
Conclusion and Future Directions

This paper introduces the Vision–Language-Logistic agent, a practical instantiation of a broader class of certifiable, conversational AI agents for logistics. Built around an uncertainty-aware intent–verification loop, VLL agent bridges the gap between natural language accessibility and symbolic verifiability. It transforms vague, free-form instructions into structured plans through a tight perception–language–reasoning pipeline, while proactively identifying and resolving ambiguities before costly execution errors occur.

Empirically, we show that our fine-tuned VLL interpreter—augmented with confidence estimation and clarification—outperforms larger foundation models on intent-matching tasks while halving inference latency. The result is a responsive, reliable system that brings symbolic assurance into the LLM loop without sacrificing usability or speed.

Future Directions.

Building on these results, we identify several promising avenues for extension:

•

Latent-Space Guarantees:
Inspired by decision-boundary analysis in RepV
[
15
]
, we aim to develop probabilistic guarantees based on distance to decision boundaries in the model’s latent space, enabling more formal verification of alignment.

•

Curriculum-Guided Self-Training:
We plan to scale up the self-training pipeline using confidence-based filtering, enabling continual learning from unlabeled data while mitigating the risks of noisy pseudo-labels.

•

Multimodal Input Integration:
Future versions of VLLM will incorporate visual inputs (such as aerial maps) via a vision encoder cascaded with a projector trained using contrastive learning to better ground goal extraction in perceptual context, improving robustness in complex logistics scenarios.

•

Symbolic Verification Layer:
We aim to integrate symbolic planning and constraint-solving engines into the feedback loop, enabling formal verification of LLM-generated outputs beyond simple post-hoc checks.

Overall, the VLL agent provides a general and extensible foundation for verifiable, user-aligned AI planning—paving the way for broader deployment of natural language interfaces in structured decision-making systems.
