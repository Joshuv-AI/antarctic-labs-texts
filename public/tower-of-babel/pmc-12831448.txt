Characterization of the mango phyllosphere microbiome during fruit-setting unveils unique community assembly and coexisting beneficial-pathogenic microbiota
Authors: Muzammil Hussain, Nazir Ahmed, Xiaona Xie, Zheng Teng, Xianbin Hou, Xi Li, Zhengzhou Yang, Zhengjie Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMango is an economically important fruit tree with rich genetic diversity, but has been threatened by a diverse range of pathogens, causing substantial losses annually. While the plant-associated microbiomes are well studied, the assembly patterns and composition of phyllosphere microbial communities for deciphering potential pathogenic and beneficial microbiota during the mango fruit-setting phase remain largely unexplored.ResultsThis study profiled the bacterial and fungal communities in mango leaves, flowers, and fruitlets using high-throughput amplicon sequencing. Both flowers and fruitlets exhibited significantly less diverse microbial communities than leaves, and the bacterial and fungal microbiota compositions of all three organs were distinct from one another. Importantly, organ-specific preferences were observed among the dominant bacterial families, with mango flowers characterized by a high abundance of Erwiniaceae, fruitlets by Acetobacteraceae, and leaves by Microbacteriaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae. In contrast, fungal families were less differentiated, with only Didymellaceae and Symmetrosporaceae showing leaf-specific enrichment relative to flowers and fruitlets. Analysis of the shared microbial genera across the three phyllosphere organs revealed the presence of core bacterial taxa Pseudomonas and Sphingomonas, while the fungal core genera were Alternaria, Aureobasidium, Cladosporium, Epicoccum, Fusarium, Hannaella, Nectria, Neodidymella, and Vishniacozyma. Notably, several of these concurrently detected microbial genera have been previously documented as either pathogenic (e.g., Alternaria, Cladosporium and Fusarium) or beneficial (Aureobasidium, Vishniacozyma, Sphingomonas and Pseudomonas). Furthermore, we found that the mango phyllosphere bacterial and fungal communities were established by parallel selection of the microbiota, instead of consecutive selection. Deciphering the functional profile of the phyllosphere microbiota revealed the association of microbial taxa with functions such as chemoheterotrophy, aerobic chemoheterotrophy, fermentation, aromatic compound degradation, plant pathogens, endophytes, and saprotrophs, indicating a balanced ecosystem in which future fruit health outcomes may be determined by subtle shifts in the microbial community structure.ConclusionOur findings offer a checklist of the core beneficial and pathogenic microbes inhabiting the mango phyllosphere, while also highlighting the selective role of mango organs in recruiting specific subsets of microbiota, which can be harnessed for disease protection and improving mango production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07943-3.Keywords: Beneficial microbiota, Community assembly, Core microbiome, Mango, Microbial community, Pathogenic microbiota, Phyllosphere

Introduction

Microorganisms associated with different plant compartments, including belowground (i.e., rhizosphere and root endosphere) and aboveground (i.e., phyllosphere) parts, perform several functions for the host, such as nutrient acquisition, flowering plasticity, abiotic tolerance, and disease suppression, leading to enhanced plant productivity [1]. Many studies have demonstrated that plant genotype, developmental stage, and (a)biotic factors are the major determinants of plant microbiome assembly [2, 3]. Importantly, the core microbiota within microbial communities has been documented to be consistently associated with specific plant species under variable environmental conditions. These specific sets of core microbiota co-evolve with host plants, and their activity ensures long-term crop health and productivity [4, 5]. Therefore, revealing the assembly cues of plant microbiota is critical for gaining insights into plant-microbe holobionts and microbiome engineering for better ecosystem services.

The phyllosphere, the aboveground parts of plants—stem, leaves, flowers, and fruits—are colonized by distinct bacterial, fungal and viral communities that influence plant health. Despite the high microbial richness in phyllosphere habitats, bacterial and fungal communities are largely dominated by relatively few bacterial phyla, such as Actinomycetota, Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Pseudomonadota, and fungal phyla Ascomycota and Basidiomycota [6]. Research into the assembly of these communities has revealed that soil acts as a common source of microbial inoculum for phyllosphere colonization [7]. Over the past decades, plant leaves have been extensively investigated for diverse communities of co-existing bacteria and fungi in different ecosystems [8]. More recently, the fruit microbiome has emerged as a new frontier in postharvest biology and biocontrol research [9]. Consequently, characterizing the structure and function of the phyllosphere microbiome during different plant growth stages may help decipher specific subsets of microbiota to promote plant productivity and suppress diseases.

Mango (Mangifera indica L.) is an economically important and widely cultivated tree in tropical and subtropical regions of the world. Mango fruit is a potent source of multivitamins (A, C, and E) and antioxidants with strong immune support and anti-inflammatory properties, making it a valuable nutritional functional food [10]. Despite their significant nutritional characteristics and agricultural importance, mango production faces tremendous challenges, such as climate change, insect infestation, and pathogen infection [11, 12]. Previous studies on profiling microbial communities in mango trees have identified microbial population dynamics in response to specific pathogen infection [13–15]. For instance, the dynamic interplay between fungal and bacterial communities colonizing the mango fruit stem end is a critical determinant of disease, where a shift in the abundance of pathogenic fungi and chitin-degrading bacteria drives the onset of stem-end rot during fruit ripening [13]. Another study profiled the endophytic and epiphytic microbiota of mangoes during fruit development and postharvest stages, providing new insights into the structural dynamics of bacterial and fungal communities [16]. However, our knowledge of the structure and assembly cues of the mango phyllosphere microbiome (i.e., leaves, flowers and fruitlets) during the fruit-setting phase to reveal co-existing pathogenic and beneficial microbiota is still in its infancy.

Understanding the detailed structure and composition of the phyllosphere microbiome is an important first step in deciphering the mutualistic (beneficial), commensalistic (neutral), and deleterious (pathogenic) microbiota that affects plant fitness. Mango trees usually take 3–5 months from flowering to the formation of ripened fruits, depending on the cultivar type and local geographical conditions [17]. The flower formation and fruit-setting phase is an important transitional period in which resource allocation shifts dramatically by the host plant [18]. Mango fruit is typically a specialized niche that inhabits a diverse community of microbes and is more prone to pathogen invasion and disease development [13]. Investigating the composition and assembly pattern of the phyllosphere microbiome during this pivotal window may reveal the underexplored co-existence of beneficial and pathogenic microbes, which may ultimately determine the future fruit trajectory towards health or disease. Previously, it was found that the microbial population on mango fruits changes with the development of growth stages [16]. However, there is no clear evidence on how bacterial and fungal communities establish in the mango phyllosphere during the fruit-setting phase, nor on which pathogenic and beneficial microbiota are enriched on leaves, flowers, and fruitlets. We hypothesized that the aboveground mango community assembly across the three organs occurs independently and simultaneously, rather than in a stepwise organ-to-organ succession. This indicates that the microbial community might have been established as a result of parallel selection. To test this hypothesis, we performed bacterial and fungal community profiling to understand the structure and assembly cues of the mango tree leaves, flowers, and early fruitlets microbiota. The specific objectives of this study were to (i) provide insights into the composition of the mango phyllosphere bacterial and fungal communities during the fruit-setting phase, (ii) decipher the assembly pattern of the bacterial and fungal communities across the three mango organs, and (iii) identify the core pathogenic and beneficial microbial consortia inhabiting the leaf, flower, and fruit, and provide a checklist for future reference. This study offers unique insights into the characteristics of mango phyllosphere microbial communities and serves as a theoretical guide for the development of synthetic microbial communities to protect mango trees and fruits from diseases.

Results

Diversity analysis of bacterial and fungal communities in different mango organs

We obtained 1207 bacterial and 698 fungal amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) based on rarefied sequence counts from mango leaf, flower, and fruitlet samples (Fig. 1A). Alpha-diversity analysis using the Shannon diversity index on rarefied counts indicated that the bacterial (Fig. 1B) and fungal (Fig. 1C) communities inhabiting the leaves were significantly more diverse than those colonizing the flowers and fruitlets (Figure S1 and S2). The observed species patterns also showed similar trends for the bacterial and fungal communities (Figure S3). The values for the observed species were not significantly different between flowers and fruitlets.

Alpha-diversity of mango phyllosphere microbiome. A Representative images of mango leaf, flower and fruitlet. The Shannon diversity index of bacterial (B) and fungal (C) communities in mango. Different letters above each box indicate statistically significant differences according to the LSD test (p < 0.05)

PCoA analysis based on the Bray-Curtis distance revealed that phyllosphere microhabitats have a strong influence on the structure of bacterial (Fig. 2A) and fungal (Fig. 2B) communities. The mango leaf microbiota was clearly separated from the flowers and fruitlets on axis 1 (PCoA1 39.8% for bacteria and PCoA1 27.9% for fungi). Flower and fruitlet samples showed significant variations in community composition on axis 2 (PCoA2 11.3% for bacteria and PCoA2 13.9% for fungi). Adonis analysis showed that the phyllosphere microhabitat exerted significant effects on bacterial (R2 = 0.44 and P = 0.001) and fungal communities (R2 = 0.25 and P = 0.03). Specifically, the leaf microbial community was significantly different from that of the flowers (bacteria, R2 = 0.482 and P = 0.0016; fungi, R2 = 0.336 and P = 0.003) and fruitlets (bacteria, R2 = 0.393 and P = 0.0016; fungi, R2 = 0.299 and P = 0.002), and the flowers and fruitlets were also significantly different from each other (bacteria, R2 = 0.383 and P = 0.0024; fungi, R2 = 0.197 and P = 0.002).

Composition of mango phyllosphere microbiome

A notable difference was observed in the abundance of bacterial and fungal phyla across the distinct phyllosphere compartments. The mango leaves were predominantly inhabited by phyla Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidetes (Fig. 3A). The relative abundance of Pseudomonadota was significantly lower in mango leaves than in flowers and fruitlets. Pseudomonadota dominated both flower and fruitlet microhabitats. In contrast, Actinomycetota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidota were more abundant in leaves than in flowers and fruitlets. Similarly, the fungal communities of the leaves, flowers, and fruitlets were dominated by members of the phyla Ascomycota and Basidiomycota (Fig. 3B). However, there were no significant differences in the relative abundances of these phyla between phyllosphere compartments.

Taxonomic profiles of mango phyllosphere microbial communities. The relative abundance of bacterial (A) and fungal (B) communities at the phylum level in mango leaves, flowers, and fruitlets. A Relative abundance of bacterial (C) and fungal (D) communities at the family level. Different letters above each bar indicate statistically significant differences according to the LSD test (p < 0.05)

At the family level, several bacterial and fungal families were highly abundant in different compartments. For example, the relative abundance of the bacterial families Microbacteriaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae was significantly higher in leaves than flowers and fruitlets (Fig. 3C). The mango flower was potentially dominated by members of the family Erwiniaceae, which have a significantly higher relative abundance than the fruitlets and leaves. The relative abundance of Acetobacteraceae was significantly higher in mango fruitlets than in flowers and leaves. For fungi, several families dominated the fungal community of leaves, flowers, and fruitlets, but only members belonging to the families Didymellaceae and Symmetrosporaceae were abundant in leaves compared to both flowers and fruitlets (Fig. 3D).

The dominant bacterial genera included Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas, Asaia, Bfidobacterium, Curtobacterium, Acidovorax, Cellulomonas, Terribacillus, Afipia, Phyllobacterium, Microbacterium, Lactococcus, Methylobacterium, and others. The list of dominant top 50 bacterial genera inhabiting mango leaves, flowers, and fruitlets are listed in supplementary Figure S4. Likewise, the dominant fungal genera included Cladosporium, Vishniacozyma, Fusarium, Nectria, Hannaella, Alternaria, Epicoccum, Symmetrospora, Aureobasidium, Neodidymella, and others. The list of dominant top 50 fungal genera inhabiting mango leaves, flowers and fruitlets are listed in supplementary Figure S5.

Identification of mango core phyllosphere microbiota

Core microbial taxa comprised ASVs assigned to the genus level and detected in at least 50% of the samples in each phyllosphere compartment with a minimum relative abundance of 0.01%. Examination of the upset plot at the genus level revealed that mango leaves had the highest number of bacterial taxa (256), followed by flowers (116) and fruitlets (65). Among these, a significant number of bacterial taxa were organ-specific, with mango leaves showing the highest number of unique taxa (169), compared to flowers (5) and fruitlets (56). Importantly, there were considerably fewer shared bacterial and fungal taxa among the leaves, flowers, and fruits, indicating strong organ-specific bacterial assemblages (Fig. 4A; Additional file 1). The pie chart analysis of shared bacterial taxa showed that Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas, Cellulomonas, Curtobacterium, and others were the shared bacterial genera across phyllosphere compartments (Fig. 4B). However, the significant taxa remained unclassified, suggesting that a significant fraction of the shared microbiome remained taxonomically unresolved. Among these shared taxa across leaves, flowers and fruitlets, the bacterial genera Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas and unclassified genera belonging to the family Erwiniaceae were identified as core taxa in all compartments. In addition, there were several unique core bacterial genera across each mango phyllosphere organ. The unique core genera in the mango leaves were Bfidobacterium, Lactococcus, Lactiplantibacillus, Aeromonas, Roseomonas, Devosia, Actinoplanes, Paenarthrobacter, Abditibacterium, Priestia, Noviherbaspirillum, and Methylorubrum. Mango fruitlets were dominantly unique to the core taxa belonging to the genus Asaia, while there were no unique core taxa identified in the mango flower compartment (Figure S6).

In the fungal community, mango leaves also exhibited the highest number of fungal taxa (177) at the genus level (Fig. 4C; Additional file 1), followed by flowers (80) and fruitlets (47). The leaf was colonized by a higher number of unique fungal taxa (113) than the flowers (18) and fruitlets (9). The shared core fungal genera across the three phyllosphere compartments included Cladosporium, Vishniacozyma, Fusarium, Nectria, Hannaella, Alternaria, Epicoccum, Aureobasidium and Neodidymella (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, we detected the genera Trichomerium, Ordus, Candida, Phaeosphaeria, Cyphellophora, Catenulostroma, Dimorphiseta, and Pallidocercospora as unique core taxa in the mango leaves. The flowers were dominated by a number of unique fungal genera, but Gibellulopsis was identified as the only core genus. No unique core fungal genera were detected in mango fruitlets (Figure S7).

Ternary plot analysis revealed several significantly enriched bacterial (Fig. 5A) and fungal (Fig. 5B) ASVs. The majority of the enriched ASVs were clustered at the leaf vertex for both bacteria (13 ASVs; Table S1) and fungi (6 ASVs; Table S3). The significantly enriched bacterial ASVs in the leaves belonged to the genera Bifidobacterium (ASV17), Lactococcus (ASV31), Curtobacterium (ASV36), Pseudomonas (ASV20 and ASV99), Lactiplantibacillus (ASV102), Klenkia (ASV45), Bacillus (ASV19), Marmoricola (ASV73), Nocardioides (ASV130), Quadrisphaera (ASV208), Jatrophihabitans (ASV53) and Acinetobacter (ASV229). The enriched fungal ASVs were annotated to the genera Epicoccum (ASV37, ASV77 and ASV78), Symmetrospora (ASV73 and ASV149), and Duportella (ASV74) (Table S1-S3; Additional file 1). We did not observe any significantly enriched bacterial and fungal ASVs in the flowers when simultaneously compared with both the leaf and fruitlet compartments. However, the mango fruitlets showed few significantly enriched ASVs for bacteria (3 ASVs; Table S2) belonging to the genus Asaia (ASV178) and unclassified genera belonging to the order Enterobacterales (ASV185 and ASV410), but not for fungi, when fruitlets were compared with both leaves and flowers. The calculation of aggregated relative abundances of leaf-enriched bacterial ASVs showed decreasing contribution in mango flower and fruitlet (13.45% mean aggregated relative abundance in leaf, 0.02% in flower, 0.04% in fruitlet; brown box plots in Fig. 5C). The aggregated relative abundance of fruitlet-enriched bacterial ASVs were completely abolished in flower and showed very low contribution in leaf (10.76% mean aggregated relative abundance in fruitlet, 0% in flower, 0.03% in leaf; green box plots in Fig. 5C). Likewise, the leaf-enriched fungal ASVs also showed decreasing contribution pattern in both flower and fruitlet (6.91% mean aggregated relative abundance in leaf, 0.05% in flower, 0.15% in fruitlet; brown box plots in Fig. 5D).

Functional prediction profile of the mango phyllosphere microbiome

Functional annotation of prokaryotic taxa (FAPROTAX) revealed clear and biologically relevant differences in the abundance of different bacterial functions in mango leaves, flowers, and fruitlets. The dominant functional groups of the mango phyllosphere bacterial community were chemoheterotrophy, aerobic chemoheterotrophy, fermentation, aromatic compound degradation, ureolysis, nitrate reduction, animal parasite or symbionts, and manganese oxidation (Fig. 6; Additional file 1). These functions were consistently more active in the mango leaves than in the flowers and fruitlets. The mango leaf samples showed high abundance of chemoheterotrophy and aerobic chemoheterotrophy functions, driven by the enrichment of several bacterial genera, including Curtobacterium, Marmoricola, Nocardioides, Pseudomonas, and Sphingomonas (Fig. 6; Additional file 1). In addition, mango leaves were characterized by a significant enrichment of the fermentation-associated taxa Bifidobacterium and Lactococcus. The aromatic compound degradation function was also elevated, which was associated with the enrichment of Nocardioides and Acinetobacter. Ureolysis abundance was linked to Massilia and Roseomonas, whereas nitrate reduction was correlated with Aeromonas and Aquabacterium. Finally, manganese oxidation was associated with Geodermatophilus. Mango flowers generally displayed a low abundance of these functions, while fruitlets exhibited intermediate abundance for several functions; in particular, some of these functions were significantly more abundant in fruitlets than in flowers.

Predicted functional profiles of the bacterial community inhabiting the mango leaf, flower, and fruitlet compartments using FAPROTAX. Representative bacterial genera annotated to each function are listed in paranthesis

The fungal functional prediction inferred by FUNGuild revealed distinct patterns of functional group composition across mango leaves, flowers, and fruitlets. The functional group animal pathogen-endophyte-lichen parasite-plant pathogen-wood saprotroph dominated all phyllosphere compartments, but was more abundant in leaves than in flowers and fruitlets because of the higher relative abundance of the fungal genus Cladosporium in mango leaves. The second most abundant function, the fungal parasite-undefined saprotroph, was more prevalent in fruitlets, followed by flowers and leaves, corresponding to the higher relative abundance of Vishniacozyma, Hannaella, Cryptococcus, Papillotrema and Genolevuria. Plant pathogens-annotated functional group was more abundant in leaves, followed by flowers and fruitlets. Several fungal genera annotated as plant pathogens included Lasiodiplodia, Curvularia, Oidium, Strelitziana, Pseudocercospora, Allophoma, Exserohilum, Catenulostroma, Apiospora, Ramichloridium, Dothiorella and Tremateia (Fig. 7; Additional file 1). Meanwhile, the functional group associated with endophyte-plant pathogen-wood saprotroph, driven by the genera Pericornia and Torula, were also more abundant in leaves than in flowers or fruitlets.

Discussion

The structure and functions of the phyllosphere microbiome have been examined in detail in many agricultural crops [6, 19]. Several studies have postulated that members of the leaf microbiome play critical roles in plant health and productivity [20, 21]. Importantly, an increasing number of studies have begun to characterize the fruit microbiome to decipher biocontrol agents that may enhance disease resistance [9]. However, there is no clear understanding of the structure and assembly cues of phyllosphere bacterial and fungal communities during mango fruitlet formation. In this study, mango leaves, flowers, and newly formed fruitlets were sampled to gain insight into the structure and assembly of the phyllosphere microbiome. Our results revealed an organ-specific composition of the bacterial and fungal microbiota, with the coexistence of both beneficial and pathogenic microbial taxa.

The bacterial and fungal community diversity was significantly higher in mango leaves than in flowers and fruitlets. The leaf is a photosynthetically active organ with a heterogeneous and stable microenvironment that facilitates colonization by a range of bacterial and fungal microbiota [22]. However, the flowers are transient structures with nutrient-rich microenvironments and the production of a diverse array of antimicrobial compounds, limiting colonization by specialized microbial taxa [23]. The low bacterial and fungal diversity in both flowers and fruitlets compared to leaves also suggests host selective pressure, probably because of structural barriers that confine microbial establishment to a range of bacteria and fungi [23, 24]. Overall, our findings support the ecological theory that organ functionality strongly shapes microbial diversity, with leaves acting as a hotspot for microbial inhabitants, whereas reproductive organs enforce stricter microbiome filtering.

The observed significant difference in the beta-diversity of bacterial and fungal communities indicates host-mediated organ-specific microbiome structuring, reflecting mango leaves, flowers, and fruitlets as unique microbial niches. We observed that the bacterial phyla Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Bacillota and Bacteroidetes dominated the phyllosphere of mango trees. These phyla have been documented to dominate phyllosphere bacterial communities in seed plants [6]. The phylum Pseudomonadota was significantly more abundant in the flowers and fruitlets than in the leaves. The phyla Actinomycetota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidota were more abundant in leaves than in flowers and fruitlets. Pseudomonadota have previously been found to dominate mango flowers and fruits [16, 25]. Members of Pseudomonadota thrive in nutrient-rich environments and provide protective benefits against pathogens [26]. In contrast, the high abundance of Actinomycetota in mango leaves may be due to their ability to withstand stress, as members of this phylum are known to be involved in plant growth promotion, antimicrobial synthesis, and disease protection [27]. Members of Bacillota have a spore-forming capacity that could enhance their survival in the fluctuating leaf microenvironment [28]. The high abundance of Bacteroidota may reflect its role in degrading complex organic matter accumulated on leaf surfaces [29].

Our results revealed that members of the bacterial genera Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas, Cellulomonas and Curtobacterium were the main bacterial microbiota shared among leaves, flowers, and fruitlets. Importantly, Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas and an unclassified genus from the family Erwiniaceae were identified as the core bacterial taxa in mango. It was previously found in eudicot plant species that their phyllosphere microbiota are enriched by a few notable members belonging to the genera Sphingomonas, Methylobacterium and Pseudomonas [30, 31]. A global examination of the apple fruit microbiome also identified a subset of core bacterial microbiota composed of Sphingomonas and Methylobacterium [32]. Recent studies have deciphered the role of the phyllosphere inhabiting Sphingomonas spp. and Pseudomonas spp. in disease suppression [20, 26]. Members of Cellulomonas and Curtobacterium have been previously detected in the soybean phyllosphere [33]. Curtobacterium spp. have been shown to protect plants against disease and improve plant growth [34]. It has also been reported that Cellulomonas spp. produces polysaccharide lyase that degrades pathogenic Fusarium and Gibberella acidic polysaccharides, thus playing a biocontrol role in agriculture [35, 36].

For the core fungal taxa, members of the genera Cladosporium, Vishniacozyma, Fusarium, Nectria, Hannaella, Alternaria, Epicoccum and Aureobasidium were detected across the three mango organs. Abdelfattah et al. [32] discovered six fungal genera (Alternaria, Cladosporium, Aureobasidium, Filobasidium, Vishniacozyma, and Sporobolomyces) as the core taxa in a global analysis of the apple fruit microbiome. Most genera such as Alternaria, Cladosporium and Fusarium are known to cause diseases in mangoes [12, 37, 38]. The presence of pathogens within the fruit microbiota can cause pre- and postharvest diseases [32]. In addition, it has been observed that the abundance of Alternaria and Cladosporium increased as the flowers senesced and young fruits formed [39]. Moreover, the detection of a number of yeast genera as core microbiota of mangoes illustrates that diverse beneficial taxa with antagonistic traits are present in the phyllosphere that can protect mangoes against a range of fungal pathogens [40].

Notably, the numbers of bacterial and fungal ASVs were enriched in the ternary plots. Most bacterial and fungal taxa were strongly clustered at the leaf vertex. Only a few bacterial ASVs clustered at the fruitlet, and there were no bacterial or fungal ASVs at the flower vertex. The aggregated relative abundance of enriched ASVs demonstrates that the bacterial and fungal community assembly may occur in parallel rather than sequentially in mango aboveground parts. The aggregated relative abundance of the enriched ASVs underscores the strong influence of mango organ-specific factors on the assembly of the mango phyllosphere microbiome. Consistent with these findings, mango organs were identified as hotspots for several functional metabolic groups, such as carbon cycling (chemoheterotrophy, aerobic chemoheterotrophy), nitrogen cycling (nitrate reduction), fermentation, aromatic compound degradation, ureolysis, animal parasite or symbionts, and manganese oxidation. These functional groups were more abundant in the mango leaves than in the flowers and fruits. For instance, the high abundance of carbon cycling in the leaf compartment was related to the enrichment of Curtobacterium, Marmoricola, Nocardioides, Pseudomonas, and Sphingomonas. This indicates the involvement of these diverse phyllosphere microbial genera in the decomposition of organic matter, thereby contributing to nutrient recycling [41, 42]. Nitrate reduction suggests a dominant role for microbes inhabiting mango leaves in nitrogen transformation, potentially influencing nitrogen availability [43]. Ureolysis indicates urea metabolism, which is likely to be associated with the release of nitrogen. The relatively high abundance of Bifidobacterium and Lactococcus in mango leaves indicates that these bacterial species contributed to fermentation. It is well documented that the naturally occurring phyllosphere microbiota plays a crucial role in plant-derived fermentation [44]. Similarly, deciphering the functional potential of fungal communities revealed a high abundance of animal pathogen, endophytes, saprotrophs and plant pathogens across mango leaves, flowers, and fruitlets. Flowers are potentially exposed to animal visitors and microbial taxa annotated as animal pathogens could have a significant negative impact on pollinators as well as other flower visitors [45]. The high abundance of functional diversity, such as endophytes and plant pathogens, in the mango phyllosphere could significantly affect both plant health and ecosystem functioning. Future research should investigate how these microbial functions vary across cultivars and environmental conditions.

Conclusion

In summary, our investigation of the mango phyllosphere microbiome revealed a highly organ-specific composition of the bacterial and fungal communities. Mango leaves possess more diverse communities of bacterial and fungal microbiota than do flowers and fruitlets. Microbial communities in different organs have developed as a result of parallel rather than consecutive selection. Importantly, we identified a list of core potential pathogenic and beneficial microbiota and their functional network active in the mango phyllosphere. This detailed information is critical for the future utilization of bacterial and fungal microbiota inhabiting the phyllosphere to enhance mango performance and protect fruits from diseases, especially under the framework of precision and sustainable agroecosystems.

Materials and methods

Mango leaf, flower and fruitlet sampling

Mango leaves, flowers, and fruitlets were collected from the 2-year old mango tree variety ‘Guiqi’ in April 2024, grown inside Baise University, Youjiang District, Baise, China. The mango variety ‘Guiqi’ used in this study was derived from 10-year-old trees grown at Tiandong County, Baise City, Guangxi, China. ‘Guiqi’ is a late-maturing cultivar known for its high yield and commercial value. It was previously selected from the progeny of Indian mango variety No. 901 in 1994 in Guangxi, China. This study sampled leaves, flowers and fruitlets from two-year-old potted trees that were transplanted at the Baise University, and was obtained from a private farmer’s field in Tiandong County, Guangxi (GPS: 23°35′58.3″N, 107°7′38.2″E) by Dr. Xi Li at the Baise University, China. As ‘Guiqi’ is a cultivated, non-endangered variety widely distributed in China, no voucher specimen was deposited. Six independent trees were sampled for leaf, flower, and early fruitlet, indicating six replicates. From each plant, three leaves selected from the nearby panicle were cut from the terminal buds of the mature branches using ethanol-sterilized scissors and stored in sterilized plastic bags. Approximately 20 fruits were carefully removed from the flowers grown on branched inflorescences using an ethanol-sterilized twizzer and stored in sterilized plastic bags. Similarly, the flowers were manually separated from the three panicles and stored in sterilized plastic bags. Subsequently, the samples were immediately transported to the laboratory on dry ice and used for DNA extraction on the same day.

DNA extraction

Microbial DNA was extracted from the mango leaves, flowers, and fruitlets. Each organ sample was first placed into a 250-mL conical flask containing 100 mL of 0.01 M sterile phosphate-buffered saline and washed twice. Double-washed leaf, flower, and fruitlet samples were sonicated (frequency, 40 kHz; power, 150 W) in an ultrasonic cleaning bath for 3 min. The washed and sonicated leaves, flowers, and fruitlets were powdered using liquid nitrogen in a mortar and pestle. The resulting powder was subjected to DNA extraction using a FastDNA Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedical, Santa Ana, USA). DNA concentrations were measured using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Finally, the extracted DNA was stored at −80 °C prior to further analysis.

Amplicon sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The V5-V7 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using PCR primers 799 F (5’-AACMGGATTAGATACCCKG-3’) [46] and 1193R (5’-ACGTCATCCCCACCTTCC-3’) [47] and the ITS1 region of the fungal ITS locus was amplified with PCR primers ITS1F (5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3’) and ITS2R (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’) [48]. The PCR conditions for amplification of the desired regions were 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 amplification cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, with a final elongation at 72 °C for 10 min. After successful amplification of the targeted regions, the PCR products were purified, pooled, and sequenced on an Illumina PE250 platform (Majorbio, Shanghai, China). The raw FASTQ files were demultiplexed using a custom Perl script and then quality filtered with fastp (v0.19.6) and merged using FLASH (v1.2.7) with the following criteria: (i) the obtained data processed by truncated sequences at any position where a10 bp sliding window had an average quality score of < 20. We discarded any sequence that were shorter than 100 bp after trimming or that contained ambiguous nucleotides; (ii) subsequently, overlapping pair-end reads were merged using a minimum overlap of 10 bp and maximum mismatch ratio of 0.2 in the overlapping region. Read pairs that could not assembled were discarded; (iii) sequences were assigned to their respective samples based on their barcodes and primers, requiring an exact barcode match and allowing for up to 2 nucleotide mismatches in the primer sequence, with sequence orientation being adjusted accordingly. Following quality control, the sequences were de-noised using DADA2 algorithm within the QIIME2 (v2024) pipeline with recommended parameters, which obtained single nucleotide resolution based on error profiles within samples. DADA2 denoised sequences are usually called amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Because rigorous quality filtering step was applied upstream, the DADA2 parameters were adjusted to be lenient. The filterAndTrim function was run with the following settings: maxEE = 2, truncQ = 0, maxN = 0, minQ = 0. All representative reads were taxonomically identified against the SILVA 16 S rRNA database (v138.2) [49] and UNITE database (v9.0) [50] for bacteria and fungi, respectively, using the Naive bayes consensus taxonomy classifier. To minimize the impact of sequencing depth on subsequent downstream analysis, all the samples were rarefied to 47070 and 60175 sequences (based on a lowest number of sequences that a sample contained) per sample for bacteria and fungi, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the mango phyllosphere bacterial and fungal communities was performed using R software (v4.4.2) [51]. Any sequences annotated as plant mitochondria and chloroplasts were removed before downstream analysis. Alpha diversity including Shannon’s diversity index and Observed species analysis of bacterial and fungal communities were performed using R package ‘vegan’. The treatment mean values of different mango organs were compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and LSD tests. Beta diversity analysis based on the Bray-Curtis distance for bacterial and fungal communities was performed using ‰ ASVs relative abundance (RAs) log2 transformed using the R-Package ‘phyloseq’. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plots were constructed based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix. Permutational multivariate ANOVA was performed by ‘adonis’ function in R package ‘vegan’ to determine the significant differences in bacterial and fungal community composition across and within mango organs. The average RAs of bacterial and fungal phyla were calculated using ‰ ASVs relative abundances (RAs) log2 transformed and bar graphs were plotted by the R package ‘ggplot2’. ANOVA and LSD tests were used to observe significant differences in the treatment mean values of the different mango organs. Core bacterial and fungal taxa were selected based on ASVs assigned to the genus level and detected in at least 50% of samples in each phyllosphere compartment with a minimum relative abundance of 0.01% as describe previously [52]. The upset plot was generated by the R package ‘UpsetR’ to show the distribution of ASVs across mango organs and the pie chart was developed to indicate the abundance of core ASVs. Bacterial and fungal ASVs enriched in the mango leaf, flower and fruitlet compartments were visualized in ternary plot using the R package ‘limma’. ASVs that significantly differed in abundance between the three groups were calculated using a moderate t-test, and the P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction method. The functions of the bacterial community were predicted using functional annotation of prokaryotic taxa (FAPROTAX) [53], and the fungal community guilds were determined using the program FUNGuild [54].
