Resampling-based multi-resolution false discovery exceedance control
Jesse Hemerik

Abstract
MaxT is a highly popular resampling-based multiple testing procedure, which controls the familywise error rate (FWER) and is powerful under dependence. This paper generalizes maxT to what we term “multi-resolution” False Discovery eXceedance (FDX) control. FDX control means ensuring that the FDP — the proportion of false discoveries among all rejections — is at most γ \gamma with probability at least 1 − α 1-\alpha . Here γ \gamma and α \alpha are prespecified, small values between 0 and 1. If we take γ = 0 \gamma=0 , FDX control is the same as FWER control. When γ = 0 \gamma=0 , the new procedure reduces to maxT. For γ > 0 \gamma>0 , our method has much higher power. Our method is then strongly connected to maxT as well. For example, if our method rejects fewer than γ − 1 \gamma^{-1} hypotheses, then maxT rejects the same. Further, the implied global tests of the two methods are the same, although the implied closed testing procedures differ. Finally, our method provides an easy-to-use simultaneous, multi-resolution guarantee: the procedure outputs a single rejection threshold q q , but ensures that with probability 1 − α 1-\alpha , simultaneously over all stricter thresholds, the corresponding FDPs are also below γ \gamma .



1
Introduction

Prominent ways of adjusting for multiple testing are familywise error rate (FWER) and false discovery rate (FDR) control
(
Goeman and Solari 2014
;
Cui et al. 2021
)
. The FWER is the probability that there are one or more false positive findings. Controlling the FWER means ensuring that this probability is at most some prespecified value
\alpha\in(0,1)
. FDR control means ensuring that the expected value of the false discovery proportion (FDP) remains below some small value. Here, the FDP is the proportion of false positives among all rejected or selected hypotheses.

Apart from FWER and FDR control, a common multiple testing criterion is false discovery exceedance (FDX) control
(
van der Laan et al. 2004
;
Korn et al. 2004
;
Korn et al. 2007
;
Romano and Wolf 2005a
;
Lehmann and Romano 2005
;
Romano and Shaikh 2006b
;
Romano and Shaikh 2006a
;
Romano and Wolf 2007
;
Guo and Romano 2007
;
Farcomeni 2009
;
Roquain 2011
;
Guo et al. 2014
;
Sun et al. 2015
;
Delattre and Roquain 2015
;
Döhler and Roquain 2020
)
.
There are also methods that provide simultaneous bounds for FDPs; these methods can in principle also be used for FDX control, although they are not designed for it

(
Genovese and Wasserman 2006
;
Goeman and Solari 2011
;
Goeman et al. 2019
;
Hemerik et al. 2019
;
Blanchard et al. 2020
;
Katsevich and Ramdas 2020
;
Vesely et al. 2023
;
Durand 2025
)
.
FDX control means ensuring that the FDP is at most
\gamma\in[0,1)
with probability at least
1-\alpha
, with
\alpha
and
\gamma
prespecified. Since both
\alpha
and
\gamma
can be chosen, each FDX approach represents a broad range of possible methods. For example, note that when
\gamma=0
, one obtains FWER control. Further, when
\alpha=0.5
, one ensures that
\mathbb{P}(FDP\leq\gamma)\geq 0.5
, which means that the
median
of the FDP is kept below
\gamma
. This is the same as FDR control, except that the median instead of the mean is controlled. This illustrates that FDX methods are very versatile.

1.1
Open challenges

Developing FDX methods is challenging, especially when the tested variables are correlated, which can lead to large variability of the FDP.
Indeed, as
Delattre and Roquain 2015
note, when no assumptions on the dependence structure are made whatsoever, existing FDX methods “are in general too conservative”.

Lehmann and Romano 2005
and the more powerful method in
Romano and Shaikh 2006a
provide p-value-based FDX control, which is valid under mild assumptions on the dependence structure of the p-values. However, for many dependence structures, these method are rather conservative. The reason is that these approaches take into account worst-case scenarios for the dependence structure, instead of adjusting to the dependence structure.

It is known that resampling-based multiple testing methods can adapt to the dependence structure in the data and thereby achieve better power than competitors
(
Meinshausen 2006
;
Hemerik et al. 2019
;
Blain et al. 2022
;
Andreella et al. 2023
)
.
Such methods can be based on permutations or bootstrapping. Using permutations can lead to exact properties for finite samples, while bootstrap methods are asymptotic.
The most popular resampling-based multiple testing method is the maxT procedure
(
Westfall and Young 1993
;
Westfall and Troendle 2008
)
, which controls the FWER.
The idea behind this procedure is that the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the maximum of the test statistics corresponding to the true hypotheses, depends on the dependence structure of the test statistics. Using permutations or bootstrapping, the procedure constructs a rejection threshold that is adapted to this dependence structure.
When permutations are used, this method can be exact and has important optimality properties
(
Meinshausen et al. 2011
;
Goeman et al. 2021
)
.
Thus, if our goal is FWER control — i.e., FDX control with
\gamma=0
— then maxT is often a good choice.

The maxT method is very popular in e.g. neuroimaging
(
Nichols and Holmes 2002
;
Eklund et al. 2016
;
White et al. 2023
)
,
economics
(
Romano and Wolf 2005b
;
Romano et al. 2010
;
Romano and Wolf 2016
;
List et al. 2019
;
List et al. 2023
;
Batista and Vicente 2025
;
Deserranno et al. 2025
;
Dupas et al. 2025
)
and
omics research
(
Steiß et al. 2012
;
Terada et al. 2015
;
Rosyara et al. 2016
;
Power et al. 2016
;
Saffari et al. 2018
)
.
However, often researchers are willing to incur a few false positives if this leads to much more true discoveries. This means that it often worthwhile to take
\gamma>0
, i.e. to consider general FDX control. A method that allows for this is the procedure in
Korn et al. 2004
and
Romano and Wolf 2007
, also see
Clarke et al. 2020
. This method is often referred to as the Romano-Wolf procedure and is further studied in
Delattre and Roquain 2015
. The Romano-Wolf method is able to use resampling to construct a threshold and rejects all hypotheses with test statistics above this threshold. The threshold is adapted to the dependence structure and consequently the procedure is powerful.
Delattre and Roquain 2015
note that the Romano-Wolf method is valid when the p-values corresponding to the true hypotheses are independent from the p-values of the false hypotheses. The resampling-based version of the Romano-Wolf method is comparable to the method proposed in this paper, in the sense that they have similar power for FDX control (see Section
6
). However, the new method additionally provides simultaneous “multi-resolution” control, as discussed below.

The method of
Hemerik et al. 2019
can also be used for resampling-based FDX control, although it is not directly built for it. This procedure provides FDP bounds that are simultaneous over multiple rejection thresholds. By choosing the most lenient threshold for which the FDP bound is still below
\gamma
, we can obtain FDX control.
We will show that the novel method proposed in this paper is more suitable for FDX control in terms of power, user-friendliness and ease of reporting results. Finally, the permutation-based method of
Vesely et al. 2023
also provides simultaneous FDP bounds and could in theory also be used for FDX control. This method is comparable to
Hemerik et al. 2019
power-wise but has lower power when the signals in the data are strong but few. In that situation, however, the new method excells, just like maxT and Romano-Wolf.

1.2
Contributions of this paper

This paper presents a resampling-based FDX method that requires exactly the same input as the maxT method: a matrix of resampled test statistics. The output of the new method is a single real number
q
. Rejecting all hypotheses with test statistics exceeding
q
, guarantees FDX control. In fact, the method provides more than FDX control: it guarantees that with probability
1-\alpha
, for all
t\geq q
the FDP for threshold
t
is at most
\gamma
. Thus, the method provides simultaneous, “multi-resolution” control over a range of thresholds
t
.
This means that the method allows “zooming in”. For example, suppose that 42 test statistics exceed the threshold
q
, i.e., we can reject 42 hypotheses, while controlling the FDX rate. Then, after seeing the data, we can additionally decide to zoom in on e.g. the 20 hypotheses with the largest test statistics. If
\gamma=0.05
, then we know that among these 20 hypotheses, at most
5\%
, so one hypothesis, is true. In fact, we also know this about the top 39 hypotheses, since
5\%
of
39
is less than 2. Moreover, we know that among the top 19 hypotheses, none are true. All these statements will be simultaneously valid with probability at least
1-\alpha
.
(See Remark
3.1
for a related example.) Our term “multi-resolution inference” is on purpose similar to the term “all-resolutions inference” used in
Rosenblatt et al. 2018
.

The mentioned “zooming” property is related to the
subsetting property
discussed in
Goeman and Solari 2014
. They note that most multiple testing methods provide a claim on a set of hypotheses, without allowing for similar claims on subsets thereof. In other words, many existing methods do not allow for “drilling down”, as it is called in
Rosenblatt et al. 2018
and
Andreella et al. 2023
. The main issue with such multiple testing methods is that when many hypotheses are rejected, very little can be said about much smaller subsets of these hypotheses, even when zooming in on the hypotheses with the largest test statistics.
However, with the new method, after seeing the data, we can freely vary the threshold
t\geq q
. This allows drilling down on smaller sets of hypotheses, for which the FDP will also be at most
\gamma
. Moreover, with probability
1-\alpha
, these statements are simultaneously valid, so that we can consider any
t\geq q
post hoc or consider multiple thresholds
t\geq q
. While
Hemerik et al. 2019
also allows for powerful, resampling-based simultaneous FDP control, we will show that the new method is more powerful and user-friendly for such inference.

Like maxT, the new method does not depend on any tuning parameters. This is an additional advantage over
Hemerik et al. 2019
, which involves tuning parameters such as
\mathbb{B}
,
\mathbb{T}
and
s
. Further, it turns out that the new procedure is strongly connected to maxT in other ways. First of all, like maxT, the new method has a single-step and a sequential version. For
\gamma=0
, these coincide with the single-step and sequential versions of maxT. As we increase
\gamma
however, the power of the new method strongly increases (see Figure
1
several pages ahead). There are also other connections with maxT. In particular, if
\gamma>0
and the new method rejects fewer than
\gamma^{-1}
hypotheses, then with probability 1, maxT rejects exactly the same hypotheses. However, when maxT rejects at least
\gamma^{-1}
hypotheses, then the new method usually rejects many more (see Figure
2
several pages ahead).
Like maxT, the new procedure can be combined with permutation-type strategies and bootstrapping, the latter being asymptotically valid. This paper primarily focuses on finite-sample properties. Our procedure is implemented in the R package
rFDP
, available on CRAN.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
we define notation, formulate the assumptions and discuss the set
\mathcal{G}
of transformations. Section
3
defines the single-step variant of our method. The sequential variant is defined in Section
4.1
, with Section
4.2
defining a faster approximation of that method. Connections with maxT are discussed in Section
5
. On a first read, Sections
4
and
5
can be skipped, without missing the most important idea of the paper.
Sections
6
through
8
provide simulation results, analyses of two real datasets and the Discussion.

2
Setting and notation

Consider data
X
taking values in sample space
\mathcal{X}
, hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{1},...,\mathcal{H}_{m}
and corresponding test statistics
T_{1}(X),...,T_{m}(X)
, taking values in
\mathbb{R}
or a subset of
\mathbb{R}
. For example, each
T_{i}(X)
might be the absolute value of a t-statistic. Often we write
T_{i}
, which is understood to mean
T_{i}(X)
. Let

\mathcal{N}:=\{1\leq i\leq m:\mathcal{H}_{i}\text{ is true}\},

\mathcal{F}:=\{1\leq i\leq m:\mathcal{H}_{i}\text{ is false}\},

where we assume for convenience that
\mathcal{N}
is not empty and
m\geq 2
.

2.1
Invariance assumption

For finite-sample validity, we need an invariance assumption. We will also discuss asymptotic validity, which is possible under weaker assumptions.

Consider a set
\mathbb{G}
of transformations
g:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{X}
, for example a set of maps that permute, rotate or sign-flip data. We assume
\mathbb{G}
is a
group
with respect to composition of maps
(
Hoeffding 1952
;
Hemerik and Goeman 2018b
;
Dobriban and Yu 2025
)
. Instead of using the full group of e.g. permutations, it can be faster and sometimes even more powerful to use a cleverly chosen subgroup, which is of course also a group
(
Chung and Fraser 1958
;
Koning and Hemerik 2024
;
Koning 2024
)
.
Write
gX
instead of
g(X)
for short.
Consider the following assumptions, the second of which implies the first.

Assumption 1
.

The joint distribution of the test statistics
T_{i}(gX)
with
i\in\mathcal{N}
and
g\in\mathbb{G}
is invariant under all transformations
g\in\mathbb{G}
of
X
.

Assumption 2
.

Conditional on
(T_{i}(X):i\in\mathcal{F})
, Assumption
1
holds.

Throughout the paper we assume Assumption
2
holds, but it is worth remarking that in our simulation studies, we found no example where the weaker Assumption
1
was not sufficient. This suggests that either Assumption
2
is unnecessary or it may be substantially weakened. Further, we show in Section
B
that asymptotically we do not need Assumption
2
.
To our knowledge, there exists no powerful, finite-sample FDX method that makes no assumption regarding dependencies between the test statistics or p-values. For example, the stepdown FDX method by
Romano and Wolf 2007
is not generally valid; it is known to be valid when the p-values corresponding to
\mathcal{N}
are independent of those corresponding to
\mathcal{F}

(
Delattre and Roquain 2015
)
, or in an asymptotic setting as in Section
B
.

Assumption
1
is standard in the literature on finite-sample valid permutation-based multiple testing
(
Meinshausen 2006
;
Goeman and Solari 2010
;
Hemerik and Goeman 2018a
;
Hemerik et al. 2019
;
Blanchard et al. 2020
)
. In a simple case-control study, for this assumption to hold it is sufficient that the joint distribution of the variables corresponding to
\mathcal{N}
is the same for the cases as for the controls. Assumption
2
would be satisfied if this still holds conditional on the observations corresponding to
\mathcal{F}
.
If we only require asymptotic exactness, then Assumption
1
is not necessary for some resampling-based multiple testing methods
(
Pollard and van der Laan 2003
)
. In particular, there is much literature on bootstrap tests in combination with maxT
(
Pollard and van der Laan 2003
;
Romano and Wolf 2005b
;
Romano et al. 2010
;
Romano and Wolf 2016
;
List et al. 2019
;
List et al. 2023
)
. Instead of Assumption
1
, one then essentially needs that asymptotically, one can sample from the distribution of the test statistics
T_{i}(X)
with
i\in\mathcal{N}
. The new method is then also asymptotically valid.

For finite samples, permutation methods tend to be reasonably robust to violations of Assumption
1
.
Robustness of permutation-based multiple testing methods against violations of Assumption
1
is illustrated in
Kisoentewari 2022
.
They found in particular that when the tested variables are strongly dependent for the cases but not for the controls, then permutation-based multiple testing methods still tend to be reliable.
When there was strong heteroscedasticity, then that was potentially more problematic.
There are however other semiparametric approaches, based on e.g. sign-flipping, that may deal better with such situations, when combined with maxT or the new method
(
Davidson and Flachaire 2008
;
Winkler et al. 2014
;
Hemerik et al. 2020
;
De Santis et al. 2025b
)
. In particular,
De Santis et al. 2025a
illustrates how to combine sign-flipping of score contributions with maxT; this method could likewise be combined with the new method.
Finally, finite-sample and asymptotic robustness of single-hypothesis permutation tests is discussed in e.g.
Romano 1990
;
Canay et al. 2017
;
Kashlak 2022
.

2.2
Equivalence classes of transformations

Sometimes
\mathbb{G}
contains subsets of equivalent transformations, which always give the same test statistic. For example, in a case-control study, if we shuffle the controls among themselves, this usually does not change the test statistic
(see
Hemerik and Goeman 2018b
, p.815)
.
This relates to the following assumption, which is typically satisfied whenever the data are continuous.

Assumption 3
.

There exists a partition
\mathbb{G}_{1},...,\mathbb{G}_{d}
of
\mathbb{G}
with
|\mathbb{G}_{1}|=...=|\mathbb{G}_{d}|
such that with probability 1, for all
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}
and
1\leq i\leq m
,
T_{i}(gX)=T_{i}(g^{\prime}X)
if and only if
g
and
g^{\prime}
are in the same set
\mathbb{G}_{j}
. Further, with probability 1, for all
1\leq i,i^{\prime}\leq m
,
T_{i}(gX)\neq T_{i^{\prime}}(g^{\prime}X)
if
g
and
g^{\prime}
are in different sets
\mathbb{G}_{j}
.

The above assumption guarantees in particular that instead of using the full group
\mathbb{G}
, we can use one transformation from each subset
\mathbb{G}_{j}
: for all methods considered, this will be equivalent to using the full group
\mathbb{G}
but faster. Further, Assumption
3
excludes ties between test statistics corresponding to different hypotheses (cf. Remark
5.1
). This is usually satisfied when the data are continuous.
Let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathbb{G}
be such that it contains exactly one element from each of
\mathbb{G}_{1},...,\mathbb{G}_{d}
. Also, assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{G}
contains the identity map
id
.
Whether
|\mathcal{G}|<|\mathbb{G}|
, depends on the setting:
for two-group comparisons, typically we will have
|\mathcal{G}|<|\mathbb{G}|
,
(
Hemerik and Goeman 2018b
, p.815)
. In case of independence testing of two continuous variables, typically all permutations give different statistics, so that we have
|\mathbb{G}_{1}|=...=|\mathbb{G}_{d}|=1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{G}
. If using all transformations in
\mathcal{G}
is computationally infeasible, we could use random transformations as in e.g.
Hemerik and Goeman 2018a
.

2.3
Rejection sets and FDP

Given data
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the set of rejected hypotheses that we will consider is of the form

\mathcal{R}(t,x)=\{1\leq i\leq m:T_{i}(x)>t\},

where
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Here
t
is the
rejection threshold
.
Note that the rejection region is simply
(t,\infty)
for every test statistic.
This could straightforwardly be extended to general rejection regions
D_{i}(t)\subset\mathbb{R}
, where the region
D_{i}(t)
shrinks as
t
increases and
D_{i}(\cdot)
potentially depends on the hypothesis index
i
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
we let

R(t,x)=|\mathcal{R}(t,x)|,

V(t,x)=|\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{R}(t,x)|,

FDP(t,x)=V(t,x)/(R(t,x)\vee 1).

Note that
R(t,x)
is the number of rejections given data
x
. When we write e.g.
R(t)
or
FDP(t)
, this will be understood to mean
R(t,X)
.
V
is the usual notation for the number of false discoveries in the literature.
Note that the function
t\mapsto FDP(t,x)
is piecewise constant and right-continuous.
Finally, let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\gamma\in[0,1)
.

3
Single-step procedure

In this section, we define the
single-step procedure
. This method is uniformly improved by the sequential procedure in Section
4
.
The sequential procedure directly builds on the single-step procedure, which forms its first step.

In Section
3.1
we explain the single-step method and provide theory for it. This is arguably the most important part of the paper, since it contains fundamentally novel ideas.
Section
3.2
discusses an algorithm for the method.
While we initially define the method based on test statistics, it is also possible to use p-values as input. This is discussed in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Definition of the single-step procedure

The threshold
q
of the single-step method is defined as follows.
For every
g\in\mathcal{G}
, let

s_{g}=s_{g}(X):=\sup\Big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{R(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}>\gamma\Big\}.

(1)

This supremum is always a real number, which follows from the fact that for all small enough
t
, the fraction in (
1
) is
m/m=1
and for all large enough
t
, the fraction is
0/(0\vee 1)=0
.
Let
q=q(X)
be the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the values
s_{g}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
— more precisely,

q:=\min\big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{|\{g\in\mathcal{G}:s_{g}\leq t\}|}{|\mathcal{G}|}\geq 1-\alpha\big\}.

The following theorem — see Section
A
for proofs — states that if we use
q
as the rejection threshold, then we have FDX control.
The intuition behind this is the following. For
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
R(t,gX)
can be seen as a conservative estimate of the number of false positives
V(t,X)

(
Tusher et al. 2001
;
Hemerik and Goeman 2018a
, this is the intuition behind the SAM methodology, see)
.
Hence,

{R(t,gX)}/{R(t,X)\vee 1}
can be seen as a conservative estimate of
FDP(t,X)
. This means that
s_{g}
can be seen as an estimate of the largest threshold
t
for which
FDP(t,X)>\gamma
. Thus, beyond
s_{g}
, the FDP is estimated to be at most
\gamma
. Finally, to have
(1-\alpha)100\%
confidence, the method takes the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of the values
s_{g}
. The actual reasoning is more complex, but that is the basic intuition.

The theorem does not only guarantee FDX control, but also that with probability
1-\alpha
, simultaneously over all
t\geq q
, we have
FDP(t)\leq\gamma
. Since the FDP bounds that the procedure provides are all
\gamma
(roughly speaking), the method allows for relatively easy reporting of results: given
T_{1},...,T_{m}
, if we simply know
q
, then all FDP statements follow from it. When desired, these FDP statements about the sets
\mathcal{R}(t)
,
t\geq q
, can be expanded to
any
subsets of hypotheses using interpolation, as already covered in e.g.
Blanchard et al. 2020
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Our single-step method provides FDX control,

\mathbb{P}\big(FDP(q)\leq\gamma\big)\geq 1-\alpha.

(2)

In fact, we have the stronger result that

\mathbb{P}\big(\forall t\geq q:FDP(t)\leq\gamma\big)\geq 1-\alpha.

(3)

Remark 3.1
.

Theorem
3.1
implies that

\mathbb{P}\big(\bigcap_{t\geq q}\{V(t)\leq\gamma R(t)\}\big)\geq 1-\alpha.

In particular, if we pick
t^{\prime}\geq q
such that
R(t^{\prime})<\gamma^{-1}
, then
\mathbb{P}\{V(t^{\prime})=0\}\geq 1-\alpha
. Thus, simultaneously with the FDX control, we obtain FWER control. Because of the simultaneity, it will hold for instance that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big\{FDP(q)\leq\gamma\big\}\cap\big\{V(t^{\prime})=0\big\}\Big)\geq 1-\alpha.

As a specific example, suppose
\gamma=0.1
,
R(q)\geq 9
and there are no ties among the test statistics
T_{1}(X),...,T_{m}(X)
. We can then pick
t^{\prime}\geq q
such that
R(t^{\prime})=9<\gamma^{-1}
.
Then we know that with probability at least
1-\alpha
, we do not only have
FDP(q)\leq\gamma
but also
V(t^{\prime})=0
.

Remark 3.2
.

Our method adapts to the dependence structure in the data, in the following way. Suppose
\alpha<0.5
. In case there is no dependence in the data, then the values
s_{g}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
are expected to be relatively close to each other, which means that their quantile
q
will be relatively small. In case of strong dependencies in the data however, the values
s_{g}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
are expected to be relatively far apart. This means that the quantile
q
will tend to be relatively large, reflecting the large variability of the FDP. Thus, our method avoids accounting for worst-case scenarios for the dependence structure; instead, it adapts to the actual dependence structure. Several other resampling-based multiple testing approaches also adapt to the dependence structure, e.g.
Westfall and Young 1993
;
Korn et al. 2004
;
Romano and Wolf 2005b
;
Romano and Wolf 2007
;
Meinshausen and Bühlmann 2005
;
Meinshausen 2006
;
Hemerik and Goeman 2018a
;
Hemerik et al. 2019
;
List et al. 2019
;
List et al. 2023
.

3.2
Computing the threshold
q

The threshold
q
is a simple quantile. The underlying quantities
s_{g}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
, however are suprema.
To compute
s_{g}
, note that for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
the function
t\mapsto R(t,x)
is a right-continuous step function which is non-increasing in
t
. Consequently, the function

t\mapsto\frac{R(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}

(4)

is also a right-continuous step function.
For all small enough
t
it equals 1.
Note that this function has a discontinuity at
s_{g}
. More precisely, the function
t\mapsto R(t,gX)
jumps down at
s_{g}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{g}
be the set of all discontinuities of the function (
4
), i.e.,

\mathcal{M}_{g}:=\Big\{T_{i}(gX):1\leq i\leq m\Big\}\cup\Big\{T_{i}(X):1\leq i\leq m\Big\}.

Let

s_{g}^{-}=\max\{t\in\mathcal{M}_{g}:\frac{R(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}>\gamma\}

(5)

where the maximum of an empty set is
-\infty
. Note that with probability 1,
s_{g}^{-}
is strictly smaller than
\max(\mathcal{M}_{g})
, since for
t=\max(\mathcal{M}_{g})
, the fraction in (

) equals
0/(0\vee 1)=0
.
Right from

must equal this next element.
Thus, we have

s_{g}=\min\{t\in\mathcal{M}_{g}:t>s_{g}^{-}\}.

This formula is useful for computing

for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
, and hence
q
. This method is also shown in Algorithm

3.3
Equivalent formulation in terms of p-values

The input for our single-step method are the test statistics
T_{i}(gX)
with
1\leq i\leq m
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
.
Sometimes is may be preferred to use p-values instead of test statistics. The method can straightforwardly be reformulated in terms of p-values
P_{1}(X),...,P_{m}(X)
, which take values in
(0,1]
. We must then adjust the assumptions by replacing
T_{i}
by
P_{i}
in Assumptions
1
-
3
.
Further, we then consider thresholds
t\in(0,1]
and for
x\in\mathcal{X}
define

\mathcal{R}(t,x)=\{1\leq i\leq m:P_{i}(x)<t\}.

Note that since our approach is nonparametric, we do not need to assume that the p-values are valid in the sense that
\mathbb{P}(P_{i}\leq c)\leq c
for
c\in[0,1]
if
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is true.

Note that if we have p-values
P_{1}(X),...,P_{m}(X)
, we can translate them into test statistics by simply taking e.g.
-P_{1}(X),...,-P_{m}(X)
or
1-P_{1}(X),...,1-P_{m}(X)
. Then, our method from Section
3.1
can be used. Equivalently, we can directly use p-values, if we slightly adjust the method as follows.

When p-values are used, we can define
s_{g}
as before, except that it is now an infimum:

s_{g}=s_{g}(X):=\inf\Big\{t\in(0,1]:\frac{R(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}>\gamma\Big\}.

Further, we now define
q
to be the
\alpha
-quantile of the values
s_{g}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
— more precisely,

q:=\min\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{|\{g\in\mathcal{G}:s_{g}\geq t\}|}{|\mathcal{G}|}\geq 1-\alpha\}.

(6)

We reject all hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{i}
with
P_{i}(X)<q
.

For each
g\in\mathcal{G}
, to compute
s_{g}
in practice, we can proceed analogously to Section
3.2
, namely as follows.
Note that the function

(0,1]\ni t\mapsto\frac{R(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}

is left-continuous and stepwise constant. It has a discontinuity at
s_{g}
. More precisely, the function
t\mapsto R(t,gX)
, which is non-decreasing, jumps up at
s_{g}
.
Let

\mathcal{M}_{g}:=\Big\{P_{i}(gX):1\leq i\leq m\Big\}\cup\Big\{P_{i}(X):1\leq i\leq m\Big\}.

and

s_{g}^{-}=\min\{t\in\mathcal{M}_{g}:\frac{R(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}>\gamma\},

where the minimum of an empty set is
\infty
.
Note that with probability 1,
s_{g}^{-}
is strictly larger than
\min(\mathcal{M}_{g})
, since for
t=\min(\mathcal{M}_{g})
, the fraction in (
5
) equals
0/(0\vee 1)=0
.
Note that we then have

s_{g}=\max\{t\in\mathcal{M}_{g}:t<s_{g}^{-}\}.

We then compute
q
using formula (
6
).
This method is also shown in Algorithm
2
.
In the rest of this paper, we will consider test statistics rather than p-values, so the definitions from Section

4
Sequential method

For several multiple testing approaches, there exists a single-step version and a sequential version, e.g, Bonferroni
(
Holm 1979
)
. Other examples are the maxT method, its generalization to
k
-FWER control
(
Romano and Wolf 2007
, Algorithm 2.1)
and the method in
Hemerik et al. 2019
.
The resampling-based FDX method from
Korn et al. 2004
and
Romano and Wolf 2007
is always sequential. The method uses a
k
-FWER method in every step. When a sequential
k
-FWER procedure is used within every step, then that FDX method is thus doubly sequential.

Sequential versions of multiple testing methods are usually uniformly more powerful than the corresponding single-step versions.
In Section
4.1
we provide a sequential method that uniformly improves the single-step method of Theorem
3.1
. The sequential procedure produces a threshold
q_{\text{lim}}\leq q
, which is potentially strictly smaller than
q
, which means that it can lead to more rejections. We show that if we replace
q
by
q_{\text{lim}}
in Theorem
3.1
, then the theorem still holds. In Section
4.2
we provide a faster method that approximates the full sequential method.

4.1
Full sequential method

Before we formally state our result in Theorem
4.1
, we explain the idea behind the construction of
q_{\text{lim}}
.
Firstly, recall that our single-step method rejects all hypotheses with
T_{i}(X)>q
.
However, the proof of Theorem
3.1
also defines a potentially strictly smaller threshold
q^{\prime}\leq q
. That proof shows that if we substitute
q^{\prime}
for
q
in Theorem
3.1
, then the result still holds true. However, computing
q^{\prime}
requires knowledge of
\mathcal{N}
, so we cannot know it. However, we can potentially get closer to
q^{\prime}
.

Indeed, in the proof of Theorem
3.1
an event
\mathcal{E}
was defined which satisfies
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\alpha
.
We showed that under
\mathcal{E}
,
FDP(q)\leq\gamma
. This means that under
\mathcal{E}
, there are at least
B_{1}:=\lceil(1-\gamma)R(q)\rceil
false hypotheses among the hypotheses with indices in
\mathcal{R}(q)
. Thus, under
\mathcal{E}
, we can choose a set

\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,...,m\}
with
\mathcal{I}^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{R}(q)
and
|\mathcal{I}^{c}|=B_{1}
, such that
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. Here
\mathcal{I}^{c}
stands for
\{1,...,m\}\setminus\mathcal{I}
. We cannot pinpoint such a set
\mathcal{I}
but we know it exists, under
\mathcal{E}
.

For such a set
\mathcal{I}
, we know that for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{R}(t,gX)|\geq|\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{R}(t,gX)|=V(t,gX)

and hence

s_{g,1}^{\mathcal{I}}:=\sup\Big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{R}(t,gX)|}{R(t,X)\vee 1}>\gamma\Big\}\geq s_{g}^{\prime}:=\sup\Big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{V(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}>\gamma\Big\}.

Recall from the proof of Theorem
3.1
that
q^{\prime}
is defined as the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the values
s_{g}^{\prime}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
.
Hence, we know that

q^{\mathcal{I}}_{1}\geq q^{\prime}
, where
q^{\prime}
is the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the values
s_{g}^{\prime}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
.
We do not know the set
\mathcal{I}
, but we can instead use the maximum over all such sets
\mathcal{I}
, i.e., we use

q_{1}=\max\big\{q^{\mathcal{I}}_{1}:\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,...,m\},\mathcal{I}^{c}\in\mathcal{R}(q)\text{ and }|\mathcal{I}^{c}|=B_{1}\big\}.

This threshold can be computed and satisfies
q\geq q_{1}
.
It can be validly used within the single-step method, i.e., we may replace
q
by
q_{1}
in Theorem
3.1
.
Now we can repeat the above process, but starting from
q_{1}
instead of
q
. Continuing like this, we find a sequence
q\geq q_{1}\geq q_{2}\geq...
of thresholds that can be computed and validly used. This sequence converges after a finite number of steps and we take
q_{\text{lim}}
to be the limit. The following theorem states that this final threshold
q_{\text{lim}}
can indeed be used. A formal proof is provided in Section
A
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
q_{0}=q
. For
i\in\{1,2,...\}
, sequentially define
q_{i}
based on
q_{i-1}
as follows. Let

B_{i}=\lceil(1-\gamma)R(q_{i-1})\rceil
and write

\mathcal{K}_{i}=\big\{\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,...,m\}:\mathcal{I}^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{R}(q_{i-1})\text{ and }|\mathcal{I}^{c}|=B_{i}\big\}.

For
\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
let

s_{g,i}^{\mathcal{I}}=\sup\Big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{R}(t,gX)|}{R(t,X)\vee 1}>\gamma\Big\}

and denote by
q^{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the values
s_{g,i}^{\mathcal{I}}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Define

q_{i}=\max\big\{q^{\mathcal{I}}_{i}:\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{K}_{i}\big\}.

(7)

Then
q\geq q_{1}\geq q_{2}\geq...

and from some
i\in\mathbb{N}
we have
q_{i}=q_{i+1}=...
. Define
q_{\text{lim}}
to be this final value. Then Theorem
3.1
still holds if we replace
q
by
q_{\text{lim}}
.

Remark 4.1
.

In the sequential method, in every step
i
we need to compute
s_{g,i}^{\mathcal{I}}
for every
\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
.
To compute this supremum, we can proceed in a manner that is analogous to the computation of
s_{g}
in Section
3.2
. Namely,
let
\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\mathcal{I}}
be the set of all discontinuities of the maps
t\mapsto|\mathcal{I}\cap R(t,gX)|
and
t\mapsto R(t,X)
.
We first compute

s_{g,i}^{\mathcal{I}-}:=\max\{t\in\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\mathcal{I}}:\frac{|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{R}(t,gX)|}{R(t,X)\vee 1}>\gamma\}.

With probability 1, we have
s_{g,i}^{\mathcal{I}-}<\max(\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\mathcal{I}})
.
Then, we have
s_{g,i}^{\mathcal{I}}=\min\{t\in\mathcal{M}^{I}_{g}:t>s_{g,i}^{\mathcal{I}-}\}
. This approach will also be used in Algorithm
3
.

4.2
Approximation of the sequential method

In step
i=1
of the sequential method, we must compute compute
q_{1}
, which can be computationally intensive, since for
i\geq 1
,
q_{i}
is a maximum over all sets
\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
, where

|\mathcal{K}_{i}|=\binom{R(q_{0})}{B_{i}}=\binom{R(q_{0})}{\lceil(1-\gamma)R(q_{0})\rceil}.

Assuming one takes
0<\gamma\leq 0.5
, it may be computationally infeasible to perform this step when
\gamma R(q_{0})
is large. As a practical solution, rather than computing a maximum over all
\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
in step 1, we can compute the maximum over some randomly chosen subset of
\mathcal{K}_{1}
, say
\mathcal{S}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}_{1}}
.
This leads to an approximation
\hat{q}_{1}
, which is smaller than or equal to
q_{1}
.

In steps
2,3,...
we can proceed analogously. More precisely, take
\hat{q}_{0}=q
, let
i\geq 1
and suppose we have just
computed
\hat{q}_{i-1}
.
Then, we let
\hat{B}_{i}=\lceil(1-\gamma)R(\hat{q}_{i-1})\rceil
and let
\mathcal{S}_{i}
be a random subset of

\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{i}:=\big\{\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,...,m\}:\mathcal{I}^{c}\in\mathcal{R}(\hat{q}_{i-1})\text{ and }|\mathcal{I}^{c}|=\hat{B}_{i}\big\}.

More precisely, the set
\mathcal{S}_{i}
collects
M

\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{i}
, where
M
is some large user-specified number. The draws could be defined to be with or without replacement; in this paper we draw with replacement.
Then, for every
\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
, we compute

\hat{s}_{g,i}^{\mathcal{I}}=\sup\Big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{R}(t,gX)|}{R(t,X)\vee 1}>\gamma\Big\}

and denote by
\hat{q}^{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the values
\hat{s}_{g,i}^{\mathcal{I}}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Then, we take

\hat{q}_{i}:=\max\{\hat{q}^{\mathcal{I}}_{i}:\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{S}\}.

We compute
\hat{q}_{1},\hat{q}_{2},...
in this manner until we reach a step where the threshold no longer decreases. Alternatively, we could stop after a predetermined number of steps. In either case, we then define
\hat{q}_{\inf}
to be the last obtained threshold, which serves as our approximation of the threshold
q_{\inf}
from Section
4
.

This defines the
approximation method
, which is also given Algorithm
3
.
This algorithm uses all transformations
g\in\mathcal{G}
, but to further increase speed these may be replaced by a random sample from
\mathcal{G}
, as already mentioned in Section

5
Connections with the maxT method

If we take
\gamma=0
, then FDX methods guarantee that
\mathbb{P}(FDP>0)\leq\alpha
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(V>0)\leq\alpha
. This means that for
\gamma=0
, FDX methods control the FWER.
The arguably most popular resampling-based FWER controlling method is the
maxT method
(defined in Section
5.1
) by Westfall and Young
(
Westfall and Young 1993
;
Westfall and Troendle 2008
;
Meinshausen et al. 2011
;
Koning 2024
)
.

Our methods proposed in the previous sections, are closely connected
to maxT.
In particular, when
\gamma=0
, our single-step and sequential FDX method coincide with respectively the single-step and sequential version of maxT. Thus, our methods are a generalization of maxT, allowing the user to pick any
\gamma\in[0,1)
rather than only
\gamma=0
. The Romano-Wolf method also coincides with maxT for
\gamma=0
. The added advantage of the new method, is that the FDX control is simultaneous over all
t\geq q
.
These properties are useful in many scientific fields.
For example, in gene expression studies there are often many false hypotheses and users are willing to accept that some small percentage, e.g.
5\%
, of the rejected hypotheses are false findings. In these cases, our method allows taking
\gamma=0.05
and then provides much more power than the maxT method. This is illustrated in Figure
1
. In addition, we can freely increase the rejection threshold beyond
q
to zoom in on e.g. the top-100 or top-10 genes, obtaining simultaneously valid FDP statements.

:

Moreover, in Section
5.2
we show that if
\gamma\in(0,1)
and the maxT method rejects fewer than

Further, we show that for every
\gamma\in[0,1)
, with probability 1, our method rejects at least one hypothesis if and only the maxT methods reject at least one hypothesis. This means that the global test implied by our procedure is the same as that of maxT. In Appendix

.

The maxT method is the special case
\gamma=0

The well-known maxT procedure is defined as follows.
The single-step maxT method simply rejects all hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{i}
for which
T_{i}(X)>Q_{0}
, where
Q_{0}
denotes the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the maxima
\max_{1\leq i\leq m}T_{i}(gX)
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
. The sequential method then continues iteratively as follows. Let
\mathcal{R}^{0}
be set of indices of hypotheses rejected by the single-step maxT method, which we call step 0.
Next, we define

\mathcal{R}^{1}=\{1\leq i\leq m:T_{i}(X)>Q_{1}\}
, where
Q_{1}
denotes the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the maxima
\max_{i\in(\mathcal{R}^{0})^{c}}T_{i}(gX)
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
.
We continue like this, in the
j
-th step rejecting all hypotheses with indices in

\mathcal{R}^{j}=\{i\in\{1,...,m\}:T_{i}(X)>Q_{j}\},

where
Q_{j}
denotes the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the maxima
\max_{i\in{(\mathcal{R}^{j-1})}^{c}}T_{i}(gX)
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
.
We obtain a sequence
Q_{0}\geq Q_{1}\geq...
, which converges to
Q_{\text{lim}}

The sequential maxT method rejects all hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{i}
for which
T_{i}(X)>Q_{\text{lim}}
.

.

Suppose
\gamma=0
. Then
our

) rejects exactly the same hypotheses as the single-step maxT method. The same holds for our sequential FDX method (Section

) and the sequential maxT method.

5.2
Other connections with maxT

The following proposition states that if the new method rejects few hypotheses, then maxT rejects the same, and vice versa. This means that when there are few signals in the data, the new method roughly behaves like maxT.

Proposition 5.2
.

Consider any
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Suppose our single-step method (Section
3
) or single-step maxT rejects strictly fewer than
\gamma^{-1}
hypotheses. Then with probability 1, they reject exactly the same hypotheses; more precisely,
q=Q_{0}
.
The same holds for the sequential versions of our method (Section
4
) and the maxT method; we then have
q_{\text{lim}}=Q_{\text{lim}}
with probability 1.

That the new method rejects the same as maxT when one of the methods rejects fewer than
\gamma^{-1}
hypotheses, is illustrated in Figure
2
.
Further, with Proposition
5.2
in mind, it is interesting to have another look at Figure
1
.
For
\gamma=0
, the new method coincides with maxT.
The figure shows that increasing
\gamma
from 0.05 to 0.1 leads to a huge power gain. Changing
\gamma
from 0 to 0.05 however, leads to a comparatively small power gain. The reason is that when
\gamma=0.05
, we have
\gamma^{-1}=20
, which is usually larger than the number of hypotheses that maxT rejects, since maxT rejected about 15 hypotheses on average. By Proposition
5.2
, if maxT rejects fewer than
\gamma^{-1}
hypotheses, then our FDX method rejects exactly the same. Thus, for
\gamma=0.05
, the two methods often reject the same, which explains the comparatively small gain in power when increasing
\gamma
from 0 to 0.05.

Corollary 5.3
.

Consider any
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
If our single-step method (Section
3
) rejects at least one hypothesis, then with probability 1 so does
single-step maxT and vice versa. The same holds for the sequential versions of the methods.

Proof.

The sequential version of each method only rejects something if the single-step version does. Hence, it suffices to prove the result for the single-step versions, which we now consider.
Note that if one of the methods rejects nothing, then by Proposition
5.2
, with probability 1, the other method rejects nothing either. Hence, if one method rejects something, the other method does so too with probability 1.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

The statements in Proposition
5.2
and Corollary
5.3
hold with probability 1, i.e., almost surely, rather than surely. The reason is that these results rely on Assumption
3
, which implies that with probability 1 there are no ties among the test statistics. This assumption is typically satisfied for continuous data. When this assumption is not satisfied, it can happen that maxT rejects fewer than
\gamma^{-1}
hypotheses while the new method rejects more. Indeed, suppose that single-step maxT rejects fewer than
\gamma^{-1}
hypotheses, i.e.,

|\{1\leq i\leq m:T_{i}(X)>Q_{0}\}|<\gamma^{-1}.

(8)

For discrete data, it may happen that multiple statistics are exactly
Q_{0}
, so that while (
8
) holds, at the same we have that

|\{1\leq i\leq m:T_{i}(X)\geq Q_{0}\}|\geq\gamma^{-1}.

It is possible that there is an
\epsilon>0
such that
for at least
(1-\alpha)100\%
of the transformations
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

R(Q_{0}-\epsilon,gX)\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad R(Q_{0},gX)=0.

In that case, the fact that for all
t<Q_{0}
we have
R(t,X)\geq\gamma^{-1}
, implies that
for at least
(1-\alpha)|\mathcal{G}|
of the transformations, for all

,

\frac{R(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma^{-1}}=\gamma

(9)

and for at least
(1-\alpha)|\mathcal{G}|
of the transformations, for all
t\geq Q_{0}
,

\frac{R(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}=\frac{0}{R(t,X)\vee 1}=0.

(10)

Inequalities (
9
) and (
10
) imply that for at least
(1-\alpha)|\mathcal{G}|
of the transformations,
for all
t>Q_{0}-\epsilon
,

\frac{R(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}\leq\gamma
, which means that

q<Q_{0}
and the single-step FDX method rejects at least
\gamma^{-1}
hypotheses, while maxT rejects strictly fewer than
\gamma^{-1}
hypotheses. However, this will be rare in practice with discrete data.

6
Simulations

We performed simulations to compare the new method with the most relevant competitors, which are known to provide powerful FDX control: the method from
Romano and Shaikh 2006a
, which uniformly improves
Lehmann and Romano 2005
; the resampling-based method
Romano and Wolf 2007
, see also
Clarke et al. 2020
; and the resampling-based procedure from
Hemerik et al. 2019
.
We pick these methods because they are known to be more powerful than various other competitors, see e.g. the simulation results in
Hemerik et al. 2019
.
The last two methods owe their good power to the fact that they adapt to the dependence structure in the data, like our new method. Details are provided below. All methods provided valid FDX control in our simulation settings, and the new method provided valid simultaneous FDX control. Hence, below, we focus on power comparisons.

6.1
Simulation settings and competitors

We simulated two-group data with a total sample size of 20 and
m
variables observed per individual.
The data were a
20
-by-
m
matrix, with the first 10 rows representing the first group and the last 10 rows the second group.
The rows were independent of each other, and in each row there was a homogeneous correlation
\rho
between the variables. For other correlation structures, the relative performances of the methods were comparable to those shown in the figures below.
The observations were standard normally distributed, except that in the columns corresponding to the false hypotheses, we added a signal
d>0
to the observations for the first group. Note that normality is not at all required for our method to be exact and is just used as an example.
The null hypotheses were
\mathcal{H}_{1},...,\mathcal{H}_{m}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is the hypothesis that the observations in column
i
are identically distribited. The corresponding test statistics
T_{1},...,T_{m}
were absolute values of two-sample t-statistics. We took
\alpha=\gamma=0.1
.
Besides
\rho
, we varied
\pi_{0}
, which is the number of true hypotheses divided by
m
.

The first competitor is the FDX method of
Romano and Shaikh 2006a
. This method takes a vector of p-values as input and is valid under mild assumptions on their dependence structure. The p-values were based on two-sided t-tests. The Romano-Shaikh method is related to
Lehmann and Romano 2005
; to implement Romano-Shaikh, we adjusted code from the R package
FDX

(
Dohler et al. 2024
)
, which implements Lehmann-Romano.

The second competitor is the resampling-based stepdown method from
Romano and Wolf 2007
, also see
Clarke et al. 2020
. The k-FWER method used within the Romano-Wolf procedure was the usual k-FWER generalization of the maxT method
(
Romano and Shaikh 2006a
)
. Like our method, this procedure is based on permutations or other transformations of the data, with the goal of obtaining good power. In this case, permutations of the group labels were used.
For computational feasibility, we sampled 50 random permutations in each simulation. Using more permutations would increase computation times and would barely improve power in our experience
(
Marriott 1979
, also see e.g.)
.

The last competitor is the powerful approach in
Hemerik et al. 2019
, which also uses resampling to adapt to the dependence structure. Within this method we also used 50 random permutations.
The procedure provides simultaneous confidence bounds for the FDP for a range of p-value thresholds
t\in\mathbb{T}\subseteq[0,1]
. We used this method for FDX control by choosing the largest
t\in\mathbb{T}
for which the bound for the FDP is at most
\gamma.
The method depends on multiple prespecified tuning parameters, namely
\mathbb{T}
and the set
\mathbb{B}
of so-called candidate envelopes, which is in fact an infinite-dimensional parameter. Here, we chose the tuning parameters in such a way that this method performed well for FDX control in our simulation settings. We did this to give this competitor a fair change against our novel method. Specifically, we took
\mathbb{T}=[0,0.05]
and took
\mathbb{B}
to be the set of envelopes defined in the first paragraph of Section 2.6 of
Hemerik et al. 2019
, with
\delta=0.001
. We observed that making
\delta
much larger or smaller, decreased the power of that method. Moreover, other choices for
\mathbb{T}
did not improve its power.

7
Real data analyses

7.1
Data analysis 1: aggregated music review scores

As a simple illustration of the new method, we applied it to a dataset containing aggregated music review scores
(
Lucas 2021
)
.
The dataset contains aggregated album review scores from the websites
Album of the Year
and
Metacritic
.
We only used the data collected from Album of the Year. There are critic scores and user scores; for this illustration, we analysed the user scores. The scores were integers between 0 and 100, so that they are essentially continuous.
The most recent albums in the data are from the year 2020.
We only selected albums (including EPs) that were released in 1980 or later. We further reduced the data by only selecting artists with at least 10 albums since 1980. This resulted in a dataset containing information on 260 artists, including many prominent artists from the most popular genres, such as pop (Mariah Carey, Robbie Williams), rock (AC/DC, Pearl jam) and hip-hop (Eminem, Snoop Dogg). For every artist, we only used the data on the first 10 albums since 1980.

Roughly speaking, we are interested in whether scores of consecutive albums by an artist are simply i.i.d. — or at least exchangeable — or whether that is not the case, perhaps due to some trend. We will assume throughout that observations from different artists are independent of each other.
As a first simple analysis, for each of the 260 artists, we computed the Pearson correlation of the album scores with their corresponding release years. We call these correlations
T_{1},...,T_{260}
.
The mean of these 260 correlations was
-0.26.
Heuristically, this suggests that there is an overall negative trend.
To test whether
T_{1},...,T_{260}
all have mean 0,
we applied a two-sided one-sample t-test to these 260 values, which gave a p-value below
10^{-15}
.

7.1.1
Familywise error rate control

Whether
\mathbb{E}(T_{i})
has a negative expectation, might depend on the artist. For example,
\mathbb{E}(T_{i})
might be influenced by the genre or home country of the artist.
Hence, we may ask for each artist separately whether their test statistic has mean 0.
More precisely, we consider hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{1},...,\mathcal{H}_{260}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is the null hypotheses that
\mathbb{E}(T_{i})=0
,
1\leq i\leq m
.
One possible multiple testing approach that controls the familywise error rate, is to perform individual permutation tests and then apply Bonferroni-Holm to the resulting p-values.
Instead we used the maxT method, based on the absolute correlations
|T_{1}|,...,|T_{m}|
.
The reason is that we expect maxT to have better power than Bonferroni-Holm, as discussed below. We also provide results for Bonferroni-Holm for comparison.

For maxT to have any power, it suffices to use
\lceil\alpha^{-1}\rceil
permutations. However, to enhance stability, we used
10^{4}
permutations.
We randomly permuted the scores
10^{4}
times — including the original order — each time applying the same permutation to every artist. There are
|G|=10!=3628800
possible permutations; the reason for using random permutations was of course the reduction in computation time. The maxT method is valid if Assumption
1
holds. This property immediately follows here, from the assumption of independence between artists in combination with the definition of our null hypotheses.
MaxT likely has somewhat better power than Bonferroni-Holm. Indeed, even if the artists are independent, there may still be trends present — indeed, this is what we wish to test. For example, the first albums of artists may score high on average. When such patterns are present, maxT tends to have better power than Bonferroni-Holm.

The sequential maxT method rejected 3 hypotheses for
\alpha=0.05
and 8 hypotheses for
\alpha=0.1
. (Bonferroni-Holm based on permutaton tests rejected only 1 hypothesis for
\alpha=0.05
and 3 for
\alpha=0.1
.)
As an illustration, we consider
\alpha=0.1
. The sequential maxT method rejected the hypotheses corresponding to the artists Pet Shop Boys, Pearl Jam, Korn, Pixies, Van Morrison, Sting, The Mountain Goats and The Flaming Lips. Six of these artists had a strongly negative test statistic, indicating a clear decline in review scores. Two of these artists, namely the last two mentioned, had a strongly positive test statistic.

7.1.2
FDX control

Finally, we applied the simultaneous FDX method from Section
3
. Note that the (theoretically) required Assumption
2
is satisfied here, if the artists are independent of each other.
The method rejected 8 hypotheses for
\gamma=0.1
, 22 hypotheses for
\gamma=0.2
, 48 for
\gamma=0.3
and 95 for
\gamma=0.5
. For example, in case we had taken
\gamma=0.5
, we would know that with
90\%
confidence, at least
50\%
of those 95 hypotheses are false.
Note that the new method allows “zooming in”. For example, suppose we had chosen
\gamma=0.2
; then we do not only know with
90\%
confidence that among the top 22 hypotheses, at most
\gamma 100\%=20\%
are true. Indeed, simultaneously, we know that among the top 9 hypotheses, at most
20\%
, so 1 out of 10, are true. Moreover, we simultaneously know that among the top 4 hypotheses, at most
20\%
are true, which means that all 4 are false. With
90\%
confidence, all those statements are simultaneously true, assuming we chose
\gamma=0.2
beforehand.

Among the 95 hypotheses that we reject for
\gamma=0.5
,
81 had a strongly negative test statistic, the other 14 a strongly positive statistic.
All in all, the results suggest that not all artists have i.i.d. album scores. Heuristically speaking,
negative trends seem more common than positive trends, although both seem to exist. There are probably multiple reasons for the overall negative trend.
One reason might be a selection bias: an artist who has a longstanding career, probaby had early albums that were appreciated by the public, encouraging the artist and their music label to continue releasing music. Another reason might be that reviewers tend to rate older albums higher.

7.2
Data analysis 2: riboflavin production

We re-analysed a dataset about riboflavin (vitamin B2) production with
B. subtilis
. This dataset
is freely available
(
Bühlmann et al. 2014
)
. It contains normalized measurements
of expression rates of 4088 genes from
n=71
samples.
Further, the dataset contains the corresponding 71 measurements of the logarithm of the riboflavin production
rate.
For each
1\leq i\leq 4088
, we are interested in the hypothesis
H_{i}
that the riboflavin production
rate was independent
of the expression level of gene
i
.
We took
\alpha=0.05
. Within every method, we used
10^{3}
random permutations of the production rates.
For every gene and permutation, we computed the test statistic as the absolute value of the Pearson correlation between that gene’s expression rates and the permuted riboflavin production
rates.

For the maxT method to be valid, we need Assumption
1
to hold. For discussions on why methods based on this assumption are reasonable in practice, see
Kisoentewari 2022
,
Hemerik et al. 2019
and Section
2.1
of this paper.
First we applied the single-step maxT method, which rejected 74 hypotheses. The sequential maxT method led to the same rejections.

For our FDX method to be theoretically valid, we need Assumption
2
to hold. Although this conditional invariance assumption may not hold, in our simulations studies we found no setting where this assumption was necessary, as mentioned in Section
6
; further, the assumption is not necessary asymptotically, see Section
B
.
We took
\gamma=0.1
and applied our simultaneous FDX method from Section

, which rejected 186 hypotheses. This means that with
90\%
confidence, we know that among the 186 hypotheses with the largest test statistics, at least
(1-\gamma)100\%=90\%
, so 168, are false. Simultaneously, we know that among the 19 hypotheses with the largest test statistics, at least

.

8
Discussion

From a user’s perspective, an important advantage of the new method is that is does not require choosing tuning parameters except
\alpha
and
\gamma
, unlike e.g.
Hemerik et al. 2019
. Further, the output of the new method is simply a single number. Despite this simple output, the procedure provides simultaneous confidence guarantees on the FDP. In particular, if the user “zooms in” far enough, they will obtain FWER-type statements, simultaneously with the other FDP statements.

The new method rejects at least one hypotheses if and only if maxT does. The same is true about Romano-Wolf. This represents a downside of these methods compared to e.g.
Rosenblatt et al. 2018
,

Hemerik et al. 2019
and
Blanchard et al. 2020
, which may still provide some weak FDP guarantees when maxT rejects nothing. However, such methods are less powerful for FDX control, i.e., for controlling
\mathbb{P}(FDP\geq\gamma)
for fixed
\gamma
.

An advantage of resampling-based multiple testing method like maxT,
Romano and Wolf 2007
and
Hemerik et al. 2019
is that they adapt to the unknown dependence structure in the data, unlike other FDP methods such as
Romano and Shaikh 2006a
and
Goeman et al. 2019
.
An further advantage of resampling-based multiple testing methods is that we do not need to assume that the tested variables have some specific distributional form, e.g. normal. Further, by using e.g. sign-flipping approaches, we can obtain robustness to variance misspecification. For example, in RNA-Seq data analyses, it is common to assume negative-binomial models with constant overdispersion parameter. However, the assumption of contant overdispersion is often not realistic.
De Santis et al. 2025a
propose a heteroscedasticity-robust approach based on sign-flipping score contributions combined with maxT.
That approach can also be combined with the method proposed here, which requires the same input as maxT and is valid under similar assumptions.

In the last decades, many resampling-based testing strategies been developed, such as bootstrap testing, wild bootstrap and permutation and rotation approaches. Methods like maxT, Romano-Wolf and the new procedure are compatible with many such approaches.
This often begs the question what resampling strategy to use in which situation.
When it is known that Assumption
1
is exactly satisfied, then finite-sample exact permutation-type strategies are often most suitable. However, when that assumption is not satisfied, then a bootstrap
(
Pollard and van der Laan 2003
;
List et al. 2019
;
List et al. 2023
)
or score-flipping approach
(
De Santis et al. 2025b
)
may be more suitable. Future research should keep addressing which method is best in which case.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Proof.

We start with an overview of the proof.

Overview of the proof.

The main strategy of the proof is to define two additional data-dependent thresholds
q^{*}=q^{*}(X)
and
q^{\prime}=q^{\prime}(X)
. Computing these thresholds requires knowledge of
\mathcal{N}
, so they cannot be used in practice.
We show that under an event
\mathcal{E}
with
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\alpha
, we have

\forall t\geq q^{*}:FDP(t)\leq\gamma.

(11)

Further, we show that

q^{*}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\leq}\limits^{\mathcal{E}}}}\,q^{\prime}\leq q,

(12)

where “
\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\leq}\limits^{\mathcal{E}}}}\,
” means that “
\leq
” holds under
\mathcal{E}
.
Thus,
\mathcal{E}
does not only imply (
11
) but also that
q^{*}\leq q
.
It follows that under
\mathcal{E}
,

\forall t\geq q:FDP(t)\leq\gamma.

Since
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\alpha
, this implies the theorem.

In step 1 we will define
q^{\prime}
and note that
q^{\prime}\leq q
. In step 2 we define
q^{*}
and
\mathcal{E}
and show that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\alpha
. In step 3, we show that (
12
) holds, which implies the theorem. Step 4 is essentially a lemma used in step 3.

Step 1.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
define

R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X)=|\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{R}(t,X)|.

For every
g\in\mathcal{G}
, let

s_{g}^{\prime}(X)=\sup\Big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{V(t,gX)}{R(t,X)\vee 1}>\gamma\Big\}=

\sup\Big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{V(t,gX)}{(V(t,X)+R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X))\vee 1}>\gamma\Big\}=

\sup\Big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:V(t,gX)-\gamma V(t,X)>\gamma R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X)\Big\},

where the supremum of an empty set is
-\infty
.
Let
q^{\prime}=q^{\prime}(X)
be the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the values
s_{g}^{\prime}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
.
Note that
q^{\prime}\leq q
, since for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
s_{g}^{\prime}\leq s_{g}
. In the remainder of the proof, we will show that the threshold
q^{\prime}
is valid in the sense that Theorem
3.1
holds if we replace
q
by
q^{\prime}
there. This immediately implies that the threshold
q
is also valid, which will finish the proof.

Step 2.

For every
g\in\mathcal{G}
, let

s^{*}_{g}(X)=\sup\Big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{V(t,gX)}{(V(t,gX)+R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X))\vee 1}>\gamma\Big\}=

\sup\Big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:V(t,gX)-\gamma V(t,gX)>\gamma R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X)\Big\}.

Note the subtle difference compared to the definition of
s_{g}^{\prime}(X)
.
Let
q^{*}=q^{*}(X)
be the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the values
s^{*}_{g}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
.

Note that due to right-continuity of
V(\cdot,gX)
, for at least
(1-\alpha)100\%
of the transformations
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we have that

\forall t\geq q^{*}(X):V(t,gX)-\gamma V(t,gX)\leq\gamma R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X).

Define the event

\mathcal{E}=\Big\{\forall t\geq q^{*}:V(t,X)-\gamma V(t,X)\leq\gamma R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X)\Big\}=\Big\{\forall t\geq q^{*}:FDP(t,X)\leq\gamma\Big\}.

Note here that
R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X)
is function of
X_{\mathcal{F}}
and the values
V(\cdot,gX)
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
, are functions of
X_{\mathcal{N}}
.

Conditional on
(T_{i}(X):i\in\mathcal{F})
,
(s^{*}_{g}:\text{ }g\in\mathcal{G})
is a function of the test statistics corresponding to
\mathcal{N}
and is hence
invariant under all transformations
g\in\mathcal{G}
, by Assumption
1
.
Hence, conditional on
(T_{i}(X):i\in\mathcal{F})
, by the group invariance principle
(
Hemerik and Goeman 2018b
;
Hemerik and Goeman 2018a
)
,

\mathbb{P}(s^{*}_{id}\leq q^{*})\geq 1-\alpha.

Note that

s^{*}_{id}\leq q^{*}\Longleftrightarrow\Big\{\forall t\geq q^{*}:FDP(t,X)\leq\gamma\Big\}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{E}.

This means that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\alpha.

This is true conditionally, hence also marginally.

Thus, if we use
q^{*}
as the threshold, then we have FDX control.
However, we do not know
q^{*}
in practice. We will show though that if
\mathcal{E}
holds, then
q^{\prime}\geq q^{*}
and hence
FDP(t,X)\leq\gamma
for all
t\geq q^{\prime}
. This is the aim of the remainder of the proof.

Step 3.

Suppose
\mathcal{E}
holds. Consider any
g\in\mathcal{G}
for which
s^{\prime}_{g}<q^{*}
.
In Step 4 below, we will show that under
\mathcal{E}
it then holds that
s_{g}^{*}<q^{*}
with probability 1. Taking that for granted now, suppose
q^{\prime}<q^{*}
.
Then

(1-\alpha)d\leq|\{g\in\mathcal{G}:s_{g}^{\prime}\leq q^{\prime}\}|\leq|\{g\in\mathcal{G}:s_{g}^{\prime}<q^{*}\}|\leq|\{g\in\mathcal{G}:s_{g}^{*}<q^{*}\}|,

which is a contradiction with the fact that
|\{g\in\mathcal{G}:s_{g}^{*}<q^{*}\}|<(1-\alpha)d

q^{\prime}\geq q^{*}
under
\mathcal{E}
as we wanted to show.

Thus, what remains to show is that under
\mathcal{E}
,
if

is such that

, then
s_{g}^{*}<q^{*}
with probability 1. For the rest of the proof, suppose

Step 4: Showing that under

We will show that

First consider the case that the inequality is strict, i.e.,
s^{*}_{g}>q^{*}
.
Then

V(t,gX)-\gamma V(t,gX)>\gamma R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X).

(13)

Consider any such

Since we assumed

V(t,X)-\gamma V(t,X)\leq\gamma R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X)<V(t,gX)-\gamma V(t,gX)

V(t,gX)-\gamma V(t,X)\geq V(t,gX)-\gamma V(t,gX).

(14)

By inequalities (
13
) and (
14

V(t,gX)-\gamma V(t,X)>\gamma R_{\mathcal{F}}(t,X)

s_{g}^{\prime}>t\geq q^{*}

Finally, consider the case that

. Note that if

, so that
s_{g}^{\prime}\geq q^{*}
. Now suppose

.
That means that at

,

makes a jump downwards.
Thus, for all sufficiently small

V(q^{*}-\epsilon,gX)-\gamma V(q^{*}-\epsilon,gX)>\gamma R_{\mathcal{F}}(q^{*}-\epsilon,X).

Due to Assumption

,
with probability 1, in a neigbourhood of

\mathcal{E}
, with probability 1, for all sufficiently small

we have

V(q^{*}-\epsilon,X)-\gamma V(q^{*}-\epsilon,X)\leq\gamma R_{\mathcal{F}}(q^{*}-\epsilon,X).

(16)

Inequalities (
15
) and (

Appendix C
Closed testing and monotonicity

Here we discuss the new methods in the context of
Goeman et al. 2021
, which provides general theory on multiple testing procedures that make confidence statements on false discovery proportions.
The present paper’s Corollary
5.3
states that the new method and maxT essentially imply the same global test. This is interesting, because in the sense of Theorem 1 of
Goeman et al. 2021
, this would imply that the closed testing procedure corresponding to the new method is the same as the closed testing procedure corresponding to maxT — which is in fact the sequential maxT procedure itself. Indeed, Theorem 1 of
Goeman et al. 2021
states that if a procedure satisfies the so-called
monotonicity
assumption
(
Goeman et al. 2021
, p.1225)
, then the global test of the procedure implies a corresponding closed testing procedure with certain good properties. However, while most multiple testing methods have the monotonicity property, this is not the case for all FDX methods. In particular, the FDX methods in
Korn et al. 2004
and
Romano and Wolf 2007
, as well as the new method, are not generally monotone. For the new method, we illustrate this with a small example below.
Thus, Theorem 1 of
Goeman et al. 2021
does apply to the new method.
In particular, it can easily be seen that the new method provides FDP statements that are not implied by the sequential maxT method. Here, it is important to note that maxT is a
consonant

(
Goeman et al. 2021
, p.1228)
multiple testing method, which means that it cannot be expanded to make FDP statements except those already implied by its FWER rejections.

We now provide the counterexample which illustrates that the new method is not monotone.

Example 1

(New method is not generally monotone)
.

For the definition of monotonicity, we refer to
Goeman et al. 2021
.
To prove that the new method is not generally monotone, it suffices to give a counterexample where adding a single hypothesis to the problem decreases some of the FDP bounds for the original hypotheses considered.

As a minimal example, consider 6 hypotheses and suppose that for every hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{i}
we have a single observation
X_{i}
. We use sign-flipping, so
\mathcal{G}=\{id,g\}
has cardinality 2 and the data are
X=(X_{1},...X_{6})
. The observation itself serves as the test statistic, i.e.
T_{i}=id
, so that we we always have
X_{i}=T_{i}(X)=T_{i}(idX)=-T_{i}(gX).

Take
\alpha=\gamma=0.5
.
Suppose
0<X_{1}<X_{2}<X_{3}<X_{4}<T_{5}(gX)=-X_{5}<X_{6}
.
Then
q=\min\{s_{id},s_{g}\}=s_{g}=T_{5}(gX)
, so
\mathcal{R}=\{6\}
, i.e., we only reject one hypothesis. Hence, for example, among
\{1,2,3,4,6\}
, we know that at least one hypothesis is false.

Now we add a 7th hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{7}
with
X_{7}>X_{6}
.
Then
q=s_{g}\leq 0
, so
\mathcal{R}=\{1,2,3,4,6,7\}
i.e., we reject six hypotheses — all except 5. With
(1-\alpha)100\%
confidence, we know that at least
(1-\gamma)\cdot 6=3
of these hypotheses are false. Hence, the method tells us that among
\{1,2,3,4,6\}
, at least two are false. We see that adding the extra hypthesis to the problem, allowed us to say more about the original six hypotheses.
