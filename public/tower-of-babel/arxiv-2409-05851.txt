QCD-sourced tachyonic phase transition in a supercooled Universe
Daniel Schmitt, Laura Sagunski

Abstract
We propose a novel gravitational wave production mechanism in the context of quasi-conformal Standard Model extensions, which provide a way to dynamically generate the electroweak scale. In these models, the cosmic thermal history is modified by a substantial period of thermal inflation, potentially supercooling the Universe below the QCD scale. The exit from supercooling is typically realized through a strong, first-order phase transition. By employing the classically conformal U ​ ( 1 ) B-L U(1)_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}} model as a representative example, we show that a large parameter space exists where bubble percolation is inefficient. In this case, the top quark condensate triggers a tachyonic phase transition driven by classical rolling of the new scalar field towards the true vacuum. As the field crosses a region where its effective mass is negative, long-wavelength scalar field fluctuations are exponentially amplified, preheating the supercooled Universe. We study the dynamics of this scenario and estimate the peak of the associated gravitational wave signal, which is detectable by future observatories in almost the entire available parameter space.

III  QCD-induced tachyonic instability

Effective potential.

Let us now introduce the thermal effective potential
V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T)
. The tree-level potential (
1
) in the
\varphi
-direction reads

V_{\mbox{\scriptsize tree}}(\varphi)=\frac{\lambda_{\varphi}}{4}\varphi^{4}-\frac{\lambda_{p}}{4}v_{h,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}}^{2}\varphi^{2}\,,

(7)

where the QCD term emerges after
\chi\mathrm{SB}
at
T\simeq 85\,\mathrm{MeV}
.
Radiative (vacuum) corrections at one-loop order are incorporated as

V_{\mbox{\scriptsize cw}}(\varphi,T)=\sum_{i}\frac{n_{i}}{64\pi^{2}}M_{i}(\varphi)^{4}\left[\log\left(\frac{M_{i}(\varphi)^{2}}{\mu(\varphi,T)^{2}}\right)-c_{i}\right]\,,

(8)

where
n_{i}
are the degrees of freedom of a species
i

M_{i}(\phi)
are the field-dependent masses, and
c_{i}=\frac{5}{6}\,\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)
for vector bosons (scalars and fermions). The renormalization group (RG) scale is denoted by
\mu
.

Interactions with the thermal medium generate temperature-dependent corrections to the effective potential of the form

\displaystyle V_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}(\varphi,T)=\frac{T^{4}}{2\pi^{2}}\sum_{i}n_{i}J_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B,F}}}\left(\frac{M_{i}(\varphi)^{2}}{T^{2}}\right)\,,

(9)

where

J_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B,F}}}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}yy^{2}\ln\left[1\mp\exp\left(-\sqrt{x+y^{2}}\right)\right]

(10)

are the bosonic/fermionic thermal integrals. Thermal resummation is accounted for by implementing the Daisy term
[

,
107
]

V_{\mbox{\scriptsize daisy}}(\varphi,T)=-\frac{T}{12\pi}\sum_{i}n_{i}\Big[(M_{i}(\varphi)^{2}+\Pi_{i}(T))^{\frac{3}{2}}-M_{i}(\varphi)^{\frac{3}{2}}\Big]\,,

(11)

where
\Pi_{i}(T)
denotes the thermal mass of a species

The field-dependent and thermal masses can be found in, e.g., refs.
[

55

Here, we only consider corrections from the

Since

\sim g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}^{8}

The full effective potential then reads

\begin{split}V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T)=V_{\mbox{\scriptsize tree}}&(\varphi)+V_{\mbox{\scriptsize cw}}(\varphi,T)\\
&+V_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}(\varphi,T)+V_{\mbox{\scriptsize daisy}}(\varphi,T)\,.\end{split}

(12)

Note that although a state-of-the-art, dimensionally reduced effective field theory

Inefficiency of bubble percolation.

With the effective potential, we are now able to study the parameter space where bubble nucleation and percolation is (in)efficient.
We start from a high temperature where the
U(1)_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}
symmetry is restored and
\varphi
is trapped in the false vacuum.
Then, at
T_{i}<T_{c}
, the Universe enters a period of thermal inflation

when the false vacuum energy becomes comparable to the energy density in the thermal bath,

\rho_{r}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{30}g_{\star,\epsilon}T_{i}^{4}=\Delta V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T_{i})\,,

(14)

where
\Delta V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T)
is the potential energy difference between the false and true vacuum, and
g_{\star,\epsilon}=110.75
denote the effective energetic degrees of freedom of the extended SM.

The Hubble parameter reads

H=\left(\frac{\rho_{r}(T)+\Delta V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}}{3M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\approx\left(\frac{1}{128\pi^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}^{2}}{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}\ \,,

(15)

where in the last approximation we have taken the zero-temperature limit of the effective potential.
Hence,

becomes approximately constant for sufficiently small temperatures.
To evaluate the efficiency of bubble nucleation, we compute the decay rate of the false vacuum

\Gamma(T)\approx T^{4}\left(\frac{\mathcal{S}_{3}}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp\left(-\mathcal{S}_{3}\right)\,.

Here,

S_{3}\left[\varphi\right]=4\pi\int{\rm d}rr^{2}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{{\rm d}\varphi}{{\rm d}r}\right)^{2}+V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T)\right]\,.

\int_{T_{n}}^{T_{c}}\frac{{\rm d}T}{T}\frac{\Gamma(T)}{H(T)^{4}}=1\,,

In these models, the inflaton efficiently preheats the post-inflationary Universe while crossing a tachyonic region of its potential.
Such models are, however, inaccessible to current and future experiments as a consequence of the large energy scale during cosmic inflation.
This is the crucial difference to our work, where the instability is dynamically triggered below the QCD scale, and thus is, excitingly, accessible to experiments.

The equation of motion for the scalar field
\varphi
in an expanding Universe reads

\ddot{\varphi}+\frac{1}{a^{2}}\nabla^{2}\varphi+3H\dot{\varphi}+\frac{\partial V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T)}{\partial\varphi}=0\,,\\

(22)

where
a
denotes the scale factor,
H
is the Hubble parameter given by eq. (
15
), and dots indicate derivatives with respect to cosmic time
t
.
As bubble dynamics is absent, the field remains largely homogeneous down to
T_{\mathrm{roll}}
. We can decompose the field into a homogeneous background field
\varphi(t)
and small fluctuations
\delta\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)
,

\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\varphi(t)+\delta\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)\,.

(23)

In Fourier space, the fluctuations are expressed as
[
117
,
118
,
119
]

\delta\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}a_{k}u_{k}(t)\exp(ikt)+h.c.\,,

(24)

where
a_{k}^{\dagger}
,
a_{k}
are creation and annihilation operators with

[a_{k},a_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(k-k^{\prime})\,.

(25)

The mode functions
u_{k}(t)
capture the time evolution of a mode carrying momentum
k
.
Inserting eq. (
23
) into (
22
) and keeping only terms up to linear order in
\delta\varphi\ll\varphi
, we obtain equations of motion for the background field and the fluctuations,

\displaystyle\ddot{\varphi}+3H\dot{\varphi}+\frac{\partial V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T)}{\partial\varphi}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(26)

\displaystyle\ddot{u}_{k}+3H\dot{u}_{k}+\left(\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T)}{\partial\varphi^{2}}\right)u_{k}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(27)

where we have employed eq. (
24
) to express the equation of motion in terms of the mode functions
u_{k}
.
Hence, a mode with momentum
k
is described by a harmonic oscillator, damped by Hubble friction, with a time-dependent frequency

\omega^{2}=\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T)}{\partial\varphi^{2}}\,.

(28)

For small field values, the scalar mass takes negative values,
\partial^{2}V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}/\partial\varphi^{2}=m_{\varphi}^{2}<0
.
Then, long-wavelength modes in the range

0\leq\frac{k}{a}\leq m_{\varphi}\,,

(29)

experience a negative effective frequency,
\omega^{2}<0
.
This corresponds to a tachyonic instability, where the solution of eq. (
27
) changes from an oscillating behavior to exponential growth,
u_{k}\propto\exp(|\omega| t)
.
Hence, scalar fluctuations are enhanced while
\varphi(t)
rolls through the concave part of the effective potential.
This is exemplified in fig.
2
, where we display
V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T_{\mathrm{roll}})
for
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}=10^{6}\,\mathrm{GeV}
,
g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}=10^{-2}
, together with the effective mass
m_{\varphi}^{2}
of the scalar field.
The unstable region is indicated by the orange shaded region.

The initial tachyonic band is determined by the effective mass parameter close to the origin,

m_{\varphi,0}^{2}=\frac{\partial^{2}V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}})}{\partial\varphi^{2}}\Big|_{\varphi\to 0}\simeq\Delta m_{\varphi,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}}^{2}+g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}^{2}T^{2}\,,

(30)

where we have only kept leading-order terms. As the temperature decreases below
T_{\mathrm{roll}}
, thermal effects quickly become negligible, such that the effective mass is dictated by the negative, QCD-induced contribution (
6
).

We display the maximal initial growth rate
\omega\simeq|\Delta m_{\varphi,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}}|
, divided by the Hubble parameter (
15
), in fig.

We observe that for large VEVs, i.e., large
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}
and small
g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}

Vice versa, the effective mass term increases for smaller VEVs.
Then,
|m_{\varphi,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}}|>H
, and the tachyonic instability becomes effective right at
T_{\mathrm{roll}}
.

IV  Scalar field amplification

Initial conditions.

To study the exponential growth of scalar fluctuations, we first need to specify the initial conditions.
Prior to
T_{\mathrm{roll}}
, the homogeneous component
\varphi(t)
is trapped in the false vacuum around the origin, while the Universe is exponentially expanding.
At the end of thermal inflation, the expectation value of the scalar field is dictated by quantum fluctuations that evolve during the inflationary period.
These may be split into a thermal
\langle\varphi^{2}\rangle_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{T}}}\approx T^{2}/12
and a vacuum
\langle\varphi^{2}\rangle_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{v}}}\approx H^{2}N/(4\pi^{2})

[

121

122

\langle\varphi^{2}\rangle=\langle\varphi^{2}\rangle_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{T}}}+\langle\varphi^{2}\rangle_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{v}}}
.
Classical rolling eventually takes over when
[
123
]

V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}^{\prime}(\varphi_{\mathrm{cl}})=-\frac{3H^{3}}{2\pi}\,.

(31)

Therefore we need to ensure the classical limit is justified.
First, this includes points where
\varphi_{\mathrm{cl}}\leq\sqrt{\langle\varphi^{2}\rangle}
is fulfilled at
T_{\mathrm{roll}}
.
In the regime where
\sqrt{\langle\varphi^{2}\rangle}<\varphi_{\mathrm{cl}}
, but
|m_{\varphi,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}}|\gg H
, the growth of quantum fluctuations typically drives the field towards the classical regime rather quickly
[
122
]
.
For the parameter space where
|m_{\varphi,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}}|\ll H
, however, thermal inflation is expected to continue for a sizable number of e-folds after
T_{\mathrm{roll}}
.
This may lead to contradictions with CMB observations
[
124
]
, or even to eternal inflation
[
121
]
.
Since an analysis of the quantum fluctuations during thermal inflation is beyond the scope of this work, we focus on the parameter space where
|m_{\varphi,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}}|>H
, and choose

\varphi_{i}=\max\Big\{\varphi_{\mathrm{cl}},\sqrt{\langle\varphi^{2}\rangle}\Big\}

(32)

as initial condition for the zero-mode.

8

\Delta m_{\varphi}^{2}\approx\Delta m_{\varphi,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}}^{2}=\mathrm{const.}
, i.e., the growth rate is largely independent of the zero-mode.

As

Numerical study.

With the above initial conditions, we are now set to study the exponential production of scalar fluctuations.
Note, however, that the linearized equations of motion (
26
) do not capture the backreaction from the the excited modes onto the zero-mode.
As the energy density of the fluctuations becomes comparable to the one of the zero-mode, fragmentation sets in.
Then the field configuration becomes fully inhomogeneous, and the scale of inhomogeneity is given by the peak momentum of the amplified modes.
Such non-linear dynamics can only be studied using dedicated lattice simulations
[
78
]
.
Nevertheless, the linearized analysis provides an intuition about the efficiency of tachyonic growth.
In addition, we obtain a reliable prediction of the peak momentum, which we will employ in sec.
VI
to estimate the peak of the associated GW spectrum.

In our linearized analysis, we solve
eqs. (
26
) and (
27
) numerically for
N_{k}=1000
momentum modes.
In the absence of non-linear effects, we then stop the simulation as the energy density of the fluctuations,
\rho_{\delta\varphi}
, approaches the total initial energy density of the scalar,
\rho_{\mathrm{tot}}=\Delta V
.
To compute the energy density of the scalar excitations, we first note that the occupation number of a harmonic oscillator with frequency
\omega_{k}
reads

n_{k}=\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}\left(\frac{|\dot{u}_{k}|^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}}+|u_{k}|^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\,.

(38)

The associated energy density is then

\rho_{\delta\varphi}=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\omega_{k}\left(n_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,.

(39)

Note that eq. (
38
) formally only holds in the adiabatic limit
\dot{\omega}_{k}/\omega_{k}^{2}\ll 1
.
This condition is violated once
\varphi
reaches larger field values, where the second derivative of
V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}
, i.e., the growth rate
\omega_{k}^{2}
, rapidly increases.
Also, the concept of a particle number density is ill-defined while the mass is tachyonic, as
\omega_{k}^{2}<0
for the unstable modes.
However, the main goal of our numerical study is to verify that the peak momentum scale is indeed dictated by the QCD-induced mass.
We will see that this peak forms during the early stage of the evolution where the adiabaticity condition holds.
A more careful treatment of the particle number will therefore not alter the location of the peak, but merely the time when tachyonic growth ends.
Once the scalar field rolls down to its minimum,
m_{\varphi}^{2}
becomes positive and the particle interpretation becomes valid.
In the tachyonic regime, the energy density is then typically computed
[
82
]
by defining either

\omega_{k}=\biggr|\frac{k}{a}\biggr|\,,\quad\mathrm{or}\quad\omega_{k}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{k}{a}\right)^{2}+|m_{\varphi}^{2}|}\,,

(40)

The impact of the choice of
\omega_{k}
on our final results proves to be small, hence we employ
\omega_{k}=|k/a|
in the remainder of this work.

V  Reheating

To ensure a consistent cosmological evolution, the energy of the excited scalar modes has to be efficiently transferred back to the SM sector.
After the tachyonic transition, the scalar field is driven to the global minimum of the effective potential, corresponding to the
\mathrm{B-L}
and electroweak vacuum,
(\langle\varphi\rangle,\langle h\rangle)=(v_{\varphi},246\,\mathrm{GeV})
.
Then all SM fields are rendered massive.
The scalar fluctuations which dominate the energy density of the Universe may now be interpreted as particles with mass

m_{\varphi}=\sqrt{\frac{\partial^{2}V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T)}{\partial\varphi^{2}}}\Biggr|_{\varphi=v_{\varphi}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}}g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}^{2}v_{\varphi}\,.

(41)

Here, we have taken the zero-temperature limit which is justified as
v_{\varphi}\gg T_{\mathrm{roll}}
.
In addition, we have neglected scalar mixing because of the small mixing angle
\lambda_{p}\ll 1
.
For simplicity, we focus on reheating via the Higgs portal term in eq. (
1
), hence we require
m_{\varphi}>2m_{h}
.
10
10

10

Note that scalar mixing may provide a successful reheating mechanism
[
126
]
if
m_{\varphi}<2m_{h}
.

The decay rate of
\varphi\to hh
reads
[
55
,
126
]

\Gamma_{\varphi}\approx\frac{\lambda_{p}^{2}v_{\varphi}^{2}}{32\pi m_{\varphi}}\,,

(42)

where we evaluate the model parameters at the EW scale,
\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z}}}
.
In most of the parameter space, this expression is smaller than the expansion rate of the Universe,
\Gamma<H
, prohibiting the scalar decay initially.
Since the scalar modes carry physical momenta
k\ll m_{\varphi}
, hence are non-relativistic, the Universe undergoes a matter-domination period until

H_{\mathrm{rh}}=H_{\star}\left(\frac{a_{\star}}{a_{\mathrm{rh}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}=\Gamma_{\varphi}\,,

(43)

where
H_{\star}
and
a_{\star}
denote the Hubble parameter and scale factor at the end of tachyonic growth.
Assuming a quick thermalization of the SM bath, the reheating temperature is approximated by

\frac{\pi^{2}}{30}g_{\star,\epsilon}T_{\mathrm{rh}}^{4}=3M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}\mathrm{min}\left(H_{\star}^{2},\Gamma_{\varphi}^{2}\right)\,.

(44)

To provide some intuition, we can for now neglect the running of the parameters. Combining eqs. (
15
), (
41
), and (
42
), we then obtain

\frac{\Gamma_{\varphi}}{H_{\star}}\approx 2\times 10^{-3}g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}\left(\frac{10^{6}\,\mathrm{GeV}}{m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}}\right)^{5}\,,

(45)

in agreement with ref.
[
55
]
.
Hence, small gauge couplings and large

masses lead to a more extended period of matter domination.
This yields

T_{\mbox{\scriptsize rh}}\approx 1.4\times 10^{4}g_{\star,\epsilon}^{-\frac{1}{4}}g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{10^{6}\,\mathrm{GeV}}{m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\,\mathrm{GeV}\,,

(46)

where
g_{\star,\epsilon}
denotes the energetic degrees of freedom in the thermal bath after reheating.

From this, we can derive an approximate bound such that the reheating temperature is larger than the temperature of big bang nucleosynthesis,
T_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{BBN}}}\sim\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{MeV})

m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}<5.8\times 10^{10}g_{\star,\epsilon}^{-\frac{1}{6}}g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}^{\frac{1}{3}}\,\mathrm{GeV}\,.

(47)

This is well outside the parameter space we consider.

(55)

Therefore, a larger QCD-induced VEV
v_{h,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}}
of the Higgs field shifts the GW signal to a higher frequency and smaller amplitude.
The same applies to the gauge coupling
g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}
.
This can be directly seen from eq. (

), as
(k_{\star}/a)^{2}\approx\Delta m_{\varphi,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}}^{2}\propto(g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}v_{h,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}})^{2}
.
The impact of the gauge boson mass is a bit more subtle.
If reheating is fast, larger
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}
moves the peak towards larger frequencies.
This is expected as
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}
sets the Hubble parameter during thermal inflation.
For slow decays of the scalar field and hence long reheating periods, this behavior changes and large
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}
decrease the peak frequency.
This is due to the strong suppression of the scalar decay rate, which leads to an extended redshift
a_{\star}/a_{\mathrm{rh}}\propto m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}^{-10/3}
.
Regarding the peak amplitude, larger gauge boson masses generally lead to stronger signals, albeit the scaling of
h^{2}\Omega_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{GW}}}
changes depending on the duration of the reheating process.

Let us stress again that for the above estimates, we neglected the running of the model parameters.
For our final results, we evaluate the decay rate at the global minimum characterized by
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z}}}
, and use the thermal RG scale
\mu\sim\pi T_{\mathrm{roll}}
to compute the QCD-induced mass relevant for tachyonic growth.

VII  Conclusions

We have presented a mechanism to end the supercooling period characteristic to classically scale-invariant SM extensions that has not been addressed in the literature so far, leading to a unique GW signal.
While previous works have mostly analyzed the exit from thermal inflation via a strong first-order phase transition, we identify, employing the
U(1)_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}
model, a large parameter space where bubble percolation is inefficient.
The breaking of classical scale invariance by the top quark condensate instead induces a cancellation of the thermal barrier, and the
U(1)_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}
scalar becomes free to roll down its effective potential towards the true minimum.
The field crosses a tachyonic instability, as its effective mass parameter is negative close to the origin.
This leads to a copious production of sub-horizon scalar field fluctuations, which quickly dominate the energy density of the Universe.
These fluctuations emerge as scalar particles after the
U(1)_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}
symmetry is broken and efficiently reheat the supercooled Universe by decaying to Higgs bosons.
Intriguingly, the exponential amplification of scalar fluctuations induces a sizable SGWB.
We have estimated the peak frequency and amplitude of the GW spectrum based on previous lattice results
[
71
,
72
]
.
The characteristic frequency scale is given by the interplay between QCD dynamics and mass of the new gauge boson,
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}\sim\mathcal{O}(10^{5}-10^{7})\,\mathrm{GeV}
, which dictates the onset of thermal inflation.
Therefore, we find promising observational prospects at the future observatories LISA, DECIGO, ET, and BBO.
This is distinct from previous works where tachyonic preheating follows cosmic inflation.
The high scale of inflation typically shifts the signal to the high-frequency band, which is not accessible in the near future.

Finally, let us comment on possible next directions.
First, our analysis may be extended by higher loop orders in the effective potential.
This allows for a more reliable prediction of the parameter space where thermal tunneling is inefficient, i.e., where the tachyonic instability becomes relevant.
To this end, a better understanding of the QCD-induced vacuum expectation value of the Higgs field is required, as well.
This can, however, only be inferred from lattice studies of massless QCD augmented by the Higgs.
Regarding the tachyonic instability, we have restricted ourselves to the linear regime.
This limits our study of the associated GW generation to order-of-magnitude estimates.
A classical lattice simulation, taking into account all non-linear effects, is therefore needed to obtain a more robust prediction of the GW signal, in particular of the spectral behavior.
Then it would be interesting to study the ability of future experiments to distinguish between the tachyonic phase transition and the standard scenario of a supercooled FOPT.
We will return to these exciting questions in the future.

Appendix A
Input parameters

In this section, we briefly describe how to determine the input parameters, following ref.
[
57
]
.
The model introduces five unknown quantities: the scalar self-coupling
\lambda_{\varphi}
, the portal coupling
\lambda_{p}
, the gauge coupling
g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}
, the scalar VEV
v_{\varphi}
, and the kinetic mixing
\tilde{g}
between the
U(1)_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}
and
U(1)_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Y}}}
gauge groups.
The scalar self- and portal coupling, however, are fixed by the requirement to generate the EW scale. In addition, we express our results in terms of
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}
instead of
v_{\varphi}
.
The procedure then is:

(i)

The scalar VEV at
\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}
is obtained from the tree-level relation
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}=2g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}v_{\varphi}
. We then demand that loop corrections preserve the VEV,

\frac{{\rm d}V_{\mbox{\scriptsize cw}}}{{\rm d}\varphi}\biggr|_{\varphi=v_{\varphi},\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}}=0\,,

(56)

which fixes

\lambda_{\varphi}(\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}})=\frac{g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}^{4}}{\pi^{2}}\,.

(57)

This is a typical relation in Coleman-Weinberg models.

(ii)

Using the one-loop
\beta
-functions
[
14
]
, we run
\lambda_{\varphi}
and
g_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{B-L}}}
down to the EW scale
\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z}}}
. From the physical masses
m_{h}
,
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z}}}
,
m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{W}}}
, and
m_{t}
, we obtain the SM gauge couplings and the top quark Yukawa. Since
v_{\varphi}\gg v_{h}
in all of the parameter space we consider, we neglect mass mixing between the two scalars. Then also
\lambda_{h}
takes its SM value.

(iii)

We minimize the
\mathrm{B-L}
potential for
\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z}}}
to find
v_{\varphi}(\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z}}})
. The portal coupling is then determined by demanding that
h
acquires its SM tree-level VEV
v_{h,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{SM}}}}\simeq 246\,\mathrm{GeV}
. This translates to

\lambda_{p}(\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z}}})=2\lambda_{h}\left(\frac{v_{h,{\mbox{\tiny\rm{SM}}}}}{v_{\varphi}}\right)^{2}\,.

(58)

(iv)

This fixes all input parameters at
\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z}}}
. For the computation of
V_{\mbox{\scriptsize eff}}(\varphi,T)
, we then use the
\beta
-functions to run the parameters to the respective scale

\mu=\max\{m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}}(\varphi),\pi T\}\,,

(59)

as outlined in the main body.

The kinetic mixing parameter
\tilde{g}
only enters our analysis via the RG evolution.
In previous studies
[
52
,
55
]
, it was shown that choosing
\tilde{g}(\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z'}}})=-0.5
renders the EW vacuum stable up to the Planck scale.
However, we find that this leads to numerical instabilities in the running of
\lambda_{p}
at low energy scales
\mu\sim T_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{QCD}}}
, where tachyonic growth takes place.
Therefore, we choose kinetic mixing to vanish at the EW scale,
\tilde{g}(\mu=m_{\mbox{\tiny\rm{Z}}})=0
, to minimize its impact on our results.
