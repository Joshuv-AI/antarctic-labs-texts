Photosynthetic induction responses of two Leymus chinensis ecotypes under drought stress
Authors: Dekun Meng, Rui Xu, Lidong Cao, Jianying Ma, Wei Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Drought often alters both steady-state photosynthesis and photosynthetic induction, yet their joint responses within species remain incompletely described. Here, we examined photosynthetic and stomatal responses of two ecotypes (gray-green, GG; yellow-green, YG) of Leymus chinensis to a step increase in irradiance under contrasting soil moisture conditions. For both ecotypes, experimentally imposed drought (three soil water content levels: well-watered 60–75%, moderate drought ~ 35–50%, severe drought ~ 10–25% v/v; 60 d) reduced steady-state photosynthetic rate and prolonged photosynthetic induction. The GG ecotype showed faster stomatal responses and higher cumulative carbon gain during light induction than the YG ecotype. Specifically, GG exhibited a faster Rubisco activation rate and smaller but more numerous stomata, which were associated with faster photosynthetic induction under drought. This combination of traits may help maintain carbon gain during transient drought episodes, while involving trade-offs in water use. In contrast, the YG ecotype required more time to reach steady state and maintained lower steady-state photosynthetic rates under drought. Overall, our results quantify how declining soil moisture differentially affects induction and steady-state photosynthesis within a species and reveal ecotypic contrasts in stomatal and biochemical responses under drought, highlighting the role of photosynthetic induction and stomatal behaviour in ecotypic adaptation to drought.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07982-w.

Introduction

Drought frequency and severity are projected to increase under global warming [1, 2], posing a major threat to plant carbon assimilation worldwide, especially in arid and semi-arid regions [3–5]. Plants have evolved diverse strategies to cope with drought, ranging from conservative water use to acquisitive photosynthetic responses. However, most studies, have focused on steady-state photosynthesis, measured under constant conditions [6, 7]. In nature, environmental drivers such as light intensity, temperature and soil moisture fluctuate continuously [8, 9]. Under such conditions, dynamic response of photosynthesis, particularly the induction process following a sudden increase in irradiance, plays a critical role in determining carbon gain and survival [10]. Dynamic photosynthesis under drought has been investigated in several recent studies [11–13], but how stomatal, biochemical and photoprotective processes are coordinated in this context is still not fully resolved.

During a rapid increase in light, photosynthetic induction reflects time-dependent limitations imposed by both stomatal conductance and the activation state of Calvin–Benson cycle enzymes, and has been associated with variation in pigment composition, mesophyll conductance, and stomatal traits, although the mechanistic links to enzyme activation and stomatal opening remain under active investigation [14, 15]. Photoprotective mechanisms, particularly non-photochemical quenching, shape dynamic photosynthesis by modulating the recovery of electron transport and PSII efficiency after light fluctuations, balancing rapid carbon gain against the risk of photoinhibition [16, 17]. In addition to photosynthetic induction, processes such as Rubisco deactivation, stomatal closure and photoprotective responses collectively determine cumulative carbon gain under fluctuating light, a functional measure of dynamic photosynthetic performance [18]. Considerable variation in dynamic photosynthesis exists among species and genotypes. For example, Populus genotype-specific differences have been reported between controlled and field conditions [10]. Together, these findings indicate that environmental context and genetic background jointly shape dynamic photosynthesis. Although numerous studies have identified key physiological determinants, such as stomatal kinetics, Rubisco activation and photoprotective processes [14, 19, 20], how these mechanisms operate under drought, particularly in perennial C3 grasses like L. chinensis remains incompletely understood.

Drought affects both steady-state photosynthesis and photosynthetic induction by constraining Rubisco activation, altering stomatal kinetics [3, 21]. Dynamic changes in mesophyll conductance can further modulate CO2 supply and have been shown to enhance photosynthesis of shade leaves under sunflecks [22], although direct evidence for its role in photosynthetic induction under drought remains limited. Stomatal characteristics, such as guard cell size and dynamics, also influence the speed of induction responses, although conflicting evidence exists across species [23–25]. Beyond stomata, drought alters mesophyll conductance by affecting cell wall thickness and chloroplast positioning, thus restricting internal CO2 diffusion [26]. Concurrent changes in chlorophyll content, PSII efficiency, and energy partitioning between carbon assimilation, photorespiration, and antioxidant metabolism further influence photosynthetic performance [17, 27, 28]. While steady-state responses to drought are relatively well characterized, much less is known about dynamic responses, particularly how stomatal kinetics, Rubisco activation, and photoprotective adjustments interact to shape drought adaptation. Addressing this gap is crucial for understanding intraspecific variation in photosynthetic resilience.

Leymus chinensis (Trin.) Tzvel., a C3 rhizomatous grass, is a widely distributed perennial forage grass in northern China, eastern Mongolia, Transbaikalia, and Russia [29]. It shows strong ecological adaptability and tolerance to drought, salt-alkaline, and low-temperature [29, 30], often dominating steppe and meadow ecosystems and thereby contributing to grassland stability and productivity. Distinct ecotypes are recognized, including gray-green (GG) and yellow-green (YG), which differ in leaf colour, pigment composition and drought tolerance [31, 32]. Variation in leaf colour has been linked to differences in chlorophyll and carotenoid concentrations that may influence light harvesting, photoprotection and the balance between carbon gain and water loss [33]. Recent studies have documented gas-exchange and physiological diversity between these ecotypes [31, 34, 35], suggesting divergent strategies for regulating both steady-state and dynamic photosynthesis under water limitation. These ecotypic contrasts provide a useful system for testing how steady-state and dynamic photosynthesis are coordinated under drought; however, direct comparisons of photosynthetic induction between GG and YG remain scarce.

We quantified steady-state photosynthetic rate and the photosynthetic induction, defined as the response of assimilation and stomatal conductance to a step increase in irradiance, in two ecotypes of L. chinensis. Plants were subjected to three contrasting water regimes (well-watered, moderate drought and severe drought). This experimental design addresses how soil moisture gradients modulate dynamic versus steady-state photosynthesis within a single species and whether ecotypic differences in stomatal and biochemical traits confer contrasting drought responses. Our objectives were to (i) compare steady-state and dynamic photosynthetic responses between the GG and YG ecotypes under the controlled drought conditions, and (ii) elucidate the stomatal behavior underlying their divergent adaptive strategies. We hypothesized that (1) dynamic photosynthetic traits (e.g. induction time and cumulative carbon gain) would be more sensitive to declining soil moisture than steady-state photosynthesis, and (2) the GG ecotype would show greater drought tolerance by maintaining faster induction and smaller losses of cumulative carbon gain across water regimes compared with the YG ecotype.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Two ecotypes of L. chinensis (GG and YG) were obtained by in situ transplantation from natural grasslands in Songnen Plain, China, in April 2022. Instead of using seeds, intact plants were carefully excavated with their root systems and transplanted into pots for subsequent experimental treatments. The transplantation was conducted with permission from the local Grassland Management Bureau, which also confirmed species identification. All experimental procedures complied with institutional, national, and international guidelines for research on wild plants, and field studies were performed in accordance with local legislation.

For this study, we used pot experiment with a completely randomized block design and six replications. Each plastic pot (20 cm in diameter and 21 cm in depth) was filled with chestnut soil (5 kg soil pot–1). In April 2022, rhizomes of the GG and YG ecotypes of L. chinensis with similar size were transplanted into the pots, with seven individuals per pot grown in monoculture. For acclimation and establishment, all pots were weighed and watered every 3 days to maintain soil moisture at 60–75% of the field capacity (FC) for 3 weeks. All the pots were placed under a plastic shed (90% light transmittance) to exclude natural precipitation. After establishment, 72 pots (36 GG and 36 YG) were selected for the experimental treatments. In this stage (60 days: May 1st - July 29th ), pots were randomly assigned to six blocks (12 pots per block; 6 GG and 6 YG), and within each block to three water regimes: well-watered (WW), moderate drought (MD) and severe drought (SD), with 2 GG pots and 2 YG pots per treatment. Soil moisture in WW pots was maintained at 60–75% FC by weighing the pots every 3 days and rewatering accordingly. For the MD treatment, soil moisture was gradually reduced to below 50% FC within 4 days after the initiation of drought and subsequently maintained at 35–50% FC. For the SD treatment, soil moisture was reduced to below 25% FC within 7 days and then maintained at 10–25% FC throughout the drought period. Field capacity was determined gravimetrically by saturating the soil and allowing free drainage until a constant weight was reached. Measurements were conducted during the vegetative growth stage from 10 to 20 July 2022.

Leaf gas exchange

Leaf gas exchange parameters were measured using a portable infrared gas analyzer (Li-Cor 6800, LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). The block temperature was set to 25℃, relative humidity was maintained at 60–65%, and reference CO2 concentration was set at 400 ppm. The light spectrum was set to Red (90%) + Blue (10%). For each treatment, leaf gas exchanges parameters were measured on one of the uppermost fully expanded leaves for both GG and YG ecotypes, with 5 replications per ecotype. Because individual leaves did not always fully cover the cuvette aperture, measurements were made in partial-chamber mode: the diameter of the leaf segment inside the cuvette was entered, and the instrument automatically calculated the enclosed leaf area used to scale gas-exchange rates. The leaves used for gas exchange measurements were marked for subsequent sampling.

To characterize photosynthetic induction, we measured the time course of leaf gas exchange in response to a simulated step increase in light intensity. To ensure the stability of light-induced gas exchange measurements, after sunset the pots for measurement were moved from the plastic greenhouse to the laboratory and kept in a dark room overnight for dark acclimation. Leaf gas exchange measurements were conducted from 06:00 h to 11:00 h (day length approx. 15 h, sunrise at 04:30 h). Preliminary tests indicated that photosynthesis was already fully induced after sunrise, and measurements starting at 6:00 h did not differ from those started later in the morning. Following overnight dark acclimation, net CO2 assimilation (A) and stomatal conductance (gs) were recorded in darkness (Rn and gn), concurrently with chlorophyll a fluorescence measurements; the saturating pulse intensity was set to 15,000 µmol m− 2 s− 1 with a 1,000 ms pulse period. The measured leaves were then equilibrated at a light intensity of 100 µmol m− 2 s− 1 for at least 20 min, until A and gs reached a steady state (changes < 5% over the last 5 min); these low-light baseline values are denoted as A100 and g100. The light intensity was then increased to 1500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and maintained at this level for 40 min. Typically, temperate C3 species reach light saturation at irradiances between 1000 and 1200 µmol m− 2 s− 1 [36]. Furthermore, under drought stress, the light saturation point is known to decrease or remain stable due to stomatal and non-stomatal limitations on photosynthetic capacity [37]. A and gs were recorded every minute throughout the induction period, and steady-state values at high light are denoted as A1500 and g1500. The notation A100, A1500 and g100, g1500 is used consistently in the text and all figures. For analyses of cumulative carbon gain and water loss during induction, we integrated A and transpiration rate (E) over the 40-min period after the light step, providing a uniform time window for comparing treatments. After finishing the measurements, the pots were moved back to the plastic greenhouse.

Stomatal morphology

Stomatal traits were measured using the imprint method on upper-most fully expanded leaves from the same tillers and canopy position as those used for leaf gas-exchange measurements (i.e. adjacent leaves of similar size and developmental stage) [38, 39]. Nail polish was applied to adaxial and abaxial epidermis of the sampled leaves (about 1 cm2, avoiding major veins). After the nail polish had dried, the film was carefully peeled off with tweezers and mounted as a temporary slice. In the laboratory, stomatal traits were observed with an inverted microscope (Ts2-FL, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Images were captured using a 40× objective and 10× eyepiece. For each sample, 3–5 fields of view were randomly selected to count stomata and calculate stomatal density (SD). In each field of view, three stomata were randomly selected to determine stomatal size (SS). Stomatal size was expressed as the area of an ellipse, calculated from the length of the major and minor axes of the guard cells.

Photosynthesis related enzyme activity

For each pot, two leaves comparable to those used for leaf gas exchange measurements were collected. Leaves were wrapped in tin foil, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and transferred back to the laboratory, then stored in an ultra-low temperature refrigerator (-80 °C) for biochemical analysis.

Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase (Rubisco) activity and reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (reduced coenzyme II, NADPH) content of frozen leaves were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Shanghai Jining Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A 1.0 g frozen leaf sample was clipped and weighed, and ground with liquid nitrogen. After the transfer of the ground leaf samples to a 50 mL vial, 9.0 mL pre-cooled phosphate buffered saline solution (PBS, 0.1 mol L− 1, pH 7.4) was added. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was pipetted to a new vial for further analysis. After adding sample, incubating, washing, enzymatic reaction, incubating, washing, color development and termination steps on the supernatant in the new vial, the concentration was determined using a multifunctional enzyme marker (ND-1000, Tumer Designs, Wilmington, DE, USA) at the corresponding wavelength (Rubisco 340 nm; NADPH 450 nm).

Statistical analysis

Statistics were done using R 4.2.0 (R Core Team, 2022). Normality and homogeneity of variance were checked using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Levene’s tests, respectively. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD tests (package “multcomp”) was used to assess treatment and ecotype effects, with differences considered significant at P < 0.05. Relationships between variables were analysed by linear regressions, and Pearson correlation coefficients were obtained using functions from the ggpmisc package. Principal component analysis (PCA) analyses were performed using “FactoMineR” and “factoextra” package. Ecotype were overlaid on the PCA biplot for visualization only, and no formal inference about group differences was based on PCA. Structural equation modelling (SEM) was carried out with the “piecewiseSEM” package to quantify hypothesized causal relationships among soil water content (SWC), mesostate, stomatal traits, Rubisco activation (enzyme), dynamic photosynthetic capacity, A1500, and ACG. Component models were fitted as (generalized) linear mixed models where appropriate. Overall model adequacy was evaluated using Fisher’s C, which summarizing the basis-set independence claims, with df = 2 k, where k is the number of independence claims. A non-significant Fisher’s C P-value indicates that the independence claims are not rejected (i.e. the model is compatible with the data). Path-specific effects are reported as standardized coefficients with P-values and R2. Linear mixed-effects models were fitted using the “lme4” package [43], and the percentage of variance explained by each variable was calculated with the “glmm.hp” package [43]. All plots were produced using the “ggplot2” package.

Results

Steady-state photosynthesis under different drought treatments

In both ecotypes of L. chinensis, the rates of net carbon assimilation rate (A) and stomatal conductance (gs) exhibited an increasing pattern when the light intensity was elevated from 100 to 1500 umol m− 2 s− 1 (Figs. 2a-d). There were no significant differences in A100, A1500, and g100 (A100, A1500 were steady-state photosynthetic rate for CO2 reached at the light intensity of 100 or 1500 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1, g100 and g1500 were stomatal conductance for H2O reached at the light intensity of 100 and 1500 µmol photons m− 2 s− 1, respectively, calculated by averaging single values over the last minute of each period) between the GG and YG ecotypes. However, the g1500 of the YG ecotype was significantly higher than that of the GG ecotype. Notably, a reduction in soil moisture level resulted in a significant decrease in A100, A1500, g100, and g1500 in both ecotypes of L. chinensis. There were no significant interactions between ecotype and drought treatment on steady-state photosynthesis (Table 1). Under the well-watered conditions, the GG ecotype had a higher A100 than the YG ecotype, which showed no difference among drought treatments. Additionally, averaged A1500 under the well-watered conditions was significantly higher than under the severe drought in both ecotypes (Table 1).

Dynamic photosynthesis under the drought treatments

During the photosynthetic induction period, both ecotypes of L. chinensis exhibited dynamic increases in A and gs, but the drought treatment weakened the magnitude of increase (Figs. 2a-d). There were no significant differences in time reached 50% (T50A) and 90% (T90A) of steady-state photosynthesis between the GG and YG ecotypes, but time reached 50% (T50g) and 90% (T90g) of steady-state gs in the YG ecotype were significantly higher than those in the GG ecotype. Reducing soil moisture significantly influenced T50A, T90A, T50g and T90g in both ecotypes of L. chinensis. There were no significant interactions between ecotype and drought treatment on dynamic photosynthesis (Table 2). In both ecotypes of L. chinensis, the averaged T50A, T90A, T50g and T90g were lower under the WW treatment than under the SD treatment (Table 2). Under the SD treatment, the YG ecotype had greater T90g value compared to the GG ecotype (Table 2).

The results of principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear differentiation in the dynamic photosynthetic characteristics of the GG and YG ecotypes (Fig. 3a). The GG ecotype was primarily associated with stomatal traits and Rubisco activation rate, whereas the YG ecotype was more strongly associated with enzyme activity characteristics (Fig. 3b). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) together explained 60.74% of the total variance, with PC1 largely reflecting a gradient in stomatal conductance and photosynthetic induction traits and PC2 being more strongly related to biochemical (enzyme) variables. We interpret this PCA as an exploratory, descriptive ordination of multivariate trait structure, while formal tests of ecotype and SWC effects are provided by the SEM and mixed-model analyses.

Stomatal morphology

There were no significant differences in the adaxial stomatal density (SDada), abaxial stomatal density (SDaba), adaxial stomatal size (SSada) and abaxial stomatal size (SSaba) between the GG and YG ecotypes. The decrease in soil moisture significantly affected the SDaba, SSada and SSaba in both ecotypes of L. chinensis. However, the SDada did not differ significantly across the drought treatments in both ecotypes of L. chinensis. We observed significant interactions between ecotype and water treatment on the SDada, SSada and SSaba (Table 3). The SDada did not differ significantly across the drought treatments in both ecotypes of L. chinensis. In the GG ecotype, the SDaba were higher under the WW treatment than under the SD treatment. In the YG ecotype, the SDaba did not differ significantly across the drought treatments (Table 3). In the GG ecotype, SSada were higher under the MD treatment compared to the WW and SD treatments; while the SSaba did not differ significantly across the treatments. In the YG ecotype, the SSada were higher under the WW treatment compared to them in the MD and SD treatments; while the SSaba were higher under the SD treatment compared to the WW and MD treatments (Table 3).

Correlation between photosynthetic induction and steady-state photosynthesis

The stomatal response time of the GG ecotype showed a significant negative correlation with the steady-state photosynthetic rate (Fig. 4a). A significant negative correlation between the steady-state photosynthetic rate and the time to achieve the steady-state photosynthetic rate was detected only in the YG ecotype (Fig. 4b). There were significant dependence of the steady-state photosynthetic rate (Fig. S1a) and T50A (Fig. S1b) on NADP in both GG and YG ecotypes. During the photosynthetic induction period, the A1500 of the GG and YG ecotypes strongly correlated with Rubisco activity (Fig. S2b), but not with the activation rate of Rubisco (Fig. S2a). The time to reach photosynthetic steady state was significantly correlated with the activation rate of Rubisco (Fig. S2c) and the activity of Rubisco (Fig. S3d). We also detected strong dependence of the steady-state photosynthetic rate on the adaxial stomatal density in the GG ecotype (Fig. S3a), and with the adaxial stomatal size in the YG ecotypes (Fig. S3b). Moreover, the stomatal response time of the GG and YG ecotypes was closely related to the abaxial stomatal characteristics (Fig. S3c, d).

Accumulative carbon gain in photosynthetic induction

With the intensification of drought stress, there were significant reduction in the accumulative carbon gain during the induction period (ACG40min) in both GG and YG ecotypes. Under the MD and SD conditions, the GG ecotype had a significantly higher accumulative carbon acquisition ability compared to the YG ecotype (Fig. 5). Piecewise SEM analysis was performed to further investigate the pathways through which soil water content (SWC) impacts the ACG40min of the GG and YG ecotypes. For both ecotypes of L. chinensis, the SWC influenced the mesostate, 1/τR, and stomatal traits, which in turn regulated the steady-state photosynthesis and dynamic photosynthesis, ultimately determining the ACG. Although direct effects of SWC on mesostate, stomatal traits, and enzyme were not significant. The combined effects of SWC on the steady-state photosynthesis and dynamic photosynthesis accounted for 67% of the changes in ACG40min in the GG ecotype (Fig. 6a), with the dynamic photosynthesis having a stronger regulatory effect (Fig. 7b). However, in case of the YG ecotype, the combined effect of SWC on the steady-state photosynthesis and dynamic photosynthesis explained 62% variation of the ACG40min (Fig. 6b), with the steady-state photosynthesis playing a dominant role in inhibiting the ACG (Fig. 7d).

Discussion

Differential effects of soil moisture reduction on leaf carbon gain

Accumulative carbon gain (ACG) during photosynthetic induction is a key parameter for evaluating dynamic photosynthesis, as it depends on both steady-state assimilation (A) and the time required to reach it (TAsteady) [14, 18]. In our study, ACG40min declined progressively with decreasing soil moisture: by 24.8% (GG) and 43.9% (YG) under the moderate drought (MD), and by 55.9% (GG) and 77.5% (YG) under the severe drought (SD) compared with the well-watered (WW) plants (Fig. 5). Under MD, reductions in ACG40min were primarily attributable to prolonged TAsteady, since A1500 did not differ significantly from the WW (Table 1). These patterns indicate that, particularly under MD, the reduction in ACG40min arises mainly from a slower approach to steady-state (longer TAsteady) rather than from a decline in A1500, and are consistent with the idea that time dependent stomatal and biochemical processes contribute to limitations on photosynthetic induction [10]. Under SD, both A1500 and TAsteady contributed to the decline in ACG40min, indicating a combined loss of photosynthetic capacity and slower induction. Although we did not directly assess thylakoid integrity or photosynthetic protein abundance, decreases in steady-state A under SD are consistent with previous reports that severe drought can induce thylakoid membrane disintegration and degradation of photosynthetic proteins, thereby inhibiting photosynthesis [44–46]. Similar reductions in A and increases in stomatal opening time under decreasing soil moisture have been reported in crops and trees [13, 45, 47] as well as in species from arid regions [48].

Ecotypic differences were evident under drought stress. Although A did not differ significantly between the GG and YG ecotypes under the MD and SD, ACG40min of GG was significantly higher than that of YG (Fig. 5). This difference was mainly attributable to TAsteady, which was significantly lower in GG under both MD and SD (Fig. 5). Previous studies indicate that stomatal dynamics play a critical role under drought [19, 24, 49], and that under certain light regimes, species with faster stomatal opening experience smaller reductions in cumulative carbon gain during the induction phase [50]. In our study, the smaller drought-induced increase in TAsteady in GG compared with YG indicates that GG maintained a faster approach to steady-state assimilation under drought. Together with its stomatal traits, this pattern may indicate that stomatal dynamics contribute to the higher drought tolerance of the GG ecotype, consistent with earlier reports that GG exhibits greater physiological integration and higher drought resistance than the YG ecotype [31, 32].

The importance of stomatal traits in photosynthetic induction

During the early stage of photosynthetic induction, the increase in assimilation is mainly constrained by the activation state of Calvin–Benson cycle enzymes such as Rubisco [21, 50, 51]. Over longer time scales (several to tens of minutes), delayed stomatal opening that regulates CO2 supply can further limit the completion of induction [52–54]. Previous studies emphasized the role of stomatal limitation in shaping induction dynamics [25, 50, 55], whereas more recent work has highlighted variation in Rubisco activation lag times [51, 56]. In our study, dynamic photosynthesis was strongly correlated with steady-state photosynthesis for both ecotypes of L. chinensis (Fig. 4), consistent with earlier findings that species with faster induction also achieve higher steady-state rates [57, 58]. During the initial induction phase, higher NADP concentrations were associated with lower assimilation rates and longer times to reach T50A (Fig. S1). Together with their relationships to Rubisco activation rate and activity (Fig. S1), these patterns suggest that the NADP redox state may contribute to reduced assimilation and slower induction. These results indicate that, beyond stomatal morphology, differences in biochemical and regulatory processes, such as NADP redox state and Rubisco activation, are closely linked to variation in photosynthetic induction and stomatal dynamics under fluctuating light [24]. These differences are ultimately controlled by upstream signalling networks (e.g. drought-related hormonal and redox signals), which were not quantified in this study and therefore remain to be elucidated. Our fluorescence data (Fig. S4) suggest that severe drought increased photoinhibition risk in both ecotypes, but did not reveal a consistently greater loss of PSII efficiency in the YG ecotype.

Stomatal size and density are known to influence maximal gs under high light, and several studies have also linked them to variation in photosynthetic induction; however, their contribution under fluctuating light appears species- and context-dependent and is still not fully resolved [12, 25, 59, 60]. In our study, steady-state assimilation was strongly correlated with stomatal traits on the adaxial surface (Fig. S3a, b), whereas the stomatal response time in the GG ecotype was more closely related to traits on the abaxial surface (Fig. S3c, d). Evidence suggests that abaxial stomata are more sensitive to light, particularly blue light, than adaxial stomata [61]. Blue light-induced stomatal opening saturates at low irradiance [62], which corresponds to the conditions used in our gas exchange measurements (10% blue light in 100 µmol m− 2 s− 1 total light). Goh et al. (1995) proposed that the concentration of blue-light photoreceptors is higher in abaxial than in adaxial stomata, although direct evidence remains scarce [63]. Taken together, our findings indicate that the GG ecotype exhibits stronger stomatal regulatory capacity during photosynthetic induction than the YG ecotype, which likely contributes to its superior drought tolerance.

Adaptive value of photosynthetic induction in drought environments

Previous studies have examined carbon acquisition through photosynthetic induction across species [64, 65], evolutionary lineages [50], and plants grown under contrasting light regimes such as sun, shade, or fluctuating conditions [40, 66]. Our results reveal a consistent pattern: the GG ecotype showed greater drought resilience than the YG ecotype in dynamic photosynthetic traits (Fig. 5), with less water loss during induction (Fig. S5). The observed differences in photosynthetic induction across soil moisture levels (Fig. 6) and in the relative contributions of dynamic and steady-state photosynthesis to cumulative carbon gain (Fig. 7) suggest that rapid induction can be an important adaptive mechanism under conditions where fluctuating light interacts with drought stress.

These findings reveal an apparent negative relationship between dynamic and steady-state photosynthetic traits: ecotypes with shorter TAsteady tended to show lower A1500, whereas those with higher A1500 exhibited slower induction. Because TAsteady is calculated from both A1500 and A100, part of this pattern likely reflects mathematical dependence rather than a purely physiological trade-off [67]. Nevertheless, within this constraint, the contrasting combinations of TAsteady and A1500 observed in the two ecotypes are consistent with divergent strategies along a continuum from faster induction to higher steady-state capacity. Importantly, most previous research has emphasized steady-state photosynthesis under high light [56, 68, 69], whereas our study underscores the ecological relevance of photosynthetic induction. The contrasting strategies of the GG and YG ecotypes highlight the adaptive value of dynamic photosynthesis in drought environments and point to a potential contribution of induction dynamics to ecotypic differentiation in semi-arid grasslands where water limitation and fluctuating light jointly shape ecosystem function.

Conclusions

This study examined how soil moisture reduction affects photosynthetic induction in two ecotypes of Leymus chinensis. We found that drought significantly constrained steady-state photosynthesis and photosynthetic induction times, but the GG ecotype maintained higher cumulative carbon gain during induction by combining faster stomatal responses with enhanced biochemical capacity. The apparent negative relationship between steady-state and dynamic photosynthetic traits suggests that the two ecotypes adopt distinct, context-dependent strategies to cope with water stress. These findings apply to short-term photosynthetic induction of potted plants under controlled light and soil-moisture conditions and highlight the ecological importance of photosynthetic induction in variable environments. Future work should evaluate whether similar ecotypic differences and apparent trade-offs occur at whole-plant and community scales under natural field conditions, and test whether these patterns are conserved across other grass species and functional groups in drought-vulnerable ecosystems.
