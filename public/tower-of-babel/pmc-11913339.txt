Aspirin for prophylaxis of VTE in patients with Hip/ Knee replacement: Systematic review and Meta-analysis of Non-Randomized studies
Authors: Raghava Kalyan Maddukuri, Hema Chava, Sri Tejaswi Kondaveeti, Mounika Venkata Mutthineni, Bindu Priyanka Vegesana
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aspirin as an agent for thromboprophylaxis in patients with total knee replacement (TKR) and total hip replacement (THR) is gaining a lot of importance owing to its efficacy and safety in preventing venous thromboembolism (VTE) complications. The current guidelines do not recommend aspirin over other anticoagulants as the data from the meta-analysis of randomized controlled trails (RCTs) lacked a significant sample to draw conclusive results. The present study was aimed to carry out a systematic review and meta-analysis of nonrandomized studies (NRSs) to determine the effect of aspirin as prophylaxis for VTE. A complete electronic search was conducted at PubMed, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Google Scholar for relevant articles published till March 2021. Any postsurgical VTE event (deep vein thrombosis and/or pulmonary embolism) is considered the primary outcome and adverse events as secondary outcomes. Both efficacy and safety outcomes were reported as pooled risk estimates with 95% confidence interval (CI) with a level of significance at P < 0.05. A total of 21 studies were identified for the analysis. The overall risk of occurrence of VTE among the patients taking aspirin was not significantly different from anticoagulants (risk ratio [RR]: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.52–1.15). Patients who underwent THR had a higher risk for VTE with aspirin (RR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.35–1.61), whereas the patients who underwent TKR showed a lower risk of VTE with aspirin (RR: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.75–0.85). Meta-analysis of NRS advocates the role of aspirin as a prophylactic agent for VTE, especially for patients who are in need for TKR. Further RCTs are required to reestablish the role of aspirin, especially in patients undergoing THR.

Introduction

Aging, an inevitable event of life, increases the burden of osteoarthritis of the hip and knee. Both these forms of osteoarthritis are a major contributors for disability-adjusted life years throughout the globe.[1] Surgical interventions such as total hip replacement (THR) and total knee replacement (TKR) were proven highly effective in patients suffering from osteoarthritis.[23] However, both the above-mentioned procedures are associated with a risk for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE), which together constitute venous thromboembolism (VTE).[4] Since VTE is associated with an increased cost of health care along with significant mortality[5] and being considered as a preventable cause of hospitalization and death,[6] there is a definitive need for thromboprophylaxis in patients undergoing joint replacement therapy.[7]

The role of aspirin as a thromboprophylactic agent in patients with TKR and THR is gaining a lot of importance owing to its efficacy and safety in preventing VTE complications.[8] Aspirin also outweighs the standard anticoagulants used for VTE prophylaxis (low molecular weight heparin [LMWH], unfractionated heparin [UH], apixaban, dabigatran, rivaroxaban, etc.,) in having less risk for bleeding and wound-related problems.[8] For the first time, monotherapy with aspirin for VTE prophylaxis after THR and TKR was endorsed in guidelines given by the American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) Organization in 2012. However, aspirin was recommended at the same level of evidence as that of other anticoagulants (LMWH and newer agents).[9] Similarly, the guidelines released by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in 2018 endorsed aspirin monotherapy along with LMWH and newer agents for the prevention of VTE only for those patients who underwent TKR. In case of patients undergoing THR, aspirin is only given after 10 days of LMWH therapy.[10]

None of the current guidelines recommend aspirin over other anticoagulants for thromboprophylaxis of VTE, and this can be related to the fact that none of the data from the meta-analysis of randomized controlled trails (RCTs) supported the superiority of aspirin over other agents.[1112] A very recent meta-analysis that included the largest RCT conducted on THR and TKR patients[13] to date, concluded that VTE outcomes were not statistically significant between aspirin and other anticoagulants.[12] However, the total sample considered in the above meta-analysis was only 6060 and it concluded by saying that large RCT’s are needed for further validation.[12]

Nonrandomized studies (NRSs) conducted on VTE prophylaxis in patients undergoing TKR and THR do provide a very large sample population compared to RCTs. Although RCTs were considered over NRS as they provide unbiased information, systematic review and meta-analysis of NRS can be carried out to supplement the evidence of the existing RCTs.[14] Owing to factors such as ease of administration, no requirement for blood monitoring, and cheaper in cost,[15] further research can be carried out to look for the superiority of aspirin for thromboprophylaxis in patients undergoing THR and TKR. At this juncture, we carried out a systematic review and meta-analysis of NRS to determine the clinical efficacy and safety of aspirin as this was not done any time before. Any positive findings in this study can be considered as an impetus for carrying out larger RCTs in the coming days.

Methodology

Inclusion criteria

All the nonrandomized, observational studies that compared the efficacy and safety outcomes pertaining to the prevention of VT between aspirin and other anticoagulants among THR and/or TKR patients were included in the analysis. To reflect the current practice of aspirin use, studies using extended aspirin regimen (e.g., an initial course of LMWH/rivaroxaban for 5–7 days followed by extended course of aspirin) were also included.[11] Studies where the patients used mechanical prophylaxis (pneumatic compression devices) along with aspirin or anticoagulant therapy were also included as long as the devices were used in both cohorts.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded articles that did not have proper information about the number of VTE outcomes after surgical prophylaxis with aspirin and anticoagulants. Furthermore, we did not include articles that did not mention VTE outcomes in terms of incidence and risk ratios (RRs). We also excluded all other research designs such as RCTs, systematic reviews, and meta-analysis.

Search strategy

A full electronic search was carried out by two authors (MM and BP) at PubMed, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Google Scholar for all the related studies published till March 2021. It was carried out using a combination of keywords related to the population (hip replacement, hip arthroplasty, knee replacement, knee arthroplasty, and total joint arthroplasty), intervention (aspirin, anticoagulants, UF, LMWH, warfarin, dabigatran, enoxaparin, coumadin, Factor Xa inhibitors, rivaroxaban, and apixaban), and outcomes of interest (VTE, DVT, PE, major bleeding events, bleeding complications, and mortality). We limited our search to articles published in English language. Titles and abstracts of the obtained articles were independently screened by another two authors (HC and SK) to select relevant articles as per the selection criteria. Full texts of the relevant articles were obtained, and the list of references in these articles was again reviewed for any additional and nonindexed articles.

Study outcomes

Any postsurgical VTE event (DVT and/or PE) is considered the primary outcome in this meta-analysis. Adverse events reported in patients receiving aspirin and anticoagulant prophylactic therapy were considered secondary outcomes.

Data extraction

A data collection form was designed to collect all the required data from the selected studies. Baseline demographic characteristics, geographic location, number of patients enrolled, type of surgical intervention, study design, number of VTE, DVT, and PE events, major bleeding events, bleeding complications, and mortality events were extracted from each study. Furthermore, information required for the quality appraisal of the included studies was also collected.

Quality assessment

Two authors carried out the process of evaluating the quality of individual studies included in the analysis using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for cohort studies.[16] This scale comprises three parameters for assessing the quality of published articles, namely, selection, comparability, and outcome. Fixed number of points was allocated to these parameters, i.e., a maximum four, two, and three points for selection, comparability, and outcome, respectively. The studies will be graded as good, fair, and poor according to the scale. Good-quality studies should score 3 or 4 stars in selection domain and 1 or 2 stars in comparability domain and 2 or 3 stars in outcome/exposure domain, whereas fair quality studies require 2 stars in selection domain and 1 or 2 stars in comparability domain and 2 or 3 stars in outcome/exposure domain. If the study scores 0 or 1 star in the selection domain or 0 stars in the comparability domain or 0 or 1 stars in outcome/exposure domain, it is considered as poor quality.

Statistical analysis

We used Revman version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen 2020) to calculate RR for both efficacy and safety outcomes. Based on the reported VTE events, risk estimates were calculated for all the individual studies included for the analysis, and the pooled risk estimate was calculated using random effects model (Dersimonian and Laird method). Cochrane Q (χ2) statistics and I2 index were used for the assessment of heterogeneity among the study. P < 0.10 and I2 > 50% were considered significant for heterogeneity according to Cochrane Q (χ2) statistic and I2 index, respectively.[17] Begg’s funnel plots were used for the assessment of publication bias.[18] In few studies, the intervention with aspirin and anticoagulant was based on risk stratification, i.e., patients who were having less risk for VTE were taking aspirin, whereas patients having a higher risk for VTE were taking anticoagulant. The inclusion of these studies may influence the overall risk estimate and therefore sensitivity analysis was carried out for those studies where administration with aspirin and anticoagulant was not based on risk stratification. If the risk estimate obtained after carrying out sensitivity analysis does not differ with the overall risk estimate, the overall risk estimate can be considered with a greater degree of certainty.[19] Both efficacy and safety outcomes were reported as RRs with 95% confidence interval (CI), and P < 5% (P < 0.05) was considered statistically significant. Further, subgroup analysis was carried out for parameters such as joint type (THR vs. TKR), type of endpoint (DVT vs. PE), and individual comparator vs. aspirin.

Results

Study identification

We obtained 810 articles after the initial search. After the removal of duplicates, a total of 274 were identified, out of which 43 relevant studies were considered for full-text assessment after going through the titles and abstracts. After reviewing the full texts of these 43 articles, 22 were excluded with reason and the remaining 21[202122232425262728293031323334353637383940] articles were included for analysis [Figure 1].

Quality assessment

After applying Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale for cohort studies, 16 studies[21222324252627293132333435363940] were of good quality, and 5 studies[2028303738] were of poor quality.

Study outcomes

The overall risk of VTE in patients receiving aspirin had no significant difference when compared to anticoagulants (RR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.55–1.20) [Figure 2]. Heterogeneity among the included studies was found to be high (97%). The overall risk of DVT (15 studies) was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.51–1.40) and the overall risk for PE (17 studies) was 1.36 (95% CI: 0.49–3.77), indicating no significant difference between aspirin and anticoagulants in the incidence of either form of VTE [Supplementary Figures 1 and 2].

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was carried out for studies,[202122232425262931323334353740] in which administration of aspirin and anticoagulants was done irrespective of risk stratification, and the overall risk of VTE events in those studies was found to be 0.96 (95% CI: 0.62–1.48). This indicates that the reduction in VTE events is not significant in either of the two groups, i.e. aspirin and anticoagulant, a finding similar to overall risk estimate obtained with all the included studies [Table 3].

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis of individual study characteristics was performed to detect for effect modification. A key finding among the subgroup analysis was the outcome seen in the subgroup of joint replacement surgery type (THR vs. TKR). Patients undergoing THR had a higher risk for VTE with aspirin (RR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.35–1.61), whereas patients undergoing TKR had reduced risk for VTE with aspirin (RR: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.75–0.85). Not all studies reported VTE outcomes for individual comparator anticoagulant drugs when multiple anticoagulants were taken in the comparator group. Subgroup analysis was carried out for those studies where data regarding the VTE outcomes for all individual comparator drugs were given. In the studies which compared aspirin with warfarin, there was a lower risk for VTE in the aspirin group which was statistically significant (RR: 0.5 95% CI: 0.29–0.85). Similarly, VTE risk associated with aspirin when compared to LMWH and Factor Xa inhibitors was found to be 1.34 (95% CI: 0.72–2.49) and 1.69 (95% CI: 0.77–3.70), respectively. Both the above findings were not significant for the increased risk of VTE in patients taking aspirin [Table 3].

Adverse outcomes

Reporting of adverse events associated with VTE prophylaxis was not uniform among all the studies included for analysis. Mortality[23242631363940] and major bleeding[2226293134383940] were the most commonly reported adverse events followed by bleeding complications.[212526283637] The overall risk associated with the above-mentioned adverse events did not differ significantly among the patients receiving aspirin versus anticoagulants [Supplementary Figure 3].

Publication bias

In the present meta-analysis, visual examination of Begg’s funnel plot revealed asymmetry for the studies included in the analysis [Supplementary Figure 4]. Along with publication bias, heterogeneity is also responsible for asymmetric funnel plot.[41] As mentioned above, there was high heterogeneity (97%) among the studies included in this meta-analysis owing to the complexity of intervention, underlying risk, etc. Risk stratification was identified to be an important cause for funnel plot asymmetry. The sensitivity analysis, excluding these studies, showed results consistent with the main analysis. Therefore, it can be concluded that studies with extreme effects in the funnel plot did not alter the overall risk estimate [Supplementary Figure 5].[42]

Discussion

Our meta-analysis of non-randomized studies shows no significant difference in VTE event reduction between patients taking aspirin and those taking anticoagulants. These findings were statistically significant and consistent for both forms of VTE (DVT and PE). Furthermore, no difference was observed among the aspirin and anticoagulant groups in terms of adverse events associated with the therapy such as major bleeding and bleeding complications. Since not all adverse events were reported uniformly in all the studies included in the analysis, careful interpretation of the results is needed. All the above-mentioned findings were similar to the recent meta-analysis of RCTs,[12] which reported that aspirin was not statistically significantly different from other anticoagulants both in terms of efficacy and safety. As previously mentioned, findings from meta-analysis of NRSs can be used to supplement the evidence of existing RCTs,[14] and the evidence obtained from our meta-analysis of NRS is derived from a total sample population of 106,924. This sample population is very high when compared to somewhat lower population of 6060 in the meta-analysis of RCTs.[12] The evidence obtained from our meta-analysis of NRS also supports the role of aspirin as prophylactic agent for VTE, especially for those patients undergoing TKR. An important finding that was observed in our analysis among the patients undergoing THR and TKR taking aspirin was the difference in VTE outcomes. Reduction in the risk of VTE outcomes was seen with aspirin when compared to anticoagulants in patients who underwent TKR, while patients who underwent THR had an increased risk for VTE with aspirin compared to anticoagulants. This finding was not consistent with the findings of meta-analysis of RCTs,[12] where VTE outcomes remained constant among both THR and TKR patients. This is considered as an important finding in our analysis as it supports the recommendations made by NICE[10] which did not recommend aspirin monotherapy as prophylaxis for patients undergoing THR. Again, as previously mentioned, NRS do suffer with potential bias compared to RCTs, and therefore, these findings direct toward robust and well-designed RCTs to look for VTE outcomes with aspirin after THR. The recent meta-analysis of RCTs[12] also mentioned the same point as the majority of RCTs included in their meta-analysis suffered with a high risk of bias. In light of the current evidence, ACCP guidelines[9] which recommend aspirin monotherapy for VTE prophylaxis in patients undergoing both THR and TKR may require further evaluation to reestablish its role after THR. Aspirin being superior to Warfarin in reducing VTE outcomes is also an important finding as it was not reported in any of the previous studies.

Limitations

Other than the potential bias associated with NRS compared to RCTs, there were a couple of limitations to our meta-analysis. The overall identification and reporting of adverse events were low and not all studies reported the adverse events associated with VTE prophylaxis. This makes it difficult to interpret the safety data.

Strengths

Meta-analysis of RCTs for VTE prophylaxis with aspirin versus anticoagulant always has limited sample population for analysis, considering the clinical condition (THR and TKR), the complexity of prophylaxis therapy (aspirin vs. anticoagulants), and its outcomes (DVT, PE). This being the first meta-analysis of NRS and with a large sample population, it can surely augment the findings of meta-analysis of RCTs and can be used as a tool of evaluation for the results obtained from meta-analysis of RCTs.

Conclusion

All the available data from NRS do not prove the superiority of aspirin but have enough evidence to say that aspirin is not statistically different from anticoagulant therapy in reducing the overall risk of VTE. Aspirin was found to be effective over anticoagulants in reducing the risk of VTE after TKR. Further research with large RCTs is needed to prove the same and also to reestablish the role of aspirin in reducing VTE events in THR.
