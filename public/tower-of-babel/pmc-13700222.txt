Influence of commonly consumed beverages on surface roughness and color stability of resin-based composites before and after thermocycling
Authors: Melisa Sahin, Dila Ozyilkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrequent consumption of acidic and chromogenic beverages is common among children and may affect the longevity of direct restorations. Resin-based composites with differing resin matrix microstructures may exhibit distinct levels of resistance to discoloration and surface changes. However, scientific data regarding this issue is lacking. The purpose of this in vitro study was to evaluate the influence of commonly consumed beverages on the surface roughness average (Ra) and color properties (color difference [ΔE00] and relative translucency parameter [RTP00]) of resin-based composites.Materials and methodsA total of 96 specimens (10 mm in diameter × 2 mm in thickness) were fabricated from 3 resin-based composites: An ormocer-based composite (Admira Fusion 5), a nanohybrid composite (Grandio SO), and a microhybrid composite (Filtek Z250). Measurements were obtained at three sequential phases: (1) baseline, (2) after thermocycling, and (3) after immersion in commonly consumed beverages. At baseline, the color coordinates of the specimens were recorded using a spectrophotometer, and Ra values were measured using a contact profilometer (Phase I). Following 5,000 thermocycles, color coordinates and Ra values were measured again (Phase II). Subsequently, all specimens were immersed for 6 days in commonly consumed beverages—distilled water (control), cola, cocoa milk, and iced tea—and the same measurements were repeated (Phase III). Color coordinates measured at each phase were used not only to determine ΔE00 between the experimental phases but also to compute RTP00 values at each phase. Data were statistically analyzed (α = 0.05).ResultsThermocycling significantly affected RTP00 and ΔE00 values of the tested composites, although its effect on surface roughness was not significant. Dental composite material significantly affected Ra, RTP00, and thermocycling-related ΔE00 values. During beverage immersion, solution type significantly affected ΔE00 values, with a significant material × solution interaction indicating material-dependent staining behavior. Grandio SO exhibited the highest discoloration after cola and iced tea immersion, whereas Admira Fusion 5 demonstrated greater color stability across beverages.ConclusionResin-based composites responded differently to thermocycling and beverage exposure according to their resin matrix composition. The ormocer-based composite showed better color stability, although the nanohybrid composite was more susceptible to translucency changes and beverage-induced discoloration.

Introduction

Traditional resin composites are extensively used direct restorative materials in contemporary pediatric dentistry owing to their favorable esthetic characteristics, adhesive properties, and reliable mechanical performance [1]. Conventional resin composites consist mainly of an organic resin matrix, inorganic filler particles, a silane coupling agent, and photoinitiator systems [2]. The organic matrix generally comprises dimethacrylate monomers including bisphenol A- glycidyl methacrylate (Bis-GMA), urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA), and triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA), that polymerize via a free-radical addition reaction following irradiation with blue light [3, 4]. The inorganic filler component, such as silica, quartz, zirconia, or glass particles with different particle sizes, is added to enhance mechanical strength, wear resistance, radiopacity, and handling characteristics while decreasing polymerization shrinkage by lowering the proportion of resin matrix [5].

In pediatric restorative dentistry, conventional composites are widely used for anterior restorations, Class I and Class II posterior restorations, preventive resin restorations, and minimally invasive cavity designs as they allow conservative tooth preparation via micromechanical bonding to enamel and dentin [6–8]. Their ability to bond directly to dental tissues allows improved preservation of sound tooth structure, which is particularly important in primary teeth where the remaining tooth structure is limited [9]. Modern adhesive systems support this approach by creating micromechanical retention through acid etching and hybrid layer formation, thus enabling durable restorations even in small or moderate-sized cavities [10]. One of the major advantages of traditional composites is their good esthetic properties. Advances in filler technology and optical properties enable contemporary nanohybrid and microhybrid composites to closely match natural tooth translucency, fluorescence, and surface texture. Also, resin composites show favorable polishability and color stability upon proper finishing and maintenance [10–14]. On the other hand, conventional composites have well-recognized limitations, mainly related to polymerization shrinkage. When light curing is applied, conversion of monomer molecules into a polymer network results in volumetric contraction, which can generate significant shrinkage stress at the tooth-restoration interface. This may lead to marginal gap formation, enamel microcracks, postoperative sensitivity, marginal discoloration, and microleakage, which may increase the risk of recurrent caries [15–17]. In primary teeth, where enamel and dentin are thinner and less mineralized than in permanent teeth, these effects may be more clinically significant. Other drawbacks in pediatric use are technique sensitivity and reduced biocompatibility due to residual monomer release in the presence of incomplete polymerization [18].

Organically modified ceramic (Ormocer) materials represent an advanced type of restorative biomaterial developed to overcome limitations of conventional methacrylate-based resin composites [19]. The term Ormocer is used to describe its hybrid molecular structure composed of an inorganic siloxane network combined with organically polymerizable side chains. Unlike traditional resin composites, which mainly rely on monomers such as Bis-GMA, UDMA, or TEGDMA, ormocer-based materials have a three-dimensional inorganic-organic copolymer matrix that contributes to improved dimensional stability and reduced polymerization stress [20]. The fundamental structural feature of Ormocer materials is the incorporation of a silicon–oxygen (Si–O–Si) backbone, forming the inorganic framework, while methacrylate-functional organic groups attached to this backbone enable light-activated polymerization [21]. This structure enables the formation of a densely cross-linked network during curing, which results in lower volumetric contraction than conventional resin-based composites [22]. Reduced polymerization shrinkage is clinically important as shrinkage stress generated during curing may compromise the adhesive interface, which may lead to marginal gap formation, microleakage, postoperative sensitivity, and eventual secondary caries [23]. A further advantage of Ormocer materials is the decreased release of residual monomers. Conventional resin composites may release unreacted monomers such as Bisphenol-A derivatives or other methacrylate components, which may raise concerns about cytotoxicity and biocompatibility. Ormocer materials, particularly newer generations (e.g., Admira Fusion), are designed to minimize conventional monomer content [24]. This has led to them being described as highly biocompatible restorative materials, which is especially important in pediatric dentistry where long-term exposure and developing tissues represent critical considerations [25].

In pediatric dentistry, the restorative treatment of primary and young permanent teeth is associated with several clinical challenges, such as limited treatment time, difficulty keeping the field dry, variable levels of patient cooperation, and the need for restorative materials with adequate durability and esthetics [26]. Under these circumstances, Ormocer composites have attracted increasing attention because their lower polymerization shrinkage may improve marginal adaptation and reduce the risk of microleakage, which is particularly critical in primary teeth due to their thinner enamel and dentin structure [27]. Additionally, frequent consumption of acidic and chromogenic beverages is common among children [28–30]. In terms of color stability and surface topography, conventional methacrylate-based resin composites may exhibit limited long-term optical and surface performance [31–33]. Low water absorption, minimized shrinkage, and high filler content of Ormocer materials may contribute to improved color stability and surface characteristics, thereby improving the longevity of direct restoration [34]. However, evidence regarding the influence of commonly consumed beverages on the optical and surface properties of ormocer-based composites in comparison with conventional counterparts remains scarce. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no previous study has simultaneously evaluated the effects of thermocycling and commonly consumed beverages on both surface roughness and color-related parameters (ΔE00 and RTP00) of the ormocer-based composites. Therefore, the purpose of this in vitro study was to evaluate the influence of commonly consumed beverages on the surface roughness average (Ra) and color properties (color difference [ΔE00] and relative translucency parameter [RTP00]) of resin-based composites (an ormocer-based composite [Admira Fusion 5 / AF5], a nanohybrid composite [Grandio SO / G-SO], and a microhybrid composite [Filtek Z250 / F-Z250]). Specimen preparation

A priori power analysis was performed using a software program (G*Power software v.3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) with a statistical power of 0.85, an effect size of f = 0.25, and a significance level of α = 0.05. This analysis indicated that a total of 96 specimens were required, which were allocated into 12 groups of 8 specimens each.

A total of 96 disc-shaped specimens (10 mm in diameter × 2 mm in thickness) were fabricated from 3 different resin-based composites with different resin matrix compositions: An ormocer-based composite (AF5), a nanohybrid composite (G-SO), and a microhybrid composite (F-Z250). Each composite material was inserted into a cylindrical plexiglas mold and covered with Mylar strips on both sides to obtain a smooth and oxygen-inhibition–free surface. Light pressure was manually applied prior to polymerization to eliminate voids and ensure uniform specimen thickness. Polymerization was performed using a LED curing device (Woodpecker DTE LED Curing Light, China) with a light intensity of 1000 mW/cm². According to the manufacturers’ instructions, AF5 was cured for 10 s, whereas G-SO and F-Z250 were cured for 20 s [35–37]. Subsequently, finishing and polishing steps were carried out on the specimens to standardize the surfaces. Polishing was done using an abrasive disc system (Super-Snap Rainbow Kit; Shofu Inc., Kyoto, Japan) under continuous water cooling supplied by the dental unit at a constant speed of 10,000 rpm. For each specimen surface, medium, fine, and superfine discs were applied for 15 s. All procedures were done by the same operator (M.Ş.). Measurements were recorded at three sequential phases: (1) baseline, (2) after thermocycling, and (3) after immersion in commonly consumed beverages.

Phase I - color and surface roughness measurements

At baseline, the color coordinates of the specimens were recorded using a spectrophotometer (Vita Easyshade Advance, Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) under standardized D65 illumination with a 2° standard observer in a windowless, climatically controlled environment maintained at 25 °C. For ΔE00 analysis, color coordinates were recorded on a gray background using a gray photographic card (L = 25.7, a = 2.8, b = 8.4) [38]. For RTP00 analysis, color coordinates were recorded on white and black backgrounds during Phase I. The device was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions prior to each measurement session and recalibrated following each change in background color (white, gray, and black). Three measurements were obtained from each specimen, and the arithmetic mean was used for statistical analysis. where the subscripts “B” and “W” for L’, C’ and H’ refer to lightness, chroma, and hue of each layer over the black and the white backgrounds, respectively. At baseline, Ra values were also measured using a contact profilometer (Perthometer M2, Mahr GmbH, Göttingen, Germany) with a tip diameter of 2.4 mm, a measurement speed of 0.5 mm/s, and a tracking length of 5.5 mm. Three measurements were obtained from each specimen, and the arithmetic mean was used for statistical analysis.

Phase II - color and surface roughness measurements

All specimens were subjected to thermal aging using a thermal cycling device (SD Mechatronik Thermocycler, SD Mechatronik GMBH, Westerham, Germany) for a total of 5000 cycles between 5 and 55 °C, with a dwell time of 30 s per cycle. Ra and RTP00 values were recalculated using the methods described in Phase I. For ΔE00 analysis, color coordinates were re-recorded on a gray background during Phase II using the method mentioned at Phase I. where ΔL’, ΔC’, and ΔH’ represent the differences in lightness, chroma, and hue between the two measurement phases. ΔE00 units of 0.80 and 1.80 were regarded as perceptibility and acceptability thresholds [39].

Phase III - color and surface roughness measurements

In the last phase, all specimens were immersed for 6 days in commonly consumed beverages—distilled water (control, pH 7.0), cola (Coca-Cola®, The Coca-Cola Company, Turkey; pH 2.5), cocoa milk (Pınar Kido Cocoa Milk, Turkey; pH 5.0), and iced tea (Lipton Ice Tea Peach, Turkey; pH 4.0–4.5). Immersion tests were done in an incubator maintained at a temperature of 37 °C. Each specimen was placed in a 10 mL solution. The immersion tests lasted for 6 days, which has been reported to correspond to approximately six months of in vivo exposure [40]. After every 24 h, the immersion solution was replaced to reduce the growth of bacteria in the solution. After completing the immersion test, the specimens were rinsed in distilled water for 10 s. Ra and RTP00 values were recalculated using the methods mentioned at Phase I. For ΔE00 analysis, color coordinates were re-recorded on a gray background during Phase III using the method mentioned at Phase I. ΔE00 values between Phase II and Phase III were then calculated by using the ΔE00 formula.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were done using a software program (IBM SPSS Statistics, v23; IBM Corp) at a significance level of α = 0.05. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. For Ra and RTP00, analyses were conducted in 2 sequential phases according to the experimental design. In the first phase, baseline (Phase I) and post-thermocycling (Phase II) values were analyzed using mixed repeated-measures ANOVA, with measurement phase as the within-subject factor and composite material as the between-subject factor. In the second phase, post-thermocycling (Phase II) and post-immersion (Phase III) values were analyzed using mixed repeated-measures ANOVA, with measurement phase as the within-subject factor and composite material and solution type as between-subject factors.

ΔE00 between baseline and post-thermocycling measurements (Phase I–II) were analyzed using one-way ANOVA according to composite material. ΔE00 values between post-thermocycling and post-immersion measurements (Phase II–III) were analyzed using two-way ANOVA, with composite material and solution type as fixed factors. When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc tests. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η²p).

Results

Interpretation of Ra values

Ra values measured at baseline and after thermocycling were analyzed using a mixed repeated-measures ANOVA with measurement phase as the within-subject factor and composite material as the between-subject factor (Table 2). No significant overall effect of thermocycling was observed on Ra values (P = 0.347), indicating that thermocycling alone did not significantly alter surface roughness across the tested composites. Similarly, the interaction between phase and composite material was not significant (P = 0.403), suggesting that the effect of thermocycling did not differ among the materials. In contrast, a significant main effect of composite material was detected (P = 0.004; partial η² = 0.114), indicating inherent variations in surface roughness among the composites irrespective of measurement phase. Descriptive data showed that AF5 exhibited higher Ra values at both baseline (0.25 ± 0.13 μm) and after thermocycling (0.25 ± 0.13 μm) compared with F-Z250 (0.18 ± 0.20 μm and 0.12 ± 0.09 μm, respectively) and G-SO (0.18 ± 0.16 μm and 0.18 ± 0.18 μm, respectively).

In the second phase, Ra values obtained after thermocycling and after beverage immersion were analyzed using a mixed repeated-measures ANOVA including measurement phase as the within-subject factor and composite material and immersion solution as between-subject factors (Table 3). Interpretation of RTP00 values

RTP00 values obtained at baseline (Phase I) and after thermocycling (Phase II) were analyzed using a mixed repeated-measures ANOVA with measurement phase as the within-subject factor and composite material as the between-subject factor (Table 5). A significant overall effect of phase was observed (P = 0.001; partial η² = 0.372), suggesting that thermocycling significantly changed RTP00 values overall. Furthermore, the interaction between phase and composite material was significant (P = 0.001; partial η² = 0.466), showing that the extent of RTP00 change after thermocycling varied across the evaluated composites. A significant effect of composite material was also observed (P = 0.001; partial η² = 0.253), suggesting that intrinsic differences in translucency existed among materials regardless of measurement phase. Descriptive results showed that AF5 revealed a slight decrease in RTP00 following thermocycling (from 3.05 ± 0.69 to 2.87 ± 0.70), while F-Z250 remained relatively stable (3.67 ± 0.76 to 3.68 ± 0.73). By comparison, G-SO demonstrated the greatest reduction, with RTP00 decreasing from 4.63 ± 1.10 at baseline to 3.04 ± 0.53 after thermocycling.

Mixed repeated-measures ANOVA for Phase I and Phase II RTP00 values

In the second phase of analysis, RTP00 values measured after thermocycling (Phase II) and after beverage immersion (Phase III) were analyzed using a mixed repeated-measures ANOVA incorporating measurement phase as the within-subject factor and composite material and immersion solution as between-subject factors (Table 6). Discussion

The present study evaluated the effects of thermocycling and subsequent immersion in commonly consumed beverages on the surface roughness, translucency, and color stability of three resin-based restorative materials representing different matrix technologies: the ormocer-based composite AF5, the nanohybrid composite G-SO, and the microhybrid composite F-Z250. Because thermocycling preceded beverage exposure in the experimental design, the optical and surface outcomes were analyzed sequentially in two phases, allowing independent interpretation of thermal aging and immersion-related effects.

H0-1, stating that thermocycling and beverage immersion would have no statistically significant effect on ΔE00 and RTP00 values, was rejected because thermocycling significantly affected both ΔE00 and RTP00, and immersion also produced significant changes in RTP00 as well as solution-dependent differences in ΔE00. H0-2, which proposed that immersion in different beverages would not produce statistically significant differences in ΔE00 values, was rejected, as solution type significantly influenced post-immersion ΔE00 values. H0-3, suggesting that the effect of beverages on color stability would not differ among composites with different resin matrix compositions, was also rejected because a significant composite material × solution interaction was identified for ΔE00, indicating material-dependent staining behavior. By contrast, H0-4, which proposed that thermocycling and immersion would not have a statistically significant effect on surface roughness, was partially rejected: thermocycling alone did not significantly affect Ra values, while immersion-related roughness changes varied significantly depending on composite material; however, solution type itself did not show a significant overall effect.

In terms of surface roughness, thermocycling alone did not significantly affect Ra values, while a significant effect of composite material was observed. This suggests that material composition had a stronger influence on surface topography than thermal cycling itself [41–43]. The consistently higher Ra values in AF5 may be due to the structural features of ormocer-based matrices. Although ormocer systems are designed to reduce polymerization shrinkage through inorganic-organic copolymer architecture, their filler-matrix interaction and surface finishing behavior may differ from methacrylate-based composites, which may result in higher baseline roughness [23, 44–46]. The lack of significant thermocycling-induced roughness increase suggests that 5,000 thermal cycles did not lead to sufficient matrix degradation or filler debonding to noticeably affect surface texture under the present conditions [47].

By contrast, the second phase showed a significant interaction between measurement phase and composite material, indicating that immersion-related roughness changes varied depending on the composite material. The absence of a significant solution effect suggests that beverages did not have a uniform degradative influence across all composites; instead, the response depended on material composition [41–43]. This was most evident in F-Z250, which showed high variability after immersion, particularly after immersion in iced tea. Microhybrid composites generally contain larger filler particles and a wider filler size distribution, making them more prone to uneven surface degradation when water sorption and pigment uptake occur together. By comparison, G-SO showed the lowest roughness values after immersion, likely due to its high filler loading and nanoscale filler distribution providing greater surface stability and resistance to matrix softening [14, 48–50].

RTP00 results showed that thermocycling significantly affected translucency, with a strong interaction between phase and composite material. Among the tested materials, G-SO exhibited the greatest reduction in RTP00 after thermocycling. This suggests that thermal aging had a greater effect on light transmission in the nanohybrid material, likely due to water uptake at the filler-matrix interface and resulting refractive index mismatch. As translucency depends on the optical matching between resin matrix and fillers, even minor hydrolytic changes can markedly affect RTP00 [51, 52]. Conversely, F-Z250 showed nearly unchanged RTP00 values after thermocycling, suggesting greater optical stability under thermal stress.

Following beverage immersion, RTP00 again showed a significant effect of phase and a significant interaction with composite material, while solution type alone was not significant. These findings indicate that immersion altered translucency mainly depending on material-specific behavior rather than beverage chemistry. The lack of a significant solution effect suggests that short-term exposure to different beverages may influence optical properties mainly through water sorption rather than pigment penetration from beverages [53, 54]. Nevertheless, descriptive values showed that distilled water produced higher RTP00 values in some groups, particularly for AF5, indicating that water sorption alone may alter internal light scattering in ormocer-based structures.

Color stability analysis demonstrated that thermocycling significantly affected ΔE00 values depending on composite type. F-Z250 showed the highest thermocycling-induced color change, whereas AF5 showed the lowest ΔE00 values, suggesting greater resistance to thermal color alteration. The lower thermocycling-related discoloration of the ormocer material may be due to its lower content of conventional dimethacrylate monomers such as Bis-GMA and TEGDMA, which contribute to water sorption and hydrolytic color instability. Similarly, previous studies have shown that ormocer-based nanohybrid composite materials exhibited less color change after exposure to staining agents compared with methacrylate or UDMA-based materials [44, 45].

In the immersion phase, solution type significantly influenced ΔE00 values, and a strong composite material × solution interaction was identified. This demonstrates that beverage-related discoloration was highly dependent on composite composition. G-SO showed the greatest susceptibility to staining in cola and iced tea, whereas cocoa milk and distilled water produced lower ΔE00 values. This may be attributed to both pigment adsorption and acidic degradation of the superficial resin matrix [28–30]. Cola contains low pH and chromogenic compounds that can promote matrix softening [49], while iced tea contains tannins with strong staining potential [55]. In contrast, AF5 showed relatively stable ΔE00 values across all beverages, supporting the hypothesis that ormocer-based matrices may exhibit lower pigment affinity due to reduced hydrophilic monomer content.

Paravina et al. [56] reported a ΔE00=0.8 as the 50% perceptibility threshold and a ΔE00=1.8 as the acceptability threshold. From a clinical perspective, most ΔE00 values exceeded commonly accepted perceptibility thresholds, indicating that discoloration would be visually detectable under clinical conditions. Several groups approached or exceeded the acceptability threshold, notably G-SO after iced tea and cola immersion, suggesting that beverage exposure may have clinically relevant consequences for long-term esthetics depending on composite selection.

Previous studies have reported that dental check-ups are mostly carried out at six-month intervals in clinical practice. The literature reports that 5,000 thermal cycles simulate approximately six months of clinical use [57]. In this context, Elmarsafy et al. [58] exposed the samples to 5,000 thermal cycles in their studies. Similarly, in the present study, specimens were aged with 5,000 thermal cycles to evaluate the behavior of composites after six months of intraoral exposure. It is known that the optical and mechanical properties of resin-based composites may change when exposed to intraoral conditions.

The sequential analytical design used in this study enhanced interpretation by distinguishing thermal aging effects from beverage-induced alterations. This methodological approach avoided confounding between factors acting at different experimental phases and offered better insight into the mechanisms underlying optical and surface changes. Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be considered. The immersion protocol represents short-term exposure under static laboratory conditions and therefore may not fully reproduce the dynamic intraoral environment, including oral clearance, salivary buffering capacity, dietary variation, and brushing-related abrasion. In addition, only one polishing protocol and one thermocycling regimen were applied, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other finishing/polishing procedures and aging conditions. Another methodological limitation is that optical measurements were performed using a spectrophotometer; although this approach is commonly used for dental color assessment, three-dimensional optical mapping systems may provide a more comprehensive evaluation by allowing spatially resolved analysis of color and surface-related changes across the specimen. Therefore, future studies using different aging protocols, polishing procedures, and advanced optical mapping methods are needed to further validate the present findings.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro comparative study, resin-based composites responded differently to thermocycling and beverage exposure depending on their resin matrix composition. The ormocer-based composite demonstrated better color stability, whereas the nanohybrid composite was more susceptible to translucency changes and beverage-induced discoloration. Surface roughness changes were not consistently influenced by beverage type but were more strongly associated with the composite material than with the immersion solution. Clinically, these findings suggest that selecting resin-based composites with better color stability may contribute to improved long-term esthetic outcomes, particularly for patients who frequently consume staining beverages.
