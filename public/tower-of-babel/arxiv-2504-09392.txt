Probabilistic Strategies: Definability and the Tensor Completeness Problem
Nathan Bowler, Sergey Goncharov, Paul Blain Levy

Abstract
Programs that combine I/O and countable probabilistic choice, modulo either bisimilarity or trace equivalence, can be seen as describing a probabilistic strategy. For well-founded programs, we might expect to axiomatize bisimilarity via a sum of equational theories and trace equivalence via a tensor of such theories. This is by analogy with similar results for nondeterminism, established previously. While bisimilarity is indeed axiomatized via a sum of theories, and the tensor is indeed at least sound for trace equivalence, completeness in general, remains an open problem. Nevertheless, we show completeness in the case that either the probabilistic choice or the I/O operations used are finitary. We also show completeness up to impersonation, i.e. that the tensor theory regards trace equivalent programs as solving the same system of equations. This entails completeness up to the cancellation law of the probabilistic choice operator. Furthermore, we show that a probabilistic trace strategy arises as the semantics of a well-founded program iff it is victorious. This means that, when the strategy is played against any partial counterstrategy, the probability of play continuing forever is zero. We link our results (and open problem) to particular monads that can be used to model computational effects.

The axiomatization problem

Program equivalence, and the question of how to axiomatize it, is a classical subject in theoretical
computer science. Sometimes, the programs studied are higher-order or
concurrent. In this paper, however, we deal with first-order, sequential programs that
perform I/O operations and make discrete probabilistic choices.
Although this may seem a limited setting, the problem of axiomatizing
trace equivalence
(a natural notion
of equivalence) has turned out to be surprisingly tough.

Axiomatizing trace equivalence

Programs with the same trace semantics are
trace equivalent
.
This is congruence relation coarser than bisimilarity. How shall we axiomatize it?

For the finitely probabilistic language, we extend the convex laws
with laws saying that I/O operations

with

Figure

2

For example

\displaystyle\mathsf{Happy?}(M,N)+_{p}\mathsf{Happy?}(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{Happy?}

\displaystyle(M+_{p}M^{\prime},N+_{p}N^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mathsf{Age?}(M_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}+_{p}\mathsf{Age?}(M^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Age?}(M_{i}+_{p}M^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}

The least congruence that includes all these laws is called

tensor-equivalence
.

Likewise, for the countably probabilistic language, we extend the

\omega
-convex laws with laws saying that each I/O operation

commutes
with
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, in the sense of the bottom equation in
Figure

2
.
For example

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{n}\cdot\mathsf{Happy?}(M_{n},N_{n})

\displaystyle\,=

\displaystyle\mathsf{Happy?}\Bigl(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle p_{n}\cdot M_{n},\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{n}\cdot N_{n}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{n}\cdot\mathsf{Age?}(M_{n,i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}

\displaystyle\,=\mathsf{Age?}\Bigl(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{n}\cdot M_{n,i}\Bigr)_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}

Again, the least congruence that includes all these laws is called

tensor-equivalence
.

It is straightforward to see that tensor-equivalence is sound for
trace semantics. In other words, trace equivalence implies tensor
equivalence. But is it complete?

In the nondeterministic language, whether the nondeterministic choice
is binary or countable, the answer is yes
[
3
]
. So we might expect
a similar result for probability.

In fact, we shall prove that tensor-equivalence is complete in two
cases:

•

when only
+_{p}
is used, but not
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
;

•

when the signature is finitary, i.e.
\mathsf{Ar}({k})
is finite for
each
k\!\in\!K
.

To summarize, we establish tensor completeness in the case that
either
the probabilistic choice

or
the I/O signature is finitary. If both are infinitary, completeness is an open
question.
Nevertheless, even in this case, we prove a weak form of completeness
called
completeness up to impersonation
. This means that for trace
equivalent programs
M
and
N
, we can—up to tensor-equivalence—see
M

as the “canonical” solution to a system of equations that also has
N
as a
solution. Moreoever, there is a program
P
such that
M+_{\frac{1}{2}}P
and
N+_{\frac{1}{2}}P
are tensor-equivalent.

I-C

The definability problem

As we have seen, each program has a trace semantics, which is
real-valued function on plays. Such a function
satisfies some basic conditions that makes it a “strategy” (again
borrowing terminology from game semantics).

Although this would be the case even for non-well-founded programs,
our programs are in fact
well-founded
. This leads to a
definability question. Which strategies are the trace
semantics of a program in our language? We answer this question in
Section

VIII
. It turns out that a strategy is definable by a program if and only if, when played against any
“partial counterstrategy” (a deterministic way of providing input
that may fail), the probability of continuing forever is zero. We
call such a strategy
victorious
.

The corresponding question for the nondeterministic languages was
answered in
[
3
]
, using the notion of a “well-foundedly total” strategy.

Definition V.6
.

A
shallow normal form
is a program of the form

\textstyle{\sum}_{k\in A}p_{k}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(M_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}
with countable
A\subseteq K
, and the
coefficients
(p_{k})_{k\in A}
are positive and sum to 1.

Proposition V.7
.

Every program is equivalent to a shallow normal form.

Now we can give the following result,
which will be used in
Section

VI
.

Lemma V.8
.

For programs
M
and
L
with
\morph{Traces}\,{\left(L\right)}
well-founded, and

p<1
such that
p\morph{Traces}\,{\left(L\right)}\leqslant\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M\right)}
, there
is a program
N
such that
M\equiv L+_{p}N
.

Proof.

We assume that
M=\textstyle{\sum}_{k\in A}a_{k}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(M_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}
is in shallow normal-form and that
L=\textstyle{\sum}_{k\in B}b_{k}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(L_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}
is in
well-founded normal form. We proceed by induction on
L
.

For
k\!\in\!B
, the inequality
p\morph{Traces}\,{\left(L\right)}\leqslant\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M\right)}
tells us that

\displaystyle 0<pb_{k}=p\morph{Traces}\,{\left(L\right)}(k)\leqslant\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M\right)}(k)

giving
k\in A
and
pb_{k}\leqslant a_{k}
. Furthermore, for all
i\!\in\!\mathsf{Ar}({k})
we have
\frac{pb_{k}}{a_{k}}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(L_{k,i}\right)}\leqslant\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{k,i}\right)}
, since
a_{k}>0
and for any passive-ending
s
we have

\displaystyle p\morph{Traces}\,{\left(L\right)}(k.i.s)\leqslant

\displaystyle\;\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M\right)}(k.i.s)

i.e.

\displaystyle pb_{k}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(L_{k,i}\right)}(s)\leqslant

\displaystyle\;a_{k}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{k,i}\right)}(s)

The inductive hypothesis gives
N_{k,i}
such that

\displaystyle M_{k,i}=\,

\displaystyle{L_{k,i}}+_{\frac{pb_{k}}{a_{k}}}N_{k,i}

Put

\displaystyle N\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\rm{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in A}\begin{cases}\frac{a_{k}-pb_{k}}{1-p}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(N_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}&(k\in B)\\
\frac{a_{k}}{1-p}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(M_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}&(k\not\in B)\end{cases}

Clearly the coefficients are nonnegative and sum to 1. Now we have

\displaystyle M\equiv\,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in A}a_{k}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(M_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}

\displaystyle\equiv\,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in A}\begin{cases}a_{k}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(L_{k,i}+_{\frac{pb_{k}}{a_{k}}}N_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}&(k\in B)\\
a_{k}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(M_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}&(k\not\in B)\end{cases}

\displaystyle\equiv\,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in A}\begin{cases}a_{k}\cdot\bigl(\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(L_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}\\
\qquad+_{\frac{pb_{k}}{a_{k}}}\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(N_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}\bigr)&\penalty\ \quad(k\in B)\\
a_{k}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(M_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}&\quad\penalty\ (k\not\in B)\end{cases}

\displaystyle\equiv\,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in B}b_{k}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(L_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}

\displaystyle\quad+_{p}\sum_{k\in A}\begin{cases}\frac{a_{k}-pb_{k}}{1-p}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(N_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}&(k\in B)\\
\frac{a_{k}}{1-p}\cdot\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(M_{k,i})_{i\in\mathsf{Ar}({k})}&(k\not\in B)\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle L+_{p}N

as required.∎

VI
Completeness up to Impersonation

Although it is an open question whether trace
equivalent programs are always
tensor-equivalent, we shall show that tensor-equivalence is complete
in a weaker sense. This uses the
following notion.

Definition VI.1
.

Let
A
be a convex space, and
a=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence in
A
.

1.

An
a
-solution
is a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in

A
satisfying
r_{n}=a_{n}+_{\frac{1}{2}}r_{n+1}
for all
n\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
.

2.

If
A
is an
\omega
-convex space, then the
canonical

a
-solution
is the sequence
\bigl(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-n-i-1}\cdot a_{n+i}\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

The
head
of a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the element

r_{0}
.

Definition VI.2
.

Let
A
be an
\omega
-convex space with elements
x
and
y
. We
say that
x
is
impersonated
by
y
when there is a sequence

a
such that

•

x
is the head of the canonical
a
-solution, i.e.
x=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}a_{i}
.

•

y
is the head of an
a
-solution.

We see that impersonation implies equivalence up to cancellation:

Lemma VI.3
.

Let
A
be an
\omega
-convex space with elements
x
and
y
. If

x
is impersonated by
y
, then there is
u\!\in\!A
such that
x+_{\frac{1}{2}}u=y+_{\frac{1}{2}}u
.

Proof.

There is a sequence
a=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and an
a
-solution
r
such that
x=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot a_{i}
and
y=r_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle y+_{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot r_{i+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,r_{0}+_{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot r_{i+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot r_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot(a_{i}+_{\frac{1}{2}}r_{i+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\Bigl(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot a_{i}\Bigr)+_{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigl(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot r_{i+1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,x+_{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-j-1}\cdot r_{j+1}

∎

We shall use yet another kind of normal form.

Definition VI.4
.

A
light pre-normal form
is a program of the form
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{n}\cdot M_{n}
, where the components
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

use only binary probabilistic choice. It is a
light normal
form
when the sequence
p
is
(2^{-n-1})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Lemma VI.5
.

Every program is light normalizable, i.e. equivalent to a light
normal form.

Proof.

Any light pre-normal form is light normalizable, by
finitary splitting and regathering. Light normalizability is
preserved by
\mathsf{Req}\,k?
via the tensor law, obviously
preserved by
+_{p}
, and preserved by countable
probabilistic choice via the following argument. Given
M_{i}\equiv 2^{-n-1}\cdot M_{i,n}

for all
i\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
, and a sequence
(p_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
positive reals summing to 1, the program
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{n}\cdot M_{n}
has light pre-normal form
\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{r}\cdot N_{r}
,
writing

\displaystyle\beta_{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\rm{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i\leqslant r}p_{i}^{i-r-1}

\displaystyle N_{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\rm{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\;\sum_{j\leqslant r}\frac{p_{i}2^{i-r-1}}{\beta_{r}}\cdot M_{i,r-i}

The result follows by induction over programs.
∎

Theorem VI.6

(Completeness up to impersonation)
.

Let
M
and
N
be trace-equivalent programs. Then
M
is
impersonated by
N
, up to tensor-equivalence.

Proof.

Lemma

VI.5
tells us that
M
is equivalent to a light normal form

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-n-1}\cdot P_{n}
, and therefore (up to
\equiv
) it is the head
of the canonical
P
-solution. For each
n\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
, we define a program
Q_{n}
trace-equivalent
to
\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot P_{n+i}
as follows.

\displaystyle Q_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\rm{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\;N

\displaystyle Q_{n}\equiv

\displaystyle\;P_{n}+_{\frac{1}{2}}Q_{n+1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{
by \lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{prop:subsplit}.}

So (up to
\equiv
) we see that
N
is the head of the
P
-solution

.
∎

Using

VI.3
, we obtain

(Completeness up to cancellation)
.

Let
M
and

U

.

VII
Completeness for Finitary Signatures

This section is devoted to proving that, for a
finitary
signature
S=(\mathsf{Ar}({k}))_{k\in K}
, any two trace-equivalent programs are tensor-equivalent.
We begin with the
following notion.

Definition VII.1
.

Let
\sigma
and
\tau
be play-measures with
w(\tau)<\infty
. We say that
\sigma

is
uniformly below

\tau
, written
\sigma\prec\tau

d>0
such
that

\displaystyle\sigma(s)+d\leqslant\;

\displaystyle\tau(s)\quad\quad\text{ for all $s\!\in\!\mathsf{supp}({\sigma})$.}

Thus
\sigma\prec\tau
implies
\sigma\leqslant\tau
and

w(\sigma)<w(\tau)
. The key property of
\prec
is the following.

Lemma VII.2
.

Let
(\tau_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of play-measures with
total weight
\lambda\in[0,1]
. For any play-measure
\sigma
such that
\sigma\prec\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\tau_{i}
, there is
n\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
such that
\sigma\prec\sum_{i<n}\tau_{i}
.

Proof.

Take a witness
d\!\in\!(0,1]
for
\sigma\prec\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\tau_{i}
. Take
n\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
such that
\sum_{i\geqslant n}w(\tau_{i})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}d
. We show that
\frac{1}{2}d
witnesses
\sigma\prec\sum_{i<n}\tau_{i}
. For any
s\in\mathsf{supp}({\sigma})
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(s)+d\leqslant\,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\tau_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\sum_{i<n}\tau_{i}(s)+\sum_{i\geqslant n}\tau_{i}(s)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,

\displaystyle\sum_{i<n}\tau_{i}(s)+w\Bigl(\sum_{i\geqslant n}\tau_{i}(s)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,

\displaystyle\sum_{i<n}\tau_{i}(s)+\frac{1}{2}d,

therefore

\displaystyle\sigma(s)+\frac{1}{2}d\leqslant\,

\displaystyle\sum_{i<n}\tau_{i}(s)

∎

Now we give the central notion of the section:

Definition VII.3
.

A
steady pre-normal form
is a program of the form
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{n}\cdot M_{n}
, where

•

for all
n\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
, the strategy
\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{n}\right)}
is
well-founded

•

for all

\sum_{n<m}p_{n}\cdot\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{n}\right)}\prec\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{n}\cdot\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{i}\right)}
.

Such steady pre-normal form is a
steady normal form
when the sequence
p
is

(2^{-n-1})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Lemma VII.4
.

Any steady pre-normal form is equivalent to a steady normal form.

Proof.

By splitting and regathering.
∎

Lemma VII.5
.

Let
M
and
N
be steady-normalizable programs. If they are

Proof.

We have steady normal forms

\displaystyle M\equiv\,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot M_{i}

\displaystyle N\equiv\,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot N_{i}

and
\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M\right)}=\morph{Traces}\,{\left(N\right)}
.
Lemma

VII.2
tells us that

•

for all
m\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
, there is
n\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i<m}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{i}\right)}\prec\sum_{i<n}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(N_{i}\right)}

•

for
all
n\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
, there is

such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i<n}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(N_{i}\right)}\prec\sum_{i<m}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{i}\right)}

Thus we obtain strictly increasing sequences
(m_{r})_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
and

of natural numbers satisfying

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i<m_{r}}2^{-i-1}

\displaystyle\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{i}\right)}

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i<n_{r}}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(N_{i}\right)}

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i<m_{r+1}}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{i}\right)}

For each

, we obtain a play-measure

\displaystyle\sum_{i<n_{r}}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(N_{i}\right)}-\sum_{i<m_{r}}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{i}\right)},

which is well-founded since

V.5
(

for some

and

. Likewise the play-measure

\displaystyle\sum_{i<m_{r+1}}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{i}\right)}-\sum_{i<n_{r}}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(N_{i}\right)}

for some
b_{r}>0
and
program
V_{r}
. Now

V.5
(
2
) gives the following.

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m_{r}\mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp}m_{r+1})}2^{-i-1}\cdot M_{i}\equiv

\displaystyle\;a_{r}\cdot U_{r}+b_{r}\cdot V_{r}

\displaystyle(r\!\in\!\mathbb{N})

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[0\mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp}n_{0})}2^{-i-1}\cdot N_{i}\equiv

\displaystyle\;U_{0}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[n_{r}\mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp}n_{r+1})}2^{-i-1}\cdot N_{i}\equiv

\displaystyle\;a_{r}\cdot V_{r}+b_{r+1}\cdot U_{r+1}

\displaystyle(r\!\in\!\mathbb{N})

So we have

\displaystyle M\equiv

\displaystyle\,\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}M_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\,\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i\in[m_{r}\mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp}m_{r+1})}2^{-i-1}M_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\,\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}(a_{r}U_{r}+b_{r}V_{r})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\,a_{0}U_{0}+\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}(b_{r}V_{r}+a_{r+1}U_{r+1})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\,\sum_{i\in[0\mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp}n_{0})}2^{-i-1}N_{i}

Lemma VII.6

1.

If
M
and

2.

If

k\!\in\!K

1.

We have steady normal forms

\displaystyle M\equiv

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot M_{i}

\displaystyle N\equiv

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot N_{i}

\displaystyle M+_{p}N\equiv

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot(M_{i}+_{p}N_{i})

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}p_{n}\cdot M_{n}\equiv

\displaystyle\;\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{r}\cdot N_{r}

\displaystyle\text{writing }\beta_{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\rm{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}

.
Since

preserves well-foundendess, we see that
\morph{Traces}\,{\left(N_{r}\right)}

, we want a

for some

\tau_{j}(s)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\;\Bigl(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}p_{j}\sigma_{j}\Bigr)(s)

as required.

For all
j<n
, we have a steady

\displaystyle M_{j}\equiv

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot M_{j,i}

\displaystyle\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(M_{j})_{j<n)}\equiv

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\cdot N_{i}

\displaystyle\text{writing}\quad N_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\rm{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\;\mathsf{Req}\,{k}?(M_{j,i})_{j<n)}

preserves

\morph{Traces}\,{\left(N_{i}\right)}
is well-founded. Given
m\!\in\!\mathbb{N}
, we want a witness
d
for

\displaystyle\sum_{i<m}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(N_{i}\right)}\prec

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(N_{i}\right)}

j<n

d_{j}

\displaystyle\sum_{i<m}2^{-i-1}\morph{Traces}\,{\left(M_{j,i}\right)}\prec

.

For a finitary signature, every program is steady-normalizable.

The finitarity hypothesis in

Proposition VII.8
.

For the signature consisting of an

X
Conclusions

We have tacked the problem of completeness of (infinitary) tensor logic of
programs with probabilistic choice, w.r.t. trace equivalence as the underlying
semantic notion of program equivalence. In contrast to the analogous case of
nondeterminism, the problem turned out to be remarkably difficult.
We managed to establish completeness for finitary signatures (
Theorem

VII.9
), by developing
a system of normalization procedures, while also demonstrating that the present
approach cannot scale to the infinitary case. The latter thus presents a hard
open problem, which we aim to attack in further work.

We have shown that the general completeness problem simplifies significantly
and resolves into positive in presence of certain natural quasiequational laws, most obviously,
in presence of the cancellativity law for the probabilistic choice. We refine this
furthermore by proving completeness
up to impersonation
(
Theorem

VI.6
), which
shows the necessary subtlety of any putative counterexample to completeness.

As noted in
Section

III-D
, the
\omega
-convex space of probabilistic strategies is cancellative.
But what about the
\omega
-convex space of programs modulo tensor equivalence?
By
Corollary

VI.7
, it is cancellative iff completeness holds, which is true for
finitary signatures but is unknown for infinitary ones.

In many fields of semantics, an important problem, closely related to completeness, is the problem of definability,
which is the problem of describing intrinsically the space of possible semantics
of programs. We provide such a description (
Theorem

VIII.8
) through the class of
victorious strategies
,
which precisely capture semantics of probabilistic programs in game-theoretic terms.

Besides the problem of tensor completeness for infinitary signatures,
future
work may consider continuous probability and the combination of probability with nondeterminism.
