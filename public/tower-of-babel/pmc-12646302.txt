PAK2-driven cytoskeleton–endosome dynamics control macrophage hyperphagia and SIRPA engagement
Authors: Julie Drieu La Rochelle, Joseph P. Cassidy, Jonathan Chernoff, Ulla G. Knaus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMembers of the p21-activated kinase family (PAK) are closely linked to solid cancer development and metastasis due to their ability to promote cell growth, survival, motility, and invasion, and thus considered promising drug targets. Yet, despite the profound effects of tumor-associated leukocytes on inflammation and cancer progression, the consequences of PAK1/2 inhibition in innate immune cells remains unknown. Based on newly created animal models and PAK inhibitor studies we show functional specialization with PAK2 controlling biophysical and biomechanical macrophage plasticity, while PAK1 mainly restrains proinflammatory triggers. PAK2 deficiency markedly amplified macrophage uptake mechanisms for particles, pathogens, and cancer cells, suggesting this serine/threonine kinase as target for macrophage-centered antitumor strategies.

Macrophages are crucial for tissue homeostasis, repair processes, and host defense. They sense apoptotic and damaged cells displaying eat-me signals, pathogens, and danger molecules, and respond by various engulfment strategies and by generating cytokines and reactive oxygen/nitrogen species. Part of their defense repertoire is their unique plasticity evident in the rapid shape changes required for extravasation, interstitial migration, and target uptake. Plasticity is essential to adapt to mechanical cues by spatial confinement and extracellular matrix, and to the microenvironment created by adjacent cells, soluble mediators, and microbial communities (1, 2). In malignancies, heterogenous populations of tumor-associated macrophages, neutrophils, and monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells coexist, often transitioning between activation states. This malleability is accomplished by coupling cytoskeletal dynamics with genetic and epigenetic programming, supported by GTPase and protein kinase signaling that regulates actomyosin-associated processes and gene transcription by spatiotemporal activation/deactivation cycles and protein–protein interactions.

Prominent effectors of RAC/CDC42 GTPases are p21-activated kinases (PAKs) that control signaling pathways by phosphorylation and protein complex formation. These highly conserved serine/threonine kinases are categorized according to domain structure and regulation into Group I (PAK1-3) and Group II (PAK4-6) (3). PAKs regulate pathways essential for cell/tissue homeostasis, host defense, and tumorigenesis, and vary in their propensity to drive oncogenic signaling via overexpression or amplification (4, 5). Their role in gene transcription and survival processes, propagating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) signaling, can promote tumor development (6, 7). Yet, how PAKs impact macrophage responses or immune disorders remains unknown, even though PAK1 and PAK2 are highly expressed in innate immune cells (8). Previous reports suggested a role for PAK1 in mast cell degranulation and lamellipodia dynamics (9–11), while PAK2 was implicated in HSPC migration/homing, myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) development, and megakaryopoiesis (12–14).

Macrophage uptake mechanisms required for acquisition of nutrients and environmental cues, removal of apoptotic corpses, and antimicrobial defense involve macropinocytosis, recognition of self and foreign targets using activating and inhibitory receptors, ligand–receptor clustering, integrin inside-out activation, and cytoplasmic signaling (15–17). Macrophages perform constitutive macropinocytosis for endosomal cargo delivery and recycling, and dedicated recognition and engulfment processes for phagocytic uptake, both governed by many biochemical and biomechanical control mechanisms. We show here that PAK2 regulates macrophage membrane expansion, macropinocytosis, opsonization-independent phagocytosis of various particles and cells, and the docking function of the inhibitory receptor signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPA). Combined loss of PAK1/2 leads to inflammation-driven disseminated hemophagocytic histiocytic sarcoma (18), yet single PAK2 ablation in myeloid cells is well tolerated in vivo and holds promise for enhanced cancer cell killing.

Results

PAK Restricts Actin Remodeling and Premature Innate Immune Cell Activation.

Understanding the impact of PAK1 and PAK2 signaling on innate immune cell functions required creating mice with myeloid compartment-specific ablation of PAK2 (Pak2fl/flLyz2tm1(cre), termed mPak2−/−) and establishing Pak1−/− and Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice to assess PAK isoform signaling specificity and compensation. All mice were born at the expected ratios and displayed no apparent signs of compromised well-being. In older Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice bone marrow (BM) cellularity was decreased, which was reflected in a fourfold to fivefold decrease in neutrophils (Fig. 1 A and B). Compensatory PAK isoform upregulation was not detected (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). PAK2 deficiency caused pronounced F-actin redistribution to cell surface domains in unstimulated neutrophils, resembling an fMLF-activated phenotype. Actin filament disruption may induce premature neutrophil granule exocytosis, and indeed PAK2- and PAK1/2-deficient neutrophils were prone to aberrant degranulation of azurophilic granules containing myeloperoxidase (MPO) (Fig. 1 C and D). BM neutrophils were analyzed for baseline, priming (TNFα), and fMLF- and phorbol ester (PMA)-induced superoxide generation as premature granule exocytosis is often associated with enhanced priming and NOX2 NADPH oxidase activation (19). Wildtype neutrophils remained relatively quiescent and responded as expected to TNFα priming and fMLF-induced activation (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Premature priming was prominent in PAK2-deficient neutrophils, while combined PAK1/2 deficiency caused rapid, exaggerated superoxide generation upon fMLF stimulation. PMA-stimulated superoxide production, reflecting NOX2 complex assembly, remained comparable between neutrophils of different genotypes. Thus, PAK2 is essential to constrain cytoskeletal remodeling and both PAKs cooperated to maintain physiological levels of the neutrophil oxidative burst.

Macrophage plasticity, a prerequisite for polarization and dedicated cell functions, is governed by the cytoskeleton. As shape indicates actin dynamics, BM macrophages, peritoneal macrophages (PM), and BM-derived macrophages (BMDM) derived from PAK ablated mice were compared (Fig. 1F). Adherent BM macrophages derived from Pak2fl/fl and Pak1−/− mice appeared small rounded or elongated, while PAK2-deficient BM macrophages were spreading. Absence of PAK1/2 led to further spreading of BM macrophages into pancake-shaped cells. Another resident macrophage population are PMs. The peritoneal cell count of Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice was highly increased (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), suggesting inflammation. While Pak2fl/fl and Pak1−/− PMs displayed typical elongated shapes, PAK2-deficient PMs featured small bodies with dendrite-like extensions, and PAK1/2-deficient PMs displayed again pancake-like morphology.

To mimic monocyte to macrophage differentiation, nonpolarized (M0) BMDMs with PAK isoform ablation were generated (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E). M0 BMDM shapes were comparable to resident PMs, with spreading progressively increasing from PAK2- to PAK1/2-deficient BMDMs, yet without displaying dendrite-like protrusions (Fig. 1F). The surface area of PAK1/2-deficient M0 BMDMs increased threefold, and retraction fibers were prominent (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). As PAKs can drive cellular processes in kinase activity dependent and independent manner, the ATP competitive PAK1/2 inhibitor FRAX1036 was utilized to link conclusively shape changes to PAK activity (Fig. 1H) (20). The observed cytoskeletal alterations prompted mass spectrometry analysis of various BMDM populations (Dataset S1). Marked changes were noted in the abundance of proteins linked to the cytoskeleton and associated regulatory proteins including GEFs and GAPs, in particularly downregulation of ARHGEF7 (βPix), a prominent PAK interacting protein (Fig. 1I). Macrophage PAK2 deficiency mimicked often but not always PAK1/2 ablation, likely due to compensation by PAK1. Membrane expansion is associated with an increased monomeric G-actin pool due to changes in filament turnover. Several kinases including PAK1 regulate LIM kinase-mediated cofilin inactivation, leading to F-actin disassembly (21). Indeed, the monomeric G-actin pool was elevated in PAK1/2-deficient BMDM (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G).

PAK2 deficiency mediated spreading resembled the typical M1 macrophage shape. Polarization with LPS/IFNγ induced M1-typical spreading in Pak2 fl/fl and Pak1−/− M0 BMDMs but never reached the surface area of mPak2−/− or Pak1−/−mPak2−/− M1 BMDMs (Fig. 1J). Macrophage shape is thought to correlate with M1 vs. M2 gene transcription profiles (22), but PAK1/2-deficient M0 BMDMs showed only minor M2 gene upregulation, albeit M1 polarization was feasible (Nos2) (Fig. 1K and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 H–J). Proteome comparison of PAK1/2-deficient M0 BMDMs with polarized wildtype BMDM indicated a mixed M1/M2 phenotype that resembled the Pak2fl/fl M0 expression profile. Thus, in absence of PAK activity nonpolarized macrophages display pronounced membrane expansion across niches.

PAK Activity Controls Macrophage Phagocytosis.

An abundance of plasma membrane can increase phagocytic uptake (23). Indeed, uptake of zymosan particles was markedly increased in PAK-deficient M0 BMDMs, with further amplification in M1 BMDMs (PAK1/2>PAK2>PAK1>fl/WT), reaching up to 70 internalized particles in 10 min. FRAX1036 treatment indicated that loss of PAK activity was required for hyperphagic behavior (Fig. 2 A and B). In PAK1/2-deficient M0 BMDMs tyrosine phosphorylated proteins accumulated beneath phagocytic cups and the actin-binding protein TAGLN2 was overexpressed, localizing to ruffles and surrounding ingested zymosan. TAGLN2 and nonmuscle myosin (NMII) recruitment are essential for phagocytic uptake. Like TAGLN2, NMIIB was upregulated and coated zymosan particles (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The phagocytosis of Escherichia coli by PAK1/2-deficient M1 BMDMs was as expected increased, yet efficient bacterial killing was dependent on the exposure time (Fig. 2C). Sufficient superoxide generation (WST-1) and lysosomal acidification were detected in PAK1/2-deficient M1 BMDM (Fig. 2 D and E), suggesting that bacterial killing was only prolonged due to the high phagocytosis rate.

Endosomal Recycling Block Hinders SIRPA Engagement.

Closer analysis of PAK1/2-deficient BM macrophages showed engulfed erythrocytes and neutrophils (Fig. 3A). Healthy self-cells express don’t eat me signals on the cell surface, enabling only phagocytosis of apoptotic/necrotic cells, aging/senescent erythrocytes (RBC), or foreign agents. As PAK1/2 deletion seems to permit erythrophagocytosis, we cocultured nonpolarized BMDMs derived from Pak2fl/fl, mPak2−/−, and Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice with freshly isolated erythrocytes derived from four mouse genotypes (all C57BL/6J) (Fig. 3 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Mix-match phagocytosis assays showed increased attachment of nonopsonized PAK1- and PAK1/2-deficient erythrocytes to M0 BMDMs of all genotypes, likely due to physical or surface changes on RBCs. Pronounced uptake of nonopsonized erythrocytes of any genotype was observed in PAK2- and PAK1/2-deficient M0 BMDMs, while WT or PAK1-deficient BMDMs only occasionally phagocytosed single erythrocytes. A prominent “don’t eat me” signal on the cell surface is CD47 that binds to the inhibitory receptor SIRPA on the phagocyte cell surface (24). PAK1/2 deletion did not affect Sirpa mRNA or SIRPA protein expression or its ADAM10/γ-secretase-mediated proteolytic degradation (Fig. 3 B and C). Imaging BM macrophages and M0 BMDMs revealed SIRPA localized to internal membranes in PAK1/2-deficient cells, while SIRPA was localized in the plasma membrane of WT and PAK1-deficient macrophages as expected. BMDMs lacking PAK2 exhibited an intermediate phenotype with outside/inside SIRPA localization (Fig. 3 D and E). Internalized SIRPA was detected at vesicular membranes enriched in EEA1, p62/SQSTM1, SNX4, and RAB11, but not in LAMP1 vesicles (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). Treatment with FRAX1036 resulted in comparable SIRPA localization (Fig. 3F), indicating that PAK activity is required for SIRPA plasma membrane localization. Other plasma membrane proteins involved in phagocytosis such as integrin CD11b and CD47 were also partially or fully internalized in PAK2- and PAK1/2-deficient macrophages (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Siglec-10/G, an inhibitory receptor enhanced by M2 polarization (25), was not expressed in PAK1/2-deficient M0 BMDMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 F and G).

Cytoskeletal filaments, microtubules, and myosin motors maintain spatial distribution of endocytic vesicle transport and facilitate vesicle fusion/fission and cargo sorting to degradation and recycling pathways (26). Proteomic analysis of M0 BMDMs derived from PAK-deficient mice showed pronounced changes in the abundance of proteins connected to the endocytic trafficking machinery including endocytic receptors (Stabilin1), ESCRT-I-III complex components, SNARE-associated proteins and sorting nexins, and WASH/Commander complex components (Fig. 3 G and H). FRAX treatment largely mimicked changes observed in PAK1/2-deficient BMDM, with differences likely reflecting kinase-independent PAK signaling, the timing of the inhibitor treatment, or FRAX off-target effects. Due to these comprehensive changes, endocytic recycling kinetics were monitored using a fluorescently labeled antibody to integrin β1 (CD29). In contrast to Pak2fl/fl BMDMs approximately 50% of CD29 was internalized in fixed PAK1/2-deficient BMDMs (Fig. 3I). In live cell recycling assays antibody-labeled CD29 was endocytosed efficiently and recycled back to the plasma membrane in Pak2fl/fl BMDMs, while endocytosed CD29 remained concentrated in intracellular patches in Pak1−/−mPak2−/− BMDMs instead of recycling back (Fig. 3J). The altered CD29 localization resembled internalized SIRPA retained in SNX4/RAB11 positive endosomes.

PAK2 Restrains Constitutive Macropinocytosis.

Macrophages internalize plasma membrane-formed vacuoles constitutively for nutrient uptake and sampling of the microenvironment with membrane proteins being recycled back to the surface. This process is dependent on large membrane ruffles that form pockets engulfing extracellular material (16). PAK1/2-deficient macrophages displayed extensive ruffling with plasma membrane expansion (Fig. 4A), suggesting that elevated macropinocytosis increased internalization of SIRPA and CD29 receptors. Macropinocytic uptake of 70 kDa dextran was accelerated in Pak1−/−mPak2−/− M0 BMDMs, and this process featured the hallmarks of macropinocytosis i.e., dependency on calcium and PI3-kinase activity, and interference by an inhibitor of the Na+/H+ exchanger (EIPA) and by F-actin disrupting cytochalasin D (Fig. 4 B and C). Enhanced dextran internalization by FRAX1036 treated wildtype BMDMs was comparable to uptake by mPak2−/− BMDMs, revealing the critical importance of PAK2 kinase activity (Fig. 4 D and E). Taken together, the inhibition of PAK2 activity leads to augmented constitutive macropinocytosis, receptor uptake, and trapping of certain receptors in recycling endosomes, thereby removing SIRPA from the plasma membrane, impeding recognition and docking of CD47 expressed on host or cancer cells.

Macrophage PAK2 Inhibition Markedly Elevates Cancer Cell Phagocytosis.

CD47 is amplified on cancer cells to evade immune detection (27, 28). Constitutive internalization of SIRPA together with the increased uptake capacity of PAK-deficient macrophages may amplify cancer cell phagocytosis. In accord, rapid and highly efficient uptake of C1498 murine leukemia cells by mPak2−/− and Pak1−/−mPak2−/− M0 BMDMs was observed (Fig. 4 F–I). This observation was translated to human monocytic THP-1 cells differentiated to macrophage-like cells. Differentiated THP-1 cells internalized 70 kDa dextran (~14 nm) via macropinocytosis, but neither M-CSF nor FRAX1036 treatment could increase uptake above the maximal stimulation induced by PMA as differentiation agent, although EIPA and cytochalasin D blocked dextran uptake as expected (Fig. 3J). Yet even in this cell type phagocytosis of Jurkat T lymphocytes (~10 to 13 μm), a human leukemia cell line, was fourfold enhanced after FRAX1036 treatment (Fig. 4 K–N).

PAK Activity Prevents Macrophage Transformation.

Erythrophagocytosis together with decreased BM cell and neutrophil counts (Fig. 1 A and B) prompted the systematic study of Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice from weaning to 40 wk. Mice appeared healthy although their weight plateaued, exhibiting sudden malaise between 25 wk and 40 wk with a sharp drop of body temperature (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). BM erythrocytes were markedly decreased, yet anemia was not present, likely due to stress erythropoiesis in the spleen. Blood analysis of Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice revealed an increase in leukocytes, large unstained cells, and reticulocytes, and immune thrombocytopenia or pseudothrombocytopenia (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C–E). Analysis of resident PAK1/2-deficient PMs showed elevated nuclear Ki67, foci of phosphorylated histone H2A.X, an early marker of DNA breaks, and multinucleation events (Fig. 5 A and B). None of these features were present in Pak1−/− or mPak2−/− mice, indicating functional compensation by PAK isoforms. Quantification of nuclei in M0 BMDMs revealed that the majority of PAK1/2-deficient BMDMs were binucleated on day 6, with triple-nucleated cells appearing later (Fig. 5C). Cytokinesis was disturbed with one centrosome separating two nuclei in interphase, followed in prophase by Ki67 expression in one or both nuclei, often with midbody mislocalization. Fusion events were not observed, but failure to progress after initial cleavage furrow regression (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F). Alterations in various kinases and cytoskeleton-associated proteins can lead to cytokinesis failure. For example, precise localization of NMIIB at the cleavage furrow is important for generating the restriction site and completing cytokinesis (29). NMIIB was mislocalized in PAK1/2-deficient BMDMs, yet only MLC2 expression but not MLC2 Ser19 phosphorylation was altered (Fig. 5 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4G). Large, multinucleated macrophages with genome duplications have been observed in granulomas, chronic inflammation, and conditions of genotoxic and oxidative stress (30). These macrophages can bypass p53 by using c-MYC to escape cell cycle arrest. Changes in p53 and c-MYC expression were not observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S4H), however proliferation of PAK1/2-deficient or FRAX treated M0 BMDMs, assessed by manual counting and metabolic activity, was increased (Fig. 5 F and G). Still, proliferative signaling pathways commonly connected to PAK including ERK and AKT signaling were not altered (SI Appendix, Fig. S4I) (31). The epigenetic reader BRD4, a key regulator of cell proliferation and pro-survival signaling, suppressed lipophagy in mycobacteria-induced granulomas (32). Lipid droplet accumulation was evident in PAK1/2-deficient PMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S4H), a phenotype reported as PAMP-induced proinflammatory response (33). Treatment of PAK1/2-deficient BMDMs with the BRD4 inhibitor NHWD-870 (34) reversed spreading and decreased macrophage proliferation (Fig. 5I), suggesting that BRD4’s transcriptional activity is enhanced when PAK activity is compromised. Proteomic analysis of PAK1/2-deficient M0 BMDMs showed changes in proteins associated with cell proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, lipid droplet metabolism, and ubiquitination including E3 ubiquitin-protein ligases (Fig. 5J), suggesting a transformed macrophage phenotype that differed from reported TLR2-induced differentiation (35).

Cooperation of PAK1 and PAK2 as Macrophage Tumor Suppressors.

Uncontrolled macrophage proliferation combined with hemophagocytosis is a feature of systemic hyperinflammatory syndromes. Organs and tissues of all created mouse strains were assessed for pathological, biochemical, and functional changes. Histology revealed splenomegaly in 16-wk-old Pak1−/−mPak2cre/cre mice that emerged delayed in Pak1−/−mPak2wt/cre mice (30 wk), with none of the other strains being affected (Fig. 6 A and B). Progression of splenomegaly was age-dependent with 4-wk-old mice being unaffected (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Splenic histology indicated red pulp expansion and prominent extramedullary hematopoiesis with numerous dysplastic megakaryocytes in Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). The parenchyma was infiltrated by pleomorphic round and polygonal cells that showed large ovoid/indented/angulated vesicular nuclei with anisocytosis and anisokaryosis, apoptotic bodies, nuclear hyperchromasia, cell binucleation with 2-3 mitotic figures/hpf and marked erythrophagocytosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). These features are consistent with a malignant round cell neoplasm. Splenic tissue showed a significant increase in Ter119+CD44+ erythrocyte progenitors and an abundance of neutrophils (MRP14) in Pak1−/−mPak2−/− spleens (Fig. 6 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). As CD163+iNOS+ splenic macrophages displayed staining for 3-nitrotyrosine (3-NT) (Fig. 6D), an oxidative stress marker, in vivo analysis of oxidant production was performed (36). Spleens recovered from L-012 injected Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice emitted high luminescence in contrast to baseline signals in spleens derived from all other strains (Fig. 6E). In accord, single-cell suspensions of Pak2fl/fl and Pak1−/−mPak2−/− splenic tissue loaded with DHR-123 showed increased fluorescence in the absence of PAK1/2. This oxidation signal was not affected by iNOS inhibition or a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant and partially inhibited by a radical scavenger (Fig. 6F). Yet, inhibiting the NOX2 generated oxidative burst (GSK2795039, DPI) or MPO (AZD5904) decreased DHR fluorescence by 80 to 90%, indicating neutrophil-generated HOCl as oxidant. Similarly, BM cells derived from Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice generated NOX2-dependent DHR-123 fluorescence with BM neutrophils expressing iNOS, revealing an RONS-rich BM environment (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E and F). Other organs were also affected in Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice as apparent by hepatomegaly with prominent CD163/iNOS expressing macrophages in diffusely pale livers (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 G and H). Pathogen or microbiota dissemination will activate splenic and hepatic cells and alter cellularity. The translocation of commensal bacteria to spleens and livers of Pak1−/−mPak2−/−mice was significantly increased (Fig. 6G) and splenic lysates contained firmicutes, actinobacteria, and proteobacteria among others. Intestinal pathology included hyperpermeability, and inflamed and shortened ilea with muscularis propria thickening, crypt hyperplasia, neutrophil infiltration, Paneth cell metaplasia, altered goblet cells, and decreased mucin production in 16-wk-old but not in 4-wk-old Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice (Fig. 6 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 I–L). Pronounced intestinal inflammation was fueled by NOX/iNOS-generated peroxynitrite (37) as illustrated by elevated ileal L-012 luminescence in Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice (Fig. 6 J and K). The lungs of Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice showed peribronchial and perivascular infiltration with CD163+iNOS+ macrophages, B cells, and T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Infiltrating CD45+ cells were prevalent in kidneys, soft tissue leg tumors, and enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes, and identified as CD163/iNOS/lysozyme positive macrophages and B/T lymphocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C–E).

The pathology of older PAK1/2-deficient mice suggests spontaneously developing hemophagocytic histiocytic sarcoma (HHS). HHS are hematopoietic neoplasms, often diagnosed at advanced stages and well-documented in canine oncology (38, 39). Canine HHS arises from spleen and BM, progressing rapidly by dissemination. As observed in Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice, liver tissue of a canine patient showed prominent CD163+iNOS+ macrophages (SI Appendix, Fig. S6F). Markers connected to tumorigenesis and inflammation including G-CSF, sTREM1, TIMP1, IL-1Ra, Il-1α, and CCL2 and lipopolysaccharide binding protein (LBP), indicating LPS exposure, were elevated in the circulation of Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice (Fig. 6 L and M). These pathological features support the notion that Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice spontaneously develop a disseminated macrophage-driven malignancy, resembling HHS in human, canine, and feline patients. Disease was not observed in older Pak1−/− or mPak2−/− mice but occurred delayed in Pak1−/−mPak2wt/cre mice. Malignancy development required combined PAK1 and myeloid PAK2 ablation, with macrophage PAK2 expression levels controlling the time-dependent progression of disease. Hyperactivated macrophages and neutrophils present in Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice promoted a highly oxidative, proinflammatory environment that will accelerate tissue damage and genetic/epigenetic changes.

PAK1 Deficiency Triggers Chronic Inflammation.

The phenotypes of PAK-deficient macrophages and the absence of disease in mPak2−/− mice suggest distinctive contributions of both PAK isoforms for disease development. 4-wk-old Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice showed normal BM cellularity, spleen weight, and ileal length, but a compromised gut barrier (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and I). We reasoned that Pak1−/− mice contribute a second, nonredundant trigger for disease development. Serum analysis of 16-wk-old Pak1−/− mice showed a significant increase in cytokines and chemokines connected to acute and chronic inflammation including upregulation of TNFα, IFNγ, CCL5, CXCL9, and IL-1α, and Tnf mRNA expression in colonic tissue (Fig. 7 A and B). Thus, Pak1−/− mice may suffer from low-grade, nonresolving inflammation, a major risk factor for tumor development (40). Persistent intestinal dysbiosis due to decreased gut barrier integrity facilitates entry of microbiota and associated inflammagens. Intestinal barrier weakening and low-grade chronic inflammation are intimately connected and linked to carcinogenesis in susceptible hosts (41). Pak1−/− mice showed elevated gut barrier permeability and bacterial dissemination to spleen and liver (Fig. 7 C and D). As splenic pathology was not evident in 4-wk-old Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice, a treatment window opened to delay or prevent HHS establishment. To assess the impact of microbiota dissemination on disease development, depletion of microbiota by antibiotic/antifungal treatment was performed for 6 wk (Fig. 7E). At 10 wk splenomegaly and other pathological changes were evident in untreated Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice, whereas microbiota depleted Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice showed no overt signs of splenomegaly, hepatomegaly or ileal pathology (Fig. 7 F–I), indicating that interaction of the microbiota with the compromised host drives the HHS phenotype in Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice. Peritoneal cell numbers in 10-wk-old microbiota-depleted Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice were comparable to WT mice, and their PMs resembled the dendritic PAK2-deficient phenotype (Fig. 7J), confirming inflammation-dependent transformation of PAK2-deficient PMs by PAK1 deletion over time. In summary, chronic low-grade inflammation triggered HHS in a susceptible host.

Discussion

P21-activated kinases have long been regarded as promising oncology targets (3), but current dual specificity PAK1/2 inhibitors have drawbacks such as cardiovascular toxicity and a narrow therapeutic window (42). The spontaneous development of disseminated HHS in Pak1−/−mPak2−/− mice calls for extra caution in this regard. Features of PAK1/2-deficient macrophages resemble activated histiocytes, with murine disease showing similarities to HS/HHS both in canine (43) and human patients (38, 39). HS/HHS are considered multigenic diseases at the intersection of immune dysregulation and cancer predisposition (43, 44), and long-term anti-inflammatory therapy markedly lowered the risk for canine HS (45, 46). Supporting this connection, Kidder et al. linked the development of secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in Sirpa−/− mice to TLR agonist-induced inflammation (47). In macrophages the relationship between cytoskeleton and tumor-associated effects is multifaceted as cytoskeletal dynamics induce morphological changes, modify the transcriptional landscape, and alter the response to environmental cues (22). This multifunctionality manifests with PAK2 governing multiple aspects of macrophage function including membrane expansion, macropinocytosis, phagocytosis, and endocytic trafficking. PAK1 acts not solely by suppressing low-grade inflammation but also restricts via functional compensation neutrophil hyperactivation and macrophage cytokinesis failure in the absence of PAK2.

In vivo, resident macrophages expressed M1 markers due to the persistent inflammation triggered by PAK1 deficiency. The hyperphagia by PAK1/2- and PAK2-deficient M0/M1 macrophages was universal, ranging from targets with softer to stiffer properties, from small to medium to large sizes, with different shapes and viscoelasticity, even though the settling time was standardized. The particle variety, absence of phagocytic receptor upregulation, and speed of uptake illustrates the wide-ranging control of PAK2 on (bio)mechanical properties of macrophages. Membrane expansion was reported in Gβ4-deficient neutrophil-like cells, leading to reduced membrane tension due to sphingolipid enrichment (23), while all functional changes reported here were mimicked by a PAK kinase inhibitor. This points to serine/threonine phosphorylated targets, yet their range is vast, and modification of commonly PAK-associated proteins was not detected (48). Proteomics indicated many alterations in transcription and degradation pathways. For example, NMII was overexpressed and mislocalized, but many kinases including PAK1, Aurora B, and ROCK have been linked to NMII regulation (49, 50). The combined impact of modifications created the force to rapidly engulf diverse particles by nonpolarized macrophages. Comparison with reported cancer cell uptake after antibody treatment, by introducing an activating RAC2 mutation, or by ablating Gβ4 is challenging as suspension conditions and/or extended coincubation times were used (23, 51, 52), but the uptake capacity of PAK2- and PAK1/2-deficient BMDM is remarkably high.

Therapeutic immunotherapy employs antibodies against inhibitory checkpoints, targeting CD47 or SIRPA, to stimulate cancer cell engulfment, often combined with antibodies engaging the Fc receptor for further enhancement. Removing PAK2 generated highly efficient cancer cell uptake without requiring blocking antibodies as SIRPA was trapped in RAB11/SNX4 positive recycling endosomes. The absence of PAK activity altered the protein composition of endocytic vesicles, affecting the endocytic fusion module and downregulating WASH/Commander complex components. The WASH/Commander complex connects directly to the ARP2/3 complex, F-actin branching, and tubulin to stabilize the endocytic network (53). The PAK inhibitor FRAX486 inhibited autophagosome-lysosome fusion in breast cancer cells due to degradation of the SNARE STX17 (54). Here, in primary macrophages FRAX1036 increased abundance of SNAP29 and its assembly partner STX17. Another example emphasizing the different signaling circuits in macrophages vs. other cell types is the reduced uptake of Salmonella after FRAX1036 treatment or disruption of the PAK1–βPix interaction in Hap1 cells (55), while the same treatment amplified the phagocytic capacity of macrophages as shown here. Removing only PAK2 led to less exaggerated neutrophil and macrophage phenotypes, while preserving enhanced uptake. Endocytic trafficking was also partially sustained with ~50% of SIRPA remaining on the cell surface. As result of this toned-down response, neither BM nor spleen hemophagocytosis, nor any signs of HHS were apparent in mPak2−/− mice. PAK1 and PAK2 with their diverse contributions to signaling pathways can exert tumor promoting and suppressing activities that are highly cell and context dependent. In macrophages PAK2 regulates localization of plasma membrane proteins including SIRPA, CD47, and certain integrins, membrane expansion, and rapid particle/cell uptake mechanisms, features that could be exploited for disabling the CD47-SIRPA immune checkpoint and augmenting cancer cell killing.

Materials and Methods

Mice.

mPak2−/− and Pak1−/−mPak2−/− colonies were obtained by breeding Pak2fl/fl with Lyz2tm1(cre)Ifo mice (mPak2), and Pak1−/− with Pak2fl/fl and Lyz2tm1(cre)Ifo mice respectively. Pak1−/− mice were crossed with C57BL/6J mice to generate control Pak1+/+ mice. The study was approved by the UCD Animal Research Ethics Committee and the Health Products Regulatory Authority of Ireland following the EU directive 2010/63/EU.

Primary Cells.

BM–derived cells, obtained from mouse femurs and tibias, were either seeded for experiments or differentiated into BM–derived macrophages (BMDM) in DMEM (10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% Glutamax, and 20 ng/mL M-CSF) for 6 d; polarization was with LPS (100 ng/mL) and IFNy (10 ng/mL) for 24 h. PMs were obtained by peritoneal lavage, and neutrophils were purified from BM and used immediately.

Detailed experimental procedures are provided in SI Appendix.
