Food types, preparation methods, and use of high-sodium seasonings in chain restaurants in Jamaica–implications for sodium consumption and food policy
Authors: Sherene T McNeil, Evelyn Walker, Suzanne Soares-Wynter, Nadia R Bennett, Karen Webster-Kerr, Marshall K Tulloch-Reid, Tamu Davidson, Andriene Grant, Joette McKenzie, Alphanso Blake, Simon G Anderson, Simone Spence, Novie Younger-Coleman, Trevor S Ferguson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesFood sold in restaurants is a major contributor to sodium consumption patterns. We conducted a cross-sectional study on the types of foods sold, preparation methods, and use of high-sodium seasonings in chain restaurants in Jamaica and discuss the implications for sodium consumption and food policy. Data were collected from chain restaurants (defined as ≥ 3 stores locally) as part of the Jamaica Salt Consumption Study. Interviewers collected information via questionnaire on restaurant characteristics, menu items, types of seasonings used during food preparation, and availability of low-salt options.ResultsOnly 24% of restaurant chains identified participated. Of the 107 menu items identified, the most frequently sold were chicken (21.5%), fish (15.9%), and soup (9.4%). The most common methods of preparation for meat were frying (20.6%), boiling (18.7%), baking (17.8%), and stewing (17.8%). Commonly used high-sodium seasonings included sea salt (37.4%), table salt (17.8%), and soy sauce (16.8%). Six of the restaurant chains implemented salt reduction measures, including portion control and the use of unsalted seasonings and herbs. Most restaurants evaluated used salt-containing seasonings but also offered lowsalt options. Healthier dietary options and meal preparation methods in restaurants will be a critical part of food policy and for national sodium reduction interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07883-7.Keywords: Salt content, Sodium content, Fast-food restaurants, Full-service restaurants, Low–salt options, Food environment, Nutrition policy, Caribbean

Introduction

Excess dietary sodium is a major contributor to adverse health outcomes globally, and accounts for substantial financial costs to economies [1–4]. High salt consumption has been linked to hypertension, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), chronic kidney disease, gastric cancers, and obesity, among other diseases [3, 5–8]. Reduction in sodium consumption is recommended as a major approach for addressing the high burden of premature mortality from these conditions [2, 4, 5]. Data from the Jamaica Health and Lifestyle Survey shows that over 60% of Jamaicans have elevated blood pressure, with one-third of the population being hypertensive [9]. In 2020, CVDs were the leading cause of death in Jamaica, accounting for 32% of total deaths [10]. Salt reduction interventions are therefore critical in reducing the impact of hypertension and CVDs on the population.

The Jamaica Salt Consumption Study was conducted to gather data on salt consumption in Jamaica as part of Jamaica’s response to the high burden of hypertension and CVD [11]. The study included investigation of the salt content and preparation practices for foods sold in restaurants, given international evidence that salt consumption is mostly from processed and restaurant foods [12–15]. Additionally, consumption of meals eaten outside the home results in lower nutritional quality and possibly increased obesity [16, 17]. Local surveys indicate that 31% of Jamaicans eat fast food 1–6 times weekly, 64% have 1–5 meals outside the home each week, and 9% eating out six or more times weekly [9, 18]. Recent systematic reviews have shown that interventions in restaurant and out-of-home food environments can improve dietary intake [13, 19]. Effective population-wide sodium reduction strategies must therefore include an overall reduction of sodium in the food supply and incorporate stakeholders in the restaurant industry [20, 21]. However, no data on sodium content of restaurant foods is available in Jamaica.

This study therefore aimed to evaluate types of food sold, preparation methods, use of high-sodium seasonings, and availability of low-sodium options in Jamaican chain restaurants. We hypothesized that current preparation practices promote high sodium content in restaurant foods.

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional survey of chain restaurants across Jamaica, defined as commercial entities with three or more locations nationally. The methods employed were previously published [7] with additional details provided in the Appendix. Approvals were obtained from the University and Government Ethics Committees. Participants provided written informed consent. Restaurants were identified using a company’s register and the Yello Directory; most had headquarters in Kingston, the capital city. Restaurants were classified as full-service (FS-R) or fast food (FF-R) restaurants. FS-R were those that had table service with waiter staff, while FF-R were quick-service restaurants that provided menu items prepared quickly and served packaged. Food establishments that sold ready-to-eat food items prepared at the restaurants were eligible for inclusion in the study. Initially, a research nurse or research assistant contacted restaurant owners or managers via telephone and introduced the study and its objectives. Follow-up was conducted using telephone, email or in-person visits. A minimum of four contacts were made before treating as a non-response. Trained interviewers (see Appendix) administered questionnaires from August to November 2022. Data were obtained on restaurant characteristics, meal items sold, information on recipes/ingredients, methods of food preparation, use of salt or high-sodium seasonings and sauces, and availability of low-salt options. The questionnaire was developed by the study investigators and included 34 questions (see copy in supplementary files). Questionnaire items allowed categorical of free text responses. Study nutritionists coded the responses into categories based on food types and preparation methods. Data were analysed using Stata 18 software, and descriptive analyses were performed, reporting proportions of food types and practices overall and stratified by restaurant types.

Results

Twenty-nine eligible chain restaurants were approached, and seven (2 FS-R; 5 FF-R) agreed to participate. A profile of individual restaurants is shown in the Appendix (Table S1). Additionally, a flow-chart outlining the response patterns is shown in Figure S1 and reasons for non-participation is given in Table S2 in the Appendix. One hundred and seven menu items were identified from the restaurants (70 in FF-R and 37 in FS-R) and subdivided into 16 food categories. Of the 16 categories of food items sold (Table 1), chicken (21.5%), fish (15.9%), and soup (9.4%) were the most frequently sold in all restaurants. Fish (21.6%) was the most frequently sold item in FS-R and chicken (25.7%) in FF-R. Table 2 indicates that of the seven methods of preparation identified in all surveyed restaurants, the most used methods were fried (20.6%), boiled (18.7%), baked (17.8%), and stewed (17.8%). The most utilised method of preparation in the FF-R was frying (27.1%), and stewing (32.4%) in the FS-R.

Table 3 shows thirteen seasoning ingredients used in all restaurant categories. Sea salt (37.4%), table salt (17.8%) and soy sauce (16.8%) were the most used seasoning ingredients, while bouillon (0.9%), baking soda (2.8%) and barbecue sauce (2.8%) were the least used. Sea salt was primarily used in FS-R (86.5%), whereas table salt was primarily used in FF-R (17.1%). Ingredients such as soy sauce, ketchup, jerk seasoning marinade, and jerk seasoning powder were more often used in FF-R compared to FS-R, while onion powder and garlic were least used. The use of seasonings by food group is shown in Table S3 in the appendix. Salty seasonings were primarily used in the meat, chicken, seafood, and fish categories, with sea salt, soy sauce, and ketchup being the most used.

Three of the seven restaurants reported having nutrition profiles, however these were not made available to the study team. Low salt options were offered by five of the seven restaurants surveyed, and six implemented steps to reduce the salt content in their menu. These steps included using natural seasonings, utilising low-sodium products, and implementing low-sodium measures directly related to the portion size of items being prepared (See Table S4 in the appendix). Six restaurants also reported offering catering services, and five offered customised meals with lower sodium. Customised meals were based on customer requests and the establishments’ standard formulation and procedures.

Discussion

Only seven of the twenty-nine chain restaurants approached participated in the survey. The food items most frequently sold were chicken, fish, and soup. Salt and salty seasonings or sauces were often used in food preparation, with sea salt, table salt, and soy sauce being the most used. Low salt options were implemented by some restaurants, but these were limited to customer requests.

With only 24% of approached restaurants participating, the findings may reflect the food industry’s limited willingness to provide data for health research. However, if a reduction in sodium levels in restaurant foods is to be achieved, restaurants will need to provide required data and participate in efforts to adjust food preparation to use less salt. This is consistent with recommendations from the WHO SHAKE package, which emphasizes monitoring the sodium content of foods and harnessing industry to promote reformulating recipes to promote sodium reduction [2, 22].

Food prepared outside the home is a major source of sodium intake and has become increasingly popular, driven by limited time for meal preparation and rapid urbanization, which makes restaurant dining convenient [19, 23]. Restaurant foods are generally higher in sodium content than home-made foods because of their larger portion sizes, sodium-dense ingredients, and diverse flavour-enhancing condiments [14, 20, 24]. One study found that the median sodium content in Chinese restaurant servings was 2.5 g [24]. In the United States, national health survey data show that foods prepared in restaurants, including poultry, fish/seafood, and soups had higher proportions of sodium and contributed more to sodium intake than similar store-bought foods [20]. A 2022 Food and Agriculture Organization report found that Jamaica has experienced increased consumption of fast-foods, especially in urban areas [25]. While this study does not establish food consumption patterns of Jamaicans outside the home, the findings offers insight regarding the types of foods eaten away from home and preparation methods used by chain restaurants, which make up an important food environment segment. This suggests that frequently sold menu items with higher sodium levels should be prioritized for sodium reduction to help decrease overall sodium intake among consumers.

Only three restaurants reported having nutrition profiles and six offered customised meals. Therefore, for consumers who want to make healthier food choices, nutrient information would not be readily available. Consumers must therefore rely primarily on personal nutrition knowledge. However, by providing customised menu options, restaurants can facilitate customers making healthier, low-sodium meal choices tailored to suit their needs. This could potentially boost sales by providing value added options which could attract patrons with higher purchasing power. Restaurants could also consider using salt substitutes or enhancers, as is the practice of some leading Canadian fast-food chains, where more than 60% of their meals contain these ingredients [23]. A similar commitment of food industry stakeholders to reduce sodium by using lower sodium seasonings would help to reduce sodium consumption in Jamaica.

All restaurants surveyed used high-sodium-containing seasonings. However, the establishments did not provide specific ingredients and the information on quantities used was not sufficiently standardized to allow for meaningful quantitative analyses, so we were unable to estimate sodium content from these data. Given the use of multiple varieties of various forms of salt and high-sodium seasonings it is likely that the sodium content of these meals is high. To determine the overall contribution to the sodium content of these meals, the details of the ingredients used, and their quantities would be needed. Future studies could consider direct nutrient analysis of purchased food samples as an alternative approach to assess sodium content.

A positive finding was that low-sodium options were available in several restaurants. However, an effective population-wide public education strategy will be required to equip consumers with the knowledge to make these sodium reduction choices. Public education is an important strategy in the WHO SHAKE Package aiming to ensure consumers know how to make informed choices while eating out and create a demand for information surrounding nutrition content. The availability of healthier low-sodium alternatives in the local food landscape is beneficial and may contribute to the overall goal of achieving a population-wide sodium reduction. However, restaurants may not voluntarily reduce the sodium content of foods, and efforts may be inconsistent [22, 26]. One reason for this is that the promotion of low-sodium or healthier foods by restaurants may be perceived as having a reverse effect and reduce sales of the respective menu items. Laws requiring labelling of menu items may be necessary to effect change [27]. Some studies suggest that customers value taste over health where restaurant meals are concerned [13, 28]. As an alternative, restaurants may opt to reduce sodium content gradually without alerting the public to such changes [29]. A reduction in sodium of up to 20% may not be detectable by consumers and would provide restaurants with the opportunity to reduce sodium content and help in retraining consumers’ taste buds over time [27, 30]. Interventions must include partnerships between the government and the local food industry. Policies regulating the food industry and its contribution to overall sodium intake and sodium in the food supply may be necessary to harness their commitment and reduce the overall impact of NCDs on the Jamaican population.

Limitations

There are some limitations for this study. The sample size was small, and the response rate was low; therefore, the information obtained may not be generalisable to all restaurants in Jamaica; however, this study provides useful preliminary data. We sought to minimize selection and information bias by making at least four contact attempts and using trained interviewers with standardized procedures.

This study did not include stand-alone food establishments, which form an important part of the local food industry. The information obtained from chain restaurants represents an initial foray in the understanding of the food landscape in Jamaica and will serve as an impetus for further studies. In addition, specific ingredients and portion sizes were not provided; therefore, detailed data on sodium and nutrient contents were not available for the restaurants. This should be explored in future studies.
