CLIP-Powered Domain Generalization and Domain Adaptation: A Comprehensive Survey
Jindong Li, Yongguang Li, Yali Fu, Jiahong Liu, Yixin Liu, Menglin Yang*, Irwin King

Abstract
As machine learning evolves, domain generalization (DG) and domain adaptation (DA) have become crucial for improving model robustness across diverse environments. Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining (CLIP) plays a central role in these tasks, offering strong zero-shot capabilities that allow models to operate effectively in unseen domains. Yet, despite CLIP’s growing influence, no comprehensive survey has systematically examined its applications in DG and DA, underscoring the need for this review. This survey provides a unified and in-depth overview of CLIP-driven DG and DA. Before reviewing methods, we establish precise and complete scenario definitions covering source accessibility (SA vs. SF), source number (SS vs. MS), and label relations (CS, PS, OS, OPS), forming a coherent taxonomy that structures all subsequent analyses. For DG, we categorize methods into prompt optimization techniques that enhance task alignment and architectures that leverage CLIP as a backbone for transferable feature extraction. For DA, we examine both source-available approaches that rely on labeled source data and source-free approaches operating primarily on target-domain samples, emphasizing the knowledge transfer mechanisms that enable adaptation across heterogeneous settings. We further provide consolidated trend analyses for both DG and DA, revealing overarching patterns, methodological principles, and scenario-dependent behaviors. We then discuss key challenges such as realistic deployment scenarios, LLM knowledge integration, multimodal fusion, interpretability, and catastrophic forgetting, and outline future directions for developing scalable and trustworthy CLIP-based DG and DA systems. By synthesizing existing studies and highlighting critical gaps, this survey offers actionable insights for researchers and practitioners, motivating new strategies for leveraging CLIP to advance domain robustness in real-world scenarios. A continuously updated list of related works is maintained at: https://github.com/jindongli-Ai/Survey_on_CLIP-Powered_Domain_Generalization_and_Adaptation .

II-B
1
Problem Definition

Domain Generalization (DG) and Domain Adaptation (DA) both aim to handle distribution shifts between domains. As shown in Fig.
4
, DG trains a model on one or multiple source domains and evaluates it on unseen target domains without any target access. DA, in contrast, trains on a labeled source domain and adapts the model to a target domain, which is typically unlabeled. Thus, DG emphasizes robustness to unseen domains, whereas DA focuses on transferring knowledge to a specific target domain.

Definition 1.

Domain Generalization (DG).
Domain generalization refers to methods that train a model
f
on one or multiple
labeled source domains
\mathcal{D}_{s}=\{D_{1},D_{2},\cdots,D_{S}\}
.
The goal is to ensure that the model performs reliably on unseen target domains
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{D^{\prime}_{1},D^{\prime}_{2},\cdots,D^{\prime}_{T}\}
without accessing any target samples during training
. This setting emphasizes robustness to distribution shifts, enabling the model to operate effectively under diverse and previously unobserved conditions rather than merely fitting the specific datasets used during training.

Definition 2.

Domain Adaptation (DA).
Domain adaptation involves training a model
f
on one or multiple labeled source domains
\mathcal{D}_{s}=\{D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{S}\}
and adapting it to a target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{t}}
whose distribution differs from that of the source domains. The target domain typically lacks labels or provides only limited supervision, depending on the specific adaptation scenario. The objective is to adjust the knowledge learned from
\mathcal{D}_{s}
so that the model remains effective on
\mathcal{D}_{t}
despite the presence of distribution shifts.
Although DA includes multiple supervision levels, this survey focuses on the widely studied
unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA)
setting, where the target domain contains no labels.

Different Scenarios about Availability of Source Information

Source-Available (SA) scenarios provide labeled data from the source domain to enhance adaptation in the target domain and are further divided into Single-Source (SS) and Multi-Source (MS) methods, as shown in Fig.
5
. SS focuses on transferring knowledge from one labeled source domain, while MS leverages information from multiple labeled source domains for improved adaptation. As illustrated in Fig.
6
, within Source-Free (SF), there are two subcategories: Source-Data-Free (SDF) allows for extracting knowledge from the source model without direct access to labeled source data, facilitating effective adaptation. In contrast, Source-Fully-Free (SFF) prohibits any information from the source domain, requiring the model to rely solely on target data, which significantly increases adaptation challenges. Their specific definitions are as follows:

Comparison of single-source (SS) and multi-source (MS) scenario.

Definition 3.

Source-Available (SA).
Source-available refers to scenarios in which labeled data from the source domain
\mathcal{D}_{s}
is accessible for adaptation. This availability facilitates the transfer of knowledge gained from the source domain to improve performance in the target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}
.

Definition 3.1.

Single-Source (SS).
Single-source-based methods are specifically designed to tackle shifts in data distribution by enabling the transfer of knowledge from a labeled source domain
\mathcal{D}_{s}
to the target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}
.

Definition 3.2.

Multi-Source (MS).
Multi-source-based methods employ strategies to address shifts in data distribution by facilitating the transfer of knowledge from multiple labeled source domains
\mathcal{D}_{s}=\{D_{1},D_{2},\cdots,D_{S}\}
to the target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}
.

Definition 4.

Source-Data-Free (SDF).
Source-data-free refers to a setting in which no data from the source domain
\mathcal{D}_{s}
are accessible during adaptation. Instead, only a model pre-trained on the source domain is available. The adaptation process must therefore be carried out using only the unlabeled target domain data
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, which necessitates effective knowledge transfer without direct access to the source data.

Definition 5.

Source-Fully-Free (SFF).
Source-fully-free denotes a strict setting where no information from the source domain
\mathcal{D}_{s}
is available. Models rely solely on a general-purpose pretrained foundation model (e.g., CLIP) and perform adaptation directly on the unlabeled target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, without any supervision or auxiliary knowledge from
\mathcal{D}_{s}
.

II-B
3
Different Scenarios about Connection between the Source Domain and Target Domain

As shown in Fig.
7
, there are 4 relationship types that describe the connection between the source domain and target domain: closed-set (CS), partial-set (PS), open-set (OS), and open-partial-set (OPS) scenarios.

(i)
In the closed-set (CS) scenario, the source and target domains share an identical set of categories, and the model only needs to focus on minimizing the distribution discrepancy between the two domains.

(ii)
In the partial-set (PS) scenario, the target domain is a subset of the source domain’s categories,
requiring the model not only to align the shared classes but also to prevent target samples from being misclassified into source-private classes.

(iii)
In the open-set (OS) scenario, the target domain includes novel categories besides those seen in the source domain, and the model must identify these unknown classes and group them accordingly.

(iv)
In the open-partial-set (OPS) scenario, both domains share some categories while also having their own unique ones, posing a more complex challenge where the model must align shared categories and isolate private ones to handle severe category shift.
Their specific definitions are as follows:

Definition 6.

Closed-Set (CS).
Closed-set scenarios refer to situations where the label space of the target domain
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is the same as that of the source domain
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, such that
\mathcal{Y}_{t}=\mathcal{Y}_{s}
.

Definition 7.

Partial-Set (PS).
Partial-set scenarios involve situations where the label space of the target domain
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is a subset of classes from the source domain
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
,
such that
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\subset\mathcal{Y}_{s}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{t}\neq\emptyset
.

Definition 8.

Open-Set (OS).
Open-set scenarios encompass cases where the label space of the target domain
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
strictly contains the classes from the source domain
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, and introduces additional novel classes, such that
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\subset\mathcal{Y}_{t}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{s}\neq\emptyset
.

Definition 9.

Open-Partial-Set (OPS).
Open-partial-set scenarios refer to situations where the label spaces
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
,
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
of the source and target domains partially overlap while also having their own private classes, such that
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{t}\neq\emptyset
,
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\nsubseteq\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, and
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\nsubseteq\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. This indicates that there are some classes present in
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
that are not in
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, while also having classes in
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
that don’t appear in
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
.

II-B
4
Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP)

CLIP learns visual concepts by jointly training on images and their textual descriptions using contrastive learning, aligning images and text in a shared space, as shown in Fig.
8(a)
. This approach provides flexibility and strong generalization, particularly in zero-shot learning and domain adaptation tasks.
The model is based on a transformer architecture and utilizes a contrastive loss function to align image and text embeddings, enabling it to perform well across different tasks and domains without requiring task-specific fine-tuning, with its zero-shot capability (as shown in Fig.
8(b)
) allowing it to generalize to new tasks and domains directly from pre-trained models
[
144
]
.

Definition 10.

CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining).

Let
\mathcal{I}=\{I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{N}\}
represent a set of images, and
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{N}\}
represent a corresponding set of textual descriptions. CLIP learns a joint embedding space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by projecting each image and its associated text into this space. The objective is to maximize the similarity of paired image–text embeddings while minimizing that of mismatched pairs. This is achieved using the following contrastive loss
[
63
,
20
,
59
,
229
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{image}\rightarrow\text{text}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{t}_{i}/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{t}_{j}/\tau)},

(1)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}\rightarrow\text{image}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp(\mathbf{t}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{i}/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\mathbf{t}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}/\tau)},

(2)

\mathcal{L}_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\text{image}\rightarrow\text{text}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}\rightarrow\text{image}}\right),

(3)

where
\mathbf{v}_{i}
and
\mathbf{t}_{i}
denote the image and text embeddings of the
i
-th pair in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\tau
is a temperature parameter. This symmetric loss encourages paired samples to be close in the embedding space while pushing apart non-matching pairs.

III-B

CLIP is Adopted as Backbone or Encoder

CLIP is commonly integrated as a backbone or encoder in domain generalization approaches, serving key roles in feature extraction and performance improvement. There are two primary adoption methods for CLIP, as illustrated in Fig.
10

The first method
(shown on the left of Fig.
10

The second method
(shown on the right of Fig.
10

In both methods, CLIP serves as a flexible feature extractor, whether as a dynamic or static model, providing robust and semantically rich embeddings that facilitate effective cross-domain learning.

III-C

Discussion

Domain generalization (DG) has witnessed a growing number of CLIP-based studies in recent years.
To examine the current status and emerging trends, we refer to the results in Table
II
, where the Office-Home
[
182
]
, PACS
[
97
]
, VLCS
[
42
]
, and DomainNet
[
138
]
benchmarks are the most widely adopted datasets for evaluating cross-domain generalization.
Several consistent tendencies can be observed:

(i) Prompt optimization has evolved from single, handcrafted templates to diversified and deeper mechanisms.
Early prompt tuning (e.g., class-agnostic or class-conditional prompts) yields modest but stable gains over zero-shot CLIP; coupling prompts across text
and
image pathways and injecting prompts at deeper layers further improves robustness; recent prompt diversification/stylization systematically enlarges coverage of latent domain shifts, delivering the strongest gains on harder benchmarks.

(ii) Methods increasingly exploit CLIP’s intrinsic vision–language alignment for semantic-level generalization rather than explicit domain alignment.
By leveraging semantic anchoring via improved training objectives, constraints, or distillation, recent approaches transfer cross-modal knowledge more effectively to the visual encoder. Hybrid strategies that combine semantic cues with lightweight alignment remain present, but the dominant trend is semantics-first.

(iii) With a fixed ViT-B/16 backbone, the main improvements stem from better use of multimodal semantics and prompt design, not architectural complexity.
PACS
[
97
]
and VLCS
[
42
]
are near-saturated, while Office-Home
[
182
]
and DomainNet
[
138
]
reveal the benefits of semantic utilization and prompt diversity; performance differences primarily reflect how well methods capitalize on CLIP’s cross-modal priors.

(iv) Source-free (SF) variants offer practical trade-offs.
Without access to source data, SF methods employ stylized or synthetic prompts/adapters to approximate domain diversity and reach competitive mid–high performance, yet they typically trail the best non-SF counterparts on the most challenging benchmarks.

Overall,
CLIP-powered DG is trending toward
knowledge-driven, semantically grounded
generalization:
efficient and diversified prompt learning
combined with
cross-modal transfer
constitutes the primary path to improved robustness under a fixed backbone, suggesting future work on more realistic shift evaluations and narrowing the gap between
SA
and
SF
.

CLIP-based DA aims to achieve reliable adaptation from labeled source domains (except SFF scenario) to unlabeled or sparsely labeled targets under distribution shifts by exploiting CLIP’s transferable vision–language representations.

Source-Fully-Free Partial-Set Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (SFF-PSUDA) a.k.a. Partial-Set Unsupervised Fine-Tuning (PS-UFT)

In SFF-PSUDA(PS-UFT), the label space of the target domain
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is a subset of the predetermined list of known classes
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\subset\mathcal{Y}_{s}
. In this scenario, the model
f:\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{C}
is adapted to a target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{t}}
consisting of unlabeled samples,
with no access to either data from the source domain
\mathcal{D}_{s}
or any source domain knowledge (e.g., source-trained models or statistics on the source domain).

In this scenario, methods adapt CLIP to target domains containing only a subset of known classes by refining feature–prototype alignment and calibrating confidence for present categories. Existing studies primarily leverage the model’s inherent knowledge to maintain stable partial-set adaptation without requiring any source data.

Liang et al. [109]
shows that Universal Entropy Optimization (UEO) excels in this scenario by effectively adapting to target domains with a subset of source classes. This method enhances classification accuracy and demonstrates robust performance despite the absence of certain classes, highlighting its potential in real-world applications.

Source-Fully-Free Open-Set Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (SFF-OSUDA) a.k.a. Open-Set Unsupervised Fine-Tuning (OS-UFT)

In SFF-OSUDA(OS-UFT), the label space of the target domain
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
completely includes the predetermined list of known classes
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
and introduces additional novel classes, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\subset\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. In this scenario, the model
f:\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{C}
is adapted to a target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{t}}
consisting of unlabeled samples,
with no access to either data from the source domain
\mathcal{D}_{s}
or any source domain knowledge (e.g., source-trained models or statistics on the source domain).

Existing studies in this scenario adapt CLIP to target domains containing both known and novel classes by enhancing zero-shot discrimination and stabilizing confidence estimation under fully source-free conditions, enabling more reliable separation of unknown samples and recognition of known ones.

UOTA
[
126
]
improves pre-trained zero-shot models using open-set unlabeled data. It enables implicit OOD detection and enhances classification for known classes with high computational efficiency by updating only a lightweight adapter.
In addition,

Liang et al. [109]
shows that UEO excels in this scenario, enhancing classification of both known and new instances, and proving robust in dynamic environments with changing class distributions.
Recently,
CLIPXpert
[
106
]
leverages adaptive thresholding and SVD-based feature filtering to dynamically separate known and unknown classes, fully exploiting CLIP’s zero-shot capability without additional training or source information.

Source-Fully-Free Open-Partial-Set Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (SFF-OPSUDA) a.k.a. Open-Partial-Set Unsupervised Fine-Tuning (OPS-UFT)

In SFF-OPSUDA(OPS-UFT), the label space of the target domain
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
partially overlaps with the predetermined list of known classes
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, and they have their own private classes, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{t}\neq\emptyset
,
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\nsubseteq\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, and
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\nsubseteq\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. In this scenario, the model
f:\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{C}
is adapted to a target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{t}}
consisting of unlabeled samples,
with no access to either data from the source domain
\mathcal{D}_{s}
or any source domain knowledge (e.g., source-trained models or statistics on the source domain).

In this scenario, existing studies exploit CLIP’s zero-shot priors and entropy-based calibration to distinguish shared from private target classes, refining confidence estimation and decision boundaries to enable reliable adaptation under partial class overlap.

Liang et al. [109]
demonstrates that UEO enhances SFF-OPSUDA by leveraging common classes between source and target domains. It improves classification of known classes and adapts well to new, unseen classes, making it effective in partial class overlap scenarios.

Source-Data-Free Closed-Set Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (SDF-CSUDA)

In SDF-CSUDA, the label space of the target domain
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is identical to that of the source domain
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}_{t}=\mathcal{Y}_{s}
. In this scenario, a model
f:\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{C}
is adapted to a target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{t}}
consisting of unlabeled samples, with no access to data from the source domain
\mathcal{D}_{s}
,
but access to source domain knowledge (e.g., a pretrained model on the source domain).

Existing studies exploit pretrained source knowledge (e.g., model weights or stored statistics) to guide adaptation when source data is unavailable. Methods typically refine pseudo labels, calibrate prediction confidence, and distill reliable supervision from vision–language priors, enabling effective closed-set alignment under data-free constraints.

DIFO
[
174
]
introduces a distillation approach for multimodal models, combining prompt learning and knowledge distillation with regularization for improved reliability.
Similarly,
Co-learn++
[
225
]
enhances target adaptation by integrating pre-trained networks. It improves pseudolabel quality through collaboration with the source model and feature extractor, leveraging CLIP’s zero-shot classification decisions.
In addition,
CDBN
[
105
]
integrates source domain class semantics into unsupervised fine-tuning for target domain generalization, preserving class information while enhancing accuracy and diversity in predictions.
Moreover,

Tian et al. [178]
introduces BBC, a black-box domain adaptation method using CLIP. It improves pseudo-label accuracy through joint label generation from a cloud API and CLIP, along with a structure-preserved strategy refining labels using k-nearest neighbors.
Further,
ProDe
[
150
]
introduces a proxy denoising framework that corrects noisy VLM predictions through a reliable proxy. It models the dynamic effect of proxy’s divergence against the latent domain-invariant space and employs mutual knowledge distilling regularization.
Notably,
DUET
[
93
]
integrates task-specific and domain-invariant predictions through dual-perspective pseudo labeling, combined with a Tsallis mutual information–based uncertainty-aware optimization that dynamically balances exploration and exploitation during CLIP vision encoder fine-tuning.

Source-Data-Free Partial-Set Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (SDF-PSUDA)

In SDF-PSUDA, the label space of target domain
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is a subset of the label space of source domain
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\subset\mathcal{Y}_{s}
. In this scenario, a model
f:\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{C}
is adapted to a target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{t}}
consisting of unlabeled samples, with no access to data from the source domain
\mathcal{D}_{s}
,
but access to source domain knowledge (e.g., a pretrained model on the source domain).

Existing studies primarily address this scenario by refining pseudo labels, filtering irrelevant categories using vision–language priors, and stabilizing learning through mutual-information or proxy-denoising mechanisms, enabling CLIP to adapt effectively when only a subset of source classes appears in the target domain.

In this context, DIFO
[
174
]
employs mutual-information maximization to refine class-relevant signals within the partial label space, enabling accurate adaptation to the target class distribution without any labeled source data.
Colearn++
[
225
]
also demonstrates strong performance in this scenario, effectively adapting to target domains with a subset of source classes and improving classification accuracy.
In parallel,
ProDe
[
150
]
maintains reliable VLM guidance by filtering irrelevant categories through proxy confidence estimation, effectively reducing negative transfer from non-overlapping classes.

In SDF-OSUDA, the label space of the source domain
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
is a subset of the label space of the target domain
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\subset\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. In this context, a model
f:\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{C}
is adapted to a target domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{t}}
that contains unlabeled samples, with no access to data from the source domain
\mathcal{D}_{s}
,
but access to source domain knowledge (e.g., a pretrained model on the source domain).

Existing studies in this scenario primarily strengthen open-set robustness by refining CLIP-based supervision, distilling transferable semantics, and incorporating confidence-aware mechanisms to detect unknown classes while preserving reliable classification of known ones.

ODA with CLIP
[
210
]
investigates adaptable methods for leveraging CLIP, assuming access to a model pre-trained on source domain samples and directly using its parameters to initialize the model for the target domain.
Meanwhile,
DIFO
[
174
]
distills knowledge from the customized VLM to the target model, enhancing generalization and novel-class recognition while maintaining robust open-set performance and effectively leveraging unlabeled target data.
Moreover,

Zhang et al. [225]
further evaluates this scenario, showing effective identification of unseen classes while maintaining robust generalization in open-set scenarios.
Recently,
ProDe
[
150
]
adopts adaptive proxy denoising to correct VLM supervision and separate unknown samples.

IV-C

Discussion

Domain adaptation (DA) has witnessed substantial progress, with a growing number of CLIP-based approaches proposed in recent years.
To analyze the current status and emerging trends of this field, we focus on the widely adopted Office-Home
[
182
]
benchmark, which serves as a representative dataset for evaluating cross-domain adaptation.
Based on the analysis throughout this section and the comparative results summarized in Table
III
, several fundamental observations and conclusion can be drawn:

(i) Diminishing reliance on source data.
On ResNet-50, zero-shot CLIP averages 71.8, while SDF methods already approach or surpass most source-dependent ones; ProDe
[
150
]
reaches 81.1, a gain of 9.3. Within ViT-B/16, SDF with CDBN
[
105
]
attains 86.2, only 2.2 points behind the best source-dependent method CMKD
[
237
]
at 88.4. SFF-UDA methods also advance the frontier; ReCLIP
[
66
]
achieves 87.0 on average and surpasses most SA-UDA and SDF-UDA approaches, reinforcing the reduced dependence on source data.

(ii) Prompt tuning dominates yet remains sensitivity-prone.
Performance varies more on difficult transfers, especially toward the Clipart domain
[
182
]
. Methods emphasizing alignment distillation and consistency are more stable under large domain shifts; for example, CMKD
[
237
]
reports 81.2 from Real to Clipart and 79.4 from Art to Clipart.

(iii) Transformer encoders generalize better across domains.
Zero-shot ViT-B/16 reaches 82.4 versus 71.8 for ResNet-50, a margin of 10.6 points. This stronger prior raises the ceiling for both SDF and source-dependent methods and enables more flexible prompt and alignment designs.

Overall.
CLIP-driven DA is moving from
data-driven adaptation
to
knowledge- and prior-driven alignment
. The gap between SDF/SFF and source-dependent methods continues to narrow, while transferring under large domain discrepancy, open-domain robustness, and semantic consistency remain open challenges.

This section introduces common benchmarks (§
V-A
) and evaluation metrics (§

V-B

Common Metrics

Both DA and DG methods evaluate performance in the target domain. For DA, metrics depend on the category shift between source set
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
and target set
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. For DG, metrics are based on the shift between predefined
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
and the actual target set
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
. Specifically:

1. For
\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\mathcal{Y}_{t}
or
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\subset\mathcal{Y}_{s}
with
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{t}\neq\emptyset
cases (i.e., closed-set and partial-set scenarios): As there are no unknown classes in the target domain, prior methods typically use either the average class accuracy (ACC) or domain average accuracy across all transfer tasks to evaluate performance.

2. For
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\subset\mathcal{Y}_{t}
with
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{s}\neq\emptyset
or
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{t}\neq\emptyset
,
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\nsubseteq\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, with
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\nsubseteq\mathcal{Y}_{t}
cases (i.e., open-set and open-partial-set scenarios): During target domain evaluation, we commonly define
\mathcal{Y}_{shared}=\mathcal{Y}_{s}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{t}
as the set of shared classes. Samples within
\mathcal{Y}_{shared}
in the target domain are considered known classes, while others are treated as unknown classes. All unknown classes are grouped into a unified ”unknown” category. For evaluation, the model must identify these unknown class samples, and performance is measured using the harmonic mean of the known class accuracy (ACC) and unknown class detection accuracy (AUC), calculated as

HOS=\frac{2\times ACC_{know}\times ACC_{unknow}}{ACC_{know}+ACC_{unknow}}.

(4)

), while others calculate accuracy across all known class samples (e.g., ODA with CLIP

Challenges and Future Directions

As CLIP-based DG and DA advance, research is moving toward more realistic and multimodal settings with incomplete labels and restricted source availability. Progress is evident, yet challenges in generalization, knowledge usage, interpretability, and stability persist. Addressing these issues is essential for developing scalable and reliable CLIP-powered systems.

Such transitions mark an important step for CLIP-based DG and DA toward practical generalization, emphasizing efficiency, scalability, and adaptability under realistic constraints,
while also raising new challenges in handling uncertainty, long-tailed categories, and deployment-time robustness.

Although CLIP demonstrates strong vision–language alignment, its inherent knowledge remains limited to paired image–text supervision and lacks broader contextual reasoning.
