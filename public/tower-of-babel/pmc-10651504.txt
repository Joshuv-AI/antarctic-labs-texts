The impact of internet use in the digital age on the subjective well-being of older adults -- an empirical study based on CGSS2021 data
Authors: Lei Jin, Zebo Zhang, Feiyue Jing
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe main objective of this study is the effect of Internet use on the subjective well-being of older adults. Based on the China General Social Survey 2021 cross-sectional data, 11 variables were selected from the perspective of sociology and demography. These variables include one dependent variable, three independent variables, and seven control variables. A binary logistic model was used to study the effects of the three dimensions of Internet use in the digital age (independent variables) on the subjective well-being of the elderly (dependent variable) with the elderly as the research object (N = 1836). The results of the study revealed that Internet use (P < 0.001, Exp(B) = 1.567), information sources (P < 0.001, Exp(B) = 1.695), and frequency of Internet use (P < 0.001, Exp(B) = 0.858) of the elderly had significant effects on their subjective well-being. To improve the subjective well-being of the elderly in the digital era, this paper proposes the following suggestions and countermeasures: 1) strengthen the digital infrastructure for the elderly and create an “Internet + elderly” model; 2) formulate relevant protective policies to promote Internet use among the elderly; 3) regulate the frequency of Internet access to maintain a balance between the virtual and real world. Conclusion: A thoughtful digitalization approach for the elderly can ensure they keep up with the times, bridge the “digital divide” between generations, extend their working lives, and enhance their subjective well-being. This study has some limitations. The object of the study is only the elderly in China, while the elderly in every country in the world have certain differences in subjective or objective conditions.Keywords: Digital age, Internet use, Older adults, Subjective well-being

Introduction

Happiness in the current stage of the new era is not solely confined to materialistic aspects but extends to the spiritual realm of subjective well-being [1]. Since the 20th National Congress of the Communist Party of China, China has consistently issued a series of significant directives aimed at enhancing people's happiness and well-being, particularly considering the progressively aging population. The significance of improving the happiness of the elderly in advancing the national governance system is self-evident. According to the 51st Statistical Report on the Development of the Internet in China released by the China Internet Information Center (CNNIC), the number of Internet users in China has reached 1.067 billion, with a penetration rate of 75.6 %. This achievement has established China as the largest digital society in the world. The reasonable use of the Internet by older adults is beneficial to enriching their spiritual world and fostering the positive development of society. This paper aims to analyze and study the factors influencing the subjective well-being of older adults, utilizing data from the 2021 China General Social Survey Report (CGSS2021). A logistic regression model will be established using SPSS software. Based on the findings, targeted recommendations will be proposed to optimize the internet environment, enhance safety measures, fulfill the desires of older adults for an improved life, and enhance their sense of security and well-being.

The subjective well-being of the elderly is an overall assessment of their quality of life, encompassing their emotional and life satisfaction over time [2]. Factors such as old age, low educational attainment, and widowhood are strongly associated with lower Internet usage [3]. In the context of a rapidly aging population and the rapid development of information and communication technologies, the use of smart electronics, such as smartphones, as information technology devices, has a positive impact on the well-being of older adults [4]. This enables them to stay connected, active, and independent, ultimately increasing their overall well-being and sense of social belonging [5]. Internet use plays a positive role in influencing the subjective well-being of older adults, with online learning, social interaction, and entertainment being important channels for enhancing their well-being [6]. Additionally, individuals with lower levels of subjective well-being benefit more from mobile Internet use [7]. Geriatric technology, which combines gerontology and technology, can significantly improve the quality of life for older adults [8]. Digital technologies also contribute to health promotion and disease prevention in an aging world, as individuals aged 50 and older can independently utilize various digital technologies for their well-being [9]. However, older adults encounter challenges related to resistance towards Internet technologies and potential health risks associated with Internet use, which can impact their well-being across various dimensions such as enjoyment, personal growth, mastery, autonomy, and social connectedness [10]. While Internet information access can directly reduce loneliness among older adults, it can also disrupt existing social networks and potentially increase loneliness [11]. Internet use may affect older adults' life satisfaction by influencing their sense of social justice. To mitigate the negative effects of Internet use, it is crucial to improve older adults' attitudes towards Internet usage, focus on their sense of fairness, enhance life satisfaction, and guide them to spend less time online [12]. Addressing mental health problems among middle-aged and older adults in the digital society requires a focus on improving their subjective well-being and facilitating their integration into the digital society [13]. Digital literacy education programs are expected to help older adults engage in online welfare services, thereby increasing their social support, self-esteem, and overall well-being [14]. Careful exploration of the complex and multidimensional connections between older adults' subjective well-being and Internet use can deepen our understanding of their experiences in the digital world [15]. While actively promoting Internet use among older adults can enhance their quality of life, stimulate entrepreneurship, and increase social interaction, it is crucial to guard against the potential risks of excessive Internet access [16].

In summary, previous domestic and foreign studies have established a strong theoretical foundation for this paper and have provided objective and scientific evidence for selecting control variables. However, upon analyzing the existing literature, we have identified the following shortcomings in previous studies:1) Most Chinese studies have focused on the effect of Internet use on the happiness of the general population, with limited research specifically targeting the elderly as a disadvantaged group. 2)Previous foreign studies on the impact of the Internet on the subjective happiness of older adults have yielded varied conclusions, which may be attributed to regional disparities and uneven Internet development within China.3) There has been a dearth of quantitative studies examining the influence of the Internet on the well-being of older adults, while accounting for endogenous factors related to aging. This may result in biased conclusions and a lack of scientific validity. In light of these gaps, this paper aims to thoroughly explore the impact of the Internet on the subjective well-being of older adults in the digital age, building upon previous research and presenting detailed, objective, and scientifically supported arguments.

Methods

Research hypothesis

There is a significant effect of Internet use on the well-being of older adults.

There is a significant effect of information sources on the well-being of older adults.

There is a significant effect of the frequency of Internet use among older adults on their well-being.

Data sources

The data used in this paper are derived from the 2021 Chinese General Social Survey (CGSS2021), which represents the first nationwide, comprehensive, and continuous large-scale social survey project in China. The survey was initiated in 2003 and employed a multi-stratified random sampling approach, encompassing urban and rural households across 31 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities directly under the central government. To ensure the inclusion of valid target data, this study focused on the Internet use and subjective well-being of older adults, eliminating invalid and missing values. Consequently, a sample size of 1836 was obtained, upon which a logistic regression model was constructed to analyze the correlation between the two variables.

CGSS2021 questionnaire reliability and validity test

In this paper, a reliability test of the questionnaire content from CGSS2021 was conducted using reliability analysis in SPSS 26. Additionally; a validity test of the questionnaire content, primarily focusing on the subjective well-being scale (comprising 21 items), was performed through factor analysis in SPSS 26. The test results are presented in Table 1. The result of the reliability test shows that Cronbach's Alpha coefficient is 0.913, which is close to 1. This suggests that the questionnaire is highly stable and exhibits strong reliability. The result of the factor analysis test reveals that the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin Measure of Sampling Adequacy is 0.950, and Bartlett's Test of Sphericity yields a significance level (sig) of 0.000, implying that p < 0.05. These results imply that the questionnaire possesses high validity. In summary, the questionnaire can be considered a reliable and valid measurement tool for assessing subjective well-being.

Model setting

In this paper, the dependent variable, subjective well-being of older adults, has been defined as a binary outcome. The criterion “do you feel happy in your life” is categorized as either happy or unhappy. Consequently, a binary logistic model was selected for the analysis:

In binary logistic regression, the regression coefficients are denoted as βi, representing the change in the odds ratio of the occurrence and non-occurrence of an event when one unit of the independent variable changes. β0 is the constant term, and the p-value corresponds to the probability of occurrence. In this paper, the control variables are introduced in Model 1, and the remaining three key independent variables are successively incorporated into Models 2, 3, and 4.

Analysis of variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this paper is the subjective well-being of older adults. The well-being of older adults represents an emotional sublimation of their sense of security and fulfillment, emphasizing the genuine feelings and experiences of individuals [17]. In the CGSS2021 questionnaire, the dependent variable, happiness of the elderly, is specifically defined as “In general, do you feel that you are happy in your life?” The corresponding code is “A36”. The responses of “very unhappy” or “relatively unhappy” are categorized as unhappy, indicating a lack of happiness among the elderly, and assigned a value of “1". On the other hand, the responses of “relatively happy” and “very happy” are categorized as happy, indicating a sense of happiness among the elderly, and assigned a value of “2". Responses such as “I can't say whether I am happy or not”, “don't know”, “refused to answer”, and missing values were excluded and not included in this measurement.

Independent variables

Internet use refers to activities such as chatting, entertainment, and shopping conducted through various media terminals such as cell phones, computers, and connected TVs. These activities aim to fulfill the physical and mental needs of users.

Independent variable 1 was selected from the CGSS2021 questionnaire item “A285. Internet (including cell phone access), in the past year, your use of the following media:”. The response option “never” indicates no prior Internet access and is assigned a value of “1". On the other hand, responses such as “often” and “very often” indicate previous Internet experience and are assigned a value of “2". Responses such as “don't know”, “refuse to answer”, and missing values were excluded from the analysis.

Independent variable 2 was selected from the CGSS2021 questionnaire item “A29. Among the above media, which is your main source of information?”. The responses “newspaper”, “magazine”, and “radio” indicate a non-Internet source of information, and are assigned a value of “1". On the other hand, responses such as “TV”, “Internet”, and “Mobile phone news” indicate the use of the Internet as a source of information, and are assigned a value of “2". Responses such as “don't know”, “refused to answer”, and missing values were excluded from the analysis.

Independent variable 3 was selected from the CGSS2021 questionnaire item “A3012. In the past year, did you often surf the Internet in your free time?”. Those who responded with “every day”, “several times a week”, or “several times a month” are considered to have a high frequency of Internet use and are assigned a value of “1". Those who responded with “several times a year or less” are considered to have a low frequency of Internet use and are assigned a value of “2". Respondents who answered “never” indicate no Internet access and are assigned a value of “3″, indicating no frequency of Internet access. Responses such as “don't know”, “refused to answer”, and missing values were excluded from the analysis.

Control variables

In this paper, several basic demographic indicators were used as control variables in the regression equation. The following seven variables were included: 1) Gender: This variable was coded as a dummy variable, with “1″ representing male and “2″ rep\resenting female. 2) Age: This variable was recoded as a dummy variable based on the date of birth. The categories were as follows: “1″ for those born between 1957 and 1937, and “2″ for those born between 1936 and 1914.3) Political affiliation: This variable was recoded as a dummy variable, with “1″ representing mass affiliation, “2″ representing democratic party affiliation, and “3″ representing communist party affiliation. 4) Highest education Level: This variable was recoded as a dummy variable to represent different levels of education. The categories were as follows: “1″ for no education, “2″ for elementary or junior high school education, “3″ for vocational high school, general high school, technical school, or junior college education, “4″ for college specialist education, “5″ for college undergraduate education, and “6″ for graduate and above. 5) Physical health status: This variable was recoded as a dummy variable to indicate different levels of physical health. The categories were “1″ for unhealthy, “2″ for average health, and “3″ for good physical health. 6) Household registration: This variable was recoded as a dummy variable to represent different types of household registration. The categories were as follows: “1″ for agricultural household registration, “2″ for non-agricultural household registration, “3″ for resident household. 7) Marital status: This variable was recoded as a dummy variable to represent different marital statuses. The categories were “1″ for unmarried, “2″ for married, and “3″ for widowed. Please note that in the analysis, responses such as “don't know”, “refused to answer”, and missing values were excluded. Building model 1: the effect of control variables on subjective well-being of older adults

In the model, the logistic regression coefficient (β) represents the effects of the control variables, namely age (X1), gender （X2）， political outlook （X3）, highest level of education （X4）, health status（X5）, household affiliation（X6）, and marital status (X7). The impact of these control variables on the dependent variable, subjective well-being of the elderly, was initially examined. The results indicate that age, gender, political outlook, highest level of education, household affiliation, and health status all have significant effects on the subjective well-being of the elderly. These findings support the consistency of previous studies by scholars regarding the factors influencing subjective well-being. Notably, marital status has a negative correlation with the well-being of older adults, suggesting that being unmarried or widowed can impact their well-being.

Building model 2: the effect of whether older adults use the internet on their well-being

The core independent variable 1 (whether or not to use the Internet，X8) was added to the seven control variables in Model 1 to form Model 2. It was found that the significance of the previous control variables did not change significantly and remained significant. The addition of whether or not the elderly use the Internet has a significant effect on the dependent variable, indicating a significant association between the use of the Internet by older adults and their happiness. Exp(B) = 1.707 for the core independent variable 1 (whether or not to use the Internet，X8) in Model 2, indicating that the probability of experiencing happiness increases by 0.707 times for older adults using the Internet.

Building model 3: the effect of older adults' information sources on their well-being

Model 3 was formed by adding the core independent variable 2 (primary source of information，X9) to the seven control variables in Model 1, and Model 2 included the addition of the core independent variable 1 (whether or not to use the Internet，X8). It was found that there was no significant change in each parameter corresponding to Model 3 compared to Model 2, and the significance of the parameters remained significant. After adding the core independent variable 2 (primary source of information，X9), it was found that it had a significant effect on the dependent variable (subjective well-being of the elderly). In the Model 3, The core independent variable 2 (primary source of information，X9) EXP(B) = 1.695, indicating that the probability of experiencing well-being among the elderly increases by 0.695 times when they obtain information through the Internet.

Building model 4: the effect of the frequency of internet use among the elderly on their well-being

Model 4 was created by adding the third core independent variable (Internet frequency of the elderly，X10) to Model 3. It was observed that Model 4 did not exhibit significant changes compared to the parameters corresponding to Model 3, and the significance of the variables remained consistent. Upon adding the third core independent variable (Internet frequency of the elderly，X10), it was found to have a significant impact on the dependent variable (subjective well-being of the elderly). The odds ratio (Exp(B)) for the third core independent variable (Internet frequency of the elderly，X10) was calculated as 0.858. This implies that a one-unit change in the Internet frequency of the elderly results in a decrease of −0.142 times in their well-being. In other words, higher Internet frequency among the elderly is associated with a decrease in their subjective well-being.

In summary, all three hypotheses have been supported by the findings of this study. Hypothesis 1, which states that Internet use has a significant effect on the happiness of the elderly, is confirmed. Hypothesis 2, which suggests that information sources of the elderly have a significant impact on their happiness, is also supported. Additionally, Hypothesis 3, which proposes a significant relationship between the frequency of Internet use among the elderly and their happiness, is validated. Therefore, the study concludes that Internet use, information sources, and frequency of Internet use have significant effects on the subjective happiness of the elderly.

Discussion

Strengthen the digital construction of the elderly and create the “internet + elderly” model

To strengthen the digital inclusion of the elderly and mitigate the risk of their marginalization, the following measures are recommended: 1) Enhance digital skills education for the elderly: Conduct training programs on digital skills, application services, and security knowledge to improve the elderly's understanding, learning ability, and application skills in the digital realm. This will enable them to better integrate into the digital world. 2) Provide user-friendly and convenient digital services: Develop an exclusive digital service platform tailored to the needs of the elderly, offering customized application software and configurable Internet + services. This will cater to the diverse and personalized requirements of the elderly, with digital service categories such as healthcare, safety, emergencies, and entertainment. 3) Establish digital social networks for the elderly: Create a dedicated digital social platform for the elderly, organizing offline gatherings, volunteer services, and other activities. This will facilitate a digital community where the elderly can support each other, share experiences, and communicate. It aims to boost their confidence, foster their interest in digital life, and alleviate feelings of loneliness and isolation. 4) Promote digital products in a targeted manner: Promote digital products like tablet PCs, smartphones, smartwatches, and security infrared monitoring devices that align with the actual needs and usage habits of the elderly. This will allow them to experience the convenience and practicality of digital life more effectively. 5)Strengthen security and privacy protection: Pay special attention to data security and privacy protection for the elderly. Establish a robust privacy protection system tailored to their needs and ensure the stability and security of their digital life through technical measures and standardized certifications.

Formulate relevant safeguard policies to increase internet usage among the elderly

To enhance internet usage among the elderly and promote their digitalization process, the following safeguard policies can be formulated: 1) Increase policy awareness: Utilize various media channels, such as television, radio, newspapers, etc., to extensively publicize government policies that actively promote internet usage among the elderly. This will ensure that the older adults are aware of the government's initiatives and encourage their participation in digital life. 2) Provide subsidies for skills training: Introduce policies that offer subsidies and support for digital skills training among the elderly. This will alleviate financial constraints and increase their motivation to learn digital technologies. 3) Organize demonstration activities: Arrange regular demonstration activities on digital life specifically targeted at the elderly. Invite professionals to explain internet usage skills and the benefits of digital life, attracting elderly participation and enhancing their awareness of digital technologies. 4) Enhance community services for digital care: Improve internet access affordability by providing preferential internet access fees for the elderly. Establish a convenient internet service network and prioritize digital services for their benefit. This will increase the trust, interest, and frequency of internet usage among the elderly. 5) Encourage elderly-friendly online applications: Foster partnerships with online businesses to develop and promote applications and software tailored for the elderly. Enhance and upgrade existing products to enhance internet and digital product utilization and trust among the elderly. This will increase internet penetration and usage rates among the elderly population.

Control the frequency of internet access, balancing virtual and reality

Excessive internet use among the elderly can have negative effects on their health, leading to detachment from reality and an over-reliance on virtual cyberspace. To maintain a balanced approach and control the frequency of internet use, the following measures should be implemented: 1) Establish time limits: Set daily time limits for internet usage. During this designated time, focus on educational and recreational activities online, and once the time is up, shift the focus back to real-life communication and activities. 2) Set goals and create plans: Develop a daily internet use plan that outlines specific tasks to be accomplished, such as learning, work, and entertainment. This will help allocate time more effectively and maintain a balance between the virtual and real world. 3) Increase awareness: Maintain vigilance and awareness about the potential negative impact of excessive internet use. Avoid getting too absorbed in virtual activities and ensure that it does not interfere with real-life responsibilities and experiences. 4) Find alternatives: Substitute excessive internet use with other activities such as reading, drawing, writing, or engaging in physical exercise. Diversifying leisure activities can help reduce dependence on the internet. 5) Foster communication: Utilize the time that would otherwise be spent on excessive internet use to strengthen communication with friends and family. Investing in real-life relationships can enhance social connections and prevent excessive immersion in the virtual world of the internet.

Conclusion

In the digital age, Internet use has a significant impact on the subjective well-being of older adults. Older adults' access to information, participation in online social interactions, and enjoyment of online entertainment through the Internet positively affect their subjective well-being. The use of information technology devices, such as smartphones, can help older adults stay connected, actively participate, and live independently, thereby increasing their overall well-being and sense of social belonging. However, older adults face the dilemma of overuse when using the Internet, which negatively affects their health and well-being. Therefore, there is a need to be alert to the potential risks that overuse of the Internet may bring and to require older adults to use Internet technology wisely, balance virtual and reality, and reduce time spent online. In general, Internet use can promote the integration of older adults into the digital society, strengthen social connections and participation, extension of working life, narrow the “digital divide” with young people, and improve the quality of life and well-being. However, while promoting Internet use among older adults, appropriate protection measures need to be put in place to ensure that older adults have a positive and safe Internet experience.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The object of the study is only the elderly in China, while the elderly in every country in the world have certain differences in subjective or objective conditions. Therefore, scholars who study the same issue later should explore the topic based on the actual local conditions to understand the happiness of the elderly all over the world.
