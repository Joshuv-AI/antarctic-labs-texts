Abstract
Carbonate-silicate weathering feedback is thought to stabilize Earth’s climate on geologic timescales. If climate warms, faster mineral dissolution and increased rainfall speed up weathering, increasing CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} drawdown and opposing the initial warming. Limits to where this feedback might operate on terrestrial exoplanets with N 2 ​ – O 2 ​ – CO 2 ​ – H 2 ​ O \text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{\hskip 1.29167pt--\hskip 1.29167pt}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{\hskip 1.29167pt--\hskip 1.29167pt}\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{\hskip 1.29167pt--\hskip 1.29167pt}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} atmospheres are used to define the ‘habitable zone’—the range of orbits around a star where liquid water can be stable on a planet’s surface. However, the impacts on long-term habitability of randomly varying volcanic outgassing, tectonic collisions, and tectonic parameters (e.g., number of continental plates, size of plates, plate velocity) remain poorly understood. In this work, we present an idealized and broadly-applicable quasi-2D model of the long-term climate stability of abiotic Earth-twins. The model tracks atmospheric CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} as ‘disks’ collide, promoting uplift and supplying new weatherable minerals through erosion. Without resupply, soils become less weatherable and the feedback’s strength wanes, making a planet susceptible to catastrophic warming events or hard snowballs where the surface becomes frozen over. We find that tectonic uplift spurred by continental collisions cannot be the sole supplier of weatherable minerals within our model framework, as such climates either become uninhabitably hot (for complex life) as soils become leached of weatherable minerals or experience extreme swings in temperature over short timescales. This conclusion is strengthened when taking into account the destabilizing effects of outgassing variability and increasing stellar luminosity. In addition to frequent collisions, other resupply mechanisms for weatherable minerals, such as wind-driven dust transport, glacial erosion, and/or seafloor weathering, are likely required for long-term stability on Earth-like terrestrial exoplanets.



I
Introduction

Earth’s surface temperature has remained ‘habitable’–permissive of surface liquid water–over almost all of its history
(
S. Wilde et al. 2001
;
D. C. Catling & J. F. Kasting 2017
)
. The carbonate-silicate weathering feedback, with the first mathematical model proposed by
J. C. G. Walker et al. 1981
(hereon WHAK), has long been argued as the primary reason for Earth’s climate self-regulation. The bounds of where this feedback cycle can conceivably operate on terrestrial planets with
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{\hskip 1.29167pt--\hskip 1.29167pt}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{\hskip 1.29167pt--\hskip 1.29167pt}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}\text{\hskip 1.29167pt--\hskip 1.29167pt}\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
atmospheres
have been used to define the ‘habitable zone’ of extrasolar planets
(
J. F. Kasting et al. 1993
)
. Without a fast-acting (
\sim 100
ky) negative feedback on changes in Earth’s temperature, its habitability would quickly be jeopardized by small perturbations in incoming solar radiation (ISR) or volcanic outgassing of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

(
R. A. Berner & K. Caldeira 1997
;
R. E. Zeebe & K. Caldeira 2008
;
T. Tyrrell 2020
)
. Whether the weathering feedback truly acts as Earth’s thermostat or its impact on Earth’s climate stability is over-emphasized due to survivorship bias remains an open question
(
T. Tyrrell 2020
;
C. W. Arnscheidt & D. H. Rothman 2022
)
. Thus, probing this feedback is crucial to understanding planetary habitability.

The feedback balances the release of carbon dioxide gas (
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) into the atmosphere through
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown spurred by the dissolution of exposed silicate rocks in water (referred to as chemical weathering). Over time, these rocks become leached of minerals available for weathering, but the surface is resupplied through processes like tectonic uplift promoting erosion of the weathering zone. Greenhouse gases (GHGs) such as
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
are needed for liquid water at Earth’s surface, as Earth’s predicted surface temperature without the greenhouse effect is
-18\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
. The atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
reservoir has a short (
\sim$300\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}$
) residence time and can be perturbed by changes and imbalances in globally-integrated weathering or volcanic outgassing fluxes. These imbalances, in turn, directly affect the atmospheric
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and can lead to climate warming or cooling.

The feedback also can be overwhelmed by external perturbations, such as increasing incoming solar radiation (ISR), that may lead to a runaway greenhouse effect where surface oceans evaporate completely, driving all life extinct
(
C. Goldblatt & A. J. Watson 2012
)
, similar to what may have happened on Venus
(
J. F. Kasting 1988
)
. Conversely, low ISR and/or GHG concentrations can lead to ‘snowball’ events where the planet is entirely ice-covered
(
P. F. Hoffman et al. 2017
)
. Little work has been done to quantify which planetary parameters are important for an Earth-like planet’s stability despite a large variability in weathering spurred by plate collisions and changes in volcanic
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
outgassing rates. Work on the stochastic randomness of volcanic outgassing rates of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

(
R. Wordsworth 2021
;
M. Baum & M. Fu 2022
)
and continental fraction/geometry
(
M. Baum et al. 2022
)
over Earth’s history in the context of initiating hard snowballs suggests that biological feedbacks to the silicate weathering cycle may have prevented snowball events throughout the Phanerozoic Eon (
$541\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{a}$-
present). However, these models assume that outgassing, continental geometry, and atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
concentrations behave as a purely random (stochastic) process, and do not track how weathering leaches minerals from soil. Many of these models also include a variety of processes that are likely unique to Earth, and lack general applicability to ‘Earth-like’ planets.

Key open questions include: How does the land fraction of a planet affect its resilience? How does the rate of volcanic outgassing of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
affect its long-term climate stability? Is resupply of minerals for weathering via tectonic uplift from collisions sufficient for long-term habitable climate?

In this work we present an idealized model, titled
DISKWORLD
, to test the resilience of the carbonate-silicate weathering feedback presented in
K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014
and
M. J. Winnick & K. Maher 2018
against the randomness of continental plate collisions over geologic timescales for an Earth-like planet. In Section
II
we describe the carbonate-silicate weathering feedback including recent refinements. In Section
III
we describe our model. In Section
IV
we present the results of our simulations over a wide variety of possible plate tectonic setups as well as test the effects of varying outgassing fluxes and our model’s applicability to the faint young Sun paradox. In Section
V
we discuss our results. In Section
VI
we present our conclusions and discuss future work regarding the role of tectonic collisions in climate stability.

II.1
The WHAK Model

J. C. G. Walker et al. 1981
propose a model for global weathering from river catchments and suggest a negative feedback capable of keeping Earth’s climate stable on geologic timescales. In this model, the rate of silicate rock weathering,
W
(in
\mathrm{t}\,\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}^{-2}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}
), is set by the globally averaged temperature
T
, river runoff rate
q
(roughly proportional to the precipitation rate), and partial pressure of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
):

W=kqe^{\frac{T-T_{ref}}{17.7$\mathrm{K}$}}\left(\frac{p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}{p\textrm{CO${}_{2,\textrm{\,ref}}$}}\right)^{0.3}.

(5)

Reference values for
T
and
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
are typically set at
12\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
and 280 ppm, respectively. Note that the e-folding temperature becomes
13.7\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
if assuming the exponential relation of
q\propto e^{(T-T_{ref})/$60\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$}
used in
J. C. G. Walker et al. 1981
, but
17.7\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
when including it as a separate parameter. This approach has been used in numerous studies, including reconstructions of Earth’s past
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
inventory
(
Y. Godderis & Y. Donnadieu 2017
;
Y. Godderis et al. 2017
;
J. Krissansen-Totton & D. Catling 2017
, e.g.,)
and testing the model’s sensitivity to parameters such as land fraction, position, and variable volcanic outgassing (e.g.
R. J. Graham & R. Pierrehumbert 2020
;
M. Baum & M. Fu 2022
;
M. Baum et al. 2022
). However, the WHAK model does not track how soils evolve as they are leached of weatherable minerals; the weatherability factor
k
lacks a physical interpretation.

Equation
5
contains a negative (stabilizing) feedback—if
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
or temperature increases, weathering will increase, increasing
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown and thereby rebalancing weathering and outgassing fluxes. In addition, runoff

is expected to increase or decrease alongside temperature, further cementing the negative feedback. This model gives a roughly constant

K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014

\sim$15\text{\,}\%\,\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}^{-1}$

\alpha

II.2
The MAC Model

K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014
(hereon MAC) present a solute transport model of the carbonate-silicate weathering feedback. Their analysis also uses weathering flux and runoff data for modern rivers. In their model, the weathering flux of dissolved minerals to the ocean is related to the ratio of the mean fluid travel time through a reactive assemblage (
T_{f}\approx L\phi/q
) to the time required for the fluid to reach chemical equilibrium (
T_{eq}\approx C_{eq}/R_{n}
). As
T_{f}/T_{eq}
increases, solute concentration in the river asymptotes to a thermodynamic maximum concentration
C_{eq}
. The ratio of the two timescales,
T_{f}/T_{eq}
, is known as the ‘Damk
\ddot{\textrm{o}}
hler number’
(
D. F. Boucher & G. E. Alves 1963
)
. Here,
L
is the reactive path length that rainwater travels through,
\phi
is the porosity of the soil, and
R_{n}
is the reaction rate. Most cation release from weathering occurs in this upper layer of soil defined by
L
and not in the underlying bedrock
(
S. L. Brantley et al. 2023
)
.

Continuing to follow
K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014
,
R_{n}=\rho_{sf}k_{eff}AX_{r}f_{w}
, where
\rho_{sf}
is the ratio of solid mineral mass to fluid volume (defined as
\rho_{sf}=\rho/\phi
, where
\rho
is the soil density),
A
is the specific area for the minerals in question,
X_{r}
is the fraction of reactive material in fresh (unweathered) rock,
k_{eff}
is the weathering rate constant, and
f_{w}
is the fraction of fresh rock in the assemblage.
f_{w}=(1+mk_{eff}A\tau_{s})^{-1}
where
\tau_{s}
is the effective soil age (or soil residence time) and
m
is the molar mass of the weatherable minerals. Here we use
\tau_{s}
instead of the original
T_{s}
to avoid confusion with temperature
T
. Soil age is related to the erosion rate
E
(in
\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}
) as
E=h/\tau_{s}
, where
h
is the erosion zone thickness, and is meant to be a proxy for the amount of fresh rock available for weathering.
K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014
fit for
R_{n,max}
(
R_{n}
value when the assemblage is all fresh minerals,
f_{w}=1

\text{An}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{20}}}

K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014
introduce a quantity
Dw
to quantify the efficiency of solute production,

Dw=\frac{L\phi}{T_{eq}}=\frac{L\phi R_{n,max}}{C_{eq}(1+mk_{eff}A\tau_{s})}

(7)

(note that

\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

(in

(8)

and

Dw

In the high

by river runoff as predicted by Equation

kinetically limited
by
Dw
, asymptotically approaching

). Due to this assumption (

Although temperature does affect the kinematics of the dissolution reactions and thus
k_{eff}

\frac{k_{eff}(T)}{k_{eff}(T_{ref})}=e^{\frac{E_{a}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_{ref}}-\frac{1}{T}\right)},

We also include additions from
M. J. Winnick & K. Maher 2018

C_{eq}

(10)

M. Baum & M. Fu 2022

A key advantage of the MAC model is that we are able to track the time-evolution of weathering fluxes in aging soils. Previous implementations of the WHAK model assume that weathering is primarily constrained by local climate types and not soil properties. For example,
Y. Godderis et al. 2017

k
used in Equation
5
. However,
k

(
A. J. West et al. 2005
)
. Varying soil age allows us to simulate the effect of resurfacing that restores minerals for the carbonate-silicate weathering cycle. For these reasons, our model largely follows MAC instead of WHAK. We note that, as found in previous studies
(
M. Baum et al. 2022
)
, MAC underestimates weathering fluxes when applied on a global scale on the order of
\sim 3-4\times
lower than modern-day estimates of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
outgassing/silicate weathering fluxes from
C.-T. Lee et al. 2019
. There is some tension in recent estimations of global
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
weathering and outgassing fluxes, with most studies adopting a rate of
F_{vol}\approx$7.5\text{\,}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
(e.g.,
J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018
;
M. Baum & M. Fu 2022
;
M. Baum et al. 2022
), whereas some studies have estimated considerably lower outgassing rates (
\sim$3.4\text{\,}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
,
L. Coogan & K. Gillis 2020
), more in-line with our model’s output. In contrast to
M. Baum et al. 2022

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

B.4

Recent studies have constrained Earth’s globally-averaged climate feedback strength. Synthesizing laboratory dissolution data over various lithologies and accounting for the distribution of kinetic-, erosive-, and runoff-limited regions on Earth,

3.2\text{\,}\%\,\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}^{-1}
. Using the global average value of
Dw=$0.03\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
, we find a similar value of
\alpha=$3.5\text{\,}\%\,\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}^{-1}$
.

III.1
Disks

We model an Earth-sized planet (radius
R_{pl}=R_{\bigoplus}
). Disks are defined as spherical caps with radius
R_{disk}
, velocities that are randomized with mean speed
V_{disk}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{V}
, and randomized starting locations. We use Euler poles to describe disk motion, where disk centers follow a great circle around a rotational Euler pole (a randomly chosen longitude and latitude) chosen for each individual disk (see Appendix
A.2
). Collisions between disks do not alter their shapes and velocities remain constant over time. This conserves total disk volume while the total land surface area is allowed to evolve over time as disks slide under/over each other. Although it is possible that continental plates have slowed as Earth’s interior has cooled over Gyr timescales, this process is not well understood, and some studies have suggested that plate speeds were
slower
in the past (e.g.,
J. Korenaga 2006
). We prescribe
V_{disk}=$4\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
and
\sigma_{V}=$2\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
, in-line with modern-day estimates of continental plate speeds from
S. Zahirovic et al. 2015
.

For book-keeping, disks are sub-divided into equiareally spaced points that co-move with the disk’s center. Points are considered ‘colliding’ when they lie within the boundary of another disk. Collisions reset the effective soil age of the point to a value
\tau_{s,mountain}
, corresponding to the uplift of new soil and creation of mountain belts caused by plate collisions.
Points that are not colliding ‘age’ over time—their effective soil ages increase and thus erosion rates decrease. This represents local soils becoming depleted of cations available for weathering over time.

\tau_{s,mountain}
was determined by fitting
\tau_{s}
in Equation
7
to the
Dw
value defined in
K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014
for ‘mountainous’ regions,
0.3\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}
. This gives
\tau_{s,moutain}\approx 8000
yr.
\tau_{s}
values, alongside approximate erosion rates, for a variety of
Dw
regimes can be seen in Table

.

In order to estimate globally-averaged temperature

and
0.7L_{\odot}<L<L_{\odot}

L_{\odot}

S. Kadoya et al. 2020

p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

III.3
Precipitation and Runoff

The MAC model creates a climate-stabilizing negative feedback if rainfall and thus river runoff increase as a planet’s surface temperature increases. This happens due to precipitation balancing increasing evaporation rates. Following
R. J. Graham & R. Pierrehumbert 2020
, we linearize our globally averaged precipitation’s (
\overline{p}
) temperature dependence:

\overline{p}(\overline{T})=\overline{p}_{ref}(1+\epsilon(\overline{T}-\overline{T}_{ref})),

(11)

where
\overline{T}
is the globally averaged surface air temperature and
\epsilon
is the fractional change in precipitation per change in temperature measured at a reference temperature and precipitation value.
\overline{p}_{ref}
and
\overline{T}_{ref}
correspond to modern-day Earth values [
\overline{p}_{ref}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$

(
P. Xie & P. A. Arkin 1997
)
and
\overline{T}_{ref}=15\degree
C]. We set
\epsilon=$3\text{\,}\%\,\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}^{-1}$
following
R. J. Graham & R. Pierrehumbert 2020
. In reality,
\epsilon
likely varies significantly with latitude
(
S. Manabe et al. 2004
;
P. A. O’Gorman & T. Schneider 2009
;
P. A. O’Gorman 2015
)
. Zonally averaged temperature (per latitude
\Phi
) is solved for using the simplified treatment in
W.-C. Wang & P. H. Stone 1980
, i.e.

T(\Phi)=\overline{T}-$32.1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$\times\frac{3\sin^{2}\Phi-1}{2},

(12)

leading to a surface temperature of
29\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
at the equator and and
-19\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
at the poles for the pre-industrial value of
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
.
Zonally-averaged precipitation is then solved for via

p(\overline{T},\Phi)=\overline{p}(1+\epsilon(T(\Phi)-\overline{T}))=\overline{p}(1+\epsilon($-32.1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$\times\frac{3\sin^{2}\Phi-1}{2})).

(13)

Our simplified latitudinal dependence is compared to values for modern-day Earth
(
P. Xie & P. A. Arkin 1997
)
in Figure
3
. Recent studies have called into question the assumption that precipitation increases with increasing global temperatures (e.g.,
R. T. Pierrehumbert 2002
;
P. A. O’Gorman & T. Schneider 2008
;
G. Le Hir et al. 2009
), and climate models have shown that increasing temperatures may cause precipitation to
decrease
when hotter than
\sim 47-$57\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$

(
J. Liu et al. 2024
)
. In this study, we consider climates cooler than
40\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, which is within the semi-linear
p-T
relationships predicted by climate models in
J. Liu et al. 2024
. However, this effect needs to be taken into consideration when analyzing hothouse climates.

Again following
R. J. Graham & R. Pierrehumbert 2020
, we take river runoff
q
to be a linear function of precipitation:

q=\Gamma p.

(14)

(1-\Gamma)
represents the fraction of precipitation lost to evaporation before being converted to runoff. We set
\Gamma=0.259
based on globally-integrated runoff estimates from
G. Ghiggi et al. 2019
.

:
A comparison of modern-day Earth precipitation estimates
(

). Red and blue dotted lines indicate effects of a

Thus, the planet-integrated
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
weathering flux is

W_{{}\mathrm{SiO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}(t)=\frac{A_{land}(t)}{n_{grid}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{grid}}W_{i}(\tau_{s,i},k_{eff}(T_{i}(p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}),\Phi_{i}),

(15)

where
A_{land}
is the total exposed land area and
n_{grid}
is the total number of grid points.
A_{land}
varies with
t
because collisions reduce the total amount of exposed land area.
\tau_{s}
,
T
, and
\Phi
are also functions of
t
for each grid point.
Weathering flux
, described by Equation
15
differs from
weatherability
, which we define as the weathering flux corresponding to a configuration of disks at
given
values of
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
T
(
280\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}
and
13\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, respectively).
Weatherability
does not take into account the time evolution of the climate given imbalances in weathering and outgassing. A planet with a larger fraction of colliding area may have a lower weathering flux at low temperatures than one with a low fraction of colliding area at high temperatures, but will have a higher weatherability.

III.4
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
fluxes and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
Consumption

In order to relate the
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
fluxes calculated in Equation
15
to
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown, we interpolate the results of
J. Gaillardet et al. 1999
, who compiled the concentrations of dissolved solids (including
\text{Ca}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
,
\text{Mg}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
,
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
, and
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) and discharge rates for 60 large rivers. They also estimated the atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
consumption from silicate weathering for each river. We exclude 11 rivers that are presented as highly-polluted. By weighting the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
ratio of each river by its discharge of dissolved solids, we find an average molar
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
ratio of 1.087—for every mole of
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
weathered and flushed out to the ocean, 1.087 moles of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
are removed from the atmosphere (Figure
18
). Thus, the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown (in
\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\,{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}
) is

W_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}(t)=1.087\,W_{{}\mathrm{SiO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}(t).

(16)

III.5
Seafloor Weathering

Another possible source of negative feedback in the global carbon cycle is seafloor weathering (SFW). SFW occurs when seawater reacts with oceanic crust basalt to release soluble cations. Cations can then react with bicarbonate ion via Reaction
2
to remove inorganic carbon from the ocean-atmosphere system. Carbon drawdown from SFW on modern Earth is estimated as
\sim 8-23\%
of that of silicate weathering
(
C.-T. Lee et al. 2019
)
. Evidence suggests that this proportion could have been much higher in the Archean Eon, possibly being the dominant
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sink
(
K. Nakamura & Y. Kato 2004
;
J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018
)
.

The overall temperature,
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and ocean
pH
dependencies of seafloor weathering are not well-constrained. Due to this, we treat seafloor weathering as a constant source of weathering only dependent on global ocean fraction, i.e.,

W_{SFW}(\gamma)=W_{SFW,\oplus}\frac{(1-\gamma)}{0.7},

(17)

where
W_{SFW,\oplus}=$0.0054\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$=$0.45\text{\,}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
is calibrated to modern-day estimates for
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown due to seafloor weathering
(
L. A. Coogan & K. M. Gillis 2013
;
J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018
)
and
\gamma
is the surface area land fraction. We explore including more complex relationships between seafloor weathering, ocean pH, and temperature at the pore spaces where weathering occurs in Section
IV.5
. Including SFW as an effective ‘baseline’ weathering flux prevents trials with low amounts of land-area/initial collisions from having unrealistically low initial outgassing fluxes.

III.6
Outgassing and Time Evolution

The model calculates the volcanic outgassing flux of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
F_{vol}
, by assuming that outgassing and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown from silicate weathering are initially in balance:

F_{vol}=1.087\,W_{{}\mathrm{SiO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}(t=0)+W_{SFW}.

(18)

This leads to different outgassing fluxes for different continental configurations, with higher
F_{vol}
for setups with initially colliding disks.
F_{vol}
remains constant throughout each simulation run (changed in Section
IV.3
). While
F_{vol}
has likely changed significantly over Earth’s 4 Gyr history (e.g.,
J. Krissansen-Totton & D. Catling 2017
), the exact long-term behavior of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
outgassing is not well-constrained, and we aim to isolate the effects of tectonic noise on long-term habitability.

Initial
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and temperature conditions are chosen to mimic that of pre-industrial Earth [
p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}(t=0)=$280\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
,
\overline{T}(t=0)=$13\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
]. Zonal temperatures are calculated as a function of
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(Section
III.2
). Disk locations, global weathering fluxes, ocean and atmospheric carbon reservoir content, temperature, and ocean pH are all calculated in adjustable time steps. We use an adaptive time step
dt\gg 1000
years so that the ocean and atmosphere equilibrate. Changes in the total inorganic carbon reservoir are calculated per time step as

\frac{\Delta C(t)}{dt}=W_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}(t)-F_{vol}.

(19)

We then calculate the effects of
\Delta C
on ocean-atmosphere partitioning (see Appendix
A.3
for details). At each time step we record globally-integrated
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown weathering flux, atmospheric
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, ocean dissolved inorganic carbon content, and globally averaged surface temperature
T
. An example run of the
DISKWORLD
model is shown in Figure
4
.

III.7
Climate Catastrophes

In order to study the climate stability of a given setup, we monitor for two possible ‘catastrophes’—uninhabitably hot ‘catastrophic warming’ events and hard snowball states.

III.7.1
Catastrophic Warming

The effect of adding large amounts of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
to the atmosphere on surface temperatures has been studied in detail. While the runaway greenhouse—where greenhouse gases effectively prevent the planet from cooling until reaching extremely high surface temperatures—is likely unreachable at present-day or lower solar luminosity
(
C. Goldblatt et al. 2013
)
, several studies suggest that a ‘moist greenhouse’ state, where water vapor is able to bypass the atmospheric cold trap and reach high concentrations in the stratosphere, may occur for
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
as low as 6 mbar
(
R. Ramirez et al. 2014
)
or 10 mbar
(
J. F. Kasting & T. P. Ackerman 1986
)
(
\sim 27-$29\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
globally-averaged temperature in our model). This moist state can lead to the gradual loss of a planet’s ocean due to enhanced photolysis of water vapor in the upper atmosphere followed by hydrogen escape to space. However, the
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
threshold for entering a moist greenhouse is still debated (e.g.,
E. Wolf & O. Toon 2015
), and atmospheric loss would need to be modeled in detail to determine ocean-loss timescales.

Instead of focusing on these two ‘greenhouse’ scenarios, we consider an uninhabitably hot average surface temperature of
40\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, corresponding to the life cycle limits for most eukaryotes
(
A. Clarke 2014
)
. While this would not necessarily spell the end of all life on Earth (bacteria and archaea can grow in temperatures as hot as
\sim$100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
and
\sim$122\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
, respectively), this represents a significant loss of biodiversity and loss of most complex life. This also roughly corresponds to the temperature regime past which precipitation in expected to decrease with increasing temperature
(
J. Liu et al. 2024
)
. As this mechanism is essential for the carbonate-silicate weathering feedback, this temperature would also correspond to the breakdown of the feedback cycle. In addition, the behavior of precipitation cycles themselves may be significantly altered past this threshold, possibly resulting in periodic episodes of extremely high rainfall rates
(
J. T. Seeley & R. D. Wordsworth 2021
)
.
This threshold corresponds to 0.11 bar
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and a total ocean + atmospheric carbon inventory of
\sim$458\,000\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{C}$
(
11\times
increase) for present-day solar luminosity.

III.7.2
Hard Snowball States

Hard snowball states occur when the entirety of a planet’s oceans become frozen over at the surface. These are distinct from ‘waterbelt’ or ‘slushball’ states, where oceans near the equator may remain unfrozen. Snowball states can occur when GHG concentrations and subsequently global temperatures become low enough to trigger a runaway ice-albedo feedback
(
R. Pierrehumbert et al. 2011
)
. While the cause of Earth’s snowball events is contentious, it has been suggested that snowball states may be initiated by the rapid introduction of low-latitude mountain belts that increase global weathering rates (e.g.,
M. M. Joshi et al. 2019
).

Water ice reflects
\sim 60\%
of sunlight, more than land (
\sim 30\%
) or open oceans (
\sim 10\%
)
(
J. Haqq-Misra et al. 2016
;
S. Kadoya & E. Tajika 2019
, e.g.,)
. When climate cools and polar ice forms, reflection of sunlight by ice causes further surface cooling which in turn leads to more ice. This can eventually lead to total glaciation of the surface. Evidence suggests that Earth escaped such snowball states
(
J. L. Kirschvink 1992
)
; freezing soil curtails weathering, allowing for buildup of atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
via volcanism, eventually warming the planet enough to melt the ice. If the poles become cold enough for
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
to condense, the snowball would likely become inescapable, although this effect is likely only important for planets that receive significantly (
\lesssim 0.62S_{\oplus}
) less instellation than modern-day Earth
(
R. Pierrehumbert et al. 2011
;
M. Turbet et al. 2017
)
.

In our simulations, we treat the hard snowball case as a ‘climate catastrophe’. While the Earth has likely survived at least two clusters of snowball events (it is still unclear whether these were hard or waterbelt snowball states), including these events helps us better represent the
overall
climate stability of varying tectonic setups. In an astrobiological point-of-view, a hard snowball would almost certainly prevent the detection of molecular biosignatures due to life being trapped under the ice. Moreover, modeling exactly
how
the climate escapes a Snowball Earth episode is still an active area of research (e.g.,
Y. Goddéris et al. 2011
;
S. Kadoya & E. Tajika 2019
).

The exact
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/temperature threshold for initiating a runaway ice-albedo feedback is still debated and is sensitive to a suite of tunable (largely unconstrained) parameters, including snow/ice albedos, heat transport efficiency during a highly-glaciated state, and cloud feedbacks.
R. Pierrehumbert et al. 2011
show that in a simple 0-D model, temperatures as high as
7\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
could initiate runaway glaciation, a threshold used by
R. Wordsworth 2021
and
M. Baum & M. Fu 2022
. 3-D general circulation model (GCM) simulations from
R. Pierrehumbert et al. 2011
suggest
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
values of
\sim 1000-$2000\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
(at
94\%\,L_{\odot}
) initiate a hard snowball, whereas
J. Yang et al. 2012
find that values as low as
18\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}
are needed.
Studies of the cycling between hard snowball and deglaciated states find runaway initiation global-average temperatures of
\sim$-8\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$

(
A. Paradise & K. Menou 2017
;
S. Kadoya & E. Tajika 2019
)
to
\sim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$

(
K. Menou 2015
)
.

Due to the above discrepancies, we use a snowball initiation global temperature of
0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, corresponding to the freezing point of surface water. We note that, even with this seemingly high threshold temperature, we expect to underestimate the amount of snowball events due to the lack of ice-albedo feedback in our
\overline{T}(p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}})
relationship, where
0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
corresponds to
11\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}

p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and a total ocean + atmospheric carbon inventory of
\sim$20\,000\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{C}$
(
\sim 2\times
decrease).

We consider effects of overall land area coverage and number of disks on climate stability. We vary total land area as
A_{land,\bigoplus}/10
,
A_{land,\bigoplus}/3
,
A_{land,\bigoplus}/2
,
A_{land,\bigoplus}
,
3A_{land,\bigoplus}/2
, and

A_{land}=A_{land,\bigoplus},N_{disk}=8
scenario represents the ‘Earth-like’ case. We run each trial for four billion years, comparable to the length of Earth’s history of surface water
(
S. Wilde et al. 2001

E. B. Watson & T. Harrison 2005

and possibly life
(

IV.1
Catastrophically Low Survival

Results for our initial trials can be seen in Figure
5
.
In these trials, which show an average survival rate of only
16\%
over 4 Gy, the fraction of fresh minerals available for weathering (and thus
\tau_{s}
) is only reset by disk collisions. Low survival rates occur because both total
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown due to weathering and the feedback climate strength
\alpha
decrease drastically as soils age without collisions. Thus, worlds require constant resupply of cations via collisions to keep the balance between weathering and outgassing stable. Trials that began with no colliding disks died within
\lesssim 1
Gy due to this effect. Those that did survive generally contained a substantial amount of colliding area throughout the 4 Gy run but had extreme variability in their
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
values over time, suggesting that these climates are not particularly stable. We conclude the climate of worlds where the amount of silicate weathering is
only
determined by the MAC model, including
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
T
dependencies of weathering reactions, are inherently not stable and (in most cases) require additional negative feedbacks to remain habitable over geologic timescales.

IV.2
Inclusion of a Maximum Effective Soil Age

On Earth, several processes contribute to cation resupply that are independent of processes modeled in
K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014
and are not included in our idealized model. Up to
\sim 10\%
of modern-day silicate weathering involves windblown dust, increasing to up to
\sim 50\%
in tectonically inactive regions
(
G. E. Hilley & S. Porder 2008
)
. Dust input aids weathering in tectonically inactive regions by resupplying cations required for Reaction
4
without tectonic activity.

Physical erosion causes the weathering front to descend into the underlying bedrock, exhuming fresh minerals. Physical erosion rates vary across the globe and are linked to tectonic uplift and precipitation. Rates vary from
4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
in tectonically inactive regions like Sri Lanka to
3000\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
in the Himalayas
(
D. Granger 2007
;
E. Portenga & P. Bierman 2011
)
and are inversely proportional to the soil residence time (or soil age,
\tau_{s}
). Evidence suggests that the presence of glaciers can also speed up local erosion rates, and that the existence of glacial-interglacial cycles may be important in resupplying cations for weathering
(
C. S. Riebe et al. 2004
)
.

We now apply a maximum effective soil age
\tau_{s,max}
, so that soils never become completely leached of weatherable minerals and always retain a minimum amount. We assume that weathering in tectonically inactive regions corresponds to, on average, the ‘cratonic’ value of
Dw=$0.01\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
defined in
K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014
, based on river solute data of tectonic inactive regions. This yields
\tau_{s,max}\approx$600\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}$
and corresponding to a ‘fresh rock’ fraction of
\sim 2\%
. The inclusion of a maximum effective soil age increases the average survival rate over 4 Gy to
95\%
. Aggregate results can be seen in Figure
6
. Almost all of the catastrophic warming events resulted from trials with initially colliding disks that broke up over the course of the trial runtime—as continents that were previously colliding break-up, soils quickly become leached of cations, gradually reducing the weatherability, overall weathering
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown, and the strength of the planet’s overall weathering feedback.

Many of our catastrophic warming collision breakup events are accompanied by a continental drift polewards (shown by an increase in average latitude), which can also contribute to
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
buildup by drastically reducing the amount of precipitation available for weathering. These trials suggest that both frequent continental collisions
and
an additional cation resupply mechanism are required for climate stability on geologic timescales.

IV.3
Volcanic Outgassing Variation

Volcanic outgassing (
F_{vol}
) has fluctuated throughout Earth’s history [for compilations of Phanerozoic outgassing estimates, see
Y. Godderis & Y. Donnadieu 2017
and
J. Krissansen-Totton & D. Catling 2017
]. The trials of Section
IV.1
-Section
IV.2
likely overstate the stability of runs with low amounts of outgassing where perturbations due to tectonic noise are few and far between due to the low amount of collisions in low
A_{land}
trials.

We follow the approach of
M. Baum & M. Fu 2022
in modeling stochastic time-variability of
{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}
outgassing, where volcanic outgassing evolves as

\frac{dF_{vol}}{dt}=\frac{1}{\tau}(\mu_{vol}-F_{vol})+\sigma B(t),

(20)

where
\mu_{vol}
is a prescribed ‘average’ outgassing flux,
\tau
is the relaxation timescale,
\sigma
represents the strength of variations, and
B(t)
is a Gaussian noise term (with a mean of 0, standard deviation of 1) evaluated at each timestep. High values of
\tau
represent
longer-term
variations whereas high values of
\sigma
represent
stronger
variations. For finite timesteps this is accomplished via an Euler–Maruyama method,

\Delta V=\frac{1}{\tau}(\mu_{vol}-F_{vol})\Delta t+\sigma\mathcal{N}(0,1)\sqrt{\Delta t}.

(21)

This approach assumes that outgassing tends towards a long-term mean
\mu_{vol}
due to a negative feedback mechanism, which may not be the case. Nevertheless, this approach is a reasonable starting point. We scale the noise term by the starting outgassing flux, causing trials to experience the same
fractional
variation, so that low
F_{vol}
trials are not inherently more unstable,

\sigma^{*}=\sigma\times\frac{\mu_{vol}}{$0.084\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$},

(22)

where
0.084\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}
(
7\text{\,}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}
) is the value of
\mu_{vol}
prescribed in
M. Baum & M. Fu 2022
. In our model,
\mu_{vol}
is set by assuming
\mu_{vol}=F_{vol}(t=0)
.

We test the effects of variable volcanic outgassing by both: (1) varying the number of disks and total land area as in previous trials and (2) varying the strength
\sigma
and timescale
\tau
of variations. In scenario (1), we use ‘intermediate’ values of
\tau=$30\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}$
and
\sigma=$2.4\text{\times}{10}^{-6}\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{C}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
(
2\text{\times}{10}^{-4}\text{\,}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}
) from
M. Baum & M. Fu 2022
.
Results can be seen in Figure
8
. These runs show that planets with low amounts of land area are particularly susceptible to variations in volcanic outgassing, due to the lack of tectonic collisions replenishing weatherable minerals and thus lack of a strong stabilizing feedback. Furthermore, the strength and duration of variability have a profound effect on climate stability, with few trials surviving with
\tau\gtrsim$50\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}$
or
\sigma\gtrsim$5\text{\times}{10}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
.
M. Baum & M. Fu 2022
note that determining
\tau
and
\sigma
values appropriate for Earth is difficult and that these values also likely evolve with time, but that estimates of Phanerozoic outgassing rates hint that
\tau\gtrsim$100\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}$
for Earth.

Both scenarios show higher rates of snowball events than previous trials relative to catastrophic warming events. This is due to carbon depletion required to initiate a snowball (
\sim$21\,000\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{C}$
) being much less than the carbon addition required to initiate catastrophic warming (
\sim$420\,000\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{C}$
) with our assumption of
\overline{T}_{0}=$13\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
, thus causing more susceptibility to source-based perturbations.

IV.4
Faint Young Sun and Solar Luminosity

Solar luminosity has risen by
\sim 40\%
over the last 4 Gy
(
J. N. Bahcall et al. 2001
)
. It is believed that GHG concentrations on early Earth must have been much higher to compensate for this ‘Faint Young Sun’ paradox
(
M. H. Hart 1978
;
J. C. Walker 1985
;
B. Charnay et al. 2020
)
. Despite a
\sim$20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
colder equilibrium temperature, it is thought that water has been present on Earth’s surface since
\sim 4.4
Gya
(
S. Wilde et al. 2001
)
, and that Earth has been ice-free for most of its history
(
D. Evans 2003
)
. At low solar luminosity, GHG concentrations much higher than present atmospheric levels would have been required to sustain ice-free conditions
(
B. Charnay et al. 2020
)
. Whether this gas was
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, methane (
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
), or a more exotic species is still up for debate.

We investigate how
DISKWORLD
’s
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
concentrations evolve with changing solar luminosity. In our model, increasing solar luminosity warming the surface would increase global weathering. The increased weathering would drawdown
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, acting as a negative feedback to the initial surface warming. Thus, we would expect the atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
concentration to decrease over time.

Following
J. Krissansen-Totton & D. Catling 2017
(originally introduced in
D. Gough 1981
), we assume that solar luminosity evolves with time as:

L(t)=\frac{L_{\bigodot}}{1+\frac{2}{5}(1-\frac{t}{4.57\textrm{ Gy}})},

(23)

where
L_{\bigodot}
is present-day solar luminosity. We test the effects of changing luminosity on
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and temperature over time. We prescribe an initial start time of 3 Gya (
t=$1.57\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{y}$)
, corresponding to a starting luminosity of
0.79L_{\odot}
.

We solve for initial conditions assuming
T(t=0)=$13\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
as with previous trials, leading to an initial
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
of
86.5\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}
and an ocean reservoir of
321\,050\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{C}
. The evolution of geothermal heat flow and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
outgassing fluxes is modeled after
J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018
. In their framework, the
relative
geothermal heat flow
Q
changes over time as

Q(t)=t^{-n_{out}},

(24)

where
n_{out}
is an assumed exponential dependence.
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
outgassing is related to
Q
by

\frac{F_{vol}}{F_{vol,0}}=\left(\frac{Q}{Q_{0}}\right)^{m},

(25)

where
F_{vol,0}
is the initial outgassing flux,
Q_{0}
is the initial geothermal heat flux, and
m
is another assumed exponential dependence. Here, we adopt the median values of
n_{out}
and
m
used in
J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018
(0.365 and 1.5, respectively), noting that these values are poorly constrained for the Earth. These values cause
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
outgassing to decrease by
\sim 44\%
over 3 Gy.

Model results for evolving solar luminosity can be seen in Figure
9
. These trials show a stark contrast with our fixed luminosity trials of Section
IV.2
; survival rate is only 32% despite including a maximum effective soil age. Trials that do survive require continuous frequent collisions in order to replenish minerals available for weathering, reinforcing our previous results. The high rate of snowball catastrophes (48%) is associated with trials that begin with a low amount of colliding land and experience short-term collisions augmented by latitudinal drift towards the equator, both effects increasing weathering rates substantially. Catastrophic warming events correspond to short-term break-up of a large area of colliding disks, causing weathering rates to plummet and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
to gradually rise. Survival rates as a function of disk number and land fraction are shown in Figure
10
.

IV.5
Temperature/
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-Dependent Seafloor Weathering

Recent models have strongly suggested that seafloor weathering (SFW) exhibits a temperature and pH dependence that acts as an additional stabilizing feedback to the slow carbon cycle
(
J. Krissansen-Totton & D. Catling 2017
;
J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018
)
. In this model, the rate of seafloor weathering
W_{sf}
depends on the pH (
{}\mathrm{pH}=-\log_{10}{[{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{+}}]}
) and temperature of the pore-space where dissolution occurs,

W_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}},sf}\propto r_{spread-rate}\,e^{\frac{-E_{bas}}{RT_{pore}}}\,[{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{+}}]_{pore}^{\gamma}.

(26)

Here
r_{spread-rate}
is the rate of seafloor spreading introducing new minerals available for weathering,
\gamma
represents the pH dependence of seafloor weathering,
E_{bas}
is the apparent activation energy for basalt dissolution (note that this is different from the quantity
E_{a}
), and
T_{pore}
is the deep water temperature in the pores where dissolution is occurring. We can directly calculate the pH of the ocean at any given time following methods in Appendix
A.3
. Following
J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018
, we assume
T_{pore}
(in K) is linearly related to the average global surface temperature,

T_{pore}=1.02\times\overline{T}-$16.7\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$.

(27)

Seafloor weathering in our model is assumed to cease when the pore temperature is below the freezing point of seawater, assumed to be
-2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
(corresponding to
\overline{T}=$9\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
). For stability purposes, we ensure
W_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}},sf}
is continuous by adding an additional
(T_{pore}-$271\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$)/($2\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$)
multiplicative term if
T_{pore}
is below
0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
.

Using median values of
\gamma=.25
and
E_{bas}=$80\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{J}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}$
from
J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018

(
B. P. Hayworth & B. J. Foley 2020
, following)
, we obtain a climate feedback strength of
\alpha=$16.2\text{\,}\%\,\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}^{-1}$
at
T=$12\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
. Simpler models of seafloor weathering used to constrain
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
values for ancient Earth from
N. H. Sleep & K. Zahnle 2001
, where

W_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}},sf}\propto p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{0.7},

(28)

show a similar climate feedback strength of
15.2\text{\,}\%\,\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}^{-1}
.

These high values of
\alpha
imply that seafloor weathering fluxes are much more sensitive, and thus more climate-stabilizing, to perturbations in global temperature than continental silicate weathering. While continental weathering likely dominates the silicate weathering feedback on modern-day Earth, the stabilizing effect of seafloor weathering would be stronger for worlds with a higher global ocean fraction, assuming active tectonic resurfacing on the ocean floor
(
C.-T. Lee et al. 2018
;
C.-T. Lee et al. 2019
)
. This would help stabilize worlds that see few continental plate collisions. While many models infer relationships based on atmospheric
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and proxies for ocean pH, which are also heavily affected by other unrelated processes, isotopic analyses have begun to constrain temperature and
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
dependencies of SFW (e.g.,
L. A. Coogan & S. E. Dosso 2015
;
L. A. Coogan & K. Gillis 2018

V
Discussion

V.1
Lack of Hard Snowballs

Very few trials without outgassing variations (Section
IV.1
and
IV.2
) with fixed present-day solar luminosity ended in the snowball climate catastrophe described in Section
III.7.2
. While evidence suggests that multiple snowball events have occurred on Earth in the past (e.g.,
P. F. Hoffman et al. 2017
), our model’s behavior is not unexpected. As solar luminosity increases, the amount of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
required to trigger a runaway glaciation of the Earth’s surface decreases significantly. The value of
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
at which this runaway occurs greatly affects the likelihood of our trials resulting in a snowball climate catastrophe. Even in highly-aged soils, the weathering feedback strength
\alpha
is strong enough to prevent
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
reaching the snowball threshold at present-day luminosity (
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
=
11\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}
). Climate feedback strength (
\alpha
) in the MAC model increases drastically as the global average surface temperature
\overline{T}
decreases (Figure
2
). For mountainous soils at the equator,
\alpha
increases by
39\%
for a temperature change
$15\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$\rightarrow$5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}$
. This value increases to
192\%
for cratonic soils. This means that, in our model, colder climates are more efficient in rebalancing weathering and outgassing fluxes.

In addition, the climate feedback strength
\alpha
of both the WHAK and MAC models decreases at lower luminosities due to the
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-dependence of weathering fluxes (see Figure
15
). This causes higher susceptibility to both snowball and catastrophic warming events at lower luminosity, as the overall feedback resilience is lower; perturbations to the slow carbon cycle are less easily counteracted. Indeed, trials run with a lower fixed luminosity show a higher climate catastrophe rate. Trials run with a fixed luminosity of
L=0.9L_{\odot}
show warming and snowball rates of 23% and 6% (Figure
16
), compared to 4.5% and 0.2% at
L=L_{\odot}
(Section
IV.2
).

V.2
Model Limitations and Future Work

Many aspects of the carbonate-silicate weathering feedback on Earth are ignored in our model for simplicity. Future improvements using our model might include some of the following.

V.2.1
Climate Model

If climate cools significantly, high latitude glaciation could locally shield silicate rocks from weathering, dependent on the local seasonality. This is a minor perturbation to global silicate weathering fluxes since most weathering occurs at low latitudes (e.g.,
J. Hartmann et al. 2014
), but may act as a stabilizing feedback against the ice-albedo runaway.

Rainfall rates decrease not only with distance from the equator (Section
III.3
), but also with distance inland from the coast
(
D. Hayward & R. Clarke 1996
)
. River runoff is also required to travel long distances to the ocean without being evaporated first for carbonate precipitation. Distance from the ocean could thus also be a variable included in Equation
11
.

p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
affects zonal temperature distribution (Equation
12
), with higher values of
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
flattening the equator-pole temperature gradient (e.g.,
J. S. S. Damsté et al. 2010
). This would likely reduce the effect of latitudinal drift on weathering fluxes in hot climates or lower solar luminosities, but exacerbate the effect in colder climates, leading to more snowball events.

V.2.2
Sulfide Oxidation and Silicate Weathering

Recent analysis of solute-chemistry datasets from mountain streams suggest that
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown due to chemical weathering is maximized at intermediate erosion rates
(
A. Bufe et al. 2024
)
. This is thought to be due to high erosion rates promoting sulfide oxidation, in which sulfide minerals (e.g., pyrite/
\text{FeS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) are oxidized by oxygen gas or other oxidized species to
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{SO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
/
\text{SO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
. The resulting sulfuric acid/sulfate ions can then interact with carbonates to release
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
gas into the atmosphere, offsetting
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
drawdown from silicate weathering.

However, significant
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
release via sulfide oxidation likely requires a highly-oxidized atmosphere rich in
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

(
M. A. Torres et al. 2017
)
. Morever, high rates of sulfide oxidation may be mediated by biological activity
(
E. S. Boyd et al. 2014
)
. In this study, we consider an abiotic atmosphere consisting of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
, and an inert background gas (i.e.,
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
). Sulfide weathering would likely not be an important control of atmospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
on such worlds, but a more detailed future model could attempt to model its effects for oxygen-rich worlds.

V.2.3
Reverse Weathering and Groundwater Carbon

Our model does not take into account ‘reverse weathering’, where clays form on the seafloor and sequester cations from silicate weathering reactions, preventing the formation of carbonates and removal of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
from the atmosphere/ocean system (e.g.,
A. G. Dunlea et al. 2017
;
T. T. Isson & N. J. Planavsky 2018
;
E. J. Trower & W. W. Fischer 2019
;
J. Krissansen-Totton & D. C. Catling 2020
). This effect is not very pronounced in the slow carbon cycle of modern-day Earth due to creatures near the seafloor efficiently sequestering silica (
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) to build their bodies. However, in an abiotic ocean, as assumed in this study, silica concentrations would be much higher, leading to more efficient clay formation. This effect would most directly affect our assumed
W_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}/W_{{}\mathrm{SiO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}
value, leading to lower
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
sequestration fluxes. Depending on the
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/temperature dependencies of reverse weathering, which are poorly-constrained (see
J. Krissansen-Totton & D. C. Catling 2020
), this may lead to either an additional stabilizing or destabilizing feedback in the slow carbon system.

Groundwater carbon fluxes could be another contributor to silicate weathering and could help explain the apparent mismatch (e.g.,
C.-T. Lee et al. 2019
) between estimates of volcanic
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and drawdown fluxes from silicate weathering (e.g.,
S. Zhang & N. J. Planavsky 2020
).

V.2.4
Glacial Impacts on Weathering

Our model also neglects the potential impact of continental ice sheets on local silicate weathering fluxes. Under the extremely cold, dry conditions that characterize a hard Snowball state, global ice sheet coverage is hypothesized to shutdown (or at least severely throttle) continental silicate weathering rates, providing a mechanism for subsequent
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
buildup and escape from glaciation
(
P. F. Hoffman et al. 2017
, e.g., )
. Some observations have suggested that the same is true locally on modern Earth; for example, Antarctica appears to contribute very little eroded/weathered material to the local waters
(
J. Edmond 1973
;
D. C. Hurd 1977
)
. By this line of argument, it might appear that we overestimate global silicate weathering rates for any climate that would be cold enough for land glaciers to develop, neglecting a negative feedback on the carbon cycle whereby ice sheet growth would slow silicate weathering and stabilize the climate against further cooling. However, more recent observations and paleoclimate proxy records suggest that the net effect of land glaciers on global silicate weathering is less clear-cut. For example, ice sheet growth leads to falling sea level, exposing unconsolidated and highly weatherable shelf sediments to subaerial weathering, potentially enhancing CO
2
drawdown rates during glacial lowstands and increasing the amplitude of glacial-interglacial climate excursions
(
S. Wan et al. 2017
)
. Similarly, although cold temperatures reduce the intrinsic kinetic weathering rates from rocks underlying ice sheets, the simultaneous immense enhancement in erosion in glacial catchments can elevate rock surface area enough to compensate for or even dominate over the temperature effect, leading to similar weathering rates between glacial and non-glacial conditions
(
G. Munhoven & L. M. François 1996
;
S. P. Anderson et al. 1997
;
S. P. Anderson 2005
;
J. Hatton et al. 2019
;
X. Li et al. 2022
)
. The precise interplay between silicate weathering and glaciation remains ambiguous and is likely not able to be represented adequately in a simple model like the one we present here.

V.3
Mineralogical and Astrophysical Diversity

Our model uses a single idealized chemical reaction (Reaction
6
)
to represent a complex range of mineral compositions present on Earth. Several values used in this study, including the solute concentration
C_{eq}
and reaction rate
R_{n}
(and their associated temperature and
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
dependencies) rely on this assumption. While previous studies have suggested that this reaction is a good first-order approximation of global weathering for Earth
(
K. Maher 2011
;
K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014
;
M. J. Winnick & K. Maher 2018
)
, including mineralogical diversity expected on Earth-like exoplanets
(
K. Hakim et al. 2021
;
S. L. Brantley et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
would paint a better picture of general habitability of rocky planets with global surface oceans. However, this would likely require a large amount of laboratory work investigating the dissolution mechanics of more diverse mineralogy.

Our model currently only considers Earth-like planets orbiting a Sun-like star. While we include varying stellar luminosity, the time evolution follows that of a Sun-like G star. Given that M stars comprise the majority of stars in the galaxy and planets around quiet M stars are the most amenable to transmission and emission spectroscopy observations, especially with the James Webb Space Telescope (e.g.,
L. Kaltenegger & W. A. Traub 2009
;
J. De Wit & S. Seager 2013
;
B. P. Coy et al. 2025b
), a logical next step could be implementing our model for Earth-like planets around M stars. However, the atmospheric compositions of such terrestrial planets are not observationally constrained, and high extreme ultraviolet (XUV) radiation fluxes early in their host stars’ lives may lead to complete loss of a secondary (outgassed) atmosphere
(
J. Zendejas et al. 2010
;
G. Van Looveren et al. 2024
;
E. K. Pass et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. The tidally-locked nature of most (if not all) of these M star habitable zone planets would also introduce complex perturbations to the hydrologic cycle difficult to capture without 3D GCMs. Recent studies have proposed statistical frameworks to constrain where the inner edge of the habitable zone begins (e.g.,
J. H. Checlair et al. 2019
;
M. Schlecker et al. 2024
), possibly giving observational evidence of the strength of the carbonate silicate weathering feedback on extrasolar worlds. Future missions designed to investigate the atmospheres of Earth-twins such as the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) will be required to carry out these feats.

VI
Conclusions and Implications for Habitability

We present a new idealized model for investigating the resilience of the carbonate-silicate weathering feedback of Earth-like planets around Sun-like stars based on the solute transport model presented in
K. Maher & C. P. Chamberlain 2014
and
M. J. Winnick & K. Maher 2018
. Our idealized model’s estimate for Earth’s globally-averaged ‘climate feedback strength’, which we define as the percentage change in weathering-induced
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-drawdown per increase in temperature, agrees with studies that consider the wide range of mineralogy and climate states present on Earth’s surface. We use this model to explore parameters relevant to the long-term climate stability of Earth-twins around Sun-like stars.

We find that the climate stability of planets with Earth-like plate tectonics and ocean depth is likely aided by more numerous continental plates, greater continental plate size, and more frequent continental plate collisions. Worlds with few and small or slow continental plates are doomed to experience catastrophic warming from the build-up of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
as their surfaces become leached of minerals for weathering over time, decreasing their overall climate feedback strength. Such worlds are also much more sensitive to small-scale perturbations in volcanic outgassing fluxes. We find that additional sources of resupply for weatherable minerals–not just tectonic uplift spurred by collisions–are required for long-term climate stability, as Earth-like worlds with only tectonic uplift as a resupply mechanism for weatherable minerals show a
84\%
climate catastrophe rate over 4 Gy. When approximating the effects of non-tectonic processes that aid weathering, such as dust transport and glacial erosion, the feedback is remarkably stabilizing, leading to a 95% aggregate survival rate when omitting outgassing variability. This suggests that these non-tectonic processes may be essential
in addition to
tectonic resurfacing for long-term climate stability on terrestrial exoplanets. When including simple stochastic volcanic outgassing variability (modeled after
M. Baum & M. Fu 2022
), we find that long-term planetary habitability becomes highly unstable at low land fractions or long-term outgassing perturbations, suggesting that land fractions similar to, or larger than that of Modern-day Earth, are conducive for long-term habitability. This conclusion is strengthened when considering the effects of solar luminosity increasing over time, i.e., the Faint Young Sun paradox. We have also shown that seafloor weathering, based on
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and temperature dependency estimates from previous studies
(
N. H. Sleep & K. Zahnle 2001
;
J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018
)
, may have a much higher climate feedback strength than continental silicate weathering, and thus may be the dominant climate feedback on worlds with tectonic activity on the seafloor but little exposed land. Future field and laboratory work is needed to constrain the true
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and temperature dependencies of seafloor weathering, as well as continental weathering at high
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and temperatures relevant to rocky planets hotter than Earth.

A.2
Euler Poles

Plates in our simulation rotate around randomized Euler poles at fixed velocities.
This is accomplished by first choosing a randomized longitude and latitude (
\Lambda,\Phi
) for each disk’s rotational (Euler) pole. Latitudes are chosen so that they are equiareally spaced. All positions are calculated in the new Euler frame (
\lambda_{e},\phi_{e}
) and subsequently rotated into the planetary frame (
\lambda_{p},\phi_{p}
).

Rotation matrices are defined as

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}

(A6)

where

\mathbf{A}=\begin{vmatrix}\cos{\alpha}&-\sin{\alpha}&0\\
\sin{\alpha}&\cos{\alpha}&0\\
0&0&1\end{vmatrix},

(A7)

\alpha=\Lambda+\frac{\pi}{2}
,

\mathbf{B}=\begin{vmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos{\beta}&-\sin{\beta}\\
0&\sin{\beta}&\cos{\beta}\end{vmatrix},

(A8)

\beta=\frac{\pi}{2}-\Phi
, and

\mathbf{C}=\begin{vmatrix}\cos{(-\alpha)}&-\sin{(-\alpha)}&0\\
\sin{(-\alpha)}&\cos{(-\alpha)}&0\\
0&0&1\end{vmatrix}.

(A9)

Rotation between the Euler and planetary frame is performed via

\vec{x}_{p}\mathbf{R}=\vec{X}_{e},

(A10)

where
\vec{x}
is the (
x,y,z
) coordinates of the disk center, which can then be converted to (
\lambda,\phi
) coordinates.

To define a starting location, another latitude and longitude point is then chosen in the Euler frame. Disk centers rotate around the Euler pole at a constant latitude (in the Euler frame, i.e.
\frac{d\lambda_{e}}{dt}=\Omega,\frac{d\phi_{e}}{dt}=0
). The velocity (
\Omega=\frac{v}{2\pi R_{pl}\cos{\phi_{e}}}
) of this rotation is determined by a Gaussian distribution (
v=V\pm\sigma_{V}
, with parameters in Table
1
). Example disk trajectories are shown in Figure
19
.

A.3
Carbon Partitioning and Ocean Chemistry

To calculate partitioning of inorganic carbon within the ocean and atmosphere, we use a modified version of the software package
csys

(
R. E. Zeebe & D. A. Wolf-Gladrow 2001
)
. In
csys
, dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) is partitioned between
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
/
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(
aq
)
,
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
, and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
, governed by the following reactions:

{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}{}{}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{)}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\leftrightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}{}{}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{aq}\mathrm{)}.

(A11)

{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}{}+{}{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}{}{}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{aq}\mathrm{)}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\leftrightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\leftrightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{+}}{}+{}{}\mathrm{HCO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{-}}.

(A12)

{}\mathrm{HCO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{-}}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\leftrightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{+}}{}+{}{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{2-}}.

(A13)

The equilibrium constants for these reactions (
K_{H}
,
K_{1}
, and
K_{2}
, respectively) depend on ocean temperature, salinity, and atmospheric pressure
(
R. E. Zeebe & D. A. Wolf-Gladrow 2001
)
. We assume an ocean salinity of
35\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{L}^{-1}
.

As carbon is added to the ocean-atmosphere system, the ocean becomes more acidic and the fraction of carbon stored in the atmosphere versus ocean increases (see Figure
20
). Ocean surface temperature also affects the solubility of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, with higher temperatures increasing the fraction of carbon stored in the atmosphere. For these reasons, in addition to
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, the amount of DIC stored in the ocean,
C_{ocean}
, and the pH of the ocean are kept track of in our simulations.

Carbon is partitioned between the atmosphere and ocean following
R. J. Graham 2021
. They assume that calcium carbonate saturation dictates atmosphere-ocean partitioning on multi-millennial timescales, i.e. the calcium carbonate saturation state,

\Omega=\frac{{}\mathrm{[}\mathrm{Ca}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{2+}}\mathrm{]}{}\mathrm{[}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{2-}}\mathrm{]}}{K_{sp}}=1,

(A14)

where
K_{sp}
is the solubility product of calcite and is a function of temperature, salinity, and pressure. A net zero charge balance constraint of

{}{}\mathrm{2}\,\mathrm{[}\mathrm{Ca}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{2+}}\mathrm{]}{}+{}{}{}\mathrm{[}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{+}}\mathrm{]}\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{{$-$}}\hss}\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{{$-$}}\mathrm{[}\mathrm{HCO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{-}}\mathrm{]}{}+{}{}{}\mathrm{2}\,\mathrm{[}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{2-}}\mathrm{]}{}+{}{}\mathrm{[}\mathrm{OH}{-}\mathrm{]}

(A15)

allows for solving for
\text{[}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}\text{]}
as a function of
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and temperature via the fourth-degree polynomial,

2K_{sp}{}\mathrm{[}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{+}}\mathrm{]}^{4}+\frac{K_{1}K_{2}}{K_{H}}p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}{}\mathrm{[}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{+}}\mathrm{]}^{3}-\left(\frac{K_{1}K_{2}}{K_{H}^{2}}p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{2}+\frac{K_{1}K_{2}}{K_{H}}K_{w}p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\right){}\mathrm{[}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{+}}\mathrm{]}-2\frac{K_{1}^{2}K_{2}^{2}}{K_{H}^{2}}p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{2}=0,

(A16)

where
K_{w}
is the (temperature- and pressure-dependent) ion-product constant of water. The resulting pH and
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
from a given addition of carbon is solved for via a minimization scheme that compares the total atmospheric and ocean carbon to the amount of added carbon. To enhance computational efficiency, the relevant equilibrium constants (
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
,
K_{H}
,
K_{w}
, and
K_{sp}
) are calculated in a 500
\times
100 grid in
\log_{10}(p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}})
and luminosity space (spanning -8 to 2 and 0.7 to 1, respectively) and linearly interpolated. This results in
<0.1\%
error for equilibrium constants over the vast majority of
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/luminosity values within this range. This approach results in a slightly larger ocean carbon reservoir (41 TtC) than typically estimated for modern-day earth (38 TtC,
R. E. Zeebe 2012
;
C.-T. Lee et al. 2019
), however recreates modern-day ocean pH estimates (8.1) when
p{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}=$420\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
.

We do not latitudinally resolve ocean temperatures in our model, and set the ocean temperature equal to the globally-averaged surface temperature
\overline{T}
.
\text{Ca}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
is a
conservative
ion, meaning that its concentration is generally unperturbed by changes in ocean temperature, salinity, and pressure. In our model, we assume that precipitation is efficient at
\Omega=1
, but in an abiotic ocean where calcium carbonate precipitation is non-biogenic
(
R. E. Zeebe & P. Westbroek 2003
, sometimes termed a “Strangelove ocean”; )
, it is possible that the ocean would need to reach a much higher saturation state than
\Omega=1
to trigger widespread carbonate precipitation. Experimental studies have suggested critical
\Omega
values for abiotic precipitation ranging from
\gg 20
to
>100

(
J. Lee & J. W. Morse 2010
;
J. V. Strauss & N. J. Tosca 2020
, e.g., )
. A higher critical
\Omega
would force the ocean to achieve larger concentrations of
{}\mathrm{[}\mathrm{Ca}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{2+}}\mathrm{]}
and
{}\mathrm{[}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}^{\mathrm{2-}}\mathrm{]}
in order to drive carbonate precipitation. The resulting system would require a higher oceanic pH and higher carbonate ion concentration to sustain a given rate of CO
2
drawdown, implying a greater capacity to buffer the climate against transient changes in weathering or outgassing fluxes, since flux changes of a given size would translate into proportionally smaller changes in the stock of molecules in the atmosphere/ocean system
(
D. Höning 2020
)
.

For trials where we vary the total amount of land, we assume a constant ocean depth, i.e. the total ocean volume scales inversely with the amount of land area. To solve for
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
partitioning for various ocean volumes, we assume that [

A.4
Closeness Factor

To smooth out numerical artifacts associated with using a finite number of grid points, we introduce a ‘closeness factor’
f
.
f
is based on the distance of the colliding point to the center of the nearest disk,
d_{min}
:

f=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $d_{min}\leq 0.8R$}\\
\frac{1-d_{min}}{0.2}&\text{if $d_{min}>0.8$}.\end{cases}

(A17)

f
is set to 0 in non-colliding points.
f
affects how local weathering fluxes are calculated:

W_{i}=\begin{cases}fW(\tau_{s}=\tau_{s,mountain})+(1-f)W(\tau_{s}=\tau_{s,max}/2)&\text{if colliding}\\
W(\tau_{s}=\tau_{s,i})&\text{if not colliding}.\end{cases}

(A18)

This causes glancing collisions to retain older effective soil ages, while more deeply penetrating collisions completely reset the soil age.

In this section, we explore the effects of varying tectonic parameters not discussed in the main text. All trials vary

B.1
Disk Velocity

To test the effects of changing disk velocity
V
, which we prescribe as
4\pm$2\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
in our main trials, we run two additional scenarios using velocity spreads of
V=40\pm$20\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
(‘fast’ case) and
V=0.4\pm$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}^{-1}$
(‘slow’ case).

In the slow case, initial disk configurations (whether or not a disk is colliding) greatly affect survival. Initial configurations lacking any colliding disks have a much lower chance to experience a collision and are essentially doomed to die on Gyr timescales. Even in systems that are colliding, the distribution of colliding and not colliding area does not change much; the non-colliding regions will age while the colliding regions will stay at the prescribed soil age (
8\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
), resulting in a slow death. Even though there are a high amount of survivors (
\sim 99\%
), they are (almost) all on a slow path to death, driven by rising
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. Increasing disk velocity greatly increases the variability of collisions, disk latitude, and weatherability. This causes most trials to have an extremely high variability in atmospheric
p
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and several end via catastrophic warming.

22\times
larger value for the thermodynamic maximum concentration of

.
