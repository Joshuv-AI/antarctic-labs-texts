Deconvoluting zavegepant drug–drug interactions: A phase I study to evaluate the effects of rifampin and itraconazole on zavegepant pharmacokinetics
Authors: Rajinder Bhardwaj, Jo Ann Malatesta, Jennifer Madonia, Matt S Anderson, Beth Morris, Kyle T Matschke, Robert Croop, Richard Bertz, Jing Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractZavegepant is a calcitonin gene‐related peptide receptor antagonist for acute migraine treatment. This Phase I, open‐label, fixed‐sequence study evaluated the effects of itraconazole (a strong cytochrome P450 3A4 [CYP3A4] and P‐glycoprotein [P‐gp] inhibitor) on the pharmacokinetics of intranasal/oral zavegepant and the effects of rifampin (a strong inducer of CYP3A4 and P‐gp; and an inhibitor of organic anion transporting polypeptide 1B3 [OATP1B3]) on oral zavegepant in healthy participants. In the intranasal/oral zavegepant–itraconazole cohort, participants received a single 10‐mg dose of zavegepant nasal spray on Day 1, followed by oral zavegepant (50 mg) on Day 3. Itraconazole 200 mg once daily was administered from Days 4 to 12. On Day 7 zavegepant nasal spray and on Day 11 oral zavegepant were coadministered with itraconazole. In the oral zavegepant–rifampin cohort, participants received oral zavegepant (100 mg) on Day 1, rifampin 600 mg once daily on Days 2–10, and rifampin with zavegepant on Day 11. No significant change in zavegepant exposure was observed following coadministration of itraconazole with zavegepant nasal spray. For oral zavegepant coadministered with itraconazole, the area under the curve from 0 to infinity (AUC0−inf) and the maximum observed concentration (C max) of oral zavegepant increased by 59% and 77%, respectively. For oral zavegepant coadministered with rifampin, the AUC0−inf and C max of oral zavegepant increased by approximately 2.3‐ and 2.2‐fold, respectively. These results suggest that OATP1B3 and intestinal P‐gp are the more prominent pathways, as opposed to CYP3A4, for a zavegepant drug–drug interaction. Coadministration of OATP1B3 inhibitors with zavegepant nasal spray should be avoided.

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Zavegepant, a calcitonin gene‐related peptide receptor antagonist for the acute treatment of migraine, is a substrate of CYP3A4, OATP1B3, and P‐gp transporters. As patients with migraine may take concomitant treatments, it is important to identify any clinically meaningful pharmacokinetic DDI between zavegepant and other medications that are inducers or inhibitors of CYP3A4 or these transporters.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

This phase I study evaluated the effects of itraconazole (a strong CYP3A4 and P‐gp inhibitor) and rifampin (a strong inducer of CYP3A4 and P‐gp, and an inhibitor of OATP1B3) on the pharmacokinetics of zavegepant nasal spray and oral zavegepant in healthy participants. WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

INTRODUCTION

Migraine is a neurological disorder that affects more than 1 billion people globally each year. 1 Migraine is characterized by headaches lasting for 4–72 h, which are often accompanied by nausea and sensitivity to light and/or sound. Calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP), a neuropeptide located within the trigeminal nerves, has a key role in migraine pathophysiology. 2 , 3 , 4 Blocking CGRP signaling with CGRP receptor antagonists has emerged as an effective and promising treatment for migraine. 5 Zavegepant 10‐mg nasal spray (ZAVZPRET™, Pfizer Inc., New York, NY, USA) administered as a single spray in one nostril is a third‐generation, high‐affinity, small‐molecule CGRP receptor antagonist approved for acute treatment of migraine. Zavegepant is the only CGRP antagonist available as a nasal formulation and has a rapid onset of action. The efficacy and safety of zavegepant nasal spray have been demonstrated in two pivotal randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled clinical trials. 6 , 7

Zavegepant is primarily metabolized by cytochrome P450 (CYP)3A4 and, to a small extent, by CYP2D6 in vitro. 8 Administration of a single 10‐mg dose of zavegepant nasal spray has been shown to reach peak plasma zavegepant concentration at approximately 30 min. Zavegepant has an effective half‐life of 6.55 h and a mean apparent clearance of 266 L/h following a 10‐mg dose of the nasal spray. No major metabolites (>10%) of zavegepant were detected in plasma after administration of a single intravenous (IV) dose of 5 mg, and the majority of the zavegepant (>80%) is eliminated via the biliary/fecal route. 8 In vitro studies have demonstrated that zavegepant is a substrate of organic anion‐transporting polypeptide (OATP)1B3, sodium/taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP), and P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) transporters. 8 OATP1B3 is an influx transporter expressed by hepatocytes, 9 NTCP is a bile salt uptake transporter, 10 and P‐gp is an efflux transporter primarily expressed in tissues with excretory functions, such as the small intestine, liver, and kidney. 11 Hence, it is important to study the potential for zavegepant drug–drug interactions (DDI) and identify any clinically meaningful pharmacokinetic (PK) interactions.

Itraconazole, an antifungal medication, is a strong inhibitor of CYP3A4 and P‐gp. Rifampin, an antibiotic typically prescribed for mycobacterial diseases, is a strong inducer of CYP3A4 and P‐gp and an inhibitor of OATP1B3 and may have a low potential to inhibit NTCP. 12 Both itraconazole and rifampin are commonly prescribed medications and are widely used prototypical drugs in clinical studies to identify potential interactions with medications that are strong inducers or inhibitors of CYP3A4 and P‐gp. 13 , 14 Based on the understanding that itraconazole is a strong inhibitor of CYP3A4 and P‐gp and rifampin is a strong inducer of CYP3A4 and P‐gp, we initially hypothesized that coadministering itraconazole with zavegepant would increase zavegepant exposure and coadministering rifampin with zavegepant would most likely decrease zavegepant exposure. 15 The current study was designed to evaluate the effect of multiple doses of itraconazole (with single‐dose zavegepant nasal spray or oral zavegepant) or multiple doses of rifampin (with single‐dose oral zavegepant) on zavegepant PK, safety, and tolerability. Both zavegepant nasal spray and oral zavegepant were included in this study in anticipation of the clinical development of both routes of administration.

METHODS

Eligibility criteria

This was a phase I, open‐label, fixed‐sequence DDI study conducted on healthy participants at a single center (Syneos Health, Canada). Eligible participants were males or females aged ≥18 and ≤ 55 years with a body mass index >18.5 and <30.0 kg/m2. The participants had to be non‐smokers (no use of tobacco or nicotine products within 3 months prior to screening), healthy (absence of clinically significant illness) and were required to have a Sheehan Suicidality Tracking Scale (S‐STS) of 0 at screening. Participants were excluded if they had liver disease, infectious diseases (including Clostridium difficile‐associated infection or diarrhea), seizure disorders, gastrointestinal diseases, or nasal conditions that interfered with drug absorption, cardiovascular disorders, and other clinically significant illnesses. Females of non‐childbearing potential were either postmenopausal (absence of menses for 12 months) or had a hysterectomy or tubal ligation at least 6 months prior to the first drug administration. Females were also required to be non‐lactating and have a negative pregnancy test at baseline.

Participants were not allowed to take prescription and over‐the‐counter medications throughout the study. Natural health products and vaccination, including the COVID‐19 vaccine, were not allowed 14 days before first dosing and throughout the study. Medications to manage an adverse event (AE) and occasional use of ibuprofen were allowed. At different points in the study, participants were required to abstain from products that could affect drug‐metabolizing enzymes and transporters, such as St. John's wort, food containing poppy seeds, food or beverages containing xanthine derivatives, energy drinks, caffeine, grapefruit, Seville orange, starfruit, pomegranate, pineapple, or pomelo.

Ethics approval

An institutional review board and an independent ethics committee reviewed and approved the study protocol and study‐associated procedures. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice, as defined by the International Conference on Harmonization, and following the ethical principles underlying the United States Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), Title 21, Part 50 (21CFR50). All participants signed informed consent before the start of any study‐related activities.

Study design

The study included two cohorts with 18 participants in each cohort. Cohort 1 included sequential intranasal/oral zavegepant coadministration with itraconazole and Cohort 2 included oral zavegepant coadministration with rifampin (Figure 1). All the treatments were administered to the participants under fasting conditions. Participants were not allowed to consume food at least 10 h before and 4 h after oral or intranasal zavegepant dosing. In addition, no food was allowed 2 h before and 2 h after rifampin and itraconazole dosing. Except for 240 mL of water given with study drugs, no fluids were allowed 1 h before and after dosing of any drug.

Study design. The study included two cohorts with 18 participants in each cohort. Cohort 1 included sequential zavegepant nasal spray and oral zavegepant coadministration with itraconazole (a). Cohort 2 included oral zavegepant coadministration with rifampin (b).

Intranasal/oral zavegepant–itraconazole cohort

The participants in Cohort 1 (intranasal/oral zavegepant–itraconazole) received a single 10 mg dose of zavegepant nasal spray (one spray in one nostril) on Day 1, and a single dose of oral zavegepant 50‐mg capsule (2 × 25 mg softgel capsules; Biohaven Pharmaceuticals, USA) on Day 3. The washout period between zavegepant nasal spray administration and intake of the oral zavegepant capsule was approximately 48 h. This was considered sufficient given that 48 h is >5 zavegepant half‐lives. From Days 4 to 12, participants received itraconazole 200‐mg oral solution (20 mL of 10 mg/mL oral solution; Sporanox®, Janssen, Canada) once daily, approximately at the same time (±20 min). On Day 7, zavegepant 10‐mg nasal spray and on Day 11 oral zavegepant 50‐mg capsule were coadministered with itraconazole 200‐mg oral solution, approximately 1 h (±5 min) after itraconazole dosing. Participants in the intranasal/oral itraconazole cohort were required to remain seated and avoid lying down or sleeping for the first 4 h after the first study drug administration on Days 1, 3, 7, and 11. Normal activity was allowed on the other days but participants were not allowed to lie down or sleep for 4 h after itraconazole administration. On Days 1 and 7, participants remained seated upright for ~10 min after intranasal zavegepant administration to avoid study drug leakage.

Oral zavegepant–rifampin cohort

On Day 1 of the study, the participants in Cohort 2 (oral zavegepant–rifampin) received a single oral dose of zavegepant 100‐mg capsule (4 × 25 mg softgel capsules; Biohaven Pharmaceuticals, USA). Rifampin 600 mg (2 × 300 mg capsules; Patheon Pharmaceuticals, USA) was administered to participants once daily from Days 2 to 11, approximately at the same time (±20 min). On Day 11, oral zavegepant 100‐mg capsule was coadministered within 5 min after rifampin dosing. Participants in the oral zavegepant–rifampin cohort were required to remain seated and avoid lying down or sleeping for 4 h after the first study drug administration on Days 1 and 11. Normal activity was allowed on the other days but participants were not allowed to lie down or sleep for 4 h after rifampin administration.

Study procedures

Blood sample collection for zavegepant PK analyses

In the intranasal/oral zavegepant–itraconazole cohort, on Days 1 and 7, blood was drawn from each participant before intranasal zavegepant dosing and at 0.17, 0.33, 0.5, 0.67, 0.83, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, and 24 h postdose. On Days 3 and 11, blood was collected before oral zavegepant dosing and 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, and 24 h postdose. Blood was also drawn at 36 and 48 h post zavegepant dosing on Days 7 and 11.

In the oral zavegepant–rifampin cohort, on Days 1 and 11, blood samples were collected from each participant before zavegepant dosing and at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, and 24 h postdose.

Bioanalytical method: liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry

High‐performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry was used to analyze the plasma concentration of zavegepant. The samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 2 min at room temperature for plasma collection. Using automated solid phase extraction, zavegepant, and its internal standard BHV3500‐d8 were extracted from 0.075 mL of human ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid K2 plasma. Extracted samples were transferred to a liquid chromatograph with an ACE EXCEL 2 C‐18‐PFP, 50 × 3.0 mm, 2‐μm column with Milli‐Q type water/acetonitrile (75/25) with 5 mM ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid for mobile phase A. Milli‐Q type water/acetonitrile (5/95) with 2 mM ammonium formate and 0.2% formic acid was used for mobile phase B. The lower limit of quantification for zavegepant was 20 pg/mL with a validation calibration range from 20 to 50,000 pg/mL. The between‐run accuracy bias of the assay was 0.85% to 3.47% (precision coefficient of variation [CV]: 1.61% to 6.44%), and the within‐run accuracy bias was −2.20% to 6.39% (precision CV: 0.98% to 7.80%). The data were captured by Analyst version 1.6.3 or higher (AB Sciex, Toronto, Canada) and then imported into Watson LIMS version 7.4.1 or higher (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Philadelphia, PA, USA).

Pharmacokinetic assessments

The PK parameters for plasma zavegepant were calculated using standard non‐compartmental methods using a validated Phoenix® WinNonlin® version 8.0 or higher and included area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) from time zero to the last non‐zero concentration (AUC0−t ), AUC from time zero to 24 h (AUC0−24), AUC from time zero to infinity (AUC0−inf), maximum observed concentration (C max), time of C max (T max), elimination half‐life (T ½ el), effective half‐life (T ½ eff), apparent total body clearance (CL/F), and apparent volume of distribution (V d /F).

Safety assessments

Safety and tolerability of zavegepant nasal spray and oral zavegepant alone or in combination with itraconazole and oral zavegepant with rifampin were evaluated through the assessment of AEs (e.g., treatment‐emergent adverse events [TEAEs], serious adverse events [SAEs]), clinical laboratory parameters (biochemistry, hematology, coagulation, serology, and urinalysis), 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG), vital signs, physical examination, nasal cavity examination, and S‐STS. AEs were collected for up to 7 days after the last visit, coded using the latest version of the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, and assessed based on their seriousness, severity, and relationship to treatment. A TEAE was defined as any event occurring after the initiation of treatment or any event already present that worsens in intensity or frequency during the treatment.

Statistical analyses

Each cohort had a planned sample size of 18 participants. In the intranasal/oral zavegepant–itraconazole cohort, assuming a within‐subject %CV of approximately 35% and a moderate increase (ratio of 135%) in zavegepant exposure when coadministered with itraconazole, 18 participants were expected to provide 81% power to exclude 100% in the lower 90% confidence interval (CI). In the oral zavegepant–rifampin cohort, assuming a within‐subject geometric mean (%CV) of approximately 35% and a moderate decrease (ratio of 70%) in zavegepant exposure when coadministered with rifampin, 18 participants were expected to provide 92% power to exclude 100% in the upper 90% CI. These sample sizes were considered sufficient to provide a reliable estimate of the magnitude and variability of drug interactions. All participants who completed the study and had adequately characterized PK profiles were included in the PK population, and all participants who received at least one dose of any treatment in each cohort were included in the safety population.

Descriptive statistics were used to report demographics, plasma concentrations versus time, PK parameters, and safety. The effects of itraconazole on the PK of zavegepant nasal spray or oral zavegepant and the effects of rifampin on the PK of oral zavegepant were evaluated separately using generalized linear modeling procedures in SAS® (version 9.4 or higher). An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on natural log‐transformed AUC0−t , AUC0−24, AUC0−inf, and C max at the alpha level of 0.05. The ratio of geometric means (itraconazole + zavegepant nasal spray on Day 7 vs. zavegepant nasal spray on Day 1; and itraconazole + oral zavegepant on Day 11 vs. oral zavegepant on Day 3; oral zavegepant + rifampin on Day 11 vs. oral zavegepant on Day 1); and 90% CIs based on least squares means from the ANOVA of natural log‐transformed data were calculated for AUC0−t , AUC0−inf, AUC0−24, and C max. An ANOVA was also performed on untransformed T max, T 1/2 el, and T 1/2 eff. The ANOVA model included treatment as the fixed effect and participants as the random effect. In the intranasal/oral zavegepant–itraconazole cohort, the model assessments for Day 7 versus Day 1 and Day 11 versus Day 3 were evaluated separately.

In Cohort 1, coadministration of oral zavegepant with itraconazole on Day 11 increased the mean plasma zavegepant concentrations at most of the timepoints compared with oral zavegepant alone on Day 3 (Figure 2b). Coadministration of oral zavegepant and itraconazole on Day 11 increased AUC0−inf and C max of zavegepant by approximately 59% and 77%, respectively, compared to oral zavegepant alone on Day 3. The zavegepant exposure comparison ratios (90% CIs) were 158.56% (115.84%, 217.02%) for AUC0−inf and 177.09% (108.48%, 289.12%) for C max (Table 3). The median T max of oral zavegepant showed a statistically insignificant change from approximately 2.0 to 2.5 h in the presence of itraconazole compared to oral zavegepant alone. The geometric mean of T 1/2 el and T 1/2 eff was comparable between the two treatments (Table 2 ).

In Cohort 2, the oral zavegepant–rifampin cohort, coadministration of oral zavegepant with rifampin on Day 11 resulted in a higher mean plasma zavegepant concentration through most timepoints compared with oral zavegepant alone on Day 1 (Figure 2c). A summary of the plasma zavegepant PK parameters after administering a single dose of oral zavegepant on Day 1 or coadministering multiple doses of rifampin with a single dose of oral zavegepant on Day 11 is provided in Table 2. The AUC0−inf and C max of zavegepant increased by approximately 2.3‐ and 2.2‐fold, respectively, after coadministration of oral zavegepant and rifampin on Day 11 compared to oral zavegepant alone on Day 1 (Table 3). The zavegepant exposure comparison ratios (90% CIs) were 232.95% (179.60%, 302.16%) for AUC0−inf and 216.93% (160.08%, 293.98%) for C max (Table 3). The median T max did not change and geometric mean T 1/2 eff was comparable between the two treatments, but the geometric mean of T 1/2el was shorter in the presence of rifampin compared to zavegepant alone (5.41 vs. 8.89 h) (Table 2).

Safety

No deaths or SAEs occurred in this study. All the treatments (zavegepant nasal spray or oral zavegepant alone or when coadministered with itraconazole, and oral zavegepant coadministered with rifampin) were generally safe and well tolerated in healthy participants.

In the intranasal/oral zavegepant–itraconazole cohort, no participant discontinued the study due to SAEs or AEs. Overall, 17 of 18 participants (94.4%) experienced at least 1 TEAE. Fifteen participants (83.3%) experienced TEAEs while receiving zavegepant nasal spray, one participant (5.6%) while receiving oral zavegepant, four participants (22.2%) while receiving itraconazole, 16 participants (88.9%) following itraconazole and zavegepant nasal spray coadministration, and one participant (5.6%) after itraconazole and oral zavegepant coadministration. The most frequently reported TEAEs overall were dysgeusia (n = 17 [94.4%]) and nasal discomfort and rash (n = 3 [16.7%] participants each).

In the oral zavegepant–rifampin cohort, two participants (11.1%) discontinued due to AEs (one due to COVID‐19 and another due to vomiting following rifampin administration). Overall, all 18 (100%) participants reported at least 1 TEAE. Two participants (11.1%) reported TEAEs while receiving oral zavegepant, and 16 participants (94.1%) while receiving rifampin. No participants experienced TEAEs following oral zavegepant and rifampin coadministration. The most commonly reported TEAEs (reported in ≥3 participants) were chromaturia (16 participants [88.9%]) and back pain and headache (three participants [16.7%] each).

In both cohorts, all the reported TEAEs were of mild or moderate severity. None of the participants had alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase >3× upper limit of normal (ULN) levels, total bilirubin >2× ULN, or alkaline phosphatase >1.5 ULN. Vital signs, ECGs, or S‐STS did not change from baseline in any of the participants in a clinically relevant manner.

DISCUSSION

In this study, zavegepant nasal spray, the marketed formulation, was administered with steady‐state itraconazole, and oral zavegepant was administered with steady‐state itraconazole or rifampin. Both formulations were included, as at the time of the study, both routes of administration were being considered for clinical development.

No significant change was observed in zavegepant exposure (AUC0−inf and C max) when zavegepant nasal spray was coadministered with itraconazole, suggesting minimal hepatic metabolism of zavegepant by CYP3A4 in vivo, as nasal spray administration bypasses intestinal CYP3A4. Based on previous itraconazole DDI studies, 16 , 17 , 18 the itraconazole oral solution was administered for 9 days to achieve adequate CYP3A inhibition. This observation is consistent with the results from the zavegepant mass‐balance study, 19 wherein unchanged zavegepant represented >80% of the total radio activity in plasma, feces, and urine following IV administration. These data also suggest that the contribution of P‐gp to zavegepant disposition is insignificant in vivo following intranasal administration. Increased zavegepant exposure after coadministration of oral zavegepant with itraconazole suggested inhibition of intestinal CYP3A4 and P‐gp, which resulted in increased bioavailability. As expected, there was no statistically significant difference in zavegepant T 1/2 el due to oral zavegepant–itraconazole coadministration. Overall, single or coadministration of all treatments was safe and well tolerated by healthy participants in this study.

Coadministration of oral zavegepant and itraconazole increased zavegepant AUC0−inf by 59% and C max by 77%. Although the expected minimum effective oral dose of zavegepant was considered to be 100 mg, the participants in the itraconazole cohort were dosed with 50 mg of oral zavegepant to avoid exceeding exposures equivalent to the 100 mg dose due to possible CYP3A4 and P‐gp inhibition. The increase in zavegepant exposure following concomitant administration of oral zavegepant with itraconazole in this study is likely due to CYP3A4 and P‐gp inhibition by itraconazole in the gastrointestinal tract. Mechanistic and quantitative understanding of the DDI between oral zavegepant and itraconazole using physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling confirmed our findings and will be reported in a separate publication. 15 Our observation is in line with the literature, which found an increased exposure after coadministration of other drugs such as digoxin 20 and fexofenadine 17 with itraconazole in healthy adults potentially via P‐gp inhibition.

Concomitant administration of oral zavegepant and rifampin was originally expected to decrease zavegepant exposure because rifampin is a strong inducer of CYP3A4 and P‐gp. However, oral zavegepant exposure increased after coadministration with rifampin, which suggests the involvement of OATP1B3 inhibition by rifampin as zavegepant is a substrate of OATP1B3 in vitro and it has been reported that rifampin is an inhibitor of OATP1B1 and OATP1B3. 21 , 22 , 23 OATP1B1 and OATP1B3 are membrane transporters expressed in hepatocytes that facilitate the uptake of many clinically important drugs from blood into hepatocytes for metabolism. 24 Although zavegepant is also a substrate of NTCP, a bile salt uptake transporter, 10 the likelihood of rifampin inhibiting NTCP is considered low given the high in vitro half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of 277 μM for rifampin inhibition of NTCP. 12 The >2‐fold increase in oral zavegepant exposure following coadministration with rifampin suggested that the net effect of OATP1B3 inhibition is greater than CYP3A4 and P‐gp induction by rifampin. The reduction in T 1/2 el of zavegepant by 3.5 h after oral zavegepant and rifampin coadministration may be due to reduced hepatic uptake or distribution of zavegepant with inhibition of OATP1B3 by rifampin as evidenced by the lower V d /F compared to zavegepant alone. It could also be due to increased elimination of zavegepant with P‐gp induction by rifampin even though there was a net increase in zavegepant exposure as a result of OATP1B3 inhibition.

To gain more insight into the mechanistic and quantitative understanding of the DDI between zavegepant and rifampin, PBPK modeling was performed. 15 The model, along with the data presented here, support the statement that inhibitors/inducers of CYP3A4 or inhibitors of P‐gp do not have a clinically relevant effect on intranasal zavegepant exposure and that OATP1B3 inhibitors may result in a significant increase in zavegepant exposure, and thus, coadministration should be avoided. 8

Overall, both zavegepant nasal spray and oral capsule alone or combined with rifampin or itraconazole were well tolerated and exhibited a favorable safety profile. Although all participants experienced at least one TEAE, all TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity and resolved by the end of the study.

This study has some limitations. All the participants in the rifampin cohort were male, although the majority of the population affected by migraine is female (global prevalence of 8.6% in males and 17% in females). 25 , 26 While the current study enrolled only healthy participants, DDIs in patients with migraine are not expected to differ from those reported in the study.

In conclusion, the coadministration of a single dose of zavegepant nasal spray with steady‐state itraconazole had no impact on the exposure of zavegepant. However, coadministration of single‐dose oral zavegepant with steady‐state rifampin or itraconazole increased the exposure of zavegepant. The results from this combined DDI study suggest that OATP1B3 and intestinal P‐gp are the more prominent pathways, as opposed to CYP3A4, for a zavegepant DDI. Coadministration of OATP1B3 inhibitors with zavegepant nasal spray should be avoided.
