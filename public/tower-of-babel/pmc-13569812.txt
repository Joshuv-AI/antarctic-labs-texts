Limited evidence for causal effects of circulating inflammatory cytokines on the risk of selected hematologic malignancies: a two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Huimin Zhang, Lihong Zhang, Dan Zhao, Bing Ma, Jianying Wang, Xinying Liu, Wanyi Yin, Yang Shen, Liu Yang, Wen Dong, Cuiyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHematologic malignancies have been linked to inflammatory cytokine levels; however, whether a causal relationship exists between inflammatory cytokines and hematologic malignancies remains uncertain. This study aimed to explore the causal association between inflammatory cytokines and hematologic malignancies using Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis.MethodsSummary statistics from genome-wide association studies of 41 inflammatory cytokines, C-reactive protein, and selected hematologic malignancies were obtained from the UK Biobank, YFS, FINRISK, and FinnGen consortia. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method with false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment was used as the primary MR method. The weighted median, MR-Egger regression, MR-Robust Adjusted Profile Score, and MR pleiotropy residual sum, and outlier methods were used as supplied analyses. MR-Egger intercept estimates and Cochran’s Q test were used to assess pleiotropy and heterogeneity. Leave-one-out analysis and the MR Steiger test were used to assess sensitivity and the direction of causality.ResultsAlthough the primary IVW analysis indicated that some inflammatory cytokines were associated with risk of selected hematologic malignancies, no significant causal relationship between cytokines and selected hematologic malignancies was detected after FDR correction.ConclusionGenetically predicted cytokine levels did not have a significant effect on the risk of the selected hematologic malignancies. Further research is warranted to confirm the potential association between cytokine levels and the risk of selected hematologic malignancies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44313-026-00164-z.Keywords: Inflammatory cytokines, C-reactive protein, Hematologic malignancies, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Hematologic malignancies are the most common cancers affecting multiple systems and organs, with a rising incidence and substantial heterogeneity, making them difficult to treat individually [1]. Hematologic malignancies include leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma, all of which are classified according to their genomic alterations [2]. The complex etiology of hematologic malignancies is a leading cause of unsatisfactory diagnosis and clinical management in patients with diverse forms of hematologic malignancies [3–6]. Therefore, understanding the causal effects of different risk factors, especially at the molecular level, is important for improving diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for hematologic malignancies.

Increasing evidence indicates that the immune system plays a critical role in the development and of hematologic malignancies and patients’ response to treatment [7, 8]. In addition to genetic factors, dysregulated inflammatory cytokine production is an important factor that exacerbates the disease course, as is cytokine-driven inflammation by both malignant clones and non-malignant hematopoietic cells in the bone marrow microenvironment [9]. A comprehensive cytokine profiling study indicated that increasing levels of interleukin (IL)−8, IL-2R, IL-12, IL-15, and interferon γ-induced protein 10 kDa (IP-10) were associated with inferior survival in patients with primary myelofibrosis [10]. Elevated tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) facilitates clonal expansion and contributes to myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) progression [11]. Increased levels of IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and TNF-α were also found in non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) and may serve as powerful predictive markers for risk stratification [12, 13]. An observational study suggests that the variants of TNF-α, IL-10, and transforming growth factor-β 1 (TGF-β1) are potentially associated with the etiopathogenesis of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [14]. Furthermore, the levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 tend to increase AML aggressiveness, whereas TGF-β and IL-10 counteract AML progression [15]. C-reactive protein (CRP) is a classical acute-phase reactant protein that belongs to pentraxin family. Elevated CRP levels may be associated with an increased risk of extranodal natural killer (NK)/T-cell lymphoma [16], myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) [17], follicular lymphoma (FL) [17], and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [18]. Although the above findings indicate an association between cytokines and the risk of hematologic malignancies, the causal relationship between CRP and the risk of hematologic malignancies remains to be explored because of potential confounders or reverse causality in previous studies.

MR analysis is a widely used epidemiological method for exploring the causal association between exposure and outcome traits by leveraging genetic variants as proxies for exposure [19]. MR effectively mitigates the effects of confounders and reverse causation bias owing to the random assignment of alleles that occurs during gamete formation. In the present study, we first used a two-sample MR analysis to explore the causal relationship between multiple inflammatory cytokines and selected hematologic malignancies.

Methods

Overview of study design

The study design is shown in (Fig. 1). In the present study, a two-sample MR analysis was performed to assess the causal effects of 41 cytokines and CRP on selected hematologic malignancies based on summary-level data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS). The instrumental variables (IVs) were selected according to the following assumptions [20–24]: (a) a strong association between genetic variants and exposure; (b) genetic variants are not associated with the confounders that link to exposure and outcome; and (c) genetic variants affect the outcome only through the exposure. Genetic variants for cytokines were selected as IVs, the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary analysis, and MR-Egger regression, the weighted median (WME), MR-Robust Adjusted Profile Score (MR-RAPS), and MR-Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) were used for sensitivity analyses. The MR-Egger intercept and Cochran's Q test were used to assess pleiotropy and heterogeneity tests. Leave-one-out analysis and the MR Steiger test were used to assess the reliability of the MR results.

Data sources

All datasets for the exposure and outcome groups are listed in (Table 1). For the exposures, GWAS summary data on the cytokines were obtained from two independent cohorts (YFS and FINRISK, and meta-analyses GWAS), comprising 8,293 participants of European ancestry [25], and 204,402 participants of European ancestry [26], respectively. For the outcomes, the GWAS summary data for MPNs (1,086 cases, and 407,155 controls) were obtained from the UK Biobank [27]. The GWAS summary data for Hodgkin lymphoma (HL, 780 cases and 299,952 controls), subtypes of NHL: FL (1,081 cases and 299,952 controls), DLBCL (1,010 cases, and 287,137 controls), mature NK/T-cell lymphoma (MTNKL, 335 cases, and 299,952 controls), and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL, 624 cases and 287,133 controls), and acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL, 184 cases and 287,136 controls) were derived from the FinnGen consortium.

Instrument variables selection

To estimate the relationship between cytokines and the risk of hematologic malignancies, we extracted the SNPs associated with exposures (41 cytokines and CRP) at genome-wide significance P < 5 × 10−8. A few SNPs were selected using a relaxed cutoff (P < 5 × 10–6) because of the limited numbers of SNPs. In addition, to exclude linkage disequilibrium (LD), SNPs with an LD R2 < 0.001 within a window of 10,000 kb were used. All SNPs were not associated with the outcomes (P > 1 × 10–5).

Statistical power and F-statistic estimation

The statistical power of the MR analysis was assessed using an online tool (https://sb452.shinyapps.io/power/). To avoid weak instrument variable bias, the R2 and F-statistics were calculated as follows: R2^=2×EAF×(1-EAF)×beta2^2×EAF×1-EAF×beta2+(2×EAF×1-AF×N×SE2), F = N-K-1×R2^K×(1-R2), where EAF is the effect allele frequency, beta is the allele effect value, SE is the standard error, and K is the number of SNPs. An F-statistics > 10, indicates the absence of weak instrumental variable bias.

Mendelian randomization analysis

In the present study, two-sample MR analyses were performed to determine the causal effects of cytokine and CRP levels on hematologic malignancies. IVW method was conducted as the primary MR analysis. MR-Egger regression, WME, and MR-RAPS were used as supplementary methods to estimate the causal effects. The IVW method is a meta-analysis approach that combines Wald estimates for each SNP to obtain an overall estimate of the effects of exposure on outcome [28]. A random-effects IVW model was applied when significant heterogeneity was present (P < 0.05); otherwise, a fixed-effects IVW model was applied (P > 0.05). MR-Egger regression is a method based on the intercept term used to assess the potential pleiotropic effects of SNPs that drive the results of MR analysis [29]. The WME method generates a causal estimate even when up to half of the information originates from invalid IVs [30]. The MR-RAPS method is robust to both systemic and idiosyncratic pleiotropy and can be used to correct pleiotropy using adjusted profile scores [31].

Pleiotropy, heterogeneity, and direction estimation

The MR-PRESSO and MR-Egger intercept methods were used to assess and correct for horizontal pleiotropy. The MR-PRESSO method contains three components: (1) detection of horizontal pleiotropy, (2) correction of horizontal pleiotropy via outlier removal, and (3) detection of significant differences in the causal estimates before and after correction for outliers [32]. The MR-Egger intercept estimate was used to detect pleiotropy [29]. Cochran’s Q test was used to assess potential heterogeneity. The leave-one-out analysis was used for sensitivity analysis by performing MR analysis after sequentially removing each SNP. The MR Steiger test is a directionality test used to assess the potential causal direction of SNPs [33].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were performed using the “TwoSampleMR” and “MR-PRESSO” packages in R. False discovery rate (FDR) was used to adjust multiple comparisons of various cytokines. An FDR < 0.05. was considered statistically significant. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a suggestive association, when the FDR was > 0.05.

Causality between cytokines and selected hematologic malignancies

The primary MR analysis of the 41 cytokines and CRP levels using the IVW method is shown in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 and Table S3. IVW analysis revealed a negative causal relationship between macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) and MPN (odds ratio [OR], 0.58; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.41–0.81, P = 0.001). The causal relationship between MIF and MPN disappeared after adjustment (FDR = 0.062) (Fig. 2). The positive causal effect of regulated normal T-cell expressed and secreted (RANTES) on HL disappeared after FDR adjustment (OR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.03–1.70, P = 0.029, FDR: 0.766) (Fig. 3). For subtypes of NHL, we observed positive effects of beta-nerve growth factor (β-NGF) and IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA) in FL but these associations were no longer significant after adjustment (Fig. 4). IVW analysis revealed positive associations of monokine induced by gamma interferon (MIG), macrophage inflammatory protein-1 alpha (MIP-1α), and stem cell factor (SCF) with DLBCL (Fig. 5). After adjustment, the link between MIG, MIP-1α, and SCF was no longer significant (FDR > 0.05). IVW analysis revealed that IP10, monocyte chemoattractant protein 3 (MCP-3), basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), IL-16, IL-1RA, and platelet-derived growth factor BB (PDGF-BB) have effects on MTNKL (Fig. 6). After FDR adjustment, these relationships were absent (FDR > 0.05). We also observed a causal effect of RANTES and IL-8 expression in CLL (Fig. 7). These relationships disappeared after adjusting for FDR (FDR > 0.05). A negative association was observed between IP-10, SCF, and ALL, but disappeared after FDR correction (Fig. 8) (FDR > 0.05). No causal effects were observed for other cytokines or CRP in the above hematologic malignancies (FDR > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted to verify the reliability of the IVW results. After FDR adjustment, the 41 cytokines and CRP showed no causal effects on hematologic malignancies, as determined by MR-Egger regression, weighted median, MR-RAPS, or MR-PRESSO methods (Table S3 and S4). Cochran’s Q test indicated heterogeneity in some MR analysis results (Q_p < 0.05). The random-effects IVW model was selected when heterogeneity was present. The MR-Egger intercept estimation results showed pleiotropy in MR analysis results between MIP-1β, RANTES, granulocyte colony stimulating factor (GCSF), and FL, as well as between CRP and DLBCL (Pleiotropy_p < 0.05). No pleiotropy was observed in the other MR analyses (Table S3). Leave-one-out analysis revealed that no IVW results were derived by a single SNP, indicating the reliability of the IVW results (Fig. 9). The MR Steiger test indicated that the causal directions were consistent with the hypothesized direction for all MR analyses (Table S5).

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the potential causal associations of 41 circulating inflammatory cytokines and CRP with selected hematologic malignancies using a two-sample MR framework based on large-scale GWASs of individuals of European ancestry. Several suggestive associations were observed in the primary IVW analysis. Genetically predicted higher levels of MIF were inversely associated with the risk of MPN, while higher levels of SCF, PDGF-BB, IL-8, IP-10, and SCF were suggestively associated with lower risks of DLBCL, MTNKL, CLL, and ALL, respectively. Conversely, genetically predicted higher levels of RANTES were suggestively associated with increased risks of HL and CLL; β-NGF and IL-1RA with follicular lymphoma (FL); MIG and MIP-1α with DLBCL; and IP-10, MCP-3, bFGF, IL-16, and IL-1RA with MTNKL. However, none of these associations remained statistically significant after FDR correction. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as suggestive evidence rather than definitive proof of causal effects [21, 22].

Inflammation is increasingly recognized as an important feature of hematologic malignancies [35, 36]. Cytokines participate in immune surveillance, hematopoietic regulation, stromal-cell interaction, tumor microenvironment remodeling, and malignant cell survival [37, 38]. In myeloid neoplasms, including AML, MPNs, and MDS, abnormal inflammatory signaling has been implicated in clonal hematopoiesis, bone marrow niche remodeling, and disease progression [38–40]. Similarly, in lymphoid malignancies, the cytokine-mediated crosstalk between malignant lymphocytes, immune cells, and stromal components may contribute to immune escape, tumor proliferation, and treatment response. These biological observations provide a plausible rationale for investigating whether circulating inflammatory cytokines play a causal role in the development of hematologic malignancies.

Several observational studies have reported associations between inflammatory cytokines and hematologic malignancies. For example, cytokine profiling studies in chronic myelomonocytic leukemia have shown that elevated levels of IL-8, IP-10, IL-1RA, TNF-α, IL-6, MCP-1/CCL2, and especially IL-10 may be associated with prognosis [41]. Similar cytokine abnormalities have been reported in primary myelofibrosis, in which circulating IL-8, IL-2R, IL-12, and IL-15 levels were independently associated with prognosis [10]. In MDS, elevated plasma CXCL10, IL-7, and IL-6 levels have been associated with inferior survival, further supporting the clinical relevance of inflammatory cytokine dysregulation in myeloid malignancies [42]. Another observational study reported that elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-8 were associated with the risk of hematologic malignancies [43]. In contrast, a previous two-sample MR study suggested that genetically predicted higher IL-8 levels were associated with a decreased risk of AML and that cutaneous T cell-attracting chemokine (CTACK) may be causally associated with NHL [44]. In addition, altered SCF-related signaling has been reported in CLL [45]. However, the biological relevance of SCF may differ across lymphoma subtypes, partly depending on c-KIT/SCF receptor expression and the local tumor microenvironment [46]. These findings indicate that cytokine dysregulation is common in hematologic malignancies. However, observational associations do not necessarily indicate causality because circulating cytokine levels can be influenced by tumor burden, immune activation secondary to malignancy, infection, treatment, comorbidities, and other confounding factors.

Our MR results differed partly from those of previous observational studies. This discrepancy may have several explanations [21, 22, 47]. First, elevated cytokine levels observed in patients with hematologic malignancies may represent the consequences of malignant transformation or disease progression rather than causal risk factors. Second, traditional observational studies are susceptible to confounding factors such as age, infection, autoimmune conditions, lifestyle factors, and treatment exposure. Third, cytokine signaling is highly context-dependent and may vary according to the disease stage, tumor subtype, immune microenvironment, and cellular receptor expression. Fourth, circulating cytokine concentrations may not fully reflect local cytokine activity within the bone marrow or lymphoid tissues where hematologic malignancies originate and progress. Finally, MR estimates reflect the lifelong effects of genetically predicted cytokine levels and may not capture short-term inflammatory changes that occur during tumor development, progression, or therapy.

Our findings are also relevant to previous MR evidence. A prior two-sample MR study reported that genetically predicted IL-8 was associated with a reduced risk of AML and suggested a causal association between CTACK and NHL [44, 48–50]. In contrast, our study focused on selected hematologic malignancy subtypes, including MPN, HL, FL, DLBCL, MTNKL, CLL, and ALL. Although we identified several nominally significant associations, they were not significant after correcting for multiple testing. This suggests that if causal effects exist, they are likely to be modest, subtype-specific, or difficult to detect with the currently available GWAS sample sizes. Therefore, the present results should not be interpreted as evidence that inflammatory cytokines are irrelevant to hematologic malignancies, but rather as indicating limited genetic support for strong linear causal effects of circulating cytokine levels on the selected outcomes analyzed in this study.

However, several specific cytokines identified in our study require further investigation. Chemokines such as IP-10, MIG, MIP-1α, MCP-3, RANTES, and IL-8 are involved in leukocyte recruitment, immune-cell trafficking, and tumor microenvironment regulation [51, 52]. Their associations with lymphoid malignancies may reflect the importance of chemokine-mediated interactions between malignant cells and surrounding immune or stromal cells. SCF is a key hematopoietic growth factor involved in stem and progenitor cell survival through the c-KIT pathway, which may partly explain its potential relevance to hematologic neoplasms [45, 53]. IL-1RA and IL-16 are immune regulatory mediators that may influence inflammatory balance and lymphocyte activation. However, because these associations were not robust after FDR correction, they should be considered hypothesis generating and require validation in larger genetic studies and functional experiments.

The major strength of this study is the use of a two-sample MR design to evaluate the potential causal relationships between circulating inflammatory markers and selected hematologic malignancies. Compared with conventional observational studies, MR can reduce bias from residual confounding and reverse causation by using genetic variants as instrumental variables [21, 47]. Additionally, we assessed a broad panel of cytokines and CRP levels, applied multiple MR methods, and performed sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the findings.

Nevertheless, this study had certain limitations. First, although we conducted sensitivity analyses including heterogeneity and pleiotropy assessments, potential horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity cannot be completely excluded [54, 55]. Second, the GWAS summary statistics were mainly derived from individuals of European ancestry, which limited the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Third, the number of available genetic instruments varied across cytokines. For some cytokines, the limited number of strongly associated SNPs may have reduced statistical power and increased the uncertainty of MR estimates. Fourth, several outcome datasets included a relatively small number of cases, which may have limited the ability to detect weak causal effects. Fifth, the present study evaluated genetically predicted circulating cytokine levels but could not assess local cytokine activity in the bone marrow, lymph nodes, or tumor tissues. Sixth, MR mainly estimates linear lifelong effects and may not capture nonlinear or threshold effects, such as potential U-shaped associations between cytokine levels and malignancy risk. Seventh, hematologic malignancies are heterogeneous. For example, HL includes nodular lymphocyte-predominant HL and classic HL, and classic HL can be further divided into nodular sclerosis, mixed cellularity, lymphocyte-depleted, and lymphocyte-rich subtypes [56, 57]. Due to the lack of GWAS data for detailed pathological subtypes, we were unable to conduct subtype-specific MR analyses. Finally, our analysis only included selected hematologic malignancies. Important entities, such as AML, multiple myeloma, and MDS, were not included because suitable GWAS summary statistics were unavailable or did not meet the requirements of the present analysis. Therefore, our conclusions are limited to the selected hematologic malignancies investigated in this study.

In conclusion, our two-sample MR analysis indicated several nominally significant associations between genetically predicted circulating inflammatory cytokines and selected hematologic malignancies. However, none of these associations remained significant after FDR correction. These findings provide limited evidence for strong causal effects of circulating inflammatory cytokines or CRP on the risk of the selected hematologic malignancies analyzed. Future studies with larger GWAS datasets, more detailed disease subtypes, multi-ancestry populations, and experimental validation are warranted to further clarify the role of inflammatory cytokines in the development of hematologic malignancies.
