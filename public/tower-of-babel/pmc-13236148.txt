Comprehensive radiological assessment of ethmoid skull-base anatomy: beyond the Keros classification
Authors: Kanav Gupta, Rabi Narayan Sahu, Ashis Patnaik, Shaurya Darbari, Bipin Chaurasia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Keros classification has been the traditional standard for stratifying olfactory fossa depth and predicting skull-base risk during endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS). However, accumulating evidence from multicenter CT studies across different populations demonstrates that Keros type alone does not correlate with other clinically important skull-base parameters, including ethmoid roof asymmetry, orbit-referenced skull-base height indices, and the position of the anterior ethmoidal artery (AEA) in relation to supraorbital ethmoid cells (SOECs). This narrative review synthesizes contemporary radiological literature to evaluate the role of additional CT-based measurements in refining preoperative risk assessment for skull-base injury. We review 15 key publications spanning populations from the Middle East, Europe, and South Asia, emphasizing that a multi-parameter approach – including Keros classification, asymmetry assessment, AEA-to-skull-base distance, and SOEC variants – provides a more comprehensive and clinically actionable description of ethmoid skull-base anatomy than traditional Keros classification alone. We also highlight the implications of these anatomical variations for surgical planning in patients with chronic rhinosinusitis, allergic fungal rhinosinusitis, and increased cerebrospinal fluid leak risk. Future prospective studies linking preoperative CT phenotypes to surgical complication rates are needed to validate these expanded parameters.

Introduction

The ethmoid skull base, comprising the cribriform plate and fovea ethmoidalis, remains one of the most challenging and high-risk anatomical zones in endoscopic sinus and skull-base surgery[1]. The lateral lamella of the cribriform plate (LLCP) is particularly vulnerable owing to its extreme thinness (often <1 mm), proximity to the anterior cranial fossa, and the critical blood supply of the anterior ethmoidal artery (AEA), which traverses this region[2,3]. Since its introduction in 1962, the Keros classification has served as the gold standard for describing olfactory fossa depth and stratifying surgical risk[6]. The classification divides patients into three categories based on the vertical height of the LLCP: Type I (1–3 mm), Type II (4–7 mm), and Type III (>7 mm or 8–16 mm depending on source)[1,6,7]. Paradoxically, Type III (deepest fossa) was initially thought to confer the highest risk of CSF leak because of the deeper olfactory fossa. However, large retrospective series of CSF leak repairs have revealed that most iatrogenic injuries occur outside the cribriform region, in other parts of the ethmoid skull base[2,8]. This finding has prompted clinicians and researchers to investigate whether additional radiological parameters – beyond Keros type alone – provide clinically relevant information for preoperative planning.

Contemporary CT-based morphometric studies have identified several independent anatomical parameters that do not correlate with Keros classification and yet carry important surgical implications. These include: (1) ethmoid roof asymmetry (side-to-side LLCP height difference), (2) orbit-referenced skull-base height indices (the relationship between skull-base insertion height and total orbital height), (3) the vertical distance from the AEA to the skull base (AEA-skull-base length), and (4) the presence and degree of supraorbital ethmoid cell (SOEC) pneumatization, which affects frontal recess configuration and AEA position[8,9]. Notably, SOEC pneumatization has been shown to significantly influence the AEA-to-skull-base distance, such that when SOEC is present, the AEA is on average 3–4 mm further from the skull base than when it is absent[3].

The purpose of this narrative review is to synthesize the contemporary evidence regarding comprehensive radiological assessment of ethmoid skull-base anatomy, critically appraise the limitations of Keros classification as a standalone predictor, and integrate recent findings on SOEC and AEA position into a coherent framework for preoperative CT interpretation. We focus on peer-reviewed CT morphometry studies from diverse populations and discuss the clinical implications for risk mitigation in ESS, particularly in high-risk subgroups such as patients with allergic fungal rhinosinusitis (AFRS).

Methods (literature review)

A narrative literature review was conducted by searching PubMed, Google Scholar, and institutional repositories for peer-reviewed publications on ethmoid skull-base anatomy, Keros classification, CT-based morphometry, AEA position, and SOEC variations. Search terms included: “Keros classification,” “ethmoid roof,” “anterior ethmoidal artery,” “supraorbital ethmoid cell,” “skull-base anatomy,” “endoscopic sinus surgery complications,” “cerebrospinal fluid leak,” and “anatomical variation.” Studies published between 2000 and 2025 in English-language peer-reviewed journals were included if they: (1) reported CT-based morphometric measurements of the ethmoid skull base, (2) included sample sizes of ≥50 subjects, (3) provided quantitative data on Keros distribution or additional skull-base parameters, or (4) discussed clinical implications of anatomical variants for surgical planning. Exclusion criteria included case reports, editorials, and studies lacking quantitative morphometric data. Fifteen key publications meeting these criteria were selected for detailed analysis, with emphasis on multicenter and large-cohort studies that provide population-level insights into Keros distribution and skull-base variation.

Results and literature synthesis

The Keros classification: definition, distribution, and clinical basis

The Keros classification, introduced by Keros and Bernal (1962), is a three-tiered categorization of olfactory fossa depth based on the vertical distance of the LLCP from the skull base[6]. Type I denotes a shallow fossa (1–3 mm LLCP height), Type II an intermediate fossa (4–7 mm), and Type III a deep fossa (8–16 mm or >7 mm depending on definition)[1,6,7]. The classification was originally intended to identify patients at higher risk of CSF leak during functional ESS, with the assumption that a deeper olfactory fossa (Type III) would be more susceptible to inadvertent penetration and CSF leakage[6].

Population-level distribution of Keros types varies significantly across ethnic and geographic populations (Table 1). In a large Iranian study of 600 patients, Keros Type II was most common at 50.5%, followed by Type I at 36.7% and Type III at 12.8%[10]. In contrast, a Saudi Arabian study of 400 subjects reported Type II as predominant at 73.3%, with Type I at 26.1% and Type III at only 0.5%[11]. A study of 200 Indian subjects (from the literature referenced herein) found similar predominance of Type II (52%) over Type I (35%) and Type III (13%)[9]. Independence of Keros classification from other skull-base parameters

A foundational study by Ramakrishnan et al measured three independent parameters in 200 adult patients: (1) Keros classification, (2) maxillary sinus-to-ethmoid height ratio (1:1, 2:1, or >2:1), and (3) skull-base height-to-orbital height ratio (Class I <25%, Class II 25–33%, Class III >33%)[1]. Using Pearson correlation analysis (appropriate for continuous data in that study), the authors found correlation coefficients of <0.12 between all three classification schemes, indicating that each provided independent anatomical information and that Keros type alone did not predict the other two indices[1]. This landmark finding suggests that relying on Keros classification as the sole preoperative risk indicator may miss substantial numbers of patients with other high-risk anatomy (e.g., low skull-base insertion on the medial orbit, shortened ethmoid depth)[1].

Ethmoid roof asymmetry: clinical significance and prevalence

Side-to-side asymmetry of the LLCP height represents a common anatomical variant with important surgical implications. An asymmetric roof means that dissection on the lower (more medial) side carries greater risk of skull-base penetration, especially when that lower side approaches 1–2 mm in height[2,8]. In the Iranian study, asymmetry was present in approximately 18–20% of subjects, with varying degrees of side-to-side difference[10]. Because the lowest point on an asymmetric roof determines the margin of safety on that side, a surgeon who focuses only on the higher (safer) side may underestimate risk[2]. Recent evidence suggests that explicit preoperative documentation of asymmetry and measurement of the lower side should be routine, particularly when approaching the medial ethmoid on that side[8].

AEA position and SOEC relationship

The AEA is a critical vascular landmark that traverses the medial orbit and crosses superior to the cribriform plate. Iatrogenic injury to the AEA during ESS can result in retraction of the artery into the orbit, leading to retrobulbar hemorrhage and permanent vision loss[11,12]. The AEA typically enters the medial orbit at a position slightly above and medial to the orbital beak (a bony prominence on the medial orbital rim)[3].

A key finding from recent studies is the strong correlation between SOEC pneumatization and the vertical distance from the AEA to the skull base (AEA–SB distance)[3,9]. In a study of 50 CT scans, when SOEC was absent, the mean AEA–SB distance was only 1.5 mm; when SOEC was present, the mean distance increased significantly to 4.86 mm (P < 0.001, highly significant)[3]. This threefold difference in AEA-to-skull-base distance has profound implications: when SOEC is absent, the AEA runs dangerously close to the skull base, increasing the risk of vascular injury if the surgeon dissects too far superior in the frontal recess[3,9]. Conversely, SOEC presence “pushes” the AEA superiorly, creating a larger safety margin[3].

Several grading systems have been proposed for AEA position relative to the skull base (Grade I: artery level with or above skull base; Grade II: artery 1–2 mm below skull base; Grade III: artery >2 mm below skull base)[9]. In a detailed assessment of 200 patients, Grade I AEA was most common (approximately 60–65%), followed by Grade II, with Grade III (highest risk) being relatively uncommon (8–12%)[9]. Importantly, there was a significant association between Keros type II and increasing AEA grading (i.e., AEA progressively lower relative to skull base), though this relationship was not perfectly predictive[9]. This reinforces the concept that Keros type provides some but not complete information about AEA risk.

AFRS and special anatomical considerations

AFRS is an aggressive form of chronic rhinosinusitis associated with massive sinus expansion and increased risk of AEA and skull-base injury during ESS[13,14]. A recent comparative study examined AEA-to-skull-base length in 50 patients with AFRS versus 50 patients with chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP)[13]. Remarkably, AFRS patients had a significantly longer AEA–SB distance (mean ≈ 5.2 mm) compared to CRSwNP patients (mean ≈ 3.1 mm, P < 0.05)[13]. This counterintuitive finding – suggesting that AFRS affords a larger safety margin for the AEA – may reflect the fact that aggressive expansion of the ethmoid sinus in AFRS pushes structures superiorly and laterally[13]. However, this must be interpreted cautiously: the massive disease burden and altered anatomy in AFRS often demands more extensive dissection, potentially offsetting the benefit of increased AEA distance[13,14]. The implication is that preoperative CT measurement of AEA–SB distance is particularly valuable in AFRS to guide the extent and trajectory of dissection.

Discussion

Limitations of Keros classification as a standalone predictor

While the Keros classification remains valuable for rapid stratification of olfactory fossa depth, accumulating evidence reveals significant limitations. First, Keros type does not correlate with other independent skull-base parameters (maxillary-to-ethmoid height ratio, skull-base-to-orbital height ratio), implying that a patient may have “favorable” Keros anatomy but unfavorable values on other indices[1]. Second, Keros type alone does not predict AEA position or SOEC presence reliably, yet both of these factors carry substantial surgical implications[3,9]. Third, the retrospective nature of CSF leak/complication literature suggests that most injuries occur in the supraethmoid region rather than specifically at the cribriform plate, implying that parameters other than Keros type – such as skull-base-to-orbit height ratio or AEA–SB distance – may be equally or more predictive of actual surgical risk[2,8].

This multi-parameter approach is not intended to replace Keros classification but rather to supplement it with additional independent anatomical information. For example, a patient with Keros Type I (low risk by traditional thinking) but a very low skull-base-to-orbital height ratio and absent SOEC (hence very close AEA) may actually warrant heightened caution and consideration of alternative approaches (e.g., staged surgery, consultation with neurosurgery, or modified dissection strategy). Conversely, a Type III patient with excellent skull-base-to-orbital height ratio and prominent SOEC may have lower-than-expected surgical risk.

Population-specific and disease-specific considerations

The considerable variation in Keros-type distribution across populations (Table 1) has important implications for surgical training and expectations. Surgeons trained in populations where Type II predominates (e.g., Saudi Arabia at 73.3% or Turkey at 76.1%) may have different baseline expectations than those in populations with higher Type I prevalence (e.g., Egyptian population at 40–50%)[10,11]. This variation underscores the importance of population-specific reference data and individual measurement-based planning rather than assumption of average population risk profiles. Furthermore, special disease states such as AFRS warrant specific attention: the finding that AFRS patients have longer AEA–SB distances than CRSwNP patients suggests that disease pathophysiology (aggressive expansion) influences anatomical relationships in ways that modify surgical risk in both positive (increased AEA safety margin) and negative (need for more extensive dissection) directions[13].

Conclusion

Contemporary evidence from multicenter CT morphometry studies across diverse populations demonstrates that the Keros classification, while useful for rapid assessment of olfactory fossa depth, does not provide complete preoperative risk stratification for endoscopic sinus surgery. The independence of Keros type from other skull-base parameters – including ethmoid roof asymmetry, skull-base-to-orbital height ratio, and AEA-to-skull-base distance – indicates that a multi-parameter radiological approach offers a more comprehensive and clinically actionable description of ethmoid skull-base anatomy. The presence of SOECs significantly influences AEA position, with SOEC absence associated with dangerously short AEA–SB distances. Disease-specific considerations, such as the altered anatomy of AFRS, further underline the need for individualized, measurement-based preoperative planning. We recommend that routine preoperative CT assessment include documentation of Keros type with asymmetry notation, skull-base-to-orbital height ratio, AEA–SB distance stratification, SOEC status, and additional skull-base variants as described herein. This expanded radiological framework, combined with surgeon experience and procedural considerations, should help reduce the incidence of skull-base injury and CSF leak complications in ESS.
