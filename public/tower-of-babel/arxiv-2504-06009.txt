Linear time-and-space-invariant relaxation systems
Tihol Ivanov Donchev, Brayan M. Shali, Rodolphe Sepulchre

Abstract
This paper generalizes the physical property of relaxation from linear time-invariant (LTI) to linear time-and-space-invariant (LTSI) systems. It is shown that the defining features of relaxation—complete monotonicity, passivity, and memory-based storage—carry over seamlessly to the spatio-temporal domain. An LTSI system is shown to be of relaxation type if and only if its associated spatio-temporal Hankel operator is cyclically monotone. This implies the existence of an intrinsic quadratic storage functional defined uniquely by past inputs, independently of any state-space realization. As in the LTI case, LTSI relaxation systems are shown to be those systems for which the state-space concept of storage coincides with the input-output concept of fading memory functional.



I
Introduction

Relaxation systems have recently received renewed attention in systems and control theory due to their relevance in structured optimal control, circuit synthesis, and modeling of physical and biological processes
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. Yet, existing literature mostly addresses relaxation from a purely
temporal
perspective. Many real-world applications—particularly in distributed-parameter systems governed by partial differential equations—require a framework that also accounts for
spatial
variations. In this paper, we propose such an extension, generalizing the classical theory of linear time-invariant (LTI) relaxation systems to systems that depend on both time and space. Specifically, we address systems that are also spatially invariant, resulting in a linear time-and-space-invariant (LTSI) system.

In the LTI setting, the relaxation property admits two classical characterizations
[
6
]
. From a state-space viewpoint, relaxation systems are (internally) reciprocal and passive, containing only one type of energy storage element. From an input-output perspective, relaxation systems correspond to convolution operators whose impulse responses are completely monotone, i.e., their memory decays monotonically and without a hint of oscillation. The recent work
[
3
]
shows that relaxation can be characterized as cyclic monotonicity of the Hankel operator. The cyclic monotonicity of the Hankel operator implies the existence of an intrinsic storage functional uniquely determined by past inputs alone. The latter is consistent with Willems’ notion of storage, hence this result identifies relaxation systems as those systems for which the state-space notion of storage coincides with the input-output notion of memory.

More recently, these ideas have been extended to the nonlinear setting. Building on the state-space notions of reciprocity and passivity,
[
5
]
defines nonlinear relaxation systems as gradient systems with a Hessian Riemannian metric satisfying both reciprocity and passivity conditions, thus ensuring energy dissipation without oscillatory behavior. An alternative definition is provided in
[
4
]
, where nonlinear relaxation systems are characterized from an input-output perspective as fading memory operators with completely monotone impulse responses. This characterization relies on the universal approximation property of nonlinear fading memory operators
[
7
]
, which shows that a nonlinear fading memory operator can be uniformly approximated by an LTI system composed with a nonlinear static readout. The input-output characterization introduced in
[
4
]
is consistent with the state-space definition of
[
5
]
in the sense that the former can be seen as a special case of the latter.

In this paper, we show that the defining features of relaxation—complete monotonicity, passivity, and memory-based storage—carry over seamlessly to the spatio-temporal domain. Specifically, we prove that an LTSI system is of relaxation type precisely when its spatio-temporal Hankel operator is cyclically monotone. As such, the latter is the gradient of a closed convex functional defined uniquely by past inputs alone. This establishes a direct link between the internal (state-space) and external (input-output) perspectives on energy storage. The results are illustrated throughout this paper with the diffusion equation as a canonical example of spatio-temporal relaxation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we
introduce
diffusion as a natural example of spatio-temporal relaxation. Section
III
contains notation and preliminaries. In Section
IV
, we define and characterize (internal) relaxation for LTSI systems. In Section
V
, we show that internal relaxation implies impedance passivity with storage determined from the external behaviour. In Section VI, we formalize the connection between internal and external by establishing properties of the Hankel operator of an LTSI relaxation system. Finally, Section
VII
summarizes our findings and discusses directions for further work.

To motivate the content of this paper, we argue that diffusion is the physical phenomenon that should underline the system theoretic property of relaxation for LTSI systems.
Historically, relaxation was first
identified
[
8
]
as the system theoretic property of passive electrical circuits with only one type of energy storage element, i.e., RC or RL circuits.

This characterization naturally extends
to spatially distributed systems by considering an infinite transmission line composed of resistors and capacitors periodically arranged along a spatial axis.
Figure
1
illustrates the schematic of such an infinite distributed RC transmission line. Taking the spatial discretization interval
\Delta x\rightarrow 0
, this discrete electrical network is precisely governed by the one-dimensional diffusion equation
[
9
]
, i.e.

C\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V(t,x)=\frac{1}{R}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}V(t,x)+I(t,x),

(1)

where
V(t,x)
and
I(t,x)

are the voltage and current
at time
t
and position
x
.
Denoting
the variables
y(t,x)=V(t,x)
,
u(t,x)=I(t,x)/C
, and the coefficient
\alpha=1/RC
, the latter simplifies to the standard (heat) diffusion equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}y(t,x)=\alpha\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}y(t,x)+u(t,x).

(2)

The explicit solution of (
2
), see, e.g.,
[
10
]
, is given by the convolution

y(t,x)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(t-\tau,x-\xi)u(\tau,\xi)\,\textrm{d}\xi\textrm{d}\tau,

(3)

where
g
is the so-called
heat kernel
given by

g(t,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\alpha t}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4\alpha t}}.

(4)

The heat kernel clearly exhibits the relaxation property
when regarded as a spatially extended function of time.
In particular, Figure
2(a)
illustrates the spatial profile of (
4
) at various times
t
. It shows the relaxation property, as the spatial profile flattens monotonically towards equilibrium as
t
increases. However, Figure
2(b)
shows the kernel’s temporal behavior at a fixed location, highlighting that the kernel is not a completely monotone function in time, as one might expect from a classical LTI relaxation system.

This apparent discrepancy is resolved by examining the spatial Fourier transform of the diffusion equation (
2
), which yields the infinite family of decoupled LTI systems

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{y}(t,\omega)=-\alpha\omega^{2}\hat{y}(t,\omega)+\hat{u}(t,\omega)

(5)

parameterized by the frequency variable
\omega
. The impulse response of the latter is given by

\hat{g}(t,\omega)=e^{-\alpha\omega^{2}t}.

(6)

In this frequency domain representation, each mode
\omega
individually defines an LTI system whose impulse response is completely monotone in time. Thus, the diffusion equation defines an infinite-dimensional system that can be decomposed into an infinite family of finite-dimensional LTI relaxation systems.

The diffusion process provides a clear physical connection from classical relaxation theory to its infinite-dimensional, spatially extended counterpart. This is further reinforced by the recent work
[
11
]
, where it is explicitly demonstrated that the spatial discretization of the diffusion equation leads to a finite-dimensional LTI relaxation system. In this paper, we extend the theory of LTI relaxation systems to a class of LTSI system, with the diffusion process as a canonical example of an LTSI relaxation system.

III
Notation and preliminaries

We use mostly standard notation. We denote the sets of nonnegative real numbers and integers by
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, respectively. We denote the inner-product on a (complex) Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}
and the induced norm by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}
. We omit the subscript if
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
or there is no risk of ambiguity. We denote the Banach space of bounded linear operators from
\mathcal{X}
to another Hilbert space
\mathcal{Y}
by
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, and the operator norm by
\lVert\cdot\rVert
. We use the shorthand notation
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})=\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{X})
. We also consider unbounded linear operators
\mathcal{A}:\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{X}
is the domain of
\mathcal{A}
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is densely defined if
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{X}
is dense. If
\mathcal{A}
is densely defined, then it has an adjoint
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}:\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\ast})\to\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\ast})\subset\mathcal{Y}
is closed. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is self-adjoint if
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A})=\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\ast})
and
\langle\mathcal{A}x,y\rangle=\langle x,\mathcal{A}y\rangle
for all
x,y\in\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A})
. We say that a self-adjoint
\mathcal{A}
is nonnegative if
\langle\mathcal{A}x,x\rangle\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A})
, and we write
\mathcal{A}\geq 0
to indicate that
\mathcal{A}
is self-adjoint and nonnegative.

III-A

Square integrable functions and the Fourier transform

We denote the Hilbert space of square integrable functions from a domain
X
to a Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
by
L_{2}(X,\mathcal{X})
, where

\langle f,g\rangle_{L_{2}(X,\mathcal{X})}=\int_{X}\langle f(x),g(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}\,\textrm{d}x.

(7)

The Fourier transform of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{s}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the function
\hat{f}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
given by

\hat{f}(\omega)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(2\pi)^{-s/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}e^{-j\langle\omega,x\rangle}f(x)\,\textrm{d}x.

(8)

It is well-known
[
12
]
that the Fourier transform converges for absolutely integrable functions. Due to Plancherel’s theorem, the Fourier transform can be extended to a
unitary
operator
\mathcal{F}\in\mathscr{B}(L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{n}))
, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}^{-1}=\mathcal{F}^{\ast}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
agrees with the Fourier transform on the dense subspace of absolutely integrable functions, we refer to
\mathcal{F}f
as the Fourier transform of
f
, and we write
\hat{f}=\mathcal{F}f
.

A key property of the Fourier transform that we exploit in this paper is that translation-invariant operators are transformed into multiplication operators. The Fourier transform of a densely defined operator
\mathcal{A}
on
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{n})
is the densely defined operator
\hat{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{F}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{F}^{\ast}
on
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{n})
, such that
\hat{\mathcal{A}}\hat{f}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{n})
is the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{A}f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{n})
. For each
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, the
translation operator

\mathcal{T}_{\bar{x}}\in\mathscr{B}(L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{n}))
is given by
(\mathcal{T}_{\bar{x}}f)(x)=f(x+\bar{x})
. A densely defined operator
\mathcal{A}
is
translation-invariant
if
\mathcal{T}_{\bar{x}}:\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathcal{T}_{\bar{x}}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{T}_{\bar{x}}
for all
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. The Fourier transform of a translation-invariant operator is a
multiplication operator

[
12
, Section 3.6.7]
, i.e., there exists a function
\hat{A}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that
(\hat{\mathcal{A}}\hat{f})(\omega)=\hat{A}(\omega)\hat{f}(\omega)
. The function
\hat{A}
is called the
symbol
of the operator
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
. It is well-known that the operators
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and, thus,
\mathcal{A}
are bounded if and only if the symbol
\hat{A}
is bounded.

Throughout this paper, we consider functions that depend on a temporal variable
t
and a spatial (frequency) variable
x
(
\omega
). We denote the dependence on the spatial (frequency) variable with a subscript, e.g., we write
f_{x}
instead of
f(x)
. In particular, we often consider functions of the form
f:\mathbb{T}\to L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{m})
, where
\mathbb{T}
is a temporal domain and
\mathbb{R}^{s}
is a spatial (frequency) domain. We interpret such functions as mapping the temporal variable
t\in\mathbb{T}
to a spatial (frequency) profile
f(t)\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{m})
. We denote the dependence on the spatial (frequency) variable with a subscript, i.e., we write
f_{x}(t)=f(t)(x)
, so that the function
f_{x}:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the temporal trajectory at a given point in space
x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Finally, we write
\hat{f}
to denote the spatial Fourier transform of
f
, i.e., the function
\hat{f}:\mathbb{T}\to L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{m})
such that
\hat{f}(t)\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{m})
is the Fourier transform of
f(t)\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{m})
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

III-B

LTI relaxation systems

Consider the linear time-invariant (LTI) system

\Sigma:\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}z(t)&=Az(t)+Bu(t),\\
y(t)&=Cz(t),\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

with input space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, state space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and output space
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The impulse response of the system
\Sigma
is the matrix-valued map
g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
given by
g(t)=Ce^{At}B
. Assuming that the system
\Sigma
is initially at rest, i.e.,
z(-\infty)=0
, its output is given by the convolution

y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}g(t-\tau)u(\tau)\,\textrm{d}\tau.

(10)

The system
\Sigma
is of
relaxation type

[
6
]
if
m=p
and its impulse response
g
is devoid of any oscillatory behaviour, expressed as
g
being
completely monotone

[
13
, Chapter IV]
, i.e.,

(-1)^{k}\frac{\textrm{d}^{k}}{\textrm{d}t^{k}}g(t)\geq 0

(11)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. By Bernstein’s theorem

[
14
]
,
g
is completely monotone if and only if it is a conic combination of decaying exponentials, i.e.,
g(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}e^{-p_{i}t}
, where
G_{i}\geq 0
and
p_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Relaxation systems are passive and reciprocal with only one type of energy storage. They admit a unique storage function that can be determined from the input-output behaviour of the system. Furthermore, every relaxation system has a state space realization that is internally of relaxation type
[
15
]
, i.e.,
B=C^{\ast}
and
A\leq 0
.

For an exponentially stable system
\Sigma
, relaxation can be characterized via the Hankel operator, i.e., the bounded linear operator
\mathcal{H}:L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{m})\to L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{p})
given by

(\mathcal{H}v)(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}g(t+\tau)v(\tau)\,\textrm{d}\tau.

(12)

The Hankel operator
\mathcal{H}
maps past inputs to future outputs in the sense that the input
u(t)=v(-t)
for all
t\leq 0
and
u(t)=0
for all
t>0
, results in the output
y(t)=(\mathcal{H}v)(t)
for all
t\geq 0
. In
[
3
]
, it is shown that the Hankel operator of a relaxation system is self-adjoint and nonnegative, hence cyclically monotone. This implies that
\mathcal{H}
is the functional derivative of a quadratic (memory) functional, which serves as an intrinsic energy storage for the system.

Finally, we note that the results in this section are also valid for LTI systems with complex input, state and output spaces, which are considered throughout this paper.

IV
LTSI systems and relaxation

In this section, we define and characterize the notion of relaxation for a class of linear time-and-space-invariant (LTSI) systems. The definition of relaxation for LTSI systems is a natural generalization of the definition for LTI systems, namely, it is expressed as complete monotonicity of the (operator-valued) impulse response. The spatial invariance of the LTSI system allows us to view it as a family of LTI systems via the Fourier transform. This allows us to characterize relaxation of the LTSI system as relaxation of the corresponding family of LTI systems.

Consider the LTSI system
\Sigma
of the form

\Sigma:\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}z(t)&=\mathcal{A}z(t)+\mathcal{B}u(t),\\
y(t)&=\mathcal{C}z(t),\end{aligned}\right.

(13)

with (infinite-dimensional) input space
\mathcal{U}=L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{m})
, state space
\mathcal{Z}=L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{n})
, output space
\mathcal{Y}=L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{p})
, and translation-invariant linear operators
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{Z}
,
\mathcal{B}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Z})
and
\mathcal{C}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Y})
, where we assume that the domain
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{Z}
is dense. The system is time-invariant because the operators
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
are constant, and space-invariant because they are translation-invariant. The operator
\mathcal{A}
is typically unbounded and the notion of solution requires some care, see, e.g.
[
16
]
. Without going into details, we assume that
\mathcal{A}
is the infinitesimal generator of a
C_{0}
-semigroup
t\mapsto\exp(\mathcal{A}t)\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{Z})
. The notation is motivated by the fact that the latter is a generalization of the exponential function.

The impulse response of the LTSI system
\Sigma
is the operator-valued map
\mathcal{G}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
given by

\mathcal{G}(t)=\mathcal{C}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\exp(\mathcal{A}t)}\mathcal{B}.

(14)

The output of
\Sigma
is given by the convolution

y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathcal{G}(t-\tau)u(\tau)\,\textrm{d}\tau,

(15)

where we have assumed that
z(-\infty)=0
, i.e., the system is initially at rest. Since
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
are translation-invariant, their Fourier transforms
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
,
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
are multiplication operators with symbols
\hat{A}
,
\hat{B}
and
\hat{C}
. Consequently, the Fourier transform of (
13
) yields the family of LTI systems

\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}:\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}\hat{z}_{\omega}(t)&=\hat{A}_{\omega}\hat{z}_{\omega}(t)+\hat{B}_{\omega}\hat{u}_{\omega}(t),\\
\hat{y}_{\omega}(t)&=\hat{C}_{\omega}\hat{z}_{\omega}(t),\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

parametrized by the frequency variable
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. The Fourier transform
\hat{\mathcal{G}}(t)
of
\mathcal{G}(t)
is a multiplication operator with symbol
\hat{g}(t)
given by
\hat{g}_{\omega}(t)=\hat{C}_{\omega}e^{\hat{A}_{\omega}t}\hat{B}_{\omega}
, where
\hat{g}_{\omega}
is the impulse response of the LTI system
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
. We assume that the symbols
\hat{A}
,
\hat{B}
and
\hat{C}
are continuous, so that
\hat{g}(t)
is continuous for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

The fact that an LTSI system can be viewed as a family of LTI systems greatly simplifies its analysis. Indeed, many system theoretic properties of
\Sigma
can be verified
pointwise
by verifying the analogous property for all
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, see, e.g.,
[
17
,
18
]
. This is the overarching theme in this paper.
With this in mind, as a natural generalization of the definition of relaxation for LTI systems, we define relaxation for LTSI systems as follows.

Definition 1

The LTSI system
\Sigma
is of relaxation type if
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{Y}
and the impulse response
\mathcal{G}
is completely monotone.

The impulse response
\mathcal{G}
is completely monotone if its
k
-th strong derivative satisfies

(-1)^{k}\frac{\textrm{d}^{k}}{\textrm{d}t^{k}}\mathcal{G}(t)\geq 0

(17)

for all
t>0
and
k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Since the Fourier transform is unitary, completely monotonicity of
\mathcal{G}
is equivalent to complete monotonicity of
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
. The latter is a multiplication operator, hence its properties can be inferred from its symbol, i.e., the impulse responses
\hat{g}_{\omega}
,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Consequently, relaxation can be verified pointwise, as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 1

The LTSI system
\Sigma
is of relaxation type if and only if the LTI system
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
is of relaxation type for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
.

Proof:

As shown in
[
16
, Theorem 2.1.13]
, the semigroup
\exp(\mathcal{A}t)
is strongly differentiable and the
k
-th strong derivative is given by
\exp(\mathcal{A}t)\mathcal{A}^{k}
for all
t>0
on the dense domain
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{A}^{k})\subset\mathcal{Z}
. Therefore, the impulse response
\mathcal{G}(t)
is strongly differentiable with
k
-th strong derivative given by

\frac{\textrm{d}^{k}}{\textrm{d}t^{k}}\mathcal{G}(t)=\mathcal{C}\exp(\mathcal{A}t)\mathcal{A}^{k}\mathcal{B}

(18)

The Fourier transform of the latter is a multiplication operator
\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{k}(t)
with
continuous
symbol
\hat{g}^{k}(t)
given by

\hat{g}^{k}_{\omega}(t)=\frac{\textrm{d}^{k}}{\textrm{d}t^{k}}\hat{g}_{\omega}(t)=\hat{C}_{\omega}e^{\hat{A}_{\omega}t}\hat{A}_{\omega}^{k}\hat{B}_{\omega}

(19)

Therefore,
(-1)^{k}\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{k}(t)\geq 0
if and only if
(-1)^{k}\hat{g}^{k}_{\omega}(t)\geq 0
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Moreover, since the Fourier transform is unitary,
(-1)^{k}\hat{\mathcal{G}}^{k}(t)\geq 0
if and only if (
17
) holds. Combining the latter two statements, it follows that
\mathcal{G}(t)
is completely monotone if and only if
\hat{g}_{\omega}
is completely monotone for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Recall that LTI relaxation systems have state-space realizations that are internally of relaxation type
[
15
]
. This is not necessarily true for LTSI relaxation systems, mainly due to the technical assumptions on the operators that define an LTSI system, see Remark
1
. Whenever necessary, we avoid this issue by simply assuming that
\Sigma
is internally of relaxation type, defined below.

Definition 2

The LTSI system
\Sigma
is
internally of relaxation type
if
\mathcal{A}\leq 0
and
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{C}^{\ast}
.

Analogously to Theorem
1
, we obtain the following characterization of internal relaxation.

Theorem 2

The LTSI system
\Sigma
is internally of relaxation type if and only if the LTI system
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
is internally of relaxation type for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
.

Proof:

Since the Fourier transform is unitary,
\mathcal{A}\leq 0
and
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{C}^{\ast}
if and only if
\hat{\mathcal{A}}\leq 0
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\ast}
. Since the symbols
\hat{A}
,
\hat{B}
, and
\hat{C}
of the multiplication operators
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
,
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
are continuous,
\hat{\mathcal{A}}\leq 0
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\ast}
if and only if
\hat{A}_{\omega}\leq 0
and
\hat{B}_{\omega}=\hat{C}_{\omega}^{\ast}
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
.
∎

We now go back to the diffusion equation as an example, after which we conclude this section with a couple of remarks.

Example 1

As expected, diffusion is an example of LTSI relaxation. Indeed, note that the diffusion equation (
2
) is an LTSI system of the form (
13
) with
\mathcal{A}=\alpha\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}
and
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}
given by the identity operator. We have already seen that the impulse response (
6
) is completely monotone for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, hence the diffusion equation is an LTSI system of relaxation type due to Theorem
1
. Furthermore, it is well-known that the second-order differential operator
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}
is self-adjoint and nonpositive, hence the diffusion equation is an example of an LTSI system that is
internally
of relaxation type.

Remark 1

It is easily seen that internal relaxation implies relaxation. For the converse, suppose that the LTSI system
\Sigma
is of relaxation type, hence, due to Theorem
1
, the LTI system
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
is of relaxation type for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. This means that
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
has a state-space realization that is internally of relaxation type, i.e.,
\hat{g}_{\omega}(t)={\bar{B}}_{\omega}^{\ast}e^{{\bar{A}}_{\omega}t}{\bar{B}}_{\omega}
, where
{\bar{A}}_{\omega}\leq 0
. Using this, we can define
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
and
\bar{\mathcal{B}}
as the translation-invariant operators whose Fourier transforms are the multiplicative operators with symbols
{\bar{A}}
and
{\bar{B}}
. Then, we can write
\mathcal{G}(t)=\bar{\mathcal{B}}^{\ast}\exp(\bar{\mathcal{A}}t)\bar{\mathcal{B}}
so that
\mathcal{G}
can be seen as the impulse response of an LTSI system that is internally of relaxation type. The issue here is that
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is not guaranteed to be the infinitesimal generator of a
C_{0}
-semigroup, and
\bar{\mathcal{B}}
is not guaranteed to be bounded. Nevertheless, examples where these conditions are not satisfied seem to be artificial mathematical constructs rather than real physical examples. Similar issues arise when dealing with (impedance) passivity, see Remark
3
.

Remark 2

Systems of the form (
13
) with
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{C}^{\ast}
are known as
collocated systems

[
16
, p.266]
. The name reflects the practice of applying control and observation actions at the same point in distributed parameter systems. We refer to a collocated system of the form (
13
) with
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
as
internally symmetric
. If, in addition,
\mathcal{A}\leq 0
, then
\mathcal{A}
is the infinitesimal generator of a
contraction semigroup
, see
[
16
, Section 2.3]
for details. The latter is a mild form of stability which is implied by, e.g., exponential stability. Therefore, internal relaxation in LTSI systems is a combination of internal symmetry and stability, just like internal relaxation in LTI systems
[
6
,
15
]
.

V
Impedance passivity and relaxation

In this section, we show that LTSI systems that are internally of relaxation type are impedance passive with energy storage that can be determined from their input-output behaviour, thus extending the analogous result on LTI relaxation systems
[
6
]
. In doing so, we also characterize impedance passivity for LTSI systems, namely, we show that it can be verified pointwise, just like (internal) relaxation.

To begin with, impedance passivity is the analogue of passivity for infinite-dimensional systems, see
[
16
, Section 7.5]
for details. It is defined as follows.

Definition 3

The LTSI system
\Sigma
is
impedance passive
if
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{Y}
and there exists a storage functional
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, given by
\mathcal{S}(z)=\langle\mathcal{Q}z,z\rangle
with
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{Z})
,
\mathcal{Q}\geq 0
, such that

\mathcal{S}(z(t))\leq\mathcal{S}(z(0))+\int_{0}^{t}\langle u(\tau),y(\tau)\rangle+\langle y(\tau),u(\tau)\rangle\,\textrm{d}t

for all
z(0)\in\mathcal{Z}
,
t\geq 0
, and
u\in L_{2}([0,\tau],\mathcal{U})
.

Impedance passivity of infinite-dimensional systems has a similar characterization as passivity of finite-dimensional systems, namely, it can be expressed as the solvability of a linear operator inequality.

Lemma 1

The LTSI system
\Sigma
is impedance passive if and only if there exists
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{Z})
,
\mathcal{Q}\geq 0
, such that

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}^{\ast}\mathcal{Q},\qquad\langle\mathcal{A}z,\mathcal{Q}z\rangle+\langle\mathcal{Q}z,\mathcal{A}z\rangle\leq 0

(20)

for all
z\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof:

See

[
16
, Lemma 7.5.4]
. ∎

Just like (internal) relaxation, impedance passivity of LTSI systems can be verified pointwise. This is shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 3

The LTSI system
\Sigma
is impedance passive if and only if there exist
\hat{Q}_{\omega}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
\hat{Q}_{\omega}\geq 0
, such that

\hat{C}_{\omega}=\hat{B}_{\omega}^{\ast}\hat{Q}_{\omega},,\qquad\hat{A}_{\omega}^{\ast}\hat{Q}_{\omega}+\hat{Q}_{\omega}\hat{A}_{\omega}\leq 0,

(21)

for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, and
\sup_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\lVert\hat{Q}_{\omega}\rVert<\infty
.

Proof:

This proof is similar to the proof of
[
17
, Theorem 2]
. The idea is that the condition
\sup_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\lVert\hat{Q}_{\omega}\rVert<\infty
is equivalent to the boundedness of the multiplication operator with symbol
\hat{Q}
, which we denote by
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{Z})
. We start by proving sufficiency. Since
\hat{Q}_{\omega}\geq 0
and (
21
) hold for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, it follows that
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\geq 0
and

\hat{\mathcal{C}}=\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{\ast}\hat{\mathcal{Q}},\qquad\langle\hat{\mathcal{A}}\hat{z},\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{z}\rangle+\langle\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{z},\hat{\mathcal{A}}\hat{z}\rangle\leq 0

(22)

for all
\hat{z}\in\mathcal{D}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})
. Recall that the Fourier transform is unitary. This implies that
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{F}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{Z})
, satisfies
\mathcal{Q}\geq 0
and the conditions of Lemma
1
, hence
\Sigma
is impedance passive.

For the proof of necessity, suppose that
\Sigma
is impedance passive.
Since the dynamics of
\Sigma
are translation-invariant, the storage is translation-invariant, i.e., there exists translation-invariant

\mathcal{Q}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{Z}),\mathcal{Q}\geq 0,
that satisfies the conditions of Lemma
1
.
This implies that
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}=\mathcal{F}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{Z})
is a multiplication operator that satisfies
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\geq 0
and (
22
). Finally, the symbol
\hat{Q}
of
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}
is such that
\sup_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\lVert\hat{Q}_{\omega}\rVert<\infty
because
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{Z})
,
\hat{Q}_{\omega}\geq 0
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
because
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\geq 0
, and (
21
) holds for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
because (
22
) holds.
∎

In
[
6
]
, it is shown that LTI relaxation systems are passive with a unique
1
1

1

assuming minimality of the state-space realization
compatible storage function that can be determined from their input-output behaviour. Deriving the analogue of this result for LTSI relaxation systems presents some minor technical challenges, see Remark
3
. We circumvent these by instead considering LTSI systems that are
internally
of relaxation type. In particular, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 4

If the LTSI system
\Sigma
is internally of relaxation type, then it is impedance passive with storage
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by
\mathcal{S}(z)=\lVert z\rVert^{2}
. Furthermore,

\mathcal{S}(z(0))=\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle u(-t),y(t)\rangle\,\textrm{d}t,

(23)

where
z:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{Z}
and
y:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{Y}
are the state and output trajectories corresponding to the input trajectory
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{U}
such that
u(t)=0
for
t\geq 0
.

Proof:

Since
\Sigma
is internally of relaxation type, we have that
\mathcal{A}\leq 0
and
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{C}^{\ast}
. Let
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{Z})
be the identity operator and note that
\mathcal{Q}\geq 0
and
\mathcal{S}(z)=\langle\mathcal{Q}z,z\rangle
. Furthermore, (
20
) holds for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, hence
\Sigma
is impedance passive due to Lemma
1
. Now, consider an input trajectory
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{U}
such that
u(t)=0
for
t\geq 0
. Let
z:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{Z}
and
y:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{Y}
be the resulting state and output trajectories. Consider the Fourier transforms
\hat{u}
,
\hat{z}
and
\hat{y}
of
u
,
z
and
y
, respectively. Note that
\hat{z}_{\omega}
and
\hat{y}_{\omega}
are the state and output trajectories of
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
corresponding to the input trajectory
\hat{u}_{\omega}
, which is such that
\hat{u}_{\omega}(t)=0
for
t\geq 0
. Due to Theorem
2
,
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
is internally of relaxation type for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and, thus,
\hat{A}_{\omega}\leq 0
and
\hat{B}_{\omega}=\hat{C}_{\omega}^{\ast}
. As shown in
[
6
, Theorem 9]
, see also
[

\lVert\hat{z}_{\omega}(0)\rVert^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle\hat{u}_{\omega}(-t),\hat{y}_{\omega}(t)\rangle\,\textrm{d}t

(24)

Since the Fourier transform is unitary, it follows that

\lVert z(0)\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{Z}}=\lVert\hat{z}(0)\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{Z}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\lVert\hat{z}_{\omega}(0)\rVert^{2}\,\textrm{d}\omega

(25)

and, similarly,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle u(-t),y(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}\,\textrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle\hat{u}_{\omega}(-t),\hat{y}_{\omega}(t)\rangle\,\textrm{d}t\textrm{d}\omega

where we have used Fubini’s theorem to interchange the order of integration. The right-hand sides of (

∎

The right-hand side of (
23
) can be seen as the inner product between past input and future output. In fact, it is the quadratic functional obtained from the Hankel operator of the system, i.e., the operator that maps the past input to the future output. We formalize this in the next section. Before we do that, we illustrate the results of this section with the diffusion equation and then conclude with a couple of remarks.

Example 2

In Example
1
, we saw that the diffusion equation (
2
) is an LTSI system that is internally of relaxation type. The state and output of this system are equal, hence, due to Theorem
4
, it is impedance passive with the well-known energy storage
E(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}y(t,x)^{2}\,\textrm{d}x
of the diffusion equation. In particular, (
23
) reduces to

\int_{\mathbb{R}}y(0,x)^{2}\,\textrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(-t,x)y(t,x)\,\textrm{d}x\textrm{d}t,

(27)

which, due to Plancherel’s theorem, is equivalent to

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert\hat{y}(0,\omega)\rvert^{2}\,\textrm{d}\omega=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{u}(-t,\omega)\hat{y}(t,\omega)^{\ast}\,\textrm{d}\omega\textrm{d}t,

(28)

Note that the future output
y(t,x)
,
t\geq 0
, is the solution of the diffusion equation (
2
) for
u(t,x)=0
,
t\geq 0
, and initial condition
y(0,x)
. Therefore, in the frequency domain, we have that
\hat{y}(t,\omega)=\hat{g}(t,\omega)\hat{y}(0,\omega)
, where

\displaystyle\hat{y}(0,\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\hat{g}(-t,\omega)\hat{u}(t,\omega)\,\textrm{d}t.

(29)

Multiplying the latter by

\hat{y}(0,\omega)^{\ast}
and rearranging yields

\lvert\hat{y}(0,\omega)\rvert^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{u}(-t,\omega)\hat{y}(t,\omega)^{\ast}\,\textrm{d}t,

(30)

where we used the fact that
\hat{g}(t,\omega)
is real and, thus,

\hat{y}(t,\omega)^{\ast}=\hat{g}(t,\omega)\hat{y}(0,\omega)^{\ast}.

(31)

Integrating both sides of (
30
) yields (
28
), hence the energy storage of the diffusion equation can be determined
from
the past input, which confirms the result of Theorem
4
.

Remark 3

LTSI relaxation systems are not necessarily impedance passive. To see this, suppose that the LTSI system
\Sigma
is of relaxation type, hence, due to Theorem
1
, the LTI system
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
is of relaxation type and, thus, passive for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. This implies that there exist
\hat{Q}_{\omega}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
\hat{Q}_{\omega}\geq 0
,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, such that (
21
) holds, but it does not imply that
\sup_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}\lVert\hat{Q}_{\omega}\rVert<\infty
. It is argued in
[
17
]
that cases where such boundedness conditions are violated are artificial mathematical constructs rather than real physical examples. This is because at large frequencies, the dominant mechanism in physical systems is dissipation, i.e., the LTI system
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
becomes “more stable” as
\omega\to\infty
.

Remark 4

In some references
[
19
,
20
]
, the definition of impedance passivity requires the storage to be strictly positive rather than merely nonnegative. In fact, it is sometimes assumed
[
21
]
that the storage is the squared norm of the state. These definitions coincide with the definition in this paper if we further impose that
\mathcal{Q}>0
such that
\lVert z\rVert_{\mathcal{Q}}=\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{Q}z,z\rangle}
defines a norm on the state space
\mathcal{Z}
. In any case, Theorem
4
shows that LTSI systems that are internally of relaxation type are impedance passive even under these modified definitions.

VI
The Hankel operator and relaxation

In this subsection, we show that relaxation can be characterized via the Hankel operator. In particular, we show that an exponentially stable LTSI system is of relaxation type if and only if its Hankel operator is self-adjoint and nonnegative. Consequently, the Hankel operator of an LTSI relaxation system is cyclically monotone and, thus, the gradient of a closed convex functional, which coincides with the (impedance) passive storage of Theorem
4
.

The Hankel operator of the LTSI system
\Sigma
is the linear operator
\mathcal{H}:\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{H})\to L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{Y})
given by

(\mathcal{H}v)(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\mathcal{G}(t+\tau)v(\tau)\,\textrm{d}\tau,

(32)

where
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{H})\subset L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U})
. The Hankel operator maps past inputs to future outputs in the sense that the input given by
u(t)=v(-t)
for
t\leq 0
and
u(t)=0
for all
t>0
, results in the output given by
y(t)=(\mathcal{H}v)(t)
for all
t\geq 0
. The domain of
\mathcal{H}
is nontrivial only if the system is stable. Here, we assume that
\Sigma
is
exponentially stable
, i.e., there exists
M,\alpha>0
such that
\lVert\exp(\mathcal{A}t)\rVert\leq Me^{-\alpha t}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, see
[
16
, Section 4.1]
for details. Then, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 5

If the LTSI system
\Sigma
is exponentially stable, then the Hankel operator
\mathcal{H}
extends to a bounded linear operator from
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U})
to
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{Y})
.

Proof:

Note that
\exp(\mathcal{A}(t+\tau))=\exp(\mathcal{A}t)\exp(\mathcal{A}\tau)
by definition of a semigroup, hence

(\mathcal{H}v)(t)=\mathcal{C}\exp(\mathcal{A}t)\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\exp(\mathcal{A}\tau)\mathcal{B}v(\tau)\,\textrm{d}\tau.

(33)

Let the linear operator
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}:L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U})\to\mathcal{Z}
be given by

(\mathcal{B}_{\infty}v)(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\exp(\mathcal{A}\tau)\mathcal{B}v(\tau)\,\textrm{d}\tau

(34)

and the linear operator
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}:\mathcal{Z}\to L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{Y})
by

(\mathcal{C}_{\infty}z)(t)=\mathcal{C}\exp(\mathcal{A}t)z

(35)

The operators
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
are known as the extended controllability and observability maps, respectively, see
[
16
, Section 6.4]
for details. They are well-defined and bounded if
\Sigma
is exponentially stable, hence
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{C}_{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{\infty}
is a bounded linear operator from
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U})
to
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{Y})
.
∎

As shown in
[
3
]
, a stable
LTI system is of relaxation type if and only if its Hankel operator is self-adjoint and nonnegative (hence cyclically monotone). Here, we extend this result to exponentially stable LTSI systems.

Theorem 6

Suppose that the LTSI system
\Sigma
is exponentially stable. Then,
\Sigma
is of relaxation type if and only if the Hankel operator
\mathcal{H}\in\mathscr{B}(L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U}))
satisfies
\mathcal{H}\geq 0
.

Proof:

Note that
\mathcal{H}\in\mathscr{B}(L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U}))
due to Theorem
5
and the assumption that
\Sigma
is exponentially stable. We first prove necessity. Suppose that
\Sigma
is of relaxation type. It follows that
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
is of relaxation type for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
due to Theorem
1
. Furthermore, due to
[
17
, Theorem 1]
,
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
is stable for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Consequently, due to
[
3
, Theorem 5]
, the Hankel operator
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}\in\mathscr{B}(L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{m}))
of
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
satisfies
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}\geq 0
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Note that

\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}\hat{v}_{\omega},\hat{w}_{\omega}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\langle\hat{g}_{\omega}(t+\tau)\hat{v}_{\omega}(\tau),\hat{w}_{\omega}(t)\rangle\,\textrm{d}\tau\textrm{d}t

(36)

for all
\hat{v}_{\omega},\hat{w}_{\omega}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{m})
, and, similarly,

\langle\mathcal{H}v,w\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\langle\mathcal{G}(t+\tau)v(\tau),w(t)\rangle\,\textrm{d}\tau\textrm{d}t.

(37)

for all
v,w\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U})
. Due to Plancherel’s theorem,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{G}(t+\tau)v(\tau),w(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{\mathcal{G}}(t+\tau)\hat{v}(\tau),\hat{w}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\langle\hat{g}_{\omega}(t+\tau)\hat{v}_{\omega}(\tau),\hat{w}_{\omega}(t)\rangle\,\textrm{d}\omega

for all
v,w\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U})
with (spatial) Fourier transforms
\hat{v},\hat{w}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U})
.
Substituting the latter in (
37
) and changing the order of integration yields

\langle\mathcal{H}v,w\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}\hat{v}_{\omega},\hat{w}_{\omega}\rangle\,\textrm{d}\omega

(38)

where
\hat{v}_{\omega},\hat{w}_{\omega}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{m})
for almost all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{H}\geq 0
because
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}\geq 0
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
.

Next, we prove sufficiency. Suppose that
\mathcal{H}\geq 0
. Consider arbitrary
\bar{v},\bar{w}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{m})
. Let
\hat{v}_{\omega}=\bar{v}
and
\hat{w}_{\omega}=\bar{w}
for all
\omega\in\Omega
, and
\hat{v}_{\omega}=\hat{w}_{\omega}=0
for all
\omega\notin\Omega
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is an arbitrary compact subset. Then,
v,w\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U})
and

\int_{\Omega}\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}\bar{v},\bar{w}\rangle-\langle\bar{v},\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}\bar{w}\rangle\,\textrm{d}\omega=0

(39)

due to (
38
) and the assumption that
\mathcal{H}
is self-adjoint. The latter holds for all
\bar{v},\bar{w}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{m})
and compact
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
, hence
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}
is self-adjoint for almost all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Similarly,
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}
is nonnegative for almost all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Due to
[
3
, Theorem 5]
, this implies that
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
is of relaxation type and, thus,
\hat{g}_{\omega}
is completely monotone for almost all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Note that
\hat{g}_{\omega}(t)
and its time derivatives are continuous in
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
for each
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
because the symbols
\hat{A}
,
\hat{B}
and
\hat{C}
are assumed to be continuous. Therefore, by continuity,
\hat{g}_{\omega}
is completely monotone for
all

\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, hence
\Sigma
is of relaxation type due to Theorem
1
.
∎

As a consequence of Theorem
6
, the Hankel operator
\mathcal{H}
of an LTSI relaxation system
\Sigma
is (maximally) cyclically monotone and, thus, the gradient of a closed convex functional
[
22
,
23
]
. In fact,
\mathcal{H}
is the functional derivative of the convex quadratic functional
\mathfrak{H}:L_{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{U})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by

\mathfrak{H}(v)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{H}v,v\rangle,

(40)

see
[
3
, Lemma 1]
. Theorem
4
shows that
\mathfrak{H}
is intimately related to the (impedance passive) storage of a relaxation system. In particular, we can write (
23
) as

\mathcal{S}(z(0))=\langle\mathcal{H}\bar{u},\bar{u}\rangle=2\mathfrak{H}(\bar{u}),

(41)

where
\bar{u}(t)=u(-t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The latter implies that the energy stored in a relaxation system is completely determined from the past input, i.e., its memory. In the LTI case
[
3
]
, the functional
\mathfrak{H}
defines a so-called
intrinsic storage
, which is used to show passivity of relaxation systems from a purely input-output perspective. An analogous derivation is also possible in the LTSI case. As always, the main idea is that the LTSI system
\Sigma
inherits the properties of the corresponding family of LTI systems
\hat{\Sigma}_{\omega}
, and vice versa.

VII
Conclusion

We extended the classical concept of relaxation from LTI to a class of LTSI systems. We showed that LTSI relaxation systems enjoy the same properties as LTI relaxation systems, i.e., they have completely monotone impulse responses, they are impedance passive, and their Hankel operators are cyclically monotone. As in the LTI case, LTSI relaxation systems reconcile the state-space concept of energy storage with the input-output concept of memory functional. Throughout this paper, we made use of the properties of the Fourier transform with respect to translation invariance to decouple the infinite-dimensional LTSI system into an infinite family of finite-dimensional LTI systems, so that properties of one can be characterized as properties of the other.

We identify several topics for future research. It would be interesting to investigate whether the apparent discrepancy between internal and external relaxation can be resolved by imposing spatial properties on the impulse response, or, alternatively, by working in a Hardy space rather than
L_{2}
. It would also be interesting to consider LTSI relaxation from a purely geometric viewpoint
[
5
]
. In any case, we intend to extend the theory developed in this paper to nonlinear systems, in the same spirit as
[
4
]
. In particular, we conjecture that spatio-temporal input-output operators that are
myopic
in space
[
24
]
with completely monotone
fading memory

[
7
]
can be universally approximated by an LTSI relaxation system composed with an absolutely monotone readout.
