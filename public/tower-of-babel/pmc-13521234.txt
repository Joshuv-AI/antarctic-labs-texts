Body weight trajectories on behavioural and physiological indicators of broiler breeder females
Authors: Thiago L Noetzold, Bryan Fancher, Marcelo Silva, Alan Thomson, Leluo Guan, Martin J Zuidhof
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this study was to evaluate effects of relaxed feed restriction, achieved by modifying growth trajectories, on feeding behaviour and welfare indicators of broiler breeder females. Two simultaneous experiments (Exp 1 and Exp 2) used a precision feeding system and target body weight (BW) trajectories derived from a 3-phase Gompertz model fitted to the Ross 308 recommended target. Exp 1 used a 6 × 2 factorial design, with six continuous coefficients for age at maximum pubertal growth: I2 = 15, 17, 19, 21 (Ross 308 standard), 22, and 23 wk; and two discrete early growth shift treatments (EG). In EG0, prepubertal (g1 = 1.77 kg) and pubertal (g2 = 1.98 kg) gains followed the Ross 308 standard target. In EG20, 20% of g2 was shifted to g1. Exp 2 used a 6 × 2 factorial design with six continuous EG shifts: -10, 0 (Ross 308 standard), 10, 20, 30, and 40% of g2 shifted to g1; and two pubertal growth rates: standard b2, or 50% faster (b2 150%). Average daily station visits, meals, meal size, and successful visits were summarized for rearing (3 to 22 wk) and laying (23 to 60 wk). Heterophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (H/L) was measured in five birds per treatment at 16, 20, 22, 24, 26, 28, 30, and 32 wk, and plasma corticosterone (CORT) at 16, 20, 24, and 28 wk. During rearing, feed-seeking behaviour, measured as station and successful visits, decreased as BW targets increased in both experiments. In Exp 1, H/L decreased by 0.05 for each week I2 was advanced, and CORT at 16 wk decreased by 149.6 pg/mL per week of advancement. In Experiment 2, H/L decreased by 0.007 per percentage-point increase in EG, and CORT from 16 to 28 wk decreased by 9.70 pg/mL per percentage-point increase. Relaxed feed restriction during rearing therefore reduced feed-seeking behaviour and improved physiological stress indicators.Keywords: Feed restriction, Welfare, Multiphasic growth, Precision livestock feeding, Metabolites

Introduction

Parent stock of meat-type chickens (broiler breeders) are feed restricted during their lifetime to control excessive growth. The degree of feed restriction is greatest during the rearing phase, and to a lesser extent during lay. Reproductive performance and economic return are optimized in breeders managed at a certain degree of feed restriction compared to ad libitum fed breeders (Heck et al., 2004; Renema and Robinson, 2004). Over decades, genetic potential of meat-type chickens has increased considerably (Zuidhof et al., 2014). Evidence suggests that the degree of feed restriction in broiler breeders has increased over time due to this genetic potential (Carney et al., 2022) despite small changes in recommended target BW over the years. Studies have shown that feed intake during the rearing period of breeders is approximately 20 to 30% of their potential (de Jong et al., 2002; Carney et al., 2022). This feed intake evaluation is usually overestimated, because a direct comparison between ad libitum and restricted fed birds at the same age does not account for BW differences. Utilizing methods that account for BW and dietary energy content showed a 50% to 60% level of feed restriction in breeders (Arrazola, A., 2018; van Emous and Mens, 2022).

In spite of the justification to feed restrict broiler breeders to optimize their reproductive performance, feed restriction during rearing is known to have negative impact in behavior and welfare of broiler breeders. Welfare concerns related to the feeding management and genetic potential of broiler breeders have been raised in the past decades (Mench, 2002; de Jong and Guémené, 2011; Korver, 2023), which include increasing stereotypical behaviours related to hunger (frustration, object pecking; de Jong et al., 2003; de Jong and Guémené, 2011), and aggressive behaviour (feather pecking, licking, and bird pecking; Jones et al., 2004; van Emous et al., 2024). The etiology of the above behaviours is not strictly related to feed restriction, but its degree might increase depending on the feed restriction level. It is important to consider what hunger behaviour represents for the chicken meat industry. Appetite is the motivational drive to obtain food or nutrients and considered to be manifested through hunger (Castonguay et al., 1983; Legg, 2012). The opposite signal to hunger is satiety, where hunger is experienced in the absence of satiety cues, and vice versa (Te Pas et al., 2020). While excessive feed restriction (and consequently hunger) is detrimental, some degree of appetite is desirable.

Since the modern welfare term and five freedoms were first described by Brambell (1965), many different definitions of animal welfare have emerged. Welfare is referred to as the state of an individual (physical and mental) and its interaction with the environment (Broom, 1991, 2022; Reimert et al., 2023). The individual bird state and its interaction with the environment can be assessed, and measurements have been refined in the last 40 years through observational (behavioural) and biological parameters. As mentioned, feeding behaviour is an important observational welfare indicator in broiler breeders. Among the biological parameters, heterophil to lymphocyte (H/L) ratio has been used previously to indicate chronic stress in chickens (Gross and Siegel, 1983). Another common physiological indicator of stress in poultry is circulating levels of corticosterone (CORT). The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA) is activated in response to a stressor, where adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) is released by the pituitary, acting at the adrenal cells and releasing CORT (Beuving and Vonder, 1986; Carsia, 2015).

Recent studies have shown that various feeding strategies might alleviate hunger indicators (observational and biological) of broiler breeders such as increased BW target (Afrouziyeh et al., 2021), supplemental alfalfa straw (van Emous, 2024) and diluted diets and feed frequency (van Emous et al., 2024). Additionally, relaxed feed restriction (increased feed allowance) has shown to increase reproductive performance and economic returns (Afrouziyeh et al., 2021, 2023; Noetzold et al., 2025). Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of relaxed feed restriction through manipulation of growth trajectories on feeding behaviour and stress indicators of broiler breeder females. We hypothesized that in Experiment 1 (Exp 1), an earlier time of maximum pubertal growth would decrease feed seeking behaviours, H/L ratio and CORT levels. Additionally, we hypothesized that in Experiment 2 (Exp 2), shifting the gain from the pubertal to prepubertal phase would decrease feed seeking behaviours, H/L ratio and CORT levels. The current study utilized a precision feeding (PF) system to feed free-run broiler breeders. The capabilities and specifics of the PF system have been tested previously (Zuidhof, 2018; Afrouziyeh et al., 2021; Zukiwsky et al., 2021, 2023). Because of the individual BW control of each individual bird in the trial, the PF system is a unique tool to control BW and decrease variability and BW effect between the BW treatments given to the broiler breeders, as shown previously by Noetzold et al. (2024, 2025, 2026).

Materials and methods

Two experiments (Exp 1 and Exp 2) were carried out simultaneously using a precision feeding system. Detailed experimental results were described previously by Noetzold et al. (2024, 2025) for Exp 1 and Noetzold et al. (2026) for Exp 2. All the animal procedures of this study were approved by the University of Alberta Animal Care and Use Committee for Livestock and followed the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines (CCAC, 2009).

Development of growth trajectories

To develop several growth trajectories systematically for Exp 1 and 2, coefficients were estimated from the recommended Ross 308 BW target (Aviagen, 2021a) using a triphasic Gompertz model:

The model includes 3 phases, where the terms prepubertal (phase 1), pubertal (phase 2), and postpubertal (phase 3) were be used to refer to the growth phases. In the model, BWt was BW (kg) at a given age (t; wk); gi was the total amount of gain (kg) accrued in phase i; bi was a rate coefficient (wk−1) for phase i; Ii was the inflection point (age in weeks) where the gain reached its maximum in phase i; and εt was the residual error at age t. The combination of the three main parameters included in the model (g, b, I) into three distinct phases (i=3) at a given age (t) represent the growth trajectory. The growth trajectories are displayed by Noetzold et al. (2024, 2026) and described in the following sections.

Experimental design

Experiment 1

The detailed experimental design was described elsewhere (Noetzold et al., 2024). Briefly, the experiment consisted of a 6 × 2 factorial design, with six ages (continuous coefficients) of maximum pubertal growth (I2) and two discrete levels of early growth shift (EG). The I2 coefficients were 15, 17, 19, 21, 22, and 23 wk. The coefficient of 21 wk was derived from the breeder recommended Ross 308 target BW. In the EG factor, treatments were: Standard (EG0), where coefficients for gain in the prepubertal phase (g1; 1.77 kg) and in the pubertal phase (g2; 1.98 kg) were estimated from the standard Ross 308 recommended BW target; and EG 20% shifted (EG20), where 20% of the gain from the pubertal phase (g2) was shifted to the prepubertal phase (g1). Treatment combinations of the two factors (totalling 12 BW growth trajectories) are shown in Fig. 1A. Each growth trajectory was applied to the individual bird in free-run pens using a precision feeding (PF) system. Thus, each bird was the experimental unit.

Designed growth trajectories for the treatments of Experiment 1 (A), where early growth (EG) was Standard, estimated from the Ross 308 breeder-recommended standard BW gain (EG0; solid lines); or 20% shifted (EG20), where 20% gain (kg) of the pubertal phase (g2) was shifted to prepubertal phase (g1; dotted lines); time of maximum pubertal growth (I2) was advanced (15, 17, and 19 wk) or delayed (22 and 23 wk) based on the Ross 308 standard inflection point (21 wk). In Experiment 2 (B), pubertal growth rate (b2) had two levels: Standard, where b2 was the estimated standard b2 (100%) of the Ross 308 recommended BW target; and 50% faster, with b2 increased by 50 percent (b2 150%). Six early growth (EG) levels were created by shifting gain (kg) from the pubertal phase (g2) to the prepubertal phase (g1): Standard (0%), estimated from the Ross 308 breeder-recommended g1 and g2 gain; delayed (EG −10%), or advanced (EG 10, 20, 30, and 40%).

Experiment 2

The detailed experimental design was described previously (Noetzold et al., 2026). Briefly, the trial utilized a 6 × 2 factorial arrangement, with six levels of EG and two levels of pubertal growth rate (b2), totalling twelve growth trajectories. The six continuous EG shifts extended in a 10% range (approximately 0.2 kg) from -10%, 0% (Ross 308 standard), 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40%; For each EG treatment, the given gain (kg) as percentage from pubertal phase (g2) was shifted to the prepubertal phase (g1), and 0% represented the breeder recommended target BW for g1 and g2 (no shift). The two b2 levels were: Standard, where b2 was the estimated standard pubertal growth rate from the breeder recommended pubertal phase (Standard b2); and 50% faster, where b2 was increased 50 percent (b2 150%). Treatment combinations of the two factors totalled 12 BW growth trajectories and are presented in Fig. 1B. Each growth trajectory was applied to the individual bird using a precision feeding (PF) system; therefore, each bird was the experimental unit.

Animals and husbandry

A detailed description of the procedures was shown before in Exp 1 (Noetzold et al., 2024; 2025) and Exp 2 (Noetzold et al., 2026), where same birds from the cited studies were utilized in the current study. Briefly, 576 Ross 308 one-day-old breeder pullets were randomly assigned to one of the twenty-four growth trajectories available (12 per experiment), totalling twenty-four pullets per growth trajectory at placement. Pullets were identified at hatch with a necktag and randomly divided into three floor pens (4.5 × 5.4 m). At 7 d of age, pullets equipped with a radio frequency identification (RFID) wing tag, in order to be fed individually with a PF system. The detailed functionality of the precision feeding system has been fully described elsewhere (Zuidhof, 2018; Noetzold et al., 2024). In brief, all birds were trained to use the PF system from placement (0 d) to 13 d. After 13 d of age, all birds were individually fed using the PF system. Each bird was fed according to its individual growth trajectory. Thus, the PF system identified and fed birds based on their assigned target BW.

Each pen had multiple feeding stations (approximately one station for every 50 birds) and each feeding station was equipped with two compartments (stages). In stage 1, the decision of feeding or not feeding the bird was made based on BW and compared to the individual target BW of the bird. Pullets with greater BW than its target BW were gently ejected from stage 1 by a panel that ran across the compartment. Birds that weighed less than the target BW were allowed to proceed to the second compartment (stage 2). In stage 2, birds were allowed to consume feed for 150 s with 50 g of feed presented in the feeder. The 50 g was enough to assure excess feed during the 150 s. Therefore, the PF system provided the birds with multiple meals each day when their real-time BW was less than the treatment-specific target BW, which was updated in real-time.

All birds from all treatments in both experiments were fed the same commercial diets throughout the study. Feed was formulated based on recommendations of the primary breeder (Aviagen, 2021b) in a three-phase feeding program on each production phase (rearing and laying). Detailed dietary nutrient composition for diets provided from 0 to 60 wk of age were reported elsewhere (Noetzold et al., 2024, 2025). Water was provided ad libitum with nipple type drinkers.

Environmental temperatures followed breeder recommendations (Aviagen, 2018, 2019). Facility photoschedule program was fully described previously (Noetzold et al., 2024, 2026). Briefly, the photoschedule was 23 Light (L): 1 Dark (D) for the first 2 days (50 lx), 19L:5D at day 3 (50 lx), 16L:8D at day 4 (50 lx), with 2 hours of D incremented each day until day 6 (30 lx), 11L:13D at day 7 (20 lx), 10L:14D at day 8 (20 lx), 9L:15D at day 9 (20 lx), and then maintained at 8L:16D (10 lx) from 10 d until 146 d of age. Photostimulation began at 147 d of age (11L:13D; 25 lx), increased one hour per week until 13L:11D (40 lx), and then maintained until the end of the study (60 wk of age). Feeding stations were equipped with low intensity (5 lx) green lights to permit feed consumption during day and night (24 h). Green lights did not stimulate hypothalamic photoreceptors (Hanlon et al., 2023), and thus, did not impact responsiveness to the house-level lighting program.

Data collection

Feeding behaviour

All birds were individually weighed daily during the training period (hatch to 13 d of age). From 14 d onward, birds were fed individually using the PF system. The system recorded individual feed intake and BW at every visit to the feeding station, and birds visited the stations multiple times throughout the day. From the PF system database, individual bird visit frequency (number of visits per day), meal frequency (number of meals per day), and meal size (average feed intake at each meal of 150 s) were calculated. Data was pooled as daily averages in five periods during rearing (3 to 6, 7 to 10, 11 to 14, 15 to 18, and 19 to 22 wk of age) and eight periods during the laying period (23 to 25, 26 to 30, 31 to 35, 36 to 40, 41 to 45, 46 to 50, 51 to 55, and 56 to 60). Successful visits to the feeding stations (visits followed by a meal) were calculated as the daily meals divided by daily visits and the result multiplied by 100 to obtain the percentage.

Blood collection and differential white blood cell count

Five birds from each treatment group on both experiments (60 birds per experiment) were randomly selected for serial blood collection at 16, 20, 22, 24, 26, 28, 30, and 32 wk of age. Blood was collected (random order) from the brachial vein 3 h after start of daily photoperiod within a 1.5 h period. Because all birds were fed using the PF stations and a substantial daily handling by barn staff, blood collection occurred quickly as birds were removed from pens to avoid waiting time and decrease stress. A 4 mL vacutainer tube (with sodium heparin) was used to collect blood from each bird, with approximately 3 mL collected at each collection time (amount necessary for further plasma recovery and analysis). Immediately after collection, blood from vacutainer tubes was used for blood smears and were prepared on glass slides and dried at room temperature. After dried, blood slides were stained with Wright-Giemsa stain following recommendations by the manufacturer (Protocol Hema 3 Fixative, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Kalamazoo, MI). A minimum of 100 leukocytes differential count was done for all blood samples, and included the following cells: heterophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, basophiles, and eosinophils. Categories of heterophils (typical, classic, and variant) and lymphocytes (small and medium) were not separated by group as described by Cotter et al. (2015). The heterophil to lymphocyte (H/L) ratio was calculated per bird at each age. After blood smears preparation, the vacutainer tubes were immediately centrifuged at 1,244 × g at 4°C for 15 min to recover plasma for corticosterone analysis. Plasma was transferred into two separate 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and then stored at -20°C until further analysis.

Corticosterone assay

For the CORT hormone assay, three plasma samples from each treatment group (32 birds per experiment) were randomly selected out of the five birds available. Samples were selected at four different ages (16, 20, 24, and 28 wk of age) totalling 36 samples in each experiment. Two months after the end of the trial (60 wk of age), samples were submitted to the liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method.

Sample preparation. Plasma samples, standards and internal standards were thawed on ice. All samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. 150 microliters of standards, QC samples and plasma samples mixed with 20 µL internal standards were pipetted into 2 mL Eppendorf tubes, 150 µL of NaCl solution (9 mg/mL) was then added to each tube and vortexed for 30 s. Toluene was then added (1000 µL) to each tube for extraction. The samples were shaken at 1,200 rpm for 30 min. After that, samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min, and 800 µL of supernatants were transferred into high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) vials and dried under nitrogen purge until completely dry. To the dried tubes, 100 µL of derivatization solution (1.5 mol/L hydroxylamine in HPLC grade water) was added and vortexed to mix thoroughly. All the tubes were then incubated at 60°C for 1 hour before they were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC/MS/MS Analysis. Analysis was carried out on Waters UPLC system coupled to an AB Sciex QTRAP® mass spectrometer equipped with a TurboIon spray. LC separation was carried out on a Waters Acquity CSH C18 column with CSH C18 guard column with flow rate of 300 µL/min. Mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (mobile phase B). The autosampler was cooled at 10°C. Column temperature was set at 40°C. Injection volume was 20 µL.

MS/MS Detection. The QTRAP was operated in positive mode for the analysis. The parameters of the source using nitrogen as a curtain gas were as follows: capillary ion spray voltage +5500 V in positive mode; temperature 550°C; collision gas “Medium”, curtain gas 40 psi, ion source gas 1 at 60 psi, and ion source gas 2 at 60 psi. The entrance potential was set to 10 V, and collision cell exit potentials were optimized for each analyte. The analytes were measured in the multiple reaction monitoring mode. Declustering potential and collision energy were optimized for each analyte. Data were acquired and processed using Analyst software, V.1.6.3.

Statistical analysis

Data from each trial were submitted to analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and ANOVA using the MIXED procedure of SAS (Version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Tukey’s test was used to separate means and differences were reported when P ≤ 0.05. Trends were reported when 0.05 < P ≤ 0.10 and the magnitude of the observed was considered biologically meaningful. Feeding behaviour variables were analyzed separately by production period (rearing and laying). The results were presented as means ± SEM.

Experiment 1

Analysis of covariance was conducted on feeding behaviour variables (visits, meals, meal size, and successful visits), H/L ratio, and CORT with EG and age as discrete sources of variation and I2 as a continuous linear regressor, where age or period was included in the model as a random effect with individual bird as the subject to account for within-bird variation. Additionally, feeding behaviour variables (visits, meals, meal size, and successful visits) and H/L ratio were analyzed in a three-way ANOVA, where EG, I2, and age were considered as discrete sources of variation. Pen was included in the model as a random source of variation and the individual bird as the subject to account for within-bird variation. Three-way interactions were not significant for any of the variables studied. Additionally, two-way interactions were not significant in the laying period for feeding behaviours and are not reported. In the rearing phase, pairwise comparisons were used to estimate significant differences for visits, meals, and successful visits for two-way interactions (EG × period and I2 × period) by the PDIFF option of the LSMEANS statement (reported as different when P ≤ 0.05).

Experiment 2

Feeding behaviour variables (visits, meals, meal size, and successful visits), H/L ratio, and CORT were submitted to ANCOVA with b2 and age as discrete sources of variation and EG as a continuous linear regressor, where age was included in the model as a random effect with individual bird as the subject to account for within-bird variation. Also, feeding behaviour variables (visits, meals, meal size, and successful visits) and H/L ratio were analyzed in a three-way ANOVA, where b2, EG, and age were considered as discrete sources of variation. Pen was included in the model as a random source of variation and the individual bird as the subject to account for within-bird variation. Three-way interactions were not significant for any of the variables studied. Additionally, two-way interactions were not significant in the laying period for feeding behaviours and are not reported. In the rearing phase, pairwise comparisons were used to estimate significant differences for visits, meals, and successful visits for two-way interactions (b2 × period and EG × period) by the PDIFF option of the LSMEANS statement (reported as different when P ≤ 0.05).

Results

Experiment 1

Feeding behaviour

Feeding behaviour during the rearing period is presented in Table 1 and Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4. A tendency (P = 0.091) to a greater proportion of successful visits (percentage of visits that resulted in a meal) was observed in the EG20 treatment compared to EG0 (Table 1). This occurred mainly due to EG and period interaction effect from 3 to 10 wk of age (Fig. 4A). Average daily visits to the feeding stations decreased by 0.3/d, and percentage of successful visits increased by 1.4%, for every week of advanced I2 (Table 1). Interaction with period showed greater average daily visits during the 3 to 6 wk period for the EG0 treatment (Fig. 2A), and no interaction differences between I2 and period (Fig. 2B). Interaction between EG and I2 showed that daily meals increased by 0.2 for every week of advanced I2 in the EG0 treatment, but to a less degree (0.1) in the EG20 treatment (Table 1). Interaction between EG and period showed greater daily meals from 3 to 14 wk of age in the EG20 treatment when compared to the EG0 treatment (Fig. 3A), whereas interaction between I2 and period showed greater daily meals as I2 was advanced from 7 to 18 wk period (Fig. 3B). Interaction between I2 and period showed greater successful visits with advanced I2 from 11 to 18 wk of age (Fig. 4B). Additionally, number of daily station visits were lowest and successful visits were highest during the 19 to 22 wk period, whereas number of daily meals increased as birds aged, except from 15 to 18 wk of age, and meal size increased as birds aged (Table 1).

During the laying phase, daily visits were 1.2 less and percentage of successful visits was 9.9% greater in the EG0 compared to EG20 treatment (Table 2). Meal size increased by 0.3 g and percentage of successful visits decreased by 1.4% for every week of advanced I2 (Table 2). Discussion

Indicators of animal welfare can be observational (behaviour) or physiological. The current study evaluated feeding behaviour indicators and blood parameters (H/L ratio and plasma corticosterone). Feeding behaviour of broiler breeders is generally observed through hunger indicators, such as time spent eating and feed seeking motivation (Arrazola et al., 2020; van Emous, 2024; van Emous et al., 2024) but also stereotypical behaviours (e.g. object and feather pecking). Excessive hunger and abnormal pecking are considered a welfare problem (Mench, 2002). Automated feeding technologies can be a useful tool to quantify feeding behaviours in chickens, where hunger and satiety level can be estimated. The current trial evaluated feeding behaviour and physiological welfare indicators in broiler breeders. In the current study, a reduction in feed seeking motivation (lower number of station visits and higher successful visits) was observed with increased BW trajectories, advanced I2 (Exp 1) and increased EG (Exp 2), specifically during the rearing phase. This suggests that hunger was reduced in broiler breeders as their feed intake increased. Studies using a variety of feeding strategies have also showed improvements in different feeding behaviour indicators in broiler breeders (Savory and Lariviere, 2000; Hocking et al., 2001; Girard et al., 2017; Aranibar et al., 2020; van Emous et al., 2024). Breeder pullets fed twice a day had less sitting, object pecking, and aggressive behaviour than once a day feeding (van Emous et al., 2024). Dietary dilution of 20% using wheat straw pellets decreased feed seeking motivation of breeder pullets at 12 and 17 wk of age (van Emous et al., 2024), and dilution of 40% using soybean hulls also reduced feed seeking motivation during early rearing (Arrazola et al., 2020). Furthermore, a similar study to the current experiment (using a comparable experimental design and PF system), observed that station visits, number of meals, and feeding motivation index (visit:meal ratio, similar to the successful visits from the current trial) were consistent with lower feeding motivation in breeder pullets fed under more relaxed feed restriction BW trajectories (Afrouziyeh et al., 2021). Nonetheless, many authors have questioned that feeding programs would significantly decrease hunger indicators of broiler breeders, using strategies such as increased feed intake (by dietary dilution or increased feed allowance), feeding frequency (skip-a-day vs. daily feeding), and feeding system (Zuidhof et al., 1995; Morrissey et al., 2014; Girard et al., 2017; Zukiwsky et al., 2021). The authors cited above did not attribute it to specific reasons but suggested that some degree of hunger was still present, where the stereotypical behaviors (aggression, object pecking, and feather pecking) were not completely eliminated with the alternative feeding strategies. A hypothesis that might explain this is that the amount of nutrients consumed is still similar between the dietary treatments of the above studies. Thus, the requirements for the growth and development are still present, also mentioned in the literature as metabolic hunger (D’Eath et al., 2009). The current trial results suggests that feed seeking behaviour decreased as feed allowance increased by advancing I2 in Exp 1, and as EG was increased in Exp 2 (and consequently nutrient intake increased) allowing birds to achieve their target BW (Noetzold et al., 2024, 2026). The difference of feed seeking behaviour towards the PF stations in the current trial was not evaluated with direct biologically markers, such as the melanocortin system (Noetzold and Zuidhof, 2025) and ghrelin (Richards, 2003). However, observational differences are important from the animal welfare perspective, a concern that needs continued evaluation.

Increased heterophil to lymphocyte (H/L) ratio has been associated with prolonged stress in chickens (Gross and Siegel, 1983; Zuidhof et al., 1995; Shini et al., 2009; Weimer et al., 2018). In the current study, H/L ratio decreased from 16 to 28 wk of age as feed restriction was relaxed with advanced I2 (Exp 1) and increased EG (Exp 2). This suggests that increasing feed allowance during the prepubertal (EG) and peak of pubertal phase (I2) reduced chronic stress in broiler breeders. The mechanism behind the changes in H/L ratio was fully described elsewhere (Scanes, 2016), where prolonged stress increased heterophils and decreased lymphocytes because of corticosterone effects (Gross and Siegel, 1983; Shini et al., 2009) or stimulation of adrenal cortical cells (Davison and Flack, 1981). On the other hand, studies have proposed that H/L ratio can not alone be an indicator of stress (Cotter, 2015, 2021, 2022; Abraham et al., 2025). The authors explained that H/L ratio might vary according to other stressors (e.g. bacterial infections), and other parameters should be evaluated together with H/L ratio, such as total white blood cell counts (Cotter, 2015, 2021, 2022). However, total white blood cell counts were not performed in the current study.

Parallel to H/L ratio, plasma CORT concentration has been reported to increase with increased feed restriction (Hocking et al., 2001; de Jong et al., 2002; Yan et al., 2021). However, lack of correlation between plasma CORT levels and feed restriction, alternative feeding strategies, and glucocorticoid precursor administration (Aranibar et al., 2020; Afrouziyeh and Zuidhof, 2022; Abraham et al., 2025) have raised questions regarding reliability and interpretation of plasma CORT concentration in response to stress. First, plasma CORT concentration can rapidly increase due to a variety of stress factors (de Jong et al., 2002). Hence, blood sampling associated stress could influence CORT levels and interfere in the treatment effects. Second, diurnal variations of plasma CORT levels were widely reported in chickens (Johnson and van Tienhoven, 1981; de Jong et al., 2002; Arrazola et al., 2019; Aranibar et al., 2020). Nonetheless, to control for these factors, all birds were subjected to similar handling and temporal collection in the current study, and it was presumed that basal plasma CORT levels were similar across the treatments. In the current study, greater plasma CORT level in response to increased feed restriction was observed in Exp 2 from 16 to 28 wk of age, and only at 16 wk in Exp 1. The growth trajectories design and objectives were different between Exp 1 and 2. While changes in the time of pubertal growth peak (I2) were made in Exp 1, growth trajectories in Exp 2 were developed by shifting growth from the pubertal phase to the prepubertal phase (EG). Thus, it is hypothesized that greater feed restriction differences between growth trajectories in Exp 2 compared to Exp 1 during the plasma collection ages (16, 20, 24 and 28 wk of age) might also have influenced the results. Nonetheless, with exception of feed intake differences at 16 wk of age (Noetzold et al., 2026) no feed intake and feeding behaviour differences are observed from 20 to 28 wk of age in the Exp 2, which may have occurred because the feed intake in Exp 2 feed intake during the period after 16 wk of age did not differ, despite the difference in BW (Noetzold et al., 2026). Greater number of meals and successful visits were observed only in Exp 1 at 16 wk of age with advanced I2 (Fig. 3B and Fig. 4B) and also increased feed intake with advanced I2 (Noetzold et al., 2024), which might explain the differences in CORT levels at 16 wk of age in Exp 1. Comparable to the current Exp 1, van Emous et al. (2014) also observed lower CORT levels with increased growth trajectories only at 16 wk of age (independently analyzed at 11, 16, 21, and 26 wk of age). Additionally, several studies showed increased CORT levels with greater feed restriction in broiler breeders (Hocking et al., 1993, 1996, 2001; de Jong et al., 2002), while other studies did not (Aranibar et al., 2020; Afrouziyeh and Zuidhof, 2022). The CORT levels in the plasma can vary in less than one hour after the stressor (Wilson et al., 1984; de Jong et al., 2002) and is considered to respond to acute and chronic stress, but dependent on the stressor type and magnitude (Müller et al., 2011; Scanes, 2016), which might explain plasma CORT contradiction between literature and between Exp 1 and Exp 2. Thus, it is implied that CORT levels should be caution and evaluated with other variables.

A positive relationship between plasma CORT and H/L ratio has been reported previously (Gross and Siegel, 1983; Shini et al., 2008, 2009; Weimer et al., 2018; Tetel et al., 2022), and it was confirmed only in Exp 1 of current study (r = 0.20; P = 0.019). Other studies also did not find correlation between plasma corticosterone level and H/L ratio (de Jong et al., 2002; Abraham et al., 2025). The inconsistent relationship between CORT and H/L ratio in the literature occurs because H/L ratio is an indicator of stress over longer period (chronic stress; Gross and Siegel, 1983; Müller et al., 2011). Additionally, plasma CORT levels have been shown to return to low concentrations with recurring stress (Rich and Romero, 2005). Thus, in the current study, increased H/L ratio with greater feed restriction in both experiments might indicate a higher level of stress relative to the degree of feed restriction. On the other hand, plasma CORT levels were punctual in Exp 1 (increased with greater feed restriction at 16 wk of age), but showed increased levels in Exp 2 with greater feed restriction. The CORT levels are also related to reproductive performance in birds. Administration of CORT during the rearing phase of broiler breeders delayed sexual maturation and decreased egg production until 35 wk of age (Shini et al., 2009). The mechanism which elevated CORT decreased egg production may be associated to suppression of follicular development by decreasing availability of circulating yolk precursors (Wang et al., 2017). Still, its role in reproduction must be further elucidated, because of its potential association with ovulation (Hillebrecht et al., 2024).

In conclusion, increasing BW targets of broiler breeder females decreased feed seeking behaviour during the rearing phase and the pullet to hen transition phase. It also decreased heterophil to lymphocyte ratio from 16 to 32 wk of age. Plasma corticosterone levels decreased with increased BW targets at 16 wk of age in Exp 1, and decreased on average with increased BW targets from 16 to 28 wk of age in Exp 2. Relaxing quantitative feed restriction can reduce negative effects associated with excessive feed restriction in broiler breeders, such as feed seeking behaviour. Genetic potential for growth and efficiency in broiler breeders have increased tremendously in the past decades. Thus, allowing increased feed intake during the prepubertal (Exp 1) and pubertal (Exp 2) phase of broiler breeder females can reduce feeding seeking behaviour and hunger parameters. Physiological stress indicators decreased with increased BW targets, demonstrating that the stress related to feed restriction could be reduced with reduced feed restriction. However, the current nutrient intake recommendations associated with current BW profiles achieve good reproductive performance (biological response). Thus, relaxing feed restriction is a matter of continuous fine tuning in modern broiler breeders. Future research should focus on fundamental physiology with a larger sample size. Additionally, commercial farms and facilities should be incorporated into future research to identify the potential benefits in practical conditions.
