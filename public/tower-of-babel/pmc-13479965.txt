Electrocardiogram Characteristics and Outcomes in an Adult Congenital Heart Disease Population
Authors: Seyedeh Somayyeh Mousavi, Cheryl L Raskind‐Hood, Alexandra Haffner, Chad Robichaux, Lindsey C Ivey, Reza Sameni, Wendy M Book
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe electrocardiogram (ECG) is a low‐cost, readily available test that may detect dysrhythmia, chamber enlargement, and ischemia in an acquired heart disease population. Use of resting ECG to predict adverse events in an adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) population is unknown. ECG characteristics, outcomes, and vital status of 8130 adults born between 1945 and 2001 with at least two available ECGs during a 10‐year surveillance period (2010–2019) were evaluated at baseline and at the end of surveillance. Outcomes included cardiac hospitalizations, cardiac surgery or intervention, or death during surveillance. Broad CHD anatomic groups were analyzed as follows: “left‐sided,” “right‐sided,” “single‐ventricle,” and “neither” to account for differences in loading conditions. Of the cohort, 7.2% died, and 33.0% remained alive without adverse events. ECG parameters associated with mortality include wider QRS duration (QRSd), longer corrected QT (QTc), greater QRS‐T angle, left bundle branch block, and rhythm change. Those who died had longer mean baseline QRSd (107.3 ms vs. 101.83 ms, p < 0.0001) and QTc (434.94 ms vs. 425.20 ms, p = 0.0004). Change in QRSd (∆QRSd) was greater in the deceased group than alive (5.18 ms vs. 2.01 ms, p < 0.0001), and in alive with an adverse event compared to those alive without an event (2.78 ms vs. 0.42 ms, p < 0.0001). Mean QRS‐T angle remained concordant in those without an adverse event but deviated in the adverse event group (5.65° vs. 22.91°) (p < 0.0001). ECG characteristics and changes over time are associated with adverse cardiac events and mortality in a CHD population. Further study is needed to develop an ECG‐based risk model to identify at‐risk CHD patients.Keywords: cardiac outcomes, congenital heart disease, electrocardiogram, mortality

Introduction

Surgical advances have provided children born with congenital heart defects (CHD) with improved survival (Erikssen et al. 2023) resulting in an estimated adult prevalence in the United States of 1.5 million (Raskind‐Hood et al. 2019). Repairs are often palliative, associated with premature mortality (McCracken et al. 2018), with 1 in 5 individuals with CHD reported to require cardiac interventions over a 15‐year follow‐up (Zomer et al. 2011). Guidelines for the care of adults with CHD (ACHD) emphasize the need for lifelong care to monitor for late complications (Egidy Assenza et al. 2021) at a specialty tertiary care center. These guidelines focus on the role of specialized imaging—echocardiography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—for long‐term CHD surveillance to determine the need for intervention. Thus, imaging studies are performed at routine intervals, commonly every 1–3 years, over a lifetime (Writing Committee Members et al. 2026; Marelli et al. 2022; Babu‐Narayan et al. 2016). Such serial imaging is often associated with economic costs (Picano 2005), radiation exposure with some modalities (Cohen et al. 2018), and patient anticipatory anxiety (Bui et al. 2021). Although evidence exists for use of longitudinal imaging to determine the need for intervention, this approach puts the burden on the patient and favors those able to travel to tertiary referral centers, leading to loss to follow‐up (Gurvitz et al. 2013; Gurvitz and Saidi 2014). Approximately 39% of ACHD patients do not meet interval follow‐up guidelines (Serfas et al. 2022), and while surveillance is important, the majority of ACHD patients will not meet imaging criteria for intervention in any given year. Care access can be impacted by numerous factors (Jackson et al. 2019) and individuals who have lapses in care return to care at a higher frequency through emergency departments and in a decompensated state (Gurvitz et al. 2013). These collective burdens for adults with CHD may contribute to adverse outcomes and higher healthcare costs. Undertaking cardiac intervention in a timely manner can reduce the risk of late complications and premature mortality. Identifying candidates for longer surveillance criteria intervals could reduce patient burden and healthcare costs.

The 12‐lead resting electrocardiogram (ECG) is an inexpensive and widely available technology that can be performed with minimal training. Currently available ECG software, such as General Electric's (GE's) Food and Drug Administration approved and validated Marquette 12SL ECG Analysis Program (GE Medical Systems Information Technologies 2011), reports on rhythm analysis, conduction delays, axes, QT‐interval, corrected QT (QTc) and QRS duration (QRSd). While most ECG studies have focused on detection of arrythmia, enlargement of cardiac chambers, and ischemia in an acquired heart disease population (Szydlo et al. 2008; Rogers et al. 2025; Mousavi et al. 2025), the evaluation of ECG data in predicting clinical outcomes in ACHD has been limited to specific defects. For instance, longitudinal changes in the ECG occur in patients with tetralogy of Fallot in response to right ventricular (RV) dilatation (Kalantarian et al. 2021; Gallo et al. 2016) Vectorcardiographic QRS vector magnitude (Cortez et al. 2017; Bokma et al. 2017; Egbe et al. 2018; Gatzoulis et al. 1995) has been proposed as a predictor of arrhythmia in tetralogy of Fallot, presumably reflecting right ventricular remodeling. Furthermore, ventricular hypertrophy and remodeling that occur as a response to progression of CHD and its sequalae may be detectable on the baseline and serial ECG. While ventricular loading conditions and ventricular remodeling differ based on the underlying heart defect and subsequent repair (Demonceaux et al. 2025), there has been limited study of ECG characteristics and changes over time as predictors of adverse outcomes in the broader ACHD population. ECG parameters could potentially identify individuals who may remain stable, benefitting from longer intervals between surveillance visits.

We sought to understand the association of routinely available ECG measurements with death and other adverse outcomes in an ACHD population with the goal of assisting general practitioners in underserved areas to identify ACHD patients in need of specialty tertiary center care while reducing the cost burden on stable ACHD patients.

Methods

Study approval by the Emory University Institutional Review Board (IRB) was granted on 04/30/2024 (IRB # STUDY00007755) which included a complete waiver of HIPAA authorization and informed consent. A dataset of individuals born between January 1, 1945, and December 31, 2001, with selected CHD‐related ICD‐9‐CM or ICD‐10‐CM CHD codes (Table S1) and at least one healthcare encounter between 2010 and 2019 (N = 11,037) was abstracted from a large tertiary multisite adult healthcare system. Demographic characteristics retained included date of birth (to calculate age and for data integrity quality control), sex (male; female), race/ethnicity (non‐Hispanic White [nHW]; non‐Hispanic Black [nHB], Hispanic; Other [American Indian/Alaskan native, Asian, native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander and multiracial]; and unknown), rurality (urban; rural; unknown), and insurance type (any public ever; private, no public; self‐pay/uninsured only; and other [no public, no private]). Native CHD was broadly classified primarily as: (1) left‐sided; (2) right‐sided; (3) single ventricle; or (4) neither (coronary anomaly or vascular ring) as classified in Table S1. These anatomic classifications were chosen to explore the hypothesis that outcomes and ECG characteristics may differ by ventricular loading conditions, while maintaining generalizable results.

Three clinical outcomes were captured from the large tertiary multisite adult healthcare system for the cohort: (1) death during the surveillance period; (2) cardiac hospitalization during the surveillance period; and (3) cardiac surgery or intervention during the surveillance period. Occurrence of a death was determined from linkage to state death certificates. The two non‐death outcomes, cardiac hospitalizations, and heart surgery or cardiac intervention, were based on procedure codes (PCS codes) for cardiac surgeries and interventions from the International Classification of Diseases, 9th revision, Clinical Modification (CM) [ICD‐9‐PCS] and ICD 10th revision, CM [ICD‐10‐PCS] and the Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code‐based definitions. Cardiac hospitalization required two consecutive days of an inpatient encounter with an ICD code for a cardiac diagnosis as the primary reason for hospitalization. Cardiac intervention and cardiac hospitalization rates were calculated as event rates per year of available follow‐up. Available follow‐up was defined as time from first encounter to either date of death or to 12/31/2019 (end of surveillance period). An adverse cardiac outcome was defined as the occurrence of one or more cardiac events, including cardiac hospitalization and/or cardiac surgery or intervention, during the surveillance period. For purposes of analysis, outcomes were also considered as follows: (1) deceased during the surveillance period, (2) alive with an adverse cardiac outcome, or (3) alive without an adverse cardiac outcome.

The demographic dataset for the cohort was cleaned, deduplicated, and then linked to a GE Healthcare MUSE (General Electric Healthcare) ECG database from the same healthcare system containing the ECG tracings, ECG‐based measurements and the FDA approved Marquette 12SL‐generated statement codes (diagnoses) (GE Medical Systems Information Technologies 2011). The following ECG waveforms and features, measured by the GE Marquette 12SL ECG Analysis Programs, were captured: ventricular rate, atrial rate, PR interval, QRSd, QT interval, heart rate adjusted QT using Bazett (QTc) and Fredericia (QTc Fredericia) correction formulas, R axis, P axis, and T axis. A subset of ECG statement codes from the MUSE system indicating technical problems were excluded from analysis: “Poor data quality,” “Powerline interference,” “Baseline wander,” “Muscle tremor,” “Electrode noise,” “Disconnected,” “Suspect arm lead reversal, interpretation assumes no reversal,” “Poor data quality, interpretation may be adversely affected,” “Less than 4 QRS complexes detected, no interpretation possible,” “No QRS complexes found, no ECG analysis possible,” and “Nonstandard lead placement, ECG interpretation not available.” Also, ECGs in which all GE Marquette 12SL features were either zero or missing were excluded from the dataset, as this indicates that the ECGs were not recorded correctly.

Individuals with two or more ECGs during the surveillance period (N = 8130) were retained in the final analytic dataset, while those with only one ECG available during the surveillance period were excluded (N = 2907). Rhythm, conduction, interval, and axes were analyzed on the first and last ECG during the surveillance period. Change in QRS duration (∆QRSd) was calculated as QRSd at last ECG minus QRSd at first ECG. The change in QTc (∆QTc) was calculated as QTc interval from the last ECG minus QTc at the first ECG. The QRS‐T angle was calculated as the absolute value of the difference in the frontal plane R and T axes. Intervals and axes were also averaged across all available ECGs for each case, as additional baseline features for each parameter. Statistical Analysis

SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) was used for all analyses. Descriptive analyses for the cohort were conducted to examine the frequency distributions and percentages of patient characteristics and ECG features for outcomes and CHD anatomy. Bivariate analyses by mortality and alive cases with and without the composite outcome were conducted to describe and compare associations and differences for demographics and ECG features applying chi‐square tests (two‐sided) for categorical variables or t‐tests for continuous ones. To determine if the means of various continuous ECG features were statistically different from one another by CHD anatomic group, the general linear model (GLM) procedure was applied. A p value level of 0.05 or less was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort Description

Of the 11,037 individuals with CHD identified from the parent database who were born between 1945 and 2001, 8130 met the inclusion criterion of having at least two ECGs during 2010–2019. A comparison of the characteristics of the included versus excluded cases is in Table S2. In summary, of these 8130 individuals, 584 (7.2%) died during the study period compared to 62 (2.2%) of those with < 2 ECGs (p < 0.0001). The group with two or more ECGs experienced more hospitalizations (64.9% vs. 33.6%, p < 0.0001), more cardiac hospitalizations (62.9% vs. 30.4%, p < 0.0001), more ED visits (19.7% vs. 8.6%, p < 0.0001), and more cardiac surgery/interventions (49.9% vs. 18.7%, p < 0.0001) compared to those with less than two ECGs. Those with two or more ECGs were also more likely to have a single‐ventricle CHD.

Characteristics of the cohort and outcomes are shown in Table 2. Of the 7.2% of the cohort who died during the surveillance period, 5.7% were left‐sided, 8.1% right‐sided, 11.3% single ventricle, and 5.5% neither (p < 0.0001), and individuals who died were older (8.8% of 1945–1970 cohort compared to 3.3% of the 1991–2001 cohort, p < 0.0001), more likely to be male than female (7.9% vs. 6.4%, p = 0.0075), nHB compared to nHW, Hispanic, Other or unknown race (10.4% vs. 6.2%, 3.4%, 7.1%, 4.6%, respectively, p < 0.0001), and have public health insurance compared to private, those uninsured or those with other insurance (10.3% vs. 4.5%, 8.5%, 2.0%, respectively, p < 0.0001). Cases who died also had a higher cardiac hospitalization rate (mean 2.0 hospitalizations per year of follow‐up vs. 1.38 hospitalizations per year follow‐up in the group alive at end of surveillance period, p < 0.0001).

The association of single encounter ECG parameters with outcomes is shown in Table 3. Those alive with an adverse cardiac outcome were older than those without an adverse outcome (48.03 years vs. 40.83 years, p < 0.0001). On initial ECG, the majority had a normal sinus rhythm (NSR) (67.1%), but those who died later during the surveillance period were more likely to be in a rhythm other than NSR (not NSR) (40.1% deceased not NSR vs. 32.3% alive with not NSR, p < 0.0001). Several ECG parameters on initial ECG were associated with mortality during the surveillance period: longer QRSd (107.33 ms vs. 101.83 ms, p < 0.0001), QTc (434.94 ms vs. 425.20 ms, p = 0.0004), and axes: P and T wave axes more rightward (49.37 ms vs. 46.81 ms, p = 0.0304, and 57.24 ms vs. 51.87 ms, p = 0.0123, respectively). Intraventricular conduction delay morphology was also associated with mortality, with the highest mortality in the left bundle branch block (LBBB) group (11.6%) compared to no intraventricular conduction abnormality (7.0%), right bundle branch block (RBBB) group (6.9%), and nonspecific intraventricular conduction delay (6.9%), p = 0.0218. Similar findings were noted for the last ECG during the surveillance period with QRSd shown to increase between first and last ECG with significant differences in mortality (114.28 ms in the deceased group and 105.04 ms in the alive group, p < 0.0001).

When comparing the alive group who had adverse cardiac events compared to those alive without adverse cardiac events, ECG parameters followed a similar pattern of significance as seen between those who died during the surveillance period compared to those who remained alive. For instance, significant increases were seen in mean ∆QRSd (2.78 ms vs. 0.42 ms, p < 0.0001) and mean ∆QTc (4.55 ms vs. −0.48 ms, p = 0.0040) when comparing those alive with adverse outcomes compared to those alive without adverse outcomes. Similarly, for the P and T wave axes parameters, those who were alive with adverse outcomes showed significant increases compared to those alive without adverse outcomes: P wave axes: 48.03 ms vs. 45.43 ms, p < 0.0001, and T wave axes: 57.40 ms vs. 47.37 ms, p < 0.0001, respectively. Importantly, the group who remained alive without an adverse cardiac outcome compared to those who experienced a cardiac outcome exhibited shorter PR interval (162.86 ms vs. 170.16 ms, p < 0.0001), QRSd (100.67 ms vs. 105.79 ms, p < 0.0001), and QTc (424.12 ms vs. 434.48 ms, p < 0.0001). Lastly, the group without adverse outcomes had concordance of the mean QRS‐T angle compared to those alive with an adverse outcome (5.65° vs. 22.91°, p < 0.0001) and deceased (26.75°).

In Table 5, native CHD anatomy was associated with differences in ECG parameters. On initial ECG, NSR was similarly proportioned across all four CHD anatomy groups, as were atrioventricular conduction delays, with no significant CHD group differences. However, PR interval, QRSd, and QTc differed significantly between native CHD anatomy groups, with the longest intervals found in the single‐ventricle group and the shortest in the neither group. Right‐sided CHD was more likely to have RBBB compared to left‐sided CHD (22.2% and 15.8%, respectively, p < 0.0001) and left‐sided CHD was more likely to have LBBB compared to right‐sided CHD. Across time, 29.4% of the left‐sided CHD group, 27.0% of the right‐sided CHD group, 49.9% of the single‐ventricle group, and 21.3% of the neither group had significant differences in rhythm noted across ECGs over time (p < 0.0001). Average QRSd was similar between left‐ and right‐sided CHD groups over the surveillance period (104.9 and 104.4 ms, respectively) compared to the single‐ventricle group at 115.5 ms (p < 0.0001). QRSd in the left‐sided group increased from 101.9 ms at baseline to 106.6 ms at follow‐up, compared to the right‐sided group (increase from 102.4 ms at baseline to 105.0 ms at follow‐up).

Discussion

ECG parameters were associated with mortality and with the adverse cardiac events of cardiac hospitalization, cardiac surgery, and intervention for ACHD. Importantly, stability of ECG parameters over time—NSR, QRSd, QTc, QRS‐T angle—and shorter ECG intervals (PR, QRS, QTc)—were all associated with survival that was free of cardiac hospitalization and intervention. Stability of the ECG may be able to distinguish a group of individuals with CHD who could benefit from longer intervals between surveillance imaging from those who require cardiac hospitalization, cardiac intervention, or those who inevitably experienced death. Multiple ECG parameters were also helpful in distinguishing the stable cohort from those who had adverse events—baseline QRSd and longitudinal ∆QRSd and QTc interval, P and T wave axes, QRS‐T angle, and LBBB were all associated with worse outcomes and death.

ECG parameters that reflect myocardial fibrosis, ischemia, ventricular dilatation, inflammation, hypertrophy, strain, and repolarization abnormalities were all associated with adverse outcomes and mortality. These same ECG parameters remained stable over time in the group who remained alive without adverse events. QRSd was more prolonged in those with CHD who died or had an adverse cardiac event. QRSd reflects intramyocardial conduction delay and may increase in the setting of ventricular dilation, hypertrophy, or myocardial scarring, identifying patients at higher risk of adverse cardiac events. Similarly, QTc was significantly longer in the CHD group who died or had an adverse event. Repolarization abnormalities can reflect ventricular dysfunction or other factors such as fibrosis. While P and T wave axes were statistically different by outcomes, these parameters remained in the normal range. The significance of a rightward shift in P and T wave axes has not been previously explored. Concordance of the R‐T wave axis (the QRS‐T angle) is a hallmark of a normal ECG. Increase in the QRS‐T angle may represent myocardial strain, hypertrophy, dilatation, or ischemia. The QRS‐T angle remained concordant in the group without adverse events but differed significantly in the groups with adverse events.

To assess the clinical relevance of the ECG in the context of heterogenous native CHD anatomy and loading conditions, both outcomes and the ECG characteristics were evaluated by broad anatomic CHD classification. Differences were revealed in both ECG characteristics and in all three outcomes—death, cardiac hospitalization, and cardiac surgery or intervention—by CHD anatomic groups. Not surprisingly, those with single‐ventricle CHD had the highest risk of death despite being younger than the other groups. Patients with right‐sided lesions exhibited higher mortality and greater incidence of adverse outcomes compared with those with left‐sided lesions. The single‐ventricle group also had the longest QRSd, PR interval, QTc interval, more rightward R and T wave axes, a higher proportion with LBBB, non‐NSR rhythm and change in rhythm during the surveillance period. Analysis by left‐sided, right‐sided, single‐ventricle, and neither CHD anatomy groupings allowed the exploration of the hypothesis that CHD anatomy may impact both outcomes and ECG parameters. The single‐ventricle group was analyzed independently given the known higher risk for adverse outcomes at a younger age and greater sensitivity of the single ventricle to pressure and volume overload. While the finding that anatomic and loading conditions may affect both ECG parameters and adverse outcomes is not unexpected, these findings support the need for CHD anatomic group‐specific machine learning modeling strategies for the CHD population.

ECG is low‐cost, routinely available across healthcare settings and can be serially obtained in adults with CHD. Therefore, ECG parameters such as QRSd, ∆QRSd, ∆QTc, QRS‐T angle, and other ECG parameters may serve as noninvasive markers of myocardial remodeling serving to identify patients who would benefit from additional evaluation, imaging, or closer surveillance. Additionally, stability of these parameters over time could also aid in identifying patients who may be candidates for longer surveillance intervals between imaging studies. Although many of the ECG parameters that differed between groups remained in the normal range, these ECG parameters changed to a greater extent in the group with adverse outcomes and remained stable over time in the group without adverse outcomes, suggesting attention to serial changes may be clinically prognostic and identify patients in need of specialist referral. Given the cost and travel burden to patients required by regional ACHD referral centers, many patients with ACHD drop out of care, returning to care in a decompensated state. Others remain stable for long periods of time and may believe they do not require ongoing care. Leveraging low‐cost tools available to general practitioners in patient communities may improve identification of those patients in need of specialist care and ultimately improve long‐term outcomes (Gurvitz et al. 2013; Gerardin et al. 2019).

Overall, these findings support the role of ECG‐derived parameters in risk stratification and predictive modeling for adverse cardiac outcomes in CHD and importantly, development of a model to identify individuals with CHD who remain stable, free of cardiac hospitalization and intervention. Given the observed heterogeneity between native CHD anatomic groups, the development of separate predictive models for right‐ and left‐sided lesions, and single‐ventricle CHD may enhance prognostic accuracy and clinical applicability.

Strengths and Limitations

This analysis is strengthened by a large cohort of individuals with CHD linked to ECGs and death certificates. The restriction of the data set to those with two or more ECGs during the surveillance period ensured selection of a clinically relevant cohort engaged in ongoing medical care. Several limitations are acknowledged, however. Change in ECG parameters over time may be influenced by surgical interventions, medical therapies, or evolving hemodynamic conditions that may not have been fully captured in this analysis. The cohort size for specific heart defects required grouping CHD lesions broadly by general loading conditions. The ECG provides an analysis of rhythm at a point in time, and changes may occur in rhythm outside of the clinical setting. The number of patients with pacemakers may be underrepresented if pacing occurs intermittently and not at the time of the ECG. Additionally, ventricular pacing can affect QRSd, QTc, and QRS‐T angle and thus may contribute to some of the findings for these parameters, although the overall proportion of paced rhythm on the ECG was small. However, practical application of ECG‐based algorithms in CHD will require broad applicability across CHD diagnoses. Lastly, the datasets accessible for this analysis contained 2010–2019 data. Future work should include more contemporary data.

Future Directions

Next steps for this work include a programmatic application designed to automate ECG interpretation of both conventionally available parameters and novel features. Future work will focus on using open‐source ECG feature extraction tools, such as the open‐source electrophysiological toolbox (OSET) (Sameni 2006) and applying ML algorithms to analyze unique ECG characteristics within and across CHD anatomic groups, while considering different time intervals from ECG recording and the occurrence of adverse events in different time windows. In future work, we may also explore using cross‐sectional and longitudinal ECG to predict patient trajectory and outcomes within an actionable future, helping mitigate risks and improve outcomes. In addition, analyzing outcomes stratified by specific types of CHD is an important direction for future work, which can provide further insights into disease‐specific effects.
