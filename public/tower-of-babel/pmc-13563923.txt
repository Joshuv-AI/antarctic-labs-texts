Cancer clinical trial recruitment for patients from rural areas: a scoping review
Authors: Patrick O’Donnell, Rose Galvin, Katie Robinson, Peter Hayes, Ruth Clifford, Niamh Peters, Deirdre Hartigan, Sarah Dillon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis work will explore the literature on recruiting patients from rural and remote areas to cancer clinical trials. By examining the definitions employed, identifying levers and barriers to recruitment, and describing initiatives to promote recruitment, we seek to inform the development of future trials with these populations.MethodsA scoping review of primary research published since 2004 relating to the recruitment of patients from rural or remote areas to cancer clinical trials was conducted. The cancer could be of any type, haematological or solid tumour. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in CINAHL, PsycINFO, PubMed/MEDLINE, C2-SPECTR Campbell Systematic Reviews and Cochrane Library. All records were screened by two independent reviewers, and data on study characteristics and findings were extracted and analysed.ResultsA total of 29,160 papers were identified in the searches. After screening, 27 studies met our inclusion criteria. Twenty-three of those papers focused on the context of the United States of America, with an additional three looking at Australia and one at Canada. There was no uniformity in researchers’ classification of rurality, despite the existence of agreed measures. Several common levers and barriers to engagement were identified, including the distances patients must travel to cancer centres.ConclusionsRurality is a significant challenge to the recruitment of cancer patients to clinical trials. The potential solutions are multifaceted and span the major determinants of health. Focus on the development of collaborative research networks between major academic cancer centres and rural healthcare facilities may help improve access to trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-02904-0.Keywords: Cancer, Clinical trials, Recruitment, Equity, Rural health, Scoping review

Introduction

Background

Cancer incidence is rising worldwide, with modelling predicting that the number of new cases diagnosed annually will reach 35 million patients by 2050, significantly more than the estimated 20 million patients diagnosed in 2022 [1]. In response to these increases, there is a constant need for innovation in the development of new ways of treating cancers. Clinical trials (CT) are essential for evaluating new therapies and improving patient outcomes, while also providing valuable information for managers, policymakers and commercial entities [2]. However, less than 8% of adult cancer patients in the United States of America (USA) participate in trials, and the profile of participants often does not reflect the general population [3]. In Europe, fragmented healthcare systems and use of a number of different trial registries make estimating cancer trial participation rates difficult [4]. This has implications for the generalisability of trial findings and the confidence of clinicians in employing novel treatments with some underrepresented groups [5]. A lack of diversity among clinical trial participants can lead to varying treatment responses across different population groups due to their physiology or disease profile, complicating risk-benefit assessments [5]. Understanding and addressing both structural and clinical barriers to trial participation is crucial for enhancing enrolment among cancer patients [2].

Work to make cancer CT more equitable and representative has become more prominent internationally in recent years [6, 7]. For example, in 2020, the American Society of Clinical Oncology released a policy statement examining equity issues across the cancer continuum, and reaffirming their commitment to achieving “equitable access to care, improving clinical research, addressing structural barriers, and increasing awareness that results in measurable and timely action toward achieving cancer health equity for all” [8, p. 3439]. In the United Kingdom (UK), the National Institute for Health Research created the ‘Innovations in Clinical Trial Design and Delivery for the Under-served’ (INCLUDE) project has begun to systemically identify barriers and provide trialists with knowledge and tools for overcoming those barriers [5]. Internationally, there is more focus on improving the representation of underserved groups in cancer research across the continuum from diagnosis to treatment [6, 9–15]. Warner et al. have succinctly defined equity in this context as “acknowledging sociodemographic circumstances and apportioning resource and opportunity allocation to eliminate disparities in outcomes” [16, p. 8]. The classification of ‘underserved groups’ varies, with Crossley and Baybutt describing them as groups that “have limited access to healthcare, experience significant health disparities globally due to various intersecting factors, including race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, geographic location, gender identity, disability, sexual orientation and setting” [17, p. 341].

Rural-urban disparities in cancer treatment and outcomes are increasingly recognised [18–20]. These differences stem from multiple factors, including inadequate clinical infrastructure, low health insurance coverage, transportation challenges, higher smoking rates, lack of information, and, importantly, barriers to clinical trial participation [21]. In Europe there is acknowledgement that rurality and geographical inequities are a concern in the context of making clinical trials accessible to all cancer patients [4]. There is also a marked difference between the number of trials taking place in Western Europe versus Central and Eastern Europe which are underserved in this regard [22]. This scoping review builds on the foundational work of Ford et al., who examined barriers to cancer CT enrolment among underrepresented groups, looking at awareness, participation opportunities and the decision-making process regarding trial involvement [23]. Of the 65 studies Ford included, it is notable that only three focused on rural physicians or cancer trial participants [23]. A more recent review by McPhee et al. identified 24 studies in 2020 and reported on potential barriers or enablers of CT participation among rural participants, some of whom did not have cancer [24]. Given the growth in publications on this topic in the intervening years, an updated synthesis of the evidence is warranted. We noted that many reviews have included papers in which some participants themselves did not have cancer or a history of cancer. They were often asked hypothetically about potential engagement in cancer CT. Whilst unaffected participants may provide useful insights on certain aspects of care such as the user-friendliness of trial materials, their lack of lived experience of cancer may affect the resulting conclusions.

Initially, we aimed to examine all underserved groups, but we subsequently agreed to focus on rural and remote populations only for this review. Rural cancer patients often experience major challenges in accessing trials, and the nuances of these barriers warrant deeper analysis [6, 25, 26]. Defining ‘rural’ itself is complex, and the United States Census Bureau, the United Nations, the World Bank and others have all sought to clarify it for inclusion in policy and research [27]. The varying definitions can also be limited in applicability outside of their country of origin as they often incorporate specific geographic factors or population indicators. The size of the rural population in the USA is significant, with the Federal Office of Rural Health Policy estimating that 87.4% of the land in the USA was rural and 20.3% of the population (62.8 million people) were rurally based in 2020, similar to the 20.6% of EU rural inhabitants estimated in 2023 [28, 29]. Therefore, this scoping review aimed to understand the extent and type of evidence published in relation to the recruitment of adults with cancer living in rural or remote areas to CT. This included the objectives of exploring the definitions of rural status used by researchers, analysing the factors that may have promoted or impeded recruitment and describing some of the recruitment initiatives employed by trialists.

Methods

Study design

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI guidelines, and we adhered to the PRISMA Scoping Review checklist (Related File 1) to support rigorous conduct and reporting of the review [30, 31]. The protocol for the work was published on PROSPERO, and it was planned to include all underserved groups and conduct a rapid review (CRD42024518076). We subsequently decided to focus on people living in rural and remote areas only, and to convert to a scoping review as the papers we discovered were more diverse in methodology than anticipated. The research question, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and databases searched remained unchanged from the protocol. This change aligned with our revised aim of mapping the available literature focused on the recruitment of adults with cancer to CT in rural or remote settings. It also reflects that rurality is a factor of paramount importance in healthcare disparities in the USA and around the world [32, 33], and differences in cancer outcomes in particular [34]. In line with scoping review methodology, we did not appraise the quality of included papers [35]. As the work was a desk-based review of published research, no research ethics committee approval was required.

Inclusion criteria

Criteria were developed using the JBI population, concept and context approach. Sources were included if they met the following criteria: they were peer-reviewed research articles published in English since 2004, focusing on the recruitment of adult patients with any type of cancer for CT related to that diagnosis. We decided on a 20-year timeframe (2004–2024) to ensure that the evidence analysed would be contemporary while also capturing changes in clinical trial design and recruitment practices over time. Qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies, as well as evidence synthesis papers, were eligible for inclusion. Research focusing on children or the experiences of patients without cancer was excluded. Additionally, commentary papers, editorials, book chapters, theses, and news articles were excluded. For a paper to be eligible, it had to explicitly mention a rural or remote context in the title or abstract. Studies with mixed participant groups (e.g. rural and urban) were included only if more than half of the participants were from rural or remote areas or if the results for rural or remote participants were reported separately.

Selecting studies

A search was conducted in February 2024, updated in January 2025, across five databases: CINAHL, PsycINFO, PubMed/MEDLINE, C2-SPECTR and the Cochrane Library. The search strategy is detailed in Additional File 1. The online review management software Rayaan was used to de-duplicate and screen all papers (ww.rayyan.ai). Initially, the title and abstract of each paper were reviewed by two independent researchers, and then the full text of all relevant papers was scrutinised by the two same independent researchers. Any conflicting decisions were brought to regular research meetings where four researchers examined the papers, and a consensus was reached. The results of this process are detailed in Figure 1 below. For evidence synthesis papers, we took the component papers and applied our inclusion and exclusion criteria to them; we then removed the ‘parent’ evidence synthesis paper.

Charting data and reporting

Data were extracted from each paper into a Microsoft Excel file with tailored categories. A narrative analysis of research findings was instead conducted, and results are reported in a series of tables. Studies that addressed psychosocial factors affecting rural patient participation in cancer CT were further analysed using the Social Ecological Model (SEM) previously adapted by Wells and Zebrack to examine cancer CT participation of underserved patients [36–38]. This approach was chosen for its nuance in categorising barriers relevant to our analysis. Their original categories included inTRApersonal, inTERpersonal, environment, community, sociocultural and institutional factors, and we adapted it by adding a seventh category of patient characteristics and by using it to explore enablers as well as barriers (see Table 1).

Categorisation of levers and barriers

These categories were not applied to papers focused on specific interventions or initiatives, or those exploring some aspects of patient decision making and knowledge of cancer trials, as these topics did not align with the scope of their analytic lens [38]. Papers focused on those aspects of clinical trial research are based on specific gaps already identified in the literature or practice.

Public and patient involvement

The idea for this evidence synthesis was derived from engagement with a Public and Patient Involvement (PPI) stakeholder group of older adults and family caregivers recruited from across the Mid-West region of Ireland. This geographical region comprises a mix of urban and rural areas where distance and access to healthcare have been reported as problematic. This PPI panel has previously participated in several health service research projects. Participation in CT was discussed with approximately 20 panel members at a two-hour meeting in May 2025. Discussion was facilitated by co-authors to explore pertinent issues, including the logistics of healthcare access from rural areas, including travel time, costs, availability of family members to support with appointments and the limited availability of public transport. Overall, there was agreement by the PPI panel with the interpretation of findings presented in this scoping review. Strategies suggested to potentially overcome these barriers included subsidised travel, adequate notification of appointments, integration of care and pooled resources to alleviate travel costs.

Results

General results

A total of 29,160 records were identified through database searches. After screening, 27 studies met the inclusion criteria, and the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1) provides an overview of the study selection process.

The 27 papers included in Table 2 comprise fourteen quantitative, nine qualitative and four mixed methods papers. Twenty-three of the papers were looking at the USA, while three examined the Australian context [41, 60] and one focused on Canada [39]. Eighteen papers focused on the rural context exclusively, while nine authors included participants from both rural and non-rural areas. Some were included as a direct comparator group, e.g. rural versus urban [43, 64], with others examining rural participants with participants from other underserved groups, such as ethnic minorities [46, 47, 51, 59].

Eighteen papers examined the experiences of patients with a current or past history of cancer, two analysed the experiences of clinicians [50, 51], two looked at patients and clinicians together [48, 56], one looked at cancer patients and carers [45], one focused on community health workers [55], one looked at a mixture of community leaders and others [49], one explored the views of researchers and clinicians [59], and one looked at clinicians, researchers and cancer patients together [65].

Not surprisingly, distance and the onerous travel often required to partake in CT emerged as key barriers in five studies [48, 53, 56, 60, 64], with seven other papers highlighting some aspects of travel-related challenges [40, 43, 52, 61–63, 65]. Friedman et al. described the existence of “clinical trial deserts” in certain parts of the USA [48], (p114) while Borno et al. found that participants from more deprived areas were travelling longer distances to trial centres than wealthier participants (58.3 versus 17.8 miles) [53]. Zdenkowski et al. surveyed cancer patients about their willingness to take part in CT, finding that many of them were willing to travel significant distances to engage with a new clinical team if it allowed them to enrol on a suitable trial [60].

Definitions

Thirteen of the 27 papers did not use any formal designation of participants; some used attendance at clinics in rural areas as a proxy identifier for the rural living status of their participants, others mentioned patients originating from predominantly rural regions or states, e.g. Appalachia, as an indicator of high likelihood of being rurally based. Five of the included papers from the USA mentioned the Rural–Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC) or Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) code classifications, which were developed by the United States (US) Department of Agriculture. The US Medically Underserved Area designation was mentioned by only one paper in our review [46], and another mentioned use of an online portal ‘Am I Rural’ [61]. One paper from Australia used an accepted national system of rural designation from that country [41], another used the Monash Modified Model [65], while the other Australian paper authors used postcodes but did not elaborate on what steps they took to stratify the rurality of the participants using those postcodes [60]. Wagoner et al. discussed the use of an adapted Canadian ‘remoteness index’ used to define inclusion in a rural population [39, 66].

Categorisation of levers and barriers

Sixteen papers were analysed using the SEM as applied by Wells and Zebrack (Additional File 2) [36–38]. Among these, 12 studies focused on patients as participants, while 4 involved combinations that included clinicians and researchers. We systematically analysed each paper across the seven categories outlined previously, noting the presence of enablers and barriers for each category.

Fourteen papers reported barriers or enablers in the inTRApersonal category. Enablers for trial participation included the belief that participating could extend their lives or provide access to novel treatments [40, 41]. Barriers included a lack of participant interest in taking part, a preference for standard treatment over experimental options, concerns about potential side effects from trial medications, and the belief that trial enrolment should be a last resort [41, 46–48, 58, 63].

Fourteen papers reported barriers or enablers in the inTRApersonal category. Enablers for trial participation included the belief that participating could extend their lives or provide access to novel treatments [40, 41]. Barriers included a lack of participant interest in taking part, a preference for standard treatment over experimental options, concerns about potential side effects from trial medications, and the belief that trial enrolment should be a last resort [41, 46–48, 58, 63].

Fourteen papers reported barriers and enablers that were categorised as inTERpersonal; relating to factors that can exert influence on an individual. Barriers included discouragement from enrolling by cancer clinicians, family physicians or family members, as well as concerns that clinicians may not have a full understanding of trials they were promoting [40, 41, 50, 58]. In contrast, enablers of trial participation included clinician encouragement, good relationships and communication with clinicians and hearing positive experiences of other patients on CT [41, 52, 59].

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to examine the body of evidence around the recruitment of adults with cancer living in rural or remote areas to CT. The 27 papers identified by the systematic search were used to examine the definitions of rurality used, as well as the levers, barriers and initiatives related to the participation of rural patients in cancer clinical trials. We will discuss the major implications for the equitable recruitment of cancer patients from rural areas to appropriate high-quality clinical trials.

The predominance of research conducted in the USA may reflect the relative size of its rural population, and potentially the documented cancer rates and inequities in cancer outcomes for these patients. It may also reflect the advanced clinical networks and research infrastructure that have been developed in rural areas of the country over many decades. Public bodies such as the National Cancer Institute (NCI), and not-for-profits like the American Cancer Society and cancer networks such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network has done much to raise the profile of cancer, clinical advances and opportunities and ongoing research in that time. Surprisingly, no primary research from Europe was discovered; this may reflect the fact that majority of cancer CT there are industry rather than publicly funded, using established centres that tend to be in urban areas, making research analysing rural participation less likely [4]. It was also notable that only one paper from Canada and three from Australia were found to meet the criteria, as these are both countries in which rural healthcare is generally perceived to be well developed [67, 68]. They have similar favourable policy environments with the long‑standing availability of universal health coverage, decentralised healthcare governance structures and workforce policies targeted at rural and remote areas [69]. No papers from low- and middle-income countries were found, and this may reflect that the conduct of CT requires significant funding and dedicated research capacity [70].

It was also notable that only one paper explored the experiences of carers in relation to cancer CT [45]. We know that carers and family members have an important role in supporting affected cancer patients in their decision-making on starting and their adherence to trials [71, 72]. We expected more involvement from this key stakeholder group, particularly in the rural context where transport to and from trial centres may be reliant on them. We also noted that some authors focused on recruitment of very specific groups of participants, such as armed forces veterans living rurally with blood cancers [48]. and older-aged low-income rural dwelling women [45]. The narrow inclusion criteria in these studies could have implications for their generalisability. Only one paper by Guadagnolo et al. mentioned American Indians as a specific indigenous group being focused on in terms of their engagement, and this could be considered surprising given that they mainly live in rural areas of the USA [46].

It cannot be presumed that by living rurally, all patients will face significant travel distances to cancer centres, but most trial activity happens in specialist centres that tend to be in urban areas. Most trials will require patients to attend a series of in-person clinic visits to assess suitability and deliver treatment, making the distances that patients must travel from home an important consideration, with the time and financial costs involved [73]. Efforts have been made in the USA to bring cancer centre expertise closer to people in underserved communities, for example, the NCI Community Oncology Research Program (NCORP), which comprises a network of centres at sites in communities across the country. The term “clinical trial deserts” [48, p. 114] closely linked to the term “medical deserts” [74, p. 785] which Brînzac et al. defined as “areas where population healthcare needs are unmet partially or totally due to lack of adequate access or improper quality of healthcare services caused by insufficient human resources in health or facilities, long waiting times, disproportionate high costs of services or other socio-cultural barriers” [74, p. 785] applies to many rural areas. When you consider that basic health and cancer care infrastructure in certain rural areas is lacking [75, 76], it is not surprising that there are low levels of clinical research or trials taking place in those areas [77]. Some researchers have extrapolated the implications of these “clinical trial deserts” for population health and particular cancers, with one study estimating that around 40 million Americans lived in areas poorly served by gastrointestinal cancer CT in 2024. It is suggested that increased use of community hospital networks and telemedicine might help to address this inequity [78].

Our analysis of the ways rurality was classified illustrates the diverse approaches of author groups, even in the 23 papers from the USA. Many papers did not adopt any formal description of the rurality of participants; some used attendance at clinics located in rural areas as a proxy identifier for the rural status of patients, others mentioned that patients originated from predominantly rural regions or states, e.g. Appalachia, as an indicator of high likelihood of being rurally based. Both approaches, however, could have included participants who were not rural. In the USA, there are accepted rural designations, and the use of the RUCC in particular has been recommended for use over other indices. It has been described as the “standardly available in national cancer registries, can be matched to patients’ county of residence for local research, and it had the least amount of fluctuation of the indices analyzed” [79, p. 78]. Possible implications of not adopting a standardised approach to identifying rurality clearly have been highlighted previously by Smith et al. who noted that “repeating the same study with the same data but applying different definitions of rural and medically underserved may result in very different conclusions” [80, p. 7]. This could then have implications for service design and potential policy responses to gaps highlighted in rural health research [80, 81]. However, there are some doubts about an over-reliance on systematic geographical designation of rural participants, and there is acknowledgement that they were not created for health research [80, 82]. It is also worth noting that rural designation does not always align with an area being medically underserved, so there are nuances that need to be taken into account before embarking on research and when making follow-up practice or policy recommendations [80]. The Appalachian region of the USA features in three of the included papers, and it is frequently described as a region with marked inequity in health outcomes [83]. According to the Appalachian Community Cancer Alliance, the region’s population is more than 25 million inhabitants living in both rural and urban settings, and it includes all of West Virginia and parts of twelve other states. Significant challenges with persistent poverty, high rates of chronic diseases and addiction, infrastructure problems, limited access to healthcare and health literacy concerns are all recognised in the region [83, 84]. In 2017, it was noted that Appalachia’s cancer mortality was 10% higher than the national average, with rural areas of the region having more marked differences [83].

Use of the SEM as employed by Wells and Zebrack to explore a subset of the papers highlighted that many factors recognised as Social Determinants of Health by Dahlgren and Whitehead have a strong influence on rural cancer CT enrolment [38, 85]. These include living conditions, education, employment status, income levels and others, and they were acknowledged as barriers across all thirteen papers under the category of environment. When these wider societal issues are at play, it can be difficult for healthcare professionals and cancer clinicians to know where to begin to address these to try to improve CT participation. It is worth noting that cancer CT trials take place in the context of a wider health ecosystem, and clinicians, patients and other relevant stakeholders should be empowered to advocate for better living conditions and social supports for patients for them to be able to even consider participation. It is also not surprising that inTRApersonal and inTERpersonal factors were commonly cited; past experiences and health beliefs, and concerns had direct effects on patient decision-making when considering trial enrolment. The inTERpersonal domain considered the influence of other stakeholders and social networks on engagement of individual patients with trials; and hearing personal stories from others who had enrolled on trials in the past may help to answer some concerns. Many of these barriers have been discussed in the literature in the past, and rural cancer patients are no different [23].

CT are complex, and patients can struggle to fully understand their potential benefits and the steps involved in taking part [86, 87]. Initiatives to increase knowledge and understanding of trials for patients have shown varying levels of success [88]. The improvement of patient and public knowledge of CT, and cancer trials in particular, has been the focus of several bodies (e.g. NIH & NCI) in recent years with information campaigns on everything from the steps involved to personal stories from participants, including some from rural areas. The American Cancer Society has its own Access to Clinical Trials programme that provides similar information, including using videos for patients with limited literacy. Education for clinicians should include a basic understanding of cancer, cancer treatments, clinical research and cancer CT for these professionals to be able to advise patients. This could take place across the educational continuum from undergraduate to clinicians-in-training, to qualified specialist levels [89, 90]. The topic also lends itself well to interprofessional education for students and professional development, as much modern cancer care is delivered by multidisciplinary teams [91]. Digital tools for education may be of particular relevance to clinicians who are working in rural or remote settings.

Taking the totality of these points into consideration, it is likely that marked underrepresentation of rural participants with cancer in research on clinical trials will persist unless fundamental changes are made to how trials are structured and delivered. This will have knock on effects on the generalisability and ‘real-world’ use of the evidence generated from trials. This may lead to an almost self-fulfilling loop of limited evidence being generated in rural settings, leading to limited understanding of the complexities and possible mitigation strategies, leading ultimately to cancer patients in these areas having less access to novel treatments and approaches.

Strengths and limitations of this study

There are several strengths and limitations in this research. Searches conducted were comprehensive, allowing us to provide analysis of a broad array of papers examining rurality and cancer CT engagement. The engagement of a team of four researchers to meet and regularly discuss any conflicts in screening or clarify any points of concern was also positive. Use of the SEM as adapted by Wells and Zebrack to analyse a subset of the papers to tease out the levers and barriers was a strength. Limitations included that we were only able to include papers published in English due to a lack of translation. The absence of an agreed international classification of rurality is a limitation, meaning we were often solely reliant on the author’s description of their included participants. There was also heterogeneity of many aspects of the papers included, making comparisons challenging. Using the SEM as adapted by Wells and Zebrack to analyse only a portion of the papers we included may result in the separation of identification of barriers and enablers from evidence of their modifiability and practical impact, possibly limiting the application of this research.

Implications for policy, practice and further research

This study highlights that living rurally brings significant challenges for potential participants in cancer CT. Distance and travel, while important, are not the only barriers to be unpacked in this context. Others include concerns about side effects, education and literacy challenges, and the patients physical condition and age. In terms of accurately defining rural status, an international agreement on a standardised way of defining it should be considered. The development of rural healthcare infrastructure, including staffing, cancer care infrastructure and the creation of research capacity at these rural sites, is vital for meaningful progress in making high-quality cancer CT more accessible for rural patients. Fostering the growth of collaborative research networks between major academic cancer centres in Europe and smaller rural health institutions may help improve coverage of CT also. Potential future research could include a specific focus on carers and family members of people with cancer and exploring the experiences of specific groups, such as indigenous or minority groups in rural settings. Training and education are required for stakeholders to try to overcome any preconceptions on CT engagement.

Conclusions

Cancer CT are vital for the evaluation of novel treatments and approaches. Rural populations have worse cancer outcomes, yet they are underrepresented in trials. This scoping review examined 27 papers identified as being relevant to the recruitment of patients from rural areas to cancer CT. The majority of these papers were from the USA, where rural cancer research appears to be more developed. There were no papers from Europe or low and middle-income country settings. Fourteen of the papers were quantitative, nine were qualitative, and four employed mixed methods. The definitions used by researchers to delineate rural participants from others varied, even in the USA where there is some degree of standardisation of definitions. The recruitment of cancer patients to CT is challenging and heavily dependent on healthcare and research infrastructure being in place in more peripheral locations to make it a viable option for participants to engage with trials. There are a variety of initiatives that have been employed to try to improve this engagement, as well as efforts to improve understanding of trials themselves. Unless there is a concerted effort made internationally to address the acknowledged challenges, the recruitment of rural-dwelling cancer patients to CT will not advance.
