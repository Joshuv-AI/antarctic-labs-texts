Commentary: White matter functional connectome gradient dysfunction in major depressive disorder
Authors: Shangping Cheng, Bing Chen, Jing Lu
Published: 2026

The investigation by Yu et al. represents a substantive contribution to the neurobiology of major depressive disorder (MDD) by transcending the conventional focus on white matter microstructure to characterize its intrinsic functional architecture (Yu et al., 2025). The authors provide robust evidence for a reconfiguration of the hierarchical organization within the white matter functional connectome in MDD, specifically a contraction of the principal superficial-to-deep gradient, which is further associated with clinical symptom burden. This work signifies an important conceptual evolution, framing depression not merely in terms of focal deficits but as a disorder of large-scale network integration and hierarchical segregation, extending gradient-based insights from prior gray-matter studies while building on the existing evidence of connectome-gradient dysfunction in gray matter (Xia et al., 2022). The implications of these findings, alongside certain methodological considerations, merit detailed scholarly reflection. Our commentary weighs the strength of this inference, identifies the methodological choices that matter most, and outlines how the approach can be tested and extended.

The methodological approach, which employs diffusion map embedding to derive continuous connectome gradients from resting-state functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) data, effectively captures a fundamental spatial ordering principle underlying white matter function. The identification of a contracted connectome-gradient range and reduced spatial variance in individuals with MDD indicate a systemic impairment in the functional differentiation between canonical superficial and deep white matter tracts. This finding is consistent with and extends previous evidence of disrupted network hierarchy and increased randomization in both gray and white matter connectomes associated with psychiatric disorders (Hong et al., 2019). Interpreted within the associative framework of this study, the observed connectome-gradient contraction corresponds to a pattern consistent with a breakdown in the efficient segregation of information processing pathways, and is concurrently linked with the co-occurrence of cognitive and affective symptoms in MDD. Furthermore, the significant correlation between specific tract connectome-gradient patterns—particularly those involving the anterior thalamic radiation, cingulum hippocampus, and uncinate fasciculus—and depressive symptom severity, as measured by BDI-II scores, suggests that these patterns may be underscored as pathophysiologically relevant factors within the framework of this functional disorganization.

The translational promise of these findings lies in their potential to bridge the gap between network-level pathophysiological insight and clinical application, contingent upon rigorous validation and refinement. The established association between connectome-gradient metrics—particularly the contraction of the hierarchical range—and clinical symptom severity positions this framework not merely as a descriptive tool, but as a candidate and exploratory source of mechanistic biomarkers. Such biomarkers could move beyond correlative observations to inform on the underlying network dysfunction driving specific symptom domains. In a clinical research setting, white matter connectome-gradient profiles could, in principle, potentially enable a form of pathophysiological stratification, distinguishing patient subgroups not by symptom checklist alone, but by their distinct patterns of hierarchical disruption within the brain’s connective architecture. At present, however, proposed links between tract-specific connectome-gradient alterations and symptom dimensions should be treated as hypothesis-generating. For instance, a connectome-gradient profile dominated by alterations in limbic-associated tracts like the uncinate fasciculus and cingulum hippocampus may delineate a subgroup with primary affective dysregulation and anhedonia, whereas a profile showing prominent involvement of sensorimotor tracts such as the corticospinal tract might identify patients with significant psychomotor agitation or retardation. Future studies could test these hypotheses by deriving symptom dimensions from standardized multi-domain measures (including factor-analytic scores), and then applying multivariate structure–symptom mapping (canonical correlation or partial least-squares) or cross-validated predictive modeling, with permutation testing and appropriate control of confounds. While these stratification proposals remain hypothetical and require prospective validation, they suggest a pathway toward treatment personalization. It could rationally guide the selection of targeted interventions, such as tailoring neuromodulation protocols. For example, a patient with a gradient profile dominated by uncinate fasciculus dysfunction might be prioritized for transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) protocols targeting the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex–amygdala circuit, whereas a profile showing corticospinal tract involvement might suggest adjunctive somatic therapies such as motor-cortex stimulation or physical activation strategies. For techniques like TMS, individual connectome-gradient maps could identify network nodes exhibiting aberrant functional positioning—such as hyperconnected regions within a fronto-limbic circuit—potentially informing a shift in targeting from standardized anatomical coordinates toward patient-specific, network-defined hubs to optimize engagement of dysfunctional pathways.

That said, the cross–sectional design of Yu et al. limits causal inference. Longitudinal work is the obvious next step, and it also offers a direct way to address the confounds listed above. First, tracking individuals from pre–morbid or early–illness stages can determine whether gradient changes precede illness or worsen with progression—and, crucially, can dissociate stable anatomical artifacts (e.g. depth–dependent vascular patterns or partial volume effects) from dynamic neural changes. Second, pre–post intervention studies (pharmacotherapy or CBT) can test whether clinical improvement parallels gradient normalization; if the gradient shifts after treatment despite unchanged vascular architecture or partial volume, that would argue for a neural origin. Third, causal mapping approaches such as TMS–fMRI can ask whether directly perturbing network nodes alters the connectome gradient, providing direct evidence of neural causality. Beyond these, longitudinal data would also help separate state–related from trait–related alterations and clarify how white–matter functional gradients respond to intervention. If validated, tracking gradient dynamics could provide an objective readout of network reorganization for precision psychiatry.

Significant unaddressed heterogeneity within the patient sample, including variations in illness duration, episode recurrence, and most critically, psychotropic medication status, presents a substantial confound. The neurotrophic and neurovascular effects of antidepressants, in particular, are likely to modulate functional connectivity signatures, and their influence on white matter gradient organization remains entirely unexplored. Future studies could address this by recruiting medication-naïve first-episode patients, incorporating medication load as a covariate in larger cohorts, or implementing longitudinal designs that track gradient changes before and after pharmacotherapy.

In addition, the biological specificity of white-matter functional connectome gradients requires further clarification. Although the principal gradient has been linked to an anatomical hierarchy through correlations with microstructural properties such as myelin content (Li et al., 2020), its precise neurophysiological substrates—whether primarily neuronal, glial, or vascular—are not delineated. Interpretation of white matter BOLD signals, which underpin the functional connectivity analysis, warrants caution due to potential vascular contributions, partial volume effects, physiological noise, and susceptibility artifacts. While Yu et al. performed commendable validations for motion and methodological parameters, future studies integrating techniques such as quantitative susceptibility mapping or dynamic physiological monitoring could help disentangle neural from non-neural contributions to the observed gradient structure.

Several methodological factors may influence the interpretation of the observed functional gradients. These include vascular patterns related to tract depth, partial volume effects from adjacent gray matter that may elevate superficial tract connectivity, physiological noise less effectively corrected in white matter, and susceptibility artifacts near tissue–air interfaces affecting specific tracts. Critically, these confounds may not simply add noise but systematically bias the functional connectivity matrix. For instance, depth-dependent vascular patterns could artificially produce or amplify a superficial-to-deep hierarchy, while partial volume effects at the gray–white matter boundary could reduce apparent functional differentiation in superficial tracts.

Thus, the reported contraction of the gradient range in MDD patients may partly reflect group differences in the impact of these confounds, rather than solely a true alteration in neural communication hierarchy.

It will also be important to integrate white-matter functional hierarchy with established gray-matter gradient abnormalities in MDD and test how the two interact in shaping clinical phenotypes. Connectome-gradient mapping in gray matter is now well established. Diffusion-embedding approaches reliably recover a principal sensory-to-transmodal axis that captures macroscale cortical hierarchy (Margulies et al., 2016). In MDD, prior studies have reported alterations in these gradients and related them to multiscale biological features and treatment-associated change (Xia et al., 2022). A practical next step is to estimate white- and gray-matter gradients within the same individuals, align them in a shared embedding space (e.g. Procrustes alignment or joint embedding), and quantify cross-tissue coupling or use multivariate fusion to link coupled gradients to symptom dimensions (Vos de Wael et al., 2020). Multimodal imaging could provide biological correlates that constrain interpretation of these functional patterns.

Finally, cross-disorder studies using harmonized pipelines are needed to determine the diagnostic specificity of connectome-gradient contraction versus its presence in other conditions with cognitive-affective dysregulation.

In conclusion, Yu et al. have provided compelling evidence that reconceptualizes white matter in MDD as a dynamically organized functional system whose hierarchical architecture is compromised. This study effectively bridges advanced computational neuroimaging and clinical psychiatry, offering a novel framework for understanding network-level pathophysiology. While questions of causality, confounds, and neurobiological specificity await resolution, this work undeniably opens a productive avenue for developing biologically grounded, network-sensitive approaches to diagnosis, stratification, and treatment evaluation in MDD.
