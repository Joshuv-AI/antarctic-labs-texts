Multimodal approach to exploring neighbourhood and transportation dynamics in HIV care in Kenya: protocol
Authors: Linda Jepkoech Kimaru, Christine Ngaruiya, Cyrus Mugo, Purnima Madhivanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHIV is a major health challenge in Kenya, where prevalence exceeds global averages. Achieving viral suppression depends on consistent adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART), but individuals in neighbourhoods perceived as disorderly often show low self-efficacy for ART adherence. Despite the importance of neighbourhood dynamics in shaping health behaviours, this area is under-explored in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), especially regarding transportation barriers. Grounded in Social-Ecological Theory (SET) and the ‘Broken Windows’ Theory (BWT), SET suggests that health outcomes are influenced by environmental factors, while BWT posits visible signs of neighbourhood disorder, such as neglect and decay, can lead to a decline in healthy behaviours, creating an environment conducive to negative health outcomes. This protocol describes a multimodal qualitative study designed to examine how transportation challenges and perceived neighbourhood dynamics influence HIV care among people living with HIV (PLWH). This intersection of structural barriers, neighbourhood dynamics and HIV outcomes in LMIC settings remains under-researched.MethodsThis theory-informed multimodal qualitative study, grounded in a Community-Based Participatory Research framework, integrates individual in-depth interviews (IDIs) and community-based photovoice focus group discussions (FGDs). 20 PLWH participated in IDIs accompanied by a survey with validated scales (~ 10 min) assessing perceived neighbourhood disorder, defined as visible physical and social signs of neglect such as crime, vandalism and deteriorated infrastructure, ART adherence self-efficacy, transportation, access to HIV care and fear of crime. An additional 20 PLWH took part in four photovoice FGD sessions. Photovoice FGD sessions were guided by the SHOWeD questioning technique, a structured reflection method that prompts participants to discuss what they see, what is happening, how it relates to their lives, why it exists and what can be done. In each component, participants were purposively sampled and stratified to ensure approximately equal representation by viral suppression status (10 suppressed, 10 unsuppressed), gender (10 women, 10 men) and age group (10 aged 18–24, 10 older than 24). Interviews and photovoice discussions were transcribed and are being analysed thematically using MAXQDA qualitative analysis software. Participant-generated photographs will be analysed alongside accompanying narratives using an integrated visual and textual coding approach combining inductive and theory-informed deductive analysis. Triangulation across interviews and validated survey measures will be conducted.Ethics and disseminationThis study has received ethical approval from the KNH-UoN Ethics and Research Committee (P635/08/2024) and the Stanford University Institutional Review Board (eProtocol #: 77260). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and a photo exhibition.DiscussionThis study will contribute to an understudied body of research on how socio-environmental conditions influence HIV care engagement in LMICs. Through the integration of individual-focused in-depth interviews and community-centred photovoice FGD sessions, the study captures both personal perceptions and shared neighbourhood realities. Findings will inform interventions and policy adjustments to improve support for PLHIV in disordered environments.

Introduction

Despite considerable progress in expanding access to antiretroviral therapy (ART), many people living with HIV (PLWH) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)1 continue to face barriers to sustained care engagement. HIV remains a significant public health challenge in Kenya, with approximately 1.4 million people living with the virus in 2023.2 Among adults, women represent the majority of cases compared with men, prevalence rate among women is 4.3%, more than double that of men at 2.1%.2 Young adults aged 15–24 years are particularly vulnerable; prevalence rates are 1.4% for young women and 0.9% for young men, highlighting the disproportionate burden on women in this age group.2 Kenya has made remarkable progress in its HIV response, surpassing some of the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) 95–95–95 targets by 2030.3 Of those living with HIV, 96% are aware of their status, 94% are on ART and 91% have achieved viral suppression.2 To fully achieve and sustain the 95–95–95 targets, especially for those on ART and achieving viral suppression, it is essential to go beyond individual-level interventions and address broader community-level factors.

A growing body of literature indicates that neighbourhood characteristics are linked to HIV-related outcomes such as risk behaviours, testing, transmission and engaging in care.47 In a scoping review, neighbourhood disorder remains one of the least examined neighbourhood characteristics in relation to HIV treatment outcomes.8 Perceived neighbourhood disorder refers to visible signs of social and physical neglect that signal a breakdown in order.9 It includes physical disorder, physical decay and social disorder. Physical disorder is referred to as visible environmental issues like litter, graffiti and discarded items in public spaces.9 While physical decay refers to structural deterioration such as abandoned buildings or damaged facilities.9 Social disorder is defined as threatening or disruptive public behaviours such as fights, loitering, intoxication, gang activity or drug dealing.9 These conditions have been linked to poor health outcomes,10 lower ART adherence11 and viral suppression status.12 Most evidence to date comes from high-income settings, highlighting a gap this study addresses in a low-income context.8 Fear of crime—defined as the emotional response to perceived risk of victimisation13—has also been associated with poor HIV treatment outcomes.14 15 Transportation is a key structural factor affecting HIV care, especially in low-income settings.16 Transportation is a system and means by which people and goods move from one place to another, encompassing infrastructure (roads, sidewalks, bike lanes, public transit systems, etc), services (buses, trains, ridesharing) and accessibility factors (cost, safety, reliability, etc). In sub-Saharan Africa, transport accessibility, costs and distance to care affect HIV outcomes.16 Although most Kenyans live within 1 hour of a facility, actual travel times are often much longer.17 Transportation vulnerability, such as needing multiple transport modes, can delay care and reduce medical adherence.18 This study examined these barriers alongside perceived neighbourhood disorder and fear of crime.

While multiple studies have used geographic or socioeconomic indicators to characterise neighbourhoods, few have focused on lived experiences,8 especially of perceived neighbourhood disorder or fear of crime in relation to HIV care. Moreover, there is limited use of qualitative or participatory methods to explore these factors in-depth. Research that integrates subjective perceptions, visual data and theory-driven analysis is needed to understand how perceived neighbourhood and mobility dynamics shape HIV outcomes. This study aims to understand ‘How do transportation challenges and perceived neighborhood dynamics impact HIV care among PLHIV?’. To address this question, this protocol outlines a multimodal qualitative design with the following objectives:

To explore the experiences and perceptions of PLWH on ART regarding neighbourhood perceived disorder and perceived fear of crime on ART adherence self-efficacy and HIV care engagement. This was accomplished using in-depth interviews (IDIs), and an accompanying survey with validated scales to conduct qualitative analysis.

Methods

Study design

This study used a theory-informed multimodal qualitative design grounded in Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) framework to examine the role of transportation barriers and perceived neighbourhood dynamics in HIV care among PLWH. The design integrates two complementary qualitative components: IDIs and photovoice focus group discussions (FGDs). Together, these methods enable examination of both individual lived experiences and shared structural conditions shaping HIV care engagement. Two data collection components were conducted in parallel:

IDIs accompanied by a survey with validated scales to explore personal narratives and contextual factors shaping individual HIV care behaviours.19

IDIs accompanied by a survey with validated scales to explore personal narratives and contextual factors shaping individual HIV care behaviours.19

Photovoice FGDs to capture collective visual and narrative insights into community-level challenges and proposed solutions. CBPR principles guide the research process, ensuring that participants’ lived experiences shape both the data and dissemination strategies.20

Photovoice FGDs to capture collective visual and narrative insights into community-level challenges and proposed solutions. CBPR principles guide the research process, ensuring that participants’ lived experiences shape both the data and dissemination strategies.20

The IDI component was grounded in an individual-focused qualitative methodology that aimed to explore personal stories, behavioural patterns and nuanced emotional responses among PLWH. These interviews provided a confidential space for participants to reflect on how their daily environments, marked by varying levels of perceived neighbourhood disorder, crime and transportation barriers, influence their health decisions, including ART adherence and clinical attendance. This approach allows for in-depth exploration of individualised experiences of fear, resilience, coping and adaptation that may not emerge in group settings.

The photovoice component follows a CBPR framework, which emphasises equitable collaboration between researchers and participants and is grounded in core goals such as co-learning, mutual benefit, shared decision-making and community empowerment.20 Through participant-generated photographs and facilitated dialogue, this method captures collective interpretations of structural and environmental barriers to HIV care. Participants engage not only in identifying challenges but also in interpreting findings and contributing to dissemination through a community photo exhibition. This is consistent with CBPR principles of shared decision-making and co-learning, this photovoice component incorporates structured participant involvement through participant-generated photographs, collaborative theme identification and co-interpretation of findings, and public engagement through a community photo exhibition that engages stakeholders and community members in dialogue around the study’s findings. The photovoice participatory process prioritises locally meaningful insights and supports knowledge translation and advocacy.

Theoretical frameworks

This study is informed by two theories. The SET emphasises that individual health behaviours are shaped by multiple layers of influence, including interpersonal, community, institutional and policy-level factors.21 This theory supports the examination of how environmental exposures (at the community level), such as perceived neighbourhood disorder, fear of crime or transportation, interact with personal decision-making around HIV care. The BWT, originally a criminological concept, suggests that minor signs of neglect and disorder, if unaddressed, can create an environment where more serious crimes feel permissible.22 When applied to health, neglected minor signs of non-healthy behaviour within an environment can lead to a decline in healthy behaviours, creating an environment conducive to negative health outcomes. Combining SET and BWT, this study provides a structure to understand how the physical and social environment jointly shape HIV care.

Study setting

The study was conducted at Kenyatta National Hospital (KNH) in Nairobi, Kenya.23 Kenya’s national languages are both English and Swahili. KNH is the country’s largest referral and teaching hospital and provides comprehensive HIV services through its Comprehensive Care Centre (CCC).24 The hospital’s wide catchment area and diverse patient population make it an ideal setting for understanding structural and environmental barriers in HIV care.24

Sample size and composition

The IDI component included approximately 20 PLWH, stratified by HIV viral suppression status (suppressed vs unsuppressed), age group (18–24 years and >24 years) and gender (male and female). The photovoice component included a separate and distinct group of approximately 20 PLWH, organised into four focus groups of five to six participants each using the same stratification criteria. Participants did not take part in both components.

A group size of five to six participants is consistent with qualitative research guidance recommending smaller focus groups for in-depth, participatory and emotionally nuanced discussions.25 In photovoice methodology, where each participant presents multiple images and engages in structured dialogue, smaller groups allow sufficient time for detailed photo discussion, promote equitable participation, reduce participant fatigue and facilitate consensus-building.26

Stratification by age group, viral suppression status and gender is applied across both components to ensure inclusion of participants with differing clinical status and developmental stage. These characteristics may influence how individuals perceive and interact with their neighbourhood environments, experience transportation barriers and navigate HIV care.

Targeting approximately 20 participants per component aligns with qualitative methodological guidance indicating that this range is generally sufficient to achieve thematic depth and data saturation in qualitative studies with defined eligibility criteria and relatively homogeneous populations.27 Participant eligibility criteria are detailed in table 1.

Recruitment strategy

Participants were identified through routine clinical visits in collaboration with KNH leadership and healthcare staff. Recruitment flyers (in English and Swahili) with general information were distributed by KNH CCC staff. Interested individuals were privately referred to the study team for further information and consent.

Data collection

Participant recruitment and data collection were conducted between 13 January 2025 and 30 June 2025.

They then proceeded to the IDI that included the following topics: the perception of their neighbourhood physical condition, social environment, safety and crime, HIV care routine and transport. The interviews were conducted by trained interviewers and audio recorded for transcription. Interviewers received training in human subjects’ protection, confidentiality, culturally responsive and trauma-informed interviewing and standardised use of the semi-structured interview guide. After completion of the interview, the participant’s most recent viral load was abstracted from their clinical record to contextualise their reported experiences. Participants provided written consent for abstraction of their most recent viral load result from their clinical record; in collaboration with authorised KNH CCC staff, the study team abstracted only this single clinical data point in accordance with institutional data protection procedures. All these procedures were completed within a single visit. Participants received 600 KES (Kenyan shilling) (~5 US$) compensation at the end of the of the interview. Compensation amounts were determined in collaboration with KNH collaborators and are consistent with local ethical guidelines and compensation norms for similar research in Kenya.

Photovoice focus groups

Participants in the photovoice component were invited to attend four photovoice FGD sessions in small, gender-balanced (male and female) groups of five to six individuals. Each photovoice FGD session was conducted by trained facilitators.28 Facilitators were trained in qualitative focus group facilitation, CBPR methodology, research ethics and confidentiality, trauma-informed engagement and ethical management of participant-generated visual data. Following informed consent, participants began with a training photovoice FGD session (session 1) focused on research ethics, safety of taking photographs, how to take good photographs with their smartphones and a discussion of the research topic and questions. If any of the participants did not have a smartphone, they were provided with one for the photo taking assignment. Each participant was asked to take two to three photographs over a 7-day period capturing their perceptions of neighbourhood dynamics and transportation as they relate to HIV care. After the photo collection phase, participants returned for a series of three group discussions, spaced approximately 1 week apart. In photovoice FGD session 2, participants sent all the photos to the study’s password protected phone. Then participants completed a SHOWeD form (online supplemental appendix C – SHOWeD form) for each photograph, shared their photos in a facilitated group dialogue and discussed initial themes emerging from their individual photos. In photovoice FGD session 3, participants identified top photos through group consensus, refined key consensus themes and co-developed a group statement about the research topic. ‘Top photos’ refers to photos selected through participant consensus based on relevance to the research question, representativeness of shared experiences and perceived importance for advocacy or dissemination. In photovoice FGD session 3, participants reviewed initial themes generated from individual photographs and collaboratively grouped, compared and refined them into higher-order consensus themes. This process involves identifying overlaps, clarifying meanings and consolidating related ideas to capture underlying mechanisms across participants’ experiences. Consensus was achieved through facilitated discussion rather than formal voting. In photovoice FGD session 4, they reflected on the overall process and collaboratively planned a community photo exhibit. The community photo exhibit served as a knowledge translation and advocacy event, inviting community members, community stakeholders, healthcare providers and policymakers to engage with participant-generated photographs and themes. Participants were invited to attend and collaborated in determining which images and narratives are displayed, consistent with CBPR principles of shared decision-making and community engagement.

Each photovoice FGD session was conducted in private rooms, audio recorded, lasted approximately 2–4 hours and concluded with participant compensation of 600 KES (~US$5) each. Participants attended four facilitated photovoice FGD sessions, the four photovoice FGD sessions were scheduled about 7 days apart which allowed participants sufficient time for photo-taking and reflection between meetings, while providing a structured, iterative process for training, theme development, consensus building and preparation for the community exhibit. Photos were interpreted using the SHOWeD form26 (See, Happening, Our lives, Why, Do), a method used to describe photographs. This method helps participants interpret their photographs in a structured way, prompting them to describe what they see, explain what is happening in the photo, connect it to broader community experiences, identify underlying causes and propose potential actions. In applying this framework, participants collaboratively unpack the social and structural dimensions of their lived environments and how these influence their health behaviours and decisions. A photo exhibition concluded the study to share insights with community members, policymakers and healthcare providers.

Data analysis

We will use MAXQDA software29 to manage, code and analyse all qualitative data, including transcripts from IDIs and photovoice group discussions. MAXQDA was selected because it allows simultaneous analysis of text and visual data, enabling us to code and interpret participant photographs from the photovoice component alongside transcripts. Interview and photovoice transcripts in addition to photographs were transcribed verbatim and imported into MAXQDA for line-by-line open coding. A codebook will be developed both deductively based on our theoretical frameworks (SET and BWT) and inductively based on emerging patterns from the data. The initial coding process will be conducted by two independent coders to enhance reliability. Qualitative data will be analysed using thematic analysis informed by principles of grounded theory, including iterative coding and constant comparison. This approach supports identification of patterns across narratives while remaining theoretically informed by SET and BWT. The thematic analysis will be guided by paradigms, tailored to each qualitative method.

For IDIs, analysis will draw on an interpretivist theory approach,30 emphasising how individuals interpret and assign meaning to their lived experiences with neighbourhood environments, transportation and HIV care. Survey responses accompanying IDIs will be scored according to published scoring procedures for each scale. Scores will be summarised descriptively and categorised (eg, low, moderate, high) where appropriate. During qualitative analysis, narratives will be examined across participants with similar score profiles to explore patterns of convergence, divergence or expansion between quantitative categorisations and lived experiences. This comparative approach supports triangulation by assessing how standardised perceptions correspond with emergent qualitative themes.

For photovoice FGD sessions, analysis will be guided by a constructivist grounded theory approach,31 recognising the co-construction of knowledge between participants and researchers. SHOWeD responses will be included as analytical memos linked to specific images, and thematic codes will be applied across both visual and narrative data. Within each photovoice FGD group (five to six participants), participants generated initial themes based on their photographs and collaboratively refined those into higher-order consensus themes. Those themes reflected group-level interpretations. All photovoice transcripts and associated photographs from the four groups will then be imported into MAXQDA and formally coded by two independent researchers. This coding will allow systematic comparison across groups to identify broader cross-group patterns and overarching themes among all 20 participants. Thus, participant-generated themes operate at the group level, while researcher coding synthesises findings across all groups to produce an integrated analysis.

This dual analytical approach, interpretivist for IDIs and constructivist for photovoice, ensures that both individual meaning-making and collectively constructed perspectives are captured within the multimodal design. Data will be reviewed iteratively, with coding discrepancies resolved through discussion and refinement of code definitions. Analytical memos will be used throughout to track the evolution of themes and theoretical insights. Themes will be compared across participant characteristics (eg, viral suppression status, gender) to identify patterns of meaning related to neighbourhood perceptions and HIV care engagement.

Patient and public involvement

This study is built on a CBPR approach. PLWH were involved in data generation through photovoice and are being engaged in interpretation of visual narratives and development of dissemination strategies, including a photo exhibition.

Ethics and dissemination

This study has received ethical approval from the KNH-UoN Ethics and Research Committee (P635/08/2024) and the Stanford University Institutional Review Board (eProtocol #: 77260). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and a photo exhibition.

Discussion

This study uses a theory-informed, multimodal qualitative design to examine how socio-environmental and structural factors impact HIV care. The integration of the SET and BWT allows us to examine both proximal and distal drivers of HIV care, while the CBPR approach ensures that community voices are central to shaping the interpretation and dissemination of findings. We anticipate that findings from this study will inform the development of interventions tailored to disordered environments for people living with HIV. Additionally, the participatory dissemination strategy, including a public photo exhibition, may stimulate dialogue among stakeholders, healthcare providers and policymakers to collaboratively address barriers to HIV care in disordered settings.

One key strength of this study is the use of triangulated qualitative methods, semi-structured interviews supplemented by a validated survey (Perceived Neighbourhood Observation Scale, Adherence Self-Efficacy Scale, Perceived Fear of Crime Scale), and participatory visual data collection through photovoice. This design enables integration of internal psychological dimensions with external environmental exposures, enhancing the validity of insights. This dual approach enables the exploration of internalised beliefs and behaviours alongside shared community experiences, providing a comprehensive picture of how environmental disorder and insecurity may erode self-efficacy and disrupt HIV care. The dual-theoretical (SET and BWT) framework further offers a robust structure for interpreting the multi-level impacts of neighbourhood environments. Importantly, this approach is adaptable to similar contexts where structural and social environments influence health, such as examining the effects of food deserts on diabetes management, housing insecurity on mental health or transportation barriers on maternal care access. In addition, photovoice empowers participants to engage as co-researchers and storytellers, which enhances the cultural validity and policy relevance of study findings.

This study is not without limitations. As a qualitative study, findings will not be generalisable beyond the study population. As a qualitative study, the findings are not intended to be generalisable beyond the study population; rather, the goal is to generate contextually grounded and transferable insights relevant to similar socio-environmental settings. Purposive sampling, while suitable for qualitative research, may not capture all perspectives within PLWH populations. Participants’ willingness to speak openly may be influenced by social desirability bias, particularly in group settings. Moreover, while the study design emphasises participant engagement and co-production of knowledge, logistical constraints may limit the degree of sustained participant engagement in later stages of the research. To minimise attrition, we used participant-centred scheduling for both IDIs and photovoice FGD sessions, reminder messages, compensation per interview or photovoice FGD session and consistent follow-up communication between photovoice FGD sessions, and we documented reasons for missed photovoice FGD sessions when feasible.

Despite these limitations, this study represents an innovative and theory-informed approach to examining socio-environmental and structural impacts on HIV care. It offers a model for participatory, theory-informed research that integrates visual, narrative and contextual data to inform interventions and practice considering neighbourhood environments.
