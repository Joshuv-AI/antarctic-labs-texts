Analysis of a one-dimensional biofilm model
Patrick Guidotti, Christoph Walker

Abstract
. In this paper a reduced one-dimensional moving boundary model is studied that describes the evolution of a biofilm driven by the presence of a reaction limiting substrate. Global well-posedness is established for the resulting parabolic free boundary value problem in strong form in Sobolev spaces and for a quasi-stationary approximation in spaces of classical regularity. The general existence results are complemented by results about the qualitative properties of solutions including the existence, in general, and, additionally, the uniqueness and stability of non-trivial equilibria, in a special case.

1.
Introduction

Microbial systems are the focus of an increasing body of research due
to their prevalence in many important fields of application. Microbes
tend to live in localized communities called biofilms. Most of the
research dedicated to biofilms is confined to the biological realm or
to the specific area of application. The systematic development of
mathematical models and their analysis is still quite
limited. Inspired by the review article
[
4
]
, we consider a
one-dimensional toy model for the evolution of a single biofilm in a
single reaction limiting susbtrate (e.g. oxygen) introduced in
that article. Even in its simplicity the model takes the form of a
non-standard moving boundary problem with interesting features. The
goal of this paper is to provide a rigorous analysis of the system
that include well-posedness as well as qualitative properties such as
global existence and long-time behavior. The model tracks the
substrate, via its concentration
c(t,z)
everywhere in the upper
half-space (or half-line since we shall assume homogeneity in the
other variables). Its concentration can be assumed constant in the
bulk fluid region far enough from the biofilm, where a well mixed
regime is typically observed. The biofilm is simply modeled by the
height (depth)
h(t)\geq 0
to which it has grown. Within the biofilm and in a
diffusive boundary layer on top of it, the substrate can freely
diffuse with (slightly) different diffusivities. It is assumed, based
on observations, that the diffusive boundary layer have a given thickness

L>0
. At the bottom a wall confines the system and a no-flux
condition is enforced. At the interface
z=h(t)
continuity of the
substrate concentration and flux is imposed along with a dynamic
boundary condition for the evolution of the biofilm thickness. The
latter captures the modeling assumption that the speed of the
interface coincides with the one generated by the pressure change due
to the biofilm growth or decay. More specifically, let
L,c_{*}>0
,

\kappa,\kappa_{L}>0
,
\varepsilon_{L},\varepsilon>0
, and consider the
system

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{L}c_{t}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{L}c_{zz},

\displaystyle h(t)<z<h(t)+L,\quad t>0,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon c_{t}

\displaystyle=\kappa c_{zz}-r(c),

\displaystyle 0<z<h(t),

(1.1c)

\displaystyle c(t,h(t)+L)

\displaystyle=c_{*},\quad c_{z}(t,0)=0,

\displaystyle t>0,

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\llbracket c\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\llbracket\varkappa c_{z}\rrbracket=0,

\displaystyle z=h(t),

(1.1e)

\displaystyle c(0,z)

\displaystyle=c_{0}(z),\>

\displaystyle 0<z<h_{0}+L,

(1.1f)

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{h}g\big(r(c(t,z))\big)\,\mathrm{d}z,

\displaystyle t>0,

(1.1g)

\displaystyle h(0)

\displaystyle=h_{0}>0,

for the unknown biofilm height
h=h(t)
and substrate
c=c(t,z)
. Here
we have set

\varkappa(z)=\begin{cases}\kappa_{L},&z\in\bigl(h(t),h(t)+L\bigr)\\
\kappa,&z\in\bigl(0,h(t)\bigr),\end{cases}

for the substrate diffusivities in the diffusive layer

[h(t)<z<h(t)+L]
and in the biofilm
[0<z<h(t)]
, respectively. The
function
r
models the substrate consumption that drives the biofilm
growth, itself captured by a function
g
of
r
. A common choice for
the function
g
is given by
g(r)=\alpha(r-b)
, where
\alpha>0

represents a yield coefficient and
b
the basic substinence level of
the biofilm. Minimal assumptions on the function
r
are that

r(0)=0
, modeling the fact that no substrate is consumed if none is
present, and that
r(c)>0
for
c>0
indicating that available
substrate is always used. The function
r
is also typically made to reflect a
saturation at increasing levels of the substrate concentration. We
shall not make use of this additional structure in this analysis but
we will assume that
r^{\prime}(c)>0
for
c>0
, which does not contradict the
saturation assumption and simply encodes the fact the consumption
increases with substrate concentration. The assumptions about the
substrate beyond the top diffusive layer and the bottom wall are
reflected in the boundary conditions (
1.1c
), while continuity
across the interface is encoded in the conditions (
1.1d
).
The kynetic condition (
1.1f
) for
h
is derived from an
incompressibility assumption for the fluid (mostly
water) and Darcy’s law connecting velocity and pressure. Full details
can be found in
[
4
]
or the earlier
[
2
]
. The remaining
equations (
1.1e
) and (
1.1g
) fix an arbitrary initial
configuration.
If diffusion in the diffusive biofilm layer is assumed to take place
at a much faster time scale than biofilm growth, it can be assumed
that
\varepsilon_{L}=0
in (
1.1a
). In that case,
c
is affine on

(h,h+L)
and it follows from (
1.1c
) and
\llbracket c\rrbracket=0
on

that

is determined on
(h,h+L)
by

through

(1.2)

c(t,z)=\frac{c_{*}-c\bigl(t,h(t)\bigr)}{L}\big[z-h(t)\big]+c\bigl(t,h(t)\bigr).

Using that
\llbracket\varkappa c_{z}\rrbracket=0
on

then yields

c_{z}\bigl(t,h(t)-\bigr)=\frac{\kappa_{L}}{\kappa}c_{z}\bigl(t,h(t)+\bigr)=\frac{\kappa_{L}}{\kappa}\frac{c_{*}-c\bigl(t,h(t)\bigr)}{L}

and one obtains the Robin type boundary condition

(1.3)

c\bigl(t,h(t)\bigr)+\frac{L\kappa}{\kappa_{L}}c_{z}\bigl(t,h(t)\bigr)=c_{*},

for

where here and in the following

c_{z}\bigl(t,h(t)\bigr)=c_{z}\bigl(t,h(t)-\bigr)
. In summary, assuming
that
\varepsilon_{L}=0
we obtain the following reduced model

(1.4a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon c_{t}

\displaystyle=\kappa c_{zz}-r(c),

\displaystyle 0<z<h(t),\quad t>0,

(1.4b)

\displaystyle c\bigl(t,h(t)\bigr)+\frac{L\kappa}{\kappa_{L}}c_{z}\bigl(t,h(t)\bigr)

\displaystyle=c_{*},\quad c_{z}(t,0)=0,

\displaystyle t>0,

(1.4c)

\displaystyle c(0,z)

\displaystyle=c_{0}(z),

\displaystyle z\in(0,h_{0}),

(1.4d)

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{h}g\big(r(c(t,z))\big)\,\mathrm{d}z,

\displaystyle t>0,

(1.4e)

\displaystyle h(0)

\displaystyle=h_{0}.

on which we shall focus in the following. Throughout, if not stated
otherwise, we shall assume that

(1.5)

g\in{\rm C}^{1-}(\mathbb{R}),\qquad r\in{\rm C}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\ \text{ with }\ sr(s)>0\ \text{ for }\ s\not=0,

where

refers to Lipschitz continuity. In fact, in all of our analysis only the restrictions of
r
to
[0,c_{*}]
and of

2.
The Evolution Problem

In this section we focus on the full evolutionary system
(
1.4
) and establish the existence of a unique
global strong solution. We then derive some qualitative properties of
the solution including the asymptotic extinction of the biofilm when
the growth rate is negative, the boundedness of the substrate
gradient for initial data with bounded gradient as well as the
preservation of susbstrate monotonicity during the evolution.

2.1.

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{h(t)^{2}}v_{y}(t,0)=0,

\displaystyle t>0,

(2.2c)

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{L}}{Lh(t)}v(t,1)+\frac{\kappa}{h(t)^{2}}v_{y}(t,1)=0,

\displaystyle t>0,

\displaystyle v(0,y)=v_{0}(y):=c_{*}-c_{0}(yh_{0}),

2.2.
Qualitative Aspects

The subsequent result says that the biofilm disappears over time for a
negative biofilm growth rate. In this case, asymptotically the domain

[0,h(t)+L]
collapses to the diffusive bulk layer
[0,L]

In this section we give a more detailed account of the dynamics
focusing on a quasi-steady approximation of (

If diffusion inside the biofilm is also assumed to take place
at a much faster time scale than biofilm growth (as is observed
experimentally), it can be assumed
that
\varepsilon=0
in (

) and the system
becomes

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\kappa c_{zz}

\displaystyle=r(c),

\displaystyle 0<z<h(t),\quad t>0,

(3.1b)

\displaystyle c\bigl(t,h(t)\bigr)+\frac{L\kappa}{\kappa_{L}}c_{z}\bigl(t,h(t)\bigr)

\displaystyle=c_{*},\quad c_{z}(t,0)=0,

\displaystyle t>0,

(3.1c)

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{h}g\big(r(c(t,z))\big)\,\mathrm{d}z,

\displaystyle t>0,

(3.1d)

\displaystyle h(0)

\displaystyle=h_{0}.

For this simplified model, we are able to provide a fairly complete
picture of the qualitative aspects of the dynamics under suitable
assumptions on the biofilm growth rate
g
and the substrate
consumption rate
r
.

As in the previous section it is convenient to introduce dimensionless
variables via

so that the moving boundary problem (

) is equivalent to the
following initial (fixed) boundary value problem

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{h^{2}(t)}u_{yy}

\displaystyle=r(u),

\displaystyle 0<y<1,\quad t>0,

(3.2b)

\displaystyle u_{y}(t,0)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle t>0,

(3.2c)

\displaystyle u(t,1)+\frac{L\kappa}{\kappa_{L}h(t)}u_{y}(t,1)

\displaystyle=c_{*},

\displaystyle t>0,

(3.2d)

\displaystyle h_{t}(t)

\displaystyle=h(t)\int_{0}^{1}g\big(r(u(t,y))\big)\,\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle t>0,

\displaystyle h(0)

In order to analyze the dynamics we establish useful properties of the solution

of the sub-problem

3.1.

Proposition 3.2

Assume (

(3.3b)

\displaystyle u_{y}(0)

\displaystyle=0,\quad u(1)+\frac{L\kappa}{\kappa_{L}h}u_{y}(1)=c_{*}.

\displaystyle u(y)=c_{*}-\frac{Lh}{\kappa_{L}}\int_{0}^{1}r\bigl(u(\eta)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\eta-\frac{h^{2}}{\kappa}\int_{y}^{1}\int_{0}^{\eta}r\bigl(u(\rho)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\eta,\quad y\in[0,1].

Step 1.
We define

by definition using the fact that the function

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{h^{2}}u_{yy}(y)

Next, we note that

Step 2.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume (
1.5
) with
r^{\prime}(s)>0
for
s\in[0,c_{*}]
.
Then
u[h]\to 0
uniformly
in any compact subinterval of
[0,1)
as
h\to\infty
.

Proof.

First notice from (
3.5a
) that
0<v(x)\leq v(0)<c_{*}
for every

x\in\left[0,1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right]
. By assumption there is

\alpha>0
such that
r^{\prime}(s)\geq\alpha
for
s\in[0,c_{*}]
.
Setting
w=v_{x}
and noticing from (
3.5b
) that
w\leq 0
in

\left[0,1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right]
we obtain by differentiating
the equation for
v
that

\displaystyle\kappa w_{xx}=r^{\prime}(v)w\geq\alpha w,\quad x\in\bigl(0,1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\bigr),

\displaystyle w(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})=0,\quad w(0)=-\frac{c_{*}-v(0)}{L\kappa}\kappa_{L}.

Introducing the differential operator
\mathcal{L}:=-\kappa\partial_{x}^{2}+\alpha
so that
\mathcal{L}w\leq 0
in
\left[0,1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right]
, it follows from the comparison principle that
w\leq\overline{w}
in
\left[0,1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right]
, where
\overline{w}
solves

\mathcal{L}\overline{w}=0,\quad x\in(0,1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}),\qquad\overline{w}(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})=0,\quad\overline{w}(0)=-\frac{c_{*}-v(0)}{L\kappa}\kappa_{L},

that is,

\overline{w}(x)=A_{\alpha}e^{-x\sqrt{\alpha}}+B_{\alpha}e^{x\sqrt{\alpha}},\quad x\in\left[0,1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right],

with

A_{\alpha}=\frac{v(0)-c_{*}}{\beta}\frac{1}{1-e^{-2\sqrt{\alpha/\varepsilon}}},\qquad B_{\alpha}=\frac{c_{*}-v(0)}{\beta}\frac{e^{-2\sqrt{\alpha/\varepsilon}}}{1-e^{-2\sqrt{\alpha/\varepsilon}}}.

In the old variables we see that

\displaystyle 0\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon}u_{y}(y)\leq\frac{c_{*}-v(0)}{\beta}\frac{1}{1-e^{-2\sqrt{\alpha/\varepsilon}}}\Big\{e^{-(1-y)\sqrt{\alpha/\varepsilon}}+e^{-(1+y)\sqrt{\alpha/\varepsilon}}\Bigr\}

for
y\in[0,1]
. This shows, that
u_{y}[h](y)\to 0

uniformly with respect to
y\in[0,1-\delta]
as
h\to\infty
for
each
\delta\in(0,1)
. Since

u_{y}[h](y)\geq\frac{h^{2}}{\kappa}yr\big(u[h](0)\big),\quad y\in[0,1],

thanks to (
3.3a
) and the monotonicity of
r
, it thus follows
that
r(u[h](0))\to 0
as
h\to\infty
, hence
u[h](0)\to 0
as
h\to\infty
. Consequently, it follows from

0<u[h](0)\leq u[h](y)=u[h](0)+\int_{0}^{y}u_{y}[h](\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta

that
u[h]\to 0
uniformly
in any compact subinterval of
[0,1)
as
h\to\infty
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The result contained in the above lemma describes a behavior that is
related to what is known in the applied literature as the existence of
micro-environments, in this case a sharp transition between regions of
high and low bio-activity. See
[
4
, Section 3.2]
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Assume (
1.5
) with
r^{\prime}(s)>0
for
s\in[0,c_{*}]
. Let
g\in{\rm C}^{1-}\bigl([0,\infty)\bigr)
satisfy
g(0)<0
and

g\bigl(r(c_{*})\bigr)>0
. Then system (
3.8
) has
a solution, i.e. there is an equilibrium solution for the original
quasi-stationary system (
3.1
).

Proof.

Since
g\bigl(r(c_{*})\bigr)>0
there exists
a\in(0,c_{*})
such that

g(r(s))>0
for
s\in[a,c_{*}]
. From Lemma
3.3
we infer that
there exists
h_{0}>0
such that
u[h](y)\in[a,c_{*}]
for each
y\in[0,1]
and
h\in(0,h_{0})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}g\big(r(u[h](y)\big)\big)\,\mathrm{d}y>0,\quad h\in(0,h_{0}).

On the other hand, since
g\big(r(0)\big)=g(0)<0
, there is
b>0
such
that
g\bigl(r(s)\bigr)\leq g(0)/2<0
for
s\in[0,b]
. Fix
\delta\in(0,1)

with

(1-\delta)\frac{g(0)}{2}+\delta\|g\|_{\infty,[0,r(c_{*})]}<0.

Lemma
3.4
implies that there exists
h_{1}>0
such that

u[h](y)\leq b
for each
y\in[0,1-\delta]
and
h\geq h_{1}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}g\bigl(r(u[h](y)\big)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{1-\delta}+\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\right)g\bigl(r\bigl(u[h](y)\bigr)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq(1-\delta)\frac{g(0)}{2}+\delta\|g\|_{\infty,[0,r(c_{*})]}<0

for
h\geq h_{1}
.
Since
u[h]
depends continuously on
h
by Proposition
3.2
,
the intermediate value theorem now yields the existence of a zero of

\int_{0}^{1}g\bigl(r\bigl(u[h](y)\bigr)\bigr)\,dy
, i.e. of an equilibrium.
∎

It turns out that more can be said in the particular case when
g
has
the special form

(3.10)

\displaystyle g(s):=\alpha(s-b),\quad s\in[0,r(c_{*})],

for some
\alpha,b>0
. Note that, for any solution
(c,h)
of
(
3.8
), assumption (
3.10
) implies that

\int_{0}^{h}g\big(r(c(z))\big)\,\mathrm{d}z=\alpha\int_{0}^{h}r(c(z))\,\mathrm{d}z-\alpha bh=\alpha\big(\kappa c_{z}(h)-bh\big).

Therefore, (
3.8
) with
g
given by (
3.10
) is equivalent to
finding
h>0
and
c\in{\rm C}^{2}\bigl([0,h]\bigr)
satisfying

(3.11a)

\displaystyle\kappa c_{zz}(z)

\displaystyle=r\bigl(c(z)\bigr),\quad 0<z<h,

(3.11b)

\displaystyle c_{z}(0)=0,\quad c(h)

\displaystyle=c_{*}-\frac{Lb}{\kappa_{L}}h,\quad c_{z}(h)=\frac{b}{\kappa}h.

While existence of an equilibrium has already been established, a
shooting argument relying on the following lemma and the special
choice of
g
also yield uniqueness of the equilibrium and its stability.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
r\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be bounded, strictly increasing,
and satisfy
sr(s)\geq 0
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Given any

c_{0}\geq 0
there is a unique global solution
c(\cdot,c_{0})\in{\rm C}^{2}\bigl([0,\infty)\bigr)
to the initial value
problem

(3.12a)

\displaystyle\kappa\,c_{zz}

\displaystyle=r(c),\quad z>0,

(3.12b)

\displaystyle c(0)

\displaystyle=c_{0},\quad c_{z}(0)=0.

The mappings
c(\cdot,c_{0})
and
c_{z}(\cdot,c_{0})
are increasing and

c(\cdot,0)\equiv 0
. In addition,

\bigl[c_{0}\mapsto c(\cdot,c_{0})\bigr]\in{\rm C}^{1}\bigl([0,\infty),{\rm C}^{2}([0,R])\bigr)

for each
R>0
. Moreover,
w:=\frac{\partial}{\partial c_{0}}c(\cdot,c_{0})\in{\rm C}^{2}\bigl([0,\infty)\bigr)
satisfies

(3.13a)

\displaystyle\kappa\,w_{zz}

\displaystyle=r^{\prime}\bigl(c(\cdot,c_{0})\bigr)w(z),\quad z>0,

(3.13b)

\displaystyle w(0)

\displaystyle=1,\quad w_{z}(0)=0,

and it holds that
w(z)\geq 1
as well as
w_{z}(z)\geq 0
for
z\geq 0
.
The function
M
, defined by

M(z,c_{0}):=\left[\frac{Lb}{\kappa_{L}}+c_{z}(z,c_{0})\right]w_{z}(z)-\frac{r\bigl(c(z,c_{0})\bigr)-b}{\kappa}w(z),\quad z\geq 0,

is strictly increasing with respect to
z\geq 0
. In particular, for
r(c_{0})\leq b
it holds
that
M(z,c_{0})>M(0,c_{0})\geq 0
for
z>0
.

Proof.

The boundedness of
r\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
ensures that, for each

c_{0}\geq 0
, there is a unique global solution
c(\cdot,c_{0})\in{\rm C}^{2}\bigl([0,\infty)\bigr)
of the initial value
problem (
3.12
) which satisfies

c(z,c_{0})=c_{0}+\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{0}^{z}(z-\sigma)r\bigl(c(\sigma,c_{0})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,\quad z\geq 0.

Note that
c(\cdot,0)\equiv 0
since
r(0)=0
. Similarly as in the
proof of Proposition
3.2
, the implicit function theorem implies
that

\bigl[c_{0}\mapsto c(\cdot,c_{0})\bigr]\in{\rm C}^{1}\bigl([0,\infty),{\rm C}^{2}\bigl([0,R]\bigr)\bigr)

for each
R>0
. In order to include
c_{0}=0
notice that the initial
value problem (
3.12
) can be solved also for
c_{0}<0
. Let

c_{0}\geq 0
be fixed. Since
s\,r(s)\geq 0
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
and

c(0,c_{0})=c_{0}\geq 0
, it readily follows from (
3.12
) that

c(\cdot,c_{0})
is convex and hence that the maps

c(\cdot,c_{0})
and
c_{z}(\cdot,c_{0})
are increasing. In particular,

c(z,c_{0})\geq 0
for
z\geq 0
.

Differentiating (
3.12
) with respect to
c_{0}
we see that

w:=\frac{\partial}{\partial c_{0}}c(\cdot,c_{0})\in{\rm C}^{2}\bigl([0,\infty)\bigr)

satisfies (
3.13
). Hence, since
r^{\prime}\bigl(c(z,c_{0})\bigr)\geq 0
as

c(z,c_{0})\geq c_{0}\geq 0
for
z\geq 0
and
w(0)=1
, we conclude that

w
is (at least initially) convex. It follows that
w_{z}(z)\geq 0
and

w(z)\geq w(0)=1
for
z\geq 0
small and then for all
z\geq 0
by
monotonicity. Hence (
3.12a
) and (
3.13a
) imply that

\frac{d}{dz}M(z,c_{0})=\frac{Lb}{\kappa_{L}\kappa}r^{\prime}\bigl(c(z,c_{0})\bigr)w(z)+\frac{b}{\kappa}w_{z}(z)>0,\quad z>0,

which makes
M
strictly increasing. It remains to notice that, for
r(c_{0})\leq b
,

M(0,c_{0})=-\frac{1}{\kappa}\left[r(c_{0})-b\right]\geq 0

due to (
3.12b
) and (
3.13b
).
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Assume (
1.5
) with
r^{\prime}(s)>0
for
s\in[0,c_{*}]
. It holds:

(i)

If
g\in{\rm C}^{1-}\bigl([0,\infty)\bigr)
is
strictly increasing and
g\bigl(r(c_{*})\bigr)\leq 0
, then the equilibrium
problem (
3.8
) has no solution.

(ii)

If
g
is given by (
3.10
) and
r(c_{*})>b
, then the
equilibrium problem (
3.8
) (or, equivalently, (
3.11
)) has a unique solution
(h_{e},c_{e})

with
h_{e}>0
and
c_{e}\in{\rm C}^{2}([0,h_{e}])
.

Proof.

(i) If
(c,h)
is a solution of (
3.8
), then
c
is
non-decreasing and
c(z)<c_{*}
for
z\in[0,h)
. It follows that

g\bigl(r\bigl(c(z)\bigr)\bigr)<0
for
z\in[0,h)
thanks to
Proposition
3.2
, so that

\int_{0}^{h}g\bigl(r\bigl(c(z)\bigr)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z<0
and,
consequently, that there is no equilibrium in this case.

(ii) In order to establish that there exists a unique
equilibrium we use a shooting argument. To this end, let

c(\cdot,c_{0})\in{\rm C}^{2}([0,\infty))
be the unique solution of
the initial value problem (
3.12
) provided by Lemma
3.7
for

c_{0}\geq 0
. Here we may assume that
r
satisfies the assumptions
of Lemma
3.7
as only
r\big|_{[0,c_{*}]}
plays a role,
see (
3.5a
).
To enforce the boundary conditions (
3.11b
) we define the
functions
A
and
B
by

A(z,c_{0}):=c(z,c_{0})+\frac{Lb}{\kappa_{L}}z-c_{*},\qquad B(z,c_{0}):=c_{z}(z,c_{0})-\frac{b}{\kappa}z,

for
c_{0},z\geq 0
. Consider
c_{0}\in[0,c_{*})
. Using Lemma

3.7
it follows that
A(0,c_{0})=c_{0}-c_{*}<0
and that

A(\cdot,c_{0})
is increasing and
unbounded above. Thus, there is a unique
h(c_{0})>0
such that

A\bigl(h(c_{0}),c_{0}\bigr)=0
. Since

\partial_{z}A(h(c_{0}),c_{0})=c_{z}(h(c_{0}),c_{0})+\frac{Lb}{\kappa_{L}}\geq\frac{Lb}{\kappa_{L}}>0,

it follows from the implicit function theorem that

h\in{\rm C}^{1}\bigl([0,c_{*})\bigr)
with
h(0)=\kappa_{L}c_{*}/(Lb)
.
Then

B\bigl(h(\cdot),\cdot\bigr)\in{\rm C}^{1}\bigl([0,c_{*})\bigr).

Since
r
is strictly increasing and
r(0)=0
, there is a unique

\underline{c}\in(0,c_{*})
such that
r(\underline{c})=b
. Thus, for

c_{0}\in(\underline{c},c_{*})
we have that

c(z,c_{0})\geq c(0,c_{0})=c_{0}>\underline{c},\quad z\geq 0,

and hence
r\bigl(c(z,c_{0})\bigr)>b
for
z>0
since

r
is increasing. This and (
3.12a
) yield that

\partial_{z}B(z,c_{0})=c_{zz}(z,c_{0})-\frac{b}{\kappa}=\frac{1}{\kappa}\big[r\bigl(c(z,c_{0})\bigr)-b\big]>0,\quad z>0,

while

. Consequently, we have that
B\bigl(h(c_{0}),c_{0}\bigr)>0
for
c_{0}\in(\underline{c},c_{*})
. On the other
hand we have that

B(h(0),0)=-\frac{\kappa_{L}c_{*}}{L\kappa}<0,

and, since
B\bigl(h(\cdot),\cdot\bigr)
depends continuously on
its argument
c_{0}
, there must exist some
{\sf c}_{e}\in(0,\underline{c})

such that
B\bigl(h({\sf c}_{e}),{\sf c}_{e}\bigr)=0
. By construction, we have
that
c(\cdot,{\sf c}_{e})\in{\rm C}^{2}\bigl([0,h_{{\sf c}_{e}}]\bigr)

and that
(h({\sf c}_{e}),c\bigl(\cdot,{\sf c}_{e})\bigr)

solves (
3.11
) (where
h({\sf c}_{e})>0
). In fact, we claim that

B\bigl(h(\cdot),\cdot\bigr)
is strictly increasing
on
[0,\underline{c}]
so that it can only possess a single zero and
the equilibrium is thus unique. To see this, we first differentiate
the identity
A(h(c_{0}),c_{0})=0
with respect to
c_{0}
and use
Lemma
3.7
to obtain

(3.14)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{Lb}{\kappa_{L}}+c_{z}(h(c_{0}),c_{0})\right)\frac{d}{dc_{0}}h(c_{0})=-w(h(c_{0}))\leq-1,

where
w=\frac{\partial}{\partial{c_{0}}}c(\cdot,c_{0})
as in Lemma (
3.7
). Next,
we observe that

\frac{d}{dc_{0}}B\bigl(h(c_{0}),c_{0}\bigr)=w_{z}\bigl(h(c_{0})\bigr)+\left[c_{zz}\bigl(h(c_{0}),c_{0}\bigr)-\frac{b}{\kappa}\right]\frac{d}{dc_{0}}h(c_{0}),

and hence, using (

) and (

\displaystyle\left(\frac{Lb}{\kappa_{L}}+c_{z}\bigl(h(c_{0}),c_{0}\bigr)\right)\frac{d}{dc_{0}}B\bigl(h(c_{0}),c_{0}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=\left(\frac{Lb}{\kappa_{L}}+c_{z}\bigl(h(c_{0}),c_{0}\bigr)\right)w_{z}(h(c_{0}))-\frac{1}{\kappa}\bigl[r\bigl(c(h(c_{0}),c_{0})\bigr)-b\bigr]w\bigl(h(c_{0})\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=M\bigl(h(c_{0}),c_{0}\bigr)>0,

where we used that

, which was defined in Lemma

, is
strictly positive since

for

. Since Lemma

also ensures that

, we indeed have that

∎

Proposition 3.9
.

{\rm C}^{1}([0,1])
. Thus, given
t_{j}\nearrow\infty
as

j\to\infty
we may assume, without loss of generality, that there is
u^{*}\in{\rm C}^{1}([0,1])
such that
u(t_{j},\cdot)\to u^{*}
in

{\rm C}^{1}([0,1])
as
j\to\infty
. But since
h(t_{j})\to h_{e}
, as just shown above, it holds that
u^{*}=u_{e}
. Therefore, we
conclude that
u(t,\cdot)\to u_{e}
in
{\rm C}^{1}([0,1])
and,
hence, in
{\rm C}^{2}([0,1])
thanks to (
3.2a
). The
proof is finished.
∎
