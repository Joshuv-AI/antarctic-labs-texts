LNGFR promoting osteogenic differentiation of ectomesenchyme stem cells via activation of GHR-JAK-STAT/IGF1 signaling pathway
Authors: Keyu Wang, Xiaoke Zeng, Yaoguang Zhang, Yanhui Zou, Jiaqi Ye, Yeke Zhao, Haoyang Jin, Jiajun Zhang, Xin Nie, Gu Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEctomesenchymal stem cells (EMSCs) are critical for craniofacial bone development, and low-affinity nerve growth factor receptor (LNGFR) is closely associated with their stemness. However, the specific role of LNGFR in EMSC osteogenesis remains unclear. Here, we investigated this using Lngfr knockout (lngfr-/-) mice and demonstrated that lngfr-/-EMSCs exhibited decreased proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation capacities in vitro. Furthermore, impaired skeletal development and reduced mineralization were observed in lngfr-/- fetal mice in vivo. Circular RNA (circRNA) sequencing identified the growth hormone (GH) pathway as a key factor involved in LNGFR-regulated osteogenesis of EMSCs. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays further confirmed the interaction between LNGFR and growth hormone receptor (GHR). lngfr-/-suppressed GHR expression and Janus kinase 2/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (JAK2/STAT3) phosphorylation, leading to downregulation of the GH/insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) signaling pathway. Modulation of the JAK/STAT pathway affected osteogenesis, while exogenous GH rescued the osteogenic defects via the GHR/JAK-STAT/IGF-1 axis. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that LNGFR promoted EMSC osteogenesis by activating the GHR/JAK-STAT/GH-IGF-1 signaling axis, providing new insights into craniofacial development and regenerative medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05052-y.Keywords: Cranial bone development, Stem cells, LNGFR, GHR, IGF1, JAK2-STAT3

Introduction

During embryonic development, cranial neural crest stem cells migrate to the mesoderm of the maxillary process prior to neural tube closure. These cells, known as ectomesenchyme stem cells (EMSCs), derive from the neural crest and represent a major cell population that contributes to craniofacial bones, teeth, and related tissues [1]. In comparison to bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), EMSCs demonstrate a stronger tissue specificity for craniofacial osteogenesis [2]. Investigating EMSCs could contribute to addressing gaps in understanding the mechanisms of craniofacial bone formation, providing insights into embryonic bone tissue development and potential applications in regenerative medicine.

Low affinity neurotrophin receptor (LNGFR), also known as CD271 or p75NTR, serves as a receptor for nerve growth factors (NGFs) [3–5]. Several studies have demonstrated that EMSCs consistently express LNGFR, and its expression is positively correlated with their stemness properties, including self-renewal capacity and multidirectional differentiation potential [6–8]. Further evidence indicates that during osteogenic differentiation of LNGFR-positive EMSCs into osteoblasts, the expression levels of osteogenic markers such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and osteocalcin (OCN) are correlated with LNGFR expression [9]. Additionally, several studies have indicated that LNGFR may enhance the osteogenic differentiation capacity of EMSCs by activating the Wnt-β-catenin signaling pathway [10, 11]. Growth hormone (GH), a 191-amino acid single-chain polypeptide secreted by the hypothalamus, promotes bone growth and maintains bone homeostasis [12]. In addition to the classical signaling pathways, such as WNT and Hedgehog pathways [13, 14], the role of GH signaling in osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) has also been investigated [15]. Activation of GH signaling has been reportedto positively regulate the proliferation of MSCs [16]. Research suggested that activation of GH signaling pathway may inhibited lipogenic differentiation and promoted osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs [17]. Despite the similarities between BMSCs and EMSCs has been proved [18], there are fewer reports on the specific role of GH signaling in the cellular properties of EMSCs, and in particular it remains unclear whether GH signaling also plays a similar role in the osteogenic differentiation of EMSCs.

Meanwhile, GH and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1) play important roles in the early embryonic growth and development, together constituting the GH/IGF1 axis. This axis operates through dual mechanisms: GH exerts biological effects directly via binding to growth hormone receptors (GHR) and indirectly by stimulating IGF1 synthesis. Subsequently, GH-induced IGF1 binds to its receptor (IGF1R), activating downstream signaling cascades that regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and metabolic activity [10, 19, 20]. Moreover, the GH/IGF1 axis is has been implicated as a critical regulator of osteogenic differentiation [12, 21, 22]. A key downstream pathway involves the Janus kinase (JAK)–signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling axis. Upon GH binding, GHR undergoes conformational changes, recruiting and activating JAK, which subsequently phosphorylates STAT family members [23]. Importantly, the promoter region of the Runx2 gene contains a functional STAT3-binding motif. In osteoblasts, GH stimulation promotes the interaction between Runx2 and STAT3, which mayregulat the transcriptional activity of Runx2 through enhanced inhibitory interactions between Runx2 and the STAT3β isoform [24].

Notably, studies in the endocrine system have suggested a potential link between LNGFR and GH signaling. For example, Scharfmann R et al. reported that GH and prolactin upregulate LNGFR expression in rat islet cell tumor cells via GHR activation [25]. However, the specific role of LNGFR in regulating GHR expression, its impact on downstream JAK–STAT signaling, and its influence on IGF1 expression remain unclear, particularly in the context of osteogenic differentiation. Especially, the GH/IGF1 axis is a well-recognized key regulator of osteogenic differentiation. This important gap in the current understanding prompts the central question of how LNGFR modulates osteogenic differentiation by regulating the GH/IGF1 and JAK–STAT signaling pathways. In the present study, we evaluated the osteogenic differentiation capacity following LNGFR knockout in vivo and in vitro. In addition, we investigated the downstream signaling pathways involved in LNGFR-mediated osteogenic differentiation using circRNAs sequencing, followed by verification through restoration experiments conducted both in vivo and in vitro.

Methods and materials

Animals

lngfr knockout (lngfr-/-) and wild-type (WT) mice were provided by The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). The lngfr-/- mutant mice carry a targeted deletion in exon III of the lngfr locus, resulting in the absence of functional full-length LNGFR protein expression. To generate heterozygous offspring for subsequent experiments, WT mice were mated with homozygous lngfr-/- mice. The mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 40 mg/kg, followed by embryo extraction. After the procedure, the mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of a solution of 1% pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg body weight). All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of School and Stomatology Wenzhou Medical University (Project title: P75NTR: Study on the Signaling Pathways and Regulatory Mechanisms of Odontogenic Differentiation and Mineralization in Ectomesenchyme Stem Cells; Approval No. 2018012; Approval date: September 26, 2018). 2018012; Approval date: September 26, 2018).

Isolation and cultivation of EMSCs

Male lngfr-/- mice were mated with female lngfr-/- mice at a 1:2 ratio. Embryos at embryonic day 16.5 (E16.5) were harvested via abdominal surgery. Maxillary processes tissues were dissected from the embryos, minced, and digested with trypsin for 7 min. Following centrifugation at 5 min, the resulting cell pellet was resuspended and seeded into culture flasks. EMSCs (passage 0, P0) were isolated from embryonic maxillofacial processes and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, NY, USA) and 1% antibiotics (100 µg/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin). Cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, and the medium was refreshed every three days. Cells were passaged when reaching 80% confluency. EMSCs at passage 3 (P3) were used for subsequent experiments. Embryonic tails were collected and genotyped using the One Step Mouse Genotyping Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to confirm the genotype of the derived EMSCs. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0(Supplementary data 1).

Flow cytometric analysis

Flow cytometric analysis was performed on P3 WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs cultured in a complete medium. When cells reached 80% confluency, they were enzymatically dissociated into single cells with trypsin (Gibco, NY, USA) for 1 min. The single-cell suspension was then incubated with primary antibodies against CD31, SCA-1, CD90.2, and SOX2 (1:100 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) for 1 h at 4 °C. Following incubation, cells were washed and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), then subjected to flow cytometry using a Cytoflex flow cytometer (BECKMAN, CA, USA). Data acquisition and analysis were conducted using FlowJo software (Version 10.8.1; TreeStar, OR, USA).

Phalloidin staining

For cytoskeleton staining of EMSCs, cells were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 1 × 10³ cells per well. Upon reaching 70% confluency, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Following three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with phalloidin (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) overnight at 37 °C. After removal of the phalloidin solution, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 10 min. after removing the phalloidin staining solution. Fluorescent images were captured using an Axio Observer 3 fluorescence microscope (Axio Observer3, Zeiss, Germany).

Osteogenic induction of EMSCs

For osteogenic differentiation induction, 5 × 105 EMSCs were cultured in osteogenic induction medium, which consisted of DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, NY, USA), 1% antibiotics, 0.1 µM dexamethasone, 10 mM β-glycerol phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), and 50 µM ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). The medium was replaced every 72 h. To modulate the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, the inhibitor AG490 (25 nmol/L, Abcam, MA, USA) and the agonist RO8191 (4 nmol/L, MCE, Shanghai, China) were added to the medium. Additionally, GH (20 nmol/L, MCE, Shanghai, China) was incorporated into the osteogenic differentiation medium to investigate its role in the osteogenic differentiation of EMSCs.

Alizarin red and alcian blue double staining

To evaluate ossification degree, Alizarin Red and Alcian Blue Double Staining was performed to compare WT and lngfr-/- fetal mice. Mandibular processes were dissected from fetal mice and fixed in 95% ethanol for at least 5 days, followed by treatment with an acetone solution for 1 week. The staining solution was prepared as a mixture of 0.1% Alizarin Blue, 0.3% Alizarin Red, acetic acid, and 70% ethanol at a ratio of 1:1:1:17. Acetone-treated fetal samples were immersed in the staining solution for 4 h, then transferred to 1% KOH solution for 24 h. Subsequently, samples were placed in a 20% glycerol solution for 7 days until the muscles and skeletons became transparent. Finally, images were captured using a stereoscopic microscope (SMZ 800 N, Nikon, Japan).

Wound-healing assay

EMSCs were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured until full confluency was achieved. A 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a uniform linear scratch across the cell monolayer. Detached cells and debris were removed by washing with PBS, and the initial scratch width (A0) was recorded. Images were captured at 0 h and 12 h using a fluorescence microscope (Axio Observer 3, Zeiss, Germany). The migration area rate was calculated using the formula: Migration area rate (%) = [(A0 - A12) / A0] × 100%, where A0 denotes the initial scratch width and A12 represents the remaining scratch width at 12 h. All quantitative analyses were performed using ImageJ software (version 1.80, National Institutes of Health, USA).

CCK8 assay

Cell proliferation of EMSCs was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay kit (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10³ cells per well. Cells were treated with 10 µmol/L AG490 or 4 µmol/L RO891 and continuously cultured for 7 days until reaching 60% confluency. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (M5, MD, USA).

ALP staining and alizarin red S(ARS)staining

WT and lngfr-/- cells (4.5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded in 24-well plates and subjected to osteogenic induction for 3, 7, and 14 days. ALP staining assay were conducted using the BCIP/NBT ALP color development kit (Beyotime, Shanghai China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells treated with osteogenic medium were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min, followed by staining with ALP staining solution in the dark for 4 h. Image J software (version 1.80, National Institutes of Health) was used for quantitative analysis. ARS staining (Beyotime, Shanghai China) was used to evaluate late-stage osteogenic differentiation of EMSCs. Cells were induced in osteogenic medium for 21 days and then fixed with 4% PFA. After washed with DD H₂O, EMSCs were stained with ARS staining solution for 0.5 h in the dark and observed under an inverted microscope. Nodules were lysed with 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) for 1 h, and the absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader (M5, MD, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs (2 × 105 cells/well) were cultured in osteogenic induction medium for 3, 7, and 14 days in 6-well plates. WT EMSCs (2 × 105 cells/well) treated with inhibitor AG490 and the agonist RO8191 were induced to osteogenic differentiation for 7 days. RNA was extracted using the RNAiso Plus Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MD, USA) was used to measure the concentration and purity of RNA. Reverse transcription was performed using the Prime Script ™ RT reagent kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and PCR was conducted using the SYBR Green PCR kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The 2ΔΔCt method was employed to quantify the fold change in mRNA expression, with Gapdh serving as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences are provided in Table 1.

High-throughput sequencing

To comprehensively explore the role of LNGFR in osteogenic differentiation, we performed circRNA sequencing. CircRNAs possess a stable circular structure that enables them to remain persistent within cells and less susceptible to degradation, rendering them ideal candidates for investigating long‑term regulatory mechanisms. High-throughput sequencing was performed to characterize the differences in osteogenic differentiation between WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs. In this assay, isolated EMSCs first underwent a quality control testing. Following qualification, linear RNA was removed. cDNA libraries were constructed using the KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit. After validating library quality against established criteria, qualified libraries were pooled based on effective concentration and target sequencing data volume requirements. CircRNA sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system at Geneseed Biotech (Guangzhou, China).

Western blot (WB)

Total protein extraction was performed using a protein extraction kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and PMSF (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min to collect the supernatant containing soluble proteins. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). WB procedures were performed as follows: Proteins were denatured at 100 °C for 10 min, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes (ISEQ00010, Millipore, USA). After blocking with skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-JAK2 (1:1000, Abcam, MA, USA), anti-phosphorylated (p)-JAK2 (1:1000, Abcam, MA, USA), anti-STAT3 (1:1000, CST, MA, USA), anti-p-STAT3 (1:2000, CST, MA, USA), anti-IGF1 (1:1000, Abclone, Wuhan, China), anti-IGFR1 (1:1000, Abcam, MA, USA), anti-Runx2 (1:1000, Abcam, MA, USA), anti-Col1 (1:1000, Abcam, MA, USA), anti-GHR (1:2000, Affinity Biosciences, Liyang, China), and anti-β-actin (1:1000, CST, MA, USA).

Membranes were then incubated with secondary antibodies [goat anti-mouse IgG (1:4000, Beyotime, China) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:4000, Beyotime, China)] for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (WBKLS0500, Millipore, USA) and imaged with a ChemiDoc™ MP imaging system (AI Image, CA, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP)

EMSCs cells, induced with osteogenic factors, were harvested and lysed on ice using Western and IP cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Whole cell extracts were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 µg of LNGFR antibody (CST, MA, USA), followed by incubation with agarose A/G beads (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 4 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times with PBS buffer, and the resulting immunocomplexes were resuspended in 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer. Samples were then boiled at 100 °C for 10 min and subjected to WB for confirmation. Primary antibodies against LNGFR (CST, MA, USA), GHR (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA), and GAPDH (CST, MA, USA) were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were washed three times with TBST (5 min per wash) before images were captured using a ChemiDoc™ MP imaging system (AI Image, CA, USA).

Cell and tissue immunofluorescence (IF)

WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs seeded in 24-well plates were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 0.5 h, then washed with PBS for 5 min. A 0.5% Triton solution was added to treat the cells for 15 min at 4 ℃ to enhance cell membrane permeability. After three additional washes, cells were sealed with goat serum for 1 h. Incubation with primary antibodies, including anti-Vimentin, anti-Nestin (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), anti-GHR (1:200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA) and anti-IGFR-1 (1:200, Abcam, MA, USA). Subsequently, cells were incubated with anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated secondary antibody (1:100, Beyotime, Shanghai China). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio, Beijing, China), then cell images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope (Axio Observer3, Zeiss, Germany).

Fresh tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 days, rinsed overnight, and then decalcified in 20% disodium ethylenediamine tetraacetate (pH 7.5). After paraffin embedding, 4-µm sections were prepared using a Leica microtome. For immunofluorescence staining, antigen retrieval was performed with pepsin at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min and blocking with 5% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) for 30 min. After three times washes with PBS, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-RUNX2 (1:500, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA), anti-Col1 (1:500, CST, Danvers, MA, USA), and anti-LNGFR (1:500, CST, MA, USA). Subsequently, sections were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 555 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at room temperature.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)staining

For IHC, 4 μm thick sections were sliced from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues. Antigen retrieval and blocking procedures were identical to those used for immunofluorescence. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-RUNX2 (1:500, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) and anti-Col1 (1:500, CST, MA, USA). After incubated with secondary antibodies secondary antibodies, including goat anti-mouse IgG (1:4000, Beyotime, China) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:4000, Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature, sections were stained with DAB reagent (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Images were captured using a Slide Scan system (Shengqiang Technology, Shenzhen, China). Quantitative analysis was performed by calculating a staining index (SI) based on staining intensity and positive cell percentage in randomly selected microscopic fields, with the results averaged across fields.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis

Dissected mandibles and femurs from postnatal 4-week-old mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde 306 (PFA) at 4 °C for 24 h. After fixation, samples were analyzed using a high-resolution micro-CT system 307 (SCANCO Medical AG, Brüttisellen, Switzerland). The scanning parameters were set as follows: voltage 308 70 kV, current 200 µA, and slice thickness 10 μm.

Docking analysis and MD (molecular dynamics)

The AlphaFold-predicted structures of LNGFR and GHR were retrieved from UniProt. Protein–protein docking was performed using HDOCK lite v1.1 with global sampling, and binding free energy was estimated using the MM/GBSA method implemented in HawkDock. Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2022 with the AMBER14SB force field. The complex was solvated in a TIP3P water box with a 1 nm buffer distance, and Na⁺/Cl⁻ ions were added to achieve a physiological concentration of 0.15 M. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the PME method with a 1 nm cutoff, and bond constraints were applied using the LINCS algorithm. After energy minimization (3000 steps of steepest descent followed by 2000 steps of conjugate gradient), a 100 ns production simulation was performed under the NPT ensemble at 310 K and 1 bar with a 2-fs time step. Structural stability and binding interactions were analyzed by calculating RMSD, RMSF, Rg, SASA, hydrogen bonds, and MM-PBSA binding free energy using the g_mmpbsa package.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with a minimum of three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software). Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test was applied for multiple-group comparisons. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Experimental results were independently verified three times to ensure reproducibility.

Results

lngfr-/- inhibited osteogenesis in fetal mice

To clarify the role of lngfr-/- in fetal mouse skeletal development, Alizarin Red-Alcian Blue double staining was performed on E16.5 fetal mice. Alizarin Red specifically labels mineralized tissues associated with osteogenic differentiation, while Alcian Blue identifies cartilage matrices involved in chondrogenic differentiation. The combination of these stains enables simultaneous evaluation of osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation potentials within tissues. Results indicated that, compared with the WT group and lngfr-/- fetal mice exhibited significant impairment in skeletal development. Specifically, in skeletal tissues such as the spine and skull, both the number of Alizarin Red-labeled mineralized nodules and the amount of Alcian Blue-stained cartilage deposits were significantly reduced in the lngfr-/- group compared with the WT group (Fig. 1A). Three-dimensional micro-CT reconstructions revealed that lngfr-/- mandibles exhibited a marked reduction in bone mass and trabecular density compared with WT controls (Supplementary data 2 Fig. S1A). Quantitative analysis further confirmed significant decreases in bone volume (BV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), and bone mineral density (BMD) in lngfr-/- mandibles (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Consistently, micro-CT analysis of femurs showed that lngfr-/- mice also displayed significantly reduced BMD, BV, BV/TV, and Tb.Th relative to WT mice (Supplementary data 2 Fig. S1C, D).

To further verify the effect of lngfr-/- on osteogenic differentiation, IHC staining of COL1 and RUNX2 was performed on embryonic tissue sections to assess the expression levels of early osteogenic differentiation markers. Results demonstrated that the positive expression levels of COL1 and RUNX2 in lngfr-/- fetal mouse tissues were significantly lower than those in the WT group (Fig. 1B, C). lngfr-/- suppressed osteogenic differentiation of EMSCs cultured in vitro

Phalloidin staining was performed to examine the cytoskeletal morphology of EMSCs. Results showed that both WT and lngfr-/- group. EMSCs exhibited a typical fibroblast-like morphology, with no significant abnormalities in cytoskeletal structure (Fig. 2A). Further detection of apoptosis-related indicators revealed no statistical difference in the expression levels of apoptotic proteins (including Bcl-2, Bax, and Caspase-3) between the two groups, suggesting that LNGFR is not significantly associated with the apoptotic process of EMSCs (Fig. 2B).

A CCK-8 assay was used to dynamically monitor the proliferative activity of the two groups of EMSCs during 7 days of in vitro culture. Results demonstrated that both WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs showed a linear proliferation trend from day 1 to day 7; however, the proliferation rate and proliferative activity of lngfr-/- EMSCs were significantly lower than those of the WT group. A wound-healing assay was conducted to evaluate cell migration ability, revealing that after 12 h of culture, the migration distance and migration rate of lngfr-/- EMSCs were significantly reduced compared with the WT group (Fig. 2C). These results indicated that LNGFR does not play a critical regulatory role in the apoptotic process of EMSCs, but its deletion can significantly inhibit the proliferation and migration abilities of EMSCs.

Assays for osteogenic differentiation revealed the following: ALP staining and quantitative analysis indicated that the ALP staining intensity and enzyme activity of EMSCs in the lngfr-/- group were significantly lower than those of the WT group at 3, 7, and 14 days of osteogenic induction (Fig. 2D). ARS staining and quantitative analysis showed that the number and area of mineralized nodules in the lngfr-/- group were significantly reduced at the same induction time points (Fig. 2E). Quantitative Statistical Graphs of ALP and ARS results are provided in Supplementary data 2 Fig. S3. RT-qPCR revealed that, compared with the WT group, the mRNA expression levels of key osteogenic regulatory factors (Alp, col1, Runx2, and Opn) in the lngfr-/- group were significantly downregulated at 3, 7, and 14 days of osteogenic induction (Fig. 2F). WB analysis further confirmed that the protein expression levels of RUNX2 and COL1 in the lngfr-/- group were significantly lower than those in the WT group at t at 3, 7, and 14 days of osteogenic induction (Fig. 2G and H).

RNA sequencing and analysis of osteogenic differentiation differences regulated by LNGFR

To investigate the potential mechanism underlying the osteogenic differences between WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs, circRNAs sequencing was performed (Fig. 3A). A volcano plot revealed 83 upregulated and 69 downregulated circRNAs in the lngfr-/- group compared to the WT group. Among these, the parent genes of multiple circRNAs associated with MSC osteogenic differentiation including circ_Ghr, circ_Ptn, and circ_Deptor were downregulated (Fig. 3B). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the parent genes of differentially expressed circRNAs were enriched in 10 signaling pathways, with the growth hormone (GH) synthesis, secretion and action pathway, AGE-ARG signaling pathway in diabetic complications, and adherens junction pathway being the most prominent (Fig. 3C), Additionally, several circRNAs parent genes (e.g., circ_Lats1 and circ_Lats2) were found to be related to the WNT pathway, a well-recognized vital signaling pathway for osteogenesis (Fig. 3D), further supporting the link between these circRNAs and EMSC osteogenic differentiation.

GO enrichment analysis revealed three circRNAs within the intersection with the highest degree: circ_Pibf1, circ_Gfra2, and circ_Ghr. KEGG pathway analysis identified circ_Ghr, circ_Pias2, and circ_Pik3r1 as the top intersecting circRNAs. Circ_Ghr was an overlap in both analyses (Fig. 3E), suggesting it is a potential hub molecule mediating the osteogenic differences between WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs. Collectively, the circRNAs sequencing results indicate that the GH signaling pathway may be critical for driving the differences in osteogenic differentiation between WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs.

Interaction between LNGFR and GHR enhanced the expression of GH/IGF1 and p-JAK2/p-STAT3

To investigate the regulatory mechanism of LNGFR on the GH/IGF1 and JAK/STAT signaling pathways in EMSCs, a series of molecular biology experiments were performed in this study. IF assays showed that compared with the WT group, the fluorescence intensities of GHR and IGF1 were significantly decreased in lngfr-/- EMSCs, indicating a marked downregulation of their expression (Fig. 4A, B, C). Meanwhile, the results of IF co-localization analysis of LNGFR and GHR showed that the expression levels of LNGFR and GHR were synchronously reduced in the lngfr-/- group, further reflecting the effect of LNGFR deficiency on GHR expression (Fig. 4D, E). Co-IP assays further confirmed a direct interaction between LNGFR and GHR in EMSCs (Fig. 4F). WB analysis further verified these findings at the protein level: on the one hand, the protein expression levels of IGF1, IGF1-R, and GHR in lngfr-/- EMSCs were significantly lower than those in the WT group; on the other hand, compared with the WT group, the expression levels of key phosphorylated proteins (p-JAK2, p-STAT3) in the JAK/STAT signaling pathway were significantly downregulated in lngfr-/- EMSCs (Fig. 4G, H). Collectively, these results suggested that lngfr knockout can inhibit the activation of the GH/IGF1 signaling pathway and reduce the phosphorylation level of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway by regulating the expression of GHR and the interaction between LNGFR and GHR, ultimately impeding the activation of both signaling pathways.

To further elucidate the structural basis of the LNGFR–GHR interaction, protein–protein docking analysis identified multiple hydrophobic interactions at the binding interface, including THR166 (GHR)–LEU262 (LNGFR), LEU168–VAL266, ILE264–LEU21, LEU172–ARG15, and THR173–MET11. Additionally, ASP271 and ILE272 of GHR formed hydrophobic contacts and a salt bridge with ARG13 of LNGFR, further stabilizing the interface. MM/GBSA calculations yielded a binding free energy of − 33.91 kcal/mol, indicating thermodynamically favorable binding. (Fig. S4 A).

Molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that the complex reached equilibrium at approximately 35 ns and remained stable thereafter. The radius of gyration (Rg) and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) decreased over time, suggesting enhanced structural compactness upon complex formation. Persistent yet dynamic hydrogen bonding interactions were observed throughout the simulation, while root mean square fluctuation (RMSF)analysis indicated limited residue fluctuations. Free energy landscape analysis revealed a distinct low-energy basin corresponding to stable conformational states. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that GHR and LNGFR formed a stable and energetically favorable complex (Fig. S4 B-G).

LNGFR promoted GH/IGF1 pathway expression via the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway

To further investigate the role of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway in GH/IGF1-mediated processes and to determine whether crosstalk exists between these pathways, we specifically modulated JAK/STAT activity. The JAK2 phosphorylation inhibitor AG490 was applied to suppress the pathway, while the STAT3 phosphorylation activator RO8191 was used to enhance it. Subsequently, the expression levels of IGF1 and IGF1R in EMSCs were assessed.

WB analysis revealed that, compared with the control group, protein expression levels of IGF1 and IGF1R were significantly decreased in the AG490-treated group; conversely, they were markedly elevated in the RO8191-treated group (Fig. 5A, B). Consistent with these findings, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated parallel changes in Igf1 and Igf1r mRNA expression, and IF assays confirmed corresponding alterations in protein fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5C, D). Collectively, these results indicate that JAK/STAT pathway activity directly influences the expression of key molecules (IGF1 and IGF1R) within the GH/IGF1 signaling pathway, thereby supporting the existence of crosstalk between the two pathways.

LNGFR enhanced osteogenic differentiation of EMSCs through the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway

Wound healing assays revealed that, compared with the control group, the migration rate of AG490-treated EMSCs was significantly decreased after 12 h of culture, whereas the migration rate of the RO8191-treated group was markedly enhanced (Fig. 6A). CCK8 assays further demonstrated that AG490 suppressed EMSCs proliferation, while RO8191 promoted cell growth (Fig. 6B). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that inhibition of JAK phosphorylation by AG490 significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of osteogenic-related genes (Runx2, Col1, Opn, Alp), whereas their expression levels were significantly upregulated in the RO8191-treated group (Fig. 6C). Consistent with these findings, WB analysis demonstrated that protein expression of RUNX2 and COL1 was markedly decreased in the AG490 group and increased in the RO8191 group (Fig. 6D). After 7 days of osteogenic induction, ALP staining confirmed reduced ALP activity in the AG490-treated group and enhanced activity in the RO8191-treated group, thereby establishing that the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway contributes to the regulation of EMSCs proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 6E).

Exogenous GH supplementation rescued osteogenic inhibition in LNGFR-knockout cells by activating GH/IGF and JAK2/ STAT3 pathways via GHR binding

To investigate whether exogenous GH supplementation can rescue osteogenic inhibition caused by LNGFR deficiency, we evaluated osteogenic markers in both WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs. WB analysis revealed that GH supplementation increased the protein levels of RUNX2 and COL1 in WT EMSCs, and significantly enhanced their expression in lngfr-/- EMSCs (Fig. 7A). Consistently, GH treatment markedly upregulated the mRNA expression of osteogenic differentiation–related genes, including Igfr-1, Runx2, Col1, Alp, and Opn in lngfr-/- EMSCs (Fig. 7B). After 7 days of osteogenic induction, ALP staining further confirmed that exogenous GH reversed the impaired osteogenic differentiation in the lngfr-/-⁻ group (Fig. 7C). Moreover, GH supplementation increased both protein and mRNA levels of IGF1R, p-JAK2, and p-STAT3 in both WT and lngfr-/- EMSCs. Together, these findings demonstrate that GH supplementation not only rescues the osteogenic deficiency caused by lngfr-/- but also promotes activation of the IGF1R–JAK2–STAT3 signaling axis (Fig. 7A, B).

Discussion

This study elucidated the molecular mechanism by which LNGFR regulates GH-mediated osteogenic differentiation. Co-immunoprecipitation assays, together with molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations, demonstrated a stable physical interaction between LNGFR and GHR, providing both experimental and structural evidence for its involvement in the regulation of GH–GHR signaling. Functionally, LNGFR was not essential for the initiation of GH–GHR signaling; rather, it acted as a modulatory factor that optimized GHR signal transduction efficiency. Specifically, LNGFR might stabilize the GHR conformation or facilitate GH–GHR complex formation, thereby enhancing the activation of the downstream JAK2/STAT3 pathway. Activated JAK2/STAT3 promoted the transcriptional activity of the key osteogenic transcription factor RUNX2 and concurrently upregulated IGF1 expression. As a major effector of GH signaling, IGF1 further amplified osteogenic signaling output. Collectively, the “LNGFR–GHR–JAK2/STAT3–IGF1–RUNX2” signaling network synergistically enhances the osteogenic differentiation capacity of EMSCs (Fig. 8).

Pattern diagram: JAK/STAT pathway-mediated regulation of EMSCs osteogenesis by the LNGFR-regulated GH/IGF axis. In WT E16.5d fetal mice, normal LNGFR expression ensures proper binding of GH to GHR, initiating dual regulatory effects: directly promoting IGF-1 expression and activating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, as evidenced by phosphorylated forms of p-JAK2 and p-STAT3. Importantly, activated JAK2/STAT3 signaling enhances IGF-1 expression through a feedback loop and increases the transcriptional activity of the osteogenic factor RUNX2. This cascade amplifies the osteogenic differentiation of EMSCs into osteoblasts, promoting osteogenesis. In lngfr-/- E16.5d fetal mice, the absence of LNGFR disrupts the interaction between GH and GHR, leading to impaired activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, reduced IGF-1 expression, and decreased RUNX2 transcriptional activity. These effects inhibit EMSCs osteogenic differentiation. Notably, exogenous GH supplementation reactivates both the GH/IGF1 pathway and the JAK2/STAT3 pathway via binding to GHR, rescuing the osteogenic inhibition caused by LNGFR deficiency. Furthermore, the JAK2/STAT3 inhibitor AG490 and the pathway activator RO8191 confirm that the JAK2/STAT3 pathway is a key mediator of LNGFR-regulated osteogenesis(This figure was created with BioRender.com.)

LNGFR has been recognized as a stem cell marker and was reportedly involved in the regulation of osteogenic differentiation of MSCs, although its effects may vary among cell types. While previous studies suggested that LNGFR promotes osteogenesis in EMSCs [6, 26, 27]. Consistent with previous reports showing that global lngfr knockout leads to osteopenia and impaired mineralization in mice, our in vivo results further confirmed that lngfr deficiency disrupted embryonic skeletal development [28].

A hallmark phenotype of abnormal skeletal development is reduced bone mass accompanied by mineralization defects. Previous studies have shown that lngfr-/- mice display decreased bone mass and reduced bone mineralization compared with WT mice [26, 28, 29]. Single-cell sequencing further demonstrated that differentially expressed genes between WT and lngfr-/- mice were enriched in pathways related to MSC biomineralization and bone mineralization, and that the proliferation and osteogenic potential of lngfr-/- MSCs were significantly reduced [30]. Additional evidence indicates that LNGFR expression in EMSCs increases with embryonic developmental stage (E19.5d > E12.5d), and EMSCs with higher LNGFR expression possess stronger osteogenic potential [31]. Meanwhile, LNGFR-positive (LNGFR⁺) EMSCs isolated at embryonic day 12.5 exhibited significantly greater osteogenic differentiation capacity than LNGFR-negative (LNGFR⁻) counterparts [9]. These results indicate that LNGFR promotes the osteogenic differentiation of EMSCs from the maxillary process across different developmental stages.

Osteogenic differentiation is primarily regulated by key transcription factors such as COL1 and RUNX2, and deficiency of either factor directly results in skeletal malformations [32–34]. In the present study, EMSCs derived from the maxillary processes of lngfr-/- fetal mice exhibited markedly reduced ALP activity and impaired mineralized nodule formation, as demonstrated by in vitro alizarin red staining. Additionally, RT-qPCR analysis further revealed significantly decreased mRNA expression of osteogenic markers (Runx2 and Col1), while protein levels of these markers were substantially downregulated, as confirmed by WB, IHC, IF. These findings confirmed that LNGFR deficiency suppresses the osteogenic differentiation capacity of EMSCs, consistent with previous findings. Beyond osteogenesis, LNGFR exerted pleiotropic effects on the functional regulation of MSCs, encompassing not only osteogenic differentiation but also proliferation and migration capabilities [35]. In this study, proliferation and migration of EMSCs were assessed using CCK-8 and wound-healing assays, respectively. Both capacities were significantly higher in WT EMSCs than in the lngfr-/- EMSCs [28] Collectively, EMSCs from the lngfr-/- mice displayed synchronized impairments in proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation. These findings suggest that LNGFR functions as a key cellular contributor underlying the bone developmental defects observed in lngfr-/- fetal mice.

This study employed circRNA sequencing with the aim of revealing the molecular mechanisms underlying LNGFR-mediated osteogenic differentiation of EMSCs through high-throughput, unbiased screening. Compared to traditional targeted detection methods, circRNA sequencing enables unbiased, genome-wide identification of differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways [36]. As a class of structurally stable, abundant, and highly specific non-coding RNAs, circular RNAs (circRNAs) have been implicated as important epigenetic regulators in the process of osteogenic differentiation, offering new perspectives for unraveling the molecular networks involved in osteogenic regulation [37].This approach provides objective evidence regarding the role of the GH–JAK2/STAT3 axis in LNGFR-mediated osteogenesis, offering an effective strategy for unraveling the downstream regulatory networks involved.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which LNGFR regulates the osteogenic differentiation of EMSCs, we focused on the GH signaling pathway, which receives signals through the GHR and activates downstream signaling [38]. Notably, this pathway plays a crucial role in maintaining the self-renewal and differentiation potential of MSCs [39]. Moreover, activation of the GH signaling pathway has been shown to enhance osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs while reducing adipocyte accumulation [40]. GH exerts its effects through two modes: direct activation of the pathway or indirect regulation via paracrine-mediated IGF1R signaling [41]. Increasing evidence indicates that the GH/IGF1R axis is a critical pathway mediating the indirect promotion of preosteoblast osteogenic differentiation [15, 42]. In parallel, studies have shown that p75NTR, as a co-receptor for neurotrophins, contributes to pituitary cell differentiation (including GH-secreting cells) and the regulation of hormone secretion [43]. In addition to the link between LNGFR and the GH signaling in the endocrine system, a recent study reported that LNGFR affected circadian rhythms through regulating clock genes and thus played a key role in the formation and mineralization of dental hard tissue [44]. Since the circadian clock interacts with GH signaling via the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which modulates systemic metabolism [45].

In this study, Co-IP experiments confirmed the physical interaction between LNGFR and GHR in EMSCs. Consistent with this, WB analysis showed that the expression levels of both GHR and IGF1R were significantly decreased in the lngfr-/- group. These results suggest that LNGFR modulates the GH signaling pathway through direct interaction with GHR, thereby influencing the downstream expression of IGF1. Furthermore, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations revealed that LNGFR and GHR form a stable complex through hydrophobic interactions and salt bridges, with a calculated binding free energy of − 33.91 kcal/mol. MD simulations also demonstrated that the LNGFR–GHR complex exhibited high structural stability during the simulation, and RMSD data indicated its stability under physiological conditions, providing insights into the interaction mechanism.

The JAK/STAT pathway, a key downstream signaling axis of GHR, also mediates signaling via the IGF/IGF1 receptor [46–48]. The JAK/STAT signaling pathway is implicated in autoimmune diseases, regulates bone homeostasis, and plays a central role in the proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts and osteoclasts [49]. Specifically, the JAK/STAT signaling pathway is important for skeletal development. A study by Huang et al. confirmed that GH signaling mediated by the JAK/STAT pathway can synergize with BMP9 to promote the osteogenic differentiation of mouse multi-lineage cells [50]. Furthermore, the JAK/STAT signaling pathway is involved in the osteogenic differentiation of stem cells from the apical papilla [51]. Mechanistically, phosphorylation of JAK2 is required for STAT3 activation; inhibition of JAK2 phosphorylation prevents STAT3 binding and, consequently, suppresses osteogenic differentiation in human MSCs [52, 53]. Activated STAT3 can directly bind to the STAT binding site in the runx2 promoter to initiate its transcriptional expression [54]. In our study, the protein levels of p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 were significantly reduced in lngfr-/- EMSCs. GO analysis further indicated that the JAK/STAT pathway is involved in the regulation of GH signaling [38]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that inhibition of the GH signaling pathway in lngfr-/- EMSCs is accompanied by a significant reduction in the expression levels of key proteins in the JAK/STAT signaling pathway in lngfr-/- EMSCs.

This study has several limitations. First, the global lngfr knockout mouse model does not exclude potential indirect effects of LNGFR deficiency in non–MSC-derived cells, making it difficult to confirm the autonomous regulatory role of MSCs. Future work should therefore employ MSC-specific conditional knockout models to clarify this issue. Second, although Co-IP confirmed the interaction between LNGFR and GHR, the precise molecular details remain unresolved, including the specific domains mediating binding and the potential involvement of co-regulators. Additional studies using GST pull-down assays and domain-specific mutant constructs will be required to address these questions.
