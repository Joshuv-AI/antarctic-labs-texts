Impact of modified Henry approach with preservation of Flexor Carpi Radialis Tendon Sheath on wrist function in the treatment of distal radius fractures
Authors: Dang Ma, Jiaqing Ji, Yimin Zhu, Xue Fang, Yanben Wang, Jian Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the clinical efficacy of a modified Henry approach (preserving the FCR tendon sheath) versus the traditional approach (incising the sheath) for distal radius fractures (DRFs) treated with volar locking plates, and to evaluate the outcomes within 12 months postoperatively.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 165 patients with unstable DRFs who underwent volar locking plate fixation from January 2023 to March 2025, who were retrospectively divided into the Preservation Group (n = 79, FCR tendon sheath preserved via the modified approach) and the Control Group (n = 86, FCR tendon sheath incised via the traditional approach). Perioperative indicators, radiological parameters, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH) scores, grip strength, wrist range of motion (ROM) and complications were compared at the follow-up time points of 1 week, 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 months postoperatively.ResultsThere were no significant differences in baseline data and postoperative radiological reduction quality between the two groups (P > 0.05). The Preservation Group had significantly lower VAS scores at all follow-up time points (P < 0.001), and lower DASH scores from 1 week to 6 months (P < 0.001), with no statistical difference at 12 months (P = 0.550). Grip strength of the Preservation Group was significantly superior at all time points from 1 month to 12 months (P < 0.05), while wrist ROM was significantly better at 1 and 2 months (P < 0.001), with no significant differences at 3, 6 and 12 months (P > 0.05). The Preservation Group had a significantly lower incidence of tendon adhesion (2.5% vs. 11.6%, P < 0.05) and overall complication rate (11.4% vs. 23.3%, P < 0.05) than the Control Group. No radial artery injury or complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) occurred in either group.ConclusionThe modified Henry approach preserving the FCR tendon sheath significantly improves early postoperative pain relief and functional recovery, with sustained benefits in pain and grip strength up to 12 months. This technique does not increase surgical difficulty, reduces complication risk, and follows the ERAS principle, showing high clinical value.Keywords: Distal radius fracture, Flexor Carpi Radialis Tendon Sheath, Wrist function recovery, Tendon adhesion

Introduction

Distal radius fractures (DRFs) account for approximately 15–20% of all adult fractures and are the most common upper extremity fractures in clinical practice, with an increasing incidence due to global aging, especially in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis [1–3]. Volar locking plate (VLP) internal fixation has become the gold standard for the treatment of unstable DRFs due to its excellent angular stability and ability to allow early postoperative functional exercise, a key tenet of the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) concept [4, 5]. The Henry approach is a classic volar surgical approach for DRF fixation, providing a clear surgical field for precise fracture reduction, but longitudinal incision of the FCR tendon sheath in the traditional approach may compromise the anatomical and functional integrity of the tendon-sheath complex [6]. The FCR tendon sheath is a sophisticated fibrocartilaginous tunnel with pulley structures and synovial membrane, which not only provides a low-friction gliding environment for the tendon but also maintains the mechanical axis of tendon movement and ensures efficient force transmission. Incision of the sheath can lead to tendon adhesion, reduced mechanical transmission efficiency, and impaired early functional recovery, which has been confirmed by our previous 3-month follow-up study [7]. However, current clinical research on the management of the FCR tendon sheath in DRF surgery is mostly limited to follow-up of ≤ 3 months, and there is a lack of follow-up data of 6 months or more to clarify whether the protective effect of sheath preservation on wrist function can be sustained, and whether there are persistent differences in key functional indicators such as grip strength and pain control at later follow-up time points [8]. In clinical practice, the recovery of grip strength over time and chronic pain are key factors affecting patients’ return to daily work and quality of life, and the lack of extended follow-up evidence makes it difficult for clinicians to fully evaluate the clinical value of sheath preservation technology over extended follow-up. This study collected 12-month follow-up data, systematically analyzed the dynamic changes in wrist functional parameters over extended follow-up, and further explored the sustained efficacy of the modified Henry approach with flexor carpi radialis (FCR) tendon sheath preservation on wrist function recovery in patients with distal radius fractures (DRFs). This study is expected to fill the gap in extended follow-up clinical evidence for FCR tendon sheath preservation, and provide a more comprehensive and reliable surgical reference for the clinical treatment of unstable DRFs.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study. Patients with unstable DRFs who underwent volar locking plate internal fixation in the Department of Orthopedics, Tongji Hospital, Tongji University from January 2023 to March 2025 were included. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital (approval number: SBKT-2025-287), and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: ① Age ≥ 18 years; ② Unilateral closed DRF classified as AO/OTA type A3, B, or C; ③ Treated with Synthes volar locking plate system; ④ Complete postoperative follow-up data at 1 week, 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 months; ⑤ Complete clinical and radiological data.

Exclusion Criteria: ① Open or pathological fractures; ② Combined with other fractures of the ipsilateral upper extremity; ③ A history of wrist surgery or dysfunction; ④ Severe osteoporosis (T-score < –3.5) confirmed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; ⑤ Intraoperative nerve or tendon repair required; ⑥ Failure to follow the unified rehabilitation protocol postoperatively.

Surgical methods and grouping

All patients were assigned to two groups based on the intraoperative management strategy of the FCR tendon sheath as follows:

Preservation Group (n = 79): anatomical interspace dissection method was adopted, with the operation performed through the natural anatomical space between the radial margin of the FCR tendon sheath and the surrounding loose connective tissue, and the specific procedures were as follows:① Incision and exposure: A longitudinal incision (3–5 cm) was made along the radial margin of the FCR tendon, starting 1 cm proximal to the wrist crease and extending distally, and layers were separated to the surface of the tendon sheath; ② Identification of anatomical space: The radial margin of the FCR tendon sheath was carefully identified under direct vision, and a potential avascular anatomical space was searched between the epitenon of the tendon sheath and surrounding loose connective tissue using a blunt dissector; ③ Sharp dissection: Hemostatic forceps or tissue scissors were used for gentle sharp dissection along this anatomical space to gradually expand the space into a safe operating channel (width ≥ 1.5 cm); ④ Retraction and exposure: A small self-retaining retractor was inserted through this channel to retract the FCR tendon together with its intact sheath to the ulnar side as a whole, fully exposing deep structures (flexor pollicis longus tendon and pronator quadratus). The flexor pollicis longus tendon was retracted to the ulnar side with a vessel loop, and the pronator quadratus was incised in an L-shape along its radial and distal margins and retracted to the ulnar side; ⑤ Reduction and fixation: The fracture was reduced under direct vision, and a volar locking plate and screws were implanted according to the fracture type. Finally, the pronator quadratus was repaired with 3 − 0 absorbable sutures, and the radial margin of the FCR tendon sheath was sutured to the radial deep fascia with 4 − 0 non-absorbable sutures, with care taken to avoid suturing the FCR tendon itself. Intraoperative photographs were taken to record the integrity of the tendon sheath (Fig. 1a–e).

Control Group (n = 86)

The standard traditional Henry approach was adopted. After exposing the FCR tendon sheath, it was longitudinally incised along the midline (length 2–3 cm), and the FCR tendon was retracted to the ulnar side with a vessel loop to expose deep structures. After fracture reduction and plate fixation, the pronator quadratus was repaired with 3 − 0 absorbable sutures, but the incised FCR tendon sheath was not sutured (Fig. 1f).

All surgeries were performed by the same team of senior physicians (with ≥ 10 years of orthopedic clinical experience), and a standardized postoperative rehabilitation protocol was implemented for all patients: the affected limb was elevated to reduce swelling, active finger flexion and extension were started 24 h postoperatively, passive wrist movement (flexion, extension, pronation, supination) was gradually performed 72 h later, and active wrist training with progressive resistance was conducted 1 week later. All patients were followed up at 1 week, 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 months postoperatively by a dedicated orthopedic nurse who was blinded to the grouping.

Follow-up and evaluation indicators

A dedicated orthopedic nurse who was blinded to the grouping conducted regular follow-up at 1 week, 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 months postoperatively. The evaluation indicators included:

General and Perioperative Indicators: Age, gender, AO classification, time from injury to surgery, operation duration, intraoperative blood loss.

Radiological Evaluation: Postoperative volar tilt, ulnar deviation angle and radial height were measured by posteroanterior and lateral X-rays of the wrist using ImageJ 1.8.0 software at 1 day postoperatively. Fracture healing time was recorded based on X-ray findings. Two independent radiologists blinded to the grouping performed the measurements, and the average value was taken.

Functional Evaluation: ① VAS pain score (0–10 points, 0 = no pain, 10 = severe pain); ② DASH score (0-100 points, 0 = no disability, 100 = severe disability); ③ Grip strength (measured by a digital dynamometer with a grip width of 5 cm, expressed as the percentage of the affected side to the contralateral side); ④ Total active wrist flexion-extension range of motion (ROM, °).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. All continuous variables in this study were first tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. The independent samples t-test was applied for intergroup comparisons of normally distributed data with homogeneous variance, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data or data with heterogeneous variance. The error bars in the line charts all represent the standard deviation (SD) of the data, reflecting the degree of intragroup data dispersion. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s); two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for the dynamic comparison of indicators at different follow-up time points. Count data were expressed as n (%), and χ² test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparison. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The DASH score of both groups decreased rapidly in the early postoperative period and tended to be stable after 6 months. The Preservation Group had significantly lower DASH scores than the Control Group from 1 week to 6 months (all P < 0.001); at 12 months postoperatively, the DASH scores of the two groups were both 8.7 (Preservation Group: 8.7 ± 1.3, Control Group: 8.7 ± 1.0), with no statistical difference (P = 0.550) (Table 3/Fig. 2b).

Grip strength of both groups showed a continuous upward trend during follow-up, and the Preservation Group had significantly higher grip strength percentage than the Control Group at all time points from 1 month to 12 months (all P < 0.05). At 12 months postoperatively, the grip strength percentage of the Preservation Group was 94.5 ± 1.7%, which was significantly higher than 93.5 ± 1.6% of the Control Group (P < 0.001) (Table 3/Fig. 3a).

Wrist ROM of both groups recovered rapidly in the early stage and reached the normal level after 3 months. Complication analysis

During the 12-month follow-up, the incidence of tendon adhesion in the Preservation Group (2.5%, 2/79) was significantly lower than that in the Control Group (11.6%, 10/86) (χ²=4.89, P = 0.027). The overall complication rate of the Preservation Group (11.4%, 9/79) was also significantly lower than that of the Control Group (23.3%, 20/86) (χ²=4.12, P = 0.042). There were no significant differences in the incidence of incision infection and subcutaneous hematoma between the two groups (P > 0.05). No radial artery injury or CRPS occurred in either group during the follow-up (Table 4).

Discussion

This study collected follow-up data at 1 week, 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 months postoperatively, and systematically revealed the dynamic changes of the efficacy of FCR tendon sheath preservation in the treatment of DRFs from early to later follow-up time points. The core findings are that preserving the FCR tendon sheath not only brings significant early advantages in pain control, functional recovery and wrist mobility, but also maintains sustained advantages in pain relief and grip strength recovery up to 12 months, while the overall wrist function (DASH) and ROM of the two groups tend to be consistent at later follow-up time points, and the complication rate of the preservation group is significantly lower throughout the follow-up period. These findings fill the gap of extended follow-up clinical evidence for FCR tendon sheath preservation technology, and further confirm its clinical value in the surgical treatment of DRFs.

Mechanism of sustained efficacy of FCR tendon sheath preservation

The sustained advantages of the Preservation Group in pain management and grip strength recovery are fundamentally attributed to the maintenance of the anatomical and functional integrity of the FCR tendon-sheath complex. First, the intact FCR tendon sheath acts as a physical barrier, which can restrict the diffusion of postoperative inflammatory mediators, reduce the direct stimulation of inflammatory factors on the tendon and surrounding soft tissues, and thus alleviate acute and chronic pain [9]. This is the fundamental reason why the VAS score of the Preservation Group is significantly lower than that of the Control Group at all follow-up time points, including the 12-month follow-up stage. In contrast, the incised tendon sheath loses this barrier function, leading to persistent low-grade inflammation around the tendon, which results in prolonged pain and even chronic tendinitis [10]. Second, the pulley structures formed by the thickened FCR tendon sheath are the key to maintaining the efficient mechanical transmission of the tendon [11]. Preserving the sheath ensures the integrity of the pulley mechanism, so that the FCR tendon can maintain the normal mechanical axis during contraction and movement, avoiding the ‘bowstring’ deformity caused by the destruction of the pulley, and thus ensuring the efficiency of muscle force transmission [12]. This mechanical advantage is reflected in the continuous superiority of grip strength in the Preservation Group from the early stage to the 12-month follow-up stage. Even when the overall wrist function (DASH) of the two groups is consistent, the grip strength, as a key indicator of motor function, still maintains a significant advantage, which is of great clinical significance for patients who need to complete fine and forceful hand movements in their daily work and life.

In addition, the intact synovial membrane of the FCR tendon sheath can continuously secrete synovial fluid, which provides a low-friction gliding environment for the tendon and maintains the nutrient supply of the avascular part of the tendon. This not only reduces the risk of tendon adhesion in the early stage, but also prevents tendon degeneration and injury caused by insufficient lubrication and nutrition over extended follow-up, which is an important reason for the low tendon adhesion rate and good sustained grip strength recovery in the Preservation Group [13, 14].

Advantages and consistency of wrist ROM and overall function

This study found that the Preservation Group had a significant advantage in wrist ROM at 1 and 2 months postoperatively, but the difference disappeared after 3 months, and the ROM of the two groups was basically the same at 6 and 12 months. This is because the early limitation of wrist ROM is mainly caused by tendon adhesion, soft tissue edema and inflammatory reaction. Preserving the FCR tendon sheath reduces the early inflammatory reaction and tendon adhesion, thus accelerating the recovery of wrist mobility; while with the extension of time, the self-repair and remodeling of soft tissues in the Control Group gradually alleviate the above adverse factors, making the ROM recover to the normal level consistent with the Preservation Group [15].Similarly, the DASH score, as a comprehensive evaluation index of upper extremity function, showed a significant advantage in the Preservation Group from 1 week to 6 months, and the two groups were consistent at 12 months. This is because the DASH score comprehensively reflects pain, function and daily activity ability. The early advantage of the Preservation Group is due to the combined effect of lower pain and better motor function; while at later follow-up time points, with the recovery of pain and ROM in the Control Group, the comprehensive functional score was basically the same as that of the Preservation Group [16, 17]. This result indicates that although the comprehensive function of the two groups was consistent at later follow-up time points, the Preservation Group can significantly shorten the functional recovery time of patients and help them return to normal life and work earlier, which is in line with the core goal of the ERAS concept [18].

Technical advantages and clinical feasibility

The ‘anatomical space dissection method’ adopted in this study to preserve the FCR tendon sheath has obvious technical advantages: first, the operation is based on the natural anatomical space of the wrist, without additional incision or special surgical instruments, and the operation duration is not significantly different from that of the traditional approach, so it does not increase the surgical difficulty [19, 20]; second, the learning curve of this technique is gentle, and it can be mastered by orthopedic surgeons with basic experience in hand and wrist surgery; third, the postoperative suture of the tendon sheath margin restores the anatomical structure of the wrist to the greatest extent, reducing the interference to the soft tissue structure [21]. In this study, the operation duration and intraoperative blood loss of the two groups were not significantly different, which fully confirmed the clinical feasibility of this modified approach.

Clinical Significance: Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID). Although statistical significance was observed for multiple outcomes, it is essential to evaluate whether the observed differences meet the threshold for minimal clinically important difference (MCID) to ensure clinical relevance beyond mathematical significance. For the DASH score, the established MCID is approximately 10.2 points [22]. In the present study, the between-group differences in DASH scores at 1 week, 1 month, 2 months, and 3 months postoperatively exceeded this MCID threshold (differences ranged from approximately 5 to 14 points), indicating clinically meaningful improvements in overall upper extremity function during the early recovery phase in the Preservation Group. However, the differences at 6 months (borderline) and 12 months (non-significant) did not reach the MCID threshold, which is consistent with the convergence of overall functional outcomes at later follow-up time points. For grip strength and VAS pain scores, which showed sustained differences at all time points in the Preservation Group, the between-group differences in grip strength consistently exceeded 10% of the contralateral side—a clinically meaningful threshold commonly referenced in the literature [23]. Similarly, VAS differences exceeding 1.5 points on a 10-point scale are generally considered clinically important, and the between-group VAS differences in this study consistently exceeded this threshold at all follow-up points. These analyses confirm that the observed benefits of FCR tendon sheath preservation are not merely statistically significant but also clinically meaningful, particularly in the early postoperative period and in sustained grip strength recovery.

Comparison with previous studies and innovation of this study

Most of the previous studies on FCR tendon sheath management in DRF surgery were limited to follow-up of ≤ 3 months, and only confirmed the early efficacy of sheath preservation [6]. Shim et al. [24] found that the traditional Henry approach with sheath incision had a higher incidence of FCR area tenderness at 3 months postoperatively, but did not further explore the pain and functional changes at later time points. Thalhammer et al. [25] confirmed that preserving the pronator quadratus can improve the 6-month DASH score of DRF patients, but did not focus on the FCR tendon sheath.

The innovation of this study is to supplement the 6- and 12-month extended follow-up data on the basis of the 3-month follow-up study, and for the first time found that preserving the FCR tendon sheath can bring sustained advantages in pain control and grip strength recovery, and clarify the dynamic change pattern of various functional indicators. This finding makes up for the lack of extended follow-up evidence in the current research, and provides a more comprehensive theoretical basis for the clinical promotion of sheath preservation technology.

Study limitations

This study is a single-center retrospective cohort study, which has inherent limitations such as selection bias and information bias, although the baseline data of the two groups are well balanced [26]. Second, the functional evaluation in this study is mainly based on clinical subjective and objective indicators, and advanced imaging techniques such as high-frequency ultrasound and shear wave elastography are not used to quantitatively detect the tendon gliding distance and adhesion degree [27]. Third, the follow-up time is 12 months, and follow-up data of ≥ 2 years are needed to clarify whether the grip strength advantage of the Preservation Group can be sustained. In the future, a multi-center prospective randomized controlled trial with a longer follow-up time should be designed, combined with advanced imaging and biomechanical detection methods, to further verify the efficacy over extended follow-up of FCR tendon sheath preservation technology [28].

Conclusion

The modified Henry approach with anatomical space dissection for preserving the FCR tendon sheath demonstrates significant clinical efficacy in both treatment of unstable DRFs. It effectively reduces postoperative pain and accelerates recovery of wrist function and mobility in the early stage (1–2 months). Additionally, it sustains benefits in pain control and grip strength recovery during the extended follow-up periods (6–12 months), while markedly lowering the incidence of tendon adhesion and overall complications. Although the overall wrist function (DASH) and flexion-extension ROM of the two groups show no significant difference at 12 months postoperatively, the Preservation Group shortens the functional recovery cycle and maintains a sustained grip strength advantage, which is highly beneficial for enhancing early rehabilitation and quality of life at extended follow-up. This technique does not increase surgical difficulty, is easy to master, and aligns well with the ERAS protocol, demonstrating significant clinical applicability and potential for widespread adoption in the surgical treatment of DRFs.
