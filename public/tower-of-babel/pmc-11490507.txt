Morphometric analysis of the hard palate in sex estimation among koreans using three-dimensional computed tomography
Authors: Seok-Ju Choi, Won-Joon Lee, Kwan Hyun Youn, Scott Lozanoff, U-Young Lee, Yi-Suk Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Accurate sex estimation is crucial for comprehensive analysis of the biological profiles of unidentified human skeletal remains. However, there is a notable lack of research specifically addressing the morphometrics of the hard palate. Therefore, this study aimed to derive discriminant equations using the hard palate and assess their applicability for sexing partial skeletal remains in a contemporary Korean population. Statistical analyses were performed for 24 measurements derived from three-dimensional models of the hard palate, generated using computed tomography scans of 301 individuals (156 males, 145 females). Descriptive statistics revealed significant sexual dimorphism in the mean comparison of hard palate sizes between Korean males and females, with males exhibiting larger palates across all measurements (p < 0.05). Discriminant function score equations were generated to aid in sex determination. Univariate analysis yielded an accuracy range of 57.8–75.1%, whereas the stepwise method achieved an accuracy of 80.7% with five selected variables: IF-PNS, GFL-GFR, IF-GFR, Pr-EcL, and Pr-EnR. The results of this metric analysis demonstrate the usefulness of the hard palate for sex estimation in the contemporary Korean population. These findings have potential implications for forensic investigations, archeological studies, and population-specific anatomical research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76436-4.

Introduction

Sex determination of human remains is a fundamental and essential step in biological profiling and plays a crucial role in identification within forensic anthropology and archeology1. Among the various skeletal elements used for sex estimation, the skull and pelvic bones are representative and classically considered the most sexually dimorphic regions of the skeleton2. Additionally, the skull is crucial in forensic dentistry for sex estimation based on teeth3. However, research on these bones is limited owing to postmortem degeneration and preservation difficulties, including fragmented or mixed remains4. To address these challenges, numerous researchers are actively conducting studies on sex estimation using bones from other parts of the body in addition to the commonly used bones5–7.

The sex of human remains can be estimated using two main approaches: morphological (non-metric) and metric analyses8. Non-metric methods rely heavily on the experience of observers because they depend on their subjective shape observation. In contrast, the metric method offers the advantage of objectivity using measurements and statistical results that can be replicated and verified through morphological evaluation9,10. Owing to these advantages, many studies are being conducted in forensics and archeology using metric methods to examine intact or fragmented skeletons, as analysis is often difficult due to incomplete or fragmented bones11,12. To compensate for this, the hard palate, which is protected within the oral cavity and less prone to severe trauma or thermal damage, can be used to estimate sex based on its morphological characteristics13,14.

Within the existing forensic anthropology literature, there is a notable lack of research focusing on sex estimation using the hard palate15. Despite its potential importance, research on the hard palate for sex determination is limited16. Previous studies have been difficult to apply in practice because they focused solely on sexual dimorphism rather than sex estimation, and they did not provide adequate guidance for working with incomplete bones17. These studies highlight the need for a more comprehensive investigation into the capabilities of the hard palate for sex estimation.

In forensic science, such as forensic anthropology and odontology, measurement information on length, depth, and width is important for sex estimation, identification, and morphology18. Studies have been conducted using calipers for this purpose. Additionally, many researchers are studying skeletal structures and components using image analysis with computed tomography (CT) as a three-dimensional (3D) research method to minimize errors in traditional measurement methods19. CT-based studies have a significant potential for sex estimation research because they provide broad sample accessibility, simplify data review and utilization, and provide measurements that can increase the accuracy of traditional methods (e.g., visual inspection)20,21.

This study aimed to provide additional information on sex estimation by conducting a 3D morphological analysis of the Korean hard palate and deriving a discriminant function equation. The results of this study can be used in the field of anthropological identification and are expected to increase the accuracy of sex estimation using fragmented bones.

Results

A summary of intra-observer reliability is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Overall, the measurements taken by the same observer over a four-month interval demonstrate a high level of reliability, with ICC values exceeding 0.81, demonstrating that they are reproducible with minimal intra-observer variation. Normality, skewness, and kurtosis were determined to evaluate data distribution (Supplementary Table S2). Due to the large number of measurement variables, the normality test was performed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov method, and most variables satisfied the test. Meanwhile, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test was violated only in one variable related to female’s mid-GFL; however, the results showed that the absolute values of kurtosis and skewness of this variable were satisfied with the center value showed that the normality violation of a single variable was not statistically significant.

The hard palates of Koreans showed sexual dimorphism in all variables, with larger dimensions observed in males than in females (Supplementary Table S3). This difference was statistically significant in both descriptive statistics and independent t-tests. In contrast, five of the nine paired variables (mid-Ec, mid-En, Prosthion [Pr]-En, incisive foramen [IF]-Ec in males, and Pr-Ec in females) showed significant differences between the left and right sides according to paired t-test results.

For univariate discriminant function analysis, variables with a significance level (p) of < 0.05 and a normality test were selected (Table 1). To confirm that each variable value was meaningful data for using discriminant analysis, we additionally analyzed Eigen value and wiki’s lambda. The higher the Eigen value and the lower the wiki’s lambda value, the higher the reliability of the discriminant function. Ten variables (EcR-EcL, mid-EcR, Pr-EcL, GFR-GFL, mid-EcL, mid-GFR, IF-EcL, Pr-EcR, mid-GFL, and IF-EcR) were selected out of the 24 measurements as they met all aforementioned criteria and demonstrated high accuracy (> 70%). In addition, setting the demarcation point to zero using the group centroid provided an easy distinction between the sexes.

The predicted sex of 19 of the 23 skulls (82.6% accuracy) was the same as the actual sex, and the results were similar to the prediction accuracy of 80.7% (Table 2). Meanwhile, in this test set, sex estimation was inconclusive in four cases. This is presumed to be attributed to the influence of paired measurement variables related to the hard palate, such as IF-GFR, Pr-EnR, and Pr-EcL, indicating the impact of hard palate asymmetry on sex estimation.

Discussion

Sex estimation of human remains is important in forensic anthropology22. Sexual dimorphism is prominent in the pelvis and skull23. Nevertheless, in cases of damage or missing bone fragments, experts rely on other bones for estimation24,25. The present study elucidated sexual dimorphism in the hard palate using a sample from Korea and assessed the accuracy of sex prediction when dealing with bone fragments in the hard palate by employing various statistical analyses. In particular, in the existing literature, it is difficult to find systematic studies on sex estimation using the hard palate26,27. These studies often do not use the characteristic anatomical landmarks of the hard palate to estimate sex differences or employ angle measurements that are impractical in forensic settings. We believe that these findings will substantiate the efficacy of hard palate measurements as a reliable method for sex prediction, thereby establishing a significant tool for forensic investigations, particularly in contexts where conventional identification methods are compromised or unavailable.

Morphological and/or quantitative analyses derived from 3D models, created using Mimics reconstructed from DICOM data, provide a non-invasive alternative to conventional maceration methods. In the present study, we introduced cranial alignment landmarks and landmark-to-landmark definitions through 3D modeling to enhance anatomical knowledge and reduce observer errors associated with landmark placement. Implementing the three principal vectors as reference coordinate systems (Frankfort horizontal, mid-sagittal, and coronal) established a viewing space for the 3D model similar to that specified in the current methodologies28–30. Automatic segmentation can effectively reduce processing time; however, the density of internal organs, measured in Hounsfield Units (HU), diminishes the accuracy of the 3D representation of skull morphology because the HU values of fat and muscle are similar31. In contrast, semiautomatic segmentation begins by automatically generating a mask based on HU values, followed by a manual iterative process to correct unclear regions. At this stage, the user must evaluate the implemented skull 3D modeling using anatomical knowledge and objectively measure the generated mask to minimize the margin of error in landmark placement and distance measurements using planes and points. Therefore, semiautomatic segmentation is preferred because it can produce more reliable and efficient results than automatic segmentation when the user wants to control accuracy31,32. Our study highlights the advantages of 3D studies in evaluating bone measurements (reduced observer error compared with traditional dry bone measurements and maintenance of a virtual record of morphology for future reference through instrumentation) and assessing unidentified remains using a semi-automated process33–35. We expect that the development of sex estimation techniques will play an effective role in the existing sex estimation methods.

In this study, investigating sex estimation based on the hard palate, significant findings emerged from both univariate and multivariate discriminant analyses (Tables 1 and 2). The accuracy of sex prediction using univariate analysis ranged from 57.8 to 75.1%. Notably, three showed high predictive accuracy: distance between prosthion and left ectomolare (Pr-EcL) at 72.4%, distance between the right and left greater palatine foramina (GFR-GFL) at 71.8%, and distance between the right and left ectomolares (EcR-EcL) at 75.1%. Exploring multivariate discriminant analysis using a stepwise method, this approach significantly increased the predictive accuracy to 80.7% (Table 2). This improvement was achieved by retaining three of the previously mentioned variables (GFR-GFL, EcR-EcL, and Pr-EcL) and adding a new variable, the length from the IF to the PNS (IF-PNS), along with two variables (IF-GFR and Pr-EnR). These specific measurements, EcR-EcL, GFR-GFL, EnR-EnL, and IF-PNS, offer a reliable method for forensic experts in the identification process. Furthermore, the accuracy improvement from univariate to multivariate discriminant analysis (from a maximum of 75.1–80.7%) underscores the significance of using a combination of variables for more accurate sex estimation. This suggests that a comprehensive approach that considers not only the perpendicular lengths (IF-PNS and GFL-GFR) of the hard palate but also the diagonal lengths (IF-GFR, Pr-EnR, and Pr-EcL) representing its shape of the hard palate can improve the accuracy of sex estimation. In the future, additional research incorporating geometric morphometric analysis is warranted for utilizing the entire shape of the hard palate for sex estimation in forensic analysis36.

In the present study, the paired t-test results for several paired variables confirmed significant left and right differences (Supplementary Table S3). Additionally, multivariate stepwise discriminant analysis for the whole hard palate showed that selecting paired variables increased the accuracy of the discriminant function (Table 2), and the analysis for the hard palates on either the left or right side showed some differences in the selected variables (Table 3). These suggest indicate asymmetry in the paired variables of the hard palate. Specifically, the asymmetry between the left and right sides in the location of the GF was less pronounced than the clear landmarks around the teeth, such as En and Ec. Additionally, the paired variable related to the Pr showed a larger left-right difference than the paired variable related to the IF. Taken together, it was confirmed that the landmarks within the dental arch on the hard palate are relatively free from asymmetry. However, considering the relatively low discrimination accuracy of their multivariate discriminant function, the overall asymmetry of the hard palate should not be ignored. It is difficult to determine from these data alone whether the reason for this asymmetry in the hard palate is a sampling issue or a population difference; however, previous reports have indicated that facial asymmetry is associated with chewing on one side in approximately 70% of the population. Based on this, part of the reason for palatal asymmetry may be cautiously estimated37,38. Future research is required to determine how the tendency of the dominant chewing side affects the shape of the hard palate.

Descriptive statistics for different populations studied by Naveen et al.2, Mustafa et al.14, and Araby et al.39 were compared with those for the Korean population (Table 5). Compared with Saudis, the distance between the endomolares in Koreans was similar for both males and females. However, the length between the anterior palatal point (APP) and the Alveolon was shorter for Koreans than for Saudis. When compared with Indians, Koreans had a greater interendomolar width (EnR-EnL) and the length between the IF-PNS for both sexes, but a shorter diagonal length than that of Indians. Compared with Jordanians, Korean males had smaller measurements in both palatal width and length, whereas Korean females had a shorter width but similar length. Overall, the palatal dimensions of Koreans were relatively smaller than those of Saudis and Jordanians, and the anterior-to-posterior length of the hard palate was relatively longer than that of Indians. Although hard palate measurements have generally been reported to show racial differences, the comparative analysis of the hard palate across multiple populations in the present study supports recent reports that marked differences exist in hard palate morphology across populations37.

The results of comparing the palatal index (PI) with that reported in other studies are presented in Table 6. For both males and females, the average PI of Koreans was higher than that of Saudis and Turkish. These findings underscore the importance of using population-specific data to derive accurate biological profiles such as sex estimation. The types of palate based on PI are classified into three categories: leptostaphylin (narrow soft palate: <80), mesostaphyllin (medium soft palate: 80–84.9), and brachystaphylline (wide soft palate: ≥85)38. It has been confirmed that both male and female Koreans typically belong to the brachystaphylline category. Additionally, variations in landmark definitions for measuring palatal length and width emphasize the need for standardized measurement techniques. For instance, Saudis measure PI using the distance between the APP and PNS, while Turkish use the distance between the IF and PNS. These discrepancies could result in inaccurate PI comparisons across populations. To enhance the accuracy of such classification comparison in the future, it will be necessary to standardize the landmarks used to derive PI.

In conclusion, this study focused on sex estimation study using bones by presenting univariate and multivariate discriminants using morphological measurements of the length and width of the hard palate in Koreans. The study findings showed that the hard palate had significant effects on sex estimation. In particular, the results of the test set using dry bone (Table 4) were similar to the accuracy of 80.7% derived by multivariate discriminant analysis. These findings can serve as an auxiliary tool for estimating the sex of an unidentified person in practical situations where sex estimation using bones is important, such as in forensic science, forensic anthropology, and archeology.

Methods

Materials

This descriptive 3D study was conducted using CT images of the base of the skull from 301 Korean individuals (156 males and 145 females) with data available on age and sex. Informed consent to use the images for research was obtained, and the images were collected from 2008 to 2009 with the cooperation of the Department of Neuroradiology at Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital. The selected images included 3D skull scans, ensuring that none of the skulls showed signs of injury or deformity. We performed skull CT and reconstructed the 3D images. The average age was 42.2 years [42.4 years (20–68 years) in males, 42.1 years (20–68 years) in females]. All methods performed in this study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Catholic University of Korea (approval number MC24EASI0004). This study was also approved by the Ethics Committee of the Catholic University of Korea, with informed consent being waived.

3D construction and measurements

CT examinations for forensic evaluation were performed using a CT scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). The imaging parameters were set as follows: tube voltage 120 kVp; 210 mAs; slice thickness 0.75 mm, pitch factor 0.35, increment 0.7 mm, and rotation time 0.3 s. The whole-body scan time ranged from 60 to 75 s, depending on the body height. Subsequently, the DICOM files obtained using CT were processed using MIMICS, 3D image-based engineering software (Materialise Mimics version 23.0, Materialize NV, Leuven, Belgium, https://www.materialise.com), with a threshold value of 673–3,071 HU. Skull images were extracted and converted into a computer-aided format for further analysis.

3DCT images were analyzed to measure the distance variables in millimeters using selected landmarks on the console of the 3D-MIMICS (Materialise Mimics version 23.0, Materialize NV, Leuven, Belgium, https://www.materialise.com) software (Fig. 1). If the hard palate was located partially on the left and right sides, six additional variables with “mid-” added for the distance from the mid-sagittal plane to the landmark (mid-ectomolare left [EcL], mid-ectomolare right [EcR], mid-endomolare left [EnL], mid-endomolare right [EnR], mid-greater palatine foramen left [GFL], and mid-greater palatine foramen right [GFR]) were added. All landmarks used for the measurements are shown in Fig. 2 and listed in Supplementary Table S4, and the distance variables between landmarks are presented in Fig. 3.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0, SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). A test for intra-observer reliability was performed on all variables across all samples, with the same observer (S.-J. Choi), who has advanced training and experience in 3D measurement, with an interval of four months between measurements. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of a two-way random effects model with absolute agreement for a single rater/measurement (ICC (2,1)) was used. ICC values less than 0.50 were considered poor; 0.50 to 0.75 moderate; 0.75 to 0.90 good, and values greater than 0.90 were considered excellent41,41. Descriptive statistics were used to determine the means and standard errors of 24 variables generated from a combination of the length and width components of the hard palate, and the mean differences between sexes were assessed using independent t-tests.

Additionally, normality, skewness, and kurtosis were evaluated to ensure the reliability of the discriminant function analysis. In this study, because the number of measured variables was large, a normality test was performed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov method. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Discriminant analysis was derived from data using a stepwise method or using one directly selected variable with a significance level (p) of < 0.05 and a variable that satisfied the normality test.
