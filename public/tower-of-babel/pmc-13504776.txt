scKSFD: federated distillation model with knowledge sharing for cell type classification of clinical transcriptome data
Authors: Nan Sun, Mengcen Guan, Piyu Zhou, Stephen S.-T. Yau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables high resolution characterization of cellular heterogeneity but poses significant challenges for cross institutional collaboration due to privacy constraints and distributional heterogeneity. To address this problem, we propose a Federated Distillation framework with Knowledge Sharing (scKSFD) for privacy-preserving cell type classification. Unlike conventional federated learning approaches that exchange model parameters, scKSFD performs knowledge aggregation in prediction space by sharing probability-level soft label outputs on a reference dataset, thereby reducing privacy risks. To better accommodate domain specific characteristics of scRNA-seq data, scKSFD integrates stratified proxy sampling to preserve rare cell populations and employs probability-level aggregation to mitigate batch specific expression shifts without explicit feature level correction. Comprehensive evaluations across 42 clinical single-cell transcriptome datasets demonstrate that scKSFD achieves higher or comparable F1 scores relative to centralized and existing federated baselines under heterogeneous settings, with statistically significant improvements in paired comparisons. In a multiple hospital COVID-19 case study, federated collaboration using scKSFD improved classification performance compared with local-only training while avoiding direct sharing of patient level expression data. Overall, scKSFD provides a federated distillation framework that balances predictive performance, robustness, and data-sharing constraints for multiple institutional single-cell transcriptomic analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12859-026-06508-x.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of scRNA-seq technology has substantially increased the resolution of biomedical studies [1–3]. By generating high dimensional gene expression matrices in which rows correspond to genes and columns correspond to cells, scRNA-seq enables the characterization of cellular heterogeneity and disease-related mechanisms at fine granularity [4–6]. A central task in scRNA-seq analysis is cell type identification, which aims to assign cells to biologically meaningful classes based on their expression profiles [7]. Studies such as immune profiling of COVID-19 patients [8] have illustrated the clinical relevance of accurate cell type annotation.

The typical workflow for cell type identification includes preprocessing gene expression matrices, feature selection or dimensionality reduction, and downstream clustering or supervised classification. Methods such as scGNN [9] and scPSD [10] have been developed to improve classification performance. For example, scGNN models cell-cell relationships through graph neural networks, while scPSD applies information theory principles for feature selection. Despite methodological advances, integrating single-cell datasets across institutions remains challenging due to regulatory and logistical constraints on data sharing [11]. Because transcriptomic datasets may be linked to patient level information [12], data exchange is often subject to governance frameworks such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [13] and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) [14]. Consequently, there is a need for computational strategies that enable collaborative analysis without requiring centralized pooling of raw data.

Federated learning (FL) [15, 16] provides a distributed training paradigm in which multiple institutions collaboratively learn a shared model without directly exchanging raw data [17, 18]. In standard FL, clients update model parameters locally and transmit parameter updates to a central server for aggregation. While this approach reduces the need for data centralization, it introduces practical challenges. Model updates may still reveal information under certain attack models [19, 20], and repeated parameter exchange can incur high communication overhead, particularly for high dimensional models [21].

To address these issues, researchers have begun to explore the application of knowledge distillation technology in federated learning. Knowledge distillation [22, 23] is a model compression technique that transfers knowledge from a well-trained teacher model to a student model, enabling the student model to learn the behavior and decision-making process of the teacher model without accessing the teacher model’s training data. In the context of federated learning, knowledge distillation can be implemented in the form of federated distillation [24, 25]. In this method, each client trains a model with its local data and then uses the model to predict a reference set, generating hard labels [26]. These hard labels are then aggregated and sent back to the clients as input for knowledge distillation. This approach not only reduces communication overhead because it only involves the transmission of labels, not the entire model parameters, but also supports heterogeneous client models. However, a potential issue with this method is that if the reference samples are not properly constructed, it may lead to underfitting in model training and even the generation of misleading knowledge [27].

Researchers have proposed the utilization of soft labels in federated distillation [22, 27]. Soft labels provide information about the probability distribution of each class, allowing the model to better understand and handle uncertainties in the data. This method is particularly suitable for non-independent and identically distributed data environments, where there may be significant differences in the data distribution across clients [28]. In such cases, using soft labels can increase the model’s generalization ability because it allows the model to learn a richer data representation.

This paper presents a federated distillation method with knowledge sharing for single-cell data classification (scKSFD). The method aims to enhance the model’s generalization and robustness by introducing soft labels within the federated learning framework to facilitate knowledge exchange across institutions. The method includes four main steps: local model training, knowledge extraction, knowledge aggregation, and knowledge distillation. Through this design, scKSFD enables collaborative analysis of clinical transcriptomic data while limiting the exchange of raw expression information.

Compared with Selective-FD [27], which focuses on selective knowledge sharing among heterogeneous clients, scKSFD introduces probability-level aggregation tailored for single-cell transcriptomic data. Specifically, stratified proxy sampling preserves rare cell populations that are common in scRNA-seq studies, and prediction space aggregation mitigates batch specific expression shifts without requiring explicit feature level correction. These adaptations address domain specific challenges such as dropout sparsity and inter-batch heterogeneity, which are less emphasized in general federated distillation frameworks.

Data collection and preprocessing

We collected 42 clinical single-cell transcriptome datasets from four independent studies [29–32], covering 11 disease types, including Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML), Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC), Neuroblastoma (NB), Metastatic Breast Cancer (MBC), Glioblastoma (GBM), Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL), Ovarian Cancer (OC), Melanoma (MM), Sarcoma (SARC), Colorectal Tumors (CRC), and Oral Cavity Tumors (OCT). Cell type complexity ranged from 3 to 17 classes, with sample sizes between 266 and 10,716 cells.

For preprocessing, we applied standard workflows: (1) gene filtering and dispersion-based feature selection to retain the top 2,000 highly variable genes; and (2) log1p normalization to stabilize variance [9]. The processed matrices were used for downstream federated classification experiments.

Results

scKSFD model workflow

The workflow of scKSFD is illustrated in Fig. 1 and consists of four stages. (1) Each client independently trains a local model using its private data. (2) The trained local models generate soft label predictions, and the resulting probability vectors are transmitted to the server. (3) The server aggregates the received predictions and returns the aggregated soft labels to all clients. (4) Each client performs knowledge distillation using the aggregated soft labels to refine its local model.

To mitigate class imbalance caused by underrepresented cell types, SMOTE [33] was applied to synthesize minority samples during local training. The dataset was first split into 80% training and 20% testing, and SMOTE was applied only to the training set, while the test set remained unchanged and contained only real samples. The training samples were evenly partitioned across K clients to simulate a balanced federated setting, allowing us to isolate the effect of the proposed method without additional variability from data imbalance. For fair comparison, all methods were evaluated using the same preprocessed datasets, while baseline methods were implemented with their default settings. The same train-test partitions were used across all comparative experiments to ensure consistency.

Comparison with centralized and local models

To evaluate the generalization and robustness of the proposed model, we compared scKSFD with the centralized model CellMLP and isolated Local models across 42 clinical single-cell transcriptome datasets (Supplementary Table S2; Fig. 2). “CellMLP” represents the model trained on all datasets in a central server, whereas “Local” represents the model trained independently on each private dataset. In the scKSFD setting, 90% of local samples were used to generate the proxy reference index set. Each experiment was repeated 10 times, and both mean F1 scores and standard deviations were reported.

scKSFD achieved superior or comparable performance across most datasets. It outperformed CellMLP in 41 out of 42 datasets, with an average F1 improvement of 6.8%, and exhibited lower variance in 38 datasets. Overall, scKSFD outperformed both comparison models, achieving F1 gains ranging from 0.16 to 0.32 over the centralized model and 0.01–0.02 over locally trained models in representative datasets, while maintaining lower or comparable variance. Performance gains were most evident in heterogeneous or imbalanced datasets such as MBC-3 and NSCLC-4, where centralized learning showed reduced generalization. By aggregating soft label predictions from distributed clients, scKSFD reduced inter-client distributional bias. In small sample datasets such as CRC-1 and CRC-2, scKSFD maintained F1 scores above 0.99, demonstrating stable performance even under limited or heterogeneous data conditions.

Comparison with privacy-preserving federated methods

To benchmark the effectiveness of scKSFD, we compared it with four privacy-preserving methods: scFedACTINN [17], scFedSVM [17], scPrivacy [18], and PPML-Omics [34] across 42 clinical single-cell transcriptome datasets (Supplementary Table S3; Fig. 3). These methods are based on parameter-sharing or secure aggregation paradigms. Original F1 scores for all methods are reported, and statistical comparisons were performed across datasets using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

Across the majority of datasets, scKSFD achieved higher F1 scores than the compared methods. The improvements were particularly evident over PPML-Omics, with differences exceeding 0.3 in datasets such as AML-1 and AML-3. Compared with scFedACTINN and scFedSVM, scKSFD showed consistent gains in heterogeneous datasets including MBC-3, NSCLC-4, and OC-3, suggesting improved robustness under non-IID data distributions. In small-sample datasets, scKSFD maintained high F1 scores, with performance comparable to or exceeding other federated baselines.

Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across the 42 datasets confirmed that scKSFD achieved statistically significant improvements over scFedACTINN (p = 4.55 × 10−13), scFedSVM (p = 2.43 × 10−8), scPrivacy (p = 9.09 × 10−13), and PPML-Omics (p = 4.55 × 10−13). These results indicate that the observed performance advantages are consistent across datasets rather than driven by isolated cases.

Overall, scKSFD demonstrated comparable accuracy and stability among privacy-preserving federated approaches under the evaluated settings.

Comparison with non-privacy-preserving centralized methods

We further evaluated scKSFD against four conventional centralized methods: ACTINN [35], scGNN [9], scPSD [8], and scTPC [36], across 42 datasets (Supplementary Table S4; Fig. 4). The performance difference was computed as the F1 score of scKSFD minus that of each baseline method.

Across the majority of datasets, scKSFD achieved higher or comparable F1 scores relative to these centralized approaches. Across datasets, the observed F1 improvements ranged from 0.01 to 0.23 over ACTINN, 0.04–0.24 over scGNN, 0.00–0.24 over scPSD, and 0.02–0.86 over scTPC. Larger improvements were observed in heterogeneous or small-sample datasets such as NB-3, MBC-3, NSCLC-4, and OCT. In particular, when centralized models exhibited substantial performance degradation under heterogeneous conditions, scKSFD maintained stable gains. For example, in NB-3, the F1 gain over scTPC reached 0.76. Such differences were primarily observed in datasets with pronounced distributional heterogeneity. In datasets where centralized methods already performed well (e.g., NB-1, AML-2, NSCLC-1), scKSFD achieved comparable or slightly higher accuracy.

Comparison between models trained with soft and hard labels

We evaluated the impact of soft labels by comparing models trained with soft labels versus hard labels across 42 datasets (Supplementary Table S5; Fig. 5). Soft label training achieved higher F1 scores in 29 out of 42 datasets, while hard label training performed better in 8 datasets and 5 datasets showed negligible differences.

Datasets with high heterogeneity or pronounced class imbalance, such as AML-1 and CLL-1, showed larger relative improvements. In representative difficult datasets, where hard-label training resulted in relatively low performance (e.g., F1 ≈ 0.61), soft label aggregation increased F1 to approximately 0.89 and reduced performance variance across repeated runs. Although hard labels slightly outperformed soft labels in a minority of datasets, these differences were generally small in magnitude.

A paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test across the 42 datasets indicated that the overall improvement of soft label training was statistically significant (p = 3.25 × 10−3). The difference distribution shown in Fig. 5 further illustrates that most datasets exhibited positive performance gains under soft label aggregation.

These results suggest that soft label aggregation can improve predictive stability and mitigate performance degradation under heterogeneous data distributions.

As summarized in Table 1, the mean F1 scores ranged from 0.9930 to 0.9964 across all proxy sampling proportions, with standard deviations between 0.0027 and 0.0083. The performance variation across proportions was limited. Notably, moderate proxy sampling proportions (30–50%) achieved mean F1 scores of 0.9945, comparable to higher proportions. Even at 10%, the mean F1 remained at 0.9930 with low variance (0.0041). Increasing the proxy sampling proportion beyond 50% did not result in substantial additional performance gains.

Overall, these results suggest that scKSFD maintains stable classification performance across a wide range of proxy sampling proportions, and moderate proportions may be sufficient to balance predictive performance with computational and communication costs.

To evaluate the computational efficiency of scKSFD, we examined the runtime across different proxy sampling proportions ranging from 10 to 100% (K = 10) on 42 transcriptomic datasets (Supplementary Table S8; Fig. 6).

Although runtime increased with higher proxy sampling proportions, the growth remained predictable and did not lead to disproportionate computational overhead.

These results suggest that moderate proxy sampling proportions (30–50%) provide a practical balance between runtime efficiency and predictive performance.

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we introduced scKSFD, a knowledge-sharing federated distillation framework that enables privacy-preserving cell type classification across clinical single-cell transcriptome datasets. By exchanging soft label predictions instead of raw expression matrices, scKSFD enables collaborative training under data-sharing constraints.

Across 42 clinical datasets, scKSFD achieved higher or comparable F1 scores relative to centralized models (CellMLP, ACTINN, scGNN, scPSD, scTPC) and locally trained models in the majority of settings. Performance differences were more evident in heterogeneous, imbalanced, or small sample datasets, where centralized training may be sensitive to distribution shifts. These results suggest that soft label aggregation can reduce inter-client distributional bias by integrating predictive information from multiple clients. The high performance observed here reflects curated benchmark datasets with well-defined cell type annotations. In this setting, scKSFD primarily preserves comparable performance under distributed training rather than substantially improving absolute accuracy.

Comparisons with other privacy-preserving federated approaches (scFedACTINN, scFedSVM, scPrivacy, PPML-Omics) showed competitive or improved performance under the evaluated configurations. The soft label mechanism provides a probability-level integration strategy that may enhance stability across heterogeneous client distributions. In addition, scKSFD maintained stable performance across varying proxy sampling proportions and different numbers of participating clients, with moderate configurations providing trade-offs between performance and system cost.

Single-cell transcriptomic data contain domain specific challenges such as dropout and batch effects. In federated settings, batch effects may be stronger because each client represents an independent experimental batch. scKSFD does not perform explicit batch correction. Instead, knowledge aggregation is conducted in prediction space through soft labels rather than raw expression features. This reduces sensitivity to batch specific expression shifts and encourages consistent decision boundaries across clients. Soft label aggregation also helps stabilize predictions affected by dropout induced sparsity by combining confidence information from multiple clients.

Beyond empirical evaluation, several domain specific design choices contributed to performance in scRNA-seq analysis. First, stratified proxy sampling preserves minority cell types during knowledge extraction. Second, probability-based soft label aggregation captures predictive uncertainty across clients. These mechanisms provide an implicit alignment effect across heterogeneous datasets while remaining compatible with privacy constraints. In addition, SMOTE was applied only during local training to alleviate class imbalance and was not used during evaluation. Although oversampling is less common in standard scRNA-seq workflows, it was introduced here to stabilize supervised learning under federated data fragmentation.

From a systems perspective, runtime and bandwidth increased approximately linearly with proxy sampling proportion. This predictable scaling behavior facilitates resource planning in distributed settings. The cross hospital COVID-19 case study further illustrated performance gains of federated collaboration compared with local-only training, under conditions where raw patient level transcriptomic data were not centrally pooled.

Despite these observations, several limitations remain. First, scKSFD does not explicitly model batch effects, and extreme cross platform differences may still influence performance. Second, extremely rare cell populations remain challenging due to limited local representation. Performance degradation was observed at very large client counts, potentially due to increased data fragmentation. The current experiments employed MLPs as local models for proof-of-concept, while future work may incorporate more advanced architectures such as CNNs, transformers, and foundation models including scGPT and Geneformer. Improved proxy sampling strategies may further enhance representation of diverse cell populations across clients. Moreover, widely used tools such as Seurat focus primarily on clustering-based annotation rather than supervised classification, making direct quantitative comparison difficult under the current evaluation protocol. Future work will therefore explore broader benchmarking across different annotation paradigms. Finally, although scKSFD reduces exposure of raw data by exchanging only soft label probabilities, it does not currently incorporate formal privacy guarantees such as differential privacy or secure aggregation. Integrating such mechanisms is an important direction for strengthening theoretical privacy assurances.

In summary, scKSFD provides a federated distillation framework for distributed single-cell transcriptomic analysis. By combining soft label aggregation with distributed training, it offers a practical approach to balancing predictive performance and data-sharing constraints. Future work will focus on improving proxy construction strategies, enhancing support for rare cell types, and integrating foundation-model-based representations to extend applicability in collaborative biomedical research.
