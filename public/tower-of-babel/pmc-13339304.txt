Survival and safety: wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy in NSCLC
Authors: Lin Qiu, Pingping Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes of three surgical methods (wedge resection, segmentectomy and lobectomy) in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 130 NSCLC patients who underwent thoracoscopic surgery. Patients were categorized into wedge resection (n = 50), segmentectomy (n = 40) and lobectomy (n = 40) groups. Baseline characteristics, perioperative parameters (operative time, drainage time, length of hospital stay, intraoperative blood loss), postoperative adverse events (reoperation, atrial arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, prolonged air leak, pulmonary infection, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, atelectasis), and survival rates stratified by tumor diameter (< 1.0 cm, 1.1–2.0 cm) were recorded and compared.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable among the three groups (P > 0.05). For tumors < 1.0 cm, the overall survival (OS) rates were similar among wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy groups (88% vs. 90.48% vs. 91.30%) (P > 0.05). In tumors measuring 1.1–2.0 cm, the observed OS rates were 92.00% for wedge resection, 63.16% for segmentectomy, and 64.71% for lobectomy (P < 0.05). The three surgical approaches showed significant differences in the number of lymph nodes sampled, the detection rate of positive lymph nodes, and the resection method (P < 0.05), while no statistically significant difference was observed in the margin status (P > 0.05). Wedge resection was associated with the shortest operative time and drainage time (P < 0.05), while length of hospital stay and intraoperative blood loss did not differ significantly across the three approaches (P > 0.05). The incidence of postoperative adverse events presented no discernible difference in the three surgical approaches (P > 0.05).ConclusionsFor tumors smaller than 1 cm, wedge resection was associated with OS similar to that of segmentectomy and lobectomy. For selected tumors measuring 1.1–2.0 cm, wedge resection was associated with higher observed OS in this cohort and with shorter operative and drainage times; prospective validation is needed.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Wedge resection, Segmentectomy, Lobectomy, Survival

Background

Accounting for about 85% of lung cancer cases, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most prevalent histological type [1], with major pathological subtypes including adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large-cell carcinoma [2]. Worldwide, the incidence of NSCLC continues to rise, and data from the World Health Organization indicate that its annual incidence and mortality rates rank among the highest of all malignant tumors [3]. Likewise, recent epidemiological surveys in China identify NSCLC as one of the leading malignancies threatening public health [4]. The etiologies of NSCLC comprise long-term cigarette smoking, air pollution, occupational exposures such as asbestos, nickel and chromium, ionizing radiation, chronic pulmonary diseases, and driver-gene mutations involving epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), and tumor protein p53 (TP53) [5–7]. These environmental and genetic factors jointly promote abnormal cell proliferation and malignant transformation. Due to insidious symptoms, most patients are diagnosed at a locally advanced stage or with distant metastases [8]. Patients with metastatic NSCLC have historically had a poor prognosis, with a five-year survival rate of 7% [9]. Treatment strategies for NSCLC have evolved from single-modality therapy to a multidisciplinary approach. Surgical resection remains the preferred option for patients with early-stage disease, while radiotherapy and platinum-based chemotherapy are reserved for those who are inoperable or have advanced disease [10].

Surgery serves as the gold standard to improve outcomes in early-stage disease, while also providing histopathological data to tailor postoperative adjuvant therapy when clinically indicated [11]. The principal operative techniques include wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy, each with distinct indications and clinical features [12]. Wedge resection removes the smallest amount of lung tissue and is appropriate for small, well-demarcated, peripheral tumors [13]. It maximizes the preservation of pulmonary function and reduces postoperative respiratory complications, although its oncologic radicality is somewhat limited. Segmentectomy involves resection of an anatomical lung segment and is often chosen for more complex, localized lesions, offering a balance between curative intent and functional preservation [14]. Lobectomy entails removal of an entire pulmonary lobe, ensuring the most thorough tumor clearance and minimizing local recurrence risk, albeit at the cost of greater loss of lung function and a heavier postoperative recovery burden [15]. The present study analyzed clinical data from patients with NSCLC to compare the outcomes of wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy across different pathological types. Our goal is to provide evidence-based guidance for thoracic surgeons when tailoring individualized operative plans and selecting subsequent adjuvant treatment strategies.

Methods

Study population and ethics statement

This study comprised 130 NSCLC patients who had surgical resection at our hospital between January 2022 and December 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Linghu People’s Hospital, Nanxun District, Huzhou (Approval No. LHYY-LLSC-LW-2025-007), using the following methods: (1) for surviving patients, via telephone or outpatient follow-up; (2) for deceased patients, from next of kin/legal representatives whenever possible; (3) patients who could not be contacted or declined participation were excluded from the analysis. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed diagnosis of NSCLC according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Lung Cancer [16]; (2) tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage IA to IB; (3) clinical suitability for both video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery and curative NSCLC surgery; (4) no prior history of radiotherapy or chemotherapy; (5) sufficient cardiopulmonary function to tolerate the surgical procedure. Exclusion criteria included hematologic problems, severe diabetes mellitus, uncontrolled hypertension, cerebrovascular illness, major thoracic surgery, cardiac or pulmonary insufficiency, or evidence of distant metastases.

Surgical procedures [17]

In order to enable single-lung breathing and total collapse of the operational lung, all procedures were carried out under general anesthesia with double-lumen endotracheal intubation. Patients were positioned with their chests raised in the lateral decubitus position. Video-assisted thoracoscopic lobectomy was performed using two incisions; a 4-cm operating port in the fourth intercostal space and a 3- to 4-cm thoracoscopic port in the sixth or seventh intercostal space. The target pulmonary lobe was divided and resected under thoracoscopic guidance. Following the isolation and division of the pulmonary vein, the lobar bronchus was dissected. The regional lymph nodes were methodically dissected, and the afflicted lobe was removed. Wedge resection followed the same anesthetic and port implantation protocol as lobectomy. Larger veins and arteries were divided with an endoscopic stapler, while smaller arteries were isolated and ligated. Without systematic lymph node dissection, a wedge of lung tissue with a margin of at least 1 cm beyond the tumor was removed. The incision and anesthetic procedures were consistent with those used for segmentectomy. Adhesions were meticulously dissected layer by layer with an ultrasonic scalpel. The target bronchus was divided, and segmental vessels were isolated and ligated. The targeted pulmonary segment was extracted via lymph node dissection after sequential dissection of segmental artery, bronchus, and vein. Postoperative anti-infective medication was routinely administered to all patients. Surgical approaches for NSCLC patients were determined through comprehensive assessment of tumor factors (tumor size, location, etc.), patient factors (lung function, comorbidities, age), and evidence-based guidelines. All patients underwent standard preoperative staging, including PET-CT scans. Wedge resection was a preferred option for tumors located peripherally. Segmentectomy was selected for tumors with an invasive component ≤ 0.5 cm, stage IA tumors, or tumors centrally located within specific lung segments. For invasive adenocarcinoma, centrally located tumors, or N1/N2 lymph node involvement, lobectomy was the standard procedure. Sublobectomy (wedge/segment) may be considered as a compromise for patients with poor pulmonary reserve (FEV1 < 1.5 L or < 40% predicted) or high-risk elderly patients, provided that the resection margin was ≥ 2 cm or ≥ tumor diameter. Final decisions on surgical procedure and lymph node dissection were made by the clinical treatment team after discussion. Patients were divided into lobectomy (n = 40), wedge resection (n = 50), and segmentectomy (n = 40) groups based on the surgical procedure, and some of them underwent systematic lymph node dissection.

Observational indicators

Baseline characteristics

Baseline clinical data, including gender, age, lesion location, pathological type, TNM stage, and tumor diameter, were collected from the three groups of patients. At the time of the initial diagnosis, information was obtained from medical records. Regular outpatient visits and medical records served as the sources of follow-up data. Follow-up was conducted via a combination of outpatient re-examination, telephone interviews, and review of inpatient medical records. The follow-up period ranged from 8 to 18 months. The follow-up data primarily included survival status, tumor recurrence and metastasis, subsequent treatments, and adverse events.

Survival rate and perioperative parameters

The survival was assessed from the time of initial diagnosis until the last follow-up, the date of death, or disease progression [18]. The primary endpoint for patient survival in the study was defined as overall survival (OS). Operative time, drainage time, length of hospital stay, and intraoperative blood loss were all considered perioperative indicators. Electronic medical records provided the data.

Adverse events

Documented postoperative adverse events included reoperation, atrial arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, prolonged air leak, pulmonary infection, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, and atelectasis. Reoperation was specifically defined as a second surgery during the same hospital stay attributed to bronchial stump fistula, hemorrhage, or a persistent air leak. Atrial arrhythmia referred to new or worsened atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter that was verified by electrocardiography or ongoing monitoring and necessitated medication or electrical intervention [19]. Dyspnea, chest discomfort, or hypoxemia following surgery that was confirmed by ventilation-perfusion scanning or pulmonary computed tomography angiography was referred to as a pulmonary embolism [20]. Prolonged air leak was defined as continuous chest air drainage for more than five days with either incomplete lung re-expansion or the requirement for additional intervention [21]. Pulmonary infection was characterized by new radiographic infiltrates and a positive culture from sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, often known as postoperative fever, purulent sputum, or leukocytosis. Pneumothorax is diagnosed when intrapleural air and lung compression are visualized on chest X-ray or computed tomography (CT), and the condition necessitates either continuous drainage or re-insertion of a chest tube. A pleural effusion was defined as a fluid buildup that was verified by imaging and necessitated thoracentesis or further drainage. According to imaging, atelectasis referred to the collapse of one or more lobes, necessitating bronchoscopic suction or other procedures.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 20.0 software (IBM Corp., USA) was utilized for all statistical analyses. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to gauge the normality of the data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are expressed as . One-way analysis of variance was utilized to analyze the variables between groups, and the least significant difference test was used for pairwise comparisons. Using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, variables with non-normal distributions were compared between groups and expressed as M (P25-P75). The rank-sum test was employed for evaluating ordinal data. A bilateral P value less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

One hundred thirty NSCLC patients were allocated to wedge resection group (n = 50), segmentectomy group (n = 40) and lobectomy group (n = 40) according to the surgical procedure performed (Table 1). Baseline characteristics included gender, age, lesion location, pathological type, TNM stage, tumor diameter, BMI, smoking status, family history, ECOG performance status, pulmonary function, combined diabetes and combined hypertension (Table 1). There were 30 males and 20 females in the wedge resection group, 26 males and 14 females in the segmentectomy group, and 25 males and 15 females in the lobectomy group (Table 1). No significant differences were detected in terms of gender distribution (Table 1, P > 0.05), age, lesion location, pathological type, TNM stage, and tumor diameter across the three groups (Table 1, P > 0.05). Baseline characteristics (including demographics, clinical pathology, pulmonary function, and comorbidities) were balanced and comparable across the three groups (Table 1, P > 0.05). Only tumor diameter showed a significant association with survival outcomes (Table 2, HR = 0.399, 95% CI 0.162–0.981, P < 0.05), while the remaining variables demonstrated no significant prognostic value (Table 2). Comparison of survival and perioperative parameters

Patients were stratified by tumor diameter (< 1.0 cm and 1.1-2.0 cm) to compare OS among the three groups. In the subgroup with tumor diameter less than 1.0 cm, no significant differences in OS rates were observed among the three groups: 88.00% for wedge resection, 90.48% for segmentectomy, and 91.30% for lobectomy (Table 5; Fig. 1, P > 0.05). In the subgroup with tumor diameter between 1.1 and 2.0 cm, wedge resection was associated with a higher OS rate (92.00%) relative to segmentectomy (63.16%) and lobectomy (64.71%) (Table 5; Fig. 2, P < 0.05). Operative time, drainage time, length of hospital stay, and intraoperative blood loss were all considered perioperative variables. Wedge resection was associated with the shortest operative and drainage times (Table 6, P < 0.05), and lobectomy required the longest operative and drainage times among the three groups (Table 6, P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed among the three groups in length of hospital stay or intraoperative blood loss (Table 6, P > 0.05).

Postoperative adverse events

Eight postoperative complications were assessed, including reoperation, atrial arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, prolonged air leak, pulmonary infection, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, and atelectasis. Reoperation occurred most frequently in the lobectomy group (10.00%) and least frequently in the wedge resection group (2.00%) (Table 7). Atrial arrhythmia was more common in the lobectomy group (7.50%), compared with 2.00% in the wedge resection group and 2.50% in the segmentectomy group (Table 7). Pulmonary embolism and pleural effusion were each reported in one patient (2.50%) in the lobectomy group and were not observed in other groups (Table 7). The incidence of prolonged air leak was 5.00% of the lobectomy group, 2.00% of the wedge resection group, and 2.50% of the segmentectomy group (Table 7). The incidence of pulmonary infection was 4.00% in the wedge resection group compared with 2.50% in both the segmentectomy and lobectomy groups (Table 7). Pneumothorax was documented in one patient each in the segmentectomy group (2.50%) and in the lobectomy group (2.50%), but was not observed in the wedge resection group (Table 7). Atelectasis occurred in one patient (2.00%) in the wedge resection group and one patient (2.50%) in the segmentectomy group, with no cases reported in the lobectomy group (Table 7). Overall, the incidence of postoperative adverse events presented no discernible difference in the three groups (Table 7, P > 0.05).

Discussion

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for early and locally advanced NSCLC [22], with the goal of complete removal of the pulmonary tumor and surrounding involved tissue to prevent progression or metastasis, thereby achieving cure or significantly prolonging survival [23]. This study compared three surgical approaches in NSCLC patients with different tumor diameters and evaluated their association with postoperative survival. In patients with tumors smaller than 1 cm, OS rates were similar among wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy groups, consistent with a large-scale Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database analysis [24]. In patients with tumors measuring 1.1–2.0 cm, wedge resection showed a higher OS rate, compared to segmentectomy and lobectomy. However, this association should be interpreted with caution, because the observed survival advantage may be influenced by unmeasured confounders, including differences in non‑cancer mortality risk and surgical skill. We did not collect cause‑specific mortality data and therefore cannot distinguish cancer‑related deaths from deaths due to other causes; if patients undergoing anatomical resection (segmentectomy, lobectomy) had more comorbidities or a higher non‑cancer mortality rate, OS in the wedge resection group might appear spuriously higher, which would not represent a true oncologic benefit. Despite a smaller excision range, wedge resection can preserve maximal lung function and reduce long-term impacts from postoperative respiratory insufficiency and pulmonary infection in lung cancer, without increasing local recurrence risk [25, 26]. These findings provide a plausible explanation for the higher OS rate observed in the subgroup after wedge resection in our study. Therefore, with strict patient selection and adequate surgical margins, wedge resection may be considered a feasible option for selected patients with early NSCLC measuring 1.1–2.0 cm, especially those with limited pulmonary reserve or significant comorbidities, contributing to individualized surgical planning. However, the differential nodal staging intensity represents a significant potential confounder in the analysis. Systematic lymph node dissection, predominantly performed with lobectomy, yields more accurate N staging and may upstage patients compared to limited sampling or the absence of nodal assessment often accompanying wedge resection. This stage migration effect could artifactually inflate apparent survival of sublobar resections by excluding occult N2 disease, whereas lobectomy outcomes may appear relatively worse due to identification of higher-stage patients. Furthermore, incomplete nodal assessment in the wedge/segmentectomy groups precludes definitive exclusion of stage IIIA disease, potentially underestimating true recurrence risk. Herein, the three surgical approaches differed significantly in lymph node management: the number of lymph nodes sampled was comparable between the segmentectomy and lobectomy groups, but higher than that of the wedge resection group. The lobectomy group also demonstrated the highest positive lymph node detection rate, suggesting that lobectomy provides more extensive systematic lymph node dissection. However, there was no significant difference among the three groups regarding margin status (P > 0.05). These findings were consistent with survival analysis results. Despite more extensive lymph node dissection in the lobectomy group, no significant survival differences existed among the three groups (P > 0.05). Tumor size emerged as the independent prognostic factor (P < 0.05).

Our study also demonstrated that wedge resection was associated with significantly shorter operative time and drainage time compared with segmentectomy and lobectomy, whereas length of hospital stay and intraoperative blood loss did not differ significantly among the three groups. These results proved that, under standardized operative protocols and careful patient selection, wedge resection can result in a shorter surgical procedure and faster postoperative recovery without compromising safety. Analyses based on the SEER database and multicenter retrospective studies indicated that wedge resection, due to its smaller operative scope and simpler technique, is generally associated with reduced operative time and drainage requirements [24, 27]. Regarding postoperative adverse events, no evident differences were observed among the three groups for reoperation, atrial arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, prolonged air leak, pulmonary infection, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, or atelectasis. Segmentectomy is generally considered more technically demanding and associated with a higher risk of postoperative air leak, compared to wedge resection [25, 28]. However, advances in thoracoscopic techniques and precise anatomic dissection strategies markedly reduce complication rates of segmentectomy, making them comparable to those of wedge resection and lobectomy [29, 30]. These observations align with our findings that the incidence of adverse events was similar among the three groups. Furthermore, surgical skill and experience were neither standardized nor recorded in this study; the outcomes of anatomical resections (segmentectomy and lobectomy) are highly dependent on the operator’s technical performance and perioperative management. Therefore, bias due to differences in surgical skill between groups cannot be excluded. Overall, wedge resection in selected early NSCLC patients not only shortened operative and drainage times but also maintained perioperative safety, which is particularly advantageous for patients with limited pulmonary reserve or multiple comorbidities, and may provide a basis for future studies concerning individualized surgical decision-making.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size and a limited follow-up period, the representativeness and statistical power of the findings may be affected, and the five-year OS rate cannot be adequately assessed. Secondly, postoperative lung function, quality of life, and disease progression were not systematically evaluated, limiting a comprehensive assessment of the overall benefits of each surgical approach. Thirdly, the absence of molecular profiling data for driver genes (e.g. epidermal EGFR, KRAS, ALK, or TP53) or other molecular markers that might influence postoperative prognosis precludes deeper molecular-level analysis. Finally, since this study did not collect preoperative and postoperative staging comparison data, we were unable to quantify the impact of stage migration on survival rates.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective cohort, wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy showed comparable survival for tumors < 1 cm. In the 1.1–2.0 cm subgroup, wedge resection was associated with higher observed survival; however, this should be interpreted cautiously given potential confounding and differences in nodal assessment. Further prospective studies with standardized nodal protocols are required.
