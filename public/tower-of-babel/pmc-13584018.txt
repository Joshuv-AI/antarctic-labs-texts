National trends, variation and short-term outcomes in surgery for non-small cell lung cancer in England, 2015–2023: a population-based cohort study using linked national cancer registration and hospital administrative data
Authors: Lauren Kari Dixon, Sarah Cook, Adrian Cook, Jessica Thomas, Joanne Boudour, Faine Chan, John Conibear, Douglas West, Neal Navani, David A Cromwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate temporal changes in surgical practice, centre-level variation and short-term postoperative outcomes among patients undergoing lung resection for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in England between 2015 and 2023.DesignPopulation-based cohort study using linked national cancer registration and Hospital Episode Statistics data.SettingSecondary care in England, including all specialist thoracic surgical centres.ParticipantsAdults (≥18 years) diagnosed with NSCLC in England between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2023. Patients with linked Hospital Episode Statistics records were included and those undergoing lung resection comprised the surgical cohort.Outcome measuresAnnual resection rates, procedure type, operative approach, centre-level variation in segmentectomy use and robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) adoption, 90-day mortality, 30-day emergency readmission and postoperative length of stay. Multivariable regression models adjusted for patient and clinical case mix.ResultsAmong 308 117 patients with NSCLC, 54 138 (17.6%) underwent lung resection. The resection rate increased from 15.4% in 2015 to 20.9% in 2023, while the proportion of patients diagnosed with stage I disease increased from 17.4% to 26.8%. Segmentectomy use increased from 11.2% to 20.6%, pneumonectomy declined fourfold and minimally invasive surgery increased from 47.1% to 79.7%. In 2023, centre-level variation was marked in segmentectomy for small stage IA tumours (risk-adjusted range 10.7%–80.3%) and RATS use (range 0%–67%). 90-day mortality decreased from 4.03% to 2.26%, length of stay shortened and readmission remained stable at approximately 10%–12%. Each successive year of diagnosis was associated with lower 90-day mortality after adjustment for case mix (adjusted OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.93 to 0.98).ConclusionsBetween 2015 and 2023, surgical management of NSCLC in England changed substantially, with increased resection rates, greater use of segmentectomy and minimally invasive approaches and improved short-term postoperative outcomes. However, persistent variation in segmentectomy use and robotic adoption suggests that implementation of contemporary surgical practice remains heterogeneous across centres.Keywords: Thoracic surgery, Lung Neoplasms, Cardiothoracic surgery, Early Detection of Cancer

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the UK and worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of cases.1 Surgical resection offers the best chance of long-term survival for patients with early-stage disease, yet only a minority of patients with NSCLC undergo surgical resection.2 3

Over recent years, the organisation and delivery of thoracic surgical care in England have evolved considerably, with lung cancer surgery delivered through specialist thoracic surgical centres by dedicated multidisciplinary teams.4 Minimally invasive approaches, including video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS), are established surgical approaches for anatomical lung resection and are associated with improved postoperative recovery compared with open thoracotomy without compromising oncological outcomes.5–9 Interest in anatomical segmentectomy has also grown following evidence supporting comparable survival outcomes to lobectomy for selected early-stage tumours.10 11 Concurrently, service reconfiguration and advances in perioperative care pathways have aimed to improve access to surgery and enhance postoperative outcomes.12 13 National initiatives promoting earlier diagnosis and curative-intent treatment are also likely to have influenced patient selection, surgical patterns and outcomes.14 15

Despite these developments, contemporary national evidence describing how surgical practice and outcomes for NSCLC have evolved across England remains limited. Although surgical outcomes for lung cancer are routinely audited,16 comprehensive national reporting of procedural trends and short-term outcomes has not been updated since patients diagnosed in 2017.17

This study aimed to evaluate national temporal changes in surgical practice patterns and short-term postoperative outcomes for patients undergoing lung resection for NSCLC in England between 2015 and 2023, using linked cancer registration and hospital administrative data.

Methods

Study design and data sources

A population-based cohort study was conducted using the cancer registration dataset18 linked to Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) admitted patient care data.19 The cohort included all adults (≥18 years) diagnosed with NSCLC in England between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2023. Linkage was performed by NHS England using unique pseudonymised patient identifiers to connect cancer registration records with all corresponding hospital admissions.20

Study population

All patients with a recorded diagnosis of NSCLC were identified from cancer registration records. Lung resections were identified from linked HES data using Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Interventions and Procedures, version 4 (OPCS-4) procedure codes for pneumonectomy (E541), bilobectomy (E542), lobectomy (E543), segmentectomy (E544), wedge resection (E545) and other/unspecified resections.21 Where multiple resections were recorded, the most extensive resection was included. Other thoracic procedures performed in patients with NSCLC that did not represent lung resection, including pleural procedures such as pleurodesis, were excluded.

Patients without a linked HES record were excluded from analyses. The annual resection rate was defined as the proportion of patients diagnosed with NSCLC who underwent lung resection. Analyses of surgical practice and postoperative outcomes were restricted to those who underwent surgery.

Variables and definitions

Variable definitions and derivation are described in online supplemental table S1. Tumour stage was classified according to TNM group eighth edition for lung cancer (I–IV).22 Performance status was recorded using Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group categories (0–4).23 Socioeconomic deprivation was measured using the 2019 Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) quintile based on the patient’s postcode at diagnosis.24 Comorbidity was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI)25 and frailty using the Secondary Care Administrative Records Frailty (SCARF) index.26 Surgical approach was categorised as open, VATS or RATS using OPCS-4 approach modifiers recorded in HES. In keeping with national coding practice, minimally invasive procedures (VATS and RATS) require specific approach modifiers, whereas open surgery does not. Therefore, cases without a recorded minimally invasive approach modifier were classified as open. Hospitals were classified as thoracic surgical centres using the list of UK hospitals providing thoracic surgery published by the Society for Cardiothoracic Surgery in Great Britain and Ireland, which reflects the national configuration of specialist thoracic surgical services.4 27

Postoperative outcomes

Postoperative outcomes included 90-day mortality, 30-day emergency readmission, length of stay of the index admission and total index bed-days, defined as the aggregate number of postoperative hospital days for index admissions across England in each calendar year.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses summarised patient demographics, tumour stage, comorbidity and surgical characteristics for all NSCLC cases and for those undergoing resection, stratified by year of diagnosis. Temporal trends in resection rates, procedure distribution and surgical approach were described graphically and numerically.

Temporal changes in surgical practice were evaluated at the national level by examining annual resection rates, distribution of procedure type and operative approach among patients undergoing lung resection. The proportion of patients undergoing segmentectomy among those with small stage IA tumours (stage IA1–IA2, corresponding to tumours T1a–T1b, ≤2 cm) was examined separately to assess adoption of parenchymal-sparing resection in the population most likely to be eligible based on contemporary evidence.

Centre-level variation in procedure selection was assessed among thoracic surgical centres. For segmentectomy use in patients with small stage IA tumours diagnosed in 2023, both unadjusted and casemix adjusted centre-level rates were estimated. Casemix adjustment was performed using multivariable logistic regression modelling the probability of receiving segmentectomy (versuslobectomy), incorporating age, sex, tumour substage, performance status, CCI, SCARF frailty group and histological subtype. Adjusted centre-level rates were derived using marginal standardisation to account for differences in patient characteristics across centres. Between-centre variation in operative approach (open, VATS, RATS) was described using centre-level proportions for 2023 and trends over time were examined descriptively.

Among patients undergoing surgery, postoperative outcomes were summarised descriptively by year. Multivariable regression models were used to examine associations between procedure type, approach type and year of diagnosis and postoperative outcomes. Logistic regression was used for 90-day mortality and 30-day readmission, while negative binomial regression was used for postoperative length of stay. Models adjusted for age, sex, IMD quintile, performance status, stage, CCI, SCARF frailty index, surgical approach, procedure type and year of diagnosis (modelled as a continuous variable).

Results are reported as adjusted ORs (aORs) or incidence rate ratios (aIRRs) with 95% CIs. Missing data were described for baseline variables. Descriptive analyses were based on available data, and regression analyses were performed using complete-case analysis. Analyses were performed using Stata/SE V.17.0 (StataCorp).28

Patient and public involvement

Members of the National Lung Cancer Audit (NLCA) Patient and Public Involvement Forum were consulted throughout the design and reporting of this study.

Results

Between 2015 and 2023, a total of 317 097 adults were diagnosed with NSCLC in England according to the Cancer Registration Dataset. Of these, 308 117 (97.2%) patients were successfully linked with HES; 8980 (2.8%) could not be linked to a HES record (online supplemental table S2).

There was an 11% increase in the number of people diagnosed with NSCLC over the study period, from 32 683 in 2015 to 36 228 in 2023. Overall, the median age at diagnosis was 74 years (IQR 67–81), and 47.8% of patients were female (online supplemental table S3). There was a pronounced shift in the stage distribution over the 9 years, with a greater proportion of patients being diagnosed with stage I disease. This increased from 17.4% in 2015 to 26.8% in 2023. Over the same period, the proportion of people with stage IV decreased from 48.0% to 36.8% (online supplemental table S3).

Overall patterns of surgery

Of the 308 117 NSCLC patients, 54 138 (17.6%) underwent surgical resection. The annual volume of lung resection procedures increased from 5031 in 2015 to 7373 in 2023, representing a 46% increase over the study period. Correspondingly, the resection rate rose from 15.4% in 2015 to 20.9% in 2023. The increase in surgical activity was predominantly driven by growth in resections for stage I disease (figure 1). Lung cancer resection surgery was provided at 29 specialist thoracic surgical centres at the start of the study period and 28 centres by the end of the study period, reflecting one service reconfiguration. The median annual centre volume in 2023 was 240 NSCLC resections (IQR 192–285).

Trends in procedure type

There was a noticeable shift in the type of procedure performed over the study period (online supplemental table S6). Lobectomy remained the predominant procedure throughout accounting for 70.8% of all resections overall. The use of segmentectomy increased, rising from 582 procedures in 2015 to 1584 procedures in 2023, representing an increase from 11.2% to 20.6% of all resections. As a result, the total proportion of sublobar resections (segmentectomy and wedge) increased from 18.5% to 25.6%, with wedge resections remaining relatively uncommon and accounting for 4.8% of all procedures during the study period. Pneumonectomy rates declined four-fold, from 5.5% in 2015 to 1.1% in 2023.

The increase in the proportion of segmentectomy procedures was predominantly in patients with stage I disease, with a corresponding fall in the use of lobectomy (figure 2). Among stage IA tumours (≤2 cm; stage IA1–IA2) diagnosed in 2023, the proportion of patients undergoing segmentectomy and lobectomy was 33.1% and 55.4%, respectively.

Changes in the type of lung resection procedure over time for patients with stage I and stage I–IIIA disease.

Among patients with stage II and IIIA disease who underwent surgery, lobectomy remained the predominant procedure and increased slightly over time. Resection rates in stage IIIA disease also rose over time, from 19.0% in 2015 to 25.1% in 2023 (online supplemental table S5).

There was substantial variation between surgical hubs in the choice of procedure for small stage IA (<2 cm) tumours in 2023 (figure 3). On an individual level, the use of segmentectomy was associated with patient age at diagnosis, frailty, performance status, comorbidity, tumour substage and histological subtype. The variation in the use of segmentectomy across surgical hubs persisted after accounting for the differences in patient casemix, with the risk-adjusted segmentectomy rates ranging from 10.7% to 80.3% in 2023 (figure 3). Trends in operative approach

There was an increasing uptake of minimally invasive surgery over the study period (figure 4; online supplemental table S8). Among lobectomy and segmentectomy cases, the proportion performed via VATS increased over time, and RATS adoption accelerated since 2021, with a corresponding decline in open surgery (online supplemental figure S2). In 2023, there was marked between-centre variation in approach for lobectomy and segmentectomy, particularly in the use of RATS (figure 5). After taking account of patient characteristics, tumour stage, operative approach and procedure type, the year of diagnosis remained significantly associated with improved postoperative outcomes (online supplemental table S9). On average, each successive year was associated with a 4% reduction in the odds of 90-day mortality (aOR 0.95 per year, 95% CI 0.93 to 0.98; p=0.001), a 2% reduction in the odds of readmission within 30 days (aOR 0.98 per year, 95% CI 0.96 to 0.99) and a 2% annual reduction in length of stay (adjusted IRR 0.98 per year, 95% CI 0.98 to 0.98; p<0.001). In adjusted analyses, compared with VATS, open surgery was associated with higher 90-day mortality and longer length of stay, while short-term outcomes following RATS were similar to those following VATS (online supplemental table S9).

Discussion

Over this period, resection rates increased substantially, driven largely by a shift towards earlier-stage diagnosis. Surgical practice evolved rapidly, with increased adoption of anatomical segmentectomy and minimally invasive approaches, including RATS. Despite increasing surgical volume and greater use of parenchymal-sparing and minimally invasive techniques, short-term outcomes remained stable or improved, with reductions in 90-day mortality and length of stay observed over time.

The proportion of patients diagnosed with stage I disease increased markedly over the study period, while stage IV diagnoses declined. This stage migration likely reflects improvements in diagnostic imaging, pulmonary nodule surveillance, and the phased implementation of lung cancer screening programmes.14 29 The increase in the absolute number of diagnoses likely reflects both earlier detection through expanding lung cancer screening and broader use of thoracic imaging in routine clinical practice. The present study was not designed to distinguish the contribution of these factors. The increase in early-stage presentation was accompanied by a rise in resection rates from 15.4% to 20.9%, suggesting expansion of curative-intent surgical treatment rather than restriction of eligibility. In addition to earlier diagnosis through expanding screening and improvements in diagnostic pathways, evolving casemix may also have contributed to changing surgical practice. For example, increasing detection of small or subsolid pulmonary nodules may have increased the proportion of patients considered suitable for parenchymal-sparing resection. However, radiological characteristics, screening status and the clinical rationale for procedure selection were not available within the linked datasets and therefore these hypotheses could not be examined. Importantly, this increase in surgical activity did not coincide with worsening short-term outcomes, indicating that expanded surgical provision has been delivered safely within the national thoracic surgical network.

We observed a substantial increase in the use of segmentectomy, particularly among patients with small stage IA tumours. This trend aligns with emerging evidence supporting oncological equivalence of sublobar resection in carefully selected early-stage disease.10 11 The increasing use of segmentectomy likely reflects both earlier tumour detection and growing confidence in parenchymal-sparing strategies. However, marked centre-level variation in segmentectomy use persisted even after casemix adjustment. This suggests that institutional practice patterns, surgical philosophy, availability of technology, or surgeon-level factors may influence procedure selection beyond measurable patient characteristics. While some variation is expected during periods of evolving evidence, continued monitoring is warranted to ensure equitable access to evidence-based care.

Minimally invasive surgery became the dominant operative approach during the study period; between 2015 and 2023, the proportion of resections performed using minimally invasive approaches (VATS or RATS) increased from 47.1% to 79.7%. The observed increase in minimally invasive resections mirrors international experience. Data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons General Thoracic Surgery Database and the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons registry demonstrate comparable growth in VATS and RATS procedures, with contemporary minimal-access rates exceeding 70% in many high-volume centres.30–32 The observed centre-level variation in RATS adoption likely reflects differences in capital investment, institutional priorities, surgical expertise and case volume. In adjusted analyses in this study, there was no evidence of a difference between VATS and RATS in 90-day mortality, 30-day readmission or postoperative length of stay. As robotic platforms become more widely available, continued evaluation will be important to ensure equitable access and value-based implementation across the surgical network.

Short-term postoperative outcomes remained favourable throughout the study period. Descriptively, 90-day mortality declined, median postoperative length of stay decreased, and 30-day emergency readmission rates changed little but showed a small reduction over time after adjustment for casemix. At the same time, total national bed-days increased, reflecting rising surgical volume despite reductions in individual length of stay. In analyses adjusting for casemix, year of diagnosis remained independently associated with lower 90-day mortality and shorter length of stay, indicating that improvements in early outcomes were not solely attributable to changes in patient characteristics over time.

These findings suggest that expansion of surgical activity and increasing adoption of minimally invasive and parenchymal-sparing techniques have not been associated with deterioration in short-term safety at the population level. It is plausible that the observed year-on-year improvements also reflect advances in perioperative care pathways, enhanced recovery protocols, anaesthetic optimisation and strengthened multidisciplinary assessment.33–35 It is also possible that maturation of specialist thoracic surgical networks and wider dissemination of minimally invasive expertise across centres contributed to these improvements.24 While causal inference cannot be established in this observational study, the overall pattern is consistent with progressive improvement in system-wide surgical performance.

These findings also have important implications for service planning. The NHS lung cancer screening programme is being rolled out nationally with the aim of full coverage by March 2030, and early programme data suggest high early-stage detection rates at scale.36 37 As screening expands, further increases in demand for thoracic surgical assessment and resection are likely. Planning for adequate operative capacity, specialist workforce and minimally invasive surgical expertise will therefore be essential to ensure that earlier diagnosis translates into timely curative treatment across the national network.

This study demonstrates the feasibility and value of linking national cancer registration data with hospital administrative datasets to evaluate surgical care for people with lung cancer at scale. Such linkage enables long-term surveillance of practice patterns and outcomes without the reporting burden associated with clinician-entered datasets.38–40 The population-based design minimises selection bias and reflects real-world delivery of care within a nationalised healthcare system. The inclusion of detailed patient-level casemix variables, including comorbidity and frailty measured using the SCARF index, strengthens adjustment for differences in patient characteristics over time and between centres.

Several study limitations should be acknowledged. First, analyses relied on routinely collected administrative data, which are dependent on accurate clinical coding.39 41 Although procedure, admission and mortality data within HES are generally robust,41 42 misclassification of operative approach is possible. In keeping with national coding practice, cases without minimally invasive modifiers were classified as open procedures; however, incomplete coding in earlier years may have influenced estimates of temporal change. Second, detailed tumour-level characteristics, such as radiological features, preoperative evaluation, consolidation-to-tumour ratio, nodal upstaging, margin status and recurrence outcomes, were not available. These factors may influence procedure selection and long-term oncological outcomes but could not be evaluated in this dataset. Third, surgeon-level variables, including experience and case volume, were not available and may contribute to residual variation between centres. Finally, as with all observational analyses, residual confounding cannot be excluded despite multivariable adjustment.

Conclusions

Between 2015 and 2023, surgical practice for NSCLC in England changed substantially. Increased early-stage diagnosis was accompanied by expansion of surgical resection rates, greater use of anatomical segmentectomy and widespread adoption of minimally invasive techniques, including robotic-assisted surgery. These changes occurred alongside stable or improving short-term postoperative outcomes despite increasing surgical volume. However, marked centre-level variation in procedural selection and adoption of robotic technology highlights the heterogeneous pace of implementation across the national network. Ongoing monitoring of practice patterns and outcomes will be essential to ensure equitable delivery of high-quality surgical care as screening expands and technologies continue to evolve.
