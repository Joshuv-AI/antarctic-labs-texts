Evaluating and comparing the signalling and functional landscape of hiPSC-CMs derived from patients with dilated cardiomyopathy
Authors: Giada Castagnola, Diego Loggia, Kyla Bourque, Karima Alim, Ida Derish, Elise Rody, Zosia Gryz, Cara Hawey, Jace Jones-Tabah, Anjali Bhardwaj, Ludovic Mouttet, Natalie Gendron, Nadia Giannetti, Renzo Cecere, Sonya K. Hui, Terence E. Hébert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-ischaemic or dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is characterised by abnormal enlargement of the left ventricle, compromising the ability of the heart to pump blood to the body. All patients with DCM are offered the same treatment regimen regardless of individual differences, with highly variable results on disease progression. Some patients fully recover cardiac function, while others continue to deteriorate, requiring heart replacement therapy or palliation. Incomplete molecular knowledge of dilated cardiomyopathy pathophysiology poses challenges for the discovery of new therapeutic agents. To address this, we use human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) to assess individual molecular signalling and functional signatures in patients with DCM. Using blood samples from two healthy controls and two patients with DCM, we generated and validated hiPSC lines, then differentiated them into cardiomyocytes. Cellular signalling was assessed in each hiPSC-CM line after treatment with several disease-relevant G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)-targeting ligands, measured using nuclear and cytosolic PKA and ERK biosensors at single cell resolution. Differences in functional properties such as calcium handling, contractility and electrophysiology revealed additional features altered in patients with DCM. We have now established a pipeline that can be used to uncover patient-specific molecular mechanisms and disease phenotypes as a pathway to potential development of personalised treatments for DCM.

Introduction

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a disease caused by ventricular dilation and thinned myocardial walls, resulting in decreased systolic function, and an impaired capacity of the heart to supply oxygenated blood to the body1–4. Underlying etiologies of DCM vary greatly, and include genetic mutations of cardiac sarcomeric proteins4–6, external triggers such as infections or toxin exposure7–9, or the use of certain drugs8,10,11. However, many cases are idiopathic3,12, which may be due to a lack of genetic testing or other unknown causes. DCM often remains undiagnosed until manifestation of heart failure symptoms, which is late in disease progression2,3. Heart failure is a leading cause of death with a five-year survival rate of only ~50%1,2. The current standard of care is to treat all heart failure with reduced ejection fraction patients in the same manner using β-adrenergic receptor blockers, angiotensin-receptor neprilysin inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and sodium-glucose transport 2 inhibitors1,8,13,14. This universal cocktail of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) is initiated regardless of the type and cause of heart failure, comorbidities or other individual patient factors3,4. Some patients fully recover cardiac function on this standardised regimen, while others unpredictably progress to end-stage heart failure requiring heart replacement therapy. As such, there is a critical need to define discrete etiologies and targetable molecular mechanisms of DCM with the hopes of elevating the standard of care with personalised, disease-modifying therapies.

At a molecular level, multiple signalling pathways are dysregulated in DCM. Advancing our understanding of these and how to therapeutically target them is essential. GPCRs play a complex role in the pathobiology of DCM. While precise mechanisms are unclear and multifaceted, these key receptors are targets for most pharmacotherapeutics given to patients with DCM15–17. Namely, the angiotensin II Type 1 receptor (AT1R), endothelin type 1 receptor (ET-1R) and adrenergic receptors (ARs) are linked to changes seen in the myocardium of patients with DCM. While AT1R and ARs are targets for several of the aforementioned GDMT, ET-1R-targeting medications are not currently commonly used for cardiovascular disease, although their implications have been long investigated18. Protein kinase A (PKA)16,19,20, extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase (ERK1/2)21 and calcium are key downstream effectors of these GPCRs in the myocardium, affecting contractility22,23 and cell survival22,23. The use of genetically encoded biosensors can distinguish signalling patterns between intracellular compartments and refine the characterisation of patient-specific molecular profiles and treatment responses24,25. By using biosensors with a nuclear export signal (NES) or nuclear localisation signal (NLS), PKA and ERK1/2 changes can be tracked in the cytoplasm or nucleus, respectively. Cytoplasmic PKA and ERK1/2 activity induces more immediate cellular responses, whereas nuclear activity induces more long-term effects on the regulation of gene expression26,27. Pairing molecular signalling data with functional measures in patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell cardiomyocytes can provide a more comprehensive understanding of individuals’ disease mechanisms. Electrophysiological signals directly affect intracellular calcium handling, which in turn regulates cardiac contraction, all of which are regulated by upstream cell signalling activity28,29. Thus, monitoring electrophysiological and contractile properties, and pairing these assays with calcium transient analysis in cardiomyocytes using biosensors such as RGECO-TnT30,31, offers deeper translational insights into DCM pathophysiology32–34. This multimodal approach distinguishes unique phenotypic signatures at a population level, but can also reveal how cell signalling and functional outcomes become uncoupled to drive DCM pathophysiology in individual patients.

Protocols to generate and use human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) derived from patient blood samples have facilitated research in more relevant translational disease models than previously used animal models and immortalised cell lines35,36. Since several factors play into an individual’s circumstances and disease, moving away from such generalised disease models may bring more relevant and applicable insight into treating patients. Numerous reports have established the use of human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) to model DCM and other cardiovascular diseases37–41. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that hiPSCs recapitulate disease phenotypes, though few studies have evaluated patient-specific phenotypes of DCM across these models and how these may or may not be linked. Here, we aim to fill this gap by describing a pipeline that generates hiPSCs from blood samples from DCM patients and provides a personalised and predictive platform for characterising molecular signalling and function in hiPSC-CMs at single-cell resolution. With hiPSC-CMs generated from an initial cohort of four subjects, including two healthy controls and two DCM patients, we aim to show that independent signalling and functional responses can be measured that reflect clinically relevant phenotypes, which may reveal insights into mechanisms altered in DCM.

Results

Subjects

We began with two patients with idiopathic DCM, and two sex- and age-matched healthy control subjects: a 45-year-old healthy female (Control 1) and a 54-year-old healthy male (Control 2). Patient 1 is a 48-year-old Caucasian female with a previous medical history of breast cancer. She was diagnosed with heart failure soon after her breast cancer surgery at age 44, when she presented to the hospital with new acute decompensated heart failure and was found to have non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy with a dilated left ventricle with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 20%, presumed to be secondary to anthracycline chemotherapy. GDMT was initiated, and the patient has not had any subsequent heart failure hospitalisations, and LVEF has fully recovered on the most recent imaging. At her most recent follow-up, she had minimal symptoms and a normal serum B-natriuretic peptide (BNP) level. Patient 2 is a 41-year-old Southeast Asian male with no known previous medical history. He was diagnosed with heart failure at age 38 when he presented with new shortness of breath and was found to have a LVEF of 10%–15% and no significant obstructive coronary artery disease on coronary angiogram. His non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy was presumed to be secondary to alcohol use. He was started on GDMT at the time and has not had any subsequent heart failure hospitalisations, and his LVEF has improved to 45%, and he remains asymptomatic. Neither Patient 1 nor Patient 2 was found to have mutations in DCM-associated genes.

Generation and validation of hiPSC- CMs from patient hiPSCs

Blood samples from patients allowed for PBMC isolation and reprogramming into hiPSCs using the Yamanaka factors. hiPSCs were validated for pluripotency, chromosomal normality and gene expression (Fig. 1A). All hiPSC lines expressed the pluripotency markers Nanog, OCT4, SSEa-4 and TRA-1-60 (Figs. 1B and S1–S3) and showed high levels of gene expression for Oct4, Nanog and Sox2 (Fig. 1C and S1-S3). All lines were capable of being differentiated into mesoderm, ectoderm and endoderm lineages, as can be seen by positive staining with brachyury, Otx2 and Sox17 respectively (Fig. 1D and S1-S3). Karyotyping of these lines also revealed normal chromosomes (Figs. 1E and S1–S3). These validated hiPSCs provided a tool to generate patient-specific hiPSC-CMs using an established protocol. The CM differentiation was deemed successful with the initiation of beating between days 7–10. The presence of functional sarcomeres in our cells and spontaneous beating was confirmed in high frame rate videos obtained using RGECO-TnT (Supplementary Videos 1–4) and still images (Fig. S4A). The expression of cardiomyocyte markers, including MYH7 (a myosin heavy chain particular to ventricular cardiomyocytes), TNNT2 (encoding troponin T2) and TNNI3 (encoding troponin I), in hiPSC-CMs further confirmed the cell identity in our cultures (Fig. S4B). Typical differentiations yield ~85% cardiomyocyte purity. Assessing nuclear and cytoplasmic PKA and ERK activity across hiPSC-CM lines using genetically encoded biosensors

We transduced hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes with adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) to transiently express our biosensors of interest. AAV6/9 are ideal for efficient transduction of cardiomyocytes in vitro, and we used the constitutive promoter CAG to drive expression of our biosensors. We expressed these biosensors in hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, and signalling was measured in 96-well plates to generate robust time-response measurements using FRET. The ratiometric nature of the biosensors means that the expression levels are intrinsically normalised, although all biosensors across all four subjects revealed similar donor fluorescence across differentiations for the ExRai PKA biosensors (Fig. S5, top row). Since the ratio of donor and acceptor is fixed in the FRET-based ERK1/2 biosensors, changes in expression levels do not lead to changes in FRET measurements, if the detection of fluorescence and FRET remains in the linear range of the instruments used (Fig. S5, bottom row).

Different forms of the FRET-based biosensors allowed us to assess the diversity of responses between individual cells and in distinct subcellular locations. By assessing more distal signalling outcomes using a small number of passive FRET-based protein kinase reporters, we avoided caveats associated with signalling measures that require overexpression of receptors. These biosensors do not alter signalling dynamics, as they are not functional as signalling molecules and rather act as passive reporters of cell status. A significant amount of signalling downstream of all four receptors of interest (AT1R, endothelin receptors, α- and β-adrenergic receptors) in cardiomyocytes converges on two critical effector pathways involving either PKA or ERK1/2. These are associated with contractility, survival and remodelling in the heart and so were selected as the biosensors of interest. Normalising to positive controls for each pathway demonstrated similar patterns across subjects (Fig. S6). However, for all observations that follow, the non-normalised data were selected to properly consider maximal activation across subjects.

ExRai-AKAR and EKAR biosensors were used to track signalling activity of PKA and ERK, respectively, in both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments in hiPSC-derived CMs. This allowed us to separate the effects in different intracellular compartments, which can be seen visually (Fig. 2B, Fig. 3B). Our results show that individual subjects displayed unique signalling profiles. Perhaps unsurprisingly, no responses were seen in any of the subjects for either cytosolic or nuclear PKA in response to multiple ligands for the angiotensin II type 1 receptor (Fig. 2C, D, left panels), regardless of whether we examined the canonical ligand, angiotensin II (Ang II) or two known β-arrestin biased ligands sarcosine1, isoleucine8 Ang II (SI) or [Sar(1), Ile(4), Ile(8)] Ang II (SII)42,43. We next examined responses to adrenergic ligands. Interestingly, the effects of forskolin, a strong activator of the PKA pathway (and so a measure of overall capacity), via activation of adenylyl cyclase, were similar between the Control 1 and Patient 1 in either compartment (Fig. 2C, D, middle panels). However, in Patient 2, forskolin revealed a reduced system capacity compared to the male Control 2 in both the cytosol and the nucleus (Fig. 2C, D, middle panel). For cytoplasmic PKA, responses to the β-adrenergic agonist isoproterenol showed similar responses compared to the positive control forskolin across the 4 subjects (Fig. 2C, middle panels). For PKA activity in the cytoplasm, the response to all drugs other than carvedilol showed significant differences between Patient 1 and Patient 2 (Fig. 2C, middle panel). Notably there were no significant differences seen between the two females or the two males for this data. For the female subjects, a non-significant increase was seen for all these ligands in the cytosolic compartment for Patient 1 compared to Control 1, which may reflect an effect on α-adrenergic receptor signalling. This is also suggested by the increased cytosolic PKA signal in Patient 1 in response to the α1-adrenergic agonist, phenylephrine, compared to all other subjects.

However, for nuclear PKA, there was no difference between the female and male control subjects.While for the female subjects there was a non-significant trend of higher nuclear PKA activity in Patient 1, the opposite is seen in the male subjects, where Patient 2 has decreased activity compared to Control 2. Patient 1, however, had a significantly higher activity with isoproterenol and epinephrine in nuclear PKA compared to Patient 2, suggesting an upregulation in β-adrenergic receptor-mediated effects. No significant response was detected for the β-adrenergic antagonist carvedilol across cytoplasmic and nuclear PKA for any patient. For epinephrine and norepinephrine, ligands that activate both α- and β-adrenergic receptors, little difference was detected between male Control 2 and Patient 2 for cytosolic or nuclear PKA.

We also examined the effects on endothelin-1 (ET-1)-mediated cytosolic and nuclear PKA activity. Little difference was detected across the four subjects in either case (Fig. 2C, D, right panels). In the case of the phorbol ester (PMA), which activates PKC, not much effect was seen in the nucleus, but interestingly, PKA was activated by some sort of crosstalk mediated by PKC for all patients.

Next, we explored activation of cytosolic or nuclear ERK1/2 in response to the same ligands. Again, regardless of whether we examined the canonical ligand, angiotensin II (Ang II) or two known β-arrestin biased ligand SI, we did not detect much activation of ERK1/2 in either compartment (Fig. 3C, D, left panels). Notably, for Patient 2, all ligands showed some trend to increase ERK1/2 in the cytoplasm more compared to all other subjects, yet only SII had a significant effect compared to Control 2 (Fig. 3C, left panel). Instead, in the nucleus, SII induced a significant increase in ERK1/2 signalling in Patient 1 only (Fig. 3D, left panel). Responses to the α- or β-adrenergic agonists were variable across the four subjects, but in most cases these ligands didn’t activate ERK1/2 signalling in either the cytosol or the nucleus, instead showing efficacy as inverse agonists (Fig. 3C, D, middle panels). Patient 1 had the strongest inverse agonist activity in cytoplasmic ERK1/2 activity, with forskolin and isoproterenol having some significant differences (Fig. 3C, middle panel). Even forskolin was inactive, except in nuclear ERK1/2 activity for Control 2, where there was weak agonist activity that is significant compared to Patient 2 (Fig. 3D, middle panel). For endothelin-1 (ET-1)-mediated cytosolic ERK1/2 activation showed no difference between any patient (Fig. 3C, left panel). Nuclear ERK1/2, however, showed significantly higher activity for Patient 1 compared to the two control subjects (Fig. 3D, right panel).

Single-cell data reveals sub-populations of cells based on cellular responses

While data from bulk drug responses can reveal the overall effects of drug stimulation, our high-content biosensor data were further analysed at the single cell level to better understand the heterogeneity of cellular responses inherent in our dataset. Data from each biosensor experiment used in Figs. 2 and 3 were further analysed using R to segregate the responses into clusters. Figure 4 shows the analysis for different ligands across the four subjects for cytosolic PKA activation. For each subject, there were between 3 and 4 clusters, or sub-groups, identified. These clusters shown range from having a negative response: indicating a decrease from baseline, no response: indicating no change from baseline, and having varying degrees of positive response: indicating an increase from baseline (Fig. 4, left panels). The ratios of these clusters vary according to the ligand used and match well with the analysis above in Figs. 2 and 3. Most cells show small responses, but the number of responding cells changes in response to ligand (Fig. 4, right panels). Such heterogeneity highlights that the extent of response seen in the bulk data is the effect of a small pool of cells having a very strong effect44,45. It is clear that in the absence of stimulation, there was little basal activity for cytoplasmic PKA. In Patient 1, there are strong increases in the number of cells responding to the α-adrenergic agonist phenylephrine compared to Control 1, matching the bulk response data (Fig. 2D, middle panel). The effects of norepinephrine and epinephrine further reflect this with an increased proportion of cells activated in Patient 1. With respect to nuclear PKA signalling, analysis also led to three similar clusters of strong activation, modest activation, or no activation, the balance of which was shifted by the different ligands (Fig. S7). In no case was there any evidence for constitutive activation of PKA signalling, and none of the ligands acted as inverse agonists. However, in the case of cytosolic and nuclear ERK1/2, there was an additional cluster evident because of higher basal activity. Many of the ligands showed reduced responses compared to vehicle alone, suggesting that they inhibited ERK1/2 that was activated either constitutively or because of other pathways of activation independent of the GPCRs we studied (Figs. Evaluating differences in calcium handling across hiPSC-CM cell lines upon β-adrenergic stimulation

We assessed differences in cardiomyocyte function by measuring calcium transients using a genetically encoded myofilament-localised biosensor for calcium, RGECO-TnT (Fig. 5A–C). Upon high-frame rate imaging, mean fluorescence values were collected at the single-cell level over time using Image J and imported into a custom R script to extract features of transients for each cell (Fig. 5D–G). Across both healthy control and DCM lines, a similar basal beat rate was observed at roughly 40 bpm. However, responses varied upon treatment with isoproterenol, a β-adrenergic receptor agonist with known positive inotropic and chronotropic effects. Though almost all subjects showed a significant increase in beat rate with isoproterenol, Patient 2 surprisingly displayed almost no chronotropic effect upon treatment. Patient 1 showed decreased values for calcium transient amplitude, upstroke velocity and area under the curve, particularly when compared with Patient 2. However, we observed no significant changes in these parameters for any subjects upon isoproterenol treatment. Though unexpected, the lack of an effect of isoproterenol on amplitude is consistent with other reports using calcium indicators in hiPSC-CMs and may be a result of less developed sarcoplasmic reticulum stores and handling machinery in these relatively immature cells46–48. Assessing electrophysiological and contractile properties in hiPSC-CM lines upon adrenergic stimulation

hiPSC-CMs were next assessed for functional effects in the absence and presence of adrenergic receptor stimulation using the CardioExcyte 96 system (Fig. 6A, B). Extracellular field potential (EFP) recordings revealed similar basal beat rates across both healthy control cell lines, with a slight decrease in Control 2, although differences between subjects or between sexes are perhaps not surprising (Fig. 6C). Isoproterenol increased beat rate by roughly 20–30 beats per minute in all subject lines. EFP amplitude, corresponding to the magnitude of depolarisation, was decreased in both patient cell lines compared to healthy control lines, although only Control 2 had statistically elevated amplitude compared to the two patients (Fig. 6D). However, no significant change was observed for EFP amplitude in response to β-adrenergic stimulation with isoproterenol. cFPD was similar across all lines, all with a decreasing, yet statistically insignificant trend upon isoproterenol treatment (Fig. 6E). Impedance-based contractility recordings from hiPSC-CMs paced at 1 Hz reveal a slightly longer duration of contraction in the male subjects, particularly in Control 2, as indicated by a higher pulse width at 50% amplitude, which expectedly showed a significant reduction upon isoproterenol treatment in all subjects other than Control 1 (Fig. 6F). The magnitude of contraction, measured through amplitude of the impedance curve, was reduced in female subjects, most notably in Patient 1, which displayed a significant reduction both with and without isoproterenol treatment compared to all other subjects (Fig. 6G). Isoproterenol resulted in a slight, though statistically insignificant increase in the magnitude of contraction, except in Control 2 where a more robust increase was observed. Determining dose-dependent functional changes in hiPSC-CMs upon adrenergic and endothelin receptor stimulation

The effects of key adrenergic and endothelin receptor agonists identified in our signalling assays were subsequently assessed on beat rate and contraction amplitude, as measured by EFP and IMP recordings, respectively, in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 7). Upon phenylephrine treatment, we observed no apparent increase in either parameter at all doses, including in Patient 1, where we observed a notable increase in cytoplasmic PKA activity. Unsurprisingly, β-adrenergic stimulation with isoproterenol resulted in a positive chronotropic response in all subjects, with the male Control 2 having the lowest change in beat rate. However, very minimal effects were observed at the level of amplitude for impedance recordings of all subjects, corresponding to almost no change in the magnitude of contraction. Upon endothelin receptor activation with ET-1, we observed a notable increase in beat rate, contrary to what was observed in cell signalling assays, where there was minor PKA activity. Discussion

DCM can broadly be divided into cases with clear genetic predisposition, cases triggered by an insult or exposure, or cases where the cause is idiopathic49–54. Ideally, by more precisely defining etiologies of DCM and how individuals behave through our translational research pipeline, we can begin to further categorise these subgroups. Many unanswered questions remain regarding patient-specific responses to treatment. When DCM leads to heart failure, multiple pharmacotherapies can improve survival, but they are not stratified across patient subpopulations. Current practice is to start all patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction on all four pillars of GDMT as quickly as possible and uptitrate to maximally tolerated doses. If there are contraindications to these medications, there are a few alternative or second-line agents available. There is limited knowledge regarding whether or why certain patients respond to specific heart failure medications. Moreover, there is no evidence regarding if customising the order in which heart failure drugs are started and dosed could provide additional individualised benefit. Uptitration of GDMT commonly takes weeks to months, and patients must wait more months to see if their cardiac function will recover in response before escalating heart failure treatment is considered. This delay in initiating advanced heart failure therapy may contribute to poor outcomes and illustrates a need for more personalised approaches to improve patient care.

We used four different hiPSC lines in this proof-of-concept study to demonstrate individual differences in cellular signalling, calcium handling, electrophysiology and contractility in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 8). This work establishes a valuable translational pipeline that must be applied to larger cohorts to investigate meaningful differences between individuals at a molecular and functional level to ultimately build detailed personalised disease profiles. The balance of different drivers in the context of DCM might differ on a patient-by-patient basis, and these pathways can be linked to the functional status of the cardiomyocyte. Alterations in sensitivity to distinct GPCR ligands could be targetable therapeutically and pave the way for personalised treatment for patients that share this molecular signature. Linking cell signalling features to functional properties adds to the power of this pipeline. However, this work reveals the complexity of matching such cellular signalling and functional data.Fig. 8Integrated schematic of signalling and functional parameters measured in hiPSC-CMs.Cytosolic PKA activity is modulated by Gαs- and Gαi-coupled GPCRs, while PKC activity and Ca2+ release are regulated by Gαq-coupled GPCRs. Notably, electrical stimulation initiates cardiac contraction, resulting in calcium transients which modulate cardiac contractility.

For instance, Patient 1 showed a unique increase in PKA activity upon treatment with phenylephrine, which may be explained by non-canonical α1-adrenergic receptor signalling or downstream cross-talk between protein kinase C (PKC) and PKA. Overexpression of α1-adrenergic receptor has been previously implicated in the generation of DCM in a mouse model55 and curiously may be of benefit in pressure overload models56. Patient 1 also had a unique high nuclear ERK1/2 activity with endothelin-1. Previous work has shown that α1-adrenergic and endothelin receptors use different mechanisms to regulate gene expression in rat neonatal ventricular cardiomyocytes57,58. While both activate Gq signalling, previous work showed that the α1-AR also activates a distinct pathway involving Gs signalling, a feature detected here in Patient 1, but not Patient 2 or the healthy subjects58. Functionally, calcium transient, extracellular field potential and impedance amplitudes were found to be lower in Patient 1 compared to the other three individuals, which may translate to an inherent need for inotropic support in this individual. When phenylephrine was tested for functional consequences, we observed no changes in beat rate and impedance amplitude with increasing doses across all four subjects. This was surprising, given the particularly high PKA activity seen in Patient 1 upon phenylephrine treatment. However, since phenylephrine induced a lower degree of PKA activity compared to β-adrenergic agonists that would increase beat rate, such as isoproterenol, this suggests that the threshold of PKA activity may not have been sufficient to induce functional changes. Alternatively, PKA activity may be differentially compartmentalised or uncoupled to functional outcomes. This could be further investigated using biosensors that localise to other subcellular compartments within the cell, including the plasma membrane, mitochondria, Golgi and endoplasmic reticulum59,60.

Signalling assays in hiPSC-CMs from Patient 2, a male patient diagnosed with alcohol-induced DCM, revealed decreased PKA activity upon adrenergic stimulation with isoproterenol and other adrenergic agonists compared to other hiPSC-CM lines in this study. This can be reflected in functional assays, where isoproterenol showed a minimal effect on beat rate in calcium transient analysis. Surprisingly, a positive chronotropic effect was observed in DCM Patient 2 upon isoproterenol treatment in extracellular field potential recordings, an effect which may be attributed to pacing at 1 Hz during concurrent impedance recordings. In this patient, maximum activation by experimental positive controls was overall lower compared to the other three individuals, which points to some desensitisation of the adrenergic system in this patient. While response to treatment may vary between individuals, it is also important to note that basal molecular signalling can also vary in hiPSC-CMs, which reflects inherent phenotypic differences between different individuals as well.

Measuring ERK1/2 signalling revealed no major differences between individuals. In part, this may be related to the complexity of ERK1/2 regulation and different factors controlling activity that are unrelated to our targeted GPCRs61. The only notable result here was Patient 1’s high nuclear ERK1/2 activity with endothelin-1. Functionally, endothelin-1 increased beat rate for all subjects and showed a decreased trend in impedance amplitude in a dose-dependent manner. This suggests that endothelin-1 causes a negative ionotropic response but a positive chronotropic effect. Conversely, isoproterenol increased both inotropy and chronotropy in a dose-dependent fashion, demonstrating a clear signalling phenotype to functional translation. Though unexpected, these functional outcomes align well with previous reports showing similar effects of acute endothelin-1 exposure in cardiomyocytes62–64. Our pilot data suggest endothelin-1 may mediate functional effects in a PKA-independent manner. These results emphasise the need to investigate both signalling and functional outcomes.

Future directions of this translational research pipeline include morphological analysis of hiPSC-CMs by quantifying the presence of key maturation markers. While computationally complex to develop, such tools would enrich our ability to cluster single-cell responses by pairing drug-induced signalling with protein expression by immunofluorescence. Beating cardiomyocytes could be treated with blebbistatin65 or mavacamten66 as required to immobilise cells for such analyses. Matching cells between successive measurements poses a technical challenge, as plates usually move within the microscope. Such obstacles can be approached using a process called image registration (see: https://github.com/jonclayden/RNiftyReg), designed to align images from different measurements into a single coordinate system. Single-cell responses could be correlated with markers of cell identity and maturation for better clustering of disease-relevant cell populations to track both disease progression and therapeutic outcomes.

The ability to perform single-cell analysis of molecular signalling activity combined with functional assays in hiPSC-CMs enables deeper investigation of disease pathophysiology and may have repercussions for drug discovery and usage. We show that cellular behaviours are multifaceted and there can be a disconnect between molecular signalling and functional activity. Examining larger groups of people might reveal inherent sex differences in molecular signalling that can be matched to clinically relevant disease phenotypes. Additionally, examining hiPSC-CMs from patients with known genetic etiologies may reveal mutation-specific disease mechanisms that were not investigated in the present study. Although our sample size is not powered to draw disease-level conclusions, this experimental throughput proves variability between individuals down to single-cell responses. As more patients are assessed through this platform, they could be clustered into subgroups based on coupled signalling and functional signatures. Matching translational hiPSC-CM data to the patient’s clinical data has the potential to reveal new personalised disease mechanisms, biomarkers and targets for pharmacotherapy. This translational research pipeline also provides a potential platform for personalised testing of drug efficacy and safety. The ability to conduct clinical trials in a dish could allow for testing new pharmacotherapy in a more relevant preclinical disease model, informing both functional and signalling outcomes. As such, there is hope we can eventually progress beyond current GDMT with patient-specific, disease-modifying therapies that preserve or restore cardiomyocyte contractility and heart function for patients with DCM.

Methods

Subject recruitment and sample processing

The Research Ethics Board of the McGill University Health Network approved the study (REB 2020-6362). Patients over 18 years of age with non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy (including heart failure with reduced, mildly reduced and preserved ejection fraction) of any aetiology were recruited from the McGill University Health Centre. Study patients provided informed consent to participate in the research study. Normal controls were defined as individuals without known cardiac or pulmonary disease. Retrospective and prospective clinical data were collected for all study patients between Sept 2023–May 2025 using patient electronic records through the McGill University Health Centre. All available Heart Failure Clinic visits and hospitalisations at our institution were reviewed, including medication records. Imaging data was collected from all available echocardiogram reports. Hemodynamic data were collected from all available right heart catheterisation reports. Aetiology of heart failure and other clinical variables were confirmed by the treating Advanced Heart Failure cardiologist when required. Forty microliters of whole blood were drawn at the time of study recruitment at bedside in EDTA tubes using standard phlebotomy equipment. Blood samples were placed on ice immediately following blood draw and refrigerated within 2 h of collection. Blood samples were processed within 24 h of collection.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) isolation

Peripheral blood from study patients was collected, placed at 4 °C and processed within 12 h of acquisition. Each sample was diluted 1:1 with EasySep™ Buffer (STEMCELL Technologies, 20144), and layered above 15 mL Lymphoprep™ (STEMCELL Technologies, 07851) in a SepMate™-50 tube (STEMCELL Technologies, 85450). Tubes were centrifuged at 1200 × g for 10 min at room temperature (RT). The upper layer containing plasma and PBMCs was removed and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at RT, and residual plasma was removed from the PBMC pellet. For cryopreservation, 1350 µL of foetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, 16141-079) was added to the cell pellet. The cell suspension was transferred to a single cryovial. Subsequently, 180 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma Aldrich, D2650-100ML) was added, and the vial was immediately placed in a rate freezing container (Sigma Aldrich, C1562). For long-term storage, cryovials were transferred to liquid nitrogen storage.

PBMC culture and reprogramming into hiPSCs

PBMCs were grown in StemSpan™ SFEM II medium supplemented with CC100 (STEMCELL Technologies, 02697) with daily media changes. After 7 days, cells were pooled and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at RT. The pellet was resuspended in StemSpan™ SFEM II medium + CC100 and counted to ensure a minimum of 1 × 10⁵ cells per transfection event. Plasmids from the Epi5™ Episomal iPSC Reprogramming Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A15958) containing OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, L-MYC, LIN28 and a shRNA modulating p53 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A15960), were thawed in the 37 °C water bath, and 1 µL per plasmid was added directly to the resuspended cells, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Electroporation was performed using the Neon™ Transfection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MPK1025) with the following settings: Voltage: 1650 V, Pulse width: 10 ms, Pulse number: 3. Immediately after electroporation, cells were resuspended in 6 mL of StemSpan™ SFEM II + CC100 medium. Two microliters of cell suspension per well was then plated onto a Matrigel™-coated plate (Corning, 354277). On day 2, 1 mL StemSpan™ SFEM II + CC100 per well was carefully added. On day 3, 1 mL of either ReproTeSR™-E7 medium (STEMCELL Technologies, 05926) or mTeSR-Plus (STEMCELL Technologies, 100-0276) medium was added to each well. On day 5, a media change with either ReproTeSR™-E7 medium or mTeSR-Plus was performed. On day 7 onwards, a daily medium change was performed with either ReproTeSR™-E7 medium or mTeSR-Plus. Between days 15 and 25, hiPSC colonies appeared.

Colonies with typical human hiPSC morphology were manually isolated and transferred to fresh Matrigel™-coated plates. Multiple colonies per patient line were passaged in parallel to preserve clonal diversity and ensure continued viability. Media was replaced daily with fresh mTeSR™ Plus. Passaging with Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (STEMCELL Technologies, 07174) was continued for several passages according to the manufacturer’s instructions until colony morphology appeared at a high degree of purity (>95%). At each passage, a portion of the cell pellet was gently resuspended in CryoStor® CS10 (STEMCELL Technologies, 07930) and frozen using a standard controlled-rate freezing protocol (approximately −1 °C per minute), followed by long-term storage in liquid nitrogen.

Immunofluorescence cell staining for pluripotency markers

To evaluate the expression of pluripotency markers, hiPSCs were seeded onto Matrigel™-coated chamber slides (15,000 cells per chamber in 4-well slides, Corning, 354114) or glass coverslips placed in 24-well plates (15,000 cells per well, Corning, 07-200-84). Cells were cultured for 48 h to allow colonies of 50–300 µm to form, before proceeding with immunostaining. Cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Electron Microscopy Sciences) in PBS (Gibco, 10010023). Cells were then permeabilised by incubating with 500 µL of 0.5% Triton X-100 (prepared in PBS) for 15 minutes at RT. Cells were then washed three times with PBS, and blocking was performed using 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma Aldrich, A7906) in PBS at RT for 1 h. The following pluripotency markers were used: rabbit anti-OCT-4 (Cell Signalling Technology) (1:200), mouse anti-SSEA-4 (Cell Signalling Technology) (1:500), rabbit anti-NANOG (Cell Signalling Technology) (1:150), and mouse anti-TRA-1-60(S) (Cell Signalling Technology) (1:650). Slides were incubated with the primary antibody solutions diluted in 3% BSA overnight at 4 °C. Cells were then washed three times with PBS. Fluorophore-conjugated AlexaFluor488 secondary antibodies (Life Technologies) diluted 1:500 in 3% BSA were added to each well and incubated for 1 h at RT. Secondary antibodies were aspirated, and cells were washed three times with PBS. For nuclear and cytoskeletal staining, NucBlue™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R37605) and ActinRed555 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R37112) were added at 2 drops per mL of PBS and cells were incubated for 30 min at RT. 3 more PBS washes were performed. To mount the stained colonies, one drop of Permafluor™ mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, TA-030-FM) was added to each sample and allowed to set at 4 °C for 24 hours. Images were acquired with a Zeiss fluorescent microscope.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR for pluripotency and cardiomyocyte marker expression

RNA extraction was performed according to the Aurum Total RNA Mini Kit (Biorad, 732682) instructions. Once isolated, the RNA was either used immediately or stored at −20 °C for up to 1 month or at −80 °C for long-term storage. cDNA Synthesis began with thawing RNA samples on ice and measuring RNA concentration using a spectrophotometer. For reverse transcription, steps were followed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, 1725037). Negative controls included a ‘no RT’ control and a ‘no template’ control. The resulting cDNA was stored on ice for immediate use, at −20 °C up to 1 month or −80 °C for long-term storage. RT-qPCR was carried out using appropriately diluted cDNA samples. The PCR reaction mix for each well included 10 µL of 2x SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 µL each of 10 µM forward and reverse primers, and 7 µL of nuclease-free water, for a final reaction volume of 19 µL. The total mix was added to each well, followed by 1 µL of the sample. RT-qPCR was then performed according to standard cycling parameters. Gene expression was quantified using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${2}^{-\mathrm{ddCt}}$$\end{document}2−ddCt method, normalising to GAPDH as a housekeeping gene. Raw Ct values < 35 were excluded from analysis and were marked as undetectable.

hiPSC validation through trilineage differentiation

Trilineage differentiation was performed in accordance with the STEMCELL Technologies protocol. Briefly, STEMdiff™ Trilineage medias (STEMCELL Technologies, 05230) corresponding to one of each lineage-specific endoderm, mesoderm, or ectoderm were added to the wells. The media was changed daily for 4 consecutive days for mesoderm and endoderm, and for 6 consecutive days for ectoderm. Cells were then fixed with either 10% formalin for 15 min at RT, or with cold methanol:acetone (1:1) for 10 min at −20 °C. Cells were washed 3 times with PBS and until ready for immunofluorescence using the Human Pluripotent Stem Cell Functional Identification Kit (R&D Systems, SC027B) to confirm presence of Otx2, Brachyury and Sox17, indicating successful differentiation of ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm, respectively, as well as F-actin.

Karyotyping of hiPSC lines

hiPSCs were dissociated and plated onto a Matrigel-coated plate. Cells were cultured at 37 °C with daily media changes for 2–3 days until 50–60% confluency. Colcemid (ThermoFisher, 15212012) was added at 10 µL/mL to arrest cells in metaphase and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, then washed twice with pre-warmed PBS. Warm Accutase (Thermofisher Scientific, 00-4555-56) was added, and plates were incubated for 5 min at 37 °C. Cells were then collected into a 15 mL conical tube. Wells were rinsed and then collected in 14 mL of warm PBS. Cells were centrifuged at 1000 RPM for 10 min at RT, and the supernatant was removed. The pellet was resuspended by flicking the tube gently. Then, 8 mL of pre-warmed 0.075 M KCl was added, and the tube was inverted gently 7 times before incubating at 37 °C (slanted at a 45° angle) for 25 min and approximately 8 drops of RT Carnoy’s fixative (prepared by mixing methanol and glacial acetic acid in a 3:1 ratio) were added to the tube. Cells were centrifuged at 1000 RPM for 10 min, supernatant was removed (leaving ~0.5 mL), and the pellet was resuspended by flicking. 1 mL of cold Carnoy’s fixative was added dropwise, then topped up to 8 mL. The tube was gently inverted 10 times, incubated at −20 °C for 1 h, inverted 10 more times, centrifuged at 1000 RPM for 20 min and the pellet was finally resuspended in 14 mL of cold Carnoy’s fixative for storage. For karyotyping, fixed cells were submitted to the Cytogenomics Facility at The Centre for Applied Genomics (The Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto).

Differentiation of hiPSCs into cardiomyocytes

Control and patient-derived hiPSCs were differentiated into cardiomyocytes following a previously published protocol with minor modifications67. hiPSCs were passaged a maximum of 10 times from the last thaw for each differentiation. Each differentiation accounted for one replicate for an experiment for each patient, and only differentiations with successfully beating cardiomyocytes were used for experiments, as seen through brightfield microscopy. Briefly, 350,000 cells were seeded onto Matrigel-coated 24-well dishes in mTeSRPlus media (STEMCELL Technologies, 100-0276) supplemented with 10 μM Rho kinase inhibitor, Y-27632-HCl (Selleckchem, S1049). The following day, the media was removed, and 1 mL fresh mTeSRPlus media was added to the hiPSCs. The differentiation protocol began on day 0 with activation of Wnt signalling by supplementing RPMI 1640 + B27 minus insulin media (ThermoFisher A1895602) with 12 μM Wnt activator and GSK-3 inhibitor, CHIR99021 (Cayman Chemical, 13122). Exactly 24 h later, on day 1, the media wash changed to fresh RPMI 1640 supplemented with B27 minus insulin. On day 3, Wnt signalling was inhibited using 5 μM IWP2 compound (Selleckchem S7085). 48 hours later, on day 5, the media was removed and exchanged with RPMI 1640 supplemented with B27 minus insulin. On day 7 and day 10 of the protocol, the media was changed for RPMI 1640 supplemented with regular B27 (ThermoFisher, 17504001). At this time, spontaneously beating hiPSC-CMs were observed using a brightfield microscope with ×10 or ×20 magnification. Starting at day 12 or 13, metabolic selection was performed to purify the cardiomyocyte population. hiPSC-CMs were starved from glucose for 4-5 days, changing media every 2–3 days with RPMI 1640 without glucose, supplemented with B27 as well as with 4 mM lactate. On day 18, a 6-well dish was coated with 0.5 ml of 1:100 fibronectin in PBS. Wells from the 24-well plate embedded with monolayers of spontaneously beating cardiomyocytes were replated into the fibronectin-coated 6-well dishes. Briefly, cells were dissociated with 300 μL of Accutase per well for 15–30 min at 37 °C until cells were in suspension. 500 uL of RPMI 640 supplemented with B27 with insulin, 5% Knock-Out Serum Reagent (KOSR) (Thermofisher Scientific, 10828028) and 1:1000 ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 was added to each well and cells were collected into a 50 mL conical tube. 1,000,000 cells were plated per well, and the volume was completed to 2 mL per well with the same media. The next day, the wells were rinsed with RPMI 640 media 3 times and finally left in 2 mL per well of RPMI 1640 media supplemented with B27. The cells were maintained with media changes twice per week until day 25 for CardioExcyte96 and RGECO-TnT experiments or day 35–40 for EKAR and ExRai-AKAR signalling experiments. hiPSC-CMs were re-seeded in the same manner into either CardioExcyte 96 sensor plates (Nanion) at a density of 150,000 cells per well or into optical bottom black 96-well plates, with 50,000 cells per well for RGECO-TnT, EKAR and ExRai-AKAR experiments (Nunc, 165305). For each experiment, the n refers to the number of plates from separate differentiations, and 3 technical replicates were done for each biological replicate.

Transduction of hiPSC-CMs with adeno-associated viruses

Serotype 6 adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) used in this study were produced by the Neurophotonics Platform Viral Vector Core at Laval University (Québec, Canada). Two to three days after replating, the plates were washed three times with RPMI 1640 media before transfection with AAVs diluted in RPMI 1640 supplemented with B27 with insulin. A multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5000, representative of 5000 viral genomes per cell, was used for all experiments. Post virus addition, hiPSC-CM cultures were kept in a 37 °C incubator and kept there for 4–6 days for two FRET-based biosensors for ERK1/2 activity, EKAR-NES, EKAR-NLS, two intensiometric biosensors for PKA activity. ExRai-AKAR-NES and ExRai-AKAR-NLS or 10 days for RGECO-TnT to allow for sufficient biosensor expression20,21,30. Transduction was optimised in our previous studies that determined expression in hiPSC-CMs, transduction amounts and timeframes44. For RGECO-TnT, the media was changed for RPMI 1640 supplemented with B27 with insulin after 5 days.

Single-fluorophore intensity-based and dual-colour FRET imaging

On experiment day, media was exchanged for HBSS without phenol red, with calcium, magnesium and sodium bicarbonate (Wisent, 311-513-CL) an hour before imaging. Cells were washed three times prior to leaving the cells in 90 μL HBSS for the assay. For EKAR-NLS and EKAR-NES-based experiments, FRET imaging was collected using a 20X air objective using a 425 nm laser for excitation of the CFP donor. Emissions were detected with filters at 435-515 nm (CFP-donor) and 500–550 nm (YFP-acceptor). For the ExRai-AKAR-NLS and ExRai-AKAR-NES biosensor, images were acquired using a 20X air objective using a 375 nm and 480 nm laser for excitation and a 500–550 nm emission filter. All measurements were taken at 37 °C and 5% CO2 using the Opera Phenix High Content Microscopy System (Revvity) and set up using the built-in Harmony software. Basal, ligand-independent, measurements were taken prior to the drugs being manually injected. For all experiments the following drugs were used: angiotensin II (Ang II) at 1 μM, SI (sarcosine1, isoleucine8 Ang II) at 10 μM, SII ([Sar(1), Ile(4), Ile(8)] Ang II) at 10 μM, isoproterenol (iso) at 10 μM, norepinephrine (nor) at 10 μM, epinephrine (epi) at 10 μM, phenylephrine (phen) at 10 μM, carvedilol (carv) at 1 μM, endothelin-1 (ET-1) at 100 nM, forskolin (fors) at 5 μM and PMA (1 μM). The microscope then continued collecting 5 measurements post-drug stimulation at 10-min intervals.

Single-fluorophore intensity-based and dual-colour FRET analysis

Columbus image analysis software (Revvity) was used for all image analysis. Using the ‘find nuclei’ function for NLS experiments and the ‘find cell’ for NES experiments, nuclei or cytoplasm were selected, respectively. Morphological features such as cell area and roundness were used to select a population that excluded debris, overlapping cells, or background fluorescence. Intensity properties were calculated for the YFP/CFP ratio as well as the GFP excitation ratio, for EKAR biosensors and ExRai-AKAR biosensors, respectively. Data of each individual nucleus or cell was then exported for further analysis with analysis R script.

Single cell datasets were treated in R based on previously published single-cell analytical approach45,68 with minor modifications (code available at: https://github.com/HebertLab/Single-Cell-FRET-version-2). Briefly, cardiomyocytes that appeared in all 6 timepoints (baseline and 5 post-stimulation) were carried forward. A threshold of fluorescence intensity, size and distance were set for each and those that did not conform to the parameters were excluded from the analysis. ΔFRET was calculated relative to basal, ligand-independent FRET and was converted to a percentage change (%ΔF/F). In this percentage change in FRET, the denominator (F) was computed by averaging the basal FRET across all nuclei in the same well. Bulk data was plotted by first averaging the FRET values post drug injection and normalising each drug to its respective vehicle. The dtwclust library in R was then used to cluster the cells by the magnitude of response over time. All figures were generated with R script.

Optical mapping and analysis of calcium transients

Single cell calcium transients were recorded using an AAV packaged RGECO-TnT, a genetically encoded, red fluorescent biosensor that localises to troponin T in the myofilament. On the day of imaging, media was exchanged with RPMI 1640 without phenol red to minimise background fluorescence. Spontaneous calcium transients at baseline were captured at 10 frames per second for 4 s with 561 nm excitation and an emission range of 570–630 nm using Opera Phenix (Revvity), with environmental controls set at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The plate was then treated with 10 μM isoproterenol and the same cells were re-imaged in the same manner. Following image acquisition, single cells were manually traced and the mean fluorescence intensity for each image stack was quantified using Image J software. A custom-written script generated through RStudio (code available at: https://github.com/HebertLab/Calcium-Transient-Analysis) was used to plot transients from individual cells and collect mean calcium transient parameters before and after isoproterenol treatment including: transient frequency (beat rate), peak amplitude, area under the curve, upstroke velocity, time to reach 50% peak, time to reach 90% peak, time between peaks and decay tau.

Electrophysiology and contractility recordings

Extracellular field potential (EFP) and impedance-based contractility recordings were generated with the Cardioexcyte 96 system (Nanion). Briefly, cells were seeded in an NSP-96 Transparent 2.0 mm plate at a density of 100,000 cells per well in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with B27 and recorded 7–9 days after replating, after ensuring a successful formation of a complete monolayer of spontaneously beating hiPSC-CMs. Twenty-second sweeps were recorded for both EFP and impedance every 5 min for 60 min pre- and post-treatment with 10 μM isoproterenol. Cells were paced at 1 Hz for impedance recordings.
