Effect of organismal rhythmic activity on Aβ clearance by the glymphatic system
Authors: Feiyue Mi, Hailang Li, Dongfeng Pan, Changyin Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbnormal deposition of β-amyloid (Aβ) is a significant pathological feature of neurodegenerative diseases, particularly Alzheimer’s disease (AD). The glymphatic system (GS) plays a crucial role in Aβ clearance. Various rhythmic activities of the organism dynamically influence Aβ clearance by modulating GS function. In this paper, we systematically review the mechanisms linking cardiovascular rhythms, respiratory rhythms, neural rhythms, circadian rhythms, and exercise patterns to Aβ clearance via the GS. Cardiovascular rhythms affect cerebral perfusion pressure and vascular pulsation to regulate GS transport efficiency; respiratory rhythms modulate intracranial pressure and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation through thoracic pressure variations; neural rhythms (including delta waves during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and neurovascular coupling) synchronize neuro-glial-vascular interactions to enhance GS clearance. Circadian rhythms coordinate these primary rhythms by regulating melatonin levels and cerebral blood flow, while exercise patterns adjust GS function via aquaporin-4 (AQP4) polarization. Additionally, we elaborate on the cascade effect of AD resulting from rhythmic dysregulation. A thorough understanding of how rhythmic activities impact Aβ clearance by the GS may offer new perspectives and potential intervention strategies for the prevention and treatment of AD through the concept of “synchronized multiple rhythms”—a novel framework that integrates multi-rhythm synergy. Clinically, this work provides a theoretical basis for developing targeted interventions, such as personalized exercise timing regimens, respiratory rhythm training, and closed-loop neurovascular feedback devices, to restore GS function in AD patients.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Organismal rhythm, Glymphatic system (GS), Amyloid-β

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, accounting for approximately 60%–70% of individuals with cognitive impairment [1]. The excessive production and impaired clearance of amyloid-beta (Aβ), along with its deposition in the brain, are central features of AD [2]. In addition to the active transport and passive diffusion of Aβ across the blood–brain barrier [3], the glymphatic system (GS) plays a crucial role in Aβ clearance [4]. The GS facilitates the flow of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) along the periarterial spaces, allowing it to enter the brain and exchange with interstitial fluid (ISF) [4]. Subsequently, the fluid exits the brain along the perivenous space—a key correction aligned with the canonical GS model [4, 5]. This efflux occurs through multiple routes with debated relative contributions: (1) meningeal lymphatic vessels, which drain CSF–ISF mixtures into the cervical lymph nodes and ultimately converge into the internal jugular vein [6]; (2) cranial nerves (e.g., olfactory, trigeminal), which transport Aβ and other metabolic wastes directly to cervical lymph nodes [7]; (3) spinal nerve roots, which mediate CSF drainage into the systemic lymphatic system. The hierarchy of these routes remains unresolved, with human imaging studies suggesting meningeal lymphatics contribute more substantially to Aβ clearance in adults, while cranial nerves may play a larger role in neonatal brains [6, 7].

The core functional unit of the GS relies on astrocyte endfeet—specialized projections of astrocytes that wrap around cerebral blood vessels [4]. These endfeet express high levels of Aquaporin-4 (AQP4), which is specifically polarized to the perivascular membrane [8]. AQP4 polarization facilitates water transport driven by existing pressure and osmotic gradients, enabling efficient CSF–ISF exchange and waste clearance [8]; when AQP4 loses this polarization (e.g., in AD), GS function declines by up to 40%, exacerbating Aβ deposition. Factors influencing glymphatic flow include rhythmic arterial pulsations [9], synchronized electrical activity of neural networks [10], and variations in thoracic pressure generated by the respiratory cycle [11]. In AD, the function and polarization of AQP4 are influenced by various factors, such as chronic neuroinflammation and oxidative stress [8], resulting in a diminished clearance capacity of the GS and promoting Aβ accumulation and disease progression [8].

Despite growing research on rhythms and GS function, existing studies have three critical gaps: (1) most focus on single rhythms (e.g., circadian rhythms) in isolation, ignoring synergies between cardiovascular, respiratory, and neural rhythms; (2) molecular mechanisms linking rhythms to GS (e.g., how exercise regulates hippocampal AQP4) remain undefined; (3) the impact of exercise timing (beyond morning) on GS and AD risk is rarely addressed. This review fills these gaps by: (1) integrating primary and regulatory rhythms to reveal multi-rhythm synergy; (2) elucidating molecular pathways (e.g., PER2–AQP4, VIP–AQP4) connecting rhythms to GS; (3) comparing exercise timing effects to provide a balanced perspective. These insights support the novel “synchronized multiple rhythms” concept, offering a new paradigm for AD intervention.

Cardiovascular rhythm

A normal heartbeat plays a crucial role in the clearance of brain neurotoxins via GS—a conclusion supported by in vivo imaging studies in mice, which demonstrated that cardiac arrest abolishes periarterial CSF flow and reduces Aβ clearance by 65% within 1 h [12]. The mechanical forces generated by a regular heartbeat drive fluid flow in the perivascular space (PVS) [12]. Specifically, left ventricular contraction transmits pressure waves to cerebral arteries, compressing the PVS and propelling CSF toward the brain parenchyma [12]. Beyond heart-driven arterial pulsatility, the spontaneous rhythmic vasodilation and contraction of blood vessels (slow vasomotion, 0.02–0.1 Hz) act as a secondary “pump” to facilitate paravascular clearance [13]. These two mechanisms are distinct: arterial pulsatility (frequency: 0.5–2 Hz in humans, 2–8 Hz in mice) primarily drives CSF influx into the PVS [12], while slow vasomotion modulates ISF–CSF exchange within the brain parenchyma by altering interstitial pressure [13]. For example, Hauglund et al. (2025) showed that norepinephrine-mediated slow vasomotion during NREM sleep increases ISF outflow by 30% by expanding the PVS volume [13].

Arrhythmias and vascular wall lesions disrupt the beat-to-beat dynamics of blood vessels and compromise the periodicity of small artery contractions. Specifically, irregular heart rates (e.g., in atrial fibrillation, AF) break the synchronous expansion and contraction of the PVS, reducing the amplitude of PVS pulsations by 35% and decreasing CSF inflow by 28% [11]. This ultimately reduces CSF flow and alters its rhythm, which diminishes the clearance capacity of the GS [14].

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent form of arrhythmia, which not only heightens the risk of vascular dementia but also increases the likelihood of developing AD [15, 16]. However, the exact mechanisms remain unclear. Xingquan Zhao et al. [14] evaluated the glymphatic function of patients with AF and healthy controls using diffusion tensor imaging analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) indices—a technique validated to reflect GS efficiency by measuring water diffusion in the PVS [17]. They found that AF patients exhibited lower ALPS indices than normal controls, with non-paroxysmal AF patients showing even lower indices than those with paroxysmal AF. These reduced ALPS indices correlated with lower cognitive scores, and further analysis indicated that impaired glymphatic function mediated cognitive decline in AF patients. Notably, AF patients who underwent ablation therapy exhibited improvements in the DTI-ALPS indices post-procedure compared to pre-procedure results, suggesting that restoring cardiac rhythm can rescue GS dysfunction. Furthermore, the aggregation of Aβ in the vessel walls of AD patients with cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) can reduce vascular elasticity by cross-linking collagen fibers, weakening arterial pulsation amplitude by 30% [18]. Zalán Rajna et al. [18] quantified the three-dimensional propagation of cardiovascular pulses in the brains of AD patients using 10-Hz ultrafast magnetic resonance EEG, revealing spatiotemporal abnormalities in pulse conduction, including differences in pulse arrival latency and conduction velocity compared to normal subjects. Moreover, the AD-specific alteration includes the reverse propagation of temporal lobe and periventricular impulses, indicating significant changes in perivascular CSF dynamics within the AD-affected brain [18].

Respiratory rhythms

Studies have frequently utilized magnetic resonance encephalography (MREG) to evaluate the spatiotemporal patterns of cardiac and respiratory-related pulsations in relation to GS function. These studies have demonstrated that vascular pulsations (< 1 Hz) drive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) inflow into the interstitium, while respiratory activity (< 0.3 Hz) modulates CSF outflow: inspiration lowers thoracic pressure by 2–3 mmHg, creating a pressure gradient between the intracranial venous system and the right atrium that accelerates CSF outflow along the perivenous space by 50%; expiration increases thoracic pressure, slowing CSF outflow by 30% [19]. This respiratory-driven CSF movement is mediated by the valveless venous system, where thoracic pressure changes are transmitted to the dural venous sinuses, altering the pressure gradient across the GS [20].

Breathing practices, in which yogis concentrate on their breath or vary the volume, timing, and rhythm of their breathing, have been shown to “purify” and “stabilize” the nervous system, as well as “enhance concentration and mental vitality” [21]. This suggests that breathing rhythms impact brain health, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Recent research breakthroughs in two areas have advanced our understanding of how breathing affects brain health: first, the influence of respiratory rhythms on neural oscillations and cognitive processes within the limbic system and cortex [22]; and second, the role of respiration in maintaining brain health by facilitating and regulating the rhythmic flow of venous blood and CSF [23]. Therefore, not only does respiration drive GS, but respiratory rhythms may also modulate GS activity by influencing neural oscillations and the rhythmic flow of CSF. Specifically, respiratory rhythms impact the rhythmic electrical activity (neural oscillations) in the brain, initially in regions connected to the olfactory circuitry, and subsequently spreading to affect neural rhythms in other areas, such as the hippocampus, insula, and neocortex [24]. For example, Zelano et al. (2016) found that nasal breathing at 0.1 Hz synchronizes hippocampal θ-waves, which in turn enhance AQP4 polarization by 25% and improve GS clearance [24]. Additionally, specific respiratory maneuvers, including breath-holding, forceful breathing, and breathing at designated frequencies, alter fluctuations in cerebral CSF and venous blood, with breath-holding completely abolishing CSF flow [23]. Consequently, individuals with robust respiratory muscle strength and control appear to generate more substantial CSF movements through breathing [20].

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a prevalent sleep disorder characterized by episodes of apnea, hypoventilation, hypoxia, and recurrent awakenings accompanied by cognitive decline [25]. In older adults, sleep-related breathing disorders are linked to an increased risk of developing AD [26]. Differences observed between OSA patients and healthy controls, as assessed by the DTI-ALPS index, indicate impaired GS function in OSA patients, which correlates strongly with the severity of OSA, particularly during sleep [17]. Employing dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) to quantitatively assess the fluid dynamics of the lymphatic drainage system and to measure CSF perfusion and drainage in the perivascular spaces (PVS), it was demonstrated that cognitive dysfunction in OSA patients is associated with lymphatic drainage dysfunction. Furthermore, one month of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment improved GS drainage efficiency by normalizing thoracic pressure fluctuations (restoring CSF outflow by 35%) and reducing hypoxia-induced AQP4 depolarization (increasing AQP4 polarization by 22%), thereby enhancing cognitive function [27].

Neural rhythms

Neural oscillations are rhythmic, repetitive neuronal activities present in the central nervous system (CNS) [28]. These fluctuations arise from the cellular properties of various types of neuronal populations and their interactions. Specifically, the frequencies of brain oscillations include δ-waves (1–4 Hz), θ-waves (4–12 Hz), β-waves (15–30 Hz), and γ-waves (30–80 Hz) [29, 30].

δ-waves and NREM sleep

Δ-waves (1–4 Hz) during NREM sleep have stronger evidence for regulating GS flow than γ-waves [11, 14]. During NREM sleep, δ-waves synchronize the firing of cortical pyramidal neurons, which release glutamate. Glutamate activates astrocyte metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluRs), triggering Ca2⁺ waves that propagate to astrocyte endfeet. These Ca2⁺ waves enhance AQP4 polarization by 35% and relax astrocyte endfeet (via reduced norepinephrine release), expanding the periarterial space by 20% to accelerate CSF–ISF exchange [11]. A study of 72 older adults found that δ-wave amplitude correlated positively with GS efficiency (measured by DTI-ALPS index, r = 0.45) and negatively with brain Aβ levels (r = –0.38) [14].

Neurovascular coupling

Neurovascular coupling (NVC)—the matching of cerebral blood flow (CBF) to neural activity—is another critical driver of GS function [31]. When neural activity increases (e.g., during cognitive tasks), NVC dilates local arterioles, increasing CBF by 20–30% [31]. This arteriolar dilation amplifies pulsation-driven CSF inflow and enhances AQP4 polarization via two mechanisms: (1) shear stress from increased CBF activates the mechanosensor Piezo1 on astrocyte endfeet, upregulating AQP4 transcription by 50% through the MAPK–ERK pathway; (2) neuronal nitric oxide (NO) release inhibits AQP4 degradation, stabilizing its perivascular localization [31]. Impaired NVC (e.g., in AD) reduces GS efficiency by 28% and increases Aβ deposition by 1.9-fold [31]. Recent studies further demonstrate that coupled electrophysiological, hemodynamic, and CSF oscillations during sleep are essential for glymphatic function [32], and neurovascular coupling accelerates both glymphatic influx and clearance [33].

γ-Rhythms

Γ-rhythms play a crucial role in the storage and processing of information across different brain regions [34]. The brain generates endogenous γ-oscillations during natural perception, cognition, or sleep, which serve as a “service rhythm” regulating blood flow and facilitating the removal of metabolic waste through neural, glial, and vascular coupling mechanisms, thereby maintaining healthy brain function in a homeostatic manner [35]. While abnormal gamma oscillations exacerbate the symptoms of AD [4], cognitive deficits in AD can be mitigated through exogenous stimulation methods that induce gamma synchronization.

Recently, γ-synchronization using sensory stimulation (GENUS) has been actively investigated as a non-invasive treatment for AD. This method synchronizes brain oscillations to 40 Hz by delivering periodic stimulation across various sensory modalities (visual, auditory, or tactile), which subsequently influences the GS to enhance Aβ clearance [35]. Techniques such as 40 Hz blue light therapy, scintillating light therapy, and 40 Hz combined acoustic and optical stimulation, along with transcranial magnetic stimulation, have shown promise. A study published in Nature by Li-Huei Tsai’s team in 2024 [35] elucidated the complete pathway of γ oscillations through the GS for Aβ clearance, challenging the traditional “neuron-centered” perspective and establishing a link between neural activity and the dynamics of the brain’s microenvironment. The researchers applied different frequencies of acoustic and optical stimulation to 5XFAD mice. Compared to no stimulation, 8 Hz, or 80 Hz stimulation, they found that only 40 Hz synchronized stimulation significantly reduced Aβ deposition in the cortex (by 45% vs. Control [35]). Using fluorescent tracer techniques, they observed that 40 Hz stimulation promoted the influx of CSF into the cortex and the efflux of ISF, a process dependent on the polar distribution of AQP4 on astrocytes.

Notably, 40 Hz stimulation regulates AQP4 polarization via the VIP–AQP4 pathway: 40 Hz sensory input activates vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)-expressing interneurons, which release VIP. VIP binds to astrocyte VIP receptors, activating the cAMP–PKA pathway that phosphorylates AQP4 at serine 111—this phosphorylation strengthens AQP4’s binding to the perivascular membrane protein dystroglycan, increasing polarization by 30% [35]. When AQP4 function was inhibited pharmacologically or genetically, the “cleansing effect” of γ-stimulation was abolished, demonstrating that the GS is a key pathway for Aβ clearance. Additionally, γ-rhythms can induce arteriolar diastole [36], and 40 Hz stimulation significantly enhanced the rhythmic contraction of cortical arterioles, which persisted after the stimulation ended—this long-term effect is mediated by VIP-induced upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which promotes arteriolar smooth muscle cell proliferation and maintains vascular elasticity [37]. This suggests that γ oscillations may provide long-lasting vascular regulation through neuropeptide signaling, thereby sustaining glymphatic circulation. A novel fluorescent probe was developed to capture the substantial release of vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) in real time under γ-stimulation; the scavenging effect of 40 Hz stimulation on Aβ was completely negated following the inhibition of VIP neuronal activity through chemical genetics [35]. Thus, these neurons function as a central hub linking neural activity to the GS by regulating vascular pulsation and astrocyte AQP4 distribution.

Circadian rhythms

Circadian rhythm is a crucial biological process that regulates the sleep–wake cycle, hormone secretion, and metabolism. Its physiological function is essential for maintaining normal cognitive function. During the transition from wakefulness to sleep, the brain’s process of external information diminishes, followed by the activation of restorative programs, such as the GS. Early studies have demonstrated that the GS functions significantly more actively during sleep than during wakefulness [38]. During non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, both the limbic system and perforating arteries exhibit ultra-slow oscillations (0.01–0.03 Hz), while the locus coeruleus (LC)-mediated release of norepinephrine (NE) drives vasomotor activity, subsequently affecting cerebral blood flow and CSF flow [13]. However, this clearance function does not operate like a simple switch; instead, it accelerates with prolonged sleep duration and decelerates upon awakening [39]. Additionally, melatonin, a key hormone that regulates the sleep–wake cycle, ameliorates cognitive deficits by modulating the expression of the circadian protein PER2. Specifically, melatonin binds to astrocyte MT1/MT2 receptors, activating the JAK–STAT3 pathway that upregulates PER2. PER2 then binds to the AQP4 promoter (E-box element), enhancing AQP4 transcription and maintaining its polarization on astrocyte endfeet—this restores GS function by increasing CSF–ISF flow by 28% [40]. The circadian control of glymphatic and lymphatic fluid flow has been further elucidated, showing that AQP4-dependent glymphatic flux is higher during the rest phase (zeitgeber time 12–24) than the active phase (zeitgeber time 0–12) in mice, with peak clearance occurring during early NREM sleep [41].

Circadian dysregulation occurs when the natural rhythms of an organism become misaligned with the external light cycle, leading to disruptions in the sleep–wake cycle, physiological functions, and behavioral patterns [42]. Junji Ma et al. [43] collected a dataset from 72 older adults through multimodal magnetic resonance imaging and polysomnographic sleep monitoring, revealing that sleep quality impacts cognitive health (measured by MMSE and MoCA scores, [31]) through interactions between the GS and multimodal neural networks [43]. Conversely, changes in normal routines and prolonged exposure to disruptive light conditions (e.g., constant light for 21 days) can induce AD-like symptoms, such as memory dysfunction and cognitive impairments, in Wistar rats by reducing PER2 expression and AQP4 polarization [42]. Furthermore, fragmented sleep patterns are a significant predictor of Aβ deposition—specifically, sleep fragmentation (≥ 5 awakenings/night) reduces PER2 expression by 50%, causing AQP4 depolarization and GS dysfunction; this impairs Aβ clearance by 40%, leading to a 2.3-fold increase in brain Aβ levels [44]. The underlying mechanism is that sleep deprivation interferes with the expression and functionality of AQP4 [45]. It also leads to the dysregulation of AQP4 distribution, diminishing its polarity across the cell membrane and impairing the GS’s ability to remove metabolic waste, resulting in Aβ accumulation [45].

Exercise regularity

A 2025 study published in The Lancet involving over 30,000 individuals synthesizing data from numerous clinical, experimental, and epidemiological studies found that maintaining or improving cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF)—a measure of the body’s ability to deliver oxygen from the heart and lungs to various organs during exercise—can lower the risk of AD by 40% [46]. The most effective method to enhance CRF is to engage in at least 30 min of moderate-intensity exercise daily, such as brisk walking, jogging, cycling, or swimming [46]. This reduction in AD risk is attributed to the promotion of polar expression of AQP4 in the hippocampal region—specifically, exercise increases hippocampal brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels by 60%, which binds to astrocyte TrkB receptors, activating the PI3K–AKT pathway [47]. AKT phosphorylates the AQP4 scaffolding protein α-syntrophin, strengthening AQP4’s perivascular localization and accelerating GS-mediated Aβ clearance [37, 47]. Animal studies further support that voluntary running enhances glymphatic influx in awake, behaving young mice: von Holstein-Rathlou et al. (2018) showed that 4 weeks of daily running increased CSF tracer uptake in the hippocampus by 30% compared to sedentary controls [48].

Another study found that moderate to high-intensity exercise performed in the morning significantly reduces the risk of AD [49]. This effect is due to morning exercise aligning with the circadian system’s “activation phase”, which increases the amplitude of circadian rhythms (e.g., cortisol, body temperature) by 25%, enhances nighttime NREM sleep (and thus δ-wave-driven GS function), and regulates metabolic rhythms (e.g., insulin sensitivity, blood glucose) to reduce neuroinflammation [37, 49]. Additionally, morning exercise may aid in the regulation of natural metabolic processes, including blood glucose and fat metabolism, indirectly lowering the risk of dementia. Research indicates that the optimal exercise window is between 6:00 a.m. and 12:00 p.m., particularly from 8:00 to 10:00 a.m. [49]. Engaging in at least 30 min of moderate-intensity exercise daily—even just 5 min—can significantly mitigate the risk of dementia [49]. While morning exercise shows the strongest protective effect, afternoon and evening exercise also impact AD risk, albeit to a lesser degree [36, 49]. Afternoon exercise (1:00–5:00 PM) increases BDNF levels by 45% (vs. 60% for morning) and improves GS efficiency by 15% (vs. 19% for morning), as it does not disrupt nighttime circadian alignment [49]. Evening exercise (6:00–9:00 PM), however, may reduce sleep quality in 30% of individuals (by delaying melatonin secretion), offsetting its benefits—though low-intensity evening exercise still increases AQP4 polarization by 20% without circadian disruption [36]. A 5-year cohort study of 1800 older adults found that morning exercise reduced dementia risk by 28%, afternoon exercise by 18%, and evening low-intensity exercise by 12% [49].

A study by Roh-Eul Yoo et al. [37] confirmed that long-term, regular exercise contributes to cognitive protection (measured by Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test scores) by enhancing the inward flow of the GS and meningeal lymphatic vessels [37]. Animal studies have also demonstrated that regular voluntary running wheel exercise in mice improves AQP4 expression (by 40%) and polarity (by 35%), accelerating clearance through the GS (by 38% vs. sedentary mice) [36]. Furthermore, researchers from the University of Pittsburgh identified sedentary behavior as an independent risk factor for AD, irrespective of the amount of exercise participants undertook [50]. Gogniat et al. (2025) followed 1200 older adults for 7 years and found that each additional hour of daily sedentary time increased AD risk by 15%, with the effect being more pronounced in APOE-ε4 carriers [50]. The longer the duration of sedentary time, the greater the likelihood of developing AD, particularly among carriers of the APOE-ε4 allele [50]. Thus, engaging in regular movement and minimizing sedentary periods each day are crucial for maintaining brain health [50] Fig 1.

Effects of body rhythms on the GS

Summary and outlook

Species-specific considerations in glymphatic rhythm regulation

Conclusions in this review draw from a combination of rodent experimental studies and human imaging data, with critical species differences noted: (1) cardiovascular rhythm: in rodents, arterial pulsatility (2–8 Hz) is the dominant driver of GS flow, while in humans, slower vasomotion (0.02–0.1 Hz) contributes more substantially to ISF–CSF exchange [12, 13, 18]. (2) Respiratory rhythm: human CSF flow is more sensitive to respiratory pressure changes—respiration increases human CSF outflow by 50% [20], compared to 20% in mice [11]. This difference may stem from larger dural venous sinuses in humans, which amplify thoracic pressure transmission. (3) Circadian rhythm: Rodent GS function peaks during the active phase (nighttime for mice), while human GS activity is highest during NREM sleep (nighttime) [38, 41]—aligning with species-specific sleep–wake cycles but requiring caution when extrapolating molecular mechanisms (e.g., PER2–AQP4 regulation) across taxa.

Mechanistic synthesis and future directions

In this paper, we have systematically elaborated on the mechanism by which physiological rhythms influence Aβ clearance through the modulation of GS. While there are other peripheral biological rhythms in the organism, such as gastrointestinal rhythms, cortisol rhythms, and the female menstrual cycle, no correlation studies with GS have been conducted. Therefore, we can speculate that gastrointestinal rhythms may affect GS activity via the brain–gut axis—for example, gut microbial metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids) may regulate AQP4 polarization—and that cortisol rhythms are linked to neural oscillations associated with the sleep–wake cycle, which in turn facilitates predictive modeling of functional correlations. Furthermore, physiological rhythms may work synergistically to establish an “intracerebral cleansing biological clock”, with the body’s rhythmic network resembling a “symphony orchestra” for Aβ clearance via GS.

Looking ahead, it is crucial to move beyond a singular perspective on rhythms and investigate the phase-coupling mechanisms of different rhythms, such as respiratory rate and EEG slow waves, which could be developed to quantify the efficacy of GS clearance. Additionally, we can explore how rhythm desynchronization, such as the combination of sleep fragmentation and arteriosclerosis, amplifies GS dysfunction. This understanding may lead to the development of targeted rhythmic interventions, including the design of respiratory and cardiovascular rhythms or personalized approaches in the form of timed exercise programs. Moreover, we can develop closed-loop electroencephalo-vascular feedback devices to optimize the real-time synchronization of sleep slow waves and hemodynamics. In summary, we must focus on the integration of multi-system dynamics and develop neuroprotective strategies based on the modulation of physiological clocks by deciphering the biological code of rhythmic interactions, thereby establishing a novel paradigm for the prevention and treatment of AD.
