Abstract
. Graph models are fundamental to data analysis in domains rich with complex relationships. Text-to-Graph-Query-Language (Text-to-GQL) systems act as a translator, converting natural language into executable graph queries. This capability allows Large Language Models (LLMs) to directly analyze and manipulate graph data, positioning them as powerful agent infrastructures for Graph Database Management System (GDBMS). Despite recent progress, existing datasets are often limited in domain coverage, supported graph query languages, or evaluation scope. The advancement of Text-to-GQL systems is hindered by the lack of high-quality benchmark datasets and evaluation methods to systematically compare model capabilities across different graph query languages and domains. In this work, we present Text2GQL-Bench , a unified Text-to-GQL benchmark designed to address these limitations. Text2GQL-Bench couples a multi-GQL dataset that has 178,184 (Question, Query) pairs spanning 13 domains, with a scalable construction framework that generates datasets in different domains, question abstraction levels, and GQLs with heterogeneous resources. To support comprehensive assessment, we introduce an evaluation method that goes beyond a single end-to-end metric by jointly reporting grammatical validity, similarity, semantic alignment, and execution accuracy. Our evaluation uncovers a stark dialect gap in ISO-GQL generation: even strong LLMs achieve only ≤ \leq 4% execution accuracy (EX) in zero-shot settings, though a fixed 3-shot prompt raises accuracy to around 50%, the grammatical validity remains ≤ \leq 70%. Moreover, a fine-tuned 8B open-weight model reaches 45.1% EX, and 90.8% grammatical validity, demonstrating that most of the performance jump is unlocked by exposure to sufficient ISO-GQL examples.

Given the established success of Text-to-SQL benchmarks like Spider and BIRD, a natural question arises:
Can we simply repurpose these mature datasets to fill the gap in the graph domain?

In practice, Text-to-SQL benchmarks cannot adequately serve this purpose, because they do not cover graph-native semantics such as reachability, variable-length paths, and structural pattern constraints.
While SQL relies on relational algebra (set operations), Graph Query Languages (GQLs) are rooted in path traversal and topological reachability.
As illustrated in
Figure

2
, the expression of “funds originating from Alice eventually flow back to her through a chain of transfers” typically involves recursive joins and manual constraints in SQL, whereas GQL states the same intent as a single path pattern.
Consequently, training on translated SQL tends to bias models toward join-centric semantics, offering limited exposure to graph-native operators such as variable-length path traversal.

Crucially, prior efforts to connect SQL and GQL have largely focused on aligning individual queries, leaving dataset construction and evaluation for Text-to-GQL underexplored.
For instance, Graphiti
(
12
)
introduces database transformers to check query equivalence across SQL and Cypher, but it is not designed as a benchmark construction pipeline: its translation is strictly unidirectional (embedding Cypher into SQL) and operates on a deliberately restricted, “featherweight” fragment.
As a consequence, it does not support scalable dataset construction with broad coverage of graph-native queries, such as path-oriented operators.
This necessitates a native Text-to-GQL benchmark supporting large-scale dataset construction explicitly covering graph-native query semantics.

Compounding this challenge is the field’s unique dual bottleneck: linguistic fragmentation and a severe data scarcity. Unlike the standardized SQL landscape, the graph domain suffers from a “Tower of Babel” phenomenon, fragmented into different GQLs (e.g., Cypher, ISO-GQL, Gremlin, and SPARQL).
Furthermore, current Text-to-GQL datasets typically cover only a limited amount of databases from a narrow set of domains. To the best of our knowledge, the most domain diverse Text-to-GQL dataset is Neo4j’s Text2Cypher
(
36
)
, which contains 18 databases from 6 domains.

Moreover, high-quality human annotation is costly: producing executable GQL queries with correct entity/constraint grounding typically requires both domain familiarity and proficiency in a specific graph query language.
To reduce annotation cost, recent work explores programmatic generation, where LLMs are guided by templates or structured scaffolds to produce
(Question, GQL)
pairs
(
50
)
.
Some Text-to-GQL datasets generation pipelines are often built with tightly coupled construction protocols, where schema exposure, annotation choices, and evaluation splits are co-designed for a specific setting, making results difficult to compare across studies.
For example, StockGQL
(
26
)
targets the stock domain and follows an integrated pipeline in which schema disclosure and ground-truth labeling are co-designed with the dataset construction process.
While effective within a fixed domain, such designs can entangle model performance with the exposed schema and recurring query patterns, making results harder to extrapolate to new schemas, new domains, or alternative GQLs.
Other more decoupled template-driven pipelines often stop at pair generation on limited avaliable graph databases for a specific GQL, thus remain limited in scale, domain diversity, or GQL coverage.
For instance, SyntheT2C
(
50
)
is built on only two databases and provides annotations only in Cypher, which restricts its use for broad cross-domain and multi-GQL evaluation.

Finally, evaluation in prior work is often reported with a single end-to-end metric. Employing diverse evaluation metrics allows us to pinpoint bottlenecks in Text-to-GQL systems more precisely and to better understand their implications for graph database query processing.
For example, CySpider
(
49
)
primarily uses execution accuracy based on result equivalence between generated and gold queries, leaving complementary aspects such as grammatical validity, edit-distance-based similarity, and GLEU-based semantic alignment between generated and gold queries under-characterized. In addition, the impact of question abstraction level w.r.t. ambiguity is not considered for fine-grained evaluation.

Taken together, the lack of multi-GQL, multi-domain datasets and the partiality of evaluation methods underscore the need for a unified Text-to-GQL benchmark with a comprehensive standardized evaluation method.
This leads us to the primary question driving this research
Q

:
How can we design a unified Text-to-GQL benchmark that scales across domains and supports comprehensive evaluation across GQLs?

Contributions.
To bridge the above gaps, we present
Text2GQL-Bench
, a unified Text-to-GQL benchmark that couples a multi-GQL multi-domain dataset with multiple question abstraction level annotations, a scalable construction pipeline, and a standardized evaluation suite.
Text2GQL-Bench is built as an executable testbed that connects graph database construction (schemas and instances) with paired natural language questions and GQL queries, supporting reproducible data generation and evaluation.

These limitations motivate a unified benchmark that scales to diverse domains, supports multiple GQL standards, and enables standardized, multi-metric evaluation.

3.
Preliminary

We start with the notations and the task definition in this paper.

Graph Database Management System (GDBMS).
It refers to a database system that natively stores and manages data as a graph. Unlike relational databases, GDBMSs prioritize relationships, making them ideal for applications like social networks, recommendation engines, and knowledge graphs.

Graph Query Language (GQL).
it refers to query languages used to query and manipulate data stored as a graph. Unlike SQL for tabular data, these languages are designed to handle complex relationships and traversals efficiently. Representative graph query languages include Cypher, ISO-GQL, Gremlin, and SPARQL.

ISO-GQL.
It refers to the new International Standard language for property graph querying. It builds on the foundations of SQL and integrates proven ideas from the existing Cypher, PGQL, GSQL, and G-CORE languages.

Text-to-GQL.
It refers to the process of converting a natural language question
Q
into a GQL query
Y
capable of retrieving relevant data from a graph database
D=(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{S})
, where
\mathcal{G}
is the data of the given graph database and
\mathcal{S}
is the schema of the database. Given a natural language question
Q
and a graph database
D=(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{S})
, Text2GQL aims to generate a GQL query
Y
such that
Y
can be executed on
D
to obtain the correct answer. The mapping can be written as:
Y=f(Q,D)
.

(iii) Query Translation & Generation: enerating executable GQL queries via automated translation from existing Cypher or SQL queries, and LLM-based synthesis; (iv) Hierarchical Question Generation: annotating GQL queries in different question abstraction levels.

4.
Benchmark Construction

In this section, we introduce the benchmark construction details.

4.1.
Framework Overview

To build a scalable Text-to-GQL benchmark, we propose a dataset construction framework (
Figure

3
) that transforms heterogeneous raw sources into a standardized Text-to-GQL dataset in four stages:

(1)

normalizing existing relational and graph-structured database schemas, and synthesizing graph schemas with domain taxonomies;

(2)

converting existing data or synthesizing data on given graph schemas to create executable graph database instances on target GDBMS;

(3)

generating executable GQL queries via automated translation from existing Cypher or SQL queries, and LLM-based synthesis;

(4)

annotating GQL queries in different question abstraction levels.

This design produces a benchmark dataset that enables end-to-end Text-to-GQL evaluation across different GQLs and multiple question abstraction levels.

4.2.
Data Collection

Existing datasets are often constrained to a single domain or limited query templates, which restricts applicability as general-purpose evaluation benchmarks
(
26
;
25
)
.
To ensure both domain diversity and query complexity, we collect data from three complementary sources: (i) existing graph datasets, (ii) existing Text-to-SQL datasets, and (iii) newly synthesized industrial domain subsets.

4.2.1.
Existing Graph Datasets

We incorporate representative graph datasets from both Text-to-Cypher datasets and graph benchmarks.

Text-to-Cypher.
To incorporate native graph query patterns, we utilize the Neo4j Text2Cypher dataset
(
36
)
, selecting 10 databases across 4 domains.
Although these queries are rich in domains, they only have cypher queries, and the database instances can only be created on Neo4j.

Graph Benchmarks.
While existing Text-to-Cypher datasets provide useful foundations, they are insufficient for evaluating complex GQL reasoning.
To introduce deeper structural patterns and long-range dependencies, we incorporate FinBench
(
38
)
, which contains complex graph queries from real financial applications.

4.2.2.
Text-to-SQL Benchmark.

We select BIRD
(
23
)
as a primary source of Text-to-SQL dataset. BIRD contains a large number of multi-table joins, which are particularly valuable for analyzing the correspondence between relational joins and graph traversals.
We collect 9 databases spanning 7 domains and transform their relational schemas into property graphs during synthesis.

4.2.3.
General-Domain Subset

Graph datasets and Text-to-SQL benchmarks do not fully capture the diversity of real-world graph applications.
To extend coverage to long-tail domains, we construct a general-domain subset via controlled LLM-based synthesis.
Using domain taxonomies derived from existing industrial scenarios as semantic guidance, we generate 14 graph databases in 5 domains.

4.3.
Dataset Construction Framework

4.3.1.
Schema Translation & Generation

To support both structural depth and domain breadth, our framework must accommodate heterogeneous sources with fundamentally different schema availability.
As discussed in
Section

4
, input data falls into two categories: (i) structured datasets with predefined schemas and (ii) industrial scenarios requiring schema synthesis.

We unify both cases through a canonical intermediate representation (IR), termed
SchemaGraph
.
This graph-native abstraction captures entities, relations, and property constraints in a model-agnostic manner, decoupling logical schema modeling from source formats (e.g., relational or graph) and target GQLs.
All schema generation and translation are centralized within this IR, enabling consistent processing across heterogeneous inputs.
By treating schema translation and schema generation as two instantiations of constructing a
SchemaGraph
, our framework eliminates fragmentation between synthesis and adaptation.
This unified abstraction enables scalable benchmark construction across domains and GDBMSs.

•

Schema Translation for Existing schemas.
Predifined schemas from relational databases and graph databases are parsed into
SchemaGraph
, where relational artifacts such as associative tables are reconciled with graph semantics by mapping them to edge types with properties. The
SchemaGraph
can be serialized into different GDBMS-compatible schema formats.

•

Schema Generation for general domain subset.
For general domain subsets,
SchemaGraph
is constructed via controlled LLM-based schema generation. Given the domain, subdomain of an industrial scenario, the LLM produces a domain-specific schema specification under explicit structural constraints, which is then deterministically parsed into a validated
SchemaGraph
, ensuring type consistency and structural completeness.

4.3.2.
Data Conversion & Generation

Once graph schemas are finalized, we populate databases with existing or synthesize data.
This stage comprises two disjoint pipelines depending on the data source:
(i) a deterministic data conversion pipeline for existing datasets with pre-populated databases, and
(ii) a generative data construction pipeline for open-domain schemas synthesized from the General-Domain subset.

•

Data Conversion from Existing Datasets.

For existing datasets such as BIRD and graph benchmarks, database are already provided.
Accordingly, data conversion focuses on schema-aligned transformation and lossless migration into graph database formats.
This process is fully rule-based and deterministic, including data file format normalization, datatype projection, and import config file generation for target GDBMS.
As this pipeline does not involve learning or generation, we omit detailed illustrations due to its engineering nature.

•

Data Generation for general-domain subset Schemas.

For general-domain subset schemas without pre-existing databases, we construct graph data via a code-mediated synthesis paradigm, as illustrated in
Figure

4
.
Rather than prompting LLMs to directly generate raw data, we employ them as logic generators that synthesize executable Python programs conditioned on the
SchemaGraph
and data distribution config.
These programs encode both structural constraints and data generation logic, and are subsequently executed to procedurally construct data for graph databases.
This design offers three key advantages.
First, data scale is decoupled from the LLM context window, enabling the synthesis of large graph instances with millions of nodes and edges.
Second, referential integrity is strictly guaranteed through programmatic ID management, ensuring all edges connect valid entities.
Third, realistic data characteristics are explicitly controlled through a distribution specification module, where the LLM instantiates statistical generation logic (e.g., power-law degree distributions) within the synthesized programs.

Figure 4.
Data Generation Pipeline. With the SchemaGraph intermediate representation and data distribution setting, the LLM-based logic generator can provide an executable Python script to generate data with execution-based verification. The data will be stored in .csv or .parquet files.

4.3.3.
Query Translation & Generation

With the databases ready, we generate the executable GQL queries.
To construct a dataset that possesses both the logical depth of mature relational datasets and the structural complexity of native graph scenarios, we design a hybrid query construction pipline, as illustrated in
Figure 5
.
This pipeline operates through two parallel pathways: a multi-stage translator that migrates existing SQL/Cypher logic, and an iterative evolutionary synthesizer that creates new graph queries. We preserve original question annotations during this pipeline.

•

Query Translation for Existing Datasets.
For existing graph datasets like FinBench and Neo4j’s Text2Cypher dataset, most of them use Cypher as their query language. In order to support more graph query languages, we design a
Graph-IR
to represent the basic semantic structure of property graph query languages. We first parse Cypher query’s AST into Graph-IR, and then translate the Graph-IR into other GQLs.
Existing Text-to-SQL benchmarks such as BIRD contain rich domain logic—such as analytical filtering and complex aggregations—that is valuable for Text-to-GQL evaluation if SQL queries can be translated into GQL queries.
To address this, we leverage Cypher as an intermediate query language by utilizing robust open-source tools
(
33
)
to first convert relational SQL queries into Cypher.
This step handles the semantic mapping of relational operations, such as converting
JOIN ON
clauses into graph edge traversals (e.g.,
-[:REL]->
).
Once translated to Cypher, we use Graph-IR to translate Cypher into other GQLs.

Figure 5.
Query Translation and Generation Pipeline. This process includes (i) Query Translation: translating existing SQL queries and Cypher queries into Graph-IR, then translating Graph-IR into target GQL queries; (ii) Query Generation: generating Cypher queries through an LLM-based query generator with an iterative complexity increasing strategy, the generated queries can then be translated into target GQL queries through Query Translation.

•

Query Generation for General-Domain Subset.

While translation faithfully preserves the logic of existing queries, it does not systematically ensure sufficient coverage of complex queries such as 3+ hop traversals, variable-length paths, and multi-step
MATCH
patterns.
Our iterative generation step therefore explicitly targets these structures to ensure the synthesized corpus reaches the desired difficulty profile.
The core of our strategy is a
dynamic evolution mechanism
that utilizes intermediate execution results as grounding context.
The process initiates with the diverse “seed” queries with original question annotations either directly generated by LLM based on the schema or from existing query templates.
Rather than hallucinating complex logic in a vacuum, we execute these seed queries on a GDBMS to retrieve actual results.
To manage context length and ensure diversity, we sample representative instances from these execution results (e.g., specific node attributes and neighbor connections) and feed them back into the LLM along with the original query.
Armed with this concrete data context, the LLM is prompted to "complicate" the query structure: evolving both the GQL logic and its corresponding natural language description simultaneously. This involves adding constraints, extending traversal paths, or nesting logic.
Because the model observes the actual data distribution, the evolved query remains semantically valid and grounded in reality.
For instance, instead of hallucinating arbitrary filters, the model can generalize observed property values into realistic range constraints or category filters.
This cycle repeats, progressively deepening the query structure. By applying strict execution validation at each step to filter out errors and empty results, the process effectively transforms a simple retrieval request into a sophisticated, logic-heavy reasoning task.

4.3.4.
Hierarchical Question Generation.

To complete and align (Question, Query) pairs, and to examine the model’s Text-to-GQL ability under different abstraction levels
(
31
)
of user input questions from diverse user personas, we design a two-stage question generator that first simulates diverse user intents and subsequently materializes the implicit knowledge required to bridge them.

•

User Simulation.
In the first stage, we focus on generating the natural language question.
Conditioned on the graph schema and the GQL query, we prompt the LLM to simulate users with varying degrees of domain expertise.
This process yields questions across three distinct abstraction levels:

(1)

Syntactic Level (Level 1):
The generated questions explicitly mirror GQL keywords, simulating engineers who are familiar with GQL syntax mechanics.

(2)

Logical Level (Level 2):
The questions abstract away specific keywords but retain the precise entity-relationship structure, simulating regular users describing data access logic.

(3)

Business Level (Level 3):
The questions reflect high-level analytical goals, completely hiding the underlying topological implementation. Simulating data analysts focusing on in-depth data analysis.

•

Knowledge Extraction.
Once the (Question, Query) pairs are generated, the crucial missing variable is the external knowledge that helps a specific abstract question map to a specific concrete query.
Instead of hallucinating External Knowledge in a vacuum, we employ a self-annotation strategy.
We feed the generated pairs back into the LLM and task it with “Reverse Rationalization”:

"Given that the user asked
L
and the system executed
q
, what implicit domain knowledge or schema assumption
E
bridges the gap between the two?"

This process materializes External Knowledge into concrete text.
For example, for a Business Level question like
“Who are the key influencers?”
, this phase might generate an
E
such as:
“In this schema, ’influencers’ are defined as Person nodes with a high degree of ’FOLLOWS’ incoming edges.”

By deriving External Knowledge from the alignment of Question and Query, we ensure that the conditioning variable is semantically grounded, completing the high-quality (Question, Query, External Knowledge) triplets required for future evaluation.

4.4.
Quality Assurance

While the synthesis pipeline described in
Section

4.3
enables the automated generation of massive-scale datasets, relying solely on model-generated content introduces potential risks of hallucinations and semantic misalignment.
For a benchmark to serve as a trustworthy evaluation standard, statistical quantities must be complemented by rigorous verification.
Therefore, we implement a hybrid quality assurance framework that cascades from automated consistency checks to expert human review, ensuring the generated instances achieve the “Gold Standard” quality required for reliable evaluation. Before human intervention, all generated data undergoes a strict automated filtration process to eliminate syntax errors and execution failures.
This pipeline operates through a two-stage filtration mechanism.
Firstly, we adopt an execution-centric validation to ensure both schema compliance and result reliability: each query is executed against the target graph database, and any query that fails to run is discarded.
Secondly, to maintain benchmark diversity, we apply semantic overlap filtering. By computing the emebdding similarity between (Question, Query) pairs, we actively prune pairs with excessively high semantic similarity to prevent corpus redundancy. For any given two (Question, Query) pairs, we remove one of them from our dataset if the embedding similarity between queries or questions is larger than 0.98.

While automated checks ensure executability, they cannot verify whether the natural language question perfectly aligns with the query intent.
To guarantee the validity of our evaluation, we adopt a
Human-in-the-Loop verification strategy
for the test set.
Specifically, we randomly sample test instances and invite three experts to manually inspect the sample (Question, Query) pairs for semantic alignment.
Their role is purely verification: they flag mismatches, ambiguities, or ill-specified questions/queries, rather than rewriting or editing the data.
For the training set, we primarily rely on automated, execution-centric validation to preserve scalability. Together, this hybrid quality assurance strategy balances corpus scale with the precision required for reliable benchmark evaluation.

4.5.
Difficulty Definition

With the corpus constructed via our dataset construction pipeline and rigorously verified through our quality assurance framework, the final step is to organize these instances into a standardized evaluation suite.
A raw collection of queries is insufficient for diagnosing specific model weaknesses; simply aggregating queries of varying structures is insufficient, as it fails to reveal how models behave under different levels of graph query complexity.
To achieve this, we define a
four-tier difficulty taxonomy
.

Instead of relying on superficial metrics like sentence length, we define difficulty based on the topological complexity of the GQL query and the reasoning depth required to solve it.
This taxonomy is calibrated against established Text-to-SQL benchmarks
(
23
;
47
;
19
)
to highlight the unique challenges of graph databases.
Traditional SQL benchmarks categorize difficulty based on the number of
JOIN
operations, where their highest difficulty tiers typically involve limited relational hops. The ubiquitous complex multi-hop path pattern and variable-length path pattern in industrial graph scenarios are left unconsidered in the SQL difficulty taxonomy, thus requiring a graph-native difficulty taxonomy.

As demonstrated in
Table

2
, our corpus is categorized into the following four difficulty levels:

•

Easy:

These queries correspond to the simplest retrieval patterns, involving single node or single edge matching without aggregation or complex conditions.
They primarily test basic pattern grounding and label/property recognition.

Characteristics
:
single node or edge pattern, no aggregation, no complex filtering.

•

Medium:

Queries at this level introduce simple relational structure through one-hop traversals and may include basic aggregation or filtering.
They reflect common analytical queries that require counting or summarizing immediate neighbors.

Characteristics
:
one-hop path, simple aggregation (e.g.,
COUNT
,
SUM
) or basic filters, no nesting.

•

Hard:

This level captures moderately complex graph reasoning with short multi-hop or variable-length traversals and multiple constraints.
Queries involve up to two hops or variable-length hops and combine several conditions or non-nested aggregations, requiring LLM to maintain intermediate structural context.

Characteristics
:
multi-hop paths (
\leq
2 hops) or variable-length paths, multiple conditions, non-nested aggregation.

•

Extra Hard:

Representing the most challenging category, these queries require deep graph reasoning across complex paths and multiple query stages.
They typically involve long traversals, multi-step
MATCH
clauses, and nested aggregation, demanding global reasoning over intermediate results.

Characteristics
:
complex paths (
\geq
3 hops), multi-step
MATCH
, nested aggregation, high structural and logical depth.

6.
Metrics

Given that practical data analytics prioritize result fidelity, text-to-GQL models must be assessed on their capacity to execute queries accurately. We therefore propose multiple metrics including Execution Accuracy (EX), Grammar, Google-BLEU, and Similarity for effective comprehensive evaluation.

Execution Accuracy (EX)
.
EX measures whether the predicted query produces the same execution result as the gold query:

(1)

\mathrm{EX}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\big[R(\hat{q}_{i})=R(q_{i}^{*})\big],

where
\mathbb{I}[\cdot]
is the indicator function. EX is invariant to syntactic variations as long as the semantics of the query are preserved.

Grammar.

Grammar measures whether the predicted query is syntactically parsable under the target GQL grammar:

(2)

\mathrm{Grammar}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\big[\mathrm{Parse}(\hat{q}_{i})\neq\varnothing\big],

where
\mathrm{Parse}(\cdot)
returns a non-empty abstract syntax tree (AST) if parsing succeeds, and
\varnothing
otherwise. Grammar reflects a model’s ability to generate well-formed queries, whereas it is independent of semantic correctness.

Google-BLEU
. We report Google BLEU (GLEU)
(
45
)
to measure token-level similarity between the predicted query
\hat{q}_{i}
and the gold query
q_{i}^{*}
. GLEU is a variant of BLEU proposed by Google that improves sentence-level stability by smoothing n-gram precision, and has been widely adopted in semantic parsing and code generation tasks. We report the corpus-level GLEU score over the test set.

Similarity
. To quantify how close a predicted query is to the reference in surface form, we report Jaro-Winkler similarity
(
44
)
, which is robust to minor typos and small character-level deviations.
Given the predicted query
\hat{q}
and gold query
q
, we first apply a lightweight normalization and compute
\mathrm{JW}(\hat{q},q)\in[0,1]
, where higher values indicate greater similarity.
We report the average
\mathrm{JW}
score over the evaluation set; when a distance form is needed, we use
\mathrm{Dist}_{JW}=1-\mathrm{JW}
.
Unlike execution accuracy, this metric does not imply semantic equivalence, but is useful for diagnosing near-miss formatting or spelling errors under strict GQL syntax.

Strategies.
We investigate different tuning strategies and In-Context Learning methods:

(1)

Zero-shot Prompting:
The model is provided with the target schema (DDL/Graph Schema) and the natural language question, directly generating the GQL query without examples. This tests intrinsic generalization.

We use a fixed

Specifically, we prepend the same three demonstrations (Question, Query) pairs to every test instance, selected from the training set and held constant across all evaluations to ensure reproducibility and avoid test-dependent retrieval.

(3)

We apply LoRA
(

We set the LoRA rank

\alpha=32

This strategy aims to verify if small models can surpass large models through domain-specific alignment.

Implementation Details.
All queries are executed and verified on their corresponding database engines: Cypher queries on TuGraph-DB, ISO-GQL queries on Spanner Graph, and SQL queries on SQLite.
Experiments for open-weights models were conducted on a server equipped with 1 NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU (32GB memory).

Dataset Selection.
We select all 2783 examples that can be executed both on TuGraph-DB and Spanner Graph out of the test set to evaluate performance on ISO-GQL and Cypher. Other 1044 examples can not be executed on Spanner Graph because it doesn’t support the return of graph elements. For comparison between GQL and SQL, we choose 357 examples from BIRD in the dev set.

8.
Conclusion

In this work, we address the fundamental obstacles that have limited progress in Text-to-GQL research: the lack of high-quality benchmark datasets and evaluation methods to systematically compare model capabilities across different graph query languages and domains.
To this end, we introduce Text2GQL-Bench, a multi-GQL dataset with 78,184 (Question, Query) pairs spanning 13 domains, a scalable dataset construction framework, and a comprehensive evaluation method that systematically supports the evaluation of Text-to-GQL systems.

The dataset construction framework enables the scalable construction of Text-to-GQL datasets with domain, GQL, and question abrstraction level diversity by leveraging mature heterogeneous resources as a source of analytical logic, translating existing datasets, and synthesizing executable graph databases with corresponding complex (Question, Query) pairs.

The evaluation method is designed to expose different bottlenecks of Text-to-GQL systems.
Through cross-schema and cross-domain splits, persona-based question abstraction, and a fine-grained difficulty taxonomy, Text2GQL-Bench enables systematic analysis of how models behave under varying schema grounding requirements, question abstraction levels, and query complexities with evaluation metrics including grammatical validity, similarity, semantic alignment, and execution accuracy.
Our empirical results demonstrate that optimization strategy and schema-aligned supervision play a more decisive role than model scale alone, and that current models still face substantial challenges when generalizing across query langugages and schema configurations. Our evaluation shows that ISO-GQL generation remains brittle without dialect exposure: zero-shot execution accuracy stays below 3%.
Even a fixed 3-shot prompt can rise EX to
\sim
50%, the Grammar remains
\leq
70%, and fine-tuning an 8B model reaches 45.1% EX and 90.8% Grammar, narrowing most of the gap to much larger LLMs.

Looking ahead, the modular design of our framework opens several promising directions.
In particular, the use of Graph IR provides a natural foundation for extending Text2GQL-Bench to additional GQLs, such as SQL/PGQ, without redesigning the entire pipeline.
Beyond query generation, the framework can be extended to support richer graph workloads, including temporal queries, dynamic updates, and analytical graph operators.
We hope that Text2GQL-Bench serves not only as a benchmark, but also as an extensible infrastructure to advance robust, standardized, and practically relevant Text-to-GQL research.
