Integrative neuroscience in traumatic brain injury: Multimodal imaging, network models, and precision medicine☆
Authors: Karen Caeyenberghs, Phoebe Imms, Andrei Irimia, Amy Kuceyeski, Alexander Olsen
Published: 2026

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major global health challenge, with growing recognition of its complexity across diverse contexts. The heterogeneity of TBI, in its presentation, mechanisms, and recovery trajectories, calls for sophisticated neuroimaging approaches to adequately capture its multifaceted nature (Covington and Duff, 2021). Ample functional and structural neuroimaging studies of TBI (e.g., Imms et al., 2024, 2023; Clemente et al., 2023; Mahoney et al., 2022; Shida et al., 2023) reveal diffuse axonal injuries, white matter disruption, widespread morphological alterations and altered functional connectivity. This Special Issue presents a collection of research using innovative methods to capture the manifold range of neurological alterations post-TBI. Emerging methodological approaches that integrate information across neuroimaging modalities and harness multidisciplinary metrics are highlighted.

Indices of neurological disruption can persist long after the initial insult and are associated with long-term cognitive, behavioral, and emotional outcomes. In this special issue, McGeown et al. (2026) review acute MRI markers that reflect long-term symptom burden and functional recovery in individuals with mild TBI (mTBI). Mixed findings across a range of structural and functional MRI modalities emerged, with symptom burden and recovery being more strongly correlated with complex, network-level measures of structural and functional brain connectivity rather than traditional morphometrics. Another systematic review by Ellery et al. (2026) synthesizes functional neuroimaging evidence [employing resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) or resting state magnetoencephalography (rs-MEG)] and linking post-concussive symptoms with alterations in functional brain networks. The authors highlighted the central yet heterogeneous role of functional connectivity changes, particularly within the default mode network, in symptom expression after mTBI. Chen et al. (2026) provide a quantitative synthesis, focussing specifically on chronic non-sports and non-combat TBI, revealing robust evidence of diffuse and lasting white matter abnormalities across the brain. Their meta-analysis identifies the corpus callosum as particularly vulnerable to chronic injury and demonstrates that diffusion MRI measures capture injury severity–dependent alterations, reinforcing their potential utility as biomarkers of long-term TBI pathology. These review papers also highlight that reliance on unimodal analyses examining single brain regions/tracts or metrics in isolation appears to incompletely capture the distributed, networked nature of TBI pathology. They call for multimodal imaging approaches to advance the identification of robust neurobiological markers of symptom burden.

TBI initiates a cascade of complex, interconnected effects on the brain, including immediate mechanical injury and functional disruption, secondary biochemical cascades, alterations in neurotransmitter systems, chronic neuroinflammation, disrupted neural connectivity, and increased risk of long-term neurodegeneration, processes that cannot be completely understood through single-modality or single-metric analyses. For example, Posis et al. (2025) integrate four structural MRI modalities (T1-, T2-, proton-density (PD)-, and diffusion-weighted images) to capture brain aging in men with a history of TBI (from the Vietnam Era Twin Study of Aging), revealing subtle long-term effects of TBI on restricted diffusion in the bilateral inferior longitudinal fasciculi and the left inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus. An integrative neuroscience approach that combines complementary neuroimaging modalities (e.g., structural MRI, functional MRI ‘fMRI’, diffusion weighted/tensor imaging ‘DWI’ or ‘DTI’, electroencephalography ‘EEG’, magnetoencephalography ‘MEG’, and Positron Emission Tomography ‘PET’) with advanced network-based analytical methods (e.g., graph theory, network diffusion models, multiplex network analyses, network control theory) has great potential to characterize the multidimensional nature of TBI pathology. Illustrative work by Zhang et al. (2026), integrates resting-state MEG, fMRI, and DTI connectome data to achieve high classification accuracy between healthy controls and individuals with sub-acute mild TBI. In another example, Mueller (2025) combines MRI, fMRI and PET in a cohort of Vietnam veterans (from the from the DoD-ADNI data repository) to identify a hyperconnected brain state associated with elevated amyloid-β load. This brain state was significantly more prevalent in individuals with a history of TBI and PTSD, suggesting that altered network synchrony may represent a potential marker of Alzheimer's disease-related pathology in aging populations with prior trauma.

Incorporating complementary data streams from genetics (e.g., Posis et al., 2025), clinical sources (e.g., Zhang et al., 2025), cognitive and mental health assessments (e.g., Dominguez et al., 2026; Roy et al., 2025), and blood-based biomarkers (e.g., Mononen et al., 2026; Waters et al., 2025) enables comprehensive understanding of how brain-level alterations relate to functional impairment, and how individual differences may shape recovery trajectories. For example, results from a multilayer network analysis by Dominguez et al. (2026) of cognitive, affective and grey matter layer data show the existence of four bridging nodes in mTBI patients, including insomnia severity, immediate verbal memory, somatisation, and processing speed. These bridging nodes can be used in future studies as strategic intervention targets for developing personalized treatment programs addressing core symptom drivers in mTBI. A study by Mononen et al. (2026) demonstrates that acute blood biomarkers (GFAP, total tau, and IL-10) are associated with white matter disruption on diffusion tensor imaging in mTBI patients, with GFAP showing sensitivity to microstructural injury markers. These findings suggest that blood-based biomarkers may have utility as a more accessible indicator of white matter pathology. Waters et al. (2025) use Linked Independent Component Analysis to integrate structural (T1-weighted) and diffusion (FA, MD) MRI metrics alongside blood biomarkers (GFAP, NFL, total tau, UCH-L1) in TBI patients. They identify an association between UCH-L1 levels, covarying brain structural patterns, processing speed deficits, and cumulative TBI burden, demonstrating the utility of multimodal data fusion in linking blood biomarkers to brain structure-cognition relationships in TBI. Using the principles of network control theory, Roy et al. (2025) integrate structural connectomes and functional time-series to quantify longitudinal trajectories of brain dynamics from acute to chronic injury phases in TBI. The study shows that after TBI, brain dynamics shift over time toward greater energy requirements for transitioning between states, reduced entropy, and altered transition asymmetries, with these changes linked to poorer attention performance. Moreover, the paper finds that individuals with TBI increasingly prefer lower-order (e.g., sensory/visual) brain states over higher-order cognitive states from subacute to chronic stages, indicating a reorganization of brain dynamics that is broadly replicated across injury severities.

This Special Issue highlights approaches that move beyond univariate neuroimaging analyses toward holistic, systems-level understanding of TBI. Highly novel approaches included in this issue include the integration of sleep spindles (polysomnography) with grey matter loss secondary to TBI (Kalantari et al., 2025); an fMRI stimulus-induced vergence oculomotor task to examine concussion-related convergence insufficiency (Sangoi et al., 2026); biomechanical modelling of magnetic resonance elastography to showcase the anatomical distribution of shear strain in mTBI (Hirad et al., 2026); individual-level analyses of T2 relaxometry as a measure of neuroinflammation (Bedggood et al., 2024); and machine learning to identify functional connectivity alterations in brainstem nuclei of patients with severe TBI and disorders of consciousness (Zhang et al., 2025). The featured articles challenge the field to embrace complex methodologies that may better reflect the nature of TBI pathology. By harnessing multimodal neuroimaging (e.g., Mueller, 2025; Posis et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026), network modelling (e.g., Dominguez et al., 2026; Roy et al., 2025), longitudinal designs (i.e., Bedggood et al., 2024; McQuaid et al., 2025; Roy et al., 2025; Stenberg et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025), blood biomarkers of neural injury (i.e., Waters et al., 2025; Mononen et al., 2026), polysomnography (Kalantari et al., 2025), and data fusion (e.g., Zhang et al., 2026; Waters et al., 2025), we gain fresh insights into TBI diagnosis, disease mechanisms, prognostic biomarkers, and potential targeted therapies. This integrated framework may facilitate the development of precision medicine in TBI, by enabling individualized risk stratification, personalized prognosis, and tailored intervention strategies that account for the profound heterogeneity inherent in TBI populations. Ultimately, an integrative neuroscience approach has the potential to deepen our mechanistic understanding of TBI and inform future advances in diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment.
