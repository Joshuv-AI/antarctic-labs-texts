Sea Urchin‐Inspired Immuno‐Instructive Ionic Flow Drives MSCs‐Macrophage Communication to Promote Bone Regeneration
Authors: Yang Lu, Kun Su, Daqian Liu, Chunyu Liu, Muyan Qin, Shuaijie Li, Pengfei Tian, Yansong Wang, Yingbo Wang, Lei Chu, Xu Cui, Haobo Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBone tissue regeneration is a complex physiological process dependent on the spatiotemporal coordination of immune cells and stem cells. Conventional research primarily elucidates the mechanism through which materials facilitate bone formation by initially modulating macrophages and subsequently encouraging the osteogenic differentiation of stem cells, while largely overlooking the proactive regulatory influence of stem cells on immune cells. Consequently, investigating the capacity of materials to concurrently attract stem cells and modulate innate immune infiltration, while establishing a tissue microenvironment response mechanism, holds substantial importance for the development of next‐generation tissue regeneration materials. This study introduces a sea urchin‐inspired immune instructional ionic flux (SUIF) platform, characterized by a radial mesoporous structure that employs B─O bond dissociation/dissolution rates and Sr2 + slow‐release mechanisms to create a “fast‐slow biphasic” ionic flow release, thereby establishing a dynamic alkaline ionic flow microenvironment. Within this microenvironment, 5B5Sr‐SUIF recruits MSCs and stimulates MSCs to enhance the expression of miR‐466m‐5p, thereby obstructing the nuclear translocation of NF‐κB in macrophages. This facilitates immunological regulatory communication between mesenchymal stem cells and macrophages, offering novel material design principles and molecular mechanism support for the exact regeneration of complicated bone deformities.

Introduction

Bone tissue regeneration is a multicellular coordinated process reliant on spatiotemporal signals, wherein the dynamic interaction between the immune system and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) is pivotal in influencing the outcome of tissue repair [1, 2, 3, 4]. The conventional perspective assumpts that macrophages facilitate inflammatory resolution and initiate osteogenesis via the promotion of M1 to M2 polarization [5, 6, 7]. However, recent research indicates that MSCs are not merely passive responders to immune signals; rather, they function as active regulators of immune outcomes, serving as “command sources” [7, 8]. The term “immuno‐instructive” microenvironment denotes the extracellular signals (such as ions, stress, and metabolic factors) that confer immune regulatory capabilities to stem cells, thereby converting them from “recipients” to “commanders.” [9, 10, 11, 12] This transformation influences macrophage polarization through the secretion of exosomes, small RNAs, and cytokines, thereby orchestrating infection management, inflammation resolution, and tissue regeneration [11, 13, 14, 15]. Consequently, bone repair is now perceived not as a singular occurrence of inflammation resolution or osteogenic induction, but as a spatiotemporal dynamic process facilitated by the three‐dimensional communication network among immune cells, stem cells, and osteogenesis [16]. The challenge resides in attaining accurate correspondence of MSCs‐immune cell interactions within both temporal and spatial frameworks.

In the initial phase of bone defect, the local environment typically experiences pathological alterations, including acidification, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and ionic imbalance, which impair the immune signaling of MSCs, resulting in a dissonance between the immune response and the onset of osteogenesis [17, 18, 19, 20]. This leads to protracted inflammation resolution, obstructed osteogenic initiation, and impaired tissue regeneration. Osteoblasts secrete alkaline substances during new bone formation, whereas osteoclasts release hydrogen ions during bone resorption, thereby establishing a localized acidic microenvironment conducive to the dissolution of bone minerals [21, 22, 23]. The acidic microenvironment facilitates the amplification of pro‐inflammatory signals through the activation of pathways like NF‐κB and HIF‐1α, resulting in sustained inflammation and increased bone resorption [24, 25, 26]. In contrast, a mildly alkaline microenvironment can upregulate osteogenic‐related genes such as ALP and RUNX2 in MSCs, inhibit osteoclastic activity, and enhance osteogenic signals [27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Consequently, regenerating the weakly alkaline stable window to facilitate the coordinated and coherent synergy of immune directives and osteogenic signals in both spatial and temporal dimensions is a crucial consideration in the design of bone regeneration materials.

Wu et al. recently emphasized that inflammation‐related acidification is not simply a passive consequence of tissue damage, but an active immune signal. Acidification associated with inflammation can impair the BRD4/MED1 transcription condensates, consequently disrupting the gene regulatory network of macrophages [32]. This discovery indicates that inflammatory acidification can directly “reprogram” the transcriptional network of immune cells, positioning local pH as a crucial factor in regulating the immune‐bone rhythm. Simultaneously, numerous studies have demonstrated that bioactive ions, including Mg2+, Ca2 +, and Zn2+, can facilitate bidirectional regulation of immune polarization and osteogenic differentiation via signaling pathways such as Wnt/β‐catenin, BMP/Smads, and Runx2/Osterix‐ZIP1, thereby offering a novel theoretical foundation for rebalancing the immune environment through modulation of the ionic microenvironment [33, 34, 35, 36]. Cell recruitment is a fundamental and essential phase in the tissue repair process [37]. Materials facilitate the mobilization and recruitment of MSCs to the site of tissue damage, whereupon MSCs can undergo directional differentiation into functional cells, including osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and fibroblasts, to repair the injured tissue [38, 39, 40]; concurrently, MSCs can meticulously regulate the proliferation and differentiation of neighboring cells through the secretion of diverse paracrine factors [41, 42]. Currently, there is a deficiency of systematic research at the mechanistic level regarding the recruitment and functional regulation of MSCs to achieve integrated regulation of “pH homeostasis window and ionic microenvironment” in vivo, as well as the dynamic coupling of immune and osteogenic signals through their synergistic effects. Consequently, the development of functional biomaterials capable of concurrently recruiting MSCs and modulating the infiltration of innate immune cells in vivo, while also restoring pH homeostasis and ionic microenvironment in the injured tissue, is not only fundamentally significant for the rational design of tissue regeneration materials but also establishes a crucial conceptual framework and offers essential engineering support for the spatiotemporal coupling regulation of immune and osteogenic signals.

The natural mineralization architecture of sea urchins provides a distinctive source of inspiration in this context [43]. Their micro‐porous structure creates an open ionic flux network that sustains a stable ionic gradient and pH range in the highly saline and stressed marine environment, thus enabling the simultaneous regulation of biomineralization and environmental homeostasis [43, 44]. Motivated by this, we propose a sea‐urchin‐inspired immuno‐instructive ionic flux platform (SUIF), utilizing a radially mesoporous channel topology as its geometric basis. This topology offers pathways for ion exchange and migration characterized by “open ionic flux”, similar to the mineralized structure of sea urchins. We establish a “fast–slow biphasic” ionic flux model by integrating B─O bond networks to control the depolymerization/dissolution rate and incorporating sustained release of Sr2 + [45, 46]. The rapid‐release phase mitigates inflammatory acidification, restoring a weak alkaline equilibrium, and stimulates MSC immune instructive output; the gradual‐release phase consistently delivers Ca2 +/Sr2 + steady‐state signals to facilitate prolonged osteogenesis.

This study systematically elucidates the function of 5B5Sr‐SUIF in orchestrating bone immunoregulatory pathways and demonstrates that it facilitates MSCs‐macrophage communication primarily by regulating local ionic flux and pH homeostasis. The continuous release of Sr2 + and other bioactive ions from the material improves the directional migration ability of MSCs, thus expediting their localization to defect sites [47, 48]. Simultaneously, the initial rapid‐release phase alleviates inflammation‐induced acidification and restores a mildly alkaline pH within the defect site, counteracting the functional suppression of MSCs caused by the pathological microenvironment and establishing a conducive niche for their osteogenic differentiation and matrix mineralization. Employing a material‐MSCs‐macrophage transwell co‐culture system, we further illustrate that 5B5Sr‐SUIF can activate MSCs to function as “immune command outputers”, which enhances the expression of miR‐466m‐5p and inhibits the nuclear translocation of NF‐κB. This consequently alters MSC‐macrophage signaling interactions, facilitating macrophage polarization from a pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti‐inflammatory/pro‐regenerative M2 phenotype, thereby creating a low‐ROS, low‐proinflammatory immune environment that promotes prolonged MSC homing and retention. Simultaneously, 5B5Sr‐SUIF precisely regulates the extent of innate immune cell infiltration and endogenous MSC recruitment, thereby facilitating temporally synchronized inflammation resolution, reducing of osteoclastogenesis, and enhancing osteogenesis (Scheme 1). This SUIF strategy collectively establishes a temporally reconstructed alkaline range and ion gradient, facilitating the dynamic integration of the three phases of “immune instruction‐immune response‐osteogenic execution,” and offers a biologically sound yet engineerable design framework for the regenerative treatment of complex bone defects.

Results

The Construction of Sea Urchin‐Inspired Mesoporous Networks

We successfully synthesized borosilicate‐based network bioactive materials with varying compositions (SUIF, 5B‐SUIF, 5Sr‐SUIF, 5B5Sr‐SUIF) to create an ionic flow platform resembling the structural characteristics of a sea urchin. A thorough examination of their structural characteristics and physicochemical properties was conducted utilizing diverse microscopic characterization methods. Figure 1a illustrates that the scanning electron microscope (SEM) distinctly revealed the overall morphology of the prepared samples. Each material exhibited a consistent spherical morphology with a highly uniform particle size distribution, averaging approximately 150–200 nm. The surface exhibited a smooth texture, with no apparent aggregation among the particles. The transmission electron microscope (TEM) elucidated a distinct mesoporous channel architecture, characterized by uniformly distributed channels traversing the interior of the spheres, demonstrating excellent dispersion and porosity, thereby facilitating permeable microscopic channels for subsequent ion exchange and flow (Figure 1b; Figure S1).

The TEM element mapping revealed that in the 5B5Sr‐SUIF sample, the elements Si, B, Ca, and Sr are uniformly distributed throughout the particles. The EDS energy spectrum distinctly revealed the characteristic peaks of each element, demonstrating that the Sr ions have been effectively integrated into the borosilicate network and are distributed stably and uniformly (Figure 1b). The SEM element mapping diagram similarly illustrated the same outcome (Figure S2). The X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed that the diffraction patterns of all sample groups exhibit broad and subtle amorphous “bread crust peaks” (Figure 1c), a characteristic indicative of an amorphous structure, signifying the absence of long‐range ordered lattices in the prepared materials. The amorphous structure facilitated enhanced chemical activity and ion migration rates, promoting the development of dynamic and adaptable ion release pathways within the physiological environment [49].

The Fourier‐transform infrared (FT‐IR) results further confirmed the material's network structure (Figure 1d). The distinctive peaks at 464, 802, and 1082 cm− 1 were attributed to the bending vibration, symmetric stretching vibration, and antisymmetric stretching vibration of the Si─O─Si bond, respectively, signifying the existence of a conventional silicon‐oxygen tetrahedral structure in the material [50, 51]. Conversely, the peak at 1402 cm− 1 corresponded to the vibration of the B─O─B bond, affirming the successful incorporation of boron into the network, thereby establishing a partially substituted boron‐oxygen framework structure [50, 52]. The establishment of this structure altered the network connectivity of the Si─O bond and conferred enhanced ionic permeability and surface activity to the material. The XPS spectral analysis of 5B5Sr‐SUIF indicated the presence of various chemical elements on its surface, including Ca, Si, B, and Sr (Figure 1e), while the XPS results for SUIF, 5B‐SUIF, and 5Sr‐SUIF confirmed the successful doping of the respective elements (Figure S3). The B 1S spectra of 5B‐SUIF and 5B5Sr‐SUIF were analyzed (Figure 1f; Figure S4), revealed two principal peaks for each, signifying the existence of [BO3] and [BO4] within the compositional network. The O 1s spectra results of SUIF and 5B5Sr‐SUIF (Figure 1g,h) indicated a shift in the binding energies of oxygen, attributed to the incorporation of B and Sr into the silicon‐oxygen network structure.

The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was conducted to assess the surface porosity of SUIF and 5B5Sr‐SUIF (Figure 1i,j). The findings indicated that both materials display reversible IV isotherms for adsorption and desorption, a fundamental trait of mesoporous materials, signifying stable pore structures that can repeatedly adsorb and desorb gas molecules [53]. The BET model was determined the specific surface areas of SUIF and 5B5Sr‐SUIF to be 235.2994 and 211.4130 m2/g, respectively. The BJH model was utilized to ascertain the average mesopore diameters of SUIF and 5B5Sr‐SUIF, measuring 7.4270 and 7.5672 nm, respectively. The findings indicated that the addition of B and Sr does not compromise the mesoporous architecture. The Zeta potential measurements of SUIF, 5B‐SUIF, 5Sr‐SUIF, and 5B5Sr‐SUIF indicated that all samples exhibit negatively charged surfaces (Figure 1k).

These detailed characterization results indicated that the synthesized nanomaterials possess a spherical mesoporous morphology, consistent elemental distribution, and a characteristic amorphous structure. This composite, characterized as “porous‐amorphous‐heterogeneously doped,” emulates the open ionic flux network of sea urchin spines, established a strong physicochemical structural basis for the eventual implementation of ion release, immune modulation, and bone regeneration.

The Establishment of a Dynamic Alkaline Ionic Flux Microenvironment

To examine the degradation behavior of the SUIF platform under physiological conditions and its regulatory impact on the local microenvironment (Figure 2a), each sample group was immersed in PBS solution, while monitoring surface morphology, pH variations, and ion release characteristics. SEM observations indicated that with prolonged immersion time, granular or layered deposits progressively emerged on the material surface, signifying that the material experienced ion exchange and dissolution reactions in the solution, along with the formation of new phases (Figure 2b).

(b) SEM images of SUIF, 5B‐SUIF, 5Sr‐SUIF, and 5B5Sr‐SUIF following immersion in PBS. (c) Time‐ dependent pH evolution of SUIF, 5B‐SUIF, 5Sr‐SUIF, and 5B5Sr‐SUIF immersed in PBS (n = 3). (d) XRD patterns of SUIF, 5B‐SUIF, 5Sr‐SUIF, and 5B5Sr‐SUIF after immersion in PBS. (e) Ion‐release profiles of SUIF, 5B‐SUIF, 5Sr‐SUIF, and 5B5Sr‐SUIF in PBS, quantified by ICP‐OES. (f) Live/dead staining of MSCs after exposure to SUIF, 5B‐SUIF, 5Sr‐SUIF, and 5B5Sr‐SUIF. (g) Fluorescent staining of F‐actin in MSCs cultured with SUIF, 5B‐SUIF, 5Sr‐SUIF, and 5B5Sr‐SUIF.

The pH value of the solution exhibited temporal fluctuations (Figure 2c). During the initial phase of immersion, the pH of the system escalated swiftly, attributed to the rapid releasing of basic cations (such as Ca2 + and Sr2 +) from the material. The 5B‐SUIF group exhibited the most significant pH elevation in the initial phase, which was directly associated with the incorporation of B3 +. B3 + substituted for Si4 + in the glass matrix, compromising the stability of the Si─O─Si bonds and diminishing network density, thereby facilitating the release of ions and swiftly establishing an alkaline ionic flow environment. Conversely, the pH alteration in the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group was comparatively mild, exhibiting no significant difference when compared with the SUIF group. It is hypothesized that this occurs because Sr2 +, as a weakly basic cation, can form partial complexes with the anions in the solution, thus buffering the system's alkalinity. The minor pH decrease was considered advantageous for preserving the homeostasis of the extracellular microenvironment and positively influenced the regulation of cell adhesion, differentiation, and immune responses [31, 46].

XRD was conducted on the post‐immersion samples to elucidate the structural alterations of the materials during degradation (Figure 2d). The results revealed distinct diffraction peaks of hydroxyapatite (HA) on the material surfaces, meaning that the material prompted a biomineralization reaction in the solution, resulting in the formation of a bioactive apatite layer. The development of this HA layer not only improved the bioaffinity of the material's surface but also supplied an ion source and structural template for subsequent cell adhesion and osteogenic differentiation.

Furthermore, Inductively Coupled Plasma‐Optical Emission Spectrometer (ICP‐OES) analysis (Figure 2e) demonstrated that the ion release rate from the B‐doped groups was markedly superior to that of the undoped groups, particularly within the initial 24 h, during which the concentrations of Ca, Si, and B ions increased rapidly. This further substantiated the regulatory influence of B on the glass network structure, wherein partial cleavage of Si–O bonds also facilitated ion migration and dissolution. In contrast, the release curve for Sr ions exhibited relative stability, and its sustained release facilitated the establishment of a durable buffered ionic environment in the solution.

To evaluate the influence of the ion microenvironment generated during material degradation on the fate of MSCs, co‐culture experiments were performed to systematically assess cell compatibility and osteogenic differentiation potential. The CCK‐8 assay results demonstrated that, at concentrations under 200 ppm, the material exerted no significant effect on cell viability (Figures S5–S7), indicating exceptional biocompatibility among all material groups. The outcomes of the Live/dead staining experiment (Figure 2f) confirmed that, when co‐cultured with materials at a concentration of 200 ppm, no significant cell mortality was observed, and the cell morphology exhibited no discernible abnormalities. The results indicated that the degradation process of the material does not cause cytotoxicity or elicit toxic responses in cells. Moreover, cytoskeletal protein staining indicated that the condition of cytoskeletal proteins was unchanged at 200 ppm, whereas the intensified F‐actin staining in the material groups implied that the material might enhance the synthesis or aggregation of F‐actin (Figure 2g).

In summary, the B/Sr co‐doping strategy alters the silica‐oxygen network structure, facilitating regulated ion release and dynamic pH buffering effects. The spatiotemporally programmable ionic flux field generated by this synergistic ion release not only alters the material's surface mineralization reaction and local chemical environment but also ensures superior biocompatibility at the cellular level, thereby establishing a solid material and mechanistic basis for the creation of a “immuno‐instructive ionic microenvironment.”

Dynamic Alkaline Ionic Flux Regulation of MSCs' Osteogenic Fate

When introducing the material into the bone defect region, it must exhibit superior biocompatibility at the cell‐material interface and possess the capability to enhance the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs, thereby promoting bone tissue regeneration [54, 55]. The aforementioned results confirm that this material exhibits excellent biocompatibility at the cell‐material interface, establishing a robust foundation for subsequent cell differentiation experiments. This study further examined the material's regulatory influence on the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs, as illustrated in Figure 3a, which presents the cell culture schematic.

The ALP staining results (Figure 3b) indicated that materials doped with B or Sr can markedly augment the early ALP activity of MSCs. The staining was most prominent in the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group, indicating a more significant enhancement of early osteogenic induction in this material group. The quantitative analysis indicated that on the third day of culture (Figure 3c), the ALP activity of the SUIF group (0.49) was significantly greater than that of the negative control group (NC group, 0.32), while the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group (0.95) exhibited significantly higher activity than the SUIF group. On the seventh day of culture (Figure 3d), this trend persisted, with the ALP activity of the SUIF group (0.77) surpassing that of the NC group (0.55), and the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group (1.70) continuing to significantly exceed the SUIF group. On the 14th day of culture, the alizarin red staining results (Figure 3b) demonstrated that the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group produced a greater quantity and density of calcium nodules, exhibiting a significantly elevated degree of mineralization compared to the other experimental groups. The quantitative colorimetric analysis of calcium deposition (Figure 3e) indicated that the calcium deposition in the SUIF group (0.91) exceeded that of the NC group (0.60), whereas the calcium deposition in the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group (2.44) was markedly superior to that of the SUIF group. The 5B5Sr‐SUIF group exhibited the most pronounced osteogenic enhancement during both the initial phase of ALP activity elevation and the subsequent mineralization phase of osteogenic differentiation.

To clarify the molecular mechanism of material‐induced osteogenic differentiation, we assessed the expression levels of osteogenic‐related genes COL1A1 (Figure 3f) and RUNX2 (Figure S8) after 3 days of osteogenic induction culture. The findings indicated that, in contrast to the NC group, the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group markedly upregulated the expression of osteogenic marker genes. Notably, the enhancement effect was further amplified in the B or Sr single doping groups, while the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group demonstrated the highest expression levels of COL1A1 and RUNX2. This suggests that the synergistic release of B and Sr ions significantly stimulated the osteogenic differentiation program of MSCs at the genetic level. The quantitative analysis of COL1A1 (Figure 3g) indicated that the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group (0.55) was significantly elevated compared to the NC group (0.18).

In conclusion, the combined release of B and Sr ions markedly improved the bioreactivity and osteogenic induction capacity of the material while preserving cell viability. The synergistic effect may arise from the disruption of the Si─O network by B ions, facilitating ionic flow formation, which in turn elevates local pH and ion concentration. The gradual release of Sr ions via a mildly alkaline buffer can stabilize the microenvironment and activate the osteogenic signaling pathway, such as the RUNX2‐mediated initiation of early osteogenesis. The ionic flow microenvironment, dynamically created by material degradation, significantly enhances extracellular matrix formation and mineralization, offering vital experimental evidence for the subsequent “immune–osteogenic” co‐regulation via biomimetic ionic flow.

The SUIF Platform Drives MSCs–Macrophage Communication In Vitro

This study initially established a Transwell co‐culture system comprising the material, MSCs, and macrophages to assess the immune regulatory capacity of the material within the cellular interaction milieu (Figure 3a). It methodically examined the impact of the ionic flow microenvironment of the material on macrophage polarization. The flow cytometry results (Figure 3h) indicated that under co‐culture conditions, each material group significantly elevated the relative proportion of CD206‐positive macrophages, an M2‐type anti‐inflammatory phenotype marker, suggesting effective induction of M2 polarization. The 5B5Sr‐SUIF group exhibited the most pronounced effect. A subsequent quantitative analysis of the proportion of CD206+CD86− (M2 type specific phenotype, with CD86 serving as the M1 type pro‐inflammatory phenotype marker) cells (Figure 3i) indicated that the proportion of CD206+CD86− cells in the LPS stimulation group (0.37%) was significantly lower than that in the SUIF + LPS group (2.67%), whereas the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group (5.77%) exhibited the highest proportion. This further validated the superiority of 5B5Sr‐SUIF in eliciting M2 polarization. This study concurrently established control experiments: “material‐macrophage” and “material‐MSCs‐macrophage.” The findings indicated that, in contrast to the material group cultured independently with macrophages, the material‐MSCs‐macrophage co‐culture system markedly facilitated the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype (Figure S9), implying that MSCs can collaboratively augment the material's induction effect on the M2 phenotype conversion of macrophages, thereby reinforcing the immune regulatory mechanism of material‐cell interaction.

The outcomes of the immunofluorescence experiments (Figure 4a,b) indicated that, under the co‐culture conditions of materials‐MSCs‐macrophage, each material group markedly enhanced the expression of CD206 and ARG‐1 in macrophages, which 5B5Sr‐SUIF group exhibited the most pronounced effect on the upregulation of CD206 and ARG‐1. This further substantiated the superiority of this material group in promoting M2‐type polarization of macrophages. Analysis of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in the material‐MSCs‐macrophage co‐culture system (Figure 4c) revealed that the LPS stimulation group significantly elevated intracellular ROS levels. Conversely, each material treatment group effectively mitigated the LPS‐induced ROS increase, with the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group exhibiting the most pronounced inhibitory effect on ROS, indicating its superior capability in alleviating LPS‐mediated oxidative stress damage. The ELISA results (Figure 4d–f) confirmed that in the material‐MSCs‐macrophage co‐culture system, the expression levels of pro‐inflammatory factors (IL‐1β, TNF‐α) and pro‐inflammatory enzymes (iNOS) in each material treatment group were significantly reduced compared to the LPS group. Following LPS induction of RAW264.7 cells, immunofluorescence detection of NF‐κB p65 was conducted upon the addition of each component material. The results indicated that all material components could inhibit the nuclear translocation of NF‐κB p65, with 5B5Sr‐SUIF exhibiting the most significant effect (Figure S10). A comparative analysis of the “material‐macrophage” and the “material‐MSCs‐macrophage” indicated that the inhibitory effect of the single culture group on pro‐inflammatory factors (IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and iNOS) was markedly inferior to that of the co‐culture group (Figure S11). The ROS detection results indicated that the material‐MSCs‐macrophage co‐culture group effectively mitigated the elevation of ROS levels in RAW264.7 cells induced by LPS stimulation, thereby substantiating its anti‐inflammatory effect from the standpoint of oxidative stress (Figure S12). The results demonstrate that within the regulated ionic flow microenvironment, MSCs can detect alterations in microenvironmental signals and may facilitate the conversion of macrophages to anti‐inflammatory and tissue repair‐promoting phenotypes by secreting soluble signaling molecules, including cytokines and exosomes, thereby synergistically augmenting the regulatory influence on local immune homeostasis.

Additionally, in the osteoclast‐related experiments (Figure 4g,h), the 5B‐SUIF, 5Sr‐SUIF, and 5B5Sr‐SUIF groups markedly diminished the osteoclastic activity stimulated by macrophages. The mechanism is believed to reside in the mildly alkaline microenvironment created by the materials, which may suppress TNF‐α‐mediated pro‐osteoclastic signals and modulate the proliferation and differentiation of macrophages, thus facilitating the coordinated regulation of immune‐bone metabolism [56]. The in vitro migratory capacity of MSCs was evaluated utilizing the Transwell chamber technique (Figure 4i). MSCs were inoculated in the upper chamber, and after 24 h of incubation, the MSCs that migrated to the lower chamber were examined and photographed. The experimental results indicated that, in comparison to the negative control group (NC group), all material groups significantly enhanced MSCs migration, with the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group demonstrating the most marked migratory effect.

Comparing the outcomes of MSCs‐macrophage co‐culture experiments with those of isolated macrophage experiments reveals that the B/Sr co‐doped materials demonstrate substantial immune regulatory effects in both contexts: effectively suppressing pro‐inflammatory responses, diminishing oxidative stress, and facilitating M2 polarization, thus establishing an immune homeostatic environment favorable for tissue repair. Their shared characteristic is the anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effects resulting from ionic flow and pH buffering mechanisms, whereas the distinctions are evident in the enhanced immune regulatory effect conferred by the presence of MSCs, indicating that MSCs not only passively respond to material signals but also actively engage in the remodeling of immune homeostasis within the ionic flow microenvironment. In summary, B/Sr co‐doped materials, via ionic flow modulation and a weak alkaline buffering effect (fast‐slow biphasic), facilitate the transition of macrophages from the pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype to the pro‐healing M2 phenotype, diminish inflammation and oxidative stress responses, and create a conducive immune microenvironment for tissue regeneration.

In Vivo Dynamic Recruitment of Endogenous MSCs and Macrophages Mediated by SUIF

In vitro experiments have demonstrated that SUIF can elicit recruitment effects at the cellular level. This study performed flow cytometric analysis to ascertain if the ionic flow microenvironment can induce the migration of endogenous cells, specifically MSCs and macrophages, to the defect region in vivo, utilizing a mouse tibial bone defect model (Figure 5a). The findings indicated that, in comparison to the NC group, both materials facilitated the accumulation of MSCs in the defect region during the early postoperative phase, with the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group exhibiting the most pronounced recruitment advantage: a notable increase in the proportion of MSCs was recorded on the first postoperative day (Figure 5b,e), peaking on the third day (Figure 5c,f), and although it diminished by the seventh day, it remained significantly elevated relative to both the control group and the SUIF parallel material group (Figure 5d,g). This time series aligns closely with the biological process of early homing and intravasation of endogenous MSCs, followed by their involvement in osteogenic reconstruction during bone defect repair, suggesting that 5B5Sr‐SUIF significantly improves the in vivo homing and retention capacity of endogenous cells in the initial phase of injury.

Quantitative analysis of the proportion of MSC‐characteristic cell populations (Sca‐1+/CD29+/CD44+/CD45−) at day 1 (e), day 3 (f), and day 7 (g) post‐surgery. Concurrently, the overall quantity of macrophages in the defect region post‐material implantation exhibited an initial peak infiltration (Figure 5h): both materials markedly elevated the macrophage proportion on the third day (Figure 5i,j), with the SUIF group demonstrating a more pronounced peak, while the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group reverted to a level approximating that of the NC group by the seventh day (Figure 5k). The findings from the in vitro co‐culture experiments involving material, MSCs, and macrophages‐demonstrating M2 polarization along with anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effects‐suggest that the immune response elicited by 5B5Sr‐SUIF is characterized not merely by enhancement or inhibition, but rather aligns with a “moderate initiation‐timely restraint” repair‐oriented inflammatory rhythm. Simultaneously, the temporal window for MSCs enrichment (the third day coinciding with the peak infiltration of macrophages) suggests that the immune microenvironment alteration induced by 5B5Sr‐SUIF aligns temporally with the MSCs recruitment process. The in vivo results demonstrate a coupling relationship of “MSCs homing amplification‐immune initiation” in the temporal sequence.

In Vivo Immuno‐Osteogenic Coupled Repair Mediated by SUIF

Preliminary in vitro experiments have confirmed that SUIF can facilitate immune homeostasis remodeling and osteogenic induction at the cellular level through the coordinated release of B and Sr ions. It additionally facilitates the in vivo recruitment of endogenous MSCs in a murine tibial bone defect model. This study employed an animal model of tibial bone defect to rigorously assess the in vivo osteogenic efficacy of SUIF and the 5B5Sr‐SUIF material groups in complex physiological environments.

Micro‐CT imaging results (Figure 6a) indicated that both materials facilitated new bone formation, with the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group exhibiting markedly enhanced bone tissue regeneration at both 2 and 4 weeks. In comparison to the SUIF group, the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group demonstrated significant new bone formation in the defect region, accompanied by a more continuous and dense trabecular architecture. The quantitative analysis of bone microstructure (Figure 6b–d) demonstrated that, at multiple time intervals, the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group significantly surpassed the SUIF group in critical bone repair metrics. At two weeks, the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group demonstrated a markedly elevated bone volume fraction (BV/TV, 24.04%), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, 0.19 mm), and trabecular number (Tb.N, 1.53 mm− 1) in comparison to the SUIF group (BV/TV: 10.84%, Tb.Th: 0.15 mm, Tb.N: 0.41 mm− 1). The findings indicate that 5B5Sr‐SUIF not only effectively facilitates the structural reconstruction of bone tissue but also exhibits progressively enhanced effects on bone volume, trabecular thickness, and quantity over time, underscoring its superior bone repair efficacy.

The ionic flow microenvironment facilitates the reprogramming of macrophage phenotypes in immune regulation. This study involved the extraction of tissue cells from the bone defect region for the assessment of ROS levels (Figure S15). The findings indicated that, in comparison to the SUIF group, the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group significantly diminished the ROS levels in the tissue cells. This further substantiated that this material could still establish a low‐inflammatory and low‐injury microenvironment for bone repair by mitigating the local oxidative stress response in vivo. This outcome aligned with the trend of ROS regulation in the in vitro co‐culture system. The immunofluorescence analysis of tissue sections from the bone defect region (Figure 7a) indicated that, in contrast to the SUIF group, the positive fluorescence signal for the anti‐inflammatory macrophage marker CD163 in the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group was markedly elevated, whereas the positive signal for the pro‐inflammatory macrophage marker iNOS was significantly diminished. This indicated that the material could efficiently modulate the polarization of macrophage phenotypes in vivo. To further quantify the in vivo differences in macrophage phenotypes, flow cytometry (Figure 7e) was conducted on immune cells from the bone defect region, demonstrating a significant reduction in the proportion of M1 macrophages (CD80+/CD86+) in the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group. Quantitative analysis (Figure 7f) established that the percentage of CD80+/CD86+ cells in the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group (10.93%) was significantly lower than that in the SUIF group (15.12%, p < 0.05), thereby directly indicating that this material can markedly diminish the population of pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages in vivo. ELISA results (Figure 7b–d) corroborated the alterations in inflammation factor levels, revealing that the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group exhibited a significant decrease in pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐1β) and a notable increase in the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 relative to the SUIF group. In conclusion, 5B5Sr‐SUIF can effectively modulate the local immune response in the bone defect region by regulating the co‐release of B/Sr ions and the mildly alkaline microenvironment, thereby promoting a shift in inflammation toward a “anti‐inflammatory‐repair” state. This outcome aligned with the trend of immune regulation observed in vitro and established a comprehensive evidence chain for the immune mechanism underlying material‐mediated bone repair in vivo.

In the regulation of bone metabolism balance, 5B5Sr‐SUIF can effectively suppress osteoclast activity, thereby preserving the dynamic equilibrium between osteogenesis and osteolysis. The TRAP staining results (Figure 7k) indicated that, in comparison to the SUIF group, the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group exhibited a significant reduction in the number of TRAP‐positive osteoclasts in the bone defect region, along with a marked decrease in staining intensity, implying diminished osteoclast activity. The findings indicated that osteoclast activity stimulated by the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group remained significantly diminished (Figure S16), thereby further substantiating the material's direct regulatory influence on osteoclast differentiation. The ionic current microenvironment created by 5B5Sr‐SUIF can modulate the local pH and ion flux, effectively suppressing osteoclast differentiation and activity, thus preserving the dynamic equilibrium between osteogenesis and bone resorption, and facilitating an advantageous metabolic microenvironment for bone defect repair.

We investigated the tissue sections of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney of mice to detect the presence of deposited materials. The examination of the tissue sections revealed no discernible abnormalities in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys (Figure S17). The 5B5Sr‐SUIF constructed ionic flow network successfully facilitated bidirectional immune‐bone repair in vivo. Initially, it established a mildly alkaline microenvironment abundant in bioactive ions via B/Sr ion release, facilitating M2 polarization, and diminishing inflammation and oxidative stress; subsequently, through the sustained release of Sr2 +, it enhanced osteoblast differentiation and mineralization while suppressing the osteoclast activity. This ionic flow network accomplished the synchronized integration of immune homeostasis restoration and bone remodeling via a spatiotemporally programmable ion release pattern, offering an experimental and mechanistic foundation for the development of immune‐directed biomimetic ionic materials.

The Ion‐miRNA‐Immune‐Osteogenesis Signaling Axis Regulated by miR‐466m‐5p

To clarify the molecular regulatory mechanisms influenced by ionic flux, transcriptomic sequencing was conducted on MSCs treated with 5B5Sr‐SUIF for 24 h, in comparison to the control group (Figure 8). A comparative analysis of the miRNA expression profiles between the control (NC) and experimental (5B5Sr‐SUIF) groups identified 173 differentially expressed miRNAs, with 112 exhibiting upregulation and 63 demonstrating downregulation. We employed a volcano plot (Figure 8a) to visually depict the comprehensive distribution of differential miRNA expression. Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Figure 8b) offered a systematic annotation of the potential biological functions of these differentially expressed miRNAs, elucidating their fundamental roles in cellular physiological processes. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis (Figure 8d) was performed, emphasizing the top 20 significantly enriched pathways and revealing critical signaling pathways that may be implicated in the regulatory functions of these differential miRNAs. A heatmap (Figure 8c) distinctly depicted the expression profiles of the 10 most significantly upregulated and 10 downregulated miRNAs, effectively showcasing the differential expression patterns of these two miRNA groups. The findings revealed that multiple miRNAs demonstrated significant alterations in expression within the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group, with miR‐466m‐5p exhibiting the most notable upregulation. To confirm the sequencing results, we conducted quantitative PCR (qPCR) on the upregulated miRNAs (miR‐466m‐5p and miR‐320‐5p) and downregulated miRNAs (miR‐142a‐5p and miR‐184‐3p). The qPCR validation demonstrated that the expression of miR‐466m‐5p was elevated by approximately 4.72‐fold, whereas miR‐320‐5p was upregulated by 1.17‐fold in the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group, corroborating the findings from the sequencing data (Figure 8e). The findings indicate that miR‐466m‐5p may function as a crucial molecular hub in the regulation of stem cell differentiation through ionic flux signaling.

The integrated findings from in vivo and in vitro studies indicate that miR‐466m‐5p serves as a pivotal molecular hub within the ionic flow regulatory network, establishing a multi‐tiered signaling linkage mechanism of “ionic flow‐miRNA‐immune‐bone”. The synchronized release of B/Sr ions can specifically enhance the expression of miR‐466m‐5p. This microRNA, in turn, promotes the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs and encourages macrophages to polarize toward the M2 anti‐inflammatory phenotype, while simultaneously inhibiting their differentiation into osteoclasts. The primary regulatory mechanism relies on the targeted suppression of the NF‐κB signaling pathway, thereby facilitating the integrated regulation of local immune homeostasis and the bone remodeling process. This signaling axis elucidates the molecular foundation of ion signal transduction within the sea urchin‐like ionic flow network material via spatiotemporal programmability, facilitating bidirectional synergy between immune and skeletal systems. It offers a crucial theoretical foundation and mechanistic support for the advancement of a new generation of “immune‐directed” biomimetic biological materials.

Discussion

The restoration and healing of intricate bone defects encounter a challenge due to the discordance in the spatiotemporal coordination of immune, stem cell, and bone formation signals [7]. Local acidification, ionic imbalances, and other pathological microenvironments can compromise the immune regulatory function of MSCs, resulting in sustained inflammation and hindered bone formation [2, 8]. While the regulation of bioactive ions and pH has been demonstrated to influence immune polarization and osteogenic differentiation, current strategies struggle to achieve temporal coordination and integrated regulation of both factors, and lack a material platform that harmonizes biological rationality with engineering controllability. This study utilizes “immunoregulatory ionic flow mimicking sea urchin” as its central concept and develops a platform capable of temporally regulating the ionic flow microenvironment. By incorporating a B─O bond to modulate the network disintegration rate and establishing a “fast‐slow biphasic” ionic flow pattern via Sr2 + slow‐release, while systematically examining its mechanism for facilitating MSCs‐macrophage communication, recruiting endogenous MSCs, and enhancing bone regeneration through the “ionic flow‐miRNA‐immune‐bone formation” multi‐level signaling axis. The integrated internal and external outcomes indicate that this system not only attains alkaline homeostasis and regulates ionic flow gradients but also fulfills the dynamic loop of “immunoregulatory instruction‐immune response‐bone formation execution” by imparting immunoregulatory capabilities to MSCs, thereby offering a theoretical foundation for the advancement of a new generation of immune‐regulated regenerative materials.

The immune instruction‐type ionic flow microenvironment facilitates communication between MSCs and macrophages, as well as their in vivo recruitment (Figure 5). Conventional research on bone regeneration primarily concentrated on the unidirectional modulation of MSCs by macrophages, specifically enhancing osteogenesis via material‐induced M2 polarization. MSCs were predominantly perceived as passive responders. This study demonstrates that within the 5B5Sr‐SUIF platform, MSCs acquired an “immune instruction” function through the activation of ionic flow signals, enabling them to modulate macrophage fate. In the material‐MSCs‐macrophage co‐culture system, each material group induced macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, with the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group exhibiting the most pronounced increase in the proportion of M2‐type and the expression of CD206/ARG‐1 protein (Figure 4). Compared to “material‐macrophage,” the “material‐MSCs‐macrophage” system demonstrated a more potent effect on M2 polarization, ROS inhibition, and the down‐regulation of IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and iNOS (Figures S9 and S10 and S12). This indicates that MSCs were activated within the ionic flow microenvironment as active participants in the remodeling of immune homeostasis, rather than merely passive receptors.

Cell recruitment is a crucial step in tissue repair processes, particularly in the repair of bone defects [37, 39]. The efficient recruitment of MSCs significantly influences the initiation efficacy and ultimate repair quality of bone regeneration. On one hand, MSCs that migrate to the defect area function as osteogenic precursor cells, directly engaging in the synthesis and mineralization of the bone matrix, serving as the primary effector cells for bone tissue regeneration [59]. Conversely, these recruited MSCs can modify the stability of the immune microenvironment in the defect area by interacting with local immune cells, such as macrophages, thus regulating the inflammatory process and the equilibrium between osteoclast and osteoblast dynamics [42, 60, 61]. The cell recruitment effect identified in this study may be intricately associated with the B and Sr ions liberated from the material. Prior research has established that Ca and Sr ions significantly enhance cell recruitment [62, 63, 64]. In this study, the 5B5Sr‐SUIF material modulates the degradation rate of the B─O bond network and the release kinetics of Sr2 +, thereby establishing a weakly alkaline stable microenvironment in the bone defect region (Figure 2). This microenvironment can counteract the functional impairment of MSCs under pathological conditions, thus fostering an appropriate physiological environment for the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs. Subsequent Transwell migration assays demonstrated that SUIF markedly enhances the in vitro migration of MSCs, with the 5B5Sr‐SUIF cohort exhibiting the highest number of migrated cells. In the murine tibial bone defect model, 5B5Sr‐SUIF expedites the remodeling of the “immune‐osteogenic” microenvironment during the initial phase of the bone defect, significantly improving the homing and retention of endogenous MSCs (increasing on Day 1, peaking on Day 3, and remaining elevated compared to the SUIF control group on Day 7), whereas macrophages display an early infiltration peak on Day 3 followed by a rapid decline to levels comparable to the control by Day 7. The sequence of “early moderate inflammatory initiation, MSCs recruitment and timely inflammation restraint” aligns with in vitro cell interaction results, suggesting that the material activates the immunological signals of MSCs, modulates macrophage phenotypes, and facilitates a positive feedback loop among activated MSCs, thereby enhancing the recruitment/regeneration signal and suppressing osteoclastic activity. Ultimately, it facilitates synchronous regulation of bone formation and suppression of osteoclastic activity, thereby expediting the high‐quality regeneration of bone defects.

This phenomenon illustrates the fundamental trait of the immune‐instructive microenvironment: external ion signals impart immune regulatory functions upon stem cells, rendering them active contributors to the reconfiguration of immune homeostasis. In the B/Sr co‐doped system, the rapid release of BO3 3 − undermines the Si─O network, facilitates ion migration, and elevates local pH, thereby offering an initial signaling window for MSCs (Figure 2). The sustained release of Sr2 + preserves a weakly alkaline microenvironment, mitigates acidic inflammation, and fosters the continuity of osteogenic signals. The “fast‐slow biphasic” ionic flow release pattern alters the temporal synchrony among cells: the initial activation of MSCs' immune instructive output prompts macrophages to adopt anti‐inflammatory and reparative phenotypes; subsequently, the sustained osteogenic signals and preservation of a low‐inflammatory milieu facilitate an ongoing integration of immunity and osteogenesis in both time and space. The femoral bone defect model further corroborated that the 5B5Sr‐SUIF group not only significantly enhanced BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N, and other indicators of bone microstructure, but also exhibited a more mature and continuous new bone structure in histological analysis and COL1A1/OCN immunofluorescence. This was accompanied by a reduction in local ROS, an increase in the M2 marker CD163, and a decrease in the ratios of the M1 markers iNOS and CD80+/CD86+ cells, indicating a robust coupling between the material‐induced reparative immune phase and bone structure reconstruction in vivo (Figures 6 and 7).

miR‐466m‐5p functions as a molecular nexus for the integration of ionic flow signals with immune‐bone signaling. Prior research has established that miR‐466m‐5p is a pivotal microRNA contained in extracellular vesicles secreted by MSCs, and it plays a role in the cellular stress response by modulating antioxidant and inflammatory pathways [65]. Its essential function is modulating the inflammatory microenvironment and preserving cellular functional equilibrium. This study demonstrates through transcriptome analysis and functional validation that miR‐466m‐5p is a crucial mediator of cell signal transduction in the alkaline ionic flow microenvironment (Figure 8).

The upregulation enhances the expression of osteogenic‐related genes (RUNX2, COL1A1, ALP, OCN, and OPN) in MSCs and simultaneously inhibits the nuclear translocation of NF‐κB in inflammatory macrophages, thereby suppressing pro‐inflammatory signals and promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. In the LPS‐induced inflammatory microenvironment, it can markedly diminish MSC apoptosis and reinstate the expression of Runx2 and COL1A1, illustrating the dual effects of osteogenic protection and anti‐inflammatory tolerance. At the level of immunological regulation, miR‐466m‐5p markedly increases CD206/ARG‐1 expression, suppresses CD80/iNOS, down‐regulates IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and iNOS, and up‐regulates IL‐10 in the LPS‐induced RAW264.7 model. It also diminishes intracellular ROS, inhibits the formation of TRAP‐positive osteoclasts, and reduces the expression of osteogenesis‐related genes (MMP9, NFATC1, RANKL, and TRAP). Mechanistic studies indicate that miR‐466m‐5p can suppress NF‐κB pathway activation, diminish p‐P65 levels, and obstruct P65 nuclear translocation. The associated effects have also been confirmed in animal models: The overexpression of miR‐466m‐5p markedly elevates M2 (CD80−/CD206+) macrophage proportions while diminishing M1 (CD80+/CD206−) macrophages, decreases ROS, TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and osteoblastic activity in the bone defect region, and suppresses the expression of RANKL, TRAP, and MMP9 (Figures 9, 10, 11).

The aforementioned results demonstrate that miR‐466m‐5p has formed a stable molecular connection between ion signals and immune signals: it activates the osteogenic differentiation program in MSCs and enhances inflammation tolerance, while simultaneously inhibiting the NF‐κB signaling pathway to promote macrophage polarization toward M2 and suppress osteoclast differentiation, thereby facilitating the longitudinal integration of ion‐miRNA‐immune‐osteogenesis. 5B5Sr‐SUIF specifically enhances the expression of miR‐466m‐5p, allowing MSCs to exhibit a triadic regulatory capacity of “immune response, osteogenesis, and bone metabolism.” This miRNA‐mediated regulatory pathway, influenced by ion signals, offers a novel framework for comprehending the mechanism of information translation between materials and cells.

Designing alkaline ionic flow microenvironments and immunological engineering with bionic topological structures is innovative. Sea urchins' radiating needle‐like channels have “open ionic flow” mineralized structures, which can be used to make functional materials with directional, continuous, and self‐stabilizing properties [43]. The 5B5Sr‐SUIF material innovatively couples “bionic form” with “ion function” deeply, creating anisotropic ion migration channels and a precise spatial gradient window for local pH values and ion fluxes at the cellular scale, creating a stable weakly alkaline ionic flow microenvironment for MSCs and macrophages. This functional microenvironment regulates biological processes like miR‐466m‐5p‐mediated NF‐κB nuclear translocation inhibition, macrophage M2 polarization, and osteogenic‐related gene upregulation, ensuring a solid structural foundation and efficient signal transduction for stem cells, immune cells, and other cells to collaborate. This design concept broadens the cognitive boundaries of the “structure‐function” correlation of materials and offers a new idea for immunological engineering material development: precisely regulating the ionic flow form to achieve dynamic cell fate shaping, promoting the material to move from passively meeting “biocompatibility” requirements to actively executing “immune instructions” at a higher level.

Nevertheless, this study also possesses specific limitations. It has elucidated the molecular mechanism and biological impact of the mimic echinoderm immune command‐type ionic flow in bone immune reconstruction; however, additional comprehensive studies are required at various levels. Future research should quantitatively examine the ion‐receptor coupling mechanism at the receptor level, elucidate the binding kinetics of boron and strontium ions on immune and osteoblast membrane receptors (such as TLR, GPCR, or CaSR). Systematically clarify the downstream signaling network of miR‐466m‐5p, identify its target genes and upstream regulatory factors through a multi‐omics approach. Develop a macro‐micro scale cross‐level modeling system, employing multi‐physical field simulation to couple the dynamic processes of ionic flow, pH variations, and cellular responses, while further validating the long‐term bone integration effects and systemic safety of the material in large animal models. These studies will enhance the immune command‐type ionic flow system and establish a robust foundation for the advancement of designable ion signal programming and personalized immuno‐osteogenic materials.

Conclusions

This study, utilizing the mimetic mineralization topological structure strategy of sea urchins, established an immune‐regulated ionic flow microenvironment. This system stimulates the immune response of MSCs via the synchronized release of multifunctional ions, enhances miR‐466m‐5p expression, suppresses the NF‐κB signaling pathway, promotes polarization from M1 to M2, and inhibits osteoclast differentiation. Simultaneously, it establishes a robust coupling of endogenous MSCs' dynamic recruitment, immune homeostasis remodeling, and in vivo bone structure reconstruction, ultimately creating a multi‐level coupling mechanism of “ionic flow‐ miRNA‐immunity‐osteogenesis.” This mechanism enhances the comprehension of the action principles of bone immune regulatory materials and offers an innovative biomimetic strategy and theoretical framework for the precise regeneration of intricate bone defects.
