Many-vs-Many Missile Guidance via Virtual Targets
Marc Schneider, Walter Fichter

Abstract




1
Introduction

The problem of guiding multiple missiles against multiple maneuvering targets represents one of the most challenging scenarios in modern missile defense systems. Many-vs-many missile guidance involves complex decision-making processes including target assignment, energy management, and trajectory optimization, all while accounting for the uncertain future behavior of maneuvering targets.

A critical challenge in guidance is energy management, which becomes evident in the midcourse phase of the missile flight.
In this phase, missiles must balance the need to maintain sufficient energy for terminal phase maneuvers while also ensuring that they can effectively intercept their targets.
Reacting to every maneuver of the target can lead to excessive energy expenditure, especially when the target performs maneuvers that are not aligned with the missile’s trajectory.
Not acknowledging any target maneuvers, on the other hand, can result in missed interceptions, particularly when the target performs significant maneuvers that deviate from its predicted trajectory.

In many-vs-many scenarios, the problem is further complicated by the need to cooperatively manage multiple interceptors, but this also gives rise to the opportunity to exploit numerical superiority.
When multiple missiles are available, they can be distributed across various predicted target trajectories, allowing for a more robust engagement strategy that increases the probability of successful interception.

Practically, the problem we address is the following.
In a two-dimensional engagement with
m
maneuvering targets and
n
interceptors, where the target’s future motion is uncertain, the goal is to maximize the number of successful interceptions.
The guidance task is to provide a centralized strategy that distributes the available interceptors across the targets by leveraging stochastic trajectory predictions to maximize the overall and generates midcourse.
This requires an effective use of the target prediction and the generation of suitable midcourse and terminal control commands that balance energy use and interception probability.
The central difficulty is representing target uncertainty in a compact, actionable form so that
n
interceptors can be assigned to representative trajectory hypotheses without relying on a large set of hand-tuned maneuver options.

Recent advances in missile guidance have explored several complementary strategies to handle target uncertainty and exploit numerical superiority.
One approach to handle target uncertainty is the multi-hypothesis method used in one-vs-one engagements.
In this approach a set of discrete future maneuvers (for example: left, straight, right) is assumed for the target and guidance is designed to hedge across those hypotheses
[
1
]
.
Such multi
-
hypothesis schemes can improve interception probability by explicitly accounting for maneuver uncertainty, but they tend to be conservative: mitigating a worst
-
case hypothesis often increases energy expenditure and can reduce overall performance when the target does not execute the assumed worst
-
case maneuver.

Another class of methods considers many
-
vs
-
one scenarios.
For example, encirclement strategies deploy multiple interceptors to surround a single target and thereby raise the likelihood of a successful hit
[
2
]
.
These methods typically assume missiles with controllable speed (e.g., cruise missiles) so that distinct approach geometries and timings can be enforced.

Many
-
vs
-
many engagements have been addressed using geometric midcourse techniques such as Earliest Intercept Geometry, which derive guidance laws from encounter geometry and timing considerations
[
3
]
.
These methods perform well for planar encounters but are typically formulated for two
-
dimensional problems and do not fully exploit the flexibility of distributing multiple interceptors across a range of plausible target behaviors.
The concept of virtual targets (VTs) offers an alternative way to organize many
-
vs
-
many engagements by decoupling interceptor guidance from direct pursuit of physical targets.
In some works, VTs are implemented as stationary intermediate waypoints that guide missiles until they select a physical target upon arrival
[
4
]
.
In other contexts, VTs appear in one
-
vs
-
one settings as a sequence of static waypoints that augment otherwise simple guidance laws
[
5
]
.

Extending the concept of multiple hypotheses and VTs, a probabilistic trajectory prediction approach presented in our previous work
[
6
]

represents future target positions as a distribution rather than propagating a small set of hand
-
picked maneuvers.
By using a Normalizing Flows (NFs) model to sample from the learned distribution and then time
-
series clustering those samples, one can construct exactly
n
VT trajectories that compactly capture the uncertainty across the
m
maneuvering targets.
These data
-
driven VTs avoid the combinatorial explosion of hand
-
tuned hypotheses and produce representative, learned trajectory hypotheses that are directly usable by midcourse guidance.
It is important to note that the term “virtual targets” in the works
[
5
,
4
]
refers to stationary waypoints, whereas in
[
6
]
the term refers to predicted future trajectories of maneuvering targets.

In this paper, we propose a novel approach to many-vs-many missile guidance using VTs generated by the method proposed in our previous paper
[
6
]
.
This means that the VTs are not stationary waypoints, but rather a mapping of the predicted position of the target over time.
These trajectories serve directly as the predicted target trajectories in the guidance law, without the need for intermediate waypoints or switching strategies.

Instead of assigning
n
missiles directly to
m
physical targets through traditional Weapon Target Assignment (WTA) algorithms
[
7
]
, we propose a centralized strategy that constructs
n
VT trajectories, each corresponding to a representative future trajectory of the
m
maneuvering targets based on probabilistic predictions.
Each missile is then guided toward its assigned VT using Zero-Effort-Miss (ZEM)
[
8
]
guidance, which takes the predicted future trajectory of the VT into account, and switches to Proportional Navigation (PN)
[
9
]
guidance in the endgame phase.

This approach fundamentally addresses the inherent limitations of conventional many-vs-many guidance by treating the problem as a many-vs-distribution scenario, meaning that instead of engaging discrete targets, the system engages a distribution of predicted target positions which are then clustered into VTs.
Each missile is guided toward a unique VT trajectory, effectively creating
n
distinct hypotheses of the targets’ future behavior rather than relying on handpicked maneuvers or a single deterministic predictions for each target.
This strategic distribution of missiles across diverse predicted trajectories exploits numerical superiority to significantly increase the probability of successful interception.

The primary contribution of this paper is the development and demonstration of this scalable guidance approach using probabilistically generated VTs, validated through Monte Carlo simulations that demonstrate improved interception probabilities compared to conventional approaches, particularly in scenarios with large numbers of interceptors.

In the following, we outline the algorithms and simulation setup used to evaluate the proposed approach.
First, the simulation scenario and parameters are described.
Second, the overall guidance structure common to the proposed approach and the baseline approach (using straight-line predictions) is outlined.
Third, the specific guidance laws used in the midcourse and endgame phases are explained in detail.
Finally, both the baseline approach and the proposed VT approach are presented in detail, enabling a fair comparison between conventional and probabilistic prediction methods.

2
Algorithms and Simulation Setup

The core concept leverages VT trajectories generated by the method proposed in
[
6
]
as predicted target trajectories in a guidance law.
These VTs represent probabilistic predictions of maneuvering target trajectories, enabling the guidance system to distribute interceptors across multiple plausible future scenarios rather than relying on a single deterministic prediction per target.

As a basis for comparison, we implement a baseline approach that uses straight-line predictions for the targets, which is a common method in missile guidance.
To ensure an unbiased evaluation, both approaches employ identical guidance laws and scenario parameters, differing only in the target trajectory prediction method. This controlled comparison isolates the impact of the prediction approach on interception performance.

2.1
Scenario

The evaluation scenario consists of a two-dimensional horizontal engagement between multiple maneuvering targets and multiple interceptors.
This simplified setup allows for a focused analysis of the guidance approach pinpointing the core concepts while maintaining computational tractability for extensive Monte Carlo simulations.
The extension to three dimensions is straightforward but for simplicity and better visualization of the approach, we limit our analysis to two dimensions.

Both targets and interceptors are modeled as point masses with lateral acceleration capability only. Their velocity is constant, i.e., neither vehicle type can adjust thrust or drag.

Targets maintain constant forward velocity while executing random lateral maneuvers according to the stochastic model described in
[
6
]
.
This model captures evasive behavior patterns without requiring detailed target intent modeling.
Interceptors employ a two-phase guidance strategy: ZEM guidance during the midcourse phase (low frequency, energy-efficient) and PN guidance during the endgame phase (high frequency, precision-focused).

To isolate the impact of trajectory prediction methods, all interceptors launch from identical positions with identical initial velocities.
Similarly, all targets start from the same location with uniform initial conditions. This eliminates assignment optimization and ensures performance differences stem purely from prediction quality rather than geometric advantages.

and are chosen such that the scenario poses a challenging, yet feasible interception problem for both approaches.

2.2
Guidance Structure

Figure

Both methods employ an identical two-phase guidance structure to ensure fair comparison, with the only difference being the trajectory prediction method used to generate the
n
target trajectories.

The dual-phase strategy balances energy management during the long midcourse flight with precision requirements during final interception.
In the midcourse phase, interceptors use ZEM guidance with a low update frequency (
f_{\mathrm{ZEM}}=0.5
Hz) until reaching the endgame threshold distance (
d_{\mathrm{endgame}}=6,000
m) from their closest physical target.
This phase prioritizes energy efficiency while maintaining effective guidance toward the predicted interception point.
Upon entering the endgame region, the system switches to PN guidance with high update frequency (
f_{\mathrm{PN}}=40
Hz), ensuring precise terminal maneuvering when accuracy is critical and time-to-go is short.
Both guidance laws generate 2-dimensional acceleration commands
\mathbf{a}_{i}
for each interceptor
i
in the inertial frame, which represent lateral accelerations, i.e., 1-dimensional acceleration commands in the body frame.

Given identical initial conditions for all vehicles, both approaches use simple sequential assignment: the
i
-th interceptor targets the
i
-th physical target (baseline) or
i
-th VT (proposed approach).
This eliminates WTA tasks and isolates the performance impact of trajectory prediction methods.
Under these uniform initial conditions, assigning multiple interceptors to a single physical target provides no performance benefit using the baseline approach, as they would follow identical trajectories due to identical starting conditions, identical predictions, and identical guidance laws.
Therefore, the baseline strategy maintains a one-to-one assignment where the number of interceptors
n
equals the number of targets
m
(
n=m
), whereas the proposed approach can leverage numerical superiority by creating
n>m
distinct VTs.

2.3
Midcourse Guidance Law: Zero-Effort-Miss (ZEM)

The midcourse phase employs ZEM guidance, a predictive guidance law that computes an acceleration command to minimize miss distance at the predicted interception time.
To achieve this, ZEM guidance uses the predicted future trajectory
\mathbf{p}_{j}(t)
of a target
j
at time
t
.

The acceleration command for interceptor
i
pursuing target
j
is:

\mathbf{a}_{i,j}=\frac{N\cdot\mathbf{r}_{i,j}(t_{\mathrm{go}})}{t_{\mathrm{go}}^{2}}

(1)

where
N
is the navigation gain,
\mathbf{r}_{i,j}(t_{\mathrm{go}})
is the predicted relative position vector at time-to-go
t_{\mathrm{go}}
, assuming zero future acceleration from the interceptor.
The relative position vector uses the predicted position
\mathbf{p}_{i}(t)
of interceptor
i
and the predicted position
\mathbf{p}_{j}(t)
of the target
j
at time
t
:

\mathbf{r}_{i,j}(t)=\mathbf{p}_{i}(t)-\mathbf{p}_{j}(t)

(2)

Here,
\mathbf{p}_{i}(t)
is predicted assuming the interceptor continues with its current velocity (i.e., zero future acceleration), and
\mathbf{p}_{j}(t)
is predicted according to the chosen prediction method (straight-line or VT).
The time-to-go
t_{\mathrm{go}}
is determined by minimizing the relative distance between the interceptor and the target along their respective predicted trajectories.
Specifically, for each discrete future time step
t
within the prediction horizon, the Euclidean distance
\left\|\mathbf{p}_{i}(t)-\mathbf{p}_{j}(t)\right\|
is computed. The value of
t_{\mathrm{go}}
is then selected as the time step at which this distance is minimized:

t_{\mathrm{go}}=\arg\min_{t}\left\|\mathbf{p}_{i}(t)-\mathbf{p}_{j}(t)\right\|

(3)

This discrete minimization is performed over all future time steps within the prediction horizon, and the corresponding
t_{\mathrm{go}}
is used in the ZEM guidance law to compute the acceleration command.

Lastly, the acceleration command calculated in Eq. (
1
) is limited by the maximum lateral acceleration
a_{\mathrm{max}}
of the interceptor:

\mathbf{a}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}{a}_{\mathrm{max}}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{a}_{i,j}}{\left\|\mathbf{a}_{i,j}\right\|}&\text{if }\left\|\mathbf{a}_{i,j}\right\|>a_{\mathrm{max}}\\
\mathbf{a}_{i,j}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(4)

2.4
Endgame Guidance Law: Proportional Navigation (PN)

Upon reaching the endgame threshold distance, interceptors switch to PN guidance for terminal precision.
PN is a well-established guidance law
[
8
]
that responds to changes in the line-of-sight between interceptor and target, making it highly effective for close-range interception scenarios.

PN guidance offers computational efficiency crucial during the endgame phase, where high update frequencies (
f_{\mathrm{PN}}=40
Hz) are necessary for precise maneuvering.
Unlike ZEM guidance, which requires trajectory prediction, PN operates on instantaneous relative motion measurements, reducing computational overhead when rapid response is critical.
In the endgame phase, interceptors engage the actual physical targets rather than the predicted trajectories, as the close proximity enables direct sensing and the short remaining flight time reduces the benefit of complex trajectory prediction.

2.5
Baseline Approach: Straight-Line Prediction

The baseline approach represents conventional many-vs-many guidance strategies commonly employed when sophisticated target behavior models are unavailable. This approach serves as the performance benchmark against which the proposed VT method is evaluated.

Instead of probabilistic VTs, the baseline assumes each target will continue on a straight-line trajectory extrapolated from current state information. For target
j
at future time
t
, the predicted position is:

\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{straight},j}(t)=\mathbf{p}_{j}(t_{0})+\mathbf{v}_{j}(t_{0})\cdot(t-t_{0})

(5)

where
\mathbf{p}_{j}(t_{0})
and
\mathbf{v}_{j}(t_{0})
represent the current position and velocity of target
j
at time
t_{0}
, respectively.

This linear extrapolation method represents a widely-used approximation in missile guidance systems
[
8
]
when detailed target maneuver models are unavailable or when computational resources are limited. While simple, this approach often struggles with maneuvering targets, as it cannot anticipate future course changes or evasive actions.

2.6
Proposed Virtual Target Approach

The proposed approach fundamentally reimagines many-vs-many guidance by treating it as a many-vs-distribution problem.
To this end, we utilize the approach described in
[
6
]
.
An NFs model is trained to predict future positions of maneuvering targets, generating a probabilistic distribution of future target positions.
NFs are a class of generative Machine Learning models that transform a simple distribution (e.g., Gaussian) into a complex distribution that captures the underlying structure of the data
[
10
]
, which in this case is the distribution of future target positions.
Subsequently, a time-series k-means clustering algorithm is applied to the predicted future positions to create
n
VT trajectories, each representing a distinct trajectory hypothesis derived from the probabilistic predictions.

Instead of assigning interceptors directly to physical targets, this method creates VTs representing probabilistic predictions of target behavior, then distributes interceptors across these diverse trajectory hypotheses.
The ZEM guidance law is particularly well-suited for this VT concept, as it naturally incorporates future trajectory predictions in its acceleration command computation.

Figure
2
illustrates the guidance approach and Fig.
1
shows the prediction of trajectories.
The process begins by using a trained NFs model to generate
n_{\mathrm{s}}=1,000
future position samples for each of the
n_{t}=20
time steps for each of the
m
physical targets.
This produces a total of
m\cdot n_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot n_{t}
predicted positions representing the uncertainty in target future behavior.

These position samples are then clustered into exactly
n
VTs using a time-series k-means clustering algorithm, effectively creating
n
VT trajectories from the
m
maneuvering targets.
Equation (
6
) describes the algorithm
C_{n}
for the generation of
n
VT trajectories
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{VT},i}(t)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
from the
m
maneuvering targets with current positions
\mathbf{p}_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
.

C_{n}:\{\mathbf{p}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}\mapsto\{\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{VT},i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{n}

(6)

Each VT
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{VT},i}(t)

represents a distinct trajectory hypothesis derived from the probabilistic predictions, allowing that the
n
interceptors can be guided toward
n
representative future trajectories of the
m
maneuvering targets rather than all inerceptors pursuing the same
m
deterministic predictions regarding the target trajectories.
The detailed NFs implementation and clustering algorithm are described in
[
6
]
.

The VT generation operates dynamically throughout the engagement, meaning that at each guidance law evaluation, new position samples are generated and re-clustered.
To prevent erratic interceptor behavior, the clustering algorithm is initialized using the previous time step’s cluster centroids, ensuring smooth trajectory evolution for each VT.

When a target is successfully intercepted, both the target and its interceptor become inactive.
The corresponding VT is removed from the set, and subsequent clustering operates with the reduced number of active VTs, maintaining the correspondence between remaining interceptors and their assigned VTs.

In hypothetical scenarios where

For instance, with 2 targets and 1 interceptor, the single VT could be positioned between the two physical targets, causing the interceptor to initially pursue an intermediate trajectory until reaching the endgame threshold, where it switches to PN guidance toward the nearest physical target.
This limitation is reflected in the simulation results, where such scenarios show reduced performance compared to balanced engagements.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented a novel many-vs-many missile guidance approach using VTs generated by a NFs-based trajectory predictor.
Instead of assigning
n
missiles directly to
m
physical targets, we proposed a centralized strategy that constructs
n
VTs, each corresponding to a representative future trajectory of the
m
maneuvering targets, based on probabilistic predictions.
Each missile is then guided toward its assigned VT using ZEM guidance as a midcourse guidance law, whereas a high-frequency PN guidance law is used in the endgame phase to ensure a successful interception.

The proposed method was compared to a baseline approach that uses straight-line predictions for the targets.
Simulation results demonstrated the effectiveness of this scalable approach, which allowed to increase the probability of successful interception in many-vs-many scenarios by increasing the number of interceptors.
The results showed that the proposed approach outperformed the baseline approach, especially in scenarios with a large number of interceptors, where the numerical superiority could be exploited to increase the probability of successful interception.
