Assessment of frontal QRS–T angle in distinguishing mild and severe acute pancreatitis in emergency department: A retrospective study
Authors: Gülbin Aydoğdu Umaç, Sarper Yilmaz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a disease that poses significant health risks with potential severe outcomes. Identifying markers for severity can aid in early intervention and improve prognoses. This study, conducted in the emergency department, investigates the QRS–T angle as a potential indicator of severity in patients with acute pancreatitis. This retrospective study included 367 patients diagnosed with AP, classified as either mild acute pancreatitis or severe acute pancreatitis based on Ranson criteria. The QRS–T angle was measured using standard 12-lead electrocardiogram, and the data were analyzed to compare the QRS–T angles between the mild acute pancreatitis and severe acute pancreatitis groups. This research adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement. The study included 367 patients, 94% (n = 345) had mild pancreatitis while 6% (n = 22) had severe pancreatitis. Severe cases had a significantly higher median age (P < .001) and higher rates of hypertension and diabetes mellitus (P = .027, P = .011). Severe cases also had higher Ranson scores (3 [IQR 3–3.25] vs 1 [IQR 1–2], P < .001) and longer hospital stays (6 [IQR 2.75–11.5] days vs 3 [2–5] days, P = .029). Additionally, severe pancreatitis group showed higher QRS–T angle (P < .001), higher rates of abnormal QRS angles (31.8% vs 10.1%, P = .002), and QRS–T angles (31.8% vs 8.1%, P < .001). The QRS–T angle may serve as a valuable clinical tool for differentiating between mild and severe forms of acute pancreatitis. This could potentially help clinicians in stratifying patients according to their risk and tailoring their management accordingly.

1. Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a disease characterized by pancreatic inflammation triggered by the activation of various inactive pancreatic enzymes, resulting in an elevation of their circulating levels. It presents initially as a mild, localized, self-limiting condition but can progress to systemic inflammatory response syndrome, sepsis, multiple organ failure, and ultimately death, accompanied by both local and systemic complications.[1–3] Early diagnosis of AP and prognostic determination are pivotal for the precise and expeditious planning of treatment. Particularly in cohorts at high risk, the expeditious application of aggressive fluid resuscitation, vigilant monitoring for local and systemic complications, appropriate antibiotic administration, and prompt implementation of specific interventions such as endoscopic procedures substantially mitigate mortality rates.[4,5]

Approximately half of AP patients exhibit electrocardiographic abnormalities, including arrhythmias, ST-T wave changes, and intraventricular conduction disturbances.[6,7] While nonspecific repolarization changes are most detected during AP, abnormalities such as low voltage and inversion of T waves are also frequently observed.[8–10] The frontal QRS–T angle, representing the angular difference between the QRS complex and T wave on an electrocardiogram (ECG), is considered indicative of ventricular repolarization heterogeneity.[11] Metabolic and physiological changes occurring during AP can significantly impact the ECG. These changes can be attributed to mechanisms such as electrolyte imbalances, proteolytic enzymes’ damaging effects on the myocardium, exacerbation of preexisting cardiac conditions, and coronary artery spasm.[6] Particularly affecting the ventricular repolarization process, these factors can lead to alterations in the frontal QRS–T angle.

We hypothesize that the frontal QRS–T angle in AP could play a significant role in differentiating between mild and severe cases of the disease, particularly by indicating the severity of the condition.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This retrospective study was conducted in the emergency department of the Kartal Dr Lütfi Kirdar City Hospital, a tertiary care center with an annual intake of approximately 4.5 million visits, approximately 1.5 million of which are to the emergency department, and a capacity of 1166 beds. The study was approved by the Kartal Dr Lütfi Kirdar City Hospital Ethics Committee (Ethics committee no: 2023/514/255/7). This research adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement.[12]

2.2. Data collection

We identified patients diagnosed with acute pancreatitis by extracting data from electronic medical records and patient charts using ICD code (K85). A dataset was compiled and organized using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). The data entry was conducted by 2 emergency medicine residents, each with at least 4 years of experience. To ensure interobserver reliability, these researchers were provided with preliminary training. Any discrepancies in the data collected by the researchers were reviewed by the principal investigator, who made the final determinations.[13]

2.3. Study population

This study includes patients diagnosed with acute pancreatitis within the period from September 1st, 2022, to September 1st, 2023. All patients aged 18 years and older, meeting the following criteria, were included: presence of at least 2 of the following criteria: acute abdominal pain and tenderness consistent with pancreatitis, serum amylase or lipase levels exceeding 3 times the upper limit of normal, and imaging findings (ultrasound or CT) compatible with acute pancreatitis.[14] In instances where patients had multiple admissions during the study period, only their first admission was considered. Patients transferred from another hospital, those with bundle branch block or pacemaker rhythm, as well as individuals with coronary artery disease, chronic pancreatitis, atrial fibrillation, or heart failure, were excluded from the study.

The ECGs of all enrolled patients were assessed. These ECGs were conducted using a 12-lead Schiller device (Schiller Cardiovit AT-102 G2, Feldkirchen, Germany) at a speed of 25 mm/s and a voltage of 10 mm/mV. The frontal QRS–T angle, derived automatically from the standard 12-lead ECG, was calculated as the absolute difference between the axes of the QRS complex and T wave. To standardize measurement methods, the angle was calculated by subtracting 360 degrees when it exceeded 180 degrees.[15] QRS–T angles exceeding 90° were considered abnormal.[16] An example calculation for a QRS–T angle is provided in Figure 1 to illustrate the standardization process.

Demonstrating the calculation of the frontal QRS–T angle within the study.

Additionally, demographic data (age, gender), vital signs upon presentation, comorbidities, laboratory results, and Ranson scores were recorded for all patients.

2.4. Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software for Windows (Version 29, Chicago, IL). Patients were classified as having mild acute pancreatitis (MAP) if they had a Ranson score ≤ 2 and severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) if they had a Ranson score ≥ 3.[17] Descriptive statistics were used to depict the data, presenting counts and percentages for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation or median [IQR 25–75th] values for continuous variables. The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and histograms. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the Pearson chi-square test for categorical variables (or Fisher exact test when assumptions were violated) and Student t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables.

To identify independent predictors of severe acute pancreatitis, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted using variables with a P-value of < .2 in the univariate analysis. Highly correlated variables were excluded to prevent multicollinearity. The model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. And the explanatory power was assessed with Cox–Snell and Nagelkerke R² values. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 for all analyses.

3. Results

The study included 367 patients (94% mild pancreatitis [n = 345], 6% severe pancreatitis [n = 22]) (Fig. 2). The severe pancreatitis group had a significantly higher median age (66.5 [IQR 60.5–81] vs 58 [IQR 46.5–72], P < .001) and higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus (63.6%, 39.7% vs 39.7%, 25.3%, P = .027, P = .011, respectively) (Table 1). There were no statistically significant differences in gender distribution (P = .480), systolic or diastolic blood pressure (P = .953, P = .732), pulse rate (P = .083), oxygen saturation (P = .688), hypercholesterolemia (P = .13), or biliary etiology (P = .268). The severe pancreatitis group had significantly higher Ranson scores (3 [IQR 3–3.25] vs 1 [IQR 1–2], P < .001), longer hospital stays (6 [IQR 2.75–11.5] vs 3 [2–5] days, P = .029), and higher mortality rates (13.6% vs 3.2%, P = .044).

There was no significant difference in QRS angles (P = .226), though the rate of abnormal QRS angles was significantly higher in the severe pancreatitis group (31.8%, n = 7 vs 10.1%, n = 35, P = .002) (Table 3). The model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with a result of 0.933 indicating good fit. 4. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the utility of the QRS–T angle in distinguishing between MAP and SAP. In our study, we observed that the QRS–T angle was significantly higher in cases of severe pancreatitis. This finding suggests that the QRS–T angle can be considered a clinical tool for understanding the severity of acute pancreatitis.

AP is a swiftly developing inflammation of the abdomen and is globally the most frequent gastrointestinal reason for hospital admissions or mortality. While the majority of patients with MAP fully recover following conservative treatment, approximately 20% may progress to SAP.[18] This progression often leads to systemic inflammatory response syndrome and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, both of which are associated with a poor prognosis and a mortality rate as high as 30%.[19] Therefore, early identification of SAP and initiation of aggressive treatment are crucial to improving outcomes and reducing mortality rates.

The heart is susceptible to the effects of pancreatic inflammation. Research indicates that AP, irrespective of its intensity, is linked to cardiac damage, and the extent of this damage aligns with the clinical form of AP. MAP involves minor myocardial injury, which is temporary and tends to resolve spontaneously. In contrast, SAP often results in significant myocardial damage and dysfunction.[20]

The underlying mechanisms of myocardial dysfunction resulting from SAP remain unclear and are not completely understood. The underlying mechanisms of myocardial dysfunction resulting SAP remain unclear and are not completely understood. Pathophysiologically, cardiac injury in SAP is influenced by various risk factors such as gallstones, alcohol, diet, and drugs. These factors damage acinar cells, leading to the release of pancreatic enzymes and the excessive activation of inflammatory cells like macrophages and neutrophils. This triggers a cascade of inflammatory responses, amplifying local inflammation and causing widespread pancreatic necrosis and hemorrhage. The resultant cytokine storm and hypercytokinemia, along with intestinal barrier dysfunction, facilitate the migration of intestinal flora, escalating to pancreatic infection and sepsis. The systemic spread of inflammatory mediators including trypsin, endotoxins, and cytokines, harms vascular endothelial cells, disrupts myocardial microcirculation, impairs autonomic nerve function, and induces abnormal autophagy, culminating in significant myocardial injury and cardiac dysfunction.[20]

Our multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of the severity of acute pancreatitis, revealing that pulse rate, platelet count, and the presence of a normal QRS–T angle were independently associated with SAP. The positive correlation between elevated pulse rate and platelet count with SAP suggests that these parameters may be linked to the inflammatory response and hemodynamic changes. Notably, the presence of a normal QRS–T angle was inversely associated with SAP, indicating that an increased QRS–T angle could serve as a significant indicator of disease severity. In contrast, other variables such as hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and amylase levels were not found to be significant predictors of SAP. These findings support the potential role of the QRS–T angle as a valuable biomarker for the diagnosis and risk stratification of SAP, which could guide more targeted therapeutic interventions. However, further validation in larger and more diverse patient populations is required to confirm these associations.

The frontal QRS–T angle has been recognized as a significant ECG parameter not only in cardiac diseases but also in the evaluation of noncardiac conditions.[21] Studies have shown its relevance in assessing disease severity, as it reflects both myocardial and systemic alterations that could be present in diverse clinical settings.[22] For instance, elevated QRS–T angles have been associated with poor outcomes in both cardiac and certain noncardiac diseases, suggesting its potential utility as a marker for identifying high-risk patients. This study reinforces the role of the QRS–T angle in evaluating the severity of acute conditions, consistent with findings in previous literature.

ECG is widely recognized for its high utility in classifying patients according to their risk of various cardiac morbidities and overall mortality due to its accessibility, cost-effectiveness, noninvasive nature, and rapid execution and result delivery. In a standard ECG, depolarization is represented by the QRS complex, while repolarization is depicted by the ST segment and T wave. The angle between the QRS axis and T axis, known as the QRS–T angle, indicates the deviation in the direction of ventricular depolarization and repolarization.[23] Structural abnormalities affecting the depolarization phase and/or localized pathophysiological changes in ion channels impacting the repolarization phase can lead to alterations in the regional action potential duration profile of the ventricle and the spatial orientation of the repolarization sequence; this increased repolarization dispersion can be associated with cardiac depression.

In this study, we observed a higher incidence of abnormal QRS–T angles in cases of SAP compared to MAP. We believe this is indicative of changes in the heart’s electrical activity, which are associated with the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the development of AP. This suggests that the QRS–T angle may serve as a valuable indicator of the severity of systemic effects in AP and could potentially guide more targeted therapeutic interventions.

5. Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design limits our ability to establish causality between the severity of AP and changes in the QRS–T angle. Additionally, the sample size, while adequate, may not fully represent the diversity of acute pancreatitis cases encountered in broader clinical settings. Furthermore, the analysis did not account for all potential confounding variables, such as underlying cardiac conditions or the complete range of medications that could influence cardiac electrophysiology. Finally, the use of a single-center dataset may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or healthcare settings.

6. Conclusion

This study highlights the potential of the QRS–T angle as a useful indicator for assessing the severity of AP. The findings suggest that significant deviations in the QRS–T angle are associated with severe cases of AP, reflecting underlying pathophysiological changes that impact cardiac electrophysiology. These insights emphasize the importance of incorporating electrocardiographic monitoring into the management of acute pancreatitis, particularly for identifying patients at higher risk of severe complications.
