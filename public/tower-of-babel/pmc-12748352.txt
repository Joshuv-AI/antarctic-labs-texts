Single-center experience comparing glass and resin microspheres in lobar Y-90 SIRT for early- to intermediate-stage hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Xinlin Zheng, Huan Xi, G Matthijs Kater, Gijs C Bloemsma, Reinoud P H Bokkers, Aryan Mazuri, Joyce van Sluis, Gilles N Stormezand, Maarten W Nijkamp, Simeon J S Ruiter, Walter Noordzij
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelective internal radiation therapy (SIRT) with yttrium-90 (Y-90) microspheres is an established locoregional treatment for early- to intermediate-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). While both resin and glass microspheres are widely used, their comparative effectiveness and safety in lobar infusion remain unclear. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness and safety of resin versus glass microspheres in patients with early- to intermediate-stage HCC undergoing lobar Y-90 SIRT.ResultsA retrospective single-center analysis was conducted on 37 consecutive patients with early- to intermediate-stage HCC who underwent lobar Y-90 SIRT (resin: n = 22; glass: n = 15). Baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups, except for age (glass: 64 ± 6.9 years vs. resin: 69 ± 7.1 years; P = 0.036). Radiation doses to the tumour and non-tumoural liver were comparable between the resin and glass groups (all P > 0.05). Patients treated with resin microspheres showed significantly longer progression-free survival (PFS) (median 9.3 vs. 6.4 months; P = 0.043) and overall survival (OS) (median 16.4 vs. 12.0 months; P = 0.035), and also demonstrated higher overall response rates, compared with those treated with glass microspheres. Univariate Cox regression identified resin microsphere use as a significant predictor of improved PFS and OS, while higher Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage, multifocal disease, and tumour burden ≥ 25% were associated with worse survival (all P < 0.05). Fine–Gray competing risk regression, accounting for post-SIRT treatments as competing events, further confirmed the OS benefit of resin microspheres compared with glass microspheres (subdistribution hazard ratio [SHR] = 0.35; P = 0.011). Subgroup analysis demonstrated that the survival advantage of resin microspheres was particularly evident in patients with a maximum tumour diameter ≥ 5 cm (PFS: hazard ratio [HR] = 0.258, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.073–0.914; OS: HR = 0.111, 95% CI: 0.013–0.962). Safety outcomes were comparable between the two groups, with no significant differences in short- to long-term adverse events graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0, or in laboratory changes at 4-month follow-up (all P > 0.05).ConclusionsIn lobar Y-90 SIRT for early-to intermediate-stage HCC, resin microspheres were associated with improved outcomes without increased toxicity compared to glass microspheres, particularly in patients with large tumours. These findings support a potential benefit of resin versus glass microspheres in lobar SIRT and warrant prospective validation in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-025-01343-2.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), Selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT), Yttrium-90, Resin microspheres, Glass microspheres, Lobar infusion, Survival analysis, Safety

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver malignancy and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. For patients with early-to intermediate-stage HCC who are ineligible for curative treatment, locoregional therapies play a key role in prolonging survival and maintaining quality of life [2]. Among these, selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT) with yttrium-90 (Y-90) microspheres has emerged as an effective treatment that allows for delivery of beta-particle radiation to tumours while minimizing radiation exposure to non-tumoural liver tissue [3].

Currently, two Y-90 microspheres are commercially available for SIRT: resin microspheres (SIR-Spheres®) and glass microspheres (TheraSphere®) [4]. While both have demonstrated clinical efficacy, they differ significantly in physical properties, which may impact treatment outcomes [5]. Glass microspheres have 50-fold higher specific activity at calibration and 2.3-fold higher specific gravity compared to resin microspheres, requiring fewer microspheres per dose while exerting minimal embolic effects. In contrast, resin microspheres have lower specific activity, necessitating a higher number of microspheres per treatment to achieve uniform dose distribution and mild embolization [6]. These differences may affect intravascular transport, deposition patterns, and hepatic toxicity, particularly in lobar SIRT, which is designed to target multifocal tumours and cover a larger perfused area [7, 8]. Additionally, flow rates are usually higher with proximal injection positions in lobar SIRT, potentially further influencing microsphere distribution and therapeutic efficacy [4, 9].

Few studies have directly compared resin and glass microspheres in lobar infusion. Current clinical guidelines do not provide specific recommendations for one product over the other. Existing research predominantly focuses on segmental SIRT [6, 10] or has not stratified outcomes based on the SIRT infusion approach (segmental, lobar, or whole-liver) [11]. Additionally, these studies often involve heterogeneous patient populations [12, 13] and report inconsistent findings: some suggesting no significant differences [6], others indicating potential advantages of one microsphere type over the other [14]. Furthermore, most lobar SIRT studies focus on surrogate endpoints, such as contralateral liver hypertrophy [15–17], rather than safety and survival, and lack direct head-to-head comparisons between the two microspheres.

To address this gap, we conducted a single-center retrospective study comparing resin and glass microspheres in lobar SIRT for early-to intermediate-stage HCC. This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of effectiveness and safety, offering evidence to optimize microsphere selection and improve clinical treatment strategies.

Methods

Patient population

This present study retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent Y-90 SIRT at our institution between November 2016 and November 2023. All patients were discussed in a multidisciplinary tumour board meeting in which the decision and treatment plan for SIRT was made. Inclusion criteria were: (1) unilobar HCC diagnosed by biopsy or non-invasive imaging per European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) guidelines [18]; (2) received lobar SIRT treatment; (3) BCLC stage A or B (early-to intermediate-stage); (4) Child–Pugh class A or B; and (5) availability of CT/MRI imaging within 12 weeks before SIRT. Exclusion criteria included: (1) received non-lobar SIRT treatments (e.g., segmental or whole-liver infusion); (2) other concomitant antineoplastic therapies within 3 months prior to or after SIRT; (3) additional malignant disease(s) at the same time; and (4) incomplete follow-up data. Follow-up data were collected and censored on January 20, 2025. After the initial SIRT procedure, patients underwent scheduled follow-up including MRI and/or CT imaging, laboratory assessments, and adverse event monitoring at 1-month post-treatment and subsequently at 3- to 4-month intervals. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the University Medical Center Groningen and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived in accordance with the Dutch Act on Medical Scientific Research involving Human Beings (WMO).

Baseline data collection

Baseline data were retrospectively collected from medical records and imaging findings. Patient demographics (age and sex), laboratory parameters (serum albumin, serum bilirubin, liver enzymes, prothrombin time, INR ratio, creatinine, Alpha-fetoprotein [AFP] and Protein Induced by Vitamin K Absence or Antagonist II [PIVKA-II]), and clinical characteristics (subsequent treatments following diagnosis, aetiology of liver disease, ascites, portal hypertension, liver cirrhosis, and tumour features such as size, number, distribution, tumour burden, tumour volume) were documented. Tumour burden and volume were measured using MIM SurePlan (v7.3.4, MIM Software, Cleveland, USA), based on manual segmentation of baseline contrast-enhanced CT or MRI scans. Additionally, the Child–Pugh score, Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage, and World Health Organization (WHO) performance status were documented.

Treatment protocol

Patients underwent the two-stage approach as previously reported [19]. Briefly, after angiographic mapping of the upper abdominal vasculature, approximately 150 MBq of technetium-99m macroaggregated albumin (99mTc-MAA) was administered into the hepatic artery branch [20]. Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging was then performed to evaluate the relative distribution of microspheres in tumour and non-tumoural liver, as well as to assess lung shunting. Patients with a lung shunt fraction greater than 20% were deemed ineligible for treatment due to the risk of excessive radiation exposure to the lungs [21].

A second angiography with subsequent Y-90 microsphere administration was performed within two weeks. The choice between glass and resin microspheres was based on the product availability at our center, without any technical difference in delivery or patient follow-up between the two groups. Treatment activity was determined in accordance with international consensus guidelines [21]. The required Y-90 activity was calculated using recommended methods: resin microspheres (SIR-Spheres, Sirtex Medical Limited, North Sydney, Australia) were dosed based on the partition model [22], whereas glass microspheres (TheraSphere, BTG, London, UK) were dosed using the single-compartment dosimetry recommended by the manufacturer [23]. The target mean dose was set at 120 Gy for the target liver volume in the case of glass microspheres [22], and at least 120 Gy in the tumour for resin microspheres, while aiming for the highest possible dose within the range that preserves liver function and does not exceed the lung dose limit (30 Gy) [21, 24]. On the following day, Y-90 positron emission tomography (PET)/CT (Biograph mCT PET/CT, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) imaging was performed to confirm microsphere distribution, verify the absence of extrahepatic uptake, and assess tumour and non-tumoural liver doses (in Gy) using the local deposition model with MIM SurePlan (v7.3.4, MIM Software, Cleveland, USA). Dose–volume histograms (DVHs) of the tumour were then generated under this model, from which D70 (minimum dose to 70% of the tumour volume) and V120 (percentage of tumour volume receiving ≥ 120 Gy) were derived to evaluate tumour dose coverage [19, 25]. All 99mTc-MAA and Y-90 SIRT procedures were performed by experienced interventional radiologists and nuclear medicine physicians.

Effectiveness and safety

Effectiveness outcomes were defined as progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and tumour response. PFS was defined as the time from SIRT to the first occurrence of disease progression or death. Progression was determined on follow-up contrast-enhanced CT or MRI according to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (mRECIST) for intrahepatic lesions, while the appearance of new extrahepatic lesions (including lymph node or distant metastases) or new vascular invasion was also considered progression. OS was defined as the time between the initial SIRT treatment and death from any cause, or was censored at the date of the last follow-up. Tumour response to SIRT was evaluated at the 4-month follow-up using contrast-enhanced CT or MRI according to mRECIST [26], categorised as complete response (CR: disappearance of any intratumoural arterial enhancement), partial response (PR: ≥30% decrease in the sum of diameters of enhancing target lesions), progressive disease (PD: ≥20% increase or new lesions), and stable disease (SD: neither PR nor PD). The objective response rate (ORR) was defined as CR + PR, and the disease control rate (DCR) as CR + PR + SD. To further evaluate the overall tumour shrinkage effect after lobar SIRT, the change in the maximum diameter of the largest target lesion between baseline and the 4-month follow-up imaging was also measured.

Safety outcomes focused on treatment-related adverse events (AEs), including both clinical toxicities and laboratory abnormalities. AEs were defined as any unfavorable and unintended medical occurrences following SIRT, irrespective of a confirmed causal relationship. Only events considered related to treatment were systematically recorded and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 [27]. AEs were categorized by timing as short-term (< 1 month), intermediate-term (1–4 months), and long-term (> 4 months), and were systematically recorded during follow-up. Laboratory monitoring at 4 months post-SIRT specifically evaluated changes in complete blood count, albumin, bilirubin, liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], and gamma-glutamyl transferase [GGT]), prothrombin time (PT), and creatinine levels.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). The Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables, with Fisher’s exact test applied when more than 20% of expected values were below 5. Continuous variables were analyzed using the independent t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data.

Survival outcomes were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared via the log-rank test. To assess the effect of each variable on PFS and OS, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed. Cox models were fitted separately for each variable to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was assessed using the Wald test. Subgroup analyses of PFS and OS were conducted with Cox regression, and the results were visualized using forest plots.

Given the presence of potential competing risks, a Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model was used to analyze the effect of microsphere type on OS, considering post-SIRT treatments (e.g., liver transplantation, systemic therapy, or additional locoregional treatments) as competing events [28, 29]. Subdistribution hazard ratios (SHRs) with 95% CIs were computed. The statistical significance threshold was set at P < 0.05.

All statistical analysis was performed with STATA version 15.0 software (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA) and R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics

After exclusion, 37 HCC patients that underwent lobar SIRT with Y-90 were included: 15 were treated with glass microspheres, whereas 22 received resin microspheres (Fig. 1). The detailed baseline characteristics of the patients are shown in Table 1. Patients in the resin group were significantly older (69 ± 7.1 years) than those in the glass group (64 ± 6.9 years, P = 0.036). The majority of patients in both groups were classified as BCLC stage B: 12 (80.0%) in the glass group and 15 (68.2%) in the resin group (P = 0.481). Child–Pugh class A was the most frequent, observed in 13 (86.7%) glass group patients and 21 (95.5%) resin group patients (P = 1.000), followed by class B in 2 (13.3%) vs. 1 (4.5%), respectively. Baseline laboratory values, including white blood cell count, platelet count, liver function tests, creatinine, and INR, were comparable between the two groups (all P > 0.05, Table 2).

Effectiveness and safety

The median follow-up duration was 15 months for the entire cohort. Patients treated with resin microspheres exhibited significantly longer PFS (median 9.3 months [95% CI: 4.2–17.3] vs. 6.4 months [95% CI: 2.8–7.2], P = 0.043) and OS (median 16.4 months [95% CI: 8.8–NR] vs. 12.0 months [95% CI: 3.7 –15.1], P = 0.035) compared to those treated with glass microspheres (Fig. 2). Radiological tumour response at 4 months is detailed in Supplementary Table 1. The ORR was significantly higher in the resin group than in the glass group (64% vs. 27%, P = 0.045), while the DCR was comparable (68% vs. 53%, P = 0.493). Tumour necrosis at 4 months was observed in 12 of 15 patients (80.0%) in the glass-based group and 19 of 22 patients (86.4%) in the resin-based group (Supplementary Table 2). Changes in the maximum diameter of the largest target lesion are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. A modest median reduction was observed in patients achieving CR, whereas those with SD or PD showed minimal or no median change, and some even demonstrated an increase in lesion size. Representative patient cases illustrating treatment response with resin and glass microspheres are shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2.

Kaplan–Meier curves for survival in patients treated with resin vs. glass microspheres. (a) Progression-free survival; (b) Overall survival

The overall incidence of AEs was comparable between the resin and glass microsphere groups across all time points (short-term, intermediate-term, and long-term; P = 0.609, 0.070, and 0.417, respectively, Supplementary Table 4). In the short-term, the most frequently reported AEs included total bilirubin elevation, fatigue, and abdominal pain, all of which were controlled with supportive care and narcotic analgesics. In the intermediate- and long-term, ascites was the most common adverse event. Notably, during long-term follow-up, two patients in the glass microsphere group and one patient in the resin microsphere group developed grade 3 ascites, all of whom were hospitalized and received albumin supplementation, diuretic therapy, and underwent paracentesis for ascitic drainage. Additionally, esophageal varices haemorrhage occurred in two patients in the long-term (one in each group), both of whom received hemostatic treatment in time. Encephalopathy was observed in two patients in the intermediate-term (both in the glass group) and three patients in the long-term (one in the glass-based group and two in the resin-based group). Only one case of radiation pneumonitis was observed in the resin microsphere group in the long-term. Apart from the aforementioned cases of grade 3 ascites, all other observed AEs were grade 1 or 2 in severity. No treatment-related death occurred in each group.

At the 4-month follow-up, laboratory tests revealed no statistically significant differences between the two groups (Table 2). Changes in albumin, total bilirubin, liver enzymes (ALT, AST, GGT), PT, and creatinine levels were comparable between the resin and glass microsphere groups (all P > 0.05, Table 4).

Prognostic factors

As shown in Supplementary Table 5, univariate Cox regression analysis identified treatment with resin microspheres as a potential protective factor for both PFS (HR = 0.476, 95% CI: 0.231–0.979, P = 0.044) and OS (HR = 0.397, 95% CI: 0.166–0.952, P = 0.038), compared to glass microspheres. In contrast, several tumour-related characteristics were significantly associated with worse outcomes. Higher BCLC stage (stage B vs. A: PFS HR = 3.616, P = 0.006; OS HR = 4.227, P = 0.022), multifocal disease (PFS HR = 3.152, P = 0.020; OS HR = 4.050, P = 0.026), and higher liver tumour burden (≥ 25% vs. <25%: PFS HR = 2.940, P = 0.032; OS HR = 4.842, P = 0.008) were each associated with increased risk of disease progression and death. Other variables—including age, sex, cirrhosis, WHO performance status, Child–Pugh class, portal hypertension, and tumour diameter—were not significantly associated with either PFS or OS (all P > 0.05).

As illustrated in Fig. 3, Fine–Gray competing risk analysis—adjusting for post-SIRT treatments as competing events—suggested a lower cumulative incidence of death in patients treated with resin microspheres compared to glass. The subdistribution hazard ratio (SHR) for resin microsphere treatment was 0.35 (95% CI: 0.15–0.78, P = 0.011), indicating a potential survival benefit in this group.

Subgroup analyses indicated that patients with a maximum tumour diameter ≥ 5 cm may particularly benefit from resin microsphere treatment, with associations observed for improved PFS (HR = 0.258, 95% CI: 0.073–0.914; Fig. 4a) and OS (HR = 0.111, 95% CI: 0.013–0.962; Fig. 4b), compared to those treated with glass microspheres. A similar benefit in PFS was observed in patients without portal hypertension (HR = 0.184, 95% CI: 0.051–0.667; Fig. 4a). No significant differences were found in other subgroups, which may be attributed to limited sample size and statistical power.

Discussion

In the current study, patients treated with resin microspheres were associated with longer PFS and OS than those treated with glass microspheres (PFS: 9.3 vs. 6.4 months, P = 0.043; OS: 16.4 vs. 12.0 months, P = 0.035), as well as a higher ORR, while safety outcomes were comparable between groups. Additionally, treatment with glass microspheres, tumour burden ≥ 25%, multifocal disease, and higher BCLC stage were identified as adverse prognostic factors for PFS and OS. Subgroup analysis showed greater survival benefits of resin microspheres compared to glass microspheres in patients with a maximum tumour diameter ≥ 5 cm. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively compare the effectiveness and safety of glass and resin microspheres in lobar SIRT within a homogeneous patient group.

Previous studies have confirmed the efficacy of lobar SIRT in controlling ipsilateral tumour growth [16, 30, 31]. In terms of survival outcomes, our findings differ from some previous studies. Van Der Gucht et al. [5] reported no significant difference in PFS and OS between resin and glass microspheres. However, their treatment approach was inherently biased toward glass microspheres, as they were preferentially used in patients with smaller tumour volumes, which may compromise the comparability between the two microsphere types. Similarly, Kim et al. [32] found no significant difference in PFS and OS between the two microspheres, though treatment with resin microspheres showed a trend toward improved OS. Notably, their study indicated that resin microspheres may provide greater survival benefits in patients with higher tumour burden, particularly those with large, multifocal, or bilobar HCC, implying potential advantages in specific patient subgroups. Our subgroup analysis results align with this observation, demonstrating improved survival outcomes with resin microspheres in patients with a maximum tumour diameter ≥ 5 cm. However, no previous study analyzed lobar SIRT separately; instead, they combined data from lobar and segmental SIRT, potentially affecting the applicability of their conclusions. Given that segmental SIRT is typically employed in patients with earlier-stage disease, lower tumour burden, a high tumoricidal dose and resulting in longer survival, its inclusion may have obscured the distinct therapeutic effects of different microspheres in lobar SIRT.

In this study, resin-based lobar SIRT was associated with improved survival outcomes, which may be attributed to differences in microsphere distribution, tumour burden, and dose homogeneity [22]. Studies have shown that the therapeutic efficacy of SIRT depends on the distal penetration and distribution of injected microspheres, as incomplete dose coverage may lead to residual tumour progression [33]. Glass microspheres, due to their higher density, tend to accumulate in proximal vessels, which may result in suboptimal dose delivery to certain tumour regions. In contrast, resin microspheres, with lower density and greater fluid drag force, are more likely to distribute evenly within the tumour and achieve better distal dose coverage [33]. This characteristic is particularly critical in lobar SIRT, as patients received lobar SIRT often present with higher tumour burden (large or multifocal lesions) and are not suitable candidates for ablative radioembolization or segmentectomy [34]. Additionally, resin microspheres have lower specific activity, requiring the administration of a greater number of microspheres to achieve the recommended therapeutic dose, which may further enhance dose uniformity [35, 36]. Previous studies have demonstrated that increasing the number of microspheres improves dose homogeneity and enhances therapeutic outcomes [37]. In this study, although DVH indices (D70 and V120) did not differ significantly between groups, the resin group achieved a higher ORR (64% vs. 27%; P = 0.045). This discrepancy may be explained by the fact that our DVH metrics do not fully reflect the spatial characteristics of intratumoural dose distribution. Similar DVH parameters may arise from different deposition patterns—such as uniform versus clustered microsphere distributions—potentially leading to distinct treatment responses [38]. Romanò et al. also reported that voxel-based dosimetry failed to capture such heterogeneity due to limited imaging resolution and demonstrated limited predictive value for treatment response [39]. Further studies using advanced dosimetric approaches are warranted to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Although resin microspheres were associated with a survival advantage over glass microspheres in lobar SIRT, the safety profiles of both microsphere types were comparable. The incidence of AEs was similar between the two groups, consistent with findings reported by Villalobos et al. [6]. This may be attributed to strict adherence to manufacturer-recommended dosimetry, pre-treatment coil embolization of non-target arteries, and the careful selection of patients with a lung shunt threshold of < 20% and preserved liver function (mostly Child–Pugh A) in our cohort. The absorbed non-tumoural liver doses were similar between groups (glass: 41.0 Gy [IQR 28.5–50.0] vs. resin: 28.0 Gy [20.3–50.8]; P > 0.05), and both median doses were within the safety threshold of < 70 Gy for lobar infusion recommended by Lau et al. [5, 40]. Additionally, our study found no significant differences in liver function parameter changes (albumin, bilirubin, liver enzymes, etc.) between the two groups at the 4-month follow-up (all P > 0.05). These findings are consistent with the study by Braat et al. [41], suggesting that the theoretically greater embolic effect of resin microspheres, due to their lower specific activity, does not lead to additional liver toxicity. Nevertheless, as this was a retrospective study with a small sample size, these results should be interpreted with caution. Further prospective validation is warranted.

Dosimetry is a critical factor influencing SIRT outcomes [42]. The DOSISPHERE-01 trial [9] demonstrated that personalized dosimetry can improve clinical outcomes; however, most patients in our study were treated before the publication of these trial results. Even today, many centres continue to follow manufacturer-recommended dose calculation methods, which may limit dose individualisation and precision. Additionally, post-therapy Y-90 PET CT-based dosimetry has been recommended to verify the actual treatment dose distribution [43]. In our cohort, neither the median tumour absorbed dose (260 Gy vs. 173 Gy; P = 0.665) nor DVH-derived indices (D70 and V120; all P > 0.05) differed significantly between glass and resin microspheres. However, these indices were derived under the local deposition model, which computes voxel-wise dose assuming local energy deposition within each voxel. While this approach preserves voxel-level dose heterogeneity, it neglects β-particle cross-voxel energy spread and cannot resolve sub-voxel (microscopic) heterogeneity [44]. In the future, more advanced dosimetric approaches should be adopted to better characterise the spatial distribution and coverage of intra-tumoural dose [19, 45], enabling lesion-level analysis and a more precise assessment of the relationship between SIRT dose and outcome across different microsphere types.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design, single-center setting, and small sample size may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the extended study period may have introduced subtle changes in clinical practice, and due to limited access to both microsphere types in the earlier years, treatment allocation was occasionally based on availability rather than standardized clinical criteria. These factors may have introduced some bias into the results. Lastly, although potential prognostic factors were analyzed, the study was not sufficiently powered to perform multivariable adjustments due to the limited sample size. Future studies with larger cohorts and prospective designs are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusion

This study provides the first direct comparison of glass and resin microspheres in lobar SIRT for early-to intermediate-stage HCC. Resin microspheres were associated with improved outcomes without an increase in treatment-related toxicity, particularly in patients with large tumours. These findings support a potential benefit of resin versus glass microspheres in lobar SIRT, warranting validation in prospective, multicenter studies.
