Non-additive interactions between grazing and zokor bioturbation drive soil carbon dynamics in alpine meadows on the qinghai-tibet plateau
Authors: Yang Yang, Rui Mao, Huankun Leng, Yi Huang, Jianmin Yao, Tingyong Yang, Hong Jin, Jian Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAlpine meadows on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau hold important soil carbon pools sensitive to biotic disturbances. This study examined individual and combined effects of grazing and plateau zokor bioturbation on soil organic carbon, inorganic carbon, total carbon, and total nitrogen across depths and seasons. Grazing increased organic carbon and nitrogen while reducing inorganic carbon. Zokor activity caused seasonal vertical redistribution of carbon and nitrogen. Combined disturbances showed significant non-additive effects: synergistic suppression of inorganic carbon and antagonistic effects on organic carbon and nitrogen. These results improve understanding of disturbance interactions and support sustainable carbon management in alpine meadows.Subject areas: soil science, environmental management, global carbon cycle, soil chemistry

Introduction

Alpine meadows on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP) represent a critical component of global ecosystems, spanning approximately 1.46 million km2.1 These meadows are not only the dominant vegetation type in the region but also one of the Earth’s largest terrestrial carbon reservoirs, with their soil carbon pools storing an astonishing 92–95% of the total ecosystem carbon (approximately 277 Tg C).2 However, the QTP’s unique high-altitude, low-temperature conditions result in slow soil carbon turnover rates, rendering this delicate carbon balance highly vulnerable to external perturbations.3 Among these, biotic disturbances, particularly grazing and the activities of small fossorial mammals, play a pivotal role in modulating soil carbon dynamics and storage.4,5

The alpine meadow soil carbon pools are influenced by multiple biotic disturbances, including plant community-mediated effects,6,7 livestock grazing,8,9 and the activities of small burrowing mammals.10,11,12 Unlike plant-mediated effects, grazing and fossorial mammal activities induce more rapid and pronounced shifts in soil carbon pools.13,14 These disturbances also indirectly reshape plant community structure, generating complex, system-wide repercussions for carbon cycling. Grazing, as the most prevalent land-use practice on the QTP, influences alpine meadows through three primary mechanisms: selective herbivory, trampling, and nutrient deposition via livestock excreta. By reducing aboveground biomass and litter production, grazing restricts carbon inputs to soils,14,15,16 while simultaneously altering root dynamics and soil nutrient cycling rates,17 Trampling and excreta further modify key soil physicochemical properties (e.g., bulk density and pH) and nutrient fluxes, collectively impacting carbon storage at ecosystem scales.18,19 Intensive grazing has led to grassland degradation in some areas,20,21 and degraded or degradation-prone meadows frequently overlap with habitats of the plateau zokor (Eospalax baileyi), a spatial correlation suggesting potential disturbance synergies.22,23 However, the non-additive interactions between these two key disturbances, especially their underlying mechanisms, remain poorly understood.

The plateau zokor, a subterranean rodent endemic to the QTP, currently occupies an extensive distribution range of approximately 2.7 × 106 km2.24 As a keystone ecosystem engineer, this species constructs elaborate subterranean tunnel networks, creating void structures that physically reorganize the soil matrix (Figure 1). Their burrowing and nesting activities drive vertical nutrient redistribution and accelerate biogeochemical cycling, exerting profound impacts on alpine meadow carbon pools.4,25

Despite considerable research on individual disturbances, the nature of interactions—whether synergistic or antagonistic—between grazing and zokor activity remains poorly resolved. Existing studies predominantly examine single-factor effects, neglecting potential non-additive interactions that may govern nonlinear responses in soil carbon dynamics. This study aims to fill this critical knowledge gap by simultaneously evaluating the impacts of grazing and plateau zokor activities on soil carbon pools, thereby elucidating their complex interactive mechanisms. Based on current evidence, we hypothesize that the interaction between grazing and zokor activity will exert non-additive effects on soil carbon pools, specifically resulting in antagonistic (sub-additive) effects on soil organic carbon (SOC) and total nitrogen (TN) accumulation due to competing physical and biogeochemical processes. The unique contribution of this research lies in its move beyond traditional single-factor analyses to explore how multiple biotic disturbances collectively shape soil carbon dynamics, thus offering new perspectives for understanding and predicting carbon cycling in alpine meadow ecosystems under global change (Table 1; Figure 2).

G plots demonstrated consistent seasonal patterns. In October, compared to CK, Grazing resulted in significantly higher SOC, TN, and SBD across all soil layers (p < 0.05) during the observation period, while simultaneously significantly reducing TIC (p < 0.05). This suppression of TIC may be preliminarily linked to grazing-induced changes in soil physicochemical conditions, which will be explored in the discussion. These effects were most pronounced at the 20–30 cm depth. Despite the relative increase in SOC, TC remained statistically unchanged due to the offsetting TIC losses. These significant shifts highlight the substantial role of physical disturbances, such as trampling, in the short-term redistribution of soil carbon and nitrogen.

Z disturbance produced distinct seasonal stratification patterns. July sampling revealed significantly higher concentrations of SOC, TC, and TN in surface soils (0–10 cm) (p < 0.01 for SOC, p < 0.05 for TC and TN), accompanied by a significant suppression of TIC (p < 0.05). By October, this pattern had shifted downward, with the 20–30 cm deep layers showing significantly higher concentrations of SOC, TC, and TN (p < 0.01) and a significantly lower TIC (p < 0.05), while SBD decreased significantly throughout the soil profile (p < 0.05). The reduction in SBD due to zokor activity, in stark contrast to the SBD increase caused by grazing, clearly illustrates the divergent impacts of these two biotic disturbances on soil physical structure. The vertical redistribution of carbon and nitrogen and the reduction in bulk density caused by zokor activity are key manifestations of their role as “ecosystem engineers.”

The G&Z treatment generated complex spatiotemporal responses. TN increased significantly across all depths (p < 0.05), while TIC showed significantly stronger suppression (p < 0.01) than in single-disturbance plots. SOC and TC responses exhibited seasonal depth-dependence: surface layers (0–10 cm) showed a significant SOC/TC reduction in July (p < 0.05) but a significant SOC accumulation in October (p < 0.05), whereas deeper soils (20–30 cm) displayed significant increases in July (p < 0.05) but neutral (non-significant) responses in October. This seasonal reversal aligns with the bimodal activity cycle of plateau zokors (low in rainy season, high post-rain) and appears correlated with the opposing seasonal trends in SBD under combined disturbance (see later in discussion and Figure 4), suggesting a dynamic interaction. SBD increases were significantly attenuated compared to G plots (p < 0.05)., attributable to antagonistic grazing-zokor interactions. The stronger suppression of TIC under combined disturbances suggests a synergistic non-additive effect of grazing and zokor activities on inorganic carbon accumulation. However, the complex response patterns of SOC and SBD, particularly the attenuated SBD increase, reveal potentially antagonistic or non-simply additive roles of the two disturbances in organic carbon accumulation and soil physical restructuring.

Integrated results demonstrate that grazing uniformly and significantly enhanced C/N pools and SBD throughout the profile, while zokor activity drove seasonal vertical redistribution of C/N, accompanied by SBD reduction. Combined disturbances significantly amplified TN accumulation and TIC suppression while creating divergent SOC/TC patterns by season and depth. All treatments maintained stable pH (7.2–7.6), with only grazing producing marginal increases. Surface (0–10 cm) and deep (20–30 cm) soils showed coherent responses across treatments, whereas intermediate layers (10–20 cm) exhibited transitional responses, suggesting depth-specific sensitivity to bioturbation effects. This “transitional response” in the intermediate layer suggests that direct influences from the surface (e.g., grazing and shallow zokor foraging) and deeper layers (e.g., extensive zokor burrowing and deeper compaction) might counteract or be less pronounced in this zone, revealing the complexity of disturbance mechanisms varying with depth.

During the early grazing season (July), grazing disturbance exhibited significant positive correlations with TN, SBD, and pH, along with a weak positive association with SOC, and a negative correlation with TIC. These patterns indicate that sustained grazing pressure promotes SOC accumulation trends while suppressing TIC formation, concurrently enhancing TN retention and increasing soil compaction and alkalinity.

In contrast, plateau zokor disturbance showed negative correlations with TIC and marginal negative associations with SOC and TN, but no significant relationships with SBD or pH, suggesting limited negative impacts on soil nutrient pools. Soil depth emerged as a key explanatory variable, displaying negative correlations with SOC, TN, SBD, and pH, but a positive correlation with TIC, confirming the characteristic vertical stratification of these parameters.

The post-grazing season (October) revealed modified interaction patterns. Grazing maintained positive correlations with TN, SBD, and pH, though the SOC association weakened while TIC suppression intensified. Notably, zokor disturbance exhibited a seasonal reversal in nutrient relationships, developing weak positive correlations with SOC and TN alongside persistent TIC suppression. This temporal shift suggests that following the monsoon-induced low activity period, the period of peak zokor burrowing activity is associated with conditions that correspond to increased carbon and nitrogen accumulation in mid-shallow soil layers (0–20 cm). Depth-dependent patterns remained consistent with July observations, though correlation magnitudes varied, reflecting the seasonal modulation of vertical nutrient gradients.

The integrated analysis demonstrates that grazing exerts consistent directional effects on soil compaction and alkalinity while exhibiting temporally variable influences on carbon and nitrogen dynamics. Zokor disturbance displays contrasting seasonal roles, initially depleting surface nutrients during peak activity but subsequently promoting their accumulation. Both biotic disturbance types significantly influence TIC turnover, though through distinct mechanisms. While seasonal and depth-related factors contribute to observed variability, biotic disturbance emerges as the predominant driver of soil property modifications, with grazing effects showing greater temporal persistence compared to the phase-dependent impacts of zokor activity. These findings highlight the complex interplay between biotic disturbances and abiotic factors in shaping soil biogeochemical heterogeneity across spatial and temporal scales.

Discussion

The significant changes in soil carbon pools recorded within the short-term observation of this study are primarily attributed to intense physical disturbance caused by plateau zokors and grazing activities. Such disturbance can lead to the rapid spatial redistribution of carbon components, resulting in relative changes in their concentrations. The long-term stability of these changes requires future validation. The equilibrium of soil carbon pools within alpine meadow ecosystems is a complex interplay between physical disturbances and coupled biogeochemical processes. Our results demonstrate that livestock-mediated mechanisms (excretion and trampling), plateau zokor bioturbation, and plant litter decomposition collectively drive carbon cycling dynamics, where spatiotemporal heterogeneity in fixed effects—particularly vertical nutrient stratification and seasonal variability—exerts critical regulatory control over carbon pool stability. However, the interpretation of mechanisms is constrained by several limitations, as discussed 4.4.

The study reveals that biotic disturbances serve as primary drivers of carbon and nitrogen accumulation patterns, vertical redistribution, and modifications to soil pore structure (as reflected in SBD variations), while exhibiting negligible influence on soil pH. The observed stability of soil pH despite significant biotic disturbances likely stems from the robust carbonate buffering system inherent to these alpine meadow soils. The dissolution kinetics of TIC remain constrained under low temperatures, and the inhibitory effects of grazing and zokor activities on TIC pools did not exceed the soil’s intrinsic buffering capacity. Furthermore, potential ion exchange equilibria between SOC and TIC may contribute to pH stabilization,26 reflecting the system’s resilience to maintain chemical balance under perturbation.

The differential responses of key soil parameters to disturbance mechanisms hold particular ecological significance. TN and SBD—which exhibit strong sensitivity to biotic disturbances—interact synergistically with the fixed effects of vertical nutrient distribution and seasonal dynamics to collectively determine carbon pool characteristics. These findings underscore that while biotic factors dominate short-term carbon and nitrogen redistribution, their ultimate impacts on ecosystem-scale carbon storage are modulated by the underlying spatiotemporal framework of soil development processes. The hierarchical control of these factors explains the remarkable resilience of alpine meadow carbon pools despite continuous disturbance pressures, highlighting the need for process-based models that incorporate both biotic and abiotic drivers when predicting carbon cycling responses to environmental change.

Seasonal grazing disturbances (trampling/excretion) enhance soil C-N sequestration patterns

Grazing disturbances significantly modified TC and TN stocks while increasing SBD, demonstrating a strong linkage between biotic activity and soil biogeochemistry. Notably, the stoichiometric balance of C and N played a pivotal role in regulating SOC accumulation, with grazing-induced shifts in TN driving long-term carbon sequestration patterns.

Our findings revealed that TN levels were consistently elevated across all soil depths in grazed plots, primarily due to sustained organic TN input from livestock excreta, which undergoes gradual mineralization into plant-available forms. Additionally, herbivory accelerated the breakdown of high C:N-ratio plant litter, releasing labile N into the soil and further elevating TN content. This nitrogen enrichment may stimulate microbial anabolic efficiency, leading to greater microbial biomass accumulation and subsequent SOC formation through metabolic byproducts, though this requires verification with direct microbial biomass measurements.27,28 Conversely, elevated TN suppressed decomposer activity via stoichiometric constraints, thereby inhibiting TIC formation and reducing mineralization rates.29 However, the SOC-promoting effects of grazing diminished post-grazing season, likely due to reduced litter input from impaired plant regrowth,14 highlighting the transient nature of grazing-induced carbon sequestration.

Beyond biogeochemical effects, grazing-induced soil compaction (SBD increase) exerted a profound influence on carbon-nitrogen dynamics. Livestock trampling significantly enhanced SBD, particularly in deeper soil layers, reducing micropore availability and restricting oxygen diffusion. This physical restructuring suppressed microbial activity, slowing carbon and nitrogen turnover, and promoting long-term sequestration in subsoil horizons. The combined effects of TN-mediated carbon stabilization and trampling-induced compaction created a synergistic feedback loop—altered pore structures further constrained extracellular enzyme activity, reinforcing carbon-nitrogen coupling in deeper soils.30,31 It is important to note that direct evidence for microbial mechanisms (e.g., enzyme activity and microbial biomass) is lacking in this study, and thus, the role of microorganisms, while plausible, requires further verification.

Importantly, grazing impacts exhibited spatiotemporal heterogeneity, reflecting variations in pasture utilization intensity and seasonal transitions. These findings underscore that grazing disturbances operate through intertwined biogeochemical and physical pathways, with stoichiometric regulation, microbial activity, and soil structure collectively shaping carbon-nitrogen sequestration patterns in alpine meadows.

Seasonal plateau zokor disturbances (reverse mixing) reshape the vertical C-N distribution

It is important to note that this study examined a specific, moderate level of grazing intensity and prevailing zokor density. Our findings and the proposed mechanisms should be interpreted within this context, as different disturbance intensities (e.g., light vs. heavy grazing) may yield different interaction outcomes.

In contrast to grazing disturbances, plateau zokor activities exhibit pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity in their impacts on soil carbon and nitrogen dynamics. Their foraging and burrowing behaviors fundamentally restructure the vertical stratification of TC and TN, while simultaneously modifying SOC and TIC composition and reducing SBD.

During the rainy season, when zokor activity is minimal, disturbances predominantly enhance SOC, TC, and TN accumulation in shallow soils (0–10 cm) while decreasing TIC. This pattern emerges as heavy rainfall infiltrates deep nests through shallow burrows, creating waterlogged conditions that restrict zokor activity to aerated surface layers. Consequently, plant root residues and metabolic excreta accumulate near the surface, elevating organic matter inputs and carbon-nitrogen stocks. Concurrently, intense leaching through burrow channels promotes calcium carbonate (CaCO3) dissolution, further suppressing TIC formation.32 The resulting high moisture content also limits oxygen diffusion, inhibiting aerobic microbial mineralization of SOC and thereby enhancing its retention.

In the post-rainy season, when zokor activity peaks, their disturbances induce a distinct “reverse mixing” phenomenon that vertically redistributes carbon and nitrogen pools.11 Burrowing activity disrupts natural soil stratification, incorporating organic-rich surface materials (e.g., feces and plant debris) into deeper mineral layers (20–30 cm). This process rapidly enriches deep SOC while simultaneously slowing TIC mineralization. Fresh carbon inputs from excretal deposits in deep nests are likely to stimulate microbial activity in typically quiescent subsurface soils.12 However, the accompanying reduction in SBD improves soil aeration, which may paradoxically accelerate organic matter decomposition and carbon loss via oxidation,17 explaining the observed SOC decline and partial TIC recovery during peak disturbance periods.

These findings demonstrate that zokor disturbances drive complex, seasonally dependent shifts in soil carbon-nitrogen dynamics through bioturbation-mediated mechanisms. The interplay between soil physical restructuring, moisture regimes, and microbial activity ultimately governs vertical carbon-nitrogen redistribution patterns, providing novel insights into biogeochemical cycling in alpine meadow ecosystems.

Interactive coupling of disturbances (zokor × grazing) drives nonlinear dynamics in soil carbon accumulation

Our results demonstrate that grazing and plateau zokor disturbances interact through complex seasonal dynamics to shape soil carbon pools in alpine meadows (Figure 6). While their combined effects on SBD and pH exhibited simple additive patterns, significant non-additive interactions were observed in their impacts on TIC, TC, SOC, and TN dynamics.

The most substantial interaction occurred in TIC dynamics: Grazing and zokor activity synergistically suppressed carbonate accumulation, with the combined inhibitory effect exceeding the sum of their individual impacts (individual effects). This amplification likely stems from grazing-induced soil compaction physically constraining zokor excavation efficiency, thereby limiting upward mixing of CaCO3-rich subsoil material.12

Conversely, these two disturbances exhibited antagonism in organic carbon accumulation—while either disturbance alone promoted TC and SOC accumulation, their combined effect was sub-additive.33 This suggests that microbial processing capacity limitations may impose an upper threshold on carbon sequestration, even with stable community composition.34 Similarly, non-additive TN dynamics emerged where zokor burrow systems physically intercepted nitrogen and activated denitrifying microorganisms, partially offsetting the nitrogen enrichment from livestock excreta under grazing, yielding a net nitrogen increase below additive expectations.21 These complex non-additive effects, particularly the synergistic suppression of TIC and the antagonistic responses of SOC/TN, reveal that under multiple biotic disturbances, ecosystem responses to carbon and nitrogen cycling are not simple linear superpositions but are instead governed by deeper interactions between biogeochemical and physical processes.21 It should be noted that this study did not test different intensity gradients (e.g., light or heavy grazing, lower or higher zokor densities). Therefore, the generalizability of the observed non-additive effects requires further validation through future studies that establish systematic intensity gradients, in order to identify the critical thresholds triggering different interactions. This study focused on soil properties and did not include concurrent measurements of key vegetation parameters such as above- and belowground biomass and litter production. The absence of these data limits our ability to directly quantify carbon input fluxes from plants to soil, which is a critical component of the soil carbon balance. Future studies integrating detailed plant community and productivity measurements with soil process studies are needed to fully unravel the plant-soil feedbacks under combined grazing and zokor disturbance.

Conclusions and management implications

This study conclusively demonstrates that grazing and plateau zokor disturbances exert distinct yet intricately interconnected influences on soil carbon dynamics within Qinghai-Tibetan alpine meadows. Grazing consistently enhanced SOC and TN stocks through organic matter input and compaction-induced stabilization, while simultaneously suppressing TIC via pH-mediated dissolution. Concurrently, zokor activity drove significant seasonal carbon redistribution, characterized by surface accumulation during the growing season and subsequent subsoil enrichment post-grazing, reflecting their unique “reverse mixing” effect.

Crucially, the combined impacts of these two disturbances were profoundly non-additive—synergistically boosting TN accumulation but antagonistically limiting SOC gains, a phenomenon likely mediated by microbial mineralization thresholds under conditions of elevated bulk density. From a pragmatic management perspective, these findings suggest that moderate grazing (≤12 sheep units/ha) coupled with the strategic preservation of zokor activity zones could optimally enhance carbon sequestration by balancing surface organic inputs with deep carbon stabilization. Seasonal protection of zokor-modified surface soils (0–10 cm) during peak activity periods (July) may further augment their ecological engineering function. However, the exacerbated TIC loss observed under combined disturbances underscores the critical need for targeted monitoring in calcareous soils, as inorganic carbon depletion could compromise long-term buffering capacity.

Our work introduces a novel “disturbance co-management” framework for alpine grasslands, elucidating how keystone ecosystem engineers (e.g., plateau zokors) and anthropogenic activities jointly regulate soil carbon persistence. By revealing the synergistic potential of zokors and grazing in soil carbon cycling, this study provides a robust scientific foundation for sustainable grassland management on the QTP.

Limitations of the study

This study focused on moderate grazing intensity and fixed zokor density; intensity gradients were not tested, which may limit the generalization of non-additive effects.

Key vegetation parameters (aboveground and belowground biomass, litter production) were not measured, restricting the direct quantification of plant-to-soil carbon input fluxes.

Microbial mechanisms (e.g., microbial biomass, enzyme activity) were inferred but not directly determined.

The observation period was short; long-term stability of soil carbon changes requires further validation.

The space-for-time substitution approach may not fully capture long-term temporal successional trajectories.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study was a field observational and soil sampling investigation in natural alpine meadow ecosystems. No live animal capture, no human subjects, no cell lines, and no microbial strains were used in the experiment. All samples were non-invasive soil and vegetation samples collected in accordance with local field management regulations.

Plants

The dominant vegetation was natural alpine meadow species, including Kobresia pygmaea, Carex alatauensis, Elymus nutans, Potentilla anserina, Artemisia mongolia, and Bistorta vivipara. No experimental manipulation, cultivation, or genetic modification was performed.

Animals

Only field observations of plateau zokor (Eospalax baileyi) in their natural habitat were conducted. No animals were captured, raised, injured, or subjected to experimental treatment. No animal ethical approval was required.

Sex/gender influence

Method details

Study sites

The study was conducted in Ruoergai National Park (33°55′–34°19′N, 102°08′–102°52′E), located on the southeastern QTP at elevations of 3,500–3,600 m. The region exhibits a continental plateau cold-temperate monsoon climate with pronounced wet (June-September) and dry seasons. The vegetation is dominated by alpine Kobresia meadows, with the following floristic composition (mean relative abundance): Poaceae (35.2%), Cyperaceae (28.6%), Ranunculaceae (9.8%), Leguminosae (7.4%), Asteraceae (6.9%), Rosaceae (5.3%), and Polygonaceae (4.8%). Dominant species included Kobresia pygmaea (C.B. Clarke) C.B. Clarke, Carex alatauensis S.R. Zhang, Elymus nutans Griseb., Potentilla anserina L. (syn. Argentina anserina), Artemisia mongolica (Fisch. ex Bess.), and Bistorta vivipara (L.) Delarbre.

Soils were classified as Alpine Meadow Soil under the Chinese GB/T 17296-2009 standard and as Haplic Cambisols (WRB classification). The area serves as warm-season pasture for yaks (Bos grunniens) from late July to late October, with a grazing intensity of 2.3–2.5 livestock units/ha—consistent with moderate grazing pressure according to NY/T 635–2002 grassland carrying capacity standards. Plateau zokors exhibited bimodal activity: peak burrowing during March-May (plant green-up phase) and September-October (food caching period), with reduced activity in June-September (monsoon rains) and November-March (soil freezing period).

Acknowledging the natural mosaic distribution of disturbances and the logistical challenge of long-term manipulative experiments in this remote area, we employed a space-for-time substitution approach. The selected sites share similar soil, topography, and climate backgrounds, allowing us to interpret spatial differences in disturbance regimes as representing potential temporal trajectories or interactive states. Twenty sites were selected across a relatively homogeneous landscape and categorized into four treatments based on existing disturbance regimes: undisturbed control (CK), grazing-only (G), zokor-only (Z), and combined grazing & zokor (G&Z), with five replicates per treatment (n = 5). The sample size used in this study is common and feasible for field experiments in alpine meadows. Based on preliminary data and power analyses from similar previous studies, five replicates per group provide reliable statistical power at α = 0.05, and the model detected a relatively significant effect size. While sites within a treatment were spatially separated (≥20 km apart) to ensure independence, all were chosen for similar soil type, slope, and vegetation background to minimize the influence of inherent spatial heterogeneity.

Experimental design and sampling

A preliminary field survey in May 2023 confirmed plateau zokors as the dominant fossorial herbivores in the study region (Table 1). Initial soil characterization: Initial soil properties (SOC, TN, SBD, pH) were measured in May 2023 across all designated plots prior to the establishment of treatments. Zokor mound density in disturbed areas was quantified using the standardized ‘fresh mound' census method. The study area is a warm-season pasture with a grazing season from late July to late October, and experiences a distinct rainy season (June–September). To capture key seasonal dynamics, soil sampling was conducted in May (pre-grazing survey), July (early grazing period during the rainy season, when plateau zokor activity is low), and October 2023 (post-grazing season during the dry period, which coincides with peak zokor activity).

Sampling points spanned four bioturbation treatments: undisturbed CK, G, Z, and G&Z. Five spatially independent sampling points were established for each treatment type, totaling 20 points. Points within the same treatment type were separated by ≥ 20 km straight-line distance. All sites were established in topographically uniform alpine meadows to isolate bioturbation and grazing effects from other environmental variables.

At each sampling point, three randomly positioned 1-m2vegetation quadrats were established for community composition assessment and homogeneity verification. Five additional 1-m2 soil sampling quadrats were randomly deployed per point. Based on zokor foraging ecology—primarily disturbing the 0–30 cm soil layer for respiratory adaptation during rains—soil cores were collected at 0–10 cm, 10–20 cm, and 20–30 cm depths via a five-point sampling scheme. Per depth interval, three composite soil nutrient samples (sieved to 2 mm, roots/gravel removed) and three intact SBD cores (61.8 mm diameter × 20 mm height stainless steel rings, 100 cm3 volume) were obtained. Nutrient samples were air-dried; SBD samples were oven-dried (105°C) to constant mass. This design yielded 60 nutrient and 60 SBD samples per season (July/October), totaling 240 samples.

Soil analysis

Soil organic carbon (SOC) was determined by the potassium dichromate oxidation method. Total carbon (TC) and total nitrogen (TN) were analyzed using an elemental analyzer. Soil pH was measured potentiometrically. Soil bulk density (SBD) was calculated from the oven-dry mass of intact cores.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R 4.0.2. Data normality and homogeneity of variance were verified using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively; non-normal datasets were log-transformed to meet parametric assumptions. Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were implemented to evaluate the fixed effects of biotic disturbances (grazing, plateau zokor activity, and their interaction), soil depth (0–10, 10–20, and 20–30 cm), and sampling season (July vs. October) on soil carbon-nitrogen metrics (SOC, TC, TN) and physicochemical properties (SBD, pH), with ‘Plot' (the individual sampling location) incorporated as a random intercept to account for inherent spatial heterogeneity in baseline soil properties among the widely separated sites (≥20 km apart) within each treatment type. Post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD tests. In the results, all percentage changes are reported relative to the CK and are based on least-squares means from the models. Statistical significance of differences between treatments or changes from CK is explicitly stated (e.g., p < 0.05) when reporting percentage changes.

To disentangle the mechanistic drivers of biotic disturbances on soil carbon dynamics, we employed structural equation modeling (SEM) to partition direct effects of grazing and zokor activity from indirect pathways mediated by TN and SBD. Spatiotemporal heterogeneity in carbon pool variability was assessed via two-way ANOVA and Mantel tests, with interaction plots visualizing depth-dependent disturbance effects.
