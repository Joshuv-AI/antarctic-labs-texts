Natural Variation in the Promoter of OsMOT1;1 Contributes to Differential Molybdenum Accumulation between Indica and Japonica Rice
Authors: Pengyuan Guo, Lingyue Zhang, Qingqing Du, Lei Huang, Fangchi Wei, Zongyue Jiang, Zihan Zhao, Mengfan Liu, Kangshun Huang, Fugang Huang, Weiwei Gao, Xingming Lian, Meng Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding genetic differences in nutrient accumulation between indica and japonica subspecies is crucial for utilizing beneficial alleles to enhance genetic improvement. Molybdenum (Mo) is an essential micronutrient for rice growth and development, playing indispensable roles in physiological processes. However, differences in Mo accumulation between indica and japonica have rarely been reported. In this study, field and hydroponic experiments conducted on 529 rice germplasm accessions revealed significant natural divergence in Mo accumulation between indica and japonica, with japonica generally accumulating higher Mo levels than indica. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and quantitative trait locus (QTL) analysis revealed that the divergence in Mo accumulation in both subpopulations was primarily driven by the Mo transporter OsMOT1;1. However, all coding region haplotypes of OsMOT1;1 from both indica and japonica displayed comparable Mo transport capability. By contrast, significant differences in OsMOT1;1 expression levels were observed between high-Mo and low-Mo varieties within each subpopulations. Moreover, the overall expression level of OsMOT1;1 was significantly higher in japonica than in indica. Furthermore, we identified several extremely high-Mo rice varieties, whose high Mo levels largely resulted from high expression levels of OsMOT1;1, thus showing great potential for improving Mo efficiency in breeding. These results suggest that natural variation in Mo accumulation between indica and japonica rice is primarily caused by differential expression of OsMOT1;1, highlighting its potential as a target for improving Mo efficiency in rice breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-025-00876-3.

Introduction

It has long been known that Molybdenum (Mo) is an essential micronutrient for almost all living organisms, including plants. As a component of plant metabolic catalyst centers, Mo plays an irreplaceable role in a number of physiological and biochemical processes in plants. Mo itself is not biologically active; only after it is converted into coenzymes or molybdenum cofactors can it exert physiological and biochemical effects (Mendel 2007; Hansch et al. 2009). In plants, at least five types of Mo-containing enzymes had been identified, including nitrate reductase, sulfite oxidase, xanthine dehydrogenase, aldehyde oxidase, and mitochondrial amidoxime reductase (Rana et al. 2020; Schwarz et al. 2009). These enzymes play essential roles in nitrogen metabolism, sulfite detoxification, stress response, abscisic acid biosynthesis and detoxification, respectively.

Plants take up Mo mainly as the molybdate anion from soil. A large number of investigations have shown that the bioavailability of Mo in soil is largely dependent on soil pH; in alkaline soils, Mo becomes more soluble, while in acidic soils, the high levels of soil oxides have a strong adsorption of molybdate, which greatly decreases its bioavailability (Wichard et al. 2009). Although the demand for Mo by plants is very small, Mo deficiency is commonly found in many plant species, especially in acidic soils. For Cruciferae, legumes, and other Mo-sensitive crops, Mo deficiency is a universal abiotic stress factor worldwide and can cause weak growth, leaf chlorosis, reduced pollen fertility, and decreased production (Rana et al. 2020). Gramineous crops are also prone to symptoms of molybdenum deficiency in acid soils, and Mo application can effectively increase yield (Yu et al. 2002). Furthermore, it is estimated that more than 50% of the world’s arable land is acidic, and the acidification of farmland soil is significantly increasing annually (Guo et al. 2010; Dai et al. 2017), making Mo deficiency in crops an increasingly serious agricultural problem.

The absorption of Mo by living organisms is an active transport process that requires the participation of Mo transporters. To date, several types of Mo transporters have been identified in different organisms. In Escherichia coli, the transport of Mo is mediated by a high-affinity ABC-type transport system composed of modABC genes (Grunden et al. 1997). Interestingly, the ABC-type Mo transporter has not been identified in eukaryotes yet; in contrast, two other types of Mo transporters have been found to be responsible for Mo accumulations in eukaryotes. The first type Mo transporter is the MOT1 family, the members of which are present in higher plants, algae, fungi, and bacteria (Tejada-Jimenez et al. 2007; Huang et al. 2022). The earliest identified members of MOT1 were CrMOT1 from C. reinhardtii and AtMOT1;1 (also known as AtMOT1) from Arabidopsis, both of which showed high-affinity molybdenum transport activity (Tomatsu et al. 2007; Tejada-Jimenez et al. 2007). CrMOT1 was the only MOT1 member of C. reinhardtii, and its knockdown could significantly inhibit the activities of Mo transport as well as the Mo-containing enzyme nitrate reductase. AtMOT1;1 was one of the two MOT1 members in Arabidopsis, and its knockout resulted in a greatly reduced Mo accumulation in both roots and shoots. AtMOT1;2, the other MOT1 member of Arabidopsis, also showed Mo transport ability and is involved in inter-organ Mo translocation (Gasber et al. 2011). LjMOT1 was identified from Lotus japonicus, which showed a high-affinity Mo transport activity and played an essential role for Mo uptake from the soil (Duan et al. 2017). MtMOT1.2 and MtMOT1.3, isolated from Medicago truncatula, were responsible for Mo supply to root nodules and for introducing Mo into nodule cells, respectively (Gil-Diez et al. 2019; Tejada-Jimenez et al. 2017). OsMOT1.2 contributed to Mo remobilization in Rice (Hu et al. 2022). Compared with the members of MOT1 that are mainly identified in plants, the second type Mo transporter MOT2 showed no sequence similarity to MOT1 members and was likely presented in both plants and animals (Tejada-Jimenez et al. 2013). The MOT2 members from C. reinhardtii and humans, i.e. CrMOT2 and HsMOT2, both showed Mo transport activity and contributed to Mo uptake from growth medium (Tejada-Jimenez et al. 2011). Additionally, a novel Mo transporter, OsDISMO1, has recently been reported to be responsible for Mo distribution in shoots (Kandwal et al. 2025).

Plants have evolved mechanisms that led to natural variation of essential nutrients including Mo, allowing them to adapt to different environmental conditions. In Arabidopsis, Mo variations have been shown to be mainly caused by expressional variations of AtMOT1;1, where a 53 bp deletion in the promoter region of a few accessions caused a decrease in its expression level and Mo accumulation (Baxter et al. 2008). OsMOT1;1, the homolog of AtMOT1;1 in rice, was also demonstrated to control the Mo variation by variations of expressional levels in the subpopulation of indica, although the exact causal sites remain unknown (Huang et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2018). However, the genetic mechanisms underlying Mo accumulation in japonica rice and the differences between indica and japonica have never been fully elucidated.

In this study, we comprehensively investigated the variation in Mo accumulation across different subspecies and subgroups, confirming that japonica varieties generally accumulate much higher levels of Mo in shoots and grains than indica varieties. Moreover, we demonstrated that OsMOT1;1 was also the major causal gene controlling Mo variations in the japonica subspecies, based on QTL mapping using two japonica varieties with extreme differences in Mo accumulation. We propose that OsMOT1;1 contributes to abundant variation of Mo concentration in natural populations through variations in expression levels, which also results in the large diversity of Mo accumulation across different subspecies and subgroups.

Results

Japonica Generally Accumulated much Higher Mo Concentrations than Indica

We conducted an overall comparative analysis of the Mo concentrations across different variety groups based on the data of five field trials of 529 diverse rice accessions generated previously (Yang et al. 2018). Of these 529 accessions, 295 and 156 belonged to indica and japonica types respectively. The indica group can be further divided into 98 accessions of IndI (mainly collected from southern China), 105 accessions of IndII (mainly from Southeast Asia) and 92 Ind-inter (intermediate type of IndI and IndII), based on the sequence polymorphisms (Xie et al. 2015; Zhao et al. 2015). Similarly, the japonica group can be further divided into 91 TeJ (temperate japonica), 44 TrJ (tropical japonica) and 21 Jap-inter (intermediate type of TeJ and TrJ). In all five field trials, regardless of straws or grains, the average Mo concentrations of the japonica group as well as the subgroups were generally higher than those of the indica groups (Fig. 1). To more strictly control the nutrient conditions, we further investigated the Mo concentrations in shoots of this natural population at vegetative growth period through a fully nutrient hydroponic experiment. The shoot Mo concentrations of the total 529 accessions showed large variations, with a range from 5.47 to 34.91 mg kg− 1 dry weight (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, the Mo concentrations in most accessions (523/529) were lower than 16 mg kg− 1 dry weight, with only a few varieties having extremely high Mo concentrations. Consistent with results at the mature period, the shoot Mo concentrations of japonica accessions at vegetative growth period were also generally higher than those of indica (Fig. 2B). We then used the shoot Mo concentrations to perform GWAS of the indica and japonica subpopulations separately (Fig. 2C). In indica group, the location of OsMOT1;1 was the only but quite strong association site with the Mo variations, indicating that OsMOT1;1 has a major contribution to the Mo variation in this subpopulation. QTL Mapping Revealed that OsMOT1;1 Played a Major Role in the Huge Variation of Mo Accumulations within Japonica

Notably, the top six varieties with extremely high Mo concentrations were all from the TrJ subgroup (Fig. 3A). To further investigate the molecular mechanism controlling Mo variations within the japonica subpopulation, we selected the variety with the highest Mo accumulation (i.e. W102, a tropical japonica rice variety named Padi_Tarab_Arab) and another variety (W022, a tropical japonica rice variety named) with almost the lowest Mo concentration in japonica to construct a F2 genetic population. The two parents W102 and W022 also presented large difference in Mo accumulations at the maturity stage when grown under field conditions, with about 6.1 times of difference in straw and about 9.8 times in brown rice (Fig. 3B, C). This indicated that the two parents had stable genetic differences in Mo accumulation. We then planted the F2 population at the experimental field in Wuhan and investigated the Mo concentrations in straw. The Mo concentrations in the F2 population varied from 0.22 to 5.59 mg kg− 1 dry weight (Fig. 3D). In particular, the frequency distribution was obviously bimodal, with a distinct gap from 1.25 mg kg− 1 dry weight to 1.43 mg kg− 1 dry weight, where no F2 individuals were observed. Moreover, of the total 219 F2 individual plants, 56 had lower Mo concentrations than the interval, and 163 accumulated higher Mo concentrations. That meant a ratio of about 1:2.91 in individual numbers between the low Mo and the high Mo groups in the F2 population, which was approximately consistent with the Mendelian single gene controlled trait segregation ratio (i.e. 1:3). To statistically test this segregation, a chi-square (χ²) goodness-of-fit test was performed. The test yielded a non-significant χ² value of 0.014 (P = 0.91), indicating that the observed segregation did not significantly differ from the expected 1:3 ratio. These results supported that the huge difference in Mo concentration between the two parents W102 and W022 was mainly controlled by a single gene.

Given the significant impact of OsMOT1;1 on variation of Mo accumulation within the indica subpopulation, we preferentially performed QTL analysis on the chromosome 8, where OsMOT1;1 is situated. The 219 F2 individual plants were genotyped by 19 molecular markers which evenly distributed on the chromosome, and the genotypes were then integrated with the straw Mo concentrations for QTL mapping by using QTL-IciMapping. An extremely significant QTL was then detected in the interval of 0–0.22 Mb on the chromosome 8, with an LOD value of 49.2 and a phenotypic contribution rate of 70.1% (Fig. 3E). Specifically, the median Mo concentration in individual plants within the QTL region, which were homozygous and consistent with the genotype of high-Mo parent W102, was 6.6 times that of individual plants consistent with the genotype of low-value parent W022, and the heterozygous individuals were intermediate between the two homozygous genotypes (Fig. 3F), indicating a semi-dominant inheritance pattern. This QTL region contained 28 annotated genes, including OsMOT1;1 (LOC_Os08g01120) (Table S3). To confirm whether OsMOT1;1 was the causal gene underlying this QTL, we investigated the expression level of OsMOT1;1 between the two parents of this population, and the results showed that the high Mo parent W102 presented 8.2 times higher transcription level of OsMOT1;1 than the low Mo parent W022 (Fig. 3G). OsMOT1;1 Contributed To Mo Accumulations Under Mo-limited Conditions

The role of OsMOT1;1 in rice Mo acquisition had been studied in a knockdown mutant of OsMOT1;1 caused by a T-DNA insertion in the promoter region (Huang et al. 2019). As this mutant still retained a certain degree of expression level, it was difficult to confirm the exact role of OsMOT1;1 played in Mo uptake for rice plants. We therefore used CRISPR-Cas9 technology to create four independent knockout lines, each with a deletion that caused frameshift of the coding sequence (Fig. 4A). The Mo accumulations in knockout and wild-type plants hydroponically grown- at different Mo supplies (1 nM, 10 nM, 100 nM, 1 µM, and 10 µM) were then investigated. The results showed that the difference in Mo accumulation between mutant and wild-type plants depended on concentrations of Mo supply. When plants grew under extremely low Mo condition (1 nM), the Mo accumulations in mutant plants were significantly lower, by 43.4–66.6% in roots and by 47.8–62.7% in shoots, compared to the wild-type plants (Fig. 4B, C). When the external supply of Mo increased to 10 nM or 100 nM, the Mo accumulations in shoots of mutants were still significantly lower than that of wild-type, which decreased by 65.7–71.7% and 38.6–53.2% respectively; however, the Mo concentrations in roots showed no significant difference between the mutant and the wild type plants under the two Mo supplies (Fig. 4D–G). When the external Mo supply was increased to as high as 1 µM and 10 µM, no significant difference was found in Mo concentrations of both roots and shoots between the mutant and wild-type plants (Fig. 4H–K). Natural Variations in Coding Sequence of OsMOT1;1 Did not Affect the Mo Accumulations

Based on SNP/INDEL data for OsMOT1;1 from RiceVarMap (http://ricevarmap.ncpgr.cn/) and subsequent sequencing verification, the coding sequence of OsMOT1;1 in natural population possessed at least five different haplotypes (Type1–Type5). Among these, Type1 and Type3 are predominantly found in the japonica group, Type4 and Type5 are mainly present in the indica group, and Type2 is shared by both groups (Fig. 5A). We had previously constructed overexpression lines for four of the five haplotypes (excluding Type 3, whose function could be inferred from the differences between Type 2 and Type 4). Our previous work showed that the two indica haplotypes (Type 4 and Type 5) have similar efficiency in Mo accumulation (Yang et al. 2018). However, it remained unclear whether the efficiency of haplotypes differed within the japonica subspecies, or between the indica and japonica subspecies. Here, four new overexpression lines different from the previously used for each haplotype were selected to investigate their Mo accumulations under different Mo supplies. As is typical for transgenic plants, these lines exhibited a gradient of OsMOT1;1 expression levels due to position effects and T-DNA copy number variation, though the overall expression range was generally comparable across genotypes (Fig. 5B). When plants grew at the low Mo supply (10 nM), all overexpression lines from different genotypes accumulated 4.7–14.4 times and 4.7–15.3 times of Mo in roots and shoots respectively, compared to the wild type (Fig. 5C, D). At the medium Mo supply (100 nM), the Mo concentrations of all overexpression lines in roots and shoots were 1.7–3.3 times and 4.3–9.5 times that of wild type, respectively (Fig. 5E, F). When the Mo supply increased to 1 µM, the Mo concentrations of overexpression lines were 1.7–3.3 times and 4.3–9.5 times that of wild type in roots and shoots respectively (Fig. 5G, H). Furthermore, the overexpression lines with similar OsMOT1;1 expression levels from different haplotypes generally showed comparable Mo accumulations under all three Mo-supplied conditions. We therefore conducted an analysis of covariance followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests (Table S4), and the results showed that Mo accumulation in overexpressing plants was predominantly influenced by expression level, while little effect was observed from haplotype variation, with the sole exception that type5 differed from type1 and type4 for root Mo accumulation at 100 nM. The Promoter Sequences of OsMOT1;1 Showed Distinct Differentiation between Indica and Japonica

To further understand the variation of OsMOT1;1 within rice natural population, we analyzed the promoter sequences of OsMOT1;1 by combining the SNP/INDEL information downloaded on RiceVarMap (http://ricevarmap.ncpgr.cn/) and sequencing verification. Within OsMOT1;1 promoters (~ 2 kb) of 156 japonica and 295 indica varieties, totally 49 SNPs and 15 INDELs were identified (Table S5). Subsequently, these varieties were divided into 13 haplotypes (Hap1–Hap13). Of these haplotypes, Hap1–Hap5 and Hap11 mainly composed of japonica accessions, and the rest mainly composed of indica accessions (Fig. 7A). Consistent with this, neighbor-joining tree of these haplotypes showed that the Hap1–Hap5 and Hap6–Hap13 respectively clustered together, indicating that the promoter sequences of OsMOT1;1 generally diverged between japonica and indica. Interestingly, Hap11 that composed of japonica accessions seemed to carry an indica-type OsMOT1;1 promoter. Furthermore, the accessions of Hap1–Hap5 generally showed higher Mo concentrations regardless of vegetative or mature stages, whereas the Mo accumulation of Hap11 were comparable to that of indica-type haplotypes (Fig. 7B, C). Discussion

Cultivated rice comprises two major subgroups, indica and japonica, which have evolved reproductive isolation and are geographically distributed in distinct regions, leading to significant genetic divergence (Gutaker et al. 2020). Consequently, identifying genes that contribute to agronomic trait differences between indica and japonica holds substantial promise for mutual improvement of their respective varieties. Our analysis of natural variation in mineral element accumulation within rice populations revealed significant differences between indica and japonica (Yang et al. 2018), indicating considerable potential for enhancing nutrient uptake and utilization. Among the differentially accumulated mineral elements, Mo exhibited the most pronounced variation. In this study, we demonstrated that the differential Mo accumulation between indica and japonica is primarily driven by variations in the expression levels of OsMOT1;1. This conclusion is supported by the following evidences: (1) Significant differences in Mo accumulation between indica and japonica under both field and hydroponic conditions (Figs. 1 and 2B); (2) Significant decrease in Mo accumulation observed in OsMOT1;1 mutants (Fig. 4); (3) Equivalent Mo transport capabilities of coding region haplotypes from indica and japonica, based on overexpression assays (Fig. 5); (4) Expression variation of OsMOT1;1 as the primary determinant of Mo accumulation variation for both indica and japonica, as indicated by GWAS and QTL mapping respectively (Figs. 2 and 3) (Yang et al. 2018); (5) Distinct promoter sequence divergence between japonica and indica, with overall higher OsMOT1;1 expression levels in japonica (Figs. 6 and 7).

Mo is an essential micronutrient for plant growth and development, serving as a critical component of catalytic centers in various metabolic processes and playing an indispensable role in plant physiology (Tejada-Jimenez et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2022). Especially in legumes, a greater uptake of Mo is necessary to fulfill the requirements of symbiotic nitrogen fixation, thereby rendering their growth more sensitive to Mo accumulation (Banerjee et al. 2022). The orthologs of MOT1 in different plant species appear to play a key role in Mo accumulation, which in turn affect plant growth, development and stress resistance. For instance, in soybean, weak haplotypes of GmMOT1.1 and GmMOT1.2 significantly reduce Mo translocation from roots to shoots, leading to yield reduction, whereas high-Mo haplotypes enhance yield (Zhang et al. 2023). Interestingly, these genes affect soybean growth not through symbiotic nitrogen fixation but by modulating the activity of the Mo-dependent enzyme aldehyde oxidase (AO), which influences indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) synthesis. High-Mo haplotypes of these genes confer a significant advantage in acidic soils and have been subject to artificial selection (Zhang et al. 2023). In non-leguminous plants such as tomato, SlMOT1 regulates Mo accumulation, affecting the activity of aldehyde oxidase 3 (AAO3), a key enzyme in abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis (Wang et al. 2025). As expected, SlMOT1 plays a crucial role in salt tolerance by modulating ABA levels. These recent studies highlight the importance of Mo in crop growth and environmental adaptation beyond symbiotic nitrogen fixation, expanding our understanding of its functional roles and application potential in non-leguminous crops. In the natural rice population, we identified six varieties with exceptionally high Mo concentrations, with the highest Mo variety exhibiting 3.78-, 6.01-, and 3.84-fold higher Mo concentrations in seedling straw, mature straw and brown rice, respectively, compared to the population means (Fig. 3A). The OsMOT1;1 dominant haplotype contributed 70.1% to this superior phenotype, demonstrating its strong potential for improving Mo accumulation in conventional varieties (Fig. 3). We have demonstrated that introducing the high-Mo haplotype of OsMOT1;1 into the mainstream cultivar HHZ significantly enhances its Mo uptake capacity without affecting key agronomic traits such as plant height and tiller number (Fig. 8). This suggests that the elite haplotype of OsMOT1;1 holds practical value for the genetic improvement of Mo efficiency. Such highly significant QTL controlling molybdenum accumulation has not been reported in other crops. Further exploration of the OsMOT1;1 dominant haplotype in enhancing environmental adaptability, such as salt and acid tolerance, could broaden its application prospects.

Currently, natural variation in Mo accumulation in many plants is primarily driven by MOT1 orthologs, but the underlying mechanisms appear to differ significantly. In tomato, functional variation in SlMOT1 is mainly driven by protein activity variation (Wang et al. 2025); in soybean, GmMOT1.1 and GmMOT1.2 exhibit variation in both expression levels and protein activity (Zhang et al. 2023); whereas in Arabidopsis and rice, expression level variation is the primary driver (Baxter et al. 2008; Yang et al. 2018; Huang et al. 2019). Due to the divergence between rice subgroups, the variation in OsMOT1;1 expression levels appears more complex than in other species. Previously, we had confirmed through GWAS that OsMOT1;1 expression level variation is the main cause of Mo accumulation variation in the indica subgroup (Yang et al. 2018). In this work, we observed significant divergence in Mo accumulation between indica and japonica, with japonica exhibiting overall higher Mo accumulation capacity (Figs. 1 and 2). Additionally, QTL mapping using parental lines with significant differences in Mo accumulation from japonica revealed that OsMOT1;1 expression level variation also underlies Mo concentration variation in japonica (Fig. 3). Further comparison of OsMOT1;1 expression levels between indica and japonica subpopulations showed significantly higher expression in japonica (Fig. 4). Based on these findings, we propose that natural variation in the OsMOT1;1 promoter region generates diverse haplotypes within and between indica and japonica, leading to varying expression levels and contributing to the rich natural variation in Mo concentration (Fig. 7). The differential sequence variation effects in the OsMOT1;1 promoter between indica and japonica also explain the observed differences in Mo concentration GWAS results between the two subgroups (Fig. 2C). Notably, one haplotype (Hap11), predominantly composed of japonica varieties but carrying an OsMOT1;1 promoter more similar to indica, exhibited Mo accumulation closer to that of indica (Fig. 7), further supporting the role of OsMOT1;1 variation in driving Mo accumulation differences between indica and japonica.

Regrettably, we have not yet identified the key causal variants in the OsMOT1;1 promoter region. Haplotypes Hap1-5, predominantly composed of japonica varieties and generally exhibiting higher Mo concentrations, share a consistent SNP in the OsMOT1;1 promoter (SNP-462 bp) compared to other haplotypes mainly composed of indica varieties (Table S5). However, it remains unclear whether this SNP is the primary determinant of OsMOT1;1 expression differences between indica and japonica. Within the indica subgroup, Hap6 and Hap10 showed significantly lower seedling Mo accumulation, with their OsMOT1;1 promoters containing several unique SNPs or INDELs, potentially explaining their reduced Mo accumulation. Additionally, several extreme high-Mo varieties were not classified separately but grouped with other tropical japonica varieties in Hap1, suggesting that the 2 kb promoter region may not contain the key regulatory sequences responsible for their elevated expression levels. Further investigation into more distal regions of the OsMOT1;1 promoter may be necessary to elucidate the mechanisms underlying their higher expression.

In conclusion, our study systematically elucidates the genetic basis of Mo accumulation divergence between indica and japonica, a key evolutionary difference offering valuable breeding opportunities. We demonstrate that extensive promoter haplotype variation of OsMOT1;1, both within and between subspecies, drives diverse expression levels, leading to the broad natural variation in Mo concentration. Critically, we identified rice varieties with exceptionally high Mo accumulation and, through genetic introgression, proved their potential to enhance Mo nutrition in elite cultivars without compromising key agronomic traits. This work provides both superior genetic resources and a practical breeding strategy for improving Mo efficiency in rice.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials

The plant materials used in this study included a diverse panel of 529 rice (Oryza sativa) accessions that had been sequenced as previously described (Chen et al. 2014; Table S1). This rice population was collected from around the world, comprising 192 micro-core cultivated rice accessions from China (Zhang et al. 2011), 132 breeding parents from the International Rice Molecular Breeding Program (Yu et al. 2003), 148 micro-core accessions from the USDA Genetic Resources Center (Agrama et al. 2009), 15 varieties used for SNP development in the OryzaSNP project (McNally et al. 2009), and 46 varieties from the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) Rice Germplasm Resource Center. These accessions had been subjected to high-throughput sequencing using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 in previous studies (Chen et al. 2014; Zhao et al. 2015). Of these total 533 accessions, 4 were discarded after sequencing due to excessively high heterozygosity. These sequence data are available on NCBI under the BioProject number PRJNA171289. The reference sequence used was the version 6.1 sequence of the japonica rice variety Nipponbare from the Michigan State University (MSU) Rice Annotation Database (http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/). The gap filling was performed using a modified k-nearest neighbor algorithm. For further details, please refer to the previous works (Chen et al. 2014; Zhao et al. 2015). The final SNP information and code used in the present study for each cultivar can be queried through the RiceVarMap v2.0 (http://ricevarmap.ncpgr.cn/) (Zhao et al. 2015).

Field Trials

The field data of the germplasm accessions from Wuhan and Youxian were extracted from our previous studies (Yang et al. 2018). The field experiments of OsMOT1;1-related transgenic materials were conducted in the experimental fields of Huazhong Agricultural University in Wuhan, with conventional water and fertilizer management. The F2 population, along with its parental lines W102 and HHZ, was cultivated in an experimental field at Guangxi University in Nanning (sown in August 2024) under conventional water and fertilizer management; the soil pH and elemental content are provided in Table S6. Plant samples at maturity were separated into straw and brown rice (dehusked) for Mo determination. Only the plants grown inside each row were harvested during sampling to avoid edge effects. Two individual plants were mixed to serve as one biological replicate, and three biological replicates were collected for each sample.

Determination of Mo Concentration

The plant samples were dried at 80 °C for 3 days, and then ground into a uniform powder. The determination of Mo was carried out by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7700 series, USA). For each sample, 0.2 g of dried powder was digested in 10 ml of 65% nitric acid using a microwave digestion system (MARS6, CEM Corporation) at a gradient of 120–180 °C for 1 h. After digestion, the samples were diluted with deionized water and then analyzed for Mo concentration using ICP-MS.

Genome-wide Association Analysis

According to the genomic sequencing, these 529 accessions include 295 indica rice varieties, 156 japonica rice varieties, 46 autumn rice varieties, and 32 intermediate types. Considering the effects of population structure and population size on GWAS results, we conducted GWAS separately for the indica and japonica subpopulations according previous studies. We first filtered all SNP loci by setting the minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05 and the minor allele count ≥ 15, and totally obtained 2,767,191 and 1,857,866 SNPs for indica and japonica, respectively. To control spurious associations, the population structure was represented as a random effect in the LMM model using the kinship (K) matrix (Chen et al. 2014; Zhao et al. 2015). Random SNPs with a uniform distribution used to analyze the population structure were used to calculate the K value. GWAS analysis was conducted using the LMM model provided by the FaST-LMM program. According to a modified Bonferroni correction described by Li et al. (2012), the effective number of independent SNPs in each population was calculated and then used to replace the total number of SNPs to determine the significance threshold for GWAS in the genome. The effective number of independent SNPs for indica and japonica were 571,843 and 245,348, respectively, and the significance thresholds were specified as 1.8 × 10− 6 and 4.1 × 10− 6. Detailed methods have been described previously (Chen et al. 2014).

QTL Mapping

An F2 population was developed from a cross between W102 (Padi_Tarab_Arab) and W022 (C_8429), two parents that display a significant difference in Mo accumulation. The insertion–deletion (InDel) markers used in this study were designed based on DNA polymorphisms between W022 and W102 obtained from RiceVarMap2 (http://ricevarmap.ncpgr.cn). A total of 19 effective INDEL markers, which were evenly distributed across chromosome 08, were used for QTL mapping. The sequences and positions of the primers are listed in Table S2. QTL mapping was performed with IciMapping software using the genotypes of the F2 population and their corresponding Mo concentrations.

Vector Construction and Plant Transformation

The OsMOT1;1 overexpression lines were generated in a previous study (Yang et al. 2018). The CRISPR vector for OsMOT1;1 was constructed using a CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing technology for rice plants as described previously (Miao et al. 2013). The construct was transformed into the japonica rice variety Zhonghua 11. The homozygous CRISPR-edited lines were obtained through PCR and sequencing screening.

Hydroponic Experiments

Hydroponic experiments were conducted based on the following rice nutrient solution: 1.44 mM NH4NO3, 0.3 mM NaH2PO4, 0.5 mM K2SO4, 1.0 mM CaCl2, 1.6 mM MgSO4, 0.17 mM Na2SiO3, 50 µM Fe-EDTA, 0.06 µM (NH4)6Mo7O24, 15 µM H3BO3, 8 µM MnCl2, 0.12 µM CuSO4, 0.12 µM ZnSO4, 29 µM FeCl3, 40.5 µM citric acid, with the pH adjusted to approximately 5.5 (Yoshida et al. 1976). After seed soaking and germination, rice plants were transferred to the corresponding nutrient solutions (e.g., full nutrient solution or solutions with different Mo concentrations) and were grown for about 4 weeks. The nutrient solution was replaced every 5 days. For samples used in expression analysis or Mo measurement, three individual plants (or the same tissue from three individual plants) were mixed as one biological replicate, with at least three biological replicates collected for each sample.

Analysis of Gene Expression Patterns

All samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using Tranzol reagent (TransGen, Beijing, China). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, first-strand cDNA was synthesized in a 20 µl reaction mixture containing 3 µg of total RNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). Prior to the reverse transcription reaction, RNA was pretreated with DNase I for 15 min. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using an ABI 7500 PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ (TaKaRa, Otsu, Japan). The rice Ubiquitin5 gene was used as an internal control, with primers qUbq-F/qUbq-R. All primers used for quantitative real-time PCR are listed in Table S2, and their amplification efficiency and specificity had been confirmed before use.

Statistical Analysis

Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA were conducted using SPSS Statistics, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).
