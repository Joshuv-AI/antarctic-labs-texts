Identification of CD109 in the extracellular vesicles derived from ovarian cancer stem-like cells
Authors: Ye Eun Kim, Jun Se Kim, Min Joo Shin, Seo Yul Lee, Dae Kyoung Kim, Nam-Kyung Lee, Yang Woo Kwon, Kyung-Un Choi, Dong-Soo Suh, Byoung Soo Kim, Sanghwa Jeong, Jae Ho Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvarian cancer is the deadliest gynecological cancer because it has few early symptoms and metastasizes to the surrounding organs at advanced stages. Cancer stem cells (CSCs), a subpopulation of cells with acquired drug resistance, contribute to the recurrence and poor prognosis of ovarian cancer. CD109, a cell surface glycoprotein, has been reported to be a marker of CSCs; however, it remains unclear whether CD109 is secreted by CSCs. In this study, we investigated the amount of CD109 in conditioned media (CM) of CSC populations from ovarian cancer cell lines and patients with ovarian cancer. The CM of sphere-forming CSCs isolated from ovarian cancer cell lines (A2780 and SKOV3) had higher levels of CD109 than those isolated from their adherent cultured parental cells. Furthermore, higher levels of CD109 were detected on the cell surface and in the CM of sphere-forming CSC populations isolated from patient-derived primary ovarian cancer cells. To clarify whether CD109 is localized to the exosomal fraction secreted from CSCs, extracellular vesicles were isolated from the CM by ultracentrifugation. In addition to the CM, the exosomal fraction of ovarian CSCs contained greater levels of CD109 than the parental cells. These results suggest that CD109 is secreted in a soluble or exosomal form from CSCs, and that the measurement of secreted CD109 may be used as a diagnostic or prognostic marker for ovarian cancer.Keywords: Cancer biomarker, Cancer stem cell, CD109, Extracellular vesicles, Ovarian cancer

INTRODUCTION

Ovarian cancer is most frequently diagnosed in women over the age of 55 and is the fifth most common cause of cancer-related deaths in women (1, 2). Since ovarian cancer is asymptomatic in its early stages and is diagnosed at an advanced stage (stages III and IV), when it metastasizes to distant organs, the 5-year survival rate is only 31% (2, 3). Taxane/platinum-based or neoadjuvant chemotherapy, in combination with surgical resection, is the first-line treatment for ovarian cancer (4). Although the initial treatment is mostly effective, tumors may recur in more than 70% of patients (5). Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are responsible for ovarian cancer recurrence and the poor prognosis of ovarian cancer (6). CSCs are small subpopulations of cancer cells that can be identified in various cancer types, and have self-renewal, differentiation, metastasis, tumorigenesis abilities and resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy (7, 8). Targeting CSCs, which presents a challenge for ovarian cancer treatment, is effective at inhibiting tumor growth and recurrence in vitro and in vivo. Reportedly, blocking NOTCH, Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, NF-κB, and Hippo signaling pathways, which are known to be involved in maintaining stemness properties, is effective at eliminating ovarian CSCs (9). Furthermore, targeted therapies that inhibit stemness markers expressed on the surface of ovarian CSCs, such as CD24, CD44, CD117, CD133, and EpCAM, reduce cell growth, motility, differentiation, chemoresistance, and tumorigenicity in vivo (9, 10, 11). However, it is necessary to identify additional biomarkers to completely overcome the intratumoral heterogeneity of ovarian cancer and to determine an immediate prognosis.

CD109 is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored glycoprotein that is highly expressed in the membranes of various malignant tumors (12). CD109 can be secreted extracellularly in a soluble form, after cleavage to a 180 kDa protein by furinase, or attached to extracellular vesicles (EVs) (13). In addition, high CD109 expression levels in tumors are associated with a poor prognosis (12, 14). In ovarian cancer, CD109-positive patients have a substantially lower overall survival rate than CD109-negative patients, and high CD109 expression levels are associated with chemoresistance by regulating the STAT3-NOTCH1 signaling axis (15, 16). CD109 plays an important role in the maintenance of the stem-like properties of cervical squamous cell carcinoma, glioblastoma, lung adenocarcinoma, and epithelioid sarcoma, and has potential as a diagnostic and therapeutic target for cancer patients (17-20). However, whether CD109 can be used as a secretory biomarker for ovarian CSCs remains unclear.

Exosomes or EVs are double-membrane vesicles of 30-200 nm in size that are produced in the endosomal compartment of most eukaryotic cells and secreted extracellularly (21). Secreted EVs exist in almost all body fluids under normal and pathophysiological conditions, including saliva, blood, urine, amniotic fluid, and ascites, and they can be easily isolated from body fluids or cell culture supernatants (22). EVs mediate intercellular communication by transporting various biomolecules, including non-coding RNA, mRNA, DNA, proteins, and lipids (23). Cancer cell-derived EVs deliver biomolecules to fibroblasts, mesenchymal stem cells, adipocytes, immune cells, and endothelial cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) to induce metabolic reprogramming, thereby promoting tumor proliferation, metastasis, drug resistance, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression (24). In particular, CSC-derived EVs are known to induce the reprogramming of TMEs and the acquisition of stemness by surrounding non-CSCs (25). Therefore, it is necessary to detect markers of ovarian CSC-derived EVs present in body fluids for early diagnosis and prognosis of the disease.

In this study, CD109 was identified as a CSC-derived exosomal marker in CSCs isolated from ovarian cancer cell lines and from patients with ovarian cancer, suggesting the possible application of exosomal CD109 as a marker for the diagnosis of ovarian cancer and the tracking of its progression.

RESULTS

Increased expression levels of CD109 in spheroid-forming ovarian CSCs

CD109 is important for maintaining the stem-cell-like properties of brain, cervical, and lung cancer cells (17-19). Moreover, CD109 is involved in the drug resistance of ovarian cancer cells (16). Based on these findings we investigated the expression level of CD109 in ovarian CSCs. In a previous study, we established a method for isolating CSCs from epithelial ovarian cancer cell lines (26). The ovarian cancer cell lines (A2780-AD and SKOV3-AD) were cultured in ultra-low-attachment culture dishes and sub-cultured for at least 7-8 passages to isolate sphere-forming CSCs (A2780-SP and SKOV3-SP) (Fig. 1A). The protein level of CD109 was markedly increased in A2780-SP and SKOV3-SP cells compared with two-dimensional (2D)-cultured A2780-AD and SKOV3-AD cells (Fig. 1B). The expression levels of CD109 were measured using flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining. The expression levels of CD109 on the cell surface greatly increased in A2780-SP and SKOV3-SP cells compared to their 2D-cultured parental cells (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, increased expression levels of CD109 in ovarian cancer spheroids were confirmed by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 1D). These results suggest that CD109 is overexpressed on the surface of ovarian CSCs in vitro.

Localization of CD109 to EVs secreted by ovarian CSC spheroids

CD109 is secreted into the conditioned media (CM) in soluble and exosome-associated form (13). To determine whether CD109 can be secreted as an exosomal protein from ovarian CSCs, CM was collected and EVs were isolated from the collected CM by ultracentrifugation, as previously reported (27). Thereafter, the isolated EVs were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). TEM analysis showed that the EVs isolated from 2D- and 3D-cultured A2780 and SKOV3 cells were cup-shaped or round (Fig. 2A). The mean size of the EVs was less than 200 nm (Fig. 2B). The mode (most frequently occurring) sizes were less for EVs from A2780 and SKOV3 sphere-forming CSCs (130.6 ± 19.0 and 133.3 ± 27.0 nm, respectively) than those from adherent parental cells (155.1 ± 2.3 and 162.5 ± 10.1 nm, respectively). A2780-SP and SKOV3-SP cells was lower than that secreted less EVs than A2780-AD and SKOV3-AD cells (Fig. 2B). To measure the levels of CD109 protein in the soluble and EV forms, the protein levels of CD109 in total CM, EV-depleted CM (ΔEV_CM), and the EV fraction were determined by western blotting. Both adherent cells and CSC-derived EVs were highly enriched in CD9 and CD81, which have been reported as exosomal markers. CD109 was detected in the CM derived from A2780-SP and SKOV3-SP cells, but not in the CM derived from their 2D-cultured parental cells (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, western blotting data showed that CD109 was present in the EV fraction of A2780-SP and SKOV3-SP cells, but not in the EV fraction of 2D-cultured cells (Fig. 2C). To quantify the levels of CD109 in EVs, we developed a sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using anti-CD109 monoclonal antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 1). The levels of CD109 in the EV fractions isolated from A2780-SP and SKOV3-SP cells (25.2 ± 2.3 and 45.7 ± 1.2 ng/ml, respectively) were significantly greater than those isolated from A2780-AD and SKOV3-AD cells (2.9 ± 0.6 and 2.4 ± 0.5 ng/ml, respectively; Fig. 2D). These results suggest that ovarian sphere-forming CSCs secrete higher levels of CD109 in soluble and exosome-associated forms than adherent cells.

Increased expression levels of CD109 in patient-derived ovarian CSCs

To determine whether CD109 is secreted as an exosomal protein from primary CSCs isolated from patients with ovarian cancer, primary epithelial ovarian cancer cells were cultured in ultra-low-attachment dishes to isolate sphere-forming primary ovarian CSCs (pOCs). Two pOC lines (pOC #1 and pOC #2) were isolated from the tumor tissues of two donors with ovarian cancer. Sphere-forming pOC #1 and pOC #2 cells formed tightly packed 3D spheroids (Fig. 3A). Similar to ovarian cancer cell line-derived CSCs, CD109 was highly expressed in pOCs, as shown by western blotting (Fig. 3B). Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining revealed high levels of CD109 expression on the surfaces of pOC #1 and pOC #2 cells (Fig. 3C, D).

CD109 is associated with EVs secreted from patient-derived ovarian CSCs

Next, we determined whether CD109 is secreted as an exosomal protein from primary CSCs isolated from patients with ovarian cancer. pOC-derived EVs were isolated by ultracentrifugation and characterized by TEM and NTA. TEM analysis of EVs derived from pOC #1 and pOC #2 cells demonstrated the presence of vesicles with a size of less than 200 nm and a typical exosome shape (Fig. 4A). Both pOC-derived EVs were small, with a mode size less than 100 nm, and the size of pOC #1-derived EVs (33.3 ± 12.4 nm) was more heterogeneous than the size of pOC #2-derived EVs (61.0 ± 2.9 nm, Fig. 4B). To verify the CD109 secretion pattern of pOCs, the protein levels of CD109 and exosome markers in total CM, ΔEV_CM, and EV fractions were measured by western blotting. Exosomal markers (CD9 and CD81) and CD109 were highly enriched in EVs isolated from the CM of pOC #1 and pOC #2 (Fig. 4C). ELISA analysis showed that the protein levels of exosomal CD109 derived from pOC #1 and #2 were 34.1 ± 8.8 and 56.3 ± 1.8 ng/ml, respectively (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that CD109 is highly expressed on the surface of pOCs and that CD109 is secreted as an EV-related protein.

DISCUSSION

Ovarian cancer cell lines and primary ovarian cancer-derived CSCs exhibit enhanced stem-like properties and drug resistance (28, 29), and ovarian CSCs are known to accelerate tumor malignancy (30). Cancer spheroids mimic spatiotemporal oxygen and nutrient gradients, thereby mimicking tumors grown in vivo (31). In this study, we identified CD109 as a cell surface marker of CSCs in ovarian cancer. Using western blotting, flow cytometry, and immunostaining, we found that the expression levels of CD109 in A2780-SP and SKOV3-SP cells were greatly upregulated compared to their levels in parental cells. CD109 is known to promote stemness properties, such as sphere-forming ability, drug resistance, tumor initiation, metastasis, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, in various cancer cells (17-20). In particular, the expression level of CD109 in ovarian epithelial cancer increases with higher tumor histological grades and is substantially increased in serous, endometrioid, and undifferentiated carcinomas (15). In addition, among patients who received combinatorial chemotherapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel, CD109 was notably upregulated in the tumors of patients who experienced recurrence within 8 months after the completion of chemotherapy (15). Recently, we reported that CD109 is overexpressed in drug-resistant cells and it stimulates drug resistance in ovarian cancer cell lines by regulating the STAT3-NOTCH1 signaling axis (16). A positive correlation was found between plasma CD109 levels and FIGO staging in ovarian cancer patients (data not shown). These results suggest that CD109 has the potential to be used as a diagnostic marker for identifying CSCs and for determining the prognosis of ovarian cancer.

EVs are valuable for cancer diagnosis and prognosis because they can deliver information about the diverse molecular composition of the parental tumor locally and at distant sites, and they are detected in various types of body fluids, such as saliva, blood, urine, amniotic fluid, and ascites (22, 23). Reportedly, 3D culture conditions affect the biogenesis, size, and cargo of EVs (32). Human gastric cancer spheroids secrete EVs at an increased rate and the EVs are smaller than those from the same cell lines in 2D culture (33). Spheroid-derived EVs appear to be more representative of those derived from patients’ plasma (34). Likewise, murine colorectal cancer spheroids secrete an increased number of EVs per cell compared to parental cells cultured in monolayers (35). Consistently, we found that the size of EV particles decreased in spheroid cultures of ovarian cancer cells compared with their size in 2D-cultured parental cells. However, in contrast to previous reports, the number of EV particles also decreased in the 3D-cultured spheroids of ovarian cancer cells. The reduced number of EVs in 3D-cultured cancer spheroids appeared to differ under different cell culture conditions. Further studies are needed to clarify the nature and composition of the EV particles secreted from cancer spheroids.

CD24, claudin-4, and heat-shock protein 70, secreted by EVs derived from ovarian cancer cells, are promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers (36-38). In addition, CD109 is a component of the exosomes secreted by HEK293 cells (13) and mesenchymal stromal cells (39). Consistent with these results, we found that the levels of exosomal CD109 secreted by CSCs were higher than those secreted by their parental cells. Although characteristics such as the number and size of secreted EVs differed between ovarian cancer cell lines and patient-derived cells, these results suggest that CD109 is a marker of EVs secreted from CSCs. The metalloproteinase meprin β cleaves CD109 at the cell surface, thereby inducing the release of soluble CD109, whereas it reduces the amount of CD109 released by EVs (40), suggesting reciprocal regulation of soluble and EV-attached forms of CD109. Interestingly, CD109 was highly enriched in EVs secreted from pOCs compared to EVs secreted from A2780-SP or SKOV3-SP cells. These results suggest that primary ovarian cancer cells secrete EVs enriched with CD109. Further studies should be conducted to explore whether exosomal CD109 levels are correlated with the clinical stage and prognosis of patients with ovarian cancer.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated for the first time that CD109 is secreted from ovarian cancer cell spheroids. CD109 is released in soluble or EV-associated forms into the spheroids. Therefore, the quantification of CSC-secreted CD109 protein may be applicable for the diagnosis and prognosis of patients with ovarian cancer.
