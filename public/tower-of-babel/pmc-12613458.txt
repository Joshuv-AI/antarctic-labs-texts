Revisiting the link between oropharyngeal dysphagia and cancer in autoimmune myositis: a descriptive study
Authors: Hao Cheng Shen, Océane Landon-Cardinal, Josiane Bourré-Tessier, Sabrina Hoa, Farah Zarka, Jessica Nehme, Anne-Marie Mansour, Jean-Paul Makhzoum, Alexandra Mereniuk, Rosalie-Sélène Meunier, Maude Bouchard-Marmen, Laurence Poirier-Blanchette, Marianne Landry, Marianne Lévesque, Rami Massie, Oliver Blanchard, Fredéric Lefebvre, Jean-Richard Goulet, Eric Rich, Jean-Pierre Raynauld, Gemma Pérez, Martial Koenig, Hugues Allard-Chamard, Julie Drouin, Sandra Chartrand, Michelle Aaron, Victoria Ivensky, Maria Infantino, Margherita Giannini, Zoe Betteridge, Valérie Leclair, Marie Hudson, Jason Karamchandani, Baptiste Hervier, Erin O’Ferrall, Benjamin Ellezam, Ira N Targoff, Minoru Satoh, Marvin J Fritzler, Jean-Luc Senécal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore the link between moderate to severe oropharyngeal dysphagia and cancer in autoimmune myositis (AIM) other than inclusion body myositis (IBM).MethodsThe medical records of patients with AIM seen in rheumatology in two university hospitals from January 2000 to December 2022 were retrospectively reviewed. Using an updated AIM subclassification, patients were classified by expert opinion as pure dermatomyositis (DM), immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), scleromyositis, lupomyositis, anti-MDA-5 syndrome, antisynthetase syndrome (ASyS) or polymyositis syndrome. Objective oropharyngeal dysphagia at myositis diagnosis was defined by an abnormal videofluoroscopic swallowing study and/or the need for percutaneous gastrojejunostomy. The presence of cancer within 3 years of myositis diagnosis was recorded.ResultsPure DM accounted for 50% (n = 20/40) of the cases of objective oropharyngeal dysphagia, while anti-MDA-5 syndrome and ASyS together represented 8% (n = 3/40). Cancers occurred predominantly in pure DM (n = 27/33), and rarely in scleromyositis (n = 1/53), anti-MDA-5 syndrome and ASyS (n = 0/62). Among patients 50 years of age or older with pure DM (n = 50), cancer was present in 40% (n = 4/10) of patients with no muscle weakness, 45% (n = 10/22) in those with proximal weakness alone, 44% (n = 4/9) in those with moderate dysphagia and 100% of those with severe dysphagia (n = 9/9) (p = 0.02 by two-sided Fisher’s exact test).ConclusionRecognizing scleromyositis, anti-MDA-5 and ASyS as distinct from pure DM improves risk stratification for cancer screening. In patients ≥ 50 years with pure DM, severe oropharyngeal dysphagia is strongly associated with cancer, suggesting a paraneoplastic myopathy with an ineffective anticancer immune response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-025-03662-0.Keywords: Anti-MDA-5 syndrome, Anti-synthetase syndrome, Autoantibodies, Autoimmune myositis, Cancer, Dermatomyositis, Esophageal dysmotility, Lupomyositis, Necrotizing myopathy, Oropharyngeal dysphagia, Paraneoplastic syndrome, Scleromyositis, VFSS, Type I interferon, Type II interferon

Introduction

The International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy (IIM)—associated Cancer Screening was recently published and it graded cancer risk according to clinical features, autoantibodies and IIM subtypes [1]. Moderate to severe oropharyngeal dysphagia was designated a clinical feature at “high-risk” for cancer, and DM a “high-risk” IIM subset.

When serology was examined in the Pro-DERM study of DM [2] in 47 patients, 16 patients had antisynthetase antibodies and 6 patients had anti-PM-Scl antibodies, highlighting a lack of consensus on how broadly or narrowly DM should be defined. On the one hand, a patient with a DM rash can be subclassified by the 2017 EULAR/ACR IIM classification criteria either into clinically amyopathic DM (CADM) or DM [3]. On the other hand, pathological autoantibody internalization in myositis as reported by Pinal-Fernandez et al. [4, 5] argues for subclassification by autoantibodies [6]. Pure DM [7–9], systemic sclerosis (SSc) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) are characterized by autoantibodies against nuclear autoantigens, while ASyS and anti-MDA-5 syndrome are defined by autoantibodies targeting cytoplasmic autoantigens.

The objective of the study was to explore the link between moderate to severe oropharyngeal dysphagia and cancer across an updated subclassification of AIM. For pure DM, the link between cancer and extent of muscle weakness focused on patients ≥ 50 years, an age group at risk for cancer.

Materials and methods

Diagnosing AIM

The 2017 EULAR/ACR IIM classification criteria were designed to include patients with a DM rash and no muscle weakness [10]. We conducted a retrospective study of 250 patients diagnosed by expert opinion with AIM, aiming to study the link between objective oropharyngeal dysphagia and cancer.

To fully capture the spectrum of AIM, the following patients were included:

patients with a DM rash with no muscle weakness

patients with a DM rash with no muscle weakness

patients with an anti-MDA-5 syndrome and at least one mucocutaneous feature

patients with an anti-MDA-5 syndrome and at least one mucocutaneous feature

patients with myositis, with the exclusion of IBM and myositis induced by immunotherapy.

patients with myositis, with the exclusion of IBM and myositis induced by immunotherapy.

Patients

This study included adult patients seen at Centre Hospitalier de l’Université de Montréal (CHUM) between January 2000 and June 2018, and those seen at Hôpital du Sacré-Coeur de Montréal (HSCM) between January 2000 and December 2022. A retrospective medical record review was done in all patients, for AIM diagnosis and subclassification of AIM.

Rationale for individualizing anti-MDA-5 syndrome and ASyS from pure DM

Anti-MDA-5 syndrome and ASyS may display unique manifestations that make them distinct from pure DM such as rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease (ILD), myocarditis [37, 38] and digital ischemia [39]. Moreover, classification criteria were proposed for anti-MDA-5 syndrome [40]. Similarly, the 239th and 273rd ENMC workshops made the case for ASyS as a distinct entity [41, 42].

Of note, there is a negative association between cancer and ILD [43], and patients with ASyS can have a DM rash [44] and a lower risk of cancer. Rounding out this perspective on subclassification, type II interferon appears to have an important role in the pathophysiology of anti-MDA-5 syndrome and ASyS [45, 46]. Therefore, anti-MDA-5 syndrome, but not CADM and DM, is considered herein as a subset distinct from pure DM.

Subclassification of AIM

Each patient had to be subclassified by expert opinion in one of seven subsets seen in rheumatology, as illustrated in Fig. 1 and suppl. Figure 1: pure DM, IMNM, scleromyositis, lupomyositis, anti-MDA-5 syndrome, ASyS, and treatment-responsive polymyositis syndrome.

Subclassification of autoimmune myositis commonly seen in rheumatology by select clinical, serological and morphological features at muscle pathology

Subclassification by expert opinion was based on cumulative clinical features, nailfold capillaroscopy [14, 47], autoantibody results, ANA patterns, and available pathology findings, as detailed in suppl. Tables 1, 2 and 3 [11–36, 48–62].

Selected electronic microscopy (EM) findings and patterns of type I and II interferon staining on muscle fibers that support the proposed subclassification are summarized in suppl. Table 3 [11, 48–62].

SSc and SLE cause myositis through mechanism different from pure DM [48–50, 57]. The terms scleromyositis and lupomyositis reflect this paradigm change, breaking from the concept of an associated disease.

Objective oropharyngeal dysphagia at myositis diagnosis was defined by an abnormal videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS) and/or the need for percutaneous gastrojejunostomy, as previously described [7, 17]. The oropharyngeal dysphagia was graded as severe if aspiration was seen on VFSS or if the patient needed a percutaneous gastrojejunostomy; it was graded as moderate if VFSS was abnormal in the absence of aspiration. VFSS was usually ordered by treating physicians when moderate to severe oropharyngeal dysphagia was suspected.

Serology

Baseline immunological studies were performed at CHUM and HSCM as previously described. They included ANAs determined by IIF on HEp-2 cells, an anti-ENA and a commercial line blot assay for myositis-specific (MSA) and myositis-associated autoantibodies (Myositis Profile 3 and 4, Euroimmun AG, Luebeck, Germany). Myositis Profile 4 does test for anti-TIF1γ, anti-NXP2 and anti-SAE antibodies and Myositis Profile 3 does not. Additional serological testing was done by MJF at Mitogen Advanced Diagnostics Corp., (Calgary, Alberta, Canada) for SSc-specific autoantibodies (including anti-Th/To, anti-SMN, anti-RuvBL1/2), anti-HMGCR and anti-NT5c1a antibodies, while protein A-assisted immunoprecipitation (IP) analysis for selected cases was done by INT, Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation, Clinical Immunology Laboratory, USA, or MS (Japan), as previously described [7, 8, 11, 17], for selected cases.

Muscle pathology

The muscle selected for biopsy was generally guided by both EMG and neurological examination with only a few patients guided by MRI only. Standard histochemical, histoenzymologic and immunohistochemical stains including MHC class I (MHCI) and C5b9 were routinely performed at our institutions. In more recent years, additional stains included MxA, MHC class II (MHCII), CD4 (Helper T cells), CD8 (Cytotoxic T cells), CD20 (B cells), CD138 (plasma cells), autophagy markers (TDP43, LC3 and p62) and collagen 4 (Col4) by immunofluorescence to evaluate capillary pathology. Ultrastructural evaluation has also been routinely performed only in more recent years. The subclassification used herein is based on accepted histopathologic features of AIM discussed in various ENMC workshops [41, 64–68].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients. Comparisons between patients stratified according to extent of muscle weakness were performed using Fisher's exact tests. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No correction for multiple testing was performed as analyses were considered exploratory. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1.

Results

Autoantibody results assist subclassification of AIM patients

The subclassification of 250 patients is summarized in Fig. 2 and Table 1. Excluding patients who died within 3 years of myositis diagnosis, all patients but one were followed for at least three years. Positive autoantibody results directly assisting subclassification were seen in 79% (n = 197/250) of patients and are summarized below, in decreasing frequency. All patients with anti-MDA-5 syndrome (n = 24) and with ASyS (n = 30) were seropositive. For IMNM, it was 94% (n = 50/53), while for lupomyositis and scleromyositis, it was 89% (n = 8/9) and 77% (n = 41/53) of patients, respectively. Lastly, in pure DM, seropositivity was documented in 68% (n = 44/65) of patients.

Summarizing subclassification of patients without a subset-defining autoantibody

For pure DM patients, all of whom had a DM rash, 11 were seronegative and 10 were not tested. For scleromyositis, 9 patients were seronegative and previously reported [17], while 3 had a HEp-2 IIF nucleolar pattern, yet were not tested for SSc-specific antibodies. One patient had serum CK of 1,689 IU/L, a severe lupus rash, anti-SSA and anti-SSB antibodies, and was subclassified as most likely having lupomyositis. As for seronegative IMNM patients, all had cancer and isolated necrosis/regeneration at muscle biopsy; only anti-HMGCR and the Myositis Profile 3 was performed (i.e. anti-NXP2 test was not performed).

Eight patients had significant ILD and were suspected of having either an anti-MDA-5 syndrome or ASyS; 6 were seronegative and 2 were not tested.

Lastly, 8 patients with endomysial lymphocytes on muscle pathology were classified as having treatment-responsive polymyositis syndrome, as seen in Fig. 2.

Subclassification as pure DM implies ruling out anti-MDA-5 syndrome, ASyS and scleromyositis

A group of 43 patients were not subclassified as pure DM, as seen in Fig. 3. This group included 24 patients with an anti-MDA-5 syndrome, 9 patients with ASyS and a DM rash, 6 patients with ILD and a suspected anti-MDA-5 syndrome or ASyS with a DM rash, and 4 patients with scleromyositis with a DM rash, with anti-PM-Scl (n = 3) or anti-RuvBL1/2 (n = 1) antibodies. Cancer was not seen in these patients, while moderate oropharyngeal dysphagia was present in only 2 patients with respectively ASyS and anti-MDA-5 syndrome.

Subclassification as pure DM implies diagnosing anti-NXP2 pure DM sine dermatitis

Anti-NXP2 pure DM sine dermatitis was diagnosed by expert opinion in 6% of patients (n = 4/65), as seen in Fig. 3. Two patients had an abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy and two had other DM rashes judged to be part of the disease. All had muscle involvement. Interestingly, one patient with a V-sign and severe oropharyngeal dysphagia had gastric and prostate cancers.

Pure DM alone accounted for 50% of cases with objective oropharyngeal dysphagia

As seen in Fig. 2, among 250 patients, 40 patients (16%) had objective oropharyngeal dysphagia at myositis diagnosis: 23 has moderate oropharyngeal dysphagia (57%) and 17 had severe oropharyngeal dysphagia (43%). Pure DM accounted for 50% of cases with objective oropharyngeal dysphagia (n = 20/40): 48% of moderate oropharyngeal dysphagia (n = 11/23) and 53% of severe oropharyngeal dysphagia (n = 9/17).

The trio of scleromyositis, IMNM and myasthenia myositis accounted for 42.5% of cases with objective oropharyngeal dysphagia

Among 40 patients with objective oropharyngeal dysphagia, 8 had scleromyositis (20%), 7 had IMNM (18%) and 2 patients had myasthenia myositis (5%). Among 8 patients with scleromyositis and oropharyngeal dysphagia, 7 (88%) had associated lower esophageal dysmotility, documented by distal esophageal dilatation on thoracic computed tomography scans.

Summarizing severe oropharyngeal dysphagia in patients without pure DM (n = 8), 50% had scleromyositis (n = 4/8), two of whom had anti-PM-Scl antibodies, and 25% had respectively IMNM (n = 2/8) and myasthenia myositis (n = 2/8).

Anti-MDA-5 syndrome and ASyS together represented only 8% of cases of oropharyngeal dysphagia

The frequency of oropharyngeal dysphagia by diagnostic subsets was 100% (n = 2/2) in myasthenia myositis, 31% (n = 20/65) in pure DM, 15% (n = 8/53) in scleromyositis, 13% (n = 7/53) in IMNM, and only 7% (n = 2/30) in ASyS and 4% (n = 1/24) in anti-MDA-5 syndrome. The frequency of oropharyngeal dysphagia in pure DM with cancer was 48% (n = 13/27), while it was 18% (n = 7/38) in pure DM without cancer. Patients with anti-MDA-5 syndrome and ASyS all had moderate oropharyngeal dysphagia.

Pure DM accounted for 82% of cases of cancer within three years of myositis diagnosis

As seen in Fig. 2, of the 250 study patients, 13% (n = 33/250) had cancer within 3 years of myositis diagnosis. All cancers were diagnosed either at myositis diagnosis or on follow-up. Overall, 82% (n = 27/33) of these cancers were accounted for by pure DM patients. Cancer was present in 42% (n = 27/65) of patients with pure DM versus only 3% (n = 6/185) patients with all other AIM diagnoses.

As shown in Table 2, the most common malignancies were ovarian (n = 6), lung (n = 5), colorectal (n = 4), head and neck (n = 3) and prostate (n = 3) cancers. Pure DM alone encompassed 87% (n = 13/15) of patients with both oropharyngeal dysphagia at presentation and cancer within three years of myositis diagnosis.

As seen in Fig. 2, in the 185 patients without pure DM, only 6 (3%) cancers were diagnosed. Two men and one woman with seronegative IMNM had respectively colon, bladder and kidney cancer. Finally, one woman with scleromyositis had lung cancer, another woman with lupomyositis had lymphoma, and a woman with pure polymyositis had lymphoma.

Exploring the link between oropharyngeal dysphagia, age and cancer in pure DM

Clinical and serological findings of 65 pure DM patients are shown in suppl. Table 4. Fifty patients were ≥ 50 years, whereas 15 patients were younger. Compared to older patients, younger patients were more often female (87% versus 60%), clinically amyopathic (47% versus 20%) while none had severe oropharyngeal dysphagia (0% versus 18%). Strikingly, no cancer was present in patients under 50 years of age (n = 0/15), as all cancers were restricted to patients ≥ 50 years (n = 27/50).

In patients ≥ 50 years, cancer was seen in 65% (n = 13/20) of men and 47% (n = 14/30) of women. Cancer rates in subsets of pure DM defined serologically in patients ≥ 50 years were as follows: 80% (n = 8/10) if not tested serologically, 60% (n = 3/5) if anti-Mi2 positive, 56% (n = 5/9) if seronegative, 47% (n = 8/17) if anti-TIF1γ positive (n = 8/17), 40% (n = 2/5) if anti-SAE positive and 25% (n = 1/4) if anti-NXP2 positive.

To further explore the link between cancer and objective oropharyngeal dysphagia, cancer was stratified according to the extent of muscle weakness and results are shown in Fig. 4. In pure DM, a statistically significant incremental risk of cancer was observed, ranging from 40% in CADM to 100% in pure DM patients with severe dysphagia (p = 0.02 by two-sided Fisher's exact test) (Fig. 4, panel A). Similarly, a significantly increased frequency of cancer was noted in the 21 patients with anti-TIF1γ or anti-NXP2 antibodies stratified according to muscle weakness, with the highest frequency in patients with severe dysphagia (p = 0.018) (Fig. 4, panel B).

Occurrence of metastatic cancer at any time, stratified by extent of muscle weakness, was also examined, as seen in suppl. Figure 2. Discussion

Scarcity of research on objective oropharyngeal dysphagia in AIM other than IBM

Studies on objective oropharyngeal dysphagia in AIM other than IBM are scarce. The 272nd ENMC Workshop on IBM [68] stated that the gold standard for assessing dysphagia in most centers is VFSS and fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES). Using VFSS to demonstrate oropharyngeal dysphagia, objective moderate or severe oropharyngeal dysphagia at myositis diagnosis was found overall in 16% (n = 40/250) of our patients. In a different AIM population, Giannini et al. [69] found 12% (n = 18/154) of patients with oropharyngeal dysphagia, defined as a pharyngo-esophageal disturbance associated with Eating Assessment Tool −10 score ≥ 3 and at least one Fiberoptic Endoscopic Evaluation of Swallowing abnormality among propulsion failure, solid or liquid stasis. Using more sensitive evaluations, Marie et al. [70] identified esophageal involvement by manometry in 39% (n = 118/301) of patients with PM or DM. More research on objective oropharyngeal dysphagia in AIM is needed [71–73].

Objective oropharyngeal dysphagia as a tool for subclassification of AIM

As for the value of objective oropharyngeal dysphagia for subclassification, we are reminded that patterns of weakness are a valuable clinical tool used by neurologists to phenotype myositis [74]. For example, finger flexor weakness hints to IBM [68], dropped head and bent spine syndromes suggests scleromyositis [75], and brachio-cervical weakness is associated with myasthenia myositis [76] and scleromyositis (SSc) [77, 78]. Consistent with low rates reported in anti-Jo-1 myositis [69, 70, 79, 80], patients herein with anti-MDA-5 syndrome or ASyS rarely had objective oropharyngeal dysphagia.

Studies of anti-TIF1γ pure DM support a link between cancer and oropharyngeal dysphagia

To further analyze the link between cancer and oropharyngeal dysphagia in pure DM, studies of anti-TIF1γ DM were reviewed. In a multicenter study of 51 anti-TIF1γ DM by Aussy et al. [81], 78% of patients had cancer, 76% had pharyngeal involvement, 38% required a feeding tube and only 2% were clinically amyopathic. In the study by Mugii et al. [82], 91% (n = 10/11) of anti-TIF1γ DM patients with objective oropharyngeal dysphagia documented by VFSS also had cancer. In the Kanaoka et al. study [83], cancer was present in 60% (n = 15/25) of patients with muscle weakness and in only 11% (n = 1/9) of those without muscle weakness.

Anti-TIF1γ pure DM is a paraneoplastic syndrome in patients ≥ 50 years

Next generation sequencing of tumour DNA samples from patients with cancer-associated anti-TIF1γ pure DM identified an increased number of genetic alterations [84], notably TRIM 33 gene somatic mutations [85], which could lead anti-TIF1γ responses, and therefore could illustrate a model where cancer leads to anti-TIF1γ pure DM. In fact, discovering a cancer in anti-TIF1γ DM patients ≥ 50 years is likely influenced by the absence of anti-Sp4 and/or anti-CCAR1 autoantibodies, which are part of an effective anticancer response [86, 87]. Interestingly, when cancers did emerge with anti-CCAR1 autoantibodies, they occurred later and were more likely to be localized, as discussed by Fiorentino et al. [88].

In the absence of anti-Sp4 and/or anti-CCAR1 autoantibodies, these ineffective anti-TIF1γ responses may still target very effectively muscle autoantigens. In the study by Hida et al. [89] on cancer-associated anti-TIF1γ pure DM, 61% of patients (n = 19/31) had oropharyngeal dysphagia, and muscle pathology findings included dense C5b-9 deposits on capillaries and vacuolated fibers. Similarly, in regional ischemic immune myopathy, described as a severe paraneoplastic dermatopathy and associated with anti-TIF1γ pure DM, oropharyngeal dysphagia was seen in 83% of patients (n = 6/7) [90].

Finally, higher levels of anti-TIF1γ autoantibodies were found in patients with muscle weakness as opposed to those without weakness [83]. A re-increase in antibody titers on recurrence of malignancy was also described, while anti-TIF1γ levels became undetectable in three out of four patients cured of their malignancies [91]. In the present study, when moderate and severe oropharyngeal dysphagia are used as surrogates for severe and very severe muscle weakness, cancer impacts the severity of muscle weakness at pure DM presentation, as more cancer and/or more advanced cancer are seen with increasing muscle weakness. Taken altogether, in a population at risk for cancer, i.e. patients ≥ 50 years, anti-TIF1γ DM is probably a paraneoplastic syndrome in most cases, as discussed by Mammen [92]. Therapeutic decisions for pure DM should therefore consider treating the cancer as aggressively as possible, when feasible [93].

Recognizing pure DM as at high risk for cancer is useful in patients ≥ 50 years who are seronegative or with non-TIF1γ autoantibodies

In patients aged ≥ 50 years with pure DM, this study found 44% of cancer in patients without severe oropharyngeal dysphagia whereas cancer was present in all patients with severe oropharyngeal dysphagia. While cancer in younger patients was not addressed herein, making the case for intensive screening in all subsets of pure DM at risk for cancer is important. Indeed, Mecoli et al. [94] found 16 patients with cancer-associated pure DM with either anti-NXP2, anti-Mi2 and anti-SAE antibodies, as opposed to 36 patients with cancer and anti-TIF1γ antibodies, whereas Fiorentino et al. [95] found that anti-TIF1γ and anti-NXP2 accounted for 83% of cancer-associated DM. Moreover, in the present study, cancer was noted in 50% of patients ≥ 50 years with anti-Mi2 or anti-SAE pure DM. Taken altogether, these data illustrate that patients with non-TIF1γ autoantibodies can have cancer, arguing for enhanced cancer screening in the presence of these autoantibodies.

In a study of muscle pathology according to autoantibody subtypes [55] not tested by the Myositis Profile 4, only 3% of patients with pure DM were found to be seronegative. In stark contrast, sensitivities for anti-TIF1γ [87, 96, 97] and anti-NXP2 antibodies [98] were suboptimal with the Myositis Profile 4 assay and therefore, as shown in the present study, seronegative pure DM should be approached suspiciously in patients ≥ 50 years.

Limitations of this study are: a retrospective design emphasizing descriptive findings, a small sample size underpowered for some statistical analysis, missing autoantibody results in patients with pure DM (as autoantibody testing evolved over the study period and some older cases lacked extended serology by Myositis Profile 4), limited access until recently to advances in muscle pathology, and referral biases (i.e. inclusion of patients seen by rheumatologists in an academic center, inclusion of hospitalized patients, inclusion of patients referred for IMNM when anti-HMGCR testing became available, absence of breast cancer in pure DM, and an apparent lack of referral in rheumatology when cancer treatment preceded the diagnosis of AIM). Also, the study did not address cancer risk in patients with pure DM aged between 40 and 49 years. Strengths of the study include objective documentation at myositis diagnosis of moderate to severe oropharyngeal dysphagia, studying the link between cancer risk and extent of muscle weakness in patients ≥ 50 years with pure DM, inclusion of patients with a DM rash and no muscle weakness and the use of an updated and long awaited subclassification of AIM that distinguishes ASyS, but also anti-MDA-5 syndrome and scleromyositis, from pure DM. Future prospective studies in anti-TIF1γ pure DM stratified by age, need for hospitalization and anti-Sp4 and/or anti-CCAR1 autoantibody status will inform on a link between the degree of muscle weakness and cancer and/or advanced cancer.

Conclusion

While pure DM accounted for half of the cases of objective oropharyngeal dysphagia and most cancers in this study, anti-MDA-5 syndrome and ASyS together represented 7% of cases and were not associated with cancer. Recognizing scleromyositis, anti-MDA-5 syndrome and ASyS as distinct from pure DM improves risk stratification for cancer screening. In patients ≥ 50 years with pure DM, severe oropharyngeal dysphagia is strongly associated with cancer, suggesting a paraneoplastic myopathy with an ineffective anticancer immune response.
