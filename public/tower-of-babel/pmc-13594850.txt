Dopamine modulation of motor and implicit learning contributions to auditory temporal attention
Authors: Arnaud Zalta, Lena-Franziska Spitz, Alfons Schnitzler, Benjamin Morillon, Esther Florin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAuditory periodic temporal attention operates optimally around 1.5 Hz when temporal structure is judged explicitly. Synchronized rhythmic movements further enhance its efficacy, an effect thought to depend on basal ganglia dopamine, crucial for both motor and temporal processing. However, whether dopamine underpins the relationship between auditory temporal attention and motor activity remains unclear. Using an auditory temporal attention task in Parkinson’s disease (PD) patients ON versus OFF the dopaminergic medication L-DOPA, alongside healthy controls, we found no evidence that dopamine modulates the ∼1.5 Hz optimal sampling rate of auditory temporal attention. However, dopamine enables motor synchronization to the auditory stream and is hence crucial for the motor benefits of auditory temporal attention. Performing the first session ON L-DOPA also improved performance in the following session. These findings underscore a selective role for dopamine in audio-motor interaction, with no evidence that the intrinsic rhythm of auditory attention depends on dopamine.

Introduction

To pay attention in time, in other words our temporal attention is essential for capturing relevant information and adapting behavior to the external world. Temporal attention plays a key role in predicting and adjusting to dynamic external stimuli. However, unlike the continuous flow of sensory input, temporal attention—even though flexible—is constrained and rhythmic, a feature observed both behaviorally and neurophysiologically.1,2,3,4

Auditory temporal attention and motor processes share similar temporal constraints, with optimal processing observed at ∼1.5–2 Hz.1,5,6,7,8 Engaging in a motor act during an auditory temporal attention task like tracking a beat further enhances performance.1,9,10,11,12,13 Notably, even in the absence of overt movement, tasks involving temporal attention or time perception recruit motor cortical areas.9,12,14,15,16 Temporal attention, grounded in active sensing, would thus structure perception as periodic sampling of sensory information over time. However, the precise neural correlates of this audio-motor interaction remain unclear, and whether these shared temporal constraints arise from a common neural substrate has yet to be determined.

Dopamine plays a central role in action, motivation, and cognitive functions such as learning and prediction.17,18,19,20 The substantia nigra pars compacta, a key dopaminergic structure, contributes to motor control and regulates fine motor skills.21,22,23 Dopamine is also essential for temporal processing, with the nigrostriatal pathway—including the basal ganglia and striatum—supporting both motor and timing functions.14,24,25,26 Disrupting the midbrain dopaminergic network impairs time estimation,26,27,28,29,30 and dopaminergic antagonists reduce timing accuracy.31,32

In Parkinson’s disease (PD), characterized by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons, the core symptoms are motor-related, including akinesia, tremor, and bradykinesia.33,34 Fine motor skills are also impaired in this pathology independently of this classical symptomatology.34,35 However, beyond these motor deficits, cognitive symptoms such as disruptions in decision-making, goal-directed behavior,36,37 and time perception38,39,40,41,42,43 are reported. Mild cognitive impairment in PD patients is well documented and affects a broad range of functions, including learning and attention.34,44,45,46,47,48 Although both motor and cognitive symptoms are prominent in PD, their relationship remains complex and not fully understood. Some studies report correlations between the severity of motor symptoms and the extent of cognitive decline and even identify potential neurophysiological markers of this association. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether motor deficits directly contribute to cognitive deterioration,49,50,51,52 and more specifically how they affect periodic temporal attention.

To examine how periodic auditory temporal attention interacts with motor function and dopaminergic modulation, we tested PD patients both ON and OFF dopaminergic medication L-DOPA, alongside age-matched healthy controls. Participants performed an auditory temporal attention task—judging whether a deviant was on or off-beat at the end of an auditory sequence containing temporally irregular distractors—under two conditions: listening and active motor tracking. In the listening condition, all groups exhibited comparable performance accuracy and shared the same optimal sampling rate, suggesting that internal temporal attention constraints remain preserved despite dopamine depletion. In contrast, motor tracking enhanced performance in healthy controls and PD patients when ON-DOPA, but not when OFF-DOPA, indicating that dopamine is necessary for motor-related performance gains. Finally, administering L-DOPA during a first session led to improved performance in a subsequent OFF-medication session, whereas starting OFF-medication did not produce such gains. These findings indicate that dopamine is not only essential for motor facilitation during auditory temporal attention but may also contribute to learning across sessions.

Results

Parkinson’s disease does not affect auditory temporal attention capacities

Participants completed two conditions in this task. A listening condition, in which they performed the task while staying completely still throughout each trial. Participants performed also a tracking condition, in which they tapped the beat in phase, with their index finger (see STAR Methods). Therefore, the absence or presence of overt movement was the single difference between the two conditions. While it is not possible to control for the covert involvement of motor and/or premotor structures during temporal attention tasks, comparing listening and tracking conditions allows us to quantify the influence of overt (relative to covert) motor activity on the precision of temporal attention.

To investigate whether dopamine dysfunction impacts auditory periodic temporal attention, PD patients and age-matched healthy participants performed the same experiment. Age and dopamine modulation do not affect the optimal rate of auditory temporal attention

We then investigated whether dopamine modulation affects the optimal rate of auditory temporal attention in this task. First, performance varied significantly with tempo across all conditions (listening and motor tracking), participant groups (healthy controls and PD patients), and sessions (ON- and OFF-DOPA; Friedman tests: all χ2(3) > 19, all p < 0.001). This notably generalizes our previous result in an elderly population, that auditory periodic temporal attention is rate restricted.

Then, for each condition (listening and motor tracking) and group (healthy controls and PD patients), the optimal rate was estimated by fitting participants’ accuracy across tempi (see STAR Methods). The optimal beat frequency estimated with individual fits was around 1.5 Hz in both conditions and groups (Figure 1E) and did not differ significantly across conditions (listening vs. motor tracking: healthy controls: Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test: Z = 0.45, p = 0.65, BF = 0.43; PD patients: Z = 0.69, p = 0.49, BF = 0.39) nor across groups (unpaired Mann-Whitney test: U = 140, p = 0.37, r = 0.15, BF = 0.49). Overall, these results confirm previous findings that periodic auditory temporal attention is rate restricted and optimal around 1.5 Hz.

To investigate whether L-DOPA medication has an impact on the auditory temporal attention capacities of PD patients, we made the distinction between sessions where patients were ON- or OFF-DOPA medication. We first observed that the optimal beat frequency estimated with individual fits was around 1.5 Hz in all (ON- or OFF-DOPA and listen or motor tracking condition) sessions and did not differ significantly across them (Friedman test: χ2(3) = 0.48, p = 0.9; Figure S1A).

Dopamine modulation of motor contribution to auditory temporal attention

We then investigated whether motor activity helps synchronize temporal fluctuations of attention with the timing of events in a task-relevant stream by comparing the listening and motor tracking conditions in both healthy controls and PD patients. We observed a significant increase in performance accuracy during motor tracking compared to listening conditions, across groups of participants and tempi (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test: Z = 3.63, p < 0.001). For PD patients, when ON-DOPA, we observed such a significant increase in performance accuracy (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test: Z = 2.53, p = 0.011; Figures 2A and S1B), but this was not the case when the same patients performed the same experiment OFF-DOPA (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test: Z = 0.26, p = 0.80, BF = 0.33; Figures 2A and S1C). More precisely, post-hoc tests indicated that overt motor tracking significantly increased performance when healthy control participants performed the task between 0.6 and 2.2 Hz (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test: 0.6 Hz: Z = 3.00, p = 0.003; 1.5 Hz: Z = 2.41, p = 0.016; 2.2 Hz: Z = 3.79, p < 0.001; 3.5 Hz: Z = 0.15, p = 0.88, BF = 0.24; Figure 2B). The motor tracking benefit was maximal at 2.2 Hz, replicating previous findings. For PD patients in the ON-DOPA state, this motor tracking benefit was also maximal and significant at 2.2 Hz (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test: Z = 3.30, p = 0.001; other tempi: all Z < 1.73, all p > 0.08 all BF < 0.56; Figure 2C).

We finally tested whether the motor tracking benefit observed in PD patients in the ON-DOPA state correlated with their L-DOPA challenge dosage, MDS-unified PD rating scale (UPDRS) part 3 scores, and disease duration since diagnosis, all assessed during the experimental session. To note, the MDS-UPDRS part 3 score measures the severity of PD by rating motor symptoms across multiple domains. Higher scores indicate more severe impairment.53 There was a significant negative correlation between motor tracking benefit and L-DOPA challenge dosage (Pearson correlation, r (15) = −0.50, p = 0.04; Figure S4C), indicating that higher doses were associated with reduced motor tracking benefit. No significant correlation was found between motor tracking benefit and MDS-UPDRS III scores measured ON-DOPA state (Pearson correlation, r (15) = 0.12, p = 0.66; Figure S4B), or disease duration since diagnosis (Pearson correlation, r (15) = −0.23, p = 0.39; Figure S4A). Moreover, neither task performance nor tapping divergence from the presented rhythm (assessed in the tracking condition only) was significantly correlated with MDS-UPDRS III scores in any task condition (tracking or listening) or dopaminergic state (ON- or OFF-DOPA) (Pearson correlations, all r(15) < 0.39, all p > 0.12). Similarly, neither task performance nor tapping divergence was significantly correlated with tremor or bradykinesia subscores in either the ON- or OFF-DOPA state (Pearson correlations, all r(15) < 0.54, all p > 0.27).

Dopamine contribution to motor adaptation

We next investigated participants’ ability to adapt their motor acts to the tempo of the auditory stream by comparing the tempo of their tapping movements to that of the concurrently presented rhythm. We computed the tapping divergence—the deviation between the presented and reproduced tempi (see STAR Methods)—and compared it between groups and sessions. Patients in the ON-DOPA state were able to match the presented tempi similarly to healthy controls (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test: z = 0.46, p = 0.64, BF = 0.34, Cohen’s d = 0.18; Figures 3A, 3B, and 3C). In contrast, in the OFF-DOPA state, patients showed a clear difficulty in adapting their movements to the tempo of the external auditory stimulus. Tapping divergence from the beat in the OFF-DOPA state significantly diverged from that of healthy controls (unpaired Mann-Whitney test: z = −3.59, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.48; Figures 3A and 3D). It also significantly differed from patients’ tapping divergence in the ON-DOPA state (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test: t = −5.88, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.77). Patients in the OFF-DOPA state tapped essentially at a frequency of around 1.5 Hz, independently of the stimulus tempo. Dopamine contribution to implicit learning

Finally, we examined whether the order in which PD patients performed the task—first ON-DOPA then OFF-DOPA, or the reverse—had any effect on the results. Our rationale was that learning effects often occur across repeated sessions of the same task, and L-DOPA intake during the first participation could positively modulate this effect. PD patients were therefore divided into two groups: those who began in the ON-DOPA state (n = 8; and performed the second session OFF-DOPA) and those who started in the OFF-DOPA state (n = 9; and performed the second session ON-DOPA). We calculated the change in overall performance (across tempi and conditions) between the first and second session to assess learning and the potential influence of medication.

We observed a significant learning effect in both listening and motor tracking conditions for patients who began ON-DOPA (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test: listening: Z = 2.52, p = 0.012; motor tracking: Z = 2.38, p = 0.017). In contrast, we didn’t find any learning effect in patients who started OFF-DOPA (listening: Z = 0.06, p = 0.95, BF = 0.23; motor tracking: Z = 1.48, p = 0.14, BF = 0.89). This learning effect was similar between the listening and motor tracking conditions (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test, listening vs. motor tracking conditions: ON-DOPA first: Z = 1.68, p = 0.09, BF = 0.40; OFF-DOPA first: Z = 1.36, p = 0.17, BF = 0.63) and significantly different in the ON-DOPA-first group compared to the OFF-DOPA-first group (unpaired Mann-Whitney test: W = 100, p = 0.006; Figures 4 and S2). Discussion

How does dopamine affect the audio-motor synergy in periodic temporal attention? Here, we extend our understanding of the behavioral and neurophysiological bases of audio-motor interaction by demonstrating that: (1) we found no evidence that dopamine modulates the intrinsic rhythm of auditory temporal attention (Figure 1E); (2) L-DOPA restores the behavioral synergy between auditory temporal attention and motor acts in patients with PD (Figure 2); (3) Dopamine affects fine motor performance: PD patients in the ON-DOPA state synchronize their motor acts with auditory stimuli like healthy controls, unlike in the OFF-DOPA state (Figure 3); (4) Dopamine may promote implicit learning: only PD patients in the ON-DOPA state during the first session showed across-session improvement in our task (Figure 4), although sleep between sessions may also contribute to this effect.

These results extend previous studies on the relationship between dopamine, motor acts, time estimation, and attention,25,26,32,54 and are consistent with findings showing that cortical areas classically associated with motor control are also engaged in timing and temporal attention functions.16,55,56 Some models specifically linked the basal ganglia and dopaminergic pathways with the motor cortical area as part of the time estimation mechanism.14 Using PD patients as a model of dopamine depletion, we found that the perceptual benefit of overt motor activity disappears when dopamine is lacking during our temporal attention task. In contrast, in healthy controls or PD patients in the ON-DOPA state, overtly tracking the beat during the experiment enhances performance.1,10 However, as our results are purely behavioral, we cannot directly infer dopaminergic modulation of audio-motor integration. We instead show that dopamine is crucial for explicit motor synchronization, which enhances auditory temporal attention. To note, even if we may not exclude covert motor activity in the passive condition, the comparison with the active condition reveals a motor-specific enhancement of performance, distinct from attentional dynamics—an effect that seems to be driven by a common mechanism in both healthy participants and PD patients in the ON-DOPA state. Dopamine, thus, could be crucial for the interactions between motor processes and high cognitive functions such as auditory temporal attention. Coupled oscillator models have been used to describe how synchronization between these systems can emerge, suggesting that motor-attention synergy may underlie the observed effects. Supporting this, coherence analysis in PD patients shows that dopamine enhances cortical and muscular connectivity during bilateral gait control.57 Reduced connectivity between attention and sensorimotor networks has also been associated with mild cognitive impairment in PD patients.58 However, further neural investigation is needed to address this question, as our behavioral study cannot disentangle the motor disruption effect from the potential role of dopamine in audio-motor connectivity.

We didn’t find a difference in task difficulty between PD patients and healthy controls, in contrast with some previous findings26,28 reporting performance deficits in PD patients in periodic timing tasks. Notably, our cohort was at a predominantly early-to-moderate stage (mean MDS-UPDRS ≈ 36 OFF / 22 ON; Table 1), so a relatively preserved level of impairment may have limited our sensitivity to baseline group differences; deficits could become apparent in more advanced patients. Our results may reflect preserved attentional capacities in PD patients, particularly when symptoms are chronically managed with L-DOPA, as suggested by some studies, despite reports of broader cognitive impairments.59,60,61,62 One possible explanation for the overall lack of difference in attentional performance between ON and OFF L-DOPA states is that chronic L-DOPA treatment may balance the synaptic degradation typically seen in PD.34 Dopamine has been shown to support synaptic maintenance and regeneration, helping to preserve connectivity.63,64 To note, our within-subject design ensures that potential biases from dopamine agonists are controlled, as only the L-DOPA condition varied between sessions. This allows us to isolate the specific effects of L-DOPA on our task. Moreover, we found no evidence that dopamine depletion affects the intrinsic frequency of temporal attention. Although only a limited number of tempi were tested—reflecting a trade-off between experimental resolution and clinical feasibility—our findings reliably replicate previous results obtained in healthy participants using a broader range of tempi. This replication supports the robustness of the present findings. Notably, this ∼1.5 Hz optimum was established with an explicit, beat-based judgment; whether the same rate governs implicit timing, where temporal regularities guide behavior without being explicitly judged, remains open. Instead, dopamine depletion selectively reduces motor-driven performance gains in our task. Future studies could investigate in more detail the effect of L-DOPA on the performance of Parkinson’s patients in relation to the “slowing internal clock” hypothesis in PD. Dopamine could be a key modulator not only of motor processes but also of the interaction between motor and cognitive processes. Interestingly, the reported ability to use the auditory rhythm in combination with the motor act to improve perception aligns with studies reporting gait improvement with auditory stimulation, suggesting its perceptual and motor benefit in a Parkinson’s population.66,67,68,69,70,71,72 This mechanism likely fails in PD patients due to motor symptoms33,34 that impair their ability to move in phase with attentional dynamics. This interpretation is supported by the increased tapping divergence observed in PD patients in the OFF-DOPA state. These results align with prior work and suggest dopamine optimizes motor flexibility, balancing rhythmic entrainment and adaptive control.73,74 A similar absence of modulation has been reported when motor tracking is deliberately decoupled from attention.10 Biologically, the motor recovery observed with L-DOPA may result from an acute effect driven by increased dopamine levels. But further studies are needed to disentangle the chronic effects from the acute motor benefits of L-DOPA.

Of note, we found no correlation between clinical scores, such as the MDS-UPDRS part 3, and the patients’ performance, tapping accuracy or motor tracking benefit observed in the task. Instead, the behavioral metric correlated negatively only with L-DOPA challenge dosage, which is expected since medication levels typically increase with symptom severity. The absence of a link between clinical and behavioral measures suggests that our task captures motor-cognitive interactions not reflected in standard clinical assessments. However, given our relatively small sample size, these correlations (or the absence of them) should be interpreted with caution. A larger sample is needed to robustly test clinical hypotheses and enable comparisons of demographic and clinical characteristics within the Parkinsonian population. This task could offer a complementary clinical tool to assess sensorimotor impairments in PD that are not fully captured by standard evaluations like the MDS-UPDRS. Nonetheless, our findings highlight the potential of this task to isolate specific motor-attention processes that warrant further investigation.

Finally, PD patients OFF-DOPA state during their first experimental participation showed no learning benefit in the second participation (Figures 4 and S2B). In contrast, when PD patients performed the task first ON-DOPA state, a learning effect was observed, indicating that training could improve performance (Figures 4 and S2A). These results also support the presence of implicit learning deficits in PD patients, a finding that has been debated in the literature.75,76 This implicit learning effects, influenced by L-DOPA, could explain why OFF-DOPA performance sometimes matched control performance or even seems to outperform ON-DOPA on average (Figure 2). This pattern is more consistent with a learning state effect rather than with a simple ceiling effect after practice. Relatedly, because task difficulty was challenging to adjust for some patients to achieve the target performance level (∼75% at 2 Hz), we focused on within-subject comparisons rather than absolute performance levels. As performance remained above chance and below ceiling, this allowed reliable assessment of relative differences across conditions and tempi. Importantly, because our experimental manipulation assessed the acute effects of dopaminergic medication over a short timeframe, these findings should be interpreted in the context of the short-duration response to levodopa rather than the long-duration response associated with chronic treatment.77 A limitation of the study is that PD patients who first performed the task in the ON-DOPA state completed the second session the following day, while those who started in the OFF-DOPA state performed both sessions on the same day. Since sleep can also positively influence learning,78,79 further investigation is needed to clarify whether the observed learning effect stems from dopaminergic modulation, sleep-related consolidation, or both—especially given that our results are consistent with all these explanations.

Conclusion

In sum, our results indicate that the dopaminergic system is not essential for auditory periodic temporal attention itself but plays a key role in modulating the motor contribution to auditory attention and in learning within this task. Dopamine thus appears crucial for regulating motor and learning processes and their interaction with cognitive functions such as auditory perception and temporal attention.

Limitations of the study

Our findings are based on behavioral measures, so they cannot directly speak to dopaminergic modulation of audio-motor connectivity, which would require complementary neural investigation. The relatively small sample size also invites some caution when interpreting the reported correlations. Only a limited number of tempi were tested, reflecting a trade-off between experimental resolution and clinical feasibility. Finally, our design captures the acute response to L-DOPA, and future work could usefully consider other neuromodulatory systems, such as the cholinergic system, in temporal and attentional processing in PD.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Twenty-six individuals with Parkinson’s disease (mean age = 66.5 years, range = 54–80 years; 24% female) and twenty healthy, age- and sex-matched control participants (mean age = 64.8 years, range = 52–86 years; 20% female) were recruited. There were no significant group differences in age (unpaired Mann-Whitney test: z = 0.88, p = 0.38) or sex distribution (Independance chi-square test: χ2(2) = 0.31, p = 0.86). Finally, nine PD patients were excluded from the analysis because they did not complete the entire experiment. In some cases, the session was terminated early for clinical reasons, particularly during the OFF-DOPA state, while in others, patients chose not to complete the OFF-DOPA session. To view population details, please refer to Table 1. The experiment followed the local ethics guidelines of Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, and was approved by the local ethics committee (study no. 2019-488_1). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before the experiments. All participants—both healthy controls and individuals with Parkinson’s disease—had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing. Healthy participants additionally reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. We did not select participants based on musical training, and a short survey administered at the end of the experiment indicated that none were professional musicians. To be included in our study, individuals with Parkinson’s disease had to be over 40 years old, capable of providing informed consent, and able to understand the study protocol. PD patients were randomly assigned to begin the protocol in either the OFF- or ON-DOPA state. To note, MDS-UPDRS III scores ON and OFF medication were assessed on the day of the experiment – after the experiment was completed – and rated by a movement disorders specialist. Exclusion criteria include the presence of additional severe neurological, psychiatric, or systemic diseases; reduced cognitive abilities; significant visual impairments that would prevent participation in the paradigm; intolerance to Levodopa; or being left-handed (all patients performed the task using their right hand for responses and their left hand for tapping). Patients were not screened based on symptom severity, clinical history, or medication dosage. We excluded only patients with an implanted deep brain stimulator. To ensure the OFF-DOPA state, patients were tested between 8 and 9 AM, having skipped their evening and morning dose. This guaranteed at least 12 hours without L-DOPA medication.

Method details

Experimental design

The experiment was adapted from Zalta et al. (2020).1 Auditory stimuli were sampled at 44 100 Hz and presented diotically -Identical sounds were presented to both ears- at a comfortable hearing level via headphones in a quiet room, using the Psychophysics-3 toolbox80 and additional custom scripts written for MATLAB (The Mathworks). The instructions were displayed visually on a mid-gray background of a laptop screen situated at a viewing distance of 50 cm. The screen had a spatial resolution of 1920 by 1080 pixels and a vertical refresh rate of 60 Hz. On each trial, participants had to fixate a cross, located at the center of the screen, to get a constant visual stimulation. Each trial consisted of a sequence of pure tones, qualified as reference, targets, and distractors (Figure 1A). Three reference tones defining the beat of the sequence preceded a mixture of on-beat (target) and off-beat (distractor) tones. Participants performed a beat discrimination task at the end of each trial, by deciding whether the last tone of the sequence, a spectral deviant (785 Hz vs. 660 Hz), was on or off beat. Responses were given by pressing the upper arrow key for “on-beat” and the lower arrow key for “off-beat” on the keyboard. We calculated participants’ performance as the proportion of correct responses relative to the total number of trials for each factor (tempo, tracking, or listening condition) and used these values to compare performance across experimental conditions. While on-beat stimuli were providing the beat, crucially, off-beat stimuli had a distracting influence. This interleaved delivery of sensory events forced participants to track the beat throughout the entire duration of the sequence while minimising the interference of aperiodic events. This protocol thus ensured that their attentional focus was temporally modulated over an extended time period. To note, because target and distractor tones differed only in their temporal position relative to the beat, participants could not perform the final discrimination based solely on the last tones of the sequence; successful performance required continuous tracking of the beat throughout the trial.

This design implies that the last inter-stimulus interval (ISI) did not always correspond to the beat period of the trial. Distractors could occur between the last target tone and the deviant. Tone frequencies were selected to avoid potential bone transmission (660/785 Hz). 4 beat frequencies (or tempi) of 0.6, 1.5, 2.2, and 3.5 Hz were presented to the participant to span the entire range of discernible beats.81,82 We orthogonalized tempi and tone duration by fixing tone duration to 22.5 ms. Tones dampening length was 10% of their duration, and tone attenuation was 40 dB. Trials had pseudo-random durations (∼2–12 s) and, for each tempo, contained at least four target tones (up to 22), excluding reference and deviant tones. These constraints were chosen to enable the deployment of temporal attention in all tempi. The density of distractors per sequence (i.e., the number of distractors per beat) was titrated individually (see below). Distractors appeared randomly between targets, with the constraint that all ISI within the sequence should be of at least 9% of the beat period (e.g., ISI > 90 ms for a 1 Hz beat). This ensured that tones did not appear concomitantly. The deviant target was occurring either on or off beat (50%). When the deviant was off beat, it appeared randomly within a window corresponding to one beat period and centered around the expected beat. The same constraint applied as for distractors in the sequence: the deviant had to occur at least 9% of the beat period away from the beat.

Participants performed ‘listening’ blocks, in which they executed the task while staying completely still during the duration of the trial, not moving any part of their body. Additionally, participants performed ‘tracking’ blocks, in which they were required to follow the beat by moving their (left or right, at their convenience) index finger, on a noiseless pad or on a key to the experiment’s laptop keyboard, from the beginning of the sequence (the 2nd reference tone). In essence, the tracking condition is a variation of the synchronisation-continuation paradigm. For PD patients, two listening and tracking conditions were performed, one with (ON) and one without (OFF) L-DOPA medication. For the OFF-L-DOPA state, patients were recorded in the clinically defined medication OFF state following withdrawal of dopaminergic medication overnight for at least 12 hours prior to study completion. For the ON L-DOPA state, patients were measured in their best clinical ON state. The ON- and OFF-DOPA states occurred on different days, if the ON measurement was performed first. Otherwise, the ON measurement was made after the OFF-state measurement and at least 30 minutes after the intake of up to 1.5 times their morning L-DOPA dosage in the form of a fast-acting dopamine. Performance was therefore compared between the ON-DOPA-first and OFF-DOPA-first subgroups to investigate the effect of dopamine on learning.

Each participant started the experiment with a short training block. The beat frequency was fixed to 2 Hz, and the density of distractors was at first equal to zero and increased progressively up to 0.4 distractors per beat. Participants were instructed not to move during the trials (as in the listening condition). Then, participants listened to the 4 tempi at least 1 time each. Following this short training block, participants performed a psychophysical staircase where the density of distractors was the varying parameter. The staircase started with a minimum distractor-to-target ratio of 1:3 and could increase up to a maximum ratio of 3:1. Every six trials, the number of distractors was adjusted by 30%, increasing or decreasing task difficulty according to the participant’s performance. The staircase consisted of 80 trials, allowing performance to reach a stable plateau, and was calibrated to achieve approximately 75% categorization accuracy at a 2 Hz tempo. This tempo was specifically chosen for training and the staircase, but was not included in the main experiment, to prevent learning or adaptation effects that might have selectively enhanced performance for any of the four tempi presented. We selected 2 Hz because it corresponds to the spontaneous rate of natural rhythmic movements and to optimal performance in timing tasks1,6,81,82; calibrating difficulty at a faster, more demanding tempo would have saturated performance at the slower tempi. Participants performed 40 trials per tempo per condition. Tempi were pseudo-randomly alternating in blocks of 20 trials, each with or without tracking demand. Feedback was provided after each trial to indicate correct/incorrect responses, and more general performance feedback, indicating the total number of correct responses was given after every block, for motivational purposes. Trials with outlier reaction times—more than two standard deviations from the participant’s mean within each condition and tempo—were excluded, with a maximum of 20 trials removed per participant.

Timing of motor acts in the tracking condition

In the tracking condition, individual taps were recorded on a noiseless pad or a laptop key. Tap timing was measured from the onset of the sequence (second reference tone), and inter-tap intervals were computed for each trial. The standard deviation of these intervals was used to quantify temporal variability, providing a precise sampling of tapping performance.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Estimation of an optimal beat frequency

To estimate the tempo at which performance would be maximal (/minimal), variations of performance across tempi were approximated at the individual level with a third-order polynomial function f(x) = ax3 + bx2 + cx + d, and the coordinates of the local maxima α (/minima β) were extracted according to the functionsα=-b-δ3a;β=-b+δ3awhere δ=b2-3ac with δ > 0. We used a third-order polynomial function, as it is the best (i.e., most flexible) model that allows estimating one maximum without ambiguity (higher order models accept multiple maxima). A second-order function was used for visualisation purposes when fitting the group-level performance profile.

Statistical procedures

All analyses were performed at the single-subject level and followed by standard non-parametric two-sided tests at the group level (Wilcoxon paired signed-rank, unpaired Mann-Whitney or Friedman tests). These procedures reduce the risk of violating assumptions inherent to parametric tests and are well suited for detecting ordinal or monotonic relationships commonly examined in behavioral data. The divergence in participants’ tapping from the tempo of the presented sequences was calculated using the norm of the difference between their tapping frequency and the beat frequency.
