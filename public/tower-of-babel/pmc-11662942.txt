The conformation of FOXM1 homodimers in vivo is crucial for regulating transcriptional activities
Authors: Chia-Chan Hsu, Xiang Yao, Shang-Yao Chen, Tsui-Chun Tsuo, I-Ching Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractConformational changes in a transcription factor can significantly affect its transcriptional activity. The activated form of the FOXM1 transcription factor regulates the transcriptional network of genes essential for cell cycle progression and carcinogenesis. However, the mechanism and impact of FOXM1 conformational change on its transcriptional activity in vivo throughout the cell cycle progression remain unexplored. Here, we demonstrate that FOXM1 proteins form novel intermolecular homodimerizations in vivo, and these conformational changes in FOXM1 homodimers impact activity during the cell cycle. Specifically, during the G1 phase, FOXM1 undergoes autorepressive homodimerization, wherein the αβα motif in the C-terminal transcriptional activation domain interacts with the ββαβ motif in the N-terminal repression domain, as evidenced by FRET imaging. Phosphorylation of the αβα motif by PLK1 at S715/S724 disrupts ββαβ–αβα hydrophobic interactions, thereby facilitating a conserved αβα motif switch binding partner to the novel intrinsically disordered regions, leading to FOXM1 autostimulatory homodimerization persisting from the S phase to the G2/M phase in vivo. Furthermore, we identified a minimal ββαβ motif peptide that effectively inhibits cancer cell proliferation both in cell culture and in a mouse tumor model, suggesting a promising autorepression approach for targeting FOXM1 in cancer therapy.

Introduction

Transcription factors (TFs) are pivotal in controlling gene expression by binding to specific responsive elements (RE) in promoters through their DNA-binding domain (DBD) coupled with a transactivation domain (TAD). The activity of TFs is finely regulated through various mechanisms, ultimately influencing the expression of the target genes and impacting numerous cellular processes (1–3). The TADs regulate TF activity by recruiting chromatin modifiers, coactivator complexes or basal transcriptional machinery either directly or indirectly through diverse protein–protein interactions. Virtually TAD exhibits an acidic nature and harbor intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), which are thought to prevent non-specific DNA binding and enhance folding flexibility during interactions with various partners through phase separation (4–6). Moreover, TFs could interact with identical molecules or members of their own gene family to form higher-order functional homodimers or heterodimers, allowing TFs to modulate DNA-binding specificity, affinity, and transcription complexes, thereby regulating transcriptional activity in many eukaryotes, exemplified by STAT family proteins (7–9). These protein–protein interactions involving the TAD or DBD of TFs can be influenced by post-translational modifications (PTMs), which consequently alter the assembly of the transcription preinitiation complexes and the binding of TFs to RE. Recent studies showed that IDRs modulate TF dimerization, ensuring proper functionality, although the precise role across all TFs remains to be fully elucidated (10).

FOXM1, a member of the Forkhead box (Fox) family of transcription factors, is characterized by the presence of a winged helix–turn–helix/Forkhead DBD, which is flanked by an N-terminal repression domain (NRD) and a predominantly disordered C-terminal TAD (11–15). Foxm1 is a proliferation-specific transcriptional activator that is expressed in a diverse range of mitotic cells examined in various biological processes, including embryogenesis (16–18), tissue regeneration (19–21) and almost all types of cancers. Genetic studies showed that knockout of Foxm1 alleles in mouse embryos leads to lethal defects in liver and heart development, attributed to dysregulated cell-cycle progression, resulting in a reduction of cell proliferation and the accumulation of polyploid cardiomyocytes and hepatoblasts (18,22,23). Additionally, Foxm1 depletion in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), human gastric cancer cells and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells led to increased senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity, concomitant with the accumulation of key senescence markers, including p21Cip1, p27Kip1, p16INK4A and p19ARF (24–26). Mechanistically, FOXM1 counteracts cellular senescence by upregulating the anti-senescence gene Bmi1 via c-Myc (27) or repressing the expression of FOXO1 through RB1 and DNMT3B complex (26).

Clinical data that correlates the expression of FOXM1 to poor clinical outcomes further substantiates FOXM1 as a target in tumorigenesis (28–32). Additionally, cancer genetic engineered mouse models have demonstrated that Foxm1 regulates the initiation and progression of cancers, including lung adenocarcinoma (33,34), small cell lung cancer (35), colorectal cancer (36), liver cancer (37,38) and breast cancer (39,40). The implication of FOXM1 is critical for tumorigenesis as its vital role in activating the transcription network of genes essential for the cell cycle, as well as cell migration, metastasis and angiogenesis (25,41). For example, FOXM1 enhances the transcription of genes associated with DNA replication TOP2A and cell cycle entry genes like c-Jun, ATF2 (42), cyclin D1 (43) and CDC25A (25,44). Additionally, FOXM1 upregulates Skp2, leading to the degradation of CDKI proteins p21Cip1 and p27Kip1, allowing the cell to proceed from the G1 to the S phase (25). FOXM1 continues its role in regulating the expression of genes including cyclin B1, CDC25B, CDC25C, Survivin and Aurora B kinase (25). By upregulating these genes, FOXM1 ensures proper progression through the G2 phase. Moreover, FOXM1 promoted the self-renewal of carcinoma cells by inducing pluripotency genes, including Oct4, Sox2 and CD133 (45–48). FOXM1 also activated the transcription of MMP-2 and VEGF to promote cell invasion and angiogenesis, which are critical for progression to the advanced tumor stage (42,49,50).

The activity of FOXM1 is mediated by PTMs and autointeraction. Previous studies indicated that FOXM1 is activated by cyclin E-CDK2-mediated phosphorylation on the C-terminal TAD in the late G1 phase, while Raf/MEK/MAPK pathway-dependent phosphorylation of TAD promotes nuclear translocation and further activation of FOXM1 in the late S phase (51,52). During G2/M, the phosphorylation of FOXM1 is increased by cyclin B-CDK1, which allows the association of TAD with Polo-Like Kinase 1 (PLK1) to stimulate FOXM1 (53,54). Once in contact with FOXM1, PLK1 directly phosphorylates FOXM1 at serine residues 715 and 724 (54). Dysregulation of this process, such as abnormal or excessive phosphorylation of FOXM1 by PLK1, can have significant implications for cell cycle progression and is closely associated with cancer development and progression (55–57). Meanwhile, FOXM1 is stabilized by cyclin D-CDK4/6 in a multisite phosphorylation manner (58). In addition, phosphorylation via cyclin A-CDK1 augments FOXM1 activity by relief of repression mediated by its NRD during the G2 phase (15). Consistently, the deletion of NRD results in a continuously active form of FOXM1, while other studies demonstrated the interaction of FOXM1 with NRD that represses FOXM1 activity (15,59). A recent study further revealed that a structural switch is associated with FOXM1 autorepression (60); however, the lack of understanding of FOXM1 TAD poses challenges to connecting FOXM1 autointeraction and activation. In this study, we identified a conserved structural motif within FOXM1 TAD that plays a crucial role in the formation of FOXM1 homodimers. When phosphorylated by PLK1, this structural motif switches its binding partner, leading to diverse autointeraction modes and conformational changes that impact FOXM1’s transcriptional activity throughout the cell cycle progression.

Materials and methods

Prediction of the potential protein secondary structure of FOXM1

The amino acid sequence of human FOXM1b (NP_973732.1) was submitted to the Jpred online server to identify the conservative sequences and potentially structured regions (61). The server automatically mapped 25 or 20 peptide sequences of different species to human FOXM1 protein sequence 51–121 or 328–748 residues, respectively. The UniRef90 ID listed below shows the top 10 peptide sequences mapped to human FOXM1 protein 51–121 residues: UniRef90_F6PQM0, UniRef90_UPI000333243F, UniRef90_G3UTQ0, UniRef90_UPI000443E4AA, UniRef90_H3AHA5, UniRef90_E9Q7Y0, UniRef90_L8Y2A8, UniRef90_UPI0003EADA45, UniRef90_L8HUQ1, UniRef90_UPI00033175B4; or the top 10 peptide sequences mapped to human FOXM1 protein 328–748 aa: UniRef90_I3MQW8, UniRef90_G3Q8W6, UniRef90_UPI0003F18AA6, UniRef90_W5NII9, UniRef90_UPI0003C104C3, UniRef90_K7GDY9, UniRef90_UPI0003AF4094, UniRef90_H2UU57, UniRef90_H3AHA5 and UniRef90_V9KH79.

Cell culture, transfection, lentivirus production and methylthiazol tetrazolium (MTT) cell viability assay

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (#12100–038, Gibco) or Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium (RPMI-1640) (#31800–014, Gibco), supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (#10437–28, Gibco), 100 IU/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin (#SV30010, HyCloneTM) and 2 mM L-glutamine (#SH30034.01, HyCloneTM), was used for culturing human cancer U2OS or A549 cells, respectively, at 37°C in a humidified incubator under a standard gas atmosphere of 5% CO2. The X-tremeGENE HP DNA transfection reagent (#06366546001, Roche) was employed to co-transfect plasmids following the manufacturer's instructions. FOXM1 ββαβ complementary DNA (cDNA) was cloned into pInducer20 (Supplementary Table S1) by the Gateway® system (62). To produce lentiviral particles, 2 × 106 293T cells were plated in a 100-mm dish for 16 h, followed by co-transfection of 4 μg of pInducer constructs, 1 μg of pMD.G and 3 μg of pCMVΔR8.91 (RNAi core, Academia Sinica) for 36 h. Thereafter, the lentivirus-containing medium was replenished and applied to target cells every 12 h for four times. The infected cells were selected using G418 (#A1720, Sigma–Aldrich) at a concentration of 2000 ng/ml for 5 days. For the cell viability assay, stable cell lines were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well and incubated overnight, followed by transduction with medium containing 1 μg/ml doxycycline (DOX) (#631311, Clontech) for 5 days. Cell viability was determined every 24 h by performing an MTT colorimetric assay (#M2128, Sigma–Aldrich) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, stable cell lines were incubated with 0.5 mg/ml MTT reagent for 1 h at 37°C. Metabolized formazan crystals were dissolved with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (#131954, PanReac AppliChem), and then measured at an absorbance of 565 nm using a Synergy™ HTX Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek). All values were calculated from four replicates, and the growth curve was plotted by the function from GraphPad Prism 6.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

A total of 8 × 104 U2OS cells were plated in each well of a 12-well plate to conduct transfection with FOXM1 fragment constructs (Supplementary Table S1), GFP-ΔN-FOXM1 construct, 6×CDX2 promoter luciferase vector and CMV-Renilla luciferase (25,59,63). Lysates were collected 24 h later and reacted with LARII followed by Stop & Glo® reagent of the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay (#E1910, Promega). The Firefly or Renilla luminescence was measured through the Synergy™ HTX Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek) or 20/20n luminometer (Turner Biosystems/Promega). FOXM1 responsive promoter activity was determined by normalization to Renilla luciferase activity. All values were calculated in triplicate.

Protein preparation, immunoprecipitation and western blot

A total of 2.4 × 106 U2OS cells were grown overnight in a 100-mm dish for co-transfection with a total of 5 μg DNA, including green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged and mCherry-tagged FOXM1 constructs (Supplementary Table S1). After 24 h of transfection, the cells were washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and scraped into 1 ml nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction (NETN) lysis buffer [150 mM NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (pH = 8); 20 mM Tris (pH = 8)], 0.5% (v/v) Nonidet®P-40 (NP-40) Substitute (#19626, USB), phosphatase inhibitor cocktail tablets (#04906837001, Roche), protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (#04693124001, Roche) and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (#P7626, Sigma–Aldrich). For immunoprecipitation, 1 mg of whole-cell extracts were incubated with 2 μg of GFP monoclonal antibody (#JL-8, Clontech) at 4°C for 16 h, then applied to 40 μl Pierce Protein G Plus Agarose beads (#22852, Thermo ScientificTM) at 4°C for 2 h. After extensive washing with NETN buffer, protein complexes were eluted from the beads by boiling in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer and subjected to SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). The protein complexes were separated under 120 mV in SDS-PAGE, blotted to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes and probed with mCherry monoclonal antibody (#E5D8F, Cell Signaling Technology). The signal of the primary antibody was amplified by the Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody and detected with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (#Bio-ECL-200, Biokit). Non-reducing PAGE was performed by loading the sample into SDS-PAGE using SDS loading buffer without β-mercaptoethanol, followed by immunoblotting. Native-PAGE was executed under SDS-free conditions, where cellular protein extracts collected in NETN buffer were separated in PAGE with Native-PAGE running buffer, blotted onto PVDF membranes using Bjerrum transfer buffer and probed with FOXM1 antibody (#EB0023, Karafast) as described (35,38).

Yeast two-hybrid assay

FOXM1 cDNA fragments (Supplementary Table S1) were cloned into the Bait vector pBTM116 for expression as fusions to LexA or the Prey vector pGAD424 for expression as fusions to GAL4, which allowed activation of the LexA promoter when co-transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain L40 (MATa ade2 his3 leu2 trp1 LYS::lexA-HIS3 URA3::lexA-LacZ). The Pellet X-gal (PXG) assay was adopted for measuring β-gal reporter activity in the yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) system (64). Briefly, yeasts were co-transformed with Bait and Prey vectors using the lithium acetate method (65) and double-selected for leucine and tryptophan prototrophy on yeast synthetic drop-out agar plates containing a complete amino acid mixture except for leucine and tryptophan. After 2–3 days of incubation at 30°C, colonies were inoculated into the selective medium overnight. Whole yeast protein extracts were prepared by pelleting down the yeasts from a selective medium followed by vortexing with glass beads (G8772, Sigma–Aldrich) and separating the protein supernatants from yeast debris by centrifugation. PXG assays were performed by incubating 20 μg of total yeast proteins and 100 μl PBS containing 500 μg/ml X-gal (#7240–90–6, Sigma–Aldrich) and 0.05% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol in a 96-well plate at room temperature. β-Gal reporter activity was determined by monitoring the color development at different time points. Images were taken using a camera (SONY α NEX-5, Japan).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling assay

The cell apoptosis rate of A549 stable cells was measured by a Click-iT™ Plus terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) Assay (#C10619, Thermo ScientificTM) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, A549 stable cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 104 cells/well in 12-well culture plates with coverslips and cultured overnight. After treatment with 1 μg/ml DOX for 48 h, cells were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA) (#15710, Electron Microscopy Sciences) in PBS, followed by fragmented DNA labeling with Alexa fluorescein-conjugated dUTP by TdT enzyme and nucleus staining with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (#H1200, Vector Laboratories, Inc.). The fluorescence signal was detected with an upright fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axioskop 2 mot plus). All values were calculated based on 10 pictures per sample.

SA-β-gal staining assay

For the SA-β-gal staining assay, 4 × 104 A549 stable cells were plated in each well of a 12-well plate and treated with 1 μg/ml DOX for 96 h. Cellular senescence was detected by incubating the cells with SA-β-gal staining solution [0.1% X-gal, 5 mM potassium ferrocyanide, 5 mM potassium ferricyanide, 150 mM sodium chloride, 2 mM magnesium chloride and 40 mM citric acid/sodium phosphate solution (pH 6.0)] at 37°C following fixation with 4% PFA. The reaction was terminated when the cells were stained blue-green by replacing the SA-β-gal staining solution with PBS. All values were calculated based on 10 microscopic pictures per sample.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

At least 5 × 105 A549 stable cells were collected and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (#15596018, Invitrogen) after induction with 1 μg/ml DOX for 48 h. Genomic DNA was removed from 500 ng RNA by RQ1 RNase-Free DNase (#M6101, Promega) treatment, followed by cDNA synthesis using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (#4368814, Applied Biosystems). Amplification was performed from 500 ng of cDNA using the QuantStudio3 real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) system and Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (#4385610, Applied Biosystems). The reactions were performed in triplicate with the primer pairs shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Construction of DNA vectors for truncated FOXM1 NRD and TAD

The cDNA fragments of FOXM1 NRD or TAD were PCR amplified from pCMV-FOXM1b (#SC128214, OriGene) using the respective primer pairs (Supplementary Table S1) and Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Kit (#F553, Thermo ScientificTM). The resulting PCR products were digested with EcoRI-HF (#R3101, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs) and BamHI (#R3136, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs), and then ligated into mCherry-C1 (#632524, Clontech), EGFP-C2 (#6083–1, Clontech), ECFP-C1 (#6076–1, Clontech), EYFP-C1 (#6006–1, Clontech), pGAD424 (#U07647, Clontech) or pBTM116 (66) vectors using T4 DNA ligase (#M202, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs). The cyan fluorescent protein (CFP) or yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) tagged FOXM1 in transient expression plasmids was subcloned with NdeI (#R0111, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs), MfeI (#R3589, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs) and EcoRI to lentiviral plasmids pLAS2w.Ppuro or pLAS2w.Pneo (National RNAi Core Facility, Taiwan).

Site-directed mutagenesis of FOXM1 plasmids for alanine scanning

Plasmids of fluorescent protein-tagged FOXM1 NRD or TAD were used as templates for generating point-mutated FOXM1 constructs by PCR with the respective primer pairs carrying specific mutations (Supplementary Table S1). The PCR products were treated with DpnI (#R0176, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs) to remove parental template plasmids and were then subjected to transformation into competent cells.

Cell cycle synchronization and live cell fluorescence resonance energy transfer microscopy

U2OS stably cells expressing FOXM1 protein tagged with CFP or YFP were plated on an eight-well chamber slide (#154534PK, Thermo ScientificTM) for synchronization and fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis. The stable cells were arrested in G1/S by incubating in DMEM containing 2mM thymidine (#S4803, Selleckchem) for 17 h, with two cycles of thymidine treatment separated by a 7-h interval. Subsequently, the cells were released from the G1/S block by replacing the culture medium with MEMO® LiveLight™ Media (#M06-100, CELL guidance systems) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, 100 IU/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin and 2 mM L-glutamine immediately before live-cell FRET imaging. The U2OS cells released from double thymidine treatment were collected at the indicated time points for propidium iodide staining followed by flow cytometry analysis (25,41). Live cell imaging was performed using a Nikon T1 inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with an automatic motorized stage and incubator. CFP, YFP and CFP-YFP FRET signals were captured every 30 min after thymidine withdrawal from synchronized U2OS stable cells. Normalized FRET (NFRET) was calculated as described previously (67). Briefly, the background signals were subtracted from the region of interest to obtain the net values for CFP, YFP and FRET for subsequent calculation. The ‘donor model’ function was used to calculate signal bleed through (SBT) from CFP, which was 0.57, and the ‘acceptor model’ was used to obtain the SBT from YFP, which was 0.24. The ‘FRET’ function was then used to output the NFRET value with the following parameters set: Gaussian blur = 2.0 and threshold = 1.0.

Chemically induced autointeraction of FOXM1 and reporter activity assay

FK506-binding protein FKBP12 (FKBP) and the FKBP-rapamycin binding domain of human FRAP (FRB) were used for chemically inducing autointeraction by rapamycin (68). FRB was inserted after the CFP of CFP-FOXM1 to generate FRB–FOXM1 using NdeI (#R0111, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs) and EcoRI-HF restriction enzymes. FOXM1–FKBP was produced by connecting FKBP after FOXM1-YFP using AseI (#R0526, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs) and BsrGI (#R0575, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs). FRBTAD insX–FOXM1 was cloned using AgeI-HF (#R3522, NEW ENGLAND BioLabs), EcoRI-HF and BamHI-HF. U2OS cells were plated in a 12-well plate at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well overnight and transfected with the aforementioned reporter plasmids along with the FRP and/or FKBP-tagged FOXM1 construct. Four hours after transfection, the U2OS cells were cultured in DMEM containing 0.1 nM rapamycin to induce interaction for 6 h. Subsequently, the lysates were collected for dual luciferase reporter (DLR) as described above.

Tumorigenicity assay in nude mice

A total of 2 × 106 A549 cells transduced with either NRD 51–121 6A (mutant) or NRD 51–121 expressing adenovirus at MOI = 10 were resuspended in 100 μl of mixtures containing equal proportions of PBS and Corning Matrigel Matrix (#354234, Corning). Cells were subcutaneously injected into the left or right flank of 8-week-old male BALB/cByJNar nude mice (n = 6) that were provided by the National Laboratory Animal Center, NARLabs, Taiwan. The tumor size was measured twice a week using a caliper. The tumor volume (mm3) was calculated by length (mm) x [width (mm)]2 × 1/2. All mice were sacrificed before their subcutaneous tumor size reached 20 mm in diameter, according to the NTHU IACUC guidelines. BALB/c nude mice used in this experiment were maintained in specific pathogen-free vivarium-filtered cages at the animal facility of the National Health Research Institute under a 12 h light/dark cycle.

Results

FOXM1 activation is associated with TAD–TAD autointeraction

FOXM1 consists of an evolutionarily conserved winged-helix DBD that adopts a structure comprising three α-helices and three β-sheets, enabling interaction with the DNA consensus site. However, apart from the well-defined DBD residues 235–327 (12), the structure and composition of other transcriptional regulator regions of FOXM1 protein have not been fully characterized. Through a Protein Secondary Structure Prediction analysis by Jpred 4, FOXM1 was shown to mainly lack a fixed or ordered structure, except for the DBD and a ββαβ motif (residues 51–121) in the NRD that is involved in inhibiting FOXM1 activity (Figure 1A). Notably, we found that the FOXM1 TAD contains a partially ordered αβα motif region at the end of the C-terminus (residues 696–748) (Figure 1B), along with previously unreported IDRs (residues 328–584), both of which are highly conserved across multiple species (Figure 1C). Consistently, the AlphaFold 3D structure prediction also annotates residues 328–584 in the TAD as an unstructured IDR (Supplementary Figure S1A–C) (14). Yet, the role of the conserved IDR in mediating FOXM1 activity remains unexplored. To determine the structural ordered and disordered regions of TAD involved in FOXM1 transactivation, constructs expressing GFP-fused TAD fragments were co-transfected with GFP-fused human FOXM1b NRD-deletion mutant (ΔN-FOXM1, the residues 229–748, representing the constitutively active form) for a 6xCDX2-LUC DLR assay. Surprisingly, the co-expression of FOXM1 GFP-TAD 328–748 and ΔN-FOXM1, both lacking NRD to presumably avoid protein autorepression, resulted in a robust increase in FOXM1 activity (Figure 1D and Supplementary Figure S1I). Specifically, we observed a significant reduction in ΔN-FOXM1 transcriptional activity when co-expressing TAD fragments that lacked either the αβα motif (TAD 328–695) or IDR (TAD 585–748) (Figure 1D). The co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay revealed that the structural αβα (residues 696–748) interacted with IDR regions (residues 328–411 and 328–584) (Figure 1E), suggesting that the superactivated FOXM1 is associated with autointeraction between the αβα motif and the IDR within the TAD, independent of the NRD. Moreover, chemically induced dimerization (CID) of FKBP and FRB by rapamycin treatment forced FOXM1–FOXM1 interaction via TAD αβα–IDR proximity, as shown by native PAGE (Figure 1F), resulting in an increase in FOXM1 activity, as demonstrated by the DLR assay (Figure 1G). In contrast, the residue 696–748 deletion (Δαβα-FOXM1) did not exhibit a detectable dimer signal on native PAGE (Figure 1F). Accordingly, we deduced that FOXM1 autoactivation occurs when the TAD αβα–IDR regions mutually interact to form an NRD-independent complex.

As FOXM1 is progressively activated throughout the cell cycle and regulates the expression of genes essential for G1/S to G2/M progression (25,42,53,69,70), we aimed to determine whether FOXM1 autoactivation changes during cell cycle progression are caused by TAD αβα–IDR interaction. We thus generated U2OS stable cells expressing FOXM1 proteins with CFP tagged to IDR and with YFP tagged to αβα, respectively (Figure 2A and Supplementary Figure S2A–C). After release from double thymidine synchronization, we tracked the interaction of FOXM1 protein domains in living U2OS cells using time-lapse FRET imaging, followed by NFRET analysis (Figure 2B). We observed the energy transfer upon FOXM1 αβα–IDR autointeraction gradually increased as cells progressed through the early S phase to the late G2 phase (Figure 2B–D), which was associated with a progressive increase in FOXM1 activity as determined by DLR analysis (Figure 2E). As FOXM1 activity can be promoted by PLK1-mediated phosphorylation on FOXM1 S715/S724 in the αβα domain, we further explored whether PLK1-mediated S715/S724 phosphorylation would also promote αβα–IDR-mediated FOXM1autoactivation. We found that treatment with the PLK1 inhibitor volasertib (7.8 nM, 6 h) resulted in the suppression of TAD autoactivation dynamics, accompanied by a reduction in FOXM1 activity (Figure 2B, C and F). Additionally, PLK1 non-phosphorylatable S715A/S724A substitutions of FOXM1 also caused suppression in αβα–IDR interaction during mitotic progression and FOXM1 activity (Figure 2B, C and G). In contrast, PLK1 phosphomimetic S715D/S724D substitutions of TAD 328–748 showed an increased binding ability to ΔN-FOXM1, as well as transcriptional activity, as revealed by DLR (Figure 2H) and co-IP assays (Figure 2I). Co-transfection with PLK1 led to increased promoter activity compared to ΔN-FOXM1 or FOXM1 alone (Supplementary Figure S2D and E). However, the PLK1 non-phosphorylatable FOXM1 S715A/S724A mutants abolished the activatoin casued by PLK1. Moreover, the high acitvity of the FOXM1 S715D/S724D phospho-mimic mutants could not be further enhanced by PLK1 (Supplementary Figure S2D and E). Taken together, we identified that phosphorylation on S715/S724 sites by PLK1 activates FOXM1 transcriptional activity via stimulating FOXM1 αβα–IDR autointeraction. Furthermore, this autointeraction also suggests the potential for in vivo homodimerization of the FOXM1 protein.

Regulating FOXM1 activity during the cell cycle involves a dynamic interaction switch between TAD-IDR and TAD-NRD

The previous autorepression model of FOXM1 suggested that exogenous expression of FOXM1 NRD (residues 1–234) suppresses FOXM1 transcriptional activity and interacts with TAD, as evidenced by co-IP assays (15,59). Recent data from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) study indicated that the purified TAD interacts with the ββαβ domain of NRD. This NRD–TAD interaction is likely disrupted by PLK1 phosphorylation (60). However, it remains unclear whether the NRD-dependent autorepression oscillates by switching NRD binding partners during cell cycle progression. Therefore, we aimed to visualize the occurrence of FOXM1 NRD–TAD interaction in mitotic cells using FRET imaging analysis (Figure 3A and Supplementary Figure S2A–C). We surprisingly found that the FRET signal of NRD–TAD interaction was declining during mitotic progression (Figure 3B and C), which was inversely correlated with the TAD–TAD autoactivation (Figure 2B and C). Blocking PLK1 phosphorylation activity on FOXM1, either by treating volasertib for 6 h or by expressing the non-phosphomimetic substitution FOXM1 S715A/S724A mutant, moderated the declining trends in NRD–TAD interaction (Figure 3B and C). Our finding further underscores the role of PLK1 in regulating FOXM1 activity through modulating the NRD–TAD interaction during mitotic progression in vivo. On the contrary, we employed the CID technique to investigate the effect of induced NRD–TAD interaction on FOXM1 activity in U2OS stable cells, in which FKBP was fused to the C-terminus of FOXM1 TAD, while FRB was N-terminal tagged to the NRD of FOXM1. Upon adding rapamycin, the NRD and the TAD of FOXM1 protein molecules were brought into proximity through FKBP and FRB dimerization, as demonstrated in the native gel assays (Figure 3D). In addition, FOXM1 activity was significantly reduced within a brief period of 6 h after inducing NRD–TAD proximity, as determined by DLR assay (Figure 3E). To further investigate potential inter- or intra-molecular FOXM1 interaction, we generated FRB–FOXM1–FKBP and FRBTAD InsX–FOXM1–FKBP constructs, double-tagged with FKBP and FRB at both ends of FOXM1 or within the central region and one end of FOXM1 for the native PAGE. A weak signal was observed only in the presence of rapamycin (arrowheads, Figure 3F and G), suggesting that FOXM1 could form a circular or lariat structure through intramolecular contacts involving NRD–TAD or IDR–TAD associations. Notably, in the absence of rapamycin, FOXM1 predominantly forms intermolecular dimers similar to the rapamycin-inducible dimer in position, although the signal is weaker (stars, Figure 3F and G). Collectively, these results indicate a dynamic NRD–TAD autointeraction mechanism that suppresses FOXM1 activity in vivo, supporting the current autorepression model. Specifically, the αβα domain in FOXM1 TAD switches its interaction partner from NRD to IDR in response to PLK1 phosphorylation during cell cycle progression, thereby activating FOXM1.

To determine the minimal NRD sequence of human FOXM1 with inhibitory function, we conducted secondary structure prediction on the FOXM1 NRD sequences across different species and identified a highly conserved ββαβ structural domain within NRD, specifically within the 51–121 residues (Figure 4A). While earlier study using NMR (60) identified ββαβ motif, our research focused on dissecting the detailed functional implications of the NRD. Various truncated NRD fragments were N-terminal tagged with mCherry and then co-transfected with ΔN-FOXM1 for DLR assay. We found that all constructed NRD fragments containing the 51–121 residues significantly suppressed ΔN-FOXM1 activity, irrespective of their lengths (Figure 4B and Supplementary Figure S3A), which was associated with ΔN-FOXM1 colocalization within the nuclei shown by confocal images (Supplementary Figure S3B). In contrast, two NRD fragments (residues 118–236 and 1–50) that both lacked the ββαβ motif did not exhibit an inhibitory function against ΔN-FOXM1 (Figure 4B). Moreover, truncates that lacked any secondary structural subunit of the ββαβ motif within NRD 51–121 diminished effectiveness in inhibiting ΔN-FOXM1, indicating that an intact ββαβ motif is required for the biological function of NRD (Figure 4C and D and Supplementary Figure S3C and D). Considering the high hydrophobicity scores in the ββαβ motif as determined by Expasy (Figure 4E, left panel), we constructed hydrophobic pocket mutants for co-transfection assays to investigate the role of hydrophobicity in the inhibitory function of the NRD. Mutations at the first three subunits of the ββαβ motif (I62A-K-I64A, V76A-A-I78A and I88A-T-A-L91A) significantly hampered the inhibitory function of NRD 51–121 [Figure 4E (right panel) and Supplementary Figure S3E]. Consistently, mutations in the first three subunits of the ββαβ motif within the NRD significantly increased the transcriptional activity of full-length FOXM1, as demonstrated by DLR assays (Supplementary Figure S3F), and increased the expression levels of endogenous FOXM1 target genes, Survivin and CDC25B (Supplementary Figure S3G and H). Interestingly, the deletion of the structured amino acid sequence 106–111 significantly compromised the inhibitory function of NRD (Figure 4D), whereas the F106A-I-L108A mutation within the fourth subunit (β) had minimal effect on NRD activity (Figure 4E). These findings indicate that the NRD 51–121 residues are both necessary and sufficient for NRD to functionally suppress activated FOXM1. Altering the hydrophobicity of the fourth subunit (β3) alone is not sufficient to impair the inhibitory function of residues 51–121. Notably, the inhibitory function of NRD is structure-dependent, in contrast to the effectiveness of TAD, which is determined by its progressive sequence length in autoactivation (Figure 1D).

The αβα motif at the C-terminus is necessary for FOXM1 to homodimerize and transition between autoactivation and autorepression

Our FRET imaging results showed two types of conformations of FOXM1 homodimerization, in which the αβα motif in TAD progressively switches its binding partner during the cell cycle progression from interphase to quiescence (G0). To determine the precise amino acid sequence of the αβα motif that interacts with the NRD ββαβ motif for FOXM1 autorepression, we co-expressed TAD constructs of varying lengths and assessed their ability to rescue ΔN-FOXM1 transcriptional activity from ββαβ inhibition through competitive binding (Figure 5A). The DLR assay results demonstrated that the loss of residues 696–748 in the TAD motif completely abolished the ability to restore ΔN-FOXM1 transcriptional activity from mCherry-NRD 51–121 repression (Figure 5B), which was associated with the loss of protein interaction with NRD 51–121 as shown in the immunoprecipitation assay (Figure 5C). These results suggested that the TAD αβα motif is required for binding to the NRD ββαβ motif, leading to the suppression of FOXM1 activity. Moreover, in the Y2H assay, co-expression of Gal4-fused NRD fragments containing the 51–121 residues and LexA-TAD 328–748 showed stronger β-gal activities (Supplementary Figure S3I). This consistently demonstrated that the TAD αβα motif is essential for interacting with the NRD ββαβ motif in vivo. Surprisingly, the shorter fragment TAD 585–748 partially rescued ΔN-FOXM1 transcriptional activity to a moderate activation level compared with TAD 328–748, likely due to the absence of the IDR required for full activation of ΔN-FOXM1 (Figure 5B). Moreover, Ala scanning on the TAD αβα motif revealed that substitutions of the hydrophobic residues to Ala in the two α subunits of TAD failed to rescue ΔN-FOXM1 activity from NRD 51–121 inhibition in the DLR assay (Figure 5D and E), suggesting the hydrophobic pockets in TAD were essential for NRD interaction. Similarly, PLK1 phosphomimetic substitutions (S715D/S724D) in TAD disrupted NRD 51–121 binding to TAD (Figure 5F) and inhibition of ΔN-FOXM1 activity (Figure 5G). These collective findings implied that FOXM1 autorepression is dependent on hydrophobic protein interaction between 51–121 and 696–748 residues, which is mediated by PLK1.

We found that the FOXM1 protein engages in two types of autointeractions, prompting speculation on whether FOXM1 naturally adopts a higher-order conformation as observed through IDR analysis (Figures 1 and 3). To explore this further, we performed non-reducing SDS-PAGE followed by FOXM1 immunoblotting, which exhibited a protein signal approximately twice the molecular weight of the FOXM1 monomer shown in both reducing and non-reducing states (Figure 5H, arrow). Consistently, Native-PAGE analysis detected two distinct molecular weight signals of FOXM1 (Figure 5I, arrow), suggesting potential dimerization of FOXM1 in its native state. This finding was consistent with the robust signal detected in the Y2H assay when LexA-FOXM1 and GAL4-FOXM1 were co-expressed (Figure 5J). Additionally, we co-transfected U2OS cells with constructs expressing FOXM1 fused to either GFP or mCherry followed by co-IP using a GFP antibody. mCherry was detectable within the immunoprecipitated protein complex of GFP-FOXM1 (Figure 5K, arrow), further indicating the potential formation of a FOXM1–FOXM1 homodimer complex in vivo. To investigate the necessity of an alternative intermolecular NRD–TAD interaction for FOXM1–FOXM1 dimerization, we performed co-IP using GFP- or mCherry-tagged ΔN-FOXM1 and full-length FOXM1. Surprisingly, our results demonstrated that mCherry-ΔN-FOXM1 remained detectable in the protein precipitate of GFP-FOXM1 or GFP-ΔN-FOXM1 by co-IP (Figure 5K, indicated by stars). This finding suggests that the FOXM1–FOXM1 dimerization can occur independently of the NRD region. We subsequently investigated whether PLK1 phosphorylation on TAD influenced the dimerization of FOXM1, and found that both FOXM1 mutants with PLK1 site substitutions did not impact the formation of the FOXM1–FOXM1 dimer (Figure 5L and M).

A minimum functional peptide, derived from FOXM1 NRD, inhibits lung cancer cell proliferation and suppresses tumor growth in vivo

Inhibition of Foxm1 has been demonstrated to halt tumorigenesis of lung adenocarcinoma as well as small-cell lung cancer in genetically engineered mouse models (34,35,71). Given that the FOXM1 NRD 51–121 residues inhibit FOXM1 transcription activity in the U2OS cells (Figure 4), we further investigated whether NRD 51–121 alone is sufficient to inhibit the growth of lung cancer cell lines in vitro and in vivo. We transduced the pInducer lentiviral vectors expressing mCherry- or His-tagged NRD 51–121 to human non-small cell lung cancer A549 cells and created stable cell lines (62). No significant difference in proliferation was found between stable cell lines when cultured in the absence of DOX (Figure 6A). Upon DOX induction, A549 cells expressing NRD 51–121 tagged by mCherry or His exhibited a significant decrease in proliferation compared with cells expressing mCherry alone (Figure 6B). Furthermore, a rescue in proliferation of the NRD 51–121 -inhibited A549 cells was observed following transduction with an adenovirus expressing GFP-FOXM1 (Figure 6C). The expression of NRD 51–121 was associated with a significant decrease in the mRNA levels of FOXM1 target genes CDC25B, Survivin and anti-apoptosis regulators SGMS-1 (Figure 6D), which was associated with a 4-fold increase in the percentage of apoptotic cells as determined by TUNEL assay (Figure 6E and F). In addition, expression of NRD 51–121 in A549 cells led to a flattened and enlarged cell morphology, decreased nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio and exhibited cellular senescence phenotypes as indicated by increased SA-β-gal staining (Figure 6G and H). These apoptosis (Figure 6J and K) and senescence phenotypes (Figure 6L and M) were reduced in Ad-GFP-FOXM1 transduced cells. RT-qPCR demonstrated an elevation in mRNA levels of CDC25B and Survivin in Ad-GFP-FOXM1 transduced cells compared to the Ad-GFP control (Figure 6I). Additionally, adenoviral transduction of NRD 51–121 (MOI = 10) abolished the formation of A549 cell subcutaneous tumor on the right flank of a lung cancer xenografted mouse model, whereas transduction with adenovirus expressing NRD 51–121 6A mutant peptide failed to suppress A549 cell growth subcutaneously on the left flank, as evidenced by the tumor growth curve and the isolated tumor after 70 days of growth (Figure 6N and O). We thus identified a minimal peptide with ββαβ motif that can effectively inhibit cell proliferation in both cell culture and xenograft mouse tumor model. This discovery shows potential for a promising repression strategy to target FOXM1 in cancer treatment.

Discussion

Our work detailed here provides significant insights into how the conformation of homodimers relates to the transcriptional activity of FOXM1 in vivo. We found that the conserved structural αβα motif at the C-terminus interacts with the non-structural IDR following PLK-1 phosphorylation on FOXM1 residues S715/S724. This interaction activates FOXM1 through the G1/S to G2/M cell cycle phases, coinciding with the loss of αβα interaction with the inhibitory ββαβ motifs within NRD. The expression of the ββαβ motif effectively inhibits FOXM1 activity, as well as the proliferation of cultured human lung adenocarcinoma cells and xenografted tumor growth in mice. Conversely, exogenous expression of TAD abolishes the inhibitory function of the ββαβ motif through interaction. In the CID systems, FOXM1 dimerization via IDR–TAD interactions enhances the transcription of human CDC25B or Survivin genes by increasing its binding to their promoter regions, as demonstrated by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR assays. In contrast, FOXM1’s promoter binding ability was decreased when influenced by NRD–TAD interaction (Figure 7A–D). Collectively, we propose a novel model regarding FOXM1 activation based on our findings (Figure 7E). In the G1 phase, the αβα–ββαβ interaction brings the NRD–TAD regions of FOXM1 molecules into proximity, leading to FOXM1 proteins being held in an autorepressed state. As the cell cycle progresses to S and the G2 phase, PLK1 phosphorylates αβα, promoting its binding to IDR instead of ββαβ. This PLK1 phosphorylation accordingly allows FOXM1 to escape from autorepression, leading to αβα–IDR-driven proximity and further activating FOXM1. Notably, FOXM1 exhibits an equivalent level of dimerization whether it carries PLK1 phosphomimetic or non-phosphorylatable mutations (see Figure 5L and M). Overall, our study illustrates that FOXM1 proteins undergo a dynamic transition from αβα–ββαβ to αβα–IDR dominated proximity, affecting FOXM1 DNA-binding as well as transcriptional activity, and highlights two alternative formations of homotypic FOXM1 dimers during the cell cycle.

The classical paradigm posits that a protein's function is predominantly determined by its well-folded 3D structure. However, a significant proportion of disordered protein sequences exist in eukaryotic proteins, presenting challenges in annotating their functions (72). Remarkably, the presence of IDR is prevalent in ∼82–94% of eukaryotic transcription factors (73). In human transcription factors, these disordered regions encompass ∼49% of the sequence (74). Specifically, these disordered regions are frequently located in the TAD of human transcription factors, where they affect transcription activation through inter-domain interactions within disordered activation domains (4,5,10,75). While the FOXM1 transcription factor is known to possess a well-defined winged-helix DBD, it is noteworthy that more than half of the FOXM1 sequence retains a flexible or unconstrained conformation. Our research reveals that the FOXM1 TAD contains a conserved long IDR (Figure 1C), primarily falling into the categories of constrained disorder or flexible disorder, strongly implying its necessity for the function of FOXM1 during molecular evolution (76). We demonstrate that CID-driven proximity of αβα–IDR enhances FOXM1 transcriptional activity, consistent with DLR assays showing a superactivated state upon IDR co-expression. The depletion experiments targeting the IDR or the αβα motif underscored the critical role of the IDR–αβα interaction in FOXM1 activation. Additionally, we showed that the IDR facilitates FOXM1 dimerization, as supported by co-IP and FRET studies. Predictive analyses further indicate that the IDR is predominantly unstructured and interacts with the αβα motif, aligning with our experimental observations (Supplementary Figure S1D–F). Collectively, our data demonstrate that the IDR plays a powerful positive regulatory role in FOXM1 activity through its interaction with the αβα motif.

Similar to the role of FOXM1, the mitotic kinase PLK1 has been shown to contribute to tumorigenicity for multiple cancers (77,78,79). Previous studies have suggested that PLK-1 enhances FOXM1 transcriptional activity throughout the cell cycle by directly phosphorylating FOXM1 (53,54) or by subsequently preventing the formation of the FoxM1/Rb/DNMT3b transcription complex, which otherwise inhibits the expression of G1 phase genes (80). Furthermore, studies suggest the existence of a positive feedback loop between PLK1 and FOXM1 (54,81), implying that elevated expression and activity of FOXM1 may lead to increased PLK1 expression, along with higher levels of FOXM1 phosphorylation by PLK1. Our research suggests that PLK1-mediated phosphorylation of FOXM1 at S715/S724 counteracts the ββαβ motif’s autorepressive targeting of the αβα motif. This phosphorylation facilitates the IDR–αβα interaction, promoting FOXM1 autoactivation as cells progress from G1/S to G2/M, as supported by the observed increase in FOXM1 activity and the gradual enhancement of the αβα–IDR interaction during cell cycle progression. Notably, the Cancer Proteogenomic Data Analysis Site (cProSite) has reported an increased level of S715 phosphorylation in FOXM1 in lung squamous cell carcinoma compared to the adjacent normal tissue (82). This observation is consistent with FOXM1 being phosphorylated by PLK1, thereby subsequently fine-tuning the αβα–IDR interaction and enabling FOXM1 to promote the transcription of target genes during tumorigenesis. Moreover, two specific local regions (349–372 and 510–538 residues) of IDR were predicted proximal to αβα with moderate confidence (Supplementary Figure S1G). Considering that Chk2 has been shown to phosphorylate FOXM1 on S361, our DLR assay showed that point mutations within the IDR (S361A and S361D) led to a decrease in ΔN-FOXM1 transcriptional activity (Supplementary Figure S1H). FOXM1 has been identified as a target for various cell cycle regulatory kinases, including cyclin D-CDK4/6, cyclin E-CDK2, cyclin A-CDK1 and cyclin B-CDK1 which phosphorylate specific sites within the FOXM1 IDR region (51–53,58,69). These findings are corroborated by the observation that FOXM1 exhibits reduced activation when point-mutated to the non-phosphorylatable multi-sites but increased activation when mutated to phosphomimetic sites. Nevertheless, further investigation is required to ascertain whether additional kinases, beyond PLK1 phosphorylation, can influence the αβα–IDR interaction and consequently mediate FOXM1 autoactivation and dimerization.

Transcription factors rely on multiple regulatory mechanisms, including PTMs and protein–protein interactions, for precise gene expression control and transcriptional versatility. One such paradigm is homodimerization, which allows TFs to bind to different DNA motifs, enabling diverse transcriptional regulation by recruiting specific coactivators (83,84). Notably, previously published crystal structure data demonstrated that FOXM1 DBD can indeed form homodimers on a tandem sequence (12); however, the formation and function of dimerization of FOXM1 in vivo remained uncertain, possibly because the unstructured regions in NRD and TAD were not considered or limited by the methodology employed. The findings that NRD repressed FOXM1 transcriptional activity through NRD–TAD or NRD–FOXM1 interactions have led to a hypothetic autorepression model for FOXM1 (15,59,85), thus raising the question of whether FOXM1 homodimerizes via such inter-domain interaction.

Autorepression and autoactivation have been reported in many transcription factors. For example, the disordered IDR in CTD of tumor suppressor p53 is involved in transcription activity in many aspects, including tetramerization, protein stabilization, cofactor recruitment, DNA binding, as well as binding-induced structural change, often triggered by PTMs (86). Importantly, the CTD of p53 has been demonstrated to regulate the DNA-binding activity of p53. As the CTD region is deleted, p53 becomes constitutively inactive in DNA binding (87). Furthermore, p53 undergoes autorepression through an interaction between its DBD and IDR of the N-terminal transactivation domain (NTD), which masks the DBD and reduces its DNA binding capacity, thereby decreasing transcriptional activity (88). In contrast, FOXM1 autorepression is mediated through an interaction between its NRD and CTD, utilizing structural ββαβ and αβα motifs. Unlike p53, IDR of FOXM1 plays a positive regulatory role, promoting transcriptional activation. Phosphorylation also plays distinct roles in these proteins. Notably, PTMs are crucial for regulating both p53 and FOXM1 transcriptional activities via protein autointeractions. In p53, phosphorylation at threonine 55 (T55) within the NTD by TAF1 kinase enhances the NTD-DBD interaction, reinforcing the autorepressed state and inhibiting DNA binding (89). Conversely, in FOXM1, phosphorylation by PLK1 on CTD activates its transcription function by disrupting the CTD-NRD interaction, thereby allowing the IDR–CTD interaction to promote superactivation. Moreover, in p53, the CTD controls sequence-specific DNA interaction by modulating the stability of a binary p53–DNA complex and regulating different p53 target genes (90). However, whether the IDR in FOXM1 mediates sequence-specific DNA binding and gene expression remains unclear.

Accumulating research has consistently demonstrated that high expression levels of FOXM1 are strongly associated with a poor prognosis in various cancers in clinical studies and play a critical role in tumor initiation and progression in mouse models of cancers (28–35). Given its implication in tumorigenicity, FOXM1 serves not only as a cancer prognostic marker but also represents an appealing target for cancer therapy. Although efforts have been dedicated to developing FOXM1 inhibitors (91–96), their specificity and clinical effects on cancer inhibition remain to be validated. Our study extends previous findings on FOXM1 NRD, showing that the ββαβ peptide is the minimal functional unit responsible for binding and suppressing FOXM1 via hydrophobic residues. This peptide inhibits tumor cell growth by inducing apoptosis and senescence. Specifically, RNA-seq analysis of A549 cells treated with NRD 51–121 revealed multiple affected networks controlling cell replication, senescence and apoptosis. Further analysis highlighted FOXM1 as a key regulator of many genes involved in these processes (Supplementary Figure S4). These exciting findings suggest a promising new targeted therapy for FOXM1 based on the ββαβ peptide, in combination with FOXM1 and PLK1 inhibition, holds potential for enhanced tumor inhibition efficacy.

Conclusions

In this study, we elucidate the formation of homodimers by transcription factor FOXM1 in living cells. Distinct homodimers are established through the αβα motif within the C-terminal TAD. This motif selectively binds to a newly identified IDR spanning residues 328–584, culminating in the emergence of a superactivated FOXM1 homodimer during the S–G2/M phases of the cell cycle. Conversely, in the early G1 phase, FOXM1 adopts a transcriptionally autorepressed homodimer as the αβα motif interacts with the ββαβ motif in the NRD. Mechanistically, we found that the auto-interaction relies on the hydrophobic nature of both the αβα motif and the ββαβ motif. During the S–G2 phase, phosphorylation of the αβα motif at S715/S724 by PLK1 changes hydrophobicity and subsequently promotes FOXM1 into an active dimeric form. These intricate conformational dynamics and regulatory mechanisms in homodimerization wield a significant impact on the transcriptional activity of FOXM1 and the expression of its target genes across the cell cycle. Furthermore, our study identifies a minimal ββαβ motif peptide that inhibits cancer cell proliferation, demonstrating efficacy in both in vitro cell culture and in vivo mouse tumor xenograft models. These findings propose potential therapeutic implications for cancer therapy through the modulation of FOXM1 autorepression.
