The wearing-off effect with natalizumab: Back to the drawing board
Authors: Saira Afzal, Devon S Conway
Published: 2026

Natalizumab is one of the most effective disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) for multiple sclerosis (MS). A monoclonal antibody targeting the α4 subunit of α4β1 integrins on circulating leukocytes, natalizumab interrupts leukocyte binding to vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), thereby inhibiting immune cell migration across the blood-brain barrier [1]. Natalizumab not only significantly reduces relapse rates and new MRI lesions, but it is also associated with meaningful improvement in patient-reported outcomes including fatigue, cognition, and overall patient wellbeing [2]. However, a substantial proportion of people with MS (PwMS) treated with natalizumab report transient worsening with recurrence or amplification of MS symptoms toward the end of dosing intervals. This phenomenon is referred to as the wearing-off effect (WOE) or end-of-dose effect and has been observed with several monoclonal antibody MS DMTs [3].

The biological basis of the WOE with natalizumab is poorly understood, but there are several proposed mechanisms. A commonly advanced hypothesis is that the WOE occurs due to declining α4-integrin receptor saturation toward the end of the dosing cycle, allowing partial lymphocyte trafficking through the blood-brain barrier and resulting in breakthrough symptoms. Additional proposed mechanisms include fluctuations in peripheral cytokine signaling across the dosing interval [4], alterations in primary motor cortex output and sensorimotor network connectivity as the drug's anti-inflammatory effects wane [5], and potential contributions from psychological conditioning or nocebo effects [6]. A decline in receptor saturation close to the end of the dosing interval has the most intuitive appeal. Receptor occupancy is known to decline over time as the circulating drug concentration falls [7]. Exactly how a low-grade restoration of lymphocyte trafficking across the blood brain barrier might exacerbate symptoms, especially considering the short interval between declining natalizumab levels and subsequent dosing, remains elusive.

The hypothesis that the WOE reflects psychological conditioning or a nocebo effect is also an interesting consideration. It proposes that PwMS can develop a conditioned anticipatory response related to their infusion schedule, and the recurrence of symptoms near the end of the dosing interval represents an expectation-driven phenomenon rather than true pharmacologic waning of treatment effect. Supporting this, the WOE has been described with other high-efficacy MS therapies as well, including B cell monoclonal therapies whose pharmacodynamic profiles differ substantially from natalizumab, suggesting that this may reflect shared experiences with cyclical treatment regimens rather than an underlying pharmacological phenomenon. Furthermore, despite the majority reporting end-of-dose symptoms, objective measures of fatigue, cognition, and mood have been shown not to worsen in the pre-infusion window, pointing to a disconnect between subjective experience and measurable functional change [8]. Depression has also emerged as an independent predictor of the WOE, with higher depression scores associated with greater odds of reporting symptoms across PwMS treated with B-cell monoclonal antibody DMTs [9]. Finally, the rapid resolution of WOE symptoms shortly after the subsequent infusion further supports the possibility that non-pharmacological factors may contribute to the phenomenon [3].

More recently, with widespread adoption of extended interval dosing (EID) to reduce the risk of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, the relationship between dosing interval and the WOE has become an increasingly relevant clinical question. Compared to standard interval dosing (SID), reduced receptor saturation with EID at the end of the dosing cycle has been reported [10]. If the WOE is pharmacodynamically driven, the symptom burden would be expected to worsen with longer dosing intervals. Indeed, one retrospective study reported a significant increase in WOE prevalence following the switch from SID to EID [11]. The published literature, however, is limited and remains divided on this point.

In the March 2026 issue of Neurotherapeutics, Freeman and colleagues directly address α4-integrin receptor saturation, challenging the prevailing mechanistic narrative [12]. Their prospective cohort included 117 individuals with relapsing-remitting MS receiving natalizumab with both SID (4-week) and EID intervals (5 and 6 weeks). Saturation of the α4-integrin receptor was measured across T-lymphocyte subsets by flow cytometry immediately prior to infusion. Subsets included naive, central memory, effector memory, and EMRA subpopulations of both CD4+ and CD8+ lineages. The WOE was measured through a structured questionnaire capturing symptom frequency, current presence, onset timing relative to infusion, and days to resolution after administration. No significant association between receptor saturation in any lymphocyte subset and the presence of WOE was seen. Mean receptor saturation was essentially identical between WOE-positive and WOE-negative PwMS. As expected, receptor saturation was significantly lower with EID compared to SID across all subsets, yet the difference did not translate into any greater WOE manifestations in the EID group. These findings corroborate prior observations by van Kempen et al. and substantially weaken the receptor saturation hypothesis as a general explanation for WOE [13]. Importantly, the frequency and pattern of WOE symptoms were similar between SID and EID groups, with no significant differences in symptom onset timing or resolution after administration, offering meaningful reassurance for clinicians employing EID in routine practice.

There are several other noteworthy observations from the study by Freeman et al. Consistent with previous research, the prevalence of the WOE was reported among 57.3% of participants [3,13]. This highlights the WOE as not just a rare occurrence but rather a common issue among PwMS receiving long-term natalizumab treatment. Another interesting observation reported is the positive relationship between the WOE and disability, as well as greater number of natalizumab treatment cycles. Although these findings do not establish causality, they raise the possibility that the WOE may be influenced by underlying disease burden or accumulated neurological disability rather than reflecting a purely pharmacodynamic phenomenon.

Certain methodological considerations need to be acknowledged when interpreting this study. Symptom assessment relied solely on patient-reported data obtained through direct questionnaire, resulting in possible confirmation, recall, or suggestion bias. In addition, the WOE-positive group was heterogeneous, including PwMS with occasional and mild symptoms to individuals with more consistent and distressing symptoms. More detailed characterization of symptom type, frequency, and severity through validated patient-reported outcome instruments, for example structured fatigue and cognitive assessments, would strengthen future investigations. Also, in this study, α4-integrin saturation was measured in T-lymphocyte subsets. However, the investigators also examined the CD3− fraction, and found strong correlation with CD3+ saturation values, supporting the use of T-cell measurements as a proxy for the broader lymphocyte population. However, B cells themselves were not analyzed as a distinct subset. Therefore, it is difficult to say with complete certainty whether receptor saturation dynamics in B cells follow the same pattern as T cells. This could also be explored in future studies.

Altogether, this study supports the conclusion that the WOE does not reliably relate to receptor saturation and does not differ significantly between SID and EID (5-week or 6-week intervals). These findings are clinically meaningful as they suggest that the presence of the WOE alone should not prompt modification of an otherwise effective and well-tolerated treatment regimen. At the same time, the study's findings highlight an important gap in our mechanistic understanding of the WOE. Future research will require a more integrated approach, combining longitudinal pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic profiling across the dosing cycle with assessment of peripheral immune signaling, direct B-cell receptor occupancy measurements, and validated functional outcome measures. Importantly, study designs should also account for expectation and conditioning effects, an underexplored but potentially important contributor to patient-reported symptom fluctuation.
