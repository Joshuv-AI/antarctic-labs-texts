Self-supervised learning for data augmentation in histopathology image segmentation
Authors: Haidar A. Almubarak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Histopathology image segmentation faces critical challenges due to the scarcity of pixel-level annotations and limited generalization across diverse tissue types and institutional settings. This paper introduces a novel self-supervised learning framework that integrates masked image modeling with contrastive learning and adaptive semantic-aware data augmentation to address these fundamental limitations. Our approach features three key innovations the first is a multi-resolution hierarchical architecture specifically designed for gigapixel whole slide images that captures both cellular-level details and tissue-level context and the second is a hybrid self-supervised learning strategy combining masked autoencoder reconstruction with multi-scale contrastive learning to learn robust feature representations without extensive annotations, and an adaptive augmentation network that preserves histological semantics while maximizing data diversity through learned transformation policies. The framework employs a progressive fine-tuning protocol with semantic-aware masking strategies and boundary-focused loss functions optimized for dense prediction tasks. Comprehensive evaluation on five diverse histopathology datasets [TCGA-BRCA, TCGA-LUAD, TCGA-COAD, CAMELYON16, and PanNuke] demonstrates substantial improvements over state-of-the-art methods, achieving a Dice coefficient of 0.825 [4.3% improvement], mIoU of 0.742 [7.8% enhancement], and significant reductions in boundary error metrics [10.7% in Hausdorff Distance, 9.5% in Average Surface Distance]. Notably, our method exhibits exceptional data efficiency, requiring only 25% of labeled data to achieve 95.6% of full performance compared to 85.2% for supervised baselines, representing a 70% reduction in annotation requirements. Cross-dataset generalization analysis reveals 13.9% improvement over existing approaches, while clinical validation by expert pathologists confirms diagnostic utility with ratings of 4.3/5.0 for clinical applicability and 4.1/5.0 for boundary accuracy. The proposed framework establishes a new paradigm for self-supervised learning in computational pathology, offering significant potential for clinical deployment where annotation resources are limited while maintaining high diagnostic accuracy across diverse institutional environments.

Introduction

In recent years, computational pathology has undergone an unprecedented development process due to novel trends in digital imaging technologies and deep learning mechanisms1. The analysis of histopathological images, especially segmentation, plays a central role in cancer diagnosis, prognosis prediction, and treatment formulation. The challenge, however, lies in the complex morphology of tissues, inconsistency of staining protocols, and the limited availability of pixel-level annotations needed for empirical learning methods2.

The conventional supervised learning approach to histopathology image segmentation requires extensive manual labeling by skilled pathologists, which is costly, time-consuming, and prone to inter-observer variability3. This is further complicated by the gigapixel format of whole slide images [WSIs], since annotating entire slides is practically impossible. Such challenges have motivated researchers to explore alternative learning paradigms that can exploit the wealth of unlabeled histopathological images available in clinical archives. Self-supervised learning has emerged as a strong candidate to overcome the annotation bottleneck in medical imaging4. SSL approaches process large quantities of unlabeled data by leveraging intrinsic structural representations. Recent advances in masked image modeling [MIM] and contrastive learning have shown remarkable success in natural image domains, and these benefits are now being adapted to medical imaging tasks5.

Another key strategy for addressing data sparsity is augmentation. While traditional augmentation techniques improve diversity, they often fail to capture domain-specific histopathological variations6. Inappropriate augmentations may also introduce artifacts that compromise semantic consistency in tissue structures, degrading segmentation performance. Recent studies emphasize the need for augmentation methods that both maintain histological semantics and enhance data diversity7.

The integration of masked image modeling, contrastive learning, and adaptive augmentation is motivated by the unique requirements of WSI analysis. MIM enables the reconstruction of fine-grained tissue structures, contrastive learning enforces feature invariance across scales and staining variations, and adaptive augmentation ensures semantic preservation while enhancing data diversity. Their combination is particularly suited to multi-scale WSI analysis, where capturing both local cellular details and global tissue context is essential8,9.

The most recent advances in automatic data augmentation have further shown potential in improving generalization in histopathology10–12. Such strategies employ meta-learning to automatically discover augmentation policies tailored to histopathological data, striking a balance between preserving semantic integrity and promoting diversity.

Unlike existing foundation models such as UNI, Virchow, and CONCH that primarily focus on classification tasks and rely on generic augmentations, our framework is explicitly tailored for segmentation tasks. The integration of semantic-aware augmentation ensures tissue morphology is preserved during training, overcoming the artifact issues often seen in general-purpose augmentation strategies. This unique design directly addresses pixel-level accuracy requirements that are not sufficiently handled by classification-centered models.

This combination of methods directly addresses the research gap identified earlier: the lack of domain-specific augmentation strategies and multi-scale feature learning in existing SSL frameworks for histopathology. By integrating complementary mechanisms into a unified architecture, our approach bridges the gap between annotation-limited environments and clinically applicable segmentation.

Literature review

Self-supervised learning Imogen Heap methods have played an important factor in the development of computational pathology13–17. The current section gives an in-depth overview of the recent progress in self-supervised learning applied to histopathology and use specifically in the segmentation of images.

Self-supervised learning in medical imaging

Self-supervised learning has proved to be the revolutionary method of medical imaging since the problem of scarce labeled data was persistent. In another study, Huang et al.18 explored the use of self-supervised learning in the classification of medical images in a systematic review study which emphasized the possibility of methods to perform well and only requires a subset of labeled data compared to supervised learning counterparts. Their study provided valuable frameworks to follow when applying self-supervised learning to medical procedures, which creates a necessity to adopt changes depending on the field19–22.

Self-supervised learning on histopathology has proven to have really promising results. The article by Azizi et al.13 showed the effectiveness of data-efficient generalization of self-supervised machine learning in diagnostic imaging, which can serve as a basis of learning more about the scale of application of these methods to various medical fields. Their overall cross-modality assessment demonstrated universal transference of the self-supervised learning principles to the medical domain.

The newest breakthroughs in masked image modeling created new horizons in self-supervised learning in histopathology. A team led by Filiot et al.15 made a breakthrough in scaling self-supervised learning on histopathology with masked image modeling. Their strategy of deep learning using iBOT and trained on more than 40 million histology images of 16 types of cancer performed state-of-the-art in a variety of downstream tasks. This study showed that large-scale self-supervised pre-training has potential in establishing foundation models in computational pathology.

Xie et al.5 also contributed to the branch when they thought to reconsider the masked image modeling in case of medical image representation. The domain-specific medical knowledge in their knowledge-driven method found representation to the masking strategy and produced self-supervised representations that were more meaningful. On the same note, Yang et al.6 proposed GMIM, a self-supervised pre-training approach to 3D medical image segmentation that has adaptive and hierarchical masked image modeling, which generalizes the advantages of masked modeling to volumetric medical images23–27.

Foundation models and large-scale pre-training

Foundation models applied to histopathology are one of the milestones of the field. Prov-GigaPath by Xu et al.2 is a whole-slide foundation model pre-trained on about 1.3 billion images of pathology images. This was a work that showed large-scale pre-training can build robust feature representations and generalize across hundreds of cancer types and downstream tasks28–32.

On this basis, Chen et al.11 have introduced UNI, a general-purpose foundation model of computational pathology that takes into account over 100 million images in up to 100,000 diagnostic whole slide H&E-stained images covering 20 major tissue types. UNI showed outstanding performance on a consequential selection of 34 typical computational pathology data sets and set novel records on the effectiveness of foundation models on histopathological tasks.

Likewise, a clinical-grade Virchow model of computational pathology foundation trained on 1.5 million whole-slide images of the 100,000 patients was developed by Vorontsov and coworkers3. They demonstrated that foundation models can learn to perform better than previous methods when detecting rare cancers, short of clinical resources to train supervised systems conventionally. The practical use of foundation models translated to practice by the clinical validation of Virchow on a variety of cancer types showed that different position models resulting in practical utility became available through the practical application of the concept of foundation models.

Lu et al.12 proposed a visual-language foundation model called CONCH [CONtrastive learning from Captions for Histopathology], which was pretrained on a wide variety of sources of histopathology images, biomedical text and especially 1.17 million image-caption pairs. CONCH introduces a paradigm change as it allows the visual features and textual information to be used together, which allows access to more advanced comprehension of the histopathological entities, in as much as multimodal learning is allowed33–37.

Vision transformers in histopathology

Vision transformers have been adopted in the analysis of histopathological images due to high performances in locating long-term trends and processes of high-resolution images. Li et al.16 constructed a vision transformer framework that builds on a weekly supervised approach to primary brain tumor analysis to produce impressive results on classification and molecular markers prediction. Their ViT-WSI model established what transformer-based architectures could do to process gigapixel whole slide images, by utilizing hierarchical processing strategies.

Chen et al.23 discussed the use of self-supervised transformers of vision in training visual concepts in histopathology. Differently to previous studies, they found that various attention heads in transformer models naturally specialize in attending to different morphological phenotypes and showed an excellent step towards understanding how transformer-based methods can be interpreted in medical images. The significance of this finding, in terms of clinical deployment, is in terms of having model interpretability as especially important in terms of allowing physician acceptance38–41.

Doerrich et al.9 made it clear the self-directed vision transformers can be used as the scalable generative models of domain generalization in histopathology. Their work demonstrated that with the help of transformer architectures, one can create synthetically generated histopathological data that exhibits morphological compatibility but with diversified datasets to solve the inherent problem of data shortage in medical imaging.

Large-scale applicability of vision transformers to images with a billion pixels or more was addressed in a hierarchy of self-supervised learning. As Chen et al.31 introduced, transformer architectures are used to offer new solutions to processes whole slide images and showed that hierarchical feature learning could be used to close the gap between patches in analysis versus the global slide level of understanding in an efficient manner.

Zhou and colleagues17 proposed pyramid-based self-supervised learning of histopathological images regarding the classification of specimens, where they ensemble wavelet transforms with vision transformers to estimate multi-scale tissue patterns. Their methodology showed how transformers could be improved in terms of presentation learning task in histopathology with pyramids42–46.

Contrastive learning applications

The forced learning has demonstrated to be successful in learning meaningful representations of histopathological images. The works of Ciga et al.32 provided very thorough studies of self-supervised contrastive learning in digital histopathology, where the models pre-trained on various histopathology datasets performed more significantly than ImageNet-pretrained models in the downstream as well. Their general assessment, which compares 57 datasets, developed significant guidelines regarding the use of contrastive learning in a histopathologic setting.

Wang et al.24 developed transformer-based unsupervised contrastive learning approaches for histopathological image classification, showing how contrastive objectives can be effectively integrated with transformer architectures. Their RetCCL framework provided valuable insights into the design of contrastive learning frameworks for high-resolution medical images.

Lu et al.25 presented innovative approaches to unsupervised representation learning for tissue segmentation through global-to-local contrast strategies. Their hierarchical contrastive learning framework effectively captured both tissue-level and cellular-level features, demonstrating the importance of multi-scale learning in histopathological image analysis.

Wang et al.26 generalized the concept of contrastive learning to deep contrastive learning-based tissue clustering in annotation-free histopathology image analysis. In their paper, they demonstrated how the method of contrastive learning could be used to perform unsupervised discovery of tissue types and in this way, provide automatic histopathological analysis without manual annotations.

Schirris et al.20 built DeepSMILE, which is a contrasted self, a self-supervised framework that specifically improves MSI and HDR classifications using only H&E whole-slide images. They have shown that contrastive learning is effectively deployed at certain tasks on biomarker prediction in computational pathology.

In addition to previously discussed contrastive learning approaches, several recent studies provide further insight into their application for histopathology and whole-slide image [WSI] analysis. Marrakchi et al. proposed a framework for addressing class imbalance through contrastive embedding, demonstrating improved performance on imbalanced medical datasets41. More recently, Cong et al. introduced Adaptive Unified Contrastive Learning with a graph-based feature aggregator, which enhances feature discriminability in imbalanced medical image classification tasks42. Alongside these, works such as SC-MIL: Supervised Contrastive MIL for Imbalanced Classification and Enhancing WSI Classification with Discriminative and Contrastive Learning highlight the role of contrastive objectives in improving robustness and representation quality. Incorporating these studies provides a more complete view of contrastive learning strategies and their potential relevance to WSI segmentation.

Recent medical image segmentation research has introduced advanced multi-scale and context-aware architectures that are highly relevant to hierarchical feature learning. Xie et al. proposed PIF-Net, a parallel interweave fusion network for knee joint segmentation that employs dense connection blocks with multi-scale dilated convolutions to capture object scale variations while establishing cross-path relations between parallel processing streams43. Building on pyramid-based approaches, the same research group developed DFPNet, which introduces a discriminative feature pyramid network consisting of a feature steered network for top-down context extraction and a border network for bottom-up boundary refinement, demonstrating superior performance on LUNA 16, RIM-ONE-R1, and VNC datasets44. Context-aware architectures have also shown promise, with CANet presenting a dual-stream pyramid approach that combines L-shaped multi-resolution processing with U-shaped encoder-decoder networks enhanced by chain residual pooling modules for background suppression45. Similarly, Wang et al. developed CHI-Net, a context hierarchical integrated network that utilizes dense dilated convolution modules with four cascaded branches and stacked residual pooling to capture multi-scale features across diverse medical imaging tasks46. These works highlight the importance of pyramid-based feature fusion and context modeling in medical image segmentation, providing foundational insights for multi-scale representation learning that complement the hierarchical processing strategies employed in transformer-based architectures for histopathological image analysis.

Data augmentation in histopathology

Medical imaging data are unique, and this is what has led to the increased attention toward data augmentation techniques that are specific to histopathological images. The impact of data augmentation and stain color normalization in the context of computational pathology were thoroughly investigated by Tellez et al.37, and these studies serve as the background knowledge on how histopathological images should be augmented. They found in their systematic review the utmost paramountness of stain-aware augmentation methods.

Active research into automatic data augmentation is also proving promising to enhance the generalization capabilities of deep learning models in H&E-stained histopathology, recent work by Marques et al.10 shows. Their meta-learning policy finding method in finding the best augmentation policies showed massive advances over hand-designed augmentation policies, proving the worth of discovered augmentation policies.

Different innovative solutions have been found to overcome the problem of maintaining semantic integrity in the case when data diversity has to be enhanced. Glastonbury et al.7 designed procedures to describe the histomorphological variability and capture the spatial relations, gaining information in the implementation of effective augmentation procedures that would not alter the structures of tissues. The morphology-aware augmentation methods have been shown essential in their self-supervised learning model of predicting spatial RNA expression.

Karimi et al. conducted an experiment of multiscale decision aggregation and data augmentation in deep learning-based Gleason grading of prostate cancer using histopathology images40. Their contribution brought to the fore the need to have well-conceived augmentation strategies that acknowledge the hierarchical arrangement of histopathological features.

Weakly supervised and semi-supervised learning

Weak supervised learning has proven a feasible format to annotation problem in histopathology. Lu et al.33 reported data-efficient and weakly supervised computational pathology algorithm that has very low annotation burden and performance was also very high. Their CLAM framework formulated significant precepts that could be used to gain deep insight into using the label at an image level to perform pixel-level segmentation.

Li et al.14 came up with weakly-supervised histopathology image segmentation using self-attention mechanisms. They have proposed SA-MIL frameworks that pioneered new methods of capturing global associations between examples of images using few labeled characteristics. This work emphasized the significance of attention mechanisms in the management of the multi-scale character of histopathological images.

The combination of attention mechanisms and weakly supervised learning has been specifically powerful. The authors in Ben Hamida et al.29 providing the attention gate-based strategies on colon cancer histopathological image segmentation showed that the attention mechanism works properly to bridge the gap between weakly-supervised and pixel-wise accuracy.

The study by Guo et al.28 focused on the task of weak and noisy labels learning during histopathology image segmentation. The task at hand in their work has emphasized the need for strong learning strategies which can cope with the noise and ambiguity inherent in medical image annotations.

Chen et al.19 examined the process of semi-supervised segmentation of tissue through histopathologic images using consistency regularization and an uncertainty estimate. Their methodology showed that consistency-based learning achieved better results on segmentation tasks when they had to work with limited labeled data.

Van Eycke et al.34 have researched methods to decrease expert supervision of deep learning-based segmentation of H&E-stained histopathological images. This detailed evaluation offered some intuitive practices on minimization of annotation needs but preserving the quality of segmentation practices.

Multi-resolution and hierarchical learning

Wang et al.4 introduced a multi-resolution self-supervised learning framework to semantic segmentation of histopathology. Their MSF-WSI method dealt with the problem of learning features at different scales concurrently, and they have shown tremendous gains in accuracy of segmentation in different types of tissues.

In a number of recent papers, the significance of hierarchical feature learning has been underlined. Li et al.27 designed one model that would allow histology image segmentation and classification simultaneously based on multi-task learning. Their strategy demonstrated that common representations can be used to advantage numerous tasks associated with histopathological analysis.

Zhang et al.30 presented DTFD-MIL a two-level feature distillation multiple instances learning method in the classification of histopathology whole slide images. Hierarchical feature learning approach proved their hierarchical feature learning strategy to be useful in analysis of gigapixel images.

Clinical implementation and validation

Bringing computational pathology methods to clinical practice will necessitate paying attention to the practical deployment issues. Niazi et al.35 provided a comprehensive review of computational pathology systems for clinical deployment, highlighting the key requirements for successful implementation in routine pathology workflows.

Recent work has emphasized the importance of clinical validation for computational pathology methods. Shaban et al.36 developed context-aware convolutional neural networks for grading colorectal cancer histology images, demonstrating the clinical utility of AI-assisted diagnosis. Their work showed how deep learning methods can achieve diagnostic accuracy comparable to expert pathologists.

Shaban et al.38 further developed digital scores for abundance of tumor infiltrating lymphocytes that predict disease-free survival in oral squamous cell carcinoma. Their work demonstrated the prognostic value of AI-derived biomarkers in clinical decision-making.

Hou et al.39 addressed the fundamental question of whether to label or synthesize data for robust histopathology image analysis. Their comprehensive evaluation provided insights into the trade-offs between data annotation and synthetic data generation for improving model robustness.

Research gap

Despite significant advances in self-supervised learning for histopathology, existing approaches exhibit critical limitations that hinder their clinical adoption and effectiveness for image segmentation tasks. Current foundation models like Prov-GigaPath, UNI, Virchow, and CONCH, while achieving impressive scale with billions of images, primarily focus on classification tasks and lack specialized architecture for dense prediction problems2,3,11,12. Most self-supervised methods employ either contrastive learning or masked image modeling in isolation, missing the complementary benefits of hybrid approaches that could better capture the multi-scale nature of histopathological images15,24,32. Furthermore, existing data augmentation strategies in self-supervised frameworks are often borrowed from natural image domains without considering the unique semantic constraints of tissue morphology, potentially introducing artifacts that compromise diagnostic accuracy4,10, 37. The lack of adaptive augmentation mechanisms that can preserve histological 7 semantics while maximizing data diversity represents a significant gap, particularly for segmentation tasks where pixel-level accuracy is crucial for clinical utility7,14,25. Additionally, most current approaches fail to effectively integrate multi-resolution hierarchical learning with domain-specific augmentation strategies, limiting their ability to capture both cellular-level details and tissue-level context essential for robust histopathology image segmentation. The clinical validation of these methods remains limited, with few studies providing comprehensive evaluation by expert pathologists or demonstrating practical deployment in real-world clinical workflows35,36.

The Average Surface Distance [ASD] provides a more robust boundary accuracy measure:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{ASD}}\left( {P,G} \right) = \frac{1}{{\left| {\partial P} \right| + \left| {\partial G} \right|}}\left( {\mathop \sum \limits_{p \in \partial P} \mathop {{\text{min}}}\limits_{g \in \partial G} d\left( {p,g} \right) + \mathop \sum \limits_{g \in \partial G} \mathop {{\text{min}}}\limits_{p \in \partial P} d\left( {p,g} \right)} \right)$$\end{document}

Discussion

The results of comprehensive clinical validation posted in Fig. 11 are the final step of our analysis, and they prove the feasibility of our framework to be used in the clinical setting. The high ratings of pathologist acceptance and the substantial workflow efficiency prove the technical excellence of our method as well as its usability in the real-world diagnostic practice. Error analysis of the tissue type-wise gives vital information on how this can be implemented in the clinical settings and the near scores on the inter-observer agreement scores to that of manual annotation showing that our automated segmentation method is reliable in aiding pathological diagnosis.

Our experimental results demonstrate that the proposed self-supervised learning framework addresses critical limitations in current histopathology image segmentation approaches while achieving substantial performance improvements across multiple evaluation dimensions. The integration of masked image modeling with contrastive learning, coupled with adaptive augmentation strategies, proves highly effective for learning robust representations from limited labeled data. The consistent performance improvements across diverse cancer types and tissue characteristics validate the generalizability of our approach, addressing a key challenge in computational pathology where methods often fail to transfer across different institutions or imaging protocols.

The data efficiency analysis reveals perhaps the most clinically significant finding of our work. By achieving 95.6% of full performance with only 25% of labeled data, our framework substantially reduces the annotation burden on pathologists while maintaining diagnostic accuracy. This capability is particularly valuable for rare cancers or specialized tissue types where comprehensive annotation is challenging to obtain. Furthermore, the superior cross-dataset generalization performance [13.9% improvement] indicates that models trained on data from one institution can effectively transfer to others, addressing the persistent challenge of domain shift in medical imaging applications.

The architectural innovations in our framework, particularly the integration of adaptive augmentation with hierarchical self-supervised learning, address fundamental limitations identified in previous studies. Unlike foundation models that focus primarily on classification tasks, our approach is specifically designed for dense prediction problems with pixel-level accuracy requirements. The hybrid SSL strategy combining masked image modeling with contrastive learning captures complementary aspects of histopathological features that individual approaches miss. Our comprehensive evaluation across multiple metrics and datasets provides stronger evidence for clinical utility compared to studies that evaluate on limited datasets or metrics. The inclusion of boundary accuracy measures [Hausdorff Distance, Average Surface Distance] and clinical validation distinguishes our work from purely algorithmic studies, emphasizing practical applicability in diagnostic workflows.

Conclusion

This paper presented a novel self-supervised learning framework that addresses the challenge of limited pixel-level annotations in histopathology image segmentation through the integration of masked image modeling, contrastive learning, and adaptive data augmentation. Experimental evaluation across five diverse histopathology datasets demonstrates substantial performance gains, achieving a Dice coefficient of 0.825 [+ 4.3% over state-of-the-art], a 7.8% improvement in mIoU, and significant reductions in boundary errors [10.7% in Hausdorff Distance and 9.5% in Average Surface Distance]. The framework also shows strong data efficiency, requiring only 25% of labeled data to reach 95.6% of full performance, compared to 85.2% for supervised baselines. Cross-dataset experiments confirm robust generalization with a 13.9% Dice improvement, and clinical validation by expert pathologists [4.3/5.0 for clinical utility and 4.1/5.0 for boundary accuracy] highlights practical diagnostic relevance. Ablation studies further show that the hybrid self-supervised strategy contributes the largest performance boost [11.2% Dice improvement], while adaptive augmentation and hierarchical architecture provide essential domain-specific advantages. In conclusion, this framework represents a significant advancement in computational pathology by offering a scalable and annotation-efficient solution for robust segmentation with high diagnostic accuracy. Limitations include its current restriction to H&E-stained images, computational overhead during pre-training, and limited evaluation on extremely rare cancers, which define directions for future work.
