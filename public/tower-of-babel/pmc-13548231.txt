Validation and psychometric properties of the Persian version of the Physician Self-Efficacy to Manage Emotional Challenges (P-PSMEC) scale among medical students: a methodological cross-sectional study
Authors: Gholamreza Hamidkholgh, Alireza Mirzaei, Reza Nemati-Vakilabad, Mahzad Yousefian, Vahid Adiban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTranslate and culturally adapt the Physician Self-Efficacy to Manage Emotional Challenges (PSMEC) scale into Persian, then assess the validity and reliability of the Persian version of the PSMEC (P-PSMEC) among medical students.DesignMethodological cross-sectional study.SettingArdabil University of Medical Sciences, Ardabil, Iran.ParticipantsA convenience sample of 237 medical students participated in the study between July and December 2024.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe main focus was on evaluating the psychometric performance of the 17-item P-PSMEC scale, particularly its intended five-factor structure, examined through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Secondary outcomes encompassed face validity, content validity, internal convergent validity, discriminant validity, internal consistency and test-retest reliability. Additionally, an ordinal CFA sensitivity analysis was performed using the weighted least squares mean-adjusted and variance-adjusted (WLSMV) estimator.ResultsThe P-PSMEC showed acceptable face and content validity with a Scale Content Validity Index/AVE of 0.871. The main maximum likelihood-based CFA indicated good model fit: χ²/df=1.415, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA)=0.041, Comparative Fit Index (CFI)=0.984 and standardised root mean square residual (SRMR)=0.021. An ordinal CFA with WLSMV produced similar results: χ²=168.234, df=109, RMSEA=0.048, CFI=0.981, Tucker-Lewis Index=0.976, SRMR=0.049, with standardised loadings between 0.52 and 0.89. Evidence for internal convergent validity was supported by average variance extracted (AVE) values above 0.50 and composite reliability values above 0.70. Heterotrait-Monotrait ratio values were below 0.85, and maximum shared variance was lower than AVE, indicating good discriminant validity. Internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s α=0.902), and test-retest reliability was also strong (intraclass correlation coefficient=0.901).ConclusionsThe P-PSMEC shows good validity and reliability for measuring self-efficacy in managing emotional challenges among Persian-speaking medical students. The results support the proposed five-factor structure, although additional multisite, longitudinal, external validation and measurement-invariance studies are necessary.

Introduction

Self-efficacy, a central concept in Albert Bandura’s social-cognitive theory, refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to perform tasks and face challenges successfully.1 Bandura identifies four sources of self-efficacy: mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion and physiological and emotional states. This concept holds particular significance in medical education, as medical students are frequently confronted with stressful situations and emotional difficulties.2 The ability to manage these challenges not only impacts their mental health but also affects the quality of care they provide to patients.3 Self-efficacy in handling emotional challenges is recognised as a protective factor against burnout and work-related stress in medical environments.4 5

Numerous studies have indicated that medical students face elevated levels of psychological and emotional stress throughout their rigorous training programmes.6 Stress in students and healthcare professionals can stem from heavy academic workloads, rigorous clinical responsibilities and the emotional challenges of patient care and dealing with loss.7–9 These pressures can significantly affect mental health and overall well-being.10 Such experiences can be particularly challenging as they confront students with the realities of life and death.11 In these high-pressure environments, self-efficacy—the belief in one’s ability to succeed in specific situations—plays a pivotal role in helping students navigate these challenges.12 Strong self-efficacy can empower students to develop effective coping strategies, seek help when needed and ultimately foster resilience, thereby mitigating the negative impacts of stress on their mental well-being and academic performance.13

Within medical education, ‘emotional challenges’ encompass distinct but related constructs including emotional labour during difficult patient interactions, stress exposure from clinical responsibilities, coping with death and bereavement and responding to professional criticism. The Physician Self-Efficacy to Manage Emotional Challenges (PSMEC) scale was created and validated by Weurlander et al,14 specifically to assess physicians’ and medical students’ confidence in handling difficult emotional situations. The scale encompasses five dimensions: patient communication with complex patients and delivering bad news; responding to difficult questions and criticisms; medical knowledge and competence; educational competence in patient interactions and establishing and maintaining relationships with healthcare professionals.14

Effective communication with complex patients and delivering bad news are among the most challenging aspects of medical practice.15 Medical students must learn to engage with patients who may be resistant or emotionally vulnerable, a skill that requires strong communication skills and the ability to manage intense emotional reactions.16 Additionally, the capacity to respond professionally and effectively to challenging questions or criticisms from patients, families or colleagues is integral to their self-efficacy.14 17 Medical knowledge and competence encompass students’ confidence in applying their medical expertise and clinical skills to real-world scenarios, including their ability to diagnose, treat and manage complex medical conditions.18 19 Educational competence in patient interactions involves effectively informing patients and their families about medical conditions and necessary care.20 21 Establishing and maintaining relationships with other healthcare team members is essential for delivering comprehensive and coordinated patient care.22

The five factors of the PSMEC scale can be analysed in light of Bandura’s self-efficacy theory. Patient communication reflects mastery experiences and vicarious learning when engaging with emotionally vulnerable patients. Being questioned relates to vicarious experiences and verbal persuasion when handling criticism. Medical competence draws on mastery experiences from successful clinical performance. Educative competence involves both mastery and verbal persuasion in patient education. Relationships with healthcare professionals encompass vicarious experiences and social persuasion within interprofessional teams.

Studies conducted in Iran indicate that medical students face significant levels of stress and anxiety.23 24 A 2020 survey at the Tehran University of Medical Sciences revealed that over 60% of medical students reported experiencing moderate to severe stress.25 Similarly, research at Islamic Azad University in Tehran found that approximately 40% of students exhibited symptoms of burnout.23 These findings highlight the urgent necessity to address the mental and emotional well-being of medical students in Iran, particularly given the unique cultural and educational factors that contribute to their stress.26

The cultural stigma surrounding mental health in Iran creates additional challenges for medical students. Discussing mental health issues remains taboo, which often leads to feelings of isolation and a reluctance to seek help.27 28 Additionally, the specific stressors associated with Iranian medical education—such as rigorous academic demands, high expectations from family and society and the competitive nature of medical training—intensify these emotional challenges.23 26 Hierarchical relationships in Iranian educational settings may particularly influence how students perceive items related to questioning authority and professional relationships. Cultural attitudes toward emotional expression may also affect responses, as emotional vulnerability may be stigmatised.

Various tools have been developed to assess self-efficacy across different domains. Established instruments such as the General Self-Efficacy Scale and the Nursing Profession Self-Efficacy Scale29 have been widely used to assess self-efficacy among health professionals and students. However, these tools are more general and do not specifically target the unique emotional challenges medical students face. Emotional intelligence scales and clinical communication competence scales also address related but distinct constructs. In contrast, the PSMEC scale is specifically designed for physicians and medical students, making it a more precise and effective tool for identifying the distinct needs of this demographic in addressing emotional difficulties.

The PSMEC scale offers significant potential for evaluating and improving medical students’ mental health and professional performance.14 However, further research is necessary to confirm its reliability and relevance across diverse medical student populations and across various cultural and educational contexts. Cross-cultural validation is essential because constructs of self-efficacy in emotional contexts may differ across cultures, item interpretation may vary and measurement invariance might fail. This is particularly important in Iran, where cultural factors such as hierarchical relationships, authority attitudes and mental health stigma may influence responses. However, no validated Persian version of the PSMEC (P-PSMEC) scale currently exists.

This study aims to translate, culturally adapt and assess the psychometric properties of the P-PSMEC scale among Iranian medical students. We specifically examine its face validity, content validity, construct validity through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), as well as convergent and discriminant validity, internal consistency and test-retest reliability.

Methods

Design

This research was conducted as a methodological study employing a cross-sectional design in Ardabil province, Iran. The psychometric evaluation was systematically performed following the standardised protocols outlined in the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) guidelines.30 This approach ensured a rigorous and systematic assessment of the instrument’s measurement properties.

Setting and samples

This study was conducted among medical students at Ardabil University of Medical Sciences in Ardabil province, Iran, to validate and evaluate the psychometric properties of the P-PSMEC scale.

Participants were recruited from various academic years to ensure a diverse and representative sample. Data collection occurred between July and December 2024, and participants were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria for participants were current enrolment in a medical degree programme at Ardabil University of Medical Sciences, willingness to provide informed consent and proficiency in Persian to ensure accurate understanding and response to the scale items. Exclusion criteria included incomplete or inconsistent responses to the questionnaire and withdrawal of consent at any stage of the study.

The sample size was chosen based on methodological guidelines for CFA. Simulations show that sample size needs vary depending on model complexity, factor loadings, indicators per factor and estimator traits. A total of 237 participants was deemed sufficient for the planned CFA, considering the moderate complexity of the five-factor model and existing methodological recommendations.31 32 We thus refrained from solely using a participant-to-parameter ratio to justify if the sample size was sufficient.

PSMEC scale

The PSMEC scale, developed by Weurlander et al,14 consisted of 17 items and five subscales: subscale 1 (patient communication, items: 3, 5, 9, 13, 14, 17), subscale 2 (being questioned, items: 7, 10), subscale 3 (medical competence, items: 1, 4, 8, 12), subscale 4 (educative competence, items: 2, 11, 15) and subscale 5 (relationships with healthcare professionals, items: 6, 16). Participants evaluated each item on a six-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). The scale’s potential minimum and maximum scores are 17 and 102, respectively. The overall scale exhibited a Cronbach’s α of 0.912, indicating high reliability. The five subscales demonstrated good internal consistency: ‘Patient communication’ (α=0.870), ‘Being questioned’ (α=0.777), ‘Medical competence’ (α=0.804), ‘Educative competence’ (α=0.727) and ‘Relationships with healthcare professionals’ (α=0.708).

Translation procedure

With approval from the tool designer, Dr. Maria Weurlander, the translation of the PSMEC scale into Persian (P-PSMEC) was conducted through a careful and systematic process to ensure linguistic precision, cultural appropriateness and conceptual consistency. The procedure adhered to internationally recognised guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation of psychometric instruments, including those outlined by Beaton et al33 and the WHO.34 The process consisted of the following steps:

Forward translation: the original English version of the PSMEC scale was independently translated into Persian by two bilingual translators with expertise in both English and Persian. One translator was a professional with a background in medical sciences, while the other was a medical professional with no prior knowledge of the scale, ensuring a balance between linguistic accuracy and clinical relevance.

One translator was a professional with a background in medical sciences, while the other was a medical professional with no prior knowledge of the scale, ensuring a balance between linguistic accuracy and clinical relevance.

Any discrepancies between the translations were discussed and resolved to produce a single reconciled Persian version.

Psychometric testing

Face validity

20 medical students from the target population participated in the face validity assessment. They evaluated each item’s clarity, understandability, relevance and appropriateness from the perspective of the intended respondents. Their feedback helped identify and revise any unclear wording.35–37 Quantitative face validity was measured using the Item Impact Score (IIS).

Content validity

The content validity of the P-PSMEC scale was assessed using both qualitative and quantitative approaches to ensure the instrument’s relevance, clarity and alignment with the intended constructs. A panel of 10 experts, including 2 clinical psychologists, 2 medical educators, 2 practicing physicians, 1 psychometrician, 1 nursing researcher and 2 psychiatrists, was invited to evaluate the P-PSMEC scale. All experts had prior experience in instrument development or validation. The experts were asked to assess each item’s relevance, clarity and representativeness of the measured theoretical constructs. Feedback was collected on the appropriateness of the items, their alignment with the theoretical framework and their comprehensibility. Based on the experts’ qualitative feedback, revisions were made to improve the instrument’s content and ensure it effectively captured the intended constructs.37

Two indices were calculated to assess content validity: the Item Content Validity Index (I-CVI) and the Scale Content Validity Index (S-CVI). Each expert evaluated the relevance of each item on a 4-point scale (1=not relevant, 4=highly relevant). The I-CVI was calculated as the proportion of experts who rated an item 3 or 4 (relevant or highly relevant). Items with an I-CVI of 0.78 or higher were deemed to have excellent content validity. The S-CVI/Ave was calculated as the average of all I-CVIs across the scale, providing an overall measure of the instrument’s content validity. The following cut-off points were established for interpreting S-CVI/Ave values: S-CVI/Ave≥0.90 indicates excellent content validity at the scale level; S-CVI/Ave between 0.80 and 0.89 suggests good content validity, with minor revisions potentially needed; and S-CVI/Ave <0.80 signals poor content validity, indicating that significant revisions are necessary.38

Construct validity

To evaluate the construct validity of the P-PSMEC scale, CFA was conducted using IBM SPSS AMOS. CFA was chosen over exploratory factor analysis (EFA) because the theoretical structure of the original PSMEC scale was well-established, and the primary objective of this study was to confirm whether the Persian version adhered to the same underlying factor structure. However, we acknowledge that cultural and contextual factors can influence concepts such as self-efficacy, and EFA may have revealed alternative structures. We therefore recommend that future studies consider EFA to explore possible cultural modifications. While it is acknowledged that cultural and contextual factors can influence concepts such as self-efficacy, the decision to forgo EFA was based on the robustness of the existing theoretical framework. Furthermore, two of the subscales in the P-PSMEC consist of only two items, which may be deemed insufficient for EFA unless they exhibit very high factor loadings (>0.70). The presence of two-item factors presents potential identification and reliability concerns, which are addressed in the Discussion section. Given the established nature of the original scale, conducting EFA was not deemed necessary for this study, as the focus was on validating the existing factor structure rather than exploring new dimensions.40 The hypothesised model was specified based on the original PSMEC scale, and the maximum likelihood (ML) method was employed to estimate the model parameters.41 Although items were rated on a six-point Likert scale, their skewness and kurtosis values stayed within acceptable bounds for normality assumptions (|skewness|<1.96, |kurtosis|<1.96). Nonetheless, we recognise that these descriptive statistics alone do not justify treating the ordinal data as continuous in CFA. To thoroughly address the reviewer’s concern, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the weighted least squares mean-adjusted and variance-adjusted (WLSMV) estimator, which is tailored for ordinal categorical data and does not depend on the assumption of multivariate normality. The findings from this analysis, reported in the Results section, support the stability of our results. Still, because of the ordinal nature of the data, we suggest that future assessments use WLSMV or robust ML as the main estimation approach.

Multiple goodness-of-fit indices were used to evaluate the model’s fit, including the χ², where a non-significant p-value (p>0.05) or a χ²/df ratio <3 indicates good fit; the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), where values <0.08 indicate excellent fit; the Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), where values <0.08 indicate good fit; the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) and Incremental Fit Index (IFI), where values >0.90 indicate acceptable fit; the Parsimony Normed Fit Index (PNFI), where values >0.50 indicate acceptable fit and the Parsimony Goodness-of-Fit Index (PGFI), where values >0.50 indicate acceptable fit.42–45

The strength and statistical significance of the factor loadings were also assessed, with items having factor loadings ≥0.40 considered acceptable and t-values >1.96 (p<0.05) indicating statistical significance.

Convergent and discriminant validity

To assess convergent validity, the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) were calculated for each subscale of the P-PSMEC scale. The AVE measures the amount of variance captured by a construct relative to the variance due to measurement error, with values >0.50 indicating that the construct explains more than half of the variance in its items. The CR assesses the internal consistency of the items within each subscale, indicating how well the items measure the same underlying construct. A CR value >0.70 indicates strong internal consistency, suggesting that the items reliably measure the same construct. In the context of convergent validity, a high CR supports the idea that the scale effectively captures the intended construct, as it reflects the degree to which items correlate with one another. This is crucial for establishing convergent validity, as it demonstrates that the scale is measuring what it is supposed to measure.46 47

To evaluate discriminant validity, the maximum shared variance (MSV) and Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio were examined. The MSV measures the highest variance shared between a construct and any other construct in the model. Discriminant validity was confirmed if the MSV was lower than the AVE for each construct, indicating that the constructs share more variance with their items than with other constructs. Additionally, the HTMT ratio compares correlations between different constructs (heterotrait) with those within the same construct (monotrait). HTMT values <0.85 were considered acceptable, suggesting that the constructs are distinct from one another.48

Reliability

To evaluate the internal consistency of the P-PSMEC scale, multiple reliability metrics were calculated. Cronbach’s α was used as a baseline measure of internal consistency. A Cronbach’s α value ≥0.70 is considered acceptable, indicating good internal consistency. McDonald’s Omega (ωt) was also calculated, as it is a more robust alternative to Cronbach’s α, particularly when the assumption of tau-equivalence (equal factor loadings) is not met. A McDonald’s Omega value ≥0.70 is considered acceptable. Coefficient H was computed to assess the reliability of the constructs based on their factor loadings, providing a measure of how well the items represent their respective latent factors. A coefficient H value ≥0.70 is considered acceptable, indicating strong reliability. Finally, the greatest lower bound (GLB), a statistical measure used to estimate the lower limit of reliability for a scale, was calculated to provide a strong lower bound estimate of reliability, ensuring the scale’s reliability was not underestimated. A GLB value ≥0.70 is considered acceptable.49–55

For the test-retest assessment, 40 participants from the main sample were invited to repeat the P-PSMEC about 2 weeks after the first test. Selection of the retest subgroup was based on their availability and willingness to participate. Since this subgroup was not prospectively stratified and no formal comparison was planned with the other participants, differences between the retest group and the rest of the sample cannot be ruled out. An intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) value exceeding 0.75 signifies strong reliability.56

Patient and public involvement

None.

Data analysis

The data analysis for this study was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows and IBM SPSS AMOS. Before proceeding with the main analyses, the data were examined for missing values, outliers and normality. No missing data were present, as all questionnaires were completed in full. Outliers were identified using the Mahalanobis distance. Cases with Mahalanobis distances exceeding the critical χ2 value at p<0.001 were flagged as potential outliers and removed from the dataset to ensure the robustness of the analyses. Four cases were removed because their Mahalanobis distances exceeded the critical value, resulting in a final sample of 237 participants for the CFA. Additionally, skewness and kurtosis values were calculated, with absolute values <1.96 considered acceptable for assuming normality.57 For discriminant validity assessment, the HTMT ratio of correlations was calculated using a custom MATLAB script (The MathWorks) implementing Henseler et al’s algorithm.48 All statistical tests were conducted at a significance level of p<0.05.

Face validity

The qualitative assessment of face validity revealed that the instrument effectively captures the intended constructs perceived by medical students in the field. Feedback from participants indicated that the items were relevant and representative of the underlying theoretical framework. Furthermore, the clarity and comprehensibility of the questions were praised, suggesting that the tool is likely to engage respondents effectively and yield meaningful data. Quantitatively, the IIS ranged from 2.5 to 3.8, indicating strong agreement among respondents on the items’ relevance.

Content validity

The qualitative evaluation of content validity indicated that the items within the P-PSMEC scale were perceived as highly relevant and representative of the constructs they aim to measure. Expert feedback indicated that the items were appropriately aligned with the instrument’s theoretical underpinnings, demonstrating clarity and comprehensibility. This qualitative assessment suggests that the instrument’s content is suitable for its intended purpose and effectively addresses the key aspects of the measured constructs.

The quantitative analysis of content validity is presented in table 2. The S-CVI/Ave score of 0.871 indicated that the P-PSMEC met relevance standards at an acceptable level. Additionally, each item demonstrated strong content validity (I-CVI>0.78, K* > 0.74), reflecting that experts consistently rated these items as highly relevant. No items were revised or removed during content validation. These overall results reaffirmed the robust content validity of the P-PSMEC scale, confirming that the items effectively measured the intended constructs.

To assess whether treating the six-point Likert-type indicators as continuous variables influenced the CFA outcomes, we conducted a supplementary sensitivity analysis using the WLSMV estimator, appropriate for ordinal categorical data. We maintained the same five-factor model and included the theoretically justified correlated residuals between items 14 and 17, as well as items 8 and 12. The WLSMV model demonstrated a good fit to the data (χ²=168.234, df=109, p<0.001, RMSEA=0.048, 90% CI 0.036 to 0.060, CFI=0.981, TLI=0.976, SRMR=0.049). Standardised factor loadings ranged from 0.73 to 0.91 (p<0.001). The factor loadings and latent factor correlations closely resembled those from the ML estimation, with mean absolute differences of 0.03 for loadings and 0.05 for correlations. Overall, this sensitivity analysis confirms that the CFA results are consistent regardless of whether Likert-type indicators are considered continuous, supporting the robustness of the primary analysis’s five-factor structure interpretation.

Reliability

The internal consistency of the P-PSMEC scale was thoroughly evaluated using various reliability metrics, which demonstrated excellent reliability for the overall instrument. The GLB was found to be 0.928, indicating a strong lower bound estimate of reliability. The coefficient H was also 0.914, further supporting the instrument’s reliability. McDonald’s Omega coefficient (ωt) was reported at 0.918, and Cronbach’s α was 0.902. These indices collectively indicate that the P-PSMEC exhibits high internal consistency, affirming its reliability for measuring the intended constructs.

In terms of stability, the P-PSMEC scale was assessed using a test-retest method involving 40 medical students, with a 2-week interval between assessments. The results showed the total instrument’s ICC of 0.901 (95% CI 0.853 to 0.956). This high ICC value reflects strong stability over time, confirming that the P-PSMEC scale maintains consistent measurements across repeated administrations. Floor and ceiling effects

Floor and ceiling effects were assessed by examining the percentage of respondents scoring at the minimum (17) and maximum (102) possible scores on the total P-PSMEC scale. No significant floor or ceiling effects were observed, with <5% of respondents scoring at either extreme (minimum: 2.1%, maximum: 3.4%), indicating that the scale has good range and sensitivity.

Discussion

The present study aimed to validate and assess the psychometric properties of the P-PSMEC scale among medical students. Self-efficacy in managing emotional challenges is a critical factor in physicians’ professional development, as it directly affects their ability to cope with the emotional demands of clinical practice. Given the high-stress nature of medical training and practice, understanding and measuring this construct is essential for identifying areas of improvement and implementing targeted interventions. The adaptation of the PSMEC scale for the Persian-speaking population will provide a valuable tool for evaluating self-efficacy in managing emotional challenges, thereby enhancing emotional resilience and overall well-being among medical students and future healthcare professionals. However, it is important to emphasise that this study provides initial psychometric validation; full validation requires replication across institutions, longitudinal analysis and measurement invariance testing.

The qualitative evaluation of face validity for the P-PSMEC scale revealed that the instrument was well-received by medical students, with participants confirming that the items were relevant, clear and representative of the intended constructs. The quantitative analysis further supported this, with an IIS ranging from 2.5 to 3.8, indicating strong agreement among respondents regarding the relevance and appropriateness of the items. This high face validity can be attributed to the rigorous adaptation process, which included translation, back-translation and pilot testing with the target population, ensuring that the items were culturally and contextually appropriate for Persian-speaking medical students. In contrast, the original PSMEC scale14 did not undergo a face validity assessment, resulting in no documented evidence of how well the target population perceived the items regarding relevance, clarity or representativeness. The original PSMEC publication did not include a formal face validity assessment, making direct comparison of face-validity evidence with the Persian version unfeasible. In this study, participants from the target population offered feedback on the clarity and understandability of the items as part of the adaptation process.

The qualitative evaluation of content validity for the P-PSMEC scale indicated that the items were highly relevant and aligned with the instrument’s theoretical framework. Expert feedback confirmed the clarity, comprehensibility and representativeness of the items. Quantitatively, the S-CVI/Ave was 0.871, and all items demonstrated strong content validity (I-CVI >0.78, K* > 0.74), meeting acceptable standards for relevance. The robust content validity of the P-PSMEC scale can be attributed to the involvement of experts in the field during the adaptation process, ensuring that the items accurately reflected the constructs of self-efficacy in managing emotional challenges while considering the cultural and linguistic context of the target population. In contrast, the original PSMEC scale14 did not undergo a content validity assessment, resulting in no documented evidence of how well the items represented the intended constructs or whether experts reviewed them for relevance and clarity. The original PSMEC publication did not include a formal content-validity assessment with the indices used in this study. As a result, the differences in content-validity evidence between the two versions should not be seen as an indication that the original items were less accurate or complete.

The construct validity of the P-PSMEC scale showed a strong fit to the data, with excellent fit indices (eg, RMSEA=0.041, CFI=0.984, SRMR=0.021) and significant factor loadings between 0.59 and 0.91. The CFA results supported the proposed five-factor structure, although the model necessitated adding correlated residuals between items 14 and 17 and between items 8 and 12 based on theoretical justification. The P-PSMEC also exhibited strong convergent validity, with all subscales showing AVE values >0.5 and CR values exceeding 0.7. Additionally, robust discriminant validity was indicated by HTMT values <0.85 and MSV values lower than AVE. While the CFA supports the five-factor structure, alternative models, such as a unidimensional model, were considered. However, the five-factor model was retained due to its superior fit indices and the theoretical justification for distinguishing between different dimensions of self-efficacy. The inclusion of correlated residuals, specified based on modification indices and theoretical content overlap, improved model fit. However, the initial model without these modifications also showed acceptable fit (χ²=208.315, RMSEA=0.068, CFI=0.928), suggesting the original structure was largely supported. Each of the five factors identified in the original PSM scale14 captures unique aspects of self-efficacy relevant to medical education. Notably, ‘patient communication’ and ‘educative competence’ exhibited the highest loadings on the global factor, further supporting the construct’s multidimensional nature. However, minor differences in factor loadings between the original and Persian versions of the PSMEC scale warrant further discussion. These variations may be influenced by cultural factors unique to the Iranian context. For instance, the emphasis on hierarchical relationships in Iranian educational settings might affect how medical students perceive their competence in patient communication and educator roles. Additionally, cultural attitudes towards authority and mentorship may affect how students engage with these dimensions of self-efficacy. Such cultural nuances could explain why certain subscales perform differently in the Persian version compared with the original scale. Hence, the retention of the 5-factor model over simpler structures is justified by its strong empirical support and theoretical relevance, providing a more nuanced understanding of self-efficacy in medical contexts.

Several factors can logically justify the differences in the findings of construct validity between the P-PSMEC scale and the original instrument. First, the P-PSMEC scale underwent a rigorous adaptation process, including translation, back-translation and pilot testing. This ensured that the items were culturally and contextually appropriate for Persian-speaking medical students. This process likely contributed to the strong goodness-of-fit indices and high factor loadings observed in the P-PSMEC scale, as the items were tailored to the specific emotional challenges faced by this population. In contrast, the original instrument was developed for a different cultural context, which may have resulted in slightly different factor structures and loadings. Second, the involvement of experts and target population participants during the validation of the P-PSMEC scale ensured that the items were theoretically sound, practically relevant and comprehensible. This dual perspective may have enhanced the clarity and relevance of the items, leading to stronger construct validity. Finally, the quantitative evidence provided by the P-PSMEC scale, such as the AVE, CR and HTMT values, strengthens the credibility and reliability of the instrument. In contrast, the original instrument did not report such detailed quantitative assessments.

The reliability results of the P-PSMEC scale demonstrated strong internal consistency and stability compared with the original instrument. For the total scale, the P-PSMEC scale showed excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha (α) of 0.902, McDonald’s omega (ωt) of 0.918, coefficient H of 0.914 and GLB of 0.928, which are comparable to the original scale’s Cronbach’s α (α=0.912).14 However, the P-PSMEC scale provided a more comprehensive reliability assessment by including additional measures (ωt, H and GLB), which were not reported for the original instrument.14 At the subscale level, the P-PSMEC scale exhibited higher internal consistency across all dimensions, with Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.828 to 0.886, compared with the original scale’s range of 0.708 to 0.870.

Regarding stability, the P-PSMEC scale demonstrated strong test-retest reliability, with ICC values ranging from 0.811 to 0.880 for subscales and 0.901 for the total scale, indicating excellent consistency over time. This reliability aspect was not assessed in the original instrument,14 highlighting a significant advantage of the P-PSMEC scale.

The overall scale shows high internal consistency (α=0.902), suggesting it is reliable. However, high reliability coefficients alone do not confirm item redundancy. Since item redundancy was not specifically examined through detailed inter-item correlation or content analyses, this should be interpreted with caution and explored further in future studies.

Because this study validates a translated instrument, measurement invariance relative to the original scale is ideally required. We did not test configural, metric or scalar invariance across cultures. Therefore, claims of construct equivalence across cultural contexts should be interpreted cautiously. This is an important direction for future research.

Potential educational implications

The P-PSMEC might be useful in medical education to identify self-efficacy in emotionally difficult clinical situations. However, this study did not assess whether P-PSMEC scores can predict resilience, burnout, clinical performance or responses to educational interventions. As a result, these uses are still hypotheses for future research, not confirmed applications. Future intervention and longitudinal studies could explore whether P-PSMEC scores help in designing or assessing targeted educational programmes.

Limitations and strengths

Several limitations should be taken into account. First, reliance on a convenience sample from a single institution may limit the generalisability of the findings. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents evaluation of predictive validity and responsiveness to change. Third, the use of self-reported data may introduce response bias. Fourth, external convergent validity was not tested using an independent, theoretically related measure. Fifth, measurement invariance across different cultural or linguistic groups was not assessed. Sixth, two subscales consist of only two items, which may limit their ability to fully represent the underlying latent constructs and may result in less stable factor estimates than factors represented by three or more indicators. Although these factors demonstrated acceptable psychometric performance in the current sample, high inter-item correlations alone do not overcome the limitations associated with two-item factors. Therefore, the five subscale scores should not be assumed to have equally strong evidence for independent interpretation. Future research should replicate these subscales in larger and more diverse samples and assess whether additional theoretically relevant items are warranted. Finally, the primary CFA employed ML estimation, although a sensitivity analysis using ordinal WLSMV yielded broadly comparable findings.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. The translation and cultural adaptation followed established procedures intended to maximise linguistic accuracy, cultural relevance and conceptual equivalence, although measurement equivalence was not empirically tested. The study also assessed multiple measurement properties, including face and content validity, CFA, internal convergent and discriminant validity, internal consistency and test-retest reliability. Additionally, the ordinal CFA sensitivity analysis using WLSMV provided complementary evidence supporting the robustness of the factor-analytic findings.

Conclusions

The P-PSMEC showed promising validity and reliability evidence for measuring self-efficacy in handling emotional challenges among Persian-speaking medical students. The results support the hypothesised five-factor structure and demonstrate acceptable internal consistency and test–retest reliability. However, external convergent validity, predictive validity and cross-cultural measurement invariance were not confirmed. Additionally, two subscales have only two items each, so their scores should be interpreted with caution. The study was conducted at a single institution with a convenience sample. As a result, these findings should be viewed as initial psychometric evidence rather than conclusive validation. More multisite research, including external criterion measures, longitudinal approaches and invariance testing, is needed before broader claims about the generalisability and independent interpretation of the P-PSMEC can be made.
