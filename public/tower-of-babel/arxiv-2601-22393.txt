Proof Complexity of Linear Logics
Amirhossein Akbar Tabatabai, Raheleh Jalali, Bernoulli Institute, University of Groningen, Department of Computer Science, University of Bath

Abstract
Proving proof-size lower bounds for 𝐋𝐊 \mathbf{LK} , the sequent calculus for classical propositional logic, remains a major open problem in proof complexity. We shed new light on this challenge by isolating the power of structural rules, showing that their combination is extremely stronger than any single rule alone. We establish exponential (resp. sub-exponential) proof-size lower bounds for 𝐋𝐊 \mathbf{LK} without contraction (resp. weakening) for formulas with short 𝐋𝐊 \mathbf{LK} -proofs. Concretely, we work with the Full Lambek calculus with exchange, 𝐅𝐋 𝐞 \mathbf{FL_{e}} , and its contraction-extended variant, 𝐅𝐋 𝐞𝐜 \mathbf{FL_{ec}} , substructural systems underlying linear logic. We construct families of 𝐅𝐋 𝐞 \mathbf{FL_{e}} -provable (resp. 𝐅𝐋 𝐞𝐜 \mathbf{FL_{ec}} -provable) formulas that require exponential-size (resp. sub-exponential-size) proofs in affine linear logic 𝐀𝐋𝐋 \mathbf{ALL} (resp. relevant linear logic 𝐑𝐋𝐋 \mathbf{RLL} ), but admit polynomial-size proofs once contraction (resp. weakening) is restored. This yields exponential lower bounds on proof-size of 𝐅𝐋 𝐞 \mathbf{FL_{e}} -provable formulas in 𝐀𝐋𝐋 \mathbf{ALL} and hence for 𝐌𝐀𝐋𝐋 \mathbf{MALL} , 𝐀𝐌𝐀𝐋𝐋 \mathbf{AMALL} , and full classical linear logic 𝐂𝐋𝐋 \mathbf{CLL} . Finally, we exhibit formulas with polynomial-size 𝐅𝐋 𝐞 \mathbf{FL_{e}} -proofs that nevertheless require exponential-size proofs in cut-free 𝐋𝐊 \mathbf{LK} , establishing exponential speed-ups between various linear calculi and their cut-free counterparts. Keywords. Proof complexity, linear logics, substructural logics, exponential lower bound, (monotone) feasible interpolation, vector addition systems, Chu’s construction.



1
Introduction

Proof complexity is an interdisciplinary field that uses tools from logic, complexity theory, and combinatorics. The main goal of proof complexity is to understand the complexity of theorem-proving procedures. In other words, it aims to determine how efficient proof systems are when they prove their theorems. This naturally leads to the central problem of finding lower bounds on proof size in proof systems. The difficulty of this problem is what makes it interesting: its deep connection with computational complexity.

The field was motivated by the seminal work of Cook and Reckhow
[
16
]
, who established a fundamental connection between proof size and the separation of complexity classes. They showed that NP
\neq
coNP iff there are superpolynomial lower bounds for
every
proof system for
classical
propositional logic,
\mathsf{CPC}
. This conjectured lower bound for arbitrary proof systems has never been established. In fact, despite decades of research, even the single case of the Frege system (the standard proof system for
\mathsf{CPC}
, polynomially equivalent to sequent calculus
\mathbf{LK}
and natural deduction) still resists all known lower-bound techniques.

Facing such a strict barrier, progress has therefore focused on weaker systems. The first natural approach was restricting the form of formulas and deduction rules (sometimes in algebraic and semi-algebraic disguise). This led to a plethora of exponential lower bound results for Resolution
[
20
]
, the Nullstellensatz system
[
7
]
, Cutting Planes
[
10
,
40
]
, Polynomial Calculus
[
15
,
44
]
, and bounded-depth Frege systems
[
1
,
8
,
39
,
34
]
.

Another successful way of avoiding the
\mathbf{LK}
barrier is to change classical logic itself, either by weakening it (e.g., forbidding full involution
\neg\neg A\to A
) or by extending the language (e.g., with modalities). As these logics are usually
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-hard, their study is closely connected to another notoriously hard problem in complexity theory, namely
\mathrm{NP}
vs.
\mathrm{PSPACE}
. Since this problem is, in a sense, easier than
\mathrm{NP}
vs.
\mathrm{coNP}
, one may expect stronger results on this front. Indeed, there are many known exponential lower bounds for these systems, including Frege systems for intuitionistic and some modal logics
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
, superintuitionistic and modal logics of infinite branching
[
28
,
29
]
, intuitionistic substructural logics
[
26
]
, and propositional default logic
[
9
]
. Owing to their expressive power and numerous applications in computer science, the proof complexity of these non-classical systems has received extensive attention in recent years.

1.1
Our Contribution

In this work, we follow the same strategy of weakening classical logic; however, our approach differs conceptually. To illustrate the role of full involution, the backbone of
classical reasoning

structural rules

By removing contraction, weakening, and the cut rule one at a time, (sometimes even with controlled structural rules provided by linear exponentials), we obtain three main results that isolate the precise contribution of each rule to the strength of classical proof systems. 1.2
Methods

To prove our main theorems as outlined above, we use three different interdisciplinary methods.

1.2.1
\mathbf{ALL}
and Chu’s Translation

Chu’s construction is a well-known categorical method for generating self-dual categories and, consequently, for constructing categorical models of classical linear logic from models of intuitionistic linear logic
[
6
,
14
]
,
[
25
, Section 3.3]
. We adapt this construction to define a translation function on formulas, which we call
Chu’s translation
. Via this translation, proofs in classical linear systems can be feasibly transformed into proofs in their intuitionistic counterparts.

A key observation is that Chu’s translation preserves a robust class of formulas. In particular, the formulas for which exponential lower bounds are known in intuitionistic substructural systems are invariant under the translation. This allows us to lift existing exponential lower bounds from intuitionistic substructural logics to their classical variants.

This provides, to the best of our knowledge, one of the first systematic uses of Chu’s construction as a novel and affective framework for establishing lower bounds in proof complexity.

1.2.2
\mathbf{RLL}
and Vector Addition Systems

Counter machines are computational models that formalize computation via transitions over vectors in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. They are closely connected to substructural and linear logics, where proofs encode computation by representing natural numbers as the multiplicity of formulas in a sequent. This correspondence links reachability in counter machines to provability in these logics
[
36
,
46
,
35
]
. In particular, for
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
and its extension
\mathbf{RLL}
, this connection, together with known hardness results for counter machine reachability, implies that deciding provability is Ackermann-hard
[
46
,
35
]
.

Our strategy for proving the main theorem for
\mathbf{RLL}
is to revisit the connection to counter machines, but restrict to the simplest type:
contractive vector addition systems
. This allows us to limit the corresponding
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
-provable formulas to a variant of Horn formulas, for which we show that short
\mathbf{LK}
-proofs exist. At the same time, the reachability problem for these machines is
\mathrm{EXPSPACE}
-hard, enabling us to establish the
\mathbf{RLL}
-hardness of these formulas, highlighting the strength of our separation.

1.2.3
Cut-freeness and Feasible Interpolation

Feasible interpolation, introduced in
[
31
,
32
]
, is one of the primary tools for proving exponential lower bounds for classical proof systems. Moreover, much of the literature on modal and superintuitionistic proof complexity revolves around the feasibility of the disjunction property, a variant of feasible interpolation
[
12
,
13
,
41
,
17
,
38
,
27
,
3
]
. See
[
2
]
for a recent survey.

Our reference point is Krajíček’s exponential separation between
\mathbf{LK}
and
\mathbf{LK}^{-}

[
33
]
, which motivates our approach to isolating the power of cut. The key step in that separation is showing that
\mathbf{LK}^{-}
has the
monotone feasible interpolation property
: given an
\mathbf{LK}^{-}
-proof
\pi
of a sequent
A(\bar{p},\bar{r})\Rightarrow B(\bar{p},\bar{s})
,
where
A(\bar{p},\bar{r})
is monotone in
\bar{p}
, there exists a monotone circuit
C(\bar{p})
of size
|C(\bar{p})|\leq|\pi|^{O(1)}
computing a Craig interpolant for
A(\bar{p},\bar{r})\rightarrow B(\bar{p},\bar{s})
. The separation then follows by combining this with known exponential lower bounds in monotone circuit complexity to produce an implication that is easily provable in
\mathbf{LK}
, but has exponentially hard interpolating monotone circuits and hence large
\mathbf{LK}^{-}
-proofs.

The strategy for our third theorem follows the same pattern, but now we need an implication with a short
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
-proof. We achieve this by simulating classical reasoning in
\mathbf{LK}
within the substructural setting of
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
using additional initial sequents. The main challenge is to mimic contraction and weakening with sequents whose formulas contain at most one atomic formula, ensuring that the monotone feasible interpolation property of
\mathbf{LK^{-}}
remains applicable.

Existing Hard Theorems

In this section, we introduce some known hard theorems for classical and non-classical proof systems that we will use later. We start by recalling the formulas
Clique_{n,k}
and

, which play a central role in all the hard theorems we present.

Definition 2
.

[
33
, Section 13.5]

Let

. We identify

(undirected,
simple and without loops) on

n

\binom{n}{2}

A graph contains a

k

containing a clique of size at least
k

admitting an

m

We define propositional formulas characterizing these sets. Take

\bullet

\bigvee_{i\in[n]}r_{ui}

\bullet

\bullet

\neg r_{ui}\vee\neg r_{vi}

\bullet

\neg r_{ui}\vee\neg r_{vj}\vee p_{ij}

\bullet

Let

Lemma 13

∎

In this subsection, we establish a form of feasible conservativity for sequent calculi of the logics in

An Exponential Separation

In this subsection, we apply the results of Subsection

.

The

-provable sequents

5
Relevant Linear Logic

Recall that a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is called
sub-exponential
if

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{2^{n^{\epsilon}}}=0,

for any
\epsilon>0
. For instance, for any constant
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the function
f(n)=2^{(\log n)^{k}}
is sub-exponential. In this section, we show that for any sub-exponential monotone function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, there exists a sequence
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
-provable formulas such that
|A_{n}|\geq n
,
A_{n}
has an
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proof of size
|A_{n}|^{O(1)}
while any
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof of
A_{n}
has size greater than
f(|A_{n}|)
. Our strategy uses the connection between reachability in a class of counter machines known as contractive vector addition systems with states and provability in
\mathbf{RLL}
, together with the computational hardness of the reachability problem for these machines. We begin by presenting the machines.

5.1
Vector Addition Systems

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be a positive natural number. By
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
we denote the vector whose components are all zero. For any
1\leq i\leq d
, we write
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
for the vector that has value one in the
i
th component and zero in all other components.
A
d
-dimensional Vector Addition System with States
(VASS, for short) is a pair
\mathcal{V}=(Q,T)
, where
Q
is a finite set of states, and
T\subseteq Q\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times Q
is a finite set of
rules
. A VASS is called
ordinary
if, for every rule
(q,a,r)\in T
, there exists an
1\leq i\leq d
such that either
a=\mathbf{e}_{i}
or
a=-\mathbf{e}_{i}
.
A
configuration
is a pair
(q,u)\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
. Given two configurations
(q,u)
and
(r,v)
, we write
(q,u)\to(r,v)
if there exists a rule
(q,a,r)\in T
such that
v=u+a
. Thus,
(q,u)\to(r,u+a)
for any rule
(q,a,r)\in T
, provided that
u+a\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
A sequence
c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
of configurations is called a
transition sequence
from
c_{0}
to
c_{m}
if
c_{i}\to c_{i+1}
for all
0\leq i<m
. The reflexive-transitive closure of
\to
is denoted by
\to^{*}
. That is,
(q,u)\to^{*}(r,v)
iff there is a transition sequence from
(q,u)
to
(r,v)
.

For any VASS
\mathcal{V}
, we are also interested in a variant of the relation
\to
, denoted by
\to_{c}
. We write
(q,u)\to_{c}(r,v)
if either there exists a rule
(q,a,r)\in T
such that
v=u+a
, or
q=r
and there exist
1\leq i\leq d
and
w\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
u=w+2\mathbf{e}_{i}
and
v=w+\mathbf{e}_{i}
. In other words,
\to_{c}
is the union of
\to
and all transitions of the form

(q,w+2\mathbf{e}_{i})\to_{c}(q,w+\mathbf{e}_{i}).

This additional transition is called
contraction
3
3

3

The dual of our contraction transition, i.e.,
(q,w+\mathbf{e}_{i})\to(q,w+2\mathbf{e}_{i})
is called the
expansion rule
. This rule is usually used in a backward manner to capture the proof-theoretical contraction rule. To simplify the presentation and avoiding carrying this backwardness throughout, we have introduced contractive transition.
. The role of this transition is similar to the contraction rule in sequent calculi. It transforms a configuration
(q,(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}+1,\ldots,a_{d}))
to the configuration
(q,(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i},\ldots,a_{d}))
, as long as
a_{i}\geq 1
. However, it doses not allow the transition when
a_{i}=0
. The situation is similar to the contraction rule in proof theory, which allows the merging of multiple occurrences of a formula into one, but not their elimination.

The reflexive-transitive closure of
\to_{c}
is denoted by
\to_{c}^{*}
and one can adapt the corresponding notion of transition sequence for
\to_{c}^{*}
. Note that for any
q\in Q
, we have

(q,(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i},\ldots,a_{d}))\to_{c}(q,(a_{1},\ldots,1,\ldots,a_{d}))
when
a_{i}\geq 1
. It is enough to apply the contraction rule repeatedly on the
i
th component till reaching
1
. This way, one can transform any configuration
(q,u)
to a configuration in the form
(q,v)
, where the components of
v
are either zero or one. We write
\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}
, for such vectors, where
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, for any
1\leq i\leq d
.

Given configurations
(q,u)
and
(r,v)
, if
(q,u)\to^{*}(r,v)
, then we say that
(r,v)
is
reachable
from
(q,u)
in
\mathcal{V}
. If
(q,u)\to^{*}(r,w)
for some
w\geq v
, then we say that
(r,v)
is
coverable
by
(q,u)
in
\mathcal{V}
. Similarly, we define
contractive reachability
by replacing
\to^{*}
with
\to_{c}^{*}
. We do not define contractive coverabillity as it is not needed.

By the
VASS reachability problem
, we mean the problem of taking as input
(\mathcal{V},q,r)
and deciding whether
(q,\mathbf{0})\to^{*}(r,\mathbf{0})
. By the
VASS coverability problem
, we mean the problem of taking as input
(\mathcal{V},q,r)
and deciding whether there exists
v\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
(q,\mathbf{0})\to^{*}(r,v)
. Similarly, we define the
contractive VASS reachability problem
by replacing
\to^{*}
with
\to_{c}^{*}
.

The following is the hardness theorem that we will use. Note its high complexity and that it concerns space complexity.

Theorem 21
.

[
37
,
42
]

The problem of VASS coverability is
\mathrm{EXPSPACE}
-complete.

Our aim is to establish a reduction from the VASS coverability problem to the contractive VASS reachability problem, showing that the latter is
\mathrm{EXPSPACE}
-hard. We first provide a simple lemma.

Lemma 22
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=(Q,T)
be a VASS, let
q,r\in Q
and
u,v\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
. If
(q,u)\to^{*}_{c}(r,v)
, then there is
w\geq v\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
(q,u)\to^{*}(r,w)
.

Proof.

The proof is easy and hence deferred to Section
7
.
∎

Theorem 23
.

There is a polynomial-time reduction from the VASS coverability problem to the contractive VASS reachability problem. Hence, the contractive VASS reachability problem is
EXPSPACE
-hard.

Proof.

For the reduction, given any VASS
\mathcal{V}=(Q,T)
and states
q,r\in Q
, we construct a new VASS
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}=(Q^{\prime},T^{\prime})
as follows. Introduce a new state
q_{\mathrm{new}}
and set
Q^{\prime}=Q\cup\{q_{\mathrm{new}}\}
. Then define
T^{\prime}=T\cup R
, where
R
consists of all the rules of the form
(r,-\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i},q_{\mathrm{new}}),\quad\text{for each }b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.
For any
q,r\in Q
, we claim that
(r,\mathbf{0})
is coverable by
(q,\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}
if and only if
(q,\mathbf{0})\to_{c}^{*}(q_{\mathrm{new}},\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Since the construction of
(\mathcal{V}^{\prime},q,q_{\mathrm{new}})
from
(\mathcal{V},q,r)
is clearly polynomial-time, we obtain a polynomial-time reduction.

Now, we prove the claim. For the forward direction of the claim, we assume that
(r,\mathbf{0})
is coverable by
(q,\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}
and we want to prove that
(q,\mathbf{0})\to_{c}^{*}(q_{\mathrm{new}},\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. If
(r,\mathbf{0})
is coverable by
(q,\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}
, then there exists
w\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
(q,\mathbf{0})\to^{*}(r,w)
in
\mathcal{V}
. Since all the rules of
\mathcal{V}
are also present in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, we have
(q,\mathbf{0})\to^{*}(r,w)
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Using the contraction rule, each non-zero component of
w
can be reduced to
1
, yielding
(q,\mathbf{0})\to_{c}^{*}(r,\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i})
for some
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Finally, applying the rule
(r,-\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i},q_{\mathrm{new}})
, we obtain
(q,\mathbf{0})\to_{c}^{*}(q_{\mathrm{new}},\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
.

For the backward direction of the claim, let
(q,\mathbf{0})\to_{c}^{*}(q_{\mathrm{new}},\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. By Lemma
22
, we obtain
(q,\mathbf{0})\to^{*}(q_{\mathrm{new}},w)
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, for some
w\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
. Let
c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
be the transition sequence from
(q,\mathbf{0})
to
(q_{\mathrm{new}},\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
.
As the only rules in
T^{\prime}
that results in
q_{\mathrm{new}}
are
(r,-\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i},q_{\mathrm{new}})
, the rule to transform
c_{m-1}
to
c_{m}
must be one of these rules. Therefore,
c_{m-1}=(r,w+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i})
. Moreover, as there is no rule in
T^{\prime}
to transform
q_{\mathrm{new}}
into another state, there cannot be any occurrence of
q_{\mathrm{new}}
in
c_{0},\ldots,c_{m-1}
. Consequently, the sequence
c_{0},\ldots,c_{m-1}
does not use the rules in
R
and hence it is also a valid transition sequence in
\mathcal{V}
. Thus,
(q,\mathbf{0})\to^{*}(r,w+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i})
. Therefore,
(r,\mathbf{0})
is coverable by
(q,\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}
.
∎

The (contractive) reachability problem for VASS is polynomial-time reducible to the (contractive) reachability problem for ordinary VASS
[
35
, Lemma 3.5]
. Therefore:

Corollary 24
.

The reachability problem for contractive ordinary VASS is
\mathrm{EXPSPACE}
-hard.

5.2
A Sub-exponential Separation

So far, we have established a computationally hard problem. Our goal is to connect contractive ordinary VASS reachability to provability in
\mathbf{RLL}
.
Such connections between a variant of VASS (alternating branching VASS with full zero tests) and substructural and linear logics with the contraction rules are well-known and have been used to prove lower bounds on the decision complexity of these logics
[
46
,
35
]
. Here, we exploit this connection to establish a
proof-size
lower bound for
\mathbf{RLL}
.

To describe the connection, let
\mathcal{V}=(Q,T)
be an ordinary VASS. Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}
be atoms in the language representing the states in
Q
, and let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
be a disjoint set of atoms representing the
d
components of a vector in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. Then, any configuration
(q,u)
can be encoded as the multiset

\theta(q,u)=\{q,p_{1}^{u_{1}},\ldots,p_{d}^{u_{d}}\},

where
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})
and
p^{m}
denotes the multiset
\{p,p,\ldots,p\}
consisting of
m
copies of
p
. Note that
p^{m}
is
not
the
m
-fold multiplication of
p
with itself.

Transitions of
\mathcal{V}
are encoded as
\mathcal{L}^{u}
-formulas as follows: a rule
(q,a,r)
is encoded as
(q\to r*p_{i})
if
a=\mathbf{e}_{i}
, and as
(q*p_{i}\to r)
if
a=-\mathbf{e}_{i}
. Note that as
\mathcal{V}
is an ordinary VASS,
a
has only these options. Let
Th_{\mathcal{V}}
denote the set of all such formulas. Then one can easily see that if
(q,v)\to^{*}_{c}(r,v)
, then

\mathbf{FL_{ec}}\vdash(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}\theta(q,u)\Rightarrow\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}\theta(r,v),

which in particular implies that if
(q,\mathbf{0})\to^{*}_{c}(r,\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}
, then

\mathbf{FL_{ec}}\vdash(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,q\Rightarrow r.

Fortunately, the converse also holds. In fact, we have the following theorem:

Theorem 25
.

[
46
]
[
35
, Claim 4.9.1]
For any ordinary VASS
\mathcal{V}=(Q,T)
and any states
q,r\in Q
, the following are equivalent:

•

(q,\mathbf{0})\to^{*}_{c}(r,\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}
,

•

\mathbf{FL_{ec}}\vdash(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,q\Rightarrow r
,

•

\mathbf{RLL}\vdash(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,q\Rightarrow r
.

Remark 26
.

For the interested reader, it is worth noting that Theorem
25
is a restricted form of a similar result usually established for alternating branching VASS with full zero tests
[
46
,
35
]
, which is a more powerful variant of VASS that we are using in this paper. The reason for this restriction is the specific form of the formulas in
Th_{\mathcal{V}}
, that will be needed later in Corollary
30
. Nevertheless, even this restricted case is sufficiently complex to yield the separation we aim to demonstrate.

Definition 27
.

An
\mathcal{L}_{u}
-formula is called
implicational Horn
if it is defined by the following grammar:

F:=p\mid 1\mid\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{i\in I}q_{i}\to p\mid p\to\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{i\in I}q_{i}\mid F_{1}\wedge F_{2},

where
p
and each
q_{i}
are atoms of
\mathcal{L}^{u}
and
I
is a
non-empty
finite set.
A sequent
S
is called implicational Horn if it has the form
\Gamma\Rightarrow p
, where
\Gamma
consists of implicational Horn formulas and
p
is an atom.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be a sub-exponential function. Then, it is easy to see that for any numbers
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n^{k})^{l}}{2^{n}}=0
.

Theorem 28
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be a sub-exponential and monotone function. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
there exists an
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
-provable implicational Horn sequent
T
such that
|T|\geq N
and any
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof of
T
have size greater than
f(|T|)
.

Proof.

For the proof, assume the contrary. Namely, there exists an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
-provable implicational Horn sequent
T

with
|T|\geq N
, there is an
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof of
T
of size bounded by
f(|T|)
.
Now, let
\mathcal{V}=(Q,T)
be an ordinary VASS and
q,r\in Q
. Let
n
be the size of the binary representation of
(\mathcal{V},q,r)
.
We claim that
(q,\mathbf{0})\to_{c}^{*}(r,\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}
iff
(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,q\Rightarrow r
has an
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof of size
f(O(n))
. For the proof of the claim,
one direction is easy. If
(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,q\Rightarrow r
has an
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof of size
f(O(n))
, then by Theorem
25
, we have
(q,\mathbf{0})\to_{c}^{*}(r,\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}
.
Conversely, if
(q,\mathbf{0})\to_{c}^{*}(r,\mathbf{0})
in
\mathcal{V}
, then by Theorem
25
, the sequent

(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,q\Rightarrow r

is
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
-provable. If
(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,q\Rightarrow r
has size below
N
, as
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
is weaker than
\mathbf{RLL}
, the sequent
(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,q\Rightarrow r
has a constant-size
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof. If
(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,q\Rightarrow r
has size greater than
N
, as it is implicational Horn, by our assumption, it has an
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof of size bounded by
f(|T|)
. As
|T|\leq O(n)
and
f
is monotone, we obtain an
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof of size
f(O(n))
. This completes the proof of the claim.

Now, by the claim, it is clear that checking whether
(q,\mathbf{0})\to_{c}^{*}(r,\mathbf{0})
reduces to searching for an
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof of
(\bigwedge Th_{\mathcal{V}})\wedge 1,q\Rightarrow r
of size
f(O(n))
. Such a search can be implemented by enumerating all possible proofs of size at most
f(O(n))
. Checking for each possible proof is possible in polynomial time and hence it requires space
(f(O(n)))^{O(1)}
. As the space can be reused, this gives a decision procedure in space
g(n)=(f(O(n)))^{O(1)}
.
This implies that the contractive ordinary VASS reachability problem is decidable in
\mathrm{SPACE}(g(n))
. By Corollary
24
, there is a polynomial time reduction from any problem in
\mathrm{SPACE}(2^{n})
into contractive ordinary VASS reachability problem. Thus,
\mathrm{SPACE}(2^{n})\subseteq\mathrm{SPACE}(g(n^{k}))
, for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. As
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{g(n^{k})}{2^{n}}=0
, this contradicts the space hierarchy theorem.
∎

It remains to show that any
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
-provable implicational Horn sequent has a short
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proof. In fact, we prove this claim for all
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-provable implicational Horn sequents. The proof is an adaptation of the
unit propagation
algorithm in
[
30
]
.

Theorem 29

(Unit Propagation)
.

Any
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-provable implicational Horn sequent
S
has an
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proof of size
O(|S|^{2})
.

Proof.

Let
S=(\Gamma\Rightarrow p)
, where
\Gamma
consists of implicational Horn formulas and
p
is an atom. We begin by applying three simplifications on the formulas in
\Gamma
, without changing the
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-provability of the sequent. First, we replace
A\wedge B
by
\{A,B\}
in all the formulas in
\Gamma
. Then, we erase all
1
’s appearing as a formula in the antecedent. Finally, we replace any formula of the form
p\to\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{i\in I}q_{i}
by the multiset
\{p\to q_{i}\}_{i\in I}
. These three operations only increase the size of the sequent by a linear factor. Moreover, since
A\wedge B\Rightarrow A
and
A\wedge B\Rightarrow B
, and
p\to\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{i\in I}q_{i}\Rightarrow p\to q_{i}
(for any
i\in I
) have linear-size
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proofs, we may apply the rule
(1w)
to assume, without loss of generality, that
\Gamma
consists of formulas of the form
p
or
\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{i\in I}q_{i}\to p
, where
I\neq\emptyset
is finite.

Now, we prove the theorem by induction on the size of
\Gamma
. If
p\in\Gamma
, it suffices to start with the axiom
p\Rightarrow p
and add the rest of
\Gamma
using weakening. The size of the proof is clearly
O(|S|^{2})
. Therefore, assume
p\notin\Gamma
. Let
\Gamma=\Sigma\cup P
, where
\Sigma
consists of the implicational formulas in
\Gamma
and
P
of atomic formulas in
\Gamma
.

We claim that there exists
q\in P
such that
q
also occurs in
\Sigma
. Assume otherwise, namely
V(P)\cap V(\Sigma)=\emptyset
. As
S
is provable in
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
, which is essentially a calculus for
\mathsf{CPC}
, the sequent
S
must be valid under all Boolean valuations, reading multiplication as conjunction.
Now, consider the valuation that maps all atoms in
P
to
1
and all the other atoms to
0
. Then
p
is mapped to
0
as
p\notin\Gamma
, all formulas in
P
are true, and all formulas in
\Sigma
are false (since the atoms in their antecedents are not in
P
), making the implicational formulas in
\Sigma
true. Since
p
is false, this contradicts the validity of
\Gamma\Rightarrow p
. Hence, there exists
q\in P
that occurs in
\Sigma
.

Let
q\in P
be such an atom. Then there exists an implicational formula
A\in\Gamma
containing
q
, of the form
A=(\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{i\in I}q_{i}\to r)
. There are three cases:

•

If
q=r
, let
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\setminus\{A\}
. Since both
q
and
\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{i\in I}q_{i}\to q
are in
\Gamma
, the sequent
\Gamma^{\prime}\Rightarrow p
is valid. By the induction hypothesis,
S^{\prime}=(\Gamma^{\prime}\Rightarrow p)
has a proof in
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
of size
O(|S^{\prime}|^{2})
. By weakening,
\Gamma\Rightarrow p
also has a proof of size
O(|S^{\prime}|^{2})+O(|S|)\leq O(|S|^{2})
.

•

If
A=\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}_{i\in I}q_{i}\to r
,
q=q_{i}
for some
i\in I
, and
|I|>1
, let
A^{\prime}
be obtained from
A
by removing
q
, and set
\Gamma^{\prime}=(\Gamma\setminus\{A\})\cup\{A^{\prime}\}
. Since
A^{\prime}
is shorter than
A
, we have
\Gamma^{\prime}
is smaller than
\Gamma
. As
q\in\Gamma
, the sequent
S^{\prime}=(\Gamma^{\prime}\Rightarrow p)
is valid, and by the induction hypothesis, it has a proof in
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
of size
O(|S^{\prime}|^{2})
. Moreover,
q,A\Rightarrow\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}A^{\prime}
has a proof of size
O(|S|)
. Combining these proofs by cut and applying contraction on
q
, we obtain a proof of
\Gamma\Rightarrow p
of size
O(|S^{\prime}|^{2})+O(|S|)\leq O(|S|^{2})
.

•

If
A=q\to r
, let
A^{\prime}=r
and set
\Gamma^{\prime}=(\Gamma\setminus\{A\})\cup\{A^{\prime}\}
. Since
A^{\prime}
is shorter than
A
, we have
\Gamma^{\prime}
is smaller than
\Gamma
. As
q\in\Gamma
,
S^{\prime}=(\Gamma^{\prime}\Rightarrow p)
is valid, and by the induction hypothesis, it has an
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proof of size
O(|S^{\prime}|^{2})
. Since
q,A\Rightarrow A^{\prime}
has a proof of size
O(|S|)
, combining the two gives a proof of
\Gamma\Rightarrow p
of size
O(|S^{\prime}|^{2})+O(|S|)\leq O(|S|^{2})
. ∎

Combining the polynomial upper bound in Theorem
29
with the sub-exponential lower bound in Theorem

, we obtain:

6
Cut-free Calculi

In this section, we assess the strength of the cut rule. We show that removing cut from the already powerful system
\mathbf{LK_{!}}
leads to a substantial loss of power: the cut-free system
\mathbf{LK^{-}_{!}}
is exponentially weaker than
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
over
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
, consequently
\mathbf{FL_{e}}\nleq_{\mathbf{FL_{e}}}\mathbf{LK^{-}_{!}}
. This is witnessed by a family of
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
-provable sequents that have polynomial-size proofs in
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
but require exponential-size proofs in
\mathbf{LK^{-}_{!}}
. Thus, even with weakening and contraction, the absence of cut cannot be compensated.

Our strategy is to build an extension
G
of
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
with simple initial sequents to feasibly mimic classical reasoning in
\mathbf{LK}
. Using this connection, we import the known short
\mathbf{LK}
-proof of the Clique–Color formula into
G
. Then, by transforming the initial sequents of
G
into formulas in the antecedent in the usual way, we obtain a version of the Clique–Color formula with short proofs in
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
. Finally, we use monotone feasible interpolation for
\mathbf{LK}^{-}
to show that any short
\mathbf{LK}_{!}^{-}
-proof of these sequents would yield a small interpolating monotone circuit for the Clique–Color formula, which is impossible.

We begin by introducing formulas in negation normal form in both classical and substructural settings.

Definition 32
.

An
\mathcal{L}_{p}
-formula
F
is in
negation normal form
(
nnf
, in short) if it is defined by the following grammar:

F:=p\mid\neg p\mid F_{1}\wedge F_{2}\mid F_{1}\vee F_{2}

Similarly, an
\mathcal{L}^{u}
-formula
F
is in
*
-negation normal form
(
*
-nnf
, in short) if it is defined by the grammar:

F:=p\mid\neg p\mid F_{1}\wedge F_{2}\mid F_{1}\vee F_{2}\mid F_{1}*F_{2}

We write
\mathcal{L}_{nn}
and
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{nn}
for the sets of nnf- and
*
-nnf-formulas.

As promised, we introduce an extension of the calculus
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
by adding a collection of initial sequents that allow us to mimic classical reasoning on nnf-formulas within the substructural setting.

Definition 33
.

Define the sequent calculus
G
over
\mathcal{L}_{u}
as
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
plus the following initial sequents:

\Rightarrow p\vee\neg p\qquad p\Rightarrow 1\qquad\neg p\Rightarrow 1\qquad 0\Rightarrow p

is an atom. Note that these initial sequents are not meant to be used as axioms and hence using their substitutions are

. Moreover,

We begin with some basic observations about the strength of the calculus
G
. The following lemmas show that, over

-nnf formulas,

admits short proofs of axiomatic forms of contraction and weakening, as well as the law of excluded middle. These proofs will be used to simulate classical reasoning within

Lemma 34
.

For any
A\in\mathcal{L}^{*}_{nn}

The first three statements are proved by induction on the structure of

If
A
is
p
or
\neg p
, both

and

are present in

as the additional initial sequents.
For the inductive step, let
A=B\circ C
with

. We only treat the case

\pi_{1}

0\Rightarrow B

\pi_{2}

0,0\Rightarrow B*C

0\Rightarrow B*C

\sigma_{1}

B\Rightarrow 1

\sigma_{2}

B,C\Rightarrow 1

B*C\Rightarrow 1

where
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are provided by the induction hypothesis. For the size upper bounds, observe that when
A=p

. In each inductive step toward the sequents

For the third sequent, namely

of

. As before, we first construct the proof and then establish the upper bound. Recall that

, the initial sequent

\Rightarrow p\vee\neg p

0\Rightarrow 0

0\Rightarrow 0

\neg p\Rightarrow\neg p\vee\neg\neg p

\Rightarrow\neg p\vee\neg\neg p

For

, where

using the induction hypothesis for

and

; the others are similar.
For

Lemma 17.

Fix
D=N=\bot
and let
B
be a fully conservative and
C
be a conservative
\mathcal{L}_{b}
-formula. Then, there are
\pi
,
\sigma
, and
\tau
such that:

1.

\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash^{\pi}B^{t}\Leftrightarrow B
,

2.

\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash^{\sigma}B^{s}\Leftrightarrow\bot
,

3.

\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash^{\tau}C^{t}\Leftrightarrow C
,

l(\pi),l(\sigma)\leq O(|B|)
and
l(\tau)\leq O(|C|)
.
If
B
and
C
are
\mathcal{L}_{!}
-formulas, then the same results hold by changing
\mathbf{IMALL}
to
\mathbf{ILL}
.

Proof.

We only prove the case of
\mathbf{IMALL}
; the case of
\mathbf{ILL}
is similar. We establish all three items
1
,
2
, and
3
simultaneously by induction on the structure of the formulas
B
and
C
. We conclude by explaining the upper bounds on the lines of the constructed proofs.

The case where
B
(resp.
C
) is an atom or constant is trivial by Definition
12
. The cases for
B=B_{1}\circ B_{2}
where
\circ\in\{\wedge,\vee\}
is straightforward by Definition
12
. Similarly, the cases for
C=C_{1}\circ C_{2}
where
\circ\in\{\wedge,\vee,*\}
(and
C=\,!C
, for
\mathbf{ILL}
) are also straightforward. The only interesting case is when
B=C\to A
, where
C
is a conservative formula and
A
is fully conservative.
By Definition
12
,
(C\to A)^{t}=(C^{t}\to A^{t})\wedge(A^{s}\to C^{s})
.
By the induction hypothesis, the following are provable in
\mathbf{IMALL}
:

C\Rightarrow C^{t}\qquad A^{t}\Rightarrow A.

Thus,
\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash C^{t}\to A^{t}\Rightarrow C\to A
and then
\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash(C\to A)^{t}\Rightarrow C\to A
. For the other direction, again by the induction hypothesis, the following are provable in
\mathbf{IMALL}
:

C^{t}\Rightarrow C\qquad A\Rightarrow A^{t}.

Thus
\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash C\to A\Rightarrow C^{t}\to A^{t}
. Moreover, by the induction hypothesis
\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash A^{s}\Rightarrow\bot
. Applying cut on the sequent
\bot,C\to A\Rightarrow C^{s}
, we get
\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash C\to A\Rightarrow A^{s}\to C^{s}
. Hence,
\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash C\to A\Rightarrow(C\to A)^{t}
.

Finally, by Definition
12
, we have
(C\to A)^{s}=C^{t}*A^{s}
. By the induction hypothesis,
\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash A^{s}\Rightarrow\bot
. By the axiom
(\bot)
, we have
\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash\bot\Rightarrow B^{t}\to\bot
. Therefore, by cut
\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash A^{s}\Rightarrow B^{t}\to\bot
which implies
\mathbf{IMALL}\vdash A^{s}*B^{t}\Rightarrow\bot
.

For the upper bound on the number of lines, note that when
B
and
C
are atomic or constant, the corresponding proofs have only constantly many lines. At each inductive step, we add only constantly many new lines. Therefore, the total number of lines is
O(|B|)
and
O(|C|)
, respectively.
∎

u_{i+1}=u_{i}+a_{i}
. Using the same rule, we obtain
(q_{i},u_{i}+w)\to(q_{i+1},u_{i}+w+a_{i})
, as
u_{i}+w+a_{i}\geq u_{i}+a_{i}=u_{i+1}
and hence positive. As
u_{i}+a_{i}=u_{i+1}
, this means
(q_{i},u_{i}+w)\to(q_{i+1},u_{i+1}+w)
. By the induction hypothesis, we have
(q,u)\to^{*}(q_{i},u_{i}+w)
. Therefore,
(q,u)\to^{*}(q_{i},u_{i+1}+w)
. For the second case, assume that
c_{i+1}
is reached from
c_{i}
by the contraction rule. Then,
q_{i+1}=q_{i}
and
u_{i}=u_{i+1}+\mathbf{e}_{j}
, for some
1\leq j\leq d
. Thus, by the induction hypothesis,
(q,u)\to^{*}(q_{i},u_{i}+w)
, which means
(q,u)\to^{*}(q_{i},u_{i+1}+w+\mathbf{e}_{j})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 31.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be a monotone sub-exponential function. There exists a sequence of
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
-provable formulas
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
|A_{n}|\geq n
, each
A_{n}
has an
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proof of size
|A_{n}|^{O(1)}
, while any
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof of
A_{n}
has size greater than
f(|A_{n}|)
. In particular, the claim holds for
f(n)=2^{(\log(n))^{k}}
, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
The same result holds when replacing
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
and
\mathbf{RLL}
with the Frege systems for
\mathsf{CPC}
and
\mathsf{RLL}
, respectively.

Proof.

For any sequent
T=(\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta)
over
\mathcal{L}_{u}
, consider its formula interpretation

I(T)=\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{$\ast$}}}\Gamma\to\mathop{\scalebox{1.5}{\raisebox{-0.86108pt}{+}}}\Delta
. It is easy to transform an
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proof
\pi
of
I(T)
into an
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proof of
T
with polynomial blow-up, say of size
|\pi|^{m}
. Let
g(n)=f(2n)^{m}
. It is clear that
g(n)
is also monotone and sub-exponential.
Now, by Corollary
30
, there exists an
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
-provable sequent
T_{n}
such that
|T_{n}|\geq n
, each
T_{n}
has an
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proof of size

, while any
\mathbf{RLL}
-proof of
T_{n}

g(|T_{n}|)
.
Now set
A_{n}=I(T_{n})
. Then it is clear that
A_{n}
is
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
-provable and
2|T_{n}|\geq|A_{n}|\geq|T_{n}|\geq n
. On the other hand, for any
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proof
\pi
of
A_{n}
, there exists an
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proof of
T_{n}
of size
|\pi|^{m}

, it follows that

|\pi|\geq f(2|T_{n}|)\geq f(|A_{n}|)
,
as
2|T_{n}|\geq|A_{n}|
and

is monotone. For the claim concerning Frege systems, it suffices to note that Frege systems and sequent calculi for

8
Conclusion and Future Work

We have isolated the power of structural rules in producing efficient proofs in the classical sequent calculus
\mathbf{LK}
by systematically removing weakening, contraction, and cut. Eliminating any single rule produces exponential proof-size blow-ups, yielding new separation results and lower bounds for substructural and linear proof systems. In particular, this answers positively the question posed in
[
26
]
regarding extending the exponential lower bound for
\mathbf{CFL_{ew}^{-}}
to sequent calculi with cut. A key observation is that these separations are not sensitive to the addition of the controlled structural reasoning supported by the linear exponential
!
. This shows that the obtained hardness is intrinsic to the absence of structural rules and cannot be compensated by controlled versions of these rules.

For future work, Table
1
highlights remaining challenges, such as establishing exponential separations between logics without weakening and contraction versus those with only weakening, as well as comparisons among cut-free calculi. Another interesting problem is to ask whether there exist
\mathbf{FL_{ec}}
- or even
\mathbf{FL_{e}}
-provable formulas with short
\mathbf{LK_{u}}
-proofs whose
\mathbf{RLL}
-proofs are
exponentially
hard rather than merely
sub-exponentially
hard. Moreover, it is interesting to find explicit formulas for this purpose.

Another promising direction is asking whether exponential lower bounds can be proved for the number of proof lines rather than overall proof size. This is closely related to extending our results from Frege systems to extended Frege (EF) systems, which formalize the practice of naming large subformulas via extension axioms to obtain shorter proofs. EF systems are more robust: bounds on EF proof size essentially correspond to bounds on the number of lines in a Frege proof, and they can be viewed as Frege systems operating with circuits, allowing many arguments to carry over more smoothly. Inspired by Question 9.2 in
[
29
]
, it is also natural to ask whether Frege and extended Frege systems can be separated in the substructural and linear setting.
