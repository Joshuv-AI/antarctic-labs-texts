Estrogen-mediated NF-κB blockade enables breast cancer sensitivity to oncolytic VSV virotherapy
Authors: Mélissa Viens, Karen Geoffroy, Elline Meuriot, Alexandra Buccilli, Victor Mullins-Dansereau, Marie-Lou Myre, Stamatios Paradisis, Angelina Bardoul, Emma Mary Kalin, Bruna Araripe Saraiva, Jeffrey Valencia, Kim Leclerc Desaulniers, Huy-Dung Hoang, Saima Hassan, John Cameron Bell, Barbara Vanderhyden, Tommy Alain, Dominic Guy Roy, David Parker Cook, Marie-Claude Bourgeois-Daigneault
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Novel therapies are urgently needed for breast cancer. While our previous work investigating the use of oncolytic viruses against the disease demonstrated its promise, heterogeneous responses were observed. Interestingly, breast cancer is classified into subtypes based on the expression of hormone receptors. We noted that triple-negative breast cancers (TNBCs), which lack the expression of the estrogen (E2) receptor (ER) were resistant to the oncolytic vesicular stomatitis virus (oVSV), whereas ER+ cancers were particularly sensitive. We found that E2 stimulation enhanced virus infection, while E2-antagonizing endocrine therapies counteract the virus. Further mechanistic studies revealed a defect of nuclear factor (NF)-κB activation with E2 stimulation. With the goal of enhancing oncolytic virotherapy independently of E2, we designed a treatment strategy that combines oVSV with the NF-κB inhibitors IKK16 and sulfasalazine and found that both drugs improved virus replication in TNBC cell lines and patient samples. Our data uncover a novel impact of E2 on oncolytic virotherapy and demonstrate that combining virus treatment with NF-κB inhibitors recapitulates the E2-mediated virus enhancement. Importantly, our treatment combinations could be beneficial to all breast cancers, including TNBCs, as well as other breast cancers that do not respond to E2.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women in North America.1 Most patients undergo surgery together with additional neoadjuvant and/or adjuvant treatments. Clinical decisions are guided by the expression of the estrogen (E2) receptor (ER), the progesterone receptor, and the human epidermal growth factor receptor-2, which allow for the molecular subtyping of breast cancers into luminal (ER+, progesterone receptor +/−), human epidermal growth factor receptor-2-enriched, and triple negative breast cancer (TNBC; negative for all 3 receptors).2 Luminal breast cancer is the most frequent subtype. It accounts for approximately 75% of all cases,2 and E2 stimulation drives its proliferation.3 Targeted treatments include selective ER modulators such as tamoxifen, selective ER degraders and ovarian suppressor drugs.4 In all cases, the goal is to prevent E2 stimulation.

Of all breast cancers, TNBCs have the worst prognosis, affect younger individuals, and lack targeted therapies. These patients undergo systemic chemotherapy, which has important quality of life-compromising toxicities, and novel treatment options are needed.5 We and others have previously shown in mouse models that oncolytic virotherapy is a promising approach against TNBC.6,7 Oncolytic viruses are viruses that kill cancers by both direct infection and the induction of anti-tumor immunity.8 As such, tumor cell infection and immune stimulation are both important for treatment efficacy. We have previously shown that oncolytic virotherapy of breast cancer was improved by either combining virus treatment with the standard-of-care drug paclitaxel that we found enhances virus replication9 or by administering the virus in the neoadjuvant setting.10,11 While these approaches are effective, complete cures could not be obtained in the most aggressive breast cancer models, and heterogeneous responses were observed upon treatment. Here, we show that human luminal breast cancers are sensitive to oncolytic vesicular stomatitis virus (oVSV) while TNBCs are resistant. oVSV is a variant of VSV that has a deletion of the methionine 51 of its matrix protein (VSVΔ51),12 a mutation that prevents the blockade of cellular anti-viral defense mechanisms and attenuates virus infection in healthy cells but not in cancer cells that often have defects in these pathways.13

While cell-intrinsic features, such as defective anti-viral pathways and immunosuppressive mechanisms that are often observed in cancer, affect intrinsic oncolytic virus sensitivity, the tumor microenvironment can also impact infection.14 For example, the vascular endothelial growth factor, an angiogenic factor that is found in cancer, allows for the infection and destruction of tumor endothelial cells and contributes to treatment efficacy.15 Another study has demonstrated that fibroblast growth factor 2 produced by cancer-associated fibroblasts could sensitize cancer cells to oncolytic viruses.16 Here, we investigated whether other factors that are found in the breast cancer microenvironment could also modulate infection. Given that luminal breast cancers are sensitive to oVSV, we tested the impact of hormones on infection and found that E2, the most common estrogen found in women of reproductive age,17 enhanced oVSV. This effect was observed in all ER+ breast cancer cell lines tested as well as ex vivo using both human breast cancer xenografts and patient samples. We also found that 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT), a cell-permeable tamoxifen metabolite with anti-E2 activity, antagonizes oVSV, thereby revealing treatment incompatibilities. Unbiased mechanistic studies then allowed for the identification of nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), which has established anti-viral functions,18 as a key player of the virus-sensitizing effect of E2. In breast cancer, NF-κB is known to stimulate cancer cell proliferation and invasion,19,20 as well as promote cancer stem cells,21 and its inhibition is therefore seen as an interesting treatment avenue.22 Interestingly, we found that NF-κB activation is delayed in the presence of E2 and in ER− compared with ER+ cells. Importantly, NF-κB can be modulated using commercially available drugs. As such, IKK16 and sulfasalazine (SSZ) are 2 IκB kinase inhibitors that act upstream in the pathway,23,24 and SSZ is already FDA approved for the treatment of various autoimmune diseases.25 We tested the combinations of NF-κB inhibitors with oVSV and show that both drugs enhance virus infection in human TNBC cell lines and ex vivo in patient samples.

Our study reveals a novel role for E2 as a modulator of oncolytic virotherapy, uncovers NF-κB blockade as a mechanism by which E2 enhances oVSV, and leverages these findings to design treatment combinations with IKK16 and SSZ that improve oncolytic virus efficacy in TNBC.

Results

E2 enhances oVSV in ER+ breast cancer

We previously found that mouse and human breast cancer cell lines had heterogeneous sensitivities to oVSV,26,27,28,29 as well as another oncolytic virus of the same family, Maraba virus.9,10 To gain a better understanding of the factors governing oncolytic virus responsiveness in breast cancer, we first compared the viral sensitivities of 6 widely used human breast cancer cell lines. To do so, we infected Hs 578T, BT-549, HCC1937, MCF-7, T-47D, and ZR-75 cells with an oVSV variant that encodes yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) at various multiplicities of infection (MOIs) and assessed infection 24 h later. Interestingly, the fluorescent signal was weak in TNBC (Hs 578T, BT-549, and HCC1937) cells compared with ER+ (luminal) cell lines, which showed a strong fluorescent signal and 100% infection, even at very low MOIs (Figures 1A and S1A). This suggests that TNBCs are resistant to infection, while ER+ cell lines (MCF-7, T-47D, and ZR-75) are sensitive. The fluorescent signal was also quantified, which revealed that higher MOIs are required to achieve 50% of the maximal fluorescence in TNBC vs. non-TNBC cell lines (Figure S1B). Given that oVSV has oncolytic abilities, we quantified cell viability by Coomassie blue assay as a readout of tumor cell killing. Consistent with reduced infection, our results revealed that higher MOIs were required to decrease viability by at least 50% in TNBC vs. non-TNBC cell lines (Figure S1C). These findings are summarized in Figure S1D. To ensure the clinical relevance of our observation, we then infected breast cancer patient tumor samples ex vivo with oVSV. Given that we found ER+ breast cancers to be sensitive to oVSV, we next investigated the impact of E2 on viral infection. Since cell culture media often contains phenol red, a pH indicator with estrogenic activity,31 and is supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS), which contains E2,32 E2 is found in regular culture conditions. To study the impact of the hormone, we used the ER+ MCF-7, T-47D, and ZR-75 breast cancer cells and performed experiments in hormone-free culture media (without phenol red and supplemented with charcoal-stripped FBS) that we supplemented or not with E2 at a concentration of 100 nM, which is physiologically relevant.33 Without E2, all 3 cell lines were found to resist oVSV-YFP better than cells that were infected in regular complete culture media (Figure 2A, upper panel, vs. Figure 1A). Interestingly, we also found that E2 supplementation enhanced infection. This enhancement was significant for all 3 cell lines (Figure 2B) and was also observed using E2 concentrations ranging from 0.032 to 200 nM at 24, 48, and 72 h post-infection (Figure S2A). We next investigated whether this phenotype could be detected earlier after infection and observed enhanced fluorescence as early as 10 h (Figure S2B). We next performed flow cytometry analyses to quantify the percentage of infected cells, as well as the mean fluorescence intensities (MFIs) ± E2. Notably, given that the cells are sensitive to infection and that we wanted to measure differences before the signal reached saturation, we used a high MOI of 100 and short time points of 6–10 h for these experiments. While these conditions did not reveal differences in the percentage of fluorescent cells for both MCF-7 and T-47D cells (Figure S2C), the MFIs were increased in the presence of E2 as early as 8 h post-infection (Figure 2C). We repeated the experiment, this time using an MOI of 1 and longer time points of 12–18 h and once again observed similar percentages of YFP+ cells (Figure S2D) but increased MFIs for both cell lines (Figure 2D). We next assessed virus spreading by performing plaque assays on MCF-7 and T-47D cells, which consist in infecting cell monolayers ± E2 and applying an agarose overlay to prevent virus diffusion in the media. We found that virus plaques produced in T-47D cells were larger with E2 (Figures 2E and S2E), a trend that was also observed, although it was not significant, in MCF-7 cells. We next wanted to determine whether E2 could also enhance oVSV in more physiological conditions. While monolayers of cells are readily infected and virus spreads rapidly through the culture supernatant, 3-dimensional tumors limit virus spread. Moreover, the tumor microenvironment is a complex network that contains various cell types, which can also affect oncolytic virotherapy.34 We therefore implanted MCF-7 xenografts and tested the magnitude of infection ex vivo ± E2. In this context, we observed that both the fluorescent signal and virus production were increased with E2 (Figures 2F and S2F). Finally, we repeated the experiment using an ER+ breast cancer patient tumor sample and found that the number of VSV+ cells was significantly increased in the presence of E2 (Figures 2G and 2H). Our data support a pro-viral effect of E2 in ER+ tumors.

ERα is required for E2 to enhance oVSV

We next wanted to understand the mechanism by which E2 stimulation enhances oVSV infection. We tested the importance of the receptor. E2 binds to homodimers or heterodimers of ERα and ERβ, as well as to the G protein-coupled ER. In the context of breast cancer, ERα promotes cell proliferation, while ERβ has the opposite effect.35 Notably, breast cancers are considered to be ER+ when at least 1% of tumor cells stain positive for ERα, while ERβ expression is not measured.36 ERα is therefore a key molecule to investigate for luminal breast cancer cells. To determine the impact of ERα and ERβ expression on oVSV infection, MCF-7 and T-47D cells were transfected with ESR1-and ESR2-targeting small interfering RNAs (siRNAs; the ERα and ERβ genes, respectively) prior to infection, and the knockdowns were confirmed by western blot (Figure S3A). Interestingly, infection was decreased with ESR1 siRNA (Figures 3A and 3B), a result that suggests that E2 exerts its virus-enhancing effect via ERα. Notably, ERβ knockdown had the opposite effect in MCF-7 cells. E2 interferes with NF-κB activation post-virus infection

While our data show that E2 enhances oVSV, supplementing the hormone to patients could be detrimental given that E2 drives breast cancer proliferation. With the objective of harnessing this effect without requiring hormone stimulation, we next investigated the mechanism by which E2 enhances oVSV. To do so, we performed an RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) experiment in which we compared gene expression from ER+ MCF-7 and ZR-75 cells in conditions ± oVSV and ± E2. Gene expression signatures revealed that oVSV triggered the most transcriptional changes (Figure 4A). A differential gene expression analysis of the genes that were induced at least 2-fold compared with controls revealed an important overlap between the oVSV and oVSV+ E2 conditions for both cell lines (Figure 4B). We then reasoned that for E2 to enhance oVSV, the hormone could either prevent the expression of anti-viral genes or induce pro-viral genes. While our analysis of pro-viral genes was not conclusive, the identification of potential anti-viral factors was more promising. Indeed, a top 10 gene set enrichment analysis of transcripts that were induced by oVSV, but not induced or induced but at lower levels in the presence of E2, revealed several immune-related pathways for both cell lines (Figure 4C). Notably, cellular responses to various cytokines and chemokines were found to be impaired in the presence of E2. We next compared the expression levels of all cytokines and chemokines that were expressed upon oVSV infection. Impressively, all cytokines and chemokines induced by infection were expressed at lower levels with E2, and E2 alone did not modulate expression without infection (Figures 4D and 4E). Given that most of these pro-inflammatory factors detected can be induced by NF-κB,39 we also analyzed the expression NF-κB-related genes (from the PROGENy tool40) from our RNA-seq data. As was observed for cytokines and chemokines, several NF-κB-related genes were induced by oVSV but expressed at lower levels with E2 (Figure 4F), therefore supporting a potential role of NF-κB blockade in the viral-sensitizing effect of E2. Once again, E2 alone did not modulate the expression of these genes. To extend the impact of E2 on cytokine and chemokine production in another ER+ cell line, we performed a cytokine array on culture supernatants collected from T-47D cells infected with oVSV ± E2. Notably, we detected many cytokines (interleukin [IL]-8, macrophage migration inhibitory factor, IL-18, and serpin) and chemokines (C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 [CCL5], C-X-C motif ligand 15 [CXCL15], CXCL12, and CCL2) in the culture media post-infection (Figure S4A). In all cases, their levels were reduced in the presence of E2 (Figure 4G). Notably, while the cytokine array revealed differences in IL-8 production, which is regulated by NF-κB, it was not the case with the RNA-seq analysis. Importantly, the RNA-seq samples were collected 24 h post-infection, while the cytokine array was performed at 18 h. We therefore performed an ELISA to measure IL-8 production in both MCF-7 and T-47D 18 h post-infection with and without E2. Consistent with the cytokine array result, we found reduced levels of IL-8 in the presence of the hormone (Figure 4H). We also measured CCL2 concentrations in the culture supernatant and obtained similar results (Figure 4I). To investigate whether NF-κB could modulate viral sensitivity in breast cancer, we measured its activation in regular culture conditions in TNBC vs. ER+ breast cancer cell lines. As a readout of NF-κB activation, we performed IF for p65, an NF-κB subunit that translocates to the nucleus when the pathway is activated. Strikingly, we observed that p65 was localized in the nucleus in nearly 100% of HCC1937 TNBC cells, but in only few MCF-7 ER+ cells after 12 h of infection (Figure 5A). We next performed a kinetic analysis in 3 ER+ and 3 TNBC cell lines and obtained similar findings, with all TNBC cell lines showing p65 translocation as early as 8 h and reaching maximal levels at 8 h (HCC1937), 14 h (BT-549) and 18 h (Hs 578T) post-infection, while nuclear p65 was only detectable after 12–14 h in ER+ MCF-7, T-47D, and ZR-75 cell lines and percentages remained lower than 50%, even at 18 h post-infection (Figure 5B). This result is in line with NF-κB activation contributing to the oncolytic virus sensitivity of breast cancer. We next repeated the experiment, but this time we used ER+ cells in hormone-free culture conditions ± E2. As opposed to what we observed in the previous experiment using regular complete culture media (Figure 5B), hormone-free culture conditions allowed for greater p65 nuclear translocation in ER+ cells, which reached almost 100% for MCF-7 and 75% T-47D cells (Figures 5C and S5B). Importantly, our results also show that in line with E2 blocking NF-κB, the nuclear translocation of p65 was delayed and less frequent in all 3 cell lines in the presence of E2. NF-κB inhibition enhances oVSV independently of E2 and ER expression

Given that the NF-κB pathway is ubiquitous, we next wanted to design an E2-independent treatment strategy. We validated that NF-κB inhibition could enhance oVSV. To do so, we used siRNAs to knock down p65 expression prior to the infection of TNBC cells. As expected, both BT-549 and Hs 578T cells produced more virus with NF-κB inhibition (Figure 6A). To design a treatment that could be applied clinically, we decided to use drugs that block NF-κB activation in combination with oVSV treatment. We selected SSZ and IKK16, 2 IκB kinase inhibitors, which act upstream of the pathway. We reasoned that if NF-κB is activated by infection and has anti-viral activity, then the drugs would be more effective at enhancing oVSV if used prior to virus treatment. In previous studies, we and others have shown that pre-treating cells for 4 h with various viral sensitizing drugs enhanced oncolytic virus efficacy.9,41 We validated that treating cells for 4 h with IKK16 and SSZ could effectively prevent NF-κB activation and found both drugs to be effective in this setting (Figures S6A and S6B). When pre-treating TNBC cells with the drugs for 4 h prior to infection, we found oVSV to be enhanced in all cell lines tested (Figure 6B). While this effect was not statistically significant for 2 of the conditions, the trend remained. Importantly, DMSO, as well as 2 other drugs that do not target NF-κB—carboplatin (a derivative of cisplatin that binds to DNA) and Mg132 (a proteasome inhibitor)—failed at enhancing infection in the same context (Figure S6C). On the contrary, Mg132 treatment at the concentration of 50 μM resulted in a decrease in infection, an effect that could result from decreased cell viability with prolonged exposure to Mg132, as other groups have already described.42,43 We next tested our treatment combinations using a TNBC patient sample and observed a similar enhancement (Figures 6C and 6D). Discussion

In this study, we reveal a novel impact of E2 on the response of breast cancer to oncolytic virotherapy. While components such as growth factors from the tumor microenvironment have been described in the past as modulators of infection,15,16 hormones, which are found in the tumor niche and directly impact cancer in many ways, have been overlooked. The importance of E2 for breast cancer is emphasized by the fact that ER+ cancers are treated with endocrine therapies to prevent E2 stimulation. Our study demonstrates that the intrinsic sensitivity of breast cancers to oncolytic viruses is impacted by ER expression and shows that oVSV therapy is incompatible with the standard of care tamoxifen. This finding is important to take into consideration for future human trials enrolling ER+ breast cancer patients.

Notably, we found that ERα knockdown hampered infection, while the opposite effect was observed for ERβ. Interestingly, while E2 binds to both receptors, the downstream effects of ERα and ERβ are opposite, with ERβ having anti-proliferative, anti-cancer activities and ERα being pro-tumoral.44 Interestingly, ERβ antagonizes ERα activity, and its expression was shown to prevent the expression of 70% of ERα-inducible genes.45 Given that ERα stimulation enhances oVSV infection, our finding that ERβ knockdown enhances infection is in line with the literature, as well as with a pro-viral role for ERα. Moreover, our results also support ERα expression and low or no ERβ expression as predictive biomarkers for oVSV responsiveness. While these criteria could be considered for patient selection in future oVSV clinical trials, it would be particularly relevant in the absence of endocrine therapy. As such, some patients who refuse endocrine therapies, have severe adverse effects,46 or terminate treatment for other reasons, such as the lack or loss of responsiveness, could benefit from oVSV treatment.

Importantly, we found that E2 could delay NF-κB activation, and that the pathway was activated faster and to greater extents in TNBC vs. ER+ breast cancer cells. While few reports have described a blockade of NF-κB by E2,47,48 this effect has never been harnessed to benefit treatment. NF-κB is well described to have anti-viral activity, notably via the induction of cytokines and chemokines that assist viral clearance,18 and a previous study by another group has shown that the drug dimethyl fumarate could enhance oVSV in various cancers, an effect that was associated with NF-κB blockade.49 Here, we show that NF-κB blockade with SSZ and IKK16 enhances oVSV in TNBC cells and in a patient sample. Given that in breast cancer NF-κB promotes cancer stem cells,21 proliferation,19 and resistance to therapy,50 its blockade is particularly relevant in the context of this disease and may have therapeutic benefits in addition to promoting oVSV infection.

Given the clinical status of VSV, which is already undergoing human trials for cancer treatment, as well as SSZ, which is already approved for the treatment of autoimmune diseases, our virus-drug combination has the potential to be rapidly translated to the bedside. Notably, our approach could be applied to all breast cancers. Indeed, NF-κB blockade enhances oVSV independently of E2 and ER expression and could therefore be applied to all breast cancer subtypes. Furthermore, given that endocrine therapy prevents the E2-mediated NF-κB blockade, our strategy could also benefit patients receiving endocrine therapy.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this study, we evaluated the impact of E2 on the efficacy of oVSV and the mechanism by which E2 modulates infection. We used human breast cancer cell lines, xenografts, and patient samples to show that ER+ breast cancers are sensitive to oVSV via E2 stimulation of ERɑ. We used an unbiased RNA-seq approach to identify the mechanism by which E2 enhances oVSV and then performed cytokine arrays and IF experiments to validate our findings. We exploited microfluidic technology to infect xenografts and breast cancer patient samples ex vivo to test the impact of various conditions on infection, as well as virus-drug treatment combinations.

Cell lines and culture

The human luminal ER+ breast cancer MCF-7, T-47D, and ZR-75 cell lines and the TNBC Hs 578T, BT-549, HCC1937, MDA-MB-231, and MDA-MB-468 cell lines, as well as the Vero African green monkey kidney epithelial cell line were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection and maintained at 37oC with 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Wisent). For experiments requiring hormone-free media, cells were plated in phenol red-free DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% charcoal-stripped FBS (Gibco).

Estrogen stimulation and drug treatments

Experiments in which E2 is supplemented were conducted in hormone-free media. Unless indicated otherwise in the figure legends, E2 (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the culture media 12–28 h pre-infection to a final concentration of 100 nM.

For all other experiments, regular complete culture media was used, as described in the cell lines and culture section above. For 4-OHT (MedChemExpress) treatment, the drug was used at final concentrations of 0.7–1 μM, as indicated in the corresponding figure legends, 4 h prior to virus infection. The NF-κB-blocking drugs SSZ (Sigma-Aldrich) and IKK16 (Tocris) were solubilized in DMSO (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and added to the culture media at the final concentrations of 50 μM (0.05% DMSO) and 5 μM (0.17% DMSO) 4 h prior to virus treatment. Carboplatin (Cayman Chemical) and Mg132 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were both used at final concentrations of 50 and 5 μM 4 h prior to virus treatment.

Virus production and quantification

The virus used in this study is a mutant of the Indiana strain of VSV for which the methionine 51 of the matrix protein was deleted (oVSV or VSVΔ51).12 For some experiments, a variant of oVSV that has been engineered to encode the fluorescent protein YFP was used to allow for the live monitoring of infection. Both viruses have been described previously.27

For virus production, Vero cells were infected at an MOI of 0.01 for 24 h, and culture supernatants were collected and filtered using 0.22-μm bottle-top vacuum filters (Ultident) and centrifuged for 90 min at 30,000 × g. The pellets were then resuspended in PBS (Gibco), and the virus suspension was aliquoted and stored at −80°C.

Infectious viral particles were quantified by plaque assay. Briefly, serial dilutions of infectious samples were added onto monolayers of Vero cells for 1 h prior to overlay with a warm solution of complete media with 1% agarose (Thermo Fisher). The plates were then incubated overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2, and the plaques were counted.

Fluorescence imaging

High-resolution pictures of oVSV-YFP-infected cells and microdissected xenografts were taken using a Zoe cell imager (Bio-Rad). For high-throughput experiments and fluorescence quantification, we used a multimode plate reader (Ensight, PerkinElmer) and its associated software Kaleido version 3.0 (PerkinElmer).

Flow cytometry

Adherent cells were detached from the plates using 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (catalog no. 25300054, Thermo Fisher) and then resuspended in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (2% FBS, 0.01% sodium azide [catalog no. S2002, Millipore Sigma] in PBS). Cell suspensions were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Yellow (Thermo Fisher) for 30 min at 4°C and then incubated in fixing buffer (2% formaldehyde [Millipore Sigma] in FACS buffer) for 20 min at 4°C. The data were acquired using a Symphony A3 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo 10.10.0 software (BD Biosciences).

Cell proliferation assay

For cell proliferation experiments, cells were seeded at 5%–10% confluency in 24-well plates (Corning) and transferred to an IncuCyte ZOOM live cell imaging system 24 h later (Sartorius). Pictures from 4 random fields per well were taken every 12 h for 6 days, and the data were analyzed using IncuCyte ZOOM software version 2018A.

Xenograft models and patient samples

All in vivo experiments were performed in accordance with the Centre Hospitalier de l’Université de Montréal (CHUM) Research Centre’s (CRCHUM’s) animal care committee guidelines. For tumor xenografts, 106 tumor cells were mixed with 100 μL Cultrex Basement Membrane Extract (R&D Systems) and injected subcutaneously into the left flanks of 6- to 8-week-old NOD.Cg-Rag1tm1MomIL2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ mice.51 Tumors were collected when they reached volumes of 1,500 mm3 as measured with manual calipers (VWR). Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula (width2 × length)/2.

Experiments using patient samples were performed in accordance with the CRCHUM’s ethics committee. All samples were obtained from tumor resection surgeries performed at the CHUM. Patient consent was obtained by the CRCHUM’s breast cancer biobank.

Ex vivo infection

Patient samples and tumor xenografts were infected ex vivo using microfluidic devices that have been described previously.30 Each device includes 4 channels, each of which contain 8 wells (36 wells per device). Tissue slices of 1 mm were cut using a McIIwain tissue chopper (Ted Pella) prior to microdissection using a 500-μm biopsy punch (Zivic Instruments). Microdissected tissues were then loaded into the devices as described previously.29 Tumor cores were incubated in complete culture media supplemented with 0.6 mg/mL amphotericin B and 55 μg/mL gentamicin (both from Wisent). The media was replenished the next morning and replaced 8 h later by different culture media as described in the figure legends (complete culture media ± 4-OHT, SSZ, or IKK16 or hormone-free media ± E2). Samples were infected the next morning with 1 × 107 plaque-forming units (PFU)/mL oVSV-YFP in the same culture media formulations. Culture supernatants were collected 24 h later, and the samples were fixed in formalin as previously described30 for IF analysis.

IF

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue slices were stained as described previously.52 Briefly, antigen retrieval was performed using a BenchMark XT Autostainer (Ventana Medical System-Roche). The slides were then incubated with a mouse anti-VSV glycoprotein antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature (RT), washed with PBS, incubated with a protein block (Agilent) for 20 min at RT, and then incubated with a goat anti-mouse cyanine 5 secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Slides were then washed with PBS and incubated with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 40 min and a solution of 0.1% Sudan black (Sigma-Aldrich) in 70% ethanol. Finally, fluoromount aqueous mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to mount coverslips, and images were acquired using an Olympus optical microscope.

For IF on coverslips, cells were cultured on hydrobromide poly-l-lysine-pretreated glass coverslips 12 h prior to media change, infection, and treatment as detailed in the figure legends. At the indicated time point, the cells were washed with PBS (Gibco) and fixed using an ice-cold solution of 1:1 methanol (Chaptec):acetone (Millipore Sigma). The coverslips were then washed with PBS and blocked using a solution of 5% FBS (Wisent) and 0.3% Triton (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 30 min. All antibodies were diluted in 1% bovine serum albumin (Wisent) in PBS. The antibody panel included a mouse anti-VSV glycoprotein (Sigma-Aldrich) and rabbit anti-NF-κB antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology). The coverslips were first incubated with a mixture of the primary antibodies for 1 h, washed with PBS, incubated for 50 min with the secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit A594 [Invitrogen] and goat anti-rabbit A488 [Invitrogen] antibodies) and then with DAPI (Abcam) for 10 min (all at RT). Finally, the coverslips were washed with PBS and deionized water and mounted onto glass microscope slides (Life Technologies) using the ProLong Gold Antifade reagent (Life Technologies). Slides were allowed to dry for 24 h, and pictures were taken using a Zeiss Observer.Z1 inverted live-cell fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Microscopy). Images were analyzed using ZEN Microscopy software (Zeiss Microscopy).

siRNA

Monolayers of tumor cells were transfected with human ESR1 (smartpool: 5′-GAAUGUGCCUGGCUAGAGA-3′, 5′-CAUGAGAGCUGCCAACCUU-3′, 5′-GAUCAAACGCUCUAAGAAG-3′, 5′-UGAAAGGUGGGAUACGAAA-3′), ESR2 (smartpool: 5′-GGGCUGAUGUGGCGCUCAA-3′, 5′-GUGUGAAGCAAGAUCGCUA-3′, 5′-CAAGAAGAUUCCCGGCUUU-3′, 5′-CUUUAGUGGUCCAUCGCCA-3′), control siRNAs (5′- UAGGACUAAACACAUCAA-3′, 5′-UAAGGUAUGAAGAGAUAC-3′, 5′-AUGUAUUGGCCUGUAUUAG-3′, 5′-AUGAACGUGAAUUGCUCAA-3′) or p65 smartpool siRNAs (all from Horizon Discovery), using RNAiMAX lipofectamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfected cells were infected 48 h post-transfection at the indicated MOIs.

Western blot

Cell pellets were lysed using commercial radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Abcam) supplemented with complete protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich) and centrifugated for 10 min at 12,000 × g. Protein concentrations from cleared lysates were measured using a DC protein assay kit (Bio-Rad) per the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were migrated on 12% acrylamide gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (both from Bio-Rad). Membranes were incubated in a solution of TBS-T (0.02 M Tris base, 0.15 M NaCl, Tween 20 at 0.05%) with 5% milk (No Name) and then probed with mouse anti-ERα, rabbit anti-ERβ (both from Abcam), or rabbit anti-vinculin (Cell Signaling Technology) antibodies. The next day, membranes were washed in TBS-T and incubated 1 h with a goat anti-mouse- or anti-rabbit-horseradish peroxidase secondary antibody. All antibodies were diluted in TBS-T 5% milk. Signals were revealed using the Clarity Max Western ECL Substrate and a Chemidoc imaging system (both from Bio-Rad).

ELISA and cytokine array

Culture supernatants were collected as indicated in the figure legends, and ELISAs and cytokine arrays were performed to quantify cytokines and chemokines. Human IL-8 and CCL2 production was measured using commercial kits as described in the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher and R&D Systems, respectively), and a human cytokine array kit (R&D Systems) was also used per the manufacturer’s protocol. For the cytokine array, signals were quantified using Adobe Photoshop and expressed as relative to internal controls and then compared to the different experimental conditions.

Spreading assay

MCF-7 and T-47D cells were seeded in 6-well plates in complete culture media to reach confluency 24 h later. A stock solution of oVSV was diluted to 102–103 PFU/mL, and 100 μL was added to the wells for 1 h. The cells were then overlaid with a warm solution of hormone-free culture media (±100 nM E2) containing 1% agarose (Thermo Fisher). The plates were incubated overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2. The next day, the plated were fixed with a solution of fixing buffer (3:1 methanol [Chaptec]:acetic acid [Fisher Scientific]) for 30 min, washed with tap water, and then stained with Coomassie blue staining solution (0.1% Coomassie blue [Bio-Rad] in fixing buffer) for 30 min. The plates were then dried, and pictures were taken. Isolated plaques were measured.

RNA-seq and analysis

Library preparation

RNA-seq libraries were generated from 250 ng total RNA as follows: mRNA enrichment was performed using the NEBNext Poly(A) Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs). cDNA synthesis was achieved with the NEBNext RNA First Strand Synthesis and NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Second Strand Synthesis Modules (New England Biolabs). The remaining steps of library preparation were done using and the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs). Adapters and PCR primers were purchased from New England Biolabs. The libraries were normalized and pooled and then denatured in 0.02 N NaOH and neutralized using HT1 buffer. The pool was loaded at 200 pM on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 lane using the Xp protocol per the manufacturer’s recommendations. The run was performed for 2 × 100 cycles (paired-end mode). A phiX library was used as a control and mixed with libraries at the 1% level. Base calling was performed with RTA version 3. Program bcl2fastq2 version 2.20 was then used to demultiplex samples and generate fastq reads.

Preprocessing and differential expression

Pseudoalignment and transcript quantification for each sample was performed using Kallisto (version 0.44.0)53 with the GRCh38 build of the human genome with bootstrapping using the parameter -b 50. Differential expression between experimental groups was performed using the R package sleuth using a likelihood ratio test.54 Significant genes were defined as those with an false discovery rate-adjusted p value <0.05 and |logFC| > 1. Gene Ontology term overrepresentation among differentially expressed genes was tested using the R package gProfileR.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 9.5.1.
