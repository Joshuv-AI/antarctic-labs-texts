Compositional Verification in Concurrent Separation Logic with Permissions Regions
Quang Loc Le

Abstract
Concurrent separation logic with fractional permissions ( CSL-Perm ) provides a promising reasoning system to verify most complex sequential and concurrent fine-grained programs. The logic with strong and weak separating conjunctions offers a solid foundation for producing concise and precise proofs. However, it lacks automation and compositionality support. This paper addresses this limitation by introducing a compositional verification system for concurrent programs that manipulate regions of shared memory. The centre of our system is novel logical principles and an entailment procedure that can infer the residual heaps in the frame rule for a fragment of CSL-Perm with explicit arithmetical constraints for memory heaps’ disjointness. This procedure enables the compositional reasoning for concurrent threads and function calls. We have implemented the proposal in a prototype tool called CoSl , tested it with 10 challenging concurrent programs, including those beyond the state-of-the-art, and confirmed the advantage of our approach.

Compositional verification for inter-procedures.

One of the key challenges in program analysis is

Separation logic (SL)

It includes separating conjunction

(\dagger{:}~\mathit{Modify(C)}\cap\mathit{FreeVar(F)}=\emptyset)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left\{P*F\right\}}}{C}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left\{Q*F\right\}}}

Frame inference, the foundation of bi-abduction

Compositional verification for concurrent threads.

There have been some preliminary efforts to transfer compositional verification to concurrency
world using

[
31

CSL extends SL

In those programs, each thread owns a separate portion of resources.
CSL proposed

is a resource whose ownership can be split into two

P_{1}
and
P_{2}

Program analysis can obtain the thread-modular reasoning via the following

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left\{P_{1}\right\}}}{C_{1}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left\{Q_{1}\right\}}}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left\{P_{2}\right\}}}{C_{2}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left\{Q_{2}\right\}}}

(\mathrm{Concurrency-CSL})

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left\{P_{1}*P_{2}\right\}}}{C_{1}||C_{2}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left\{Q_{1}*Q_{2}\right\}}}

The rule reads: If

C_{2}
are non-interference concurrent processes (i.e.,

does not modify free variables in
P_{2}
and
Q_{2}
and vice versa),
then we can analyse these threads independently in the separated ownership

P_{1}
and
P_{2}
and combined their analysis results
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}

afterwards.
However, the rule above does not work for

in which threads interferes each other

To support concurrent fine-grained programs, Bornat

They introduced fractional permission over shared heaps,

Furthermore, the logic specifies shared heaps using

To bridge the gap of

Recently,

Overview and Illustrative Examples

In this section,
we explain basic concepts, including
distribution principles, frame inference procedure, and modular verification through
examples.

Distribution principles.

In practice, sequential code
and concurrent code are often mixed together.
When entering concurrent code from sequential code,
an analysis may split
heap permissions on shared resources
with multiple threads, e.g., using the distribution principle

@_{\alpha}A*@_{\beta}B\models@_{\alpha}A\circledast@_{\beta}B
to distribute
*
over
\circledast

[
8
]
.
Later, when joining the threads and the permissions,
the analysis may want to recover the disjointness for a stronger post-state.
However,
@_{\alpha}A\circledast@_{\beta}B\not\models@_{\alpha}A*@_{\beta}B

as we have lost information when applying the previous distribution.
In this paper, we propose new logical principles to retain the heaps’ disjointness.
For instance, we fix the above distribution as
@_{\alpha}A*@_{\beta}B=@_{\alpha}A\circledast@_{\beta}B\land{\hbox{\pagecolor{bluebell}$\alpha\mathsf{\bot}\beta$}}

where
\alpha\mathsf{\bot}\beta
is a pure/non-heap formula that specifies the heaps’ disjointness;
it enables the analysis to recover the disjointness.

Frame inference.

We propose a procedure, called
FrInfer
, to
solve the following frame inference problem:

\vskip-2.84526pt\begin{array}[]{l}\text{Given:}~A\mbox{ and goal }G\\
\text{To find:}~\mbox{a collection of frame assertions }F\mbox{ such that }$$A\models G\circledast F$$\end{array}

where
F
includes the residual heap (of
A
) and arithmetical constraints
that instantiate those logical variables and existentially quantified variables in
G

with those terms in
A
, and

\circledast
is
the weak separating conjunction.
FrInfer
naturally automates
the frame rule with weak separating conjunction.
Moreover,
FrInfer
also implements the distribution principles
in
a function, called
\textsf{ws{-}norm}({A})
,
so that
F
includes disjointness constraints that helps transforming

G\circledast F
to
G*F
, when applicable. And so,

FrInfer
can support the automation of the frame rule
with strong separating conjunction as well.

We illustrate how
FrInfer
finds frame
F_{1}

for the following
entailment problem:
@_{\alpha}{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{1/2}}}{{\mapsto}}{y}*@_{\beta}{y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{1/2}}}{{\mapsto}}{\mathsf{nil}}\wedge x\neq y\models\exists w.@_{\gamma}{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{1/2}}}{{\mapsto}}{w}
.
Here,

, a shorthand for
(@_{\alpha}{x}\mapsto y)^{1/2}
,
says that

points to a heap with
1/2
permission, named
\alpha
, and its content is

\alpha
,
\beta

and

Our entailment procedure

outputs
\{F_{1}\}

with

F_{1}\equiv@_{\beta}{y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{1/2}}}{{\mapsto}}{\mathsf{nil}}\wedge x\neq y\wedge\alpha\mathsf{\bot}\beta\land{\hbox{\pagecolor{bluebell}$\gamma=\alpha\land w=y$}}
.

FrInfer
infers that if we instantiate the logical variable

with
\alpha
and the existentially quantified variable

with
y

(the constraint in the
greyed area
shows the instantiation).

includes the cell pointed to by

.
Furthermore,

specifies the heaps’ disjointness
and helps to recover the disjointness whenever

is re-combined with the LHS of

Similarly, given the entailment, referred to as

@_{\alpha}{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{1/2}}}{{\mapsto}}{y}*@_{\beta}{y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{1/2}}}{{\mapsto}}{\mathsf{nil}}\wedge x\neq y\models\exists w.@_{\gamma}{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{1/4}}}{{\mapsto}}{w}

The residual heaps include two cells: while the cell pointed to by

Compositional verification.

Our verification system,

CoSl
, verifies a program bottom-up; it
verifies the callees before the caller and split/join threads before
the main thread.
It statically executes
a program by calculating symbolical constraints
on values stored in program variables. This execution amounts to
the transformation of the constraints over
program statements whose meanings are given via their abstractions
in the form of Hoare triples.
For instance
Hoare triple
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left\{\mathsf{pre}\right\}}}{C}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left\{\mathsf{post}\right\}}}

of program

and post-condition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{post}}

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{pre}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{post}}
are

Proof Search Algorithm

Given an entailment
A\models G
,

FrInfer
build a forest of proofs.
Initially, it creates a proof tree where the root is
A\models G
. It marks this node as an on-going leaf.
For each on-going leaf, it
attempts to match the entailment with the conclusion of the inference
rules in the following order.

1.

If
G\equiv\mathsf{emp}\wedge\mathsf{true}
, mark this node as done with frame
\{A\}
;

2.

For each atomic pure formula of
G
, attempt rule
\mathrm{Hypothesis}
;

3.

If
A\equiv B^{\pi}\land\mathsf{p}
and
G\equiv C^{\pi}\land\mathsf{p}^{\prime}

\mathrm{\pi}

4.

Attempt matching rules
\mathrm{M}
,
\mathrm{M*}
,
\mathrm{M}\mapsto
, and
\mathrm{M*}\mapsto

5.

If
G
is a conjunction of
\circledast

G

with the RHS of the conclusion of rule
\mathrm{Split}\,\circledast

Otherwise, try rule
\circledast/*
;

6.

If
G
is a conjunction of
*
, match
A

with the LHS of the conclusion of rule
\mathrm{Join}\,\circledast

Otherwise, try rule
*/\circledast
;

7.

Attempt
\mathrm{LU}
. If it fails, try
\mathrm{RU}
;

8.

Lastly, other rules.

For all the rule, except
\mathrm{RU}
, if a rule can be applied,
FrInfer
extends the tree with new on-going leaves each of which
is one of its premise.
For
\mathrm{RU}
, it clones a new tree before do the above extension.
If no rule applies,
FrInfer
searches another proof tree. If it finds a tree with all done leaves, it stops the search
and collects a disjunctive set of frames at the leaves.
If there is no remaining tree to search, it returns an unknown exception.

We have implemented

Our implementation supports

A fork takes a procedure name

creates a new thread executing the procedure

the newly-created thread.

waits for the thread

CoSl
uses

to find the frames between
the current program state and
the pre-condition of

\circledast

then normalize)

proc
.

Furthermore, we enhance the inference rules in

FrInfer

by maintaining a data structure that captures
equivalence classes of variables in every pure formula. For example, with
the entailment
@_{\alpha}{x}\mapsto{\mathsf{nil}}\land x=y\land y=z\models@_{\beta}{z}\mapsto{\mathsf{nil}}
,
it embeds the pure formula in the LHS with
an equivalence class as a set
\{x,y,z\}
.
To apply rule

\mathrm{M}\mapsto
,
FrInfer
checks whether
the two points-to predicates of the two side pointed to by the same variable.
The equivalence class of the LHS contains three elements,
so it re-arranges the head of the points-to predicate and
tries the following three entailments:

@_{\alpha}{x}\mapsto{\mathsf{nil}}\land x=y\land y=z\models@_{\beta}{z}\mapsto{\mathsf{nil}}
,

@_{\alpha}{y}\mapsto{\mathsf{nil}}\land x=y\land y=z\models@_{\beta}{z}\mapsto{\mathsf{nil}}
, and

@_{\alpha}{z}\mapsto{\mathsf{nil}}\land x=y\land y=z\models@_{\beta}{z}\mapsto{\mathsf{nil}}
.
Since the two points-to predicates in the last entailment match,
the original entailment
@_{\alpha}{x}\mapsto{\mathsf{nil}}\land x=y\land y=z\models@_{\beta}{z}\mapsto{\mathsf{nil}}
holds.

Related Work and Conclusion

Our work innovatively extends the reasoning system over

{\mathsf{SL}_{\mathsf{LP}}}

[
8
]
.
It surpasses
[
8
]
by introducing modular reasoning
using a frame inference procedure and an implemented compositional
analysis algorithm. Furthermore, our principles on distribution among
sharing conjunction
\circledast
and separating conjunction
*
are more precise than
the ones in
[
8
]
. These principles
help our system to generate stronger post-states during
the verification and thus make
CoSl
more powerful.

Many revisions of CSL have been
proposed to analyse interference and shared resources in
concurrent fine-grained programs.
Their representatives include
CSL-Perm
- the family of
CSL with permissions regions
[
3
,
8
,
27
]
,
CSL
+
Rely/Guarantee
[
39
]
,
CaReSL
[
37
]
, iCAP
[
35
]
, and
Views
[
14
]
, and “protocol-based” notions of disjointness
in program logic such as
FCSL
[
29
]
and Iris
[
21
]
.

CSL-Perm
are amenable to automation as they
are simpler and more uniform than protocol-based logics.
Furthermore, while a few could support automation
(for instance, SmallfootRG
[
11
]
for CSL
+
Rely/Guarantee,
Caper
[
15
]
for CAP family, and
Starling
[
40
]
for Views),
only
CSL-Perm
has demonstrated the compositionality with a frame inference procedure.

Our modular verification system for concurrent programs closely relates to
Smallfoot
[
2
]
, jStar
[
18
]
,
Verifast
[
20
]
, and ShareInfer
[
27
]
.
Unlike ours, these works support CSL with only one separating conjunction
and are much simpler than ours. Smallfoot discussed local reasoning for concurrency in the original CSL

[
31
]
. As the logic reused the separating conjunction
to represent the ownership over separating resources, it could be
implied that the compositional analysis might be obtained straightforwardly
using the frame inference presented in
[
1
]
. Indeed,
such a frame inference was studied in jStar.
Furthermore, as shown in
[
5
]
and
[
3
]
,
the ownership transfer paradigm cannot
describe
passivity
in programs that
share read access among threads.
Verifast is a mechanised analyser
built on CSL with fractional permission
[
4
]
.
ShareInfer, a bi-abductive procedure,
was based on the logic that
revamps the disjointness of permission heap regions
using tree shares. In contrast to ShareInfer, the underlying logic helps
CoSl
to
reason programs beyond precise formulas. Furthermore,
it is a modular verification, which goes beyond
a frame inference procedure.
While our system, like Smallfoot, jStar, and ShareInfer,
could be fully automated, we envision extending our frame inference with
abduction
[
10
,
12
,
23
,
36
]
and lazy initialization
[
32
,
33
]
to infer procedure’s
and a thread-modular summary. Additionally, we see potential in enhancing reasoning over inductive predicates
with an induction method, such as cyclic proofs like the one in
[
24
,
26
]
.
These future research directions demonstrate the scalability and adaptability of our system.

Our work, which develops an automatic verification
for concurrent programs, shares the same motivation with SmallfootRG
[
11
]
, Starling
[
40
]

and Caper
[
15
]
.
These systems rely on ordinary separation logic solvers
(like GRASShopper

[
34
]
), it takes them a high cost to translate concurrent verification
conditions. In contrast,
CoSl
builds a solver for
\mathsf{SL}_{\mathsf{aLP}}

so that it can discharge the verification conditions directly and is thus more efficient.

SmallfootRG is built on an extension of CSL with rely/guarantee and can verify
fine-grained concurrent programs. However, as it is based on the paradigm of
rely/guarantee, it maintains auxiliary global variables.
Hence, it could not preserve the local reasoning,
the basis for compositional verification, like ours.
Starling is an instantiation of the Views framework
[
14
]
;
it computes for
each thread a separating view and then combines them, via a concurrency rule,
for the view of
their parent thread.
Starling neither supports functional correctness, frame inference, or modular verification.
Caper is based on concurrent abstract predicates
[
16
]
,
a separation logic with
auxiliary guard algebras, shared regions, and actions. Although it can verify
the functional properties of
concurrent programs, unlike ours,
the paper did not present a proof system to infer frame assertions. Therefore,
it is unclear how Caper can compositionally reason about
programs.
Finally, Diaframe implements a mechanised proof system based on Iris
[
28
]
.
While its proof rules are based on bi-abduction concept, our system develop
frame inference, the foundation for a bi-abductive inference for
\mathsf{SL}_{\mathsf{aLP}}
.

Conclusion

We develop foundational principles for the distribution between
strong and weak separating conjunctions.
Based on that, we build the frame inference procedure
FrInfer

and the compositional verifier
CoSl
.
The proposed principles enable our system to verify fine-grained concurrent programs
which are beyond the current systems.
One of our future works is to extend
FrInfer

with bi-abduction to infer specifications for procedures
and concurrent threads.
Another direction is to explore cyclic proofs for
the frame inference problem in
\mathsf{SL}_{\mathsf{aLP}}
.
Yet another complementary direction is to study an under-approximation
of
CSL-Perm
to find concurrency bugs.
These works would advance
the verification of presence and absence of bugs in concurrent programs.
