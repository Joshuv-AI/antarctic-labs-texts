Provably Data-driven Projection Method for Quadratic Programming
Anh Tuan Nguyen, Viet Anh Nguyen

Abstract
Projection methods aim to reduce the dimensionality of the optimization instance, thereby improving the scalability of high-dimensional problems. Recently, Sakaue and Oki 2024 proposed a data-driven approach for linear programs (LPs), where the projection matrix is learned from observed problem instances drawn from an application-specific distribution of problems. We analyze the generalization guarantee for the data-driven projection matrix learning for convex quadratic programs (QPs). Unlike in LPs, the optimal solutions of convex QPs are not confined to the vertices of the feasible polyhedron, and this complicates the analysis of the optimal value function. To overcome this challenge, we demonstrate that the solutions of convex QPs can be localized within a feasible region corresponding to a special active set, utilizing Carathéodory’s theorem. Building on such observation, we propose the unrolled active set method , which models the computation of the optimal value as a Goldberg-Jerrum (GJ) algorithm with bounded complexities, thereby establishing learning guarantees. We then further extend our analysis to other settings, including learning to match the optimal solution and input-aware setting, where we learn a mapping from QP problem instances to projection matrices.

Introduction

Linear programs (LPs) and the more general quadratic programs (QPs) are simple forms of convex optimization problems, yet they play crucial roles in many industrial
(
Gass 2003
;
Dostál 2009
)
and scientific domains
(
Amos 2022
)
. Practical LP and QP instances are usually computationally intensive to solve due to the enormous problem size, which can reach millions of variables and constraints. As a result, accelerating solving approaches for large-scale LPs and QPs are important directions in the operations research literature, of which two most prominent approaches include accelerated solvers and dimensionality reduction methods. Accelerated solvers focus on improving the speed of widely-used solvers on large-scale problems via parallelization, randomization, or wisely leveraging the cheap first-order (i.e., gradient) information, to name a few. Some of the recent advances include parallelized simplex methods
(
Huangfu and Hall 2018
)
, randomized interior point methods
(
Chowdhury et al. 2022
)
, and primal-dual hybrid gradient methods
(
Applegate et al. 2021
)
.

Another complementary, solver-agnostic approach for large-scale LPs and QPs is the dimensionality reduction technique, of which the general idea is to reduce the size of the problem instances while preserving the properties of the objective values and variables. A promising candidate for this approach is through random projections
(
d’Ambrosio et al. 2020
;
Vu et al. 2019
;
Vu et al. 2018
)
, where a random projection matrix is used to map the variables and feasible regions of the original problem instances onto a low-dimensional space to form projected problem instances, which can be solved much faster. The solutions of projected problem instances can then be mapped back to the original space in the hope that their quality is sufficiently comparable to the optimal solution of the original problem instances. Importantly, this solver-agnostic approach can be combined with accelerated solvers to further improve the solving of large-scale LPs and QPs.

However, random projection matrices neglect the geometric property of the problem instances, and this negligence potentially leads to inferior solution quality of the projected problem instances compared to that of the original problem instances. Recently,
Sakaue and Oki 2024
proposed a data-driven approach for learning the projection matrix, specifically targeting LPs. Assume that there are not one, but multiple LPs
\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}}=(\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})\in\Pi_{\textup{LP}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
that have to be solved in the form

\textup{OPT}(\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}})=\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}\mid\bm{Ax}\leq\bm{b}\}.

The parameters
\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}}
are drawn from some application-specific and potentially unknown problem distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\textup{LP}}
over
\Pi_{\textup{LP}}
.
Sakaue and Oki 2024
proposed to learn the projection matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
, where
k\ll n
is the dimensionality of the projection space, by minimizing the expected optimal objective of the projected LPs
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\textup{LP}}}[\ell_{\textup{LP}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}})]
, where

\ell_{\textup{LP}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}})=\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\{\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{y}\mid\bm{A}\bm{Py}\leq\bm{b}\}

is the optimal objective value of the projected LP. Because
\mathcal{D}
is unknown, minimizing
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\textup{LP}}}[\ell_{\textup{LP}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}})]
is intractable, and we instead learn
\bm{P}
via
empirical risk minimization (ERM)
using LP problem instances drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{\textup{LP}}
. It is easy to see that
\ell_{\textup{LP}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}})
upper-bounds
\textup{OPT}(\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}})
, and therefore the smaller
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\textup{LP}}}[\ell_{\textup{LP}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}})]
is, the closer the quality of solutions of projected problem instances to that of original problem instances. Along with promising empirical results,
Sakaue and Oki 2024
provided generalization guarantees for learning
\bm{P}
via ERM by analyzing the learning-theoretic complexity (i.e., pseudo-dimension
Pollard 1984
) of the corresponding loss function class
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{LP}}=\{\ell_{\bm{P}}:\Pi_{\textup{LP}}\rightarrow[-H,0]\mid\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}\}
, where
\ell_{\bm{P}}(\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}}):=\ell_{\textup{LP}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}})
, and
H
is some real-valued upper-bound for the function class.

Inspired by this success, a natural direction is to extend this framework to convex QPs. Similarly, given QP problem instances
\bm{\pi}=(\bm{Q},\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})\in\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
coming from an application-specific, unknown problem distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\Pi
, the idea is to learn a projection matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
with
k\ll n
that achieves small population loss
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})]
via ERM, where

\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})=\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\biggr\{\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{Py}+\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{Py}\mid\bm{A}\bm{Py}\leq\bm{b}\biggr\}.

Again, to ensure the generalization guarantee for
\bm{P}
learned via ERM, we need to analyze the function class
\mathcal{L}=\{\ell_{\bm{P}}:\Pi\rightarrow[-H,0]\mid\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}\}
, where
\ell_{\bm{P}}(\bm{\pi}):=\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
.

At first glance, the extension to QPs may seem straightforward because the previous ideas seem readily applicable to the form of QPs, and the gradient update can also be derived using the envelope theorem (see Appendix
D
for details). However, the optimal solutions of QPs exhibit fundamentally different geometrical structures, and it turns out that extending the existing theoretical framework to QPs requires developing new tools tailored to these specific problems.

Contributions.

We formalize the data-driven projection method for convex QPs and analyze the generalization guarantees for learning the projection matrix. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.

We establish generalization guarantees for the data-driven learning projection matrix for QPs in Theorem
5.7
. Our new result is more general and strictly tighter than the previous bound proposed by
Sakaue and Oki 2024
, which is applicable only to LPs. For completeness, we also instantiate a lower bound for the convex QP case in Proposition
5.8
.

2.

We propose and analyze a novel learning scenario, where the goal is to match the optimal solution in Section
6.1
. This setting is particularly useful in practical applications where the focus is on the solution to be implemented. The guarantee result is presented in Theorem
6.1
.

3.

We consider the input-aware settings, where we instead learn a neural network that maps a convex QP to a customized projection matrix in Section
6.2
. The guarantee result is presented in Theorem
6.2
.

1.1
Technical Challenges and Overviews

For LPs, the crucial observation is that for any LP with parameters
\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}}=(\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})
and any projection matrix
\bm{P}
, the solution of the projected LP always lies on one of the vertices of the feasible polyhedron. Leveraging such observation,
Sakaue and Oki 2024
describes the computation of the projected LP’s optimal value
\ell_{\textup{LP}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}})
by enumerating all potential vertices, and identifies the vertex
\bm{y}^{*}
that produces the lowest objective
\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{y}^{*}
. The computation of
\ell_{\textup{LP}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\textup{LP}})
can then be described by a bounded number of distinct conditional statements involving polynomials in the entries of
\bm{P}
; see Section
3.2
for details.

This favorable property, however, does not extend to QPs, as the solution of QPs can be anywhere within the feasible polyhedron, not just at its vertices. This makes directly locating the solution and calculating the optimal objective
\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
very challenging. To overcome this issue, we propose a four-step analytical approach. First, we will construct a perturbed objective
\ell_{\bm{P},\gamma}(\bm{\pi})
that is well-behaved and can approximate
\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
with arbitrarily precision shown in Lemma
5.1
and Proposition
5.2
. Second, we leverage the structure of this perturbed problem to develop the
unrolled active set method
, an algorithm that exactly computes its optimal value in Lemma
5.4
. Third, we demonstrate that our method can be framed as a GJ algorithm with bounded complexities in Lemma
5.5
, which enables us to bound the pseudo-dimension of the perturbed function class. Finally, by relating the perturbed objective to the original, we extend this bound to the true QP loss function, thereby proving our main generalization guarantee in Theorem
5.7
.

Related Works

Projection methods for LPs and QPs.

Projection methods aim to accelerate the solution of LPs and QPs by reducing the size of the problem instances. Prior works have investigated random projection for reducing the number of constraints
(
Vu et al. 2019
;
Poirion et al. 2023
)
and variables
(
Akchen and Misic 2025
)
. Recently,
Sakaue and Oki 2024
;
Iwata and Sakaue 2025
considered a data-driven approach, learning the projection matrix for a specific problem distribution instead of random projection, targeting LPs specifically. Our paper extends this framework to convex QPs.

Learning to optimize.

Learning to optimize leverages machine learning to develop optimization methods, i.e., by either predicting an initial solution for the exact algorithm, approximating the exact solution directly, or adapting specific components of optimization algorithms
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Amos et al. 2023
;
Bengio et al. 2021
)
. Learning to project for LPs
(
Sakaue and Oki 2024
;
Iwata and Sakaue 2025
)
and convex QPs (this work) belongs to this broad category, where the learned projection matrices are used to accelerate off-the-shelf solvers and produce approximate solutions that are guaranteed to be feasible, unlike prior methods that approximate optimal solutions directly using neural networks.

Data-driven algorithm design.

Data-driven algorithm design
(
Balcan 2020
;
Gupta and Roughgarden 2020
)
is an emerging algorithm design paradigm that proposes adapting algorithms by configuring their hyperparameters or internal components to the specific set of problem instances they must solve, rather than considering the worst-case problem instances. Assuming that there is an application-specific, potentially unknown problem distribution from which the problem instances are drawn, data-driven algorithm design aims to maximize its empirical performance using the observed problem instances, with the hope that the adapted algorithm will perform well on future problem instances drawn from the same problem distribution. Data-driven algorithm design is an active research direction in both empirical validation and theoretical analysis across various domains, including sketching and low-rank approximation
(
Indyk et al. 2019
;
Bartlett et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
)
, (mixed) integer linear programming
(
Balcan et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2023
)
, tuning regularization hyperparameters
(
Balcan et al. 2022
;
Balcan et al. 2023
)
, and other general frameworks for theoretical analysis in data-driven settings
Bartlett et al. 2022
;
Balcan et al. 2025a
;
Balcan et al. 2025b
. Data-driven projection methods for LPs
(
Sakaue and Oki 2024
)
and QPs are specific instances of data-driven algorithm design.

3
Backgrounds on Learning Theory

3.1
Pseudo-dimension

We recall the notion of
pseudo-dimension
, the main learning-theoretic complexity we use throughout this work.

Definition 1

(Pseudo-dimension,
Pollard 1984
)
.

Consider a real-valued function class
\mathcal{L}
, of which each function
\ell
takes input
\bm{\pi}
in
\Pi
and output
\ell(\bm{\pi})\in[-H,0]
. Given a set of inputs
S=(\bm{\pi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\pi}_{N})\subset\Pi
, we say that
S
is shattered by
\mathcal{L}
if there exists a set of real-valued threshold
r_{1},\dots,r_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\left|\{({\text{sign}}(\ell(\bm{\pi}_{1})-r_{1}),\dots,{\text{sign}}(\ell(\bm{\pi}_{N})-r_{N}))\mid\ell\in\mathcal{L}\}\right|=2^{N}
. The pseudo-dimension of
\mathcal{L}
, denoted as
\text{Pdim}(\mathcal{L})
, is the maximum size
N
of a input set that
\mathcal{L}
can shatter.

It is widely known from the learning theory literature that if a real-valued function class has bounded pseudo-dimension, then it is PAC-learnable with ERM.

Theorem 3.1

(
Pollard 1984
)
.

Consider a real-valued function class
\mathcal{F}
, of which each function
\mathcal{L}
takes input
\bm{\pi}
in
\Pi
and output
\ell(\bm{\pi})\in[-H,0]
. Assume that
\text{Pdim}(\mathcal{L})
is finite. Then given
\epsilon>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, for any
M\geq m(\delta,\epsilon)
, where
m(\delta,\epsilon)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{H^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}(\text{Pdim}(\mathcal{L})+\log(1/\delta))\right)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over the draw of
S=(\bm{\pi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\pi}_{M})\sim\mathcal{D}^{M}
, where
\mathcal{D}
is a distribution over
\Pi
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\hat{\ell}_{S}(\bm{\pi})]\leq\inf_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(\bm{\pi})]+\epsilon.

Here
\hat{\ell}_{S}\in\arg\min_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\ell(\bm{\pi}_{i})
is the ERM minimizer.

3.2
Goldberg-Jerrum Framework

Goldberg-Jerrum (GJ) framework, originally proposed by
Goldberg and Jerrum 1993
with a refined version instantiated by
Bartlett et al. 2022
, is a convenient framework for establishing pseudo-dimension upper-bound for parameterized function classes, of which the computation can be described by a
GJ algorithm
using conditional statements, intermediate values, and outputs involving rational functions of their parameters. The formal definition of the GJ algorithm can be described as follows.

Definition 2

(GJ algorithm,
Bartlett et al. 2022
)
.

A
GJ algorithm

\Gamma
operates on real-valued inputs, and can perform two types of operations:

•

Arithmetic operators of the form
v^{\prime\prime}=v\odot v^{\prime}
, where
\odot\in\{+,-,\times,\div\}
, and

•

Conditional statements of the form
``\text{if }v\geq 0\dots\text{ else }\dots"
.

In both cases,
v
and
v^{\prime}
are either inputs or values previously computed by the algorithm.

The immediate values
v,v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime}
computed by the GJ algorithm are rational functions (fractions of two polynomials) of its parameters. The complexities of the GJ algorithm are measured by the highest degree of rational functions it computes and the number of distinct rational functions that appear in the conditional statements. The formal definition of its complexities is as follows.

Definition 3

(Complexities of GJ algorithm,
Bartlett et al. 2022
)
.

The
degree
of a GJ algorithm is the maximum degree of any rational function that it computes of the inputs. The
predicate complexity
of a GJ algorithm is the number of distinct rational functions that appear in its conditional statements. Here, the degree of rational function
f(\bm{x})=\frac{g(\bm{x})}{h(\bm{x})}
, where
g
and
h
are two polynomials in
\bm{x}
, is
\deg(f)=\max\{\deg(g),\deg(h)\}
.

The following theorem asserts that if any function class of which the function’s computation can be described by a GJ algorithm with bounded degree and predicate complexities, then the pseudo-dimension of that function class is also bounded.

Theorem 3.2

(
Bartlett et al. 2022
)
.

Suppose that each function
\ell_{\bm{P}}\in\mathcal{L}
is specified by
n
real parameters
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose that for every
\bm{\pi}\in\Pi
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
, there is a GJ algorithm
\Gamma_{\bm{\pi},r}
that, given
\ell_{\bm{P}}\in\mathcal{L}
, returns "true" if
\ell_{\bm{P}}(\bm{\pi})\geq r
and “false" otherwise. Assume that
\Gamma_{\bm{\pi},r}
has degree
\Delta
and predicate complexity
\Lambda
. Then,
\text{Pdim}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{O}(n\log(\Delta\Lambda))
.

Note that the GJ algorithm
\Gamma_{\bm{\pi},r}
described above corresponds to each fixed input
\bm{\pi}
and threshold value
r
. The input of the GJ algorithm
\Gamma_{\bm{\pi},r}
is the hyperparameters
\bm{P}
(the projection matrix in our case) parameterizing
\ell_{\bm{P}}
, and the intermediate values and conditional statements involve in rational functions of
\bm{P}
. Moreover, the GJ framework only serves as a tool for analyzing
learning-theoretic complexity
(e.g., pseudo-dimension) of the parameterized function class
\mathcal{L}
, and does not describe how the function
\ell_{\bm{P}}(\bm{\pi})
is computed in practice. In our framework, the computation of
\ell_{\bm{P}}(\pi)
utilizes our proposed
unrolled active set method
in Algorithm
1
, which we show to be a GJ algorithm with bounded complexities in Lemma
5.5
. In practice, it might be computed using the
active set method

(
Nocedal and Wright 2006
)
or interior-point method
(
Dikin 1967
)
for computational efficiency; however, these methods cannot be cast as GJ algorithms.

Problem Settings

This section formalizes the problem of learning the projection matrix for QPs in the data-driven setting.

4.1
Original QPs and Projected QPs

Consider the
original QPs
(OQPs)
\bm{\pi}=(\bm{Q},\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})\in\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
with inequality constraints:

\textup{OPT}(\bm{\pi})=\min_{\bm{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{x}+\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}\mid\bm{Ax\leq b}\right\},

where
\bm{Q}
is a positive semi-definite (PSD) matrix, while
n
and
m
are the number of variables and constraints, respectively.
Here, we suppose that the variable size
n
and number of constraints
m
are large, and solving the OQP is a computationally expensive task. The core idea of the projection method evolves around a
full column-rank
projection matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
, where
k\ll n
is the projection dimension. By setting
\bm{x}=\bm{Py}
, we obtain the
projected QPs
(PQPs) corresponding to the OQPs
\bm{\pi}
and the projection matrix
\bm{P}

\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})=\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{P}\bm{y}+\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{Py}\mid\bm{APy}\leq\bm{b}\right\}.

Similar to prior works
(
Sakaue and Oki 2023
;
Vu et al. 2019
)
, we make the following assumptions for the OQPs.

Assumption 1

(Regularity conditions)
.

The OQPs:

(1)

take inequality-constrained form as (
4.1
),

(2)

have
\bm{0}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a feasible point,

(3)

have the feasible region
is bounded by
R
, and

(4)

have bounded optimal objective value from
[-H,0]
, for some constant positive
H
.

Remark 1
.

As discussed in prior works
(
Sakaue and Oki 2024
;
Vu et al. 2019
)
, Assumption
1
is not that restrictive. First, any QPs that also have equality assumptions can also be converted into the inequality form (Assumption
1
.1 by considering the null space of the equality constraints (see Appendix C,
Sakaue and Oki 2024
for details). For Assumption
1
.2, one can instead assume that there exists a feasible point
\bm{x}_{0}
, and linearly translate the feasible region so that
\bm{x}_{0}
coincides with
\bm{0}_{n}
, without changing the form of QPs. Assumption
1
.3 is standard from the optimization literature
(
Vu et al. 2019
)
, and Assumption
1
.4 is simply a consequence of Assumption
1
.1 and
1
.2.

Under Assumption
1
, the PQPs also have a favorable structure, which can be formalized as follows.

Proposition 4.1
.

Under Assumption
1
, then for any OQP
\bm{\pi}
and projection matrix
\bm{P}
, the corresponding PQP: (1) has
\bm{0}_{n}
as a feasible point, and (2)
\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
is less bounded by
\textup{OPT}(\bm{\pi})
, and therefore takes a value between
[-H,0]
.

Proof.

Since
\bm{0}_{n}
is a feasible point of OQP
\bm{\pi}
,
\bm{y}=\bm{0}_{k}
satisfies
\bm{AP}\bm{y}\leq\bm{b}
, meaning that
\bm{0}_{k}
is a feasible point of PQP. Moreover, let
\bm{y}^{*}
be an optimal solution of PQP, then
\bm{x}^{\prime}=\bm{P}\bm{y}^{*}
is a feasible point of OQP, thus
\textup{OPT}(\bm{\pi})\leq\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
.
∎

4.2
Data-driven Learning of the Projection Matrix

In the data-driven setting, we assume that there is an application-specific and potentially unknown problem distribution
\mathcal{D}
over the set of QPs
\Pi
. The optimal projection matrix
\bm{P}
is the one that minimize the population PQPs’ optimal objective value

\bm{P}^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\in\argmin_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})].

From Proposition
4.1
, we know that the smaller
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})]
, the closer the PQP optimal objective value
\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
to
\textup{OPT}(\bm{\pi})
, and the better
\bm{P}
is. However, since
\mathcal{D}
is unknown, we instead learn
\bm{P}
using the observed PQPs
S=\{\bm{\pi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\pi}_{N}\}
drawn i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
via ERM

\hat{\bm{P}}_{S}\in\argmin_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{i}).

Object of study.

We aim to answer the standard generalization guarantee question: given a tolerance
\epsilon>0
and a failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
, what would be the sample complexity
M(\epsilon,\delta)
such that w.p. at least
1-\delta
over the draw of problem instances
S=\{\bm{\pi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\pi})_{N}\}
, where
N\geq M(\epsilon,\delta)
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(\hat{\bm{P}}_{S},\bm{\pi})]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(\bm{P}^{*}_{\mathcal{D}},\bm{\pi})]+\epsilon.
Consider the function class
\mathcal{L}=\{\ell_{\bm{P}}:\Pi\rightarrow[-H,0]\mid\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}\}
, Theorem
3.1
suggests that the generalization guarantee question above can be answered by bounding the pseudo-dimension of
\mathcal{L}
, where
\bm{\ell}_{\bm{P}}(\bm{\pi})=\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
.

5
Generalization Guarantee for Data-driven Input-agnostic Projection Method for QPs

In this section, we will provide the generalization guarantee for data-driven learning of the projection matrix
\bm{P}
for QPs.

5.1
Regularizing via Perturbing OQPs and the Perturbed Function Class

The main obstacle to analyzing the generalization guarantee for data-driven learning the projection matrix in QPs is that the optimal solution of QPs can lie arbitrarily anywhere in the feasible polyhedra. Moreover, when the matrix
\bm{Q}
is singular, there can be infinitely many optimal solutions. To address this issue, we first introduce the perturbed function class
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
, constructed by adding Tikhonov’s regularization to the input OQPs
\bm{\pi}
. After the perturbation: (1) the objective function of perturbed OQPs
\pi_{\gamma}
and any perturbed PQPs becomes strictly convex, which favorably helps us localize the unique optimal solution and constructing the unrolled active set method; and (2) the perturbed function class
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
can approximate
\mathcal{L}
with arbitrary precision, and therefore analyzing the
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
can recover the guarantee for
\mathcal{L}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Given a OQP
\bm{\pi}=(\bm{Q},\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})
, then there exists
\gamma
(that is independent on
\bm{P}
) such that for any perturbed OQP
\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}=(\bm{Q}_{\gamma},\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})
, where
\bm{Q}_{\gamma}=\bm{Q}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}
and any projection matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, we have
0\leq\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\gamma})-\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})\leq\frac{\gamma\cdot R^{2}}{2}
, where
R
comes from Assumption
1
.3.

Proof.

Let
\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P})
is an optimal solution of the PQP, that is,
\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P})}^{\top}\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{P}\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P})+\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P})
, and let

1.

f_{\bm{P}}(\bm{y})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{P}\bm{y}+\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{y}
be the objective function of the PQP, and

2.

f_{\bm{P},\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{y})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{P}^{\top}(\bm{Q}+\epsilon\bm{I}_{n})\bm{P}\bm{y}+\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{y}=f_{\bm{P}}(\bm{y})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\bm{Py}\|_{2}^{2}
be the objective function of the perturbed PQP.

Then
f_{\bm{P}}(\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P}))=\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
by definition, and note that
\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P})
is a feasible point of the perturbed PQP
\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}
, meaning that
f_{\bm{P},\gamma}(\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P}))\geq\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\gamma})
. Besides, PQP and perturbed PQP have the same feasible region, with the objective of PQP
f_{\bm{P}}(\bm{y})
is smaller than that of perturbed PQP
f_{\bm{P},\gamma}(\bm{y})
, meaning that
\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\gamma})\geq\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
. Combining the facts above, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\gamma})-\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})\leq f_{\bm{P},\bm{\pi}}(\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P}))-f_{\bm{P}}(\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\bm{P}\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\gamma\cdot R^{2}}{2},

where the final inequality comes from the fact that
\bm{P}\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P})
is a feasible point of OQP, and the feasible region is bounded by
R
by Assumption

.3. ∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Any perturbed PQP corresponding to a perturbed OQP
\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}
with a projection matrix
\bm{P}
has a unique optimal solution.

Proof.

First, notice that the matrix
\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}_{\gamma}\bm{P}
is positive definite. To see that, for any
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\bm{y}\neq\bm{0}
, we have
\bm{P}\bm{y}\neq\bm{0}_{n}
since
\bm{P}
is a full-column rank matrix. Therefore
\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{Q}_{\gamma}\bm{P}\bm{y}=(\bm{P}\bm{y})^{\top}\bm{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{P}\bm{y})>0
as
\bm{Q}_{\gamma}=\bm{Q}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}
is a positive definite matrix. This implies that the objective value of the perturbed PQP
\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}
is also strictly convex. Moreover, the perturbed PQP is feasible (admitting
\bm{0}_{k}
as a feasible point) and bounded as
-H\leq\textup{OPT}(\bm{\pi})\leq\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})\leq\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\gamma})
. Therefore, the perturbed PQP
\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}
has a unique optimal solution.
∎

We now temporarily change the object of study to analyzing the pseudo-dimension of

5.2
Localizing the Solution of Perturbed PQPs

We now formalize the following result, which essentially says that the solution of the perturbed PQP can be described using a simpler equality-constrained QP, of which the constraint matrix
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{B}}
, extracted from the constraint matrix
\tilde{\bm{A}}
of the perturbed PQP, has linearly independent rows. This serves as a localizing scheme for the optimal solution of a perturbed PQP, and is the foundation for the unrolled active set method (Algorithm
1
) that we describe later.

Lemma 5.3
.

Consider the perturbed PQP corresponding to a projection matrix
\bm{P}
and the perturbed OQP
\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}=(\bm{Q}_{\gamma},\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})
, and for convenience, let
{\bm{Q}}=\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}_{\gamma}\bm{P}
,
\tilde{\bm{c}}=\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{c}
, and
\tilde{\bm{A}}=\bm{AP}
. Let
\bm{y}^{*}
be the (unique) optimal solution of perturbed PQP with the corresponding active set
\mathcal{A}(\bm{y^{*}})=\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\mid\tilde{\bm{A}}_{i}\bm{y}^{*}=\bm{b}_{i}\}
. Then there exists a subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}(\bm{y}^{*})
such that:

1.

The matrix
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{B}}
has linearly independent row. Here
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{B}}
is the matrix formed by the row
i^{th}
row of
\bm{A}
for
i\in\mathcal{B}
.

2.

\bm{y}^{*}
is the unique solution for the equality-constrained QP
\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{y}+\tilde{\bm{c}}^{\top}\bm{y}\mid\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{B}}\bm{y}=\bm{b}_{\mathcal{B}}\right\}
.

Proof sketch.

The detailed proof can be found in Appendix
B
. Using KKT conditions, we claim that
-(\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{y}^{*}+\tilde{\bm{c}})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}(\bm{y}^{*})}\bm{\lambda}^{*}_{i}\cdot\tilde{\bm{A}}_{i}
. Note that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}(\bm{y}^{*})}\bm{\lambda}^{*}_{i}\cdot\tilde{\bm{A}}_{i}
is a conic combination, and using Conic’s Carathéodory theorem (Proposition
B.3
), we claim that there exists a subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}(\bm{y}^{*})
such that there exists
\bm{\mu}_{i}\geq 0
for
i\in\mathcal{B}
such that

-(\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{y}^{*}+\tilde{\bm{c}})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}}\bm{\mu}_{j}\cdot\bm{A}_{j}\Leftrightarrow\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{y}^{*}+\tilde{\bm{c}}+\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{B}}^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{\mathcal{B}}=\bm{0},

and
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{B}}
has linearly independent rows. Finally, we will show that
\bm{y}^{*}
is the unique solution of the equality-constrained QP
\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{y}+\tilde{\bm{c}}^{\top}\bm{y}\mid\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{B}}\bm{y}=\bm{b}_{\mathcal{B}}\right\}
by showing that
(\bm{y}^{*},\bm{\mu}_{\mathcal{B}})
is a KKT point of that problem.
∎

Remark 2
.

In Lemma
5.3
, since
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{B}}
has linearly independent row and
\tilde{\bm{Q}}
is positive definite, one can easily show that the KKT matrix
\bm{K}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{Q}}&\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\\
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{A}}&\bm{0}.\end{bmatrix}
corresponding to the equality-constrained QP (Equation
2
) is invertible. Therefore, there exists
\bm{\mu}_{\mathcal{B}}
such that
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}^{*}\\
\bm{\mu}^{*}_{\mathcal{B}}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{K}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}-\tilde{\bm{c}}\\
\bm{b}_{\mathcal{A}}\end{bmatrix}
. This point is very helpful in designing the unrolled active set method for computing
\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\gamma})
as follows.

5.3
The Unrolled Active Set Method

Using the observations from Lemma
5.3
and Remark
2
, we now introduce the
unrolled active set method
, which we show to be a GJ algorithm with bounded complexities, and exactly compute the optimal objective value of the perturbed PQP
\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{\gamma})
corresponding to the perturbed OQP
\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}
and the projection matrix
\bm{P}
.

5.3.1
Intuition.

The details of the unrolled active set method are demonstrated in Algorithm
1
. Here, the algorithm is defined for each perturbed OQP
\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}
and takes the projection matrix
\bm{P}
as the input. The general idea is to check all the potential active subsets
\mathcal{A}
of rows of
\tilde{\bm{A}}
up to
\min\{m,k\}
elements. If we find
\mathcal{A}
such that KKT matrix
\bm{K}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{Q}}&\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\\
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{A}}&\bm{0}.\end{bmatrix}
is invertible, then we can use it to calculate the potential optimal solution
\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}}
and Lagrangian
\bm{\lambda_{\textup{cand}}}
. We then check if
(\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}},\bm{\lambda}_{\textup{cand}})
is a KKT point of the perturbed PQP corresponding to the perturbed OQP
\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}
and the projection matrix
\bm{P}
. If yes, then
\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}}
is the optimal solution for the perturbed PQP, and we output the optimal objective value; else we move on to the next potential active subset
\mathcal{A}
.

In this section, we demonstrate that the unrolled active set method indeed yields the optimal solution for the perturbed PQP. Then, we will show that the algorithm is also a GJ algorithm, and we will bound its predicate complexity and degree.

Extension to Other Settings

6.1
Learning to Match the Optimal Solution

In many practical cases, it is not the optimal objective value but the optimal solution that we want to recover. In this case, one wants to learn the projection matrix
\bm{P}
such that the optimal solution of the PQP is mapped back to the OQP, with the hope that it is as close to the optimal solution of the OQP as possible. Such a solution can then be used to warm-start an exact solver and accelerate the solving process. In this section, we propose an alternative objective value for learning
\bm{P}
in such a scenario. First, we further assume the strict convexity of the problem instance, so that the optimal solution of the OQP is well-defined (unique).

Assumption 2
.

For any
\bm{\pi}=(\bm{Q},\bm{c},\bm{A}.\bm{b})\in\Pi
, the matrix
\bm{Q}
is positive definite.

Under such assumption, we seek the projection matrix
\bm{P}
such that the recovered solution is close to the optimal solution of the PQP in expectation, i.e.,

\bm{P}^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\in\argmin_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell_{\textup{match}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})],

where

\ell_{\textup{match}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})=\|\bm{x}^{*}_{\bm{\pi}}-\bm{P}\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})\|_{2}^{2}

is the matching loss,

\bm{x}^{*}_{\bm{\pi}}=\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{x}+\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}\mid\bm{Ax}\leq\bm{b}\right\}
is the optimal solution of the OQP, and
\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})=\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{P}\bm{y}+\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{y}\mid\bm{AP}\bm{y}\leq\bm{b}\right\}
is the optimal solution of the PQP. Again, since
\mathcal{D}
is unknown, we are instead given
N
problem instances
S=\{\bm{\pi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\pi}_{N}\}
drawn i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
, and learn
\bm{P}
via ERM

\hat{\bm{P}}_{S}\in\argmin_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{\textup{match}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}_{i}).

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{match}}=\{\ell_{\textup{match},\bm{P}}:\Pi\rightarrow[-H,0]\}\mid\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}\}
, where
\ell_{\textup{match},\bm{P}}(\bm{\pi}):=\ell_{\textup{match}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
. The following result provides the upper-bound for the pseudo-dimension of
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{match}}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Assuming that all the QPs satisfies Assumption
2
so that
\bm{x}^{*}_{\bm{\pi}}
is defined uniquely. Then
\text{Pdim}(\mathcal{L}_{\textup{match}})=\mathcal{O}(nk\min(m,k\log m)).

Proof sketch.
The detailed proof is presented in Appendix
C
. Given
\bm{\pi}
, the general idea is using a variant of the unrolled active set method (Algorithm
1
) to calculate the optimal solution
\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
. Then
\ell_{\textup{match}}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
can also be calculated with a GJ algorithm with bounded predicate complexity and degree, based on the GJ algorithm calculating
\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
. Finally, Theorem
3.2
gives us the final guarantee.
∎

6.2
Input-aware Learning of Projection Matrix

In this section, we consider the setting of input-aware data-driven learning the projection matrix for QPs, recently proposed by
Iwata and Sakaue 2025
in the context of LPs. Here, instead of learning a single projection matrix
\bm{P}
, we learn a mapping
f_{\bm{\theta}}:\Pi\rightarrow\mathcal{P}
, e.g., a neural network, that takes a problem instance
\bm{\pi}
drawn from
\mathcal{D}
and output the corresponding projection matrix
\bm{P}_{\bm{\pi}}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi})
. With some computational tradeoff for generating the projection matrix, this method has shown promising results, generating a better, input-aware projection matrix that achieves better performance than an input-agnostic projection matrix while using the same projection dimension
k
.

6.2.1
Network architecture.

Inspired by
Iwata and Sakaue 2025
, we assume that
f_{\bm{\theta}}
is a neural network parameterized by
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{W}
, where
W
is the number parameters of the neural network, that takes the input
\bm{\pi}_{\textup{flat}}
of size
n^{2}+n+nm+m
which is formed by flattening
\bm{Q},\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b}
in
\bm{\pi}
. Let
L
be the number of hidden layers, and let
f_{\bm{\theta}}
is the network of
L+2
layers, with the number of neurons of input layer is
W_{0}=m^{2}+n+mn+m
, that of the output layer is
W_{L+2}=nk
, and that of
i^{th}
layer is
W_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,L\}
. Each hidden layer uses ReLU as the non-linear activation function, and let
U=\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}
be the number of hidden neurons. Consider the function class
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{ia}}=\{\ell_{\bm{\theta}}:\Pi\rightarrow[-H,0]\mid\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\}
, where

\ell_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}):=\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{\textup{flat}}),\bm{\pi}).

Then we have the following result, which bounds the pseudo-dimension of
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{ia}}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Assume that the output
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi})
has full column rank, then
\text{Pdim}(\mathcal{L}_{\textup{ia},\gamma})=\mathcal{O}(W(L\log(U+mk)+\min(m,k\log m)))
.

Proof sketch
.
The detailed proof is deferred to Appendix
C
. The main idea is first to bound the pseudo-dimension of the surrogate function class

\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}

\ell_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi})

To establish the pseudo-dimension upper-bound for

\Theta

\bm{\theta}
into bonded connected components, and in each component,

\bm{\theta}
, for any

. We then use Lemma

∎

7
Conclusion and Future Works

We introduced the task of data-driven learning of a projection matrix for convex QPs. By a novel analysis approach, we establish the first upper bound on the pseudo-dimension of the learning projection matrix in QPs. Compared to the previous bound by
Sakaue and Oki 2024
, our new result is more general because it applies to both QPs and LPs and is strictly tighter. We further extend our analysis to learning to match the optimal solution and the input-aware setting. Our analysis opens many interesting directions. First, a natural question is to extend the framework to a more general case, including conic programming and semi-definite programming. Secondly, the current framework is only applicable to continuous optimization, and extending the framework to (mixed) integer programming remains a critical question.

In this section, we will present the formal proof for Theorem

Using the fact above, we will slightly modify the unrolled active set method (Algorithm
1

\bm{P}
and output the recovered solution
\bm{P}\bm{y}^{*}

\bm{P}

\bm{\pi}

We first show that Algorithm
2
correctly output the optimal solution for the PQP corresponds to the OQP
\bm{\pi}
and the projection matrix
\bm{P}
.

Proposition C.1
.

Given a OQP
\bm{\pi}
, the algorithm
\Gamma_{\bm{\pi}}
, described by Algorithm
2
and corresponding to
\bm{\pi}
, correctly computes the optimal solution
\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
(i.e.,
\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}}=\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})

\bm{P}\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}}

Proof.

Again, the proof idea is similar to that of Lemma
5.4
. For the
existence
part, from Lemma
5.3
, given the (unique) optimal solution of the PQP
\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
, there exists a subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}(\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi}))
of the active set corresponding to
\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
such that
\tilde{\bm{A}}
has linearly independent rows, and that
\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
is the unique optimal solution of the new equality-constrained QP:

\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{Q})=\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{*}\tilde{\bm{Q}}\bm{y}+\tilde{\bm{c}}^{\top}\bm{y}\mid\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{B}}\bm{y}=\bm{b}_{\mathcal{B}}\right\}.

Note that Algorithm
2
considers all subset
\mathcal{A}\subset\{1,\dots,m\}
that has at most
\left|\mathcal{A}\right|\leq k
elements, it will eventually check
\mathcal{B}
. And when
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
, we can easily show that
(\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}},\bm{\lambda}_{\textup{cand}})
will pass all the primal and dual feasibility checks, meaning that
\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}}=\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
. Moreover, we can easily verify that
(\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}},\bm{\lambda}_{\textup{cand}})
is a KKT point of the equality-constrained QP above, meaning that
\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}}
is also its unique optimal solution.

For the
correctness
part, also similar to Lemma
5.4
, we also show that given the output
(\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}},\bm{\lambda}_{\textup{cand}})
, we can construct the point
(\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}},\bm{\lambda})
, where
\bm{\lambda}_{i}=0
if

and
\bm{\lambda}_{i}=\bm{\lambda}_{\textup{cand},i}
if
i\in\mathcal{A}
, that satisfies the KKT conditions of the PQP. This means that
\bm{y}_{\textup{cand}}
is the optimal solution of the PQP. Besides, it’s easy to check that
(\bm{y},\bm{\lambda})
also satisfies the KKT conditions of the equality-constrained QP corresponding with the same objective of PQP and the constraints
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{y}=\bm{b}_{\mathcal{A}}

\bm{\pi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\pi}_{N}
, the outputs

admits piecewise polynomial structure, with bounded number of pieces.

Proposition C.3
.

Given any set of
N
OQPs
\bm{\pi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\pi}_{N}
, we can partition the space
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{W}
of neural network parameters into connected components
\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{C}\}
, where

C\leq 2^{L+1}\left(\frac{2eN(U+2nk)}{W}\right)^{(L+1)W}.

Given a connected component
\mathcal{C}_{i}
, the projection matrix
\bm{P}_{\bm{\pi}_{i}}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{i,\textup{flat}})
, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}

\bm{\theta}
) of degree at most

.

Proof.

The result is a direct consequence of the result by
Anthony and Bartlett 2009
, later adapted to the context of data-driven algorithm selection by
Cheng et al. 2024
(see e.g., Theorem 2.6). We simply adapt the results in the context of input-aware data-driven learning, the projection matrix, where the output of the neural network is
nk
, which is the size of the projection matrix.
∎

To give a proof for Theorem
6.2
, the strategy is to consider the surrogate function class
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{ia},\gamma}=\{\ell_{\bm{\theta},\gamma}:\Pi\rightarrow[-H,0]\mid\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\}
, where
\ell_{\bm{\theta},\gamma}(\bm{\pi})=\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{\textup{flat}}),\bm{\pi}_{\gamma})
and
\bm{\pi}_{\gamma}=(\bm{Q}+\gamma\cdot\bm{I},\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})
is the perturbed OQP. After establishing the pseudo-dimension upper-bound for
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{ia},\gamma}\}
, we use Proposition
A.1
to recover the learning guarantee for
\mathcal{L}_{\textup{ia}}
.

Lemma C.4
.

Assume that the output
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi})
has full column rank, then
\text{Pdim}(\mathcal{L}_{\textup{ia},\gamma})=\mathcal{O}(W(L\log(U+mk)+\min(m,k\log m)))
.

Proof.

Given
N
OQPs
\bm{\pi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\pi}_{N}
and
N
real-valued thresholds
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{N}
, we first need to bound the number of sign pattern

\{{\text{sign}}(\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{1,\textup{flat}}),\bm{\pi}_{1,\gamma})-\tau_{1}),\dots,{\text{sign}}(\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{N,\textup{flat}}),\bm{\pi}_{N,\gamma})-\tau_{N})\mid\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\}

when varying
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
. Here,
\bm{\pi}_{i,\gamma}
and
\bm{\pi}_{i,\textup{flat}}
are the perturbed PQP and a flattened vector of problem instance
\bm{\pi}_{i}
, respectively.

From Proposition
C.3
, the parameter space
\Theta
can be partitioned into connected components
\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{C}\}
, where

C\leq 2^{L+1}\left(\frac{2eN(U+2nk)}{W}\right)^{(L+1)W},

And in each connected component
\bm{\mathcal{C}}\subset\Theta
, the projection matrix
\bm{P}_{\bm{\pi}_{i}}=f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{i,\textup{flat}})
, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, is a matrix with polynomials entries (in the neural network parameters
\bm{\theta}
) of degree at most
L+2
. Now, in each connected components
\mathcal{C}
, from Lemma
5.5
,
{\text{sign}}(\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{i,\textup{flat}}),\bm{\pi}_{i,\gamma})-\tau_{1})

\{{\text{sign}}(\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{1,\textup{flat}})-\tau_{1}),\dots,{\text{sign}}(\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{N,\textup{flat}})-\tau_{N})\mid\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\}

\{{\text{sign}}(\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{1,\textup{flat}}),\bm{\pi}_{1,\gamma})-\tau_{1}),\dots,{\text{sign}}(\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\pi}_{N,\textup{flat}}),\bm{\pi}_{N,\gamma})-\tau_{N})\mid\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\}

Appendix D
Gradient update for data-driven learning the projection matrix for QPs

In this section, we will formalize derive the gradient update for learning the projection matrix for QPs in the data-driven framework. Recall that given a problem instance
\bm{\pi}=(\bm{Q},\bm{c},\bm{A},\bm{b})
and a projection matrix
\bm{P}
, we have

\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi)}=\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{P}\bm{y}+\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{y}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\bm{APy}\leq\bm{b}.

To calculate
\nabla_{\bm{P}}\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})
, we first recall the Envelope theorem.

Lemma D.1

(Envelope theorem,
Milgrom and Segal 2002
)
.

Let
f(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha})
and
g_{j}(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha)}
, where
j=1,\dots,m
be real-valued continuously differentiable function, where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and variables, and
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
are parameters, and consider the parametric optimization problem

\ell(\bm{\alpha})=\min_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha})\quad\textup{subject to}\quad g_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha)}\leq 0,i=1,\dots,m.

Let
\mathcal{L}(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\lambda)}
be the corresponding Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\lambda})=f(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha}),

where
\bm{\lambda}
is the Lagrangian multiplier. Let
\bm{x}^{*}(\alpha)
,
\bm{\lambda}^{*}(\alpha)
be the solution that minimizes the objective subject to the constraints, and let
\mathcal{L}^{*}(\bm{\alpha})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\alpha}),\bm{\alpha},\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\alpha)})
.
Assume that
\ell(\bm{\alpha})
and
\mathcal{L}^{*}(\bm{\alpha})
are continuously differentiable, then

\nabla_{\bm{\alpha}}\ell(\bm{\alpha)}=\nabla_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{x},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\lambda})|_{\bm{x}=\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\alpha}),\bm{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{\alpha})}.

Assuming that the regularity condition holds, using Lemma
D.1
, we have

\nabla_{\bm{P}}\ell(\bm{P},\bm{\pi})=(\bm{QP}\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P})+\bm{c}+\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{P}))\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{P})^{\top}.
