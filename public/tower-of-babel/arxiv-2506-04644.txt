Gordian split links in the Gehring ropelength problem
Friedrich Bauermeister

Abstract
A thick link is a link in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} such that each component of the link lies at distance at least 1 1 from every other component. Strengthening the notion of thickness, we define a thickly embedded link to be a thick link whose open radius- 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} normal disk bundles of all components are embedded. The Gehring ropelength problem asks how large the sum of the lengths of the components of a thick (respectively thickly embedded) link must be, given the link homotopy (respectively isotopy) class of the link. A thick homotopy (isotopy) is a link homotopy (isotopy) of a thick (thickly embedded) link that preserves thickness throughout, and such that during the homotopy the total length of the link never exceeds the initial total length. These notions of thick homotopy and isotopy are more permissive than other notions of physical link isotopies in which the length of each individual component must remain constant (no “length trading”). We construct an explicit example of a thickly embedded 4-component link which is topologically split but cannot be split by a thick homotopy, and thick links in every homotopy class with 2 components that are non-global local minima for ropelength. This is the first time such local minima for ropelength have been explicitly constructed. In particular, we construct a thick 2-component link in the link homotopy class of the unlink which cannot be split through a thick homotopy.

Introduction

In 2015, Coward and Hass
[
5
]
defined the notion of a physical link isotopy. A physical link isotopy is a link isotopy that keeps a link thickly embedded throughout, as well as keeping the length of each component constant. This made it possible to study a physical knot- and link theory that corresponds to knots and links tied from real pieces of rope. In the same paper, Coward and Hass construct a
2
-component
Gordian split link
which they define as a thickly embedded, topologically split link that cannot be split by a physical link isotopy. More generally, a
Gordian pair of links
is a set of two links in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which are "topologically equivalent", but which are not equivalent under "physical deformations". Here "topologically equivalent" refers either to being in the same link isotopy or homotopy class, and "physical deformation" refers to either link homotopy or link isotopy, subject to additional geometric constraints on length and thickness. For length, there are two common constraints: either demanding that the length of each component individually does not increase throughout the homotopy/isotopy, or that their sum does not increase throughout the homotopy/isotopy. If only the sum of lengths is constrained, we say that "length trading" between components is permitted. The thickness constraint usually demands that links stay thick links (see Definition
3.3
) or thickly embedded links (see Definition
3.4
) throughout the homotopy or isotopy respectively. When considering link isotopy, sometimes additional curvature constraints are imposed.

The original Gehring ropelength problem asked for the minimal length of a closed curve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, given that it is linked to another curve from which it keeps at least unit distance. The answer was quickly shown to be
2\pi
, realized by two Hopf-linked unit circles. Given some link homotopy class, the general Gehring ropelength problem
[
3
]
asks for the minimal ropelength of a thick link in that homotopy class. A link realizing this minimal length is called an
ideal link
of the homotopy class. While the precise length of ideal links is known only in a few cases, there are various upper and lower bounds. Cantarella, Fu, Kusner, Sullivan, and Wrinkle
[
3
]
defined a notion of criticality for the general Gehring ropelength problem, which is a necessary but not sufficient criterion for being an ideal link.

There are good reasons to study thick homotopies even though they are the least physical of the "physical deformations". First, once it is proven that a pair of "topologically equivalent" links does not have a thick homotopy between them, the pair of links will a fortiori be a Gordian pair in all other reasonable notions of physicality. Secondly, the lack of thick homotopies between two links in the same link homotopy class is related to local minima in the general Gehring ropelength problem: Two links in a given link homotopy class, one minimizing ropelength globally and one only locally, should not have a thick homotopy between them. This will be the strategy we use to construct Gordian pairs in every link homotopy class of two components.

If

\mathrm{len}(L)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{len}(C_{i}).

Let

3.4 Definition
.

We say that
L
is

is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and if the open radius-
\tfrac{d}{2}
normal disk bundle around

\tau_{e}(L)
as the largest such

3.5 Definition
.

Let
H:[0,1]\times\left(S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup S_{n}\right)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a link homotopy (isotopy). We call

a
d
-thick homotopy (isotopy)
if for all

:

1.

L_{t}=H(t,\cdot)
has thickness

(is
d
-thickly embedded),

2.

\mathrm{len}(L_{t})\leq\mathrm{len}(L_{0})
.

If

a

3.6 Definition
.

For thick links
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
we define

\displaystyle L_{1}\preceq L_{2}

\displaystyle\iff\text{ There is a thick link homotopy from }L_{2}\text{ to }L_{1}.

\displaystyle L_{1}\simeq L_{2}

\displaystyle\iff L_{1}\preceq L_{2}\text{ and }L_{2}\preceq L_{1}

For thickly embedded links

and

\displaystyle L_{1}\preceq_{e}L_{2}

\displaystyle\iff\text{ There is a thick link isotopy from }L_{2}\text{ to }L_{1}.

\displaystyle L_{1}\simeq_{e}L_{2}

\displaystyle\iff L_{1}\preceq_{e}L_{2}\text{ and }L_{2}\preceq_{e}L_{1}.

If

or

we say that

is

from

.

3.7 Proposition
.

If
L_{1}\preceq L_{2}
, then

\mathrm{len}(L_{1})=\mathrm{len}(L_{2})\iff L_{2}\preceq L_{1}\iff L_{1}\simeq L_{2},

and the same for
\preceq_{e}
.

Proof.

Only the implication
\mathrm{len}(L_{1})=\mathrm{len}(L_{2})\implies L_{2}\preceq L_{1}
is interesting. Let
H_{t}
be the thick homotopy (isotopy) from
L_{1}
to
L_{2}
, then
H_{1-t}
is a thick homotopy (isotopy) from
L_{1}
to
L_{2}
.
∎

3.8 Definition
.

A pair of thick (thickly embedded) links
(L_{1},L_{2})
is called
Gordian as thick links (as thickly embedded links)
if
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are in the same link homotopy (isotopy) class, but neither is reachable from the other.

We will refer to a pair that is Gordian as thick links as
homotopically Gordian
, and to a pair that is Gordian as thickly embedded link as
isotopically Gordian
. When it is clear from context if we are working in the setting of thick links or thickly embedded links, we will simply call such a pair of links a
Gordian pair
.

3.9 Definition
.

A thick (thickly embedded) link
L=\bigcup_{i\in I}C_{i}
is called
separated
if there is some
\emptyset\subsetneq J\subsetneq I
such that

\mathrm{conv}\left(\bigcup_{i\in J}C_{i}\right)\cap\mathrm{conv}\left(\bigcup_{i\in I\setminus J}C_{i}\right)=\emptyset.

A thick (thickly embedded) link
L
is called
split as a thick (thickly embedded) link
if there is a thick (thickly embedded) link homotopy (isotopy) from
L
to some separated link
L^{\prime}
.

3.10 Definition
.

A thick (thickly embedded) link
L
is called a homotopically (isotopically)
Gordian split link
if
L
is topologically split through a link homotopy (isotopy), but not split as a thick (thickly embedded) link.

It is easy to see that every Gordian split link
L
is part of a Gordian pair. If we can write
L=L_{1}\cup L_{2}
where
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are not topologically linked with each other, define
L^{\prime}=L_{1}\cup T(L_{2})
for some translation
T
sufficiently far away from
L_{1}
. Clearly
L
and
L^{\prime}
are in the same link homotopy (isotopy) class. But since
L
was a Gordian split link, we have
L^{\prime}\npreceq L
. Since
\mathrm{len}(L)=\mathrm{len}(L^{\prime})
, we have
L\npreceq L^{\prime}
. Hence
(L,L^{\prime})
is a Gordian pair.

3.11 Remark
.

For the link homotopy class with one component, we have

L_{1}\preceq L_{2}\iff\mathrm{len}(L_{1})\leq\mathrm{len}(L_{2}).

Therefore, no Gordian pair can exist in the
1
-component link homotopy class.

3.12 Remark
.

It is not known whether there is a Gordian pair of thickly embedded knots. There is some computational evidence for the existence of a Gordian unknot, i.e., a thickly embedded knot in the isotopy class of the unknot which has no thick isotopy to a circle
[
10
]
.

3.13 Definition
.

We call a thick link
L
a global minimum for ropelength if for every thick link
L^{\prime}
in the same link homotopy class as
L
, we have

\mathrm{len}(L^{\prime})\geq\mathrm{len}(L).

3.14 Definition
.

We call a thick link
L
a local minimum for ropelength if there is some
\varepsilon>0
such that for every thick link
L^{\prime}

||L-L^{\prime}||<\varepsilon\implies\mathrm{len}(L^{\prime})\geq\mathrm{len}(L).

Global and local minima of ropelength are interesting in and of themselves, and much is still unknown about them. For example, up until now, the only local minima proven to exist were also global minima. In
[
3
]
, Cantarella, Fu, Kusner, Sullivan, and Wrinkle use their criterion of ropelength criticality to construct suspected local and global minimizers in different link homotopy classes, for example for the Borromean rings. When studying thick homotopies, there is another interesting kind of minimization.

3.15 Definition

(sink of ropelength)
.

If a thick link
L
is a minimum of the preorder
\preceq
, then we call
L
a
sink
of the ropelength functional.

Sinks are exactly the configurations which cannot be shortened by a thick homotopy. We briefly define the notion of a sink more generally, and see how it relates to the notion of local minima.

3.16 Definition
.

Let
X
be a topological space,
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
a (not necessarily continuous) function. We call
x\in X
a
sink
of
f
if for all continuous
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
with
\gamma(0)=x
, we have

(f\circ\gamma)(t)\leq f(x)\text{ for all t}\implies f\circ\gamma\text{ constant. }

It is easy to see that every global minimum and every strict local minimum is a sink. However, even for "nice" spaces
X
and continuous functions
f
, there are local minima which are not sinks and sinks which are not local minima.

3.17 Example
.

Take
X=\mathbb{R}
and
f(x)=\min(x,0)
. Then
x=1
is a local minimum, but not a sink.

3.18 Example
.

Take
X=\mathbb{R}
and
f(x)=\begin{cases}x\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right),&x\neq 0\\
0,&x=0\end{cases}

Then
x=0
is a sink but not a local minimum.

3.19 Definition
.

Let
X
be a topological space and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that a subset
S\subseteq X
is a strictly locally minimizing set of
f
if
f
is constant on
S
and if for all
x\in S
, there is an open neighborhood
U\subseteq X
of
S
such that

y\in U\implies f(y)\geq f(x)

with equality only if
y\in S
.

3.20 Proposition
.

Let
X
be a topological space,
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
, and let
S
be a strictly locally minimizing set of
f
. If
S\subseteq X
is closed, then every
x\in S
is a sink of
f
.

Proof.

Let
c=f(x)
, let
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
with
\gamma(0)=x
and with
f(\gamma(t))\leq c
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Let
J=\gamma^{-1}(S)
. Clearly
0\in J
, so
J\neq\emptyset
. Further,
J
is the preimage of a closed set under a continuous map, hence closed. Next we show that
J
is open. Let
t\in J
. Since
\gamma(t)\in S
we have
f(\gamma(t))=c
. Further there is a neighborhood
U_{t}
around
t
with

t^{\prime}\in U_{t}\implies c=f(\gamma(t))\leq f(\gamma(t^{\prime}))

and with equality if and only if
\gamma(t^{\prime})\in S
. By assumption
f(\gamma(t^{\prime}))\leq c
. Therefore
f(\gamma(t^{\prime}))=c
and hence
\gamma(t^{\prime})\in S
. This shows that
J
is open. Since
J
is a non-empty, closed and open subset of
[0,1]
we have
J=[0,1]
. So
\gamma([0,1])\subseteq S\subseteq f^{-1}(c)
, hence
f\circ\gamma
is constant.
∎

4
Bounds on ropelength

4.21 Theorem

(Original Gehring Problem)
.

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be two linked closed curves in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\mathrm{dist}(C_{1},C_{2})\geq 1
. Then
\mathrm{len}(C_{1})\geq 2\pi
.

An elegant proof of this theorem was found by Marvin Ortel, and was first published in
[
3
]
. The statement can be strengthened and generalized in various ways. We will give a few of the most useful generalizations and some important consequences.

4.22 Definition
.

Let
C:S^{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a closed curve, and let
p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We define the
p
-cone of

as the disk
K:\left(S^{1}\times[0,1]\right)/_{\sim}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}

(x,y)\sim(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\iff y=0=y^{\prime}
. If
p
is the center of mass of

, we call

the center-of-mass spanning disk of
C
.

4.23 Theorem
.

Let
C
be a closed curve,
p
a point in the convex hull of
C
,
K
the
p
-cone of
C
. Let
r\geq 0
and let

be the vertices of a convex polygon

, such that
\mathrm{dist}(x_{i},C)\geq r
for all

. Then

\mathrm{len}(C)\geq 2r\pi+\mathrm{circumference}(P).

In
[
5
]
, Coward and Hass prove a version of this theorem for the case where

is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve, where

, and where

. Their proof easily generalizes to a proof for the statement with arbitrary

and

. By approximating arbitrary rectifiable curves by

curves, we get our desired result. For

, we get the following version of this theorem.

4.24 Proposition
.

Let
C
be a closed curve,

,
K
the
p
-cone of
C
. Let

and let

be such that
\mathrm{dist}(x,C),\mathrm{dist}(y,C)\geq r
. Then

\mathrm{len}(C)\geq 2\pi r+2||x-y||.

We will use this result for

or for

for small

.

4.25 Proposition
.

Let
C
be a closed curve,
p
a point in the convex hull of
C
,
K
the
p
-cone of
C
. Let

be such that
\mathrm{dist}(A,C)\geq 1

\mathrm{len}(C)\geq 2\pi+2\mathrm{diam}(A).

We can now use these results to give some lower bounds for the lengths of curves in a thick link that are linked to a certain number of other curves. We already know from the original Gehring problem that if
(C_{1},C_{2})
is a thick link with
C_{1}
and

\mathrm{len}(C_{1})\geq 2\pi
. It is natural to ask about the general case: If
(C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{n+1})
is a thick link with

topologically linked to each of
C_{2},\dots,C_{n+1}
, what is the minimal length that

can have? This question was answered by Cantarella, Kusner, and Sullivan in
[

]
. Their formulation was originally only for the case of thickly embedded links, but it holds just as well for thick links.

4.26 Theorem
.

Let
(C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{n+1})
be a thick link such that
C_{1}
is topologically linked to each of
C_{2},\dots,C_{n+1}
. Then

\mathrm{len}(C_{1})\geq 2\pi+Q_{n}
, where

is the length of the shortest closed curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
surrounding

points of at least unit distance to each other. Here
Q_{1}=0
,
Q_{2}=2
,
Q_{3}=3
,
Q_{4}=4
. Asymptotically,
Q_{n}\sim\sqrt{n}

[
4
]
.

If
L=(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})
is a thick link such that each curve

of

is topologically linked to

curves, then
\mathrm{len}(L)\geq 2n\pi+\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{k_{i}}
. If
\mathrm{len}(L)=2n\pi+\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{k_{i}}

[

.

4.27 Proposition
.

Let
C
be a closed curve,
p
a point in the convex hull of
C
,

the

-cone of

. Let

Let

\displaystyle\mathrm{len}(C)\geq

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2\pi(1-\varepsilon)+2||x-y||-4\varepsilon

\displaystyle=

∎

5
An isotopically Gordian split link exists

The construction of our Gordian split link, as well as the basic structure of the proof, has a very similar flavor to that of Coward and Hass
[
5
]
. Both approaches derive a lower bound for the length needed to split a thickly embedded link by assuming that there is a thick homotopy/physical isotopy, tracking the intersections of a center-of-mass spanning disk and a curve throughout that homotopy/isotopy, and finally arguing that there needs to be some point in time where the intersections yield some lower bound through applications of propositions from section
4
. The main difference is in the "book-keeping" of how intersections are tracked. Coward and Hass need to resolve multiple kinds of singularities that might occur during the tracking of intersections. Our arguments use the homotopy invariance of the Brouwer degree and the mountain climbing theorem instead. We start out by stating the main proposition of this chapter, which establishes a lower-bound for the length necessary to split some thick links.

5.1
A lower bound on ropelength needed to split some thick links

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left(\mathrm{len}(C_{t})\right)\geq 2\pi+4

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\mathrm{len}(H_{t})=

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left(\mathrm{len}(M_{t})+\mathrm{len}(A^{1}_{t})+\mathrm{len}(A^{2}_{t})+\mathrm{len}(C_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(2\pi+2\right)+2\pi+2\pi+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\mathrm{len}(C_{t})

\displaystyle 8\pi+6,

and
\mathrm{len}(A^{1}_{t}),\mathrm{len}(A^{2}_{t})\geq 2\pi
by Theorem

The main step omitted in this sketch is how, given a homotopy
H
preserving thickness, we can continuously keep track of the intersection points

and

The first tool we need to use for our book-keeping will be the homotopy invariance of the Brouwer degree of a function around
0
. We will therefore briefly recall the following existence and uniqueness theorem defining the degree. This formulation is taken from
Fixed Point Theory
by Granas and Dugundji
[

.

5.29 Theorem

(Brouwer degree around
0
)
.

Let

\mathcal{M}=\{(U,F)\mid U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ open, bounded },F:\overline{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ continuous},0\notin F(\partial U)\}.

Then there exists a unique function
d:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{Z}
such that

1.

(Normalization) If
0\in U
, then
d(U,\mathrm{id}_{\overline{U}})=1
.

2.

(Additivity) If
F^{-1}(0)\subseteq U_{1}\cup U_{2}\subseteq U
with
U_{1},U_{2}
open and disjoint, then
d(U,F)=d(U_{1},F)+d(U_{2},F)
.

3.

(Homotopy Invariance) If
F_{t}
is a homotopy of functions such that for every
t
we have
0\notin F_{t}(\partial\Omega)
, then the function
t\mapsto d(U,F_{t})
is constant.

We further need the following two facts about the Brouwer degree. If
F:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and if
0
is a regular value of
F
, then

d(U,F)=\sum_{x\in F^{-1}(0)}\mathrm{sgn}(\det(DF(x))).

If
0\notin F(\partial U)
, then
0\notin F(U)\implies d(U,F)=0.

5.30 Lemma
.

Let
\{L_{t}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
and
\{D_{t}:D^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
be homotopies of a curve and an
n
-dimensional disk respectively, such that

1.

L_{0}
and
D_{0}
are
\mathcal{C}^{1}
embeddings,

2.

L_{0}
intersects
D_{0}
exactly one time and such that this intersection is transverse,

3.

L_{1}
and
D_{1}
are disjoint,

then there is some point in time
t\in[0,1]
such that
L_{t}
and
D_{t}|_{\partial D^{n}}
intersect or such that
L_{t}|_{\partial[0,1]}=L_{t}|_{\{0,1\}}
and
D_{t}
intersect.

Proof.

We define

F_{t}:[0,1]\times D^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1},\quad F_{t}(x,y)=D_{t}(y)-L_{t}(x).

Let
D^{n+1}=[0,1]\times D^{n}
. Since
L_{0}
and
D_{0}
intersect exactly once and since this intersection is transverse, we have
d(D^{n+1},F_{0})=\pm 1
. Further, since
L_{1}
and
D_{1}
do not intersect, we have
0\notin F_{1}(D^{n+1})
, hence
d(D^{n+1},F_{1})=0
. Since the Brouwer degree is homotopy invariant, this is only possible if there is some
t\in[0,1]
such that
0\in F_{t}(\partial D^{n+1})
. This proves our claim.
∎

The second tool we will need is the mountain climbing theorem.

5.31 Theorem

(Mountain Climbing Theorem)
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}:[0,1]\to[0,T]
be continuous such that
\gamma_{1}(0)=0=\gamma_{2}(0)
and
\gamma_{1}(1)=T=\gamma_{2}(1)
, and such that
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are piecewise monotone, then there exist continuous functions
\gamma^{\prime}_{1},\gamma^{\prime}_{2}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
fixing endpoints with

\gamma_{1}\circ\gamma^{\prime}_{1}=\gamma_{2}\circ\gamma^{\prime}_{2}.

This result was first stated and proved by Whittaker in 1966
[
11
]
. It has been strengthened since then, for example in 1969 by Huneke
[
7
]
.

5.32 Theorem

(Disk separation lemma)
.

Let
D^{2}
be a disk and let
K,L\subseteq D^{2}
be closed such that
D^{2}=K\cup L
and such that
K\cap S^{1}\times\{0\}=I_{A}\cup I_{B}
and
L\cap S^{1}\times\{0\}=I_{X}\cup I_{Y}
are each the disjoint union of two compact intervals of positive length, with
I_{A}\cap I_{X}
,
I_{B}\cap I_{X}
,
I_{A}\cap I_{Y}
, and
I_{B}\cap I_{Y}
each containing exactly one element. Then either
I_{A}
and
I_{B}
are in the same connected component of
K
or
I_{X}
and
I_{Y}
are in the same connected component of
L
.

Proof.

Up to homeomorphism we can assume
D^{2}=[0,1]^{2}
and that
W\coloneqq\{0\}\times[0,1]=I_{A}
,
E\coloneqq\{1\}\times[0,1]=I_{B}
,
S\coloneqq[0,1]\times\{0\}=I_{X}
,
N\coloneqq[0,1]\times\{1\}=I_{Y}
. Let
K_{1}
be the connected component of
W
in
K
and let
K_{2}=K\setminus K_{1}
. Let
L_{1}
be the connected component of
S
in
L
and let
L_{2}=L\setminus L_{1}
. Then
D^{2}=K_{1}\cup K_{2}\cup L_{1}\cup L_{2}
. Toward a contradiction, assume that
E\subseteq K_{2}
and
N\subseteq L_{2}
. Since
K
and
L
were closed, so are
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
,
L_{1}
, and
L_{2}
. By Urysohn’s Lemma, there are functions
g_{K}:D^{2}\to[0,1]
and
g_{L}:D^{2}\to[0,1]
with
g_{K}(z)=1
for all
z\in K_{1}
,
g_{K}(z)=0
for all
z\in K_{2}
and
g_{L}(z)=1
for all
z\in L_{1}
,
g_{L}(z)=0
for all
z\in L_{2}
. We define the continuous map

f:D^{2}\to D^{2},\quad f(z)=(g_{K}(z),g_{L}(z))

Note that

, hence
f
maps
K_{1}
to
E\subseteq K_{2}
. Similarly

maps
K_{2}
to
W\subseteq K_{1}
and
L_{1}
to
N\subseteq L_{2}
and
L_{2}
to
S\subseteq L_{1}
. Any
z\in D^{2}
must be in at least one of the sets
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
,
L_{1}
, or
L_{2}
, and

will be in a set disjoint to it. This means that

cannot have a fixed point, a contradiction to Brouwer’s fixed point theorem. We conclude that either

and
W
are in the same connected component of
K
, or
S
and
N
are in the same connected component of
L
.
∎

5.33 Lemma
.

Consider two closed sets

such that both

and

are locally path connected with

, and such that

and

Let

to

5.34 Lemma

(Tracking Lemma)
.

Let
M:S^{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
D:D^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, such that
M
and
D
intersect in exactly two points, and such that these intersections are transverse. Let
M_{t}
and
D_{t}
be homotopies of
M
and
D
respectively, so that
M_{t}
never intersects
D_{t}|_{\partial D^{n}}
and so that
M_{1}
and
D_{1}
are disjoint. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there is some
T\in[0,1]
, some continuous
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,T]
with
\gamma(0)=0
and
\gamma(1)=T
, and two continuous maps
x_{1},x_{2}:[0,1]\to S^{1}
such that

1.

x_{1}(0)
and
x_{2}(0)
are the points at which
M
intersects
D
,

2.

x_{1}(1)=x_{2}(1)
,

3.

\mathrm{dist}(M_{\gamma(t)}(x_{1}(t)),D_{\gamma(t)}),\mathrm{dist}(M_{\gamma(t)}(x_{2}(t)),D_{\gamma(t)})<\varepsilon
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

This lemma tells us that if we have a closed curve and an
n
-dimensional disk in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
that intersect nicely in exactly two spots, and if we have any homotopy splitting the curve and the disk without the curve passing through the boundary of the disk, then we can continuously track points in the parameter space of the curve such that the curve evaluated at these points stays arbitrarily close to the disk, and such that the parameter values eventually merge. This tracking does require us to "slide the homotopies back and forth through time", i.e. instead of considering
M_{t}
and
D_{t}
, we have to consider
M_{\gamma(t)}
and
D_{\gamma(t)}
for some curve
\gamma
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that there is some
c>0
such that for
t\in[0,c]
we have
D_{t}=D
and
M_{t}=M
. Let

G:[0,1]\times S^{1}\to\mathbb{R},\quad G(t,x)=\mathrm{dist}(M_{t}(x),D_{t}),

S=\{(t,x)\in[0,1]\times S^{1}\mid G(t,x)=0\}.

Note that
G
is continuous and hence
S
is closed. Let
\delta>0
be such that for all
(t,x)\in[0,1]\times S^{1}
with
G(t,x)=0
we have
(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\in U_{\delta}(t,x)\implies G(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})<\varepsilon.
Let
Z\subseteq[0,1]\times S^{1}
so that

1.

Z
contains an open neighborhood of
S

2.

Z
is contained in the
\delta
-neighborhood of
S

3.

Z
and the closure of its complement are locally path connected

4.

Whenever there is a path in
Z
connecting two points in
Z^{\circ}
, there is also a path in
Z^{\circ}
connecting the same two points

5.

Whenever there is a path in the closure of the complement of
Z
connecting two points in the complement of
Z
, there is a path in the complement of
Z
connecting the same two points.

6.

\left(\{1\}\times S^{1}\right)\cap Z=\emptyset

7.

\left(\{0\}\times S^{1}\right)\cap Z
is a disjoint union of two compact intervals
I_{A}
and
I_{B}
of positive length, each containing one point in
S
.

Such a set
Z
always exists, for example we could cover
S
with a finite number of closed radius-
\tfrac{\delta}{2}
balls in a suitable way. Let
I_{X}
and
I_{Y}
be the disjoint compact intervals so that
I_{X}^{\circ}\cup I_{Y}^{\circ}=\left(\{0\}\times S^{1}\right)\setminus Z
. Let
A\subseteq Z
be the connected component of
I_{A}
and let
B\subseteq Z
be the connected component of
I_{B}
in
Z
.

Toward a contradiction, assume
\mathbf{A\neq B}
.
By Lemma
5.33
, there is a path
(\alpha,f):[0,1]\to[0,1]\times S^{1}
from a point
(0,x)\in I_{X}^{\circ}
to a point
(0,y)\in I_{Y}^{\circ}
avoiding
Z^{\circ}
. By the properties of
Z
, we can assume that this path avoids
Z
. Since
Z
is closed, we can perturb the curve
(\alpha,f)
in the open set
\left([0,1]\times S^{1}\right)\setminus Z
so that the curve
\alpha
is piecewise monotone. Let
T
be the maximum value of
\alpha
. Without loss of generality assume that
\alpha(\tfrac{1}{2})=T
, else reparametrize
(\alpha,f)
. We now define
(\alpha_{1},f_{1}),(\alpha_{2},f_{2}):[0,1]\to[0,1]\times S^{1}
through
(\alpha_{1},f_{1})(t)\coloneqq(\alpha,f)(\tfrac{t}{2})
and
(\alpha_{2},f_{2})\coloneqq(\alpha,f)(1-\tfrac{t}{2})
. Note that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}:[0,1]\to[0,T]
agree on endpoints and that both are piecewise monotone. We may assume that
\gamma\coloneqq\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
, else we appeal to the mountain climbing theorem (
5.31
) for the existence of curves
\alpha^{\prime}_{1},\alpha^{\prime}_{2}:[0,1]\to[0,1]

\gamma\coloneqq\alpha_{1}\circ\alpha^{\prime}_{1}=\alpha_{2}\circ\alpha^{\prime}_{2}

(\gamma,f_{1}\circ\alpha^{\prime}_{1})
and

. Let

be the standard covering. Lift

and
f_{2}

\overline{f}_{1}(t)

\overline{f}_{2}(t)

and

The curve

is simply a reparametrization of a segment of

These sets are marked

6

Besides the many missing details, there are two problems with this proof. \displaystyle+2\mathrm{diam}(A)-2\varphi(\mathrm{diam}(A))

\displaystyle+2\mathrm{diam}(B)-2\varphi(\mathrm{diam}(B))

\displaystyle+2\mathrm{diam}(C)-2\varphi(\mathrm{diam}(C))

\displaystyle+2\mathrm{diam}(D)-2\varphi(\mathrm{diam}(D))

\displaystyle+2\mathrm{diam}(E)-2\varphi(\mathrm{diam}(E))

\displaystyle+2\mathrm{diam}(F)-2\varphi(\mathrm{diam}(F)),

6.2
Circle decompositions - the details

We now formally define what we mean by a circle decomposition and a conjoined circle decomposition. Throughout the rest of this paper

6.36 Definition
.

Let
C
be a closed curve such that the definition domain of

decomposes into

is a compact interval of positive length with
\max(I_{i})=\min(I_{i+1})
, for all

(here indices are understood to be cyclic and the definition domain is seen as an interval with endpoints identified). We define
a_{i}
via
[a_{i},a_{i+1}]=I_{i}
. Let
\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and
\{\alpha_{l}\}_{l=1}^{k}

\{1,2,\dots,n\}
into non-empty sets. We say that
\left(C,\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m},\{\alpha_{l}\}_{l=1}^{k}\right)
is a

of

if for all

and

:

1.

There is a bijection
\{1,2,\dots,c\}\to A_{j}
,

and some

, such that

parametrizing a unit circle. Here

and

is defined to be the curve obtained by inverting the orientation of

.

2.

C(a_{i_{1}})=C(a_{i_{2}})\iff\text{ there is some }l\text{ with }i_{1},i_{2}\in\alpha_{l}

The length of a circle decomposition
\left(C,\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m},\{\alpha_{l}\}_{l=1}^{k}\right)
is equal to

.

.

From here on we will write circle decompositions just as

6.45 Lemma

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{len}(l_{1})+\mathrm{len}(l_{2})+2r_{1}\arcsin\left(\frac{||v_{1}-v_{2}||}{2r_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2r_{1}\arcsin\left(\frac{\mathrm{len}(l_{1})+\mathrm{len}(l_{2})}{2r_{1}}\right)+2r_{1}\arcsin\left(\frac{||v_{1}-v_{2}||}{2r_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\arcsin\left(\mathrm{len}(l_{1})+||v_{1}-v_{2}||+\mathrm{len}(l_{2})\right)

\displaystyle\arcsin(||w_{1}-w_{2}||+2||w_{2}-w_{3}||+||w_{2}-w_{4}||)-||w_{2}-w_{3}||

\displaystyle\arcsin(2||w_{1}-w_{4}||)-||w_{1}-w_{4}||.

6.46 Lemma
.

Let

with
||x-y||,||x-m||,||y-m||<\tfrac{1}{2}

Toward a contradiction, assume

. Then

, which is a contradiction to

. If

and

are not collinear, then by taking the intersection of

and

with

, the problem reduces to Lemma

∎
