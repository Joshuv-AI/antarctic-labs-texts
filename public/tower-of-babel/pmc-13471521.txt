A novel risk stratification model based on creatine kinase and direct bilirubin for identifying severe Chlamydia psittaci pneumonia: a double-center, hypothesis-generating retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yanmei Huang, Qingcai Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChlamydia psittaci is a global zoonotic pathogen causing psittacosis, which can progress to severe pneumonia and multi-organ dysfunction. Identifying reliable predictors for severe disease is crucial for improving outcomes, yet current community-acquired pneumonia severity scores may not adequately capture psittacosis-specific systemic manifestations. However, the proposed model is intended to identify patients who already meet criteria for severe pneumonia at presentation, not to predict progression from non-severe to severe disease.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 36 hospitalized patients with confirmed C. psittaci pneumonia, classified into severe (n = 11) and non-severe (n = 25) groups. We employed Firth’s penalized-likelihood logistic regression with bootstrap validation to address small sample size limitations and potential model overfitting.ResultsOur analysis establishes a comprehensive framework organized around four key pillars: (1) Radiological evolution - serial computed tomography demonstrated characteristic temporal progression with peak severity at 2 weeks (median Total CT Severity Score: 7), followed by gradual resolution; (2) Biomarker validation - severe cases showed numerically higher creatine kinase (CK) (415.0 vs. 170.0 U/L) and direct bilirubin (DBil) (9.4 vs. 3.8 µmol/L), with exceptional bootstrap stability (relative bias: -8.9% and -7.5%, respectively); however, no variable reached statistical significance (P < 0.05) in univariable Firth regression, and confidence intervals were wide and included the null; (3) Clinical translation - we developed a risk stratification model categorizing patients into four distinct risk groups based on CK and DBil thresholds; (4) Pathophysiological basis - we propose the “lung-liver-muscle” axis as a hypothesis-generating conceptual framework for systemic disease progression.ConclusionThis exploratory study identifies CK and DBil as stable, readily available biomarkers associated with psittacosis severity at presentation. The integrated four-pillar approach provides hypothesis-generating risk stratification tools and pathophysiological insights, potentially aiding early identification of high-risk patients. All findings require external validation in larger prospective cohorts before any clinical application.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13738-0.

Background

Chlamydia psittaci, the etiological agent of psittacosis (ornithosis), is an obligate intracellular bacterium transmitted to humans from infected birds [1, 2]. While bird exposure is the main risk factor, limited human-to-human transmission has been documented, which can pose an additional challenge in outbreak settings [3, 4]. It is an underdiagnosed cause of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) that can lead to severe complications, including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), septic shock, and multi-organ dysfunction, with a reported mortality rate of up to 15% if untreated [5–8].

The clinical presentation of psittacosis is highly variable and non-specific, often mimicking other types of CAP, which frequently leads to delays in diagnosis and appropriate antibiotic therapy [9–12]. The diagnostic challenge is underscored by a high misdiagnosis rate of 50–80% [2]. While rapid diagnostic methods like metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) have improved detection, predicting which patients will develop severe illness remains a significant clinical challenge [13–16]. Current severity scores for CAP, such as CURB-65 and PSI, may not fully capture the unique systemic and extrapulmonary manifestations of psittacosis, which can include hepatitis and myositis [7, 17, 18]. Furthermore, comprehensive quantitative description of temporal radiographic evolution remains limited.

Several routinely available biomarkers have been identified as potential predictors of disease severity and prognosis in psittacosis. The profile includes decreased hemoglobin and elevated levels of absolute lymphocyte count, neutrophils, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, lactate dehydrogenase, creatine kinase (CK), alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, D-dimer, and interleukin-6 [3, 17, 19–21]. This pattern of biomarker elevation reflects the bacterium’s recognized capacity to directly affect multiple organ systems, including cardiac, hepatic, muscular, and central nervous system function [22]. Consequently, these markers could enhance risk stratification for the spectrum of extrapulmonary involvements. However, findings across existing studies remain inconsistent. A major confounding factor is the high prevalence (31.8%–73.3%) of suspected co-infections, which creates diagnostic ambiguity and makes it difficult to definitively attribute organ dysfunction solely to C. psittaci versus a concurrent infection [14, 23, 24]. A key methodological strength of the present study is that, upon enrollment, we systematically excluded all cases with any laboratory evidence of co-infection based on comprehensive mNGS testing. This design uniquely allows for the attribution of observed systemic manifestations—such as hepatic and muscular injury—specifically to C. psittaci infection, minimizing diagnostic ambiguity. Among these biomarkers, CK, a marker of muscle injury, and direct bilirubin (DBil), a marker of hepatocellular or cholestatic injury, are routinely available, but their combined prognostic value and stability for risk stratification in psittacosis require systematic evaluation.

This study aimed to comprehensively describe clinical features and radiographic progression using serial Total CT Severity Score (CTSS) measurements and identify robust predictors of severe disease [25]. We specifically focused on evaluating CK and DBil as potential biomarkers. Utilizing Firth’s regression with bootstrap validation to address small sample size limitations, we sought to develop a biomarker-based risk stratification tool and investigate the pathophysiological model underlying systemic progression.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at two tertiary care hospitals. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of the participating institutions, which waived the requirement for informed consent.

We screened patients aged ≥ 18 years hospitalized with pneumonia and positive mNGS for C. psittaci from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or blood between April 2020 and July 2025. A total of 65 patients were initially identified. Pneumonia was defined by new pulmonary infiltrates on imaging plus relevant clinical symptoms.

Exclusion criteria included: (a) co-infection with other respiratory pathogens; (b) incomplete medical records; (c) hospital-acquired pneumonia. After applying these criteria, 36 patients were included in the final analysis, and 29 were excluded.

mNGS was performed on bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (n = 31) or blood (n = 5). Positivity for C. psittaci was defined as reads per million ≥ 3. Co-infection was defined as concurrent detection of other bacterial or viral pathogens meeting standard diagnostic criteria (e.g., read count ratio ≥ 10 times background reads or recognized pathogenic species with clinical correlation). The median turnaround time for mNGS results was 48 h (IQR 36–72 h). All patients also underwent routine conventional microbiological testing, including blood cultures, sputum cultures, and respiratory viral PCR panels (influenza, RSV, adenovirus, etc.); no additional respiratory pathogens were identified.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), including Firth’s penalized-likelihood regression and bootstrap validation. Data management and preliminary analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent samples t test, while non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables, expressed as No. (%), were compared using Fisher’s exact test or the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test, as applicable (Supplementary Appendix 1).

Handling of missing data

Variables with > 20% missing data were excluded. For variables with < 20% missingness, multiple imputation was performed using the Fully Conditional Specification (FCS) method under the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) framework to create five complete datasets [28]. The imputation model was run for 10 iterations and incorporated predictive mean matching for scale variables. The imputed variables included cardiac troponin I (cTnI), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). Subsequent statistical analyses were performed on each imputed dataset, and the results were pooled using Rubin’s rules to obtain final estimates and standard errors that account for the uncertainty due to missing data (Supplementary Appendix 3).

Regression modeling and bootstrap validation

Given the small sample size (N = 36) and complete separation, Firth’s penalized-likelihood logistic regression was employed (Supplementary Appendix 4). Due to the small sample size, multivariable regression was not performed; univariable Firth regression results are presented as hypothesis‑generating only. The absence of multivariable adjustment means we cannot exclude confounding by demographic factors, comorbidities, or other laboratory abnormalities. Internal bootstrap validation with 1000 resamples evaluated coefficient stability, rated by relative bias: <10% (Excellent), 10–20% (Acceptable), > 20% (Concerning) (Supplementary Appendix 5).

Power and sample size consideration

Because post-hoc power depends on the observed effect size, it does not provide additional information beyond the confidence interval and should not be used to conclude that the study was adequately powered for any specific effect. Post hoc power analysis indicated 59.9% power to detect large effect sizes (Cohen’s h = 0.8, OR = 3.51) (Supplementary Appendix 4). However, power to detect minimum clinically important effect sizes (Cohen’s h = 0.3, OR = 1.60) was limited, requiring approximately 371 patients for 80% power. These results indicate adequate power for identifying strong predictors while highlighting the exploratory nature for factors with more modest effects.

Radiological data analysis

The Friedman test was used to compare the Total CTSS across the four disease stages to evaluate the temporal evolution of radiographic severity. The cubic polynomial model was selected based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) comparison among linear, quadratic, cubic, and spline models; the cubic model provided the lowest AIC, indicating the best fit (Supplementary Appendix 2).

Risk stratification model development

CK and DBil were selected a priori based on prior literature and clinical relevance, and because they demonstrated the most stable bootstrap performance (relative bias < 10%) among all routinely available laboratory parameters examined. No hypothesis‑free discovery analysis (e.g., LASSO or stepwise selection) was performed due to the small sample size; therefore, the selection of CK and DBil should be considered hypothesis‑generating rather than data‑driven. Based on the distribution characteristics and bootstrap stability results of CK and DBil, clinically relevant cut-off points were selected to create a risk stratification matrix. The proposed thresholds are hypothesis‑generating, based on observed distributions and bootstrap stability; they require external validation before clinical application. This model categorizes patients into distinct risk groups to guide clinical decision‑making.

Results

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes

A total of 36 patients were included. Eleven patients (30.6%) developed severe pneumonia. Severe patients were older (67 ± 11 vs. 60 ± 12 years, P = 0.108) with higher prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (36.4% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.006). All severe patients presented with cough (100% vs. 64%, P = 0.034), with significantly higher gastrointestinal symptoms (45.5% vs. 8.0%, P = 0.018).

Clinical outcomes revealed significant disparities. While overall improvement rate was 94.4%, all non-severe patients improved (100%) versus 81.8% in severe group (P = 0.087). ICU admission was exclusively required for severe cases (81.8% vs. 0%, P < 0.001) (as shown in Table 1).

Complications and organ dysfunctions

As expected, complications were more frequent in the severe group. Respiratory failure was the most defining complication, occurring in 9 (81.8%) severe patients compared to 1 (4.0%) in the non-severe group (P < 0.001). Hypoalbuminemia was universal in the severe group (100% vs. 60%, P = 0.016). Hepatic dysfunction was common in both groups but more so in severe cases (81.8% vs. 60.0%, P = 0.268). Cardiac dysfunction was significantly more prevalent in the severe group (45.5% vs. 4.0%, P = 0.006). Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores were significantly higher in the severe group (6.3 ± 2.1 vs. 2.1 ± 1.4, P < 0.001).

Laboratory findings and predictors

Key laboratory differences are shown in Table 1. Neutrophil percentage was significantly higher in the severe group (89.2% vs. 83.8%, P = 0.022), while lymphocyte counts were lower (0.6 vs. 0.9 × 10⁹/L, P = 0.063). Coagulation parameters like international normalized ratio were higher in severe patients (1.32 vs. 1.16, P = 0.049). Markers of inflammation and organ damage, including procalcitonin, cTnI, NT-proBNP, IFN-γ, and IL-6, were markedly elevated in the severe group.

The most notable findings were the differences in CK and DBil. The median CK level was more than twice as high in the severe group (415.0 U/L vs. 170.0 U/L, P = 0.058). Similarly, the median DBil level was 2.5 times higher in the severe group (9.4 µmol/L vs. 3.8 µmol/L, P = 0.049).

Firth regression and bootstrap validation

Given the sample size limitations, Firth’s penalized-likelihood logistic regression was used to assess the association between variables and disease severity. Consistent with the exploratory nature of this study, no variable reached the traditional threshold of statistical significance (P < 0.05) in univariable analysis. Numerically elevated odds ratios were observed for several variables, including CK (aOR 1.00, 95% CI 1.00–1.00) and DBil (aOR 1.07, 95% CI 0.45–2.59); however, confidence intervals were wide and included the null, indicating that these findings may be consistent with no true effect (Table 2).

Radiological evolution

Among the 36 patients, a total of 62 CT scans were performed. The median number of CT scans per patient was 2 (IQR 1–3); patients with severe pneumonia underwent a median of 3 scans (IQR 2–4), while non-severe patients underwent a median of 1 scan (IQR 1–2). The temporal evolution of chest CT findings is summarized in Supplementary Appendix 2, with the continuous change in radiographic severity over time detailed in Fig. 1. The Total CTSS demonstrated characteristic temporal evolution, peaking in early stages (Stage-1: median 7; Stage-2: median 7) and decreasing thereafter (Stage-3: median 4; Stage-4: median 1); however, the Friedman test across the four stages was not statistically significant (P = 0.392). Therefore, the observed trend should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis‑generating, not as definitive evidence of temporal change. The cubic polynomial fit (y = 0.0003x³ − 0.023x² + 0.300x + 6.266) is provided for visualization only and does not imply distinct biological phases. The apparent decline at later time points may be influenced by selective imaging practices (e.g., fewer scans in recovered patients) rather than true disease evolution. This progression pattern was further elucidated by continuous modeling, which demonstrated that the dynamic change in the Total CTSS over the first 60 days was best described by a third-order polynomial equation (y = 0.0003x³ − 0.023x² + 0.300x + 6.266). The cubic term is primarily descriptive; it may reflect the complex interplay of acute inflammation, resolution, and fibrotic remodeling phases, but this model is hypothesis‑generating rather than biologically prescriptive (Supplementary Appendix 6).

Proposed clinical risk stratification model

We propose a practical risk stratification model based on admission levels of CK and DBil. This model is intended to identify patients who already meet the criteria for severe pneumonia at presentation, rather than to predict future progression from mild to severe disease. Given the retrospective design, the model serves as a risk‑stratification tool for early recognition of high‑risk patients upon hospital admission, facilitating timely escalation of monitoring and treatment. The model categorizes patients into four groups based on CK and DBil thresholds: (1) Low Risk: DBil < 5 µmol/L and CK < 200 U/L; (2) Intermediate Risk: DBil 5–7.5 µmol/L or CK 200–400 U/L; (3) High Risk: DBil 7.5–15 µmol/L or CK 400–1000 U/L; (4) Very High Risk: DBil > 15 µmol/L or CK > 1000 U/L (Table 3).

Discussion

This exploratory study identifies CK and DBil as promising biomarkers associated with severity in psittacosis pneumonia, synthesizing findings into four key pillars: radiological evolution, biomarker association, risk stratification, and pathophysiological framework. The proposed model is designed to identify patients who already have severe pneumonia at presentation, not to predict future progression from mild to severe disease.

Radiological evolution and pathophysiological basis

Serial CT revealed a characteristic temporal pattern peaking within two weeks, followed by a phase of gradual resolution and occasional fibrotic residue [26]. This evolutionary trajectory can be attributed to the underlying pathophysiological processes. The initial peak in severity scores corresponds to the acute phase of intense host immune-inflammatory response to C. psittaci infection. Following inhalation, this obligate intracellular bacterium replicates within alveolar macrophages and epithelial cells, triggering a robust release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and a massive cellular infiltration into the lung interstitium and alveoli. This results in widespread ground-glass opacities and consolidation, manifesting as the highest Total CTSS. Subsequent resolution parallels the control of bacterial replication with appropriate antibiotic therapy and the subsequent dampening of the inflammatory response, leading to the gradual absorption of exudates. The occasional development of fibrotic streaks in the convalescent phase represents aberrant tissue repair following severe alveolar injury. In cases of extensive initial damage, the normal healing process is superseded by a fibroproliferative response, which manifests as persistent linear opacities on CT. Although the overall Friedman test across the four disease stages did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.392), the descriptive trend and the fitted third-order polynomial curve provide a valuable representation of the disease’s natural history, aiding clinicians in contextualizing imaging findings throughout the disease course.

CK and DBil as stable biomarkers

Elevations in CK and DBil specifically signal extrapulmonary organ injury—myocyte damage and hepatic/cholestatic dysfunction, respectively. This pattern pathophysiologically aligns with C. psittaci’s tropism for the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) [29, 30]. The liver, with its abundant Kupffer cells, and skeletal muscle, with its resident macrophage network, become primary targets after hematogenous dissemination from the lung. Their infection establishes the “lung–liver–muscle” axis, directly linking bacterial spread to end-organ damage and systemic inflammation. Methodologically, despite limited sample size, both biomarkers showed numerically elevated odds ratios per unit increase (CK: aOR 1.00 per 1 U/L [1.00–1.00]; DBil: aOR 1.07 per 1 µmol/L [0.45–2.59]).

Clinical risk stratification model

It is important to recognize a key limitation regarding potential exploratory value. Because the proposed model identifies patients who already meet criteria for severe pneumonia at presentation (e.g., need for ventilation, ICU admission, septic shock), its incremental value beyond routine clinical assessment remains unclear. Clinicians can typically identify such patients based on readily apparent features such as hypoxemia, tachypnea, hemodynamic instability, or altered mental status. Therefore, whether CK and DBil measurement provides meaningful additional information for early triage decisions requires prospective comparison against clinical judgment alone.

We operationalized CK and DBil levels into a practical, four-tier risk stratification model (Low, Intermediate, High, Very High). This tool translates biomarker levels into specific clinical actions regarding monitoring frequency, care level, and treatment intensity. Using routinely available lab parameters, it may potentially assist in the identification of high-risk patients, suggesting a possible role in timely intervention and resource allocation, but this requires prospective validation. In an exploratory comparison, the proposed CK/DBil two-marker model (using thresholds CK ≥ 400 U/L or DBil ≥ 7.5 µmol/L) showed a numerically higher area under the ROC curve (AUROC, 0.76) than CURB-65 (0.68) and PSI (0.71) for identifying severe pneumonia, although confidence intervals overlapped substantially, indicating no statistically significant improvement in this small cohort (Supplementary Appendix 8).

Integrative pathophysiological model

We propose the “lung–liver–muscle” axis as a hypothesis-generating conceptual framework to guide future mechanistic studies. This integrative paradigm posits the pulmonary infection as the inciting event, with subsequent bacteremic seeding of organs enriched in phagocytic cells. The liver and skeletal muscle are selectively vulnerable due to their dense and functionally distinct macrophage populations. Hepatic infection, centered on Kupffer cells, can incite bystander hepatocellular injury or disrupt biliary transport, manifesting as elevated DBil. In skeletal muscle, the resident macrophage network, typically dedicated to tissue homeostasis and repair, is subverted by the infectious process. An initial pro-inflammatory macrophage response likely mediates myocyte damage and CK release, mirroring a sterile injury response. Consequently, the rising levels of DBil and CK serve not merely as biomarkers but as direct reflections of active extrapulmonary organ involvement. This injury, in turn, fuels a systemic inflammatory response, establishing a vicious, self-perpetuating cycle of immune dysregulation and progressive organ dysfunction that culminates in multi-organ failure. The tropism of C. psittaci for the mononuclear phagocyte system has been well described [29–31], with Kupffer cell infection documented in animal models and skeletal muscle macrophage involvement implicated in myositis pathogenesis. By framing CK and DBil as specific indicators of targeted extra-pulmonary organ damage, this model transcends a purely pneumocentric view of psittacosis and illuminates novel potential targets for future therapeutic strategies aimed at interrupting this deleterious cycle. A detailed schematic illustration of this “lung–liver–muscle” axis pathophysiological framework is provided in Supplementary Appendix 7.

It is critical to emphasize that the proposed risk stratification model is designed to identify patients who already have severe pneumonia at presentation, not to predict progression from mild to severe disease. Its potential exploratory value lies in early recognition, not risk forecasting.

Limitations and future directions

This study’s retrospective design, small sample size, and lack of a stable multivariable model limit generalizability. Specifically, the absence of multivariable adjustment means we cannot exclude confounding by age, comorbidities, coinfections, or other laboratory parameters. The observed associations between CK/DBil and severity may be confounded by unmeasured variables. The four-tier risk stratification thresholds lack empirical validation for discrimination and should be interpreted as illustrative pending confirmation in larger cohorts. DNA-based mNGS may not reliably detect RNA viruses; however, concurrent PCR testing mitigated this limitation. While Firth’s regression mitigated small-sample bias, external validation of the proposed CK and DBil cut-offs in larger, prospective cohorts is essential before clinical adoption. We recommend incorporating these biomarkers into initial patient assessment, particularly for high-risk individuals. Future work should focus on multi-center validation and model refinement.

Conclusions

This study integrates four key pillars of severe psittacosis: a characterized radiological evolution, validated CK and DBil as stable biomarkers, a practical risk stratification model, and the “lung-liver-muscle” axis pathophysiological framework. The consistent performance of CK and DBil supports further investigation of their potential role in identifying high-risk patients, but immediate clinical use is not recommended pending future validation studies in larger, prospective cohorts.
