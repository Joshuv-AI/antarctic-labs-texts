Alkyl shikimates promote proliferation of human dermal fibroblasts and exhibit structure–activity relationships
Authors: Hidetada Morishita, Koji Umezawa, Masanobu Kojima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlkyl shikimates (SAEs) bearing linear and branched alkyl groups (R) ranging from CH3 to C5H11 were synthesized and assayed for proliferative activity toward human dermal fibroblasts; proliferation increase (PRA), measured using a CCK-8 assay kit, was used as the primary endpoint. Several SAEs exhibited significant PRA, depending on the concentration added to the culture medium and alkyl group structure. However, shikimic acid (SA) added to the culture medium did not induce fibroblast proliferation. SAEs with CH3, C2H5, and C3H7 alkyl groups (SAE-1, SAE-2, and SAE-3a,b) induced a 30–50% increase in cell proliferation at a concentration of 5.5 mM, while SAEs with a C4H9 groups (SAE-4a,b,c) showed low PRA and those with a C5H11 groups (SAE-5a,b,c) exhibited strong cytotoxicity (CYT: decrease in cell viability) at this concentration. The proliferation rates of SAEs at 5.5 mM correlated significantly with their calculated log P (ClogP) values, which reflect hydrophobicity and cell membrane permeability (quadratic regression; coefficient of determination, r2 = 0.977–0.953. Based on these results, we proposed a mechanism in which membrane-permeable SAEs could form complexes with carboxylesterase (CES) present in dermal fibroblasts and be subsequently hydrolyzed into SA and alcohols (ALs) intracellularly, with the resulting SA potentially inducing PRA. In contrast, SA added extracellularly cannot enter cells due to its high hydrophilicity and therefore cannot induce PRA. In order to evaluate the stability of the proposed complexes, we estimated binding energies between CES and SAEs by quantum mechanical methods; however, the results showed no significant differences in binding energy attributable to alkyl chain length or steric effects of the SAE alkyl groups. The most plausible explanation for the differences in PRA among SAEs is that intracellularly regenerated SA promotes fibroblast proliferation, whereas the regenerated ALs may cause CYT. Alkyl group-dependent differences in AL toxicity may account for the concentration-dependent variation in SAE-induced PRA. In fact, when the CYT of linear alkyl alcohols (ALs-1, 2, 3a, 4a, and 5a) was measured by CCK-8 at a concentration of 100 mM, the results showed that differences in AL alkyl chain length significantly influenced cell viability, which was consistent with their ClogP values (CYT order; R = C5H11 > C4H9 > C3H7 > C2H5 ≥ CH3).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55244-y.Keywords: Shikimate bioactivity, Fibroblast proliferation, Structure–activity relationships, Lipophilicity, Ester hydrolysisSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Cell biology, Drug discovery

Introduction

Shikimic acid (SA) is a central metabolic intermediate in the biosynthesis of aromatic amino acids in plants and microorganisms; the shikimate biosynthetic pathway provides the biosynthetic starting point for a wide range of downstream aromatic metabolites1. SA and its derivatives have attracted interest for a wide range of biological activities2. Previous studies using component-rich extracts, reference standards, and derivative compounds have reported antioxidant activity3, antiviral4,5, anti-inflammatory3,5,6, antibacterial7–9, hypolipidemic10, bone-protective11,12, skin-protective13,14, neuroprotective15, antidiabetic16, anticariogenic17, hair growth-promoting18, and anticonvulsant19 effects. However, activities attributed to SA and its derivatives require verification using pure reference standards, as many reported effects derive from component-rich extracts rather than isolated compounds.

SA has also attracted attention as an optically active starting material for the synthesis of oseltamivir, an antiviral drug used in the treatment of influenza20,21. Consequently, numerous chemical synthesis routes22 and microbial production methods23,24 have been developed for SA production. One of the authors also reported that irradiation of oyster mushroom mycelia with monochromatic blue LEDs markedly enhances SA biosynthesis compared to dark cultivation25. However, associated with the development of other agents for influenza treatment26–29, demand for SA as a pharmaceutical starting material has declined. Against this backdrop, we became interested in an alternate application of SA as a cosmetic ingredient, given its reported keratolytic, antibacterial, and deodorizing effects in the epidermis30. However, purified SA did not exhibit notable biological antioxidant, anti-glycation, or cell proliferation activity in our assays.

In the course of our research and development on novel biological activities of SA, we became interested in the effect of highly lipophilic, cell membrane-permeable, long-chain alkyl ester substituents on the biological activity of ascorbic acid31. We therefore synthesized 10 alkyl shikimates (SAEs, bearing alkyl groups ranging from CH3 to C5H11; Fig. 1) to introduce systematic variation in lipophilicity and to investigate the structure–activity relationships of SAEs. With the exception of the CH3 and C2H5 derivatives, all SAEs are novel compounds. Systematic examination of their effects on human dermal fibroblasts showed that, whereas SA added to the culture medium does not promote cell proliferation, SAEs act as proliferative agents, exhibiting structure–activity relationships. To elucidate the mechanism of action of SAEs, we examined the relationships between fibroblast proliferation on SAE concentration and lipophilicity, with the latter serving as an index of cell membrane permeability. To investigate the mechanism by which PRA (the increase in cell proliferation rate) depends on the alkyl group of each SAE, we focused on carboxylesterase (CES), an ester hydrolyzing enzyme present in human skin32,33. Owing to methodological limitations, direct intracellular hydrolysis and inhibition studies using human-derived CES were not feasible. We therefore investigated the proposed mechanism using docking geometries and binding energies of SAE–CES interactions derived from quantum mechanical calculations. We also assessed the cytotoxicity (CYT; i.e., decrease in cell viability) of the ALs generated upon SAE hydrolysis. Based on these results, we propose that intracellularly generated SA may be responsible for the observed PRA, whereas the concurrently generated ALs may contribute to CYT.

Materials and methods

General method for SAE synthesis

Unless stated otherwise, all reagents and solvents were used as received from commercial suppliers. For organic synthesis, ALs and 1,4-dioxane were dried over 3 Å molecular sieves overnight before use. Reaction progress and purity of the synthesized compounds were monitored by HPLC analysis. HPLC was performed using an Inertsil HILIC column (GL Sciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and a Shimadzu LC workstation (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan), consisting of a CBM-20A system controller, an LC-10ADvp pump, a DGU-14A degasser, an FCV-10ALvp flow-path switching valve, a CTO-10ASvp column oven, and an SPD-10Avp UV detector, with UV detection at 210 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 1% H3PO4 in MeCN (10:90 v/v), and the flow rate was 0.6 mL/min. The column temperature was set at 30 °C and the injection volume was 1–5 µL. SA was detected with a retention time of 11.5 min under these HPLC conditions. For the purification of synthesized compounds, medium-pressure liquid chromatography (LC) was carried out using a Purif-Rp2 apparatus (GL Sciences Inc.) with octadecylsilylated silica gel (ODS)-packed columns (Purif-Pack ODS-50 series; SIZE-20 or SIZE-60, GL Sciences Inc.). For LC purification, the mobile phase consisted of a mixture of methanol and water, and gradient elution was performed at flow rates of 10 mL/min (SIZE-20) or 20 mL/min (SIZE-60). The methanol content of the mobile phase was adjusted depending on the compound being purified. Synthesized compounds were identified by measurements of melting point (m.p.), 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), 13C NMR, and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS). Melting points were measured without correction using a microscopic hot-stage apparatus. 1H NMR spectra were recorded in CD3OD or CDCl3 at 400 or 500 MHz, and 13C NMR spectra were measured at 100 or 125 MHz under similar conditions. HRMS analyses were performed using an ESI mass spectrometer. All data associated with SAEs are provided in the supplementary information available online.

All SAEs are chemically stable and can be stored at room temperature and atmospheric pressure. As a representative SAE, SAE-1 was confirmed to remain unhydrolyzed after 65 h under cell culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator).

Cell culture

Normal human adult dermal fibroblasts (NHDFa) were purchased from Kurabo Biomedical Division (KF-4109; Kurabo Industries, Inc., Osaka, Japan) and maintained in defined FibroLife S2 Comp Kit medium (Kurabo Industries, Inc.; Lifeline Cell Technologies, Frederick, MD, USA) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. This medium was prepared by supplementing FibroLife Basal Medium (Kurabo Industries, Inc.; Lifeline Cell Technologies) with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 7.5 mM L-glutamic acid, 5 ng/mL hFGF-B, 5 µg/mL insulin, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 1 µg/mL hydrocortisone, 30 µg/mL gentamicin, and 15 ng/mL amphotericin. Cells were harvested for the cell proliferation assays after reaching approximately 80% confluence, following trypsinization. Passage 4 cells were used for all cell proliferation assays. The medium used for all cell proliferation assays was 1% FBS medium, prepared by two-fold dilution of FibroLife S2 Comp Kit medium with FibroLife Basal Medium. Cell proliferation responses to added samples were more clearly observed in 1% FBS medium than in undiluted FibroLife S2 Comp Kit medium (data not shown). SA, SAEs, or ALs were dissolved directly in the culture medium, filter-sterilized, and prepared at several concentrations for use in assays. For all cell culture and proliferation assays, FibroLife S2 Comp Kit medium and 1% FBS medium were pre-equilibrated by incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for at least 1 h prior to use, in the same manner as media containing SA, SAEs, or ALs. The manufacturer confirmed that the purchased cells were obtained with the donor’s informed consent and were free from mycoplasma, hepatitis B and C viruses, HIV-I, and HIV-II. All culture media, containers, and other consumables used in cell assays passed sterility testing.

Cell proliferation assay in SA- or SAE-containing cell cultures

A total of 2500 cells were seeded into 100 µL of FibroLife S2 Comp Kit medium per well and incubated overnight in 96-well plates. After 24 h, the medium in the wells was aspirated, and 100 µL of fresh 1% FBS medium containing 0, 0.275, 0.825, 1.65, 2.75, 4.125, or 5.5 mM SA or SAEs was added to each well. After 48 h of culture at 37 °C in 5% CO2, cell proliferation was measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan)34,35. The medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh 1% FBS medium supplemented with 10 μL of CCK-8 solution. After incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 1 h, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer. The cell proliferation rate (%) was calculated using the following formula: (Average absorbance of cells in SA- or SAE-containing medium / Average absorbance of control cells) × 100. Prior to calculation, background absorbance values (Average absorbance of cell-free medium containing CCK-8) were subtracted from all measurements. Results were expressed as relative values (%) compared with control cells cultured in medium without SA or SAEs. All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates. All data are presented as the mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. For comparisons at 5.5 mM, a 2-tailed unpaired Welch’s t-test was applied; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses, including SD calculation and quadratic regression curve fitting (Figs. 4 and 5), were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Cell viability assay in AL-containing cell culture

The cell viability assay in AL-containing cell culture medium was performed using the same procedure described above. The AL addition concentrations were selected based on differences in water solubility. AL-1, AL-2, AL-3a, and AL-3b were prepared in 1% FBS medium at concentrations of 15, 100, 350, 500, and 750 mM for the assay. The butanol isomers (AL-4a, AL-4b, and AL-4c) and the pentanol isomers (AL-5a, AL-5b, and AL-5c) were tested at concentrations of 15, 25, 50, 75, 100, and 350 mM and 15, 25, 50, 75, and 100 mM, respectively. All data are presented as the mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. For comparisons at 100 mM, a two-tailed unpaired Welch’s t-test was applied; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses, including SD calculation and quadratic regression curve fitting (Fig. 6), were performed using Microsoft Excel.

Docking simulation and calculation of binding energy for complexes of SAEs with CES

Initial three-dimensional molecular models of SA and SAE compounds were built using ChemDraw (ver. 22.0.0; Revvity Signals, Waltham, MA, USA). The resulting conformations were geometry-optimized using quantum mechanical calculations at the B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) level with GAMESS (ver. 2024 R2)36. Partial atomic charges were assigned using the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) method implemented in Antechamber (AmberTools24)37. The optimized structures with RESP charges were saved in mol2 format for subsequent docking simulations.

Human carboxylesterase 1 (hCES1, UniProt ID: P23141) and carboxylesterase 2 (hCES2, UniProt ID: O00748) were used as docking targets. For each protein, five tertiary structure models were generated from the amino acid sequences using AlphaFold2 (ver. 2.3.2)38. The global pLDDT values of the five predicted models were 92.76, 92.60, 92.39, 91.73, and 90.74 for hCES1, and 91.19, 90.38, 90.38, 89.78, and 89.72 for hCES2. The Amber ff99 protein force field was assigned to the protein structures using tplgeneX in MyPresto (ver. 4.40)39. To account for structural variability, docking simulations were performed against all five AlphaFold2-generated models for both hCES1 and hCES2 using sievgene in MyPresto (ver. 4.40)40. The grid box was defined based on the position of the cocrystallized benzoic acid in the hCES1 experimental structure (PDB ID: 1YAJ); the AlphaFold2-predicted models were superimposed onto the 1YAJ structure, and the benzoic acid atomic coordinates were used as the docking target. The number of grid points was set to 60, with grid spacings of 0.294, 0.284, and 0.290 Å. Docking scores were calculated using a flexible docking protocol. Each compound was docked to the active site of each model, yielding five predicted binding energies and corresponding docking poses per compound. Mean values and standard deviations (SD) were calculated from the five independent docking simulations.

Results and discussion

Membrane permeability (Hydrophobicity) of SAEs

To enhance the membrane permeability of SA, 10 types of SAEs with different alkyl chain lengths and structures were synthesized. Methyl shikimate (SAE-1)41,42 and ethyl shikimate (SAE-2)43 are previously reported compounds, whereas all other SAEs synthesized in this study are novel compounds (Fig. 1). The hydrophobicity of these ester compounds varies with both the length and branching of the alkyl chain. In this study, our objective was to evaluate the general trend of water/octanol partitioning, an important factor for membrane permeability, focusing on the relative impact of various alkyl groups rather than determining their absolute values. Therefore, we utilized predicted log P values. We evaluated hydrophobicity by determining the calculated log P (ClogP) values for SAEs using ChemDraw (ver. 25.0.2)44. As shown in Table 1, for SAEs with linear alkyl groups, the ClogP values increase in the order SA < SAE-1 < SAE-2 < SAE-3a < SAE-4a < SAE-5a. Thus, increasing the carbon number is expected to enhance cell membrane permeability. Furthermore, when comparing structural isomers with the same carbon number, the ClogP values follow the order 3b < 3a (-C3H7), 4b < 4c < 4a (-C4H9), and 5b < 5c < 5a (-C5H11). These results suggest that SAEs with linear alkyl groups exhibit higher membrane permeability than those with branched alkyl groups.

Effect of SAEs with a linear alkyl ester group on fibroblast proliferation

Cell culture medium was supplemented with SA or SAE-1, SAE-2, SAE-3a, SAE-4a, or SAE-5a at concentrations ranging from 0 to 5.5 mM. Comparison of the concentration-dependent effects on dermal fibroblast proliferation showed that SA did not induce cell proliferation; instead, cell proliferation decreased with increasing SA concentration, as shown in Fig. 2. In contrast, for SAEs, at low concentrations (0.275–0.825 mM), cell proliferation increased regardless of the carbon number of the alkyl group. However, as the added concentration increased, SAEs bearing alkyl groups with higher carbon numbers (R) caused a more pronounced decrease in the cell proliferation rate. The cell proliferation rates observed after addition of SAEs at 5.5 mM, relative to cells cultured without SAE addition (defined as 100%), were: SAE-1 (150%) > SAE-2 (140%) > SAE-3a (120%) > SAE-4a (80%) > SAE-5a (20%). Furthermore, SAE-5a, which possesses the alkyl group with the highest carbon number, exhibited strong CYT.

All differences were statistically significant (p < 0.01 or p < 0.05), except between SAE-1 and SAE-2 (p > 0.05). Effect of SAEs with a branched alkyl ester group on fibroblast proliferation

Figure 3 shows changes in cell proliferation rates for SAEs with alkyl groups having the same carbon number in the ester moiety. At low concentrations (0.275–0.825 mM), cell proliferation increases similarly to SAEs with linear alkyl groups, but decreases as the added concentration increases. However, the decrease in cell proliferation rate (i.e., CYT) with increasing concentration is less pronounced for isomers with branched carbon chains: 3a ≥ 3b, 4a > 4c > 4b, 5a > 5c > 5b. This order largely corresponds to the hydrophobicity (membrane permeability) indicated by the ClogP values for SAEs shown in Table 1. These results suggest a strong correlation between the effect of SAEs on cell proliferation rate and their membrane permeability.

SAEs-induced cell proliferation activity (PRA) and CYT

Several ascorbic acid derivatives exhibit increased hydrophobicity through the introduction of long-chain alkyl groups, thereby facilitating cellular uptake of ascorbic acid31. This approach is conceptually related to prodrug development in pharmaceutical research45. Oseltamivir, an influenza agent synthesized from SA, is also an example of a prodrug20,21. However, in the case of SAEs, as observed for SAE-4a and SAE-5a, higher cell membrane permeability is associated with increased CYT at higher concentrations. These findings indicate that cell proliferation efficiency is not determined solely by the membrane permeability of SAEs.

Correlation between cell proliferation rate at 5.5 mM SAE concentration and ClogP values for SAEs and ALs

To clarify the cause of the marked decrease in cell proliferation rate (i.e., CYT) observed with increasing concentrations of SAE-4 and SAE-5, the cell proliferation rates measured at an SAE concentration of 5.5 mM were plotted against the ClogP values for SAEs or the corresponding ALs to examine potential correlations. For SAEs with linear alkyl groups (SAE-1, SAE-2, SAE-3a, SAE-4a, and SAE-5a), the plots showed excellent agreement with a quadratic regression curve, with coefficients of determination (r2) of 0.9773 for SAEs and 0.9812 for ALs, as shown in Fig. 4. Even when SAEs with branched alkyl groups (SAE-3b, SAE-4b, SAE-4c, SAE-5b, and SAE-5c) were included, the plots still showed good correlation with a quadratic approximation curve, as shown in Fig. 5, with r2 values of 0.9525 for SAEs and 0.958 for ALs. These results indicate that both the proliferation-promoting effect and CYT associated with alkyl ester substitution of SA correlate strongly with the chemical properties of the corresponding ALs.

CYT evaluation of ALs with linear alkyl groups

To evaluate the CYT of ALs with linear alkyl groups (AL-1, AL-2, AL-3a, AL-4a, and AL-5a), cell viability was measured at different AL concentrations (see Fig. 6a). As shown in Fig. 6b, plotting cell viability at 100 mM of ALs against the ClogP values for ALs showed a clear correlation with a quadratic regression curve (r2 = 0.9536), which is similar to that observed in Fig. 4b. These results indicate that the decrease in cell proliferation rate observed at an SAE concentration of 5.5 mM is attributable to the CYT of the corresponding ALs. However, compared to the cytotoxic effects exhibited by SAEs at 5.5 mM, the concentrations at which ALs with linear alkyl groups induced CYT were approximately 5- to 10-fold higher. This difference may be attributable to greater cellular uptake of SAEs relative to the corresponding ALs, possibly facilitated by carboxylic acid ester transport mechanisms in the cell membrane, although this has not been directly demonstrated.

CYT evaluation of ALs with branched alkyl groups

Comparison of the CYT of AL isomers with branched alkyl groups and ALs with linear alkyl groups having the same carbon number revealed that ALs with linear alkyl groups exhibit higher CYT, as shown in Fig. 7. In addition, when focusing on the RO- group, isomers with a greater number of hydrogen atoms on the carbon atom directly bonded to the oxygen atom showed higher CYT, i.e., 3a (-OCH2-, ClogP = 0.294) > 3b (-OCH- = 0.074). When the number of hydrogen atoms on the carbon atom bonded to the oxygen atom was the same, the isomer with the greater number hydrogen atoms on the adjacent carbon atom exhibited higher CYT (see Table 1): 4a (-OCH2CH2- = 0.823 > 4c (-OCH2CH- = 0.693) > 4b (-OCH- = 0.603); 5a (-OCH2CH2CH2- = 1.352) > 5c (-OCH2CH2CH- = 1.222) > 5b (-OCH2C- = 1.092). These results show that ALs with higher cell membrane permeability are more cytotoxic.

However, the CYT of ALs with branched alkyl groups was considerably lower than that of the corresponding SAEs bearing the same branched alkyl groups. In particular, AL-5b exhibited markedly lower cytotoxicity. This difference may be attributable to greater steric hindrance of ALs relative to SAEs with the same substituents during cell membrane penetration, and entry into the cytoplasm46, thereby reducing AL membrane permeability. Accordingly, carboxylic acid ester transport mechanisms in the cell membrane may facilitate SAE uptake, even in the presence of steric bulk comparable to that of the corresponding ALs, although direct experimental evidence for this mechanism is lacking.

Docking poses of SAEs to CES and binding energies of their complexes

SAEs that permeate into the cytoplasm are recognized xenobiotic substances in vivo47. Therefore, SAEs are expected to be rapidly hydrolyzed into SA and ALs by CES. Three-dimensional structures of two CES isoforms (hCES1 and hCES2) present in human dermal fibroblasts were predicted from their gene sequences48–50. For hCES1, the predicted structure was confirmed to be consistent with reported X-ray crystallographic data51. Docking simulations were performed to obtain optimal binding poses between all SAEs examined in this study and CES, and the corresponding binding energies were compared (see Fig. 8).

Binding energies and representative docking poses of SAEs with carboxylesterase (CES). (a) Predicted binding energies for hCES1 (black bars) and hCES2 (gray bars). Data are presented as the mean ± SD. The symbols (R) and (S) for SAE-4b denote enantiomeric forms. (b) Representative docking pose of SAE-5a within the active site of hCES1, corresponding to the lowest docking energy (− 8.64 kcal/mol). (c) Enlarged view of the docking site shown in panel (b). Hydrogen bonds with Gly143 and Ser221 in hCES1 are indicated by cyan dashed lines.

To evaluate the binding affinities of SAEs, we used benzoic acid, the cocrystallized ligand in the 1YAJ structure, as a reference ligand. The docking energies of benzoic acid were − 4.466 ± 0.059 kcal/mol and − 4.416 ± 0.265 kcal/mol for the AlphaFold2-predicted hCES1 and hCES2 models, respectively. Comparison with these reference scores suggests that the alkyl shikimates exhibit binding affinity comparable to that of benzoic acid. All predicted binding energies were below –5 kcal/mol (Fig. 8a) for both hCES1 and hCES2, suggesting that the SAEs bind effectively to both isoforms. As the alkyl chain length increased up to SAE-4, binding affinity increased, as reflected by decreasing binding energies. Notably, SAE-4 exhibited binding affinity comparable to that of SAE-5.

To investigate the effect of chirality within the alkyl group, docking simulations were performed for the chiral isomers of SAE-4b, denoted as (R) and (S). The nearly identical binding energies of these enantiomers indicate that chirality does not significantly affect the stability of the CES-SAE complex. Similarly, differences in alkyl group architecture (linear vs. branched) resulted in no significant difference in docking energies with CES for the SAE-3, SAE-4, and SAE-5 series.

The representative docking pose of SAE-5a (Fig. 8b, c) illustrates that the binding pocket has sufficient volume and a hydrophobic environment to accommodate various alkyl moieties. The stability of the hCES1-SAE-5a complex is further supported by two hydrogen bonds, in which the ester carbonyl oxygen interacts with the backbone amide of Gly143, and a hydroxyl group of the shikimic acid moiety forms a hydrogen bond with the side chain of the catalytic residue Ser22151. Given the structural conservation of the binding pocket in hCES2, these results suggest that SAEs form stable complexes with both CES isoforms.

Predicted action scheme of SAEs inducing cell proliferation

We hypothesized that cell membrane permeable SAEs interact with CES, which may hydrolyze SAEs to generate SA and ALs intracellularly (see Fig. 9). No significant differences in binding energy were observed among SAEs forming complexes with CES, regardless of substituent structure (binding energy, ΔG = –7 to –8 kcal/mol; see Fig. 8). On this basis, all SAEs transported into cells may be efficiently hydrolyzed into SA and ALs. The regenerated SA may promote cell proliferation, whereas ALs may exert cytotoxic effects depending on their intrinsic toxicity (see Figs. 6 and 7). At low SAE concentrations, the PRA induced by SA may exceed the CYT of ALs (PRA > CYT), resulting in comparable PRA for all SAEs (see Fig. 2). If intracellular AL concentrations increase with increasing SAE concentrations, the balance may shift to PRA ≈ CYT or PRA < CYT, depending on the CYT of the corresponding ALs. Since AL-1 and AL-2 exhibit relatively low CYT (Fig. 6a), cell proliferation would be observed across the SAE concentration range of 0.275–5.5 mM. The slight difference in cell PRA between SAE-1 and SAE-2 (see Fig. 2) is likely attributable to a small difference in CYT between AL-1 and AL-2 (see Fig. 6a).

Predicted action scheme of SAE-induced cell proliferation in skin fibroblasts mediated by docking to carboxylesterase (CES).

The maximum concentration of SAEs used in the cell proliferation experiments (5.5 mM) was considerably lower than the concentration at which CYT of the corresponding ALs was observed (see Figs. 2, 3, 6a, and 7). Although no significant differences were observed in the ClogP values between SAEs and ALs bearing the same alkyl groups (see Table 1), the concentrations required for AL-induced CYT were markedly higher than those for SAEs. These results suggest that SAEs possess physicochemical properties that facilitate cell membrane penetration. Specifically, the alkyl ester moiety of SAEs may be transported across the cell membrane via carboxylic acid ester transport mechanisms present in the membrane, although direct experimental evidence for this mechanism is lacking.

SA, which lacks a carboxylic acid ester structure, exhibits high hydrophilicity and does not appear to readily permeate cell membranes. The slight CYT observed with increasing SA concentrations may arise from a modest increase in culture medium acidity attributable to the carboxyl group (-COOH) of SA. In fact, the pH decreased slightly from 7.88 for 1% FBS medium to 7.11 for medium containing 5.5 mM SA. The absence of dedicated alcohol transporters in the cell membrane suggests that ALs (3a, 4a, and 5a) with higher carbon numbers and greater hydrophobicity enter cells primarily by passive diffusion. Consequently, membrane penetration rates of ALs are lower than those of SAEs, and CYT is unlikely to occur unless extracellular alcohol concentrations greatly exceed intracellular levels. Furthermore, increased steric bulk of the alkyl groups may reduce membrane permeability, and ALs with branched alkyl groups therefore appear to exhibit lower CYT than those with straight-chain alkyl groups (CYT order: 3a ≫ 3b, 4a > 4c > 4b, 5a > 5c ≫ 5b).

In humans, 22 fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) have been identified and reported to play important roles in the proliferation and differentiation of various cells and tissues52. Intracellularly generated SA may therefore promote FGF production. The results of this study are expected to stimulate further research on SAE-induced FGF gene expression.

Effects of plant-derived polar extracts containing SA and chlorogenic acid (CA) on cell proliferation

Antognoni et al. reported that polar extracts derived from the berry-like fruits of Hypericum androsaemum L., containing SA (10.8–11.6%) and CA (4.6–5.7%) as major components, exhibited PRA in human dermal fibroblasts14. Based on the extract concentrations used in their experiments, the corresponding concentrations of SA and CA were estimated to be 0.054–23.2 μg/mL and 0.023–11.4 μg/mL, respectively. However, the significant cell proliferation effects reported were limited to low extract concentrations (0.5, 1, and 10 μg/mL) and short incubation times (6 and 12 h), relative to a negative control in which fibroblasts were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium supplemented with 15% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, penicillin (100 U/mL), 2 mM L-glutamine, and streptomycin (100 mg/mL). It was also reported that the proliferative effect decreased with increasing incubation time, and that marked toxicity was observed at an extract concentration of 200 μg/mL (data not shown in the original report). Given that no cell proliferation experiments using purified SA and CA standards were reported, it remains unclear whether SA and CA themselves directly induce cell proliferation.

In our experiments, the added concentration of SA ranged from 0.275 to 5.5 mM (48–958 μg/mL), and cell proliferation rates were measured relative to an untreated control in which fibroblasts were cultured in 1% FBS medium (i.e., two-fold dilution of FibroLife S2 Comp Kit medium with FibroLife Basal Medium). After 48 h of culture, sufficient time for cell division, SA exhibited no cell PRA. We also examined the PRA of CA at concentrations ranging from 0.275 to 5.5 mM (97–1949 μg/mL) in parallel with SA for comparison. As shown in Fig. 10, CA exhibited strong CYT across this concentration range. In experiments using plant-derived extracts, the influence of unidentified components other than SA and CA cannot be excluded, as these components account for approximately 80% of the aqueous extract and approximately 85% of the methanolic extract. Under our experimental conditions, reliable measurements could not be obtained at lower concentrations or shorter incubation times. Collectively, these results show that neither SA nor CA exhibits significant cell PRA within the concentration examined.

Cell proliferation rate (%) as a function of the concentration of chlorogenic acid (CA) and SA (mM). Data represent the mean ± SD of results obtained from three independent experiments (n = 3).

Conclusions

Highly hydrophilic SA does not readily permeate cell membranes and therefore does not induce fibroblast proliferation. Conversely, SAEs, which possess increased hydrophobicity, may permeate cell membranes via ester transport mechanisms and be hydrolyzed into SA and ALs by intracellular carboxylesterases (hCES1 and hCES2). The data suggest that intracellularly generated SA promotes fibroblast proliferation. Quantum mechanical calculations were used to predict the three-dimensional structures of hCES1 and hCES2, and docking simulations of SAE-CES complexes revealed no significant differences in binding energies attributable to alkyl chain length or steric hindrance. The observed differences in SAE-induced cell proliferation rates are therefore most plausibly explained by variations in the CYT of ALs generated intracellularly during SAE hydrolysis. The present findings suggest that SA, which does not exhibit cell PRA in the extracellular environment, may exert biological activity as a proliferation-promoting biomolecule if delivered intracellularly as a membrane-permeable ester derivative and subsequently hydrolyzed. These results may provide new insights into the physiological functions of SA. The use of SAEs as proliferative agents may contribute to further research, including in pharmaceutical development and cosmetic applications53.
