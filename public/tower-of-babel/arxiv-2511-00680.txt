Accelerating Trust-Region Methods: An Attempt to Balance Global and Local Efficiency
Yuntian Jiang, Chuwen Zhang, Bo Jiang, Yinyu Ye

Abstract
Historically speaking, it is hard to balance the global and local efficiency of second-order optimization algorithms. For instance, the classical Newton’s method possesses excellent local convergence but lacks global guarantees, often exhibiting divergence when the starting point is far from the optimal solution [ 41 , 16 ] . In contrast, accelerated second-order methods offer strong global convergence guarantees, yet they tend to converge with slower local rate [ 7 , 11 , 27 ] . Existing second-order methods struggle to balance global and local performance, leaving open the question of how much we can globally accelerate the second-order methods while maintaining excellent local convergence guarantee. In this paper, we tackle this challenge by proposing for the first time the accelerated trust-region-type methods, and leveraging their unique primal-dual information. Our primary technical contribution is Accelerating with Local Detection , which utilizes the Lagrange multiplier to detect local regions and achieves a global complexity of O ~ ( ϵ − 1 / 3 ) \tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-1/3}) , while maintaining quadratic local convergence. We further explore the trade-off when pushing the global convergence to the limit. In particular, we propose the Accelerated Trust-Region Extragradient Method that has a global near-optimal rate of O ~ ( ϵ − 2 / 7 ) \tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-2/7}) but loses the quadratic local convergence. This reveals a phase transition in accelerated trust-region type methods: the excellent local convergence can be maintained when achieving a moderate global acceleration but becomes invalid when pursuing the extreme global efficiency. Numerical experiments further confirm the results indicated by our convergence analysis.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the unconstrained convex optimization problem

f^{*}=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x),

(1.1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex function.
When the Hessian of the objective is available, second-order methods (SOMs) are particularly effective for solving (
1.1
), especially when high-accuracy solutions are desired. Their power stems from a central property: Newton’s method achieves quadratic convergence in a neighborhood of any non-degenerate local minimum
[
49
,
41
,
47

. Many SOMs are thus designed to approximate the Newton direction, allowing them to inherit its excellent local convergence behavior.

In addition to local convergence, another important criterion for evaluating the effectiveness of optimization methods is their non-asymptotic global oracle complexity, which refers to the number of second-order subproblems (the oracle calls) needed to find an
\epsilon
-approximate solution
[
10
]
. Over the past decades, a few SOMs have been developed with such emphases
[
46
,
38
,
32
,
23
]
. In convex optimization, these methods can be further improved through acceleration techniques.

In this context, two major approaches have emerged. The first approach, accelerated cubic regularized Newton (CRN) method
[
42
]
, achieves a global oracle complexity of
O(\epsilon^{-1/3})
based on the cubic regularization oracle
[
46
]
. A different strategy is adopted in the accelerated Newton proximal extragradient (A-NPE) method
[
39
]
, which improves the oracle complexity to
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-2/7})
and is compatible with second-order oracles that satisfy certain error bound conditions
[
7
]
.
These developments, largely based on the estimating sequence technique
[

,

[

,

,

,

,

,

,
26
,
17
]
.
Recently, the oracle complexity of the A-NPE method was improved to be an optimal rate in
O(\epsilon^{-2/7})

[

,
33
]
. These acceleration techniques are broadly applicable and are by no means limited to the domain of SOMs
[
56
,
57
,
34
,
19
]
.

However, it is hard to balance the global and local efficiency of second-order optimization algorithms. For instance,
the classical Newton’s method possesses excellent local convergence but lacks global guarantees, often exhibiting divergence when the starting point is far from the optimal solution
[
41
,
16
]
. In contrast, stronger worst-case complexity results of accelerated SOMs often come at the cost of local efficiency.
Empirical evidence shows that in many tasks like logistic regression, accelerated SOMs are often outperformed by unaccelerated ones

How much can we accelerate SOMs globally while maintaining their excellent local convergence?

1.1
Motivation and our approach

It is folklore that the strong local performance of SOMs ultimately stems from the Newton step. Thus, to design an accelerated method with excellent local performance, the second-order oracle should have the ability to detect the local geometry near the optimal solution, providing a signal indicating that the iterates may have
entered the quadratic convergence region, allowing a Newton step to be active.
For that sake, one particular choice could be the trust-region (TR) oracle
[
40
,
12
]
. At a given point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the classic TR oracle solves the following subproblem:

\displaystyle\underset{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\min}

\displaystyle\nabla f(x)^{T}d+\tfrac{1}{2}d^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x)d

(1.2)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|d\|\leq r,

where
r
denotes the trust-region radius. We denote
(d,\lambda)=\textrm{TR}(x,r)
as the primal-dual solution to this problem. For an overview of TR methods, we refer readers to two excellent monographs
[
61
,
62
]
.

Two features make (
1.2
) particularly appealing. Near a non-degenerate minimizer, the step reduces to the pure Newton direction once the ball constraint becomes inactive, preserving quadratic convergence, which is the capability absent from other second-order oracles.
Moreover, TR oracles can be solved in
O(n^{3}+n^{2}\log\log(\epsilon^{-1}))
arithmetic operations
[
54
,
59
]
, incurring no significant overhead compared to solving a Newton equation.
On the practical side, the TR oracle has been thoroughly analyzed
[
31
,
51
,
24
,
55
]
, so that highly competitive solvers have been developed
[
51
,
2
,
1
]
for it and its extensions.
To this end, TR oracles have formed the algorithmic core of general-purpose nonlinear programming solvers such as Knitro
[
6
]
, COPT
[
18
]
, and PDFO
[
50
]
. Similar success can be found in other cross-cutting applications ranging from adversarial training
[
58
]
to reinforcement learning
[
52
]
. These advantages serve as the motivation to use TR to address the global-local balance in accelerated SOMs.

However, to our best knowledge, TR methods have not been accelerated yet as
using TR oracles introduces technical challenges. Classical TR methods cannot match the global oracle complexity of other unaccelerated SOMs
[
8
,
13
]
because the dual variable
\lambda
associated with the ball constraint in (
1.2
) is determined a posteriori and requires careful monitoring
[
14
]
. Achieving global acceleration demands relative stability between this multiplier and the step size, which roughly means that
\lambda
should not vary too fast relative to the step size, but enforcing such stability can interfere with transitions to Newton steps near the optimum.

To address this challenge, we design, for the first time, the accelerated trust-region methods that leverage a modified TR oracle, which we call the trust-region oracle for acceleration denoted as
(TR+)
. In particular, at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
(TR+)
oracle solves

\displaystyle\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\nabla f(x)^{\top}d+\tfrac{1}{2}d^{\top}(\nabla^{2}f(x)+\sigma I)d,

(TR
+
)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|d\|\leq r.

The dual variable associated with the ball constraint is denoted by
\lambda
, which can detect local geometric information of the optimal solution.
The primal regularization
\sigma
in
(TR+)
is used to effectively modulate
\lambda
, and maintains the stability required for global acceleration, enabling our accelerated trust-region method to systematically balance fast local convergence with provable global guarantee.

1.2
Contribution

This work provides an answer to the question posed earlier: we demonstrate that the compatibility between the global and local efficiency of the SOMs depends on the degree of global acceleration.
Specifically, based on the
(TR+)
oracles
[
32
]
, we develop two accelerated TR-type methods for problem (
1.1
). One can simultaneously achieve both global acceleration and quadratic local convergence, and the other has a faster global convergence rate but loses local efficiency.

The first method,
Accelerated Trust-Region Method with Local Detection
(
Algorithm

1
), implements a local detection mechanism (
1
) that automatically identifies and “dives into” the quadratic convergence regions. This design yields an improved global worst-case oracle complexity of
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-1/3})
, while achieving a local quadratic rate of convergence. To our best knowledge, this is the first accelerated SOM that provably achieves both
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-1/3})
global oracle complexity and local quadratic convergence.

The second method,
Accelerated Trust-Region Extragradient Method
(
Algorithm

2
), is designed to persuit the limits of global performance, reaching the near-optimal global oracle complexity of
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-2/7})
, but loses the excellent local convergence. Thus, our analysis and experiments reveal a phase transition in accelerated trust-region type methods: pushing global efficiency to its limits naturally entails a trade-off with local performance.
Such a phase transition occurs due to the loss of local detection in
Algorithm

2
when pursuing the near-optimal global convergence rate,
where the primal regularizer
\sigma
is set prior to determining the extrapolation point that is used in the
(TR+)
oracle (as in
Algorithm

1
).

Our theoretical findings are corroborated by numerical experiments. On a global scale, all accelerated SOMs, including ours and
[
42
,
39
]
, consistently outperform the non-accelerated trust-region method
[
32
]
and cubic Newton method
[
46
]
. Locally,
Algorithm

1
exhibits quadratic convergence, and
Algorithm

2
cannot retain superlinear convergence near the solution, similar to the empirical conclusions in
[
7
,
30
,
26
]
. These findings again confirm the necessity of local detection.

1.3
Related works

We review related works along two main lines: TR-type methods and accelerated SOMs that aim to achieve faster-than-sublinear efficiency. Despite the strong empirical success of TR-type methods, its theoretical analysis mainly focuses on nonconvex problems
[
60
,
13
,
15
,
63
,
21
,
20
,
22
]
, while
its global complexity analysis for convex problems remains incomplete.
In fact, in convex optimization, the classical TR has worse convergence rates than other unaccelerated mainstream SOMs. This gap was recently closed in
[
32
]
. However, whether TR oracles can further benefit from acceleration, as achieved for other SOMs, remains an open question.

As discussed earlier, unaccelerated SOMs often exhibit superior local performance near the optimal solution compared with their accelerated counterparts. A common remedy for this issue is to employ restart strategies in accelerated frameworks
[
44
]
. However, this introduces a new challenge of deciding when to restart since the optimal restart frequency depends on unknown, problem-specific parameters. In contrast, when stronger global regularity conditions (akin to strong convexity) are imposed on the objective function, the restart schedule can be determined in a more structured form
[
48
,
35
,
25
]
or even becomes unnecessary
[
29
,
37
]
. Under such assumptions, these methods can accommodate global performance, and thus the intrinsic trade-off between global convergence and local efficiency becomes a secondary concern.

Lemma B.2
.

Let

satisfy

and

. Define

(B.5)

0<\sigma\leq\bar{\sigma}
, then we have

\left(\frac{\sigma}{\bar{\sigma}}\right)^{2}\psi(\sigma,y)\leq\psi(\bar{\sigma},y)\leq\frac{\sigma}{\bar{\sigma}}\psi(\sigma,y).

(C.3)

Proof.

Note that

\sigma\psi(\sigma,y)=\left\|\left(\nabla^{2}f(y)+\sigma I\right)^{-1}\nabla f(y)\right\|.

Since
\nabla^{2}f(y)+\bar{\sigma}I\succeq\nabla^{2}f(y)+\sigma I\succ 0
, we have

\sigma\psi(\sigma,y)\geq\bar{\sigma}\psi(\bar{\sigma},y),

which is the second argument. Similarly,

\sigma^{2}\psi(\sigma,y)=\left\|\left(\frac{\nabla^{2}f(y)}{\sigma}+I\right)^{-1}\nabla f(y)\right\|,

since
\frac{1}{\sigma}\nabla^{2}f(y)+I\succeq\frac{1}{\bar{\sigma}}\nabla^{2}f(y)+I\succ I
, we have
\sigma^{2}\psi(\sigma,y)\leq\bar{\sigma}^{2}\psi(\bar{\sigma},y)
, which finished the proof.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Suppose Assumption
2.2
holds, for any
y,\bar{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\sigma>0
, then

\left|\psi(\sigma,y)-\psi(\sigma,\bar{y})\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\|y-\bar{y}\|+\frac{M}{\sigma^{2}}\|y-\bar{y}\|^{2}+\frac{2M}{\sigma}\|y-\bar{y}\|r,

(C.4)

where
\delta:=\min\{\psi(\sigma,\bar{y}),\psi(\sigma,y)\}
. Further, we have

\psi(\sigma,y)\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\|y-\bar{y}\|+\frac{M}{\sigma^{2}}\|y-\bar{y}\|^{2}+\left(\frac{2M}{\sigma}\|y-\bar{y}\|+1\right)\psi(\sigma,\bar{y}).

(C.5)

Proof.

We denote

\displaystyle x=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ \nabla f(y)^{T}(x-y)+\frac{1}{2}(x-y)\nabla^{2}f(y)(x-y)+\frac{\sigma}{2}\|x-y\|^{2},

\displaystyle\bar{x}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ \nabla f(\bar{y})^{T}(x-\bar{y})+\frac{1}{2}(x-\bar{y})\nabla^{2}f(\bar{y})(x-\bar{y})+\frac{\sigma}{2}\|x-\bar{y}\|^{2},

the optimality conditions of the above are

\displaystyle\nabla f(y)+\nabla^{2}f(y)(x-y)+\sigma(x-y)=0,

\displaystyle\nabla f(\bar{y})+\nabla^{2}f(\bar{y})(\bar{x}-\bar{y})+\sigma(\bar{x}-\bar{y})=0.

Denote

\displaystyle v=\nabla f(y)+\nabla^{2}f(y)(x-y),

\displaystyle\bar{v}=\nabla f(\bar{y})+\nabla^{2}f(\bar{y})(\bar{x}-\bar{y}),

\displaystyle u=\nabla f(\bar{y})+\nabla^{2}f(\bar{y})(x-\bar{y}).

Plugging them into the optimality conditions gives

\displaystyle v+\sigma(x-y)=0,

(C.6)

\displaystyle\bar{v}+\sigma(\bar{x}-\bar{y})=0,

(C.7)

\displaystyle\left|\psi(\sigma,y)-\psi(\sigma,\bar{y})\right|=\left|\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\|v\|-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\|\bar{v}\|\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\|v-\bar{v}\|.

(C.8)

Subtracting (
C.7
) from (
C.6
), we have

\sigma(x-\bar{x})+v-\bar{v}=\sigma(y-\bar{y}).

Plus both sides by
u
, we have

\sigma(x-\bar{x})+u-\bar{v}=\sigma(y-\bar{y})+u-v.

It is easy to show that

\langle x-\bar{x},u-\bar{v}\rangle=\langle x-\bar{x},\nabla^{2}f(\bar{y})(x-\bar{x})\rangle\geq 0,

thus from triangle inequality, we have

\|u-\bar{v}\|\leq\|\sigma(x-\bar{x})+u-\bar{v}\|\leq\sigma\|y-\bar{y}\|+\|u-v\|,

hence

\|v-\bar{v}\|\leq\sigma\|y-\bar{y}\|+2\|u-v\|.

(C.9)

Now we bound
\|u-v\|
,

\displaystyle\|u-v\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\nabla f(\bar{y})+\nabla^{2}f(\bar{y})(x-\bar{y})-\left(\nabla f(y)+\nabla^{2}f(y)(x-y)\right)\right\|

(C.10)

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\nabla f(\bar{y})+\nabla^{2}f(\bar{y})(y-\bar{y})-\nabla f(y)\right)+\left(\nabla^{2}f(\bar{y})(x-y)-\nabla^{2}f(y)(x-y)\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left(\nabla f(\bar{y})+\nabla^{2}f(\bar{y})(y-\bar{y})-\nabla f(y)\right)\right\|+\left\|\left(\nabla^{2}f(\bar{y})-\nabla^{2}f(y)\right)\left(x-y\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{2}\|y-\bar{y}\|^{2}+M\|y-\bar{y}\|\|x-y\|

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{2}\|y-\bar{y}\|^{2}+\sigma M\|y-\bar{y}\|\psi(\sigma,y).

Plugging (
C.9
) and (
C.10
) into (
C.8
), we have

\left|\psi(\sigma,y)-\psi(\sigma,\bar{y})\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\|y-\bar{y}\|+\frac{M}{\sigma^{2}}\|y-\bar{y}\|^{2}+\frac{2M}{\sigma}\|y-\bar{y}\|\psi(\sigma,y).

Similarly, we can prove

\left|\psi(\sigma,y)-\psi(\sigma,\bar{y})\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\|y-\bar{y}\|+\frac{M}{\sigma^{2}}\|y-\bar{y}\|^{2}+\frac{2M}{\sigma}\|y-\bar{y}\|\psi(\sigma,\bar{y}).

Hence we have proved (
C.4
), by applying triangle inequality, we have (
C.5
).
∎

Lemma C.3

Proof.

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{A+a(\sigma)}x+\frac{a(\sigma)}{A+a(\sigma)}v

\displaystyle=x+\tau(\sigma)(v-x),

Its derivative is

\tau^{\prime}(\sigma)=-\frac{2A}{\sqrt{1+4A\sigma}\left(1+\sqrt{1+4A\sigma}\right)^{2}},

therefore for all
\sigma>0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\tau^{\prime}(\sigma)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2A}{\left(1+\sqrt{1+4A\sigma}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2A}{4A\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma}.

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\|y(s)-y(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\tau^{\prime}(\xi)\right|(s-t)\|x-v\|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\tau^{\prime}(\xi)\right|(s-t)\left(\|x-x^{*}\|+\|v-x^{*}\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2M_{0}\left|\tau^{\prime}(\xi)\right|(s-t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M_{0}}{t}(s-t).
