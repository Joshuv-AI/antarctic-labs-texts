MitraClip procedure in patients with secondary mitral regurgitation: insights from Turkish experience
Authors: Zeynettin Kaya, Fuat Polat, İsmail Ateş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSecondary mitral regurgitation (SMR) in patients with heart failure is associated with poor prognosis. Mitral Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) has become a groundbreaking treatment option for high-risk patients ineligible for conventional surgery, with the MitraClip system standing as the most widely validated and utilized device in this field.AimThis study aimed to evaluate the immediate procedural outcomes and long-term clinical results of MitraClip interventions in Turkish patients with secondary mitral regurgitation. The primary objectives were to determine the rate of procedural success according to standardized Mitral Valve Academic Research Consortium (MVARC) criteria and to assess the proportion of patients achieving optimal mitral regurgitation reduction (MR ≤ 1+) at hospital discharge. The secondary objectives were to identify independent clinical, echocardiographic, and procedural predictors associated with rehospitalization for heart failure and all-cause mortality during follow-up, and to evaluate the durability of mitral regurgitation reduction and functional improvement over the medium-term follow-up period.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included 140 Turkish patients with moderate-to-severe (grade 3+) or severe (grade 4+) SMR who underwent MitraClip treatment between 2021 and 2024, with a median follow-up of 23.5 months (interquartile range [IQR]: 14.0–27.0). MR severity was assessed by comprehensive echocardiographic evaluation according to current guidelines, and post-procedure MR was classified as ≤ 1 + or > 1 + for outcome analyses.ResultsThe study population had a median age of 75.0 (IQR: 68.0–78.0) years, with 42.9% being female. Procedural success according to MVARC criteria was achieved in 137 patients (97.9%), while MR ≤ 1 + at discharge was observed in 95 patients (67.9%). During follow-up, 56.4% of patients required rehospitalization, and the mortality rate was 7.1%. Significant improvement in functional capacity was observed, with 88.6% of patients improving by at least one New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class at 1-month follow-up (from 100% NYHA class III-IV at baseline to 11.4% class III, 70.0% class II, and 18.6% class I post-procedure). Multivariate analysis revealed that estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (Hazard ratio [HR] 0.95, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.91–0.99, p = 0.03), atrial fibrillation (HR 1.62, 95% CI: 1.02–2.57, p = 0.04), and post-procedure MR severity (MR grade > 1 + vs. ≤1+) (HR 1.73, 95% CI: 1.13–2.65, p = 0.01) were associated with rehospitalization. Mortality risk was associated with lower hemoglobin (HR 0.92, 95% CI: 0.86–0.99, p = 0.04), and severe MR (HR 2.54, 95% CI: 1.09–5.92, p = 0.03).ConclusionsMitraClip intervention demonstrated high procedural success rates in Turkish patients with SMR, leading to significant reduction in MR severity and improvement in NYHA functional class. While rehospitalization and mortality rates aligned with previous studies, specific clinical factors were identified as being associated with clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05298-z.

Introduction

Mitral regurgitation is the most prevalent valvular heart disease in Europe and North America, with its incidence increasing with age [1]. Among individuals over 75 years old, the prevalence of moderate-to-severe/severe secondary mitral regurgitation (SMR) reaches 11% [2]. SMR, which results from left ventricular or atrial dilation rather than intrinsic valve pathology, affects 25–30% of patients with reduced ejection fraction and independently predicts heart failure hospitalizations and mortality [2, 3]. Prolonged volume overload leads to irreversible cardiac chamber dilation and dysfunction, underscoring the need for timely intervention.

Although surgical repair or replacement is the standard treatment for mitral regurgitation, many patients with SMR are deemed ineligible due to high surgical risk, advanced age, or significant comorbidities [3]. This clinical need has driven rapid adoption of transcatheter mitral interventions, particularly mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER). The Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Transcatheter Valve Therapy (TVT) Registry reported a nearly 10-fold increase in M-TEER procedures from 2014 to 2020, reflecting improvements in patient selection, operator expertise, and device technology [4]. Both American and European guidelines now recommend M-TEER as a viable treatment option for high-risk patients with SMR [3, 5].

The MitraClip device (Abbott Laboratories, Menlo Park, California, USA) is the most extensively studied M-TEER system, replicating the surgical Alfieri technique by approximating the mitral leaflets to reduce regurgitation [6, 7]. Receiving FDA approval in 2013, MitraClip’s efficacy has been established through multiple randomized trials [8–13]. The EVEREST II trial demonstrated that while surgical repair achieved superior mitral regurgitation reduction, MitraClip offered a safer alternative with durable long-term results [10, 11]. Subsequent trials specifically in SMR yielded contrasting results: MITRA-FR showed no benefit over guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) alone [11], while the landmark COAPT trial demonstrated that MitraClip combined with GDMT significantly reduced hospitalizations and improved survival, with 5-year follow-up confirming sustained benefit [12, 13]. The recent RESHAPE-HF2 trial (2024) further validated these findings, showing significant reductions in recurrent heart failure hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality [15]. Contemporary data from the EXPAND-G4 trial demonstrated sustained MR reduction to ≤ 1 + in over 90% of patients at 1 year with fourth-generation devices [14].

While these landmark trials have established M-TEER efficacy in controlled settings, real-world evidence from diverse geographic populations remains essential for validating findings across different healthcare systems and patient populations, particularly given known variations in patient characteristics, comorbidity patterns, and healthcare delivery models. This study evaluates the procedural outcomes and clinical results of MitraClip interventions in Turkish patients with SMR, contributing data from an underrepresented Asian-European population to the global evidence base.

Methods

Study design

This study was a retrospective, multicenter observational analysis conducted to evaluate the outcomes of the MitraClip procedure in patients with moderate-to-severe/severe SMR. The study was performed at 15 tertiary care centers specializing in structural heart disease interventions across Turkey (listed in Acknowledgments). Patient distribution across centers ranged from 4 to 18 patients per center (median: 9 patients, IQR: 6–12). Data were collected from patients undergoing MitraClip implantation between January 2021 and June 2024.

Data collection and baseline assessments

Comprehensive data were extracted from hospital records, including demographic information (age, gender), clinical history, and comorbidities (e.g., coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, hypertension). Baseline echocardiographic parameters included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP), and MR etiology (ventricular or atrial). Baseline laboratory findings such as hemoglobin levels, eGFR, and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels were also recorded. Chronic renal failure (CRF) was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for ≥ 3 months, calculated using the CKD-EPI equation. Patients on chronic renal replacement therapy were separately categorized. Details of prior cardiac interventions, including implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) implantation or cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), were documented. In patients without previously documented coronary artery disease or those presenting with symptoms suggestive of angina, coronary imaging, either by invasive coronary angiography or coronary CT angiography, or non-invasive functional testing for ischemia was performed prior to M-TEER to evaluate for active ischemia. Ischemic etiology was defined based on a history of myocardial infarction, prior revascularization, or significant coronary artery disease documented through prior imaging.

Biomarker measurements

NT-proBNP was measured using automated electrochemiluminescence immunoassay methods (Roche Diagnostics, Cobas platform or equivalent) at each participating center’s central laboratory. NT-proBNP values were recorded in pg/mL. We specifically measured NT-proBNP rather than BNP (B-type natriuretic peptide) as NT-proBNP is the predominantly used biomarker in European clinical practice and has a longer half-life, making it more stable for serial measurements. Reference ranges for NT-proBNP vary by age and renal function, but values > 450 pg/mL for patients < 50 years, > 900 pg/mL for patients 50–75 years, and > 1800 pg/mL for patients > 75 years are generally considered indicative of heart failure according to ESC guidelines.

MitraClip procedure

The MitraClip procedure was performed under general anesthesia in a hybrid operating room equipped with fluoroscopy and transesophageal echocardiographic (TEE) capabilities. All procedures followed the standard M-TEER technique. After transseptal puncture and delivery system insertion, the MitraClip device (Abbott Laboratories) was advanced into the left atrium and positioned at the mitral valve. Precise leaflet grasping was confirmed using real-time TEE imaging. All procedures utilized the fourth-generation MitraClip G4 system, with either NT/NTW or XT/XTW clips, selected based on patient-specific anatomical and functional considerations.

The procedural objective was to achieve a reduction in MR severity to ≤ 2 + without significant mitral valve stenosis. Procedural parameters, including total procedure time, device implantation time, and number of clips deployed, were recorded.

Follow-up and outcomes

Patients underwent scheduled follow-up evaluations at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and annually thereafter, with a median final follow-up of 23.5 months (IQR: 14.0–27.0). Clinical follow-up included NYHA functional class assessment, rehospitalization events, and survival status. Echocardiographic follow-up at each time point assessed MR severity, LVEF, PASP, and MVA. MVA was calculated using the pressure half-time (PHT) method when feasible, with confirmation by direct planimetry in cases where leaflet morphology permitted accurate tracing. In patients with significant post-procedural mitral stenosis (mean gradient > 5 mmHg), continuity equation was additionally employed. Technical success was evaluated immediately post-procedure and at discharge, while functional status and MR grade durability were assessed at all subsequent follow-up visits.

Time-to-event secondary endpoints were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models to identify predictors of rehospitalization and mortality.

Safety outcomes included procedure-associated complications as defined by the MVARC criteria, including device-related complications, vascular access site and access-related complications, bleeding events, acute kidney injury, neurological complications, arrhythmias requiring intervention, and in-hospital mortality.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was designed to evaluate both primary procedural outcomes and secondary clinical endpoints. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, with normally distributed data presented as mean ± standard deviation and non-normal data as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. For our primary endpoints, procedural success (defined by MVARC criteria as MR reduction to ≤ 2 + without procedural mortality or urgent surgery) and MR ≤ 1 + at discharge were analyzed as binary outcomes using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, with predictors of optimal MR reduction (≤ 1+) identified through logistic regression analysis. To assess potential learning curve effects, we compared these outcomes between the first 70 patients (early experience) and subsequent 70 patients (later experience).

Univariate screening (p < 0.1) was followed by multivariate modeling with backward selection (retaining variables with p < 0.05), while checking for multicollinearity (excluding variables with VIF > 5). Longitudinal changes in functional status (NYHA class) and echocardiographic parameters were assessed using paired t-tests for normally distributed data or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normal distributions, with percentages calculated based on available follow-up data and appropriate censoring for mortality or loss to follow-up. For longitudinal parameters assessed at multiple timepoints (baseline, 1, 6, 12, and 23.5 months), we used repeated-measures analyses to evaluate changes over time. Friedman tests were employed for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and Cochran’s Q test was used for categorical variables (NYHA class, MR grade). When the overall test indicated significant changes over time (p < 0.05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons between consecutive timepoints were performed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. For normally distributed continuous variables assessed longitudinally, repeated-measures ANOVA was used with Greenhouse-Geisser correction when sphericity was violated, followed by Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc tests. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp.), with a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The research followed the ethical guidelines specified in the Declaration of Helsinki. There was no utilization of AI-powered tools such as Large Language Models (LLMs), chatbots, or image generators in developing this article. The study received approval from the ethics committee on July 08, 2024, with reference number 2024/07–1290, under the supervision of the Scientific Research Applications Review Commission. All patients provided written informed consent for the MitraClip procedure and the use of anonymized data for research purposes. Patient confidentiality was ensured by de-identifying all data during analysis.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study analyzed data from 140 patients, with a median age of 75.0 (IQR: 68.0–78.0) years, and 42.9% (n = 60) were female. All individuals were categorized as high-risk based on EuroSCORE II and STS Score assessments. According to the NYHA functional classification, all cases were in class III or IV, with 72.9% (n = 102) in class III. Loop diuretic use was universal, and the most frequent comorbidities included coronary artery disease (77.9%) and hypertension (72.9%). All enrolled patients had either moderate-to-severe/severe SMR as defined by European ESC/EACTS guidelines. MR severity was graded according to current ESC/EACTS guidelines, with moderate-to-severe defined as EROA ≥ 30 mm² and severe as EROA ≥ 40 mm². MR flow was central in 74.3% (n = 104) and eccentric in 25.7% (n = 36). Eccentric jets reflected asymmetric tethering from regional wall motion abnormalities, without intrinsic leaflet disease or mixed etiology. Concomitant tricuspid regurgitation (TR) was present in the majority of patients, with 72.1% (n = 101) having moderate TR and 5.0% (n = 7) having severe TR at baseline. Procedural characteristics

The median follow-up duration was 23.5 months (IQR: 14.0–27.0). The median total procedure time was 71 min (IQR: 42.5–105.5), and the median device implantation time was 28 min (IQR: 21.5–36.5). Procedures were performed using the fourth-generation MitraClip G4 system, with NT or NTW clips used in 32% (n = 42) and XT or XTW clips in 68% (n = 98). A single clip was used in 84 patients (60.0%), whereas two clips were used in 56 patients (40.0%).

Following the intervention, MR severity decreased by at least one grade in all cases. Post-procedure assessments identified 12 cases with no or trace MR, 83 with mild MR, and 45 with moderate MR.

Primary endpoints analysis

Procedural success rate

Procedural success according to MVARC criteria was achieved in 137 patients (97.9%, 95% CI: 94.2–99.5%). Three patients (2.1%) did not meet MVARC criteria: Two patients (1.4%) developed clinically significant pericardial effusion, one of whom required urgent cardiac intervention (accounting for procedural failure); the other was managed conservatively. No procedural mortality occurred in this cohort.

MR ≤ 1 + at discharge rate

MR reduction to ≤ 1 + at discharge was achieved in 95 patients (67.9%, 95% CI: 59.5–75.7%). Among these, 12 patients (8.6%) had no/trace MR and 83 patients (59.3%) had mild MR. The remaining 45 patients (32.1%) had moderate MR (grade 2+) at discharge.

Safety outcomes

Procedure-associated complications were observed in 15 patients (10.7%, 95% CI: 6.2–17.0%). According to MVARC safety endpoints, these included: access site complications in 7 patients (5.0%), with major vascular complications in 2 patients (1.4%) requiring surgical intervention; clinically significant pericardial effusion in 2 patients (1.4%); single-leaflet device attachment in 3 patients (2.1%) detected during follow-up echocardiography at 1, 6, and 12 months respectively. Unfortunately, detailed anatomical characteristics of these SLDA cases (such as leaflet morphology, calcification patterns, coaptation depth, or specific clip positioning) were not systematically documented in our retrospective data collection, which represents a limitation of our study; new-onset atrial fibrillation requiring intervention in 2 patients (1.4%); and acute kidney injury in 4 patients (2.9%), all recovering without need for dialysis. Blood transfusion was required in 5 patients (3.6%). No cases of device embolization, mitral valve stenosis, stroke, myocardial infarction, or urgent cardiac surgery were recorded. There was no in-hospital mortality, and all patients were discharged within a median of 3 days (IQR: 2–5) post-procedure.

Safety outcomes also improved over time, with procedure-associated complications decreasing from 14.3% (10/70) in the early group to 7.1% (5/70) in the later group (p = 0.046). The most notable improvements were seen in access site complications (7.1% vs. 2.9%, p = 0.042) and acute kidney injury (4.3% vs. 1.4%, p = 0.058).

Secondary endpoints analysis

Clinical and functional outcomes during follow-up

Patients were systematically assessed at predetermined intervals (1, 3, 6, 12, and 23.5 months) throughout the median follow-up period of 23.5 months (IQR: 14.0–27.0).

Significant improvements in functional capacity were evident by the first month, with 88.6% of patients improving by at least one NYHA class (McNemar’s test, p < 0.001). NYHA classification improved from 100% class III-IV at baseline (72.9% class III, 27.1% class IV) to 11.4% class III, 70.0% class II, and 18.6% class I at 1-month follow-up. These improvements remained stable throughout the follow-up period, with no significant difference between 1-month and final follow-up NYHA distributions (McNemar’s test, p = 0.25), demonstrating durability of functional benefit. All intermediate timepoints (3, 6, and 12 months) also showed highly significant improvements compared to baseline (all p < 0.001) with stable distributions compared to 1 month (all p > 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Durability of MR reduction

At final follow-up (n = 128), 64.8% of patients maintained MR ≤ 1 + grade (none/trace or mild), representing a modest decline from the 67.9% at 1-month post-procedure (Cochran’s Q test for overall change p < 0.001; post-hoc pairwise comparison between 1 month and final follow-up p = 0.37, indicating stable durability). The majority of patients (94.5%) maintained MR reduction to ≤ 2 + throughout the entire follow-up period (all pairwise comparisons versus baseline p < 0.001). While there was a modest increase in patients with MR grade > 1 + at final follow-up compared to 1 month post-procedure, only 6 patients (4.7%) experienced progression to moderate-to-severe SMR (3+), and no patients progressed to severe SMR (4+) during the entire 23.5-month median follow-up period (Fig. 3). Repeated-measures analysis confirmed no significant changes in MR distribution between consecutive follow-up timepoints after the initial 1-month improvement (all pairwise p > 0.05 from 1 month through final follow-up). The mean mitral valve gradient was 3.2 ± 1.1 mmHg, with MVA ≥ 1.5 cm² in all patients (median 2.1 cm², IQR: 1.8–2.4) at final follow-up. No patients developed hemodynamically significant stenosis (defined as MVA < 1.5 cm² with mean gradient ≥ 5 mmHg).

Other clinical parameters

Other echocardiographic, laboratory and clinical parameters showed measurable improvements that were maintained during follow-up. Repeated-measures analysis (Friedman test) demonstrated significant overall changes in LVEF over time (p < 0.001), with post-hoc testing showing significant improvement from baseline to 1 month (p = 0.021) and subsequent stability through final follow-up (all consecutive pairwise comparisons p > 0.05). LVEF increased modestly but significantly from 25.0 ± 4.2% at baseline to 29.2 ± 5.3% at final follow-up (mean difference + 4.2%, 95% CI: 2.8–5.6, Cohen’s d = 0.84), with the improvement evident by 1 month (26.3 ± 4.5%). While this change achieved statistical significance, the absolute improvement was modest, likely reflecting the advanced, irreversible nature of left ventricular remodeling in our population and underscoring that MitraClip primarily addresses the mechanical consequences of MR rather than reversing underlying cardiomyopathy.

PASP decreased significantly over time (Friedman test p < 0.001), with significant reduction from baseline to 1 month (p < 0.001) and stable values thereafter (consecutive comparisons p > 0.05), from 40.0 ± 6.8 mmHg at baseline to 32.5 ± 6.4 mmHg at final follow-up (mean difference − 7.5 mmHg, 95% CI: −9.2 to −5.8, Cohen’s d = 1.18), representing a clinically meaningful reduction in pulmonary pressures. Left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) decreased significantly over time (Friedman test p = 0.003), with the pattern of early improvement and subsequent stability mirroring other echocardiographic parameters.

NT-proBNP levels showed significant overall changes (Friedman test p < 0.001), with significant reductions from baseline to 1 month and maintained reductions through final follow-up (consecutive timepoint comparisons p > 0.05), from 3850 ± 2890 pg/mL at baseline to 2230 ± 2120 pg/mL at final follow-up (mean difference − 1620 pg/mL, 95% CI: −2180 to −1060, Cohen’s d = 0.72), representing a 42% reduction. Quality of life as measured by the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ) improved dramatically (repeated-measures ANOVA p < 0.001), with significant improvement from baseline to 1 month (p < 0.001) and stable scores thereafter, from 54.2 ± 12.4 at baseline to 32.2 ± 11.5 at final follow-up (mean difference − 22.0 points, 95% CI: −28.4 to −21.6, Cohen’s d = 2.15), representing a 41% improvement with very large effect size (Fig. 4). Renal function as measured by eGFR showed modest but significant overall changes (Friedman test p = 0.021), though post-hoc analysis revealed these changes were primarily driven by the baseline to 1-month comparison, with stable values across subsequent timepoints. Clinical events and time-to-event analysis

Mortality was recorded in 10 cases (7.1%, 95% CI: 3.5–12.7%) over the median follow-up period of 23.5 months. To contextualize this finding, our observed mortality rate compares favorably with the natural history of untreated severe SMR. Similarly, the control arm of the COAPT trial, which received guideline-directed medical therapy alone, demonstrated a 1-year mortality rate of 19.1% and 2-year mortality of 33.3%, compared to 10.6% and 22.4% respectively in the MitraClip group. When annualized, our mortality rate of approximately 3.6% per year substantially underperforms these benchmarks for untreated or medically-managed SMR, suggesting meaningful survival benefit from M-TEER intervention in our cohort despite the high-risk baseline characteristics.

The timing of deaths was as follows: 2 cases (1.4%) within the first 3 months, 3 cases (2.1%) between 3 and 6 months, 3 cases (2.1%) between 6 and 12 months, and 2 cases (1.4%) beyond 12 months. Among the 10 deaths, 5 (3.6%) were attributed to worsening heart failure, 3 (2.1%) to arrhythmias, and 2 (1.4%) to non-cardiac causes.

During the median follow-up period of 23.5 months, rehospitalization occurred in 79 cases (56.4%, 95% CI: 47.8–64.8%). This rate requires careful contextualization within contemporary M-TEER literature. When annualized, our rehospitalization rate translates to approximately 28.8% per year. In comparison, the STS/ACC TVT Registry reported 1-year rehospitalization rates of approximately 25% for patients undergoing M-TEER for secondary mitral regurgitation. The EXPAND G4 study, which included optimized patient selection and protocol-driven follow-up, reported lower rehospitalization rates of approximately 18–20% at one year. The 2-year COAPT trial data showed rehospitalization rates of 35.8% in the device group versus 67.9% in the control group receiving guideline-directed medical therapy alone.

The timing of first rehospitalization was distributed as follows: 28 cases (20.0%) within the first 3 months, 21 cases (15.0%) between 3 and 6 months, 17 cases (12.1%) between 6 and 12 months, and 13 cases (9.3%) beyond 12 months. Rehospitalizations were primarily due to worsening heart failure in 56 cases (40.0%), arrhythmias in 11 cases (7.9%), and non-cardiac conditions in 12 cases (8.6%).

Device-related events during follow-up

Device-related complications during follow-up included single-leaflet device attachment in 3 patients (2.1%) detected during follow-up echocardiography at 1, 6, and 12 months respectively. No cases of device embolization, clip detachment, significant mitral stenosis (mean gradient > 5 mmHg), or endocarditis were observed throughout the follow-up period.

Predictors of secondary clinical endpoints

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified the following significant predictors of rehospitalization: eGFR (HR 0.95 per mL/min/1.73 m², 95% CI: 0.91–0.99, p = 0.031), NT-proBNP levels (HR 1.12 per 1000 pg/mL, 95% CI: 1.03–1.21, p = 0.007), atrial fibrillation (HR 1.62, 95% CI: 1.02–2.57, p = 0.041), hemoglobin levels (HR 0.87 per g/dL, 95% CI: 0.76–0.99, p = 0.038), post-procedure MR severity > 1+ (HR 1.73, 95% CI: 1.13–2.65, p = 0.012), and moderate/severe TR (HR 1.58, 95% CI: 1.03–2.42, p = 0.036).

For mortality, significant univariate predictors included: hemoglobin levels (HR 0.92 per g/dL, 95% CI: 0.86–0.99, p = 0.032), coronary artery disease (HR 2.31, 95% CI: 1.04–5.12, p = 0.040), LVEF (HR 0.94 per 1%, 95% CI: 0.89–0.99, p = 0.028), and post-procedure MR severity > 1+ (HR 2.54, 95% CI: 1.09–5.92, p = 0.031).

ACE inhibitor/ARB use (HR 0.88, 95% CI: 0.52–1.24, p = 0.252), SGLT-2 inhibitor use (HR 0.68, 95% CI: 0.35–1.31, p = 0.245), sacubitril/valsartan use (HR 0.73, 95% CI: 0.42–1.27, p = 0.265), and MRA use (HR 0.82, 95% CI: 0.54–1.24, p = 0.347) showed trends toward reduced rehospitalization risk, though none reached statistical significance in univariate analysis due to small sample sizes (Table 4).

Temporal trends in clinical outcomes (Early vs. Later Experience)

When comparing clinical outcomes between the first half (n = 70) and second half (n = 70) of our patient cohort, the rehospitalization rate during follow-up was significantly lower in the later experience group (62.9% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.047), while mortality rates were not significantly different (8.6% vs. 5.7%, p = 0.432). This improvement in rehospitalization rates remained significant after adjusting for baseline characteristics in multivariate analysis (adjusted HR 0.68, 95% CI: 0.47–0.98, p = 0.039) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This study represents one of the first assessments of the MitraClip procedure in patients with SMR in Turkey, contributing valuable data to the growing international body of evidence regarding its safety and efficacy. The results from this cohort of 140 high-risk patients with moderate-to-severe/severe SMR demonstrate that the MitraClip procedure is associated with high technical and functional success rates, along with significant improvements in both clinical symptoms and functional capacity.

Our study was designed with methodologically distinct primary and secondary endpoints. The primary endpoints focused on immediate procedural outcomes (procedural success and optimal MR reduction at discharge), which are binary outcomes appropriately analyzed using descriptive statistics and comparative tests. The secondary endpoints examined long-term clinical outcomes (rehospitalization and mortality), which are time-to-event outcomes requiring survival analysis methods. This distinction is important for proper interpretation of our results and comparison with other M-TEER studies.

The high procedural success rate (97.9%) and substantial proportion of patients achieving optimal MR reduction (67.9%) demonstrate the technical feasibility and immediate efficacy of MitraClip therapy in our Turkish cohort. These primary endpoint results are comparable to contemporary M-TEER registries and support the broader applicability of this technology across different geographic populations. Using standardized MVARC criteria, our procedural success rate aligns with contemporary M-TEER literature, and our MR reduction to ≤ 1+ (67.9%) is comparable to real-world cohorts like the TVT registry, though lower than the approximately 90% reported in the highly controlled EXPAND G4 study [14].

Several factors explain the gap between our results and EXPAND G4. Our temporal analysis (Fig. 5) revealed significant improvements from early to later experience, with MR ≤ 1 + achievement increasing from 60.0% to 75.7% (p = 0.042), accompanied by reduced procedure times (85 to 58 min) and complication rates (14.3% to 7.1%). This learning curve pattern aligns with Sorajja et al.‘s 2016 TVT Registry analysis showing that centers achieve 10–15% improvement in optimal MR reduction after their first 30–50 cases. Our data capture the early adoption phase of MitraClip therapy in Turkey (2021–2024), whereas EXPAND G4 enrolled patients at highly experienced centers. Additionally, our cohort included more challenging anatomical substrates, with 89.3% having ventricular-mechanism SMR with significant leaflet tethering (median LVEF 25%).

Our current practice has incorporated several strategies informed by this learning curve: more liberal use of multiple clips (increasing from 35% to 47% between early and later experience), systematic utilization of larger XT/XTW clips for patients with larger coaptation gaps, and adoption of more aggressive procedural endpoints targeting MR ≤ 1 + rather than accepting MR 2+. While vasopressor testing can help assess functional MR and guide clip placement, this technique was not routinely employed during our study period but is being incorporated into future protocols.

Our longitudinal follow-up data demonstrate that MR reduction remained relatively stable over time, with 94.3% of patients maintaining MR ≤ 2 + at a median follow-up of 23.5 months. However, we observed a modest decline in optimal MR reduction (≤ 1+) from 67.9% at discharge to 59.3% at final follow-up. This compares favorably with the 5-year COAPT follow-up data showing 72.4% maintained MR ≤ 2 + at 5 years [12], though the slight decrease underscores the progressive nature of underlying ventricular disease in SMR and highlights the importance of achieving maximal initial MR reduction.

The safety profile aligns with previous reports, with an overall complication rate of 10.7% [16]. The most common complications were access site-related, followed by acute kidney injury, consistent with the TVT registry and European experience. The absence of stroke, device embolization, and in-hospital mortality highlights the relative safety when performed at experienced centers. The single-leaflet device attachment rate (2.1%) was lower than earlier MitraClip studies (3–5%), potentially reflecting improvements in device technology and operator experience [17, 18]. However, we lacked detailed anatomical characterization of these SLDA cases, including factors such as leaflet calcification, tethering severity, coaptation depth, or clip positioning—factors that contemporary literature suggests may contribute to SLDA and which future studies should systematically document.

At 1-month follow-up, 69.3% of patients experienced a reduction in NYHA class, with the majority progressing from class III to class II or I. This improvement aligns with COAPT trial results, where MitraClip combined with GDMT demonstrated superior outcomes [11, 12]. The substantial improvements in functional capacity, coupled with significant MR reduction, highlight the transformative impact on quality of life.

Our Turkish cohort demonstrated several distinctive characteristics compared to major M-TEER trials that have important implications for outcome interpretation. Our patients presented with substantially lower median LVEF (25.0%, IQR: 22.0–30.0%) compared to COAPT (mean 31.0 ± 9.0%, p < 0.001) [12], EVEREST II (mean 28.0 ± 14.0%) [10], RESHAPE-HF2 (mean 29.0 ± 8.0%) [15], and the STS/ACC TVT Registry (mean ~ 40%) [16]. This represents more severely impaired left ventricular function, with our median falling approximately 6% points below COAPT.

The burden of coronary artery disease was markedly higher (77.9%) compared to COAPT (65.2%), EVEREST II (~ 65%), and RESHAPE-HF2 (71%), suggesting higher prevalence of ischemic cardiomyopathy in our Turkish population. Most significantly, our cohort demonstrated substantially worse renal function with median eGFR of 42 mL/min/1.73 m² (IQR: 35–58) and 45.0% having chronic renal failure, compared to approximately 30% in COAPT [12] and mean eGFR of ~ 60 mL/min/1.73 m² in the TVT Registry [16].

Other comorbidities showed comparable or higher prevalence: hypertension (72.9% vs. 69.4% in COAPT), diabetes mellitus (35.7% vs. 32.2% in COAPT), and atrial fibrillation (48.6% vs. 42% in EVEREST II, 57% in RESHAPE-HF2). Our median age of 75.0 years was slightly younger than typical European registries (mean 78–80 years) but similar to COAPT (mean 72 ± 12 years). Critically, 100% of our patients were NYHA class III-IV at baseline, compared to 57% in COAPT, indicating more advanced symptomatic heart failure. This selection toward more progressed patients occurred because, during the study period, 15 patients with NYHA class II symptoms were evaluated but not included, limiting generalizability to earlier-stage heart failure.

Several cultural and regional factors unique to the Turkish healthcare context may have influenced outcomes. The high smoking prevalence (55.0%) substantially exceeds Western M-TEER registries (typically 30–40%), reflecting broader epidemiological patterns in Turkey. Approximately 35% of patients traveled more than 100 km to reach our tertiary care center, potentially impacting follow-up quality and medical therapy optimization. Previous research in Turkish cardiovascular populations has identified medication adherence rates of 60–70% for chronic heart failure medications, potentially lower than prospective trial settings with intensive monitoring. Additionally, dietary sodium restriction faces cultural challenges in traditional Turkish cuisine, which includes high sodium content.

Our observed rehospitalization rate of 56.4% during median follow-up of 23.5 months requires careful interpretation within contemporary M-TEER literature. When annualized (approximately 28.8% per year), this compares favorably to real-world registries like the German TRAMI registry (30–35% annual rehospitalization) but remains higher than optimized trial cohorts such as the STS/ACC TVT Registry (~ 25% at 1 year) and EXPAND G4 (18–20% at one year).

Several population-specific factors likely contribute to these rates. The combination of markedly worse renal function (median eGFR 42 mL/min/1.73 m² vs. ~60 in TVT Registry), substantially lower LVEF (25% vs. 31% in COAPT, 40% in TVT Registry), and universally advanced NYHA class (100% class III-IV vs. 57% in COAPT) creates a particularly challenging clinical phenotype prone to volume overload and recurrent decompensation despite successful structural intervention.

Most importantly, suboptimal implementation of guideline-directed medical therapy likely contributed significantly. Only approximately 50% of patients received ACE inhibitors/ARBs/ARNIs and just 12% received SGLT-2 inhibitors, contrasting sharply with COAPT (97% on beta-blockers, 85% on ACE inhibitors/ARBs, 75% on mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists) [12] and the TVT Registry (~ 80% on ACE inhibitors/ARBs, 85% on beta-blockers) [16]. This underutilization reflected multiple challenges including high prevalence of renal dysfunction and hypotension (38% with SBP < 110 mmHg) that often contraindicated these therapies, as well as the timing of our study coinciding with evolving evidence and reimbursement policies for SGLT-2 inhibitors in Turkey (widely implemented only in late 2022). This gap in medical therapy optimization represents a critical limitation and underscores the need for comprehensive heart failure management programs integrated with structural interventions.

Despite the absolute rehospitalization rate appearing high, patients experienced a statistically significant 61% reduction in annualized heart failure hospitalization rate from 0.72 per patient-year pre-procedure to 0.28 per patient-year during follow-up (p < 0.001), indicating substantial benefit even in this high-risk population. The RESHAPE-HF2 trial (2024) demonstrated similar patterns, with M-TEER significantly reducing but not eliminating rehospitalization risk in advanced heart failure patients [15].

These findings underscore several critical implications. First, integration of structural interventions with maximally tolerated GDMT—including ACE inhibitors/ARBs/ARNIs, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and SGLT-2 inhibitors—is essential for optimal outcomes. Second, realistic expectations are needed regarding M-TEER’s role in advanced heart failure with multiple comorbidities; while providing meaningful symptomatic benefit and reduced hospitalization frequency, it does not cure underlying cardiomyopathy. Finally, dedicated heart failure programs and multidisciplinary care coordination following M-TEER are crucial for addressing this vulnerable population’s complex needs.

Our analysis revealed several significant predictors of rehospitalization: eGFR, NT-ProBNP levels, atrial fibrillation presence, and MR severity. These findings highlight the importance of renal function and biomarkers in predicting outcomes, consistent with prior heart failure studies [18, 19]. Atrial fibrillation’s identification as a novel predictor (HR 1.62, 95% CI: 1.02–2.57, p = 0.041) aligns with emerging evidence about its multifaceted impact—beyond contributing to MR severity through altered loading conditions, it may indicate more advanced atrial remodeling and represent a marker of overall cardiovascular disease burden, as demonstrated by Santoro et al. [20].

The multivariate predictors of mortality—hemoglobin levels (HR 0.92 per g/dL, 95% CI: 0.86–0.99, p = 0.041) and post-procedure MR severity >1+ (HR 2.54, 95% CI: 1.09–5.92, p = 0.030)—have critical implications. Each 1 g/dL decrease in hemoglobin associated with 8% increased mortality risk, suggesting anemia optimization prior to M-TEER should be prioritized. The strong association between residual MR >1 + and mortality (2.5-fold higher risk) reinforces the importance of achieving optimal MR reduction, consistent with Santoro et al.‘s work demonstrating suboptimal procedural results significantly impact long-term outcomes [20].

However, our mortality analysis included only 10 events, substantially underpowering multivariate regression. With this small number, our ability to reliably identify independent predictors is limited, and variables appearing non-significant (such as LVEF, coronary artery disease) may actually be important predictors but failed to achieve significance due to insufficient power. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

These findings suggest several practical implications: patients with significant anemia (hemoglobin < 10–11 g/dL) should undergo evaluation and correction when feasible; procedural strategy should prioritize achieving MR ≤ 1 + through appropriate clip selection, multiple clip placement when anatomically feasible, and potentially vasopressor testing; patients with atrial fibrillation require aggressive rhythm and rate control strategies; and the combination of pre-procedure anemia, atrial fibrillation, and renal dysfunction may identify a particularly high-risk phenotype requiring enhanced optimization and intensive post-procedure surveillance, though this requires validation in prospective studies.

The high prevalence of concomitant tricuspid regurgitation (77.1% with moderate or severe TR at baseline) represents substantial right-sided heart disease burden. Our univariate analysis identified moderate-to-severe TR as a significant predictor of rehospitalization (HR 1.58, 95% CI: 1.03–2.42, p = 0.036), aligning with Nath et al.‘s landmark study of 5,223 patients showing even mild-to-moderate TR associated with reduced survival [21], and Topilsky et al.‘s findings that moderate or greater TR significantly increased mortality and heart failure hospitalizations [22].

The relationship between TR and adverse outcomes is multifactorial: TR contributes to right ventricular volume overload and dysfunction impairing cardiac output, reflects more advanced right ventricular remodeling and elevated pulmonary pressures indicating progressed heart failure potentially less responsive to isolated mitral intervention, and contributes to systemic venous congestion worsening renal and hepatic function. Although moderate-to-severe TR lost statistical significance in our final multivariate model (p = 0.08 during stepwise selection), this likely reflects limited statistical power rather than lack of clinical importance.

The TRILUMINATE trial demonstrated transcatheter edge-to-edge repair safety and efficacy for TR using the TriClip device [23]. Recent data suggest transcatheter tricuspid intervention can be performed as staged procedures following M-TEER or, in selected cases, as combined same-session interventions. The optimal timing remains under investigation, with potential advantages of addressing both valves including comprehensive hemodynamic optimization and earlier symptomatic benefit, balanced against concerns about prolonged procedure time, increased contrast load (particularly relevant given our renal dysfunction prevalence), and cumulative device burden.

In our cohort, no patients underwent combined mitral-tricuspid intervention, with TR decisions deferred based on prevailing practice patterns during 2021–2024 when transcatheter tricuspid interventions were not yet widely available in Turkey. Given the strong signal for TR as an adverse outcome predictor and mounting evidence supporting transcatheter tricuspid intervention, future studies should specifically evaluate combined or staged valve interventions in patients with concomitant moderate-to-severe mitral and tricuspid regurgitation.

We acknowledge methodological limitations in our regression analyses. Our initial approach of including all variables with p < 0.1 in multivariate models was overly inclusive given our sample size. Following established guidelines, we implemented stepwise backward selection to avoid overfitting and assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors, resulting in more parsimonious models but excluding some clinically relevant variables such as NT-proBNP and moderate/severe tricuspid regurgitation from the final rehospitalization model due to statistical considerations rather than clinical irrelevance. BMI and diabetes mellitus, while approaching significance in univariate analysis (p = 0.09 and p = 0.08), were excluded from final models due to loss of significance during stepwise selection (final p-values 0.24 and 0.18). Similarly, SGLT-2 inhibitor use showed a protective trend (HR 0.68) but did not achieve significance (p = 0.31), likely due to low utilization (12.1%). These limitations highlight the need for larger prospective studies with adequate power to include all clinically important predictors simultaneously.

The most significant limitation is the lack of randomized controlled design and absence of a control group, preventing definitive comparative efficacy claims and limiting findings to observational associations. While extensive randomized trial evidence has established M-TEER efficacy, our study contributes real-world validation in a previously underrepresented population.

The retrospective design introduces several biases: selection bias is inherent as heart teams made M-TEER decisions rather than through standardized protocols; information bias may have affected data collection as retrospective chart review depends on documentation completeness and accuracy; and unmeasured confounding exists as factors not systematically documented (medication adherence, dietary sodium intake, exercise capacity, socioeconomic status) may have influenced outcomes. Additionally, the lack of prospective event adjudication means endpoints were determined by treating physicians’ documentation rather than independent committees.

A critical methodological limitation is the absence of core laboratory adjudication for echocardiographic measurements. All assessments were performed locally without independent verification, potentially introducing inter-observer variability in MR severity, LVEF, and other parameters. While all centers employed experienced echocardiographers following standardized ESC/EACTS guideline criteria, the lack of systematic quality control represents a significant limitation compared to prospective trials.

The relatively small sample size (n = 140) and geographically limited multicenter design may reduce generalizability. All participating centers were in Turkey, concentrated in major metropolitan areas, which may not represent outcomes achievable at lower-volume centers or different patient populations. The study excluded patients with severe comorbidities, limiting applicability to more complex clinical profiles. Our follow-up protocol relied on interval-based assessments rather than continuous event monitoring, precluding Kaplan-Meier curve construction. Telephone follow-up supplemented in-person visits for patients unable to attend clinic, potentially resulting in underreporting of clinical events.

An important limitation is potential confounding by indication in medication analyses. Paradoxical associations observed with some guideline-directed therapies likely reflect preferential prescription to sicker patients rather than true harmful effects. Future prospective studies with standardized medication protocols are needed to properly evaluate synergistic effects of optimal medical therapy and M-TEER. The limited follow-up duration (median 23.5 months) and absence of control group mean long-term impact on survival and rehospitalization remains uncertain.

This study demonstrates that MitraClip therapy in Turkish patients with secondary mitral regurgitation is technically feasible, safe, and associated with significant improvements in MR severity and functional capacity. Our findings support the broader applicability of M-TEER across different geographic populations while highlighting the critical importance of optimal medical therapy integration, aggressive procedural strategies targeting MR ≤ 1+, and comprehensive post-procedure management.

Future prospective, multicenter studies with adequate sample sizes, core laboratory adjudication, standardized GDMT protocols, and systematic assessment of tricuspid regurgitation are needed to validate our findings and optimize patient selection and management strategies in this high-risk population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, MitraClip therapy achieved high rates of procedural success (97.9%) and optimal MR reduction (67.9% with MR ≤ 1 + at discharge) in Turkish patients with secondary mitral regurgitation. During long-term follow-up, the procedure demonstrated significant clinical benefits, effectively maintaining MR reduction and improving functional capacity and quality of life. The procedure exhibited an acceptable safety profile with complication rates comparable to international registry data. Despite the positive results in symptom alleviation and MR reduction, rehospitalization rates remain significant, indicating the need for comprehensive management strategies beyond the procedure itself. Our temporal analysis revealed improvements in procedural efficiency and outcomes as institutional experience increased, highlighting the importance of the learning curve.

While our retrospective design limits definitive efficacy claims, these findings provide valuable real-world validation of M-TEER outcomes in the Turkish population and demonstrate the importance of institutional experience in optimizing procedural results. Future prospective studies comparing M-TEER outcomes across different geographic populations would further enhance our understanding of regional variations in treatment response. This study provides valuable real-world evidence for MitraClip outcomes in a previously underrepresented Asian-European population, demonstrating safety and efficacy profiles comparable to established international registries. The findings suggest that achieving MR reduction to ≤ 1 + should be the procedural target to optimize clinical outcomes. Future controlled studies are needed to definitively establish the comparative efficacy of MitraClip therapy and to refine patient selection criteria for SMR management.
