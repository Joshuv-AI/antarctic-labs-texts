An End-to-end Interactive Software for Pediatric Pneumonia Severity Assessment from Lung Ultrasound Images
Authors: Shuo Li, Xue Yang, Yue Jiang, Lingling Zhang, Qili Zhang, Zufei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop and validate an end-to-end interactive software that integrates lung ultrasound radiomics and machine learning for the objective assessment of pediatric pneumonia severity at the point of care.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included a dataset of lung ultrasound images from 293 pediatric patients (157 with mild pneumonia and 136 with severe pneumonia). A total of 104 radiomics features were extracted from clinician-delineated regions of interest. Feature selection was performed using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression, and 10 machine learning algorithms were constructed and evaluated. The optimal model was interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations. The finalized classifier was integrated into an interactive software platform that guides users from image upload to severity prediction.ResultsThe Light Gradient Boosting Machine classifier, which forms the core of the software, demonstrated superior performance on the independent test set, achieving an accuracy of 89.8%, a sensitivity of 91.7%, a specificity of 88.6%, and an area under the curve of 90.1%. SHapley Additive exPlanation analysis identified and ranked the contribution of key predictive features, with morphological characteristics being the most influential.ConclusionsWe present an end-to-end interactive software that successfully leverages lung ultrasound radiomics and machine learning to provide an objective, accurate, and rapid assessment of pediatric pneumonia severity. This tool has significant potential to standardize diagnosis and support clinical decision-making in real-world settings.

Introduction

Pneumonia remains a leading global cause of mortality from infectious diseases, making an accurate assessment of its severity critically important. 1​ ​2​ Approximately 120 million new cases of childhood pneumonia occur annually worldwide, with children under 5 years of age accounting for about 80% of all pediatric cases. Among these, an estimated 740,000 deaths occur each year in the under 5 age group, representing 14% of total mortality in this population, posing a severe threat to children’s health and lives. 2​ Distinguishing between mild and severe pneumonia is crucial, as the mortality rate of severe pneumonia is significantly higher than that of mild cases. 3​ Mild pneumonia refers to a localized lung infection without hypoxia or systemic organ dysfunction, typically manageable in outpatient settings. In contrast, severe pneumonia may involve respiratory failure, septic shock, or multi-organ failure, often requiring urgent hospitalization or intensive care unit (ICU) intervention. 1​ 3​​ ​4​

Currently, the assessment of pediatric pneumonia severity relies primarily on clinical scoring systems such as the pneumonia severity index (PSI) and CURB-65, as well as computed tomography (CT) imaging. However, these approaches have notable limitations, including dependence on specialized equipment and, in the case of CT, considerable radiation exposure. 5​ Given that children are in a critical stage of growth and development, minimizing radiation exposure is essential to avoid interfering with normal physiological processes. Lung ultrasound (LUS) has emerged as a valuable diagnostic tool for pneumonia due to its lack of ionizing radiation and high bedside accessibility. Nevertheless, conventional LUS evaluation remains highly operator-dependent and lacks standardized, objective quantitative criteria.

The differentiation between mild and severe pneumonia using LUS is primarily based on characteristic sonographic patterns. According to multiple international guidelines and expert consensus, the typical LUS features of mild pneumonia include focal B-lines, reflecting localized pulmonary edema or inflammation; limited pleural abnormalities such as localized thickening or irregularity; minimal pleural effusion, which is a non-essential finding; and normal or only mildly reduced lung sliding. 3​ 4​ 5​ 6​ 7​ ​8​ In contrast, severe pneumonia is commonly characterized by diffuse or confluent B-lines, often manifesting as a “white lung” pattern indicative of extensive alveolar–interstitial syndrome; lung consolidation with dynamic or static air bronchograms, suggesting lobar atelectasis or tissue hepatization; moderate pleural effusion, also non-essential; significantly reduced or absent lung sliding; and diffuse pleural abnormalities.

In recent years, radiomics—a high-throughput approach for extracting sub-visual image features—has shown considerable promise in the differential diagnosis of various diseases. While previous studies have explored radiomics in medical imaging, its application in pediatric LUS for severity assessment remains limited. To bridge this gap and directly translate these technical advantages into clinical utility, this study goes beyond model development to introduce an end-to-end interactive software that integrates ultrasound radiomics with machine learning (ML). This software is designed to objectively differentiate between mild and severe pediatric pneumonia by extracting discriminative radiomics features from LUS images, thereby addressing the key limitation of operator dependency in conventional LUS. Our work demonstrates how these data-driven features not only complement existing clinical assessments but are seamlessly operationalized through a dedicated platform, highlighting the direct clinical applicability and transformative potential of the proposed approach.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

This retrospective study enrolled 293 pediatric patients with etiologically or clinically confirmed pneumonia, comprising 157 mild and 136 severe cases. The mean age of the cohort was 10.2±5.4 years, with a distribution of 158 men and 135 women. All patients underwent a standardized 12-zone LUS examination within 24 hours of admission. Exclusion criteria included: coexistence of other pulmonary diseases, suboptimal image quality, and incomplete clinical data.

Pneumonia was diagnosed by the presence of both imaging findings (chest CT, chest X-ray, or LUS showing new infiltrates or consolidation) and clinical features (fever, cough/sputum, rales, or elevated white blood cell count). Severity was classified as mild or severe using clinical criteria independent of AI interpretation: mild pneumonia required good mental status, no obvious respiratory distress, SpO 2 ≥95%, and normal feeding; severe pneumonia was defined by any of central cyanosis, SpO 2 <92%, obvious respiratory distress (nasal flaring, chest indrawing, or grunting), refusal to feed/dehydration/lethargy/convulsions, or tachypnea (≥60/min if <2 mo, ≥50/min if 2–12 mo, or ≥40/min if >1 y).

Software architecture and implementation

The core of this study is an end-to-end interactive software platform designed to support the entire workflow from image analysis to severity prediction. The system was developed using a multi-technology framework: the backend and ML pipelines were implemented in Python, the web-based user interface was built using HTML for frontend rendering, and specific functional modules were engineered in Java to ensure robust integration and performance. This architecture allows for seamless data flow, from user interaction to algorithmic processing and result visualization.

Image acquisition and region of interest delineation

Standardized dynamic LUS images were acquired using a uniform ultrasound equipment (Philips EPIQ7 or Mindray systems, equipped with linear array L12-4 and convex array SC6-1 probes). Three experienced sonographers independently delineated regions of interest (ROIs) covering both pathological areas and adjacent normal lung tissues, as illustrated in Fig. 1 .

Radiomics feature extraction

A total of 104 ultrasound radiomics features were automatically extracted from each ROI using the pyRadiomics package in Python, following the standardized methodology established by Griethuysen et al., 9​ The full specification of these features is available at: https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io .

Feature selection and model development

Feature selection was performed using a two-stage Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression strategy. The process involved: (1) initial screening to remove features with evident outliers; (2) iterative dimensionality reduction via LASSO if more than 20 features remained, continuing until a final set of no more than 20 discriminative features was retained to ensure model simplicity and clinical practicality. Subsequently, 10 distinct ML classifiers were constructed and evaluated. The dataset was randomly split into training and test sets at a 6:4 ratio. Model performance was assessed on the independent test set using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC).

Model interpretation

To enhance the interpretability of the optimal model, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was employed to quantify and rank the contribution of each selected feature to the model’s predictions.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the enrolled patients are summarized as follows. Patients in the severe pneumonia group were significantly older than those in the mild group (12.1±6.3 y vs. 8.2±2.4 y, p <0.001). Comparison of traditional ultrasonic parameters revealed that the consolidation area was markedly larger in the severe group (32.5±11.2 cm 2 vs. 12.8±6.5 cm 2 , p <0.001).

Feature selection and model construction

During the feature quality control process, an abnormal feature “ngtdm_Coarseness” was identified and excluded. The initial LASSO regression screened 21 features from the original 104, and a subsequent second and third LASSO applications further refined the feature set to 17 final predictors. The 17 selected features comprised 5 first-order statistical features (“calc_variance”, “calc_energy”, “calc_10Percentile”, and “calc_Maximum”), 4 shape-based features (“Perimeter”, “MinorAxisLength”, “MajorAxisLength”, “MaximumDiameter”), 3 Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) features (“glcm_Clustershade”, “glcm_Autocorrelation”, “glcm_ClusterProminence”), 2 Gray-Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM) features (“glszm_LargeAreaHighGrayLevelEmphasis”, “glszm_SizeZoneNonUniformity”), and 1 feature each from the Gray-Level Run-Length Matrix (GLRLM, “glrlm_RunLengthNonUniformity”) and 2 Neighborhood Gray-Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM, “ngtdm_Complexity”) categories.

Model performance

Among the 10 ML algorithms evaluated, the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) model demonstrated superior performance (see Table 1 ). On the independent test set, it achieved an accuracy of 89.8%, a sensitivity of 91.7%, a specificity of 88.6%, and an AUC of 90.1%. The confusion matrix and ROC curve are presented in Fig. 3 . The matrix provides an intuitive breakdown of the test set predictions: among the 70 mild pneumonia cases, 62 cases were correctly classified, while 8 cases were misclassified as severe; among the 48 severe cases, 44 cases were correctly identified, with only 4 cases misclassified as mild.

SHapley Additive exPlanation-based feature interpretation

The SHAP analysis quantified and ranked the contribution of the 17 features to the LightGBM model’s predictions. As illustrated in the summary plot ( Fig. 4 ), “MinorAxisLength” emerged as the most influential feature, followed by “calc_RootMeanSquared”, “MajorAxisLength”, “calc_Maximum”, and “MaximumDiameter”. Other notable contributors included texture features such as “glszm_LargeAreaHighGrayLevelEmphasis” and “glcm_ClusterProminence”, underscoring the critical role of both morphological and textural information in severity stratification.

A graph of the results of the SHAP analysis. SHAP, SHapley Additive exPlanations.

Software implementation and workflow

The validated LightGBM classifier was successfully integrated into the end-to-end interactive software platform. When uploading a LUS image, the clinician can delineate the ROI using the interactive tools. The software then automatically extracts radiomics features and processes them through the trained model, generating a real-time prediction of pneumonia severity (“mild” or “severe”) along with a confidence score. A comprehensive, step-by-step guide to operating the software, from image upload to result interpretation, is provided in the supplementary material, ensuring ease of adoption and reproducibility in clinical settings.

Discussion

This study successfully developed and validated an end-to-end interactive software platform that integrates LUS radiomics with ML for the objective classification of pediatric pneumonia severity. Moving beyond a pure algorithm, our work delivers a functional tool that translates technical innovation into direct clinical utility. The core LightGBM model within this software achieved robust performance on the test set (accuracy: 89.8%; AUC: 90.1%), demonstrating its potential for practical applications.

The SHAP analysis provided critical insights into the model’s decision-making process, revealing that morphological shape features, such as MinorAxisLength, MajorAxisLength, and Perimeter, were among the most influential predictors. This suggests that the geometric characteristics of the lesion, which can be intuitively related to the consolidation size and spread, are highly discriminative for severity. Furthermore, the contribution of first-order statistical features (e.g., calc_RootMeanSquared) and texture features (e.g., glszm_LargeAreaHighGrayLevelEmphasis) underscores the model’s ability to quantify underlying tissue heterogeneity and structural changes that may elude visual assessments. This multi-dimensional feature set objectively quantifies pathological changes, effectively compensating for the inherent subjectivity of traditional LUS evaluation.

The primary advantage of our approach lies in the seamless integration of this analytical power into clinician-friendly software. Unlike standalone ML models reported in the literature, our platform directly addresses the workflow from image upload to severity prediction. This eliminates the need for separate feature extraction and model inference steps, reducing operational complexity and the potential for human error. By providing an objective, quantifiable standard at the point of care, the software can help mitigate diagnostic variability among different physicians.

Compared to traditional methods, our solution offers distinct benefits. Clinical scoring systems (e.g., PSI and CURB-65) often rely on parameters that are not immediately available, leading to diagnostic delays. 10​ 11​ 12​ ​13​ In contrast, our software enables the rapid, bedside assessment. More importantly, unlike CT, it completely avoids ionizing radiation, making it exceptionally suitable for the repeated monitoring required in pediatric cases.

The clinical significance of this study is therefore two-fold. First, it validates a high-performance radiomics model for severity stratification. Second, and more critically, it delivers this capability through an operational software system. This has immediate implications for clinical workflows: in the emergency department, it can aid in rapid triage; in the ICU, it can support dynamic monitoring; and in primary care settings, it can enhance diagnostic accuracy and referral efficiency. 14​

The technological innovation of this work is not merely the application of radiomics to pediatric LUS but the creation of a complete, interactive diagnostic aid. We have established a streamlined process from feature selection to model interpretation and, ultimately, software implementation. This end-to-end system represents a significant step towards translating artificial intelligence from a research concept into a tangible clinical tool.

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size of 293 patients. The model’s performance across different patient demographics, pneumonia etiologies (bacterial vs. viral), and operator skill levels requires further validation in larger, multi-center prospective studies. Furthermore, the current software, while interactive, still requires manual ROI delineation. Future work will focus on developing automated segmentation algorithms to further streamline the process and integrating dynamic image analysis to capture more pathophysiological information.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that an end-to-end software platform based on LUS radiomics and ML can effectively and objectively classify pediatric pneumonia severity. By providing a user-friendly interface that bridges advanced analytics and clinical workflow, our tool holds significant promise as a decision-support system to standardize diagnoses and guide treatment, ultimately contributing to more precise and safer care for children with pneumonia.
