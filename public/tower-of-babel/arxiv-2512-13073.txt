Spectral Equivariance and Geometric Transport in Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces: A Unified Framework for Orthogonal Polynomial and Kernel Estimation
Jocelyn Nembé

Abstract
We develop a unified geometric framework for nonparametric estimation based on the notion of Twin Kernel Spaces, defined as orbits of a reproducing kernel under a group action. This structure induces a family of transported RKHS geometries in which classical orthogonal polynomial estimators, kernel estimators, and spectral smoothing methods arise as projections onto transported eigenfunction systems. Our main contribution is a Spectral Equivariance Theorem showing that the eigenfunctions of any transported kernel are obtained by unitary transport of the base eigenfunctions. As a consequence, orthogonal polynomial estimators are equivariant under geometric deformation, kernel estimators correspond to soft spectral filtering in a twin space, and minimax rates and bias–variance tradeoffs are invariant under transport. We provide examples based on Hermite and Legendre polynomials, affine and Gaussian groups, and illustrate the effectiveness of twin transport for adaptive and multimodal estimation. The framework reveals a deep connection between group actions, RKHS geometry, and spectral nonparametrics, offering a unified perspective that encompasses kernel smoothing, orthogonal series, splines, and multiscale methods.



Keywords:
Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, orthogonal polynomials, spectral methods, group actions, nonparametric estimation, equivariance, minimax rates.

1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation

Nonparametric estimation constitutes a cornerstone of modern statistical inference, with applications spanning density estimation, regression, and functional data analysis
(
Tsybakov 2009
;
Wasserman 2006
)
. Two classical paradigms have dominated this field: orthogonal series estimators based on polynomial expansions
(
Efromovich 1999
;
Johnstone 2017
)
and kernel smoothing methods
(
Wand and Jones 1995
;
Silverman 1986
)
. While these approaches appear methodologically distinct—the former relying on spectral truncation in a fixed basis, the latter on local averaging with bandwidth selection—both share deep connections to reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS)
(
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2004
;
Cucker and Smale 2002
)
.

The present work is motivated by the observation that many seemingly different nonparametric methods can be understood as projections in different geometric “reference frames” of a common underlying RKHS structure. Specifically, we show that classical orthogonal polynomial systems—Hermite, Legendre, Laguerre, Jacobi, and their variants—arise naturally as eigenfunctions of transported kernels under group actions. This perspective unifies diverse estimation procedures and reveals fundamental geometric invariances that have not been systematically exploited in the statistical literature.

The theory of RKHS and Mercer kernels provides a natural framework for studying such connections
(
Aronszajn 1950
;
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
)
. Classical results on spectral decomposition of integral operators
(
Riesz and Sz.-Nagy 1955
;
Dunford and Schwartz 1988
)
establish that Mercer kernels admit eigenfunction expansions, which in the case of weighted
L^{2}
spaces often coincide with classical orthogonal polynomials
(
Szegö 1975
;
Ismail 2005
)
. Our contribution is to show that this spectral structure is preserved—in a precise sense—under geometric transformations induced by group actions.

1.2
Contributions of the paper

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

(i)

Twin Kernel Spaces framework.
We introduce the notion of Twin Kernel Spaces as orbits of a base reproducing kernel under a group action on the input space. This provides a minimal yet powerful geometric framework for studying families of related RKHS structures (Section
3
).

(ii)

Spectral Equivariance Theorem.
We prove that the eigenfunctions of any transported kernel are obtained by applying the unitary transport operator to the base eigenfunctions, with eigenvalues preserved. This result (Theorem
4.6
) establishes that spectral structure is equivariant under geometric deformation.

(iii)

Unified interpretation of estimators.
We demonstrate that orthogonal polynomial estimators correspond to hard spectral truncation while kernel smoothing corresponds to soft spectral attenuation, both operating in potentially different twin geometries. Bandwidth selection is reinterpreted as choice of twin space (Section
6
).

(iv)

Invariance of statistical properties.
We establish that convergence rates, bias–variance tradeoffs, and minimax risks are invariant under twin transport, providing a geometric explanation for the robustness of spectral methods (Sections
8
–
10
).

(v)

Explicit examples.
We provide detailed worked examples for Hermite polynomials with Gaussian geometry and Legendre polynomials with affine geometry, including a multimodal estimation example demonstrating the practical relevance of twin transport (Section
7
).

1.3
Related work

The connection between orthogonal polynomials and kernel methods has been explored from various angles.
Smola et al. 2000
and
Schölkopf and Smola 2002
discuss spectral properties of kernel operators in machine learning contexts. The role of orthogonal polynomials in density estimation dates to
Cencov 1962
and was developed systematically by
Efromovich 1999
.
Wahba 1990
established foundational connections between spline smoothing and RKHS theory.

Group-theoretic perspectives on kernel methods appear in
Kondor and Trivedi 2008
and
Fukumizu et al. 2009
, primarily in the context of invariant kernels for machine learning. The equivariance properties we establish differ in focusing on transport of spectral structure rather than invariance of the kernel itself.

The notion of “twin” structures in functional analysis has appeared in different contexts, notably twin spaces in operator theory
(
Halmos 1982
)
. Our usage is distinct but shares the philosophy of paired geometric structures connected by canonical maps.

Recent work on adaptive estimation
(
Lepski et al. 1997
;
Goldenshluger and Lepski 2011
)
and spatially inhomogeneous smoothing
(
Fan and Gijbels 1996
)
addresses related concerns about geometry-dependent inference, though without the explicit group-theoretic framework we develop.

2
Orthogonal polynomial estimation revisited

2.1
Orthogonal polynomial bases

Let
(E,\mathcal{E},\mu)
be a probability space with
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}
an interval (bounded or unbounded) and
\mu
a probability measure with finite moments of all orders. A sequence of polynomials
\{P_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is
orthonormal
with respect to
\mu
if
\deg(P_{k})=k
and

\int_{E}P_{k}(x)P_{\ell}(x)\,\mu(dx)=\delta_{k\ell},\quad k,\ell\geq 0.

By the Gram–Schmidt process applied to the monomial basis
\{1,x,x^{2},\ldots\}
, such a system always exists and is unique up to sign
(
Szegö 1975
)
.

Classical examples include:

•

Hermite polynomials

\{H_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
: orthonormal with respect to the standard Gaussian measure
d\gamma(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}\,dx
on
\mathbb{R}
.

•

Legendre polynomials

\{P_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
: orthonormal with respect to the uniform measure on
[-1,1]
.

•

Laguerre polynomials

\{L_{k}^{(\alpha)}\}_{k\geq 0}
: orthonormal with respect to the Gamma measure
x^{\alpha}e^{-x}dx
on
[0,\infty)
.

•

Jacobi polynomials

\{P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\}_{k\geq 0}
: orthonormal with respect to
(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}dx
on
[-1,1]
.

These systems satisfy three-term recurrence relations and have well-understood asymptotic properties
(
Ismail 2005
;
Levin and Lubinsky 2001
)
.

2.2
Classical orthogonal series estimators

Given i.i.d. observations
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
from a distribution with density
f
(with respect to
\mu
), the classical orthogonal series density estimator is

\hat{f}_{K}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\hat{\theta}_{k}P_{k}(x),\quad\hat{\theta}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{k}(X_{i}),

(1)

where
K=K(n)
is a truncation parameter
(
Efromovich 1999
;
Wasserman 2006
)
.

Under standard regularity conditions, if
f=\sum_{k\geq 0}\theta_{k}P_{k}
with
\sum_{k\geq 0}\theta_{k}^{2}k^{2s}<\infty
(Sobolev-type smoothness of order
s
), choosing
K\asymp n^{1/(2s+1)}
yields the minimax optimal rate

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}_{K}-f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}=O(n^{-2s/(2s+1)}).

This rate is known to be unimprovable over Sobolev balls
(
Tsybakov 2009
)
.

2.3
RKHS interpretation

The orthogonal series estimator admits a natural RKHS interpretation. Define the Mercer kernel

K_{e}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}P_{k}(x)P_{k}(y),

(2)

where
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a summable sequence of positive eigenvalues (e.g.,
\lambda_{k}=\rho^{k}
for some
\rho\in(0,1)
). The associated RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{e}
consists of functions
f=\sum_{k}\theta_{k}P_{k}
with
\sum_{k}\theta_{k}^{2}/\lambda_{k}<\infty
, equipped with inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{e}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\theta_{k}\phi_{k}}{\lambda_{k}},\quad f=\sum_{k}\theta_{k}P_{k},\quad g=\sum_{k}\phi_{k}P_{k}.

The integral operator
T_{e}:L^{2}(\mu)\to L^{2}(\mu)
defined by

(T_{e}f)(x)=\int_{E}K_{e}(x,y)f(y)\,\mu(dy)

is compact, self-adjoint, and positive, with eigenpairs
(\lambda_{k},P_{k})
.

The truncated estimator (
1
) can be written as

\hat{f}_{K}=S_{e,K}\hat{f}_{\mathrm{emp}},\quad S_{e,K}f=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\langle f,P_{k}\rangle P_{k},

where
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{emp}}
represents the empirical measure and
S_{e,K}
is the spectral projection onto the first
K+1
eigenfunctions. This establishes that orthogonal series estimation is fundamentally a spectral truncation operation in an RKHS geometry
(
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2004
;
Cucker and Smale 2002
)
.

3
Twin Kernel Spaces: a minimal framework

3.1
Group actions on the input space

Definition 3.1

(Twin Space)
.

A
twin space
associated with
(E,G)
is the orbit
G\cdot E
, equipped with the induced structure.

More precisely, let
G
be a group acting measurably on
E
via
\tau:G\times E\to E
,
(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x
. We assume throughout that for each
g\in G
, the map
\tau_{g}:x\mapsto g\cdot x
is a measurable bijection of
E
.

Typical examples include:

•

The
affine group

G=\{(a,b):a>0,b\in\mathbb{R}\}
acting on
E=\mathbb{R}
by
g_{a,b}\cdot x=ax+b
.

•

The
multiplicative group

G=(0,\infty)
acting on
E=\mathbb{R}
by
g_{\alpha}\cdot x=\alpha x
(dilations).

•

The
translation group

G=\mathbb{R}
acting on
E=\mathbb{R}
by
g_{b}\cdot x=x+b
.

3.2
Transport of functions

Definition 3.2

(Twin Transform)
.

Given
f:E\to\mathbb{R}
, define the
transported function

(g_{1},g_{2})\cdot f
by

(g_{1},g_{2})\cdot f(x)=g_{2}\cdot f(g_{1}\cdot x).

In our context, we focus on the case where the action on the function space is determined by the geometric action through change of variables with appropriate Jacobian correction.

3.3
Twin Kernel Spaces

Definition 3.3

(Twin Kernel Space)
.

A
Twin Kernel Space
is the family of kernels

K_{g}(x,y)=g_{2}\cdot K_{e}(\overline{g_{1}}\cdot x,\,\overline{g_{1}}\cdot y)

generated from a base kernel
K_{e}
, where
\overline{g_{1}}
denotes the inverse action.

The precise form of
g_{2}
is determined by requiring the transported kernel to define a valid reproducing kernel in the same
L^{2}(\mu)
space, which necessitates Jacobian correction factors as detailed in Section
4
.

4
Spectral Equivariance Theorem

In this section we formalize the notion of spectral equivariance in Twin Kernel Spaces and show how orthogonal polynomial systems arise as eigenfunctions of transported kernels. We work in a general
L^{2}
–framework and specialize to orthogonal polynomials when needed.

4.1
Setting and assumptions

Let
(E,\mathcal{E},\mu)
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space and denote by

L^{2}(\mu)=\left\{f:E\to\mathbb{R}\;\Big|\;\int_{E}f(x)^{2}\,\mu(dx)<\infty\right\}

the usual Hilbert space with inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\int_{E}f(x)g(x)\,\mu(dx).

Let
K_{e}:E\times E\to\mathbb{R}
be a symmetric, measurable, positive definite kernel such that the associated integral operator

(T_{e}f)(x)=\int_{E}K_{e}(x,y)f(y)\,\mu(dy),\quad f\in L^{2}(\mu),

(3)

is Hilbert–Schmidt (in particular, compact and self-adjoint). Then there exists an orthonormal basis
\{P_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
of
L^{2}(\mu)
and non-negative eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
such that

T_{e}P_{k}=\lambda_{k}P_{k},\quad k\geq 0,

(4)

and the kernel admits the convergent spectral expansion

K_{e}(x,y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{k}P_{k}(x)P_{k}(y)\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\mu\otimes\mu).

(5)

In the classical orthogonal polynomial setting, the functions
P_{k}
form an orthonormal polynomial system with respect to
\mu
.

We now introduce a group action on
E
and the corresponding transport operators.

Definition 4.1

(Group action and Jacobian)
.

Let
G
be a group and let

\tau:G\times E\to E,\quad(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x,

be a measurable left action of
G
on
E
. For each
g\in G
, we write
\tau_{g}(x)=g\cdot x
. We assume that for every
g\in G
, the pushforward measure
\mu\circ\tau_{g}^{-1}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
, and we denote by

J_{g}(x)=\frac{d(\mu\circ\tau_{g}^{-1})}{d\mu}(x)

its Radon–Nikodym derivative (Jacobian).

The following operator implements the action of
G
on
L^{2}(\mu)
in a unitary way.

Definition 4.2

(Twin transport operator on
L^{2}
)
.

For each
g\in G
, define
U_{g}:L^{2}(\mu)\to L^{2}(\mu)
by

(U_{g}f)(x)=J_{g}(x)^{1/2}f(g^{-1}\cdot x).

(6)

Lemma 4.3

(Unitarity of
U_{g}
)
.

For each
g\in G
, the operator
U_{g}
is unitary on
L^{2}(\mu)
, that is,

\langle U_{g}f,U_{g}h\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\langle f,h\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}\quad\text{for all }f,h\in L^{2}(\mu),

and
U_{g_{1}g_{2}}=U_{g_{1}}U_{g_{2}}
,
U_{e_{G}}=\mathrm{Id}
.

Proof.

Let
f,h\in L^{2}(\mu)
and fix
g\in G
. Then

\langle U_{g}f,U_{g}h\rangle=\int_{E}J_{g}(x)^{1/2}f(g^{-1}\cdot x)\,J_{g}(x)^{1/2}h(g^{-1}\cdot x)\,\mu(dx)=\int_{E}J_{g}(x)f(g^{-1}\cdot x)h(g^{-1}\cdot x)\,\mu(dx).

Perform the change of variables
y=g^{-1}\cdot x
, so that
x=g\cdot y
and

\mu(dx)=\mu(d(g\cdot y))=J_{g}(g\cdot y)\,\mu(dy).

By definition of
J_{g}
we have
J_{g}(g\cdot y)\,\mu(dy)=(\mu\circ\tau_{g}^{-1})(d(g\cdot y))
, and the Radon–Nikodym identity yields

J_{g}(g\cdot y)\,\mu(dy)=\mu(dy)\quad\text{a.e.\ }y.

Hence

\langle U_{g}f,U_{g}h\rangle=\int_{E}f(y)h(y)\,\mu(dy)=\langle f,h\rangle.

The group property
U_{g_{1}g_{2}}=U_{g_{1}}U_{g_{2}}
follows from a direct computation using the chain rule for Radon–Nikodym derivatives and the composition law of the action, and
U_{e_{G}}=\mathrm{Id}
by definition.
∎

We now define the transported kernels and operators.

Definition 4.4

(Transported kernel and operator)
.

For
g\in G
, define a kernel
K_{g}:E\times E\to\mathbb{R}
by

K_{g}(x,y)=J_{g}(x)^{1/2}J_{g}(y)^{1/2}K_{e}(g^{-1}\cdot x,g^{-1}\cdot y),

(7)

and the associated integral operator
T_{g}:L^{2}(\mu)\to L^{2}(\mu)
by

(T_{g}f)(x)=\int_{E}K_{g}(x,y)f(y)\,\mu(dy).

(8)

The expression (
7
) is the analytic incarnation of the twin transport of the base kernel
K_{e}
under the action of
G
.

Lemma 4.5

(Conjugation identity)
.

For each
g\in G
and
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
,

T_{g}=U_{g}T_{e}U_{g}^{-1},

(9)

and in particular
T_{g}
is self-adjoint, positive and compact.

Proof.

Fix
g\in G
and
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
. Using (
6
) and (
3
), we compute

(U_{g}^{-1}f)(y)=J_{g^{-1}}(y)^{1/2}f(g\cdot y),

so that

(T_{e}U_{g}^{-1}f)(z)=\int_{E}K_{e}(z,y)\,J_{g^{-1}}(y)^{1/2}f(g\cdot y)\,\mu(dy).

Applying
U_{g}
and using (
6
) again gives

(U_{g}T_{e}U_{g}^{-1}f)(x)=J_{g}(x)^{1/2}\int_{E}K_{e}(g^{-1}\cdot x,y)\,J_{g^{-1}}(y)^{1/2}f(g\cdot y)\,\mu(dy).

Perform the change of variables
y=g^{-1}\cdot u
(so
u=g\cdot y
). As in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, the Jacobian factors satisfy

\mu(dy)=J_{g}(u)^{-1}\mu(du),\quad J_{g^{-1}}(y)=J_{g}(u)^{-1}.

Substituting, we obtain

\displaystyle(U_{g}T_{e}U_{g}^{-1}f)(x)

\displaystyle=J_{g}(x)^{1/2}\int_{E}K_{e}(g^{-1}\cdot x,g^{-1}\cdot u)\,J_{g^{-1}}(g^{-1}\cdot u)^{1/2}f(u)\,J_{g}(u)^{-1}\mu(du)

\displaystyle=J_{g}(x)^{1/2}\int_{E}K_{e}(g^{-1}\cdot x,g^{-1}\cdot u)\,J_{g}(u)^{-1/2}f(u)\,\mu(du).

Comparing with (
8
) and (
7
), we see that

(U_{g}T_{e}U_{g}^{-1}f)(x)=\int_{E}K_{g}(x,u)f(u)\,\mu(du)=(T_{g}f)(x),

which proves (
9
). Self-adjointness, positivity and compactness follow from the corresponding properties of
T_{e}
and the fact that
U_{g}
is unitary.
∎

4.2
Spectral Equivariance Theorem

We are now ready to state and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.6

(Spectral Equivariance Theorem)
.

Assume that
T_{e}
admits the spectral decomposition (
5
) with eigenpairs
(\lambda_{k},P_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, where
\{P_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mu)
. For each
g\in G
, define

P_{k}^{(g)}=U_{g}P_{k},\quad k\geq 0.

Then:

(a)

For every
g\in G
,
\{P_{k}^{(g)}\}_{k\geq 0}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mu)
.

(b)

For every
g\in G
and
k\geq 0
,

T_{g}P_{k}^{(g)}=\lambda_{k}P_{k}^{(g)}.

In particular, the spectrum of
T_{g}
coincides with that of
T_{e}
.

(c)

The transported kernel
K_{g}
admits the spectral expansion

K_{g}(x,y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{k}P_{k}^{(g)}(x)P_{k}^{(g)}(y)\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\mu\otimes\mu).

(10)

Proof.

(a) Unitarity of
U_{g}
(Lemma
4.3
) implies that for all
k,\ell\geq 0
,

\langle P_{k}^{(g)},P_{\ell}^{(g)}\rangle=\langle U_{g}P_{k},U_{g}P_{\ell}\rangle=\langle P_{k},P_{\ell}\rangle=\delta_{k\ell}.

Moreover, since
\{P_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is an orthonormal basis and
U_{g}
is surjective (unitary), the family
\{P_{k}^{(g)}\}_{k\geq 0}
is again an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mu)
.

(b) Using the conjugation identity (
9
) and the eigen-relation (
4
), we compute

T_{g}P_{k}^{(g)}=U_{g}T_{e}U_{g}^{-1}(U_{g}P_{k})=U_{g}T_{e}P_{k}=U_{g}(\lambda_{k}P_{k})=\lambda_{k}U_{g}P_{k}=\lambda_{k}P_{k}^{(g)}.

Thus
P_{k}^{(g)}
is an eigenfunction of
T_{g}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
.

(c) Since
\{P_{k}^{(g)}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a complete orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mu)
and
T_{g}
is self-adjoint, we have the usual spectral representation

T_{g}f=\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{k}\langle f,P_{k}^{(g)}\rangle P_{k}^{(g)},\quad f\in L^{2}(\mu),

with convergence in
L^{2}(\mu)
. In kernel form, this is equivalent to

K_{g}(x,y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{k}P_{k}^{(g)}(x)P_{k}^{(g)}(y)\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\mu\otimes\mu),

which is precisely (
10
).
∎

Remark 4.7

(Orthogonal polynomials in twin spaces)
.

In the classical situation where
\mu
is supported on an interval of
\mathbb{R}
and
\{P_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
are orthonormal polynomials with respect to
\mu
, the functions
P_{k}^{(g)}
can often be identified with orthogonal polynomial systems in the transformed coordinates
g\cdot x
, possibly up to multiplicative Jacobian factors. In this sense, Theorem
4.6
shows that orthogonal polynomial bases in different twin spaces are obtained by transporting a single base system
\{P_{k}\}
through the group action.

Corollary 4.8

(Equivariance of spectral truncation)
.

For any
K\in\mathbb{N}
and
g\in G
, define the spectral truncation operators

S_{e,K}f=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\langle f,P_{k}\rangle P_{k},\quad S_{g,K}f=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\langle f,P_{k}^{(g)}\rangle P_{k}^{(g)}.

Then, for all
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
,

S_{g,K}=U_{g}S_{e,K}U_{g}^{-1}.

In particular, spectral truncation is equivariant under twin transport.

Proof.

Using
P_{k}^{(g)}=U_{g}P_{k}
and the unitarity of
U_{g}
, we have

\langle f,P_{k}^{(g)}\rangle=\langle f,U_{g}P_{k}\rangle=\langle U_{g}^{-1}f,P_{k}\rangle.

Therefore

S_{g,K}f=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\langle f,P_{k}^{(g)}\rangle P_{k}^{(g)}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\langle U_{g}^{-1}f,P_{k}\rangle U_{g}P_{k}=U_{g}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K}\langle U_{g}^{-1}f,P_{k}\rangle P_{k}\right)=U_{g}S_{e,K}U_{g}^{-1}f,

which yields the desired identity.
∎

Corollary 4.9

(Spectral Equivariance for Classical Orthogonal Polynomials)
.

Let
(E,\mu)
be either
([-1,1],dx)
or
(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
where
\gamma
is the standard Gaussian measure. Let

K_{e}(x,y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{k}P_{k}(x)P_{k}(y)

be the classical Mercer expansion of the base kernel in the corresponding orthogonal polynomial basis:

•

Legendre polynomials
P_{k}
on
[-1,1]
with weight
w(x)\equiv 1
,

•

Hermite polynomials
H_{k}
on
\mathbb{R}
with respect to
d\gamma(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\,dx
.

Let
G
act on
E
by:

g\cdot x=\begin{cases}ax+b,&\text{Legendre case},\\
\alpha x,&\text{Hermite case}.\end{cases}

Then for each
g\in G
, the transported kernel

K_{g}(x,y)=J_{g}(x)^{1/2}J_{g}(y)^{1/2}K_{e}(g^{-1}\cdot x,g^{-1}\cdot y)

admits the spectral expansion

K_{g}(x,y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{k}P_{k}^{(g)}(x)P_{k}^{(g)}(y),

where:

•

in the Legendre case, the transported basis
P_{k}^{(g)}
is a normalized affine transform of
P_{k}
;

•

in the Hermite case, the transported basis is

H_{k}^{(g)}(x)=\alpha^{-1/2}H_{k}(\alpha^{-1}x),

forming again an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\gamma)
.

Proof.

The Legendre case corresponds to the affine group acting on
[-1,1]
with a constant Jacobian. The Hermite case corresponds to the multiplicative group
(0,\infty)
acting on
\mathbb{R}
by dilations
x\mapsto\alpha x
with the Gaussian Jacobian

J_{\alpha}(x)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha^{2})x^{2}\right).

In both cases, the assumptions of Theorem
4.6
hold:
U_{g}
is unitary on
L^{2}(\mu)
,
T_{g}=U_{g}T_{e}U_{g}^{-1}
, and
P_{k}^{(g)}:=U_{g}P_{k}
forms an orthonormal basis that diagonalizes
T_{g}
. The explicit formula for
H_{k}^{(g)}
follows from

(U_{\alpha}f)(x)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{4}(1-\alpha^{2})x^{2}\right)f(\alpha^{-1}x),

combined with the well-known identity

H_{k}(\alpha^{-1}x)=\alpha^{-k}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{k}{2j}(1-\alpha^{2})^{j}H_{k-2j}(x).

This yields the announced expression up to normalization.
∎

5
Orthogonal series estimators as projections in Twin Kernel Spaces

In this section we show that classical orthogonal polynomial estimators arise naturally as orthogonal projections in Twin Kernel Spaces. The key idea is that an estimator constructed in a twin space
g\cdot E
is obtained by transporting the base estimator in
E
through the unitary operator
U_{g}
.

5.1
Projection in the base RKHS

Let
K_{e}
be the base kernel with spectral expansion
K_{e}(x,y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\lambda_{k}P_{k}(x)P_{k}(y)
. Given a target function
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
and a truncation level
K\in\mathbb{N}
, the classical orthogonal series estimator is

\hat{f}_{e,K}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\hat{\theta}_{k}P_{k}(x),\quad\hat{\theta}_{k}=\langle\hat{f}_{\mathrm{emp}},P_{k}\rangle,

(11)

where
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{emp}}
is the empirical estimator.

Proposition 5.1

(Projection property)
.

The estimator
\hat{f}_{e,K}
is the orthogonal projection of
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{emp}}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{e,K}=\mathrm{span}\{P_{0},\ldots,P_{K}\}
.

5.2
Equivariance of orthogonal series estimators

Theorem 5.2

(Equivariance of spectral estimators)
.

For every
g\in G
and truncation level
K
,

\hat{f}_{g,K}=U_{g}\hat{f}_{e,K}.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.8
,
S_{g,K}=U_{g}S_{e,K}U_{g}^{-1}
. Applying this to
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{emp}}^{(g)}=U_{g}\hat{f}_{\mathrm{emp}}
gives the result.
∎

This theorem reveals that orthogonal polynomial estimators
commute with the twin transport induced by the group action
.

5.3
Geometric interpretation

Theorem
5.2
implies:

•

The same statistical procedure produces consistent estimators in all twin spaces.

•

Choice of geometry modifies basis functions but not the estimator form.

•

The diversity of orthogonal polynomial bases reflects different geometric frames rather than fundamentally different estimators.

Relationship with classical kernel estimators

This section shows that classical smoothing kernels correspond to transported versions of a base kernel, and that Parzen–Rosenblatt and Nadaraya–Watson estimators can be interpreted as projections in a twin geometry.

6.1
Bandwidth as a group action

Consider the multiplicative group
G=(0,\infty)
acting on
E=\mathbb{R}
via
g_{h}\cdot x=x/h
. The Jacobian is
J_{h}(x)=h^{-1}
, and the transported kernel is

K_{g_{h}}(x,y)=h^{-1}K_{e}\left(\frac{x}{h},\frac{y}{h}\right).

Thus a classical smoothing kernel
K_{h}
is a transported version of the base kernel:
K_{h}=K_{g_{h}}
.

Key insight:
Bandwidth selection = choice of twin space geometry.

Proposition 6.1
.

The Parzen–Rosenblatt estimator
\hat{f}_{h}
is the orthogonal projection of
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{emp}}
onto the twin RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{g_{h}}
.

6.2

•

soft spectral cutoff
\lambda_{k}^{\mathrm{new}}=\lambda_{k}\exp(-c_{k}h^{2})

•

Orthogonal series:

Both are spectral operations in twin RKHSs.

(Equivariance of kernel smoothing)

Let
K_{h}=K_{g_{h}}
be a transported kernel. Then
\hat{f}_{h}=U_{g_{h}}\hat{f}_{e}
.

Examples and illustrations

7.1
Gaussian Twin Spaces and Hermite Polynomials

Let
E=\mathbb{R}
with Gaussian measure. The base kernel is the Mehler kernel
(
Mehler 1866
)

K_{e}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\rho^{k}H_{k}(x)H_{k}(y),\quad|\rho|<1.

For dilation
g_{\alpha}:x\mapsto\alpha x
, the Jacobian is
J_{\alpha}(x)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha^{2})x^{2}\right)
, and the transported basis is

H_{k}^{(g_{\alpha})}(x)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{4}(1-\alpha^{2})x^{2}\right)H_{k}(\alpha^{-1}x).

Interpretation:
Small
\alpha
emphasizes fine-scale oscillations, large
\alpha
smooths them.

Affine Twin Spaces and Legendre Polynomials

Let
E=[-1,1]
with Lebesgue measure. For affine transformation
g_{a,b}:x\mapsto ax+b
:

P_{k}^{(g_{a,b})}(x)=a^{-1/2}P_{k}\left(\frac{x-b}{a}\right).

Interpretation:
Affine deformations transport the Legendre basis while preserving eigenvalues.

7.3
Multimodal Transport Example

Consider a bimodal density
f(x)=0.5\,\varphi(x+2)+0.5\,\varphi(x-2)
. Define transported bases centered at each mode:

H_{k}^{(g_{1})}(x)=U_{g_{1}}H_{k}(x),\quad H_{k}^{(g_{2})}(x)=U_{g_{2}}H_{k}(x),

where
g_{1}:x\mapsto x+2
and
g_{2}:x\mapsto x-2
.

The multimodal density admits efficient representation:

f(x)\approx 0.5\sum_{k=0}^{K}\theta_{k,1}H_{k}^{(g_{1})}(x)+0.5\sum_{k=0}^{K}\theta_{k,2}H_{k}^{(g_{2})}(x).

This demonstrates that Twin Kernel Spaces provide a principled mechanism for mode-adaptive estimation.

Convergence in Twin Kernel Spaces

8.1
Assumptions

Let
K_{e}
be a base Mercer kernel with eigenvalues
(\lambda_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and eigenfunctions
(P_{k})_{k\geq 0}
. Assume:

(A1)

Spectral decay:

\lambda_{k}\asymp k^{-2s}
or
\lambda_{k}\asymp e^{-ck^{\alpha}}
.

(A2)

Smoothness:

f\in\mathcal{H}_{e}^{t}
for some
t>0
.

(A3)

i.i.d. sampling:

\|\hat{f}_{\mathrm{emp}}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

8.2
Main result

Theorem 8.1

(Convergence in Twin Kernel Spaces)
.

Let
K=K(n)\to\infty
with
K(n)/n\to 0
. For every
g\in G
:

\|\hat{f}_{g,K}-f^{(g)}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\xrightarrow{p}0,

and the convergence rate is identical to that in the base geometry.

Proof.

Using unitarity:
\|\hat{f}_{g,K}-f^{(g)}\|=\|U_{g}(\hat{f}_{e,K}-f)\|=\|\hat{f}_{e,K}-f\|
.
∎

Key message:
Convergence is geometry-invariant.

9
Bias–variance analysis in Twin Kernel Spaces

9.1
Bias

The bias term is
\|\mathbb{E}\hat{f}_{g,K}-f^{(g)}\|^{2}=\sum_{k>K}\theta_{k}^{2}=O(K^{-2t})
.

9.2
Variance

The variance term is
\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}_{g,K}-\mathbb{E}\hat{f}_{g,K}\|^{2}\asymp K/n
.

9.3
Optimal choice of
K

Balancing bias and variance:
K\asymp n^{1/(2t+1)}
, yielding the optimal rate

\|\hat{f}_{g,K}-f^{(g)}\|\asymp n^{-t/(2t+1)}.

Key message:
Bias–variance tradeoff is invariant under twin transport.

10
Minimax rates in Twin Kernel Spaces

10.1
Function classes

Define
\mathcal{F}_{t}(R)=\left\{f=\sum_{k\geq 0}\theta_{k}P_{k}:\sum_{k\geq 0}\theta_{k}^{2}\lambda_{k}^{-t}\leq R^{2}\right\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(g)}(R)=U_{g}(\mathcal{F}_{t}(R))
.

Theorem 10.1

(Minimax risk equivalence)
.

For every
g\in G

\inf_{\hat{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{t}(R)}\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}^{(g)}-f^{(g)}\|^{2}=\inf_{\hat{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{t}(R)}\mathbb{E}\|\hat{f}-f\|^{2}\asymp n^{-2t/(2t+1)}.

Key message:
Minimax risk is entirely geometry-invariant.
