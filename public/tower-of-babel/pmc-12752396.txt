Single-cell multi-omics analysis reveals drivers of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma metastasis
Authors: Zetian Zhang, Hao Dou, Shangyumeng Zhao, Jiaqi Wang, Meiru Chen, Xiaoli Xie, Xiangcong Tian, Dongqiang Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is a highly heterogeneous malignancy with poor prognosis, in which tumor metastasis represents a critical event driving disease progression and treatment failure. Understanding the cellular drivers of ICC metastasis and establishing effective risk assessment models are essential for improving patient outcomes.MethodsWe analyzed the GSE201425 single-cell RNA sequencing dataset to explore the cellular origins and mechanisms of ICC metastasis. Copy number variation (CNV) profiling and clonal evolution analysis were performed to identify malignant epithelial cells. Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed metastasis-associated epithelial cells (MAECs). Key biomarkers within MAECs were screened, and a Metastasis Index (Met-Index) based on one-class logistic regression was constructed to assess metastasis risk in bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA-CHOL cohort. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed in 34 clinical ICC specimens. Functional validation was conducted in HuCCT1 cells using siRNA-mediated gene silencing, followed by CCK-8 proliferation assays and wound healing assays.ResultsMAECs were identified as a distinct epithelial subpopulation enriched in metastatic ICC samples. Biomarkers including MMP7, FXYD2, and PTHLH were upregulated in MAECs and strongly correlated with metastatic status. The Met-Index effectively stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups, with the high Met-Index group exhibiting significantly worse overall survival (p = 0.005) and progression-free survival (p = 0.015). Immunofluorescence validation confirmed increased expression of MMP7, FXYD2, and PTHLH in metastatic tumors, which correlated with advanced clinicopathological features. Functional assays demonstrated that knockdown of these biomarkers inhibited HuCCT1 cell proliferation and suppressed cell migration capacity.ConclusionsThis study delineates the cellular origin and molecular drivers of ICC metastasis at the single-cell level, identifies MAECs as a metastasis-related subpopulation, and establishes a clinically relevant Met-Index with prognostic value. These findings provide potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for the early identification and management of ICC patients at high risk of metastasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15253-y.

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) refers to a primary malignant tumor originating from the epithelial cells of the bile ducts. It is the second most common type of primary liver cancer, accounting for 20% of all hepatic malignancies and 3% of all gastrointestinal malignancies. Over the past four years, the incidence of ICC has risen by 140% [1]. The prevalence of ICC exhibits significant geographical variation, with incidence rates in East Asia compared to Western countries. This is thought to be due to a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility and a prevalence of geographic risk factors [2, 3]. Additionally, ICC tends to occur more frequently in males and elderly patients, but in recent years, there has been an increase in incidence among females and younger patients [4].

ICC may originate from small bile ducts, often characterized by small-sized tubular or acinar adenocarcinomas, with little to no mucin production. These cancers are more frequently associated with aberrations in fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) fusion genes or gain-of-function mutations in isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 or 2 (IDH1 or IDH2) [5]. Genetic alterations in FGFR2 or IDH1/2 have therapeutic implications. Additionally, ICC is recognized as a highly desmoplastic cancer with a rich tumor stroma.

Currently, ICC is considered a highly heterogeneous malignancy with poor prognosis, with approximately 30% of diagnosed patients being eligible for curative resection. However, the recurrence rate after surgery is 50%−60%, and the 5-year survival rate ranges from 8% to 30%. Tumor metastasis is a crucial factor in ICC malignant progression and a major reason for treatment failure [6–8]. Despite multiple hypotheses regarding the causes of ICC metastasis, treatment options remain limited. Therefore, analyzing the differences in the immune microenvironment between primary tumors and metastatic lesions, uncovering the clonality and heterogeneity of tumor metastasis, and identifying metastasis-associated malignant epithelial cell subsets are crucial for the early detection of ICC metastasis. Recently, with the rise of immunotherapy and research into the tumor immune microenvironment, researchers have also begun to focus on the interactions between cells in the microenvironment and tumor cells. However, related studies are still in the preclinical stage.

This study focuses on ICC metastasis. Through analyzing single-cell transcriptomic data from primary and metastatic samples, we identified differences in the tumor microenvironment and malignant epithelial cell subpopulations, termed metastasis-associated epithelial cells (MAECs). For clinical application, we developed the Metastasis (Met) Index based on MAEC abundance and identified MAEC-associated biomarkers, including MMP7, PTHLH, and FXYD2. Among them, MMP7 and PTHLH have been reported in multiple tumors to be associated with metastasis [9, 10]. Our research lays the foundation for identifying ICC patients at high risk of metastasis and provides inspiration for further studies on the metastatic immune microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Collection of primary and metastatic samples of ICC and data acquisition

We downloaded a publicly available single-cell RNA-seq dataset (GSE201425) from the study by Shi et al. (2022), which includes primary and metastatic ICC tumor samples from two patients [11]. The TCGA-CHOL cohort was retrieved from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov), which includes extensive bulk RNA-seq data, comprising raw counts and TPM normalization values, along with comprehensive clinicopathological and follow-up information for 29 patients. Additionally, 34 intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) patients treated in the General Surgery Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Hebei North University between March 2020 and March 2024 were selected for the study, of which 17 cases had metastasis and 17 had no metastasis. Tissue sections were used for multiplex immunofluorescence staining to validate the expression of MAEC-associated biomarkers (MMP7, FXYD2, and PTHLH). Clinical information, including TNM stage, metastasis status, and other relevant parameters, was recorded for subsequent correlation analyses. The inclusion criteria were: (1) Age over 18 years and having complete clinical case data; (2) Pathologically diagnosed with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Received radiotherapy or chemotherapy; (2) Presence of autoimmune diseases or tumors in other locations; (3) Inability to cooperate with the study. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Hebei North University (K2024079). Patient information is shown in Supplementary Table 1. We have included a schematic overview of the single-cell RNA-seq analysis workflow to illustrate the experimental and computational procedures used in this study (Supplementary Figure S1).

scRNA-seq data processing and cell type annotation

Publicly available scRNA-seq data were obtained from the GSE201425 dataset. Seurat was used for data processing, quality control, and visualization [12]. A total of 8178 cells from metastatic samples and 8959 cells from primary samples were initially available. After stringent quality control and normalization (Supplementary Figs. 2–4), 8688 primary cells and 7832 metastatic cells were retained for downstream analysis [12]. Cells with fewer than 200 detected genes, more than 5000 detected genes, fewer than 500 UMIs, or greater than 10% mitochondrial gene content were excluded during the quality control process. To ensure the accuracy of cell clustering and cell type annotation, we applied the DoubletFinder package to identify and remove potential doublets prior to batch correction and dimensionality reduction. Doublets were detected based on artificially generated synthetic doublets and neighborhood enrichment models. Cells with high doublet scores were excluded from further analysis to avoid misclassification and artificial cluster formation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed based on highly variable genes (HVGs) to identify principal components (PCs). Cell clustering analysis was conducted based on the first 20 PCs, and dimensionality reduction and cell cluster classification were performed using the UMAP algorithm. Detailed parameter choices for dimensionality reduction can be found in Supplementary Fig. 5. To eliminate batch effects, integration algorithms such as Harmony [13], CCA [14], RPCA [15], and MNN [16] were used to integrate the samples (Supplementary Fig. 6), with the best-performing CCA integration algorithm selected for further use [12]. Marker genes for each cell cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2[FoldChange (FC)]| > 0.5 as screening criteria (Supplementary Fig. 7). Initial cell cluster annotation was completed automatically using the SingleR package with the Human Primary Cell Atlas (HPCA) as the reference dataset, and was subsequently manually verified and corrected using classic marker genes from the CellMarker database [17, 18].

Copy number variation (CNV) evaluation and subclone analysis

Using scRNA-seq expression and human chromosome sorting data, the CopyKat algorithm [19], along with inferCNV, was used to distinguish malignant epithelial cells (MECs) from non-malignant epithelial cells (ECs) [20]. Detailed CopyKat prediction results are provided in Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 2. InferCNV analysis was conducted under the following settings: cutoff = 0.1, cluster_by_groups = TRUE, denoise = TRUE, and HMM = TRUE. To reduce false positives in CNV inference, a default Bayesian latent mixture model with a threshold of 0.5 was applied to determine the posterior probability of variation for each cell. Subsequently, k-means clustering was applied to hierarchically cluster the CNV scores of all epithelial cells and reference cells (fibroblasts, endothelial cells). Epithelial cells with low CNV scores clustering with reference cells were considered non-malignant, while high-score cells were defined as malignant epithelial cells. To demonstrate tumor clonality and evolution, we further applied the “subclusters” mode to classify malignant cells into nine distinct subgroups based on different CNV patterns generated by the HMM. Each CNV was annotated as an amplification or deletion of either the p arm or q arm of the chromosome [21]. Subclones sharing similar arm-level CNVs were integrated to construct an evolutionary tree (Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Fig. 9). The phylogenetic tree visualizing the CNV structure of the subclones was generated using the Uphyloplot2 software [22], based on subclonal CNV profiles generated by inferCNV. Malignant epithelial cells were first divided into subclusters according to their CNV patterns inferred using a Hidden Markov Model. UPhyloplot2 then used these subclusters to visualize the evolutionary relationships among different tumor cell clones.To improve clarity, and branches with fewer than 5% of the total cells were not displayed in the final tree.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

Pathways with the most significant features were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (msigDB), and pathway enrichment analysis of the two cell groups was performed using the GSVA package [23]. The enrichment scores of each group’s cells were then compared, and pathways with |t-value| > 4 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched and different between the two cell groups [24].

Estimating intratumor heterogeneity with diversity scores

To estimate the degree of intratumor heterogeneity in the primary (Pri) and metastatic (Met) samples, a diversity score was calculated based on the gene expression data of all malignant cells. Dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA based on highly variable genes (HVGs), and the first 20 PCs were selected to represent the overall pattern of malignant epithelial cells. The diversity score for each sample was then determined by calculating the average distance of all malignant epithelial cells from their centroid, excluding outliers [25].

Pseudotime trajectory analysis of malignant epithelial cells

Based on the gene expression profiles of malignant epithelial cells, a pseudotime trajectory was constructed using Monocle v2.18.0 [26]. After dimensionality reduction and cell ordering, all malignant epithelial cells were projected and arranged along a trajectory with distinct branches, with cells within the same branch considered to be in the same cellular state. Branch-dependent gene expression modeling (BEAM) was further used to identify genes exhibiting branch-dependent expression patterns, providing insights into the mechanisms driving cell fate decisions [26].

Constructing a met-index using bulk RNA-seq data

A one-class logistic regression (OCLR) analysis was performed to calculate the Met-Index, representing the relative abundance of MAECs in primary intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL) samples. Using the survminer package in R, CHOL patients were divided into high and low Met-Index groups according to the optimal Met-Index cutoff. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis was used to evaluate overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) between the high and low Met-Index patients, with comparisons made using two-sided log-rank tests. The Met signature was generated using the gelnet v1.2.1 package in R [27], based on the mean-centered gene expression profiles of MAECs. A Spearman correlation analysis was then conducted between the weight vector of the Met signature and the gene expression profiles of TCGA-CHOL samples. The BM-Index of CHOL primary tumor (PT) patients was scaled between 0 and 1 using a linear transformation of the Spearman correlation coefficients (r). The specific details of the construction process are provided in Wang et al.’s study [28].

Drug prediction for patients with high met-index

Drug sensitivity data were obtained from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) v2 (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp.v2.1) [29] and the PRISM Repurposing dataset 19Q4 (https://depmap.org/portal/prism/) [30]. The pRRophetic algorithm was used to predict chemotherapy drug sensitivity, determined by the area under the dose-response curve (AUC) [31]. Ridge regression was applied to estimate the AUC of each drug in clinical cohorts, with a lower AUC value indicating higher drug sensitivity. The limma package was used to analyze the differential drug response between MAECs and other malignant epithelial cells. Drugs with significantly lower AUC values (log2FC > 0.10 and P < 0.05) were identified as candidate drugs for MAECs. Similarly, candidate drugs for patients with high Met-Index were identified based on significantly lower AUC values and a negative correlation between Met-Index and AUC (r < −0.3 and P < 0.05). Sorafenib was preliminarily predicted to inhibit ICC metastasis.

Immunofluorescence (IF) and quantitative analysis

Immunofluorescence experiment (IF) and quantitative analysis, including 17 samples of primary intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and 17 metastatic samples, were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin (FFPE), and then immunofluorescence experiment was carried out on sections. The antibody and concentration ratio used for each slice are: anti-MMP 7(10374-2-AP, Pt, 1: 400), anti-FXYD 2(11198-1-AP, Pt, 1: 100), anti-PTHLH(DF 6532, Affinity, 1: 400) and secondary antibody. Immunofluorescence images were recorded by panoramic digital slide scanner (3D HISTECH), the percentage of positive cells was calculated by imagej software, and the GraphPad Prism was used for analysis and mapping.

Cell lines

HIBEpiC and HuCCT1 cell lines were purchased from ATCC and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), 1% L-glutamine (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). All cells were routinely checked every 2 months for mycoplasma contamination using the EZ-PCR Mycoplasma Test Kit (Biological Industries, Israel).

siRNA transfection

For gene silencing experiments, siRNA was transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, siRNA (20 nM) targeting the gene of interest was mixed with the transfection reagent in Opti-MEM medium (Gibco), and the mixture was incubated for 10–15 min at room temperature to allow complex formation.

HuCCT1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well 24 h prior to transfection. On the day of transfection, cells were washed once with PBS and then treated with the siRNA-lipid complex in complete growth medium without antibiotics. After 6 h of incubation at 37 °C in a CO₂ incubator, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh complete medium containing antibiotics.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from HuCCT1 cells 48 h after siRNA transfection using RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay, and equal amounts of lysates were subjected to SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against MMP7 (1:1,000), FXYD2 (1:1,000), PTHLH (1:1,000), and GAPDH (1:5,000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and developed using ECL. Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ.

Cell proliferation assay (CCK-8 assay)

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Japan). Briefly, HuCCT1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10³ cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. After transfection with siRNAs targeting MMP7, FXYD2, or PTHLH, cells were cultured for 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. At each time point, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Each condition was tested in triplicate, and experiments were repeated three times independently.

Wound healing assay

Cell migratory ability was evaluated by wound healing assay. Transfected HuCCT1 cells were seeded into six-well plates and grown to nearly 90% confluence. A sterile 200-µL pipette tip was used to generate a straight scratch in the cell monolayer. Detached cells were gently removed with PBS, and serum-free medium was added. Statistical analysis

Experimental results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), with statistical analysis primarily performed using R (v4.2.3) software. For comparisons between two groups, if the data met the assumptions of normality (Shapiro-Wilk test, p > 0.05) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test, p > 0.05), a t-test was used; otherwise, a nonparametric test (Wilcoxon test) was employed. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomics reveals distinct cellular composition and immune microenvironment in primary and metastatic ICC samples

To explore the characteristics and potential mechanisms of ICC (intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma) metastasis, we utilized dataset GSE201425, which contains single-cell transcriptomic data from two patients, each with samples from both primary ICC tumors and their corresponding metastatic sites. We followed a standard single-cell sequencing data analysis pipeline, including quality control (QC), dimensionality reduction, clustering, and cell subtype identification.

QC is a critical step in processing single-cell sequencing data, where low-quality cells are filtered out to ensure they do not affect downstream analyses. The comparison of the data before and after QC is shown in Supplementary Figs. 1–3. Since the data includes samples from two patients, batch effects were present (Fig. 1A). To remove these effects, we tested four integration algorithms: CCA, RPCA, Harmony, and MNN. After integration, the cells from the four samples were evenly distributed across each cluster (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating successful removal of batch effects. CNV analysis reveals the origin of metastatic tumor cells

In the previous step, we identified epithelial cells but could not determine whether they were malignant tumor cells or normal epithelial cells. Therefore, we used the inferCNV algorithm to assess the copy number variations (CNVs) of epithelial cells based on RNA expression, aiming to identify whether they were malignant (as malignant epithelial cells typically exhibit extensive chromosomal CNVs). Details can be found in the Methods section. After successfully identifying the malignant epithelial cells (Fig. 2A), we constructed a tumor evolution tree to visualize the specific CNV patterns in both primary (Pri) and metastatic (Met) samples. The results showed that all malignant epithelial cells in the metastatic tumor (Met) exhibited a 1q gain and 1p loss (Fig. 2B- right panel), while all malignant epithelial cells in the primary tumor (Pri) displayed a 13q loss and 6p gain (Fig. 2B- left panel). Further analysis of the specialized branches of the tumor evolution tree revealed that 57 genes on chromosome 1 shared CNVs between the Met and Pri samples (Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that the malignant epithelial cells (ECs) in the Met samples may originate from a Pri subclone that acquired CNVs in chromosome 1. Pseudotime trajectory analysis identifies specific epithelial cell subpopulations associated with metastasis

To further elucidate epithelial cell (EC) subpopulations and their roles in promoting metastasis (Met), we classified malignant ECs from Pri and Met samples into 10 subpopulations, referred to as epithelial clusters (ECs) 0–9 (Fig. 3A). Using pseudotime ordering, we conducted trajectory analysis and mapped the malignant cells onto different branches. ECs from both Pri and Met generally followed similar differentiation trajectories, participating in the transition between five distinct cell states (Fig. 3B). Given the uniqueness of the shared EC0-6 clusters between Pri and Met, we performed further pseudotime trajectory analysis on these clusters, which yielded three cell states (Supplementary Table 6) organized into one root and two branches (Fig. 3C). State S2 was predominantly enriched in Met cells (94.7%), while state S3 was mainly enriched in Pri cells (98.0%). State S1 was enriched in both Pri (75.2%) and Met (24.8%) samples (Fig. 3C). GSVA analysis revealed a high diversity of activated molecular pathways across the three cell states (Fig. 3D). For instance, EMT was significantly activated in state S2, suggesting that EMT may play a key role in promoting progenitor cell differentiation and the development of Met-associated cell subpopulations. Subsequently, we performed branch expression analysis modeling using Monocle v2.18.0 to identify branch-dependent genes for the three states. A total of 50 genes were identified as regulators of cell differentiation from state S1 (Pre-branch) to S2 (Cell fate 1) and S3 (Cell fate 2) (Fig. 3E). Overall, as the tumor cells in shared clusters experienced reversible switching from progenitor cells to two distinct cell fates, we believe this plasticity may reveal mechanisms driving metastasis. Thus, the cells in the shared clusters were defined as metastasis-associated epithelial cells (MAECs). Constructing the Met-Index model and exploring its association with clinical-pathological features in the TCGA-CHOL cohort

Based on bulk RNA-seq data from 29 intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) patients in the TCGA database, we applied the OCLR algorithm to quantify the relative abundance of MAECs within the tumor microenvironment and calculated the Met-Index (Fig. 5A). Using the surv_cutpoint function in the R “survminer” package, the optimal cutoff value of the Met-Index was determined as 0.245214, which divided patients into high and low Met-Index groups. Survival analysis demonstrated that patients in the high Met-Index group had significantly shorter overall survival (OS, log-rank p = 0.005) and also exhibited an unfavorable trend for progression-free survival (PFS, log-rank p = 0.015) compared with those in the low Met-Index group (Fig. 5B, top). External validation using the GEO dataset GSE107943 confirmed the robustness of the model, showing that high Met-Index patients had significantly shorter OS (p = 0.036) and PFS (p = 0.006) than the low Met-Index group (Fig. 5B, bottom). Moreover, ROC curve analysis indicated that the Met-Index achieved good predictive performance for ICC metastasis, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.807 (Fig. 5C). Finally, univariate Cox regression analysis further demonstrated that the Met-Index was an independent risk factor for OS in ICC patients (HR = 10.52, 95% CI: 1.38–80.26; p = 0.023; Fig. 5D). Identifying therapeutic drugs for patients with high Met-Index and targeting MAECs

Further, we adopted a dual screening strategy to identify candidate drugs that inhibit iCCA metastasis: first, based on the Tumor Cell Line Drug Sensitivity Database (CTRP) and the Genomic Drug Sensitivity Database (GDSC), we screened out 16 and 24 compounds with high sensitivity to MAECs, respectively; second, for the patient subpopulation with high Met index, we obtained 17 and 84 compounds significantly associated with sensitivity from CTRP and GDSC, respectively. Through cross-analysis, sorafenib (Sorafenib) was identified as a potential therapeutic agent. In both the MAECs model and data from patients with high Met index, sorafenib showed significantly lower AUC values (Fig. 5E), and its sensitivity was significantly positively correlated with the Met index (Fig. 5F). However, the clinical value of this compound in inhibiting ICC metastasis still needs to be validated through more studies targeting MAECs and patients with high Met index.

Discussion

Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) ranks as the second most common primary liver cancer after hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), accounting for approximately 10–15% of cases [35–40]. It is known for its aggressive clinical behavior and poor prognosis, largely due to its asymptomatic nature in early stages. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, by which point curative surgery is often no longer feasible. Although surgical resection remains the most effective treatment, only 20–30% of patients qualify for this option [41, 42]. Even in cases where curative surgery is possible, the 5-year survival rate remains only 30% to 40%, and the recurrence rate is as high as 80% [43]. Patients with unresectable ICC who receive current treatment strategies have a median overall survival (OS) of only 11.7 months [40]. Metastasis is the main cause of treatment failure in ICC, and studies have shown that lymph node invasion is an independent risk factor for ICC prognosis [44]. The metastatic process is thought to be driven by a small population of malignant cells with stem cell-like properties, often referred to as metastatic initiating cells (MICs). These cells possess the ability to disseminate, survive dormancy, and eventually seed secondary tumors at distant sites [45–47]. Each stage of metastasis reflects a dynamic balance between MICs and host immunity, culminating in the outbreak of large metastatic colonies [48]. Understanding the origins, characteristics, and vulnerabilities of MICs and their host ecosystems is crucial for developing strategies to improve metastasis prevention and treatment.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a critical role in modulating tumor progression and metastasis. In our analysis, immune cell infiltration—particularly of T cells—was markedly reduced in metastatic ICC compared to primary tumors. Similar observations have been reported in other malignancies. For example, in colorectal cancer, metastatic tumors showed fewer cytotoxic T cells than non-metastatic ones, despite having comparable mutation profiles [49]. Spatial analyses of the TME in breast cancer patients revealed that different cancer subclones were associated with varying degrees of local immune infiltration [50]. These studies suggest that subclonal genetics in metastatic tumors may influence the establishment of the local microenvironment [51, 52].

Single-cell analysis of clinical samples has revealed transcriptional heterogeneity within and between tumors in advanced cancer. This heterogeneity cannot be explained by acquired genomic changes [53]. Phenotypic plasticity, the dynamic non-genetic adaptation to the metastatic cascade, and the ability to respond to changes in the TME allow MICs to enter dynamic dormancy, undergo transdifferentiation, and adjust their MIC status to cope with metabolic, redox, and immune stressors [54]. Primary tumor subclone populations with high plasticity are highly enriched in matching metastatic sites, indicating that plasticity at the primary site is a prerequisite for metastasis [55].

Through our single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, we identified a distinct epithelial subpopulation not previously characterized, which we termed metastasis-associated epithelial cells (MAECs). These cells showed unique transcriptional profiles and copy number variations (CNVs) compared to other epithelial subsets lacking metastatic traits. Their intermediate positioning along the epithelial differentiation trajectory, as revealed by pseudotime analysis, and their elevated CNV complexity support the idea that MAECs may represent a transitional state predisposed to dissemination.

To further evaluate the clinical significance of MAECs, we constructed a Metastasis Index (Met-Index) based on the relative abundance of these cells. When applied to the TCGA-CHOL dataset, the Met-Index effectively distinguished metastatic from non-metastatic cases and exhibited significant prognostic value for patient survival (Fig. 5A–B). Importantly, the predictive performance of the Met-Index remained stable across independent datasets and different cell compositions, underscoring its robustness and potential clinical utility as a biomarker for risk stratification in ICC (Fig. 5C–D). In addition to the computational analyses, we confirmed the expression of three candidate markers—MMP7, FXYD2, and PTHLH—in ICC clinical specimens through multiplex immunofluorescence (IF) staining. Functional validation experiments were subsequently performed in HuCCT1 cells. Western blotting demonstrated efficient knockdown of each target gene using two independent siRNAs (Fig. 7A). CCK-8 assays revealed that suppression of MMP7, FXYD2, or PTHLH significantly reduced cell proliferation over a 96-hour period, indicating their potential contribution to tumor growth (Fig. 7B). To further address the reviewer’s concern regarding their role in cell motility, we additionally carried out wound healing assays. The results showed that silencing of each of these genes led to a marked reduction in migratory ability compared with control cells (Fig. 7C), providing direct experimental evidence that they also facilitate ICC cell migration.

From a mechanistic perspective, the functions of these genes in cancer progression may differ. MMP7, a member of the matrix metalloproteinase family, has been widely implicated in extracellular matrix degradation, thereby promoting tumor invasion and metastatic dissemination. PTHLH has been associated with metastatic progression in several malignancies through its role in bone remodeling and tumor–stroma interactions, which may contribute to the colonization of distant organs. In contrast, the biological function of FXYD2 in cancer is less clearly understood. Given its role as a regulatory subunit of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, we hypothesize that FXYD2 may influence metastasis by altering ion transport, cell volume regulation, or intracellular signaling pathways, thereby facilitating the survival and migration of tumor cells within the metastatic niche. This warrants further mechanistic investigation in both in vitro models such as transwell migration/invasion assays and in vivo models to confirm and expand upon our current findings.Together, these results suggest that MMP7, FXYD2, and PTHLH play important roles in both the proliferation and migration of ICC cells, and that the Met-Index, when combined with spatial profiling strategies, may serve as a promising tool for the early detection, prognostic assessment, and therapeutic stratification of patients with ICC.

In parallel, we observed a marked reduction in immune cell infiltration—particularly T cells—in metastatic lesions compared to primary tumors. This finding reflects prior observations that metastatic tumors often exhibit an immune-cold phenotype, which could favor the emergence of clones such as MAECs that are more capable of immune evasion. Therapeutically, these insights suggest that reactivating anti-tumor immunity or selectively targeting immune-silent epithelial subclones could be a valuable approach in preventing or managing metastatic ICC.

Undeniably, there are several limitations to this study. First, although we observed distinct differences in the immune microenvironment between primary and metastatic ICC, it remains unclear whether specific immune cell subsets directly drive the metastatic process, and further in-depth mechanistic studies are required. Second, although our scRNA-seq analysis identified MMP7, FXYD2, and PTHLH as potential metastasis-associated markers, the functional characterization of these molecules is still incomplete. MMP7 and PTHLH have been reported to promote extracellular matrix remodeling, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and tumor–stroma interactions, thereby contributing to tumor invasion and metastasis. In contrast, the role of FXYD2 in malignancies has rarely been reported. Given its function as a regulatory subunit of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, it is reasonable to hypothesize that FXYD2 may influence metastatic potential through the modulation of ion channel activity, cellular ion homeostasis, or downstream pathways such as MAPK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling. Although our in vitro assays (CCK-8 proliferation and wound healing migration experiments in HuCCT1 cells) provided functional evidence that silencing these genes suppresses both proliferation and migration, more comprehensive studies—such as transwell migration and invasion assays, electrophysiological assessments of ion transport, and in vivo metastasis models—are still needed to confirm the detailed molecular mechanisms, particularly regarding FXYD2. Third, while our drug prediction analysis identified sorafenib as a potential therapeutic option for patients with high Met-Index, this result was primarily derived from computational databases (CTRP and GDSC). The lack of direct pharmacological validation (e.g., IC₅₀ assays) and large-scale retrospective clinical analyses may limit the immediate clinical applicability of these findings. Lastly, the scRNA-seq dataset employed in this study consisted of only two ICC samples, without detailed clinical annotations, which may not fully capture the heterogeneity of real-world cases. To enhance the robustness of our conclusions, we further validated the expression of candidate markers in 34 ICC clinical specimens using multiplex immunofluorescence (IF) and confirmed their functional impact on proliferation and migration in vitro. Nevertheless, larger-scale multi-center cohorts, combined with rigorous mechanistic studies and drug sensitivity experiments, will be essential to substantiate and extend our findings, and to establish the clinical utility of the Met-Index and its associated targets in guiding therapeutic strategies for ICC.

In summary, this study analyzed scRNA-seq data to characterize the immune microenvironment of ICC lesions, which are prone to metastasis. A cell subgroup, MAECs, was identified that may represent the origin of metastasis, along with potential biomarkers FXYD2, MMP7, and PTHLH. These results were then validated with clinical samples. These findings provide the potential to identify patients at high risk of metastasis earlier and more accurately in the future. Additionally, we predicted therapeutic agents that may be effective in patients with a high tendency for metastasis, laying the foundation for future related clinical studies.
