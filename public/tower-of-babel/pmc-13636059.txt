Enteric bacterial etiology, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and evaluation of epidemiological risk factors among elderly patients with diarrhea in an urban Kenyan hospital: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Eunice Maina, Perpetual Ndung’u, Peter Karanja, Gervason Moriasi
Published: 2026

Introduction

Diarrheal disease remains a leading cause of preventable mortality and morbidity worldwide, accounting for an estimated 1.31 billion cases and 1.53 million deaths annually, with burden heavily concentrated in low- and middle-income countries [1,2], and bacterial pathogens contributing substantially to this global mortality burden [3]. While epidemiological attention has historically focused on children under five, accumulating evidence indicates that elderly individuals aged 60 years and above represent an equally vulnerable group, with disproportionately higher rates of hospitalization, complications, and mortality [4,5]. Global Burden of Disease 2021 analyses estimate that diarrheal disease mortality among adults aged ≥70 years is among the highest of any demographic group in low- and middle-income countries, yet this population remains systematically underrepresented in surveillance programs and clinical research [4,6,7], including in relation to healthcare-associated infection risk during hospitalization [8].

The biological vulnerability of older adults to enteric infections is multifactorial. The progressive deterioration of innate and adaptive immune function with advancing age (immunosenescence) impairs host responses to enteric bacteria, increasing susceptibility to infection, disease severity, and the risk of systemic spread [9,10]. Additional host factors contributing to increased risk among the elderly include reduced gastric acidity, impaired gut motility, and intestinal microbiome dysbiosis [10,11]. These physiological vulnerabilities are compounded by the high prevalence of comorbid conditions, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and human immunodeficiency virus/Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS), which further compromise immune resilience and complicate clinical management [12,13]. Polypharmacy disrupts gut microbial ecology and increases risk of healthcare-associated enteric infections, particularly Clostridioides difficile [14,15].

In Kenya, as in much of sub-Saharan Africa, enteric bacterial infections are among the most frequent causes of hospital attendance, contributing substantially to the burden on public health infrastructure [16]. The country's growing elderly population—projected to expand significantly as life expectancy rises—will amplify demand for age-appropriate infectious disease care [17,18]. Yet national surveillance and treatment guidelines for diarrheal disease have largely overlooked older adults, with policies predominantly targeting children under five [19]. This creates a critical evidence gap, particularly in the context of rapidly evolving antimicrobial resistance (AMR), which threatens to undermine available therapeutic options for common enteric bacteria, including Escherichia coli, Shigella, Salmonella, and Campylobacter species [20].

AMR is now recognized as one of the foremost global public health threats, with Kenya documenting high-resistance rates among enteric bacteria to commonly used first-line antibiotics, including ampicillin, cotrimoxazole, and fluoroquinolones [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. The rapidly rising incidence of AMR carries particularly grave clinical implications for elderly patients who are more susceptible to infection and less tolerant of toxic alternative therapies [26,27]. Despite this urgency, a structured search of PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the African Journals Online database, conducted in December 2024 and updated in March 2025, identified no studies specifically characterizing the enteric bacterial etiology and AMR profiles of diarrheal illness in elderly Kenyan patients. The search strategy combined the Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords ‘diarrhea’ OR ‘diarrhea,’ ‘elderly’ OR ‘older adults’ OR ‘aged,’ ‘Kenya,’ and ‘enteric bacteria’ OR ‘antimicrobial resistance’ OR ‘antibiotic resistance.’ A total of 214 records were identified across the three databases; after de-duplication (n = 47 duplicates removed) and title/abstract screening (n = 152 excluded as irrelevant to the elderly population or Kenyan setting), 15 full-text records were reviewed, none of which addressed the enteric bacterial etiology and AMR profiles of diarrheal illness specifically in elderly Kenyan patients. This confirms a substantive and verifiable evidence gap that the present study addresses.

This study was therefore designed to determine the prevalence and species distribution of enteric bacterial isolates recovered from elderly patients (≥60 years) presenting with diarrhea at Mama Lucy Kibaki Hospital, Nairobi, to characterize antimicrobial susceptibility and multidrug resistance (MDR) profiles of recovered isolates, and to evaluate sociodemographic, clinical, and environmental risk factors associated with bacterial infection. By generating locally relevant evidence in this understudied population, this study contributes to the knowledge base required to inform clinical management, antimicrobial stewardship, and elderly-inclusive public health policy in Kenya, comparable sub-Saharan African settings, and globally.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted at Mama Lucy Kibaki Hospital (1°16′26″S, 36°53′56″E), a Level IV public referral hospital situated in Embakasi Sub-County, Nairobi County, Kenya. Established as a primary facility and subsequently upgraded to referral status, the hospital serves as a key tertiary point of care for residents of eastern Nairobi, drawing patients from a broad and socioeconomically heterogeneous catchment area that encompasses both planned formal residential estates and densely populated informal settlements, including Umoja, Kayole, Komarock, and Mathare North. This mixed urban demographic is epidemiologically significant, as residents of informal settlements are disproportionately exposed to risk factors for enteric illness, including inadequate access to safe drinking water, poor sanitation infrastructure, household food insecurity, and overcrowded living conditions [28,29].

The hospital operates fully functional outpatient and inpatient departments, including dedicated medical wards that regularly admit elderly patients presenting with acute gastrointestinal illness of varying severity. At the time of the study, the facility had an approximate bed capacity of 280, with inpatient admissions averaging several hundred patients per month across all wards. The elderly medical admissions unit provided a clinically rich recruitment environment, enabling enrollment of patients across the full spectrum of diarrheal disease severity.

The hospital’s diagnostic laboratory is equipped and staffed for a comprehensive range of microbiological investigations relevant to this study. These include routine aerobic stool culture on selective and differential media, anaerobic and microaerophilic incubation for the isolation of fastidious enteric bacteria, conventional biochemical characterization and identification of bacterial isolates, lateral flow immunoassay and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay-based antigen detection, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method with reference to current CLSI interpretive criteria. Laboratory operations were conducted in accordance with the facility’s standard operating procedures, and quality control measures, including the use of reference strains (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853), which were applied routinely throughout the study period to ensure the validity and reproducibility of microbiological results.

Study design and period

A hospital-based cross-sectional study design was employed, in which exposure variables and outcome data were collected simultaneously from each eligible participant at a single healthcare contact. This design is well suited to estimating the point prevalence of enteric bacterial infections and to characterizing the distribution of associated demographic, clinical, and behavioral risk factors within a defined clinical population presenting to a healthcare facility [30]. Cross-sectional studies are particularly appropriate in resource-limited healthcare settings where longitudinal follow-up presents logistical and financial challenges, and where rapid generation of prevalence data can inform immediate public health responses and clinical management protocols. A key limitation of this design is its inability to establish temporality between exposure and outcome; causal inferences drawn from the findings should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Data collection was conducted over 11 consecutive months, from February 1, 2025, to December 31, 2025. This extended period was deliberately chosen to capture the full range of climatic variability characteristic of Nairobi, spanning four seasonally distinct phases: the short dry season (January-February), the long rainy season or masika (March-May), the long dry season (June-September), and the short rainy season or vuli (October-December).This climatic variation is of potential epidemiological relevance, as the long rainy season is commonly associated with increased surface water contamination, flooding of pit latrines and sanitation infrastructure, and a higher burden of waterborne and foodborne enteric bacteria in urban Kenyan settings [31,32]. However, given the primarily descriptive objectives of this study and the sample size considerations underpinning the study design, seasonal variation in pathogen prevalence and AMR profiles was not formally analyzed as a primary study objective. This limitation should be addressed in future prospective or longitudinal studies with adequate power to detect seasonal differences.

Study population and eligibility criteria

Target and accessible population

The target population for this study was community-dwelling and institutionalized elderly individuals aged 60 years and above residing within Nairobi County who sought healthcare at Mama Lucy Kibaki Hospital during the study period. The accessible study population comprised elderly patients who presented to the hospital's outpatient department or were admitted to inpatient medical wards with a primary complaint of acute diarrhea during the 11-month data collection window. Both care settings were included to ensure representation of the full clinical spectrum of diarrheal illness in the elderly, from mild, self-limiting episodes managed in the outpatient setting to severe or complicated disease requiring hospitalization and intravenous supportive care.

The decision to enroll both outpatient and inpatient participants was deliberate and methodologically important. Restricting enrollment to inpatients alone would introduce a severity bias, potentially over-representing atypical or severe pathogens and underestimating the true community burden of more common but clinically milder infections. Conversely, restricting enrollment to outpatients would exclude the most critically ill elderly individuals, in whom the public health and clinical consequences of enteric bacterial infection are greatest.

Case definition

Diarrheal illness was operationally defined in accordance with current World Health Organization (WHO) criteria as the passage of three or more loose or liquid stools within any 24-h period [20]. Importantly, the case definition was applied at the point of initial clinical assessment and triage by the attending clinician, not re-verified independently by the research team at the point of enrollment. Enrollment could occur up to 24 h after initial presentation, during which time stool frequency may have declined from its peak as part of the natural clinical course or in response to initial supportive treatment. The stool frequency data presented therefore reflect frequency at the time of the structured enrollment assessment rather than peak presentation frequency and should be interpreted accordingly. All 271 participants met the WHO case definition at the time of clinical presentation, based on attending clinician documentation in the medical record.

Acute diarrhea was further defined as an episode of illness with a duration of less than 14 days at the time of presentation, consistent with standard clinical and epidemiological case definitions used in the literature [33]. Patients presenting with persistent diarrhea (14-29 days) or chronic diarrhea (≥30 days) were not eligible for enrollment, as the etiology, clinical management, and microbiological findings in these categories differ substantially from acute illness and would introduce etiological heterogeneity that could confound interpretation of the results.

Laboratory analysis

Stool sample collection and handling

Each participant provided a freshly passed stool specimen (5-10 g) in a sterile, wide-mouthed, leak-proof container. Specimens were labeled with unique participant codes, transported to the laboratory immediately, or refrigerated at 2-8°C for processing within 4 h of collection to minimize bacterial overgrowth and preserve pathogen viability. Strict biosafety level 2 protocols were observed throughout specimen receipt, processing, and disposal.

Culture, isolation, and identification

Each stool specimen was inoculated onto a panel of selective and differential media tailored to the expected enteric pathogen spectrum. For standard aerobic pathogens, all specimens were plated onto MacConkey agar for Gram-negative enteric bacteria, xylose lysine deoxycholate agar for Salmonella and Shigella spp., and thiosulfate citrate bile salts sucrose agar for Vibrio spp. Inoculated plates were incubated aerobically at 35-37°C for 18-24 h and examined for characteristic colonial morphology. Presumptive colonies were subcultured onto nutrient agar for purification and subsequently identified by Gram staining and a standard biochemical battery, including triple sugar iron agar reactions, indole production, methyl red and Voges–Proskauer tests, citrate utilization (IMViC panel), urease activity, motility testing, and lysine decarboxylase, applied as appropriate to each suspected genus [38]. Salmonella and Shigella species were serotyped by slide agglutination using commercial polyvalent and monovalent antisera (Difco, BD Diagnostics) per the manufacturer's instructions. E. coli isolates were confirmed by biochemical methods; formal pathotype characterization (ETEC, EPEC, EAEC, STEC, or EIEC), which requires virulence gene detection or confirmatory serotyping, was beyond the scope of this study. These isolates are accordingly described throughout as E. coli recovered from diarrheal stool specimens, and their causal role in diarrhea is inferred from recovery in the context of clinical illness rather than from confirmed virulence typing.

For C. difficile, all stool specimens were simultaneously inoculated onto cycloserine–cefoxitin–fructose agar (Oxoid) prepared per the manufacturer's specifications and incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 48-72 h. Anaerobic conditions were generated using AnaeroGen sachets (Oxoid AN0025A), achieving an atmosphere of less than 1% O₂ and at least 10% CO₂ in sealed jars [39]. Plates were examined for characteristic C. difficile colonies (circular to irregular, flat, ground-glass in appearance with a dull gray-white surface and a distinctive horse-manure odor) and suspicious colonies were Gram stained, where Gram-positive rods with subterminal oval spores were considered consistent with the organism, before subculture for purity. To ensure reproducibility, glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) and toxin A/B testing were performed on a saline suspension prepared from these purified, subcultured colonies recovered by anaerobic culture on cycloserine–cefoxitin–fructose agar, and not directly on unprocessed stool. Each suspect colony suspension was confirmed by a two-step immunoassay: GDH antigen enzyme immunoassay (EIA) followed by toxin A/B EIA, both using the C. DIFF QUIK CHEK COMPLETE system (TechLab), applied per the manufacturer's instructions for use on bacterial isolates. Only isolates that were both GDH-positive and toxin A/B-positive were classified as confirmed C. difficile; GDH-positive, toxin-negative isolates, a result that may reflect non-toxigenic strains or assay timing variability, were not classified as confirmed cases, in keeping with the study's conservative confirmatory threshold. This culture-based, isolate-confirmatory approach differs from the direct-stool GDH/toxin testing algorithm used in many high-income clinical laboratories; the culture step was retained here because it also yielded the isolate required for the Etest susceptibility panel described in Section 2.5.3, and because colony morphology and Gram-stain review provided an additional specificity check appropriate to a setting with limited prior laboratory experience diagnosing this organism.

For Campylobacter jejuni, all specimens were inoculated onto modified Campylobacter charcoal deoxycholate agar (Oxoid CM0739) supplemented with a selective growth supplement containing cefoperazone and amphotericin B (Oxoid SR0155E) and incubated under microaerophilic conditions (5% O₂, 10% CO₂, 85% N₂, generated by CampyGen sachets; Oxoid CN0025A) at 42°C for 48-72 h, following established protocols [40,41]. Characteristic colonies, flat, gray-white, spreading and moist with a watery edge, were Gram stained for slender curved or spiral-shaped Gram-negative rods and confirmed by positive oxidase and catalase reactions. Species-level identification was assigned by hippurate hydrolysis: a positive result was recorded as C. jejuni, while a negative result was consistent with Campylobacter coli; as C. coli was not an objective of this study, hippurate-negative isolates were not further characterized.

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were read at the point of ellipse–strip intersection. CLSI M11 [43] (content reaffirmed without revision, April 2025) provides validated clinical breakpoints for metronidazole (resistant ≥ 32 µg/mL) and vancomycin (resistant ≥ 4 µg/mL) against C. difficile, and general anaerobe breakpoints for moxifloxacin; these three agents were therefore classified as susceptible or resistant per CLSI M11. Neither CLSI nor EUCAST has published an approved clinical breakpoint for fidaxomicin or rifaximin against C. difficile; for these two agents, MICs are reported descriptively only (as raw values and as the proportion exceeding published epidemiological reference thresholds), and no susceptible/resistant classification is claimed. Disk diffusion results against the standard six-drug panel were also obtained for C. difficile for comparative reporting purposes; however, these are presented as epidemiological data only, and clinical management decisions for C. difficile infection should be based exclusively on the Etest MIC results described above.

MDR was defined, per Magiorakos et al. [44], as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more of the six antibiotic categories tested by disk diffusion. Consistent with this definition, three refinements were applied before calculating organism-specific and pooled MDR estimates. First, C. difficile was excluded, as previously described, because its resistance to beta-lactams and fluoroquinolones is intrinsic rather than acquired; its resistance profile is reported separately via Etest MIC data. Second, C. jejuni and the two genera lacking a validated organism-specific interpretive standard for the panel used (A. hydrophila, V. cholerae) were excluded from the MDR denominator, for the reasons set out above and in Table 1; their susceptibility data are reported separately as exploratory or descriptive observations only (Section 3.3.43). Third, categories representing organism-specific intrinsic (rather than acquired) resistance were excluded from that organism’s MDR category count: ampicillin for K. pneumoniae, which possesses a chromosomal sulfhydryl reagent variable (SHV)-type penicillinase conferring intrinsic resistance to aminopenicillins, and tetracycline for P. vulgaris, which EUCAST’s intrinsic resistance tables list as intrinsically resistant to tetracyclines (in addition to agents not tested here, including colistin, tigecycline, and nitrofurantoin). No other organism in the panel is recognized as intrinsically resistant to any of the six agents tested. The resulting validated-organism, intrinsic-resistance-adjusted MDR estimate (n = 80 isolates; Section 3.3) is reported as the primary MDR outcome of this study; the unadjusted estimate across all non-C. difficile disk-diffusion isolates (n = 100), which does not apply these refinements, is also reported for transparency and comparability with prior surveillance reports that do not routinely exclude intrinsic resistance or organism-validity limitations.

Data collection instrument development and validation

Instrument design and conceptual framework

A structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire was developed de novo for this study, as no validated instrument specifically designed to capture the sociodemographic, clinical, dietary, and environmental risk factors for diarrheagenic bacterial infections in elderly Kenyan populations was identified in the existing literature. The instrument was grounded in a conceptual framework drawing on established models of enteric disease transmission in resource-limited settings [45], the social determinants of health framework for older adults [20], and Kenya-specific evidence on diarrheal disease risk in urban populations [16,46]. The framework guided the selection of constructs to be measured and ensured theoretical alignment between each questionnaire domain and the study's specific objectives.

The instrument was structured into four thematic modules: (i) sociodemographic characteristics—age, sex, level of formal education, occupation, and marital status; (ii) clinical profile—pre-existing comorbidities, prescribed medications and polypharmacy status, diarrheal symptom onset and duration, stool frequency and appearance, and associated symptoms including fever, abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting; (iii) WASH practices—primary drinking water source, method of water treatment, toilet facility type and condition, handwashing frequency and technique, and food consumption habits including consumption of street food, uncooked or undercooked vegetables, raw meat, unpasteurized milk, and water of uncertain safety; and (iv) observed environmental sanitation — interviewer-assessed household sanitation status using a standardized observation checklist adapted from WHO Environmental Health criteria [20]. Clinical record review supplemented self-reported data for comorbidities and medication history wherever hospital records were accessible and available.

Instrument development process

The questionnaire was developed through a three-stage iterative process. In Stage 1, an initial item pool was generated through a systematic review of published instruments used in comparable studies of diarrheal disease risk factors in sub-Saharan Africa [16,46,47] and through review of WHO and Kenya Ministry of Health standardized tools for WASH assessment [19,20]. Items were drafted in English by the principal investigator, with particular attention to age-appropriate phrasing, cultural sensitivity, and the cognitive and functional considerations relevant to elderly participants.

In Stage 2, the draft instrument underwent expert review. A panel of five subject matter experts was constituted, comprising a senior clinical microbiologist, a public health physician with experience in diarrheal disease epidemiology, a geriatrician with knowledge of elderly patient assessment in Kenyan healthcare settings, a medical laboratory scientist specializing in enteric bacteria, and a community health expert with experience in WASH programming in Nairobi. Each panelist independently reviewed the instrument for domain completeness, item relevance, construct clarity, and appropriateness of response categories. Their feedback was consolidated and used to eliminate redundant items, refine ambiguous wording, and add constructs identified as important but initially absent; notably polypharmacy assessment and prior hospitalization history.

In Stage 3, the revised draft was translated into Swahili and locally spoken languages by certified medical translators to ensure accessibility for participants with limited English proficiency. Back-translation into English by independent translators was performed to verify semantic equivalence, and any discrepancies between the original and back-translated versions were resolved through consensus review by the translation team and the principal investigator.

Determination of instrument validity

Content validity was assessed using the Content Validity Index (CVI) at both the item level (I-CVI) and scale level (S-CVI/Ave). Each expert panelist independently rated the relevance of every item on a four-point ordinal scale (1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant, 3 = quite relevant, 4 = highly relevant), and the I-CVI for each item was calculated as the proportion of experts assigning a rating of 3 or 4. Items with an I-CVI below 0.78 were either substantially revised or deleted, following the criterion established by Polit and Beck [48]. Three items were deleted and seven were substantially revised as a result of this process. The S-CVI/Ave, calculated as the mean of all item-level CVIs, was 0.94, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.90 and indicating excellent overall content validity [49].

Face validity was evaluated through structured cognitive interviews with five elderly patients at Mama Lucy Kibaki Hospital who were not subsequently enrolled in the main study. Using a think-aloud method, participants were asked to read or listen to each item, provide their response, and then explain in their own words what they understood the question to be asking. This process identified four items that were consistently misunderstood, particularly those relating to water treatment methods; these were reworded using locally familiar terms, and response options were verified against community usage patterns before finalization.

Criterion validity was assessed by comparing a subset of self-reported clinical data items, specifically comorbidity diagnosis and medication use, against corresponding entries in hospital records for 30 participants whose records were retrievable. Although this subset was modest in size, agreement between self-report and medical records was high for all major comorbidities examined: 93.3% for diabetes mellitus, 90.0% for hypertension, and 96.7% for HIV/AIDS, collectively confirming acceptable criterion validity for the clinical module. WASH behavioral items were not subjected to criterion validation, as no gold-standard reference criterion exists for these exposures in this context; their susceptibility to recall and social desirability biases is acknowledged as a study limitation.

Determination of instrument reliability

Internal consistency of multi-item subscales within the WASH module was assessed using Cronbach's alpha (α), calculated from pilot data. The handwashing behaviors subscale (3 items) yielded α = 0.81, the food safety practices subscale (5 items) yielded α = 0.76, and the water safety subscale (4 items) yielded α = 0.79. All values exceeded the widely accepted threshold of α ≥ 0.70 [50], indicating satisfactory internal consistency across the WASH domains.

Temporal stability was evaluated in a subsample of 20 pilot participants who completed the questionnaire on two occasions separated by a 7-day interval, a duration sufficient to reduce recall effects while minimizing the likelihood of true attitudinal change in the interim. Agreement between administrations was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous items and Cohen's kappa (κ) for categorical items. ICC values for continuous items ranged from 0.83 to 0.91 (all P < 0.001), and weighted κ values for categorical WASH items ranged from 0.74 to 0.89, indicating good to excellent test-retest reliability throughout [51].

Inter-rater reliability was assessed during the pilot phase to ensure consistency of questionnaire administration across the research team. Three trained research assistants independently administered the instrument to five volunteer participants, and their responses were compared item by item. Overall percentage agreement was 94.2%, with κ = 0.88 for structured categorical items, indicating near-perfect inter-rater agreement. For the interviewer-assessed sanitation observation module, the ICC between raters was 0.91, confirming that the standardized observation checklist was applied consistently across observers.

Pilot testing and finalization

The instrument was piloted with a convenience sample of 20 elderly patients meeting the study's eligibility criteria at the study site; these participants included the subsample used for test-retest and inter-rater reliability assessments, and none were subsequently enrolled in the main study. The pilot evaluated interviewer training adequacy, questionnaire administration time, participant burden, data completeness, and the practical logistics of integrating questionnaire administration with stool sample collection. Mean administration time was 18.4 min (range: 12-27 min), which was considered acceptable for elderly participants. Missing data rates were below 3% for all items. Three minor wording refinements were made following a structured debrief with research assistants. The instrument was locked before commencement of main data collection, and no further modifications were made during the study period.

All research assistants completed a structured 2-day training program prior to data collection. The program covered the theoretical background of the study, standardized administration techniques for each questionnaire module, procedures for obtaining informed consent from elderly and cognitively impaired participants, completion of the sanitation observation checklist, and the management of refusals and partial responses. Calibration exercises were conducted to identify and resolve discrepancies in administration between team members before field deployment, providing an additional quality assurance step ahead of the inter-rater reliability assessment described above.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and managed in a password-protected database with double-entry validation. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables and categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Associations between categorical exposure variables and bacterial infection status (positive vs negative) were assessed using Fisher exact test for 2×2 comparisons and the Freeman–Halton extension of Fisher exact test for unordered multi-category variables (water source, water treatment method, toilet facility type, and handwashing practice). Each dietary exposure variable, uncooked or undercooked vegetables, street food consumption, water of uncertain safety, unpasteurized milk, and raw meat, was treated as an independent dichotomous exposure (exposed vs unexposed) and analyzed by a separate 2×2 Fisher exact test; these are not mutually exclusive categories of a single variable and were therefore not combined into an omnibus test. Unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for all subcategory comparisons using the first-listed category as the reference group, and for each dietary exposure using non-consumers as the reference. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied given the exploratory nature of the analyses. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.R-project.org).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Jomo Kenyatta University of Agriculture and Technology’s Institutional Scientific and Ethics Review Committee (Ref: JKU/ISERC/02316/1734) and a research permit was issued by the Kenya National Commission for Science, Technology and Innovation, NACOSTI (Ref: NACOSTI/P/25/4174493). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. For participants unable to consent independently, consent was obtained from legally authorized guardians or caregivers in accordance with national ethical guidelines. All data were de-identified using unique alphanumeric codes. Participation was entirely voluntary and the participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without consequence to their clinical care. Clinically actionable AST and Etest results were communicated to attending clinicians to inform appropriate patient management.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 271 elderly patients were enrolled, each contributing one stool specimen for culture. The majority were male (165/271; 60.9%), and the largest age stratum was 60-65 years (137/271; 50.6%). Nearly half of participants were widowed (135/271; 49.8%); educational attainment was bimodal, with 36.9% reporting no formal education and 35.1% attaining tertiary-level education. The most common occupational category was retired (121/271; 44.7%). Sociodemographic characteristics are presented in Table 2.

For E. coli, however, isolates were confirmed biochemically only; pathotype (ETEC, EPEC, EAEC, STEC, or EIEC) was not determined, and its contribution to the diarrheal illness in this cohort therefore cannot be established with certainty from these data. AST and MDR profiles

Organisms with validated, organism-specific interpretive criteria

This section covers the nine organisms for which the six-drug Enterobacterales disk-diffusion panel has a validated, organism-specific CLSI interpretive standard (Table 1): E. coli, S. flexneri, S. sonnei, S. typhi, S. enterica non-typhoidal strains (NTS), S. enteritidis, S. paratyphi A, P. vulgaris, and K. pneumoniae (n = 80 isolates in total). C. difficile (Section 3.3.2), C. jejuni (Section 3.3.43), and the two organisms lacking a validated interpretive standard for this panel, A. hydrophila and V. cholerae (Section 3.3.4), are reported separately.

Among these 80 isolates, resistance was calculated from organism-level aggregate resistance proportions rather than isolate-level cross-tabulated profiles; the proportion of isolates simultaneously non-susceptible in three or more categories therefore cannot be determined exactly, and the estimates below should be interpreted as best approximations from the available data. Among E. coli isolates (n = 34), resistance was highest for ampicillin (85.3%), cotrimoxazole (76.5%), and tetracycline (64.7%), with substantial non-susceptibility also to chloramphenicol (55.9%), ciprofloxacin (41.2%), and ceftriaxone (35.3%) (Table 3); an estimated 70.6% (24/34) of isolates were MDR. Salmonella typhi (n = 8) showed universal resistance to ampicillin (100%), chloramphenicol (100%), and cotrimoxazole (100%), with 75.0% resistance to tetracycline, yielding a 100% MDR rate (8/8), consistent with the classical multidrug-resistant typhoid phenotype; ceftriaxone retained full activity and ciprofloxacin remained active against 75.0% of isolates. S. flexneri and S. sonnei both demonstrated high resistance to ampicillin, tetracycline, and cotrimoxazole, with MDR rates of 70.0% and 83.3% respectively; ceftriaxone retained the greatest activity for both species. Salmonella NTS, S. enteritidis, and S. paratyphi A showed full susceptibility to all tested agents other than an isolated ampicillin signal in the NTS group (Table 4).

Two organisms in this group required a further, organism-specific adjustment before their MDR status could be validly classified, because each carries a well-characterized intrinsic (chromosomal, not acquired) resistance to one of the six panel agents, which the Magiorakos et al. [44] definition explicitly excludes from MDR category counts.

K. pneumoniae (n = 3) displayed 100% resistance to ampicillin and 66.7% resistance to ceftriaxone, cotrimoxazole, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and ciprofloxacin (Table 4). K. pneumoniae possesses a chromosomally encoded SHV-type penicillinase that confers intrinsic resistance to aminopenicillins; ampicillin non-susceptibility in this organism is therefore not an acquired-resistance category and cannot count toward its MDR classification. Because all five of the remaining categories showed an identical 66.7% (2/3) non-susceptibility rate, this is consistent with the same two isolates being non-susceptible across ceftriaxone, cotrimoxazole, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and ciprofloxacin, while the third isolate was susceptible to all five and resistant only to the intrinsically resistant ampicillin category. Excluding ampicillin, K. pneumoniae corrected MDR rate is therefore 66.7% (2/3), not the 100% (3/3) that would be obtained if intrinsic ampicillin resistance were incorrectly counted as an acquired MDR category; this correction is consistent with, and fully explained by, the aggregate marginal proportions available. All values in Table 4 and throughout this manuscript reflect this correction.

P. vulgaris (n = 6) showed complete resistance to tetracycline (100%) and high resistance to ampicillin (83.3%), cotrimoxazole (66.7%), and chloramphenicol (66.7%), with lower resistance to ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone (33.3% each) (Table 4). EUCAST’s intrinsic resistance tables list P. vulgaris as intrinsically resistant to tetracyclines (in addition to colistin, tigecycline, nitrofurantoin, and fosfomycin, none of which were tested here); P. vulgaris is not known to possess a chromosomal beta-lactamase, so its ampicillin resistance is considered acquired. Excluding tetracycline, non-susceptibility remained ≥66.7% across ampicillin, cotrimoxazole, and chloramphenicol, consistent with an MDR rate that remains approximately 66.7% (4/6) using only acquired-resistance categories (ampicillin, cotrimoxazole, chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone). Because isolate-level resistance profiles were not retained for this organism, this corrected estimate could not be verified with the same precision as for K. pneumoniae and should be regarded as an approximation rather than an exact recalculation.

Applying both refinements described in Section 2.5.3, restricting the denominator to the 80 isolates from organisms with a validated interpretive standard, and excluding the two intrinsic-resistance categories identified above, the pooled MDR prevalence among validated organisms was 62.5% (50/80). For transparency and comparability with reports that do not apply these refinements, the unadjusted pooled estimate across all 100 non-C. difficile disk-diffusion isolates (i.e., also including Campylobacter, Aeromonas, and Vibrio, and treating K. pneumoniae intrinsic ampicillin resistance as an acquired MDR category) was 60.0% (60/100). The two estimates are similar in magnitude because excluding Campylobacter (a high-resistance organism) was largely offset by excluding Aeromonas and Vibrio (both fully susceptible organisms under this panel); however, only the 62.5% (50/80) estimate reflects organisms and categories for which MDR classification is methodologically valid, and it is this estimate that should be used in any subsequent citation of this study’s pooled MDR finding.

difficile

For C. difficile (n = 18), Etest results on supplemented Brucella blood agar demonstrated full susceptibility to metronidazole (MIC range 0.25-1.0 µg/mL; all isolate MICs were ≤ 2 µg/mL, below the CLSI M11 resistance threshold of ≥ 32 µg/mL) and vancomycin (MIC range 0.5-2.0 µg/mL; all isolate MICs were ≤ 2 µg/mL, below the CLSI M11 resistance threshold of ≥ 4 µg/mL), confirming susceptibility to both first-line C. difficile-directed treatment agents by a validated clinical breakpoint. Moxifloxacin resistance, using the CLSI M11 general anaerobe breakpoint, was detected in 8/18 isolates (44.4%; MIC ≥ 8 µg/mL). For fidaxomicin (MIC range 0.016-0.125 µg/mL) and rifaximin (elevated MICs >8 µg/mL in 3/18 isolates, 16.7%), no CLSI- or EUCAST-approved clinical breakpoint currently exists for C. difficile; these values are therefore reported descriptively only, and we do not classify fidaxomicin or rifaximin results as “susceptible” or “resistant.” All fidaxomicin MICs observed (≤0.125 µg/mL) were well below published epidemiological reference values from surveillance cohorts elsewhere (typically ≤1-2 µg/mL), which is reassuring descriptively but does not constitute a validated susceptibility classification. MDR classification [44] was not applied to C. difficile, since its resistance to beta-lactams (including ampicillin and ceftriaxone) and fluoroquinolones (including ciprofloxacin) on the standard six-drug disk diffusion panel is intrinsic rather than acquired (Table 4).

jejuni, and organisms without a validated interpretive standard (exploratory data)

C. jejuni (n = 12) showed broad non-susceptibility on the standard six-drug panel to ampicillin (83.3%), ciprofloxacin (75.0%), cotrimoxazole (75.0%), tetracycline (66.7%), chloramphenicol (58.3%), and ceftriaxone (50.0%); 9 of 12 isolates (75.0%) were non-susceptible to at least one agent in three or more of the six categories (Table 4). We deliberately do not describe this figure as an “MDR rate” and it is excluded from the pooled MDR estimate reported in Section 3.3.1. As stated in Section 2.5.3, disk diffusion is not validated by CLSI or EUCAST for Campylobacter spp., and the panel used omitted azithromycin and erythromycin, the antibiotics most used to treat Campylobacter infection clinically. This resistance pattern is therefore reported here strictly as descriptive, exploratory epidemiological information; it should not be interpreted as a clinically valid MDR estimate and must not be used to guide individual patient management.

A. hydrophila (n = 6) showed 100% non-susceptibility to ampicillin (consistent with this genus’s well-documented intrinsic chromosomal beta-lactamases) and low-level non-susceptibility (16.7%, 1/6) to cotrimoxazole, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and ceftriaxone, with full susceptibility to ciprofloxacin. V. cholerae Ogawa (n = 2) showed non-susceptibility to cotrimoxazole (100%), ampicillin and chloramphenicol (50.0% each), and full susceptibility to tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, and ceftriaxone. As neither genus is Enterobacterales, and CLSI’s organism-specific breakpoints for these genera (M45) were not applied in this study (Section 2.5.3), these results are reported descriptively only, are not classified as susceptible/resistant against a validated standard and are excluded from all MDR estimates. Given sample sizes of n ≤ 6 for these organisms, findings for both are in any case treated as descriptive only.

C. difficile isolates were associated with the highest comorbidity burden (88.9% had ≥ 1 comorbidity), consistent with the established predilection of this organism for immunocompromised and healthcare-exposed hosts. C. difficile also showed the lowest proportion of bloody diarrhea (0%), in keeping with its predominantly non-invasive toxin-mediated pathogenesis. These descriptive patterns are internally consistent and hypothesis-generating, hence formal statistical comparisons across organism groups were not performed given the modest per-organism sample sizes.

WASH risk factors

Associations between WASH indicators, dietary exposures, and sanitation status with bacterial infection status are presented in Table 7. The overall positivity rate was 43.5% (118/271). Across all 20 exposure subcategories, including water source, treatment method, toilet facility type, handwashing frequency, dietary risk behaviors, and observed household sanitation, infection positivity was remarkably uniform, ranging from 40.0% to 45.2%, a spread of only 5.2 percentage points. No comparison reached statistical significance; exact Fisher/Freeman–Halton P-values ranged from 0.85 to 1.00 across all 20 subcategories (Table 7).

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic characterization of potential enteric bacterial isolates, AMR profiles, and associated risk factors among elderly patients at an urban public hospital in Kenya. The 43.5% positivity rate (31.0% excluding untyped E. coli), and the pooled MDR prevalence of 62.5% (50/80) among organisms with validated interpretive criteria, demonstrate a substantial burden of bacterial diarrheal illness and resistance in this population. The organism spectrum, spanning community-acquired and foodborne organisms alongside the healthcare-associated anaerobe C. difficile, points to a heterogeneous, multifactorial etiology and underscores the diagnostic and therapeutic complexity of managing this condition in a resource-limited setting.

Sociodemographic and age patterns

The male preponderance (60.9%), the concentration of both participants and isolates in the youngest elderly stratum (60-65 years, 50.6% and 50.8% respectively), and the progressive decline in both measures across older strata are all consistent with the known care-seeking pattern of hospital-based convenience samples in sub-Saharan Africa: men and the “young-old” are more mobile and more able to present independently, whereas the oldest-old and women—particularly widows facing mobility, financial, or social barriers — are more likely to be underrepresented in facility-based surveillance despite bearing at least as much of the true community burden [4,52,53], consistent with broader evidence that age-specific diarrheal disease burden across sub-Saharan Africa is systematically under-ascertained by facility-based data alone [54]. Community-based surveillance is needed to close this selection gap. The absence of a significant sex or age-subgroup difference in infection positivity (Section 3.2) is consistent with immunosenescence acting broadly across the elderly spectrum rather than peaking in a specific subgroup [9,10].

The bimodal educational profile (36.9% no formal schooling, 35.1% tertiary) reflects generational inequities in schooling access rather than any detectable effect on infection risk: no significant association between education and infection status was observed, most plausibly because biological vulnerability and comorbidity burden drive risk in an elderly hospital cohort largely irrespective of socioeconomic position [4]. Similarly, the high proportion of widowed participants (49.8%) and the occupational profile dominated by retirees and subsistence farmers (44.7% and 36.9%) describe a population with elevated social vulnerability — widowhood in older age is independently linked to social isolation, reduced nutritional adequacy, and diminished immune resilience [55] — and limited capacity to absorb healthcare costs, reinforcing the case for embedding elderly-focused diarrheal disease care within free-at-point-of-access public health infrastructure.

Organism spectrum

E. coli was the leading isolate (28.8%), concordant with global surveillance identifying it as a principal cause of bacterial diarrhea across age groups [23,56,57]. As pathotype characterization was not performed, its diarrheagenic role remains uncertain (Section 3.2); ETEC, EPEC, and STEC virulence gene detection would strengthen this attribution, as already achieved in other Kenyan settings [58].

C. difficile was the second most prevalent organism (15.3%), and its confirmation on cultured isolates by GDH antigen and toxin A/B EIA (Section 2.5.2) is a notable contribution given that this organism is widely underrecognized in sub-Saharan Africa owing to limited anaerobic laboratory capacity [59,60], despite being the leading cause of healthcare-associated diarrhea in high-income settings [61,62]. The high comorbidity burden among C. difficile-positive participants (88.9%) is consistent with its well-established risk profile and strengthens the biological plausibility of this finding, which warrants investment in anaerobic diagnostic capacity and C. difficile-specific infection control protocols in medical wards.

C. jejuni (10.2%) is increasingly recognized as an important cause of diarrheal illness in African adults [[63], [64], [65]]. Its detection, together with NTS serovars (S. enteritidis, non-typhoidal S. enterica), points to contaminated animal-source foods as a plausible transmission pathway, consistent with the limited hygienic controls and widespread veterinary antimicrobial use characteristic of informal urban food markets [66,67], and with the recognized epidemiology of invasive NTS in Nairobi’s informal settlements specifically [68]; elderly consumers, with diminished immune tolerance to foodborne contamination, are at heightened risk from this route. Both Shigella species (S. flexneri, 8.5%; S. sonnei, 5.1%) are classically associated with fecal-oral transmission via contaminated water and person-to-person spread [69], consistent with the substantial global shigellosis burden that continues to challenge elimination efforts [70]; their recovery in proportions comparable to Campylobacter and Salmonella reflects the coexistence of foodborne and waterborne pathways in this cohort. The typhoidal serovars recovered (S. typhi, n = 8; S. paratyphi A, n = 3) further implicate contaminated water and food as transmission vectors, and their molecular pathogenesis and propensity for MDR mirror patterns described for typhoidal and NTS elsewhere [[71], [72], [73]].

The presence of K. pneumoniae (n = 3) and A. hydrophila (n = 6), both opportunistic organisms, is consistent with hospital-associated colonization and/or secondary infection in immunocompromised elderly patients [[74], [75], [76]]; in the absence of a nondiarrhoeic control group, true infection cannot be distinguished from incidental colonization for these organisms, and their contribution to diarrheal etiology should be interpreted with caution. K. pneumoniae corrected resistance profile, 66.7% MDR after excluding intrinsic ampicillin resistance (Section 3.3.1), with resistance to ceftriaxone and four other agents consistent with possible extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production, remains a clinical concern given this organism’s increasing prevalence in Kenyan hospitals [24], though phenotypic ESBL screening and genotypic confirmation were not performed here. The isolation of V. cholerae Ogawa (n = 2), while descriptive only given the small number of isolates and the lack of a validated interpretive standard for this genus in the panel used (Section 3.3.4), is a sentinel signal of ongoing cholera endemicity in Nairobi warranting continued surveillance [[77], [78], [79]]; in accordance with Kenya’s Public Health Act (Cap. 242) and the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response framework, both cases were reported to the Nairobi County Director of Public Health at the time of confirmation and managed per national cholera case management guidelines.

Resistance patterns

The pooled MDR prevalence of 62.5% (50/80) among validated organisms (Section 3.3.1) represents a substantial resistance burden among Kenyan enteric isolates from elderly patients, consistent with rising national resistance trends [32]. Near-universal non-susceptibility to ampicillin and cotrimoxazole across E. coli, S. typhi, and Shigella species is consistent with decades of antimicrobial overuse without adequate culture-guided prescribing in Kenyan healthcare settings [[22], [23], [24],57]. The 100% MDR rate in S. typhi — universal resistance to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and cotrimoxazole — confirms the consolidation of MDR typhoid in Nairobi, mirroring reports from informal settlements [80]. Ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility in E. coli (41.2%) and S. flexneri (60.0%) is concerning given the role of fluoroquinolones in managing moderate-to-severe bacterial diarrhea in adults and mirrors the broader trend of rising fluoroquinolone resistance among enteric bacteria across the African region [81]; resistance to ceftriaxone in 35.3% of E. coli isolates signals erosion of this reserve agent.

Two organisms’ resistance data warrant interpretation within their distinct methodological context: resistance of C. difficile to beta-lactams and fluoroquinolones on the standard panel (Table 3) reflects well-characterized intrinsic mechanisms rather than emerging AMR; the clinically meaningful C. difficile finding is instead the Etest data confirming full susceptibility to metronidazole and vancomycin; moxifloxacin resistance in 44.4% of isolates is nonetheless notable given its association with hypervirulent, epidemic-prone ribotypes such as RT027 [82]. C. jejuni extensive non-susceptibility pattern (Section 3.3.4) is, by contrast, exploratory and epidemiological only, given the lack of a validated interpretive standard and the panel’s omission of azithromycin and erythromycin; it must not be used as a clinically valid MDR estimate. The signal is nonetheless broadly consistent with reports linking fluoroquinolone non-susceptibility in Campylobacter to veterinary antimicrobial use regionally [83,84], supporting a macrolide-inclusive Etest panel as a priority for future work.

WASH risk factors

The complete absence of a statistically significant association between any of 20 WASH or dietary exposure subcategories and infection status (all exact P ≥ 0.85) warrants critical interpretation rather than acceptance as a true null effect. Three explanations are plausible. First, the urban setting may have compressed exposure variability: most participants used relatively improved infrastructure, unlike peri-urban and rural settings where WASH gradients typically drive detectable associations [45,85], and the most-cited WASH–diarrhea meta-analyses [86] address childhood populations whose exposure–outcome relationships may not generalize to elderly cohorts. Second, all WASH and dietary data were self-reported, introducing possible recall and social desirability bias [87], plausibly explaining the near-identical positivity between ‘never’ (41.9%) and ‘always’ (43.8%) handwashing. Third, and of greatest weight, the study was powered for prevalence rather than subgroup risk-factor detection, so these null findings should be treated as inconclusive rather than as evidence of absence of effect.

Biologically, host-level determinants, such as immunosenescence, reduced gastric acidity, gut dysbiosis, and the high comorbidity burden documented here (>70% with ≥1 chronic condition), may lower the infectious dose threshold sufficiently that hygienic water and food handling alone cannot fully protect a severely immunocompromised host, consistent with the high C. difficile prevalence, whose risk is driven primarily by antibiotic exposure and healthcare contact rather than WASH conditions. Prior antibiotic use, polypharmacy, and healthcare contact frequency were not captured here and represent important unmeasured confounders for future risk-factor studies in this population.

Clinical and policy implications

The convergence of substantial resistance among validated organisms and a high comorbidity burden creates a demanding clinical scenario: patients most vulnerable to diarrheal infection are simultaneously least tolerant of the alternative therapies sometimes required when first-line agents fail, and dehydration may exacerbate pre-existing cardiac or renal disease [12,27]. It is important to emphasize, however, that many bacterial diarrheal illnesses are self-limiting and do not require antimicrobial therapy at all; international guidance restricts antibiotic treatment to specific clinical indications, such as fever or bloody diarrhea with a suspected treatable enteropathogen, or severe or invasive disease, rather than treating all culture-positive diarrhea empirically [88]. The resistance data presented here are therefore best used to inform when not to prescribe a given agent empirically and to justify investment in diagnostic and susceptibility-testing capacity for selective, evidence-based prescribing, rather than to recommend specific replacement regimens. We deliberately do not recommend alternatives to ampicillin, cotrimoxazole, or tetracycline: doing so responsibly would require evaluating the complete range of clinically recommended agents, together with outcome, safety, availability, and organism-specific treatment-indication data, none of which was within this study’s scope.

While the single-center, convenience-sampled design of this study limits extrapolation to the national level, the findings support the following priorities, pending validation through multi-site studies: (i) explicitly incorporating elderly patients into national diarrheal disease surveillance systems, from which they are currently excluded; (ii) strengthening antimicrobial stewardship and diagnostic susceptibility-testing capacity so that antibiotic use—where clinically indicated at all—can be culture- and susceptibility-guided rather than empirical; (iii) mandating anaerobic culture capacity and C. difficile GDH/toxin diagnostics in public hospitals serving elderly patients; (iv) implementing targeted infection prevention and control protocols, including contact precautions, for confirmed C. difficile infections; and (v) engaging One Health surveillance platforms to further characterize the veterinary antimicrobial use that may contribute to the exploratory fluoroquinolone non-susceptibility signal observed in Campylobacter and to resistance in ESBL-suspect Klebsiella, using methods with validated clinical interpretive standards.

Conclusion

E. coli, C. difficile (confirmed by GDH/toxin testing of cultured isolates), C. jejuni, and Shigella species were the most frequently recovered potential enteric bacterial isolates among elderly patients presenting with diarrhea at a major urban public hospital in Nairobi, Kenya, with an overall positivity rate of 43.5% (31.0% excluding untyped E. coli, whose pathogenic contribution cannot be confirmed without pathotyping). Among the 80 isolates from organisms with a validated, organism-specific interpretive standard, and after excluding intrinsic resistance categories, the pooled MDR prevalence was 62.5% (50/80); S. typhi showed 100% MDR, and K. pneumoniae showed a corrected MDR rate of 66.7% after its intrinsic ampicillin resistance was excluded, consistent with possible ESBL activity. Campylobacter and organisms lacking a validated interpretive standard for the panel used (Aeromonas and Vibrio) were analyzed separately as exploratory or descriptive data and are not part of this pooled estimate. All 18 confirmed C. difficile isolates retained susceptibility to metronidazole and vancomycin by a validated breakpoint, a clinically reassuring finding, while moxifloxacin resistance in 44.4% of isolates signals the potential for epidemic-associated ribotypes. Resistance to ampicillin and cotrimoxazole was near-universal among validated organisms, underscoring that these agents are unlikely to be reliable choices for empirical use in this population; however, because many bacterial diarrheal illnesses are self-limiting and do not require antimicrobial therapy at all, this finding argues for selective, susceptibility-guided prescribing and expanded diagnostic capacity rather than for adopting specific replacement regimens, which was beyond the scope of this study. The null WASH findings, in the context of high comorbidity burden and confirmed C. difficile prevalence, are most plausibly explained by the primacy of biological and healthcare-associated determinants of infection in this population. These findings make a compelling case that elderly patients in urban Kenya are a high-risk, diagnostically underserved population facing a convergence of substantial organism burden, meaningful AMR, and limited policy recognition. Priorities include expansion of anaerobic and microaerophilic diagnostic and pathotyping capacity, strengthened antimicrobial stewardship and infection control programs, and the explicit integration of elderly patients into Kenya’s national diarrheal disease surveillance agenda. Molecular characterization of key isolates and prospective multi-site cohort studies, incorporating a validated Campylobacter susceptibility method, are the essential next steps.

Limitations and future directions

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should inform cautious interpretation of its findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; temporal relationships between exposures and infection cannot be established, and prospective data collection is required to confirm the directionality of any associations identified here. Compounding this, consecutive convenience sampling within a single facility restricts generalizability to the immediate hospital catchment area and limits representativeness for community-dwelling elderly Kenyans or patients in other clinical settings. Multi-facility replication is necessary before findings can meaningfully inform national policy.

Statistical power presents a further interpretive constraint. The study was powered for the primary prevalence objective; WASH risk-factor analyses were exploratory in nature and likely underpowered to detect subgroup effects. Null findings in these analyses should therefore be treated as inconclusive rather than as evidence of absence of effect.

Several microbiological and clinical characterization gaps also limit interpretation. E. coli isolates were not pathotype-characterized, meaning their diarrheagenic role is inferred from clinical context rather than confirmed by virulence gene detection (ETEC, EPEC, EAEC, STEC, EIEC). The absence of a nondiarrheal elderly control group similarly prevents distinction between true infection and asymptomatic colonization for organisms such as K. pneumoniae and A. hydrophila. The antibiotic susceptibility panel for Campylobacter omitted azithromycin and erythromycin, the agents most commonly deployed in its clinical management, which limits the therapeutic utility of the resistance data generated. Additionally, organisms represented by three or fewer isolates (n ≤ 3) lack sufficient statistical weight for clinical or epidemiological inference and should be treated as descriptive observations only.

A further set of limitations concerns the interpretive standards applied to antimicrobial susceptibility data, described in detail in Sections 2.5.3 and 3.2 (Table 1). Organism-specific MDR classification was recalculated from organism-level aggregate resistance proportions rather than isolate-level cross-tabulated profiles; for K. pneumoniae, the correction for intrinsic ampicillin resistance was fully consistent with the available aggregate data, but for P. vulgaris, the correction for intrinsic tetracycline resistance could not be verified with the same precision and should be regarded as an approximation. A. hydrophila and V. cholerae were tested against a panel validated for Enterobacterales rather than the CLSI M45 standard applicable to these genera; their results are accordingly reported descriptively only and excluded from MDR estimates, and should be retested against M45 criteria in future work. Similarly, no CLSI- or EUCAST-approved clinical breakpoint currently exists for fidaxomicin or rifaximin against C. difficile; these MICs are reported descriptively rather than as a validated susceptible/resistant classification. Finally, the underlying participant-level dataset could only be audited for internal consistency against the numbers reported in this manuscript; we recommend that the corresponding author additionally verify the two duplicate entries identified in Section 3.2 against the original laboratory register.

Molecular confirmation was beyond the scope of this study and represents a significant evidence gap. ESBL-encoding genes (e.g., blaCTX-M, blaSHV, blaTEM) were not confirmed in phenotypically ESBL-suspect K. pneumoniae isolates. C. difficile ribotyping and whole-genome sequencing were not performed, precluding identification of epidemic-associated ribotypes such as RT027 and RT078, which carry direct infection control implications. These omissions are particularly consequential given the moxifloxacin resistance observed in C. difficile, which warrants molecular investigation as a matter of priority.

Finally, several data quality limitations affect the breadth of analysis possible. All WASH and dietary exposure data were self-reported, introducing potential recall and social desirability biases. Prior antibiotic use was not systematically collected, limiting analysis and causal interpretation of the C. difficile burden documented here, which cannot be attributed to healthcare-associated antibiotic exposure without these data. No clinical outcome data, including hospitalization duration, in-hospital mortality, or treatment response, were recorded, which substantially constrains the study's utility for informing clinical management guidelines and prevents attribution of direct burden to specific resistant pathogens. Regarding stool frequency, all participants met the WHO case definition of three or more stools per day at presentation, based on clinical records and clinician enrollment assessment; however, this was not independently verified by the research team, and frequency at peak illness was not captured as a distinct variable, meaning assessment-point data may not fully reflect severity at the height of presentation.

Future directions

Prospective cohort studies are the most pressing methodological need, as they would allow causal risk factors for diarrheagenic infection in elderly Kenyan patients to be established with greater confidence. Such studies should incorporate systematic collection of prior antibiotic use, polypharmacy, healthcare contact frequency, and length of hospital stay, and should record both peak and assessment-point stool frequency to enable more accurate characterization of illness severity over time.

Improvements to standard microbiological practice would also strengthen the evidence base considerably. Pathotype characterization of E. coli isolates by virulence gene detection should be treated as a methodological standard rather than an optional enhancement in settings where this organism is frequently isolated. Azithromycin and erythromycin should be incorporated routinely into Campylobacter susceptibility panels to enable clinically actionable resistance profiling. Molecular and genomic characterization of C. difficile, including ribotyping and whole-genome sequencing, should be prioritized given the fluoroquinolone resistance signal identified here, as it would clarify epidemic potential, transmission dynamics, and the mechanisms underlying observed resistance. Confirmation of ESBL genotypes in K. pneumoniae isolates is likewise essential for understanding resistance determinants and informing stewardship interventions. Future studies should retain isolate-level (rather than only aggregate) resistance profiles to allow exact, verifiable MDR recalculation after excluding intrinsic resistance; apply CLSI M45 criteria when testing Aeromonas and Vibrio spp.; and, pending publication of a validated clinical breakpoint, continue to report C. difficile fidaxomicin and rifaximin results by MIC only.

At the population level, multi-facility and community-based studies are necessary to generate prevalence estimates that extend beyond single-center hospital populations, and longitudinal AMR surveillance with age-stratified analysis is urgently needed to track resistance trends in this understudied demographic. Moreover, One Health studies integrating human, veterinary, and environmental AMR surveillance in urban Kenyan settings would strengthen the cross-sectoral evidence base required for effective stewardship policy, particularly for resistant pathogens whose reservoirs extend well beyond the clinical environment.
