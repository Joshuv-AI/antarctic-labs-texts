Higher levels of social support predict lower psychological distress for Pacific peoples living in Aotearoa New Zealand
Authors: Sarah A Kapeli, Sam Manuela, Chris G Sibley
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent research indicates that rates of psychological distress are almost 1.5 times higher for adult Pacific peoples versus non-Pacific peoples1. Leveraging data from the New Zealand Attitudes and Values Study and utilising cross-lagged regression modelling, we examined changes in psychological distress across time for adult Pacific peoples in Aotearoa New Zealand (n = 514). Our study found that experiences of psychological distress were associated with a greater likelihood of experiencing psychological distress in the future. Additionally, experiencing higher levels of social support was associated with a lower likelihood of experiencing psychological distress in the future. Our research is vital because psychological distress is a serious mental health issue, and research that signals protective factors towards psychological distress for our Pacific peoples can help in creating better mental health outcomes for our Pacific communities. Preview on terminology: We use the term Pacific peoples, which are inclusive of a group of people in Aotearoa New Zealand that have ethnic roots from many Pacific nations. Unless a research study specifically uses the term mental illness or mental disorder, the study uses the terms mental distress and mental health issues/concerns to broadly refer to diagnosis of a mental illness or any other challenges or experiences with mental health. The choice in terminology is to shift the focus away from a deficit model of health, to those that are more consistent with Pacific views of health and wellbeing1.Subject terms: Psychology, Psychiatric disorders

Introduction

Pacific peoples are an ethnic minority group in Aotearoa New Zealand (NZ), who have a high prevalence rate of mental illness and low engagement with mental health services1. Such evidence is a major concern and signals the need for approaches that better address the poorer mental health outcomes experienced by our Pacific peoples in Aotearoa NZ. As a mental health researcher of Pacific-Tongan heritage (Lapaha, Tongatapu), developing Pacific-centric mental health research is a priority. Such research strengthens our knowledge and evidence base, which can be drawn upon to develop resources, inform policy, and develop prevention and intervention strategies that address the mental health issues that face our Pacific communities. Therefore, this study contributes to the area of Pacific mental health in Aotearoa NZ. More specifically, this study explores experiences of psychological distress by utilizing data from the New Zealand Attitudes and Values Study (NZAVS), a national longitudinal study that has measured psychological distress with a sample of Pacific peoples in Aotearoa NZ.

Psychological distress

Psychological distress is broadly defined as a state of emotional suffering characterised by symptoms of depression and anxiety2. Psychological distress is commonly measured through the utility of the 10-item Kessler scale (K10) and a reduced 6-item Kessler scale (K6) or 5-item Kessler Scale (K5). Respondents self-rate across a series of questions on a scale from 0 to 4, and higher total scores are indicative of greater self-reported psychological distress3.

Factors to consider for psychological distress

Risk and protective factors of psychological distress generally fall into three categories: (1) sociodemographic factors (characteristics that are inborn i.e. age, gender, ethnicity); (2) stress-related factors (events and life conditions that exert stress on an individual i.e. poverty); (3) personal resources (resources that are available to an individual to prevent the occurrence of psychological distress i.e. income)4.

Sociodemographic factors indicate that psychological distress is higher for women than men in most countries5–7 and generally appears to decrease across the lifespan5, 7–9. Immigrants and minority groups often report higher distress1, 10–14. Stress-related risk factors tend to increase psychological distress but can have varying effects across age and gender4, 15. Job security was a greater risk factor for men whilst death in the family and a relationship ending were greater risk factors for women6. Further, health related events were a greater risk factor for older people16 and academic stress was a greater risk factor for younger people17. Both internal and external personal resources have been found to contribute to overall experiences of psychological distress. For instance, high levels of self-esteem and high perceived levels of social support are associated with lower levels of psychological distress5, 6, 9, 16, 18, 19. Whereas lower socioeconomic status is a greater risk factor for psychological distress5, 7, 20–22.

Psychological distress and Pacific peoples

There is mixed ethnic data in regard to psychological distress, not only because ethnicity is complex but because there can be additional challenges for particular ethnic groups, including experiences of discrimination, stigma, and other socioeconomic factors11, 12, 14. As mentioned, minority groups often report higher levels of psychological distress, a trend that is seen in Aotearoa NZ where Māori, Pacific peoples and Asian peoples experience higher levels of psychological distress comparatively with NZ Europeans23. There is also an assumption that identifying with an ethnic minority serves as a risk factor for psychological distress. However, it has been argued that although ethnic minorities might be more exposed to risk factors, i.e. lower socioeconomic status, that does not necessarily mean they are more vulnerable24.

Differences in experiences of psychological distress between ethnic groups in Aotearoa, NZ, are widely documented1, 13, 25–27. Currently, there are no existing measures that are tailored towards Pacific ideas of psychological distress. However, the Kessler scales have demonstrated construct validity and reliability across diverse populations, including Pacific peoples in Aotearoa NZ4, 27, 28. Te Kaveinga, a report drawing on K10 data from the New Zealand Health Monitor (NZHM) and Health Lifestyles Survey (HLS), found that Pacific peoples experience psychological distress 1.5 times higher than the general adult population in Aotearoa NZ. Further, almost a quarter of Pacific peoples had experienced moderate levels of psychological distress in the 4 weeks prior, and that moderate to high levels were significantly higher for younger Pacific peoples and Pacific women1, 23.

Research also indicates that Pacific men and women have differing experiences of mental health issues, whereby women are more likely to experience an internalised mental health issue (i.e., anxiety, depression, eating disorders), and men are more likely to experience an externalised mental health issue (i.e., substance abuse)29,30,31,32. Suicide deaths also remain significantly higher for our Pacific men than Pacific women, with the highest rate of suicide deaths seen in young Pacific men aged 15 to 24 years old33,34,31.

Longitudinal research has highlighted many factors that can contribute to increased psychological distress over time, such as relationship conflict35; lower socio-economic status18; stress15, 18; living in temporary housing post-natural disaster36; and food insecurity37. On the other hand, factors that have been linked to decreased psychological distress across the lifespan include increased sleep38; residing in more rural areas39; enhanced physical health18; higher social support18.

The prevalence of psychological distress has been studied extensively, and experiencing higher levels of psychological distress is often highlighted as a precursor to developing further mental health challenges. Which in turn can lead to an increased need for mental health services and support. In this respect, preventative or early intervention measures focused on psychological distress may not only reduce subjective suffering but could see a reduction in subsequent mental health challenges and associated healthcare costs2, 18. Further, allocating resources to groups at higher risk of developing psychological distress, such as Pacific peoples, who are a youthful and growing population, will likely have long-term benefits. In our study, we utilised longitudinal data from the NZAVS to demonstrate the cross-lagged effects of psychological distress for Pacific peoples, particularly focusing on factors that are culturally and contextually important to consider for Pacific peoples and psychological distress in Aotearoa NZ, these included age, gender, birthplace, religiosity, deprivation, and social support.

Social support

Social support is conceptualised as the perception of having a supportive social network and is often renowned for enhancing mental and physical health. Social support is multidimensional and can be offered and received in different ways40, 41: (1) emotional (e.g. empathy, love, care); (2) informational (e.g. advice, guidance); (3) instrumental (e.g. tangible assistance, financial aid); (4) appraisal (e.g. feedback, affirmation).

Research has also explored the structure of social support networks and emphasises the differential impact of support received depending upon the relationship across the lifespan. For example, Manalel and Antonucci42 describe how social convoys (or personal networks) change structure and composition across the lifespan. As well as how different interpersonal relationships within the social convoy can offer support that contributes to wellbeing in different ways. Additionally, the composition and importance of social support networks can vary across the lifespan. For example, family support is often central in childhood and adolescence, while friendships and romantic relationships may become more central in adulthood. In later life, support from peers and community organisations may gain prominence43.

It is also important to recognise that cultural factors can play a crucial role in shaping the meaning, expression, and impact of social support. For instance, family and community support may be prioritised in collectivist cultures whereas individualistic cultures may focus predominantly on dyadic relationships44. Furthermore, cultural differences in values beliefs, and practices can influence the forms of support that are sought, offered, and perceived44, 45. Acknowledging cultural considerations for social support leads us to think about its relevance with Pacific peoples. Whilst social support is a valuable construct, the concept of vā more accurately encapsulates the depth and breadth of relationality and connectedness within and across Pacific communities. Thus, what the western world understands as social support, we, as Pacific peoples, make a connection to vā46.

The centrality of vā to our Pacific communities

Albert Wendt describes the concept of vā as the “space between, the betweenness, not empty space, not space that separates, but space that relates, that holds separate entities and things together in the unity-in-all, the space that context, giving meaning to things” (as cited in Refiti 2006)47. Rather than a concept, vā is a way of being, knowing, and understanding. It is how we, as Pacific peoples, interpret and view the world. The multi-Pacific understanding of vā can be loosely translated as a spatial way of conceiving the secular and spiritual dimensions of relationships and relational order, that facilitates both personal and collective wellbeing48, 49. In Pacific research, caretaking of our vā is imperative as the collaborative aspect of our research allows us to nurture relationships, work together, work cooperatively, and work reciprocally with our communities to develop research that serves our people, our communities, and our spaces.

In this research, the data that we drew upon from the NZAVS includes variables that examine social support, but no variables examine vā or have been developed under a vā theoretical framework. Whilst social support and vā are conceptually unique, both concepts emphasize the importance of social connections for wellbeing. Therefore, we can make connections between social support and vā to consider how the wellbeing of Pacific peoples connects to social support and experiences of psychological distress for Pacific peoples over time.

As an ethnic minority, Pacific peoples are believed to be more exposed to risk factors for psychological distress24. There is a wealth of research demonstrating the positive and protective factors of having strong social connections, including enhanced mental health and wellbeing46, 50–52. Given the positive links with social connectedness, exploring how to maintain strong and secure social connectedness is vital, especially for our Pacific peoples who experience higher rates of psychological distress than the general adult population in Aotearoa NZ1, 23. Pacific peoples’ reporting of social support has also been found to be significantly lower than Māori, non-Māori and non-Pacific in Aotearoa NZ1. However, these findings may be a reflection of social support as a western construct44 and not relationality or connectedness as it is understood through vā. Whilst vā was not developed as a construct for this study, there is a theoretical opportunity to do so. We also know that social support decreases across the lifespan for Pacific peoples46 and younger Pacific peoples tend to draw support from friends rather than family52–54. Whilst it is expected that lower levels of social support result in higher levels of psychological distress, it is important to consider how we foster vā (grow, nurture, and maintain relationships) as we age for our Pacific communities.

Overview of the current study

Using longitudinal data from the NZAVS, we conducted a cross-lagged regression to assess the relationship between ratings of psychological distress during Wave 5 (2013) and Wave 6 (2014) for those identifying with a Pacific ethnicity. We also included other variables as indicators of psychological distress in 2014. Due to the smaller sample size and not wanting to compromise statistical power, we were mindful of the number of indicators used and their cultural significance. Thus, we chose these variables because of their importance in understanding the health and wellbeing of Pacific peoples in Aotearoa NZ:

Guiding hypotheses

There is information about psychological distress experienced by Pacific peoples in Aotearoa NZ. There is also information about psychological distress over time and its causes. However, there is limited information on the factors that contribute to psychological distress over time for Pacific peoples. The cross-lagged model that we present here aims to fill this gap in the literature. A cross-lagged model collects information at two or more time points to estimate the directional effects that one variable has on another at different points in time. In this way, we can examine causal effects within longitudinal data61, 62. As mentioned, a cross-lagged longitudinal analysis is an essential approach to examining changes and contributing factors to psychological distress for our Pacific communities.

We know that Pacific knowledge highlights vā as a crucial element to overall wellbeing, suggesting that the relational nature of Pacific worldviews puts emphasis on positive relationships as a protective factor for wellbeing. Although there are no quantitative measures of vā that currently exist, there are measures we can draw upon that connect to vā, such as social support. We hypothesized that higher social support at Wave 5 (2013) would be associated with decreased psychological distress at Wave 6 (2014). We also hypothesized that age, gender, country of birth, religiosity, and deprivation would have a significant change (positive or negative) in psychological distress over time.

Method

This research was conducted in accordance with general ethical guidelines in Psychology. Data was examined from the NZAVS, which at the time of collection was approved by the University of Auckland Human Participants Ethics Committee (UAHPEC) on 17/02/2012 until 9/9/15, Reference Number 6171. Current ethics approval by UAHPEC was granted on 26/05/2021 for six years, Reference Number UAHPEC22576. Sampling procedure

Participants were posted a copy of the NZAVS questionnaire, with a postal follow up sent two months later. Participants that provided an email address were also emailed and invited to complete the questionnaire online if preferred. Those that did not respond were emailed a follow up reminder approximately two months later. Non-respondents were then contacted using provided numbers, to encourage participation. This occurred at 1-week intervals, where three phone call attempts were made. A voice message was left upon the third attempt if possible. The 2013 wave of the NZAVS sampled a total of 18,261 participants (including 625 Pacific peoples), and the 2014 wave of the NZAVS sampled a total of 15,820 participants (including 526 Pacific peoples), all of whom completed the NZAVS questionnaire. From each sample, 14,875 participants completed a questionnaire from both the 2013 and 2014 wave (including 514 Pacific peoples). Informed consent was obtained from all participants and full sampling details for the NZAVS, copies of the full questionnaire and all other materials are available on the NZAVS website66.

Discussion

Our results indicate that experiences of psychological distress predict higher levels of psychological distress one year later. Further, social support was associated with decreased reported symptoms of psychological distress. This relationship between social support and psychological distress is an important consideration for Pacific health and wellbeing and has serious implications for the (re)development of mental wellbeing support and services. For instance, how social connectedness can be fostered through community activities51, 68 and mental health service provision45. We hope that this research provides knowledge that empowers our Pacific communities to lead the change in our health and wellbeing arena.

Social support buffers psychological distress

Our research findings indicate that higher levels of psychological distress predicted higher levels of psychological distress one year later. However, higher levels of perceived social support predicted lower levels of psychological distress one year later. Gender, age, country of birth, religiosity, and deprivation did not predict changes in psychological distress over the one-year time period. Given the findings, we shift our focus towards social support and its buffering effect towards experiences of psychological distress for Pacific peoples in Aotearoa, NZ.

Good levels of social support are largely considered a protective factor against psychological distress. Further, protecting against psychological distress may prevent subjective suffering and subsequent mental health challenges and reduce mental health care costs2, 18. Developing good levels of social support could include cultivating and strengthening relationships in your social support network. This could be through nurturing existing relationships, expanding social networks, seeking support when needed, offering support to others, and joining support or community groups5, 16, 18, 19.

We also know that social support decreases across the lifespan for Pacific peoples, and more significantly for Pacific men46. Suggesting that older Pacific peoples, particularly older Pacific men, may be more at risk of experiencing psychological distress. Whilst a decline in social support across the lifespan is not an uncommon experience46, 69, 70, specific challenges and mitigating factors will differ between Pacific and non-Pacific communities. For instance, our Pacific communities in Aotearoa NZ place an emphasis on interdependence and relationality, which is meaningfully expressed through vā71, and could lend to stronger relationships as we age. By recognising the unique challenges faced by our Pacific communities and implementing culturally appropriate social support or vā-centred approaches, we can potentially mitigate the negative consequences of declining social networks over time and enhance overall wellbeing.

Strengthening knowledge as a protective factor towards psychological distress

Preventing psychological distress and subsequent mental health challenges is paramount. Prioritising the mental health needs and aspirations of our Pacific communities also requires an understanding of the beliefs, knowledge, attitudes, and help-seeking behaviours around mental health for our Pasifika – or Pasifika mental health literacy72. By enhancing Pasifika mental health literacy, our Pacific communities can be empowered with the knowledge to seek mental health support and reduce stigma around mental illness. In turn, this could not only alleviate subjective suffering but also encourage our Pacific communities to seek mental health support earlier as a preventative towards experiencing psychological distress and subsequent mental health challenges.

Connecting social support and vā

Our research has identified social support as a protective factor towards psychological distress for Pacific peoples in Aotearoa NZ. Given the shared conceptual underpinnings of social support and vā, we can make connections between social support and vā in the scope of the present study. Acknowledging vā is important because with our growing Pacific population in Aotearoa NZ, research centring vā can help shape the future of Pacific health and wellbeing. Vā is not just about relationships but it is a way of understanding, a way of being, and a way of viewing the world. Historically, research has not applied a Pacific lens to social support, and this may be due to the difficulty in quantifying vā in the way that social support has been. Nonetheless, this research signals an opportunity to develop a construct of vā that can be utilised in a quantitative framework.

Limitations and future directions

The measure of social support from the NZAVS was not developed under a Pacific lens and is not the most appropriate for measuring connectedness across our Pacific communities. There is extensive literature that examines vā theoretically through qualitative research e.g. Talanoa73, and as mentioned, there are theoretical opportunities to develop a quantitative construct of vā.

We have highlighted that young Pacific peoples are an at-risk age group, and an obvious limitation of our dataset is that it excludes anyone below 18 years of age. Especially when we consider age as an important factor for Pacific mental health. Future studies could explore Pacific youth, potentially looking at the youth 2000 study in Aotearoa NZ, which includes data from secondary school students.

We are also limited by the use of a small sample size, but due to the booster sampling for the NZAVS, we expect future waves to have larger Pacific samples that can provide larger data pools for quantitative modelling. Such research could focus on research exploring mixed ethnic backgrounds (e.g. NZ European/Pacific, multiple Pacific ethnicities, or another ethnicity/Pacific), or exploring differences between Aotearoa NZ born Pacific peoples and Pacific born Pacific peoples.

It would also be beneficial to explore other variables that we know impact our Pacific communities. We know from previous studies that discrimination12 and ethnic identity74 can affect psychological distress. No effects were found when including discrimination and ethnic identity as indicators in preliminary analyses. But if we look over time, perhaps we might see changes with more time points and a greater sample size.

Finally, this research can also support efforts that advocate for further resourcing to enhance social connectedness. Prior research has demonstrated positive outcomes in community-based support groups51, 75, mentoring programmes76, 77, and online social support78–80, all of which foster the sacred relational space – vā.

Conclusion

Pacific wellbeing can be understood as the quality of relationships with others. Therefore, good social support is expected to be associated with lower psychological distress scores over time. Whilst good social support is optimal for all ethnic groups, it is important to acknowledge cultural considerations when exploring relationships across diverse cultural contexts. It is also important for our theorising and interpretations to be relevant to the communities we are working alongside. So, what non-Pacific-Indigenous peoples might understand as social support, we, as Pacific peoples, better understand as vā – and this should shape the direction of future research, developing culturally relevant and appropriate tools for our Pacific communities.

Future generations of Pacific peoples will thrive through strengths-based and action-focused research approaches that incorporate Pacific knowledges, Pacific worldviews, and Pacific understandings. It is the passing down of this knowledge that has led our peoples for many generations, and although this passing of knowledge may look different now (through research narratives), the essence is still the same – Ko e Kai i.a. ‘a e Pasifika (Enriching Aotearoa with Pacific Wisdom).
