Cost-Effectiveness of Maternal RSVpreF Vaccination Compared with Newborn Nirsevimab RSV Prophylaxis or No RSV Prophylaxis in Switzerland
Authors: Jamie Bainbridge, Stuart Mealing, Charlotte Graham, Mathias Jenal, Nicolas von Dombrowski, Amy Dymond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRSVpreF is a vaccine that immunises against respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), a seasonal respiratory virus, that can lead to lower respiratory tract infections. In Switzerland, RSVpreF is indicated for the passive protection of infants against RSV following maternal immunisation.ObjectiveThe Swiss Federal Office of Public Health commissioned a health technology assessment (HTA) of RSVpreF for maternal vaccination, evaluating the clinical and cost effectiveness of the vaccine compared with nirsevimab (administered directly to newborns and infants) or no RSV prophylaxis. This study reports the cost-effectiveness model developed for the Swiss HTA.MethodsThe model used a Markov model structure, a 1-year time horizon, a one-month cycle length, a Swiss healthcare payers' perspective, and a 3% discount rate for costs and health benefits. Health states included no RSV infection, RSV infection leading to hospitalisation, primary care visits or emergency department visits, post-RSV infection, and death. MATISSE informed RSVpreF efficacy, and MELODY and HARMONIE informed nirsevimab efficacy. Health outcomes were expressed as quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Probabilistic and deterministic sensitivity analyses were undertaken to explore the impact of parameter uncertainty on the model results.ResultsWhen compared with nirsevimab, RSVpreF reduces total costs (CHF −9,878,738) and QALYs (−18.4), with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of 536,008 per QALY (N = 38,844). Compared with no RSV prophylaxis, RSVpreF increases total costs (CHF +470,441) and QALYs (+27.1), with an ICER of 17,377 per QALY (N = 38,844).ConclusionsRSVpreF is highly likely to be cost-effective versus both comparators at a range of plausible threshold values. The results of the model contributed to guidance updates in Switzerland, with RSVpreF now reimbursed by mandatory health insurance.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a seasonal respiratory virus that causes annual winter epidemics. The RSV season in Switzerland typically lasts from November to April and peaks in January [1]. RSV symptoms typically include a cough, sore throat, sneezing and/or a runny nose. However, severe RSV infections can lead to life-threatening lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI), such as pneumonia, bronchitis and bronchiolitis [2, 3].

Populations at a higher risk of severe RSV infection include young children and older adults [4], premature newborns and infants [5], and people living with underlying medical conditions [2, 6]. RSV is a leading cause of acute respiratory infections in infants and young children, infecting almost 90% of children by the age of two [7, 8]. Moreover, involvement of the lower respiratory tract occurs in 15–50% of infants and young children with primary RSV infection [1]. In the long term, an RSV infection in early life can increase the risk of developing asthma, worsen the symptoms of asthma, and/or increase the risk of recurrent wheezing [9–11].

An analysis of administrative data in Switzerland identified that the number of hospitalisations with RSV as a main diagnosis increased by almost three-fold between 2003/04 and 2019/20 (from 1,024 to 2,847) [12]. Mean inpatient medical costs were estimated at EUR 8,046 per RSV hospitalisation, and two-thirds of these hospitalisations occurred in infants [12]. The study reported that > 90% of Swiss infants (< 12 months of age) with RSV did not have the following risk factors for severe RSV infection: premature, bronchopulmonary dysplasia or congenital heart disease [12]. Approximately 1–2% of each annual birth cohort in Switzerland is admitted to intermediate care units or intensive care units (ICUs) because of RSV [13].

Until 2024, no RSV prophylaxis was the Swiss standard of care for RSV infection in newborns and infants. This was updated in September 2024, when RSV prophylaxis with nirsevimab (a monoclonal antibody) became the standard of care and was reimbursed by mandatory health insurance [1]. Palivizumab (a monoclonal antibody) is also available but is not recommended for routine administration in children [14]. In August 2024, Swissmedic approved an RSV vaccine (Abrysvo; RSVpreF) for the passive protection of infants following maternal immunisation, as well as the active protection of adults aged 60+ years [15]. Two other vaccines are also available for older adults: Arexvy (RSV pre-fusion F protein [16]) and mRESVIA (RSV mRNA vaccine – nucleoside modified [17]).

The Federal Office of Public Health (FOPH) is the primary organisation responsible for health technology assessment (HTA) in Switzerland. In 2025, the FOPH commissioned York Health Economics Consortium to conduct an HTA of RSVpreF for maternal vaccination against RSV, evaluating the clinical and cost effectiveness of the vaccine compared with nirsevimab or no RSV prophylaxis from a Swiss Healthcare payers’ perspective. RSVpreF was not yet reimbursed by the mandatory health insurance in Switzerland when the HTA commenced. This article summarises the economic model that was developed as part of the FOPH assessment of RSVpreF [18].

Methods

Population, Intervention and Comparators

An economic model was developed to estimate the cost-effectiveness of vaccination with maternal RSVpreF during pregnancy compared with nirsevimab, which is administered directly to newborns and infants, or no RSV prophylaxis for newborns and infants. No other monoclonal antibodies (e.g. palivizumab) or vaccines (e.g. Arexvy) were incorporated as comparators in the model. Palivizumab was excluded because it is not recommended for routine administration in children [14]. Both the RSV pre-fusion F protein and RSV mRNA vaccine—nucleoside modified are only authorised for RSV vaccination in adults aged 60+ years [16], which is not relevant to the population of interest.

The population entering the model was defined as newborns whose mothers did or did not receive RSV vaccination with RSVpreF during pregnancy. The model only included newborns born between October and March in 2023 (to align with the Swiss RSV vaccination recommendation) [19]. A 1-year time horizon was used to ensure that each individual was exposed to a full RSV season regardless of the timing of their birth. A cycle length of 1 month was used to capture the impact of seasonality on baseline infection rates. The size of the modelled cohort reflected the number of live Swiss births between these months in 2023 (N = 38,844) [20].

Newborns with a term status < 32 weeks were excluded because RSVpreF is indicated for women between 32 and 36 weeks of pregnancy. The population was also stratified into full-term births (≥ 37 weeks of gestational age [wGA]) or late preterm (32–36 wGA) births to allow the impact of term status at birth on the following components to be considered: the efficacy of RSVpreF, the proportion of RSV-associated hospitalisations requiring an ICU admission, the background rate of newborn/infant mortality and the in-hospital general ward rate of mortality [21].

Model Structure

Model conceptualisation was informed by a rapid systematic review of economic evaluations of RSV vaccination strategies [18]. An Excel-based Markov model was deemed preferable over a dynamic transmission model because it allowed the essential consequences of each vaccination strategy to be captured without the additional data requirements, additional complexity, and potential uncertainty of a transmission model. A schematic of the model structure. ICU intensive care unit, RSV respiratory syncytial virus

A hypothetical cohort of newborns entered the model in the ‘no RSV infection’ health state immediately after birth. The mothers of the newborns in the intervention arm received an RSV vaccination with RSVpreF during pregnancy (subject to coverage). The cohort in the comparator arms received either RSV prophylaxis with nirsevimab (subject to coverage) or no RSV prophylaxis (assumed equivalent to the pre-nirsevimab standard of care).

A proportion of the cohort experienced an RSV infection that resulted in healthcare-seeking behaviour each monthly cycle. Individuals who did not die or develop an RSV infection remained in the ‘no RSV infection’ health state. In comparison, individuals who did develop an RSV infection transitioned to one of the following health states: hospitalisation (general ward or ICU), emergency department (ED) or primary care (PC) visit. Each health state was associated with different health-related quality of life and healthcare resource use. Asymptomatic or mild cases of RSV infections were not considered in the model owing to their anticipated negligible impact on model outcomes. Therefore, the analysis only accounted for RSV infections that resulted in healthcare-seeking behaviour.

Individuals transitioned to the ‘post-RSV’ health state after one cycle in any of the RSV health states. They remained in this health state until the end of the time horizon (1 year) or they died. In reality, a newborn/infant may experience multiple RSV infections. However, a paucity of data was available to reliably parameterise the possibility of multiple infections without introducing considerable uncertainty into the model. As such, it was assumed that each individual could only develop one RSV infection within the model time horizon. Related studies have similarly implemented this assumption [22–29].

Transition probabilities for the ‘no RSV prophylaxis’ arm were determined by the incidence of RSV under standard care. By contrast, the transition probabilities for the RSVpreF and nirsevimab arms incorporated both the standard care incidence and the efficacy of the respective treatment.

In alignment with guidance from the FOPH, future costs and effects were discounted at 3% per annum in the base case scenario. Discount rates of 0% and 5% were applied in scenario analyses.

Model Input Parameters

Inputs were informed by a rapid systematic review of clinical data where possible and supplemented by pragmatic desk-based research where necessary. All key assumptions and model input parameters were validated by a clinical expert. The key clinical inputs are presented throughout this section and full documentation of all inputs is available on the FOPH website [18].

RSV Incidence

The baseline rate of RSV was stratified by age and care setting (i.e. hospitalisation, ED and PC). Data from the literature were available to inform the incidence of RSV infections requiring hospitalisation in Switzerland [12]. However, no Swiss-specific data were available to inform the incidence of RSV cases requiring an ED or PC visit. Spanish data from a retrospective observational study were also identified [30]. The incidence of RSV hospitalisations was higher in the Spanish data because a greater number of International Classification of Diseases codes were used. The Swiss-specific incidence rates for RSV-related hospitalisation and Spanish-specific incidence rates for RSV-related ED and PC visits were used in the base case analysis. A scenario analysis was run using only data from the Spanish study, given the notable differences in the rates of RSV-related hospitalisation between each source.

Infants admitted to hospital were further stratified into those treated in an ICU or a general ward. Seasonality of RSV was accounted for by considering the distribution of RSV infections by calendar month. Specifically, baseline RSV rates were multiplied by the proportion of RSV infections occurring in each calendar month.

Clinical Effectiveness: No RSV Prophylaxis

The transition probabilities, which are presented in Table 2, in the ‘no RSV prophylaxis’ arm were informed by the baseline RSV incidence rates (Table 1).

Clinical Effectiveness: RSVpreF

The efficacy of RSVpreF was informed by MATISSE, a double-blind, Phase 3 trial of RSVpreF versus placebo in women between 24 and 36 weeks of pregnancy [33]. Efficacy against severe, RSV-associated, medically attended LRTI was used as a proxy for efficacy against RSV cases requiring hospitalisation. Likewise, efficacy against RSV-positive medically attended LRTI from MATISSE was used as a proxy for efficacy against RSV cases treated in the ED or PC setting.

RSVpreF efficacy input values were applied as relative risks and were multiplied by the baseline incidence of RSV [22]. Data were available to inform the efficacy of RSVpreF for each month up to six months of age. Therefore, it was assumed that efficacy would wane linearly to 0% by age 9 to < 10 months in the base case analysis. An additional scenario with no treatment waning (i.e. equivalence with no RSV prophylaxis from six months onwards) was also conducted.

The efficacy of RSVpreF in late preterm infants was assumed to be 83.3% of the efficacy in full-term infants [22]. It was also anticipated that not all mothers in the ‘RSVpreF during pregnancy’ cohort would elect to receive the vaccination. The base case vaccine coverage (68%) was assumed to be equivalent to the mid-point between the vaccine coverage for pertussis (86.2%) and influenza (49.8%) observed in pregnant women in Switzerland [36]. The remaining 32% of individuals in this cohort received no vaccination; these newborns and infants had no RSV immunisation.

Clinical Effectiveness: Nirsevimab

The efficacy of nirsevimab against RSV-related ED visits and PC visits was informed by MELODY, a Phase 3, randomised, double-blind trial of nirsevimab versus placebo in healthy late-preterm and full-term infants [35]. The results of a time-to-event analysis showed that infants who received nirsevimab had a lower rate of medically attended, RSV-associated LRTI than those who received a placebo (hazard ratio: 0.23; 95% CI 0.12–0.47). Therefore, this hazard ratio was multiplied by the baseline incidence rate to estimate the annual rate of RSV infections requiring an ED or PC visit. The hazard ratio was based on 150 days of follow-up data. Therefore, for consistency with RSVpreF, treatment efficacy was assumed to wane linearly from five months onwards to 0% by age 9 to < 10 months.

MELODY did not report a hazard ratio to inform the difference in the rate of newborns and infants requiring hospitalisation for an RSV infection between nirsevimab and no prophylaxis [35]. Therefore, a hazard ratio from the HARMONIE trial, which investigated the prevention of hospitalisations owing to RSV in infants, was used instead [34]. An efficacy of 89.6% (95% CI 73.8–96.8%), which was equivalent to a hazard ratio of 0.104 (1–0.896). The hazard ratio was based on 180 days of follow-up data. Therefore, for consistency with RSVpreF, treatment efficacy was assumed to wane linearly from six months onwards to 0% by age 9 to < 10 months.

The coverage rate of nirsevimab was assumed equal to vitamin K prophylaxis coverage at birth. Vitamin K prophylaxis is recommended in 85.0% of Swiss healthcare facilities, and there is a 0.5% refusal rate within this population [37]. Therefore, a coverage rate of 84.6% was assumed.

Treatment-Related Adverse Events

Based on literature identified in the clinical review, no serious adverse events in infants or mothers were considered to be related to RSVpreF or nirsevimab [24, 26, 28, 33, 37] and no substantial safety concerns were identified in HARMONIE [34]. Therefore, no treatment-related adverse events were included in the model.

Mortality

In alignment with published cost-effectiveness models, RSV-associated mortality was applied to newborns and infants in the ‘hospitalisation’ sub-health state [22, 24, 28, 38]. A general baseline annual mortality rate was applied to all newborns and infants in the remaining health states (Table 3). It was anticipated that late preterm newborns/infants would have a higher risk of mortality than those that were full-term. Therefore, the baseline rate of mortality was also adjusted by term status. Utilities

In alignment with the approach adopted in previous cost-effectiveness models [22, 24], individuals were assigned a baseline utility level of one (equal to full health) when residing in the ‘no RSV infection’ or ‘post-RSV infection’ health states. A QALY loss per RSV infection was applied as a utility decrement which was adjusted by the duration of illness.

Swiss-specific data to inform a QALY loss per RSV infection were not identified in published literature. Therefore, utility values from Getaneh et al. (2023), which used data from four European countries, were assumed applicable for Swiss newborns/infants requiring ED or PC visits [24] (Table 4). While Getaneh et al. (2023) did report a QALY loss for hospitalisations, the study applied the same QALY loss to both hospitalisations and ED visits [24]. It was anticipated that the QALY loss for hospitalisation would be greater than that of an ED visit. Therefore, a Canadian cost-effectiveness study (Gebretekle et al.) with strong face validity was used instead to source data relating to QALY loss for hospitalisations [23] (Table 4). Costs

Costs of healthcare services covered by the Swiss mandatory health insurance were analysed, irrespective of the actual payer (mandatory health insurer or other social insurer). The analysis did not include indirect costs owing to informal care or productivity losses, nor additional non-medical costs for patients, such as travel costs. All costs were inflated to the 2024 cost year using the FSO inflation index for health, where necessary [45].

Individuals in the RSVpreF cohort were assigned a vaccine unit cost upon model entry (subject to coverage). The clinical expert advised that a vaccine administration cost would not be relevant for RSVpreF because it would be administered simultaneously with a scheduled obstetrician visit during pregnancy. Individuals in the nirsevimab cohort were assigned a treatment cost (subject to coverage) and an administration cost.

Sensitivity and Scenario Analyses

A probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was undertaken to investigate second-order uncertainty. All model inputs were sampled 1,000 times using underlying parameter distributions. The baseline rate of RSV incidence and QALY loss associated with RSV was varied using the gamma distribution. The data to inform the efficacy of RSVpreF and the relative risks of mortality were varied using the log-normal distribution. All other clinical inputs were varied using the beta distribution. Unit costs were not varied within the PSA because they are not associated with uncertainty.

Deterministic sensitivity analysis (DSA) was performed to account for first-order uncertainty around the data. Where possible, the range applied to each parameter was based on 95% confidence intervals obtained from the original source data. Where statistical parameters were not available, a range of values (± 25%) were applied around the base case point estimate.

Scenario analyses were also performed, as described in more detail in the results.

Results

In a cohort of 38,844 individuals, the probabilistic base case indicates that maternal vaccination with RSVpreF in Switzerland over a 1-year time horizon reduces both total costs and total QALYs compared with nirsevimab (Table 6). There is no accepted cost-effectiveness threshold in Switzerland. Therefore, the results were run for a range of thresholds which were pre-determined by the FOPH. RSVpreF was found to be cost effective in over 99.9% of 1,000 iterations, at all cost-effectiveness thresholds tested (Table 6). As RSVpreF reduces both costs and QALYs, a higher ICER indicates that the vaccine is more cost effective. Convergence of probabilistic outcomes was achieved after approximately 250 iterations (Fig. 2A). Discussion

This is the first model to estimate the cost-effectiveness of maternal vaccination against RSV from a Swiss healthcare payers’ perspective. The number of births, baseline rate of RSV-associated hospitalisations, and unit costs associated with RSV-related healthcare resources were informed by Swiss-specific data. Overall, the analysis presented in this study influenced the decision for RSVpreF to be reimbursed by mandatory health insurance for immunisation of people between 32 and 36 weeks of pregnancy, with a due date between October and March [46].

RSVpreF Compared with Nirsevimab

The probabilistic base case indicates that RSVpreF reduces both total costs and total QALYs compared with nirsevimab, resulting in an ICER of CHF 536,008. As both total costs and QALYs are reduced, RSVpreF lies in the south-west quadrant of the cost-effectiveness plane compared with nirsevimab. A technology is cost-effective despite reducing total QALYs if the cost savings outweigh the reduction in QALYs (as determined by the cost-effectiveness ratio). The implementation of RSVpreF in the south-west quadrant may result in foregone QALYs within this particular population. In this scenario, a higher ICER indicates that RSVpreF is more cost effective, and RSVpreF would be cost-effective if the cost-effectiveness threshold falls below CHF 536,008.

The reduction in total QALYs is because RSVpreF has lower effectiveness compared with nirsevimab, which causes an increase in the total number of RSV infections that result in healthcare-seeking behaviour. However, any increases in expenditure relating to the healthcare-seeking behaviour are offset by the substantially lower treatment and administration costs. Lower treatment costs are the largest contributor to RSVpreF reducing overall costs compared with nirsevimab. This is a result of both a higher coverage (84.6% versus 68.0%) and treatment unit cost (CHF 395.60 versus CHF 207.53) for nirsevimab compared with RSVpreF, respectively.

RSVpreF remains cost effective in 100% of PSA iterations, cost saving in 100% of iterations and QALY increasing in 6.4% of iterations. Therefore, the increase in QALYs estimated by adopting nirsevimab over RSVpreF does not outweigh the increase in costs—an outcome highly robust to all cost-effectiveness threshold values adopted and the sensitivity analysis conducted.

In terms of clinical outcomes, RSVpreF increases the total number of RSV infections resulting in healthcare-seeking behaviour (5,170 versus 6,906) and, as such, increases the total expenditure required to treat RSV infections. However, the lower treatment costs (CHF 395.60 versus CHF 207.53) and administration costs (CHF 150.00 versus CHF 0.00) result in RSVpreF decreasing overall costs compared with nirsevimab.

While this study found RSVpreF to be cost effective compared with nirsevimab, economic evaluations conducted in other countries have reported contrasting results. For example, a Canadian analysis reported that nirsevimab is cost effective over RSVpreF for infants at moderate or high risk of RSV with a catch-up program [23]. In Denmark, England, Finland, Italy, Scotland and the Netherlands, seasonal nirsevimab programs were cost saving or cost effective (at different WTP thresholds) versus year-round nirsevimab, RSVpreF and no catch-up program [24].

RSVpreF Compared with No RSV Prophylaxis

The probabilistic base case indicates that RSVpreF increases both total costs and total QALYs compared with no RSV prophylaxis (i.e. RSVpreF falls within the north-east quadrant of the cost-effectiveness plane), resulting in an ICER of CHF 17,377 per QALY (i.e. RSVpreF would be cost-effective if the cost-effectiveness threshold is above CHF 17,377). There is no accepted cost-effectiveness threshold in Switzerland; however, increasing the cost-effectiveness threshold increases the number of iterations that RSVpreF is cost effective because more value is placed on the QALY gained with RSVpreF treatment compared with no RSV prophylaxis. For example, RSVpreF is costeffective in 80.0% of iterations at a threshold of CHF 50,000 per QALY gained and cost effective in 100.0% of iterations at a threshold of CHF 200,000 per QALY gained. Moreover, RSVpreF is cost saving in 27.8% of iterations and increases QALYs in 100.0% of iterations when compared with no prophylaxis.

In the univariate sensitivity analyses, changing four of the parameters (proportion of hospitalisations requiring an ICU admission to a high value, the RSVpreF vaccine unit cost to a low value, the unit cost of an ICU episode to a high value, and the unit cost of a general ward episode to a high value) results in the conclusion that RSVpreF becomes cost saving and, therefore, dominant. RSVpreF also becomes dominant in six of the scenarios (when the cost of the vaccination was reduced). RSVpreF is also dominant when the Spanish data are used to inform the baseline rate of hospitalisations.

In terms of clinical outcomes, RSVpreF decreases the total number of RSV infections resulting in healthcare-seeking behaviour (9,087 versus 6,896) and, therefore, decreases the healthcare expenditure required to treat RSV infections. However, the treatment costs associated with RSVpreF (CHF 207.53) cause an increase in overall costs.

The increase in costs associated with RSVpreF is likely to be outweighed by the accompanying reduction in RSV infections, resulting in less healthcare-seeking behaviour and a subsequent increase in health-related quality of life. This conclusion is more likely to be true when higher values of the cost-effectiveness threshold are adopted, and the sensitivity analysis indicates that model outcomes are robust to the scenarios considered.

The findings of this study were consistent with a Canadian economic evaluation, which reported that RSVpreF was cost effective compared with no RSV prophylaxis, where the willingness-to-pay threshold was $50,000 per QALY gained [23]. The results are more favourable in a Spanish setting, where RSVpreF dominates (i.e. was more effective and less costly) no RSV prophylaxis [22].

Strengths and Limitations

The robust model structure is a major strength. The structure aligns with the models identified from the rapid systematic review and allows for the direct implementation of efficacy data from the key MATISSE (RSVpreF), MELODY (nirsevimab) and HARMONIE (nirsevimab) clinical trials. The primary limitations associated with the modelling approach are related to the assumptions made owing to data limitations. However, all key assumptions were validated by a clinical expert.

It was necessary to limit the time horizon to 1 year owing to the uncertainties in the long-term impact of RSVpreF and nirsevimab after 1 year on other conditions. While this approach has been recorded in previous studies [24, 28], the limited time horizon may underestimate the benefits of RSVpreF and nirsevimab. Furthermore, the model does not consider the effects of vaccinations on disease transmission and herd immunity because a static modelling approach was used, and there is limited evidence to inform the indirect effectiveness of the vaccinations in an unvaccinated population. Therefore, the cost effectiveness of RSVpreF may be underestimated. The model also did not consider that maternal immunisation with RSVpreF could prevent RSV in mothers, which may further underestimate the benefits associated with RSVpreF. This does not apply to nirsevimab, which is administered directly to newborns and infants.

It was not possible to model risk-group-specific subgroups (for example, people with comorbidities, by term status or weight) because of a lack of baseline RSV infection rates for each reference category and efficacy data stratified by subgroup. Furthermore, MATISSE was not powered to provide estimates of efficacy for late preterm individuals. Therefore, it was assumed that vaccine efficacy for late preterm individuals was equal to 83.3% of the efficacy for full-term individuals. This approach was adopted by Alvarez et al. (2024) [22] and informed by a study of antibody transplacental transfer [14]. This was a necessary assumption, and the clinical expert confirmed that the approach was reasonable. It was also necessary to derive the efficacy of RSVpreF and nirsevimab from separate trials because no direct head-to-head trials were identified from the systematic searches (MATISSE, MELODY and HARMONIE [33–35]). Therefore, the model did not account for differences in the trial populations (i.e. treatment effect modifiers) or design.

It was also necessary to seek input from the clinical expert regarding the assumption of long-term efficacy and subsequent treatment waning. In the base case, it was assumed that treatment efficacy waned linearly to 0% by age 9–10 months, and it was not certain whether a scenario of instant equivalence with no RSV prophylaxis or a longer period of treatment waning would be more reflective of clinical practice. However, as shown by the scenario analyses, the impact of adopting alternative waning assumptions on model outcomes was relatively marginal.

While the model was populated with Swiss-specific data where possible, data to inform the baseline incidence of RSV-related primary care and ED visits in Switzerland were not available. Therefore, these inputs were informed by a Spanish source, which may not be directly generalisable to the Swiss population. Furthermore, the incidence of RSV is likely to have been underestimated because there will be many cases of RSV that are not identified. Healthcare resource use providers are not required to treat all cases of RSV infections (i.e. asymptomatic or mild cases) and, therefore, these cases will not be recorded. It was also conservatively assumed that individuals could only experience one RSV infection over the model time horizon and could not have re-visits to healthcare institutions. Therefore, the overall RSV disease burden and intervention benefits may be underestimated. However, the impact of these assumptions and alternative data sources on model outcomes was assessed during the sensitivity analyses, and outcomes remained highly robust.

The anticipated coverage rates associated with both RSVpreF and nirsevimab are uncertain. Furthermore, the RSVpreF vaccine is recommended in the third trimester of pregnancy; this may lead to a proportion of preterm birth mothers not receiving the vaccine before the birth or in time for effective transplacental antibody transfer. A lower coverage was assumed in scenario analyses.

Conclusions

This analysis estimates that RSVpreF increases the number of RSV infections, decreases costs and decreases QALYs compared with nirsevimab. However, the results are reversed (decreased number of RSV infections, increased costs, increased QALYs) when compared with no RSV prophylaxis. The model has helped to inform recent guidance updates in Switzerland, with RSVpreF now reimbursed by mandatory health insurance.
