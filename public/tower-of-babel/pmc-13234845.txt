A naphthyl polymer with STING-activating property for antigen and adjuvant co-delivery to achieve potent cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Lanfang Ren, Qianqian Chen, Yan Lou, Yang Chen, Chunyang Yuan, Yunfeng Liu, Jia Lv, Zhoufang Mei, Yiyun Cheng, Xiaopan Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTherapeutic cancer vaccines remain constrained by insufficient intracellular antigen trafficking and weak activation of innate immunity. Here, we report an immunoactive dendrimer-based nanovaccine platform that integrates efficient antigen cytosolic transport with intrinsic stimulation of innate immunity. Through screening a library of thiourea-modified dendrimers, we identify a naphthyl-functionalized polymer (D15) that forms stable antigen nanocomplexes via noncovalent interactions, enabling rapid endosomal escape and efficient cytosolic antigen release in dendritic cells. Mechanistic investigations demonstrated that D15 triggered STING-associated signaling in a cGAS-independent manner, likely related to endosomal membrane perturbation, thereby promoting type I interferon secretion and dendritic cell activation. Owing to its modular structure, D15 also enabled flexible incorporation of additional immunoadjuvants. Incorporation of CpG enhanced lymph node dendritic cell activation and antigen presentation, whereas loading of cGAMP further potentiated STING signaling and intratumoral immune responses. Both CpG- and cGAMP-adjuvanted D15 nanovaccines elicit potent antitumor immune responses in melanoma-bearing mice. Moreover, combination with PD-1 blockade markedly improved tumor suppression, cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, and survival outcomes. Collectively, this work establishes D15 as a multifunctional immunoactive nanocarrier with considerable potential for the construction of next-generation cancer nanovaccines.Keywords: Dendrimer, Nanovaccine, Cytosolic protein delivery, Naphthyl polymer, Cancer immunotherapy

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has emerged as a transformative strategy for cancer treatment by harnessing endogenous immune responses against malignant cells [1]. Diverse therapeutic approaches, including immune checkpoint inhibition, adoptive T-cell therapy, cytokine-based treatment, and therapeutic vaccination, have shown substantial clinical promise[[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Among these strategies, therapeutic cancer vaccines are particularly attractive because they can induce antigen-specific immune responses capable of eliminating residual tumor cells and preventing recurrence [7,8]. Nevertheless, their clinical performance is still limited by several major barriers, such as inefficient cytosolic delivery of antigens to antigen-presenting cells, insufficient coordination between antigen and adjuvant delivery, inadequate activation of dendritic cells, and the lack of delivery systems with intrinsic immunostimulatory activity[[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. These challenges highlight the necessity of developing multifunctional nanoplatforms that can simultaneously facilitate intracellular antigen transport, endosomal escape, and innate immune activation.

To address these challenges, a variety of nanovaccine platforms have been engineered to enhance antigen–adjuvant co-delivery and promote DC maturation[[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Despite these advances, most nanocarriers still exhibit suboptimal endosomal escape, rely on ancillary adjuvants, or involve complex and non-scalable fabrication procedures [8,21,22]. Recent efforts have focused on designing “minimalist nanovaccines”—nanocarriers capable of simultaneously achieving efficient antigen cytosolic transport and innate immune activation[[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Examples include azole-modified polyethylenimine activating the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway [27], cationic fluoropolymers engaging Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling [28], and polysaccharide-based systems triggering TLR2/4-mediated maturation [29]. Efforts employing combinatorial polymer screening have identified functional copolymers with tailored monomer composition or side-chain chemistry to achieve potent immunostimulatory activity [30,31]. Yang and co-workers recently advanced this concept by engineering a polymeric nanovaccine that intrinsically couples STING agonism with adjuvant activity, enabling antigen condensation via simple electrostatic assembly and producing highly streamlined nanovaccines [24]. Overall, nanocarriers that unify robust endosomal escape with intrinsic immuno-activation represent an increasingly compelling route towards next-generation cancer vaccines. Relative to covalent conjugation, noncovalent antigen–carrier self-assembly offers a structurally conservative and operationally minimal strategy, facilitating cytosolic transport while preserving the antigen's native conformation[[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Mechanistically, the synergy and fine-tuned balance among electrostatic, hydrophobic, and hydrogen-bonding interactions dictate nanocomplex stability, cellular uptake efficiency, and ultimately the fidelity of antigen release[[36], [37], [38], [39]].

Here, we constructed a library of dendrimers bearing thiourea groups—bidentate hydrogen-bond donors—combined with hydrophobic moieties to investigate how balanced noncovalent interactions govern antigen protein delivery. Through systematic screening, we identified a naphthylthiourea–functionalized dendrimer (D15) with potent endosomal-disruptive capability and efficient antigen cytosolic delivery, despite its moderate protein loading capacity. Further mechanistic studies revealed that D15 facilitated rapid antigen release into the cytoplasm through membrane perturbation, which subsequently enhanced MHC-I-associated antigen presentation in bone marrow–derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). Moreover, D15 was found to intrinsically stimulate STING-related signaling, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of tank-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), accompanied by elevated secretion of type I interferons. Thus, D15 functioned simultaneously as an antigen carrier and an innate immune activator. By simply mixing D15 with ovalbumin (OVA), we established a minimalist nanovaccine that induces dose-dependent dendritic cell maturation and robust antigen cross-presentation. The addition of CpG further amplified dendritic cell activation both in vitro and in draining lymph nodes, ultimately eliciting potent tumor-specific immunity and marked suppression of B16F10-OVA tumor growth. Furthermore, combination treatment with anti-PD-1 antibodies produced improved therapeutic outcomes and extended animal survival. Incorporation of cGAMP into the D15 nanoplatform additionally intensified STING-mediated signaling and innate immune activation, thereby further augmenting antitumor efficacy (Fig. 1). Collectively, these results demonstrate an effective design strategy for polymeric cancer nanovaccines through the integration of efficient endosomal disruption, optimized noncovalent antigen interactions, and inherent immunostimulatory activity. This study also underscores the promise of multifunctional cationic polymers as adaptable platforms for advanced cancer immunotherapy.

Experimental section

Materials

All isothiocyanate small molecules shown in Fig. 1a were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. The compounds, listed in the order corresponding to the figure, are as follows: ethoxycarbonyl isothiocyanate (1, Macklin, China), butyl isothiocyanate (2, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), hexyl isothiocyanate (3, Adamas, China), decyl isothiocyanate (4, J&K Scientific, China), dodecyl isothiocyanate (5, J&K Scientific), cyclohexyl isothiocyanate (6, Macklin), 3-(diethylamino)propyl isothiocyanate (7, Aladdin, China), 2-piperidinoethyl isothiocyanate (8, Sigma-Aldrich), 2-(4-morpholino)ethyl isothiocyanate (9, Aladdin), phenyl isothiocyanate (10, Adamas), benzyl isothiocyanate (11, Macklin), phenethyl isothiocyanate (12, Macklin), benzoyl isothiocyanate (13, Adamas), 4-cyanophenyl isothiocyanate (14, Macklin), 1-naphthyl isothiocyanate (15, Macklin), 4-fluorophenyl isothiocyanate (16, Adamas), 2,4-difluorophenyl isothiocyanate (17, Adamas), and 3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)phenyl isothiocyanate (18, Adamas). G5 PAMAM, OVA, OVA-FITC, Trypan blue, TransEx™, CCK8, Lysotracker Red, and Hoechst 33342 were purchased from Dendritech (USA), Sigma-Aldrich (USA), Solarbio (China), Yeasen (China), Cenji Biotech (China), Beyotime (China), Meilun Biotech (China), and Beyotime (China), respectively. The Class B CpG oligonucleotide (Mouse TLR9 ligand) was obtained from InvivoGen (USA). The InVivoMab anti-mouse PD-1 (CD279) antibody was sourced from BioXcell (USA), and 2′,3′-cGAMP was provided by MedChemExpress (USA). For flow cytometric analyses, the antibodies listed below were employed: CD11c, CD80, CD86, SIINFEKL-H-2Kb, CD3, CD8, and IFN-γ from BioLegend (USA), and Grzm B from eBioscience (USA). The appropriate concentrations of each antibody were used as recommended by the manufacturer. The antibodies for Western Blot analysis, except p-IRF3 (Immunoway Biotech, China) and p-TBK1 (Biodragon, China) were purchased from Abcam (UK). The ELISA detection kits used in this study were obtained from MultiSciences (China), unless otherwise specified.

Synthesis and characterization of thiourea-modified dendrimers

G5 PAMAM dendrimers and isothiocyanate small molecules were each dissolved in methanol. The small-molecule solution was slowly introduced into the dendrimer solution with constant stirring, according to the predefined feeding ratios listed in Table S1. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 h and then purified via dialysis, initially in methanol followed by deionized water. After lyophilization, the obtained polymers were characterized by 1H NMR spectroscopy.

Preparation and characterization of polymer/protein complexes

The synthesized D15 dendrimer was incubated with OVA in deionized water for 30 min at various mass ratios (0.4, 0.6, 0.8 1.0, and 2.0) to form D15/OVA nanoparticles. Adjuvant-loaded complexes were prepared by mixing D15 and OVA in deionized water at a 1:1 mass ratio for 10 min, CpG was then added at weight ratios ranging from 0.02 to 0.08, followed by a 20-min incubation. The complexes' hydrodynamic diameter and surface zeta potential were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS 90 (Malvern Instruments, UK), and their nanoparticle morphology was examined via transmission electron microscopy (H-7700, Hitachi, Japan).

Protein loading efficiency was evaluated by fluorescence spectroscopy. Polymer/OVA–FITC complexes (16 μg/mL each) were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, and the fluorescence intensity of the supernatant was measured (excitation at 470 nm; emission at 518 nm). The free OVA–FITC concentration was determined using a standard calibration curve (Y = 622.1X + 2405, R2 = 0.9947), where Y denotes fluorescence intensity and X denotes protein concentration (μg/mL). Protein binding was further confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. The supernatants obtained after centrifugation were loaded onto a 2% agarose gel and electrophoresed at 90 V for 20 min.

Cell culture and intracellular protein delivery

HeLa cells, both with or without stable Gal8–YFP expression, were cultured in DMEM, while DC2.4 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium. Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.

HeLa cells were seeded in 48-well plates or confocal dishes for cellular uptake experiments and left to adhere overnight. Polymer/EGFP complexes (16 μg/mL for polymers, 20 μg/mL for EGFP) were prepared in serum-reduced medium, incubated for 30 min, diluted appropriately, and then added to cells when confluency reached approximately 90%. Following a 6-h incubation, cells were extensively washed with PBS, and trypan blue was used to quench extracellular fluorescence. Intracellular fluorescence was examined using flow cytometry and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

DC2.4 cells were exposed to D15/OVA-FITC complexes for varying durations (1, 2, 4, or 6 h) to study cytosolic antigen delivery and endosomal escape in antigen-presenting cells. Post-treatment, cells were washed, stained with LysoTracker Red and Hoechst 33342, and analyzed using CLSM. Gal8-YFP-expressing HeLa cells were incubated with D15/OVA complexes for 6 h, followed by PBS washing and nuclear staining with Hoechst 33342. Gal8 recruitment was visualized by CLSM, and intracellular Gal8 puncta were quantified. For endocytosis pathway analysis, DC2.4 cells were pretreated for 1 h with various inhibitors, including Cytochalasin D (CytoD, 10 μM), Ethylisopropylamiloride (EIPA, 50 μM), Wortmannin (Wort, 0.1 μM), Genistein (Geni, 700 μM), Nystatin (Nyst, 25 μg/mL), Chlorpromazine (CPZ, 20 μM), Dynasore (Dyna, 20 μM), Methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD, 5 mM), Bafilomycin A1 (Baf A1, 1.3 μM), Dextran (Dex, 100 μg/mL), or Chloroquine (CQ, 100 μM), before incubation with D15/OVA complexes for 6 h. Unless specified stated, the concentrations of both OVA (or OVA–FITC) and D15 were maintained at 10 μg/mL.

To assess the influence of D15 dosage on antigen delivery and cytotoxicity, DC2.4 cells were exposed to D15/OVA complexes prepared with a constant OVA concentration (10 μg/mL) and increasing D15 concentrations (4, 6, 8, 10, and 20 μg/mL). Antigen uptake was quantified by flow cytometry, while cell viability was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay. In addition, hemocompatibility was evaluated by a hemolysis assay. Red blood cells (RBCs) were incubated with D15 at indicated concentrations (2.5–200 μg/mL) for 1 h at 37 °C. After centrifugation, hemoglobin release in the supernatant was measured at 540 nm, and hemolysis (%) was calculated using PBS and Triton X-100 as negative and positive controls, respectively.

In vitro studies on DC maturation and antigen cross-presentation

BMDCs were extracted from the femurs and tibias of 6–8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice and differentiated using established protocols. On day 6, immature BMDCs were collected. BMDCs were cultured in 96-well plates (6 × 104 cells per well) and exposed to D15/OVA complexes with D15 concentrations ranging from 4 to 20 μg/mL and a constant OVA concentration of 10 μg/mL for 24 h, after which cytotoxicity was evaluated using CCK-8 analysis. In maturation studies, BMDCs were cultured in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and exposed to formulations of OVA, OVA/CpG, D15/OVA, or D15/OVA/CpG for 24 h. OVA and D15 were consistently used at 10 μg/mL, with CpG concentrations varying from 0 to 0.08 μg/mL. Flow cytometry was used to analyze cells stained with antibodies targeting CD11c, CD80, and CD86. Cytokine concentrations (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) in culture supernatants were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturers' guidelines. BMDCs, prepared as previously described, were stained with CD11c and SIINFEKL–H-2Kb antibodies for antigen cross-presentation analysis and analyzed via flow cytometry.

DC maturation and cross-presentation in lymph nodes

Mice were subcutaneously injected with the indicated vaccine formulations. Three days after immunization, inguinal lymph nodes were harvested, mechanically dissociated, and enzymatically digested to obtain single-cell suspensions. DC maturation was assessed by flow cytometry. Five days post-injection, antigen cross-presentation was evaluated based on SIINFEKL–H-2Kb staining.

Investigation of STING pathway activation

BMDCs were cultured in 6-well plates and exposed for 24 h to treatments including OVA, cGAMP, OVA/cGAMP, D15, D15/OVA, and D15/OVA/cGAMP. The concentrations of OVA and D15 were maintained at 10 μg/mL, while cGAMP was used at 0–2 μM as indicated. After treatment, IFN-β, a canonical downstream effector of STING pathway activation, was quantified in the culture supernatant using a commercial ELISA kit to evaluate pathway activation. Cells were then lysed on ice for 30 min in RIPA buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and a 2× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail for subsequent protein analysis. Cell lysates were sonicated by a VCX150 ultrasonic homogenizer (Sonics & Materials Inc., USA) at 30% amplitude, following a cycle of 10 s on and 15 s off for a total of 30 s. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA assay kit from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). Proteins were combined with loading buffer, denatured at 100 °C for 5 min, and separated using 12.5% SDS-PAGE. Proteins were then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes, blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting TBK1, phospho-TBK1 (p-TBK1), IRF3, and phospho-IRF3 (p-IRF3) at a 1:1000 dilution. GAPDH (1:1000) served as the loading control. Post-washing, membranes were treated with suitable secondary antibodies, and protein bands were detected using the Chemidoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA).

As to detect cytosolic mtDNA level, DC2.4 cells were treated with D15 or D15/OVA for the indicated time. Cytosolic fractions were isolated by differential centrifugation to remove nuclei and mitochondria. Briefly, cell lysates were first centrifuged at low speed to pellet nuclei, followed by high-speed centrifugation to remove mitochondria. The resulting supernatant was collected as the cytosolic fraction. Cytosolic DNA was extracted using a DNA purification kit and subjected to qPCR analysis on a CFX Duet Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) using primers specific for the mitochondrial gene mt-Nd1. Relative cytosolic mtDNA levels were normalized to the Blank group.

Antigen-specific T-cell activation in the spleen

Splenocytes were isolated from immunized mice on day 7 post-vaccination. IFN-γ ELISpot assays were performed using pre-coated plates (SinoBiological, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Splenocytes were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well and restimulated ex vivo with the OVA-derived peptide SIINFEKL (5 μg/mL) for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. After incubation, plates were processed according to the manufacturer's protocol, including washing, incubation with detection antibody and enzyme conjugate, and color development. Plates were then air-dried, and spot-forming units (SFU) were quantified using an automated ELISpot reader (ImmunoSpot, CTL, USA). Results are expressed as SFU per 106 splenocytes.

In vivo tumor immunotherapy

The Ethics Committee of East China Normal University approved all animal studies (Approval No. ARXM2023002) and following National Institutes of Health guidelines. To evaluate systemic inflammatory responses, blood samples were collected from C57BL/6 mice at indicated time points after administration of D15 or D15/OVA/cGAMP. Serum TNF-α levels were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For in vivo biodistribution analysis, OVA was labeled with the near-infrared dye Cy5.5 and formulated with D15. Mice were intradermally (i.d.) injected with the labeled formulations, and major organs and lymph nodes were harvested at indicated time points for ex vivo fluorescence imaging using an IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer, USA). For antitumor studies, C57BL/6 mice received subcutaneous (s.c.) implants of B16-OVA melanoma cells in their right flank. Following tumor development, mice were randomly divided into six experimental groups. The doses of OVA and D15 were maintained at 5 mg/kg for all relevant treatments. For CpG-adjuvanted vaccination, mice (n = 5) received intradermal immunizations on days 7, 14, and 21, with CpG dosed at 0.2 mg/kg. For cGAMP-adjuvanted vaccination, immunizations (n = 6) were given intradermally on days 6, 13, and 20, with cGAMP dosed at 0.75 mg/kg. The aPD-1 antibody was administered intravenously (i.v.) at 1 mg/kg according to the treatment schedule. Measurements of tumor size and body weight were conducted bi-daily. At the study endpoint, tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 4 μm slices. Tumor sections underwent TUNEL staining to assess apoptosis, and major organ sections were stained with H&E to evaluate biosafety. Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), urea, creatinine (CREA), and uric acid (UA) were assessed to evaluate hepatic and renal function. A complete blood count was conducted to assess levels of white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), platelets (PLT), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC).

Tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte analysis

Tumors were excised 5 days post-vaccination, and minced into small fragments, followed by enzymatic digestion. Tissue samples were digested at 37 °C for approximately 90 min in complete RPMI-1640 medium containing collagenase I (1.5 mg/mL), collagenase IV, hyaluronidase, and DNase I (0.2 mg/mL). The digested tissues were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove any remaining debris and muscle fragments. After washing with PBS, single-cell suspensions were obtained. Cells were then stained with anti-CD8 (PE) together with anti-IFN-γ (APC) or anti-Grzm B (APC) antibodies and subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

For all statistical analyses and data visualization, GraphPad Prism 10 was used. Data normality was assessed prior to analysis, and appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests were applied as needed. Unless otherwise specified for animal studies, all in vitro biological experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3). Results are displayed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise indicated. One-way ANOVA was utilized to assess group differences. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted and evaluated with the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. A p value below 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Synthesis of isothiocyanate-modified dendrimers for cytosolic protein delivery

A library of fifth-generation poly (amidoamine) (G5 PAMAM) dendrimers was synthesized, featuring modifications with 18 different isothiocyanate-derived compounds (Fig. 1a-b). Introduction of thiourea moieties endows the dendrimers with bidentate hydrogen-bonding capability, which can cooperate with electrostatic interactions to enhance polymer–protein complexation, while the structural diversity of the small molecules enables modulation of hydrophobic interactions. To eliminate the influence of grafting density on delivery efficiency, the feeding molar ratios of the compounds to dendrimer were carefully controlled to afford comparable grafting densities across all polymers (Table S1). Consistent with our previous reports showing optimal protein delivery is achieved at approximately 50% surface modification [[40], [41], [42]], each PAMAM dendrimer was functionalized with an average of approximately 60 compounds, as confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S1–S4). The dendrimer library's intracellular protein delivery performance was systematically assessed under standardized conditions to facilitate a direct structure–activity relationship analysis, utilizing enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) as a model protein in HeLa cells. Among the synthesized dendrimers, five formulations (D10, D14, D15, D16, and D17) exhibited markedly enhanced intracellular fluorescence comparable to or exceeding that of commercial protein delivery reagents Pulsin and TransEx™ (TE), indicating their strong capability for cytosolic protein delivery (Fig. 1d-e). Notably, these top-performing dendrimers predominantly feature aromatic or conformationally rigid hydrophobic substituents, suggesting that while thiourea-mediated hydrogen bonding contributes to protein complexation, variations in hydrophobic moiety architecture play a dominant role in governing intracellular delivery efficiency [43].

Performance and mechanism of D15 in cytosolic antigen delivery

We further evaluated the antigen delivery performance of the top five polymers on DC2.4 cells. FITC-labeled OVA (OVA-FITC) was used as the model antigen. Upon complexation with OVA-FITC, D15 formed stable nanocomplexes that enabled markedly enhanced antigen internalization in DC2.4 cells, as evidenced by both significantly increased mean fluorescence intensity and homogeneous cytosolic distribution compared with other candidate polymers (Fig. 2a-b), highlighting its superior capacity to facilitate cellular uptake and cytosolic release. Both fluorescence spectroscopy and agarose gel electrophoresis demonstrated that the five polymers were capable of efficiently complexing with OVA-FITC (Fig. 2c–e). Although D15 exhibit a relatively lower OVA loading efficacy compared to other four polymers, its protein release is more efficient as revealed by the agrose gel electrophoresis analysis. D15/OVA nanocomplexes effectively recruited galectin-8 (Gal8), a cytosolic sensor for endosomal membrane damage, in HeLa cells expressing Gal8-yellow fluorescent protein (Gal8–YFP), demonstrating significant endosomal disruption (Fig. 2f-g). Among all tested polymers, D15 triggered the highest number of Gal8 puncta per cell, providing direct and quantitative evidence of its superior endosomal membrane-disruptive capability. Time-resolved confocal imaging further demonstrated rapid dissociation of OVA-FITC from lysosomal compartments, with consistently low Pearson's correlation coefficients between antigen and Lysotracker signals even at early incubation times (Fig. 2h), confirming efficient endosomal escape. Mechanistic studies using inhibitors revealed that D15/OVA nanocomplexes were internalized through multiple endocytic pathways, with clathrin-mediated endocytosis and macropinocytosis contributing most prominently to intracellular antigen accumulation (Fig. 2i). Optimizing the polymer-to-antigen mass ratio significantly improved intracellular delivery efficiency in a dose-dependent fashion, while ensuring low cytotoxicity across tested concentrations in DC2.4 cells (Fig. 2j-k). In addition, D15 exhibited minimal hemolytic activity even at elevated concentrations, indicating good hemocompatibility (Fig. S5). Taken together, these results demonstrated D15 as a highly efficient and low cytotoxic carrier that integrates balanced antigen binding/release, rapid endosomal escape, and favorable intracellular trafficking behavior for effective antigen cross-presentation.

D15/OVA nanocomplex promotes BMDC maturation and antigen cross-presentation

Building on the demonstrated advantages of D15 in intracellular antigen transport and endosomal escape, we next investigated its potential to function as an immunostimulatory nanocarrier for antigen presentation and dendritic cell activation. D15 formed stable nanoparticles with OVA across a broad range of carrier-to-antigen mass ratios, exhibiting well-defined hydrodynamic sizes, low polydispersity indices (PDI), and positively charged surface potentials (Fig. 3a). Increasing the D15/OVA ratio resulted in a gradual decrease in nanoparticle size accompanied by a moderate increase in zeta potential, suggesting enhanced electrostatic condensation without excessive aggregation. Additionally, D15/OVA nanocomplexes displayed negligible cytotoxicity toward BMDCs over the tested range, with cell viabilities remaining above 85%, except at the highest mass ratio (Fig. 3b). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further confirmed the formation of spherical and uniform nanostructures at the optimized mass ratio of 1:1 (Fig. 3c).

We next evaluated the capacity of D15/OVA nanocomplexes to induce BMDC maturation. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that treatment with D15/OVA nanocomplexes led to a dose-dependent upregulation of costimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86, indicating effective DC activation (Fig. 3d–f). Simultaneously, there was a notable increase in the percentage of SIINFEKL–H-2Kb-positive BMDCs, demonstrating effective antigen presentation via the MHC I pathway (Fig. 3g). The D15/OVA nanocomplex at a 1:1 mass ratio demonstrated the highest DC maturation and antigen presentation with strong cytocompatibility, making it the preferred nanoformulation for further research.

To further enhance the immunostimulatory potency of the nanovaccine, the TLR9 agonist CpG was incorporated into the D15/OVA nanocomplex via electrostatic interaction (Fig. 3h). The resulting D15/OVA/CpG nanocomplexes retained nanoscale sizes and positive surface charges across different CpG loading ratios (Fig. 3i-j). CpG incorporation significantly enhanced BMDC activation, demonstrated by increased expression of CD80 and CD86 and improved SIINFEKL–H-2Kb+ cross-presentation (Fig. 3k-l). The D15/OVA/CpG formulation, with a mass ratio of 1:1:0.04, demonstrated superior DC maturation and antigen presentation, making it the preferred choice for further research. Aligned with these results, the optimized D15/OVA/CpG nanocomplex significantly induced the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (Fig. 3m–o). These findings indicate that D15 functions both as an effective antigen delivery system and an intrinsic immunostimulatory scaffold. It works synergistically with CpG to promote dendritic cell maturation and antigen cross-presentation, thereby strengthening antitumor immune responses.

In vivo immune activation and antitumor efficacy of D15/OVA/CpG nanovaccine

The in vivo immunological efficacy of the D15-based nanovaccine was assessed by subcutaneously immunizing mice with PBS, OVA, OVA/CpG, D15/OVA, or D15/OVA/CpG, and subsequently analyzing dendritic cell activation in the draining lymph nodes (Fig. 4a). Flow cytometry revealed that D15/OVA/CpG induced the highest proportion of mature CD80+CD86+ DCs among CD11c+ populations on day 3 post-immunization (Fig. 4b and c and S6). The D15/OVA/CpG group showed a marked increase in antigen cross-presentation, demonstrated by a higher frequency of SIINFEKL–H-2Kb+ CD11c+ DCs on day 5 (Fig. 4d), suggesting effective in vivo antigen delivery and processing.

Given the robust immune responses elicited in lymph nodes, we next investigated the therapeutic efficacy of the nanovaccine in a B16-OVA melanoma model, either alone or in combination with immune checkpoint inhibition [44] (Fig. 4e). Administration of OVA/CpG, D15/OVA, or anti–PD-1 (aPD-1) alone produced only limited suppression of tumor growth. In contrast, mice treated with D15/OVA/CpG exhibited substantially delayed tumor progression (Fig. 4f-g). More importantly, combining D15/OVA/CpG with aPD-1 generated the strongest therapeutic response among all groups, resulting in durable inhibition of tumor development and significantly extended survival (Fig. 4h), while causing no observable loss in body weight (Fig. S7). Flow cytometry was employed to examine tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, aiming to explore the immunological mechanisms. Vaccination with D15/OVA/CpG markedly increased the proportion of CD8+ T cells within the CD3+ T-cell population in tumors, and this effect became more prominent following combination with aPD-1 therapy (Fig. 4i–j), consistent with PD-1 blockade's role in restoring T-cell function and promoting intratumoral T-cell accumulation [45]. The findings indicate that the D15/OVA/CpG nanovaccine successfully initiates antigen-specific cellular immunity in vivo and works synergistically with immune checkpoint blockade to improve cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and antitumor effectiveness.

D15-based nanovaccines activate the STING pathway

The STING pathway is crucial for innate immune detection and the activation of antitumor immunity through the induction of type I interferon responses [[46], [47], [48]]. Upon activation, STING undergoes conformational changes and recruits TBK1, leading to phosphorylation of IRF3 and subsequent production of type I interferons, predominantly IFN-β (Fig. 5a). To determine whether D15 activates the STING pathway, BMDCs were exposed to free OVA, D15, or D15/OVA nanocomplexes. Western blot analysis showed that both D15 and D15/OVA significantly increased phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 without affecting total protein levels (Fig. 5b), accompanied by robust IFN-β secretion (Fig. 5c and S8a), confirming effective activation of the STING signaling cascade. Importantly, mechanistic studies demonstrated that D15-induced STING activation is independent of the canonical cGAS pathway, as inhibition of cGAS using RU.521 did not affect IFN-β production (Fig. S8b). In addition, no significant increase in cytosolic mitochondrial DNA was observed upon D15 treatment (Fig. S9), excluding mtDNA-mediated activation. Further studies indicated that endosomal membrane disruption and associated ionic perturbation contribute to STING activation, as inhibition of endosomal escape or restoration of intracellular K+ levels significantly attenuated the response (Fig. S8c–S8d). This observation is consistent with previous reports identifying potassium efflux as a critical upstream signal in innate immune activation [49]. Consistently, elevated levels of IL-1β were also observed (Fig. 5d), indicating broad activation of downstream inflammatory signaling. Collectively, these results demonstrate that D15 functions as an effective cGAS-independent activator of the STING pathway through a mechanism involving endosomal disruption and ionic imbalance.

Although D15 itself can activate the STING pathway, its intrinsic activity is relatively moderate compared to canonical STING agonists. Given that cyclic dinucleotides such as cGAMP are well-established STING agonists but suffer from poor cellular uptake due to their negative charge [50,51], we next incorporated cGAMP into the D15/OVA nanocomplex to enhance immunostimulatory potency via electrostatic interactions. Flow cytometry demonstrated that cGAMP introduction enhanced BMDC maturation in a concentration-dependent manner, indicated by increased CD80 and CD86 expression (Fig. 5e). Interestingly, despite enhanced DC maturation, excessive cGAMP loading led to a reduction in antigen cross-presentation efficiency compared with D15/OVA alone (Fig. 5f), suggesting a delicate balance between innate immune activation and antigen processing. Western blot analysis showed that D15/OVA/cGAMP treatment markedly increased the phosphorylation levels of TBK1 and IRF3 compared with either OVA/cGAMP or D15/OVA, indicating enhanced activation of STING-related signaling pathways (Fig. 5g). In parallel, D15/OVA/cGAMP induced substantially higher secretion of IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-1β in a concentration-dependent manner than either free cGAMP or D15/OVA alone (Fig. 5h–j). These results demonstrate that D15 serves not only as an efficient antigen carrier but also as an intrinsic STING-activating material. Moreover, incorporation of cGAMP further strengthened innate immune activation, dendritic cell maturation, and antigen presentation, supporting the utility of the D15/OVA/cGAMP formulation as a multifunctional immunostimulatory nanoplatform for coordinated activation of innate and adaptive immunity.

In vivo antitumor efficacy and immune activation of the D15/OVA/cGAMP nanovaccine

The in vivo biosafety of the D15/OVA/cGAMP nanovaccine was first evaluated. Serum analysis showed consistently low levels of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, at all examined time points (Fig. S10), indicating that vaccination did not induce obvious systemic inflammatory toxicity. We next investigated the in vivo fate of the nanovaccine. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging revealed that D15-based formulations exhibited enhanced accumulation in lymph nodes and major organs compared to free OVA, indicating improved biodistribution and delivery efficiency (Fig. S11). The therapeutic efficacy of the D15/OVA/cGAMP nanovaccine was then evaluated in mice bearing B16-OVA melanoma tumors. As illustrated in Fig. 6a, mice were subcutaneously immunized according to the designated treatment schedule and randomly divided into six groups: blank, OVA/cGAMP, D15/OVA, aPD-1, D15/OVA/cGAMP, and D15/OVA/cGAMP combined with aPD-1. Compared with control groups, treatment with D15/OVA/cGAMP significantly inhibited tumor progression and prolonged survival (Fig. 6b–d). Notably, co-administration of D15/OVA/cGAMP with aPD-1 achieved the most pronounced therapeutic benefit, producing the strongest suppression of tumor growth and the longest survival duration among all groups (Fig. 6b–c), indicative of a markedly synergistic antitumor effect. Consistent with the tumor inhibition results, TUNEL staining revealed extensive tumor cell apoptosis in both the D15/OVA/cGAMP and combination treatment groups, with the highest apoptotic signal observed following combined therapy with aPD-1 (Fig. 6e).

To further clarify the underlying immune mechanisms, tumors and lymph nodes were collected for immunological analysis 5 days post-final vaccination. As shown in Fig. 6f, mice receiving D15/OVA/cGAMP displayed markedly reduced tumor weights compared with those treated with OVA/cGAMP or D15/OVA alone. Flow cytometric analysis of lymph nodes further demonstrated that D15/OVA/cGAMP vaccination induced the highest proportion of mature dendritic cells, while the addition of aPD-1 further promoted dendritic cell activation (Fig. 6g–h and S12). The findings demonstrate that the D15-based nanovaccine efficiently enhances antigen presentation and dendritic cell maturation in vivo. In parallel, D15/OVA/cGAMP treatment markedly increased immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment, particularly the proportion of CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which correlated with improved antigen presentation efficiency (Fig. 6i–j and S13). Functional characterization further showed that CD8+ T cells isolated from the D15/OVA/cGAMP group expressed higher levels of granzyme B (Grzm B) and IFN-γ, indicating enhanced cytotoxic activity and effector function (Fig. 6k-l). Consistently, IFN-γ ELISpot analysis of SIINFEKL-restimulated splenocytes revealed a significantly higher frequency of aantigen-responsive IFN-γ–producing T cells in the D15/OVA/cGAMP-treated mice relative to the control groups (Fig. S14), further confirming enhanced functional T-cell responses. Importantly, combination treatment with aPD-1 further amplified these T-cell-mediated immune responses, underscoring the synergistic interplay between nanovaccine-induced immune priming and immune checkpoint blockade. These findings suggest that the D15/OVA/cGAMP nanovaccine may possess the capacity to induce durable antitumor immune responses, although long-term immunological memory was not directly examined in the present study. Future work will focus on evaluating long-term immune protection through tumor rechallenge experiments and memory T-cell profiling. In addition to therapeutic performance, systemic biosafety was comprehensively assessed. Serum biochemistry parameters and complete blood counts collected 5 days post-vaccination remained within normal ranges compared with untreated mice (Fig. 6m). Furthermore, histological examination of major organs by H&E staining revealed no evident pathological abnormalities (Fig. S15). Taken together, these results demonstrate that the D15/OVA/cGAMP nanovaccine combines potent antitumor activity with favorable biocompatibility, supporting its promise as a safe and effective platform for cancer immunotherapy.

Conclusion

In summary, we established an immunoactive dendrimer-based nanovaccine platform that integrates efficient cytosolic antigen delivery with intrinsic innate immune activation to elicit robust antitumor immunity. D15, selected from a library of functional dendrimers, serves as an optimal carrier by efficiently delivering protein antigens, facilitating endosomal escape, and activating the STING pathway in dendritic cells, thus acting as both a delivery vehicle and an immunostimulatory scaffold. Integrating CpG into D15/OVA nanovaccines significantly improved dendritic cell maturation and antigen cross-presentation, effectively priming tumor-specific CD8+ T-cell responses in draining lymph nodes. In parallel, loading of the canonical STING agonist cGAMP into the D15/OVA nanocomplex further amplified STING-mediated immune responses and promoted intratumoral T-cell activation. In melanoma models, these D15-based nanovaccines effectively reshaped the tumor immune microenvironment and improved the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 blockade. Overall, this study provides a practical strategy for constructing multifunctional cancer nanovaccines and highlights the promise of immunoactive polymeric carriers for combination immunotherapy.
