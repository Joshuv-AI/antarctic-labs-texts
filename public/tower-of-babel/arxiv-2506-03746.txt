Dropout-Robust Mechanisms for Differentially Private and Fully Decentralized Mean Estimation Conference: ; ;
César Sabater, Sonia Ben Mokhtar, Jan Ramon

Abstract
. Achieving differentially private computations in decentralized settings poses significant challenges, particularly regarding accuracy, communication cost, and robustness against information leakage. While cryptographic solutions offer promise, they often suffer from high communication overhead or require centralization in the presence of network failures. Conversely, existing fully decentralized approaches typically rely on relaxed adversarial models or pairwise noise cancellation, the latter suffering from substantial accuracy degradation if parties unexpectedly disconnect. In this work, we propose IncA , a new protocol for fully decentralized mean estimation, a widely used primitive in data-intensive processing. Our protocol, which enforces differential privacy, requires no central orchestration and employs low-variance correlated noise, achieved by incrementally injecting sensitive information into the computation. First, we theoretically demonstrate that, when no parties permanently disconnect, our protocol achieves accuracy comparable to that of a centralized setting—already an improvement over most existing decentralized differentially private techniques. Second, we empirically show that our use of low-variance correlated noise significantly mitigates the accuracy loss experienced by existing techniques in the presence of dropouts.



1.
Introduction

The training of machine learning models commonly relies on the centralized processing of large datasets. While this approach offers simplicity, it presents substantial privacy implications when sensitive data is involved, or when data aggregation is limited by sharing constraints. For instance, private companies or hospitals might be reluctent to share their data for jointly training prediction models due to competitive secrecy or juridic regulations.
For these reasons, algorithms where the data remains local to their owners have gained significant popularity in the last decade. One prominent example is Federated Learning
(
McMahan et al. 2017
;
Kairouz et al. 2021b
)
, a framework in which participating entities iteratively perform local model training and subsequently transmit intermediate model updates to a central server for aggregation. While this architecture enhances data governance, it nonetheless presents residual challenges in terms of privacy and robustness. Indeed, computational processes relying on a large participant base introduce vulnerabilities to participant behavior, including failures or corruption by adversaries. This may compromise both the integrity of the outcome and participant privacy through inference attacks
(
Melis et al. 2019
;
Geiping et al. 2020
;
Mrini et al. 2024
;
Touat et al. 2024
;
Kariyappa et al. 2023
;
Pasquini et al. 2023
)
. Furthermore, the centralized visibility of all model updates to a single party in federated learning establishes a high-risk single point of failure.

To address the aforementioned limitations, entirely decentralized machine learning algorithms have been developed
(
Hegedűs et al. 2021
;
Beltrán et al. 2023
)
. These solutions present a promising alternative to their federated counterparts, as they remove the need for any centralized entity. In this context, decentralized averaging algorithms, which are the primary focus of this paper, constitute a fundamental component enabling parties to average their model parameters or gradients towards model convergence.
Specifically, we concentrate on the canonical task of averaging a set of private values held by participants. Despite its apparent simplicity, averaging serves as a critical primitive in decentralized learning and various other machine learning and data mining tasks, including recommendation systems
(
Nicolas et al. 2025
)
, clustering, matrix factorization
(
Nicolas et al. 2024
)
, decision trees, empirical cumulative distribution functions
(
Barczewski et al. 2025
)
and linear regression. Fundamentally, its applicability extends to any task amenable to decomposition into local computations followed by private averaging.

Our objective is then to analyze the privacy of decentralized averaging algorithms through the lens of
differential privacy (DP)

(
Dwork et al. 2006b
)
. This framework has emerged as a gold standard in privacy research due to its solid theoretical guarantees. Nevertheless, demonstrating that an algorithm satisfies DP is often challenging, typically necessitating the injection of noise into computations, which degrades accuracy. This challenge is particularly acute within the local differential privacy (LDP) framework
(
Duchi et al. 2013b
;
Kasiviswanathan et al. 2011
;
Kairouz et al. 2014
;
Kairouz et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2020
)
. In LDP, where data is fully privatized before it is used in collaborative computations,
the required noise levels are frequently prohibitively high, rendering computations practically unusable in many real-world scenarios.
The integration of cryptographic primitives, such as secure multiparty computation (specifically, Secure Aggregation
(
Bonawitz et al. 2017
;
Bell et al. 2020
)
) or fully homomorphic encryption, into decentralized computations offers a promising solution to substantially reduce the noise levels required for achieving differential privacy (DP) guarantees. This approach can yield privacy-accuracy trade-offs comparable to those observed in central DP settings
(
Agarwal et al. 2021
;
Kairouz et al. 2021a
;
Heikkilä 2025
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Jayaraman et al. 2018
;
Dwork et al. 2006a
)
, where a single trusted party or curator is responsible for noise injection. However, a significant drawback of these cryptographic methods is their high computational cost, particularly as the number of involved parties increases, or their reliance on a trusted central entity for orchestration and fault tolerance (e.g., handling participant drop-outs).

In the decentralized setting, several techniques have been proposed to reduce the noise of local DP. These solutions rely on gossip algorithms in which exchanges are similar to the protocols proposed in
(
Boyd et al. 2006
)
. However, they often rely on DP relaxations
(
Cyffers and Bellet 2022
;
Cyffers et al. 2022
)
, which can limit practical applicability, or necessitate correlated bounded noise (e.g., pairwise canceling Gaussian noise
(
Sabater et al. 2022
;
Allouah et al. 2024
;
Vithana et al. 2025
)
). The latter approaches either incur high communication costs or demand the cancellation of high-variance noise, which substantially impacts computational efficiency, particularly in the presence of dropouts.

In our work, we propose protocols that (i) have the same privacy accuracy trade-offs of Central DP when no drop-outs are present, which is similar to the trusted curator model of DP, (ii) satisfy the classical notion of DP, (iii) are robust to a percentage of parties colluding to infer information of the honest participants and (iv) have a bounded impact in drop-outs thanks to the use of low variance correlated noise.

1.1.
Contributions

Our contributions are the following:

(1)

We propose novel protocol for private averaging.
It involves iterative exchanges of messages in a way similar to the gossip protocols in
(
Boyd et al. 2006
)
with the addition of correlated noise. The latter ensures privacy with a minimal harm in accuracy. We provide a generic construction in which the private values as well as the noise injected in the computation are customizable, allowing to choose the most adequate strategy for different scenarios. Our approach differs from the common technique of previous correlated noise approaches
(
Sabater et al. 2022
;
Allouah et al. 2024
;
Vithana et al. 2025
)
and Secure Aggregation
(
Dwork et al. 2006a
;
Bonawitz et al. 2017
;
Bell et al. 2020
;
Taiello et al. 2024
;
Bell et al. 2023
)
, that are based in pairwise noise masks shared between users. In our approach, each user knows its own noise terms and cancels them in progressive updates.

(2)

When parties do not drop out of the protocol, we prove differential privacy guarantees even if the adversary (i) corrupts a proportion of the participants to passively share their gathered information, (ii) observes a proportion the exchanges, and (iii) knows all the network interactions (i.e., who communicated with whom).
While proven to be differentially private, our protocol matches the utility of Secure Aggregation combined with local noise
(
Agarwal et al. 2021
;
Kairouz et al. 2021a
;
Heikkilä 2025
;
Chen et al. 2022
)
without the use cryptographic primitives or a requiring a central orchestrator. This privacy-utility trade-off is comparable to Central DP.
We provide two characterizations of our privacy guarantees: the first is more accurate while the second is more interpretable. For the latter, we show that our guarantees hold when a sufficiently large and diverse set of exchanges have been performed between honest parties.

(3)

We prove that, given the graph that models the hidden exchanges between honest parties, certain topologies provide sufficient conditions for privacy or the lack of it. We provide positive and negative results. The former are for strongly connected topologies. The latter are for graphs where no party changes their neighbors across iterations and the adversary continually observes at least two honest parties.

(4)

We extend our privacy analysis to the case of dropouts, where correlated noise does not cancel as when all participants finish their contribution correctly. We show that our approach is especially resilient to dropouts when the injection of private values is done incrementally among the noise, therefore keeping correlated DP noise smaller in size.
The analysis is done both in theory and in practice. First, we show theoretically that DP noise that is not supposed to cancel is bounded. Next we show empirically that correlated noise is smaller than with existing techniques in the same setting
(
Sabater et al. 2022
;
Cyffers et al. 2022
;
Vithana et al. 2025
)
.

2.1.
Problem Statement

We consider a set of parties
P=[n]
. Each party
i\in P
has a private value
\mathsf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
where
\mathcal{X}
is a convex set in a vector space
\mathbb{X}
.
These parties want to collaboratively estimate
{\bar{\mathsf{x}}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in P}\mathsf{x}_{i}
while keeping each value
\mathsf{x}_{i}
(differentially) private.
While for simplicity of our explanation we average scalars, our approach can be easily extended to vectors (such as machine learning models or gradients).

2.2.
Differential Privacy

We evaluate the privacy of our protocols using the differential privacy framework (DP)
(
Dwork and Roth 2014
)
. This framework allows one to quantify how distinguishable is the output of a randomized algorithm on two
neighboring
datasets.
More formally, a dataset is a vector of elements of
\mathcal{X}
, we denote the space of all datasets by
\mathcal{X}^{*}
. Two datasets
\mathsf{x}^{(A)},\mathsf{x}^{(B)}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
are neighboring if there are
\mathsf{x}^{(0)}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
, permutation matrices
Q^{(A)}
and
Q^{(B)}
, and
u^{(A)},u^{(B)}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathsf{x}^{(A)}=Q^{(A)}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u^{(A)}\\
\mathsf{x}^{(0)}\end{array}\right]
and
\mathsf{x}^{(B)}=Q^{(B)}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u^{(B)}\\
\mathsf{x}^{(0)}\end{array}\right]
.

Definition 2.1.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta\in[0,1]
. A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{*}\to\mathcal{Y}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-
DP
if for any pair of neighboring datasets
D,D^{\prime}
and any subset
Y\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
of possible outputs
we have that

(1)

\Pr(\mathcal{A}(D)\in Y)\leq\exp(\varepsilon)\Pr(\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})\in Y)+\delta

where the probability is taken over the randomness of
\mathcal{A}
.

In our problem, we set
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
for the simplicity of our explanation. Then, a dataset is a vector
\mathsf{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in[0,1]^{n}
.
The output of
\mathcal{A}
is the set of all observations an adversary makes, which may exceed the intended input and could include intercepted messages or information obtained from corrupted parties. This output therefore depends on the threat model considered.
Consider a particular party
i\in P
.
One can consider two extreme cases. First, if the threat model allows for all parties
P\setminus\{i\}
to be corrupted, then the best
i
can do is to privatize
x_{i}
before starting anything, and the best utility which can be obtained for a given privacy level
(\varepsilon,\delta)
is the utility of Local DP
(
Duchi et al. 2013a
)
. Second, if the threat model makes more favorable assumptions, the best possible utility which can be reached for a given privacy level is the utility of Central DP, where there exist a trusted entity to whom all parties can send their messages and the adversary cannot see them until this entity releases his output.

Gaussian Mechanism

Our protocols use Gaussian noise to achieve differential privacy. Using the Gaussian Mechanism
(
Dwork et al. 2014
)
, it is possible to compute
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP estimations of averages with a mean squared error of
2\ln(1.25/\delta)/\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}
in the central model. In the local model this error is
n
times bigger.

2.3.
Threat Model

In our work, we assume all parties are
semi-honest
in that they follow the protocol as prescribed. A stochastic decentralized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that takes a dataset
D
produces a transcript
\mathcal{T}(D)=(y_{k})_{k=1}^{M}
of all
M
messages exchanged by the protocol, where
y_{k}=((i,j),v)\in(P\times P)\times\{0,1\}^{*}
means that party
i
sent value
v
to
j
. We assume that the adversary knows the tuple
\mathcal{W}=(p)_{(p,v)\in\mathcal{T}(D)}
of pairs sender-receiver of all of user interactions, e.g., as they may corrupt participants or track network traffic. We assume that parties communicate via secure channels, however, we consider that the adversary may succeed to break this security and observe a subset of messages
\mathcal{V}_{val}\subseteq\mathcal{T}(D)
, e.g., by intercepting messages, exploiting side channels or other attacks.

Definition 2.2 (Eavesdrop DP).

We say that a decentralized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
satisfies (
\varepsilon
,
\delta
,
\mathcal{V}_{val}
)-Eavesdrop DP (or (
\varepsilon
,
\delta
,
\mathcal{V}_{val}
)-E-DP) if it satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP for the output
\mathcal{A}(D)=(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{V}_{val})
.

Note that (
\varepsilon
,
\delta
,
\mathcal{T}(D)
)-E-DP (i.e. where all messages are known by the adversary) is equivalent to Local DP. We also assume an adversary that may corrupt some subset of parties
\mathcal{C}\subset P
. If a party
c\in\mathcal{C}
is corrupted, the adversary can learn both its private value
\mathsf{x}_{i}
and its incoming/outgoing messages. In that case, we denote by
P^{H}=P\setminus\mathcal{C}
to the set of
n_{H}=n-|\mathcal{C}|
honest parties that are not controlled by the adversary.

Definition 2.3 (Collussion DP).

We say that a decentralized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is (
\varepsilon
,
\delta
,
\mathcal{C}
)-Collussion DP (or (
\varepsilon
,
\delta
,
\mathcal{C}
)-C-DP) if it is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP for the output
\mathcal{A}(D^{H})=(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{V}_{val})
, where the input dataset
D^{H}
is the set of private values of honest parties
(\mathsf{x}_{i})_{i\in P^{H}}
and
\mathcal{V}_{val}=\{((i,j),v)\in\mathcal{T}(D):i\in\mathcal{C}\text{ or }j\in\mathcal{C}\}
.

We remark that parties executing algorithm
\mathcal{A}
do not have knowledge of the observed messages
\mathcal{V}_{val}
or corrupted parties
\mathcal{C}
.

The view of the adversary in E-DP and C-DP is similar to other correlated noise techniques such as the presented in
(
Sabater et al. 2022
;
Allouah et al. 2024
)
and in Secure Aggregation
(
Bell et al. 2020
)
, where the set of interactions
\mathcal{W}
is known. It is stronger than the view of the adversary in Network DP
(
Cyffers and Bellet 2022
)
which only knows
\mathcal{V}_{val}
and ignores
\mathcal{W}
. We remark that the knowledge of
\mathcal{W}
is a dangerous piece of information as it can be exploited to completely compromise privacy
(
Mrini et al. 2024
)
. Our adversary is also stronger than Pairwise Network DP
(
Cyffers et al. 2022
)
where
\mathcal{W}
is also unknown and the reported privacy loss of a party is the average over all possible placements of the adversary, whereas it corresponds to the worst-case in C-DP.

2.4.
Communication Model

We study decentralized protocols in the synchronous setting as defined in
(
Guerraoui and Rodrigues 2006
)
. In this setting, parties perform exchanges in
T
iterations and there exists a known finite time bound for a message to reach its destination.

At each iteration
t\in[T]
, all parties wake up and interact, among others sending a message to a set of neighbors. We first model this communication structure. For every iteration
t\in[T]
, let
E_{t}\subseteq P\times P
such that
(i,j)\in E_{t}
if and only if party
i
sends a message to
j
in iteration
t
. If
E_{t}=E_{1}
for all
t\in[T]
, we will say that our protocol has
static
exchanges, otherwise these are
dynamic
. For
i\in P
and
t\in[T]
, let
N^{(t)}_{i\leftarrow}=\{j\mid(j,i)\in E_{t}\}
and
N^{(t)}_{i\to}=\{j\mid(i,j)\in E_{t}\}
to be respectively the sets of incoming and outgoing neighbors of
i
.

2.5.
Failures

We will consider that, at each iteration, parties could be unexpectedly absent of the computation due to a temporary or permanent crash, disconnection from the network, or other problems. We call this behavior a
dropout
. We denote by
\mathcal{O}^{(t)}\in P
to the set of parties that did not drop out in the computation at iteration
t\in[T]
. We consider that a party
i\in P
dropped out
permanently
if he is not present in the last iteration (i.e.,
i\not\in\mathcal{O}^{(T)})
. We assume that messages have bounded delays
(
Guerraoui and Rodrigues 2006
)
and that there is sufficient time in an iteration that parties can confirm they received a message. Therefore, if a party
i
send a message to
j
in an iteration where
j
dropped out,
i
will detect it.

4.
Privacy Analysis

We now present our privacy results. In Section
4.1
we show how the adversary’s knowledge can be structured as a set of linear equations. Next, in Section
4.2
we present an abstract result that accurately accounts for the privacy loss of
IncA
. After that, we present in Section
4.3
interpretable results on the conditions to obtain differential privacy with accuracy comparable to that of Central DP when no dropouts occur. Finally, in Section
4.4
, we present sufficient positive and negative conditions on the graph that models parties interactions to obtain privacy.

4.1.
Knowledge of the Adversary

We prove Eavesdrop DP and Collussion DP guarantees. Hence, we assume that the adversary knows the set
\mathcal{W}=\{W_{1},\dots,W_{T}\}
and online activity
\mathcal{O}=(\mathcal{O}^{(t)})_{t\in[1,T]}
. For the subset of values of messages it knows, we will slightly abuse notation with respect to the definition of
\mathcal{V}_{val}
in E-DP and C-DP so that
\mathcal{V}_{val}\subseteq P\times[0,T]
denotes the subset of observed iteration-party messages.
Hence the messages
\{((i,t),y_{i}^{(t)})\}_{(i,t)\in\mathcal{V}_{val}}
will be known.

For E-DP, we assume that the adversary can observe the final messages of the
Mixing
Phase of all parties, i.e.,

\mathcal{V}_{val}\supseteq\{(i,T)\}_{i\in P}.

For
C-DP, they will additionally see all messages seen by corrupted parties. That is,

\mathcal{V}_{val}=\{(i,t)\in P\times[0,T-1]:(\{i\}\cup N^{(t+1)}_{i\to})\cap\mathcal{C}\not{=}\emptyset\}\cup\{(i,T)\}_{i\in P}.

Knowledge as Linear Equations

The knowledge of the adversary can be structured as a set of linear equations where the unknowns are private values
\mathsf{x}
and noise terms
(\eta^{\star},\eta)
. We show below how these equations are constructed.

Let
k_{H}\coloneq n_{H}T
be the number of canceling noise terms unknown by the adversary before they make any observations. We denote by
\mathsf{x}^{H}\coloneq(\mathsf{x}_{i})_{i\in P^{H}}\in\mathcal{X}^{n_{H}}
,

\eta^{\star H}\coloneq(\eta^{\star}_{i})_{i\in P^{H}}\in\mathbb{X}^{n}_{H}
and
\eta_{(:)}^{H}\coloneq(\eta_{i,:})_{i\in P^{H}}\in\mathbb{X}^{k_{H}}

the vectors of private values and noise terms of honest parties.
Then, from an execution
\mathcal{E}=(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{V}_{val})
of Algorithm
2
, the adversary can construct a set of linear equations described by

(10)

B(\mathsf{x}^{H}+\eta^{\star H})+A\eta_{(:)}^{H}=y_{\mathcal{V}}

where for
m\coloneq|\mathcal{V}_{val}|\leq n_{H}T
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n_{H}}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k_{H}}
are matrices with public coefficients such that
(B,A)
is a full rank,
y_{\mathcal{V}}\in\mathbb{X}^{m}

is the set of messages observed by the adversary after removing known constants and redundant information (e.g., linearly dependent equations), and
(\mathsf{x}^{H},\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})
are the unknowns. In Appendix
A.2
we show the details on how to construct the system of Equation (
10
).

Discussion

Let’s inspect the system of Equation (
10
). For each
i\in P^{H}
, variables
\mathsf{x}_{i}
and
\eta^{\star}_{i}
only appear coupled in the term
\mathsf{x}_{i}+\eta^{\star}_{i}
. Therefore, the adversary will never be able to isolate them and we can consider them as a single variable
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}_{i}=\mathsf{x}_{i}+\eta^{\star}_{i}
. In the worst case, the adversary observes all the messages of honest parties, meaning that
m=n_{H}+k_{H}
and that
(B,A)
is a square invertible matrix. Then, the adversary is able to recover
\tilde{\mathsf{x}}_{i}
for all
i\in P^{H}
by computing
(B,A)^{-1}y_{\mathcal{V}}
. In that case, the only way to protect private values is to set
\eta^{\star}_{i}
large enough, i.e. using the same noise required for Local DP. Hence, we emphasize the importance of setting
n_{H}+k_{H}>m
to prevent this situation, which justifies the large amount of noise terms (one per iteration) each party generates.

Comparison with Related Techniques

We compare our protocol with the GOPA
(
Sabater et al. 2022
)
and Cor-DP-DME
(
Vithana et al. 2025
)
, that are fully decentralized and provide similar MSE when there are no dropouts. These protocols follow the same base protocol. In a first round parties exchange pairwise canceling noise terms that they add to their private value to protect privacy. In a second round, they release the privatized noisy values, whose sum cancels the majority of the noise. In the case of dropouts, some of these terms remain uncanceled and increase the error. The more exchanges parties perform with each other, the smaller is the variance of correlated noise and the lower is the error under dropouts.
(
Vithana et al. 2025
)
is a particular case of GOPA when all parties communicate as much as possible to reduce the noise variance.
GOPA provides an additional dropout mitigation phase in the second round, where parties rollback their noise. However, if dropouts occur in this round, uncanceled terms remain.

Both experiments are done in 1000 runs. For Cor-DP-DME, we ignore the error induced by missing private values due to drop-out and only compute the error induced by the variance of noise terms when they are uncanceled, which is a lower bound on the real error and can be computed theoretically.

Discussion

Each protocol may experience a different number and distribution of dropouts.
In practice, this is influenced by factors such as the runtime complexity (which is discussed in more detail in
(
Gupta et al. 2016
;
Dutta et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2019
)
) and the ability of parties to rejoin the computation. The former depends on the number of iterations (
T
for
IncA
) and their duration. The duration of an iteration is influenced by the number of computations and messages (
k
for
IncA
).
IncA
allows dropped parties to rejoin the computation. Therefore, permanent dropouts do not necessarily increase with
T
, as longer runtime may increase the probability of a party to rejoin. GOPA requires only two iterations but substantially more messages per iteration than
IncA
and rejoining is only allowed until parties start the rollback phase.

6.
Related Work

Many distributed data processing tasks can be accomplished through local computations and subsequent aggregation of their outcomes across participating entities
(
Bharadwaj and Cormode 2024
;
McMahan et al. 2017
;
Kairouz et al. 2021b
;
Nicolas et al. 2024
;
Nicolas et al. 2025
;
Barczewski et al. 2025
)
. This makes averaging a simple, yet powerful primitive. Averages can be computed in a federated way
(
Bharadwaj and Cormode 2024
;
McMahan et al. 2017
)
or in a decentralized way using gossip protocols
(
Boyd et al. 2006
;
Berthier et al. 2020
;
Pittel 1987
;
Demers et al. 1987
)
. However, many early approaches required that parties share their data in the clear, which may be exploited by external attackers
(
Melis et al. 2019
;
Geiping et al. 2020
;
Mrini et al. 2024
;
Touat et al. 2024
;
Kariyappa et al. 2023
;
Pasquini et al. 2023
)
.

Differentially Private Mean Estimation

We study approaches that prevent information leakage by providing differential privacy guarantees
(
Dwork et al. 2006b
)
. Satisfying DP guarantees typically requires to perturb the computation with noise, which compromises accuracy. In the central model of DP (CDP), where a trusted curator performs the computation, one can achieve a mean squared error of
O(1/n^{2})
induced by privacy noise
(
Dwork et al. 2014
)
. If such a party is not available, parties could add noise before sharing their sensitive information, which further compromises accuracy. In the local model of DP (LDP)
(
Duchi et al. 2013b
;
Kasiviswanathan et al. 2011
;
Kairouz et al. 2014
;
Kairouz et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2020
)
, where parties fully privatize their data before using it in a collaborative computation,
the estimation error is a factor of
n
greater than in CDP and only yields acceptable accuracy when the number of parties is massive
(
Erlingsson et al. 2014
;
Ding et al. 2017
)
.

Approaches based on Cryptographic Primitives

Instead of a trusted curator, one can also use
cryptographic primitives such as Secure Aggregation
(
Bonawitz et al. 2017
;
Bell et al. 2020
;
Taiello et al. 2024
;
Bell et al. 2023
)
, other types of Multiparty Computation
(
Yao 1982
;
Damgård et al. 2013
)
or Shuffling
(
Ishai et al. 2006
)
. Using these techniques, one can recover similar privacy utility trade-offs of Central DP
(
Kairouz et al. 2021a
;
Agarwal et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Cheu et al. 2019
;
Balle et al. 2020
;
Ghazi et al. 2020
;
Heikkilä 2025
)
or even exactly match them
(
Jayaraman et al. 2018
;
Dwork et al. 2006a
;
Sabater et al. 2023
)
. Implementing secure shuffling or general purpose multiparty computation approaches such as

(
Damgård et al. 2013
)
requires a large communication cost or substantially relaxes assumptions by considering multiple non-colluding servers or trusted parties that anonymize messages by shuffling them. Notably, some secure aggregation protocols
(
Bell et al. 2020
;
So et al. 2021
)
require only
O(\log(n))
communication per party, however, they rely on structured interaction approaches and central coordination.

Fully Decentralized Approaches

Fully decentralized approaches can reduce the noise required to achieve DP by relaxing adversary models or using correlated noise techniques. Existing relaxation techniques consider Network DP
(
Cyffers and Bellet 2022
)
and Pairwise Network DP
(
Cyffers et al. 2022
;
Cyffers et al. 2024
)
. Both of these models substantially restrict the view of the adversary, making these models less applicable in practice (see our detailed discussion of Section
2.3
).
(
Cyffers and Bellet 2022
;
Cyffers et al. 2024
)
focus on decentralized stochastic gradient descent, while
(
Cyffers et al. 2022
)
is a protocol for private mean estimation. In Section
5.1
, we show that our approach outperforms
(
Cyffers et al. 2022
)
in accuracy despite considering a significantly stronger adversary. Correlated noise approaches
(
Imtiaz et al. 2019
;
Sabater et al. 2022
;
Allouah et al. 2024
;
Vithana et al. 2025
)
use pairwise canceling noise to protect privacy while keeping accuracy comparable to central DP when no dropouts occur. However, their accuracy degrades significantly if parties disconnect in the middle of the computation as pairwise noise does not cancel. This effect can be reduced up to some point with dropout mitigation techniques proposed by
(
Sabater et al. 2022
)
but do not entirely solve the problem, as more dropouts can also occur in the mitigation phase. In Section
5.2
we provide a detailed comparison with
(
Sabater et al. 2022
;
Vithana et al. 2025
)
. We do not compare with
(
Imtiaz et al. 2019
)
and
(
Allouah et al. 2024
)
since the former relies on Secure Aggregation, which was discussed previously, and the latter does not address the mean estimation task. Finally some correlated noise techniques also consider malicious adversaries who corrupt parties to actively deviate from the protocol to bias the computation
(
Sabater et al. 2022
;
Allouah et al. 2024
)
.

7.
Conclusion and Future Work

We propose
IncA
, a protocol for fully decentralized mean estimation, a key primitive for decentralized and privacy-preserving computation. We demonstrate that
IncA
not only satisfies theoretical privacy guarantees but also maintains accuracy despite party failures and collusion, all while incurring reasonable communication costs.
Directions of future work include the evaluation of our technique within larger systems such as the computation of federated learning models, the derivation of explicit bounds on the variance of correlated noise and the theoretical quantification of the probability that our conditions hold under random communication graphs.

\eta_{(:)}^{H}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h>\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h.

To bound the probability of the above to hold, we will use the tail bound

where

is the standard deviation of

. We set

(29)

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\left(\left(B,A\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\eta^{\star H}\\
\eta_{(:)}^{H}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h

(30)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h.

and start by computing
\sigma_{w}
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{w}^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{var}\left(\left(\left(B,A\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\eta^{\star H}\\
\eta_{(:)}^{H}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h\right)

\displaystyle=h^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\text{var}\left(\left(B,A\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\eta^{\star H}\\
\eta_{(:)}^{H}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)\Sigma^{-1}h

(31)

\displaystyle=h^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h.

By Equation (
28
), proving that

\frac{\sigma_{w}}{\gamma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(\frac{-\gamma^{2}}{2\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2}

implies our privacy guarantee. The above is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{w}}\exp\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right)\geq\frac{2}{\delta\sqrt{2\pi}}

and, applying logarithms to both sides, to

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{w}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{2}\geq\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta\sqrt{2\pi}}\right).

The above is implied if

(32)

\ln\left(\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{w}}\right)\geq 0

and

(33)

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{w}}\right)^{2}\geq\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta\sqrt{2\pi}}\right).

Below, we replace
\gamma
and
\sigma_{w}
using Equations (
30
) and (
31
). Then by noticing that Equation (
32
) is equivalent to
\gamma\geq\sigma_{w}
, we have that Equations (
32
) and (
33
) can be rewritten as

(34)

\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h>\sqrt{h^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h}

and

(35)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left(\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h\right)^{2}}{h^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h}\geq\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)

respectively. Finally, using Lemma
A.1
, equations (
34
) and (
35
) are implied for

h^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}h\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{c^{2}}.

where
c^{2}>2\ln(1.25/\delta)
.
∎

Let
B
,
A

2

Lemma

4.3
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
.
Let
\mathcal{E}=(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{V}_{val})
be defined as in Theorem
12
. For a pair of neighboring datasets
\mathsf{x}^{(A)},\mathsf{x}^{(B)}\in\mathbb{X}^{n_{H}}
, let
\xi^{\star}\in\mathbb{X}^{n_{H}}
and
\Delta_{(:)}\in\mathbb{X}^{k_{H}}
be such that

\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{E}}\left(\mathsf{x}^{(A)}+\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H}\right)=\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{E}}\left(\mathsf{x}^{(B)}+\eta^{\star H}+\xi^{\star},\eta_{(:)}^{H}+\Delta_{(:)}\right)

for any
\eta^{\star H}
,
\eta_{(:)}^{H}
. Recall that
\Sigma_{\eta}
is the covariance matrix of
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\eta^{\star H}\\
\eta_{(:)}^{H}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Then, execution
\mathcal{E}
is (
\varepsilon
,
\delta
,
\mathcal{V}_{val}
)-E-DP (or (
\varepsilon
,
\delta
,
\mathcal{C}
)-C-DP) if
for all such neighboring datasets
\mathsf{x}^{(A)}
and
\mathsf{x}^{(B)}

\left((\xi^{\star})^{\top},\Delta_{(:)}^{\top}\right)\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{\star}\\
\Delta_{(:)}\\
\end{pmatrix}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{c^{2}}

where

c^{2}>2\ln(1.25/\delta)
.

Proof.

It follows directly from
(
Sabater et al. 2022
, Theorem 1)
. We adapt the proof to our setting.
Let
\mathsf{x}^{(A)}
and
\mathsf{x}^{(B)}
be two neighboring datasets. Let
y_{\mathcal{V}}
be the view of the adversary after execution
\mathcal{E}
. Let

S^{(A)}=\{(\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})\in\mathbb{X}^{n_{H}+k_{H}}:\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{E}}\left(\mathsf{x}^{(A)}+\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H}\right)=y_{\mathcal{V}}\}

and analogously

S^{(B)}=\{(\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})\in\mathbb{X}^{n_{H}+k_{H}}:\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{E}}\left(\mathsf{x}^{(B)}+\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H}\right)=y_{\mathcal{V}}\}.

Let
t=(\xi^{\star},\Delta_{(:)})
. The precondition of the lemma implies that
S^{(A)}+t=S^{(B)}
. Our
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy guarantee given by

\Pr(y_{\mathcal{V}}|\mathsf{x}^{(A)})\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr(y_{\mathcal{V}}|\mathsf{x}^{(B)})+\delta

is implied if

(37)

\Pr((\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H}))\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr((\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})+t)+\delta

Indeed if Equation (
37
) holds we have

\displaystyle\Pr(y_{\mathcal{V}}|\mathsf{x}^{(A)})

\displaystyle=\int_{(\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})\in S^{(A)}}\Pr((\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H}))\mathrm{d}\eta^{\star H}\mathrm{d}\eta_{(:)}^{H}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})\in S^{(A)}}(e^{\varepsilon}\Pr((\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})+t)+\delta)\mathrm{d}\eta^{\star H}\mathrm{d}\eta_{(:)}^{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{(\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})-t\in S^{(A)}}(e^{\varepsilon}\Pr((\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H}))+\delta)\mathrm{d}\eta^{\star H}\mathrm{d}\eta_{(:)}^{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{(\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})\in S^{(B)}}(e^{\varepsilon}\Pr((\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H}))+\delta)\mathrm{d}\eta^{\star H}\mathrm{d}\eta_{(:)}^{H}

\displaystyle=e^{\varepsilon}\Pr(y_{\mathcal{V}}|\mathsf{x}^{(B)})+\delta.

Therefore, it suffices to prove Equation (
37
) to prove our
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantee.

We will do that by proving that
\Pr((\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H}))\leq e^{\varepsilon}P((\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})+t)
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Denoting
\gamma=(\eta^{\star H},\eta_{(:)}^{H})
for convenience, we need to prove that with
probability
1-\delta
it holds that

|\log(P(\gamma)/P(\gamma+t))|\leq\varepsilon.

We have that

\displaystyle\Big|\log\frac{P(\gamma)}{P(\gamma+t)}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}\gamma+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma+t)^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}(\gamma+t)\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{2}(2\gamma+t)^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t\Big|.

To ensure that
|\log(P(\gamma)/P(\gamma+t))|\leq e^{\varepsilon}
holds with probability at least
1-\delta
, since we are interested in the absolute value, we show that

P\Big(\frac{1}{2}(2\gamma+t)^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t\geq\varepsilon\Big)\leq\delta/2,

the proof of the other direction is analogous.
This is equivalent to

(38)

P(\gamma\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t\geq\varepsilon-t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t/2)\leq\delta/2.

The variance of
\gamma\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t
is

\displaystyle\text{var}(\gamma\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-\top}\text{var}(\gamma)\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-\top}\Sigma_{\eta}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t.

For any centered Gaussian random variable
Y
with variance

\sigma_{Y}^{2}
, we have that

(39)

P(Y\geq\lambda)\leq\frac{\sigma_{Y}}{\lambda\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\lambda^{2}/2\sigma_{Y}^{2}\right).

Let
Y=\gamma\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t
,
\sigma_{Y}^{2}=t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t
and
\lambda=\varepsilon-t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t/2
, then satisfying

(40)

\frac{\sigma_{Y}}{\lambda\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\lambda^{2}/2\sigma_{Y}^{2}\right)\leq\delta/2

implies Equation (
38
).
Equation (
40
) is equivalent to

\frac{\lambda}{\sigma_{Y}}\exp\left(\lambda^{2}/2\sigma_{Y}^{2}\right)\geq 2/\delta\sqrt{2\pi},

or, after taking logarithms on both sides, to

\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sigma_{Y}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sigma_{Y}}\right)^{2}\geq\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta\sqrt{2\pi}}\right).

To make this inequality hold, we require

(41)

\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sigma_{Y}}\right)\geq 0

and

(42)

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sigma_{Y}}\right)^{2}\geq\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta\sqrt{2\pi}}\right).

Equation (
41
) is equivalent to

\lambda\geq\sigma_{Y}
.
Substituting
\lambda
and
\sigma_{Y}
we get

(43)

\varepsilon-t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t/2\geq(t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t)^{1/2}.

Substituting
\lambda
and
\sigma_{Y}
in Equation (
42
)
gives

(44)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t)^{2}}{t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t}\geq\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)

By Lemma
A.1
, equations (
43
) and (
44
) are satisfied for

t^{\top}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}t\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{c^{2}}
for
c^{2}=2\ln(1.25/\delta)
.

∎

\displaystyle=W_{t+1;j,i}^{\mathcal{O}}\frac{w_{i}\beta}{1-W_{t+1;i,i}^{\mathcal{O}}}+w_{j}(\tilde{\mathsf{x}}_{j}^{\prime}-\tilde{\mathsf{x}}_{j}).

Running Algorithm
