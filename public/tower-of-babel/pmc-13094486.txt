Divergent Patient-Reported Outcomes to Periodontal Therapy: A Prospective Study of Gingivitis Subtypes
Authors: Guifen Cao, Xuan Ying, Xiaojun Li, Yue Dai, Yanli Huang, Guannan Bai, Xia Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsSubtype-specific differences in patient-reported outcomes (PROs) following periodontal therapy for gingivitis remain poorly defined. This study compared changes over time in clinical parameters, oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), and dental anxiety (DA) following periodontal therapy among individuals with localized gingivitis (LG), generalized gingivitis (GG), and periodontally healthy controls (PH).Materials and MethodsNinety participants (30 per group) underwent scaling, polishing, and oral hygiene instruction. Clinical parameters (bleeding on probing [BOP], gingival index [GI], plaque index [PLI]) and patient-reported outcomes (Oral Health Impact Profile-14 [OHIP-14] and Corah's Dental Anxiety Scale [C-DAS]) were recorded at baseline (t0), 1 month (t1), and upon achieving periodontal health (t2; BOP < 10%).ResultsAll groups showed significant improvements in GI and PLI (P < .05). BOP decreased significantly in LG and GG (both P < .05), but not in PH. PROs exhibited distinct, subtype-specific patterns: OHRQoL (OHIP-14) improved significantly in GG only at t2 (P < .05), whereas the LG improved at both t1 and t2 (all P < .05). In addition, dental anxiety (C-DAS) decreased significantly in the GG at both t1 and t2 (P < .05).ConclusionPeriodontal therapy provides dual clinical and psychological benefits, with the pattern of improvement in OHRQoL and reduction in dental anxiety contingent on gingivitis subtype.The registration number: ChiCTR2300076378.

Introduction

Plaque-induced gingivitis is a reversible inflammation limited to the gingival tissues, initiated by bacterial biofilm accumulation and clinically manifesting as erythema, oedema, and bleeding on probing without attachment loss.1,2 As a highly prevalent global disease3 and the clinically recognized precursor stage of periodontitis,4 its control constitutes a fundamental preventive strategy against periodontal breakdown.5 According to the 2018 classification of periodontal diseases,2,6 periodontal health is defined as <10% bleeding on probing (BOP)-positive sites. Plaque-induced gingivitis is subsequently stratified as either localized gingivitis (10%-30% BOP-positive sites) or generalized gingivitis (>30% BOP-positive sites). This classification system provides objective thresholds for clinical assessment and therapeutic targeting. While existing evidence confirms that professional mechanical plaque removal (PMPR) combined with oral hygiene instruction (OHI) is effective in treating gingivitis, therapeutic responses vary significantly across different subtypes of gingivitis severity.7,8 Notably, current research has paid insufficient attention to how periodontal therapy impacts patient-reported outcomes (PROs) − such as oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) and dental anxiety − in cohorts with varying severity of gingivitis.9,10 This gap hinders the development of stratified protocols that could optimize both clinical efficacy and patient-centred outcomes.

Oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), quantifiable via validated instruments like the OHIP-14,11 reflects the functional, psychological, and social impacts of oral disease. While non-surgical periodontal treatment (NSPT) improves OHRQoL in periodontitis,12 its effects in gingivitis-specific cohorts remain underexplored, particularly regarding disease severity stratification. Compared to localized disease, generalized gingivitis demonstrates inferior treatment responsiveness, reflected in lower clinical resolution rates.8 It is also associated with a greater impairment of quality of life, likely attributable to its persistent inflammatory burden.8,9,13 Crucially, no prospective studies have compared OHRQoL trajectories between generalized/localized gingivitis and periodontally healthy cohorts following standardized therapy − a significant knowledge gap given the prognostic implications of gingivitis subtype.

Dental anxiety (DA), an irrational fear driving treatment avoidance, is inversely related to OHRQoL; that is, higher anxiety correlates with poorer quality of life.14, 15, 16 NSPT may mitigate DA by resolving inflammation and functional deficits.17,18 Studies in periodontitis patients have consistently demonstrated concurrent improvements in DA and OHRQoL following active treatment.12,14 In contrast, the relationship between DA and gingivitis is more complex and less clearly defined. Emerging evidence suggests that the benefits of therapy may vary according to gingivitis subtype: improvements in self-efficacy following treatment appear greater in localized rather than generalized forms of gingivitis,8 while inflammatory reduction may be particularly effective in reducing DA among high-anxiety subgroups.17 Nevertheless, DA outcomes across different gingivitis subtypes have not been well characterized, impeding the development of tailored, psychologically informed care.

Prospective, controlled analyses of psychosocial outcomes following gingivitis therapy, particularly comparing subtypes to healthy controls, are lacking. This gap hinders stratified management based on psychological risk and obscures whether OHRQoL/DA improvements are treatment-specific or placebo-driven. To address this, we conducted a prospective controlled study comparing OHIP-14 and DA scores in participants with generalized/localized gingivitis and periodontally healthy individuals before and after NSPT. This study evaluated whether NSPT improves OHRQoL/DA in gingivitis and whether effects differ by subtype (generalized/localized).

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-centre, prospective, non-randomized, interventional cohort study enrolled systemically healthy individuals and categorized them into three groups based on baseline periodontal status according to the 2018 classification of periodontal diseases2,6: periodontal health (PH, BOP < 10%), localized gingivitis (LG, BOP 10%-30%) and generalized gingivitis (GG, BOP > 30%).

A cornerstone of the design was that all participants received the same standardized non-surgical periodontal therapy to eliminate treatment as a confounding variable. Therapy was individually titrated, requiring 1 to 4 sessions to achieve periodontal health − a range consistent with our published data.8

Consequently, the first follow-up (t1) was scheduled at a fixed 1-month interval for all participants. This study was implemented within the Department of Periodontology, Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Ethical clearance was obtained from the institution’s Stomatology Research Ethics Committee (Hangzhou, China; Approval No. 2020-44), and the study protocol was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration ID: ChiCTR2300076378).

Participants

Participants meeting eligibility requirements were enrolled between July 2021 and March 2025 following advertisement-based recruitment. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) age 18 to 70 years; (2) absence of major systemic conditions, confirming good general health; (3) having either periodontal health (BOP < 10%), localized gingivitis (BOP 10%-30%), or generalized gingivitis (BOP > 30%)2,6; (4) ≥20 natural teeth present.

Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) pregnancy or lactation; (2) current use of anti-inflammatory drugs or antibiotic therapy (topical/systemic) within 30 days prior to enrolment; (3) active tobacco use; (4) ongoing orthodontic treatment; (5) periodontal treatment within the preceding 6 months; (6) Urgent dental conditions requiring immediate care (eg, acute abscesses, pulpitis); (7) Extensive non-urgent conditions that could confound the assessment of patient-reported outcomes (eg, widespread untreated caries, extensive prosthetic needs); and (8) clinical attachment loss (CAL) ≥1 mm at any site (assessed at six points per tooth).

Interventions

A calibrated periodontal specialist, blinded to participant’s group identity, performed full-mouth ultrasonic debridement followed by prophylaxis paste application using a rubber cup on low-speed handpiece. Standardized oral hygiene instruction (OHI) comprised: (1) digital education via Bass technique/interdental cleaning video, (2) supervised hands-on demonstration on typodont models, and (3) personalized feedback using plaque disclosure (Plaque Checker, Ci Medical CO. LTD, Japan). Periodontal health was strictly defined as BOP < 10% at 1-month re-evaluation. Non-responders underwent identical intervention/OHI cycles with monthly reassessments until endpoint attainment (Figure 1). Anti-gingivitis agents (therapeutic toothpastes/mouthwashes) were prohibited throughout the study period.

Periodontal variables

A single calibrated examiner (intra-examiner ICC = 0.976) performed all periodontal assessments. While the baseline examination (t0) was necessarily unblinded to establish the group diagnosis, the examiner was blinded to participants’ group identity during all follow-up assessments (t1 and t2). Full-mouth recordings were performed at six sites per tooth (mesiobuccal, midbuccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, midlingual, and distolingual) using a Williams periodontal probe (Hu-Friedy). Plaque visualization preceded scoring via dual-tone disclosing solution (Plaque Checker, Ci Medical CO. LTD). Standardized evaluations were performed at baseline and monthly thereafter until periodontal health was attained.

Bleeding on probing (BOP)2,19 was recorded as the percentage of sites exhibiting bleeding within 15 seconds after gentle probing. BOP% was calculated as (number of bleeding sites / total number of sites) × 100.

Gingival index (GI) (Löe and Silness)20 was scored as: 0 = normal; 1 = mild inflammation (colour change, oedema, no bleeding on probing); 2 = moderate inflammation (redness, edema, bleeding on probing); 3 = severe inflammation (marked redness and oedema, tendency to spontaneous bleeding).

Plaque index (PLI) was assessed using the Turesky modification of the Quigley-Hein plaque index,21 scored on a 0–5 scale: 0 = no plaque; 1 = separate flecks of plaque at the gingival margin; 2 = a thin continuous band of plaque (≤1 mm) at the gingival margin; 3 = plaque covering more than 1 mm but less than one-third of the tooth surface; 4 = plaque covering at least one-third but less than two-thirds of the tooth surface; 5 = plaque covering two-thirds or more of the tooth surface.

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs)

Participants completed the Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14)22,23 and the Corah Dental Anxiety Scale (C-DAS)24,25 questionnaires at baseline (t0) and follow-up visits (t1 and t2).

OHIP-1422,23 measures oral health-related quality of life across seven domains (Functional Limitation, Physical Pain, Psychological Discomfort, Physical Disability, Psychological Disability, Social Disability, Handicap), with two items per domain. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never, 1 = hardly ever, 2 = occasionally, 3 = fairly often, 4 = very often). The total score ranges from 0 to 56, with higher scores indicating greater oral health impact.

C-DAS consists of four items assessing dental anxiety. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = relaxed to 5 = extremely anxious). Total scores range from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating higher dental anxiety levels.24,25

As no formally validated Chinese version of the C-DAS was available, the original English version was translated following standard forward-backward procedures. The translated version was reviewed by a panel of bilingual dental experts to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence.

In the present sample, the Chinese C-DAS demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.88). Exploratory factor analysis revealed a single factor (eigenvalue = 2.92) explaining 72.9% of the variance, with all item loadings > 0.80. Sampling adequacy was confirmed (KMO = 0.72; Bartlett’s test: χ² = 222.00, df = 6, P < .001).

Sample size

An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1) based on data from a pilot study involving 10 participants per group. Sample size estimates were calculated separately for the two primary outcomes − OHIP-14 and C-DAS − using the paired t-test module, which provides a conservative approximation for the planned Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. The analysis was conducted with a two-sided α level of 0.05 and 80% power, based on the change from baseline to periodontal health achievement (Δt0–t2).

For OHIP-14, the mean change in the LG group was 1.5 ± 2.3 (Cohen’s d = 0.65), requiring 21 participants; the corresponding values in the GG group were 4.5 ± 6.3 (Cohen’s d = 0.71), requiring 18 participants. For C-DAS, the mean change in the LG group was 0.9 ± 1.52 (Cohen’s d= 0.59; n = 25 required), whereas the GG group showed a mean change of 2.1 ± 2.0 (Cohen’s d = 1.05; n = 10 required).

To ensure statistical power for all analyses, the larger estimate (n = 25/group) was adopted. Allowing for a 20% dropout rate, 30 participants were recruited per group.

Statistical analyses

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using parametric tests; non-normally distributed variables are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using non-parametric tests. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages and were analyzed using the chi-square test.

For baseline characteristics and clinical parameters (BOP, GI, PLI) at all timepoints, intergroup comparisons among the three groups (PH, LG, GG) were performed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate, followed by post-hoc tests (Tukey’s HSD for ANOVA; Bonferroni-corrected Mann–Whitney U tests for Kruskal–Wallis). The Hodges–Lehmann estimator was used to quantify median differences where appropriate. Intra-group changes from baseline to t1 and t2 were evaluated using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

Between-group differences in patient-reported outcomes at t1 and t2 were analyzed using generalized linear models with log link. All models included Group (PH, LG, GG) as a fixed factor and were adjusted for Baseline BOP%, Baseline Total Score of the respective outcome, and Time to Health (for t2 models only). Time to Health was defined as the number of monthly treatment cycles required to first achieve BOP < 10% (set to 0 for the PH group, and 1–4 for participants with gingivitis based on their actual recovery time). For OHIP-14, a Tweedie distribution was applied; for C-DAS, a Gamma distribution. Huber-White robust standard errors were used. Results are presented as exponentiated coefficients (Exp[β]) with 95% confidence intervals, Wald χ² statistics, and exact P-values. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction.

Intra-group changes in patient-reported outcomes from baseline to t1 and t2 were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with effect sizes reported as matched-pair rank-biserial correlation coefficients (r) and 95% confidence intervals estimated via nonparametric bootstrapping (10,000 resamples). Associations between oral health–related quality of life and dental anxiety were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation.

All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Clinical periodontal findings

At baseline (t0), significant inter-group differences in clinical parameters were observed (Figure 2; detailed data in Table S1). From baseline to t1 and t2, significant improvements in GI and PLI were observed in all groups (all P < .05). For BOP, the LG and GG groups showed significant improvements (both P < .05), whereas the PH group did not (P = .089). The magnitude of improvement differed substantially between the gingivitis subtypes: the LG and GG groups exhibited marked reductions (eg, BOP decreased by 11.8-31.8 percentage points). Notably, the GG group achieved significantly greater reductions in BOP and GI compared to the LG group (inter-group P < .05 for Δt0-t1 and Δt0-t2). At t1, mean BOP fell below 10% in the LG group, meeting the health criterion, whereas the GG group remained above this threshold. By t2, all participants had attained periodontal health according to study criteria. Patient-reported outcomes

At baseline, the three groups were comparable in both OHIP-14 and C-DAS scores (Table 1). Baseline scores were relatively low, with median OHIP-14 scores ranging from 5.5 to 9.5 (out of a possible 56), indicating limited oral health impact at study entry. Generalized linear models revealed no significant overall effect of group on OHIP-14 scores at t1 (Wald χ² = 0.89, df = 2, P = .640) or t2 (Wald χ² = 0.49, df = 2, P = .782), nor on C-DAS scores at either timepoint (t1: Wald χ² = 0.34, df = 2, P = .844; t2: Wald χ² = 0.32, df = 2, P = .852). Detailed pairwise comparisons, including uncorrected and Bonferroni-corrected P-values, are presented in Table 2. The only exceptions were two OHIP-14 subdomains: at t1, the GG group reported higher Physical Pain scores than the LG group (Exp(β) = 2.23, 95% CI [1.37, 3.63], corrected P = .003); at t2, the GG group showed higher Functional Limitations scores than the LG group (Exp(β) = 6.17, 95% CI [1.63, 23.33], corrected P = .021). No significant differences were observed for other subdomains (all corrected P > .05; detailed results in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). The specific effects of the covariates (Baseline BOP%, Baseline Total Score, and Time to Health) included in these models are detailed in Table S2. Notably, significant within-group improvements from baseline were observed for both outcomes within the gingivitis cohort, while no such changes occurred in the PH group. The following sections detail these within-group trajectories for each gingivitis subtype, presented separately for OHIP-14 and C-DAS.

Oral health impact profile-14 (OHIP-14)

Distinct within-group trajectories were observed between the two gingivitis subtypes (detailed data in Table 3). Discussion

Despite the well-established utility of patient-reported outcomes (PROs) in evaluating periodontal care,12,13 direct comparative evidence on differential psychosocial responses across gingivitis subtypes remains sparse.8 To address this, our prospective cohort study was conducted with the primary aim of directly comparing these outcomes among localized gingivitis, generalized gingivitis, and periodontally healthy controls, following an initial confirmation of improvement in the combined gingivitis cohort. Our findings confirm that non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) significantly improved both oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL, measured by OHIP-14) and dental anxiety (DA, measured by C-DAS) in the overall gingivitis cohort − consistent with prior evidence.13 However, the subtype-level analysis revealed a critical nuance: while OHRQoL improved in both gingivitis subtypes, a significant reduction in DA was exclusive to the generalized subtype. This subtype-selective pattern, rather than the magnitude of change per se, represents the primary contribution of this study, highlighting a previously unreported dimension of psychosocial response heterogeneity and refining the personalized outlook of gingivitis management.

The improvement in OHRQoL common to both subtypes merits closer examination. In our study, significant gains in OHIP-14 scores were observed exclusively in participants with gingival inflammation but not observed in PH controls. This finding aligns with the established model linking OHRQoL improvements to the resolution of clinical inflammation.13 The consistency of this response across localized and generalized disease suggests that enhanced patient-perceived oral health represents a core, universal outcome of successful inflammatory control in gingivitis. It should be noted, however, that the absolute improvements were modest, with median scores decreasing by 1.5 points in the LG group and 2.5 points in the GG group (from baseline medians of 5.5 and 9.5, respectively, on a 0-56 scale). These modest changes are likely attributable to the relatively low baseline scores in this young, healthy cohort, which left limited room for dramatic improvement (a ceiling effect).

The association between inflammatory control and OHRQoL is well-documented in periodontitis, where clinical improvements correlate with enhanced oral health-related quality of life.26 Our findings extend this principle to the gingivitis population. This link is further reinforced by evidence from systemically compromised populations (eg, adolescents with type 1 diabetes), where gingival health exerts bidirectional influences on both oral and general well-being,27 and by public health interventions (eg, the Brazilian School Health Program) reporting positive OHRQoL outcomes in young cohorts.28 Together, these lines of evidence underscore that improving OHRQoL in gingivitis is fundamentally tied to the achievement of clinical gingival health, through both individual clinical therapy and population-level public health measures.

Notably, our current results build upon and clarify earlier work from our group.8 The present analysis elucidates the temporal pattern of OHRQoL improvement: whereas localized gingivitis showed improvement at both intermediate (t1) and final (t2) assessments, generalized gingivitis exhibited significant OHRQoL gains only at t2 − coinciding with the achievement of clinical periodontal health. This pattern underscores that the full magnitude of OHRQoL improvement in generalized gingivitis is intrinsically tied to the complete resolution of inflammation, highlighting the need for sustained, effective therapy in patients with more extensive gingivitis to realize meaningful quality-of-life benefits.

Further analysis of OHIP-14 subdomains revealed distinct patterns of improvement between gingivitis subtypes. In the LG group, significant gains were predominantly observed in the Physical Pain domain, aligning with the clinical improvement of gingival inflammation and bleeding.8 This domain’s responsiveness to inflammation reduction is well-documented, as pain perception directly correlates with inflammatory mediator levels (eg, PGE₂, IL-1β) in gingival crevicular fluid.29, 30, 31, 32 Conversely, the GG group at t2 showed significant improvements across all subdomains except Functional Limitations. The minimal baseline impact of gingivitis itself on this domain likely explains the absence of significant changes following therapy.

The finding that GG patients reported greater functional limitations and pain even after achieving clinical health suggests that resolution of clinical inflammation does not immediately equate to full restoration of patient-perceived well-being. This observation aligns with recent evidence showing that patients with more severe periodontal conditions continue to report higher functional limitation and physical pain domain scores despite clinical improvement following nonsurgical therapy.33 The present findings extend this concept to gingivitis, demonstrating that functional and sensory recovery may lag behind clinical healing, particularly in patients with more extensive initial disease. This dissociation between clinical and patient-reported outcomes underscores the importance of extending outcome assessment beyond traditional clinical endpoints to capture the full spectrum of patient recovery.

The significant gains observed across multiple domains in the GG group − including Physical Pain, Psychological Discomfort, Physical Disability, Psychological Disability, Social Disability, and Handicap − further underscore the broad spectrum of OHRQoL impairments associated with generalized gingivitis, encompassing not only localized symptoms but also psychosocial and adaptive functions. The variation in improvement across subdomains highlights that OHRQoL restoration in gingivitis is multidimensional; for the GG subtype, symptomatic and functional recovery may require extended observation or adjunctive therapies, a finding that should be considered preliminary given the modest effect sizes.

The present study demonstrated no statistically significant differences in baseline C-DAS scores among the PH, LG and GG groups. This pattern differs from previous studies34,35 conducted in periodontitis populations, where higher levels of dental anxiety have been consistently associated with greater disease severity. The absence of intergroup differences in pre-treatment dental anxiety (DA) may be explained by the comparatively low inflammatory burden associated with gingivitis.13,35,36 Even in generalized cases, gingival inflammation may remain below a threshold necessary to elicit significantly higher levels of anxiety relative to periodontally healthy individuals. Consequently, patients with gingivitis may perceive the urgency and perceived threat of dental treatment similarly to those with healthy periodontal tissues, resulting in comparable C-DAS scores across groups.

The observed differential effects of non-surgical therapy on dental anxiety (DA) support the extrapolation of psychological benefits from periodontitis to generalized gingivitis. Participants with GG showed significant reductions in DA at both post-treatment and periodontal health attainment timepoints, particularly in the ‘Waiting Room’ and ‘Scale and Polish’ domains of the C-DAS. This aligns with reports in periodontitis,12,17 where non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) also reduced anxiety, likely due to shared features such as inflammatory burden, treatment procedures, and subsequent clinical improvement. By contrast, participants with LG exhibited no significant changes in DA, possibly due to subclinical baseline anxiety levels insufficient for measurable desensitization. Consistent with previous findings,8 these results suggest that improvements in self-efficacy are more pronounced in LG than in GG. Together, these findings underscore that DA response is modulated by both inflammatory extent and post-treatment recovery, positioning GG as a key target for integrated psychological and periodontal management. The absolute reduction in C-DAS scores in the GG group was modest (median decrease from 8.5 to 7.5 on a 4-20 scale), suggesting that while the effect is statistically significant and subtype-specific, its clinical significance warrants further investigation in larger, more diverse populations.

Baseline analysis revealed weak but significant correlations between scaling/polishing-specific anxiety and OHRQoL impairments, particularly in OHIP-14 total score, Physical Pain, and Psychological Discomfort domains. This suggests that, even in gingivitis patients, domain-specific dental anxiety is tangibly associated with reduced oral health-related quality of life. Although the correlations were modest, they align with established literature12,37 indicating that dental anxiety contributes to avoidance behaviors and perceptual amplification of oral symptoms, which may in turn exacerbate quality-of-life burdens. The association with pain and discomfort domains underscores how anticipatory anxiety related to routine procedures − such as scaling and polishing − may heighten perceived discomfort both during and beyond dental visits. These findings reinforce the importance of recognizing and addressing procedure-specific anxiety early in gingivitis management, as it may play a role in shaping patients’ overall oral health experience and compliance with care.

Several study limitations warrant consideration when interpreting the findings. First, the exclusion of smokers and individuals with systemic diseases limits applicability to these clinically relevant subpopulations. This concern is underscored by recent evidence demonstrating that both cigarette smoking and waterpipe use are associated with significantly poorer peri-implant clinical outcomes,38 highlighting the need to investigate the impact of these habits on patient-reported outcomes in future studies. Second, the single-centre recruitment strategy may restrict generalizability to broader demographic and socioeconomic groups; future multi-centre studies involving more diverse cohorts, including medically compromised patients and smokers, are warranted to validate these findings. Additionally, the relatively short-term follow-up (1-4 months) – while sufficient to assess initial treatment responses – precludes evaluation of long-term outcomes, such as the sustainability of anxiety reduction or OHRQoL improvements beyond the active treatment phase. Extended observational periods are warranted to determine the durability of these benefits.39 Finally, the modest effect sizes observed in this young, healthy cohort with low baseline scores should be considered when interpreting the clinical relevance of the findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that non-surgical periodontal therapy yields measurable improvements in both clinical and patient-reported outcomes, with the pattern of improvement differing between gingivitis subtypes. While OHRQoL improved in both localized and generalized gingivitis − with modest but significant changes − a reduction in dental anxiety was specific to the generalized subtype. These differential response patterns represent the study’s primary contribution, highlighting the potential for personalized care that integrates patient-centred outcomes with traditional clinical endpoints. For patients with generalized gingivitis, who experienced both improved quality of life and reduced dental anxiety, integrated psychological support may further enhance treatment success. Future research should validate these findings in populations with greater baseline PRO impairment and evaluate the long-term sustainability of these psychosocial benefits.
