Geochemical tales of individual lives in the industrial revolution: Untangling the impact of pollutant exposure in two English towns
Authors: Sara A. McGuire, Mark Hubbe, William J. Pestle, Arash Sharifi, Ali Pourmand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
England’s Industrial Revolution transformed natural environments. However, studies of this period are often limited to broad generalizations that neglect individuals’ lived experiences. Here, we explore industrialization’s impact at the individual scale by analyzing skeletal concentrations of pollutants (arsenic, barium, and lead) and evaluating biogenic versus diagenetic contributions. Analysis of the skeletal remains of 94 individuals from two 18th/19th century English towns, South Shields and Barton-upon-Humber, showed biogenic exposure to contaminants and variation in exposure by environment and biosocial identity. South Shields individuals showed higher concentrations of arsenic (P < 0.001) and barium (P = 0.013), while Barton-upon-Humber individuals showed higher concentrations of lead (P = 0.020). Skeletal lead isotope ratios match geologic samples from throughout the United Kingdom, suggesting a wide network of anthropogenic lead consistent with widespread industrial activities. These findings reveal a spectrum of realities and lived experiences hidden between the dichotomy of rural versus urban industrial England.

INTRODUCTION

England’s Industrial Revolution (1760 to 1850 CE) saw the development of new manufacturing practices and technologies that transformed the production of goods and established industrial centers throughout the country (1–3). Industrial innovation resulted in the creation of jobs, lowered the cost of goods, and enabled global product distribution. However, industrial processes went unregulated in England for more than 100 years, introducing new and increased quantities of pollutants into the environment and creating hazardous occupational conditions for England’s growing working-class proletariat (4–13). Here, we ask how far-reaching the effects of industrial pollutant exposure were and if any populations were spared their impact.

While there has been copious research into the impact of industrialization, these studies often focus on specific regions, industries, or populations [e.g., (2, 3)], economic or demographic changes [e.g., (14, 15)], the development of the factory system [e.g., (16)], and the causes of an industrial revolution in England [e.g., (1, 3, 17)], creating generalizations of life experiences that are inherently incomplete. Furthermore, historical documentation and more recent analyses of industrialization frequently stress a dichotomy of the insalubrious smog-filled industrial city versus the clean and idyllic countryside [e.g., (11, 18, 19) and as described in (20)] or assume a continuum that is not explicitly defined [e.g., (21)] [note that this is not to imply that rural populations were immune to poor health or that urban residents were without health care resources; rather, the polluted, overcrowded, and miasmic conditions in urban and industrialized areas facilitated more numerous and diverse adverse health outcomes. Increased stature was found among the more urban population studied here compared to the more rural—a phenomenon likely attributed to increased societal resources such as doctors and apothecaries within the varied urban environment (22, 23)]. This approach fails to recognize the existence of a spectrum of realities throughout England that occurred in between this dichotomy and that contained invisible vulnerabilities for the populations that lived between these extremes. Furthermore, this binary focus is limited in scope and fails to recognize the wide-reaching effects of industrialization and the consequences of exposure to unregulated hazardous pollutants produced by industry during this time. Consequently, this discussion often overlooks, and thus erases, the life experiences of individuals who lived through this age of transformation.

The combination of geochemistry and bioarchaeology provides a distinct perspective to examine the physiological impact and life experiences of individuals living through documented historic changes and events [e.g., (4, 24)]. This is especially true for populations whose life histories have been hidden because of an insistence upon and perpetuation of assumed dichotomies, such as in the study of the industrial period in England. In the present study, we approach industrialization as a wide-reaching phenomenon, the consequences of which created a spectrum of realities more complex than the opposed archetypes of the idyllic and “clean” agrarian countryside and overcrowded and polluted industrial city centers (e.g., London and Manchester). Such an approach is necessary to understand the lived experiences of populations hidden between these assumed extremes and to determine whether the presence of hazards in these historically hidden environments resulted in differential exposure to industrial pollutants and diverse health outcomes. To accomplish this, we explore the heavy metal content and isotopic composition of soil samples from burial sites along with the skeletal remains of individuals from two English towns [South Shields (SS) and Barton-upon-Humber (BuH); Fig. 1], each of which falls mid-way along the scale from small, rural village to large, industrial city. This approach enables us to consider not only the environments in which these individuals lived but also how pollutant exposure interwove with aspects of their biosocial identity that affected individuals’ life histories (e.g., osteological sex, age at death, and socioeconomic status).

Reconstructing lived experiences of industrialization and inequality

During the industrial period in England, the types of hazards present within any given environment varied because of both the environment itself and individuals’ biosocial identities, which enabled them access to identity-specific occupations and positions within 18th and 19th century British society [see (22, 23)]. To explore this notion further, it has been shown that working-class women and children were frequently employed in industries or positions classified as “less skilled” than those of men during this period (4–8) or that traditionally involved female labor, such as the making of textiles (9, 10). In comparison, working-class men were often employed in positions requiring substantially more specialized skills or whose work required a great deal of physical strength, such as in coal mining, operating and maintaining factory machinery, the smelting of metals, and industries associated with the United Kingdom’s new railway infrastructure (4, 6, 8, 9). Accordingly, the work environments that men and women (and adults and children) occupied inherently varied and, because of the industries themselves, the pollutants and hazards they were exposed to also varied.

Concepts of gendered work and gendered environments are not unique to the industrial period. The same processes and constructs that, in the past, dictated the environments to which an individual had access on the basis of aspects of their identity persist in societies throughout the world today. These processes continue to create hazards for disadvantaged groups and identities, particularly regarding the types and quality of environments that individuals can access and in which they spend their time. The World Health Organization has released numerous reports on the effects of social-, gender-, and identity-based inequalities on the individuals experiencing them [e.g., (25–27)]. Studies of contemporary populations [e.g., (28–31)] have identified the persistent effects of gender and identity on health, air pollutant exposure, and environmental heavy metal exposure. While the specific causation and hazards may have changed from those in the past, the snapshot of studies cited here proves that underlying socioeconomic and identity-related factors can result in differential exposure to pollutants and associated health outcomes today, nearly two centuries after the individuals in this study were subject to hazardous environments in a society that prioritized productivity over individuals’ equity, safety, and well-being. The effects of this inequality can become embodied by those living through these experiences (32). Individuals’ skeletal remains contain chemical, physiological, and anatomical records that reflect the environments they inhabited, social structures they navigated, locations where they lived and traveled, occupations they held, and other choices and actions they made during the course of their lives (32–34). These records include chemical elements present as pollutants in their environments.

Industrial pollution and health

During the industrial period in England, numerous industries released waste products, including heavy metals, into the environment without restraint. The release and disposal of industrial waste were not regulated for over a century, from the historic beginning of the industrial period in ~1760 CE until the passage of the first industrial pollution acts in ~1863 CE (11, 12). These 1863 Alkali Acts made the first attempts, although inadequate, to mitigate local environmental impacts of the production of alkali (i.e., sodium carbonate, sodium hydroxide, potassium carbonate, and potassium hydroxide) on nearby farmed plants and domesticated livestock (11, 12). However, there was no guidance or mitigation for occupational conditions or workers’ exposure to harmful chemicals and pollutants until the 1883 Factory and Workshop Act (13, 35), and neither industry owners nor policymakers considered the long-term impact of industrial waste products and pollutants on the environment or local inhabitants.

Among industrial waste products are elements that can make their way into bone and be stored there during an individual’s lifetime, and can thus be analyzed in both modern and archaeological human skeletal remains. These pollutants include arsenic (As), barium (Ba), and lead (Pb), which were produced by many diverse industries during England’s Industrial Revolution including the mining of coal and associated collieries, chemical works, metal smelting and refinement, and the manufacture of a variety of products (36–40). Modern medical research has revealed the many health outcomes associated with exposure to harmful pollutants and hazards, including As, Ba, and Pb. These studies have also determined threshold concentrations for these elements within various human tissues, including bone, that are associated with adverse outcomes (Table 1). While differences in geography, geology, and cultural practices exist between modern and archaeological contexts, analysis of fresh or modern bone from medical studies can be used to establish threshold ranges for trace element pollutants specifically associated with adverse health, as we do here, which is critical for comparative studies with archaeological remains (41). Hence, we use known trace element pollutant compositions within the body (e.g., in bone) from medical studies to extrapolate the potential health outcomes that individuals would have experienced in life. Such an understanding of the relative health of populations can increase our understanding of the lived experiences of these individuals. This includes how they suffered or became sick because of historical processes such as industrialization, the potential health conditions and disabilities they may have experienced, and the consequences of structural violence or social inequality on the quality of environments they occupied. Furthermore, extrapolating the health outcomes associated with exposure to industrial-era pollutants increases our understanding of the impact that a lack of understanding of, involvement in (i.e., governmental or employer action, such as research on the effects of workers’ conditions, including pollution, or the implementation of protections), or legislation on pollution can have on the bodies of people inhabiting polluted environments.

Mitigating and evaluating diagenesis and contamination

There is a vast body of literature that emphasizes caution against the use of trace element content of archaeological human bone to assess biogenic (in vivo) signatures. This is because diagenesis is a complex process whose effects can never entirely be mitigated (41) and that includes the modification of elemental signatures in bone via ionic exchange with the burial environment, often obscuring biogenic signatures. Not only is the chemical “starting point” of any given burial soil inherently unknown (and unknowable), but the numerous processes and changes it undergoes over time are also unknown. These processes and other factors that affect burial environment composition and elemental availability include underlying geology, weathering, mineral solubility, soil porosity, temperature, precipitation, pH, hydrology, salinity, and time elapsed since burial (41).

Furthermore, even if the mineral portion of bone appears intact and complete, this does not guarantee that the chemical composition of a sample reflects antemortem (in vivo) conditions. Diagenetic processes are facilitated by bone’s porous nature, which anatomically contains Haversian and Volkmann’s canals in addition to openings and cracks that appear with time as the organic fraction of bone (i.e., collagen) deteriorates. These openings provide numerous pathways for extraneous material (e.g., ground water) to enter and disperse within bone. Accordingly, no chemical element is immune to postdepositional changes; even low soil concentrations can accumulate in bone over time, and both site geology and diagenetic processes can variably influence elemental concentrations across a site, within individuals, and even within different sampled locations on a single bone (41). Studies of diagenetic alteration of bone include how surface contaminants can cause rapid changes to exposed remains over time [e.g., (42)], how bone and dentine are more susceptible to contamination due to increased porosity or thin cortical bone in elements such as vertebrae [e.g., (43–45)], how trace element concentrations can vary given both lifetime exposure and unique burial conditions [e.g., (46–51)] or skeletal pathologies [e.g., (52, 53)], and how modern pollutants can cause contamination in archaeological bone [e.g., (54)].

In consideration of these and additional studies (see the Supplementary Materials) and drawing upon recommended mitigation strategies from the literature, we took numerous precautions to mitigate contamination from the burial environment and to evaluate diagenesis in bone samples. These include the use of physical means of mitigation [initial cleaning followed by the removal of bone on exterior periosteal surfaces to a depth of at least 1 mm or deeper, i.e., until a color change indicative of lack of soil intrusion was observed; this amount is over twice the depth of diagenetic groundwater and soil intrusion observed in archaeological human bone in (55)] and analytical parameters for both soil and bone samples (calcium/phosphorus ratio Ca/P, strontium isotope ratio 87Sr/86Sr, radiogenic Pb isotope composition, and trace concentration data—As, Ba, Ca, P, and Pb). Complementing bone analyses, we collected and analyzed burial soil samples associated with each site (see Materials and Methods), given that comprehensive burial soil analysis is crucial for characterizing the geochemistry of the burial environment (41). We also evaluated strontium (Sr) concentrations in bone and soil samples, both generally and against 87Sr/86Sr ratios and Ba concentration data. However, these analyses contributed little to our evaluation of diagenesis, contamination, and ionic exchange with the burial environment and so are only presented in the Supplementary Materials. All methods are detailed in Materials and Methods and in the Supplementary Materials.

RESULTS

We analyzed long bone samples (primarily femora) that originated from a subset of largely adult and adolescent (osteological age ≥ ~15 years) human skeletal remains dating to the industrial period from the larger, industrial town of SS and the smaller, more agrarian town of BuH, both of which fall midway along the continuum between being an entirely agrarian village versus an entirely industrial city. Major and trace element analyses (As, Ba, Ca, Pb, and P), Sr isotope analyses (87Sr/86Sr), and Pb isotope analyses (206Pb/204Pb, 207Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/206Pb, and 207Pb/206Pb) were undertaken on bone samples. The same elemental and isotopic analyses were undertaken on burial soil samples associated with each respective site (Fig. 1). Calcium-to-phosphorus ratios

To assess bone preservation and diagenesis, we measured the concentrations of Ca and P for all bone samples, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Standard Reference Material 1400, bone ash, and all soil samples. These data are summarized in table S3. The NIST Standard Reference Material 1400 Ca/P value measured in this study was identical, within published uncertainties, to that calculated from certificate concentrations and literature values (Ca/P = 2.13). Eighty-four individuals (Fig. 3A) in this study fell within the range for bone least affected by diagenetic alteration of these two elements (Ca/P = 1.81 to 2.56; see the Supplementary Materials). The 10 individuals whose Ca/P values exceeded this range (SS: CS1, CS2, CS3, CS4, CS5, CS6, CS7, and CS9; BuH: SP1 and SP7) were determined to be above the age of ~50 years, and therefore we cannot determine whether their elevated ratios are a consequence of decreased bone turnover rates with age seniority (56, 57). The Ca/P ratios measured in all burial soil samples showed higher values compared to the human bone samples, with no overlap observed (Fig. 3A).

Arsenic

Soil As concentrations were about 100 times greater than skeletal concentrations of this element (P < 0.001; Tables 2 and 3 and table S7). The skeletal As concentrations in 94.7% (n = 89) of individuals were above the natural exposure level of As in bone (>6 ng/g; Table 4) (37). The As concentrations measured in samples from the remaining five individuals from BuH (SP8, SP10, SP23, SP25, and SP33) were below instrument detection limits. Among the individuals from SS, there were three outliers with elevated As concentrations (CS13, 4.76 μg/g; CS18, 3.58 μg/g; CS41, 7.01 μg/g; see Fig. 2A) compared to collection means (Table 2). Evaluation of these individuals’ Ca/P ratios did not indicate diagenetic alteration, nor did their remaining trace element concentration data suggest diagenesis [e.g., (58, 59)]. Furthermore, these individuals’ Sr and Pb isotope ratios were not outlier values within the sampled SS population, so these individuals were retained in this study.

The individuals from SS had greater skeletal As concentrations compared to the individuals from BuH (P < 0.001). In addition, when the females from both collections were combined, they showed greater concentrations of skeletal As than males (P = 0.004; see Fig. 2A, Table 2, and table S7). Females from SS also showed higher skeletal As concentrations compared to their counterparts from BuH (P = 0.002). Within collections, the females from SS had greater skeletal As than the males (P = 0.002), although there was no difference in skeletal As between the males and females of BuH.

When As concentration values were evaluated by age at death (Fig. 2B, Table 2, and table S7), a weak positive correlation was found between age at death and As concentrations (r = 0.212, P = 0.046). Young adults were observed to have lower skeletal As concentrations than prime adults (P = 0.039) and older adults (P = 0.017). However, when this analysis was undertaken within each collection, these differences only persisted within BuH (Table 2 and table S7), where young adults had lower concentrations of skeletal As compared to prime (P = 0.043) and older adults (P = 0.023). When individuals were compared against exposure ranges established from the literature (Tables 1 and 4), there was a broad distribution of As concentrations indicative of a range of in vivo health outcomes (Fig. 2B).

Barium

Soil Ba concentrations were between two and five times greater than skeletal concentrations (P < 0.001; Tables 2, 3, and 5). All individuals in this study had skeletal Ba values above the natural exposure value of 2 μg/g in bone (Table 4) (37), indicating that they were likely anthropogenically exposed to this element during life. Similar to the observation made regarding As concentrations, skeletal Ba concentrations varied between collections and by osteological sex (Table 2 and Fig. 2B). Among the sampled populations, there are three individuals from BuH (SP5, 156.48 μg/g; SP6, 245.30 μg/g; SP7, 186.40 μg/g) and two individuals from SS (CS10, 245.48 μg/g; CS13, 151.51 μg/g) with exceptionally high Ba concentrations (Fig. 2A) compared to collection means (Table 2). As with individuals with higher arsenic concentrations, evaluation of these individuals’ Ca/P ratios and trace element concentration data did not suggest diagenetic alteration [e.g., (58, 59)]. These individuals’ Sr and Pb isotope ratios were also not outlier values within their respective sampled populations, so these individuals were retained in this study.

Individuals from SS showed greater concentrations of skeletal Ba than individuals from BuH (P = 0.013; Table 2 and table S7). There were no differences in mean Ba concentration when skeletal values of all males were compared to all females nor when the males from the two collections were compared with each other (see Fig. 2A). Lead

Soil Pb concentrations were between 2 and 15 times greater than skeletal concentrations (P < 0.001; Tables 2 and 3 and table S7). Of the 94 individuals in this study, 93.6% (n = 88; see Table 4) had skeletal Pb concentrations above 2.7 μg/g, a concentration used here as a rough threshold for natural lead in bone (see additional information in the Supplementary Materials) (60, 61). Skeletal Pb concentrations varied between collection and among distinct aspects of biosocial identity (Fig. 2A, Table 2, and table S7). There was one female from SS (CS4, 112.73 μg/g) and two individuals from BuH (SP15, 119.17 μg/g; SP28, 128.18 μg/g) with exceptionally high skeletal Pb concentrations compared to collection means. As with outlier As and Ba values, these individuals’ Ca/P ratios were within the range for acceptable, nondiagenetic bone: Their other trace concentrations did not indicate diagenetic alteration [e.g., (58, 59)], and neither their Pb isotope ratios nor their 87Sr/86Sr isotope ratios were outliers among their respective sampled population means. Hence, these individuals were retained in analyses.

Individuals from BuH showed higher concentrations of skeletal Pb than the individuals from SS (P = 0.020), although this difference is seen primarily between the males of each collection (P = 0.034; Fig. 2A, Table 2, and table S7). The females from the two collections had similar skeletal Pb concentrations, and there were no differences in the Pb content of individuals’ skeletal remains when all females were compared to all males nor when the males and females were compared within each collection.

When individuals’ skeletal Pb concentrations were analyzed by age-at-death categories, there was no association between skeletal Pb concentration and age at death (r = 0.039, P = 0.710; Fig. 2B). There were also no differences in lead concentrations between any of the subgroups (Table 2 and table S7), either between or within each sampled population. Most individuals from SS (85.2%, n = 46) and many from BuH (45%, n = 18) have skeletal Pb values at the normal exposure level (Table 4 and Fig. 2B), although there are 10 individuals—nine from BuH and one from SS—that fall within the acute range for skeletal Pb concentrations at which significant health outcomes are known to arise (see Table 1).

Radiogenic isotopes

To further assess diagenesis and contamination, explore individuals’ residence locations and mobility, and assess potential origins of anthropogenic pollutant exposure, we measured Sr and Pb isotope ratios for all human bone and soil samples. A summary of these data can be found in Table 5; because of the large size of this dataset, all isotope data for each individual and each soil sample can be found in tables S4 and S5, respectively. A statistical comparison of Pb and Sr isotope data for soil samples and human bone samples overall and by meaningful subgroups can be found in table S8.

Strontium isotopes, 87Sr/86Sr

The surface soil samples from BuH had a mean 87Sr/86Sr ratio of 0.71542 ± 0.00133 1σ (one SD; Table 5). In comparison, SS skeletal soil samples had a mean 87Sr/86Sr ratio of 0.71494 ± 0.00127 1σ. BuH deep soil samples were less radiogenic regarding Sr isotopes (0.71454 ± 0.00141 1σ). In contrast, the human bone samples from both sites showed a less radiogenic Sr isotopic composition (independent samples Mann-Whitney U test, P < 0.001; BuH, 0.70873 ± 0.00028 1σ; SS, 0.70998 ± 0.00024 1σ) relative to the isotopic signatures of any soil samples from either burial site (Fig. 3B). The Sr isotope values of human bone samples from BuH are also less radiogenic compared to the human bone samples from SS (P < 0.001). When compared with biosphere domain Sr isotope ratios (primarily from plants and water) (62–64) from each location, human bone samples from each site largely fell within the ranges established by the British Geological Survey (BGS) for BuH (~0.7077 to 0.7094) and SS (~0.7091 to 0.7148; see Fig. 4A). Strontium isotope ranges for burial soils from BuH (0.71225 to 0.71654) differed from BGS biosphere values, but from SS (0.71251 to 0.71650), they overlapped with BGS biosphere values.

In the assessment of Sr isotope signatures for a population, individuals with 87Sr/86Sr ratios within 2σ from population means are generally considered locals, and individuals with values outside of this range are considered nonlocals (65). We used this criterion alongside bioavailable strontium data because our soil sample values largely fell outside the ranges associated with local bedrock values at each site (see details in the Supplementary Materials). When we evaluate our data against these criteria (BuH 2σ range of 0.70814 to 0.70929 and SS 2σ range of 0.70950 to 0.71047), there are five outliers: two individuals from BuH (SP1, 87Sr/86Sr of 0.70964; SP28, 87Sr/86Sr of 0.70949) have higher, more radiogenic 87Sr/86Sr values that exceed 2σ from the site mean. Compared to the bioavailable values for BuH from the BGS (above), these individuals are the only ones who present as nonlocals. In SS, three individuals (CS5, 87Sr86Sr of 0.71063; CS46, 87Sr86Sr of 0.71048; CS60, 87Sr86Sr of 0.71059) have more radiogenic 87Sr86Sr values that exceed 2σ from the site mean. Compared to the bioavailable values for SS from the BGS, these SS individuals fall within the range for expected locals.

Lead isotopes, 206Pb/204Pb, 207Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/206Pb, and 207Pb/206Pb

Surface soil samples from BuH were isotopically different compared to deep soil samples collected from the same sites (see Table 5, table S8, and fig. S1) for all Pb isotope ratios apart from 207Pb/204Pb and 208Pb/206Pb. The soils from BuH, both surface and deep samples, also had uniquely different Pb isotope ratios compared to the soil samples collected from among the skeletal remains from SS for the ratios 208Pb/204Pb (P = 0.029) and 208Pb/206Pb (P = 0.003). In contrast, the human bone samples from both sites showed a less radiogenic Pb isotopic composition relative to the isotopic signatures (all Pb ratios) of any soil samples from either burial site (Mann-Whitey U tests, all P ≤ 0.003; see Fig. 3, B and C; Table 5; and table S8). The BuH soil samples were not collected directly from burial contexts; accordingly, the potential for modern environmental contamination to have modified the Pb isotope ratios exists, particularly for the BuH surface samples.

The human bone samples from BuH also had unique Pb isotope compositions compared to the human bone samples from SS regarding all Pb isotope ratios (P < 0.001) except 207Pb/204Pb and 208Pb/204Pb. When individuals are analyzed by osteological sex, mean values for both the males of each site and the females of each site are statistically different (P < 0.001). Within BuH, age appears to have a small effect on Pb composition, but this is only observed in the mean isotope ratio for 206Pb/204Pb; prime adults have greater mean values of 206Pb/204Pb than both young adults (P = 0.047) and mature adults (P = 0.031; Table 5). However, when compared with the Pb isoscape for 206Pb/204Pb (63, 66) based on bioavailable geologic samples, the human bone samples and soil samples from each site do fall within the range established for natural lead from each location (both ~18.33 to 18.88; see Fig. 4B).

DISCUSSION

Biogenesis or diagenesis?

The trace element (As, Ba, and Pb) composition of soil samples showed greater values than the skeletal compositions of these elements (Table 3 and table S7; all P < 0.001). Burial soil samples also had distinctly unique Ca and P compositions (Fig. 3A) compared to human bone samples. While this does not necessarily indicate that diagenesis did not occur (which can cause accumulation of elements over time), these data, particularly the ratio of Ca/P that tells us about the quality of bone preservation, are good indicators that minimal alteration occurred, especially when taken alongside the additional analyses below.

Sr and Pb isotope systematics were able to differentiate between the soil and human bone samples from each site (Fig. 3, B and C). At BuH, differences were observed between mean human bone and soil values for 207Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/206Pb, 207Pb/206Pb, and 87Sr/86Sr (table S8; all P < 0.001). At SS, there were differences between mean human bone and soil values for 206Pb/204Pb, 207Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/206Pb, and 87Sr/86Sr (table S8; all P < 0.001). These data reflect the presence of distinct Pb end members (i.e., see slopes of regression lines in plot of 207Pb/204Pb versus 208Pb/204Pb for human bone and soil samples in Fig. 3C) for each of the soil and human skeletal remains; the isotopic composition differs on the basis of either the geologic composition and/or contributions from more modern pollutants (i.e., in burial soil samples), such as leaded petrol from the late 20th century (67), or biogenic values reflective of past anthropogenic activities and processes (i.e., human bone samples; Fig. 3C). Again, caution must be taken as we cannot know the isotopic composition of the burial soil at the time that these individuals were interred, but combined with other data, these findings yield support that the bones were minimally affected by diagenetic alterations. For this reason and considering the similar chemical behavior of Ba, Ca, and Sr and of P and As, as well as significant differences in the isotopic signatures (Sr and Pb) and major element ratios (Ca/P) of the soil samples and the human bone samples (Fig. 3A), we argue that we have successfully mitigated contamination and diagenesis. Accordingly, we maintain that the heavy metal concentrations measured in bone samples here are largely indicative of biogenic signatures resulting from in vivo processes.

Arsenic exposure

There is a scarcity of research on the presence of As in human skeletal remains, the majority of which uses the presence of this element as an indicator of diagenesis [e.g., (68–70)] as opposed to a biogenic signature. However, recent research has found that human tissues can naturally (normally, per medical literature) contain between 0.02 and 3 μg/g (71) of As, although the higher concentration was recovered from hair samples and may suggest acute toxic exposure. In addition, archaeological human remains from cultures known to have exploited a variety of metals (e.g., the Romans) have documented arsenic concentrations as high as 35 μg/g (72), far beyond the concentration associated with acute toxicity per medical research. In consideration of the mitigation measures used in this study and the elemental and isotopic values used to assess diagenesis, we are confident that our values reflect biogenic signatures that may further reflect bioaccumulation over time (36, 73, 74).

The data presented in this study reveal that the individuals living in the larger and more industrial town of SS had greater As concentrations in their long bones than individuals from the smaller and more agrarian town of BuH. This result was expected, as individuals in SS would have had greater exposure to As as a by-product of the industries located in or nearby town: the processing of coal from the Durham coalfields north of the city, the smelting of metals used in the manufacture of ships and steam trains, and a local chemical works (73, 75, 76). Compared with the individuals in BuH, the routes of exposure to As are greater in number in SS.

BuH had very few industries in which As was an expected hazard—the most intense local industry in this small agrarian town was chalk mining, an industry not associated with As and which had been undertaken in the region for centuries (77, 78). However, the individuals from the more agrarian site are still elevated in As concentration compared to the natural exposure threshold (Table 1). This may reflect specific practices in 19th century agrarian communities, such as using As-based sheep dip to rid livestock of fleas (13, 37). Besides As contamination via farm runoff from these dips, those farm workers who were conducting the dips or otherwise treating animals would have been directly exposed to As, and those consuming the animals that had been treated would have been exposed indirectly.

The females from SS also showed greater skeletal As concentrations than males (Fig. 2A, Table 2, and table S7). This may be explained by women’s employment in the local chemical works, as the chemical industry was known to employ women often (similar to how women were employed in the making of lead paste) (6, 10). In contrast, a lack of differences between males and females in BuH is likely the result of both men and women having had similar responsibilities in such a small-town agrarian environment, in accordance with the positions they held within society, as either local aristocracy or in occupations or lifestyles that pertain to domestic work, mercantile businesses, or in farming (77, 78).

The concentrations of skeletal As in both sites fall within all medical exposure ranges for this element. Of these individuals, 42 from SS and 16 from BuH have skeletal As concentrations at or above the acute exposure level at which negative health outcomes occur (see Tables 1 and 4). As such, these individuals likely experienced a range of health issues associated with exposure to As. The younger adults in BuH also had lower skeletal As values compared with the older adults. It is possible, however, that the older individuals bioaccumulated As, regardless of the number of exposure events they experienced, since this element can accumulate in the body over time with little to no excretion (36, 73). However, it is equally possible that the older individuals at BuH encountered more severe exposure events or used medicines made with As, such as a popular curative called Fowler’s solution (79), more frequently than the younger members of the population.

Barium exposure

As with As, there was more skeletal Ba among the individuals from the more industrial town of SS. While high concentrations of skeletal Ba can reflect diagenetic processes, high biogenic skeletal values have been observed in humans and other mammals (between 115.79 and 1200 μg/g) (80–84). Our assessment of diagenesis via trace element and isotopic analyses suggests that high skeletal Ba occurred because of lifetime exposure. High values among the individuals at SS were expected because of the presence of industries in or near SS that exposed individuals to Ba as a by-product (e.g., mining and smelting of coal) (85), which were absent in BuH. In addition, females’ increased Ba levels may also have occurred as a consequence of working in the nearby chemical industry in SS. However, the route of exposure to Ba in BuH was likely very different. Ba is present naturally in the soil (86), and individuals may have consumed foods grown on their own or local farms, the soils of which may have varied in Ba concentration in the past.

The amount of Ba in individuals’ skeletal remains also falls within all medical exposure ranges. Of the individuals in this study, 48 from SS and 23 from BuH have concentrations at or above the acute exposure range (Table 4 and Fig. 2B) associated with the health outcomes summarized in Table 1. These individuals would likely have experienced the health outcomes associated with exposure, although their skeletal concentrations may also reflect numerous exposure events leading to gradual bioaccumulation as opposed to a single acute and toxic event.

Lead exposure

Pb exposure varied widely between the sampled populations studied, from levels considered to be natural in origin to 128.18 μg/g. These high values are not impossible for individuals exposed to anthropogenic Pb; previous studies have found biogenic skeletal Pb values between 20 and 424 μg/g associated with anthropogenic exposure among modern and archaeological populations (47, 48, 87). In the present study, the highest mean skeletal Pb levels were unexpectedly observed among the males from the more agrarian town of BuH. In SS, the routes of exposure to Pb via industry are numerous and include those industries previously mentioned, such as the smelting of metal. The results of this study, however, suggest that there was some buffer in the more industrial environment, or some hazard in the more agrarian environment, such that the skeletal Pb concentrations of the individuals from SS are lower than those of the individuals from BuH: The difference between the mean skeletal Pb concentrations from males of each collection is an astounding 28.63 μg/g.

As with arsenic and barium, potential routes of exposure to Pb in a more agrarian environment during this time include the burning of coal mined from throughout the country (40). While previous studies have found specifically that lead-containing molecules from coal usage are too large for absorption via inhalation to occur (88), the isotope values of local lead ores align with our data, suggesting that lead-bearing coal smoke and particles were being inhaled or ingested in some manner such that they were deposited within the body. Specifically, bone ranges for 206Pb/204Pb (BuH, 18.4121 to 18.4786; SS, 18.3844 to 18.4671), 207Pb/204Pb (BuH, 15.5878 to 15.6528; SS, 15.5897 to 15.6459), 208Pb/204Pb (BuH, 38.2914 to 38.5185; SS, 38.3042 to 38.4990), 208Pb/206Pb (BuH, 2.0762 to 2.0873; SS, 2.0822 to 2.0870), and 207Pb/206Pb (BuH, 0.8446 to 0.8482; SS, 0.8466 to 0.8488) align with those of local coal sources in Yorkshire and Nottinghamshire near BuH (208Pb/206Pb, 2.075 ± 0.005) (89) and the Durham coalfields near SS (206Pb/204Pb, 18.599 ± 0.226; 207Pb/204Pb, 15.612 ± 0.033; 208Pb/204Pb, 38.290 ± 0.557; 208Pb/206Pb, 2.073 ± 0.011; 207Pb/206Pb, 0.840 ± 0.009) (89, 90). Crucially, we cannot definitively rule out modern contamination in BuH soil samples, considering that these did not originate from direct burial contexts, thereby limiting representation of the local baseline at this site. Nevertheless, our bone 206Pb/204Pb isotope data are within the range associated with bioavailable lead from the BGS (206Pb/204Pb, 18.33 to 18.88), derived from a variety of samples that include galenas, ores, and other lead-bearing minerals (63, 64, 91). For this reason, we cannot rule out the lead in coal, and therefore the inhalation or ingestion of coal smoke particulates containing lead, or local ores, as sources of lead exposure.

The medical exposure ranges of the skeletal Pb concentrations in this study also vary. Among the individuals from the more urban SS, five have above-normal ranges and one falls within the acute exposure range. At BuH, there are nine individuals who fall within the above normal range and another nine individuals who fall within the acute exposure range. All 24 of these individuals likely experienced different degrees of the health outcomes associated with exposure to Pb in Table 1. Nevertheless, as with the cases of Ba and As, Pb can also bioaccumulate over time and across numerous exposure events.

Sourcing anthropogenic Pb

Individuals whose skeletal Pb concentrations exceed normal exposure values (above 50 μg/g; see Table 1) are certainly anthropogenically exposed to lead. A lower threshold is possible; some papers suggest that this baseline could be between 0.5 and 2.7 μg/g in bone, although these thresholds are population and culturally specific (50, 60, 61, 92) and values below 2.7 μg/g can still reflect anthropogenic exposure. For this reason, we do not consider Pb concentrations above 2.7 μg/g to be necessarily strongly indicative of anthropogenic exposure; rather, anthropogenic exposure can include low skeletal concentrations of lead and varies on the basis of cultural and geological context. For our purposes, because many individuals exceed this value, their skeletal lead concentrations may reflect the source, or mixture of sources, of anthropogenic ores (49, 91, 93) in whatever material caused their exposure, and lead isotope ratios help elucidate the potential sources of this exposure. Of the 94 individuals whose long bone samples we analyzed, 88 had skeletal Pb concentrations above 2.7 μg/g. While there were differences in collection means for the ratios of 206Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/206Pb, and 207Pb/206Pb, the ranges of values for each collection overlapped significantly (see Table 5 and table S4). This overlap, as well as the lead isotope ratios for both sampled populations, may reflect similarities in anthropogenic lead exposure, the mixture of anthropogenic and natural lead from the environment, or the reuse of leaded items (94).

To explore sources of anthropogenic Pb exposure for these individuals, we compared Pb isotope values for human bone samples against values from geological samples reported in the literature, particularly Pb-bearing minerals (e.g., galena) and coal, from throughout the United Kingdom and Ireland [focusing primarily on data compiled in (66, 95); see the Supplementary Materials]. All five Pb isotope ratios measured for the 88 individuals in this study (ratios plotted against concentration in Fig. 5) with Pb concentrations above 2.7 μg/g matched geological samples collected from throughout the United Kingdom and Ireland (see Fig. 6A). The geographic distribution of geological materials with Pb isotope values that match the individuals analyzed here suggests a broad network of Pb exploitation throughout the United Kingdom and Ireland. Pb, or natural materials containing Pb, and coal were mined and shipped extensively throughout the region; some of these materials such as coal were used directly and burned in the home and factory, and still others were used in factory settings in the production of various goods (see below).

The remaining six individuals had skeletal Pb concentrations below 2.7 μg/g: four from BuH (SP12, SP25, SP31, and SP32) and two from SS (CS33 and CS48). Similar to those individuals anthropogenically exposed, the Pb isotope ratios of these six individuals also match locations throughout the UK and Ireland (Fig. 6B), which suggests that even individuals with lower bone lead concentrations experienced anthropogenic exposure during the industrial period in England, were exposed to a mixture of anthropogenic and natural Pb, or were exposed to entirely natural Pb. If natural, while none of these individuals’ Sr isotope ratios suggest residence elsewhere (see table S4 and the “Mobility during industrialization” section), it is possible that the variation observed in their Pb isotope ratios reflects time spent in locations with a similar Sr isotope composition to their respective parishes, but with varying naturally available Pb. This sheds additional light on the movement of populations during the industrial period, which may be obscured because of similarities in biospheric Sr values (Fig. 4A) and bedrock geology. What we can say from these data, however, is that the use of a cutoff value such as 2.7 μg/g is insufficient to detect differences in anthropogenic versus natural lead, as low concentrations may themselves be anthropogenic.

The radiogenic Pb isotope data further allow us to explore the concept of cultural focusing and its implications for identifying anthropogenic exposure (Fig. 5 and see the Supplementary Materials). Cultural focusing refers to the phenomenon where Pb isotope values in human bone become tightly constrained, reflecting the dominant average isotopic signature of anthropogenic Pb sources within a population’s cultural sphere rather than direct local geological background variation (93, 94, 96). This focusing occurs because populations often exploit Pb from a variety of ore deposits, as well as due to the historical practice of repeated melting, mixing, and reuse of lead materials, which, together, average the available Pb isotope ratios (94).

Figure 5 plots lead isotope ratios measured in this study against individuals’ skeletal lead concentrations (logarithmic scale). As shown in Fig. 5E, the measured 207Pb/206Pb ratios for nearly all individuals from this study plot between 0.845 and 0.850. This focused range is considered broadly indicative of anthropogenic sources of exposure, likely due to cultural focusing on industrially or historically used lead ores (93, 94). This cultural focusing is seen in other studies of Britain during the industrial period for 207Pb/206Pb ratios (88) and other lead isotope ratios examining lead exploitation over time in England [e.g., (97)]. Low elemental concentrations might still indicate anthropogenic exposure; even very small amounts of anthropogenic lead can significantly alter lead isotope signatures in bone (94).

Alternative anthropogenic Pb source(s): Soot, aerosols, and wind trajectories

One possible contribution to Pb pollutant exposure in each parish is by means of smoke and particulates generated in industrial areas, which subsequently travel in natural air currents throughout the country. Seasonal winds transported the smoke released by industry, via tall chimneys, into the air and by the burning of coal in homes throughout the country (Fig. 7). The HYSPLIT 6-hour air mass back-trajectory ensemble conducted for winter (mid-January) and summer (mid-August) for the 4 years spanning 2020–2023 CE revealed that air masses reaching BuH and SS may have passed over large cities with large coal consumption and expanding industries during the industrial period. This air mass movement can easily mobilize small-sized particles and transport them to the study sites within 6 hours. This suggests that both sites were potential receptors of polluted air masses that originated from large, industrial cities to the west (e.g., Lancaster, Leeds, and Manchester) and to the south (e.g., London and Birmingham) of BuH and SS.

Invisible hazards and silent killers

In both agrarian and industrial environments, another means of exposure to hazardous pollutants lies in an invisible route: the industrial goods made in factories and mills using toxic materials and that were used at work and in the home. The burning of coal alone, either in the workplace or home, exposed individuals to As, Ba, and Pb in the form of noxious smoke and coal dust, especially in confined spaces or when ventilation was poor (38–40, 98).

Numerous products manufactured and distributed throughout the British Isles during this time were made using the toxic elements analyzed in the present study. Many goods contained As, including paints and medicines. Items made with a pigment called Scheele’s green, in particular, contained the toxicant; this arsenical pigment was used to color everything from wallpaper and candy wrappers to stationary and women’s gowns (13, 75). Women may have been particularly susceptible to As exposure in urban environments through regular use and contact in the home with goods and products made or dyed with As.

Exposure to Ba can also occur in the form of industrial goods that were used regularly. Items that contained or were made with Ba include textiles, steel, paper, pesticides, and even cosmetics (86).

Pb was particularly prevalent in goods during the industrial period. A potential route of exposure to Pb, especially among the more mixed-status individuals living in BuH, is in the form of purchased goods that were made with or contained Pb, such as ceramics (i.e., Pb-glazed pottery), paints, cosmetics, glassware, medicines, and food containers (46, 99). While certain household items would have been used by individuals of all socioeconomic statuses (e.g., pottery), due to their lower status as working-class proletariat, the individuals from SS likely had less disposable income to spend on more extravagant items compared to their more mixed-status compatriots at BuH, and would likely have spent a greater percentage of their pay on food (18). Accordingly, the fact that at least part of the population of BuH had discretionary income, which was not spent solely on subsistence items and housing, likely contributed to the increased Pb exposure observed in this more rural environment. Furthermore, childhood exposure [e.g., (88)] may have contributed to the skeletal lead levels observed in this study, particularly for adolescents and younger adults whose long bones have not completely turned over. Since lead is not efficiently removed from the body and is largely reincorporated into new bone during turnover, lead accumulated during childhood represents a fundamental component of the mean lifetime lead burden of an individual. Therefore, this childhood exposure must be considered in the interpretation of skeletal Pb levels. To explore the relative timing of lead accumulation, future research should determine the lead composition of enamel samples from the individuals in this study, and these data should be compared to skeletal Pb concentrations to assess childhood versus mean lifetime lead contributions.

The exact sources of exposure to Pb, or indeed to any of the trace element pollutants in this study, cannot be determined with accuracy because of the endemic nature of these toxic elements in both industrial goods and industrial waste and pollution. However, the possible routes of exposure during the industrial period were numerous. Regardless of the pathway, exposure to these trace element pollutants was significant enough to accumulate in bone to levels considered harmful.

Mobility during industrialization

The common assumption regarding mobility during industrialization was that more individuals from rural areas moved to new industrial cities and growing mill towns to seek employment in the opportunities industrialization created, and this certainly did occur (100). This mobility can be seen in the growing number of individuals living in towns and urban centers during this period, which increased from ~20% of Britain’s population in 1700 CE to about 50% by 1850 CE and lastly to about 80% by 1900 CE (101, 102), although this certainly also includes self-reproducing urban populations in addition to immigration. Our data, however, suggest that mobility occurred in both directions (e.g., similar to the movements between rural and urban environments of tailor James Carter between ~1810 and 1836 CE) (103). In addition to elucidating differences in regional bedrock geology, biosphere Sr, and diagenetic alteration, Sr isotope signatures provide insight into aspects of mobility and residence locations.

As previously discussed, the 87Sr/86Sr isotope signatures in the long bone samples from individuals from both SS and BuH largely align with the values associated with the BGS strontium biosphere data from each location (62, 64). However, there are outliers within each sampled population. The two outliers from BuH (SP1 and SP28) overlap with SS Sr isotope values, suggesting time spent in locations with a similar bedrock geology composition as SS (from Fig. 4A), which may include nearby Winterton (~11.3 km from BuH), Brigg (~15.3 km), Scunthorpe (~18.1 km), Selby (~42.3 km), and York (~51.5 km), although this may have included SS (~159.3 km). In SS, the three outliers (CS5, CS46, and CS60) have Sr isotope values that fall within the local range of BGS biosphere values but that are more than 2σ outside of the sampled population mean, suggesting that they may be nonlocal. The nearest locations with bedrock geology that have similar Sr isotope values (from Fig. 4A) are south around Billingham and Wolviston (~42.7 km from SS) or west toward Brampton (~83.2 km), Carlisle (~97.2 km), and Penrith (~92.5 km). All five of these outlier individuals have skeletal Pb at or above anthropogenic concentrations (Pb ≥ 12.05 μg/g), and for this reason, we cannot further isolate areas in which they resided beyond the broad bands associated with various Sr isotope signatures depicted in Fig. 4A.

While our analysis of Pb concentrations assessed anthropogenic thresholds, we acknowledge that this does not preclude the influence of anthropogenic Pb even for individuals with low skeletal concentrations. For the six individuals whose skeletal lead concentrations fell below 2.7 μg/g (SP12, SP25, SP31, SP32, CS33, and CS48), their 87Sr/86Sr isotope values match the strontium biosphere values and bedrock geology of the two locations studied (table S4). Alternatively, their Pb isotope ratios match various locations throughout the UK and Ireland (see Fig. 6B). Many of these locations share Sr biosphere isotope ratios (Fig. 4A) with these individuals’ towns of burial. Accordingly, the use of galena and feldspar data was necessitated by the lack of published bioavailable lead isoscapes (excepting 206Pb/204Pb from the BGS) (64, 91). Furthermore, a notable limitation is the sparse availability of biosphere Pb isotope data across many parts of the UK, particularly in the southwest region of England. This region includes London and areas in Fig. 6A with Sr values matching SS (dark blue, teal, and dark green sections on the bottom right) and has not been sampled as extensively for Pb-bearing geological materials. Consequently, we cannot definitively exclude this large region of the country as a potential origin for analyzed individuals. Future research on Pb isotope ratios of geological materials from these undersampled regions, combined with Pb concentration analyses and isotope data from dental enamel, may further elucidate individuals’ origins.

This study sought to determine whether we can see the effects and determine potential sources of diverse anthropogenic and polluted environments in the human skeleton, particularly those that occurred because of invisible vulnerabilities created through industrialization and a failure to recognize or take action to address the consequences of exposure to hazardous pollutants in 18th and 19th century England. Discrepancies in As, Ba, and Pb concentrations in individuals’ skeletal remains in both environments are tied into individuals’ occupations, as determined by their biosocial identity and the industries in which men and women worked, as well as those environments and industries present in SS and BuH.

Trace element pollutant signatures in the skeletal remains of individuals from both sites in this study indicate the presence of toxicants in numerous environments during this time in English history. There were toxic hazards present in the more agrarian town of BuH resulting in increased exposure to and uptake of Pb, but, likewise, there were also toxic hazards present in the more industrial town of SS that caused individuals to have greater concentrations of skeletal As and Ba. These hazards occurred in the form of occupation and industry, as well as by means of “silent killers”: industrial goods used in the home that were assumed to be innocuous but were composed of toxic and harmful materials. Accordingly, historical biases that focus on the dichotomy between the industrial city and the agrarian village are inappropriate; they are limited in scope and fail to recognize the wide-reaching effects of industry, especially at the individual scale and among populations hidden within these assumptions. The belief that the countryside provided an idyllic escape from the smog and filth of industry, therefore, would appear to be an incorrect oversimplification of a complex and multilayered problem: Industrialization in England affected all English populations, although at different scales and in different and sometimes unexpected ways.

Sr and Pb isotope data not only allowed us to assess diagenesis and contamination but also highlighted the movement and residence locations of individuals during the past 10 to 20 years of their lives and, in the case of individuals with high concentrations of Pb, the extensive nature of Pb exploitation throughout the UK and Ireland during the industrial period in England. While 206Pb/204Pb provided limited information about sourcing due to similarities in bedrock geology in large areas of the UK, the incorporation of additional Pb isotope ratios allowed us to explore the exploitation and usage of Pb mined throughout the UK in a more nuanced manner. In particular, we observed that assessing anthropogenic Pb and cultural focusing shed light on similarities in exploited lead across time and space. Comparison of additional lead isotope ratios with geological samples including ores, coal, and galenas from throughout the UK and Ireland revealed a wide range of signatures, which include and extend beyond regions surrounding each respective site. As such, cultural focusing and anthropogenic Pb exposure must be analyzed on a case-by-case basis. Pb isotope values in the geological background of the areas where study populations resided must be considered in conjunction with skeletal Pb concentration data, and a variety of Pb isotopes should be used to explore anthropogenic sources.

While our interpretations broadly reflect life during England’s industrial period, caution is warranted because of the inherent complexities of trace element analysis in human skeletal remains and the temporal context of the individuals studied. For interred human skeletal remains, postdepositional contamination often obscures biogenic signals, rendering the precise interpretation of total elemental abundance ambiguous. Despite our rigorous mitigation and evaluation efforts, we acknowledge that diagenetic alteration is extensive in archaeological bone and can never fully be mitigated. Therefore, our focus remains on identifying relative differences and spectrums of exposure to known trace element pollutants rather than establishing absolute thresholds, reinforcing the need for careful interpretation of biochemical data. With regard to temporal challenges, the individuals interred at BuH (see Materials and Methods) had an extended burial time frame. This means that some individuals may have lived a portion of their lives before industrialization began in earnest ~1760 CE. However, as BuH experienced minimal industrialization and individuals would have persisted in similar life courses as earlier populations, we do not consider this to be a limitation. In addition, the use of toxic materials in goods and medicines predates the industrial era: To see this, we only need to look at technological lead exposure due to metal processing and handling among Late Medieval English populations (12th to 16th centuries CE) (97). Lead pipes were also present in some areas of England during the Roman era and medieval period but were not in widespread use in England until the 1880s (99), so we do not expect that they had an impact on individuals studied here.

The limitations of dichotomous interpretations and a focus on the metaphorical forest rather than the trees are not only true of the Industrial Revolution in England; they apply to all historical and anthropological analyses of past human societies. While it is true that history is made up of people, people also make history: Individual experiences are key to understanding the real, lived consequences of historic events. In this regard, it is important to incorporate tools from anthropology, geochemistry, and other disciplines to obtain a more holistic study of historic populations and explore lived experiences at the individual scale. Such an approach reveals insight into the stresses, risks, and vulnerabilities experienced by past populations and that influence both these individuals’ experiences of historic events as well as our understanding and interpretation of those experiences.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study populations

Our study focused on two collections of human skeletal remains from England that we received permission to study: the first from SS and maintained at the University of Sheffield, and the second from BuH and maintained by Historic England and English Heritage. We acknowledge that expanding our study to include more sites with individuals of more diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and tighter temporal ranges for burial, dates of birth, and dates of death would provide a more comprehensive understanding of life during England’s Industrial Revolution, and future work will seek to undertake this expanded research. However, our aim here is to focus on those skeletal collections available to us to study and to provide insights within the constraints of the dataset and historical context.

St. Hilda’s Parish, SS

The first study population comes from the large industrial town of SS (Fig. 1), maintained as the Coronation Street collection by the University of Sheffield. The individuals in this collection were urban, working-class people buried at the parish burial ground of St. Hilda’s Parish Church in SS: a large and rapidly industrializing maritime town during the 18th and 19th centuries (76). The individuals sampled from St. Hilda’s (n = 40) were interred between 1816 and 1855 CE and were likely employed in local coal mines, maritime shipbuilding industries, the building of steam trains, glass making, and a local chemical works (76, 104).

St. Peter’s Church, BuH

The second study population comes from the small rural town of BuH (Fig. 1), a more agrarian and less industrialized town than SS. BuH was a market village founded alongside the River Humber during the Middle Ages, after which it grew into a small town reliant primarily on agriculture until the early 19th century, when small businesses and local brick and tile-making industries developed (77, 105). The osteological collection is maintained by English Heritage and Historic England at St. Peter’s Church in BuH, the church where they had been buried. An analysis of parish records revealed that the individuals interred at St. Peter’s were of mixed socioeconomic status from throughout the community. However, ~37.2% of the individuals at St. Peter’s from the 18th and 19th centuries for whom occupations were listed (718 of 1930 total individuals) were employed as some type of laborer (22). The individuals sampled here from St. Peter’s (n = 54) were interred between 1700 and 1855 CE (77, 105).

Summary of methods

All materials and methods are described in detail in the Supplementary Materials. On the basis of the wealth of literature on trace element analyses and diagenesis, we took numerous precautions to mitigate the effects of diagenesis during sample collection and to evaluate potential contamination and diagenetic alteration in skeletal samples. All human remains in this study were treated ethically and with the utmost respect at all times. Archaeological samples were collected with permission from relevant universities and UK governmental entities. Teeth were not chosen for analysis in this study, despite enamel’s increased resistance to diagenesis, because they form during the early stages of an individual’s life and therefore reflect individuals’ infancy and childhood. Our focus in this study was on the period after childhood, when individuals would have been employed in their respective occupations and industries, particularly those skeletal elements that are more resistant to diagenesis and that reflect mean lifetime, such as long bones. Solid cortical bone samples were collected from primarily the mid-proximal anterior diaphysis of femora or other nearby regions in consideration of individual bone preservation.

Osteological analyses were undertaken to estimate osteological sex and age at death using a variety of traditional and established methods. Bone and soil dissolution chemistry and preparation protocol were developed in accordance with those in the literature and were undertaken in a class 100 clean laboratory at the University of Miami, Neptune Isotope Laboratory (UM-NIL). All samples were analyzed via multicollector inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry at UM-NIL. Mean analytical uncertainties for trace element analyses are as follows: P, 0.59%; Ca, 1.21%; As, 3.50%; Ba, 1.21%; and Pb, 1.30%. Diagenesis was mitigated using sample cleaning and collection protocols and evaluated using trace element concentration (As, Ba, Ca, P, and Pb; Ca/P ratio) and isotope ratio (87Sr/86Sr, 206Pb/204Pb, 207Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/206Pb, and 207Pb/206Pb) data from soil and bone samples. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s HYSPLIT Atmospheric Transport and Dispersion Modeling System was used to evaluate the movement of particulates via air mass.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were undertaken in IBM SPSS Statistics version 28. Descriptive statistics were calculated for each trace element pollutant for the entire sample, as well as by meaningful subgroups (osteological sex, age groups, and location) to explore variation across populations. Normality tests indicated that all variables cannot be assumed to come from normally distributed populations (Shapiro-Wilk test, P < 0.05). Accordingly, nonparametric analyses were used to evaluate whether there were differences in trace element pollutant exposure and isotope data by environment and aspects of biosocial identity. Spearman correlation tests (a measure of the strength and direction of association between two ranked variables) were used to explore the relationship between age and trace element concentrations. Comparisons were also made between age categories and trace element concentration using Kruskal-Wallis tests. Last, the differences in trace element concentration between locations and osteological sexes were tested via Mann-Whitney U tests.
