Acid-base bifunctional porous liquids
Authors: Liqi Qiu, Tao Wang, Hongjun Liu, Arvind Ganesan, Errui Li, Kevin Michael Siniard, Jian Zhi Hu, Lilin He, Gergely Nagy, Alexander S. Ivanov, Xin Wang, Leighanne C. Gallington, Victoria Cooley, De-en Jiang, Shannon M. Mahurin, Yong Wang, Zhenzhen Yang, Sheng Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Basic solutions are essential for gas-involved catalytic transformations, yet their ultralow free volume limits gas solubility and diffusivity. Engineering permanent porosity in such reactive liquids has remained a longstanding challenge. Here, we introduce a surface-sacrifice strategy that incorporates Brønsted-acidic zeolite nanocrystals into strong organic bases to create permanently porous reactive liquids. Stereochemically controlled acid–base neutralization at the external zeolite surface forms a thin ionic solvation shell that stabilizes the dispersion while fully preserving the internal microporosity and crystallinity of the zeolite framework. The resulting basic media exhibit persistent microporosity, confirmed by inert-gas sorption, 129Xe nuclear magnetic resonance, neutron scattering, and theoretical simulations. These porous liquids show significantly enhanced catalytic performance in CO2 conversion and hydrogenation reactions. The coexisting acidic and basic sites further enable antagonistic cascade catalysis within a single liquid phase. This surface-sacrifice approach provides a general route to introduce permanent porosity into reactive media, enabling boosted gas transformations.

Introduction

Gas–liquid reactions play a central role in many catalytic transformations in chemical manufacturing and energy conversion, including hydrogenation, CO2 utilization, and electrocatalytic processes1,2. However, their efficiency is often limited by the inherently low solubility and slow diffusion of gaseous reactants in liquid media, which restricts mass transport to catalytic sites and frequently necessitates elevated pressures or complex reactor designs3. In such systems, physicochemical parameters, including polarity and particularly available free volume, strongly influence gas solubility, diffusion, and transport efficiency4,5. Strategies such as introducing bulky substituents or disrupting molecular packing can modestly increase free volume, yet conventional liquids fundamentally lack the permanent porosity characteristic of rigid porous frameworks such as metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and zeolites, which exhibit superior gas storage and transport capabilities6,7. Improving gas uptake and transport remains particularly challenging in strongly reactive liquid media, where the absence of permanent and accessible porosity often limits the amount of dissolved gas available near catalytic sites and may require elevated pressures to achieve sufficient reactant concentrations8,9. Such limitations affect many base-mediated catalytic reactions involving gaseous substrates. For example, transformations such as the isomerization of terminal alkynes or the hydrogenation of aromatic nitriles rely on sufficient dissolved H2 concentrations to control catalytic efficiency and selectivity10,11. Developing strategies that enhance gas solubility in reactive basic liquids while preserving their chemical functionality, therefore, remains an important and largely unexplored challenge.

Porous liquids (PLs), which integrate permanent porosity into fluid media12–14, have advanced considerably since their conceptual proposal in 2007 and first synthesis in 201515–17, with applications spanning gas storage, separation, and catalysis16,18–29. For example, microporous water, generated by dispersing nanocrystals with hydrophobic cavities (e.g., silicalite-1) in water, enhances electrocatalysis by increasing local O2 concentration25. Macroporous water constructed from surface-modified hollow carbon spheres exhibits over sixfold higher gas uptake and largely improves the oxygen reduction reaction26. In hydrogenation28 and CO2 conversion reactions29, PLs could enhance local H2 and CO2 concentration near catalytic sites, leading to markedly improved gas diffusion and catalytic efficiency. Despite these advances, current PL systems are largely restricted to neutral media, including bulky organic solvents27, ionic liquids (ILs)24,29, and water25,26, limiting their utility under strongly acidic or basic conditions. Developing robust PLs that retain permanent porosity in harsh reactive environments remains a grand challenge.

Here, we report a surface-sacrifice strategy to introduce permanent porosity into dense organic base media by incorporating Brønsted-acidic zeolite nanocrystals, creating a class of porous reactive liquids with markedly enhanced performance in gas-involved transformations (Fig. 1). Selective acid–base neutralization occurs at the zeolite external surface, forming a thin ionic solvation shell that stabilizes dispersions of negatively charged zeolites, prevents pore blockage, and preserves both internal porosity and acidic protons. This structural integrity is confirmed by density measurements, inert-gas sorption, 129Xe nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, neutron scattering, titration, and theoretical modeling. Catalytically, the porous liquid base delivers over fourfold improvement in benzonitrile hydrogenation and CO2 conversion. The coexistence of Brønsted-acidic and basic sites further enables antagonistic cascade catalysis, exemplified by a one-pot deacetalization–Knoevenagel condensation in a single liquid phase. The surface-sacrifice strategy is broadly applicable to diverse basic media and rigid porous frameworks with tunable porosity. This work establishes a generalizable approach for embedding permanent porosity into dense reactive liquids, providing a versatile platform to substantially enhance gas-involved reactions under mild conditions. The central hypothesis of this work is that selective surface acid–base neutralization can create a stabilizing interfacial solvation shell while preserving the internal porosity of rigid porous hosts in highly reactive liquid media. Results and discussion

Design principles for engineering acidic zeolites in basic solutions

To evaluate this hypothesis, we investigated whether stable PL formation could be achieved in a strongly basic medium while preserving the structural integrity of the porous host. As a representative system, 1,8-diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec-7-ene (DBU, pKa in CH3CN = 24.54), a widely used strong organic base and solvent, was selected as the liquid medium30,31. Constructing a DBU-based PL is challenging due to its strong basicity, low viscosity, and lack of ionic functionality, which can lead to degradation and aggregation of the porous host23,32. Two design criteria were employed: (1) choosing porous hosts with pore sizes smaller than the DBU kinetic dimensions (9.31 Å × 7.37 Å × 5.68 Å, Supplementary Figs. 1, 2), establishing strong liquid–solid surface interactions to ensure uniform and long-term stable dispersion. Therefore, protonated Zeolite Socony Mobil–5 (H-ZSM-5) was selected to provide permanent porosity (Supplementary Fig. 2). Its micropores exclude DBU from entering the cavities while surface Brønsted-acidic protons can undergo neutralization with the DBU base, forming ionic pairs at the external surface that stabilize the zeolite dispersion (Fig. 1). H-ZSM-5 was synthesized via hydrothermal crystallization followed by ion exchange, with tailored modifications to achieve a uniform particle size around 200 nm and high dispersity. The resulting material exhibited a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of 397 m2 g−1, predominantly from micropores around 0.76 nm (Supplementary Figs. 3–5), and high crystallinity as confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern (Supplementary Fig. 6). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images further confirmed uniform particle size of H-ZSM-5 around 200 nm (Supplementary Fig. 7). The PL was prepared by dispersing H-ZSM-5 (30 wt%) in an acetonitrile solution of DBU, followed by solvent removal to yield porous base solution (denoted as PL-DBU) (Fig. 2a). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) under air indicated a final zeolite loading amount of 28.7 wt% in PL-DBU (Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). The viscosity of the PL-DBU system increases with zeolite loading, from 0.035 Pa·s (20 wt%) to 0.073 Pa·s (30 wt%) and 0.102 Pa·s (40 wt%), compared to 0.014 Pa·s for pure DBU (Supplementary Fig. 10). The dispersions exhibit nearly shear-independent behavior, indicating stable flow characteristics. A 30 wt% loading was therefore selected as a balance between dispersion stability and manageable viscosity. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis of PL-DBU showed a narrow particle size distribution around 200 nm (Fig. 2b), indicating uniform zeolite dispersion with minimal aggregation. Upon PL-DBU formation, the crystalline structure of H-ZSM-5 was well preserved, as evidenced by the XRD pattern of PL-DBU, which retained characteristic peaks at 2θ = 7.98°, 8.82°, and 23.14°, corresponding to the (011), (020), and (051) planes of the Mobil Five (MFI) framework (Fig. 2c)33. These reflections correspond to interplanar spacings of 11.1, 10.0, and 3.8 Å, as calculated using Bragg’s Law. X-ray scattering further confirmed that the H-ZSM-5 framework remained well-retained within the liquid basic medium (Supplementary Fig. 12). The local structures of H-ZSM-5, DBU, and PL-DBU (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Figs. 13−15) were probed via synchrotron X-ray atomic pair distribution function (PDF) analysis34. For pristine H-ZSM-5, the PDF peak at 1.47–1.74 Å corresponds to the average Si–O and Al–O bond lengths, while the peak at ~2.5 Å arises from O–O correlations (Fig. 2d), consistent with simulated radial distribution functions from the crystallographic model (Supplementary Fig. 16). In PL-DBU, phase and amplitude changes between 2–5 Å indicate strong interactions between H-ZSM-5 and DBU, with peak shifts reflecting perturbations in the local chemical environment of DBU, possibly arising from neutralization of DBU with acidic hydroxyl groups on the zeolite surface.

Small-angle neutron scattering (SANS) was employed to probe the real-space mesoscale structure, nanoparticle dispersion and interfacial interactions in DBU, H-ZSM-5 and PL-DBU (Fig. 2e)35,36. Pure DBU exhibited a featureless profile, consistent with a homogeneous liquid lacking ordered local structure. H-ZSM-5 displayed sharp high-q peaks at 0.56, 0.64, and 1.71 Å−1, corresponding to characteristic distances of 11.2, 9.8, and 3.8 Å, in agreement with XRD data (Fig. 2c) and reflective of its crystalline microporous framework. A low-q slope of –3.85 indicated relatively smooth granule surfaces of H-ZSM-5 (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 17). Upon formation of PL-DBU, the high-q features were retained, confirming preservation of the intrinsic zeolite microporous framework, while the low-q slope changed to –4.17 (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 17), suggesting formation of a modified interfacial region due to DBU interactions with zeolite. The combination of change of power law decay and well-preserved high-q peaks indicates strong DBU–zeolite surface interactions, which stabilize the dispersion and suppress zeolite particle aggregation. Given that the PL with air–filled pores contributed a larger volume than a solvent-filled system23, density measurements were employed as an initial indicator of preserved pore volume in PL-DBU (Fig. 2f). Mixtures of DBU with varying H-ZSM-5 content (0–15 vol%) were measured, with density values obtained from three parallel measurements and presented with corresponding error bars. The observed density trends with increasing zeolite loading remain consistent with predictions assuming air-filled (no solvent filling) micropores, calculated from the intrinsic density and pore volume of the zeolite. Although density measurements alone do not conclusively establish pore accessibility, these results provide supporting evidence that the zeolite micropores are likely not occupied by the liquid phase and are consistent with PL formation.

Probing permanent porosity and interfacial acid–base interactions

Having established the structural stability of PL-DBU, we next sought to determine whether the internal pores remain accessible and identify the interfacial interactions responsible for maintaining stable dispersion. To directly probe the origin and accessibility of the internal porosity, we next employed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to examine interfacial structure and pore exclusion at the molecular level. MD simulations were performed using a model system comprising an H-ZSM-5 slab and 120 DBU molecules in a periodic box to probe interfacial behavior. As shown by the representative snapshot (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 18) of the MD trajectory after reaching equilibrium, DBU molecules preferentially accumulate near the external surface of H-ZSM-5, while steric constraints prevent their entry into the internal micropores (Fig. 3b). In addition, the DBU molecules do show inhomogeneous distribution at the interface with the H-ZSM-5 surface, not due to the intermolecular H-bonding and cluster formation37,38, but because of the interaction with the surface. The accessibility of these preserved micropores was further validated experimentally using 129Xe NMR spectroscopy, a highly sensitive probe of local free volume and confinement. 129Xe NMR exploits the pronounced chemical-shift sensitivity of Xe to its confinement environment39,40. In dense liquids, Xe experiences restricted mobility, whereas the formation of accessible cavities, as in PLs, induces an upfield shift due to reduced steric confinement. For Xe-saturated DBU, a resonance at 195 ppm was observed (Fig. 3c). Introduction of H-ZSM-5 to form PL-DBU shifted the Xe signal upfield to 190 ppm, with only a single resonance observed because Xe undergoes rapid exchange between dissolved Xe in the liquid phase and Xe occupying accessible pore environments on the NMR timescale. Under this fast-exchange regime, the measured chemical shift represents an averaged Xe environment rather than separately resolved liquid- and pore-confined Xe populations. The observed upfield shift indicates that Xe experiences a less sterically constrained average environment, consistent with Xe sampling accessible free volume within the porous framework and providing evidence for enhanced internal free volume in PL-DBU.

Having established that the internal pores remain accessible, we next probed the interfacial interactions between DBU and the zeolite framework to elucidate the mechanisms underlying stable dispersion. The acidity of zeolite was probed by ammonia temperature-programmed desorption (NH3-TPD) (Supplementary Fig. 19). After PL-DBU formation, solid-state NMR analysis reveals that PL-DBU contains both free and protonated DBU species. To distinguish these environments, single-pulse (SP) and cross-polarization (CP) 13C magic-angle spinning (MAS) NMR were employed due to their complementary sensitivity to molecular mobility (Fig. 3d). SP 13C MAS NMR quantitatively detects all carbon species regardless of their motional state, allowing observation of both mobile DBU molecules in the liquid phase and more rigid species that are either interacting with the solid surface or present in the solid state. In contrast, CP 13C MAS NMR relies on efficient 1H–13C dipolar coupling and therefore selectively enhances signals from motion-restricted species, whereas highly mobile molecules whose dipolar interactions are averaged to near zero, such as those in the liquid phase, contribute little or no signal to the CP spectrum. Consequently, the CP spectrum preferentially detects DBU species immobilized through interactions with the zeolite surface, including the 165 ppm C = NH⁺ resonance of protonated [DBUH]+ formed at external zeolitic Brønsted acid sites (Fig. 3d). Complementary 1H NMR identifies a 9.7 ppm C = NH⁺ proton, further confirming proton transfer (Fig. 3e). The combined SP and CP measurements therefore distinguish freely mobile DBU in the liquid phase from surface-associated or immobilized DBU species, providing direct spectroscopic evidence for DBU–zeolite interfacial interactions.

A control experiment was performed to assess the role of surface acid–base interactions in stabilizing H-ZSM-5 dispersions in DBU (Supplementary Fig. 20). Both Na-ZSM-5 and H-ZSM-5 initially formed stable dispersions in acetonitrile after ultrasonication, and the addition of DBU had minimal immediate effect. Comparatively, after 1 h of standing, the Na-ZSM-5/DBU dispersion showed pronounced white precipitation, indicating zeolite aggregation, whereas H-ZSM-5/DBU remained homogeneous. Therefore, unlike conventional PLs, which are limited to neutral conditions, PL-DBU leverages interfacial interactions between H-ZSM-5 Brønsted sites and basic DBU to form abundant ionic pairs at the external surface, as supported by the formation of protonated [DBUH]+ species identified by solid-state 13C and 1H NMR, acting as a robust solvation shell surrounding H-ZSM-5 nanoparticles and maintaining dispersion stability in highly basic environments.

Creation and tuning of permanent porosity across diverse basic media

After successfully constructing DBU-derived PLs via the surface-sacrificing strategy, we next examined the generality of this approach by extending it to basic media with different molecular structures, basicity, and polarity, to further tailor permanent porosity across various basic media. Diverse basic media possessing different basicity (see details in Supplementary Table 2), size, and polarity with DBU such as 1,1,3,3-tetramethylguanidine (TMG, pKa (in CH3CN) = 22.48), 1,5-diazabicyclo[4.3.0]non-5-ene (DBN, pKa = 23.49), phosphazene base P1-t-Bu-tris(tetramethylene) (PtBu, pKa = 25.46), primary amine (poly(propylene glycol) bis(2-aminopropyl ether) (PPG-2NH2, pKa = 17.04)30, and basic (trihexyl(tetradecyl)phosphonium hydroxide) ([P66614][OH]) were explored (Fig. 4a), and the resulting solution with H-ZSM-5 (30 wt%) was denoted as PL-TMG, PL-DBN, PL-PtBu, PL-PPG-2NH2, and PL-[P66614][OH] respectively. Then, XRD patterns, SANS profiles, gas uptake isotherms, and NMR detection were subsequently employed to assess the structural integrity of the PLs and to probe their crystallinity, porosity, and interfacial ionic characteristics. Optical images of these PLs revealed a homogeneous and stable colloidal solution formation (Supplementary Fig. 21). XRD analyses show that the crystalline framework of H-ZSM-5 remains intact across a broad spectrum of basic media—from moderately basic TMG and DBN to strongly basic PtBu—as well as in linear amines (PPG-2NH2) and highly ionic environments such as [P66614][OH] (Fig. 4b). Complementary SANS measurements confirm that zeolite-derived porosity is preserved in all PL formulations, with high-q peaks at 0.56, 0.64, and 1.71 Å−1 corresponding to the characteristic micropore features of H-ZSM-5 (Fig. 4c). Notably, PL systems exhibiting nearly identical low-q slopes (Porod exponents approaching 4; Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 22) display high dispersion stability, consistent with suppressed aggregation and well-defined particle interfaces. The porosity tunability in basic media was further examined using a layered Mobil Twenty-Two (MWW) framework type zeolite (H-MCM22) combined with DBU, forming the PL-DBU/H-MCM22 system (Supplementary Fig. 23). H-MCM22 exhibited a BET surface area of 386 m2 g−1, microporosity of 0.58 nm (Supplementary Fig. 24), and characteristic PXRD reflections at 2θ = 7.2°, 8.2°, and 9.8°, corresponding to the (100), (101), and (102) planes, respectively (Fig. 4b). The H-MCM22 framework maintained its crystallinity after PL formation. SANS profiles of both solid H-MCM22 and liquid PL-DBU/H-MCM22 displayed distinct peaks at q ≈ 0.25, 0.52, 0.58, and 0.72 Å−1, corresponding to real-space distances of 25.1, 12.1, 10.8, and 8.7 Å (Fig. 4c). These features reflect the ∼25 Å unit-cell-level cage and ∼13 Å intralayer cavity of the H-MCM22 framework, demonstrating that its intrinsic microporosity and intralayer order are preserved after PL formation (Supplementary Fig. 25). The decrease in low-q intensity is attributed to the reduced contrast between the zeolite particles and the surrounding medium (Supplementary Fig. 26). The preserved high-q features further indicate the high stability of the PLs, even in the presence of strong interfacial acid–base interactions between DBU and H-MCM22.

Probing the accessibility of internal acidic sites in basic environments

After demonstrating preserved framework integrity and pore accessibility, we next investigated whether internal Brønsted acidic sites remain chemically accessible within the basic PL environment. Given that SANS profiles, density measurements, 129Xe NMR, and molecular simulations (Fig. 2e, f and 3a–c) show steric exclusion of basic media such as DBU from internal micropores of H-ZSM-5, it is reasonable to conclude that the internal Brønsted acidic sites within H-ZSM-5 are preserved and chemically intact in PL-DBU. Conventional acidity probes such as NH3-TPD and pyridine IR are unsuitable for PL-DBU because the liquid environment and strong DBU–zeolite interactions obscure the characteristic signatures of adsorbed pyridine41. As confirmed by MD simulations, pyridine (6.5 Å × 6.5 Å × 3.4 Å, Supplementary Fig. 27) exhibited a ~ 1.0 nm penetration depth (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29) in H-ZSM-5, providing sufficient accessibility to probe internal Brønsted acidic sites in PL-DBU. 15N-labeled pyridine (15N-Py) serves as a sensitive NMR probe of internal Brønsted acid sites, as protonation of pyridine generally induces a characteristic downfield 15N chemical shift reflecting local acidity42. To directly probe the internal Brønsted acid sites, we employed solid-state 1H NMR spectroscopy, which is highly sensitive to protons associated with the canonical Brønsted acid sites (Si–OH–Al) in zeolites43. As shown in Fig. 5c, dehydrated H-ZSM-5 shows the characteristic 1.7 ppm silanol signal and a 4.3 ppm resonance from Si–OH–Al groups. These features are absent in neat DBU and [P66614][OH], confirming that the liquid bases do not obscure the zeolite signatures (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31). After removing excess base, both PL-DBU and PL-[P66614][OH] retain the 4.3 ppm signal, demonstrating that internal Brønsted acid sites remain accessible within the basic medium. PL-DBU additionally shows the 9.7 ppm [DBUH]⁺ resonance (Fig. 3e), while PL-[P66614][OH] exhibits a broad 5.0–5.5 ppm feature attributed to water formed via surface neutralization through a surface-sacrificing mechanism (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, NMR results revealed that the formed solvation shell, composed of [DBUH]⁺ and −[O–ZSM-5], further modifies the zeolite surface and suppresses water diffusion (Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33).

Enhanced gas solubility in basic media via porosity engineering

Having confirmed preserved porosity and internal accessibility, we next examined the functional consequences of porosity engineering on gas uptake behavior. By introducing porosity into the liquid phase of PLs, the free volume of the media is significantly increased, thereby greatly enhancing gas solubility. In PL-DBU, the strong basicity of DBU renders acidic gases unsuitable (e.g., CO2), so inert N2 was selected for its kinetic diameter (3.64 Å) smaller than the H-ZSM-5 micropores (0.76 nm, Supplementary Fig. 5), allowing unhindered diffusion into the internal framework. Pressure-swing adsorption up to 80 bar shows that PL-DBU (30 wt% H-ZSM-5) exhibits a > 10-fold increase in N2 uptake relative to neat DBU, reaching 32.6 cm3 g−1 at 80 bar, with 93–96% of adsorption consistently arising from the zeolite cavities (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 34). Similar behavior is observed for PL-PPG-2NH2, where the N2 uptake increases linearly with zeolite loading and reaches 49.2 cm3 g−1 at 40 wt% (Fig. 6b). Across the 20–40 wt% loading range, the zeolite cavities consistently account for approximately 99.99% of the total uptake (Fig. 6c). At a common loading of 30 wt%, both PL-DBU and PL-PPG-2NH2 achieve comparable capacities (~33 cm3 g−1). Porosity engineering in PPG-2NH2 is further verified using CH4 as a probe: while pristine PPG-2NH2 adsorbs virtually no CH4, the corresponding PL-PPG-2NH2 displays 10- to 27-fold enhancements at 0.5 and 1 bar (Fig. 6d), exceeding reported COF-based PLs44. 129Xe NMR spectroscopy further confirmed the increased free volume and the successful incorporation of permanent porosity in PL-PPG-2NH2 (Supplementary Fig. 35).Fig. 6Characterization of permanent porosity in liquid amine-derived PL.a N2 uptake isotherm of DBU and PL-DBU at 298 K. b N2 uptake isotherms of PPG-2NH2 and PL-PPG-2NH2 at 298 K. c N2 Uptake of PPG-2NH2 with varying zeolite loadings and corresponding zeolite contributions. d CH4 uptake isotherms of PPG-2NH2 and PL-PPG-2NH2 at 298 K. Boosted gas conversion and antagonistic cascade catalysis

To determine whether the enhanced gas uptake and preserved multifunctional environments translate into practical advantages, catalytic reactions involving gaseous reactants and cascade transformations were evaluated. The introduction of permanent porosity into organic base media establishes these PL systems as powerful platforms for gas-involved catalysis by enhancing gas solubility and mass transport18. This capability is clearly demonstrated in nitrile hydrogenation, a key route to primary amines45, where the benzonitrile-to-benzylamine transformation typically relies on elevated H2 pressures and strong bases11. PL-DBU exhibits substantially higher N2 solubility than neat DBU and, given the smaller kinetic diameter of H2, is expected to further facilitate H2 uptake. PL-DBU exhibits substantially higher N2 solubility than neat DBU (Fig. 6a) and, given the smaller kinetic diameter of H2, is expected to further enhance H2 uptake. Under Ru-catalyzed conditions (H2, 2 MPa, 100 °C, 3 h), PL-DBU with 30 wt% H-ZSM-5 achieves 94% conversion and 80% yield of benzylamine, significantly outperforming pure DBU (30% conversion, 19% yield) (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 3). Notably, the hydrogenation in the PL-DBU system proceeds under 2 MPa H2 and a 3 h reaction time, compared with previously reported studies employing higher H2 pressures (5 MPa) and/or longer reaction times (5.5 h)11,46, while delivering superior catalytic performance, highlighting the efficiency of PL-enabled gas utilization. In contrast, DBU containing 30 wt% Na-ZSM-5 shows only 41% conversion and 24% yield, due to phase separation that limits interfacial mass transport and suppresses catalytic efficiency. Beyond increasing free volume, the surface-sacrifice strategy stabilizes zeolite dispersions via ionic-pair formation, preserving coexisting acidic and basic sites within a homogeneous phase. This bifunctionality enables multistep transformations such as CO2 carboxylation47. The PL-PPG-2NH2 system, combining high CO2 uptake, surface ionic pairs, and residual H-ZSM-5 acidity, catalyzes the carboxylation of 2-amino-3-fluorobenzonitrile under CO2 (1 MPa, 100 °C), achieving 92% conversion and 78% yield, compared to 43% conversion and 20% yield in neat PPG-2NH2 (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Table 4). Notably, these enhancements are achieved under identical gas pressures, indicating that improved performance may arise from improved local gas accessibility and transport within the PL system, rather than increased external pressure. The coexistence of accessible acid–base sites and confined microenvironments may contribute to the observed catalytic enhancement, highlighting the promise of PL platforms for sustainable and selective catalysis.

Furthermore, harnessing zeolite acid sites in basic media enables one-pot cascade catalysis. In the deacetalization–Knoevenagel reaction (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Table 5), nanosheet H-MCM22 (Si/Al = 15) combined with [P66614][OH] to form PL-[P66614][OH] achieves 98% conversion and 95% yield of benzylidene malononitrile, whereas H-MCM22 alone (98% conversion, 10% yield), [P66614][OH] alone (24% conversion, 19% yield), and their physical mixture (93% conversion, 56% yield) show significantly inferior performance. Based on these catalytic results and control experiments, the reaction is proposed to proceed across multiple spatial domains (Supplementary Fig. 36). First, in the zeolite micropores (acid catalysis), Brønsted acid sites catalyze the deacetalization step, affording up to 96% yield of benzaldehyde from benzaldehyde dimethyl acetal. Second, in the bulk liquid phase (base catalysis), the dissolved superbase ([P66614][OH]) promotes the Knoevenagel condensation, yielding benzylidene malononitrile in up to 98% yield from benzaldehyde. Third, at the solid–liquid interface (cooperative region), the close proximity of acid and base sites facilitates intermediate transfer and enhances overall catalytic efficiency. As a result, this spatially separated yet cooperative mechanism, involving micropores, bulk liquid, and interfacial regions, may contribute to efficient tandem catalysis and accounts for the superior performance of the PL system.

Liquid-phase media are indispensable in catalysis, yet their intrinsic dense nature restricts gas dissolution and transport, imposing fundamental limits on gas-involved reactivity. By integrating acidic zeolite nanocrystals into strong organic bases via a surface-sacrifice strategy, we generate reactive liquids that maintain their chemical functionality while acquiring permanent, accessible porosity. Multimodal characterization confirms enhanced gas uptake and preservation of active sites, translating into substantially improved catalytic performance. The concurrent presence of acidic and basic sites further enables antagonistic cascade transformations within a single fluid phase. Owing to its modular design, this approach can be extended to diverse liquid matrices and porous frameworks, offering a general route to engineer mobile media with solid-like porosity. This strategy broadens the design space for next-generation catalytic liquids, enabling more efficient gas conversion and multistep transformations.

The tests were carried out at a controlled temperature using the Peltier plate system, and the viscosity was recorded as a function of shear rate. Additional rheological procedures, including frequency sweep and time sweep measurements, were also conducted to evaluate the viscoelastic stability of the system.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Classical MD simulations were carried out with the GROMACS package51. DBU were modeled with the general Amber force field (GAFF)52. The H-ZSM-5 zeolite model from MFI P1 structure was parameterized based on the INTERFACE force field53 with 2 T14 site Si atoms (the Si-Si distance is around 13 Å) replaced by Al to reach a Si/Al ratio of 47:154. Bronsted acid H atoms were added to Bronsted acid O sites. Surface cleavage along the (1 0 0) direction leads to a sinusoidal channel and along the (0 1 0) direction to a straight channel. Dangling Si–O bonds and under-coordinated Si sites were saturated with H and OH groups, respectively. A 2 × 2 × 2 H-ZSM-5 slab supercell was employed to model the interface between DBU and the zeolite surface. A total of 120 DBU molecules were inserted between slabs to generate a sufficiently thick liquid region and establish bulk-like fluid behavior away from the interface under periodic boundary conditions. The model was designed to probe local interfacial interactions and pore accessibility rather than reproduce the macroscopic composition of the porous liquid system. Following geometry optimization, the system was equilibrated for 100 ns under the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 bar, followed by an additional 100 ns production simulation under the NVT ensemble for structural analysis.

Synthesis of H-ZSM-5

TPAOH solution (25 wt%, 6.1 g) was initially mixed with deionized water (9.6 g), followed by the addition of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 6.25 g). The mixture was stirred at 80 °C for 24 h to form a silica precursor. In parallel, aluminum nitrate nonahydrate (Al(NO3)3 9H2O, 0.23 g) and NaOH (0.12 g) were dissolved in water (2 g) to generate the alumina source, which was introduced dropwise into the silica precursor under stirring. The resulting gel was transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and crystallized at 170 °C for 24 h. The solids were isolated by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with water and ethanol, and dried under vacuum at 100 °C overnight. Calcination at 550 °C for 6 h (1 °C min−1) yielded Na-ZSM-5. Proton-exchange with aqueous NH4Cl followed by a second calcination at 550 °C afforded the H-ZSM-5 form.

Synthesis of [P66614[OH]

An anion-exchange resin in the hydroxide form (Amber Rec UP550 OH) was packed into a column. Trihexyl(tetradecyl)phosphonium chloride [P66614]Cl (5.09 g, 10.0 mmol) was dissolved in absolute ethanol (50 mL) in a 100 mL round-bottom flask to obtain a clear solution. The solution was then passed through the hydroxide-form resin column at room temperature at a moderate flow rate. After loading, the column was rinsed with additional ethanol (20–30 mL), and the combined eluates were collected. The solvent was removed under reduced pressure at 50 °C, and the resulting viscous residue was dried under high vacuum at 50 °C overnight, affording [P66614[OH] IL. The absence of chloride in the final product was confirmed by the AgNO3 test (no AgCl precipitation).

Synthesis of porous basic solutions

The as-prepared H-ZSM-5 (300 mg) was dispersed in acetonitrile (3 mL), stirred for 1 h, and then sonicated for another 1 h. DBU (700 mg) was added and sonicated for 1 h to form a uniform solution, which was then combined with the H-ZSM-5 dispersion. The mixture was further stirred overnight at room temperature to yield a stable suspension. The resulting homogeneous mixture was concentrated using a rotary evaporator, and the porous liquid was obtained after drying under vacuum at 60 °C overnight. The final product was designated as 30 wt% H-ZSM-5 in DBU. PL-TMG, PL-DBN, PL-PtBu, PL-PPG-2NH2, and PL-[P66614][OH] samples were prepared following analogous procedures.

Synthesis of [DBUH][Br]

In a 50 mL round-bottom flask, DBU (1.52 g, 10.0 mmol) was cooled in an ice bath and stirred with a magnetic bar. A 48 wt% aqueous HBr solution (≈1.1 mL, 10.0 mmol) was added dropwise over 5–10 min under vigorous stirring. After complete addition, the mixture was allowed to warm to room temperature and stirred for 1 h to ensure full protonation. The resulting material was dried under high vacuum at 60 °C overnight to afford [DBUH][Br], which was used without further purification.

Synthesis of H-MCM22

MCM-22 was synthesized following a gel composition of H2O: NaOH: NaAlO2: hexamethyleneimine: dicyclohexylamine: SiO2 = 18: 0.1: 1/15: 0.3: 0.6: 1. Deionized water, NaOH, hexamethyleneimine, and dicyclohexylamine were added sequentially under stirring until fully dissolved, after which NaAlO2 was introduced and dissolved completely. Silica sol was added dropwise to the precursor mixture, which was stirred at room temperature for 4 h and subsequently transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave. Crystallization proceeded at 150 °C for 5 days. The solids were collected by centrifugation, washed with deionized water, and dried under vaccum at 110 °C overnight. Calcination at 550 °C for 6 h (1 °C min−1) yielded Na-MCM-22. Ion exchange with NH4Cl and a second calcination produced the H-form MCM-22.
