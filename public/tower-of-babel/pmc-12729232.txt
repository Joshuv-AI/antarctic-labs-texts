Identification of coagulation-related hub genes in ischemic stroke based on bioinformatics integration analysis and investigation of their immune regulatory mechanisms
Authors: Hepeng Li, Junli Liang, Yuan Lan, Menghua Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIschemic stroke (IS) is a cerebrovascular disease resulting from insufficient blood supply to specific areas of the brain, often due to atherosclerosis and thrombosis. While the association between polymorphisms in coagulation-related genes (CRGs) and thrombosis has been suggested, the precise relationship between CRGs and IS remains unclear and requires further exploration.MethodsThis study utilized IS-related datasets (GSE16561, GSE9877, GSE58294, GSE224273) and CRGs to identify differentially expressed genes (IS-DEGs) through differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed coagulation-related genes (DE-CRGs) were determined by overlapping CRGs and IS-DEGs. Machine learning models, including random forest, gradient boosting decision trees, and support vector machines, were applied to screen for potential hub genes. These candidate hub genes were then validated using an independent dataset (GSE9877) and confirmed by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) in clinical samples from IS patients. Single-cell RNA-seq of GSE224273 was processed via Seurat to map hub-gene expression across endothelial, smooth-muscle, macrophage, monocyte, T, NK, and B-cell clusters.ResultsA total of 18 DE-CRGs were acquired by overlapping 450 IS-DEGs and 203 CRGs. Furthermore, 517 cluster-differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 1,021 coagulation-score-RGs were obtained based on 18 DE-CRGs. The 29 candidate genes were obtained by overlapping IS-DEGs, cluster-DEGs and coagulation-score-related genes (RGs), five hub genes (REPS2, LIME1, MYH9, C5AR1, ECHDC2) were successfully screened. Subsequently, in GSE9877 dataset, only REPS2, MYH9, C5AR1 had the same expression tendency with that in GSE16561 dataset, while our RT-qPCR results suggested that the expression trend of LIME1, MYH9, C5AR1, ECHDC2 were consistent with that in GSE16561 dataset besides REPS2. Single-cell analysis localized MYH9 predominantly to smooth-muscle and endothelial cells, whereas C5AR1 was enriched in macrophages and monocytes, with both genes significantly upregulated in IS-derived cells. Two hub genes, C5AR1 and MYH9, were eventually selected from this group. Further analyses showed that these genes are significantly enriched in pathways related to complement and coagulation cascades and platelet activation.ConclusionThe two hub genes, C5AR1 and MYH9, may contribute to the development of IS by promoting thrombosis. These findings offer new insights into the molecular mechanisms of IS and suggest potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for IS management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03477-4.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) is a cerebrovascular disease caused by ischemia and hypoxia in specific areas of the brain due to insufficient blood. Atherosclerosis and thrombosis leading to arterial obstruction or stenosis are important causes of IS [47]. Currently, effective treatments for IS include intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombolysis. Thrombolysis involves clot-dissolving medications, while mechanical thrombectomy physically removes the blood clot [2]. However, thrombolysis must be done within a specific time window, carries a risk of bleeding, and requires careful risk–benefit assessment [55]. Mechanical thrombectomy requires skilled operators and carries risks such as vessel injury. At present, the diagnosis of IS mainly relies on clinical evaluation and neuroimaging examinations [2, 55], but these methods have significant limitations. CT scanning has insufficient sensitivity to early ischemic changes. Although MRI is more accurate, it requires high equipment requirements and may be contraindicated or unusable for certain patients. More critically, these techniques cannot reflect key molecular mechanisms such as thrombosis and inflammatory response [26, 38]. Biomarkers have great potential in the diagnosis and prognostic evaluation of IS [29]. They can be quickly detected in the early stages of stroke, have high sensitivity and specificity, reflect the pathophysiological mechanisms of stroke, are easy to obtain and detect, and are cost-effective.

Therefore, to explore the pathogenesis and new diagnostic biomarkers of IS is essential for its effective treatment.

Recently, the relationship between the polymorphism of clotting CRGs and thrombosis has gained research attention. These polymorphisms involve different alleles or variants, impacting the regulation of the coagulation system and clot formation. For example, rs1042579 (c.1418C > T) is a mutation on a CRGs associated with increased thrombosis risk. This mutation alters thrombomodulin function, leading to heightened clot formation. Other gene polymorphisms like rs2289252, rs2036914, rs2066865, and rs13146272 also correlate with thrombosis risk [21]. Genetic variants in certain CRGs (coagulation factor VII, platelet membrane glycoprotein IIIa) have been found to increase the risk of thrombosis, and polymorphisms in these genes may lead to the formation of a procoagulant state by affecting coagulation factor activity, anticoagulant protein function or platelet function [12, 70]. However, the expression level and functional role of CRGs differ among different stroke subtypes. For example, Vincent et al. found that certain CRGs were significantly increased in cardioembolic and large atherosclerotic stroke, but not in arteriolar occlusion [58]. This suggests that different stroke subtypes may involve different coagulation mechanisms and expression patterns of CRGs. Some studies have explored the relationship between the expression level of CRGs and the prognosis of patients with IS. For example, polymorphisms in some CRGs (e. g., thrombin regulatory protein, protein C) are associated with poor functional recovery after stroke [25, 44]. These studies suggest that CRGs not only play a role in the pathogenesis of stroke, but may also be potential biomarkers for prognostic assessment. However, the mechanism by which they lead to IS remains unclear.

This study used IS-related datasets (GSE16561 and GSE9877) and CRGs to screen CRGs that related to thrombosis and IS, and qPCR validation was performed in peripheral blood collected from patients with IS. Through differential gene expression analysis, clustering analysis, and coagulation score analysis, we identified and verified key genes that related to thrombosis in IS. Further bioinformatics analysis revealed the potential significance of these genes in predicting IS, exploring molecular mechanisms, and guiding treatment approaches.

In this study, we first explored the interaction network between multiple CRGs and their specific role in the mechanism of stroke through multigene integration analysis; second, we used various bioinformatics analysis methods to further deepen the understanding of the role of CRGs in the pathogenesis of IS. Overall, our study provides important insights for understanding the pathogenesis of IS and identifying novel therapeutic targets, thus contributing to the development of personalized medicine.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The transcriptomic data of ischemic stroke (IS) and normal samples were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), specifically from the datasets GSE16561,GSE9877, GSE58294 and GSE224273. The GSE16561 dataset was generated using the GPL6883 platform (Illumina HumanRef-8 v3.0 expression beadchip) and includes gene expression profiles from 63 samples, consisting of 39 IS patients and 24 control samples. All samples were derived from whole blood. The data were initially made public on June 1, 2010, and was last updated on October 4, 2019. The GSE9877 dataset was generated using the GPL96 platform (Affymetrix Human Genome U133A Array) and includes a total of 47 samples, with 20 IS patients and 27 control samples, also derived from whole blood. The dataset was first made available on December 18, 2007, and was last updated on August 10, 2018. The GSE58294 dataset was generated using the GPL570 platform (Affymetrix Human Genome U133 chip) and contains a total of 92 samples, among which 69 were from IS patients and 23 were from the control samples, also sourced from whole blood. The data were obtained on July 22, 2025. The GSE224273 dataset was sequenced using the GPL24676 platform (Illumina NovaSeq 6000), and it contained a total of 6 samples, among which 1 was from an IS patient and 5 were from a control samples. The clinical information is shown in Table S1-3. To identify coagulation-CRGs, 203 genes were extracted from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, specifically from the hsa04610 (complement and coagulation cascades) [https://www.kegg.jp/entry/hsa04610] and hsa04611 (platelet activation) pathways [https://www.kegg.jp/entry/hsa04611].

Data standardization processing

In this study, gene expression data were subjected to standardized preprocessing: first, duplicate genes and missing data were removed, and the data distribution was evaluated. If the data distribution showed excessive discrepancies, log2(dat + 1) transformation was applied. After the logarithmic transformation, the discrepancies were reduced before analysis, which prevented highly expressed genes from dominating the analysis. The data after logarithmic transformation became closer to a normal distribution, and the addition of a pseudo-count (+ 1) addressed the issue of zero expression levels, making the data more approximate to a normal distribution and satisfying the statistical assumptions for subsequent analyses.

Differentially expressed coagulation-CRGs

To identify genes with significantly different expression levels between the IS and normal groups, DEGs (written as IS-DEGs) between IS and control samples in GSE16561 dataset were identified using “limma” (version 3.46.0) package [48]. Genes with adjusted p < 0.05 (adj.p < 0.05) and |Log2 fold change (Log2FC)|> 0.5 [57] were classified into DEGs. “ggplot2” (version 3.3.3) package [20] was utilized to generate the volcano plot. Next, Venn analyses of DEGs and 203 CRGs was operated to obtain DE-CRGs [https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=venn].

Functional enrichment analysis of DE-CRGs

To understand the biological processes, molecular functions, and other aspects that these genes were involved in. The “clusterProfiler” (version 3.18.0) package [64] was used for Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DE-CRGs (adj.p < 0.05 and count ≥ 1) [57]. GO analysis involved biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). A barplot and bubble diagram for visualization were plotted using “enrichPlot” (version 1.10.2) package [https://github.com/guangchuangyu/enrichplot].

Differentially expressed cluster-related genes (cluster-DEGs)

To identify DEGs between coagulation-related subgroups, consensus clustering was performed on the 39 IS samples from the GSE16561 dataset using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package (version 1.54.0) [63]. The parameters for consensus clustering were set as follows: the number of clusters (k) was varied from 2 to 10, and 1000 iterations were performed for each k to ensure the stability of the clustering results. The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot and the proportion of ambiguously clustered pairs (PAC).

DEGs between clusters (referred to as cluster-DEGs) were screened using the “limma” package [57]. For this analysis, genes with an absolute |Log2 fold change (Log2FC)|> 0.5 and an adj.p < 0.05 were considered significant. The p-values were adjusted using a Bayesian correction method to control for multiple testing and to increase the robustness and reliability of the results.

Coagulation-score-RGs

To obtain genes associated with coagulation scores, based on the dataset GSE16561, coagulation scores of IS and control samples were calculated by “GSVA” package [15]. Wilcoxon test [40] was selected to examine coagulation scores difference between IS and control samples. Furthermore, performing a correlation analyses via spearman correlation analysis [17] on coagulation scores and all genes in GSE16561 expression matrix, coagulation-score-RGs were screened (|cor|> 0.4, p < 0.05).

Hub genes were identified

To screen hub genes, we first conducted Venn analyses on IS-DEGs, cluster-DEGs and coagulation-score-RGs to identify candidate genes. Then, random forest (RF), gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) and support vector machines (SVM) diagnostic models were constructed via “classification and regression training (caret)” package (version 6.0–93) [https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=caret] based on above candidate genes to acquire candidate hub genes. The performance of the model was evaluated using a machine learning framework with fivefold cross-validation. Samples were evenly divided into 5 parts through stratified sampling, and a random seed was set to ensure reproducibility. The random forest model (ntree = 10, mtry = floor, importance = T) evaluated feature importance via the Gini index; the support vector machine optimized parameters using grid search; the gradient boosting decision tree (n.trees = 1000, shrinkage = 0.01) determined the optimal number of iterations through cross-validation. The “DALEX” package (version 2.4.2) [https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=DALEX] was utilized to interpret models and rank genes in importance. Furthermore, hub genes were obtained via overlapping these candidate hub genes identified using above three diagnostic models.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted to evaluate diagnostic models and hub genes [14]. Area under the curve (AUC) value of ROC curve was calculated by pROC (version 1.17.0.1) [49]. Candidate hub genes also were validated in GSE9877 and GSE58294 datasets. The P-value test of goodness-of-fit was conducted using Hosmer–Lemeshow.

Validation set and RT-qPCR to determine hub genes

An independent dataset and RT-qPCR were used to validate the expression of candidate hub genes in IS patients. This study collected patients with cerebral infarction who were hospitalized in the neurology department of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University from June 2022 to January 2023. And select healthy individuals who underwent physical examinations at hospital during the same period as the normal control group. The total RNA of 20 freshly collected blood samples (NC = 10, IS = 10) was extracted at 4 ℃ low temperature using TRIzol kit (Ambion) according to instruction manual. All procedures were approved by the ethics committee of Guangxi Medical University. Next, the qualified RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA using SureScript-First-strand-cDNA-synthesis-kit. Then, cDNA diluted 5–20 times with ddH2O (RNase/DNase free) was saved for later. The reaction system was as follows: 3 ul cDNA, 5ul 2 × Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix, 2 ul primers (10 µM forward and reverse primers was 1 ul each). Amplification conditions: 95 ℃ for 1 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s (s) and 55 °C for 20 s, 72 °C for 30 s, results were analyzed by 2−△△Ct method[35]. Finally, Graphpad Prism 5 was used for plotting and calculating the p value. Genes with consistent expression trends in the GSE16561 and GSE9877 datasets, as well as in the RT-qPCR analysis, were named hub genes. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Establishment and validation of nomogram for hub genes

The nomogram was drawn via logistic regression [23] to further determine the risk of IS in clinical applications. In the dataset GSE16561, calibration curve and decision curve analysis (DCA) plotted by “rmda” package (version 1.6) [https://github.com/mdbrown/rmda] were used to evaluate prediction accuracy and clinical benefit of the nomogram. In addition, validation was also conducted on the datasets GSE9877 and GSE58294. To evaluate the ROC performance of individual genes and combined genes, we constructed ROC graphs based on key genes for comparison.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was performed via “clusterProfiler” package (version 3.18.0) based on KEGG database. Correlation coefficients between hub genes and others in GSE16561 microarray data were calculated for ranking, which was then used for GSEA. The top 10 pathways in high and low expression groups of hub gene were mapped by “enrichPlot” (version 1.10.2) (https://github.com/guangchuangyu/enrichplot).

Tissue-specific expression

In addition, we selected GeneAtlas U133A, gcrma dataset from BioGPS (http://biogps.org) to analyze the hub genes tissue specificity. Highly tissue-specific criteria were as follows: (1) the tissue-specific expression level was more than 10 times the median; (2) the second highest expression level was less than one-third of the highest level [60].

Estimation of immune cell infiltration

Immune cell infiltration was assessed to understand the relationship between hub genes and immune responses in IS patients. The composition of immune cells in GSE16561 dataset were calculated by quantiseq algorithm of “immunedeconv” package (version 2.0.3) [https://github.com/grst/immunedeconv]. A boxplot for visualization was drawn via “ggplot2” package. Difference analyses of immune cells in IS and control samples and visualization were implemented by Wilcoxon test and boxplot. Association between hub genes and immune cells was analyzed using spearman correlation analysis, a heat map was used for display.

Transcription factor (TF)–miRNA–mRNA network

A TF–miRNA–mRNA interaction network was constructed to explore the regulatory elements influencing the expression of hub genes. This study used NetworkAnalyst database (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/) to predict TFs targeted to hub genes. Additionally, miRNAs target to hub genes were forecasted on miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) databases. Afterwards, the TF–miRNA–mRNA interaction network construction were executed by Cytoscape (version 3.8.2) [51].

Potential targeting drugs

To identify small molecular compounds with potential therapeutic effects in cases of IS, potential targeting drugs of hub genes were predicted on DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com/). The top 25 drugs were presented in a network plotted by Cytoscape.

Single-cell analysis

To analyze the cellular heterogeneity in the microenvironment of IS, in the GSE224273 dataset, quality control (QC) was carried out using the "Seurat" software package (version 5.0.1) [16] to filter out cells with mitochondrial genes accounting for more than 20%, and cells with nCount_RNA values lower than 90,000 and gene numbers exceeding 2,000. Then, in light of the GSE224273 dataset, data were normalized by the "NormalizeData" function in the "Seurat" package (v 5.0.1), and highly variable genes (HVGs) were selected by the "FindVariableFeatures" function. Next, the "ScaleData" function in the "Seurat" package (v 5.0.1) was applied to scale data before principal components analysis (PCA). Subsequently, the "JackStraw" function within the "Seurat" package (v 5.0.1) was applied to execute PCA on HVGs (P < 0.05). The "ElbowPlot" function within the "Seurat" package (v 5.0.1) was thereafter applied to draw a scree plot of the top 25 principal components (PCs), aiming to identify PCs that notably contributed to variation for subsequent analysis (p < 0.05). Afterward, cell cluster analysis was conducted on cells after dimensionality reduction utilizing "FindNeighbors" and "FindClusters" functions (resolution = 0.5). After that, the cells were clustered by the uniform UMAP clustering method. Finally, through singleR marker genes of cell clusters were searched for and cell-type annotation was undertaken [36].

Differences in proportions between IS and controls were then compared using Chi-square tests (p < 0.05), and difference-in-difference cell analyses were performed using the “ggplot2” package (v 3.5.1) [68] for subsequent studies. In order to identify the key cells, based on the GSE224273 dataset, the expression of hub genes in the differential cells was detected, and the cells with the highest expression level of hub genes were selected as the key cells.

Adenovirus packaging and transfection

We cloned the Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting C5AR1 and the control shRNAs into vectors using the double-enzyme digestion method, and transferred the transfection mixture containing the helper packaging plasmid (5 μg), the vector plasmid (5 μg), Lipofectamine 2000, etc., into HEK 293 cells for adenovirus packaging. Approximately 10–15 days after transfection, HEK 293 cells began to float, and some cells showed cytopathic effect (CPE). When most cells exhibited typical CPE, cells and viral supernatant were collected by low-speed centrifugation. The viral supernatant from the second round of cell amplification was further subjected to ultrafiltration and purification processes, and the adenovirus titer was calculated. (The information of plasmids and vectors is shown in Table S4-10.)

Experimental animals

This experiment selected SPF grade C57BL/6 male mice aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 18–22 g. The experimental animal production license number is SCXK (E) 2022–0029, and the experimental animal use license number is SYXK (E) 2022–0124. Forty male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into four groups after 7 days of adaptive feeding, with 10 mice in each group: control group, middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) group, MCAO + sh-NC group, and MCAO + sh-C5AR1 group.

Adenovirus localization injection

After 7 days of appropriate care, mice were fasted for 12 h and allowed to drink water freely before modeling. After anesthesia, the mice were placed in a stereotaxic apparatus and injected with virus into the left cortex using a microinjection device at a rate of 0.2 μL/min. Three injection sites on the left side (5 μl each): Site 1, 0.3 mm away from the front of the beam, 3 mm from the outer side to the midline, 2 mm deep; Part 2, 0.8 mm behind the flower bud, 3 mm from the midline on the outer side, 2 mm deep; Part 3, 1.9 mm behind the short bud, 3 mm from the outer side to the midline, 2 mm deep. After 7 days of injection, MCAO modeling was performed on the left brain (Figure S1 A).

Establishment of mouse MCAO model

First, make a midline incision in the mouse's neck, and then carefully expose and separate the left external carotid artery (ECA). Then insert a 15-mm thread plug into the left internal carotid artery through the ECA to block the blood flow in the left middle cerebral artery (MCA) (Figure S1 B). After occlusion for 30 min, remove the suture plug for reperfusion and close the wound. The mice in the sham operation control group underwent the same surgery but without inserting a suture plug. 72 h after surgery, evaluate the neurological deficits in mice and collect samples.

Mouse neurological function score

The modified neurological severity scores (mNSS) were used to measure the neurological damage in various aspects such as forelimb extension function, limb symmetry, climbing ability, and sensory function in MCAO mice 72 h after modeling.

2,3,5-Triphenyltetrazolium chloride(TTC) staining

After intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium to anesthetize mice, the chest cavity was cut open, the right atrial appendage was cut, and 5 ml of physiological saline was slowly perfused into the left ventricle. After the perfusion was completed, the mouse brain was completely removed and frozen in a − 80° C refrigerator for about 5 min. Then it was taken out, cut into 5 equal parts with a blade, and stained in the preheated TTC staining solution for 30 min, turning it over every 10 min. After staining, place the stained brain slices in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h to fix the color. Finally, place the brain slice on the background board and take a photo. By using the Image-J image analysis system to accurately measure the normal brain tissue area of the infarcted hemisphere and the area of the infarcted contralateral hemisphere for each brain tissue slice.

qRT-PCR

Take 100 mg of fresh frozen mouse brain tissue, extract RNA using the Trizol method, and measure RNA concentration. Reverse transcribe RNA into cDNA using HiScript II qRT SuperMix for qPCR, dilute and store for future use. The qRT-PCR reaction system consists of 4 μl of cDNA, 0.4 μl of forward and reverse primers, 10 μl l of SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 μl of 50 × ROX Reference Dye 2, and 4.8 μl of dd H2O. Amplification at 95 ℃ for 10 min, at 95 ℃ for 15 s, at 60℃ for 60 s, and at 95 ℃ for 15 s, with 40 cycles. Melting curves were collected, and the results were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Finally, Graphpad Prism 5 was used for the calculation and plotting of p values. The list of primers for qRT-PCR is in Table S11 of the supplementary file.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Take a small amount of fresh mouse brain tissue and extract total protein using a lysis buffer containing 2 μl PMSF and 2 μl phosphatase inhibitor. Measure the protein concentration using the BCA method. Mix the extracted protein supernatant with 5 × protein loading buffer and heat denature for later use. After adding samples with a total protein content of 40 μg, they were subjected to constant pressure 180 V electrophoresis for 60 min. Take out the gel, directly transfer the whole gel onto the PVDF film, and use an electric tachometer to transfer the film. Soak the PVDF membrane in TBST containing 5% skim milk powder for 2 h, clean it, and then soak the PVDF membrane overnight in a primary antibody incubation solution at 4℃ (mouse monoclonal antibody β-actin 1:10,000, mouse monoclonal antibody C5AR1 1:1500). After thoroughly washing the PVDF membrane with TBST, the membrane was incubated in a room temperature shaker with HRP labeled sheep anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:10,000) for 2 h. The protein bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence assay kit, and the bands were quantitatively analyzed using Image-J software.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

This experiment places the complete mouse brain into an embedding frame and completes dehydration, transparency, and wax immersion in a biological tissue dehydrator. Then put it into the embedding machine for embedding and slice it using a Leica pathology slicer, with a thickness of about 4 μm. Place the slices in a 60℃ oven for 3 h, and then deparaffinize them sequentially with xylene and anhydrous ethanol. After antigen thermal repair of the slices, 3% hydrogen peroxide prepared was dropped onto the sliced tissue to block endogenous peroxidase, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Rinse with PBS, dry the glass slide with absorbent paper, draw circles around the tissue with an immunohistochemistry brush, add diluted normal goat serum dropwise, and seal at room temperature for 30 min. Shake off the liquid and add 1:100 C5aR1 antibody dropwise. Incubate overnight (15 h) in a 4 °C wet box. After rinsing the primary antibody clean, add HRP labeled goat secondary antibody dropwise and incubate at 37℃ for 30 min. Add DAB color solution dropwise onto the slices, and then counterstain with Mayer hematoxylin. Take photos after dehydration and sealing.

Immunofluorescence(IF)

After baking, dewaxing, and antigen repair of tissue paraffin sections, they were sealed with normal goat serum for 30 min. Then add 1:100 C5aR1 antibody and incubate overnight (15 h) in a wet box at 4 °C. After washing the slices with PBST, dilute the fluorescent secondary antibody (Cy3 goat anti mouse IgG 1:400) and incubate at 37 ℃ for 1 h in a wet box. After washing the slices, add DAPI and incubate in the dark for 5 min to stain the nuclei of the specimens. Finally, seal the film with a sealing solution containing anti fluorescence quencher and observe the collected images under a fluorescence microscope.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

After baking the paraffin sections of the mouse brain, they were successively placed in xylene, anhydrous ethanol and 95% alcohol for dewaxing, and then washed with distilled water. The sections were stained with Mayer's hematoxylin solution for 5 min and then rinsed with tap water to turn blue again. Then, the sections were soaked in 95% alcohol solution for 5 min and stained with 1% alcohol-soluble eosin staining solution for 2 min. After that, they are successively added to 95% alcohol solution, anhydrous ethanol and xylene solution. After air-drying, seal the sections with neutral resin and examine it under a microscope.

ELISA

The chopped mouse brain tissue and the corresponding volume of PBS (1:9) were added to the tissue grinder and thoroughly ground in an ice bath. Finally, centrifuge the homogenate at 3000 rpm for 10 min and collect the supernatant for detection. Take out the microplate, set up standard wells and sample wells, respectively. For each standard point, set up two wells in sequence, and add 50 μL of the corresponding standard to each well. Add 10 μL of the sample to be tested to the sample well, then add 40 μL of sample diluent, and incubate at 37℃ for 30 min. After washing the plate five times, add 50 μL of the detection antibody to each well, mix thoroughly, and incubate at 37 ℃ for 30 min. After washing the plate five times, add 50 μL of chromogenic reagent A and 50 μL of chromogenic reagent B to each well, shake and mix well, then incubate at 37 ℃ in the dark for 10 min, and add 50 μL of stop solution to each well. The optical density (OD value) of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R using appropriate packages. Differential expression analysis employed Bayesian p-value adjustment, while GO enrichment used Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Significance levels were denoted as: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Identification and enrichment analysis of DE-CRGs in IS

Via “limma” package, 450 IS-DEGs between IS and control samples were screened, including 343 upregulated and 107 downregulated IS-DEGs (Fig. 2A, Table S12). Venn analysis of 450 IS-DEGs and 203 CRGs (Table S13) was conducted to obtain 18 DE-CRGs (Fig. 2B). To reveal the underlying molecular biological processes in DE-CRGs, we performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. Results showed that 22 GO terms and 11 KEGG terms were identified (Fig. 2C). For GO-BP terms, blood coagulation, hemostasis and coagulation were significantly enriched. For GO-CC terms, secretory granule membrane, focal adhesion and cell–substrate junction were evidently enriched. For GO-MF terms, only complement receptor activity and complement binding progress were enriched. KEGG pathway enrichment analyses indicated that DE-CRGs were not only significantly associated with platelet activation, complement and coagulation cascades, but also Rap1 signaling pathway and cGMP − PKG signaling pathway (Fig. 2D).

Identification and screening of hub genes

Primarily, we identified 29 candidate hub genes by overlapping 450 IS-DEGs, 517 cluster-DEGs and 1,021 coagulation-score-RGs (Fig. 5A, Table S17). AUC value of RF, SVM and GBDT was 0.996, 0.943 and 0.971 in sequence, indicating that the 3 models had good performance (Fig. 5B). The inhibitory effect of C5AR1 adenovirus on C5AR1 expression in MCAO mice

We have confirmed that the expression level of C5AR1 in the blood samples of patients with cerebral infarction shows a significantly increased trend. To further explore the role of C5AR1 in the pathological process of cerebral infarction, we constructed an adenovirus vector that inhibits the expression of C5AR1. Subsequently, by means of stereotactic injection technology, the adenovirus was accurately injected into the left cerebral cortex region of mice, aiming to effectively inhibit the expression of C5AR1 in this way and further study its mechanism of action in the occurrence and development of cerebral infarction.

As shown in Figure S5 A, in the MCAO group and the MCAO + sh-NC group, the mRNA expression level of C5AR1 in the cerebral infarction tissue of mice was significantly higher than that in the sham operation group (P < 0.0001). However, the mRNA expression level of C5AR1 in the MCAO + sh-C5AR1 group of mice was significantly inhibited, forming a significant contrast with the MCAO group and the MCAO + sh-NC group (P < 0.0001). The WB results showed that compared with the MCAO group and the MCAO + sh-NC group, the C5AR1 protein level in the MCAO + sh-C5AR1 group was significantly decreased (P < 0.05, Figure S5 B-C). The above research results suggest that the expression level of C5AR1 can be knocked down through adenovirus transfection.

To further clarify the inhibitory effect of C5AR1 adenovirus on the expression of C5AR1 in MCAO mice, we conducted IHC and IF. The results showed that cortical injection of C5AR1 adenovirus had an inhibitory effect on the expression of C5AR1 protein in cerebral infarction tissues (P < 0.05, Figure S6 A-D).

The above experimental results suggest that this adenovirus can significantly inhibit the mRNA and protein expression levels of C5AR1 in the cerebral infarction tissue of MCAO model mice.

The protective effect of inhibiting C5AR1 expression on MCAO mice

In order to comprehensively evaluate the behavioral effects of C5AR1 on MCAO mice, we used the important indicator of mNSS score to assess the mice. The neurobehavioral score is one of the important indicators for evaluating cerebral ischemic injury. It determines whether the thrombus has been successfully inserted into the middle cerebral artery and blocked its blood flow by observing the consciousness state, motor function, balance function, etc., of rats. It can not only observe the pathological and physiological development process of cerebral ischemic areas, but also mark the success of the mouse model of cerebral ischemic injury. The value of mNSS score is negatively correlated with the symptoms of neurological deficits in mice. After 2 h of successful modeling, the mice gradually regained consciousness and showed symptoms and signs of neurological deficits. We observed that the MCAO group mice showed signs of nerve damage, while the sham surgery group mice did not show signs of nerve damage. The difference in mNSS scores between the two groups of mice was statistically significant (P < 0.0001), indicating that the establishment of the MCAO model successfully led to damage in mouse nerve function. After 72 h of adenovirus injection, the mNSS scores of MCAO + sh-C5AR1 group mice were significantly reduced compared to MCAO group and MCAO + sh-NC group mice (P < 0.0001, Figure S7 A), which strongly indicates that inhibiting C5AR1 expression can improve the neurological function status of MCAO mice.

In addition, we also used TTC staining method to analyze the staining of mouse cerebral infarction tissue and evaluate the degree of nerve damage in mice. In the experiment, TTC can mark the extent of the infarcted area. Normal tissue appears red in TTC staining, while necrotic brain tissue appears white. In the experiment, we observed that no white cerebral infarction lesions were found in the brains of mice in the sham surgery group; and the model showed white cerebral infarction lesions in the right middle cerebral artery supply area, including the frontal, temporal, and parietal cortex (Figure S7 B). The TTC staining results showed that the cerebral infarction volume of mice in the MCAO + sh-C5AR1 group was significantly reduced compared to the MCAO group and MCAO + sh-NC group, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.0001, Figure S7 C), further confirming that inhibiting C5AR1 expression has a positive protective effect on cerebral infarction.

Inhibiting the expression of C5AR1 alleviates neuroinflammation in MCAO mice

To explore the effect of C5AR1 on the inflammation of cerebral infarction, we detected the expressions of inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β in cerebral infarction tissues by ELISA. The levels of TNF-α in the four groups were as follows: control group 4257.498; MCAO group 8289.366 MCAO + sh-NC group 7732.181; MCAO + sh-C5AR1 group 4970.239. The levels of IL-1β in the four groups were as follows: control group 324.673; MCAO group 1063.180; MCAO + sh-NC group 1086.246; MCAO + sh-C5AR1 group 565.496. The examination results indicated that the levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in the MCAO group were significantly increased (P < 0.0001), suggesting that middle cerebral artery occlusion triggered an inflammatory response. The levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in the MCAO + sh-NC group were similar to those in the MCAO group, indicating that the negative control shRNA had no significant effect on the inflammatory response. The levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in the MCAO + sh-C5AR1 group were significantly lower than those in the MCAO group and the MCAO + sh-NC group, suggesting that inhibiting the expression of C5AR1 may have the effect of suppressing the inflammatory response (P < 0.001, Figure S8 A-B).

In HE staining, we could observe that in the sham operation group, the morphology of nerve cells was normal, the structure was intact, and no severe changes such as edema and necrosis occurred. The intercellular matrix is uniform. In the MCAO group, nerve cell swelling, deep nuclear staining, incomplete cell wall structure, and obvious pericellular edema could be observed in the blood supply area of the right middle cerebral artery. However, compared with the MCAO group and the MCAO + sh-C5aR1 group, the tissue morphology of the MCAO + SH-C5AR1 group was improved, the range of cell necrosis was significantly reduced, and the pericellular edema was significantly alleviated. It is suggested that inhibiting the expression of C5aR1 has a certain protective effect on the tissue damage of the cortex around infarction (Figure S9). The above research results suggest that inhibiting the expression of C5AR1 can suppress the inflammatory response and may have a protective effect on tissue damage caused by microvascular occlusion in the peri-infarct cortex.

Discussion

Thrombosis or embolism is an important cause of cerebral infarction [47]. Platelet activation and aggregation, initiation of clotting pathway, and enhancement of blood coagulation play an important role in the process of thrombosis and are closely related to the occurrence and development of IS [69]. In recent years, a number of studies have found that the polymorphism of CRGs can promote thrombosis by affecting the activity and function of coagulation factor FXI, making prothrombin increase abnormally, and blocking the role of coagulation regulatory protein [21, 22]. In this study, we systematically screened and verified that MYH9 and C5AR1 were the coagulation-related IS hub genes. Further bioinformatics analyses showed that these 2 hub genes were mainly enriched in complement and coagulation cascades. Our study suggests that these hub genes may become biomarkers for the diagnosis of IS and provide targets for the treatment of IS.

Multiple studies have shown that C5AR1 is highly expressed in IS and can serve as an inflammatory biomarker and potential therapeutic target for IS [4, 53, 54, 66]. BRANDOLINI L et al. demonstrated that the novel C5aR antagonist, DF3016A, protects neurons from ischemic neuroinflammatory injury by modulating complement components [3]. SKENDROSP et al. confirmed that blocking C5AR1 can attenuate thrombogenesis driven by platelet-mediated neutrophil extracellular trap (NETs) [56]. In addition, C5AR1 can control the formation of tissue neovascularization by inducing the release of CXCL4 from platelets [43]. These studies are consistent with our GSEA analysis results, which indicate that coagulation-related pathways, complement and coagulation cascades, and platelet activation pathways are enriched in the high expression group of C5AR1. Based on the important role of C5AR1 in IS, especially in promoting inflammatory responses and blood–brain barrier disruption, antagonists or inhibitors targeting C5RA1 have great therapeutic potential. Both clinical studies and animal experiments have supported the reduction of brain damage and improved functional recovery through C5AR1 inhibition [6]. Thus, C5AR1 may be a promising therapeutic target, especially in the acute phase of stroke, and inhibition of C5AR1 may reduce inflammatory damage and neuronal death.

MYH9 encodes non-muscular myosin heavy chain type II A (NMMHC IIA), which is involved in a wide range of biological processes requiring intracellular chemo-mechanical force generation and actin cytoskeletal translocation [46]. MYH9-related diseases refer to a syndrome where MYH9 mutations cause congenital thrombocytopenia, platelet megaphagy, and other manifestations [1]. Some studies have demonstrated that circ-EIF6 interacts with ribosomes to encode EIF6-224aa, which directly interacts with the MYH9 protein, which in turn activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and promotes the oncogenic function of circEIF6 [27]. These studies have confirmed that MYH9 is associated with platelet activation and ribosome related pathways. In the pathological process of IS, platelet activation and aggregation are the core steps in thrombosis. While high expression or mutation of MYH9 leads to enhanced platelet function, which increases the risk of thrombosis [42]. This mechanism may explain the importance of MYH9 in patients with IS, and its level of expression may be associated with the frequency and severity of post-stroke thrombosis. In this study, MYH9 was highly expressed in the IS group, and MYH9 was negatively correlated with NK cells and M2 macrophages, and positively correlated with neutrophils. Furthermore, studies have shown that cerebral ischemia is closely related to NK cells [11], and changes in NK cell function and quantity are involved in the pathogenesis of various autoimmune diseases. In patients with severe aplastic anemia (SAA), NK cells activate high expression of receptors NKp46 and NKG2D and inhibit low expression of receptor NKG2A, thereby increasing cytotoxicity [5]. M2 macrophages promote inflammation resolution, phagocytic apoptotic cells, drive collagen deposition, coordinate tissue integrity, and release anti-inflammatory mediators [59], and play a neuroprotective role in ischemic stroke [28]. Neutrophils can exert anti-inflammatory effects by releasing anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-4 and IL-10 and down-regulating the pro-inflammatory transcription factor nuclear factor κB [31]. Neutrophils that form NETs have been found in the brain tissues of stroke patients, and the levels of plasma NETs markers are positively correlated with poor prognosis [7]. To sum up, MYH9 may affect the occurrence and development of ischemic stroke by influencing the level of immune cell infiltration.

There is increasing evidence that activated immune cells can be recruited and infiltrate into the brain parenchyma to release inflammatory mediators during the acute phase of IS, leading to neurons death and disruption of the blood–brain barrier [10, 37]. It has been shown that the reduced stability of atherosclerotic plaque will increase the risk of IS, and the proportion of CD8 (+) T cells and NK cells in unstable plaque is significantly lower than that in stable plaque[61]. Another study proposed that the central memory CD8 + T cells, effector memory CD8 + T cells, and B cells in the IS-24 h group are significantly lower than those in the normal control group [33]. Consistent with the above findings, our results showed that B cells, Neutrophil, NK cells, CD8 + T cells, Tregs, and myeloid DC were involved in the pathology of IS.

NK cells, which are lymphocytes of the innate immune system, are an immunotherapeutic target for IS. Our findings suggested that NK cells were significantly associated with 2 hub genes. It has been shown that NMMHC IIA, encoded by MYH9, is defined as a key regulator of NK cytotoxicity [50]. The study by KARSTEN C M et al. observed that there was expression of C5AR2 in a subset of NK cells, and that the activation of C5AR2 on NK cells controlled IL-12/IL-18-mediated IFN-γ production. However, C5AR2 can inhibit the function of C5AR1 by capturing excess C5a and become a negative regulator of C5AR1 [24]. This study suggests that NK cells may be negatively associated with C5AR1, which is consistent with our analysis. In addition, some scholars believe that C5AR1 (+) tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) may lead to CD8 (+) T cell dysfunction, while blocking C5AR1 can reduce the immunosuppressive function of TAMs and restore CD8 (+) T-cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. It indicates that CD8 + T cell is significantly negatively correlated with C5AR1 [39, 67].

Through the TF–miRNA–mRNA interaction network, we predicted that 21 TFs and 44 miRNAs target these 2 hub genes. Furthermore, NFIL3, a predicted transcription factor of MYH9, was regarded as a regulatory transcription factor for key immunosuppression-associated crosstalk genes in IS in a study, and may be considered as a potential therapeutic target for IS [62]. This is in agreement with our results. Besides, the predictive transcription factor CREB1 of C5AR1 may exert neuroprotective effects on IS by affecting synaptic plasticity [65].

In addition, artesunate, a potential target drug of C5AR1, may ameliorate inflammatory damage in IS by reducing neutrophil infiltration and microglia activation, inhibiting inflammatory cytokines [34]. In terms of diet, higher intake of linoleic acid may prevent IS through the potential mechanisms such as lowering blood pressure, reducing platelet aggregation and enhancing erythrocyte deformability [19]. Although a study [32] has identified new coagulation genes (TLN1, F5, ACTN1, WAS and JMJD1C) as potential biomarkers of IS, the expression levels of TLN1 and WAS in the qPCR results of this study were inconsistent with the sequencing results, which to some extent affected the reliability of its biomarkers. Our study improved the correlation between the target genes and the coagulation pathological mechanism of IS by integrating the triple screening criteria of the CRGs set (KEGG), cluster-DEGs and coagulation- score-RGs. The hub genes (C5AR1 and MYH9) were screened through consistent trends in hub genes expression levels in the dataset and qPCR, ensuring the reliability of the hub gene in this study. In addition, we conducted an in-depth exploration of the association between hub genes and immune cell infiltration, and for the first time proposed that ELF1 may serve as an upstream regulatory factor controlling the expression of C5AR1 and MYH9, which may contribute to understanding the pathogenesis of IS.

We further verified the expression trends of 2 hub genes in the GSE16561 dataset, the GSE9877 datasets, and through RT-qPCR in vitro experiments. Only the expression trends of C5AR1 and MYH9 were consistent among the three validations. However, the expression levels of the remaining three genes differ from those in the dataset. REPS2 was upregulated in all disease groups of the dataset, but downregulated in the disease group of the experiment. LIME1 and ECHDC2 showed low expression in both the experimental group and the GSE16561 dataset, but high expression in GSE9877. Some studies have pointed out that this phenomenon may stem from an overly small sample size or the unpredictability of the data source [8, 9]. By comparing the expression levels in different data, not only can the accuracy of verification be improved, but also the result fluctuations caused by sample randomness can be effectively reduced. Based on the current experimental results, C5AR1 and MYH9 are closely related to IS. They may be involved in the pathogenesis of IS and may potentially become new targets for the treatment of IS.

In the development of IS, the activation of the complement system can not only amplify the inflammatory response and aggravate brain tissue damage, but also directly act on nerve cells, leading to cell damage and death. It is also involved in the destruction of the blood–brain barrier, further exacerbating brain edema and nerve damage. C5aR1, as the primary receptor of C5a in the complement system, mainly functions to efficiently bind to the complement fragment C5a and mediate various biological responses, including the regulation of inflammatory responses, chemotaxis and activation of immune cells, through intracellular signal transduction pathways [13]. These studies are consistent with our research results. C5AR1 is associated with related pathways such as complement and coagulation cascade, formation of neutrophil extracellular traps, and platelet activation, and shows a strong positive correlation with neutrophils. During the pathogenesis of IS, the expression level and functional activity of C5aR1 change significantly [3, 30]. To explore the connection between C5aR1, inflammatory factors and IS, we established the MCAO mouse model. The study found that the expressions of C5aR1, TNF-α and IL-1β were all increased in the MCAO group. To further explore the relationship between C5aR1 and inflammatory factors, we constructed an adenovirus to inhibit the expression of C5AR1. We found that compared with the MCAO group, the MCAO + sh-C5AR1 group significantly reduced the cerebral infarction volume of the MCAO model and significantly decreased the expression of inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β. The research by Shi Yunwei et al. found that after inhibiting C5aR1, it could protect rats from injury, reduce cellular inflammation and apoptosis by inhibiting the TLR4 and NF-κB signaling pathways and reducing the production of TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6[54]. In addition, some studies have found that after reducing the expression of C3, C5AR1, and C5, it can indirectly affect the downstream purine metabolism, pentose phosphate pathway, and glycerophospholipid metabolism-related pathways by regulating the expression of downstream inflammatory cytokines (including IL-6, IL-1β, and MMP-9), thereby exerting neuroprotective and anti-neuroinflammatory effects [18].

Not only that, through HE staining, we found that knocking down C5aR1 might reduce the occlusion of cortical microvessels around the infarcted tissue by inhibiting the inflammatory response, thereby achieving a protective effect on the cerebral infarcted tissue. The study by Nording H et al. also confirmed that platelet-specific deletion of C5aR1 was accompanied by an increase in collateral circulation, capillarization and pericytes coverage. Mechanistically speaking, the activation of C5aR1 induces platelets to preferentially release an important anti-angiogenic paracrine effect molecule CXCL4, which has an inhibitory effect on endothelial cell function [43]. Therefore, we speculate that inhibiting the expression of C5aR1 can promote protective effects on cerebral infarction tissues by reducing inflammatory responses and increasing angiogenesis.

In recent years, several studies have found that cAMP response element binding protein-1 (CREB1) IS associated with IS [41, 52]. Zeng S et al. discovered by establishing the MCAO model that the CCR5/CREB signaling pathway might exert a protective effect on IS by influencing synaptic plasticity [65]. In addition, Zhang W et al. found that cerebral ischemic injury could be protected by regulating the PI3K/Akt/CREB1 and TNF pathways [45]. In this experiment, we predicted through the TF–miRNA–mRNA interaction network that CREB1 was included among the transcription factors of C5aR1. Therefore, we speculate that the C5AR1/CREB1 pathway may play a role in the occurrence and development of IS.

However, this study has certain limitations. Firstly, the sample size of the clinical dataset we used is limited and the population representation is insufficient. The batch effect of different platforms may weaken the possibility of excluding genes. Furthermore, although the gene expression trend was verified by RT-qPCR, the clinical sample size was relatively small, and the universality of the results needed to be considered. Furthermore, we only constructed sh-C5AR1 adenovirus and did not verify the role of MYH9 in the disease model. The specific molecular mechanisms by which key genes regulate pathways such as coagulation and immune response have not yet been clarified. Finally, the predicted targeted drugs still remain at the level of bioinformatics prediction.

In response to these limitations, future research should delve deeper into the following aspects. Firstly, we will establish animal and cell models of ischemic stroke (such as oxygen–glucose deprivation models), and clarify the functional roles of C5AR1 and MYH9 in thrombosis and blood–brain barrier disruption through gene knockout/overexpression experiments. Secondly, multi-center cooperation should be carried out to expand the clinical sample size and population diversity, establish a prospective cohort, systematically collect complete etiological, imaging and follow-up data, and combine single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptome technology to analyze the specific role of key genes in IS. Furthermore, the specific regulatory mechanisms of the C5AR1/CREB1 and MYH9/NFIL3 pathways were clarified by using protein interaction and signaling pathway analysis techniques. Finally, pharmacodynamic evaluations of predicted drugs (such as Artesunate) are conducted in disease models to provide experimental evidence for their clinical transformation. These in-depth studies will help promote the transformation of these molecular targets into clinical diagnostic and therapeutic applications, providing new strategies for the precision medicine of IS.

Conclusions

This study successfully identified C5AR1 and MYH9 as key coagulation-related genes of IS through the integration of bioinformatics analysis and experimental verification. These two genes may be involved in the process of thrombosis by regulating the complement-coagulation cascade and the platelet activation pathway. Furthermore, studies have shown that these genes are closely related to immune cell infiltration. In the future, they can serve as potential diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets, providing new ideas for the precise diagnosis and treatment of ischemic stroke.
