Exploring the genomic landscape of gummy stem blight resistance in watermelon through QTL-Seq
Authors: Purushothaman Natarajan, Akilan Rathnagiri, Luis A. Rivera-Burgos, Carlos Lopez-Ortiz, Yan Tomason, Padma Nimmakayala, Nebahat Sari, Todd C. Wehner, Amnon Levi, Umesh K. Reddy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWatermelon is a nutritionally and economically significant crop in the US and globally. Gummy Stem Blight (GSB), caused by three cryptic Stagonosporopsis species, is one of the most devastating diseases affecting watermelon in the US, impacting most of the plant’s above-ground parts. This study aimed to identify key Quantitative Trait Variants (QTVs) that include SNPs and In/Dels associated with GSB resistance in selfed derivatives of advanced multicross interspecific derivatives population derived from intercrosses between the most resistant lines of Citrullus amarus and highly susceptible cultivars of Citrullus lanatus.ResultsResistant and susceptible bulks were created by combining equimolar DNA concentrations from 30 extremely resistant derivatives and 30 extremely susceptible lines. These bulks underwent whole-genome sequencing, generating over 1 billion reads per bulk to achieve comprehensive genome coverage. The mapping percentage of the bulks to the parental genomes ranged from 92 to 99%. More than 6 million SNPs and 1 million indels were identified from the resistant parental genome, compared to fewer than 2 million SNPs and 0.4 million indels from the susceptible parental genome. QTNs associated with GSB resistance were identified using single-nucleotide polymorphism-index and Gprime methods. Statistically significant variants/loci linked to GSB resistance were found on chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 11. Notably, the genes Lipase class 3 family protein, Ribosome hibernation promotion factor (CaU02G00010), Ubiquitin-like-specific protease 1D (CaU03G04260), and Zinc finger CCCH domain-containing 15 (CaU03G10970) harbored the highest delta SNPs. Several previously published genes, including Avr9/Cf-9 Rapidly Elicited Protein (CaU07G12990) on chromosome 7, were also identified.ConclusionsIdentifying significant loci associated with GSB resistance has facilitated the development of PACE assays, which will aid in breeding GSB-resistant watermelon cultivars. These findings provide critical insights into the genetic basis of GSB resistance and represent a significant step towards improving the resilience of watermelon crops against this devastating disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05839-2.

Background

Watermelon (Citrullus lanatus) is a vital crop in the cucurbit family, widely cultivated in temperate and tropical regions. Renowned for its refreshing taste, watermelon is a cultural emblem of hot summer and a nutritional powerhouse, offering an abundance of vitamins, minerals, and phytochemicals with significant health benefits [1–3]. With escalating global demand, safeguarding watermelon yield and quality against phytopathogenic diseases is crucial. Among these, Gummy Stem Blight (GSB) emerges as a particularly pernicious threat, posing a substantial challenge to watermelon production.

GSB is primarily caused by Stagonosporopsis cucurbitacearum ( previously known as Didymella bryoniae) and Phoma cucurbitacearum, a devastating disease of watermelon, including many other cucurbits. Recent studies have identified three distinct species within the Stagonosporopsis genus – S. cucurbitacearum, S. caricae, and S. citrulli – as causative agents of GSB [4, 5]. The disease thrives in warm, humid environments, promoting fungal growth and dissemination. GSB manifests as stem lesions, leaf spots, and gummy exudations, leading to defoliation, vine decline, and compromised fruit integrity, reducing yield and market value. While chemical control is the primary management strategy, the rising concerns over environmental impact and the development of fungicide-resistant strains call for sustainable alternatives [6–8]. The persistence of the pathogen in crop debris and soil complicates GSB management, posing a continuous threat to successive crops. The development of watermelon varieties with inherent resistance to GSB is impeded by the quantitative nature of resistance and the genetic complexity of watermelon, necessitating further research in this area [4, 5, 9].

In this context, the application of Quantitative Trait Loci (QTL) mapping, particularly QTL-seq, has revolutionized the identification of plant resistance traits. QTL-seq is an innovative approach that combines the principles of Bulk Segregant Analysis (BSA) with high-throughput next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, enabling the rapid identification of genomic regions associated with specific phenotypic traits [10, 11]. This method has been successfully employed in various crops to identify genes resistant to a plethora of pathogens, offering a more efficient alternative to traditional QTL mapping [11–19]. The potential of QTL-seq to reveal the genetic underpinnings of complex traits is particularly compelling in plant-pathogen interactions. By comparing allele frequencies between resistant and susceptible bulks derived from a segregating population, QTL-seq can pinpoint candidate genomic regions harboring resistance genes.

Recent studies have focused on identifying quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with Gummy Stem Blight (GSB) resistance in watermelon. Lee et al. (2021) made a significant contribution by discovering three QTLs on Chromosomes 8 and 6 in an F2 population derived from a cross between the susceptible line ‘920533’ and the resistant line ‘PI189225’ [18]. In a similar research, Hong et al. (2022) identified three QTLs, ClGSB1.1, ClGSB10.1, and ClGSB11.1, using genotyping by sequencing (GBS) in an F2 population developed from crosses between C. lanatus accession PI 279,461’ (resistant) and ‘PI 223,764’ (susceptible) [17]. Additionally, Adams and McGregor (2022) reported QTLs on chromosomes 2, 5, 9, and 11 using QTL-seq in an interspecific population from Sugar Baby × PI 189225 (Citrullus amarus) [12]. Furthermore, Gimode et al. (2021) identified three QTLs (ClGSB3.1, ClGSB5.1, and ClGSB7.1) associated with GSB resistance from a cross between Crimson Sweet (C. lanatus) and the GSB-resistant PI 482276 (C. amarus) [16]. Despite these advancements, the complete genetic architecture of GSB resistance in watermelon remains to be fully elucidated, necessitating further research in this area.

A population of three hundred recombinant inbred lines (RILs) was developed, exhibiting a range of resistance to GSB and possessing high-quality fruit traits. This was achieved through an intermating process that crossed resistant plant introductions with elite cultivars, followed by multiple rounds of intercrossing and self-pollination without selection for resistance up to the S3 generation. These 300 interspecific derivatives underwent evaluation for their disease severity and various fruit morphological and quality characteristics. This evaluation was conducted in greenhouse and field settings, over three years with ten replications [20]. The current study focuses on applying the QTL-seq technique to the constituted resistant and susceptible bulks to explore the genetic basis of resistance to GSB in watermelons.

Materials and methods

Plant material, inoculum preparation, and application

An isolate of Stagonosporopsis cucurbitacearum used for inoculations in the current study. Pycnidia were identified under a dissecting microscope (20x) and transferred to Petri plates containing potato dextrose agar (PDA, 25 mL per plate). Isolates were selected from the first subculture on PDA based on macroscopic characteristics, including dark colonies with concentric growth circles, and transferred to fresh PDA. Cultures free from contamination were further transferred to a 25% PDA medium to encourage abundant sporulation. The presence of pycnidia/pseudothecia and spores was verified to match the morphological characteristics of S. cucurbitacearum [5, 20, 21].

Inoculum was prepared by flooding the culture plates with sterile distilled water and gently scraping the surface to dislodge spores. The spore concentration was adjusted to 5 × 105 spores/mL, and Tween 20 was added to facilitate proper spore dispersion. In the greenhouse, plants were inoculated at the second true-leaf stage (approximately 2 weeks after planting) using a hand-pumped spray bottle, after gently brushing the trichomes on the leaf surface. In the field, inoculation occurred at the fourth true-leaf stage (approximately 4 weeks after planting) using a backpack sprayer, with inoculation repeated every two weeks. High humidity was maintained in the greenhouse using a humidity chamber, while overhead irrigation was used in the field to encourage disease development. Plants were rated for disease severity using a scale of 0 (no disease) to 9 (plant death) at 3 weeks post-inoculation in the greenhouse and 1 week after the second inoculation in the field [5, 20, 21].

Construction of segregating bulks for sequencing

Based on the disease scores from 288 interspecific derivatives (Table 1) [5, 20] evaluated in the field (2017, 2018, and 2019) and greenhouse (Supplementary Figs. 1), 30 lines exhibiting extreme resistance and another 30 lines showing extreme susceptibility to gummy stem blight were selected using an outlier detection algorithm (https://www.r-bloggers.com/2020/08/outliers-detection-in-r/). These extremes were utilized to form a Resistant bulk (R-Bulk) and a Susceptible bulk (S-Bulk). Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves using the DNeasy Plant Pro kit (Qiagen, Germany), and the integrity and concentration of the extracted genomic DNA were assessed through agarose gel electrophoresis and a Qubit fluorimeter (Thermo Scientific, USA). Genomic DNA from each individual was pooled in equal quantities to construct the R-Bulk and S-Bulk, which were subsequently used for DNA library preparation and genome sequencing.

Whole genome resequencing and genome mapping

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the bulks were used to prepare the DNA sequencing libraries using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA). DNA was fragmented to have an average insert size of 450 bases, and the ends were repaired; subsequently, Illumina adapters were added to the fragmented and repaired DNA. The adapter-ligated DNA libraries were further amplified and sequenced on the NextSeq500 system (Illumina, USA) to generate paired-end reads with a read length of 2 × 150 bp and sequencing coverage of more than 250X to the genome. The quality of the raw reads was assessed using the FASTQC tool (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The raw paired-end sequencing reads from the bulks were quality-filtered to remove adapters and low-quality bases (Phred score < 30) using Trimmomatic [22]. The quality-filtered FASTQ files (paired-end) for each of the bulks were mapped using Burrows-Wheeler Alignment (BWA) to the watermelon reference genome build USVL246 and Charleston Grey (http://cucurbitgenomics.org/), and the whole genome alignment was performed using ‘mem’ with default parameters [23]. The resulting BAM file from the genome alignment was sorted using Picard tools v. 2.18.27 (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) and indexed using SAMtools v. 1.15.1 (http://samtools.sourceforge.net/). Using Picard tools version 2.18.27, duplicates were detected, and read groups were created (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). The GATK tool ‘haplotypecaller’ (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/) was used with the default settings to call variants from the sorted BAM file. The resultant variant calling file (VCF) generated the SNP index to identify the closely linked SNPs and QTLs for the target trait in each bulk.

QTL-Seq analysis

SNP-index and Delta SNP-index for each bulk were calculated using the QTL-seqr package [24] and QTL-seq [11]. In QTLseqr, specific criteria were applied to refine the SNP selection using the ‘filterSNPs’ function. These criteria included a reference allele frequency threshold of 0.20, a total depth range between 50 and 900, a minimum sample depth of 40, and a genotype quality threshold of 99. Following this, the software computed the genome-wide G statistics and the SNP index for every SNP site in the designated window bandwidth. Using the runGprimeAnalysis and runQTLseqAnalysis functions, the tricube-smoothed G prime (G’) and delta SNP-index values for each SNP were determined. Additionally, we employed the Benjamini-Hochberg method for genome-wide false discovery rate (FDR) to adjust the p-values for every SNP. Using the FDR method, the significant thresholds for G’ were identified, and QTLs were pinpointed based on an FDR of 0.01. The delta SNP-index, smoothed with the tricube method, was derived using a 1 Mb sliding window. The runQTLseqAnalysis script was employed to ascertain two-sided confidence intervals (at 95% and 99%) for each delta SNP index. For visualization, the plotQTLStats function generated graphs showcasing the SNP count, delta SNP-index, G’ values, and the -log10(p-value). The software’s ‘getSigRegions’ and ‘getQTLTable’ functions highlighted regions deemed significant within the chosen confidence range to pinpoint potential QTLs.

Identification of candidate genes and pathway mapping

The QTV region across various chromosomes was explored to determine the genes within, leveraging the watermelon genome annotation. Genes of significance were pinpointed using peak Delta SNP and G prime metrics. Through functional annotations and understanding the role of genes in disease resistance, those related to GSB resistance were distinguished. The KEGG Orthology-Based Annotation System (KOBAS) (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) was utilized for mapping the KEGG pathways of genes unearthed in the QTLseq analysis. In the specified QTL regions, genes associated with disease resistance were annotated and cataloged. This process was facilitated using the Plant Resistance Genes database (PRGdb 4.0), an extensive resource tailored for this purpose. The database’s application programming interface (API) was also employed to streamline and enhance the annotation process [25].

PACE-based SNP genotyping

PCR allelic competitive extension (PACE) was conducted using allele-specific primers for the variants based on their identified physical locations, as determined by the data obtained from QTLseq analysis. Twelve single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sites were explicitly selected to genotype the RILs utilized to form two distinct extreme bulks. The components used in the PACE PCR consisted of 5 µL of PACE Genotyping Master Mix (2X) (3CR Bioscience, Essex, UK) comprising FAM, HEX, and ROX fluorophores, 0.11 µL of primer assay mix (72X), 2 µL of template DNA, and 3 µL of molecular biology grade water. The PCR conditions were implemented in a three-stage process. Firstly, there was a single cycle at a temperature of 94 ℃ for 15 min. Secondly, there were 10 cycles, each consisting of template denaturation at 94 ℃ for 20 s, followed by annealing/extension with a temperature drop of 0.8 ℃ per cycle, ranging from 65 to 57 ℃, for a duration of 60 s. Lastly, there were 30 cycles, each involving denaturation at 94 ℃ for 20 s, followed by annealing/extension at a constant temperature of 57 ℃ for 60 s (3crBioscience, United Kingdom). Additionally, 3 to 9 cycles of final denaturation and annealing/extension were conducted to enhance amplification and produce densely populated and well-distinguished clusters. The StepOne Plus AutoCaller (Applied Biosystems, USA) grouped the genotypes.

Results

Disease response of the RIL population

In the field environment, the distribution of disease scores showed a peak around scores 4 to 6, indicating a moderate level of disease severity among most derivatives. Similarly, in the greenhouse environment, the scores displayed a peak of 5, suggesting consistent disease pressure in controlled conditions. When combining data from both environments, the overall disease scores exhibited a bimodal distribution with distinct peaks corresponding to the resistant (R bulk) and susceptible (S bulk) lines. This bimodal distribution underscores the genetic variation within the RIL population for resistance to gummy stem blight, with clear groups of lines demonstrating either high resistance or high susceptibility. These results indicate segregating loci influencing resistance to gummy stem blight in this RIL population.

Whole genome resequencing of bulks and variant calling

In our analysis, the initial objective was to delineate the genetic architecture underlying the observed resistance and susceptibility phenotypes of GSB disease. To achieve this, two contrasting DNA bulks were established: resistant (R-bulk) and susceptible (S-bulk). These bulks were constituted using equimolar DNAs from a carefully selected set of 30 Recombinant Inbred Lines (RILs) that exhibited pronounced extreme phenotypes. The phenotypic distribution of these RILs is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

For bulked segregant analysis, two genomic DNA libraries were prepared, each representing the DNA composition of one of the bulks for use in high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq500 platform, employing a paired-end sequencing approach with a read length configuration of 2 × 150 bp. The sequencing depth achieved was substantial. Specifically, the R-bulk yielded 1,394,934,030 reads, translating to a 563X coverage, while the S-bulk produced 1,750,401,242 reads, equivalent to a 707X coverage. Such sequencing depth ensures the robustness of downstream analyses.

A rigorous quality control step was implemented post-sequencing, leading to the retention of 1,353,303,124 and 1,704,898,399 high-quality reads for the R-bulk and S-bulk, respectively. These curated reads were then aligned to the reference genome of Citrullus amarus (USVL246 variant). The alignment efficacy was noteworthy, with 1,346,984,333 reads from the R-bulk and 1,693,263,106 reads from the S-bulk successfully mapped. The overall mapping efficiency surpassed 99% for both bulks, underscoring the quality of the sequencing data and the alignment process. In a parallel approach, the quality-filtered reads were also mapped against the Citrullus lanatus (WCG_v2 variant) reference genome, which resulted in the successful mapping of 1,344,506,187 reads from the R-bulk and 1,681,510,931 reads from the S-bulk, maintaining the overall mapping efficiency at 99%. QTL-Seq analysis

The Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) and Insertions or Deletions (INDELs) underwent QTLseq analysis to pinpoint causative variants or SNPs through SNP-index estimation. For this purpose, SNP indices for both the resistant and susceptible genomes, representing the S-bulk and R-bulk, were independently calculated. The analysis involved determining the average SNP indices for the S-bulk and R-bulk, along with their respective ΔSNP-indices, which were smoothed using the tricube method within a 1 Mb sliding window. The publicly available reference genomes, “USVL246” and “WCG_v2,” were utilized to construct ΔSNP-index plots across the lengths of various chromosomes. Genomic regions that exhibited significant peaks or valleys in the SNP-index plot for both S and R bulks, with a False Discovery Rate (FDR) cutoff of 0.01, were identified as Quantitative Trait Nucleotides (QTNs) associated with the trait, following the criteria set by Takagi et al. (2013) [11]. Notably, the FDR cutoff of 0.01 did not reveal any QTLs when using “WCG_v2” as the reference genome, highlighting a distinct genomic influence on the traits of interest compared to the USVL246 genome. This difference in QTL discovery emphasizes potential genetic variances between the two genomes concerning the analyzed phenotypic traits. Consequently, further analyses focused on QTLs identified using the “USVL246” reference genome. Comprehensive details of all SNPs and their corresponding SNP indices from the USVL246” reference genome are presented in Fig. 2, Tables 4, and Table S1.

Among the 11 chromosomes in the USVL246 reference genome, the highest number of variants were noted on Chr9, followed by chromosomes 1, 2, 4, 6, and 11. In this study, forty Quantitative Trait Nucleotides (QTNs) were identified across the eleven chromosomes of USVL246, including 26 QTNs and 14 INDELs (Table 4).

Five QTNs are on chromosome 1 and four on chromosome 10, chromosomes 2, 6, 7, and 9 each carry three QTNs, suggesting polygenic inheritance. Chromosome 3 and 11 each have 2 QTNs, chromosome 5 has 1 QTN, and chromosome 4 has 1 QTN. Chromosome 8 carries no QTNs. Notably, the QTNs clustered together encompassing large genomic regions, indicative of the presence of long haplogroups, and could be of substantial interest for trait association or potential regions under selection. For instance, the longest QTLs were found on chromosome 1, with one such region spanning approximately 4.67 million base pairs (bp) and containing 61,625 SNPs, representing a high-density area with an average of 1,419 SNPs per megabase (Mb). Conversely, some QTLs were much shorter, pointing to more localized genomic regions of interest. The average SNP density across all QTLs in the current study is higher because of involvement of multiple resistant parents in intercross. Peak delta SNP values, which indicate the highest negative SNP index difference between two bulks, varied among the QTLs. Some of the genes located to the peak delta SNP are Lipase class 3 family protein (CaU01G01960), Ribosome hibernation promotion factor (CaU02G00010), Ubiquitin-like-specific protease 1D (CaU03G04260), and Zinc finger CCCH domain-containing 15 (CaU03G10970), which are known resistant genes.

Pathways enriched in the QTLs

Genes found within the significant quantitative trait loci (QTLs) were subjected to a detailed pathway enrichment analysis using the KOBAS tool, following the methodology outlined by Bu et al. (2021) [26]—this analysis aimed to uncover the biological pathways potentially involved in the traits governed by these QTLs. The study revealed significant enrichment in 34 distinct pathways, determined based on a stringent False Discovery Rate (FDR) cutoff of 0.05, underscoring the complexity of the genetic interactions (Fig. 3). The most important pathways enriched with significant roles in plant defense are “Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites,” “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis,” “Plant hormone signal transduction,” “Plant-pathogen interaction,” “Peroxisome, alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism,” “MAPK signaling pathway.” The details of the pathways and their enrichment levels are presented in Table S2.

Disease resistance genes enriched in QTLs

This study annotated disease resistance genes within the identified QTL regions across various chromosomes using the Plant Resistance Genes database (PRGdb 4.0) [25]. A comprehensive annotation yielded 389 disease-resistance genes from the QTL regions (Table S3). The chromosomal distribution of these genes is detailed in Fig. 4, illustrating a varied presence across the chromosomes. Notably, chromosomes 1, 2, and 3 exhibited a higher concentration of these genes, suggesting a possible cluster of resistance traits in these regions, whereas the lowest number of disease resistance genes characterized chromosome 4. In addition to the chromosomal mapping, the study delved into the prevalent protein domains in these annotated genes. A thorough analysis examined the enrichment of specific protein domains within the disease-resistance genes. The results of this analysis, depicted in Fig. 4, revealed that certain protein domains were more predominant. These include the transmembrane domain (TM), Kinase, KIN (a variant of kinase), Leucine-rich repeat (LRR), Receptor-like kinase (RLK), Coiled-coil (CC), Receptor-like protein (RLP), lipoxygenase, and Nucleotide-binding site (NBS) domains. The higher representation of these domains in the annotated genes underscores their potential functional significance in disease resistance. We also noted five genes associated with disease resistance that exhibited the peakDeltaSNP and maxGprime values. These genes were found on chromosomes 1 (CaU10G07250, CaU10G18550, CaU11G17680), 3 (CaU03G19350), and 6 (CaU06G00820). The protein domains present in these genes include LYK, LYSM, RLK, LRR, Kinase, TM, N, NBS, and RLP.

PACE assay genotyping for marker validation

We used variants from QTLseq analysis based on the ΔSNP indices to develop PACE assays consisting of two allele-specific primers (susceptible and resistant) and one common reverse primer (Table S2). Of 24 PACE markers designed, only eleven assays (Chr01:31177996, Chr01:1783719, Chr01:32233404, Chr01:1813990, Chr02:36426344, Chr02:593126, Chr02:7646157, Chr02:36879552, Chr06:940211, Chr10:6115160, and Chr11:5067901) were informative to resolve three distinct groups - homozygous resistant, heterozygous, and homozygous susceptible (Fig. 5). Among them, eight markers assays resolved differences between resistant and susceptible individuals with the highest precision. Furthermore, we used one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s test to analyze the relationship between genotypes and disease severity as resolved by PACE assays (Table S5). ANOVA indicated highly significant differences (p < 0.001) between the A (homozygous resistant), B (homozygous susceptible), and H (heterozygous) groups in all the above assays, indicating their potentiality in marker assisted selection programs. The post-hoc analysis of Tukey’s test further confirmed that above mentioned in this study are highly effective in discriminating between resistant and susceptible groups.

Discussion

GSB poses a significant global threat to cucurbit cultivation, primarily threatening watermelon cultivation. Since this disease is attributed to three distinct yet morphologically indistinguishable fungal species sharing similar geographic distributions and host preferences, the management of GSB is complicated by two primary factors: the scarcity of cultivars exhibiting genetic resistance to the disease and the notable propensity of the causal pathogens to acquire resistance against systemic fungicides [9]. In light of these challenges, the current study evaluates introgression of resistant alleles from multiple wild species of watermelon using QTLseq. By resolving genetic regions differentiating resistant and susceptible bulks, we developed a set of marker panels useful for ongoing genome-assisted breeding programs for GSB resistance, thus offering a sustainable and practical approach to combat this pervasive problem. Further, current QTL-Seq study, has provided valuable insights into the complex genetic architecture of GSB resistance by discovering 40 QTLs spread across the watermelon genome underscores the polygenic nature. The QTNs identified in the current research have been corroborated with previously published studies of GSB resistance [5, 16–19].

Annotation of the 80 candidate genes present in the QTL regions with maximum PeakDeltaSNP and MaxGprime revealed valuable insight on multiple pathways relevant to biotic stress responses in watermelon. Notably, the genes encoding Lipase Class 3 Family Protein, Amine Oxidase, Myosin-1, 2-oxoglutarate (2OG) and Fe(II)-dependent Oxygenase Superfamily Protein, Zinc Finger Family Protein, Ribosome Hibernation Promotion Factor, Isoflavone Reductase-Like Protein, Exostosin Family Protein, Ubiquitin-like-specific Protease 1D, Beta-glucosidase, Receptor-like Protein Kinase, DNA-directed RNA Polymerase, Avr9/Cf-9 Rapidly Elicited Protein, and various Receptor-like Kinases are involved in complex biological pathways that contribute to plant responses to biotic stresses, such as GSB. These genes are known to interact via multiple signaling networks, which include lipid signaling, oxidative stress response, transcriptional regulation, protein ubiquitination, and cell wall biosynthesis.

Lipase Class 3 Family Protein is involved in various stress responses, including disease resistance, by modifying lipid-based signaling pathways. L3 lipases release fatty acids from membrane lipids, which can act as signaling molecules activating defense responses. These signaling lipids, such as jasmonic acid (JA), trigger gene expression in defense pathways [27]. Amine Oxidase produces hydrogen peroxide, a key molecule in plant defense against pathogens. Amine Oxidase can indirectly contribute to cell wall reinforcement by influencing lignin biosynthesis. Lignin is a complex polymer that strengthens the cell wall, acting as a physical barrier against pathogen invasion [28]. Myosin-1 plays a role in maintaining cell wall integrity, which serves as a crucial barrier against pathogen invasion [29].

The 2-oxoglutarate (2OG) and Fe(II)-dependent oxygenase superfamily protein, located within the QTL on chromosome 1, has been demonstrated to confer resistance to the fungal pathogen Aspergillus flavus in cotton [30]. This protein is critical in enhancing the plant’s defense mechanisms against the fungus, which is notorious for causing significant damage to cotton crops, similar to the impact of GSB in watermelon. Zinc Finger Family Protein, which is also associated with the QTL on chromosome 1, can bind directly to pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), triggering defense responses against pathogens [31].

Our study has identified the Avr9/Cf-9 Rapidly Elicited Protein, a significant disease-resistance gene located on the QTL associated with chromosome 7, which confers resistance to GSB and other fungal diseases in watermelon and other plants. Ghimode et al. (2021) reported that a gene encoding an Avr9/Cf-9 rapidly elicited disease resistance protein, containing a non-synonymous point mutation in the DUF761 domain, was significantly associated with GSB resistance [16]. Similarly, Hu et al. (2018) identified this gene as conferring resistance to GSB in Cucumis melo [32]. The mechanism by which Avr9/Cf-9 rapidly elicited proteins confer fungal disease resistance involves a sophisticated gene-for-gene interaction system. In tomato plants, the Cf-9 resistance gene encodes a membrane-anchored glycoprotein with leucine-rich repeats that recognize the Avr9 avirulence gene product from the fungus Cladosporium fulvum. The Avr9 gene encodes a pre-protein processed into a cysteine-rich peptide, adopting a cysteine-knot structure essential for its function. Upon fungal infection, recognizing Avr9 by Cf-9 triggers a cascade of signal transduction events, activating plant defense mechanisms, including the hypersensitive response, which involves localized cell death to prevent pathogen spread. Although the precise details of the signaling pathways remain unclear, it is evident that the Avr9/Cf-9 interaction plays a crucial role in initiating the plant’s immune response to combat fungal invasion effectively [33–36].

Another significant gene for fungal disease resistance, Leucine-rich Repeat Receptor-like Protein Kinase Family Protein, is located in the QTL on chromosome 10. The Leucine-rich Repeat Receptor-like Protein Kinase (LRR-RLK) family proteins are crucial components of the plant immune system, vital in recognizing and responding to various pathogenic attacks, including those from fungi. This gene has demonstrated resistance to fungi in several plants, such as Cochliobolus heterostrophus in maize and Fusarium in cereals [37, 38]. Therefore, it could also be a candidate gene for GSB resistance in watermelon.

The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using genes from QTL regions associated with GSB resistance highlights critical biological processes involved in plant defense. Genes such as Lipase Class 3 Family Protein, Amine Oxidase, Myosin-1, and others contribute to these enriched pathways and mitigate GSB stress. The ‘Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites’ pathway, involving proteins like Beta-glucosidase and Isoflavone Reductase-Like Protein, suggests enhanced production of defensive compounds. Enrichment of the ‘Plant hormone signal transduction’ pathway involves Receptor-like Protein Kinases and Zinc Finger Family Proteins, indicating the role of salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, and ethylene in defense signaling. The ‘Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis’ pathway, potentially influenced by Exostosin Family Protein, contributes to lignin synthesis and cell wall reinforcement. ‘Starch and sucrose metabolism’ and ‘Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism’ pathways highlight metabolic adjustments, possibly facilitated by enzymes like Beta-glucosidase, to reinforce plant tissues during pathogen attack. Enrichment of the ‘Plant-pathogen interaction’ pathway involves Receptor-like Protein Kinases and Leucine-rich Repeat Receptor-like Proteins, which detect pathogens and initiate downstream responses. ‘Spliceosome’ pathway enrichment suggests post-transcriptional regulation of defense transcripts, while the ‘MAPK signaling pathway’ underscores signaling cascades in coordinating protective responses.

These insights and the development of eleven PACE markers associated with GSB resistance on chromosomes 1, 2, 6, 10, and 11 support the use of these markers in breeding programs to develop GSB-resistant varieties by leveraging enriched defense pathways and associated gene networks.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study has successfully employed QTL-seq to uncover the genetic underpinnings of GSB resistance in watermelon. Identifying numerous QTLs across the watermelon genome and enriching key defense-related pathways provide valuable insights into the genetic complexity of this trait. These findings not only enhance our understanding of plant-pathogen interactions in watermelon but also offer promising avenues for developing GSB-resistant varieties, contributing to the sustainability and productivity of watermelon cultivation.
