To the Editor,

Twenty-first-century medicine has cultivated an ethos of inclusivity, moving away from paternalistic practices to focus on patient-centered care that promotes mutual respect to improve patient outcomes. This article discusses the importance of inclusive language in dermatology for relationship building and its impact on patient outcomes and research accuracy.

Language shapes the connection between patients and clinicians. Principles like person-first language (eg, “person experiencing obesity”) or identity-first language (eg, “autistic patients”) can be used to emphasize patient autonomy and highlight human dignity, although clinicians should always clarify preferred terminology. In dermatology specifically, language around psychocutaneous disorders warrants particular care.1 Terms like “trichotillomania” and “delusions of parasitosis” may imply patient blame and contribute to poor outcomes if patients refrain from healthcare to avoid stigma. Attention to terminology and patient preferences around language is an important determinant of patient engagement, maintaining sensitivity to the cultural histories and needs of communities.

Language also matters in the electronic health record, where linguistic choices can perpetuate clinician bias even before the patient encounter. Goddu et al. found that reading stigmatized descriptions that implied patient responsibility for their condition or contained irrelevant indicators of race or status increased negative provider attitudes toward the patient and led to disparities in pain management.2 Another study found that the use of quotations – a linguistic tool that can imply the writer doubts the truth of a patient’s statement – is more likely in written notes for women than for men.3 As electronic health record documentation becomes increasingly available to patients, stigmatizing language like that described above may further impact outcomes by damaging trust in healthcare providers and the medical system.

Finally, language shapes how scientific data are interpreted and published. Using patient-first diagnostic labels plays a significant role in reducing the stigma associated with certain medical conditions.4 Likewise, inclusive language in scholarly publishing is essential for engaging broader audiences and building partnerships between researchers and the populations they study. Publications like the Journal of the American Chemical Society have developed guides to promote inclusive language and imagery in academic literature for these reasons.

In recent years, professional societies like the American Medical Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics have issued general guidelines for inclusive language.5 These directives outline various equity-focused language principles, including the use of patient-first language. The National Cancer Care Network takes these ideas a step further by inviting ongoing suggestions for the improvement of their language guide, promoting flexibility and longevity of these efforts. Other professional organizations like the American Association of Family Physicians have modules on patient-centered language as part of their available continuing medical education offerings. Dermatologic professional organizations and academic journals should strive to create similar initiatives around inclusive language.

Creating a more inclusive dermatology is a multifaceted endeavor, and adjustments to language are an integral part of these efforts. Emphasis on the importance of inclusive language practices must start with national dermatologic organizations establishing professional best practices and educational opportunities for dermatology professionals at all levels of training.
