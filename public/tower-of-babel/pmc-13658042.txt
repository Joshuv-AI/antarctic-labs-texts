Palatal Donor‐Site Healing and Patient‐Reported Outcome Measures Following Chitosan and Injectable Platelet‐Rich Fibrin‐Enriched Chitosan Application Compared with Gelatine Sponge: A Three‐Arm Randomised Controlled Clinical Trial
Authors: Ayse Ege Selman, Bahar Alkaya, Burcu Karaduman, Mustafa Ozcan, Mehmet Cenk Haytac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo compare clinical outcomes and patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) of palatal donor‐site healing following the use of an absorbable gelatine sponge, a chitosan sponge or an injectable platelet‐rich fibrin (i‐PRF)‐enriched chitosan sponge after free gingival graft harvesting.Materials and MethodsIn this three‐arm randomised controlled clinical study, a total of 68 patients were allocated to three groups: i‐PRF + chitosan (n = 22), chitosan (n = 23) and gelatine sponge (control; n = 23). The primary outcome was the palatal early healing index (PEHI). Secondary outcomes included PROMs, namely postoperative pain, burning sensation and changes in dietary habits, which were evaluated using a visual analogue scale (VAS) at postoperative days 7, 14, 28 and 56. Bleeding and painkiller consumption were recorded daily. Colour match was assessed using VAS.ResultsBoth chitosan‐containing groups showed significantly higher PEHI scores, particularly on days 14 and 28, than the control group (p < 0.001). At Day 14, the median PEHI scores were 8.0 (Q1–Q3: 7.0–8.0; 95% CI for the median: 7.0–8.0), 8.0 (Q1–Q3: 8.0–9.0; 95% CI: 8.0–9.0) and 9.0 (Q1–Q3:8.0–9.0; 95% CI: 8.0–9.0) in the control, chitosan and i‐PRF‐enriched chitosan groups, respectively. The i‐PRF + chitosan group showed significantly lower pain and burning sensation during early healing and less changes on dietary habits on days 3 and 7 compared with the other groups (p < 0.05). Bleeding and painkiller consumption were similar among groups (p > 0.05). Both test groups showed significantly better colour match than the control group at days 14, 28 and 56 (p < 0.001).ConclusionWithin the limitations of the present study, chitosan improved early palatal wound healing, while the addition of i‐PRF enhanced patient comfort. Both chitosan‐containing approaches resulted in improved colour match compared with control. Nevertheless, further studies incorporating histological and biomolecular assessments of wound healing are needed to confirm these findings.Keywords: chitosan, injectable platelet‐rich fibrin, pain, palate, wound healing

Introduction

Keratinised gingiva (KG) plays an important role in maintaining the long‐term health and stability of periodontal tissues. Although the minimum width of KG required for periodontal health remains controversial, inadequate KG may increase plaque accumulation, discomfort during oral hygiene procedures and susceptibility to inflammation in certain clinical situations (Lang and Löe 1972; Lang and Bartold 2018; Golmayo et al. 2021). Therefore, periodontal plastic surgery procedures aimed at augmenting KG remain an important component of contemporary periodontal therapy.

Free gingival graft (FGG) is a predictable and widely used procedure for increasing keratinised tissue width around teeth and dental implants (Prato et al. 1995). However, palatal donor‐site morbidity remains a major limitation of the procedure. Secondary intention healing may cause pain, bleeding, burning sensation, impaired oral function and reduced quality of life, potentially limiting patient acceptance of periodontal plastic surgery (Del Pizzo et al. 2002; Hall 1977; Silva et al. 2010; Ziyaettin et al. 2026).

To reduce palatal donor‐site morbidity and improve postoperative healing, various wound management approaches have been proposed, including the use of haemostatic agents, periodontal dressings, collagen‐based materials, cyanoacrylate adhesives, palatal stents and platelet concentrates (Basma et al. 2023; Scott et al. 2024; Thoma et al. 2012; Yilmaz et al. 2022). Nevertheless, there is still no consensus regarding the optimal palatal donor‐site management strategy capable of improving both clinical healing and patient comfort.

Recently, platelet‐rich fibrin (PRF) and its derivatives have gained attention as autologous biomaterials that support wound healing by releasing growth factors and bioactive mediators involved in angiogenesis, cellular migration and tissue maturation (Miron and Choukroun 2017). Injectable PRF (i‐PRF) is particularly promising because of its liquid formulation, ease of application and ability to be combined with other biomaterials (Miron et al. 2017; Ucak Turer et al. 2020; Demirbilek et al. 2025). Previous studies have suggested that PRF‐based approaches may improve patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) after palatal graft harvesting, although their effects on clinical wound healing remain inconsistent (Ozcan et al. 2017; Scott et al. 2024; Sen et al. 2024).

Chitosan is a natural polysaccharide widely used in oral wound management because of its haemostatic, bioadhesive and biocompatible properties, and its potential to support epithelialisation and tissue repair (Aksungur et al. 2004; Dash et al. 2011; Uraz et al. 2012; Franco et al. 2020). In periodontal applications, chitosan‐based dressings have demonstrated favourable effects on early palatal wound healing and bleeding control following graft harvesting procedures (Yadav et al. 2024). Beyond mechanical wound protection, chitosan may stabilise the wound environment and retain bioactive molecules at the surgical site.

Combining i‐PRF and chitosan may therefore represent a biologically relevant approach by integrating the scaffold‐forming and wound‐protective properties of chitosan with the potential bioactive effects of platelet‐derived mediators. However, whether incorporation of i‐PRF into a chitosan‐based dressing provides additional clinically meaningful benefits beyond chitosan alone, particularly regarding PROMs, remains unclear.

Therefore, the aim of this randomised controlled clinical trial was to compare chitosan‐based palatal wound management, with and without i‐PRF enrichment, against an absorbable gelatine sponge following FGG harvesting in terms of palatal donor‐site healing assessed using PEHI as the primary outcome and PROMs, postoperative bleeding, analgesic consumption and colour match as secondary outcomes. The null hypothesis was that chitosan or i‐PRF + chitosan would not improve palatal donor‐site healing compared with gelatine sponge control.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This three‐arm, parallel‐group, single‐blind randomised controlled clinical trial with a 56‐day follow‐up period was conducted at the Department of Periodontology, Faculty of Dentistry, Cukurova University. The study protocol was approved by the Cukurova University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee (protocol no: 152–27, 07/02/2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association 2013). The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07072546) and is reported according to the CONSORT 2010 guidelines (Figure S1).

Eligible patients undergoing FGG surgery were randomly allocated into three groups according to palatal donor‐site management: control (absorbable gelatine sponge), chitosan and i‐PRF + chitosan.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size calculation was based on a previously published clinical study evaluating PEHI scores at postoperative day 14 (Bozkurt and Ozdemir 2025). The standardised effect size (Cohen's f = 0.39) was calculated using reported group means and pooled within‐group standard deviations. With an α error probability of 0.05% and 80% power, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 66 participants (22 per group). Considering a possible 10% dropout rate, the target sample size was increased to 72 participants (24 per group).

Patient Selection

Participants were recruited between 12 July and 15 October, 2025. Follow‐up for the primary outcome was completed on 10 December 2025. Individuals aged ≥ 18 years who were periodontally healthy and demonstrated good oral hygiene (plaque score < 20%) were considered eligible. FGG was indicated in sites with insufficient KG (< 2 mm) associated with discomfort during tooth brushing or difficulty maintaining plaque control. Only non‐smokers and donor‐sites with a palatal mucosal thickness of ≥ 4 mm were included. Patients with systemic conditions contraindicating periodontal surgery or intraoperative conditions compromising protocol standardisation, including excessive palatal bleeding, were excluded.

Calibration

Before study initiation, the surgical and palatal donor‐site management protocols were discussed among the investigators. The blinded assessor (M.O.) underwent intra‐examiner calibration using 10 patients not included in the study, evaluated twice under identical conditions. Reliability was assessed using Cohen's κ for individual PEHI domains and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for the total PEHI score.

Randomisation and Blinding

Participants were randomly assigned using a computer‐generated randomisation sequence prepared by an independent investigator (M.C.H.) who was not involved in surgical procedures or outcome assessments. Simple randomisation with a 1:1:1 allocation ratio was applied without stratification or minimisation. Allocation was concealed using sealed, opaque envelopes opened immediately before palatal donor‐site management.

Clinical Evaluations

Outcome Assessment

Palatal donor‐site healing was assessed using PEHI at postoperative days 7, 14, 28 and 56 (Scott et al. 2024). The index includes bleeding on palpation, incomplete epithelialisation (assessed by immediate bubble formation after hydrogen peroxide application), redness, swelling and granulation tissue formation. Each parameter was scored dichotomously as yes (1) or no (2). The total score ranged from 5 to 10, with higher scores indicating more favourable healing. PEHI assessments were performed clinically by a single calibrated examiner (M.O.) during follow‐up visits.

Postoperative pain, burning sensation and changes in dietary habits were evaluated as PROMs using a 10‐cm visual analogue scale (VAS) at postoperative days 1, 3, 7, 14, 28 and 56. Participants marked symptom intensity on a 0–10 horizontal scale, where 0 indicated no symptoms or dietary change and 10 indicated the worst symptoms or maximum dietary change. Patients additionally recorded daily analgesic consumption and postoperative bleeding during the first postoperative week using standardised forms.

Colour match was assessed on postoperative days 14, 28 and 56 using a VAS from 0 (complete mismatch) to 10 (perfect match) by comparing the treated palatal area with adjacent and contralateral tissues (Yildirim et al. 2018). All assessments were conducted under identical conditions by the same blinded examiner.

Surgical Procedure and Postoperative Care

All surgical procedures were performed by an experienced periodontist (B.A.) under local anaesthesia (Ultracain, Aventis, Turkiye) following a standardised protocol. A supraperiosteal recipient bed was prepared using a trapezoidal partial‐thickness flap design.

FGGs were harvested from the palate between the maxillary first premolar and first molar, at least 2 mm apical to the gingival margin. Graft dimensions were measured using a periodontal calliper, and grafts were stabilised with 5–0 non‐resorbable monofilament sutures (Dogsan, Turkiye). Prior to graft harvesting, palatal mucosal thickness was assessed at three points using an endodontic reamer under local anaesthesia. This allowed the harvesting of an approximately 2‐mm‐thick graft while preserving adequate residual palatal tissue thickness (Zucchelli et al. 2010).

Immediately after graft harvesting, palatal donor‐site management was performed according to the group allocation. In the control group, the wound surface was compressed for 2 min and covered with an absorbable gelatine sponge (Surgispon, Aegis, India). In the chitosan group, a chitosan sponge (Axiostat, Axio, India) was applied.

In the i‐PRF + chitosan group, venous blood was collected into two sterile 10‐mL tubes without anticoagulant and immediately centrifuged in a fixed‐angle DuoCentrifuge system (Process for PRF, France; 40° rotor, 88‐mm radius) at 700 rpm for 3 min (approximately 60g) at 22°C–23°C, with relative centrifugal force calculated according to Miron et al. (2017). The upper liquid fraction was aspirated as i‐PRF, and the chitosan sponge was immersed in it for 15 min to ensure homogeneous scaffold absorption before application to the palatal donor site (Figure 1) (Ucak Turer et al. 2020).

(a) Chitosan sponge and i‐PRF. (b) i‐PRF‐enriched chitosan sponge.

All palatal donor‐site materials were stabilised using 4–0 absorbable poly(glycolide‐co‐lactide) sutures (Dogsan, Turkiye). Acrylic stents and periodontal dressings were avoided to prevent additional mechanical protection that could influence postoperative outcomes. Postoperative care was standardised: no antibiotics were prescribed; 500‐mg paracetamol was used as needed and recorded daily; and 0.12% chlorhexidine spray was prescribed twice daily for 4 weeks. Patients avoided brushing the surgical areas until suture removal and followed a soft diet for 1 week. Sutures were removed after 7 days, and follow‐up visits were carried out according to the study schedule (Figure 2).

Statistical Analysis

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The Kruskal–Wallis test was applied for data not showing normal distribution, and the Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc test was applied in cases where significant differences were detected. Within‐group changes in PEHI scores over time were analysed using the Friedman test. Each PEHI domain was analysed separately, with intergroup comparisons at each follow‐up performed using the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test. Cross‐sectional comparisons were performed at each time point using nonparametric tests, while within‐group changes over time were assessed using the Friedman test to account for the repeated‐measures design. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 72 participants were initially enrolled. Four participants did not complete the scheduled follow‐up protocol: two were lost after the 7‐day visit, and two attended outside the predefined schedule (days 12 and 14 instead of Day 7), resulting in protocol deviations. Consequently, 68 participants completed the study according to protocol and were included in the final analysis (Figure S1).

Cohen's κ coefficients for the individual PEHI domains ranged from 0.737 to 1.000. The ICC for the total PEHI score was 0.980 (95% CI: 0.926–0.995), indicating excellent intra‐examiner reliability.

No delayed wound healing, clinically significant postoperative bleeding requiring intervention, infection, allergic tissue reaction or material‐related complications were observed. Baseline demographic characteristics and intraoperative graft‐related parameters were comparable among groups (Table 1).

At Day 7, the chitosan group demonstrated significantly higher PEHI scores than the control group (p = 0.047), whereas the i‐PRF + chitosan group showed comparable early scores to the other groups. At days 14 and 28, both chitosan‐containing groups demonstrated higher scores than the control group (all p < 0.05). By postoperative day 56, PEHI scores became comparable among groups (p = 0.137).

Individual PEHI domains showed no intergroup difference in bleeding on palpation at any time point or in any domain at Day 56 (p > 0.05). Significant differences were found for incomplete epithelialisation at Day 28 (p = 0.003), redness at days 7, 14 and 28 (p = 0.031, 0.026 and 0.031), swelling at Day 14 (p < 0.001) and granulation tissue at Day 14 (p = 0.009), with unfavourable findings mainly in the control group (Table S1).

Postoperative pain and burning sensation scores decreased progressively over time in all groups. However, the i‐PRF + chitosan group consistently showed lower symptom severity during the early postoperative period compared with the control and chitosan groups (Figures 3 and 4).

Postoperative Bleeding and Painkiller Consumption

Postoperative bleeding frequencies decreased in all groups during the first week, with no intergroup differences at any time point (p > 0.05; Table S2).

No significant differences in painkiller consumption were observed during the first four postoperative days (p > 0.05). However, on postoperative days 5, 6 and 7, both chitosan‐containing groups demonstrated lower analgesic consumption than the control group (p < 0.05). The corresponding mean ± SD values in the control group were 0.39 ± 1.12, 0.35 ± 0.93 and 0.83 ± 1.03, respectively, compared with 0.00 ± 0.00 in both chitosan‐containing groups. Total painkiller consumption remained comparable among groups (p = 0.50).

Colour Match Scores

Both chitosan‐containing groups showed superior colour match scores throughout the healing period compared with the control group (Table S3). Significant intergroup differences were observed at days 14, 28 and 56 (all p < 0.001). At each interval, both the chitosan and i‐PRF + chitosan groups exhibited significantly higher colour match scores than the control group (p < 0.05), whereas the outcomes remained comparable between the two chitosan‐containing approaches.

Improved colour integration persisted throughout follow‐up in both test groups, compared with the control group.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that chitosan‐based palatal wound dressings improved early donor‐site healing compared with the absorbable gelatine sponge, while i‐PRF‐enriched chitosan also provided more favourable PROMs. Accordingly, the null hypothesis was rejected, as significant differences in palatal donor‐site healing were observed between both chitosan‐containing groups and the control group.

The development of wound dressings capable of supporting early tissue repair has gained attention in periodontal plastic surgery. Among these materials, chitosan‐based dressings have demonstrated promising clinical potential because of their haemostatic properties, bioadhesive behaviour, biocompatibility and ability to support epithelialisation and wound stabilisation (Franco et al. 2020; Yadav et al. 2024). The present study showed that chitosan‐based wound coverage was the principal determinant of improved early palatal donor‐site healing following FGG harvesting. Both chitosan‐based interventions resulted in superior PEHI scores compared with the control group particularly at days 14 and 28, whereas the adjunctive i‐PRF provided no additional measurable improvements in healing outcomes. Overall, our findings suggest that chitosan may play a major role in promoting observable palatal wound healing following FGG harvesting. Higher PEHI scores in the test groups appeared to be mainly driven by reduced redness, swelling, granulation tissue formation and incomplete epithelialisation rather than bleeding on palpation. However, by Day 56, healing scores converged among all groups, suggesting that chitosan accelerated early healing dynamics without altering the final healing outcome. A similar pattern has been reported in previous studies evaluating palatal donor‐site healing, where complete wound closure was eventually achieved in most patients regardless of the wound protection method used (Scott et al. 2024; Yadav et al. 2024). Femminella et al. (2016) also observed near‐complete healing of palatal donor sites during later follow‐up periods and emphasised that the differences between studies may largely reflect the methods used to assess wound healing rather than true biological differences in the final healing outcome.

The favourable healing in both chitosan groups may reflect its mechanical protection and bioadhesive scaffold properties, which stabilise the clot, maintain moisture, reduce irritation and support epithelial migration (Dash et al. 2011; Yadav et al. 2024). These effects are particularly relevant to palatal wounds exposed to trauma during speech and mastication. Consistent with our findings, Yadav et al. (2024) reported improved wound healing and accelerated re‐epithelization of palatal donor sites treated with a chitosan dressing and attributed these benefits to the haemostatic, bioadhesive, antimicrobial and tissue‐regenerative properties of chitosan. The absence of additional improvement in PEHI scores despite adjunctive i‐PRF application may indicate that the beneficial effects of chitosan‐based wound coverage predominate in the clinical healing response, thereby limiting the detectability of further improvements using composite clinical indices such as PEHI.

As i‐PRF was applied exclusively in combination with chitosan, its independent effect could not be assessed in the present study; therefore, the findings reflect a chitosan‐based scaffold rather than standalone i‐PRF efficacy. Nevertheless, although adjunctive i‐PRF application did improve clinical healing scores, the i‐PRF + chitosan group demonstrated more favourable PROMs during the early healing phase. Specifically, patients in this group reported lower pain and burning sensation scores, with fewer changes in dietary habits, particularly during the first postoperative week. Evidence regarding the effect of PRF on postoperative patient experience remains inconsistent. A recent systematic review reported that 4 of 10 studies comparing PRF with conventional palatal protection methods demonstrated an advantage in pain perception (Jankowski et al. 2024). Likewise, Basma et al. (2023) observed reductions in postoperative pain, bleeding, swelling, analgesic requirements and functional limitations following PRF application. In contrast, Scott et al. (2024) reported comparable pain scores and analgesic consumption between PRF and haemostatic agent groups. Similarly, Gatti et al. (2023) reported no significant differences between L‐PRF and haemostatic‐agent groups in analgesic consumption or postoperative discomfort. Importantly, direct comparisons between studies should be interpreted cautiously because the present study evaluated i‐PRF within a chitosan scaffold, which may alter local retention characteristics, degradation dynamics and temporal availability of platelet‐derived bioactive molecules.

From a clinical perspective, these findings are particularly relevant because palatal donor‐site morbidity remains one of the major limitations of FGG procedures (Zuhr et al. 2014). Previous studies have shown that discomfort following palatal graft harvesting peaks during the initial postoperative days and gradually decreases thereafter (Burkhardt et al. 2015). A similar temporal pattern was observed in the present study; however, patients treated with i‐PRF‐enriched chitosan experienced consistently lower pain and burning sensation scores during the early postoperative period. Even modest reductions in VAS pain scores may be clinically meaningful by improving oral function, dietary tolerance and patient comfort during the most symptomatic healing phase. PROMs are increasingly recognised as essential components of periodontal clinical research because they capture dimensions of treatment experience that are not always reflected by clinician‐reported healing indices (Tonetti et al. 2025).

Although analgesic consumption may reflect postoperative discomfort to some extent, it is not a direct surrogate for pain intensity because it is influenced by several patient‐related factors, including pain tolerance, medication preferences and attitudes towards analgesic use. In the present study, both chitosan‐containing groups demonstrated lower analgesic consumption than the control at specific time points, whereas pain reduction was more pronounced in the i‐PRF + chitosan group. These findings suggest that analgesic intake and perceived pain are related but not interchangeable outcomes. Therefore, analgesic consumption should be interpreted alongside PROM findings rather than as an isolated measure of postoperative discomfort.

Another clinically relevant finding was the superior colour match observed in both chitosan‐containing groups throughout the healing period. Colour integration is considered an important indicator of tissue maturation and inflammatory resolution during palatal healing (Zucchelli and Mounssif 2015). In the present study, evaluation was initiated at Day 14 because the first postoperative week is characterised by incomplete epithelialisation (Tavelli et al. 2023) and the presence of the chitosan dressing, both of which may affect the visual appearance of the wound surface. Superior colour match through Day 56 suggests that chitosan may support qualitative tissue remodelling in addition to early epithelialisation. Colour match improvements paralleled higher PEHI scores, supporting the relationship between accelerated early healing and improved tissue maturation.

Several limitations should be considered. First, this single‐centre study involved a single experienced operator, which may limit its generalisability. Second, the nature of the interventions precluded operator and patient blinding. This may have introduced expectation‐related bias, particularly in PROMs. Third, although PEHI provides a standardised clinical assessment tool, certain parameters remain partially subjective and examiner dependent. Fourth, although adequate palatal tissue thickness was confirmed before graft harvesting, it was not recorded as a study variable. Consequently, its potential association with wound‐healing outcomes and PROMs could not be investigated. Furthermore, the absence of histological, biomolecular or digital wound analysis limits mechanistic interpretation of the potential biological effects associated with i‐PRF and chitosan. Repeated measurements across time points may increase type I error, and the absence of mixed‐effects modelling precluded evaluation of group × time interactions. Therefore, temporal trends should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, because i‐PRF was not evaluated alone, its independent clinical contribution could not be determined.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this randomised clinical trial, chitosan‐based wound coverage significantly improved early palatal donor‐site healing following FGG harvesting compared with a gelatine sponge. The superior healing outcomes observed in both chitosan‐containing groups suggest that chitosan served as the principal determinant of the clinical healing benefit. Although adjunctive i‐PRF application did not provide additional improvements in clinician‐assessed healing outcomes, more favourable PROMs were observed during the early postoperative period. Collectively, these findings support the use of chitosan as an effective palatal wound management strategy and suggest that the addition of i‐PRF may improve postoperative patient comfort. Future adequately powered studies evaluating the independent effects of i‐PRF are needed.
