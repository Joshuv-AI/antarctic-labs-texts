Joint distributions of error terms for primes in arithmetic progressions modulo 11 11
Kübra Benli, Greg Martin, Paul Péringuey

Abstract
We provide a formula for the logarithmic density of the set of positive real numbers on which two prime counting functions ψ ⁡ ( x , q , a ) \psi(x;q,a) and ψ ⁡ ( x , q , b ) \psi(x;q,b) are simultaneously larger than their asymptotic main terms, as well as a method for calculating the numerical values of such densities with rigorously bounded errors. We apply these formulas to the pairwise races in the case q = 11 q=11 , determining which pairs of residues a a and b b are more or less correlated in this way. The outcomes when q = 11 q=11 provide a deeper mathematical illumination of the “mirror image” and “cyclic ordering” phenomena observed by Bays and Hudson.



1
Introduction

The prime counting functions in arithmetic progressions

\pi(x;q,a)=\#\{p\leq x\colon p\equiv a{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\psi(x;q,a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv a{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{k}\leq x\\
p^{k}\equiv a{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\end{subarray}}\log p

(for integers
q\geq 3
and
(a,q)=1
) are central objects of study in both classical analytic number theory and comparative prime number theory. Both functions have asymptotic formulas, and the normalized error terms

\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;q,a)=\frac{\varphi(q)\pi(x;q,\alpha)-\pi(x)}{\sqrt{x}/\log x}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a)=\frac{\varphi(q)\psi(x;q,\alpha)-\psi(x)}{\sqrt{x}}

(1.1)

are believed to have limiting logarithmic distribution functions that are symmetric about their mean values. Indeed, Rubinstein and Sarnak
[
9
]
established the existence of these distribution functions under two hypotheses on the zeros of Dirichlet
L
-functions:

•

the generalized Riemann hypothesis (GRH), which asserts that all nontrivial zeros of
L(s,\chi)
are of the form
\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma
for
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
;

•

the linear independence hypothesis (LI), which asserts that the collection of all nonnegative such ordinates
\gamma
is linearly independent over the rational numbers.

In particular, spurred by observations (going back to Chebyshev) that some arithmetic progressions seem to contain more primes than others, Rubinstein and Sarnak’s work allowed them to examine comparisons between two prime counting functions (equivalently, between their normalized error terms). For instance, they showed how to calculate the logarithmic density of the set of positive real numbers
x
for which
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;q,a_{1})>\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;q,a_{2})
(which is the same as the set for which
\pi(x;q,a_{1})>\pi(x;q,a_{2})
, since jointly normalizing the error terms preserves their ordering).

In this paper, we are interested not in direct comparisons of these normalized error terms but rather in correlations between them. Our work is motivated by observations of Bays and Hudson
[
2
]
in the case
q=11
, where they plotted the ten normalized error terms
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,a)
for
1\leq a\leq 10
. (Their plots actually represented normalized error terms corresponding to the integrals
\int_{0}^{x}\pi(u;11,a)\,du
, but we will consider the unintegrated version in this paper.) Bays and Hudson pointed out two striking phenomena regarding the leaders (the residue class
a
that maximizes
\pi(x;11,a)
for given values of
x
) and trailers (the residue class
a
that minimizes
\pi(x;11,a)
) of this ten-way prime number race.

•

Mirror image phenomenon
: The leader and the trailer tended to be additive inverses of each other modulo
11
; for example,
\pi(x;11,8)
tend to be largest at the same time that
\pi(x;11,3)
was smallest.

•

Cyclic ordering phenomenon
: The leaders came from the set of quadratic nonresidues
8,6,10,2,7
while the trailers came from the set of quadratic residues
3,5,1,9,4
(this much was familiar to those acquainted with comparative prime number theory); surprisingly, however, the leaders and trailers tended to cycle through those lists
in that particular order
as
x
increased.

In theory, one could study the mirror image phenomenon by computing the logarithmic densities of all
10!
possible orderings of the
\pi(x;11,a)
, restricting to the
9!
orderings where the leader is
8
(for instance), calculating the total of the
8!
such densities for which
3
is simultaneously the trailer, and comparing this total to the corresponding totals for other potential trailers. Unfortunately, this ten-dimensional problem is computationally intractable in practice. Instead, we focus in this paper on pairs of error functions
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,a_{1})
and
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,a_{2})
, calculating how often they are both larger than their mean values. The mirror image phenomenon suggests that
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,8)
and
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,3)
(for instance) are negatively correlated and that the density of the set of
x
where both are larger than average should be less than
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{4}
, the product of the densities of the sets where the two error terms are individually larger than average. (We remark in passing that Fiorilli and the second author
[
3
, Section 4.3]
took a different approach to analyzing the mirror image phenomenon by calculating variances of the logarithmic distributions of sums
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,a_{1})+\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,a_{2})
.) Since we are interested in comparing these error terms
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,a)
to their mean values (which depend on whether
a
is a quadratic residue or nonresidue), we actually consider the related error terms
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;11,a)
, which have the same logarithmic distribution except that all their mean values vanish.

Studying the cyclic ordering phemonenon using the tools of comparative prime number theory seems unlikely, since the limiting logarithmic distribution (essentially a continuous normalized histogram of the values taken by the function) loses all information about the sequence in which those values were taken. Upon reflection, though, it seems reasonable to again use the correlations of pairs of error terms as a proxy for this phenomenon. Consider a real number
x
for which
10
is the leader, for instance, so that
\pi(x;11,10)
is largest among the ten prime counting functions: if it is more likely that
6
is the previous (distinct) leader and
2
is the subsequent leader, then it seems that both
\pi(x;11,6)
and
\pi(x;11,2)
should be reasonably large as well, since
\pi(x;11,6)
was recently the largest of the ten and
\pi(x;11,2)
soon will be. We postulate, therefore, that
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,10)
is more positively correlated with both
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,6)
and
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,2)
than it is with either
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,8)
or
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;11,7)
.

With this discussion in mind, we define the following correlation densities.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a modulus
q\geq 3
, two distinct reduced residue classes
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
modulo
q
, and two signs
s_{1},s_{2}\in\{-,+\}
, define
\delta^{s_{1},s_{2}}(q;a_{1},a_{2})
to be the logarithmic density of the set of positive real numbers
x
for which both
s_{1}\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a_{1})>0
and
s_{2}\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a_{2})>0
. For example,
\delta^{++}(q;a_{1},a_{2})
is the logarithmic density of the set where
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a_{1})
and
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a_{2})
are simultaneously positive, or equivalently the logarithmic density of the set where the vector-valued function
\bigl(\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a_{1}),\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a_{2})\bigr)
lies in the open first quadrant.

Furthermore, when
q
is understood from context, let
\delta^{s_{1},s_{2}}_{a}
be a shorthand for
\delta^{s_{1},s_{2}}(q;a,1)
, and indeed for
\delta^{s_{1},s_{2}}(q;ab,b)
for any reduced residue class
b{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}
. (We will see that
\delta^{s_{1},s_{2}}(q;ab,b)
is independent of the choice of

in Proposition
2.2
below; this symmetry is also implicit in the work of Feuerverger and the second author
[
3

, although there they treated
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\pi}(x;q,a)
rather than
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a)
.)

We may now state our main result, concerning these correlation densities modulo
11
.

Remark 1.3
.

The bottom left entry, which is the smallest density in the table, confirms the mirror image phenomenon: it shows that
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a)
and
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,10a)=\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,11-a)

Remark 1.4
.

The rows with even exponents in the right-hand table contain densities relevant to the cyclic ordering phenomenon for trailers, since
\delta_{a}^{++}=\delta_{a}^{--}
measures how often
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a)
and
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,1)
are simultaneously negative. Note that

to write down a formula for these densities in terms of an integral involving a two-dimensional Fourier transform. In the second half, we specialize to the case

and describe how to compute these densities with rigorously bounded error terms, and compare them with the aforementioned model.

2
A formula for the probabilities

The tools of comparative prime number theory allow us to write down a formula for densities associated with the error terms
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a)
, in terms of integrals involving the Fourier transform of the limiting logarithmic distributions of those error terms. In this section we present these tools in a somewhat different way, involving tempered distributions, which we hope clarifies the process of calculating such formulas. First, however, we begin by writing down that Fourier transform and noting a connection to a particular random variable.

2.1
A random variable and its characteristic function

In this section, we set our notation and focus on the two-dimensional random variable relevant to the densities in Theorem
1.2
. All the material in this section is well known to experts, although the statements from this section are hard to find exactly as written in the literature—primarily because most authors focus on
\pi(x;q,a)
instead of
\psi(x;q,a)
, and also because we prefer explicitly using random variables rather than having them implicit in the background. The reader can refer to
[
1
, Section 1]
,
[
4
, Section 2.1]
,
[
7
, Section 12.1]
, and
[
9
, Section 2.1]
for closely analogous formulas.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a modulus
q\geq 3
and a reduced residue class
a{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}
, define the random variable

X(q;a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}(1,\chi(a))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{Z_{\gamma}}{1/2+i\gamma},

where the
Z_{\gamma}
are random variables uniformly distributed on the unit circle in
\mathbb{C}
, independent except that
Z_{-\gamma}=\overline{Z_{\gamma}}
; because of this convention and the symmetries of zeros of Dirichlet
L
-functions, the components of
X(q;a)
are actually real-valued. When
q
and
a
are understood from context, we write
X
and
d\mu_{X}
for the random variable
X(q;a)
and its distribution, respectively. Also, the characteristic function of
X(q;a)
is

\phi_{X}(t_{1},t_{2})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}J_{0}\biggl(\frac{2|t_{1}+\chi(a)t_{2}|}{\sqrt{1/4+\gamma^{2}}}\biggr),

(2.1)

which follows from the standard computation that the characteristic function of
(Z_{\gamma}+\overline{Z_{\gamma}})(v_{1},v_{2})
is
J_{0}\bigl(2|v_{1}t_{1}+v_{2}t_{2}|\bigr)
for any
(v_{1},v_{2}),(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It is obvious that
\phi_{X}(t_{1},t_{2})=\phi_{X}(-t_{1},-t_{2})
; it turns out also to be true that
\phi_{X}(t_{1},t_{2})=\phi_{X}(t_{2},t_{1})
when
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, since

|t_{2}+\chi(a)t_{1}|=\bigl|\chi(a)(\overline{\chi}(a)t_{2}+t_{1})\bigr|=|t_{1}+\overline{\chi}(a)t_{2}|=|t_{1}+\chi(a)t_{2}|.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume GRH and LI. The limiting logarithmic distribution of the vector
\bigl(\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,\alpha),\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,\beta)\bigr)
exists, and the Fourier transform of that distribution is

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}J_{0}\biggl(\frac{2|t_{1}+\chi(\alpha\beta^{-1})t_{2}|}{\sqrt{1/4+\gamma^{2}}}\biggr),

(2.2)

where
J_{0}(z)
denotes the standard Bessel function of order zero. In particular, the limiting logarithmic distribution of
\bigl(\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,\alpha),\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,\beta)\bigr)
is the same as the distribution of the random variable
X(q;a)
where
a\equiv\alpha\beta^{-1}{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}
.

Proof.

We begin with the explicit formula (assuming GRH)

\displaystyle\psi(x,q,a)=\frac{\psi(x)}{\varphi(q)}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi}(a)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\\
|\gamma|\leq T\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{x^{1/2+i\gamma}}{1/2+i\gamma}+E_{T}(x)

for
x,T\geq 2
,
where
\varphi
denotes the Euler totient function, and
E_{T}(x)
is an error term for which suitable bounds exist both pointwise and in
L^{2}
-norm. It follows from the definition (
1.1
) that

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,\alpha),\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,\beta)\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\bigl(\overline{\chi}(\alpha),\overline{\chi}(\beta)\bigr)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\\
|\gamma|\leq T\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{x^{i\gamma}}{1/2+i\gamma}+\frac{E_{T}(x)}{\sqrt{x}}.

This formula allows us to apply
[
1
, Theorem 1.9]
to conclude that the limiting logarithmic distribution of the left-hand side does indeed exist and that its Fourier transform is

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}J_{0}\biggl(\frac{2|{-}\overline{\chi}(\alpha)t_{1}-\overline{\chi}(\beta)t_{2}|}{\sqrt{1/4+\gamma^{2}}}\biggr).

Because
|{-}\overline{\chi}(\alpha)t_{1}-\overline{\chi}(\beta)t_{2}|=\bigl|{-}\overline{\chi}(\alpha)\bigl(t_{1}+\chi(\alpha\beta^{-1})t_{2}\bigr)\bigr|=|t_{1}+\chi(\alpha\beta^{-1})t_{2}|
,
this formula is equivalent to equation (
2.2
). The final assertion of the proposition follows upon comparison to equation (
2.1
).
∎

2.2
Tempered distributions

In this section, we gather just enough information about tempered distributions for our purposes in the next section.

Definition 2.3
.

The
Schwartz space

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the vector space of infinitely differentiable functions
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
such that every partial derivative of
\phi
decays to zero (in all directions) even when multiplied by any polynomial. In particular,

\|\phi\|_{a_{1},\dots,a_{d},b_{1},\dots,b_{d}}=\sup\biggl\{\biggl|t_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots t_{d}^{a_{d}}\frac{\partial^{b_{1}+\cdots+b_{d}}}{\partial t_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\partial t_{d}^{b_{d}}}\phi(\vec{t})\biggr|\colon\vec{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\biggr\}

(2.3)

exists for all nonnegative integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{d},b_{1},\dots,b_{d}
.

The space of
tempered distributions
is the dual space of Schwartz space; in other words, a tempered distribution is a function (or operator)
T\colon\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{C}
that is both linear and continuous.
We write
\langle T,\phi\rangle
as a synonym for
T(\phi)
.

The
Fourier transform
of a tempered distribution
T
is the tempered distribution
\mathcal{F}(T)
such that
\langle\mathcal{F}(T),\phi\rangle=\langle T,\mathcal{F}(\phi)\rangle
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\mathcal{F}(\phi)
is the usual Fourier transform of an integrable function on
\mathbb{R}
. This Fourier transform is an automorphism of the space of tempered distributions, and indeed it is straightforward to check that

\mathcal{F}^{-1}(T)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\overline{\mathcal{F}(\overline{T})}.

(2.4)

Remark 2.4
.

One example of a tempered distribution is the Dirac delta operator
\delta_{d}
which satisfies
\langle\delta_{d},\phi\rangle=\phi(\vec{0})
.
Another example is an integral operator
\langle F,\phi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\vec{t})\phi(\vec{t})\,d\vec{t}
where
f
is a function of at most polynomial growth.
Indeed we will be primarily interested in this latter tempered distribution with
f
being the indicator function
\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)}
(because of equation (
2.7
) below); its Fourier transform will involve singular tempered distributions such as
\delta_{2}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

The following operators are all tempered distributions:

(a)

the Dirac
\delta_{2}\colon\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which is the tempered distribution such that
\langle\delta_{2},\phi\rangle=\phi(0,0)
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

(b)

the “left-variable Cauchy principal value”
PV_{\ell}\colon\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which is the tempered distribution such that
\langle PV_{\ell},\phi\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|u|\geq\varepsilon}\frac{\phi(u,0)}{u}\,du
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

(c)

the “right-variable Cauchy principal value”
PV_{r}\colon\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which is the tempered distribution such that
\langle PV_{r},\phi\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|v|\geq\varepsilon}\frac{\phi(0,v)}{v}\,dv
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

(d)

the two-dimensional Cauchy principal value
PV_{2}\colon\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which is the tempered distribution such that
\langle PV_{2},\phi\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|u|\geq\varepsilon}\int_{|v|\geq\varepsilon}\frac{\phi(u,v)}{uv}\,dv\,du
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof.

The nontrivial part of the proof is to show that the limit in part (d) actually exists; we confirm that fact first and then establish the proposition in its entirety.

Given
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\varepsilon>0
, we write

\displaystyle\int_{|u|\geq\varepsilon}\int_{|v|\geq\varepsilon}\frac{\phi(u,v)}{uv}\,dv\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{\phi(u,v)-\phi(u,-v)-\phi(-u,v)+\phi(-u,-v)}{uv}\,dv\,du

\displaystyle=I_{0}+I_{u}(\varepsilon)+I_{v}(\varepsilon)+I_{uv}(\varepsilon),

(2.5)

where we define

\displaystyle I_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\phi(u,v)-\phi(u,-v)-\phi(-u,v)+\phi(-u,-v)}{uv}\,dv\,du

\displaystyle I_{v}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{\phi(u,v)-\phi(u,-v)-\phi(-u,v)+\phi(-u,-v)}{uv}\,dv\,du

\displaystyle I_{u}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\phi(u,v)-\phi(u,-v)-\phi(-u,v)+\phi(-u,-v)}{uv}\,dv\,du

\displaystyle I_{uv}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{\phi(u,v)-\phi(u,-v)-\phi(-u,v)+\phi(-u,-v)}{uv}\,dv\,du.

Certainly
I_{0}
converges, indeed

\displaystyle|I_{0}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{|uv\phi(u,v)|+|u(-v)\phi(u,-v)|+|(-u)v\phi(-u,v)|+|(-u)(-v)\phi(-u,-v)|}{u^{2}v^{2}}\,dv\,du

\displaystyle\leq 4\|\phi\|_{1,1,0,0}

in the notation of equation (
2.3
). For
I_{v}(\varepsilon)
, we use the identity
\phi(u,v)-\phi(u,-v)=\int_{-v}^{v}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial v}(u,t)\,dt
to write

\displaystyle|I_{v}(\varepsilon)|

\displaystyle=\biggl|\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\int_{-v}^{v}\frac{u\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial v}(u,t)-u\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial v}(-u,t)}{u^{2}v}\,dt\,dv\,du\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\int_{-v}^{v}\frac{2\|\phi\|_{1,0,0,1}}{u^{2}v}\,dt\,dv\,du\leq 4\|\phi\|_{1,0,0,1},

and in particular
I_{v}(\varepsilon)
converges by the dominated convergence theorem. A similar argument shows that
|I_{u}(\varepsilon)|\leq 4\|\phi\|_{0,1,1,0}
. Finally,

\displaystyle|I_{uv}(\varepsilon)|

\displaystyle=\biggl|\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\int_{-v}^{v}\frac{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial v}(u,t)-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial v}\phi(-u,t)}{uv}\,dt\,dv\,du\biggr|

\displaystyle=\biggl|\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\int_{-v}^{v}\int_{-u}^{u}\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial v\partial u}(s,t)}{uv}\,ds\,dt\,dv\,du\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\int_{-v}^{v}\int_{-u}^{u}\frac{\|\phi\|_{0,0,1,1}}{u^{2}v}\,ds\,dt\,dv\,du\leq 4\|\phi\|_{0,0,1,1}.

In light of equation (
2.5
), these calculations show that

\bigl|\langle PV_{2},\phi\rangle\bigr|=\biggl|\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|u|\geq\varepsilon}\int_{|v|\geq\varepsilon}\frac{\phi(u,v)}{uv}\,dv\,du\biggr|\leq 4\bigl(\|\phi\|_{1,1,0,0}+\|\phi\|_{1,0,0,1}+\|\phi\|_{0,1,1,0}+\|\phi\|_{0,0,1,1}\bigr)

(2.6)

and that the limit in part (d) exists. Moreover, a bound of the shape (
2.6
) is enough to guarantee that
PV_{2}
is continuous (see
[
8
, page 134]
). Since linearity is trivial, we conclude that
PV_{2}
is a tempered distribution.

As for the other parts of the proposition, part (c) can be confirmed by replacing
\phi(u,v)
with say
\frac{u}{2}e^{-|u|}\phi(0,v)
, in part (d). Part (b) can be confirmed similarly, and part (a) follows in the same way or by direct verification.
∎

2.3
Application to the first quadrant density

Given a modulus
q\geq 3
and a reduced residue class
a{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}
, we would like to compute
\delta^{++}_{a}
, the logarithmic density of the set

\{x\geq 0\colon\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a)>0,\,\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,1)>0\}.

By Proposition
2.2
, this quantity is the same as

\delta^{++}_{a}=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}>0,\,X_{2}>0)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,d\mu_{X}=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)}(x,y)\,d\mu_{X}

(2.7)

in the notation of Definition
2.1
. To evaluate the right-hand side, we will use Plancherel’s theorem so that we can access our information about the characteristic function
\phi_{X}
, which is an element of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
. Consequently, we need to calculate the (inverse) Fourier transform of the tempered distribution corresponding to integrating against the function
\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

In the sense of tempered distributions,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)})

\displaystyle=\pi^{2}\delta_{2}-i\pi(PV_{\ell}+PV_{r})-PV_{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\delta_{2}+\frac{i}{4\pi}(PV_{\ell}+PV_{r})-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}PV_{2}.

Remark 2.7
.

The second formula is analogous to the two-dimensional case of
[
3
, Theorem 4]
; both formulas can also be derived abstractly using the fact that the tempered distribution corresponding to integrating against
\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)}
is the tensor product of the Heaviside distribution with itself. On the other hand, a direct and concrete proof is short enough to be worth including in its entirety.

Proof.

For any Schwartz function
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

\displaystyle\langle\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)},\mathcal{F}(\phi)\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)}(\xi,\eta)\iint\phi(x,y)e^{-i(x\xi+y\eta)}\,dx\,dy\,d\xi\,d\eta

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\iint\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)}(\xi,\eta)\iint\phi(x,y)e^{-i(x\xi+y\eta)}e^{-\varepsilon(\xi+\eta)}\,dx\,dy\,d\xi\,d\eta

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\iint\phi(x,y)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi(ix+\varepsilon)}d\xi\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\eta(iy+\varepsilon)}d\eta\right)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\iint\phi(x,y)\frac{1}{(ix+\varepsilon)(iy+\varepsilon)}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\iint\phi(x,y)\frac{1}{(ix+\varepsilon)(iy+\varepsilon)}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\iint\phi(x,y)\frac{\varepsilon^{2}-i\varepsilon(x+y)-xy}{(x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})(y^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})}\,dx\,dy.

We evaluate this last integral in four parts. In the first part, we use the change of variables
u=x/\varepsilon
and
v=y/\varepsilon
to see that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\varepsilon^{2}\iint\frac{\phi(x,y)}{(x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})(y^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\iint\frac{\phi(\varepsilon u,\varepsilon v)}{(1+u^{2})(1+v^{2})}\,du\,dv=\pi^{2}\phi(0,0)=\langle\pi^{2}\delta_{2},\phi\rangle.

In the second one, we set
v=y/\varepsilon
to obtain

\displaystyle-i\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\varepsilon\iint\frac{\phi(x,y)x}{(x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})(y^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})}\,dx\,dy=-i\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\iint\frac{\phi(x,\varepsilon v)x}{(x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})(1+v^{2})}\,dx\,dv

\displaystyle=\langle-i\pi PV_{\ell},\phi\rangle

in the notation of Proposition
2.5
;
an analogous argument shows that

\displaystyle-i\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\varepsilon\iint\frac{\phi(x,y)y}{(x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})(y^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})}\,dx\,dy=\langle-i\pi PV_{r},\phi\rangle.

Finally,

\displaystyle-\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\iint\frac{\phi(x,y)xy}{(x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})(y^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\langle-PV_{2},\phi\rangle.

These establish the first assertion of the proposition. The second assertion follows directly from equation (
2.4
).
∎

We are now ready to obtain an expression for the logarithmic density of interest in terms of the characteristic function
\phi_{X}
(analogous to formulas in
[
3
, end of Section 2D]
).

Proposition 2.8
.

Assume GRH and LI. For any modulus
q\geq 3
and any reduced residue class
a{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}
,

\delta^{++}_{a}=\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|\xi|>\varepsilon}\int_{|\eta|>\varepsilon}\frac{\phi_{X}(\xi,\eta)-\phi_{X}(0,\eta)\phi_{X}(\xi,0)}{\xi\eta}\,d\xi\,d\eta.

Proof.

By equation (
2.7
) and Proposition
2.6
,

\displaystyle\delta^{++}_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0})=\langle\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)},d\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)},\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bigl(\mathcal{F}(d\mu)\bigr)\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)}),\phi_{X}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\phi_{X}(0,0)+\frac{i}{4\pi}\langle PV_{\ell}+PV_{r},\phi_{X}\rangle-\frac{i}{4\pi^{2}}\langle PV_{2},\phi_{X}\rangle.

For the first term,
\phi_{X}(0,0)=1
since
d\mu
is a probability measure. Next,

\langle PV_{\ell},\phi_{X}\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|\xi|>\varepsilon}\frac{\phi_{X}(\xi,0)}{\xi}d\xi=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{\phi_{X}(\xi,0)-\phi_{X}(-\xi,0)}{\xi}d\xi=0

since
\phi_{X}(\xi,0)
is an even function; similarly
\langle PV_{r},\phi_{X}\rangle=0
, as well. Finally

\langle PV_{2},\phi_{X}\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|\xi|>\varepsilon}\int_{|\eta|>\varepsilon}\frac{\phi_{X}(\xi,\eta)}{\xi\eta}\,d\xi\,d\eta=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|\xi|>\varepsilon}\int_{|\eta|>\varepsilon}\frac{\phi_{X}(\xi,\eta)-\phi_{X}(0,\eta)\phi_{X}(\xi,0)}{\xi\eta}\,d\xi\,d\eta,

again since
\phi_{X}(0,\eta)\phi_{X}(\xi,0)
is an even function (of either variable).
∎

3
Evaluating double integrals with rigorous error bounds

We have seen in Proposition
2.8
that the key to understanding
\delta_{a}^{++}
is the limiting integral

I=\lim_{\upsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{|\xi|>\upsilon}\int_{|\eta|>\upsilon}\frac{\phi_{X}(\xi,\eta)-\phi_{X}(0,\eta)\phi_{X}(\xi,0)}{\xi\eta}\,d\xi\,d\eta

(3.1)

(where we have changed
\varepsilon
to
\upsilon
to avoid an imminent clash of notation). In the method of Feuerverger and the second author
[
3
]
, which itself was adapted from Rubinstein and Sarnak
[
9
]
, three steps are required to convert this unbounded integral of a function involving an infinite product into a finitely calculable quantity.
We devote the next sections to each of these steps—discretizing the integral, bounding the range of summation of the resulting sum, and truncating the infinite product—with attention to rigorous bounds for the error terms in each step. All of the material in this section applies to general moduli
q
; we specialize to
q=11
in Section
4
.

3.1
Discretizing the integral

To discretize the integral
I
, we introduce the quantities

S(\varepsilon)=4\sum_{m,n\ \text{odd}}\frac{\phi_{X}(\frac{m\varepsilon}{2},\frac{n\varepsilon}{2})}{mn}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptE_{1}(\varepsilon)=I-S(\varepsilon).

(3.2)

(All indices of summation are restricted to integer values throughout.)
We bound this first error
E_{1}(\varepsilon)
using the Poisson summation formula and the symmetries of our integrand. Fortunately, we are able to invoke prior work to quickly dispose of this step.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X=(X_{1},X_{2})
be the random variable from Definition
2.1
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
.

|E_{1}(\varepsilon)|\leq 8\pi^{2}\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa,\lambda\geq 0\\
(\kappa,\lambda)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\min\bigl\{\mathbb{P}(X_{1}>2\pi\kappa/\varepsilon),\mathbb{P}(X_{2}>2\pi\lambda/\varepsilon)\bigr\}.

Proof.

It was shown in
[
3
, equations (3-7) and (3-9)]
that

\biggl|I-4\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{\phi_{X}(\frac{m\varepsilon}{2},\frac{n\varepsilon}{2})-\phi_{X}(\frac{m\varepsilon}{2},0)\phi_{X}(0,\frac{n\varepsilon}{2})}{mn}\biggr|\\
\leq 8\pi^{2}\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa,\lambda\geq 0\\
(\kappa,\lambda)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\min\bigl\{\mathbb{P}(X_{1}>2\pi\kappa/\varepsilon),\mathbb{P}(X_{2}>2\pi\lambda/\varepsilon)\bigr\}.

(3.3)

(Unfortunately, a factor of
2
was omitted from the right-hand side of
[
3
, equation (3-8)]
.) That calculation was carried out for an integrand involving a specific function
\hat{\rho}_{8;3,5,7}
, but the calculation is valid for any Schwartz function symmetric about the origin, including
\phi_{X}
. The proposition follows upon noting that the part of the left-hand sum involving
{\phi_{X}(\frac{m\varepsilon}{2},0)\phi_{X}(0,\frac{n\varepsilon}{2})}/{mn}
vanishes due to the fact that
\phi_{X}
is even in either variable.
∎

3.2
Truncating the range of summation

To truncate the infinite sum
S(\varepsilon)
, we now introduce the quantities

S(\varepsilon,C)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|m|,|n|\leq C\\
m,n\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{\phi_{X}(\frac{m\varepsilon}{2},\frac{n\varepsilon}{2})}{mn}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptE_{2}(\varepsilon,C)=\frac{1}{4}S(\varepsilon)-S(\varepsilon,C)

(3.4)

for any real
C\geq 1
, so that

\bigl|E_{2}(\varepsilon,C)\bigr|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|m|>C\text{ or }|n|>C\\
m,n\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}\biggl|\frac{\phi_{X}(\frac{m\varepsilon}{2},\frac{n\varepsilon}{2})}{mn}\biggr|\leq 4\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\
|n|\leq m\\
m,n\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}\biggl|\frac{\phi_{X}(\frac{m\varepsilon}{2},\frac{n\varepsilon}{2})}{mn}\biggr|

(3.5)

thanks to the symmetries noted at the end of Definition
2.1
(we have double-counted those points for which
|m|=|n|
, but the upper bound remains valid).
In the arguments to come we will need to treat individual Dirichlet characters differently, and so we introduce the following notation.

Definition 3.2
.

Fix a modulus
q\geq 3
. For every Dirichlet character
\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}
, define

\displaystyle F(z,\chi)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}J_{0}\left(\frac{2z}{\sqrt{1/4+\gamma^{2}}}\right),

(3.6)

which allows us to rewrite equation (
2.1
) as

\displaystyle\phi_{X}(t_{1},t_{2})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}F(\left|t_{1}+\chi(a)t_{2}\right|,\chi).

(3.7)

Further define

B_{2}(a,\varepsilon,C)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\
|n|\leq m\\
m,n\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}\biggl|\frac{1}{mn}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}F\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|m+\chi(a)n|,\chi\biggr)\biggr|,

so that the upper bound in equation (
3.5
) is simply
\bigl|E_{2}(\varepsilon,C)\bigr|\leq 4B_{2}(a,\varepsilon,C)
.

It is harder to get an upper bound for
F\bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|m+\chi(a)n|,\chi\bigr)
when
\mathop{\rm Re}\chi(a)
is close to
-\frac{m}{n}
, since
F(t,\chi)
is largest when
t\in\mathbb{R}
is near
0
. Consequently, for various sizes of
m
and
n
we choose to restrict to appropriate subsets of characters when creating such upper bounds.
The following proposition, which implements this strategy, is written in as flexible a form as possible to facilitate use by future researchers; it is not as ugly as it seems.

Proposition 3.3
.

Fix a modulus
q\geq 3
. For each nontrivial character
\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}
, fix positive constants
d_{+}(\chi),d_{-}(\chi),d(\chi)
and
e_{+}(\chi),e_{-}(\chi),e(\chi)
such that

\displaystyle|F(x,\chi)|\leq\min\{1,d_{+}(\chi)|x|^{-e_{+}(\chi)},d_{-}(\chi)|x|^{-e_{-}(\chi)},d(\chi)|x|^{-e(\chi)}\}

(3.8)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Choose real numbers
b>1
,
c\in[0,1]
,
c_{+}\in[0,1]
and
c_{-}\in[-1,0]
, and set

\displaystyle d_{c_{+}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))\geq c_{+}\end{subarray}}d_{+}(\chi)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}

\displaystyle e_{c_{+}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))\geq c_{+}\end{subarray}}e_{+}(\chi)

\displaystyle d_{c_{-}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))\leq c_{-}\end{subarray}}d_{-}(\chi)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}

\displaystyle e_{c_{-}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))\leq c_{-}\end{subarray}}e_{-}(\chi)

\displaystyle d_{c}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
|\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))|\leq c\end{subarray}}d(\chi)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}

\displaystyle e_{c}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
|\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))|\leq c\end{subarray}}e(\chi).

If
e_{c}>1
, then for any real numbers
\varepsilon>0
and
C\geq 1
,

B_{2}(a,\varepsilon,C)\leq B_{2}^{+}(a,\varepsilon,C)+B_{2}^{-}(a,\varepsilon,C)+\widetilde{B}_{2}(a,\varepsilon,C),

(3.9)

where

\displaystyle B_{2}^{+}(a,\varepsilon,C)

\displaystyle=\frac{bd_{c_{+}}}{4e_{c_{+}}}\left(1-\frac{1}{b}\right)\left(\varepsilon\left(1+\frac{c_{+}}{b}\right)\right)^{-e_{c_{+}}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{C}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right)^{-e_{c_{+}}}

\displaystyle B_{2}^{-}(a,\varepsilon,C)

\displaystyle=\frac{bd_{c_{-}}}{4e_{c_{-}}}\left(1-\frac{1}{b}\right)\left(\varepsilon\left(1-\frac{c_{-}}{b}\right)\right)^{-e_{c_{-}}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{C}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right)^{-e_{c_{-}}}

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{2}(a,\varepsilon,C)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{c}}{e_{c}-1}\left(\frac{C}{2b}+1\right)\left(\varepsilon\left(1-\frac{c}{b}\right)\right)^{-e_{c}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{C}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right)^{-e_{c}}.

Remark 3.4
.

The existence of suitable constants
d(\chi)
and
e(\chi)
in equation (
3.8
) is assured by
[
3
, equation (3.19)]
; there is a trade-off between the sizes of
d(\chi)
and
e(\chi)
, and the three variants present allow for different choices to be made in different parts of the estimation argument. In Section
4
we demonstrate how to compute such constants in the case
q=11
.

Proof.

For each
m
in the sum defining
B_{2}(a,\varepsilon,C)
, we break the sum over
n
into three ranges depending on its size relative to
m
. In each range, we are free to omit whichever characters
\chi
we wish from the product, thanks to the bound
|F(x,\chi)|\leq 1
from the hypothesis (
3.8
). Specifically, when
n
is large and positive we keep only characters
\chi
for which
\mathop{\rm Re}\chi(a)
is sufficiently positive; when
n
is large and negative we keep only those for which
\mathop{\rm Re}\chi(a)
is sufficiently negative; and when
|n|
is small we keep only those for which
|\mathop{\rm Re}\chi(a)|
is also small.
In this manner we write

\displaystyle B_{2}(a,\varepsilon,C)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\
m\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\Biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{m}{b}<n\leq m\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|mn|}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\\
\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))\geq c_{+}\end{subarray}}\biggl|F\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|m+\chi(a)n|,\chi\biggr)\biggr|

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt{}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-m\leq n<-\frac{m}{b}\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|mn|}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\\
\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))\leq c_{-}\end{subarray}}\biggl|F\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|m+\chi(a)n|,\chi\biggr)\biggr|

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt{}+\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}|n|\leq\frac{m}{b}\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}}\frac{1}{|mn|}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\\
|\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))|\leq c\end{subarray}}\biggl|F\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|m+\chi(a)n|,\chi\biggr)\biggr|\Biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\
m\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\Biggl(b\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{m}{b}<n\leq m\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m^{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\\
\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))\geq c_{+}\end{subarray}}d_{+}(\chi)\left|\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(m+\chi(a)n)\right|^{-e_{+}(\chi)}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt{}+b\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-m\leq n<-\frac{m}{b}\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m^{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\\
\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))\leq c_{-}\end{subarray}}d_{-}(\chi)\left|\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(m+\chi(a)n)\right|^{-e_{-}(\chi)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt{}+\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}|n|\leq\frac{m}{b}\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}}\frac{1}{m}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\\
|\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))|\leq c\end{subarray}}d(\chi)\left|\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(m+\chi(a)n)\right|^{-e(\chi)}\Biggr).

In the first product we have
|m+\chi(a)n|\geq\mathop{\rm Re}(m+\chi(a)n)\geq m+c_{+}n>m(1+\frac{c_{+}}{b})
, while in the second product we have
|m+\chi(a)n|\geq\mathop{\rm Re}(m-\chi(a)n)\geq m-c_{-}n\geq m(1-\frac{c_{-}}{b})
. Thus the first term on the right-hand side is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\
m\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}b\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{m}{b}<n\leq m\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\\
\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))\geq c_{+}\end{subarray}}d_{+}(\chi)\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}m\bigl(1+\frac{c_{+}}{b}\bigr)\Bigr)^{-e_{+}(\chi)}

\displaystyle=bd_{c_{+}}\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigl(1+\frac{c_{+}}{b}\bigr)\Bigr)^{-e_{c_{+}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\
m\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}m^{-e_{c_{+}}-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{m}{b}<n\leq m\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\leq bd_{c_{+}}\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigl(1+\frac{c_{+}}{b}\bigr)\Bigr)^{-e_{c_{+}}}\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{b}\Bigr)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\
m\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}m^{-e_{c_{+}}-1}.

Since

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\
m\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}m^{-e_{c_{+}}-1}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq\lfloor C/2\rfloor\end{subarray}}(2k+1)^{-e_{c_{+}}-1}<\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq\lfloor C/2\rfloor\end{subarray}}(2k)^{-e_{c_{+}}-1}<2^{-e_{c_{+}}-1}\frac{1}{e_{c_{+}}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{C}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right)^{-e_{c_{+}}}

by comparison to an integral, the first term on the right-hand side of equation (
3.10
) is bounded above by
B_{2}^{+}(a,\varepsilon,C)
. A parallel argument shows that the second term on that right-hand side is bounded above by
B_{2}^{-}(a,\varepsilon,C)
.

Finally, in the last product in equation (
3.10
), we have
|m+\chi(a)n|\geq|\mathop{\rm Re}(m+\chi(a)n)|\geq m-n\mathop{\rm Re}\chi(a)\geq m(1-\frac{c}{b})
, and thus

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\
m\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}|n|\leq\frac{m}{b}\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}}\frac{1}{m}

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\\
|\mathop{\rm Re}(\chi(a))|\leq c\end{subarray}}d(\chi)\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}m\left(1-\frac{c}{b}\right)\right)^{-e(\chi)}

\displaystyle=d_{c}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(1-\frac{c}{b}\right)\right)^{-e_{c}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\
m\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}m^{-e_{c}-1}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}|n|\leq\frac{m}{b}\\
n\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}}1

\displaystyle=d_{c}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(1-\frac{c}{b}\right)\right)^{-e_{c}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>C\\

and thus the last term on the right-hand side of equation (

3.3
Truncating the product over the zeros

Our approximation

still involves the infinite products

defined in equation (

A bound for the error involved in this last approximation step is fairly clean to state, but only once we introduce several more pieces of helpful notation. We always have in mind some fixed reduced residue class

that these quantities depend upon; we will suppress

Definition 3.5
.

For any Dirichlet charater
\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}
, write

F(z,\chi)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\

Furthermore, when

by the symmetries of zeros of Dirichlet
L
-functions .

Our next piece of notation is motivated by the idea of replacing the tail of an infinite product with its quadratic (and indeed cubic) Maclaurin approximation.

Definition 3.6
.

For any Dirichlet charater
\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}
and any

, define

\displaystyle b_{1}(T,\chi)=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\gamma\geq T}\\

L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{1/4+\gamma^{2}},

noting that if
\chi
is real and

then

.
Further define

\displaystyle F_{T}(z,\chi)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{0<\gamma<T}\\

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{T}(z,\chi)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{-T<\gamma<T}\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}J_{0}(\alpha_{\gamma}z)\biggr)(1+\widetilde{b}_{1}(T,\chi)z^{2}).

We now set

\displaystyle\Delta_{T}(z,\chi)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\frac{1}{1+b_{1}(T,\chi)z^{2}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\gamma\geq T}\\

\displaystyle=\biggl(\frac{1}{1+\widetilde{b}_{1}(T,\chi)z^{2}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}{|\gamma|\geq T}\\

so that

and

Lemma 3.7

For any Dirichlet charater

5
An explanatory model

Assuming GRH and LI, the normalized error term
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a)
has the same limiting logarithmic distribution as the distribution of the random variable

2\mathop{\rm Re}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi}(a)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{Z_{\gamma}}{1/2+i\gamma},

where the
Z_{\gamma}
are independent random variables each uniformly distributed on the unit circle. Individually this random variable has the same distribution as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{2\mathop{\rm Re}Z_{\gamma}}{\sqrt{1/4+\gamma^{2}}}

since
Z_{\gamma}
is invariant under rotations. Since
\mathop{\rm Re}Z_{\gamma}
has variance
\frac{1}{2}
, the variance of this random variable is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}\biggl(\frac{2}{\sqrt{1/4+\gamma^{2}}}\biggr)^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{1/4+\gamma^{2}}.

Accounting for symmetries among the zeros of the
L(s,\chi)
, the double sum is precisely twice the sum of the numbers in Lemma
4.2
, and thus the variance of the limiting logarithmic distribution of
\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a)
is

\approx 2(0.371959+0.304227+0.817511+0.359942+0.253757)\approx 4.21479.

This distribution is certainly not normal (its tails decay more quickly than those of a normal distribution, for example), but a crude model for the distribution is therefore a normal random variable with mean
0
and variance
4.21479
.

For
a\not\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}
, the limiting logarithmic distribution of
\bigl(\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,1),\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,a)\bigr)
is the same as the distribution of either of the random variables

\displaystyle 2\mathop{\rm Re}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\bigl(1,\overline{\chi}(a)\bigr)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{Z_{\gamma}}{1/2+i\gamma}

\displaystyle 2\mathop{\rm Re}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod\penalty\ $q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\bigl(1,\overline{\chi}(a)\bigr)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma>0\\
L(1/2+i\gamma,\chi)=0\end{subarray}}\frac{Z_{\gamma}}{\sqrt{1/4+\gamma^{2}}}

However, in this multidimensional distribution we cannot replace
\overline{\chi}(a)
by
1
, as we did in the one-dimensional distribution, without destroying the correlation between the coordinates of the vector
\bigl(1,\overline{\chi}(a)\bigr)Z_{\gamma}
. Each coordinate of this random vector can be modeled crudely by a normal random variable with mean
0
and variance
4.21479
; however, if we declared that those normal variables were independent, the two coordinates would be completely uncorrelated (for example, the probability that both were positive would simply equal
\frac{1}{4}
).

Let’s turn to the
q=11
case specifically. It turns out that one of the Dirichlet
L
-functions (mod
11
) has a particularly low-lying zero, and we want to explore the extent to which the correlations explored in this paper are mediated by this single zero. More specifically, recall that
\chi_{7}
is the Dirichlet character (mod
11
) characterized by
\chi(2)=(e^{2\pi i/10})^{7}
and thus
\chi(8)=e^{2\pi i/10}
, and let
\rho_{1}\approx\frac{1}{2}+i\cdot 1.23119
be the first zero of
L(s,\chi_{7})
above the real axis. It turns out that all other zeros of Dirichlet characters (mod
11
) above the real axis have height at least
2.47724
. The single zero
\rho_{1}
contributes
\frac{2}{1/4+\gamma_{1}^{2}}\approx 1.13262
to the variance of each coordinate, which is nearly
27\%
of the total variance
4.21479
, and over
3.6
times as much as any other zero. We crudely model the contributions of all other zeros as independent normal variables of the appropriate variance; our model for
\bigl(\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,1),\mathord{\mathring{E}}^{\psi}(x;q,8^{k})\bigr)
is therefore

2\mathop{\rm Re}\biggl(\bigl(1,\overline{\chi_{7}}(8^{k})\bigr)\frac{Z_{\gamma_{1}}}{\sqrt{1/4+\gamma_{1}^{2}}}\biggr)+N(0,4.21479-1.13262)\\
\approx 1.50507\mathop{\rm Re}\bigl((1,e^{-\pi ik/5})Z_{\gamma_{1}}\bigr)+N(0,3.08218).

(5.1)

Let’s examine the probability that both coordinates of this random variable are positive. Let
F(t)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\mathop{\rm erf}(x/\sqrt{2\cdot 3.08218}))
be the cumulative distribution function of the normal variable
N(0,3.08218)
. For any real number
c
, the probability that
c+N(0,3.08218)
is positive is
1-F(-c)=F(c)
. Therefore the probability that the right-hand side of equation (
5.1
) lies in the first quadrant equals

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F\bigl(1.50507\cos(t)\bigr)F\bigl(1.50507\cos(t-\tfrac{k\pi}{5})\bigr)\,dt.

(5.2)

This integral can be evaluated numerically for
1\leq k\leq 9
and compared to the calculated values in Theorem
1.2
(see the table and plots below). Even discarding the bottom four rows with their values duplicating the top four rows,
the relative
\ell^{2}
error is about
6.4\%
.
We see that the true densities and the differences between them are reasonably well simulated by the probabilities from this model, demonstrating that the single low-lying zero
\rho_{1}
(with its complex conjugate) is the primary cause of the cyclic ordering phenomenon.
