Suicide across occupational groups in Australia, 2001–2022: a retrospective mortality study
Authors: Humaira Maheen, Tania King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe study aims to examine suicide mortality across occupational groups by sex and to assess temporal trends between 2001 and 2022 among the employed working-age population in Australia.MethodsWe analysed data from the National Coronial Information System (NCIS), restricted to individuals aged 15–64 who were employed at the time of death. Suicide risk was estimated for 43 occupational groups for both males and females. Joinpoint regression was used to assess suicide trends and identify statistically significant changes in suicide rates over time.ResultsA total of 20 055 suicides were included, 16 708 males and 3347 females. 10 occupational groups were identified as high-risk for suicide, characterised by significantly elevated incident rate ratio (IRR) with no significant change in suicide rates over time. This included Arts and Media Professionals (IRR 1.31); Health Professionals (IRR 1.43); Construction Trades Workers (IRR 1.27); Skilled Animal, Agricultural and Horticultural Workers (IRR 1.26); Hospitality Workers (IRR 1.39); Protective Service Workers (IRR 1.60); Clerical and Office Support Workers (IRR 1.50); Machine and Stationary Plant Operators (IRR 2.11); Construction and Mining Labourers (IRR 4.79) and Farm, Forestry and Garden Workers (IRR 1.85). Males working at the higher skill level occupations demonstrated a significantly lower risk of suicide when compared with other workers; whereas those working in low-skilled level occupations have a significantly higher risk of suicide. This skill-level gradient was not observed among females. Occupations where elevated suicide risk was observed only among female workers included Health Professionals (IRR 2.13), Automotive and Engineering Trades Workers (IRR 3.33) and Mobile Plant Operators (IRR 2.62).ConclusionsThese results highlight the intersecting impact of sex, occupation and skill levels on suicide risk. Prevention efforts must address structural and contextual factors within high-risk occupations, especially where occupational demands override typical protective factors.Keywords: Mental Health, Occupational Medicine, Sociodemographic Factors

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Suicide risk varies by occupation, with workplace stressors, working conditions, access to lethal means and employment type recognised as key contributors. In Australia, certain occupations, particularly those in male-dominated industries, have been identified as high risk, but evidence has largely focused on individual occupations in isolation. Comprehensive, population-level comparisons of suicide risk across all occupational groups, stratified by sex and tracked over time, are lacking.

Introduction

Suicide is one of the leading causes of death among working-age adults in Australia. The burden is especially high among men and those aged 45–64 years.1 Although employment is known to be protective against suicide,2 rates of suicide vary substantially by occupational group.3 Understanding the patterning and role of occupation in shaping suicide risk is essential for suicide prevention. Research has consistently identified elevated suicide rates among low-skilled workers, particularly in sectors such as construction and agriculture.4–6 This is attributed to a combination of structural factors (eg, hazardous conditions, low wages, insecure contracts) and psychosocial challenges (eg, bullying, stigma, low job autonomy) that workers in these industries face.7 Higher suicide rates have also been observed among some occupational groups with ready access to means of suicide, such as health professionals, police officers and farmers.8,9

In Australia, an examination of suicide among selected occupational groups between 1990 and 2006 identified higher suicide rates among agriculture, construction and transport workers.10 More recently, national-level evidence on occupational suicide has identified other occupational groups, such as construction and mining workers,11,12 health professionals,13,14 emergency and protective service workers15 and welfare support workers,16 at higher risk of suicide relative to other workers. However, such research has typically focused on single or selected occupational groups, and none has comprehensively compared suicide risk across different occupational groups in Australia. Evidence on suicide mortality trends over time across different occupational groups is lacking, and little is known about how occupational suicide risk varies by sex.

To address these gaps, this study examined suicide risk and temporal trends from 2001 to 2022 across 43 occupational groups among employed males and females aged 15–64 years in Australia.

Methods

Data source

The study used data from the National Coronial Information System (NCIS). The NCIS is a national web repository of data, containing detailed information on deaths reported by coroners in Australia and New Zealand.17 This includes demographic details about the deceased, police narrative of the circumstances of death, coronial findings, autopsy and toxicology reports. Deaths are coded according to standardised fields. The database enables the retrieval of key demographic variables, for example, age, sex, employment status, country of birth, marital status, occupational information in free text, mechanism of death and International Classification of Disease (ICD) codes. Restricted access to the NCIS web portal is granted to academics and researchers for research purposes, subject to ethics approval.

Case ascertainment

Cases were included if they met all of the following criteria (see online supplemental file 1):

The death was classified as intentional self-harm. Occupation coding

Occupational information recorded in free-text format was manually coded to the two-digit level of the Australian and New Zealand Standard Classification of Occupations (ANZSCO).18 ANZSCO is a framework developed by the ABS to classify occupations based on skill level and specialisation. The two-digit classification captures sub-major groups sitting within broader one-digit major groups. For example, within Professionals, the two-digit groups include Health Professionals, Education Professionals and Design, Engineering, Science and Transport Professionals.

Population estimates

Population estimates were derived from the ABS Census data for the years 2006, 2011, 2016 and 2021. Estimates were stratified by ANZSCO two-digit occupational groups, year, age and sex and limited to individuals aged 15–64 years who were employed at the time of enumeration. The age categories used for extraction were 15–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54 and 55–64 years. To generate population estimates for non-Census years, data from the closest Census year were used as reference points: 2006 Census data for 2001–2006; 2011 Census data for 2007–2012; 2016 Census data for 2013–2019; and 2021 Census data for 2020–2022. The 2016 reference period was extended to include 2019 because the 2021 Census was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, when employment distributions or occupation information may have been substantially altered by pandemic-related disruptions and therefore may not accurately reflect pre-pandemic population composition.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Age-adjusted suicide rates (ASR) per 100,000 person-years were pooled for 2001–2022 and calculated for males and females, as well as for all workers within each ANZSCO 2-digit category (online supplemental file 2). Age standardisation was performed using the 2001 Australian Standard Population. Pooling across this period provides an average estimate of occupational suicide risk over time; annual estimates are also presented separately.

In accordance with ABS guidelines for direct age standardisation, ASRs with fewer than 20 total events should be interpreted with caution19 and are therefore flagged throughout tables 2 and 3. This was most commonly observed among female workers, reflecting the lower frequency of suicide deaths in this group across many occupational categories.

Incident rate ratio

Negative binomial regression was used to compare suicide risk across occupational groups in relation to a baseline of ‘other workers’. For each model, the reference category comprised all workers not in the occupational group under examination, allowing each occupational group to be compared with the remainder of the employed workforce. Negative binomial regression was preferred over Poisson regression due to overdispersion in the dataset. The models were adjusted for age and year of death and stratified by sex. The model coefficients were converted into rate ratios for interpretation. Stata V.16.0 was used for the analysis.20

Suicide trends

To assess trends in suicide rates by occupational group, we used Joinpoint regression analysis. This method segments a trend line into distinct linear components, with joinpoints marking years where statistically significant change in trend direction or magnitude occurred.21 When no joinpoints are identified, the APC is equivalent to the average annual percentage change (AAPC) for the study period. All trend analyses were conducted using Joinpoint Regression Software developed by the Surveillance Research Program of the US National Cancer Institute.22

Identification of high-risk groups

For this study, we defined high-risk groups for suicide as those with a significantly elevated incident rate ratio (IRR) and either no significant change or an increasing trend in suicide rates over time (ie, non-significant or positive AAPC).

Patient and public involvement

This specific paper did not directly involve patients or the public in the initial design or conduct of the quantitative data analysis, as it was based on secondary analysis of coronial data. However, the broader research programme that informed this study has been developed through extensive consultation with industry, suicide prevention charities and individuals with lived experience of suicide.

Overall, ten occupational groups among males showed a significantly higher suicide risk relative to other workers (see table 3). Elevated risk was particularly evident in lower-skill occupations, such as Machine and Stationary Plant Operators; Construction and Mining Labourers; and Farm, Forestry and Garden Workers. In contrast, this skill-level gradient was not observed among females, although suicide rates among females overall are substantially lower than among males. A total of 15 occupational groups among females exhibited a higher suicide risk than other workers, with no clear distinction based on skill level. That is, elevated suicide risk was present in high, medium, and low-skill occupations. Occupational groups where only females had more than twice the suicide risk than other workers (i.e, the similar pattern was not observed for males in the same occupational group) included Health Professionals (IRR 2.13), Automotive and Engineering Trades Workers (IRR 3.33) and Mobile Plant Operators (IRR 2.62).

A statistically significant decline in suicide rates was observed in six groups: Business, Human Resource and Marketing Professionals; Automotive and Engineering Trades Workers; Other Technicians and Trades Workers; Sales Representatives and Agents; Storepersons and Cleaners and Laundry Workers. Barring a minority of low-skilled occupational groups, declines in suicide were predominantly observed among high or medium-skilled groups, with one exception of Sales Representatives and Agents, which have significantly high suicide risk (IRR 1.80; AAPC −2.46*).

Discussion

This is the first Australian study to include all 43 submajor occupational groups (ANZSCO 2) in analysis to examine the suicide patterning by occupation and how these change over time and by sex. It builds on the existing literature on occupational patterns in suicide mortality in Australia and shows industry-specific patterns in suicide risk across several sectors, including construction, protective services and health professions. Employment in higher-skilled occupations appears to be beneficial for males with these groups exhibiting a significantly lower risk of suicide as opposed to those in low occupational skill levels. This advantage was not observed among females, where elevated suicide risk was observed across different occupations regardless of skill level. Six occupational groups demonstrated a significant decline in suicide rates over the 22-year period across different skill levels.

Occupational suicide risk

The industry context in which workers are employed has been identified as amplifying suicide risk, particularly in sectors such as construction, agriculture and protective services.4,5,23 Workers in these industries are routinely exposed to hazardous environments, irregular working hours and a combination of physical and psychosocial demands.3,9,24 These jobs are often physically strenuous, involving repetitive tasks, awkward postures and heavy manual handling, all of which are associated with musculoskeletal injuries and long-term physical strain, factors known to contribute to poor mental health.25,26

Occupational stressors can arise from the nature of the work itself, its demands and the broader workplace environment.27,28 These stressors can negatively impact mental health and, in turn, increase the likelihood of suicidal thoughts and behaviours. Limited autonomy and poor working conditions can further exacerbate stress and feelings of helplessness.4 In our study, elevated suicide risk among male construction workers, machine and stationary plant operators and labourers may reflect the combined impact of these industry-specific exposures. Notably, our study found that while females generally have lower suicide rates, those employed in some risk occupations exhibit elevated suicide risk. For example, Construction and Mining female workers have an ASR 45.2 per 100 000; IRR 13.0, suggesting that the work environment may override gender-based protective effects.29

In Australia, suicide trends within the general population between 2013 and 2023 remained broadly stable for both males and females.30 Our findings reflected a similar pattern: across most occupations (36 of 43), there were no statistically significant changes in suicide rates over time. Statistically significant declines were limited to six occupations, most of which had relatively low baseline rates. These low baseline rates may reflect protective factors associated with higher-skilled occupations, such as greater job security, higher income and better access to mental health resources. The only exception was Sales Representatives and Agents, who, despite a higher suicide risk than the reference group, showed a notable 3% decline over time. Important to note that this occupational group showed elevated suicide risk among both males and females as well. This changing trajectory warrants further investigation to identify the factors driving the decline and to inform strategies for sustaining it.

Sex and occupational suicide risk

Among males, the risk of suicide is generally lower in high-skill occupations compared with other workers. In this analysis, elevated suicide risk was particularly evident in lower-skill occupations, such as Machine and Stationary Plant Operators, Construction and Mining Labourers and Farm, Forestry and Garden Workers. These findings are consistent with global literature that highlights suicide rates are higher in males working in manual or low-skilled occupations, particularly within the construction industry, where the male suicide rate has been noted as a significant issue.10,31 A recent study reported a decline in suicide rates of male construction workers over time.11 While we found that some high skilled construction related occupations showed a significant decrease in suicide rates over time, this was not observed across all construction-related occupations, including low-skilled construction workers (Machine and Stationary Plant Operators or Construction and Mining Labourers).

Among females, a total of 15 occupational groups exhibited a higher suicide risk compared with other workers. There was no clear pattern based on skill level, with elevated suicide risk observed across all skill levels, a finding consistent with the existing literature.3 The groups for which female suicide rates were more than twice that of other workers have also been identified as high-risk in international literature. For example, creative industries, such as those involving artists, writers and entertainers, have consistently been identified as high-risk occupations for women.6,23 A similar Australian study10 reported a significantly elevated risk among female artists (IRR 2.5; 95% CI 1.33 to 4.67), which is consistent with our findings for Arts and Media Professionals (IRR 1.81, 95% CI 1.32 to 2.49). While Roberts et al reported a 54% increase in suicide rates among artists over time in the UK, our study found no significant change in suicide risk over time for this occupational group.23

Elevated suicide risk among female health professionals has been previously reported in Australia.13,14 While one of these studies examined trends over time14 and found a temporal increase in suicide rates of female medical professionals, our analysis found no significant variation in suicide risk over time. This may be related to the application of different ANZSCO codes in our study (ANZSCO 2) versus the selected ANZSCO 4 codes in Petrie et al’s14 work. Health Professionals within ANZSCO 2 include a broader group, including ‘Health Diagnostic and Promotion Professionals’, ‘Health Therapy Professionals’, ‘Medical Practitioners’ and ‘Midwifery and Nursing Professionals’, which likely explains the variation in findings.

Strengths and limitations

This is one of the first Australian studies to apply a comprehensive list of 43 occupations coded under ANZSCO guidelines to examine 22 years of coronial suicide data at a national level. The only previous Australian study to include comprehensive occupational data was conducted in 2010 and used state-level data.10 Despite these strengths, several limitations should be noted. First, there are differences between coronial practices between jurisdictions, which could lead to differences in how intent is determined and reported, which may lead to an undercount of suicide deaths.32 Second, there may be some misclassification of occupational data, even with a rigorous coding framework designed to map occupational text to ANZSCO codes (as outlined in previous research)11,13,16; thus, occupation misclassification remains a possibility. There is a delay of about 18 months to 2 years for some coronial cases to be closed, which may affect suicide counts in recent years.17 To maximise the proportion of closed cases included in analyses, we restricted the analysis to suicide data up to 2022. A further limitation is the small number of suicide cases in some occupational groups, particularly when stratified by sex. This limits the precision of age-standardised rates in these groups and should be considered when interpreting sex-specific findings.

Conclusions

Our findings build on established evidence of associations between occupational factors and suicide and provide more granular evidence of occupational groups at greater risk of suicide than others. Our results also highlight that a combination of sex, skill level and occupational context shapes suicide risk. This highlights complexities for suicide prevention interventions, but indicates the importance of prevention approaches going beyond individual-level approaches and instead targeting some of the systemic issues that impact multiple groups, such as insecure employment, poor working conditions and limited access to mental health support.
