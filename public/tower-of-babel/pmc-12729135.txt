Fecal carriage, risk factors, and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of Salmonella from lactating cows and contact humans at the dairy farm level in Mekelle and Southeast Zones of Tigrai, Northern Ethiopia
Authors: Atsebaha Gebrekidan Kahsay, Tsehaye Asmelash, Enquebaher Kassaye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundZoonotic pathogens are pathogens that can be transmitted from animals to humans, which seriously endangers public health when the interaction between animals and human populations is not properly managed. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) affects all age groups in all regions, especially in low- and middle-income countries, and impacts human and animal health, food production, and the environment. This study aimed to determine fecal carriage, risk factors, and antimicrobial susceptibility of Salmonella from lactating cows and contact humans at dairy farm level in Mekelle and Southeastern Zones of Tigrai, Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from January to June 2025. After we collected the risk factors using questionnaires, 199 cow feces and 199 human stools were collected from 199 dairy farms. Pre-enrichment, selective enrichment, selective media, and a series of biochemical tests were used to identify and isolate Salmonella. An antimicrobial susceptibility tests were performed using the disk diffusion method. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 23 was used to analyze the data and the p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered as statistically significantly associated with Salmonella. The chi-square test was used to determine the strength of risk factors.ResultsSalmonella was isolated from 1% (2/199) of contact human stools and 2% (4/199) of lactating cow feces. Salmonella isolates showed statistically significant associations with cleaning of cow feces using bare hands (X²: 4.44; p-value: 0.035), handling of cow feces (X²: 4.119; p-value: 0.042), and lack of regular cleaning of dairy farm barns (X²: 5.227; p-value: 0.022). All Salmonella isolates showed resistance to ampicillin. Both of the isolates from contact humans were susceptible to cefotaxime, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, meropenem, and ciprofloxacin. Three isolates from the lactating cow and two isolates from the contact humans showed multidrug resistance.ConclusionThe prevalence of Salmonella among contact humans and lactating cows was low. Handling of cow feces with bare hands and lack of regular cleaning of barns were statistically associated with Salmonella, which may have a significant impact on the transmission of Salmonella in the dairy farms. Ampicillin was inefficient to all Salmonella isolates. The multidrug-resistant Salmonella reported was 75% in cows to 100% in humans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12092-x.Keywords: Fecal carriage, Antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, Salmonella, Lactating cows, Contact humans, Dairy farms, Tigrai, Ethiopia

Background

Livestock has a growing need for food as the world’s population continues to rise [1]. For instance, we expect the 1.2 billion people currently residing in Africa to double in number by 2050 [2, 3]. Global human-animal fecal biomass is estimated to reach 4.6 trillion kg per year by 2030 [4]. Africa is predicted to see the highest average yearly change in fecal biomass through 2030, with an estimated difference of 14 billion kg/year. The Food and Agricultural Organization (FAO) states that domestic animals generate 85% of the animal feces produced worldwide, which is more than what humans create [5].

When the interaction between animal and human populations is not properly managed, there might be the transmission of zoonosis in the community, which may endanger the public health [6]. Zoonotic diseases are not only threatening consumers’ health and safety but also threaten animal welfare [7]. Zoonoses account for more than 60% of emerging infectious diseases globally and represent a major interface challenge between human, animal, and environmental health [8]. Around two-thirds of infectious diseases affecting human health are transmitted from animals [9, 10]. Over a billion cases and one million fatalities are attributed to zoonotic infections annually, and their emergence and being pandemic cost the global economy [11]. Humans can contract zoonotic diseases like salmonellosis from the feces of sick or reservoir animals, like cattle and goats [12, 13], that are present in homes and farms. This is because contaminated hands, food, and water supplies are essential sources of infections for human beings [14]. Human gastrointestinal tract illnesses, diarrhea, stunted growth, impaired cognitive development, and mortality have all been related to exposure to animal manure, according to reports [4, 15, 16]. One of the four major infections that are linked to one million annual deaths from badly handled animal feces is Salmonella [14]. Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS), such as Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella Enteritidis [17], are the most common causes of invasive sickness in sub-Saharan Africa, with a 15% fatality rate [18].

Intestinal carriage of Salmonella is described as the excretion of the bacteria in stool without any indication of infection. Those that do this are referred to as “Salmonella carriers.” It has been reported that Salmonella serovars exhibit this condition. Currently, human intestinal Salmonella carriage is reported from countries in Sub-Saharan Africa, including Guinea-Bissau (2.4%) and Senegal (1%) [19], Burkina Faso (1%) [20], Kenya (3.5%) [21], and Ethiopia (13.6%) [22] and (8%) [23]. Similarly, fecal carriages of Salmonella among lactating cows were documented from different regions of the globe, including the United States of America (2.6%) [24] and (7.3%) [25], Canada (6.6%) [26], Italy (9.6%) [27], Burkina Faso (53%) [28], Kenya (2.6%) [29], and Ethiopia (2.3%-11.5%) [22, 23, 30–33].

Traditionally, researchers investigating the effectiveness of Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) interventions mainly focused on human-related factors. In contrast, the veterinary public health community has emphasized animal-related factors and routes of disease transmission [34]. This gag calls for a One Health approach, an integrated strategy that offers significant advantage in reducing the transmission of zoonosis infections, including Salmonella, which can occur through multiple pathways. One of the transmissions of Salmonella from animal to human can be effectively controlled by reducing its prevalence in animals and protecting humans from the infection [35].

Ethiopia is a country in the Horn of Africa with a dense human and livestock population [36]. There are five million estimated cattle in Ethiopia’s Tigrai regional state [37]. If a suitable solution, such as the One Health approach, can’t be implemented to address these current issues and future outbreak concerns, it could result in the spread of zoonotic illnesses [38]. The people in Ethiopia, especially those in the study region, may not have full awareness about the transmission of zoonoses like Salmonella [39].

One of today’s urgent global health and socioeconomic crises is antimicrobial resistance. According to projections, drug-resistant bacterial infections caused 1.27 million global deaths in 2019 [40]. It affects all age groups in all regions, especially in low- and middle-income countries, and its impact is on human and animal health, food production, and the environment. It is affecting the attainment of multiple Sustainable Development Goals. By 2030, there will be 1 to 3.4 trillion US$ in annual losses to gross domestic product, as estimated by the World Bank [41]. It is currently becoming acknowledged that certain strains of Salmonella are extremely virulent and resistant to medicines, especially the primary antibiotics used in human and veterinary treatment, and that they increase human morbidity and mortality [42]. One of the main global public health issues at this time is resistance to Salmonella in both human and veterinary medicine [43]. In animal farms, they can spread from animal to human and develop resistance to antibiotics, which are crucial for clinical practice. The health of both humans and animals is at risk due to the antibiotic-resistant strains that are spreading in animal farms [44]. The overuse of antibiotics in both human and veterinary medicine may be the cause of drug resistance [45]. A total of 33% of the Salmonella isolates from the cow fecal samples displayed resistance to streptomycin, but only 7% of the isolates were resistant to cotrimoxazole and chloramphenicol [22]. In 2018, Davidson and colleagues also reported that ceftriaxone resistance was present in 40.1% of the Salmonella isolates from California dairy farms, with 50.8% MDRs [24]. Salmonella species showed resistance to tetracycline (16.3%) and streptomycin (86.7%), with 70% of the isolates showing resistance to three or more antimicrobials [30]. Furthermore, Salmonella that produce multidrug resistance (MDR) are finding their way into both human and animal therapy. For instance, 84 (86%) of the Salmonella examined in one study conducted in Brazil were demonstrated to be MDR [46].

However, little attention has been given to the study of Salmonella in the feces of lactating cows and the stool of contact humans, as well as the sanitation factors of the dairy farms of the Tigrai regional state of Ethiopia. Hence, this study aimed to determine the fecal carriage, risk factors, and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of Salmonella among lactating cows and contact humans at the dairy farm level in Mekelle and the Southeastern Zones of Tigrai, Northern Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study area, design and period

Tigrai region of Ethiopia has latitudes ranging from 120 14’50.50” to 140 53’48.03” and longitudes from 360 26’48.74” to 390 59’0.09”. The South East Zone of Tigrai is one of the seven administrative zones in the Tigrai region of Ethiopia. It is bordered on the south by the Southern Zone, on the Southeast by the Amhara Region, on the northeast by the Central Zone, on the north by the East Zone, and on the East by the Afar Regional State and surrounds the Mekelle. The Federal Republic of Ethiopia Central Statistical Agency (CSA) 2019/20 estimated that there are over 4.9 million cattle in the Tigrai region. Over 523 thousand cattle are available in the Southeast Zone of Tigrai. Annually, about 230,000 cattle acquire diseases in Tigrai [37]. A cross-sectional study design was used to conduct the study from January 1 to June 30, 2025 in Mekelle and Southeast Zones of Tigrai, Ethiopia as indicated in Fig. 1.

Sample size determination

A simple random sampling method was employed to recruit feces of lactating cows and stool of contact humans from the dairy farms of Mekelle city and towns of the Southeast Zone of Tigrai, Ethiopia. The sample size for lactating cows and contact humans was determined using a population proportion formula. The sample size was calculated based on a single-proportion population formula for each study subject based on the previous prevalence of fecal carriage of lactating cows (7.6%) [30] and stool of contact humans (13.63%) [22] at a 95% confidence interval with a 5% degree of precision.

We calculated separate sample sizes for lactating cows (n = 119) and contact humans (n = 199) because we were unable to get pooled prevalence from previous studies conducted in dairy farms. Hence, we took 199 to represent the dairy farms with two samples per dairy farm (one feces sample from lactating cows and one stool sample from contact human).

Proportional allocation

Total population (1161) of area; selected three sub-cities and two towns. Population of Areas: Ayder (204), Semen (297), Hawelti (385), Hagereselam (215) and Adigudem (66).

Therefore, the result of the proportional allocations for each study area were 35 (Ayder), 51 (Semen), 66 (Hawelti), 36 (Hagereselam) and 11 (Adigudem).

Sampling technique

A stratified random sampling technique with proportional allocation was used, where the sample size for each site in Southeast and Mekelle zones were determined based on their share of the total target population. Random assignment within each site was done using lottery method.

Survey data collection

A questionnaire-based survey may extract important information from the dairy farms of Mekelle and the Southeast Zones of the Tigrai Region, Northern Ethiopia. The questionnaire was translated to the local language, “Tigrigna,” and we performed pretests on 20 participants to avoid unclear questions and to increase the validity and reliability of the study, whereas 5 participants were included for the post-pretest to measure the improvement changes. We granted an Ethical Clearance letter from the Institutional Review Board of the Mekelle University College of Health Sciences, Tigrai, Ethiopia (MU-IRB 2439/2024). The survey was administered using the Kobo toolbox, and face-to-face interviews were performed with all of the contact persons who engaged in all the activities of the dairy farms. Some of the content of the questionnaire is about how to clean the feces of lactating cows? Do you handle the feces of lactating cows with your hands? Is there a latrine in the dairy farm? Do you clean the barns of lactating cows regularly? Service year of contact with humans on the farm, dairy farm establishment, farm size, type of cow feces collected, and how often are animals screened for infections? Do you isolate sick or potentially infected animals from the rest of the herd/flock? Aside from providing a questionnaire, a direct examination of the general cleanliness and sanitary practices of the dairy farms was conducted and recorded. The survey was administered at the time of lactating cow feces and contact human stool sample collection for Salmonella identification in the Mekelle University College of Health Sciences Microbiology laboratory.

Human stool and cow feces collection

After collected the questionnaire based data from the dairy farms owners, about 30 g of lactating cow feces had taken from the different locations of the newly dropped feces into screw-capped bottle having 250 ml volume. Similarly, we consulted the contact humans in the dairy farm to bring about 2 g of stool into screw-capped bottles having 10 ml volume. All samples were labeled with sampling date, type of sample and the name of the farms with a given codes. Collected samples were transported in an ice box at 4 degrees Celsius within 4 h of collection into the Microbiology laboratory at College of Health Sciences, Mekelle University, Tigrai regional state of Ethiopia. Sample collection, isolation and identification procedures were performed based on the World Health Organization Global Foodborne Infections Network protocol [47] and ISO 6579-1 [48].

Bacteriological examination of Salmonella

Isolation and identification of Salmonella from feces of lactating cows were carried out using buffered peptone water (HIMEDIA, India) as pre-enrichment, Rappaport Vassiliadis broth (HIMEDIA, India) and Selenite Cysteine broth (HIMEDIA, India) as selective enrichment, and Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (HIMEDIA, India) and Brilliant green (HIMEDIA, India) agar as selective isolation media. Similarly, isolation and identification of Salmonella from stool samples of contact humans were carried out using Cary-Blair media (HIMEDIA, India) as transport media, Selenite Cysteine broth (HIMEDIA, India) as enrichment and Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (HIMEDIA, India) as selective isolation and MacConkey (HIMEDIA, India) agar as differential media, followed by identification using a series of biochemical tests.

Non-selective pre-enrichment

The fecal samples were pre-enriched in BPW (HIMEDIA, India) in the ratio of one to nine (i.e. 25 g of the sample was inoculated into 225 mL of BPW) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Similarly, 2 g of contact humans stool was collected and transported using Carry-Blair Transport media (HIMEDIA, India) into Mekelle University, College of Health Sciences Microbiology Laboratory.

Selective enrichment

Rappaport-Vassiliadis medium (RV, HIMEDIA, India) and Selenite Cysteine broth (HIMEDIA, India) were used for selective enrichment. From the pre-enriched sample, 0.1 mL was transferred into a tube containing 10 mL of RV broth and incubated at 43 °C for 24 h. Another 1 mL was transferred into a tube containing 10 mL of Selenite Cysteine broth and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Gently agitated stool sample in Carry-Blair transport media was inoculated into MacConkey (HIMEDIA, India) agar using a standard wire loop with a volume of 1 µl. At the same time, the stool sample was inoculated into a tube containing Selenite Cysteine broth (HIMEDIA, India) [49].

Isolation and identification

Xylose lysine desoxycholate (HIMEDIA, India) agar, brilliant green (HIMEDIA, India) agar and MacConkey (HIMEDIA, India) agar plates were used for plating out and colony morphology identification. A loop of inoculum from each RV (HIMEDIA, India) and Selenite Cysteine broth (HIMEDIA, India) culture was plated onto XLD (HIMEDIA, India), BGA (HIMEDIA, India) and MacConkey (HIMEDIA, India) agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the plates were examined for typical and suspect Salmonella colonies. Typical colonies of Salmonella grown on XLD (HIMEDIA, India) agar have black centres and a lightly transparent zone of reddish color, while the color of Salmonella on BGA (HIMEDIA, India) agar was red to pink-white opaque colonies and surrounded by a red halo in the medium. Typical colonies of Salmonellagrown on MacConkey (HIMEDIA, India) agar have colorless and transparent colonies.

Typical and suspected colonies of Salmonella were picked and streaked onto the surface of nutrient agar (HIMEDIA, India) plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h according to ISO 6579-1 [48]. All suspected non-lactose fermenting Salmonella colonies were picked from the nutrient agar and inoculated into triple sugar iron (TSI) agar, Simmons citrate agar, urea agar with 40% urea solution, lysine iron agar (LIA), Sulfide Indole Motility (SIM) media (MIMEDIA, India) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. A colony was considered Salmonella if an alkaline slant (Red) with an acid butt (yellow) on TSI with hydrogen sulfide production, positive for lysine (purple slant with purple butt), negative for urea hydrolysis (red), and negative for tryptophan utilization-indole test (yellow-brown ring). Salmonella isolated from the feces of lactating cows and the stools of contact humans in the dairy farms were subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility tests.

Antimicrobial susceptibility patterns

Salmonella isolated in the current study were subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility tests. The Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method was used to test the effectiveness or resistance profiles of different antimicrobial discs according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [50]. Briefly, pure colonies of Salmonella were picked using a wire loop from the growth in nutrient agar and transferred to a tube containing five milliliters of sterile normal saline and then emulsified. The emulsified culture was kept in an incubator at 37 °C until it reached the McFarland turbidity criterion of 0.5. A sterile applicator cotton swab was used to inoculate the Salmonella suspension across the Muller Hinton agar plate surface while enclosed in a safety cabinet. The plates were left at room temperature for 15 min to allow for drying. After carefully placing antibiotic discs with defined concentrations of antimicrobials on the plates, the plates were incubated at 36 °C for 18 h. The HIMEDIA from India was the source of the following antibiotic discs: Twelve antimicrobial discs from nine classes or categories were used for the antimicrobial susceptibility patterns. These are Penicillin category: ampicillin (AMP, 10 µg); Penicillin + β-lactam category: amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (20/10 µg); Amphenicoles category: chloramphenicol (C, 30 µg); Fluoroquinolones category: ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg); Folate Pathway Inhibitors category: Cotrimoxazole (Cot, 25 µg); Cephalosporins category: ceftriaxone (CTR, 30 µg), cefotaxime (CTX, 30 µg) and ceftazidime (CTZ, 30 µg); Tetracycline category: tetracycline (TT, 30 µg); Carbapenem category: meropenem (MER, 10 µg); Aminoglycosides category: streptomycin (S, 10 µg) and Gentamicin (GEN, 10ug). Once the bacteria were incubated at 36 °C for 16–18 h, the diameters of the clear zones that the antibiotic formed were measured using a caliper to the closest millimeter. Based on the published clinical laboratory standard institute guidelines, the zones of inhibition were reported as either susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) [50]. Multidrug resistance (MDR) is defined as resistance of a bacterial isolate to at least three different classes of antimicrobial agent, according to the criteria described by Magiorakos et al. in 2012 [51].

Quality management and data analysis

Before the investigation, every tool used to process the samples was inspected. Quality control strains of E. coli (ATCC 25922) as negative control and Salmonella Typhimurium (14028) as positive control were used. Data were coded, entered and maintained in SPSS version 23. Chi-square test was used to cross-tabulate the prevalence of Salmonella with associated risk factors. Significance was considered at a 95% confidence interval with a p-Value ≤ 0.05.

Ethical clearance

An ethical clearance letter was collected from the Institutional Review Board of the College of Health Sciences at Mekelle University, and its Institutional Review Board (IRB) number is MU-IRB 2439/2024. A support letter was collected from Mekelle University College of Veterinary Sciences. The study was carried out in accordance with relevant national, international and scientific guidelines’ along with our study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. After briefing the objectives of the study and before collecting the data, informed consent were collected from participants and guardians of the participants who were unable to read and write during the study time. Furthermore, the study participants tested culture positive for Salmonella were consulted. The data and samples were kept confidential and used for the specified objectives only.

Results

A total of 199 dairy farms were included in the study from the Mekelle and the Southeast zones of Tigrai, regional sate of Ethiopia. The dairy farms included from Ayder, Semen, Hawelti in Mekelle city and Hagereselam and Adigudem from the Southeast zones of Tigrai were 35, 51, 66, 36 and 11, respectively. The response rates in all of the study areas were 100%.

Sociodemographic characteristics and prevalence of Salmonella among contact humans

From a total of 199 dairy farms, we collected 199 stools of contact humans, achieving a 100% response rate. The ages of 47.9% (96/199) of the contact humans working in the dairy farms were between 35 and 54, with a mean age of 44.91 and a standard deviation of 13.90. Only 47.7% (95/199) of the participants had completed elementary school and 71.9% (143/199) of the contact humans were men. Out of the study participants, 81.4% (162/199) were married. The overall prevalence of Salmonella among contact humans was 1% (2/199) as indicated in Table 1.

Factors associated with Salmonella among contact humans

Of the 199 contact humans in dairy farms, 19 (9.5%) do not wash their hands after defecation. Seventeen (8.5%) of the people who participated in the study can handle the feces of lactating cows with their hands, and they cleaned the barn with their bare hands. Latrines were absent from around three-fourths (74.4%) of dairy farms. Fifteen (7.5%) people on the dairy farms don’t undertake routine cleaning. Of the people who had contact on the dairy farms, 148 (74.4%) had service years that ranged from one to ten years.

Salmonella isolates were shown to be statistically significantly associated with not washing hands after defecation (X²: 3.828; p-value: 0.05), cleaning feces with bare hands (X²: 4.44; p-value: 0.035), handling cow feces (X²: 4.119; p-value: 0.042), and not routinely cleaning the dairy farm barns (X²: 5.227; p-value: 0.022) as shown in Table 3.

Antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of Salmonella among lactating cows and contact humans

All Salmonella isolates (100%) from the feces of lactating cows and the stool of contact humans were sensitive to ceftazidime and meropenem and resistant to ampicillin. Three (75%) of the Salmonella isolates from the feces of lactating cows were resistant to streptomycin, chloramphenicol, and tetracycline. Discussion

In places where people and animals live together, such as dairy farms, improperly managed animal feces may expose people to zoonotic illnesses [52], especially in low-income countries like Ethiopia. One of the most common bacterial pathogens involved in zoonotic illness is Salmonella. In our study, the overall prevalence of Salmonella among lactating cow feces was 2% (4/199), which is in line with studies conducted in Ethiopia (2.3%) [30], Kenya (2.6%) [29], and northern California, USA (2.3%) [24]. However, our finding was lower than other studies reported from different areas of Ethiopia (4.8% to 11.5%) [22, 23, 31–33], Burkina Faso (53%) [28], Italy (9.6%) [27], Canada (6.6%) [26] and the USA (7.3%) [25].

Similarly, the prevalence of Salmonella in the stool of contact humans in the dairy farms of our study was 1% (2/199). It was in line with studies conducted in Ethiopia (1.94%) [53], Guinea-Bissau and Senegal (1%-2.4%) [19], and Burkina Faso (1%) [20]. But this finding was lower than other studies from Ethiopia (13.6%) [22], (8%) [23] and Kenya (3.5%) [21]. Variation in prevalence might be due to differences in geographical variations, hygiene practices, and microbiological method limitations (sample size, data collection, isolation, and identification methods, such as differences in culture media and protocols).

In the present study, we assessed the interaction of contact humans with lactating cows in relation to Salmonella contamination. Cleaning of dairy farms using bare hands (X2: 4.44; p-value: 0.035), handling of cow feces (X2: 4.119; p-value: 0.042), lack of regular barn cleaning practice (X2: 5.227; p-value: 0.022), and farm size (X2: 8.853; p-value: 0.032) were significantly associated with the prevalence of Salmonella (Supplementary document). The association between the risk factors and the Salmonella positivity may be due to the lack of awareness of hygiene conditions among the contact humans in the dairy farms. Cohabitation with animals showed poorer rates of satisfactory hygiene practices than those without animals, as reported from Vientiane, Lao People’s Democratic Republic [54]. Shortage of information among contact humans in the dairy farms may hinder the implementation of actual actions of disease prevention strategies [54]. Animals, including lactating cows, may carry Salmonella, which can pose a major public health problem in the Africa region [28]. Therefore, Penakalapati et al. (2017) stated a recommendation that humans should limit their contact with animal feces [52]. Salmonellosis outbreaks linked to animal contact results in more hospitalizations compared to food outbreaks. The impact is more significant among children under five years [55].

The misuse of commonly used antibiotics in both human and veterinary medicine has led to the development of resistance in bacterial infections. As a consequence, the resistant strains pose a high risk to the health of humans and animals [44]. All Salmonella isolates identified from lactating cow feces were resistant to ampicillin, which is in line with studies from Ethiopia [22, 31, 33] but higher than another study from Ethiopia [53]. Although we did not assess sources of antibiotics for the treatment of lactating cows, another study in Northwestern Ethiopia indicated that contact humans bought most of the antibiotics from the private pharmacies without prescriptions. Tetracycline (76.9%) and ampicillin (72.5%) were the most frequently used antibiotics for the treatment of their animals [56]. Such practical activities may be the leading factors for the emergence of antibiotic resistance in Northwest Ethiopia, which is probably expected in our study area. Humans in contact with the dairy farms may not have full awareness that the use of antibiotics is considered as one of the contributing factors for the growing of antibiotic resistance [56].

Three (75%) of the Salmonella isolates from the feces of lactating cows were resistant to streptomycin, which is similar to a study from Ethiopia [22] but higher than another study from Ethiopia [33]. Three Salmonella isolates identified from the lactating cow feces showed MDR to four, five, and six different antibiotic classes, which is in line with another study from Ethiopia [53] but lower than a study conducted in the Southwestern United States of America [57]. Both Salmonella isolates recovered from the stool of contact humans were resistant to ampicillin and tetracycline, which is higher than a study from Ethiopia [58]. Salmonella isolates identified from the stool of contact humans exhibited MDR to four antibiotic classes. It seems to be an indication of the growing of antibiotic-resistant Salmonella in the study area, which is also supported by a report from Kenya [29]. Overuse of antibiotics in human medicine and agriculture, inadequate regulatory compliance, and environmental pollution from agricultural runoff and treated sewage all contribute to the growth and spread of AMR in Salmonella [59]. MDR Salmonella is an escalating public-health threat because it limits effective therapeutic options, increases morbidity and mortality, and raises the cost and complexity of disease control. Cattle serve as important reservoirs for several Salmonella serovars that can infect humans. Transmission occurs through food, direct contact, and the environment, and antimicrobial resistance in animal populations directly impacts human clinical outcomes through spillover and gene exchange [60, 61]. Hence, introducing the newly emerged intersecteral approach “One Health” [62] is required to improve the hygiene practices and to overcome the transmission of Salmonella and its drug resistance strains in the dairy farms of the study area.

This study assessed the prevalence of Salmonella, AMR, and risk factors from the feces of lactating cows and the stools of contact humans in the dairy farms of the Tigrai regional state of Ethiopia, which is considered strength of the study because there was no data for the last 13 years in the region. However, this study was unable to conduct serotyping and molecular characterization of the invA and drug resistance genes, which was considered a limitation of the study. It was due to budget constraints, and this limits us from inferring the transmission of Salmonella between cows and humans.

Conclusion and recommendation

The prevalence of Salmonella isolates identified from the feces of lactating cows and the stool of contact humans was low. Those who cleaned the dairy farms using bare hands, handled cow feces, did not have regular barn cleaning practices, and had a large farm size were significantly associated with Salmonella isolates. Being unaware of the aforementioned hygiene practices among the contact humans seems to make them vulnerable to Salmonella infection even if the number of isolates in the current study is small and might not be enough to conclude in confidence. All Salmonella isolates identified from the feces of lactating cows and stool of contact humans showed resistance to ampicillin. Although the number of isolates in the current study was small, the resistant patterns of Salmonella to the commonly available antibiotics and the formation of MDR in the study area seem to be alarming.

The outcome of this study recommends that humans in contact have to improve their hygiene practices to avoid the emergence of zoonotic pathogens like salmonellosis and the occurrence of future outbreaks. Moreover, this study recommends that veterinarians follow the prudent use of antimicrobials by contacting humans in the study area. This study also forwarded its recommendation to local health authorities to perform continuous surveillance and awareness-creating programs in the region. Overall, effective control requires strict hygiene practice in dairy farms, prudent antibiotic use by farm workers, and implementation of integrated One Health surveillance linking animals, humans, and environmental health.
