Area-level socioeconomic status inequalities shape patterns of antimicrobial resistance in China, 2014–2023: a Bayesian spatiotemporal modelling analysis
Authors: Yi Yang, Jiani Jiang, Can Chen, Rongrong Qu, Yi Liu, Chen Xu, Xiangkun Zeng, Xiaofan Li, Yi Li, Weili Lin, Lili Huang, Mi Zhou, Mengsha Chen, Rong Zhang, Shigui Yang, Ning Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents an escalating global health challenge, with socioeconomic status (SES) being a significant influencing factor. This study aimed to quantify area-level SES and assess its association with drug-resistant bacteria in China.MethodsAMR data were collected from China Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance System (CARSS, 2014–2023). Area-level SES across China was captured by County-level Area Deprivation Index (CADI) and Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI). Spatiotemporal distributions of drug-resistant bacteria were explored by spatial autocorrelation and spatiotemporal scan analyses. Key AMR risk factors were identified by GeoDetector analysis. Six Bayesian models were established through the Bayesian spatiotemporal modelling analysis. The Bayesian Spatiotemporal Interaction Hierarchy Model (BSTIHM), demonstrating superior estimative accuracy, was selected to forecast nationwide AMR patterns.FindingsAMR profiles were obtained for totally 34,442,268 isolates of thirteen types of drug-resistant bacteria. The distributions of these bacteria exhibited manifest spatiotemporal heterogeneity nationwide. Both CADI and TDI consistently revealed a distinct socioeconomic gradient, with low area-level SES in western regions, medium in central regions, and high in eastern regions. Spatiotemporal clusters of drug-resistant bacteria were mainly observed in low SES regions. Lower area-level SES (odds ratio (OR) range: 1.054–1.254) and higher antimicrobial usage intensity (OR range: 1.022–1.174) contributed to higher risk of all thirteen types of drug-resistant bacteria. Meanwhile, total wastewater discharge (OR range: 1.064–1.280), PM2.5 (OR range: 1.031–1.135), and number of healthcare technicians per 10,000 people (OR range: 1.035–1.310) were correlated with risks of most drug-resistant bacteria. Estimated risks for all thirteen types of drug-resistant bacteria were increased in low and middle area-level SES regions based on the BSTIHM.InterpretationArea-level SES is a pivotal driver of AMR risk, which might be caused by antibiotic overuse and environmental pollution. Targeted investments in healthcare and environmental systems in lower area-level SES regions are essential to effectively reduce AMR burden.FundingNational Key R&D Program of China, National Natural Science Foundation of China, Natural Science Foundation of Zhejiang Province, and Natural Science Foundation of Jiangsu Province.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed and medRxiv for articles published from the database's inception through May 31, 2025, using the following key words: (“socioeconomic status” OR “SES” OR “area-level SES” OR “individual-level SES”) and (“antimicrobial resistance” OR “bacterial resistance” OR “antibiotic resistance”). Research examining socioeconomic status (SES) in relation to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) remains limited. Notably, a recent study published in Nature Medicine highlighted SES as a critical social determinant influencing the global burden of AMR. Nonetheless, these earlier studies primarily relied on single and limited indicators to assess socioeconomic conditions, lacking comprehensive and validated quantitative metrics for accurately characterizing the intricate relationship between area-level SES inequalities and AMR risk. Furthermore, large-scale, region-specific analyses explicitly addressing area-level SES inequalities and AMR risk remain notably limited in China. This critical knowledge gap underscores an urgent public health imperative to conduct rigorous investigations tailored to area-level SES inequalities in China, facilitating targeted and equitable interventions against AMR.

Added value of this study

This is the first study to quantitatively characterize area-level SES inequalities in China by utilizing two validated metrics, the County-level Area Deprivation Index (CADI) and the Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), revealing marked area-level SES gradients characterized by low in western regions, medium in central regions, and high in eastern regions. Leveraging comprehensive national surveillance data including 34,442,268 bacterial isolates from CARSS from 2014 to 2023, we systematically identified pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity in the distributions of thirteen major types of drug-resistant bacteria (CtxCroR-Eco, QnR-Eco, CR-Eco, CR-Aba, CR-Kpn, CtxCroR-Kpn, CR-Pae, MRSA, MRCNS, ERSP, PRSP, VR-Efa, and VR-Efm) across China. Our results demonstrated a robust relationship between lower area-level SES and higher AMR risk, revealing substantially elevated AMR burdens in socioeconomically disadvantaged regions. Additionally, our study extended beyond socioeconomic dimensions, uncovering significant associations between AMR risk and both environmental factors (e.g., wastewater discharge, PM2.5) and healthcare resources (e.g., intensity of antimicrobial consumption, healthcare technician density per 10,000 population). Moreover, low and medium area-level SES regions showed higher predicted AMR risk based on the advanced Bayesian Spatiotemporal Interaction Hierarchy Model (BSTIHM). These findings discovered the multifactorial drivers of AMR, highlighting the imperative of integrating area-level SES inequalities into future comprehensive national strategies aimed at mitigating AMR in China, especially in low SES regions.

Implications of all the available evidence

The distributions of thirteen major types of drug-resistant bacteria showed obvious spatiotemporal heterogeneity across China. Our findings highlighted that low areal-level SES is a key risk factor of AMR. These findings called for a decisive shift in AMR mitigation strategies, moving beyond conventional biomedical interventions towards integrated policy frameworks that systematically address underlying area-level SES inequities. Future public health strategies should integrate comprehensive area-level SES inequities metrics (CADI and TDI) into AMR surveillance and resource allocation frameworks, facilitating targeted interventions in socioeconomically disadvantaged regions to effectively reduce AMR burdens.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as an urgent global public health crisis.1 In 2021, AMR directly caused approximately 1.14 million deaths worldwide, with a further 4.71 million indirectly associated deaths; without effective intervention, these numbers could rise sharply to 1.91 million attributable and 8.22 million related deaths every year by 2050.2 The drivers behind this escalating crisis are multifaceted, involving widespread antibiotic overuse, insufficient infection prevention and control measures, and persistent gaps in health system capacity.3 China warrants particular attention in the fight against AMR due to its uniquely high antibiotic consumption, being the world's largest producer and consumer of antibiotics.4 As early as 2013, China's total antibiotic consumption had already reached approximately 162,000 tons, with 48% designated for human use and 52% for livestock, accounting for nearly half of global antibiotic usage.5 Such intensive antibiotic use provides an optimal environment for the emergence and dissemination of AMR. For instance, the prevalence of Escherichia coli resistant to third-generation cephalosporins in China was approximately 55% in 2021, markedly exceeding the average level of about 17% reported across the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) member countries.6

The emergence and persistence of AMR are driven not only by microbial evolution and clinical practices but are also profoundly influenced by socioeconomic conditions at the area-level.7 Low area-level socioeconomic status (SES) contributes substantially to structural barriers such as inadequate sanitation, limited access to clean water, under-resourced healthcare infrastructure, and insufficient pharmaceutical regulation, all of which amplify vulnerabilities to AMR.8 In China, the burden of AMR is further compounded by regional variations in diagnostic capacity, shortages of healthcare personnel, and gaps in public health literacy and stewardship.9 Importantly, the relationship between SES and antibiotic consumption in China is complex. Although antibiotic products are highly accessible even in lower-SES areas, prescribing practices, diagnostic use, and patterns of self-medication vary substantially across socioeconomic contexts. This heterogeneity underscores the need to investigate SES as a structural determinant shaping AMR risk, rather than assuming a simple linear association. Therefore, addressing AMR effectively necessitates transitioning from a purely biomedical approach towards an integrated public health strategy that explicitly incorporates socioeconomic determinants.

Evidence from South Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and the United States indicated that SES is closely associated with AMR risk.10,11 However, existing research had largely relied on single SES indicators, such as the Human Development Index, access to medical services, or health expenditure, rather than more integrated measures. In China, comprehensive indicators that capture the complex interplay between SES and AMR remain scarce. To address this crucial gap, we first utilized two robust metrics, both the County-level Area Deprivation Index (CADI) and Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), to quantify area-level SES across China.12,13 Additionally, we incorporated comprehensive assessments of healthcare resources and environmental factors, enabling a nuanced analysis of their roles in AMR risks. Furthermore, AMR risks across China were estimated according to the advanced Bayesian spatiotemporal modelling analysis, offering crucial foresight for public health planning. By meticulously exploring how resource inequities and environmental stressors shape AMR in economically disadvantaged regions, our findings not only illuminate underlying socioeconomic drivers but also inform targeted interventions. This study provides compelling evidence to guide policy decisions and enhance surveillance systems, ultimately reducing the AMR burden, particularly benefiting resource-limited low SES regions.

Methods

Study design

AMR data were obtained from the China Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance System (CARSS), in which all secondary and tertiary hospitals are mandated to participate and to report all detected isolates under standardized protocols, ensuring comparability and minimizing potential bias due to sampling variation. This nationwide mandate aims to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship, enhance infection control, and ensure systematic monitoring of resistance trends. Participating hospitals are required to report all detected isolates, and although the number of isolates varies by province with hospital coverage, the system is structured to reduce selection bias and provide data that are broadly representative of provincial resistance patterns. The dataset encompassed AMR profiles of thirteen types of drug-resistant bacteria, with a total number of 34,442,268 isolates obtained from over 2.89 million clinical specimens collected nationwide from 2014 to 2023 (appendix 1 pp 3, appendix 2 pp 2–8).14 These isolates tested for AMR included 505,945 carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CR-Kpn), 1,588,186 cefotaxime- and chloramphenicol-resistant K. pneumoniae (CtxCroR-Kpn), 1,321,272 carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CR-Aba), 116,010 carbapenem-resistant E. coli (CR-Eco), 3,737,521 cefotaxime- and chloramphenicol-resistant E. coli (CtxCroR-Eco), 3,655,357 quinolone-resistant E. coli (QnR-Eco), 573,602 carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CR-Pae), 863,365 erythromycin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae (ERSP), 18,544 penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae (PRSP), 991,678 methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), 776,738 methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (MRCNS), 18,066 vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (VR-Efm), and 3366 vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecalis (VR-Efa).

Spatial heterogeneity analysis was utilized to examine the distributions of the thirteen types of drug-resistant bacteria. The applicability and effectiveness of CADI and TDI were compared and validated by bivariate spatial autocorrelation analysis in capturing area-level SES inequalities in China (eastern regions: Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Liaoning, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Shandong, Guangdong, and Hainan; central regions: Shanxi, Jilin, Heilongjiang, Anhui, Jiangxi, Henan, Hubei, and Hunan; and western regions: Chongqing, Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Tibet, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia, Xinjiang, Guangxi, and Inner Mongolia) (appendix 1 pp 3–4, appendix 2 pp 9–12).15 This regional classification was used solely as a descriptive framework to contextualize area-level socioeconomic patterns and to assess the consistency of CADI and TDI with established gradients of regional development. CADI and TDI were constructed using indicators obtained from the China Statistical Yearbook and census data (National Bureau of Statistics of China). The CADI and TDI indices were divided into tertiles to derive area-level SES: provinces in the lowest tertile were classified as high SES, those in the middle tertile as medium SES, and those in the highest tertile as low SES. Consistency between SES classifications derived from CADI and TDI was examined using bivariate spatial autocorrelation analysis. Key risk factors shaping AMR distributions were identified by GeoDetector analysis. Nationwide AMR risks were estimated through Bayesian spatiotemporal modelling analysis (Fig. 1). The vector map of China used in this study was obtained from the National Geospatial Information Public Service Platform (https://www.tianditu.gov.cn/) (Review Number: GS (2024) 0650).Fig. 1The flowchart of this study.

Spatiotemporal analysis

Global spatial autocorrelation was assessed using Moran's I statistic to quantify nationwide heterogeneity in drug-resistant bacteria. Local Moran's I was then applied to detect province-level clusters and outliers, categorized as high–high clusters, low–low clusters, high-low outliers, or low-high outliers. Statistical significance was evaluated with a Z test under the null hypothesis of spatial randomness (H0: I = 0). To further characterise spatial patterns, we performed hot-spot and cold-spot analyses using the Getis-Ord Gi∗ statistic, which detects statistically significant clustering of high (hot-spot) or low (cold-spot) incidence of AMR. Furthermore, the first primary spatial–temporal clustering areas (two or more clustering areas with the highest relative risk (RR) value within the same period) of drug-resistant bacteria were detected by spatiotemporal scan statistic (appendix 1 pp 5–7).

Covariates

Given the multifactorial nature of the relationship between area-level SES and AMR risk, we incorporated a comprehensive set of contextual variables to enhance the analysis.7 These factors were as follows: (i) intensity of antimicrobial consumption: defined daily doses (DDDs) (CARSS); (ii) seventeen meteorological indicators: number of hazy days, number of floating and sinking days, number of blowing sand days, number of gale days, number of fog days, number of rainy days, average air pressure, pressure maximum, pressure minimum, maximum wind speed, average 2-min wind speed, average temperature, average maximum temperature, average minimum temperature, mean relative humidity, minimum relative humidity, and precipitation (National Meteorological Science Data Center); (iii) seven air quality indicators: air quality index, PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, CO, and O3 (China Research Data Services); (iv) five healthcare resource indicators: number of healthcare institutions, number of beds in healthcare institutions, number of primary healthcare institutions, number of beds in primary healthcare institutions, and number of healthcare technicians per 10,000 people (China Statistical Yearbook); (v) thirteen wastewater pollution indicators: total wastewater discharge, chemical oxygen demand discharge, ammonia nitrogen discharge, total nitrogen discharge, total phosphorus discharge, petroleum discharge, volatile phenol discharge, total lead discharge, total mercury discharge, total cadmium discharge, total chromium discharge, total arsenic discharge, and hexavalent chromium discharge (China Statistical Yearbook) (appendix 2 pp 13–44). All socioeconomic, environmental, and healthcare resource data were harmonized at the provincial level to ensure comparability across datasets. When raw data were available at a finer resolution (e.g., county level), variables were aggregated to provinces using population-weighted averages. Conversely, when only national- or provincial-level statistics were available, they were directly matched to provincial units.

Variable selection

Key drivers of AMR risk were identified via GeoDetector analysis.16 Variables from six major domains (area-level SES, intensity of antimicrobial consumption, meteorological, air quality, healthcare resources, and wastewater pollution) were standardized using Z-scores prior to analysis. The association between each variable and AMR risk was quantified using Q-statistic. Variables identified through GeoDetector, Spearman correlation, and collinearity analyses were utilized to establish the Bayesian spatiotemporal model (appendix 1 pp 7).

Bayesian spatiotemporal modelling analysis

Bayesian inference framework was employed to construct estimative AMR risk models nationwide. The modelling process comprised several key steps: (i) model specification and diagnostic evaluation; (ii) data loading and compilation; (iii) parameter initialization; (iv) simulated annealing; (v) Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling; (vi) extraction of posterior results.17 Furthermore, six candidate model structures were implemented to estimate future AMR risks nationwide, including a purely spatial model, a purely temporal model, a spatiotemporal model, and three spatiotemporal interaction models. To enhance robustness, annealing procedures were applied to assess model stability and convergence. An initial burn-in of 10,000 iterations was implemented, with these samples discarded to eliminate transient effects. Posterior estimation was then based on a further 10,000 iterations, applying a thinning interval of five to reduce autocorrelation and maintain Monte Carlo error within acceptable limits under Gibbs sampling. Convergence and stability were assessed visually through diagnostic trace plots (appendix 1 pp 35). Estimated provincial-level risks of drug-resistant bacteria were subsequently stratified into high, medium, and low categories using Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) clustering. The performance of these different models was evaluated using the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) (appendix 1 pp 8–10).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (Version 4.4.3). Continuous variables were analyzed with t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests and reported as mean ± SD or median (IQR); categorical variables were assessed using chi-square or Fisher's exact tests and presented as counts and percentages. Missing data, primarily in meteorological (number of gale days and fog days) and air quality variables (CO and O3), were addressed via multiple imputation using "mice" R package (Version 3.18.0). Fifty imputed datasets (m = 50) were generated with 100 iterations per chain. Convergence was assessed via the potential scale reduction factor (R-hat), with all parameters <1.1. Reliability was evaluated by comparing observed and imputed distributions, reporting the fraction of missing information (FMI) and relative efficiency (RE). To assess robustness to violations of the missing-at-random assumption, δ-adjusted sensitivity analyses (±0.2 SD) were performed. Consistency in CADI and TDI indicators was validated using GeoDa (Version 1.22.0.14). Collinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with VIF<10 indicating acceptable levels. Spatiotemporal heterogeneity of drug-resistant bacteria was uncovered in ArcGIS (Version 10.8), SaTScan (Version 10.3.1). GeoDetector analysis using "geodetector" R package. Bayesian spatiotemporal models were established by "R2WinBUGS" R package and WINBUGS (Version 1.4.3). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Distributions of drug-resistant bacteria in China exhibited significant spatial heterogeneity from 2014 to 2023 (p < 0.05) (appendix 1 pp 12–18, appendix 2 pp 45–49). Among the Gram-negative bacteria, CR-Kpn, CtxCroR-Kpn, and CR-Eco showed high–high clusters predominantly in low and medium area-level SES regions, which indicated significant localized transmission, whereas low–low clusters appeared in high area-level SES regions. CtxCroR-Eco and QnR-Eco showed nationwide high–high clustering, yet high area-level SES regions featured diverse clustering patterns (high-low outliers, low-high outliers, and low–low clusters), which uncovered complex localized heterogeneity. CR-Aba and CR-Pae demonstrated nationwide high–high clustering with mixed patterns in low and high area-level SES regions, highlighting both widespread and regionally nuanced AMR dynamics. Among Gram-positive bacteria, MRSA exhibited high–high clusters concentrated in medium area-level SES regions, with high-low outliers and low-high outliers in low area-level SES regions. MRCNS and ERSP presented consistent nationwide high–high clusters. PRSP and VR-Efm displayed notably heterogeneous clustering patterns nationwide, suggesting widespread AMR with complex regional interplay. VR-Efa primarily exhibited diverse clustering in high area-level SES regions, with high-low clusters prominent in low and medium area-level SES regions, reflecting regional differences (appendix 1 pp 18–26).

Among Gram-negative pathogens, CR-Kpn exhibited notable hot-spot aggregation primarily in medium area-level SES regions (e.g., Anhui, Hubei, Henan, and Shanxi). For CtxCroR-Kpn, CR-Eco, and QnR-Eco, hot-spots concentrated in low and medium area-level SES regions, while cold-spots were mainly observed in high area-level SES regions. Hot-spots of CtxCroR-Eco were mainly found in low area-level SES regions, whereas cold-spots were predominantly located in high area-level SES regions (e.g., Guangdong and Hainan each year). Hot-spots of CR-Aba primarily concentrated in medium area-level SES regions (e.g., Anhui, Henan, and Shanxi each year), whereas cold-spots were observed mainly in high area-level SES regions (e.g., Hainan every year). CR-Pae exhibited similar hot-spot aggregation in medium area-level SES regions, notably in Henan and Shanxi each year, while cold-spots concentrated predominantly in high area-level SES regions (e.g., Hainan). Among Gram-positive pathogens, hot-spots of MRSA, MRCNS, PRSP, VR-Efm, and VR-Efa concentrated in low and medium area-level SES regions, while cold-spots primarily observed in high area-level SES regions. Hot-spots of ERSP clustered in low and medium area-level SES regions (e.g., Shaanxi and Henan each year), while cold-spots appeared in high area-level SES regions (e.g., Fujian, Guangdong, Hainan, and Zhejiang each year) (appendix 1 pp 26–32).

Discussion

In this nationwide Bayesian spatiotemporal modelling study, we first utilized two comprehensive socioeconomic indicators, CADI and TDI, to systematically evaluate and quantify area-level SES inequalities across China (low in western regions, medium in central regions, and high in eastern regions). We also identified substantial spatiotemporal clustering of thirteen major types of drug-resistant bacteria across China from 2014 to 2023. Our analysis demonstrated that low area-level SES was significantly associated with AMR risk, with low and medium area-level SES regions displayed higher AMR risk. Additionally, antibiotic overuse, healthcare resources, and environmental pollution were identified as significant AMR risk factors. Our findings highlighted the necessity of integrating area-level SES into strategies aimed at mitigating the spread and public health impact of AMR.

Previous studies have typically relied on single, simplified socioeconomic indicators, neglecting the essential interplay between area-level SES and AMR, thus hindering a thorough exploration of the societal drivers of AMR. To address this critical limitation, our study employed two validated and robust composite indices (CADI and TDI) to rigorously quantify area-level SES across China. We discovered a distinct socioeconomic gradient, high in eastern regions, medium in central regions, and low in western regions. Such geographic socioeconomic inequalities have been consistently discovered in previous studies, underscoring the validity and robustness of CADI and TDI as precise and reliable area-level SES evaluation tools.12,15 This approach significantly enhanced our understanding of the complex socioeconomic mechanisms underpinning AMR.

Previous study has demonstrated distinct global distribution patterns of AMR, with notably higher average prevalence observed in regions such as South Asia, the Middle East and North Africa, and sub-Saharan Africa.10 Meanwhile, our analysis revealed pronounced spatial heterogeneity and distinct spatiotemporal clustering patterns for thirteen types of drug-resistant bacteria across China. Notably, CtxCroR-Eco, QnR-Eco, CR-Kpn, CtxCroR-Kpn, CR-Aba, CR-Pae, MRSA, MRCNS, and ERSP exhibited pronounced spatiotemporal clustering in lower area-level SES regions from 2019 to 2023. While healthcare resources and diagnostic capacity were already incorporated into the subsequent Bayesian spatiotemporal models, the exploratory cluster analysis itself suggested that under-resourced settings may face challenges in timely diagnosis, antimicrobial stewardship, and infection control, thereby providing a plausible context for the observed hot spots.18 Antibiotic overuse in livestock within these major agricultural areas further accelerated the emergence and environmental spread of AMR, leading to clear spatiotemporal clustering.19 Additionally, heightened antibiotic consumption during the COVID-19 pandemic intensified AMR challenges, disproportionately affecting already strained healthcare systems in these resource-constrained regions.20 In 2015, CR-Eco demonstrated significant spatiotemporal clustering nationwide. This clustering likely resulted from increased clinical use of carbapenem antibiotics, which exerted substantial selective pressure, promoting bacterial resistance mechanisms such as carbapenemase production and thus facilitating rapid dissemination and aggregation of resistant strains.21 From 2014 to 2018, pronounced spatiotemporal clusters of VR-Efa and PRSP were concurrently concentrated in lower area-level SES regions, likely driven by prolonged antibiotic misuse and exacerbated by seasonal increases in respiratory tract infections typical of winter.22 In 2023, a significant emergence of VR-Efm clusters in Hunan, Guangdong, Guangxi, and Hainan has occurred, potentially attributable to heightened nosocomial transmission within healthcare settings, increased interprovincial patient mobility, and accelerated bacterial dissemination associated with livestock production and environmental contamination.23 While direct transmission data are unavailable in our surveillance dataset, independent genomic investigations detected by Gouliouris et al. have documented persistent hospital-based transmission and clonal divergence, underscoring the relevance of hospital settings in VR-Efm dissemination.24 In addition to the general socioeconomic gradients observed in AMR, we also noted pathogen-specific clustering patterns in northern China, particularly around Beijing and neighboring provinces, for CR-Kpn, CR-Pae, and VR-Efa. One likely explanation is that Beijing and its surrounding region host a higher concentration of tertiary and teaching hospitals, where broad-spectrum antibiotics such as carbapenems and vancomycin are more frequently prescribed, thus exerting greater selective pressure for resistance.25,26 These findings highlight the importance of considering regional clinical practice patterns, antibiotic stewardship policies, and healthcare utilization structures alongside socioeconomic determinants when interpreting the geographic distribution of AMR.

Area-level SES inequality was detected as a key risk factor of AMR. For lower area-level SES regions, particularly in western China (e.g., Tibet, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia, Xinjiang, and Inner Mongolia), consistently show higher AMR risks, highlighting the role of resource limitations and weaker healthcare systems in facilitating the spread of resistant pathogens.27 This finding discovered the protective role of socioeconomic factors in reducing AMR risk, reinforcing the need for targeted interventions in resource-limited regions, consistent with Maugeri's findings of thirty European countries.28 These results underscore the urgent need for regionally targeted interventions. First, strengthening diagnostic and laboratory capacity in provincial and county-level hospitals in these western provinces is essential to improve the detection and reporting of resistant pathogens. Second, expanding antimicrobial stewardship programs, including stricter prescription regulations, standardized clinical guidelines, and systematic training of healthcare providers, can help reduce inappropriate antibiotic use. Third, investment in environmental and wastewater management should be prioritized, as inadequate infrastructure in these areas may accelerate the transmission of resistant bacteria. Finally, equitable allocation of healthcare financing and human resources is critical to narrow the gap in access to quality medical services and infection control measures between high- and low-SES regions. By addressing both healthcare system limitations and environmental vulnerabilities, these targeted policies can help reduce the observed socioeconomic inequalities in AMR burden and provide a framework for evidence-based, region-specific public health action. We also identified the significant association between antibiotic overuse and higher AMR risk, highlighting an urgent need to prioritize standardized antibiotic stewardship practices to mitigate this pressing public health threat. Our findings further established air pollution like PM2.5 as a significant determinant in the escalation of AMR. An extensive global analysis covering data from 116 countries indicated that each annual 10% increase in PM2.5 concentrations corresponded to a 1.1% rise in AMR.29 These results uncovered the critical influence of air quality on AMR risk and highlight an urgent imperative to prioritize stringent pollution mitigation strategies within public health policy. Meanwhile, our findings uncovered wastewater discharge as a substantial contributor to the dissemination of AMR, highlighting it as a critical environmental risk factor, consistent with recent wastewater-based epidemiological studies from the UK and South Africa.30,31 Additionally, our study identified the availability of healthcare resources and intensity of antimicrobial use as critical determinants significantly associated with AMR risk, findings that are highly consistent with evidence reported by Okeke et al.32 Our study also identified an association between air pressure and AMR risk, potentially attributable to climate change.33

Furthermore, Bayesian spatiotemporal models are widely utilized in epidemiology owing to their superior ability to elucidate and estimate risks of many diseases.34 Our BSTIHM model revealed a notable gradient in estimated AMR risk, with significantly elevated levels identified in lower area-level SES regions. This result aligned with previous studies, reflecting underlying regional differences in healthcare infrastructure, antibiotic stewardship practices, and socioeconomic determinants.35 Sensitivity analyses excluding the data of 2023 demonstrated that the risk of VR-Efm was consistent with the broader pattern observed for other drug-resistant pathogens. This finding indicates that the apparent divergence was largely driven by the abrupt emergence of VR-Efm clusters in Hunan, Guangdong, Guangxi, and Hainan during 2023. While VR-Efm is shaped by underlying socioeconomic gradients, it is also acutely responsive to short-term outbreak dynamics and localized healthcare-associated transmission. These findings highlighted the urgent need for tailored regional AMR interventions, especially strengthening healthcare infrastructure, antimicrobial stewardship, and environmental management in lower area-level SES regions, to effectively mitigate the public health threat posed by AMR.

This study has some advantages. First, two validated indices, CADI and TDI, were employed for the first time to assess area-level SES across China, demonstrating robust applicability and effectiveness. Second, using over a decade of nationally representative data, we provided the first large-scale empirical evidence linking area-level SES to AMR risk, addressing a critical gap in the literature. Third, we introduced a multidimensional analytical framework that integrated area-level SES, antibiotic consumption, climate variables, healthcare capacity, air quality, and wastewater pollution, capturing the complex factors that drive AMR. Fourth, we applied the advanced BSTIHM model to effective forecast AMR risk across China by integrating temporal trends, geographical variation, area-level SES inequalities, healthcare resources, and environmental factors. These advancements offered a robust, evidence-based foundation for targeted policy interventions, particularly in lower area-level SES regions.

In China, nationally representative and systematically collected AMR data are currently available primarily through national surveillance networks, whose standardized public reporting units are at the provincial level. Due to the absence of county-level AMR data with consistent quality and nationwide coverage, we were unable to characterize intra-provincial variations, such as contrasts between coastal and inland areas or between urban and rural settings, potentially obscuring more subtle spatial patterns. Although comparable sub-provincial analyses within China are lacking, evidence from other contexts supports our findings. A study encompassing 574 counties across 39 US states demonstrated that lower SES was significantly associated with higher AMR risk, consistent with the pattern observed in our analysis and indirectly reinforcing the link between SES and AMR risk.11 Given that CARSS reports AMR data only at the provincial level, consistent with China's administrative and policy structure, we aggregated CADI to the same level to ensure analytical alignment. This aggregation was performed using population-weighted means derived from official national and provincial statistical yearbooks, a method that minimizes computational bias while maintaining representativeness.36 Furthermore, the application of a Bayesian spatio-temporal framework helps to mitigate potential aggregation effects by explicitly modelling spatial dependence and uncertainty, which is widely recommended to enhance the stability and interpretability of spatial associations.37, 38, 39 Nevertheless, the absence of sub-provincial AMR data remains a structural limitation of current surveillance capacity. Expanding CARSS to incorporate prefectural or county-level reporting would enable finer-grained analyses, elucidate the gradient between socioeconomic inequality and AMR risk, and ultimately support the development of regionally adaptive antimicrobial stewardship policies.

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, the AMR data were aggregated on a yearly scale, limiting the ability to capture finer temporal dynamics, such as seasonal variations and their interaction with area-level SES. Second, owing to limitations in data availability and reporting within the CARSS surveillance system, our analyses were restricted to the provincial scale, which may have attenuated the observed associations and obscured localized high-risk clusters. Meanwhile, the relationship of area-level SES and AMR risk reported in this study likely reflect broad disparities between more affluent and less developed provinces, but cannot capture the nuanced relationships between local SES variations and AMR risk within provinces due to the database limitation. This inherent aggregation may lead to either over- or under-estimation of effect sizes. Third, the associations between area-level SES and AMR risk were based on nationwide analyses within China. Fourth, while we included a broad range of covariates, factors such as livestock density, rural-urban disparities in antibiotic use, corruption perception index, and local policy enforcement could not be included due to data limitations, this might limit the explanatory power of the BSTIHM model. Fifth, the absence of isolate-level information in CARSS database, which precludes direct assessment of multidrug-resistance (MDR), underscoring the need for future studies using isolate-level datasets to delineate MDR patterns and their socioeconomic determinants. Sixth, although the AMR estimates reflect detection rates, the mandatory and standardized reporting requirements of CARSS across all secondary and tertiary hospitals substantially mitigate the risk of sampling bias. Moreover, the consistent spatial gradients observed across multiple pathogens further reinforce the robustness of our findings. Furthermore, all data utilized in this study were harmonized at the provincial level, which may introduce bias due to the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP) and potentially mask important within-province variations. Future research should expand data sources and adopt interdisciplinary approaches to better understand the complex interplay of socioeconomic, environmental, and policy determinants of AMR risk worldwide.

In summary, this study demonstrated thirteen major types of drug-resistant bacteria in China exhibited pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity, strongly shaped by area-level SES and environmental factors. Compared with high area-level SES regions, those with lower area-level SES face higher AMR burdens, likely driven by increased antibiotic overuse and suboptimal environmental conditions. These insights highlighted the urgent need for equity-oriented, region-specific strategies, particularly in lower area-level SES regions, that address both the social and environmental drivers of AMR, offering a critical framework for informed policymaking and targeted intervention.
