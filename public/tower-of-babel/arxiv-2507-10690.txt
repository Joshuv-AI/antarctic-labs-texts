Natural super-orbitals representation of many-body operators
Maxime Debertolis

Abstract
We introduce the concept of natural super-orbitals for many-body operators, defined as the eigenvectors of the one-body super-density matrix associated with a vectorized operator. We first analyze the general analytical properties of these objects in various contexts, including the time-evolution operator of non-interacting Hamiltonians and Haar-random unitaries. We then perform a numerical investigation of the natural super-orbitals corresponding to both the time-evolution operator and a time-evolved local operator, focusing on two many-body systems: the fermionic t ​ - ​ V t\text{-}V chain and an impurity model, using tensor network simulations. Our results reveal that the t ​ - ​ V t\text{-}V model lacks a preferred super-orbital basis, while in the impurity model, the occupations of the natural orbitals for both operators decay exponentially at all times. This indicates that only a small number of orbitals contribute significantly to quantum correlations, enabling a compact matrix-product-operator representation in the natural orbital basis. Finally, we examine the spatial spread of the natural orbitals for time-evolved local operators in the impurity model and show that the complexity of this operator in the natural orbital basis saturates over time. This new framework opens the door to future research that leverages the compressed structure of operators in their natural super-orbital basis, enabling for instance the computation of out-of-time-order correlators in large interacting systems over extended time scales.



I
Introduction

Strong correlations between particles, arising from their interactions, are at the heart of a wide array of exotic phenomena in condensed matter physics, ranging from
high-temperature superconductivity
Keimer et al. 2015
to quantum spin liquids
Savary and Balents 2016
and many-body
localization
Nandkishore and Huse 2015
;
Abanin et al. 2019
. Understanding the physics of such strongly correlated systems is notoriously difficult, as it
typically requires handling exponentially large Hilbert spaces—especially in non-perturbative regimes where analytic approaches fail. To overcome these challenges, a wide range
of numerical techniques have been developed over the past decades, including exact diagonalization
Lanczos 1952
;
Benner and Faßbender 1997
, quantum Monte
Carlo
Sandvik and Kurkijärvi 1991
;
Foulkes et al. 2001
, and tensor network algorithms
Schollwöck 2011
;
Bañuls 2023
. Among these, tensor network
methods—particularly due to the efficient and practical matrix product states (MPS) and matrix product operators (MPO) representations—have become a method of choice for
tackling one-dimensional systems where entanglement remains sufficiently bounded.

Operators play a central role in all these approaches. They encompass observables used to extract physical quantities, time-evolution operators that govern the dynamics of
states or operators (in either the Schrödinger or Heisenberg picture), and the Hamiltonian itself, which is the cornerstone of simulations such as density matrix renormalization
group (DMRG)
White 1992
;
Östlund and Rommer 1995
;
Schollwöck 2011
. Moreover, operators can encode information about a quantum system that is inaccessible from the states
alone—for instance, through out-of-time-order correlators (OTOCs), which provide insight into the scrambling of quantum information across space and
time
Larkin and Ovchinnikov 1969
;
Maldacena et al. 2016
;
Bohrdt et al. 2017
;
Hémery et al. 2019
;
Xu and Swingle 2020
;
Gisti et al. 2025
.

In tensor network simulations, operators are typically encoded as MPOs. The efficiency of such representations depends critically on the internal correlations of the operator:
higher correlations translate into larger bond dimensions and computational overhead. This is especially true for unitary operators, such as the time-evolution operator or
time-evolved local operators, whose von Neumann entanglement entropy tends to grow rapidly—following either a volume or area law—thus making their simulation particularly
challenging
Calabrese and Cardy 2005
;
Fagotti and Calabrese 2008
;
Eisert et al. 2010
;
Alba 2025
;
Murciano et al. 2024
;
Gisti et al. 2025
. This
growth is even more severe than in the MPS case, as the operator acts on a doubled Hilbert space, effectively squaring the system size and amplifying entanglement barriers.

A crucial but often under-explored aspect of MPS and MPO simulations is the choice of the one-particle basis. The complexity of these tensor network representations is not
intrinsic to the physical system alone, but also depends on how the degrees of freedom are organized. Specifically, it is desirable to identify an orbital basis in which the
correlations—and hence the entanglement—between subsystems are minimized. Recent advances have demonstrated that, for specific problems such as quantum impurity models, the
entanglement growth during a quench can be overcome by implementing a suitable time-dependent basis rotation
Núñez-Fernández et al. 2025
. In these systems, consisting
of a strongly interacting impurity coupled to a large non-interacting fermionic bath, long-range correlations emerge naturally. Yet, it has been shown that expressing states in
terms of natural orbitals—eigenvectors of the one-body density matrix—enables significant compression, due to the exponential decay of their occupation in both ground
states
Zgid and Chan 2011
;
He and Lu 2014
;
Lu et al. 2014
;
Bravyi and Gosset 2017
;
Boutin and Bauer 2021
;
Debertolis et al. 2021
;
Debertolis et al. 2022

and certain out-of-equilibrium settings
Núñez-Fernández et al. 2025
. Natural orbitals have also proven useful in characterizing many-body localized systems, where
different basis choices reveal distinct one-particle
properties
Bera et al. 2015
;
Bera et al. 2017
;
Buijsman et al. 2018
;
Hopjan and Heidrich-Meisner 2020
.

Building on this idea, we explore whether similar reductions in complexity can be achieved directly at the level of operators by introducing the concept of
natural super-orbitals
associated to
operators. We define these as the eigenvectors obtained from a vectorized form of an operator and its corresponding
one-body super-density matrix
, a natural generalization of the concept used
for quantum states. Inspired by the structure of many-body states in particular physical contexts, we investigate the properties of unitary operators in spin chains and quantum impurity problems, expecting that
the behavior of their natural super-orbitals will vary significantly between these two settings. Our main result shows that, in quantum impurity models, the complexity of MPO
representations can often be exponentially reduced by working in the natural super-orbital basis of the operator. This suggests that the compression seen in specific states (e.g., ground
states) actually stems from fundamental properties of operator themselves—properties that extend across the full many-body Hilbert space.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
II
, we review the properties of the one-body density matrix for quantum states, focusing on the features we aim to
generalize to operators. In Section
III
, we introduce the notion of natural super-orbitals for operators and define orbital rotations in terms of super-orbitals in a
doubled Hilbert space. We also analyze the properties of such rotations when the operator is unitary, and illustrate the formalism with a simple case of a factorized operator.
In Section
IV
, we consider two limiting cases: non-interacting systems, where the natural orbital basis trivializes the MPO structure, and Haar-random
unitaries, where no basis is preferred. The main numerical results are presented in Section
V
, where we investigate the time-evolution operator and time-evolved
local observables in the
t
–
V
model and in a quantum impurity model. We show that in the latter case, the MPO complexity can be significantly reduced using the natural
orbital basis. Finally, in Section
VI
, we summarize our findings and outline possible future directions stemming from the concepts introduced in this work.

II
One-body density matrix and natural orbitals for states

We start this article by reviewing the definition of the usual one-body density matrix defined for an arbitrary state
|\psi\rangle
, and some of its properties that we extend for
operators later on. Given a fermionic system in which the particle can occupy
L
orbitals of any kind (we only consider orbitals with occupation zero or one here), equipped with the
fermionic creation and annihilation operators
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{l}
and
\hat{c}_{l}
respectively creating and removing a fermion in the orbital
l
, a state

|\psi\rangle
can be written in a complete basis of the ermionic Fock space:

|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}a_{i}|i\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{L}}a_{i}\left[\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{l}\right)^{n^{i}_{l}}\right]|0\rangle,

(1)

where
a_{i}
are complex coefficients which for a normalized state are constrained as
\sum_{i}|a_{i}|^{2}=1
and
|i\rangle
are product states forming a complete
basis
\sum_{i}|i\rangle\langle i|=\mathds{1}
.
n^{i}_{l}=\langle i|\hat{n}_{l}|i\rangle
is the occupation of the orbital
l
in the state
|i\rangle
and
|0\rangle
is
the vacuum state. The one-body density matrix, which we label here
Q
, is a
(L\times L)
hermitian matrix (and not an operator like the state density matrix), whose elements
are defined for such a state as follows:

Q_{ij}=\langle\psi|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}c_{j}|\psi\rangle.

(2)

Q
being hermitian, there exist a unitary matrix
V
such that
Q=VDV^{\dagger}
, such that
V
corresponds to a rotation of the one-particle basis of orbitals
\hat{c}
in
the so-called natural orbitals basis
\hat{q}
. The basis rotation can be written as a linear combination of the first set of orbitals with the elements of
V
:

\hat{q}_{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}V^{*}_{i\alpha}\hat{c}_{i},\quad\quad\hat{c}_{i}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{L}V_{i\alpha}\hat{q}_{\alpha}.

(3)

The elements of the diagonal matrix
D
are the eigenvalues of
Q
, which are the occupations of the natural orbitals
\hat{q}
in the state
|\psi\rangle
, namely:

D_{\alpha,\beta}=\langle\psi|\hat{q}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\hat{q}_{\beta}|\psi\rangle\delta_{\alpha,\beta}=n_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha,\beta}.

(4)

The property of the natural orbitals is that it is the basis in which the sum of the occupations
\sum_{a=1}^{r}n_{a}
of the first
r
orbitals sorted in descending order is
maximized. This object is widely used in the quantum chemistry community to organize molecular orbitals according to their degree of
correlations
Löwdin 1955
;
Davidson 1972
;
Roos et al. 1980
. Therefore, if the state
|\psi\rangle
can be represented as a product state in a given (known or
unknown) one-particle basis, it will be revealed by the natural orbital basis, whose occupations will be strictly
n_{\alpha}=1
or
0
, if the orbital
\alpha
is occupied by a
fermion in the state (
n_{\alpha}=1
) or not (
n_{\alpha}=0
). Correlations lead to occupations that are different from zero or one.

III
Natural super-orbitals for operators

The matrix
Q
that we defined above serves as a tool to assess the complexity of a given state by measuring its proximity to a product state. We now extend this line of inquiry
to operators: can we construct an analogous object that captures the complexity of an operator—specifically, one that indicates whether the operator can be expressed as a
product operator, with a suitable definition of what constitutes a product operator? This question is particularly relevant for the numerical simulation of quantum systems in
the Heisenberg picture, as discussed in the introduction. In this framework, evolving operators are often represented as matrix product operators (MPOs), whose complexity
depends on the chosen basis. A notable example is that of free systems, where the time-evolution operator can be expressed as an MPO with bond dimension one in the orbitals
basis in which the Hamiltonian matrix is diagonal, but it becomes unmanageable in any other one-particle basis.

In order to follow the same derivation as before, it is always possible to
vectorize
operators such that their mathematical formulation is similar to the one for
states. The vectorization of an operator
\hat{O}
is usually defined through the following isomorphism:

\begin{split}\hat{O}&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2^{L}}O_{ij}|i\rangle\langle j|,\\
|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle&:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2^{L}}O_{ij}|i\rangle|j\rangle.\end{split}

(5)

Following the form of a state as defined in Eq. (
1
), we can write an arbitrary operator
\hat{O}
using creation and annihilation operators:

\begin{split}\hat{O}&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2^{L}}O_{ij}\left[\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{l}\right)^{n^{i}_{l}}\right]|0\rangle\langle 0|\left[\prod_{l=L}^{1}\left(\hat{c}_{l}\right)^{n^{j}_{l}}\right],\\
|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2^{L}}O_{ij}\left[\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{l}\right)^{n^{i}_{l}}\right]\otimes\left[\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\hat{\tilde{c}}_{l}\right)^{n^{j}_{l}}\right]^{T}\!\!\!|0\rangle\!\rangle,\end{split}

(6)

where the transpose is taken on the operators acting on the dual space labelled with a tilda, since they act from the left to the right in Eq. (
5
). The vacuum state
is the tensor product of the vacua of the direct and dual spaces,
|0\rangle\!\rangle=|0\rangle\otimes|0\rangle
, or equivalently the vacuum of a
super-space
corresponding to a doubled
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
. We can simplify the form of Eq. (
6
), by introducing the following
super-operators
acting on the doubled Hilbert
space, which we label with a double hat:

\hat{\hat{d}}^{\dagger}_{m}=\begin{cases}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\otimes\mathds{1}\hskip 41.25641pt\text{if }\quad m\leq L\\
\mathds{1}\otimes\hat{\tilde{c}}_{m-L}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(7)

where
m\in[1,2L]
and
\hat{\tilde{c}}
means that the matrix representation of the operator is transposed, since it should be applied from its left and not from its right as
usual. Using these super-operators, the vectorized operator from Eq. (
6
) can be written:

|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{4^{L}}O_{k}\left[\prod^{2L}_{m=1}\left(\hat{\hat{d}}_{m}^{\dagger}\right)^{n^{k}_{m}}\right]|0\rangle\!\rangle,

(8)

which is now very similar to the state in Eq. (
1
). The former indices
i
and
j
from Eq. (
6
) can be recovered from the reshape of the
matrix
O_{ij}
of shape
(2^{L}\times 2^{L})
into the vector
O_{k}
of dimension
4^{L}
. A graphical notation for the vectorization of an operator can be the following:

where
h_{i}
(resp.
\tilde{h}_{i}
) refers to the local
direct
(resp.
dual
) Hilbert space at site
i
, associated with the gray (resp. orange) color.

The introduction of the super-operators
\hat{\hat{d}}_{m}
allows us to extend the definition of the one-body density matrix to operators in terms of their vectorized form and the

2L
super-operators of Eq. (
7
). We call
R[\hat{O}]
the
(2L\times 2L)
dimensional hermitian super-density matrix associated to the vectorized form of the operator
\hat{O}
, whose elements read:

\begin{split}R[\hat{O}]_{m,n}&=\langle\!\langle\hat{O}|\hat{\hat{d}}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{\hat{d}}_{n}|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle\\
&=\langle\!\langle\hat{O}|\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{c}_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}&\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\otimes\hat{\tilde{c}}_{n}\\
\hat{c}_{m}\otimes\hat{\tilde{c}}^{\dagger}_{n}&\mathds{1}\otimes\hat{\tilde{c}}_{m}\hat{\tilde{c}}^{\dagger}_{n}\end{pmatrix}|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle,\end{split}

(9)

which naturally takes the form of a matrix with four blocks, the off-diagonal onces mixing the direct and dual spaces of the operator. As for the matrix
Q
,
R
can be
diagonalized by a unitary matrix
W
such that
R=WDW^{\dagger}
, which also represents a rotation of the super-orbitals, forming the basis of
super-natural-orbitals
:

\hat{\hat{q}}_{\alpha}=\sum_{m=1}^{2L}W^{*}_{m\alpha}\hat{\hat{d}}_{m},\quad\quad\hat{\hat{d}}_{m}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{2L}W_{m\alpha}\hat{\hat{q}}_{\alpha}.

(10)

The specificity of the rotation
W
compared to the previous rotation
V
comes from the off-diagonal blocks of
R
, and is not simply a rotation of the system’s orbitals

\hat{c}
but also mixes the dual and direct spaces of the operator. The occupation
n_{\alpha}=\langle\!\langle\hat{O}|\hat{\hat{q}}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\hat{q}}_{\alpha}|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle
of the
super-orbitals in the vectorized operator are also given by the elements of the diagonal matrix
D
, corresponding to the eigenvalues of
R
. In the following, we refer to these
eigenvectors as the correlation spectrum of the operator. Using the graphical notation for tensors, the elements of the matrix
R
can be computed as depicted in the following
sketch:

The one-body super-density matrix of an operator as defined in Eq. (
9
) has been already introduced in
Refs.
Murciano et al. 2024
;
Rath et al. 2023
;
Siva et al. 2022
as a conformal field theory tool
to study bipartite entanglement properties, but no attention has been put to these objects as measures of the complexity of operators. As stated for the case of states, the
natural super-orbitals of the operator can reveal if there exist (or not) a basis in which the operator can be expressed as a product operator, for which Eq. (
8
) has only a single
element. However, due to the mixing of the direct and dual spaces, the operator might not be an MPO anymore but an MPS in the doubled Hilbert space, unless the natural super-orbitals
preserve the tensor product structure between the direct and the dual space. In practice, the matrix
R
can be computed efficiently without having to vectorize the operator, by
noticing the following equivalences:

\begin{split}\langle\!\langle\hat{O}|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{c}_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle&=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{O}^{\dagger}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{c}_{n}\hat{O}\right),\\
\langle\!\langle\hat{O}|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\otimes\hat{\tilde{c}}_{n}|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle&=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{O}^{\dagger}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{O}\hat{c}_{n}\right),\\
\langle\!\langle\hat{O}|\mathds{1}\otimes\hat{\tilde{c}}_{m}\hat{\tilde{c}}^{\dagger}_{n}|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle&=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{O}^{\dagger}\hat{O}\hat{c}_{m}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\right),\\
\end{split}

(11)

such that elements of
R
can be efficiently computed by computing the trace of the product of MPOs, which is a common operation in tensor network simulations. Note that for states, the normalization condition is such that
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1
, which is not the case for operators anymore. Thus, we normalize the elements of

R
by the norm of the operator, which reads
\langle\!\langle\hat{O}|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{O}^{\dagger}\hat{O}]
.

In the following, we present some properties of the matrix
R
for specific types of operators, namely unitary operators and density matrices, and we show a simple example of the
meaning of a product operator in the sense presented above.

Unitary operators

Unitary operators have the property that
\hat{O}^{\dagger}\hat{O}=\mathds{1}
, which leads to some simplifications in the definition of the operator-one-body density matrix
as is clear from Eqs. (
11
). By using the cyclicity of the trace, we can rewrite the following elements as:

\begin{split}\langle\!\langle\hat{O}|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{c}_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle&=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\mathds{1})}{2}\delta_{mn},\\
\langle\!\langle\hat{O}|\mathds{1}\otimes\hat{\tilde{c}}_{m}\hat{\tilde{c}}^{\dagger}_{n}|\hat{O}\rangle\!\rangle&=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\mathds{1})}{2}\delta_{mn},\\
\end{split},

(12)

where trace of the identity disappears with the division by the norm of the operator. Thus, only off-diagonal blocks of the matrix
R
are non-trivial and need to be
computed for unitary operators. The matrix
R
has then the following form:

R=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{L}&A\\
A^{\dagger}&\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{L}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\mathds{1}_{2L}}{2}+\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\
A^{\dagger}&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

(13)

where
A_{mn}=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{O}^{\dagger}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{O}\hat{c}_{n})
is the only-non trivial
(L\times L)
block of
R
. With such a form, the eigenvalues
of
R
can be found through the eigenvalues of
A^{\dagger}A
only. If we denote the eigenvalues of
A^{\dagger}A
as
\mu_{\alpha}
, the eigenvalues of
R
come by pairs as
\lambda_{\alpha}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\pm\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}=\frac{1}{2}\pm|\mu_{\alpha}|
. If
A
is normal (hermitian, unitary, etc. such that
A^{\dagger}A=AA^{\dagger}
), then
\mu_{\alpha}
are the eigenvalues of
A
. The eigenvalues of
R
being comprised between
0
and
1
, the eigenvalues of
A
are thus restricted to the
interval
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
. The eigenvectors of
R
are just two copies of the eigenvectors of
A^{\dagger}A
with a sign depending on the corresponding eigenvalue
one for each off-diagonal block of the matrix:
W_{\alpha,\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[v_{\alpha},\pm v_{\alpha}]
, where
v_{\alpha}
is the eigenvector of
A^{\dagger}A
associated to
the eigenvalue
\mu_{\alpha}
. We recall that the doubled Hilbert space is divided into the operators acting on the direct space first, then on the dual space, as

\{\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1},\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2},\cdots,\hat{\tilde{c}}_{1},\hat{\tilde{c}}_{2},\cdots\}
. With the eigenvalues coming in pairs, the eigenvectors also come in
pairs:

\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{\alpha,\pm}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{v_{i\alpha}^{*}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\pm\hat{\tilde{c}}_{i}\right),

(14)

We notice that the local direct and dual spaces are mixed in a simple way in each natural orbital, namely
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\pm\hat{\tilde{c}}_{i}
. We are allowed to
perform a rotation of the super-orbitals such that the direct and dual spaces are not mixed anymore, one being associated to one natural orbital and one to the other:

\hat{q}^{\dagger}_{\alpha,\mathrm{direct}}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}v_{i\alpha}^{*}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\quad;\quad\hat{q}^{\dagger}_{\alpha,\mathrm{dual}}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}v_{i\alpha}^{*}\hat{\tilde{c}}_{i}\,.

(15)

The previous equation shows that the natural super-orbitals of a unitary operator are just a standard orbital rotation
v\hat{O}v^{\dagger}
. The super-orbitals
\hat{\hat{q}}
are then
simply the orbitals
\hat{q}
associated to the space on which they act (direct or dual), such that the operator, which was vectorized as an MPS in a system with
2L
orbitals,
can again be written as an MPO in the super-orbitals basis. The following sketch illustrates the difference of the two types of rotations, in the super-orbital space for a
generic operator (left panel) and in the standard orbital space for unitary operators (right panel).

Density matrices

We focus now on density matrices, especially for pure states as they are represented by rank one matrices. In this case,
R
is completely equivalent to
Q
for the
corresponding states. The density matrix of a system in a pure state
|\psi\rangle
can be written and vectorized:

\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\rightarrow|\rho\rangle\!\rangle=|\psi\rangle\otimes|\tilde{\psi}\rangle.

(16)

with the normalization
\langle\!\langle\rho|\rho\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\langle\tilde{\psi}|\tilde{\psi}\rangle=1
. Note that for a pure state,
|\tilde{\psi}\rangle=|\psi\rangle
, but it does not matter to keep them different. The corresponding matrix
R
reads, if we write it by blocks involving the original fermion operators as follows:

R=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\psi|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{c}_{n}|\psi\rangle&\langle\psi|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}|\psi\rangle\langle\tilde{\psi}|\hat{\tilde{c}}_{n}|\tilde{\psi}\rangle\\
\langle\psi|\hat{c}_{n}|\psi\rangle\langle\tilde{\psi}|\hat{\tilde{c}}^{\dagger}_{m}|\tilde{\psi}\rangle&1-\langle\tilde{\psi}|\hat{\tilde{c}}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{\tilde{c}}_{n}|\tilde{\psi}\rangle\end{pmatrix}.

(17)

The terms not preserving the particle number like
\langle\psi|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}|\psi\rangle
are simply
0
, such that:

R[\rho]=\begin{pmatrix}Q[\tilde{\psi}]&0\\
0&1-\tilde{Q}[\tilde{\psi}]\end{pmatrix}

(18)

since
\tilde{Q}=Q
for a pure state, the
R
matrix is just twice the copy of the
Q
matrix of the corresponding state when it’s particle-hole symmetric. For a mixed state
holding a well defined charge, the matrices
Q
and
\tilde{Q}
are different but the dual and direct space do not mix as well. We can show the same results by using the
definition of the elements of
R
through the trace of operators as defined in Eq. (
11
) equivalently for density matrices. A pure state holds the
following properties:
\rho^{\dagger}=\rho
and
\rho^{\dagger}\rho=\rho
. In this case, only the diagonal blocks of the
R
matrix are non-zero contrary to unitary
operators and they read:

\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{c}_{n}\right]=\langle\psi|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{c}_{n}|\psi\rangle

(19)

Correlation entropy

Similar to the case of states, we can define a single measure of the complexity of an operator based on the occupations of its natural super-orbitals. This measure is commonly referred to
as the correlation entropy, and is defined analogously to the correlation entropy of states:

S_{\mathrm{corr}}=-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{2L}n_{\alpha}\log\left(n_{\alpha}\right).

(20)

When the operator can be factorized as a product operators, the occupations of the natural super-orbitals are either
0
or
1
, and the correlation entropy will be

S_{\mathrm{corr}}=0
. In the opposite case, the furthest from a product operator an operator is, the more natural super-orbitals have their occupation
n_{\alpha}
close to

\frac{1}{2}
, and the correlation entropy will be maximal when every orbital reaches this value, leading to the extensive maximal correlation entropy
S_{\mathrm{max}}=L\log(2)
. We point out here that the spectrum of
R
does not depend on the basis in which the operator is defined, operators which can be related through a one-body rotation
(in the sense defined above) will have the same natural super-orbitals and thus the same correlation entropy.

A simple example

In order to illustrate the notion of product operator, we provide here a simple example of an operator that can be factorized and whose natural super-orbitals occupations are either

0
or
1
. In general, an operator can be written as a sum of projectors between different product states in the basis it is defined, as we wrote in Eq. (
5
).
Usually, operators are defined in the computational basis
|i\rangle
, in which each index corresponds to a fermion in a site:
|101\rangle=\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}|0\rangle
. We take here the example of the operator
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}
, which we aim to write in a factorized form, for
L=3
:

\begin{split}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}&=|001\rangle\langle 000|-|011\rangle\langle 010|\\
&-|101\rangle\langle 100|+|111\rangle\langle 110|.\end{split}

(21)

We accounted for the fermionic signs, and the effect of the operator on an incoming state is to add a fermion at site
0
when allowed. Its vectorized form looks like:

\begin{split}|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}\rangle\!\rangle&=|001\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\rangle\!\rangle-|011\tilde{0}\tilde{1}\tilde{0}\rangle\!\rangle\\
&-|101\tilde{1}\tilde{0}\tilde{0}\rangle\!\rangle+|111\tilde{1}\tilde{1}\tilde{0}\rangle\!\rangle\\
&=(\mathds{1}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{\tilde{c}}_{1}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{\tilde{c}}_{2}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{\tilde{c}}_{2}\hat{\tilde{c}}_{1})\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}|0\rangle\!\rangle.\end{split}

(22)

The vectorized operator can be normalized by adding a factor
1/2
. In the first line of the above expression, the dual and direct spaces are mixed and the tildes indicate the
states from the dual space. The operator
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}
has a lot of redundancy, and it is possible to find a combination the creation and annihilation operators such that
a more compact form can be written. We assume the following rotation:

\begin{split}\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{1}&=\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0},\quad\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{3}=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}+\hat{\tilde{c}}_{1}\right),\quad\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{5}=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2}+\hat{\tilde{c}}_{2}\right),\\
\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{2}&=\hat{\tilde{c}}_{0},\quad\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{4}=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}-\hat{\tilde{c}}_{1}\right),\quad\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{6}=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2}-\hat{\tilde{c}}_{2}\right).\end{split}

(23)

In this basis, it is easy to reconstruct Eq. (
22
) from the new super-operators
\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{m}
:

|\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}\rangle\!\rangle=\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{1}|0\rangle\!\rangle=|000001\rangle\!\rangle,

(24)

In the new basis, the vectorized operator is now expressed in a single product state in the doubled Hilbert space. The rotation that we applied is unique (up to a phase), as it
is the one diagonalizing the matrix
R[\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}]
. The spectrum of
R[\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}]
in this case is then:

\begin{split}\langle\!\langle\hat{\hat{n}}_{1}\rangle\!\rangle&=1,\langle\!\langle\hat{\hat{n}}_{3}\rangle\!\rangle=0,\langle\!\langle\hat{\hat{n}}_{5}\rangle\!\rangle=0,\\
1-\langle\!\langle\hat{\hat{n}}_{2}\rangle\!\rangle&=1,\;1-\langle\!\langle\hat{\hat{n}}_{4}\rangle\!\rangle=1,\;1-\langle\!\langle\hat{\hat{n}}_{6}\rangle\!\rangle=1\\
&\equiv[0,0,1,1,1,1],\end{split}

(25)

in which we can see that the occupations are
1
away from half-filling, indicating that the operator
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}
creates one particle. Hence, in a similar sense as
the matrix
Q
is working for states, the power of the one-body density matrix
R
is to reveal the basis in which a vectorized operator is written as a product state.

Note that the transformation above can be equivalently understood as by working in a Pauli-like basis
\{\mathds{1},\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger},\hat{c}_{i},\hat{n}_{i}\}^{\otimes L}
(mixing the local direct and dual spaces) for the operator, which reads
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{0}\mathds{1}\mathds{1}
, being clearly a factorized form of the operator.

IV
Special cases

We introduced above the theoretical framework for computing the one-body density matrix of an operator and illustrated its significance through a simple example. In this
section, we apply the framework to two important cases frequently encountered in quantum many-body physics: the time-evolution operator of a non-interacting system and a Haar
random unitary. These examples are particularly insightful because they reflect contrasting notions of complexity. In non-interacting systems, the Hamiltonian—and hence the
time-evolution operator—can be diagonalized within the one-particle basis. In contrast, Haar random unitaries resemble infinite-temperature thermal states, where no
simplification is expected from a one-body perspective. We demonstrate how both cases fit within the one-body density matrix framework: the time-evolution operator can be
decomposed into a product of orbitals, while Haar unitaries exhibit maximal correlation entropy.

IV.1
Non-interacting systems

The Hamiltonian of a non-interacting system can be put in a diagonal form by diagonalizing its Hamiltonian matrix, which constitutes an orbital rotation. In the corresponding
basis, it has the following local form:

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{free}}=\sum_{n=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{n}\hat{n}_{n},

(26)

where
\varepsilon_{n}
and
\hat{n}_{n}
are respectively the onsite energy and the occupation operator on the orbital
n
: each orbital is thus disconnected and all the terms
commute with each other. The time-evolution operator reads:

\hat{U}(t)=\exp\left(-it\sum_{n=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{n}\hat{n}_{n}\right)=\prod^{L}_{n=1}e^{-it\hat{n}_{n}},

(27)

where the last equality uses the commutation relations of each term of
H
. We expand the exponential, and use the fact that
\hat{n}^{m}=\hat{n}
:

\hat{U}(t)=\prod^{L}_{n=1}\left(\hat{\mathds{1}}+\hat{n}_{n}\left(e^{-it\varepsilon_{n}}-1\right)\right)=\prod^{L}_{n=1}\hat{O}_{n}.

(28)

The form above is quite compact, and most importantly is a factorized form. Note that the vectorized form of the operator has to be normalized as previously stated. The vectorized
form of
\hat{O}_{n}
in its local basis reads:

\begin{split}|\hat{O}_{n}\rangle\!\rangle&=|0\rangle\langle 0|+|1\rangle\langle 1|+(e^{-i\varepsilon_{n}t}-1)|1\rangle\langle 1|\\
&=(1+e^{-i\varepsilon_{n}t}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{\tilde{c}}_{n})|0\rangle\!\rangle.\end{split}

(29)

Similarly to Eq. (
23
), each term in the product can be rotated into the following pair of orbitals (since
\hat{U}(t)
is unitary):

\begin{split}\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{n}&=(\sqrt{A}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}+\hat{\tilde{c}}_{n}),\\
\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{L+n}&=(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}-\sqrt{A}\hat{\tilde{c}}_{n}),\\
\end{split}

(30)

where
A=1-e^{-i\varepsilon_{n}t}
, such that
q^{\dagger}_{n}q^{\dagger}_{L+n}|0\rangle\!\rangle
correctly reproduces Eq. (
29
). Note that these orbitals must be correctly
normed. Each factorized operator
\hat{O}_{n}
in Eq. (
28
) can thus be decomposed in two such kind of orbitals, which leads to:

|\hat{U}(t)\rangle\!\rangle=\prod^{L}_{n=1}\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}_{L+n}|0\rangle\!\rangle=|11\cdots 11\rangle\!\rangle.

(31)

The spectrum of
R[\hat{U}(t)]
consists of equal numbers of zeros and ones, as all orbitals are occupied —half of the spectrum corresponds to
\langle\!\langle\hat{\hat{n}}\rangle\!\rangle
, and
the other half to
1-\langle\!\langle\hat{\hat{n}}\rangle\!\rangle
). Consequently, the operator
\hat{U}
becomes trivial in a suitable super-orbital basis. Additionally, one can demonstrate that the
operator
\hat{U}
factorizes by directly evaluating matrix elements of
A
, defined in Eq. (
13
). Substituting Eq. (
28
) into this expression shows
that
A_{mn}=\delta_{mn}\frac{e^{it\varepsilon_{n}}}{2}
. Using the relation between the spectra of
R
and
A
(i.e.
\lambda^{\pm}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\pm|\mu_{\alpha}|
), it follows immediately that
\lambda^{\pm}_{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}
.

The only condition imposed above is that the Hamiltonian must be quadratic in the fermionic operators. According to the Thouless theorem
Thouless 1960
, any unitary
operator representing a one-body rotation can be expressed as the exponential of a quadratic Hamiltonian. Hence, if the spectrum of the corresponding
R
matrix consists only of
zeros and ones, the associated unitary operator can be interpreted as an orbital rotation. However, this factorization does not extend to the Hamiltonian itself: as evident from
Eq. (
26
), a sum of local operators cannot, in general, be written as a single product operator.

IV.2
Haar random operators

The case of random unitary operators is much simpler. It is possible to calculate the matrix elements
A_{mn}
on average using the common machinery of Weingarten
calculus
Collins and Nechita 2010
, as shown in Appendix
A
. We find that for the time-evolution operator, these elements are all always zero, such that the
spectrum of
R
is fully degenerate with
\lambda=1/2
, corresponding to the worst case in which the maximal correlation entropy
S_{\mathrm{max}}=L\log(2)
is reached. In such
a case, there exist no one-particle basis in which a random unitary can be simplified. As for
\hat{U}(t)
the eigenvalues of
A
are simply zero, we also investigate the
behavior of the natural super-orbitals for a local operator which is rotated by a random unitary. In this case, we show that the eigenvalues of the matrix
A
are decaying with the system size as

2^{-L}
which converges exponentially to a maximally correlated operator.

V
Results

We present the numerical study of the correlation spectrum for two specific unitary operators, the time-evolution operator
\hat{U}(t)
and an evolved local operator of the type

(\hat{n}_{i}(t)-1/2)
for two different models. The first model is the
t-V
model, in which every site is interacting with their neighbors, and the second one is the interacting
resonant level model (IRLM) in which the interaction is localized at an edge of a tight-binding chain. Our numerical simulations are based on a
U(1)
-symmetric MPO
representation of the corresponding operators, and employ the time-evolving block decimation (TEBD) algorithm
Vidal 2004
;
Schollwöck 2011
in real space with a maximum bond
dimension
\chi=1024
, as well as a
4^{\mathrm{th}}
-order Trotter decomposition with a time step
dt=10^{-2}
to ensure the convergence of the data up to the times shown in
the plots.

V.1
t\text{-}V
model

The first system investigated here is the
t-V
model of fermions in a one-dimensional open chain. This model is equivalent to the Heisenberg spin chain (or
XXZ
model) through
a Jordan-Wigner transformation
Jordan and Wigner 1928
, and is studied here as a prototypical interacting system, in which interaction and hopping compete at every site. The
Hamiltonian is the following:

\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{t-V}}&=\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{c}_{i+1}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i+1}\hat{c}_{i}\right)\\
&+V\left(\hat{n}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\hat{n}_{i+1}-\frac{1}{2}\right),\end{split}

(32)

state-specific properties, which are typically well understood, do not always exist into this framework. Conventionally, the regimes
V>0
and

are associated with
anti-ferromagnetic and ferromagnetic phases, respectively, in the underlying spin model, each exhibiting distinct physical behavior. However, within our formalism, these two
regimes are entirely equivalent. Specifically, one can perform a basis rotation (as defined above) within the operator space
\{\mathds{1},\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i},\hat{c}_{i},\hat{n}_{i}\}^{\otimes L}
, whereby all number operators
\hat{n}_{i}
on even (or equivalently, odd) sites acquire a minus sign—e.g.,
(\hat{n}_{0},\hat{n}_{1},\hat{n}_{2},\cdots)\rightarrow(-\hat{n}_{0},\hat{n}_{1},-\hat{n}_{2},\cdots)
. This transformation is formally equivalent to changing

. As discussed in Sec.
III

Hamiltonians
\hat{H}(V)
and
\hat{H}(-V)

, are related by such an orbital rotation and therefore share the
same correlation spectrum. A similar argument applies to the sign of the hopping parameter
\gamma
, which can be reversed by applying a rotation to every other creation (or
equivalently annihilation) operator
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}
(or
\hat{c}_{i}
).

The two aforementioned distinct groups of orbitals can also be identified by their spatial dispersion. The first group, composed of the most strongly correlated orbitals,
exhibits a delocalized structure. As the orbital correlation strength decreases (corresponding to increasing

The highly correlated, delocalized orbitals encode the strong, shorter-ranged correlations induced by the local impurity interaction, akin to the formation of a Kondo cloud in
the ground state of quantum impurity models. In contrast, the second group captures the propagation of the local perturbation through the system via nearest-neighbor hopping,
representing the light-cone structure of the time-evolved operator. At long times and for sufficiently large systems, these orbitals become effectively localized on individual

The other regime

V.3
Early growth of the correlation entropy

Finally, we study the short time behaviour of the operators studied above for the two models presented here, the
t-V
(or Heisenberg) model and the IRLM. At short times, the
elements of the matrix
R
of these unitary operators defined in Eq. (
11
) can be approximated using the following form of the Baker-Hausdorff-Campell
(BHC) formula:

e^{\hat{X}}\hat{Y}e^{-\hat{X}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}[\hat{X},\hat{Y}]_{p},

(35)

where
[\hat{X},\hat{Y}]_{p}=[\hat{X},[\hat{X},\hat{Y}]]_{p-1}
and
[\hat{X},\hat{Y}]_{0}=\hat{Y}
.

We first consider the time-evolution operator
\hat{U}(t)=e^{-i\hat{H}t}
and compute the matrix elements of
A
to leading orders in
t
. In this framework, we define

\hat{X}=i\hat{H}t
and
\hat{Y}=\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}
, while
\hat{c}_{n}
remains time-independent and serves as a spatial probe for the spread of
\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}
under
time evolution, in a manner analogous to an OTOC.

. For the off-diagonal elements, we find that

\lambda_{\alpha,+}=1-t^{2}c_{\alpha}
and

. Due to the isotropy
of the Heisenberg model, all eigenvalues exhibit the same scaling behavior, leading to an extensive number of natural super-orbitals becoming more correlated at the same time.

In contrast, for the IRLM, the interaction anisotropy causes the diagonal elements of
A
in the bath to vanish as
t^{|m|}
for

remain dominant at leading order. In this case, the scaling of eigenvalues remains
\lambda_{\alpha,+}=1-t^{2}c_{\alpha}
and
\lambda_{\alpha,-}=t^{2}c_{\alpha}

The case of the local operator
\hat{n}_{i}(t)-1/2

\hat{X}=i\hat{H}t
, but now choose
\hat{Y}=\hat{n}_{i}

For the

(or Heisenberg) model, the site index

is taken at the center of the chain (

This highlights that the short-time scaling of the correlation spectrum of the time-evolution operator may be insensitive to fine details of the Hamiltonian. In contrast,
evolving local operators provide a more refined probe, capturing model-specific features. This sensitivity is reminiscent of the behavior of OTOCs, which also involve the
dynamics of local operators.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced the concept of natural super-orbitals associated with many-body operators, defined as the eigenvectors of the one-body super-density matrix constructed from a
vectorized operator. To illustrate the meaning of these orbitals in the super-Hilbert space of
2L
particles, we analyzed several simple cases. We demonstrated that a suitable
super-orbital rotation can render an operator trivial in the MPO representation for non-interacting systems, while no such simplification is possible for random operators.
Additionally, we showed that for unitary operators, a super-orbital rotation corresponds to a standard basis change that preserves the meaning of the operator in the physical Hilbert
space of
L
particles.

We then applied this framework to characterize the natural super-orbitals of both the time-evolution operator and a time-evolved local operator in two interacting models: the
t-V

model and a quantum impurity model. In the impurity model, the natural super-orbitals reveal a compressed structure in both operators, evident through the exponential decay of their
occupation numbers. This suggests that expressing these operators in the natural orbital basis allows for a significantly more compact MPO representation at all times, enabling
the study of larger systems and longer time scales beyond the reach of current real-space tensor network methods. Using the correlation entropy, we observed that the complexity
of a local operator initially located on the impurity site saturates over time, with the saturation timescale increasing with interaction strength. This behavior suggests that
quantum correlations becomes absent at large scales, even at late times.

Future directions include the development of a few-body detection algorithm, analogous to that introduced for quantum states in Ref.
Núñez-Fernández et al. 2025
, to
efficiently simulate OTOCs in quantum impurity models at long times and for large systems, in order to test the Lieb-Robinson bound in these systems. Such a tool would validate and extend the
present findings, shedding light on the scrambling behavior in these systems. Finally, this work opens up new perspectives for studying orbital correlations in operator space across different
physical contexts—for example, in many-body localized systems, where natural orbitals associated with quantum states have shown intriguing
properties
Bera et al. 2015
;
Bera et al. 2017
;
Buijsman et al. 2018
;
Hopjan and Heidrich-Meisner 2020
. Another promising
avenue is the investigation of natural super-orbitals in mixed-state density matrices represented as MPOs, aiming to understand the impact of interaction with the environment and to provide
a one-body framework for analyzing the loss of correlations due to dissipation.
