Devil is in the Details: Density Guidance for Detail-Aware Generation with Flow Models
Rafał Karczewski, Markus Heinonen, Vikas Garg

Abstract
Diffusion models have emerged as a powerful class of generative models, capable of producing high-quality images by mapping noise to a data distribution. However, recent findings suggest that image likelihood does not align with perceptual quality: high-likelihood samples tend to be smooth, while lower-likelihood ones are more detailed. Controlling sample density is thus crucial for balancing realism and detail. In this paper, we analyze an existing technique, Prior Guidance, which scales the latent code to influence image detail. We introduce score alignment, a condition that explains why this method works and show that it can be tractably checked for any continuous normalizing flow model. We then propose Density Guidance, a principled modification of the generative ODE that enables exact log-density control during sampling. Finally, we extend Density Guidance to stochastic sampling, ensuring precise log-density control while allowing controlled variation in structure or fine details. Our experiments demonstrate that these techniques provide fine-grained control over image detail without compromising sample quality. Code is available at https://github.com/Aalto-QuML/density-guidance .

Introduction

Diffusion models are a family of generative models that learn to map noise to a data distribution
p_{0}
, which allows realistic image sampling
(
Ho et al., 2020
;
Song et al., 2021b
;
Song et al., 2021a
;
Vahdat et al., 2021
)
. In the quest towards high-fidelity sampling it is natural to ask whether perceptual quality of images aligns with their likelihood
p_{0}({\bm{x}})

(
Karczewski et al., 2025
)
?

Remarkably, the density
p_{0}
correlates
negatively
with the amount of detail: within the
typical samples

(
Nalisnick et al., 2019
)

{\bm{x}}\sim p_{0}
higher-density images tend to lack detail and be smooth, while lower-density images tend to be richly textured and detailed
(
Sehwag et al., 2022
)
(See
Fig. 1
). Outside the typical samples, extremely low density leads to broken images
(
Karras et al., 2024a
)
, while extremely high density strips detail to the point of resembling sketch drawings or blurs
(
Karczewski et al., 2025
)
.

. Bottom: EDM2

Somewhat surprisingly the common sampling strategies in flow models do not optimise for sample density
(
Karras et al., 2022
)
. Recently,
Karczewski et al. (2025)
proposed an approach towards controlling the sample density by biasing the sampling towards the extremely high likelihood regions of
p({\bm{x}}_{0}|{\bm{x}}_{t})
, and demonstrated that these correspond to unrealistic images. Their work is limited in three ways. The approach is only derived for SDE models with linear drift. The exact procedure is not tractable so the authors resort to approximations in practice. Finally, it only allows targeting the highest possible likelihoods, which do not produce realistic images.
This highlights the need for a more general approach that allows fine-grained control over sample density while preserving realism.

In this paper, we build upon prior observations that scaling the latent code affects image detail
(
Song et al., 2021b
)
.
We refer to this method as
Prior Guidance
and we provide a theoretical explanation for this phenomenon by introducing
score alignment
, a condition under which Prior Guidance provably increases or decreases log-density.
We show that this condition often holds in practice.

Beyond this analysis, we introduce
Density Guidance
, a novel procedure that allows explicit control over the log-density of generated samples.
Assuming knowledge of the score function, we derive an alternative ODE that guarantees the log-density of the trajectory evolves exactly as specified.
Empirically, we show that this method achieves similar results to Prior Guidance.

Finally, we extend Density Guidance to incorporate stochastic sampling.
This enables precise control over the log-density of generated samples even when randomness is introduced.
By injecting noise at different stages of the generation process, we can selectively influence variations in high-level structure (e.g., shape and composition) or fine-grained details.
Our experiments demonstrate that this stochastic extension allows for enhanced diversity while preserving control over the desired level of detail.

In summary, in this paper we

•

introduce
Score Alignment
, a condition that explains how latent code scaling affects image detail and can be tractably checked for any CNF model -
section 3
;

•

derive a modification of the generative ODE that enables exact log-density control during sampling -
Density Guidance
-
section 4
;

•

extend Density Guidance to stochastic sampling, retaining exact log-density control while allowing controlled variation in structure or details -
section 5
.

\displaystyle\quad\>\>\>+\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\Big(\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big).

2.2
Diffusion models

A notable case of flow models are diffusion models given by a forward process
p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}{\bm{x}}_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{D})

A CNF with drift

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{t}^{\textsc{pf-ode}}({\bm{x}}_{t})=f(t){\bm{x}}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}(t)\underbrace{\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}_{\text{score}}

shares marginals

with

when

are shared
(
Song et al., 2021b
)
. This Probability-Flow ODE (PF-ODE) is an efficient, deterministic, sampler in diffusion models.

2.4
Neural network approximations

In all our experiments, we assume access to pre-trained neural networks to approximate

and
\nabla\log p_{t}

3
Scaling the latent code – when and why?

In this section we evaluate whether the simple latent rescaling approach of
Song et al. (2021b)
is sufficient to control for sample density, and demonstrate its shortcomings.

Song et al. (2021b)
observed that scaling the latent noise
{\bm{x}}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
down with
\beta<1
monotonically decreases the amount of detail in the decoded images
{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\textsc{pf-ode}}(\beta{\bm{x}}_{T})
, while preserving the image semantics (See
Fig. 2
).
Interestingly,
Karczewski et al. (2025)
recently showed that the log-density
\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})
assigned by a diffusion model correlates with the amount of detail in the generated image.
Most diffusion models use a Gaussian noise
p_{T}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}_{D})
, whose score
\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})=-{\bm{x}}_{T}
simply reduces the latent norm towards zero. Thus (down)scaling at
t=T
is equivalent to maximizing
\log p_{T}
.

These two observations suggest a simple hypothesis:

Prior guidance
: To increase (decrease)
\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})
, it suffices to move
{\bm{x}}_{T}
in the positive (negative) direction of
\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})
, and then decode.

We are interested in studying whether (steepest)
\log p_{T}
increase in latent
{\bm{x}}_{T}
leads to a monotonic increase in
\log p_{0}
of the decoding
{\bm{x}}_{0}({\bm{x}}_{T})

Eq. 2
. To formalise this notion, we assume a latent curve
c:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
at
t=T
, whose tangent is given by the score
c^{\prime}(s)=\nabla\log p_{T}(c(s))
. A monotonic curve decoding has

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\log p_{0}\Big({\bm{x}}_{0}\big(c(s)\big)\Big)\geq 0,\qquad\forall s,

(10)

which is equivalent to
Score Alignment
(
Appendix C
):

\mathrm{SA:}\quad\underbrace{\nabla\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})^{T}}_{\text{decoding score}}\underbrace{\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{0}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{T}}\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})}_{\text{push-forward score }{\bm{v}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\geq 0,

(11)

for all
{\bm{x}}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, where
{\bm{v}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{T})\coloneqq\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{T}}\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})
.
In
Appendix

C.2
we show that
Eq. 11
always holds when
{\bm{u}}_{t}
is linear in
{\bm{x}}
, as in trivial diffusion models. We also show that it does not hold in general by providing a counterexample.
See
Fig. 2
for a visualisation.

To evaluate the SA

we need to solve for
{\bm{v}}_{0}
. We use sensitivity equations (i.e. forward differentiation)

d\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{x}}_{t}\\
{\bm{v}}_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\\
\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{\partial{\bm{x}}}{\bm{v}}_{t}\end{bmatrix}dt,

(12)

with

to describe the

evolution, and specifically, to solve
{\bm{v}}_{0}({\bm{v}}_{T})

(

)
.

\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})-\int_{t}^{T}b_{\tau}({\bm{x}}_{\tau})d\tau=\phi_{t}(q),

(19)

which is satisfied for
b_{t}({\bm{x}})\coloneq\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{t}(q)
. The quantile function
\phi_{t}(q)
can be estimated by sampling
K
independent samples
{\bm{x}}_{T}\sim p_{T}
, estimating
\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
with
Eq. 3
and finding empirical quantiles for target values of
q
.

\phi_{t}(0.99)
and

Implicit quantile matching

A considerable drawback of explicit quantile matching is the need to estimate
\phi_{t}
.
This becomes especially problematic for conditional generation, where the distribution of
\log p_{t}
can differ significantly for different classes (
Fig. 8
).
For applications such as text-to-image, this would require estimating the distribution of
\log p_{t}
for every possible text prompt, which is not feasible.

using the EDM2 model
(
Karras et al., 2024b
)
.

The
Eq. 15
gives a recipe for altering the flow based on how we want
\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
to evolve.
However, the challenge is to determine what are the
reasonable
values of
b_{t}
so that
\log p_{t}
does not deviate from what is typical.
We tackle this problem by analyzing the stochastic view of CNFs.
Specifically, for
{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim p_{t}
,
Eq. 7
says that when evaluating
{\bm{x}}_{t-dt}
, we can add random noise and stay within the typical region of
p_{t-dt}
as long as we correct for it by subtracting the score from the drift.
Furthermore,
Eq. 8
says how
\log p_{t}
changes under this stochastic evolution.

Concretely, the
average
change in log-density, when adding noise of strength
\varphi(t)
is given by

\mathbb{E}\big[d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\big]=-\left(\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}(t)h({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)dt,

(20)

where
h({\bm{x}})=\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})+\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Interestingly, in
Appendix F
, we empirically (and theoretically in simplified cases) show that in diffusion models
\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}h({\bm{x}}_{t})}{\sqrt{2D}}
approximately follows
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
when
{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim p_{t}
and dimension
D
is high.
A reasonable choice is then

b_{t}({\bm{x}})=-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}})-\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}(t)\frac{\sqrt{2D}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\Phi^{-1}(q),

(21)

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
q
is the desired quantile.
We found that choosing
\varphi
to match the diffusion strength in
Eq. 5
, i.e.
\varphi\equiv g
works well in practice.
Thus, in our experiments we use

b^{q}_{t}({\bm{x}})=-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}})-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}(t)\frac{\sqrt{2D}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\Phi^{-1}(q).

(22)

After plugging this definition of
b
to
Eq. 16
, we get

{\bm{u}}^{\textsc{dg-ode}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=f(t){\bm{x}}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}(t)\eta_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}),

(23)

which is equivalent to simply rescaling the score by

\eta_{t}({\bm{x}})=1+\frac{\sqrt{2D}\Phi^{-1}(q)}{\|\sigma_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}}.

(24)

We call
Eq. 23

Density-Guided Sampling
(DGS).
Importantly, DGS comes at no extra cost since the score is evaluated at each sampling step anyway.
Note that, as shown in
Fig. 4
, the correlation of
\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
with image detail is the strongest for
t^{*}\approx\log\mathrm{SNR}^{-1}(1)
and thus in our experiments we only use guidance in the
[T,t^{*}]
interval.
In

we show samples generated with DGS with different values of

.
Interestingly, the samples are perceptually very similar to those from Prior Guidance (

). See

for more samples and quantitative results.

5
Stochastic density guidance

In previous sections we discussed two methods for controlling
\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})
during ODE sampling of form
Eq. 1
.
However, it has been reported that adding stochasticity during sampling can improve sample quality
(
Song et al., 2021b
;
Karras et al., 2022
)
.
Neither of the previously discussed methods supports stochastic sampling. Recently
Karczewski et al. (2025)
lifted the first roadblock towards this by showing how to
evaluate

\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})
for an SDE. We now ask: Is it possible to also
control

\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})
during stochastic sampling?

Recall a stochastic CNF sampler with noise strength
\varphi
:

d{\bm{x}}_{t}=\Big({\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}(t)\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big)dt+\varphi(t)d\overline{{\textnormal{W}}}_{t}.

(
7
)

In
Appendix G
, we show that, similarly to density guidance
Eq. 23
, it can be altered to enforce the desired evolution of log-density over time.
Specifically, suppose that
{\bm{u}}_{t}={\bm{u}}_{t}^{\textsc{PF-ODE}}
and that we require
\frac{d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}=b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
for
b
defined in
Eq. 22
.
Then, the stochastic process

d{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{u}}_{t}^{\textsc{dg-sde}}({\bm{x}}_{t})dt+\varphi(t)P_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})d\overline{{\textnormal{W}}}_{t}

(25)

approximately
satisfies
\frac{d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}=b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
, where

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}^{\textsc{dg-sde}}_{t}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}^{\textsc{dg-ode}}_{t}({\bm{x}})

(26)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}(t)\frac{\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})}{\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})}_{\text{correction for added stochasticity}}

(27)

and

\hskip-4.2679ptP_{t}({\bm{x}})={\bm{I}}_{D}-\left(\frac{\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})}{\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|}\right)\hskip-2.84526pt\left(\frac{\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})}{\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|}\right)^{T}

(28)

is the “score-orthogonal” projection, which ensures that the
\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
changes deterministically even though
{\bm{x}}_{t}
is stochastic.
In
Appendix G
we provide the formula
Eq. 120
of the SDE drfit that
exactly
achieves
d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})dt
for any choice of
b_{t}
and
{\bm{u}}_{t}
, which we omit here for presentation clarity. In
Appendix H
we experimentally demonstrate that we can obtain exact likelihoods even for stochastic sampling, provided the number of sampling steps is large enough.

The
Eq. 25
allows increasing variation in the samples by injecting noise to DGS
Eq. 23
whilst maintaining the desired evolution of the log-density.
Furthermore, since it is known that diffusion models first generate high-level features and then the details
(
Ho et al., 2020
;
Deja et al., 2022
;
Wang & Vastola, 2023
)
, DGS can be combined with stochasticity by introducing noise at specific stages of the generation process, allowing for controlled variation in either high- or low-level features while preserving the desired level of detail.
We demonstrate this approach in
Fig. 10
and provide more samples in
Appendix K
.

6
Related Work

Sehwag et al. (2022)
proposed a method for generating samples from low-density regions of diffusion models. However, due to the intractability of likelihood in diffusion models, their approach relies on approximations. Subsequent work by
Karczewski et al. (2025)
demonstrated that likelihood is, in fact, tractable even in stochastic diffusion models, challenging the need for such approximations. Building on this, our proposed methods provide explicit likelihood control for both deterministic sampling—via Prior Guidance (
section 3
) and Density Guidance (
section 4
)—and stochastic sampling through Stochastic Density Guidance (
section 5
).

Song et al. (2021b)
observed that scaling the latent code alters the amount of detail in deterministically generated images.
While this phenomenon has been widely acknowledged, we provide a rigorous analysis (
section 3
) and prove that it is a direct consequence of Score Alignment
Eq. 11
, which guarantees that scaling leads to a monotonic change in the likelihood of the generated image,
\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})
.
Furthermore, we introduce tractable numerical tools (
Fig. 6
) that can verify whether any given CNF model (not necessarily score-based) exhibits this behavior.

Karras et al. (2024a)
proposed auto-guidance as a method for improving sample quality by targeting high-density regions. However,
Karczewski et al. (2025)
found that the highest-density regions in diffusion models contain cartoon-like or blurry images, which raises concerns about the effectiveness of purely maximizing likelihood. In contrast, we introduce multiple cost-free methods for explicitly controlling the likelihood of generated samples. Additionally, while
Karras et al. (2024a)
observed that scaling the score function leads to oversimplified images, we demonstrate that DGS
Eq. 23
enables effective control over image detail—both increasing and decreasing it—when the scaling is adapted both temporally and spatially
Eq. 24
.

Yu et al. (2023)
introduced Riemannian Langevin Dynamics, an SDE with a non-diagonal diffusion matrix, similar in structure to our Stochastic Density Guidance (
section 5
). However, a key distinction is that our diffusion matrix is a projection onto the orthogonal complement of the subspace spanned by the score function. As a result, it is not positive definite and cannot serve as a Riemannian metric tensor, making our approach fundamentally different in its mathematical formulation and behavior.

Recently,
Kamkari et al. (2024)
proposed a method for measuring local intrinsic dimension, which, in the case of images, corresponds to the amount of detail present. However, we show that negative
\log p
is a more effective measure of image detail and provide empirical comparisons in
Fig. 4
. Moreover, while
Kamkari et al. (2024)
focus on measuring image detail, our methods enable direct manipulation of it, allowing for finer control over generative model outputs.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced methods for controlling sample density in flow models, enabling manipulation of image detail through likelihood-guided sampling. We provided a theoretical explanation of latent code scaling by introducing score alignment, a condition that can be tractably checked for any CNF model. Building on this, we derived Density Guidance, a principled modification of the generative ODE that allows for exact log-density control during sampling. Finally, we extended this approach to stochastic sampling, demonstrating that it retains precise detail control while allowing controlled variation in image structure and detail. Our findings deepen the understanding of likelihood in flow models and provide practical tools for better sample control.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work that advances the understanding and controllability of sample density in diffusion-based generative models. By introducing techniques for precise log-density control, our work contributes to improved interpretability and fine-grained control over image generation. While these advancements could enhance applications in creative and scientific domains, they also raise considerations around synthetic media generation and potential misuse. However, our contributions primarily aim at improving theoretical understanding and control in generative modeling, without introducing new ethical risks beyond those already associated with generative AI.

A.1
Constrained optimization

In multiple sections, we will be solving constrained optimization problems, which can be written in the following way.
Suppose
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
{\bm{v}}\neq\mathbf{0}
, any
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
The problem we will encounter is

\begin{split}&\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|^{2}\\
&\text{s.t.}\quad{\bm{x}}^{T}{\bm{v}}=a.\end{split}

(29)

We solve this by introducing the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|^{2}+\lambda({\bm{x}}^{T}{\bm{v}}-a)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
By setting
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}=\mathbf{0}
, we get

{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}+\lambda{\bm{v}}=0\Rightarrow{\bm{x}}={\bm{y}}-\lambda{\bm{v}}.

(30)

To find
\lambda
we substitute for
{\bm{x}}
in the constraint and find

{\bm{y}}^{T}{\bm{v}}-\lambda\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}=a\Rightarrow\lambda=\frac{{\bm{y}}^{T}{\bm{v}}-a}{\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}}.

(31)

Combining the two, we get that the solution is given by

{\bm{x}}={\bm{y}}+\frac{a-{\bm{v}}^{T}{\bm{y}}}{\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}}{\bm{v}}.

(32)

A.2
Divergence-gradient identity

We will make use of an identity connecting the gradient of the divergence with the divergence of a Jacobian vector product.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
with continuous 2-nd order derivatives and
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Define
g({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\operatorname{div}f({\bm{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial f^{i}}{\partial x_{i}}({\bm{x}})
and
G({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{x}}}({\bm{x}}){\bm{v}}
.
Then
g:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
is a scalar function and
G:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a vector function satisfying

\nabla g({\bm{x}})^{T}{\bm{v}}=\operatorname{div}G({\bm{x}}).

(33)

Equivalently, we write it as

\left(\nabla\operatorname{div}f({\bm{x}})\right)^{T}{\bm{v}}=\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{x}}}({\bm{x}}){\bm{v}}\right)

(34)

Proof.

\displaystyle\nabla g({\bm{x}})^{T}{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{D}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{j}}({\bm{x}})v_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{D}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial f^{i}}{\partial x_{i}}({\bm{x}})\right)v_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D}\frac{\partial f^{i}}{\partial x_{j}}v_{j}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{x}}}({\bm{x}}){\bm{v}}\right)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}G^{i}({\bm{x}})=\operatorname{div}G({\bm{x}}).

∎

A.3
Optimality of projection

In
Appendix G
we will be interested in finding a linear operator
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
satisfying
{\bm{A}}{\bm{v}}=\mathbf{0}
for some
{\bm{v}}
, so that the distance between
{\bm{A}}
and the identity
{\bm{I}}_{D}
is minimal.
The following lemma provides a solution.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\mathbf{0}\neq{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
The solution of

\begin{split}&\min_{{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}}\|{\bm{A}}-{\bm{I}}_{D}\|\\
&\text{s.t. }{\bm{A}}{\bm{v}}=\mathbf{0},\end{split}

(35)

where
\|\cdot\|
can be either the spectral or Frobenius norm, is given by the projection matrix

{\bm{A}}^{\textsc{opt}}={\bm{P}}={\bm{I}}_{D}-\left(\frac{{\bm{v}}}{\|{\bm{v}}\|}\right)\left(\frac{{\bm{v}}}{\|{\bm{v}}\|}\right)^{T}.

(36)

Proof.

First note that for any
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
satisfying
{\bm{A}}{\bm{v}}=\mathbf{0}
, we have

\|{\bm{A}}-{\bm{I}}_{D}\|_{F}\geq\|{\bm{A}}-{\bm{I}}_{D}\|_{2}=\max_{{\bm{w}}\neq 0}\left|\frac{{\bm{w}}^{T}({\bm{A}}-{\bm{I}}_{D}){\bm{w}}}{\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}}\right|\geq\left|\frac{{\bm{v}}^{T}({\bm{A}}-{\bm{I}}_{D}){\bm{v}}}{\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}}\right|=\left|\frac{{\bm{v}}^{T}(0-{\bm{v}})}{\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}}\right|=\frac{{\bm{v}}^{T}{\bm{v}}}{\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}}=1.

(37)

On the other hand
{\bm{P}}-{\bm{I}}_{D}=\left(\frac{{\bm{v}}}{\|{\bm{v}}\|}\right)\left(\frac{{\bm{v}}}{\|{\bm{v}}\|}\right)^{T}
, which has only a single non-zero eigenvalue
\lambda=1
and thus

\|{\bm{P}}-{\bm{I}}_{D}\|_{F}=\|{\bm{P}}-{\bm{I}}_{D}\|_{2}=1

(38)

and

{\bm{P}}{\bm{v}}={\bm{v}}-\frac{{\bm{v}}^{T}{\bm{v}}}{\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}}{\bm{v}}=\mathbf{0}.

(39)

Therefore
{\bm{A}}={\bm{P}}
satisfies
{\bm{A}}{\bm{v}}=\mathbf{0}
and minimizes both
\|{\bm{A}}-{\bm{I}}_{D}\|_{2}
and
\|{\bm{A}}-{\bm{I}}_{D}\|_{F}
.
∎

Appendix B
Derivation of CNF density evolutions

We reproduce the continuous-time normaling flow (CNF) density evolution of
Chen et al. (2018)
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}),

(40)

and the generalised CNF density evolution of
Karczewski et al. (2025)
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}(\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})),

(41)

with a unified derivation.

We assume a time-dependent particle
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
evolving through continuous time
t\in\mathbb{R}
governed by an ordinary differential equation (ODE)

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bm{x}}_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}),

(42)

where
{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a time-dependent vector field that maps any state vector
{\bm{x}}
to its time derivative vector
{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
.

CNF density evolution

We are interested in the time evolution of the spatiotemporal log-likelihood
\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
for particles evolving under the ODE.
We write the log density total derivative wrt time by using the chain rule

\displaystyle\frac{d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\frac{dp_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}

(43)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\left(\frac{\partial p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{\partial{\bm{x}}}\cdot\frac{d{\bm{x}}_{t}}{dt}\right),

(44)

which describes the density evolution of a particle moving under a flow.
We assume that the ODE is a continuous-time normalizing flow, where the density is conserved over time. This is described by the continuity equation
(
Finlay et al., 2020
;
Xu et al., 2024
)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\Big(p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big)=0,

(45)

which describes the change in particle density as a result of a vector field
{\bm{u}}_{t}
transporting the particles, at location
{\bm{x}}
. By substitution we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\Big(-\nabla\cdot(p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}))+\nabla p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big)

(46)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\Big(-p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\nabla p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\nabla p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big)

(47)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})

(48)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})

(49)

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}).

(50)

Generalised CNF density evolution

Next, we derive the evolution of log-density
\log p_{t}
of a particle that is moving in some non-canonical direction, ie.
\dot{{\bm{x}}}_{t}=\tilde{{\bm{u}}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\not={\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
. Notably, the continuity equation remains with the
{\bm{u}}_{t}
as we are describing the particle density in the marginal induced by the original transport
{\bm{u}}_{t}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\left(-\nabla\cdot\Big(p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big)+\nabla p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)

(51)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\Big(-p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\nabla p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\nabla p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big)

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\Big(-p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\nabla p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\cdot\Big(\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big)\Big)

(53)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\frac{1}{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\nabla p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\cdot\Big(\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big)

(54)

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\cdot\Big(\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big),

(55)

which is the generalised instantaneous change of density.

Appendix C
Derivation of Score Alignment

In this section, we prove the Score Alignment condition, a necessary and sufficient condition for prior guidance to be effective in controlling
\log p_{0}
.
Formally, assume a latent curve
c:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
following the score at
t=T
,
c^{\prime}(s)=\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{0}(c(s)))
. In a Gaussian prior
p_{T}
the curve becomes a line of scaled latents
{\bm{x}}_{T}
.
The
\log p_{0}
is monotonic on the decoded curve when

\frac{d}{ds}\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0}(c(s)))\geq 0,\qquad\forall s\in(0,1).

(56)

The chain rule gives the derivative

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0}(c(s)))

\displaystyle=\nabla\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0}(c(s)))^{T}\frac{d}{ds}{\bm{x}}_{0}(c(s))

(57)

\displaystyle=\nabla\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0}(c(s)))^{T}\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{0}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{T}}(c(s))c^{\prime}(s)

(58)

\displaystyle=\nabla\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0}(c(s)))^{T}\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{0}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{T}}(c(s))\nabla\log p_{T}(c(s))

(59)

Therefore, for
Eq. 56
to hold for a curve passing through some arbitrary
{\bm{x}}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
at some point
s\in(0,1)
it must hold

\nabla\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0}({\bm{x}}_{T}))^{T}\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{0}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{T}}({\bm{x}}_{T})\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})\geq 0,

(60)

where in
Eq. 11
we omit the
{\bm{x}}_{T}
in the parentheses for brevity.

C.1
Score alignment time evolution

In this subsection, we derive
Eq. 13
, i.e. how the SA condition can be checked without knowing
\nabla\log p_{t}
for
t<T
.
Let
c
be a latent curve following the score and passing through
{\bm{x}}_{T}
, i.e.
c:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
c^{\prime}(s)=\nabla\log p_{T}(c(s))
and
c(0)={\bm{x}}_{T}
.
Define

\psi(t,s)\coloneqq\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}(c(s)))\>\text{for }t\in[0,T],\ s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon).

(61)

The SA condition at
{\bm{x}}_{T}
(
Eq. 60
) is given by

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial s}(0,0)=\nabla\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0}({\bm{x}}_{T}))^{T}\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{0}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{T}}({\bm{x}}_{T})\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T}).

(62)

Note that

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial s}(T,0)=\frac{d}{ds}\log p_{T}(c(s))\bigg\rvert_{s=0}=\nabla\log p_{T}(c(s))^{T}c^{\prime}(s)\bigg\rvert_{s=0}=\|\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})\|^{2}.

(63)

Therefore
Eq. 62
can be equivalently written as

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial s}(0,0)=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial s}(T,0)+\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial s}(0,0)-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial s}(T,0)\right)=\|\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})\|^{2}+\int_{T}^{0}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial t\partial s}(t,0)dt,

(64)

where we applied the fundamental theorem of calculus.
We arrived at a seemingly more complex formula.
However, we can now swap the order of the derivatives (assuming that
\log p\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{D})
):

\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial t\partial s}(t,s)=\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial s\partial t}(t,s)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}(t,s)\right).

(65)

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}
is given by
Eq. 3
:

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}(t,s)=\frac{d}{dt}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}(c(s)))=-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}(c(s)))

(66)

and by the chain rule and denoting
\nabla\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}
the gradient of the scalar function
{\bm{x}}\mapsto\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}})
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial s\partial t}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{ds}-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}(c(s)))

(67)

\displaystyle=-\left(\nabla\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}(c(s)))\right)^{T}\frac{d}{ds}({\bm{x}}_{t}(c(s))

(68)

\displaystyle=-\left(\nabla\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}(c(s)))\right)^{T}\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{0}}(c(s))c^{\prime}(s)

(69)

\displaystyle=-\left(\nabla\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}(c(s)))\right)^{T}\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{0}}(c(s))\nabla\log p_{T}(c(s)).

(70)

After setting
s=0
we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial s\partial t}(t,0)

\displaystyle=-\left(\nabla\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{T}))\right)^{T}\underbrace{\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{0}}({\bm{x}}_{T})\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})}_{={\bm{v}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{T})}

(71)

\displaystyle=-\left(\nabla\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{T}))\right)^{T}{\bm{v}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{T})

(72)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:div-identity})}}{{=}}-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{v}}_{t}\right),

(73)

where
{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{x}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{T})
and
{\bm{v}}_{t}={\bm{v}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{T})
.
After plugging into
Eq. 64
:

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial s}(0,0)=\|\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})\|^{2}+\int_{T}^{0}-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{v}}_{t}\right)dt

(74)

which after plugging into
Eq. 62
becomes

\nabla\log p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})^{T}\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{0}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{T}}\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})=\|\nabla\log p_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})\|^{2}+\int_{T}^{0}-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{v}}_{t}\right)dt

(75)

and crucially, the score function for
t<T
does not appear in the RHS, which can be estimated purely from derivatives of
{\bm{u}}_{t}
.

Appendix D
Derivation of Density Guidance

In this section we derive
Eq. 16
. From
Eq. 15
, we see that if
{\bm{x}}_{t}
is following a trajectory given by
d{\bm{x}}_{t}=\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})dt
, then

\displaystyle\frac{d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}=b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\quad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}(\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}))=b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})

(78)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})

(79)

Whenever
\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\mathbf{0}
, then RHS is satisfied only when
b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
. In other words, when the score function vanishes, the infinitesimal change in
\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
is the same and equal to
-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
regardless of the choice of
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}
.

Assume now that
\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\neq\mathbf{0}
. For fixed
(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})
, we can treat the condition in
Eq. 78
as a linear equation with
{\bm{w}}\coloneqq\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
being the unknown quantity we want to solve for. It is a single equation with
D
variables (dimensionality of
{\bm{w}}
), i.e., it does not have a unique solution. We can choose one that satisfies additional criteria out of all possible solutions. Specifically, we choose a solution that diverges from the original trajectory
{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
the least. We therefore solve the following constrained optimization problem

\begin{split}&\min_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{w}}-{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\|^{2}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&{\bm{w}}^{T}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}),\end{split}

(80)

which is treated in
Appendix

A.1
. The solution is

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\frac{b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\|^{2}}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})

(81)

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\frac{b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\|^{2}}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}),

(82)

which matches
Eq. 16
.

Appendix F
Asymptotic behaviour of
\Delta\log p({\bm{x}})+\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}

In this section, we discuss an observation that proved useful in determining
typical
values
\frac{d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}
in diffusion models. Specifically, we observed that for some distributions
p_{0}
after diffusing into
p_{t}
via the forward process
p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}{\bm{x}}_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{D})
, the following holds

h({\bm{x}})=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}\left(\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})+\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}\right)}{\sqrt{2D}}\xrightarrow[D\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(83)

where
D
denotes the dimension of the distribution
p_{t}
and “
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}
” denotes convergence in distribution.

F.1
Single data point

We begin by showing
Eq. 83
for the simplest possible case, where
p_{0}=\delta_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}
. In that case
p_{t}=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}{\bm{x}}_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{D})
and

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})=\frac{\alpha_{t}{\bm{x}}_{0}-{\bm{x}}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})=-\frac{D}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

Since
{\bm{x}}=\alpha_{t}{\bm{x}}_{0}+\sigma_{t}{\bm{\varepsilon}}
for
{\bm{\varepsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}_{D})
and our expression becomes

h({\bm{x}})=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}\left(-\frac{D}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}\right)}{\sqrt{2D}}=\frac{\sum_{j}(\varepsilon_{j}^{2}-1)}{\sqrt{2D}}.

(84)

Since
\{\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\}_{j}
are i.i.d. random variables with
\chi^{2}_{1}
distribution, we have that
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{j}^{2}]=1
,
\mathrm{Var}[\varepsilon_{j}^{2}]=2
, and the claim follows from the central limit theorem.

F.2
Non-isotropic Gaussian distribution

When
p_{t}
is Gaussian, but with non-diagonal covariance an analogous result holds when the covariance matrix satisfies some additional conditions. We begin with a useful lemma.

Lemma F.1

(Quadratic CLT
(
de Jong, 1987
)
)
.

Suppose
A=[a_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
is a real symmetric matrix with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{D}
. Let
\{\varepsilon_{j}\}_{j=1\dots D}
be independent variables such that
\varepsilon_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

If

\lim_{D\to\infty}\frac{\max_{j\leq D}\lambda_{j}^{2}}{\sum_{j\leq D}\lambda_{j}^{2}}=0,

(85)

then

\frac{{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{T}A{\bm{\varepsilon}}-\mathrm{Tr}(A)}{\sqrt{2}\|A\|_{F}}\xrightarrow[D\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(86)

Let
p_{t}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{\mu},\Sigma)
for
\Sigma
satisfying the following conditions. Denoting
\Sigma=LL^{T}
, and
\tilde{\Sigma}=L^{-1}(L^{T})^{-1}
with
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{D}
eigenvalues of
\tilde{\Sigma}
, we assume

\lim_{D\to\infty}\frac{\max_{j\leq D}\lambda_{j}^{2}}{\sum_{j\leq D}\lambda_{j}^{2}}=0

(87)

Note that for
\Sigma=\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{D}
, we have
\tilde{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\bm{I}}_{D}
,
\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}
, and all the above conditions becomes
\lim_{D\to\infty}\frac{1}{D}=0
, which of course holds. Then

h({\bm{x}})=\frac{\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})+\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}}{\sqrt{2}\|\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|_{F}}\xrightarrow[D\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(88)

For
{\bm{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{\mu},\Sigma)
, we can represent
{\bm{x}}=\mu+L{\bm{\varepsilon}}
for
\Sigma=LL^{T}
and
{\bm{\varepsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}_{D})
. In this case, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})=\Sigma^{-1}(\mu-{\bm{x}})=-(L^{T})^{-1}{\bm{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})=-\Sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})=-\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma^{-1}).

Note that
\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}={\bm{\varepsilon}}^{T}\tilde{\Sigma}{\bm{\varepsilon}}
, where
\tilde{\Sigma}=L^{-1}(L^{T})^{-1}
. Since
\Sigma^{-1}=(L^{T})^{-1}L^{-1}
and
\mathrm{Tr}(AB)=\mathrm{Tr}(BA)
, we have
\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})=-\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\Sigma})
and
\|\tilde{\Sigma}\|_{F}=\|\Sigma^{-1}\|_{F}=\|\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|_{F}

We can now write

h({\bm{x}})=\frac{\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})+\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}}{\sqrt{2}\|\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|_{F}}=\frac{{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{T}\tilde{\Sigma}{\bm{\varepsilon}}-\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{\Sigma})}{\sqrt{2}\|\tilde{\Sigma}\|_{F}}\xrightarrow[D\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

(89)

from
Lemma F.1
.

Appendix G
Stochastic density guidance

In this section, we derive the stochastic density guidance method.
A central tool in this section is Itô’s lemma
(
Itô, 1951
)
, a generalization of the total derivative to stochastic processes.

Lemma G.1

(Itô’s Lemma)
.

Let
d{\bm{x}}_{t}=\mathbf{\mu}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})dt+{\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})d{\textnormal{W}}_{t}
be a
D
-dimensional Itô process with
\mu:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
{\bm{G}}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
and
W
the Wiener process in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. For a smooth function
h:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
, it holds that
h(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})
is also an Itô process with the following dynamics

\begin{split}dh(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})=&\Big(\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})+\mathbf{\mu}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\frac{\partial h}{\partial{\bm{x}}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\Big({\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\nabla^{2}h(t,{\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})\Big)\Big)dt\\
&+\frac{\partial h}{\partial{\bm{x}}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}{\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})d{\textnormal{W}}_{t},\end{split}

(92)

where
\nabla^{2}h
is the Hessian matrix of
h
w.r.t
{\bm{x}}
. In the case when
{\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}})=\varphi(t){\bm{I}}_{D}
, the dynamics simplify to

dh(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})=\Big(\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})+\mathbf{\mu}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\frac{\partial h}{\partial{\bm{x}}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}(t)\Delta_{\bm{x}}h(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})\Big)ds+\varphi(t)\frac{\partial h}{\partial{\bm{x}}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}d{\textnormal{W}}_{t}

(93)

In contrast to the total derivative for deterministic processes, the forward and reverse-time dynamics are not the same for stochastic processes.
We now prove the reverse-time Itô’s lemma, which will be useful in our derivation as our convention is that sampling happens backward in time, from
t=T
to
t=0
.

Corollary 1

(Reverse-time Itô’s lemma)
.

Let
d{\bm{x}}_{t}=\mathbf{\mu}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})dt+{\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})d\overline{{\textnormal{W}}}_{t}
,
dt<0
,
\overline{{\textnormal{W}}}
the Wiener process running backwards in time from
t=T
to
t=0
and

Recall

(

We now ask: how can modify the stochastic dynamics (

) so that

d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})

.
Suppose that
{\bm{x}}
is following

d{\bm{x}}_{t}=\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})dt+{\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})d\overline{{\textnormal{W}}}_{t}

for some
\tilde{\bm{u}}

{\bm{G}}
.
To evaluate the change log-density we will use

applied to

:

\begin{split}d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=&\Big(\frac{\partial\log p_{t}}{\partial t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left({\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})\right)\Big)dt\\
&+\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}{\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})d\overline{{\textnormal{W}}}_{t}.\end{split}

(99)

Since we assumed that
d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})dt
, the stochastic component of
d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})
must vanish, i.e.
\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})^{T}{\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}})=\mathbf{0}
.
There are many
{\bm{G}}
that satisfy this condition including a trivial
{\bm{G}}\equiv\mathbf{0}
.
However, standard stochastic sampling (
Eq. 7
) assumes isotropic noise, i.e.
{\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}})=\varphi(t){\bm{I}}_{D}
and we want to match that as closely as possible.
An optimal solution (
Lemma A.2
) to this problem is the projection
{\bm{G}}(t,{\bm{x}})=\varphi(t){\bm{P}}_{t}({\bm{x}})
for:

{\bm{P}}_{t}({\bm{x}})={\bm{I}}_{D}-\left(\frac{\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})}{\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|}\right)\left(\frac{\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})}{\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|}\right)^{T}.

(100)

Clearly
{\bm{P}}_{t}({\bm{x}})^{T}={\bm{P}}_{t}({\bm{x}})
. Furthermore, since
{\bm{P}}_{t}
is a projection matrix, it also holds that
{\bm{P}}_{t}({\bm{x}}){\bm{P}}_{t}({\bm{x}})={\bm{P}}_{t}({\bm{x}})
.
Now we can plug this into

d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\Big(\frac{\partial\log p_{t}}{\partial t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}(t)\mathrm{Tr}\left({\bm{P}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{P}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)\Big)dt.

(101)

(102)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\right)-\frac{1}{\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})^{T}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\right)

represents the Rayleigh quotient of the Hessian evaluated at

.

\begin{split}b_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\frac{d\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}{dt}=&-\operatorname{div}{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})^{T}(\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-{\bm{u}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}))\\

Practical approximation

The Laplacian,
\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
, is given by the trace of the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
, which corresponds to the sum of its eigenvalues. In contrast,

\mathcal{R}(\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}),\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}))=\frac{\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})^{T}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})}{\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}}

represents the Rayleigh quotient of the Hessian evaluated at
\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
. This quantity is bounded in absolute value by the largest absolute eigenvalue of the Hessian, i.e., its spectral norm. Intuitively, when the eigenvalues of the Hessian are relatively uniform—such as when it is close to a scaled identity matrix—the Laplacian scales linearly with the dimension, whereas the Rayleigh quotient remains bounded by a constant. This suggests that in high-dimensional settings, the Laplacian dominates.

Empirically, we verified this intuition by estimating both quantities on real data.
Specifically, we used a VP-SDE model trained on CIFAR-10 (
32\times 32
resolution,
D=3072
)
(
Karczewski et al., 2025
)
and a VE-SDE model trained on ImageNet (
64\times 64
resolution,
D=12288
)
(
Karras et al., 2022
)
and sampled uniformly values of
t\in[0,T]
and corresponding
{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim p_{t}
(used 8192 and 16384 samples respectively) and found that the ratio was negligibly small in practice

\left|\frac{\mathcal{R}(\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}),\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}))}{\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}\right|\approx\begin{cases}\text{0.0003 {\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\textpm 0.00006}}&\text{ for CIFAR-10}\\
\text{0.00006 {\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\textpm 0.00003}}&\text{ for ImageNet64}\end{cases}

(122)

This confirms that, in practice,
\mathcal{R}(\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}),\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}}))
is negligible compared to
\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
.
Therefore, in practice, we use

{\bm{u}}^{\textsc{dg-sde}}_{t}({\bm{x}})\coloneqq{\bm{u}}^{\textsc{dg-ode}}_{t}({\bm{x}})+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}(t)\frac{\Delta\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})}{\|\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}}\nabla\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})}_{\text{correction for added stochasticity}}.

(123)

\bm{x}_{T}

There exist various metrics measuring the perceptual quality of image generation models. Most popular include LPIPS
(
Zhang et al., 2018
)
and SSIM
(
Wang et al., 2004
)
. However, these are
reference-based
quality measures, meaning that they require the ground truth to compare to, which is not available in unconditional image generation. We therefore used NIQE
(
Mittal et al., 2012
)
, which is a non-reference perceptual quality measure, reported to strongly correlate with human judgement. It provides a single number per image, which indicates whether an image has been distorted (a lower number - higher quality). It was used, e.g., by
Sami et al. (2024)
to evaluate super-resolution diffusion models.

Appendix N
Gaussian Mixture asymptotics

Lemma N.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\>f(x)=\exp(-\frac{1}{2}x)x
. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}

f(x)\leq 1

(125)

Proof.

f^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x\right)x+\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{2}x\right).

f^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x<2
and
f^{\prime}(x)<0
for
x>2
. Therefore for all
x\in\mathbb{R}

f(x)\leq f(2)=\exp(-1)2=\frac{2}{e}<1.

∎

Lemma N.2
.

For any
a>0
and
x>0

\left(\frac{a}{x}+x\right)^{2}\geq 4a.

(126)

Proof.

0\leq\left(\frac{a}{x}-x\right)^{2}=\frac{a^{2}}{x^{2}}-2a+x^{2}=\left(\frac{a}{x}+x\right)^{2}-4a

∎

Theorem 1
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be drawn from a mixture of
K
Gaussian components:

\Pr(Y=k)=\pi_{k},\quad X\mid(Y=k)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k},\sigma^{2}I_{D}),

(127)

where
\mu_{j}\neq\mu_{i}
for
i\neq j
(note that this assumption is not restrictive, because when different components share the mean, the mixture can be rewritten with distinct
\mu
and updated
\pi
). Define

h(X)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2D}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}w_{j}(X)\frac{\|X-\mu_{j}\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-D\right],

(128)

where

w_{j}(x)=\frac{\pi_{j}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x-\mu_{j}\|^{2}\right]}{\sum_{m=1}^{K}\pi_{m}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x-\mu_{m}\|^{2}\right]}.

(129)

Then
h(X)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)
as
D\to\infty
.

Proof.

Step 1: It suffices to show
h(X)\mid(Y=k)\to N(0,1)
.

Indeed,

\varphi_{h(X)}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{ith(X)}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{ith(X)}\mid Y=k\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\varphi_{h(X)|Y=k}(t).

Thus if each conditional law converges to
N(0,1)
(point-wise convergence of characteristic functions), so does the unconditional mixture.

Step 2: Rewrite
X=\mu_{k}+\sigma{\bm{\varepsilon}}
.

Conditioning on
Y=k
, we have
X=\mu_{k}+\sigma{\bm{\varepsilon}}
,
{\bm{\varepsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}_{D})
. Thus

\frac{\|X-\mu_{k}\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{D},\quad\frac{\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}-D}{\sqrt{2D}}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)

Step 3: Define remainder
\mathcal{R}_{D}
.

Set

\sum_{j=1}^{K}w_{j}(X)\frac{\|X-\mu_{j}\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}+\mathcal{R}_{D},

so

h(X)=\frac{\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}-D}{\sqrt{2D}}+\frac{\mathcal{R}_{D}}{\sqrt{2D}}.

Using Slutsky’s theorem, it suffices to show
\mathcal{R}_{D}/\sqrt{2D}\xrightarrow{d}0
, which is a weaker condition than convergence in probability.

It thus suffices to show

\frac{\mathcal{R}_{D}}{\sqrt{2D}}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0

(130)

Step 4: Showing
\mathcal{R}_{D}/\sqrt{2D}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0
.

Let

\Delta_{j,k}:=\frac{\mu_{k}-\mu_{j}}{\sigma}\neq\mathbf{0}.

Note

\frac{\|X-\mu_{j}\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}+\Delta_{j,k}\|^{2}=\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}+2{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{T}\Delta_{j,k}+\|\Delta_{j,k}\|^{2}=\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}+b_{j}

for
b_{j}=2{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{T}\Delta_{j,k}+\|\Delta_{j,k}\|^{2}
and
b_{k}=0
since
\Delta_{k,k}=0
.
Hence

\mathcal{R}_{D}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}w_{j}(X)\left[2{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{T}\Delta_{j,k}+\|\Delta_{j,k}\|^{2}\right]=\sum_{j\neq k}w_{j}(X)b_{j}.

Also:

w_{j}(X)=\frac{\pi_{j}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x-\mu_{j}\|^{2}\right]}{\sum_{m=1}^{K}\pi_{m}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x-\mu_{m}\|^{2}\right]}=\frac{\pi_{j}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}+b_{j})\right]}{\sum_{m=1}^{K}\pi_{m}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}+b_{m})\right]}=\frac{\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})}{\sum_{m=1}^{K}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}.

Then

\mathcal{R}_{D}=\frac{\sum_{j\neq k}\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})b_{j}}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\neq k}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}.

We will now separate the sum in the numerator of
\mathcal{R}_{D}
into positive and negative
b_{j}
. Define
J^{+}=\{j\neq k|b_{j}\geq 0\}
and
J^{-}=\{j\neq k|b_{j}<0\}
:

\mathcal{R}_{D}=\frac{\sum_{j\in J^{+}}\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})b_{j}}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\neq k}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}+\frac{\sum_{j\in J^{-}}\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})b_{j}}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\neq k}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}.

From
Lemma N.1
,
\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})b_{j}\leq 1
and thus
\sum_{j\in J^{+}}\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})b_{j}\leq 1
.
Therefore (note shrinking denominators to achieve upper bounds):

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{R}_{D}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\sum_{j\in J^{+}}\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})b_{j}}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\neq k}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}\right|+\left|\frac{\sum_{j\in J^{-}}\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})b_{j}}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\neq k}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\neq k}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}+\frac{\sum_{j\in J^{-}}\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})|b_{j}|}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\neq k}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{k}}+\frac{\sum_{j\in J^{-}}\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})|b_{j}|}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\in J^{-}}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{k}}+\frac{\sum_{j\in J^{-}}\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})|b_{j}|}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\in J^{-}}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}=\frac{1}{\pi_{k}}+\sum_{j\in J^{-}}\tilde{w}_{j}|b_{j}|,

where
\tilde{w}_{j}=\frac{\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\in J^{-}}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}
and
\sum_{j\in J^{-}}\tilde{w}_{j}=\frac{\sum_{j\in J^{-}}\pi_{j}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{j})}{\pi_{k}+\sum_{m\in J^{-}}\pi_{m}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}b_{m})}\leq 1
. Thus

\sum_{j\in J^{-}}\tilde{w}_{j}|b_{j}|\leq\sum_{j\in J^{-}}\tilde{w}_{j}\max_{j\in J^{-}}|b_{j}|\leq\max_{j\in J^{-}}|b_{j}|=\max(-\min_{j\neq k}b_{j},0),

where the last equality comes from the definition of
J^{-}
. In summary

|\mathcal{R}_{D}|\leq\frac{1}{\pi_{k}}+\max(-\min_{j\neq k}b_{j},0).

Now, for any
\delta>0
:

\Pr\left(\frac{|\mathcal{R}_{D}|}{\sqrt{2D}}>\delta\right)=\Pr\left(|\mathcal{R}_{D}|>\delta\sqrt{2D}\right)\leq\Pr\left(\max(-\min_{j\neq k}b_{j},0)>\delta\sqrt{2D}-\frac{1}{\pi_{k}}\right).

Now choose
D
large enough so that
a=\delta\sqrt{2D}-\frac{1}{\pi_{k}}>0
.
Then

\Pr\left(\max(-\min_{j\neq k}b_{j},0)>a\right)=\Pr\left(\exists_{j\neq k}\>b_{j}<-a\right)\leq\sum_{j\neq k}\Pr\left(b_{j}<-a\right).

Now plugging in the definition of
b_{j}
and using the fact that
Z=\frac{{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{T}\Delta_{k,j}}{\|\Delta_{k,j}\|}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

\Pr\left(b_{j}<-a\right)=\Pr\left(2{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{T}\Delta_{j,k}+\|\Delta_{j,k}\|^{2}<-a\right)=\Pr\left(Z<-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{a}{\|\Delta_{j,k}\|}+\|\Delta_{j,k}\|\right)\right).

And using the standard Gaussian tail bound
\Pr(Z<-t)=\Pr(Z>t)\leq\exp(-\frac{1}{2}t^{2})
for
t>0
:

\Pr\left(b_{j}<-a\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{a}{\|\Delta_{j,k}\|}+\|\Delta_{j,k}\|\right)^{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}a\right),

where the last inequality comes from
Lemma N.2
. Therefore

\Pr\left(\frac{|\mathcal{R}_{D}|}{\sqrt{2D}}>\delta\right)\leq\sum_{j\neq k}\Pr(b_{j}<-a)\leq\sum_{j\neq k}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}a\right)=(K-1)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta\sqrt{2D}-\frac{1}{\pi_{k}}\right)\right)\xrightarrow[D\to\infty]{}0

We have shown that for all
\delta>0

\lim_{D\to\infty}\Pr\left(\left|\frac{\mathcal{R}_{D}}{\sqrt{2D}}\right|>\delta\right)=0,

(131)

which means

\frac{\mathcal{R}_{D}}{\sqrt{2D}}\xrightarrow[D\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0.

∎
