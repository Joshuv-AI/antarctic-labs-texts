On the Fitness Landscape in the N ​ K N\!K Model
Wei-Kuo Chen, Si Tang

Abstract
The N ​ K N\!K model, introduced by Kauffman, Levin, and Weinberger, is a random field used to describe the fitness landscape of certain species with N N genetic loci, each interacting with K K others. The model has wide applications in understanding evolutionary and natural selection as it captures ruggedness feature of the fitness landscape. Earlier literature has been focused on the case K K being a fixed positive integer and used tools from Ergodic and Markov theory. In this paper, by viewing it as a statistical physics object, we investigate the N ​ K N\!K model in the regime K / N → α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] K/N\to\alpha\in(0,1] via the spin glass methodologies. Our main result identifies the exact limits for the free energy at any temperature and the maximum fitness. Moreover, we show that the N ​ K N\!K model exhibits a multiple-peak structure, namely, the number of near-fittest genomes that are asymptotically orthogonal to each other is exponentially large. Based on establishing the overlap gap properties, we obtain quantitative descriptions for the geometry of the fitness landscape and deduce that, in particular, near-fittest evolutionary paths become impossible as the fitness levels of the genomes approach the global maximum for any α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \alpha\in(0,1] . Nevertheless, we also show that by choosing α \alpha sufficiently small, an evolutionary path maintained at a given fitness level can be constructed with high probability.

Introduction

The
N\!K
model is a random field that describes the fitness landscape of some species consisting of
N
loci (or genes) in the genome, each interacting with
K
other loci. It was first proposed by Kauffman, Levin and Weinberger
[
25
,
26
,
46
]
in the late 80s, who studied the ruggedness of the fitness landscape of the
N\!K
model and the evolutionary dynamics on this landscape via adaptive walks. The model has drawn wide attentions of evolutionary biologists
[
19
,
30
,
42
]
, as it captures the
epistatic
phenomenon of gene interactions
[
29
]
, i.e., the effect on fitness at one locus also depends on the states of several other loci. In recent years, more mathematicians become interested in the model
[
17
,
18
,
28
,
48
]
, as the fitness landscape of the
N\!K
model exhibits a tunable ruggedness
[
49
]
as
K
varies, which promotes the existence of exponentially many local attractors in the space of genomes, a crucial feature shared by many statistical physics models (e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
33
,
45
]
) and verified by many experimental evolutionary processes in the lab (e.g.,
[
14
,
36
,
37
]
).

In the classic setup of the
N\!K
model, a genome

{\sigma}=(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{N-1})

is a binary sequence of length
N
, and at each genetic locus
i
, the allele
\sigma_{i}
may have two possible types, denoted as
0
and
1
(e.g., mutant and wildtype, or ressesive and dominant). For each genome
{\sigma}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, its fitness
H_{N,K}({\sigma})
is expressed as the sum of the
N
fitness components
X_{i}
, contributed by each locus
i
. The gene interaction structure is incorporated into the fitness component
X_{i}
, which is determined by the
i
-th locus as well as
K
other epistatic loci, that is

\displaystyle H_{N,K}({\sigma})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}X_{i}(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{\!\!j^{(i)}_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{\!j^{(i)}_{K}}).

(1.1)

Previous studies have concerned about different variations of the
N\!K
model. In
[
24
,
25
]
, the
K
epistatic loci were chosen randomly, whereas in
[
17
,
18
,
26
,
28
,
48
]
, they were either the
K
successors or the
K
nearest loci. The state space may also be different, depending on the goal of the study. For example, besides the binary setting
[
17
,
18
,
25
,
28
]
,

{\sigma}
may represent base pairs along a DNA sequence with each
\sigma_{i}\in\{A,G,C,T\}
, the set of nucleotides, and this setup is suitable to study the fitness contribution of the single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the genome. In addition,
when considering the evolution of protein molecules, people often chose
\sigma_{i}\in\{\text{all amino acids}\}
and view the functionality of proteins, such as structural stability and selectivity, as a measure of fitness.
[
30
,
39
,
40
]
.

The parameter
K
controls the degree of epistasis, which results in different levels of ruggedness of the fitness landscape in the
N\!K
model. One extreme case,
K=0
, is trivial,
because in this case, genes simply do not interact with each other (i.e., there is no epistasis) and the fittest genome
{\sigma}^{\ast}
can be obtained by
maximizing each fitness component
X_{i}(\sigma_{i})
separately, i.e.,
\sigma_{i}^{\ast}=\arg\max_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(\sigma_{i})
for each
i=0,1,\ldots,N-1
.
There is only one local maximal fitness value in this setting, and the landscape of the fitness values is smooth over the space of all genomes. For larger values of
K
,
the landscape becomes more rugged. The case when
K=N-1
is the other extreme, in which fitness values of different genomes are mutually independent,and the model is
known as the random energy model (REM)
[
15
,
16
]
. In this case, the expected number of local optima grows exponentially as
N
increases; the largest fitness value is the maximum of
2^{N}
i.i.d. random variables, which is well-understood using tools from extreme-value theory
[
38
]
. Many properties of REM have been thoroughly explored in recent years — see surveys
[
23
,
31
]
and references therein.

For other values of
K
, most previous results were based on numerical simulations, e.g.,
[
25
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
, or some heuristic arguments
[
46
]
. Mathematically rigorous results are very limited, and we briefly summarize here. When
K
is fixed, the asymptotic behavior of the global maximum was characterized independently in
[
18
]
and
[
17
]
using different approaches. In
[
18
]
, Evans and Steinsaltz related the global maximum with the max-plus product of certain random matrices and identified the exact limit in the case when
K=1
and the fitness components are independent standard exponential random variables. Similar studies were done using the substochastic Harris chains in
[
17
]
for the negative exponential case with
K=1
.
For local maximum,
[
18
]
and
[
17
]
computed the expected fitness value, and in
[
17
]
a central limit theorem and a large deviation result were also obtained for the negative exponential case with
K=1
. Both studies proved an exponential growth for the number of local maxima as
N\to\infty
and identified the exponents in the aforementioned special cases. The methods in
[
17
,
18
]
apply to other values of
K
provided
K
stays fixed as
N\to\infty
. However, analytical computations for the cases when
K>1
or when the fitness components follow other distributions are quite involved. The other scheme in which
K
could grow with
N
is also biologically relevant as indicated in
[
24
]
. This case was first studied heuristically by Weinberger in
[
46
]
, and some of the claims were later made rigorous in
[
28
]
. Among other things, Limic and Pemantle showed that when
K/N\to\alpha
as
N\to\infty
, the probability of a given genome being a local maxima behaved like
N^{-\alpha}
when the fitness components are independent Gaussians. They also conjectured this would hold universally for other fitness component distributions and proved partial results that support their conjecture.

Despite of the results mentioned above, many fundamental questions are still unaddressed, especially concerning the fitness landscape in the regime when
K/N\to\alpha
for some fixed constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
. A key direction is to study the precise limiting behavior of the maximum fitness, as well as the geometric structure of its level sets.
From the perspective of evolutionary dynamics, it is also of great interest to understand how one fittest genome could evolve into another. In this paper, we pursue these directions within the
N\!K
model with adjacent neighborhoods in the limit
K/N\to\alpha
, employing tools from mathematical spin glass theory
[
34
,
43
,
44
]
. We investigate its statistical physics properties such as the asymptotic behavior of the overlap between spin configurations (i.e., genomes), the Gibbs measure, and the free energy. Ultimately, we aim to use these results to address the questions posed above and offer insights into the underlying evolutionary dynamics.

1.1
Model Setup and Main Results

For each locus
i
, we consider its epistatic loci to be its
K
successors, i.e.,
(j^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,j^{(i)}_{K})=(i+1,i+2,\ldots,i+K)
in (
1.1
), with
K
chosen to be asymptotically proportional to
N
as
N\to\infty
. Specifically, for every
N\geq 1,
let
K=\lfloor\alpha(N-1)\rfloor
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
fixed. The boundaries are identified, namely,
\sigma_{i+N}=\sigma_{i}
for each
i
. The alleles are binary, however, we set the the two possible alleles to be
+1
and
-1
, adopting the convention in statistical physics studies. We also denote
\Sigma_{n}:=\{+1,-1\}^{n}
for any integer
n\geq 1
. For each
i=0,1,\ldots,N-1
, the fitness components
\{X_{i}(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1},\ldots,\sigma_{i+K}):(\sigma_{i},\ldots,\sigma_{i+K})\in\Sigma_{K+1}\}
at the
i
-th locus are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables; these fitness components are also assumed to be independent of each other.

With this setup,
(H_{N,K}({\sigma}))_{{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N}}
may be viewed as a centered Gaussian process over
\Sigma_{N}
, whose covariance structure is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}H_{N,K}({\sigma}^{1})H_{N,K}({\sigma}^{2})

\displaystyle=NQ({\sigma}^{1},{\sigma}^{2}),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall{\sigma}^{1},{\sigma}^{2}\in\Sigma_{N},

where
Q({\sigma}^{1},{\sigma}^{2})
is called the
epistatic overlap
between the two genomes
{\sigma}^{1}
and
{\sigma}^{2}
, defined as

Q({\sigma}^{1},{\sigma}^{2}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}I(\sigma_{i}^{1}=\sigma_{i}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{i+K}^{1}=\sigma_{i+K}^{2}).

We remark that,
Q
is invariant to the exactly value (no matter
\{0,1\}
,
\{+1,-1\}
, or some other binary set) that we assign to the alleles and only depends on the agreement of the alleles at each locus. We refer to
Q
as the epistatic overlap to distinguish it from the usual
overlap
, a fundamental quantity considered in many spin glass models and is defined as the (normalized) scalar product,

\displaystyle R({\sigma}^{1},{\sigma}^{2})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

This is another quantity that measures how similar the two genomes are. For any two genomes
\sigma^{l},\sigma^{l^{\prime}}\in\Sigma_{N}
, we will abbreviate their overlaps as
Q_{l,l^{\prime}}:=Q(\sigma^{l},\sigma^{l^{\prime}})
and
R_{l,l^{\prime}}:=R(\sigma^{l},\sigma^{l^{\prime}})
, when there is no ambiguity.

Remark 1.1
.

Note that the overlap
R
is asymptotically stable
as long as the signs of no more than
o(N)
loci are flipped,
however, this is not the case for the epistatic overlap
Q
.
To see this, for example, for any
\sigma^{1}
, letting
\sigma^{2}
be constructed from
\sigma^{1}
by flipping the sign of the first locus,
\sigma^{2}_{0}=-\sigma^{1}_{0}
, we have
R_{1,2}=(N-1)/N\approx R_{1,1}
, but
Q_{1,2}=(N-K-1)/N\approx 1-\alpha<Q_{1,1}
.

In this paper, we view the
N\!K
model as a statistical physics model by interpreting the genome
\sigma
and the fitness function
H_{N,K}
as the spin configuration and the Hamiltonian (or energy), respectively. The maximal fitness and the fittest genome, namely,

\displaystyle M_{N,K}:=\max_{{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N}}\frac{H_{N,K}({\sigma})}{N}\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \sigma^{*}:=\argmax_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\frac{H_{N,K}(\sigma)}{N},

(1.2)

are correspondingly called the ground state energy and the ground state. The fundamental idea of statistical physics for handling these objectives is to introduce the Gibbs measure, which is defined as, for a given (inverse) temperature
\beta>0
,

G_{N,K,\beta}({\sigma})=\frac{e^{\beta H_{N,K}({\sigma})}}{Z_{N,K}(\beta)},\ \ \forall{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N},

where
Z_{N,K}(\beta):=\sum_{\sigma}e^{\beta H_{N,K}(\sigma)}
is called the partition function. The Gibbs measure describes the chance that a given genome
\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}
is observed for a given parameter
\beta>0
,
which can be interpreted as a measure of natural selection in evolution: when
\beta
is large, the Gibbs measure is highly concentrated on genomes with higher fitness values and natural selection would favor much strongly toward these genomes; while if
\beta
is small, all genomes would have comparable chances to be selected by evolution. Denote by
{\sigma}^{1},{\sigma}^{2},\ldots
a collection of independently sampled genomes (called the
replicas
) from the Gibbs measure
G_{N,K,\beta}
and by
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\beta}
the expectation with respect to
G_{N,K,\beta}
. The free energy is defined as

\displaystyle F_{N,K}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\ln Z_{N,K}(\beta),

which plays the role as a soft approximation for
M_{N,K}
through the simple bounds,

M_{N,K}\leq\frac{F_{N,K}(\beta)}{\beta}\leq\frac{\ln 2}{\beta}+M_{N,K}.

(1.3)

1.1.1
Free Energy, Maximum Fitness, and Multiple Peaks

Our first main result characterizes the limiting behavior of the free energy and the maximum fitness as
N\to\infty
. While they appear to be not depending on the choice of
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the free energy exhibits a phase transition at the critical temperature
\beta_{c}:=\sqrt{2\ln 2}\approx 1.1774
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1],
we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}F_{N,K}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ln 2+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2},&\forall\beta<\beta_{c},\\
\\
\beta\beta_{c},&\forall\beta\geq\beta_{c}.\end{array}\right.

It follows from (
1.3
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}M_{N,K}=\beta_{c}
.

As mentioned before, when
K=N-1
(i.e.,
\alpha=1
), the
NK
model is exactly the same as REM. Our results in Theorem
1.1
show that the limiting free energy and maximum fitness of the
N\!K
model agree with those of REM, and this holds for all values of
\alpha\in(0,1]
. The next result characterizes a multiple-peak property for the near-fittest genomes in the
N\!K
model.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1].
For any
0<\varepsilon<\beta_{c},
there exist two absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
independent of
N
such that with probability at least
1-C_{1}e^{-N/C_{1}}
, there exists a set
S_{N}(\varepsilon)\subset\Sigma_{N}
with the following properties

1.

|S_{N}(\varepsilon)|\geq e^{C_{2}N}
;

2.

\frac{H_{N,K}(\sigma)}{N}\geq M_{N,K}-\varepsilon
for all
\sigma\in S_{N}(\varepsilon)
;

3.

Q(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=0
and
|R(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})|<\varepsilon
and for any two distinct
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in S_{N}(\varepsilon).

Remark 1.2
.

Theorem
1.2
states that for any value
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the landscape of the
N\!K
model contains exponentially many near-fittest genomes that are approximately pairwise orthogonal (with respect to either
Q
or
R
).
Note that, with
R_{1,2}\approx 0
, any near-fittest genome pairs
\sigma^{1}\neq\sigma^{2}
from
S_{N}(\varepsilon)
would disagree at approximately
N/2
loci. Furthermore, since
Q_{1,2}=0
, these mismatched loci can not be too clustered together in the genome such that a perfect match of
K+1
(or more) consecutive loci may still exist.

Remark 1.3
.

In an earlier work
[
9
]
, Chatterjee showed that there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
N>K\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
for
\delta:=C(\ln K)^{-1/2}
, there is a subset
A\subset\Sigma_{N}
satisfying that
|A|\geq 1/\delta,

H_{N,K}(\sigma)/N\geq(1-\delta)M_{N,K}
for all
\sigma\in A,
and
Q(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\leq\delta
for all distinct
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in A
. Under our setting
K\approx\alpha N
, Chatterjee’s result suggests that the
|A|
is at least of logarithmic scale in
N
. Our Theorem
1.2
improves this estimate by establishing an exponential growth for the size of the set, and moreover, the orthogonality of these genomes also holds under the metric
R(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
. The statements can be further strengthened in the regime
\alpha>\alpha_{\ast}
(see Corollary
1.1
later), where the set
S_{N}(\varepsilon)
and the constant
C_{2}
can be described explicitly.

Remark 1.4
.

Previous work
[
17
,
18
,
28
,
47
]
have concerned about the number of local fitness maxima of the
N\!K
model for various distributions of the fitness components and for
K
being either fixed or growing linearly with
N
. Note a genome
\sigma
is called an local fitness maximum if
H_{N,K}(\sigma)\geq H_{N,K}(\sigma^{\prime})
for any
\sigma^{\prime}
that differs from
\sigma
at exactly one locus. In these studies, local fitness maxima are found to be exponentially many — however, this does not imply that the number of near-fittest genomes behaves the same way. In fact, in a recent large-scale evolutionary experiment
[
36
]
, it was found that most local maxima had low fitness values; there were only 74 high-fitness peaks out of the 514 local maxima identified in the experiment. Our result in Theorem
1.2
confirms that the number of near-fittest genomes also grows exponentially with
N
as
N\to\infty
.

1.1.2
Overlap Gap Properties

Following the multiple-peak property, several question naturally arise. For instance,
are
all
near-fittest genomes pairwise orthogonal, as those in the set
S_{N}(\epsilon)
?
How are near-fittest genomes interconnected?
In particular, as proposed by S. Evans and discussed in
[
10
, Open Problem 4.5]
,
is it possible to evolve from one near-fittest genome to another along a path where all intermediate genomes remain near-fittest?

More precisely, one is interested in evolutionary paths connecting two near-fittest genomes of
O(1)
many steps such that (i) in each step only a small fraction of the
N
loci mutate, and (ii) all intermediate genomes along the path remain near-fittest.
These requirements are especially relevant in the context of evolution: firstly, for natural populations, it is unlikely to have too many mutations in one generation; secondly, genomes of lower fitness are more likely to be eliminated under natural selection, making evolutionary paths through such states significantly less probable.

We address the questions mentioned above by characterizing the fitness landscape in terms of the overlaps
Q
and
R
. Let

\displaystyle\alpha_{\ast}:=3-2\sqrt{2}\approx 0.172.

(1.4)

We arrange our results in this subsection according to the value of the epistasis parameter
\alpha
, starting from
\alpha>\alpha_{*}
, the high-epistasis regime.
Our first result below shows that the epistasis overlap
Q_{1,2}
of two independently sample genomes undergoes a phase transition at
\beta_{c}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume
\alpha_{*}<\alpha\leq 1
and recall that
\beta_{c}=\sqrt{2\ln 2}
. For any
\beta\leq\beta_{c}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle I(Q_{1,2}=0)\bigr\rangle_{\beta}=1,

(1.5)

and for any
\beta>\beta_{c}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle I(Q_{1,2}=0)\bigr\rangle_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{c}}{\beta},

(1.6)

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle I(Q_{1,2}=1)\bigr\rangle_{\beta}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\beta_{c}}{\beta}.

(1.7)

This theorem states that at weaker strength of natural selection (
\beta<\beta_{c}
), two independently sampled genomes from the Gibbs measure are always
completely decoupled
, i.e., a mismatch exists in any segment of length
K+1
; whereas at higher strength of natural selection (
\beta>\beta_{c}
), they will either align perfectly with each other or are completely decoupled.

Next, we establishes the so-called
overlap gap property
in the sense that the overlaps between genomes whose fitness exceed a certain level are forbidden to charge values in some sub-intervals. To this end, for any nonempty set
S\subset\Sigma_{N}\times\Sigma_{N}
, we define the coupled maximum fitness constrained by
S
as

\displaystyle\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K}(S)

\displaystyle:=\max_{(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})\in S}\Bigl(\frac{H_{N,K}(\sigma^{1})}{N}+\frac{H_{N,K}(\sigma^{2})}{N}\Bigr).

(1.8)

The following result provides quantitative bounds for the level of fitness and the overlaps of the genomes in the high-epistasis regime.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume
\alpha_{*}<\alpha\leq 1.

The follow statements hold.

1.

We have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K}(\{0<Q_{1,2}<1\})\\
&\leq 2\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}M_{N,K}-2\beta_{c}\Bigl(1-\frac{3-\alpha}{2\sqrt{2}}\Bigr).\end{split}

(1.9)

2.

For any
\delta\in(0,1),

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K}(\{\delta<|R_{1,2}|<1\})\\
&\leq 2\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}M_{N,K}-2\beta_{c}\min\Bigl(1-\sqrt{\frac{h(\delta)}{2}},1-\frac{3-\alpha}{2\sqrt{2}}\Bigr),\end{split}

(1.10)

where for
u\in[0,1],

\displaystyle h(u)

\displaystyle:=1-\frac{1+u}{2}\log_{2}\frac{1+u}{2}-\frac{1-u}{2}\log_{2}\frac{1-u}{2}

(1.11)

is a strictly decreasing function with
h(0)=2
and
h(1)=1.

Note that the maximum fitness
M_{N,K}
and
\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K}(S)
here are all concentrated with high probability (see Lemma
2.6
below) so that the above results are essentially valid without taking the expectations, where an additional error on which can be made as small as possible will be introduced to the above inequalities.
For the epistasis overlap
Q
, noting that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}M_{N,K}=\beta_{c},
we readily see from (
1.9
) that with high probability, if
\sigma^{1}\neq\sigma^{2}
are two genomes with fitness at least
N\beta_{c}(3-\alpha)/(2\sqrt{2}),
then they must be completely decoupled, i.e.,
Q_{1,2}=0
. The inequality (
1.10
) quantifies how the overlap
R_{1,2}
changes with the fitness level on the landscape: for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, if
\sigma^{1}\neq\sigma^{2}
are two genomes with fitness at least
NE(\delta)
, where

\displaystyle E(\delta):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}M_{N,K}-\beta_{c}\min\Bigl(1-\sqrt{\frac{h(\delta)}{2}},1-\frac{3-\alpha}{2\sqrt{2}}\Bigr),

(1.12)

then their overlap must satisfy
|R_{1,2}|\leq\delta
, and thus, their distance is in between
\sqrt{2(1-\delta)}
and
\sqrt{2(1+\delta)}
.
Consequently, this
implies that whenever
\eta,\delta\in(0,1)
with
\eta<(1-\delta)/2
, it is not possible to exist an evolutionary path, along which the genome in each generation maintains fitness level at last
E(\delta)
, in the meantime, any two consecutive generations have no more than
\eta N
mutations since, in this case, their overlaps will exceed
1-2\eta>\delta.
In particular,
as
\delta\to 0
,
E(\delta)
becomes closer to the optimal fitness and an evolution path consisting of near-fittest genomes must involve more than
\eta N\gtrapprox N/2
mutations in at least one generation, which is biologically improbable.
See Figure
1
(a) for the structure of the near-fittest genomes when
\delta\approx 0
and Figure
2
(Left) for how the fitness level
E(\delta)
changes with
\delta
for a few
\alpha
values in the high-epistasis regime
(\alpha_{\ast},1]
.

. In (a), the genomes are approximately mutually orthogonal so that their distances are almost

As a corollary, when
\alpha>\alpha_{*}
, we get a stronger result than Theorem
1.2
, for which the set
S_{N}(\varepsilon)
can be taken as the level set,
\mathcal{L}_{N}(\varepsilon):=\{\sigma:H_{N,K}(\sigma)/N\geq M_{N,K}-\varepsilon\}

for

, and its cardinality can be described more explicitly.

Corollary 1.1
.

Assume
\alpha_{*}<\alpha\leq 1
. For any
0<\delta<1
, consider the level set
\mathcal{L}_{N}(\varepsilon)
with

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\frac{\beta_{c}}{7}\min\Bigl(1-\sqrt{\frac{h(\delta)}{2}},1-\frac{3-\alpha}{2\sqrt{2}}\Bigr).

(1.13)

Then, for any
\gamma>0
, with probability at least
1-Ce^{-N/C}
for some
C=C(\delta,\gamma)
,
\mathcal{L}_{N}(\varepsilon)
satisfies the following

1.

While we investigate the

1.

Let

. We anticipate that similar approach can be used to understand the limiting free energy, but the study for the overlaps requires new ideas.

. Does Chatterjee’s result

]
elaborated in Remark

3.

Suppose

Our formulation of the

Moment Method

In this section, we will employ the second moment approach to computing the limiting free energy in the
N\!K
model. Our result below shows that it can be explicitly obtained at any temperature if
\alpha\in[\alpha_{*},1]
. However, when
0<\alpha<\alpha_{*}
, it can only be achieved in part of the high temperature regime depending on
\alpha.

Theorem 2.1
.

The following limits hold.

(i)

If
\alpha_{*}\leq\alpha\leq 1,
then

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}F_{N,K}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ln 2+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2},&\forall\beta<\beta_{c},\\
\\
\beta\beta_{c},&\forall\beta\geq\beta_{c}.\end{array}\right.

(ii)

If
0<\alpha<\alpha_{*}
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}F_{N,K}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\ln 2+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2},\,\,\forall 0<\beta<\sqrt{\ln 2}(1+\sqrt{\alpha}).

For the rest of this section, we establish this theorem. For any
s\in\mathbb{R},
define the cardinality of the level set as

\displaystyle L_{N}(s)

\displaystyle=\bigl|\bigl\{\sigma:H_{N,K}(\sigma)\geq sN\bigr\}\bigr|.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}L_{N}(s)

\displaystyle=2^{N}\mathbb{P}(H_{N,K}(\mathbbm{1})\geq sN)=2^{N}\mathbb{P}(z\geq s\sqrt{N})=2^{N}\Phi(-s\sqrt{N}),

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal random variable.
To control the second moment, write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}L_{N}(s)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{P}\bigl(H_{N,K}(\sigma^{1})\geq sN,H_{N,K}(\sigma^{2})\geq sN\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\bigl|\{(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2}):Q(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})=l/N\}\bigr|\mathbb{P}\bigl(X_{1}(l/N)\geq s\sqrt{N},X_{2}(l/N)\geq s\sqrt{N}\bigr),

where for any
t\in[0,1]
,
(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t))
is a bivariate centered normal vector with
\mathbb{E}X_{1}(t)^{2}=\mathbb{E}X_{2}(t)^{2}=1
and
\mathbb{E}X_{1}(t)X_{2}(t)=t.
Consider the epistasis overlap of any genome with
\mathbbm{1}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
, defined as

\displaystyle Q(\sigma):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}I(\sigma_{j}=1,\,\,\forall i\leq j\leq i+K).

(2.1)

Observe that for any fixed
\sigma^{1},
the mapping
\sigma^{2}\mapsto(\sigma_{0}^{1}\sigma_{0}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{N-1}^{1}\sigma_{N-1}^{2})

is a bijection so that

\bigl|\{\sigma^{2}:Q(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})=t\}\bigr|=\bigl|\{\sigma:Q(\sigma)=t\}\bigr|

and thus,

\displaystyle\bigl|\{(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2}):Q(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})=t\}\bigr|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma^{1}}\big|\{\sigma^{2}:Q(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})=t\}\bigr|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}\bigl|\{\sigma:Q(\sigma)=t\}\bigr|=2^{N}\bigl|\{\sigma:Q(\sigma)=t\}\bigr|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}L_{N}(s)^{2}

\displaystyle=2^{N}\sum_{l=0}^{N}\bigl|\{\sigma:Q(\sigma)=l/N\}\bigr|\mathbb{P}\bigl(X_{1}(l/N)\geq s\sqrt{N},X_{2}(l/N)\geq s\sqrt{N}\bigr).

(2.2)

The next subsection establishes the bounds on the two quantities on the right-hand side of (
2.2
).

Lemma 2.1
.

For any

\displaystyle Q(\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N-K-1}{N}.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that

(K+1)
many 1’s, starting from
\sigma_{i}
. Thus for each

(K+1)

\displaystyle\bigl|\{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}:NQ(\sigma)=l\}\bigr|

3.1
N\!K
Model with
p
-spin Interaction

Let
p
be a fixed even number. For any
N\geq K\geq 1,
the
N\!K
model with
p
-spin interaction is defined as the Gaussian process
H_{N,K}^{p}
with covariance

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}H_{N,K}^{p}(\sigma^{1})H_{N,K}^{p}(\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\bigl(R_{N,K,i}(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})\bigr)^{p}

for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{N},

where

R_{N,K,i}(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2}):=\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{j=0}^{K}\sigma_{i+j}^{1}\sigma_{i+j}^{2}

and as the original
N\!K
model, the spins live on a periodic ring of length
N.
Set the free energy as

F_{N,K}^{p}(\beta)=\frac{1}{N}\ln\sum_{\sigma}e^{\beta H_{N,K}^{p}(\sigma)}.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1]
. From now on, let
K=\lfloor\alpha(N-1)\rfloor
as in the original
N\!K
model. If
\alpha=1,
let
n=k=1.
Let
1\leq k\leq n<\infty
so that

\alpha\leq k/n.

Take
l=\lceil N/n\rceil.
Set
N_{l}=nl
and
K_{l}=kl
. The lemma below establishes a continuity of the free energy in the
p
-spin
N\!K
model in the ratio
K/N
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
\beta
and
p
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\bigl|\mathbb{E}F_{N,K}^{p}(\beta)-\mathbb{E}F_{N_{l},K_{l}}^{p}(\beta)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(k/n-\alpha)}{\alpha}.

Proof.

We adapt an interpolation argument to establish our proof.
Note that
N_{l}\geq N
and
K_{l}\geq K.
For any
0\leq t\leq 1,
define

\displaystyle F(t)=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\ln\sum_{(\sigma,\rho)\in\Sigma_{N}\times\Sigma_{N_{l}-N}}\exp\beta\bigl({\sqrt{t}H_{N,K}^{p}(\sigma)+\sqrt{1-t}H_{N_{l},K_{l}}^{p}(\sigma,\rho)}\bigr).

(3.1)

Denote by
(\sigma^{1},\rho^{1})
and
(\sigma^{2},\rho^{2})
independent samples from the Gibbs measure associated to this free energy and by
\langle\cdot\rangle_{t}
the corresponding Gibbs expectation.
Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(0)&=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\ln\sum_{\tau\in\Sigma_{N_{l}}}\exp\beta H_{N_{l},K_{l}}^{p}(\tau),\\
F(1)&=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\ln\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\exp{\beta H_{N,K}^{p}(\sigma)}+\frac{N_{l}-N}{N}\ln 2.\end{split}

(3.2)

Now, using the Gaussian integration by parts yields

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\Bigl\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{l}-1}R_{N_{l},K_{l},i}((\sigma^{1},\rho^{1}),(\sigma^{2},\rho^{2}))^{p}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\bigl(R_{N,K,i}(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})\bigr)^{p}\Bigr\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\Bigl\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Bigl(R_{N_{l},K_{l},i}((\sigma^{1},\rho^{1}),(\sigma^{2},\rho^{2}))^{p}-\bigl(R_{N,K,i}(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})\bigr)^{p}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=N}^{N_{l}-1}R_{N_{l},K_{l},i}((\sigma^{1},\rho^{1}),(\sigma^{2},\rho^{2}))^{p}\Bigr\rangle_{t}.

(3.3)

Here, observe that for all
0\leq i\leq N-1,
the spins appearing in the overlaps
R_{N_{l},K_{l},i}((\sigma^{1},\rho^{1}),(\sigma^{2},\rho^{2}))
and
R_{N,K,i}(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})
are different from each other by at most
N_{l}-N+K_{l}-K
many locations, it follows that there exists a constant
C
independent of
i
such that

\displaystyle\Bigl|R_{N_{l},K_{l},i}((\sigma^{1},\rho^{1}),(\sigma^{2},\rho^{2}))-R_{N,K,i}(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(N_{l}-N+K_{l}-K)}{K}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\bigl|F^{\prime}(t)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq C\beta^{2}p\frac{N_{l}-N+K_{l}-K}{K}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\frac{N_{l}-N}{N}.

Since this inequality holds for all
t\in(0,1),
by taking integral and noting (
3.2
), our assertion follows.

Fix an even integer

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}H_{{\text{\tiny MS}},l}^{p}(\sigma^{1})H_{{\text{\tiny MS}},l}^{p}(\sigma^{2})=N\xi\bigl(R_{{\text{\tiny MS}},0}(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2}),\ldots,R_{{\text{\tiny MS}},n-1}(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})\bigr),

35

Proposition 3.1
.

We have that for any
\beta>0,

\lim_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}F_{{\text{\tiny MS}},l}^{p}(\beta)

exists.

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to\infty}\mathbb{E}F_{{\text{\tiny MS}},l}^{p}(\beta)

\displaystyle\beta_{p}^{2}=\inf_{0<u<1}(1+u^{-p})I(u)

\xi

\displaystyle F_{{\text{\tiny MS}},\infty}^{p}(\beta)=\ln 2+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2},\,\,\forall 0<\beta<\beta_{p}.

\displaystyle F_{{\text{\tiny MS}},\infty}^{p}(\beta)\geq F_{{\text{\tiny MS}},\infty}^{p}(\beta_{p})+\frac{dF_{{\text{\tiny MS}},\infty}^{p}}{d\beta}(\beta_{p})(\beta-\beta_{p})=\ln 2+\beta_{p}\beta-\frac{\beta_{p}^{2}}{2},\,\,\forall\beta>\beta_{p},

(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})

Z_{N,K,s}^{1}(\beta)\times Z_{N,K,s}^{2}(\beta).
Define
\phi_{\beta}(s)=\mathbb{E}\langle Q(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})\rangle_{\beta,s}

for
s\geq 0.
By applying
[
10
, Lemma 10.3]
to
v\geq 0\mapsto\phi_{\beta}(e^{-v})
, we have that for any
0\leq v_{0}\leq v_{1}<\infty,

\displaystyle\phi_{\beta}(e^{-v_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{\beta}(1)^{1-\frac{v_{0}}{v_{1}}}\phi_{\beta}(e^{-v_{0}})^{\frac{v_{0}}{v_{1}}}.

From this inequality, taking
v_{0}=-\ln s_{0}
and
v_{1}=-\ln{s_{1}}
and then sending
\beta\to\infty
complete our proof.

\sqcap
\sqcup

Now we turn to the proof of Theorem
1.2
. Fix an arbitrary
0<\varepsilon<1
.
Fix
0<s_{0}<1
such that

\displaystyle\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{0}}}-1+\sqrt{\frac{1-s_{0}}{s_{0}}}\Bigr)(\sqrt{2\ln 2}+1)<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

(5.1)

Note that from Lemmas
2.6
and
2.7
, there exists a positive constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(M_{N,K}\leq(\sqrt{2\ln 2}+1),|M_{N,K}-\mathbb{E}M_{N,K}|\leq 4^{-1}\varepsilon\bigr)\geq 1-Ce^{-N/C}.

(5.2)

Next, for any
S\subset[0,1]
and
s\in[0,1]
, define

\displaystyle\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K,s}(S)

\displaystyle=\max_{(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})\in S}\Bigl(\frac{H_{N,K}^{1}(\sigma^{1})}{N}+\frac{H_{N,K}^{2}(\sigma^{2})}{N}\Bigr).

Note that from (
4.4
)
and

\mathbb{E}(H_{N,K,s}^{1}(\sigma^{1})+H_{N,K,s}^{2}(\sigma^{2}))^{2}=2N(1+ts),

Lemma
2.7
implies that for any
t=i/N
with
1\leq i\leq N-K,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K,s}(\{Q_{1,2}=t\})\leq 2\sqrt{(1+ts)(2-\alpha-t+\epsilon_{N})\ln 2},

(5.3)

where
\epsilon_{N}=(2+\ln N)/N.

To control the right-hand side, notice that there exist some
0<s_{1}<s_{0}
small enough so that as long as
N
is large enough, for any
t\in(0,1-\alpha]
,

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{(1+ts_{1})(2-\alpha-t+\varepsilon_{N})\ln 2}\leq 2\sqrt{(1+s_{1})(2-\alpha/2)\ln 2}<2\sqrt{2\ln 2}.

Consequently, similar to the proofs of (
1.6
) and (
1.7
),

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K,s_{1}}(\{0<Q_{1,2}<1\})<2\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}M_{N,K}.

(5.4)

Also, from Lemma
2.7
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K,s_{1}}(\{Q_{1,2}=1\})\leq 2\sqrt{(1+s_{1})\ln 2}<2\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}M_{N,K}.

Putting these two together yields

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K,s_{1}}(\{Q_{1,2}>0\})<2\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}M_{N,K}.

(5.5)

Since
\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K,s_{1}}(\{Q_{1,2}>0\})
and
M_{N,K}
are concentrated and
M_{N,K},M_{N,K,s_{1}}^{1},M_{N,K,s_{1}}^{2}
are identically distributed, there exist some
\eta,C^{\prime}>0
such that with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}e^{-N/C^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K,s_{1}}(\{Q_{1,2}>0\})\leq M_{N,K,s_{1}}^{1}+M_{N,K,s_{1}}^{2}-\eta.

Therefore, with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}e^{-N/C^{\prime}},

Q(\sigma^{s_{1},1},\sigma^{s_{1},2})=0,
where
\sigma^{s_{1},1}
and
\sigma^{s_{1},2}
are the fittest genomes of
H_{N,K,s_{1}}^{1}
and
H_{N,K,s_{1}}^{2}
respectively.
Consequently, from Lemma
5.1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Q(\sigma^{s_{0},1},\sigma^{s_{0},2})

\displaystyle\leq(C^{\prime}e^{-N/C^{\prime}})^{\frac{\ln s_{0}}{\ln s_{1}}}.

It follows by the Markov inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q(\sigma^{s_{0},1},\sigma^{s_{0},2})>0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Q(\sigma^{s_{0},1},\sigma^{s_{0},2})\geq N^{-1})\leq N(C^{\prime}e^{-N/C^{\prime}})^{\frac{\ln s_{1}}{\ln s_{0}}}.

(5.6)

Now, similar to (
4.11
),

\displaystyle\max_{|r|\geq\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K,s_{0}}(\{Q_{1,2}=0,R_{1,2}=r\})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{h(\varepsilon)\ln 2}<2\sqrt{2\ln 2}.

Hence, since
\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K,s_{0}}(\{Q_{1,2}=0,R_{1,2}=r\})
is concentrated, there exist positive constants
\eta^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
so that with probability at least
1-C^{\prime\prime}e^{-N/C^{\prime\prime}},

\displaystyle\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptM\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,K,s_{0}}(\{Q_{1,2}=0,|R_{1,2}|\geq\varepsilon\})<M_{N,K,s_{0}}^{1}+M_{N,K,s_{0}}^{2}-\eta^{\prime}.

In particular, this and (
5.6
) readily imply that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q(\sigma^{s_{0},1},\sigma^{s_{0},2})=0,|R(\sigma^{s_{0},1},\sigma^{s_{0},2})|<\varepsilon)\geq 1-\Delta_{N},

(5.7)

where

\Delta_{N}:=N(C^{\prime}e^{-N/C^{\prime}})^{\frac{\ln s_{1}}{\ln s_{0}}}+C^{\prime\prime}e^{-N/C^{\prime\prime}}.

Finally, denote by
(\sigma^{s_{0},\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
a sequence of fittest genomes associated to
H_{N,K,s_{0}}^{\ell}
, defined in a similar manner as
H_{N,K,s_{0}}^{1}
and
H_{N,K,s_{0}}^{2}.
Let
S_{N}(\varepsilon)
be the collection of all
\sigma^{s_{0},\ell}
for

1\leq\ell\leq\min\bigl\{\Delta_{N}^{-1/4},(4Ce^{N/C})^{1/2}\bigr\}=:L_{N}.

From (
5.2
) and (
5.7
), with probability at least

1-\Delta_{N}|S_{N}(\varepsilon)|^{2}-4Ce^{-N/C}|S_{N}(\varepsilon)|\geq 1-\sqrt{\Delta_{N}}-\sqrt{4Ce^{N/C}},

we have that for any two distinct
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in S_{N}(\varepsilon),

\displaystyle Q(\sigma^{s_{0},\ell},\sigma^{s_{0},\ell^{\prime}})=0\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,|R(\sigma^{s_{0},\ell},\sigma^{s_{0},\ell^{\prime}})|<\varepsilon.

and that for any
\ell\in S_{N}(\varepsilon),

\displaystyle\frac{H_{N,K}({\sigma^{s_{0},\ell}})}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{H_{N,K,s_{0}}^{\ell}(\sigma^{s_{0},\ell})}{N}+\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{0}}}-1\Bigr)\frac{H_{N,K,s_{0}}^{\ell}(\sigma^{s_{0},\ell})}{N}-\frac{\sqrt{1-s_{0}}}{\sqrt{s_{0}}}\frac{H_{N,K}^{\ell}(\sigma^{s_{0},\ell})}{N}

\displaystyle\geq M_{N,K,s_{0}}^{\ell}-\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{0}}}-1+\frac{\sqrt{1-s_{0}}}{\sqrt{s_{0}}}\Bigr)(\sqrt{2\ln 2}+1)

\displaystyle\geq M_{N,K}-\varepsilon,

where (
5.1
) was used in the last inequality. Noting that
L_{N}
is of exponential order completes our proof.

6
Existence of Near-Fittest Paths

This final section is dedicated to establishing the existence of the near-fittest paths, i.e., Theorem
1.6
.
First of all, note that although we focus on the
N\!K
model where
N
and
K
satisfy the relation
K=\lfloor\alpha(N-1)\rfloor
, it can still be defined in the same way as before for any given integers
N,K\geq 1
without this relation. In particular, whenever
K\geq N-1
, this model is the same as the REM. The following lemma establishes the monotonicity of
\mathbb{E}M_{N,K}
in
K
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For any
N\geq 1
and
1\leq K_{1}\leq K_{2}
we have that
\mathbb{E}M_{N,K_{1}}\leq\mathbb{E}M_{N,K_{2}}.

Proof.

Observe that obviously, for any
\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2}\in\Sigma_{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}H_{N,K_{1}}(\sigma^{1})H_{N,K_{1}}(\sigma^{2})\geq\mathbb{E}H_{N,K_{2}}(\sigma^{1})H_{N,K_{2}}(\sigma^{2})

and the two sides equal each other when
\sigma^{1}=\sigma^{2}
. By Slepian’s lemma, our assertion holds.

\sqcap
\sqcup

Recall the disorders
X_{i}(\sigma_{i},\ldots,\sigma_{i+K})
in
H_{N,K}.
For any
N_{1},N_{2}\geq 1
with
N_{1}+N_{2}=N,
write

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{N_{1}-1})\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,\rho=(\rho_{0},\ldots,\rho_{N_{2}-1}).

Set

\displaystyle\begin{split}V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau)&=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{1}-K-1}X_{i}(\tau_{i},\ldots,\tau_{i+K})\,\,\mbox{if $N_{1}\geq K+1$ and $V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau)=0$ if $N_{1}<K+1$},\\
V_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho)&=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{2}-K-1}X_{N_{1}+i}(\rho_{i},\ldots,\rho_{i+K})\,\,\mbox{if $N_{2}\geq K+1$ and $V_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho)=0$ if $N_{2}<K+1$}.\end{split}

(6.1)

Note that these functions are not the Hamiltonians for the
N\!K
models under our setting since their spins do not have the cyclic structures. From now on, we let
K=\lfloor\alpha(N-1)\rfloor.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\eta,\alpha\in(0,1)
satisfy (
1.22
).
There exists some
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
depending on
\alpha
and
\eta
such that for any
c\in(0,1)
and
N,N_{1},N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying

\displaystyle N_{1}+N_{2}=N\geq N_{0}\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,\min\Bigl(\frac{N_{1}}{N},\frac{N_{2}}{N}\Bigr)\geq c,

(6.2)

we have that with probability at least
1-\omega e^{-\eta^{2}c^{2}N/\omega},
whenever
\sigma_{*}\in\Sigma_{N}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{H_{N,K}(\sigma^{*})}{N}\geq M_{N,K}-\eta,

(6.3)

the following inequality holds

\displaystyle\min\Bigl(\frac{V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau^{*})}{N_{1}},\frac{V_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho^{*})}{N_{2}}\Bigr)\geq M_{N,K}-\frac{4\eta}{c},

(6.4)

where
\tau^{*}:=(\sigma_{0}^{*},\ldots,\sigma_{N_{1}-1}^{*})
and
\rho^{*}:=(\sigma_{N_{1}}^{*},\ldots,\sigma_{N-1}^{*}).
Here,
\omega>0
is an absolute constant independent of all other variables.

Proof.

We divide our proof into two cases.

Case I:
N_{1}\geq K+1
and
N_{2}\geq K+1
.

For any
\sigma\in\Sigma_{N},
write

\displaystyle H_{N,K}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau)+E_{N}^{1}(\sigma_{N_{1}-K},\ldots,\sigma_{N_{1}+K-1})

\displaystyle+V_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho)+E_{N}^{2}(\sigma_{N-K},\ldots,\sigma_{N+K-1}),

where we set

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tau&=(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{N_{1}-1})=(\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{N_{1}-1}),\\
\rho&=(\rho_{0},\ldots,\rho_{N_{2}-1})=(\sigma_{N_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{N-1}),\end{split}

(6.5)

and

\displaystyle E_{N}^{1}(\sigma_{N_{1}-K},\ldots,\sigma_{N_{1}+K-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=N_{1}-K}^{N_{1}-1}X_{i}(\sigma_{i},\ldots,\sigma_{i+K}),

\displaystyle E_{N}^{2}(\sigma_{N-K},\ldots,\sigma_{N+K-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=N-K}^{N-1}X_{i}(\sigma_{i},\ldots,\sigma_{i+K}).

Let
{H}_{N_{1},K}^{1}
and
{H}_{N_{2},K}^{2}
be the Hamiltonians for the
N\!K
models associated to the parameter pairs
(N_{1},K)
and
(N_{2},K)
, respectively. Then for any
\tau\in\Sigma_{N_{1}}
and
\rho\in\Sigma_{N_{2}}
, we can write them as

\displaystyle{H}_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau)+\tilde{E}_{N}^{1}(\tau_{N_{1}-K},\ldots,\tau_{N_{1}+K-1}),

\displaystyle{H}_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho)

\displaystyle=V_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho)+\tilde{E}_{N}^{2}(\rho_{N_{2}-K},\ldots,\rho_{N_{2}+K-1}),

where

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{N}^{1}(\tau_{N_{1}-K},\ldots,\tau_{N_{1}+K-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=N_{1}-K}^{N_{\ell}-1}\tilde{X}_{i}^{1}(\tau_{i},\ldots,\tau_{i+K}),

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{N}^{2}(\rho_{N_{2}-K},\ldots,\rho_{N_{2}+K-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=N_{2}-K}^{N_{2}-1}\tilde{X}_{i}^{2}(\rho_{i},\ldots,\rho_{i+K})

for i.i.d. standard normal random variables
\tilde{X}_{i}^{\ell}
,
\ell=1,2
, which are independent of all other randomness. Let

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N}

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma}\Bigl|\frac{H_{N,K}(\sigma)}{N}-\frac{{V}_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau)}{N}-\frac{{V}_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho)}{N}\Bigr|,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N}^{1}

\displaystyle=\max_{\tau}\Bigl|\frac{{V}_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau)}{N_{1}}-\frac{{H}_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau)}{N_{1}}\Bigr|,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N}^{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{\rho}\Bigl|\frac{{V}_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho)}{N_{2}}-\frac{{H}_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho)}{N_{2}}\Bigr|.

Using Lemma
2.7
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\max_{x\in\Sigma_{2K}}|E_{N}^{1}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{2K-1})|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\max_{x\in\Sigma_{2K}}|E_{N}^{2}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{2K-1})|\leq\frac{2K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{N}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\max_{x\in\Sigma_{2K}}|\tilde{E}_{N}^{\ell}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{2K-1})|\leq\frac{K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N_{\ell}},\,\,\ell=1,2.

Note that similar to Lemma
2.6
, the extremal processes here are all concentrated with a Gaussian-tailed bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\Lambda_{N}-\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{N}|\geq t)

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{-\frac{N^{2}t^{2}}{8K}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\Lambda_{N}^{\ell}-\mathbb{E}\Lambda_{N}^{\ell}|\geq t)

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{-\frac{N_{\ell}^{2}t^{2}}{4K}},\,\,\ell=1,2.

It follows that with probability at least

\displaystyle 1-2e^{-N^{2}t^{2}/8K}-2e^{-N_{1}^{2}t^{2}/4K}-2e^{-N_{2}^{2}t^{2}/4K},

(6.6)

the following inequalities hold simultaneously,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N}

\displaystyle\leq t+\frac{2K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N},

(6.7)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq t+\frac{K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N_{\ell}},\,\,\ell=1,2.

(6.8)

Next, note that for
\ell=1,2
,

0<\alpha\leq\alpha N/N_{\ell}
.
Since the expected maximal fitness in the
N\!K
model is nondecreasing in the parameter
K
, by Lemma
6.1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{M}_{N_{\ell},K_{\ell}}^{\ell}\leq\mathbb{E}{M}_{N_{\ell},K}^{\ell}\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2},

where
K_{\ell}:=\lfloor\alpha(N_{\ell}-1)\rfloor
, and
M_{N_{\ell},K_{\ell}}^{\ell}
and
M_{N_{\ell},K}^{\ell}
are the optimal fitnesses of the
N\!K
models associated with the parameter pairs
(N_{\ell},K_{\ell})
and
(N_{\ell},K)
, respectively. Since
\mathbb{E}M_{N_{\ell},K_{\ell}}^{\ell}\to\sqrt{2\ln 2}
by Theorem
1.1
,
there exists some
n_{0}\geq 1
depending on
\eta
and
\alpha
such that whenever
N\geq n_{0}
and
N_{1},N_{2}
satisfy the second inequality in (
6.2
), we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\ln 2}-\eta\leq\mathbb{E}M_{N_{\ell},K}^{\ell}\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2},

(6.9)

which combining with Lemma
2.6
implies that
with probability at least

\displaystyle 1-2e^{-Nt^{2}/4}-2e^{-N_{1}t^{2}/4}-2e^{-N_{2}t^{2}/4},

(6.10)

the following inequality holds

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\ln 2}-\eta-t\leq M_{N,K},{M}_{N_{1},K}^{1},{M}_{N_{2},K}^{2}\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2}+t.

(6.11)

To establish our proof, let
E_{N}
be the event on which (
6.7
), (
6.8
), and (
6.11
) hold simultaneously. Assume that on the event

, the inequality (

\displaystyle\frac{V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau_{*})}{N_{1}}\leq M_{N,K}-\frac{4\eta}{c},

then from (
6.8
) and (

\displaystyle\frac{{H}_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau_{*})}{N_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2}-\frac{4\eta}{c}+2t+\frac{K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N_{1}}.

On the other hand, from (
6.3
) and (
6.11

\displaystyle\frac{H_{N,K}(\sigma_{*})}{N}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{2\ln 2}-2\eta-t,\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{H_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho_{*})}{N_{2}}\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2}+t.

These together with (
6.7
) and (
6.8
) imply that

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\ln 2}-2\eta-t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{H_{N,K}(\sigma_{*})}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N_{1}}{N}\frac{{H}_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau_{*})}{N_{1}}+\frac{N_{2}}{N}\frac{{H}_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho_{*})}{N_{2}}+3t+\frac{4K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N_{1}}{N}\Bigl(\sqrt{2\ln 2}-\frac{4\eta}{c}+2t+\frac{K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N_{1}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\frac{N_{2}}{N}\bigl(\sqrt{2\ln 2}+t\bigr)+3t+\frac{4K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2}-4\eta+6t+\frac{5K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2}-4\eta+6t+5\sqrt{2\ln 2}\alpha,

where the 4th inequality used the condition (
6.2
).
Due to (
1.22
), if we take

, this inequality can not hold.
Our proof is then completed by noting that from (
6.6
) and (
6.10
), the probability of
E_{N}
is at least
1-\omega e^{-Nc^{2}\eta^{2}/\omega}
for some absolute constant
\omega>0
.

Case II: Either
N_{1}<K+1
or
N_{2}<K+1
.
Assume that
N_{1}<K+1
, then
V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau^{*})=0
and

is at most
N_{1}/N\leq\alpha
. Due to Lemma
2.6
and Theorem
1.1

On the other hand, since
N_{2}=N-N_{1}>K

6.8
) with
\ell=2
and (
6.11
), letting
t=\sqrt{2\ln 2}
, as long as
N

\displaystyle\frac{V_{N_{2},K}(\rho^{*})}{N_{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{2\ln 2}-2t-\frac{K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N_{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{2\ln 2}-3t-\frac{\alpha\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{1-\alpha}\geq-3\sqrt{2\ln 2}.

Putting these together yields that on the event where the above inequalities hold,

\displaystyle\min\Bigl(\frac{V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\tau^{*})}{N_{1}},\frac{V_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\rho^{*})}{N_{2}}\Bigr)\geq-3\sqrt{2\ln 2}>-17\sqrt{2\ln 2}\geq M_{N,K}-\frac{4\eta}{c}.

\sqcap

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Fix
0<\eta<1
and
0<\alpha<1
that satisfy (
1.22
). Let
n\geq 10
be fixed. Denote
k=\lfloor N/(n+1)\rfloor.
Recall the genomes
\sigma^{(0)},\sigma^{(1)},\ldots,\sigma^{(n)}
defined in (
1.1.3
).
Let
I_{0},\ldots,I_{n-1}
be a partition of
\{0,1,2,\ldots,N-1\}
with

I_{l}=\{lk,\,lk+1,\,\ldots,\,lk+k-1\}

for
l=0,\ldots,n-2

and

I_{n-1}=\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}\setminus\bigl(\cup_{l=0}^{n-2}I_{l}\bigr).

Note that
|I_{l}|=k
for
0\leq l\leq n-2
and
k\leq|I_{n-1}|<2k.
For
0\leq l\leq n-1
, write
I_{\leq l}=\cup_{r=0}^{l}I_{r}
and for
0\leq l\leq n-2
, write

By definition, for any
0\leq l\leq n-1,

\sigma^{(l+1)}
and
\sigma^{(l)}
could differ only at the loci in the set
I_{l}
, which readily yields that

\displaystyle Q(\sigma^{(l+1)},\sigma^{(l)})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{N-(|I_{l}|+2K)}{N}\geq 1-\frac{2}{n+1}-2\alpha

and

\displaystyle R(\sigma^{(l+1)},\sigma^{(l)})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{N-2|I_{l}|}{N}\geq 1-\frac{4}{n+1}.

Next, we show that if
\hat{\sigma}
and
\check{\sigma}
are near-fittest genomes satisfying (
1.23
), then the
\sigma^{(l)}
’s satisfy (
1.25
). Fix
1\leq l\leq n-1.
Let
N_{1}=|I_{\leq l-1}|=kl
and
N_{2}=|I_{>l}|=N-N_{1}.
Note that for
N
large,

\displaystyle\min\Bigl(\frac{N_{1}}{N},\frac{N_{2}}{N}\Bigr)\geq\frac{k}{N}\geq\frac{1}{N}\Bigl(\frac{N}{n+1}-1\Bigr)=\frac{1}{n+1}-\frac{1}{N}\geq\frac{1}{2(n+1)}=:c.

Recall
V_{N_{1},K}^{1}
and
V_{N_{2},K}^{2}
from (
6.1
). Similarly to (
6.5
), write
\hat{\sigma}=(\hat{\tau}^{(l)},\hat{\rho}^{(l)})
and
\check{\sigma}=(\check{\tau}^{(l)},\check{\rho}^{(l)})
. From Lemma
6.2
, there exists some
N_{0}
depending only on
\alpha,\eta
such that for any
N\geq N_{0}
, with probability at least
1-2\omega e^{-\eta^{2}c^{2}N/\omega}
, if both
\hat{\sigma}
and
\check{\sigma}
have fitnesses at least
M_{N,K}-\eta
,
then

\displaystyle\min\Bigl(\frac{V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\hat{\tau}^{(l)})}{N_{1}},\frac{V_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\hat{\rho}^{(l)})}{N_{2}}\Bigr)\geq M_{N,K}-\frac{4\eta}{c},

\displaystyle\min\Bigl(\frac{V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\check{\tau}^{(l)})}{N_{1}},\frac{V_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\check{\rho}^{(l)})}{N_{2}}\Bigr)\geq M_{N,K}-\frac{4\eta}{c}.

Let
E_{N,l}
be the event on which this statement holds.
Note that
\sigma^{(\ell)}=(\check{\rho}^{(l)},\hat{\tau}^{(l)}).
From the same argument as (
6.7
), it can be shown that with probability at least
1-2e^{-N^{2}\eta^{2}/8K}\geq 1-2e^{-N\eta^{2}/8},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\Bigl|H_{N,K}(\sigma^{(l)})-V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\check{\tau}^{(l)})-V_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\hat{\rho}^{(l)})\Big|\leq\eta+\frac{2K\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{N}\leq\eta+2\alpha\sqrt{2\ln 2}.

Denote by
\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptE\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,l}
the event on which this inequality holds.
It follows that on
E_{N,l}\cap\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptE\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,l},
if (
1.23
) holds, then

\displaystyle\frac{H_{N,K}(\sigma^{(l)})}{N}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{V_{N_{1},K}^{1}(\check{\tau}^{(l)})}{N}+\frac{V_{N_{2},K}^{2}(\hat{\rho}^{(l)})}{N}-\eta-2\alpha\sqrt{2\ln 2}

\displaystyle\geq M_{N,K}-\frac{4\eta}{c}-\eta-2\alpha\sqrt{2\ln 2}

\displaystyle\geq M_{N,K}-(8n+10)\eta,

(6.12)

where we used
c=(2(n+1))^{-1}
and (
1.22
).
Finally, let
E_{N}=\cap_{l=0}^{n-2}(E_{N,l}\cap\,\overline{\kern-1.99997ptE\kern-1.00006pt}_{N,l})
. Then as long as
N\geq N_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{N})

\displaystyle\geq 1-2n\omega e^{-\eta^{2}c^{2}N/\omega}-2ne^{-N\eta^{2}/8}

and on
E_{N},
if (
1.23
) holds, then from (
6.12
),

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq\ell\leq n-1}\frac{H_{N,K}(\sigma^{(l)})}{N}\geq M_{N,K}-(8n+10)\eta.
