Convergence of Clipped SGD on Convex ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -Smooth Functions
Ofir Gaash, Kfir Yehuda Levy, Yair Carmon

Abstract
We study stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with gradient clipping on convex functions under a generalized smoothness assumption called ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -smoothness. Using gradient clipping, we establish a high probability convergence rate that matches the SGD rate in the L L smooth case up to polylogarithmic factors and additive terms. We also propose a variation of adaptive SGD with gradient clipping, which achieves the same guarantee. We perform empirical experiments to examine our theory and algorithmic choices.

Introduction

Gradient clipping is a common method for stabilizing neural network training. Despite its wide use, little thought is given to the choice of the clipping
threshold
, with many works fixing it at 1 without attempting to tune it
[
3
,
5
,
34
,
35
,
2
,
20
,
21
]
. In pursuit of a theory-driven threshold choice, recent research tries to better understand the benefits of gradient clipping.

Experiments suggest that clipping is effective in situations where small changes in input can lead to significant variations in gradient norms
[
43
,
42
]
. This observation has led
Zhang et al. [43]
to formally define this behavior as the following “relaxed” smoothness property.

Definition 1
.

A twice-differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth if for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds that
\|\nabla^{2}f(x)\|\leq L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x)\|
.

In words,
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness allows the Hessian norm to increase linearly in the gradient norm. This is opposed to the traditional smoothness property, which says that the Hessian norm is bounded by a constant, coinciding with the new definition for
L_{1}=0
. Building on this definition,
Zhang et al. [43]
show instances where clipped SGD outperforms standard SGD. Specifically, they denote
M=\sup\left\{\|\nabla f(x)\|\;|\;f(x)<f(x_{0})\right\}
, and show that the complexity of SGD with a fixed stepsize is larger than the complexity of SGD with gradient clipping by a factor of
L_{1}M
(assuming both algorithms are initialized at
x_{0}
). This factor can be very large: in particular, it may be exponential in
L_{1}R_{0}
, where
R_{0}
is the initial distance from the optimum (see
Appendix

G
).

The connection between
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness and gradient clipping has led researchers to attempt to characterize the complexity of
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth optimization, mainly in terms of the dependence on
L_{1}
. The non-convex setting is well understood: the state-of-the-art rate of gradient norm convergence comprises of the rate for
L_{0}
-smooth functions and additional low-order terms that depend on
L_{1}
. This holds for both clipped GD
[
38
]
and clipped SGD
[
37
]
(see
Section

2
for details).

Motivation for studying the convex setting.

Despite being originally motivated by the behavior of (non-convex) neural networks, generalized smoothness is also compelling to study in the convex setting. There are several instances where convex analysis closely aligns with empirical behavior; among them are AdaGrad-based algorithms and the momentum technique, in which theory preceded practice
[
29
,
7
,
32
]
. Furthermore, a body of work show that neural networks have convex-like behavior
[
14
,
45
,
22
]
. From a theoretical perspective, it allows to examine whether the pattern observed for gradient norm convergence—an
L_{0}
-smooth rate with low-order
L_{1}
-dependent terms—also holds for
optimality gap
convergence.

These considerations have motivated recent work to study the convex,
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth setting
[
15
,
18
,
11
,
38
,
37
,
24
]
. For clipped GD,
[
11
,
38
,
37
,
24
]
prove an optimality gap convergence rate following the aforementioned pattern. However, prior work does not provide a corresponding result in the stochastic setting (we discuss a concurrent work by
Lobanov and Gasnikov [23]
in
Section

2
). The difficulty of the stochastic, convex regime stems from the fact that clipping biases stochastic gradients. Bias is arguably more challenging in the convex setting than in the non-convex setting: the former intimately relies on stochastic gradients being unbiased, while for the latter it suffices to average enough stochastic gradient such that the noise drops below the required degree of stationarity
[
15
]
. This is potentially the reason previous studies of the convex setting
[
18
,
15
]
proved convergence only in the deterministic regime.

Our contribution.

In this work, we analyze gradient clipping in the convex, stochastic,
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth setting with light-tailed noise. Our contributions are as follows.

•

We show that the pattern of an
L_{0}
-smooth convergence rate with
L_{1}
-dependent low-order terms extends to the stochastic convex setting. For clipped SGD with
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian gradient noise, we prove a sub-optimality bound of
O\left(\frac{\log\left({T}/{\delta}\right)L_{0}R_{0}^{2}}{T}+\frac{\log^{2}\left({T}/{\delta}\right)\sigma R_{0}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)
that holds with probability at least
1-\delta
for
T=\Omega(\log\left(\frac{T}{\delta}\right)L_{1}^{2}R_{0}^{2})
. That is, we bound the stochastic gradient query complexity of achieving optimality gap
\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
by
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{L_{0}R^{2}}{\epsilon}+\frac{\sigma^{2}R_{0}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}+(L_{1}R_{0})^{2}\right)
, where
\widetilde{O}(\cdot)
hides factors poly-logarithmic in
\frac{1}{\delta\epsilon}
. This matches the best known bounds for (fixed step-size) SGD in the
L_{0}
-smooth case, which are
\Omega\left(\frac{L_{0}R^{2}}{\epsilon}+\frac{\sigma^{2}R_{0}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

[
16
]
. Our bound depends on
L_{1}
only through an additive term, with no implicit dependence on
\exp\left(L_{1}R_{0}\right)
.

•

We show the same complexity bound for two variations of SGD: adaptive SGD
[
25
]
with clipping, and SGD with a variable stepsize which we refer to as “implicit clipping.”

•

We show that, to achieve the bounds above, precise knowledge of the parameter
L_{1}
is not required: simply replacing
L_{1}
by the conservative choice
\widetilde{O}(T^{1/2}/R_{0})
suffices. This result does not appear in papers studying the deterministic regime.

•

We perform numerical experiments that demonstrate the benefits of gradient clipping for SGD on convex,
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions, and assess the empirical effect of some of our algorithmic choices.

For technical reasons, we employ a
double sampling
technique that uses two independent stochastic gradient samples in each update: one to estimate the direction of the update, and another to estimate its magnitude. This technique is necessary in order to apply some of the probabilistic tools we use, which assume sequences of unbiased random variables, but our empirical analysis suggests it might not be helpful in practice. We elaborate on this technique in the analysis as well as in the experiments.

The paper organization is as follows. In
Section

2
we survey related work. In
Section

3
we outline our algorithmic framework and prove our theoretic results. In
Section

4
we describe our experiments and discuss their results. In
Section

5
we provide a short conclusion.

Gradient clipping for

-smooth functions.

The non-convex regime was the first to be explored.

Koloskova et al. [15]

Vankov et al. [38]

O\left(\sqrt{{L_{0}\Delta}/{T}}+{L_{1}\Delta}/{T}\right)

Zhang et al. [43]

\sigma

Zhang et al. [43]

Li et al. [18]

Koloskova et al. [15]
consider
\sigma
-bounded gradient noise variance.
Li et al. [18]
prove a rate that has a dependency on the initial gradient norm, and
Koloskova et al. [15]
show an unavoidable bias term when considering all clipping thresholds at once.
Tyurin [37]
consider light-tailed noise, and using a batch size of
O\left(\sigma^{2}T^{2}\right)
, obtain a rate of
O\left({L_{1}\Delta}/{T}+\sqrt{{L_{0}\Delta}/{T}}\right)
.

The convex regime recently received much attention.
Koloskova et al. [15]
,
Li et al. [18]
prove a convergence rate for clipped GD where the dominating term is
O\left({(L_{0}+ML_{1})R_{0}^{2}}/{T}\right)

L_{1}R_{0}
.
Gorbunov et al. [11]
,
Vankov et al. [38]
independently prove a convergence rate of

with additive factors of

and
\min\left\{L_{1}^{2}R_{0}^{2},L_{1}R_{0}\log(\Delta T)\right\}
, respectively.
Lobanov et al. [24]
show that in some initial phase of the algorithm, clipped GD enjoys linear convergence.
Gorbunov et al. [11]
,
Vankov et al. [38]
both propose acceleration methods; The former has an exponential dependency on
R_{0}
, and the latter requires to solve a one-dimensional optimization problem in each iteration.

In the stochastic convex case,
Gorbunov et al. [11]
consider finite-sum functions, with an additional assumption that all the functions share a common minimizer. They show a convergence rate of
O\left({L_{0}R_{0}^{2}}/{T}\right)
in expectation for
T=\Omega\left(nL_{1}^{2}R_{0}^{2}\right)
, where
n
is the number of functions. In concurrent and independent work,
Lobanov and Gasnikov [23]
present a general framework and study both first- and zero-order methods. Assuming bounded noise variance, they obtain bounds for arbitrary clipping thresholds, and prove linear convergence in some special cases. However, their rate of convergence depends on
M
and
\Delta
, both of which may be exponential in
L_{1}R_{0}
.

To the best of our knowledge, our work provides the first rate of convergence for stochastic, convex,
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth optimization without exponential dependence on
L_{1}R_{0}
, and with a leading-order term independent of
L_{1}
. For the case of
\sigma=0
, our results match the state-of-the-art rate from the deterministic regime up to a logarithmic factor.

Adaptive SGD for
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions.

Faw et al. [8]

consider adaptive SGD for non-convex functions with an affine variance assumption. In the context of bounded variance, their rates translate to

Claim 1

1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}\leq 2\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{T}{\delta}\right)R_{0}^{2}-4\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{T}{\delta}\right)R_{0}^{2}\frac{|\mathcal{T}_{1}|}{T}.

This implies
|\mathcal{T}_{1}|\leq\frac{T}{2}
and therefore
|\mathcal{T}_{2}|\geq\frac{T}{2}
. In particular, this shows that the output of the algorithm is
\bar{x}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{2}|}\sum_{t\in\tau_{2}}x_{t}
.

In unclipped iterations we have
\|g_{t}^{c}\|\leq c
. The norm
\|g_{t}^{c}\|
appears only in the
denominator
of
\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}
, so we can bound
\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}
from below by substituting
\|g_{t}^{c}\|
with
c
. Thus, we show that
\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\geq\frac{1}{16}\left(11L_{0}+\tfrac{\sigma\sqrt{T}}{R_{0}}\right)^{-1}:=\gamma
(see details in
Lemma

7
). By this we have

\displaystyle\gamma\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}\Delta_{t}\leq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}\leq 2\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{T}{\delta}\right)R_{0}^{2}.

Dividing by
\gamma|\mathcal{T}_{2}|
and using the bound on
|\mathcal{T}_{2}|
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{2}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}\Delta_{t}\leq\frac{64\left(11L_{0}+\tfrac{\sigma\sqrt{T}}{R_{0}}\right)\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{T}{\delta}\right)R_{0}^{2}}{T}.

Using Jensen’s inequality completes the proof.

Proof sketch of
Claim

2

(full proof is in
Lemmas

6
and
7
).
Consider iterations where
t\in\mathcal{T}_{1}
, that is, iterations where
\|g_{t}^{c}\|>c
: By our choice of threshold we have
c\geq 6\sigma
. Therefore, for the sample to be above the threshold, the gradient norm must dominate over the noise, implying
\|g_{t}^{c}\|\approx\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|
. A known property of
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions is that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\leq 2\left(L_{0}+\|\nabla f(x)\|L_{1}\right)\left(f(x)-f(x^{\star})\right).

Substituting
x=x_{t}
, using our choice of
\alpha_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
, and substituting
\|g_{t}^{c}\|\approx\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|
, we get

\displaystyle\|g_{t}^{c}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(L_{0}+\|g_{t}^{c}\|L_{1}\right)\Delta_{t}\leq(2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t})^{-1}\Delta_{t}.

Multiplying by
2(\eta_{t}\alpha_{t})^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}\geq 2\left(\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\|g_{t}^{c}\|\right)^{2}.

Clipping implies

\displaystyle\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\|g_{t}^{c}\|\overset{(i)}{\approx}\eta_{t}c\overset{(ii)}{\geq}2\sqrt{\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{T}{\delta}\right)}\frac{R_{0}}{\sqrt{T}},

where
(i)
is an equality in the case
\alpha_{t}=\min\left\{1,\frac{c}{\|g_{t}^{c}\|}\right\}
and
(ii)
is due to the choice of
\eta_{t}
and
c
. Combining the last two inequalities, we get the bound
\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}\geq 8\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{T}{\delta}\right)\frac{R_{0}^{2}}{T}
.

Proof sketch of
Claim

1

(full proof is in
Lemma

8
). We split the signal from the noise by expressing the sum
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\langle\nabla f(x_{t}),x_{t}-x^{\star}\rangle
as

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\langle g_{t},x_{t}-x^{\star}\rangle}_{S_{1}}+\underbrace{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\langle\nabla f(x_{t})-g_{t},x_{t}-x^{\star}\rangle}_{S_{2}}.

To bound
S_{2}
, we use techniques from
Attia and Koren [1]
. The random variables
\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}
and
g_{t}
are independent conditionally on
x_{t}
due to the double sampling, and therefore the elements of
S_{2}
form a
martingale difference sequence
w.r.t.
\xi_{t}=(x_{t},g_{t}^{c})
. This allows us to bound
S_{2}
using a martingale concentration bound and standard analysis. We get that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle S_{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{8}+\frac{5}{16}\log\left(\frac{T}{\delta}\right)\right)R_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.

To bound
S_{1}
, we use standard analysis and show that

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R_{0}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.

By the convexity of
f
and the above displays we find that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\langle\nabla f(x_{t}),x_{t}-x^{\star}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq S_{1}+S_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{T}{\delta}\right)R_{0}^{2}+\frac{3}{4}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.

To bound
\eta_{t}^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}
, we consider the cases of high noise and low noise: when
\|g_{t}\|\geq 6\sigma
, like in clipped iterations, we have
\|g_{t}\|\approx\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|
and therefore
\eta_{t}^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}
. when
\|g_{t}\|\leq 6\sigma
, we use
\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\leq\frac{R_{0}}{8\sigma\sqrt{T}}
and get
\eta_{t}^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\leq\frac{R_{0}^{2}}{T}
.

Plugging everything in and rearranging, we have that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}\leq 2\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{T}{\delta}\right)R_{0}^{2}.

Obtaining the result for light-tailed noise.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an unbiased gradient oracle with
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian noise.
Attia and Koren [1, Appendix A]
show that there exists an unbiased gradient oracle
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
with
3\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{T}{\delta}\right)}\sigma
-bounded noise that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, has the exact same output as
\mathcal{G}
throughout the entire algorithm. Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have the same guarantee as when assuming
3\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{T}{\delta}\right)}\sigma
-bounded noise. By using a union bound, we get that the desired guarantee holds under
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian noise with probability at least
1-2\delta
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we analyze stochastic gradient descent with gradient clipping on convex,
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions. We prove a high-probability convergence rate for clipped SGD, and introduce a clipped variation of adaptive SGD that has a similar rate.

There are various possible directions for future work. First, since the double-sampling approach is not supported by empirical data, it is interesting to study convergence without it. Another direction is extending our analysis to a more generalized smoothness assumption such as
\ell
-smoothness
[
18
]
. Lastly, exploring tuning-free methods that require no knowledge on problem parameters could be of both theoretical and practical interest.

(L_{0},L_{1})
-Smooth Functions

Lemma 1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and suppose
Assumption

2
holds. Then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\leq 2\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x)\|\right)\left(f(x)-f(x^{\star})\right).

Koloskova et al. [15, Lemma A.2]
prove this by simply using
Zhang et al. [42, Lemma A.3]
, which assumes the
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness definition we use.

Lemma 2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and suppose
Assumption

2
holds. Then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq\max\left\{3L_{1}(f(x)-f(x^{\star})),6\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}}\right\}.

Proof.

Denote
\Delta:=f(x)-f(x^{\star})
.
Lemma

1
shows that

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}-2L_{1}\Delta\|\nabla f(x)\|-2L_{0}\Delta\leq 0.

This is a quadratic inequality in
\|\nabla f(x)\|
. Since
L_{0},L_{1},\Delta
and
\|\nabla f(x)\|
are non-negative, the solution is less than the parabola’s largest root. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(2L_{1}\Delta+\sqrt{4L_{1}^{2}\Delta^{2}+8L_{0}\Delta}\right)=L_{1}\Delta+\sqrt{L_{1}^{2}\Delta^{2}+2L_{0}\Delta}\leq 2L_{1}\Delta+\sqrt{2L_{0}\Delta}.

If
L_{1}^{2}\Delta^{2}\geq 2L_{0}\Delta
then we get

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq 2L_{1}\Delta+\sqrt{L_{1}^{2}\Delta^{2}}=3L_{1}\Delta.

If
L_{1}^{2}\Delta^{2}<2L_{0}\Delta
: Without loss of generality, we assume
\Delta>0
(since for
\Delta=0
the result is immediate from
Lemma

1
), and therefore
\Delta<2L_{0}/L_{1}^{2}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq 2\sqrt{2L_{0}\Delta}+\sqrt{2L_{0}\Delta}=3\sqrt{2L_{0}\Delta}\leq 3\sqrt{4L_{0}^{2}/L_{1}^{2}}=6L_{0}/L_{1}.

Overall, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|\leq\max\left\{3L_{1}\Delta,6\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}}\right\}

∎

Lemma 3
.

.

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}Z_{t}\leq\frac{3}{4}\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}~.

To handle the light tails assumption, we use a reduction from sub-Gaussian noise to bounded noise presented by

Lemma 5
.

The value of
\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}
is always smaller under “standard clipping” than under “implicit clipping.” Additionally, if

Proof.

First, let us compare conservative clipping and standard clipping: The definition of
\eta_{t}
is equivalent in both methods. Due to the assumption on
T

c
is smaller in conservative clipping than in standard clipping. This immediately leads to the stated relationship regarding
\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}

Now let us compare standard clipping and implicit clipping: For the rest of the proof, let
\eta_{t}
,
\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle\eta_{t}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{11L_{0}},\frac{1}{L_{0}+\frac{\sigma\sqrt{T}}{R_{0}}}\right\}=\frac{1}{L_{0}+\max\left\{10L_{0},\frac{\sigma\sqrt{T}}{R_{0}}\right\}}=\frac{1}{L_{0}+cL_{1}}

Consider the case of unclipped iterations, which satisfy

∎

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\leq 4\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}.

The two cases imply that every iteration satisfies
\alpha_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\leq 4\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}+\left(6\sigma\right)^{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{16}\sigma R\sqrt{T}+4\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}+2R\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\left(4\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}+\left(6\sigma\right)^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{16}\sigma R\sqrt{T}+4\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}+2R\sqrt{4\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}+\left(6\sigma\right)^{2}T}

The rest of the proof is pure algebra, which we show separately in the subsequent lemma (
Lemma

13
).
∎

Lemma 13
.

If

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{16}\sigma R\sqrt{T}+4\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}+2R\sqrt{4\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}+\left(6\sigma\right)^{2}T},

then

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 25\left(15L_{0}R^{2}+\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right).

Proof.

Denote
A:=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}
. Then we have

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{16}\sigma R\sqrt{T}+4\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}+2R\sqrt{4\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)A+\left(6\sigma\right)^{2}T}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\left(\frac{3}{16}\sigma R\sqrt{T}+4\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)^{2}}+2R\sqrt{4\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)A+\left(6\sigma\right)^{2}T}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\left(\frac{3}{16}\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)^{2}+2\left(4\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)^{2}}+2R\sqrt{4\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)A+\left(6\sigma\right)^{2}T}.

Squaring both sides leads to

\displaystyle A^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sqrt{2\left(\tfrac{3}{16}\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)^{2}+2\left(4\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)^{2}}+2R\sqrt{4\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)A+\left(6\sigma\right)^{2}T}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\left(\tfrac{3}{16}\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)^{2}+2\left(4\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)^{2}\right)+2\cdot 4R^{2}\left(4\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)A+\left(6\sigma\right)^{2}T\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9}{64}\left(\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)^{2}+4\left(4\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)^{2}+32R^{2}(L_{0}+cL_{1})A+8\cdot 36R^{2}\sigma^{2}T

\displaystyle=32R^{2}(L_{0}+cL_{1})A+\left(289+64\log^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)\left(\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)^{2}.

This is a quadratic inequality (in
A
) of the form
A^{2}\leq bA+c
(
b,c\geq 0
). In such cases, every solution is less than or equal to the largest root of
A^{2}-bA-c
. Therefore,

\displaystyle A\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\left(b+\sqrt{b^{2}+4c}\right)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\left(b+\sqrt{b^{2}}+\sqrt{4c}\right)\leq b+\sqrt{c}.

In our context, this leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 32\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)R^{2}+\sqrt{289+64\log^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)}\cdot\sigma R\sqrt{T}

\displaystyle\leq 32\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)R^{2}+\left(17+8\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)\cdot\sigma R\sqrt{T}

\displaystyle\leq 32\left(L_{0}+cL_{1}\right)R^{2}+9\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}

From the assumption on
c
, we have

\displaystyle cL_{1}R^{2}\leq\frac{1}{L_{1}}\max\left\{10L_{0},\frac{\sqrt{T}}{R}\sigma\right\}\cdot L_{1}R^{2}\leq 10L_{0}R^{2}+\sigma R\sqrt{T}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T}\alpha_{t}\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 32\left(L_{0}R^{2}+10L_{0}R^{2}+\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right)+9\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}

\displaystyle\leq 352L_{0}R^{2}+25\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}

\displaystyle\leq 25\left(15L_{0}R^{2}+\log_{+}\left(\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)\sigma R\sqrt{T}\right).

∎

Appendix G
The potentially exponential dependence of
\|\nabla f(x)\|
and
f(x)-f(x^{\star})
on
L_{1}\|x-x^{\star}\|

Let
L_{0},L_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and let
f(x)=\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}^{2}}\cosh(L_{1}x)
. We will show that
f
is
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth. The first and second derivatives are

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}}\sinh(L_{1}x)\;;\;f^{\prime\prime}(x)=L_{0}\cosh(L_{1}x).

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, by basic properties of
\cosh
and
\sinh
, it holds that

\displaystyle|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|=L_{0}\cosh(L_{1}x)=L_{0}\left(e^{-L_{1}x}+\sinh(L_{1}x)\right)=L_{0}e^{-L_{1}x}+L_{1}f^{\prime}(x).

When
x\geq 0
we have
e^{-L_{1}x}\leq 1
and
f^{\prime}(x)=|f^{\prime}(x)|
. Therefore,

\displaystyle|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq L_{0}+L_{1}|f^{\prime}(x)|.

When
x<0
we have
e^{-L_{1}(-x)}\leq 1
and
f^{\prime}(-x)=|f^{\prime}(x)|
. Therefore,

\displaystyle|f^{\prime\prime}(-x)|\leq L_{0}+L_{1}|f^{\prime}(x)|,

and since
f^{\prime\prime}
is an even function, the same holds for
|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|
. In both cases we end up with the inequality that defines
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness.

Let us discuss, in the context of the above
f
, quantities that appear in bounds of related work. Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. The minimizer of
f
is
x^{\star}=0
, therefore
\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|=|x_{0}|
. The gradient norm at
x=x_{0}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|=\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}}|\sinh(L_{1}x_{0})|=\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}}\sinh(L_{1}|x_{0}|)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L_{0}}{2L_{1}}\left(\exp(L_{1}|x_{0}|)-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{0}}{2L_{1}}\left(\exp(L_{1}\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|)-1\right).

Additionally, the sub-optimality at
x=x_{0}
satisfies

\displaystyle f(x_{0})-f(x^{\star})=\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}^{2}}\left(\cosh(L_{1}x_{0})-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}^{2}}\left(\cosh(L_{1}|x_{0}|)-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L_{0}}{2L_{1}^{2}}\left(\exp(L_{1}|x_{0}|)-2\right)=\frac{L_{0}}{L_{1}^{2}}\left(\exp(L_{1}\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|)-2\right).

Both quantities demonstrate an exponential dependence on
L_{1}\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|
.

H.1
Implementation details of real-data experiments

Data and computational resources.

Our experiments use the California Housing dataset
[
28
]
and the Parkinsons Telemonitoring dataset
[
36
]
, which are published under “CC0” and “CC-BY 4.0” licenses, respectively. All experiments provided in this paper were run on Google Colab (with a free account) using an NVIDIA T4 GPU.

Data preprocessing.

The data preparation begins by obtaining the dataset
(X,y)
, where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
represents
n
samples with
d
features per sample, and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents the targets. Missing data in numerical features is replaced with the mean value, and missing data in categorical features is replaced with the most frequent value. Numeric features, as well as the targets, are standardized to have zero mean and unit variance, and categorical features are encoded as one-hot vectors. The samples are then shuffled. Finally, a column
\vec{\mathbf{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
is prepended to
X
in order to have a bias term in the regression task.

Stepsize and clipping threshold tuning.

We determine the clipping threshold
c
of each method by tuning it, avoiding reliance on theoretical quantities from the definitions in
Table

1
. Similarly, we modify the parameter
\eta_{t}
by replacing theoretical quantities with some tunable variable, which we denote as
lr
. For methods with a fixed stepsize, we simply set
\eta=lr
. For methods based on Adaptive SGD we set
\eta_{t}=lr\cdot(\sum_{i=0}^{t}\alpha_{i}^{2}\|g_{i}\|^{2})^{-1/2}
, and for “implicit clipping” we set
\eta_{t}=lr\cdot c/\left(c+\|g_{t}^{c}\|\right)
(see Section 3.1 on
Zhang et al. [43]
for intuition).

We tune
lr
and
c
by performing a two-level, two-dimensional grid search. In the first-level grid, the values are geometrically spaced by a factor of 10: The values for
c
are
(10^{2},\ldots,10^{7})
. The values for
lr
are
(10^{-10},\ldots,10^{-5})
for SGD,
(10^{-7},\ldots,10^{-2})
for clipped SGD, and
(10^{-3},\ldots,10^{2})
for both Adaptive SGD and clipped Adaptive SGD. We verify that the best candidate is never at the edge of the grid. Denoting the best candidate as
(lr^{1},c^{1})
, the second-level grid is defined as
\left\{(lr,c)\mid lr\in(\frac{1}{4}lr^{1},\frac{1}{2}lr^{1},lr^{1},2lr^{1},4lr^{1}),c\in(\frac{1}{4}c^{1},\frac{1}{2}c^{1},c^{1},2c^{1},4c^{1})\right\}
