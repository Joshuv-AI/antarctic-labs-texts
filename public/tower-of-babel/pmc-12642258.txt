Motivations, satisfaction, and career aspirations of undergraduate medical students in West Bank, Palestine: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mahdi W Suboh, Mones H Atatre, Bessan Hamed Dababseh, Ala’a S Ghnimat, Lubna W AbuHamdiya, Mahmoud S Alhaddad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelecting a profession in medicine is a major decision shaped by institutional, societal, and personal factors. In Palestine, medical students face unique challenges due to limited local training opportunities, political instability, and socioeconomic constraints. Understanding the motivations driving these students, their satisfaction with their choice, and their future goals is essential for guiding medical education and workforce planning. This study investigates the motivations, satisfaction levels, and career plans of Palestinian undergraduate medical students, situating findings within motivational theories such as Self-Determination Theory.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 635 medical students from five universities in West Bank. Data were collected using a validated questionnaire that included demographic information, motivations for studying medicine, satisfaction levels (measured on a Likert scale), and future career preferences. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences), employing descriptive statistics and chi-square tests for multivariate analysis.ResultsOf the 635 participants, 66.9% were females, with a mean age of 20.61 years. Medicine was the first career choice for 81.4% of students, and 96.5% expressed a desire to specialize after obtaining their Doctor of Medicine (MD). The most cited motivation was the desire to make a positive impact on patients’ lives (79.1%), followed by an interest in health-related professions (74.4%). A majority (73.7%) aspired to specialize abroad, and 70% of them preferred to return to work in Palestine post-specialization. Satisfaction with studying medicine was high, with only 5.8% expressing regretion over their choice.ConclusionsPalestinian medical students demonstrate strong intrinsic motivation, particularly a commitment to patient care and community service. Despite systemic challenges, the high levels of satisfaction with their career choice, coupled with aspirations to specialize abroad and return home, highlight both opportunities and risks for workforce planning. Policy implications include establishing partnerships with international training institutions, offering government incentives for returning specialists, and expanding local residency programs to reduce dependency on external training opportunities. These findings also carry broader relevance when compared with students in other conflict-affected regions, emphasizing the need for targeted educational and workforce strategies in resource-constrained settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08230-3.Keywords: Undergraduate medical students, Motivation, Career choice, Specialization, Satisfaction, Workforce planning.

Introduction

Career motivations in medicine vary significantly among students worldwide, influenced by a combination of personal, social, and institutional factors [1].Among the key drivers are the potential financial benefits, the prestige and social standing associated with the medical profession, which attract many students to this field [1]. Additionally, opportunities for specialization and career progression play an important role in shaping students’ aspirations [1]. However, institutional challenges such as unclear specialist training pathways, delayed medical internship placements, excessive workloads, and low pay, can negatively affect students’ intentions to pursue certain specialties or continue practicing in their home countries [1].

Consequently, many medical graduates from low- and middle-income countries plan to migrate to more industrialized nations that offer higher incomes and stricter medical practice requirements, contributing to a brain drain that undermines the ability of their countries to provide quality healthcare [2].

In the Palestinian context, the ongoing Israeli-Palestinian conflict, deteriorating economic conditions, movement restrictions, and the gap between the reality of occupation and the aspiration for an independent State create a stressful environment for both the population and healthcare providers [3]. These political and structural barriers not only negatively impact health outcomes in the Occupied Palestinian Territories but also limit opportunities for medical students to receive adequate clinical education and training [4]. Within this challenging context, the Palestinian healthcare system presents additional structural difficulties for medical students, including limited exposure to subspecialty programs [5]. and insufficient integrated research opportunities within the curriculum [6]. Together, these obstacles influence students’ career planning and may drive some to pursue training opportunities abroad [7].

Medical students are exposed to various specialties during their training, such as surgery, pediatrics, and internal medicine. It is often believed that students finalize their specialty choices after graduation; however, many candidates and current students already hold strong preferences or biases toward particular medical fields [8]. While some students find the process of choosing a specialty stressful and confusing, others perceive it as straightforward [9]. The quality of healthcare delivery in every country, making diversity in specialization critical [10]. Factors influencing specialty choice include family and peer advice, admiration for mentors, and genuine personal interest, lifestyle preferences, opportunities for private practice, interest in particular diseases, a desire for a diverse scope of practice, involvement in research and teaching, or the potential for higher earnings [9].

Gender also plays an important role [11]. Male students often gravitate toward surgical and technical fields due to factors like income and prestige [12].While on the other hand, female students tend to focus on specialties with opportunities for relational aspects and more contact with patient [13].

Academic motivation refers to the psychological factors that drive students’ desire to learn and their capacity to effectively interact with and respond to environmental challenges in order to achieve their educational goals [14]. Academic motivation among medical students can be classified as intrinsic or extrinsic. Intrinsic motivation encompasses social and intellectual fulfillment derived from patient care and collaboration with colleagues, as well as the personal gratification from acquiring new knowledge and skills [14]. In contrast, extrinsic motivation arises from external factors such as the high social status, job security, and financial rewards associated with a medical career [14].

Upon reviewing the literature, several regional studies have explored medical students’ satisfaction, motivation, and career plans. In Jordan, financial security was identified as a key factor influencing specialty choice [8], while in Saudi Arabia, mentorship was found to play a significant role [15]. Similarly, in Egypt, lifestyle considerations strongly shaped. However, no previous studies have examined these factors among Palestinian medical college students. Therefore, this study aims to assess the motivations and satisfaction levels of undergraduate Palestinian medical students in choosing medicine as a career, as well as their future career plans.

Method

Study design

A cross-sectional study design was carried out at the Colleges of Medicine and Health Sciences, Palestine Polytechnic University, An-Najah National University, Al-Quds University, Hebron University, Arab American University.

Questionnaire development

The questionnaire was adapted from a validated tool used in prior research and was modified for this study [16–19].Face and content validation were conducted by an expert from the College of Medicine at Palestine Polytechnic University, For face validation, it was shown to medical students and who responded smoothly and understood it, Content validation was performed by the expert and the content validity index was assessed using a 4-point scale.

The pilot study was in English to assess whether the questionnaire was understandable or not, 25 students was participating in it, the responses from the pilot study were not included in the analysis of the main study. The reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82).

The initial version of the questionnaire was piloted before full distribution. An English language version of the questionnaire is attached as Supplementary File 1.

Strengths and limitations

One important limitation of this study is the potential for selection bias due to the use of convenience sampling and online survey distribution. Students who were more interested in clinical research may have been more likely to participate, which may have led to an overestimation of awareness levels. This limits the external validity of the study and may restrict the generalizability of our findings to the broader population of medical students in West Bank.

Questionnaire structure

The questionnaire was divided into four sections. The first section was concerned with respondents’ demographics, such as gender, university name, year of study, and Grade Point Average (GPA). Section two was enquired about students’ reasons for choosing to study medicine. The third section was concerned with students’ satisfaction regarding their choice of medicine as a major. The final section was asked about students’ future career plans. In this section, students were given a list of possible specialization paths for Medical Doctors and asked to choose one. Since students were allowed to select only one option, the total percentage of students’ choices could sometimes sum to 100%.

Study population

All medical students at the Colleges of Medicine, Palestine Polytechnic University, An-Najah National University, Al-Quds University, Hebron University, Arab American University, were eligible to participate in this study, All students were enrolled in the same 6-year medical program (MD program), the exclusion criteria for preventing participation was: Non medical student, student from outside the West Bank and student not willing to participate.

Data collection technique

A group of 5th-year medical students were recruited as data collectors. The responses were collected by distributing the questionnaire through social media platforms which were sent directly to medical students on a personal basis from October 17, 2024 to December 4, 2024 in academic year 2024–2025.This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Palestine Polytechnic University) Ref No: EA/2025/14(. Ethical approval for the study was obtained prior to data collection. All participants provided informed consent electronically. A brief description of the study and its purpose was written on the coverpage of the questionnaire, and it was explained that their participation in the study would be voluntary and that all data would remain confidential. The questionnaire generally took less than 5 min to complete.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 22.0 was used to code, input, and analyze the survey data. Chi-square and Fisher Exact tests were utilized to determine the relationship between the students’ demographic profiles and their responses to the three main study sections, while frequencies and percentages were employed to characterize the findings. These tests were chosen because they are appropriate for comparing two independent categorical variables. Multivariate analysis was not performed as the primary objective was to explore bivariate associations rather than to adjust for confounding or build predictive models. P-values were regarded as significant if they were less than 0.05.

Discussion

This study illuminates the motivations, satisfaction, and career aspirations of West Bank medical students. Given the specific and complex framework of medical education and healthcare in Palestine, especially in light of the dire humanitarian circumstances in the Gaza Strip, understanding these dimensions becomes essential. The profound impact of intrinsic motivation on students’ career choices is evident, with a substantial majority of participants (81.4%) indicating that medicine was their first choice. The most frequently cited reasons for this choice were altruistic and intrinsic: the desire to make a positive impact on patients’ lives (79.1%), a genuine interest in working in a health-related profession (74.3%), and the career aspiration to become a physician (63.1%). This group’s motivation demonstrates a strong feeling of commitment and is consistent with modern theories of motivation that highlight internal motivators such individual passion and the belief that medicine is a noble profession. In particular, these intrinsic motivations stemming from a desire to help others and a personal interest in the field are consistent with the core concepts of the Self-Determination Theory, which holds that people are motivated by their innate desires for relatedness, competence, and autonomy [20, 21].

Even while internal causes predominate, outside stimuli can have an impact. 11.7% of students cited family influence as a deciding factor in their career choice, and chi-square analysis showed a significant correlation between this factor and whether the decision to pursue a medical degree was influenced by family or personal reasons (p = 0.009). Similarly, a student’s interest in a health-related vocation (p = 0.000) and their desire to become a physician (p = 0.000) were substantially correlated with their first or second choice for medicine. These results demonstrate the nuanced relationship between personal drive and external encouragement.

While only a small proportion of medical students in the West Bank (6.8%) identified financial aid and scholarships as an influencing factor in their decision to pursue medicine, this low percentage likely reflects the scarcity of such resources within the region. Nevertheless, broader financial considerations appear to exert a significant influence on career choices, a finding supported by the substantial 73.7% of students who plan to pursue specialization outside of Palestine. This strong preference for international specialization can be attributed to a combination of factors, including the scarcity of specialized training programs locally, the possibility of earning more money overseas, and the desire for better job opportunities in more developed healthcare systems. The unstable political environment and geopolitical barriers are also key contributing factors that affect career choices and academic opportunities, and their impact is reflected in the high percentage of Palestinian students seeking specialization abroad. This trend aligns with a similar study in Syria, where 78.7% of students intended to specialize abroad [22],. and contrasts sharply with a Turkish study, which found this number to be only 29.7% [23]. This striking disparity highlights how regional political, economic, and educational contexts affect career choices and emphasizes the challenges faced by the Palestinian healthcare industry in retaining its talent [4].

Despite this, the majority (70.4%) of students expressed a preference to return to the West Bank to work after their specialization. This dual tendency demonstrates a strong feeling of national duty as well as an intention to support the local healthcare system following advanced education and expertise.

Conversely, the least common motivator mentioned was the influence of teachers or mentors, cited by only 20.2% of participants. This contrasts with a Namibian study where 52.94% of students were influenced by a specific instructor [24].

In the context of the West Bank’s medical education system, this disparity might suggest that students’ career decisions are more strongly influenced by personal interests and altruistic motives than by direct mentorship. owing in part to the curriculum’s emphasis, the way academic advising is organized, and the high student-to-faculty ratio, all of which limit the chances for students to form meaningful and lasting connections with their mentors. This view aligns with the results of a study on research methods among Palestinian medical students, which found that inadequate faculty assistance and a lack of mentorship were major obstacles [25].

According to modern motivation theories on career choice, which place an emphasis on the importance of internal drivers, this high degree of commitment is probably the result of students’ intrinsic motivation and their perception of medicine as a noble profession [21].

Additionally, only 15.7% of students reported regretting their decision to enroll in medical school, a stark contrast to a study conducted in Jordan, where 76.3% of respondents had such regrets [18]. This discrepancy is likely explained by differences in the academic stages of the student groups: while most participants in the Jordanian study (72.4%) were in their clinical years a stage associated with a heavier workload and elevated stress, our participants were primarily in the basic science phase.

Regarding medical specialization: Surgery (16.1%) and Internal Medicine (6.8%) were the most popular medical specializations among students, consistent with data from a study conducted in Jordan [8]. Students’ interest in these fields may be influenced by the desire to pursue them, the prospect of higher income, or the presence of role models [26]. Conversely, forensic medicine and academic specializations had the lowest selection rates at 1.3% and 0.8%, respectively. These findings are similar to those reported in studies conducted in Saudi Arabia and India, indicating a consistent trend across the region [27, 28].

Notably, our study found that 35.1% of medical students had not yet decided on their future specialty. This proportion is lower than that reported in an international study conducted in 2024, which included medical students from several Middle Eastern countries and found that 42.6% were undecided [29]. Our results are consistent with the larger regional trend, showing that a significant percentage of medical students throughout the Middle East may suffer comparable difficulty in establishing a clear career decision.

The relatively high percentage of medical students in our study who have not yet chosen a future specialty can be explained by the fact that most of the participating students are from the basic science phase where they have not experienced the practical fields of medicine to become familiar with the specializations. This was confirmed by a study that showed a significant statistical correlation, as it was found that students who underwent practical training in various specialties during the clinical phase were more able to make a decision about their future medical specialization compared to their colleagues in the basic phase [30]. Therefore, we recommend the introduction of practical training programs and early career counseling within the curriculum to increase students’ awareness of specializations and reduce indecision. Additionally, enhancing opportunities for students to engage directly with physicians across various specialties, whether through workshops or individual mentorship programs, could help them better understand the challenges and opportunities of each field.

The main factors influencing students’ future career plans were personal interest (89.1%), demand for the specialty (76.4%), and expected income (66.9%). According to Chi-square analysis, there were significant correlations between personal interest and whether the decision was made independently or with family influence (p < 0.001), as well as between personal interest and choosing medicine as a first or second choice (p = 0.007). These results demonstrate the significant influence of intrinsic desire on students’ career decisions. This supports research conducted in Lebanon that found 79.3% of students chose medicine because they truly loved the profession, highlighting the importance of personal considerations in such choices [31].

Similarly, 76.4% of students cited the perceived need for doctors in society as a key motivator. A Jordanian survey found that 64.4% of students saw financial compensation as a strong motivator [10],. indicating that financial concerns were also significant, with 66.9% mentioning income as an influencing element. In contrast, the length of residency training was cited by only 35.6% of participants, suggesting that students’ genuine interest in the field outweighs concerns about the duration and intensity of medical training.

This study has several notable strengths. The inclusion of a sizable sample size of 635 medical students from five distinct Palestinian universities improved the generalizability of our findings to the larger group of Palestinian medical students. Furthermore, the trustworthiness of our data collection is strengthened by the use of a validated questionnaire that was modified from prior research. This study’s emphasis on the particular Palestinian environment is one of its strongest points; it offers insightful information about the goals and motivations of students dealing with particular difficulties including instability in politics and a lack of local training options.

However, it is important to acknowledge that the study has several limitations. The most notable of these is the potential for selection bias due to our convenience sampling and online survey methodology. This approach may have inadvertently excluded eligible students who were less inclined or able to participate, which could limit the findings’ generalizability to the broader population of medical students in the West Bank. In addition, as with all survey-based research, participant responses may not fully reflect their actual experiences or views due to self-report bias. Moreover, the unequal distribution of participants across academic years, with a higher percentage from the second year (22.8%) and a lower representation from other years, may restrict the applicability of our findings to different academic stages. Finally, while we have established correlations between variables, we are unable to infer causal relationships due to the cross-sectional design of the study.

Conclusion

This study shows that intrinsic factors, such as a desire to improve community health and assist others, are the main drivers of Palestinian medical students. The majority of students expressed satisfaction with their job decision and a strong desire to pursue specialization, especially overseas, despite the difficulties presented by the lack of local training possibilities and political unrest. Interestingly, the majority also expressed a desire to return and work in the community’s healthcare system.

These findings underscore the need to improve local medical training infrastructure, provide more specialization opportunities, and implement structured career guidance programs within medical schools.

Future studies should look at the long-term career paths of Palestinian medical graduates, how foreign training affects regional healthcare systems, and how well institutional interventions like mentorship or early clinical exposure influence students’ choice of specialization and retention in the country’s workforce.
