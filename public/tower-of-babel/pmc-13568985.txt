Mechanotransduction of Magnetically Applied Forces Leads to Mechanical Stiffening of Platelet‐Rich Plugs
Authors: Nikita Taparia, Ava M Obenaus, Yoeur‐Man T Mach, Nakul Sridhar, Nathan J Sniadecki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe mechanobiology of platelets governs early hemostasis, where their contractile forces compact and stiffen the nascent plug. However, the dynamic changes in the stiffness of a nascent platelet‐rich plug have been difficult to measure. Here, we introduce a microfluidic assay integrating microscale blocks with magnetic microposts to assess platelet forces and plug stiffness. Rotation of a multipole ring magnet beneath the microfluidic channel generates oscillatory motion in the magnetic microposts. As blood enters the channel and platelets aggregate around the blocks and microposts, the oscillatory motion is progressively dampened, providing a direct measure of plug stiffness. Additionally, the deflections of the microposts are used to quantify platelet‐generated forces during intermittent periods without magnetic actuation. With this approach, we find that the rate of stiffening and overall plug stiffness correlate not only with the magnitude of platelet forces, but also with the magnitude of the magnetically applied load. In contrast, pharmacological inhibition with acetylsalicylic acid or blebbistatin markedly reduced the process of plug stiffening, indicating that platelet activation and myosin‐based contractility are required. Together, these findings establish a novel microfluidic platform for probing platelet biomechanics under pathophysiological shear and demonstrate that platelet mechanotransduction governs the mechanical maturation of early hemostatic plugs.Keywords: blood, contraction, magnetic actuation, mechanotransduction, microfluidics, platelet, stiffening

Introduction

Platelets play a central role in hemostasis by rapidly forming a hemostatic plug at sites of vascular injury [1, 2]. This plug initially consists of a loose aggregate of platelets that adhere to each other through molecular bridges formed by surface receptors and adhesive ligands [3]. Subsequent activation of the coagulation cascade drives the formation of a polymerized fibrin mesh that encases platelets and red blood cells together, transforming the nascent plug into a mature clot [4]. Beyond the well‐established biochemical pathways that drive the formation of a hemostatic plug, there is a growing need to understand the mechanobiology of platelets as they are able to sense and respond to physical cues within the developing plug and surrounding hemodynamic environment [5, 6, 7, 8, 9].

Platelet‐generated forces are pivotal for a hemostatic plug as they provide biomechanical changes that strengthen the structure. Upon activation, platelets reorganize their actin cytoskeletal filaments and activate myosin motor proteins to generate contractile forces that act upon the vessel wall or neighboring platelets in a nascent plug [5, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. The ensuing compaction of the plug not only reduces its drag forces but also increases the adhesion between aggregated platelets and confines soluble agonists within the intercellular spaces [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Disruption of myosin‐generated forces, whether through pharmacological inhibition or genetic defects, results in unstable plugs and impaired hemostasis, underscoring the essential contribution of platelet forces to thrombus integrity [24, 25].

The stiffness of the extracellular environment profoundly influences the force generation of platelets, ultimately shaping thrombus architecture and stability. Experimental evidence has found that platelets exert greater contractile forces and exhibit enhanced spreading on stiffer substrates, promoting integrin clustering and cytoskeletal remodeling through RhoA/ROCK and myosin [26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. Similarly, the evolving elasticity of a mature, fibrin‐based clot provides feedback that regulates platelet contractility, creating a dynamic interplay between platelet forces and fibrin network mechanics [31, 32, 33].

Platelets, long viewed primarily as circulating mediators of hemostasis, are now understood to be sensitive to the mechanical forces they encounter within the bloodstream [5, 6, 7, 8]. Shear gradients, rapid changes in flow, and tensile forces generated during thrombus formation all elicit biophysical responses that shape platelet adhesion, activation, and cytoskeletal organization [34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. Platelets respond to force through their adhesion receptors, which initiate intracellular signaling and aggregation [38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. This mechanotransduction response reflects a broader principle seen across a diverse array of cell types that similarly rely on force‐dependent signaling through integrins, membrane receptors, and their actin network to interpret and react to their mechanical environment [44].

The mechanotransduction pathways that connect platelet forces, applied forces, and extracellular elasticity ensure that hemostatic plugs maintain sufficient structural integrity to resist shear forces. In general, soft clots fail to staunch the bleeding, while stiff ones are associated with thrombosis, stroke, or myocardial infarction [45, 46]. The elastic modulus of clots made from whole blood can range from 0.1 to 2 kPa, while those made from platelet‐rich plasma range from 0.6 to 11 kPa [46, 47]. While the stiffness of a clot can depend upon the concentration of red blood cells and fibrinogen, there is a strong contribution by platelets to clot stiffness [48, 49, 50]. Platelets have a cellular elasticity of approximately 10 kPa, and their contractile forces can increase the stiffness of a clot by stretching its fibrin strands to induce strain hardening [12, 18, 29, 31, 50, 51, 52, 53]. However, to the best of our knowledge, the stiffness of a nascent platelet‐rich plug is unknown.

Here, we present the development of an approach to measure the stiffness of a plug and the contractile force of platelets during shear‐induced aggregation. The approach builds upon a microfluidic assay that uses microscale blocks and microposts to measure the forces within a platelet‐rich plug that forms under shear flow [13]. Since magnetic material has been added to polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) microstructures previously [54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59], we embedded iron particles into the microposts and used them as magnetic actuators that apply external loads to aggregated platelets. With our assay, we find that the stiffness of a plug not only depends on the strength of platelet forces, but also the stiffening process is mediated by platelet activation and myosin‐based forces in a manner that is dependent on the strength of the magnetically applied load.

Results

Overview of Microfluidic Approach

The microfluidic device contained microchannels with an array of microscale blocks and posts made from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) with embedded iron particles (Figure 1a). Magnetic actuation of the posts was accomplished by rotating a multipole ring magnet that was positioned underneath the microfluidic channel. The microfluidic devices were made by a soft lithography process in which iron particles were concentrated in the upper portion of the microscale blocks and posts using an applied magnetic field (Figure 1b). The microchannels were 50 µm in height, 200 µm in width, and 3 cm in length. The blocks were 25 µm in length, 25 µm in height, and 15 µm in width. The posts were 25 µm in height, 6 µm in diameter, and positioned 9 µm behind each block. Due to the lack of space between the stage and objective of the microscope, a stepper motor and timing belt system was utilized to control the rotation of a ring magnet (Figure 1c). In this manner, the magnetic field acting on the embedded iron particles varied with the rotation θ of the ring magnet, thereby causing the post to deflect due to the change in the magnetic force acting on it (Figure 1d).

Characterization of Magnetic Actuation

We developed a model to describe the movement of a magnetic post based on the rotation of the ring magnet. Specifically, we used an energy minimization model for a flexible, idealized cantilever to find the angle of deflection at the base of a post, which is an approach that has been used previously [55, 57, 60]. Here, the total energy of the system includes two parts: a) the elastic energy of the post based on its deflection and b) the magnetic energy of a cluster of iron particles due to the orientation of its magnetic dipole moment relative to the applied magnetic field. We considered the post to be an idealized cantilever of diameter D that contained an elastic section of length Le at its base and a magnetic section of length Lm at its top that was subjected to an applied force Fapp from the magnetic field (Figure 2a). We noted that the microscale blocks contained iron particles, but their magnetic deflections were considerably less than the posts due to their second moment of area (19,531 vs. 66 µm4).

The multipole ring magnet contained four quadrants where adjacent quadrants had dipoles with opposite orientations, which caused the magnetic field to curve across the interface between the quadrants (Figure 2b). We defined our coordinate system to be centered at the position where the microchannel was placed above the ring magnet and the x‐direction to be oriented along the length of the microchannel. Accordingly, the deflection of the magnetic post δ could be described by an angle ϕ in the xz‐plane due to the magnetic field B⃗ acting at an angle ψ (Figure 2c). To characterize the magnetic field, we rotated the ring magnet and measured its strength in three orthogonal directions at the position where a microchannel would be placed (Figure S1a). From these measurements, we calculated the strength of the field |B| (Figure 2d) and its angle ψ (Figure 2e).

The magnetic forces that act on an object like a magnetic post involve 1) a torque that rotates the magnetic post to align its dipole moment to an external magnetic field and 2) a force that pulls the magnetic post into the gradient of the magnetic field. Accordingly, we made the following assumptions: first, the contribution to the magnetic energy due to the field strength dominated over the contribution due to the field gradient (Figure S1b), and second, the field strength was below the level needed for saturating the iron particles [55, 60]. Mechanical Analysis of Platelet‐Rich Plugs

For each test, a sample of whole blood was injected into the microfluidic channel at a shear rate of 16 000 s−1 for 15 s to promote the rapid adhesion and aggregation of platelets between the microscale blocks and posts (Figure 3a). Before injecting a blood sample, the magnetic posts were actuated five times by rotating the ring magnet to determine Lm , kpos t, and Fapp , as mentioned earlier (Figure 3b). After blood entered the microchannel (denoted as time t = 0), we repeated a pattern of five magnetic actuations over a period of 40 s that was followed by a period of 20 s with no actuations. After 15 s, the flow was reduced to a shear rate of 500 s−1 for the remainder of the test to reduce the attachment of additional platelets to the plug.

We note that this measurement of plug stiffness is greater than the stiffness of gels made from platelet‐rich plasma [52] and less than the stiffness of individual platelets [31] (Table S1). To quantify platelet forces, we analyzed the deflection of the magnetic post for periods without magnetic actuation, for example, t = 60 to 80 s. In accordance with Hooke's law, the deflection was multiplied by the bending stiffness kpost to quantify the contractile force of platelets within the plug (Figure 3f). For this test, we observed that the force of the platelets within the plug was initially zero prior to the first period of magnetic actuation and then increased steadily over time. From all blood samples we tested, we found that platelet plugs measured within our device produced 64.8 nN of force on average for a period between 60 and 300 s (Figure 3g), which is similar to our previous results [13, 14].

Additionally, we measured the projected area of each plug and tracked its size over time (Figure 3h). For this example, the plug increased in area due to aggregation under flow and then decreased in size due to detachment of platelets that were loosely bound to the plug. From all blood samples we tested, we found that platelet plugs reached an average area of 916 µm2 over a period from 60 to 300 s (Figure 3i). In summary, we demonstrate that our microfluidic approach using magnetic posts can provide dynamic measurements of the stiffness of a plug along with the strength of its contractile forces and its projected area due to platelet aggregation.

Stiffening Behavior of Platelet‐Rich Plug Depends on Magnetically Applied Load

Platelets sense and respond to force, so we used our microfluidic device to examine whether magnetically applied forces can influence the biomechanical stiffening of a plug. As mentioned earlier, we noted that during the initial formation of a platelet‐rich plug, there was a sharp decay in the oscillatory movement of the magnetic post, for example, t = 20 to 60 s. As a point of comparison, we observed that when a magnetic force of 40.3 nN was applied (Figure 4a), the decay was more rapid than when a lesser force of 11.8 nN was applied (Figure 4b). To further examine the effect of applied force on plug stiffness, we compared the oscillatory motion of the posts during the last period of actuation, for example, t = 260 to 300 s. Using the same comparison of data, we observed that when a magnetic force of 40.3 nN was applied (Figure 4d), the amplitude of the oscillatory movement was smaller than when a lesser force of 11.8 nN was applied (Figure 4e). Consequently, we compared our measurements of plug stiffness to the applied load for all data and found a high level of correlation (R2 = 0.96) (Figure 4f). Taken together, the strength of an applied force has a strong effect on both the initial stiffening rate and the steady‐state stiffness of a platelet‐rich plug. Moreover, the data indicate that there are force‐induced responses in platelets, that is, mechanotransduction, that lead to biomechanical changes in the stiffness of the plug.

Plug Stiffening Requires Platelet Activation and Myosin‐Based Contraction

Platelets contribute to the stiffening of a clot by generating cytoskeletal forces, so we sought to examine this relationship within platelet‐rich plugs using our microfluidic device. Our analysis revealed a strong correlation between plug stiffness and platelet forces (R2 = 0.89) (Figure 5a), affirming that platelet contractility is a critical component in the stiffening of a plug. In contrast, we found a moderate correlation between applied forces and platelet forces (R2 = 0.26, Figure S3). Consequently, we performed a multivariate analysis to examine the interrelationships between the three factors: applied forces versus platelet forces versus plug stiffness. We found that the magnitude of an applied force has a positive effect on plug stiffness that was significantly greater than the effect of platelet forces (Figure 5b). Thus, this analysis suggests that externally applied forces contribute to a rise in platelet forces, which subsequently impacts the stiffness of a plug.

To directly examine if platelet activation and platelet forces impact plug stiffness, we treated whole blood samples with acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) to inhibit the generation of thromboxane A2 or blebbistatin to impair myosin activity. Blood samples treated with 0.3 mM ASA or 5 µM blebbistatin produced platelet plugs with platelet forces that were significantly lower than those for untreated blood samples (p‐value = 0.022 for ASA and 0.043 for blebbistatin) (Figure 5c). Moreover, blood samples treated with ASA or blebbistatin produced platelet plugs that were significantly lower in plug stiffness than those from untreated blood samples (p‐value = 0.038 for ASA and 0.051 for blebbistatin) (Figure 5d). For both conditions with inhibitors, we found that the initial stiffening rate not only had a negative relationship with the magnetically applied forces, but there was a weak correlation in the data (R2 = −0.03 for ASA and 0.26 for blebbistatin) (Figure 5e). In contrast, we observed a positive relationship between plug stiffness and applied force, as seen previously with control data, but the slopes of the best fit lines were lower, and the correlations in the data were moderate (R2 = 0.41 for ASA and 0.73 for blebbistatin) (Figure 5f). Thus, these results indicate that platelet activation and myosin‐based forces are necessary for the mechanotransduction response to applied forces that affect plug stiffening.

Discussion

We developed a microfluidic assay that utilizes an array of magnetic block and post structures to assess the mechanobiology of platelets during the early formation of platelet‐rich plugs. Through the application of magnetically applied forces, we observed that platelets exhibited a mechanically activated contractile response, in which the extent of external loading triggered increased platelet‐generated tension that stiffened the plug. Inhibition of myosin activity and thromboxane‐mediated activation attenuated the response, demonstrating that the stiffening process depends on intact biochemical signaling pathways. The proportional increase in plug stiffness with applied force may reflect the extent of mechanosensory structures, like integrins or PIEZO1 ion channels, that could be activated during magnetic actuation [8, 9]. However, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying platelet mechanotransduction in response to magnetically applied forces remain to be determined.

This assay takes advantage of magnetic actuation principles that rely on the use of soft magnetic materials and the rotation of a ring magnet to induce a magnetic torque. We chose to embed iron particles into the micropost, as we and others have done previously [59, 61], rather than cobalt nanowires, magnetite nanoparticles, or nickel shells [55, 56, 57, 62, 63]. We integrated this deposition method into the soft lithography of our microfluidic assay and found it was able to produce a robust number of magnetic posts. In contrast to these previous studies, we chose to rotate a multipole ring magnet underneath the assay because it applied a local field in a compact design, without the need for electromagnetic coils [54]. As modeled previously by Evans et al. [55], our posts behaved similarly to a flexible magnetic rod whose motion is dominated by the strength of a magnetic field and not the field gradient due to the distance between our microfluidic device and the magnet underneath it. Batch‑to‑batch variation in particle loading resulted in modest differences in post magnetization, but this variability proved advantageous by enabling examination of plug stiffening across a range of applied forces.

Our measurements of plug stiffness are on the order of 10 kPa, which corresponds with prior measurements for single platelet assays and tissue‐level elastometry [31, 40, 64, 65]. These measurements are 10‐fold stiffer than platelet‐rich fibrin clots and 100‐fold stiffer than platelet‐free fibrin clots [51, 53], which indicates the contribution due to the contractile forces of platelets. We found that platelet‐rich plugs generate forces in the range of tens to hundreds of nanonewtons, similar to our prior measurements using a nonmagnetic version of this assay [13, 14]. These measurements fall within the range of tens of nanonewtons produced by single platelets and hundreds of newtons by platelet aggregates [11, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Similar to Chen et al. [40], we found that platelet forces and plug stiffness have a strong correlation and that the inhibition of platelet activation and myosin‐generated forces abrogates this response. However, our approach can simultaneously measure platelet forces and plug stiffness during shear‐induced aggregation.

Our results indicate that platelet contraction is vital for a plug to stiffen in response to dynamic loading. However, platelets respond directly to stiffer environments, around the same order of magnitude as our plugs, with enhanced adhesion, activation, and contraction [26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. We measured plug stiffness to be 1.5 kPa on average during the first period of actuation, but the amount of platelet forces for that period was negligibly small. We observed over time that platelet contractility and plug stiffening evolve simultaneously. As such, we cannot determine from the data whether platelets increase their contractile forces in response to a progressively stiffer plug environment, or whether the plug becomes stiffer as a direct consequence of platelet force generation. It is likely that both processes occur concurrently, making their individual contributions difficult to isolate.

One conjecture is that an increase in plug stiffness could arise from enhanced platelet‐to‐platelet adhesion if the oscillating magnetic post compresses adjacent platelets and drives their membranes into closer contact, thereby modestly strengthening the cohesion between platelets within the plug. However, the results with ASA and blebbistatin show that this passive mechanism is not the primary driver. For both inhibitors, the rate of stiffening exhibited a small, negative relationship with applied force, indicating that platelet‐generated forces are needed to accelerate the early phase of plug stiffening. The steady‐state value of plug stiffness with these inhibitors showed a small, positive relationship with applied force, which suggests that externally applied compression may marginally enhance plug stiffness through increased platelet cohesion when contractility is impaired. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the dominant source of plug stiffening is the active, myosin‐driven forces generated by the platelets themselves.

While this study represents an important first step in measuring platelet mechanobiology in real time, several limitations and future directions merit consideration. The surfaces of the microfluidic device were functionalized with collagen type I to capture and activate platelets, but future studies should explore the incorporation of other extracellular matrix proteins, either individually or in combination, to more closely recapitulate the complex composition of the subendothelial matrix and its differential effects on platelet activation [3, 8, 9]. Additionally, the current experiments were performed using blood samples collected in sodium citrate, which limits the generation of thrombin and the formation of fibrin that are pertinent to secondary hemostasis. Future iterations of this platform could incorporate tissue factor and/or the use of recalcified blood to capture the full continuum from primary to secondary hemostasis, thereby providing a more complete picture of the mechanical evolution of a hemostatic plug.

Conclusion

We introduce a novel microfluidic approach that encloses an array of magnetic block‐post pairs to assess the mechanobiology of platelet‐rich plugs during shear‐induced aggregation. Each magnetic post acts as a force transducer and mechanical actuator: its deflection is used to measure platelet forces and its movement in response to a magnetic field is used to assess plug stiffness. We demonstrate that plug stiffness is dependent on myosin‐based platelet forces, specifically in response to the applied force from these posts. By design, this assay can further investigate the mechanobiology of platelet‐rich plugs in the context of new pharmaceutical drugs to assess their ability to affect hemostasis or thrombosis.

Experimental Section

Microfluidic Device Fabrication

Soft lithography was used to create the microfluidic device from a silicon master by a two‐step casting approach. The silicon master was made from SU‐8 photoresist. For the soft lithography process, a negative mold was cast using PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning). The surface of the negative mold was exposed to plasma (Plasma Prep II, Structure Probe, Inc.) and then treated with the vapor phase of (tridecafluoro‐1,1,2,2‐tetrahydrooctyl)‐1‐trichlorosilane (T2492‐KG, United Chemical Technologies) while under vacuum pressure in a desiccator for 2 h to passivate the surface of the negative mold and prevent bonding to uncured PDMS in subsequent steps of the process. Next, iron particles with a diameter of 800 nm (US Research Nanomaterials, Inc) were mixed into uncured PDMS at a 50:50 ratio by weight and then degassed. The Fe‐PDMS mixture was then poured into negative molds and centrifuged for 3 min at 1000 RCF to drive the iron particles to the bottom of the mold so they would be concentrated at the ends of the microscale posts. The molds were placed above a 10 mT magnet for 1 min to cluster the iron particles together. The excess amount of the Fe‐PDMS mixture on the top of the negative mold was scraped away with a glass slide to increase the optical transparency of the final devices for imaging under a microscope. Uncured PDMS without iron particles was added onto the mold, which was then inverted and placed onto a clean glass slide and cured at 110°C for more than 15 h. After curing, the mold was peeled away to produce the base layer of the microfluidic device, which consisted of open‐topped, microfluidic channels with an array of magnetic block and post structures. A top layer of PDMS was made using a custom‐built aluminum mold with pegs for the placement of silicon tubing (0.040‐inch ID/0.085‐inch OD, HelixMark) that formed the inlet and outlet ports of the microfluidic channels. The top and base layers of the microfluidic device were exposed to plasma (Plasma Prep II, Structure Probe, Inc.) for 10 s and then brought into contact to create a permanent seal between them.

Surface Coatings

The inner surfaces of the sealed microfluidic channels were coated with rat tail collagen type I (BD Bioscience) by injecting it as a solution at concentration of 200 µg mL−1 in 0.1 M acetic acid into each outlet port and letting it adsorb for 1 h. Afterwards, the microfluidic channels were rinsed by injecting Tyrode's buffer (10 mM 4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 138 mM NaCl, 5.5 mM glucose, 12 mM NaHCO3, 0.36 mM Na2HPO4, 2.8 mM KCl, 0.4 mM MgCl2) into the ports. The devices were stored at 4°C with Tyrode's buffer in the microfluidic channels until the time of the experiments.

Characterization of Magnetic Field

To measure the strength of the magnetic field, a multipole ring magnet (Torque Polymagnet #1001122, Correlated Magnetics) was attached to a servo motor (Futaba). As the ring magnet was rotated, the strength of the magnetic field in each orthogonal direction was measured using a Hall effect gaussmeter (Model 410, Lake Shore Cryotonics). The probe tip of the gaussmeter was positioned at a 1 mm distance above the magnet and 8 mm distance from its center for measurements of the magnetic field, which corresponded to the location of the microfluidic channel during experiments.

Characterization of Magnetic Actuation

To characterize the deflection of the magnetic posts due to the change in magnetic field, a microfluidic device was placed above the rotating magnet at the same position where the measurements of the magnetic field were taken. Using a metallurgical microscope (Nikon LV‐100) and a 20× objective, a video was taken at a frame rate of 2 Hz by a camera (Hamamatsu C11440) to record the deflection of the magnetic posts at different rotation angles of the ring magnet. To actuate the magnetic posts during experiments, the ring magnet was mounted on a rotating axle that was situated underneath the microfluidic device and driven by a stepper motor (#1205, Pololu) and a custom‐built pulley system. The rotation of the ring magnet by the stepper motor was controlled at 1/1600 steps and 0.125 Hz by a microprocessor (Arduino Uno v3) with a motor driver (A4988 Allegro). The ring magnet was swept between −10 and 10 degrees of rotation for five times during a period of 40 s, followed by a period of 20 s without any rotations. This pattern was performed six times during the duration of an experiment. The period when the magnet was rotated was when the stiffness of the plug was assessed, while the contractile force of the platelets was measured when the magnet was not rotated.

Human Blood Collection

Whole blood up was collected from human subjects in sodium citrate tubes (0.109 M/3.2%, 2.7 mL, BD Vacutainer) under a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of Washington. A blood sample up to 50 mL was collected in multiple tubes and then transferred to a 50‐mL conical centrifuge tube (Falcon Corning). Each blood sample was mixed on a slow rocking agitator for at least ninety minutes prior to testing. To inhibit platelets, acetylsalicylic acid (0.3 mM) or blebbistatin (5 µM, Sigma‐Aldrich) was added to a blood sample and incubated for 10 min prior to testing. In these inhibition experiments, an equivalent volume of either 10 mM HEPES and 2 mM CaCl2 or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added.

Microfluidic Testing Protocol

Blood was drawn into a 3‐mL syringe (BD Scientific) and then injected into the microfluidic channel using a syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus). We used flow parameters that had been previously shown to produce the rapid formation of platelet plugs between the microscale blocks and posts [13, 14]. Specifically, a blood sample was flowed through a microchannel for a wall shear rate of 16 000 s−1 for the first 15 s to induce the adhesion and aggregation of platelets onto the blocks and posts. Subsequently, the flow rate was reduced to produce a wall shear rate was 500 s−1 for the remaining time of the experiment to reduce the rate of aggregation and avoid occluding the microchannel with platelets.

Measurement of Platelet Forces, Plug Stiffness, and Plug Area

Using a metallurgical microscope (Nikon LV‐100) encased in a live‐cell chamber (In Vivo Scientific, Inc) to maintain a 37°C environment, the experiments with blood were recorded by a camera (Hamamatsu C11440) using a 20× objective. Video recordings were initiated 30 s prior to when blood entered the channel and continued until the end of the test. There were eight pairs of microscale blocks and posts within the field of view of the camera. After a full video was taken, the distance between the centroid of each post and the edge of its corresponding block was analyzed using a thresholding algorithm in Fiji/ImageJ. This distance was tracked over time and used to either measure the movement of the posts when the ring magnet was rotated or deflection of the posts when the ring magnet was not rotating. There was no flow during the first period when the ring magnet was rotated, so the data from this period was used to calibrate the magnetic actuation of each post. In addition, the projected area of the plug was measurement using manual tracing in Fiji/ImageJ to assess the size of the platelet plug.

Statistical Analysis

One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post‐hoc test was used to determine the significance between multiple groups, and significance was denoted for p < 0.05. Linear regression analysis and model fitting were performed in MATLAB (Mathworks) using the Curve Fitter toolbox and the least absolute residual option.
