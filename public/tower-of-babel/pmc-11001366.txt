Clinical findings, underlying pathogenetic processes and treatment of vascular dysfunction in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease
Authors: Jinjun Zhu, Fei Liu, Jianhua Mao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is an inherited disorder characterized by the development of fluid-filled cysts in the kidneys. The primary cause of ADPKD is mutations in the PKD1 (polycystic kidney disease 1) or PKD2 (polycystic kidney disease 2) gene. Patients with ADPKD often develop a variety of vascular abnormalities, which have a major impact on the structure and function of the blood vessels and can lead to complications such as hypertension, intracranial aneurysm (ICAN), and atherosclerosis. The progression of ADPKD involves intricate molecular and cellular processes that lead to the development of these vascular abnormalities. Our understanding of these processes remains incomplete, and available treatment options are limited. The aim of this review is to delve into the underlying mechanisms of these vascular abnormalities and to explore potential interventions.Keywords: Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), clinical manifestations, intervention, vascular dysfunction

Introduction

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is a hereditary disorder distinguished by the emergence of fluid-filled cysts within the kidneys. It is estimated to have an incidence rate ranging from 1/1000 to 1/2500 [1,2]. ADPKD is the third most common cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD), following diabetes and hypertension [3]. One aspect that has attracted widespread attention is the abnormal changes associated with vasculature, particularly vascular dysfunction.

In studying the vascular abnormalities of ADPKD, we encounter two distinct points. To begin with, the primary cause of ADPKD resides in mutations within the PKD1 (polycystic kidney disease (1) and PKD2 (polycystic kidney disease (2) genes, responsible for encoding crucial proteins involved in intracellular signaling pathways and fundamental biological processes. Mutations in these genes may not only affect the intrarenal vascular system but may also extend their effects to the broader extrarenal vascular system.

Mutations in PKD1 and PKD2 are the major cause of ADPKD. The causative factor in 78% of these cases is due to a mutation in the PKD1 gene located on chromosome 16 (16p13.3), and in 15% of the cases, it is due to a mutation in the PKD2 gene located on chromosome 4 (4q21). The remaining 5%–10% of cases either lack genetic determinants or are due to rare mutations at other loci [4–6]. These mutations result in structural changes and impaired function of polycystin-1 (PC-1) and polycystin-2 (PC-2) proteins. These proteins play critical roles in maintaining the normal structure and function of the renal tubules and blood vessels. Together with these genetic changes, dysregulation of various signaling pathways, such as the cAMP [3,7,8], mTOR [9,10], and Wnt pathways [11,12], has been suggested to be involved in the development and progression of ADPKD. These pathways interact with polycystins and other proteins involved in cyst formation and growth, leading to altered cell proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation [13].

Recent studies have identified several novel genes, such as GANAB and DNAJB11, which appear to play a role in the pathogenesis of ADPKD and related diseases [4]. GANAB encodes the α subunit of glucosidase II, a resident enzyme of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) that participates in N-linked glycosylation, a critical quality control process governing the folding, maturation, and trafficking of membrane and secreted proteins [14]. Mutations in GANAB disrupt the maturation and localization of PC1 [15]. The product encoded by DNAJB11 stands as one of the most abundant cofactors of the endoplasmic reticulum chaperone, binding immunoglobulin protein (BiP, also called HSPA5 and GRP78). BiP functions as a crucial heat shock protein chaperone that is indispensable for the proper folding and assembly of secretory or membrane proteins within the endoplasmic reticulum [16]. What these genes have in common is their possible roles in promoting suboptimal maturation and transportation of membrane or secreted proteins, with a specific susceptibility to PC1 dosage, ultimately leading to the development of renal or liver cysts [17].

Abnormal expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and other factors involved in angiogenesis and vascular remodeling is also believed to be involved in the pathogenesis of ADPKD [18,19]. These changes can lead to abnormal blood vessel growth and remodeling, which in turn promote the development of hypertension, aneurysms and other vascular complications in patients with ADPKD [20]. Coronary aneurysms and entrapments are a source of coronary syndromes and death in ADPKD. Mutations in ADPKD-associated genes may predispose patients to coronary artery abnormalities, especially aneurysms [21]. Individuals with ADPKD experience a variety of vascular abnormalities that significantly affect the structure and function of blood vessels, leading to a variety of consequences, such as impaired blood circulation and organ ischemia [20,22].

At the same time, patients with ADPKD are often confronted with secondary vasculopathy, which arises directly from the renal lesion itself and encompasses a range of vascular-related pathophysiological changes, such as hypertension, renal ischemia, and proteinuria [23]. Therefore, when discussing vascular anomalies in ADPKD, it is important that we explicitly analyze both topics in order to gain insight into the mechanisms and clinical implications of these anomalies.

Vascular abnormality affecting the majority of patients with ADPKD-related CKD (chronic kidney disease). Activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) is thought to be the main cause of hypertension in patients with ADPKD [24], leading to vasoconstriction and retention of sodium and water [25,26]. Hypertension may also exacerbate the growth of renal cysts, leading to further deterioration of kidney function and increased risk of cardiovascular disease [22,27,28]. Hypertension has been reported to occur in nearly 60% of patients prior to deterioration of renal function [29]. Advanced ADPKD kidneys are characterized by dense fibrotic bands between cysts, generalized glomerulosclerosis and tubular atrophy (i.e., signs of ischemia), and loss of normal blood vessels [30]. Proteinuria is a common complication of adult ADPKD and a risk factor for disease progression [31].

The arterial walls of patients with ADPKD become weak and prone to aneurysm formation, especially ICAN, with a prevalence of 12.4% (95% CI, 8.95%–15.82%) in ADPKD patients of both sexes. The prevalence increases with age, peaking at 23.3% (95% CI, 16.85–29.75%) in the 60–69-year-old age group [32]. Studies from China, Japan, and Europe (Germany, Poland) reported a higher prevalence of ICAN. Twenty-three percent (95% CI 15–31%) of patients had multiple aneurysms [33].

Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) is a powerful independent risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [34]. Forty-eight percent of hypertensive ADPKD patients have LVH [35]. In these patients, a higher left ventricular mass index (LVMI) is associated with worse renal and patient outcomes [36].

In a study identifying 71,531 adult inpatients with ADPKD aged ≥ 18 years from 2006 to 2014 and collecting relevant clinical data, the prevalence of CVD among ADPKD patients was 42.6% [37]. The most common CVDs were ischemic heart disease (19.3%), arrhythmias (14.2%), and heart failure (13.1%). In another study, the mean age of all patients was 53.2 ± 13.7 years, 82.8% had a family history of ADPKD, and 32.5% had entered ESRD. The most common self-reported cardiovascular events were arrhythmia (25.9%), peripheral vascular disease (16.5%), heart valve problems (14.4%), heart enlargement (9.5%), stroke or cerebral hemorrhage (7.5%), myocardial infarction (6%), and cerebral aneurysm (5.0%) [38].

Atherosclerosis may also be present in patients with ADPKD; this can include carotid artery dissection [39], wall thickening [40], stenosis [41], and tortuosity [42,43], all of which may lead to cardiovascular events.

The pathogenesis of ADPKD involves intricate molecular and cellular processes, culminating in vascular dysfunction. Crafting effective treatment strategies for ADPKD demands meticulous consideration. Among the concerns, vascular abnormalities loom large, as they can significantly impact patients’ health and overall quality of life. The pathogenesis underpinning these vascular irregularities remains only partially understood, and available treatment options are limited. Guided by this context, the core objective of this article is to deliver a comprehensive review of the contemporary literature exploring potential mechanisms and interventions aimed at addressing vascular dysfunction. We aspire to establish a robust groundwork for research in this specialized area, propelling advancements in our comprehension and management of these intricate challenges.

Clinical manifestations of vascular lesions

The vascular lesions caused by ADPKD can involve multiple organs and systems and are mainly manifested as follows, which are sorted by system/body part:

Impaired renal function

Due to renal vascular lesions causing renal damage, kidney function may gradually decline, and pain, stones, proteinuria, and osteoporosis are some of the many symptoms caused by the decline in kidney function, eventually leading to ESRD [4,44].

Renal vascular disease is the cause of severe hypertension in ADPKD. Renal artery occlusion appears to be rare in ADPKD, but because it is not usually looked for, renal artery abnormalities may be underestimated [45,46]. The occurrence of uncontrollable or accelerated hypertension in ADPKD considered be sought in the case of coexisting renal artery disease [41,47].

ADPKD-infected renal cyst rupture is a rare complication but has been described in cases of intraperitoneal rupture with peritonitis [48–50]. Renal cysts may cause severe symptoms such as back pain, fever, nausea, and vomiting due to rupture or infection. Infectious renal cysts should be differentiated in patients with ADPKD with leukocytosis and/or fever. The cause of the infection should be investigated because if a fistula is present, the treatment method will be different, requiring nephrectomy instead of intravenous administration of antibiotics alone [51].

In ADPKD patients with renal vasculopathy, the kidney blood vessels may become stiff and narrow, leading to obstruction of blood flow, which in turn affects the normal function of the kidneys. Renal vasculopathy can lead to blockage of the urinary tract and increase the incidence of kidney stones [52].

Cardiovascular disease

Hypertension is one of the most common cardiovascular diseases in patients with ADPKD, with approximately 60% of patients developing hypertension [29]. Due to long-term hypertension and impaired renal function, the heart of ADPKD patients is subjected to a greater load, leading to left ventricular hypertrophy, which may lead to serious consequences such as arrhythmias and heart failure (HF) [53]. Heart failure caused by myocardial damage, reduced cardiac function, and other reasons may lead to symptoms such as dyspnea, edema, fatigue, and even life-threatening conditions [54]. If risk factors such as hypertension and high cholesterol are present in ADPKD patients, spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) may also occur, which may lead to serious consequences such as myocardial ischemia, angina pectoris, and myocardial infarction. For patients who have angina pectoris and positive cardiac biomarkers, coronary artery dissection should be considered [55].

Symptoms such as cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure in ADPKD patients may lead to pericardial effusion, causing discomfort such as chest pain and difficulty breathing [56]. Due to chronic hypertension and left ventricular hypertrophy, patients with ADPKD may develop mitral valve prolapse, leading to arrhythmia, cardiac insufficiency, and other consequences [57]. Patients with ADPKD may develop aortic aneurysms, and these lesions may lead to serious complications such as aortic coarctation and rupture [58].

Cerebrovascular lesions

Intracranial aneurysms (ICANs) and arachnoid cysts are more common in patients with ADPKD than in the general population [59]. ICANs, the most common type of cerebrovascular lesion, are caused by weaknesses or defects in the walls of cerebral arteries. The incidence of ICANs in patients with ADPKD is many times higher than that in the general population [33]. If a cerebral aneurysm ruptures, this will result in subarachnoid hemorrhage [60], a serious complication that often leads to disability or death. Cost–utility analysis suggests that screening for ICANs can be recommended for all patients with ADPKD [61].

Hepatic vascular lesions

In ADPKD patients, the liver is the second most favorable cystic site. Liver cysts were found in almost 85% of patients over the age of 30 years in the CRISP cohort at baseline [62]. Polycystic liver disease (PLD) is a disease most commonly seen in patients with ADPKD and less commonly as isolated liver disease [63]. Patients with ADPKD may have a large number of cysts in the liver, resulting in enlargement of the liver, compression of surrounding organs and tissues, abdominal pain, bloating, pressure, loss of appetite, changes in body image and physical discomfort, as well as mental discomfort [64].

In addition, encapsulated peritoneal sclerosis (EPS) is a potentially fatal complication after prolonged peritoneal dialysis for which tamoxifen may be used for prevention and treatment. However, it has been reported that ADPKD patients with EPS may be at risk of hepatic infarction and venous thromboembolism after treatment with tamoxifen [65].

Retinal vasculopathy

Individuals grappling with ADPKD are more likely to encounter central retinal vascular occlusion at a comparatively young age. Emerging from several pertinent research articles is the proposition that retinal vascular occlusion might provide an additional perspective on the systemic vasculopathy inherent to ADPKD [66].

Notably, vasoconstriction and arterial remodeling [67] are increased in Pkd2± mice, a model that parallels ADPKD, in their conduit and resistance arteries [68]. Such phenomena may increase the risk of ADPKD in humans. Increased awareness of this manifestation could lead to an earlier diagnosis and treatment before irreversible vision loss develops.

Mechanism of vascular functional abnormalities

Deficiency of PC-1 or PC-2 leads to lower NO levels [23]. An impaired endothelial response to shear stress with attenuation of vascular relaxation is caused by a defect in NO release correlating with a reduction in Ca2+-dependent endothelial NO synthesis activity [69]. PC-1 and PC-2 are involved in the formation and function of primary cilia [70,71]. Their mutations lead to ciliary protein dysfunction and/or abnormal ciliary structure, resulting in ADPKD [72]. Primary endothelial cilia protruding from the inner surface of the vessel wall can sense changes in blood flow and convert this mechanosensation into intracellular biochemical/molecular signals that trigger a cellular response. However, primary endothelial cilia dysfunction may lead to impaired this response and directly contribute to the development of vascular abnormalities such as hypertension and aneurysms [73].

Furthermore, the interaction between PC-1 and PC-2 has a central role in the regulation of intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis [74]. It becomes evident that the dysregulated metabolism of cAMP and purinergic signaling induces heightened sensitivity among principal cells in the collecting ducts and tubular epithelial cells in the distal nephron to the unrelenting tonic effects of vasopressin. This amplifies their responsiveness to vasopressin, thereby intensifying the perturbation of calcium signaling that is set in motion by mutations in PC1 or PC2. Furthermore, this heightened response triggers downstream signaling pathways, giving rise to a sequence of events including impaired tubulogenesis, escalated cell proliferation, augmented fluid secretion, and interstitial inflammation [69]. Altered Ca2+ homeostasis plays a role in the cardiovascular pathogenesis of ADPKD by reducing Ca2+ release and restoring intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis, thereby inhibiting the growth of cysts in ADPKD [75].

The cAMP pathway plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of ADPKD [76]. Mutations in the PKD1 or PKD2 genes may lead to a surge in intracellular cAMP levels, thereby influencing related signal transduction processes. cAMP specifically binds to protein kinase A (PKA), triggering a cascade reaction that activates PKA and affects numerous downstream targets [77]. In ADPKD, activation of PKA can promote cell proliferation, induce changes in adhesion and polarity, and promote repeated cyst development. Upon activation, PKA phosphorylates the cellular metabolic protein CREB, thereby influencing gene transcription. It can be envisioned that in the context of ADPKD, the CREB pathway may regulate genes associated with proliferation, survival, and differentiation [78].

Simultaneously, elevated cAMP levels promote the activation of Akt, a kinase responsible for coordinating cell survival, proliferation, and metabolic processes [79]. The activation of Akt can inhibit the crucial negative-regulatory TSC1-TSC2 complex of the mTOR pathway, thereby suppressing mTOR activity. The TSC1-TSC2 complex is composed of the proteins TSC1 and TSC2, which when active prevents the activation of mTORC1 (mTOR complex 1) [80]. When the TSC1-TSC2 complex is obstructed, mTORC1 becomes activated. mTORC1 becomes a pivotal signaling complex within the cell, exerting control over cellular growth and metabolic activities. Its activation can catalyze protein synthesis, propelling cellular expansion. In ADPKD, excessive activation of mTORC1 leads to uncontrolled cell proliferation and intracystic overgrowth [81].

Mutations in the PKD1 and PKD2 genes may trigger the activation of the Wnt signaling pathway, a process that can be facilitated through various mechanisms, including interactions between PKD1, PKD2, and molecules involved in Wnt signal transduction [12]. Upon activation, the Wnt signaling pathway exerts its effects by stabilizing intracellular β-catenin [82]. Conversely, when the Wnt pathway is in an inactive state, β-catenin is degraded. The stabilized form of β-catenin translocates to the cell nucleus, where it binds to transcription factors to orchestrate changes in gene transcription. Importantly, stabilized β-catenin forms a complex with Tcf/Lef transcription factors, thereby regulating the transcription of Wnt pathway target genes [83]. The activation of Wnt signaling can drive cell proliferation and promote growth within the cyst. This activation is consistent with the regulation of cell cycle proteins and growth factors, thus guiding the dynamic behavior of cells.

ADPKD originates from the initial mutations in the PKD1 and PKD2 genes, with the key proteins encoded by these genes closely linked to cyst formation. These mutations can activate multiple signaling pathways, one of which is VEGF [19]. Mutations in PKD1 and PKD2 result in alterations of intracellular pathways, triggering abnormal overproduction of VEGF, a well-recognized protein with pro-angiogenic properties. The increase in VEGF significantly increases the vascularization of renal tissue [84]. The elevation of VEGF stimulates neovascularization, predominantly around the cysts. This enhanced blood supply shapes the growth environment of the cysts. As a collateral effect, VEGF stimulates cell growth, resulting in an increase in the number of cells within the cysts. Furthermore, heightened levels of VEGF facilitate an augmentation in vascular permeability, enabling the influx of fluids and proteins into the cysts. The influx of fluid ultimately leads to fluid accumulation, resulting in cyst formation [85]. Angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and alterations in permeability collectively drive the formation of cysts. Most importantly, the activation of VEGF plays a pivotal role in orchestrating angiogenesis and cell proliferation, thereby exerting regulatory influences on these processes.

Cardiovascular complications are thought to be the result of RAAS pathway activation [86]. Enlarged renal cysts can stimulate the RAAS because the adjacent parenchyma of the cyst compresses the renal vascular system, leading to renal ischemia and vascular changes in the area [87]. Activation of the RAAS in PKD has been demonstrated in clinical studies [25]. The RAAS causes multiple effects, including vasoconstriction, sodium retention, tissue remodeling, and proinflammatory and profibrotic effects, though every organ is affected by RAAS activation and the resulting hypertension, cell proliferation, inflammation, and fibrosis (Figure 1) [29,88,89].

Major ADPKD signaling pathways.

Cysts formed in the kidney as a result of ADPKD exert mechanical stress on surrounding tissues and blood vessels and secrete cytokines and growth factors that activate pathways associated with cyst progression, leading to a snowball effect and the formation of more cysts [90]. For example, VEGF is an important angiogenic factor that promotes the growth and expansion of new blood vessels [91]. Renal tubular cells in ADPKD exhibit irregular expression of VEGF. This, in turn, triggers a chain reaction stimulating not only tubular cells but also mesenchymal fibroblasts and endothelial cells, thereby driving the advancement of vascular abnormalities. Consequently, directing interventions toward the VEGF signaling cascade within the PKD scenario seems to be a promising therapeutic avenue [92].

After renal injury, a complex interplay of both innate and adaptive immune responses comes into play to address the tissue damage. During this process, injured tubular epithelial cells release several pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and adhesion molecules. These constituents contribute to the orchestrated recruitment and activation of immune cells, thereby orchestrating a multifaceted response to injury [93]. Increased inflammation and fibrosis tend to exacerbate ADPDK. A number of cells and molecules asso­ciated with inflammation and/or fibrosis can affect renal function and the progression of ADPKD. For example, macrophages, proinflammatory chemo attractants such as monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1), and chemokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-1, and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β can contribute to accelerating disease progression [94].

TGF-β is an important cytokine that can affect the processes of cell proliferation, apoptosis and differentiation. In ADPKD, the expression level of TGF-β is increased, and it can promote fibrosis and the inflammatory response around cysts, thus accelerating the growth and expansion of cysts [95]. Moreover, in ADPKD, the expansion and proliferation of cysts can lead to a response of renal tubular and interstitial cells. These cells can secrete a variety of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) [96] and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [97]. They can stimulate cell proliferation and inflammatory responses around cysts, thereby accelerating cyst growth and expansion. Cytokines can also activate transcription factors and signaling pathways, such as NF-κB [98] and STATs [99]. These signaling pathways further contribute to the development and progression of vascular abnormalities [100,101].

Intervention

Hypertension is an important predictor of ADPKD progression and a risk factor for the development of CVD, which is the most common cause of death in patients with ADPKD. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) and angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) are widely used as first-line therapeutic agents in the treatment of hypertension in ADPKD [102]. The therapeutic benefit of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibition (ACEIs) in diminishing renal cyst growth, treating hypertension, and reversing the increased left ventricular mass in ADPKD has been reported [103]. Compared with angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), the life expectancy of patients taking ACEIs increased by 1.39 years, and the 10-year survival rate of patients taking ACEIs was approximately 47%, compared with 34% for ARBs. ACEIs have a higher cost-effectiveness, so ACEIs are better than ARBs as the first-line treatment for hypertension management in patients with ADPKD [102].

The pressor V2 receptor antagonist tolvaptan has emerged as the sole pharmaceutical agent demonstrating nephroprotective effects in the context of ADPKD. Importantly, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) has granted approval for the utilization of tolvaptan as a treatment option for individuals navigating rapidly progressive ADPKD and CKD stages I–III [104,105].

Regrettably, though these drugs are rooted in robust pathogenetic principles, clinical trials involving alternative medications such as mTOR inhibitors and somatostatin analogs (SSAs) have failed to yield compelling evidence of their efficacy. Consequently, neither the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) nor the European Medicines Agency (EMA) has granted approval for their utilization [106–108]. The outcomes of statin treatment in children and young adults dealing with ADPKD show promise, though the findings need validation through multicenter studies involving adults [109].

Treatment of ADPKD involves multiple signaling pathways, some of which have been explored in clinical studies or as blockers in laboratory studies. Overactivation of the mTOR pathway in ADPKD is associated with cyst growth and expansion. Some drugs that inhibit mTOR may be effective against ADPKD, such as thalidomide (sirolimus) and etoricoxib (everolimus). The cAMP pathway plays an important role in ADPKD, so some drugs aim to modulate cAMP levels, such as vasopressin V2 receptor antagonists (e.g., toremifene) and theobromine (caffeine). Activation of the Wnt signaling pathway is associated with the development of ADPKD. Although specific drugs targeting the Wnt pathway are still under investigation, drugs such as disulfiram may have a modulatory effect on the Wnt signaling pathway. The VEGF pathway is involved in angiogenesis and cyst growth in ADPKD. Some anti-VEGF drugs may have a role in ADPKD treatment, such as bevacizumab (bevacizumab).

In addition to the drugs mentioned above, new drugs are in preclinical trials to determine their efficacy and safety. Bosutinib (Src/Abl tyrosine kinase inhibitor) has been shown to inhibit renal cyst formation in a rat model of PKD [110]. In addition, S-CR 8 (analog of roscovitine) inhibited renal cyst and hepatic cyst formation [111]. Metformin has been safely and widely used to treat type 2 diabetes. Preclinical studies suggest that it may play a role in slowing disease progression in ADPKD [112]. Curcumin (a spice derived from the rhizome of the plant turmeric) reduces cyst formation and delays renal failure in Pkd1-deletion mice [113], but a recent randomized controlled trial showed that curcumin supplementation did not improve vascular function or slow kidney growth in children/young adults with ADPKD [114].

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) has long been considered a relative contraindication due to the potentially limited intraperitoneal space. In recent years, several studies have shown that it is also a valid option for patients with ADPKD, concluding that it is a first-line treatment that may be appropriate for some [115]. For the majority of ADPKD patients, cyst debridement is highly effective in treating the chronic pain associated with the disease and can provide long-lasting pain relief for this patient population [116]. Patients with ADPKD may require bilateral nephrectomy (BN) in addition to a kidney transplant (KT) to control symptoms [117]. Laparoscopic nephrectomy for ADPKD is a safe procedure with an acceptable complication rate. Unilateral nephrectomy is superior to bilateral nephrectomy in terms of perioperative complication rates. Although the timing of nephrectomy is controversial, it appears that simultaneous nephrectomy and renal transplantation does not increase surgical morbidity or affect graft survival rates [118]. Prior to kidney transplantation, the kidney may be surgically removed to increase the abdominal space. However, not all individuals are candidates for this procedure. Transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) of the renal arteries may provide a noninvasive method of reducing renal volume in ADPKD [119].

ADPKD treatment should be multidirectional and include lifestyle and dietary optimization. Dietary optimization should include drinking plenty of water, reducing sodium intake, quitting smoking and drinking coffee [120,121].

In ADPKD patients with normal renal function, increasing the amount of solute-free water consumed uniformly throughout the day will reduce plasma AVP concentrations and attenuate the effect of cAMP on renal cysts [122]. The matter of water intake remains a topic of contention primarily owing to the absence of clinical trials that definitively showcase its impact on the progression of ADPKD in human subjects. Notably, the KHA-CARI ADPKD guidelines advocate that individuals with ADPKD should consume water to satiate their thirst. This recommendation is grounded in the understanding that there is no substantiated evidence indicating that heightened fluid intake offers benefits in terms of mitigating cyst growth [123].

As per the Canadian Expert consensus on Assessing Risk of Disease Progression and Pharmacological Management of ADPKD [124], the recommendation aligns with that provided for hypertensive individuals by Hypertension Canada guidelines. This suggests a prudent approach toward salt restriction, aiming for an intake of approximately 5 grams of salt or 87 mmol of sodium daily [125].

In cultured human PKD cells, caffeine inhibition of PDE increases the accumulation of cAMP and, through this mechanism, activates the ERK pathway for cell proliferation and increases transepithelial fluid secretion in the cystic epithelium of ADPKD. Thus, caffeine is a risk factor promoting cyst enlargement in ADPKD patients [126]. However, in a study of 239 ADPKD patients (mean age = 32.3 ± 8.9 years; 188 caffeine consumers) with a median follow-up of 12.5 years, no significant adverse effect of caffeine on the disease progression of ADPKD was found [127]. In another study, ADPKD patients participated in the ongoing Swiss ADPKD study between 2006 and January 2014, making at least 151 visits per year, and data from this prospective longitudinal study also did not confirm coffee drinking as a risk factor for ADPKD progression [128]. The KDIGO meeting on the ADPKD controversy noted that avoidance of high caffeine intake has been proposed, but ‘high caffeine’ has not been defined [129].

Defective glucose metabolism is closely linked to the pathobiology of ADPKD, so its pathways are potential therapeutic targets for ADPKD [130]. Food restriction (FR) effectively slows disease progression in a mouse model of ADPKD [131]. Prior to exploring the ketogenic diet, caloric restriction (which is also a driver of ketogenesis) was shown to be associated with ADPKD. The ketogenic diet, a high-fat, low-carbohydrate, adequate-protein dietary strategy, induces nutritional ketosis that improves disease progression in an animal model of PKD and significantly inhibits cyst growth, fibrosis, and PKD-related signaling pathways [132]. In a study of 131 patients with ADPKD, seventy-four were on a ketogenic diet (KD), and fifty-two were on a time-restricted diet (TRD) for an average duration of six months. A total of 86% of participants reported that ketogenic diet interventions (KDIs) improved their overall health, 67% described improvements in ADPKD-related health problems, 90% observed significant weight loss, 64% of hypertensive participants reported improvements in blood pressure, and 66% noted frequently observed adverse effects of KDIs [133].

Discussion

One of the main mechanisms of vascular abnormalities in ADPKD is mutations in two genes, PKD1 and PKD2, which lead to structural changes and impaired function of polycystin-1 and polycystin-2, resulting in ciliary protein dysfunction and/or abnormal cilia structure, leading to ADPKD. Their mutation also leads to an imbalance in intracellular homeostasis, such as Ca2+ homeostasis. In addition, an enlarged renal cyst can stimulate the RAAS, as the adjacent parenchyma of the cyst compresses the renal vascular system, leading to renal ischemia and vascular changes. The RAAS influences a variety of processes, including vasoconstriction, sodium retention, tissue remodeling, and proinflammatory and profibrotic effects. Another factor contributing to the development of abnormal blood vessels in ADPKD is the dysregulation of cytokines and growth factors such as VEGF and tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1, and transforming growth factor-β. These factors are regulated in patients with ADPKD and may contribute to the development of abnormal blood vessels.

Individuals in some families with GANAB mutations have indeed exhibited vascular manifestations such as ICAN. However, ICAN has only been observed in the M641 family, and the association between GANAB mutations and vascular phenotypes in the other families remains unconfirmed. Therefore, the relationship between GANAB mutations and vascular abnormalities cannot be definitively established at this time [14].

In recent years, research on epigenetics, organoids, and multiomics has deepened our understanding of the pathogenic mechanism of ADPKD and provided an opportunity to better explore the vascular abnormalities associated with ADPKD.

Epigenetic regulation of gene expression plays a crucial role in the progression of ADPKD. Epigenetic studies offer us a new perspective that reveals the potential linkage between gene regulation and the development of ADPKD. Epigenetic changes, such as DNA methylation and histone modification, may play a key role in the formation and growth of ADPKD cysts. The findings of alterations at the molecular level have contributed to a better understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of ADPKD [134]. On the other hand, organoid studies allow us to establish ADPKD pathological models in a more refined manner. By utilizing organoid models, researchers can simulate the formation and growth of cysts while exploring relevant cellular signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms. These models offer a more experimentally controllable platform for investigating the pathogenic mechanisms of ADPKD, complementing other approaches focusing on anatomical kidney development and renal function research, thereby aiding hypothesis validation and the discovery of new therapeutic targets [135]. The application of multiomics approaches has further expanded our understanding of ADPKD. The integrated use of techniques such as multimodal single-cell analysis enables the exploration of global molecular changes associated with ADPKD, leading to a more precise description of the cellular state of the specific disease at the system level. These methodologies supply a wealth of data that aid in the identification of key molecules and pathways related to the pathogenesis of ADPKD, along with supplying hints for potential therapeutic strategies [136].

Despite significant progress in both research and practice, the study of vascular abnormalities in ADPKD still encounters various limitations and challenges. The ‘second hit’ hypothesis in ADPKD refers to the notion that a second mutation or event plays a role in accelerating disease progression within the context of polycystic kidney disease. Under this hypothesis, the initial mutation, or the first ‘hit,’ is typically hereditary, but the progression of the disease may require the influence of a second event [137]. This event could involve further mutations, epigenetic modifications, environmental factors, and more. The second event could contribute to the hastened advancement of the disease, leading to the formation and enlargement of kidney cysts. Even within the same familial context, individuals carrying the same PKD gene mutation may exhibit varying disease presentations and progression rates. The possibility of the ‘second hit’ hypothesis underscores the intricacy of the disease, encompassing the interplay of numerous genes and environmental factors, which might manifest differently in diverse patients.

ADPKD is a highly complex, heterogeneous disease in which the etiology, clinical manifestations, and pathophysiological mechanisms may significantly differ among patients. This makes the generalization and dissemination of research findings difficult, necessitating consideration of the influence of multiple factors. Despite the crucial roles that organoid and animal models play in ADPKD research, they have limitations. Models cannot fully replicate the complex physiological environment within the human body, leading to the possibility that certain pathological processes may not be entirely reflective of reality. Currently, there is a lack of effective biomarkers to accurately predict the occurrence and progression of vascular abnormalities in ADPKD, thereby complicating early diagnosis and the formulation of treatment strategies. Although numerous treatment methods aim to slow the progression of ADPKD, therapy remains a challenge.

Vascular abnormalities involve a variety of intricate signaling pathways, so developing effective drugs to target these abnormalities is not an easy task. Clinical trials for ADPKD vascular abnormalities face numerous challenges, including identifying appropriate end point markers and controlling for other variables influencing outcomes. These challenges could affect the reliability of studies and the interpretability of results. ADPKD is a chronic disease, and its course may require several decades to manifest noticeable symptoms and effects. This makes long-term follow-up and research complex. Investigating vascular abnormalities in ADPKD necessitates multidisciplinary collaboration, involving expertise from various fields such as biology, genetics, molecular medicine, and clinical medicine. Despite these limitations and challenges, with the continuous advancement of technology and the progression of research, we have reason to believe that these issues can gradually be addressed, leading to a better understanding and intervention of vascular abnormalities in ADPKD. Through collaborative efforts on a global scale, more effective treatment and management strategies can be discovered to enhance the quality of life for patients with ADPKD.

Furthermore, the psychological and societal pressures induced by ADPKD are deserving of thorough examination. The inherent hereditary nature of the disease may result in anxiety and unease among family members. Simultaneously, the detection of known cardiovascular anomalies can trigger psychological stress for both patients and their families, particularly when long-term monitoring and treatment are needed. Balancing treatment choices, familial support, and individual emotions poses a multifaceted ethical challenge. The intricate task of harmonizing these factors warrants in-depth deliberation within the context of ADPKD research. Understanding how to navigate these complexities is pivotal to offering holistic care to patients and their families while considering the broader social and ethical implications of the disease.

Conclusion

ADPKD is a genetic disease characterized by the formation of multiple cysts in the kidney, leading to renal failure and various extrarenal manifestations. One of the most common complications of ADPKD is vascular abnormalities, including impairment of renal function, cardiovascular pathologies, cerebrovascular lesions, hepatic vascular lesions and retinal vascular lesions.

Several interventions have been proposed to prevent or manage vascular abnormalities in patients with ADPKD, including pharmacological agents, surgical interventions and lifestyle modifications. Despite the promising results of these interventions, significant challenges remain in the management of vascular abnormalities in patients with ADPKD. For example, optimal blood pressure targets for patients with ADPKD remain unclear, and the effects of various new and old medications on vascular abnormalities in patients with PKD are poorly understood. In addition, although surgical interventions are effective in the treatment of ADPKD, they carry significant risks and may not be possible for all patients. Therefore, there is a need to delve deeper into the mechanisms of vascular abnormalities in ADPKD and to develop more targeted and effective interventions for this purpose.

In conclusion, vascular abnormalities are a common and serious complication of ADPKD, the effective management of which is critical to improve patient prognosis. Although several interventions are currently available, further research is needed to better understand the underlying mechanisms and to develop more targeted and effective treatments for vascular abnormalities associated with ADPKD.
