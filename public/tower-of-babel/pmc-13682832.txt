Keratin 8 enhances the efficacy of mesenchymal stem cells in preventing acute graft-versus-host disease
Authors: Ya Zhou, Rui Wang, Zheng Wang, Hao Yu, Qing Xu, Yuxi Xu, Yuxuan He, Ziyi Hu, Qi Wang, Xiaodong Xie, Shijie Yang, Lingyu Zeng, Xiaoqi Wang, Qingxiao Song, Xi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Acute graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). The development of methods that preserve graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) activity while preventing aGVHD remains a long-sought goal. Recent studies suggest that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), particularly human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (HUC-MSCs), are effective at mitigating aGVHD, yet their varied efficacy remains poorly understood.Methods:We performed a comprehensive analysis of MSCs heterogeneity through single-cell transcriptomic mapping across nine human tissue sources and identified the keratin 8 high-expression (KRT8+) subpopulation of HUC-MSCs with enhanced functional properties. We further validated the functional roles of the KRT8+ subpopulation through lentivirus-mediated KRT8 overexpression. Bulk RNA sequencing was used to analyze the genomic impact of KRT8 overexpression. Additionally, we examined the effects of KRT8 overexpression on cellular senescence via Western blotting and β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining. The immunosuppressive function of HUC-MSCs was evaluated by coculturing them with CD3+ T cells and liver organoids. Cell migration ability was assessed through Transwell assays and PKH26 dye labeling in vivo. An MHC-mismatched GVHD mice model (C57BL/6J to BALB/c) was established to test the therapeutic effect of HUC-MSCs. Clinical signs of aGVHD were monitored, and pathological changes were analyzed using hematoxylin and eosin staining. The GVL effect was evaluated through the inoculation of luciferase-transfected A20 cells and bioluminescence imaging.Results:KRT8 was expressed predominantly in HUC-MSCs. Compared with the KRT8 low-expression (KRT8lo) subpopulation of HUC-MSCs, the KRT8+ subpopulation presented increased stemness and reduced immunogenicity. KRT8 overexpression in HUC-MSCs significantly ameliorated aGVHD in vivo. Importantly, increased KRT8 expression in HUC-MSC did not impaire GVL activity in A20 lymphoma cells. Functional experiments further confirmed that HUC-MSCs with increased KRT8 expression significantly reduced cellular senescence by increasing enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2)-mediated histone h3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3). This upregulation resulted in increased secretion of hepatocyte growth factor (HGF). This, in turn, suppressed T-cell proliferation and activation, significantly reducing liver organoid damage in vitro.Conclusions:Our results suggest that the infusion of KRT8+ HUC-MSCs enhances the efficacy of preventing aGVHD but does not interfere with the GVL effect. This strategy represents a promising therapeutic advancement in the setting of allo-HSCT.

Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) represents a curative therapy for both hematologic malignancies and non-malignant hematologic disorders.[1,2] The success of allo-HSCT largely relies on the graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effect.[3] Despite its therapeutic potential, allo-HSCT is often complicated by acute graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD), a substantial clinical challenge characterized by donor-derived immune cells attacking the recipient’s tissues indiscriminately.[4,5] aGVHD primarily affects the skin, liver, and gastrointestinal (GI) tract, markedly impacting morbidity and mortality rates post allo-HSCT.[4,5] The current management of aGVHD predominantly involves systemic immunosuppressive agents.[6] However, they introduce further complications, such as increased susceptibility to infections and relapse of the underlying disease in the patient.[7]

An emerging and promising area of cell therapy is the use of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), particularly those derived from human umbilical cords, because of their immunomodulatory properties.[8,9] MSCs possess multiple abilities to exert immunosuppressive effects through various mechanisms. These mechanisms include the inhibition of T-cell proliferation and activation via the production of immunosuppressive molecules, such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β),[10] hepatocyte growth factor (HGF),[11] and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2).[12] Additionally, they influence the differentiation and function of dendritic cells (DCs)[13] and inhibit B-cell proliferation, differentiation, and immunoglobulin secretion through both direct cell–cell contact and soluble factors such as interleukin (IL)-10 and TGF-β.[14,15] Furthermore, MSCs have been reported to increase the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs)[16,17] and alter macrophage polarization.[18]

Given their diverse and potent immunoregulatory capabilities, the therapeutic potential of MSCs in aGVHD has been extensively explored. Studies have demonstrated that MSCs can ameliorate the excessive immune response that leads to tissue damage in transplant recipients, as evidenced in both murine models[19] and human patients.[20,21] However, despite encouraging preclinical results in various animal disease models, many registered clinical trials applying MSC therapy have fallen short of expectations.[22] Poor quality control and inconsistent characteristics of MSCs in terms of immunocompatibility, stability, heterogeneity, differentiation, and migratory capacity have contributed to the failure of MSCs in clinical trials.[23–25]

MSCs exhibit heterogeneity at multiple levels, including variability between donors, differences across tissues, and distinctions within individual tissues and cells.[26] Recent studies utilizing single-cell resolution have identified distinct MSC subpopulations that demonstrate enhanced capacities for adipogenic differentiation and immune modulation.[27] In our previous study, we successfully identified tissue-specific and conserved MSC subpopulations, elucidating the key molecular and signaling pathways that govern their biological functions, including immune regulation and lineage differentiation. Importantly, genes related to the extracellular matrix (ECM), particularly those associated with the core matrisome, play a significant role in MSC heterogeneity across different donors and tissues, even within the same tissue. For example, matrix metalloproteinase 1 (MMP1) and microfibril-associated protein 2 (MFAP2) have been shown to negatively regulate immune-modulating genes such as indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PDL1), underscoring their influence on MSC immune functions.[28]

Keratins play a critical role in regulating cellular stiffness by modulating the cytoskeletal architecture and influencing the mechanical properties of cells.[29] This regulation can enhance the adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation of porcine adipose-derived stem cells (pASCs).[30] Among keratins, keratin 8 (KRT8) is particularly recognized for its essential role in maintaining the structural integrity and resilience of epithelial cells. However, its contributions extend well beyond serving as a cellular scaffold.[31,32] Recent research has revealed that KRT8 significantly contributes to immune regulation, effectively orchestrating a delicate balance within the immune system.[33] It modulates the expression and secretion of cytokines and regulates inflammasome activity, playing a vital role in controlling inflammation and tumorigenesis.[34] Additionally, KRT8 protects epithelial cells from apoptosis induced by cellular stress, thereby preserving the integrity of critical barriers.[35,36] Moreover, KRT8 influences the behavior of immune cells, impacting their migration and adhesion, which helps shape the immune landscape at epithelial boundaries.[37] Notably, mutations or dysfunctions in KRT8 have been associated with autoimmune diseases,[34,35] highlighting its crucial role in maintaining immune tolerance.

Previous studies have indicated that KRT8 expression is elevated during the transition of MSCs into epithelial-like cells,[38] suggesting a critical role in lineage specification. This expression pattern not only underscores KRT8 as a marker for identifying specific MSC subsets but also reflects the stemness and plasticity of these cells. Moreover, the influence of KRT8 on cell migration and adhesion is essential for the ability of these proteins to bind to and repair damaged tissues.[39,40] Recently, our group demonstrated that KRT8 is expressed predominantly on HUC-MSCs,[28] suggesting that it is a lineage marker of HUC-MSCs. However, whether KRT8 regulates the immunomodulatory effect of HUC-MSCs is unknown. Our study sought to elucidate the immunomodulatory mechanisms of KRT8 expression on HUC-MSCs in preventing aGVHD and evaluate their impact on GVL activity.

Methods

Mice

BALB/c (H-2Kd) and C57BL/6J (H-2Kb) mice were purchased from Vital River (Beijing, China). All the mice were maintained in a pathogen-free room at Xinqiao Hospital of Army Medical University (Chongqing, China). All murine experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the animal care and use committee of Xinqiao Hospital of Army Medical University (Approval number: AMUWE2020544).

Cell culture

Human umbilical cord MSCs (HUC-MSCs) were purchased from iCell (Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM/F12 (GIBCO Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. A20-luc cells were purchased from Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 (GIBCO) supplemented with 10% FBS and 0.05 mmol/L 2-Mer (GIBCO) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. When the cell density reached approximately 80%, the cells were dissociated with or without trypsin and inoculated into T25 cell culture flasks for other experiments. HUC-MSCs were identified by detecting cell surface phenotypes and their multipotent potential for differentiating into adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic lineages and were maintained as previously described.[41]

The detailed methods used for lentiviral vector construction, packaging, and transduction of HUC-MSCs; quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR); Western blot analysis; enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA); SA-β-gal staining; transwell assay; T-cell proliferation assay; flow cytometry; fluorescence detection for in vivo tracking of HUC-MSCs; single-cell transcriptomic data of human MSCs; murine GVHD and GVL model; and liver organoid-T-cell coculture model are provided in the Supplementary Materials, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Body weight, diarrhea, cutaneous damage scoring, GVHD incidence, and survival in different groups were compared using the rank sum test or the log-rank test, appropriately. Comparisons of three means were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance for multiple comparisons, whereas comparisons of two means were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test (Prism [version 6.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA]). P values <0.05 were considered significant.

Results

A cross-tissue MSC landscape at the single-cell level to identify KRT8+ HUC-MSC subpopulations with superior therapeutic functions

In our previous research, we systematically analyzed the heterogeneity of MSCs derived from four different tissues, namely, bone marrow (BM), umbilical cord, dermis, and adipose tissue, at the single-cell level. Our findings highlighted significant differences in the tissue-specific characteristics of these MSCs.[28] The results of functional validation experiments revealed that, compared with MSCs from other sources, HUC-MSCs presented enhanced immunosuppressive capabilities.[19] Clinical studies further supported the superior efficacy of HUC-MSCs in treating aGVHD.[41] However, effective methodologies for identifying and characterizing subpopulations within HUC-MSCs that account for their functional advantages remain undeveloped.

To address this, we performed quality control and data integration, establishing a comprehensive MSC landscape comprising 256,709 single-cell transcriptomes from nine distinct human tissue sources, including adipose tissue, amniotic membrane, BM, deciduous incisors, dermis, endometrium, human embryonic stem cell-derived lines, oral mucosa, and umbilical cord. This analysis reaffirmed the strong tissue-specific signatures exhibited by MSCs, with little similarity observed between cells from different tissues, as demonstrated through unsupervised clustering using the Leiden algorithm in the Scanpy toolkit (v1.10.2) (https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/en/1.10.x/) [Supplementary Figure 1A, B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Further differential expression analysis identified KRT8 as a specific marker predominantly found in MSCs from the umbilical cord and amniotic membrane tissues. Notably, the KRT8 expression level varied across HUC-MSC subpopulations [Supplementary Figure 1C, D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12].

Further unsupervised clustering analysis of HUC-MSCs revealed two subpopulations with high KRT8 expression (KRT8+1 and KRT8+2) and one subpopulation with low KRT8 expression (KRT8lo) [Supplementary Figure 1E, F, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Gene ontology (GO) analysis highlighted significant functional heterogeneity between the KRT8+ and KRT8lo subpopulations [Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Notably, the differentially expressed genes between these subpopulations revealed distinct functional enrichment, with genes in the KRT8+ subpopulation associated with pathways related to the cell cycle, cell cycle checkpoints, and hemostasis. In contrast, the KRT8lo subpopulation was enriched in lipid metabolism pathways.

An examination of the top 10 differentially expressed genes revealed that the KRT8+ subpopulation presented elevated levels of serpin family E member 1 (SERPINE1), Caveolin-1 (CAV1), and galectin 1 (LGALS1). Conversely, the KRT8+ subpopulation presented notably lower expression levels of apolipoprotein E (APOE), matrix metallopeptidase 2 (MMP2), and complement system genes, such as C1S and C1R. Previous studies have suggested that SERPINE1 is associated with the maintenance of cancer cell stemness,[42] whereas CAV1 and LGALS1 are known to enhance the immunosuppressive functions of MSCs.[43] In addition, APOE is implicated in promoting cellular senescence by destabilizing heterochromatin through the degradation of the nuclear lamina and heterochromatin-associated proteins,[44] whereas MMP2 is linked to the development of age-related diseases and cellular senescence.[45] Moreover, our analysis revealed that KRT8+ subpopulations presented significantly reduced expression of inflammatory-related genes, such as IL-1R, and lower levels of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules (HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C), with little effect on the MHC-I molecule HLA-F and the major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) molecule HLA-DA [Supplementary Figures 3, 4A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12], indicating that KRT8+ subpopulations possess lower immunogenicity.

Then, we employed pySCENIC (https://github.com/aertslab/pySCENIC) to predict the transcription factor (TF) activity of the KRT8+ and KRT8lo subpopulations. The results indicated that critical TFs, such as sry-box transcription factor 5 (SOX5) and Yin Yang 1 (YY1), which are instrumental in maintaining stemness,[46,47] were significantly activated in the KRT8+ subpopulation [Supplementary Figure 4B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Additionally, we observed increased activity of cell cycle regulators, including E2F1, E2F7, E2F8,[48] and breast cancer associated gene 1,[49] indicating that KRT8+ cells possess increased proliferative potential and regulatory capacity for cell cycle progression and stemness. In contrast, the KRT8lo subpopulation exhibited increased activity of TFs related to inflammation and senescence, such as HIF1A,[50] HMGA1, STAT5B, STAT6, RFX2, and IRF3,[51] highlighting the potential role of KRT8 in modulating stemness, senescence, and inflammatory pathways. To further explore the stemness potential of KRT8+ HUC-MSCs, we performed developmental trajectory analysis and confirmed that this subpopulation exhibited significantly greater stemness than KRT8lo cells did [Supplementary Figure 4C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Our trajectory analysis using DDRTree revealed gradual upregulation of genes associated with MSC self-renewal, such as CAV1 and SERPINE1, along with downregulation of genes linked to aging and inflammation, including MMP2, IL1R1, and HLA-C, in KRT8+ HUC-MSCs compared with KRT8lo HUC-MSCs [Supplementary Figure 4D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12].

Furthermore, the comparative analysis of the KRT8+1 and KRT8+2 subpopulations revealed distinct biological characteristics through differential gene expressions, functional pathway enrichment, and marker gene profiling. The dot plot highlighted that the KRT8+1 subpopulation exhibited elevated expression levels of cell cycle regulators (e.g., CCND1 and MAP2K2) and ubiquitin-related genes (e.g., UBE2M), whereas the KRT8+2 subpopulation showed enrichment in extracellular matrix components (e.g., DCN and LUM) and metabolic enzymes (e.g., CYB5A) [Supplementary Figure 4E, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. The volcano plot further supported these differences [Supplementary Figure 4F, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. GO and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed distinct biological characteristics between the KRT8+1 and KRT8+2 subpopulations, with enrichment in mitotic regulation, protein degradation, nuclear transport, autophagy, mitochondrial processes, cell cycle, DNA repair, and neurodegenerative pathways [Supplementary Figure 4G, H, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Collectively, these findings indicate that KRT8+ HUC-MSCs display increased stemness and reduced senescence and inflammation. These characteristics may increase their potential for advanced therapeutic applications in aGVHD.

KRT8 overexpression in HUC-MSCs enhances their therapeutic efficacy in preventing aGVHD while preserving GVL activity

To further investigate how KRT8 influences the functionality of HUC-MSCs, we transduced the cells with lentiviruses carrying the EGFP (HUC-MSCs-GFP) and KRT8 (HUC-MSCs-KRT8) genes. The transduction was successful, as evidenced by a robust green, fluorescent signal in more than 90% of the cells [Supplementary Figure 5A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Real-time quantitative PCR (RT–qPCR) confirmed a tenfold increase in KRT8 mRNA levels in the modified MSCs [Supplementary Figure 5B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12], and Western blot analysis revealed a significant increase in KRT8 protein expression [Supplementary Figure 5C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Flow cytometry confirmed that HUC-MSCs-KRT8 cells retained positive markers for CD90, CD105, and CD73 and were negative for CD34, CD11b, CD19, CD45, and HLA-DR [Supplementary Figure 5D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. These results indicate that KRT8 overexpression did not alter the fundamental characteristics of HUC-MSCs.

To test the impact of KRT8 on the immunoregulatory functions of HUC-MSCs in the setting of allo-HSCT, we tested whether the injection of KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs could prevent aGVHD and preserve GVL activity. Accordingly, irradiated BALB/c recipients were engrafted with splenocytes (2.5 × 106) and bone marrow cells (5 × 106) from BALB/c donors or major histocompatibility complex–mismatched C57BL/6 donors. Recipients given splenocytes and bone marrow cells from BALB/c donors were used as GVHD-free controls. Recipients were treated with HUC-MSCs-KRT8 or HUC-MSCs-GFP control on Days 0, 9 and 15 after allo-HSCT. Consistent with our previous report,[41] the administration of HUC-MSCs-GFP significantly improved the survival of recipients [Figure 1A]. More importantly, HUC-MSCs-KRT8 treatment further improved the overall survival rate and reduced damage to GVHD target tissues (liver, small intestine, and colon) compared with HUC-MSCs-GFP treatment [Figure 1A–C].

To assess the effect of HUC-MSCs-KRT8 treatment on GVL activity, BALB/c recipients were given T cell depleted bone marrow (TCD-BM) cells with or without splenocytes from C57BL/6 mice and inoculated with luciferase-transfected A20 cells (A20-Luc, 5 × 106/mouse, intraperitoneal) at the time of allo-HSCT. A20-Luc tumor cells grew rapidly in recipients engrafted with TCD-BM, and all the mice died from uncontrolled tumor growth [Figure 1D–F]. The mice without HUC-MSC treatment eliminated the tumors quickly; however, all the recipients died of severe aGVHD within 25 days after allo-HSCT [Figure 1D–F]. A20-Luc tumor cells grew transiently in human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells transduced with a GFP-expressing lentiviral vector (HUC-MSC-GFP)-treated recipients engrafted with splenocytes and TCD-BM cells, but 80% of the recipients died with aGVHD within 30 days after allo-HSCT. A20-Luc tumor cells also grew transiently in HUC-MSCs-KRT8-treated recipients but were eliminated by Day 24, and 50% of the HUC-MSCs-KRT8-treated mice survived for more than 30 days without tumor relapse [Figure 1D–F]. Taken together, these results show that the infusion of HUC-MSCs-KRT8 effectively controlled GVHD while preserving GVL activity mediated by alloreactive T cells.

KRT8 overexpression increased h3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) in HUC-MSCs

To understand the genomic impacts of KRT8 overexpression, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on both HUC-MSCs-KRT8 and HUC-MSCs-GFP cells. The analysis revealed 212 differentially expressed genes, with 167 upregulated and 45 downregulated genes in HUC-MSCs-KRT8. Consistent with previous results [Supplementary Figure 5D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12], the MSC markers NT5E (CD73), THY1 (CD90), and ENDOGLIN (CD105) did not significantly change [Supplementary Figure 6A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. The upregulated genes were associated with DNA transcription and repair (e.g., NFKB2, PPP1R15A, PER1, and DDB2), cell cycle regulation (PLK2), and p53-associated pathways (e.g., CDIP1, PIDD1). The downregulated genes were linked to cellular senescence and inflammatory responses (e.g., Interleukin 6 cytokine family signal transducer, HMGA2, TNFAIP6, XIST, PTPN13, and DYNC2H1) [Supplementary Figure 6B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12].

In our analysis of gene expression and associated pathways, GO enrichment revealed that the genes whose expression levels were upregulated were associated primarily with processes, such as transcription based on DNA templates, protein and DNA binding, and chromatin organization [Supplementary Figure 6C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. The KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the differentially expressed genes were connected mainly to pathways related to cell survival, apoptosis, and cellular senescence. Key pathways, such as the p53, mitogen-activated protein kinas (MAPK), Ras, and Phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) signaling pathways were notably involved [Supplementary Figure 6D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Importantly, KRT8 overexpression resulted in significant changes in histone proteins, with gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealing an increase in pathways related to H3K27me3 [Supplementary Figure 6E–J, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Specifically, the pathway linked to the Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) complex, which involves enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2)’s trimethylation of nucleosomes associated with the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A) promoter, was markedly elevated in HUC-MSCs-KRT8 compared with HUC-MSCs-GFP [Supplementary Figure 6K, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. CDKN2A, which encodes the critical proteins p16INK4a and p14ARF, is vital for cell cycle regulation and cellular senescence.[52,53] Moreover, KRT8 overexpression downregulated several KEGG pathways related to lipid metabolism, including fatty acid biosynthesis, fatty acid metabolism and the PPAR signaling pathway [Supplementary Figure 6L–N, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. The disruption of lipid metabolism is recognized as a sign of cellular senescence, suggesting that the impact of KRT8 overexpression extends beyond structural integrity to encompass metabolic and epigenetic changes. Notably, this may lead to the silencing of senescence-related genes in HUC-MSCs through EZH2-mediated histone H3K27 trimethylation.

Further investigation revealed that C2H2-type zinc finger (ZF-C2H2) TFs could modulate cellular senescence by regulating the expression of genes implicated in cell cycle control, apoptosis, and stress responses. These factors may influence the expression of key regulators, such as p53 and p21, as well as components of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP).[54–56] Our findings revealed significant enrichment of the ZF-C2H2 TF family in HUC-MSCs-KRT8 [Supplementary Figure 6O, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that KRT8 overexpression profoundly influences the genomic and epigenetic profiles of HUC-MSCs. The upregulation of genes associated with DNA repair, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis, combined with alterations in lipid metabolism and modifications to H3K27me3, suggests that KRT8 overexpression may increase the resilience of HUC-MSCs and reshape their dynamics concerning cellular senescence.

EZH2-mediated H3K27me3 controls the senescence of HUC-MSCs

To further confirm the bulk RNA-Seq findings at the protein level, we treated HUC-MSCs-KRT8 with an EZH2 inhibitor (XNW5004), which is known to reduce global H3K27me3 levels.[57] We then performed Western blotting analysis to evaluate H3K27me3 protein expression. Consistent with the RNA-Seq results, KRT8 overexpression increased H3K27me3 levels in HUC-MSCs compared with those in control cells, whereas the EZH2 inhibitor significantly reduced H3K27me3 expression [Figure 2A]. Additionally, we examined cellular senescence and the SASP by assessing the proteins p16INK4A, p21, and p53. KRT8 overexpression decreased the expression of these proteins [Figure 2B]; however, this effect was reversed by the EZH2 inhibitor, as indicated by increased p16INK4A levels [Figure 2C]. The SA-β-gal assay further suggested that KRT8 overexpression suppressed HUC-MSCs senescence, an effect blocked by the EZH2 inhibitor [Figure 2D].

Inhibition of T-cell proliferation is commonly reported as a characteristic of MSCs, independent of their tissue of origin.[58] To explore the role of H3K27me3 and reduce SASP in inhibiting T-cell activation, we cocultured Thy1.2+ T cells with HUC-MSCs-KRT8 or HUC-MSCs-GFP. Consistent with previous findings, both HUC-MSCs-KRT8 and HUC-MSCs-GFP inhibited the proliferation of CD3/CD28-activated Thy1.2+ T cells on Days 3 and 4 after coculture in vitro [Figure 2E, F]. Notably, the proliferation rate of T cells was significantly lower in the HUC-MSCs-KRT8 group than in the HUC-MSCs-GFP group [Figure 2E, F]. This suppressive effect of HUC-MSCs-KRT8 on both murine [Figure 2G, H] and human T cells [Figure 2I, J] could be partially reversed by the addition of the EZH2 inhibitor, indicating that KRT8 overexpression increased the immunosuppressive function of HUC-MSCs in an H3K27me3-dependent manner.

EZH2 controls the secretion of HGF by KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying H3K27me3-mediated maintenance of stemness and inhibition of the SASP, we examined the expression levels of molecules associated with the immunoregulatory function of HUC-MSCs by RT–qPCR. Our results indicate that, compared with HUC-MSCs-GFP, KRT8 overexpression in HUC-MSCs significantly increased the expression levels of HGF, CXCL5, TLR4, PGE2, and TLR6 [Figure 3A] while decreasing the levels of IL-6, TLR5, CCL-2, and TLR3 [Figure 3B]. Further analysis revealed a significant increase in the HGF concentration in the culture medium of HUC-MSCs-KRT8, which was reduced upon EZH2 inhibition [Figure 3C]. These findings suggest that KRT8 overexpression enhances HGF secretion through H3K27me3 modulation.

We then examined whether the increased secretion of HGF contributed to the suppression of T-cell proliferation. Coculturing HUC-MSCs-KRT8 or HUC-MSCs-GFP with anti-CD3/CD28 bead-activated T cells and adding an anti-HGF antibody to the culture medium revealed that HUC-MSCs-KRT8 had a stronger suppressive effect on murine T cells than did HUC-MSCs-GFP [Figure 3D, E]. This suppression was entirely reversed by the HGF-blocking antibody [Figure 3D, E]. A similar effect was observed in human PBMCs [Figure 3F, G], indicating that the enhanced immune suppression by HUC-MSCs-KRT8 is HGF dependent. Overall, KRT8 overexpression in HUC-MSCs enhances HGF secretion through H3K27me3 modulation, substantially strengthening the immunosuppressive capacity of these cells.

HGF from KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs mitigates liver damage

The therapeutic effect of MSCs largely depends on their ability to reach GVHD target organs.[59] To assess the migration potential of engineered HUC-MSCs in vitro, HUC-MSCs-KRT8 and HUC-MSCs-GFP were analyzed in a Transwell migration assay. As shown in Figure 4A, B, KRT8 overexpression significantly increased HUC-MSC migration. Furthermore, to examine the fate and migration pattern of HUC-MSCs in vivo, we injected allo-HSCT recipients with PKH26-labeled HUC-MSCs-KRT8 or HUC-MSCs-GFP on Days 0, 5 and 9 after allo-HSCT. Spleen, liver, lung, and intestinal tissues were collected on Day 16, and PKH26-labeled HUC-MSCs-KRT8 or HUC-MSCs-GFP were analyzed by fluorescence microscopy. Our observations confirmed that KRT8 overexpression significantly enhanced HUC-MSC migration into the liver in vivo, with no difference in other organs [Figure 4C, D, Supplementary Figure 7, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12].

To assess whether KRT8-promoted HGF secretion could mitigate alloreactive T-cell-induced liver damage, we established a coculture system of T cells and liver organoids, enabling live monitoring of interactions and potential cytotoxicity.[60] We observed that alloreactive T cells significantly reduced the viability of liver organoids, as indicated by the increase in caspase fluorescence intensity [Figure 4E, F]. However, liver organoids pulsed with HGF exhibited more than double the viability of those without HGF at 24 h, 48 h and 72 h after coculture initiation [Figure 4E, F], suggesting that HGF treatment provides significant protection to liver organoids against the cytotoxic actions of HLA-mismatched T cells. These findings indicate that KRT8 overexpression facilitates the migration of HUC-MSCs to the liver and suggest that elevated HGF levels effectively mitigate liver damage caused by alloreactive T cells, thereby preserving the structural integrity and functionality of liver organoids.

Discussion

In this study, we elucidated the distinct characteristics and functional advantages of KRT8+ HUC-MSC subpopulations and demonstrated their superior therapeutic effect in preventing aGVHD while preserving GVL activity. Our results not only confirm the safety and phenotypic stability of KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs but also indicate profound genomic and epigenetic modifications that underpin their improved therapeutic efficacy. Our initial investigations revealed pronounced heterogeneity among MSCs derived from various tissues, with KRT8+ HUC-MSCs exhibiting greater stemness and immunosuppressive capabilities than their KRT8lo counterparts. We also investigated the role of KRT8 in regulating the function of HUC-MSCs via lentiviral transduction. The therapeutic effects of KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs were validated in a clinically relevant mouse model of allo-HSCT. Compared with control recipients, recipients treated with KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs presented significantly reduced symptoms of aGVHD, improved survival rates, and preservation of target organ integrity. More importantly, the infusion of KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs did not impair GVL activity, further supporting their clinical translational potential.

The findings from our bulk RNA sequencing analysis highlight the intricate role of KRT8 overexpression in shaping the genomic landscape of HUC-MSCs. Identifying differentially expressed genes underscores the complexity of the cellular responses elicited by KRT8, which encompasses a diverse array of functions, from DNA transcription and repair to cell cycle regulation and senescence pathways. The preservation of MSC markers indicates that KRT8 overexpression does not perturb core stem cell identity but rather imposes significant genomic and metabolic changes that warrant further exploration. Notably, the engagement of pathways, including the p53, MAPK, and PI3K/AKT pathways, suggests that KRT8 might act as a critical mediator of stress responses in these cells, potentially enhancing their resilience to various stimuli. The observed downregulation of pathways linked to fatty acid metabolism implicates metabolic shifts that may accompany the impact of KRT8 on cellular senescence. Moreover, the strong correlation with histone modifications, particularly H3K27 trimethylation, highlights the role of KRT8 in modulating epigenetic landscapes that drive the silencing of senescence-related genes. The enrichment of ZF-C2H2 TFs raises compelling questions about their specific contributions to cellular processes and highlights the potential mechanisms through which KRT8 may influence stem cell behavior and longevity. Collectively, our results suggest that KRT8 overexpression fundamentally reshapes the genomic, metabolic, and epigenetic profiles of HUC-MSCs.

In addition, the immunomodulatory capacity of MSCs is well documented. Our results also demonstrated that KRT8 overexpression further augmented this effect. The enhanced immunosuppression ability was suggested by the inhibition of T-cell proliferation in KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs. This suppression was found to be partially mediated by H3K27me3 epigenetic modification, reinforcing the link between KRT8-induced epigenetic changes and improved immunoregulation. A key outcome of this enhanced immunoregulatory function is the increased secretion of HGF by KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs. HGF is known for its regenerative and anti-apoptotic properties, and our findings suggest that the administration of HGF confers substantial protective effects, as liver organoids exposed to HGF exhibited greater than double the viability through the 24- to 72-h period compared with those that were not treated. These findings indicate that HGF plays a critical role in mitigating T-cell-mediated liver damage; notably, this protective effect occurs after 3 days in vitro, further suggesting that HGF can provide long-term cytoprotective benefits regardless of prolonged alloreactive T-cell activity. Notably, the use of HGF-blocking antibodies reversed the immunosuppressive benefits of KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs, underscoring the centrality of HGF in this process. Moreover, our in vitro and in vivo migration assays demonstrated that KRT8 overexpression significantly enhances the migratory capacity of HUC-MSCs, particularly to the liver, a key target in aGVHD. This enhanced migration is likely due, in part, to increased HGF secretion, which has known chemotactic properties. This highlights the need for further exploration into the mechanisms underlying the protective role of HGF and its potential therapeutic applications in the setting of allo-HSCT.

Although these findings collectively underscore the functional interplay among KRT8, epigenetic regulation, and immunomodulation, certain mechanistic aspects remain to be fully elucidated. Notably, our transcriptomic analyses further resolved the KRT8+ MSC population into two distinct subpopulations, KRT8+1 and KRT8+2, which exhibited divergent gene expression profiles [Supplementary Figure 8A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. This transcriptional heterogeneity suggests that KRT8+ MSCs may comprise functionally distinct cellular states, warranting further investigation into their respective biological roles and contributions to immunomodulation. Although our functional studies provide robust evidence that the KRT8–EZH2–H3K27me3 pathway regulates cellular senescence; however, ATAC-seq analysis revealed no significant alterations in chromatin accessibility at the CDKN2A promoter [Supplementary Figure 8B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/D12]. These findings suggest that the observed effects may occur through mechanisms independent of changes in chromatin structure. Direct ChIP-seq analysis of H3K27me3 deposition and EZH2 occupancy at the CDKN2A promoter would provide more definitive evidence to strengthen these conclusions and clarify the precise molecular mechanism involved. Notably, our data revealed that the expression of KRT8 decreased in HUC-MSCs as the number of cells divided increased. The increased prevalence of KRT8 in early-passage HUC-MSCs indicates that these cells may possess superior stemness and immunosuppressive capabilities, which could be advantageous in clinical settings, especially in managing inflammatory conditions or promoting tissue regeneration. Understanding this decline could elucidate the underlying mechanisms governing HUC-MSC behavior and help inform strategies to optimize cell preparation protocols, ensuring that therapeutically relevant populations are utilized in clinical applications. Future research should focus on identifying the factors contributing to this decline, exploring methods to maintain or enhance the immunomodulatory characteristics of HUC-MSCs through subsequent passages, and determining the implications of such changes in their functional outcomes in vivo.

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence that KRT8 overexpression in HUC-MSCs enhances their therapeutic efficacy in aGVHD by stabilizing their phenotype, improving cellular resilience through genomic and epigenetic modifications, augmenting immunoregulatory functions, and facilitating targeted migration to damaged tissues. Our findings suggest that KRT8-overexpressing HUC-MSCs constitute a promising advanced therapy for aGVHD, offering the dual benefit of ameliorating aGVHD while preserving GVL effects. Integrating KRT8-driven genetic and epigenetic reprogramming sets a new benchmark for MSC-based interventions, potentially transforming the management of allo-HSCT-associated complications. Future clinical trials will be critical in translating these preclinical insights into therapeutic realities, aiming to increase patient outcomes and expand the therapeutic horizon of MSC applications.
