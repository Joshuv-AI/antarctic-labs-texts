Deep Neural Nets as Hamiltonians
Mike Winer, Boris Hanin

Abstract
Neural networks are complex functions of both their inputs and parameters. Much prior work in deep learning theory analyzes the distribution of network outputs at a fixed a set of inputs (e.g. a training dataset) over random initializations of the network parameters. The purpose of this article is to consider the opposite situation: we view a randomly initialized Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) as a Hamiltonian over its inputs. For typical realizations of the network parameters, we study the properties of the energy landscape induced by this Hamiltonian, focusing on the structure of near-global minimum in the limit of infinite width. Specifically, we use the replica trick to perform an exact analytic calculation giving the entropy (log volume of space) at a given energy. We further derive saddle point equations that describe the overlaps between inputs sampled iid from the Gibbs distribution induced by the random MLP. For linear activations we solve these saddle point equations exactly. But we also solve them numerically for a variety of depths and activation functions, including tanh , sin , ReLU \tanh,\sin,\text{ReLU} , and shaped non-linearities. We find even at infinite width a rich range of behaviors. For some non-linearities, such as sin \sin , for instance, we find that the landscapes of random MLPs exhibit full replica symmetry breaking, while shallow tanh \tanh and ReLU networks or deep shaped MLPs are instead replica symmetric.

The Replica Trick and Multilayer Perceptrons

3.1
Defining the Multilayer Perceptron

Our Hamiltonian
H
is a complicated function of the inputs
x
. We will define

\displaystyle z_{i}^{(1)}(x)\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{N^{(0)}}W^{(1)}_{ij}x_{j}\quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq N^{(1)}

(3)

\displaystyle z_{i}^{(\ell+1)}(x)\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{N^{(\ell)}}W^{(\ell+1)}_{ij}\sigma\left(z^{(\ell)}_{j}\right)\quad\text{for }1\leq\ell\leq L-1,1\leq i\leq N^{(\ell+1)}

(4)

\displaystyle H(x)\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{N^{(L)}}W^{(L+1)}_{i}\sigma(z^{(L)}_{i})

(5)

In words, our function is calculated in terms of
layers
. The
\ell
th layer has width
N^{(\ell)}
, and is calculated in terms of the previous layer. We will take the limits where all of the
N^{(\ell)}
are proportional to a single large parameter
N
, which goes to infinity

N^{(\ell)}=a^{(\ell)}N,\quad\ell=1,\ldots,L,\quad N\rightarrow\infty.

We will call
\sigma(z^{(\ell)}_{i})
the activation of the
i
th neuron in layer
\ell
, and
z^{(\ell)}_{i}
the preactivation. Note that
W^{(\ell)}
is an
N^{(\ell)}\times N^{(\ell-1)}
matrix. We take its entries to be iid centered Gaussians with variance
1/N^{(\ell-1)}
, except for
W^{(L+1)}
whose entries have variance
1.
This convention is chosen so that the scale of pre-activations
z_{i}^{(\ell)}
is independent of
N
, but the overall Hamiltonian will be extensive in
N
.

3.2
Warmup: Single System Partition Function and Three Kinds of Averages

By definition, for a given realization of the network weights
W_{ij}^{(\ell)},
the partition function of a single system is

Z_{\beta}=\int\exp(-\beta\Htot(x))\frac{d^{N^{(0)}}x}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{N^{(0)}}}=\int\exp(-\beta H(x))\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\norm{x}^{2}\right)\frac{d^{N^{(0)}}x}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{N^{(0)}}}.

(6)

This partition function can also be thought of as the expected value of
\exp(-\beta H(x))
when
x
is drawn from a multivariate Gaussian. Since we are selecting a random neural net with random weights,
Z
is a random variable. At this point, it is worth making clear the three types of average we are juggling:

•

Disorder Averages
: There are averages over the weights of the neural nets, which will be denoted by
\mathbb{E}
. It is in this context that it makes sense to talk about the average partition function of a given architecture, average free energy, or the Kernel (covariance between outputs of two fixed inputs). The, terminology disorder average is borrowed from condensed matter physics where random variations in physical samples results in random Hamiltonians.

•

Thermodynamic Averages
: These are averages over the thermodynamic ensemble, i.e. with respect to the Boltzmann distribution

p(x)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{N^{(0)}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\norm{x}^{2}-\beta H(x)\right).

In this sense, it makes sense to talk about the average value of
x
or
H(x)
for a fixed neural net. These two types of averages can also be combined, one can take
\mathbb{E}\left(\int dxp(x)\bullet\right)
. This is the disorder average of the thermodynamic average of a quantity.

•

Gaussian Averages
: These are averages over a Gaussian input distribution

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{N^{(0)}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\norm{x}^{2}\right).

These averages are just mathematical tricks, we can make use of the fact that the partition function for normalizing thermodynamic averages is the average of
e^{-\beta H(x)}
under this type of average. We will sometimes combine the Gaussian and Disorder average, since the Partition function is the disorder average of the Gaussian average of
e^{-\beta H}
.

We will be interested in
\mathbb{E}(Z_{\beta}^{n})
, the value of the
n
replica partition function

Z_{\beta}
averaged over the microscopic weights. Even more important to us, however, will be
\mathbb{E}(\log Z_{\beta})
because if we are interested in something like the disorder average of the thermodynamic average of the energy
H(x)
, this can be expressed as

\text{disorder average of }H(x)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\mathbb{E}(\log Z_{\beta})\propto\mathbb{E}\left(\int H(x)\exp(-\beta H(x))\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\norm{x}^{2}\right)d^{N^{(0)}}x\right).

(7)

Other similar observables, besides energy, are also best understood in terms of
\mathbb{E}(\log Z_{\beta})
. In some areas, one can make the approximation
\mathbb{E}(\log Z)\approx\log\mathbb{E}(Z)
. However, as we will see, this approximation is not even close for the Hamiltonians studied in this work. As we explain in the next section this is true even in the very simplest Hamiltonians we consider. In order to evaluate
\mathbb{E}(\log Z_{\beta})
we rely on a method called the
replica trick
, which we introduce next.

3.3
The Replica Trick For Rookies

The replica trick relies on two key insights, both simple in their way. The first is that

\mathbb{E}(\log Z_{\beta})=\lim_{n\to 0}\partial_{n}\mathbb{E}(Z_{\beta}^{n})=\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left(Z_{\beta}^{n}\right).

(8)

The second is that for positive integer
n

\mathbb{E}\left(Z_{\beta}^{n}\right)=\int\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{a=1}^{n}H(x_{a})\right)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{n}x_{a}^{2}\right)\frac{d^{nN^{(0)}}x_{a}}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{nN^{(0)}}}.

(9)

The
n
independent vectors
x_{a}
, each independent of the others conditioned on the value of the network parameters, are called the
n
replicas. In words, this equation is saying that the
n
th power of the partition function of a supersystem consisting of
n
copies, or replicas, of our original neural net acting on
x
. The surprising insight of the replica trick is that one can find a formula for equation
9
directly computing saddle points equations for the effective action in (
9
) and then analytically continuing
n\to 0
.

The rest of this section will be spent working through an extremely simple example so as to illustrate two key ideas: the usefulness of
overlap matrices
, and the power of the
saddle-point approximation
. The Hamiltonian we will consider in this section is simply

H(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}x_{i},

(10)

where the elements of
h
are drawn iid from the standard normal distribution. This is technically a special case of the multilayer perceptron Hamiltonian discussed earlier, with no hidden layers and a linear activation function. The partition function of this system is then

Z_{\beta}=\int\exp\left(-\beta h\cdot x-\norm{x}^{2}/2\right)\frac{d^{N}x}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{N}}=\exp\left(\beta^{2}h^{2}/2\right).

(11)

Hence,

\log\mathbb{E}(Z_{\beta})=-\frac{N}{2}\log\left(1-\beta^{2}\right),

(12)

while

\mathbb{E}(\log Z)=N\beta^{2}/2.

(13)

These two quantities are obviously different from each other! On the other hand, let us fix
n\geq 1
and compute the replicated partition function:

Z_{\beta}^{n}=\int\exp\left(-\sum_{a=1}^{n}\beta h\cdot x-x^{2}/2\right)\frac{d^{nN}x}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{nN}}

For this simple system, we can of course calculate
\mathbb{E}(Z_{\beta}^{n})
directly for arbitrary
h
, but for illustrative purposes we will take the
h
integral first. This gives

\mathbb{E}(Z_{\beta}^{n})=\int\exp\left(-\frac{N\beta^{2}}{2}\sum_{a,b=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}x_{a}\cdot x_{b}-\sum_{a=1}^{n}x^{2}/2\right)\frac{d^{nN}x}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{nN}}

(14)

By integrating out
h
, we have correlated the different replicas. This is reflected in the appearance of the matrix
Q_{ab}=\frac{1}{N}x_{a}\cdot x_{b}
, which is called the
overlap matrix
and is a measure of how similarity between the configurations of replicas
a
and
b
. To further simplify the expression in (
14
) we will make use of the standard fact that

f(u)=\frac{2\pi}{N}\int D\Sigma DQ\exp(N\Sigma(Q-u))f(Q)

(15)

for an essentially arbitrary function
f
, where the contour for the
\Sigma
integral is parallel to the imaginary axis. In terms of the saddle point approximation we will perform later, this actually means we will be minimizing with respect to
\Sigma
, not maximizing. This allows us to write

\mathbb{E}(Z_{\beta}^{n})=\int D\Sigma DQ\exp\left(-\frac{N\beta^{2}}{2}\sum_{a,b=1}^{N}Q_{ab}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}\Sigma_{ab}\left(NQ_{ab}-x_{a}\cdot x_{b}\right)-\sum_{a=1}^{n}\norm{x_a}^{2}/2\right)\frac{d^{nN}x}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{nN}},

(16)

where we drop some pesky factors for
\sqrt{2\pi/N}^{n^{2}}
which will go to 1 anyways. We will adopt the notation from physics literature, where
\Sigma
is considered a
self-energy
. However, we note that in neural network literature this quantity is sometimes written
\hat{Q}
, where it is considered a
dual kernel
. At this point, the
x
integral is purely Gaussian and we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(Z_{\beta}^{n})&=\int D\Sigma DQ\exp\left(S[\Sigma,Q]\right),\\
S&=-\frac{N\beta^{2}}{2}\sum_{a,b=1}^{N}Q_{ab}+\frac{N}{2}\sum_{ab}\Sigma_{ab}Q_{ab}-\frac{N}{2}\log\det\left(\Sigma+\delta_{ab}\right).\end{split}

(17)

Notice that the quantity in the exponent of (
17
) is proportional to
N
. For large
N
, we can use steepest descent (a generalization of Laplace’s method) to argue that the integral should be approximated by
\exp\left(S(\Sigma^{*},Q^{*})\right)
, where
\Sigma^{*},Q^{*}
is a stationary point of
S
. To find the stationary point we take derivatives to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial\Sigma_{ab}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N}{2}(\Sigma+\delta_{ab})^{-1}_{ab}+\frac{N}{2}Q_{ab},\qquad\frac{\partial S}{\partial Q_{ab}}=\frac{N}{2}\Sigma_{ab}-\frac{N\beta^{2}}{2}.

Setting these both to zero we get

Q_{ab}^{*}=\left(\delta_{ab}-\beta^{2}\right)^{-1}.

(18)

In other words, we need to invert the matrix which is equal to the identity minus
\beta^{2}
times the all
1
’s matrix. This matrix has one eigenvector which is proportional to
(1,1,1,...,1)
, whose eigenvalues is
1-n\beta^{2}
. The other
n-1
eigenvalues are degenerate, and equal to 1. Therefore

Q_{ab}^{*}=\delta_{ab}+\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{1-n\beta^{2}}-1\right).

(19)

Plugging this back into the action and using the steepest descent approximation gives

\log\mathbb{E}(Z_{\beta}^{n})=-\frac{nN\beta^{2}}{2}\frac{1}{1-n\beta^{2}}+\frac{nN}{2}-\frac{nN}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{1-n\beta^{2}}\right)\right)-\frac{N}{2}\log(1-n\beta^2).

(20)

This simplifies a quite bit to

\log\mathbb{E}(Z^{n})=-\frac{N}{2}\log(1-n\beta^2).

(21)

The key point is that this agrees with our
n=1
calculation in (
12
) and also, by taking
n\rightarrow 0
in (
8
) with the
n=0
result in equation (
13
).

Our strategy for this simple problem was to rewrite our partition function in terms of an
n\times n
overlap matrix
Q
and Lagrange multiplier
\Sigma
, get a large action proportional to system size, solve the stationary point of this action, and then take the
n\to 0
limit. It is this same basic strategy we will use to compute
\mathbb{E}(Z_{\beta}^{n})
and
\mathbb{E}\left(\log(Z_\beta)\right)
for the multilayer perceptron below.

3.3.1
An Aside on Replica Symmetry

The action in (
17
) has an
S_{n}
permutation symmetry, where switching any two replicas leaves the action unchanged. This is, in fact, a general feature of any system: when you have a collection of identical objects, switching their configurations won’t change the energy or entropy. In the case of the toy model in this subsection, not only did the action and corresponding equations of motion respect this symmetry, the unique solution does as well. This is not always the case. Systems from the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model
[
56
,
57
]
to hard spheres
[
58
]
to quantum optical systems
[
59
]
exhibit a spontaneous breaking of this symmetry. This goes by the name
Replica Symmetry Breaking
, or RSB.

Although it may seem like a strange artifact of mathematical trickery, RSB actually has a clear physical interpretation: for a complicated energy function, there are several competing scenarios for the nature of the local minima. There maybe a unique local minimum, as one can manifestly see in the toy problem from the previous section. There may also be exponentially many local minima, with the the Boltzmann measure spread out between them. Or there may be many local minima, but the Boltzmann measure is condensed in just a few. In the first scenario, every pair of replicas is in the same minima. In the second, every pair will be in different minima. It is this last scenario that leads to RSB. Two replicas have a noticeable probability of inhabiting the same minima, or of different minima, thus different overlaps can be different. We will see later in this paper that certain activation functions can lead to the rich phenomenology of RSB (see Figure
2
).

3.4
The Replicated Action

In this section, we will derive a path integral expression (i.e an explicit action) for the average value of
Z_{\beta}^{n}
:

\mathbb{E}\left(Z_{\beta}^{n}\right)=\int\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{a=1}^{n}H(x_{a})\right)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\norm{x_a}^{2}\right)\frac{d^{nN^{(0)}}x_{a}}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{nN^{(0)}}}.

(22)

To do this, we define an
n\times n
overlap matrix
Q^{(\ell)}
in each layer:

\displaystyle Q_{ab}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{(0)}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{(0)}}x_{ia}x_{ib},\qquad Q^{(\ell)}_{ab}=\frac{1}{N^{(\ell)}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{(\ell)}}\sigma(z^{(\ell)}_{ia})\sigma(z^{(\ell)}_{ib})\quad\text{for }1\leq\ell\leq L

Notice that the integral in (
22
) requires us to compute the Laplace transform of replica summed Hamiltonian
\sum_{a}H(x_{a})
. Our strategy for performing this computation starts by noticing that while
\sum_{a}H(x_{a})
is a complicated function of
x_{a}
, it is in fact a simple function of the overlap matrix
Q^{(L)}
. We will then need to compute the distribution of
Q^{(L)}
as a function of
x_{a}
. This is again a complicated distribution. However, just as in the toy model from §
3.3
, will introduce dual overlap matrices
\Sigma^{(\ell)}
to efficiently compute the distribution of
Q{(\ell)}
given
Q^{(\ell-1)}
for all
\ell=L,\ldots,1
. Finally, the distribution of
Q^{(1)}
given
x_{a}
is simple and we will obtain an explicit action for
\mathbb{E}(Z_{\beta}^{n})
.

To implement this plan, the key insight is that conditional on a given
Q^{(\ell)}_{ab}
, the preactivations of the next layer
z^{(\ell+1)}_{ia}
are jointly Gaussian with mean 0 and covariance

\textrm{Cov}\left(z^{(\ell+1)}_{ia},z^{(\ell+1)}_{jb}\right)=\delta_{ij}Q^{(\ell)}_{ab}.

Hence,

\mathbb{P}(Q^{(\ell+1)}|Q^{(\ell)})=\int D^{N^{(\ell+1)}}z^{(\ell+1)}D\Sigma^{(\ell+1)}_{ab}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}\Sigma^{(\ell+1)}_{ab}\left(N^{(\ell+1)}Q^{(\ell+1)}_{ab}-\sum_{i}\sigma(z^{(\ell+1)}_{ia})\sigma(z^{(\ell+1)}_{ib})\right)\right),

where the integral is over the space of Lagrange multiplier
n\times n
matrices
\Sigma^{(\ell+1)}_{ab}
and the measure
D^{N^{(\ell+1)}}z^{(\ell+1)}
is shorthand for the Gaussian integrand

D^{N^{(\ell+1)}}z^{(\ell+1)}=\frac{d^{nN^{(\ell+1)}}z^{(\ell+1)}_{ia}}{\sqrt{\det 2\pi Q^{(\ell)}}^{N^{(\ell)}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{ab}z^{(\ell+1)}_{ia}\left(Q^{(\ell)}_{ab}\right)^{-1}z^{(\ell+1)}_{ib}\right).

To simply the notation, let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma_{ab};Q_{ab}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}z_{a}\left(Q_{ab}\right)^{-1}z_{b}+\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{ab}\sigma(z_{a})\sigma(z_{b})\right)\frac{d^{n}z}{\sqrt{\det 2\pi Q}}

\displaystyle=\int\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}\Sigma_{ab}\sigma(z_{a})\sigma(z_{b})\right)Dz

(23)

to be the expected value of
\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{ab}\sigma(z_{a})\sigma(z_{b})\right)
where
z
is a Gaussian with covariance
Q
. Now we can write

\mathbb{P}(Q^{(\ell+1)}|Q^{(\ell)})=\int D\Sigma^{(\ell+1)}_{ab}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}N^{(\ell+1)}\sum_{ab}\Sigma^{(\ell+1)}_{ab}Q^{(\ell+1)}_{ab}+N^{(\ell+1)}\log\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma^{(\ell+1)};Q^{(\ell)}}\right).

Just as we derived the distribution of
Q^{(\ell+1)}
conditioned on
Q^{(\ell)}
, we can also derive the distribution of
H
conditioned on
Q^{(L)}
. If we condition on a given value of
Q^{(L)}_{ab}
, the
n
variables
H(x_{ia})
are joint Gaussian with covariance
N^{(L)}Q^{(L)}_{ab}
. We thus have

\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{n}(b)|Q^{(L)}_{ab}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(-b\sum_{a}H(x_{ia}\right)|Q^{(L)}_{ab}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}N^{(L)}\sum_{ab}Q^{(L)}_{ab}\right)

We now have a way to get the distribution of
H
conditioned on
Q^{(L)}
and the distribution of
Q^{(\ell+1)}
conditioned on
Q^{(\ell)}
. All we need is an expression for the probability distribution of
Q^{(0)}
in the Gaussian input distribution, and we can derive an expression for the disorder+Gaussian average for
\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{a=1}^{n}H(x_{a})\right)
, i.e. the replicated partition function. We can write

\begin{split}p(Q^{(0)})=\int DxD\Sigma^{(0)}_{ab}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}\Sigma^{(0)}_{ab}\left(N^{(0)}Q^{(0)}_{ab}-\sum_{i}x_{ia}x_{ib})\right)\right)\\
=\int D\Sigma^{(0)}_{ab}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}N^{(0)}\left(\tr\Sigma^{(0)}Q^{(0)}+\tr\log\left(I-\Sigma^{(0)}\right)\right)\right)\end{split}

We can use a saddle-point approximation for
\Sigma^{(0)}
, getting
\Sigma^{(0)}=I-Q^{(0)-1}
. Plugging this in gives

p(Q^{(0)})=\exp\left(-N^{(0)}\textrm{KL}\left(Q^{(0)}|I\right)\right),

where
\textrm{KL}\left(Q^{(0)}|I\right)
the Kullback-Liebler (KL) divergence between a multivariate Gaussian with covariance
Q^{(0)}_{ab}
and one with covariance
\delta_{ab}
. This can be written

\textrm{KL}\left(Q^{(0)}|I\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tr Q^{(0)}-\tr\log Q^{(0)}-n\right)

Combining everything, we have the following expression for the expected value of the replicated partition function:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(Z_{\beta}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\int D^{L+1}\Sigma D^{L+1}Q\exp\left(S[\Sigma,Q]\right)

\displaystyle S[\Sigma,Q]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}N^{(L)}\sum_{ab}Q^{(L)}_{ab}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}N^{(\ell)}\left(\log\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma^{(\ell)};Q^{(\ell-1)}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}\Sigma^{(\ell)}_{ab}Q^{(\ell)}_{ab}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}N^{(0)}\left(\tr\Sigma^{(0)}Q^{(0)}+\tr\log\left(I-\Sigma^{(0)}\right)\right).

(24)

We note in passing that one might also be interested in the extreme value statistics of the squared magnitude of layer
L
rather than the statistics of a linear combination. Since the squared magnitude of replica
a
is just
N^{(L)}Q^{(L)}_{aa}
, the action is the same with the first term replaced with
\beta N^{(L)}\sum_{a}Q^{(L)}_{aa}
.

3.5
Saddle-Point Equations for The Replicated Action

The equations (
24
) provide an exact expression (up to the saddle point approximation for the distribution of
Q^{(0)}
) for calculating the replicated partition function
\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{n}\right)
for multilayer perceptrons in terms of model hyperparameters like
L
and the
N
s. But actually evaluating this integral over
2Ln^{2}
variables is infeasible (especially when
n
isn’t even an integer). Instead, we derive explicit forms of the
saddle point equations
for the action
S
:

\frac{\partial S}{\partial Q^{(\ell)}}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial S}{\partial\Sigma^{(\ell)}}=0,\qquad\text{for all $0\leq\ell\leq L$.}

Evaluating the derivatives of
S
with respect to the
Q
s and
\Sigma
s will involve taking derivatives of
\log\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma;Q}
. For this it is useful to define

\left\langle\bullet\right\rangle_{\Sigma;Q}\equiv\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma;Q}}\int\bullet\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}z_{a}\left(Q_{ab}\right)^{-1}z_{b}-\Sigma_{ab}\sigma(z_{a})\sigma(z_{b})\right)\frac{d^{n}z}{\sqrt{\det 2\pi Q}}.

In words,
\left\langle\bullet\right\rangle_{\Sigma;Q}
is the expectation value of
\bullet
with respect to a probability density over
n
variables
z_{a}
whose joint density is proportional to the exponential in the integrand. A direct computation then gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma;Q}}{\partial Q_{ab}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}Q^{-1}_{a\mu}\left\langle z_{\mu}z_{\nu}-Q_{\mu\nu}\right\rangle_{\Sigma;Q}Q^{-1}_{\nu b},\qquad\frac{\partial\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma;Q}}{\partial\Sigma_{ab}}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\sigma(z_{a})\sigma(z_{b})\right\rangle_{\Sigma;Q}.

After a bit of algebra, our saddle point equations take the following form:

\displaystyle Q^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\left(I-\Sigma^{(0)}\right)^{-1}

(25a)

\displaystyle Q^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sigma(z_{a})\sigma(z_{b})\right\rangle_{\Sigma^{(\ell)};Q^{(\ell-1)}}\quad\textrm{for }1\leq\ell\leq L

(25b)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{(\ell-1)}}{N^{(\ell)}}\left(\frac{1}{2}Q^{(\ell)-1}_{a\mu}\left\langle z_{\mu}z_{\nu}-Q^{(\ell)}_{\mu\nu}\right\rangle_{\Sigma^{(\ell+1)};Q^{(\ell)}}Q^{(\ell)-1}_{\nu b}\right)\quad\textrm{for }0\leq\ell\leq L-1

(25c)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(L)}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}

(25d)

3.6
The
n\to 0
Limit and the Zipper Method

In this subsection, we will focus on evaluating the right hand sides of the saddle-point equations in the limit
n\to 0
, especially in the replica-symmetric case where we will write

Q^{(\ell)}_{ab}=c^{(\ell)}\delta_{ab}+q^{(\ell)},\qquad\Sigma^{(\ell)}_{ab}=\Lambda^{(\ell)}\delta_{ab}+\Gamma^{(\ell)}.

From equation
25a
we obtain

c^{(0)}=(1-\Lambda^{(0)})^{-1},\qquad\frac{q^{(0)}}{c^{(0)}}=\frac{\Gamma^{(0)}}{1-\Lambda^{(0)}}.

Moreover, equation
25d
directly yields

\begin{split}\Lambda^{(L)}=0,\qquad\Gamma^{(L)}=\beta^{2}\end{split}

The difficult part, of course, is to evaluate the right hand sides of equations
25b
and
25c
. We discuss this calculation in appendix
A
. Once we have our equations of motion, finding satisfying choices for the
c
s,
q
s,
\Lambda
s and
\Gamma
s is surprisingly straightforward. We use what we call the
zipper method
.

1.

Initialize all
c
s,
q
s,
\Lambda
s and
\Gamma
s. For small and even intermediate
\beta
s, initializing to zero works. For larger
\beta
it may be wise to initialize using the solution from a smaller
\beta
and work recursively.

2.

Use equations
25a
,
25b
to solve for
c^{(\ell)},q^{(\ell)}
one at a time, starting with
\ell=0
and continuing to
\ell=L
.

3.

Use equation
25d
,
25c
to solve for
\Lambda^{(\ell)}
one at a time, starting with
\ell=L
and continuing backwards to
\ell=0
.

4.

Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the answers start to converge. This usually takes no more than four or five repetitions.

This method is guaranteed to work at high temperatures (small
\beta
), and empirically it seems to converge and agree with experiments even at large
\beta
. One word of caution, however. The zipper method cannot spontaneously break replica symmetry. If one starts with replica-symmetric matrices, the matrices will remain RS at every step in the zipper algorithm, even if an RSB solution exists and is more thermodynamically favored. To deal with this, we implement the system with a very slight amount of RSB, and then see if that amount increases or decreases as we iterate the zipper algorithm.

Before concluding this section and moving on to solving the analyzing the saddle points for various particular architectures and activation function, we briefly remark in the next section on an apparent incompatibility between overlap matrices satisfying the Parisi Ansatz and overlap matrices that appear
after training in LLMs.

3.7
Beyond the Parisi Ansatz: Parallelograms Wanted

Throughout this work, we assume that the overlap matrices obey the
Parisi Ansatz
: that is that they obey the hierarchical structure discussed in detail in Appendix
A.2
. This ansatz implies that the likely states of the Boltzmann distribution obey the so-called ultrametric triangle inequality for distance
\forall ABC,d(A,C)\leq\textrm{max}(d(A,B),d(B,C))
. However, states obeying such a structure are incompatible with some of the most important observations of how concepts are embedded in activation space. One clear example is the famous word-arithmetic identity Man-Woman=King-Queen. Identities like this essentially state that the four relevant words form a parallelogram. Unfortunately, parallelograms cannot obey the ultrametric triangle inequality.

This follows from the fact that for any parallelogram
ABCD
,
AC^{2}+BD^{2}=2(AB^{2}+BC^{2})
. From this it follows that
\textrm{max}(AC,BD)^{2}\geq AB^{2}+BC^{2}
. But if
AC^{2}\geq AB^{2}+BC^{2}
, then the inequality is violated. Likewise if
BD^{2}\geq AB^{2}+BC^{2}=DC^{2}+BC^{2}
.

Although we cannot scan the entire space of
n\times n
matrices to prove that the dominant ones satisfy the Parisi ansatz and imply ultrametric structure, we have good reason to think they always do. For instance, one can generalize the famous replicon analysis to study the case of layers of overlaps; we find no instability corresponding to parallelograms in activation space.

4
Deep Linear Networks

In this section, we specialize the computations from section
3
to the setting of deep linear networks in which the activation function is
\sigma(t)=t
. This is a generalization of the toy problem solved in earlier in subsection
3.3
. Specifically, in subsection
4.1
we will solve equations
25
in this case. Then, in subsection
4.2
we confirm these results for the overlaps by deriving them independently using only simple facts from random matrix theory.

4.1
Replica Trick

Note that when
\sigma
is the identity,
H(x)
is linear in
x
. Hence, the function
\norm{x}^{2}/2+\beta H(x)
will only have one local minimum. This guarantees replica symmetry for the saddles points of the replicated action from (
24
). Hence, we can write

Q^{(\ell)}_{ab}=c^{(\ell)}\delta_{ab}+q^{(\ell)}

(26)

Moreover, when for linear network, our expression (
23
) for
\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma^{(\ell)};Q^{(\ell-1)}}
simplifies to

\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma^{(\ell)};Q^{(\ell-1)}}^{-1}=\sqrt{\det\left(I-\Sigma^{(\ell)}Q^{(\ell-1)}\right)}.

Substitute this into (
25c
) allows us to exactly solve for the equation of motion of
\Sigma^{(\ell)}
:

\Sigma^{(\ell)}=\left(Q^{(\ell)}\right)^{-1}-\left(Q^{(\ell-1)}\right)^{-1}.

(27)

Plugging equation
27
back into equation
24
gives us the following action in terms of just the
Q
s:

S=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}N^{(L)}\sum_{ab}Q^{(L)}_{ab}-\sum_{i=0}^{L}N^{(i)}KL\left(Q^{(i)}|Q^{(i-1)}\right).

where we define
Q^{(-1)}
to be the identity. Using (
26
) this action becomes

S/n=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}N^{(L)}c^{(L)}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{L}N^{(i)}\left(\frac{c^{(i)}}{c^{(i-1)}}-\log\frac{c^{(i)}}{c^{(i-1)}}+\left(\frac{q^{(i)}}{c^{(i)}}-\frac{q^{(i-1)}}{c^{(i-1)}}\right)\left(\frac{c^{(i)}}{c^{(i-1)}}-1\right)\right).

(28)

where
c^{(-1)}\equiv 1
,
q^{(-1)}\equiv 0
. Re-computing the new equations of motion for
q^{(i)}
shows that they are only satisfied if
\frac{c^{(i)}}{c^{(i-1)}}=1
for all
i
, meaning all
c^{(i)}
s are equal to 1 (yes, we just used the equations of motion for
q
to solve for
c
). We can now differentiate with respect to the
c^{(i)}
s and substitute
c^{(i)}=1
to get that for
0\leq i<L

N^{(i)}(q^{(i)}-q^{(i-1)})=N^{(i+1)}(q^{(i+1)}-q^{(i)}),

while when
i=L
we have

\beta^{2}=q^{(L)}-q^{(L-1)},

which means
q^{(i)}-q^{(i-1)}=\frac{N^{(L)}}{N^{(i)}}\beta^{2}
for all
i\geq 0
, with
q^{(-1)}=0
by definition. We thus find

c^{(i)}=1,\qquad q^{(i)}=\beta^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{i}\frac{N^{(L)}}{N^{(j)}}

(29)

for all
0\leq i\leq L
. Let’s take a moment to translate equation
29
into words. As we go deeper into the network, the (thermodynamically) random part of the activation vectors (parameterized by the
c
s) doesn’t change, but the deterministic part (parameterized by
q
) gets longer and longer, making the angle between activation vectors of different replicas (
\arccos\left(q^{(i)}\right)/\left(c^{(i)}+q^{(i)}\right)
) smaller and smaller.

5
Deep Networks With Shaped Activation

Figure 4:
For
\psi=-0.2
,
c(t)
decreases with depth. The final value of
c
also decreases as
\beta^{2}
increases. Since
c
can be interpreted as the replica-to-replica variance in the position, we see that as the system gets colder the activations cluster more and more closely around the global minimum.

In this section we will deal with networks of depth
L
having a shaped activation

\sigma(z)=z+\frac{\psi z^{3}}{3L}.

(31)

Such activations have been studied
[
34
,
7
,
60
,
61
,
19
]
as a model of deep MLPs which retain reasonable behavior in the large depth limit.

The temperature regime we consider sends the depth
L
to infinity while holding
\beta^{2}L
constant. This is a sensible limit since it keeps the last-layer inter-replica correlator
Q^{(L)}
constant for the linear case. One important thing to keep in mind is that although we are taking depth
L
and width
N
to infinity, the width is going to infinity first, and is much larger than
L
. In the case of shaped activations, we can still evaluate
\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma^{(\ell)};Q^{(\ell-1)}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma_{ab}^{(\ell)};Q_{ab}^{(\ell-1)}}

\displaystyle=\int\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}\Sigma_{ab}^{(\ell)}\sigma(z_{a})\sigma(z_{b})\right)Dz

\displaystyle=\int\left(1+\frac{2\psi}{3L}\sum_{ab}\Sigma_{ab}^{(\ell)}z_{a}z_{b}^{3}\right)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}\Sigma_{ab}^{(\ell)}z_{a}z_{b}\right)Dz+O(L^{-2})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\det\left(I-\Sigma^{(\ell)}Q^{(\ell-1)}\right)}}\left(1+2\frac{\psi}{L}\left(\sum_{ab}\Sigma_{ab}^{(\ell)}\left\langle z_{a}z_{b}\right\rangle_{\Sigma^{(\ell)};Q^{(\ell-1)}}\left\langle z_{b}^{2}\right\rangle_{\Sigma^{(\ell)};Q^{(\ell-1)}}\right)\right)+O(L^{-2})

(32)

We can use the
\Sigma^{(\ell)}
equations of motion to replace
\left\langle z_{a}z_{b}\right\rangle_{\Sigma^{(\ell)};Q^{(\ell-1)}}
with
Q^{(\ell-1)}
. This is not exactly accurate, since it ignores the contribution of
\Sigma
to the average. However, we are picking a temperature regime where
\Sigma
is small, so this can be ignored. With this in mind we find

\log\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma_{ab}^{(\ell)};Q_{ab}^{(\ell-1)}}=-\frac{1}{2}\tr\log\left(I-\Sigma^{(\ell)}Q^{(\ell-1)}\right)+\frac{\psi}{L}\sum_{ab}\Sigma^{(\ell)}_{ab}Q_{aa}^{(l-1)}Q_{ab}^{(l-1)}.

At this point we can ”integrate out” the
\Sigma^{(\ell)}
s, essentially solving the
\Sigma^{(\ell)}
equations of motion for fixed
Q^{(\ell)}
and substituting in this value of
\Sigma^{(ell)}
to get an action exclusively in terms of the
Q^{(\ell)}
s. Equation
24
becomes

S=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}N^{(L)}\sum_{ab}Q^{(L)}_{ab}-\sum_{i=0}^{L}N^{(i)}KL\left(Q^{(i)}|{\tilde{Q}^{(i-1)}}\right),

where we define

\tilde{Q}_{ab}=\left(1+\frac{2\psi}{L}Q_{aa}\right)Q_{ab}

Since
Q^{(\ell)}
and
Q^{(\ell-1)}
will be close to each other (differing by an
O(L^{-1})
) amount, we approximate the KL divergence by the distance in the Fisher information metric. Taking the replica symmetric ansatz and forgetting the
\psi

\frac{1}{n}KL\left(Q+dQ|Q\right)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{c^{2}}(1-\frac{2q}{c})dc^{2}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}dcdq

Restoring the
\psi

\frac{1}{n}KL\left(Q+dQ|Q+\frac{2\psi(c+q)}{L}Q\right)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{c^{2}}(1-\frac{2q}{c})(dc-A_{c}/L)^{2}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}(dc-A_{c}/L)(dq-A_{q}/L),

where

A_{c}=2\psi(c+q)c,\quad A_{q}=2\psi(c+q)q

represent the expected drifts of

and
q
as we go from layer to layer, without postselecting on the last layer. If we now define a continuous analog
t=\ell/L
of the layer index, then at large

into an integral over

\frac{L}{N}S=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}Lc+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left(1-\frac{2q}{c}\right)(c^{\prime}-A_{c})^{2}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}(c^{\prime}-A_{c})(q^{\prime}-A_{q})dt.

with initial conditions
c(0)=1
,
q(0)=0
. If we make the substitutions
u=q/c
,
v=\ln c
, our action becomes

\frac{L}{N}S=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}Le^{v}+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}(v^{\prime}-A_{v})^{2}+(v^{\prime}-A_{v})u^{\prime}dt,

with
A_{v}=2(1+u)e^{v}
represents the expected flow of
A
(the expected flow of

is 0). The Euler-Lagrange equations for this action give

\begin{split}p_{u}^{\prime}=-2\psi e^{v}(v^{\prime}-A_{v})-2\psi e^{v}u^{\prime}\\
p_{v}^{\prime}=-A_{v}(v^{\prime}-A_{v})-A_{v}u^{\prime}\end{split}

(33)

for
0<t<1
. Here
p_{u}
and
p_{v}
are the conjugate momenta to

and

\begin{split}p_{u}=v^{\prime}-A_{v}\\
p_{v}=u^{\prime}+v^{\prime}-A_{v}\end{split}

(34)

This is where our boundary conditions come into play. Our initial conditions are

. Our end condition is
u^{\prime\prime}(1)=0,v^{\prime\prime}(1)-A_{v}^{\prime}(1)=\beta^{2}e^{v(1)}.
These four conditions are enough to uniquely specify a solution. Figure

shows increasingly deep shaped activations approaching the solution to this ODE.

At

6.1
Evaluating

Recall that the expected energy at a given temperature is given by

E(\beta)=-\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}\left(\log Z_{\beta}\right)}{\partial\beta},

(35)

Hence, using a saddle point approximation to
\log(Z_\beta)
, we find

\displaystyle n\mathbb{E}\log{Z_\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}N^{(L)}\sum_{ab}Q^{(L)}_{ab}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}N^{(\ell)}\left(\log\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma^{(\ell)};Q^{(\ell-1)}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}\Sigma^{(\ell)}_{ab}Q^{(\ell)}_{ab}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}N^{(0)}\left(\tr\Sigma^{(0)}Q^{(0)}+\tr\log\left(I-\Sigma^{(0)}\right)\right),

\Sigma
and
Q
are the critical points of the action. Hence,

E(\beta)=-\frac{1}{n}\beta N^{(L)}\sum_{ab}Q^{(L)}_{ab}.

Given how complicated our expression for free energy is, it is remarkable that energy works out so simply (though, of course, evaluating it means finding the
\beta
-dependent
\Sigma
and
Q
which extremize the complicated free energy functional).

In the case of the deep linear perceptron,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{ab}Q^{(L)}_{ab}=c^{(L)}=1
, so the energy is precisely
-N^{(L)}\beta.
We can solve the equations of motion using our zipper algorithm and extract the thermodynamics for a wide variety of hyperparameter choices. Figure

Finally, let us comment on how these thermodynamic results can be recast in the language of large deviation theory. In this field, it is common to study the large deviations of some extensive quantity of many variables. In this case the quantity is the output of a multilayer perceptron as a function of the inputs. It is common to find the tails of the probability distribution have the form

P(E)\sim\exp\left(Ns\left(\frac{E}{N}\right)\right).

(36)

This fact is just equivalent to the common thermodynamic fact that entropy density and energy density are extensive variables, while entropy and energy scale with
N
.

6.3
Replica Symmetry Breaking

In this subsection, we deal with replica symmetry breaking. Physically, this corresponds to Boltzmann distributions where the measure concentrates into a few valleys of
H
. In terms of the overlap matrices
Q
, it means that instead of being replica symmetric, they take on a hierarchical structure as expounded upon in Appendix
A.2
.

For fixed
k
and fixed sequence of
m
s
m_{0\leq i<k}
we can find a solution to equations
25
with that pattern of
m
s. Oftentimes, this solution will have
q_{0}=q_{1}...=q_{k}
. Such a solution is replica symmetric even though the ansatz can accommodate RSB.

Figure
2
illustrates the presence of replica symmetry breaking for a specific architecture: an activation function of
\sin z
with one hidden layer. But although it is certainly possible to come up with architectures displaying RSB, many of the most natural choices, including shallow circuits with
\tanh
or ReLU activations, seem to be replica symmetric.

7
Conclusion

This work is fundamentally about asking a new type of question: what are the statistical behaviors of the special inputs of a neural network. This general question can take many specific forms: here we took ‘special’ to mean ‘produces of a very negative output’ and ‘a neural network’ to mean ‘an MLP at initialization.’ But there are other kinds of special inputs, and other neural network cases of interest. For instance, one could look at the inputs with the largest gradient at the output or the largest magnitude of the final activation layer. More broadly, we believe the techniques we developed here will be of use for studying the statistical mechanics of neural networks in which we treat both weights and data as quenched.

First, we rewrite

\begin{split}\mathcal{Z}_{\Sigma;Q}=\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab}z_{a}\left(Q_{ab}\right)^{-1}z_{b}+\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{ab}\sigma(z_{a})\sigma(z_{b})\right)\frac{d^{n}z}{\sqrt{\det 2\pi Q}}=\\

Appendix B
Robustness of Results to Training

One natural question is how robust these results are. For instance, would the slightest amount of training negate them completely? In this appendix, we argue that our results are resilient, and apply to all but an exponentially small fraction of neural nets. We show this by applying a second layer of large-deviation theory.

Recall that our results can be cast in several different languages: they can be read as statements about the thermodynamics of a system with an MLP Hamiltonian, but also as the large deviation theory for a typical MLP taking in Gaussian noise as an input. We showed that for a typical MLP, the volume of space with output
E
goes as
e^{Ns(E/N)}
, for some definite
s
we can calculate.
The natural question, then, is to ask about atypical MLPs. In other words, we are calculating the large deviations of our thermodynamic quantities like free energy or entropy. We can probe the deviations for
F(\beta)
by calculating exponents of
F
. Fortunately,
\mathbb{E}e^{-n\beta F}=\mathbb{E}Z(\beta)^{n}.
Fortunately, this is the very quantity we already studies with the replica trick, just without the explicit
n\to 0
limit. We know that for any choice of
n
, the expectation will be some exponential quantity in
N
. This corresponds to a large-deviation principle for
F(\beta)
with extensive (in
N
) fluctuations being exponentially (in
N
) unlikely. This means that a small amount of training will not be enough to change our results, we need enough training to get to the exponentially small region of MLP-space where our theory doesn’t apply.
