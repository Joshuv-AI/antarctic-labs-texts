The Frenkel line and the pseudogap: an analogy between classical and electronic fluids
J. Fournier 1, P.-O. Downey 1, O. Gingras 2,3, C.-D. Hébert 1, M. Charlebois 1,4, A.-M. S. Tremblay 1

Abstract
Asymptotically close to critical end-points of first-order transitions, maxima in thermodynamic quantities occur along a line called the Widom line, a concept first introduced in classical fluids. This concept has been extended to strongly correlated electronic fluids in the context of the Mott transition. Namely, upon increasing interaction strength in the Hubbard model at half-filling, one finds a first-order Mott metal-insulator transition with a critical end-point at high temperature above which a number of crossover lines are observable. Using the dynamical cluster approximation for the triangular-lattice Hubbard model, we compute the Frenkel line, another concept borrowed from classical fluids, useful to define a sharp crossover between the pseudogap and the correlated Fermi liquid. The Frenkel line in the electron fluid is defined by the appearance of back-scattering when entering the pseudogap. The signature of back-scattering is the existence of a negative value in the time-domain optical conductivity.

A key limitation of these methods is that they focus mainly on low-frequency behavior, potentially overlooking other spectral features of the pseudogap at higher energies.

Another potential way to identify the border of the pseudogap is to use the concept of Widom line

;
Sordi et al. 2012

In classical fluids, a dynamic crossover line dubbed the Frenkel line has recently been proposed to separate the rigid (liquid-like) and nonrigid (gas-like) regimes in the supercritical state.
This line is defined with time-dependent dynamic quantities
Bolmatov et al. 2015
;
Pruteanu et al. 2021
;
Trachenko 2023
such as the onset of oscillations in the time-dependent velocity-velocity autocorrelation function
Brazhkin et al. 2012
.
In the electronic fluid, we claim that an analog quantity identifies a crossover line in the supercritical state of the Mott transition between the Fermi liquid, and the pseudogap regime where various physical quantities reflect a reduction of the density of states near the Fermi level. The analogy with the classical fluids suggests to study the time-dependent current-current autocorrelation function.

However, instead of directly computing the current-current correlation function, we focus on the optical conductivity, which is related to it through
\sigma_{1}(\omega)=\chi_{j_{x}j_{x}}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)/\omega
and is more readily accessible experimentally.
Here, we investigate the pseudogap to correlated Fermi liquid crossover rather than the rigid non-rigid crossover, hence an analogous but new criterion is needed to define the Frenkel line.
We suggest that the Frenkel line is crossed when oscillations in the time-dependent Fourier transform of the frequency-dependent conductivity
\sigma_{1}(\omega)
are sufficiently strong to lead to negative
\sigma_{1}(t)
.

Let us explain this. Recall that the time-dependent
retarded
conductivity
\sigma^{R}(t)
is obtained simply from the Fourier transform of the complex frequency-dependent one.
It is related to the response of the electrical current to a time-dependent electric field through
j(t)=\int\sigma^{R}(t-t^{\prime})E(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}
.
Since one can prove (End Matter
A
) that
\sigma^{R}(t)=2\sigma_{1}(t)\Theta(t)
, where
\sigma_{1}(t)
is the Fourier transform of the frequency-dependent observed real part of the optical conductivity, from now on we focus on
\sigma_{1}(t)
.

In a simple metal, the optical conductivity is dominated by a Drude peak, yielding a strictly positive and monotonically decaying
\sigma_{1}(t)
.
In the pseudogap regime, the transfer of spectral weight away from the Fermi level contributes to a redistribution of the optical conductivity from low to mid-infrared frequencies.
This can lead to negative
\sigma_{1}(t-t^{\prime})
for some interval of
t-t^{\prime}
, indicating that a mid-infrared peak can cause the current at time
t
to have contributions from the electric field at former times
t^{\prime}
that are in opposite direction to the current at time
t
. This can be understood as a consequence of back-scattering.
Therefore, the appearance of
\sigma_{1}(t)<0
is an indication that the metallic Drude-like transport no longer dominates, which in turn, implies that the system is no longer in a correlated Fermi liquid state.
The redistribution of the spectral weight away from the Fermi level is responsible for the decrease of the DOS at the Fermi level and for the negative
\sigma_{1}(t)
. Both are consequences of the same dynamical transition.

Discussion—

The electronic Frenkel line, that we defined here, separates the pseudogap and correlated Fermi liquid in the proximity to the Widom line
U_{W}
, and to the two dynamic lines
U_{inc}
and
U_{\sigma(\omega)}
. This means that the appearance of negative
\sigma(t)
is a legitimate way to define the crossover from the pseudogap to the correlated Fermi liquid regimes.

The electronic Frenkel line emerges from the Mott critical point. This differs from the Frenkel line found in classical fluids, where it emerges from a point at a temperature below
T_{c}
, and lies in the liquid phase for
T<T_{c}

Pruteanu et al. 2021
;
Bryk et al. 2017
;
Brazhkin et al. 2012
;
Yang et al. 2015
. This indicates that, in classical fluids, structural and dynamic lines are different. In our case, we study a transition between the pseudogap and correlated Fermi liquid, which is dynamical rather than structural. That the Frenkel line starts at the Mott critical point is expected because this point reflects a dynamical, rather than structural, form of criticality.

Another difference between the electronic Frenkel line and the one found in classical fluids is that, as explained in introduction, the criterion for the crossover is different. In classical fluids, the criterion for the dynamic line is the temperature and pressure where the local minima and oscillations disappear in the velocity autocorrelation function
Brazhkin et al. 2012
. This criterion for the Frenkel line in classical fluids comes from the monotonic decay of the velocity autocorrelation function in the gas phase. In the triangular lattice Hubbard model, we do not find any temperature or interaction
U
for which the autocorrelation function does not present oscillations or local minima.

Another difference between the Frenkel lines found in electronic and classical fluids is the interaction responsible for the change of dynamics.
In classical fluids, the crossing of the Frenkel line is associated with the disappearance of transverse
acoustic
phonon modes
Trachenko 2023
;
Bolmatov et al. 2015
. Here, crossing the Frenkel line by decreasing
U
corresponds to the disappearance of back-scattering.

Experimentally, in cuprates, peaks associated with
optical
phonons are found in optical conductivity
\sigma(\omega)
at energies between
60~\text{cm}^{-1}
and
700~\text{cm}^{-1}

Mirzaei et al. 2013
;
LaForge et al. 2008
;
Basov et al. 1999
.

Therefore, in order to experimentally observe the proposed Frenkel line, it is necessary to suppress or filter the contribution of the optical phonon modes to the signal.

The advantage of the electronic Frenkel line over the dynamic lines
U_{\sigma(\omega)}
and
U_{inc}
is that the Frenkel line is based on a sharp criterion, namely negative
\sigma(t)
. Contrary to the Widom line
U_{W}
it extends to very high temperature. It marks a real qualitative change in the behaviour of the electrons, namely the appearance of back scattering. Contrary to all other lines, this negative
\sigma(t)
also reflects the high-frequency behavior.

Conclusion—

We report the existence of an electronic Frenkel line, analogous to the Frenkel line in classical fluids, that can be used to separate the pseudogap and metallic regimes of the supercritical state. The Frenkel line in the electronic problem is given by a sharp criterion, namely negative
\sigma(t)
, and is found in the vicinity of other lines used to define the pseudogap state in the phase diagram. It emerges from the critical point and extends to high temperature. Comparison with experiment calls for subtraction of phonons and measurements over wide frequency range. We did not find data satisfying both requirements.

While we focused on the half-filled triangular lattice Hubbard model, a critical point and pseudogap regime are also found at finite doping for that model
Downey et al. 2024
;
Fournier et al. 2024
;
Gull et al. 2013
, as well as for the square lattice Hubbard model
Semon 2014
;
Kyung and Tremblay 2006
;
Bragança et al. 2018
. We speculate that the electronic Frenkel line is a general concept that will also be useful for these cases and other electronic phase transitions.

\mathbf{k}
-space, and the self-energy is constant within the patches of the Brillouin zone. The derivative of the self-energy is nonzero only at the border of every patch. The three-point vertex correction is thus

\mathbf{\Gamma}^{\text{vertex}}_{ab}(i\omega_{n},iq_{m},\mathbf{k})=\left[\Sigma^{b}(i\omega_{n}+iq_{m})-\Sigma^{a}(i\omega_{m}+iq_{m})\right]\\
\times\delta([\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}_{ab}]\cdot\mathbf{n}_{ab})\mathbf{n}_{ab},

(15)

where
\mathbf{n}_{ab}
is the normal vector to the border between the patches labelled
a
and
b
, and
\mathbf{k}_{ab}
is the vector defining the border between the two patches. To obtain a real current autocorrelation function, we need to symmetrize the three-point vertex in the equation for
\chi_{j_{x}j_{x}}
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}^{ab}(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n},iq_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{\Gamma}^{\text{vertex}}_{ab}(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n})+\mathbf{\Gamma}^{\text{vertex}}_{ab}(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n}+iq_{m})}{2}.

The same result follows without need for symmetrization in frequency if, in Eq.(
9
), we write the right-hand side as
\mathcal{G}^{-1}(i\omega_{n}+iq_{m},\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2})-\mathcal{G}^{-1}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k}-\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2})
.

Fourier transform—

We use the maximum entropy method implemented in Ref.
Bergeron et al. 2011
to perform the analytic continuation of the real-frequency bosonic spectral function
\sigma_{1}(\omega)=\left[{\chi_{j_{x}j_{x}}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)}/{\omega}\right]
. The retarded response in frequency has both a real (
\sigma_{1}(\omega)
) and an imaginary (
\sigma_{2}(\omega)
) part so that its Fourier transform is given by

\displaystyle\sigma^{R}(t)

\displaystyle=\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\sigma_{1}(\omega)+i\sigma_{2}(\omega)\right)\Theta(t)e^{-i\omega t}d\omega\right].

(16)

with
\Theta(t)
the Heaviside function. The imaginary part of the optical conductivity is obtained through the Kramers-Kronig relation.

In mathematics, this is a Hilbert transformation.
Computing
\sigma^{R}(t)
with this formula is not ideal because Kramers-Kronig introduces numerical errors in
\sigma_{2}(\omega)
. The following property of Hilbert transformations can be used to remove the need for the numerical computation of
\sigma_{2}(\omega)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left[H(u)\right](t)=-i\text{sgn}(t)\cdot\mathcal{F}[u](t),

(17)

where
\mathcal{F}
stands for Fourier transform,
H
for Hilbert transformation, and
u
is a real function of frequency. Inserting this relation in the formula for
\sigma^{R}(t)
, we find

\displaystyle\sigma^{R}(t)

\displaystyle=\left[\mathcal{F}[\sigma_{1}(\omega)\right]+i\mathcal{F}\left[\sigma_{2}(\omega)]\right]

(18)

\displaystyle=\left[\mathcal{F}[\sigma_{1}(\omega)\right]+i\mathcal{F}\left[H\left[\sigma_{1}(\omega)]\right]\right]

(19)

\displaystyle=\left[\mathcal{F}\left[\sigma_{1}\right](t)-i^{2}\text{sgn}(t)\mathcal{F}\left[\sigma_{1}\right](t)\right]

(20)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\left[\sigma_{1}\right](t)\left\{1+\text{sgn}(t)\right\}.

(21)

Thus, for
t>0
,
\sigma^{R}(t)
equals
2[\mathcal{F}[\sigma_{1}]]\Theta(t)
and we do not need to compute
\sigma_{2}(\omega)
nor
\sigma_{2}(t)
. Also, as required,
\sigma^{R}(t)
is real.

Critical point—

The Mott critical point can be found from the double occupancy as a function of interaction
U
for different temperatures. At temperature
T<T_{c}
, there is a first order transition for the double occupancy at
U_{c1}
and
U_{c2}
, whereas at
T>T_{c}
, the double occupancy is continuous. The critical inverse temperature
\beta_{c}=1/T_{c}=8.5
is the highest temperature for which we find a discontinuous double occupancy. The lowest temperature where we found a continuous double occupancy is
T=1/8
. The double occupancy as a function of
U
for
\beta=8.0
,
\beta=8.5
and
\beta=8.7
is shown in Fig.
4
.

We obtain the value of the critical interaction
U_{c}
by fitting the data for the double occupancy
U
at
\beta_{c}=8.5
with the function

\displaystyle D-D_{c}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\text{sgn}(\delta U)|\delta U|^{1/\delta}+c_{2}|\delta U|^{2/\delta}+c_{3}\delta U,

(22)

where
\delta U=U-U_{c}
, and
D_{c}
,
U_{c}
,
\delta
,
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
are fitting parameters
Sémon and Tremblay 2012
.

Another method to verify the accuracy of the critical point is to study the convergence of each simulation. At the critical point, the correlation length diverges. This leads to critical slowing down, which causes convergence problems. At

\mathcal{G}_{\text{DCA}}
vs
\mathcal{G}_{\text{lat}}
—

In this letter, we use
\mathcal{G}_{\text{DCA}}
to compute the current autocorrelation function.
\mathcal{G}_{\text{DCA}}
is a coarsed grained Green’s function constant within each patch of the Brillouin zone. However, in DCA, it is also possible to compute the lattice Green’s function

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\text{lat}}(\mathbf{K},\tilde{\mathbf{k}},i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\left(i\omega_{n}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{K}+\tilde{\mathbf{k}}}+\mu-\Sigma(\mathbf{K})\right)^{-1}

(23)

where
\mathbf{\tilde{k}}
is the wavector inside the different patches. Using this definition of the Green’s function changes the sum over the wave vectors in the computation of
\chi_{j_{x}j_{x}}
. Fig.
6
shows the results for
\sigma_{1}(\omega)
and
\sigma(t)
obtained with the DCA Green’s function and the lattice Green’s function. We find that the different Green’s functions give similar results for the conductivity, and that the location of the Frenkel line does not depend significantly on the Green’s function used.
