Efficient Quantum Hermite Transform
Siddhartha Jain, Vishnu Iyer, Rolando D. Somma, Ning Bao, Stephen Jordan

Abstract
We present a new primitive for quantum algorithms that implements a discrete Hermite transform efficiently, in time that depends logarithmically in both the dimension and the inverse of the allowable error. This transform, which maps basis states to states whose amplitudes are proportional to the Hermite functions, can be interpreted as the Gaussian analogue of the Fourier transform. Our algorithm is based on a method to exponentially fast forward the evolution of the quantum harmonic oscillator, which significantly improves over prior art. We apply this Hermite transform to give examples of provable quantum query advantage in property testing and learning. In particular, we show how to efficiently test the property of being close to a low-degree in the Hermite basis when inputs are sampled from the Gaussian distribution, and how to solve a Gaussian analogue of the Goldreich-Levin learning task efficiently. We also comment on other potential uses of this transform to simulating time dynamics of quantum systems in the continuum.

Introduction

Hermite polynomials and their corresponding Hermite functions are central in physics, signal analysis, statistics, and beyond. For example, Hermite functions are the well-known eigenfunctions of one of the fundamental models in quantum mechanics known as the quantum harmonic oscillator (QHO)
[
GS18
]
. Hermite functions can also be used to approximate arbitrary functions or probability distributions, since they form an orthonormal basis of functions weighted by a Gaussian envelope (cf.
[
BM98
]
).
Therefore, an efficient Hermite transform that performs a change of basis
to the Hermite functions would provide a fundamental tool to quickly switch into the “natural” basis of many quantum systems and functions.
Moreover, the number of primitives from which most quantum algorithms are constructed, such as the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) and amplitude amplification, is relatively limited
[
NC00
]
. This scarcity restricts the range of problems for which quantum algorithms offer speedups, thereby motivating the search of other novel quantum primitives. Given that the QFT represents a quantization of a pre-existing classical numerical transform, it is logical to attempt to quantize other classical transforms, such as the Hermite transform
[
LRP+08
]
, and to consider its applications in quantum computing.

This work is concerned with a quantum Hermite transform (QHT):
constructing a quantum circuit that maps basis states into ‘Hermite’ states. These Hermite states are superposition states with amplitudes given by the Hermite functions. While formally a Hermite transform would be defined in the continuum (i.e., a mapping
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
),
we consider a discrete and finite-dimensional version that can be implemented by a quantum circuit.

For a QHT to be useful and provide quantum advantage, we require it to be efficient and hence implemented using a quantum circuit of complexity that is logarithmic in the dimension of the Hilbert space on which it acts. It is also desirable to
achieve complexity scaling polylogarithmically in the inverse of the allowable error. Remarkably, we are able to achieve this: our main result is a quantum circuit
U
of
\mathcal{O}((\log N+\log 1/\varepsilon)^{3}\times\log(1/\eps))
gates that performs the desired basis change into the Hermite states labeled by
n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
to within additive error
\varepsilon
. Note that a classical discrete Hermite transform would require time polynomial in
N
to be implemented. Our result is then comparable to the QFT, in that the QFT also transforms into another ‘natural’ basis given by Fourier states, in time logarithmic in
N
.

Throughout this manuscript, we primarily consider the one-dimensional Hermite transform, acting on an
N
-dimensional Hilbert space. That is, we consider the position basis on a line, discretized as a one-dimensional lattice of
N
points, and convert to the the Hermite functions of degree zero to
N-1
, which are also interpretable as the
N
lowest-energy eigenstates of the quantum Harmonic oscillator. In some contexts, particularly property testing of oracles, it is also useful to apply the
n
-dimensional Hermite transform. This is the
n
-fold tensor power of the one-dimensional Hermite transform, much as the
n
-dimensional Fourier transform is the
n
-fold tensor power of the one-dimensional Fourier transform.

The existence of an efficient QHT is also a fundamental algorithmic question in Harmonic analysis, since it allows one to perform Hermite sampling. That is, given a quantum state
\sum_{\vec{x}}f(\vec{x})\ket{\vec{x}}
for some function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, one can use the (
n
-dimensional) QHT to sample from the probability distribution
\mathrm{Pr}(\vec{k})=|\widehat{f}(\vec{k})|^{2}
, where
\widehat{f}(\vec{k})
is the coefficient of the Hermite polynomial with index
\vec{k}
in the expansion.
In the finite field setting of QFT, the analogous Fourier sampling subroutine features in many of the most prominent quantum algorithms
[
Sim97
,
Sho97
,
Reg09
,
Aar10
,
RT22
,
YZ24
,
JSW+25
]
. Using the QHT we expect that one can design continuous analogues of problems with quantum query advantage like Forrelation
[
AA18
,
Wu20
]
. We also expect QHT to be useful for learning geometric concepts over the Gaussian distribution
[
KOS08
]
, and simulating nonlinear differential equations with Gaussian noise which wecan be done by projecting onto low-degree Hermite polynomials
[
BMSZZ25
]
. A recent work of Marwaha et al.
[
MFGH25
]
also showed that the recent DQI algorithm
[
JSW+25
]
can be thought of as performing a
Kravchuk
transform, which is a particular discretization of the Hermite transform. While closely related, we do not believe our work easily gives an efficient Kravchuk transform, which would scale logarithmically in the degree.

Problem statement

In the continuum, a Hermite transform is a basis transformation that maps into the Hermite functions defined as

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x):=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}}}e^{-x^{2}/2}H_{n}(x)\;,

(1)

where

Hermite polynomial
1
1

1

There are two widely used normalizations for the Hermite polynomials: the physicist’s Hermite polynomials and the probabilist’s Hermite polynomials. We use the physicist’s normalization throughout.
.
The goal of a QHT is then to construct a quantum circuit that performs a similar basis transformation but in a discrete, finite dimensional space. To this end, we introduce the Hermite states

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{n}}:=\left(\frac{2\pi}{M}\right)^{1/4}\sum_{j=-M/2}^{M/2-1}\psi_{n}(x_{j})\ket{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}\;,

(2)

where

The problem of a QHT is to find a quantum circuit that maps

Summary of results

Our QHT relies on a novel result to fast-forward the evolution of the QHO.
In this context, a bounded Hamiltonian
H
is said to be fast-forwardable if one can construct a quantum circuit

that approximates the time-evolution operator

for time

[
AA17
,
GSŞ21
]
. Such result would bypass a number of no-fast-forwarding theorems that show that this is not always possible
[

,
CK10
,
AA17
,
HHKL21
]
.
However, the lower bound
\Omega(\|H\|t)

applies only to the worst case and, in contrast,
quantum systems like the QHO can indeed be fast-forwarded. In the continuum, the QHO models a particle in a quadratic potential with Hamiltonian

\hat{\mathrm{H}}=(\hat{x}^{2}+\hat{p}^{2})/2
, where

\hat{x}
is the position operator and
\hat{p}
is the momentum operator.
We introduce a discretized,
M
-dimensional version of the QHO with Hamiltonian
\overline{\mathrm{H}}
, and show the following.

Theorem 1

(Exponential fast-forwarding of the QHO)
.

Let
N
be the target dimension for fast-forwarding,

\overline{\mathrm{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
be the Hamiltonian of a discrete QHO
, and
t\in[-\pi,\pi]
.
Then, we can choose
M=\Theta(N\log N)
such that the evolution operator
e^{-{\rm i}\overline{\mathrm{H}}t}

can be simulated within error
\mathcal{O}(\exp(-\lowenergy/10))
in the subspace spanned by the first
N
eigenvectors of
\overline{\mathrm{H}}
using
{\mathcal{O}}(\log^{2}N)
gates.

Note that we don’t lose any generality due to the condition

since the eigenvalues of the Harmonic oscillator are of the form

,

Theorem

shows that exponential fast-forwarding of the QHO is possible.

The complexity in

is dominated by that of multiplication using coherent arithmetic, which is

The ability to fast forward a Hamiltonian also
allows one to perform tasks like quantum phase estimation (QPE) more efficiently
[
AA17

GSŞ21
]
.
Our efficient QHT relies on fast QPE and hence builds upon

.

Theorem 2

(Efficient QHT, informal)
.

There exists a quantum circuit of complexity

\mathcal{O}((\log N+\log(1/\eps))^{3}\times\log(1/\eps))

that can perform an
\varepsilon

.

Being equipped with an efficient QHT, we then consider some problems where it can be applied.

Property testing and learning.

One main application of an efficient QHT is Hermite sampling, discussed in detail in
Section

5
. In this setting we are given black-box access to a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and we want to sample from its Hermite spectrum. To do so, we must first use queries to the black box to construct a state whose amplitudes are proportional to
f
. Then we use the (inverse) QHT
n
times, to map Hermite states to basis states.

Using Hermite sampling as a subroutine, we are also able to show that quantum algorithms can solve all the following tasks efficiently over the Gaussian distribution:

(1)

Test if
f
is close to a product of
k
sign functions;

(2)

Test if
f
is close to being degree
d
;

(3)

Test if
f
is close to a Hermite polynomial;

(4)

Agnostically learn what we call sparse concepts.

We describe quantum algorithms for (1–3) in
Section

5.3
and give the learning application (4) in
Section

5.4
. We also prove that we have quantum advantage for tasks (1) and (2) in
Section

5.3
. A recent independent work of Lewis et al.
[
LGM25
]
also gave quantum advantage in learning real-valued functions, but our work is the first to show quantum advantage for property testing with real-valued functions.

We note that these quantum advantages are already attained in the limit of large
n
with
N=\mathrm{poly}(n)
. Consequently, the polylogarithmic scaling in
N
that we achieve with our QHT circuit only achieves a polynomial quantum advantage in circuit size for property testing. Now that we have shown that the quantum Hermite transform can be efficiently applied at exponentially large
N
, we invite others to use this as a building block for new quantum algorithms achieving exponential advantage.

Hamiltonian simulation.

Hamiltonian simulation is the problem of simulating quantum dynamics, induced by a Hamiltonian
H
and for time
t
, on a quantum computer. In particular, the Hermite functions represent what in physics are known as Fock states. Such states appear naturally in many quantum systems, where the QHO is only one example, and we expect the QHT to be generally applicable for Hamiltonian simulation in these systems.

Quantum systems defined in the continuum involve the position
\hat{x}
and momentum
\hat{p}
operators. These become sparse when represented using the Fock states or Hermite functions. Our QHT can then perform the change of basis efficiently, allowing us to work with sparse representations of the Hamiltonians. As the most efficient quantum algorithms for quantum dynamics or Hamiltonian simulation assume the sparse matrix access model (cf.
[
BCC+15
,
LC17
]
),
our efficient QHT might be used to reduce the complexity of Hamiltonian simulation in these systems.

Similarly, the QHT is expected to bring novel examples of fast-forwarding. Consider for example the well-known Jaynes-Cummings model that models the interaction of a two-level atom with the electromagnetic field (in the continuum)
[
JC05
]
.
When described in the Fock basis, the Hamiltonian simply becomes a direct sum of
2\times 2
blocks that can be simply diagonalized. Hence,
our QHT can also exponentially fast-forward the discrete version of this model.

Like the QFT, our efficient QHT opens the path to other case studies and potential applications. To this end, in
Section

6
we list open problems, charting the path for more examples of quantum advantage.

Technical overview

Our main result is an efficient quantum circuit for an
M
-dimensional unitary
U
, that transforms from the computational basis to the basis of Hermite states given in
Equation

2
, with arbitrary accuracy.
Hermite states are also accurate approximations of the eigenvectors of the ‘discrete’ QHO
[
Som16
]
. That is, we approximate the transformation defined by
U\ket{n}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{n}}
, where the Hermite states
\ket{\psi_{n}}
are a discretization of the
n^{\textrm{th}}
eigenstate of the QHO Hamiltonian in the continuum
\hat{\mathrm{H}}=(\hat{x}^{2}+\hat{p}^{2})/2
.
Here,
\hat{x}
is the position operator and
\hat{p}
is the momentum operator:
\hat{x}f(x)=xf(x)
and
\hat{p}f(x)=-{\rm i}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}f(x)
, for arbitrary
f(x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

The steps in our efficient QHT are as follows. First, given index
n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
for some
N>0
, we prepare the Hermite state
\ket{\psi_{n}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
while keeping a copy of
\ket{n}
in some register. The actual dimension
M=2^{m}
satisfies
M>N
and has to be chosen properly, as we discuss. This is done via a unitary transformation conditional on
\ket{n}
. To reset
\ket{n}
to
\ket{0}^{\otimes m}
, i.e., to ‘uncompute’
\ket{n}
, we infer the value of
n
from
\ket{\psi_{n}}
. That is, the sequence of state preparation steps to define the QHT is

\begin{array}[]{lcll}\ket{n}&\to&\ket{n}\ket{0}^{\otimes m}&\textrm{adjoin $m$ ancillas}\\
&\to&\ket{n}\ket{\psi_{n}}&\textrm{Hermite state preparation}\\
&\to&\ket{0}^{\otimes m}\ket{\psi_{n}}&\textrm{uncomputation}\\
&\to&\ket{\psi_{n}}&\textrm{discard $m$ ancillas.}\end{array}

We give an overview of how the main steps are accomplished.

Hermite state preparation.

Given
n
, to prepare the state
\ket{\psi_{n}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
we proceed in two steps: First, we prepare an approximation
\ket{\phi_{n}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
such that
\braket{\phi_{n}|\psi_{n}}=\Omega(1)
, and second, we use fixed-point quantum search
[
YLC14
]
to transform
\ket{\phi_{n}}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{n}}
efficiently. Amplitude amplification requires a sequence of reflections over
\ket{\psi_{n}}
and
\ket{\phi_{n}}
. The latter can be efficiently implemented
because the state
\ket{\phi_{n}}
can also be efficiently prepared, but the reflection over
\ket{\psi_{n}}
is more intricate. To this end, we design a ‘filtering’ algorithm that flags
\ket{\psi_{n}}
and allows us to simulate this reflection by a reflection over a state
\ket{0}
of an ancilla qubit. This filtering is based on fast QPE; it uses our fast-forwarding result for the QHO and can be implemented efficiently.

The states
\ket{\phi_{n}}
are chosen as follows. A useful result for this task is that of Plancherel–Rotach asymptotics
[
Sze39
]
, which gives approximate expressions for the Hermite functions
\psi_{n}(x)
with a provable error bound. Such approximations are only valid in the domain
|x|<\sqrt{2n+1}
, where the points
\pm x_{\rm tp}=\pm\sqrt{2n+1}
are known as the ‘turning’ points in physics. Explicitly, these approximations read:

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}2^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}n^{\frac{1}{4}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sin\varphi(x)}}\left(\sin\left[\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)(\sin(2 \varphi(x))-2\varphi(x))+\frac{3\pi}{4}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\;,

(4)

where
\varphi(x):=\arccos(x/\sqrt{2n+1})
is such that
\pi>\varphi(x)>0
.

We show how to use this approximation formula to construct the states
\ket{\phi_{n}}
efficiently, which are now simply superposition states
with amplitude modulated by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sin\varphi(x)}}
and also a phase that depends on
\varphi(x)
. We also show that the states
\ket{\phi_{n}}
have nonzero, constant overlap with the
\ket{\psi_{n}}
.

Fast-forwarding.

Central to this result is a factorization of the evolution operator
e^{-{\rm i}\hat{\mathrm{H}}t}
, where
\hat{\mathrm{H}}=(\hat{p}^{2}+\hat{x}^{2})/2
is the QHO Hamiltonian in the continuum. We exploit the canonical commutation relations, namely
\left[\hat{x},\hat{p}\right]={\rm i}I
, where
I
is the identity such that

. Then
[
QA07
]

\displaystyle\exp(- \ri\hamiltonian t)=\exp(-\frac{ \ri\tan(t/2)\momentum^2}{2})\exp(-\frac{\ri\sin(t)\position^2}{2})\exp(-\frac{\ri\tan(t/2)\momentum^2}{2})\;.

(5)

We then consider a discrete version of the QHO, referred to as

\overline{\mathrm{H}}
, and analyze the time complexity and the discretization error of this factorization. To this end,
we define
\overline{x}
and
\overline{p}
to be a specific choice of discretizations of
\hat{x}
and
\hat{p}
, defined in
[
Som16
]
, and let
\overline{\mathrm{H}}:=(\overline{p}^{2}+\overline{x}^{2})/2
.
We show that the factorization of
Equation

5
, when replacing
\hat{x}\rightarrow\overline{x}

and
\hat{p}\rightarrow\overline{p}
,
reproduces the evolution operator
e^{-{\rm i}\overline{\mathrm{H}}t}

within accuracy that is exponentially small in
N
in the low-energy subspace, where
N\leq M=\mathrm{poly}(N)
, and
M
is the Hilbert dimension of
\overline{\mathrm{H}}
.
The low-energy subspace is that spanned by the
N
eigenvectors of
\overline{\mathrm{H}}
of smallest eigenvalue.

To prove this result, we consider the commutation relations of the discrete operators
\overline{x}
and
\overline{p}
and show they approximate those between
\hat{x}
and
\hat{p}
, in the low-energy subspace, the space spanned by the first
N
eigenvectors of
\overline{\mathrm{H}}
. For example, in that subspace,
\left[\overline{x},\overline{p}\right]-{\rm i}I
can be shown to have norm exponentially small in
N
, where
I
is now the
N
-dimensional identity.
We also need to bound the errors of the
nested
commutators.
Let
\left[\overline{x}^{2},\overline{p}^{2}\right]_{k}=\left[\overline{x}^{2},\left[\overline{x}^{2},\overline{p}^{2}\right]_{k-1}\right]
be the

-th nested commutator, where
\left[\overline{x}^{2},\overline{p}^{2}\right]_{1}=\left[\overline{x}^{2},\overline{p}^{2}\right]
.
We cannot directly use the fact that the norm of

\left[\overline{x},\overline{p}\right]-{\rm i}I
is small on the low-energy subspace, because when nesting one would naively incur a cost which scales as
\approx M^{k-1}
due to the multiplication of
k-1
matrices after using the relation once. We show how to control the growth of these terms within the low-energy subspace by proving that (i) polynomials in the operators with degree
t
have norm which scale as
N^{t}
in the low-energy subspace, and (ii) certain operators which capture the discretization errors have norm
\mathrm{poly}(M)
on the entire space whose growth can be controlled by

when

is sufficiently greater than

. See
Section

3
for more details. We believe this technique would be useful for more Hamiltonian simulation results as well.

The approximated evolution operator of the discrete QHO also involves three exponentials. The exponential of
{\rm i}\sin(t)\overline{x}^{2}/2
is a diagonal unitary that can be simply implemented with
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}N)
two-qubit arbitrary gates regardless of

. (For simplicity throughout this paper we assume that arbitrary gates can be implemented perfectly, without any overhead in precision.)
To this end, we use coherent arithmetic for multiplication and then use phase kickback.
Since
\overline{p}
is obtained from

via conjugation with the (centered) QFT, the exponentials involving
\overline{p}^{2}
can also be implemented with
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}N)
two-qubit arbitrary gates. This is then an example of exponential fast-forwarding, since it avoids the no-fast-forwarding bound
\Omega(\|H\|t)
, which would be linear in

in this case.

3
Fast-forwarding of the QHO

In this section we discuss
Theorem

1
and present the formal results on the evolution of the QHO. First, let us discuss the algebraic factorization of the time-evolution of the quantum harmonic oscillator in the infinite-dimensional case. Since the Hamiltonian is the sum of two terms,
\hat{\mathrm{H}}=(\hat{x}^{2}+\hat{p}^{2})/2
, if the operators were commuting, we could simply factor by breaking up the sum. But the position and momentum operator do not commute. What we do know is the canonical commutation relation between them,

[\hat{x},\hat{p}]={\rm i}I
. Combined with the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula, this allows us to factor the time-evolution of the Hamiltonian. First, we notice these relations which can be checked by computation:

1.

[\hat{x}^{2},\hat{p}^{2}]=2{\rm i}\left\{\hat{x},\hat{p}\right\}

2.

[\hat{x}^{2},\{\hat{x},\hat{p}\}]=4{\rm i}\hat{x}^{2}

3.

[\hat{p}^{2},\{\hat{x},\hat{p}\}]=-4{\rm i}\hat{p}^{2}

Further,
Item

2
implies that
\left[\hat{x}^{2},\left[\hat{x}^{2},\left\{\hat{x},\hat{p}\right\}\right]\right]=0
.
Item

3
implies the symmetric statement about momentum. One thus finds that the Lie algebra generated by
\hat{x}^{2}
and
\hat{p}^{2}
is three dimensional, namely the span of
\hat{x}^{2}
,
\hat{p}^{2}
, and
\{\hat{x},\hat{p}\}
.
These facts about the nested commutators ‘kill off’ infinite terms in the BCH expansion and give us the following factorization for the evolution operator, also discussed in
[
QA07
]
.

Theorem 3

(Factorization of the QHO evolution
[
QA07
]
)
.

The evolution operator of the QHO admits the following factorization:

\displaystyle\exp(-\ri\hamiltonian t)=\exp(-\frac{ \ri\tan(t/2)\momentum^2}{2})\exp(-\frac{\ri\sin(t)\position^2}{2})\exp(\frac{-\ri\tan(t/2)\momentum^2}{2})\;.

(6)

Remark.

You might notice that these statements do not necessarily imply that the

eigenstate of the discrete Hamiltonian

is close to the
k^{\text{th}}
discrete Hermite state, in the case that eigenvalue gaps are exponentially small.
We point out that we do not have a rigorous proof that the eigenvalue gaps are not exponentially small; in principle one could worry that eigenstates from the high energy subspace, upon discretization, could jump down to the low energy subspace and land exponentially close to a low eigen-energy. In fact, numerically one observes that the high eigen-energies, though less accurately conforming to their continuum values than the low eigen-energies, never cross all the way into the low energy subspace.
Nonetheless, since we only care about the closeness to the discrete Hermite states, our claims about fast-forwarding are true irrespective of the eigenvalue gaps.

Note that our convention for basis states is such that

Transforming from one convention to the other is a cyclic permutation, i.e.,

Lemma 7

4
Quantum Hermite transform

Building on our fast-forwarding result, we can implement an efficient QHT if we can implement the state preparation, filtering, and QPE algorithms efficiently. We now discuss these steps and then present the algorithm. The main result of this section is the formal version of

Theorem 19

(Quantum Hermite transform, formal)
.

Let

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}\ket{n}\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}\ket{\psi_{n}}

Lemma 20

Let
N>0
be the dimension for the QHT and
and
\varepsilon>0
be the error.
For all
0\leq n\leq N-1
, let

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(x):=\frac{2^{\frac{1}{4}}}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}n^{\frac{1}{4}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sin\varphi(x)}}\left(\sin\left[\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)(\sin(2 \varphi(x))-2\varphi(x))+\frac{3\pi}{4}\right]\right)\times g_{n}(x)\;,

(41)

where
\varphi(x):=\arccos(x/\sqrt{2n+1})
,
be the Plancherel-Rotach approximation of the
n^{\rm th}
Hermite function.
The function
g_{n}(x)
is some smooth approximation to the indicator function and satisfies

\displaystyle g_{n}(x):=\left\{\begin{matrix}0&{\rm if}\ |x|\geq\sqrt{(3/4)(2n+1)}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})\;,\\
1&{\rm if}\ |x|\leq\sqrt{(3/4)(2n+1)}\;,\\
\in(0,1)&{\rm if}\ \sqrt{(3/4)(2n+1)}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})>|x|>\sqrt{(3/4)(2n+1)}\;.\end{matrix}\right.

(42)

For any
M>N
, define the
M
-dimensional ‘Plancherel-Rotach’ quantum states

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{n}}:=\left(\frac{2\pi}{M}\right)^{1/4}\sum_{j=-J(n)}^{J(n)-1}\phi_{n}(x_{j})\ket{j}\;,\;0\leq n\leq N-1\;,

(43)

where
x_{j}:=j\sqrt{2\pi/M}
denotes the discretized space coordinate, and

\displaystyle J(n):=\left\lceil\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}\frac{(2n+1)M}{2\pi}}\right\rceil

(44)

is such that
J(n)\sqrt{2\pi/M}\approx\sqrt{(3/4)(2n+1)}
and
J(n)<M/2
. Let
\ket{\psi_{n}}
be Hermite states of
Equation

2
, that is,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{n}}:=\left(\frac{2\pi}{M}\right)^{1/4}\sum_{j=-M/2}^{M/2-1}\psi_{n}(x_{j})\ket{j}\;,\;0\leq n\leq N-1\;.

(45)

Then, there exist constants
c>0
and
c^{\prime}>0

such that, for all
M\geq c(N)^{9/4}/\varepsilon^{13/4}
and all

0\leq n\leq N-1
, the overlap is

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{n}}\psi_{n}\rangle\geq c^{\prime}\;.

(46)

For every such
M
there exists
N_{\rm high}=\mathcal{O}(N/\varepsilon)
satisfying
N<N_{\rm high}<M

and,
for all
n\leq N-1
,

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{>N_{\rm high}}\ket{\phi_{n}}\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon\;,

(47)

where
\Pi_{>N_{\rm high}}
is
a ‘high-energy’ projector onto the subspace
orthogonal to the subspace spanned by
\{\ket{\psi_{n}}\}_{0\leq n\leq N_{\rm high}}
. (Note that

N_{\rm high}\ll M
asymptotically.)

Proof.

The quantum states
\ket{\phi_{n}}

are suggested by the Plancherel-Rotach approximation of
Lemma

20
. Asymptotically, they can be shown to be subnormalized for
n\leq N-1
because we are cutting off the domain by choosing
J(n)<M/2
.
Our goal is then to carry the Plancherel-Rotach approximation to the discrete space, which involves approximating integrals by finite sums. We also note that in the subspace of interest, where
n\leq N-1
, we will satisfy
n\ll M
asymptotically.

For each
n\leq N-1
, we are selecting as the domain of interest one where
|x|\leq x_{\max}:=\sqrt{(3/4)(2n+1)}
.
In the definition of
\ket{\phi_{n}}
, we let
x_{j}

run up to
\pm J(n)\sqrt{2\pi/M}
, and our choice of
J(n)
is such that
|x_{j}|
is upper bounded by
x_{\max}
up to an asymptotically small correction. This domain is purposely far from, and does not include, the turning points
\pm x_{\rm tp}=\pm\sqrt{(2n+1)}
where the Plancherel-Rotach approximations are known to fail (e.g.,
\varphi(x)=0
at
x=x_{\rm tp}
).

We will start by obtaining some properties of these approximations in the domain of interest. Note that rather than considering
Lemma

20
directly in this domain, we are modifying the approximations slightly to avoid issues when considering the momentum operator and the Fourier transform. For example, rather than assuming the functions to be exactly
0
for all
x
such that
|x|>x_{\max}
and oscillating otherwise, we introduced smooth envelope functions
g_{n}(x)
in the definition with the following properties (for each
n\geq 0
):

\displaystyle g_{n}(x):=\left\{\begin{matrix}0&{\rm if}\ |x|\geq x_{\max}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})\;,\\
1&{\rm if}\ |x|\leq x_{\max}\;,\\
\in(0,1)&{\rm if}\ x_{\max}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})>|x|>x_{\max}\;.\end{matrix}\right.

(48)

There is nothing special about the term

1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})
, other that it still guarantees the relevant
x
to be far from the turning points, and that the magnitudes of the derivatives of
g_{n}(x)
can be properly bounded. A good choice for these functions is given in Ref.
[
Som19
]
and obtained by convolving the indicator function with the bump function. Formally, if
\delta:=1/(20\sqrt{2n+1})
, these are the convolutions

\displaystyle g_{n}(x):=\frac{2a}{\delta}\int_{-x_{\max}-\delta}^{x_{\max}+\delta}{\rm d}x^{\prime}\exp(-\frac{1}{1-4(x'-x)^2/\delta^2})\;

(49)

The constant is
a\approx 2.25
.
We will use these to prove
Lemma

21
, but note that other choices can also work. Besides the properties in
Equation

48
, they also satisfy

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}g_{n}(x)=\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}g_{n}(x)=0
for
|x|>x_{\max}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})
and
|x|<x_{\max}
.
Also,
|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}g_{n}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{2n+1})

and
|\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}g_{n}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(2n+1)
.
From now on the domain where
|x|\leq x_{\max}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})
will be referred to as the ‘domain of interest’.

We will first establish the desired properties by working in the continuum, and use the following properties of the relevant functions.
In the domain of interest,
the Hermite functions satisfy

|\psi_{n}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(1/n^{1/4})
and we can use the property

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\psi_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sqrt{n}\psi_{n-1}(x)-\sqrt{n+1}\psi_{n+1}(x))

to show that
|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\psi_{n}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(n^{1/4})
, and Schrödinger equation

\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}\psi_{n}(x)=(x^{2}-(2n+1))\psi_{n}(x)

to show that

|\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}\psi_{n}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(n^{3/4})
.
According to
Lemma

20
, we have
\phi_{n}(x)=\psi_{n}(x)+\mathcal{O}(1/n^{5/4})
in this domain.
Also, we can use the bounds in
Appendix

C
and the chain rule for
\phi_{n}(x)=\tilde{\phi}_{n}(x)\times g_{n}(x)
to determine

|\phi_{n}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(1/n^{1/4})
,

|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\phi_{n}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(n^{1/4})
, and

|\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}\phi_{n}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(n^{3/4})

in the domain of interest.

These properties allow us to establish a lower bound on the overlap:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;\psi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-x_{\max}-1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})}^{x_{\max}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})}{\rm d}x\;\psi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}(x)

(50)

\displaystyle=\int_{-x_{\max}-1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})}^{x_{\max}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})}{\rm d}x\;\psi_{n}(x)(\psi_{n}(x)+\mathcal{O}(1/n^{5/4}))

(51)

\displaystyle=\int_{-x_{\max}}^{x_{\max}}{\rm d}x\;|\psi_{n}(x)|^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(1/(n+1/2)\right)\;.

(52)

In addition,

\displaystyle\int_{-x_{\max}}^{x_{\max}}{\rm d}x\;|\psi_{n}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-2\int_{x_{\max}}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;|\psi_{n}(x)|^{2}

(53)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{2}{(x_{\max})^{2}}\int_{x_{\max}}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;x^{2}|\psi_{n}(x)|^{2}

(54)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{2}{(x_{\max})^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;x^{2}|\psi_{n}(x)|^{2}

(55)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2(x_{\max})^{2}}(2n+1)

(56)

\displaystyle=1/3\;.

(57)

Hence, the overlap is lower bounded by
1/3+\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.
Numerical calculations show that this overlap actually approximates
2/3
and remains close to
2/3
for all
n\geq 0
.

Next, we show that the functions
\phi_{n}(x)
have negligible overlap with the high-energy sector, defined by some
N_{\rm high}
. This proof is the one that will use the smoothness property of
g_{n}(x)
(i.e., a bounded second derivative); otherwise, a sharp cutoff could result in high energies. Recall that in the continuum, the QHO Hamiltonian is

\frac{1}{2}(-\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}+x^{2})
.
The expected value of
x^{2}
on
\phi_{n}(x)
, corresponding to the potential term, satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;x^{2}|\phi_{n}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-x_{\max}}^{x_{\max}}{\rm d}x\;x^{2}|\phi_{n}(x)|^{2}

(58)

\displaystyle=\int_{-x_{\max}}^{x_{\max}}x^{2}|\psi_{n}(x)+\mathcal{O}(1/n^{5/4})|^{2}

(59)

\displaystyle=\int_{-x_{\max}}^{x_{\max}}{\rm d}x\;x^{2}|\psi_{n}(x)|^{2}+\mathcal{O}((x_{\max})^{3}/n^{3/2})

(60)

\displaystyle=\int_{-x_{\max}}^{x_{\max}}{\rm d}x\;x^{2}|\psi_{n}(x)|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(1)\;,

(61)

and we also know for the Hermite functions

\displaystyle\int_{-x_{\max}}^{x_{\max}}{\rm d}x\;x^{2}|\psi_{n}(x)|^{2}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;x^{2}|\psi_{n}(x)|^{2}=n+1/2\;.

(62)

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;x^{2}|\phi_{n}(x)|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n+1/2)\;.

(63)

The expected value of the kinetic term satisfies

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;\phi_{n}(x)\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}\phi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-x_{\max}-1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})}^{x_{\max}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})}{\rm d}x\;\phi_{n}(x)\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}\phi_{n}(x)

(64)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(n+1/2)\;,

(65)

where we used the above properties of
\phi_{n}(x)
.
It follows that the energy given by these functions, which is the expectation of the QHO Hamiltonian on
\phi_{n}(x)
, satisfies

\displaystyle{\rm E}\left[\frac{1}{2}(-\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}+x^{2})\right]\leq C(n+1/2)\;,

(66)

for some
C>0
that can be determined.
We can then use Markov’s inequality
to bound the support of
\phi_{n}(x)
in the high-energy space, spanned by Hermite functions with
n^{\prime}>N_{\rm high}
; that is

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime}=N_{\rm high}+1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;\phi_{n}(x)\psi_{n^{\prime}}(x)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{C(n+1/2)}{N_{\rm high}+1/2}\;.

(67)

We readily proved some desired features of the approximated Hermite functions
\phi_{n}(x)
in the oscillatory domain of interest. Our next goal is to prove that the finite-dimensional Plancherel-Rotach states of
Equation

43
satisfy similar properties: their overlaps with the discrete Hermite states
\ket{\psi_{n}}
is bounded by a constant, and their support on the high-energy subspace can be arbitrarily bounded.
We will obtain these results via approximations of integrals by finite sums. We will make repeatedly use of a standard trapezoidal rule:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{a}^{b}{\rm d}x\;f(x)-h\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{f(x_{k-1})+f(x_{k})}{2}\right|\leq\frac{(b-a)h^{2}}{12}\max_{x\in(a,b)}\left|\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}f(x)\right|\;.

(68)

Here
h
is the size of the discretization,
M=(b-a)/h
, and
x_{k}=a+kh
.
While this rule will suffice because the scaling of the algorithm is only logarithmic in the dimension, we note that improved results could be obtained with a detailed analysis that uses exponentially convergent trapezoidal rules, since the functions are smooth. Nevertheless, we follow the standard trapezoidal rule to simplify the proof.

Consider the overlap

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{n}}\phi_{n}\rangle=\left(\frac{2\pi}{M}\right)^{1/2}\sum_{j=-M/2}^{M/2}\psi_{n}(x_{j})\phi_{n}(x_{j})\;,

(69)

which approximates
Equation

50

using a discretization of size
\sqrt{2\pi/M}
. Indeed, using the trapezoidal rule above we can show that

\bra{\psi_{n}}\phi_{n}\rangle\approx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;\psi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}(x)
within additive error

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n+1/2}{M}\right)

(70)

since
\phi_{n}(x)
is zero for
|x|\geq x_{\max}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})
. For this we used
the properties of the functions to show

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}(\psi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}(x))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}\psi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}(x)\right|+2\left|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\psi_{n}(x)\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\phi_{n}(x)\right|+\left|\psi_{n}(x)\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}\phi_{n}(x)\right|

(71)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(n^{1/2})\;

(72)

Then, a lower bound to
\bra{\psi_{n}}\phi_{n}\rangle

is

\displaystyle 1/3+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n+1/2}{M}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n+1/2}\right)\;.

(73)

Note that we obtained a constant lower bound in the asymptotic regime, which suffices for our goal of the efficient Hermite transform (i.e., we will always choose
M
large enough to make
(n+1/2)/M
very small), but a suitable value of
c>0
in
Equation

46

for all
n\geq 0
can be obtained with a detailed analysis. Indeed, numerical simulations show that this overlap is approximately
2/3
for many
n
’s. See
Figure

3
.

Figure 3:
The overlap between the Plancherel-Rotach states and the Hermite states for dimension
M=10^{5}
and
0\leq n\leq 100
. The overlap approaches
2/3
, which corresponds to the probability mass of the Hermite functions in the domain
|x|\leq\sqrt{(3/4)(2n+1)}
.

Our next goal is to prove that the support of the Plancherel-Rotach states
\ket{\phi_{n}}
, where
0\leq n\leq N-1
, is arbitrarily small in the subspace of the discrete QHO states specified by an
N_{\rm high}>N
. In the continuum we already proved that, expressing
\phi_{n}(x)=\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n,n^{\prime}}\psi_{n^{\prime}}(x)
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime}>N_{\rm high}}|\alpha_{n,n^{\prime}}|^{2}\leq C\frac{n+1/2}{N_{\rm high}+1/2}\;.

(74)

This is equivalent to
Equation

67
since Hermite functions are orthogonal.
Consider now
\ket{\phi_{n}}
and
\ket{\psi_{n^{\prime}}}
for
0\leq n\leq N-1
and
0\leq n^{\prime}\leq N_{\rm high}
. Note that, in general for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{n^{\prime}}(x)\right|\lesssim 1.086\;,\;\left|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\psi_{n^{\prime}}(x)\right|\leq c(2n^{\prime}+1)^{1/4}\;,\;\left|\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}\psi_{n^{\prime}}(x)\right|\leq c^{\prime}(2n^{\prime}+1)^{3/4}\;.

(75)

for some constants
c>0
and
c^{\prime}>0
.
Since
n^{\prime}
can be larger than
n
, then we cannot use improved bounds for these functions. It follows that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}\left(\psi_{n^{\prime}}(x)\phi_{n}(x)\right)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left((N_{\rm high}+1/2)^{3/4}\right)\;.

(76)

Then, using the trapezoidal rule we have

\bra{\psi_{n^{\prime}}}\phi_{n}\rangle\approx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\;\psi_{n}^{\prime}(x)\phi_{n}(x)
within additive error

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(n+1/2)^{1/2}(N_{\rm high}+1/2)^{3/4}}{M}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(N_{\rm high}+1/2)^{5/4}}{M}\right)\;.

(77)

since we only need to integrate in the domain of interest.
Then,

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{n^{\prime}}}\phi_{n}\rangle=\alpha_{n,n^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(N_{\rm high}+1/2)^{5/4}}{M}\right)\;.

(78)

The trapezoidal rule also implies

\displaystyle\|\ket{\phi_{n}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{-x_{\max}-1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})}^{x_{\max}+1/(10\sqrt{2n+1})}{\rm d}x\;|\phi_{n}(x)|^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(n+1/2)^{5/4}}{M}\right)

(79)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{n,n^{\prime}}|^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(n+1/2)^{5/4}}{M}\right)\;,

(80)

since the prior bounds imply
|\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}\phi_{n}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(n^{3/4})
in the domain of interest.

Let
M>N_{\rm high}>N
.
Consider the subspace spanned by

\{\ket{\psi_{n}}\}_{0\leq n\leq N_{\rm high}}

and its orthogonal complement; both span
\mathbb{C}^{M}
.
Let
\Pi_{>N_{\rm high}}
be the projector onto the latter. Without loss of generality we can write

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{n}}=\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{N_{\rm high}}\beta_{n,n^{\prime}}\ket{\psi_{n^{\prime}}}+\ket{\phi_{n}^{\perp}}

(81)

where
\ket{\phi_{n}^{\perp}}=\Pi_{>N_{\rm high}}\ket{\phi_{n}}
is subnormalized and the amplitudes are
\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{N_{\rm high}}|\beta_{n,n^{\prime}}|^{2}\leq 1
.
Note that
\bra{\psi_{n^{\prime}}}\phi_{n}\rangle=\beta_{n,n^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}(N_{\rm high}\exp(-\Omega(M)))
for
n^{\prime}\leq N_{\rm high}
.
Our goal is to bound
\|\ket{\phi_{n}^{\perp}}\|
.
Combining the prior results we have

\displaystyle\|\ket{\phi_{n}^{\perp}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\ket{\phi_{n}}\|^{2}-\|\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{N_{\rm high}}\beta_{n,n^{\prime}}\ket{\psi_{n}}\|^{2}

(82)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{n,n^{\prime}}|^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(n+1/2)^{5/4}}{M}\right)-\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{N_{\rm high}}|\beta_{n,n^{\prime}}|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(N_{\rm high}\exp(-\Omega(M)))

(83)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{n,n^{\prime}}|^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(n+1/2)^{5/4}}{M}\right)-\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{N_{\rm high}}|\bra{\psi_{n^{\prime}}}\phi_{n}\rangle|^{2}+\mathcal{O}((N_{\rm high})^{2}\exp(-\Omega(M)))

(84)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{n,n^{\prime}}|^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(n+1/2)^{5/4}}{M}\right)-\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{N_{\rm high}}|\alpha_{n,n^{\prime}}|^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(N_{\rm high}\frac{(N_{\rm high}+1/2)^{5/4}}{M}\right)

(85)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n^{\prime}>N_{\rm high}}|\alpha_{n,n^{\prime}}|^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{N_{\rm high}^{9/4}}{M}\right)

(86)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{N+1/2}{N_{\rm high}+1/2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(N_{\rm high}+1/2)^{9/4}}{M}\right)\;.

(87)

We dropped the term exponentially small in
M
since it is asymptotically subdominant.
To establish
\|\ket{\phi_{n}^{\perp}}\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
it suffices to choose
N_{\rm high}=\Omega(N/\varepsilon)
and

M=\Omega((N_{\rm high})^{9/4}/\varepsilon)
.
This shows
Equation

47
and completes the proof.

∎

Efficient preparation of the Plancherel-Rotach states.

Thus far we considered the Plancherel-Rotach states defined in
Equation

43
as good initial states to prepare the corresponding
\ket{\psi_{n}}
, since they have overlap bounded by a positive constant. For the quantum Hermite transform to be efficient, we need to show that these states can be efficiently prepared.
Note that it suffices to produce
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
-approximations to the
\ket{\phi_{n}}
since this will imply a QHT with error
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
. Unfortunately, we cannot allow for a constant-error approximation to
\ket{\phi_{n}}
because we need to make sure that the support of the state in the high-energy space is still bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
.

Lemma 22

(Efficient state preparation)
.

Let
0\leq n\leq N-1
and
\varepsilon
the error. Then there is a quantum circuit that performs the map

\displaystyle\ket{n}\mapsto\ket{n}\frac{{\ket{\phi_{n}}}}{\|{\ket{\phi_{n}}}\|}

(88)

within error
\varepsilon
using
\mathcal{O}((\log N+\log(1/\eps))^{3})
gates.

Proof.

The states
\ket{\phi_{n}}
admit a simple form, being a linear combination of basis states with amplitudes proportional to

\displaystyle A_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sin\varphi(x)}}\times g_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{x^{2}}{2n+1}}}\times g_{n}(x)

(89)

and phases

\displaystyle\pm\Theta_{n}(x):=\pm\left[\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)\left(\sin(2\varphi(x) - 2 \varphi(x) )+\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)\right]\;,

(90)

where
\varphi(x)=\arccos(x/\sqrt{2n+1})
.
That is,
\phi_{n}(x)\propto A_{n}(x)(e^{+{\rm i}\Theta_{n}(x)}-e^{-{\rm i}\Theta_{n}(x)})
.
We will describe a method to prepare
a state with one of these phases, say
+\Theta_{n}(x)
, and the linear combination can be obtained via a simple Hadamard gate. This is because, while
\ket{\phi_{n}}
are subnormalized, they have norm bounded by a positive constant.
There are several known state preparation methods that can be useful for our context. For example, we could repurpose the algorithm of conditional rotations
[
Zal98
,
GR02
]
and, to restrict the domain to within the oscillatory region we can use quantum rejection sampling
[
ORR12
]
. A more recent approach is that based on inequality testing in
[
SLSB19
]

and is the one we will follow here.

For each
0\leq n\leq N-1
, the states
\ket{\phi_{n}}

are superpositions over basis states
\ket{j}
where, in our convention,
-J(n)\leq j\leq J(n)-1
, and
J(n)
is described in
Lemma

21
. They are
in the space
\mathbb{C}^{M}
, where the number of qubits is

m=\log_{2}M
.
To simplify the details of state preparation, we are going to use the standard convention where basis states are labeled as
\{\ket{0},\ket{1},\ldots,\ket{M-1}\}
. As mentioned, we can go from one convention to the other via a cyclic permutation (see below). Initially, we will consider setting the amplitudes proportional to
A_{n}(x)
and then we set the phases
e^{{\rm i}\Theta_{n}(x)}
. For the amplitudes we assume access to
an oracle
\mathfrak{amp}
that computes
A(x)

with
r
bits of precision in an additional register.
Note that
A(x)
is bounded by a constant in the oscillatory region of interest. We are going to introduce ancillary registers in many steps, but the relevant
parts are flagged by the states
\ket{0}
.

•

Step 1: Given
n
, compute the smallest integer
q
that satisfies
2^{q}\geq 2J(n)
.
Apply
q
Hadamard gates to map
\ket{0}^{\otimes m}\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{q}}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{q}-1}\ket{j}
.
Apply an inequality test to obtain
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{q}}}(\sum_{j=0}^{2J(n)-1}\ket{j}\ket{0}+\sum_{j=2J(n)}^{2^{q}-1}\ket{j}\ket{1}
, which flags
the relevant support.

•

Step 2: Use
\mathfrak{amp}
and inequality test
to perform the map
\ket{j}\mapsto\ket{j}\ket{\bar{A}_{n}(x_{j})}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{r}}}(\sum_{z=0}^{\bar{A}_{n}(x_{j})-1}\ket{z}\ket{0}+\sum_{\bar{A}_{n}(x_{j})}^{2^{r}}\ket{z}\ket{1})
, where
\bar{A}_{n}(x_{j})
is an
r
-bit approximation of
A_{n}(x_{j})
(rescaled by a constant).

•

Step 3: Apply
r
Hadamard gates and the inverse of
\mathfrak{amp}
to map the prior state to

\ket{j}\frac{\bar{A}_{n}(x_{j})}{2^{r}}\ket{0}^{\otimes r+1}+\ket{\omega_{j}^{\perp}}
, where
\ket{\omega_{j}^{\perp}}
is orthogonal to
\ket{0}^{\otimes r+1}
and subnormalized.

Following this steps we have essentially prepared
the state

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{q}}}\sum_{j=0}^{2J(n)-1}\frac{\bar{A}_{n}(x_{j})}{2^{r}}\ket{j}\ket{0}^{\otimes r+2}+\ket{\omega_{n}^{\perp}}

(91)

where again
\ket{\omega_{n}^{\perp}}
is subnormalized
and orthogonal to
\ket{0}^{\otimes r+2}
.
We can use fixed point amplitude amplification to select the desired part of this state and obtain an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
approximation to

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2J(n)-1}\frac{\bar{A}_{n}(x_{j})}{2^{r}}\ket{j}}{\|\sum_{j=0}^{2J(n)-1}\frac{\bar{A}_{n}(x_{j})}{2^{r}}\ket{j}\|}\;.

(92)

Amplitude amplification introduces a multiplicative cost that is
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\eps))
, since the relevant overlaps are constant.

We discuss the relevant complexities of this approach.
First note that it suffices to prepare an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)

approximation to
\ket{\phi_{n}}
, since the QHT is performed within this precision and
\ket{\phi_{n}}
has overlap with
\ket{\psi_{n}}
bounded by a constant.
It then suffices to compute the amplitudes
A(x)

within multiplicative error
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, but since these are bounded from above by a constant, we can compute them within additive error
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
. It follows that the number of bits of precision is
r=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\eps))
.
We can then construct
\mathfrak{amp}
using coherent arithmetics; one procedure would involve first computing
\varphi(x)
within error
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, which follows if
x/\sqrt{2n+1}
is obtained within that complexity, and then computing

1/\sqrt{\sin\varphi(x)}
within this error.
For this step it suffices to first compute

\sqrt{2\pi/M}/\sqrt{2n+1}
within error
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon/M)
,
which can be accomplished with cost
\mathcal{O}((\log M+\log(1/\eps))^{2})
. Then we multiply with
j
, and since
j
is expressed with
\log M
bits, this is subdominant. We also need to compute
g_{n}(x)
within error
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
. Since
g_{n}(x)
can be obtained by rescaling some
g(x)

[
Som19
]
, this translates to computing
g(x)

within error
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon/\sqrt{n})
.
This function involves the bump function and for this precision the cost is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}((\log n+\log(1/\eps))^{2})
, hiding a doubly logarithmic cost.
This function is smooth and also involves an integral, and this increases the cost by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\eps))
. So the total cost of computing
g_{n}(x)
is
\mathcal{O}((\log n+\log(1/\eps))^{2}\log(1/\eps))
.

There are additional gates used in the procedure, the Hadamard gates and those for inequality tests, which are also subdominant.

The next step involves including the phases.
Again, it suffices to compute these phases within
r^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\eps))
bits of precision.
We can construct an oracle
\mathfrak{phase}

that performs the map
\ket{j}\mapsto\ket{j}\ket{\bar{\Theta}(x_{j})}
and then use phase kickback and the inverse of
\mathfrak{phase}
and obtain
e^{{\rm i}\bar{\Theta}(x_{j})}\ket{j}
. To this end it suffices to compute
\varphi(x)
within additive error
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon/N)
.
This follows if
x/\sqrt{2n+1}
is computed with that error which again can be done with cost
\mathcal{O}((\log M+\log(1/\eps))^{2})
, as explained in the construction of
\mathfrak{amp}
.

The last step is to perform the cyclic permutation, since in the standard convention the states
\ket{\phi_{n}}

involve a superposition of basis states from
-J(n)+M/2
to
J(n)-1+M/2
. This can be accomplished
with gate cost that is
\log^{2}M
using the standard QFT,
as explained in
[
Som16
]
.

According to
Lemma

21
,
M=\mathrm{poly}(N,1/\varepsilon)
and including the cost of amplitude amplification to obtain the normalized state, the result follows.

∎

Fast eigenstate filtering.

The next step in the QHT is that of state ‘filtering’, which is a transformation that ideally would flag the Hermite state and perform the map

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{n}}\mapsto\beta_{n}\ket{\psi_{n}}\ket{0}^{\otimes m}+\ket{0_{n}^{\perp}}\;,

(93)

where
\beta_{n}=\bra{\psi_{n}}\phi_{n}\rangle
is the (bounded) overlap, and
\ket{0}^{\otimes q}
and
\ket{0_{n}^{\perp}}

are orthogonal states of the ancilla qubits.
Since we would like to implement this transformation for an exponentially large set of values of
0\leq n\leq N-1

Section

3
with the conventional QPE algorithm
[

]
.

Lemma 23
.

and
\varepsilon

For given

and

(97)

again for

As a next step we need to show that amplitude amplification enables

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\|\ket{\phi_{n}}\|}\ket{n}(\beta_{n}\ket{\psi_{n}}\ket{0}^{\otimes m}+\ket{0^{\perp}_{n}})\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}\ket{n}\ket{\psi_{n}}\;,

These will ultimately give the desired mapping even when the overlaps

Lemma 24
.

Let
N>0
and
\varepsilon
the error.

Lemma 25

(Fixed-point amplitude amplification, Thm. 27 of Ref.
[
GSLW19
]
)
.

Let
U
be a unitary and
\Pi
be an orthogonal
projector such that

, and
a>\delta>0

\tilde{U}
such that

\|\ket{\Psi_{G}}-\tilde{U}\ket{\Psi_{0}}\|\leq\varepsilon
, which uses a single ancilla qubit and consists of
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\eps)/\delta)

U
,
U^{\dagger}
,
C_{\Pi}NOT
,
C_{\outerproduct{\Psi_0}{\Psi_0}}NOT
, and
e^{{\rm i}\phi\sigma_{Z}}
one qubit gates.

Here,
a
is unknown but the lower bound
\delta
is known. The operation

is simply an operation that coherently checks whether the state is supported in
\Pi

\ket{0}

A generalization of this lemma, which we give below as it can be of independent interest, is used in the proof of

Lemma 26

(Fixed-point amplitude amplification in subspaces)
.

Let
N>0
be the dimension.
For all
0\leq n\leq N-1
, let
U_{n}
be a unitary,

\Pi_{n}
be an orthogonal projector such that
a_{n}\ket{\Psi_{G}^{n}}=\Pi_{n}U_{n}\ket{\Psi_{0}^{n}}
, and
a_{n}>\delta>0
.
There is a unitary quantum circuit
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
such that

\|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}\ket{n}\ket{\Psi_{G}^{n}}-\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}\ket{n}\ket{\Psi_{0}^{n}}\|\leq\varepsilon
,
where the amplitudes
\alpha_{n}
are arbitrary and
\sum_{n}|\alpha_{n}|^{2}=1

\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\eps)/\delta)

\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
,
C_{\Pi}NOT
,
C_{\Pi_{0}}NOT
, and
e^{{\rm i}\phi\sigma_{Z}}
one qubit gates, where
\mathcal{U}=\sum_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}\otimes U_{n}

Proof.

Fixed-point amplitude amplification is based on Chebyshev approximations; in the case of
Lemma

25
,
we can assume these to be, for example, linear combinations of Chebyshev polynomials of

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}0&A\cr A^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}\;,\;A:=\outerproduct{\Psi_0}{\Psi_0}U^{\dagger}\Pi=\bar{a}\ket{\Psi_{0}}\bra{\Psi_{G}}\;,

(100)

where
\bar{a}
is the complex conjugate.
The linear combination can be implemented with techniques like QSVT and implements the map
\ket{\Psi_{0}}\mapsto\ket{\Psi_{G}}
.
The complexity is determined by the degree of the Chebyshev polynomials in the approximation, and this is
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\eps)/\delta)
; even when
a
is unknown (but
\delta
is known), the polynomial allows for the correct mapping.

To work in subspaces, we simply replace
B
by the direct sum:
B:=\bigoplus_{n}B_{n}
, where

\displaystyle B_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&A_{n}\cr A_{n}^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}\;,\;A_{n}:=\outerproduct{\Psi_0^n}{\Psi_0^n}U_{n}^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}=\bar{a}_{n}\ket{\Psi_{0}^{n}}\bra{\Psi_{G}^{n}}\;.

(101)

\ket{\Psi_{0}^{n}}\mapsto\ket{\Psi_{G}^{n}}
, within error
\varepsilon
, and the transformation carries to an arbitrary state
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}\ket{n}\ket{\Psi_{0}^{n}}
due to linearity. Note that while fixed-point amplitude amplification uses an ancilla qubit, the ancilla state stays in
\ket{0}
at the end, within error
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, for all
n
.
Since we are using the same approximation, the degree is unchanged and still

\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\eps)/\delta)
.

We also note that
B\equiv\sum_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}\otimes B_{n}
,
and accordingly we can define

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\left(\sum_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}\otimes\outerproduct{\Psi_0^n}{\Psi_0^n}\right)\left(\sum_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}\otimes U_{n}^{\dagger}\right)\left(\sum_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}\otimes\Pi_{n}\right)=\sum_{n}\outerproduct{n}{n}\otimes A_{n}\;.

(102)

It follows that we can build the walk operator to implement the Chebyshev polynomials using
\mathcal{U}
,
C_{\Pi}NOT
,
C_{\Pi_{0}}NOT

Uncomputation via quantum phase estimation.

The last step is to ‘uncompute’ the register that contains
\ket{n}
, that is, to perform the map

\displaystyle\ket{n}\ket{\psi_{n}}\mapsto\ket{0}^{\otimes m}\ket{\psi_{n}}\;.

(104)

Similar to eigenstate filtering, we can leverage the fast-forwarding property to perform exponentially-precise QPE that would allows us to obtain
\ket{n}
on input
\ket{\psi_{n}}
. QPE can then be used to uncompute.

Lemma 27
.

Let
0\leq n\leq N-1
and
\varepsilon
the error, where
N>\log(1/\eps)
. Then there is a quantum circuit that performs the map in

Equation

104
within error
\varepsilon
using
\mathcal{O}((\log N+\log(1/\eps))^{3})
gates.

Proof.

As before, for given
N
and
\varepsilon
, we set the dimension
M=2^{m}
and
N_{\rm high}
as in
Lemma

21
.
Let
V_{j}:=V(2^{j}(2\pi/M))e^{{\rm i}2^{j}(2\pi/M)/2}
for
0\leq j\leq m-1
, where
V(t)
is the approximation to
U(t)=e^{-{\rm i}\overline{\mathrm{H}}t}
using the three exponentials in
Theorem

5
. Consider the action of the QPE algorithm using
m
ancilla qubits initially in uniform superposition and using
controlled-
V_{j}
, on the Hermite state.
Prior to the action of the QFT, the state is transformed as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{l_{0},\ldots,l_{m-1}\in\{0,1\}}\ket{l_{0}\ldots l_{m-1}}\ket{\psi_{n}}\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{l_{0},\ldots,l_{m-1}\in\{0,1\}}\ket{l_{0}\ldots l_{m-1}}(V_{m-1})^{l_{m-1}}\ldots(V_{0})^{l_{0}}\ket{\psi_{n}}\;,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{l_{0},\ldots,l_{m-1}\in\{0,1\}}e^{-{\rm i}(2\pi n/M)(l_{0}+\ldots+2^{m-1}l_{m-1})}\ket{l_{0}\ldots l_{m-1}}\ket{\psi_{n}}+\mathcal{O}(\exp(-\Omega(N)))\;.

4.2
The algorithm

We are now ready to provide a quantum algorithm that implements the QHT. This essentially follows the steps outlined in

Algorithm 2

Quantum Hermite Transform

1:

Let
N>0
be the dimension of the QHT and
\epsilon>0
be the error. Set
M=\mathcal{O}(N^{9/4}/\varepsilon^{13/4})

2:

For an

5
Hermite sampling

In this section we discuss an application of the Hermite transform in theoretical computer science. We solve problems in property testing and learning using Hermite sampling as a subroutine. The goal here is to return a sample
v\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with probability
|\widehat{f_{v}}|^{2}
, where
\widehat{f_{v}}
is the coefficient of the Hermite polynomial corresponding to
v
in the representation of
f
. This gives a simple algorithm to solve the Gaussian Goldreich-Levin problem, an analogue of the well-known Goldreich-Levin problem in boolean functions and cryptography, in the Hermite function basis. We also show some property testing results which also hold for boolean functions using QFT as a subroutine, although we do not elaborate on that here.

Suppose we have a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-1,1]
and a discrete set of input points
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. We fix
P\in\mathbb{N}
assume that
f
is constant over subcubes of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose endpoints are integer multiples of
2^{-P_{1}}
. In other words,
f
acts with
P_{1}
bits of precision. Furthermore, we assume the output of
f
is recorded with
P_{2}
bits of precision. Finally, we assume that
f
is “well-conditioned” in the sense that there exists a
distortion

\kappa>0
such that
\left\lVert f\right\rVert\geq\kappa^{-1}
. This distortion parameter captures the “spikiness” of
f
, it is the max value of
f
(one can imagine the tails are cut off) divided by the average
\ell_{2}
-norm of
f
under the Gaussian distribution. The dependence on this parameter comes from the complexity of preparing a state whose amplitudes are proportional to the values of
f
using phase kickback, conditional rotations, and postselection.

We note that technically we do not need the full power of the Hermite transform in this section. In particular, we do not need to perform the uncomputation step (
Lemma

27
) to be able to sample proportional to
|\widehat{f_{v}}|^{2}
, since the absolute value is agnostic to the relative phase.

In the following section, we give an algorithm that takes an oracle to
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
and implements Hermite sampling, for a cleaner exposition. Later in
Section

5.2
, we generalize this to general functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Finally, in
Sections

5.3
and
5.4
we give applications in property testing and learning, respectively.

5.1
Boolean-output functions

In this section we restrict our attention to Boolean-output functions – that is, functions whose outputs are in
\{-1,+1\}
. Since there is no output precision, we write
P
, as opposed to
P_{1}
, to be the input precision of
f
, for simplicity.

For a
resolution parameter

h
and bound

define

\mathcal{S}_{h}[-L,L]=\left\{kh:k\in\mathbb{Z},-L\leq kh<L\right\}.

This constitutes a set of points with

bits of precision over which we will discretize the QHO eigenstates. Define

. This is the number of evaluation points. We will use the state preparation algorithm from
Section

4.1
to construct approximate hermite states of dimension

M=\max\{2C^{-1}L\cdot 2^{P}P\cdot(n+\log n+\log(8/\eps)),40\gamma D\log(2 \degree)\}.

For simplicity, we first give an algorithm for the case where the output of

is

.

In the remainder of the section, we will prove the correctness of
Algorithm

3
. For succinctness we write for some
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
\ket{\overline{\psi_{x_{1}}}}\otimes\ldots\ket{\overline{\psi_{x_{n}}}}
as
\ket{\overline{\psi_{x}}}
in the rest of this section. We begin by employing a standard result in approximation theory which states that Riemann sums converge exponentially fast for analytic functions such as the hermite functions.

Fact 28

(
[
TCW14
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be analytic on a strip
\mathcal{S}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\real(z)\in[a,b],\left\lvert\imaginary(z)\right\rvert\leq a\}
and suppose further that
|f(z)|\leq Q
for
z
in this strip. Then we have

\left\lvert\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\ell=0}^{M-1}f\left(a+\frac{(b-a)\ell}{M}\right)-\int_{a}^{b}f(t)dt\right\rvert\leq\frac{2\pi Q}{e^{2\pi aM}-1}\leq 4\pi Qe^{-2\pi aM}.

We show that the product of convergent univariate Riemann sums converges to the product of the corresponding integrals.

Proposition 29
.

Let
\{a_{i}^{(k)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
be finite sequences for
k\in[n]
. For each
k
choose a
b_{k}
such that

\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(k)}-b_{k}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

Furthermore, suppose that there exists an
Q
such that for all
k

\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(k)}\right\rvert\leq L,\quad\left\lvert b_{k}\right\rvert\leq Q.

Then we have

\left\lvert\prod_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(k)}-\prod_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}\right\rvert\leq nQ^{n-1}\varepsilon.

Proof.

The proof proceeds via a simple hybrid argument. Let

H_{0}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(k)},\ H_{1}=b_{1}\cdot\prod_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(k)},\ ...,\ H_{n}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}.

For
\ell\in[n]
we bound the difference between the hybrids
H_{\ell}
and
H_{\ell-1}
. We have

\displaystyle\left\lvert H_{\ell}-H_{\ell-1}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}b_{k}\cdot\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(k)}-\prod_{k=1}^{\ell-1}b_{k}\cdot\prod_{k=\ell}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(k)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\prod_{k=1}^{\ell-1}b_{k}\cdot\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(k)}\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(\ell)}-b_{\ell}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=Q^{\ell-1}\cdot Q^{n-\ell}\cdot\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(\ell)}-b_{\ell}\right\rvert\leq Q^{n-1}\varepsilon.

Since this holds for any choice of
\ell\in[n]
, we have

\left\lvert\prod_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{(k)}-\prod_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}\right\rvert=\left\lvert H_{0}-H_{n}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left\lvert H_{\ell+1}-H_{\ell}\right\rvert\leq nQ^{n-1}\varepsilon.\qed

Next, we bound the probability mass of hermite functions outside the region
L
. In the sections that follow, we denote by
\nu
the Gaussian probability density function
e^{-x^{2}}/\sqrt{2\pi}
. Recall that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{k}(x)H_{\ell}(x)\nu(x)dx=\delta_{k\ell}
.

Proposition 30
.

Fix
L>1
and let
R=[-L,L]
. We have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus R^{n}}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)\leq e^{-nL^{2}/4}

Proof.

Define
R=[-L,L]^{n}
. We have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus R}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus R}f(x)\nu^{1/2}(x)H_{v}(x)\nu^{1/2}(x)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus R}f(x)^{2}\nu(x)}\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus R}H_{v}(x)^{2}\nu(x)}

(Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus R}\nu(x)}\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H_{v}(x)^{2}\nu(x)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(2\int_{L}^{\infty}\nu(x)\right)^{n/2}\leq e^{-nL^{2}/4}.\qed

As our first major step towards proving the correctness of
Algorithm

3
, we show that Riemann sums that are approximately induced by the discretized QHO eigenstates converges to the correct quantity.

Proposition 31
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-1,1]
be constant on subsets of the form
[2^{-P}k,2^{-P}(k+1)]
. For a sufficiently large
M\in\mathbb{N}
, take
h=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{M}}
,
L=\sqrt{\frac{\pi M}{2}}
, and
S_{1}=\mathcal{S}_{h}[-L,L]
. Then for any
v\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,

\left\lvert\sum_{x\in S_{1}^{n}}M^{-n}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)\right\rvert\leq 2n\cdot(2L\cdot 2^{P})^{n}e^{-2^{-P}\cdot(M/2L)^{2}}+e^{-nL^{2}/4}

Proof.

For each
x\in S
, define
S_{2}(x)=\mathcal{S}_{h}[x-2^{-P},x]
and
T_{2}(x)=\{[x,x+h]:x\in S_{2}(x)\}
. We remark that the
T_{2}(x)^{n}
are cubes over which
f
is constant, by assumption.
We denote

R=\sum_{x\in S_{1}^{n}}M^{-n}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x).

This is a Riemann sum that approximates the integral
f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)
within the region
[-L,L]
. First we bound the error of this approximation and later show using
Proposition

30
that restricting to this region accrues very little error.

\displaystyle\left\lvert R-\int_{[-L,L]^{n}}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{x\in S_{2}^{n}}\left(M^{-n}\sum_{y\in S_{2}(x)^{n}}f(y)H_{v}(y)\nu(y)-\int_{T_{2}(x)}f(y)H_{v}(y)\nu(y)dy\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{x\in S_{2}^{n}}f(x)\left(M^{-n}\sum_{y\in S_{2}(x)^{n}}H_{v}(y)\nu(y)-\int_{T_{2}(x)}H_{v}(y)\nu(y)dy\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in S_{2}^{n}}\left\lvert M^{-n}\sum_{y\in S_{2}(x)^{n}}H_{v}(y)\nu(y)-\int_{T_{2}(x)}H_{v}(y)\nu(y)dy\right\rvert

We will bound the inner term of the above sum. First, we observe that the both the sum and the integral can be expressed as products of individual sums and integrals. Thus, applying
Proposition

29
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert M^{-n}\sum_{y\in S_{2}(x)^{n}}H_{v}(y)\nu(y)-\int_{T_{2}(x)}H_{v}(y)\nu(y)dy\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\max_{i\in[n]}\left\lvert\frac{1}{M}\sum_{z\in S_{2}(x_{i})}H_{v_{i}}(z)\nu(z)-\int_{T_{2}(x_{i})}H_{v_{i}}(z)\nu(z)dz\right\rvert

Note that the variable
z
above is univariate. We can now use
28
to bound the inner term. Indeed, invoking the application of the trapezoid rule in
Equation

17
, we have for any

\left\lvert\frac{1}{M}\sum_{z\in S_{2}(x_{i})}H_{v_{i}}(z)\nu(z)-\int_{T_{2}(x_{i})}H_{v_{i}}(z)\nu(z)dz\right\rvert\leq e^{-2^{-P}\cdot(M/2L)^{2}}

whenever
M>3\cdot 2^{P}
.
The sum over
S_{2}^{n}
results in an additional multiplicative factor of
(2L2^{P})^{n}
. As such, we have

\left\lvert R-\int_{[-L,L]^{n}}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)dx\right\rvert\leq 2n\cdot(2L\cdot 2^{P})^{n}e^{-2^{-P}\cdot(M/2L)^{2}}

We now conclude the proof by showing that the exclusion of the region
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus[-L,L]^{n}
results in a very small additive error. Indeed, applying
Proposition

30
we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert R-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert R-\int_{[-L,L]^{n}}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)dx\right\rvert+\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus[-L,L]^{n}}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2n\cdot(2L\cdot 2^{P})^{n}e^{-2^{-P}\cdot(M/2L)^{2}}+\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus[-L,L]^{n}}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x)dx\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2n\cdot(2L\cdot 2^{P})^{n}e^{-2^{-P}\cdot(M/2L)^{2}}+e^{-nL^{2}/4}.\qed

Now we show that applying the oracle to the discrete, unnormalized ground state
\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}
and measuring in the
\ket{\psi_{v}}

\widehat{f}(v)

Lemma 32
.

For each
v\in[D]^{n}
, let
\ket{\psi_{v}}=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}\ket{\psi_{v_{i}}}
, where each
\ket{\psi_{v_{i}}}

\left\lvert\bra{\psi_{v}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}-\widehat{f}(v)\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon/4.

Proof.

We can write

\bra{\psi_{v}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}=M^{-n}\sum_{x\in S_{1}^{n}}f(x)H_{v}(x)\nu(x).

By
Proposition

31
, we have the bound

\left\lvert\left\lvert\bra{\psi_{v}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}-\widehat{f}(v)\right\rvert\right\rvert\leq 2n\cdot(2L\cdot 2^{P})^{n}e^{-2^{-P}\cdot(M/2L)^{2}}+e^{-nL^{2}/4}.

For our choice of

\varepsilon/8+\varepsilon/8=\varepsilon/4
.
∎

Since the (orthonormal) approximations
\ket{\overline{\psi}}
of the discrete QHO eigenstates are very close in trace distance to the
\ket{\psi}

Corollary 33
.

For each
v\in[D]^{n}

\ket{\overline{\psi_{v}}}=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}\ket{\overline{\psi_{v_{i}}}}
, where each
\ket{\overline{\psi_{v_{i}}}}

\left\lvert\bra{\overline{\psi_{v}}}U_{f}\ket{\overline{\psi_{0^{n}}}}-\widehat{f}(v)\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon/2.

Now we argue that replacing
\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}

\ket{\overline{\psi_{0^{n}}}}

\dist_{\text{tr}}\left(\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}},\ket{\overline{\psi_{0^{n}}}}\right)\leq n\dist_{\text{tr}}\left(\ket{\psi_{0}},\ket{\overline{\psi_{0}}}\right)\leq n\exp(-\sommaconstant M).

Thus we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bra{\overline{\psi_{v}}}U_{f}\ket{\overline{\psi_{0^{n}}}}-\widehat{f}(v)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\bra{\overline{\psi_{v}}}U_{f}\ket{\overline{\psi_{0^{n}}}}-\bra{\overline{\psi_{v}}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\bra{\overline{\psi_{v}}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}-\widehat{f}(v)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq n\exp(- \sommaconstant M)+\left\lvert\bra{\overline{\psi_{v}}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}-\widehat{f}(v)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon/12+\left\lvert\bra{\overline{\psi_{v}}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}-\bra{\psi_{v}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\bra{\psi_{v}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}-\widehat{f}(v)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\bra{\overline{\psi_{v}}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}-\bra{\psi_{v}}U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}\right\rvert+\varepsilon/3

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\left(\bra{\overline{\psi_{v}}}-\bra{\psi_{v}}\right)U_{f}\ket{\psi_{0^{n}}}\right\rvert+\varepsilon/3

\displaystyle\leq n\exp(-\sommaconstant M)+\varepsilon/3\leq\varepsilon/2.\qed

Algorithm

(label=thm:apx-hermite)

For each
v\in[D]^{n},

\left\lvert p_{v}-\widehat{f}(v)^{2}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon

The correctness of the algorithm follows from

\displaystyle\left\lvert p_{v}-\widehat{f}(v)^{2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\left\lvert\bra{\overline{\psi_{S}}}U_{f}\ket{\overline{\psi_{0^{n}}}}\right\rvert^{2}-\widehat{f}(v)^{2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\left\lvert\bra{\overline{\psi_{S}}}U_{f}\ket{\overline{\psi_{0^{n}}}}\right\rvert+\widehat{f}(v)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\left\lvert\bra{\overline{\psi_{S}}}U_{f}\ket{\overline{\psi_{0^{n}}}}\right\rvert^{2}-\widehat{f}(v)^{2}\right\rvert

Claim 69

Proof.

Let

If we can prove

By direct calculation,

Using the Campbell–Hausdorff lemma

e^{X}Ye^{-X}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}[X,Y]_{j},\qquad[X,Y]_{0}=Y,\;[X,Y]_{1}=[X,Y],\;[X,Y]_{2}=[X,[X,Y]],\ldots,

we compute the conjugations step by step:

Substituting back and simplifying trigonometrically shows that
