Within-person differences in night-shift burden and well-being after ICU versus non-ICU night shifts among resident physicians: a paired survey study
Authors: Kun Li, Tingting Liu, Xuejia Pan, Zhuojun Shen, Xueling Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNight shifts are integral to residency training but may disrupt sleep and recovery. ICU versus non-ICU comparisons are often confounded by between-person differences, and night-shift exposure is often operationalized using simple shift counts rather than more proximal indicators of sleep disruption and recovery.MethodsWe conducted a single-center within-person paired survey study (August 2025–January 2026) in a tertiary teaching hospital. Residents completed the same questionnaire within 24 h after one non-ICU night shift (T1) and after their second ICU night shift (T2). Night-shift burden was assessed using self-reported indicators including duty duration, sleep during the shift, work-related awakenings/calls, and post-shift recovery. Within-person ICU versus non-ICU differences were examined using paired analyses. Associations with emotional exhaustion were evaluated using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a Gaussian family, exchangeable working correlation structure, robust standard errors, and prespecified burden indicators.ResultsSixty-one residents provided paired responses. Compared with non-ICU night shifts, ICU night shifts were characterized by higher duty duration category (P < 0.001), sleep during the shift shifted toward lower categories (1.95 ± 0.72 vs. 2.72 ± 0.92; P < 0.001), and work-related awakenings/calls shifted toward higher categories (3.39 ± 0.71 vs. 2.79 ± 0.99; P < 0.001). Emotional exhaustion was higher after ICU night shifts (mean paired difference + 3.39; 95% CI 1.00 to 5.79; P = 0.006), and life satisfaction was lower (− 8.90; 95% CI − 11.41 to − 6.40; P < 0.001). In the adjusted GEE model, ICU timepoint remained associated with higher exhaustion (β = 2.94; 95% CI 0.12 to 5.76; P = 0.041), and work-related awakenings/calls were strongly associated with higher exhaustion (β = 3.37; 95% CI 1.37 to 5.38; P < 0.001).ConclusionsWithin the same residents, ICU night shifts were associated with greater night-shift burden and poorer immediate well-being than non-ICU night shifts. These findings should be interpreted in light of the single-center design, modest sample size, self-reported measures, and fixed measurement order. A multidimensional burden framework may help training programs identify modifiable aspects of night duty, including sleep opportunity, interruption load, and recovery time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09633-6.Keywords: Residency training, Medical education, Intensive care unit, Night shift, Sleep disruption, Work-related interruptions, Emotional exhaustion, Life satisfaction, Burnout, Learning environment, Trainee well-being

Introduction

Residency training is a high-stakes phase of medical education in which trainees must rapidly develop clinical competence while assuming increasing responsibility for patient care. In teaching hospitals, residents are expected to learn effectively within complex clinical environments characterized by heavy workloads, frequent transitions, and irregular schedules. Night-shift work is a core component of hospital-based residency programs because continuous patient care is required; however, it is also among the most physiologically disruptive elements of training. Reflecting a growing emphasis on safe learning environments and trainee well-being, graduate medical education bodies have increasingly highlighted duty hours and humane working conditions as integral to professional development and patient safety [1].

Night shifts can impose a multidimensional “burden” through circadian misalignment, sleep restriction, frequent interruptions, and limited opportunities for recovery. Classic studies in intensive care settings showed that eliminating extended work shifts among interns increased sleep and reduced attentional failures during night work [2]. In parallel, redesigning duty schedules to reduce prolonged shifts was associated with fewer serious medical errors in intensive care units, underscoring that the biological costs of night work can translate into clinically meaningful consequences [3]. These findings helped establish fatigue and sleep loss as central concerns in residency scheduling and patient safety.

More recent evidence suggests that the impact of night work is heterogeneous and depends on specific scheduling models (e.g., overnight call versus night-float systems), the availability of protected rest, and the regularity of sleep patterns. Objective and near-objective assessments have linked extended shifts and schedule characteristics to sleep and alertness outcomes among trainees [4, 5]. In a recent cohort study conducted in a Singapore-based teaching hospital, interns working 24-hour call schedules experienced poorer sleep and well-being outcomes than those on float schedules, and naps during night shifts appeared beneficial for vigilance [6]. Similarly, studies of night-float rotations have continued to raise concerns regarding changes in sleep architecture, fatigue, and affect during night work [7]. Collectively, these findings highlight that “night-shift exposure” is not a single, dose-like construct; rather, its intensity and downstream impact depend on how night duty is organized and experienced, including sleep obtained during duty, work-related awakenings, and post-shift recovery.

From the perspective of medical education, resident well-being is increasingly viewed as an outcome of the training environment rather than an individual issue alone. Psychological and affective symptoms among residents are common [8]. Burnout is also highly prevalent among residents across specialties, as supported by systematic review evidence [9]. Beyond personal suffering, burnout has been associated with adverse consequences for clinicians and health systems—implicating workforce sustainability and potentially shaping professionalism and the learning climate within training programs [10]. Understanding how scheduling demands relate to resident well-being is therefore central to improving both training quality and trainee support.

The intensive care unit (ICU) is a distinctive clinical learning environment that may amplify night-shift burden. ICU night shifts often involve high-acuity decision-making, sustained vigilance, frequent interruptions, and emotionally demanding care under conditions of reduced staffing and circadian vulnerability. Evidence comparing ICU and general medicine rotations suggests that rotation context may matter for sleep-related outcomes; for example, a secondary analysis from the iCOMPARE trial reported differences in sleep and alertness-related measures between ICU and general medicine rotations [11]. Observational work has also linked night-shift work among resident physicians to mood and cognitive outcomes, although patterns may vary by specialty and setting [12]. These findings motivate education-focused inquiry into ICU versus non-ICU night work as potentially different training exposures with distinct implications for fatigue, burnout-related symptoms, and overall well-being.

Despite growing attention, several important gaps remain. First, many studies operationalize night-shift exposure using single indicators (e.g., number of night shifts), which may not capture the multidimensional burden of night work—including sleep obtained during duty, work-related awakenings, recovery time, and perceived fatigue. Second, evidence regarding the educational and well-being impact of duty-hour reforms and scheduling interventions remains mixed and context dependent. Reviews of the 2003 ACGME duty hour rules and subsequent policy changes have documented variable effects across outcomes and settings, suggesting that local structures and service intensity may shape results [13]. Large contemporary trials of duty-hour flexibility have similarly highlighted trade-offs and complex effects across education-related outcomes and sleep-related outcomes, reinforcing the need for context-specific evidence [14–16]. Third, comparisons of ICU and non-ICU experiences are often confounded by between-person differences, because different trainees may rotate through different services. A within-person approach—comparing ICU and non-ICU post–night-shift experiences within the same residents—can reduce confounding by stable individual characteristics (e.g., baseline coping style and personal circumstances) and provide a more direct estimate of setting-related differences.

Accordingly, we conducted a paired, within-person survey in a teaching hospital residency training base, in which the same resident physicians reported their experiences after ICU and non-ICU night shifts. We aimed to: (1) estimate within-person ICU versus non-ICU differences in night-shift burden indicators, including sleep disruption and recovery opportunities, and resident well-being outcomes, including emotional exhaustion, professional efficacy, and life satisfaction; and (2) examine associations between prespecified core indicators of night-shift burden (night-shift duration, sleep obtained during duty, work-related awakenings/calls, and post-shift recovery, operationalized as rest days after the shift) and well-being outcomes using repeated-measures models that account for clustering within individuals. We specified directional hypotheses that, within the same residents, ICU night shifts would be associated with greater night-shift burden, higher emotional exhaustion, and lower life satisfaction than non-ICU night shifts. We further hypothesized that sleep-disruption indicators, particularly less sleep obtained during duty and more frequent work-related awakenings/calls, would be associated with higher emotional exhaustion.

Methods

Study design, reporting, and setting

We conducted a single-center, within-person paired (repeated-measures) survey study between August 2025 and January 2026 at a standardized residency training base in a tertiary teaching hospital in Zhejiang, China. The study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [17].

This study used a within-person repeated-measures (paired) design. Resident physicians were invited to complete the same questionnaire after night shifts in two clinical settings: (1) a non-ICU clinical department (T1) and (2) the intensive care unit (ICU; T2). To minimize recall bias and standardize the exposure window, the non-ICU questionnaire (T1) was completed within 0–24 h after a non-ICU night shift. The ICU questionnaire (T2) was completed within 0–24 h after the resident’s second ICU night shift, with a prespecified preferred completion window of 6–18 h after shift end. The second ICU night shift was selected to reduce first-shift orientation or adaptation effects and to capture a more typical ICU night-duty experience after residents had gained initial familiarity with ICU workflow. Because residents rotated through departments according to routine training schedules, the interval between T1 and T2 varied across participants. Based on routine rotation schedules, this interval was expected to range approximately from 1.5 to 3.5 months; exact individual-level intervals were not systematically recorded.

In routine rotations, not all residents undertake ICU night shifts during the study period; therefore, some participants may complete only one survey. In the present dataset, incomplete responses occurred only as T1-only surveys.

Ethics approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2025 − 0869). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because the study involved an anonymous online survey, posed minimal risk, and collected no directly identifying information. Before accessing the questionnaire, participants reviewed the study information in the online survey system and indicated their willingness to participate by clicking an agreement button. Participation was voluntary, and all procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants, recruitment, and data linkage

Eligible participants were resident physicians enrolled in standardized residency training (postgraduate year [PGY] 1–3) who undertook night shifts during the study period. Recruitment notices were posted in residency program communication groups that included approximately 310 residents. Because recruitment was conducted through open communication channels, the number of residents who viewed the invitation and met all eligibility criteria during the study period could not be verified; therefore, a formal response rate could not be calculated. Each participant was permitted to submit the survey once per setting.

To enable within-person linkage while protecting participant privacy, the survey platform generated a study-specific anonymous linkage identifier after enrollment. Participants were instructed to use the same linkage identifier when completing the subsequent ICU survey. The identifier did not contain directly identifying information and was used solely to link T1 and T2 responses. The questionnaire did not collect names, employee or student identification numbers, telephone numbers, or other directly identifying information. The analytic dataset contained only the linkage identifier and survey responses; therefore, the analysis team could not identify individual participants. A “complete pair” was defined as a participant with both a linked non-ICU (T1) and ICU (T2) questionnaire. Primary within-person comparisons were performed on complete pairs. Participants with only one completed questionnaire were retained for descriptive analyses and were included in prespecified attrition assessments.

Survey instrument and variables

A structured questionnaire was used to collect demographics (e.g., age, sex), training-related characteristics (e.g., PGY), night-shift burden indicators, lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, caffeine intake, supplement use), and well-being outcomes. The questionnaire was developed for this study and included study-specific items assessing night-shift burden, coping strategies, and lifestyle factors, as well as well-being items assessing burnout-related symptom domains and life satisfaction. The burnout-related items were intended to assess symptom domains aligned with emotional exhaustion and professional efficacy within the Maslach burnout framework [18]. The study did not administer the full MBI-GS or the MBI-GS9; therefore, these scores should be interpreted as burnout-related symptom domain scores rather than validated MBI-GS or MBI-GS9 scale scores. Life satisfaction was assessed using a 9-item measure developed for this study. Internal consistency of multi-item outcomes was assessed separately at T1 and T2 using Cronbach’s α. An English-language version of the full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1. The English version was produced by forward translation and reviewed by bilingual investigators for accuracy and clarity.

Night-shift burden indicators (exposures)

Night-shift burden was assessed using multiple self-reported indicators reflecting workload, sleep disruption, and recovery opportunities, including: (1) number of night shifts in the past 30 days; (2) duration of a single night shift; (3) sleep obtained during the night shift; (4) sleep duration before and after night shifts; (5) frequency of work-related awakenings/calls during the night shift; and (6) number of rest days after night shifts.

For inferential analyses, we prespecified a parsimonious set of core indicators to limit model complexity and reduce overfitting risk given the sample size: night-shift duration, sleep obtained during the night shift, frequency of awakenings/calls, and rest days after the night shift. These indicators were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and face validity as measures of sleep disruption and recovery. Where indicators were collected as ordered response categories, they were treated as ordinal-coded variables.

Outcomes

Primary outcome: emotional exhaustion

The primary outcome was emotional exhaustion, assessed using five items rated on a 7-point frequency response format (0 = “never” to 6 = “every day”). A total emotional exhaustion score was computed by summing the five items, with higher scores indicating greater exhaustion. The items were selected to reflect the emotional exhaustion domain within the Maslach burnout framework [18]. The study did not administer the full MBI-GS or the MBI-GS9; therefore, this score was interpreted as a burnout-related emotional exhaustion domain score rather than a validated MBI-GS or MBI-GS9 scale score.

Secondary outcomes: professional efficacy and life satisfaction

Professional efficacy was assessed using six positively worded items rated on the same 0–6 frequency scale, with higher scores indicating greater professional efficacy. Consistent with the multidimensional conceptualization of burnout, emotional exhaustion and professional efficacy were examined as distinct domains [18]. Because the study did not administer a fully validated complete burnout instrument, these scores were interpreted as burnout-related symptom domain measures.

Life satisfaction was assessed using a 9-item study-specific measure (Supplementary File 1) rated on a 7-point Likert scale. A total life satisfaction score was computed by summing items, with lower scores indicating worse life satisfaction. The 9 items were treated as a single scale based on prespecified scoring and high internal consistency in the present sample.

Covariates

We considered covariates a priori based on their potential associations with work schedules and well-being, including age, sex, PGY level, and selected lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, caffeine intake, and supplement use). In within-person analyses, stable individual characteristics are inherently controlled by the paired design; therefore, time-proximal factors (e.g., time from shift end to survey completion) were prioritized for adjustment in repeated-measures models.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses

Participant characteristics and key variables were summarized using mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables.

Primary within-person comparisons (complete paired cases)

For residents with linked ICU and non-ICU surveys (complete pairs), within-person differences in night-shift burden indicators and outcomes were evaluated using paired t tests for approximately continuous outcomes and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for ordinal-coded indicators and other non-normally distributed paired measures, as appropriate. We reported paired differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), two-sided P values, and standardized paired effect sizes (Cohen’s dz, calculated as mean[Δ]/SD[Δ]). For ordinal-coded indicators, paired nonparametric tests were used for inference; mean (SD) summaries are additionally reported descriptively for transparency and comparability across indicators. To address multiple testing across paired comparisons, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted q values were calculated as a sensitivity analysis, while the prespecified primary outcome was interpreted separately.

Repeated-measures modeling (complete paired observations)

To estimate the association of ICU setting with emotional exhaustion while accounting for within-subject correlation, we fitted generalized estimating equation (GEE) models with a Gaussian family and an exchangeable working correlation structure. Robust (sandwich) standard errors were used. Models included setting (ICU vs. non-ICU) and the prespecified core burden indicators (night-shift duration, sleep obtained during the night shift, awakenings/calls, rest days), and were additionally adjusted for hours since shift end at survey completion to reduce timing-related measurement variability. Hours since shift end was retained a priori as a measurement-timing covariate because survey completion timing could influence self-reported post-shift well-being. Ordinal-coded burden indicators were entered as numeric category scores to estimate linear trend associations; as a sensitivity analysis, we refitted the GEE model treating these ordinal indicators as categorical predictors. Model fit was summarized using the quasi-likelihood under the independence model criterion (QIC) and QICu. Results are reported as regression coefficients (β) with 95% CIs.

Prespecified sensitivity and secondary analyses

We conducted prespecified robustness checks: (1) restricting analyses to surveys completed within the preferred 6–18-hour window after shift end at both timepoints; (2) exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by PGY level; and (3) assessing attrition bias by comparing baseline (T1) distributions between participants with complete pairs and those completing only T1. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed; the sample size was determined by the number of eligible residents who completed both surveys during the study period. Subgroup analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

As an exploratory analysis, we examined whether resident training base status modified within-person ICU-associated changes. Training base was dichotomized as ICU-base versus non–ICU-base based on whether the self-reported training-base name contained the Chinese term for critical care (“重症”) in either survey. We evaluated subgroup-specific paired differences and tested effect modification using an interaction term (timepoint × ICU-base) in a GEE model (reported in Supplementary materials).

Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 20.0) for statistical testing and Python for reproducible data processing and figure generation.

Missing data and data quality procedures

Primary within-person comparisons were restricted to complete paired cases. For repeated-measures models, complete paired observations were used. Participants with missing items that precluded computation of an outcome score were excluded from outcome-specific analyses. For covariates with low missingness (e.g., < 5%), complete-case analyses were performed.

To enhance data integrity, we applied prespecified consistency rules for selected variables prior to analysis (e.g., correcting out-of-range category codes to valid ranges based on questionnaire definitions and enforcing logical consistency between binary use variables and corresponding frequency variables for supplement intake). When platform-export anomalies were identified (e.g., values outside prespecified categorical ranges), recoding was performed according to the original questionnaire coding rules.

Results

Participant flow and analytic sample

During the study period, 128 post–night-shift surveys were submitted, including 67 non-ICU surveys (T1) and 61 ICU surveys (T2). After data cleaning and verification of unique anonymous identifiers, 67 unique participants were identified. Of these, 61 participants provided complete paired responses (both T1 and T2) and comprised the primary analytic sample for within-person comparisons. The remaining six participants completed only the non-ICU survey (T1 only) and were retained for descriptive summaries and assessment of attrition bias (Fig. 1).

Primary outcome: emotional exhaustion (paired comparison)

Emotional exhaustion was higher after ICU night shifts than after non-ICU night shifts (T2 23.43 ± 9.21 vs. T1 20.03 ± 8.46; Table 2, Panel A). The mean within-person paired difference was + 3.39 points (95% CI 1.00 to 5.79; paired t test P = 0.006; FDR-adjusted q = 0.013; Cohen’s dz = 0.36). A nonparametric paired comparison was consistent (Wilcoxon signed-rank P < 0.001). Figure 1 A shows paired within-person changes, indicating an overall increase with substantial inter-individual variability.

Secondary outcomes: life satisfaction and professional efficacy (paired comparisons)

Life satisfaction was lower after ICU night shifts than after non-ICU night shifts (T2 32.41 ± 9.51 vs. T1 41.31 ± 10.95; Table 2, Panel A). The mean within-person paired difference was − 8.90 points (95% CI − 11.41 to − 6.40; paired t test P < 0.001; FDR-adjusted q < 0.001; Cohen’s dz = − 0.91), indicating a large within-person decline (Fig. 1B).

Professional efficacy showed a small decrease after ICU night shifts (T2 22.52 ± 8.36 vs. T1 24.15 ± 8.36; Δ = −1.62; 95% CI − 4.03 to 0.78). The paired t test was not statistically significant (P = 0.182), whereas the Wilcoxon signed-rank test suggested a small distribution-sensitive shift (P = 0.018; FDR-adjusted q = 0.026). Given the small effect size (Cohen’s dz = − 0.17), inconsistency across tests, and exploratory status of this outcome, professional efficacy was interpreted cautiously.

Mechanistic signal: changes in awakenings and changes in exhaustion

Within-person changes in work-related awakenings were positively associated with changes in emotional exhaustion (Spearman ρ = 0.37, P = 0.0038; Fig. 1C). Participants reporting larger increases in awakenings during ICU night shifts tended to report larger increases in exhaustion.

Adjusted repeated-measures model for exhaustion (GEE)

Adjusted repeated-measures results for emotional exhaustion are summarized in Table 3. In a GEE model accounting for within-subject correlation and including prespecified burden indicators and survey completion timing, ICU timepoint (T2 vs. T1) remained associated with higher exhaustion (β = 2.94, SE = 1.44; 95% CI 0.12 to 5.76; P = 0.041). Work-related awakenings/calls during the shift were strongly associated with higher exhaustion (β = 3.37, SE = 1.02; 95% CI 1.37 to 5.38; P < 0.001). Sleep during the shift showed a borderline association (β = 1.54; P = 0.070). Rest days after the shift were inversely associated with exhaustion (β = −1.80; 95% CI − 3.57 to − 0.02; P = 0.048). Subgroup, sensitivity, and attrition analyses

Training-year (PGY) subgroup patterns

Exploratory subgroup analyses by training year are summarized in Supplementary Table S1 and visualized in Fig. 1D. Among PGY1 residents, the estimate was imprecise and compatible with both lower and higher exhaustion after ICU night shifts (Δ = −1.88; 95% CI − 7.59 to 3.83; P = 0.495), indicating that this subgroup analysis was underpowered and should not be interpreted as evidence of no association. Increases were observed among PGY2 (Δ = +6.13; 95% CI 1.54 to 10.72; P = 0.012) and PGY3 residents (Δ = +5.07; 95% CI 2.14 to 7.99; P = 0.001). These findings were interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Timing-window sensitivity analysis (6–18 h)

Restricting analyses to participants who completed both surveys within the prespecified 6–18-hour window (n = 45 pairs) yielded consistent and slightly larger effects for exhaustion (Δ = +4.51; 95% CI 2.43 to 6.59; P < 0.001) and life satisfaction (Δ = −9.13; 95% CI − 12.32 to − 5.95; P < 0.001). Professional efficacy remained non-significant (Δ = −2.00; P = 0.15).

Attrition bias assessment

Compared with paired participants (n = 61), the six T1-only participants did not differ meaningfully at baseline on T1 exhaustion, efficacy, or life satisfaction (all P ≥ 0.33; Supplementary Table S2). However, the sleep-during-shift category at T1 differed between groups (P = 0.049), suggesting that sleep-related factors may have contributed to non-completion and should be considered when interpreting results. Additional analyses of coping strategies and lifestyle factors are provided in Supplementary Tables S3–S4.

Training-base subgroup (exploratory)

Stratified analyses by training base (ICU-base vs. non-ICU-base; base name containing the Chinese term for critical care [“重症”]) did not suggest differential ICU-associated changes by base status (between-group P = 0.428 for exhaustion; P = 0.588 for life satisfaction; Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Figure S1). In an exploratory GEE model including a timepoint × training-base interaction, there was no evidence of effect modification for emotional exhaustion (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

In this single-center paired survey of resident physicians reporting post–night-shift experiences in both ICU and non-ICU settings, ICU night shifts were followed by poorer immediate well-being, including higher emotional exhaustion and lower life satisfaction. ICU night shifts were also characterized by longer duty duration category, less sleep obtained during duty, and more frequent work-related awakenings/calls. The within-person design reduced confounding by stable individual characteristics, but the fixed measurement order means that these results should be interpreted as setting-associated differences rather than causal effects of ICU night work.

A central contribution of this study is the conceptual framing of “night-shift burden” as multidimensional. Rather than treating night work as a dose-like exposure (e.g., number of night shifts), our findings suggest that the lived architecture of night duty—particularly sleep opportunity, interruption load, and recovery opportunity—may be important for understanding immediate resident well-being. This framing is relevant to medical education because these features are potentially measurable and modifiable components of the training environment.

Positioning the findings in the duty-hour and sleep literature

Concerns about resident fatigue have long shaped graduate medical education policy. The ACGME duty-hour standards situate scheduling within a broader commitment to safe, humane learning environments that support both professional development and patient care [1]. Biologically, night work induces circadian misalignment and sleep restriction, which can impair vigilance and emotional regulation. Landmark ICU studies demonstrated that eliminating extended shifts increased sleep and reduced attentional failures [2], and that reducing interns’ work hours was associated with fewer serious medical errors in the ICU [3]. A large national survey similarly linked extended-duration shifts to increased attentional failures and adverse events, reinforcing the patient-safety relevance of fatigue mitigation [19].

At the same time, the broader duty-hour reform literature cautions against overly simple narratives. Duty-hour limits have produced mixed effects across resident well-being, education, and patient outcomes, with substantial context dependence highlighted in reviews of the post-2003 era and in subsequent “standard vs flexible” duty-hour trials [13–16]. An updated systematic review concluded that focusing on duty hours alone has not reliably improved resident well-being or patient care and may have unintended educational consequences [20]. These findings imply that features of night work most proximal to sleep disruption and recovery—rather than shift counts alone—may be more actionable targets.

Our results align with this direction by emphasizing interruption load and recovery opportunity. In paired analyses, ICU shifts involved markedly less sleep during duty and more frequent work-related awakenings/calls, and within-person increases in awakenings were correlated with within-person increases in exhaustion. In adjusted repeated-measures modeling, ICU timepoint remained associated with higher exhaustion, while awakenings/calls showed a strong association. These findings are compatible with a plausible pathway in which sleep fragmentation and sustained interruption burden may contribute to emotional strain; however, the observational design and fixed measurement order preclude causal inference. Sleep disruption is closely linked to emotional processing and regulation [21, 22].

Resident well-being as an education-relevant outcome

Resident well-being is increasingly conceptualized as an outcome of the training environment rather than an individual problem [23]. Burnout-related symptoms are common during residency [9], and burnout has been associated with adverse consequences for clinicians and health systems, including workforce sustainability and the learning climate [10]. Within this framework, emotional exhaustion can be interpreted as a training-environment signal with educational implications, potentially affecting engagement, learning efficiency, and perceptions of program support.

Although our study did not assess depressive symptoms, external evidence supports an association between workload and mental health outcomes in early-career physicians. Higher work hours have been associated with greater increases in depressive symptoms among first-year physicians [24], and similar associations have been reported in other national contexts [25]. This broader literature supports the plausibility that modifiable schedule and recovery features—particularly those that curtail sleep or extend physiological strain—may influence resident well-being in the short term and potentially cumulatively over time [24, 25].

Moreover, resident burnout has been linked to both working hours and coping-related factors in multi-institutional samples [26]. Together, these findings support a program-level approach that treats well-being as an educational quality signal rather than solely an individual resilience issue.

Why ICU night work may feel different: intensity, interruptions, and recovery

ICU night shifts may be associated with greater burden because of high acuity, sustained vigilance, rapid decision-making, and frequent interruptions that fragment available sleep opportunity [11, 12]. Prior analyses suggest that rotation context can shape sleep and alertness and that ICU rotations may have distinctive workload patterns [11]. Our within-person comparison foregrounds these setting-specific features, although fixed ordering prevents separation of ICU setting from temporal and rotation-sequence effects. In addition to less sleep during duty and more frequent awakenings, residents reported greater compensatory sleep after ICU shifts, consistent with acute recovery needs. The convergence of paired ICU–non-ICU differences, the within-person correlation between increased awakenings and increased exhaustion, and adjusted modeling that retains an ICU association supports the interpretation that ICU night duty may carry an incremental “interruption-plus-recovery” burden.

Professional efficacy showed a smaller and less consistent pattern than exhaustion and life satisfaction. The paired t test did not indicate a statistically significant mean difference, whereas the Wilcoxon signed-rank test suggested a small distribution-sensitive shift. This discrepancy may reflect non-normality, ordinal response behavior, and/or outlier sensitivity. Given the small effect size, exploratory status of this outcome, and lack of formal validation of the modified item set, definitive conclusions about professional efficacy cannot be drawn. From an education perspective, emotional exhaustion and life satisfaction may be more sensitive short-term indicators of high-intensity night work than professional efficacy, which may reflect more stable self-appraisal or longer-term adaptation.

Educational implications: what programs can measure and change

Several practical implications follow for residency program evaluation and quality improvement [23].

First, programs may benefit from monitoring night-shift burden using a small set of interpretable measures beyond shift counts—such as sleep obtained during duty, frequency of work-related awakenings/interruptions, and post-shift recovery time. These indicators are proximal to biological mechanisms and directly reflect residents’ lived experience.

Second, redesign efforts may focus on improving the quality of night work, not only reducing its quantity. Strategies could include triage systems to reduce avoidable overnight interruptions, clearer escalation pathways, and protected recovery time following high-intensity duties. This approach is consistent with evidence that reducing duty hours alone has not reliably improved outcomes [20], whereas interventions aligned with sleep opportunity and recovery may better match the underlying pathway [6, 27].

Third, organizational approaches to clinician well-being appear to yield measurable, although often modest, improvements, reinforcing that burnout is not merely an individual resilience issue [28]. Recent meta-analytic evidence from randomized trials suggests that physician burnout interventions may produce statistically detectable but clinically modest changes, underscoring the need for mechanism-informed and context-specific program redesign [29]. Systematic reviews focused on resident wellness interventions also emphasize the importance of theory-informed, program-integrated strategies rather than isolated add-ons [30]. In practice, schedule redesign may be paired with supportive supervision, mentorship, and accessible mental health resources—especially in high-intensity rotations such as ICU.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths supporting interpretability in a medical education setting. We used a within-person design to compare ICU versus non-ICU post–night-shift experiences, reducing confounding by stable individual characteristics. We operationalized night-shift burden using multiple indicators reflecting workload, sleep disruption, and recovery, prespecified core indicators to limit analytic flexibility, and demonstrated high internal consistency for multi-item outcomes at both timepoints. We also conducted sensitivity analyses addressing survey timing, multiple comparisons, and the modeling of ordinal burden indicators.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although the within-person design reduced confounding by stable individual characteristics, the order of exposure was not randomized or counterbalanced: T1 always referred to a non-ICU night shift and T2 to the second ICU night shift. Therefore, ICU-associated differences cannot be fully separated from temporal effects, seasonal variation, training-time accumulation, or within-rotation adaptation and fatigue carry-over. Exact individual-level intervals between T1 and T2 were not systematically recorded, which further limited our ability to adjust for elapsed time. These findings should therefore be interpreted as within-person setting-associated differences rather than causal effects of ICU night work. Second, all measures were self-reported; future work should triangulate with objective schedule data and objective or semi-objective sleep measures (e.g., actigraphy, sleep diaries). Third, the study used burnout-related domain items rather than administering the full MBI-GS or MBI-GS9, and formal construct validation of the modified item set (e.g., factor analysis) was not performed because of the modest sample size. Fourth, the convenience sample and single-center setting limit generalizability and precision, particularly for subgroup and multivariable analyses. Fifth, we did not systematically collect detailed histories of sleep disorders, prior mental health diagnoses, or family and caregiving responsibilities such as children at home, which may have influenced exhaustion, sleep recovery, and life satisfaction. Finally, although completion timing was standardized (0–24 h) and results were robust in the prespecified 6–18 h sensitivity analysis, timing-related measurement variability cannot be fully excluded. Attrition may also have introduced selection bias.

Future directions

Future research should extend these findings using multi-center and longitudinal designs with counterbalanced or more precisely timed measurement schedules to clarify temporal relationships between night-shift burden and resident outcomes. Pragmatic interventions that reduce avoidable interruptions, improve sleep opportunity during duty, and protect recovery time could be evaluated using implementation-focused designs that preserve educational opportunities while mitigating fatigue. Mixed-methods work may also help identify which components of ICU night work residents find most burdensome and which supports are most feasible and acceptable, enabling better alignment between scheduling design and educational goals.

Conclusion

Higher night-shift burden—particularly reduced sleep opportunity during duty, frequent overnight interruptions, and constrained recovery—was associated with poorer immediate well-being among resident physicians. Compared with non-ICU night shifts, ICU night shifts were followed by higher emotional exhaustion and substantially lower life satisfaction. Conceptualizing night-shift burden as a multidimensional feature of the residency learning environment may help programs identify practical targets for scheduling design and supportive interventions; however, findings should be interpreted as setting-associated differences rather than causal effects because of the fixed measurement order and observational design.
